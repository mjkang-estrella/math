10. Representing measures for extremal functions



We now apply the square-function estimates to extremal functions on a
fixed convex domain. Let $\Omega$ be a bounded convex domain whose
boundary is a Jordan curve, let $A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})$, and assume





$W(A)\subset\overline{\Omega},\qquad\sigma(A)\subset\Omega.$



We write $\mathcal{A}(\overline{\Omega})$ for the functions that are continuous
on $\overline{\Omega}$ and holomorphic in $\Omega$. For
$h\in H^{\infty}(\Omega)$, the matrix $h(A)$ is defined by the Cauchy
integral in the introduction. Put





$\psi_{\Omega}(A)=\sup\{\left\lVert h(A)\right\rVert:h\in H^{\infty}(\Omega),\ \left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\infty,\Omega}\leq 1\}.$



We call $f$ a normalized extremal function for $(A,\Omega)$ if





$\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty,\Omega}=1,\qquad\left\lVert f(A)\right\rVert=\psi_{\Omega}(A).$





Proposition 10.1.


Under the preceding assumptions, $\psi_{\Omega}(A)\leq 2$. The supremum is
attained, and every normalized extremal function has the form





$f=B\circ\varphi,$



where $\varphi:\Omega\to\mathbb{D}$ is a conformal bijection and $B$ is a
finite Blaschke product, that is, a finite product of automorphisms of
$\mathbb{D}$, of degree at most $n-1$. In particular,




(10.1)

$\left\lvert f\right\rvert=1\qquad\text{on }\partial\Omega.$



If $\Omega=W(A)^{\circ}$, then





$\psi_{\Omega}(A)=\psi(A).$






Proof.


Let $\varphi:\Omega\to\mathbb{D}$ be a conformal bijection. Since
$\partial\Omega$ is a Jordan curve, $\varphi$ and $\varphi^{-1}$ extend
continuously to the closures [19, Chapter 2]. For $h\in H^{\infty}(\Omega)$ and
$0<r<1$, put





$h_{r}(z)=h\bigl(\varphi^{-1}(r\varphi(z))\bigr).$



Then $h_{r}\in\mathcal{A}(\overline{\Omega})$,
$\left\lVert h_{r}\right\rVert_{\overline{\Omega}}\leq\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\infty,\Omega}$, and
$h_{r}(A)\to h(A)$ as $r\to 1^{-}$. By Mergelyan’s theorem, $h_{r}$ can be
approximated uniformly on $\overline{\Omega}$ by polynomials. Since
$W(A)\subset\overline{\Omega}$, Theorem 1.3 gives





$\left\lVert h_{r}(A)\right\rVert\leq 2\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\infty,\Omega}.$



Letting $r\to 1^{-}$ proves $\psi_{\Omega}(A)\leq 2$.



Crouzeix’s finite-dimensional extremal theorem gives attainment and the
stated form of every normalized extremal function
[10, Theorem 2.1]. If
$\Omega=W(A)^{\circ}$, polynomials give
$\psi_{\Omega}(A)\geq\psi(A)$. Repeating the radial approximation above
and using the definition of $\psi(A)$ in place of the constant $2$
gives the reverse inequality.
∎




Put





$K=\psi_{\Omega}(A),$



and assume $K>1$. Choose a normalized extremal function $f$ as in
Proposition 10.1 and a unit vector $x$ such that





$\left\lVert f(A)x\right\rVert=K.$



Write





$F=f(A),\qquad y=\frac{1}{K}Fx.$



Then





$Fx=Ky,\qquad F^{*}y=Kx.$



Apply Lemma 5.2 at the scalar level to the
homomorphism $h\mapsto h(A)$ and to the extremal function $f$. This
gives




(10.2)

$\left\langle Fx,x\right\rangle=0,\qquad x\perp y.$



This is the usual extremal orthogonality relation. See also
[6, Section 4.1].
The next lemma is [6, Theorem 4.5]. We include the proof because the
uniqueness statement is used below.



Lemma 10.2.


There is a unique probability measure $\mu$ on $\partial\Omega$ such
that




(10.3)

$\left\langle h(A)x,x\right\rangle=\int_{\partial\Omega}h\,d\mu\qquad(h\in\mathcal{A}(\overline{\Omega})).$



Moreover,




(10.4)

$\int_{\partial\Omega}f\,d\mu=0.$






Proof.


Define





$\ell(h)=\left\langle h(A)x,x\right\rangle\qquad(h\in\mathcal{A}(\overline{\Omega})).$



Let $h\in\mathcal{A}(\overline{\Omega})$ and suppose that $\Ree h\geq 0$ on
$\Omega$. For $t\geq 0$,





$\left\lVert e^{-th}f\right\rVert_{\overline{\Omega}}\leq 1.$



Extremality therefore gives





$\left\lVert e^{-th(A)}Fx\right\rVert\leq K.$



The function
$t\mapsto\left\lVert e^{-th(A)}Fx\right\rVert^{2}$ has a maximum at the endpoint $t=0$
of $[0,\infty)$. Since $F$ commutes with $h(A)$ and $F^{*}Fx=K^{2}x$, its
right derivative at $0$ is nonpositive. This gives





$\Ree\ell(h)\geq 0.$





Let $\mathcal{R}$ be the real parts on $\partial\Omega$ of functions in
$\mathcal{A}(\overline{\Omega})$, and define





$\mathcal{L}(\Ree h)=\Ree\ell(h).$



This is well defined. If $\Ree h=0$ on $\partial\Omega$, then $h$ is a
purely imaginary constant. If $\Ree h\geq 0$ on $\partial\Omega$, the minimum principle
gives
$\Ree h\geq 0$ in $\Omega$. The preceding argument and
$\mathcal{L}(1)=1$ therefore show that $\mathcal{L}$ is a positive
functional of norm one on $\mathcal{R}$. Let
$\varphi:\Omega\to\mathbb{D}$ be a conformal bijection, extended
continuously to the closures. The real and imaginary parts of
$\varphi^{k}$, restricted to $\partial\Omega$, belong to $\mathcal{R}$
and, when $\varphi(\zeta)=e^{it}$, equal $\cos(kt)$ and
$\sin(kt)$. Since
the real trigonometric polynomials are uniformly dense in
$C(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})$, the space $\mathcal{R}$ is uniformly dense in
$C(\partial\Omega,\mathbb{R})$. Hence $\mathcal{L}$ extends uniquely
to a positive functional of norm one on that space. The Riesz representation theorem
gives a unique probability measure $\mu$ such that





$\Ree\ell(h)=\int_{\partial\Omega}\Ree h\,d\mu.$



Applying this identity to $-ih$ also gives the imaginary parts and
proves (10.3). Finally,
(10.2) gives (10.4).
∎




The following two identities are the only matrix coefficients needed
below.



Proposition 10.3.


For every $h\in\mathcal{A}(\overline{\Omega})$,




(10.5)

$\displaystyle\left\langle h(A)y,y\right\rangle$
$\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}h\,d\mu,$



(10.6)

$\displaystyle K\left\langle h(A)y,x\right\rangle$
$\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}hf\,d\mu.$






Proof.


Since $F$ commutes with $h(A)$,





$\displaystyle K^{2}\left\langle h(A)y,y\right\rangle$
$\displaystyle=\left\langle h(A)Fx,Fx\right\rangle$





$\displaystyle=\left\langle h(A)x,F^{*}Fx\right\rangle$





$\displaystyle=K^{2}\left\langle h(A)x,x\right\rangle.$



This proves (10.5). Using $F^{*}y=Kx$ and then
(10.5),





$\displaystyle K\left\langle h(A)y,x\right\rangle$
$\displaystyle=\left\langle h(A)y,F^{*}y\right\rangle$





$\displaystyle=\left\langle Fh(A)y,y\right\rangle$





$\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}hf\,d\mu.$



∎




Put





$m=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{W(A)},\qquad s=\frac{K}{m}.$



Since $m\leq 1$ and the scalar Crouzeix inequality applied to $f$ on
$W(A)$ gives $K\leq 2m$, one has $m>0$ and $1<s\leq 2$.
For a Borel map $g$ and a measure $\lambda$, the
pushforward measure $g_{*}\lambda$ is defined by
$g_{*}\lambda(E)=\lambda(g^{-1}(E))$ for every Borel set $E$. We write
$m_{\mathbb{T}}$ for normalized arclength measure on $\mathbb{T}$.



Theorem 10.4.


Set





$\varepsilon_{K,m}=m^{2}\vartheta(s),\qquad s=\frac{K}{m}.$



Then, for every $h\in\mathcal{A}(\overline{\Omega})$,




(10.7)

$\left|\int_{\partial\Omega}fh\,d\mu\right|\leq\varepsilon_{K,m}\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\overline{\Omega}}.$



Moreover,




(10.8)

$0\leq\varepsilon_{K,m}\leq 2m\sqrt{4m^{2}-K^{2}}\leq 2\sqrt{4-K^{2}}.$



If $\nu=f_{*}\mu$, then $\nu$ is a probability measure on $\mathbb{T}$ and




(10.9)

$\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}}\zeta^{k}\,d\nu(\zeta)\right|\leq\varepsilon_{K,m}\qquad(k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}).$



Let $(A_{j},\Omega_{j},f_{j},x_{j})$ be a sequence satisfying the
assumptions above, and define
$K_{j},m_{j},s_{j},\mu_{j}$, and $\nu_{j}=(f_{j})_{*}\mu_{j}$ as above. If
$s_{j}=K_{j}/m_{j}\to 2$, then $\nu_{j}$ converges weakly to $m_{\mathbb{T}}$.




Proof.


The normalized matrix $T=F/m$ satisfies the two square-function
estimates for every $0<\tau<1$, has norm $s$, and attains its norm at
$x$. Theorem 9.7 gives





$\left\lVert T^{2}x\right\rVert\leq\vartheta(s).$



Since $Fy=F^{2}x/K$,




(10.10)

$\left\lVert Fy\right\rVert\leq\frac{m^{2}\vartheta(s)}{K}.$



By (10.6) and commutativity,





$\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}fh\,d\mu$
$\displaystyle=K\left\langle h(A)y,x\right\rangle$





$\displaystyle=\left\langle Fh(A)y,y\right\rangle$





$\displaystyle=\left\langle h(A)Fy,y\right\rangle.$



The definition of $K$ gives
$\left\lVert h(A)\right\rVert\leq K\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\overline{\Omega}}$. Together with
(10.10), this proves
(10.7).



Since $\vartheta(s)\leq\eta(s)$,
Lemma 9.4 gives





$\varepsilon_{K,m}\leq 2m^{2}\sqrt{4-\frac{K^{2}}{m^{2}}}=2m\sqrt{4m^{2}-K^{2}}.$



Since $m\leq 1$ and $K\leq 2m$, both factors on the right in





$4-K^{2}-m^{2}(4m^{2}-K^{2})=(1-m^{2})\bigl(4(1+m^{2})-K^{2}\bigr)$



are nonnegative. This proves the second inequality in
(10.8).



By (10.1), $\nu$ is supported on $\mathbb{T}$. Taking
$h=f^{k-1}$ in (10.7) gives
(10.9) for $k\geq 1$. The estimates for negative $k$
follow by conjugation. For a sequence in the final assertion,
$\vartheta(s_{j})\to 0$ and $m_{j}\leq 1$, so
$\varepsilon_{K_{j},m_{j}}\to 0$. Thus every nonzero Fourier coefficient of
$\nu_{j}$ tends to zero. Weak convergence follows from the uniform
density of the trigonometric polynomials in $C(\mathbb{T})$.
∎




Corollary 10.5.


If





$K=2m,\qquad m=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{W(A)},$



then





$\int_{\partial\Omega}fh\,d\mu=0\qquad(h\in\mathcal{A}(\overline{\Omega})),$



and





$f_{*}\mu=m_{\mathbb{T}}.$






Proof.


The assumption is $s=2$, so $\vartheta(s)=0$. The first assertion
follows from Theorem 10.4. Taking $h=f^{k-1}$
gives vanishing of all positive Fourier coefficients of $f_{*}\mu$. The
negative coefficients are their complex conjugates, so
$f_{*}\mu=m_{\mathbb{T}}$.
∎
