3. Positive kernels and iterated square functions



For $q\geq 1$, let





$\mathbb{B}_{q}=\{w\in\mathbb{C}^{q}:\left\lVert w\right\rVert<1\}.$



We write $w^{\mathsf{T}}a=\sum_{j=1}^{q}w_{j}a_{j}$. This expression is
bilinear.



Definition 3.1.


A scalar kernel $K$ on $\mathbb{B}_{q}$ is positive if





$[K(w_{i},w_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{N}\succeq 0$



for every finite family $w_{1},\ldots,w_{N}\in\mathbb{B}_{q}$. An
$M_{d}(\mathbb{C})$-valued kernel is positive when the corresponding block
matrices are positive.




Lemma 3.2.


Let $a\in\overline{\mathbb{B}_{q}}$ and put





$c_{a}(w)=\frac{1+w^{\mathsf{T}}a}{1-w^{\mathsf{T}}a}.$



Then




(3.1)

$K_{a}(w,z)=\frac{c_{a}(w)+\overline{c_{a}(z)}}{1-z^{*}w}$



is a positive kernel on $\mathbb{B}_{q}$.




Proof.


A direct calculation gives




(3.2)

$K_{a}(w,z)=\frac{2}{(1-w^{\mathsf{T}}a)(1-\overline{z^{\mathsf{T}}a})}\left(1+\frac{z^{*}\{I-\overline{a}(\overline{a})^{*}\}w}{1-z^{*}w}\right).$



The matrix $I-\overline{a}(\overline{a})^{*}$ is positive. If $M\succeq 0$,
then





$\frac{z^{*}Mw}{1-z^{*}w}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(z^{*}Mw)(z^{*}w)^{k}$



is a positive kernel. Hence the expression in parentheses in
(3.2) is positive. Multiplication by a scalar function
of $w$ and its conjugate at $z$ preserves positivity.
∎




We next give the double-layer formula in the form used below. This is
the positive operator-valued double-layer calculus of Delyon–Delyon
and Crouzeix–Palencia
[17, 16]. A detailed recent account
is given in [33].



Lemma 3.3.


Let $B\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C})$, and let $\Omega$ be a bounded convex domain with
smooth boundary such that $W(B)\subset\Omega$. There is a positive map preserving the identity





$\Phi:C(\partial\Omega)\longrightarrow M_{d}(\mathbb{C})$



with the following property. If $h$ is holomorphic on a neighborhood
of $\overline{\Omega}$, then




(3.3)

$2\Phi(h)=h(B)+C_{h}^{*},\qquad C_{h}\in\alg(B).$






Proof.


Let $\nu(\zeta)$ be the outward unit normal to $\partial\Omega$, viewed
as a complex number, and let $s$ be arclength. Set




(3.4)

$\mu(\zeta,B)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\nu(\zeta)(\zeta I-B)^{-1}+\overline{\nu(\zeta)}(\overline{\zeta}I-B^{*})^{-1}\right).$



Put $Z=\zeta I-B$ and $R=Z^{-1}$. Convexity gives





$\nu Z^{*}+\overline{\nu}Z\succeq 0.$



Indeed, its quadratic form at a unit vector $x$ is





$2\operatorname{Re}\left\{\overline{\nu}(\zeta-x^{*}Bx)\right\}\geq 0.$



Congruence by $R$ gives
$\nu R+\overline{\nu}R^{*}\succeq 0$. Thus
$\mu(\zeta,B)\succeq 0$.



For the positive orientation of $\partial\Omega$, one has
$\nu\,ds=-i\,d\zeta$. The Cauchy formula and its adjoint give





$\int_{\partial\Omega}\mu(\zeta,B)\,ds=2I.$



Define





$\Phi(g)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}g(\zeta)\mu(\zeta,B)\,ds.$



Then $\Phi$ is positive and $\Phi(1)=I$. Splitting the two terms in
(3.4) gives (3.3),
where





$C_{h}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\Omega}\overline{h(\zeta)}(\zeta I-B)^{-1}\,d\zeta.$



Every resolvent of $B$ belongs to $\alg(B)$. Hence $C_{h}\in\alg(B)$.
∎




Let $F=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{q})^{\mathsf{T}}$, where the $f_{j}$ are holomorphic
near $\overline{\Omega}$ and




(3.5)

$\sum_{j=1}^{q}\left\lvert f_{j}(z)\right\rvert^{2}\leq 1\qquad(z\in\overline{\Omega}).$



For $w\in\mathbb{B}_{q}$, define




(3.6)

$h_{w}(\zeta)=\frac{1+w^{\mathsf{T}}F(\zeta)}{1-w^{\mathsf{T}}F(\zeta)},\qquad H(w)=\Phi(h_{w}).$



The denominator does not vanish. The function $H$ is holomorphic and
$H(0)=I$.



Lemma 3.4.


The kernel




(3.7)

$\mathcal{L}_{H}(w,z)=\frac{H(w)+H(z)^{*}}{1-z^{*}w}$



is positive on $\mathbb{B}_{q}$.




Proof.


For $\zeta\in\partial\Omega$, put $a=F(\zeta)$. By
(3.5), $a\in\overline{\mathbb{B}_{q}}$. For finite points
$w_{1},\ldots,w_{N}$, Lemma 3.2 and
(3.4) show that





$[K_{F(\zeta)}(w_{i},w_{j})\mu(\zeta,B)]_{i,j=1}^{N}\succeq 0.$



Integration gives





$[\mathcal{L}_{H}(w_{i},w_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{N}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}[K_{F(\zeta)}(w_{i},w_{j})\mu(\zeta,B)]_{i,j=1}^{N}\,ds.$



∎




Put





$T_{j}=f_{j}(B),\qquad T(w)=\sum_{j=1}^{q}w_{j}T_{j}.$



The double-layer formula applied to $h_{w}$ gives




(3.8)

$H(w)-(I-T(w))^{-1}\in\alg(B^{*}).$



Indeed,





$h_{w}(B)=(I+T(w))(I-T(w))^{-1}=2(I-T(w))^{-1}-I,$



and (3.8) follows from
(3.3). The sample points and the additional
point at the origin used below are the multivariable form of the
one-variable sampling argument in [24].



Proposition 3.5.


Let $B\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C})$ have simple spectrum. Let $\Omega$ be a bounded
smooth convex domain with $W(B)\subset\Omega$. Suppose that
$f_{1},\ldots,f_{q}$ are holomorphic near $\overline{\Omega}$ and satisfy
(3.5). Then, for every $0<\tau<1$,




(3.9)

$\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\tau^{2k}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{W}_{k}}f_{\alpha}(B)^{*}f_{\alpha}(B)\preceq\frac{1}{1-\tau}I.$






Proof.


Choose a diagonalization





$B=S\diag(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d})S^{-1}$



and put $G=S^{*}S$. For $1\leq i\leq d$, set





$\lambda_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}(\beta_{i})\\
\vdots\\
f_{q}(\beta_{i})\end{pmatrix}\in\overline{\mathbb{B}_{q}}.$



For $1\leq j\leq q$, let





$\Lambda_{j}=\diag(f_{j}(\beta_{1}),\ldots,f_{j}(\beta_{d})).$



Thus $T_{j}=S\Lambda_{j}S^{-1}$. Put





$D(w)=\sum_{j=1}^{q}w_{j}\Lambda_{j}=\diag(w^{\mathsf{T}}\lambda_{1},\ldots,w^{\mathsf{T}}\lambda_{d}).$



Since the eigenvalues $\beta_{i}$ are distinct, polynomial interpolation
gives





$\alg(B^{*})=\{(S^{-1})^{*}\Delta S^{*}:\Delta\text{ is diagonal}\}.$



It follows from (3.8) that there is a diagonal
holomorphic matrix $\Psi(w)$, with $\Psi(0)=0$, such that




(3.10)

$S^{*}H(w)S=G(I-D(w))^{-1}+\Psi(w)G.$





Fix $0<\tau<1$. Set





$c_{ij}=\lambda_{i}^{*}\lambda_{j},\qquad w_{i}=\tau\overline{\lambda_{i}}.$



Some of the points $w_{i}$ may coincide. Repeated sampling points are
allowed in the positivity condition. We have





$w_{j}^{*}w_{i}=\tau^{2}c_{ij},\qquad w_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\lambda_{j}=\tau c_{ij}.$



Define Hermitian matrices $P,Q,Y$ by




(3.11)

$P_{ij}=\frac{G_{ij}}{1-\tau^{2}c_{ij}},\qquad Q_{ij}=\frac{G_{ij}}{1-\tau c_{ij}},\qquad Y=Q-P.$





Let $e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}$ be the standard basis of $\mathbb{C}^{d}$. Fix
$u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}$. In the positive-kernel
inequality for (3.7), first take the congruence by $S$ in
each block. Use the points $w_{i}$ and the vectors $u_{i}e_{i}$. Add the
point $0$ with the vector




(3.12)

$v=-G^{-1}Pu.$



Write $\psi_{i}$ for the $i$th diagonal entry of $\Psi$. The contribution
of $\Psi$ to the resulting quadratic form is





$2\operatorname{Re}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\overline{u_{i}}\,\psi_{i}(w_{i})(Gv+Pu)_{i},$



which is zero by (3.12).



The contribution of $G(I-D(w))^{-1}$ from the nonzero sample points is
the matrix $M$ with entries





$M_{ij}=\frac{2G_{ij}}{(1-\tau c_{ij})(1-\tau^{2}c_{ij})}.$



The scalar identity





$\frac{2}{(1-\tau x)(1-\tau^{2}x)}=\frac{2}{1-\tau^{2}x}+\frac{2}{1-\tau}\left(\frac{1}{1-\tau x}-\frac{1}{1-\tau^{2}x}\right)$



gives




(3.13)

$M=2P+\frac{2}{1-\tau}Y.$



The block between the nonzero sample points and the origin is $G+Q$,
and the origin block is $2G$. Positivity and
(3.12) give





$0\preceq M-(G+Q)G^{-1}P-PG^{-1}(G+Q)+2PG^{-1}P.$



Using (3.13) and $Q=P+Y$, we obtain




(3.14)

$\frac{2}{1-\tau}Y-YG^{-1}P-PG^{-1}Y\succeq 0.$





Define





$\widetilde{T}_{j}=G^{1/2}\Lambda_{j}G^{-1/2},\qquad\widehat{P}=G^{-1/2}PG^{-1/2},\qquad\widehat{Q}=G^{-1/2}QG^{-1/2},\qquad\widehat{Y}=\widehat{Q}-\widehat{P}.$



For $\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})$, put





$\Lambda_{\alpha}=\Lambda_{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\Lambda_{\alpha_{k}},\qquad\widetilde{T}_{\alpha}=\widetilde{T}_{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\widetilde{T}_{\alpha_{k}},$



with $\Lambda_{\varnothing}=\widetilde{T}_{\varnothing}=I$. For every
$k\geq 0$,





$\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{W}_{k}}(\Lambda_{\alpha}^{*}G\Lambda_{\alpha})_{ij}=G_{ij}c_{ij}^{k}.$



Expanding the denominators in (3.11) gives norm-convergent
series




(3.15)

$\displaystyle\widehat{P}$
$\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\tau^{2k}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{W}_{k}}\widetilde{T}_{\alpha}^{*}\widetilde{T}_{\alpha},$



(3.16)

$\displaystyle\widehat{Q}$
$\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\tau^{k}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{W}_{k}}\widetilde{T}_{\alpha}^{*}\widetilde{T}_{\alpha}.$



Since





$\tau^{k}-\tau^{2k}\geq\frac{1-\tau}{\tau}\tau^{2k}\qquad(k\geq 1),$



we have $\widehat{P}\succeq I$ and




(3.17)

$\widehat{Y}\succeq\frac{1-\tau}{\tau}(\widehat{P}-I).$



Congruence of (3.14) by $G^{-1/2}$ gives




(3.18)

$\frac{2}{1-\tau}\widehat{Y}-\widehat{Y}\widehat{P}-\widehat{P}\widehat{Y}\succeq 0.$



Let $e$ be a unit eigenvector of $\widehat{P}$, with
$\widehat{P}e=\gamma e$, and put $y=e^{*}\widehat{Y}e$. Then $\gamma\geq 1$
and





$0\leq 2\left(\frac{1}{1-\tau}-\gamma\right)y.$



If $\gamma>1/(1-\tau)$, then (3.17) gives $y>0$, which is
impossible. Hence




(3.19)

$\widehat{P}\preceq\frac{1}{1-\tau}I.$





The polar decomposition $S=UG^{1/2}$ gives
$T_{j}=U\widetilde{T}_{j}U^{*}$. Thus (3.15) and
(3.19) are unitarily equivalent to
(3.9).
∎




We use two elementary approximation facts.



Lemma 3.6.


Let $K\subset\mathbb{C}$ be compact and convex, and let $V$ be an open set
containing $K$. There is a bounded convex domain $\Omega$ with smooth
boundary such that





$K\subset\Omega,\qquad\overline{\Omega}\subset V.$



Moreover, matrices with simple spectrum are dense in $M_{d}(\mathbb{C})$, and




(3.20)

$d_{\mathrm{H}}(W(A),W(B))\leq\left\lVert A-B\right\rVert.$






Proof.


Choose $\delta>0$ such that
$K+\delta\overline{\mathbb{D}}\subset V$. Let $d_{K}$ be the distance to $K$.
This is a convex $1$-Lipschitz function. Let $v_{\eta}$ be a smooth
convolution of $d_{K}$ at scale $\eta$, and put





$u_{\eta}(z)=v_{\eta}(z)+\eta\left\lvert z\right\rvert^{2}.$



The Lipschitz estimate gives $\left\lvert v_{\eta}-d_{K}\right\rvert\leq\eta$. Choose $R$ with $K\subset R\overline{\mathbb{D}}$, and take $\eta>0$ so small
that





$\eta(1+R^{2})<\delta/4,\qquad\delta/2-\eta>\delta/4.$



Then the set





$\Omega=\{z:u_{\eta}(z)<\delta/4\}$



contains $K$. If $d_{K}(z)\geq\delta/2$, then
$v_{\eta}(z)\geq d_{K}(z)-\eta>\delta/4$, so
$\Omega\subset K+(\delta/2)\mathbb{D}$. The function
$u_{\eta}$ is smooth, proper, and strictly convex. It has at most one
critical point, which is its global minimum. The minimum is less than
$\delta/4$, so the gradient does not vanish on the displayed level
set. Thus $\Omega$ has smooth boundary.



Simple-spectrum matrices are dense because the discriminant of the
characteristic polynomial is a nonzero polynomial in the matrix
entries. Finally, for every unit vector $x$,





$\left\lvert x^{*}(A-B)x\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert A-B\right\rVert.$



Applying this with $A$ and $B$ interchanged proves
(3.20).
∎




Proof of Theorem 1.3 for matrices.


First suppose that every $f_{j}$ is holomorphic on an open neighborhood
of $W(A)$. Fix $\varepsilon>0$. Lemma 3.6
gives a bounded smooth convex domain $\Omega$ such that
$W(A)\subset\Omega$, every $f_{j}$ is holomorphic near
$\overline{\Omega}$, and





$\sup_{z\in\overline{\Omega}}\sum_{j=1}^{q}\left\lvert f_{j}(z)\right\rvert^{2}\leq 1+\varepsilon.$



Put $\widetilde{f}_{j}=(1+\varepsilon)^{-1/2}f_{j}$. Choose
simple-spectrum matrices $B_{n}\to A$. By
(3.20), $W(B_{n})\subset\Omega$ for all
large $n$. Proposition 3.5 applies to $B_{n}$ and
the functions $\widetilde{f}_{j}$.



Fix $N$. Retain the terms with $k\leq N$ and let $n\to\infty$.
Continuity of the holomorphic functional calculus gives





$\sum_{k=0}^{N}\tau^{2k}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{W}_{k}}\widetilde{f}_{\alpha}(A)^{*}\widetilde{f}_{\alpha}(A)\preceq\frac{1}{1-\tau}I.$



Letting $\varepsilon\to 0^{+}$ gives the same inequality with $f_{\alpha}$
in place of $\widetilde{f}_{\alpha}$. The partial sums are increasing and
bounded. In finite dimensions their positive increments have summable
traces, and therefore summable norms. This proves norm convergence and
the column estimate.



For general $f_{j}\in\mathcal{A}(W(A))$, choose polynomial tuples converging
uniformly to $(f_{1},\ldots,f_{q})$. After multiplication by a scalar
converging to one, the polynomial tuples satisfy the pointwise column
bound. Applying the preceding result to each finite partial sum and
passing to the limit proves the column estimate. The row estimate
follows by applying the column estimate to $A^{*}$ and to





$f_{j}^{\#}(z)=\overline{f_{j}(\overline{z})}.$



The functions of $A$ commute, so reversal of a word does not change
its product.
∎




We now pass to bounded operators. The following argument is the
finite-dimensional Krylov compression used in numerical-range
functional-calculus estimates [11].



Completion of the proof of Theorem 1.3.


Suppose first that $f_{1},\ldots,f_{q}$ are polynomials, each of degree at
most $m$. Fix a unit vector $x\in H$ and an integer $N\geq 0$. Let





$\mathcal{K}_{N,x}=\operatorname{span}\{x,Ax,\ldots,A^{mN}x\},\qquad B=P_{\mathcal{K}_{N,x}}A|_{\mathcal{K}_{N,x}}.$



The numerical range of the compression $B$ is contained in $K_{A}$.
For every word $\alpha$ with $|\alpha|\leq N$,




(3.21)

$f_{\alpha}(B)x=f_{\alpha}(A)x.$



Indeed, the degree of $f_{\alpha}$ is at most $mN$, and
$B^{j}x=A^{j}x$ for $0\leq j\leq mN$. Applying the matrix case to $B$ and
using (3.21) gives





$\sum_{k=0}^{N}\tau^{2k}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{W}_{k}}\left\lVert f_{\alpha}(A)x\right\rVert^{2}\leq\frac{1}{1-\tau}.$



Since this holds for every unit vector $x$, the corresponding operator
partial sum is bounded by $(1-\tau)^{-1}I$.



Now let $f_{1},\ldots,f_{q}\in\mathcal{A}(K_{A})$ satisfy
(1.6). For each $n$, choose polynomials
$p_{j,n}$ converging uniformly to $f_{j}$ on $K_{A}$. There are numbers
$\varepsilon_{n}\to 0^{+}$ such that





$\sum_{j=1}^{q}\left\lvert p_{j,n}(z)\right\rvert^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon_{n})^{2}\qquad(z\in K_{A}).$



Apply the polynomial result to $p_{j,n}/(1+\varepsilon_{n})$. For each
fixed $N$, uniform convergence and continuity of the functional
calculus permit passage to the limit. Thus every column partial sum is
bounded by $(1-\tau)^{-1}I$.
The increasing sequence of partial sums therefore converges
strongly to an operator satisfying the same bound.



In fact, the convergence is in operator norm. Fix
$\tau<\sigma<1$ and put





$S_{k}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{W}_{k}}f_{\alpha}(A)^{*}f_{\alpha}(A).$



For $M>N$, the bound already proved at $\sigma$ gives





$\displaystyle 0$
$\displaystyle\preceq\sum_{k=N+1}^{M}\tau^{2k}S_{k}$





$\displaystyle\preceq\left(\frac{\tau}{\sigma}\right)^{2(N+1)}\sum_{k=N+1}^{M}\sigma^{2k}S_{k}$





$\displaystyle\preceq\frac{(\tau/\sigma)^{2(N+1)}}{1-\sigma}I.$



Thus the partial sums are Cauchy in operator norm.



Apply the column estimate to $A^{*}$ and the reflected functions to
obtain the row estimate. Keeping only the terms of degrees zero and one
gives





$\sum_{j=1}^{q}f_{j}(A)^{*}f_{j}(A)\preceq\frac{1}{\tau(1-\tau)}I,$



and the corresponding row inequality. The minimum of the scalar
factor occurs at $\tau=1/2$, which proves
(1.10). For sharpness, take $q=1$,
$f_{1}(z)=z$, and





$A=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.$



Then $W(A)=\overline{\mathbb{D}}$ and $\left\lVert A\right\rVert=2$.
∎
