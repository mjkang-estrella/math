                                                      Barnette’s Conjecture claims that all cubic, 3-connected, planar, bipartite graphs
                                                   are Hamiltonian. We give a translation of this conjecture into the matching-theoretic
                                                   setting. This allows us to relax the requirement of planarity to give the equivalent
                                                   conjecture that all cubic, 3-connected, Pfaffian, bipartite graphs are Hamiltonian.
                                                      A graph, other than the path of length three, is a brace if it is bipartite and
                                                   any two disjoint edges are part of a perfect matching. Our perspective allows us to
                                                   observe that Barnette’s Conjecture can be reduced to cubic, planar braces. We show
                                                   a similar reduction to braces for cubic, 3-connected, bipartite graphs regarding four
                                                   stronger versions of Hamiltonicity. Note that in these cases we do not need planarity.
                                                      As a practical application of these results, we provide some supplements to a
                                                   generation procedure for cubic, 3-connected, planar, bipartite graphs discovered by
                                                   Holton et al. [Hamiltonian Cycles in Cubic 3-Connected Bipartite Planar Graphs,
                                                   JCTB, 1985 ]. These allow us to check whether a graph we generated is a brace.


                                           1 Introduction
                                           In 1884, Tait conjectured that every cubic, 3-connected, planar graph is Hamiltonian [Tai84],
                                           which Tutte disproved in 1946 [Tut46]. Subsequently, Tutte conjectured that the result might
                                           hold if the graph was cubic, 3-connected, and bipartite [Tut71], which was disproven by Horton
                                           [BM76]. Since all known counterexamples to Tait’s conjecture are non-bipartite and all known
                                           counterexamples to Tutte’s conjecture are non-planar, Barnette’s conjecture from 1969 seems
                                           like a sensible compromise. Let B be the class of all cubic, 3-connected, planar, bipartite graphs.

                                           Conjecture 1.1 (Barnette [Bar69]). All graphs in B are Hamiltonian.

                                           This conjecture has received considerable attention and is known to be true for some subclasses
                                           of B, but an approach to the problem in general continues to be elusive. See Goodey’s [Goo75]

paper from 1975 for the historically most significant example of a partial solution and see the
work of Bagheri et al. [BGFFS21] for a current partial solution which generalises Goodey’s
result. A related conjecture, also due to Barnette, which asserts that all cubic, 3-connected,
planar graphs in which every face has size at most six, was recently resolved by Kardoš [Kar20].
Many parts of the proof make heavy use of the constraint on the size of the faces, which leaves
us with no direct way to translate the methods used there. Significant effort has also been
devoted to finding strengthenings of Conjecture 1.1; that is statements which also concern B,
but demand more than a simple Hamiltonian cycle. For examples of strengthenings see [Her05]
for an overview.
Using the perspective of Matching Theory, we formulate a result reducing the Conjecture 1.1 to
a subset of B. In a graph G, we call a set of mutually disjoint edges a matching and we call it
a perfect matching if the unions of the edges in the set contain all vertices of G. A connected
graph G with |V (G)| ≥ 2k + 2 is called k-extendable if for every matching F ⊆ E(G) with
|F | ≤ k there exists a perfect matching M with F ⊆ M . If G is isomorphic to C4 or a bipartite,
2-extendable graph, it is called a brace.

Lemma 1.2. Conjecture 1.1 holds if and only if every cubic, planar brace is Hamiltonian.

We show that this result can be derived from a combination of statements from across the litera-
ture (see Section 4). It hints at an extension of the conjecture to a larger class of graphs. Despite
Tutte’s Conjecture on the matter being false, there is a substantial class of cubic, 3-connected,
non-planar, bipartite graphs which are Hamiltonian if and only if Barnette’s conjecture holds.
We relegate the introduction of the notion of Pfaffian graphs to Section 4.

Theorem 1.3. Conjecture 1.1 holds if and only if all cubic, 3-connected, Pfaffian, bipartite
graphs are Hamiltonian.

To prove this result, we discuss how Hamiltonian cycles interact with a certain type of cut
central to structural Matching Theory. We call a connected graph G matching covered if it is 1-
extendable. A cut ∂(X) in a matching covered graph G is called tight if for any perfect matching
M of G, we have |∂(X) ∩ M | = 1. We call a tight cut non-trivial if each side of the cut contains
at least two vertices. If we contract one of the sides of a tight cut into a single vertex, we call the
resulting graph a tight cut contraction. By repeatedly searching out non-trivial tight cuts and
repeating this contraction operation, we arrive at a list of graphs which do not have non-trivial
tight cuts. Such graphs are called bricks if they are non-bipartite and braces otherwise. The
procedure we allude to here is called the tight cut decomposition due to Lovász [Lov87].
Focusing on matching covered graphs might at first seems like a strong restriction, but the
following result by Kőnig implies that all k-regular, bipartite graphs are in fact matching covered.

Theorem 1.4 (Kőnig [Kön16]). For all k ∈ N, the edge set of a k-regular, bipartite graph can
be partitioned into k perfect matchings.

Corollary 1.5. Any k-regular, connected, bipartite graph is matching covered.

In particular, if we consider a Hamiltonian graph G with an even number of vertices, then it
turns out that all the structure belonging to its Hamiltonian cycles is contained in the subgraph



                                                  2
induced by those edges of G that are contained in a perfect matching. We call this subgraph
the cover graph of G.

Observation 1.6. Let G be a Hamiltonian graph with an even number of vertices, then every
Hamiltonian cycle of G can be decomposed into two perfect matchings.

It turns out that many important properties are preserved under taking tight cut contractions
and are thus also preserved for the braces of a matching covered, bipartite graph. We prove that
this is also true for some types of Hamiltonicity in cubic, 3-connected, bipartite graphs. This is
interesting on its own, but it also contributes to the proof of Theorem 1.3.
By Pk we denote a path on k vertices and note that Pk has length k − 1. We call a graph G
with at least k vertices Pk -Hamiltonian if any path of length k − 1 in G is contained in some
Hamiltonian cycle of G. Furthermore, we say that a graph G has the H − -property, respectively
the H +− -property, if for any edge in G there exists a Hamiltonian cycle in G which avoids said
edge, respectively for any two distinct edges e and f there exists a Hamiltonian cycle containing
e and avoiding f .

Theorem 1.7. Let G be a cubic, 3-connected, bipartite graph. The following hold
   i) If the braces of G are P4 -Hamiltonian, then G is P4 -Hamiltonian.
  ii) G has the H − -property if and only if the braces of G have the H − -property.
 iii) G is P3 -Hamiltonian if and only if the braces of G are P3 -Hamiltonian.
 iv) G has the H +− -property if and only if the braces of G have the H +− -property.

There have also been computational efforts concerning Conjecture 1.1. The first of which, by
Holton et al. [HMM85] in 1985, also introduced an elegant generation method for the graphs in
B and this is still the method used in recent efforts (see [BGM21]). To support the relevance
of Lemma 1.2 to the effort of resolving Conjecture 1.1, we provide a short list of amendments
to the generation procedure in Section 5 that allow us to keep track of whether a graph we
have generated is a brace or not. For each generated graph this requires us to save some more
information. This amounts to at most half the number of vertices of the graph. Using this
additional information we can check in linear time whether the generated graph is a brace. The
current state of the computational effort is that all graphs in B with at most 90 vertices are
Hamiltonian, announced by Brinkmann et al. [BGM21].
More or less separate from the rest of the presentation, we present the theorem which motivated
this project. It is almost trivial to prove, but hopefully motivates further inquires into the
connections between Matching Theory and the study of Hamiltonicity.

Theorem 1.8. The bricks and braces of the cover graph of a Hamiltonian graph with an even
number of vertices are Hamiltonian.

In Section 2, we start by introducing many of the basic matching-theoretic concepts needed for
our approach, including some more rigorous definitions for concepts mentioned in this introduc-
tion. We then give a short proof of Theorem 1.8 in Section 3. Following this, we get into the
core of the article and tackle cubic, Pfaffian, bipartite graphs in Section 4. This is then followed
by a discussion on the generation of graphs in B and how to keep track of their tight cuts in
Section 5. In Section 6, we discuss our results and provide some open questions.

2 Preliminaries
All graphs we consider are simple. Since we will mostly deal with bipartite graphs, we establish
that by G = (A ∪ B, E(G)) we denote a bipartite graph G with the edge set E(G) and a vertex
set V (G), such that A and B are the two colour classes that partition V (G). A graph G is called
connected if all pairs of vertices can be connected by a path and it is called k-connected if for
any set S ⊆ V (G) with |S| ≤ k − 1 the graph G − S remains connected. The components of a
graph are its maximal connected subgraphs. A component containing only one vertex is called
trivial.
Given a cycle or path, we will call it even if it contains an even number of edges and odd
otherwise. We call a graph G k-regular if all vertices in G have degree exactly k. If a graph
is 3-regular, we also call it cubic. A subgraph H of a graph G is called spanning if it contains
all vertices of G. A spanning cycle is commonly called a Hamiltonian cycle 1 , which leads to a
graph containing such a cycle being called Hamiltonian.
Given a subgraph H of a graph G, we call H conformal if G − H contains a perfect matching
and H is called M -conformal if there exists an M ∈ M(G) such that M contains no edges with
one endpoint in G − H and the other in H.
A cut around X in a graph G, for a set X ⊆ V (G), is a set ∂G (X) ⊆ E(G), such that an edge
uv is in ∂G (X) if and only if it has exactly one endpoint in X. We will drop the index G if the
graph is clear from the context. Any set of edges F ⊆ E(G) is called a cut, if there exists a set
of vertices X ⊆ V (G) such that F = ∂(X). The order of a cut ∂(X) is defined as |∂(X)|. We
also set X = V (G) \ X and call X and X the shores of the cut ∂(X). If X or X only contain
one element, we call ∂(X) trivial. A cut ∂(X) with the property that |∂(X) ∩ M | = 1 for all
perfect matchings M of G is called tight. Given a tight cut ∂(X) in a matching covered graph
G, we denote the result of contracting X into a single vertex c 6∈ V (G) and deleting all resulting
parallel edges and loops as G/(X→ c), which is called a tight cut contraction.

Lemma 2.1. If G is a matching covered graph, then the following properties of G are preserved
for its tight cut contractions: planarity, (1-)connectivity, and being bipartite.

Proof. Proving that both planarity and connectivity are preserved through tight cut contractions
is easy using common methods. The proof that being bipartite is preserved however uses an
argument which we will make use of frequently.
Let G = (A ∪ B, E(G)) be a matching covered, bipartite graph, let ∂(X) be a tight cut in G, and
let M be a perfect matching of G, with ab ∈ M ∩ ∂(X). We can assume w.l.o.g. that a ∈ X ∩ A
and note that ab is the only edge of M found in ∂(X), since it is tight. Thus if n = |X ∩ A|,
only one the vertices from X ∩ A is matched with a vertex in X and thus |X ∩ B| = n − 1.
Suppose there exists an edge uv ∈ ∂(X)\{ab} with u ∈ (X ∩B) and let M ′ be a perfect matching
of G such that uv ∈ M ′ . It is now easy to observe that it is impossible for M ′ ∩ E(G[X]) to
cover the n vertices in A, as only n − 2 vertices from B can be matched with them. Therefore all
endpoints of edges from ∂(X) which lie in X also lie in A. Thus we can colour the contraction

Lemma 2.2. Let G be a matching covered, bipartite graph and let ∂(X) be a tight cut in G.

In general, for any graph G with a perfect matching, it suffices to consider the spanning subgraph
of G which contains only those edges which can be found in some perfect matching of G, at
least when we are interested in the matching-theoretic properties of G. We call this graph the
cover graph of G. By repeatedly contracting non-trivial tight cuts in the cover graph of G and
applying the same procedure to the tight cut contractions produced this way, we perform a tight
cut decomposition. This results in a collection of graphs which cannot be decomposed further,
and thus have no non-trivial tight cuts. These graphs are called braces, if they are bipartite,
and bricks, if they are not bipartite. The tight cut decomposition has the notable property that
it always results in the same list of bricks and braces, no matter in which order the contractions
are applied [Lov87]. For our purposes it will suffice to only consider braces, since we concentrate
on bipartite graphs. There is a more useful characterisation of braces using extendability, a
proof for which can for example be found in [LP09].

Theorem 2.3. A graph is a brace if and only if it is either isomorphic to C4 or 2-extendable.

We now introduce an operation which can be seen as a reversed tight cut contraction. Let G1
and G2 be two matching covered graphs with specified vertices u ∈ V (G1 ) and v ∈ V (G2 ), such
that they are incident to the same number of edges, V (G1 ) ∩ V (G2 ) = ∅, and there exists a
bijection ϕ : NG1 (u) → NG2 (v). We call H a splice of G1 and G2 at u and v, if H is the result
of taking (G1 − u) + (G2 − v) and adding the edges {xy | x ∈ NG1 (u) and ϕ(x) = y}. The cut
∂H (V (G1 ) \ {u}) is called a splicing cut.
Since G1 and G2 are matching covered and the splicing cut F corresponds to two trivial tight
cuts, one in G1 and the other in G2 , it is easy to see that H is again matching covered and in
particular for every edge e in H there exists a perfect matching of H which contains e and exactly
one edge in C. Additionally, the splice of two bipartite graphs is again bipartite. Using these
observations, a result by Carvalho et al. [dCLM02] (see Corollary 2.22.) implies the following.

Lemma 2.4. Let H be the splice of G1 and G2 and let F be the corresponding splicing cut in
H. If H is bipartite, then F is tight.

Lastly, let us note two useful properties concerning k-extendable graphs.

Theorem 2.5 (Plummer [Plu80]). If G is k-extendable for some k > 1, then it is also (k − 1)-
extendable and (k + 1)-connected.

Theorem 2.6 (Plummer [Plu86]). A bipartite graph G = (A ∪ B, E(G)) is k-extendable
if and only if for all distinct a1 , . . . , ak ∈ A and all distinct b1 , . . . , bk ∈ B the graph
G − {a1 , . . . , ak , b1 , . . . , bk } has a perfect matching.

3 A Note on Hamiltonian cycles in general graphs
We first formalise an observation concerning the interaction of tight cuts and conformal cycles.

Lemma 3.1. Let G be a matching covered graph with a tight cut ∂(X) and a perfect matching
M of G. For every M -conformal cycle C with E(C) ∩ ∂(X) 6= ∅, we have |E(C) ∩ ∂(X)| = 2.

Proof. Clearly, since C is a cycle, we have |E(C) ∩ ∂(X)| ≥ 2. We can assume towards a
contradiction that |E(C) ∩ ∂(X)| > 2. Since C is even, the set (M \ E(C)) ∪ (E(C) \ M ) is a
perfect matching, which contradicts the tightness of ∂(X).

Though the consequence of this lemma for Hamiltonian graphs is easy to deduce, to the best
of the authors’ knowledge it has gone unstated so far. The following immediately implies
Theorem 1.8.

Lemma 3.2. Any tight cut contraction of a the cover graph of a Hamiltonian graph with an
even number of vertices is Hamiltonian.

Proof. Let G be an even graph, ∂(X) be a tight cut in G, and C be a Hamiltonian cycle in G. It
is easy to see that E(C) contains two disjoint perfect matchings M and M ′ of G, making C an M -
conformal cycle. Using Lemma 3.1, we thus know that |E(C) ∩ ∂(X)| = 2. Set G′ = G/(X→ c)
and C ′ = C/(X→ c), then clearly C ′ is a Hamiltonian cycle of G′ .

4 Hamiltonicity in cubic, bipartite, Pfaffian graphs
To prove our main theorem, we will need to discuss tight cuts in cubic, bipartite graphs a bit
more. Both lemmas that follow can be found in Section 7 of [McC00]. The first lemma can be
generalised to arbitrary k-regular graphs via a simple application of Menger’s theorem, though
the forward direction only holds when k is odd.

Lemma 4.1 (McCuaig [McC00]). Let G be a cubic, 3-connected, bipartite graph. A non-trivial
cut in G contains three mutually disjoint, pairwise non-adjacent edges if and only if it is tight.

Lemma 4.2 (McCuaig [McC00]). Let G be a cubic, 3-connected, bipartite graph with a non-
trivial tight cut ∂(X). Then both G/(X→ c) and G/(X→ c) are cubic, 3-connected, and bipar-
tite.

The following strengthenings of Conjecture 1.1 will prove central to our efforts.

Theorem 4.3 (Hertel [Her05] (corollary of a result by Kelmans [Kel86])). Conjecture 1.1 holds
if and only if all graphs in B have the H − -property.

Theorem 4.4 (Hertel [Her05]). Conjecture 1.1 holds if and only if all graphs in B are P4 -
Hamiltonian.

It should be noted that for the cubic, bipartite graphs the H − -property is equivalent to P3 -
Hamiltonicity. We are now ready to prove the first major lemma of this section.

Lemma 4.5. Let G be a cubic, 3-connected, bipartite graph, with a non-trivial tight cut ∂(X),
and let G1 = G/(X→ c) and G2 = G/(X→ c) be the two tight cut contractions belonging to
∂(X). The following hold
   i) If G1 and G2 are P4 -Hamiltonian, then G is P4 -Hamiltonian.
  ii) G has the H − -property if and only if G1 and G2 have the H − -property.
 iii) G is P3 -Hamiltonian if and only if G1 and G2 are P3 -Hamiltonian.
 iv) G has the H +− -property if and only if G1 and G2 have the H +− -property.

Proof. Let Eci be the set of edges in Gi which are incident with c. There are three edges in ∂(X)
and these are contracted into three edges in Eci for each i ∈ {1, 2}. We say that an edge e ∈ Eci ,
for i ∈ {1, 2}, corresponds to the edge f ∈ ∂(X), if e ∩ f 6= ∅.
Towards the first item, suppose that G1 and G2 are P4 -Hamiltonian. Let P ⊆ G be a path of
length three in G. Suppose first that P ⊆ G1 and let H1 be a Hamiltonian cycle of G1 with
P ⊆ G1 . Let e, f ∈ ∂(X) be the two edges in G which correspond to the two edges in E(H) ∩ Ec1
and let e′ , f ′ ∈ Ec2 be the two edges in G2 which correspond to e and f respectively.
Clearly, e′ and f ′ together form a path of length two and since G2 is P4 -Hamiltonian, there
therefore exists a Hamiltonian cycle H2 in G2 which uses both e′ and f ′ . In G we can now find
the Hamiltonian cycle ((H1 − c) + (H2 − c)) + {e, f } which uses P . Of course an analogous
construction can be carried out if P ⊆ G2 .
If P is neither contained in G1 nor in G2 it must use at least one edge of e ∈ ∂(X), with ei being
the edge in Gi corresponding to e, for i ∈ {1, 2}. We note that due to the edges in ∂(X) being



                                                7
mutually disjoint and pairwise non-adjacent (see Lemma 4.1), P cannot use a second edge of
∂(X). Let us further suppose that P uses one edge f1 of E(G[X]) and one edge f2 of E(G[X])
each. For our construction, we also choose an edge g ∈ ∂(X) \ {e} and the corresponding edges
g1 and g2 in G1 and G2 . Since both G1 and G2 are P4 -Hamiltonian, we can now choose a
Hamiltonian cycle Hi in Gi containing the edges ei and gi for each i ∈ {1, 2}. As in the previous
case, this lets us construct a Hamiltonian cycle ((H1 − c) + (H2 − c)) + {g, e} for G which uses
P.
Finally, we need to consider the case in which two of the edges of P lie in G1 , or G2 respectively.
W.l.o.g. we can assume that |E(P ) ∩ E(G1 )| = 2 and let e ∈ ∂(X) ∩ E(P ) again be the edge of
the path which lies in the tight cut, with ei being the edge in Gi corresponding to e, for i ∈ {1, 2}.
We choose a Hamiltonian cycle H1 in G1 which uses the three edges in (E(P ) ∩ E(G1 )) ∪ {e1 },
which form a path. Let f be the edge in H1 which is incident with c that is distinct from e
and let f ′ be the corresponding edge in G, with f ′′ being the edge corresponding to f ′ in G2 .
This allows us to choose a Hamiltonian cycle H2 in G2 which uses the edges e2 and f ′′ . A
Hamiltonian cycle in G which contains P can then be constructed using the following expression
((H1 − c) + (H2 − c)) + {f ′ , e}.
Now we can move on to considering the second point of the statement. For the forward direction,
we assume that G has the H − -property. Let e ∈ E(G1 ) be an edge and let us first suppose that
it is not incident to c. In G there exists a Hamiltonian cycle H that avoids e. Clearly, H/(X→ c)
is a Hamiltonian cycle of G1 , which still avoids e. If on the other hand e ∈ E(G1 ) is incident
to c, then let e′ ∈ ∂(X) be the corresponding edge in G. We can now proceed exactly as in the
previous case. Of course, these arguments also work for any edge in E(G2 ).
We can now concern ourselves with the backward direction and assume that both G1 and G2
have the H − -property. Let e ∈ E(G) be an edge which does not lie in ∂(X). W.l.o.g. we
can assume that e ∈ E(G1 ). Let H1 be a Hamiltonian cycle in G1 which avoids e and let
f, g ∈ E(H1 ) be the two edges incident with c, let f ′ , g′ ∈ ∂(X) be the corresponding edges in
G, and let f ′′ , g ′′ ∈ E(G2 ) be the corresponding edges to f ′ and g′ in G2 . We can now choose
a Hamiltonian cycle H2 in G2 which avoids the edge incident with c which is neither f ′′ nor g ′′ .
Since G2 is cubic, by Lemma 4.2, H2 must therefore use f ′′ and g′′ , which allows us to build the
Hamiltonian cycle ((H1 − c) + (H2 − c)) + {f ′ , g′ } for G.
In the final case, we have e ∈ E(G) ∩ ∂(X). Let f, g ∈ (∂(X) \ {e}) be the two remaining edges
in ∂(X) and let ei , fi , gi be the corresponding edges in Gi for i ∈ {1, 2}. For both i ∈ {1, 2}, we
can now choose a Hamiltonian cycle Hi in Gi which avoids ei and this allows us to construct
the Hamiltonian cycle ((H1 − c) + (H2 − c)) + {f, g} for G which avoids e.
For the third point of the statement, we only need to observe that a cubic, 3-connected, bipartite
graph is P3 -Hamiltonian if and only if it has the H − -property. This has been noted in the
literature before (see [Her05]).
For the forward direction of the fourth point, we assume that G has the H +− -property. Let
e, f ∈ E(G1 ) be two distinct edges. We will want to include e and avoid f . Clearly, as in
the previous points, we can simply find an appropriate Hamiltonian cycle in G using e, or its
corresponding edge, and avoiding f , or its corresponding edge, and then contract it to fit G1 .
This of course also works for any two edges in G2 .

If this is not the case, then either e or f are exclusively to one of the two tight cut contractions.
W.l.o.g. let us say that e or its corresponding edge is found in G1 and f or its corresponding

A graph G is called Pfaffian if it has an orientation of its edges such that for every perfect
matching M of G and every M -conformal cycle C, the number of edges in C oriented in the
same way is odd, for either direction of traversal. Pfaffian graphs have a myriad of connections
to other problems, both in mathematics and outside of it (see [McC04] for an excellent overview).
In particular, the following remarkable statement holds.

Theorem 4.6 (Kasteleyn [Kas67]). All planar graphs are Pfaffian.

The problem of characterising Pfaffian graphs ended up being one of the central, motivating
questions in Matching Theory. As with many problems in matching theory, it can be reduced
to bricks and braces.

Theorem 4.7 (Vazirani and Yannakakis [VY89]). A matching covered graph is Pfaffian if and
only if its bricks and braces are Pfaffian.

a cycle C of length four and we have V (Gi )\V (C) 6= ∅ for all i ∈ {1, 2, 3}. Further, let S ⊆ E(C)

be some subset of the edges of C. A trisum of G1 , G2 , G3 at C is a graph ( 3i=1 Gi )−S. For later
use, we call a trisum cubic if S = E(C). Note that the cubic trisum of three cubic, bipartite
graphs is itself again cubic.

Theorem 4.8 (McCuaig [McC04], Robertson et al. [RST99]). A brace is Pfaffian if and only if
it is the Heawood graph (see Figure 2b) or can be constructed from planar braces by repeated
use of the trisum operation.

Corollary 4.9. A cubic brace is Pfaffian if and only if it is the Heawood graph or can be
constructed from cubic, planar braces by repeated use of the cubic trisum operation.

Proof. If a cubic brace is constructed from cubic, planar braces by repeated use of the cubic
trisum operation, then it is clearly constructed from planar braces by repeated use of the trisum
operation and is thus Pfaffian due to Theorem 4.8. Of course, if we are considering the Heawood
graph, there is nothing to prove.
Now, let us assume to the contrary of the forward direction that G is a cubic, Pfaffian brace
which cannot be constructed by repeated use of the cubic trisum operation starting from cubic
planar braces. We can assume that G is non-planar, not the Heawood graph, and a minimal
counterexample to the statement.
According to Theorem 4.8, there exist three Pfaffian braces G1 , G2 , and G3 whose pairwise
intersection is a cycle C of length four, such that G is the trisum of G1 , G2 , G3 at C. Suppose
for some i ∈ {1, 2, 3} there exists some v ∈ V (Gi − C) which has degree greater than three in
Gi , then clearly G would contain a vertex with degree greater than three and thus not be cubic.
We can thus make the narrower supposition that in fact a vertex u ∈ V (C) has degree greater
than three in Gi for some i ∈ {1, 2, 3}. However, note that exactly two edges of u are contained
in E(C) and all other edges are kept in the construction of G and thus u also has degree greater
than three in G, again contradicting that it is cubic.
Thus G1 , G2 , G3 are cubic, Pfaffian braces which, due to the minimality of G, were constructed
from cubic, planar braces by repeated application of the cubic trisum operation. Therefore the
only option left is for E(G) ∩ E(C) to be non-empty, but this would again create vertices of
degree greater than three. This lets us conclude that G was indeed constructed in the way
suggested by the statement, which completes our proof.

Let us now briefly observe how a Hamiltonian cycle in a cubic graph interacts with a C4 .



                                                 10
Observation 4.10. Let G be a cubic graph that is not isomorphic to K4 , let C be a cycle of
length four in G, and let H be a Hamiltonian cycle in G. Then C ∩ H is either isomorphic to
P4 or to two copies of P2 .

Using this observation, we can easily deduce the following if the graph is also P4 -Hamiltonian.

Observation 4.11. Let G be a cubic, P4 -Hamiltonian graph that is not isomorphic to K4
and let C be a cycle of length four in G. For any path P ⊆ C of length four, there exists a
Hamiltonian cycle H of G, such that C ∩ H = P . Similarly, for any perfect matching M of C,
there exists a Hamiltonian cycle H of G, such that E(C ∩ H) = M .

We can now prove a key lemma that lets us preserve P4 -Hamiltonicity through cubic trisums.

Lemma 4.12. Let G1 , G2 , and G3 be cubic, Pfaffian braces and let G be the result of a cubic
trisum of G1 , G2 , G3 at C. If G1 , G2 , and G3 are P4 -Hamiltonian, then G is P4 -Hamiltonian.

Proof. Let P be a path of length three in G. Suppose P ⊂ G1 and let H1 be a Hamiltonian
cycle of G1 with P ⊂ H1 , which exists thanks to the P4 -Hamiltonicity of G1 . We note that due
to Observation 4.10, the non-trivial components of H1 − E(C) must be paths of even length
ending on two vertices which are adjacent on C. Furthermore, we let V (C) = {u0 , u1 , u2 , u3 },
such that E(C) = {{ui , uj } | i ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3} and i + 1 ≡ j (mod 4)}.
First, we consider the case in which H1 − E(C) contains a single non-trivial component Q. Note
that Q is a path which must have all vertices in V (G1 ) \ V (C) as its internal vertices. W.l.o.g.
we can assume that u0 and u1 are the endpoints of Q.
Now, let us make use of Observation 4.11. We let H2 be a Hamiltonian cycle of G2 with
E(H2 ) ∩ E(C) = {u0 u1 , u2 u3 }, and let H3 be a Hamiltonian cycle of G3 with E(H3 ) ∩ E(C) =
{u3 u0 , u0 u1 , u1 u2 }. Let R1 be the component of H2 − {u0 u3 , u1 u2 } that is a path with the
endpoints u1 and u2 , and let R2 be the other component of H2 − {u0 u3 , u1 u2 }. Further, let R3
be the non-trivial component of H3 − {u3 u0 , u0 u1 , u1 u2 }, which means it has the endpoints u2
and u3 . It is now easy to see that Q + R1 + R3 + R2 is a Hamiltonian cycle of G.
Similarly, if H1 − E(C) contains two non-trivial components, we can choose two Hamiltonian
cycles in G2 and G3 to complete these two paths into a Hamiltonian cycle of G. We can of
course proceed analogously if P is entirely contained in G2 or G3 .
Let us therefore assume that P ⊂ G, but P 6⊂ Gi , for any i ∈ {1, 2, 3}. We note that, since G is
cubic, there must therefore exist exactly one j ∈ [3] with E(P ) ∩ E(Gj ) = ∅. W.l.o.g. we assume
that j = 3 and further, we can also assume that |E(G1 ) ∩ E(P )| = 1 and |E(G2 ) ∩ E(P )| = 2.
Since P runs through one of the vertices in V (C), where we performed the cubic 4-sum, we also
know that |P ∩ V (C)| = 1 and we can assume that u0 ∈ V (P ).
In G2 , we choose a Hamiltonian cycle H2 with P ∩ G2 ⊂ H2 such that u0 u1 ∈ E(H2 ). Note
that this immediately implies u2 u3 ∈ E(H2 ). Suppose now that u1 u2 ∈ E(H2 ). (We know that
u0 u3 6∈ E(H2 ), since H2 enters C through an edge outside of C at u0 .) Let v1 be the neighbour
of u1 in G1 which is not on C. Inside of G1 , we choose a Hamiltonian cycle H1 using u0 u1 , u1 v1 ,
and the lone edge in E(P ) ∩ E(G1 ). Note that H1 must then also use the edge u2 u3 , but cannot
use u0 u3 or u1 u2 , since G1 is cubic. We can now choose a Hamiltonian cycle H3 in G3 which




                                                11
uses all edges of C except for u1 u2 . We can now piece together a Hamiltonian cycle for G which
contains P as in the previous cases.
If on the other hand we have u1 u2 6∈ E(H2 ), we choose a Hamiltonian cycle H1 in G1 which
uses u0 u3 , u3 u2 , and the sole edge in E(P ) ∩ E(G1 ). For H1 , we observe that this implies
u2 u1 ∈ E(H1 ), since G1 is cubic. Finally, we then choose a Hamiltonian cycle H3 for G3 which
uses all edges of C except for u2 u3 . As in all previous cases, this allows us to construct a
Hamiltonian cycle for G which uses P , concluding our proof.

Before we prove our main theorem, we note that while the fact that it suffices to consider braces
for Conjecture 1.1 is a novel statement in the literal sense, a quick trip through the literature
yields a proof of Lemma 1.2 independent of our approach. Kelmans proved the following in 1986.
We call a graph cyclically k-connected, for k ∈ N, if for any cut F of order less than k, at most
one component of G − F contains a cycle.

Theorem 4.13 (Kelmans [Kel86]). Conjecture 1.1 holds if and only if every cyclically 4-
connected graph in B is Hamiltonian.

The interested reader may find it easier to get an overview of the work of Kelmans on
Conjecture 1.1 in an article by Alt et al. [APSW16] in which translations of his results along
with sketches for their proofs are provided. The next result was proven as part of a larger project
on n-extendability.

Theorem 4.14 (Holton and Plummer [HP91]). Let k ∈ N be a positive integer and let G be a
(k + 1)-regular, (k + 1)-connected, bipartite graph. Then if G is at least cyclically k 2 -connected,
G is also k-extendable.

Corollary 4.15. If G is a cubic, 3-connected, bipartite graph, then G is cyclically 4-connected
if and only if G is a brace.

Proof. With k = 2, Theorem 4.14 covers one direction. For the other, let G be a brace. By defi-
nition, G cannot have a non-trivial tight cut and thus, using Lemma 4.1 and the 3-connectivity
of the graph, we conclude that G possesses no non-trivial cuts of order 3 or less. Hence G is
cyclically 4-connected.

Thus, as a corollary of Theorem 4.14, we know that Conjecture 1.1 holds if and only if every
brace in B is Hamiltonian, which was claimed in the introduction as Lemma 1.2. This leads us
into the proof of our main theorem.

Theorem 4.16. The following statements are equivalent:
    i) All graphs in B are Hamiltonian.
   ii) All braces in B are Hamiltonian.
  iii) All braces in B are P4 -Hamiltonian.
  iv) All cubic, Pfaffian braces are P4 -Hamiltonian.
   v) All cubic, 3-connected, Pfaffian, bipartite graphs are P4 -Hamiltonian.
  vi) All cubic, 3-connected, Pfaffian, bipartite graphs are Hamiltonian.




                                                 12
Proof. The equivalence of (i) and (ii) can be taken from Lemma 1.2. That (i) implies (iii)
is a consequence of Theorem 4.4. To have (iii) imply (iv), we need to use Lemma 4.12 and
Corollary 4.9. (We note that the Heawood graph is also P4 -Hamiltonian.) Since the first point
of Theorem 1.7 allows us to infer the P4 -Hamiltonicity of a set of cubic, bipartite, matching
covered graphs from their braces, we also know that (iv) implies (v). The implication from (v)
to (vi) is trivial and (vi) to (i) is implied by Theorem 4.6.

We conclude this section by giving a reversed version of Lemma 4.12. However, in this direction
we only ask for Hamiltonicity. This might prove useful should Conjecture 1.1 fail, or if there
exists a way to show that non-planar, cubic, Pfaffian braces are Hamiltonian.
For this purpose, we first note that there exists an earlier characterisation of Pfaffian bipartite
graphs. Since this characterisation did not yield a polynomial time algorithm for recognising
Pfaffian bipartite graphs, the search continued, but for our purposes it will prove quite useful. A
bisubdivision of a graph H is the result of replacing the edges of H with pairwise disjoint paths
of odd length (possibly just P2 ).

Theorem 4.17 (Little [Lit75]). A bipartite graph with a perfect matching is Pfaffian if and
only if it does not contain a bisubdivision of K3,3 as a conformal subgraph.

We will also need a method for finding these subgraphs. Let C be a cycle of length four in
a brace G with V (C) = {a, b, c, d} and {a, c}, {b, d} 6∈ E(C). We call a pair of paths L and
R—with L having the endpoints a and c, and R having the endpoints b and d—a conformal
cross over C if C + L + R is a conformal subgraph of G.

Lemma 4.18 (Giannopoulou and Wiederrecht [GW21]). Let G be a brace and let C be a cycle
of length four in G. There is a conformal cross over C if and only if C is contained in a conformal
bisubdivision of K3,3 .

And with this, we turn to the proof of the lemma promised above.

Lemma 4.19. Let G1 , G2 , and G3 be Pfaffian braces, and let G be cubic and the result of a
cubic trisum of G1 , G2 , G3 at C. If G is Hamiltonian, then G1 , G2 , and G3 are Hamiltonian.

Proof. It is easy to observe that the fact that G is cubic implies that G1 , G2 , and G3 will also
be cubic. Now, let H be a Hamiltonian cycle in G. We will prove that we can construct a
Hamiltonian cycle for G1 . The construction for G2 and G3 can then be performed analogously.
Since H is a Hamiltonian cycle, H1 = G[V (G1 )] ∩ H consists of either one or two non-trivial
components, each of which is a path with both endpoints in V (C), such that H1 − V (C) is a
spanning subgraph of G1 − V (C).
Suppose P is the sole non-trivial component of H1 , where v, w ∈ V (C) are the endpoints of P ,
such that e = {v, w} ∈ E(C), then (P + C) − e is a Hamiltonian cycle in G1 . Similarly, if H1
consists of two paths P1 , P2 , each with endpoints vi , wi ∈ V (C), such that ei = {vi , wi } ∈ E(C)
for both i ∈ {1, 2}, then (P1 + P2 + C) − {e1 , e2 } is a Hamiltonian cycle in G1 .
On the other hand, we claim that it is impossible for H1 to consist of two paths P and Q, such
that each path has endpoints which are non-adjacent in C. Note that therefore P and Q are
paths of even length, as they have endpoints of the same colour. Let M be a perfect matching



                                                 13
of G such that M ⊂ E(H). Due to their even length, both P and Q respectively have exactly
one edge eP , respectively eQ , from M which covers one of its endpoints, but is not actually an
edge in E(P ), respectively in E(Q). In particular, the points of eP and eQ which lie on C are
necessarily adjacent on C. Let {x, y} = E(C) ∩ (eP ∪ eQ ) and note that (E(H1 ) ∩ M ) ∪ {xy}
is a perfect matching of G1 and in particular of H1 + C. Thus H1 + C contains a conformal
cross over C, meaning C is contained in a conformal bisubdivision of K3,3 in G1 , according to
Lemma 4.18. By Theorem 4.17, G1 can therefore not be Pfaffian, contradicting our assumption.
Therefore our claim at the beginning of the paragraph is valid.
We are left with one more case in which H1 has only one non-trivial component P , whose
endpoints are not adjacent on C. But in this situation, there must exists a path Q, whose
endpoints are the two vertices in V (C) \ V (P ), which either spans G2 − V (P ) or G3 − V (P ).
W.l.o.g. we suppose that Q ⊆ G2 − V (P ). Note that in G3 there exists a perfect matching M
for which C is conformal, since G3 is 2-extendable. If we now consider that (P + Q) − C is a
spanning subgraph of (G1 +G2 )−C, it is easy to see that P and Q are again paths of even length
and form a conformal cross over C in G + E(C), with M \ E(C) being a matching which covers
the part of G3 not involved in the cross. As in the previous case, Lemma 4.18 and Theorem 4.17
tell us that G + E(C) is not Pfaffian. However, according to Theorem 4.8, the graph G + E(C)
should in fact be Pfaffian, as it can be constructed via a trisum from three Pfaffian braces.

It is nice to know that it suffices to look at braces to resolve Conjecture 1.1, but that is of limited

we discuss how the generation procedure for members of B presented by Holton, Manvel, and
McKay [HMM85] can be amended such that we can track which of the generated graphs are
braces. This removes the burden of checking whether the non-braces produced are Hamiltonian.

                                                  14
Let G = (A∪B, E(G)) ∈ B be a matching covered, planar, bipartite graph and let uv, xy ∈ E(G),

We can in fact remove the requirements that G is planar and along with it, that the cycle C is
facial, and still get a useful operation. In this case we call H a general C4 -expansion.
We note that the requirements on the parity of the paths of (C − uv) − xy require u, v, x, y to be
distinct from each other. If we start with the cube, it turns out that cube- and C4 -expansions
suffice to construct the entirety of B.

Theorem 5.1 (Holton et al. [HMM85] (see Theorem 4)). A graph is cubic, 3-connected, planar,
and bipartite if and only if it can be constructed from the cube by repeated applications of the
cube- and C4 -expansion.

We observe that the cube-expansion creates a cut consisting of three mutually disjoint, pairwise
non-adjacent edges and thus a tight cut, by Lemma 4.1. One side of the tight cut created by the
cube-expansion contracts into the cube, justifying its name. It is not hard to prove that the cube-
expansion preserves being cubic, 3-connected, planar, bipartite, and Hamiltonian. However, in
the published literature concerning Conjecture 1.1, it seems to be the only known expansion—or
rather reduction applied in reverse—which allows us to keep both the structural properties of a
graph relevant to Conjecture 1.1 and also preserve its Hamiltonicity, strengthened or otherwise.
In particular, the sticking point for all other operations tends to be that they reduce connectivity.
The fact that the cube-expansion behaves more nicely can be attributed to the fact that it is
special case of a splice of two graphs in B which preserves planarity. In fact, if we consider the
splice of any two graphs in B, we can easily show that this graph remains 3-connected, bipartite,
and cubic. According to Lemma 4.1, the splicing cut is therefore tight. Thus the cube-expansion
is simply a special case of a splice preserving planarity.
In contrast to the cube-expansion, the C4 -expansion is only known to preserve the structural
properties of a graph in B. We add to the list of properties it is known to preserve by showing
that it also interacts nicely with 1- and 2-extendability. In fact, this is true for a much more
general class of graphs than B.

Lemma 5.2. Let H be a general C4 -expansion of a matching covered, bipartite graph G, then

At this point, we note that it is entirely possible to use several cube-expansions on a graph in
B and then apply a single C4 -expansion to build a brace. This then begs the question of how
and when a C4 -expansion destroys tight cuts. Some intuition on this is given in Figure 4, where
we see that a large number of tight cuts can be disrupted if a C4 -expansion touches both shores
of those tight cuts, but the C4 -expansion does not destroy tight cuts for which it takes place
entirely on one of the shores.
For the remainder of this section, we will work towards formalising this intuition. We observe
that the two edges used for the C4 -expansion cannot be part of the same tight cut.

Lemma 5.3. Let G be a cubic, bipartite graph and let H be a C4 -expansion of G at uv and
xy. No tight cut in G can contain both uv and xy.

Proof. Once more, we will use the names provided by Figure 3b in this proof.
Suppose that there exists a tight cut ∂(X) with uv, xy ∈ ∂(X). Note that according to
Lemma 2.2, we can assume w.l.o.g. that u, y ∈ X. Let C be the cycle on which u, v, x, y lie
together. Note that E(C) ∩ ∂(X) must be even and |E(C) ∩ ∂(X)| ≥ 2. Thanks to Lemma 4.1,
we also know that |∂(X)| = 3 and we can therefore conclude that E(C) ∩ ∂(X) = {uv, xy}.
Thus, if P and Q are the two path components of (C − uv) − xy, then P and Q lie in distinct
components of G − ∂(X). W.l.o.g. we can assume that u, y ∈ V (P ) and in particular this means
that u and y are the endpoints of P , which makes P a path of even length, contradicting the
requirements set for the application of the C4 -expansion at uv and xy. Thus no tight cut of G
can contain both uv and xy.

Two cuts ∂(X) and ∂(Y ) are called laminar if X ⊆ Y or Y ⊆ X. As Lovász [Lov87] proves,
the results of a tight cut decomposition are independent of which tight cuts are chosen and the

are the edges added as part of the C4 -expansion. We will again use the names provided by
Figure 3b during this proof. To set up the rest of our arguments, we partition F into three sets:
   • F1 := {∂G (X) ∈ F | |{u, v, x, y} ∩ X| ∈ {0, 4}},
   • F2 := {∂G (X) ∈ F | |{u, v, x, y} ∩ X| ∈ {1, 3}}, and
   • F3 := F \ (F1 ∪ F2 ) = {∂G (X) ∈ F | |{u, v, x, y} ∩ X| = 2}.
We will start by proving that the cuts in F1 ∪ F2 can be translated into tight cuts in H. Let
∂G (X) be a cut in F1 and let M be a perfect matching of G, with ab ∈ (M ∩ ∂(X)). It is
easy to observe that ∂G (X) ∩ E ′ = ∅ in this case. Therefore ∂G (X) remains a cut in H and we
can assume w.l.o.g. that |{u, v, x, y} ∩ X| = 0. Furthermore, we can assume that X contains all
endpoints of the edges of ∂G (X) which lie in A, according to Lemma 2.2. From this circumstance
it is evident that ∂G (X) is also tight in H, as G[X] ⊆ H and we must match exactly one vertex
from A ∩ X with a vertex outside of X to find a perfect matching of H. Thus F1 is a family of
non-trivial, laminar tight cuts in H.
Next, we consider a cut ∂G (Y ) ∈ F2 . W.l.o.g. we assume that u ∈ Y and v, x, y ∈ Y . To
separate u from v in G, we must have uv ∈ ∂G (Y ), which also means that xy 6∈ ∂G (Y ) according
to Lemma 5.3. We claim that (∂G (Y ) \ {uv}) ∪ {uu′ } is a tight cut in H. This can be shown
using an argument analogous to the one used in the case from the previous paragraph, as we
again have G[Y ] ⊆ H. This is the situation which requires the renaming of an edge, if we truly
wanted to translate the tight cuts of G into H. Note that |F2 | ≤ 4, since we only have four
vertices which can be exclusive to a shore. This justifies our bound on the number of edges that
need to be renamed.
We will now consider F3 , or more explicitly, we will prove that all tight cuts in H must be related
to the cuts in F1 ∪ F2 . For this discussion, we can assume F1 ∪ F2 to be empty, or equivalently,
we can consider G and H to be the results of contracting all tight cuts in F1 ∪ F2 and renaming
vertices appropriately, i.e. if we contract a shore from a cut of F2 which contained only u, but
not v, x, or y, then we rename the contraction vertex to u. Since the family of tight cuts in F is
laminar, any tight cuts we find in this reduced version of the graphs can also be found if we do
not contract F1 ∪ F2 , up to some relabelling of edges incident to u, v, x, y. Note that even if we
had to contract tight cuts to arrive in this simplified case, we know that according to Lemma 4.2
our graphs are still found in B. We claim that under these assumptions H is a brace.




                                                18
Suppose towards a contradiction that ∂H (Z) is a non-trivial tight cut in H and note that since
H ∈ B, according to Lemma 4.1, the cut ∂H (Z) is a set of three mutually disjoint, pairwise
non-adjacent edges. If |{u, v, x, y} ∩ Z| ∈ {0, 1, 3, 4} then ∂H (Z) corresponds to a tight cut in
F1 ∪ F2 , which we assumed to be empty. Thus |{u, v, x, y} ∩ Z| = 2 and therefore some edges
from E ′ must be part of ∂H (Z).
We distinguish three cases, the first being that u and v lie on the same shore of ∂H (Z). Clearly,
∂H (Z) must involve some edges in E ′ . However, due to the restrictions Lemma 2.2 places on
the colours of the endpoints of these edges, we would in fact need at least three edges from E ′ to
separate {u, v} and {x, y} in H − ∂H (Z). Furthermore, due to our limited choices, these edges
cannot be mutually disjoint, contradicting Lemma 4.1. Thus we can go on to assuming that u
and v, and respectively x and y, lie on different shores with respect to ∂H (Z).
If u and x lie on the same shore, then ∂H (Z) must again involve at least two edges of E ′ whilst
also respecting the constraints provided by Lemma 2.2. Again, this is not possible if we choose
only two such edges from E ′ and any three suitable edges are not mutually disjoint, again
contradicting Lemma 4.1.
We move on to assuming that u and y lie on the same shore of the tight cut ∂H (Z). Using the
arguments above, especially those based on Lemma 2.2, we can deduce that uu′ , yy ′ ∈ ∂H (Z),
leaving us with one edge e ∈ ∂H (Z) ∩ E(G). This implies ∂H (Z) = {uv, xy, e} is tight in G,
which directly contradicts Lemma 5.3.

We observe that this theorem also solidifies the intuition that any tight cut in a graph in B
was initially introduced by a cube-expansion. This idea together with Theorem 5.4 suggests the
following amendments to the generation procedure for B.
   • Associate with each generated graph G a family FG , which contains an inclusion-wise
     maximal family of non-trivial tight cuts in G. (As we will see, we do not have to check
     these properties.)
   • If we use a cube-expansion on a vertex v in a graph G to generate H, we add the tight
     cut created by this operation into FG . Any edges of cuts in FG incident to v, are then
     renamed appropriately. The result of this is FH . Note that the new tight cut is guaranteed
     to be laminar to the others, since we expanded a single vertex.
   • Whenever we use a C4 -expansion in a graph G at uv and xy to generate H, we again
     rename the edges in cuts of FG which involve uv or xy. Furthermore, we remove any
     cuts ∂G (X) from FG with |{u, v, x, y} ∩ X| 6= 2. The result of this is FH , which is an
     inclusion-wise maximal family of non-trivial tight cuts in G according to Theorem 5.4. Of
     course if FG = ∅, then FH = ∅. The family FH can be constructed in linear time (see
     Lemma 5.6).
If we generate the graphs in B in this way, we only have to check the Hamiltonicity of a graph
G ∈ B if FG = ∅, which means that it is a brace. This drives up the space-requirements of
the procedure somewhat, but the time saved by not checking graphs with tight cuts for their
Hamiltonicity should make up for this fact, at least for small numbers of vertices. For some
pairs of graphs, this also makes it easier to check whether they are isomorphic, since for two
isomorphic graphs G and G′ , we must have |FG | = |FG′ |.

to save in addition to the graph. We give a sharp upper bound to this.

If we save every tight cut as a set of three edges, we thus need additional space for each generated
graph adding up to at most half the number of its vertices. Let us now argue that we can compute
FH from FG in linear time if we performed a C4 -expansion, under the assumption that FG was
saved in a way that lets us directly link the edges in the graphs with the edges in the cuts
contained in FG . This could for example be realised by labelling the edges of G.

Lemma 5.6. Let G ∈ B and let FG be an inclusion-wise maximal family of non-trivial, pairwise
laminar tight cuts in G. If H is a C4 -expansion of G at uv and xy, then an inclusion-wise maximal
family FH of non-trivial, pairwise laminar tight cuts in H can be constructed from FG in linear
time.

Proof. We first make some observations on the structure of the graph, which we can then use
to simplify our computation. Let ∂G (X) ∈ FG be such that |{u, v, x, y} ∩ X| = 2 and recall that
according to Theorem 5.4 these are the tight cuts we must identify and remove to construct FH .
We claim that ∂G (X) cannot separate u and v. Suppose that this is not true. This requires that
uv ∈ ∂G (X). Now, independent of whether u and x, or u and y lie on the same shore, we must
also have xy ∈ ∂G (X), which contradicts Lemma 5.3. Thus our claim holds and ∂G (X) must
separate u, v and x, y in G. In fact, as a consequence of this claim, we know that we must remove
a cut from FG if and only if it separates u, v and x, y in G. Let us call such a cut removable.
We further remark that for any removable cut ∂G (X), we therefore have ∂G (X) ∩ {uv, xy} = ∅.
Next, let us consider a path P ⊆ G with the endpoints u and x, which must exist since G is
connected. We now analyse how P interacts with some arbitrary ∂G (Y ) ∈ FG . W.l.o.g. we



                                                   20
assume that u ∈ Y . The graph P − ∂G (Y ) is composed of components P0 , . . . , Pt that are paths,
which we can name such that u ∈ V (P0 ), x ∈ V (Pt ), and the paths appear in increasing order
when we traverse P from u to x. It is now easy to see that Pi ⊆ G[Y ], for an i ∈ {0, . . . , t}, if
and only if i is even. Therefore ∂G (Y ) is removable if and only if t is odd, which is equivalent
to |E(P ) ∩ ∂G (Y )| being even.
Let us turn to constructing FH . We assume that an edge e ∈ E(G) has the label ℓZ if e ∈
∂(Z) ∈ FG . A path P with endpoints u and x can be found in linear time using a breadth first
search. We can then count the occurrences of all labels, noting the results separately for each
label. If any specific label occurs an odd number of times, we delete this label from all edges
of the graph, which can again be done by traversing the graph once after we have determined
which labels have to be deleted. After performing the C4 -expansion and the requisite renaming
of edges mentioned in Theorem 5.4, which can be performed in constant time, the labels which
are left form FH . If necessary FH can be constructed explicitly by traversing H once more,
again adding a linear effort.

While the above arguments take care of the generation of braces in B, the procedure used in
[HMM85] to check the Hamiltonicity of the generated graphs involves checking the H +− -property
for small graphs in B. For larger graphs, we can then find certain configurations that allow us to
reduce to the smaller graphs we have generated. The H +− -property can then be used to confirm
that the larger graphs are Hamiltonian, though the actual H +− -property cannot be preserved.
We illustrate this with the following result.

Theorem 5.7 (Brinkmann et al. [BGM21]). Let G ∈ B, with |V (G)| = n, then
   i) n ≤ 90 implies that G is Hamiltonian,
  ii) n ≤ 78 implies that G is P2 -Hamiltonian, and
 iii) n ≤ 66 implies that G has the H +− -property.

The first and second item are implied by the third through the use of several results from
[HMM85]. Lemma 4.5 tells us that we can preserve the H +− -property through tight cuts. In
particular, combining the above theorem and Lemma 4.5 tells us that, if G ∈ B is a graph whose
braces each have 66 vertices or less, then G has the H +− -property. Thus our amendments to the
generation procedure can be adapted in a straight-forward fashion to checking the H +− -property.
Finally, we want to mention that using Lemma 4.1, our results can be rephrased such that
any tight cut is replaced with a cut of order three consisting of mutually disjoint, pairwise
non-adjacent edges, if we are only concerned with cubic, 3-connected, bipartite graphs. This
basically translates everything into the language of cyclically 4-connected graphs, in line with
Corollary 4.15. Whilst this might make some of the statements more intuitive for the reader,
we are not sure if a straightforward translation of the proofs exist.


6 Discussion
We mentioned splices briefly in Section 3 and said that reversed versions of Theorem 1.8 let us
construct new Hamiltonian graphs. This can in fact be done with Lemma 4.2 and, though the
lemma does not concern tight cuts, Lemma 4.12 can be used similarly. In particular, the K3,3



                                                21
and the Heawood graph are P3 - and P4 -Hamiltonian, and have the H − - and the H +− -property.
Additionally, Theorem 5.7 provides us with a large class of small cubic braces with the H − - and
the H +− -property. Thus we can generate a large class containing both planar and non-planar,
cubic, bipartite graphs with strong Hamiltonicity properties by repeatedly using splices.
In Section 4, we both mention a reduction of Conjecture 1.1 to braces and then provide what is
essentially a relaxation of planarity for the conjecture. With this reformulated conjecture, we
can again attempt to relax or strengthen certain properties. Let us start with planar, cubic
braces, which are 3-connected by Theorem 2.5. Using Euler’s formula, it is fairly easy to prove
that a planar, bipartite graph cannot have minimum degree four. Thus we cannot demand
4-connectivity. We cannot drop planarity either, as the Georges-Kelmans graph GK, discovered
by Georges [Geo89] and Kelmans [Kel86][Kel88] independently, is a brace, as the authors verified
by hand. Thus the only option left is to drop regularity, leading us to the following question.

Question 6.1. Is every planar brace Hamiltonian?

Concerning non-Pfaffian graphs, it is fairly easy to find a conformal bisubdivision of the K3,3
in GK, but we did not check whether all counterexamples to Tutte’s conjecture are Pfaffian. If
any of them are, then this would disprove Conjecture 1.1 via the sixth item of Theorem 4.16.
Whilst investigating non-planar braces, the following question comes naturally in the vein of the
main subject of [BGM21] and GK already gives an upper bound of 50.

Question 6.2. What is the least number of vertices of a non-planar, non-Hamiltonian brace?

One of the key lemmas for proving Theorem 4.16 was Lemma 4.5. Missing from the items
there are both P2 -Hamiltonicity and Hamiltonicity itself. If we have a P2 -Hamiltonian, cubic,
3-connected, bipartite graph, then it is easy to show, using the methods we presented, that all
of its tight cut contractions will also be P2 -Hamiltonian and for Hamiltonicity this fact is stated
in Theorem 1.8. One might wonder if the other direction holds for either property.
This however can be disproven by considering the first graph presented by Horton [BM76] as
a counterexample to Tutte’s conjecture. First we note that this graph can be constructed by
taking a K3,3 and splicing a copy of the graph in Figure 5a into each of the three vertices of
one of its colour classes. Each of these splices creates a tight cut. The fact that the Horton
graph can be constructed this way confirms that it is not Pfaffian according to Theorem 4.7
and Theorem 4.17, since the K3,3 is one of its braces. We have already noted that the K3,3 has
several strong Hamiltonicity properties. Unsurprisingly, it is also P2 -Hamiltonian. This is also
true for the graph in Figure 5a, which also happens to be non-Pfaffian. Thus if two tight cut
contractions of a given cubic, 3-connected, bipartite graph G are (P2 -)Hamiltonian, the graph
G itself may still be non-Hamiltonian.
In contrast to this, we are unsure whether the first point of Lemma 4.5 can be reversed. This is
somewhat surprising, since for the other Hamiltonicity properties we considered, this direction
was actually the easier one to prove.

Regarding our suggestions for the generation procedure for B, one might ask why we cannot
just check each generated graph and see whether it is a brace. This is indeed possible. A
polynomial time algorithm for determining the extendability of a bipartite graph was first given
by Lakhal and Litzler [LL98] and an improvement to a runtime in O(mn), which is in O(n2 ) for
cubic graphs, was then provided by Zhang and Zhang [ZZ06]. Of course, we could also just check
whether the generated graph is cyclically 4-connected, but the known algorithms (see [DKKP04]
and [LL09]) do not improve on a quadratic runtime. Either way, unless space is scarce and we
have much more processor time to burn, it seems like checking whether a generated graph is a
brace in this way would not be advisable, though it might still improve on simply generating all
graphs and checking Hamiltonicity indiscriminately.
