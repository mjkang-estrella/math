                                                                    ON p-SPREAD MEASURES


                                                                                   CHEN LI


                                                  Abstract. We study p-spread probability measures on the Boolean lattice.




arXiv:2609.08967v2 [math.CO] 6 Oct 2026
                                                  We show that if a family of sets A is large under the product Bernoulli-p
                                                  measure, then no p-spread measure can be supported on sets that are not
                                                  covered by the union of two members of A, answering the fractional version of
                                                  Talagrand’s discrete convexity problem. Consequently, we establish a coupling
                                                  theorem between p-spread and product Bernoulli-p measures.




                                                                             1. Introduction
                                          1.1. Background and main results. Talagrand’s convexity problem asks if one
                                          can create convexity by applying a bounded number of Minkowski sums to a large
                                          but otherwise arbitrary set in high-dimensional Gaussian space [14, 15, 17]. In
                                          [8], Hua, Song, and Tudose resolved this problem, building on Song’s formulation
                                          in [13] that the convexity problem is equivalent to a Gaussian-sum problem for
                                          sub-Gaussian vectors.
                                              Talagrand also raised two discrete counterparts of the convexity problem: one
                                          integral and one fractional, replacing Gaussian variables with Bernoulli random
                                          variables and Minkowski sums with unions [14, 15, 16, 17]. This paper settles the
                                          fractional version of the discrete convexity problem.
                                              We now introduce the two problems. For a positive integer N , write [N ] =
                                          {1, . . . , N } and identify 2[N ] with {0, 1}N . For a probability measure η on 2[N ]
                                          and S ⊆ [N ], we abbreviate η({S}) as η(S). For p ∈ (0, 1), let µp be the product
                                          Bernoulli-p measure on 2[N ] :
                                                            µp (S) = p|S| (1 − p)N −|S|        for every S ⊆ [N ].
                                          For I ⊆ [N ], define its principal up-class as
                                                                          HI = {S ⊆ [N ] : I ⊆ S}.
                                          For a positive integer m and a family of sets A ⊆ 2[N ] , define

1.2. Proof strategy. According to Talagrand, a central difficulty underlying the
discrete convexity problems is that we do not have a good handle on the operation
“taking the union of two sets in A”, and the main challenge is to find a way to
express that the resulting set family is, in some sense, “much larger than A” [17,
Section 3]. Our approach to Theorems 1.14 and 1.15 borrows techniques from the
study of intersecting families to work with the union operation by taking advantage
of its binary nature. In particular, we adopt the spectral methods developed by
Friedgut [6] and by Ellis, Filmus, and Friedgut [3] to study the maximum size of
t-intersecting families and triangle-intersecting families, respectively. We next illus-
trate their relevance to Problems 1.3 and 1.12 by considering a particular example
from the latter result.
    Suppose N = n2 for a positive integer n, and 2[N ] represents the set of subgraphs
                    

of the complete graph Kn on n vertices. A family F ⊆ 2[N ] is triangle intersecting
if, for every G, H ∈ F, G ∩ H contains a triangle. The following conjecture of
Simonovits and Sós was settled in [3].
Theorem 1.20 (Simonovits-Sós conjecture). For any triangle-intersecting family
F, µ1/2 (F) ≤ 81 .

   In other words, under the uniform measure, any triangle-intersecting family can-
not be larger than the principal up-class generated by a particular triangle.
   To motivate our proof of Theorems 1.14 and 1.15, let us emphasize a couple of
key steps shared with the proof of Theorem 1.20. See [3, Section 1.3] for a narrative
of the proof methods for the Simonovits-Sós conjecture. For a set S ⊆ [N ], we use
S c = [N ] \ S to denote the complement of S and S △ S ′ to denote the symmetric
difference of S, S ′ ⊆ [N ].
   As stated in [3, Section 1.2], the starting point of the proof of Theorem 1.20
is the following observation, also used by Chung, Graham, Frankl, and Shearer
to obtain a bound of µ1/2 (F) ≤ 1/4 in [2, Theorem 9], despite using an entropy
method rather than a spectral method: If B is a bipartite graph and F1 , F2 ∈ F,
then B c must intersect F1 ∩ F2 since B cannot contain a triangle. Concretely put,
                             G ∈ F =⇒ G △ B c ∈
                                              / F.
Therefore, bipartite graphs encode information about triangle-intersecting families:
any bipartite graph B represents a mechanism that converts a graph in F to a
graph outside of F by flipping every edge in B c .
   We now apply this observation to our setting. Far2 (A) encodes information
about A in a similar manner: For every S ∈ Far2 (A),
                      S ∩ X c ∩ Y c ̸= ∅   for every X, Y ∈ A.
Consequently, for S ∈ Far2 (A) and X ∈ A, flipping every coordinate of X in S
removes it from A. Lifting the above construction to the level of operators, we
define
                              TS f (x) = f (x + S),
where we use bitwise addition (XOR operation) on {0, 1}N . An advantage of TS is
that whenever f is supported in A, TS f (x) = 0 for x ∈ A. Therefore, it defines a
vanishing quadratic form on functions supported on A. This is the building block
of the operator constructed in [3, Section 2.2] to prove Theorem 1.20. The operator
we eventually use in the proof of Theorems 1.14 and 1.15 resembles the construction
in [3, Section 4.1], an extension of TS tailored to arbitrary Bernoulli-p measures.
   Once we have an operator whose quadratic form vanishes on functions supported
on A and whose spectrum has a suitable lower bound, applying the spectral lower
bound to the indicator of A gives an upper bound on µp (A). This spectral argument
is reminiscent of the Hoffman bound, which controls the independence number of
a regular graph by the smallest eigenvalue of its adjacency matrix. We refer the
reader to [7] for an account of this method.
   One potential difficulty of the spectral approach is obtaining good spectral infor-
mation on the constructed operators. The good news is that any linear combination
of TS for S ∈ Far2 (A) preserves the required vanishing property. To solve Prob-
lem 1.12, we naturally look to average over the provided spread measure. This
leads to a modification of TS such that the desired lower bound on the spectrum
follows from the spread inequalities. For more sophisticated linear combinations of
the operators indexed by bipartite graphs, see [3, Section 2.4 and Section 4.2].

1.3. Subsequent developments. Motivated by finding “the proper formulation”
of Problem 1.12 devoid of unions, Talagrand raised in [15] a sequence of increasingly
strong conjectures about p-spread measures, each implying a positive solution to
the fractional discrete convexity problem. See Fang and Wang [4] for a proof of
6                                          CHEN LI


[15, Conjecture 7.8] that adapts the techniques of this paper and establishes that,
up to a constant, p-spread measures are stochastically dominated by Bernoulli-p
measures after independently retaining elements with a fixed probability. See Park
and Talagrand [12] for a proof of a logarithmic projection inequality that resolves
[15, Conjecture 9.1], providing an alternative route to Theorem 1.14 along the
same line that exploits spreadness via the Fourier basis but proceeds with a second
moment instead of a spectral calculation.
