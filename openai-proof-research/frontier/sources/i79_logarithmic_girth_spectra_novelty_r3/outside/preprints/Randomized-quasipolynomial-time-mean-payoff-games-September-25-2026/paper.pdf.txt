       Randomized quasipolynomial-time mean-payoff games

                                               Abstract
        We give a randomized algorithm that computes the complete zero-threshold winning set of
    a finite mean-payoff game with arbitrary signed integer edge weights encoded in binary. For
                                                        2
    total explicit input length L, it uses 2O((log(L+2)) ) bit operations on every random tape and is
    correct with probability at least 7/8. A polynomial-time check certifies the winning regions
    and positional strategies for both players or reports failure. Independent repetition therefore
    gives an always-correct algorithm with the same expected quasipolynomial bit bound.

1      Introduction
A mean-payoff game asks whether a player can maintain a nonnegative long-run average reward
while an opponent chooses some of the moves. The arena is finite, but plays are infinite and integer
edge weights may have large magnitudes. Positional strategies provide finite certificates for the
two winning regions. The algorithmic difficulty is to find those regions with a cost controlled by
the binary description, rather than by the numerical size of a weight.
    This paper gives a randomized quasipolynomial-time algorithm. Its central output is an exact
bounded integer potential: with probability at least 7/8, every coordinate of that potential is
correct. Thresholding it returns the complete winning set. The method also gives order guarantees
in finite integer boxes with arbitrary endpoints and the allowed reference width. Each guarantee
concerns a fixed comparison vector and all its coordinates at once.

    The probability statement concerns the whole returned set, and the time bound holds even
when that output is wrong. Large weights are allowed: the resource parameter is their binary
description together with the rest of the explicit input. The asserted bound is quasipolynomial; a

verified winning set with positional strategies for both players, or a failure symbol. It returns the
verified output with probability at least 7/8 and never accepts an incorrect one. Corollary 8.1
proves this consequence and an always-correct, or Las Vegas, version obtained by repeating until
the check succeeds. The repeated version has quasipolynomial expected bit cost; each individual
trial retains the bound on every tape. The strategies certify the zero-threshold regions, with

    The companion article Deterministic quasipolynomial-time mean-payoff games [21, Theorem 1.1]
gives a deterministic complete winning-set algorithm with the same form of bit bound. Its Section 7
also reduces exact values and globally optimal positional strategies to polynomially many winning-
set queries. The value at a start is the best worst-case mean payoff from that start; the two
strategies attain these values simultaneously at all starts. Using amplified calls to the algorithm of
Theorem 1.1, that reduction returns the entire value and strategy output correctly with probability
at least 7/8, with the same form of bit bound on every random tape [21, Section 7].
    The present paper retains different outputs and a different method: pointwise whole-vector
comparisons in boxes with arbitrary integer endpoints and allowed reference width, exact recovery
of the top-box potential, and a randomized orientation recursion. The proof of Theorem 1.1 is
independent of the deterministic algorithm.

1.2   Historical context and related algorithms
Ehrenfeucht and Mycielski proved the positional structure of mean-payoff games [13]. A long
algorithmic line uses potentials, numbers assigned to vertices whose endpoint differences reweight
edges without changing cycle sums. Gurvich, Karzanov, and Khachiyan developed this approach
for cyclic games [15]; related algorithms and analyses were given by Karzanov and Lebedev [16] and
Pisaruk [23]. Zwick and Paterson established complexity results and pseudopolynomial algorithms
for mean-payoff threshold and value problems [27]. A pseudopolynomial cost may be polynomial in
the weight magnitude while exponential in its binary length, which is the distinction relevant here.
     Energy games express a related finite-prefix requirement: starting with some credit, keep every
cumulative total nonnegative. Resource interfaces and energy constraints provide a verification
setting for this question [9, 5]. Brim, Chaloupka, Doyen, Gentilini, and Raskin use bounded integer
progress measures and monotone lifting [6]; their threshold algorithm has O(|E|nM ) arithmetic
cost for an absolute weight bound M [6, Theorem 8]. Lifshits and Pavlov give a paired potential
characterization and an exponential algorithm [18]. Cadilhac, Casares, and Ohlmann develop a
uniform account of sound potential updates and accelerated value iteration for energy games [7].
Our box comparison method uses this potential viewpoint, with a symmetric boundary convention
and a locally proved game reduction.
     Strategy improvement supplies another√       route. For graphs without parallel edges, Björklund
and Vorobyov obtain an expected 2       O(  n log n) arithmetic-operation bound for the threshold par-
tition [4, Theorem 7.1], following earlier work with Sandberg [3]. Andersson and Vorobyov use
discounted linear programs for randomized strongly subexponential value and strategy algorithms
[2]. Subsequent analyses include Zwick’s bounds for Random-Action-Removal [26] and the bounds
of Dorfman, Kaplan, and Zwick for Switch-All and Random-Edge in energy games [12]. These com-
parisons require two distinctions. Arithmetic counts must be supplemented by operand bit lengths.
Moreover, an expected Las Vegas bit bound can be converted to a bounded-error bound on every
tape by a fixed cutoff. Thus the quantitative contribution of Theorem 1.1 is its quasipolynomial
dependence on L, together with the specified complete output and potential guarantees.
     The 2017 quasipolynomial parity-game algorithm of Calude, Jain, Khoussainov, Li, and
Stephan [8] opened a further direction. In a parity game, the winner is determined by vertex
priorities appearing infinitely often. Extensions to quantitative objectives retain additional
parameters: Daviaud, Jurdziński, and Lazić combine succinct parity progress measures with
energy bounds in a pseudo-quasipolynomial algorithm for mean-payoff parity games [11]. Fijalkow,


                                                  3
Gawrychowski, and Ohlmann obtain universal-graph bounds involving numerical weight magnitude
or an exponential dependence on the number of distinct weights [14]. The later theory also proves
mean-payoff universal-graph size lower bounds [10, Section 7]. Such lower bounds concern that
representation of the objective and do not bound the complexity of every algorithm.
    Randomness in the input leads to a different comparison. Loff and Skomra prove high-
probability polynomial bounds for independent Gaussian perturbations of normalized payoffs on
ergodic game graphs [19, Theorem 1]. Their bound also depends on the perturbation parameter.
Here the input is arbitrary and fixed; only the algorithm is random.
    A dominion in a parity game is a vertex set in which one player can keep the play and win.
Parys’s recursion uses separate bounds on the sizes of the two players’ dominions. Among calls
with a reduced bound, it makes one call retaining both current bounds [22, Algorithm 2 and
Section 5]. Recursive refinements are presented by Lehtinen, Parys, Schewe, and Wojtczak [17,
Sections 3–4]. Our separate support budgets parallel these bounds on protected objects. Here the
objects are potential vectors, and each probabilistic comparison is stated for one fixed vector; the
parity-game contracts cover all qualifying dominions simultaneously.
    Ohlmann’s symmetric recursion for mean-payoff games supplies a related potential viewpoint
and leaves its subexponential complexity analysis open [20, Sections 3–5 and 7]. We prove the
weighted support invariant, the sampled orientation argument, and the resulting bit bound below,
without importing a recursive solver as an oracle.
    Akian, Gaubert, and Guterman establish reductions between tropical feasibility and winning
initial states in mean-payoff games [1, Theorem 3.2 and Corollary 3.7]. There are also strongly
polynomial claims based on tropical optimization and max-atoms [24, 25]. Appendix A examines
the printed substitution rule in Truffet’s arXiv version 4 of August 29, 2026 and gives an explicit
instance on which it loses the optimum. The exact theorem and procedure text of the distinct
2025 HAL/MSR claim was unavailable to us; the appendix makes no assertion about that proof.
Its objection is specific to the cited version and rule. Neither claim is an input to our proof.

1.3   How the solver finds the potential
First transform each weight to (n + 1)w(e) + 1. This familiar integer perturbation separates the
signs of simple-cycle sums [4, Section 9]. Given a vector p, let Fi (p) be the maximum, at a Max
vertex, or minimum, at a Min vertex, of a transformed outgoing-edge weight plus the coordinate
at its head. The operator preserves order and commutes with common scalar shifts. In a finite
integer box [a, b] = {p ∈ ZV : a ≤ p ≤ b}, with coordinatewise inequalities, impose yi ≤ Fi (y) only
where yi > ai for a subsolution, and impose the reversed inequality only where a supersolution lies
below bi . Every subsolution lies below every supersolution. Clipping, or truncating each coordinate
of F to its interval, therefore yields a unique fixed point. In a sufficiently wide constant box its
coordinates fall into two separated bands, from which positional winning strategies follow.
    Ordinary monotone iteration can take time proportional to the box width. We replace
that dependence by recursion on the supports of the comparisons. The support consists of the
coordinates where the defining inequality is imposed. Each side receives positive rational vertex
weights and its own budget. The recursive answer is constrained by its order relative to each fixed
admissible comparison; the answer itself need not satisfy the comparison inequalities. Reducing a
budget, or halving weights on a suitable concentrated part of a support, decreases a scalar rank
parameter.
    At a large box the solver first tightens its bounds, then chooses which boundary to truncate.
The auxiliary procedure called orientation returns a choice of side and a list of vertex sets of small


                                                  4
auxiliary measure. For each fixed comparison whose support is sufficiently concentrated near the
threatened boundary, it either protects that side or returns a set containing more than the required
share of its support weight. Reweighting on such a set makes a smaller-rank call applicable.
    The orientation proof follows a sequence of centers. At each center, two smaller-rank calls
reduce the two budgets separately, and the next center is their clipped average. Successful
comparisons ensure that the maximum deviation of the fixed comparison never grows. Whenever
its projection fits the reduced budget, that deviation decreases by a controlled amount. Many
such decreases force a visible displacement of the extreme coordinates. If there are few, a newly
sampled uniform iterate is likely to have a heavy projected support in a smaller box. The sampled
comparison is fixed before the child’s fresh random bits, and support containment transports the
child’s conclusion back.
    This order of conditioning is the reason pointwise guarantees suffice. No event protecting all
admissible vectors in one run is used. Amplification controls the fresh smaller-rank calls. The
one continuation with unchanged support parameters is kept unamplified, and its entire chain is
analyzed together, both for errors and for work. At the top box, the unique deterministic solution
is an admissible comparison on both sides. Intersecting its two whole-vector success events gives
exact recovery with probability at least 7/8.

Section 2 establishes finite-box comparison and the game reduction. Section 3 proves the support

2     Integer boxes and the winning-set reduction
The game will be solved by recovering one integer potential. We first construct its operator and
prove a comparison principle in an arbitrary finite box. The principle gives both a unique clipped
fixed point and an exact, possibly slow, iteration. We then identify the winning set from the fixed
point in a particular wide box. This order separates the reusable comparison result from the width
needed for the game reduction.
    Potential transformations originate in cyclic-game algorithms [15]; bounded monotone integer
updates also underlie energy progress measures [6, Definitions 1–2 and Lemmas 4–6]. The
symmetric box statements below are proved directly.

5.5    Amplifying the orientation alternative
A trial can succeed either through its choice or through its list. The majority operation applies to
the choices, while concatenation preserves the possible certificates from every trial. The following
argument uses precisely this output rule, including the lists of minority trials.

case. The same bounds hold conditional on a history that fixes the input and comparison before the
call’s fresh randomness. On every tape its list has length at most J(2g + 2), and each listed set has
measure at most 1/2.

A     A counterexample to a tropical substitution rule
This appendix is independent of the game algorithm. It examines the minimization variable-
selection rule printed in Truffet’s arXiv:2603.26423v4, dated August 29, 2026 [25]. That version
claims a strongly polynomial tropical optimization algorithm and a mean-payoff consequence
through reductions. The example below loses its optimum under the stated rule; the conclusion is
confined to that version and procedure.
    In ordinary arithmetic, consider

                min x1   subject to   max(x1 , x2 ) ≥ 0,     x1 ≥ max(x2 + 1, −2).             (A.1)

The point (0, −1) is feasible. Conversely, a feasible point with x1 < 0 would have x2 ≤ x1 − 1 < 0,
contradicting the first constraint. Thus the true minimum is exactly zero.

Homogenization and the input conditions. The cited procedure homogenizes the constraint
rows as
                    max(x1 , x2 ) ≥ h, x1 ≥ max(x2 + 1, h − 2),
and the objective as z ≥ x1 ; dehomogenization sets h = 0. Equation (5b) of the cited paper holds
for both ordered pairs of constraint rows. Each left side lacks an h term, whereas the other row’s
right side has one. Setting x1 = x2 = 0 and increasing h therefore rules out the forbidden functional
domination by any finite additive constant. Both variables also have lower or upper occurrences,
as required by Definition 4.2 and Equation (34). Choose the search endpoints µ = 0 and λ = −4.
They include the finite optimum witness (x1 , x2 , h) = (0, −1, 0) and satisfy Equation (23b) for the
only mixed lower function, max(x2 + 1, h − 2), since

                                   1 + λ = −3 < −2 + µ = −2.

The lower function h has no competing x term.

The forced first selection. Numerotation convention 4.1 assigns index 0 to the objective row.
The lower and upper occurrences in Equations (24) and (32) and Definition 4.1 are drawn only
from constraint rows 1, . . . , m. For this instance they are

                         variable lower-bound rows upper-bound rows
                            x1           1, 2             ∅
                            x2            1                2

In particular, the objective row is not an additional upper occurrence of x1 . The lower branch for
x2 is x2 ≥ h in row 1; its upper bound is x2 ≤ x1 − 1 in row 2. Equation (53a) therefore makes
the dominating-variable set exactly {x2 }. Equation (53b) restricts eligible lower inequalities to
that set, leaving the unique pair (1, 2). Theorem 4.3, Equation (47), and Section 4.2.3, Case 2.2.2,
prescribe saturation of this selected inequality:

                                                x2 = h.

There is no tie-breaking choice in this step.




                                                  38
The resulting loss of the optimum. After substitution the second row requires x1 ≥ h + 1.
The next eligible saturation consequently sets x1 = h + 1. The stopping test in Equation (39) fails
before each of these two substitutions. At both stages the objective still depends on x1 , so the
separate rule for an objective-independent switch to maximization does not apply. Normalizing to
h = 0 gives objective value 1, whereas the original minimum in Equation (A.1) is 0.
    The first substitution already removes every optimum: selecting a lower-bound disjunct on

constraints. This calculation does not address a different variable-selection rule, a repaired
