                                                Abstract
         We resolve the Mahler conjecture for general convex bodies positively. For every convex body
      K ⊂ Rn , n ≥ 1, with Santaló point s(K), |K| |(K − s(K))◦ | ≥ (n + 1)n+1 /(n!)2 , with equality
      exactly for simplices.


1    Introduction
The Mahler conjecture asks which convex bodies have the smallest product of their volume and
the volume of a suitably centered polar body. We resolve the conjecture positively: simplices are
exactly the minimizers in every positive dimension.
   A convex body in Rn is a compact convex set with nonempty interior. We write |A| for the
n-dimensional Lebesgue volume of a measurable set A, and ⟨·, ·⟩ for the Euclidean inner product.
For z ∈ int K, define

                        (K − z)◦ = {y ∈ Rn : ⟨y, x − z⟩ ≤ 1 for every x ∈ K}.

The minimum of |(K − z)◦ | is attained at a unique point s(K) ∈ int K, the Santaló point (a proof
is included in Section 2). The volume product is

                         P (K) = |K| |(K − s(K))◦ | =      inf |K| |(K − z)◦ |.
                                                          z∈int K

It is invariant under invertible affine maps. A simplex is the convex hull of n + 1 affinely independent
points.

Theorem 1.1 (General Mahler conjecture). For every integer n ≥ 1 and every convex body K ⊂ Rn ,

Equality holds if and only if K is a simplex.

    Theorem 1.1 applies without symmetry or boundary regularity assumptions. It also gives the
displayed lower bound for |K| |(K − z)◦ | at every interior translation point z. The symmetric
Mahler conjecture asks for the different, stronger constant 4n /n! on the restricted class of centrally
symmetric bodies; that sharper statement is separate from Theorem 1.1 and is proved in the
companion paper [24, Theorem 1.1].




                                                    1
History and context
The lower-volume-product problem grew out of Mahler’s work on polarity and the geometry of
numbers. His polygon paper established the general planar inequality and its triangle equality case
among polygons [15]; his transference paper formulated the higher-dimensional symmetric problem
[16]. Meyer later gave a new proof of the planar general inequality and completed its equality
characterization for arbitrary planar convex bodies: triangles are exactly the minimizers [18]. The
affine center used here belongs to the classical Santaló theory [26]; we give its elementary existence
and uniqueness argument in Section 2.
    Several lines of work explain the distinction between an asymptotic lower bound and the exact
simplex constant. The inverse Santaló theorem of Bourgain and Milman gives |K| |K ◦ | ≥ cn |B2n |2 for
origin-symmetric bodies, where c > 0 is absolute and B2n is the Euclidean unit ball [2]. Kuperberg
obtained explicit lower bounds through Gauss linking integrals, with separate conclusions in the
symmetric and general settings [14]. Nazarov developed a complex-analytic proof using Bergman
kernels and Hörmander estimates [21]; Mastrantonis and Rubinstein extended that direct analytical
method to nonsymmetric bodies [17]. These approaches establish exponential-order lower bounds
without the sharp constant asserted in Theorem 1.1.
    Exact results in substantial special classes also preceded the general statement. Meyer and
Reisner used shadow systems, one-parameter deformations of convex bodies, to prove the sharp
inequality and simplex equality for polytopes with at most n+3 vertices. Their key input is convexity
of the reciprocal polar volume at the Santaló point along such a deformation [19, Theorems 1 and 10].
Kim and Reisner established strict local minimality, with a quantitative estimate, at every simplex
[12, Theorem 1]; this is local information about the space of convex bodies. In dimension three,
Iriyeh and Shibata proved the symmetric conjecture [11]. The 2026 preprint of Chen, Li, Xi, and Xu
proves P (K) ≥ 64/9 for every three-dimensional convex body, with equality exactly for tetrahedra
[6]. Their shadow flows constrain the permitted deformations so that they can treat arbitrary
polytopes in dimension three and then pass to general bodies. In contrast, the argument below works
with projections onto a fixed cone and their Gaussian averages. Theorem 1.1 concerns arbitrary
convex bodies in all dimensions, including its global equality classification.

    This sharp lower bound applies to general log-concave functions through their convex potentials;
no centering or normalization of φ is required. Its derivation uses Theorem 1.1 in all dimensions, not

uses the functional equivalence of Gozlan, as stated in [9, Theorem 1.3]; essential continuity is an
additional hypothesis, not a requirement on the convex bodies in Theorem 1.1.

2     Polarity, cone reduction, and the scalar input
We first express the volume product as a product of Laplace integrals on dual cones. This relation is
the one used by Klartag [13, Lemma 2.1 and Corollary 4.1], with positive duality and a normalization
adapted to the Gaussian argument. We include the calculation to fix the signs and factorials. The
rest of the proof estimates the integrals after a suitable linear change of coordinates.
