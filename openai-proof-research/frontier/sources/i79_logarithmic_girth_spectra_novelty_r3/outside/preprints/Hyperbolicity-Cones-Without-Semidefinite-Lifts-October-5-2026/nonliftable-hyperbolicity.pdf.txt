                Hyperbolicity Cones Without Semidefinite Lifts

                                                  Abstract
          We prove that not every hyperbolicity cone is a spectrahedral shadow: some closed hyperbol-
      icity cones admit no finite affine semidefinite lift, even with arbitrary real coefficients and any
      finite number of auxiliary variables. This disproves the Projected Lax Conjecture and hence the
      generalized Lax conjecture.


1    Introduction
A real homogeneous polynomial p on a finite-dimensional real vector space is hyperbolic with respect
to e if p(e) > 0 and, for every x, all roots of t ↦→ p(te − x) are real. Its closed hyperbolicity cone is

                      K(p, e) = {x : every root of t ↦→ p(te − x) is nonnegative}.

Gårding proved that this is a convex cone [4]. The determinant on real symmetric matrices, with
direction the identity, gives the positive semidefinite cone as a basic example. Hyperbolicity cones
also support logarithmic self-concordant barriers, as shown by Güler [6], and hence form a natural
setting for convex optimization beyond semidefinite programming.
    A spectrahedron is a set defined by a finite affine linear matrix inequality. A spectrahedral shadow,
or a set with a finite semidefinite lift, is a set S ⊂ Rd of the form

where a and the common size of the real symmetric matrices are finite. Equality in this definition is
exact; taking the closure of the displayed projection is not part of the definition. The generalized
Lax conjecture asserts that every hyperbolicity cone is a spectrahedron. The weaker Projected
Lax Conjecture, formulated by Netzer and Sanyal [17], asks whether every hyperbolicity cone is a
spectrahedral shadow.

Theorem 1.1. There exists a real homogeneous hyperbolic polynomial whose closed hyperbolicity
cone has no finite semidefinite lift.

    Thus both conjectures have a negative resolution. The assertion excludes every finite choice in
(1.1), with arbitrary real coefficients and any number of auxiliary variables. The construction is an
existence argument in high dimension, not a lower bound on the size of a particular representation.




                                                      1
1.1   Context and earlier obstructions
The original Lax question concerned homogeneous polynomials in three variables [13]. The deter-
minantal theorem of Helton and Vinnikov [8], together with the equivalence explained by Lewis,
Parrilo, and Ramana [15], gave an affirmative answer: a ternary hyperbolic polynomial has a definite
symmetric determinantal representation. The distinction between a representation of a polynomial
and a representation of its cone becomes essential in higher dimension. Brändén [2] constructed a
real-zero polynomial for which no positive integral power has a definite determinantal representation.
This does not exclude another polynomial with the same positivity region. Indeed, Kummer [9]
proved that the related specialized Vámos polynomial has a spectrahedral hyperbolicity cone. Nor
does failure of a specified sums-of-squares relaxation, such as those studied by Kummer, Plaumann,
and Vinzant [11], exclude all finite lifts.
     Positive representation results cover substantial classes. Brändén [3] proved spectrahedrality for
elementary symmetric hyperbolicity cones. Building on the representability criteria of Helton and
Nie [7], Netzer and Sanyal [17, Theorem 1.1] proved that a hyperbolicity cone is a spectrahedral
shadow when each nonzero boundary point is a smooth point of its defining hyperbolic polynomial.
Scheiderer [19] subsequently obtained second-order-cone lifts for hyperbolicity cones with Nash-
smooth boundary. Recent preprints prove spectrahedrality for five-variable hyperbolic cubics [16] and
for strictly hyperbolic polynomials [10]. These results concern special families or impose regularity
hypotheses; they do not yield an exact finite lift for every hyperbolicity cone.
     For general convex semialgebraic sets, Scheiderer [18] established nonexistence of semidefinite
lifts by local positivity obstructions. His finite-dimensional sum-of-squares pullback criterion and
local arguments explain how a putative lift constrains functions near a smooth point. He explicitly
asked whether these methods could settle the hyperbolicity-cone question [18, Section 5.4]. The
argument here is closely related to that approach, but tests analytic Gram identities directly by a
finite positive moment functional. Moment and sum-of-squares semidefinite methods are classical
[12]; our functional need not be integration against a measure. The general relationship between
cone lifts and positive factorizations is developed by Gouveia, Parrilo, and Thomas [5]. We do not
need to invoke their factorization theorem.

1.2   The construction and proof strategy
We begin with a matrix-valued homogeneous quadratic map Q : Rm → Symr that is positive
semidefinite at every real input. Here Symr denotes the real symmetric r-by-r matrices. Section 2
associates to Q a homogeneous polynomial pQ and proves its hyperbolicity by a symmetric determi-
nant identity on each line in the input space. It also identifies an exact affine section and projection
of its closed cone:

                    EQ = {(A, t, y) : A ⪰ 0, ran Q(y) ⊆ ran A, t ≥ tr(A† Q(y))}.

The symbol A† denotes the Moore–Penrose inverse. It is enough to choose Q so that EQ has no
finite lift.
    The difficulty is to force such an obstruction while keeping Q(y) positive semidefinite for every
y. Sections 3 and 4 address this constraint. Let H be the 36-dimensional space of quadratic forms
in eight variables. Each linear functional y on quartic forms determines a Hankel matrix Y (y)
on H by Y (y)(f, g) = y(f g). We construct a rank-twenty positive semidefinite Hankel matrix
whose kernel has strong multiplication properties. After normalizing translations and dilations,
its geometry yields a twenty-dimensional subspace W ⊂ H and a finite set of projective classes of
nonzero symmetric forms on W . The class of every nonzero limit of inverses of compressions to

inverse-limit construction, they supply the proof of Theorem 1.1.

3     Hankel matrices and finitely many inverse limits
We construct a positive semidefinite Hankel matrix of rank 20 and a 20-dimensional subspace
W with a finiteness property: certain nonzero limits of inverses of compressions to W have only
finitely many projective directions. The construction has two parts. A rank calculation, proved in
Appendix A, supplies a point at which a kernel-projection map has sufficiently large derivative. We
then divide out translations and dilations, choose a finite fiber of the resulting map, and use that
fiber to classify the inverse limits.

3.3   Selecting a finite fiber
We now show that the positive semidefinite part of X has enough distinct projected kernels to avoid
both infinite normalized fibers and limits at the boundary of M. We use the standard dimension
and fiber-dimension theorems for semialgebraic sets, as well as the fact that the frontier S \ S of a
semialgebraic set has smaller dimension than S; see [1]. A zero-dimensional semialgebraic set is
finite.

                                {Y ′ ∈ H : Y ′ (J) ⊂ T (L)} = (JH0 )⊥ .                           (3.8)
