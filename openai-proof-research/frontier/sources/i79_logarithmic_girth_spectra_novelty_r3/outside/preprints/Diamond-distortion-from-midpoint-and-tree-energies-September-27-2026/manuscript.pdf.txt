                                                Abstract
         We derive quantitative distortion bounds for countably branching diamond graphs from
     midpoint estimates and direct tree energies. In the dual of a finite-height segment forest, every
     distortion-D embedding of the depth-k diamond satisfies D2 ≥ 1 + k/4. The same bound holds
     in the infinite-height coordinate predual. We also obtain power-type distortion bounds for
     path-cost and recursive tree norms.


1    Introduction
Countably branching diamond graphs test whether a Banach space can accommodate many
separated approximate midpoints at every scale. Each edge is replaced by countably many two-
edge paths with common endpoints. A target space that forces these midpoint families away
from the sphere must lose some edge stretch at every replacement. The question is what quantity
decreases, and how the resulting distortion bound depends on the geometry used to prove that
decrease.
   We use the normalized diamond Dk : the original edge has length one, and the two edges in
each replacement path have half the length of their parent. Thus Dk has finite depth and countably
many vertices; write dk for its shortest-path metric. A map f has distortion at most D if some
s > 0 satisfies
                    sdk (u, v) ≤ ∥f (u) − f (v)∥ ≤ Dsdk (u, v)   (u, v ∈ Dk ).
The scale s may depend on the map and on k. Its removal by multiplying f by a scalar does not
change D.

1.1   Midpoint methods and earlier work
Diamond graphs connect metric embeddings with convexity of Banach-space norms. For binary
diamonds, Lee and Naor obtain quantitative lower bounds in Lp , 1 < p ≤ 2, by summing a convexity
inequality over the replacement levels [8, Lemma 2.2]. Johnson and Schechtman characterize
superreflexivity by the failure of uniformly bounded binary-diamond embeddings; their lower-bound
proof iterates a modulus-of-convexity estimate [7, Section 4]. The countably branching graphs
studied here make a further selection possible: infinitely many midpoint images can be compared
after their projections onto a fixed finite-dimensional space have become arbitrarily close.
    Dilworth, Kutzarova, Randrianarivony, Revalski, and Zhivkov introduced asymptotic midpoint
uniform convexity and characterized it by uniform smallness of the noncompactness of symmetric
approximate-midpoint sets [4, Theorem 2.1 and Definition 2.2]. In finite-codimensional terms, for
each t > 0 there is δ > 0 such that every unit center x admits a finite-codimensional subspace on
whose unit directions y one has max{∥x+ty∥, ∥x−ty∥} ≥ 1+δ. Replacing the maximum by ∥x+ty∥
gives asymptotic uniform convexity. Baudier, Causey, Dilworth, Kutzarova, Randrianarivony,
Schlumprecht, and Zhang turn this property into a countably branching diamond obstruction [2,
Section 4]. Their Theorem 4.1 includes a quantitative conclusion: a midpoint modulus bounded
below by a positive multiple of tp for 0 < t < 1, with 1 < p < ∞, gives distortion bounded below
by a positive multiple of k 1/p . Their proof combines control of midpoint sets with an iterated
improvement of endpoint distances. The slope-loss arguments here use that mechanism and retain
the constants furnished by the stated vector estimates. Malthaner gives another proof of the
power-type lower bound through coloring, a path lemma, and separated approximate-midpoint
configurations [9, Theorem 3.5 of arXiv v2]. He also proves a power-type obstruction for metric
targets satisfying a diamond inequality [9, Equation (2) and Theorem 3.7 of arXiv v2]. The
squared-lens inequality used here is its p = 2 Banach-space form; our coefficient comes from the
stated norm-specific tail estimate.

    Converse embedding results use additional geometry. For reflexive spaces with unconditional
asymptotic structure, Baudier et al. characterize existence of an equivalent asymptotically
uniformly convex norm by the failure of uniformly bounded diamond embeddings [2, Corollary 5.3].
Swift extends the characterization to countably branching bundle graphs with these hypotheses
[15, Corollary 3.4 and Theorem 6.9]. Perreau gives a dual characterization for separable Y when
Y ∗ has weak-star unconditional asymptotic structure, and a characterization for reflexive spaces
with unconditional asymptotic structure [13, Theorems 4.8 and 5.3]. These assumptions remain
part of the corresponding converses.
    Normalized diamond slopes also occur in the sprawling-tree construction of Basset, Lancien, and
Procházka [1, Definition 3.7 and Proposition 6.4]. Our direct proofs specialize that construction to
finite height and then supply coordinate selections, primal corrections, and predictions of atomic
segment coefficients for the norms considered here.
    We distinguish numerical conventions for the midpoint modulus. Dilworth et al. [4, Defi-
nition 2.2] and Baudier et al. [2, Section 4] use the maximum of the two signed norms. The
Baudier–Lancien book manuscript uses their average [3, Definition 5.1.15 of arXiv v1]. An average
lower bound implies the corresponding maximum lower bound; each application below specifies
which estimate it uses.

1.2   Direct proofs and the organization of the paper
The forest also admits direct graph arguments that construct their own energy budgets. These
proofs use the segment norm itself, without invoking the companion’s midpoint inequality. The
common first step is to select one replacement path at each retained edge. Infinite branching
permits coordinate choices at this step; subsequent descent uses only the two half-edges of each
selected path.
    Sections 3 and 4 develop the forest foundations and two primal-test constructions. A test is
a finitely supported vector in J, evaluated by the current slope in X. One construction adds
a correction at the first vertices outside a finite head, so that a gain in evaluation costs only a
bounded increase in the squared test norm. The other balances prefix sums at each dyadic size
and bounds the final accumulated test by charging segment sums to their largest encountered size.
Both compare growing evaluations with the norm of the test.
    Section 5 uses a different budget. Fair choices between the two half-edges make the selected
slopes a finite martingale. After projection to a common finite head, the terminal slopes are
represented exactly by random sums of disjoint-segment functionals whose squared coefficients
sum to at most one. As more of a segment’s location is revealed, conditional predictions of its
coefficient have bounded total squared change. Counting segment endpoints, or only segment
starts, finds an interval of levels where this prediction energy cannot sustain all the large new
coordinate layers selected from the graph. The coordinate selections and test estimates here are
specific to our norm.
    Section 6 then treats the linear path-cost norm. It states each imported energy estimate
alongside the graph construction that meets its support and timing hypotheses. Some constructions
keep the exact graph slopes and allow small tails outside a head; others replace the slopes by
finitely supported approximations while preserving their exact averaging identity.
    Section 7 collects the remaining midpoint transfers after these direct proofs. They distinguish
squared or higher-power losses, a fixed contraction from a separated lens, and growth of an actual
child slope. Sections 8 and 9 introduce the other path-cost, segment-start and recursive-array
norms and apply these transfers. Each application states its intrinsic input from the complete

2     From infinitely many midpoints to diamond distortion
A symmetric midpoint estimate becomes a graph obstruction because each edge of a countably
branching diamond has infinitely many replacement midpoints. Finite-dimensional compactness
lets us compare two of them while making any prescribed finite set of coordinates negligible. We
give the transfer with all scaling factors and deduce the principal squared-loss bound. Section 7
develops further ways to accumulate midpoint estimates. These are instances of the midpoint
method for countably branching diamonds; see [2, Section 4]. The quantitative constants below
come from the estimates stated here.
    For x ∈ X and R ≥ 0, write LR (x) = {z ∈ X : ∥x + z∥ ≤ R, ∥x − z∥ ≤ R} for the symmetric
R-lens at x. Its members are deviations from the common midpoint, rather than the two endpoints
themselves.

2.1   The graph and its slopes
Let D0 consist of two vertices joined by an edge of length one. To obtain Dj+1 , replace every edge
[a, b] of Dj by paths [a, mi , b], indexed by i ∈ N, whose middle vertices are distinct and new. Both
new edges have half the length of their parent. Let dk be the shortest-path metric on the vertices
of Dk . We call an edge present at stage j a stage-j edge; it need no longer be an edge at stage
k > j.
     Distances between old vertices are unchanged at a replacement. Indeed, an old path can be
replaced by paths of the same total length. Conversely, a path between old vertices consists of
two-edge portions through new vertices; each portion either returns to the same old vertex or
traverses the endpoints of one old edge. Compressing these portions gives an old walk of no
greater length. It follows that a stage-j edge has endpoint distance ℓj = 2−j in Dk . Its distinct
replacement midpoints have mutual distance ℓj , and distance ℓj /2 from either endpoint. At their
introduction the assertion is immediate because two distinct middle vertices are nonadjacent, and
every incident edge has length ℓj /2; subsequent replacements preserve it.

2.2   The finite-dimensional pair selection
The next observation records the approximation errors that will be used throughout. It requires
bounded finite-rank projections, rather than contractivity of the complementary projections.

Lemma 2.1. Let X be a Banach space, R > 0, and let (zi )i∈N satisfy ∥x ± zi ∥ ≤ R with
∥zi − zj ∥ ≥ ε > 0 for i ̸= j. Every half-difference w = (zi − zj )/2 with i ̸= j satisfies ∥w∥ ≥ ε/2
and ∥x ± w∥ ≤ R. Fix η > 0. The following two selections are available.

   1. Suppose p has finite support, ∥p−x∥ < η, and P is a bounded finite-rank coordinate projection
      satisfying P p = p. There are distinct i, j for which, with w = (zi − zj )/2 and y = w − P w,

                   ∥P w∥ < η,    ∥y∥ ≥ ε/2 − η,      ∥p∥ ≥ ∥x∥ − η,        ∥p ± y∥ ≤ R + 2η.    (2.4)

   2. If F is a closed finite-codimensional subspace, there are distinct i, j whose half-difference
      satisfies dist(w, F ) < η. This selection requires no coordinate structure on X.

Proof. For the common endpoint bound use

               x + w = 12 ((x + zi ) + (x − zj )),       x − w = 21 ((x − zi ) + (x + zj )).

In the first selection, the bounded sequence (P zi ) has two distinct members at distance less than

step. Earlier diamond obstructions include Lee–Naor’s telescoping squared-distance inequality

pays a bounded squared-norm cost for each increase in its evaluation. The second balances prefix
sums of comparable sizes and controls the final test all at once.

5    Direct graph obstructions from atomic prediction energy
We give two direct proofs that the countably branching diamonds do not embed with uniformly
bounded distortion into the finite-height segment-forest dual. Both proofs first select finitely many
edge slopes from the infinitely branching graph. An atomic representation then turns these slopes
into conditional means. The first proof counts segment endpoints and predicts the coefficients
of segments crossing a long interval of layers. The second counts segment starts and follows
each segment until it terminates. Their energy estimates concern these finite martingales; no
midpoint-modulus estimate is needed.
