                                                Abstract
        We prove the circulant Hadamard conjecture: a real circulant Hadamard matrix has
     order 1 or 4. As a consequence, Barker sequences of length greater than one exist exactly at
     lengths 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 11, 13, proving the Barker-sequence conjecture.


1    Introduction
A real Hadamard matrix of order n is a matrix H with entries in {−1, 1} such that HH T = nIn .
It is circulant if there are signs h0 , . . . , hn−1 for which

                               Hij = hj−i mod n          (0 ≤ i, j < n).

Thus every row is a cyclic shift of the first. For such a sign matrix, define the periodic
autocorrelations by
                                       n−1
                                       ∑
                            Ph (t) =         hj hj+t mod n       (0 ≤ t < n).
                                       j=0

Here Ph (0) = n, and the Hadamard condition is equivalent to Ph (t) = 0 for every 1 ≤ t < n.
The circulant Hadamard conjecture, traditionally attributed to Ryser [12, 5], asserts that the
only possible orders are 1 and 4. We prove this conjecture.
Theorem 1.1. For a positive integer n, a real circulant Hadamard matrix of order n exists if
and only if n ∈ {1, 4}.
   The theorem also completes the classification of Barker-sequence lengths. For an integer
n > 1, a sign sequence a = (a0 , . . . , an−1 ) ∈ {−1, 1}n is a Barker sequence if its nontrivial
aperiodic autocorrelations satisfy
                                                                        n−t−1
                                                                         ∑
                      |Ca (t)| ≤ 1     (1 ≤ t < n),          Ca (t) =           aj aj+t .
                                                                         j=0

The Barker-sequence conjecture asserts that no such sequence has length greater than 13. Turyn
and Storer established the odd-length nonexistence theorem [16]; we use the later proof of
Schmidt and Willms [14] for the exact list. For even length greater than two, the Barker
inequalities imply that all nontrivial periodic autocorrelations vanish [16, 17]. Theorem 1.1
therefore settles the remaining even case.
Corollary 1.2 (Barker-sequence lengths). For an integer n > 1, a Barker sequence of length n
exists if and only if
                                 n ∈ {2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 11, 13}.
    Section 5 gives the short periodic-autocorrelation argument and records examples at every
listed length.

Conversely, such a difference set supplies a circulant Hadamard row by assigning a negative

additional arithmetic conclusion.
    Turyn studied these cyclic difference sets through the arithmetic of cyclotomic character
sums and the Fourier relations imposed by their common coefficient array [17, pp. 319–323].
His nonexistence results show that an order greater than four must be 4u2 , where u is odd and
not a prime power [17, Corollary 2 to Theorem 6, p. 333, and Theorem 8 with its Corollary,
pp. 335–336]. The proof in Section 2 gives a streamlined group-ring form of his descent for the
2-primary coefficients.
    Later work sharpened the arithmetic restrictions. For a positive integer M , let ζM be a
primitive M -th root of unity. For X ∈ Z[ζM ] with XX = m ∈ Z>0 , Bernhard Schmidt’s field
descent places a multiple of X by a root of unity in a cyclotomic subfield determined explicitly by
the ambient conductor M and m [13]. Leung and Schmidt developed group-ring decompositions
that refine these restrictions [4], followed by further exclusions in their 2012 work and their
anti-field-descent method [5, 6]. Logan and Mossinghoff’s 2017 computation, using searches for
double Wieferich prime pairs, excluded all but 4 489 integers n with 4 < n ≤ 4 · 1030 as possible
circulant Hadamard orders [7]. These 4 489 integers remained as candidates under those tests.
    Complementary work examines the coefficients more directly. Euler, Gallardo and Ra-
havandrainy obtained combinatorial restrictions on the blocks of signs in the first row [1].
Approximate orthogonality has a different range of possibilities: Steinerberger used flat Little-
wood polynomials, which have sign coefficients and uniformly controlled modulus on the unit
circle, to show that in every order there is a real sign circulant whose singular values lie between
                                 √
two fixed positive multiples of n [15]. The conjecture requires exact equality of all singular
           √
values to n. Complete proofs of the conjecture have also been claimed in [10, 11, 9, 2, 8].
    The comparison of character values also has precedents in Turyn’s work. His preliminary fact
(4) obtains a root-of-unity quotient from equality of principal ideals and absolute values. His
Theorem 5 fixes a character and compares the values obtained by multiplying it by characters in
a subgroup. It assumes equality of their principal ideals and that the subgroup order is coprime
to both the difference-set norm parameter k − λ and the order of the fixed character [17, p. 321,
item (4), and Theorem 5, pp. 328–329].

3.2   From local units to roots of unity
The comparison supplies control at the primes dividing u. The norm identity supplies both the
remaining local control and the complex absolute values. We use two elementary facts to turn
these properties into an order restriction. The first is Kronecker’s criterion [3, Statement I].
Lemma 3.3.
  (i) Let B ⊆ C be generated over Z by finitely many roots of unity. If z ∈ B satisfies |σ(z)| = 1
      for every unital ring homomorphism σ : B → C, then z is a root of unity.
 (ii) In a local domain with residue characteristic p, a root of unity with residue 1 has p-power
      order. In particular, if its order is coprime to p, it equals 1.
Proof. For (i), let d be the finite rank of the free additive group of B, as in the proof of
Lemma 2.1. Multiplication by an element of B is thus an integer matrix. On complexifying this
representation, the matrices for the generating roots of unity commute and have finite order.
Each is diagonalizable, since its minimal polynomial divides some X m − 1, which has distinct
complex roots. They are simultaneously diagonalizable: the eigenspaces of one are invariant
under the others, and one repeats the argument on those eigenspaces.
    On a common eigenvector, multiplication by any b ∈ B acts as a scalar σ(b). This is a
well-defined unital ring homomorphism because it comes from the multiplication representation,
so all relations between the root generators are respected. The multiplication matrix of z is
diagonal in the same basis, with eigenvalues λ1 , . . . , λd of modulus one. For each positive integer
a, its a-th power has an     integer characteristic polynomial. The coefficient of degree d − j has
                          (d )
absolute value at most j . Only finitely many such polynomials can occur, so the union of their
sets of roots is finite. Each sequence λaj therefore takes only finitely many values. Since λj ̸= 0,
two equal powers imply that λj has finite order. A common multiple of these orders makes the
multiplication matrix the identity; applying it to 1 ∈ B proves that a positive power of z is 1.
    For (ii), write the order as pa m with p ∤ m. If m > 1, raising the root to the pa -th power
gives an element ζ of order m and residue 1. In a domain, (ζ − 1)(1 + ζ + · · · + ζ m−1 ) = 0 then

5    Barker sequences
We finish by proving the length classification stated in Corollary 1.2. The new input is the
circulant Hadamard theorem for even lengths; the odd-length classification is classical.

Proof of Corollary 1.2. Suppose first that a is a Barker sequence of even length n > 2. Its
periodic autocorrelation at a nontrivial shift is
                            n−1

summands are negative. Consequently Pa (t) ≡ n (mod 4). Also Ca (t) ≡ n − t (mod 2). For
t = 2, both Ca (2) and Ca (n − 2) are even; the Barker bound makes them zero. Thus Pa (2) = 0,
and 4 | n. For every nontrivial shift, the Barker bounds give |Pa (t)| ≤ 2, while the congruence
