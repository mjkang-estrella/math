                     Odd Characteristic

                                                 Abstract
          We construct a counterexample to Kaplansky’s direct-finiteness conjecture in odd character-
      istic. For one specified odd prime p, we obtain a field K of order p4 , a finitely generated group
      G containing torsion, and finite sums a, b ∈ K[G] with ab = 1 but ba ̸= 1. The same elements
      define a cellular automaton on K G that is injective but not surjective.


1    Introduction

torsion. The result here is a negative answer in one specified odd characteristic.

    The word “specified” refers to finite formulas and choices in ordered finite sets. The field is
a quadratic tower, the group is built from finitely many finite groups and explicit identifications,
and the sums are defined without solving the word problem of that group. The construction is not
claimed to have been evaluated as a practical computation. It concerns the displayed prime, not
every odd prime.

The finiteness problem. For finite G, the algebra and all its finite matrix rings are finite-
dimensional over K. A one-sided inverse between their left multiplication operators is therefore
two-sided. This argument works even when the characteristic divides |G|. The difficulty for an
infinite group is to obtain a corresponding conclusion from finite support alone.
    Kaplansky proved stable finiteness in characteristic zero using finite von Neumann algebras, and
asked whether the one-sided-inverse property persists in positive characteristic [Kap72, pp. 122–123].
Ara, O’Meara, and Perera proved stable finiteness for free-by-amenable groups over arbitrary division
rings [AOP02]. Elek and Szabó [ES04, Corollary 4.7] established the same conclusion for all sofic

distinct elements separated. These theorems are constraints on the example, not hypotheses about
its group. In particular, Elek–Szabó’s theorem implies, after the scalar construction is completed,
that G cannot be sofic.
    A separate characteristic-two construction uses incidence structures, polynomial pairings, a
planar group-embedding argument, and character packing [Ope26, Theorem 1.1]. The construction
here has a different local mechanism. A high-degree subspace of a modular translation representation
is annihilated on one side; a weighted pairing gives a compatible annihilation on the other. The
characteristic-two theorem is not an input to this odd-prime argument, and the two methods retain
distinct coefficient hypotheses.

The dynamical consequence. A configuration over a finite alphabet A is any function G → A.
A cellular automaton has one finite memory set E ⊂ G and one rule applied to (x(gu))u∈E at
every g. A group is surjunctive when every injective such automaton, over every finite alphabet, is
surjective. Gottschalk proposed this for all groups [Got73]; Gromov and Weiss proved it for sofic
groups [Gro99, Wei00]. The group-ring connection is recorded by Elek–Szabó and developed in the
linear theory of Ceccherini-Silberstein–Coornaert [ES04, CSC06]. Phung proved stable finiteness
over every field for surjunctive groups [Phu23, Theorem B]; subsequent proofs and extensions
include [CSCP25a, CSCP25b].
    Section 8 gives the elementary transfer with its actual multiplication convention. The coefficients
of b define an injective automaton, those of a give a left inverse, and a nonzero coefficient of ba − 1
specifies a missing point indicator. The argument excludes preimages of arbitrary support. It is a
consequence of the scalar construction, not a further existence theorem.
    All matrices below act on columns of free right modules. Matrix entries therefore multiply
coordinates on the left. Transpose is ordinary transpose over K, with no involution. Cardinalities
and dimensions in rank comparisons are integers, not elements of the coefficient field. For a positive

4     Gluing the finite groups without collapsing them
The quotient G0 of Section 3 identifies a cyclic subgroup across each edge of Γ. It must retain
every vertex group: otherwise a later nonzero element of a finite group algebra could disappear.
We first prove a general injectivity criterion and then verify it for the groups used here. The local
injectivity argument belongs to the C(4)–T (4) theory of graphical complexes of groups [Pry24,
Section 8]. We give a direct proof using only labelled spherical maps and Euler’s formula.

    We describe the spherical maps needed to encode this sequence. A map is a finite connected
graph drawn tamely on the sphere, allowing loops and parallel edges. An edge is either neutral,
with value 1, or is labelled by an edge γ of ∆. In the latter case its two oriented traversals, called
darts, have mutually inverse values in Cγ \ {1}. Every face has a type i ∈ V (∆) incident to every
label in its boundary walk. Its boundary word is evaluated in Di , using the specified embeddings.
A face is ordinary when this evaluation is the identity. Call the map exceptional if precisely one face
has nonidentity evaluation and all other faces have identity evaluation. Changing the starting point
conjugates a boundary product, and reversing the walk inverts it, so identity versus nonidentity is
well-defined.

block on the longer path. The strip faces then read either a letter followed by its inverse, or the
inserted relator, with neutral edges interspersed. If a level is empty, the adjacent nonempty word is
the entire inserted or deleted block; one face between these two levels suffices. Assign the relator’s
vertex type to its face, and either endpoint type of a letter’s label to an unchanged-letter face. All
these faces evaluate to 1. Include the neutral edges along the two vertical sides of the rectangle.

    We shall prove that no exceptional map exists. Choose one with the smallest possible number
of edges. This number is positive: the only face of an isolated vertex has the empty word, which
evaluates to 1. The reductions below will make every vertex degree and every ordinary face length
at least four away from a possible loop. Euler’s formula will then rule out both the loop-free case
and the remaining one-loop case.

Boundary walks and elementary reductions. Fix an orientation of the sphere. At each
vertex let σ be the cyclic successor of outgoing darts and let α reverse a dart. Boundary walks
are the cycles of σα: after traversing a dart, take the successor of its reverse. For a disconnected
drawing we use each component’s own boundary walks, counting one empty walk for an isolated
vertex. If a drawing has v vertices, e edges, F such walks and c components, then

                                          v − e + F = 2c.                                      (4.2)

For completeness, in a connected spherical drawing a nonbridge edge lies on a simple circuit. Its
two sides are therefore on distinct boundary walks; deleting it merges those walks and preserves
connectedness. Repeated deletion leaves a tree, which has one boundary walk and e = v − 1. This
proves the formula for a connected drawing; summing proves the displayed formula. The same
walk rules follow by taking small vertex discs and thin edge bands.
    A neutral nonloop edge can be contracted, with its neutral darts omitted from the boundary
words. This preserves every face evaluation and contradicts minimality. For a neutral loop based
at b, retain the closed side containing the exceptional face, then remove the loop. The retained
graph is connected: each side of the loop attaches to the other only at b. The other dart slots on
the retained side form an interval between the loop slots; closing that interval gives precisely the
old walks on that side with the neutral loop dart removed. Thus their evaluations are unchanged.
If only b remains, its walk is empty and could not be exceptional. In every possible case a smaller
exceptional map results. Hence every edge is nonneutral.
    There are no bridges. To see this, delete a bridge with value s, leaving two components. Its
incident face walk splits into words W1 , W2 , one in each component, possibly empty. Both receive
the old face type. With suitable starting point and orientation, the old word was

ordinary, the evaluations of W1 and W2 are either both identity or both nonidentity. In the first
case retain the component with the original exceptional face. In the second retain the component
without that face: its new boundary walk is now its unique exceptional face. All choices give a
smaller exceptional map.

Proposition 4.2. Every natural map Hi → G0 is injective. In particular, the maps K[Hi ] → K[G0 ]

Proof. We verify the short-word hypothesis of Lemma 4.1. At a fixed vertex, every incident edge

                                       (j̄2 − j̄1 )(j̄3 − j̄1 )(j̄3 − j̄2 ) ̸= 0.

    For a mixed word of length two or three, one of U, V supplies exactly one syllable. After a cyclic
permutation, a putative relation would equate that nonidentity syllable to a product of syllables
from the other subgroup. This contradicts U ∩ V = {1}. For lengths two and three, cyclically
distinct consecutive labels are precisely distinct labels, so all required words have been covered.

injections. The group-algebra assertion follows because distinct elements of an embedded subgroup
remain distinct basis elements of K[G0 ].

Finite prescriptions and scope. Every choice of character, basis, field element, or finite inverse
occurs in an explicitly finite set. Products of the resulting group words define finite group-algebra
sums even when different words happen to coincide. No word comparisons are needed to define the
elements, and the kernel certificate and injective matrix block certify the nonzero reverse defect.
The countable permutation actions used to certify group embeddings are not coefficient-search
inputs.
    Containing torsion does not say that every element has finite order. The group must be infinite,
since finite-dimensional left multiplication proves direct finiteness for every finite group algebra, in
modular characteristic as well as otherwise. The coefficient field throughout remains the specified
K of order p4 .
    More generally, the supports of any two finite sums in a group algebra generate a finitely
generated subgroup. Its group algebra embeds by the group bases, preserving both a one-sided
product and its nonzero reverse defect. Thus a universal direct-finiteness theorem for finitely
generated groups would already imply the assertion for all groups.
