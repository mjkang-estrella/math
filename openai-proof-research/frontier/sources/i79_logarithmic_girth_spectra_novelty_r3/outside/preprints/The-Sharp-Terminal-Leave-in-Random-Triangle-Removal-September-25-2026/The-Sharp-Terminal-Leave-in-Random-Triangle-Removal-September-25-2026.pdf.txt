           The sharp terminal leave in random triangle removal
                                                 OpenAI

                                          September 25, 2026


                                                 Abstract
          Starting from the complete graph on n vertices, repeatedly remove the three edges of a
      uniformly chosen remaining triangle. We  √ prove that the number of edges left at termination,
      divided by n3/2 , converges in L2 to 1/(2 2). This proves the triangle case of the sharp-constant
      conjecture of Joos and Kühn. In particular, the same limit holds in probability and for the
      normalized expectation.

1     Introduction
Begin with the complete graph Kn . At each step, choose uniformly from the triangles whose three
edges are still present, and delete those edges. Stop when no triangle remains. The removed triangles
form an edge-disjoint packing; the edges left over form its leave. Let Fn be the number of edges in
this terminal graph. The problem is to determine the typical size of this leave, including its leading
constant.
    The accepted triangles form a partial Steiner triple system, and Fn measures its uncovered
pairs. Bollobás and Erdős conjectured in 1990 that the expected leave has order n3/2 , as reported in
[BFL15, Introduction]. Spencer’s branching-process analysis and the independent nibble argument
of Rödl and Thoma showed that the leave is o(n2 ) with high probability [Spe95, RT96]. Grable
proved bounds O(n11/6+ξ ) for each fixed ξ > 0 and outlined the higher-order calculations leading
to O(n7/4+ξ ) [Gra97, Theorems 5 and 7]. Bohman, Frieze and Lubetzky gave a short proof of
the bound O(n7/4 log5/4 n) [BFL10, Theorem 1] and subsequently proved Fn = n3/2+o(1) with high
probability [BFL15, Theorem 1]. Their analysis uses self-correcting dynamic concentration and
carefully chosen triangular extensions to determine the exponent.
    Joos and Kühn developed removal estimates for general strictly balanced hypergraphs, including
pseudorandom starting hypergraphs [JK25, Theorems 1.2–1.3]. Their Conjecture   √      16.2 predicts an
asymptotic constant for the terminal leave; its triangle specialization is 1/(2 2) in probability. The
following theorem proves this triangle case with L2 convergence. Numbered references to Joos and
Kühn refer throughout to version 2.

1.2   Conventions
All graphs are finite and simple. An embedding is injective and preserves edges; it need not preserve
nonedges. Thus an embedding of a fixed labeled cycle counts its ordered vertex labels, without
division by automorphisms. The notation (1 ± a)b means a number in [(1 − a)b, (1 + a)b]. All
unspecified constants below are independent of n and of the realized good prefix graph. They may
depend on the fixed parameters introduced next. A statement holds with superpolynomially high
probability if its failure probability is n−ω(1) , meaning smaller than n−C for every fixed C > 0 when
n is sufficiently large.

3     Exact queries and independent unfoldings
Fix a graph G supplied by Proposition 2.2, and retain E = E(G) and the family T of triangle edge
sets. We first express edge survival by a finite recursive test. We then give each occurrence in that
recursion fresh randomness. The resulting auxiliary model has exact product identities, which we
will estimate before comparing it with the finite process.

3.1    Priorities and the finite test
Independent priorities give the standard random greedy packing representation; see Spencer [Spe95,
Section 1], Grable [Gra97, Section 1], and the triangle-removal accounts [BFL15, Section 1.2] [JK25,
Section 14.1].
    Assign independent uniform priorities UT ∈ [0, 1] to the triangles T ∈ T . Scan the triangles
in increasing priority order, accepting a triangle precisely when all three of its edges remain, and
deleting those edges on acceptance. We work on the probability-one event that all priorities are
distinct. An edge is untouched before t if no accepted triangle with priority strictly below t contains
it.

Lemma 3.1 (Priority representation). For any finite simple graph G, the graph sequence indexed by
the accepted triangles in this scan has the law of uniform sequential triangle removal started from G.

Proof. The priority order is a uniform random permutation of T . Conditional on any scanned
prefix, the unscanned triangles have uniform relative order. Every currently present triangle is
unscanned: an accepted triangle has lost its edges, and a rejected triangle already had a deleted
edge and can never become present again. Until the next acceptance the graph stays fixed. Thus
the next accepted triangle, if there is one, is the first currently present triangle in the remaining
permutation, and is uniform among those triangles. This also identifies the absorbing state when
none remains.

    In applying the lemma after the good prefix, the priorities are fresh random variables on the
triangles of the fixed graph G. The original process has the same continuation law because its
next-step rule depends only on its current graph.
    A call consists of a focus (one or two edges), a threshold t, and, for a two-edge focus, a parent
triangle. The finite test has two forms:
    • A root call has focus {e}, threshold t ∈ [0, 1], and no parent. Its candidates are all triangles
      containing e.
    • A non-root call has focus {f, g} ⊂ T , parent triangle T , and threshold t = UT . Its candidates
      are all triangles meeting the focus, except T .
Distinct triangles meet in at most one graph edge. Consequently every candidate S contains exactly
one focus edge, denoted e(S). Its child call has focus S \ {e(S)}, parent S, and threshold US .
   At a call the test performs the following steps.
    1. Expose the priorities of all candidates, in a fixed deterministic order of triangle types. Each
       candidate occurs once in this list.
    2. Sort the candidates by priority and consider those with US < t in that order. For a pair focus
       this is one joint ordering of the candidates at both focus edges.
    3. Evaluate each considered candidate’s child. At the first child returning true, stop and return
       false. If no child returns true, return true.


                                                  7
Exposing a candidate does not require visiting its child: priorities above the threshold and candidates
skipped after an earlier success are also exposed. All calls use the same finite family (UT )T ∈T . The
test has no side effects; it does not delete edges or change any priority.

Lemma 3.2 (Exact paired test). For any finite simple graph with distinct triangle priorities, the
finite test terminates. A root call at (e, t) returns true exactly when e is untouched before t in the
priority scan. A call with focus {f, g} ⊂ T and threshold UT returns true exactly when both f and g
are untouched before UT .

Proof. Along a recursive path the thresholds strictly decrease through the priorities of triangle types.
No type can occur twice on that path, so its length is at most |T |. The branching is finite, proving
termination.
    We prove correctness by induction on the number of triangle priorities strictly below the call’s
threshold. Every child has a smaller threshold, so the induction hypothesis applies to it. Write F
for the focus of the present call. We claim that some edge of F is deleted before t if and only if
some candidate S with US < t has both edges of S \ {e(S)} untouched before US .
    For the forward implication, take an accepted triangle that deletes a focus edge before t. It is a
candidate and all its edges are untouched just before its acceptance. The omitted parent, when
present, is at the threshold itself and cannot be this triangle. Conversely, suppose such a candidate
exists and every focus edge is untouched before t. Its focus edge is then present at US , as are its
two other edges. The scan accepts it, contradicting the assumed survival of the focus. This proves
the claim. By induction the candidate condition is precisely that its child returns true, and the test
returns false exactly when at least one such child exists.

   The parent omission is local to its call and is justified by the strict threshold. It does not erase
that triangle from descendant lists. Also, the two finite survival events in a pair call need not be
independent. We retain their joint chronological test, including its stopping rule, throughout the
comparison argument.

3.2   Fresh randomness at each occurrence
Backward exploration and comparison with independent branching occur in Spencer’s analysis
[Spe95, Sections 2–4]. We retain the paired query rule above and all candidate-list exposures for
the finite comparison.
     For a fixed root type, first construct the deterministic tree of all possible calls. Each node has
one child for each candidate on its list, with the focus and parent specified above. The tree is
countable and finitely branching. A triangle type may label many different child occurrences.
     Assign an independent uniform variable to each child occurrence. This variable serves both as
the candidate priority in its parent’s list and as the child’s threshold if the child is visited. Apply
the same joint scanning and stopping rule on this tree. Different occurrences of a triangle type now
have independent priorities; different root queries use disjoint trees with independent variables. We
call this the independent unfolding.
     We also allow a singleton root of type e with a specified triangle T ∋ e omitted from its root
list. The omission affects that list only. Descendant occurrences of T remain available whenever
their own parent rule allows them. A two-edge root with focus {f, g} ⊂ T , omitted parent T , and
prescribed threshold t is defined by the same independent construction.
     The finiteness argument uses factorial decay along decreasing-priority paths, as in the local
construction of graph adsorption processes by Penrose and Sudbury [PS05, Proposition 1 and its
proof]. We give the argument for the present occurrence trees.

4     Stable unfolded probabilities
We now compute the survival probabilities in the independent unfolding. Throughout this section,
G is any graph satisfying Proposition 2.2; every estimate is uniform over such graphs. Spencer’s
branching model has survival probability (1 + Qc)−1/Q [Spe95, Section 3, equation (9)]. With Q = 2
and c = Dt, its scalar comparison function is

                                 q(t) = (1 + 2Dt)−1/2 ,          0 ≤ t ≤ 1.                         (4.1)

It solves q ′ (t) = −Dq(t)3 with q(0) = 1. We will show that the probabilities qe (t) and qe,T (t) from
Section 3 are uniformly asymptotic to q(t). The essential issue is stability: small discrepancies

5    Coupling the finite and independent tests
Fix a graph G satisfying Proposition 2.2. The independent probabilities from Proposition 4.2
approximate survival only if repeated uses of a finite triangle priority are sufficiently rare. We first
give an exact coupling and then reduce its failure to a graph configuration supported on two visited
call paths. Throughout this section, a triangle type means a member of T , as distinguished from its
possibly many occurrences in an unfolding.
    Fix k ∈ {1, 2}. Sample k independent uniform oriented edges of G, with replacement and
independently of all priorities. There are 2m possible oriented edges. The orientation names the two
endpoints and does not affect the test. With these roots, compare two experiments: the finite tests
sharing one priority for each triangle type, and the independent unfoldings with disjoint occurrence
trees for different roots. Every root has threshold 1. Execute the roots in a fixed order, including
every root regardless of the preceding answers.
    At each visited call, expose the priorities of all candidates in a fixed type order before sorting
them for the test. Thus an exposed list includes candidates above the threshold and candidates
never traversed because an earlier child returned true. In the independent experiment, let Ck be the
event that a triangle type appears at least twice among all these exposed lists. There is no repetition
within one list: a candidate meeting both edges of a pair focus would be its omitted parent triangle.

5.1   The graph of visited calls
We now work entirely in the independent experiment. Its visited calls form a finite rooted forest
almost surely. Construct a graph Γ and a label map λ : V (Γ) → V (G) from this forest. Each root

with all roots first and every parent before its children. The following properties hold in any such
order:
  (i) Every formal edge has adjacent labels in G, and the three vertices in any one gluing have

We now bound the probability of the marked path unions furnished by Lemma 5.2. The required
