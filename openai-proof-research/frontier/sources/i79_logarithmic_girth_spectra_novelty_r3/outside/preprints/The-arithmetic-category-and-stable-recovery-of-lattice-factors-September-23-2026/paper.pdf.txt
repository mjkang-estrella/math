   THE ARITHMETIC CATEGORY AND STABLE RECOVERY OF
                   LATTICE FACTORS

                                             OPENAI


     Abstract. We prove stable canonical recovery for scalar-twisted group factors of ICC
     groups commensurable with property-(T ) lattices over characteristic-zero local fields. Ev-
     ery specified stable isomorphism to a scalar-twisted factor of any countably infinite ICC
     group recovers the group, the cocycle up to a normalized cochain, and equal amplification
     scales, with an implementing partial isometry in the given matrix corners. We compute the
     arithmetic correspondence subcategory for arbitrary countable ICC groups, including all
     bounded maps, summands, conjugates, and Connes fusion, and combine it with the com-
     panion’s arithmetic exhaustion theorem. Multiplication also recovers arbitrary finite-index
     factor neighbors, with the exact projective condition on the matched cocycle ratio.

                                      1. Introduction
   A group factor is built from the multiplication of a discrete group, but an isomorphism
of factors need not preserve its distinguished group unitaries. We ask when the group, a
scalar twist of its multiplication, and the given factor isomorphism can all be recovered. For
the lattice class below, we prove this recovery even after amplification and with an arbitrary
ICC comparison group.
   Connes proved countability of the fundamental group of a property-(T ) group factor [9];
his later problems ask whether nonisomorphic ICC property-(T ) groups have nonisomorphic
factors, and ask for the fundamental groups of those factors [10, Chapter V, Appendix B.ε,
Problems 1–2]. The stronger stable formulation displayed by Popa asks for recovery of the
given isomorphism up to a character and inner conjugacy, with amplification scale one [24,
Section 3, Equations (3.2) and (3.2′ )].
   The proof uses the more general relation of finite correspondence: two factors act nor-
mally and commute on one Hilbert space, with finite module dimensions on both sides.
We determine these correspondences through isomorphisms between finite-index subgroups
and finite-dimensional projective representations. The representation records the ratio of
the two scalar cocycles on the matched subgroups. Neither cocycle separately need have a
finite-dimensional realization.
   Our algebraic calculation identifies every bounded map, summand, and composition of
these arithmetic models for arbitrary countable ICC groups. The geometric exhaustion the-
orem of the companion [20, Theorem 1.2] makes the models exhaustive for the lattice class.
Their integral dimensions recover stable isomorphisms; their compatibility with multiplica-
tion also recovers arbitrary finite-index factor neighbors.

1.3. Arbitrary finite-index factor neighbors. We next allow the second factor to have
no given group presentation. A finite-index factor neighbor of M is a factor joined to M by
a nonzero bifinite correspondence. The arithmetic data describing such a neighbor involve
finite-index subgroup isomorphisms rather than an isomorphism of the whole groups.
   For a group A, a normalized projective unitary representation with multiplier β is a map
σ : A → U(V ) satisfying σ(a)σ(b) = β(a, b)σ(ab) and σ(e) = I. We call β finite type when
such a representation exists with 0 < dimC V < ∞; its image need not be finite. For a group
homomorphism δ, the pullback cocycle is δ ∗ ω(a, b) = ω(δ(a), δ(b)).

1.5. History and the role of the arithmetic category. Popa’s deformation/rigidity
theory produced rigidity theorems for crossed products and Bernoulli actions. Ioana’s W ∗ -
superrigidity theorem for Bernoulli actions of ICC property-(T ) groups concerns an action
together with its crossed product [14]. Ioana–Popa–Vaes obtained the first W ∗ -superrigid
groups, in classes of generalized wreath products [15]. Chifan–Ioana–Osin–Sun constructed
the first such groups with property (T ), using wreath-like products [8]. Rigidity of an action
and recovery from a bare group factor are distinct problems, and the correspondence category
asks additionally for finite index, multiplicities, and composition.
   The geometric route for lattices has its own antecedents. For lattices in Sp(n, 1)/{±1},
n ≥ 2, Cowling–Haagerup’s completely bounded approximation invariant already determines
n from the group factor [11, Main theorem and Propositions 6.1–6.2]. Bekka classified the
characters of SLn (Z) for n ≥ 3, and Peterson proved a finite-factor representation dichotomy
for irreducible higher-rank property-(T ) lattices [1, 22]. Boutonnet–Houdayer developed sta-
tionary and noncommutative boundary methods and recovered character rigidity in that
framework [6]. These results provide important context for the companion’s geometry. They
do not by themselves identify all finite correspondences to arbitrary ICC groups. The compan-
ion proves the exhaustion input in the full mixed-local-field class stated here, with reducible
products and arbitrary scalar twists.

   Jones’s index and the expectation inequality of Pimsner–Popa provide the dimension and
positivity framework [16, 23]. We use Connes’s correspondence category and relative tensor
product [10, Chapter V, Appendix B]. Complete finite-index classifications and explicit fusion
rules already occur in Vaes’s work on generalized Bernoulli crossed products [26, Theorem 2.2
and Section 6.2]. Theorem 2.2 allows scalar twists at both endpoints and a finite-dimensional

graph bundles for twisted group factors. The full stabilizer of a virtual-isomorphism germ

that mix smaller presenting fibres.

tain left-right wreath products [12]. Their Proposition 6.5 reconstructs an intermediate factor
from finite-dimensional homogeneous spaces and their actual products, and Theorem 6.6 ap-

their homogeneous-product and minimal-corner argument from automorphism degrees to a
grading by virtual automorphism germs of Γ; finite-index domains and boundedness of the

1.6. Proof strategy and navigation. There are three algebraic steps. First, the graph
models admit orthogonal finite-dimensional fibres indexed by graph cosets. Fibres with the
same virtual-isomorphism germ must be grouped together before passing to a summand.
The full stabilizer of a germ has only one finite orbit on the germ space. Square summability
of each column of a bounded intertwiner therefore forces that map to preserve each grouped
fibre. This proves the Hom formula and makes the two dimensions sums of integer fibre
ranks times subgroup indices.
   Second, a bifinite correspondence with an unknown factor produces a finite-index extension
of the known group factor. Arithmetic exhaustion grades its L2 space by finite-dimensional
germ fibres. The Pimsner–Popa inequality turns those vectors into bounded operators. Prod-
ucts can then be computed on a common subgroup domain, and they multiply the germ
degrees. In a minimal corner of the identity fibre, every nonzero degree is a unitary line.
Those lines, with their actual products and trace, recover the group and scalar cocycle. The
identity block supplies the projective representation of the cocycle ratio.
   Third, the equivalence correspondence of a specified stable isomorphism has reciprocal
dimensions. Their integrality forces both to be one, leaving one full graph and one one-
dimensional projective fibre. The correspondence identification recovers the cochain. The
column realizations in the given matrix corners then recover the implementing partial isom-
etry for the specified map. Figure 1 separates these two consequences of the category calcu-
lation.
   Section 2 records the relative tensor convention and exact companion interface. Section 3
proves all model formulas, then applies exhaustion. Section 4 reconstructs factor neighbors
and specified stable maps. Section 5 proves the further consequences described above.

                  2. Finite modules and the exhaustion interface
   Our algebraic calculations need only countability and ICC. The geometric class enters
at one precise point: the exhaustion statement below says that the explicit models contain
every bifinite correspondence. We first fix the norm convention for their construction.
   For completeness, Lµ (Γ) is a factor when Γ is ICC: the absolute values of the Fourier
coefficients of a central L2 vector are constant on conjugacy classes, so square summability
kills every nonidentity coefficient.
2.1. Relative tensor products and elementary dimensions. All Hilbert-space inner
products are linear in the second variable. A vector ξ in an M –N correspondence is right
bounded if Rξ â = ξa extends to a bounded map from L2 (N ). For an N –P correspondence,
the relative tensor product is the completion, after quotienting out zero-norm vectors, of the
tensors with right-bounded first vector and inner product
(2.1)                        ⟨ξ ⊗N η, ξ ′ ⊗N η ′ ⟩ = ⟨η, (Rξ∗ Rξ′ )η ′ ⟩.
The right-module map Rξ∗ Rξ′ is left multiplication by an element of N . Balancing means
(ξa) ⊗N η = ξ ⊗N (aη) for a ∈ N . This is the finite tracial form of Connes’s relative tensor
product [10, Chapter V, Appendix B, Theorems 13 and 20].
   A finite right N -module has a column realization pL2 (N )n , with dimension (Trn ⊗τN )(p).
The vectors in pN n are right bounded and dense. The projection model and its dimension are
independent of the chosen realization [17, Theorem 10.1.1 and Proposition 10.1.3]. We prove
the normality of the graph models directly in Lemmas 3.2 and 3.3. The same elementary
construction precedes the geometric argument in the companion, so its exhaustion proof has
no dependence on our all-maps or summand classification. For n = dim σ, the elementary
dimensions are n[Λ : B] and n[Γ : A] in left/right order. The dimensions of actual summands
will require the full-stabilizer calculation.

2.2. The geometric input. The real simple factors in Definition 1.1 have higher rank or
are locally Sp(n, 1), n ≥ 2, or F4(−20) . At a finite place their relative rank is at least two.
Complex places are treated as real Lie groups in differential arguments. Property (T ) passes
between a locally compact group and a lattice and between a discrete group and a finite-
index subgroup; see [2]. In particular, that class is closed under abstract commensurability
among countably infinite ICC groups. The geometric companion treats arbitrary lattices in
the permitted finite products, without a dense-projection hypothesis. We use exactly the
following theorem.
Proposition 2.1 (Arithmetic exhaustion, companion Theorem 1.2). Let Γ ∈ K , let Λ be
any countably infinite ICC group, and let µ, ω be arbitrary normalized scalar cocycles. Every
nonzero separable bifinite Lµ (Γ)–Lω (Λ) correspondence is a closed bimodule summand of a
finite direct sum of the models E(A, B, δ, σ) in Equation (1.3), with the exact multiplier in
Equation (1.2).
   This is the Arithmetic exhaustion theorem of the complete companion [20, Theorem 1.2].
Its conclusion retains actual finite-index subgroup isomorphisms and their exact scalar ratios.
We do not assume that the projective representations have finite image, that either cocycle is
finite type, or that the original correspondence is irreducible. The algebraic proofs that follow
are independent of this proposition until its explicit application at the end of Section 3.

Lemma 3.1 (Full stabilizers and the finite-orbit test). The maps g ↦ [Ad(g)] and h ↦
[Ad(h)] are injective. The group Cd is the graph of an isomorphism δd : Ad → Bd between
finite-index subgroups. It contains Cδ at finite index, and δd extends δ. If a finite-index
subgroup of Cd fixes another germ x from Γ to Λ, then x = d. Equivalently, the only finite
Cd -orbit in the space of these germs is {d}. The same conclusion holds with Cd replaced by
Cδ .
Proof. The centralizer of a finite-index subgroup of an ICC group is trivial: an element of
that centralizer has a centralizer of finite index and therefore a finite conjugacy class. This
proves injectivity of the inner-germ maps.

(3.2)                                 Ad(h) = d Ad(g)d−1 .

   The finite-orbit conclusion will make germ labels intrinsic to the induced models. Cosets
of Cd correspond to actual germs, whereas several cosets of a presenting graph may represent
the same germ. After grouping those cosets, a bounded intertwiner will have matrix blocks
between the resulting finite-dimensional fibres. Equivariance makes a block’s norm constant
along a stabilizer orbit. The lemma will therefore force such a map to preserve each germ
label, once square summability of its columns is used.

exactly one orbit, a one-dimensional fibre, and Ad = SL3 (Z). The graph is therefore an
isomorphism δ : SL3 (Z) → Bd , where Bd has finite index in the same group. Lemma 5.5
makes Bd the whole group. The left dimension formula now gives [M : θ(M )] = 1, so
θ(M ) = M .                                                                              □
