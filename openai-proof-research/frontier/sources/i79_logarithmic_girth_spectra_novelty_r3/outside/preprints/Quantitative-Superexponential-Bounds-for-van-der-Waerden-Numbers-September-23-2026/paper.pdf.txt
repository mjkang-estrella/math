                                               Abstract
          We prove that there are absolute constants c > 0 and K0 such that Wr (k) > k ck⌊log2 r⌋
      for every k ≥ K0 and r ≥ 2. Consequently Wr (k)1/k → ∞ for each fixed r ≥ 2, giving a
      quantitative positive resolution of Erdős’s superexponential-growth question, including
      the two-color case.


1    Introduction
For positive integers r and k, let Wr (k) be the least positive integer N such that every map
[N ] → [r] is constant on some arithmetic progression

                 a, a + d, . . . , a + (k − 1)d,       a, d ≥ 1,   a + (k − 1)d ≤ N.

Here [N ] = {1, . . . , N }, and using fewer than r colors is allowed. The finiteness of these
numbers is van der Waerden’s theorem [24]. The lower-bound problem asks how long an
interval can be colored while avoiding a monochromatic progression of a prescribed length.
We prove a bound with one threshold on the length, valid for every number of colors.

Theorem 1.1. There is an absolute integer K0 such that, for every integer k ≥ K0 and
every integer r ≥ 2,
                         Wr (k) > k ck⌊log2 r⌋ , c = 10−5 .

    In particular, for each fixed r ≥ 2,

                                         lim Wr (k)1/k = ∞.
                                        k→∞

Erdős explicitly asked for this full limit in the two-color case [4, p. 90, (2)]. Thus Theorem 1.1
gives a quantitative positive resolution of his superexponential-growth question. It is stronger
than the assertion that W2 (k)/2k tends to infinity.

Earlier bounds. Early lower bounds were obtained by Erdős and Rado [6] and by
Schmidt [19]. Berlekamp’s algebraic construction gives W2 (p + 1) > p2p for every prime p
[2, Theorem 2]. The local lemma of Erdős and Lovász [5] provided another route to strong
coloring bounds. Szabó [23, Theorem 2] proved W2 (k) ≥ 2k /k ε for every fixed ε > 0 and all
sufficiently large k. Kozik and Shabanov [15, Theorem 2] subsequently obtained an absolute
β > 0 such that Wr (k) ≥ βrk−1 for all r ≥ 2 and k ≥ 3. Hunter [13, Theorem 1] improved
the exponential base for each fixed r ≥ 5, with a threshold allowed to depend on r.
    Recent results distinguish the two questions posed by Erdős. Fox and Hunter [8, Theorem 1]

    Growth in the number of colors, with the progression length fixed, has a different history.
Behrend’s construction and Rankin’s extension to longer progressions supply dense progression-

of Kelley and Meka gives a quasipolynomial coloring upper bound [14, Theorems 1.1–1.2].
For each fixed length at least five, Leng, Sah, and Sawhney obtained further density bounds,
improving the earlier general coloring upper bounds [16, Theorem 1.1].

Thus, for every fixed progression length k ≥ 3, as integer r tends to infinity, growth in the

The construction and its quantitative ingredient. We first construct a two-coloring
of a cyclic group ℤ/q D ℤ with q D ≥ k ck , where q is a power of a prime exceeding k. Let λ be
an integer dilation, to be chosen below. The homomorphisms

                 xi (n) = n/q i   (mod 1),      yi (n) = λxi (n),    1 ≤ i ≤ D,

supply two systems of circle coordinates. Partition each coordinate circle into small intervals,
and label a point by the tuple of intervals containing its coordinates. An outer color is
assigned to each such label. The intervals for y become finer near the cut used to select real
representatives. We arrange that every k-term cyclic progression with nonzero step either
contains a positive proportion of each outer color or has globally affine real representatives in
the y coordinates.
    A heavily repeated label determines a short rational period h. The quantitative obstacle
is to control such returns when the dimension grows with k: constants depending arbitrarily
on the dimension would not suffice. The quantitative choice is to let D grow and to make λ
contain the full primary part, at every prime at most D2 , of every possible period h ≤ 2⌈k 1/2 ⌉.
After dilation, each scalar rational period is either one or exceeds D2 . This separation permits

where p = k −1/20 and ỹ ∈ [−1/2, 1/2)D is the centered representative. Here ‖ · ‖2 is the

four times along a progression. Making a progression rich in both outer colors monochromatic
therefore requires many rare flips. In the possibly overlapping affine case, monochromaticity
prescribes values for many distinct flip variables. The global label-word count, together with
the three coefficients of the quadratic squared norm, gives only a small family of words of
outer colors and actual keys; identical words prescribe the same event. With D = ⌈k 1/10 ⌉,
their logarithmic count is O(k 9/10 log k) = o(pk). The two resulting probability estimates
eliminate every progression. A deterministic digit product then gives all r ≥ 2.
    The squared-norm geometry continues the tradition of Behrend’s construction [1]; Green
and Wolf [11] give a torus-annulus proof of Elkin’s refinement. For the asymmetric problem
of excluding a red three-term progression and a blue long progression, Green [10] uses
translates of a randomly perturbed ellipsoidal annulus, while Hunter [12] chooses annulus
radii independently at many centers. Here the randomness is attached to coordinate boxes
and integer squared-norm bands. Least-common-multiple dilations in cyclic groups appear in
Fox and Hunter [8]; digit carry control and finite-field norm colorings for a related symmetric-
progression problem appear in Shi and Dong [21]. We prove all geometric, counting, and
probabilistic ingredients needed here, including the finite local lemma.

Organization. Section 2 fixes the cyclic group and the two meshes. Section 3 proves the
global and anchored pattern counts, and Section 4 uses them to construct the outer coloring.
Section 5 turns its local tests into the balanced-or-affine alternative. Section 6 introduces the
norm-indexed perturbation and proves the cyclic two-color theorem. Section 7 transfers that
theorem to integer intervals and all color counts, and proves the additional growth statements.
Appendix A supplies the elementary finite upper bound.

A     An elementary finite upper bound
We record a coarse quantitative form of the classical block-and-focus induction; see van der
Waerden [25, pp. 142–145]. The recursion below proves finiteness directly, involves only
previously defined integers, and applies to every positive number of colors.

Theorem A.1. Define F (r, 1) = 1 and F (r, 2) = r + 1 for every integer r ≥ 1. For k ≥ 3,
with r and k fixed, define

           m0 = 1,       mt+1 = 2mt F (rmt , k − 1)        (0 ≤ t < r),     F (r, k) = mr .   (A.1)

Proof. The recursion is well founded: construct all F (r, k) at a given length k after constructing
them for every number of colors at length k − 1. Each of the r steps in Equation (A.1) then
uses finite positive integers.
    A one-term progression is a singleton, so Wr (1) = 1. Any r-coloring of [r + 1] has two
positions of the same color, and those positions form a two-term progression with positive
difference. An injective coloring of [r] shows that Wr (2) = r + 1. Also, in one color [k]
contains a k-term progression and [k − 1] does not, proving W1 (k) = k.
    It remains to prove the asserted upper bound for k ≥ 3. We induct on k, simultaneously for
all r, with the induction statement that every r-coloring of an interval of F (r, k) consecutive
integers contains a monochromatic k-term progression. The preceding paragraph proves the
cases k = 1, 2. Fix k ≥ 3, assume the statement at length k − 1 for every color count, and fix
r ≥ 1.
