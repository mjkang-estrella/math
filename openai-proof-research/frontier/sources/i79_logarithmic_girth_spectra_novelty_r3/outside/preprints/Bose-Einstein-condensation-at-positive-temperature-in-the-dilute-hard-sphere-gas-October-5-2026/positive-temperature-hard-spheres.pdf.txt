                       in the dilute hard-sphere gas

                                                 Abstract
         We prove Bose–Einstein condensation at positive temperature in the three-dimensional dilute
      hard-sphere gas. For each fixed exclusion distance and every sufficiently small fixed density,
      there is a strictly positive temperature, independent of the volume, at which the exact canonical
      Gibbs state has a positive condensate fraction in the thermodynamic limit. The condensate
      occupies the constant orbital.

1     Introduction
Bose–Einstein condensation is a statement about coherence across a macroscopic sample. For an
interacting gas, local density estimates or accurate thermodynamic energies do not by themselves
give that coherence. The question addressed here is whether a dilute three-dimensional gas with an
impenetrable core has a macroscopically occupied orbital at a positive temperature that stays fixed
as the volume grows.

1.2   Historical context and related methods
The statistical origin of Bose–Einstein condensation is Bose’s counting of light quanta and Einstein’s
extension to material particles [9, 16, 17]. For interacting particles, Penrose and Onsager formulated
condensation through a macroscopic eigenvalue of the one-particle density matrix [37]. In a
translation-invariant gas, the constant orbital is the natural candidate. Bogoliubov’s theory expands
about its macroscopic occupation to obtain the low-energy excitation spectrum [8]; the hard-sphere
calculation of Lee, Huang and Yang predicts the leading energy correction and depletion [27].
Establishing the occupation for the exact equilibrium state is a separate mathematical question
from deriving these expansions.
    For hard spheres, rigorous energy bounds began with Dyson [15], and Lieb and Yngvason obtained
the leading dilute asymptotics [31]. Fournais and Solovej proved the Lee–Huang–Yang lower bound
for interactions including hard cores [21]; Basti, Brooks, Cenatiempo, Olgiati and Schlein proved the
matching upper bound for hard spheres and then for general nonnegative short-range interactions
[4, 5]. At positive temperature, Fournais, Junge, Girardot, Morin, Olivieri and Triay obtained
a low-temperature free-energy lower bound that includes hard cores [19]. These results control
thermodynamic energies. They do not by themselves give a volume-uniform lower bound on a single
orbital’s occupation: the one-particle kinetic gap tends to zero as the box grows.
    Rigorous continuum condensation has been established in several scaling regimes. Lieb and
Seiringer proved complete ground-state condensation in the trapped Gross–Pitaevskii limit [30].
At positive temperature, Deuchert, Seiringer and Yngvason treated a trapped gas in a combined
thermodynamic and Gross–Pitaevskii limit [14], and Deuchert and Seiringer treated the homogeneous
canonical gas in Gross–Pitaevskii scaling [12]. The latter theorem allows hard cores, but their
scattering length is proportional to L/N and therefore vanishes at fixed density as N grows. Further
results of Fournais, Chong, Liang and Nam, and Junge allow increasing box sizes coupled to
increasing dilution [10, 20, 26]. Such coupled limits do not permit unrestricted volume growth
at a fixed positive gas parameter. Deuchert and Seiringer also established positive-temperature
condensation in a combined semiclassical and mean-field limit for a trapped gas [13].
    Fixed-parameter thermodynamic condensation also has precedents with different interactions
and state formulations. Aizenman, Lieb, Seiringer, Solovej and Yngvason proved low-temperature


                                                     3
condensation for a hard-core lattice gas at half filling [2]. Sütő’s continuum theorem assumes
both the potential and its Fourier transform to be nonnegative, with integrability and regularity
hypotheses that exclude a literal hard core [39, Theorem 1.2]. Galanda and Pinamonti construct
algebraic equilibrium states for smooth interactions in coupled weak-interaction limits and, after
renormalization, in Gross–Pitaevskii scaling with vanishing temperature [22, 23]. The present
theorem concerns the exact canonical hard-sphere state with the exclusion distance, positive density,
and positive temperature all fixed before the volume grows. It gives a positive condensate fraction
at sufficiently low temperature; it does not determine the transition temperature or the leading
depletion.
    Two companion articles provide the immediate starting point. Ground-state condensation in the
dilute hard-sphere gas [36] treats the fixed-parameter ground-state problem and develops hard-core
bridge replacements, movable groups of particles, routes avoiding unusable cells, and cancellation
of conditional normalizers. A density-uniform condensate bound for dilute Bose gases [35] treats
bounded finite-range repulsive potentials at positive temperature and supplies localization and
short-slab trajectory estimates, as well as the underlying lattice-path construction. We reproduce the
arguments needed here. The additional issue is to obtain joint spatial and trajectory estimates for
the hard-core canonical Gibbs state, and to make the short-path comparison control its one-particle
density matrix.
    The local analysis builds on established tools. The hard-core ray estimate belongs to the
Dyson local-energy method, and the subdivision and two-particle bounds are related to the work
of Lieb and Yngvason and of Lundholm, Portmann and Solovej [15, 31, 34]. Particle insertion has
antecedents in the nearest-neighbor trial states of Dyson and Lieb, Seiringer and Yngvason [15, 32].
Localization into two particle species uses the Fock-space localization formalism of Dereziński and
Gérard, its many-body development by Lewin, and its thermal application by Deuchert, Seiringer
and Yngvason [11, 14, 28]. Strong subadditivity and monotonicity of relative entropy provide the
entropy comparisons [29, 33]. The use of positive imaginary-time paths goes back to Feynman’s
formulation [18]; Ueltschi’s account distinguishes cycle information from one-particle off-diagonal
order [41]. Here the overlap identity addresses the one-particle occupation directly.
    The long-distance comparison has a separate probabilistic ancestry. Benjamini, Pemantle and
Peres constructed paths with exponential intersection tails [6]; Häggström and Mossel developed a
construction using random biases on blocks at several scales [25]. The finite-endpoint averaging
method of Abbe, Massoulié, Montanari, Sly and Srivastava, and its adaptation by Garban and
Spencer, turn intersection control into long-range information or order [1, 24]. Averaging path
likelihoods before taking their second moments also appears in trail detection and in the point-
reassignment argument of Peres and Sly [3, 7, 38]. Our lattice construction follows the explicit
skeleton in OpenAI [35, Section 8.1] and the obstacle detours in OpenAI [36, Section 7], using
Timár’s boundary-connectivity theorem [40, Theorem 4]. The hard-core comparison retains the
exact interaction normalizers; after averaging two route changes, their cost is controlled by mutual
encounters rather than by the length of either route.

1.3   The argument
The starting point is the short-path comparison developed for the hard-sphere ground state in
OpenAI [36]. Its use at positive temperature requires two changes. First, the local hypotheses must
hold jointly for arbitrarily many prescribed cells under a thermal path measure. Second, the path
comparison must control the one-particle density matrix of a mixed state. We prove the needed
thermal estimates and retain the deterministic comparison mechanism. The bridge, lattice, and
measure comparison arguments used below are included with proofs.

of cells, with a bounded exponential moment for the encounters of two independently drawn routes
in the same environment.

assignments. Removing a different tagged particle from each leaves the same bath midpoints and
the same W ′ , as required by the overlap identity. To compare the resulting measure with the two
original midpoint laws, we average over routes before taking the second moment of its density.
Conditional normalizers cancel away from encounters between two routes, so the cost is controlled
by their encounters rather than by their lengths. The lattice estimate bounds that cost, and a
common-measure inequality gives positive overlap for a positive fraction of all cell pairs.
    The order of the choices is essential. The path duration and the group size are fixed before
the density threshold, and all are fixed before the volume tends to infinity. Section 10 returns to
physical units and verifies directly that the resulting temperature is independent of that volume.

8.2   Finite components and their exterior boundaries
We record the deterministic facts used to move a lattice path around bad vertices. For a finite
∗-connected nonempty set C ⊂ Z3 , let fill(C) consist of C and all finite nearest-neighbor components

any box containing C, nearest-neighbor vertices communicate, and every infinite component meets
that region.

Lemma 8.3 (Exterior shell). The set fill(C) lies in the coordinate bounding box of C. The exterior
vertices at maximum distance one from C form a nearest-neighbor connected set, denoted ∂∗ext C,
with at most 26|C| vertices. If C is a ∗-component of a larger bad set, every vertex of this shell is
good.

Proof. A vertex outside the coordinate box has a coordinate ray to infinity that misses C, so
it is not in a finite complementary component. For the shell’s connectivity we apply Kesten’s
boundary-connectivity theorem in the formulation of Timár [40, arXiv version 2, Theorem 4]:
for a finite ∗-connected subset of Z3 , its exterior ∗-neighbors, accessible from infinity through its
nearest-neighbor complement, are nearest-neighbor connected. These are exactly the vertices in the
shell just defined. There are at most 26 possible ∗-neighbors per vertex of C. A bad shell vertex
would be ∗-adjacent to C and hence belong to the same bad component, which is impossible for a
vertex outside C.

9     A common measure and positive occupation
We now turn the supply of movable labels into a lower bound on the constant-orbital fraction Bocc .
Recall from Proposition 5.1 that the midpoint configuration X and the retained configuration W ′
have density Φ(X, W ′ )2 , where Φ is the nonnegative kernel of the square root of the canonical state.
The input is the conditional path law of Section 7 and the good lattice routes of Proposition 8.1.
Along one route we construct two copies of the selected trajectories. Their midpoint assignments
leave the same observed bath after different tags are removed. We then bound the second moment
of the resulting change of measure by encounters between two routes.

is reproduced below. The thermal modification is to retain W ′ in every observation and in the exposed
data. It is never resampled. All constants obtained here are uniform in D, K and 0 < T ≤ D−1
once the earlier fixed parameters have been chosen.
