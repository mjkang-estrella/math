         Elliptic squares and Zilber–Pink for curves in A2
                                          OpenAI

                                          Abstract
         We prove that every Hodge-generic algebraic curve in A2 over Q contains only
     finitely many points whose abelian surface is isogenous to the square of an elliptic
     curve without complex multiplication (CM). Combining this result with the companion
     E × CM and quaternionic-division finiteness theorems, we prove the curve case of
     Zilber–Pink in A2 over Q, without a boundary hypothesis.

1     Introduction
Let A2 be the coarse moduli variety of principally polarized abelian surfaces. A curve in
A2 is Hodge generic if it is contained in no proper Shimura-special subvariety. The curve
case of the Zilber–Pink conjecture predicts that such a curve meets the union of all special
subvarieties of dimension at most one in only finitely many points. The word “all” includes
every Hecke translate; neither an isogeny degree nor an endomorphism order is fixed.
    We first prove the elliptic-square part of this prediction, then combine it with the two
companion branch theorems to obtain the full conclusion. For a point s ∈ A2 (Q), write
(As , λs ) for its principally polarized abelian surface, and set

         ΣE 2 (C) = s ∈ C(Q) : As ∼ E 2 for an elliptic curve E/Q without CM .
                   {                                                             }


Here ∼ denotes isogeny over Q, without any requirement that it respect the principal
polarization. The condition is equivalent to EndQ (As ) ⊗Z Q ≃ M2 (Q).

Theorem 1.1 (The elliptic-square case). Let C ⊂ A2 be a nonempty reduced irreducible
closed algebraic curve over Q. If C is Hodge generic, then ΣE 2 (C) is finite.

   No condition is imposed on the closure of C at the boundary of a compactification. In
particular, the theorem includes complete curves. The elliptic curve, its isogeny degree
and the principal polarization may all vary.

1.1   The companion branches and the full theorem
The two remaining special-curve loci are treated by the following companion theorems. We
state their exact conclusions to specify the inputs to the full finiteness theorem.

Theorem 1.2 (The two companion branches). For every nonempty reduced irreducible
closed Hodge-generic curve C ⊂ A2 over Q, the following two subsets of C(Q) are finite:
 (A) the points for which As is isogenous over Q to E1 × E2 , with at least one elliptic
     factor having CM;
 (B) the points for which the full algebra EndQ (As ) ⊗Z Q is an indefinite quaternion
     division algebra over Q.
These statements allow all CM fields, endomorphism orders, quaternion algebras, isogeny
degrees and principal polarizations. They impose no boundary, reduction, integrality, height
or field-degree condition.

    In (B), indefiniteness means that the algebra becomes M2 (R) over R; the division
condition excludes M2 (Q). An embedding of a quaternion algebra into a larger endomor-
phism algebra does not satisfy (B). In (A), both-CM products are included. The complete
proofs of these conclusions are given respectively in the companion articles [Ope26b, Theo-
rem 1.1] and [Ope26a, Theorem 1.1]. The proof below keeps their use separate from the
elliptic-square theorem.

1.2    History and the arithmetic step
Zilber’s atypical-intersection principle for semiabelian varieties [Zil02] and Pink’s formu-
lation for mixed Shimura varieties place this problem in a common unlikely-intersection
framework. Pink’s formulation predicts finiteness for a Hodge-generic curve meeting special
subvarieties of codimension at least two [Pin05, Conjecture 1.3]. In the three-dimensional
space A2 , the special curves have three generic types: elliptic products with a CM factor,
non-CM elliptic squares, and quaternion-division multiplication. Pila and Tsimerman
established André–Oort for A2 , which supplies the special-point case [PT13, Theorem 1.1].
Daw and Orr developed the corresponding unlikely-intersection arguments for these curve
loci [DO21, DO22].
    The arithmetic-to-geometric strategy compares lower bounds coming from Galois
conjugates with upper bounds for rational points in definable sets. Pila and Zannier
introduced this strategy in their proof of Manin–Mumford [PZ08], using the counting


                                             3
theorem of Pila and Wilkie [PW06]. In Siegel moduli, the arithmetic difficulty is to
obtain a sufficiently large orbit uniformly as the special subvariety varies. Daw and Orr’s
quantitative reduction theory supplies the geometric and counting implication for the
elliptic-square and quaternionic loci. Their unconditional quaternionic theorem assumes
that the curve meets the zero-dimensional Baily–Borel boundary [DO22, Theorem 1.4];
the orbit-to-finiteness implication we use has no such hypothesis.
    Daw and Orr subsequently proved the full special-curve finiteness conclusion, including
elliptic squares, under the same total multiplicative-degeneration hypothesis [DO25a,
Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 1.2]. Papas studied splitting points using periods centered at
an interior E × CM or elliptic-square point. For an everywhere potentially-good center, his
height bound depends polynomially on the point degree and the number of supersingular
places of proximity; his finiteness corollary fixes an upper bound for that number [Pap25,
Theorem 1.5 and Corollary 1.6]. His analogous bad-reduction statements are conditional
on comparison conjectures [Pap25, Theorem 1.8 and Corollary 1.9]. In the product Y (1)3
of three modular curves, Daw, Orr and Papas obtain finiteness from an interior basepoint,
allowing bad reduction but restricting the growth of the number of supersingular places
of proximity [DOP26, Theorems 1.6 and 1.8]. The present height estimate permits bad
reduction of the center and does not bound that number of places.
    For the elliptic-square locus, the implication we use is Daw–Orr’s [DO22, Theorem 1.3]:
a suitable polynomial lower bound for Galois orbits in terms of the endomorphism-order
discriminant implies finiteness. Their arithmetic hypothesis is stated in [DO22, Conjec-
ture 6.2]. We prove the required bound for the entire elliptic-square locus before applying
that theorem. The constants may depend on C; effectivity is not required.
    The main arithmetic result is a polynomial height bound for selected lifts t to one
fixed fine-level curve. Write d = max{2, [K(t) : K]}, where K contains the fixed data, and
let h(t) ≥ 2 be a shifted ample logarithmic height. If s is the coarse image of t, let Δs
be the discriminant of the full geometric order EndQ (As ), using the trace of its regular
representation. The two arithmetic estimates are

     Several classical inputs make this reduction quantitative. André’s normality theorem
for connected algebraic monodromy identifies the monodromy group of our Hodge-generic
family [And92, §5, Theorem 1]. Strong approximation then permits independent markings
at two auxiliary levels [MVW84]. Deligne’s regularity theorem and Schmid’s Hodge-
norm estimates control fixed algebraic frames near the punctures [Del70, Sch73], while
rational crystalline comparison identifies horizontal transport at nearby good reductions
[BO83, Ber82]. The interpolation argument uses effective polynomial ideal membership
and arithmetic intersection bounds [Her26, Dub90, Asc04, KPS01]; its graph construction
is what turns those bounds into the height estimate needed here.
     The use of arithmetic auxiliary functions and period equations belongs to the tradition
of Bombieri’s and André’s work on G-functions [Bom81, And89]. For curves in products

simultaneously at all nonarchimedean places, obtained from Tate uniformization [DO25b,

1.3   The proof mechanism
Suppose the square locus is infinite, and fix one of its points t0 on a smooth fine-level cover.
This interior point will be the center of our local equations. On first homology, put

                  P = {D : D† = D, Tr(D) = 0},            ⟨D, E⟩ = Tr(DE),

where † is the adjoint for the polarization. This is a rank-five quadratic system. At a
non-CM elliptic square, its actual rational endomorphisms form a positive plane. Choose
labeled integral pairs B = (B1 , B2 ) and P = (P1 , P2 ) spanning the center and target planes,
with the target pair chosen using the endomorphism estimate. Let a = x(t) for a local
parameter x vanishing at t0 , and let Y (a) be horizontal back-transport in a fixed de Rham
frame from the target to the center, with Y (0) = 1. We study the four cross pairings

The residue characteristic no longer occurs in this equation. Grouping places by the two
labeled conjugate tuples gives only polynomially many systems in d, even when the number
of proximity primes is unbounded. At the archimedean and finitely many exceptional
places we instead use a single equation ⟨B, Y (a)P⟩ = r with a rational matrix r.
    Two fixed prime levels make these equations usable. At the first, ℓ1 , the separately
saturated center and target plane lattices are marked so that their reductions span a
degenerate four-space; at the second, ℓ2 , their sum is nondegenerate. Both conditions can
be imposed on one connected finite cover. The second incidence excludes sufficiently close
targets at a place where the center is not potentially good: both planes would preserve the
same toric two-space, forcing their sum to be degenerate. Thus every retained finite place
admits the good-reduction comparison just described.
    The first incidence serves a different purpose. Full monodromy rules out identities
among the equations unless the target and its conjugate lie on one of a fixed finite list of
polarized Hecke correspondences. On that list, isogenies of fixed multiplier transport the
primed pairs back to the unprimed fibers. Monodromy then forces any identity to identify
the transported pairs with the original pairs by a scalar on the center pair and its reciprocal
on the target pair. The center norms force that scalar to be +1 or −1. The positive sign
would make a rational square equal to p/m, where p is the residue characteristic and m is a
fixed isogeny multiplier prime to p. The negative sign contradicts the degenerate incidence
at ℓ1 . The Hecke list and its multipliers are fixed before choosing these levels, so ℓ1 can
avoid all those multipliers.

Here the weights are the normalized absolute-height weights. The center divisor supplies
the large proximity sum; integral lattices preserved by endomorphisms and bounded center
frames supply the much smaller coordinate sum. These estimates hold before any bound
for h in terms of d is known.
    Theorem 2.1 turns this imbalance, together with the nonidentity just explained, into
h ≤ CdC . Its proof constructs a polynomial whose degree is small compared with its
order of local smallness. The product formula either bounds h or forces the target into a
proper algebraic subvariety. The process repeats in that subvariety, which explains why
nonidentity must hold on boundary branches of arbitrary algebraic varieties through the
target, even when their operator coordinates are not Hodge classes. Section 2 explains how
a graph construction makes this dimension descent quantitative.

the second incidence, the integral coordinate bounds and the nonidentity analysis for split
endomorphism algebras, are proved below. The interpolation theorem and the separation
of bad-center disks from good-reduction comparisons are the parts of the construction that
may be useful in other height problems.

1.4    Conventions and organization
All heights are absolute logarithmic heights. Place sums use normalized absolute-height
weights, so extending a field preserves the sum for pulled-back quantities. Constants may
depend on the curve, the center, fixed levels, frames and other fixed data. They never
depend on the varying target, its height or degree, or a varying residue characteristic. In
an estimate described as polynomial, the exponent is fixed.
    We use covariant first homology and its de Rham and prime-to-residue- characteristic
étale realizations. The term ordinary point for a differential equation means a point where
the connection has no singularity; it places no ordinarity condition on the reduction of
an abelian variety. Every reduction of an endomorphism plane uses its saturation in the
specified integral lattice.
    Section 2 states the interpolation theorem and explains its proof mechanism. Section 3
constructs the geometric systems and controls the exceptional Hecke correspondences.
Section 4 gives the two incidences and excludes bad-center disks. Section 5 proves the
coordinate estimates, and Section 6 constructs the local equations with their quantitative
bounds. Section 7 rules out identities and obtains the height bound. Finally, Section 8
proves Theorems 1.1 and 1.3 and the special-closure corollary. The complete proof of the
interpolation theorem is given in Appendix A.


2     Interpolation with a common small parameter

differential systems. Its hypotheses distinguish the global height of the target from the
much smaller positive coordinate logarithms at the places used for interpolation. We state
the theorem and explain the graph construction that exploits this distinction here. The
full proof is given in Appendix A.

3     The geometric systems and a finite list of correspondences
We first fix the curves, the family, and an elliptic-square center. The trace-free self-adjoint
endomorphisms form a five-dimensional orthogonal system. Its monodromy gives an
alternative for two varying points: the two systems have product monodromy, or their joint
image lies in a polarized isogeny correspondence. The final result of this section reduces
the latter alternative to a fixed finite list. This list is chosen before the auxiliary levels of
Proposition 4.2.

4.1   Finite-field positions of two planes

Lemma 4.1. There is an absolute constant q0 with the following property. Let k be a
finite field of odd cardinality q > q0 , let H0 ⊂ Vk be a nondegenerate plane, and let H ⊂ Vk

Bruhat cell of the split group Sp4 has coordinates A4 × G2m × A4 : multiply the four negative

entries of bounded degrees in these ten coordinates, independently of k. The same assertion
holds for a big cell of a split parabolic, using its Levi root subgroups and unipotent radical.
Conjugating a parabolic or inserting a fixed group element changes coefficients and does
not change these degree bounds.

representation can be tested on either chart by a polynomial of degree at most an absolute
constant D. If the condition holds at some geometric point, it holds on the corresponding

minor that is not identically zero, clear its torus denominators, and multiply by the torus
coordinates. The resulting nonzero polynomial has a degree bounded by another absolute
constant. A nonzero polynomial of total degree less than q cannot vanish on all of k n . This
follows by induction on n, applying the univariate root bound to its nonzero coefficients.
Thus every such geometrically nonempty open condition has a k-point once q > q0 , for one

4.3    A common toric subspace in a strict local model

8.4   The special-curve classification
The passage from the three branch statements to the full conjecture requires a classification
of the special curves, including their Hecke translates. We record the precise consequence

Proposition 8.3 (The three special-curve types). Let Z ⊂ A2 be a special curve and let
s ∈ Z(Q) be Hodge generic on Z. Exactly one of the following holds:
   (i) As is isogenous to E × F , where E is non-CM and F is CM;
  (ii) As is isogenous to E 2 , where E is non-CM;
 (iii) the full algebra End0 (As ) is an indefinite quaternion division algebra over Q.
Every point of a special curve that is not Hodge generic on it is a special point.

Proof. The Hodge group is the smallest connected Q-algebraic subgroup whose real points
contain the Hodge circle; adjoining the weight homotheties gives the Mumford–Tate group.
At a Hodge-generic point of a special subvariety, its associated Hermitian domain has
the dimension of that subvariety. We apply this with dimension one, retaining the actual
rational Hodge structure and polarization of As .
     Suppose first that As is simple up to isogeny. The surface classification in [MZ99,
§2(2.2)] gives four possibilities for its full rational endomorphism algebra: Q, a real
quadratic field, an indefinite quaternion division algebra, or a quartic CM field. The
corresponding Hodge groups are respectively Sp4 , a restriction of scalars of SL2 from the
real quadratic field, the norm-one quaternion group, and a torus. Their Hermitian domains
have dimensions 3, 2, 1, 0: respectively the genus-two Siegel space, two copies of the upper
half-plane, one upper half-plane, and a point. Thus only the quaternionic case can occur
on a special curve at a Hodge-generic point. This gives (iii) as an equality of the full
endomorphism algebra.
     If As is not simple, Poincaré reducibility gives an isogeny As ∼ E1 × E2 . When the
two elliptic factors are isogenous, their product has the same Hodge group as either factor
[MZ99, §1(1.2)]. For a non-CM factor this group is SL2 , with one-dimensional domain,
whereas a CM factor has a torus Hodge group and a zero-dimensional domain. This gives
(ii). When the factors are nonisogenous, their Hodge group is the product of the two
elliptic Hodge groups [MZ99, Corollary (3.9)]. Each non-CM factor contributes one domain
dimension and each CM factor contributes zero. Dimension one therefore means precisely
one CM factor, giving (i). The alternatives are disjoint, either by simplicity or by the full
endomorphism algebras Q × F and M2 (Q).
     These assertions are invariant under rational Hodge isomorphism, so they apply to
every polarization and Hecke translate. The elliptic factors and isogenies at an algebraic
point can be defined over Q, by the descent explained in Proposition 8.2. Finally the
special closure of any point on Z is a special subvariety contained in Z. If it is proper, it
has dimension zero and is a special point. This proves the last assertion.

   If this polynomial height bound does not hold, we have proved P (z ′ ) = 0. Substitute
q = F/am and clear denominators:

                               H(a, U ) = amL P (a, U, F (a, U )/am ).

This is a polynomial. It vanishes at z because a(z) ̸= 0, and it is not identically zero on
W , because P is nonzero on the irreducible graph closure and its graph over a ̸= 0 is
dense. Its degree is at most mL + L max(1, deg F ); expanding the substitution bounds
its coefficient height by haff (P ) + Lhaff (F ) plus a polynomial degree error. Thus it has
exactly the controls (A.10). Choose an irreducible component containing z of the proper
intersection W ∩ {H = 0}. By Lemma A.1 its defining equations have the same types of
degree and height bounds, and its dimension is strictly smaller.
    Repeat with this component as W . Condition (iv) applies to it because it is an
irreducible algebraic variety containing the same target. After at most N drops one either
has obtained the polynomial height bound or reaches the empty-boundary case, which
gives that bound. There are finitely many fixed-step polynomial bounds in this procedure,
so their maximum is bounded by CdC for a constant depending only on the fixed data.
This completes the proof.
