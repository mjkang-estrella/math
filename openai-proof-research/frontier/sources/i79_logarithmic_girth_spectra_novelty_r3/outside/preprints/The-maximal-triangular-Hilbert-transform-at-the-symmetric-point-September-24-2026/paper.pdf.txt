          For arbitrary complex inputs in L3 (ℝ2 ), we prove the pointwise maximal L3 × L3 →
       3/2

1.1   Previous work
The flat operator appears in Demeter and Thiele’s discussion of bilinear averages for two
commuting transformations [3, Section 6]. The three inputs of the scalar form depend on the
three pairs of variables, making it a basic example of an entangled singular integral. Kovač,
Thiele and Zorin-Kranich proved estimates for a Walsh model with one structurally restricted
input [13, Theorem 1.7].
    Tao proved sublogarithmic cancellation for one-dimensional multilinear Hilbert trans-
forms [17, Theorem 1.2]. Zorin-Kranich extended this cancellation to simplex Hilbert trans-
forms [18, Theorem 1.3]. Square-function estimates of Durcik, Kovač, Škreb and Thiele gave
square-root cancellation for the triangular form across finitely many smooth scales [5, Corol-
lary 5]. Durcik, Kovač and Thiele proved power-type cancellation
                                                              √      with hard truncations [6,
Theorem 1]; at the symmetric tuple the bound grows like log(R/ε), by permutation and
interpolation of their (4, 4, 2) estimates. These finite-scale bounds retain a dependence on
the scale range.


                                                   2
     Bounds for averages over direction parameters were obtained by Durcik and Roos [7,
Theorem 1] and, for rough angular weights, by Lin and Slavíková [16, Theorem 1.1]. For
curved translations, Christ, Durcik and Roos proved bounds along (t, t2 ) [1, Theorem 1].
Hsu and Lin treat more general curves under geometric hypotheses excluding the flat curve
(t, t) [10, Theorem 1.37]. The curvature in these results provides an additional source of
cancellation that is unavailable for the flat curve.
     Our argument uses a nonnegative matrix energy and Gaussian heat flow. Energy telescop-
ing for entangled forms appears in Kovač’s twisted-paraproduct argument [12, Lemma 2] and
his Bellman-function framework [11, Section 2]. Continuous Gaussian telescoping appears in
Durcik [4, Lemma 3 and Section 3]. These methods concern bipartite forms; Kovač discusses
the obstacle posed by the triangular cycle in [11, Section 6.2]. The cyclic trace representation
itself also occurs in [13, Equation (1.5)]. We prove a dimension-independent mixed trace
inequality and compare the energy derivatives at neighboring vertices. The key additional
estimate controls the total change in energy when individual matrix entries are switched
on and off at independently chosen scales. This permits the pointwise maximum without a
factor depending on the number of scales or the matrix dimensions.

1.2   Proof overview
The change of variables x = −a, y = −b, t = a + b + c writes the bilinear output in terms of

                    G1 (b, c) = F (b + c, −b),       G2 (c, a) = G(−a, c + a).

Both pair maps preserve L3 norms. We first sample these inputs, together with a dual
input G0 (a, b), on a finite mesh. Multiplying entries by Gaussian weights gives maps
Wv : Hv+1 → Hv between finite-dimensional complex Hilbert spaces, indexed by v ∈ ℤ/3ℤ.
The three vertex spaces correspond to the sampled a-, b- and c-coordinates. Thus the two
indices of the dual array record the output position (x, y) = (−a, −b). At a vertex, combine
its two incident maps into Rv = [ Wv Wv−1  ∗ ] and use the energy tr((R∗ R )3/2 ).
                                                                          v v
    Sections 2 and 3 prove the matrix inequalities. Section 4 then integrates the sum of the
three energies over Gaussian centers satisfying p0 + p1 + p2 = 0. For fixed arrays, its decrease
as the variance grows controls the quadratic costs needed to estimate a cyclic trace with
three odd Gaussian windows.
    To select different truncations at different output points, the dual array entry at (i, j)
must be present only between its own two scales. The energy is therefore decreasing between
finitely many switching scales and may jump at those scales. Section 5 bounds the sum of
the absolute jumps by the discrete L3 masses of the three arrays. The underlying matrix fact
is that the derivative with respect to an entry is controlled by the product of its row and
column norms. After Gaussian integration, one-dimensional maximal functions bound these
products uniformly in the switching scales.
    Section 6 uses this bound to linearize any finite maximum of smooth truncations. Mesh
limits and rational exhaustion give the full smooth maximum. A pointwise comparison of its
kernel with the hard Hilbert kernel completes the estimate for compactly supported smooth
inputs. Finally, Section 7 uses density and a vanishing tail-oscillation argument to prove
Theorem 1.1, the joint principal values, and the scalar equivalence.

5     Output-dependent truncations and energy jumps
We now allow each entry of one edge array to have its own truncation interval. Between

every entry switches at most twice. We first establish two inputs to this bound: estimates
for Gaussian averages that are uniform in the scale, and a formula for changes in the matrix
energy that remains valid when the rank changes.
