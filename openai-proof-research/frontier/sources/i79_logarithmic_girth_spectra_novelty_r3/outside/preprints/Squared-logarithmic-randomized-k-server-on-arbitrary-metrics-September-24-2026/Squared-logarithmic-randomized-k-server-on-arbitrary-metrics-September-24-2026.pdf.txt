                        on arbitrary metrics

                                              Abstract
          We prove that randomized k-server has competitive ratio O((log(k + 1))2 ) on every
      metric space against oblivious request sequences, matching the known worst-case lower
      bound. For each metric and initial configuration, one policy works for all finite request
      sequences, including on infinite and unbounded spaces. When the initial server positions
      are distinct, no additive term is needed.

1    Introduction
The k-server problem asks how to move k servers in a metric space when requests arrive one
at a time. A request is a point that must be occupied by a server; the cost is the distance moved.
The offline optimum knows all future requests, whereas an online policy must decide from
the revealed prefix. We determine the sharp worst-case order of the randomized competitive
ratio.
    More precisely, let ( X, d) be a metric space and let s = (s1 , . . . , sk ) ∈ X k be the initial
positions of k labeled servers. Repeated positions are allowed. To serve rt , a policy chooses
one server and moves it to rt , leaving all others fixed. A server already at rt can be chosen
at zero cost. For a finite request sequence σ, let cost A,s (σ ) be a policy’s total movement and
let OPTX,d,k,s (σ ) be the minimum such cost with full knowledge of σ. Requests are fixed
independently of the policy’s random choices, the usual oblivious-adversary model.
Theorem 1.1. There is an absolute constant C < ∞ such that, for every integer k ≥ 2, every metric
space ( X, d) with at least k + 1 points, and every s ∈ X k , there exist a randomized online policy A
and a finite B ≥ 0 satisfying

for every finite request sequence σ. The constant C is independent of every property of the metric. The
number B may depend on ( X, d), k, s, but not on the sequence or its length. The same policy serves all
finite sequences and horizons. If the initial positions are distinct, one may take B = 0.

1.1   Context and previous work
Manasse, McGeoch, and Sleator introduced the k-server problem as a common framework for
online service problems [25, 26]. They proved the deterministic lower bound k on every metric
with more than k points and conjectured that it is attainable. Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou
established the (2k − 1)-competitive work function algorithm [20]. A recent preprint of
Coester, Koutsoupias, and Zbysiński announces a k-competitive analysis of that algorithm [13].
The present paper concerns the different asymptotic behavior made possible by randomization
against oblivious requests.
    Paging, the case of a uniform metric, gave the basic reason to expect such an improvement.
Fiat, Karp, Luby, McGeoch, Sleator, and Young proved a harmonic lower bound and a 2Hk -
competitive randomized marking algorithm [16]; McGeoch and Sleator attained the exact
bound Hk = ∑ik=1 i−1 [27]. For weighted paging, Bansal, Buchbinder, and Naor obtained the
optimal order O(log k ) through a fractional primal-dual algorithm and online rounding [3].
These results formed part of the evidence for the folklore randomized k-server conjecture,
which predicted O(log k ) on every metric.
    That conjecture was disproved by Bubeck, Coester, and Rabani [10, Theorem 11 and
Proposition 8]: there exist (k + 1)-point metrics requiring competitive ratio Ω(log2 k ), even
allowing a fixed additive constant. Their result must be distinguished from a lower bound on
every metric. The latter developed through the decomposition and metric Ramsey methods
of Karloff, Rabani, and Ravid [18], Blum, Karloff, Rabani, and Saks [8], Bartal, Bollobás, and
Mendel [6], and Bartal, Linial, Mendel, and Naor [7]; Bubeck, Coester, and Rabani ultimately
obtained the sharp universal order Ω(log k ) [10, Corollary 20]. Theorem 1.1 matches the
larger, worst-case lower bound. It establishes the sharp squared-logarithmic order after the
negative resolution of the original logarithmic conjecture.
    The main upper-bound approach passes through hierarchically separated trees. Bartal
introduced probabilistic approximation by these metrics [4, 5], and Fakcharoenphol, Rao, and
Talwar obtained expected distortion O(log n) for n-point metrics [15]. Recursive allocation
methods were developed by Coté, Meyerson, and Poplawski [14] and by Bansal, Buchbinder,
and Naor [2]. Bansal, Buchbinder, M ˛    adry, and Naor then gave the first polylogarithmic
bound for arbitrary finite metrics, O(log2 k log3 n log log n) [1]. Bubeck, Cohen, Lee, Lee, and
M˛ adry achieved O(log2 k ) on HSTs using multiscale entropic regularization [11]. The static
embedding reduction yields O(log2 k log n) on general finite metrics. Its dependence on n
does not give a bound in k alone. Coester and Cosson recently made the HST and finite-metric
bounds polynomial-time, using a total number of random bits independent of the request
length [12, Theorem 1 and Corollary 2]. Their general finite-metric ratio retains the factor
log n.
    Changing the embedding as requests arrive is also an established direction. The dynamic
construction of [11, Section 4] gives O(log3 k log(1 + ∆)) on finite metrics, where ∆ is the
ratio of the largest distance to the smallest positive distance. Lee’s evolving, fusible HST
framework used heavy-ball nets to merge clusters near concentrated mass and introduced
a fission potential intended to pay for later splits [24, Sections 3.1.2–3.2 and 5.3]. Its general
competitive-ratio claim was withdrawn after a gap in Section 5.3.2 [23]. Heavy-ball protection
and accounting for changing partitions are methodological antecedents of our construction.
Here the partition schedule is driven by the posterior measure of a hidden comparator; it
produces a fixed tree of label strings. We prove the required edit and retained-anchor travel
estimates in Sections 7 and 8, without invoking a theorem from the withdrawn argument.

1.2   Main ingredients
We first establish a finite distributional bound. Given a known law of requests, select one
offline optimum for each input and use only its posterior distributions conditional on the
revealed prefix. This does not reveal the realized future.
    The construction starts with partitions of the finite metric at several distance scales. A
point’s complete tuple of cell labels identifies a leaf of a fixed tree. The hidden optimum
then gives posterior distributions of the server counts below each tree vertex. We use those
distributions to allocate fractional server mass on the tree, represent that mass by points
of the original metric, and round it to exactly k servers. Changes of the partitions and of
the representatives must be charged along with movement of mass through the tree. One
logarithm comes from the partition estimate and one from the allocation estimate; rounding
and realization preserve their product.
    For exposition we prove the reusable allocation theorem first, then construct its partition
input. The four components are as follows.

Allocation from subtree count distributions. On a fixed separated tree, we maintain frac-
tional subtree amounts with movement O(log(k + 1)) times that of the hidden comparator.
The amounts dominate the serving requirement and are bounded above by a constant multiple
of the posterior expected counts. They may park at internal vertices, keeping unused mass
above children while their posterior proportions change. The proof uses convex rank func-
tions and slowly varying proportions. A separate rule for a nearly dominant child chooses the
allocations to the other children first and gives that child the remaining parent amount, subject
to its cap. This fits all children within their rank bounds without an additional logarithm. The
resulting allocation theorem applies to general observed hidden trajectories, independently of
the later metric embedding.

A compact variational potential. The partition analysis uses a finite-dimensional minimum
over separated auxiliary sites with positive-part capacity constraints. Concentration forces a
nearly full site near the request. A feasible relocation of that site makes the optimal integrand
have small positive slope there. Ball-mass ratios and inner moments then telescope over
scales, giving a single logarithm in the measure-motion budget. All geometric radii in this
argument are fixed numerical multiples of the scale.

Partitions adapted to local mass ratios. Heavy balls protect highly concentrated requests.
Other requests use chronological rosters in doubling qualification tiers. Their first-hit loss
depends on a local mass ratio rather than on one worst-case logarithm at every level. Ran-
dom retirements keep the rosters bounded; a separate short-lived auxiliary roster pays the
retirement errors. The resulting finite label sets are fixed independently of the request horizon.

Moving representatives and rounding. Fractional mass parked at a tree vertex is repre-
sented by an anchor in the original metric. Retained anchors can move even when no mass
crosses a tree edge. A normalized affine potential and a pointwise sum of parked-mass
coefficients pay this travel without multiplying the tree movement by another logarithm. A


                                                4
dynamic balanced-rounding lemma, allowing correlated parent and child states, incurs only
an absolute loss.
    These components give two logarithms in total. The final step removes the finite-metric
additive term by independent episodes and reset blocks, then uses finite-dimensional separa-
tion and compactness. This yields a single policy on all finite inputs of an arbitrary metric,
including unbounded spaces. A matching simulation handles repeated initial positions.

Section 2 fixes the probabilistic conventions and the movement accounting. Sections 3, 4,

2     Finite laws, posterior motion, and the proof architecture
We first allow the policy to know a finite input law. This permits conditional distributions of
an offline optimum, while still forbidding the policy from observing the realized future. The
estimate obtained in this setting must have an additive term uniform in the law and horizon.
Section 10 uses precisely this uniformity to construct one policy on an arbitrary metric.

2.2   Hidden comparators and the two parts of a posterior update
Fix the data of Proposition 2.2. For each input, choose one optimum trajectory of the k labeled
servers, resolving ties by a fixed order. Such a choice exists: each request has only k possible
server labels, so a length-H input has only k H candidate trajectories. The chosen optimum is a
hidden random variable determined by the input. Set

                                              Q = E OPT .

The policy uses conditional laws of this trajectory, not its hidden realization.
    Let Ft contain the request prefix through time t and the independent random tape used
to construct the partitions. It is convenient to include that entire tape from time zero. The
rounding procedure will use a separate independent tape that is excluded from Ft . Thus
conditioning on Ft does not alter the true-metric comparator posterior beyond conditioning
on the request prefix.

Here L denotes a probability law, not the pilot potential introduced later. During filtering, an

    All signed filtering estimates are applied over every step, with old parameters held fixed,
before selecting resets or partition edits. In particular, no argument conditions a martingale
estimate on the later event that a reset is necessary.
    For probability estimates concerning random radii or retirements, we use a different
conditioning: fix the true input and its comparator trajectory, and average over the partition
tape. The relevant insertion schedules will then be deterministic. This does not assert
independence after conditioning on the entire tape in Ft .
    At a fixed finite horizon all relevant random outcomes can be taken finite. A radius draw
need only specify which subset of the finite space is its ball; a roster lifetime has a finite
maximum age. Only finitely many insertions can occur. More explicitly, preallocate at most H
insertion slots per level and structure, discretize each radius at all pairwise distances in Y,
and preallocate each entry’s truncated lifetime. This gives a finite-valued relevant tape, even
before the centers of the inserted balls are known. Any unused portion of an ambient infinite
tape is independent and can be ignored; all fractional states and selected rounding kernels
below depend only on this finite projection and the request prefix. Realizing a kernel also
uses the current rounded state and the independent rounding randomness. Conditional laws,
minimizing choices, and randomized rounding kernels can therefore be specified history by
history. Arbitrary continuations are assigned to zero-probability histories.

5.4   Uniform initialization and the tree sum
We record why the finite constants just used are uniform over laws and horizons. For a fixed
star there are finitely many rank trackers, namely k ranks per child and the ranks used at the
parent. Each elementary reference potential is p(1 − p) + ( p − p◦ )2 with p, p◦ ∈ [0, 1], and
hence has a fixed bounded range. Its tracking tolerance is fixed once k is fixed. This bounds
all initial and terminal reference allowances uniformly over the posteriors. Superadditivity
gives ∑i Mi ≤ k; active size totals and core counts are at most Ck, and D ≤ Ck. The coarse
refresh accounting thus has a uniform finite initialization allowance as well.

terminal values therefore have a uniform finite lower bound, as required for their telescopes.
The switch potential is nonnegative and bounded by Ck. These bounds use finitely many
fixed tracker coordinates and one pair of endpoints for each telescope, never the number of
histories, requests, or epochs. Wholesale reinitializations are paid jumps within those same
telescopes. Trackers remain attached to the fixed vertices even while their coordinates are

Lemma 7.3 (Local age count). In a subset of Y of diameter at most 20r, there are at most A
chronological entries of age less than the positive integer A. In particular the bounds are K and K2 at
the two age cutoffs, regardless of how many entries have already retired.

Proof. If there were n > A such entries, the earliest of them would have at least n − 1 ≥ A
later insertions within 20r of its center, contradicting its age. Repeated centers cause no
exception.

   When a request qualifies, its final tier covers x deterministically. Indeed an insertion
supplies an age-zero center at x; if there is no insertion, the test supplies an entry of age less
than K within r/2, and no ages change. That entry is present and its radius is at least r. We
shall refer to this as the tier’s guaranteed coverage.

Lemma 7.4 (Fixed labels and anchors). The construction can use fixed finite label pools at each
level, independent of the input law and horizon. Labels can be assigned so that distinct current records
have distinct labels, including shadowed records, and a freshly chosen label was absent throughout the
preceding step and all earlier intermediate states of the current step. The sole exception is the prescribed
heavy-ball reuse. Every point with a given current label is within 20r of its anchor; its cell has diameter
at most 40r. A label common to consecutive maps has the same anchor or is a reused heavy label with
anchor displacement at most 40r.

Proof. Write n = |Y |. Applying Lemma 7.3 to each singleton shows that a tier has at most
M = nK2 chronological records of age less than K2 . Reserve a record’s label until that age
cutoff, even if its actual lifetime has ended or its ball is completely shadowed by earlier
structures. This convention bounds all reserved labels by M; it does not alter the partition.
During a step at most one new record is inserted. The records present at any intermediate
time during two consecutive steps belong to their two starting record sets and at most two
new records, so their total number is at most 2M + 2. A pool of 4M + 4 slots suffices to choose
a fresh slot outside this entire set whenever needed. A slot released on the previous step is
still excluded on the current step. This is a finite implementation of the stated cooldown rule,
not a growing list of all historical labels.
     The heavy structure has at most n centers and one insertion per step, so a separate pool of
4n + 4 slots works by the same rule, with reuse allowed precisely for the unique intersected
ball. Use disjoint pools for different tiers and the heavy structure, and n additional fixed
singleton labels. Pool sizes are chosen for every level in advance and do not depend on the


                                                    43
realized center schedule. A chronological record that persists keeps its label and center even
while shadowed. Thus any common slot can only change its anchor by the explicit heavy
reuse.
    Heavy and tier keys are anchored at their centers, and singleton keys at their points. Every
key cell is a subset of its defining ball, whose radius is at most 20r; restricting by precedence
only shrinks it. The diameter and anchor bounds follow. Heavy precedence further implies
that a heavy key’s region is its entire ball. Empty key regions cause no difficulty; their reserved

9.2   The joint rounding process
We apply Lemma 9.1 in a fixed order from the root toward the leaves. At an internal vertex
v, the coordinates of its star are its ordinary children together with one extra coordinate for
its park. The target vector is (qi )i child of v together with p◦v , whose sum is qv . The prescribed
integer total is the already generated nv . At a leaf simply set cv = nv .
    At time zero, put nroot = k and use the initialization assertion successively at every internal
vertex. The resulting conditional means are those in (9.1), by induction over the fixed order.
Consistency holds in every outcome, so summing all parks gives exactly k. No movement is
charged for this choice of the initial simulated configuration; its mismatch from the actual
starting tuple is charged once below.
    Consider an update from t − 1 to t. The old rounding variables are functions of Ft−1 and
the independent rounding tape. The fractional algorithm and the requests do not depend
on that tape. Therefore revealing Ft preserves their conditional distribution from Ft−1 ; in
particular,

                E[ n v ( t − 1) | F t ] = q v ( t − 1),        E[cv (t − 1) | Ft ] = p◦v (t − 1).   (9.6)

Formally, if U denotes the independent rounding tape and Z = g(Ft−1 , U ) is one of these
variables, its conditional law given Ft is obtained by integrating g over the unchanged law of
U. Its dependence on the observed data is already fixed by Ft−1 ⊆ Ft . This also shows why
the additional rounding tape must not affect any of the fractional observations.
    Condition now on a fractional history through time t. At a star v, use the joint law of its
old child and parking counts and its newly generated parent count nv (t). This is a finite law
determined by the known past kernels and the kernels already used above v at this step. The
parent count has conditional mean qv (t) by the top-down induction, while (9.6) supplies the
old coordinate means. The lemma therefore applies even though this newly generated parent
can be correlated with all the old local counts. Select a minimizing finite kernel and sample it
at the actual state encountered. Subsequent stars do not change counts already assigned, so
the induction gives all of (9.1) jointly.
    This description defines causal kernels, rather than merely unrelated couplings at separate
times. Each kernel uses the joint law conditional on the fractional history, then acts on the
realized local state and parent total. It need not impose its marginal targets separately on
the events specifying individual ancestor roundings. At a fixed finite horizon there are only
finitely many relevant request and partition outcomes: on Y, a radius is needed only through
the finite ball it defines, and only finitely many insertion and retirement draws are used. We
can therefore choose one minimizing kernel at each relevant history without a measurable-
selection issue. Choices at histories of probability zero can be arbitrary balanced continuations.
The distributional policy is allowed to depend on the given law and horizon at this stage.

Choose p in this intersection. No bound on the diameter or cardinality of X enters this
argument. Boundedness is needed only for the cost of one fixed finite input, which was
established before Equation (10.14).
    It remains to realize these choices as one randomized online policy. At each request
use the current history h and choose the next label with probabilities ph (1), . . . , ph (k ). For
example, take independent uniform random numbers in [0, 1) and choose the first label whose
cumulative probability exceeds the next number. Only the revealed request prefix and past
labels enter this rule. One can also implement all times on the single probability space [0, 1)
with Lebesgue measure: subdivide its current interval into half-open pieces with relative
lengths ph (1), . . . , ph (k ), choose the piece containing the initial uniform point, and continue
within that piece. Pieces of length zero are empty. On a fixed finite input, the set of seeds
giving any specified label prefix is an interval whose length is the product of the corresponding
probabilities. Therefore each label choice and the finite total cost are measurable, and their
expectation is precisely (10.14).
    In particular, the policy is defined for every history before any input or horizon is chosen.
Measurability here is exactly that required for each fixed oblivious input; no probability
distribution on the request space, or joint measurability in a random request variable, enters
the construction. The inequalities already proved for p give (10.13).
