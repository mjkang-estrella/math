1    Introduction
The Zariski cancellation problem asks whether an affine variety is determined by its cylinder
when that cylinder is affine space. Over the complex numbers, its algebraic formulation is the
implication
                                A[w] ≃ C[n+1] =⇒ A ≃ C[n] ,                              (1.1)
where A is a finitely generated commutative unital complex algebra, w is an independent
polynomial variable, and all isomorphisms fix the complex scalars. Here C [r] denotes a polynomial
ring in r variables over a ring C. The hypothesis says that adjoining one variable turns A into a
polynomial ring; the question is whether that variable can be cancelled. An isomorphism of
cylinders need not respect the given projection to the affine line, so its existence alone does not
identify polynomial coordinates on A.
    Cancellation for the affine line follows from the work of Abhyankar, Heinzer, and Eakin [1,
Corollary (2.8)]. Cancellation for the complex affine plane was established through the work of
Fujita, Miyanishi, and Sugie [10, 21], using the structure of affine surfaces and cylinderlike open
sets. In positive characteristic, Asanuma’s work on polynomial fiber rings supplied threefolds
whose one-variable cylinders are affine four-space [2]. Gupta proved that suitable members of
this family are not affine three-space over their ground field [12], then established failure of
cancellation in every dimension at least three over every field of positive characteristic [13]. The
latter result uses a separate family of examples: adjoining variables to a threefold whose first
cylinder is already polynomial would not produce higher-dimensional counterexamples.
    The characteristic-zero problem in dimensions at least three is still listed as open in Gaifullin–
Petrov’s July 2026 preprint [11, p. 1]. Gupta’s survey [14] explains its history and its connections
with coordinates, polynomial fibrations, and the recognition of affine space. We give a negative
answer to (1.1) over C for n = 4. The defining polynomial also gives a negative answer to the
Stable Coordinate Conjecture over C in ambient dimension five, already after adjoining one
variable.

The polynomial cylinder. The stabilization belongs to the theory of residual and stable
coordinates. A polynomial in C[p][4] is a residual coordinate if its image is a polynomial
coordinate over the residue field of every prime of C[p]; it is one-stably a coordinate if it becomes
a coordinate after adjoining one variable. Our H is a residual coordinate: it is a coordinate
after inverting p, while a linear change in F, J makes its reduction at p = 0 a coordinate. Its
one-stability therefore follows from the theorem of El Kahoui–Ouali [8, Theorem 1.1], generalized
by Dutta–Lahiri [6, Theorem 3.4].
    The Stable Coordinate Conjecture asks whether a polynomial f ∈ C[n] that becomes a
coordinate in C[n+1] after adjoining one variable must already be a coordinate in C[n] [18, p. 342].
The assertion concerns polynomial automorphisms of the ambient spaces.

Corollary 1.2 (Failure of the Stable Coordinate Conjecture). The polynomial H in (1.2) is not
a coordinate of P = C[p, s, u, F, J], but it is a coordinate of P [w] for one independent variable
w. Thus the Stable Coordinate Conjecture over C fails in ambient dimension five after one
stabilization.

Proof. Since H is a residual coordinate of P = C[p][4] , the theorem of Dutta–Lahiri [6, Theo-
rem 3.4], applied with R = C[p] and four relative variables, gives G2 , G3 , G4 , G5 ∈ P [w] such
that
                   P [w] = C[p][H, G2 , G3 , G4 , G5 ] = C[p, H, G2 , G3 , G4 , G5 ].
Hence H is a coordinate of P [w] as a C-algebra. If H were a coordinate of P as a C-algebra,
quotienting a polynomial coordinate system containing H by (H) would give A = P/(H) ≃ C[4] ,
contrary to Theorem 1.1.

    A derivation D is locally nilpotent if every element is killed by some iterate of D; over C, its
exponential is an algebraic action of the additive group Ga . We make the stabilization method
explicit. A locally nilpotent derivation of P sends H to p3 . Exponentiating it converts H into
H + p3 w, and a coordinate calculation identifies the resulting quotient with a polynomial ring.
This exponential-shift argument follows the method of El Kahoui–Ouali [8, Lemma 3.1 and proof
of Theorem 1.1]; the final coordinate change realizes the transfer criterion of Edo–Vénéreau [7,
Theorem 7]. We include its polynomial inverse and the divisibility that makes it valid at p = 0.

3.3   Inducing locally nilpotent derivations
We now transfer a derivation of A to its associated graded algebra. The exact representative
statement in Theorem 3.2 is the needed input: a bound on the five generators then bounds
the degree shift on every element of A. This is the filtered-to-homogeneous derivation method
developed by Kaliman–Makar-Limanov [15, Sections 3–5]. We give the argument for the present
integer-indexed filtration, including the possibility of negative degrees.

hD(h′ ) + h′ D(h). It is locally nilpotent if for each h ∈ C there is an integer N ≥ 1 such that
DN h = 0; all subsequent iterates then vanish as well. We abbreviate this by LND. On a graded
algebra a derivation is homogeneous of degree shift j if it sends the component of degree d to
that of degree d + j for every d ∈ Z.

4.2    A normalized lift on a line bundle

6    The rigidity obstruction
We now exclude the derivation supplied by Theorem 5.2. Recall that
                    e = C[a, d, b, c, u]/(ac − bd − 1),
                    R                                         v = a3 b − a2 u3 − d2 ,

and that the fiber weights of (a, d, b, c, u) are (1, 1, −1, −1, 0). A derivation has fiber shift e if it
sends each fiber-weight-i component into the fiber-weight-(i + e) component.
    The proof first passes to the highest part for a third, auxiliary grading. It then studies
the polynomial orbit of N = d2 + a2 u3 . The polynomial abc inequality forces this highest
derivation to fix u, and a nonsquare in the original fraction field forces it to fix a, d as well. The
determinant relation leaves only derivations whose fiber weight is positive.
    The use of polynomial abc to constrain locally nilpotent derivations has precedents in Finston–
Maubach [9, Section 2]. We include the Wronskian proof of the Mason–Stothers inequality
[19, 23], followed by the elementary one-variable fact needed at the end of the argument.

Lemma 6.1 (Mason–Stothers polynomial abc). Let F be a field of characteristic zero, and let
X, Y, Z ∈ F [q] be nonzero, pairwise relatively prime polynomials satisfying X + Y = Z. If they
are not all constant, then

                             max{deg X, deg Y, deg Z} ≤ r(XY Z) − 1,

where r(XY Z) is the number of distinct roots of XY Z in an algebraic closure of F .



                                                   21
Proof. Primes in this proof denote differentiation with respect to q. Consider the Wronskian

                                       W = X ′ Y − XY ′ .

It is nonzero. Indeed, if W = 0, relative primality implies that X divides X ′ . In characteristic
zero this is impossible for nonconstant X, so X is a nonzero constant. Then Y ′ = 0, hence Y
and Z are constant as well, contrary to the hypothesis.
    The same Wronskian, up to sign, is obtained from either of the other two pairs, because
Z = X + Y . Work over an algebraic closure only to count roots. A root of multiplicity h of
one of the three polynomials is not a root of either of the others. Computing W from a pair
containing that polynomial shows that W vanishes at the root to order at least h − 1. These
roots are distinct across the three polynomials, so, on writing T = deg X + deg Y + deg Z, we
obtain
                                     deg W ≥ T − r(XY Z).
On the other hand, compute W from the two polynomials other than one of maximum degree
M . Differentiation lowers the degree of every nonconstant polynomial by one, and therefore

                                      deg W ≤ T − M − 1.

Together these inequalities give M ≤ r(XY Z) − 1.
   The nonconstant hypothesis is necessary: for three nonzero constants the right side would
be −1. The nonzero hypothesis also causes no missing nonconstant case: if one member of a
pairwise relatively prime triple X + Y = Z is zero, the other two must be nonzero constants.
The all-zero triple is not pairwise relatively prime.

The residual-coordinate property of H determines the fibers of two natural morphisms. We first
use it to prove that the morphisms are smooth, then use the nonpolynomiality of A to rule out
Zariski local triviality.
