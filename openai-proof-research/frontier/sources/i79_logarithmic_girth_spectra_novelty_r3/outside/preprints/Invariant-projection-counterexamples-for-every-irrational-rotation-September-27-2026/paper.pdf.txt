                     Invariant-projection counterexamples
                          for every irrational rotation

                                              Abstract
          For every irrational angle, we construct a continuous nonnegative circle weight with
      exactly one zero and logarithmic integral −∞ whose weighted rotation has no nontrivial
      invariant projection in the associated hyperfinite type II1 factor. This answers negatively
      the question of Zhu, Fang, and Shi, with the angle prescribed in advance. The resulting
      operator is nonzero and norm-quasinilpotent.

1.1   Hyperinvariant subspaces and commutants
For a bounded operator T on a complex Hilbert space H, write {T }′ = {A ∈ B(H) : AT =
T A}. A closed subspace is hyperinvariant for T if every member of {T }′ leaves it invariant.
An operator algebra is transitive if its only common closed invariant subspaces are 0 and H.

Lemma 1.2 (Commutant algebra). If T ∈ B(H) is nonscalar, then {T }′ is a proper, strong-
operator closed, unital complex algebra. It is transitive exactly when T has no nontrivial
hyperinvariant subspace.

Proof. The commutation equation is preserved by addition, multiplication and scalar multi-
plication, and the identity commutes with T . If Aλ → A strongly and Aλ T = T Aλ , then for
every ζ ∈ H both sides converge, giving AT ζ = T Aζ. Thus the algebra is strongly closed.
If every bounded operator commuted with T , then every rank-one orthogonal projection
would commute with T , so every nonzero vector would be an eigenvector. Applying this to
x, y, x + y for linearly independent vectors shows that the eigenvalues are equal; hence T
would be scalar. Properness follows. The last assertion is precisely the two definitions.

    The bridge to the containing von Neumann algebra is the elementary observation recorded
in Dykema [4, Proposition 2.1].

Lemma 1.3. Let M ⊂ B(H) be a von Neumann algebra and T ∈ M. The orthogonal
projection onto any hyperinvariant subspace for T belongs to M and is invariant for T .

Proof. Every unitary W ∈ M′ and its inverse commute with T . Hyperinvariance therefore
makes the subspace reducing for W . Its orthogonal projection commutes with all such
unitaries and hence belongs to M′′ = M. Since T ∈ {T }′ , its range is invariant under T .

Corollary 1.4. Every separable infinite-dimensional complex Hilbert space carries a nonzero
norm-quasinilpotent operator with no nontrivial hyperinvariant subspace. Its commutant is a
proper strongly closed transitive unital algebra.

Proof. The tracial representation of Rθ is separable and infinite-dimensional. Apply Theo-
rem 1.1, Corollary 6.2, and Lemma 1.3 there. A nonzero quasinilpotent operator is nonscalar,
so Lemma 1.2 applies. Unitary equivalence transfers these properties to any separable
infinite-dimensional complex Hilbert space.

    These conclusions do not exclude ordinary invariant subspaces in the representation. A
separate companion gives a direct 2-adic weighted shift with a transitive commutant, including
an explicit norm estimate, backward intertwiners and a description of its polar conjugates
[11]. It uses only Lemmas 1.2 and 3.1 from this paper. The present proof uses no result from
that companion.
Remark 1.5. Corollary 1.4 contradicts the hyperinvariant-subspace assertion of Chō et al. [3,
Corollary 2.8, p. 801] at µ = 1: for the nonzero quasinilpotent T just constructed and any
conjugation C, their stated hypotheses apply to I + T , but {I + T }′ = {T }′ , so I + T has no
nontrivial hyperinvariant subspace. Only that hyperinvariant-subspace assertion is at issue
here.

1.2   Prior work
The determinant of Fuglede and Kadison [8] and Brown’s spectral distribution [2] form a
major route to invariant subspaces in finite factors. Haagerup and Schultz associate an
affiliated hyperinvariant projection to each Borel subset of the plane, with trace equal to
its Brown mass [9, Theorem 1.1]. Thus a Brown measure that is not a point mass gives a

                                              2
nontrivial projection. For a point-mass Brown measure, the trace formula gives only trivial
projections.
    Point-mass Brown measure can nevertheless coexist with many invariant subspaces.
Dykema and Haagerup construct a one-parameter family of nontrivial affiliated hyperinvariant
subspaces for the quasinilpotent DT-operator [5, Theorems 2.2 and 2.9]. Thus degeneration
of Brown measure to a point mass does not itself exclude invariant projections. Tucci
also constructed quasinilpotent generators of the hyperfinite factor with nontrivial affiliated
invariant projections [13, Theorems 3.1 and 3.3 and the discussion following Theorem 3.3].
These examples further distinguish quasinilpotence from the absence of factor-invariant
projections.
    For weighted irrational rotations, the measurable-action theorem of Dykema and Schultz
identifies the Brown measure as uniform measure on the circle whose radius is the determinant
[6, Theorem 5.4]. Zhu, Fang, and Shi give a direct proof, explicitly crediting that earlier
result [14, Theorem 5.3]. At determinant zero this measure is the point mass at zero, so the
Brown-measure projections are trivial.
    Continuous weights with one zero and determinant zero were already exhibited by Zhu,
Fang, and Shi [14, pp. 262–263]. The additional conclusion of Theorem 1.1 is the exclusion
of every nontrivial invariant projection for a suitable weight at each prescribed angle. Zhu,
Fang, and Shi’s spectral-radius formula [14, Theorem 2.3] implies quasinilpotence in this case.
We give an elementary proof of this special case, using only uniform averaging of continuous
functions under irrational rotation. Their positive-determinant invariant-subspace result [14,
Corollary 5.5] has a hypothesis complementary to the one studied here.

1.3   Proof strategy
The obstruction begins with a measurable weight. Suppose its weighted rotation had a
nontrivial invariant projection. In the shift representation, this projection becomes a field
of projections. The finite trace and ergodicity force its central diagonal entry to lie strictly
between zero and one almost everywhere. Thus both the projection and its complement have
vectors with a nonzero central coordinate.
    Invariance imposes weighted orthogonality identities on these vectors. We first obtain
the identities along rotation orbits and then pass them to suitably coupled pairs of points.
The weight has drops that are rare enough to leave a chosen finite window under control, but
strong enough to suppress contributions outside that window. The central coordinates force
at least one finite convolution coefficient to stay away from zero. The suppressed tail cannot
cancel it, contradicting the exact orthogonality identity. Conditioning separately on each
endpoint controls that tail even though it has infinitely many terms.
    The measurable weight is then replaced by a continuous weight with one zero. Smoothing
and moving the logarithmic drops changes their sum by a constant plus a measurable
coboundary. This relation supplies an intertwining multiplier, possibly unbounded. A domain
and trace argument shows that an invariant projection for the continuous operator would
produce one for the measurable operator with exactly the same trace. This transfers the
obstruction to the desired continuous weight.
    The construction realizing this scheme is as follows. Use the additive circle X = R/Z,
with Haar probability µ, and write σx = x + θ. Write {t} ∈ [0, 1) for fractional part. We
construct divisible integers 1 = q0 | q1 | q2 | · · · and positive costs ci . The measurable model
is the weight

many sampling tests. The frequencies will be divisible integers; the angle θ will never be
changed.

as desired and qi /qi−1 is as large as desired. This is possible because the positive multiples of

ratios tend to infinity and all αi → λi . The Haar law of (ui (x))i∈Jj then converges weakly
to product Haar measure. For each fixed nonzero integer character vector, the last nonzero
coefficient times its frequency dominates the sum of the earlier terms, once the ratios are
sufficiently large. That Fourier coefficient is therefore exactly zero eventually. Trigonometric
approximation proves the weak convergence. The joint law including the deterministic phases
converges to product Haar in the coordinates and the fixed vector of trial phases.
    For fixed m the finitely many squared sampling tests are bounded functions of these
coordinates and phases. Their discontinuities have zero limiting measure: every selected
{nλi } avoids the phase cut, and for that phase only finitely many coordinate values can
lie on a cutoff or interval boundary. Weak convergence therefore gives convergence of their
integrals. This familiar extension from continuous tests follows, for example, by continuous
upper and lower approximations outside open neighborhoods of the null discontinuity sets.
It applies to the squared differences from one as well. Choose a sufficiently late finite block
choice. Its sampling norms are below 1/j, all the strict spread and angle bounds persist, and
all frequency ratios determined so far satisfy their required bounds. The ratio connecting
the previous block to this one can be made large enough to meet that block’s outstanding
condition; the ratio at the end of this block is imposed at the next stage. This completes the
induction.

statement also holds for a summand of mass zero. Averaging over n shows that the total
discarded mass is bounded by (A.21) times the uniformly bounded untrimmed mass. Hence
the difference of the two measures tends to zero in total variation and ηI,l =⇒ λI .
    Each trimmed marginal is a submeasure of the corresponding untrimmed marginal. Its
Radon–Nikodym density is therefore nonnegative and bounded almost everywhere by the
untrimmed density, so its L2 norm cannot increase. Trimming preserves the finite set of
positive orbit powers and now imposes order in every coordinate. All conclusions hold
on the single probability-one event already constructed, for every finite I, proving the
Proposition.

    We now fix one realization θ for which rational independence, Proposition A.2, and
Proposition A.3 all hold, and henceforth write σ = σθ . The scales and this rotation are fixed
before any candidate invariant projection is introduced. For this fixed rotation, each sampling
sequence has actual weak convergence against every continuous function; its validity is no
longer restricted to the countable tests used to obtain the probability-one event. Thus later
choices of continuous tests or compact sets depending on a candidate projection require no
further intersection of events in the angle probability space.

A.6     Finite atomic algebras and a diffuse limit
The model also distinguishes the spectral information in finitely many polar conjugates from
that in the full orbit. This distinction follows from the explicit weight and the rotation,
independently of the convolution argument.

Proposition A.9. For the operator T constructed above, its polar decomposition is T = U |T |,
with |T | = π(f ) and ker |T | = {0}. The spectral measure of |T | is countably atomic. For
every finite F ⊂ Z, the abelian von Neumann algebra

                                   AF = W ∗ (U n |T |U −n : n ∈ F )

is atomic. The full algebra
                                    A = W ∗ (U n |T |U −n : n ∈ Z)
is diffuse.

                                                   45
Proof. Since f > 0 almost everywhere, T ∗ T = π(f )2 has zero kernel, and |T | = π(f ). The
unitary U is therefore its polar factor. On the conull set where the sum in (A.23) has finite
support, the value of f is determined by a finite subset of the countable coordinate set. There
are only countably many such subsets. Consequently f takes values in a countable subset of
(0, 1], up to a null set. Its nonnull level sets give countably many spectral projections whose
sum is 1, which proves the assertion about spectral type.
     The representation convention gives
                                   U π(g)U ∗ = π(g ◦ σ −1 ).
For a finite F , the tuple (f ◦ σ −n )n∈F has countable range on a common conull set. The
nonnull joint level sets therefore give an atomic partition of the generated algebra AF . This
includes F = ∅, when the generated unital algebra is scalar.
    For the infinite family, A is an invariant abelian subalgebra of π(L∞ (X)). The rotation
is totally ergodic. Indeed, for any integer q ̸= 0 and any nonconstant finite-support character
indexed by v = (vi ), rational independence implies q i vi θi ∈/ Z. The Fourier argument used

in Section A.1 thus proves ergodicity of σ .q

    Suppose e were an atom of A. Its integer translates under Ad U are atoms of equal
positive trace. Distinct atoms in an abelian algebra are orthogonal, so only finitely many
can occur. The orbit of e is therefore finite, and for some positive integer q, U q eU −q = e.
Because e ∈ π(L∞ (X)), total ergodicity forces e = 1. An abelian algebra whose identity is
an atom is scalar. But A contains the nonconstant multiplication operator π(f ): a positive
almost-everywhere constant would have finite logarithmic integral, contrary to Lemma A.5.
Hence A has no atoms, as claimed.
    The factor construction, the norm and determinant calculation, the exclusion of invariant
