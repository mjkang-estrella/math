            Polynomial removal fails for ordered binary matrices
                                                  OpenAI

                                          September 25, 2026

                                               Abstract
         We construct a fixed 66×66 binary matrix for which ordered matrix removal has no polynomial
      bound. This disproves the polynomial ordered binary matrix-removal conjecture. Ordered copies
      preserve the separate row and column orders and match both zeros and ones; removal permits
      changing entries in either direction.


1    Introduction
A binary matrix has both a row order and a column order. For a fixed binary k × k matrix H, an
ordered copy of H in a binary n × n matrix A is a choice
                                    r1 < · · · < rk ,        c1 < · · · < ck
with A(ri , cj ) = H(i, j) for every i, j ∈ [k], where [k] = {1, . . . , k}. Thus a copy matches the zeros
as well as the ones, and the row and column choices are independent. Write NH (A) for the number
of such copies and call A H-free if NH (A) = 0. The normalized distance from H-freeness is
                                    1
                      distH (A) = 2 min |{(r, c) ∈ [n]2 : A(r, c) ̸= B(r, c)}|,
                                    n B is H-free
where the minimum is over binary n × n matrices. Every cell may be changed, in either direction.
We say that A is ϵ-far from being H-free when distH (A) ≥ ϵ.
    The polynomial ordered binary matrix-removal conjecture asserts that for each fixed H there
are constants cH , CH > 0 such that, for every integer n ≥ 1, every 0 < ϵ < 1, and every binary
n × n matrix A,
                             NH (A) ≥ cH ϵCH n2k whenever distH (A) ≥ ϵ.
We disprove this conjecture with an explicit pattern of size 66.

History and significance. Alon, Fischer and Newman raised the ordered-matrix question after
proving polynomial-query testing for fixed finite forbidden families when row and column order is
ignored [AFN07, Section 7]. Alon and Ben-Eliezer formulated the polynomial removal question for
finite families of binary matrices [ABE20, Problem 1.4]. The singleton version appears explicitly
as Conjecture 4.5 in the first arXiv version of the survey by Gishboliner and Shapira [GS25].
Theorem 1.1 resolves that conjecture negatively and answers the finite-family question negatively
already for one square pattern.
    Qualitative ordered matrix removal was established by Alon, Ben-Eliezer and Fischer [ABEF17,
Theorem 1.8 in the full version]: a positive copy-density bound exists for each fixed positive
distance. Our examples concern its quantitative dependence. Polynomial bounds also hold for finite
families closed under row permutations, or under column permutations by transposition [ABE20,
Theorem 1.5]. The polynomial induced removal classification for ordered graphs of Gishboliner and
Tomon [GT22, Theorem 1.1] concerns a different model, with a single vertex order and symmetric
adjacency.
    Nonpolynomial lower bounds were already known for ternary hosts: Fischer and Rozenberg
obtained them for a fixed two-symbol 2 × 2 pattern, with row and column order ignored [FR07]. Alon
and Ben-Eliezer give a related ternary-matrix construction based on progression-free sets [ABE20,
Section 5.1]. These examples use a third symbol in the host matrix; our construction uses only 0
and 1.
    A particularly relevant positive result is the polynomial lower bound for matrices containing
many entry-disjoint copies of a fixed pattern [ABE20, Theorem 1.2]. It yields a small set of cells
meeting all existing copies when the copy density is small. Changing those cells can create new
copies. Our construction makes this distinction explicit: all original copies meet a set of 2h cells,
while any binary repair changes at least 4h cells.

4     Copies are confined to the leaf diagonal
The distance proof used the prescribed anchor representatives. To bound the number of copies in
the unedited matrix Ah , we must now locate every possible copy. Its 64 × 64 prefix will lie in the
anchor groups of a single mode, and its two remaining rows and columns will then belong to that
mode’s designated sets.

4.1    The anchor and the non-anchor traces
The anchor matrix S has two complementary features. Its lower-right corner contains every binary
word of length five. We will show that non-anchor rows cannot realize all these words on five
non-anchor columns. This will force more than 32 positions on at least one axis of any copy to
be anchors. The dense first 32 rows and columns, contrasted with the sparse variable-to-anchor
incidences and the zero entries between different modes, will then force the whole copy into one
mode.
Lemma 4.1 (Properties of the anchor). The rows of S are pairwise distinct, as are its columns.
Every column has at most one zero in the first 32 rows, and every row has at most one zero in the
first 32 columns. Each row has at least 58 ones and each column has at least 48 ones. Finally, the
restrictions of rows 33, . . . , 64 to columns 60, . . . , 64 are the 32 distinct binary words of length five.

Lemma 4.4 (Anchor rigidity). Every ordered copy of S in Ah uses the row and column anchor

anchor groups of different modes are zero. Write t for this common mode. Every selected anchor row
also belongs to t. Indeed an anchor row of another mode would be zero on the at least 63 selected
anchor columns and would have at most one one in the entire copy, contradicting Lemma 4.1.
    A selected dummy column is now impossible: its entries to the selected anchor rows of mode
t contain at most one one, because its entries to that mode form either a unit vector or the zero

selected row must also be an anchor of t.
