1     Introduction
The word problem for a group with a specified finite generating set asks whether a given word in
the generators and their inverses represents the identity. It is decidable if an algorithm answers this
question for every word. A group is simple here if it is nontrivial and its only normal subgroups
are the identity subgroup and the whole group.

Theorem 1.1. For every finitely generated group G, the following are equivalent:
    (i) G has decidable word problem.
 (ii) G embeds in a finitely presented simple group.

    An embedding throughout means an injective group homomorphism. In the constructive
implication, G is supplied with a finite generating set and a word-problem decider; it need not be
finitely presented. Theorem 1.1 resolves the Boone–Higman conjecture positively.
    Higman’s embedding theorem characterizes the finitely generated subgroups of finitely presented
groups as those admitting a recursively enumerable set of defining relations [10]; see also [1,
Theorem A]. Decidability of the word problem is stronger: it supplies an algorithm for both
equality and inequality. Boone and Higman characterized this condition by the existence of
embeddings
                      G ≤ H ≤ K,            H simple, K finitely presented
[6, Theorem I]. They asked whether the simple overgroup H could itself be chosen finitely presented
[6, p. 43]. The two properties in their theorem belong to different groups, and finite presentation
does not in general pass to subgroups. Thompson subsequently obtained a finitely generated
simple overgroup with decidable word problem [18]; a later proof appears in [9, Theorem 7.1].
This strengthens the conclusion to finite generation of the simple overgroup, but does not supply
its finite presentation.
     Positive cases of the conjecture include hyperbolic and contracting self-similar groups [4,
Theorem A and Corollary D], Baumslag–Solitar groups and finite-rank free-by-cyclic groups [8,
Theorem A], and automorphism groups of finite-rank free groups and mapping class groups of
orientable punctured surfaces of finite type [5, Theorem A and Corollary B]. The survey [3]
describes further results and the development of the conjecture.
     Our principal intermediate result concerns permutation actions. An action on a set X is
faithful if only the identity acts trivially, and two-transitive if it is transitive on the ordered pairs
of distinct points of X.

Theorem 1.2. Every finitely generated group with decidable word problem embeds in a finitely
presented group Γ admitting a faithful two-transitive action on a countably infinite set. Every point
stabilizer of this action is finitely generated.



                                                   2
    The action is designed for the twisted Brin–Thompson construction of Belk and Zaremsky,
which embeds an acting group in a simple group [2, Section 3 and Theorem 3.4]. Their original finite-
presentation theorem used finitely many orbits on finite subsets of each size and finitely presented
finite-set stabilizers [2, Theorem D]. Zaremsky subsequently showed that finite presentation of the
acting group, finite generation of point stabilizers, and finitely many orbits on unordered pairs
suffice [20, Theorem A and Corollary B]. Two-transitivity supplies the last condition here. We
state this criterion precisely in Theorem 2.3; the construction below provides its faithful action
and the embedding of the prescribed group.

The proof
The action is affine. Translations move the first point of an ordered pair to zero, so two-transitivity
reduces to transitivity of the linear part on nonzero vectors. A simple algebra A provides the
required coefficient operations: if 0 ̸= m ∈ A, then its identity belongs to AmA, and hence is a
finite sum of left and right multiples of m. Elementary row operations can use this identity to
move vectors. Finite presentation, however, will come from a different algebra.
    Over k = F2 , we construct a finitely presented algebra B, a simple subalgebra A ⊆ B with its
own identity, and an injective nonunital map ϕ : B → B with image in A. The additive direct limit
M∞ = lim(B, ϕ) is the carrier for the action on X = M∞    6 . Every finite collection of its coordinates

    The coefficient ring R = B ⊗k B op acts on B by left and right multiplication. We give an
explicit finite presentation of the affine Steinberg group

                                          Γ0 = B 6 ⋊ St6 (R).

The Steinberg group maps onto the elementary matrix group E6 (R), but its kernel is an obstruction
to the desired faithful affine action. We remove that entire kernel by a single application of the
coefficient map induced by ϕ. The key algebraic input consists of two splitting pairs commuting
with the coefficient image. Their corner identities give an exact calculation in the Steinberg
presentation; no centrality hypothesis on the kernel is needed.
     The resulting endomorphism of Γ0 defines a finitely presented mapping torus Γ. In its direct-
limit description the Steinberg kernel has disappeared, leaving elementary matrices acting on
M∞ 6 , together with translations and a stage shift. Simplicity of A gives the nonzero-vector orbit

required for two-transitivity. Faithfulness uses two further properties of the algebra construction:
a separating vector detects every nonzero coefficient after one advance, and the proper support
ϕ(1B ) ̸= 1B makes the stage shift detectable. The stabilizer of zero is the quotient by the translation
subgroup, so it is finitely generated. To embed the original G, we first use an HNN extension to
express its elements as controlled commutators; balanced elementary diagonals then place this
copy of G in the linear part.
     It remains to construct the algebras with these simultaneous properties. We do so through a
finitely presented monoid and a decidable monoid that encode one another. A finite presentation is
compiled syntactically from a program index, before any totality assertion is available. Its partial
evaluator controls the length of every call to the encoded program. Long codes make the calls
needed to normalize a word strictly shorter than that word. A fixed-point argument and induction
on length then establish totality and compatibility with multiplication. Finite-tuple selectors
isolate individual basis terms in a contracted monoid algebra, producing A; a separate separator
distinguishes ordered pairs of basis terms, producing the faithful coefficient detector.

    The finite-presentation construction follows the machine-encoding tradition of Post and Murskii
[15, 17]. Here the bounded evaluator permits the two monoids to be constructed together. The
selectors strengthen the contextual-collapse property in the semigroup counterpart of the Boone–
Higman theorem [6, pp. 49–52]: isolating each term of a finite linear combination gives the
simplicity needed for the affine action.

action and prove the two theorems from those data. Section 6 constructs the algebras from a
precise monoid theorem, proved by bounded self-reference in Section 7. Appendix A gives the full
finite compiler and its evaluator proof.

and elementary operations. The translations form the additive group of B 6 . We first give their
finite presentation relative to a Steinberg group, and then construct the endomorphism used in
the next two sections.

    We first establish stability. For a fixed generator word v, its static rule is determined by the
same tests and the same candidates of length at most |v| at every larger bound. Consider a tuple
with Nj ≤ k ≤ n. Each tested body has length less than Nj , hence less than k. New static
left sides longer than k cannot occur in it. Induction over earlier tuple numbers shows that the
previously selected rules that can occur in the bodies also agree. Hence the decision for tuple j
agrees at both bounds. A new tuple with Nj > k has every selector side longer than k, so cannot
add a rule on a word of length at most k. This proves stability, including the decisions for selected
rules too long to act at the smaller bound.
     It remains to prove local confluence for Rn . All reductions stay inside the length-at-most-n
ball. Disjoint occurrences can be reduced in either order. If the word contains ζ, every branch
still contains it: absorption retains ζ, and no other rule has a left side containing that letter.
Absorption joins all branches at ζ. We check the other overlaps explicitly.
Selectors from different stages. Let the older left side have total length L, and let the newer one be
dlN wrN h. By (34), L < N and |w| < N . A nonempty old suffix cannot equal a new prefix: the

former ends in h, while a prefix of length at most L lies in the initial dlN . An old prefix cannot
equal a new suffix, since their first letters are respectively d and either r or h. These exclude both

the new initial d, because its terminal h would lie in the initial l-run. Any other start must be
in the body. It cannot leave the body: it cannot end in the final r-run, and reaching the final h

Selectors from one stage. A second start strictly inside one side must lie in its body. There are
then fewer than N body letters left before the final r-run, so that start cannot be followed by N

also covers an empty body.
A code and a selector. Code letters are disjoint from the envelope markers. An overlapping code

A     The finite compiler
We prove Lemma 7.1. The input is a program index η for a partial function fη : Σ∗ ⇀ Σ∗ on
the fixed finite alphabet Σ. We construct a finite monoid presentation and its partial evaluator.
The main issue is to control arbitrary words in that presentation, including malformed machine
configurations.
    Encoding a finite machine by contextual equations, and checking the consequences of using those
equations in reverse, are classical ideas [17, pp. 3–6]. Murskii used finite machine presentations to
embed finitely generated recursively presented semigroups in finitely presented semigroups [15,
Theorem 1.2 and Section 1.5]; his construction also controls malformed initial configurations [15,
Section 2.4]. For the present construction we also need a single evaluator on all presentation
words, normalization paths that never increase grade, and invariance under equations in arbitrary
contexts within the grade bound. We prove these properties explicitly below.
    Fix a deterministic one-tape machine for fη , with tape cells indexed by the nonnegative integers.
A left move at cell zero stays there. The input begins at cell zero and is followed by blanks; a
returned string also begins at zero and ends at its first blank. We use an effective compilation of
the acceptable programming system into this convention. The resulting finite transition table is
part of the compiler’s input; constructing the presentation never runs the program.

A.2    A finite transition table for the computation
We specify the remaining machine equations explicitly through finite scan instructions. This makes
the construction uniform in η while avoiding a separate list of rules for each tape symbol.
    Every state has one designated reading side. A right-reading state has, for each tested tagged
cell, at most one rule of either form

                                  qbR −→ b′L q ′   or qbR −→ q ′ b′R ;                              (45)

a left-reading state uses the mirrored forms. These instructions cross the cell or stay in the same
gap, respectively. The cells b, b′ always have the same base. Boundary tests are stationary rules
αq → αq ′ or qω → q ′ ω on the designated side. A failed test has no rule. A finite cell predicate
expands into the list of tagged cells satisfying it; complementary cases are disjoint. Finite registers
are incorporated into the state set by a finite Cartesian product.
    Here are the scan instructions used below, together with their complete expansion convention.
   • To rewind, cross every cell on the left unchanged. At α, change state without moving and
     start the specified right-reading continuation. If α is absent, the scan gets stuck at the left
     end of the word.
   • To find the first cell satisfying a predicate, test cells on the right. At a failure cross unchanged
     and repeat. At a match use the specified stationary update and continuation. At ω, use the
     specified end continuation, or get stuck if failure was prescribed.
   • To go to the right boundary, cross all right cells unchanged and use the specified stationary
     continuation at ω.
All invocations are expanded with distinct phase states, apart from explicit repetitions of a loop.
In particular qstart is fresh: its only incoming rule is the validator handoff, and its only outgoing
rule is qstart ω → qrewind ω, where qrewind starts the first rewind. No continuation returns to qstart or
to a validator state.
    The following finite control program supplies all further internal machine equations.
   1. Copy the input. Rewind, and find the first anchor whose read bit is unset. Set that bit, carry
      its base a in finite state, rewind, and find the first workspace cell whose filled bit is unset.
      Write a on its work track and set its filled bit. Failure to find that workspace cell is stuck.
      Rewind and repeat the anchor search.
      When the anchor search reaches ω, rewind and check that an unfilled workspace cell remains;
      otherwise get stuck. Rewind once more and locate the first workspace cell for simulation.


                                                   30
      On (43) this writes exactly xy in anchor order into the initial workspace cells, followed by a
      blank.
   2. Run the given machine. The simulated head reads a workspace cell on its right. For each
      machine state and work symbol, expand its single transition using (45). A stationary
      move writes and stays. A right move writes and crosses the current cell, and the next
      simulation state requires another workspace cell on the right. A left move first writes without
      crossing, then enters a left-reading intermediate state. That state crosses an immediately left
      workspace cell unchanged, or stays if the immediately left base is C; any other case is stuck.
      It then returns to a right-reading simulation state. This implements the left-end convention.
      A halt is recognized only at a workspace cell in bounds.
   3. Read the output. After halt, rewind and find the first workspace cell whose output-reading
      bit is unset. A blank work symbol ends the output. For a work symbol a ∈ Σ, set that bit,
      carry a in finite state, rewind, and place output mark a on the first anchor whose output
      mark is none. Failure to find such an anchor is stuck. Rewind and repeat. An invalid output
      symbol, or exhaustion of the workspace without a blank, is stuck. When output ends, go to
      ω and enter a fresh state qok , with no outgoing internal transition.
    Each instruction uses only the finite predicates, finite registers, and updates just specified.
Expanding all cases gives a finite list of equations computable from the machine table. Each state
tests only its designated side and has disjoint cases. Consequently the internal forward transition
relation has outdegree at most one on every sector, including sectors with missing boundaries and
arbitrary tags. Every transition preserves the number and ordered bases of the cells.
    On a passing entry, simulation success computes fη (xy) genuinely. Conversely, if fη (xy) is
defined and has length at most |xy|, then some finite quota s permits success: take more workspace
cells than the input length, the largest tape index visited by the halting computation, and the
output length. The extra cell supplies its blank delimiter. There are |xy| anchors for the output
marks. This includes empty input: at least one workspace cell is supplied, and a length bound
forces the output to be empty.
    Add the exit equation qok ω → Zω and the following cleanup equations. An anchor cell b with
output mark a ∈ Σ has
                                             bL Z −→ ZaR .                                     (46)
For every other tagged cell use bL Z → Z, and add αZ → αQ. Cleanup is deterministic and never
increases grade, even on dirty data. Applied to a successful passing entry, it leaves

                                            αQfη (xy)R ω.                                        (47)

The leftward sweep places successive output letters on its right in the correct order. All unused
anchors and all nonanchor cells disappear.

A.3    Finite components on arbitrary sectors
We now analyze sectors with arbitrary tags and optional boundaries. For a validator or machine
sector, form the graph of internal forward transitions, excluding entry from P and exit to Z. We
use its entire weak connected component. The ordered bases of its cells and its boundary flags
are fixed. If there are k cells and h internal head states, with at most c tags per cell, there are at
most (k + 1)hck possible configurations with these data. Its weak component can therefore be
found by a finite search through elementary compiled steps. This search does not call fη or wait


                                                 31
for a computation to halt. In particular, the quota insertion rule is excluded: finite grade alone
would not bound the number of grade-zero workspace cells.

Lemma A.1. Every internal weak component has at most one entry and at most one sector whose
head is qok immediately before its final ω. If both occur, the forward computation from the entry
reaches that sector and has the clean form and genuine output described in (43) and (47).

Proof. An entry has internal indegree zero: the only possible predecessor of an I-sector is a left
sweep, whose output has clean W immediately right of the head, whereas an entry has clean D.
Every other validator sector has indegree at most one. For head J, its possible predecessor types
are distinguished by clean C versus an anchor immediately right. For J′ they are distinguished
by α versus an anchor immediately left; for N by clean C versus W immediately left; and for V
by clean D versus an anchor immediately left. There are no transitions from machine states to
validator states.
    The validator phases progress in their displayed order, and each sweep is finite. An entry whose
validation fails before handoff therefore belongs to an isolated finite chain: its vertices have at
most one predecessor and one successor, the entry has no predecessor, and the failed end has no
successor. It shares its component with no other entry. Every passing entry has (43). Its fixed
base sequence and boundary flags determine it uniquely, including its clean tags and the head gap
immediately before the unique D. Hence there is at most one entry in any component.
    Finally, a finite directed graph with outdegree at most one has a single eventual cycle or
terminal vertex in each weak component. Indeed the unique forward trajectories of adjacent
vertices have the same eventual object, and this propagates along weak paths. A sector ending in
qok ω is terminal. Thus it is the only such sector in its component, and every forward trajectory
there reaches it. If an entry is present, it therefore passes validation and genuinely succeeds.

A.4    The partial evaluator
The component lemma leaves a choice when both an entry and a successful terminal occur.
Returning to the entry, removing its quota, and undoing the merge, then sliding the raw heads left,
gives raw(x) raw(y); cleaning up the terminal gives raw(fη (xy)). We give the entry priority, so that
internal equations have identical preprocessing outputs. The final combination of consecutive raw
sectors will identify the two routes in (44), using the contextual identity assumed in Lemma 7.1
when other raw sectors are adjacent.
    This priority also applies to a failed or malformed entry; in that case quota removal need
not produce two full raw sectors. Conversely, a successful component without an entry need not
encode a genuine input computation. It can use its unique terminal cleanup directly. The following
algorithm includes all these cases.
    The algorithm Nη preserves the empty word. It sends every word containing Ω, and every
nonpath, to Ω, using the zero relations. For a nonempty path, first preprocess its sectors as follows.
   1. Leave sectors without heads unchanged. Move Q to its leftmost reachable gap using (40),
      across clean anchors only.
   2. If P has clean DR immediately right, remove all immediately preceding clean WL by the
      quota equation. If clean CL is then immediately left, unmerge CL PDR into QωαQ and
      normalize the two resulting raw heads as above. Otherwise keep the trimmed sector. A P
      without immediately right clean DR is kept.
   3. Run Z cleanup until it is stuck or becomes Q; in the latter case normalize that raw head.

                                                 32
  4. For any validator or machine head, find its finite internal weak component. If there is an
     entry, move to it along a weak path, revert to P, and use the preceding rule. If there is no
     entry but there is a sector ending in qok ω, move there and use cleanup. If neither exists, use
     the least sector in the component in shortlex order.
Lemma A.1 makes the choices in the last rule unambiguous. The ordering is fixed independently
of any grade budget. All these operations preserve the original outer boundaries; only unmerge
creates an interior ωα and splits a sector.
    Replace each maximal run of t ≥ 2 consecutive full raw sectors, with payloads u1 , . . . , ut , by

                                         αQfη (u1 · · · ut )R ω.                                 (48)

A run of length one is left unchanged, without evaluating fη . This completes the definition of Nη .
Its only calls to that function are in (48).
    Assume now the budget hypothesis of Lemma 7.1 at m. Every preprocessing operation has a
derivation that never raises grade. Internal component paths preserve every base and hence grade.
Raw slides and entry reversal preserve grade. Quota cells have grade zero. Unmerge preserves

head right across its anchors, merge at QωαQ, insert a sufficient grade-zero workspace quota,

intermediate word remains within its required budget. This proves termination and the asserted

A.5    Invariance under equations and contexts
We verify that one contextual equation step preserves Nη when both endpoints have grade at most
m. All nonzero equation sides are nonempty paths with the same initial and terminal vertices.
This includes the deletion bL Z → Z. A replacement therefore preserves whether the whole word is
a path. Zero equations and nonpaths give Ω on both sides, so it remains to compare paths, using
their sectors in the whole contextualized word.
    Raw slides have the same leftmost reachable gap. Quota changes have the same trimmed
sector. Entry from P has the same preprocessing on both sides, since the internal component gives
priority to its entry, even if that entry is malformed and validation fails. Internal validator or
machine equations leave the weak component unchanged. Cleanup equations give the same result
by forward determinism, including the last step to Q. A merge is immediately undone by quota
preprocessing before normalizing its two raw heads. This remains true when the two sectors have


                                                   33
incomplete outer boundaries or dirty cells. In each case the preprocessed sector list is identical, so
its subsequent raw-run replacements agree.
    The only remaining equation is qok ω → Zω. If its internal component has no entry, both sides
use the same unique terminal cleanup. If it has an entry, Lemma A.1 says that the entry is (43)
and its computation returns fη (xy). Entry-priority preprocessing on one side gives two full raw
sectors for x, y; cleanup on the other gives a single full raw sector for fη (xy). With no full raw
neighbors, the pair is replaced by exactly that singleton. Otherwise let a, b be the concatenations
of the neighboring raw words on the left and right. The final runs have outputs

                                   fη (axyb)   and fη afη (xy)b ,
                                                                 


which agree by the budget hypothesis. The anchor count |axyb| on the entry side is at most the
original grade, so this use is legitimate. Passing validation forces both α and ω; an incomplete
sector cannot occur in this exceptional comparison. This proves equation-step invariance in every
context.
    If Nη (u) = Nη (v) and both xuy, xvy have grade at most m, transplant the nonincreasing
normalization paths of u and v into x(−)y. Additivity keeps every intermediate word within budget.
Equation-step invariance identifies each input’s answer with the answer of its contextualized normal
form. Those two middle strings are literally equal, proving bounded context compatibility. This
argument does not require sector boundaries to survive the attachment of context.
    If the hypotheses hold at every budget, every finite derivation is covered by some budget.
Invariance then implies that equal monoid words have equal Nη answers. The converse follows
from each word’s derivation to its answer. Thus Nη is an exact word-problem evaluator. Its
definition always leaves each raw singleton αQuR ω literal, distinct from Ω and from the empty
word; on two such sectors it gives the raw encoding of fη (uv) whenever the relevant budget
hypothesis holds. All parts of Lemma 7.1 follow.
