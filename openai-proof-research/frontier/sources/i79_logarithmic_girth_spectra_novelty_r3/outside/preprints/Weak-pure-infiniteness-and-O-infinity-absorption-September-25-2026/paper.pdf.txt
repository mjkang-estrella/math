                                                  Abstract
          We prove that every complex C ∗ -algebra in which each positive element is properly infinite
      is strongly purely infinite. This answers the ordinary-to-strong part of Kirchberg–Rørdam’s
      comparison question. For exact algebras, we also prove that proper infiniteness of one fixed
      finite amplification of every positive element implies proper infiniteness of each positive element.
      Consequently, every separable nuclear algebra with this fixed-amplification property absorbs
      O∞ , without assumptions of unitality or simplicity.


1     Introduction
Pure infiniteness expresses the ability of positive elements of a C ∗ -algebra to contain several copies
of themselves in Cuntz comparison. For a nonsimple algebra, an individual comparison and the
simultaneous comparison of two elements are different demands: the latter must also control a
mixed entry. We prove that proper infiniteness of every positive element already provides this
compatibility. We then give two ways to remove a fixed finite amplification, one for exact algebras
and an independent construction for nuclear algebras. In the separable nuclear case the result yields
absorption of the Cuntz algebra O∞ .

1.1   Infiniteness notions and main results
All C ∗ -algebras are complex and may be nonunital. Let K denote the compact operators on an
infinite-dimensional separable Hilbert space. For u, v ∈ (C ⊗ K)+ , write u ≾ v if some sequence
xj ∈ C ⊗ K satisfies x∗j vxj → u in norm, and write u ∼Cu v if comparison holds in both directions.
This is stable Cuntz comparison [7]. Finite matrices over C occupy the standard corners of C ⊗ K;
comparisons between different sizes use zero padding in the other coordinates. Write h⊕n for the
direct sum of n copies of h. A positive element h is properly infinite if h ⊕ h ≾ h. We include zero
as properly infinite. For h ∈ C+ , let

            Her(h) = hCh,          Id(h) = the closed two-sided ideal of C generated by h.

    We will use the following two comparison conditions:

               h is properly infinite for every h ∈ C+ ,                                                      (P)
                ⊕n
               h     is properly infinite for every h ∈ C+ ,     for one fixed integer n ≥ 1.                (Fn )

In (Fn ), the same n is chosen before all positive elements are tested. It therefore applies to every
scalar spectral cutoff used later. The premise concerns scalar positives in C, with their indicated
amplifications.


                                                       1
    Condition (P) is equivalent to pure infiniteness in the nonsimple sense of Kirchberg–Rørdam [11,
Theorem 4.16]; the equivalence is also recalled in [21, Section 21]. The existence of an n satisfying
(Fn ) is weak pure infiniteness in the fixed-amplification sense. We use the following formulation of
strong pure infiniteness, valid for arbitrary C ∗ -algebras [13, Definition 3.1]: for every x, y ∈ C+ and
ε > 0, there are s, t ∈ C such that

                      ∥s∗ x2 s − x2 ∥ < ε,    ∥t∗ y 2 t − y 2 ∥ < ε,    ∥s∗ xyt∥ < ε.              (1.1)

The coefficients in this formulation have no prescribed norm bound.
    Kirchberg–Rørdam introduced the weak and strong notions in [12]. Strong pure infiniteness
implies ordinary pure infiniteness, and (P) implies (Fn ) with n = 1. The reverse implications
form the comparison question in Kirchberg–Rørdam’s Question 9.5, as recalled in [21, Section 21,
Problem LXXII]. In version 2 of that problem collection, the question asks whether the three notions
agree in general and in the nuclear setting. Our first theorem proves the implication from ordinary
to strong pure infiniteness for arbitrary complex C ∗ -algebras, in the following more flexible form.

Theorem 1.1. Let C be a complex C ∗ -algebra satisfying (P). For all a, b ∈ C+ , c ∈ C, and ε > 0,
there are s, t ∈ C such that

                         ∥s∗ as − a∥ < ε,      ∥t∗ bt − b∥ < ε,        ∥s∗ ct∥ < ε.                (1.2)

Consequently C is strongly purely infinite.

     The cross term c is arbitrary and need not be a product of the diagonal inputs. Taking
(a, b, c) = (x2 , y 2 , xy) gives the stated consequence. Arbitrary-entry diagonalization is a known
equivalent form of strong pure infiniteness [13, Remarks 3.2(i)–(ii)]; our proof obtains it directly
from (P). It also gives coefficient bounds for positive contraction inputs that are independent of the
requested mixed-entry accuracy; see Proposition 5.4.
     Recall that a C ∗ -algebra is exact if its minimal tensor product preserves short exact sequences.
Exactness supplies the additional uniformity needed to remove the fixed amplification.

Theorem 1.2. Let C be an exact complex C ∗ -algebra. Suppose that there is one integer n ≥ 1 such
that h⊕n is properly infinite for every h ∈ C+ . Then every positive element of C is properly infinite.
Consequently C is strongly purely infinite.

    These theorems give a positive answer to the ordinary-to-strong part of Problem LXXII in
general and to the full comparison question for exact algebras, hence also for nuclear algebras. The
weak-to-ordinary implication for arbitrary nonexact algebras remains outside these conclusions. The
statements include nonseparable and nonunital algebras. Section 6 gives an independent proof of
the first conclusion of Theorem 1.2 under nuclearity. Its statement is a restriction of that theorem,
while its finite-tuple obstruction and merger of hereditary witnesses provide a different method. The
same method extends to exact algebras by the deeper theorem that exactness passes to quotients;
see Remark 6.7. The direct exact proof avoids that permanence theorem.
    Let O∞ be the Cuntz algebra generated by countably many isometries with mutually orthogonal
ranges. Kirchberg–Rørdam proved the equivalence between strong pure infiniteness and O∞ -
absorption for separable nuclear algebras that are stable or have an approximate unit of projections
[12]. Kirchberg’s later central-sequence results cover general nonunital separable nuclear algebras
[10, Proposition 4.4 and Remark 4.6]; see also the paragraph following [13, Definition 3.1]. At that
scope they give the following tensorial consequence.

    For separable nuclear algebras, therefore, weak pure infiniteness, pure infiniteness, strong pure
infiniteness, and O∞ -absorption are equivalent. The reverse implication from absorption to strong
pure infiniteness is due to Kirchberg–Rørdam [12]; see also the discussion after [13, Definition 3.1].
Thus a single finite amplification test on positive elements characterizes this tensorial regularity
even when the algebra is nonsimple or nonunital.
    For example, the cone C0 ((0, 1])⊗O∞ is separable nuclear and absorbs O∞ by the self-absorption
of O∞ . It is nonsimple, nonunital, and has no nonzero projections: the norm of a continuous
projection-valued function takes only the values zero and one and must tend to zero at the missing
endpoint.

1.2   Earlier results and the role of ideal structure
The comparison question has been settled under several structural conditions. Kirchberg–Rørdam
treat simple, real-rank-zero, and approximately divisible cases [12]; the real-rank-zero conclusion is
also recalled in [21, Section 21]. Blanchard–Kirchberg prove that ordinary pure infiniteness implies
strong pure infiniteness when the primitive ideal space is Hausdorff [4, Theorem 5.8]. Rørdam’s
proof of [20, Theorem 5.2] gives the same implication for Z-absorbing purely infinite algebras, where
Z is the Jiang–Su algebra. These two implications do not require exactness or nuclearity.
    Pasnicu–Rørdam establish equivalence of the three infiniteness notions for algebras with the
ideal property, meaning that projections separate ideals [16, Proposition 2.14]. For algebras of
topological dimension zero, Pasnicu–Rouzbehani prove the implication from ordinary to strong
pure infiniteness [17, Theorems 3.10(i) and 3.13]. Here topological dimension zero means that the
primitive ideal space has a basis of compact open sets. Theorem 1.1 requires no restriction on this
space or on the ideal structure.
    The weak-to-ordinary implication is closely related to the problem of finding sufficiently many
orthogonal equivalent elements in a hereditary subalgebra. Kirchberg–Rørdam showed that the
Global Glimm Halving property permits this implication [12, Proposition 4.15]; see [21, Theorem 39].
An equivalent formulation of the Global Glimm Property is that, for every a ∈ C+ and ε > 0, there
is x ∈ Her(a) with x2 = 0 such that (a − ε)+ belongs to the closed two-sided ideal generated by x
[22, Remark 3.2 and Theorem 3.6(3)]. Elliott–Rouzbehani prove weak-to-ordinary pure infiniteness
in topological dimension zero [8]. Ng–Thiel–Vilalta recover that implication through the Global
Glimm Property in a proof that includes nonseparable algebras [15, Theorem 2.3 and Corollary 2.5].
The initial and range squares of a square-zero element are orthogonal and Cuntz equivalent. Our
exact argument constructs such elements while retaining a nonzero image throughout a prescribed
set of primitive quotients. The relevant sets are the norm slices {P ∈ Prim(C) : ∥h + P ∥ ≥ r},
where h ∈ C+ and r > 0; they are quasi-compact even when Prim(C) is not Hausdorff. Repeated
halving on the same slice then supplies enough copies to remove the fixed amplification. The nuclear
argument instead combines representation-dependent positive elements into one element that covers
a prescribed cutoff.

Comparison transport. Section 2 supplies the common tool. A Cuntz comparison, after a
spectral cutoff, can be implemented by a rectangular partial isometry in the bidual. Its products
with the cutoff hereditary algebra lie in the original algebra, and conjugation preserves norms
in every representation, also at matrix levels. Thus one can place several orthogonal copies in a
hereditary subalgebra and transport an entire finite tuple through the same map. These are stronger
implementation data than a comparison of classes alone.

From individual to simultaneous comparison. For h ≥ 0, let eh be its support projection in
the universal bidual. Starting from positive contractions a, b and a contraction c, the first goal is to
find positive contractions p ∈ Her(a), q ∈ Her(b) with

                                                   ∥ep ceq ∥ < 1,

while requiring represented norm one of p or q whenever the corresponding input has represented
norm greater than 1/2. This retention of represented norms is what later gives ideal comparison.
    Section 3 builds one corner as a graph inside several orthogonal copies of a cutoff of a. The
graph has finitely many freely chosen algebra entries. If the positive defect b(1 − c∗ p2 c)b vanishes
in a representation that detects the relevant part of b, equality in contraction inequalities forces
all of those entries to equal fixed measurements, computed before the entries were chosen. Under
(P), Section 4 excludes these equalities with four entries. The first pair forces an isometry with
a nonzero defect, using the scaling-element construction of Blackadar–Cuntz [3, 16]; the second
pair is forced both into and away from that defect. Spectral cutoffs place the relevant supports in
actual operator ranges, and a directed family of finite tests makes the contradiction work without
separability. Two ideal cutoffs then give the strict support estimate above.

                                                         4
   Section 5 upgrades strict contraction to arbitrarily small compression. Uniformly bounded
comparison rows show that (P) passes to bounded products. A product contradiction therefore
supplies one contraction factor below one, and iteration preserves both represented norm signals
while reducing the mixed compression. Finally, high spectral parts of a, b are compared into the
separated corners. The coefficients have bounds determined by the diagonal cutoffs, independent of
the requested mixed-entry accuracy. This order of choices completes Theorem 1.1.

A separate nuclear proof of amplification removal. Section 6 reuses the graph construction
with a different obstruction. A finite tuple witnessing nonexactness has linear matrix norms that
no tuple in an exact algebra can realize at every matrix level. Finite-dimensional compressions
nevertheless approximate each fixed norm. Under (Fn ), one rectangular carrier transports each
entire compressed tuple into Mn (C). If every choice failed to exclude the graph relations, their
limiting norms would occur in a quotient of Mn (C). Nuclearity makes this quotient nuclear, hence
exact, giving a contradiction. The matrix norm and fixed-element quotient arguments are related to
Pisier’s operator-space localization methods [18, pp. 162–165].
    It remains to obtain the positive inputs that the graph needs and combine them. The fixed-
amplification hypothesis and Kadison transitivity [9] give, at each irreducible representation detecting
a positive u, a positive contraction in Her(u) whose every spectral cutoff is properly infinite and
whose norm in that representation is one. This element may depend on the representation. Repeated
graph shrinking and a polar deformation merge finitely many such elements while retaining all their
norm signals. A finite ideal argument covers each cutoff of u by one merged element and then
compares two copies of that cutoff back to u. This proves (P) under nuclearity, independently of
the exact argument. The use of hereditary elements to cover ideals is related to the filling-family
criteria of [13, Theorem 1.1 and Definitions 4.2, 4.7]; here the merger supplies the simultaneous
norm control.

The exact proof and its quantitative completion. Section 7 starts from the nonexact
universal unital C ∗ -algebra of one contraction. Its finite matrix tests and its embedded copy of
C ∗ (F2 ) were studied by Courtney–Sherman [6], with Wassermann’s nonexact free-group example
as a predecessor [23]. Finite-word compression at all matrix levels turns nonexactness into one
positive error threshold: no contraction in a unital exact algebra can contain approximately reducing
copies of every finite matrix contraction. The same copy must approximately intertwine both the
contraction and its adjoint.
     Combining this obstruction with (Fn ) gives a perturbation that prevents cancellation simultane-
ously in every primitive quotient of a prescribed norm slice. Its lower norm bound is proportional
to the perturbation size, with a constant independent of the slice and of n. Section 8 applies this
estimate through three finite coupling steps. For a hereditary subalgebra nonzero on a prescribed
quasi-compact subset of Prim(C), it constructs a square-zero element nonzero at every point of that
subset. Repeated halving on one fixed norm slice puts n copies of a positive element below the
input in Cuntz comparison; ideal comparison and a cutoff limit prove (P). The general theorem
then completes Theorem 1.2.
     Section 9 gives an alternative final step under exactness. It constructs positives in two prescribed
hereditary subalgebras with arbitrarily small product, while retaining a quotient norm floor fixed
before the product error is chosen. Two compatible inverse perturbations preserve the two floors.
Clipping the later comparison coefficients bounds their norms, so the small product survives
implementation and gives strong pure infiniteness directly. Finally, for a separable nuclear algebra,
the central-sequence absorption criterion applied in Section 6 gives Corollary 1.3.

this use: the copies must have a common implementation, their products must remain in the algebra,

the ideal and cutoff rules used to retain norm signals.

Rørdam’s comparison lemma [19, Proposition 2.4], in the form recalled in [1, Section 2.1]. We give
the rectangular and representation statements needed here with their proofs.

Definition 2.4. A positive contraction a is rich if (a − ε)+ is properly infinite for every ε ≥ 0.

    Zero is rich, and under (P) every positive contraction is rich. A ramp is a continuous nondecreasing
function with values in [0, 1], zero on a neighborhood of zero and constant at 1 beyond a specified
larger threshold. A ramp of a rich contraction is rich. Indeed, for 0 ≤ τ < 1, the open positivity set

6      Removing amplification under nuclearity
We now remove the fixed amplification under nuclearity. This proof uses the graph construction of
Section 3: a finite tuple of matrix entries rules out the graph’s failure relations, and the resulting
shrinking operation allows us to join rich positive elements detected by different representations. Its
second output need not be rich; the merger retains n orthogonal copies so that (Fn ) makes their
sum rich. A finite ideal argument then reaches every positive element. The proof of the following
theorem is independent of the exact localization argument in Sections 7–8.

Theorem 6.1 (Removing amplification under nuclearity). Let C be a nuclear complex C ∗ -algebra.
Suppose that one integer n ≥ 1, fixed for the whole algebra, makes h⊕n properly infinite for every
h ∈ C+ . Then every positive element of C is properly infinite.

    Theorem 6.1 assumes neither separability nor a unit. We keep the fixed integer n throughout
this section. In the tuple construction it controls the number of graph entries, while the dimensions
of the finite matrix approximations may vary.

7    Uniform perturbation from exactness
We now begin the exact proof that (Fn ) implies (P). Its first step is a perturbation estimate that
applies simultaneously to every primitive quotient in which a fixed positive element has norm at least
a prescribed positive threshold. The constant in that estimate comes from the following obstruction:
there is one positive error threshold, independent of the unital exact algebra and the matrix size,
below which a contraction cannot contain approximately reducing copies of every finite matrix
contraction.

8     Exact localization and removal of amplification
We now use Proposition 7.8 to prove the fixed-amplification part of Theorem 1.2. Throughout this
section, let C be an exact C ∗ -algebra satisfying (Fn ) for one fixed integer n ≥ 1, and retain the
constant 0 < σ < 1 from (7.12). For P ∈ Prim(C), write aP = a + P , with its norm computed in
any irreducible representation having kernel P . A hereditary subalgebra D ⊂ C is nonzero at P
when D ̸⊂ P .
    Our immediate objective is to construct a square-zero element in a hereditary subalgebra that
is nonzero on a prescribed quasi-compact subset of Prim(C). Its initial and range squares are
orthogonal and Cuntz equivalent. Repeating the construction on one fixed norm slice will remove
the amplification integer n and give (P). Theorem 1.1 will then supply the strong pure infiniteness
conclusion. All finite covers in the construction concern the primitive ideal space; the partition of
unity used below is on a real interval.
