1     Introduction
The word problem asks whether a word in a fixed finite generating set and its inverses represents the
identity. A finitely generated group has decidable word problem if an algorithm answers this question
for every word. Embedding theorems connect this algorithmic condition with the algebraic structure
of an overgroup. Higman’s embedding theorem characterizes the finitely generated subgroups
of finitely presented groups as the recursively presented groups: those admitting a recursively
enumerable set of defining relations [14]. Boone and Higman obtained the finer characterization [8,
Theorem I]: a finitely generated group has decidable word problem if and only if it embeds in a
simple group which in turn embeds in a finitely presented group. They asked whether the simple
overgroup itself can always be finitely presented [8, p. 43]. This is the Boone–Higman conjecture.
Thompson subsequently showed that the intermediate simple group can be chosen finitely generated
with decidable word problem [19].
    We consider the strengthening that asks for finiteness in every homotopical dimension. A group
H has type Fn if it admits a classifying space K(H, 1) with finite n-skeleton, and has type F∞ if
it admits such a space with finitely many cells in each dimension. Here a classifying space is a
connected CW complex with fundamental group H and contractible universal cover. Type F1 is
finite generation and type F2 is finite presentation. The question of a simple overgroup of type F∞
appears in the discussion following Question 5.6 of Belk et al. [4]; Question 5.6 itself asks about
overgroups of type F∞ for arbitrary finitely presented groups.

Theorem 1.1. Every finitely generated group G with decidable word problem admits an injective
homomorphism into a nontrivial simple group H of type F∞ .

    The input need not be finitely presented, and its word-problem algorithm may have arbitrary
running time. The classifying space of H may have infinitely many dimensions; type F∞ imposes
neither torsion-freeness nor finite cohomological dimension. The theorem answers the higher-
finiteness question positively and in particular implies the Boone–Higman conjecture.
    The classical converse gives an algebraic characterization here as well [8]. A nontrivial finitely
presented simple group has decidable word problem: in parallel, enumerate consequences of its
defining relations and consequences after adding the input word as a relation. A trivial input word
is detected by the first search; a nontrivial one normally generates the simple group, so the second
search eventually proves all generators trivial. These outcomes are exclusive because the original
group is nontrivial. Restricting this algorithm along an embedding solves the word problem in any
finitely generated subgroup. Thus the groups in Theorem 1.1 are exactly the finitely generated
subgroups of simple groups of type F∞ .

1.1   Earlier embeddings and the higher-finiteness obstacle
Thompson-type groups have long supplied simple groups with strong finiteness properties. Brown
established type F∞ for the Thompson–Higman families [10, Section 4]. More recently, Belk and
Zaremsky constructed a single simple group of type F∞ containing all finitely generated right-angled
Artin groups [2, Corollary E]; these are the groups presented by generators indexed by the vertices
of a finite graph, with commutation relations for its edges. Belk, Hyde and Matucci constructed
a simple group of type F∞ containing every countable abelian group [3, Theorems 4.4–4.6]. Such
results give substantial classes of examples without a general construction from a word-problem
algorithm.
    Other recent advances concern the finite-presentation conclusion of the Boone–Higman conjecture.
Belk, Bleak, Matucci and Zaremsky proved it for hyperbolic groups and contracting self-similar


                                                  2
groups [5, Theorem A and Corollary D]. Belk, Fournier-Facio, Hyde and Zaremsky proved it for
Aut(Fn ) and mapping class groups of orientable punctured surfaces of finite type [6, Theorem A
and Corollary B]. Here Fn denotes the free group of rank n. These conclusions do not by themselves
give simple overgroups of type F∞ . Higher finiteness requires control of additional relations among
relations in every dimension.
    Our final step uses the twisted Brin–Thompson construction of Belk and Zaremsky [2]. Its input
is a faithful action of a group Λ on a countably infinite set S. The action must be oligomorphic,
meaning that it has finitely many orbits on finite subsets of each fixed cardinality. If Λ and all its
finite-set stabilizers have type F∞ , their theorem gives a simple group SVΛ of type F∞ containing
Λ. Consequently the problem is to embed G in a group equipped with such an action, including
the finiteness properties of its stabilizers. We construct a highly transitive action: any two ordered
tuples of distinct points of the same finite length lie in the same orbit. This supplies the orbit
condition. We prove type F∞ for the pointwise stabilizers; the corresponding setwise stabilizers are
finite extensions and inherit the property.
    The stabilizer requirement is a genuine restriction on the choice of acting group. Fournier-Facio,
Kropholler, Lyman and Zaremsky prove that an oligomorphic action on an infinite set of a group
of finite virtual cohomological dimension, or of a countable group linear over a field, always has a
finite-set stabilizer which is not of type F∞ [12, Theorem 1.1]. The construction below therefore
builds the overgroup and its stabilizers together; it does not ask the original group to possess an
action with these properties.

1.2   Constructing the action and proving finiteness
The action begins with a finitely presented ring B, the elementary matrix group Q = E7 (B), and its
action on the column set X = B 7 . Here Er (B) is generated by the off-diagonal elementary matrices.
We construct compatible injections
                                   δ : Q −→ Q,        σ : X −→ X.
At each new stage, matrices can permute any finite collection of the compressed columns σ(X). The
direct limits of these two systems, with the stage shift adjoined to the group, give the action Λ ↷ S.
This makes high transitivity a local matrix calculation. The same construction embeds G into Q.
    The ring has to encode more than the given group. Its units must also encode prescribed
enlargements of the action of E7 (B) on B 7 , so the objects being encoded already involve the ring
being constructed. We resolve this self-reference by a length induction. A finite compiler from the
companion article Finite algebraic envelopes and the Boone–Higman conjecture [18] gives equality
calculations at each finite bound when its stated hypotheses hold. The encoding ensures that
every recursive call uses a strictly smaller bound. Kleene’s recursion theorem first supplies the
program, and the length induction then proves its totality and compatibility with multiplication.
The companion also supplies a binary corner construction and a Steinberg-kernel annihilation
theorem; their exact statements appear where they are used.
    Finiteness is supplied by a separate ascending-torus criterion. For an injective endomorphism
f : U → U , write
                               T (U, f ) = ⟨U, t | t−1 ut = f (u) for u ∈ U ⟩.
A factorization of f through a finitely presented group gives a finite presentation of T (U, f ). To
obtain higher finiteness, we use two finite systems of homomorphisms whose images commute in
specified pairs. Their product identities come from orthogonal idempotents over F2 and F3 . After
lower-dimensional terms have been killed, commuting products act additively on homology. The
two systems then force the same class to be annihilated by both 2 and 3.

                                                  3
    The essential point is uniformity. We use simplices whose vertices are elements of U , and regard
two simplices as having the same type when they differ by a simultaneous left translation. One
iterate and one finite set of output types work for every cycle using a given finite set of input types.
Chain lengths and coefficients may be arbitrarily large. This control allows fillings with coefficients
in products of copies of the group ring, and the Bieri–Eckmann finiteness method then gives finite
skeleta in every dimension. The result is Theorem 2.2.
    The acting group and each finite-point stabilizer are themselves ascending tori to which this same
criterion applies. For stabilizers, the defining endomorphism factors through a finitely presented
Steinberg group. The second factor sends the entire Steinberg group into the stabilizer, not merely
the image of the first factor. The commuting diagrams preserve the fixed points as well. These two
properties establish type F∞ for all stabilizers and complete the input to the twisted Brin–Thompson
theorem.

1.3   Organization and conventions
Section 2 proves the ascending-torus criterion. Section 3 constructs the ring and the encoded
actions. Section 4 constructs Λ ↷ S and proves the stabilizer theorem. Section 5 applies the twisted
Brin–Thompson theorem and proves Theorem 1.1.
    All rings used for matrices are unital and associative. Ring embeddings and endomorphisms
need not preserve the identity when this is expressly stated. All homology is integral. Group actions
are on the left; coefficient modules for group homology are right modules.


2     A homological device for ascending tori
An ascending torus will have type F∞ for two distinct reasons. Factoring its defining endomorphism
through a finitely presented group provides finite presentation. Two finite systems of commuting
splittings then give the higher-dimensional finiteness: one system annihilates the relevant homology
classes by 2, and the other annihilates the same classes by 3. The factorization also starts this
second argument, by placing the image of the endomorphism in a finitely generated subgroup.
    We first construct the finite systems of idempotents that index the splittings. The resulting
criterion is Theorem 2.2. Its proof must control a finite set of simplex types uniformly over all
cycles; it will impose no bound on chain lengths or coefficients.

2.2    The ascending-torus theorem
For a group U and an endomorphism f : U → U , write
                                 T (U, f ) = ⟨U, t | t−1 ut = f (u) (u ∈ U )⟩.
Here the notation includes all relations already holding in U .
Theorem 2.2. Let f : U → U be an injective endomorphism. Assume the following.
  (i) There are a finitely presented group P and homomorphisms a : U → P , b : P → U with f = ba.
  (ii) For each ℓ ∈ {2, 3} there are homomorphisms hE : U → U , indexed by E ∈ Pℓ , such that
       hI = f . Whenever E, P ′ , E + P ′ ∈ Pℓ and EP ′ = P ′ E = 0, the subgroups hE (U ) and hP ′ (U )
       commute elementwise, and
                                     hE+P ′ (u) = hE (u)hP ′ (u)        (u ∈ U ).

Then T (U, f ) is of type F∞ .
    We first establish finite presentation. A product-module criterion then reduces the remaining
assertion to a precise homology-vanishing statement. Uniformly controlled fillings will supply that
vanishing through the homology sequence of the ascending torus.
Lemma 2.3. Under assumption (i) of Theorem 2.2, the group T (U, f ) is finitely presented. This
assertion does not require f to be injective.
Proof. Set g = ab : P → P , and let s be the stable letter of T (P, g). There is a homomorphism
                           T (U, f ) −→ T (P, g),         u 7−→ a(u),     t 7−→ s.
An inverse sends p ∈ P to tb(p)t−1 and sends s to t. The defining conjugation relations verify that
these are homomorphisms; their composites are the identity because tf (u)t−1 = u and sg(p)s−1 = p.
This is the algebraic interleaving of the two direct systems. No injectivity of g is needed. Finally, a
finite presentation of P , together with s and its conjugation relations on a finite generating set of P ,
is a finite presentation of T (P, g).

2.3    A product-module criterion
The product-module test below is a form of the Bieri–Eckmann finiteness criterion [7, Proposition 1.2].
For the cellular passage from homological to geometric finiteness, see also Brown [9, Section 3]. We
give the argument for the now finitely presented group J = T (U, f ): vanishing with products of
regular modules will yield finite skeleta of a classifying space.
    All group homology below has integral coefficients, and coefficient modules are right modules.
For a group J and a set I, the notation (ZJ)I means the direct product, not the direct sum, with
its coordinatewise right action.

2.4   Uniformly bounded simplex types
Let E(U ) be the simplicial set whose q-simplices are tuples (u0 , . . . , uq ) of elements of U . Faces
delete entries and degeneracies repeat entries. Its normalized integral chain complex is denoted by
C∗ (U ). Thus tuples with consecutive equal entries are zero, and the boundary is the alternating
sum of faces. The augmentation sends each vertex to 1. Simultaneous left translation gives a free
U -action. Its augmented chain complex is contractible as a complex of abelian groups: prepending
the identity gives a contraction. Consequently its augmented chain complex is a free resolution of
the trivial left ZU -module.

3     Encoding the diagrams and their actions
The ring needed for the action theorem must encode an action built from its own elementary group

their equality algorithms have been proved to terminate. A calculation on a code word of length

length will prove these algorithms total and justify the required ring data.

3.5   Calculations in a bounded system
The code normalizer will compare expressions and replace them by shorter codes for the same

equality algorithms.

4      A highly transitive action with finite-type stabilizers
We strengthen the embedding problem to a statement about an action and all its finite-point
stabilizers.

Theorem 4.1. For every finitely generated group G with decidable word problem, there is a group
Λ containing G and acting faithfully on a countably infinite set S such that:
    (i) the action is transitive on ordered tuples of distinct points of every finite length;
 (ii) Λ and every pointwise stabilizer of a finite tuple are of type F∞ .

   The action will be obtained by iterating an embedding of a group and its set of columns.
Finite permutations become available after one transition, giving high transitivity in the limit. We
then identify each finite-point stabilizer as an ascending torus and verify the two hypotheses of
Theorem 2.2: commuting splittings and a factorization through a finitely presented group.

This proves the factorization. For m = 0, the sums in Equation (4.13) are empty and d = 1; all the

Proof of Theorem 4.1. The embedding G ,→ Q ,→ Λ was constructed above, and Lemma 4.4 proves

5    The simple overgroup
The action constructed in Theorem 4.1 provides the input for the twisted Brin–Thompson construc-
tion. We describe the group and then apply the simplicity and finiteness theorems of Belk and
Zaremsky.
    Let A act faithfully on a countably infinite set S, and put C = {0, 1}N . A brick in C S prescribes
a finite binary prefix α(s) in each coordinate s, with all but finitely many prefixes empty. Its chart
hα : C S → Bα prefixes each coordinate by α(s). For a ∈ A, the coordinate permutation is

                                            (τa ξ)(s) = ξ(a−1 s).

The twisted Brin–Thompson group SVA consists of the homeomorphisms represented by two finite
brick partitions and a bijection between their pieces, with each piece map of the form

                                           hβ τa h−1
                                                  α : Bα −→ Bβ .

Common refinements define composition. The one-piece maps τa embed A because its action on S
is faithful. This is the construction of Belk and Zaremsky [2, Section 1].

   An action is oligomorphic if it has finitely many orbits on finite subsets of each fixed cardinality.
The transfer theorem below combines Belk and Zaremsky [2, Theorem D, with n = ∞, and
Theorem 3.4].
Theorem 5.1 (Belk–Zaremsky). Let A act faithfully and oligomorphically on a countably infinite
set S. Suppose A and the setwise stabilizer of every finite subset of S have type F∞ . Then SVA is a
nontrivial simple group of type F∞ and contains A through its global coordinate action.
Proof of Theorem 1.1. Fix the finitely generated group G and its word-problem decider. Theorem 4.1
supplies an embedding G ,→ Λ and a faithful highly transitive action of Λ on a countably infinite
set S. In particular the action is oligomorphic: every set of distinct m points can be sent to any
other such set.
    The same theorem gives type F∞ for Λ and for each pointwise stabilizer Λ(F ) , where F ⊂ S
is finite. The setwise stabilizer Λ{F } acts on F ; its kernel is Λ(F ) , and its image lies in the
finite symmetric group Sym(F ). Lemma 2.7 therefore gives type F∞ for Λ{F } . All hypotheses of
Theorem 5.1 hold. Taking H = SVΛ , we obtain the required inclusions

                                            G ,→ Λ ,→ H,

with H nontrivial, simple, and of type F∞ .

   The same overgroup H can be generated by two elements of finite order. Indeed, type F∞
implies finite generation, and the additional conclusion of Belk and Zaremsky [2, Theorem 3.4]
applies to the twisted Brin–Thompson group H = SVΛ constructed above.
