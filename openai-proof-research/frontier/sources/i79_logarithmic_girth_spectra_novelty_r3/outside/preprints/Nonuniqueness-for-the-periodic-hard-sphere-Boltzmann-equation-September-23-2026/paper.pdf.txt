Nonuniqueness for the periodic hard-sphere Boltzmann equation

                                               Abstract
        We prove nonuniqueness for the periodic hard-sphere Boltzmann equation by constructing
     two distinct global renormalized solutions with the same nonnegative initial density on T3 ×R3 .
     This density has bounded velocity support and finite mass, energy, and absolute entropy.
     Both solutions conserve local mass and total momentum and satisfy the global energy and
     entropy-dissipation inequalities. On a common initial interval, they are strongly continuous
     in L1 , and their collision gains and losses are integrable.

1.1   Uniqueness and related constructions
Classical uniqueness theorems impose additional control, such as Maxwellian upper bounds
[10]. For periodic cutoff hard potentials, Ukai’s perturbative theory gives global existence and
uniqueness near a Maxwellian in a velocity-weighted spatial Sobolev class, with mass, momentum,
and energy matched to that Maxwellian [14, Theorem 1.2].
    The distinction between spatially homogeneous and inhomogeneous evolution is also impor-
tant. For the homogeneous hard-sphere equation, Mischler and Wennberg prove uniqueness in a
conservative class from finite mass and energy alone [12, Theorem 1.1]. Wennberg’s homogeneous
nonuniqueness examples for hard potentials permit increasing energy [15]. Theorem 1.1 instead
concerns a spatially inhomogeneous datum, with the energy and entropy-dissipation inequalities
retained.
    Recent instability constructions give another relevant comparison. Chen and Holmer prove
failure of uniform continuity in weighted Sobolev spaces for the constant collision kernel [2,
Theorem 1.3]; Chen, Guo, Shen, and Zhang establish failure of uniform continuity for the
hard-sphere solution map on R3x × R3v , in Gaussian-weighted L2v Hxs spaces for 0 ≤ s < 1 [1,
Theorem 1.1]. Those results compare solutions with arbitrarily close initial data. Here the two
initial data agree exactly, on periodic space, and both resulting solutions satisfy the admissibility
conditions above. The instability in [1] is driven by collision loss and dispersion. In the
construction below, collision gain amplifies a vanishing hot seed and produces a nonzero weak
limit near the initial spatial singularity.
    For the related Krieger–Strain model, Gismondi, Golding, and Novack construct rough
stationary solutions in a mollifier-confluent sense: the collision operators of mollified densities
tend to zero in distributions for every nonnegative Schwartz mollifier of mass one [7, Definition 1.3
and Theorem 1.5]. They also conjecture nonuniqueness of renormalized inhomogeneous Landau
and Boltzmann solutions for a dense class of initial data. Theorem 1.1 concerns one periodic
hard-sphere datum in the specified admissibility class.

1.2   One datum and two approximation families
Write x = (y, z) ∈ R2 × R in a lift of the torus, and v = (v̄, v3 ) ∈ R2 × R. The datum consists
of narrow velocity distributions, called cold jets, near the axial directions ±e3 . They occupy
transverse annuli of geometrically decreasing radii sj ↓ 0. Within each annulus the two velocity
signs occupy disjoint, finely alternating stripes. Their thermal widths are much smaller than
the stripe gaps. An expanding axial velocity profile bounds the spatial density of each freely
transported jet at scale s by C/(t + s). Annular mass is O(s2 ), and annular absolute entropy is
O(s2 (1 + | log s|)), so all scales together define a single finite-entropy datum.
    Assign a color to each cold jet. A collision within one color retains that color; collisions
between different colors, or with particles already declared hot, produce hot particles. This is an
exact decomposition: the sum of the colored equations is the Boltzmann equation. The narrow
velocity distributions suppress self-collisions, and the spatial gaps suppress collisions between
distinct cold jets at early times.
    Retain the first N annuli and impose a collision cutoff that is removed as N → ∞. Let hN
denote the hot component. The estimates use an integrable velocity weight H(v), Gaussian
at infinity and locally comparable to |v̄|−q near the velocity axis, with 1 < q < 2. A cap is an
almost-everywhere upper bound on hN /H. The two approximation families have the following
properties:

   1. With no hot particles initially, the hot density remains bounded by t2 H(v). We call this
      the dormant family.
   2. For every sufficiently short observation horizon T > 0, a smooth hot seed of vanishing
      mass, energy, and entropy can be selected so that hN ≤ (t + sN )H on [0, T ], with equality
      in the weighted essential supremum at some time. Here sN is the smallest retained annular
      radius.
At each fixed horizon, uniform integrability of both the densities and their two-particle products
gives admissible limits with exactly the same initial datum. A vanishing seed and a cap hit
alone do not imply that these limits differ: the hit might carry arbitrarily little mass. Excluding
that loss of information is the central step.

1.3   From a cap hit to separation
Along a sequence of observation horizons shrinking to zero, rescale time and space at a cap time
t∗,N by rN = t∗,N + sN , and divide the hot density by rN . The resulting cap is of order one.
The cold density has a different normalization: multiplying it by rN produces a locally bounded
velocity measure at positive times. Its two axial velocity signs coexist in the limit because
the increasingly fine spatial stripes average to their occupied fractions. Strong convergence of
hot velocity averages identifies the cold–hot product. This use of velocity averaging follows
the uniformly integrable formulation of [8, Proposition 3]. The limit obeys a linear Boltzmann
equation in that two-sign background. This dilation is distinct from the fixed-horizon compactness
used to construct admissible solutions.
    To show that the dilated hot limit is nonzero, we analyze normalized Carleman gain kernels.
Carleman’s representation expresses a collision gain as an integral over velocity planes; its
normalization here is derived in Section 3. Iterated-gain regularization has an earlier role in
the homogeneous theory [12, Lemma 2.1]. In the present construction the estimates must be
uniform as the cold velocity distributions collapse onto the axis, despite the singularity of H.
Two complementary arguments provide this control. Near the velocity axis, two successive gains
have vanishing normalized mass: the first has a uniformly non-atomic axial input, while a second
large gain would force that input to match a small collection of characteristic cold speeds. Away
from the axis, five gains suffice. Their times and angular parameters map to final time, position,
and velocity with rank seven almost everywhere. A compact submersion argument then turns
vanishing local hot mass into a vanishing gain contribution. Together these facts prevent the
cap hit from disappearing in the weak limit.
    The limiting hot equation also has a branching interpretation. A hot particle colliding with
a cold jet creates two hot outputs. We retain finite collision histories with bounded velocities
and show that their mass grows by a fixed factor inside an expanding spatial box. If the dilated
observation horizon were infinite, this growth would exceed the polynomial mass bound supplied
by the cap. The horizon is therefore finite, so the cap scale remains comparable to the physical
observation horizon along the extracted sequence.
    Finally, the dormant solution vanishes off the velocity axis under the corresponding shrinking-
horizon dilation. If cap limits agreed with it at arbitrarily small fixed horizons, a diagonal choice
of approximants would force the nonzero dilated hot limit to vanish as well. This contradiction
yields two distinct early solutions of the original equation with the same datum. The periodic
existence theorem then continues each solution globally while preserving admissibility. Figure 1
separates the two compactness procedures and their roles in this final comparison.
