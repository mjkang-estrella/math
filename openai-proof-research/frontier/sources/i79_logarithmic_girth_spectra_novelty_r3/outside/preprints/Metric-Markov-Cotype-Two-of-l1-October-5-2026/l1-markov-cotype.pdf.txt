                              Metric Markov Cotype Two of ℓ1

                                                         Abstract
         We prove that the real Banach space ℓ1 has metric Markov cotype two, answering a
      question of Mendel and Naor. As a consequence, every Lipschitz map from an arbitrary
      subset of a real Hilbert space into ℓ1 extends to the whole Hilbert space with a universal
      multiplicative loss in its Lipschitz constant, resolving Ball’s extension problem for this target.


1    Introduction
Metric Markov cotype asks whether the values of a map on a finite reversible Markov chain can
be modified so that their one-step variation becomes small, while the cost of the modification
is controlled by the original variation over a longer time interval. The modified values are not
prescribed linear averages. This freedom is essential for the result proved here.

History and significance
Ball introduced Markov type and cotype in his study of Lipschitz extension [1]. His Markov cotype
condition for Banach spaces uses specified linear averages, and ℓ1 fails that stronger condition.
Ball also proposed a metric definition. Mendel and Naor developed the Cesàro formulation above,
proved its equivalence to Ball’s metric definition, and asked explicitly whether N2 (ℓ1 ) is finite [4,
Question 1.15 and Section 7]. Theorem 1.2 answers that question affirmatively.
                                                                  √
    For 1 < p ≤ 2, the uniformly convex theory gives N2 (ℓp ) ≤ C/ p − 1 with a universal constant
C [1]; see also [4, Section 1.1]. These bounds deteriorate as p decreases to one and do not settle
the endpoint treated here.
    There is a second distinction worth making. Using a construction of Kalton, Mendel and Naor
found a closed linear subspace of ℓ1 that fails metric Markov cotype at every finite exponent [4,
Theorem 1.14]. This does not rule out Theorem 1.2, because the points yi in Definition 1.1 need
not belong to a given subspace containing the original data. Our construction uses this freedom.
    One important consequence concerns extension of Lipschitz maps. For a map f between
metric spaces, Lip(f ) denotes the least L ≥ 0 such that d(f (x), f (y)) ≤ Ld(x, y) for all x, y in
its domain. Ball’s extension problem asks whether a Lipschitz map from an arbitrary subset of
Hilbert space into ℓ1 admits an extension with a universal multiplicative loss in its Lipschitz
constant. Mendel and Naor explain that an affirmative answer to their metric Markov cotype
question gives such an extension [4, Corollary 1.13 and the discussion after Question 1.15]. The
extension problem is also discussed in [5, Section 1]. We record the consequence precisely in
Section 6; the proof of Theorem 1.2 itself is elementary and independent of extension theorems.

The nonlinear construction
Finite subsets of ℓ1 admit exact representations by weighted cuts; see [3]. We retain not only
their distances but also a contractive map back to the given ambient ℓ1 . Nonlinear cut smoothing
with the cubic ϕ(r) = 3r2 − 2r3 also appears in the work of Cheng, Wang, and Xiang on sharp
metric cotype [2]; metric cotype and metric Markov cotype are different invariants.
    The additional ingredient here is a martingale estimate for the cubic. For a finite family B of
cuts, the representation gives binary vectors zi ∈ {0, 1}B in a weighted Hilbert space H, with

                                      ∥zi − zj ∥2H = ∥xi − xj ∥1 .

It also gives a map T with T (zi ) = xi that is contractive for the weighted ℓ1 norm on the cut
coordinates. Let Q = [0, 1]B , and let Φ : Q → Q apply ϕ coordinatewise. Stop an A-walk (Xs )
after an independent nonnegative geometric number S of steps with mean t. Writing Ei for
expectation when the walk starts at i, we choose

                                  hi = Ei zXS ,        yi = T (Φ(hi )).

     For the estimate, start the walk from its stationary law π and record stopping as a separate
transition after the S walk steps. Attach hj to the current index j while alive and zj when
the walk stops at j, keeping that value thereafter. These values form a martingale. Because
ϕ′ vanishes at the binary values, a fourth-power Hilbert potential controls the total squared
weighted-ℓ1 variation after applying Φ. Centering at zX0 makes the terminal potential equal
to ∥xXS − xX0 ∥21 . The stopping jump accounts for the approximation cost, and the expected t
preceding jumps account for the one-step variation. Contractivity of T transfers these bounds to
ℓ1 .

Corollary 6.2. There is a universal constant K < ∞ such that, for every real Hilbert space H,
