                                                    Abstract
        We construct a separable reflexive real Banach space whose given norm is asymptotically
     midpoint uniformly convex but which admits nopasymptotically uniformly convex equivalent
     norm. Its averaged midpoint modulus is at least
                                                 p    1 + t2 /12 − 1, and the countably branching
     diamond of depth k has distortion at least 1 + k/12 in this space. This gives a negative
     answer to the reflexive diamond converse for asymptotic uniform convexifiability.


1    Introduction
Asymptotic convexity describes the convexity that remains after finitely many directions are
discarded. The question considered here is whether symmetric midpoint convexity can persist in a
reflexive Banach space which admits no asymptotically uniformly convex equivalent norm.

The norm is asymptotically uniformly convex (AUC) if δ N (t) > 0 for all t > 0, and asymptotically
midpoint uniformly convex (AMUC) if δbN (t) > 0 for all t > 0. Thus AUC requires a gain in every
sufficiently far-out direction; AMUC permits the two opposite directions to be tested together. A
norm N is equivalent to a given norm ∥ · ∥ if a∥x∥ ≤ N (x) ≤ b∥x∥ for some 0 < a ≤ b < ∞ and
all x. A space is AUC renormable if it admits an equivalent AUC norm.
    Dilworth, Kutzarova, Randrianarivony, Revalski, and Zhivkov introduced AMUC and con-
structed an AMUC norm on ℓ2 which is not AUC [6, Definition 2.2 and Theorem 2.4]. Their question
about equivalence up to renorming [6, Section 5] has already been answered negatively by Baudier
[4, Theorem 1 and Corollary 1], using the Kadets–Werner modification of the Bourgain–Rosenthal
space [10, Theorem 2.5]. We give a reflexive example with an explicit midpoint estimate.
    The historical definition of AMUC uses the maximum of the two signed norms in (1.2). This
gives the same positivity property. The average never exceeds the maximum. Conversely, intersect
any chosen finite-codimensional subspace with the kernel of a functional norming x. Both signed

norms are then at least one, so a gap for their maximum gives half that gap for their average. Our
quantitative modulus always uses the average in (1.2).
    The example also addresses a metric question. Define the countably branching diamonds by
taking D0 to be a single edge and replacing every edge at each step by countably many internally
disjoint two-edge paths. Each current edge has length one; let dk be the shortest-path metric on
Dk . An embedding of Dk into a Banach space has distortion at most D if it is a map f for which
some scale s > 0 satisfies

                      s dk (u, v) ≤ ∥f (u) − f (v)∥ ≤ Ds dk (u, v)           (u, v ∈ Dk ).               (1.3)

The family embeds uniformly when one finite D works for all k. For finite binary diamonds,
Lee and Naor obtained quantitative Lp distortion bounds by summing convexity losses [11,
Lemma 2.2], and Johnson and Schechtman developed a self-improvement proof [9, Section 4].
Baudier, Causey, Dilworth, Kutzarova, Randrianarivony, Schlumprecht, and Zhang proved that
an equivalent AMUC norm prevents uniform embeddings of the countably branching diamonds
[3, Theorem 4.1]. For reflexive spaces with unconditional asymptotic structure, they also proved
that failure of AUC renormability implies uniform diamond embeddings [3, Theorem B and
Corollary 5.3]. Swift extended the embedding construction to countably branching bundle graphs
under the same reflexive, unconditional, and non-AUC-renormable hypotheses [14, Theorem 3.2 and
Corollary 3.4]. Perreau subsequently treated duals of separable spaces with weak-star unconditional
asymptotic structure, retaining unconditional asymptotic structure in the reflexive formulation [13,
Theorems 4.8 and 5.3]. The example below shows that the diamond implication fails for general
reflexive spaces.
    More recently, Basset, Lancien, and Procházka related AMUC to separated families of midpoints
and obtained the diamond obstruction even when the maps are controlled only on specified vertex
pairs [2, Definition 6.15 and Propositions 6.16 and 6.20]. Malthaner obtained quantitative
obstructions for countably branching diamonds using a Matoušek-type coloring and path argument
[12, Theorems 3.5 and 3.7]. We use the full bi-Lipschitz convention (1.3) and prove the required
midpoint estimate directly.

1.2   How the argument fits together
Section 2 establishes reflexivity and the coordinate projections used throughout the proof. A sum
of coordinate functionals along one root path has norm one, while its next coordinate increment
is a weakly null unit vector as the child varies. In Section 3, a hypothetical AUC norm forces
repeated multiplicative gains along one such path. Arbitrarily high finite trees contradict its
bounded norm.
    The midpoint argument in Section 4 must control segment tests crossing from a finite ancestral
set (one closed under taking ancestors) to its complement. Simply adding norming tests for the
two parts creates uncontrolled positive cross terms. We add one correction at their first exit
vertices. The correction makes both resulting tests inexpensive, and its evaluations cancel when
the two endpoint norms are averaged. Lemma 4.1 expresses the result as a lower bound in terms
of the squared head and tail norms. Section 4.1 extends this estimate to coordinate completions
on general rooted forests.
    Finally, the infinitely many middle vertices of a diamond supply a separated family of ap-
proximate midpoints. Two of their finite coordinate projections can be made arbitrarily close.
Their half-difference therefore reduces the metric problem to the head–tail estimate. Section 5
turns this into a loss in squared stretch at every replacement and sums the losses. This gives a
direct quantitative instance of the midpoint and self-improvement methods for these graphs [3,
Section 4].

3    Why no equivalent norm is AUC
The renorming obstruction uses bounded root paths and weakly null child increments. We first
isolate the elementary finite-codimensional argument; its midpoint counterpart will be proved
separately.
