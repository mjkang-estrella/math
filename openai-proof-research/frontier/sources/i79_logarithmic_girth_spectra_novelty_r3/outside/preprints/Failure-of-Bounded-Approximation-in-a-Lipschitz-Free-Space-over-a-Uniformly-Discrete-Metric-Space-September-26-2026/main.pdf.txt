A uniformly discrete counterexample to bounded approximation in
                       Lipschitz-free spaces
                                               OpenAI

                                        September 26, 2026


                                               Abstract
        We construct a countable uniformly discrete metric space whose real Lipschitz-free space has
     the approximation property but fails the bounded approximation property, answering Kalton’s
     question negatively. The identity can be approximated on every compact set by finite-rank
     operators, but no uniform bound on their norms is possible.


1    Introduction
Uniform discreteness keeps distinct points of a metric space a fixed positive distance apart. For
its Lipschitz-free space, this separation guarantees the approximation property: the identity
can be approximated on each compact set by finite-rank operators. Kalton asked whether the
approximating operators can always be chosen with one bound on their norms [12, Proposition 4.4
and following remarks, p. 185]. The question was restated by Godefroy and Ozawa [11, Question 1]
and in Godefroy’s survey [9, Problem 6.2]. We construct a countable uniformly discrete space for
which no such bound exists.
    We work over the real scalars. For a pointed metric space (M, d, o), let Lip0 (M ) be the
Banach space of real Lipschitz functions vanishing at o, with the Lipschitz constant as norm. Its
Lipschitz-free space is

                   F(M ) = span{δx : x ∈ M } ⊆ Lip0 (M )∗ ,           δx (f ) = f (x).

Thus δo = 0 and ∥δx − δy ∥ = d(x, y). The latter identity follows from the Lipschitz inequality and
the test function z 7→ d(z, y) − d(o, y). The construction originates in the molecular spaces of
Arens and Eells [2, §2]; its linearization properties and complemented free subspaces are central to
the work of Godefroy and Kalton [10, §2].
    A Banach space X has the approximation property (AP) if for every norm-compact set
C ⊆ X and every ε > 0 there is a bounded finite-rank linear operator T : X → X with
supu∈C ∥T u − u∥ < ε. For λ ≥ 1, it has λ-BAP if these operators can always be chosen with
∥T ∥ ≤ λ. The bounded approximation property (BAP) means λ-BAP for some finite λ, and the
metric approximation property (MAP) means 1-BAP. In AP the norm may depend on C and ε; in
BAP one bound must work for all of them. A metric space is proper if its closed bounded balls
are compact.

Theorem 1.1. There is a countable, unbounded, nonproper pointed metric space (M, d, o) such
that d(x, y) ≥ 1 for distinct points and F(M ) has AP but fails λ-BAP for every finite λ ≥ 1.

    The distinction between discrete and uniformly discrete matters here. Smith gives countable
complete discrete spaces whose free spaces have AP and fail BAP, without asserting a common
positive lower bound on all nonzero distances [16, Theorem 1.3 and the surrounding discussion].
Theorem 1.1 supplies that lower bound. The example is necessarily outside the countable proper
case: Dalet proved MAP for free spaces over countable proper metric spaces [7, Theorem 2.6].
Our example contains an infinite separated subset in a bounded ball. Uniform discreteness also
imposes strong sequential structure: Cúth and Kalenda prove a quantitative strengthening of the
Schur property for these free spaces [6, Theorem 1.1], so weakly convergent sequences converge in
norm. Their April 2026 introduction still records the BAP question addressed here.
    The same example has a nonlinear consequence. Kalton asked whether every separable Banach
space is approximable [13, Problem 1, p. 1260]. For such a space X, this means that there is an
equi-uniformly continuous sequence of maps ψn : X → X, each with relatively compact range, such
that ψn (x) → x for every x ∈ X. Equi-uniform continuity means that the choice of δ for a given ε
in the uniform-continuity condition works for every ψn . The space X = F(M ) in Theorem 1.1 is
not approximable, although it has AP. Section 2.4 explains the short implication from Kalton’s
net theorem that gives this consequence.

1.1   A finite obstruction in each bounded block
The main construction concerns a prescribed operator norm bound p. It produces a bounded
metric space and a finite set of points, called anchors, at which every finite-rank operator of norm
at most p makes a definite error. The distinction from earlier finite extension obstructions is
important. For a finite pointed metric pair A ⊆ N containing the basepoint, a linear extension
operator E : Lip0 (A) → Lip0 (N ) must satisfy (Ef )|A = f for every f . Brudnyi and Brudnyi give
finite subsets of integer ℓ1 cubes for which the least possible norm of an exact linear extension
operator grows without bound [5, preprint Lemma 10.5, pp. 46–47]. Godefroy and Ozawa [11,
Proposition 7] obtain arbitrarily large lower bounds for finite metric pairs from a compact free
space failing AP. By finite-dimensional duality, an extension operator corresponds, with the same
norm, to a projection from F(N ) onto the span of the evaluations from A. These bounds therefore
concern projections with a prescribed range. On the finite-dimensional space F(N ), the identity
remains a finite-rank operator of norm at most one. Our block is infinite, and its fixed finite anchor
set obstructs approximation by every operator of norm at most p whose range is finite-dimensional.

projection that approximates the chosen compact set. Then approximate its images in those
blocks, using their individual BAPs. Only finitely many operator norms occur at this stage, so the
resulting operator is bounded. The bound is allowed to grow as more blocks are retained.

1.2   The obstruction: signed measures and opposing tests
The block itself consists of two kinds of points: vertices of finite source graphs and anchors that
join those graphs into a bounded metric space. After a finite-rank operator is given, we choose
a finite subset S of these same points to support the range of a nearby operator. Thus S may
contain source vertices or anchors and may depend on the operator.
     The source graphs have a fixed large degree. For every fixed radius, the frequencies of their
colored neighborhoods approach the corresponding probabilities in a regular tree of that degree
with independent uniform vertex colors. A vertex is labeled by its capped distances to the color
classes. These labels vary by at most one across an edge, and each label has only a bounded
number of coordinates below the cap. Vertices with the same label attach to the same anchor by
a long edge. There are only finitely many possible labels, hence finitely many anchors. Joining
the anchors makes the whole block bounded, while sufficiently small metric balls remain inside a
single source graph and contain a uniformly bounded number of points.
     Suppose a finite-rank operator of small norm approximates the anchor evaluations. An
arbitrarily small perturbation gives an operator T with ∥T ∥ ≤ 2p whose range has a common
finite support S ⊂ Mp . Adding a coefficient at the basepoint allows us to write its values as signed
measures µx on S, each of mass one and uniformly bounded total variation. For the height L = 10p,
a nonnegative bump bx : Mp → [0, L] detects support points whose labels are close to the label of
x. It takes its maximum L at the anchor a(x) attached to x and has a small Lipschitz constant,
in particular at most one. The operator norm controls the comparison from x to a(x), while the
anchor approximation controls the value there. The small slope of bx makes their combined loss
from the anchor value at most L/4. The affine representation therefore gives

1.3    Methods and organization
For the graph construction we use the independent-permutation model described by Friedman [8]
and prove the local convergence we need directly. The empirical rooted-neighborhood viewpoint
is that of Benjamini and Schramm [4]. The scalar extension step is an instance of McShane’s
classical extension principle [14]; we give the needed formula for finite data and its proof. A
related comparison of nonbacktracking continuation lengths is used to select parent directions in
the graph algorithm of Ben-David, Childs, Gilyén, Kretschmer, Podder, and Wang [3, §6.1.2]. Our
continuation records also depend on the support state and are used to construct the scalar tests.
    Section 2 proves the coefficient estimate, the renorming corollary, and the wedge reduction,
assuming Theorem 1.2, and derives the nonlinear consequence from Kalton’s theorem. Section 3
constructs the colored graphs and bounded block. Sections 4 to 6 prove Theorem 1.2: they pass
from an arbitrary finite-rank operator to signed measures, bound matched walks, and derive the
contradiction by scalar tests and incidence averaging.

The following sections will construct the blocks of Theorem 1.2. Here we assume that theorem,

theorem. A bounded space with separation at least one has a coefficient description by ℓ1 , with

obstructions, while retractions onto finitely many blocks provide the approximations required for
AP.

2.4   Failure of nonlinear approximability
We verify the nonlinear consequence stated in the introduction using Kalton’s characterization
by nets [13, Theorem 4.4 and p. 1260]. Let X = F(M ) for the wedge just constructed. It is
separable because M is countable, and {δx : x ∈ M } is 1-separated in X. Extend this set to a
maximal 1-separated set NX ⊆ X. Every point of X is then within distance one of NX , so NX
is a net: a separated set within a fixed distance of every point of X. If X were approximable,
Kalton’s theorem would give one Lipschitz bound Λ ≥ 1 for maps g : NX → NX with relatively
compact range that approximate any prescribed finite subset as closely as desired. Separation
makes each such range finite and turns approximation within distance less than one into exact
fixing. For a finite F ⊆ M containing o, choose g fixing every δx with x ∈ F . The restriction
x 7→ g(δx ) is then a pointed Λ-Lipschitz map from M into X. The Banach-valued linearization
property of free spaces [12, Lemma 3.2] gives a finite-rank operator TF : X → X of norm at most
Λ satisfying TF δx = g(δx ) for all x ∈ M . To approximate a compact set, approximate a finite net
in it by finite evaluation combinations and put all their supports in F . The operator TF fixes
those combinations, and ∥TF − Id∥ ≤ Λ + 1 controls the remaining errors. Thus these operators
give BAP, contrary to the preceding obstruction. Hence X is not approximable.

3     Colored graphs and the bounded block
We now construct the metric space and anchors in Theorem 1.2. The graphs below supply
independently colored tree patterns in the empirical limit. We join their disjoint union through
finitely many anchors, making the resulting space bounded while keeping the small balls used to
count support transitions inside the source graphs. The lower bound for finite-rank operators will
use this local limit together with the properties in Lemma 3.2.
    We first fix the graph conventions. Multiple edges are distinct edge instances. Each instance
has two oriented incidences e, ē, exchanged by reversal, even when the edge is a loop. Thus a
loop contributes two to the degree. Write e : x → y when x and y are the initial and terminal
vertices, and let D(x) be the set of outgoing incidences at x. A walk is nonbacktracking if it never
immediately takes the reverse incidence of its preceding step. It may revisit vertices or take the
same loop incidence again. Graph distance uses unit edge lengths. A rooted radius-r ball is the
induced multigraph on vertices at graph distance at most r from the root; its isomorphism type
retains the colors, loops, and edge multiplicities. Throughout the construction, only vertices are
points of the metric spaces: edge interiors are not included.

3.1   A deterministic sequence with a colored tree limit
We use the independent-permutation multigraph model described by Friedman [8, §1, p. 2], in the
empirical rooted-neighborhood viewpoint of Benjamini and Schramm [4, §1.2]. The precise colored
limit and deterministic selection needed here follow from the elementary exploration argument
below; no spectral estimate is used.

Lemma 3.1. For every even integer ∆ ≥ 4 and positive integer k, there is a deterministic sequence
of nonempty finite ∆-regular multigraphs Gm , with vertices colored by {1, . . . , k}, such that, for
every fixed integer r ≥ 0 and every possible induced rooted colored radius-r type τ , the fraction of
vertices of Gm whose ball has type τ converges to the probability of τ for the ∆-regular tree with
independent uniform vertex colors.

Proof. On a vertex set of size N , choose ∆/2 independent uniform permutations σ1 , . . . , σ∆/2 . For
each pair (a, v), create a distinct undirected edge instance joining v to σa (v). For each a, every
vertex has one incidence directed to its image and one directed to its preimage. A fixed point
gives a loop with these two incidences, and a permutation 2-cycle gives two parallel edge instances.
The resulting multigraph is therefore exactly ∆-regular. Assign independent uniform colors in
{1, . . . , k} to its vertices, independently of the permutations.
    Fix r ≥ 0. Explore from a prescribed root in breadth-first order, revealing every permutation
image and inverse image at each vertex discovered at distance at most r. Record newly discovered
vertices at distance r + 1 without expanding them. This extra layer reveals every edge of the
induced radius-r ball, including edges between boundary vertices. The number of queries and
discovered vertices is bounded by a constant C = C(∆, r), independent of N .
    Conditional on all revelations so far, the unrevealed part of each permutation is a uniform
bijection between its unused domains and ranges. A query already exposed reuses its edge instance.
A genuinely unexposed image or inverse image is uniform among at least N − C unused possibilities,
at most C of which are previously discovered vertices. Its conditional probability of such a collision
is at most C/(N − C) when N > C. Before a collision, each fresh query adds one vertex and one
edge to the exploration tree; reused queries add nothing. In particular, a loop or a second parallel
edge is detected when its fresh query hits a known vertex. A union bound over the queries makes

5     Rare matched walks and continuation records
Fix a finite set S ⊆ Mp containing o. Its points will serve as states, and its labels and metric
distances remain fixed throughout the following argument. The set may be the support obtained
from an operator in Lemma 4.1; its points may lie in any of the graphs or among the anchors. We
will select a graph only after S has been fixed.

incidences e, ē, exchanged by reversal, including when the edge is a loop. A loop therefore counts
twice toward degree. Write e : x → y and let D(x) be the set of ∆ outgoing incidences at x. A
walk is nonbacktracking if it never immediately takes the reverse incidence of its preceding step.

another. This section supplies the combinatorial rule for choosing those subsets from the fixed
graph sequence and the labels and distances on S.

                             ∥h(x) − h(j)∥∞ ≤ K + 2L + 1 = D − 4.
