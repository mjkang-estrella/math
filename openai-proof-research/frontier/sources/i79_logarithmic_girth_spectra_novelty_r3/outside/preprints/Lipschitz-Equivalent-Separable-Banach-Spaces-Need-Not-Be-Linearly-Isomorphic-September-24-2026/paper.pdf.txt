                   Need Not Be Linearly Isomorphic

                                            Abstract
         There are separable real Banach spaces that are globally bi-Lipschitz equivalent but
      not linearly isomorphic. This gives a negative answer to the separable Banach-space
      Lipschitz-isomorphism problem.


1    Introduction
A Banach-space norm defines both a linear topology and a metric. A bounded linear
isomorphism preserves the metric up to multiplicative constants, but a map with that metric
property need not preserve addition or scalar multiplication. The Lipschitz-isomorphism
problem asks whether a bijection with two-sided Lipschitz bounds nevertheless forces two
Banach spaces to be linearly isomorphic. We consider the separable real case and obtain a
negative answer.
   Throughout, ℓ2 denotes the real separable Hilbert space of square-summable scalar
sequences. For a Banach space H, set

                c0 (H) = {(vi )i≥1 : vi ∈ H, ∥vi ∥ −→ 0},      ∥(vi )∥ = sup ∥vi ∥.
                                                                          i

Ordinary c0 means c0 (R). A linear embedding means a bounded linear map that is bounded
below; its range is consequently closed.

Theorem 1.1. There are separable real Banach spaces X, Y and a bijection Ψ : X → Y such
that, for every s, t ∈ X,
                          4                            76
                            ∥s − t∥X ≤ ∥Ψ(s) − Ψ(t)∥Y ≤ ∥s − t∥X .                              (1.1)
                         21                            25
The space X contains a linearly isometric copy of c0 (ℓ2 ), while Y contains no closed subspace
linearly isomorphic to c0 (ℓ2 ). In particular, X and Y are not linearly isomorphic.

   The obstruction in this theorem is deliberately block-valued: each coordinate of c0 (ℓ2 ) is
an entire Hilbert space. It does not say that Y contains no ordinary c0 . The Hilbert block in
each coordinate is essential to the obstruction proved below.

Historical setting
Ribe proved that uniformly homeomorphic normed spaces have the same finite-dimensional
linear structure up to a uniform distortion bound [14, Theorem 1]. That result shows
how much linear information can survive even a weaker form of metric equivalence. At
the same time, Aharoni and Lindenstrauss constructed Lipschitz equivalent, nonisomorphic

                                                1
nonseparable Banach spaces and explicitly asked for separable examples [1, pp. 281–282]. Ribe
later constructed separable uniformly homeomorphic, nonisomorphic spaces [15]. Aharoni and
Lindenstrauss then gave uniform pairs that are not Lipschitz equivalent [2], while Johnson,
Lindenstrauss and Schechtman developed further separable uniform examples and left the
separable Lipschitz question open [9, Theorem 5.8 and Section 8(3)]. These developments make
the global two-sided estimate in (1.1) a substantive requirement, rather than a reformulation
of a uniform counterexample.
     Several positive results explain why the separable question resists a direct transfer of those
examples. Godefroy, Kalton and Lancien proved that a Banach space Lipschitz isomorphic
to c0 is linearly isomorphic to c0 , and that being linearly isomorphic to a subspace of c0
is invariant under Lipschitz isomorphism [8, Theorems 2.1–2.2]. Godefroy and Kalton’s
lifting theorem places every separable Banach space linearly and isometrically inside its
Lipschitz-free space; their quotient corollary converts a Lipschitz right inverse of a linear
quotient onto a separable space into a bounded linear right inverse [7, Theorem 3.1 and
Corollary 3.2]. The construction below uses free spaces; Remark 3.2 explains the distinction
between the lift used here and a right inverse of the linearized correction.
     In the quasi-Banach setting, Albiac and Kalton obtained separable counterexamples for
every 0 < p < 1 [4, Theorem 5.4]. The strict inequality p < 1 is part of that theorem, so it
does not settle the Banach-norm case. Kalton recorded the separable Lipschitz-isomorphism
question as Problem 3 in his 2008 survey [11, Section 2.2]; Albiac, Ansorena, Bíma and
Cúth still described the Banach case as open in the first paragraph of their paper published
online in December 2025 [3]. Theorem 1.1 resolves that question negatively for separable real
Banach spaces. It asserts neither reflexivity nor a complex-scalar analogue.
     For comparison, every surjective isometry between the unit spheres of nonzero real Banach
spaces extends uniquely to a surjective real-linear isometry [13, Theorem 1.1]. That result
concerns exact distances on unit spheres; here we allow fixed multiplicative distortion but
require a bijection of the whole spaces.

Proof overview
The proof separates the metric construction from the linear obstruction. Its intermediate
map acts between Hilbert spaces:
                         M = U ⊕2 V,         V = ℓ2 ,     h : M −→ U.
            L
Here U = ( n≥1 Un )2 is a Hilbert sum of finite-dimensional coordinate blocks. The map
h is an onto bi-Lipschitz change of variables fixing zero, close to an isometry. Its relevant
nonlinear part is
                                  D(u, v) = h(u, v) − u.
The construction arranges that, near any finite family of distinct points of M , the sufficiently
high output coordinates of D lie in mutually orthogonal coordinate subspaces. The cutoff
index and the neighborhoods may depend on that finite family. We call this property local
orthogonality.
    To turn this geometry into a linear obstruction, use the Lipschitz-free space E = F(M ),
the closed linear span of evaluation functionals on real zero-preserving Lipschitz functions.
The evaluation map δ : M → E preserves distances. Every zero-preserving Lipschitz map has
a bounded linearization, so D determines q : E → U by
                                     qδ(u, v) = h(u, v) − u.
Section 4 proves that local orthogonality makes q completely continuous: a weakly null
sequence in E has norm-null image. This is the one place where the local coordinate geometry
enters the Banach-space obstruction.

Set X = ZQ1 and Y = Zq . Applying B in each coordinate gives the desired bijection:

weakly sequentially complete.
    Two analytic steps supply the inputs just described. For localized linearization, a
hypothetical non-null image of a weakly null sequence produces uniformly Lipschitz scalar
tests di . Orthogonality allows only finitely many points where their limiting local Lipschitz
constants remain large. Cutoffs remove neighborhoods of those points; a shrinking-ball lemma
shows that the removed tests pair negligibly with the weakly null sequence. The compact
reduction theorem of Aliaga, Noûs, Petitjean and Procházka [5, Theorem 2.3] then reduces
the remainder to a compact set, where a finite cover controls its Lipschitz constant.
    For the Hilbert map, a unit vector in each block is designated as a slot. With the slots
frozen, perpendicular components stay in their blocks, old slot coordinates move to even
blocks, and the additional ℓ2 coordinates fill odd blocks. This is an onto linear isometry. The
slots are then allowed to depend on the input so that near different points they eventually use
disjoint coordinates. Finite approximants freeze all but finitely many slots and have uniform
global inverse bounds. Section 5 constructs the slots with a common radial derivative budget,
proves global invertibility of the finite approximants, and passes to an onto limit. Its decisive
estimate bounds every tail of the inverse images by the corresponding tail of a fixed output,
with an exact cancellation of slot terms. Section 6 combines the results and computes the
constants in (1.1).


2     Free spaces and the graph-space obstruction
The metric construction will provide a bounded linear operator q : E → U that is completely
continuous, with E weakly sequentially complete. This section explains why those two
properties exclude c0 (ℓ2 ) from the associated graph space. We first record the free-space facts
that produce E, then prove the obstruction without assuming compactness of any operator
into c0 (U ).

Theorem 2.1 (Compact reduction for a weakly null sequence). Let (M, d, 0) be a complete
pointed metric space, and let µi → 0 weakly in F(M ). For every τ > 0, there is a compact

                             dist(µi , F(K)) < τ       for every i.

    This is the sequential consequence of the tightness theorem of Aliaga, Noûs, Petitjean and
Procházka [5, Theorem 2.3, arXiv version cited in the bibliography]. Their theorem states
that every weakly precompact subset W of F(M ), for complete M , is tight: for each ϵ > 0
there is compact K0 ⊂ M with W ⊂ F(K0 ) + ϵBF (M ) , where for an arbitrary subset K0 this
notation means its closed evaluation span inside F(M ). Here weak precompactness means
that every sequence in W has a weakly Cauchy subsequence. The set {0, µ1 , µ2 , . . .} has
that property: any sequence in it has either a constant subsequence or a subsequence whose
indices tend to infinity, which converges weakly to zero. Apply the theorem with ϵ = τ /2 and
enlarge K0 by 0. This gives the strict inequality above. Kalton’s finite-radius localization
lemma [10, Lemma 4.5] is a precursor to this complete-space compact reduction.
Theorem 2.2 (Weak sequential completeness of a free space). If M is a superreflexive real
Banach space, then F(M ) is weakly sequentially complete. In particular, this applies to every
real Hilbert space M .
    This is [5, Corollary 2.5, arXiv version cited in the bibliography]. Its compact-set input is
the Main Theorem of Kochanek and Pernecká [12], which concerns free spaces over compact
subsets of superreflexive Banach spaces, not directly the whole Hilbert space.

An onto bi-Lipschitz Hilbert-space change of variables h will produce a bi-Lipschitz equivalence

not linearly isomorphic.

5.5   Local orthogonality of the slots
Lemma 5.3. For every finite family of distinct points of M there are neighborhoods Oj and
an integer l such that the coordinate supports of {wn (x) : x ∈ Oj , n ≥ l} are disjoint for
different j.

Proof. For an empty family, take l = 1 and the empty family of neighborhoods. For a
singleton, take l = 1 and O1 = M . In either case there is no disjointness condition to
check. Now consider two distinct points. Some pj has different values at them. Choose
k0 ≥ j sufficiently large and neighborhoods on which all values of that pj on the two sides
are separated by more than 2/k0 . At any stage k ≥ k0 , a coordinate label that occurs
on both sides would require the jth factor ψ(kpj − aj ) to be nonzero at points x, y on
opposite sides. Then both pj (x) and pj (y) would lie in ((aj − 1)/k, (aj + 1)/k), contradicting
|pj (x) − pj (y)| > 2/k0 ≥ 2/k. Thus the unions of the supports on the two neighborhoods are
disjoint at every common stage k ≥ k0 .
