           A product counterexample to the simplex maximum
                       for projection-body volume
                                               OpenAI

                                        September 24, 2026


                                               Abstract
         The product of two ten-dimensional simplices has larger normalized projection-body volume
      than a twenty-dimensional simplex. This gives a counterexample to Brannen’s proposed simplex
      maximum.


1    Introduction
Throughout, a convex body in Rd is compact and convex with nonempty interior, and d ≥ 2. Write
|K| for its d-dimensional volume and hC (x) = maxy∈C ⟨x, y⟩ for the support function of a compact
convex set C. The projection body Πd K, also written ΠK when the dimension is evident, is defined
by

extended homogeneously to all of Rd . Here S d−1 is the Euclidean unit sphere and proju⊥ is
orthogonal projection. We write B2j for the Euclidean unit ball in Rj and κj = |B2j |. The functional
under consideration is
                                          |ΠK|             (d + 1)dd
                              Rd (K) =          ,    cd =            .                            (1)
                                         |K|d−1                d!
    The extremal values of this affine-invariant functional are classical questions in convex geometry.
Petty established the affine covariance of the projection-body operator [6]; see also [5, Equation (1)].
Brannen conjectured in 1996 that simplices maximize Rd among all convex bodies [1]. The simplex
value is cd , as we verify below. Thus the proposed simplex upper bound is

                                          |ΠK| ≤ cd |K|d−1 .                                         (2)

It is the volume of ΠK itself that occurs here, not the volume of its polar: the classical projection
inequalities involving the polar concern a different functional. For d ≥ 3, Petty’s separate minimum
problem asks whether ellipsoids are the only minimizers of the same functional Rd ; see [7, Section 1]
for these distinctions.
    In dimension three, Chen, Feng, Li, Xi, and Xu settle the minimum problem and also prove
the bound R3 (K) ≤ 18 = c3 for all three-dimensional convex bodies, with tetrahedra attaining
equality [2, Theorems 1.1 and 1.2]. Feng, Hu, Liu, and Xu give counterexamples to (2) in every
dimension d ≥ 9, using polytopes with at most d+2 facets [3, Theorem 1.3]. Their result already gives
a negative answer to the unrestricted-dimensional simplex-maximum question. Their construction
uses perturbed projections of a cube and Minkowski’s existence theorem [3, Section 4]. The present
paper supplies a direct Cartesian-product construction and its exact value: two ten-dimensional
simplices suffice.

   The exact witness in Theorem 1 provides an elementary illustration of the failure of the simplex
maximum. The optimal upper constant and its maximizing bodies remain separate questions.
   The useful mechanism is a product test: for full-dimensional polytopes A ⊂ Rr and B ⊂ Rs ,
with r, s ≥ 2,
                                Rr+s (A × B) = Rr (A)Rs (B).
Consequently, the product cr cs of the simplex values is a necessary lower bound for any universal
upper constant in dimension r + s. The dimension-twenty counterexample comes from comparing
two copies of the ten-dimensional simplex value with the twenty-dimensional simplex value. All
identities used in that comparison are proved below.
    The growth of upper constants with dimension is a further quantitative question. For centrally
symmetric bodies, Henk gives the lower bound 2d (9/8)⌊d/3⌋ for the optimal upper constant [4].
Iterating our product identity gives an exponential excess over the simplex benchmark: for some
fixed λ > 1 and every sufficiently large n, a product of simplices Kn ⊂ Rn satisfies Rn (Kn ) ≥ λn cn .
Corollary 6 proves this consequence by repeating ten-dimensional factors and using one additional
simplex to reach each dimension.
    Section 2 derives the facet formula, the product identity, and affine covariance. Section 3
computes the simplex value by expressing its projection body as a cube plus one segment. Section 4
combines the identities and gives the exact integer comparison, then proves the exponential excess
in all sufficiently large dimensions.


2    Facet vectors and Cartesian products
We first express projection bodies of polytopes as sums of segments. The facets of a Cartesian
product then separate into two families, which will make its normalized projection-body volume
multiplicative. The facet formula is the polytopal case of Cauchy’s projection formula; see [7,
Section 1]. We include its proof to fix the factor 1/2 on which the subsequent constants depend.
    We use Minkowski addition C + D = {c + d : c ∈ C, d ∈ D}. Support functions add under this
operation and determine compact convex sets uniquely. For the latter assertion, if x ∈/ C and y ∈ C
is nearest to x, minimization along each segment [y, z] ⊂ C gives ⟨x − y, z − y⟩ ≤ 0 for every z ∈ C.
The linear functional with vector x − y therefore separates x from C.

and homogeneity gives the general case.

Proposition 3. Let A ⊂ Rr and B ⊂ Rs be full-dimensional convex polytopes, where r, s ≥ 2. In
the orthogonal product space Rr+s = Rr × Rs ,

                                   Π(A × B) = (|B|ΠA) × (|A|ΠB).                                       (5)

                                     Rr+s (A × B) = Rr (A)Rs (B).                                      (6)

Proof. The face exposed by a linear functional (u, v) on A × B is the product of the faces exposed by

face of a full-dimensional body, so if both u and v are nonzero, the product face has codimension at
least two. A facet therefore has one facet factor and one whole factor. Thus the facets are F × B
and A × G, with area-normal vectors

                                   (|B|sF νF , 0)    and   (0, |A|sG νG ),

respectively. The area factors are products because the two coordinate spaces are orthogonal.

dimension have the same value. The following affine covariance formula supplies that fact.
