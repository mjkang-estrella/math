                                               Abstract
          We prove the sharp one-dimensional Lieb–Thirring inequality for every finite matrix
      size and every exponent 1/2 < γ < 3/2. The optimal constant is the scalar one-bound-
      state constant, independently of the matrix size. The inequality bounds the full sum of
      negative eigenvalue  moments for every measurable Hermitian positive semidefinite potential W
      satisfying R tr(W γ+1/2 ) < ∞. The matrices may have arbitrary rank and need not commute
                 R

      at different points.

1     Introduction
Lieb–Thirring inequalities bound moments of the negative eigenvalues of a Schrödinger operator
by an integral of its potential. Their sharp constants measure how much binding is possible for a
prescribed potential cost. When the wave function has several components, the potential becomes
a matrix, and its eigenspaces may change with position. The question addressed here is whether
this additional freedom increases the optimal constant in one dimension.

1.1   Historical context and relationship to prior work
Lieb and Thirring introduced their eigenvalue inequalities in the study of fermionic kinetic energy
and the stability of matter [8, 9]. The one-state variational problem goes back to Keller [6]. In
one dimension its optimal constant exceeds the semiclassical constant for 1/2 < γ < 3/2. The
scalar conjecture in this interval asks whether summing all negative eigenvalues changes that
one-state constant. The companion paper [11, Theorem 1.1] proves that it does not. The present
paper establishes the same constant for arbitrary finite matrix size, allowing the eigenspaces of
the potential to vary with position.
    Sharp matrix inequalities at the two endpoint exponents have an established history. Weidl
proved finiteness at the scalar lower endpoint [14], and Hundertmark, Lieb and Thomas obtained

                                                            2
the sharp γ = 1/2 inequality with constant 1/2 [5, Theorem 1]; Hundertmark, Laptev and Weidl
proved the operator-valued version [4, Theorem 3.1]. At γ = 3/2, Laptev and Weidl established
the sharp matrix estimate by trace identities and extended it to operator-valued potentials [7,
Theorems 2.1 and 2.2]. Aizenman–Lieb integration in the spectral parameter gives the larger
exponents [1]. Laptev and Weidl’s dimension-lifting argument then yields the semiclassical
constant in every spatial dimension for γ ≥ 3/2 [7, Theorem 3.1]. Benguria and Loss gave a proof
of the matrix upper-endpoint estimate by Riccati factorization and commutation [2].
    In the open interval, Hundertmark, Laptev and Weidl obtained the operator-valued upper
bound with twice the semiclassical constant [4, Theorem 4.1]. Dolbeault, Laptev and Loss
developed the method of orthonormal vector-valued functions at exponent
                                                                      √      one [3, Theorems 1
and 3]. More recently, Read and Schulz proved the sharp value 4/(3 3π) for γ = 1, including
operator-valued potentials [12, Proposition 4 and Equation (2.1)]. Thus the exponent-one case of
our theorem is already contained in their result. Our additional range is 1/2 < γ < 3/2, γ ̸= 1.
    The proof adapts the finite-interval action and continuation framework of [11, Theorem 2.1,
Proposition 5.1 and Section 6]. The companion’s comparison applies when the pointwise density
has rank at most one [11, Proposition 4.1]. The new analytic step removes that restriction,
making the action method applicable to vector-valued wave functions.

1.2    The matrix comparison and the proof strategy
The proof uses the finite-interval action method of the scalar companion [11]. To explain the
extension, choose finitely many orthonormal real vector-valued trial functions u1 , . . . , un and place
them in the rows of a matrix function U (x). A lower triangular coefficient matrix C produces
A(x) = CU (x), so that the ith row of A remains in the span of u1 , . . . , ui . For trial functions
ordered by their negative energies, this preserves the spectral inequalities needed to bound the
action from above. The action subtracts a power of the matrix density ρ(x) = A(x)A(x)T , and
a complementary lower bound recovers the sum of the chosen eigenvalue moments. A fixed
realification of the potential will subsequently give the complex Hermitian case.
    The lower bound is obtained by connecting two prescribed diagonal matrices K and −K,
where the diagonal entries of K are the positive square roots of the absolute values of the negative
trial energies. A regularized matrix field M drives an equation with the penalized constraint
M (B) = ρ. In the scalar-potential argument, A has one column and this constraint has rank at
most one. For matrix potentials, ρ can have any rank. The essential new estimate controls tr(ρq )
in this setting, where q is conjugate to p.
    We prove that estimate as an independent result in Section 3. For Hermitian B, V such that
(sI −B)2 +V is uniformly positive for real s, we form a weighted integral N of resolvent differences.
When N ≥ 0, the result is tr(N V ) ≥ tr(N q ). The hypothesis permits indefinite V , which is
necessary when V is later obtained from K 2 − B 2 . Half-line solutions of a constant-coefficient
differential equation give two boundary matrices. Their sum identifies the integrated resolvent,
and their difference accounts for the noncommutative error. A logarithmic determinant identity
and a nonnegative matrix square then prove the trace comparison with the required constant.
    Section 2 states the action inequality and isolates its relation to spectral estimates. Sections 4–
6 construct the field, connect the endpoints, and remove the penalty. The continuation argument
works modulo independent signs of the rows of C. After smoothing and perturbation, parity of
the boundary of the quotient zero set forces an endpoint solution. Finally, Section 7 applies the
action inequality to smooth trial spaces and passes to general potentials and the full spectral
moment. All of these steps are proved here; the companion is cited for the method and its scalar

7    The spectral inequality and its sharp constant
We now apply Theorem 2.1 to finite families of negative eigenfunctions, following the spectral
passage of the scalar companion [11, Section 7]. The lower triangular coefficients in that theorem
preserve the spectral bound on each successive span. A trace version of Young’s inequality then
bounds the action by the potential integral. We first justify this passage for the measurable
potentials in Theorem 1.1.
