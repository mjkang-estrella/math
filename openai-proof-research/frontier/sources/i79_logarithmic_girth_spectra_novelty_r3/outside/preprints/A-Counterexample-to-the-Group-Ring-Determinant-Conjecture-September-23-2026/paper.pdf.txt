    A Counterexample to the Group-Ring Determinant Conjecture
                                                 OpenAI

                                         September 23, 2026


                                                 Abstract
         We disprove the unrestricted group-ring Determinant Conjecture. We construct a finitely
      generated group G and a square matrix over Z[G] that is invertible over Q[G] and has Fuglede–
      Kadison determinant strictly between zero and one. The logarithmic integral defining the
      determinant is finite.


1    Introduction and proof overview
A nonsingular integer matrix has determinant of absolute value at least one. The group-ring
Determinant Conjecture asks for an analogue when the entries are finite integer combinations of
elements of a discrete group. The relevant determinant, introduced by Fuglede and Kadison [4],
uses the trace of a logarithm rather than a finite-dimensional determinant polynomial.
    Here are the conventions that fix this question. Let G be a discrete group and let Z[G] denote
its ordinary group ring of finite sums. Use the left regular representation

The integral in Equation (1.1) is finite and strictly negative. Thus the unrestricted group-ring

The exact existence input. A unital ring is directly finite if ab = 1 implies ba = 1. We use the
following scalar failure of direct finiteness from the complete characteristic-two companion.

Theorem 1.2 ([8, Theorem 1.1]). There exist a finite field K2 of characteristic two, a finitely
generated group H2 , and finite sums a2 , b2 ∈ K2 [H2 ] with a2 b2 = 1 and b2 a2 ̸= 1.

    The companion’s Section 6 specifies its field, group presentation and scalar sums by a terminating
finite prescription. We use the existence theorem, not an executed numerical instance or a group-
word decision procedure. The coefficient field is finite but is not asserted to be F2 . There is no
torsion-free hypothesis; the companion construction uses torsion. All steps after this exact input
are proved below. In particular, a result only in odd characteristic would not supply the binary
premise used here.

Context and mathematical ancestry. Determinant bounds are part of the theory of L2 -
invariants [7]. Approximation and closure arguments established such bounds for large classes of
groups, including amenable groups [9, Theorem 1.21, with its erratum]; Elek and Szabó proved the
determinant bound for sofic groups [3, Theorem 5]. Their proof uses finite graph approximations
and the integrality of products of nonzero eigenvalues of positive integral matrices. Our construction
instead starts from failure of direct finiteness in characteristic two. The cited sofic theorem implies,
as a separate consequence, that its resulting group is not sofic.
    The lower-bound question is distinct from determinant approximation. Kammeyer’s counterex-
ample to a finite-quotient limiting formula has determinant 2 over the discrete Heisenberg group,
while its principal congruence quotients of levels 3i , i ≥ 1, have determinants at most 51/3 [5,
Theorem 2 and Section 2]. That discrepancy does not violate the lower bound one.
    The finite-coordinate projection construction below is related to the commuting wreath-product
projections of Dicks and Schick [2, Section 3] and of Lehner, Neuhauser and Woess [6, Section 2]. Here
the projections separate one coefficient of a binary defect; we do not import a spectral-distribution
theorem from those works. The analytic step is the classical tracial logarithmic derivative [4, Lemma
2], also present in the path-integral determinant viewpoint of de la Harpe and Skandalis [1, Lemma
1 and Proposition 2(d)]. We give its proof and its application to block shears locally.

3    Lifting the factorization and preserving integrality
We now work with any group G, an orthogonal rational projection P ∈ Mm (Q[G]), and a factorization
of P̄ through r columns over F2 [G]. Assume that an odd integer D clears the denominators of P .
Here orthogonality means P 2 = P = P ∗ , where ∗ transposes the matrix and inverts group elements.
    For a power q > 1 of two, the trace gap suggests the absolute value of the operator we want:
expand r coordinate summands by q and contract the range of P by q −1 . Its logarithmic determinant
should be (r − τm (P )) log q, which is negative when τm (P ) > r. The difficulty is to realize this
determinant by an integral matrix. We will add block shears whose off-diagonal entries come from a
factorization of P modulo q. The analytic justification that these shears preserve the determinant is
given in Section 4.
    The first lemma lifts the binary factorization to every power q of two; only idempotence is
needed for this step.

P 2 = P and DP ∈ Mm (Z[G]). Suppose P̄ = uv over F2 [G], where u and v have respective sizes
m × r and r × m. For q = 2k , k ≥ 1, the reduction e = Pq has a factorization e = uq vq of the same
sizes over (Z/qZ)[G].

Proof. Since D is odd, every coefficient of P has a well-defined reduction modulo q. Set R =
(Z/qZ)[G] and choose arbitrary finite lifts u0 , v0 of u, v over R. In the corner ring e Mm (R)e, whose
identity is e, put

Modulo two, Z is P̄ uv P̄ = P̄ 3 = P̄ . Every coefficient of W is therefore even. A product of k such
coefficients is divisible by 2k , giving W k = 0. This is nilpotence in the even ideal of the corner ring;
the corner ring itself need not be nilpotent.
    Because Z = e − W , the finite geometric sum

                                       Q = e + W + · · · + W k−1

                                       uq = Qeu0 ,       vq = v0 e.

Then uq vq = Qeu0 v0 e = QZ = e. Only finite sums and products were used, so the resulting supports
remain finite.

    The target group differs from the companion’s group and contains the cyclic coordinates of order
three. The construction has a finite negative logarithmic determinant and no zero spectral mass; it
is a failure of the asserted lower bound, not of determinant class. Applying the sofic determinant
bound of [3, Theorem 5] to the positive integral matrix A∗ A proves that GD is not sofic. This last
implication is an external consequence, not an ingredient in constructing A.
