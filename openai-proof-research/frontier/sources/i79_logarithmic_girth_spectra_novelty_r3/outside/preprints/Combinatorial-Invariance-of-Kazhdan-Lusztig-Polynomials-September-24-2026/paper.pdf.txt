                                           Abstract
         We prove that an isomorphism of Bruhat intervals in arbitrary Coxeter systems
      preserves their equal-parameter Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials. This resolves the full
      combinatorial invariance conjecture positively.

1     Introduction
The defining recursion for Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials uses the simple generators of a
Coxeter system, while geometric and categorical constructions use root labels. An abstract
Bruhat interval poset retains neither kind of data.
   For a Coxeter system (W, S), write ℓ for its length function and ≤ for Bruhat order. For
u ≤ b, the interval
                               [u, b] = {x ∈ W : u ≤ x ≤ b}
is a finite graded poset of rank ℓ(b) − ℓ(u), even when W is infinite. Let Pu,bW (q) denote

the usual equal-parameter Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomial, normalized by Pu,u       W = 1 and
       W
deg Pu,b < (ℓ(b) − ℓ(u))/2 for u < b. The combinatorial invariance conjecture asks whether
isomorphic interval posets have the same polynomial. An isomorphism here is only a
bijection preserving and reflecting order; it carries no labels or root data. The following
theorem gives a positive answer in full equal-parameter generality.

Theorem 1.1 (Combinatorial invariance). Let (W, S) and (W ′ , S ′ ) be arbitrary Coxeter
systems, and let u ≤ b in W and u′ ≤ b′ in W ′ . If

                                      ι : [u, b] −→ [u′ , b′ ]

is an isomorphism of abstract Bruhat interval posets, then their equal-parameter Kazhdan–
Lusztig polynomials satisfy
                                     W              ′
                                   Pu,b (q) = PuW′ ,b′ (q).

    The statement includes infinite and noncrystallographic groups. Restricting ι to subin-
tervals also determines every polynomial Px,y on the interval; reciprocity then recovers all
its R-polynomials (Corollary 6.1).

1.1   History and the missing label data
Kazhdan and Lusztig introduced these polynomials through the canonical basis of the
Hecke algebra [27]. Their geometric interpretation identifies them with local intersection-
cohomology invariants of Schubert varieties in the Weyl-group setting [28]. Thus combinato-
rial invariance asks how much of an invariant initially defined by algebra, and subsequently
understood geometrically, is already recorded by a finite poset. The conjecture is attributed
to Lusztig and Dyer [11]. Dyer’s published formulation appears in the setting of finite
Coxeter systems in [17, Section 3.5]. His reconstruction of the directed Bruhat graph
from the interval order supplies all reflection edges, including edges that are not covers
[17, Proposition 3.3]. Section 3 gives a closure proof for arbitrary Coxeter systems. The
reconstruction does not supply root labels.
    A principal lower interval is an interval [1, b] starting at the identity. Special matchings
gave an order-theoretic approach to recursion on these intervals. Du Cloux developed an
abstract model for Bruhat intervals [13] and proved that isomorphisms of principal lower
intervals preserve all corresponding Px,y when one ambient group belongs to a class including
all finite and affine Coxeter groups [14]. Brenti established the corresponding result for
symmetric groups [7]. Brenti, Caselli, and Marietti proved it for arbitrary Coxeter systems
[8, Theorem 7.8 and Corollary 8.4], as did Delanoy [11, Theorem 7.2 and Corollary 7.3].
Their input includes the positions of x and y inside the supplied principal lower interval;
the general conjecture asks for Px,y from the abstract interval [x, y] alone.
    Caselli and Marietti more recently proved that every special matching of a Bruhat
interval in a symmetric group satisfies Brenti’s R-polynomial recursion [9, Theorem 8.6].

The same preprint leaves its proposed algorithm for all type-A intervals conjectural [9,
Conjecture 8.13 and Proposition 8.15].
     For intervals with arbitrary bottom elements, Incitti obtained short-interval results in
classical types [26]. Patimo proved invariance of the coefficient of q in finite simply-laced
Weyl groups [30]. Barkley, Gaetz, and Lam established the same coefficient invariance
for arbitrary Coxeter systems and deduced full invariance in interval rank at most six
[3, Corollaries 1.3 and 1.4]. Combining the flipclass approach with this coefficient result,
Esposito, Marietti, and Stella obtained full invariance through rank ten in finite Weyl groups
and rank twelve in finite type A [21, Corollary 1.3(ii)].
     Another approach uses hypercube decompositions of Bruhat intervals. Blundell, Buesing,
Davies, Veličković, and Williamson developed a recurrence for P -polynomials in symmetric
groups, guided by analyses of trained machine-learning models. They proved it for a
particular decomposition defined using the permutation labels of the vertices and conjectured
it for every hypercube decomposition of such an interval [5, Theorem 3.7 and Conjecture 3.8].
Barkley and Gaetz proved invariance of the normalized R-polynomial R            e for elementary
intervals in symmetric groups [1, Theorem 1.6]. They later proved the BBDVW recurrence
for every hypercube decomposition of a principal lower interval [1, v] in a symmetric group
[2, Theorem 1.3].
     Our approach uses the moment-graph form of the theory. The fixed-point descriptions
of equivariant cohomology developed by Goresky, Kottwitz, and MacPherson provide the
geometric ancestry of its polynomial congruence conditions [25]. Braden and MacPherson
constructed sheaves that recover intersection cohomology from moment graphs [6, Theo-
rems 1.5–1.6]. Soergel’s categorification [31], Fiebig’s moment-graph and Coxeter-category
constructions [24, 23, 22], and the character theorem of Elias and Williamson [20] make
the relevant sheaves available for arbitrary Coxeter systems over a suitable real realization.
Lanini studied invariance through isomorphisms of labeled moment graphs. Her pullback
statement compares Braden–MacPherson sheaves when the graph isomorphism includes com-
patible vertexwise automorphisms of a common label space: they carry edge labels to unit
multiples and agree modulo the edge label at neighboring vertices [29, Definitions 2.3–2.4,
Lemma 5.1, and Remark 5.1]. An abstract Bruhat interval isomorphism does not include
these label automorphisms. We retain the two realizations separately and compare images
in their sheaves through an order on edges.
     The reflection-subgroup theory of Deodhar and Dyer, together with Dyer’s reflection-
order theory, supplies the combinatorial inputs [12, 15, 16, 17, 18]. The reflection-path
formula already appears in his thesis [15, Theorem 6.23]. His reconstruction of dihedral coset
intersections by four-cycle moves [17, Section 3.4] precedes the relative-interval transport
argument below. Dyer also describes polynomials indexed by initial sections of a reflection
order and interpolating between q −d/2 Pu,b (q) and q d/2 Pu,b (q −1 ), where d = ℓ(b) − ℓ(u) [18,
Introduction]. His rank-two manuscript contains an integer model of the dihedral graph
and the planar length-sum identity used below, proves scalar congruence and intersection
statements, and sketches localization of projective modules along cosets [19, Sections 1.9–
1.11 and 3.16]. The proof here combines an exact later-edge count for transported relative
intervals with a bound for the two endpoint images in a sheaf. A reciprocal comparison
forces every such bound to be attained, with freeness supplied by the same induction.

both directions then proves polynomial equality.

therefore forces every intermediate bound to be exact, giving Ie = fe Je for every edge.

kernels and closes the simultaneous induction.

2     Real realizations and moment-graph sheaves
We first construct a real realization to which the character theorems apply. We then state
the reflection-order and sheaf inputs, ending with the two graded ranks that will be the
endpoints of the comparison in Section 6. All constructions are made separately for the two
Coxeter systems. All sheaf constructions use the coefficient field R, and linear forms have
degree one. Theorem and section locators for [23, 20] refer to the arXiv versions specified in
the bibliography.

2.1    Reduction to finite rank
Let J be the finite set of simple reflections occurring in a reduced expression for b. Every
element below b lies in the standard parabolic subgroup WJ , by the subword criterion for
Bruhat order [4, Theorem 2.2.2]. The Bruhat order on WJ is the restriction of the ambient
order [4, Proposition 2.4.1], and its equal-parameter Hecke algebra is the corresponding
standard subalgebra. The defining recursions therefore give the same R- and Kazhdan–
Lusztig polynomials on this subgroup. The interval graph also restricts. For an ambient
edge x → y with x, y ∈ WJ , its label t = yx−1 belongs to WJ . Strong exchange applied
to tt = 1 and a reduced J-word for t expresses t as a WJ -conjugate of a generator in J [4,
Corollary 1.4.4]; the converse is immediate. Consequently we may, and do, assume that
both Coxeter systems have finite rank. The groups themselves need not be finite.

possible dimension of such an extension. This is also minimal under the independent-root

which the construction attains. For distinct simple reflections with mst < ∞, their root

mst = ∞ impose no relation. The action restricts to the faithful geometric action on V0 [4,
Theorem 4.2.7], so it is faithful on V as well. The simple roots and their corresponding
linear forms are independent by nondegeneracy.
     For clarity, reflection faithfulness can also be seen directly. If an orthogonal transfor-
mation has rank-one difference from the identity, write it as g = 1 + u ⊗ φ. Orthogonality
first implies u♭ = cφ for some nonzero scalar c, and then implies φ(u) = −2. Thus g 2 = 1
and (u, u) ̸= 0: g is an orthogonal reflection. If g ∈ W , the finite-subgroup theorem for
Coxeter groups [10, Theorem 12.3.4(i)] puts ⟨g⟩, after conjugation, in a finite standard
parabolic subgroup. Its root span is positive definite, and its orthogonal complement in V
is fixed by that parabolic subgroup. The restriction of g to the root span still has rank-one
difference from the identity, and finite Coxeter geometry identifies it as a Coxeter reflection.
Conversely every Coxeter reflection has rank-one difference from the identity. This is the
reflection-faithful realization used in [20, Section 3.1].

A real coroot has either nonnegative or nonpositive simple-coroot coefficients. Applying this
observation to w−1 αs gives the displayed length-sign criterion. The same argument applies
to roots under the symmetric identification.
     The chosen ρ has trivial stabilizer. If w ̸= 1, take a left descent s of w; then (wρ)(αs ) <
0 < ρ(αs ), so wρ ̸= ρ. The identification of V with its dual therefore supplies a regular orbit.
The comparison constructions of [23, Sections 2 and 5] also use an orbit with stabilizer {1, s}

point outside their union exists over R and has stabilizer exactly {1, s}.

2.3    Reflection orders and their path formula
A reflection order is a total order of the positive roots whose restriction to the positive roots
in every rank-two root subsystem is one of their two angular orders. We shall use geometric
reflection orders. Choose a linear functional h strictly positive on the simple roots, and
therefore on every positive root. Choose another linear functional f such that the numbers

                                      f (α)
                                            ,       α ∈ Φ+ ,
                                      h(α)

are pairwise distinct, and order the roots by these numbers. Such an f exists: the root set
is countable, and each equality to be avoided cuts out a proper hyperplane in the space of
functionals. Within a root plane, the normalized positive roots α/h(α) lie on an affine line;
their order is consequently one of the angular orders. This produces a reflection order even
when the root system is infinite. Reversing it produces another reflection order.

Theorem 2.2 (Dyer’s reflection-order path formula). Fix a reflection order and use left
reflection labels on Bruhat edges. Let R    e x,y (T ) be the sum of T r over directed paths of r
edges from x to y having strictly increasing labels. The empty path contributes 1 when x = y.
Then

The same polynomial counts paths with strictly decreasing labels.

    The path formula is due to Dyer [18, Corollary 3.4]; its left-label version follows from the
right-label version by inversion. The decreasing statement follows by reversing the reflection
order. Every path between x and y lies in the finite interval [x, y], so all these sums are
finite. Finally, edges with the same reflection label are pairwise disjoint: a reflection pairs
each vertex w only with tw. Thus any schedule obtained by ordering reflection labels has
no interacting ties.

2.4    The sheaf and its full upward kernels

the set.

3     Dihedral intervals and transported reflection orders
We first establish the plane and graph facts that let an interval isomorphism transport full
relative dihedral intervals. For an edge, a nonnegative integer attached to each root plane
then measures a difference of later-edge counts; the sum of these integers is half the excess
of the edge’s length difference over one. Dyer’s reconstruction of dihedral coset intersections
by four-cycle moves [17, Section 3.4] precedes the transport argument needed for the later
sheaf comparison.

4    Localization at a root plane
We now establish the local description needed for the edge comparison. Localization along
reflection-subgroup cosets appears in Dyer’s nil-Hecke and projective-module approach to

a dihedral moment graph, and on that graph every indecomposable Braden–MacPherson
sheaf is a structure sheaf. We will also prove the resulting direct-sum decomposition over
the local coefficient ring; no preservation of indecomposability under localization is asserted
or needed.

stalks and edge modules are denoted by B a and B e , respectively. We use the generation,
flabbiness, and upper-end quotient properties from Theorem 2.3, with the normalization of
Proposition 2.4.
