1     Introduction
For a normed space Y , a vector x ∈ Y , and R > 0, consider the symmetric lens of displacements

                           L(x, R) = {y ∈ Y : ∥x + y∥ ≤ R, ∥x − y∥ ≤ R}.

When ∥x∥ is close to R, convexity restricts this set. In an infinite-dimensional space one can ask
for smallness after projecting away finitely many coordinates. We prove such an estimate for a
particular tree norm, with a bound determined by the squared-radius deficit R2 − ∥x∥2 of the
center.
    Approximate-midpoint lenses are central to asymptotic midpoint uniform convexity: Dilworth,
Kutzarova, Randrianarivony, Revalski, and Zhivkov characterized that property in terms of the
measure of noncompactness of suitable lenses [3, Theorem 2.1 and Definition 2.2]. Their framework
concerns finite-codimensional control of symmetric displacements. The estimate here gives explicit
control outside each finite ancestral set for the specified norm. Its proof uses disjoint tree segments,
following the norm construction introduced by James [5, p. 739].

    For comparison with the tree-space history, Girardi proved asymptotic uniform convexity (AUC)

is not imported from the binary-tree result.

1.2   The mechanism of the proof
Section 2 proves the coordinate structure locally, so no companion theorem is needed to read the
argument. The tail splits into an ℓ2 sum over the descendant subtrees starting just outside a finite
ancestral set.
    In Section 4, we represent points of each finite head unit ball by convex combinations of disjoint
segment functionals. A functional nearly norming the center makes the atom coefficients close
to its sums over the same segments. The difficulty is cancellation: two represented atoms can
have opposite sums when they leave the head at the same vertex. Their head segments are nested.
Splitting the longer one improves its squared segment test, so the opposite-sign product is bounded
by the amount that the two tests fall short of one. Section 5 proves this crossing comparison.
    The two endpoint distributions can be combined with a random sign. Keeping the coefficient
error and the unused head-test energy in one budget gives Theorem 1.1, proved in Section 6. The

                                                  2
same head controls every displacement, so finite-coordinate approximation yields an asymptotic
midpoint modulus uniformly over all centers.
    The obstruction to an equivalent AUC norm uses a different feature of the spaces. Every
root-path functional has norm one, while its child increments are unit coordinate vectors and are
weakly null. A positive one-sided asymptotic modulus would permit successive children whose
path norms grow by a fixed factor. This contradicts the uniform bound on path norms. The
forest supplies arbitrarily long finite paths; the infinite tree also permits an argument using the
supremum of all path norms. Sections 8 and 9 prove these assertions for the two completions
separately.

1.3   The other quantities measured by the methods
A second proof method works directly with primal tests. Clipping their cumulative sums along each
exterior path produces two perturbations with a quadratic norm cost. Testing the two endpoints
on these perturbations controls their average. Corollary 10.3 gives the resulting uniform gain
for perturbations in a suitable finite-codimensional subspace. The construction and its root-shift
variants occupy Sections 10–11.
    The later atomic arguments compare what survives when one changes the normalization or
retains more of the original representation. A common head test can control several endpoint
distributions with unequal weights or a nonzero center tail. A bound on their mixture does
not automatically give the same bound on each conditional law. Proposition 12.3, for example,
records separate coefficient errors and boundary

geometric constants: the radial bound ( 2 + 6) R(R − ∥x∥) of Proposition 12.1 estimates the
two errors separately, while the original-product route incurs a fourth-root loss. These calculations
record the losses of their respective methods. Sign selection and maximal-gain trimming instead
isolate which crossing contributions can be retained with a common sign.
    These estimates have metric consequences. The companion Distortion of countably branching
diamonds from midpoint and tree energies [6, Theorem 1.1] proves that Theorem 1.1 forces
D2 ≥ 1 + k/4 for embeddings of the depth-k countably branching normalized diamond. That
graph starts from an edge of length one and replaces each edge by countably many two-edge paths
of the same total length; distortion means a common ratio between upper and lower all-pairs
Lipschitz factors. The companion proves all infinite-family selections and graph recurrences, and
also preserves the independent radial-deficit route and direct energy methods.
    The broader question whether asymptotic midpoint uniform convexity implies an equivalent
asymptotically uniformly convex norm already has a negative answer. Baudier proved midpoint
convexity for the Kadets–Werner modification of the Bourgain–Rosenthal space, which has no
equivalent AUC norm [2, Theorem 1 and Corollary 1]. Here the finite-height forest is reflexive,
and the infinite-height coordinate predual is a different completion. Their exact identification
statements and local estimates are part of the proofs below. The numerical bounds are not claims
of optimality or priority.
