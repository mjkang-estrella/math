              An asymptotic formula for the number of totients
                                                   OpenAI

                                            September 25, 2026


                                                   Abstract
          Let V (x) count the distinct values of Euler’s totient function up to x. We give an explicit
      asymptotic equivalent for V (x). Its coefficient is a uniform limit of functions defined from
      finite arithmetic data. We also prove that V (cx)/V (x) → c as x → ∞ for every fixed c > 0,
      answering a question of Erdős and Hall.

1    Introduction
Euler’s totient function φ(n) counts the integers in {1, . . . , n} that are relatively prime to n. We
write
                        V = {φ(n) : n ≥ 1},     V (x) = #{v ∈ V : v ≤ x}.
Counting distinct values requires more than counting integers with a prescribed factorization:
different integers can have the same totient. This distinction is decisive in an asymptotic formula
for V (x).
    Throughout the paper logarithms are natural, and logj denotes the j-fold iterated logarithm.

The counting problem. Since φ(p) = p − 1 for primes p, the prime number theorem gives
V (x) ≥ π(x + 1) ∼ x/ log x. Pillai proved that the totient values have density zero [12]. Erdős used
the normal number of prime factors of p − 1 to sharpen the upper bound to V (x) ≪ε x/(log x)1−ε
for every ε > 0 [1, Section 2]. His later count of distinct values φ(pr) with p, r prime and pr ≤ x
gives the lower bound (1 + o(1))x log2 x/ log x [2, pp. 542–543]. Erdős and Hall obtained a much
larger factor exp{a(log3 x)2 } for every 0 < a < 1/ log 16 [6, pp. 1–3]. Pomerance brought the
upper bound onto the same logarithmic scale [14, Equation (1.4)]. Maier and Pomerance then
determined the leading constant [10, Section 1]:

Fixed dilation.     Erdős and Hall asked whether
                                    V (cx)
                                           −→ c        (c > 1 fixed)
                                    V (x)

in the final paragraph of [6, p. 3]. Erdős repeated the question in [4, p. 80]. Ford proved that
V (cx) − V (x) ≍c V (x) for every fixed c > 1 [7, Theorem 4]. This controls the number of values in
a fixed multiplicative interval, but neither it nor (1.1) determines the limiting ratio.
    We obtain an explicit asymptotic equivalent and prove the fixed-scale limit, thereby answering
this question of Erdős and Hall positively. The coefficient in the equivalent retains finite arithmetic
information. It is defined in Section 2 by bounded prime and integer sums, inclusion–exclusion,
and a uniformly convergent limit; its definition does not use V .

The source of the coefficient. Order the prime factors of a typical preimage from largest to
smallest, and separate a long prefix from a shorter tail. Ford’s normal-structure estimates put the
double logarithms of the large primes in a simplex. Most of that simplex can be counted by volume.
Near its last coordinates the primes remain discrete, however, and several tail factorizations may

                                                   2
give the same totient. We retain these tails exactly and take the volume of the union of the regions
that they permit. Inclusion–exclusion of this finite union produces the arithmetic coefficient.
    Erdős’s semiprime argument already controls collisions between products of shifted primes
[2, Lemma 1]. The quantitative shifted-prime normality and layered comparison used here were
developed by Maier and Pomerance [10, Sections 2–3] and Ford [7, Section 5]. Here the comparison
is proved under the hypotheses needed for the long prefix, with explicit costs for recovering the
tuple data inside the residual factors. The resulting control of all ordered prefix collisions, combined
with the exact finite-tail union, converts the volume into a count of distinct values. Counting
the largest prime with common endpoint parameters first gives regular variation. Identifying the
resulting mass with the arithmetic coefficient then gives the explicit equivalent. The errors are
uniform in the phase; neither conclusion assumes continuity of the coefficient.
    The prefix uniqueness used here is not uniqueness of the entire preimage: different arithmetic
tails may still give the same value. A separate result on extreme complete fibers gives, for every
fixed ε > 0, infinitely many positive integers v with #{n ≥ 1 : φ(n) = v} > v 1−ε [11, Theorem 1.1].
That result concerns large individual fibers, rather than the asymptotic number of distinct values,
and is not an input to the present proof.

Least preimages.       For v ∈ V, let

                                   ℓ(v) = min{n ≥ 1 : φ(n) = v}.

For each fixed positive integer k, the same passage of Erdős [4, p. 80] asks about the number of
values v ≤ x for which kx < ℓ(v) ≤ (k + 1)x. Section 7 gives a weighted asymptotic and proves
that this count has order V (x) precisely when some totient d satisfies ℓ(d) > kd; otherwise the
count vanishes identically. The positive alternative holds for k = 1, 2. We do not determine which
other integers k satisfy this seed condition. In particular, the separate question whether ℓ(d)/d is
unbounded, posed in [5, Section I.9], remains unresolved here.

2.4    Proof strategy
Two parts of the argument ensure that this construction counts values once. First, outside an
exceptional set, every preimage has the required form. Second, collisions between different long
prefixes have negligible total multiplicity. For the latter we use the layered shifted-prime method
of Maier and Pomerance [10, Section 3], in the form developed by Ford, and adapt his comparison
[7, Lemma 5.1] to a different set of hypotheses, proving the necessary uniform bound in Section 4.
The number of layers grows, but each sieve application concerns only two or three linear forms.
The comparison and the subsequent control of tuple multiplicities are the ingredients that turn a
volume estimate into an asymptotic for distinct values.
    The end of the continuous prefix and the end of the retained prime list are different cuts.
Their separation makes the exceptional-set estimates small enough to absorb the smooth tail.
Section 3 obtains the required preimages directly from Ford’s Theorems 10 and 16 and develops

                                                  6
the volume estimates. Section 5 proves prefix uniqueness and transfers tuple counts to distinct
values. Section 6 counts the largest prime using common endpoint parameters, obtaining fixed
dilation before identifying the arithmetic limit. Finally, Section 7 weights that prime count to
locate least preimages and proves the positive and zero alternatives.

4     Comparing products of shifted primes
We next bound the number of ways that two ordered lists of primes can give the same product
of their shifts. The largest prime factors of the shifts lie in separated intervals. We expose the
factors one interval at a time, starting with the smallest interval. At each stage only two or three
simultaneous prime conditions are needed. This keeps the constants uniform when the number of
intervals grows.
    The layer decomposition and the comparison of dual factorizations go back to Maier and
Pomerance [10, Section 3] and were developed further in Ford’s proof of Lemma 5.1 [7]. We prove
the version needed here, including its dependence on the number of layers. In particular, we
assume squarefreeness of the whole product above the last cutoff; squarefreeness of each individual
shift would not suffice for our factor-allocation estimate.
