    Theorem 1.1 resolves positively the qualitative Nagata–Biran conjecture for surfaces, also
called the qualitative Nagata–Biran–Szemberg conjecture. The threshold is allowed to depend
on the polarized surface. The conclusion concerns the nef boundary class of self-intersection zero.
Thus it excludes every curve whose ratio of degree to total point multiplicity falls below the
numerical bound, even when those multiplicities are unequal. The sharp threshold r ≥ 10 and
strict nonhomogeneous inequality in the plane Nagata problem at very general complex points are
a separate refinement, proved in [14, Theorem 1.1]. Corollary 5.5 extends eventual maximality to
uncountable algebraically closed fields of characteristic zero.

1.1    Background
Nagata formulated his plane conjecture in his work on Hilbert’s fourteenth problem and proved its
strict multiplicity inequality when the number of points is a square at least 16 [12]; see also [13,
Chapter III, Section 1, Proposition 1]. Multipoint Seshadri constants place that problem in the
geometry of arbitrary polarized surfaces. The qualitative formulation asks for equality in (1.1) for
every sufficiently large integer r; see Syzdek–Szemberg [18, Conjecture 4.3]. Strycharz-Szemberg–
Szemberg [17, Conjecture 2.1] formulate a stronger prediction specifying the threshold in terms of
a linear system on the surface.
    Biran’s stability theorem gives full symplectic packings for sufficiently large numbers of equal
balls, for rational symplectic classes on closed four-manifolds [2]; see also [3, Theorem 6.3]. Buse–
Hind–Opshtein subsequently proved strong packing stability, allowing unequal sufficiently small
balls, for all closed symplectic four-manifolds [4, Theorem 1]. The algebraic statement asks for
nefness on blow-ups of the fixed complex surface. Its packing interpretation uses Kähler forms, as


                                                      2
made precise by Eckl [8, Theorem 0.5]; symplectic packing stability alone does not fix the required
complex structure.
    On the algebraic side, Harbourne’s estimates include maximality for all sufficiently large r with
rL2 a square, as well as asymptotic lower bounds [10, Theorem I.1 and Proposition I.2]. The stated
results assume very ampleness; for an ample L, apply them to a very ample power aL and use
ε(S, aL; p) = aε(S, L; p). For these square values of rL2 , intersecting over C the countably many
open loci for bounds approaching the maximum gives equality at very general tuples. Assuming
maximality for the corresponding plane constant, Roé’s transfer gives the maximal bound when
the surface has a point with maximal one-point Seshadri constant [15, Theorem 3 and Corollary 7].
The multipoint product inequality, due to Biran for surfaces and stated in greater generality by
Roé–Ross, propagates maximality from a fixed configuration to suitable multiples of its size when
the corresponding plane constants are maximal [16, Theorem 1.1 and Remark 1.2]. Our argument
treats arbitrary ample polarizations and all integers beyond one threshold directly.
    There is also a distinction between eventual maximality and exact values on particular surfaces.
Dionne–Roth compute multipoint constants for substantial families of polarizations on P1 × P1 √   [6].
The recent preprint of Laface–Ugaglia [11, Theorem 1] claims the maximal irrational value 1/ 5
for O(1, 1) at ten very general points of that surface. These fixed-surface and fixed-point-count
results address a different question from the uniform eventuality in Theorem 1.1.

1.2    Proof overview
The proof turns the problem into finite interpolation on the algebraic torus (C× )2 . An injective
jet test of order m on a vector space of functions means that only the zero function vanishes to
order at least m at all the test points.
                                     p      We will show that, for every sufficiently large r and all
positive integers k, m with m > k H/r, the sections of kL admit such a test at some r distinct
surface points. The threshold in r must work for arbitrarily large jet orders. This uniformity is
different from the asymptotic postulation theorem of Alexander–Hirschowitz [1, Theorem 1.1 and
Corollary 1.2], where the degree threshold depends on a fixed bound for the point multiplicities.
    Section 2 establishes the transfer principle: a full-rank jet matrix for the initial Taylor monomials
remains full rank after a sufficiently small analytic rescaling. We then choose one integral divisor
V ∈ |dL| with an ordinary node. In local coordinates its two branches are the coordinate axes.
The intersection number kL · V bounds the least Taylor exponent of a section under weights (1, t),
after any copies of V are removed. The resulting exponents lie in a quadrilateral kQt . A change
of torus coordinates, followed by compression of the slices parallel to (1, 1), gives a triangle kPt
of area k 2 H/2 (Section 3). The slice compression has its own finite jet-matrix limit. Applying
these two transfers in succession carries an injective torus test for this triangle to sections on the
surface. Monomial diagrams and nonzero interpolation minors are central in Dumnicki’s diagram
method [7, Proposition 13 and Theorem 14]; the particular weighted-exponent bound and diagonal
compression needed here are proved in full.
    The next step uses the continuously
                                 p          varying shape of Pt . The equality area(Pt ) = H/2 makes
the torus jet threshold exactly H/r. For every sufficiently large integer r, we tune this shape to
a primitive lattice direction and choose a subgroup of (C× )2 with exactly r points. The subgroup
is the kernel of two monomial equations with exponent lattice of index r; it need not be a square
grid. The parameter t and the subgroup are fixed before the jet orders k, m are chosen. Projection
onto its characters reduces the jet test to p  a Laurent polynomial supported in a triangle of area
(kw)2 /2 and vertical range kw, where w = H/r. An elementary estimate using horizontal slices
bounds its multiplicity at the identity by kw (Section 4).

    Finally, the transferred surface tuple can depend on k, m, but injectivity is a Zariski-open rank
condition. Countably many such conditions give simultaneous tests at very general tuples. To
pass from equal vanishing orders to all curves, suppose that a curve has negative intersection with
the square-zero boundary class. Subtracting a small multiple of that curve and making a rational
perturbation produces a positive-square divisor beyond the boundary. Riemann–Roch makes a
multiple effective; adding back the curve yields a section with equal vanishing orders larger than
the critical slope, contradicting the jet tests (Section 5).

3      A nodal divisor and its exponent diagram
We now bound the initial exponents of sections of an ample line bundle by intersecting their divisors
with one fixed nodal curve. Varying the weight in the two branch coordinates produces a family
of quadrilaterals. The next subsection will compress these diagrams to triangles. The variable
Newton–Okounkov quadrilaterals of Ciliberto, Farnik, Küronya, Lozovanu, Roé, and Shramov [5,
Section 5.1 and Corollary 5.8] suggested this weighted-diagram approach. The nodal bound on an
arbitrary polarized surface and the diagonal compression used here are proved directly; the plane
corollary in that source is not a premise.

4     Finite subgroups as jet tests
We now find an injective jet test for the triangle spaces M(kPt ). The test points will form a
finite subgroup of the complex torus. Averaging over this group separates a Laurent polynomial
into components supported on cosets of a sublattice. In the coordinates of that sublattice, each
component lies in a triangle for which a one-point multiplicity estimate applies.

argument.
