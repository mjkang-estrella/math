                                                Abstract
         We give a uniform randomized classical algorithm for Subset Sum with bounded error and
      worst-case running time O(20.49n ) on polynomial-bit inputs in a word-RAM model, where n is
      the number of input integers. The time bound holds on every random execution.


1     Introduction
An instance of Subset Sum, with n ≥ 2, consists of positive integers a1 , . . . , an and a nonnegative
integer t. The question is whether there is a set I ⊆ [n] = {1, . . . , n} with i∈I ai = t. Equal input
                                                                               ∑︁

integers are allowed, and the empty subset is allowed. We measure the exponential part of the
running time in the number n of input integers, rather than in their total encoding length.
     Subset Sum is one of the foundational NP-complete problems, appearing as Knapsack in Karp’s
list [10]. Its simple formulation has made it a basic problem in exact exponential-time algorithms
and cryptanalysis. The meet-in-the-middle algorithm of Horowitz and Sahni [8] divides the input
into two parts, lists their subset sums, and searches for complementary sums. This gives the familiar
2n/2 time scale. Schroeppel and Shamir [17] preserved that time scale while reducing the space
exponent to 1/4. More recently, Chen, Jin, Randolph, and Servedio [6] obtained a randomized
worst-case running time O(2n/2 n−γ ) for a constant γ > 0.5023 in word-RAM and circuit-RAM
models with polynomial word length. A fixed reduction of the exponential rate below 1/2 is a
different objective from such polynomial-factor savings. We establish that improvement, with
ordinary O(20.49n ) running time, in the precise model below.

1.2   Related approaches
Two other directions explain both the progress on Subset Sum and the difficulty of a worst-case
improvement in n. Pseudopolynomial algorithms measure their complexity in the numerical target
t. Bellman’s dynamic-programming approach [3] yields O(nt) time. Koiliaris and Xu [12] improved
this bound deterministically, and Bringmann [4] obtained a randomized O(n ˜︁ + t) algorithm. Chan [5]
subsequently obtained a deterministic near-linear pseudopolynomial algorithm. These bounds are
particularly effective for small targets; polynomial bit length, however, permits much larger targets.
Here and in historical comparisons, O  ˜︁ suppresses polylogarithmic factors.
    Representation methods provide a different kind of saving. The method introduced by Howgrave-
Graham and Joux [9], and developed by Becker, Coron, and Joux [2], represents one solution by
many pairs of partial objects and then searches a filtered fraction of those representations. Their
improvements concern random instances, not a guarantee for every input. Austrin, Kaski, Koivisto,
and Nederlof [1] developed algorithms exploiting input blocks with many distinct sums through
representations, or blocks with few distinct sums through faster enumeration of those sums. Nederlof
and Węgrzycki [14] used related ideas to improve the space bound below the Schroeppel–Shamir
exponent.
    Related work concerns Subset Balancing: given integers vi and a fixed finite coefficient set
C ⊂ Z, find a nonzero x ∈ C n satisfying i vi xi = 0. Randolph and Węgrzycki [15] give worst-
                                              ∑︁

case exponent savings for several coefficient sets, though not for ordinary Subset Sum. Their
compatibility certificates recover valid representation pairs without inspecting all invalid pairs. Our
alias extraction uses the representation and distinct-sum perspective for a different task: listing
modular false positives so that their contributions can be removed from an estimated checksum.

    To analyze coverage of a fixed digit pair, we temporarily change the random-shift law at each

tests are chosen separately for each pattern. Grouping coordinates into blocks makes the number of
patterns and the cost of generating block tables subexponential. Comparing this auxiliary law with
the actual table law shows that, with high probability, every frequency appears in a match for its
own pattern. Two fixed inequalities for circle densities control the second moment. Section 3 states
these inequalities, and Appendix A proves them with rational enclosures and analytic quadrature
bounds.

record weights. A small auxiliary modulus reduces the record lists. The analysis proves a dichotomy:
either the preliminary distinct-sum test succeeds with high probability, or enough representations
survive to recover every alias with high probability. The enumeration omits every pair satisfying
the corresponding exact integer equation, including pairs with overlapping masks. This omission is
crucial: each remaining pair has a nonzero integer discrepancy, so a random-prime divisor bound
controls the number of pairs that pass the main modulus. Disjoint masks are checked only after
this bounded enumeration.

anti-concentration theorem for sums of phases. Section 6 assembles the procedures and proves
Theorem 1.1, including finite-precision arithmetic, capped random sampling, and a running-time
bound on every execution.


2     Isolation, distinct sums, and prime collisions
We first reduce detection to finding an isolated exact sum. We then give an exact preliminary search
that succeeds when a random half of the coordinates has sufficiently few distinct subset sums. The
complementary case supplies the combinatorial information needed later to recover modular aliases.
Finally, we record a random-prime collision bound for the nonzero integer discrepancies arising in
alias extraction.

The following application of the Isolation Lemma of Mulmuley, Vazirani, and Vazirani [13] allows a
complex-valued checksum to detect a solution without an anti-concentration argument. Choose a
power of two N0 with 12n ≤ N0 < 24n, and draw independent uniform integers ui ∈ {1, . . . , N0 }.

Proof. The identity c(I) = ts gives C(a(I) − t) = s − u(I). Since s, u(I) ∈ [0, nN0 ] and C > nN0 ,
this forces a(I) = t and u(I) = s. Conversely, every original solution represents the transformed

     Let F = {I ⊆ [n] : a(I) = t}, and suppose F ̸= ∅. Fix an index i and all tie weights other
than ui . The minimum tie weight among members of F containing i has the form Ai + ui , while
the minimum among members not containing i is a constant Bi . A missing family can be assigned
minimum +∞. If both families are nonempty, their two minima coincide for at most one value of
ui . Consequently, the probability that they coincide is at most 1/N0 .
     If u has two distinct minimum-weight members of F, they differ at some index i, and the two
minima just described coincide there. A union bound shows that the minimum is unique with
probability at least 1 − n/N0 ≥ 11/12. Its tie weight s then indexes an exact bin containing just
this subset, by the first part of the proof.

2.3   A collision bound for random primes
Reduction modulo a random prime makes any fixed false integer equality unlikely. The following
elementary bound is the only number-theoretic estimate needed for that purpose.

Lemma 2.5 (Random-prime collisions). There are absolute constants K and L∗ such that the
following holds. For an integer L ≥ L∗ , let p be uniform among the primes in [2L , 2L+1 ). If D is a
nonzero integer with |D| < 2B , where B ≥ 1, then

                                 Pr{D ≡ 0      (mod p)} ≤ KBL 2−L .

The same bound applies to the collision of two fixed unequal integers whose difference has absolute
value less than 2B .

Proof. The prime number theorem gives at least c0 2L /L primes in the specified interval for an
absolute c0 > 0 and all sufficiently large L. An explicit bound of this form is also given by Rosser
and Schoenfeld [16, Corollary 3]. The nonzero integer D has fewer than B distinct prime divisors,
since their product is at most |D| and each is at least 2. Dividing this count by the number of
available primes proves the claim. For two unequal integers, apply it to their difference.

below n∗ , divide the input into two halves, enumerate all their subset sums, sort one list, and search
for a complementary sum for each element of the other. This includes the empty subsets, is exact
on every input, and uses 2n/2 poly(n + b + 2) operations. Each original subset sum has at most
b + ⌈log(n + 1)⌉ bits, so it fits in a word. The two lists, their sorting buffers, and their indices need
only O(2⌈n/2⌉ ) one-word records; a direct mergesort implementation fits the specified address space
for every n ≥ 2. No high-precision complex arithmetic is used in this fallback. For n ≥ n∗ , the
polynomial storage used by the guard itself fits by the same log(n + b + 2) = o(n + b) bound as
above. Thus the program terminates and is correct on inputs outside the asymptotic guarded range
as well.
    Finally fix any positive integer c. For all sufficiently large n, every b ≤ nc satisfies the guard and
n ≥ n∗ . Substituting this inequality in (6.7) gives

                               K0 20.489995n (n + nc + 2)K = Oc (20.49n ),

because the fixed positive gap 0.49 − 0.489995 = 0.000005 absorbs every fixed polynomial. The
constants in this final comparison may depend on c, but the program, its cutoffs, its numerical coef-
ficients, and all its sampling rules do not. This is exactly the uniform polynomial-bit quantification
of Theorem 1.1.
