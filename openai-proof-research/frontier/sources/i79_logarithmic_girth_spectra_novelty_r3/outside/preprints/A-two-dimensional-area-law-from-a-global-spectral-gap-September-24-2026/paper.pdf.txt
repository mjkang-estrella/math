          A two-dimensional area law from a global spectral gap

                                                Abstract
         We prove an entropy area law for the unique ground state of a finite-range Hamiltonian on
     any finite induced subgraph of the square lattice. A lower bound on the spectral gap of the
     full Hamiltonian and fixed bounds on the local dimension, interaction range, and interaction
     strength suffice. For every set of sites, its entanglement entropy is bounded by a constant times
     the number of edges crossing its boundary, independently of the size and shape of the domain.

1     Introduction
The entanglement entropy of a pure quantum state measures how much information is shared by a
region and its complement. Its elementary upper bound is proportional to the number of sites in
the region. An area law replaces this volume by the number of interactions crossing the region’s
boundary. We prove such a bound for the unique gapped ground state of a general finite-range
Hamiltonian in two dimensions.

1.2   History and related work
The relation between entanglement and boundary area arose in quantum-field calculations motivated
by black-hole entropy. Bombelli, Koul, Lee, and Sorkin related entropy from tracing inaccessible
quantum-field degrees of freedom to black-hole area [11]; Srednicki subsequently found area scaling
numerically for a spherical region in the vacuum of a regulated free massless scalar field [36]. These
calculations motivated the entanglement area-law question through regulated field models. For
finite-dimensional lattice spins, the gap-to-area conjecture asks whether a unique ground state
has an entropy bound proportional to boundary area when local dimension, interaction range and
strength are bounded and the spectral gap has a size-independent positive lower bound [21, 8].
Theorem 1.1 gives an affirmative answer in the stated two-dimensional induced-domain setting.
    In one dimension, Hastings proved a length-independent entropy bound for the unique ground
state of a finite-range Hamiltonian with uniformly bounded interactions, fixed local dimension, and
a positive spectral gap [21]. Arad, Landau, and Vazirani improved the dependence on the inverse
gap for frustration-free chains using approximate ground-state projectors [8]. These are operators
that preserve the ground vector while contracting its orthogonal complement, with controlled growth
of Schmidt rank. Arad, Kitaev, Landau, and Vazirani extended this approach to general, possibly
frustrated chains [7]. A complementary result of Brandão and Horodecki derives an area law for
pure one-dimensional states from exponential decay of correlations between arbitrary separated
regions [13]. This region-wise hypothesis is stronger than decay of single-site correlation functions.
More generally, the exponential clustering theorem relates a spectral gap to decay of connected
correlations of separated observables [30]; an entropy estimate requires control of the reduced state’s
full spectrum. Anshu, Harrow, and Soleimanifar obtained boundary-dependent bounds on smooth
entanglement spread, which measures the width of the significant part of that spectrum, including
square-root boundary scaling on lattices up to logarithmic factors [5]. Such a width estimate leaves
the location of the spectrum, and hence its entropy, to be controlled.
    Strict area-law bounds in higher dimensions are also known for restricted model classes. Cramer,
Eisert, Plenio, and Dreißig proved such a bound for arbitrary regions in cubic harmonic lattices
with a finite-range positive potential matrix whose condition number is uniformly bounded [18].
These Gaussian models have infinite-dimensional oscillator sites and provide a comparison with the
finite-dimensional spin setting considered here.
    Other higher-dimensional results make additional spectral or thermodynamic assumptions.
Masanes obtained bounds with logarithmic corrections from correlation decay and bounds on the
number of low-energy states of restricted Hamiltonians [28]; Brandão and Cramer obtained a loga-
rithmically corrected bound for cubic regions under a low-temperature heat-capacity hypothesis [12].
Cho proved strict area scaling for balls along boundary-growth families satisfying uniform gaps and
an additional compatibility condition on successive reduced ground states [16]. These conditions
supply information beyond the gap of the single full Hamiltonian considered here.
    For two-dimensional spin systems, Anshu, Arad, and Gosset proved a subvolume bound for
rectangular regions in a rectangular lattice with plaquette projector interactions, a unique frustration-
free ground state, and a uniform local gap [3]. At fixed local dimension and local gap, the bound is
O(b5/3 log7/3 (2 + b)), where b is the rectangle’s boundary size. Their subsequent result gives

spectra on symmetric tensor powers using Schur–Weyl duality [19], whose connection with spectrum
estimation is developed by Keyl and Werner [22]. The passage back to one-copy vectors uses finite
quantum de Finetti measures [17, 25] and the product calculus of symmetrized finite observables [35].
Weighted operator geometric means [34, 24] combine the changing partitions, while the modular
comparison starts from the resolvent-compression identity of Carlen and Vershynina [14, Lemma 2.1].
The detailed construction uses these ingredients to compare entropy gain and excitation energy in
the same family of states; the next overview explains how that comparison leads to the area bound.

1.3   A tensor-network consequence
A projected entangled-pair state (PEPS) is specified by one tensor at each lattice site, with a physical
index for that site’s state and one virtual index for each incident edge. Contracting the paired
virtual indices gives a many-body vector. The dimension of an edge index is its bond dimension.
The approximation problem asks how large these dimensions must be to approximate the ground
vector on the original lattice.
    The companion [32, Theorem 1.1] proves a polynomial bound for the original open L × L
square grid, L ≥ 2, with on-site and nearest-neighbor interactions. For fixed local dimension q ≥ 2,
term-norm bound J > 0, and global gap ∆ > 0, every unique ground vector in this class has a PEPS
approximation with maximum bond dimension at most CLc and global vector error at most L−1
after normalization and phase choice. The constants depend only on q, J, ∆. This is an existence
result; it does not assert an efficient tensor construction or contraction algorithm.
    The approximation uses more than the entropy inequality. This paper also provides positive
operators that annihilate the ground vector and have rapidly decaying spatial tails (Proposition 4.3),
quantitative information bounds across collars (Propositions 9.6 and 10.2), and two families of
geometrically separated regions (Proposition 11.2), with the region shapes and clearances specified in
those results. The companion combines these estimates and the geometric ordering with additional
compression and routing arguments; its PEPS result is a consequence of that further analysis.

1.4   How the proof is organized
Fix the set A whose entropy is to be bounded, and let ZA be its crossing-edge endpoints. For
disjoint physical regions B, C, write

                             IΩ (B : C) = SΩ (B) + SΩ (C) − SΩ (B ∪ C)

for their mutual information. Our analytic goal is to make this quantity small when B lies inside
A and C is beyond a suitable collar, even when C includes all of Λ \ A. Here is why this suffices.
Suppose that, after removing a set D of O(|∂Λ A|) sites, we can partition A into two ordered families
of regions. If each region shares little information with the complement of A together with the
earlier regions of its own family, two applications of the entropy chain rule cancel all individual
region entropies. Purity then bounds SΩ (A) by |D| log q and the sum of the information errors.
Section 11 constructs regions with the required ordering and separation.
    The analytic estimates concern intersections of A with ambient boxes or finite unions of rectangles
and triangles. Their clearance is from ZA ; they may meet holes or the edge of the physical domain.
Hamiltonian locality and propagation always use neighborhoods in the induced graph. The argument
has five stages.

1. Obtain a first exponent below volume. Section 3 uses the global gap to bound fluctuations of
   the negative logarithms of positive marginal eigenvalues. It then optimizes products of positive
   operators on nested rectangles. Two bounds on the optimized norm, one using the energy estimate
   and global gap, give a conditional-entropy discount, and hence

                                   SΩ (A ∩ Q) ≤ Cr1+e0 ,      e0 < 1,

   for a box Q of side at most r sufficiently far from ZA .

2. Control information and energy in the same interpolation. Section 4 replaces the Hamiltonian by
   a sum of positive operators that annihilate Ω separately, retain a global gap, and have rapidly
   decaying graph-local tails. Sections 5–7 associate positive operators to partitions of the full
   system and interpolate between them as a small subsystem changes parts. These operators act on
   symmetric tensor powers, whose vectors are fixed by every permutation of the copies. Applying
   an inverse power of the interpolating operator to a vector built from ground-state copies and an
   auxiliary entangled pair defines the filtered vector whose norm we study. As the copy number
   grows, logarithmic norms per copy can be compared with combinations of marginal entropies.
   The decrease of a suitable logarithmic squared norm is bounded below by averaged conditional
   mutual information, up to a small error. The same transported states bound the energy cost.
   This common control permits a comparison of information gained with excitation energy spent.

3. Compare the norms and scan a collar. Section 8 gives two bounds on the negative logarithm of
   the filtered vector’s norm. Auxiliary dimensions are chosen from a high-probability part of the
   target’s Schmidt spectrum. Projecting the target and an auxiliary system onto an entangled pair
   gives the upper bound. The lower bound uses the global gap: low physical energy forces most
   copies into the ground state. Section 9 moves the partitions through a collar according to a finite
   randomized schedule. Each interaction is rarely split by a moving boundary, and the schedule
   supplies an interpolation with sufficiently low energy. The two norm bounds first improve the
   box exponent repeatedly; their sharper form then gives

                                  IΩ (A ∩ T : Λ \ (A ∩ TL )) ≤ Cn1−ε .


                                                  6
    Here T is one of the finite unions of controlled rectangles and triangles specified in Definition 9.3,
    ε > 0 is fixed, and TL is the ambient sup-norm enlargement by L = ⌊n1−ε ⌋. These targets and
    their allowed enlargements have O(n) boundary edges. The entire finite physical and auxiliary
    system and schedule are fixed before the copy number tends to infinity.
4. Amplify the information bound. Section 10 uses typical spectral subspaces to construct a candidate
   operator for restoring a target moved to an auxiliary reference. Its norm is controlled by the
   collar information. A localized product of contractions derived from the positive constraints
   suppresses the excited component, and polar decomposition yields a unitary comparison near
   the target. This unitary leaves both the reference and the remote physical system untouched;
   comparison with a state in which they are independent bounds their original mutual information.
   Enlarging the collar by a sublinear radius makes the bound smaller than any prescribed inverse
   power of n.
5. Arrange the regions for entropy cancellation. Section 11 builds the two ordered families described
   above. The collar around each region avoids the complement of A and all earlier regions of its
   own family. Narrow strips and recursive repairs at point contacts produce these separations for
   arbitrary cuts while leaving only O(|∂Λ A|) sites uncovered. The information errors are summable
   over the regions, so the two entropy chains prove (1.2).

Lemma 2.2 (A cut with no crossing edge). Under the hypotheses of Theorem 1.1, if ∂Λ A = ∅,

Proof. Such a set A is a union of graph components. Every support of finite graph diameter lies in

will be fixed in the order stated in the proof.


3     Marginal tails and an initial box estimate
This section supplies two inputs to the proof: a moment bound for marginal spectra and an entropy
bound strictly below the volume of a rectangle that stays away from the prescribed cut. The spectral
gap first controls the fluctuations of the negative logarithms of marginal eigenvalues. To bound the
entropy itself, we then optimize a product of positive operators on nested rectangles. An energy
estimate and a trial using a Schmidt vector give opposite bounds on the norm of the filtered ground
vector. Comparing them discounts the conditional entropy of the innermost rectangle. Averaging
the entropy chain rule over orders of small rectangles turns this discount into a subvolume bound.

3.1   A marginal spectral estimate
Boundary-dependent concentration of entanglement spectra was established by Anshu, Harrow, and
Soleimanifar through smooth entanglement spread, with square-root boundary scaling on lattices
up to logarithmic factors [5]. The estimate below instead controls the moment-generating function
of the marginal surprisal centered at its entropy. We prove it directly by tilting the ground vector
and comparing its excitation energy with the global gap.
    The following lemma does not require a lattice. Its dependence on the logarithm of each
interaction’s supporting dimension will also allow us to apply it to the truncated interactions

7     Transport through finite trees of metrics
The relative metric estimate of Lemma 6.3 measures the entropy gained by one change of partition.
We now combine such changes through a finite tree of matrix means. The resulting derivative is an
average of entropy gains against positive, trace-one states. The same states control the energy of
the filtered vector. A second occurrence of the probability that a term is split is essential in this
energy estimate. All trees, one-copy spaces, and partitions in this section are finite and are fixed
before the number of copies tends to infinity. The matrix means carry perturbations from leaves to
the root. The trace adjoints of their normalized derivatives carry a density matrix at the root back
to each leaf; this reverse passage will identify the states used in both estimates.

9     Scanning collars and improving the entropy bound
The preceding estimates compare the entropy gained by moving a small set across a partition with
the energy cost of changing its metric. We now construct a finite schedule of such moves. Random
initial offsets make any fixed interaction unlikely to cross a moving boundary; random choices of
local moves give each crossing interaction a chance to be moved in full. Together these two features
produce a small-energy interpolation. The rough norm comparison then improves the safe-box
exponent, and the sharp comparison gives a sublinear mutual-information bound across a collar.
    Fix the cut A, and write Z for its crossing-edge endpoints. Throughout this section, depth
means ambient integer sup-norm distance. Interaction supports and their truncations still use the
graph metric of Λ. In particular, a depth row is a set of sites at a fixed distance from the target,
and need not be a horizontal lattice row.

11     Two families of regions and the area bound
The amplified collar estimate controls the mutual information between one region and any collection
of sites outside its collar. To use it in an entropy chain, we need to divide A into two families: within
each family, the collar of a region must avoid all regions that precede it. We group cells in distance
layers into primary tiles separated by sparse strips. Filling the strips with suitable labels leaves
only point contacts between regions of the same family. We separate those contacts by recursively
replacing small squares around them. The sites left at the end of this construction, and all the
mutual-information errors, will be bounded by the number of cut edges.
    The construction takes place in the ambient plane. Its use of ambient distance only supplies
sufficient conditions for separation in the induced graph: every graph path of length r has ambient
displacement at most r. We never require an ambiently short path to exist in the domain.

Along each resulting elementary segment, the opposing region is either one primary or dummy tile,
or one elementary segment of another belt cell. A layer boundary cannot change in the interior of
such a segment: layer edges lie on the coarser dyadic grids.
    Join the center of each belt cell to the endpoints of its elementary segments. This makes a fan
of at most eight triangles, with edges of slopes 0, ∞, 1, −1. A triangle facing a primary or dummy
tile receives the opposite label. On a segment shared by two belt cells, assign any opposite pair of
labels to its two incident triangles, consistently on both sides. Within each belt cell merge each
cyclic run of consecutive triangles with the same label into one tile identifier. Distinct runs remain
distinct identifiers; if all labels agree, the whole cell is one tile.
    Every positive-length interface between distinct identifiers now joins opposite labels. Across a
cell side this follows from the assignment; inside a fan it follows from merging equal-label neighbors.
Primaries were already separated in the same layer, and adjacent-layer primaries have opposite
labels. Thus a same-label contact between distinct tiles can only be a point, and every such contact
involves a belt cell.
    Mark the center, the four corners, and the four side midpoints of every belt cell. Some midpoint
marks are unnecessary for its fan, but adding them simplifies the recursion. Deduplicate coincident
marks, assigning a mark v the scale Sv equal to the smallest side length of a belt cell that marked
it. There are at most nine times as many initial marks as belt cells.

Lemma 11.3 (Isolated stars of the initial mesh). There is an absolute constant a0 > 0, independent
of the layer and the chosen shifts, such that, on taking k0 sufficiently large:

  1. Distinct marks v, w satisfy ∥v − w∥∞ ≥ a0 max(Sv , Sw ).

  2. In the square v + [−a0 Sv , a0 Sv ]2 , all active boundaries are straight rays from v in the eight axis
     and diagonal directions. Each sector between consecutive active rays has one tile identifier,
     and consecutive sectors have opposite labels. If there is no active ray, the square is in one tile.

An active boundary here means an interface of distinct identifiers; seams removed by a merge are
not boundaries.

Proof. Consider a belt cell of side t = tk . In its 10t-neighborhood only its own and adjacent layers
can occur, by (11.6) and tk /2k → 0. All fine-cell sides there are t/2, t, or 2t. Cell corners, fan
centers, and side midpoints therefore lie on the affine mesh o + (t/4)Z2 . All active lines have one
of the four allowed slopes and intercepts on this mesh. Marks have local scales comparable to t.
Distinct marks have distance at least t/4, and a supporting line that does not contain a given mark
is at sup distance at least t/8 from it. The same bounds apply to the nearest other endpoint of
an incident segment. Choosing a0 small gives both assertions locally. Apply this argument at the
larger scale for a pair of nearby marks; pairs outside the local neighborhood already satisfy the

    The preceding coloring shows that two sectors separated by an active ray have opposite labels.
Coincident boundary segments or inactive seams are counted only once, so this describes the actual
tile regions near the mark.

11.6    Separation from earlier actual regions
Order tile identifiers as follows: all primaries first, all initial belt tiles second, and then the
replacement tiles by their generation of birth. Ties within each class can be broken arbitrarily. The
dummy tile is excluded from this order. Primaries already have the required same-label separation
from other primaries. The next lemma handles all remaining identifiers and explains why the order
uses actual earlier regions.

Lemma 11.5 (Birth-template separation). There is a fixed c1 > 0 such that the following holds.
Let B be the closed birth region of an initial belt tile of side scale u = tk , or of a replacement tile
whose removal square has halfside u. Then its distance from the final actual region of every earlier
or peer identifier of the same label is at least c1 uρ0 . A peer means another initial belt tile, or another
replacement born at the same generation, respectively.

Proof. At the birth of an initial belt tile, compare its closure with the primary and belt closures at
that time. At the birth of a replacement, compare it with the old remnants after that generation’s
removal squares have been cut out, and with the peer replacements. Each final actual region is a
subset of the corresponding compared region. In particular an ancestor is compared after its square
has been removed; its original birth region need not be disjoint from the new one.
    Restrict attention to a closed box obtained by enlarging the tile’s cell or removal square by 2u in
each direction. Points outside this box are at distance at least 2u from the birth region. Translate
by its cell corner, or by its removal center, and rescale lengths by u. Only finitely many local pairs
of closed regions can arise:

   • For belts, the nearby active lines and endpoints lie on the affine quarter mesh, with their four
     prescribed slopes. In a bounded box there are only finitely many such lines and endpoints.
     The local closures are unions of faces of their arrangement; allowing all possible identifier
     groupings still leaves only finitely many pairs of unions. We classify only the restrictions to
     this box: disconnected pieces of an identifier outside it do not change the local pattern.

   • For a replacement, the enlarged box lies in the isolated star of its parent, by (11.10). It
     sees only the old radial layout and the new square and sectors. These lie on a fixed finite
     arrangement in the rescaled coordinates. Other parents’ simultaneous modifications do not
     enter this box.

Thus there is a common positive scale for the isolation neighborhoods used in all these comparisons.
    The two compared closures of the same label have no positive-length interface. Their intersections,
if any, are contact marks by the belt construction or Lemma 11.4. At a belt contact the mark scale
is comparable with u (neighboring belt scales differ by at most two); at a replacement contact it is
u. Choose disjoint open contact squares of radius λu inside the isolated stars, for a fixed sufficiently
small λ > 0.
