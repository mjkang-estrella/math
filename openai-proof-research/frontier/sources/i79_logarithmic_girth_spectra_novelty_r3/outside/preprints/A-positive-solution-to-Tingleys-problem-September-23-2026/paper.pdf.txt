                                                    Abstract
          Every surjective isometry between the unit spheres of real Banach spaces extends uniquely to
      a surjective real-linear isometry, giving an affirmative solution to Tingley’s problem. If distances
      between different radii are not preserved, we realize the positive maximal defect in a possibly
      enlarged pair of Banach spaces. We then align extremal chords using common supports and
      Darbo’s fixed-point theorem and obtain a contradiction from support and convexity estimates.


1    Introduction
For a real normed space X, write SX = { x ∈ X : ∥ x ∥ = 1}, with the distance inherited from X.
Tingley’s problem asks whether every surjective isometry f : SX → SY extends to a real-linear
isometry of the spaces [21]. Any such extension must be the radial map below. This candidate
preserves norms and distances at each fixed radius; the difficulty is to recover distances between
points at different radii from the metric on a single sphere. We prove the full assertion.

Theorem 1.1. Let X, Y be nonzero real Banach spaces and let f : SX → SY be a surjective isometry. Then

is a surjective real-linear isometry. It is the unique linear extension of f .

    The spaces may have arbitrary dimension and need not be separable, reflexive, smooth, or
strictly convex. Applied to complex Banach spaces regarded as real spaces, the conclusion is real
linearity; complex linearity is not asserted.
    The problem belongs to the extension theory of metric isometries. Mazur and Ulam proved
that every surjective isometry between real normed spaces is affine [13]; Mankiewicz obtained an
extension theorem for surjective isometries between convex sets with nonempty interiors [12]. A
unit sphere has empty ambient interior, so this does not directly provide the desired extension.
Tingley’s original paper established preservation of antipodal points for surjective isometries
between finite-dimensional Banach-space spheres [21].
    Early positive results used the structure of concrete spaces. Wang treated surjective isometries
between spheres of complex C0 -spaces [24], and Ding obtained extension results for ℓ p (Γ)-type
sequence spaces with p > 1, p ̸= 2 [6]. It is useful to distinguish results for specified source and
target classes from the Mazur–Ulam property: every surjective sphere isometry from the given space
to an arbitrary real Banach target extends linearly. Cheng and Dong formulated this property
explicitly [3]. Tan proved it for real L p (µ) on σ-finite measure spaces when 1 < p < ∞, p ̸= 2 [19].
    Facial geometry provides another route. Following work of Cheng and Dong [3], Tanaka proved
that surjective sphere isometries preserve maximal convex subsets of the sphere [20, Lemma 3.5].
The operator-algebra program led to extension theorems between arbitrary complex von Neumann
algebras, due to Fernández-Polo and Peralta [8], and between their preduals, due to Mori [14]. Mori

                                                         1
and Ozawa subsequently proved the arbitrary-target Mazur–Ulam property for unital complex
C ∗ -algebras viewed as real spaces, and for real von Neumann algebras [15]. Our common-support
propagation has a geometric affinity with facial methods; the invariant set and its return-map
estimate are constructed directly below.
     In finite dimensions, Banakh proved the Mazur–Ulam property for every two-dimensional real
Banach space [2]. Other recent results concern specific spaces or sphere self-isometries: Fakhoury
proved extension results for sphere self-isometries of Schreier spaces and their p-convexifications
[7]. Huang and Zhu proved class-to-class extension theorems for Haagerup noncommutative
L p -spaces with 1 < p < ∞, p ̸= 2, associated with arbitrary von Neumann algebras [9], and
Schatten p-classes for 0 < p < ∞, p ̸= 2, including quasi-Banach cases [10].

Proof strategy. The argument measures the largest error in preserving a distance between two
radius levels. Its central geometric step turns a hypothetical positive error into two aligned chords.
Extremality propagates through common supports, producing an invariant closed convex set of
directions. On this set an opposite-endpoint return map contracts the Kuratowski measure of
noncompactness. Darbo’s fixed-point theorem then supplies an aligned direction. The proof-specific
work is the construction of this invariant set and the quantitative return-map estimate, followed by
the chord contradiction; the supporting-functional, ultrapower, fixed-point and affine-extension
tools are classical.
    Section 2 supplies the finite-dimensional supports and continuous radial contacts. Section 3
makes a positive defect attain its maximum in a new pair of Banach spaces while retaining the
bound at every radius. Section 4 constructs the invariant directions and applies the fixed-point
theorem. Section 5 eliminates the resulting aligned configuration: one parameter regime contradicts
maximality after a small radial perturbation, and the remaining regime contradicts a preserved
sphere distance by convexity. Section 6 deduces the onto radial metric extension and proves real
linearity by midpoint reflection. All auxiliary arguments are included, with the fixed-point proofs
in Appendix A. Norm subscripts are omitted when their spaces are clear.


2   Supporting functionals and radial contact
A support at a vector a in a finite-dimensional normed space is a linear functional φ with ∥ φ∥ ≤ 1
and φ( a) = ∥ a∥. All supporting functionals below may be taken on the finite-dimensional span of
the vectors under discussion. The next lemma is the finite-dimensional Hahn–Banach extension
argument [1]; we include its elementary proof.

6   The linear extension
Proof of Theorem 1.1. If the defect of the original sphere map were positive, Lemma 3.1 and Proposi-
tion 5.3 would contradict one another. Thus M = 0. For a = αx and c = βy, where x, y are unit and
α ≥ β > 0, put q = β/α. The radial map satisfies

                     ∥ T ( a) − T (c)∥ = α ∥ f ( x ) − q f (y)∥ = α ∥ x − qy∥ = ∥ a − c∥ .

Exchanging the points handles the other ordering; distances involving zero follow from norm
preservation. For b ∈ Y \ {0}, choose y ∈ SX with f (y) = b/ ∥b∥. Then T (∥b∥ y) = b. Hence T is a
surjective metric isometry fixing zero.
    For completeness we prove the affine step of Mazur–Ulam [13] in the short reflection presenta-
tion of Nica [16], who explicitly credits Väisälä and Vogt [22, 23]. If F : E → F0 is an onto isometry
of real normed spaces and a, c ∈ E, let

                   m = ( a + c)/2,       m′ = ( F ( a) + F (c))/2,      δ = F (m) − m′ .

The triangle inequality gives δ ≤ ∥ a − c∥ /2. Reflection R(z) = 2m′ − z is an onto isometry of F0 .
Its conjugate H = F −1 RF interchanges a, c and has midpoint defect

                                ∥ H (m) − m∥ = ∥ RF (m) − F (m)∥ = 2δ.

Apply the same construction repeatedly to the resulting onto self-isometries of E. Their midpoint
defects double but obey the same bound ∥ a − c∥ /2, so δ = 0. Thus every onto isometry preserves
midpoints.
     In particular T (( a + c)/2) = ( T ( a) + T (c))/2. Since T (0) = 0, first T (2a) = 2T ( a) and then
T ( a + c) = T ( a) + T (c). Rational homogeneity follows from additivity, and real homogeneity from
continuity. Finally, a linear extension is determined on every nonzero vector by its norm and its
unit direction, proving uniqueness.

Lemma A.2. Every continuous self-map of a finite-dimensional probability simplex has a fixed point.
Proof. The zero-dimensional case is immediate. Let P be a continuous self-map of the simplex
∆m = { a ∈ Rm+1 : ai ≥ 0, ∑i ai = 1}. Subdivide it into simplices, and label each subdivision vertex
a by an index i such that ai > 0 and ai ≥ P( a)i . Such an index exists: otherwise summing over the
positive coordinates of a would give 1 < ∑i P( a)i = 1. The label of a vertex lies among the vertices
of every original face containing it.
    Here is the required combinatorial fact, including its parity proof. In a finite face-compatible
triangulation of an m-simplex with labels 0, . . . , m satisfying that boundary condition, the number
of small simplices carrying all labels is odd. Count incidences of small m-simplices with facets
labeled 0, . . . , m − 1, modulo two. Internal facets count twice. Such a boundary facet can lie only in
the original face missing label m; by induction its number is odd, starting with the single vertex in
dimension zero. A small m-simplex contributes one incidence if it carries all labels, two if its vertex
labels include 0, . . . , m − 1 with one of these repeated, and none otherwise. This proves the claim.
    There are finite compatible subdivisions of arbitrarily small mesh: subdivide faces first and
cone their subdivisions to the barycenter. The vertices of each resulting simplex are barycenters of
a nested chain of faces. If two nested faces have k and l vertices, k ≤ l, their barycenters differ by at
most (1 − k/l ) times the original diameter. Thus a subdivision in dimension m ≥ 1 reduces each
diameter by a factor at most m/(m + 1). Repeat.
    In each subdivision choose a fully labeled small simplex. Compactness and the shrinking mesh
give a subsequence whose vertices converge to one point a. For every coordinate i, the vertex with

in every coordinate, so a = P( a).
