              A stable coordinate that is not a coordinate
                           in four variables

                                              Abstract
          We construct an explicit degree-five polynomial over C that is not a coordinate in four
      variables but becomes one after adjoining a single variable. This gives a counterexample
      to the stable coordinate conjecture in four variables. Every fiber is isomorphic to affine
      three-space, yet its embedding in affine four-space is not rectifiable. Thus the example
      also disproves the Abhyankar–Sathaye embedding conjecture in ambient dimension four.


1    Introduction
Let k = C. An element f ∈ k[x1 , . . . , xn ] is a coordinate if there are elements f2 , . . . , fn
such that
                          k[x1 , . . . , xn ] = k[f, f2 , . . . , fn ]
as a polynomial ring. Equivalently, a polynomial automorphism sends f to x1 . It is a
one-stable coordinate if it is a coordinate in k[x1 , . . . , xn , w], where w is independent. The
stable coordinate conjecture asks whether an element that becomes a coordinate after
adjoining finitely many variables was already a coordinate. The one-variable formulation
appears in [12, Conjecture 3]; successive applications give the formulation with finitely
many added variables. Over C, the answer is affirmative in two and three variables by [12,
Proposition 1.1(c)]. The case of one original variable is elementary. Thus four variables are
the first possible dimension for a counterexample, and one added variable already suffices.

Theorem 1.1. In R = C[x1 , x2 , x3 , x4 ], put

                 Q = x22 − x24 + x1 x3 ,
                                                                                   
                                              f = x1 − 2Q Q(x2 + x4 ) + x1 x4 .                     (1.1)

There is a C-algebra automorphism of R[w] sending f to x1 , but there is no such automor-
phism of R.

     The example also has a geometric consequence. A closed embedding An−1 ,→ An is
rectifiable if an automorphism of the ambient affine space takes its image to a coordinate
hyperplane. The Abhyankar–Sathaye embedding conjecture asserts, in characteristic zero,
that a polynomial g with k[x1 , . . . , xn ]/(g) ≃ k [n−1] is a coordinate; see [2, p. 8429]. Here
k [r] denotes a polynomial ring in r variables. Corollary 1.2 gives a negative answer to
this conjecture in ambient dimension four. In fact, every fiber is affine three-space and its
inclusion in A4 is nonrectifiable.

Corollary 1.2. For every λ ∈ C, the hypersurface f −1 (λ) ⊂ A4 is isomorphic to A3 and
its embedding in A4 is not rectifiable.

History and related methods
The coordinate question retains the embedding in affine space: it asks whether a given
function can be made into a coordinate projection by an ambient automorphism. Cancellation
asks instead whether an affine variety whose product with an affine line is an affine space
must itself be an affine space. Makar-Limanov, van Rossum, Shpilrain, and Yu established
an implication from the stable coordinate conjecture to cancellation in [10, Theorem 1.2]. In
our example the ambient variety is already A4 , and the obstruction concerns its distinguished
polynomial.
    The low-dimensional proof in [12, proof of Proposition 1.1(c)] uses curve cancellation
and the Abhyankar–Moh embedding theorem in two variables. In three variables it combines
affine-surface cancellation, using work of Fujita and Miyanishi–Sugie, with Kaliman’s theorem
that a polynomial on C3 with general fiber C2 is a coordinate [6, Main Theorem].
    The four-variable Vénéreau polynomials have been an important testing ground for these
coordinate questions. Lewis proved that the second Vénéreau polynomial is a coordinate
[9]. Blanc and Poloni developed a description in terms of affine bundles and bivariables,
revisited that result, and proved one-stability for a broader family of related polynomials
[1, Proposition 2.3]. These results illustrate the distinction between exhibiting polynomial
coordinates after stabilization and proving that a polynomial cannot occur in any original
coordinate system. For the polynomial in Theorem 1.1, the first problem is settled by
explicit substitutions; the second requires a derivation obstruction.

The construction and the obstruction
The proof uses two presentations of the same four-variable polynomial ring. We first write
it as a hypersurface A = P/(H) in a five-variable polynomial ring P , with a distinguished
element p. A determinant-preserving matrix transformation makes H linear in one new
coordinate. It identifies A with R and carries p to the polynomial in (1.1). A second
construction, using the exponential of a locally nilpotent derivation, shows that p belongs
to polynomial coordinates on A[w]. These explicit changes of coordinates occupy Section 2.
    To show that p is not a coordinate, we put an integer-indexed filtration on A. Its
associated graded ring has the form

              G = B[τ, I/τ ] ⊂ B[τ, τ −1 ],     B = k[x, y, z, u]/(xy − z(z + 1)),

where I is an explicitly defined two-generated ideal of B and τ is the initial form of p. The
notation B[τ, I/τ ] means the subalgebra generated by B, τ , and all elements h/τ with h ∈ I.
If p were a coordinate, differentiation in a suitable other coordinate would induce a nonzero
locally nilpotent derivation E on B whose kernel contains a nonzero element of I. Section 3
proves this implication, including the care needed for negative filtration degrees.
    The last two sections exclude that invariant. Section 4 passes from Spec B to the
complement of the zero section in a line bundle. In its coordinate ring

                               C = k[a, d, b, c, u]/(ac − bd − 1),



                                                2
the ideal I becomes principal, generated by v = a3 b + a2 u2 − d2 . The additive action defined
by E lifts to this bundle and commutes with fiber scaling. A nonzero invariant in I would
force the lifted derivation to annihilate v. In Section 5, a second grading reduces this to a
derivation fixing a, d, u. Such a derivation has a coefficient that would simultaneously have
to be a polynomial in a, d, u and have negative fiber weight. This is impossible.
    The graded-ring and line-bundle strategy adapts the construction in [11, Sections 2–
6]. That paper studies an affine-space cancellation example. Here the quadratic choice
in the defining equation has two different uses: it permits the determinant-preserving
change that makes A polynomial, and it makes the highest auxiliary part of v factor as
(au − d)(au + d). The latter factorization gives the final rigidity argument without a
polynomial abc estimate. The passage to leading derivations follows the filtered methods
of Kaliman and Makar-Limanov [7, Sections 3–5], and the lift of an additive action uses
the line-bundle normalization argument of Brion [3, Lemma 2.9]. Every construction and
obstruction needed for Theorem 1.1 is proved below.

3     A graded obstruction to coordinateness
We now turn to the assertion that p is not a coordinate of A. The first step is to pass to a
weighted degeneration of A. If p were a coordinate, differentiation in another coordinate
would then produce a nonzero locally nilpotent derivation of a simpler quadric ring, with a
nonzero invariant in an explicit ideal. The rest of the proof will exclude that possibility. The
use of leading derivations follows the filtration methods of [7, Sections 3–5]; the particular
quadric construction is adapted from [11, Sections 3–5].

3.1   The associated graded ring
Give the generators of P = k[p, s, u, F, J] the weights

                                          p s u F J

                                  weight −1 0 0 1 1.

Proposition 3.1. The filtration on A is exhaustive and separated. Its associated graded

 G := gr A ≃ k[p, s, u, F, J]/(H∗ ),      H∗ = x2 F −(1+2sx)J −pJ 2 ,    x = s2 −u2 +pF, (3.2)

−1, 0, 0, 1, 1.
Proof. Exhaustiveness follows because every element of A has a polynomial representative.

                                          F−r A ⊆ pr A.

                                deg a = min{d ∈ Z : a ∈ Fd A}

vanish. Consequently the ideal of initial relations is exactly (H∗ ). For completeness, the
natural graded map P → gr A sends a homogeneous polynomial of weight d to its class

polynomial as its highest part. Thus the kernel is the ideal of initial relations, proving (3.2).

                                       pH∗ = xy − z(z + 1).

   The lifting argument has a useful general form. Its normalization step is the standard
one for linearizing a line bundle under an additive action; compare [3, Lemma 2.9].

Lemma 4.4. Let X be an affine integral k-variety such that every line bundle on A1 × X
is the pullback of its restriction at zero. Every Ga -action on X lifts to any line bundle on X
by maps linear on fibers, commuting with fiber scaling.

Proof. Write R = k[X], let M be the line bundle, and let α : A1 × X −→ X be the action.
If π : A1 × X −→ X is projection, then α∗ M and π ∗ M restrict to M at parameter zero.
The hypothesis therefore supplies an isomorphism

                                      η : π ∗ M −→ α∗ M.

At zero, η is an automorphism of M, hence multiplication by a unit of R. Multiplying η by
the inverse pullback of that unit normalizes it to the identity at zero.
    The isomorphism η gives a fiberwise linear lift of α. To check the group law, compare,
over A2 × X, the map on fibers obtained by applying parameters S and then T with the map
obtained by applying T + S. Both are global isomorphisms of the same source and target
line bundles, with fiber maps from Mx to Mα(T +S,x) . Their ratio is an automorphism of
the source line bundle. Its endomorphism sheaf is canonically O, so this automorphism is
multiplication by a global unit in R[T, S]. Since R is a domain, this unit is independent of
T, S. At (T, S) = (0, 0) it equals one by the normalization of η. Thus the group law holds.
The resulting action is linear on fibers, so it commutes with fiber scaling and preserves the
complement of the zero section.

The element q is nonzero, since otherwise E ′ would kill every generator. We claim that q
belongs to the polynomial subring k[a, d, u].
    The elements a, d, u are algebraically independent by (5.3). Put K = k(a, d, u) and
localize C at the nonzero elements of k[a, d, u]. Because these elements lie in ker E ′ ,
the localized derivation remains locally nilpotent. The resulting algebra is K[b], with
c = (1 + bd)/a. A locally nilpotent K-derivation of K[b] sends b into K: if its value P (b)
had degree at least one, iterating P (b) d/db on b would never give zero. More explicitly,
differentiation of a nonconstant polynomial multiplies its nonzero leading coefficient by its
positive degree, and multiplication by P then leaves the result nonconstant. Equation (5.4)
therefore gives q ∈ K.
    To compute C ∩ K inside the fraction field of C, use both coordinate charts:

                  C[a−1 ] = k[a, a−1 , d, u][b],        C[d−1 ] = k[a, d, d−1 , u][c].

An element of the first polynomial ring lying in K has no b terms, and an element of the
second lying in K has no c terms. Hence

                     C ∩ K ⊂ k[a, a−1 , d, u] ∩ k[a, d, d−1 , u] = k[a, d, u].

The last equality follows from unique factorization in k[a, d, u]: a reduced denominator that
is both a power of a and a power of d is a unit. The reverse inclusion is immediate, proving
the claim.
     Finally, preservation of fiber weights makes E ′ (b) have weight −1. By (5.4), the nonzero
polynomial q has weight −2. This is impossible in k[a, d, u], whose generators have weights
1, 1, 0. The contradiction proves the proposition.
