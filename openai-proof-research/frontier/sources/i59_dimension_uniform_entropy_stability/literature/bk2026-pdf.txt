                                                                                 1. Introduction and main results
                                               The topic of almost commuting matrices dates back to Rosenthal [23] and Halmos [18], with the
                                           original question that can be traced back to von Neumann [26, 27]. Suppose, X and Y are two self-
                                           adjoint matrices such their commutator [X, Y ] is small in some way. Can one find commuting matrices
                                           X ′ , Y ′ that are close to X and Y , respectively, in the same way? Many versions and generalizations of
                                           this question exist, depending on the notion of smallness and specific types of matrices, with two main
                                           paths to obtaining positive results. Abstract approaches usually involve proofs by contradiction. That
                                           is, non-existence of a dimension-independent estimate leads to an element with impossible properties
                                           in a certain C ∗ -algebra associated to sequences of matrices. Quantitative approaches, on the other
                                           hand, usually deal with the original matrices directly, in a more model-specific way, but are often still
                                           motivated by C ∗ -algebraic intuition.
                                               In the case when smallness/closeness is understood in terms of the operator norm, a major break-
                                           through was obtained by Lin in [22], resulting in an abstract approach implying the the existence of
                                           a dimension-independent distance estimate to commuting pairs in terms of ∥[X, Y ]∥. The proof was
                                           later simplified in [8]. A quantitative result demonstrating existence of X ′ , Y ′ such that
                                           (1.1)                                 ∥X − X ′ ∥ + ∥Y − Y ′ ∥ ≤ C∥[X, Y ]∥1/2
                                           was obtained much later in [21] (see also [17, 19]), where the exponent 1/2 cannot be improved further.
                                             The original question of Rosenthal was posed in terms of the Hilbert – Schmidt norm. For the
                                           purpose of dimension-independent estimates, one naturally arrives to normalized Hilbert–Schmidt norm

                                           The first result that matches Lin’s theorem in this setting was obtained in [13] (see also [16, 14, 15]), in
                                           a bigger generality that allows to deal with an arbitrary finite number of almost commuting matrices.
                                           An independent proof for two matrices was obtained in [7]. In both cases, the proofs are of an
                                           abstract existence-only kind. The first quantitative result involving the norm (1.2) was obtained in
                                           [11], providing an analogue of (1.1) with the exponent 1/6 in the right hand side. The exponent was
                                           later improved to 1/4 in [7] (see also [24, 9]). In these results, the key feature is that X ′ commutes
                                           with X, which allows to iterate the construction in order to extend the estimates to a larger number
                                           of almost commuting matrices with meaningful distance estimates.

obtained in our Theorem 1.3.

1.2. Three or more matrices. The complexity of operator norm version of the Rosenthal – Halmos
problem increases drastically, once one departs from the case of two self-adjoint matrices. Indeed,
topological obstructions appear in the case of two unitary matrices [25, 4, 12, 3] as well as in the case of
three self-adjoint matrices [2, 10], and the situation is only expected to become harder in more involved
cases. On the other hand, as first observed in [13], many of these obstructions do not appear in the
case of the normalized Hilbert – Schmidt norm (however, we refer the reader to [1, 20] for some more
delicate cases). As was noted in [11, Theorem 3] (see also [5, Theorem 3]), the constructions of [11]
and [5] can be iterated in order to obtain quantitative bounds for arbitrary number of mutually almost
commuting matrices.
   The above iterations rely on the fact that the operator X ′ in [11, 5] does not depend on Y , which
does not apply directly to our case of Theorem 1.1. However, one can observe by inspecting the proof
of Theorem 1.1 that the discrete optimization part of the proof can be modified in order to treat a
k-tuple of matrices Y instead of a single one, as follows.

1.3. Structure of the paper. In Section 2 we show that, if one additionally prescribes the mul-
tiplicities of the eigenvalues of X ′ , then the of X ′ and Y ′ can be obtained by an explicitly solvable
optimization problem, thus reducing the original question to optimizing the choice of said multiplicities,
which is a discrete optimization problem.
   In Section 3 we discuss two technical lemmas, which collectively conclude that, without loss of
generality one can assume that X has no large spectral gaps.
   In Section 4 we prove the main result, Theorem 1.1. The main idea is based on carefully estimating
a particularly chosen weighted average of the solution candidates to the discrete optimization problem,
thus providing an upper bound on the actual minimizer.
   In Section 5, we use the ideas obtained from Section 2 to prove Theorem 1.2, which provides a lower
bound on the exponent in Theorem 1.1.
   As mentioned in the previous subsections, the proof of Theorem 1.3 is contained in Remarks 2.2,
3.5, and 4.1 With all the preparations, the proof of Corollary 1.4 is a straightforward consequence
of the iteration argument described in the same subsection. We refer the reader to [5] for additional
details.
