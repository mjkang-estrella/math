                                        An FPRAS for Counting Common Bases of Two Matroids

                                                                         Xiaoyu Chen∗                  Kuikui Liu†


                                                                                           Abstract
                                                 We design the first polynomial-time algorithms for approximately counting and uniformly
                                              sampling common bases of two matroids given by their independence oracles. Moreover, our
                                              algorithms generalize far beyond this to Hadamard products of two probability measures on the
                                              Boolean cube satisfying a simple nonnegative curvature condition. These algorithmic primitives




arXiv:2610.06724v1 [cs.DS] 5 Oct 2026
                                              have myriad applications in statistical physics, polyhedral combinatorics, the study of quantum
                                              many-body systems, and beyond.
                                                 Our approach has two key ingredients.
                                               • We relax the intersection by imposing an overlap penalty on the product measure formed by
                                                 the two input measures. We prove, via an integrated Bochner-type method, that this “soft
                                                 intersection” satisfies a Poincaré Inequality uniformly over all external fields.
                                               • We solve a dual maximum entropy convex program to compute external fields under which
                                                 the hard constraint is satisfied with high probability under the soft intersection measure. We
                                                 bound this success probability directly using the uniform Poincaré Inequality and smallness
                                                 of the gradient norm.

                                              in this paper. A more complete discussion is included in Section 1.5.

1     Introduction
In this paper, we design the first polynomial-time algorithms that solve the following fundamental
problems in full generality: Let M, N be two matroids with the same rank r on a common ground
set U of size n, both given via an independence oracle, and write B(M) (resp. B(N )) for the
collection of bases of M (resp. N ); for background on matroids, see Section 3.2.
       Approximate Counting: Estimate the number |B(M) ∩ B(N )| of common bases up to (1 ± ε)-
       multiplicative error, for any desired target accuracy 0 < ε < 1. Note that exact counting is
       #P-hard and hence, unlikely to admit an efficient algorithm [Val79].
       Approximate Sampling: Sample a common basis from the uniform distribution over B(M) ∩
       B(N ) up to ±ε-additive total variation error, for any 0 < ε < 1.
These are fundamental algorithmic problems in theoretical computer science that have abundant
applications in statistical physics, high-dimensional statistics, and combinatorics; see Appendix C
for a more comprehensive discussion.
    Counting and sampling common bases of two matroids has received significant attention in the
literature over the past several decades. For the moment, we highlight two landmark results that
previously solved important special cases of the problem. Jerrum–Sinclair–Vigoda designed the
first polynomial-time algorithms for counting and sampling (edge weighted) perfect matchings
in bipartite graphs [JSV04]. As a result, they also obtained the first FPRAS for estimating the
permanent of any nonnegative matrix. Anari–Liu–Oveis Gharan–Vinzant gave the first polynomial-
time algorithms for counting and sampling bases of a single matroid [Ana+19]. Both results are
based on the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method.
    Our algorithms are also based on MCMC and hence, are randomized. We note that randomiza-
tion is necessary when the matroids can only be accessed via their independence oracles. Indeed,
even for a single matroid M, no efficient deterministic algorithm can approximate |B(M)| to within
a simply exponential multiplicative factor in the oracle model [ABF94]. This lower bound is nearly
matched by the deterministic 2O(r) -approximation algorithm of Anari–Oveis Gharan–Vinzant
[AOV18], which also applies to common bases of two matroids.

time randomized approximation scheme (FPRAS) for Z is a randomized algorithm that, given the
instance and an accuracy parameter 0 < ε ≤ 1, returns Zb ≥ 0 satisfying

Theorem 1.2 (Counting Common Bases). There is an FPRAS that, given independence oracles for two
matroids M, N on a common ground set U , estimates the number |B(M) ∩ B(N )| of common bases.
Remark 1.3. The running time of our algorithm is O    e (n13 /ε2 ), which certainly isn’t optimal. Since
our goal is to present our algorithms and proofs in the simplest way possible, we made no attempt
to optimize the running time.
    All problems we consider in this paper will be self-reducible in the sense of [JVV86]. Hence,
approximate counting and sampling are equivalent under polynomial-time reductions and we will
move fluidly between them. In particular, Theorem 1.2 also implies a polynomial-time algorithm
for approximately sampling a uniformly random common basis.
    As a special case, Theorem 1.2 gives an FPRAS for counting strong orientations and undirected Euler
tours, resolving the approximate counting questions in [Jer24, Section 3.5] and [GŠ10], respectively;
see Appendices C.1 and C.2. A variant of Theorem 1.2 for weighted common bases (proved below)
applies to network reliability, where the goal is to estimate the probability of directed root-to-all
reachability, or of an unpaired vertex-disjoint linkage between two terminal sets, under independent
edge failures; see Appendices C.3 and C.4. The former settles the general directed reachability
question in [GJ19, Section 7]; the latter extends the two-terminal result of [FFG26] to multiple
disjoint paths. Finally, we mention that Theorem 1.2 also yields a new FPRAS for perfect matchings
in bipartite graphs (and the permanent of nonnegative matrices more generally).

1.4   Prior Work
Trickle-down and field dynamics. Trickle-down arguments grew out of earlier local-to-global
ideas [Gar73; KM17; DK17] and establish rapid mixing of “global” random walks by analyzing
related “local” random walks, which are usually easier to study [Opp18; KO20; AL20; Ana+19;
ALO20]. Refinements and extensions appear in [GM20; ALO21; AO23; GK23; AKV24; LLO25;
LO25; GZ26; BLO26]. Related sampling results appear in [CLV23; CLV21; Fen+22; Bla+22; Liu21;
Ana+24]. Field dynamics [Che+24] provides a mechanism for reducing sampling from a “hard”
distribution to sampling from relatively “easier” conditional distributions with smaller external
fields. This approach and related methods [Eld13; EKZ22; CE22; Che+22] have led to rapid-mixing
results for the hardcore model (and more general antiferromagnetic two-spin systems) in the
tree-uniqueness regime and at the uniqueness threshold [Che+25b; Che+26b]. Our curvature
condition builds on top of a criterion that lies between these two lines of work [Che+25a].

Bochner-Type Methods. Bochner-type methods bound the spectral gap of random walks by
relating it to the local “curvature” of the underlying space. The terminology originates from
Bochner’s formula in Riemannian geometry [Boc46; Lic58], and its application to continuous
diffusions in high-dimensional probability is classical [BÉ85]; see also [BGL14]. Adaptations of
these techniques to discrete spaces appear in [Bou+06; CP07; Cap08; CDP09; KKO13; DP13; Joh17;
JY17; WZ21; Ped25]. A recent flurry of work, starting with [Göb+26], applies a variant of this
method and closely related arguments for spin systems on random regular and planar graphs
[Göb+26; GZ26], the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick model [Wan26; BLO26], graph colorings [CL26a;
Haq26], and “ultra-log-concave” distributions [CW26]. It also gives simpler proofs of known
trickle-down results; see [CL26b] and references therein. Our proof of Theorem 1.14 uses this
method.

Geometry of polynomials. Properties of generating polynomials have been used to prove in-
equalities for combinatorial counts and probabilities [BBL09; Gur09; Huh12; HK12; HW17; AHK18;
AOV18; Ana+18; BH20].
   Gurvits capacity and related entropy maximization programs connect these inequalities to
approximate counting and optimization [Gur06; Gur07; NS16; SV17; GL21]. Applications include
permanents [LSW00; Gur06], bases and common bases of matroids [AO17; SV17; AOV18], contin-
gency tables and integer flows [Bar09; BLP23], and lattice points in bounded totally unimodular


                                                      8
polytopes [LM26]. These methods often give polynomial-time algorithms with exponential ap-
proximation ratios. Recent work has improved deterministic approximation guarantees for the
permanent [Ana26; KLM26; DJ26].
   For sampling and randomized counting on matroids, earlier algorithms covered special classes
[FM92; JS02; AOR16]. Later advances based on log-concavity handled general matroids and
improved the running times [Ana+19; CGM21; Ana+21; AD20].

Annealing and adjusting weights. The Markov-chain approach to approximating the permanent
was initiated by Broder [Bro86] and developed by Jerrum and Sinclair [JS89]. Jerrum, Sinclair,
and Vigoda [JSV04] gave the first FPRAS for the permanent of an arbitrary nonnegative matrix.
This algorithm and subsequent FPRASes for the permanent [Bez+08; CVY26; Che+26a] and
two-terminal reliability [FFG26] add extra configurations to make sampling easier, and gradually
change the input weights (annealing; see [ŠVV09; Guo+26]) while learning weights that keep
desired configurations, such as perfect matchings, from becoming rare. Those algorithms learn
correction weights along a carefully designed annealing path; our method chooses fields directly by
stochastic gradient descent. The resulting fields make the desired pairs frequent. We then estimate
the total weight using the standard reduction from sampling to counting [JVV86]. See Section 2.3
for a detailed discussion.

gradient descent. It can serve as a black box for a broad class of counting problems admit-
ting the above formulation, once the sampling and uniform Poincaré hypotheses are veri-
fied.

3.2   Matroids
A matroid M on U has a family I(M) ⊆ 2U of independent sets satisfying:
 • ∅ ∈ I(M).
 • If I ∈ I(M) and J ⊆ I, then J ∈ I(M).
 • If I, J ∈ I(M) and | I | < | J |, then I ∪ {e} ∈ I(M) for some e ∈ J \ I.
A basis is a maximal independent set; write B(M) for the family of bases. The rank of S ⊆ U is

                                   rkM (S) := max{| I | : I ⊆ S, I ∈ I(M)}.

All bases have size rkM (U ). An independence oracle tests membership in I(M). Greedily adding
elements of S while preserving independence finds a largest independent subset of S, and hence its
rank, with at most |S| oracle queries. The dual matroid M∗ is the matroid on U whose independent
sets are the sets I ⊆ U such that U \ I contains a basis of M. Equivalently, B(M∗ ) = {U \ B : B ∈
B(M)}; see [Oxl11] for further background.
    The basis generating polynomial gM ( x) := ∑ B∈B(M) x B is log-concave [AOV18; BH20]. Addi-
tional matroid terminology and operations used in the applications are recalled in Appendix B.

A.5    Arithmetic for the counting algorithm
The field-finding and counting algorithms use only arithmetic operations. Ceilings of binary
logarithms are computed by repeated doubling; other logarithms in sample counts can be replaced
by these upper bounds, changing only absolute constants. In the coordinate-descent application,
L = σ = 1. Take s = log(1 + η/8), which lies in [η/16, η/8], and maintain zu directly: an update
multiplies or divides one field coordinate zu by 1 + η/8. Each update costs O(1) operations,
and neither s nor y needs to be evaluated. The self-reduction uses membership tests, empirical
frequencies, and products and quotients of oracle values, fields, and integer powers of t, so it
requires no further logarithms or exponentials.
Remark A.4 (Bit complexity). For rational input parameters and polynomial-time value oracles
returning rationals of polynomial encoding length, the algorithm in Theorem 1.6 has an implemen-
tation whose running time is polynomial in the input length and ε−1 on every execution. Indeed, the
update rule above gives zu = (1 + 1/(32n))ku for integers k u , and the field bound Eq. (23) implies
∑u |k u | = O(n2 B). Each field coordinate can therefore be stored using polynomially many bits. The
probabilities for choosing which element to add back are ratios of sums of at most n weighted oracle
values, and empirical frequencies are ratios of sample counts. The final estimate uses products and
ratios of these numbers, fields, and powers of t. All these numbers have polynomially many bits.
    We simulate the required rational probabilities using fair coin flips; see [NP05, Proposition 13].
Each binary choice can be simulated with probability at most 2−r of requiring more than r bits. If
N is a polynomial bound on the total number of binary choices in the homogeneous algorithm,
aborting and returning zero when any choice needs more than ⌈log2 (64N )⌉ bits adds at most
1/64 to its failure probability. The success probability proved in Section 5 is at least 15/16, so
it remains above 3/4. Conditioning, amplification, and summation over homogeneous layers
preserve polynomial bit complexity. In particular, the rational six-vertex instances in Appendix C.7
satisfy these oracle assumptions.

B.1    Further matroid terminology and examples
We use the definitions and notation from Section 3.2. A set is spanning if it contains a basis. An
element e is a loop if rkM ({e}) = 0; distinct nonloops e, f are parallel if rkM ({e, f }) = 1. Together
with equality, this partitions F
                               the nonloops into parallel classes.
    In a partition matroid, U = i Ei and independence means | I ∩ Ei | ≤ bi for prescribed nonnegative
integer bounds. In a graphic matroid, independent edge sets are forests and bases are spanning
forests, one tree per connected component. In a column matroid, independent sets index linearly
independent columns of a matrix over a field.

B.2    Operations and oracles
The restriction M| A keeps the independent sets contained in A; deletion of A is restriction to U \ A.
The contraction M/A has ground set U \ A. The ranks of the dual and contraction satisfy

                         rkM∗ ( T ) = | T | + rkM (U \ T ) − rkM (U ),                             (29)
                        rkM/A (S) = rkM (S ∪ A) − rkM ( A)          ( S ⊆ U \ A ).

Thus T is independent in M∗ exactly when its complement is spanning. If B A is a basis of M| A ,
contraction independence is tested by B A ∪ S ∈ I(M), even when A is dependent.
    The direct sum of matroids on disjoint ground sets has independent sets whose restriction to
each component is independent. The union of matroids on a common ground set has independent
sets admitting a partition I = I1 ⊔ · · · ⊔ Ik , with Ii ∈ I(Mi ).
    The rank formulas above, together with the greedy rank computation in Section 3.2, give
independence oracles for duals and contractions. With separate independence oracles, matroid
intersection finds a maximum common independent set in polynomial time [Edm79], and matroid
union has a polynomial-time independence oracle [BF10, Section 4.1].

B.3    Submodularity and integral polymatroids
A set function f : 2[m] → R is submodular if

                       f ( A) + f ( B) ≥ f ( A ∪ B) + f ( A ∩ B)    ( A, B ⊆ [m]).

It is monotone if A ⊆ B implies f ( A) ≤ f ( B), and normalized if f (∅) = 0. Matroid rank functions

Remark C.15. An Eulerian orientation specifies the direction of each edge, with equal indegree and
outdegree at every vertex. If G is connected, this guarantees the existence of a directed Euler tour;
otherwise, each connected component admits one. The six-vertex partition function includes one
term per orientation, regardless of how many directed Euler tours it admits.

D.1     A Matroid Intersection Analog of the Monomer-Dimer Model
In this section, we consider a generalization of the classical monomer-dimer model over matchings
of a graph to matroid intersection. We use Theorem 1.14 to prove a spectral gap for the standard
heat-bath Glauber dynamics that decays smoothly as the parameter λ grows. In particular, the
model does not exhibit a dynamical phase transition in λ. The motivation for studying this problem
was discussed in Section 1.5.
    Let M, N be two matroids on a common ground set, and fix a parameter λ ≥ 0. We consider
the following probability measure over common independent sets of all sizes:

                                     µλ (S ) ∝ λ|S| ,       ∀S ∈ I(M) ∩ I(N ).

The discrete-time heat-bath Glauber dynamics is a Markov chain whose evolution can be described
