                                                                                          Abstract
                                                     We develop a framework to predict whether a family of Selmer groups has average size
                                                 that is bounded or unbounded. Applying this framework to certain geometric families
                                                 of abelian varieties over Q, we give a conjectural characterization of which such families
                                                 have ℓ-Selmer groups of unbounded average size for a given prime ℓ. In the case that the
                                                 ℓ-torsion Galois module is constant across the family, we show that our characterization
                                                 is correct.
                                                     The key tool of our technique is the Greenberg–Wiles’ formula, which expresses the
                                                 ratio of the sizes of a Selmer group and the corresponding dual Selmer group as a product
                                                 of local factors. This formula gives a purely local lower bound for the size of a Selmer
                                                 group that we conjecture is close to sharp most of the time.

    Methods from the geometry of numbers give many interesting cases of this conjecture.
Besides the papers mentioned above, we also mention work of Bhargava and Ho for Selmer
groups in families with certain marked rational points [3] and work of Bhargava, Elkies, and
Shnidman in the family y 2 = x3 +u1 [2]. The families considered in these papers have β(κ) = 0

    This conjecture is also known for ℓ = 2 in a number of quadratic twist families. For this
paper, the two most salient prior results are the following:

Example 1.1 (Koymans–Pagano–Sofos [38]). Given a nonzero polynomial P ∈ Q[u1 , . . . , un ]
and distinct rational numbers r1 , r2 , r3 , Conjecture 1 holds for the 2-Selmer groups of the
family
                    A/Q(u1 , . . . , un ) : y 2 = (x − r1 P )(x − r2 P )(x − r3 P )
with height h(b1 , . . . , bn ) = max |bi |.
    Like work of Kane [32], Swinnerton-Dyer [56], and Heath-Brown [30] before it, this result
takes advantage of the fact that the 2-Selmer group of a quadratic twist of an elliptic curve
with full rational 2-torsion can be calculated in terms of a matrix of Legendre symbols of
the primes dividing the twist. The novelty of this result is its use of sieve methods, which
are used to control most but not all of this matrix; specifically, the entries invoking the very
largest primes dividing the twist are left uncontrolled. The use of sieve methods precludes
determining the exact size of the Selmer moments, but is strong enough to prove Conjecture
1 for this family.

     In this theorem, as b changes, the Galois module Ab [ℓ] is fixed, but the local conditions that
decorate it change. The most general result of this paper, Theorem 2.7, proves the analogue
of Conjecture 1 in families of decorated Galois modules where the underlying module over Q
is fixed. To make this work, we assume that the local conditions are effectively equidistributed ;
see Definition 2.6 for details.
     To closely approximate the distribution of the ℓ-Selmer groups in A≤H , we would need
to have a handle on the local conditions of an elliptic curve drawn from this family at all of
its places of bad reduction. This is possible in some cases, as in Example 1.2, but is usually
infeasible. Instead, we follow the approach of Example 1.1 and ignore the primes of bad
reduction above a certain threshold relative to H. As E varies up to height H, the product
of primes of bad reduction below this threshold has a distribution approximately given by a
calculable multiplicative function. These results are proved by employing a general form of
a sieve originally considered by Erdős [24] and later considered by Shiu [52], Wolke [59] and
Nair–Tenenbuam [45].
     Our next step is to consider the set of objects in this family whose product of bad primes
below the threshold lies in a set of the form
                                  {p1 . . . pk : pi ∈ Xi for all i},
where the Xi are judiciously chosen sets of primes. This step is known as gridding, and is a
modification of a similar step in [54].
    With the grids set up, the final step in the proof of Theorem 2.7 is to estimate the char-
acter sums that give the average size of the Selmer groups over these grids. This again uses
techniques from [54], but with complications due to the more general setup of Theorem 2.7.
    Once the proof of Theorem 2.7 is finished, the main work that remains is to show that
it implies our results for elliptic curves and abelian varieties, including Theorem 1.3 and its
generalization Theorem 6.4. This means that we need to show that the families of decorated
modules in these theorems have local conditions that are effectively equidistributed. This
step, which is trivial in the quadratic twist case, is much more complicated in full generality.
We end up relying on a quantifier elimination result of Denef for p-adic fields [19, 20] and its
uniform generalization due to Pas [47]. With this input from model theory, we are able to
translate our questions about local conditions into solvable problems in algebraic geometry.

1.4   Overview of the paper
In Section 2, we review the Greenberg–Wiles’ formula and use it to give the general heuristic
underpinning Conjecture 1, which is Heuristic 2.3. This heuristic concerns the behavior of
Selmer groups of families of decorated modules over Q. In this section, we also state our main
result, Theorem 2.7, which shows that the heuristic is correct in constant module families with
effectively equidistributed local conditions.
    Over Sections 3, 4, and 5, we prove Theorem 2.7. This starts in Section 3 with the
development of a suitable sieve, both for an upper bound (Theorem 3.2) and a lower bound
(Theorem 3.3). In Section 4, we adapt the theory of grids [54, Section 8] to this setup. This
reduces the Selmer group moments we are bounding to a character sum that we estimate in
Section 5. This approach is based on [54, Sections 5-7], but requires several novel arguments as
our character sum involves a complicated multiplicative weight coming from the sieve. With
the character sums estimated, we then prove Theorem 2.7 in Section 5.5.
    In Section 6, we state a generalization of Theorem 1.3 for abelian varieties, Theorem 6.4,
and show that it is a consequence of Theorem 2.7. This involves proving that the associated
family of decorated modules has effectively equidistributed local conditions.
    To this point, our main results have shown that sums of Selmer group sizes are of a similar
magnitude to the corresponding sums of Tamagawa ratios. In Section 6.4, we give tools for
estimating sums of Tamagawa ratios, in the process giving a closed form for our estimate of
the sums of Selmer group sizes. These tools are particularly concrete in the case where we are
looking at a family of elliptic curves, and we prove Theorem 1.3 at the end of Section 6.
    Finally, in Section 7, we use the theory developed throughout the paper to study a plethora
of examples, including 8 families of elliptic curves. This section has the proofs of Theorem
1.7 and Theorem 1.4 as consequences of Theorem 6.4. Finally, we derive Theorem 1.5 and
Theorem 1.6 directly from Theorem 2.7 at the end of this section.

6.3    The Denef–Pas language
Throughout this subsection, we will assume that X is affine.
    We recall the Denef–Pas language LPR defined on [47, p. 140]. This language has three
sorts: a valued field K, its residue field K, and a codomain for the valuation map, which we
always take to be Z ∪ {∞}. A formula in this language is built out of:
   • The constants 0, 1 and the operations +, ·, −, ÷ for both K and K.

   • A valuation map ord : K → Z ∪ {∞}.

   • An angular component map ac : K → K.

   • The constants 0, 1 and ∞ in Z∪{∞} in addition to the operator + and the relationships
     ≤ and ≡ mod n for arbitrary n > 1.

                                                      52
   • Variables and quantifiers of any of the three sorts, and boolean operations on formulae.

structure, K is Fp , ord : K → Z ∪ {∞} is the standard valuation map, and ac is defined by

group of a finite Galois extension K of Q whose average is 1. The Lang–Weil bound and
Lemma 6.8 gives that

                          #Zi (Z/p2 Z) − f (Frob p)p2n−1 = O(p2n−3/2 )

for some Frobenian function of average 1, where the implicit constant does not depend on p.
    Now suppose L is some quadratic extension of κ(Zi ). Then we may choose some dense
open subscheme Vi of Zi and a degree 2 map Wi → Vi from another separated scheme whose
generic fiber corresponds to L/κ(Zi ). Lang–Weil gives that Wi (Fp ) has size pn−1 times a
Frobenian function of average one, up to manageable error. This implies that the image of
Wi (Fp ) in Vi (Fp ) has size 21 pn−1 times a Frobenian function of average one up to manageable
error.
    But if an unexceptional point x ∈ X (Zp ) has its corresponding Fp point x lying in Vi , we
find that Lx /Fp is a nontrivial extension exactly when x lies outside the image of Wi (Fp ). We
will repeatedly use this fact to control Tamagawa ratios.
