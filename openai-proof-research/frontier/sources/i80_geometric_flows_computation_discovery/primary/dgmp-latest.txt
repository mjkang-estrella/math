                                                                                    S IGNIFICANCE S TATEMENT

                                              The Navier-Stokes equations govern the behaviour of viscous fluids and are at the heart of clas-
                                           sical physics, engineering, and applied mathematics. Despite their ubiquity, fundamental ques-
                                           tions about their solutions remain unresolved. This work establishes, for the first time, the ex-
                                           istence of stationary fluid flows governed by the Navier-Stokes equations that can simulate any
                                           computation performable by a universal Turing machine. These Turing complete solutions are
                                           constructed on geometrically natural 3-manifolds using tools from cosymplectic geometry and
                                           the theory of harmonic forms. The result reveals that undecidability—a hallmark of computa-
                                           tional complexity—can arise even in the steady state of viscous fluid motion, reshaping our un-
                                           derstanding of what fluids can (and cannot) compute.

through which ideal fluids can be interpreted. Unfortunately, the aforementioned correspondence
does not allow one to construct solutions to the Navier-Stokes equations.
   In this article, we aim to construct Turing complete solutions to the Navier-Stokes equations
on some Riemannian 3-manifolds, making use of some geometric ideas that extend the Etnyre-
Ghrist correspondence. Specifically, using the machinery of cosymplectic geometry, we construct
harmonic vector fields that simulate any Turing machine. These harmonic fields are stationary so-
lutions to the Navier-Stokes equations, providing the Turing completeness of these flows. Notice
that, as a consequence, the resulting flow has undecidable long-term behaviour.

   Now we are ready to state our main result. A straightforward but remarkable consequence of
this theorem is the existence of viscous fluid flows exhibiting undecidable trajectories.

Theorem A. Given any Hodge-admissible Riemannian 3-manifold (M, g), there exists a (generally not
small) deformation gϵ of the metric g and a solution of the stationary Navier-Stokes equations associated to
gϵ that is Turing complete, for any value of the viscosity ν ≥ 0.

   The proof of Theorem A exploits a connection between harmonic forms and weak cosymplectic
structures. We recall that a weak cosymplectic structure on a (2n + 1)-dimensional manifold M is a
pair (α, ω) of closed forms, where α ∈ Ω1 (M ) and ω ∈ Ω2n (M ), such that α ∧ ω is a volume form.
This generalizes cosymplectic structures, where ω = β n for a closed β ∈ Ω2 (M ). This structure
contrasts with contact forms, where the somehow opposite maximal non-integrability is required.
While contact structures exist on all closed 3-manifolds, cosymplectic structures impose topologi-
cal constraints, such as nontrivial first Betti number, which have been studied in detail in [7], and
are fully characterized in the compact case in [10]. Cosymplectic structures also arise naturally as
critical hypersurfaces in b-symplectic or log symplectic manifolds [11], where they model degen-
erate limits of symplectic structures, often in connection with manifolds with boundary or partial
compactifications.
   The following elementary lemma, which is crucial for the proof of Theorem A, shows that there
is a correspondence between harmonic vector fields and weak cosymplectic structures in any di-
mension.

Lemma B (The cosymplectic correspondence). There exists a correspondence between pairs (X, g) of
nonvanishing harmonic vector fields and Riemannian metrics on a manifold M , and weak cosymplectic
structures (α, ω) satisfying α = ιX g.

   Furthermore, by means of this geometric link, the harmonic field X corresponds, up to a pro-
portionality factor, to the Reeb vector field of the weak cosymplectic structure (α, ω), i.e., the unique

codifferential d∗ : Ωk (M ) → Ωk−1 (M ) can be explicitly written as d∗ = (−1)k ⋆ d ⋆. In particular,

since M is compact and without boundary, this implies that a differential form α is harmonic for
the Hodge Laplacian ∆ = dd∗ + d∗ d if and only if both α and ⋆ α are closed.
   Now, consider a pair (X, g) of a nonvanishing harmonic vector field X and a Riemannian metric
g on M . This means that the 1-form α = ιX g is harmonic for the Hodge Laplacian, where ιX
denotes the contraction of the first index by X. Then, the pair (α, ⋆ α) defines a weak cosymplectic
structure. Indeed, since α is harmonic, both α ∈ Ω1 (M ) and ⋆ α ∈ Ω2n (M ) are closed, and also

                                        α ∧ ⋆ α = g(X, X) µ

is proportional to the Riemannian volume form µ, via a positive proportionality factor.
   For the converse implication, let (α, ω) be a weak cosymplectic structure with Reeb vector field
X. Since α ∧ ω is nonzero, we have that ker(ω) ∩ ker(α) = 0, so in particular ω defines a volume
form on ker(α). Pick a metric on ker(α) whose Riemannian volume is ω, and then extend it to a
metric g on the whole tangent space by setting g(X, X) = 1 and imposing that ker(α) and X are
orthogonal. Notice that, with these conditions, the Riemannian volume of g is precisely α ∧ ω and
that α♯ = X.
   With this metric, observe that

                ⋆ α = ⋆(1 ∧ α) = ια♯ (⋆ 1) = ια♯ (α ∧ ω) = α(α♯ ) ∧ ω + α ∧ ια♯ ω = ω.

This implies that d ⋆ α = dω = 0. Hence, since dα = 0 by hypothesis, then X = α♯ is harmonic,
concluding the proof.

                          3. T URING C OMPLETE D YNAMICAL S YSTEMS

3.1. Review of the Theory of Turing Machines. A Turing machine is a theoretical model of com-
putation that formalizes the idea of an automatic calculation. Informally, a Turing machine is a
read/write mechanism that operates on a bi-infinite tape serving as memory, where the tape is
divided into discrete cells. Each cell contains a symbol from an alphabet, which can be taken to be
binary. The machine is equipped with a head positioned over a single cell at any given time. In
each computational step, the head reads the symbol in the current cell, and based on this symbol
and its internal state, it performs three actions: it rewrites the symbol in the cell, moves one cell
to the left or right, and updates its internal state. This process repeats either until a designated
halting state is reached, at which point the computation terminates, or it continues indefinitely.
   In formal terms, let us fix an alphabet Σ = {0, 1} of binary symbols. A (binary) Turing machine
is a quadruple T = (Q, q0 , qhalt , δ) where:
     • Q is a finite set of states,
     • q0 ∈ Q is the designated initial state,
     • qhalt ∈ Q, with qhalt ̸= q0 , is the halting state,
     • δ : Q × Σ → Q × Σ × {−1, 0, 1} is the transition function.
  The space of configurations is Q × Σ∗ , where Σ∗ is the set of two-sided sequences t = (ti )i∈Z with
values in Σ = {0, 1} such that ti = 1 for finitely many indices i. In this way, a configuration is a
         TURING COMPLETE NAVIER-STOKES STEADY STATES VIA               COSYMPLECTIC GEOMETRY             5

pair c = (q, t) for which q ∈ Q is the current state of the machine and t ∈ Σ∗ represents the state of
the tape of the machine. The initial configuration is of the form (q0 , tin ), where the tape tin contains
the input data to the Turing machine.
  The evolution of a Turing machine follows these rules:
   (1) If the current configuration is of the form (qhalt , t), the computation halts and t is returned
       as output.
   (2) Otherwise, if the configuration is (q, t), the machine reads the symbol t0 at the initial tape
       position and computes
                                         δ(q, t0 ) = (q ′ , t′0 , ε).
       With this information, the machine writes the new symbol t′0 on the tape, shifts it by ε ∈
       {−1, 0, 1} (where by convention, +1 denotes a left shift), and transitions to state q ′ . The
       new configuration becomes (q ′ , t′ ), and the process repeats.
   This iterative process of a Turing machine T defines a discrete dynamical system on the space
of configurations, known as the global transition function

                                        ∆T : Q × Σ∗ → Q × Σ∗ ,

mapping a configuration to its immediate successor under the machine dynamics.
   One of the foundational results in the theory of computation, proved by Alan Turing in 1936,
is the undecidability of the halting problem: there exists no algorithm that can determine whether
an arbitrary Turing machine eventually reaches a configuration with halting state starting from a
given input.
   An important concept for our purposes is the universal Turing machine. Notice that the informa-
tion of the quadruple T = (Q, q0 , qhalt , δ) corresponding to a Turing machine can be encoded in
binary as a finite sequence tT ∈ Σ∗ . Given a pair of sequences t, t′ ∈ Σ∗ , let us denote by t ∗ t′ their
juxtaposition.

Definition 3.1. A Turing machine TU is said to be universal if, for any other Turing machine T
and initial tape tin , if we run TU on the tape tT ∗ tin , then TU halts if and only if T halts on tin .
Furthermore, if TU halts, the resulting tape agrees with the final tape computed by T on tin .

  Universal Turing machines exist and can be explicitly described (see [15]). As a direct conse-
quence of the undecidability of the halting problem, any universal Turing machine admits config-
urations for which the halting status is true or false but not provable.

3.2. Turing Complete Dynamical Systems. We now introduce a formal notion of Turing complete-
ness for dynamical systems, capturing the idea that the system can simulate the computational
behaviour of a universal Turing machine. Let X denote a dynamical system (either discrete or
continuous) on a topological space M .

Definition 3.2. A dynamical system X on M is said to be Turing complete if there exists a universal
Turing machine TU such that for each initial configuration c of TU and each tape t ∈ Σ∗ , there
exists a computable point pc ∈ M and a computable open set Uc,t ⊂ M satisfying:

    TU halts on c with output t     ⇐⇒      the positive trajectory of X through pc intersects Uc,t .
6             SØREN DYHR, ÁNGEL GONZÁLEZ-PRIETO, EVA MIRANDA, AND DANIEL PERALTA-SALAS

   In this setting, the question of whether a Turing machine halts becomes equivalent to whether a
particular trajectory in M intersects a prescribed region. Crucially, both pc and Uc,t must be com-
putable in a concrete sense: for example, if M is a smooth manifold, computability of pc means its
coordinates (in some chart) are explicitly determined from c and representable as rational num-
bers. Similarly, computability of Uc,t means it can be approximated effectively to any desired
precision.
   It follows that Turing complete dynamical systems necessarily exhibit undecidable behaviour:
for some initial conditions, long-term dynamics are unpredictable in a rigorous computational
sense.
   In foundational work, Moore [14, 13] constructed explicit low-dimensional analytic vector fields
on R3 , given by closed-form differential equations inspired by idealized mechanical models, in
which the flow carries out the state transitions of a universal Turing machine, thereby yielding
trajectories whose reachability and long-term behaviour are rigorously undecidable. Building on
this insight, Koiran and Moore [12] subsequently exhibited concrete closed-form analytic maps
in one and two dimensions—using elementary analytic functions to encode tape symbols and
head-movement rules—whose iterates simulate any Turing computation, proving that even pla-
nar analytic dynamics suffice for full computational universality.
   It is worth mentioning that there exists a useful shortcut to prove Turing completeness of a
smooth vector field X on a 3-dimensional manifold M by means of Poincaré return maps. Recall
that a Poincaré section of X is an embedded 2-dimensional disk D ⊂ M that is transverse to the
flow of X and nonwandering, i.e., for every x ∈ D the flow of x by X hits D again in positive
time. A Poincaré section D ⊂ M induces the so-called Poincaré return map D → D given by the
first hitting point of the flow after leaving D.

Theorem 3.3 ([6, Theorem 5.2]). There exists an area preserving diffeomorphism f : D → D, which is
the identity on an open set of the boundary of D, such that, if X is a vector field admiting a Poincaré section
whose Poincaré return map is f , then X is Turing complete.

    In this situation, Proposition 4.3 allows us to embed any area preserving diffeomorphism of the
disk f : D → D which is the identity near the boundary as the time-c return map on a Poincaré
section D ⊂ M of the Reeb field X̃ of a cosymplectic structure that is a deformation of (α, ⋆α) on
a solid torus containing this disk. The proposition also gives a metric g̃, which coincides with g in
the complement of the aforementioned solid torus, so that α is harmonic with respect to the metric
g̃, and hence the dual field X̃ is harmonic.
    Choosing f to simulate a universal Turing machine (cf. Theorem 3.3) yields a harmonic field X̃
whose Poincaré map is f . By Proposition 4.1, X̃ is thus a Turing complete stationary solution to
the Navier-Stokes equations for any value of the viscosity ν ≥ 0. This completes the proof of the
theorem.
                                                                                                   □

                                               C ONCLUSION

   In this work, we have constructed stationary solutions to the Navier-Stokes equations on a wide
class of Riemannian 3-manifolds that simulate a universal Turing machine (i.e., they are Turing
complete). These solutions are harmonic fields, and they are constructed using the machinery of
cosymplectic geometry; being harmonic, we emphasize that they are also Euler steady states.
   Crucially, we construct vector fields that are solutions to the stationary Navier-Stokes equations
for any value of the viscosity ν ≥ 0. The computational power is then not jeopardized because the
fields are not perturbed when the internal friction is added, and therefore the fragility of Turing
completeness under perturbations [1] is not affected by the value of ν.
   Our construction yields stationary (i.e., time-independent) solutions. Whether such computa-
tional universality extends to genuinely time-dependent solutions remains an open and intriguing
question. In the inviscid case, this was successfully accomplished in [5], where time-dependent
Turing-complete Euler flows were constructed.
