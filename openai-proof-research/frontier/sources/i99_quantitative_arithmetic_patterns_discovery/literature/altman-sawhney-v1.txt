On polynomial progressions via transference



Daniel Altman

Address: Department of Mathematics, Stanford University, 450 Jane Stanford Way,
Building 380, Stanford, CA 94305, USA

Email address: daniel.h.altman@gmail.com

 and 
Mehtaab Sawhney

Address: Department of Mathematics, Columbia University, New York, NY 10027

Email address: m.sawhney@columbia.edu




Abstract.
    

We prove new cases of reasonable bounds for the polynomial Szemerédi theorem both over \mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z} with N prime and over the integers. In particular, we prove reasonable bounds for Szemerédi’s theorem in the integers with fixed polynomial common difference. That is, we prove for any polynomial P(y)\in\mathbb{Z}[y] with P(0)=0, that the largest subset A\subseteq[N] avoiding the pattern
    





x,x+P(y),\ldots,x+kP(y)


    

has size bounded by \ll_{P,k}N(\log\log\log N)^{-\Omega_{P,k}(1)}.
  




1. Introduction




Consider any set of distinct polynomials P_{1}(y),\ldots,P_{t}(y)\in\mathbb{Z}[y] with P_{j}(0)=0 for each j. Let r_{P_{1},\ldots,P_{t}}([N]) denote the size of largest subset A of [N]:=\{1,\ldots,N\} avoiding nontrivial copies of the pattern





x+P_{1}(y),\ldots,x+P_{t}(y).



By nontrivial pattern here we require that P_{i}(y)-P_{j}(y)\neq 0 for all i\neq j. The celebrated polynomial Szemerédi theorem of Bergelson and Leibman [2] states that





r_{P_{1},\ldots,P_{t}}([N])=o_{P_{1},\ldots,P_{t}}(N).






Due to the use of ergodic techniques the results of Bergelson and Leibman [2] are qualitative and give no effective bounds for the rate of decay of r_{P_{1},\ldots,P_{t}}([N])/N. In the case of Szemerédi’s theorem [42, 43], seminal work of Gowers [6, 8] proved that





r_{y,\ldots,ky}([N])\ll N(\log\log N)^{-\Omega_{k}(1)}.






However, progress towards genuinely polynomial cases of the polynomial Szemerédi’s theorem remained more limited and was highlighted by Gowers [7] as a problem of interest. Results of Sarközy [41] gave effective bounds for the pattern x,x+y^{2} via the circle method. The underlying method has been generalized to give effective bounds for the case of x,x+P(y) when P(y)\in\mathbb{Z}[y] with P(0)=0 by Lucier [30].11
            1
            
            
            
            
            
            
            
          This result applies more generally to the case where P(y) is intersective. P(y) is said to be intersective if for all integers q\geq 1 there is n\in\mathbb{Z} such that q|P(n).




The cases where the underlying pattern contains more than two terms proved more difficult. A result of Green handled the case of three–term arithmetic progressions where the underlying difference was a sum of two squares [9]. A result of Prendiville [40] handled the case of k-term arithmetic progressions where the common difference is a perfect power. These results however both relied on homogeneity to pass to “perfect power” differences in order to run a density increment strategy. Therefore it was a substantial breakthrough when Peluse and Prendiville [38, 37] provided effective bounds for the case of x,x+y,x+y^{2}. This was extended substantially in work of Peluse [36] to the case of x,x+P_{1}(y),\ldots,x+P_{t}(y) where the P_{j}(y) are of differing degrees. Finally, work of Peluse, Sah and the second author [39] handled the case of 3-term arithmetic progressions with difference y^{2}-1, and this was extended to general P(y) with a simple integer root implicitly in work of Kravitz, Kuca and Leng [17].




The first result of this paper is a version of Szemerédi’s theorem allowing for the difference to be equal to any fixed polynomial difference.




Theorem 1.1.



Fix a polynomial P(y)\in\mathbb{Z}[y] with P(0)=0. Let \vec{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{t})\in\mathbb{Z}^{t} with a_{i}\neq a_{j} for i\neq j. Then there exists c=c(\operatorname{deg}(P),t)>0 such that the following hold.


• 



If t=3 and P^{\prime}(0)\neq 0 then





r_{a_{1}P,\ldots,a_{t}P}([N])\ll_{P,\vec{a}}N\exp(-c(\log\log N)^{c}).




• 



If P^{\prime}(0)\neq 0 then





r_{a_{1}P,\ldots,a_{t}P}([N])\ll_{P,\vec{a}}N\exp(-c(\log\log\log N)^{c}).




• 



If P^{\prime}(0)=0 then





r_{a_{1}P,\ldots,a_{t}P}([N])\ll_{P,\vec{a}}N(\log\log\log N)^{-c}.









Remark 1.2.



The final item in this theorem can likely be improved via improving a result of Prendiville [40] which handles the case P(y)=y^{k} and invoking recent results regarding the inverse theorem for the Gowers norm [28]. We refer the reader to Remark 2.12.





We also consider the polynomial Szemerédi theorem over \mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z} where N is prime. This model setting is often simpler than the corresponding setting over the integers as the image of a polynomial Q(y) over \mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z} is dense. Given a sequence of polynomials Q_{1}(y),\ldots,Q_{t}(y), we define r_{Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{t}}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}) to be the size of the largest subset of \mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z} avoiding copies of





x+Q_{1}(y),\ldots,x+Q_{t}(y)



with x\in\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z} and y\in\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z} such that Q_{i}(y)-Q_{j}(y)\neq 0 for all i\neq j.




In \mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}, the first progress was due to work of Bourgain and Chang [3], giving quantitative bounds for r_{0,y,y^{2}}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}). This was generalized in work of Peluse [34] and independently Dong, Li and Sawin [5] to r_{0,Q_{1}(y),Q_{2}(y)}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}) if Q_{1} and Q_{2} are linearly independent. These results were greatly extended in work of Peluse [35] to x,x+Q_{1}(y),\ldots,x+Q_{t}(y) where Q_{i} are linearly independent. Notably, this work introduced the degree lowering method which was subsequently used over the integers. There are also additional results of Kuca [19] which, for example, covers the case of x,x+y,\ldots,x+(m-1)y,x+y^{m},\ldots,x+y^{m+k-1} and work of Leng [26] which handles the case of x,x+P(y),x+Q(y),x+P(y)+Q(y).




Our second result provides a unified framework which proves all known cases of effective bounds in the polynomial Szemerédi theorem, as well as proving several new cases. Furthermore these results essentially resolve several conjectures of Leng [26, Conjecture 1–3] (although as stated there are counterexamples to [26, Conjecture 1]).




To state our results, we will require the notions of a kernel system and homogeneous polynomial patterns, and will introduce the notion of a transferable polynomial pattern. The first two were considered in work of Kuca [20] while the third arises when we apply transference.




We first define the kernel system of a set of polynomials.




Definition 1.3.



Given polynomials P_{1},\ldots,P_{t}\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}], the kernel system \kappa(\mathcal{P}) of pattern \mathcal{P}=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{t}) is the \mathbb{Q}-vector space of tuples of polynomials \vec{Q}=(Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{t})\in\mathbb{Q}[z] such that





\sum_{i=1}^{t}Q_{i}(P_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}))=0.







We now define the notions of homogeneous and transferable polynomial patterns.




Definition 1.4.



Consider \mathcal{P}=(x+P_{1}(y),\ldots,x+P_{t}(y))\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y] with P_{i}(0)=0 and P_{i} distinct. Let d=\max_{i\in[t]}\deg P_{i} and define the (unique) homogeneous linear polynomials P_{i}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{Z}[y_{1},\ldots,y_{d}] such that





P_{i}^{\ast}(y,y^{2},\ldots,y^{d})=P_{i}(y).



We say that \mathcal{P} is transferable if whenever \vec{Q}=(Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{t})\in\kappa(\mathcal{P}) holds we must in fact have





\sum_{i=1}^{t}Q_{i}(x+P_{i}^{\ast}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d}))=0.



We say that \mathcal{P} is homogeneous if \kappa(\mathcal{P}) is spanned by tuples of the form (c_{1}z^{i},\ldots,c_{t}z^{i}).





Remark 1.5.



By way of example, observe that the polynomial pattern \mathcal{P}_{1}=(x,x+y,x+2y,x+y^{2}) is not homogeneous due to the following identity:





2(x+y^{2})=2x+x^{2}-2(x+y)^{2}+(x+2y)^{2}.



We next have the existence of polynomial patterns which are not transferable, but which are homogeneous. In particular, consider \mathcal{P}_{2}=(x,x-y^{2},x+y^{2},x+y,x+y^{3},x+y+y^{3}) and observe that





(x+y^{2})^{2}-2x^{2}+(x-y^{2})^{2}=(x+y+y^{3})^{2}-(x+y^{3})^{2}-(x+y)^{2}+x^{2}=2y^{4}.



Finally we have that all transferable patterns are homogeneous: the kernel system of a set of linear patterns is easily seen to be spanned by tuples of the form (c_{1}z^{i},\ldots,c_{t}z^{i}) by considering the degree of terms arising in a relation determined by \vec{Q}\in\kappa(\mathcal{P}).





The main result of this work over finite fields is the following transference result.




Theorem 1.6.



Let P_{i}(y)\in\mathbb{Z}[y] with P_{i}(0)=0 and suppose that \mathcal{P}=(x+P_{1}(y),\ldots,x+P_{t}(y)) is transferable. Let d=\max_{1\leq i\leq t}\operatorname{deg}(P_{i}) and define P_{i}^{\ast}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d}) as in Definition 1.4. Let N be prime and consider f_{i}:\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C} with \lVert f_{i}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1.




There exists c=c_{\ref{thm:main-poly}}(P_{1},\ldots,P_{t})>0 such that





\Big|\mathbb{E}_{x,y\in\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}f_{i}(x+P_{i}(y))-\mathbb{E}_{x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}f_{i}(x+P_{i}^{\ast}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d}))\Big|\ll_{\mathcal{P}}\exp(-c(\log\log N)^{c}).






Therefore there exists c^{\prime}=c_{\ref{thm:main-poly}}^{\prime}(P_{1},\ldots,P_{t})>0 such that if \mathcal{P} is transferable then





r_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})\ll_{\mathcal{P}}N\exp(-c^{\prime}(\log\log\log N)^{c^{\prime}}).







1.1. Proof outline




The proof of both Theorem 1.1 and Theorem 1.6 rely on the use of transference to deduce our results regarding polynomial patterns from supersaturation results on other patterns. This general scheme of comparing counts and then applying supersaturation results stems from work of Wooley and Ziegler [46] regarding finding patterns x+P_{1}(y),\ldots,x+P_{t}(y) where y is additionally one less than a prime. This idea of transference has now been applied in the context of the polynomial Szemerédi theorem in several instances including work of Kuca [19], Leng [26] and Peluse, Sah and the second author [39]. The main technical improvement in our work is avoiding the use of degree–lowering and instead noting that the required transference claim follows if it holds for nilsequences. We reduce to the more algebraic problem of nilsequence analysis via an iterative application of stashing and applications of the inverse theorem. The algebraic problem is then solved via adapting the arguments of the first author [1] to transferable polynomial patterns.




We first outline the proof of Theorem 1.6 and then briefly discuss the technical differences which arise when handling Theorem 1.1. For the sake of concreteness consider the case of x,x+y,x+2y,x+y^{3},x+2y^{3}. Let f_{i}:\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C},\ i=1,\ldots 5 be one-bounded, that is, \lVert f_{i}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1. We wish to upper bound the difference





\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}[f_{1}(x)f_{2}(x+y)
\displaystyle f_{3}(x+2y)f_{4}(x+y^{3})f_{5}(x+2y^{3})]



(1.1)


\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}[f_{1}(x)f_{2}(x+y)f_{3}(x+2y)f_{4}(x+z)f_{5}(x+2z)]\Big|.



Given an appropriate upper bound, the claim about the polynomial Szemerédi theorem then follows from supersaturation results for variants of Szemerédi’s theorem for linear patterns.




Suppose that





\displaystyle\delta
\displaystyle\leq\Big|\mathbb{E}[f_{1}(x)f_{2}(x+y)f_{3}(x+2y)f_{4}(x+y^{3})f_{5}(x+2y^{3})]





\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\mathbb{E}[f_{1}(x)f_{2}(x+y)f_{3}(x+2y)f_{4}(x+z)f_{5}(x+2z)]\Big|.



We now perform a Cauchy–Schwarz maneuver known as stashing, due to Manners [32]. Define the dual function





\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{1}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{y,z}(f_{2}(x+y)f_{3}(x+2y)f_{4}(x+y^{3})f_{5}(x+2y^{3})-f_{2}(x+y)f_{3}(x+2y)f_{4}(x+z)f_{5}(x+2z)).



By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have that





\displaystyle\delta^{2}\leq\Big|\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}_{1}(x)f_{2}(x+y)f_{3}(x+2y)
\displaystyle f_{4}(x+y^{3})f_{5}(x+2y^{3})]





\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}_{1}(x)f_{2}(x+y)f_{3}(x+2y)f_{4}(x+z)f_{5}(x+2z)]\Big|.



Applying a PET induction argument, we know that there exists s\geq 1 such that





\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}
\displaystyle[\mathcal{D}_{1}(x)f_{2}(x+y)f_{3}(x+2y)f_{4}(x+y^{3})f_{5}(x+2y^{3})]





\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}_{1}(x)f_{2}(x+y)f_{3}(x+2y)f_{4}(x+z)f_{5}(x+2z)]\Big|\ll\lVert\mathcal{D}_{1}\rVert_{U^{s}(\mathbb{F}_{p})}+p^{-\Omega(1)}.



Thus, ignoring the p^{-\Omega(1)} term for the sake of simplicity in this outline, we find that \lVert\mathcal{D}_{1}\rVert_{U^{s}(\mathbb{F}_{p})}\gg\delta^{O(1)}. By applying the inverse theorem for the Gowers norm [27], we obtain that there exists a nilsequence \psi_{1}(x) (of appropriately bounded complexity) and \delta_{1}=\exp(-(\log(1/\delta))^{O(1)}) such that





\displaystyle\delta_{1}
\displaystyle\leq\Big|\mathbb{E}[\psi_{1}(x)f_{2}(x+y)f_{3}(x+2y)f_{4}(x+y^{3})f_{5}(x+2y^{3})]





\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\mathbb{E}[\psi_{1}(x)f_{2}(x+y)f_{3}(x+2y)f_{4}(x+z)f_{5}(x+2z)]\Big|.



We now iterate this procedure. In particular, we find nilsequences \psi_{2},\psi_{3},\psi_{4},\psi_{5} such that there is \delta_{2}=\exp(-(\log(1/\delta))^{O(1)}) with





\displaystyle\delta_{2}
\displaystyle\leq\Big|\mathbb{E}[\psi_{1}(x)\psi_{2}(x+y)\psi_{3}(x+2y)\psi_{4}(x+y^{3})\psi_{5}(x+2y^{3})]





\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\mathbb{E}[\psi_{1}(x)\psi_{2}(x+y)\psi_{3}(x+2y)\psi_{4}(x+z)\psi_{5}(x+2z)]\Big|.






This is now a largely algebraic problem of comparing the orbits of the polynomial sequences g_{1}(x,y)=(g(x),g(x+y),g(x+2y),g(x+y^{3}),g(x+2y^{3})) and g_{2}(x,y)=(g(x),g(x+y),g(x+2y),g(x+z),g(x+2z)) on a nilmanifold of the form G^{5}/\Gamma^{5}. Crucially, if g:\mathbb{Z}\to G is suitably irrational, it transpires that g_{1}(x,y) and g_{2}(x,y) equidistribute on the same Leibman nilmanifold G^{\Psi}/\Gamma^{\Psi}\leq G^{5}/\Gamma^{5}. Such a result is proven without a quantitative treatment by Kuca [20, Theorem 5.3]. We provide a differing treatment of this result, utilizing the perspective in work of the first author [1] in order to give quantitative bounds. We remark here that our analysis proceeds via a reduction to “totally equidistributed” nilsequences; for further improvements to quantitative aspects it may be interesting to prove such a comparison via a “step reduction” procedure.




The proof of Theorem 1.1 proceeds in a broadly similar manner; however certain technical differences arise in the course of the proof. First various algebraic considerations are simpler in this setting as we wish to compare the orbit (g(x+a_{1}P(y)),\ldots,g(x+a_{t}P(y))) to that of (g(x+a_{1}y)),\ldots,g(x+a_{t}y)). For our purposes, we may in fact fix x and then compare orbits of the form g^{\ast}(y) and g^{\ast}(P(y)), which substantially simplifies matters.




This brief summary omits a number of technical details present over the integers which do not arise in the model setting of \mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}. We first introduce an archimedean weight on the associated measure for y (as in [39]); this accounts for the fact that as y grows the spacing of the polynomial P(y) grows. Next when operating over the integers, p-adic biases of P(y) arise, which we handle via the W-trick of Green [10]. This however is an oversimplification: observe that if P^{\prime}(0)=0 but P^{\prime\prime}(0)\neq 0 then comparing a W-trick version of P(y) with y is not prudent as they do not agree p-adically. In this case, one may hope to first apply the W-trick to P(y) and then compare the underlying progression to arithmetic progressions with square common difference. This nearly works; however, if P(y)=y^{2}-y^{4} (say), observe that the common difference under consideration is almost always negative, and yet the transferred progression has positive common difference. This tension at “infinity” and p-adically requires us to find positivity from an additional pattern before comparing. In particular, instead of comparing differences coming from the W-tricked version of P(y)=y^{2}-y^{4} (which is y^{2}-W^{2}y^{4}) with y^{2}, we compare it with y^{2}(-Wz+1). This requires bootstrapping Prendiville’s result [40] to obtain positivity for arithmetic progressions with common difference (-Wz+1)y^{2} and then comparing to this modified pattern.





1.2. Organization of the paper and notation




We prove Theorem 1.1 in Section 2 and prove Theorem 1.6 in Section 3. Various tools are developed within subsections; we refer to the beginning of each section for more refined outlines thereof. Throughout the paper we assume familiarity with various concepts associated with nilsequences. These conventions are developed in Appendix A.




We use standard asymptotic notation for functions on \mathbb{N} throughout. Given functions f=f(x) and g=g(x), we write f=O(g), f\ll g, or g\gg f to mean that there is a constant C such that |f(x)|\leq Cg(x) for sufficiently large x. Subscripts indicate dependence on parameters. Additionally we let [X]=\{1,\ldots,\lfloor X\rfloor\} and [\pm X]=\{-\lfloor X\rfloor,\ldots,\lfloor X\rfloor\}.






2. Transference in the integers




In this section we prove Theorem 1.1. In the first subsection Section 2.1, we prove various p-adic comparison results between polynomials. These results are essentially extensions of classical Hensel lifting arguments. The second subsection Section 2.2 introduces Gowers and Gowers–Peluse norms formally and deduces Gowers norm control for the relevant operators. While Gowers norm control follows immediately for the operator with differences P(y) via the work of Peluse [36], deducing the appropriate Gowers norm control for the operator with differences of the form (\pm zW+1)y^{2} requires an additional argument. In Section 2.3, we establish the crucial nilseqeunce comparison result Lemma 2.9. Finally in Section 2.4 we carry out the stashing procedure and deduce Theorem 1.1.




Before proceeding, we reduce to the case where the bottom coefficient of P(y) is 1. Suppose that P(y)=\sum_{j=d^{\prime}}^{d}b_{j}y^{j} with b_{j}\in\mathbb{Z} where d\geq d^{\prime}\geq 1. Firstly note that we may assume that b_{d^{\prime}}\geq 1 by potentially replacing (a_{1},\ldots,a_{t}) with -(a_{1},\ldots,a_{t}). We claim that we may in fact assume that b_{d^{\prime}}=1 without loss of generality (up to losing a constant factor implicit in Theorem 1.1). To this end observe that





b_{d^{\prime}}^{-d^{\prime}-1}P(b_{d^{\prime}}y)=\sum_{j=d^{\prime}}^{d}b_{j}y^{j}\cdot b_{d^{\prime}}^{j-d^{\prime}-1}\in\mathbb{Z}[y].



Our claim then follows by subdividing [N] into subprogressions with common difference b_{d^{\prime}}^{d^{\prime}+1}, rescaling, and replacing P(y) by b_{d^{\prime}}^{-d^{\prime}-1}P(b_{d^{\prime}}y) and N by \lfloor b_{d^{\prime}}^{-(d^{\prime}+1)}N\rfloor. Henceforth we assume that b_{d^{\prime}}=1.




2.1. Hensel lifting




A key feature of our results will be considering W-tricked versions of the polynomial P(y). Let w>1 (we will later choose w=(\log N)^{1/2}, but it will be convenient to treat it as a parameter in the meantime). We set




(2.1)

W=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq w\\
(p,d)=1\end{subarray}}p\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq w\\
(p,d!)\neq 1\end{subarray}}p^{2d}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq w^{1/2}\end{subarray}}p^{w^{1/3}},



where here and throughout this section we will reserve p to range over primes. Note that \lfloor\log_{p}w\rfloor\leq w^{1/3}, so we have importantly that \operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,w)|W.




Let




(2.2)

P_{W}(y)=W^{-d^{\prime}}P(Wy)\in\mathbb{Z}[y].



The point of this construction is that P_{W}(y) will match the distribution of y^{d^{\prime}} modulo integers all of whose prime factors are at most w. We will now prove two lemmas towards this claim; they both follow from Hensel-type lifting.




Lemma 2.1.



Fix a prime p and let Q(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}y^{j}\in\mathbb{Z}[y]. Suppose that p|c_{j} for j\geq 2 and (c_{1},p)=1. Then for all k\geq 1, we have for all \ell\in[p^{k}] that





\mathbb{P}_{y\in[p^{k}]}[Q(y)\equiv\ell~\mathrm{mod}~p^{k}]=p^{-k}.







Proof.



We proceed by induction on k and note that the base case of k=1 is immediate.




Suppose now that we have handled k. We would like to show that Q(y^{\prime}) distributes uniformly modulo p^{k+1} as y^{\prime} ranges over [p^{k+1}]. We decompose [p^{k+1}] as p^{k}\cdot[p]+[p^{k}], and let y^{\prime}=p^{k}y+b, where y\in[p] and b\in[p^{k}]. Observe that





Q(y^{\prime})=Q(p^{k}y+b)=\left[Q(p^{k}y+b)-Q(b)\right]+Q(b)=\left[\sum_{j=2}^{d}c_{j}((p^{k}y+b)^{j}-b^{j})+c_{1}p^{k}y\right]+Q(b).



Since p|c_{j} for j\geq 2, we have that each term in the sum is equal to zero modulo p^{k+1}. Furthermore, by induction, Q(b) distributes uniformly modulo p^{k} as b ranges over [p^{k}]. Finally, for any fixed multiple a_{b} of p^{k} which arises when instead viewing Q(b) modulo p^{k+1}, we have that c_{1}y+a_{b} distributes uniformly over \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} as y ranges over [p], since (c_{1},p)=1. This completes the proof.
∎





Lemma 2.2.



Let q\in\mathbb{N} be such that all prime factors of q are at most w and let 1\leq r\leq d. Let Q(y)=\sum_{j=r}^{d}c_{j}y^{j}\in\mathbb{Z}[y] satisfy the following properties for all p\leq w. Firstly, (c_{r},p)=1. Secondly, c_{j}\equiv 0~\mathrm{mod}~p for j\geq r+1, and if (p,d!)\neq 1, then c_{j}\equiv 0~\mathrm{mod}~p^{2d} for j\geq r+1. Finally, let \widetilde{c_{r}}\in\mathbb{Z} be such that \widetilde{c_{r}}\equiv c_{r}~\mathrm{mod}~p, and if (p,d!)\neq 1 then \widetilde{c_{r}}\equiv c_{r}~\mathrm{mod}~p^{2d}.




Then we have that




(2.3)

\mathbb{P}_{y\in[q]}[Q(y)\equiv\ell~\mathrm{mod}~q]=\mathbb{P}_{y\in[q]}[\widetilde{c_{r}}y^{r}\equiv\ell~\mathrm{mod}~q].







Proof.



First observe via the Chinese remainder theorem that it suffices to prove the result for q=p^{k} when p\leq w and k\geq 1. We now fix p for the remainder of the analysis and proceed by induction on k. The case where k=1 is immediate.




We first consider the case where (\ell,p)\neq 1 and reduce this case by induction. Observe that if p|Q(y) then p|y and similarly if p|\widetilde{c_{r}}y^{r} then p|y. Observe that if p|y then p^{r} divides Q(y) and \widetilde{c_{r}}y^{r}. Replacing \ell by \ell\cdot p^{-r} and Q(y) by p^{-r}Q(py) we may reduce k to \max(k-r,0) and proceed by induction.




Therefore we may now assume that (\ell,p)=1. We now break into cases based on whether or not (p,d!)=1. Assume firstly that (p,d!)=1. Then writing [p^{k}]=p\cdot[p^{k-1}]+[p], we let y^{\prime}=py+b with y\in[p^{k-1}] and b\in[p]. Computing similarly to the proof of Lemma 2.1, we write





Q(y^{\prime})=[Q(py+b)-Q(b)]+Q(b)=\left[\sum_{j=r+1}^{d}c_{j}((py+b)^{j}-b^{j})+c_{r}((py+b)^{r}-b^{r})\right]+Q(b).



Note that p divides both Q(py+b)-Q(b) and \widetilde{c}_{r}(py+b)^{r}-\widetilde{c}_{r}b^{r}, so since we have reduced to the case that (\ell,p)=1, it suffices to only consider b with (b,p)=1. Fix now b with (b,p)=1. With a view towards applying Lemma 2.1 to Q_{1}(y):=(Q(py+b)-Q(b))/p, observe firstly that p^{2}|\sum_{j=r+1}^{d}c_{j}((py+b)^{j}-b^{j}). Thus the linear coefficient of Q_{1}(y) is congruent to rc_{r}b^{r-1} modulo p, which is nonzero modulo p (since (b,p)=1, and since r\leq d and (p,d!)=1). Furthermore observe that p^{2}|((py+b)^{r}-b^{r}-rb^{r-1}(py)), so the quadratic and higher coefficients of Q_{1}(y) are divisible by p. Thus we may apply Lemma 2.1 to Q_{1}(y), and a similar analysis shows that we may do the same to the polynomial Q_{2}(y):=(\widetilde{c_{r}}(py+b)^{r}-\widetilde{c_{r}}b^{r})/p. This says that both Q(py+b)-Q(b) and \widetilde{c}_{r}(py+b)^{r}-\widetilde{c}_{r}b^{r} distribute uniformly over the multiples of p in \mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z} as y ranges over [p^{k-1}]. But note also that Q(b)\equiv\widetilde{c}_{r}b^{r}~\mathrm{mod}~p, and so we are able to conclude in this case (where, similarly to in the proof of Lemma 2.1, one uses that any multiple a_{b} of p obtained when viewing this modulo p^{k} simply shifts the already uniform distribution of Q(py+b)-Q(b) and \widetilde{c}_{r}(py+b)^{r}-\widetilde{c}_{r}b^{r}).




We now handle the case where (p,d!)\neq 1. In this case we have c_{j}\equiv 0~\mathrm{mod}~p^{d} for j\geq r+1, so we may assume without loss of generality that d<k. Then decompose [p^{k}]=p^{d}\cdot[p^{k-d}]+[p^{d}], set y^{\prime}=p^{d}y+b, and restrict to (b,p)=1 since the case b\equiv 0~\mathrm{mod}~p yields no solutions as before. Then,





Q(y^{\prime})=[Q(p^{d}y+b)-Q(b)]+Q(b)=\left[\sum_{j=r+1}^{d}c_{j}((p^{d}y+b)^{j}-b^{j})+c_{r}((p^{d}y+b)^{r}-b^{r})\right]+Q(b).






Observe that p^{2d}|\sum_{j=r+1}^{d}c_{j}((p^{d}y+b)^{j}-b^{j}), so the largest power of p dividing the linear term of Q(p^{d}y+b)-Q(b) is indeed v_{p}(c_{r}rp^{d}b^{r-1})=d+v_{p}(r) since d+v_{p}(r)\leq d+\lfloor\log_{2}(d)\rfloor<2d. Also note that p^{2d}|c_{r}((p^{d}y+b)^{r}-b^{r}-rb^{r-1}(p^{d}y)). Thus taking Q_{1}(y)=(Q(p^{d}y+b)-Q(b))/p^{d+v_{p}(r)} and similarly Q_{2}(y)=(\widetilde{c_{r}}(p^{d}y+b)^{r}-\widetilde{c_{r}}b^{r})/p^{d+v_{p}(r)}, both have linear coefficient not divisible p, but quadratic and higher coefficients which are. We may then apply Lemma 2.1 and deduce the required result much as we did in the previous case (where now we use that Q(b)\equiv\widetilde{c}_{r}b^{r}~\mathrm{mod}~p^{d+\nu_{p}(r)}).
∎






2.2. Gowers norms and transferred operators




We first recall the standard Gowers norm.




Definition 2.3.



Given f\colon\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C} and s\geq 1, we define





\lVert f\rVert_{U^{s}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})}^{2^{s}}=\mathbb{E}_{x,h_{1},\ldots,h_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}}\Delta_{h_{1},\ldots,h_{s}}f(x)



where \Delta_{h}f(x)=f(x)\overline{f(x+h)} is the multiplicative discrete derivative (extended to lists by composition). Given a natural number N and a function f\colon[N]\to\mathbb{C}, we choose a number \widetilde{N}\geq 2^{s}N and define \widetilde{f}\colon\mathbb{Z}/\widetilde{N}\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C} via \widetilde{f}(x)=f(x) for x\in[N] and 0 otherwise. Then





\lVert f\rVert_{U^{s}[N]}:=\lVert\widetilde{f}\rVert_{U^{s}(\mathbb{Z}/\widetilde{N}\mathbb{Z})}/\lVert\mathbbm{1}_{[N]}\rVert_{U^{s}(\mathbb{Z}/\widetilde{N}\mathbb{Z})}.







Remark.



This is known to be well-defined and independent of \widetilde{N}. Furthermore it is indeed a norm if s\geq 2; see [13, Lemma B.5].





We additionally recall the Gowers–Peluse norms which will be used when handling integer polynomial progressions.




Definition 2.4.



Given a function f:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C} with finite support and h,h^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}, we define




(2.4)

\Delta_{(h,h^{\prime})}f(x)=f(x+h)\overline{f(x+h^{\prime})}.



Given a multiset \Omega=\{\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}\} of probability measures on \mathbb{Z} and a positive integer scale N\geq 1, we define the Gowers–Peluse norms as




(2.5)

\lVert f\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\Omega]}^{2^{k}}=\lVert f\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}]}^{2^{k}}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{h_{i},h_{i}^{\prime}\sim\mu_{i}}\Delta_{(h_{1},h_{1}^{\prime})}\cdots\Delta_{(h_{k},h_{k}^{\prime})}f(x).






If \Omega=\{\mu,\ldots,\mu\} where the measure \mu is repeated k times, we write that





\lVert f\rVert_{U^{k}_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu]}=\lVert f\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\Omega]}.







When using the above definition, we will let \mu_{E} denote the uniform measure on a multiset E for the sake of simplicity.




We now define the crucial counting operators which we will ultimately be used in our transference strategy. Recall that





P(y)=\sum_{j=d^{\prime}}^{d}b_{j}y^{j}



with b_{d^{\prime}}=1, and recall the definitions of W and P_{W}(y) from (2.1) and (2.2) respectively.




We will consider the following pair of operators. Given that these operators are rather more technical than the corresponding choices in (say) [39] we will provide motivation for the definitions after introducing them.




Definition 2.5.



Given functions f_{i}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}, i=1,\ldots,t, we define the operator \Lambda_{W} as follows. Let





\Lambda_{W}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim[1/2,1]}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N]\\
y\in[(zN/(|b_{d}|W^{d-d^{\prime}}))^{1/d}]\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}f_{i}(x+a_{i}P_{W}(y)).



Let \varepsilon=b_{d}/|b_{d}|\in\{\pm 1\} and let





\nu(y)=\Big(\frac{d^{\prime}}{d}\cdot\Big(\frac{N}{W^{2}}\Big)^{(d-d^{\prime})/(2dd^{\prime})}\Big)\cdot y^{-(d-d^{\prime})/d}.



We define the operator \Lambda_{\operatorname{Model}} by





\Lambda_{\operatorname{Model}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N]\\
y\in[(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}]\\
z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]\end{subarray}}\nu(y)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{t}f_{i}(x+a_{i}(\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)y^{d^{\prime}}).







To motivate these definitions, suppose for the sake of simplicity that we are attempting to transfer 3-term arithmetic progressions with common difference y^{2}-y^{4}, that is, \vec{a}=(0,1,2) and P(y)=y^{2}-y^{4}. A naive W-trick compares counts of the pattern x,x+y^{2}-W^{2}y^{4},x+2(y^{2}-W^{2}y^{4}) with that of x,x+y^{2},x+2y^{2}. As y grows, the sequence y^{2}-W^{2}y^{4} is far sparser than y^{2}; the weight \nu(y) is the corresponding archimedean correction factor (a nearly identical device is used in [39]). Observe however that when y is large, y^{2}-W^{2}y^{4} is negative whereas y^{2} is positive. We correct this (without disturbing the W-trick) by comparing with 3-APs with difference (-zW+1)y^{2}. This however reintroduces an archimedian bias, which we then correct with the parameter z in \Lambda_{W}.




Our next task will be to prove that \Lambda_{W} and \Lambda_{\operatorname{Model}} exhibit the appropriate Gowers norm control. We first obtain control on \Lambda_{W}; this is immediate given the results of Peluse [36, Theorem 6.1].




Lemma 2.6.



There exist integers s=s(d,t)\geq 1 and C=C(d,t)\geq 1 such that the following holds. Let \delta\in(0,1/2), f_{i}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C} be 1-bounded functions supported on [\pm N], N\geq(\delta^{-1}W^{d})^{C} and suppose that





|\Lambda_{W}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{t})|\geq\delta.



Then





\min_{1\leq j\leq t}\lVert f_{j}\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}^{s}[N;\mu_{W^{d-d^{\prime}}\cdot[N/W^{(d-d^{\prime})}]}]}\gg_{P,\vec{a}}\delta^{O_{d,t}(1)}.







Note that the implicit constant here only depends on the coefficients of P(y) and not on the coefficients of P_{W}(y).




Proof.



Let M_{z}=(zN/(|b_{d}|W^{d-d^{\prime}}))^{1/d}. Applying the triangle inequality we have that





\mathbb{E}_{z\in[1/2,1]}\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N]\\
y\in[M_{z}]\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}f_{i}(x+a_{i}P_{W}(y))\Big|\geq\delta.



Taking the supremum in z, there exist z_{0} such that





\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N]\\
y\in[M_{z_{0}}]\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}f_{i}(x+a_{i}P_{W}(y))\Big|\geq\delta.



The desired result then follows from Theorem B.3 which is essentially an immediate consequence of [36, Theorem 6.1].
∎





We next obtain control for the operator given by \Lambda_{\operatorname{Model}}. This proof requires opening up the inputs into concatenation estimates to a greater extent than the proof of Lemma 2.6.




Lemma 2.7.



There exist integers s=s(d,t)\geq 1 and C=C(d,t)\geq 1 such that the following holds. Let \delta\in(0,1/2), f_{i}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C} be 1-bounded functions supported on [\pm N], N\geq(\delta^{-1}W^{d})^{C} and suppose that





|\Lambda_{\operatorname{Model}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{t})|\geq\delta.



Then





\min_{1\leq j\leq t}\lVert f_{j}\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}^{s}[N;\mu_{[N]}]}\gg_{d,\vec{a}}\delta^{O_{d,t}(1)}.







Proof.



Let \delta^{\prime}=\delta^{O_{d}(1)} and taking the implicit constant suitably large, we have that





\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N]\\
y\in[\delta^{\prime}\cdot(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})},(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}]\\
z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]\end{subarray}}\nu(y)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{t}f_{i}(x+a_{i}(\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)y^{d^{\prime}})\Big|\geq\delta/2.



Thus there exists V\in[\delta^{\prime}(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})},(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}] such that





\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N]\\
y\in[V,(1+\delta^{\prime})V]\\
z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]\end{subarray}}\nu(y)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{t}f_{i}(x+a_{i}(\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)y^{d^{\prime}})\Big|\geq\delta/2.



Note that \nu(y) is nearly constant on the range y\in[V,(1+\delta^{\prime})V] and furthermore \nu(y)\ll\delta^{\prime-1} on this range. This allows us to remove the weight \nu(y) to obtain





\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N]\\
y\in[V,(1+\delta^{\prime})V]\\
z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}f_{i}(x+a_{i}(\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)y^{d^{\prime}})\Big|\gg\delta^{O_{d}(1)}.



Now writing [(1+\delta^{\prime})V]=[V]\sqcup[V,(1+\delta^{\prime})V], we have for at least one choice V^{\prime}=V or V^{\prime}=(1+\delta^{\prime})V that





\Big|\mathbb{E}_{z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N]\\
y\in[V^{\prime}]\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}f_{i}(x+a_{i}(\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)y^{d^{\prime}})\Big|\gg\delta^{O_{d}(1)}.






Fixing z, we will apply concatenation results to the average over x and y. To first obtain Gowers-Peluse norm control, we would like to apply Theorem B.3. However there is an unspecified dependence on the ratio (|\varepsilon\cdot zW+1|\cdot V^{\prime d^{\prime}})/N, which may depend on \delta. To remedy this, note that by an averaging argument (this time in the variable x), there exists a subinterval I of [N] of length |I|=|\varepsilon\cdot zW+1|V^{\prime d^{\prime}}\gg\delta^{O_{d}(1)}N such that





\Big|\mathbb{E}_{z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in I\\
y\in[V^{\prime}]\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}f_{i}(x+a_{i}(\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)y^{d^{\prime}})\Big|\gg\delta^{O_{d}(1)}.



We now replace f_{i} by \widetilde{f_{i}}, which is given by restricting f_{i} to I\pm 2\max_{i}|a_{i}|N^{1/2}W\cdot V^{\prime d^{\prime}}, so





\Big|\mathbb{E}_{z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in I\\
y\in[V^{\prime}]\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\widetilde{f_{i}}(x+a_{i}(\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)y^{d^{\prime}})\Big|\gg\delta^{O_{d}(1)}.






We may now apply Theorem B.3 and Lemma B.1(iii-iv) to obtain that





\mathbb{E}_{z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]}\lVert\widetilde{f_{i}}\rVert^{2^{s}}_{U^{s}_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{(\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)\cdot[\pm N^{1/2}/W]}]}\gg_{\vec{a}}\delta^{O_{d,t}(1)}.



As stated \widetilde{f_{i}} is a restriction of f_{i} to an interval; however one may Fourier expand the corresponding interval away (via Lemma B.2; see the proof of Theorem B.3 for similar computations) to obtain that





\mathbb{E}_{z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]}\lVert f_{i}\rVert^{2^{s}}_{U^{s}_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{(\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)\cdot[\pm N^{1/2}/W]}]}\gg_{\vec{a}}\delta^{O_{d,t}(1)}.






We now use the key concatenation inequality in the work of Kravitz, Kuca, and Leng [18, Corollary 6.4]; we work with the statement in [11, Lemma 5.5]. Let m be a power of 2 to be chosen later. Following on from the previous displayed equation, by [11, Lemma 5.5] there exists \ell\ll_{m,s}1 such that





\mathbb{E}_{\vec{z}\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]^{\ell}}\lVert f_{i}\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\Omega[\vec{z}]]}\gg_{\vec{a}}\delta^{O_{d,t,m}(1)},



where





\Omega^{\prime}[\vec{z}]=(\mu_{(\varepsilon\cdot z_{i_{k_{1}}}W+1)\cdot[\pm N^{1/2}/W]}\ast\cdots\ast\mu_{(\varepsilon\cdot z_{i_{k_{m}}}W+1)\cdot[\pm N^{1/2}/W]})_{1\leq k_{1}<k_{2}<\cdots<k_{m}\leq\ell}



and \Omega[\vec{z}] is obtained via duplicating the measures in \Omega^{\prime}[\vec{z}] s times.




The final claim for this proof is that for most choices of z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}, the measures given in \Omega[\vec{z}] distribute mass over [\pm N] roughly uniformly. In particular, we shall soon prove that if m is larger than some absolute constant then




(2.6)

\mathbb{E}_{z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]}\lVert\mu_{(\varepsilon\cdot z_{1}W+1)\cdot[\pm N^{1/2}/W]}\ast\cdots\ast\mu_{(\varepsilon\cdot z_{m}W+1)\cdot[\pm N^{1/2}/W]}\rVert_{2}^{2}\ll\frac{1}{N}.



We will now complete the argument given this statement. Firstly note then by a union bound that





\mathbb{P}_{\vec{z}}[\max_{\nu\in\Omega[\vec{z}]}\lVert\nu\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq L/N]\ll_{m,s}\frac{1}{L}.



Thus taking L=\delta^{-O_{d,t,m}(1)}, we have that





\mathbb{E}_{\vec{z}\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]^{\ell}}\mathbbm{1}[\max_{\nu\in\Omega[\vec{z}]}\lVert\nu\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq L/N]\cdot\lVert f_{i}\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\Omega[\vec{z}]]}\gg_{\vec{a}}\delta^{O_{d,t,m}(1)}.



The desired result then follows from [11, Corollary 5.3]. (This result provides control by a genuine Gowers norm; this may be converted to a Gowers–Peluse norm via Lemma B.1 which gives that for s\geq 2 these norms are equivalent up to polynomial losses.)




It remains to show the key claim (2.6); we do so via exponential sum estimates. Note that for \Theta\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z},





|\hat{\mu}_{(\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)\cdot[\pm N^{1/2}/W]}(\Theta)|^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{h_{1},h_{2}\in[\pm N^{1/2}/W]}e(-\Theta(\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)(h_{1}-h_{2})),



so by Parseval’s identity





\mathbb{E}_{z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]}\lVert\mu_{(\varepsilon\cdot z_{1}W+1)\cdot[\pm N^{1/2}/W]}\ast\cdots\ast\mu_{(\varepsilon\cdot z_{m}W+1)\cdot[\pm N^{1/2}/W]}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\int_{\Theta}F(\Theta)^{m}~d\Theta,



where





\displaystyle F(\Theta)
\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]\\
h_{1},h_{2}\in[\pm N^{1/2}/W]\end{subarray}}e(\Theta(\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)(h_{1}-h_{2}))





\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in[1,N^{1/2}/2]\\
h_{1},h_{2}\in[2N^{1/2}/W]\end{subarray}}e(\Theta(\varepsilon\cdot(z+N^{1/2}/2)W+1)(h_{1}-h_{2}))\pm N^{-1/4}.



Suppose that |F(\Theta)|\geq\eta. Then standard log–free exponential sum estimates (see e.g. [44, Lemma 1.1.16]) imply that there exists q\ll\eta^{-O(1)} such that





\lVert qW\Theta\rVert_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\ll\frac{\eta^{-O(1)}\cdot W}{N}\text{ and }\lVert q\Theta\rVert_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\ll\frac{\eta^{-O(1)}\cdot W}{N^{1/2}}.



Since W^{O(1)}\leq N (by assumption), if \eta^{-O(1)}\leq N^{1/2}/W these inequalities together imply that there exists q\ll\eta^{-O(1)} such that





\lVert q\Theta\rVert_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\ll\frac{\eta^{-O(1)}}{N}.



Upon choosing m sufficiently large in terms of this O(1) exponent of \eta^{-1}, the desired result follows via direct integration on the sizes of level sets of |F(\Theta)|; we omit the details.
∎






2.3. Nilsequence comparison result




The main algebraic computation in this section is proving that one may compare the orbit of a nilsequence on P_{W}(y) and (\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)y^{d^{\prime}}, and that the underlying averages will be close. This will be proven by iterative step–reduction, using the following equidistribution result of Leng [24, Theorem 4]. We refer the reader to Appendix A for relevant definitions.




Theorem 2.8.



Fix an integer \ell\geq 1, and let \delta\in(0,1/10), M,D\geq 1. Suppose that G is a dimension D, at most s-step connected, simply connected, nilpotent Lie group with a given degree k filtration, and the nilmanifold G/\Gamma is complexity at most M with respect to this filtration. Let F\colon G/\Gamma\to\mathbb{C} and let g be an \ell-variable polynomial sequence on G with respect to this filtration.




Furthermore suppose that \lVert F\rVert_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1 and F has G_{(s)}-vertical frequency \xi such that the height of \xi is bounded by M/\delta. Suppose that \min_{1\leq i\leq\ell}N_{i}\geq(M/\delta)^{O_{k,\ell}(D^{O_{k,\ell}(1)})} and





\big|\mathbb{E}_{\vec{n}\in[\vec{N}]}F(g(\vec{n})\Gamma)\big|\geq\delta.



There exists an integer 0\leq r\leq\dim(G/[G,G]) such that:


• 



We have horizontal characters \eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{r}\colon G\to\mathbb{R} with heights bounded above by (M/\delta)^{O_{k,\ell}(D^{O_{k,\ell}(1)})},


• 



For all 1\leq i\leq r, we have \lVert\eta_{i}\circ g\rVert_{C^{\infty}[\vec{N}]}\leq(M/\delta)^{O_{k,\ell}(D^{O_{k,\ell}(1)})},


• 



For any w_{1},\ldots,w_{s}\in G/[G,G] such that w_{i} lie in the joint kernel of \eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{r}, we have





\xi([[[w_{1},w_{2}],w_{3}],\ldots,w_{s}])=0.









Our main result in this subsection is a uniform bound on pairs of nilsequence averages. Handling this problem via an iterative reduction to the case of abelian nilsequences is a key trick in this work, whereas previous approaches have worked directly at the level of fixed step nilsequences. In what follows we will call a congruence class \ell admissible if there exists y such that P_{W}(y)\equiv\ell~\mathrm{mod}~W^{d-d^{\prime}}. We may also use this terminology for moduli other than W^{d-d^{\prime}}. Recall that P_{W}(y) has degree d and leading coefficient |b_{d}|W^{d-d^{\prime}}. In the statement and throughout its proof below we treat d as fixed (that is, we allow all constants to depend on d).




Lemma 2.9.



Let \delta\in(0,1/2) and F(g(n)\Gamma) be a univariate nilsequence of degree k, dimension D and complexity M. Furthermore suppose that \max_{i}|b_{i}|\leq M, N\geq W^{O_{k}(1)}\cdot(M/\delta)^{O_{k}(D^{O_{k}(1)})} and w\geq(M/\delta)^{O_{k}(D^{O_{k}(1)})}.




Then for all \ell\in[W^{d-d^{\prime}}] which are admissible, we have that





\displaystyle\bigg|\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\sim[1/2,1]\\
y\in[(zN/(|b_{d}|W^{d-d^{\prime}}))^{1/d}]\\
P_{W}(y)\equiv\ell~\mathrm{mod}~W^{d-d^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}F(g(P_{W}(y))\Gamma)-\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}]\\
z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]\\
(\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)y^{d^{\prime}}\equiv\ell~\mathrm{mod}~W^{d-d^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\nu(y)F(g((\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)y^{d^{\prime}})\Gamma)\bigg|\leq\delta.







Remark 2.10.



A slightly subtle point (which initially caused the authors substantial confusion) is that such a comparison is best handled by considering residue conditions for the polynomials P_{W}(y) and (\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)y^{d^{\prime}}, rather than for y and z separately. We note that this phenomenon does not arise when P(y) has a simple root, in which case the the W-trick is rather simpler (cf. the difference between Lemma 2.1 and Lemma 2.2).





Proof.

The argument for the second case is rather similar so we will only sketch it. Note that writing [N^{1/2}]=[N^{1/2}/2]\sqcup[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}], splitting the average into two intervals, and replacing \tau_{i}/2 by \tau_{i}/8, we may assume that the interval over which z ranges starts at zero. The argument then proceeds in much the same way as the first case, where one does not need to worry about the leading coefficient V, but instead deals with a bivariate polynomial in y,z. Analysing the leading coefficient z\binom{y}{k} allows for a similar downward induction on the degree of g_{i}, yielding similar information on the coefficients g_{i\ell}, and ultimately (2.12). This concludes our casework; we now proceed from (2.12) and use it to interpret the averages in (2.7) on a lower step nilmanifold.




Let W_{i+1}=W\cdot\widetilde{W}_{i}; we will soon see that this is an appropriate modulus for the next step of the induction. To this end, we write the averages in (2.7) conditionally with respect to admissible values \widetilde{\ell}_{i+1} of \ell_{i} modulo W_{i+1}. By Lemma 2.2,





\mathbb{P}_{y\in[W_{i+1}]}(P_{W}(y)\equiv\widetilde{\ell}_{i+1}~\mathrm{mod}~W_{i+1})=\mathbb{P}_{y,z\in[W_{i+1}]^{2}}(Q(z,y)\equiv\widetilde{\ell}_{i+1}~\mathrm{mod}~W_{i+1}),



so it follows by averaging that there exists a residue class \ell_{i+1} modulo W_{i+1} such that

y\in[(zN/V)^{1/d}]\\

where we lose \tau_{i}/4, say, to account for the fact that the ranges of y,z may not be exact multiples of W_{i+1}. Now let \widetilde{\ell}_{i}\equiv\ell_{i+1}~\mathrm{mod}~\widetilde{W}_{i} (we will abusively also consider \widetilde{\ell}_{i} to be an element of [\widetilde{W}_{i}]). Define h(x)=g_{i}(\widetilde{W}_{i}x+\widetilde{\ell}_{i}).




Given (2.12) and via [24, Lemma A.1], we may factor h=\varepsilon\cdot\widetilde{h}\cdot\gamma where \gamma is (M_{i}/\tau_{i})^{O_{k}(D^{O_{k}(1)})}-rational, \varepsilon is ((M_{i}/\tau_{i})^{O_{k}(D^{O_{k}(1)})},N/\widetilde{W}_{i})-smooth, and \widetilde{h} takes values in K=\cap_{j}\ker\eta_{j}. The rationality of \gamma implies that the sequence \gamma(n)\Gamma_{i} is (M_{i}/\tau_{i})^{O_{k}(D^{O_{k}(1)})}-periodic, and since (M_{i}/\tau_{i})^{O_{k}(D^{O_{k}(1)})}\leq w, it is W-periodic. That is, \gamma(n)\Gamma_{i} is constant on progressions modulo W. For brevity let x_{P}=(P_{W}(y)-\widetilde{\ell}_{i})/\widetilde{W}_{i} so h(x_{P})=g_{i}(P_{W}(y)). Note that x_{P} lies in a fixed progression modulo W when P_{W}(y)\equiv\ell_{i+1}~\mathrm{mod}~W_{i+1}, so \gamma(x_{P})\Gamma_{i}=\gamma_{0}\Gamma_{i} is fixed; assume without loss of generality that \gamma_{0}=\{\gamma_{0}\}. Then updating \gamma(n) to \gamma_{0}^{-1}\gamma(n), \widetilde{h} to \gamma_{0}^{-1}\widetilde{h}\gamma_{0} and \varepsilon to \varepsilon\gamma_{0}, we have reduced to the case that g_{i}(P_{W}(y))\Gamma_{i}=\varepsilon(x_{P})\widetilde{h}(x_{P})\Gamma_{i} and g_{i}(Q(z,y))\Gamma_{i}=\varepsilon(x_{Q})\widetilde{h}(x_{Q})\Gamma_{i}, with the obvious notation.




To remove the dependence on the smooth part \varepsilon, we now break [N/\widetilde{W}_{i}] into segments of length (N/\widetilde{W_{i}})\cdot(M_{i}/\tau_{i})^{-O_{k}(D^{O_{k}(1)})}; call these segments S_{j}=[N_{j},N_{j+1}]. Choosing the (M_{i}/\tau_{i})^{-O_{k}(D^{O_{k}(1)})} quantity appropriately, we may write F_{i}(g_{i}(P_{W}(y))\Gamma_{i}) as \sum_{j}\mathbbm{1}_{x_{P}\in S_{j}}F_{i}(\varepsilon(N_{j})\widetilde{h}(x_{P})\Gamma_{i}) up to suitably small L^{\infty} error (\tau_{i}/8, say). Doing the same for Q(z,y) noting that the average of \nu is bounded by 2, this implies that





\displaystyle\sum_{j}\bigg|
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\sim[1/2,1]\\
y\in[(zN/V)^{1/d}]\\

\displaystyle\bigg|
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\sim[1/2,1]\\
y\in[(zN/V)^{1/d}]\\
P_{W}(y)\equiv\ell_{i+1}~\mathrm{mod}~W_{i+1}\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{x_{P}\in S_{j}}F_{i}(\varepsilon(N_{j})\widetilde{h}(x_{P})\Gamma_{i})

Finally, note that defining h_{i+1} such that h_{i+1}(P_{W}(y))=\widetilde{h}(x_{P}) defines a polynomial sequence in K. We obtain F_{i+1} by taking a vertical Fourier expansion of \widetilde{F_{i+1}}. This completes the inductive step when s\geq 2.




Notice that the above inductive analysis did not require the step of the underlying nilmanifold to be larger than 1 except (perhaps) in the final paragraph where our notation suggests that \ker\xi_{i}\subseteq K. In the case of a 1-step nilmanifold (i.e. a torus), note that the iterative step reduces to the case of \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z} and a function with a vertical frequency. Furthermore, running the argument in this case reduces to a linear phase function.




Step 2: Handling the case of linear exponential phases.




It therefore suffices to consider the case where \widetilde{W}=W^{O(1)}, \ell\in\widetilde{W} is admissible and \Theta\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z} such that





\displaystyle\bigg|\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\sim[1/2,1]\\
y\in[(zN/V)^{d}]\\
P_{W}(y)\equiv\ell~\mathrm{mod}~\widetilde{W}\end{subarray}}e(\Theta\cdot P_{W}(y))-\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}]\\
z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]\\
Q(z,y)\equiv\ell~\mathrm{mod}~\widetilde{W}\end{subarray}}\nu(y)e(\Theta\cdot Q(z,y))\bigg|\geq(M/\delta)^{-O_{k}(D^{O_{k}(1)})}.






At least one of these averages must be suitably large, and after removing the weight \nu(y) in the situation that only the latter average is large (e.g. as we did in Lemma 2.7), an application of a log-free version of Weyl’s inequality (see e.g. [44, Lemma 1.1.16]) yields that \Theta=\frac{a}{q}+\Theta^{\prime} where q\leq W^{O(1)}, |\Theta^{\prime}|\leq W^{O(1)}/N and (a,q)=1 (or a=0 and q=1). We write q=q_{1}q_{2} where q_{1} has all primes factors of q_{1} less than or equal to w and q_{2} has prime factors larger than w.




We first handle the case where q_{2}\neq 1. Note that each average above is invariant (up to suitably small error) under shifting the variables y,z by any t\in\widetilde{W}q_{1}\cdot[q_{2}]. Furthermore, since |\Theta^{\prime}|\leq W^{O(1)}/N, we have that e(\Theta^{\prime}\cdot P_{W}(y+t)) and e(\Theta^{\prime}\cdot P_{W}(y)) differ negligibly when t\in\widetilde{W}q_{1}\cdot[q_{2}]. Analogously, we have that \nu(y+t_{2})e(\Theta^{\prime}\cdot Q(z+t_{1},y+t_{2})) and \nu(y)e(\Theta^{\prime}\cdot Q(z,y)) differ negligibly when t_{1},t_{2}\in\widetilde{W}q_{1}\cdot[q_{2}].




Therefore it suffices to bound






\displaystyle\bigg|\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\sim[1/2,1]\\
y\in[(zN/V)^{1/d}]\\
P_{W}(y)\equiv\ell~\mathrm{mod}~\widetilde{W}\\
t\in\widetilde{W}q_{1}\cdot[q_{2}]\end{subarray}}e(\Theta^{\prime}\cdot P_{W}(y))\cdot e(a/q\cdot P_{W}(y+t))





\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}]\\
z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]\\
Q(z,y)\equiv\ell~\mathrm{mod}~\widetilde{W}\\
t_{1},t_{2}\in\widetilde{W}q_{1}\cdot[q_{2}]\end{subarray}}\nu(y)e(\Theta^{\prime}\cdot Q(z,y))\cdot e(a/q\cdot Q(z+t_{1},y+t_{2}))\bigg|



Observe that we may write a/q=a_{1}/q_{1}+a_{2}/q_{2} and that the shifts by t,t_{1},t_{2} leave y and z invariant modulo q_{1}. Thus we have that the above is bounded by





\displaystyle\leq\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in[q_{2}]\end{subarray}}e(a_{2}/q_{2}\cdot P_{W}(t))\Big|+2\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1},t_{2}\in[q_{2}]\end{subarray}}e(a_{2}/q_{2}\cdot Q(t_{1},t_{2}))\Big|



where we use that the expectation of \nu is bounded by 2. These expectations are precisely exponential sums in \mathbb{Z}/q_{2}\mathbb{Z} and we have that all the coefficients of Q(t_{1},t_{2}) and P_{W}(t) are coprime to q_{2}. By using the Chinese remainder theorem, it suffices to consider the case where q_{2} is a power of a prime p larger than w. For Q(t_{1},t_{2}) the result is immediate as fixing any t_{2} such that (t_{2},p)=1, we obtain a linear exponential sum with complete cancellation. In the case of P_{W}(y), this is a complete exponential sum over a modulus p^{b} with p>w and b\geq 1. For b>1, it suffices to bound





\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in[p^{b}]\end{subarray}}e(a^{\prime}/p^{b}\cdot P_{W}(t))\Big|
\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[p^{b-1}]\end{subarray}}\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in[p]\end{subarray}}e(a^{\prime}/p^{b}\cdot P_{W}(p^{b-1}t+r))\Big|





\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[p^{b-1}]\end{subarray}}\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in[p]\end{subarray}}e(a^{\prime}/p^{b}\cdot p^{b-1}t\cdot P_{W}^{\prime}(r))\Big|;



here P_{W}^{\prime}(y) denotes the derivative of P_{W}(y) and (a^{\prime},p)=1. The inner sum evaluates to zero whenever P_{W}^{\prime}(r)\not\equiv 0~\mathrm{mod}~p so noting that P_{W}^{\prime} is not identically zero modulo p we obtain a bound of the form \ll w^{-1} in this case as well. Finally the case of b=1 follows via the Weil bound [15, Theorem 11.23] and we obtain a bound of the form \ll w^{-1/2}.




Therefore we have reduced to the case where q_{2}=1. In this case we will in fact establish the stronger bound






\displaystyle\bigg|\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\sim[1/2,1]\\

Observe that \mathbbm{1}[P_{W}(y)\equiv\ell~\mathrm{mod}~\widetilde{W}]=\mathbb{E}_{t\in\widetilde{W}}e(t/\widetilde{W}\cdot(P_{W}(y)-\ell)) and similarly for Q(z,y). Via Lemma 2.2, and potentially replacing \Theta by a fixed shift \Theta+t_{0}/\widetilde{W}, it suffices to prove that






\displaystyle\bigg|\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\sim[1/2,1]\\
y\in[(zN/V)^{1/d}]\end{subarray}}e(\Theta\cdot P_{W}(y))-\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}]\\
z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]\end{subarray}}\nu(y)e(\Theta\cdot Q(z,y)))\bigg|\leq N^{-\Omega_{d}(1)}.



Recall that \Theta=a/q+\Theta^{\prime} with |\Theta^{\prime}|\leq W^{O(1)}/N and |q|\leq W^{O(1)} with all prime factors at most w; note that this is unaffected by absorbing t_{0}/\widetilde{W} into the a/q part. It thus suffices to obtain the bound






\displaystyle\bigg|\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\sim[1/2,1]\\
y\in[(zN/V)^{1/d}]\\
t\in[q]\end{subarray}}e(\Theta^{\prime}\cdot P_{W}(y))\cdot e(a/q\cdot P_{W}(y+t))





\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}]\\
z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]\\
t_{1},t_{2}\in[q]\end{subarray}}\nu(y)e(\Theta^{\prime}\cdot Q(z,y))\cdot e(a/q\cdot Q(z+t_{1},y+t_{2}))\bigg|\leq N^{-\Omega_{d}(1)}.



Note that (y+t) and (z+t_{1},y+t_{2}) range over \mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z} and (\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{2} respectively. As all prime factors of q are at most w, we may apply Lemma 2.2 and deduce that inner expectation over t and t_{1},t_{2} is a constant independent of y,z and is the same for both averages. Therefore it suffices to bound






\displaystyle\bigg|\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\sim[1/2,1]\\
y\in[(zN/(|b_{d}|W^{d-d^{\prime}}))^{1/d}]\end{subarray}}e(\Theta^{\prime}\cdot P_{W}(y))-\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}]\\
z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]\end{subarray}}\nu(y)e(\Theta^{\prime}\cdot(\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)y^{d^{\prime}})\bigg|\leq N^{-\Omega_{d}(1)}.



Observing that |y^{d-1}|\leq N^{(d-1)/d} for the first integral and |y^{d^{\prime}}|\leq N^{1/2} for the second, it suffices to prove that






\displaystyle\bigg|\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\sim[1/2,1]\\
y\in[(zN/(|b_{d}|W^{d-d^{\prime}}))^{1/d}]\end{subarray}}e(\Theta^{\prime}\cdot b_{d}W^{d-d^{\prime}}y^{d})-\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}]\\
z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]\end{subarray}}\nu(y)e(\Theta^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon\cdot zWy^{d^{\prime}})\bigg|\leq N^{-\Omega_{d}(1)}.






We now remove the contribution of z. Observe that if z is a uniformly random integer in [N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}], then zN^{-1/2} is the uniform distribution on [1/2,1] up to a L_{\infty}-error of N^{-1/2}, and so we have





\bigg|\mathbb{E}_{z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]}\nu(y)e(\Theta^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon\cdot zWy^{d^{\prime}})-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim[1/2,1]}\nu(y)e(\Theta^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon\cdot zWy^{d^{\prime}}\cdot N^{1/2})\bigg|\ll W^{O(1)}N^{-1/2},



uniformly for y\in[(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}]. Therefore it suffices to obtain the bound






\displaystyle\sup_{z\in[1/2,1]}\bigg|\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[(zN/(|b_{d}|W^{d-d^{\prime}}))^{1/d}]\end{subarray}}e(\Theta^{\prime}\cdot b_{d}W^{d-d^{\prime}}y^{d})-\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}]\end{subarray}}\nu(y)e(\Theta^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon\cdot zWy^{d^{\prime}}\cdot N^{1/2})\bigg|\leq N^{-\Omega_{d}(1)}.



Converting the Riemann sum to an integral, it suffices to obtain the same shape bound on






\displaystyle\sup_{z\in[1/2,1]}\bigg|((zN/(|b_{d}|W^{d-d^{\prime}}))^{-1/d})\int_{0}^{(zN/(|b_{d}|W^{d-d^{\prime}}))^{1/d}}e(\Theta^{\prime}\cdot b_{d}W^{d-d^{\prime}}y^{d})~dy





\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-(N/W^{2})^{-1/(2d^{\prime})}\int_{0}^{(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}}\nu(y)e(\Theta^{\prime}N^{1/2}\cdot\varepsilon\cdot zWy^{d^{\prime}})~dy\bigg|.



Via a change of variable this is equal to






\displaystyle\sup_{z\in[1/2,1]}\bigg|(Nz)^{-1/d}\int_{0}^{(Nz)^{1/d}}e(\varepsilon\cdot\Theta^{\prime}\cdot y^{d})~dy-(Nz)^{-1/d^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{(Nz)^{1/d^{\prime}}}\nu(y\cdot(N^{1/2}\cdot zW)^{-1/d^{\prime}})e(\Theta^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon\cdot y^{d^{\prime}})~dy\bigg|.



This is equal (after substituting in for \nu(y)) to





\displaystyle\sup_{z\in[1/2,1]}
\displaystyle\bigg|(Nz)^{-1/d}\int_{0}^{(Nz)^{1/d}}e(\varepsilon\cdot\Theta^{\prime}\cdot y^{d})~dy





\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-(Nz)^{-1/d^{\prime}}\cdot(d^{\prime}/d)\int_{0}^{(Nz)^{1/d^{\prime}}}y^{-(d-d^{\prime})/d}(Nz)^{(d-d^{\prime})/(dd^{\prime})}e(\Theta^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon\cdot y^{d^{\prime}})~dy\bigg|.



Observe that (Nz)^{-1/d^{\prime}}\cdot(Nz)^{(d-d^{\prime})/(dd^{\prime})}=(Nz)^{-1/d} and then making the change of variable y^{\prime}=y^{d^{\prime}/d} the two integrals are (intentionally) identical. This completes the proof.
∎






2.4. Stashing and completing the proof




We now establish the crucial positivity property of \Lambda_{\operatorname{Model}}(f,\ldots,f). In the following statement, implicit constants depend on d,\vec{a}.




Lemma 2.11.



Consider a function f:[N]\to[0,1] with \mathbb{E}[f]\geq\delta. We have the following bounds:


• 



Suppose that d^{\prime}=1 and t=3. If N\geq\exp(\log(1/\delta)^{O(1)}), then





\Lambda_{\operatorname{Model}}(f,\cdots,f)\geq\exp(-(\log(1/\delta))^{O(1)}).




• 



Suppose that d^{\prime}=1 and t\geq 4. If N\geq\exp(\exp(\log(1/\delta)^{O(1)})), then





\Lambda_{\operatorname{Model}}(f,\cdots,f)\geq\exp(-\exp(\log(1/\delta)^{O(1)})).




• 



Suppose that d^{\prime}>1. If N\geq\exp(\exp(1/\delta^{O(1)})), then





\Lambda_{\operatorname{Model}}(f,\cdots,f)\geq\exp(-\exp(\delta^{-O(1)})).









Proof.



In each case it suffices to prove for all z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}] that





\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N]\\
y\in[(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}]\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}f(x+a_{i}(\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)y^{d^{\prime}})



is suitably large. We have used here that \nu(y)\gg_{d}1. Observe that we may break f into progressions modulo |\varepsilon\cdot zW+1| and be left with arithmetic progressions of length N^{1/2}/W. At least a \delta/3-fraction of these have average at least \delta/3 and hence breaking into these progressions, shifting and rescaling, it suffices to establish a lower bound for the operator





\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N^{1/2}/W]\\
y\in[(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}]\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\widetilde{f}(x+\varepsilon\cdot a_{i}y^{d^{\prime}})



where \mathbb{E}_{x\in[N^{1/2}/W]}\widetilde{f}(x)\geq\delta/3. When d^{\prime}=1, this is a standard supersaturation argument for (essentially) arithmetic progressions. The first bound follows from (an essentially trivial) modification of the results of Kelley–Meka [16]. The second bound for t=4 follows from (trivial adaptions to) work of Green–Tao [12] while the case of t\geq 5 follows from (trivial adaptions to) work of Leng, Sah and the second author [27]. The case d^{\prime}>1 follows from noting that supersaturation applies to the work of Prendiville [40]. Let \tau=\exp(-\exp(\delta^{-O(1)})). In particular, choosing any m\in[\tau\cdot(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}/2,\tau\cdot(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}] we may break x into progressions modulo m^{d^{\prime}} and consider y which are multiples of m. Observe that this gives a change of variable which preserves the pattern (precisely as we are in the homogeneous case). An \Omega(\delta)-fraction of these have average at least \Omega(\delta). Observe that these progressions have length \tau^{-1} and hence any progression with density \Omega(\delta) contains the desired pattern by [40, Theorem 1.1].
∎





Remark 2.12.



It appears likely using that the configurations considered by Prendiville [40] are controlled by global Gowers norms via the results of Peluse [36], and thus that the results of [28] may be used to improve the obtained bounds. In particular, likely one may adopt the strategy in [27] by at each stage passing to progressions where one guarantees that the common difference is an appropriate perfect power. We leave the details to the interested reader.





We now state the quasipolynomial inverse theorem of Leng, Sah and the second author [28, Theorem 1.2]. Various conventions regarding nilsequences are discussed carefully in Appendix A; our conventions are completely standard.




Theorem 2.13.



Fix \delta\in(0,1/2). Suppose that f\colon[N]\to\mathbb{C} is 1-bounded and





\lVert f\rVert_{U^{s+1}[N]}\geq\delta.



Then there exists a nilmanifold G/\Gamma of degree s, complexity at most M, and dimension at most d, a polynomial sequence g(n) on G, as well as a function F on G/\Gamma which is at most K-Lipschitz such that





|\mathbb{E}_{n\in[N]}[f(n)\overline{F(g(n)\Gamma)}]|\geq\varepsilon,



where we may take





d\leq\log(1/\delta)^{O_{s}(1)}\emph{and}\varepsilon^{-1},K,M\leq\exp(\log(1/\delta)^{O_{s}(1)}).







We now proceed to the proof of Theorem 1.1.




Proof.



Let w=(\log N)^{1/2} and recall that





W=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq w\\
(p,d)=1\end{subarray}}p\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq w\\
(p,d!)\neq 1\end{subarray}}p^{2d}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq w^{1/2}\end{subarray}}p^{w^{1/3}}.



Standard elementary estimates yield that W\ll_{d}4^{w}, which is subpolynomial in N.




We begin by supposing that f_{i}:[N]\to\mathbb{C} are 1-bounded and





\Big|\Lambda_{W}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{t})-\Lambda_{\operatorname{Model}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{t})\Big|\geq\delta.



Define





\Lambda_{\operatorname{Diff}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{t}):=\Lambda_{W}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{t})-\Lambda_{\operatorname{Model}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{t}),



and





\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{j}
\displaystyle(f_{1},\ldots,f_{j-1},f_{j+1},\ldots,f_{t})(x)





\displaystyle:=\mathbbm{1}_{x\in[N]}\cdot\Big(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim[1/2,1]}\mathbb{E}_{y\in[(zN/(|b_{d}|W^{d-d^{\prime}}))^{1/d}]}\prod_{i\in[t]\setminus\{j\}}f_{i}(x+(a_{i}-a_{j})P_{W}(y))





\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}]\\
z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]\end{subarray}}\nu(y)\cdot\prod_{i\in[t]\setminus\{j\}}f_{i}(x+(a_{i}-a_{j})(\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)y^{d^{\prime}})\Big).



Observe that if f_{i} are all supported on [N] then





\Lambda_{\operatorname{Diff}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{x\in[N]}\mathcal{D}_{j}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{j-1},f_{j+1},\ldots,f_{t})(x)\cdot f_{j}(x).



This implies via Cauchy–Schwarz that





\mathbb{E}_{x\in[N]}[|\mathcal{D}_{j}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{j-1},f_{j+1},\ldots,f_{t})(x)|^{2}]\geq\delta^{2}.



However, observe that





\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\in[N]}
\displaystyle[|\mathcal{D}_{j}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{j-1},f_{j+1},\ldots,f_{t})(x)|^{2}]





\displaystyle=\Lambda_{\operatorname{Diff}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{j-1},\overline{\mathcal{D}_{j}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{j-1},f_{j+1},\ldots,f_{t})},f_{j+1},\ldots,f_{t}).



Observe that combining Lemma 2.6 and Lemma 2.7, we obtain that there exists s=s(d,t)\geq 1 such that





\lVert\mathcal{D}_{j}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{j-1},f_{j+1},\ldots,f_{t})\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}^{s}[N;\mu_{W^{d-d^{\prime}}\cdot[N/W^{-(d-d^{\prime})}]}]}\gg_{P,\vec{a}}\delta^{O_{d,t}(1)},



or





\lVert\mathcal{D}_{j}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{j-1},f_{j+1},\ldots,f_{t})\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}^{s}[N;\mu_{[N]}]}\gg_{P,\vec{a}}\delta^{O_{d,t}(1)}.



Therefore we may apply the quasipolynomial inverse theorem for the Gowers norm [28, Theorem 1.2] combined with Lemma B.1 to deduce that there exists F_{j} which is 1-bounded and is a nilsequence of complexity \exp((\log(1/\delta)^{O(1)}) along each progression of common difference W^{d-d^{\prime}} and that





\Big|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim[N]}[F_{j}(x)\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{x\in[N]}\cdot\mathcal{D}_{j}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{j-1},f_{j+1},\ldots,f_{t})(x)]\Big|\gg\exp(-\log(1/\delta)^{O(1)}).



This is precisely equivalent to





|\Lambda_{\operatorname{Diff}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{j-1},F_{j}(\cdot)\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\cdot\in[N]}\mathbbm{,}f_{j+1},\ldots,f_{t})|\gg\exp(-\log(1/\delta)^{O(1)}).



This previous set of manipulations (in particular defining the associated dual function and then applying Cauchy–Schwarz in this way) has been termed stashing by Manners [33].




Iterating this procedure from j=1 to j=t, we may obtain





|\Lambda_{\operatorname{Diff}}(F_{1}(\cdot)\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\cdot\in[N]},\ldots,F_{t}(\cdot)\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\cdot\in[N]})|\gg\exp(-\log(1/\delta)^{O(1)}).



Here F_{j} are each nilsequences on progressions of common difference W^{d-d^{\prime}}.




We now apply a Fourier expansion to \mathbbm{1}_{\cdot\in[N]}, and after possibly twisting F_{1} by a phase and abusively denoting this as F_{1}, obtain that





|\Lambda_{\operatorname{Diff}}(F_{1},\ldots,F_{t})|\gg\exp(-\log(1/\delta)^{O(1)}).






Expanding this condition out gives





\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}_{z\sim[1/2,1]}
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N]\\
y\in[(zN/(|b_{d}|W^{d-d^{\prime}}))^{1/d}]\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}F_{i}(x+a_{i}P_{W}(y))





\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N]\\
y\in[(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}]\\
z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]\end{subarray}}\nu(y)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{t}F_{i}(x+a_{i}(\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)y^{d^{\prime}})\Big|\geq\exp(-(\log(1/\delta)^{O(1)}).



This implies that





\displaystyle\sup_{x\in[N]}\Big|\mathbb{E}_{z\sim[1/2,1]}
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[(zN/(|b_{d}|W^{d-d^{\prime}}))^{1/d}]\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}F_{i}(x+a_{i}P_{W}(y))





\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}]\\
z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]\end{subarray}}\nu(y)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{t}F_{i}(x+a_{i}(\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)y^{d^{\prime}})\Big|\geq\exp(-\log(1/\delta)^{O(1)}).



We next consider the set of residue classes attained by P_{W}(y) modulo W^{d-d^{\prime}}; call this set of residue classes \mathcal{W} and consider \ell\in\mathcal{W}. Via Lemma 2.2, we have that





\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N]\\
\ell\in\mathcal{W}\end{subarray}}\Big|\mathbb{E}_{z\sim[1/2,1]}
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[(zN/(|b_{d}|W^{d-d^{\prime}}))^{1/d}]\\
P_{W}(y)\equiv\ell~\mathrm{mod}~W^{d-d^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}F_{i}(x+a_{i}P_{W}(y))





\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[(N/W^{2})^{1/(2d^{\prime})}]\\
z\in[N^{1/2}/2,N^{1/2}]\\
(\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)y^{d^{\prime}}\equiv\ell~\mathrm{mod}~W^{d-d^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\nu(y)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{t}F_{i}(x+a_{i}(\varepsilon\cdot zW+1)y^{d^{\prime}})\Big|\geq\exp(-\log(1/\delta)^{O(1)}).



Fixing x and \ell\in\mathcal{W}, we are exactly in the position to apply Lemma 2.9. In particular, if w\geq\exp(\log(1/\delta)^{O(1)}) we obtain a contradiction. Therefore we have that





\big|\Lambda_{\operatorname{Diff}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{t})\big|\leq\exp(-(\log\log N)^{\Omega(1)}).






Now suppose that A\subseteq[N] is free of nontrivial progressions of the form





x+a_{1}P(y),\ldots,x+a_{t}P(y).



We then have that





\big|\Lambda_{W}(\mathbbm{1}_{A},\ldots,\mathbbm{1}_{A})\big|\leq N^{-\Omega(1)}.



The desired result then follows by combining our above bound on \Lambda_{\operatorname{Diff}} with Lemma 2.11.
∎







3. Transference in finite fields




The main theorem for this section is the following result. By combining Theorem 3.1 with standard supersaturation results we will immediately be able to deduce Theorem 1.6. We omit the details of this deduction because they are essentially the same as in the previous section.




Theorem 3.1.



Let \mathcal{P}=(x+P_{1}(y),\ldots,x+P_{t}(y)) be a transferable polynomial pattern with P_{i}(0)=0 for all i. Let \mathcal{P}^{\ast}:=(x+P_{1}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{y}),\ldots,x+P_{t}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{y})) denote the associated transferred (linear) pattern. Let f_{i}:\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C} be 1-bounded for i=1,\ldots,t and N be prime. Then





\left|\Lambda_{\mathcal{P}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{t})-\Lambda_{\mathcal{P}^{\ast}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{t})\right|\leq\exp(-c_{\mathcal{P}}(\log\log N)^{c_{\mathcal{P}}}).







3.1. Distributional tools




We now develop the distributional tools on nilsequences which will play a crucial role in the proof of Theorem 3.1. In this section we will ultimately derive our results using totally equidistributed nilsequences. Therefore we essentially rely on the equidistribution results of Green and Tao [14] (as quantified in [45]); we first require a periodic variant.




Theorem 3.2.



Fix an integer \ell\geq 1, \delta\in(0,1/10), M,D\geq 1, and F\colon G/\Gamma\to\mathbb{C}. Suppose that G is a dimension D simply connected nilpotent Lie group with a given degree k filtration, and the nilmanifold G/\Gamma is complexity at most M with respect to this filtration. Let g be an \ell-variable polynomial sequence on G with respect to this filtration.




Furthermore suppose that \lVert F\rVert_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1 and that N\geq(M/\delta)^{\exp(D^{O_{k,\ell}(1)})} with N prime. Furthermore suppose that g is N-periodic in each coordinate (i.e. g(\vec{n})g(\vec{n}+N\cdot e_{k})^{-1}\in\Gamma for all \vec{n}).




If





\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\vec{n}\in[(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{\ell}]}F(g(\vec{n})\Gamma)-\int_{G/\Gamma}F\Big|\geq\delta,



then we may write





g(\vec{n})=\varepsilon\cdot g^{\prime}(\vec{n})\cdot\gamma(\vec{n})



where g^{\prime} takes values G^{\prime} with \operatorname{dim}(G^{\prime})\leq\operatorname{dim}(G)-1, \gamma(\vec{n})\in\Gamma and \varepsilon\in G such that d_{G}(\varepsilon,\operatorname{id}_{G})\leq(M/\delta)^{\exp(D^{O_{k,\ell}(1)})}. Furthermore if g(0)=\operatorname{id}_{G} then we may take \varepsilon=\operatorname{id}_{G}.





Proof.



Note that one may write g(\vec{0})=\{g(\vec{0})\}[g(\vec{0})] with [g(\vec{0})]\in\Gamma and \psi_{G}(\{g(\vec{0})\})\in[-1/2,1/2)^{D} (the Mal’cev coordinates of the second kind). Replacing g by \widetilde{g}=\{g(\vec{0})\}^{-1}g[g(\vec{0})]^{-1} and F by F(\{g(\vec{0})\}\cdot) we may assume g(\vec{0})=\operatorname{id}_{G}.




Observe that





\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\vec{n}\in(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{\ell}}F(g(\vec{n})\Gamma)-\int_{G/\Gamma}F\Big|\geq\delta



implies that for all t\geq 1





\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\vec{n}\in[[tN]^{\ell}]}F(g(\vec{n})\Gamma)-\int_{G/\Gamma}F\Big|\geq\delta.



By Fourier expansion (see e.g. [39, Lemma A.9]) and then applying Theorem 2.8, there exist a nonzero horizontal character \eta_{t} such that |\eta_{t}|\leq(M/\delta)^{O_{k,\ell}\big(2^{D^{O_{k,\ell}(1)}}\big)} with





\lVert\eta_{t}\circ g\rVert_{C^{\infty}[[tN]^{\ell}]}\leq(M/\delta)^{O_{k,\ell}\big(2^{D^{O_{k,\ell}(1)}}\big)}.



By the Pigeonhole principle there exists an infinite subsequence of t where \eta_{t}=\eta. Taking t\to\infty along this infinite subsequence we obtain that in fact





\lVert\eta\circ g\rVert_{C^{\infty}[[N]^{\ell}]}=0.






Then the proof of [24, Lemma A.1] yields





g(\vec{n})=g^{\prime}(\vec{n})\gamma(\vec{n})



where \gamma is Q-periodic with Q\leq(M/\delta)^{\exp(D^{O_{k,\ell}(1)})}, g^{\prime}(\vec{0})=\gamma(\vec{0})=0 and g^{\prime}\in\operatorname{ker}(\eta). Letting Q^{\prime} be such that QQ^{\prime}\equiv 1~\mathrm{mod}~N, we have that





\displaystyle g(\vec{n})
\displaystyle=g(QQ^{\prime}\vec{n})\cdot g(QQ^{\prime}\vec{n})^{-1}g(\vec{n})





\displaystyle=g^{\prime}(QQ^{\prime}\vec{n})\cdot\gamma(Q(Q^{\prime}\vec{n}))\cdot g(QQ^{\prime}\vec{n})^{-1}g(\vec{n}).



Observe that \gamma(Q(Q^{\prime}\vec{n}))\cdot g(QQ^{\prime}\vec{n})^{-1}g(\vec{n})\in\Gamma as desired. To see that g^{\prime}(QQ^{\prime}\vec{n}) is appropriately periodic note that





\displaystyle g^{\prime}(QQ^{\prime}(\vec{n}+Ne_{\ell}))
\displaystyle=g(QQ^{\prime}(\vec{n}+Ne_{\ell}))\gamma^{-1}(QQ^{\prime}(\vec{n}+Ne_{\ell}))





\displaystyle=g(QQ^{\prime}\vec{n})\cdot(g(QQ^{\prime}\vec{n})^{-1}g(QQ^{\prime}(\vec{n}+Ne_{\ell})))\cdot\gamma^{-1}(QQ^{\prime}(\vec{n}+Ne_{\ell}))





\displaystyle=g^{\prime}(QQ^{\prime}\vec{n})\cdot\gamma(QQ^{\prime}\vec{n})\cdot(g(QQ^{\prime}\vec{n})^{-1}g(QQ^{\prime}(\vec{n}+Ne_{\ell})))\cdot\gamma^{-1}(QQ^{\prime}(\vec{n}+Ne_{\ell})).



Noting that \gamma(QQ^{\prime}\vec{n})\in\Gamma, (g(QQ^{\prime}\vec{n})^{-1}g(QQ^{\prime}(\vec{n}+Ne_{\ell})))\in\Gamma and \gamma^{-1}(QQ^{\prime}(\vec{n}+Ne_{\ell}))\in\Gamma completes the proof.
∎





We additionally require the following periodic factorization theorem.




Theorem 3.3.



Fix A\geq 2. Suppose that G is a dimension D simply connected nilpotent Lie group with a given degree k filtration, and the nilmanifold G/\Gamma is complexity at most M^{\prime} with respect to this filtration. Let g be a polynomial sequence with respect to this filtration such that g(0)=\operatorname{id}_{G} and g is N-periodic.




Then there exists M\in\bigg[M^{\prime},(M^{\prime})^{A^{(D+2)^{O_{k}(1)}}}\bigg] such that:


• 



g=g^{\prime}\gamma with g^{\prime}(0)=\gamma(0)=\operatorname{id}_{G}, and g^{\prime} is N-periodic on G^{\prime}/(G^{\prime}\cap\Gamma),


• 



G^{\prime} is an M-rational subgroup of G and G^{\prime}/\Gamma^{\prime} has a Mal’cev basis \mathcal{X}^{\prime} which comprises M-rational combinations of the Mal’cev basis \mathcal{X} of G/\Gamma,


• 



Let g^{\prime}=g_{1}^{n}\cdots g_{k}^{n^{k}}. For each nonzero i-th horizontal character \eta of height at most M^{A} on G^{\prime} (given filtration G_{i}^{\prime}=G^{\prime}\cap G_{i}) we have that





\lVert\eta(g_{i})\rVert_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\geq M^{A}\cdot N^{-i},




• 



\gamma takes values only in \Gamma.







Proof.



A qualitative version of this proposition appears as [4, Proposition 5.2]. For the quantitative bound, [45, Theorem A.6] tracks the quantitative details of the nonperiodic analogous factorization algorithm from [14] and inputting Theorem 3.2, we get the stated quantitative bounds.
∎





We now come to the crux of this section regarding the equidistribution of transferable polynomial patterns. We remark that we operate essentially throughout with the Lie algebra as in [1].




Theorem 3.4.



Let \delta\in(0,1/2), suppose that G is a dimension D simply connected nilpotent Lie group with a given degree k filtration and the nilmanifold G/\Gamma is complexity at most M with respect to this filtration. Let g be an N-periodic polynomial sequence with respect to this filtration with g(0)=\operatorname{id}_{G} and N\geq(M/\delta)^{\exp(O_{\mathcal{P}}(D^{O_{k}(1)}))}.




Let \mathcal{P}=(x+P_{1}(y),\ldots,x+P_{t}(y)) be a transferable polynomial pattern with P_{i}(0)=0. Let \Psi:=\mathcal{P}^{\ast} denote the transferred pattern. Let G^{\Psi}/\Gamma^{\Psi} denote the corresponding Leibman nilmanifold.




Consider an M-Lipschitz function F:G^{\Psi}/\Gamma^{\Psi}\to\mathbb{C} such that




(3.1)

\bigg|\mathbb{E}_{x,y\in\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}}F((g(x+P_{1}(y)),\ldots,g(x+P_{t}(y))))-\int_{G^{\Psi}/\Gamma^{\Psi}}F\bigg|\geq\delta.






Let the Taylor expansion of g(n) be





g(n)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}g_{j}^{n^{j}}.



Then there exists a nontrivial \ell-th horizontal character \eta_{\ell} of height at most \leq(M/\delta)^{\exp(O_{\mathcal{P}}(D^{O_{k}(1)}))} such that





\lVert\eta_{\ell}(g_{\ell})\rVert_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}=0.







Proof.



Let





g(n)=\exp\bigg(\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\widetilde{g}_{i}n^{i}\bigg),



with \widetilde{g}_{i}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\ell}:=\log(G_{\ell}); that is, \mathfrak{g}_{\ell} is the Lie algebra associated with G_{\ell}.




We will frequently need to operate within the Lie algebra \mathfrak{g}^{t}:=\log(G)^{t}. We identify this vector space with \mathfrak{g}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{t} via the following map on elementary tensors (in the backwards direction)





a\otimes v\mapsto(v_{1}a,\ldots,v_{t}a).



Note that under this identification we inherit the Lie bracket operation





[a\otimes v_{1},b\otimes v_{2}]=[a,b]\otimes v_{1}v_{2},



where the final product is entrywise.




Recall that





G^{\Psi}=\langle g_{\ell}^{v_{\ell}}:g_{\ell}\in G_{\ell},v_{\ell}\in\Psi^{[\ell]}\rangle.



To see that (g(x+P_{1}(y)),\ldots,g(x+P_{t}(y))) takes values in G^{\Psi}, we just need to observe that ((x+P_{1}(y))^{\ell},\ldots,(x+P_{t}(y))^{\ell}) lies in \Psi^{[\ell]} for each \ell. But this is clear since





\{((x+P_{1}(y))^{\ell},\ldots,(x+P_{t}(y))^{\ell})\}_{x,y\in\mathbb{Z}}\subset\{((x+P_{1}^{*}(\boldsymbol{y}))^{\ell},\ldots,(x+P_{t}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{y}))^{\ell})\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}.






We now note that the complexity of G^{\Psi}/\Gamma^{\Psi} can easily be bounded by M^{O_{\mathcal{P}}(D^{O_{k}(1)})}. Appendix A Conventions regarding nilsequences




We begin this appendix by giving the precise definition of the complexity of a nilmanifold; this definition is exactly as in [45, Definition 6.1].




Definition A.1.



Let s\geq 1 be an integer and let K>0. A filtered nilmanifold G/\Gamma of degree s and complexity at most K consists of the following:


• 



a nilpotent, connected, and simply connected Lie group G of dimension m, which can be identified with its Lie algebra \log G via the exponential map \exp\colon\log G\to G;


• 



a filtration G_{\bullet}=(G_{i})_{i\geq 0} of closed connected subgroups G_{i} of G with





G=G_{0}=G_{1}\geqslant G_{1}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant G_{s}\geqslant G_{s+1}=\mathrm{Id}_{G}



such that [G_{i},G_{j}]\subseteq G_{i+j} for all i,j\geq 0 (equivalently [\log G_{i},\log G_{j}]\subseteq\log G_{i+j});


• 



a discrete cocompact subgroup \Gamma of G; and


• 



a linear basis \mathcal{X}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\} of \log G, known as a Mal’cev basis.



We, furthermore, require that this data obeys the following conditions:


(1) 



for 1\leq i,j\leq m, one has Lie algebra relations





[X_{i},X_{j}]=\sum_{i,j<k\leq m}c_{ijk}X_{k}



for rational numbers c_{ijk} of height at most K (we will often refer to these as the Lie bracket structure constants);


(2) 



for each 1\leq i\leq s, the Lie algebra \log G_{i} is spanned by \{X_{j}\colon m-\dim(G_{i})<j\leq m\}; and


(3) 



the subgroup \Gamma consists of all elements of the form \exp(t_{1}X_{1})\cdots\exp(t_{m}X_{m}) with t_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}.







We note that the conditions imply [G,G_{s}]=\mathrm{Id}_{G}, i.e., G_{s} is contained in the center of G (commutes with every element).




Next, we will define polynomial sequences in filtered nilpotent groups. This concrete definition is equivalent (by [14, Lemma 6.7]) to the one given in [14].




Definition A.2.



We adopt the conventions of Definition A.1. Let G be a filtered nilpotent group of degree s. A function g\colon\mathbb{Z}\to G is a polynomial sequence if there exist elements g_{i}\in G_{i} for i=0,\ldots,s such that





g(n)=g_{0}g_{1}^{\binom{n}{1}}\cdots g_{s}^{\binom{n}{s}},



where \binom{n}{i}=\frac{1}{i!}\prod_{j=0}^{i-1}(n-j), for all n\in\mathbb{Z}. We say a polynomial sequence g(n) is N-periodic with respect to a lattice \Gamma if





g(n)g(n+N)^{-1}\in\Gamma



for all n\in\mathbb{Z}.





We will denote the set of polynomial sequences g\colon\mathbb{Z}\to G relative to the filtration G_{\bullet} of G by \operatorname{poly}(\mathbb{Z},G_{\bullet}). It turns out that \operatorname{poly}(\mathbb{Z},G_{\bullet}) is a group under the natural multiplication of sequences–this is due to Lazard [21] and Leibman [22, 23].




Now we can define Mal’cev coordinates, the explicit metrics on G and G/\Gamma used in our work, and the precise definition of the Lipschitz norm of functions on G/\Gamma. These definitions are exactly as in [14, Appendix A].




Definition A.3.



We adopt the conventions of Definition A.1. Given a Mal’cev basis \mathcal{X} and g\in G, there exists (t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m} such that





g=\exp(t_{1}X_{1}+t_{2}X_{2}+\ldots t_{m}X_{m}).



We define Mal’cev coordinates of first kind \psi_{\exp}=\psi_{\exp,\mathcal{X}}\colon G\to\mathbb{R}^{m} for g relative to \mathcal{X} by





\psi_{\exp}(g):=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}).



Given g\in G there also exists (u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m} such that





g=\exp(u_{1}X_{1})\cdots\exp(u_{m}X_{m}),



and we define the Mal’cev coordinates of second kind \psi=\psi_{\mathcal{X}}\colon G\to\mathbb{R}^{m} for g relative to \mathcal{X} by





\psi(g):=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}).



We then define a metric d=d_{\mathcal{X}} on G by





d(x,y):=\inf\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min(\lVert\psi(x_{i}x_{i+1}^{-1})\rVert,\lVert\psi(x_{i+1}x_{i}^{-1})\rVert)\colon n\in\mathbb{N},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}\in G,x_{1}=x,x_{n+1}=y\bigg\},



where \lVert\cdot\rVert denotes the \ell^{\infty}-norm on \mathbb{R}^{m}, and define a metric on G/\Gamma by





d(x\Gamma,y\Gamma)=\inf_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma}d(x\gamma,y\gamma^{\prime}).



Furthermore, for any function F\colon G/\Gamma\to\mathbb{C}, we define





\lVert F\rVert_{\mathrm{Lip}}:=\lVert F\rVert_{\infty}+\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in G/\Gamma\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\frac{|F(x)-F(y)|}{d(x,y)}.







We recall the notion of a horizontal character and the notion of a function F having a vertical frequency; our definitions are exactly as in [14, Definitions 1.5, 3.3, 3.4, 3.5].




Definition A.4.



Given a filtered nilmanifold G/\Gamma, the horizontal torus is defined to be





(G/\Gamma)_{\mathrm{ab}}:=G/[G,G]\Gamma.



A horizontal character is a continuous homomorphism \eta\colon G\to\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z} that annihilates \Gamma; such characters may be equivalently viewed as characters on the horizontal torus. A horizontal character is nontrivial if it is not identically zero.




Furthermore, if the nilmanifold G/\Gamma has degree s, the vertical torus is defined to be





G_{s}/(G_{s}\cap\Gamma).



A vertical character is a continuous homomorphism \xi\colon G_{s}\to\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z} that annihilates \Gamma\cap G_{s}. Setting m_{s}=\dim G_{s}, one may use the last m_{s} coordinates of the Mal’cev coordinate map to identify G_{s} and G_{s}/(G_{s}\cap\Gamma) with \mathbb{R}^{m_{s}} and \mathbb{R}^{m_{s}}/\mathbb{Z}^{m_{s}}, respectively. Thus, we may identify any vertical character \xi with a unique k\in\mathbb{Z}^{m_{s}} such that \xi(x)=k\cdot x under this identification G_{s}/(\Gamma\cap G_{s})\cong\mathbb{R}^{m_{s}}/\mathbb{Z}^{m_{s}}. We refer to k as the frequency of the character \xi, we write |\xi|:=\lVert k\rVert_{\infty} to denote the magnitude of the frequency \xi, and say that a function F\colon G/\Gamma\to\mathbb{C} has a vertical frequency \xi if





F(g_{s}\cdot x)=e(\xi(g_{s}))F(x)



for all g_{s}\in G_{s} and x\in G/\Gamma. We may also refer to |\xi| as the height of \xi.





We will also need the following notion of smoothness of polynomials which take values in the torus.




Definition A.5.



Let p:\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z} be a polynomial of the form





p(\vec{n})=\sum_{\vec{j}}\alpha_{\vec{j}}\binom{\vec{n}}{\vec{j}},



where \binom{\vec{n}}{\vec{j}}=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\binom{n_{i}}{j_{i}}.
Then, for N_{1},\ldots,N_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}, define:





\lVert p\rVert_{C^{\infty}[\vec{N}]}:=\sup_{\vec{j}\neq\vec{0}}\lVert\alpha_{\vec{j}}\rVert_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\vec{N}^{\vec{j}},



where \vec{N}^{\vec{j}}=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}N_{i}^{j_{i}}.





We finally record the following technical lemma about coefficients of polynomial sequences when lifted to the Lie algebra.




Lemma A.6.



Let G have a degree k filtration G_{\bullet} and g(n)=g_{1}^{n}\cdots g_{k}^{n^{k}} be a polynomial sequence adapted to G_{\bullet}. Let \mathfrak{g} be the Lie algebra of G and \mathfrak{g}_{\bullet} be the corresponding filtration. Then \log(g(n)):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\widetilde{g}_{\ell}n^{\ell} is a polynomial sequence adapted to \mathfrak{g}_{\bullet} and





\exp(a_{\ell})=g_{\ell}\mod\langle G_{\ell+1},[G_{j},G_{\ell-j}]\rangle,



for all \ell.





Proof.



That \log(g(n)) is adapted to \mathfrak{g}_{\bullet} is an easy induction using the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula. For each \ell, let \mathfrak{h}_{\ell} be the smallest Lie subalgebra of \mathfrak{g} containing \mathfrak{g}_{\ell+1} and [\mathfrak{g}_{j},\mathfrak{g}_{\ell-j}]. The following consequence of the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula was observed in [1, Lemma 5.8]: for univariate polynomial sequences q,q^{\prime} in \mathfrak{g} adapted to \mathfrak{g}_{\bullet}, we have





(\log(\exp(q)\exp(q^{\prime})))_{\ell}=(q)_{\ell}+(q^{\prime})_{\ell}\mod\mathfrak{h}_{\ell},



where (q)_{\ell} denotes the coefficient of the degree \ell term of q. Iterating this we see that see that for each \ell





\widetilde{g}_{\ell}=(\log(\prod_{i=1}^{k}g_{i}^{n^{i}}))_{\ell}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\log(g_{i}^{n^{i}}))_{\ell}\mod\mathfrak{h}_{\ell}=\log(g_{\ell})\mod\mathfrak{h}_{\ell}.



Exponentiating yields the result.
∎






Appendix B Gowers and Gowers-Peluse norm control




We now recall a series of estimates regarding the Gowers and Gowers–Peluse norms.




Lemma B.1.



Fix s\geq 2, C\geq 1, \delta\in(0,1/2) and let N\geq 1 be an integer. Let f:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C} be a 1-bounded function supported on [\pm N] and let \mu be the uniform measure on [\pm N].


(i) 



If \lVert f\rVert_{U^{s}[N]}\geq\delta, then \lVert f\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}^{s}[N;\mu]}\geq\delta^{O_{s}(1)}.


(ii) 



If \lVert f\rVert_{U^{s}_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu]}\geq\delta, then \lVert f\rVert_{U^{s}[N]}\geq\delta^{O_{s}(1)}.


(iii) 



Let \mu_{i},\mu_{i}^{\prime} be probability measures on \mathbb{Z} with \mu_{i}(\cdot)\leq C\cdot\mu_{i}^{\prime}(\cdot). Then





\lVert f\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\mu_{s}^{\prime}]}\geq C^{-s/2^{s-1}}\lVert f\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{s}]}.




(iv) 



Let \mu^{\prime} be either the uniform measure on [\pm N/C] or q\cdot[\pm N/q] with 1\leq q\leq C. If \lVert f\rVert_{U^{s}_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu]}\geq\delta, then \lVert f\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}^{s}[N;\mu^{\prime}]}\geq\delta^{O_{s}(1)}.







Proof.



We have that (ii) is established as [17, Lemma A.3] and (iii) and (iv) are established in [18, Lemma 3.5], we only prove (i). Let \tilde{\mu} denote the uniform measure on [\pm 3N]. We proceed inductively, proving by downwards induction for j=s,s-1,\dots,0 that




(B.1)

\sum_{x,h_{1},\ldots,h_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h_{i}^{\prime}\sim\tilde{\mu}\\
j+1\leq i\leq s\end{subarray}}\Delta_{h_{1}}\cdots\Delta_{h_{j}}\Delta_{(h_{j+1},h_{j+1}^{\prime})}\cdots\Delta_{(h_{s},h_{s}^{\prime})}f(x)\geq c^{j-s}\delta^{2^{s}}N^{j+1}



for a constant c=c_{s}>0. Note that the assumption in (i) corresponds to the case j=s, and that the case j=0 gives \lVert f\rVert_{U^{s}_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\tilde{\mu}]}\geq c^{-s/2^{s}}\delta, which immediately implies (i) by using (iii).




Assume we know B.1 for some value of j; we shall deduce it for j-1. Replacing the dummy variable h_{j} by t and permuting the derivatives (which commute), the statement B.1 may be written as





\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{x,h_{1},\ldots,h_{j-1}\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h_{i}^{\prime}\sim\tilde{\mu}\\
j+1\leq i\leq s\end{subarray}}\Delta_{h_{1}}\cdots\Delta_{h_{j-1}}\Delta_{(h_{j+1},h_{j+1}^{\prime})}\cdots\Delta_{(h_{s},h_{s}^{\prime})}(\Delta_{t}f(x))\geq c^{j-s}\delta^{2^{s}}N^{j+1}.






As s\geq 2 one may observe that for fixed t, the internal sum is always non-negative. Furthermore the sum is only nonzero when t\in[\pm 2N]. As the sum in x ranges over all integers, for any \ell\in\mathbb{Z} we may substitute x+\ell for x, and thereby replace \Delta_{t}f by \Delta_{(\ell,\ell+t)}f, leaving the sum unchanged. Averaging over such \ell weighted by \mu gives





\sum_{t\in[\pm 2N]}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\mu(\ell)\sum_{x,h_{1},\ldots,h_{j-1}\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h_{i}^{\prime}\sim\tilde{\mu}\\
j+1\leq i\leq s\end{subarray}}\Delta_{h_{1}}\cdots\Delta_{h_{j-1}}\Delta_{(h_{j+1},h_{j+1}^{\prime})}\cdots\Delta_{(h_{s},h_{s}^{\prime})}(\Delta_{(\ell,\ell+t)}f(x))\geq c^{j-s}\delta^{2^{s}}N^{j+1},



which on substituting h_{j} for \ell and h^{\prime}_{j} for \ell+t gives





\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{j},h^{\prime}_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
h^{\prime}_{j}-h_{j}\in[\pm 2N]\end{subarray}}\mu(h_{j})\sum_{x,h_{1},\ldots,h_{j-1}\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h_{i}^{\prime}\sim\tilde{\mu}\\
j+1\leq i\leq s\end{subarray}}\Delta_{h_{1}}\cdots\Delta_{h_{j-1}}\Delta_{(h_{j+1},h_{j+1}^{\prime})}\cdots\Delta_{(h_{s},h_{s}^{\prime})}(\Delta_{(h_{j},h^{\prime}_{j})}f(x))\geq c^{j-s}\delta^{2^{s}}N^{j+1}.



The sum on the left is over a subset of all pairs (h_{j},h^{\prime}_{j}) with h_{j},h^{\prime}_{j}\in[\pm 3N], with the weight attached to each pair being (2N+1)^{-1}(4N+1)^{-1}\leq 5(6N+1)^{-2}. It follows (using the nonnegativity of the inner sum over x,h_{1},\dots,h_{j-1}) that





\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{h_{j},h_{j}^{\prime}\sim\tilde{\mu}}
\displaystyle\sum_{x,h_{1},\ldots,h_{j-1}\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h_{i}^{\prime}\sim\tilde{\mu}\\
j+1\leq i\leq s\end{subarray}}\Delta_{h_{1}}\cdots\Delta_{h_{j-1}}\Delta_{(h_{j+1},h_{j+1}^{\prime})}\cdots\Delta_{(h_{s},h_{s}^{\prime})}(\Delta_{(h_{j},h_{j}^{\prime})}f(x))\geq 5^{-1}c^{j-s}\delta^{2^{s}}N^{j}\geq c^{j-s-1}\delta^{2^{s}}N^{j}.



This is B.1 in the case j-1 and thus completes the proof of the inductive step, and hence of item (i) in the lemma.
∎





We next require the following standard Fourier approximation lemma.




Lemma B.2.



Let \delta\in(0,1/2) and N\geq\delta^{-1}. There exists G_{\delta}:\mathbb{Z}\to[0,1] such that





\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}|\widehat{G_{\delta}}(\Theta)|
\displaystyle\ll\delta\log(1/\delta)




\displaystyle G_{\delta}(x)
\displaystyle=1\text{ for }x\in[\delta N,(1-\delta)N]




\displaystyle G_{\delta}(x)
\displaystyle=0\text{ for }x\notin[-\delta N,(1+\delta)N].







Proof.



Let





G_{\delta}(x)=\frac{\mathbbm{1}[\cdot\in[N]]\ast\mathbbm{1}[\cdot\in[\delta\cdot N]]}{2\lfloor\delta N\rfloor+1}.



As





\widehat{\mathbbm{1}[\cdot\in[X]]}(\Theta)\ll\min(X,\lVert\Theta\rVert_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}^{-1})



the result follows via direct integration.
∎





We next recall a special case of [36, Theorem 6.1] which will serve as the primary PET control statement over the integers for our work.




Theorem B.3.



Let N\geq 1, C\geq 1, 1\leq d^{\prime}\leq d and a_{1},\ldots,a_{t}\in\mathbb{Z} be distinct integers. Let \ell be a nonzero integer.




Let f_{i}:[\pm N]\to\mathbb{C}, P(y)=\sum_{j=d^{\prime}}^{d}b_{i}y^{j}\in\mathbb{Z}[y] with b_{d^{\prime}}\neq 0 and define P_{W}(y)=W^{-d^{\prime}}P(Wy). Suppose that \max_{i}|b_{i}|\leq C, \max_{i}|a_{i}|\leq C, that M(|\ell|\cdot W^{-d+d^{\prime}}/N)^{1/d}\in[1/C,C] and that





\sum_{x\in[N]}\sum_{y\in[M]}f_{1}(x+a_{1}\cdot\ell P_{W}(y))\cdots f_{t}(x+a_{t}\cdot\ell P_{W}(y))\geq\delta NM.






Let \widetilde{\mu} denote the uniform measure on \ell W^{d-d^{\prime}}\cdot[\pm N/(\ell\cdot W^{d-d^{\prime}})]. Then there exists s=s(d,t)\geq 2 such that





\min_{i\in[t]}\lVert f_{i}\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}^{s}[N;\widetilde{\mu}]}}\gg_{C}\delta^{O_{d,t}(1)}.







Proof.



There are several slight technical differences between Theorem B.3 and the statement appearing in [36, Theorem 6.1]; we perform the necessary maneuvers to reduce to the statement there.




If a_{i}\neq 0 for all i, we encode the x\in[N] via introducing a function f_{t+1}(x)=\mathbbm{1}[x\in[N]] and adding a_{t+1}=0. If there exits a_{i}=0, we replace f_{i} by \widetilde{f_{i}}(x)=f_{i}(x)\cdot\mathbbm{1}[x\in[N]]. Observe that if \lVert\widetilde{f_{i}}\rVert_{U^{s}_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\widetilde{\mu}]}\geq\delta^{\prime} then taking \delta^{\prime\prime}=\delta^{\prime O_{s}(1)} we have that \lVert f_{i}\cdot G_{\delta^{\prime\prime}}\rVert_{U^{s}_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\widetilde{\mu}]}\geq\delta^{\prime}/2 using Lemma B.2. Via applying Fourier inversion G_{\delta}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\widehat{G_{\delta}}(\Theta)e(\Theta x)~d\Theta and using that the Gowers–Peluse norm satisfies triangle inequality, we have \sup_{\Theta}\lVert f_{i}\cdot e(\Theta\cdot)\rVert_{U^{s}_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\widetilde{\mu}]}\geq(\delta^{\prime})^{O_{s}(1)}. As s\geq 2, a direct computation gives \lVert f_{i}\cdot e(\Theta\cdot)\rVert_{U^{s}_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\widetilde{\mu}]}=\lVert f_{i}\rVert_{U^{s}_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\widetilde{\mu}]}. Therefore it suffices to operate under the alternate assumption that





\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{y\in[M]}f_{1}(x+a_{1}\cdot\ell P_{W}(y))\cdots f_{t}(x+a_{t}\cdot\ell P_{W}(y))\geq\delta NM.






Observe that in this situation, by changing variables x\to x+2\ell\cdot\max_{i}|a_{i}|P_{W}(y) we may additionally assume that a_{i}>0. Furthermore by shifting f_{i} we may instead assume that \operatorname{supp}(f_{i})\subseteq[C^{2}N,2C^{2}N]. Given these shifted constraints, we may additionally add f_{0}(x)=\mathbbm{1}[x\in[4C^{2}N]].




We then apply [36, Theorem 6.1] with N^{\prime}=4C^{2}N and M as defined and obtain that





\min_{i\in[t]}\lVert f_{i}\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{s}]}\gg_{C}\delta^{O_{d,t}(1)}.



Here \mu_{i} are uniform measures on sets of the form \ell W^{d-d^{\prime}}\cdot b_{d}\cdot(a_{i}-a_{j})d!\cdot[M^{d}] or \ell W^{d-d^{\prime}}\cdot b_{d}\cdot a_{i}d!\cdot[M^{d}]. Applying Lemma B.1(iii-iv) then immediately gives the desired result.
∎





We now state the version of PET control which is required for Section 3; the statement we require appears as [35, Proposition 2.2].




Theorem B.4.



Let P_{1}(y),\ldots,P_{m}(y)\in\mathbb{Z}[y] be pairwise distinct polynomials and f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}:\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C} be 1-bounded. Then there exists s=s(P_{1},\ldots,P_{m})\geq 1, \beta=\beta(P_{1},\ldots,P_{m})\in(0,1] such that





\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big[\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}(x+P_{i}(y))\Big]\Big|\leq\min_{i}\lVert f_{i}\rVert_{U^{s}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})}^{\beta}+O_{\mathcal{P}}(N^{-\beta}).
