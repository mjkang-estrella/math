                                                                                          Abstract
                                                    Let Φ be a unital completely positive (UCP) map on the space of operators on some
                                                Hilbert space. We assume that Φ is η-idempotent, namely, ∥Φ2 − Φ∥cb ⩽ η, and construct an
                                                associated ε-C ∗ algebra (of almost-invariant observables) for ε = O(η). This type of structure
                                                has the axioms of a unital C ∗ algebra but the associativity and other axioms involving the
                                                multiplication and the unit hold up to ε. We prove that any finite-dimensional ε-C ∗ algebra
                                                A is O(ε)-isomorphic to some genuine C ∗ algebra B. These bounds are universal, i.e. do not
                                                depend on the dimensionality or other parameters. When A comes from a finite-dimensional
                                                η-idempotent UCP map Φ, the O(η)-isomorphism and its inverse can be realized by UCP
                                                maps. This gives an approximate factorization of the quantum channel Φ∗ into a decoding
                                                channel, producing a state on B, and an encoding channel.

Some common notation. The Hermitian inner product ⟨ · , · ⟩ is conjugate linear in the first
argument and linear in the second argument. The Hermitian conjugation and similar operations
are denoted by a dagger. The unit element of an algebra A is denoted by IA , whereas 1L stands for
the identity map on a vector space L. The space of bounded linear maps L → M between Banach
spaces is denoted by B(L, M). In particular, B(L) is the Banach (or C ∗ ) algebra of operators
acting on a Banach (or Hilbert) space L ̸= 0. (We consider only unital algebras with I ̸= 0.) For
example, Mm,k = B(Ck , Cm ) and Mn = B(Cn ) (for n > 0) are the matrix spaces and algebras.
This notation should not be confused with Br (x) and B̄r (x), which refer to the open and closed
balls of radius r with center at x in some metric space.


1     Motivation: How to recognize an encoded subsystem?
Under favorable circumstances, some physical systems encode states and observables of smaller,
“logical” systems. This may be arranged by design or happen naturally, for example, in so-called
topological phases of matter that feature protected degrees of freedom. In practice, the encoding
is always approximate and may also be implicit (especially if the system is natural rather than
engineered). The informal goal of this paper is to reconstruct the logical algebra of observables in
such a situation. This kind of task is common in quantum many-body physics, where identifying
“effective degrees of freedom” is necessary for the understanding of complex systems. Recent
work [1] provides a rigorous reconstruction of topological degrees of freedom in spin systems on
a 2D lattice under specific conditions, which involve only exact equalities. We will formulate and
solve the approximate reconstruction problem in an abstract form, in hope that the results could
be applied broadly. However, let us begin with the ideal case, where everything is explicit and
exact.

1.2    Idempotent and almost idempotent UCP maps
We will work with the duals of quantum channels, i.e. UCP maps Φ : B(H) → B(H). Such Φ
is idempotent if and only if T = Φ∗ is idempotent. The following structure theorem will be
proved in Section 11.3. It is related to the characterization of operators that are invariant under
an arbitrary finite-dimensional UCP map [2, Theorems 6.12 and Lemma 6.4] and of quantum
channels stabilizing a given set of density matrices [3], [4, Theorem 10].

Let ε be a sufficiently small nonnegative number. Individual definitions and statements will require
(in many cases, implicitly) that ε be bounded by certain constants. For example, condition (2.5)
below makes sense if ε < 1.
Definition 2.1. An ε-Banach algebra is a Banach space A endowed with a bilinear multiplication
map A × A → A such that

                                   ∥XY ∥ ⩽ (1 + ε)∥X∥∥Y ∥           (X, Y ∈ A),                       (2.1)
                    ∥(XY )Z − X(Y Z)∥ ⩽ ε∥X∥∥Y ∥∥Z∥                 (X, Y, Z ∈ A).                    (2.2)

                            ∥XY − Y X∥ ⩽ ε′ ∥X∥∥Y ∥             (X, Y ∈ A).                           (2.3)

Theorem 2.3. Any finite-dimensional ε-C ∗ algebra A is O(ε)-isomorphic to some C ∗ algebra B.
(Importantly, the implicit constant in O(ε) does not depend on A or its dimensionality.)

     To actually prove the theorem, we take a different approach, but a cohomological argument is
still used. We construct an O(ε)-isomorphism between some C ∗ algebra B and the ε-C ∗ algebra
A incrementally. Before the process is complete, the map v : B → A is an O(ε)-inclusion, and we
enlarge B and modify v in little steps. This involves solving two problems. To even make the first
step, from B0 = C to B1 = C ⊕ C, we need to find a nontrivial projection in A. An approximate
projection, satisfying the conditions P † = P and ∥P 2 − P ∥ ⩽ δ for a suitable constant δ, is
sufficient as long as P is not too close to ±I. The existence of such a projection (assuming that
dim A > 1) is the first important lemma. We prove it by first constructing (in Section 5) an
approximate unitary group U ⊆ A as a C 1 manifold using standard analytic methods such as
the implicit function theorem. The group laws are satisfied with O(ε) accuracy, and therefore,

                                                    8
they also hold up to homotopy. Now, P is a projection if and only if U = 2P − I is Hermitian
and U 2 = I. In other words, U is a fixed point of the inversion map σ : U 7→ U −1 acting on U.
We show that σ has sufficiently many (i.e. more than two) fixed points using the Lefschetz-Hopf
theorem and certain properties of H-spaces (see Section 6).
      Once the lemma is proved, we can keep building the finite-dimensional C ∗ algebra B in a
similar fashion. At each step, B is described by a partitioned index set and the basis {Ejk } with
j and k in the same part. The multiplication rules are Ejk Elm = δkl Ejm . At the beginning of
the incremental process, the parts are singletons so that all basis elements are diagonal and B is
commutative. Each step involves replacing some j and the corresponding Ejj with {j ′ , j ′′ } and
Ej ′ j ′ + Ej ′′ j ′′ , respectively. When that is no longer possible, we add off-diagonal elements, Ejk
with j ̸= k, until v : B → A becomes bijective. The detailed argument is structured in terms of
merging (Corollary 9.2) and extension (Lemma 9.4) procedures.
      The second problem is to control errors. We must make sure that v is a δ0 -inclusion for some
fixed δ0 = O(ε). Simply extending the current v to a new, larger B gives a δ-inclusion for δ
greater than δ0 . Thus, we need the following error reduction result (Corollary 8.3): if there exists
a δ-inclusion of a finite-dimensional C ∗ algebra B into an ε-C ∗ algebra A for δ less than a certain
constant, then there is also an δ0 -inclusion, where δ0 = O(ε) does not depend on δ. This can be
shown by cohomological methods because B does have a diagonal. Similar results are found in
the literature; they are concerned with the existence of a C ∗ subalgebra near an approximate C ∗
subalgebra [9] and a homomorphism of C ∗ algebras near an approximately multiplicative map [10].
Our argument is similar to the proof of Theorem 3.1 in [10] but actually simpler due to the finite
dimensionality condition.

A projection in an ε-C ∗ algebra A is a Hermitian element P such that P 2 = P . Such elements
