Boundaries of open symplectic manifolds and the failure of packing stability
Dan Cristofaro-Gardiner and Richard Hind
Date: August 24, 2026
Abstract.
A finite volume symplectic manifold is said to have “packing stability” if the only obstruction to symplectically embedding sufficiently small balls is the volume obstruction. Packing stability has been shown in a variety of cases and it has been conjectured that it always holds. We give counterexamples to this conjecture; in fact, we give examples that cannot be fully packed by any domain with smooth boundary nor by any convex domain.
The examples are symplectomorphic to open and bounded domains in ℝ4\mathbb{R}^{4}, with the diffeomorphism type of a disc.
The obstruction to packing stability is closely tied to another old question, which asks to what extent an open symplectic manifold has a well-defined boundary; it follows from our results that many examples cannot be symplectomorphic to the interior of a compact symplectic manifold with smooth boundary. Our results can be quantified in terms of the volume decay near the boundary, and we produce, for example, smooth toric domains that are only symplectomorphic to the interior of a compact domain if the boundary of this domain has inner Minkowski dimension arbitrarily close to 44.
The growth rate of the subleading asymptotics of the ECH spectrum plays a key role in our arguments. We prove a very general “fractal Weyl law”, relating this growth rate to the Minkowski dimension; this formula is potentially of independent interest.
1. Introduction
1.1. Context
The existence of symplectic ball packings, that is, symplectic embeddings of disjoint balls, has been understood as a basic measure of symplectic flexibility at least since the work of Gromov, see the discussion in [2, Sec. 1.1].
This flexibility can be quantified by the packing numbers pk​(M,ω)p_{k}(M,\omega) for k≥1k\geq 1. When (M,ω)(M,\omega) has finite volume we define
pk​(M,ω):=supck​vol​(B⁡(c)¯)vol⁡(M,ω)p_{k}(M,\omega):=\sup_{c}\frac{k\mathrm{vol}(\overline{B(c)})}{\mathrm{vol}(M,\omega)}
where the supremum is over all cc such that there exists a symplectic embedding ⊔B⁡(c)¯↪(M,ω)\sqcup\overline{B(c)}\hookrightarrow(M,\omega) of kk disjoint closed balls.
As explained in [37, Ch. 6], a theorem of McDuff and Polterovich [28, Rem. 1.5.G] shows that
(1.1)
limk→∞pk​(M,ω)=1\lim_{k\to\infty}p_{k}(M,\omega)=1
for all finite volume symplectic manifolds.
The volume filling limit is already a strong contrast with the Riemannian case. For example, for isometric ball embeddings into domains in ℝ2\mathbb{R}^{2}, it has been known for ages that the limit of the analogous packing numbers is π/12\pi/\sqrt{12}, see [39] for a rigorous proof; for domains in ℝ3\mathbb{R}^{3}, the corresponding limit
is π/18\pi/\sqrt{18} by celebrated work of Hales [19]; and, in any dimension, a general upper bound due to Rogers [33] shows that the analogue of (1.1) never holds.
We say that a connected symplectic manifold has packing stability
if there exists a (stability number) K0K_{0} such that pk​(M,ω)=1p_{k}(M,\omega)=1 for all k≥K0k\geq K_{0}.
This represents perhaps a surprising level of flexibility, but packing stability has now been established for an ever increasing subset of symplectic manifolds. We say that a symplectic manifold (M,ω)(M,\omega) is rational if [ω]∈H2​(M,ℚ)⊂H2​(M,ℝ)[\omega]\in H^{2}(M,\mathbb{Q})\subset H^{2}(M,\mathbb{R}). Then the manifolds shown to have packing stability include closed rational 4-manifolds [2], closed rational manifolds of any dimension [4, 5], all closed 4 manifolds [6], open ellipsoids [5], open 4 dimensional polydisks and pseudoballs [6], and rational convex toric domains [10].
The question of determining which finite volume symplectic manifolds have packing stability was first raised in Biran’s seminal work [2]; it is featured as Problem 4 in [9].
Based on the results above, and the asymptotic estimate (1.1), it was plausible to conjecture that
the answer is simply that all do.
Indeed, this is stated as Conjecture 13.2 in the survey article [36].
1.2. Main results
We now explain our main theorems. In some cases, we can prove more general statements than these, see Remark 1.6, but the theorems we present in this section get at the heart of our results while prioritizing brevity; we discuss how they fit together in Remark 1.6.

The XfX_{f} can be assumed to have smooth boundary, but since they are not compact this hides the behavior at infinity; we show below that they are symplectomorphic to interiors of compact domains, and it is more natural to study the boundary from this point of view. In smooth topology, it is a fundamental problem to determine whether a given open manifold can be smoothly embedded as the interior of a smooth compact manifold with boundary: this is called the problem of “finding a boundary”, see for example [3]. It is natural to ask the analogous question in the symplectic setting; prior to the present work, to our knowledge no symplectic obstructions to this problem have been known. Remark 1.5.
Theorem 1.1 and Theorem 1.4 have an interesting connection with the phenomenon known as “long-term superrecurrence”: this refers to situations where the images of any Darboux ball under iterates of a Hamiltonian diffeomorphism intersect more often than one would expect solely from volume considerations; for brevity, we refer the reader to the survey [32] for a fuller explanation.
To our knowledge, it had been unknown prior to the current work whether there are any natural symplectic manifolds XX for which long-term superrecurrence occurs. It follows immediately from Theorem 1.1 that this pheonmenon occurs whenever XX is an unbounded concave toric domain XfX_{f}. Theorem 1.4 allows for a generalization of this, proving
long-term superrecurence for a large class of open subsets of XfX_{f}.
Remark 1.6.
In combination Theorem 1.4 and Theorem 1.2 suggest the following perspective: the XfX_{f} have provably complicated boundary, and this obstructs any full filling by a domain with qualitatively simpler boundary. A more general statement, which implies both of the aforementioned theorems, appears in Theorem 5.2. We further discuss this perspective in section 5.
Remark 1.7.
As observed by S. Nemirovski [30], the domains XpX_{p} give examples of the following phenomenon: they are logarithmically convex and hence holomorphically convex, but are not biholomorphic to a bounded domain (as they contain copies of the complex plane ℂ\mathbb{C}); on the other hand, in the symplectic category, it follows from our results that they are symplectomorphic to bounded domains but not to convex domains.
1.3. The nature of the obstructions and the fractal Weyl law
The main idea in proving the above theorems is to study the ‘‘subleading asymptotics” of ECH capacities11
1
One could alternatively use the subleading asymptotics of the “elementary ECH capacities” defined in [23], and our proofs would hold verbatim for these as well, since they only rely on formal properties that are shared by both sets of capacities. ckc_{k}.
These are symplectic capacities, defined for any subset of a symplectic 44-manifold; we summarize what we need to know about ECH capacities in section 2.2. The leading asymptotics of these capacities were studied in [14], with the conclusion (under some hypotheses that are not relevant to our discussion here, but which hold, for example, for any bounded open subset of ℝ4\mathbb{R}^{4}) that
ck​(X)≈4​v​o​l​(X)​k.c_{k}(X)\approx\sqrt{4vol(X)}\sqrt{k}.
In other words, the ckc_{k} grow like k1/2k^{1/2}, and the coefficient at highest asymptotics measures the volume of the manifold.
Much less is currently understood about the subleading asymptotics
ek​(X):=ck​(X)−2​v​o​l​(X)​k.e_{k}(X):=c_{k}(X)-2\sqrt{vol(X)k}.
The eke_{k} are the invariants that we use to obstruct packing stability, and another consequence of our work is a better understanding of the eke_{k} themselves. More precisely,
we can extract well-defined growth rates taking a wide range of values for bounded open connected subsets of ℝ4\mathbb{R}^{4}, contrasting the case of the leading asymptotics.

In view of the above proposition, it is natural to ask
to what degree the growth rate of the eke_{k} sees information about a general finite volume domain UU. In fact, we can prove the following.
Define the ECH dimension
dE​C​H:=2+4​lim supk→∞(ln⁡(−ek′​(Z))ln⁡(k))d_{ECH}:=2+4\limsup_{k\to\infty}\left(\frac{\ln(-e^{\prime}_{k}(Z))}{\ln(k)}\right)

We can think of this as a “fractal Weyl law”, bounding the Minkowski dimension from below by a symplectic invariant, in analogy with formally similar fractal Weyl laws which abound in other areas of geometry, see e.g. [31, 17, 1, 24, 29] and the references therein.
1.4. Outline of the paper and the main proofs
We now explain the structure of the paper.
Section 2 reviews what we need to know about the
volume growth near the boundary of Riemannian manifolds, the Minkowski dimension, and ECH capacities, and collects some key preliminary results.
The next two sections are about ECH capacities. Theorems 1.1 and 1.4 can be read as obstructions to symplectic embeddings between domains of equal volume, where, by the definition of packing numbers, we allow small rescalings of the domain. Since the ECH capacities of domains of equal volume have the same leading asymptotics, the subleading asymptotics can give obstructions. Section 3 establishes general lower bounds on the subleading asymptotics
for any symplectic manifold, via the aforementioned fractal Weyl law,
extending and quantifying an argument of Hutchings [22]. Section 4 establishes upper bounds for unbounded concave toric domains.
Based on this, we establish our main proofs in section 5. Concave toric domains are shown to be symplectomorphic to bounded subsets of ℂ2\mathbb{C}^{2} in section 6. The final section discusses packing stability in view of our new results.

2. Preliminaries
2.1. Volume decay and the Minkowski dimension
We begin by collecting some facts about the Minkowski dimension.
Our first order of business in this section involves clarifying some definitions. Let U⊂ℝ4U\subset\mathbb{R}^{4} be open, and assume that ∂U\partial U is compact. Recall the Minkowski dimension of ∂U\partial U, which can be defined as follows [38]. 2.2. ECH capacities and concave toric domains
The proof of Theorem 1.1 uses the subleading asymptotics of the ECH capacities ckc_{k}, applied to a particular limit of concave toric domains. In this section, we briefly recall what we need to know about ECH capacities and concave toric domains.
The ECH capacities are a sequence of nonnegative real numbers ck​(M,ω)c_{k}(M,\omega) associated to four-dimensional symplectic manifolds; they were defined in [21]. One can think of ECH capacities as measurements of symplectic size. A key axiom that they satisfy is the Monotonicity Axiom, which states that each ckc_{k} is monotone under symplectic embeddings. We also need to recall the Ball Axiom, which states that ck​(B​(a))c_{k}(B(a)) is the (k+1)s​t(k+1)^{st} smallest entry in the matrix (m​a+n​a)(m,n)∈ℤ≥0×ℤ≥0(ma+na)_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}, and the Disjoint Union axiom, which states that ck​(X⊔Y)=s​u​pi+j=k​(ci​(X)+cj​(Y))c_{k}(X\sqcup Y)=sup_{i+j=k}(c_{i}(X)+c_{j}(Y)) for compact four-dimensional Liouville domains XX and YY. The definition of ECH capacities arises from the “embedded contact homology” of closed three-manifolds with contact forms, and ECH capacities are most naturally defined for compact four-dimensional Liouville domains. However, in this paper we will need to consider slightly more wild objects. What we require for our purposes is that, as observed by Hutchings, the definition of ECH capacities extends to any open subset MM of a four-dimensional symplectic manifold by defining
ck​(M):=supZck​(Z),c_{k}(M):=\sup_{Z}\hskip 2.84526ptc_{k}(Z),
where ZZ is a compact four-dimensional Liouville domain in MM, with many of the same properties holding. For more, we refer the reader to [21].
Another important axiom about ECH capacities is the Volume Axiom, which states that for compact Liouville domains the ckc_{k} recover the Volume in their limit; see [14] for the precise formula. Our obstruction to packing stability will come from the subleading asymptotics, calculated for the domains in Theorem 1.1. These domains are limits of concave toric domains and we now recall what we need to know about such domains from [8]. A concave toric domain is a toric domain XΩX_{\Omega}, where Ω\Omega is the subset of the first quadrant bounded by the axes and a convex function f:[0,a]→[0,b]f:[0,a]\to[0,b] with f⁡(a)=0f(a)=0. (Recall in section 1.2 we defined an unbounded concave toric domain to be defined by a convex function [0,∞)→(0,b][0,\infty)\to(0,b].) For example, a ball is a concave toric domain. Any concave toric domain XΩX_{\Omega} has a canonical (possibly infinite) packing by balls B⁡(ai)B(a_{i}), via the procedure explained in [8, Sec. 1.3]; we call the aia_{i} the weights associated to Ω\Omega. To help the reader visualize the decomposition, we note that this packing comes from decomposing the region Ω\Omega into a union of triangles, each of which are equivalent under the action of the affine group to an isoceles right triangle; for more, see [8, Fig. 1].
An important fact about concave toric domains [8] is that the weights determine the ECH capacities, via the formula:

ck(XΩ)=ck(⊔iB(ai)).c_{k}(X_{\Omega})=c_{k}(\sqcup_{i}B(a_{i})).
This formula is proved for concave toric domains such that the weight expansion is finite in [8, Thm. 1.4], and then [8, Rem. 1.6] explains how to extend this to arbitrary concave toric domains; only the finite case is needed as an input for this paper.
We will also need the following bound which immediately follows via induction from the definition of the weight sequence; it also follows as a special case of the more involved [22, Lem. 3.6].

Then XΩ⊂{π|z1|2≤M}⊂ℂ2.X_{\Omega}\subset\{\pi|z_{1}|^{2}\leq M\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}.
3. The subleading asymptotics of ECH capacities and the Minkowski dimension
We now begin the work towards the proofs of the main results.
Our main theorems all come from considerations of the subleading asymptotics of the ECH capacities.
The aim of this section is to prove the following general theorem, which is equivalent to the fractal Weyl law Theorem 1.9, but stated in a form that is more useful for our purposes.
Theorem 3.1.
Let ZZ be a relatively compact and open
subset of a symplectic manifold (M,ω)(M,\omega). Suppose that the volume decay near ∂Z\partial Z is of order qq. Then there exists a positive constant CC, depending only on ZZ, such that

Remark 5.1.

A series of works, see e.g. [27, 7, 10, 11, 13, 15, 9, 34, 35] and the references therein, study the “ellipsoid embedding function” cX​(a)c_{X}(a) of a symplectic manifold; in the case where (X,ω)(X,\omega) is a 44-manifold, this is defined to be the the supremum, over cc, such that the symplectic ellipsoid c⋅E⁡(1,a)c\cdot E(1,a) can be symplectically embedded into (X,ω)(X,\omega). 7. Discussion
Given our Theorem 1.1, and the context surveyed in the introduction, it is natural to now revisit the question of which finite volume symplectic manifolds have packing stability.
In view of the positive results for closed manifolds surveyed in the introduction, the natural guess would be that all closed manifolds do.
On the other hand, as we have shown here, packing stability can fail for non-compact manifolds even though it sometimes holds in this class. One would like to get a better handle on the interface between these phenomena: a natural question is what properties of a non-compact manifold guarantee that packing stability holds.
In the cases presented here, the boundary behavior certainly appears to be playing a key role.
Whether the singular character of the boundary in our examples is necessary for packing stability to fail seems a natural starting point for further study. Here is a sample question:
Question 7.1.
Must packing stability hold for every compact Liouville domain with smooth boundary?
All known non-compact examples for which packing stability holds have quite a lot more structure than this. In this regard, let us also note that our obstruction here comes from the divergence of the subleading asymptotics of ECH capacities for our domains. However, various conjectures imply that in sufficiently nice cases these should converge; see e.g. [22, Conj. 1.5].
In a different direction, one would like to know if analogues of our results holds in higher dimensions. The perspective conveyed in Remark 1.6 certainly seems like it should hold in arbitrary dimension, but the obstructions we use here come from ECH capacities, which do not currently have a good generalization in higher dimensions.
Thus the following two basic questions remain open:
Question 7.2.
Can packing stability fail in dimension >4>4?
Question 7.3.
Are there “boundaryless” discs in ℝ2​n\mathbb{R}^{2n} when n>2n>2? More precisely, do there exist bounded and open subsets of ℝ2​n\mathbb{R}^{2n}, diffeomorphic to discs, that can not be symplectomorphic to the interior of any compact symplectic manifold?
It would also be interesting if one can improve on the condition p<2p<2 in Theorem 1.4, for example with respect to packings by smooth domains: this would follow immediately from our results if one had better lower bounds on the growth rate of the subleading asymptotics of ECH capacities of smooth compact manifolds, which is an interesting problem of independent interest.
