Dusa McDuff has led three mathematical lives.
In her early twenties she worked on von Neumann algebras, and in [Mc69a, Mc69b]
established the existence of uncountably many different algebraic types of II1{\rm II}_{1}-factors.
After an inspiring six months studying with Gel’fand in Moscow and a
two-year post-doc in Cambridge studying topology,
she wrote a “second thesis” while working with Graeme Segal
on configuration spaces and the group-completion theorem,
and investigated the topology of various diffeomorphism groups,
in particular symplectomorphism groups.
This brought her to symplectic geometry, which was revolutionized around 1985
by Gromov’s introduction of JJ-holomorphic curves [Gr85].
In this short text I will describe some of McDuff’s wonderful contributions to
symplectic geometry.
After reviewing what is meant by ‘symplectic’
I will mostly focus on her work on symplectic embedding problems.
Some of her other results in symplectic geometry are discussed at the end.
More personal texts about Dusa can be found in [Mc11b].
Parts of this text overlap with the ‘Perspective’
in [Mc11b] written jointly with Leonid Polterovich.
1. Symplectic
There are many strands to and from symplectic geometry.
The most important ones are classical mechanics and algebraic geometry.
I do not list these strands
but refer you to [McSa17, Sch18].
Here, I simply give the
Definition 1.
Let MM be a smooth manifold.
A symplectic form on MM is a non-degenerate closed 2-form ω\omega.
A diffeomorphism φ\varphi of MM is symplectic (or a symplectomorphism)
if φ∗​ω=ω\varphi^{*}\omega=\omega.
The non-degeneracy condition implies that symplectic manifolds are even-dimensional.
An example is ℝ2​n\mathbbm{R}^{2n} with the constant differential 22-form
ω0=∑i=1nd​xi∧d​yi.\omega_{0}\,=\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}dx_{i}\wedge dy_{i}.
Other examples are surfaces endowed with an area form, their products,
and Kähler manifolds.
If you begin your first lecture on symplectic geometry like this,
you may very well find yourself alone the following week.
You may thus prefer to start in a more elementary way:
Let γ\gamma be a closed oriented piecewise smooth curve in ℝ2\mathbbm{R}^{2}.
If γ\gamma is embedded, assign to γ\gamma the signed area of the disc DD bounded by γ\gamma,
namely area⁡(D)\operatorname{area}(D) or −area⁡(D)-\operatorname{area}(D),
as in Figure 1.1.
Figure 1.1. The sign of the signed area of an embedded closed curve in ℝ2\mathbbm{R}^{2}
If γ\gamma is not embedded, successively decompose γ\gamma into closed embedded pieces as illustrated in
Figure 1.2,
and define A⁡(γ)A(\gamma) as the sum of the signed areas of these pieces.
Figure 1.2. Splitting a closed curve into embedded pieces
Definition 2.
The standard symplectic structure of ℝ2​n\mathbbm{R}^{2n} is the map
A⁡(γ)=∑i=1nA⁡(γi),γ=(γ1,…,γn)⊂ℝ2​n.A(\gamma)\,=\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}A(\gamma_{i}),\quad\;\gamma=\left(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}\right)\subset\mathbbm{R}^{2n}.
A symplectomorphism φ\varphi of ℝ2​n\mathbbm{R}^{2n} is a diffeomorphism that preserves the signed area of
closed curves:
A⁡(φ⁡(γ))=A⁡(γ) for all closed curves ​γ⊂ℝ2​n.A(\varphi(\gamma))\,=\,A(\gamma)\quad\mbox{ for all closed curves }\gamma\subset\mathbbm{R}^{2n}.
A symplectic structure on a manifold MM is an atlas whose transition functions are (local) symplectomorphisms,
and a symplectomorphism of MM is then a diffeomorphism that preserves this local structure.
The standard symplectic structure of ℝ2​n\mathbbm{R}^{2n} is thus given by assigning to
a closed curve
the sum of the signed areas of
the nn curves obtained by projecting to the coordinate planes ℝ2​(xi,yi)\mathbbm{R}^{2}(x_{i},y_{i}).
And a symplectic structure on a manifold is a coherent way of assigning a
signed area to sufficiently local closed curves.
The equivalence of the two definitions follows from
Darboux’s Theorem.
Around every point of a symplectic manifold (M,ω)(M,\omega) there exists
a coordinate chart φ\varphi such that φ∗​ω0=ω\varphi^{*}\omega_{0}=\omega.
The group of symplectomorphisms of a symplectic manifold is very large.
Indeed, for every compactly supported smooth function
H:M×[0,1]→ℝH\colon M\times[0,1]\to\mathbbm{R} each time-tt map ϕHt\phi_{H}^{t} of its Hamiltonian flow
is a symplectomorphism.
Symplectomorphisms of this form are called Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms.
The Hamiltonian flow is the flow generated by the vector field XHX_{H}
implicitely defined by
ω⁡(XH,⋅)=−d​H​(⋅).\omega(X_{H},\cdot)\,=\,-dH(\cdot).
For (ℝ2​n,ω0)(\mathbbm{R}^{2n},\omega_{0}) one has XH=J0∇HX_{H}=J_{0}\;\!\nabla H, where J0J_{0} is the usual
complex structure on ⊕iℝ2(xi,yi)\oplus_{i}\mathbbm{R}^{2}(x_{i},y_{i}).
2. Symplectic embedding problems
By Darboux’s Theorem, symplectic manifolds have no local invariants
beyond the dimension.
But there are several ways to associate global numerical invariants
to symplectic manifolds.
One of them is by looking at embedding problems.
Take a compact subset KK of (ℝ2​n,ω0)(\mathbbm{R}^{2n},\omega_{0}).
By a symplectic embedding K→MK\to M
we mean the restriction to KK of a smooth embedding φ:U→M\varphi\colon U\to M
of an open neighbourhood of KK that is symplectic, φ∗​ω=ω0\varphi^{*}\omega=\omega_{0}.
In this case we write K​↪𝑠​(M,ω)K\overset{\raisebox{-0.60275pt}[0.0pt][-0.60275pt]{\mbox{$\scriptstyle s$}}\mskip 2.5mu}{\hookrightarrow}(M,\omega).
For every KK, the largest number λ\lambda such that the dilate λ​K\lambda K
symplectically embeds into (M,ω)(M,\omega) is then a symplectic invariant of (M,ω)(M,\omega).
Seven further reasons to study symplectic embedding problems can be found
in [Sch18].
2.1. The Nonsqueezing Theorem
Now take the closed ball B2​n⁡(a)\operatorname{B}^{2n}(a) of radius a/π\sqrt{a/\pi} centred at the origin of ℝ2​n\mathbbm{R}^{2n}.
(The notation reflects that symplectic measurements are 2-dimensional.)
By what we said above, there are very many symplectic embeddings
B2​n⁡(a)​↪𝑠​ℝ2​n\operatorname{B}^{2n}(a)\overset{\raisebox{-0.60275pt}[0.0pt][-0.60275pt]{\mbox{$\scriptstyle s$}}\mskip 2.5mu}{\hookrightarrow}\mathbbm{R}^{2n}.
However, none of them can make the ball thinner,
as Gromov proved in his pioneering paper [Gr85]:
Nonsqueezing Theorem.
B2​n⁡(a)​↪𝑠​B2⁡(A)×ℝ2​n−2\operatorname{B}^{2n}(a)\overset{\raisebox{-0.60275pt}[0.0pt][-0.60275pt]{\mbox{$\scriptstyle s$}}\mskip 2.5mu}{\hookrightarrow}\operatorname{B}^{2}(A)\times\mathbbm{R}^{2n-2} ​ only if ​ a≤Aa\leq A.
The identity embedding thus already provides the largest ball that
symplectically fits into the cylinder B2⁡(A)×ℝ2​n−2\operatorname{B}^{2}(A)\times\mathbbm{R}^{2n-2} of infinite volume.
While there are many forms of symplectic rigidity, this theorem is its most fundamental manifestation.
The theorem shows that some volume preserving mappings cannot be approximated
by symplectic mappings in the C0C^{0}-topology.
It also gave rise to the useful concept of a symplectic capacity, [EkHo89].
In [LaMc95a], Lalonde and McDuff generalized the Nonsqueezing Theorem to all symplectic manifolds:
General Nonsqueezing Theorem.
For any symplectic manifold (M,ω)(M,\omega) of dimension 2​n−22n-2,
B2​n⁡(a)​↪𝑠​(B2⁡(A)×M,ω0⊕ω)\operatorname{B}^{2n}(a)\overset{\raisebox{-0.60275pt}[0.0pt][-0.60275pt]{\mbox{$\scriptstyle s$}}\mskip 2.5mu}{\hookrightarrow}\bigl(\operatorname{B}^{2}(A)\times M,\omega_{0}\oplus\omega\bigr) ​ only if ​ a≤Aa\leq A.
Every good tool in symplectic geometry can be used to prove the Nonsqueezing Theorem.
However, the technique of JJ-holomorphic curves used by Gromov is the most influential
one, and also the most important tool in McDuff’s work.
An almost complex structure JJ on a manifold PP is a smooth collection {Jp}p∈P\{J_{p}\}_{p\in P},
where JpJ_{p} is a linear endomorphism of Tp​PT_{p}P such that Jp2=−idJ_{p}^{2}=-\mbox{id}.
The ‘almost’ indicates that such a structure does not need to be a complex structure,
i.e. does not need to come from a holomorphic atlas.
Not all symplectic manifolds admit complex structures, but they all admit almost complex structures.
A JJ-holomorphic curve in an almost complex manifold (P,J)(P,J) is a map uu
from a Riemann surface (Σ,j)(\Sigma,j) to (P,J)(P,J) such that
d​u∘j=J∘d​u.du\circ j\,=\,J\circ du.
This equation generalizes the Cauchy–Riemann equation defining holomorphic maps ℂ→ℂn\mathbbm{C}\to\mathbbm{C}^{n}.
In this text, the domain of a JJ-holomorphic curve will always be the usual Riemann sphere,
namely the round sphere S2⊂ℝ3S^{2}\subset\mathbbm{R}^{3} whose complex structure jj rotates a vector v∈Tp​S2v\in T_{p}S^{2}
by π2\frac{\pi}{2}.
Even in this case, it is usually impossible to write down a JJ-holomorphic curve
for a given JJ.
But this is not a problem, since one usually just wants to know that such a curve exists.
Now assume that φ:B2​n⁡(a)​↪𝑠​B2⁡(A)×ℝ2​n−2\varphi\colon\operatorname{B}^{2n}(a)\overset{\raisebox{-0.60275pt}[0.0pt][-0.60275pt]{\mbox{$\scriptstyle s$}}\mskip 2.5mu}{\hookrightarrow}\operatorname{B}^{2}(A)\times\mathbbm{R}^{2n-2}.
Choose kk so large that after a translation the image of φ\varphi is contained in
B2⁡(A)×(0,k)2​n−2\operatorname{B}^{2}(A)\times(0,k)^{2n-2}.
Compactifying the disc to the sphere S2​(A′)S^{2}(A^{\prime}) with its usual area form of area A′>AA^{\prime}>A
and taking the quotient to the torus T2​n−2=ℝ2​n−2/k​ℤ2​n−2T^{2n-2}=\mathbbm{R}^{2n-2}/k\mathbbm{Z}^{2n-2},
we then obtain a symplectic embedding
Φ:B2​n⁡(a)​↪𝑠​S2​(A′)×T2​n−2=:(P,ω)\Phi\colon\operatorname{B}^{2n}(a)\overset{\raisebox{-0.60275pt}[0.0pt][-0.60275pt]{\mbox{$\scriptstyle s$}}\mskip 2.5mu}{\hookrightarrow}S^{2}(A^{\prime})\times T^{2n-2}=:(P,\omega)
where ω\omega is the split symplectic structure on the product PP.
We will see that a≤A′a\leq A^{\prime}. Since A′>AA^{\prime}>A was arbitrary, Gromov’s theorem then follows.
Denote by J0J_{0} the usual complex structure on B2​n⁡(a)⊂ℂn\operatorname{B}^{2n}(a)\subset\mathbbm{C}^{n},
and let JJ be an almost complex structure on PP that restricts
to Φ∗​J0\Phi_{*}J_{0} on Φ⁡(B2​n⁡(a))\Phi(\operatorname{B}^{2n}(a)) and that is ω\omega-tame,
meaning that ω⁡(v,J​v)>0\omega(v,Jv)>0 for all nonzero v∈T​Pv\in TP.
Such an extension exists, since
ω\omega-tame almost complex structures can be viewed as sections
of a bundle over PP whose fibers are contractible.
Lemma.
There exists a JJ-holomorphic sphere u⁡(S2)u(S^{2}) through Φ⁡(0)\Phi(0)
that represents the homology class of S2​(A′)S^{2}(A^{\prime}).
This existence result follows from Gromov’s compactness theorem for JJ-holomorphic curves
in symplectic manifolds.
The key point for the proof of the compactness theorem is that JJ is ω\omega-tame,
implying that JJ-holomorphic curves cannot behave too wildly.
The compactness theorem implies the lemma because the class of S2​(A′)S^{2}(A^{\prime}) is primitive in H2​(P,ℤ)H_{2}(P;\mathbbm{Z}).
The Nonsqueezing Theorem readily follows from the lemma:
The set
S=Φ−1​(Φ⁡(B2​n⁡(a))∩u⁡(S2)){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}S}\,=\,\Phi^{-1}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\Phi(\operatorname{B}^{2n}(a))\cap u(S^{2})}\right)
is a proper 2-dimensional complex surface in B2​n⁡(a)\operatorname{B}^{2n}(a) through 00.
By the Lelong inequality from complex analysis, the area of SS with respect to the
Euclidean inner product is at least aa.
Using also that Φ\Phi is symplectic we can now estimate
a≤area⁡(S)=∫Sω0=∫SΦ∗​ω=∫Φ⁡(S)ω≤∫u⁡(S2)ω=A′.a\,\leq\,\operatorname{area}(S)\,=\,\int_{S}\omega_{0}\,=\,\int_{S}\Phi^{*}\omega\,=\,\int_{\Phi(S)}\omega\,\leq\,\int_{u(S^{2})}\omega\,=\,A^{\prime}.
This proof of the Nonsqueezing Theorem also works, for instance, for closed symplectic manifolds (M,ω)(M,\omega)
for which the integral of ω\omega over spheres vanishes.
In the general case, however, there are troubles with holomorphic spheres of negative Chern number.
By now, these troubles are overcome thanks to the definition of Gromov–Witten invariants for
general closed symplectic manifolds,
by work of several (teams of) authors.
McDuff helped to clarify the approach of
Fukaya–Oh–Ono–Ohta in her joint work with Wehrheim, see [McWe17, Mc19].
In [LaMc95a], however, Lalonde and McDuff circumvented
all technical issues by using a chain of beautiful geometric constructions
that reduced the General Nonsqueezing Theorem essentially to the case
proved by Gromov.
The most important and influential of these is a multiple folding construction.
Its simple version
was used in [LaMc95a] to show how the General Nonsqueezing Theorem implies
that for all symplectic manifolds Hofer’s metric on the group of compactly supported Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms
is non-degenerate and hence indeed a metric, see §3.3.
These two theorems were the first deep results in symplectic geometry proven for
all symplectic manifolds.

The proof of the Ball Packing Theorem is a beautiful story in three chapters,
each of which contains an important idea of McDuff.
The original symplectic embedding problem is converted
to an increasingly algebraic problem in three steps.
The starting point is the symplectic blow-up construction,
that goes back to Gromov and Guillemin–Sternberg, and was first used by
McDuff [Mc91a] to study symplectic embeddings of balls.
Recall that the complex blow-up Bl⁡(ℂ2)\operatorname{Bl}(\mathbbm{C}^{2}) of ℂ2\mathbbm{C}^{2} at the origin 00
is obtained by replacing 00
by all complex lines in ℂ2\mathbbm{C}^{2} through 00.
At the topological level, this operation can be done as follows:
First remove from ℂ2\mathbbm{C}^{2} an open ball B4∘\overset{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}[0.0pt][-0.90417pt]{\mbox{$\!\!\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}\mskip 2.0mu}{\operatorname{B}^{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle 4$}}}.
The boundary S3S^{3} of ℂ2∖B4∘\mathbbm{C}^{2}\setminus\overset{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}[0.0pt][-0.90417pt]{\mbox{$\!\!\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}\mskip 2.0mu}{\operatorname{B}^{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle 4$}}} is foliated by the Hopf circles
{(α​z1,α​z2)∣α∈S1}\{(\alpha z_{1},\alpha z_{2})\mid\alpha\in S^{1}\}, namely the
intersections of S3S^{3} with complex lines.
Now Bl⁡(ℂ2)\operatorname{Bl}(\mathbbm{C}^{2}) is obtained by replacing each such circle by a point.
The boundary sphere S3S^{3} becomes a 2-sphere ℂ​P1\operatorname{\mathbbm{C}P}^{1} in Bl⁡(ℂ2)\operatorname{Bl}(\mathbbm{C}^{2})
of self intersection number −1-1, called the exceptional divisor.
The manifold Bl⁡(ℂ2)\operatorname{Bl}(\mathbbm{C}^{2}) is diffeomorphic to the connected sum ℂ2​#​ℂ​P¯2\mathbbm{C}^{2}\#\overline{\operatorname{\mathbbm{C}P}}^{2}.
This construction can be done in the symplectic setting:
If one removes B4∘​(a)\overset{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}[0.0pt][-0.90417pt]{\mbox{$\!\!\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}\mskip 2.0mu}{\operatorname{B}^{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle 4$}}}(a) from ℝ4\mathbbm{R}^{4},
then there exists a symplectic form ωa\omega_{a} on Bl⁡(ℝ4)\operatorname{Bl}(\mathbbm{R}^{4}) such that ωa=ω0\omega_{a}=\omega_{0}
outside a tubular neighbourhood of the exceptional divisor ℂ​P1\operatorname{\mathbbm{C}P}^{1} and such that ωa\omega_{a} is symplectic
on ℂ​P1\operatorname{\mathbbm{C}P}^{1} with ∫ℂ​P1ωa=a\int_{\operatorname{\mathbbm{C}P}^{1}}\omega_{a}=a.
Given a symplectic embedding φ:B4⁡(a)→(M,ω)\varphi\colon\operatorname{B}^{4}(a)\to(M,\omega) into a symplectic 4-manifold,
we can apply the same construction to φ⁡(B4⁡(a))\varphi(\operatorname{B}^{4}(a))
in MM to obtain the symplectic blow-up of (M,ω)(M,\omega) by weight aa.
There is also an inverse construction:
Given a symplectically embedded −1-1 sphere Σ\Sigma in a symplectic 44-manifold (M,ω)(M,\omega)
of area ∫Σω=a\int_{\Sigma}\omega=a, one can cut out a tubular neighbourhood of Σ\Sigma
and glue back B4⁡(a)\operatorname{B}^{4}(a), to obtain the ‘symplectic blow-down’ of MM.
Now let MkM_{k} be the smooth manifold obtained by blowing up the complex projective plane ℂ​P2\operatorname{\mathbbm{C}P}^{2} in kk points.
Its homology H2​(Mk,ℤ)H_{2}(M_{k};\mathbbm{Z}) is generated by the class LL of a complex line and by the classes
E1,…,EkE_{1},\dots,E_{k} of the exceptional divisors.
Let ℓ,ei∈H2​(Mk,ℤ)\ell,e_{i}\in H^{2}(M_{k};\mathbbm{Z}) be their Poincaré duals.
Every symplectic form ω\omega on MkM_{k} defines a first Chern class c1​(ω)c_{1}(\omega),
namely the first Chern class of any ω\omega-tame almost complex structure.
If we take a symplectic form ω\omega on MkM_{k} constructed as above via kk symplectic ball embeddings

3. Other contributions to symplectic geometry
3.1. Construction of (counter-)examples
McDuff again and again has given explicit constructions that are
both elementary and profound.
You can grasp them quickly, yet they
can be used to do many other things.
We already encountered some of them in the previous section,
and I now mention three more.
A simply-connected symplectic manifold that is not Kähler
While Kähler manifolds are symplectic, the converse is not true.
For instance, the odd-index Betti numbers of compact Kähler manifolds are even.
The first example of a compact symplectic manifold that is not Kähler
was found by Thurston; his example (M,ω)(M,\omega) is a T2T^{2}-bundle over T2T^{2} with b1​(M)=3b_{1}(M)=3.
In [Mc84], McDuff constructed simply-connected examples.
She symplectically embedded the Thurston manifold MM into ℂ​P5\operatorname{\mathbbm{C}P}^{5}
and showed that the symplectic blow-up of ℂ​P5\operatorname{\mathbbm{C}P}^{5} along MM
is simply-connected and has b3=b1​(M)=3b_{3}=b_{1}(M)=3.
Cohomologous symplectic forms that are not diffeomorphic
Consider two symplectic forms ω0\omega_{0} and ω1\omega_{1} on a closed symplectic manifold
that are cohomologous.
If these two forms can be connected by a smooth path ωt\omega_{t} of cohomologous symplectic forms,
then there exists an isotopy of MM deforming ω0\omega_{0} to ω1\omega_{1}, by Moser’s trick.
This is the case, for instance, for any two area forms on a closed surface.
In general, however, there may be no such path, as McDuff showed in [Mc87].
Take M=S2×S2×T2M=S^{2}\times S^{2}\times T^{2} with the standard split symplectic form ω\omega
giving all factors area one.
Define
Ψ⁡(z,w,(s,t))=(z,Rz,t​(w),s,t)\Psi\bigl(z,w,(s,t)\bigr)\,=\,\bigl(z,R_{z,t}(w),s,t\bigr)
where Rz,tR_{z,t} is the rotation by angle 2​π​t2\pi t of the round sphere
about the axis through z,−zz,-z.
Then the symplectic forms ωk=(Ψk)∗​ω\omega_{k}=(\Psi^{k})^{*}\omega, k∈ℤ≥0k\in\mathbbm{Z}_{\geq 0},
are all cohomologous, and they can be joined by a path of symplectic forms, but by no such path in
the same cohomology class.
The last part of the statement was McDuff’s first result
obtained by using JJ-holomorphic curves.
She studied the space of ωk\omega_{k}-tame JJ-holomorphic spheres
in the class of the first S2S^{2}-factor,
and showed that such spheres wrap kk-times around the second factor.
Since a diffeomorphism isotopic to the identity acts trivially on homology,
an isotopy deforming ωk\omega_{k} to ωℓ\omega_{\ell} can therefore only exist if k=ℓk=\ell.
Also in [Mc87] McDuff improved this construction to obtain cohomologous symplectic forms
on an 8-dimensional symplectic manifold that are not even diffeomorphic.
Disconnected contact boundaries
The natural boundaries of symplectic manifolds are those of contact type,
meaning that there exists a vector field XX defined near ∂M\partial M that is transverse to ∂M\partial M,
pointing outwards, and is conformally symplectic: ℒX​ω=d​ιX​ω=ω{\mathcal{L}}_{X}\omega=d\iota_{X}\omega=\omega.
Symplectic manifolds with boundary of contact type are analogous in many ways to
complex manifolds with pseudo-convex boundaries, as was shown by Eliashberg and Gromov [El89, ElGr89, El90].
In the latter situation, the boundary is always connected.
However, McDuff [Mc91b] explicitly constructed a compact symplectic 4-manifold
whose boundary is of contact type and disconnected.
She starts with the cotangent bundle T∗​ΣT^{*}\Sigma over a closed orientable surface of genus ≥2\geq 2,
endowed with its canonical symplectic form d​λd\lambda, where λ=∑ipi​d​qi\lambda=\sum_{i}p_{i}\>\!dq_{i}.
Also take a Riemannian metric of constant curvature on Σ\Sigma.
It induced the connection 11-form β\beta of the Levi-Civita connection on T∗​ΣT^{*}\Sigma
and the radial coordinate rr on the fibers.
Then one finds smooth functions f,gf,g on [0,∞)[0,\infty) such that
on the annulus bundle {x∈T∗​Σ∣12≤r⁡(x)≤1}\{x\in T^{*}\Sigma\mid\frac{1}{2}\leq r(x)\leq 1\} the 2-form
ω=d⁡(f⁡(r)​β+g⁡(r)​λ)\omega\,=\,d\bigl(f(r)\;\!\beta+g(r)\>\!\lambda\bigr)
is symplectic and makes the boundary of contact type.
3.2. The structure of rational and ruled symplectic 4-manifolds
The classification of compact complex surfaces is an old and beautiful topic
in complex and Kähler geometry.
Before 1990, the only result on symplectic 4-manifolds in this direction
was a theorem of Gromov for the complex projective plane.
In [Mc90], McDuff proved the following generalization:
If a closed symplectic 4-manifold contains a symplectically embedded sphere
with non-negative self-intersection number,
then it is symplectomorphic to either ℂ​P2\operatorname{\mathbbm{C}P}^{2} with its standard Kähler structure,
to a ruled symplectic manifold,
or to a symplectic blow-up of one of these manifolds.
Here, a ruled symplectic 4-manifold is the total space of an S2S^{2}-fibration
over a closed oriented surface with
a symplectic structure that is non-degenerate on the fibers.
In later work with Lalonde [LaMc96a], McDuff classified ruled symplectic surfaces:
If (M,ω)(M,\omega) is a ruled symplectic 4-manifold, then ω\omega is determined up to symplectomorphism
by its cohomology class and is isotopic to a standard Kähler form on MM.
Li–Liu [LiLiu95] complemented this result by showing that if (M,ω)(M,\omega) is the total space of an
S2S^{2}-fibration over a closed surface, then there is a ruling of MM by symplectic spheres, i.e. (M,ω)(M,\omega) is a ruled symplectic manifold.
See [LaMc96b] for a survey on this classification.
An elementary but important point in the proofs is that two cohomologous symplectic forms
ω0\omega_{0} and ω1\omega_{1} are diffeomorphic
if they tame the same almost complex structure JJ.
Indeed, all the cohomologous forms ωt=(1−t)​ω0+t​ω1\omega_{t}=(1-t)\omega_{0}+t\omega_{1}, t∈[0,1]t\in[0,1],
then tame JJ and hence are symplectic,
and therefore ω0\omega_{0} and ω1\omega_{1} are diffeomorphic by Moser’s argument.
These works showed that symplectic geometry has something to say about 4-manifolds,
and they helped establish symplectic geometry as one of the core geometries.
McDuff has also done much interesting work on the topology of
the group of symplectomorphisms for several symplectic manifolds.
For S2S^{2}-fibrations over S2S^{2} with any symplectic form
she has in particular shown in joint work with Abreu [AbMc00]
that two symplectomorphisms are isotopic through symplectomorphisms
whenever they are isotopic through diffeomorphisms.
3.3. Hofer geometry
Recall that any symplectic manifold (M,ω)(M,\omega) locally looks like
the standard symplectic vector space of the same dimension.
Our dear geometric intuition from everyday life,
so useful in Riemannian geometry to see distances and curvatures,
is thus useless in symplectic geometry.
However, on the automorphism group Hamc⁡(M,ω)\operatorname{Ham^{c}}(M,\omega) of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms
that are generated by compactly supported functions,
there is a bi-invariant Finsler metric, that to some extent serves as a substitute
for the absence of local geometry in (M,ω)(M,\omega).
Given a time-dependent Hamiltonian function H:M×[0,1]→ℝH\colon M\times[0,1]\to\mathbbm{R}
with compact support,
take the integrated oscillation
‖H‖:=∫01(maxx∈M⁡H⁡(x,t)−minx∈M⁡H⁡(x,t))​𝑑t.\|H\|\,:=\,\int_{0}^{1}\left(\max_{x\in M}H(x,t)-\min_{x\in M}H(x,t)\right)dt.
For φ∈Hamc⁡(M,ω)\varphi\in\operatorname{Ham^{c}}(M,\omega) now define
d⁡(φ,id)=infH‖H‖d(\varphi,\mbox{id})\,=\,\inf_{H}\|H\|
where HH runs over all compactly supported Hamiltonian functions
whose time-1 flow map is φ\varphi.
Then d⁡(φ,ψ)=d⁡(φ​ψ−1,id)d(\varphi,\psi)=d(\varphi\psi^{-1},\mbox{id}) defines a bi-invariant metric on Hamc⁡(M,ω)\operatorname{Ham^{c}}(M,\omega).
The only difficult point to check is non-degeneracy.
This was done by Hofer [Ho90] for ℝ2​n\mathbbm{R}^{2n} by variational methods,
by Polterovich [Po93] for tame rational symplectic manifolds
by using a rigidity property of Lagrangian submanifolds,
and for all symplectic manifolds by Lalonde–McDuff [LaMc95a] who used
their General Nonsqueezing Theorem discussed in §2.1
and the symplectic folding construction:
Figure 3.1. The symplectic folding construction, schematically
Assume that φ≠id\varphi\neq\mbox{id}.
Then we find a symplectically embedded ball B2​n⁡(a)\operatorname{B}^{2n}(a) in MM
such that φ⁡(B2​n⁡(a))∩B2​n⁡(a)=∅\varphi(\operatorname{B}^{2n}(a))\cap\operatorname{B}^{2n}(a)=\emptyset.

and assume that φ\varphi is generated in time 11 by HH.
In three steps, the small green ball B2​n+2⁡(a2)⊂B2​n+2⁡(a)\operatorname{B}^{2n+2}(\frac{a}{2})\subset\operatorname{B}^{2n+2}(a) is folded on top of
its complement,
as illustrated in Figure 3.1:
First, one views B2​n+2⁡(a)\operatorname{B}^{2n+2}(a) as a B2​n\operatorname{B}^{2n}-fibration over the disc B2⁡(a)\operatorname{B}^{2}(a)
and separates the small fibres from the large ones.
In the key step, one then uses the flow ϕHt\phi^{t}_{H}, t∈[0,1]t\in[0,1],
to lift the small green ball.
The projection of the red band in the image of λ\lambda to ℝ2\mathbbm{R}^{2}
has area ‖H‖\|H\|.

one can thus turn the green ball over the blue part to obtain an embedding
B2​n+2⁡(a)​↪𝑠​B2⁡(a2+‖H‖)×M.\operatorname{B}^{2n+2}(a)\,\overset{\raisebox{-0.60275pt}[0.0pt][-0.60275pt]{\mbox{$\scriptstyle s$}}\mskip 2.5mu}{\hookrightarrow}\,\operatorname{B}^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{a}{2}+\|H\|\bigr)\times M.
Hence ‖H‖≥a2\|H\|\geq\frac{a}{2} by the General Nonsqueezing Theorem.
You can readily grasp the construction from the three figures on pages 473, 474, 475 of [McSa17].
Symplectic folding found many other applications to symplectic geometry,
see for instance [Hi15].
In [LaMc95b], Lalonde and McDuff made a deep study of geodesics in this Finsler geometry
on Hamc⁡(M,ω)\operatorname{Ham^{c}}(M,\omega),
that lead to completely new geometric intuitions on this group.
It then became possible to think about Hamiltonian dynamics
in geometric terms such as geodesic, conjugate point, cut-locus, etc.
See Polterovich’s book [Po01] for more on this.
The books.
While in her papers Dusa shows herself to be an impressive and creative problem solver,
the two books she wrote with Dietmar Salamon were (and remain)
crucial for the foundation of symplectic geometry and its dissemination.
The “Introduction to symplectic topology” [McSa17]
explains the methods and results of the field in clear and modern
geometric language.
A key for the success of this book is that it discusses the main results in the most important
and typical cases, without striving for generality.
I was very lucky that this book came out (in 1995) just when I wanted to learn the subject.
It is one of the main reasons for the transformation of the then
small community of symplectic geometers into a large family.
In the third edition from 2017, the authors added a chapter with 54 open problems,
proving that symplectic geometry is not a closed chapter but
rather remains an exploding field.
The second book [McSa12] provides a rigorous foundation of the theory of
JJ-holomorphic curves and explains their applications to symplectic topology.
The exposition is so precise and to the point that one can often just cite the
result one needs.
This book transformed the formerly somewhat romantic theory of JJ-holomorphic curves
into a well established tool of enormous impact.
In addition to her incredible mathematical legacy,
Dusa’s extraordinary generosity to young (and not so young) researchers,
her enthusiasm and her (sometimes overwhelming) energy,
and her heartfelt commitment in scientific, political, and social issues
should be recognized.
