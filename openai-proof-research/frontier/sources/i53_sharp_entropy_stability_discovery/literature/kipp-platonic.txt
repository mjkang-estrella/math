   A polytope P ⊂ Rn is the convex hull of finitely many points. Throughout this article,
we restrict our attention to polytopes that have non-empty interior. A polytope is called
simplicial if every facet of P is a simplex. We say that a vertex v ∈ P is simplicial if every
facet F ⊂ P with v ∈ F is a simplex.
   In [20], Rademacher proved the following remarkable result, which provides supporting
evidence for (1.1).
Theorem 1.1 (Rademacher). Let P ⊂ Rn be a polytope that locally maximizes the
isotropic constant. If P is simplicial, then P is a simplex.
   The proof strategy of Theorem 1.1 presented in [20] also forms the basis of the present
article. Broadly speaking, Theorem 1.1 can be interpreted as saying that a polytopal local
maximizer of K 7→ LK with “generic” boundary structure has to be highly regular. In the
following, we present several new results in this spirit. As we vary the exact assumptions
on P , each of the three high-dimensional “Platonic solids” (regular n-polytopes) will make
its appearance.
   We say that two facets F1 , F2 of an n-dimensional polytope P are adjacent if the face
F1 ∩ F2 is a ridge of P , i.e., an (n − 2)-dimensional face of P . We note that a simplicial
polytope P has the following property: for every pair of adjacent facets F1 , F2 , there exists
an affine map that maps F1 to F2 and fixes F1 ∩ F2 pointwise. In Section 2, we show that
a polytopal maximizer of the isotropic constant with this property must be a simplex,
generalizing Theorem 1.1.
   The proof of Theorem 1.1 given in [20] consists of two parts: using a first-order condition
for the facets, it is first shown that the local maximizer P must be symmetric with respect
to a hyperplane H; then a result due to Campi, Colesanti and Gronchi [4, Thm. 3.6]
is used to show that it cannot be a local maximizer unless it is a simplex. Using the
techniques from [4], we show in Section 3 that a polytope P with a hyperplane symmetry
“locally at a simplicial vertex” cannot be a maximizer, leading to the following alternative
strengthening of Theorem 1.1.
Theorem 1.2. Let P ⊂ Rn be a polytope that locally maximizes the isotropic constant. If
P has a simplicial vertex, then P is a simplex.

   Finally, we consider zonotopes. A zonotope Z is an affine image of a cube or, equiva-
lently, the Minkowski sum of finitely many line segments, which are called the generators
of Z. A zonoid is a convex body that can be approximated by zonotopes (with respect to
the Hausdorff metric). The isotropic constant conjecture is known to be true for zonoids
[18]. It seems that the best known upper bound is LZ ≤ 12 [19, 3]. We note that the
class of zonoids not only contains the conjectured maximizers from (1.2), but also the
minimizers in every dimension n ≥ 1, which are known to be ellipsoids.
   The set of all faces of a zonotope Z that have a given generator as a Minkowski summand
is called a zone of Z. We say that a zone is cubical if it contains only parallelepipeds. In
Section 5, we show the following result, which can be interpreted as further supporting
evidence for (1.2).
Theorem 1.4. Let Z ⊂ Rn be an isotropic zonotope that locally maximizes the isotropic
constant in the class of centrally symmetric convex bodies. If Z has a cubical zone, then
Z is a cube.
   It was shown by McMullen [16] that if all ridges of a polytope P are centrally symmet-
ric, then P is a zonotope; in particular, this holds if all facets of P are parallelepipeds.
Therefore, Theorem 1.4 implies that a polytopal maximizer of K 7→ LK in the class of
centrally symmetric convex bodies must be a cube if its facets are all parallelepipeds.
   We emphasize that all theorems up to this point are hypothetical in the sense that it is
unknown whether polytopes with the respective properties actually exist. In particular,
Theorem 1.4 yields no upper bound on isotropic constants of zonotopes. In Section 6,
we turn to zonotopes with at most n + 1 generators and show that (1.2) holds in this
class. We also determine the minimizers in this class, which in three dimensions turn out
to be affine images of the regular rhombic dodecahedron (and an n-dimensional analogue
thereof in the general case).

   Evidently, the existence of Euclidean reflectors in the boundary of P imposes strong
restrictions on the structure of P . More specifically, if every pair of facets is connected by
a path of Euclidean reflectors (i.e., a path in the graph of P ∗ , the polar of P ), then P is
monohedral, i.e., all facets of P are congruent. Moreover, if every ridge of P is a Euclidean
reflector, then P is even isohedral, i.e., the (full) symmetry group of P acts transitively on
the facets of P . This follows from the following lemma, whose statement is used in [20].
We spell out the simple proof for later reference.
Lemma 2.4. Let P ⊂ Rn be a polytope. If every ridge of P is a Euclidean reflector,
then P is symmetric with respect to the hyperplane spanned by any ridge G ⊂ P and the
centroid of P .

   Combining Lemma 2.3 and Lemma 2.4, we obtain the following result, which generalizes
[20, Thm. 1]. Here and in the following, we call a hyperplane linear if it contains the origin.
Theorem 2.5. Let P be an isotropic polytope at which the isotropic constant has a local
extremum. If every ridge G ⊂ P is an affine reflector, then P is symmetric with respect
to the linear hyperplane spanned by any ridge and, in particular, isohedral.
   As discussed in Remark 2.1, there are no polytopal local minimizers of the isotropic
constant. Consequently, the statement about local minimizers in Theorem 2.5 is vacuously
true if the term “local extremum” is interpreted as “local extremum in the class of convex
bodies”. However, it is easy to see that (2.2) and (2.3) are necessary conditions for a
polytopal local extremal body P in the class of polytopes with at most fn−1 (P ) facets,
where fn−1 (P ) denotes the number of facets of P . Interpreting the term “local extremum”
in this way, we strengthen Theorem 2.5 and obtain a non-vacuous statement for local
minimizers.
   Up to this point, we have only used the conditions (2.2) and (2.3), which cannot dis-
tinguish between local maximizers and local minimizers of the isotropic constant. This
brings us to the second ingredient of the proof of Theorem 1.1 used in [20], namely, the
following result from [4].
Theorem 2.6 (Campi-Colesanti-Gronchi). Let H ⊂ Rn be a linear hyperplane and K ⊂
Rn a convex body. Moreover, let L := H ⊥ be the orthogonal complement of H. If K is
symmetric with respect to H, then K cannot be a local maximizer of the isotropic constant
unless the function x 7→ vol1 [K ∩ (L + x)] is affine on K.

behind this result will be discussed in the next section. Adding Theorem 2.6 as a second
ingredient to our previous results, we arrive at the main result of this section.

every ridge G ⊂ P is an affine reflector, then P is a simplex.

                               5. Cubical zones of zonotopes

                     6. Zonotopes with at most n + 1 generators
   In the preceding sections, we obtained structural information about local maximizers
of the isotropic constant under various assumptions concerning the presence of affine re-
flectors. However, the results above do not yield upper bounds on the isotropic constant
(even for the respective subclasses of the space of convex bodies) because it is a priori
unclear whether local maximizers with the respective properties exist. This is due to two
independent reasons:
    (1) the classes under consideration (polytopes whose ridges are all affine reflectors,
         polytopes with a simplicial vertex, zonotopes with a cubical zone) are not closed
         with respect to the Banach-Mazur distance;
    (2) the first-order condition (2.3) is only valid for a local maximizer P in the class of
         polytopes with at most fn−1 (P ) facets.
