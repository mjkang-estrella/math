Abstract.
We give an alternative proof of the 5/4 result of Guth-Iosevich-Ou-Wang on the Falconer distance conjecture: if a compact set E in the plane has Hausdorff dimension >5/4, then there exists x\in E such that the pinned distance set
\Delta_{x}(E):=\{|x-y|:y\in E\}
has positive Lebesgue measure. Moreover, we are able to beat the 5/4 barrier if the packing dimension of E is <2. More generally, we obtain sufficient conditions involving both the Hausdorff and packing dimensions of E. These conditions improve the 5/4 threshold when the packing dimension of E is <2, and recover the second author’s result for \dim_{\textup{H}}E=\dim_{\textup{P}}E>1. The proof is based on L^{2} ball inflation and does not use decoupling inequalities.
Key words and phrases: Falconer’s distance problem, Hausdorff dimension, packing dimension
2010 Mathematics Subject Classification51E30, 28A75
1. Introduction
For a set E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}, define its distance set to be
\Delta(E):=\{|x-x^{\prime}|:x,x^{\prime}\in E\}.
We also define the pinned distance set:
For p\in\mathbb{R}^{2}, define
\Delta_{p}(E):=\{|x-p|:x\in E\}.
Let \dim_{\textup{H}} denote the Hausdorff dimension, and \dim_{\textup{P}} denote the packing dimension.
The goal of this paper is to prove the following result.
Theorem 1.1.
Let E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} be a compact set and let \alpha=\dim_{\textup{H}}(E)>1,\beta=\dim_{\textup{P}}(E). If
(1)
\alpha>\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{2\beta}
and
(2)
P(\alpha,\beta):=\alpha^{3}-(2\beta-1)\alpha^{2}-2\alpha+(3\beta-1)<0,
then there exists a point x\in E such that the pinned distance set \{|x-y|\}_{y\in E} has positive Lebesgue measure.
As a corollary, we recover the result of Guth, Iosevich, Ou and Wang [8].
Corollary 1.2.
If E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} is compact and satisfies
\dim_{\textup{H}}(E)>\frac{5}{4},
then there exists a point x\in E such that the pinned distance set \{|x-y|\}_{y\in E} has positive Lebesgue measure.
Proof.
Denote \alpha:=\dim_{\textup{H}}(E)>\frac{5}{4},\beta:=\dim_{\textup{P}}(E).
Since \beta\leq 2, we have
\alpha>\frac{5}{4}\geq\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{2\beta}.
Noting that the coefficient of \beta in P(\alpha,\beta) is 3-2\alpha^{2}<0 and \beta\in[\alpha,2], we have
P(\alpha,\beta)\leq P(\alpha,\alpha)=-\alpha^{3}+\alpha^{2}+\alpha-1=-(\alpha-1)^{2}(\alpha+1)<0.
By Theorem 1.1, the corollary holds.
∎
Remark 1.3.
It is surprising to us that our proof does not use decoupling, but nevertheless reaches the “\frac{5}{4}-threshold”.
We also recover the equal Hausdorff/packing dimension case proved by the second author [12].
Corollary 1.4.
If E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} is compact and satisfies
\dim_{\textup{H}}(E)=\dim_{\textup{P}}(E)>1,
then there exists a point x\in E such that the pinned distance set \{|x-y|\}_{y\in E} has positive Lebesgue measure.
Proof.
Denote \alpha:=\dim_{\textup{H}}(E)=\dim_{\textup{P}}(E)>1. Then \alpha>\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{2\alpha}, and P(\alpha,\alpha)=-(\alpha-1)^{2}(\alpha+1)<0. By Theorem 1.1, the corollary holds.
∎
If one wants to improve the \frac{5}{4}-threshold, one observes a specific structure in the set E:
Corollary 1.5.
If E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} is a compact set satisfying \dim_{\textup{H}}(E)=\frac{5}{4}
and |\Delta_{x}(E)|=0 for all x\in E, then \dim_{\textup{P}}(E)=2.
Proof.
Suppose \beta:=\dim_{\textup{P}}(E)<2, then \alpha:=\dim_{\textup{H}}(E)=\frac{5}{4}>\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{2\beta}. By Theorem 1.1, we must have
P(\frac{5}{4},\beta)\geq 0.
However, P(\frac{5}{4},\beta)\leq P(\frac{5}{4},\frac{5}{4})<0, which is a contradiction.
∎
1.1. Historical results
Falconer [5] proved that if E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} is compact and \dim_{\mathrm{H}}(E)>(d+1)/2, then |\Delta(E)|>0. He conjectured that the conclusion should hold under the hypothesis
\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(E)>d/2. In the plane, Wolff [22] proved the result for \dim_{\mathrm{H}}(E)>4/3.
In higher dimensions, Erdogan [4] proved it for \dim_{\mathrm{H}}(E)>d/2+1/3. Later, Du, Guth,
Ou, Wang, Wilson, and Zhang [2], and Du and Zhang [3], developed weighted L^{2}
restriction estimates and used them to obtain further progress on Falconer’s distance problem.
In 2019, the second author [11] proposed an L^{2} approach to the pinned distance set problem. A
breakthrough was subsequently obtained by Guth, Iosevich, Ou, and Wang [8], who proved that if
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} is compact and \dim_{\mathrm{H}}(E)>5/4, then |\Delta_{x}(E)|>0 for some x\in E. Their proof
introduced the good-bad decomposition and applied decoupling inequalities to the distance set problem. This remains
the best known threshold in dimension two.
The other direction on Falconer’s distance problem is to give a lower bound on the Hausdorff dimension of \Delta(E),\Delta_{x}(E) assuming \dim_{H}(E)>\frac{d}{2}.
In the plane, the best record was obtained by Stull [20], who used computability theory to show that
\dim_{\textup{H}}(E)>1 implies \dim_{\textup{H}}(\Delta_{x}(E))\geq\frac{3}{4} for some x\in E. Also, see the work of Katz and Tao [9], Bourgain [1], Wang and Zheng [21], Shmerkin [19], Keleti and Shmerkin [10], Shmerkin and Wang [16], Fiedler and Stull [6] [7]. In [6], Fiedler and Stull studied the distance problem taking into account both Hausdorff dimension and packing dimension. They proved that for E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} with \alpha=\dim_{\textup{H}}(E)>1,\beta=\dim_{\textup{P}}(E), if \beta<\frac{(3+\sqrt{5})\alpha-1-\sqrt{5}}{2}, then \dim(\Delta_{x}(E))=1 for some x\in E. Comparing with Theorem 1.1, on the one hand, our conclusion is |\Delta_{x}(E)|>0; on the other hand, we obtain some new range for the pair (\alpha,\beta) (for example, (\alpha,\beta)=(\frac{5}{4},2-\varepsilon) is in our range but not in their range).
The distance set problem is also studied under some regularity assumption. See the work of Orponen [13] [14], Shmerkin [17] [18].
Very recently, the second author [12] proved that \dim_{\textup{H}}(E)=\dim_{\textup{P}}(E)>1 implies |\Delta_{x}(E)|>0 for some x\in E. He introduced a multiscale good-bad decomposition and a multiscale Mizohata-Takeuchi-type estimate.
1.2. Structure of the paper
In Section 2, we sketch the proof of the main theorem. In Section 3, we introduce the terminology
used throughout the paper. In Section 4, we prove a lemma for Lipschitz functions. In Section 5, we
reduce Theorem 1.1 to Proposition 5.1. In Section 6, we perform the multiscale
good-bad decomposition and estimate the bad part. In Section 7, we estimate the good part. In
Section 8, we prove Theorem 1.1. Finally, in Section 9, we prove the weighted
L^{2} estimate.
1.3. Notation
We use A\lesssim B to denote A\leq CB, where C is some universal constant. If A\lesssim B and B\lesssim A, then we denote it by A\sim B. For a fixed scale R\geq 1, we use A\lessapprox B to denote that A\leq C_{\gamma}R^{\gamma}B for any \gamma>0. Similarly, we use A\approx B to denote A\lessapprox B and B\lessapprox A. For R\geq 1, we use \textup{RapDec}(R) to denote a quantity that \lesssim C_{N}R^{-N} for any N\geq 1.
2. An overview of the proof
2.1. Weighted L^{2} estimate
The key task in the paper is to prove a weighted L^{2} estimate, which we formulate as follows.
Fix u\in[1,2]. Let X\subset B(0,1) be a union of disjoint R^{-1}-balls. We assume X satisfies:
(3)
\begin{split}&|X\cap B(x,r)|\lesssim R^{-2}(rR)^{u}\ \ \ \textup{for~any~}x\in B(0,1)\textup{~and~}R^{-1}\leq r\leq 1,\\
&|X|=R^{-2}R^{u}.\end{split}
(Here, |E| is the Lebesgue measure of the set E.)
Suppose F:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C} is any function with \mathrm{supp}\widehat{F}\subset S^{1}_{R}, where S^{1}_{R} is the circle of radius R. We are interested in finding the smallest number D(u) such that
(4)
\frac{1}{|X|}\|F\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\lessapprox R^{D(u)}\|F\|_{L^{2}(B(0,1))}^{2}
holds for all R\geq 1 and X,F as above. We use \mathbf{F}(u) to denote the following statement.
\mathbf{F}(u): If E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} is a compact set of Hausdorff dimension greater than u, then there exists x\in E such that \Delta_{x}(E) has positive Lebesgue measure.
To help understand the ideas, we will make several informal claims in this section without proofs. The rigorous proofs will be given in later sections.
We have the following implication from (4) to Falconer’s distance problem.
Claim 1.
Suppose (4) holds for D(u) and D(u)\leq u-1, then \mathbf{F}(u) holds.
Remark 2.1.
A classical weighted L^{2} estimate shows that (4) holds for D(u)=1-\frac{u}{2} (see, for example, [3, Corollary 1.7]). Plugging into D(u)\leq u-1, we see that \mathbf{F}(\frac{4}{3}) holds. This corresponds to the result of Wolff [22].
It was observed by Guth-Iosevich-Ou-Wang [8] that one does not need the full strength of (4) in order to show \mathbf{F}(u). Indeed, one may throw away the “bad” part of F which can be handled by Orponen’s radial projection theorem [15], and prove an estimate for the remaining “good” part. Such an estimate suffices to derive \mathbf{F}(u). The trick nowadays is known as the good-bad decomposition. Recently, the second author [12] introduced a multiscale good-bad decomposition, which we will adopt in this paper. Since it is too complicated to state here, we postpone the definition later. At present, we just use it as a black box and assume for any function F as above, we have a multiscale good-bad decomposition
F=F_{\textup{good}}+F_{\textup{bad}}.
Here, F_{\textup{good}} is obtained from F by throwing away some “heavy” wave packets. So, we can still assume \mathrm{supp}\widehat{F}_{\textup{good}}\subset S^{1}_{R} and \|F_{\textup{good}}\|_{2}\lesssim\|F\|_{2}.
Similar to (4), we are now interested in
(5)
\frac{1}{|X|}\|F_{\textup{good}}\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\lessapprox R^{D_{\textup{good}}(u)}\|F_{\textup{good}}\|_{L^{2}(B(0,1))}^{2}.
We have a similar implication:
Claim 2.
Suppose (5) holds for D_{\textup{good}}(u) and D_{\textup{good}}(u)\leq u-1, then \mathbf{F}(u) holds.
Remark 2.2.
Guth-Iosevich-Ou-Wang [8] proved that (5) holds for D_{\textup{good}}(u)=\frac{2-u}{3}. Plugging into D_{\textup{good}}(u)\leq u-1, we see that \mathbf{F}(\frac{5}{4}) holds.
Recently, the second author [12] showed that D_{\textup{good}}(u)=0 under the assumption that u>1 and X is AD-regular. (Here, AD-regular means |X\cap B(x,r)|\sim R^{-2}(rR)^{u} for all x\in X and R^{-1}\leq r\leq 1.)
2.2. Packing dimensions
To prove Theorem 1.1, we also need to incorporate the packing dimension. Recall that (3) is a discretized interpretation that X has Hausdorff dimension \geq u. Now we introduce a discretized interpretation about the packing dimension.
Let |X|_{r} denote the smallest number of r-balls needed to cover X, also called the r-covering number of X. Let \kappa\in[u,2]. In addition to (3), we also require X to satisfy:
(6)
|X|_{r}\lesssim r^{-\kappa}\textup{~for~all~}R^{-1}\leq r\leq 1.
We are interested in the following estimate:
(7)
\frac{1}{|X|}\|F_{\textup{good}}\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\lessapprox R^{D_{\textup{good}}(u,\kappa)}\|F_{\textup{good}}\|_{L^{2}(B(0,1))}^{2},
where X satisfies (3) and (6). Denote the following statement:
\mathbf{F}(u,\kappa): If E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} is a compact set of Hausdorff dimension greater than u and packing dimension smaller than \kappa, then there exists x\in E such that \Delta_{x}(E) has positive Lebesgue measure.
We have the following informal results.
Claim 3.
Suppose (7) holds for D_{\textup{good}}(u,\kappa) and D_{\textup{good}}(u,\kappa)\leq u-1, then \mathbf{F}(u,\kappa) holds.
Claim 4.
(7) holds for D_{\textup{good}}(u,\kappa)=\max\{\frac{\kappa-u}{2\kappa-1},\frac{\kappa-u}{(u+1)(2\kappa-u-1)}\}. Therefore, by the previous Claim, \mathbf{F}(u,\kappa) holds if u>\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{2\kappa} and P(u,\kappa)<0.
2.3. Ball inflation process
We discuss the strategy to prove Claim 4. It is through a ball inflation process. Recall that X is a union of R^{-1}-balls satisfying (3) and (6). To pass from the left-hand side to the right-hand side in (7), we choose a sequence of scales 1=r_{0}>r_{1}>\dots>r_{k+1}=R^{-1}. Let N_{r_{i}}(X) be the r_{i}-neighborhood of X. We may morally view N_{r_{i}}(X) as a union of r_{i}-balls. Essentially, N_{r_{k+1}}(X) is X and N_{r_{0}}(X) is B(0,1). For each 1\leq i\leq k, we are going to prove an estimate
(8)
\frac{1}{|N_{r_{i}}(X)|}\|F_{\textup{good}}\|_{L^{2}(N_{r_{i}}(X))}^{2}\lessapprox R^{D_{i}}\frac{1}{|N_{r_{i-1}}(X)|}\|F_{\textup{good}}\|_{L^{2}(N_{r_{i-1}}(X))}^{2}.
Each time, the balls are inflated from radius r_{i} to r_{i-1}. Combining all the estimates gives us
\frac{1}{|X|}\|F_{\textup{good}}\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\lessapprox R^{D_{1}+\dots+D_{k+1}}\|F_{\textup{good}}\|_{L^{2}(B(0,1))}^{2}.
Hence, our goal becomes to carefully choose a sequence \{r_{i}\} so as to minimize \sum_{i=1}^{k+1}D_{i}.
Next, we will focus on the one-step ball inflation estimate (8). Before doing that we introduce some notation. First of all, we may assume X satisfies the uniform property:
For any R^{-1}\leq r\leq 1, the quantities |B_{r}\cap X| are comparable for all dyadic r-cubes B_{r} such that B_{r}\cap X\neq\emptyset.
By the uniform property of X, one may see that |X|_{r}\sim\frac{|X|}{|B_{r}\cap X|}.
We define the branching function associated to X to be f:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,\infty) in the following way. For x\in[0,1], let r=R^{-x}. Then f(x) is defined so that
|X|_{r}\sim R^{f(x)}.
(Note that f is defined up to an error about O(\frac{1}{\log R}).)
We summarize the properties of f.
(i)
f is increasing; f(0)=0 since |X|_{1}=1; f(1)=u since |X|_{R^{-1}}=R^{2}|X|=R^{u}.
(ii)
f(x)\geq ux, since R^{f(x)}=|X|_{r}\sim\frac{|X|}{|B_{r}\cap X|}\gtrsim\frac{R^{-2}R^{u}}{R^{-2}(rR)^{u}}=R^{ux}, where r=R^{-x}.
(iii)
\textup{Lip}(f)\leq 2. Indeed, for x^{\prime}>x, r=R^{-x},r^{\prime}=R^{-x^{\prime}}, one has R^{f(x^{\prime})-f(x)}\sim\frac{|X|_{r^{\prime}}}{|X|_{r}}\sim\frac{|B_{r}\cap X|}{|B_{r^{\prime}}\cap X|}\lesssim\frac{|B_{r}|}{|B_{r^{\prime}}|}\sim R^{2(x^{\prime}-x)}.
(iv)
f(x)\leq\kappa x. Indeed, for r=R^{-x}, one has R^{f(x)}=|X|_{r}\lesssim r^{-\kappa}=R^{x\kappa}. Here, we used (6).
Now we give the following definitions.
Definition 2.3.
Let f be the branching function associated to X. For [x_{1},x_{2}]\subset[0,1] and s\in[0,2], we say f is s-Frostman on [x_{1},x_{2}] if for all x\in[x_{1},x_{2}],
f(x)\geq f(x_{1})+s(x-x_{1}).
We say f is s-Katz-Tao on [x_{1},x_{2}] if for all x\in[x_{1},x_{2}],
f(x)\geq f(x_{2})+s(x-x_{2}).
We state the three main ingredients that are key steps in the proof of Claim 4. The first one was introduced by the second author in [12].
Ingredient 1 (Lossless ball inflation (informal)).
Let f be the branching function associated to X. Let [x_{1},x_{2}]\subset[0,1] and r_{i}=R^{-x_{i}} (i=1,2). If f is 1-Frostman on [x_{1},x_{2}] and 1-x_{1}\leq 2(1-x_{2}), then
(9)
\frac{1}{|N_{r_{2}}(X)|}\|F_{\textup{good}}\|_{L^{2}(N_{r_{2}}(X))}^{2}\lessapprox\frac{1}{|N_{r_{1}}(X)|}\|F_{\textup{good}}\|_{L^{2}(N_{r_{1}}(X))}^{2}.
Ingredient 2.
Let f be the branching function associated to X. Let x\in[0,1] and r=R^{-x}. If f is u-Katz-Tao on [x,1], then
(10)
\|F_{\textup{good}}\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\lessapprox(\frac{|X|_{R^{-1}}}{|X|_{r}})^{\frac{u}{u+1}}\frac{1}{rR}\|F_{\textup{good}}\|_{L^{2}(N_{r}(X))}^{2}.
Remark 2.4.
Our weighted L^{2} estimate is different from that proved by Du and Zhang [3]. Comparing with [3, (1.11)], our weighted L^{2} inequality contains a factor |X|_{R^{-1}} on the right hand side. Therefore, we are able to obtain some gain when the size of X is small.
Ingredient 3 (A structure lemma for Lipschitz functions).
Suppose 1<u<\kappa\leq 2. Let f:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,\infty) be a piecewise linear Lipschitz function satisfying: f is increasing, f(0)=0,f(1)=u, ux\leq f(x)\leq\kappa x, \textup{Lip}(f)\leq 2. Then there exists a sequence 0=x_{0}<x_{1}<\dots<x_{k}<x_{k+1}=1 such that f is 1-Frostman on [x_{i-1},x_{i}] and 1-x_{i-1}\leq 2(1-x_{i}) for 1\leq i\leq k and f(x_{k})=(2\kappa-1)x_{k}-\kappa+1. (Geometrically, (x_{k},f(x_{k})) lies on the line segment \overline{IF} in Figure 1.)
Remark 2.5.
We imposed a technical assumption: f is piecewise linear. Indeed, without loss of generality, one may always assume f is piecewise linear by perturbing the original f. The piecewise linear assumption is for the following use: In the proof of Ingredient 3, an algorithm is carried out to extract a sequence \{x_{i}\}. This assumption guarantees that the algorithm will stop in finite steps.
We are ready to give the proof of Claim 4.
Proof of Claim 4.
Assume first that u<\kappa. Using Ingredient 3, we obtain a sequence 0=x_{0}<x_{1}<\dots<x_{k}<x_{k+1}=1. By Ingredient 1 and Ingredient 2, we have
(11)
\frac{1}{|X|}\|F_{\textup{good}}\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\lessapprox\frac{|N_{r_{k}}(X)|}{|X|}(\frac{|X|_{R^{-1}}}{|X|_{r_{k}}})^{\frac{u}{u+1}}\frac{1}{r_{k}R}\|F_{\textup{good}}\|_{L^{2}(B(0,1))}^{2},
where r_{k}=R^{-x_{k}}.
It remains to check
(12)
\frac{|N_{r_{k}}(X)|}{|X|}(\frac{|X|_{R^{-1}}}{|X|_{r_{k}}})^{\frac{u}{u+1}}\frac{1}{r_{k}R}\lesssim R^{\max\{\frac{\kappa-u}{2\kappa-1},\frac{\kappa-u}{(u+1)(2\kappa-u-1)}\}}.
Note that |N_{r_{k}}(X)|\sim r_{k}^{2}|X|_{r_{k}}, |X|_{r_{k}}\sim R^{f(x_{k})}, |X|\sim R^{-2}|X|_{R^{-1}}\sim R^{-2}R^{u}. The left-hand side in the above inequality is
Rr_{k}(\frac{|X|_{r_{k}}}{|X|_{R^{-1}}})^{\frac{1}{u+1}}\sim R^{1-x_{k}+\frac{f(x_{k})-u}{u+1}}.
Using f(x_{k})=(2\kappa-1)x_{k}-\kappa+1, we see that the above quantity is
R^{1-x_{k}+\frac{(2\kappa-1)x_{k}-\kappa+1-u}{u+1}}.
It suffices to check
(13)
1-x_{k}+\frac{(2\kappa-1)x_{k}-\kappa+1-u}{u+1}\leq\max\{\frac{\kappa-u}{2\kappa-1},\frac{\kappa-u}{(u+1)(2\kappa-u-1)}\}.
This inequality is linear in x_{k}. Noting that ux_{k}\leq f(x_{k})\leq u, we obtain
\frac{\kappa-1}{2\kappa-u-1}\leq x_{k}\leq\frac{u+\kappa-1}{2\kappa-1}.
Plugging the two endpoints of x_{k} will finish the proof when u<\kappa.
If 1<u=\kappa<2, apply this argument with any \kappa^{\prime}\in(u,2]: the resulting exponent tends to 0 as \kappa^{\prime}\downarrow u, giving the claim in the \lessapprox sense. If u=\kappa=2, the claim follows directly from X\subset B(0,1) and |X|=1.
∎
2.4. Parameters
In the paper, we will use parameters \varepsilon,\eta,T,\delta to handle some technicalities. The relation between them is
(14)
\delta\ll T^{-1}\ll\eta\ll\varepsilon\ll 1.
A precise choice will be given later.
3. Uniform measures and branching functions
In this section, we show that for a given measure, we can decompose it as a sum of uniform measures plus a negligible term. We also define the branching function for a uniform measure.
For a number 0<\rho\leq 1, we use \mathcal{D}_{\rho}=\{Q_{\rho}\} to denote the set of dyadic \rho-cubes. In other words, each cube in \mathcal{D}_{\rho} is of form [n\rho,(n+1)\rho)\times[m\rho,(m+1)\rho) for some n,m\in\mathbb{Z}. Throughout the paper, we fix a large integer T, which will be determined later.
Definition 3.1.
Given a sequence w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{T})\in[0,1]^{T} and R\gg 1, we say a measure \nu is (w;T)-uniform at scale R if for any 1\leq i\leq T and any Q_{R^{-\frac{i}{T}}}\in\mathcal{D}_{R^{-\frac{i}{T}}} with Q_{R^{-\frac{i}{T}}}\cap\mathrm{supp}\nu\neq\emptyset, we have
\nu(Q_{R^{-\frac{i}{T}}})\in[w_{i},2w_{i}).
Lemma 3.2.
Let 0<\delta<1.
Let \nu be a probability measure supported in [0,1)^{2}. Let R\gg 1. Then there exist N\lesssim(\log R)^{2T} measures \nu^{1},\dots,\nu^{N+1} such that:
(1)
\sum_{i=1}^{N+1}\nu^{i}=\nu;
(2)
\nu^{i}([0,1)^{2})\geq R^{-\delta^{2}} for 1\leq i\leq N, and \nu^{N+1}([0,1)^{2})\leq R^{-\delta^{3}};
(3)
For 1\leq i\leq N, \nu^{i} is (w^{i};T)-uniform for some w^{i}\in[0,1]^{T}.
To prove Lemma 3.2, we use the following one-step refinement.
Lemma 3.3.
Let \nu be a measure supported in [0,1)^{2} and suppose 1\geq\nu([0,1)^{2})\geq R^{-\delta^{3}}. Let R\gg 1. Then there exists a measure \nu^{1} such that: \nu^{1}\leq\nu; \nu^{1} is (w^{1};T)-uniform for some w^{1}=(w^{1}_{1},\dots,w^{1}_{T}); and
\nu^{1}([0,1)^{2})\geq\frac{1}{(C\log R)^{T}}\nu([0,1)^{2}).
Here, C is a universal constant. If R is sufficiently large, then \nu^{1}([0,1)^{2})\geq\frac{1}{(C\log R)^{T}}R^{-\delta^{3}}\geq R^{-\delta^{2}}.
Proof of Lemma 3.3.
The proof follows from a standard pigeonhole argument. We first sort Q\in\mathcal{D}_{R^{-1}} according to \nu(Q). Let
\mathcal{D}_{R^{-1},w}:=\{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{R^{-1}}:\nu(Q)\in[w,2w)\},
\mathcal{D}_{R^{-1},\textup{tiny}}:=\{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{R^{-1}}:\nu(Q)<R^{-3}\}.
Then
\mathcal{D}_{R^{-1}}=\mathcal{D}_{R^{-1},\textup{tiny}}\sqcup\bigsqcup_{w\textup{dyadic},w\in[R^{-3},1]}\mathcal{D}_{R^{-1},w}.
Note that
\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{R^{-1},\textup{tiny}}}\nu(Q)\leq R^{-3}R^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\nu([0,1)^{2}).
By pigeonholing, there exists w_{1}\in[R^{-3},1], such that
\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{R^{-1},w_{1}}}\nu(Q)\gtrsim\frac{1}{\log R}\nu([0,1)^{2}).
We let \nu_{1}=\nu|_{X}, where X={\cup_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{R^{-1},w_{1}}}Q}. Then
\nu_{1}([0,1)^{2})\gtrsim\frac{1}{\log R}\nu([0,1)^{2}).
Iterating this argument T steps, we obtain w^{1}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{T}) and a w^{1}-uniform measure \nu^{1}, such that
\nu^{1}([0,1)^{2})\geq\frac{1}{(C\log R)^{T}}\nu([0,1)^{2}).
∎
Proof of Lemma 3.2.
Applying Lemma 3.3, we obtain a (w^{1};T)-uniform measure \nu^{1} with \nu^{1}([0,1)^{2})\geq\frac{1}{(C\log R)^{T}}\nu([0,1)^{2}). Equivalently,
(\nu-\nu^{1})([0,1)^{2})\leq(1-\frac{1}{(C\log R)^{T}})\nu([0,1)^{2}).
Applying Lemma 3.3 to \nu-\nu^{1}, we obtain a (w^{2};T)-uniform measure \nu^{2} satisfying
(\nu-\nu^{1}-\nu^{2})([0,1)^{2})\leq(1-\frac{1}{(C\log R)^{T}})^{2}\nu([0,1)^{2}).
Iterating this process N steps, we obtain \nu^{1},\dots,\nu^{N}. Here, N is chosen so that \nu^{N+1}:=\nu-\nu^{1}-\dots-\nu^{N} satisfies
\nu^{N+1}([0,1)^{2})\leq R^{-\delta^{2}}
for the first time.
Since \nu^{N+1}([0,1)^{2})\leq(1-\frac{1}{(C\log R)^{T}})^{N}\nu([0,1)^{2})=(1-\frac{1}{(C\log R)^{T}})^{N} and noting that (1-\frac{1}{(C\log R)^{T}})^{C_{1}(\log R)^{2T}}<R^{-\delta^{2}} for large C_{1}, we have
N\lesssim(\log R)^{2T}.
∎
Next, we define the branching function of a uniform measure. Recall that \nu is (w;T)-uniform at scale R if for each 0\leq i\leq T, \nu is supported in a union of dyadic R^{-\frac{i}{T}}-cubes, such that the mass of \nu in each such cube is \sim w_{i}. Recall that |X|_{\rho} is the \rho-covering number of X. We may allow the measure to have total mass <1.
Definition 3.4 (Branching function).
Let \nu be a measure that is (w;T)-uniform at scale R, and is supported in [0,1)^{2}. Define f:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,\infty) as follows.
For i\in\{0,1,\dots,T\}, define f(\frac{i}{T}) so that
|\mathrm{supp}\nu|_{R^{-\frac{i}{T}}}=R^{f(\frac{i}{T})}.
Then define f(x) by linearly interpolating the values \{f(\frac{i}{T})\}.
Remark 3.5.
To be more flexible, we may define f in the way that
|\mathrm{supp}\nu|_{R^{-\frac{i}{T}}}\sim R^{f(\frac{i}{T})}.
In this sense, the branching function is determined up to an error O(\frac{1}{\log R}), which is allowable for our use if R is big enough.
It is easy to see \nu([0,1)^{2})\sim R^{f(\frac{i}{T})}w_{i}\sim R^{f(\frac{i}{T})}\nu(Q_{R^{-\frac{i}{T}}}) for each i. For each x\in[0,1], since x is \frac{1}{T}-close to some \frac{i}{T} and f is 2-Lipschitz, we have
(15)
\frac{\nu(Q_{R^{-x}})}{\nu([0,1)^{2})}\in[R^{-\frac{2}{T}-f(x)},R^{\frac{2}{T}-f(x)}]
whenever Q_{R^{-x}}\cap\mathrm{supp}\nu\neq\emptyset. If T is large enough, then one may essentially view
\nu(Q_{R^{-x}})\sim\nu([0,1)^{2})R^{-f(x)}.
We recall the definition of Frostman/Katz-Tao conditions.
Definition 3.6.
Let f:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,\infty) be a Lipschitz function. For [x_{1},x_{2}]\subset[0,1] and s\in[0,2], we say f is s-Frostman on [x_{1},x_{2}] if for all x\in[x_{1},x_{2}],
f(x)\geq f(x_{1})+s(x-x_{1}).
We say f is s-Katz-Tao on [x_{1},x_{2}] if for all x\in[x_{1},x_{2}],
f(x)\geq f(x_{2})+s(x-x_{2}).
For 0<\gamma\ll s,
we say f is s-Frostman with error \gamma on [x_{1},x_{2}] if for all x\in[x_{1},x_{2}],
f(x)\geq f(x_{1})+s(x-x_{1})-\gamma.
We say f is s-Katz-Tao with error \gamma on [x_{1},x_{2}] if for all x\in[x_{1},x_{2}],
f(x)\geq f(x_{2})+s(x-x_{2})-\gamma.
If f is s-Frostman (resp. s-Katz-Tao) with error \gamma on [0,1], we simply say f is s-Frostman (resp. s-Katz-Tao) with error \gamma.
For a measure \nu that is (w;T)-uniform at scale R, we say \nu is s-Frostman (resp. s-Katz-Tao) if its branching function is s-Frostman (resp. s-Katz-Tao).
The next lemma tells us that we can partition an s-Frostman measure into several measures that are both s-Frostman and s-Katz-Tao, up to small errors.
Lemma 3.7.
Suppose 0<\gamma\ll s.
Let \nu be a measure that is (w;T)-uniform at scale R and is s-Frostman with error \gamma. Then there are M\sim\frac{|\mathrm{supp}\nu|_{R^{-1}}}{R^{s-C\gamma}} measures \nu^{(1)},\dots,\nu^{(M)} such that each \nu^{(j)} is s-Frostman up to an exponent error C\gamma+O(1/\log R) with uniform comparability constant, and satisfies |\mathrm{supp}\nu^{(j)}|_{R^{-1}}\sim R^{s-C\gamma}. Also, \nu=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\nu^{(j)}. Moreover, in the comparability convention of the preceding remark, all \nu^{(j)} admit a common branching profile \widetilde{f} with \widetilde{f}(\frac{i}{T})=\min\{f(\frac{i}{T}),s-C\gamma\} and |\mathrm{supp}\nu^{(j)}|_{R^{-i/T}}\sim R^{\widetilde{f}(i/T)}. Here, C is a universal constant.
Proof.
Let X be the union of cubes in \mathcal{D}_{R^{-1}} that intersect \mathrm{supp}\nu. Let f be the branching function of \nu. Then
|X|_{R^{-x_{i}}}\sim R^{f(x_{i})}
for 0\leq i\leq T. Here we let x_{i}=\frac{i}{T}. That f is s-Frostman with error \gamma means f(x)\geq sx-\gamma. We need to modify f so that its value at x=1 is s-C\gamma.
More precisely, our goal is to find a partition X=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{M}X^{(j)} such that for each j,
|X^{(j)}|_{R^{-x_{i}}}\sim R^{\widetilde{f}(x_{i})}
for 0\leq i\leq T. Then we just set \nu^{(j)}=\nu|_{X^{(j)}} and finish the proof.
Next, we construct these X^{(j)}. First, let i be the minimal index such that |X|_{R^{-x_{i}}}>R^{s-C\gamma}. For each Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{R^{-x_{i-1}}} such that Q^{\prime}\cap X\neq\emptyset, we equally divide X\cap Q^{\prime} into A\sim R^{f(x_{i})-s+C\gamma} pieces
X\cap Q^{\prime}=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{A}X_{i}(Q^{\prime}),
each of which is a union of about \frac{|Q^{\prime}\cap X|_{R^{-x_{i}}}}{R^{f(x_{i})-s+C\gamma}} dyadic R^{-x_{i}}-cubes.
This is allowable since
\frac{|Q^{\prime}\cap X|_{R^{-x_{i}}}}{R^{f(x_{i})-s+C\gamma}}\sim\frac{|X|_{R^{-x_{i}}}/|X|_{R^{-x_{i-1}}}}{R^{f(x_{i})-s+C\gamma}}\sim\frac{R^{s-C\gamma}}{|X|_{R^{-x_{i-1}}}}\geq 1.
The last inequality is because of the minimality of i.
Now let X_{i}=\bigcup_{Q^{\prime}}X_{i}(Q^{\prime}). One can check the branching function of each X_{i} is
g(x)=\begin{cases}f(x)&x\leq x_{i-1}\\
f(x)-f(x_{i})+s-C\gamma&x\geq x_{i},\end{cases}
and the value of g in (x_{i-1},x_{i}) is by linear interpolation at the two endpoints.
Then we repeat the process for each X_{i} whose branching function is g. After finitely many steps, we obtain \{X^{(j)}\} with the common branching profile \widetilde{f} in the above comparability sense.
∎
4. A structure lemma for Lipschitz functions
In this section, we prove a structure lemma for Lipschitz functions.
For convenience, we make the following definitions.
Definition 4.1 (Admissible interval).
For [a,b]\subset[0,1], we say [a,b] is admissible if 1-a\leq 2(1-b).
Definition 4.2.
Let f:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}. If there exists 0=a_{0}<a_{1}<\dots<a_{M}=1 such that f|_{[}a_{i-1},a_{i}] is linear for all 1\leq i\leq M, then we say f is piecewise linear with M pieces. Each [a_{i-1},a_{i}] is called an f-piece.
The main result is as follows.
Lemma 4.3.
Let 1<u\leq\kappa\leq 2. Then there exists \varepsilon(u,\kappa)>0 such that for any 0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon(u,\kappa), the following holds. Let f:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,\infty) be a piecewise linear function, satisfying: f is increasing, f(0)=0, f(1)=u, ux\leq f(x)\leq\kappa x, \textup{Lip}(f)\leq 2.
Then there exists a sequence 0=x_{0}<x_{1}<\dots<x_{k}<x_{k+1}=1 such that
(1)
For 1\leq i\leq{k}, f is (1+\varepsilon)-Frostman on [x_{i-1},x_{i}] and [x_{i-1},x_{i}] is admissible.
(2)
f(x_{k})=(2\kappa-1)x_{k}-\kappa+1+O(\varepsilon).
(3)
At most C(\varepsilon,u,\kappa) of the selected endpoints x_{0},\dots,x_{k} are not endpoints of the given f-pieces. In particular, k\leq\#\{f\textup{-pieces}\}+C(\varepsilon,u,\kappa) for some C(\varepsilon,u,\kappa).
Figure 1.
Proof.
We first draw a graph as in Figure 1. The coordinates of marked points are:
(1)
O=(0,0);
(2)
P=(1,0);
(3)
E=(\frac{u}{\kappa},u);
(4)
F^{\prime}=(\frac{u+\kappa-1}{2\kappa-1},u);
(5)
G=(1,u);
(6)
I^{\prime}=(\frac{\kappa-1}{2\kappa-u-1},\frac{u(\kappa-1)}{2\kappa-u-1});
One can also check the following property. Given any point Y on the line segment \overline{F^{\prime}I^{\prime}} (here \overline{AB} means the line segment with endpoints A,B), draw a line through Y with slope 1. Suppose this line intersects lines \overline{OE} and \overline{GP} at Y_{1} and Y_{2}, then Y is the midpoint of \overline{Y_{1}Y_{2}}. Indeed, one only needs to check for Y=F^{\prime},I^{\prime}. One can also check that the line through F^{\prime},I^{\prime} is
y=(2\kappa-1)x-\kappa+1.
Take 0<\varepsilon<(u-1)/4 and put s=1+\varepsilon,
h(x):=f(x)-sx.
Let q be the first intersection of the graph of f with the line
L(x):=(2\kappa-s)x-\kappa+s+\varepsilon.
Since L(0)<f(0) and L(1)>f(1), we have 0<q<1. Moreover,
(16)
f(q)=(2\kappa-1)q-\kappa+1+\varepsilon(2-q)
and
1-q\geq\frac{\kappa-u+\varepsilon}{2\kappa-s}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.
Starting with b=q, put a_{0}=\max\{0,2b-1\} and let a be the leftmost minimizer of h on [a_{0},b].
•
If a_{0}=0, then h(b)\geq(u-s)b>h(0);
•
If a_{0}>0, the upper bound f(x)\leq\kappa x and the choice of q give
h(a_{0})\leq(\kappa-s)a_{0}<h(b).
In particular, a\neq b, thus a<b. Then the minimum property on the choice of a shows that f is s-Frostman on [a,b], and a\geq a_{0}\geq 2b-1 makes this interval admissible. Repeat with b=a until reaching 0, then list the endpoints in increasing order, with x_{k}=q.
Since h is piecewise linear, each new left endpoint is either an endpoint of a given f-piece or a=a_{0}=2b-1. In the latter case the interval has length 1-b\geq 1-q\geq\varepsilon/3. These intervals have disjoint interiors, so there are at most 3/\varepsilon+1. This proves finite termination and (3).
The displayed identity (16) for f(q) proves (2), that completes the proof.
∎
As a remark, the preceding construction only uses the upper bound f(x)\leq\kappa x at a_{0}. Choose \varepsilon_{0}=(u-1)/(4(3-u)). If 0<a_{0}<\varepsilon_{0}, then b=(1+a_{0})/2, and the Lipschitz and lower bounds give instead
h(a_{0})\leq(2-s)a_{0}<\frac{u-s}{2}(1+a_{0})\leq h(b).
Thus the same construction gives the following stronger version.
Lemma 4.4.
Let 1<u\leq\kappa\leq 2. Then there exists \varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(u,\kappa)>0 and \varepsilon(u,\kappa)>0 such that for any 0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon(u,\kappa), the following holds. Let f:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,\infty) be a piecewise linear function, satisfying: f is increasing, f(0)=0, f(1)=u, f(x)\geq ux for x\in[0,1], f(x)\leq\kappa x for x\in[\varepsilon_{0},1], \textup{Lip}(f)\leq 2. Then there exists a sequence 0=x_{0}<x_{1}<\dots<x_{k}<x_{k+1}=1 such that
(1)
For 1\leq i\leq{k}, f is (1+\varepsilon)-Frostman on [x_{i-1},x_{i}] and [x_{i-1},x_{i}] is admissible.
(2)
f(x_{k})=(2\kappa-1)x_{k}-\kappa+1+O(\varepsilon).
(3)
At most C(\varepsilon,u,\kappa) of the selected endpoints x_{0},\dots,x_{k} are not endpoints of the given f-pieces. In particular, k\leq\#\{f\textup{-pieces}\}+C(\varepsilon,u,\kappa) for some C(\varepsilon,u,\kappa).
We need a more quantitative version of Lemma 4.4, which takes care of the number of linear pieces for a Lipschitz function. We give the following definition which is the same as [19, Definition 4.2].
Definition 4.5.
We say f is linear with error \varepsilon on [x_{1},x_{2}] if for all x\in[x_{1},x_{2}],
|f(x)-f(x_{1})-s_{f}(x_{1},x_{2})(x-x_{1})|\leq\varepsilon(x_{2}-x_{1}).
Here, s_{f}(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{f(x_{2})-f(x_{1})}{x_{2}-x_{1}}.
We recall [19, Lemma 4.4].
Lemma 4.6.
For every \eta>0, there is \tau=\tau(\eta)>0 such that the following holds: for any 2-Lipschitz function f:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}, there exists a family of non-overlapping intervals \{[c_{j},d_{j}]\} such that:
(1)
f is linear on [c_{j},d_{j}] with error \eta.
(2)
d_{j}-c_{j}\geq\tau.
(3)
|[0,1]\setminus\bigcup_{j}[c_{j},d_{j}]|\leq\eta.
Now, we state the structure lemma.
Lemma 4.7.
Suppose 1<u\leq\kappa\leq 2. Then there exists \varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(u,\kappa)>0, \varepsilon(u,\kappa)>0. For any 0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon(u,\kappa), there exists \eta(\varepsilon,u,\kappa)>0. For any 0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon(u,\kappa) and 0<\eta<\eta(\varepsilon,u,\kappa), there exists \tau=\tau(\eta)>0 such that the following holds. Let f:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,\infty) be a Lipschitz function, satisfying: f is increasing, f(0)=0, f(1)=u, f(x)\geq ux for x\in[0,1], f(x)\leq\kappa x for x\in[\varepsilon_{0},1], \textup{Lip}(f)\leq 2. Then there exist k\leq 3\tau^{-1}+C(\varepsilon,u,\kappa) and k disjoint intervals \{[a_{j},b_{j}]\}_{j=1}^{k} such that
(1)
For 1\leq j\leq{k}, [a_{j},b_{j}] is admissible and
f(x)\geq f(a_{j})+(1+\varepsilon)(x-a_{j})-\eta(b_{j}-a_{j})\ \ \ \ x\in[a_{j},b_{j}].
(2)
|[0,b_{k}]\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}[a_{j},b_{j}]|\leq\eta.
(3)
f(b_{k})=(2\kappa-1)b_{k}-\kappa+1+O(\varepsilon).
Moreover, the intervals may be chosen with b_{j}-a_{j}\geq\ell_{0}(\varepsilon,\eta,u,\kappa)>0.
Proof.
First, we apply Lemma 4.6 to f and with \eta replaced by \eta^{4}, to obtain intervals \{[c_{l},d_{l}]\}_{l=1}^{L} so that the three properties in Lemma 4.6 hold:
(1)
f is linear on [c_{l},d_{l}] with error \eta^{2}. (If it is with error \eta^{4}, then it is with error \eta^{2}.)
(2)
d_{l}-c_{l}\geq\tau=\tau(\eta).
(3)
|[0,1]\setminus\bigcup_{l}[c_{l},d_{l}]|\leq\eta^{4}.
We define a new function g:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R} that is the piecewise-linear interpolation of the data (0,f(0)),(c_{l},f(c_{l})),(d_{l},f(d_{l}))(1\leq l\leq L),(1,f(1)), with (\varepsilon_{0},f(\varepsilon_{0})) also included. Thus g has at most 2\tau^{-1}+2 linear pieces and satisfies the upper bound on [\varepsilon_{0},1]. Also, since g is defined by linear interpolation, the original \eta^{4} approximation implies
|f(x)-g(x)|\leq\eta^{2}(d_{l}-c_{l})
when x\in[c_{l},d_{l}]. Also, \|f-g\|_{\infty}\leq 2\eta^{4} because the total length of the gaps is at most \eta^{4}.
Applying Lemma 4.4 to g, we obtain a sequence 0=x_{0}<x_{1}<\dots<x_{m}<x_{m+1}=1 so that
(1)
For 1\leq i\leq m, [x_{i-1},x_{i}] is admissible and g is (1+\varepsilon)-Frostman on [x_{i-1},x_{i}].
(2)
g(x_{m})=(2\kappa-1)x_{m}-\kappa+1+O(\varepsilon).
(3)
At most C(\varepsilon,u,\kappa) endpoints are not among the interpolation nodes.
Let \mathbb{I}_{1} be the collection of [x_{i-1},x_{i}] such that both x_{i-1} and x_{i} belong to \{0,1,c_{l},d_{l}:1\leq l\leq L\}; let \mathbb{I}_{2}:=\{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]\}_{i=1}^{m}\setminus\mathbb{I}_{1}. Then \#\mathbb{I}_{2}\leq M for some M=M(\varepsilon,u,\kappa)\geq 1, independent of f and \eta.
Now, we are ready to define \{[a_{j},b_{j}]\}_{j=1}^{k}. Denote I_{b}:=[0,1]\setminus\bigcup_{l}[c_{l},d_{l}]. Then
|I_{b}|\leq\eta^{4}.
Define \mathbb{I}^{\prime}_{1} to be those [x_{i-1},x_{i}]\in\mathbb{I}_{1} such that
|I_{b}\cap[x_{i-1},x_{i}]|\leq\eta^{2}(x_{i}-x_{i-1}).
Define \mathbb{I}^{\prime}_{2} to be those [x_{i-1},x_{i}]\in\mathbb{I}_{2} such that x_{i}-x_{i-1}\geq\eta/(4M). Finally, we define \{[a_{j},b_{j}]\}_{j=1}^{k} to be \mathbb{I}_{1}^{\prime}\bigcup\mathbb{I}_{2}^{\prime}.
We verify (1) in the lemma. For convenience, we set d_{-1}=0. By definition, each [a_{j},b_{j}] is contained in some [x_{i-1},x_{i}], so [a_{j},b_{j}] is admissible. We need to verify for any x\in[a_{j},b_{j}] that
f(x)\geq f(a_{j})+(1+\varepsilon)(x-a_{j})-\eta(b_{j}-a_{j}).
Consider the case [a_{j},b_{j}]\in\mathbb{I}^{\prime}_{1}. Then [a_{j},b_{j}] is the union of intervals [c_{l},d_{l}] and [d_{l-1},c_{l}]. For example, if a_{j}=c_{s},b_{j}=d_{t} for some s\leq t, then
[a_{j},b_{j}]=[c_{s},d_{s}]\bigcup[d_{s},c_{s+1}]\bigcup\dots\bigcup[c_{t},d_{t}].
If x\in[c_{r},d_{r}]\subset[a_{j},b_{j}], then by the assumption that f is linear on [c_{r},d_{r}] with error \eta^{2}, we have
f(x)\geq g(x)-\eta^{2}(d_{r}-c_{r}).
By the assumption that g is (1+\varepsilon)-Frostman on [a_{j},b_{j}], we have
g(x)\geq g(a_{j})+(1+\varepsilon)(x-a_{j}).
Combining the two estimates and noting g(a_{j})=f(a_{j}), we obtain
f(x)\geq f(a_{j})+(1+\varepsilon)(x-a_{j})-\eta^{2}(b_{j}-a_{j}).
If x\in[d_{r-1},c_{r}]\subset[a_{j},b_{j}], then by the previous discussion (noting d_{r-1}\in[c_{r-1},d_{r-1}]), we have
f(d_{r-1})=g(d_{r-1})\geq f(a_{j})+(1+\varepsilon)(d_{r-1}-a_{j}).
Since [a_{j},b_{j}]\in\mathbb{I}^{\prime}_{1}, we have (c_{r}-d_{r-1})\leq\eta^{2}(b_{j}-a_{j}). Therefore using that f is increasing and 1+\varepsilon\leq 2, we have
f(x)\geq f(d_{r-1})\geq f(a_{j})+(1+\varepsilon)(x-a_{j})-2\eta^{2}(b_{j}-a_{j}).
Assuming \eta<1/2, we finish this case.
Now, consider the case [a_{j},b_{j}]\in\mathbb{I}^{\prime}_{2}. Since b_{j}-a_{j}\geq\eta/(4M) and \|f-g\|_{\infty}\leq 2\eta^{4}, we have, for x\in[a_{j},b_{j}],
(17)
\displaystyle f(x)
\displaystyle\geq f(a_{j})+(1+\varepsilon)(x-a_{j})-4\eta^{4}
\displaystyle\geq f(a_{j})+(1+\varepsilon)(x-a_{j})-\eta(b_{j}-a_{j}),
provided 16M\eta^{2}\leq 1.
Next, we verify (2) of the lemma. Note that b_{k}\leq x_{m}. We will actually prove a stronger estimate:
(18)
|[0,x_{m}]\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}[a_{j},b_{j}]|\leq\eta.
Recall \{[a_{j},b_{j}]\}_{j=1}^{k}=\mathbb{I}_{1}^{\prime}\bigcup\mathbb{I}^{\prime}_{2} and [0,x_{m}]=\bigcup_{I\in\mathbb{I}_{1}\cup\mathbb{I}_{2}}I. We just need to verify
\sum_{I\in\mathbb{I}_{1}\setminus\mathbb{I}^{\prime}_{1}}|I|+\sum_{I\in\mathbb{I}_{2}\setminus\mathbb{I}^{\prime}_{2}}|I|\leq\eta.
If I\in\mathbb{I}_{1}\setminus\mathbb{I}^{\prime}_{1}, then |I_{b}\cap I|>\eta^{2}|I|. Hence, we get
\sum_{I\in\mathbb{I}_{1}\setminus\mathbb{I}^{\prime}_{1}}|I|\leq\eta^{-2}|I_{b}|\leq\eta^{2}.
By definition of \mathbb{I}^{\prime}_{2}, we have
\sum_{I\in\mathbb{I}_{2}\setminus\mathbb{I}^{\prime}_{2}}|I|\leq M\frac{\eta}{4M}=\frac{\eta}{4}.
Combining the two estimates, we finish the proof of (2) in the lemma. Every retained interval in \mathbb{I}_{1} contains a complete approximation interval, so all retained intervals have length at least \ell_{0}=\min\{\tau(\eta),\eta/(4M)\}>0.
Finally, we show (3) in the lemma. By (18), we have |x_{m}-b_{k}|\leq\eta. Since
g(x_{m})=(2\kappa-1)x_{m}-\kappa+1+O(\varepsilon),
we obtain
g(b_{k})=(2\kappa-1)b_{k}-\kappa+1+O(\varepsilon+\eta).
The uniform approximation gives
|f(b_{k})-g(b_{k})|\leq 2\eta^{4}.
Letting \eta<\eta(\varepsilon,u,\kappa)\leq\varepsilon, we obtain
f(b_{k})=(2\kappa-1)b_{k}-\kappa+1+O(\varepsilon).
∎
For some technicalities, we need the following version, in which the condition f(x)\geq ux is modified to f(x)\geq ux-\delta_{0}, and the condition f(1)=u is modified to f(1)\in[u-\delta_{0},u]. If \delta_{0} is much smaller than other parameters, one can expect that the structure lemma still holds.
Lemma 4.8 (Structure lemma).
Suppose 1<u\leq\kappa\leq 2. Then there exists \varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(u,\kappa)>0 and \varepsilon(u,\kappa)>0. For any 0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon(u,\kappa), there exists \eta(\varepsilon,u,\kappa)>0. For any 0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon(u,\kappa) and 0<\eta<\eta(\varepsilon,u,\kappa), there exists \tau=\tau(\eta)>0 and \delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(\eta,\varepsilon,u,\kappa)>0 such that the following holds. Let f:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,\infty) be a Lipschitz function, satisfying: f is increasing, f(0)=0, f(1)\in[u-\delta_{0},u], f(x)\geq ux-\delta_{0} for x\in[0,1], f(x)\leq\kappa x for x\in[\varepsilon_{0},1], \textup{Lip}(f)\leq 2. Then there exist k\leq 3\tau^{-1}+C(\varepsilon,u,\kappa) and k disjoint intervals \{[a_{j},b_{j}]\}_{j=1}^{k} such that
(1)
For 1\leq j\leq{k}, [a_{j},b_{j}] is admissible and
(19)
f(x)\geq f(a_{j})+(1+\varepsilon)(x-a_{j})-\eta(b_{j}-a_{j})\ \ \ \ x\in[a_{j},b_{j}].
(2)
|[0,b_{k}]\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}[a_{j},b_{j}]|\leq\eta.
(3)
f(b_{k})=(2\kappa-1)b_{k}-\kappa+1+O(\varepsilon).
Proof.
We briefly explain how to prove it. Apply Lemma 4.7 with f replaced by \widetilde{f}(x)=\max\{f(x),ux\}, for which 0\leq\widetilde{f}-f\leq\delta_{0} and \eta replaced by \frac{1}{2}\eta. Instead of (19), we have
f(x)+\delta_{0}\geq f(a_{j})+(1+\varepsilon)(x-a_{j})-\frac{1}{2}\eta(b_{j}-a_{j}).
By Lemma 4.7, b_{j}-a_{j}\geq\ell_{0}(\varepsilon,\eta/2,u,\kappa). Taking \delta_{0}\leq\min\{\varepsilon,\frac{1}{2}\eta\ell_{0}(\varepsilon,\eta/2,u,\kappa)\} absorbs the error and preserves the stopping relation.
∎
5. A standard reduction
We first make a standard reduction to Theorem 1.1. Recall that \alpha=\dim_{\textup{H}}(E),\beta=\dim_{\textup{P}}(E). Without loss of generality, we assume E\subset[0,1)^{2} and there exist E_{1},E_{2}\subset E so that \textup{dist}(E_{1},E_{2})\gtrsim 1 and \dim_{\textup{H}}(E_{1})=\dim_{\textup{H}}(E_{2})=\alpha. We may decrease \alpha slightly, so that (1) and (2) still hold. By Frostman’s lemma, E_{1} (resp. E_{2}) admits a probability measure \mu (resp. \nu) supported on them satisfying
(20)
\mu(B(x,r))\lesssim r^{\alpha}\ (\textup{resp.~}\nu(B(x,r))\lesssim r^{\alpha}).
By a classical result in geometric measure theory, packing dimension equals the modified upper box dimension.
Since \overline{\dim}_{\textup{MB}}(E)=\dim_{\textup{P}}(E_{2})\leq\beta, for any \gamma>0, there exists a covering E_{2}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}E_{2}^{i} with
\overline{\dim_{\textup{B}}}(E_{2}^{i})\leq\beta+\gamma.
Here, \overline{\dim_{\textup{B}}} is the upper box dimension. Recall the definition
\overline{\dim_{\textup{B}}}(E):=\limsup_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log|E|_{\delta}}{-\log\delta},
\overline{\dim}_{\textup{MB}}(E)=\inf_{E\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}E_{j}}\sup_{j}\overline{\dim_{\textup{B}}}(E_{j}).
By pigeonholing, there exists i_{0} such that \nu(E_{2}^{i_{0}})\gtrsim\frac{1}{i_{0}^{2}}. Now, we replace E_{2} with E_{2}^{i_{0}}, and replace \nu with \frac{1}{\nu(E_{2}^{i_{0}})}\nu|_{E_{2}^{i_{0}}}. Since i_{0} is now fixed, we allow all implicit constants below depending on i_{0}. Then we still have the \alpha-Frostman condition \nu(B(x,r))\lesssim r^{\alpha}.
Since \overline{\dim_{\textup{B}}}(E_{2})\leq\beta+\gamma, for any \gamma>0, there exists R_{\circ}\gg 1 such that for any R\geq R_{\circ}, we have
|E_{2}|_{R^{-1}}\leq R^{\beta+2\gamma}.
We may replace \beta with \beta+2\gamma and let \gamma be small enough so that (1) and (2) still hold.
From our reduction, we see that in order to prove Theorem 1.1, we just need to show the following.
Proposition 5.1.
Suppose 1<\alpha\leq\beta\leq 2 satisfy
(1) and (2).
Let E_{1},E_{2}\subset[0,1)^{2} with \textup{dist}(E_{1},E_{2})\gtrsim 1. Assume E_{1} (resp. E_{2}) admits a probability measure \mu (resp. \nu) supported on them satisfying
(21)
\mu(B(x,r))\lesssim r^{\alpha}\ (\textup{resp.~}\nu(B(x,r))\lesssim r^{\alpha})
for all x\in[0,1)^{2} and r>0.
Also assume there exists R_{\circ}>1 such that
(22)
|E_{2}|_{R^{-1}}\leq R^{\beta}
for all R\geq R_{\circ}.
Then there exists x\in E_{2} such that |\Delta_{x}(E_{1})|>0.
Next, we follow the argument in [8, Section 2] to perform a wave packet decomposition.
We choose R_{0} that is much larger than R_{\circ} in Proposition 5.1 (to be determined later). Indeed, we will choose
(23)
R_{0}\geq R_{\circ}^{CT},
where T is the parameter we introduced before, C is a large constant.
Let R_{\iota}=2^{\iota}R_{0} (\iota=1,2,\dots). For each \iota\geq 1, cover the unit circle by R_{\iota}^{-1/2}-caps \theta. For each such \theta, find a rectangular block B_{\theta} with dimensions \sim R_{\iota}^{1/2}\times R_{\iota}, with the long direction of each \tau radial. \{B_{\theta}\} is chosen so that they cover the annulus R_{\iota-1}\leq|\omega|\leq R_{\iota} (in the frequency space). We choose a partition of unity subordinate to this cover, so that
(24)
1=\psi_{0}+\sum_{\iota\geq 1}\sum_{\theta:R^{-1/2}_{\iota}\textup{-cap}}\psi_{\iota,\theta}.
Let \delta>0 be a small number to handle the rapidly decaying tail.
For each (\iota,\theta), cover [-1,2]^{2} (in the physical space) by tubes T of dimensions \sim R_{\iota}^{-1/2+\delta}\times 1 with long axis parallel to the long axis of B_{\theta}. Let \mathbb{T}_{\iota,\theta} be the collection of all these tubes, and let \eta_{T} be a partition of unity subordinate to this covering, so that for each choice of \iota and \theta, \sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota,\theta}}\eta_{T}=1 on B(0,10). Denote \mathbb{T}_{\iota}=\bigcup_{\theta}\mathbb{T}_{\iota,\theta}.
Define M_{0}f:=(\psi_{0}\widehat{f})^{\vee}.
For each T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota,\tau}, define
(25)
M_{T}f:=\eta_{T}(\psi_{\iota,\theta}\widehat{f})^{\vee}.
Morally speaking, the physical support of M_{T}f is T, while the Fourier support of M_{T}f is B_{\theta}.
The rest of the paper is devoted to the proof of Proposition 5.1.
Let \nu be the measure as in Proposition 5.1. For \iota\in\{1,2,\dots\}, apply Lemma 3.2 to \nu and scale R_{\iota}. Then there exist N_{\iota}\lesssim(\log R_{\iota})^{2T} measures \nu^{1}_{\iota},\dots,\nu^{N+1}_{\iota} such that:
(1)
\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\iota}+1}\nu_{\iota}^{i}=\nu;
(2)
\nu_{\iota}^{i}([0,1)^{2})\geq R_{\iota}^{-\delta^{2}} for 1\leq i\leq N_{\iota}, and \nu_{\iota}^{N_{\iota}+1}([0,1)^{2})\leq R_{\iota}^{-\delta^{3}};
(3)
For 1\leq i\leq N_{\iota}, \nu^{i}_{\iota} is (w_{\iota}^{i};T)-uniform for some w_{\iota}^{i}\in[0,1]^{T}.
Let us just fix a \nu^{i}_{\iota} (i\leq N_{\iota}) and denote its branching function by f_{\iota}^{i}. We claim that f_{\iota}^{i} is \big(\alpha-O(\frac{1}{T}+\delta^{2})\big)-Frostman. By (15), we have
R_{\iota}^{-\frac{2}{T}-f_{\iota}^{i}(x)}\leq\frac{\nu_{\iota}^{i}(Q_{R_{\iota}^{-x}})}{\nu_{\iota}^{i}([0,1)^{2})}\leq\nu(Q_{R_{\iota}^{-x}})R_{\iota}^{\delta^{2}}\lesssim R_{\iota}^{-x\alpha}R_{\iota}^{\delta^{2}},
where the last inequality is by (21). We see that
f_{\iota}^{i}(x)\geq\alpha x-O(\frac{1}{T}+\delta^{2})\geq\alpha x-O(T^{-1}),
where the last equality is by (14).
In other words, f_{\iota}^{i} is \alpha-Frostman with error O(T^{-1}).
We apply Lemma 3.7 with parameters \gamma=O(T^{-1}), s=\alpha and \nu=\nu_{\iota}^{i}, to obtain a decomposition
\nu_{\iota}^{i}=\sum_{j}\nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)},
where each \nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)} has the branching function f_{\iota}^{i,(j)} that is \alpha-Frostman with error O(T^{-1}), and
(26)
f_{\iota}^{i,(j)}(1)=\alpha-C_{1}T^{-1}.
That f_{\iota}^{i,(j)} is \alpha-Frostman with error O(T^{-1}) means
(27)
f_{\iota}^{i,(j)}(x)\geq\alpha x-O(T^{-1}).
Since \mathrm{supp}\nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)}\subset\mathrm{supp}\nu\subset E_{2}, by (22), we have
|\mathrm{supp}\nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)}|_{R_{\iota}^{-x}}\leq R_{\iota}^{\beta x},
provided R_{\iota}^{x}\geq R_{0}^{x}\geq R_{\circ}.
We choose R_{0} such that R_{0}\geq R_{\circ}^{CT} (recall (23)), so we have
f_{\iota}^{i,(j)}(x)\leq\beta x
for x\in[\frac{1}{T},1].
Let us fix a triple (\iota,i,j) and hence the measure \nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)}. For simplicity, we write \nu^{*}=\nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)} and f^{*}=f_{\iota}^{i,(j)}. When we deal with a fixed triple (\iota,i,j), we use this abbreviation; when we need to distinguish between them, we restore the indices (\iota,i,j). This will not cause any confusion.
We apply Lemma 4.8 with parameters u=\alpha,\kappa=\beta,f=f^{*}. To verify the assumption for f^{*}, we just need to properly choose parameters T^{-1}\ll\eta\ll\varepsilon\ll 1 (see (14)). Now we think of \alpha,\beta as fixed constants. As a result of Lemma 4.8, we obtain k\leq C(\eta) and k disjoint intervals \{[a_{j},b_{j}]\}_{j=1}^{k} such that
(1)
For 1\leq j\leq{k}, [a_{j},b_{j}] is admissible and
(28)
f^{*}(x)\geq f^{*}(a_{j})+(1+\varepsilon)(x-a_{j})-\eta(b_{j}-a_{j})\ \ \ \ x\in[a_{j},b_{j}].
(2)
|[0,b_{k}]\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}[a_{j},b_{j}]|\leq\eta.
(3)
f^{*}(b_{k})=(2\beta-1)b_{k}-\beta+1+O(\varepsilon).
6. Multiscale good-bad decomposition
We follow the multiscale good-bad decomposition from [12, Section 3.2].
Let
(29)
\rho_{j}:=R_{\iota}^{-a_{j}},r_{j}=R_{\iota}^{-b_{j}}
for 1\leq j\leq k.
Then we have
1\geq\rho_{1}>r_{1}\geq\rho_{2}>r_{2}\dots\geq r_{k}>R_{\iota}^{-1}.
Let us reinterpret the properties for \{a_{j},b_{j}\} in terms of \{\rho_{j},r_{j}\}.
Since each [a_{j},b_{j}] is admissible, we obtain the key property
(30)
R_{\iota}\rho_{j}\leq(R_{\iota}r_{j})^{2}.
By (26), (27) and (15), for R_{\iota}^{-1}\leq r\leq 1 and each Q_{r}\in\mathcal{D}_{r} with Q_{r}\cap\mathrm{supp}\nu^{*}\neq\emptyset, we have the following \alpha-Katz-Tao condition:
(31)
|\mathrm{supp}\nu^{*}\cap Q_{r}|_{R_{\iota}^{-1}}\sim\frac{\nu^{*}(Q_{r})}{\nu^{*}(Q_{R_{\iota}^{-1}})}\lesssim R_{\iota}^{O(T^{-1})}R_{\iota}^{f^{*}(1)-f^{*}(-\log_{R_{\iota}}r)}\lesssim R_{\iota}^{O(T^{-1})}(R_{\iota}r)^{\alpha}.
From (28), we have the following (1+\varepsilon)-Frostman condition for 1\leq j\leq k:
(32)
\nu^{*}(Q_{r})\lesssim(\frac{\rho_{j}}{r_{j}})^{\eta}(\frac{r}{\rho_{j}})^{1+\varepsilon}\nu^{*}(Q_{\rho_{j}}),
for all r\in[r_{j},\rho_{j}] and Q_{r}\subset Q_{\rho_{j}}.
The other two properties are
(33)
\frac{1}{\rho_{1}}\frac{r_{1}}{\rho_{2}}\frac{r_{2}}{\rho_{3}}\dots\frac{r_{k-1}}{\rho_{k}}\leq R_{\iota}^{\eta},
and
(34)
|\mathrm{supp}\nu^{*}|_{r_{k}}\lesssim r_{k}^{1-2\beta}R_{\iota}^{-\beta+1+O(\varepsilon)}.
Now we are ready to perform the multiscale good-bad decomposition.
For each 1\leq j\leq k, we use \{\theta_{j}\} to denote a collection of (r_{j}/\rho_{j})-caps that form a partition of S^{1}.
For each cube Q with \nu^{*}(Q)>0, define the normalized restriction
\nu_{Q}^{*}:=\frac{1}{\nu^{*}(Q)}\,\nu^{*}|_{Q}.
This is the conditional measure associated with the already fixed measure \nu^{*}.
Definition 6.1 (Bad caps).
Let j\in\{1,\dots,k\}. For each (r_{j}/\rho_{j})-cap \theta_{j}, let \mathbb{T}^{\prime}_{j,\theta_{j}} be a family of grid r_{j}\times\rho_{j}-tubes covering \mathbb{R}^{2} in direction \theta_{j}. For each dyadic r_{j}-cube Q_{j}, we say \theta_{j} is bad for Q_{j} if there exists T\in\mathbb{T}^{\prime}_{j,\theta_{j}} with 10T\cap Q_{j}\neq\emptyset such that
(35)
\nu^{*}_{\widetilde{Q}_{j}}(T)>R_{\iota}^{T^{-1/3}}(\rho_{j}/r_{j})^{10\eta}\cdot(r_{j}/\rho_{j}),
where \widetilde{Q}_{j}\in\mathcal{D}_{\rho_{j}} is the dyadic \rho_{j}-cube containing Q_{j}. For each Q_{j}\in\mathcal{D}_{r_{j}}, denote
(36)
\Theta_{j}(Q_{j}):=\{\theta_{j}:(r_{j}/\rho_{j})\text{-caps, bad for }Q_{j}\}.
Definition 6.2 (Bad caps at all scales).
Fix R_{\iota}^{-1}\leq r\leq 1 and Q_{r}\in\mathcal{D}_{r}. For each 1\leq j\leq k such that r\leq r_{j}, we define
(37)
\Theta_{j}(Q_{r}):=\Theta_{j}(Q_{j}),
where Q_{j}\in\mathcal{D}_{r_{j}} is the r_{j}-cube containing Q_{r}. Define
(38)
\Theta(Q_{r}):=\bigcup_{j:r_{j}\geq r}\Theta_{j}(Q_{r}),
which is the set of bad caps for Q_{r} at all scales.
For a tube T, we use \theta(T)\in S^{1} to denote the direction of T.
Definition 6.3 (Bad/good tubes).
Fix R_{\iota}^{-1}\leq r\leq 1 and Q_{r}\in\mathcal{D}_{r}. For each 1\leq j\leq k such that r\leq r_{j}, we define
(39)
\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q_{r},j}:=\{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota}:2T\cap Q_{r}\neq\emptyset\text{ and }\exists\,\theta_{j}\in\Theta_{j}(Q_{r})\text{ with }\theta(T)\in\theta_{j}\}.
Define
(40)
\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q_{r}}:=\bigcup_{j:r_{j}\geq r}\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q_{r},j},
which is the collection of tubes in \mathbb{T}_{\iota} whose directions lie in some cap in \Theta(Q_{r}). These are bad tubes.
Also define the good tubes
(41)
\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q_{r},good}:=\mathbb{T}_{\iota}\setminus\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q_{r}}.
Now we are ready to decompose \mu into good and bad parts. Recall the definitions of the operators M_{0},M_{T} from (25). By [8, Lemma 3.4],
we have the following decomposition
\mu=M_{0}\mu+\sum_{\iota\geq 1}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota}}M_{T}\mu+\mu_{\textup{error}},
where \|\mu_{\textup{error}}\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\textup{RapDec}(R_{0})\|\mu\|_{L^{1}}.
We also denote \mu_{\iota}=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota}}M_{T}\mu. Then we have
(42)
\mu=M_{0}\mu+\sum_{\iota\geq 1}\mu_{\iota}+\mu_{\textup{error}}.
We fix the \iota as we did at the end of the last section.
For each dyadic r-cube Q\in\mathcal{D}_{r}, define
(43)
\mu_{good,\iota,Q}:=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q,good}}M_{T}\mu.
For each y\in\mathrm{supp}\nu^{*}, define
(44)
\mu_{good,\iota,y}:=\mu_{good,\iota,Q(y)},
where Q(y) denotes the dyadic R_{\iota}^{-1}-cube containing y.
For any x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}, define d^{x}(y):=|x-y|. For any measure \mu, define d^{x}_{*}(\mu) as the pushforward measure.
For each y\in\mathrm{supp}\nu^{*},
\displaystyle\|d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{good,\iota,y})-d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{\iota})\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}
\displaystyle=\|\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q(y)}}d_{*}^{y}(M_{T}\mu)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}
\displaystyle\leq\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q(y)}}\|d_{*}^{y}(M_{T}\mu)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}
\displaystyle(\textup{by \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{guth2020falconer}{}{}, Corollary 3.3]}})
\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q(y)}}\mu(2T)+\textup{RapDec}(R_{\iota})
Since \mathrm{supp}\mu and \mathrm{supp}\nu are \gtrsim 1 apart, we see that the tubes in \mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q(y)}, which are centered at Q(y), are O(1)-overlapping on \mathrm{supp}\mu. Letting
Bad_{\iota}(y):=\bigcup_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q(y)}}2T,
we have the inequality
(45)
\|d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{good,\iota,y})-d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{\iota})\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\mu(Bad_{\iota}(y))+\textup{RapDec}(R_{\iota}).
Then, with
(46)
\displaystyle Bad_{\iota}:=
\displaystyle\{(x,y)\in\mathrm{supp}\mu\times\mathrm{supp}\nu:x\in Bad_{\iota}(y)\}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\{(x,y)\in\mathrm{supp}\mu\times\mathrm{supp}\nu:\exists\,T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q(y)},\,x\in 2T\},
it follows that
(47)
\begin{split}\int\|d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{good,\iota,y})-d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{\iota})\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}d\nu^{*}(y)\lesssim&\int\mu(Bad_{\iota}(y))d\nu^{*}(y)+\textup{RapDec}(R_{\iota})\\
=&\mu\times\nu^{*}(Bad_{\iota})+\textup{RapDec}(R_{\iota}).\end{split}
Recall that \nu^{*}=\nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)} by our convention at the end of the last section.
Then we need the following geometric observation on pairs (x,y)\in Bad_{\iota}: by our definition of “bad” in (35)-(40) and (46), there exist 1\leq j\leq k and T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota} such that x,y\in 10T and \theta(T) lies in a (r_{j}/\rho_{j})-cap \theta_{j} that is bad for Q_{j}(y)\in\mathcal{D}_{r_{j}} (Q_{j}(y) is the r_{j}-cube containing y). Therefore, if we use
T_{r_{j}\times\rho_{j}}(y;x)
to denote the r_{j}\times\rho_{j}-tube centered at y parallel to the line l_{x,y} connecting x,y, then
100T_{r_{j}\times\rho_{j}}(y;x)\supset T_{r_{j}\times\rho_{j}},
where T_{r_{j}\times\rho_{j}} is a tube in direction \theta_{j} from (35), with 10T_{r_{j}\times\rho_{j}}\cap Q_{j}(y)\neq\emptyset. Consequently,
\nu^{*}_{\widetilde{Q}_{j}}(100T_{r_{j}\times\rho_{j}}(y;x))>R_{\iota}^{T^{-1/3}}(\rho_{j}/r_{j})^{10\eta}\cdot(r_{j}/\rho_{j}),
and therefore the set Bad_{\iota} is a subset of
(48)
\{(x,y)\in E_{1}\times E_{2}:\exists\,j,\,\nu^{*}_{\widetilde{Q}_{j}(y)}(100T_{r_{j}\times\rho_{j}}(y;x))>R_{\iota}^{T^{-1/3}}(\rho_{j}/r_{j})^{10\eta}\cdot(r_{j}/\rho_{j})\}.
Here, recall E_{1},E_{2} are as in Proposition 5.1.
We prove the following estimate for the bad part:
Lemma 6.4.
Let Bad_{\iota} be defined as above. Then
(49)
\mu\times\nu^{*}(Bad_{\iota})\lesssim R_{\iota}^{-\delta}\nu^{*}([0,1)^{2}).
To prove Lemma 6.4, we just need to prove the following result.
Proposition 6.5.
Let \mu,\nu^{*} be the measures as above. Recall that \rho_{j}=R_{\iota}^{-a_{j}},r_{j}=R_{\iota}^{-b_{j}} from the beginning of this section. Then there exists a subset Bad\subset[0,1)^{4} such that
•
\mu\times\nu^{*}(Bad)\lesssim R_{\iota}^{-\delta}\nu^{*}([0,1)^{2}),
•
for all pairs of points (x,y)\notin Bad and all j\in\{1,\dots,k\}, the r_{j}\times\rho_{j} tube T_{r_{j}\times\rho_{j}}(y;x) centered at y parallel to the line l_{x,y} connecting x,y satisfies
(50)
\nu^{*}_{\widetilde{Q}_{j}(y)}(100T_{r_{j}\times\rho_{j}}(y;x))<R_{\iota}^{T^{-1/3}}(\rho_{j}/r_{j})^{10\eta}\cdot(r_{j}/\rho_{j}),
where the notation is the same as in (48).
From the property of Bad, we see that (x,y)\notin Bad\Rightarrow(x,y)\notin Bad_{\iota}. Hence, Bad_{\iota}\subset Bad, and this gives the proof of (49).
Before proving the proposition, we recall the radial projection theorem of Orponen [15]. We use the formulation as in [8, Theorem 3.7]. Denote \pi^{y}(x):=\frac{y-x}{|y-x|} as the radial projection.
Lemma 6.6 (Orponen, [15]).
For every \alpha>1, there exists p=p(\alpha)>1 so that the following holds. Suppose \mu and \omega are measures on the unit disk with disjoint supports and that for every ball B(x,r), \mu(B(x,r))\lesssim r^{\alpha} and \omega(B(x,r))\lesssim r^{\alpha}. Then
(51)
\int\|\pi^{y}_{*}\mu\|_{L^{p}}^{p}d\omega(y)\lesssim 1.
Proof of Proposition 6.5.
Fix j\in\{1,\dots,k\}.
Step 1. We first consider pairs (x,y)\in\mathrm{supp}\mu\times\mathrm{supp}\nu^{*} with
\mu(T_{r_{j}/\rho_{j}\times 1}(x;y))>R_{\iota}^{T^{-1/2}}\cdot r_{j}/\rho_{j},
and denote the set of such pairs by Bad_{j,\mu}, which is an open subset of [0,1)^{4}.
For each y\in\mathrm{supp}\nu^{*}, as \textup{dist}(y,\mathrm{supp}\mu)\sim 1, one can cover \mathrm{supp}\mu by \sim\rho_{j}/r_{j} many r_{j}/\rho_{j}\times 1 tubes containing y of bounded overlapping. Denote this collection of tubes by \mathbb{T}_{y,r_{j}/\rho_{j}\times 1}. Then, for each x with (x,y)\in Bad_{j,\mu}, there exists T\in\mathbb{T}_{y,r_{j}/\rho_{j}\times 1} such that
T_{r_{j}/\rho_{j}\times 1}(x;y)\subset 2T.
Because of bounded overlapping, there are \lesssim R_{\iota}^{-T^{-1/2}}\cdot\rho_{j}/r_{j} many T\in\mathbb{T}_{y,r_{j}/\rho_{j}\times 1} with
\mu(2T)>R_{\iota}^{T^{-1/2}}\cdot r_{j}/\rho_{j}.
Consequently, for each y\in\mathrm{supp}\nu^{*},
\{x:(x,y)\in Bad_{j,\mu}\}
is contained in a union of \lesssim R_{\iota}^{-T^{-1/2}}\cdot\rho_{j}/r_{j} many r_{j}/\rho_{j}\times 1 tubes containing y and hence has measure \lesssim R_{\iota}^{-T^{-1/2}}. Therefore, if we denote \pi^{y}(x):=\frac{y-x}{|y-x|} as the radial projection, then
\mu\times\nu^{*}(Bad_{j,\mu})=\int\mu(\{x:(x,y)\in Bad_{j,\mu}\})\,d\nu^{*}(y)=\int\left(\int\pi^{y}_{*}\mu\right)d\nu^{*}(y),
and for each y\in\mathrm{supp}\nu^{*}, the support of \pi^{y}_{*}(\mu) is contained in \lesssim R_{\iota}^{-T^{-1/2}}\cdot\rho_{j}/r_{j} many r_{j}/\rho_{j}-arcs in S^{1}.
By (26),(27) and (15), we see that
\nu^{*}(B(x,r))\lesssim R_{\iota}^{O(T^{-1})}r^{\alpha}\nu^{*}([0,1)^{2}).
Let \omega=R_{\iota}^{-O(T^{-1})}\Big(\nu^{*}([0,1)^{2})\Big)^{-1}\nu^{*}, then \omega(B(x,r))\lesssim r^{\alpha}.
Hence, by Orponen’s radial projection estimate (51), there exists p>1 such that
(52)
\mu\times\nu^{*}(Bad_{j,\mu})\lesssim R_{\iota}^{O(T^{-1})}\nu^{*}([0,1)^{2})R_{\iota}^{-T^{-1/2}/p^{\prime}}\cdot\int\|\pi^{y}_{*}\mu\|_{L^{p}}d\omega(y)\lesssim R_{\iota}^{-CT^{-1/2}}\nu^{*}([0,1)^{2}),
where the constant C in the exponent only depends on \alpha, and R_{\iota}^{O(T^{-1})} is absorbed into R_{\iota}^{-CT^{-1/2}} since T\gg 1.
Step 2. On [0,1)^{4}\backslash Bad_{j,\mu}, write
\mu\times\nu^{*}=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{\rho_{j}}}\nu^{*}(Q)\cdot\mu\times\nu^{*}_{Q}.
For each Q\in\mathcal{D}_{\rho_{j}} and y\in Q, consider pairs (x,y)\in[0,1)^{2}\times Q with
(53)
\nu^{*}_{Q}(100T_{r_{j}\times\rho_{j}}(y;x))>R_{\iota}^{T^{-1/3}}(\rho_{j}/r_{j})^{10\eta}\cdot(r_{j}/\rho_{j}),
and denote the set of these pairs by Bad_{j,Q}\subset(Bad_{j,\mu})^{c}.
Choose \sim\rho_{j}/r_{j} many r_{j}/\rho_{j}-separated directions \{e_{i}\}\subset S^{1}. For each selected direction e_{i}, cover Q by \sim\rho_{j}/r_{j} many r_{j}\times\rho_{j} tubes parallel to e_{i} of bounded overlapping. Denote the collection of all selected r_{j}\times\rho_{j} tubes by \mathbb{T}_{Q,r_{j}\times\rho_{j}}.
We find another characterization for Bad_{j,Q}.
Here comes the key observation in this step: for each (x,y)\in Bad_{j,Q}, there exists T\in\mathbb{T}_{Q,r_{j}\times\rho_{j}} such that T_{r_{j}\times\rho_{j}}(y;x)\subset 2T. T is associated to a r_{j}/\rho_{j}\times 1 tube T_{r_{j}/\rho_{j}\times 1} that has the same central line as T. We denote their relationship by T_{r_{j}/\rho_{j}\times 1}\sim T. (We remark that different T\in\mathbb{T}_{Q,r_{j}\times\rho_{j}} may be associated with the same T_{r_{j}/\rho_{j}\times 1}.)
We see that
100T_{r_{j}\times\rho_{j}}(y;x)\subset 200T\text{ and }x\in T_{r_{j}/\rho_{j}\times 1}.
This observation implies that, if we call T\in\mathbb{T}_{Q,r_{j}\times\rho_{j}} bad when
\nu_{Q}^{*}(200T)>R_{\iota}^{T^{-1/3}}(\rho_{j}/r_{j})^{10\eta}\cdot(r_{j}/\rho_{j}),
then
Bad_{j,Q}\subset\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\in\mathbb{T}_{Q,r_{j}\times\rho_{j}},\,bad\\
T_{r_{j}/\rho_{j}\times 1}\sim T\end{subarray}}T_{r_{j}/\rho_{j}\times 1}\times T.
Since (x,y)\notin Bad_{j,\mu}, by our construction from Step 1, it follows that
\mu(T_{r_{j}/\rho_{j}\times 1})<R_{\iota}^{T^{-1/2}}\cdot r_{j}/\rho_{j}.
Therefore,
\mu\times\nu^{*}_{Q}(Bad_{j,Q})\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\in\mathbb{T}_{Q,r_{j}\times\rho_{j}},\,bad\\
T_{r_{j}/\rho_{j}\times 1}\sim T\end{subarray}}\mu(T_{r_{j}/\rho_{j}\times 1})\nu^{*}_{Q}(T)\leq R_{\iota}^{T^{-1/2}}\cdot r_{j}/\rho_{j}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{Q,r_{j}\times\rho_{j}},\,bad}\nu^{*}_{Q}(T)
=R_{\iota}^{T^{-1/2}}\cdot r_{j}/\rho_{j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{i}\in S^{1}\\
r_{j}/\rho_{j}\text{-separated}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\in\mathbb{T}_{Q,r_{j}\times\rho_{j}}\\
T\parallel e_{i},\,bad\end{subarray}}\nu^{*}_{Q}(T).
As tubes in \mathbb{T}_{Q,r_{j}\times\rho_{j}} have bounded overlap, by (53), for each e_{i}, the number of tubes T in the sum is \lesssim R_{\iota}^{-T^{-1/3}}(\rho_{j}/r_{j})^{10\eta}(\rho_{j}/r_{j}). So, by Cauchy-Schwarz,
\displaystyle\left(\mu\times\nu^{*}_{Q}(Bad_{j,Q})\right)^{2}
\displaystyle\lesssim
\displaystyle(R_{\iota}^{T^{-1/2}}r_{j}/\rho_{j})^{2}\cdot\rho_{j}/r_{j}\cdot R_{\iota}^{-T^{-1/3}}(\rho_{j}/r_{j})^{-10\eta}(\rho_{j}/r_{j})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{i}\in S^{1}\\
r_{j}/\rho_{j}\text{-separated}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\in\mathbb{T}_{Q,r_{j}\times\rho_{j}}\\
T\parallel e_{i},\,bad\end{subarray}}\nu^{*}_{Q}(T)^{2}
\displaystyle\leq
\displaystyle R_{\iota}^{2T^{-1/2}-T^{-1/3}}(\frac{\rho_{j}}{r_{j}})^{-10\eta}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{Q,r_{j}\times\rho_{j}}}\nu^{*}_{Q}(T)^{2}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle R_{\iota}^{2T^{-1/2}-T^{-1/3}}(\frac{\rho_{j}}{r_{j}})^{-10\eta}\iint\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{Q,r_{j}\times\rho_{j}}}\chi_{T}(y)\chi_{T}(y^{\prime})\,d\nu^{*}_{Q}(y)\,d\nu^{*}_{Q}(y^{\prime}).
Fix y^{\prime} and consider |y-y^{\prime}|\sim 2^{-l} with 2^{-l}\in[r_{j},\rho_{j}]. Then the number of T\in\mathbb{T}_{Q,r_{j}\times\rho_{j}} containing both y,y^{\prime} is \lesssim 2^{l}\rho_{j}. Also, since \nu^{*} satisfies (32), it follows that
\nu^{*}_{Q}(B(y,2^{-l}))\lesssim(\frac{\rho_{j}}{r_{j}})^{\eta}(\frac{2^{-l}}{\rho_{j}})^{1+\varepsilon}.
Together we have
\left(\mu\times\nu^{*}_{Q}(Bad_{j,Q})\right)^{2}\lesssim R_{\iota}^{-\delta}(\frac{\rho_{j}}{r_{j}})^{-9\eta}\left((r_{j}/\rho_{j})^{1+\varepsilon}+\sum_{l:2^{-l}\in[r_{j},\rho_{j}]}2^{l}\rho_{j}\cdot(2^{l}\rho_{j})^{-1-\varepsilon}\right).
Here, the first term in the bracket comes from |y-y^{\prime}|\leq r_{j}, while the second term comes from |y-y^{\prime}|\sim 2^{-l}.
We see that
(54)
\mu\times\nu^{*}_{Q}(Bad_{j,Q})\lesssim R_{\iota}^{-\delta}.
Step 3. Finally take
Bad:=\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}Bad_{j,\mu}\cup\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{\rho_{j}}}Bad_{j,Q}.
From (52) and (54),
\mu\times\nu^{*}(Bad)\lesssim\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(R_{\iota}^{-CT^{-1/2}}\nu^{*}([0,1)^{2})+\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{\rho_{j}}}\nu^{*}(Q)R_{\iota}^{-\delta}\right)\lesssim R_{\iota}^{-\delta}\nu^{*}([0,1)^{2}).
Here, we used that k\leq C(\eta) is bounded by a constant.

7. An estimate for the good part
We still work with the \mu,\nu^{*} as we did in the last section. Recall that \mu_{good,\iota,y} is defined in (44). The purpose of this section is to estimate
\int\|d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{good,\iota,y})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}d\nu^{*}(y).
The main result is stated as follows.
Proposition 7.1.
Let \mu,\nu^{*} be as in the last section. Then
(55)
\int\|d^{y}_{*}(\mu_{good,\iota,y})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}d\nu^{*}(y)<R_{\iota}^{-\delta}\nu^{*}([0,1)^{2}).
By the observation in [11], for each y\in\mathrm{supp}\nu^{*},
\|d^{y}_{*}(\mu_{good,\iota,y})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}|\mu_{good,\iota,y}*\sigma_{t}(y)|^{2}t^{2}dt,
where \sigma_{t}=d\sigma_{t} denotes the normalized arc-length measure on S^{1}_{t}, the circle of radius t. This is
\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}|\mu_{good,\iota,y}*\sigma_{t}(y)|^{2}tdt
because \mu_{good,\iota,y} is essentially supported near \mathrm{supp}\mu, away from y\in\mathrm{supp}\nu^{*} (see Lemma 5.2 in [8]). Then, by the L^{2}-identity proved in [11], for every y\in\mathbb{R}^{2},
\int_{0}^{\infty}|\mu_{good,\iota,y}*\sigma_{t}(y)|^{2}tdt=\int_{0}^{\infty}|\mu_{good,\iota,y}*\widehat{\sigma_{r}}(y)|^{2}rdr.
By the definition of \mu_{good,\iota,Q(y)}, \mu_{good,\iota,y} in (43), (44), we see that the Fourier support of \mu_{good,\iota,y} essentially lies in |\xi|\sim R_{\iota}. We hence have
(56)
\int_{0}^{\infty}|\mu_{good,\iota,Q(y)}*\widehat{\sigma_{r}}(y)|^{2}rdr\lesssim\int_{r\sim R_{\iota}}|\mu_{good,\iota,Q(y)}*\widehat{\sigma_{r}}(y)|^{2}rdr+\textup{RapDec}(R_{\iota}).
Notice that the dependence on y in \mu_{good,\iota,y} has not yet made any difference because so far everything is pointwise.
Now we fix R\sim R_{\iota} and consider the integral with respect to d\nu^{*}(y):
(57)
\int|\mu_{good,\iota,Q(y)}*\widehat{\sigma_{R}}(y)|^{2}d\nu^{*}(y)=\int|(\widehat{\mu_{good,\iota,Q(y)}}d\sigma_{R})^{\vee}(y)|^{2}d\nu^{*}(y).
To deal with the dependence on Q(y), a careful analysis on bad caps associated with different y is required.
Recall (29) that \rho_{j}=R_{\iota}^{-a_{j}},r_{j}=R_{\iota}^{-b_{j}}, and
R_{\iota}^{-1}<r_{k}<\rho_{k}<\dots<r_{1}<\rho_{1}\leq 1.
For convenience, we introduce the following notation.
Definition 7.2.
Define
F:=(\widehat{\mu}_{\iota}d\sigma_{R})^{\vee}=(\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota}}\widehat{M_{T}\mu}\ d\sigma_{R})^{\vee}.
For each T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota}, define
F_{T}:=(\widehat{M_{T}\mu}\ d\sigma_{R})^{\vee}.
For each R_{\iota}^{-1/2}-cap \theta, define
F_{\theta}:=(\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota,\theta}}\widehat{M_{T}\mu}\ d\sigma_{R})^{\vee}=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota,\theta}}F_{T}.
For r\geq R_{\iota}^{-1/2} and an r-cap \tau, define
F_{\tau}:=\sum_{\theta\subset\tau}F_{\theta}.
Lemma 7.3.
For each T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota}, F_{T}1_{B(0,1)} is essentially supported in T.
Proof.
Let S^{1}_{R} be the circle of radius R.
Note that \widehat{M_{T}\mu} is essentially supported in a R_{\iota}^{1/2}\times R_{\iota}-rectangle. Therefore \widehat{M_{T}\mu}\ d\sigma_{R} is essentially supported in an arc of length R_{\iota}^{1/2} in S_{R}^{1}, denoted by J. We have
F_{T}\approx(\widehat{M_{T}\mu}\ 1_{J}d\sigma_{R})^{\vee}=M_{T}\mu*(1_{J}d\sigma_{R})^{\vee}.
Since inside B(0,1), M_{T}\mu is essentially supported in T and (1_{J}d\sigma_{R})^{\vee} is essentially supported in a R_{\iota}^{-1/2}\times 1-rectangle T_{0}, which is the translation of T to the origin, we see that F_{T} is essentially supported in T.

We also define the good functions which may be compared with Definition 6.3.
Definition 7.4.
Fix R_{\iota}^{-1}\leq r\leq 1 and Q_{r}\in\mathcal{D}_{r}. Define
(58)
F_{Q_{r}}:=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q_{r},good}}F_{T}.
Remark 7.5.
We only care about the value of F_{Q_{r}} inside Q_{r}.
From the definition, we see that for each Q\in\mathcal{D}_{R_{\iota}^{-1}} and any y\in Q,
(\widehat{\mu_{good,\iota,y}}d\sigma_{R})^{\vee}=F_{Q}.
Now we can rewrite the integrand in (57) as
(59)
\int|(\widehat{\mu_{good,\iota,y}}d\sigma_{R})^{\vee}(y)|^{2}d\nu^{*}(y)=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{R_{\iota}^{-1}}}\int_{Q}|F_{Q}(y)|^{2}d\nu^{*}(y).
Since F_{Q} is the sum of some wave packets from \mathbb{T}_{\iota}, we see that \widehat{F}_{Q} is essentially supported in B(0,R_{\iota}). By the locally constant property, F_{Q} is essentially constant in each Q\in\mathcal{D}_{R_{\iota}^{-1}}. Now, for each r, we denote
\mathcal{D}_{r}(\nu^{*}):=\{Q_{r}\in\mathcal{D}_{r}:Q_{r}\cap\mathrm{supp}\nu^{*}\neq\emptyset\},

Therefore, up to a rapidly decaying error, we may bound (59) by
(60)
\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{R_{\iota}^{-1}}(\nu^{*})}\frac{\nu^{*}(Q)}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|F_{Q}(y)|^{2}dy.
Since \nu^{*}(Q) are comparable for all Q\in\mathcal{D}_{R_{\iota}^{-1}}(\nu^{*}), we may bound the above quantity by
(61)
\frac{\nu^{*}([0,1)^{2})}{|X_{R_{\iota}^{-1}}|}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{R_{\iota}^{-1}}}\int_{Q}|F_{Q}|^{2}.
Now, we estimate (61) by a ball inflation process. It consists of the following three lemmas.
Lemma 7.6.
For j\in\{1,\dots,k\}, we have
\frac{1}{|X_{\rho_{j}}|}\sum_{\widetilde{Q}\in\mathcal{D}_{\rho_{j}}(\nu^{*})}\int_{\widetilde{Q}}|F_{\widetilde{Q}}|^{2}\lesssim(\frac{r_{j-1}}{\rho_{j}})^{2}\frac{1}{|X_{r_{j-1}}|}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{r_{j-1}}(\nu^{*})}\int_{Q}|F_{Q}|^{2}.
Here, r_{0}=1.
Proof.
This is a trivial estimate.
We note that for \widetilde{Q}\in\mathcal{D}_{\rho_{j}},Q\in\mathcal{D}_{r_{j-1}} with \widetilde{Q}\subset Q, then F_{\widetilde{Q}}=F_{Q} on \widetilde{Q} up to rapidly decaying tail. Noting that the integration domain on the right-hand side contains the integration domain on the left-hand side, hence the factor we lose is \leq\frac{|X_{r_{j-1}}|}{|X_{\rho_{j}}|}=\frac{|Q|}{|\widetilde{Q}|}=(\frac{r_{j-1}}{\rho_{j}})^{2}.
∎
Lemma 7.7.
For j\in\{1,\dots,k\}, we have
\frac{1}{|X_{r_{j}}|}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{r_{j}}(\nu^{*})}\int_{Q}|F_{Q}|^{2}\lesssim R_{\iota}^{O(T^{-1/3})}(\frac{\rho_{j}}{r_{j}})^{O(\eta)}\frac{1}{|X_{\rho_{j}}|}\sum_{\widetilde{Q}\in\mathcal{D}_{\rho_{j}}(\nu^{*})}\int_{\widetilde{Q}}|F_{\widetilde{Q}}|^{2}.
Proof.
Fix a \widetilde{Q}\in\mathcal{D}_{\rho_{j}}(\nu^{*}). Noting that |X_{r_{j}}|/|X_{\rho_{j}}|\sim|X_{r_{j}}\cap\widetilde{Q}|/|\widetilde{Q}|, it suffices to prove
\frac{1}{|X_{r_{j}}\cap\widetilde{Q}|}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{r_{j}}(\nu^{*}),Q\subset\widetilde{Q}}\int_{Q}|F_{Q}|^{2}\lesssim R_{\iota}^{O(T^{-1/3})}(\frac{\rho_{j}}{r_{j}})^{O(\eta)}\frac{1}{|\widetilde{Q}|}\int_{\widetilde{Q}}|F_{\widetilde{Q}}|^{2}.
Let \{\tau\} be the same partition into r_{j}/\rho_{j}-caps used to define \Theta_{j} in (36). Let
(62)
F_{Q,\tau}:=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q,good},\theta(T)\in\tau}F_{T}.
In particular, F_{Q,S^{1}}=F_{Q}.
(Recall the definition of \mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q,good} in (41).)
Note that \widehat{F}_{Q,\tau} is supported in the Rr_{j}/\rho_{j}-cap R\tau=\{R\xi:\xi\in\tau\} in S^{1}_{R} (where S^{1}_{R} is the circle of radius R), and the convex hull of R\tau is a rectangle of dimensions Rr_{j}/\rho_{j}\times R(r_{j}/\rho_{j})^{2}. Noting that \{R\tau\} are Rr_{j}/\rho_{j}-separated and R\sim R_{\iota}, then since (30) implies that Rr_{j}/\rho_{j}\geq r_{j}^{-1}, we use local L^{2}-orthogonality to deduce that
\int_{Q}|F_{Q}|^{2}\lesssim\int_{Q}\sum_{|\tau|=r_{j}/\rho_{j}}|F_{Q,\tau}|^{2},
up to rapidly decaying tails.
For each \tau, let \mathbb{T}^{\prime}_{\tau} consist of the tubes in the fixed family \mathbb{T}^{\prime}_{j,\tau} that meet \widetilde{Q}.
We have
(63)
\begin{split}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{r_{j}}(\nu^{*}),Q\subset\widetilde{Q}}\int_{Q}|F_{Q}|^{2}&\lesssim\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{r_{j}}(\nu^{*}),Q\subset\widetilde{Q}}\sum_{|\tau|=r_{j}/\rho_{j}}\sum_{T^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}^{\prime}_{\tau}}\int_{T^{\prime}}|F_{Q,\tau}|^{2}1_{Q}\\
&=\sum_{|\tau|=r_{j}/\rho_{j}}\sum_{T^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}^{\prime}_{\tau}}\int_{T^{\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{D}_{r_{j}}(\nu^{*}),\ Q\subset\widetilde{Q}\\
Q\cap T^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}|F_{Q,\tau}|^{2}1_{Q}.\end{split}
Fix a \tau and T^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}^{\prime}_{\tau}. For Q\in\mathcal{D}_{r_{j}}(\nu^{*}) with Q\subset\widetilde{Q} and Q\cap T^{\prime}\neq\emptyset,
we explore the relationship between F_{Q,\tau} and F_{\widetilde{Q},\tau}. Recalling Definition 6.3, we see that
(64)
\bigg(\mathbb{T}_{\iota,\widetilde{Q}}\cap\{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota}:2T\cap Q\neq\emptyset\}\bigg)\bigcup\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q,j}=\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q}.
We introduce a convenient notation. For a collection of tubes \mathbb{T} and a cap \tau\subset S^{1}, we denote
\mathbb{T}[\tau]:=\{T\in\mathbb{T}:\theta(T)\in\tau\}.
In other words, \mathbb{T}[\tau] is the subcollection of tubes whose directions lie in \tau.
Restricting (64) to direction \tau, we have
(65)
\bigg(\mathbb{T}_{\iota,\widetilde{Q}}\cap\{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota}[\tau]:2T\cap Q\neq\emptyset\}\bigg)\bigcup\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q,j}[\tau]=\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q}[\tau].
We discuss two cases.
Case 1: \nu_{\widetilde{Q}}^{*}(T^{\prime})>R_{\iota}^{T^{-1/3}}(\frac{\rho_{j}}{r_{j}})^{10\eta}(r_{j}/\rho_{j}). Fix any Q\in\mathcal{D}_{r_{j}}(\nu^{*}) with Q\cap T^{\prime}\neq\emptyset. By (35), we see that the r_{j}/\rho_{j}-cap \tau (corresponding to the direction of T^{\prime}) is bad for Q. Hence \mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q}[\tau]\supset\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q,j}[\tau] contains all tubes in \mathbb{T}_{\iota}[\tau] whose 2-dilations intersect Q. This implies \{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q,good}[\tau]:2T\cap Q\neq\emptyset\}=\emptyset. By (62) and Lemma 7.3, |F_{Q,\tau}|1_{Q}=\textup{RapDec}(R_{\iota}).
Case 2: \nu_{\widetilde{Q}}^{*}(T^{\prime})\leq R_{\iota}^{T^{-1/3}}(\frac{\rho_{j}}{r_{j}})^{10\eta}(r_{j}/\rho_{j}). For each Q meeting T^{\prime}, if \tau\in\Theta_{j}(Q), then |F_{Q,\tau}|1_{Q}=\textup{RapDec}(R_{\iota}) as above. Otherwise, \mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q,j}[\tau]=\emptyset, and (65) gives
\mathbb{T}_{\iota,\widetilde{Q}}\cap\{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\iota}[\tau]:2T\cap Q\neq\emptyset\}=\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q}[\tau].
It further implies
|F_{Q,\tau}|1_{Q}\lesssim|F_{\widetilde{Q},\tau}|+\textup{RapDec}(R_{\iota}).
Call T^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}^{\prime}_{\tau} retained if it meets a cube Q\in\mathcal{D}_{r_{j}}(\nu^{*}) with Q\subset\widetilde{Q} and \tau\notin\Theta_{j}(Q), and put Y:=X_{r_{j}}\cap\widetilde{Q}. By the two cases above, (63) is bounded by (up to negligible error)
(66)
\begin{split}&\sum_{|\tau|=r_{j}/\rho_{j}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}^{\prime}_{\tau}\\
T^{\prime}\textup{ retained}\end{subarray}}\int_{T^{\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{D}_{r_{j}}(\nu^{*}),\ Q\subset\widetilde{Q}\\
Q\cap T^{\prime}\neq\emptyset,\ \tau\notin\Theta_{j}(Q)\end{subarray}}|F_{\widetilde{Q},\tau}|^{2}1_{Q}\\
\leq&\sum_{|\tau|=r_{j}/\rho_{j}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}^{\prime}_{\tau}\\
T^{\prime}\textup{ retained}\end{subarray}}\int_{T^{\prime}\cap Y}|F_{\widetilde{Q},\tau}|^{2}.\end{split}
Note that the integration domain T^{\prime}\cap Y is morally a collection of r_{j}-cubes. We partition \tau=\bigsqcup\sigma of \tau into (R\rho_{j})^{-1/2}-caps \{\sigma\}. This is allowable since (R\rho_{j})^{-1/2}\leq r_{j}/\rho_{j} by (30). We obtain
F_{\widetilde{Q},\tau}=\sum_{\sigma\subset\tau}F_{\widetilde{Q},\sigma}.
By the local L^{2}-orthogonality, we have
(67)
\int_{T^{\prime}\cap Y}|F_{\widetilde{Q},\tau}|^{2}\lesssim\int_{T^{\prime}\cap Y}\sum_{\sigma\subset\tau}|F_{\widetilde{Q},\sigma}|^{2}.
Similar to the discussion in the paragraph below (62), we have that \mathrm{supp}\widehat{F}_{\widetilde{Q},\sigma} is essentially contained in a R(R\rho_{j})^{-1/2}\times R(R\rho_{j})^{-1}-rectangle. By locally constant property, |F_{\widetilde{Q},\sigma}| is essentially constant on rectangles of dimensions R^{-1/2}\rho_{j}^{1/2}\times\rho_{j} whose long direction lies in \sigma. Noting that R^{-1/2}\rho_{j}^{1/2}\leq r_{j} by (30), we can tile T^{\prime} by R^{-1/2}\rho_{j}^{1/2}\times\rho_{j}-tubes T_{\sigma} whose direction is \sigma, denoted by T^{\prime}=\bigcup T_{\sigma}. We therefore have
(68)
|F_{\widetilde{Q},\sigma}|^{2}\sim\sum_{T_{\sigma}}c_{T_{\sigma}}1_{T_{\sigma}}
up to rapid decaying error inside T^{\prime}.
If T^{\prime} is retained, choose such a cube Q_{0}. Cover 4T^{\prime} by O(1) neighboring grid tubes S. Since T^{\prime}\subset 10S, each 10S meets Q_{0}, so \tau\notin\Theta_{j}(Q_{0}) implies
\nu^{*}_{\widetilde{Q}}(4T^{\prime})\lesssim R_{\iota}^{T^{-1/3}}(\rho_{j}/r_{j})^{10\eta}(r_{j}/\rho_{j}).
Every r_{j}-cube meeting T^{\prime} lies in 4T^{\prime}. Thus, by (15),
\displaystyle N(T^{\prime})
\displaystyle:=\#\{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{r_{j}}(\nu^{*}):Q\subset\widetilde{Q},\ Q\cap T^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\}
\displaystyle\lesssim R_{\iota}^{O(T^{-1/3})}(\rho_{j}/r_{j})^{10\eta}\frac{r_{j}}{\rho_{j}}\frac{|Y|}{r_{j}^{2}}.
Writing w=R^{-1/2}\rho_{j}^{1/2}, we have |T_{\sigma}\cap Q|\lesssim wr_{j} for each r_{j}-cube Q. Consequently,
|T_{\sigma}\cap T^{\prime}\cap Y|\lesssim wr_{j}N(T^{\prime})\lesssim R_{\iota}^{O(T^{-1/3})}(\rho_{j}/r_{j})^{10\eta}\frac{|T_{\sigma}|}{|\widetilde{Q}|}|Y|.
Combining with (68) and plugging into (67), we see that
\int_{T^{\prime}\cap Y}|F_{\widetilde{Q},\tau}|^{2}\lesssim R_{\iota}^{O(T^{-1/3})}(\frac{\rho_{j}}{r_{j}})^{10\eta}\frac{|\widetilde{Q}\cap X_{r_{j}}|}{|\widetilde{Q}|}\int_{T^{\prime}}\sum_{\sigma\subset\tau}|F_{\widetilde{Q},\sigma}|^{2}.
This implies
(66)
\displaystyle\lesssim R_{\iota}^{O(T^{-1/3})}(\frac{\rho_{j}}{r_{j}})^{10\eta}\frac{|\widetilde{Q}\cap X_{r_{j}}|}{|\widetilde{Q}|}\sum_{|\tau|=r_{j}/\rho_{j}}\int_{\widetilde{Q}}|F_{\widetilde{Q},\tau}|^{2}
\displaystyle\lesssim R_{\iota}^{O(T^{-1/3})}(\frac{\rho_{j}}{r_{j}})^{10\eta}\frac{|\widetilde{Q}\cap X_{r_{j}}|}{|\widetilde{Q}|}\int_{\widetilde{Q}}|F_{\widetilde{Q}}|^{2}.
In the last inequality, we used that F_{\widetilde{Q},\tau} are orthogonal on \widetilde{Q}. We have shown that they are orthogonal on Q, so they are also orthogonal on bigger cube \widetilde{Q}.
This finishes the proof.

Lemma 7.8.
Recalling (31), assume
(69)
|\mathrm{supp}\nu^{*}\cap Q_{r}|_{R_{\iota}^{-1}}\lesssim R_{\iota}^{O(T^{-1})}(R_{\iota}r)^{\alpha}

\mathbb{T}_{\iota,Q}=\mathbb{T}_{\iota,\widetilde{Q}}\cap\{T:2T\cap Q\neq\emptyset\}.

|F_{Q}|1_{Q}\lesssim|F_{\widetilde{Q}}|1_{Q}

(72)
\int_{{X_{R_{\iota}^{-1}}}\cap\widetilde{Q}}|F_{\widetilde{Q}}|^{2}\lesssim R^{O(T^{-1})}(\frac{|\mathrm{supp}\nu^{*}|_{R_{\iota}^{-1}}}{|\mathrm{supp}\nu^{*}|_{r_{k}}})^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}\frac{1}{R_{\iota}r_{k}}\int_{\widetilde{Q}}|F_{\widetilde{Q}}|^{2}.

We finish the proof of Proposition 7.1 by combining Lemma 7.6, 7.7, 7.8 together.
Proof of Proposition 7.1.
By (56), it suffices to show that
\int_{R\sim R_{\iota}}RdR\int|\mu_{good,\iota,y}*\widehat{\sigma}_{R}(y)|^{2}d\nu^{*}(y)\lesssim R_{\iota}^{-\delta^{4}}\nu^{*}([0,1)^{2}).

(75)
\int_{R\sim R_{\iota}}RdR\bigg(\frac{1}{|X_{R_{\iota}^{-1}}|}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{R_{\iota}^{-1}}(\nu^{*})}\int|F_{Q}(y)|^{2}dy\bigg)\lesssim R_{\iota}^{-O(T^{-1})}.
We remark that F_{Q} depends on R.
Combining Lemma 7.6, 7.7, 7.8, we see the left-hand side of (75) is bounded by

Here, F=(\widehat{\mu}_{\iota}d\sigma_{R})^{\vee} is given in Definition 7.2.
By (33), (\frac{1}{\rho_{1}}\frac{r_{1}}{\rho_{2}}\dots\frac{r_{k-1}}{\rho_{k}})^{2}\lesssim R_{\iota}^{O(\eta)} is negligible. Since k\leq C(\eta), we can choose T^{-1} sufficiently small so that kO(T^{-1/3})\leq\varepsilon, and hence R_{\iota}^{kO(T^{-1/3})}\leq R_{\iota}^{\varepsilon}.
Next, we estimate
\int_{R\sim R_{\iota}}RdR\bigg(\int|F(y)|^{2}dy\bigg)=\int_{R\sim R_{\iota}}\int|(\widehat{\mu}_{\iota}d\sigma_{R})^{\vee}(y)|^{2}RdydR.
Note that d\sigma_{R}\sim\frac{1}{R}1_{S^{1}_{R}} is the normalized measure. The above integral is
\sim\int_{R\sim R_{\iota}}\int_{S^{1}_{R}}|\widehat{\mu}_{\iota}(\omega)|^{2}d\omega R^{-1}dR\sim R_{\iota}^{-1}\int_{|\xi|\sim R_{\iota}}|\widehat{\mu}_{\iota}(\xi)|^{2}.
Recall that the s-dimensional energy of the measure \mu (in \mathbb{R}^{2}) is defined by
I_{s}(\mu):=\int\int|x-y|^{-s}d\mu(x)d\mu(y)=c_{s}\int|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{-2+s}.
Since \mu satisfies the ball condition (21), we may choose \alpha^{\prime}<\alpha slightly smaller, so that (1) and (2) hold for (\alpha^{\prime},\beta), and I_{\alpha^{\prime}}(\mu)<\infty. For simplicity, we relabel \alpha^{\prime}=\alpha. As a result,
R_{\iota}^{-1}\int_{|\xi|\sim R_{\iota}}|\widehat{\mu}_{\iota}(\xi)|^{2}\lesssim R_{\iota}^{1-\alpha}.
Combining the above discussion, the left-hand side of (75) is bounded by
(76)
R_{\iota}^{O(\varepsilon)}\frac{|X_{r_{k}}|}{|X_{R_{\iota}^{-1}}|}(\frac{|\mathrm{supp}\nu^{*}|_{R_{\iota}^{-1}}}{|\mathrm{supp}\nu^{*}|_{r_{k}}})^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}\frac{1}{R_{\iota}r_{k}}R_{\iota}^{1-\alpha}.
Now, we do the same calculation as we did to estimate (12). We remark that the u,\kappa in (12) become \alpha,\beta in (76).
We see that (76) is bounded by
R_{\iota}^{O(\varepsilon)}R_{\iota}^{1-\alpha}R_{\iota}^{\max\{\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2\beta-1},\frac{\beta-\alpha}{(\alpha+1)(2\beta-\alpha-1)}\}}.
Since \alpha,\beta satisfy (1) and (2), when \varepsilon is sufficiently small, the above quantity is bounded by R_{\iota}^{-\delta}.
This finishes the proof of Proposition 7.1.
∎
8. Proof of Proposition 5.1
We prove Proposition 5.1 in this section. This will imply Theorem 1.1.
Recall that at the end of Section 5, we denoted \nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)} by \nu^{*} for convenience. Now we will recover the omitted indices. By (42), we have
(77)
\begin{split}1&=\int\bigg(\int_{\Delta_{y}(E_{1})}d_{*}^{y}(\mu)\bigg)d\nu(y)\\
&=\int\bigg(\int_{\Delta_{y}(E_{1})}d_{*}^{y}(M_{0}\mu)\bigg)d\nu(y)+\sum_{\iota\geq 1}\int\bigg(\int_{\Delta_{y}(E_{1})}d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{\iota})\bigg)d\nu(y)+\textup{RapDec}(R_{0})\\
&\leq\bigg|\int\bigg(\int_{\Delta_{y}(E_{1})}d_{*}^{y}(M_{0}\mu)\bigg)d\nu(y)\bigg|+\sum_{\iota\geq 1}\bigg|\int\bigg(\int_{\Delta_{y}(E_{1})}d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{\iota})\bigg)d\nu(y)\bigg|+\textup{RapDec}(R_{0}).\end{split}
We study the second term on the right-hand side.
Fix \iota\geq 1. Recall that
\nu=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\iota}}\sum_{j}\nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)}+\nu_{\iota}^{N_{\iota}+1}.
Here, \nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)} were abbreviated as \nu^{*} in the last few sections, and \nu_{\iota}^{N_{\iota}+1}([0,1)^{2})\leq R_{\iota}^{-\delta^{3}} (see the paragraph below (25)).
We have
\displaystyle\bigg|\int\bigg(\int_{\Delta_{y}(E_{1})}d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{\iota})\bigg)d\nu(y)\bigg|
\displaystyle\leq\bigg|\int\bigg(\int_{\Delta_{y}(E_{1})}d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{\iota})-d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{good,\iota,y})\bigg)d\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\iota}}\sum_{j}\nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)}(y)\bigg|
\displaystyle+\bigg|\int\bigg(\int_{\Delta_{y}(E_{1})}d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{good,\iota,y})\bigg)d\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\iota}}\sum_{j}\nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)}(y)\bigg|
\displaystyle+\bigg|\int\bigg(\int_{\Delta_{y}(E_{1})}d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{\iota})\bigg)d\nu_{\iota}^{N_{\iota}+1}(y)\bigg|
\displaystyle=:I+II+III.
\displaystyle I
\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\iota}}\sum_{j}\bigg|\int\bigg(\int_{\Delta_{y}(E_{1})}d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{\iota})-d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{good,\iota,y})\bigg)d\nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)}(y)\bigg|
\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\iota}}\sum_{j}\int\|d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{good,\iota,y})-d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{\iota}))\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}d\nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)}(y)
\displaystyle(\textup{by~}\eqref{badpartest},\eqref{measure-Bad-i})
\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\iota}}\sum_{j}R_{\iota}^{-\delta}\nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)}([0,1)^{2})
\displaystyle\leq R_{\iota}^{-\delta}.
\displaystyle II
\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\iota}}\sum_{j}\bigg|\int\bigg(\int_{\Delta_{y}(E_{1})}d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{good,\iota,y})\bigg)d\nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)}(y)\bigg|
\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\iota}}\sum_{j}\bigg|\int|\Delta_{y}(E_{1})|^{1/2}\|d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{good,\iota,y})\|_{L^{2}}d\nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)}(y)\bigg|
\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\iota}}\sum_{j}\bigg(\int|\Delta_{y}(E_{1})|d\nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)}(y)\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\int\|d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{good,\iota,y})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}d\nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)}(y)\bigg)^{1/2}
\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\iota}}\sum_{j}\int|\Delta_{y}(E_{1})|d\nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)}(y)\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\iota}}\sum_{j}\int\|d_{*}^{y}(\mu_{good,\iota,y})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}d\nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)}(y)\bigg)^{1/2}
\displaystyle(\textup{by~Proposition~}\ref{prop-our-L2-good})
\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y\in E_{2}}|\Delta_{y}(E_{1})|^{1/2}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\iota}}\sum_{j}R_{\iota}^{-\delta}\nu_{\iota}^{i,(j)}([0,1)^{2})\bigg)^{1/2}
\displaystyle\leq{R_{\iota}^{-\delta/2}}\sup_{y\in E_{2}}|\Delta_{y}(E_{1})|^{1/2}.
\displaystyle III\leq\|\mu_{\iota}\|_{L^{1}}\|\nu_{\iota}^{N_{\iota}+1}\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\|\mu\|_{L^{1}}R_{\iota}^{-\delta^{3}}\leq R_{\iota}^{-\delta^{3}}.
We still need to bound the first term on the right-hand side of (77).
\displaystyle\bigg|\int\bigg(\int_{\Delta_{y}(E_{1})}d_{*}^{y}(M_{0}\mu)\bigg)d\nu(y)\bigg|
\displaystyle\leq\int|\Delta_{y}(E_{1})|^{1/2}\|d_{*}^{y}(M_{0}\mu)\|_{L^{2}}d\nu(y)
\displaystyle\lesssim\sup_{y\in E_{2}}|\Delta_{y}(E_{1})|^{1/2}\bigg(\int\|d_{*}^{y}(M_{0}\mu)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}d\nu(y)\bigg)^{1/2}
\displaystyle\lesssim R_{0}^{O(1)}\sup_{y\in E_{2}}|\Delta_{y}(E_{1})|^{1/2}.
The last inequality follows the same argument as in the proof of Proposition 7.1, but we only need a crude bound.
Now, plugging everything into (77), we obtain
(78)
1\leq R_{0}^{O(1)}\sup_{y\in E_{2}}|\Delta_{y}(E_{1})|^{1/2}+\sum_{\iota\geq 1}(R_{\iota}^{-\delta}+{R_{\iota}^{-\delta/2}}\sup_{y\in E_{2}}|\Delta_{y}(E_{1})|^{1/2}+R_{\iota}^{-\delta^{3}})+\textup{RapDec}(R_{0}).
By choosing R_{0} large enough, we see that
\sup_{y\in E_{2}}|\Delta_{y}(E_{1})|^{1/2}>0.
This finishes the proof.
9. Weighted L^{2} estimate
Let \sigma_{S} be the surface measure on the unit circle S, and we define the extension operator E:S\to\mathbb{R}^{2} as
(79)
Ef(x)=\int e^{ix\cdot\xi}f(\xi)d\sigma_{S}(\xi).
Consider also its “broad” variant:
Definition 9.1.
Let K\geq 1 and A\in\mathbb{N}.
Let \Sigma=\{\sigma\} be a set of 2-overlapping K^{-1}-arcs that covers S.
Let f_{\sigma}=f\boldsymbol{1}_{\sigma}.
For any f:S\to\mathbb{C} and x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}, define
(80)
{\rm{Br}}_{A}Ef(x):=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Sigma^{\prime}\subset\Sigma,\\
\#\Sigma^{\prime}=A\end{subarray}}\min_{\sigma\in\Sigma^{\prime}}|Ef_{\sigma}(x)|.
We also recall the classical definition of a Katz-Tao (R^{-1},s)-set.
Definition 9.2.
Let R>1, s>0. Let X\subset B(0,1) be a disjoint union of R^{-1}-balls. We say X is a Katz-Tao (R^{-1},s,C)-set if
|X\cap B(x,r)|\leq CR^{-2}(rR)^{s}
for any B(x,r). When C is a fixed large constant, we simply say X is a Katz-Tao (R^{-1},s)-set.
Lemma 9.3.
Let X\subset B_{R} be a union of unit balls such that the R^{-1}-dilate of X is a Katz-Tao (R^{-1},s)-set.
Then for all \varepsilon>0,
(81)
\|Ef\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\leq C_{\varepsilon}R^{\varepsilon}|X|^{\frac{s}{s+1}}\|f\|_{2}^{2}.
Consequently, we obtain
(82)
\|Ef\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\leq C_{\varepsilon}R^{\varepsilon}|X|^{\frac{s}{s+1}}R^{-1}\|Ef\|_{L^{2}(B(0,2R))}^{2}.
Proof.
The proof relies on the standard broad-narrow reduction and the bilinear restriction theorem.
See [23] for a similar argument.
For a given \varepsilon in (81), pick K=R^{\varepsilon^{2}} and A=8 in Definition 9.1.
Clearly, we have
(83)
\|Ef\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\lesssim\sum_{\sigma}\int_{X}|Ef_{\sigma}|^{2}+K^{O(1)}\int_{X}|\text{Br}_{8}Ef|^{2}.
For a K^{-1}-interval \sigma\subset[-1,1] centered at \xi_{\sigma}, let \mathcal{L}_{\sigma} be the parabolic rescaling
(84)
\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}(x_{1},x_{2})=(K^{-1}(x_{1}+2x_{2}\xi_{\sigma}),K^{-2}x_{2}).
We distinguish two cases.
Case 1.
Suppose the first term in (83) dominates.
Fix \sigma. Let \mathcal{B}_{\sigma} be a family of finite-overlapping K\times K^{2}-rectangles oriented with direction \sigma that forms a cover of X.
By dyadic pigeonholing, there exist m\in[1,K^{3}] and a set \mathcal{B}_{\sigma,m}\subset\mathcal{B}_{\sigma} such that the following is true:
(1)
For all B_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{B}_{\sigma,m}, |X\cap B_{\sigma}|\sim m.
(2)
We have, since |Ef_{\sigma}| is essentially constant on each B_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{B}_{\sigma,m},
\int_{X}|Ef_{\sigma}|^{2}\approx\int_{X\cap(\bigcup_{\mathcal{B}_{\sigma,s}}B)}|Ef_{\sigma}|^{2}\lesssim mK^{-3}\int_{\bigcup_{\mathcal{B}_{\sigma,m}}B}|Ef_{\sigma}|^{2}.
Thus, either we can easily conclude the lemma, or by dyadic pigeonholing, there exists a union of K\times K^{2}-rectangles \tilde{X}_{\sigma}\subset\bigcup_{\mathcal{B}_{\sigma,m}} so that
(1)
\|Ef_{\sigma}\|_{L^{2}(B_{\sigma})} are about the same for all K\times K^{2}-rectangles B_{\sigma}\subset\tilde{X}_{\sigma}.
(2)
We have
(85)
\int_{X}|Ef_{\sigma}|^{2}\lessapprox mK^{-3}\int_{\tilde{X}_{\sigma}}|Ef_{\sigma}|^{2}.
For any r\in[1,R/K^{2}], let Q_{r} be an rK\times rK^{2}-rectangle oriented with direction \sigma. Since the R^{-1}-dilate of X is a Katz-Tao (R^{-1},s)-set, we know that
|X\cap Q_{r}|=\sum_{B_{r}\subset Q_{r}}|X\cap B_{r}|\lesssim K(Kr)^{s},
where \{B_{r}\} are finite-overlapping rK-balls.
As a result,
(86)
\#\{B_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{B}_{\sigma,m}:B_{\sigma}\subset Q_{r}\}\lesssim K^{1+s}r^{s}m^{-1}.
By random sampling, there exists a union of K\times K^{2}-rectangles \tilde{X}_{\sigma}^{\prime}\subset\tilde{X}_{\sigma} such that
(1)
|\tilde{X}_{\sigma}^{\prime}|\approx mK^{-(1+s)}|\tilde{X}_{\sigma}|.
(2)
Recall (84).
Inside a unit ball, the (R/K^{2})^{-1}-dilate of \mathcal{L}_{\sigma}(\tilde{X}_{\sigma}^{\prime}) is a Katz-Tao ((R/K^{2})^{-1},s)-set.
Let g(\xi)=f\big(K^{-1}(\xi-\xi_{\sigma})\big), where \xi_{\sigma} is the center of \sigma.
Put the above information back into (85) so that
(87)
\int_{X}|Ef_{\sigma}|^{2}\approx K^{s-2}\int_{\tilde{X}_{\sigma}^{\prime}}|Ef_{\sigma}|^{2}=K^{s-1}\int_{\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}(\tilde{X}_{\sigma}^{\prime})}|Eg|^{2}.
Since |\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}(\tilde{X}_{\sigma}^{\prime})|=K^{-(1+s)}|X|, apply (81) at scale R/K^{2} to get
(88)
\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}(\tilde{X}_{\sigma}^{\prime})}|Eg|^{2}
\displaystyle\leq C_{\varepsilon}(R/K^{2})^{\varepsilon}|\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}(\tilde{X}_{\sigma}^{\prime})|^{\frac{s}{s+1}}\|g\|_{2}^{2}
\displaystyle=K^{-2\varepsilon}K^{1-s}\cdot C_{\varepsilon}R^{\varepsilon}|X|^{\frac{s}{s+1}}\|f_{\sigma}\|_{2}^{2}.
Put (88) and (87) back to (83) and sum over \sigma to obtain
\|Ef\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\lessapprox K^{-2\varepsilon}C_{\varepsilon}R^{\varepsilon}{|X|^{\frac{s}{s+1}}}\sum_{\sigma}\|f_{\sigma}\|_{2}^{2}\leq C_{\varepsilon}R^{\varepsilon}{|X|^{\frac{s}{s+1}}}\|f\|_{2}^{2}.
Case 2. Suppose the second term in (83) dominates.
Then, by Definition 9.1,
\|Ef\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\lesssim K^{O(1)}\|\mathrm{Br}_{8}Ef\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\lesssim R^{O(\varepsilon^{2})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\Sigma,\\
\textup{dist}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\geq K^{-1}\end{subarray}}\int_{X}|Ef_{\sigma_{1}}Ef_{\sigma_{2}}|.
Via Cauchy-Schwarz, we have
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\Sigma,\\
\textup{dist}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\geq K^{-1}\end{subarray}}\int_{X}|Ef_{\sigma_{1}}Ef_{\sigma_{2}}|\lesssim|X|^{1/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\Sigma,\\
\textup{dist}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\geq K^{-1}\end{subarray}}\Big(\int_{X}|Ef_{\sigma_{1}}Ef_{\sigma_{2}}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2},
which, by using the bilinear restriction theorem, is bounded above by
K^{O(1)}|X|^{1/2}\sum_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\Sigma}\|f_{\sigma_{1}}\|_{2}\|f_{\sigma_{2}}\|_{2}\lesssim K^{O(1)}|X|^{1/2}\|f\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim K^{O(1)}|X|^{{\frac{s}{s+1}}}\|f\|_{2}^{2}.
Since K=R^{\varepsilon^{2}}, the above calculations show that
\|Ef\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\lesssim R^{O(\varepsilon^{2})}|X|^{{\frac{s}{s+1}}}\|f\|_{2}^{2}.\qed
