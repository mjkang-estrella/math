Abstract
In this paper we construct smooth, non-radial solutions of the defocusing nonlinear Schrödinger equation that develop an imploding finite time singularity, both in the periodic setting and the full space.

1 Introduction
1.1 Historical Background
We consider the defocusing nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) equation given by
i\partial_{t}v+\Delta v-v|v|^{p-1}=0
(1.1)
for some odd integer p . Here v is a complex valued function. Other than the mass, which is the L^{2} norm square of v , the Hamiltonian given by
E(v)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla v|^{2}+\frac{1}{p+1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{p+1}
is also conserved.
The natural scaling of this equation is given by v_{\lambda}(x,t)=\lambda^{2/(p+1)}v(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t) , and if v is a solution, then v_{\lambda} is also a solution for any \lambda>0 . Note that if we consider the periodic setting, i.e. the space variable x belongs to a certain torus {\mathbb{T}}^{d} , then the torus needs to be rescaled appropriately. The critical exponent s_{c} for which the homogeneous Sobolev norm \dot{H}^{s_{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}) remains invariant under scaling is given by s_{c}=\frac{d}{2}-\frac{2}{p-1} . The goal of this paper is to show that even in the defocusing case, we can exhibit non-radial or periodic blowup for the initial value problem associated to equation (1.1) in the energy supercritical regime, namely when the initial data is in H^{s} and s>s_{c}>1 .
Before stating precisely the main results, we will touch upon the history of well-posedness and blow up for the initial value problem (1.1) both in \mathbb{R}^{d} and {\mathbb{T}}^{d} . At this point the literature is vast hence here we will recall only the few works that are more strictly related to the main results we present below.
Let us first review the results in \mathbb{R}^{d} . In the subcritical case s>s_{c} , local well-posedness was originally proved by Ginibre and Velo [29] using Strichartz estimates, see also [11, 10]. Since in this case the proof shows that the time of existence depends on the H^{s} norm of the initial data, for s\leq 0 , using the mass conservation, or for s\leq 1 , using conservation of the Hamiltonian,
by iteration global well-posedness in L^{2} or H^{1} follows easily if the data are respectively in those spaces. On the other hand using the I- method, which is based on the concept of almost conserved energies, in certain cases it is possible to extend the local well-posedness to global also below the conserved integrals, see for example [15]. If s=s_{c} local well-posedness is still available via Strichartz estimates using the same techniques in the works mentioned above, but in this case the local time of existence depends also on the profile of the initial data and in order to obtain global well-posedness one needs to treat the problem not as a perturbation of the linear one, but as a truly nonlinear system. The first results in this direction were obtained for the energy critical case ( s_{c}=1 ), in the 3D radial case [3, 31]. The radial assumption was removed in [16] and in higher dimensions the problem was solved in [53, 65].
For the mass critical problem ( s_{c}=0 ), global well-posedness in the radial case was proved in [42, 62] , and the radial assumption was removed in [23, 24, 25]. When s_{c}\not\in\{0,1\} the problem is more challenging since a priori the H^{s_{c}} norm of the solution is no longer bounded. First in [40] in 3D and later in [49, 67] in all dimensions, the authors proved that if one assumes the uniform boundedness of the H^{s_{c}} norm of the solutions then global well-posedness follows. Recently in [26] it was shown that global well-posedness and scattering is available without the a-priori bound in H^{s_{c}} . However the data belongs not to H^{s_{c}} but to a certain Besov space that scales like H^{s_{c}} .
We now address the global well-posedness in supercritical spaces, namely when s<s_{c} . Before the work of [46] there was strong belief that also in the energy supercritical regime, s_{c}>1 , the a priori boundedness of the H^{s_{c}} norm should result in global well-posedness. This in fact was conjectured in [4], see also [41, 43]. Numerically such a conjecture was also supported in [17]. In contrast, in [60, 61] models of equation very close to NLS or wave equations at supercritical regimes were proved to exhibit blow-up, while other initial value problem of NLS or wave type with only a logarithmic supercriticality were proved to have global large solutions [58, 44, 18]. Finally, results of norm inflation were obtained for very low supercritical regularity such as in [14, 63].
We now consider well-posedness in the periodic setting. Before the proof of Strichartz estimates by Bourgain in [2] local well-posedness was only known for smooth data, i.e. s>d/2 , [39, 64]. Strichartz estimates, first partially proved for rational tori [2] and later for all tori [5], allowed for local well-posedness to be proved for subcritical cases s>s_{c} . Also in this case global well-posedness follows if s is below the mass or energy regularity by iteration as in \mathbb{R}^{d} . In some cases the I- method is still applicable [19, 54] and if the torus is irrational some number theoretical observation can be used to attain global well-posedness for the same range of exponents that gives local well-posedness [33]. Unfortunately at the critical level s=s_{c} the Strichartz estimates have a derivative \log loss and even local well-posedness is distinctly harder to obtain. One such example is the energy critical problem in 3D (hence quintic nonlinearity) proved to be first locally well-posed in [34] and then globally well-posed in [36]. When the critical exponent s_{c}\not\in\{0,1\} a conditional result similar to that of [41, 43] has been proved in 2D for s_{c}=1/2 in [66].
To the best of our knowledge, before our work there were no results or conjecture concerning blow up for periodic supercritical defocusing initial value problems like (1.1). In fact even in the focusing case the results are very few. We recall [50] in 1D, see also [51], the numerical study in [59], and [52] where the authors extend the \log–\log blow-up dynamics in [45] to the L^{2} -critical nonlinear Schrödinger equation in a domain.
In this paper we are interested in imploding solutions. These are solutions for which the L^{\infty} norm of the function blows up as t approaches the singularity time. This is in contrast with shock solutions, where the L^{\infty} norm stays bounded and the gradient blows up, which are more ubiquitous in PDE with conservation laws such as compressible Euler or NLS. Specifically, Guderley [32] was the first to construct radial, self-similar imploding singularities for compressible Euler, though non-smooth. See also further work by Jenssen–Tsikkou
[37, 38].
Merle–Raphaël–Rodnianski–Szeftel [47] made a breakthrough by constructing the first smooth, radially imploding solutions to the compressible Euler equations. They also extended this work to create imploding singularities for both the compressible Navier-Stokes equations (with decaying density at infinity) [48], and the energy-supercritical defocusing nonlinear Schrödinger equation [46]. More specifically, they proved that there exists a sequence of scalings r_{k} and a sequence of smooth self-similar imploding profiles with such scalings. This was done for almost every value of \gamma (the adiabatic exponent relating the pressure to the density). In [7] (see also the review paper [6]), the first two authors together with Buckmaster improved the result to cover all cases of \gamma . Moreover, they showed the existence of non-decaying imploding singularities for Navier-Stokes. One of the open questions that Merle–Raphaël–Rodnianski–Szeftel highlight is the construction of nonradial blow-up since their setting only allows for radial perturbations. In [9], we solved this problem. The difficulty introduced by the nonradiality is deep since it essentially turns every ODE analysis into PDE analysis, which is a lot harder. After our work,
Chen–Cialdea–Shkoller–Vicol [13] constructed solutions to the 2D compressible Euler equation for which vorticity exhibits finite time blow-up. Chen [12] extended this analysis to any d\geq 2 . In the context of other equations, Shao–Wei–Zhang [55, 56] proved the existence of imploding solutions to the nonlinear wave and the relativistic Euler equations. We also point out to the numerical work of Biasi [1].
1.2 Main result
In [9] we managed to answer a question of [48] concerning the construction of non-radial imploding solutions for compressible Euler and Navier-Stokes equations. Our goal in this paper is to extend the previous construction to the case of the NLS equation (1.1). Broadly speaking, we are able to construct finite time imploding singularities with either:
1.
smooth periodic initial data (Theorem 1.1).
2.
smooth, non-radially symmetric initial data that does not vanish at infinity (Theorem 1.2).
The proof of Theorems 1.1 and 1.2 starts with the transformation of the defocusing NLS equation into the compressible Euler equation via the Madelung transform (1.2). In order to perform the non-radial stability argument, we are inspired by our previous work [9]. We extend the new symmetry-breaking analysis that we developed there from 3D to higher dimensions and use it to perform the linearized non-radial stability estimate. This also requires a new angular repulsitivity condition on the profile to close the energy estimate.
As a byproduct, we improve and generalize the techniques used in [46]. The key part of the non-linear stability argument hinges on involving the logarithm of the density, which leads to a new simplified version of the highest-order energy estimate without derivative losses. This energy estimate is new, and is a combination of the compressible Euler energy and the Hamiltonian of the defocusing NLS. The result in the periodic setting is obtained by introducing an argument involving a refined cut-off.
1.3 Setup of the problem
The equation in hydrodynamical variables is obtained as follows. Let
v=\sqrt{\rho}e^{i\psi}.
(1.2)
From (1.1), we have that
\displaystyle i\frac{\partial_{t}\rho}{2\sqrt{\rho}}e^{i\psi}-\partial_{t}\psi\sqrt{\rho}e^{i\psi}
\displaystyle=\left(\sqrt{\rho}e^{i\psi}\right)\rho^{\frac{p-1}{2}}-\Delta(\sqrt{\rho})e^{i\psi}-\frac{\nabla\rho}{\sqrt{\rho}}i\nabla\psi e^{i\psi}-\sqrt{\rho}\mathrm{div\,}\left(i\nabla\psi e^{i\psi}\right)
\displaystyle=\sqrt{\rho}e^{i\psi}\left(\rho^{\frac{p-1}{2}}-\frac{\Delta(\sqrt{\rho})}{\sqrt{\rho}}-\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}i\nabla\psi-i\Delta\psi+|\nabla\psi|^{2}\right).
(1.3)
Now
\Delta(\sqrt{\rho})=\mathrm{div\,}\left(\frac{\nabla\rho}{2\sqrt{\rho}}\right)=\frac{\Delta\rho}{2\sqrt{\rho}}-\frac{1}{4}\frac{|\nabla\rho|^{2}}{\rho^{3/2}}.
We first divide (1.3) by e^{i\psi} . After that, since both \rho and \psi are real, we can identify real and imaginary parts, and obtain
\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\psi&=-\rho^{\frac{p-1}{2}}+\frac{1}{2\rho}\Delta\rho-\frac{1}{4}\frac{|\nabla\rho|^{2}}{\rho^{2}}-|\nabla\psi|^{2}\\
\partial_{t}\rho&=2\left(-\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla\psi-\rho\Delta\psi\right)\end{split}
(1.4)
In order to see the similarity with the compressible Euler equations, we define u=\nabla\psi and observe that the system (1.4) takes the form
\displaystyle\partial_{t}u
\displaystyle=-\nabla\left(\rho^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\right)-2u\nabla u+\nabla\left(\frac{\Delta\rho}{2\rho}-\frac{1}{4}\frac{|\nabla\rho|^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)
\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho
\displaystyle=-2u\nabla\rho-2\rho\mathrm{div\,}(u)=-2\mathrm{div\,}(u\rho)
We rescale time via \partial_{t^{\prime}}=2\partial_{t} . We have
\displaystyle\partial_{t^{\prime}}u
\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\left(\rho^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\right)-u\nabla u+\nabla\left(\frac{\Delta\rho}{4\rho}-\frac{1}{8}\frac{|\nabla\rho|^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)
\displaystyle\partial_{t^{\prime}}\rho
\displaystyle=-\mathrm{div\,}(u\rho)
which corresponds to the compressible Euler equations for \gamma=\frac{p+1}{2} and forcing \frac{1}{2}\nabla\frac{\Delta\sqrt{\rho}}{\sqrt{\rho}} (the coefficient in front the pressure term \nabla\rho^{\gamma} can also be adjusted by rescaling \rho by a constant).
We let \alpha=\frac{\gamma-1}{2}=\frac{p-1}{4} and postulate the self-similar ansatz
\displaystyle\psi=\frac{(T-t)^{\frac{2}{r}-1}}{r}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{(T-t)^{1/r}},-\frac{\log(T-t)}{r}\right),
(1.5)
\displaystyle\rho=\frac{(T-t)^{\frac{1}{\alpha r}-\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r}P\left(\frac{x}{(T-t)^{1/r}},-\frac{\log(T-t)}{r}\right)
in the new variables
s=-\frac{\log(T-t)}{r},\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad y=\frac{x}{(T-t)^{1/r}}=xe^{s}
We define accordingly
s_{0}=-\frac{\log T}{r},
which we will take to be sufficiently large and positive (that is, we will take T>0 to be sufficiently small). Our new time variable will be s\geq s_{0} and our new space variable is y\in\mathbb{R}^{d} or y\in e^{s}\mathbb{T}^{d} , corresponding to the euclidean case or periodic case respectively.
From (1.4), (1.5), we obtain
\displaystyle-(2-r)\Psi+y\nabla\Psi+\partial_{s}\Psi
\displaystyle=-r^{-2\alpha+2}P^{2\alpha}-|\nabla\Psi|^{2}+(T-t)^{2-\frac{4}{r}}\left(\frac{\Delta P}{2P}-\frac{|\nabla P|^{2}}{4P^{2}}\right)
\displaystyle-\frac{1-r}{\alpha}P+y\nabla P+\partial_{s}P
\displaystyle=-2\nabla P\cdot\nabla\Psi-2P\Delta\Psi
Therefore, we obtain
\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{s}\Psi&=(2-r)\Psi-y\nabla\Psi-|\nabla\Psi|^{2}-r^{-2\alpha+2}P^{2\alpha}+e^{(4-2r)s}\left(\frac{\Delta P}{2P}-\frac{|\nabla P|^{2}}{4P^{2}}\right)\\
\partial_{s}P&=\frac{1-r}{\alpha}P-y\nabla P-2\nabla P\cdot\nabla\Psi-2P\Delta\Psi\\
\end{split}
(1.6)
Defining
S=\frac{r^{1-\alpha}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}P^{\alpha}
(1.7)
and letting U=\nabla\Psi , we have
\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{s}\Psi&=-(r-2)\Psi-y\nabla\Psi-|\nabla\Psi|^{2}-\alpha S^{2}+e^{(4-2r)s}\frac{\Delta(S^{1/(2\alpha)})}{S^{1/(2\alpha)}}\\
\partial_{s}S&=-(r-1)S-y\nabla S-2\nabla S\cdot U-2\alpha S\text{div}(U).\end{split}
(1.8)
One can also write the equation in U,S variables, namely
\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{s}U&=-(r-1)U-y\nabla U-2U\cdot\nabla U-2\alpha S\nabla S+e^{(4-2r)s}\nabla\frac{\Delta(S^{1/(2\alpha)})}{S^{1/(2\alpha)}}\\
\partial_{s}S&=-(r-1)S-y\nabla S-2\nabla S\cdot U-2\alpha S\text{div}(U).\end{split}
(1.9)
Notice that when r>2 , (1.9) is a perturbation
of the compressible Euler equation in dimension d .
From [7, 47], we have a self-similar profile neglecting the inhomogeneous part e^{(4-2r)s}\frac{\Delta(S^{1/(2\alpha)})}{S^{1/(2\alpha)}} when 2\leq d\leq 9 .
That is, there exist S_{p} , \Psi_{p} , satisfying:
\displaystyle\begin{split}0&=-(r-2)\Psi_{p}-y\nabla\Psi_{p}-|\nabla\Psi_{p}|^{2}-\alpha S_{p}^{2}\\
0&=-(r-1)S_{p}-y\nabla S_{p}-2\nabla S_{p}\cdot\nabla\Psi_{p}-2\alpha S_{p}\Delta\Psi_{p}\end{split}
(1.10)
Let us mention that here we have an extra coefficient 2 in (1.10) compared to the equation in [7, 47], which just corresponds with a rescaling of the profiles by a factor of 2 that needs to be done in order to pass from compressible Euler to NLS. Thus, rigorously speaking, our profiles correspond to the ones from [7, 47] divided by 2 . Letting \overline{U}_{p,R}=\partial_{R}\Psi_{p} , the constructions from [7, 47] guarantee the following properties:
\displaystyle S_{p}
\displaystyle>0,
(1.11)
\displaystyle|\nabla^{j}\overline{U}_{p,R}|+|\nabla^{j}S_{p}|
\displaystyle\lesssim\langle R\rangle^{-(r-1)-j},\quad\forall j\geq 0,\quad\mbox{ and }\quad S_{p}\gtrsim\langle R\rangle^{-r+1}.
(1.12)
\displaystyle\partial_{R}S_{p}(0)
\displaystyle=0,
(1.13)
\displaystyle R+2\overline{U}_{p,R}-2\alpha S_{p}
\displaystyle=0\text{ when }R=1.
(1.14)
Moreover, the profiles satisfy repulsivity properties. That is, there exists \widetilde{\eta}>0 such that:
\displaystyle 1+2\partial_{R}\overline{U}_{p,R}-2\alpha|\partial_{R}S_{p}|
\displaystyle>\widetilde{\eta},
(1.15)
\displaystyle 1+2\frac{\overline{U}_{p,R}}{R}-2\alpha|\partial_{R}S_{p}|
\displaystyle>\widetilde{\eta},
(1.16)
The properties (1.11)-(1.14) follow from the construction of the profiles easily, and we refer to [47, 7] for their justifications. The property (1.15) is essential for the radial stability and also proved in those papers. The property (1.16) is needed for the non-radial stability and requires substantial amount of work. We prove it in Lemma 4.3 in the Appendix, for the choice (d,p)=(8,3) . We remark that the constants 2 disappear in properties (1.14)–(1.16) in the previous literature [47, 7, 9], due to the fact that the profiles are multiplied by 2 with respect to our current notation (in particular, those profiles satisfy a rescaled version of (1.9) without the factors 2 in front of the nonlinear terms).
Theorem 1.1.
Let \Psi_{p},S_{p} be self-similar profiles solving (1.10) for (d,p)=(8,3) and satisfying (1.11)–(1.14), for some r>2 . Let T>0 sufficiently small.
Then, there exists C^{\infty} initial data v_{0} for which equation (1.1) on \mathbb{T}^{d} blows up at time T in a self-similar manner. More concretely, for any fixed y\in\mathbb{T}^{d} , v(y,t)=\sqrt{\rho}(y,t)e^{i\Psi(y,t)} with \rho\in\mathbb{R}^{+} , \Psi\in\mathbb{R} , we have:
\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow T^{-}}r(T-t)^{1-\frac{2}{r}}\Psi\left((T-t)^{\frac{1}{r}}y,t\right)
\displaystyle=\Psi_{p}(|y|),
\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow T^{-}}\left(\alpha^{-1}r(T-t)^{1-\frac{1}{r}}\right)^{1/\alpha}\rho\left((T-t)^{\frac{1}{r}}y,t\right)
\displaystyle=S_{p}(|y|)^{1/\alpha}\,.
Moreover, there exists a finite codimension set of initial data satisfying the above conclusions.
Theorem 1.2.
Let \Psi_{p},S_{p} be self-similar profiles solving (1.10) for (d,p)=(8,3) and satisfying (1.11)–(1.14), for some r>2 . Let T>0 sufficiently small.
Then, there exists C^{\infty} initial data v_{0} for which equation (1.1) on \mathbb{R}^{d} blows up at time T in a self-similar manner. More concretely, for any fixed y\in\mathbb{R}^{d} , v(y,t)=\sqrt{\rho}(y,t)e^{i\Psi(y,t)} with \rho\in\mathbb{R}^{+} , \Psi\in\mathbb{R} , we have:
\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow T^{-}}r(T-t)^{1-\frac{2}{r}}\Psi\left((T-t)^{\frac{1}{r}}y,t\right)
\displaystyle=\Psi_{p}(|y|),
\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow T^{-}}\left(\alpha^{-1}r(T-t)^{1-\frac{1}{r}}\right)^{1/\alpha}\rho\left((T-t)^{\frac{1}{r}}y,t\right)
\displaystyle=S_{p}(|y|)^{1/\alpha}\,.
Moreover, there exists a finite codimension set of initial data satisfying the above conclusions.
Remark 1.3.
For simplicity, we have chosen to fix a particular instance of (d,p) . In principle, one could prove a similar result for other values of (d,p) using a similar study of the angular repulsivity property, and even a larger class by means of a computer-assisted proof as in [7].
Remark 1.4.
The Sobolev norm \|\nabla^{s}v\|_{L^{2}} blows up in both \mathbb{R}^{d} or \mathbb{T}^{d} when s>\frac{d}{2(r-1)}-\frac{2}{p-1}. In fact we have
\displaystyle\quad\|\nabla^{s}(\sqrt{\rho}e^{i\psi})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
\displaystyle\geq\int_{|x|\leq(T-t)^{\frac{1}{2}}}|\nabla^{s}(\sqrt{\rho}e^{i\psi})^{2}|dx
\displaystyle\gtrsim\int_{|y|\leq 1}|(T-t)^{\frac{1}{2\alpha r}-\frac{1}{2\alpha}}(T-t)^{\frac{-s}{r}}|^{2}|\nabla_{y}^{s}(\sqrt{P(y)}e^{i\Psi(y)(T-t)^{\frac{2}{r}-1}})|^{2}dy(T-t)^{\frac{d}{r}}
\displaystyle\gtrsim(T-t)^{\frac{1}{\alpha r}-\frac{1}{\alpha}-\frac{2s}{r}+\frac{d}{r}+2s(\frac{2}{r}-1)}
\displaystyle=(T-t)^{\frac{1}{\alpha r}-\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{d}{r}-2s(1-\frac{1}{r})}.
Then when s>\frac{\frac{1}{\alpha r}-\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{d}{t}}{2(1-\frac{1}{r})}=\frac{d}{2(r-1)}-\frac{2}{p-1},
we have blow up of the \dot{H}^{s} norm.
Remark 1.5.
In recent years several works have been put forward in which
it was proved that when one studies the long time dynamics of a periodic NLS
the nature of the periodicity, namely the rationality or irrationality11
1
We say that a torus {\mathbb{T}}^{d} of periods T_{1},T_{2},\dots T_{d} is irrational if there is no non-zero vector \vec{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d} such that \sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}T_{i}=0 . of the domain {\mathbb{T}}^{d} , matters, see [33, 20, 22, 21, 57, 28, 30, 8, 35]. This physically makes sense since as time evolves the nonlinear solution starts seeing the different effects of the periodic boundary conditions. In our case, as one can gather from the conclusion of Theorem 1.1, we do not distinguish between a rational or irrational torus {\mathbb{T}}^{d} . This is because the blow up result we prove is localized and not close to the boundary. Of course we do not exclude that there could be other types of blow up mechanism that indeed are sensitive to the nature of the periodicity of the boundary, but the study of this phenomenon is not the scope of the present paper.
1.4 Structure of the proof of Theorems 1.1 and 1.2
We define the smooth cut-off function,
\widehat{\mathfrak{X}}_{d}(x)=1\text{ when }|x|\leq\frac{1}{2},\quad\widehat{\mathfrak{X}}_{d}(x)=0,\text{ when }|x|\geq\frac{2}{3}.
(1.17)
\widetilde{\mathfrak{X}}_{d}(x)=0\text{ when }|x|\leq\frac{1}{8},\quad\widetilde{\mathfrak{X}}_{d}(x)=1,\text{ when }|x|\geq\frac{1}{4}.
(1.18)
With n_{d} sufficiently large, we define
\mathfrak{X}_{d}(x)=1\text{ when }|x|\leq\frac{1}{2},\quad\mathfrak{X}_{d}(x)=\langle x\rangle^{-n_{d}},\text{ when }|x|\geq\frac{2}{3}.
(1.19)
For the periodic case, for the sake of simplicity, we set the torus be [-1,1]^{d} . Tori of other size/shape can be treated similarly. In order to fit the periodic boundary condition, we define the damped profile
as follows:
\Psi_{d}(y)=\Psi_{p}(y)\widehat{\mathfrak{X}}_{d}\left(\frac{y}{e^{s}}\right)
(1.20)
S_{d}(y)=S_{p}(y)\widehat{\mathfrak{X}}_{d}\left(\frac{y}{e^{s}}\right)+e^{-(r-1)s}\widetilde{\mathfrak{X}}_{d}\left(\frac{y}{e^{s}}\right)
(1.21)
For the whole space y\in\mathbb{R}^{d} , notice that from (1.12) and (1.2), the corresponding solution of (1.1) has infinite Hamiltonian and mass. We can use a similar damped profile as in [46, 7]:
S_{d}(y)=S_{p}(y)\mathfrak{X}_{d}\left(\frac{y}{e^{s}}\right).
(1.22)
\Psi_{d}(y)=\Psi_{p}(y).
(1.23)
Let us remark here that the factor e^{s} is not zero at our initial time s_{0} . Thus, one can think of \mathfrak{X}_{d}\left(\frac{y}{e^{s}}\right) as \mathfrak{X}_{d}\left(\frac{y}{Le^{s-s_{0}}}\right) for some L sufficiently large. Both expressions are equivalent by taking L=e^{s_{0}} , and s_{0} will also be sufficiently large in our argument (we will start sufficiently close to the singularity time).
Remark 1.6.
From now on our proof will focus on the periodic case and use (1.21), (1.20) as damped profile. Our proof can be easily adapted to the whole space.
We then define the perturbations of the damped profile:
\widetilde{S}(y,s)=S(y,s)-S_{d}(y,s),\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\widetilde{\Psi}(y,s)=\Psi(y,s)-\Psi_{d}(y).
(1.24)
We obtain, from (1.6) and (1.10), that the perturbations \widetilde{S},\widetilde{\Psi} satisfy the equations:
\displaystyle\partial_{s}\widetilde{\Psi}
\displaystyle=\underbrace{-(r-2)\widetilde{\Psi}-y\nabla\widetilde{\Psi}-2\nabla\Psi_{p}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\Psi}-2\alpha S_{d}\widetilde{S}}_{\mathcal{L}_{\Psi}}\underbrace{-\alpha\widetilde{S}^{2}-|\nabla\widetilde{\Psi}|^{2}}_{\mathcal{N}_{\Psi}}
(1.25)

1.5 Organization of the paper
The paper is organized as follows: Section 2 is devoted to the study of the linearized operator around S_{d},\Psi_{d} , and it consists of the proof of Propositions 2.1, 2.3 and Lemma 2.2, concerning maximality, smoothness of the unstable modes and dissipativity of the linear operator. Section 3 upgrades the linear estimates to nonlinear, bootstrapping the bounds of the perturbation. Finally, the Appendix contains a proof of property (1.16) for the globally self-similar solution, as well as some other properties of the solution and some functional analytic results.
1.6 Notation
Throughout the paper we will use the following convention:
We will use C_{x} (resp. C ) to denote any constant that may depend on x (resp. independent of all the other parameters). These constants may change from line to line but they are uniformly bounded by a universal constant dependent on x (independent of all other parameters). Similarly, we will denote by
X\lesssim Y and by
X\lesssim_{x}Y whenever X\leq CY and X\leq C_{x}Y for some C , C_{x} respectively.
We use \nabla^{l}f for the l -th order tensor containing all l order derivatives of f . In the case where f is a vector (typically f=U ) then \nabla^{l}U is a (l+1) -th tensor. We also denote by |\nabla^{k}f| its \ell^{2} norm. Note that this norm controls all the derivatives accounting for reorderings of indices. For example, we have
|\nabla^{j}f|^{2}=\sum_{\beta\in[3]^{j}}|\partial_{\beta}f|^{2}\geq\binom{j}{j^{\prime}}|\partial_{1}^{j^{\prime}}\partial_{2}^{j-j^{\prime}}f|^{2}.
Thus, we see that |\nabla^{j}f| has a stronger control of the mixed derivatives by a combinatorial number, just because any mixed derivative appears multiple times in \nabla^{j}f as the indices are reordered.
For \beta=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2}...,\beta_{K}) , we let \partial_{\beta}=\partial_{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}...\beta_{K}} , and \partial_{\beta^{(j)}}=\partial_{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},...\beta_{j-1},\beta_{j+1}....\beta_{K}} (that is, \beta_{j} denotes the j -th component of \beta and \beta^{(j)} denotes the subindex obtained by erasing the j -th component in \beta ).
We will use y as our self-similar variable and we will denote R=|y| for the radius (given that r is reserved, since 1/r is the self-similar exponent of our ansatz). We will denote with \widehat{R} the unitary vector field in the radial direction, that is, \widehat{R}=\frac{y}{|y|}=\frac{y}{R} .

2 Linear estimates
2.1 Linear estimates for the truncated operator
This part is very similar as in [9, Section 2] except for the fact that we work with \nabla\Psi instead of U . We consider \chi_{1}(x),\chi_{2}(x) to be cut-offs with value 1 near the origin and transition regions [6/5,7/5] and [8/5,9/5] respectively. Notice that in the support of \chi_{1} and \chi_{2} , as long as s_{0} is sufficiently large, we have (\Psi_{p},S_{p})=(\Psi_{d},S_{d}) .
Figure 1: The graph of J(1-\chi_{1}) and \chi_{2} .
Using those cut-offs, we define the cut-off linear operator as:
\mathcal{L}_{\Psi,t}=\chi_{2}(\frac{y}{C_{0}})\mathcal{L}_{\Psi}-J(1-\chi_{1}(\frac{y}{C_{0}})),\quad\mathcal{L}_{S,t}=\chi_{2}(\frac{y}{C_{0}})\mathcal{L}_{S}-J(1-\chi_{1}(\frac{y}{C_{0}})),
(2.1)
where J,C_{0} are sufficiently large constant.
We will study the cut-off linearized operator \mathcal{L}_{t}=(\mathcal{L}_{\Psi,t},\mathcal{L}_{S,t}) in the space X , which we define as follows. First, we consider \widetilde{X} to be the space H^{m+1}_{\Psi}(Q,\mathbb{R})\times H^{m}_{s}(Q,\mathbb{R}) where the \Psi,s subscripts make reference to \widetilde{\Psi} (vector field) and \widetilde{S} (scalar field). Q is a cube centered at the origin of length 4C_{0} and we take periodic boundary conditions. We select m to be a sufficiently large integer. We define X to be the subspace of \widetilde{X} formed by (\Psi,S)\in\widetilde{X} such that both U and S are compactly supported on B(0,3C_{0}) . One can thus think of X as a subspace of H^{m+1}_{\Psi}(B(0,3C_{0}),\mathbb{R})\times H^{m}_{s}(B(0,3C_{0}),\mathbb{R}) with the appropriate vanishing conditions at the boundary. Whenever we take Fourier series of (\Psi,S)\in X it should be understood that we do Fourier series on the cube Q .
We put in our space X the standard inner product
\langle(f_{\Psi},f_{s}),(g_{\Psi},g_{s})\rangle_{X}=\int_{B(0,3C_{0})}\left(\nabla^{m+1}f_{\Psi}\cdot\nabla^{m+1}g_{\Psi}+\nabla^{m}f_{s}\cdot\nabla^{m}g_{s}+f_{\Psi}\cdot g_{\Psi}+f_{s}g_{s}\right)
and we obtain the inherited norm
\|(f_{\Psi},f_{s})\|_{X}^{2}=\int_{B(0,3C_{0})}\left(|\nabla^{m+1}f_{\Psi}|^{2}+(\nabla^{m}f_{s})^{2}+|f_{\Psi}|^{2}+f_{s}^{2}\right),
(2.2)
where \nabla^{m}f_{s} is an m -tensor indexed by [d]^{m}=\{1,2,...d\}^{m} containing all possible combinations of directional derivatives. In the case of \nabla^{m+1}f_{\Psi} , we have an (m+1) -tensor since we have an extra index indicating the component of the vector f_{\Psi} .
Similar as in the [9], we have finite-dimension unstable space (Proposition 2.1) and smoothness of the eigenvectors in the unstable space (Proposition 2.3).
Proposition 2.1 ([9, Proposition 1.8]).
For \delta_{g} sufficiently small, m sufficiently large and J defined in (2.1) sufficiently large, the following holds. The Hilbert space X can be decomposed as V_{\rm{sta}}\oplus V_{\rm{uns}} , where both V_{\rm{sta}},V_{\rm{uns}} are invariant subspaces of \mathcal{L}_{t} . V_{\rm{uns}} is finite dimensional and formed by smooth functions. Moreover, there exists a metric B of V_{uns} such that the decomposition satisfies:
\displaystyle\begin{split}\Re\langle\mathcal{L}_{t}v,v\rangle_{B}&\geq\frac{-6}{10}\delta_{g}\|v\|_{B}^{2},\qquad\forall v\in V_{\rm{uns}},\\
\left\|e^{s\mathcal{L}_{t}}v\right\|_{X}&\leq e^{-s\delta_{g}/2}\|v\|_{X},\qquad\forall v\in V_{\rm{sta}},\end{split}
(2.3)
where we use \Re to denote the real part.
Proof.
The proof of Proposition 2.1 is the same as in [9]. It follows from the dissipativity and maximality of \mathcal{L}_{t} in lemma 2.2.

Let X_{K} be the finite codimension subspace of X where the Fourier modes smaller than K vanish, that is
\mathcal{F}(\Psi)(k)=0,\quad\mathcal{F}(S)(k)=0\text{ for }|k|<K.
We have the following lemma:
Lemma 2.2 (Dissipativity and Maximality).
Taking K sufficiently large in terms of J , and J large enough depending on m , there is a universal constant C (independent of m ) such that
\int_{B(0,3C_{0})}\nabla^{m}\mathcal{L}_{\Psi,t}\cdot\nabla^{m}\widetilde{\Psi}+\nabla^{m}\mathcal{L}_{S,t}\cdot\nabla^{m}\widetilde{S}\leq-\|(\widetilde{\Psi},\widetilde{S})\|_{X}^{2},\qquad\forall(\widetilde{\Psi},\widetilde{S})\in X_{K}.
(2.4)
Moreover, there exists some \lambda (depending on m , K and J ) such that \mathcal{L}-\lambda:X\to X is a surjective operator.
Proof.
Here (\nabla\widetilde{\Psi},\widetilde{S}) corresponds to (\widetilde{U},\widetilde{S}) in [9] with the properties of profile (1.16) and (1.15). The only difference is that, since we replace \widetilde{U} by \nabla\widetilde{\Psi} , the cut-off in the definition of \mathcal{L}_{t} (2.1) is performed on (\widetilde{\Psi},\widetilde{S}) instead of (\widetilde{U},\widetilde{S}) .
Both the dissipativity and maximality work in a similar way since the change does not effect the leading order estimate ( \dot{H}^{m+1}_{\Psi}(Q)\times\dot{H}^{m}_{S}(Q) ). All the terms where derivatives enter the cut-off are bounded as error terms, so it is irrelevant if the cut-off is performed at the level of \nabla\widetilde{\Psi} or \widetilde{\Psi} .
The error terms are with lower derivative and can be controlled easily by taking K sufficiently large.

2.2 The truncated equation and extended equation
In order to choose the initial data of equation (1.25), let
\displaystyle\begin{cases}\widetilde{\Psi}_{0}=\widetilde{\Psi}_{0}^{{}^{\prime}}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}v_{i,\psi}\\

(2.5)
where

(2.6)
and (v_{i,u},v_{i,s}) is an orthogonal and normal basis of V_{\rm{uns}} . Here {a_{i}} is an N -dimensional vector to be fixed later.
In order to use the linear estimate (Proposition 2.1), we define a truncated equation of (1.25) as:
\begin{cases}\partial_{s}\Psi_{t}=\chi_{2}\left(\frac{y}{C_{0}}\right)\mathcal{L}_{\Psi}(\Psi_{t},S_{t})-J\left(1-\chi_{1}\left(\frac{y}{C_{0}}\right)\right)\Psi_{t}+\chi_{2}\left(\frac{y}{C_{0}}\right)\mathcal{F}_{\Psi}\\
\partial_{s}S_{t}=\chi_{2}\left(\frac{y}{C_{0}}\right)\mathcal{L}_{S}(\Psi_{t},S_{t})-J\left(1-\chi_{1}\left(\frac{y}{C_{0}}\right)\right)S_{t}+\chi_{2}\left(\frac{y}{C_{0}}\right)\mathcal{F}_{S},\\
(\Psi_{t}(y,s_{0}),S_{t}(y,s_{0}))=(\Psi_{t,0},S_{t,0})=\chi_{2}\left(\frac{y}{C_{0}}\right)(\widetilde{\Psi}^{{}^{\prime}}_{0},\widetilde{S}^{{}^{\prime}}_{0})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}(v_{i,\psi},v_{i,s}),\end{cases}
(2.7)
where \mathcal{F}_{\Psi}=\mathcal{N}_{\Psi}+\mathcal{E}_{\Psi}+e^{(4-2r)s}\mathcal{D} and \mathcal{F}_{S}=\mathcal{N}_{S}+\mathcal{E}_{S} are the corresponding terms in (1.25). Notice that \chi_{2}(\frac{y}{C_{0}})\mathcal{E}_{S} and \chi_{2}(\frac{y}{C_{0}})\mathcal{E}_{\Psi} vanishes because both terms vanishes in the support of \chi_{2}(\frac{y}{C_{0}}). Here we have
P_{\rm{sta}}(\Psi_{t,0},S_{t,0})=\chi_{2}\left(\frac{y}{C_{0}}\right)(\widetilde{\Psi}^{{}^{\prime}}_{0},\widetilde{S}^{{}^{\prime}}_{0}),\quad P_{\rm{uns}}(\Psi_{t,0},S_{t,0})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}(v_{i,\psi},v_{i,s}).
P_{\rm{sta}} and P_{\rm{uns}} are the projections to the stable and unstable spaces of \mathcal{L}_{t} that appears in 2.1. Let us mention that the initial data (\Psi_{t,0},S_{t,0}) is compactly supported since the unstable modes will consist on finitely many compactly supported functions (and therefore P_{\rm{uns}} is always compactly supported).
Let us also note that the truncated equation is not a closed equation. On the contrary, one needs to provide the truncated equation with the forcings \mathcal{F}_{\Psi},\mathcal{F}_{S} , and in particular, to compute \mathcal{N}_{\Psi},\mathcal{N}_{S},\mathcal{D} one needs to know \widetilde{\Psi},\widetilde{S} . Note also that the initial data of the system is the initial data of the original (extended) equation with the appropriate cut-off.
We will rewrite the truncated equation (2.7) as follows
\partial_{s}(\Psi_{t},S_{t})=\mathcal{L}_{t}(\Psi_{t},S_{t})+\mathcal{F}_{t}
(2.8)
where \mathcal{L}_{t} includes the linear operator with a cut-off and the artificial damping, while \mathcal{F} includes both coordinates of the forcing :
\mathcal{L}_{t}(\Psi_{t},S_{t})=\left(\chi_{2}\left(\frac{y}{C_{0}}\right)\mathcal{L}_{\Psi}(\Psi_{t},S_{t})-J\left(1-\chi_{1}\left(\frac{y}{C_{0}}\right)\right)\Psi_{t},\chi_{2}\left(\frac{y}{C_{0}}\right)\mathcal{L}_{S}(\Psi_{t},S_{t})-J\left(1-\chi_{1}\left(\frac{y}{C_{0}}\right)\right)S_{t}\right)
and
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\left(\chi_{2}\left(\frac{y}{C_{0}}\right)\mathcal{F}_{\Psi},\chi_{2}\left(\frac{y}{C_{0}}\right)\mathcal{F}_{S}\right).
We will study the truncated equation in the space X defined in (2.2).
This setting has the following two fundamental properties. The first one is that \mathcal{L}_{t} has finitely many unstable directions, and the second one that the truncated equation agrees with the original equation for |y|\leq C_{0} .
Now, we state a lemma describing the agreement of the truncated equation with the original system:
Lemma 2.4.
Let (\widetilde{\Psi},\widetilde{S}) be a smooth solution of the original (extended) equation (1.25) for s\in[s_{0},s_{1}] . Then, there exists a solution (\Psi_{t},S_{t}) of (2.8) for s\in[s_{0},s_{1}] . Moreover, both solutions agree for |y|\leq\frac{11}{10}C_{0} .
Proof.
The existence part follows from \mathcal{L}_{t} being a contraction semigroup modulo finitely many modes, together with the facts (\Psi_{t,0},S_{t,0})\in X and \mathcal{F}_{t}\in X .
Let us show that they agree in the indicated region. We define (\Psi^{\rm{diff}},S^{\rm{diff}})=(\widetilde{\Psi},\widetilde{S})-(\Psi_{t},S_{t}) . Since the forcing of the truncated equation is computed from the extended equation, that term cancels, and one obtains
\partial_{s}(\Psi^{\rm{diff}},S^{\rm{diff}})=\chi_{2}\left(\frac{y}{C_{0}}\right)\mathcal{L}(\Psi^{\rm{diff}},S^{\rm{diff}})+\left(1-\chi_{2}\left(\frac{y}{C_{0}}\right)\right)\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{\Psi},\widetilde{S})+J\left(1-\chi_{1}\left(\frac{y}{C_{0}}\right)\right).
If we restrict ourselves to |y|\leq\frac{11}{10}C_{0} , the last two terms are zero due to the cut-offs, and in that region, we obtain
\begin{cases}\partial_{s}\Psi^{\rm{diff}}&=-(r-2)\Psi^{\rm{diff}}-y\nabla\Psi^{\rm{diff}}-2\nabla\Psi_{p}\cdot\nabla\Psi^{\rm{diff}}-2\alpha S_{d}S^{\rm{diff}}\\
\partial_{s}S^{\rm{diff}}&=-(r-1)S^{\rm{diff}}-y\nabla S^{\rm{diff}}-2\nabla\Psi_{p}\cdot\nabla S^{\rm{diff}}-2\alpha S_{d}\Delta\Psi^{\rm{diff}}-2\nabla S_{d}\cdot\nabla\Psi^{\rm{diff}}-2\alpha S^{\rm{diff}}\Delta\Psi_{p}\end{cases}
(2.9)
Furthermore, we can perform an energy estimates in the equation above and defining E^{\rm{diff}}=\int_{|y|\leq\frac{11C_{0}}{10}}|\nabla\Psi^{\rm{diff}}|^{2}+|S^{\rm{diff}}|^{2} we get that
\displaystyle\partial_{s}E^{\rm{diff}}
\displaystyle\leq CE^{\rm{diff}}-\int_{|y|=11C_{0}/10}\widehat{R}\left(\frac{y}{2}+\nabla\Psi_{p}\right)\left(|\nabla\Psi^{\rm{diff}}|^{2}+|S^{\rm{diff}}|^{2}\right)-2\alpha\int_{|y|=11C_{0}/10}S_{d}\cdot\left(S^{\rm{diff}}\nabla\Psi^{\rm{diff}}\right)
\displaystyle\leq CE^{\rm{diff}}-\int_{|y|=11C_{0}/10}\left(\frac{|y|}{2}+\partial_{R}\Psi_{p}-\alpha|S_{d}|\right)\left(|\nabla\Psi^{\rm{diff}}|^{2}+|S^{\rm{diff}}|^{2}\right).
Properties (1.15) and (1.14) ensure that g(y)=|y|/2+\partial_{R}\Psi_{p}-\alpha|S_{p}| is increasing and g(1)=0 . Therefore, g(11C_{0}/10)>0 if we ensure C_{0}>1 . Taking C_{0}<\frac{1}{10}e^{s_{0}} , we also have that |S_{d}(C_{0})|=|S_{p}(C_{0})| . Therefore, as long as 1\leq C_{0}\leq\frac{1}{10}e^{s_{0}} we obtain that \partial_{s}E^{\rm{diff}}\leq CE^{\rm{diff}} and since E^{\rm{diff}} is zero at time zero (the initial data for (\widetilde{\Psi},\widetilde{S}) and (\Psi_{t},S_{t}) coincides for |y|\leq\frac{11}{10}C_{0} ), we conclude that E^{\rm{diff}}=0 . This implies that \nabla\Psi_{t}=\nabla\widetilde{\Psi} and S_{t}=\widetilde{S} in this range of y . Thus, we have that for |y|\leq\frac{11}{10}C_{0} , \Psi^{\rm{diff}}=f(s) for some function f(s) (constant in space) and S^{\rm{diff}}=0 , and we just need to show f(s)=0 .
We evaluate the first equation of (2.9) at y=0 , and use that S^{\rm{diff}}(0,s)=0 with \nabla\Psi_{p}(0)=0 . We get
\partial_{s}f=-(r-2)f(s)
and since f(s_{0})=0 because of the agreement of the initial data, we conclude that f(s)=0 for all s\in[s_{0},s_{1}] .

3 Nonlinear stability
3.1 Choice of parameters
We will choose our parameters as follows:
\frac{1}{s_{0}}\ll\delta_{\rm{low}}\ll\frac{1}{E}\ll\frac{1}{k}\ll\frac{1}{m^{\prime}},\frac{1}{R_{0}}\ll b,\varepsilon\ll O(1)\ll E_{0,0},\ll E_{1,0}\ll...E_{\frac{k}{10},0}\ll E.
(3.1)
Here, b,\varepsilon are small constants that just depend on the profile and m^{\prime},R_{0} are big constants that we use in order to close a low derivative L^{2} energy estimate. This energy estimate uses the compressible Euler structure, so it is able to close by itself except for the quantum pressure term e^{(4-2r)}\mathcal{D} . However, that term carries a small constant e^{(4-2r)s_{0}} that is smaller than any other constant involved, so can be closed using higher derivative estimates where the control is much worse.
k measures the number of derivatives in the higher derivative estimates, and E bounds that norm. The precise structure for NLS requires us to consider different energies E_{l,0} at different levels of derivatives in order to close our bounds. Finally, \delta_{\rm{low}} measures the size of the initial data.
Importantly, none of those constants depends on \delta_{\rm{low}} , s_{0} , E_{l,0} .
m^{\prime} is used in the (3.6) as the order of the low derivative energy estimate.
R_{0} , b are the parameter used to define the weight \beta (3.3) and \phi (3.3).
\varepsilon is used to estimate the decay in (3.4), (3.5), (3.6).
s_{0} is the initial time taken to be sufficiently large, it can depend on all other constant.
\delta_{\rm{low}} is a sufficiently small number used in the definition of initial conditions (3.2) and bootstrap assumptions Proposition (3.1) to bound the decay.
k , E_{l,0} with 0\leq l\leq\frac{k}{10} are used in the (3.8) to control the high derivative bound.

Part I. R<1 : Right of the phase portrait.
We start showing Lemma 4.3 for \xi<0 . The proof is analogous to [9, Lemma 4.1], but with different formulas. We refer to that paper for more details. First, changing to W,Z variables and multiplying by D_{W}D_{Z} , we are left to show
N_{W}D_{Z}+N_{Z}D_{W}>0.
(4.22)
The curve solving N_{W}D_{Z}+N_{Z}D_{W}=0 inside the region D_{W}>0,D_{Z}<0 can be solved in (U,S) variables more easily as
b_{S}=2\sqrt{b_{U}+1}\sqrt{r+b_{U}}\sqrt{\frac{b_{U}}{8b_{U}+2(r-1)}},\quad\mbox{ and }\quad b_{U}\in\left(U(P_{s}),\frac{-(r-1)}{4}\right).
We have that \lim_{r\to r^{\star}}U(P_{s})=1-\sqrt{2} and U(P_{0})=\frac{-(r-1)}{4} (with limit \frac{6-5\sqrt{2}}{4}=-0.267\ldots as r\to r^{\ast} ), therefore -1<U(P_{s})<U(P_{0})<0 for r sufficiently close to r^{\ast} . We change again (b_{U},b_{S}) to W,Z coordinates and denote such curve by b . See Figure 3 for a picture of b(t) .
The approach is to show (4.22) close to P_{s} and P_{0} . Then, if (4.22) was going to fail, it would need to cross b in both directions (one to go into N_{W}D_{Z}+N_{Z}D_{W}<0 and another to come back). We will also show that the vector field (N_{W}D_{Z},N_{Z}D_{W}) along b points always to the same side of b . Then, one concludes that (4.22) always holds.
Figure 3: Phase portrait in (W,Z) coordinates for r=2.01 (with d=8,p=3 ). The brown curve represents N_{W}D_{Z}+N_{Z}D_{W}=0 and the lower branch, to the right of P_{s} , would correspond to the curve b(t) for t\geq 0 . See Figure 2 for the meaning of the rest of the colors in the plot.
Part I.1. Constant sign along b
Since N_{W}D_{Z}=-N_{Z}D_{W} along b and \nabla(N_{W}D_{Z}+N_{Z}D_{W}) is a normal vector to b , it suffices to show that
(-1,1)\cdot\nabla(N_{W}D_{Z}+N_{Z}D_{W})=-(W-Z)(-1+r+2W+2Z),
has a constant sign. We see that W-Z>0 from (4.20). Regarding the second factor note \frac{W+Z}{2}<U(P_{0})=\frac{-(r-1)}{4} , so we have that -1+r+2(W+Z)<r-1-(r-1)=0 , so it also has a constant sign.
Part I.2 Taylor analysis at P_{s} and P_{0} .
Regarding checking (4.22) at P_{s} , we need to show W_{1}+Z_{1}<0 . The proof of W_{1}+Z_{1}<0 is deferred to Lemma 4.5. Regarding checking (4.22) at P_{0} , we have that the solution near R=0 has the form:
W=\frac{w_{0}}{R}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}w_{i}R^{i-1},\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad Z=\frac{-w_{0}}{R}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}w_{i}(-R)^{i-1},
where w_{1}=\frac{-(r-1)}{4} and w_{2},w_{3},\ldots can be expressed in terms of w_{0} (changing w_{0} corresponds to a dilation of the profile, so it leaves w_{1} unchanged but modifies the rest of the coefficients). Plugging this into expression (4.22) and using w_{1}=\frac{-(r-1)}{4} :
\frac{N_{W}}{D_{W}}+\frac{N_{Z}}{D_{Z}}=\left(\frac{(r-5)(r-1)(3r+1)}{8w_{0}^{2}}-16w_{3}\right)R^{2}+O(R^{4}).
(4.23)
The equation \partial_{\xi}W=\frac{N_{W}}{D_{W}} allows us to solve for w_{3} in terms of the previous coefficients, getting
w_{3}=\frac{(r-5)(r-1)(3r+1)}{160w_{0}^{2}},
(4.24)
and therefore
\frac{N_{W}}{D_{W}}+\frac{N_{Z}}{D_{Z}}=\frac{(r-5)(r-1)(3r+1)}{40w_{0}^{2}}R^{2}+O(R^{4}).
(4.25)
which is smaller than 0 since 1<r<r^{\ast}=\frac{10}{2+2\sqrt{2}}<5 . Thus, N_{W}/D_{W}+N_{Z}/D_{Z} is negative for R>0 sufficiently small.
Part II: R>1 . Left of the phase portrait
Before delving into the proof, we will show a few properties regarding the behaviour of the solution for R>1 that will be used later in the proof of (4.15).
Part II.1
First of all, let us argue that the solution stays inside the triangle determined by D_{Z}>0 , W>Z and W<W_{0} . The two first inequalities come from (4.20). With respect to the last one, we perform a barrier argument. In order to show that W<W_{0} , it suffices to show that (N_{W}/D_{W},N_{Z}/D_{Z})\cdot(-1,0)>0 . Since D_{W}>0 (due to W>Z and D_{Z}>0 ), this just corresponds to showing that N_{W}<0 in the vertical segment from P_{s} to (W_{0},W_{0}) . We have that
\displaystyle N_{W}(W_{0},Z_{0}+t)
\displaystyle=\underbrace{-\frac{2}{49}\left(-2r^{2}+2r\sqrt{r^{2}-44r+92}+5\sqrt{r^{2}-44r+92}-59r+110\right)}_{a_{0}}
\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\frac{1}{28}\left(-5\sqrt{r^{2}-44r+92}+5r-82\right)t+\frac{7t^{2}}{8}}_{a_{1}t+a_{2}t^{2}}
With respect to the second term we have that for r=r^{\ast} :
a_{1}t+a_{2}t^{2}=\frac{1}{8}t\left(7t+20\sqrt{2}-52\right)<0,\quad\mbox{ for }t\in(0,W_{0}-Z_{0}]=(0,8-4\sqrt{2}].
Regarding a_{0} , we have that \lim_{r\to r^{\ast}}a_{0}=0 , so we need a more delicate analysis to show that it is negative for r close to r^{\ast} from below. It suffices to show that
(2r+5)\sqrt{r^{2}-44r+92}>2r^{2}+59r-110
sufficiently close to r^{\ast} . Squaring and rearranging and taking out a factor 392 , we are left to show that -r^{3}-9r^{2}+35r-25>0 for r sufficiently close to r^{\ast} from below. The value of such polynomial at r^{\ast} is 0 , but the value of its derivative is 20(-5+3\sqrt{2})<0 , so this shows that it is positive in some interval (r^{\ast}-\varepsilon,r^{\ast}) for some \varepsilon>0 sufficiently small.
Part II.2. Showing that the solution lies in N_{W}<0 .
Let us show moreover that the solution has to lie in the region where N_{W}<0 . This follows again the strategy from [9]. N_{W}=0 is a hyperbola and one of its two branches can be parametrized by Z as follows
p_{W}(Z)=\frac{1}{13}\left(\sqrt{16r^{2}+8rZ+92Z^{2}}-4r-Z\right).
It satisfies the following properties:
•
N_{W}(W,Z)<0 if W>p_{W}(Z) , in other words, N_{W}<0 for points to the right of the branch.
•
The minimum of p_{W}(Z) is 0 at Z=0 . Thus, the leftmost point of the branch is (0,0) . Moreover, p_{W}(Z) is decreasing for Z<0 and increasing afterwards.
•
(p_{W}(Z),Z) lies on S>0 (that is p_{W}(Z)>Z ) for Z<0 .
See Figure 4 for a picture of N_{W}=0 .
Figure 4: Phase portrait in (W,Z) coordinates for r=2.01 (with fixed d=8,p=3 ). The green line represents N_{W}=0 and we have also marked the point P_{\star} which is the only solution to N_{W}=N_{Z}=0 in the semiplane W>Z . The shaded quadrilateral corresponds to the restrictions W<W_{0} , W>Z , D_{Z}>0 , U>U(\overline{P}_{s}) .
Defining
P_{i}=(W_{i},Z_{i})=\left(\frac{1}{26}\left(3\sqrt{9r^{2}-20r+92}-9r+10\right),\frac{1}{26}\left(-\sqrt{9r^{2}-20r+92}+3r-38\right)\right),
which is the intersection of (p_{W}(Z),Z) with D_{Z}=0 , one just needs to show that the solution cannot traverse (p_{W}(Z),Z) for Z\in(Z_{i},0) (we refer again to [9] for details and Figures illustrating the situation).
Since N_{W}=0 over (p_{W}(Z),Z) , the barrier condition just reads (0,N_{Z}/D_{Z})\cdot(1,-p_{W}^{\prime}(Z))>0 , so using that D_{Z}>0 and that p_{W} is decreasing for Z<0 , we just need to show N_{Z}>0 over (p_{W}(Z),Z) for Z\in(Z_{i},0) .
The only solution to N_{W}=N_{Z}=0 in the open halfplane W>Z is given by
(W_{\ast},Z_{\ast})=\left(\frac{1}{5}\left(2\sqrt{2}-1\right)r,-\frac{1}{5}\left(1+2\sqrt{2}\right)r\right).
(4.26)
It is obvious that Z_{\ast}<0 , and moreover we have that
\displaystyle Z_{\ast}-Z_{i}\Big|_{r=r^{\ast}}=0,\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\frac{d}{dr}(Z_{\ast}-Z_{i})\Big|_{r=r^{\ast}}=-\frac{6(113+111\sqrt{2})}{1915}<0
Therefore, when r is sufficiently close to r^{\ast} from below, we have that Z_{i}<Z_{\ast}<0 . The sign of N_{Z}(p_{W}(Z),Z) is constant for Z\in(Z_{\ast},0) and moreover positive due to the expression N_{Z}(p_{W}(Z),Z)=-rZ+O(Z^{2}) . Thus, we just need to rule out the solution traversing (p_{W}(Z),Z) from right to left for Z\in(Z_{i},Z_{\ast}) .
We argue by contradiction. We define the region \Delta determined by N_{Z}<0 , N_{W}>0 , D_{Z}>0 and check that if the solution was going to traverse (p_{W}(Z),Z) for Z\in(Z_{i},Z_{\ast}) (thus entering \Delta ) it would not be able to exit \Delta from any of the three sides. We refer to [9, Lemma 4.1, Part II.1] for the detailed argument.
Once equipped with the knowledge that the trajectory lies on N_{W}<0 , we divide the proof in showing:
1+\frac{\overline{U}_{R}}{R}+\alpha\partial_{R}\overline{S}>0,\quad\mbox{ and }\quad 1+\frac{\overline{U}_{R}}{R}-\alpha\partial_{R}\overline{S}>0.
(4.27)
Going to (U,S) variables, equation (4.27) can be written as
\left(1+\frac{W+Z}{2}\pm\alpha\frac{W-Z}{2}\right)\pm\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\frac{N_{W}}{D_{W}}-\frac{N_{Z}}{D_{Z}}\right)>0
(one inequality per choice of \pm ). Given that the left parenthesis is D_{W} or D_{Z} (depending on the sign), and both D_{W},D_{Z}>0 , it suffices to show
\displaystyle\Xi_{1}:=D_{W}^{2}D_{Z}+\frac{\alpha}{2}N_{W}D_{Z}-\frac{\alpha}{2}N_{Z}D_{W}
\displaystyle>0\,,\mbox{ and }
(4.28)
\displaystyle D_{Z}^{2}D_{W}+\frac{\alpha}{2}N_{Z}D_{W}-\frac{\alpha}{2}N_{W}D_{Z}
\displaystyle>0\,,
(4.29)
where we remind the reader that because of our choice of parameters (specifically \gamma=2 ), we have that \alpha=\frac{1}{2} .
Part II.3 Showing (4.28)
First, we express \Xi_{1} in (U,S) coordinates, obtaining
\Xi_{1}=-\frac{1}{4}S(r(U+2)+U(7U+6)-2)-\frac{1}{4}S^{2}(U+1)+(U+1)^{3}.
There are two real solutions S_{-}(U)<S_{+}(U) solving \Xi_{1}=0 . Since U=\frac{W+Z}{2}=\frac{D_{Z}+D_{W}}{2}-1>-1 , we have that \Xi_{1} is positive when S_{-}<S<S_{+} . Since S>0 for our solution ( W>Z ), we just need to check S<S_{+}(U) .
We will show that the solution lies in the region U>U(\overline{P}_{s}) (we defer that proof to the end of Part II.3). Thus, the solution lies inside the open quadrilateral region Q determined by D_{Z}>0 , W<W_{0} , W-Z>0 , U>U(\overline{P}_{s}) . In Figure 4, Q corresponds to the shaded region. We will show \Xi_{1}|_{\partial Q}\geq 0 . This means that S\leq S_{+}(U) for every point of \partial Q , implying that S<S_{+}(U) on Q , and concluding the proof of (4.28).
The side W=Z obviously satisfies S<S_{+}(U) since S_{+}(U)>0 .
Restricting \Xi_{1} to W=W_{0} we obtain a third-degree polynomial in Z vanishing at P_{s} (due to N_{Z}(P_{s})=D_{Z}(P_{s})=0 ). We have:
\displaystyle\frac{\Xi_{1}}{Z-Z_{0}}=\frac{1}{4}(r-Z_{0}(Z_{0}+2)-2)+\frac{1}{16}(r-15Z_{0}-23)(Z-Z_{0})+\frac{5}{16}(Z-Z_{0})^{2}
All the coefficients on the right hand side are positive for r sufficiently close to r^{\ast} , since they converge to -3+\frac{9}{2\sqrt{2}} , \frac{1}{16}(17-10\sqrt{2}) and \frac{5}{16} respectively as r\to r^{\ast} (and all those numbers are positive).
Regarding the sign of \Xi_{1} on the side D_{Z}=0 of Q , we note that on this side \Xi_{1}=\frac{-\alpha}{2}N_{Z}D_{W} , which has the sign of -N_{Z} . Solving D_{Z}=0 , we have that N_{Z} over the line D_{Z}=0 is the second degree polynomial N_{Z}(W,-(4+W)/3) , which vanishes at \overline{P}_{s} and P_{s} (solutions of N_{Z}=D_{Z}=0 ). Since the quadratic coefficient is \frac{7}{9}>0 , we have that the second-degree polynomial is negative between the roots and positive outside.
Lastly, we need to treat the side U=U(\overline{P}_{s}) of our quadrilateral region Q . We know that S=S_{+}(U) at \overline{P}_{s} , since \Xi_{1}=0 there ( D_{Z}=N_{Z}=0 ). Therefore, we have that S<S_{+}(U) for any other point along the line U=U(\overline{P}_{s}) with smaller value of S .
Let us finally show that U>U(\overline{P}_{s}) . We construct the barrier b(t)=(\overline{W}_{0}-t,\overline{Z}_{0}+t) and in order to ensure that the solution remains on its upper-right part we need to show that the following expression is positive:
\displaystyle\Xi_{3}
\displaystyle=N_{W}(b(t))D_{Z}(b(t))+N_{Z}(b(t))D_{W}(b(t))
\displaystyle=\frac{1}{49}t\left(t(35r-14\mathcal{R}_{1}-133)+6r^{2}+6r\mathcal{R}_{1}+58r-6\mathcal{R}_{1}-64\right).
We just need to show that the parenthesis is positive, and since it is affine, it suffices to do so at the endpoints t=0 and t=\frac{\overline{W}_{0}-\overline{Z}_{0}}{2} . We have that
\displaystyle\left(6r^{2}+6r\mathcal{R}_{1}+58r-6\mathcal{R}_{1}-64\right)\Big|_{r=r^{\ast}}=-984+764\sqrt{2}>0
\displaystyle\left(\frac{\overline{W}_{0}-\overline{Z}_{0}}{2}(35r-14\mathcal{R}_{1}-133)+6r^{2}+6r\mathcal{R}_{1}+58r-6\mathcal{R}_{1}-64\right)\Big|_{r=r^{\ast}}=-492+382\sqrt{2}>0.
Part II.4. Showing (4.29).
Multiplying by D_{Z}D_{W} , we reduce the proof of (4.29) to show positivity for
\displaystyle\Xi_{2}:=N_{Z}D_{W}-N_{W}D_{Z}=S\left(-\frac{S^{2}}{2}+U(9U+10)+r(U+2)\right).
In particular, in the halfplane S>0 we have that \Xi_{2}>0 whenever
S<S_{+}(U)=\sqrt{2r(2+U)+2U(10+9U)}.
Note that this definition of S_{+}(U) is different from the one we gave in Part II.2. Each definition applies to its respective section. Note that S_{+}(U) may be complex, and in that case there is no possible S such that \Xi_{2}\geq 0 .
We divide our argument in two regions U\geq U(P_{s}) and U<U(P_{s}) . Regarding U\geq U(P_{s}) , we start noting that \Xi_{2}\geq 0 along the line D_{Z}=0 , for points with U\geq U(P_{s}) . This comes from the observation that \Xi_{2}=-N_{Z}D_{W} along D_{Z}=0 , the fact that D_{W}>0 along D_{Z}=0 , W>Z and the observation from Part II.3 that, along D_{Z}=0 , N_{W} is positive between its two roots ( P_{s} and \overline{P}_{s} ) and N_{Z}<0 outside. Denoting S_{DZ}(U)=2(1+U) to be the value of S such that (U,S) lies in the nullset of D_{Z} , we have that S_{DZ(U)}\leq S_{+}(U) , for U\geq U(P_{s}) with equality only at U=U(P_{s}) . Since the solution will be in D_{Z}>0 for R>1 , we have that S<S_{DZ}(U) from the condition D_{Z}>0 .
Figure 5: Phase portrait in (W,Z) coordinates for r=2.01 (with out fixed d=8,p=3 ). The brown line represents \Xi_{2}=0 , which is also b(t) for t\geq 0 when looking at the part that lies to the left of P_{s} . P_{\star} is the intersection of the green and black curves ( N_{W}=0 and N_{Z}=0 respectively). We have shaded the region D_{Z}>0,N_{Z}<0 . The brown curve enters the shaded region at P_{s} and exits the region at P_{\star} (difficult to distinguish the brown and black curves in the picture since they are almost coincident between P_{s} and P_{\star} ).
Regarding U\leq U(P_{s}) , we denote by b(t) the conic branch of \Xi_{2}=0 , fixing b(0)=P_{s} , and with the orientation fixed by D_{Z}(b(t))>0 for small t>0 . We will show:
•
b(t_{\star})=P_{\star} for some t_{\star}>0 (recall P_{\star} is a solution to N_{Z}=N_{W}=0 defined at the beginning of the proof).
•
b(t) is in the region N_{Z}<0 , D_{Z}>0 , for t\in(0,t_{\star}) .
•
The solution does not traverse b(t)
This would conclude the proof, since we can use N_{W}=0 to connect P_{\star} with P_{\infty} , and we know that the solution remains in N_{W}<0 due to Part II.2 (in particular, \Xi_{2}>0 , since the only intersection of \Xi_{2} with N_{W}=0 [under D_{W}>0 , W>Z ] is given by P_{\star} ).
Regarding the first two items, we note that b(t) starts in the region D_{Z}>0 from its orientation. Since N_{W}(P_{s})<0 (Lemma 4.5) and D_{W}(P_{s})>0 , from the expression of \Xi_{2}=N_{Z}D_{W}-N_{W}D_{Z} , b(t) has to lie in the region N_{Z}<0 for t\geq 0 sufficiently small. We denote the region defined by N_{Z}<0,D_{Z}>0 by E . E is bounded, since its boundary is described by the segment of D_{Z}=0 from P_{s} to \overline{P}_{s} and the piece of N_{Z}=0 also going from P_{s} to \overline{P}_{s} . E is contained on the halfplane W>Z since the hyperbola N_{Z}=0 only intersects W=Z twice: at (0,0) and (-r,-r) . The intersection at (0,0) corresponds to the upper branch (which is not the one bounding E ) and the intersection (-r,-r) is located in D_{Z}<0 (thus, it is not on the boundary of E . Moreover, b(t) has to exit E at some point (if b(t) is unbounded that is obvious, if it is an ellipse, it needs to reach P_{s} from the side D_{Z}<0 ).
Now, we show that the first point of exit of b(t) from E is actually P_{\star} , thus proving the first two items that we claimed. If b(t) exits through a point with D_{Z}>0 (and therefore N_{Z}=0 ) of \partial E , then from \Xi_{2}=0 , we conclude N_{W}D_{Z}=0 , so N_{W}=0 . Thus, that point of exit satisfies N_{W}=N_{Z}=0 , and since E is contained in the semiplane W>Z , must be P_{\star} (the only solution to N_{W}=N_{Z}=0 on that semiplane). Therefore, we just need to discard the possibility that b(t) exits E through D_{Z}=0 . In such case, one would have \Xi_{2}=N_{Z}D_{W}=0 at the point of exit, so N_{Z}=0 , meaning that the exit point is \overline{P}_{s} (the only other solution to N_{Z}=D_{Z}=0 apart from P_{s} ). However, we see from Lemma 4.5 that N_{W}(P_{s})<0 . Since \lim_{r\to r^{\ast}}N_{W}(\overline{P}_{s})=\frac{60}{49}\left(13\sqrt{2}-17\right)=1.69\ldots has a different sign, if b(t) goes from P_{s} to \overline{P}_{s} , it would need to cross N_{W}=0 at some intermediate time. That is impossible while in the region E defined by D_{Z}>0,N_{Z}<0 , since it would imply \Xi_{2}=N_{Z}D_{W}=0 . We conclude that b(t) exits E through point P_{\star} at some time t_{\star} and remains in E for t\in(0,t_{\star}) .
Lastly, let us show that the solution does not traverse b(t) . Given the implicit definition of b(t) as \Xi_{2}=0 , a normal vector of \Xi_{2} pointing down-left is given by \nabla(N_{W}D_{Z}-N_{Z}D_{W}) . We have that
\left(-1,-1\right)\cdot\nabla(N_{W}D_{Z}-N_{Z}D_{W})=\frac{1}{2}(W-Z)(10+r+9W+9Z)=S\left(10+r+18U\right).
We show that this quantity is positive. Since the parenthesis is increasing with U it suffices to show that it is positive for U=U(P_{\star}) in Lemma 4.5. This concludes that in both of our cases, on the arc \Xi_{2}=0 , the field points always in the lower-left direction.
As a conclusion, we know that the solution cannot traverse b(t) for t\in(0,t_{\star}) and cannot traverse N_{W}=0 by Part II.2 (in particular, it cannot traverse the branch from P_{\star} to P_{\infty} ). Therefore, the concatenation of b(t) and N_{W}=0 is a curve joining P_{s} and P_{\infty} that the solution cannot traverse. We clearly have S\leq S_{+}(U) along that curve ( S=S_{+}(U) along b(t) and then N_{W} does not intersect b(t) again, so S<S_{+}(U) ). Since we have that S\leq S_{+}(U) at the boundary of our region, we conclude S<S_{+}(U) in the interior, and therefore the solution satisfies \Xi_{2}>0 .
∎
Lemma 4.5.
For (d,\gamma)=(8,2) and r sufficiently close to r^{\ast} from below, we have:
•
W_{1}+Z_{1}<0 .
•
N_{W}(P_{s})<0 .
Proof.
Item 1
We have that
\frac{7r(-29r+29\mathcal{R}_{1}+16)-448\mathcal{R}_{1}+\sqrt{2}\mathcal{R}_{2}-1624}{147(r-\mathcal{R}_{1}-8)}
and since \lim_{r\to r^{\ast}}(r-\mathcal{R}_{1}-8)=14(-2+\sqrt{2})<0 , we just need to show that the numerator is positive. Since \sqrt{2}\mathcal{R}_{2} is trivially positive, we just need to show that it dominates the rest. That is, we need to show A>0 for
\displaystyle A
\displaystyle=(\sqrt{2}\mathcal{R}_{2})^{2}-\left(7r(-29r+29\mathcal{R}_{1}+16)-448\mathcal{R}_{1}-1624\right)^{2}
\displaystyle=-4704(r-1)\left(725r-1070+6r^{3}-4r^{2}+(85-6r^{2}-128r)\mathcal{R}_{1}\right)
Therefore, we just need to show that the right parenthesis is negative. Since r>1 , \mathcal{R}_{1}>0 , it is obvious that (85-6r^{2}-128r)\mathcal{R}_{1}<0 , so we just need to show that this term dominates the other one. That is, we need to show that B>0 for
B=((85-6r^{2}-128r)\mathcal{R}_{1})^{2}-(725r-1070+6r^{3}-4r^{2})^{2}=-19208(r-1)(3r+1)(r^{2}+10r-25).
Therefore, the conclusion follows from r^{2}+10r-25<0 , which holds because r^{\ast}=5(\sqrt{2}-1) is the upper root of the polynomial r^{2}+10r-25 .
Item 2
We have that
N_{W}(P_{s})=\frac{2}{49}\left(\left(2r^{2}+59r-110\right)-(2r+5)\mathcal{R}_{1}\right)
Therefore, in order to show that it is negative, it suffices to show that the term (2r+5)\mathcal{R}_{1} dominates the parenthesis. Therefore, we just need to show C>0 for
C=\left((2r+5)\mathcal{R}_{1}\right)^{2}-\left(2r^{2}+59r-110\right)^{2}=-392(r-1)(r^{2}+10r-25)
Therefore, we see that C>0 using again that r<r^{\ast}=5(\sqrt{2}-1) and noticing that r^{\ast} is the upper root of the polynomial r^{2}+10r-25 .
∎
Lemma 4.6.
We have that the profiles (\overline{U},\overline{S}) satisfy
R+\overline{U}_{R}-\alpha\overline{S}>(R-1)\eta
(4.30)
for R>1 .
Proof.
From [47], [7] we have that the profiles constructed satisfy R+\overline{U}_{R}-\alpha\overline{S}=0 for R=1 (i.e. D_{Z}(P_{s})=0 ). Therefore, using the fundamental theorem of calculus and (1.15), we have
\displaystyle R+\overline{U}_{R}-\alpha\overline{S}=\int_{1}^{R}\left(\widetilde{R}+\overline{U}_{R}^{\prime}(\widetilde{R})-\alpha\overline{S}^{\prime}(\widetilde{R})\right)d\widetilde{R}>\int_{1}^{R}\eta d\widetilde{R}.
∎
