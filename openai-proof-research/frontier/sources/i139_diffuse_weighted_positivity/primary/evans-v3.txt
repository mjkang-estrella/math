Note: This work was supported by EPSRC

Abstract
We explore Seshadri constants associated to weighted blow-ups
of complex projective varieties and demonstrate how to use this
notion to construct symplectic embeddings of ellipsoids. We
illustrate the utility of this point of view by providing
constructions of full fillings of ℂ​ℙ2\mathbb{CP}^{2} by ellipsoids
corresponding to all of the exceptional (post-Fibonacci) steps
of the McDuff–Schlenk staircase and some non-obvious
embeddings of ellipsoids in ellipsoids.
Dedicated to Kai Cieliebak, on his five-dozenth birthday.
1 Introduction
1.1 Seshadri constants and symplectic geometry
Recall that:
•
the Gromov width of a symplectic manifold
(Z,ω)(Z,\omega) is the quantity π​r2\pi r^{2} where rr is the
supremum over all radii of symplectically embedded balls in
(Z,ω)(Z,\omega);
•
the Seshadri constant of a projective variety ZZ
at a point pp with respect to an ample divisor DD is
defined to be the supremum
sup{ε:g∗D−εC is
ample}\sup\{\varepsilon\,:\,g^{*}D-\varepsilon C\text{ is
ample}\}, where g:Y→Zg\colon Y\to Z is the blow-up of ZZ
at pp and C=exc⁡(g)C=\operatorname{exc}(g) is the exceptional divisor.
It has been known since the work of McDuff and Polterovich
[25] that there is a close connection
between the Gromov width of a Kähler manifold and its
Seshadri constants: the Seshadri constant gives a lower bound on
the Gromov width (see Lazarsfeld’s book [21, Theorem
5.1.22]). One can generalise this to the problem
of embedding several symplectic balls pairwise disjointly, which
relates to the multi-point Seshadri constant. Biran
[3, 4] (for the case when
[ω]∈H2​(Z,ℚ)[\omega]\in H^{2}(Z;\mathbb{Q})) and Buse, Hind and Opshtein
[8] (in general) proved that one can fully fill any
symplectic 4-manifold by balls of equal size provided one has
enough balls. In his ECM survey [5], Biran pointed
out that one should be able to use weighted blow-ups
instead of ordinary blow-ups and thereby study symplectic
embeddings of ellipsoids.
Definition 1.1.1.
Given a vector 𝝈\bm{\sigma} of positive real numbers, the symplectic ellipsoid
𝔼⁡(𝝈)\mathbb{E}(\bm{\sigma}) is
𝔼⁡(𝝈)={𝒛∈ℂn:∑j=1n|zj|2σj≤1}⊂ℂn\mathbb{E}(\bm{\sigma})=\left\{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\,:\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{|z_{j}|^{2}}{\sigma_{j}}\leq 1\right\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
(1.1)
where ℂn\mathbb{C}^{n} is equipped with the standard
symplectic form ∑k=1nd​xk∧d​yk\sum_{k=1}^{n}dx_{k}\wedge dy_{k} and
zk=xk+i​ykz_{k}=x_{k}+iy_{k}. In the case when n=2n=2, we will refer to
σ2/σ1\sigma_{2}/\sigma_{1} as the slope of the ellipsoid. If
𝔼⁡(𝝈)\mathbb{E}(\bm{\sigma}) embeds symplectically into a symplectic
4-manifold (Z,ω)(Z,\omega) then we refer to the ratio
vol⁡(𝔼⁡(𝝈))/vol⁡(Z)\mathrm{vol}(\mathbb{E}(\bm{\sigma}))/\mathrm{vol}(Z) as the packing
ratio.
The connection between weighted blow-up and ellipsoid embeddings
was explored by McDuff [24]; McDuff showed
that the problem of embedding a symplectic ellipsoid into the
ball (or ℂ​ℙ2\mathbb{CP}^{2}) is equivalent to an associated ball packing
problem. In fact, similarly to Biran’s results, it is known that
most symplectic manifolds admit full fillings by symplectic
ellipsoids of sufficiently large slope; see for example Buse and
Hind [7, Theorem 1.2] (when the cohomology class of the
symplectic form is rational) and Edtmair [14, Theorem B]
(for 4-manifolds in general). For example, ℂ​ℙ2\mathbb{CP}^{2} can be
fully filled by any ellipsoid of slope at least
28936\frac{289}{36} (see [26, Corollary
1.2.4]). The complete answer of which
ellipsoids embed symplectically into ℂ​ℙ2\mathbb{CP}^{2} was worked out by
McDuff and Schlenk in their seminal paper
[26]. The size of the biggest ellipsoid
embedding into ℂ​ℙ2\mathbb{CP}^{2} depends strongly on the slope of the
ellipsoid: the set of possible pairs (a,b)∈ℝ2(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2} for which
there exists an embedding of 𝔼⁡(a,b)\mathbb{E}(a,b) forms a
staircase-like shape. This staircase has infinitely many
piecewise-linear steps; there is an infinite sequence of steps
whose coordinates are related to the Fibonacci numbers, and
which accumulate when the slope approaches the fourth power of
the Golden Ratio. There are also nine “exceptional steps”
beyond this slope. For slopes outside these steps, ℂ​ℙ2\mathbb{CP}^{2} can
be fully filled (i.e. the packing ratio can be made arbitrarily
close to 11).
Whilst much of the subsequent work on ellipsoid embeddings has used
McDuff’s equivalence with ball-packings as a basis, the idea of
working directly with weighted blow-ups has been used to great effect
by Entov and Verbitsky [15] to show that full
ellipsoid packings exist for Campana simple manifolds
(Kähler manifolds which are not unions of their proper
subvarieties). In the current paper, we adopt this point of view and
show that it can also be used to find all of the ellipsoid embeddings
in ℂ​ℙ2\mathbb{CP}^{2} corresponding to the post-Fibonacci steps in the
McDuff–Schlenk staircase.
1.2 Inflation
Biran’s main tool for constructing multiple balls and McDuff’s
main tool for constructing ellipsoids is symplectic
inflation, where one modifies a symplectic form along some
curve in a 4-manifold; see for example [24, Proof of
Proposition 2.1, Step 2]. Inflation along
unicuspidal and sesquicuspidal plane curves is used by McDuff
and Siegel in [27, Theorem A, E]
to construct the full ellipsoid embeddings in ℂ​ℙ2\mathbb{CP}^{2}
corresponding to the Fibonacci portion of the
staircase. Opshtein [30] showed that
ellipsoids with slope p/qp/q could be constructed by inflating
along a curve with “multi-cusp” singularities (points where
several irreducible branches each modelled analytically on
yp=xqy^{p}=x^{q}). In each case, one needs to pay careful attention
to the local model for the inflationary curve.
Already in his ECM survey [5, Section 2.1], Biran
pointed out that inflation is a symplectic analogue of using the
Nakai–Moishezon criterion to find an ample divisor. In the current
paper, we bypass inflation and just use Nakai–Moishezon, giving a
purely algebro-geometric construction of symplectic ellipsoid
embeddings.
1.3 Construction of ellipsoids
To a weighted blow-up g:Y→Zg\colon Y\to Z (see Definition
3.2.5) and an ample divisor DD on ZZ,
we will associate a weighted Seshadri constant
ε⁡(Z,D,g)\varepsilon(Z,D;g) (see Definition
3.1.2: this is the reciprocal of the
Cutkosky–Ein–Lazarsfeld ss-invariant [11, Definition
1.1] of a certain ideal). One can use
a lower bound on the weighted Seshadri constant to produce
ellipsoids of a certain size. This is made precise in Theorem
1.3.1 below; essentially the same result
appears in the paper of Entov and Verbitsky
[15] with a complete and detailed proof in the
masters thesis of Gudiev [18]. It was also proved in a
different way by Luef and Wang [23, Theorem A], who
also give an equivalent definition of weighted Seshadri
constants in terms of plurisubharmonic functions with specified
local behaviour [23, Definition 1.5 and Theorem
3.2]. We include a slightly different11

Theorem 1.3.1.
Let ZZ be a smooth complex projective variety, p∈Zp\in Z be a
point and D⊂ZD\subset Z be an irreducible ample ℚ\mathbb{Q}-divisor;
let ζ\zeta be the associated symplectic form Poincaré-dual to
π​D\pi D (see Remark 2.1.4). Let g:Y→Zg\colon Y\to Z be a
weighted blow-up with weight vector
𝐚=(a1,…,an)\bm{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}). Then (Z,ζ)(Z,\zeta) admits a symplectic
embedding of the ellipsoid
𝔼⁡(εa1,…,εan)\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{a_{1}},\ldots,\frac{\varepsilon}{a_{n}}\right)
for any rational ε<ε⁡(Z,D,g)\varepsilon<\varepsilon(Z,D;g).
To apply the theorem, you need a suitable divisor DD, which
can be hard to find; this plays the role of the inflationary
curve. We will illustrate the use of this theorem by
constructing all of the exceptional steps in the McDuff–Schlenk
staircase and constructing some new ellipsoid embeddings into
ellipsoids. See Section 7 for a wide array of
applications.
1.4 Advantages
There are several advantages to working in the category of
complex projective varieties. First, the weighted blow-up is
almost always singular, and the intersection theory of curves on
singular projective varieties is well-developed and convenient to
use. One could further resolve and work with a smooth manifold,
but this ends up clouding the simplicity of computations with
additional irrelevant terms. Second, one doesn’t need to make
any assumptions on what the inflationary divisors look like, or
how they intersect the exceptional divisor of the weighted
blow-up: one simply applies the Nakai–Moishezon criterion to
find an ample divisor and hence a symplectic form. Finally, we
observe that the original construction of the exceptional steps
by McDuff and Schlenk was quite involved: they had to enumerate
all possible obstructive curves and find the strongest
obstructions in each given interval of slopes. By contrast, as
soon as we find a curve DD to which we can apply our
construction, we find an ellipsoid.
1.5 Disadvantages and difficulties
There are also several disadvantages. The main disadvantage is
that the method is wholly unsuitable for constructing ellipsoids
whose slopes lie outside the steps of the McDuff–Schlenk
staircase, that is when there is a one-parameter family of
slopes which yield full fillings. To construct something
arbitrarily close to the volume constraint one would need to use
a certain infinite sequence of divisors DD and weighted
blow-ups adapted to them, so that the associated weighted
Seshadri constant gets closer and closer to a limiting value but
never quite achieves it; see Example 7.4.2. It
seems likely that this infinite sequence of divisors is
precisely what one needs to prove the Nagata conjecture.
When working with weighted blow-ups there are also subtleties:
there is not a single operation which, given a point and a
slope, produces a canonical weighted blow-up. Indeed, one can
construct weighted blow-ups by iteratedly blowing up at
infinitely-near points and then contracting all but the last
exceptional curve; the choices of which points one blows up give
moduli for the construction; we explore this subtlety in Section
6.2. Rather than being a difficulty, this is really an
advantage: we can make a “general” choice of points to
blow up, which can be helpful for constructing the right divisor
DD.
1.6 Remarks on the proof of Theorem 1.3.1
Theorem 1.3.1 depends on some intermediate
results (Proposition 5.7.1 and Corollary
5.7.2) which are weighted analogues of a result of
McDuff and Polterovich’s [25, Corollary 2.1.D]
for ordinary blow-ups. The strategy of our proof is very similar, but
there are subtleties in modifying the proof to handle weighted
blow-ups and ellipsoids:
1.
The McDuff–Polterovich proof uses Kähler forms
constructed by pulling back the standard Kähler form on
(ℂ×)n(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n} via a map of the form
𝒛↦h⁡(|𝒛|)|𝒛|​𝒛\bm{z}\mapsto\frac{h(|\bm{z}|)}{|\bm{z}|}\bm{z} for some
strictly increasing function hh. These maps are called
monotone embeddings. If one uses
∑j=1naj​|zj|2\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}|z_{j}|^{2}} instead of |𝒛||\bm{z}|
then the pulled back form is not Kähler (it has
(2,0)(2,0) and (0,2)(0,2)-parts), and it would be harder to
check tameness. On a related note, the Hamiltonian circle
action generated by
H⁡(𝒛)=12​∑j=1naj​|zj|2H(\bm{z})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}|z_{j}|^{2} extends to a
holomorphic ℂ×\mathbb{C}^{\times}-action with weights
a1,…,ana_{1},\ldots,a_{n}, but this does not carry level sets of
HH (ellipsoids) to level sets of HH unless
𝒂=(1,…,1)\bm{a}=(1,\ldots,1).
2.
The weighted blow-up of ℂn\mathbb{C}^{n} sits inside
ℂn×ℙ⁡(𝒂)\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{P}(\bm{a}) where ℙ⁡(𝒂)\mathbb{P}(\bm{a}) is a weighted
projective space. The “standard” symplectic form on the ordinary
blow-up can be written as a linear combination of two contributions:
the pullback of the Fubini–Study form from the projection to
ℙ⁡(1,…,1)\mathbb{P}(1,\ldots,1), and the pullback of the standard symplectic
form on ℂn\mathbb{C}^{n}. However, the symplectic form on the weighted
blow-up cannot be written in this form: the inclusion of the
weighted blow-up into the product has vanishing derivatives along
the exceptional locus, so the pullback of the standard symplectic
form has degeneracies along the normal directions to the exceptional
locus of the weighted blow-up. Note that this is in addition
to the complication that the weighted blow-up is singular.
To get around both of these issues, we use the
Guillemin–Abreu theory of toric Kähler metrics and
symplectic potentials on orbifolds to find “standard”
symplectic forms and to interpolate between standard forms on
the weighted blow-up and weighted blow-down.
1.7 Examples
In Section 7.1, we use the nodal cubic curve in
ℂ​ℙ2\mathbb{CP}^{2} as our divisor to construct embeddings of
𝔼⁡(σ1,σ2)\mathbb{E}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}) into ℂ​ℙ2\mathbb{CP}^{2} with:
•
slope s≔σ2/σ1∈(7+452,7]s\coloneqq\sigma_{2}/\sigma_{1}\in\left(\frac{7+\sqrt{45}}{2},7\right]
and packing ratios up to 9​s(1+s)2\frac{9s}{(1+s)^{2}}, and
•
slope s≔σ2/σ1∈(7,649]s\coloneqq\sigma_{2}/\sigma_{1}\in\left(7,\frac{64}{9}\right]
and packing ratios up to 64​s9\frac{64s}{9}.
These represent the optimal ellipsoid embeddings for the first
post-Fibonacci step in the McDuff–Schlenk staircase. In Section
7.2, we use more complicated curves of higher
degree to construct the remaining post-Fibonacci steps; these curves
appear in the work of Dumnicki, Harbourne, Küronya, Roé and
Szemberg [13] as “supraminimal curves”. In that
context these curves are responsible for steps in a different but
related staircase: the graph of the function μ^\hat{\mu} which is
another way of getting at what we are calling the weighted Seshadri
constant. Indeed, this paper grew out of my attempts to understand the
relationship between their work and that of McDuff–Schlenk
[26].
In Section 7.3, we explain how to reproduce
the results of McDuff and Siegel [27] on ellipsoid
embeddings via inflation along unicuspidal curves in our language,
which in particular recovers the Fibonacci part of the staircase. In
Section 7.4, we discuss the difficulties of
using our method to produce embeddings when the staircase is not
piecewise-linear. Finally, in Section 7.5,
we give some examples of ellipsoid embeddings in other ellipsoids.
1.8 Outline
After recapping some prerequisites and fixing conventions
(Section 2), we define what we mean by
weighted blow-up and the associated Seshadri constants (Section
3). Then there follow two sections in
which we explain how to iron out a Kähler form along the
exceptional locus of a weighted blow-up (Section
4) and how to extend an ironed form over the
blow-down (Section 5). Subsection
5.7 contains the proof of Theorem
1.3.1. Section 6 works
out in detail how to calculate the weighted Seshadri constant in
the case of complex surfaces and Section 7
applies this to a range of examples.

Remark 2.1.4 (Symplectic forms from ample divisors).
Recall that if ZZ is a complex projective variety and DD

Remark 2.1.5 (Orbifold Kodaira embedding).
We refer the reader to the lucid account of Ross and Thomas
[34] for a helpful user’s guide to orbifolds. If
ZZ is an orbifold then one can ask whether a Kähler
form on the smooth locus extends to a Kähler orbifold
differential form over the orbifold locus. Recall that an
orbifold differential form is the same thing as a coherent
choice of Γ\Gamma-invariant differential form on each local
uniformising chart ℂn→ℂn/Γ\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}/\Gamma. Note that the
Kähler form constructed in Remark 2.1.4 is
degenerate along the orbifold locus when pulled back along a
local uniformising chart ℂn→ℂn/Γ\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}/\Gamma. One can fix
this by working instead with an orbi-ample orbibundle LL
[34, Section 2.4 and Definition 2.7]. An
orbibundle is locally just an equivariant line bundle on each
orbifold chart; in particular, the stabiliser subgroup at a
point z∈Zz\in Z acts on the line LzL_{z}. If we define the
order ord⁡(Z)\mathrm{ord}(Z) to be the least common multiple of the
sizes of point-stabilisers then L⊗ord⁡(Z)L^{\otimes\mathrm{ord}(Z)} is
an honest line bundle. Ross and Thomas define an orbibundle to
be locally ample if this action is faithful for all
z∈Zz\in Z, and orbi-ample if it is locally ample and
L⊗ord⁡(Z)L^{\otimes\mathrm{ord}(Z)} is ample. Given an orbi-ample line
bundle, one can construct an orbifold embedding
Z→ℙ⁡(𝒏)Z\to\mathbb{P}(\bm{n}) into a weighted projective space
ℙ⁡(𝒏)\mathbb{P}(\bm{n}) built out of sections of powers of LL (see
[34, Proposition 2.11]) and the pullback of the
Fubini–Study orbifold Kähler form [34, Definition
3.3] yields an orbifold Kähler form on
ZZ.

3 Weighted Seshadri constants
3.1 Seshadri constants
Setup 3.1.1.
Let YY and ZZ be normal complex projective varieties and

3.2 Weighted blow-ups
In this paper, we will focus on a specific class of ideal
sheaves defining non-reduced points; we will call the
corresponding blow-ups weighted blow-ups.

Remark 3.2.6.
Weighted blow-ups are more subtle than the ordinary blow-up,

•

whereas more general weighted blow-ups depend heavily on

•

than (1,…,1)(1,\ldots,1) is always singular.

Remark 3.2.9.
Note that 𝒞\mathcal{C} is actually “orbi-Cartier” in the sense
that it is the vanishing set of a section of an orbifold line

line bundle is locally ample in the sense of Remark
2.1.5. If g:Y→Zg\colon Y\to Z is

remarked in Remark 2.1.5, the

4 Ironing
Given a Kähler manifold, McDuff and Polterovich have a
procedure for “ironing” a symplectic form to make it flat near a
point whilst keeping it tame (see [25, Lemma
5.5.B]). To blow down, we need to be able
to iron our symplectic forms to make them “standard” along
the exceptional locus. We need some lemmas, whose proofs closely
follow those of McDuff and Polterovich.

ω𝒵\omega_{\mathcal{Z}} for the standard symplectic form on 𝒵\mathcal{Z}.

5 Kähler forms
5.1 Review of Guillemin–Abreu theory
We will make extensive use of the Guillemin–Abreu theory of
toric Kähler geometry. We briefly recap what we need from
this theory; the reader can learn more from the original paper
by Guillemin [19] or the subsequent work by Abreu
[1, 2] which also treats the orbifold
setting. Recall that a Kähler structure on a manifold XX
comprises a complex structure JJ and a symplectic form
ω\omega which are compatible in the sense that, at every
point pp,
ω⁡(J​v,J​w)=ω⁡(v,w)\omega(Jv,Jw)=\omega(v,w)
for all tangent vectors
v,w∈Tp​Xv,w\in T_{p}X and ω⁡(v,J​v)>0\omega(v,Jv)>0 for all nonzero
tangent vectors vv (this second condition is called tameness: ω\omega tames JJ). If XX is an orbifold
locally modelled on a quotient ℂn/Γ\mathbb{C}^{n}/\Gamma by the action of
a finite group Γ⊂G​L​(n,ℂ)\Gamma\subset GL(n,\mathbb{C}) then we can still
talk about Kähler structures: they just come from
Γ\Gamma-invariant Kähler structures on ℂn\mathbb{C}^{n}.
Let ℓ1,…,ℓm:ℝn→ℝ\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R} be linear maps and
R1,…,RmR_{1},\ldots,R_{m} be real numbers. The linear
inequalities ℓi​(𝝁)≥Ri/2\ell_{i}(\bm{\mu})\geq R_{i}/2 cut out a polytope
P={𝝁∈ℝn:ℓi(𝝁)≥Ri/2 for i=1,…,m}.P=\left\{\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,\ell_{i}(\bm{\mu})\geq R_{i}/2\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,m\right\}.
Given
this polytope, we can construct:
•
the normal toric variety 𝒳\mathcal{X} (a complex orbifold)
associated with the inward normal fan to PP. This
admits a holomorphic (ℂ×)n(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n}-action which has a
free Zariski-open orbit. We identify this Zariski-open torus
with ℂn/(2​π​ℤ)n\mathbb{C}^{n}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{n} via the exponential map, writing
complex coordinates 𝝀+i​𝜽\bm{\lambda}+i\bm{\theta} with
𝝀=(λ1,…,λn)∈ℝn\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n} and
𝜽=(θ1,…,θn)∈ℝn/(2​π​ℤ)n\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{n}, so that the action of
𝒕=(t1,…,tn)∈Tn⊆(ℂ×)n\bm{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\in T^{n}\subseteq(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n} is
given by 𝝀+i⁡(𝜽+𝒕)\bm{\lambda}+i(\bm{\theta}+\bm{t}). We refer to these as
complex coordinates. The notation 𝝀\bm{\lambda} is
intended to evoke “log-radius”.
•
a toric symplectic orbifold (𝔛,ω)(\mathfrak{X},\omega) together with
a moment map 𝝁:𝔛→P\bm{\mu}\colon\mathfrak{X}\to P. This construction is
due to Lerman and Tolman [22], following
Delzant’s construction in the case of manifolds
[12]. This admits a Hamiltonian action of the torus
TnT^{n} which is free over the interior

ω=∑j=1nd​μi∧d​θi\omega=\sum_{j=1}^{n}d\mu_{i}\wedge d\theta_{i} where
(μ1,…,μn)(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}) are linear coordinates on ℝn\mathbb{R}^{n} and
(θ1,…,θn)∈Tn=ℝn/(2​π​ℤ)n(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})\in T^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{n} are

as symplectic (or action-angle) coordinates.
The orbifolds 𝒳\mathcal{X} and 𝔛\mathfrak{X} are diffeomorphic, and the
Guillemin–Abreu theory tells us how to combine the symplectic
and complex geometry to obtain a (torus-invariant) Kähler structure on either
side. There are many torus-invariant Kähler structures, and
choosing one is equivalent to choosing a symplectic
potential:

A symplectic potential is a strictly convex function

•

the limit of

Remark 5.1.2.
Guillemin and Abreu use the function 12​t​log⁡t\frac{1}{2}t\log t

Changing the symplectic potential by a linear term doesn’t

and the moment map.

6.2 The Farey tree
Even if ZZ is toric and pp is a toric fixed point, not all
weighted blow-ups at pp are globally toric: there is a lot of
freedom in forming weighted blow-ups, and, in this section, we
will discuss the moduli space of weighted blow-ups at

Discussion 6.2.1.
Since Ei1E_{i_{1}} is a −1-1-curve (Remark

neighbouring curves Ei2E_{i_{2}} must become a

Ei3,…,EimE_{i_{3}},\ldots,E_{i_{m}} until we get back to

blow-up can be obtained by iteratedly blowing up at points
infinitely near to pp: write F1=EimF_{1}=E_{i_{m}} for the first

we will call such a blow-up process a Farey process.

have blown up at a satellite point, we encounter no further
free points, but there is always a choice between two
satellite points. We think of this as a decision tree (see
Figure 12).

7.3 Unicuspidal curves
McDuff and Siegel [27] use unicuspidal and sesquicuspidal curves to produce ellipsoids

7.4 Full fillings outside the steps?
As remarked in Section 1.5, this method usually
does not seem to produce optimal ellipsoids whose slopes lie in
intervals where full fillings exist, in other words for any slope
ss between 7+452\frac{7+\sqrt{45}}{2} and ∞\infty which does not
lie on one of the nine McDuff–Schlenk steps. The exception is when
the slope is a square number s=d2s=d^{2}. Such full fillings were
constructed by Opshtein [29, Lemma 2.1]: the
neighbourhood of a smooth curve of degree dd is a ball subbundle of
its symplectic normal bundle, and the total space of the restriction
of this bundle to an open ball in the smooth curve is an ellipsoid of
slope d2d^{2}. McDuff gave an alternative construction of the same
ellipsoid [24, Proposition 2.1] by inflating
along the smooth curve of degree dd. To illustrate the connection
with our method, we explain his result in our language before going on
to show why things are harder for other slopes.

7.5 Ellipsoids in ellipsoids

Theorem 7.5.1.
Let β,α\beta,\alpha be coprime positive integers with
s=β/α>12​(k​ℓ−2+k2​ℓ2−4​k​ℓ)s=\beta/\alpha>\frac{1}{2}(k\ell-2+\sqrt{k^{2}\ell^{2}-4k\ell}). For
any ε<k​ℓ​α​βα+β\varepsilon<\frac{k\ell\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta} there is a
symplectic embedding
𝔼⁡(ε/α,ε/β)→𝔼⁡(k,ℓ)\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon/\alpha,\varepsilon/\beta)\to\mathbb{E}(k,\ell).
Proof.
Take weighted homogeneous coordinates [x:y:z][x:y:z] on
Z≔ℙ⁡(k,ℓ,1)Z\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(k,\ell,1) and consider the curve
D={xyzk​ℓ−k−ℓ=xℓ+yk}D=\{xyz^{k\ell-k-\ell}=x^{\ell}+y^{k}\} of weighted degree k​ℓk\ell. This
is an ample Cartier divisor with D2=k​ℓD^{2}=k\ell (note that DD
is numerically equivalent to k​ℓ​Tk\ell T and T2=1/(k​ℓ)T^{2}=1/(k\ell)). In
the smooth affine chart z=1z=1, DD is locally analytically
equivalent to x​y=0xy=0 (or x​y=y2xy=y^{2} if k=2k=2), so it has a
nodal singularity. Taking a Farey process with weights
(α,β)(\alpha,\beta) guided by a branch of DD we obtain a
weighted blow-up g:Y→Z=ℙ⁡(k,ℓ,1)g\colon Y\to Z=\mathbb{P}(k,\ell,1) such that the
proper transform D~\widetilde{D} intersects the left- and
right-most curves E1E_{1} and EmE_{m} each once transversely,
so
C⋅D~=1β+1α=α+βα​βC\cdot\widetilde{D}=\frac{1}{\beta}+\frac{1}{\alpha}=\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha\beta}. We have
μ⁡(ℙ⁡(k,ℓ,1),D,g)=min⁡(α+β,k​ℓ​α​βα+β).\mu(\mathbb{P}(k,\ell,1),D;g)=\min\left(\alpha+\beta,\frac{k\ell\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\right).
We are in the potentially obstructive
regime when
k​ℓ​α​βα+β<α+β\frac{k\ell\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta}<\alpha+\beta, which
is equivalent to
s≔β/α>12​(k​ℓ−2+k2​ℓ2−4​k​ℓ).s\coloneqq\beta/\alpha>\frac{1}{2}(k\ell-2+\sqrt{k^{2}\ell^{2}-4k\ell}).
In this
case, the weighted Seshadri constant is
ε⁡(ℙ⁡(k,ℓ,1),D,g)=μ⁡(ℙ⁡(k,ℓ,1),D,g)=k​ℓ​α​βα+β=k​ℓ​s​α1+s\varepsilon(\mathbb{P}(k,\ell,1),D;g)=\mu(\mathbb{P}(k,\ell,1),D;g)=\frac{k\ell\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta}=\frac{k\ell s\alpha}{1+s}
so Theorem 1.3.1 should give us
the ellipsoids we seek. However, there is a minor issue here:
in Theorem 1.3.1, we assumed ZZ to be
smooth, but now ZZ is only an orbifold, and DD is only
ample (not orbi-ample). We can fix this by instead using the
orbi-ample divisor N​D+TND+T for sufficiently large NN; this
works because TT is locally ample. The only other place
where smoothness of ZZ is needed is in the proof of
Corollary 5.7.2, where we used Moser’s
argument to show that (Z,ζ)(Z,\zeta) (the symplectic manifold
we started with) is symplectomorphic to (Z,ω)(Z,\omega) (the
symplectic manifold obtained by weighted blow-down). Provided
we work with orbifold symplectic forms, nondegenerate along
the orbifold locus, Moser’s argument works just as well for
orbifolds: one simply constructs the orbifold diffeomorphism
by working Γ\Gamma-invariantly in the local uniformising
cover. Since the resulting Moser-diffeomorphisms preserve the
orbifold locus, the ellipsoids we construct are disjoint from
the orbifold locus.
The ellipsoids we produce in (Z,ω)(Z,\omega) are manifestly
disjoint from TT, and the arguments of [25, Remark 2.1.E
and Corollary 4.1.B] apply in the
orbifold setting, using the symplectic tubular neighbourhood
theorem for suborbifolds [28, Proposition 17],
to show that we can modify the Moser isotopy to ensure that
the ellipsoids in (Z,ζ)(Z,\zeta) remain in the complement of
TT. Therefore we produce symplectic embeddings
𝔼⁡(ε/α,ε/β)→𝔼⁡(k,ℓ)\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon/\alpha,\varepsilon/\beta)\to\mathbb{E}(k,\ell).
∎
Remark 7.5.2.
As ε\varepsilon approaches the Seshadri constant, the packing
ratio approaches k​ℓs+s−1+2\frac{k\ell}{s+s^{-1}+2}. As
s→12​(k​ℓ−2+k2​ℓ2−4​k​ℓ)s\to\frac{1}{2}(k\ell-2+\sqrt{k^{2}\ell^{2}-4k\ell}), this approaches
11, so we get fuller and fuller fillings. Note that the
slopes of these fillings are substantially larger than
ℓ/k\ell/k, the slope of 𝔼⁡(k,ℓ)\mathbb{E}(k,\ell).
Remark 7.5.3.
If we take k=1k=1 then the proof breaks down: the curve DD
is no longer nodal. Instead, if ℓ>1\ell>1, one should look at
A={xyzℓ−1=xℓ+y2}A=\{xyz^{\ell-1}=x^{\ell}+y^{2}\} of weighted degree 2​ℓ2\ell and take
D=12​AD=\frac{1}{2}A. The conclusion is modified accordingly:
for all slopes s>2​ℓ−1+4​ℓ2−ℓs>2\ell-1+4\sqrt{\ell^{2}-\ell} we get an embedding of
𝔼⁡(ε/β,ε/α)\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon/\beta,\varepsilon/\alpha) into 𝔼⁡(1,ℓ)\mathbb{E}(1,\ell)
whenever ε<2​ℓ​α​βα+β\varepsilon<\frac{2\ell\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta}. If
k=1k=1 and ℓ=1\ell=1 then we need to pass to cubics and we are
back in the situation of Proposition 7.1.1.
Remark 7.5.4.
Clearly this is just one family of ellipsoid embeddings,
analogous to the first post-Fibonacci step of the
McDuff–Schlenk staircase explained in Proposition
7.1.1. The full story of ellipsoids in
ellipsoids remains to be worked out.
