Abstract.
Let XX be a projective variety of dimension nn and LL be a nef divisor on
XX. Denote by ϵd​(r,X,L)\epsilon_{d}(r;X,L) the dd-dimensional Seshadri constant of
rr very general points in XX. We prove that
ϵd​(r​s,X,L)≥ϵd​(r,X,L)⋅ϵd​(s,ℙn,𝒪ℙn​(1)) for ​r,s≥1.\epsilon_{d}(rs;X,L)\geq\epsilon_{d}(r;X,L)\cdot\epsilon_{d}(s;\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(1))\quad\text{ for }r,s\geq 1.
1. Introduction
Let LL be a nef divisor on a projective variety XX of dimension nn.
The Seshadri constant of dimension d≤nd\leq n at rr points p1,…,prp_{1},\ldots,p_{r} in
the smooth locus of XX
is defined (see [14]) to be the real number
ϵd​(p1,…,pr,X,L)=inf{(Ld⋅Z∑multpi⁡Z)1/d:Z⊂X​ effective cycleof dimension ​d}.\epsilon_{d}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{r};X,L)=\inf\left\{\left(\dfrac{L^{d}\cdot Z}{\sum\operatorname{mult}_{p_{i}}Z}\right)^{1/d}:\begin{matrix}Z\subset X\text{ effective cycle}\\
\text{of dimension }d\end{matrix}\right\}.
By semicontinuity of multiplicities if the points are in very general position then
the Seshadri constant does not depend on the actual points chosen
(2.3). Thus one can define
ϵd​(r,X,L)=ϵd​(p1,…,pr,X,L)\epsilon_{d}(r;X,L)=\epsilon_{d}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{r};X,L)
where p1,…,prp_{1},\ldots,p_{r} is any collection of rr very general points in
XX. We shall prove the following inequality comparing Seshadri
constants of very general points XX and those in projective space.
Theorem 1.1.
Let XX be an nn dimensional projective variety, LL be a nef
divisor on XX and r,s,d≥1r,s,d\geq 1 be integers, with d≤nd\leq n. Then
ϵd​(r​s,X,L)≥ϵd​(r,X,L)⋅ϵd​(s,ℙn,𝒪ℙn​(1)).\epsilon_{d}(rs;X,L)\geq\epsilon_{d}(r;X,L)\cdot\epsilon_{d}(s;\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(1)).
Remark 1.2.
When d=1d=1 the Seshadri constant ϵ1\epsilon_{1} reduces to the usual Seshadri constant as introduced by Demailly [5], and in this case Theorem 1.1 is due to Biran [2]. When r=1r=1, d=n−1d=n-1 the Theorem is due to Roé [12].
It is well known and not hard to see that
ϵd​(r,X,L)≤Ln/rn\epsilon_{d}(r;X,L)\leq\sqrt[n]{L^{n}/r}
for d=1d=1 and d=n−1d=n-1 (see remark 1.4). On the other hand,
explicit values or even lower bounds are in general
hard to compute. Thus Theorem 1.1 should be seen as a lower
bound on ϵd​(r​s,X,L)\epsilon_{d}(rs;X,L), which will be useful if Seshadri
constants on projective space are known. As an example, let us recall
the most general form of a famous conjecture by Nagata:
Conjecture 1.3 (Nagata-Biran-Szemberg, [14]).
Let XX be an nn dimensional projective variety, LL be a nef
divisor on XX and 1≤d≤n1\leq d\leq n be an integer. Then there is a
positive integer r0r_{0} such that for every r≥r0r\geq r_{0},
ϵd​(r,X,L)=Ln/rn.\epsilon_{d}(r;X,L)=\sqrt[n]{L^{n}/r}.
Remark 1.4.
Already Demailly observed that ϵ1​(r,X,L)≤ϵd​(r,X,L)\epsilon_{1}(r;X,L)\leq\epsilon_{d}(r;X,L) for all
d=2,…,nd=2,\dots,n (which combined with the obvious equality
ϵn​(r,X,L)=Ln/rn\epsilon_{n}(r;X,L)=\sqrt[n]{L^{n}/r} gives the upper bound
ϵ1​(r,X,L)≤Ln/rn\epsilon_{1}(r;X,L)\leq\sqrt[n]{L^{n}/r}). Standard arguments also show
that ϵn−1​(r,X,L)≤Ln/rn=ϵn​(r,X,L)\epsilon_{n-1}(r;X,L)\leq\sqrt[n]{L^{n}/r}=\epsilon_{n}(r;X,L) and
that ϵ1​(r,X,L)=Ln/rn\epsilon_{1}(r;X,L)=\sqrt[n]{L^{n}/r} implies
ϵd​(r,X,L)=Ln/rn\epsilon_{d}(r;X,L)=\sqrt[n]{L^{n}/r} for all d=2,…​nd=2,\dots n, so if
the conjecture above holds for d=1d=1 then it holds for all dd. In
view of these facts, it is tempting to ask if the inequalities
ϵd1​(r,X,L)≤ϵd2​(r,X,L)\epsilon_{d_{1}}(r;X,L)\leq\epsilon_{d_{2}}(r;X,L) hold for all d1≤d2d_{1}\leq d_{2}.
In this context Theorem 1.1 implies, for instance,
that if (1) the Nagata-Biran-Szemberg conjecture is true for
dd-dimensional Seshadri constants on ℙn\mathbb{P}^{n},
and (2) ϵd​(1,X,L)=Lnn\epsilon_{d}(1;X,L)=\sqrt[n]{L^{n}}, then the
Nagata-Biran-Szemberg conjecture is true for the dd-dimensional Seshadri
constants of (X,L)(X,L). See [12]
for more applications along this line.
The proof uses the degeneration to the normal cone of rr very general
points of XX. That is, let p1,…,prp_{1},\ldots,p_{r} be very general
points in XX and π:𝒳→𝔸1\pi\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{A}^{1} be the blowup of X×𝔸1X\times\mathbb{A}^{1} at points pi×{0}p_{i}\times\{0\} for 1≤i≤r1\leq i\leq r. The central
fibre of 𝒳\mathcal{X} over 0∈𝔸10\in\mathbb{A}^{1} is reducible, having one component
which is the blowup of XX at p1,…,prp_{1},\ldots,p_{r} (with exceptional
divisor Ei≃ℙn−1E_{i}\simeq\mathbb{P}^{n-1} over pip_{i}) and rr exceptional
components Fi≃ℙnF_{i}\simeq\mathbb{P}^{n}, with each EiE_{i} glued to FiF_{i} along a
hyperplane.
Regard a collection of r​srs points in XX as coming from rr
sub-collections each consisting of ss points. Shrinking 𝔸1\mathbb{A}^{1} if
necessary for each 1≤i≤r1\leq i\leq r we can find ss sections of π\pi that
pass through very general points in Fi\EiF_{i}\backslash E_{i}. Then the inequality in
Theorem 1.1 follows by showing that the existence of highly
singular cycles at r​srs points of XX implies the existence of highly
singular cycles at r​srs points of the central fiber chosen at will,
which means cycles with higher multiplicities at the ss chosen points.
It is clear that this argument actually produces something stronger.
For instance we do not have to divide the collection of r​srs points
evenly:
Theorem 1.5.
Let XX be an nn dimensional projective variety and LL be a nef divisor on
XX. Suppose 1≤s1≤s2≤⋯≤sr1\leq s_{1}\leq s_{2}\leq\cdots\leq s_{r} are integers and
set s=∑i=1rsis=\sum_{i=1}^{r}s_{i}. Then
ϵd​(s,X,L)≥ϵd​(r,X,L)⋅ϵd​(sr,ℙn,𝒪ℙn​(1)).\epsilon_{d}\left(s;X,L\right)\geq\epsilon_{d}(r;X,L)\cdot\epsilon_{d}(s_{r};\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(1)).
We can also consider weighted Seshadri constants as in
[6], defined as follows.
In addition to the nef divisor LL on XX and rr points p1,…,prp_{1},\ldots,p_{r} in
the smooth locus of XX, let a nonzero real vector
ℓ=(l1,⋯,lr)∈ℝ+r{\bf\ell}=(l_{1},\cdots,l_{r})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{r} be given with each li≥0l_{i}\geq 0. The Seshadri constant of dimension d≤nd\leq n at these points with weights ℓ{\bf\ell}
is defined to be the real number
ϵd​(l1​p1,…,lr​pr,X,L)=inf{(Ld⋅Z∑li​multpi​Z)1/d:Z⊂X​ effective cycleof dimension ​d}.\epsilon_{d}(l_{1}p_{1},\dots,l_{r}p_{r};X,L)=\inf\left\{\left(\dfrac{L^{d}\cdot Z}{\sum l_{i}\operatorname{mult}_{p_{i}}Z}\right)^{1/d}:\begin{matrix}Z\subset X\text{ effective cycle}\\
\text{of dimension }d\end{matrix}\right\}.
Again by semicontinuity if the points are in very general position then
the weighted Seshadri constant does not depend on the actual points chosen
(2.3). Thus one can define
ϵ⁡(r,ℓ,X,L)=ϵ⁡(l1​p1,…,lr​pr,X,L)\epsilon(r,{\bf\ell};X,L)=\epsilon(l_{1}p_{1},\ldots,l_{r}p_{r};X,L)
where p1,…,prp_{1},\ldots,p_{r} is any collection of rr very general points in
XX.
Theorem 1.6.
Let XX be an nn dimensional projective variety, let LL be a nef
divisor on XX, and let r,d≥1r,d\geq 1
be integers with d≤nd\leq n. Suppose 1≤s1≤s2≤⋯≤sr1\leq s_{1}\leq s_{2}\leq\cdots\leq s_{r} are integers and
set s=∑i=1rsis=\sum_{i=1}^{r}s_{i}. For each ii let ℓi{\bf\ell}_{i} be a vector in ℝ+si\mathbb{R}_{+}^{s_{i}} and set ℓ=(ℓ1,ℓ2,…,ℓr)∈ℝ+s{\bf\ell}=({\bf\ell}_{1},{\bf\ell}_{2},\ldots,{\bf\ell}_{r})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{s}.
Then
ϵd​(s,ℓ,X,L)≥ϵd​(r,X,L)⋅infi=1,…,rϵd​(si,ℓi,ℙn,𝒪ℙn​(1)).\epsilon_{d}(s,{\bf\ell};X,L)\geq\epsilon_{d}(r;X,L)\cdot\inf_{i=1,\ldots,r}\epsilon_{d}(s_{i},{\bf\ell}_{i};\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(1)).
Remark 1.7.
Since the LL-degree and multiplicity at a point of a dd-dimensional
scheme coincide with those of the associated dd-dimensional cycle, the
Seshadri constant can be equivalently defined as
ϵd​(p1,…,pr,X,L)=inf{(Ld⋅Z∑multpi⁡Z)1/d:Z⊂X​ closed subschemeof dimension ​d}.\epsilon_{d}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{r};X,L)=\inf\left\{\left(\dfrac{L^{d}\cdot Z}{\sum\operatorname{mult}_{p_{i}}Z}\right)^{1/d}:\begin{matrix}Z\subset X\text{ closed subscheme}\\
\text{of dimension }d\end{matrix}\right\}.
For convenience, we shall work with this definition.
Conventions:
By a variety
XX we mean a possibly reducible reduced scheme of finite type
over an algebraically closed uncountable field KK.
If XX is a variety then a very general point p∈Xp\in X is a
point that lies outside a countable collection of proper subvarieties
of XX. We say that a collection of rr points p1,…,prp_{1},\ldots,p_{r} is
very general if the point (p1,…,pr)∈Xr(p_{1},\ldots,p_{r})\in X^{r} in the rr-th
power of XX is very general. The uncountable hypotheses on the base
field guarantees that a set of very general points is nonempty and
dense; over a countable or finite field claims on very general points
are void and ϵ⁡(r,X,L)\epsilon(r;X,L) is not well defined.
2. Preliminaries
2.1. Semicontinuity of Seshadri constants
Our proof of Theorem 1.1 will rely on the
semicontinuity property according to which Seshadri constants
can only “jump down” in families. When d=1d=1 this semicontinuity
is well-known and comes from interpreting Seshadri constants in terms of
ampleness of certain line bundles on a blowup of XX
[7, Thm. 5.1.1]. To deal
with the general case d≥1d\geq 1, let p:𝒳→Bp\colon\mathcal{X}\to B be a projective
flat morphism, and for b∈Bb\in B, denote 𝒳b=p−1​(b)\mathcal{X}_{b}=p^{-1}(b).
We assume that 𝒳\mathcal{X} is a variety, BB is a reduced and
irreducible scheme, and all the fibres 𝒳b\mathcal{X}_{b} are
(possibly reducible)
varieties. The following result, well known in singularity theory,
follows from the Hilbert-Samuel stratification
[8]
(in particular, from the finiteness proved in
[8, Theorem 4.15]).
Theorem 2.1.
Let 𝒴⊂𝒳\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathcal{X} be a closed subscheme, and let mm be a nonnegative integer.
The set of y∈𝒴y\in\mathcal{Y} such that 𝒴p⁡(y)\mathcal{Y}_{p(y)} has multiplicity at least mm at yy
is Zariski-closed in 𝒴\mathcal{Y}.
For our purposes, 𝒴→B\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow B will be a family of subschemes of dimension
dd in the family of (possibly reducible) varieties 𝒳→B\mathcal{X}\rightarrow B. Since we are
interested in the multiplicities at several points at a time for the computation
of Seshadri constants, we need a multipoint analogue of Theorem 2.1.
Denote 𝒳Br:=𝒳×B⋯𝑟×B𝒳→prB\mathcal{X}_{B}^{r}:=\mathcal{X}\times_{B}\overset{r}{\cdots}\times_{B}\mathcal{X}\overset{p^{r}}{\rightarrow}B
(respectively 𝒴Br→B\mathcal{Y}_{B}^{r}\rightarrow B) the family whose fiber over bb is (𝒳b)r(\mathcal{X}_{b})^{r}
(respectively (𝒴b)r(\mathcal{Y}_{b})^{r}). The next Corollary is an immediate consequence of (2.1).
Corollary 2.2.
Let 𝒴⊂𝒳\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathcal{X} be a closed subscheme, and let m1,…,mrm_{1},\ldots,m_{r} be nonnegative integers.
The set of tuples
(y1,…​yr)∈(𝒴b)r(y_{1},\ldots y_{r})\in(\mathcal{Y}_{b})^{r}, b∈Bb\in B,
such that 𝒴b\mathcal{Y}_{b} has multiplicity at least mim_{i} at each yiy_{i}
is Zariski-closed in 𝒴Br\mathcal{Y}^{r}_{B}.
Given a closed subscheme 𝒴⊂𝒳\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathcal{X} and a sequence 𝐦=(m1,…,mr)\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{r}), denote
ℐ𝐦​(𝒴)⊂𝒴Br\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathcal{Y})\subset\mathcal{Y}^{r}_{B} the closed set given by 2.2.
Fix be a relatively nef divisor ℒ\mathcal{L} on 𝒳\mathcal{X}, and denote ℒb=ℒ|𝒳b\mathcal{L}_{b}=\mathcal{L}|_{\mathcal{X}_{b}}.
Since the definition of multipoint Seshadri constants only makes sense
for distinct smooth points,
we will usually restrict to the complement of the diagonals and the
singularities of fibers in 𝒳Br\mathcal{X}^{r}_{B}, which is Zariski open.
Lemma 2.3.
Let rr be a positive integer, let 𝒳∘Br\accentset{\circ}{\mathcal{X}}_{B}^{r} be the complement of the
diagonals and the singularities in 𝒳Br\mathcal{X}^{r}_{B}, and let a real number ϵ>0\epsilon>0
and a nonzero real vector ℓ=(l1,⋯,lr){\bf\ell}=(l_{1},\cdots,l_{r}) be given with each li≥0l_{i}\geq 0.
Then the set of tuples
(p1,…,pr)∈𝒳∘Br(p_{1},\ldots,p_{r})\in\accentset{\circ}{\mathcal{X}}_{B}^{r} such that
ϵd​(l1​p1,…,lr​pr,𝒳b,ℒb)≤ϵ\epsilon_{d}(l_{1}p_{1},\ldots,l_{r}p_{r};\mathcal{X}_{b},\mathcal{L}_{b})\leq\epsilon
is the union of at most countably many
Zariski closed sets of 𝒳∘Br\accentset{\circ}{\mathcal{X}}_{B}^{r}.
Proof.
Let Hilbd⁡(𝒳/B)\operatorname{Hilb}_{d}(\mathcal{X}/B) denote the relative Hilbert scheme of subschemes of 𝒳\mathcal{X} of
(relative) dimension dd.
It has countably many irreducible components, which are irreducible projective schemes over BB
(see [9]). Let HH be one of these components, and
let ℋ⊂𝒳×BH→H\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{X}\times_{B}H\rightarrow H be the corresponding universal family.
By the standard properties
of the Hilbert scheme, the intersection with ℒb\mathcal{L}_{b} is constant, i.e., ℒbd⋅ℋb,h\mathcal{L}_{b}^{d}\cdot\mathcal{H}_{b,h}
does not depend on b∈Bb\in B, h∈Hh\in H. Denote this number by ℒd⋅H\mathcal{L}^{d}\cdot H.
For each choice of a sequence 𝐦=(m1,…,mr)\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{r}) of rr nonnegative integers,
we apply corollary 2.2 above to the universal family
ℋ→H\mathcal{H}\rightarrow H and get a Zariski closed set
ℐ𝐦​(ℋ)⊂ℋHr⊂(𝒳×BH)Hr=𝒳Br×BH.\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathcal{H})\subset\mathcal{H}_{H}^{r}\subset(\mathcal{X}\times_{B}H)_{H}^{r}=\mathcal{X}_{B}^{r}\times_{B}H.
Let 𝒫𝐦​(ℋ)⊂𝒳Br\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathcal{H})\subset\mathcal{X}^{r}_{B} be the image of ℐ𝐦​(ℋ)\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathcal{H}) by projection
on the first factor.
Since HH is projective, 𝒫𝐦​(ℋ)\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathcal{H}) is Zariski closed, and
𝒫∘𝐦​(ℋ):=𝒫𝐦​(ℋ)∩𝒳∘Br\accentset{\circ}{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathcal{H}):=\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathcal{H})\cap\accentset{\circ}{\mathcal{X}}_{B}^{r} is Zariski closed in
𝒳∘Br\accentset{\circ}{\mathcal{X}}_{B}^{r}. By the definition
of Seshadri constants, for all (p1,…,pr)∈𝒫∘𝐦​(ℋ)(p_{1},\dots,p_{r})\in\accentset{\circ}{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathcal{H})
with pi∈𝒳bp_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{b}, ϵd​(l1​p1,…,lr​pr,𝒳b,ℒb)≤(ℒd⋅H/∑li​mi)1/d\epsilon_{d}(l_{1}p_{1},\dots,l_{r}p_{r};\mathcal{X}_{b},\mathcal{L}_{b})\leq\left(\mathcal{L}^{d}\cdot H/\sum l_{i}m_{i}\right)^{1/d}.
Since every dd-dimensional subscheme is represented by a point in some component of the Hilbert scheme, it follows that
ϵd​(p1,…,pr,𝒳b,ℒb)=inf(p1,…,pr)∈𝒫∘𝐦​(ℋ){(Ld⋅H∑li​mi)1/d}.\epsilon_{d}(p_{1},\dots,p_{r};\mathcal{X}_{b},\mathcal{L}_{b})=\inf_{(p_{1},\dots,p_{r})\in\accentset{\circ}{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathcal{H})}\left\{\left(\frac{L^{d}\cdot H}{\sum l_{i}m_{i}}\right)^{1/d}\right\}.
(2.4)
Thus, for each ϵ∈ℝ\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}, the set of tuples (p1,…,pr)(p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}) such that
ϵd​(p1,…,pr,X,L)≤ϵ\epsilon_{d}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{r};X,L)\leq\epsilon is exactly
⋃{H:Ld⋅H∑li​mi≤ϵd}𝒫∘𝐦(ℋ),\bigcup_{\{H:\frac{L^{d}\cdot H}{\sum l_{i}m_{i}}\leq\epsilon^{d}\}}\accentset{\circ}{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathcal{H}),
hence the claim.
∎
Remark 2.5.
From the previous Lemma (2.3) applied to 𝒳=X\mathcal{X}=X, B=Spec⁡KB=\operatorname{Spec}\,K,
for very general points p1,…,prp_{1},\ldots,p_{r} the Seshadri constant
ϵd​(p1,…,pr,X,L)\epsilon_{d}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{r};X,L) and its weighted counterparts are independent of the
points chosen,
and thus ϵd​(r,X,L)\epsilon_{d}(r;X,L) is well defined. Moreover, (2.4)
shows that
ϵd​(r,X,L)=inf𝒫𝐦​(ℋ)=Xr{(Ld⋅H∑mi)1/d}.\epsilon_{d}(r;X,L)=\inf_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathcal{H})=X^{r}}\left\{\left(\frac{L^{d}\cdot H}{\sum m_{i}}\right)^{1/d}\right\}.
Remark 2.6.
If d=1d=1, then the infimum in (2.4) can be taken over
all 𝐦\mathbf{m} and all components ℋ\mathcal{H} of the Hilbert scheme of XX
in all dimensions (including, e.g., the isolated point
corresponding to the whole variety XX). Doing so,
the Nakai-Moishezon for ℝ\mathbb{R}-divisors
[3] implies
that the infimum is attained by some ℋ\mathcal{H} and 𝐦\mathbf{m} (see the proof
of [13, Proposition 4], and also
[4],
[1]) hence
the set of values effectively taken by the Seshadri constant as
points vary is either finite or countable.
Remark 2.7.
For surfaces, a stronger version of Lemma (2.3) is known to hold. Namely a finite number of Zariski closed sets (hence a single one) suffices, and moreover the set of values
effectively taken by the Seshadri constant is either finite or has exactly one accumulation
point which is L2/r\sqrt{L^{2}/r}. This has been proved by Oguiso [10]
for r=1r=1 and follows from Harbourne-Roé [6] for r>1r>1. Unfortunately, the methods used to
prove such finiteness do not seem to extend to varieties of
higher dimension.
Remark 2.8.
For Seshadri constants at higher dimensional centers (i.e. measuring
the multiplicities at non-closed points) as defined by Paoletti in
[11]
similar semicontinuity results hold; in this case, the Hilbert
scheme has to be used as parameter space in the place of the rrth
product of the variety.
3. Proofs of Theorems
3.1. Proof of Theorems 1.1 and 1.5
We start with Theorem 1.1 and consider the degeneration to
the normal cone of very general points p1,…,prp_{1},\ldots,p_{r} in XX. In
detail set B=𝔸1B=\mathbb{A}^{1} and let π:𝒳→X×B\pi\colon\mathcal{X}\to X\times B be the blowup
at the points p¯i=(pi,0)∈X×B\bar{p}_{i}=(p_{i},0)\in X\times B. The exceptional
divisor FF is a disjoint union F=⋃iFiF=\bigcup_{i}F_{i} where
Fi=ℙ⁡(Tpi⊕ℂ)≃ℙnF_{i}=\mathbb{P}(T_{p_{i}}\oplus\mathbb{C})\simeq\mathbb{P}^{n} is the projective
completion of the tangent space Tpi​XT_{p_{i}}X of XX at pip_{i}. We denote
by q1,q2q_{1},q_{2} the projections from X×BX\times B to the factors and let
q=q2∘π:𝒳→Bq=q_{2}\circ\pi\colon\mathcal{X}\to B.
Set δ=ϵd​(r,X,L)\delta=\epsilon_{d}(r;X,L) and fix 1≤i≤r1\leq i\leq r. By replacing BB with an open set around 00 if
necessary we can find sections σi,j′\sigma^{\prime}_{i,j} for j=1,…,sj=1,\ldots,s
which pass through pip_{i} and have very general tangent direction
there. Denote by σi,j\sigma_{i,j} the section obtained from the proper
transform of σi,j′\sigma^{\prime}_{i,j} in 𝒳\mathcal{X}. Then the collection
𝒮i={σi,1​(0),…,σi,s​(0)}\mathcal{S}_{i}=\{\sigma_{i,1}(0),\ldots,\sigma_{i,s}(0)\} is a set of ss
very general points in Fi≃ℙnF_{i}\simeq\mathbb{P}^{n}.
Denote σ′:=(σ1,1′,…,σr,s′):B→Xr​s\sigma^{\prime}:=(\sigma^{\prime}_{1,1},\dots,\sigma^{\prime}_{r,s}):B\rightarrow X^{rs}
and σ:=(σ1,1,…,σr,s):B→𝒳Br​s\sigma:=(\sigma_{1,1},\dots,\sigma_{r,s}):B\rightarrow\mathcal{X}_{B}^{rs}.
Each component of the relative Hilbert scheme of X×BX\times B is of the
form H′=Z×BH^{\prime}=Z\times B where ZZ is a component of the Hilbert scheme of
XX; for each such H′H^{\prime} there is a unique
component HH of the Hilbert scheme of 𝒳\mathcal{X} with
H′∩q2−1​(B∖{0})=H∩q−1​(B∖{0})H^{\prime}\cap q_{2}^{-1}(B\setminus\{0\})=H\cap q^{-1}(B\setminus\{0\}).
We resume notations as in the previous section, with
