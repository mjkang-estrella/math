                                                   Abstract. Consider the Monge-Kantorovich optimal transport problem between two Eu-
                                                   clidean densities µ and ν and quadratic cost. The ε-Schrödinger bridge is the solution to
                                                   the entropic regularized problem with regularization parameter ε. By taking the conditional
                                                   expectation of the second coordinate given the first under this coupling, we obtain the ε-




arXiv:2609.22595v1 [math.PR] 18 Sep 2026
                                                   entropic Brenier map. As ε goes down to zero, it is known that the entropic Brenier map
                                                   converges to the quadratic cost optimal transport map between the two measures, i.e., the
                                                   Brenier map. We show that, under some smoothness and log-concavity constraints on the
                                                   marginals, the difference between the entropic Brenier map and the Brenier map is equal
                                                   to ε times one-half of the score function of the first marginal plus an error that is o(ε) in
                                                   L2 (µ). This expansion holds irrespective of the second marginal ν. The proof relies on an
                                                   approximation of the Schrödinger bridge by a noisy version of the McCann interpolation.
                                                   Under additional assumptions we show a second approximation to the Schrödinger bridge
                                                   via so-called Mirror Langevin diffusions, which are Langevin diffusions on the Hessian man-
                                                   ifold generated by the Brenier map. These two approximations, that appear quite different
                                                   at first glance, are nonetheless shown to be closely connected. Our proofs utilize a random
                                                   surface and a novel stochastic operation called the tangent Markov projection.

Reciprocal processes are expected in this study since the dynamic Schrödinger bridge (defined
later in (41)) is itself one. However, as opposed to the dynamic Schrödinger bridge, other
reciprocal processes are not Markov processes. In fact, the dynamic Schrödinger bridge is
the unique intersection of the set of reciprocal processes (with fixed bridge distribution and
endpoint marginals) and the set of Markov processes, a fact used in the Iterative Markov
Fitting (IMF) procedure for computing Schrödinger bridges proposed by [SDBCD23, Pel23].
Since the reciprocal process with endpoint distribution ℓε is non-Markovian, we perform an
operation called the tangent Markov projection to make it Markovian. This operation is a
modification of the Markov projection studied in [Gyö86, BS13], and it is defined properly later
in Section 3.4. For now it suffices to say that a Markov projection of a stochastic process is a
Markov process (typically a diffusion) that has the same marginal distribution as the original
process at each point in time. Hence, both the stochastic process and its Markov projection
trace out the same curve of marginal probabilities, but have different joint distributions across
time. We introduce the concept of a tangent Markov projection of a stochastic process which,
roughly, is a Markov projection diffusion for which the drift is a function of the gradient type.
This is consistent with the metric theory of 2-Wasserstein spaces.

Technical Assumptions and Td Setting. The conclusion of Main Theorem 2 holds under
Assumptions 1 and 2. While Assumption 1, assuming strong log-concavity of the marginals
and the smoothness of Brenier maps, may be considered standard in this literature, Assump-
tion 2 is a more severe smoothness and growth requirement on the marginal densities of the
reciprocal processes and their derivatives. Establishing this level of regularity solely under
assumptions on µ and ν remains a technical challenge. In certain cases one may directly verify
that these assumptions hold, such as when the Brenier map from µ and ν is an affine map (for
example, when µ = ν) where it follows from [AHMP26, MP25]. We provide some examples
for which our assumptions hold: a Gaussian example in Section 8.3, and a non-Gaussian one
in Proposition 13.
To further bolster our case, we also prove an analogous statement to the process approximation
given in Main Theorem 2 on the flat d-dimensional torus Td under the simplified Assumption
3. In Section 7 we state and prove an analogous result to this main theorem on Td . We
retain the Euclidean statements with the technical assumptions for two reasons. One, in
some Euclidean cases one can directly verify them, and, two, perhaps for some other cases it
may follow from veins of existing literature (or future research) that we are not aware of.
1.1. Related Works. The most similar predecessors to this paper are previous works by the
authors and collaborators in [AHMP26, MP25]. [AHMP26] analyzes the same marginal case,
in which e−f = e−h , x∗ = ∇φ(x) = x. In this case, the noisy McCann (12) as well as both the
Primal and the Dual MLD processes (13) all reduce to the same standard Langevin diffusion.
In this case, [AHMP26, Theorem 1] establishes that the symmetric relative entropy between
π ε and ℓε = Law(X0 , Xε∗ ) is o(ε2 ). For different marginals, [MP25, Theorem 4] establishes
that H(ℓε |π ε ) is o(1) under regularity assumptions on the Brenier map.
Small Temperature Schrödinger Bridge. The convergence of the Schrödinger bridge to
the Monge Kantorovich optimal transport problem is a rich area of research dating back to
[Mik04] and recounted in [Léo12a, Léo14]. It has been studied in various levels of generality,
from Rd to manifolds and metric measure spaces. The motivation for such attention is the
use of the small temperature Schrödinger bridge and its associated quantities as proxies for
the unregularized problem in machine learning and data science applications, a perspective
initiated by [Cut13, GS09]. For a presentation of the statistical aspects of (regularized)
optimal transport, consult the recent text [CNWR25].
6                             GARRETT MULCAHY AND SOUMIK PAL


There are many ways to analyze the convergence of the Schrödinger bridge problem to the
unregularized Monge Kantorovich problem in (1) as ε ↓ 0. One approach is to study the
convergence of entropic cost (the optimal value of the objective function in (3)) to W22 . For
a slightly more general cost, such an analysis is achieved via the so-called Gaussian ap-
proximation in [Pal24]. A more abstract analytical argument in [CT21] studies regularity
of ε ∈ [0, +∞) 7→ H(π ε |rε ) and establishes the first three terms of its Taylor expansion
about ε = 0. This Taylor expansion plays a key role in our proofs, see Section 2. More
recent works have studied this entropic cost convergence for different classes of cost functions
[CPT23, MS25, NZ26, AG25]. More general Γ-convergence and large deviation statements
are given in [Léo14, BGN22].
Similarly, the convergence of entropic potentials in (5) to their unregularized counterparts
(and their gradients) has received a great deal of attention in the literature. For the conver-
gence of the potentials to the so-called Kantorovich potential, [NW22] establishes this for a
large class of cost functions on Polish spaces via a weak compactness argument. The corre-
sponding gradient limit in the Euclidean quadratic cost setting was established in [PNW22] as
a consequence of an empirical process analysis. A more general gradient limit was established
in [CCGT23] in the manifold setting based on estimates provided from a curvature-dimension
condition. Other related works have studied convexity bounds for the entropic potentials and
how these persist under vanishing regularization [NW23, CP23, DNWP25, GS25, FGP20].
There has been limited progress in determining the rate of convergence for potentials, a chal-
lenge that we take up here.
