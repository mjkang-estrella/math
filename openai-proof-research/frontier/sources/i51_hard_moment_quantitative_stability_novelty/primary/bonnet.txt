                                                                                             Abstract
                                                     The moment measure problem consists in finding a convex function ψ whose moment
                                                 measure, i.e., the pushforward by ∇ψ of the measure with density e−ψ( · ) , is prescribed. It is
                                                 highly non-linear and less understood than the related optimal transport problem. We establish
                                                 a quantitative stability estimate for this problem. This estimate validates, as well as leads us to
                                                 introduce, an approach to the numerical resolution of the moment measure problem inspired
                                                 by semi-discrete optimal transport, consisting in approximating the prescribed measure by a
                                                 finitely supported one. We describe a Newton method for solving the discrete problem thus
                                                 obtained, and perform numerical experiments, studying the experimental rates of convergence
                                                 of the approximation beyond the predictions of the stability estimate.

1    Introduction
The moment measure of a convex function ψ : Rd → R ∪ {∞} is defined as the pushforward of the
measure e−ψ(x) dx by the map ∇ψ,

                                       mm(ψ) := (∇ψ)# (e−ψ(x) dx)

(noting that ∇ψ(x) is defined for Lebesgue a.e. x by Rademacher’s theorem). The moment measure
problem (MMP) consists of inverting mm, or more precisely, of finding a convex function ψ whose
moment measure is a given (prescribed) positive, finite mass, measure µ on Rd ,

                                                 mm(ψ) = µ.                                          (1.1)

When the measure µ admits a density f , i.e., µ = f (y) dy, the MMP is a weak formulation of

    As illustrated by the formulation (1.1), mm is a highly non-linear second-order operator. In
particular, it is not as well-understood as the closely related, classical, Monge–Ampère operator.
Our aim in this article is twofold: (i) to study the first numerical method for the MMP, (ii) to
establish a quantitative stability estimate for solutions to (1.1). Of course, (i) hinges on (ii), since
our goal is, naturally, to prove that the numerical method converges to the solution.
    In retrospect, it is in some sense a miracle of convexity that mm even possesses a decent
pluripotential theory reminiscent of the one for the much simpler Monge–Ampère operator.
    The study of the so-called second boundary value problem (1.2) originates in the problem of
finding Kähler–Ricci solitons on toric manifolds. There, ψ is assumed to have linear growth so that
supp µ is a (bounded) convex   body, (in fact, a polytope P ), and f is of the form e⟨a, · ⟩ for a constant
vector a ∈ R so that P f dy = 0, i.e., µ is centered. Under these assumptions, a foundational

    In order to prove Theorem 1.3, we consider an intermediate problem in which we look for a
convex function which is not necessarily essentially continuous, but whose support is included in
some given compact convex set K with nonempty interior. This intermediate problem, which we call
the K-moment measure problem (K-MMP), may be interpreted as replacing Rd by K in the PDE
formulation (1.2). When K is large, it can be interpreted as an approximation of the space Rd . In
addition to making the proof of Theorem 1.3 cleaner, we believe that the K-MMP is of independent
interest : for instance, it arises naturally in some approaches to the numerical resolution of the
moment measure problem [17]. It has only been briefly discussed in the literature [23, 27], so we
give some details in subsection 2.2.
    In this approach, the proof of Theorem 1.3 naturally boils down to establishing two estimates:
   • A quantitative stability estimate (Proposition 3.3) for the K-MMP just discussed.

   • An error estimate (Proposition 3.4) between the K-MMP and the original MMP.

1.2    Numerical resolution
We study a numerical method consisting in approximating ψµ by the function ψν , where ν is a
finitely supported measure that should be chosen close to µ in the 1-Wasserstein distance. Thanks
to ν being finitely supported, the problem of computing ψν becomes a discrete problem that can be
solved computationally.
    This numerical method can be interpreted as a rather direct translation of the idea of semidiscrete
optimal transport [1, 18, 22, 24, 25] to the setting of moment measures. Its validity in this setting is
justified by our stability estimate, Theorem 1.3.
    In our experiments, we compute ψν using a damped Newton method, that we describe in section 4.
We investigate the effect of the choice of ν on the convergence of this Newton method and on the
approximation error between ψµ and ψν . We observe that, experimentally, the rates of convergence
of the numerical method with respect to W1 (µ, ν) are typically better than what is guaranteed by
Theorem 1.3, and that the convergence can be improved by applying some specific rules for the
construction of the empirical measure ν.

1.3    Relation to other work
The proof of Theorem 1.3 makes use of the sharp quantitative stability estimate for the Prékopa–
Leindler inequality recently obtained by Figalli–van Hintum–Tiba [13]. It is also inspired by
quantitative stability estimates for the optimal transport problem, see e.g. [8]; especially, in both
settings, the 1-Wasserstein distance is made to appear using Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality. The
two main differences between (1.6) and similar estimates in the optimal transport setting, such as
the one in [8], are the weaker norm appearing on the left-hand side of (1.6), and the logarithm on
the right-hand side. The former originates from the estimate in [13], and can be explained by the
fact that the energy functional associated to the moment measure problem is different from the
one associated to the optimal transport problem. The logarithm appears because the Legendre


                                                   5
transform of the potential ψµ in the moment measure problem is typically not Lipschitz continuous;
our intermediary result Proposition 3.4 may be interpreted as an estimate of this lack of Lipschitz
continuity.
    Quantitative stability of the moment measure problem was also recently and independently
studied by Machado–Ramos [23]. A version of our Proposition 3.3 can also be found in [23], while,
to our knowledge, Proposition 3.4, and thus Theorem 1.3, are original. Some stability estimates for
the moment measure problem in the standard, non-compact case are also proved in [23], but under
different assumptions and with smaller exponents. Observe that [23] also features estimates for the
error between the Legendre transforms of ψµ and ψν , which is a topic that we do not discuss herein.
Quantitative stability for a quadratically regularized version of the moment measure problem was
also studied by Delalande–Farinelli in [7].
    The idea of adapting techniques from semidiscrete optimal transport to the numerical resolution
of the moment measure problem is rather natural. Klartag–Mérigot–Santambrogio previously
performed numerical experiments using this approach and presented results in some talks, e.g., [20].
    Finite-difference discretization of the system (1.2) was first considered in the thesis of Ji [17]. In
a forthcoming article joint with Ji [3] we synthesize our approach here with his, and also pursue
applications to geometry, such as the Ricci flow and Kähler–Ricci solitons.
    It is worth pointing out that the (special) moment measure problems arising in Kähler geometry
have attracted intense efforts in the mathematical physics community, as they describe Kähler–Ricci
solitons that in turns can be used to construct Klebanov–Tseytlin like supergravity solutions, see,
e.g., [4, 10, 11, 12, 14, 15, 16]. The numerical results in those works employ completely different
methods, based mainly on quantization (as opposed to PDE, as in the present article). Moreover,
some of these works have attempted to use such solitons to study the Ricci flow, that itself has
applications to renormalization flow in the mathematical physics literature.

1.4    Outline
In section 2, after briefly reviewing some aspects of the theory of moment measures, we discuss
the intermediary problem involving a compact approximation of Rd that we use in the proof of
Theorem 1.3. Section 3 is devoted to the proof of Theorem 1.3, and of the intermediary results
Proposition 3.3 and Proposition 3.4. In section 4, we discuss the approximation of ψµ by ψν , where ν
is a finitely supported measure, and a Newton method for computing ψν . We present some numerical
results in section 5.

4     Semidiscrete MMP
In this section, we assume that ν is a centered probability measure on Rd that is not supported on a
hyperplane and that has finite support. The measure ν should be thought of as approximating the
measure µ for which we are trying to solve the MMP, i.e., find mm−1 (µ) (1.5). Then, according
to Theorem 1.3, ψν (1.4) is an approximation of ψµ (modulo a translation). The idea is then to
recast the MMP for the finitely-supported ν as the minimization of an energy over RN . The latter,
in turn, is equivalent to considering piecewise affine competitors for a bona fide MMP. Here, one
makes use of Legendre duality to go back and forth between vectors and such competitors. This
approach gives a solution of the MMP for such ν (Proposition 4.2).
    The discrete problem and its associated energy functional Eν is introduced in subsection 4.1,
and its relation to the original (continuous) MMP and its energy Eν is described in Propositions 4.1
and 4.2. In subsection 4.2, we compute the first and second derivatives of the discrete energy
functional Eν . This is motivated by subsection 4.3, in which we describe a damped Newton method
for solving the discrete MMP.

4.3    Damped Newton method
In view of the above computations, one can attempt to compute a minimizer of Eν using a damped
Newton method, that we describe as Algorithm 4.1. Observe that this method is largely inspired by
a similar approach used in the setting of the optimal transform problem, see e.g. [18].

Require: a tolerance ε > 0, an initialization Φ(0) ∈ U .

that all iterates belong to the set U . This is motivated by the fact that the formulas (4.7) and (4.8)

Results and comments. The errors between ψµ , φµ and ψν , φν in all test cases and for some
values of the discretization parameter n are displayed in Figure 1.
    In test cases 1 and 2, all errors experimentally scale like N −1/2 , i.e., like W1 (µ, ν). This is better
than the rate W1 (µ, ν)1/2 that could have been expected in view of Theorem 1.3, and of the estimate
(5.5). This result is not surprising, as we do not know if the exponent in Theorem 1.3 is optimal.
    In test cases 3 and 4, the modified rule for choosing ν leads to experimentally larger rates of
convergence in the L2 (ν) and L1 (ν) norms. This highlights the importance of the choice of ν, beyond
what is reflected by the Wasserstein distance W1 (µ, ν), which scales like N −1/2 in all first fourth test
cases. We however do not currently know any theoretical guarantee that following the approach used
in test cases 3 and 4 for choosing the measure ν always leads to such improved rates of convergence.
The rate of convergence in the L∞ norm is experimentally not improved, although the asymptotic
constant seems smaller than in test cases 1 and 2.
    In test case 5, all errors scale like N −1 . This improvement of the rates of convergence is consistent
with the fact that supp ν has been chosen to be adapted to the regularity of φµ .
    The residuals at each iteration of Algorithm 4.1 in all experiments are displayed in Figure 2.
The results in test cases 1, 2, 3, and 4 are similar, and illustrate superlinear convergence of the
Newton method, up to some artifacts in the last iterations that may be related to numerical precision
issues. Damping experimentally only occurs in the first few iterations of Algorithm 4.1. In test
case 5, superlinear convergence is still observed, but more iterations are required. Specifically,
the number of iterations at which damping occurs is larger, and grows faster with respect to the
discretization parameter n. This could be related to the fact that, for a fixed value of n, the quantity
miny∈supp ν ν({y}) is significantly smaller in test case 5 than in the other cases.
