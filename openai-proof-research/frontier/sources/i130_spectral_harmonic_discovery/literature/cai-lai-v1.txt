Abstract.
Let \mathcal{H}_{d}(M) denote the space of harmonic functions of polynomial growth at most degree d on a complete Riemannian manifold. Yau raised two fundamental questions regarding \mathcal{H}_{d}(M) on complete manifolds with nonnegative Ricci curvature. The first question is the finite dimensionality of \mathcal{H}_{d}(M), which is confirmed by Colding and Minicozzi. The second question asks whether a sharp upper bound by its Euclidean analog \operatorname{dim}\mathcal{H}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n}) is true. We verify that if (M,g) is a locally conformally flat manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature, then the sharp Euclidean bound holds, i.e., \operatorname{dim}(\mathcal{H}_{d}(M))\leq\operatorname{dim}(\mathcal{H}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n})).
1. Introduction
The study of harmonic functions on complete noncompact Riemannian manifolds with nonnegative Ricci curvature is a central topic connecting geometric analysis, potential theory, and the large-scale geometry of manifolds. The subject originated with Yau’s classical Liouville theorem [29], which establishes that every positive harmonic function must be constant on a complete manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature. The local gradient estimates of Cheng and Yau [6] subsequently provided a fundamental analytic tool, producing Harnack inequalities and quantitative control of positive harmonic functions under lower Ricci curvature bounds. Li and Tam [18, 21] developed a complementary potential-theoretic approach that relates spaces of bounded and positive harmonic functions to the number and geometry of the ends of a manifold.
Yau’s seminal result also opened an interesting direction of research : the study of the space of harmonic functions with at most polynomial growth.
For every fixed d>0, let
\mathcal{H}_{d}(M):=\{u:\Delta u=0,\ |u(x)|\leq C(1+r(x))^{d}\}
denote the space of harmonic functions of polynomial growth of at most d. Yau raised two fundamental questions regarding \mathcal{H}_{d}(M) when M has nonnegative Ricci curvature:
Q1 (finite dimensionality): is it true that \dim\mathcal{H}_{d}(M)<\infty?
Q2 (sharp Euclidean comparison): is it true that \dim\mathcal{H}_{d}(M)\leq\dim\mathcal{H}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n})?
For brevity, write h_{d}(M)=\dim\mathcal{H}_{d}(M). Due to the gradient estimate, any harmonic function with polynomial growth on \mathbb{R}^{n} must be a harmonic polynomial. For any fixed integer d\geq 1, it is a fun exercise to show that
\displaystyle h_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\binom{n+d-1}{n-1}+\binom{n+d-2}{n-1}.
Regarding Q1, Li-Tam [20] first established this for surfaces with finite total curvature. Colding and Minicozzi [8] verified the finite dimensionality of \mathcal{H}_{d}(M) in general dimension. Li [16] also gave an independent proof.
Later, Colding and Minicozzi [9] proved a Weyl type bound
h_{d}(M)\leq C(n)\operatorname{vol}(M)d^{n-1}+o(d^{n-1}),
which is asymptotically sharp in the power of d since h_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\sim\frac{2}{(n-1)!}d^{n-1} as d\to\infty.
For Q2, the case d=1 is known by [19]. One also has rigidity in view of [7]. More precisely, let M be a complete noncompact manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature, and suppose that h_{1}(M)=h_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=n+1, then M must be isometric to \mathbb{R}^{n}.
However, for d>1, the Euclidean comparison is false. Donnelly[10] constructed, in every dimension n\geq 5, a complete noncompact manifold with \operatorname{Ric}\geq 0 carrying more than n+1 linearly independent harmonic functions of subquadratic growth. Hence, for a suitable 1<d<2, Donnelly’s example verifies
h_{d}(M)>h_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=n+1.
Nevertheless, the integer-degree Euclidean comparison remains an open question. Under the assumption of maximal volume growth and uniqueness of the tangent cone at infinity, Huang [11, 12] established the Euclidean comparison asymptotically with a sharp coefficient, i.e.,
\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{h_{d}(M)}{d^{n-1}}=\frac{2\beta}{(n-1)!},
where \beta is the asymptotic volume ratio of M.
Li-Wang [22] proved a similar result when M has nonnegative sectional curvature. Indeed, the Euclidean comparison is still an open question on non-negatively curved manifolds. There is a recent breakthrough by Lin-Wang-Xu[24], in which the authors showed that on a three-dimensional manifold with nonnegative sectional curvature and \beta>0, for any integer d\geq 1, h_{d}(M^{3})\leq h_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{3}). Moreover, if the equality holds for some d, then M is isometric to \mathbb{R}^{3}.
In this paper, we give an affirmative answer to Q2 on locally conformally flat manifolds:
Main Theorem: let (M^{n},g)(n\geq 3) be a complete noncompact locally conformally flat manifolds with nonnegative Ricci curvature, then for each fixed d\geq 0
h_{d}(M)\leq h_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).
Moreover, if the equality holds for some integer d\geq 1, then M is isometric to \mathbb{R}^{n}.
Let (M,g) be a complete noncompact manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature. Denote by \beta the asymptotic volume ratio of (M,g), i.e.,
\beta=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{\operatorname{vol}(B(p,r))}{\omega_{n}r^{n}}.
The above limit is independent of the choice of the base point p\in M. When \beta>0, M is said to have Euclidean volume growth.
We establish our main result separately in two cases: \beta=0 and \beta>0. Based on the characterization of complete
conformally flat manifolds with nonnegative Ricci curvature, the main assertions that lead to the main conclusion are as follows
Theorem 1.1.
Let g=e^{2f}g_{0} be a smooth and complete metric on \mathbb{R}^{n} with nonnegative Ricci curvature, suppose \beta=0, then for every finite d\geq 0,
\mathcal{H}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n},g)=\mathbb{R}.
Theorem 1.2.
Let g=e^{2f}g_{0} be a smooth and complete metric on \mathbb{R}^{n} with nonnegative Ricci curvature, suppose \beta\in(0,1), then for every integer d\geq 1,
\dim\mathcal{H}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n},g)\leq\dim\mathcal{H}_{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n},g_{0})<\dim\mathcal{H}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n},g_{0}).
In spirit, the study in this paper can be regarded as an extension of the Li-Tam’s result [20] to higher dimension. As a precursor for the main result, we first need to establish a comparison between the distance induced by the conformal metric g=e^{2f}g_{0} and the underlying Euclidean distance.
Theorem 1.3.
Let g=e^{2f}g_{0} be a conformal metric on \mathbb{R}^{n} with nonnegative Ricci curvature and \beta>0. Let r(x) denote the distance function to the origin induced by g, then
(1.1)
\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln r(x)}{\ln|x|}=1-m.
A key technical input to prove Theorem 1.3 is the asymptotic analysis of the conformal factor by Ma-Qing[27]. Under the assumptions as in Theorem 1.3, then there exists m\in[0,1] such that
\liminf_{x\to\infty}\frac{f(x)}{\ln|x|}=-m.
Moreover, there exists an exceptional set E, which is n-thin at infinity such that
\lim_{x\to\infty,x\notin E}\frac{f(x)}{\ln|x|}=-m.
In a recent breakthrough [26], Ma refined the above estimate to the following: setting \underline{f}(r)=\inf_{|x|=r}f(x), there exists a strong \mathcal{E}-set E, such that
(1.2)
\displaystyle f(x)=\underline{f}(|x|)+o(1),\quad x\to\infty\quad\text{and}\quad x\notin E.
Using this refined radial symmetry of f, Ma [26, Theorem 1.2] showed that
(1.3)
\displaystyle\beta^{\frac{1}{n-1}}=1-m.
Moreover, he provided a precise value for the scale-invariant integral of scalar curvature \lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{r^{n-2}}\int_{B_{r}}R_{g}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g} on locally conformally flat manifolds with nonnegative Ricci curvature.
The proof of Theorem 1.2 is heavily dependent on the finer analysis of the conformal factor. We also remark that the relation (1.1) is implicitly contained in Ma’s argument, which even holds when \beta=0.
The organization of the paper is as follows. In Section 2, we prove Theorem 1.1. In Section 3, we provide a proof of Theorem 1.3 even though this result is now subsumed by [26]. In Section 4, we discuss the blow-down scheme and weighted G convergence. In Section 5, we prove Theorem 1.2. In the last section, we conclude the proof of the main result.
2. The case with vanishing asymptotic volume ratio
Kasue and Carron proved Liouville theorems under sublinear diameter growth of geodesic spheres [13, 14, 3]. Carron’s formulation is particularly convenient here
Proposition 2.1.
Let (M^{n},g) be complete with \operatorname{Ric}_{g}\geq 0. Assume that, for some p\in M,
\operatorname{diam}_{g}\bigl(\partial B_{R}^{g}(p)\bigr)=o(R).
Then every harmonic function of polynomial growth of finite degree in M must be constant.
Proof of Theorem 1.1.
Adopting the notation in [26], i.e., set \underline{f}(r)=\inf_{|x|=r}f(x), in view of the assumption that \beta=0,
it follows that \lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{\underline{f}(r)}{\ln r}=-1. Consider the radial metric \underline{g}=e^{2\underline{f}}g_{0}. Introducing the intrinsic radial coordinates
\rho(r)=\int_{0}^{r}e^{\underline{f}(s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s and the warping function
a(\rho)=re^{\underline{f}(r)},
we find
\underline{g}=d\rho^{2}+a(\rho)^{2}g_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}.
Based on the asymptotic radial symmetry (1.2), Ma [26, Page 29] showed that
(2.1)
\displaystyle B^{\underline{g}}_{R}\subset B^{g}_{R}\subset B^{\underline{g}}_{R+o(R)},
Combining this with the volume growth assumption \beta=0 and f\geq\underline{f}, we get
\displaystyle 0=\lim_{R\to+\infty}\frac{vol_{g}(B^{g}(p,R))}{|\mathbb{B}^{n}|R^{n}}
\displaystyle\geq\lim_{R\to+\infty}\frac{vol_{\underline{g}}(B^{\underline{g}}(p,R))}{|\mathbb{B}^{n}|R^{n}}
\displaystyle=\lim_{R\to+\infty}\frac{area_{\underline{g}}(\partial B^{\underline{g}}(p,R))}{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|R^{n-1}}=\lim_{\rho\to+\infty}\frac{a^{n-1}(\rho)}{\rho^{n-1}}
It follows that
(2.2)
a^{\prime}(\rho)\to 0,\qquad\frac{a(\rho)}{\rho}\to 0,\qquad\text{as }\rho\to+\infty.
Therefore,
\operatorname{diam}(\partial B^{\underline{g}}_{R})\leq\pi a(R)=o(R).
By 2.1 one easily see that \operatorname{diam}(\partial B^{g}_{R})=o(R) as well. Hence the conclusion follows from above-mentioned Carron’s criterion.
∎
We also include a simple ODE argument for radially symmetric conformal metric (\mathbb{R}^{n},g=e^{2f(|x|)}g_{0}) with nonnegative Ricci curvature and \beta=0.
Theorem 2.2 (Radial case).
Let
g=e^{2f(|x|)}g_{0}
be a complete and smooth rotationally symmetric metric on \mathbb{R}^{n} with nonnegative Ricci curvature. Assume
\beta=0, then for every d\geq 0
\mathcal{H}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n},g)=\mathbb{R}.
Proof.
As above, we can now assume
g=d\rho^{2}+a(\rho)^{2}g_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}} under the intrinsic radial coordinates and a(\rho) satisfies (2.2).
Let u be a smooth harmonic function of polynomial growth. Expand it in spherical harmonics:
u(\rho,\theta)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m}v_{\ell,m}(\rho)Y_{\ell,m}(\theta),
where
-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}Y_{\ell,m}=\lambda_{\ell}Y_{\ell,m},\qquad\lambda_{\ell}=\ell(\ell+n-2).
Every coefficient satisfies
(2.3)
v^{\prime\prime}+(n-1)\frac{a^{\prime}}{a}v^{\prime}-\frac{\lambda_{\ell}}{a^{2}}v=0.
For \ell=0, equation (2.3) gives
(a^{n-1}v^{\prime})^{\prime}=0.
The regularity at the origin forces v^{\prime}=0, so the radial mode is constant.
Now suppose \ell\geq 1. A nonzero coefficient that is regular at the origin has, after changing its sign if necessary,
v(\rho)>0,\qquad v^{\prime}(\rho)>0
for all \rho>0. Indeed, the divergence form
(a^{n-1}v^{\prime})^{\prime}=\lambda_{\ell}a^{n-3}v
prevents a positive regular solution from acquiring a first zero or a first nonpositive derivative.
Define
s(\rho)=\int_{\rho_{0}}^{\rho}\frac{dt}{a(t)}.
In the s-variable, (2.3) becomes
v_{ss}+(n-2)a^{\prime}(\rho(s))v_{s}-\lambda_{\ell}v=0.
Set q=v_{s}/v>0. Then
(2.4)
q^{\prime}=\lambda_{\ell}-(n-2)a^{\prime}q-q^{2}.
Since \beta=0, we could argue as before to derive
(2.5)
\displaystyle 0=\lim_{R\to+\infty}\frac{vol_{g}(B^{g}(p,R))}{|\mathbb{B}^{n}|R^{n}}=\lim_{R\to+\infty}\frac{area_{g}(\partial B^{g}(p,R))}{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|R^{n-1}}=\lim_{\rho\to+\infty}\frac{a^{n-1}(\rho)}{\rho^{n-1}}=\lim_{\rho\to+\infty}a^{\prime}(\rho)^{n-1}.
Choose c>0 and S sufficiently large so that
c^{2}+(n-2)a^{\prime}(\rho(s))c<\lambda_{\ell}\qquad(s\geq S).
Equation (2.4) shows that q cannot cross the level c downward for large s, and if q<c for a long interval, then q^{\prime} is uniformly positive. Hence, after increasing S,
q(s)\geq c\qquad(s\geq S).
Therefore
(2.6)
v(\rho)\geq C\exp\bigl(cs(\rho)\bigr).
Finally, (2.5) implies
(2.7)
\frac{s(\rho)}{\log\rho}\longrightarrow\infty.
Indeed, for every \varepsilon>0, one has a(t)\leq\varepsilon t for all sufficiently large t, and hence
s(\rho)\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\varepsilon}}.
Combining (2.6) and (2.7), every nonzero mode with \ell\geq 1 grows faster than every power of \rho. Since u has polynomial growth, all such coefficients vanish. Only the constant \ell=0 mode remains.
∎
3. Distance comparison for Euclidean volume growth
We carry out the proof of Theorem 1.2 in the following three sections. The general strategy is inspired by Lin’s work on asymptotically conic elliptic operators [23]. In this section, we compare the intrinsic distance of the conformal metric with the Euclidean distance, thereby translating intrinsic polynomial growth into growth estimates adapted to Euclidean rescaling. In Section 4, we establish a weighted compactness theorem for the rescaled harmonic equations and show that normalized blow-down sequences converge to harmonic functions on a fixed metric cone. In Section 5, we exploit the spectral decomposition and logarithmic convexity on the limiting cone to obtain a uniform doubling propagation. A scale-dependent Gram-Schmidt procedure then allows us to blow down a finite-dimensional space of harmonic functions simultaneously while preserving linear independence, leading to the desired dimension estimate.
In this section, we shall prove Theorem 1.3. Two fundamental constants that we frequently refer to are:
•
\beta: the asymptotic volume ratio of a complete noncompact manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature, i.e.,
\beta=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{\operatorname{vol}(B(p,r))}{\omega_{n}r^{n}}.
•
m: the asymptotic exponent of the conformal factor. Let (\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{2\phi}g_{0}),(n\geq 3) be a complete Riemannian manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature, then there exists m\in[0,1] (see [27, Theorem 1.3]), such that
\liminf_{x\to\infty}\frac{\phi(x)}{\ln|x|}=-m.
We shall first derive a technical tool: a refined Harnack inequality Proposition 3.2. Then we divide the proof into two steps, following the strategy of Li-Tam [20]. The first step appeals to the pointwise lower bound of the conformal factor \phi due to Ma-Qing, and a lower bound of the g-volume of the Euclidean ball is obtained. Then an elementary inclusion relation between the geodesic ball and the Euclidean ball implies that
\displaystyle\liminf_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln r(x)}{\ln|x|}\geq 1-m.
The refined Harnack inequality is used in this part.
The difficulty of the second step lies in the fact that there is no pointwise upper bound of the conformal factor \phi. In contrast, inspired by [27, Theorem 3.1, Proposition 4.1], an upper bound of the g-volume of the Euclidean ball could be derived via several involved results in nonlinear potential theory. It follows analogously that
\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln r(x)}{\ln|x|}\leq 1-m.
Once again, the refined Harnack inequality Proposition 3.2 is used in this step.
We also include a proof relating the the comparison of two distance functions to the asymptotic volume ratio \beta. The conformal invariance of the n-Laplace operator indicates that \ln|x| is the n-Green function on (M^{n},g). Meanwhile, \int_{1}^{r(x)}A(t)^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t is the n-Green function when (M^{n},g) is radially symmetric. Combining with the refined Harnack inequality, a comparison principle argument is applied to these two functions to show that
\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln r(x)}{\ln|x|}\leq\beta^{\frac{1}{n-1}}.
Remark 3.1.
In view of (2.1), the conclusion of Theorem 1.3 follows. Moreover it also holds when \beta=0. We present our proof anyway as it has independent interest.
3.1. A refined Harnack inequality
In this subsection, we prove a refined Harnack inequality which serves as a basis analytical tool in each step.
Proposition 3.2 (Refined Harnack inequality).
Let (\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{2\phi}g_{0}) be a complete Riemannian manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature and \beta>0.
Then
\displaystyle s(R)\leq i(R)e^{o(1)}\quad\text{as }R\to+\infty,
where s(r):=\sup_{\partial B^{g}(0,r)}\ln|x|, i(r):=\inf_{\partial B^{g}(0,r)}\ln|x|.
We first establish several simple lemmas.
Lemma 3.3.
Let (\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{2\phi}g_{0}) be a complete Riemannian manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature. Denote r(x):=\operatorname{dist}_{g}(x,0) the distance to the origin.
Then
\displaystyle-\Delta_{n}\ln r(x)\geq 0.
Proof.
The proof is by a direct calculation.
\displaystyle-\Delta_{n}^{g}\ln r
\displaystyle=-\mathrm{div}^{g}(|\nabla^{g}\ln r|^{n-2}\nabla^{g}\ln r)=-\mathrm{div}^{g}(r^{1-n}\nabla^{g}r)
\displaystyle=r^{1-n}(-\Delta^{g}r+\frac{n-1}{r})\geq 0.
∎
Lemma 3.4.
Let (\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{2\phi}g_{0}) be a complete Riemannian manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature.
Then s(r):=\sup_{\partial B^{g}(0,r)}\ln|x|,\ i(r):=\inf_{\partial B^{g}(0,r)}\ln|x| are both non-decreasing functions as r\to+\infty.
Proof.
By the conformal invariance of the n-Laplace operator, we have
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}^{g}\ln|x|=0,\quad\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}.
Note that \ln|x|\to-\infty as x\to 0, we could apply the maximum principle to \ln r_{0} on B^{g}(0,R_{2})\setminus B^{g}(0,r), where 0<r\ll 1,\ r<R_{2}, to conclude that
\displaystyle\sup_{\partial B^{g}(0,R_{1})}\ln|x|\leq\sup_{\partial B^{g}(0,R_{2})}\ln|x|,\quad\forall\,0<R_{1}<R_{2}.
Similarly, noting that \ln|x|\to+\infty as x\to\infty, we apply the minimum principle to \ln r_{0} on B^{g}(0,R)\setminus B^{g}(0,r_{1}), where 0<r_{1}<R,\ 1\ll R, to conclude that
\displaystyle\inf_{\partial B^{g}(0,r_{2})}\ln|x|\geq\inf_{\partial B^{g}(0,r_{1})}\ln|x|,\quad\forall\,0<r_{1}<r_{2}.
∎
Lemma 3.5.
Let (\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{2\phi}g_{0}) be a complete Riemannian manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature. Define G(x):=\ln|x| and f(x)=(n-1)^{2}\frac{|\nabla^{g}G|_{g}^{2}}{G^{2}} on \mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(0,e). Then for B^{g}(p,R)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(0,e), the following holds.
(1)
There exist constants b_{1}=b_{1}(n)\geq n^{3} and C=C(n)>0 such that
\displaystyle|f|_{L^{\infty}(B^{g}(p,\frac{R}{2}))}\leq C(n)\left(\fint_{B^{g}(p,\frac{3R}{4})}f^{b_{1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}\right)^{\frac{1}{b_{1}}},
where \fint_{B^{g}(p,R)}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}:=\frac{1}{Vol_{g}(B^{g}(p,R))}\int_{B^{g}(p,R)}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}.
(2)
There exists a constant C(n) such that
\displaystyle|f|_{L^{\infty}(B^{g}(p,\frac{R}{2}))}\leq C(n)\left(\fint_{B^{g}(p,\frac{3R}{4})}f^{\frac{n}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}\right)^{\frac{2}{n}}.
Proof.
By conformal invariance of the n-Laplace operator, there holds
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}^{g}G=\Delta_{n}G=0,\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(0,e).
Define u:=-(n-1)\ln G, by a straightforward computation, it follows
\displaystyle\mathrm{div}^{g}(|\nabla^{g}u|_{g}^{n-2}\nabla^{g}u)=|\nabla^{g}u|_{g}^{n}.
Moreover, notice that f=|\nabla^{g}u|_{g}^{2}, a Moser iteration argument as that in [28, Theorem 1.1] would derive the item 1 (see [28, (2.18)]) verbatim.
Now we shall deduce the item 2.
We claim that for any \sigma\in(0,1), there holds
\displaystyle|f|_{L^{\infty}(B^{g}(y,\sigma R))}\leq\frac{C(n)}{(1-\sigma)^{2}}\left(\fint_{B^{g}(y,R)}f^{\frac{n}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}\right)^{\frac{2}{n}},\quad\text{provided }B^{g}(y,R)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(0,e),
from which the item 2 follows.
In fact, fix some B^{g}(y,R)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(0,e). Notice that B^{g}(x,(1-\sigma)R)\subset B^{g}(y,R)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(0,e) if x\in B^{g}(y,\sigma R), the item 1 implies
\displaystyle|f(x)|
\displaystyle\leq C(n)\left(\fint_{B^{g}(x,\frac{3}{4}(1-\sigma)R)}f^{b_{1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}\right)^{\frac{1}{b_{1}}}
\displaystyle\leq C(n)\left(\frac{\operatorname{vol}_{g}(B^{g}(y,R))}{\operatorname{vol}_{g}(B^{g}(x,\frac{3}{4}(1-\sigma)R))}\fint_{B^{g}(y,R)}f^{b_{1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}\right)^{\frac{1}{b_{1}}}
\displaystyle=C(n)\left(\frac{\operatorname{vol}_{g}(B^{g}(y,R))}{\operatorname{vol}_{g}(B^{g}(x,(1+\sigma)R)}\,\frac{\operatorname{vol}_{g}(B^{g}(x,(1+\sigma)R)}{\operatorname{vol}_{g}(B^{g}(x,\frac{3}{4}(1-\sigma)R))}\fint_{B^{g}(y,R)}f^{b_{1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}\right)^{\frac{1}{b_{1}}}
\displaystyle\leq C(n)\frac{1}{(1-\sigma)^{\frac{n}{b_{1}}}}\left(\fint_{B^{g}(y,R)}f^{b_{1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}\right)^{\frac{1}{b_{1}}},
where in the last line, we used the fact that B^{g}(y,R)\subset B^{g}(x,(1+\sigma)R) and the Bishop-Gromov volume comparison theorem.
It follows from Young’s inequality that
\displaystyle\sup_{B^{g}(y,\sigma R)}|f|
\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(n)}{(1-\sigma)^{\frac{n}{b_{1}}}}\left(\fint_{B^{g}(y,R)}f^{b_{1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}\right)^{\frac{1}{b_{1}}}
\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(n)}{(1-\sigma)^{\frac{n}{b_{1}}}}(\sup_{B^{g}(y,R)}|f|)^{1-\frac{n}{2b_{1}}}\left(\fint_{B^{g}(y,R)}f^{\frac{n}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}\right)^{\frac{1}{b_{1}}}
\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sup_{B^{g}(y,R)}|f|+\frac{n}{2b_{1}}(2-\frac{n}{b_{1}})^{\frac{2b_{1}}{n}(1-\frac{n}{2b_{1}})}\frac{C(n)}{(1-\sigma)^{2}}\left(\fint_{B^{g}(y,R)}f^{\frac{n}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}\right)^{\frac{2}{n}}.
Then a standard hole filling iteration lemma implies that (see for example [2, lemma 3])
\displaystyle\sup_{B^{g}(y,\sigma R)}|f|\leq\frac{C(n)}{(1-\sigma)^{2}}\left(\fint_{B^{g}(y,R)}f^{\frac{n}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}\right)^{\frac{2}{n}}.
∎
Proof of Proposition 3.2.
Define G(x):=\ln|x|. Consider f\>=|\nabla^{g}\ln G|_{g}^{2}.
By the conformal invariance of the n-Dirichlet energy in n-dimensional space, there holds
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(0,e)}f^{\frac{n}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}
\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(0,e)}|\nabla^{g}\ln G|_{g}^{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(0,e)}|\nabla\ln G|^{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
\displaystyle=|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|\int_{e}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{t(\ln t)^{n}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|\int_{1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{t^{n}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
\displaystyle=\frac{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}{n-1}<+\infty.
Therefore, there exists a function \eta(r) satisfying 0\leq\eta(r)\leq\frac{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}{n-1}, \eta(r)\to 0 as r\to+\infty and
(3.1)
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B^{g}(0,r)}f^{\frac{n}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}\leq\eta(r).
It follows from Lemma 3.5 and the assumption that \beta>0 that
(3.2)
\displaystyle\sup_{B^{g}(p,r)}|\nabla^{g}\ln G|_{g}=(\sup_{B^{g}(p,r)}f)^{\frac{1}{2}}
\displaystyle\leq C(\beta,n)r^{-1}(\int_{B^{g}(p,\frac{3}{2}r)}f^{\frac{n}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g})^{\frac{1}{n}},\quad\text{if }B^{g}(p,2r)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(0,e).
For fixed r\gg 1, assume x_{0}\in\partial B^{g}(0,r) satisfying G(x_{0})=s(r), and y_{0}\in\partial B^{g}(0,\frac{r}{2}) satisfying G(y_{0})=i(\frac{r}{2}). Then by Bishop-Gromov volume comparison theorem, there exists some constant N=N(n) such that there are at most N disjoint geodesic balls each with radius \frac{r}{4} contained in B^{g}(0,r)\setminus B^{g}(0,\frac{r}{2}). Therefore, there exists a curve \gamma_{0} and (N+2) geodesic balls B^{g}(p_{i},\frac{r}{4})\subset B^{g}(0,r)\setminus B^{g}(0,\frac{r}{2}), such that \gamma_{0}\subset\cup_{i=1}^{N+2}B^{g}(p_{i},\frac{r}{4}) and \mathrm{Length}(\gamma)\leq\frac{r}{2}(N+2).
Then we get from 3.2 and 3.1 that
\displaystyle\ln s(r)-\ln i(\frac{r}{2})
\displaystyle=\ln G(x_{0})-\ln G(y_{0})
\displaystyle\leq\int_{\gamma_{0}}|\nabla^{g}\ln G|_{g}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}l_{\gamma_{0}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N+2}\frac{r}{2}\sup_{B^{g}(p_{i},\frac{r}{4})}|\nabla^{g}\ln G|_{g}
\displaystyle\leq\frac{r}{2}C(\beta,n)r^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N+2}\left(\int_{B^{g}(p_{i},\frac{3}{8}r)}f^{\frac{n}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}
\displaystyle\leq C(\beta,n)(N+2)\left(\int_{B^{g}(0,\frac{5}{4}r)\setminus B^{g}(0,\frac{1}{4}r)}f^{\frac{n}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}
\displaystyle\leq C(\beta,n)\,\eta^{\frac{1}{n}}\left(r/4\right).
Combined with Lemma 3.4 and the decay of \eta(r), the above estimate yields that
\displaystyle s(r)\leq i(\frac{r}{2})e^{C\eta^{\frac{1}{n}}(r/4)}\leq i(r)e^{o(1)},\quad\text{as }r\to+\infty.
∎
Remark 3.6.
Recall that the Harnack inequality for a nonnegative n-harmonic function u on B(0,R)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n} states as: there exists a constant C(n) such that
\displaystyle\sup_{B(0,R/2)}u\leq C(n)\inf_{B(0,R/2)}u,
see, for example, [25, Theorem 2.20].
In contrast, Proposition 3.2 claims an asymptotically sharp constant e^{o(1)} for the n-harmonic function \ln r_{0}(\cdot) on the conformal flat manifold (\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{2\phi}g_{0}) with nonnegative Ricci curvature.
3.2. Lower bound by m
Proposition 3.7.
Let (\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{2\phi}g_{0}),(n\geq 3) be a complete Riemannian manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature and \beta>0.
Then
\displaystyle\liminf_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln r(x)}{\ln|x|}\geq 1-m.
Proof.
There is nothing to prove if m=1. So we assume 0\leq m<1. C is a constant which may be different from line to line and is independent of r.
Define \underline{\rho}(r):=\inf_{\partial B^{g}(0,r)}|x|=e^{i(r)}, where i(r):=\inf_{\partial B^{g}(0,r)}\ln|x|. It is easy to see that
B(0,\underline{\rho}(r))\subset B^{g}(o,r). Then it follows that
(3.3)
\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}_{g}(B(0,\underline{\rho}(r))\leq\operatorname{vol}_{g}(B^{g}(0,r)).
On the one hand, by [27, Theorem 1.3], there exist some constants C and \rho_{0} such that
(3.4)
\displaystyle\phi(x)\geq-m\ln|x|-C,\quad\forall\ |x|\geq\rho_{0}.
Therefore, for \rho(r)>\rho_{0} we have
\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}_{g}(B(0,\underline{\rho}(r))
\displaystyle=\int_{B(0,\underline{\rho}(r))\setminus B(0,\rho_{0})}e^{n\phi(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{B(0,\rho_{0})}e^{n\phi(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
\displaystyle\geq|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|\int_{\rho_{0}}^{\underline{\rho}(r)}Ct^{-mn}t^{n-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
\displaystyle\geq C\underline{\rho}(r)^{n(1-m)}-C\quad\quad(\ \text{since }0\leq m<1),
On the other hand, by Bishop-Gromov volume comparison, there holds
\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}_{g}(B^{g}(0,r))\leq Cr^{n},\quad\forall r>0.
Insert these into 3.3, and we derive that
\displaystyle C\underline{\rho}(r)^{n(1-m)}-C\leq Cr^{n}.
Therefore,
\displaystyle\frac{n\ln r+C}{i(r)}\geq\frac{\ln(C\underline{\rho}(r)^{n(1-m)}-C)}{i(r)}=\frac{\ln(e^{n(1-m)i(r)}-C)+C}{i(r)}
Taking liminf, we obtain
\displaystyle\liminf_{r\to\infty}\frac{\ln r}{i(r)}\geq 1-m.
Finally we apply Proposition 3.2 to complete the proof as follows:
\displaystyle\liminf_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln r(x)}{\ln|x|}\geq\liminf_{r\to\infty}\frac{\ln r}{s(r)}\geq\liminf_{r\to\infty}\frac{\ln r}{i(r)e^{o(1)}}\geq 1-m.
∎
3.3. Upper bound by m
Proposition 3.8.
Let (\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{2\phi}g_{0}),(n\geq 3) be a complete Riemannian manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature and \beta>0.
Then
\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln r(x)}{\ln|x|}\leq 1-m.
Since there is no pointwise upper bound of the conformal factor \phi like the lower bound 3.4, the argument in Proposition 3.7 could not be applied directly to prove Proposition 3.8.
Instead, inspired by Ma-Qing’s work on the asymptotic upper bound of \phi out of some exceptional set (see [27, Theorem 3.1]), we shall utilize several deep results in nonlinear potential theory to derive an upper bound of the g-volume of Euclidean balls.
We start with the main technical lemma, whose main part is a modification of [27, proposition 4.1].
Lemma 3.9.
Let (\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{2\phi}g_{0}) be a complete Riemannian manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature.
Then for any \epsilon>0, there exist some constants R_{0}>0 and C=C(n)>0 such that
\displaystyle\int_{B(0,2R)\setminus B(0,R)}e^{n\phi(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq C(n)R^{n(1-m)+\epsilon},\quad\forall R\geq R_{0}.
Proof.
Let w(y):=\phi(\frac{y}{|y|^{2}})-2\ln|y|, it follow that w\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}). Then g=e^{2\phi(x)}|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x|^{2}=e^{2w(y)}|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y|^{2}, where x=\frac{y}{|y|^{2}}. By [27, (2.18)], it follows that
(3.5)
\displaystyle-\Delta_{n}w=\mathrm{Ric}_{g}\left(\frac{\nabla^{g}w}{|\nabla^{g}w|_{g}},\frac{\nabla^{g}w}{|\nabla^{g}w|_{g}}\right)|\nabla w|^{n-2}e^{2w}=:f\quad\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}.
Since g=e^{2w}|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y|^{2} is complete at the origin and its scalar curvature is nonnegative, we know from
[5, proposition 8.1] that
\displaystyle\lim_{y\to 0}w(y)=+\infty.
Then, from [1, proposition 1.1] (see also [27, Theorem 2.4]), we know that f\in L^{1}(B(0,2)) and there exists a constant \kappa\geq 0 such that
\displaystyle-\Delta_{n}w=f+\kappa\delta_{0}\quad\text{on }B(0,2)
in the distributional sense.
Therefore we could define a nonnegative Radon measure \mu as \mu:=\mu_{f}+\kappa\delta_{0}, where
\displaystyle\mu_{f}(\Omega):=\int_{\Omega}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,\quad\forall\,\Omega\subset B(0,2).
Then we could interpret 3.5 as
\displaystyle-\Delta_{n}w=\mu\geq 0\quad\text{on }B(0,2).
Hence we could apply [27, Lemma 3.7, Theorem 3.1] and obtain that, there exists a constant m_{1} such that, for any fixed \alpha\in(0,1), there holds
(3.6)
\displaystyle\lim_{y\to 0}\frac{\inf_{B(y,\alpha|y|)}w}{\ln\frac{1}{|y|}}=m_{1}.
It turns out that m_{1}=2-m\in[1,2] (see [27, (5.5),(5.6)]).
Recall the Wolff potential
\displaystyle W_{1,n}^{\mu}(x,r):=\int_{0}^{r}\mu(B(x,t))^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\frac{1}{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.
For any fixed y\in B(0,1), apply the foundational estimates [15, Theorem 1.6] (see also [27, Theorem 2.6]) to w-\inf_{B(y,\frac{3}{4}|y|)}w in B(y,\frac{3}{4}|y|), we obtain: there exists some constants C_{1}(n)>0,\ C_{2}(n)>0 such that
(3.7)
\displaystyle\frac{w(y)}{\ln\frac{1}{|y|}}\leq\frac{\inf_{B(y,\frac{3}{4}|y|)}w}{\ln\frac{1}{|y|}}+C_{1}\frac{\inf_{B(y,\frac{1}{4}|y|)}w}{\ln\frac{1}{|y|}}-C_{1}\frac{\inf_{B(y,\frac{3}{4}|y|)}w}{\ln\frac{1}{|y|}}+C_{2}\frac{W_{1,n}^{\mu}(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)}{\ln\frac{1}{|y|}}.
It follows from 3.6 and 3.7 that, for any \epsilon>0, there exists R_{1}>1 such that
\displaystyle\frac{w(y)}{\ln\frac{1}{|y|}}\leq m_{1}+\frac{\epsilon}{n}+C_{2}\frac{W_{1,n}^{\mu}(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)}{\ln\frac{1}{|y|}},\quad\forall\,|y|\leq\frac{1}{R_{1}}.
Equivalently,
\displaystyle e^{nw(y)}\leq\left(\frac{1}{|y|}\right)^{nm_{1}+\epsilon}e^{nC_{2}W_{1,n}^{\mu}(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)},\quad\forall\,|y|\leq\frac{1}{R_{1}}.
Hence if R\geq R_{1}, we have
\displaystyle\int_{B(0,2R)\setminus B(0,R)}e^{n\phi(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
\displaystyle=\int_{B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R})}e^{nw(y)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y
\displaystyle\leq\int_{B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R})}\left(\frac{1}{|y|}\right)^{nm_{1}+\epsilon}e^{nC_{2}W_{1,n}^{\mu}(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y
\displaystyle\leq(2R)^{nm_{1}+\epsilon}\int_{B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R})}e^{nC_{2}W_{1,n}^{\mu}(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y
(3.8)
\displaystyle\leq C(n)R^{n(2-m)+\epsilon}\int_{B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R})}e^{nC_{2}W_{1,n}^{\mu}(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.
To complete the proof of the lemma, it suffices to show for sufficiently large R, that
(3.9)
\displaystyle\int_{B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R})}e^{nC_{2}W_{1,n}^{\mu}(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\leq C(n)R^{-n}.
In fact, if f\equiv 0 near the origin, then \mu(B(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|))=\mu_{f}(B(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|))=\int_{B(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=0 provided |y| is small enough. It follows that W_{1,n}^{\mu}(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)\equiv 0 and 3.9 holds trivially.
If f is not identically zero in any neighborhood of the origin, then for any R\geq R_{2}:=2, define a nonnegative measure \mu_{R} as
\displaystyle\mu_{R}(\Omega):=\frac{\mu_{f}(\Omega)}{\mu_{f}(B(0,\frac{2}{R}))},\quad\forall\,\Omega\subset B(0,\frac{2}{R}).
It is clear that 0\leq\mu_{R}(\Omega)\leq 1 for \Omega\subset B(0,\frac{2}{R}) and B(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)\subset B(0,\frac{2}{R}) for y\in B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R}). Moreover, it is straightforward to see that
\displaystyle W_{1,n}^{\mu_{R}}(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)=\frac{W_{1,n}^{\mu_{f}}(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)}{\mu_{f}(B(0,\frac{2}{R}))^{\frac{1}{n-1}}}=\frac{W_{1,n}^{\mu}(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)}{\mu_{f}(B(0,\frac{2}{R}))^{\frac{1}{n-1}}}.
For conciseness, let \alpha_{y}^{n-1}:=\mu_{R}(B(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|))\in[0,1], for y\in B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R}). Then
\displaystyle W_{1,n}^{\mu_{R}}(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)
\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}|y|}\mu_{R}(B(y,t))^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\ln t
\displaystyle=\big(\mu_{R}(B(y,t))^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\ln t\big)|_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}|y|}+\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}|y|}\ln\frac{1}{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{R}(B(y,t))^{\frac{1}{n-1}}.
Consider the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function of f:
\displaystyle Mf(y):=\sup_{t>0}\frac{\int_{B(y,t)}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{|B(y,t)|}=\mu_{f}(B(0,\frac{2}{R}))\sup_{t>0}\frac{\mu_{R}(B(y,t))}{|B(y,t)|}.
Hence there holds
\displaystyle\mu_{R}(B(y,t))\leq\frac{|B(0,1)|}{\mu_{f}(B(0,\frac{2}{R}))}t^{n}Mf(y)
for almost every y\in B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R}). Therefore,
\displaystyle\big(\mu_{R}(B(y,t))^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\ln t\big)|_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}|y|}=\alpha_{y}\ln(\frac{1}{2}|y|).
If \alpha_{y}>0, then by Jensen’s inequality,
\displaystyle e^{W_{1,n}^{\mu_{R}}(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)}
\displaystyle=(\frac{1}{2}|y|)^{\alpha_{y}}e^{\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}|y|}\ln\frac{1}{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{R}(B(y,t))^{\frac{1}{n-1}}}
\displaystyle\leq(\frac{1}{2}|y|)^{\alpha_{y}}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}|y|}\frac{1}{t^{\alpha_{y}}}\frac{1}{\alpha_{y}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{R}(B(y,t))^{\frac{1}{n-1}}
\displaystyle\leq(\frac{1}{2}|y|)^{\alpha_{y}}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{y}}\frac{1}{t^{\alpha_{y}}}\mu_{R}(B(y,t))^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\Big|_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}|y|}+\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}|y|}\mu_{R}(B(y,t))^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\frac{1}{t^{\alpha_{y}+1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right)
\displaystyle=(\frac{1}{2}|y|)^{\alpha_{y}}\left(\frac{1}{(\frac{1}{2}|y|)^{\alpha_{y}}}+\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}|y|}\mu_{R}(B(y,t))^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\frac{1}{t^{\alpha_{y}+1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right)
\displaystyle\leq(\frac{1}{2}|y|)^{\alpha_{y}}\left(\frac{1}{(\frac{1}{2}|y|)^{\alpha_{y}}}+\frac{1}{\frac{n}{n-1}-\alpha_{y}}\left(\frac{|B(0,1)|}{\mu_{f}(B(0,\frac{2}{R}))}\right)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\Big(Mf(y)\Big)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\Big(\frac{1}{2}|y|\Big)^{\frac{n}{n-1}-\alpha_{y}}\right)
\displaystyle=1+\frac{1}{\frac{n}{n-1}-\alpha_{y}}\left(\frac{|B(0,1)|}{\mu_{f}(B(0,\frac{2}{R}))}\right)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\Big(Mf(y)\Big)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\Big(\frac{1}{2}|y|\Big)^{\frac{n}{n-1}}.
If \alpha_{y}=0, then W_{1,n}^{\mu_{R}}(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)=0 and there still holds
\displaystyle e^{W_{1,n}^{\mu_{R}}(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)}\leq 1+\frac{1}{\frac{n}{n-1}-\alpha_{y}}\left(\frac{|B(0,1)|}{\mu_{f}(B(0,\frac{2}{R}))}\right)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\Big(Mf(y)\Big)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\Big(\frac{1}{2}|y|\Big)^{\frac{n}{n-1}}.
Hence for \lambda\geq 2, the weak type Hardy-Littlewood maximal inequality yields
\displaystyle\Big|\Big\{y\in B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R}):e^{W_{1,n}^{\mu_{R}}(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)}\geq\lambda\Big\}\Big|
\displaystyle\leq
\displaystyle\bigg|\bigg\{y\in B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R}):Mf(y)\geq\frac{2\mu_{f}(B(0,\frac{2}{R}))(\frac{n}{n-1}-\alpha_{y})^{n-1}}{|B(0,1)|\ |y|^{n}}\lambda^{n-1}\bigg\}\bigg|
\displaystyle\leq
\displaystyle\bigg|\bigg\{y\in B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R}):Mf(y)\geq\frac{2\mu_{f}(B(0,\frac{2}{R}))(\frac{1}{n-1})^{n-1}}{|B(0,1)|}R^{n}\lambda^{n-1}\bigg\}\bigg|
\displaystyle\leq
\displaystyle\frac{C_{3}(n)|B(0,1)|}{2\mu_{f}(B(0,\frac{2}{R}))\,(\frac{1}{n-1})^{n-1}R^{n}\lambda^{n-1}}\int_{B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R})}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\frac{C_{4}(n)}{R^{n}}\left(\frac{\int_{B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R})}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{\mu_{f}(B(0,\frac{2}{R}))}\right)\lambda^{-(n-1)}.
Finally, notice that f\in L^{1}(B(0,2)), there exists R_{3}>0 such that 0<\mu_{f}(B(0,\frac{2}{R_{3}}))<(\frac{n-1}{nC_{2}(n)})^{n-1}, that is to say, q:=nC_{2}(n)\mu_{f}(B(0,\frac{2}{R_{3}}))^{\frac{1}{n-1}}<n-1. Now for R>\max\{R_{2},\ R_{3}\}, we could verify 3.9 as follows:
\displaystyle\int_{B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R})}e^{nC_{2}W_{1,n}^{\mu}(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\int_{B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R})}e^{nC_{2}\mu_{f}(B(0,\frac{2}{R}))^{\frac{1}{n-1}}W_{1,n}^{\mu_{R}}(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y
\displaystyle\leq
\displaystyle\int_{B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R})}e^{qW_{1,n}^{\mu_{R}}(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\Big|\Big\{y\in B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R}):e^{W_{1,n}^{\mu_{R}}(y,\frac{1}{2}|y|)}\geq t^{\frac{1}{q}}\Big\}\Big|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
\displaystyle\leq
\displaystyle\int_{2^{q}}^{+\infty}\frac{C_{4}(n)}{R^{n}}\left(\frac{\int_{B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R})}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{\mu_{f}(B(0,\frac{2}{R}))}\right)t^{\frac{-(n-1)}{q}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{2^{q}}|B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R})|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle C_{4}(n)\left(\frac{\int_{B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R})}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{\mu_{f}(B(0,\frac{2}{R}))}\right)\frac{q}{n-1-q}(\frac{1}{2})^{n-1-q}R^{-n}+2^{q}|B(0,\frac{1}{R})\setminus B(0,\frac{1}{2R})|
\displaystyle\leq
\displaystyle C_{5}(n)R^{-n}.
Combining with 3.8 and 3.9, we conclude that R_{0} could be chosen as \max\{R_{1},R_{2},R_{3}\}+1 and the proof is completed.
∎
Now we are ready to prove Proposition 3.8
Proof of Proposition 3.8:
Fix \epsilon>0, by Lemma 3.9, there exists R_{0}>0 such that, for any R\geq R_{0} and 1\leq i\leq k, we have
\displaystyle\int_{B(0,2^{i}R)\setminus B(0,2^{i-1}R)}e^{n\phi(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq C(n)(2^{i-1}R)^{n(1-m)+\epsilon}=C(n)2^{(i-k-1)(n(1-m)+\epsilon)}(2^{k}R)^{n(1-m)+\epsilon}.
Sum from i=1 to k, we get
(3.10)
\displaystyle\int_{B(0,2^{k}(R))\setminus B(0,R)}e^{n\phi(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{2^{-(n(1-m)+\epsilon)}}{1-2^{-(n(1-m)+\epsilon)}}C(n)(2^{k}R)^{n(1-m)+\epsilon}\leq C(n)(2^{k}R)^{n(1-m)+\epsilon}.
Now for any \tilde{R}\geq 2R_{0}, there exists some integer k\geq 1 such that 2^{-k}\tilde{R}\in[R_{0},2R_{0}). Therefore, 3.10 implies that
\displaystyle\int_{B(0,\tilde{R})}e^{n\phi(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
\displaystyle=\int_{B(0,\tilde{R})\setminus B(0,2^{-k}\tilde{R})}e^{n\phi(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{B(0,2^{-k}\tilde{R})}e^{n\phi(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
(3.11)
\displaystyle\leq C(n)\tilde{R}^{n(1-m)+\epsilon}+\int_{B(0,2R_{0})}e^{n\phi(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
Define \bar{\rho}(r):=\sup_{\partial B^{g}(0,r)}|x|=e^{s(r)}, where s(r):=\sup_{\partial B^{g}(0,r)}\ln|x|. It is easy to see that
B(0,\bar{\rho}(r))\supset B^{g}(o,r).
Then it follows that
(3.12)
\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}_{g}(B(0,\bar{\rho}(r))\geq\operatorname{vol}_{g}(B^{g}(0,r)).
On the one hand, 3.11 yields that for \bar{\rho}(r)\geq 2R_{0},
\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}_{g}(B(0,\bar{\rho}(r))
\displaystyle=\int_{B(0,\bar{\rho}(r))}e^{n\phi(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq C(n)\bar{\rho}(r)^{n(1-m)+\epsilon}+\int_{B(0,2R_{0})}e^{n\phi(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.
On the other hand, by our assumption that \beta>0, there exists some constant C=C(\beta,n)>0 such that
\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}_{g}(B^{g}(0,r))\geq C(\beta,n)r^{n},\quad\forall r>0.
Inserting these into 3.12, then we derive that
\displaystyle C\bar{\rho}(r)^{n(1-m)+\epsilon}+\int_{B(0,2R_{0})}e^{n\phi(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\geq Cr^{n}.
Therefore,
\displaystyle\frac{n\ln r+C}{s(r)}
\displaystyle\leq\frac{\ln(C\bar{\rho}(r)^{n(1-m)+\epsilon}+\int_{B(0,2R_{0})}e^{n\phi(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)}{s(r)}
\displaystyle=\frac{\ln(e^{(n(1-m)+\epsilon)s(r)}+C\int_{B(0,2R_{0})}e^{n\phi(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)+C}{s(r)}
After taking limit superior, we obtain
\displaystyle\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\ln r}{s(r)}\leq 1-m+\frac{\epsilon}{n}.
Since \epsilon is arbitrary, we derive that
\displaystyle\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\ln r}{s(r)}\leq 1-m.
Finally we apply Proposition 3.2 to complete the proof as follows:
\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln r(x)}{\ln|x|}\leq\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\ln r}{i(r)}\leq\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{e^{o(1)}\ln r}{s(r)}\leq 1-m.
∎
3.4. Upper bound by \beta
In this subsection, we also include a result showing that the ratio of two distances is bounded from above by the asymptotic volume ratio. This is similar to that of [20, Theorem 2.5].
Proposition 3.10.
Let (\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{2\phi}g_{0})(n\geq 3) be a complete Riemannian manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature and \beta>0.
Then
\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln r(x)}{\ln|x|}\leq\beta^{\frac{1}{n-1}}.
Define the area function
A(r):=\operatorname{Area}_{g}(\partial B^{g}(0,r)).
We begin with a lower bound of the integral of some power of A(r).
Lemma 3.11.
Let (\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{2\phi}g_{0}) be a complete Riemannian manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature and \beta>0. Then for any \epsilon>0, there exists R_{0}>0 such that
\displaystyle|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}A^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\geq(\beta+\epsilon)^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}(\ln R_{2}-\ln R_{1}),\quad\forall R_{2}>R_{1}\geq R_{0}.
Proof.
It suffices first to work with regular radii R_{1} and R_{2},
for which the corresponding distance spheres are smooth up to a
set of measure zero. The general statement then follows by
approximation and the coarea formula.
By the definition of \beta and the Bishop-Gromov volume comparison theorem, for any \epsilon>0, there exists R_{0}>0 such that
\displaystyle\beta+\epsilon\geq\frac{A(r)}{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|r^{n-1}},\quad\forall r\geq R_{0}.
Equivalently,
\displaystyle|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|^{\frac{1}{n-1}}A(r)^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}\geq(\beta+\epsilon)^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}\frac{1}{r},\quad\forall r\geq R_{0}.
Integrating this inequality from R_{1} to R_{2}, we get the desired inequality.
∎
Now we turn to an upper bound of \int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}A^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.
Lemma 3.12.
Let (\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{2\phi}g_{0}),(n\geq 3) be a complete Riemannian manifold. Denote G(x):=\ln|x| and s(r):=\sup_{\partial B^{g}(0,r)}G,\,i(r):=\inf_{\partial B^{g}(0,r)}G. Then
\displaystyle s(R_{2})-i(R_{1})\geq|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\left(\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}A^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right),\ \forall\,0<R_{1}<R_{2}.
Proof.
Let h solve the equation
\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta_{n}^{g}h=0&\text{in }B^{g}(0,R_{2})\setminus B^{g}(0,R_{1}),\\
h=i(R_{1})&\text{on }\partial B^{g}(0,R_{1}),\\
h=s(R_{2})&\text{on }\partial B^{g}(0,R_{2}).\end{cases}
It follows that
\displaystyle\int_{B^{g}(0,R_{2})\setminus B^{g}(0,R_{1})}
\displaystyle|\nabla^{g}h|_{g}^{n}dV_{g}=-\int_{B^{g}(0,R_{2})\setminus B^{g}(0,R_{1})}h\Delta_{n}^{g}hdV_{g}
\displaystyle+\int_{\partial B^{g}(0,R_{2})}h|\nabla^{g}h|_{g}^{n-2}\frac{\partial h}{\partial r}dA_{g}-\int_{\partial B^{g}(0,R_{1})}h|\nabla^{g}h|_{g}^{n-2}\frac{\partial h}{\partial r}dA_{g}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle s(R_{2})\int_{\partial B^{g}(0,R_{2})}|\nabla^{g}h|_{g}^{n-2}\frac{\partial h}{\partial r}dA_{g}-i(R_{1})\int_{\partial B^{g}(0,R_{1})}|\nabla^{g}h|_{g}^{n-2}\frac{\partial h}{\partial r}dA_{g}
(3.13)
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle(s(R_{2})-i(R_{1}))\int_{\partial B^{g}(0,R_{2})}|\nabla^{g}h|_{g}^{n-2}\frac{\partial h}{\partial r}dA_{g}.
Let f satisfy
\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta_{n}^{g}f=0&\text{in }B^{g}(0,R_{2})\setminus B^{g}(0,R_{1}),\\
f=G&\text{on }\partial B^{g}(0,R_{1}),\\
f=s(R_{2})&\text{on }\partial B^{g}(0,R_{2}).\end{cases}
Applying the comparison principle for n-harmonic functions ( [23, Theorem 2.15]) to f and h, we get
\displaystyle f\geq h\quad\text{on }B^{g}(0,R_{2})\setminus B^{g}(0,R_{1}).
It follows from f=h on \partial B^{g}(0,R_{2}) that
\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\leq\frac{\partial h}{\partial r}\quad\text{on }\partial B^{g}(0,R_{2}).
We claim that
(3.14)
\displaystyle|\nabla^{g}f|_{g}^{n-2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\leq|\nabla^{g}h|_{g}^{n-2}\frac{\partial h}{\partial r}\quad\text{on }\partial B^{g}(0,R_{2}).
In fact, there holds \nabla_{\partial B^{g}(0,R_{2})}^{g}f=\nabla_{\partial B^{g}(0,R_{2})}^{g}h since f=h on \partial B^{g}(0,R_{2}). Then We shall verify 3.14 case by case using a trick in [27, Theorem 5.2].
•
If 0\leq\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}, then
\displaystyle|\nabla^{g}f|_{g}^{2}=|\nabla_{\partial B^{g}(0,R_{2})}^{g}f|_{g}^{2}+(\frac{\partial f}{\partial r})^{2}\leq|\nabla^{g}h|_{g}^{2}\quad\text{on }\partial B^{g}(0,R_{2}).
Hence 3.14 holds.
•
If \frac{\partial f}{\partial r}<0\leq\frac{\partial h}{\partial r}, then
\displaystyle|\nabla^{g}f|_{g}^{n-2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}<0\leq|\nabla^{g}h|_{g}^{n-2}\frac{\partial h}{\partial r}\quad\text{on }\partial B^{g}(0,R_{2}).
•
If \frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\leq\frac{\partial h}{\partial r}<0, then
\displaystyle|\nabla^{g}f|_{g}^{2}=|\nabla_{\partial B^{g}(0,R_{2})}^{g}f|_{g}^{2}+(\frac{\partial f}{\partial r})^{2}\geq|\nabla^{g}h|_{g}^{2}\quad\text{on }\partial B^{g}(0,R_{2}).
Hence 3.14 still holds in this case.
Similarly, we apply the comparison principle for n-harmonic functions to f and G to derive that
\displaystyle f\geq G\quad\quad\text{on }
\displaystyle B^{g}(0,R_{2})\setminus B^{g}(0,R_{1})
(3.15)
\displaystyle|\nabla^{g}f|_{g}^{n-2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\geq|\nabla^{g}G|_{g}^{n-2}\frac{\partial G}{\partial r}\quad\text{on }
\displaystyle\partial B^{g}(0,R_{1}).
Therefore, by 3.14 and 3.15, we could get a lower bound of 3.13:
\displaystyle\int_{B^{g}(0,R_{2})\setminus B^{g}(0,R_{1})}|\nabla^{g}h|_{g}^{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}=
\displaystyle(s(R_{2})-i(R_{1}))\int_{\partial B^{g}(0,R_{2})}|\nabla^{g}h|_{g}^{n-2}\frac{\partial h}{\partial r}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}A_{g}
\displaystyle\geq
\displaystyle(s(R_{2})-i(R_{1}))\int_{\partial B^{g}(0,R_{2})}|\nabla^{g}f|_{g}^{n-2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}A_{g}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle(s(R_{2})-i(R_{1}))\int_{\partial B^{g}(0,R_{1})}|\nabla^{g}f|_{g}^{n-2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}A_{g}
(3.16)
\displaystyle\geq
\displaystyle(s(R_{2})-i(R_{1}))\int_{\partial B^{g}(0,R_{1})}|\nabla^{g}G|_{g}^{n-2}\frac{\partial G}{\partial r}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}A_{g}
On the other hand, consider
\displaystyle H(x):=\frac{(s(R_{2})-i(R_{1}))\int_{R_{1}}^{r(x)}A^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}{\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}A^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}+i(R_{1}),\quad x\in B^{g}(0,R_{2})\setminus B^{g}(0,R_{1}).
It is straightforward to see that H|_{\partial B^{g}(0,R_{1})}=i(R_{1}),\,H|_{\partial B^{g}(0,R_{2})}=s(R_{2}) and
\displaystyle\nabla^{g}H=\frac{s(R_{2})-i(R_{1})}{\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}A^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}A^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}(r)\nabla^{g}r,\quad x\in B^{g}(0,R_{2})\setminus B^{g}(0,R_{1}).
Therefore we could derive an upper bound of 3.13:
\displaystyle\int_{B^{g}(0,R_{2})\setminus B^{g}(0,R_{1})}|\nabla^{g}h|_{g}^{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}\leq
\displaystyle\int_{B^{g}(0,R_{2})\setminus B^{g}(0,R_{1})}|\nabla^{g}H|_{g}^{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{g}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}\left(\int_{\partial B^{g}(0,r)}\frac{(s(R_{2})-i(R_{1}))^{n}}{(\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}A^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t)^{n}}A^{-\frac{n}{n-1}}(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}A_{g}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}\frac{(s(R_{2})-i(R_{1}))^{n}}{(\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}A^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t)^{n}}A^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r
(3.17)
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle(s(R_{2})-i(R_{1}))^{n}\left(\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}A^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right)^{-(n-1)}.
Combine with 3.16 and 3.17, we derive:
\displaystyle\left(\int_{\partial B^{g}(0,R_{1})}|\nabla^{g}G|_{g}^{n-2}\frac{\partial G}{\partial r}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}A_{g}\right)\left(\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}A^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right)^{n-1}\leq\big(s(R_{2})-i(R_{1})\big)^{n-1},\ \forall\,0<R_{1}<R_{2}.
Finally, recall that
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}G=\Delta_{n}\ln|x|=|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|\delta_{0},\quad\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the distribution sense. Hence we could complete the proof by
\displaystyle\int_{\partial B^{g}(0,R_{1})}|\nabla^{g}G|_{g}^{n-2}\frac{\partial G}{\partial r}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}A_{g}=\int_{B^{g}(0,R_{1})}\Delta_{n}^{g}\ln r_{0}\ dV_{g}=\int_{B^{g}(0,R_{1})}\Delta_{n}\ln|x|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|.
∎
Proof of Proposition 3.10:
By Lemma 3.11 and Lemma 3.12, for any \epsilon>0, there exists R_{0}>0 such that \forall R_{2}>R_{1}\geq R_{0}
\displaystyle(\beta+\epsilon)^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}(\ln R_{2}-\ln R_{1})\leq|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\left(\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}A^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right)\leq s(R_{2})-i(R_{1}).
Then Proposition 3.2 implies that
\displaystyle(\beta+\epsilon)^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}(\ln R_{2}-\ln R_{1})+i(R_{1})\leq s(R_{2})\leq i(R_{2})e^{o(1)},\quad\text{as }R_{2}\to+\infty.
It follows that
\displaystyle\limsup_{|x|\to+\infty}\frac{\ln r(x)}{\ln|x|}\leq\limsup_{R_{2}\to+\infty}\frac{\ln R_{2}}{i(R_{2})}\leq(\beta+\epsilon)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}.
The proof is finished by sending \epsilon to zero.
∎
4. Weighted G-convergence
In this section we prove the compactness statement needed in the blow-down argument. The terminology “weighted G-convergence” is used only to emphasize the analogy with the classical G-compactness theorem for uniformly elliptic divergence-form operators.
Let
g=e^{2w}g_{0}
be a complete conformal metric on \mathbb{R}^{n}, n\geq 3, with
\operatorname{Ric}_{g}\geq 0 and \beta>0. We write
\underline{w}(R):=\inf_{|x|=R}w(x).
Let m\in(0,1) be the asymptotic exponent of w and set
a:=1-m\in(0,1).
Let h be a g-harmonic function, then the conformal transformation formula gives
\Delta_{g}h=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\operatorname{div}\bigl(e^{(n-2)w}\nabla h\bigr)=0,
where \operatorname{div},\nabla are the divergence and the gradient in the standard Euclidean space respectively.
In view of the asymptotic exponent of the conformal factor, one may expect that a tangent cone at infinity of (\mathbb{R}^{n},g) is the metric cone (\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{-2m}g_{0}). It is probably difficult to fully establish this geometric statement due to the presence of that strong \varepsilon set. Nevertheless, one can blow down a g-harmonic function to a harmonic function on the limit cone, as we now explain.
For R>0, define
(4.1)
P_{R}(y):=\exp\bigl((n-2)(w(Ry)-\underline{w}(R))\bigr),\qquad Q_{R}(y):=\exp\bigl(n(w(Ry)-\underline{w}(R))\bigr).
The limiting weights are
P_{\infty}(y):=|y|^{-m(n-2)},\qquad Q_{\infty}(y):=|y|^{-mn}.
Set u_{R}(y):=c_{R}^{-1}h(Ry),(c_{R}\neq 0), then it solves
\operatorname{div}(P_{R}\nabla u_{R})=0.
We shall prove that a proper blow down limit of a g harmonic function converges to a solution of the limiting equation
(4.2)
L_{\infty}u:=\operatorname{div}(P_{\infty}\nabla u)=\operatorname{div}\bigl(|y|^{-m(n-2)}\nabla u\bigr).
Up to multiplication of the metric by the constant a^{2}, this is the
Laplace–Beltrami operator of the metric cone
C_{a}=\bigl((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1},d\rho^{2}+a^{2}\rho^{2}g_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\bigr),\qquad\rho=|y|^{a}.
4.1. Convergence of the rescaled weights
We first isolate the precise consequence of the asymptotic analysis of the
conformal factor that will be used below. The proof uses the convergence of
r\underline{w}^{\prime}(r), the spherical exponential-average estimate, and the
annular exceptional-set estimate established in [26].
Proposition 4.1.
For every \Lambda>0, as R\to\infty,
(4.3)
P_{R}\longrightarrow P_{\infty}\quad\text{in }L^{1}(B_{\Lambda}),
(4.4)
Q_{R}\longrightarrow Q_{\infty}\quad\text{in }L^{1}(B_{\Lambda}).
Moreover, there exists c_{\Lambda}>0 and R_{\Lambda}>0 such that
(4.5)
e^{w(Ry)-\underline{w}(R)}\geq c_{\Lambda}\qquad\text{for all }y\in B_{\Lambda},\quad R\geq R_{\Lambda}.
In particular, for R\geq R_{\Lambda},
(4.6)
P_{R}\geq c_{\Lambda}^{n-2}\quad\text{on }B_{\Lambda},
and
(4.7)
\sup_{R\geq R_{\Lambda}}\int_{B_{\Lambda}}P_{R}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y<\infty,\qquad\sup_{R\geq R_{\Lambda}}\int_{B_{\Lambda}}Q_{R}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y<\infty.
Proof.
The radial analysis in [26, Lemma 2.6] gives
(4.8)
r\underline{w}^{\prime}(r)\longrightarrow-m\qquad\text{as }r\to+\infty.
Hence, for every 0<\delta<\Lambda<\infty,
\underline{w}(Rs)-\underline{w}(R)\longrightarrow-m\log s,\qquad\text{as }R\to+\infty,
uniformly for s\in[\delta,\Lambda]. Therefore, for q=n-2 and
q=n,
(4.9)
\exp\bigl(q(\underline{w}(R|y|)-\underline{w}(R))\bigr)\longrightarrow|y|^{-mq},\qquad\text{as }R\to+\infty,
uniformly on every compact annulus.
On a noncompact region, we do not have uniform convergence in general. Nevertheless, a direct integration of 4.8 implies the following estimate: for any \epsilon>0, there exists R_{\epsilon}>0 such that for all R\geq R_{\epsilon} and s\geq\frac{R_{\epsilon}}{R}, there holds
(4.10)
\displaystyle\exp\bigl(q(\underline{w}(Rs)-\underline{w}(R))\bigr)\leq s^{-q(m+\epsilon)},\qquad q\in\{n-2,n\}.
We first prove the convergence of P_{R}. Set
\underline{P}_{R}(y):=\exp\bigl((n-2)(\underline{w}(R|y|)-\underline{w}(R))\bigr).
Since w(x)\geq\underline{w}(|x|), there holds P_{R}\geq\underline{P}_{R}.
For a fixed annulus A_{\delta,\Lambda}, we have
\displaystyle\int_{A_{\delta,\Lambda}}(P_{R}-\underline{P}_{R})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y
(4.11)
\displaystyle\quad=|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|\int_{\delta}^{\Lambda}s^{n-1}\exp\bigl((n-2)(\underline{w}(Rs)-\underline{w}(R))\bigr)\bigl(F(Rs)-1\bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,
where
F(t):=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\exp\bigl((n-2)(w(t\theta)-\underline{w}(t))\bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta.
By the spherical exponential-average estimate in
[26, Lemma 4.7],
(4.12)
F(t)\longrightarrow 1\qquad\text{as }t\to+\infty.
It follows from
(4.11), (4.9), and
(4.12) that
\|P_{R}-\underline{P}_{R}\|_{L^{1}(A_{\delta,\Lambda})}\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{as }R\to+\infty.
Together with (4.9), this proves
(4.13)
P_{R}\longrightarrow P_{\infty}\quad\text{in }L^{1}(A_{\delta,\Lambda}).
To pass from annuli to the whole ball, we prove uniform integrability near 0. Fix T>1. On the central disk B_{T/R}, changing variables x=Ry yields
\displaystyle\int_{B_{T/R}}P_{R}(y)\mathrm{d}y=R^{-n}e^{-(n-2)\underline{w}(R)}\int_{B_{T}}e^{(n-2)w(x)}\mathrm{d}x.
Since \underline{w}(R)=-m\log R+o(\log R) and n-m(n-2)>0, the right-hand side tends to zero for each fixed T. On B_{\delta}\backslash B_{T/R}, decomposing into dyadic annuli and use (4.10) together with (4.11) and 4.12, we obtain
(4.14)
\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\limsup_{R\rightarrow\infty}\int_{B_{\delta}}P_{R}\mathrm{~d}y=0
Since the same estimate is immediate for P_{\infty}, combining (4.13) and (4.14) proves (4.3).
We now turn to Q_{R}. Define
\underline{Q}_{R}(y):=\exp\bigl(n(\underline{w}(R|y|)-\underline{w}(R))\bigr).
Again Q_{R}\geq\underline{Q}_{R}, and \underline{Q}_{R}\longrightarrow Q_{\infty}
locally in L^{1}. In the proof of [26, Page 25], one obtains, uniformly for
\tau\in[1/2,1],
(4.15)
\frac{\displaystyle\int_{A_{\tau R/2,\tau R}}\bigl(e^{nw(x)}-e^{n\underline{w}(|x|)}\bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{\displaystyle\int_{A_{\tau R/2,\tau R}}e^{n\underline{w}(|x|)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}\longrightarrow 0,\qquad\text{as $R\to\infty$}.
Here the numerator is nonnegative because
w(x)\geq\underline{w}(|x|). After the change of variables x=Ry,
(4.15) implies
\|Q_{R}-\underline{Q}_{R}\|_{L^{1}(A_{\delta,\Lambda})}\longrightarrow 0
for every fixed annulus. Thus
Q_{R}\longrightarrow Q_{\infty}\quad\text{in }L^{1}(A_{\delta,\Lambda}).
The same central-disk and dyadic-annulus argument as above, now using
mn<n, 4.10 and (4.15), gives
\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\limsup_{R\to\infty}\int_{B_{\delta}}Q_{R}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=0.
This proves (4.4).
It remains to prove the lower bound 4.5. We shall verify it in three cases separately.
If 0<|y|\leq 1 and R|y| sufficiently large, then \underline{w} is decreasing due to 4.8. Therefore,
w(Ry)-\underline{w}(R)\geq\underline{w}(R|y|)-\underline{w}(R)\geq 0.
If 0<|y|\leq 1 and R|y| remains in a fixed compact interval, then w(Ry) is bounded
from below while \underline{w}(R)\to-\infty as R tends to +\infty, so the same expression is bounded from below.
If 1\leq|y|\leq\Lambda,
(4.8) gives
w(Ry)-\underline{w}(R)\geq\underline{w}(R|y|)-\underline{w}(R)\geq-(m+1)\log\Lambda
for all sufficiently large R. This proves
(4.5).
The remaining assertion 4.7 follows from
(4.3) and (4.4).
∎
4.2. Weighted compactness for harmonic functions
We denote
d\nu_{R}:=Q_{R}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y,\qquad d\nu_{\infty}:=Q_{\infty}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y,
and, for r>0, define the normalized weighted averages
\operatorname{Av}_{R,r}(f):=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{R}}{\displaystyle\nu_{R}(B_{r})},\qquad\operatorname{Av}_{\infty,r}(f):=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{\infty}}{\displaystyle\nu_{\infty}(B_{r})}.
We can now prove the weighted G-compactness theorem used in the next section.
Theorem 4.2.
Let R_{j}\to\infty and \Lambda>1 is fixed. Assume u_{j} are weak solutions of
(4.16)
\operatorname{div}(P_{R_{j}}\nabla u_{j})=0\qquad\text{in }B_{\Lambda},
with
(4.17)
\sup_{j}\operatorname{Av}_{R_{j},\Lambda}(u_{j}^{2})<\infty.
Then, after passing to a subsequence, there exists a function u_{\infty}
with the following properties.
(1)
For every 0<\Lambda^{\prime}<\Lambda,
(4.18)
u_{j}\rightharpoonup u_{\infty}\quad\text{weakly in }H^{1}(B_{\Lambda^{\prime}}),
(4.19)
u_{j}\longrightarrow u_{\infty}\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(B_{\Lambda^{\prime}}).
(2)
The limit satisfies
(4.20)
L_{\infty}u_{\infty}=0\qquad\text{in }B_{\Lambda}\setminus\{0\}
in the weak sense, where L_{\infty} is defined in 4.2. Moreover, u_{\infty} is locally bounded near the
origin and extends across the origin as a weak solution of
(4.21)
L_{\infty}u_{\infty}=0\qquad\text{in }B_{\Lambda}.
(3)
For every 0<r<\Lambda,
(4.22)
\operatorname{Av}_{R_{j},r}(u_{j}^{2})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Av}_{\infty,r}(u_{\infty}^{2}).
More generally, if
u_{1,j},\ldots,u_{q,j} are finitely many sequences satisfying the
same assumptions and converging simultaneously, then
(4.23)
\operatorname{Av}_{R_{j},r}\bigl(u_{p,j}u_{q,j}\bigr)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Av}_{\infty,r}\bigl(u_{p,\infty}u_{q,\infty}\bigr)
for all p,q and every fixed r\in(0,\Lambda).
(4)
If
\operatorname{Av}_{R_{j},1}(u_{j}^{2})=1,
then
(4.24)
\operatorname{Av}_{\infty,1}(u_{\infty}^{2})=1,
and hence u_{\infty}\not\equiv 0.
Proof.
Set
\widehat{g}_{j}:=e^{2(w(R_{j}y)-\underline{w}(R_{j}))}g_{0}.
Then
\widehat{g}_{j}=(R_{j}e^{\underline{w}(R_{j})})^{-2}D_{R_{j}}^{*}g,\qquad D_{R_{j}}(y)=R_{j}y.
Consequently, (\mathbb{R}^{n},\widehat{g}_{j}) is a complete manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature,
and its asymptotic volume ratio is equal to the asymptotic volume ratio
\beta>0 of (\mathbb{R}^{n},g). Moreover,
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{\widehat{g}_{j}}=Q_{R_{j}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{R_{j}},
and (4.16) is equivalent to
\Delta_{\widehat{g}_{j}}u_{j}=0.
Step 1: Local L^{\infty} bounds.
Fix 0<\Lambda^{\prime}<\Lambda_{0}<\Lambda. By
4.5, there exists c_{\Lambda}>0 such
that
\widehat{g}_{j}\geq c_{\Lambda}^{2}g_{0}\qquad\text{on }B_{\Lambda}
for all sufficiently large j. Hence one can choose
\rho_{0}=\rho_{0}(\Lambda-\Lambda_{0},c_{\Lambda})>0 such that
B_{\widehat{g}_{j}}(p,2\rho_{0})\subset B_{\Lambda}\qquad\text{for every }p\in B_{\Lambda_{0}}.
Since u_{j}^{2} is \widehat{g}_{j}-subharmonic, the mean-value inequality on
manifolds with nonnegative Ricci curvature [17, Theorem 2.1] gives
(4.25)
|u_{j}(p)|^{2}\leq\frac{C(n)}{\operatorname{vol}_{\widehat{g}_{j}}(B_{\widehat{g}_{j}}(p,\rho_{0}))}\int_{B_{\widehat{g}_{j}}(p,\rho_{0})}u_{j}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{R_{j}},\quad\forall p\in B_{\Lambda_{0}}.
The numerator in (4.25) is bounded above by
\int_{B_{\Lambda}}u_{j}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{R_{j}}. By
(4.17) and
4.7,
\sup_{j}\int_{B_{\Lambda}}u_{j}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{R_{j}}<\infty.
By Bishop–Gromov and the positivity of the asymptotic volume ratio,
(4.26)
\operatorname{Vol}_{\widehat{g}_{j}}(B_{\widehat{g}_{j}}(p,\rho_{0}))\geq\beta|\mathbb{B}^{n}|\rho_{0}^{n}.
Therefore, combining 4.26 and 4.25, we have
(4.27)
\sup_{j}\|u_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\Lambda_{0}})}<\infty.
Step 2: Caccioppoli inequality and ordinary H^{1} compactness.
Choose \eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{\Lambda_{0}}) with
\eta\equiv 1 on B_{\Lambda^{\prime}}. Testing
(4.16) with \eta^{2}u_{j} gives
\int_{B_{\Lambda_{0}}}P_{R_{j}}\eta^{2}|\nabla u_{j}|^{2}\leq 4\int_{B_{\Lambda_{0}}}P_{R_{j}}u_{j}^{2}|\nabla\eta|^{2}.
The right-hand side is uniformly bounded by
(4.27) and
(4.7). Hence
(4.28)
\int_{B_{\Lambda^{\prime}}}P_{R_{j}}|\nabla u_{j}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\leq C.
Using (4.6), we obtain
(4.29)
\|u_{j}\|_{H^{1}(B_{\Lambda^{\prime}})}\leq C.
The Banach–Alaoglu and Rellich compactness theorems now give
(4.18) and
(4.19).
Step 3: Limit solution.
Fix a compact set
K\subset B_{\Lambda}\setminus\{0\} and
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(K). We claim that
(4.30)
\int_{K}(P_{R_{j}}-P_{\infty})\nabla u_{j}\cdot\nabla\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\longrightarrow 0.
In fact, for \varepsilon>0, set
E_{j}^{\varepsilon}:=\{y\in K:|P_{R_{j}}-P_{\infty}|>\varepsilon\},\qquad G_{j}^{\varepsilon}:=K\setminus E_{j}^{\varepsilon}.
By (4.3), we have
(4.31)
\epsilon|E_{j}^{\varepsilon}|\leq\int_{B_{\Lambda}}|P_{R_{j}}-P_{\infty}|\to 0,\qquad\text{as }j\to+\infty.
Further notice that
\int_{E_{j}^{\varepsilon}}P_{R_{j}}\leq\int_{E_{j}^{\varepsilon}}|P_{R_{j}}-P_{\infty}|+\int_{E_{j}^{\varepsilon}}P_{\infty},
and P_{\infty} is bounded on K, there holds
(4.32)
\displaystyle\int_{E_{j}^{\varepsilon}}P_{R_{j}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\to 0\quad\text{as }j\to+\infty.
On the good set G_{j}^{\epsilon}, by 4.29,
(4.33)
\left|\int_{G_{j}^{\varepsilon}}(P_{R_{j}}-P_{\infty})\nabla u_{j}\cdot\nabla\varphi\right|\leq C\varepsilon.
On the bad set, the P_{R_{j}}-part of 4.30 is estimated by Cauchy–Schwarz, 4.28 and 4.32:
(4.34)
\displaystyle\left|\int_{E_{j}^{\varepsilon}}P_{R_{j}}\nabla u_{j}\cdot\nabla\varphi\right|\leq\left(\int_{E_{j}^{\epsilon}}P_{R_{j}}|\nabla u_{j}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{E_{j}^{\varepsilon}}P_{R_{j}}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\longrightarrow 0.
Since P_{\infty} is bounded on K, the remaining part satisfies, by 4.31 and 4.29,
(4.35)
\displaystyle\left|\int_{E_{j}^{\varepsilon}}P_{\infty}\nabla u_{j}\cdot\nabla\varphi\right|\leq C\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}|E_{j}^{\varepsilon}|^{1/2}\|\nabla u_{j}\|_{L^{2}(K)}\longrightarrow 0.
Combining (4.33),
(4.34), and
(4.35), and then letting
\varepsilon go to zero, proves
(4.30).
Since, for any \varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(K),
\int_{K}P_{R_{j}}\nabla u_{j}\cdot\nabla\varphi=0,
we have
\int_{K}P_{\infty}\nabla u_{j}\cdot\nabla\varphi\to 0,\quad\text{as }j\to+\infty.
Using the weak H^{1} convergence of \{u_{j}\} and the fact that P_{\infty} is bounded
on K, we conclude
(4.36)
\displaystyle\int_{K}P_{\infty}\nabla u_{\infty}\cdot\nabla\varphi=0.
This proves (4.20).
Step 4: Removability of the cone vertex.
The local L^{\infty} bound 4.27 passes to the limit, so u_{\infty} is bounded
near 0. Let \chi_{\varepsilon} be a radial cutoff satisfying
\chi_{\varepsilon}=0\text{ on }B_{\varepsilon},\qquad\chi_{\varepsilon}=1\text{ on }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2\varepsilon},\qquad|\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}|\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}.
Then
(4.37)
\displaystyle\int_{B_{\Lambda}}P_{\infty}|\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\leq C\varepsilon^{-2}\int_{\varepsilon}^{2\varepsilon}r^{n-1-m(n-2)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\leq C\varepsilon^{(1-m)(n-2)}\to 0.
Choose an outer cutoff \eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{\Lambda}).
Testing the equation 4.36 with
\eta^{2}\chi_{\varepsilon}^{2}u_{\infty} and using the boundedness of
u_{\infty} gives
\int_{B_{\Lambda}}P_{\infty}\eta^{2}\chi_{\varepsilon}^{2}|\nabla u_{\infty}|^{2}\leq C\|u_{\infty}\|_{L^{\infty}(\operatorname{supp}\eta)}^{2}\left(\int_{B_{\Lambda}}P_{\infty}|\nabla\eta|^{2}+\int_{B_{\Lambda}}P_{\infty}|\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\right).
Letting \varepsilon\downarrow 0, we see from 4.37 that
P_{\infty}^{1/2}\nabla u_{\infty} is locally square integrable across the
origin. Furthermore, for an arbitrary
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{\Lambda}), use
\varphi\chi_{\varepsilon} as a test function in equation 4.36.
Then, by 4.37, we have
\displaystyle\left|\int_{B_{\Lambda}}P_{\infty}\chi_{\epsilon}\nabla u_{\infty}\cdot\nabla\varphi\right|=
\displaystyle\left|\int_{B_{\Lambda}}P_{\infty}\varphi\nabla u_{\infty}\cdot\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\right|
\displaystyle\leq
\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(\text{supp}\nabla\chi_{\epsilon})}\left(\int_{B_{2\varepsilon}}P_{\infty}|\nabla u_{\infty}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{B_{2\epsilon}}P_{\infty}|\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\to 0.
Thus u_{\infty} satisfies (4.21).
Step 5: Convergence of weighted masses.
Fix 0<r<\Lambda. By (4.27),
|u_{j}|+|u_{\infty}|\leq M_{r}\qquad\text{on }B_{r}.
After passing to the almost-everywhere convergent subsequence,
|u_{j}^{2}-u_{\infty}^{2}|\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{almost everywhere in }B_{r}.
Using the dominated convergence theorem and (4.4),
(4.38)
\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}}|u_{j}^{2}-u_{\infty}^{2}|Q_{R_{j}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\leq\int_{B_{r}}|u_{j}^{2}-u_{\infty}^{2}|Q_{\infty}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y+2M_{r}^{2}\|Q_{R_{j}}-Q_{\infty}\|_{L^{1}(B_{r})}\longrightarrow 0.
Combining with the local boundedness of u_{\infty} in B_{\Lambda}, it follows that
\int_{B_{r}}u_{j}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{R_{j}}\longrightarrow\int_{B_{r}}u_{\infty}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{\infty}.
Also, by 4.4
\nu_{R_{j}}(B_{r})\longrightarrow\nu_{\infty}(B_{r})>0.
This proves (4.22).
For finitely many sequences, the products
u_{p,j}u_{q,j} are locally uniformly bounded and converge almost
everywhere to u_{p,\infty}u_{q,\infty}. Repeating
(4.38) proves
(4.23).
Finally, (4.24) follows immediately from
(4.22).
∎
5. The positive asymptotic volume ratio case
In this section we combine the distance comparison established in
Section 3 with the weighted compactness theorem from
Section 4 to prove the sharp dimension estimate in the
positive asymptotic volume ratio case. The structure of the proof follows
Lin’s argument for asymptotically conic elliptic operators
[23].
Let g=e^{2w}g_{0} be a complete conformal metric on \mathbb{R}^{n}, n\geq 3, satisfying \operatorname{Ric}_{g}\geq 0, and suppose that its asymptotic volume ratio \beta is positive. Let m\in[0,1) be the asymptotic exponent of the conformal factor, and set a:=1-m.
By the exact volume-ratio formula recalled in the introduction 1.3,
\beta=a^{n-1}.
If m=0, then \beta=1; the equality case in Bishop–Gromov volume
comparison implies that (\mathbb{R}^{n},g) is isometric to Euclidean space.
Thus the only case in which a strict dimension drop can occur is
(5.1)
0<m<1,\qquad 0<a<1.
Below in this section we assume
(5.1).
For d\geq 0, recall
\mathcal{H}_{d}(g):=\left\{h:\Delta_{g}h=0,\quad|h(x)|\leq C_{h}\bigl(1+d_{g}(0,x)^{d}\bigr)\right\}.
For a nonzero g-harmonic function h, define its normalized weighted
L^{2} mass on the Euclidean ball B_{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n} by
(5.2)
S_{h}(R):=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}h^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{g}}{\displaystyle\mu_{g}(B_{R})},
where d\mu_{g}:=e^{nw(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x is the volume measure of (\mathbb{R}^{n},g).
5.1. The input from the distance comparison
Using distance comparison from Section 3, we turn intrinsic growth rate to a growth rate with respect to the Euclidean distance.
Lemma 5.1.
Let h\in\mathcal{H}_{d}(g). For every \varepsilon>0 there exist constants
C_{h,\varepsilon} and R_{h,\varepsilon} such that
(5.3)
\sup_{|x|\leq R}|h(x)|\leq C_{h,\varepsilon}R^{d(a+\varepsilon)}
for all R\geq R_{h,\varepsilon}. Consequently,
(5.4)
S_{h}(R)\leq C_{h,\varepsilon}R^{2d(a+\varepsilon)}
for all sufficiently large R.
Proof.
Theorem1.3 states that
\frac{\ln r(x)}{\ln|x|}\longrightarrow a\qquad\text{as }|x|\to\infty.
Hence, for every \varepsilon>0, there exists R_{\varepsilon} such that
r(x)\leq|x|^{a+\varepsilon}\qquad\text{whenever }|x|\geq R_{\varepsilon}.
After increasing the constant to absorb the compact region
B_{R_{\varepsilon}}, we obtain
(5.5)
\sup_{|x|\leq R}r(x)\leq C_{\varepsilon}R^{a+\varepsilon}
for all sufficiently large R. The defining growth bound for
h\in\mathcal{H}_{d}(g) now gives (5.3).
Finally, (5.4) follows immediately from 5.3.
∎
The rescaled measures and averages are those introduced in
Section 4. Let
u_{R}(y):=\frac{h(Ry)}{S_{h}(R)^{1/2}},
then the definitions of Q_{R} 4.1, d\nu_{R}, and S_{h} 5.2
give the exact scaling
identity
(5.6)
\operatorname{Av}_{R,r}(u_{R}^{2})=\frac{S_{h}(rR)}{S_{h}(R)}\qquad(r>0).
In fact,
\displaystyle\operatorname{Av}_{R,r}(u_{R}^{2})=\frac{\int_{B_{r}}h^{2}(Ry)Q_{R}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y}{S_{h}(R)\int_{B_{r}}Q_{R}(y)dy}=\frac{\int_{B_{Rr}}h^{2}(x)e^{nw(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{S_{h}(R)\int_{B_{Rr}}e^{nw(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}=\frac{S_{h}(rR)}{S_{h}(R)}.
This identity is the bridge between the growth function S_{h} and the
weighted compactness theorem.
5.2. The homogeneous spectrum of the limiting cone
Let \mathcal{Y}_{\ell} be the space of spherical harmonics of degree \ell
on \mathbb{S}^{n-1}, and let
d_{\ell}:=\dim\mathcal{Y}_{\ell},\qquad\lambda_{\ell}:=\ell(\ell+n-2).
We use the normalized spherical measure
d\omega:=|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|^{-1}d\theta
and choose an orthonormal basis
\{Y_{\ell,q}\}_{q=1}^{d_{\ell}} of \mathcal{Y}_{\ell} in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1},d\omega). We have the following homogeneous expansion of harmonic functions on the limiting cone.
Proposition 5.2.
For each \ell\geq 0, let \sigma_{\ell}\geq 0 be the positive solution of
(5.7)
\sigma_{\ell}\bigl(\sigma_{\ell}+a(n-2)\bigr)=\ell(\ell+n-2).
Equivalently,
\sigma_{\ell}=\frac{-a(n-2)+\sqrt{a^{2}(n-2)^{2}+4\ell(\ell+n-2)}}{2}.
Every L_{\infty}-harmonic function v that is locally bounded at the cone
vertex has an expansion
(5.8)
v(r,\theta)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{q=1}^{d_{\ell}}c_{\ell,q}r^{\sigma_{\ell}}Y_{\ell,q}(\theta),\qquad r=|y|,
with smooth convergence on compact annuli. Moreover,
(5.9)
S_{v}^{\infty}(r):=\operatorname{Av}_{\infty,r}(v^{2})=\sum_{\ell,q}\frac{an}{2\sigma_{\ell}+an}|c_{\ell,q}|^{2}r^{2\sigma_{\ell}}.
Consequently,
t\longmapsto\log S_{v}^{\infty}(e^{t})
is convex whenever v\not\equiv 0.
Proof.
In Euclidean polar coordinates the limiting equation
L_{\infty}v=0 is
(5.10)
v_{rr}+\frac{1+a(n-2)}{r}v_{r}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}v=0.
Expanding v(r,\cdot) in spherical harmonics and projecting
(5.10) onto \mathcal{Y}_{\ell} gives the
ODE
b^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1+a(n-2)}{r}b^{\prime}-\frac{\lambda_{\ell}}{r^{2}}b=0.
By setting b(r)=r^{t}, the above equation becomes a quadratic equation of t and the two roots are \sigma_{\ell} and
-a(n-2)-\sigma_{\ell}. Local boundedness at the vertex excludes the
negative root.
Standard spherical harmonic estimates give smooth
convergence on compact annuli.
This proves 5.8.
Since
d\nu_{\infty}=r^{-mn}r^{n-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta=r^{an-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta,
orthogonality with respect to the normalized measure d\omega yields
(5.9). After setting r=e^{t}, the right-hand
side of 5.9 is a positive sum of exponentials e^{2\sigma_{\ell}t}. The logarithm
of such a sum is convex by a direct calculation of the second derivative and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
∎
Lemma 5.3.
For every integer d\geq 1, there holds
\sigma_{d}>ad.
Consequently, one may choose
(5.11)
\max\{ad,\sigma_{d-1}\}<N<\sigma_{d},\qquad N\notin\{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\ldots\}.
For every such N,
(5.12)
\{\ell:\sigma_{\ell}<N\}=\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}.
Proof.
At \sigma=ad, the left-hand side of
(5.7) equals
ad\bigl(ad+a(n-2)\bigr)=a^{2}d(d+n-2)<d(d+n-2),
since 0<a<1. Hence the positive root satisfies
\sigma_{d}>ad. Since \sigma_{d-1}<\sigma_{d}, the interval in
(5.11) is nonempty, and (5.12) follows.
∎
5.3. Lin’s uniform one-step propagation
We now use Theorem 4.2 to transfer the exact
log-convexity of the limiting cone to a uniform large-scale statement on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},g).
Proposition 5.4.
Let N>0 satisfy
N\notin\{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\ldots\}.
There exists R_{0}=R_{0}(N,g)>0 such that, for every g-harmonic function
h and every R\geq R_{0} satisfying S_{h}(2R)\leq 2^{2N}S_{h}(R), there holds
(5.13)
S_{h}(R)\leq 2^{2N}S_{h}(R/2).
where S_{h} is defined in 5.2.
Proof.
Suppose for contradiction that the assertion is false. Then there exist R_{j}\to\infty and
nonzero g-harmonic functions h_{j} such that
S_{h_{j}}(2R_{j})\leq 2^{2N}S_{h_{j}}(R_{j}),
but
S_{h_{j}}(R_{j}/2)<2^{-2N}S_{h_{j}}(R_{j}).
Define
u_{j}(y):=\frac{h_{j}(R_{j}y)}{S_{h_{j}}(R_{j})^{1/2}}.
The scaling identity (5.6) gives
\operatorname{Av}_{R_{j},1}(u_{j}^{2})=1,\qquad\operatorname{Av}_{R_{j},2}(u_{j}^{2})\leq 2^{2N},\qquad\operatorname{Av}_{R_{j},1/2}(u_{j}^{2})<2^{-2N}.
Apply Theorem 4.2 on B_{2}. After passing to
a subsequence, u_{j} converges locally to a nonzero L_{\infty}-harmonic
function u_{\infty} in B_{2}. The convergence of weighted masses gives
(5.14)
S_{u_{\infty}}^{\infty}(1)=\mathrm{Av}_{\infty,1}(u_{\infty}^{2})=1,\qquad S_{u_{\infty}}^{\infty}(1/2)=\mathrm{Av}_{\infty,\frac{1}{2}}(u_{\infty}^{2})\leq 2^{-2N}.
For every r<2, monotonicity of the unnormalized integral gives
\displaystyle\operatorname{Av}_{R_{j},r}(u_{j}^{2})
\displaystyle\leq\frac{\nu_{R_{j}}(B_{2})}{\nu_{R_{j}}(B_{r})}\operatorname{Av}_{R_{j},2}(u_{j}^{2}).
Using Proposition 4.1, Theorem 4.2 and then letting
j\to\infty yields
S_{u_{\infty}}^{\infty}(r)\leq\frac{\nu_{\infty}(B_{2})}{\nu_{\infty}(B_{r})}2^{2N}.
Letting r go to 2, we obtain
(5.15)
S_{u_{\infty}}^{\infty}(2)\leq 2^{2N}.
Set
F(t):=\log S_{u_{\infty}}^{\infty}(e^{t}),\qquad\tau:=\log 2.
From
(5.14) and (5.15),
F(0)=0,\qquad F(\tau)\leq 2N\tau,\qquad F(-\tau)\leq-2N\tau.
By Proposition 5.2, convexity of F gives
0=F(0)\leq\frac{F(-\tau)+F(\tau)}{2}\leq 0.
Thus equality holds throughout and F is affine on
[-\tau,\tau] with slope 2N. By
(5.9), the logarithm is affine only when all
nonzero homogeneous components have the same exponent. Then the slope of F forces 2N=2\sigma_{l} for some l\geq 0, contradicting
N\notin\{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\ldots\}.
This finishes the proof.
∎
5.4. Uniform growth control on \mathcal{H}_{d}(g)
From now on, fix an integer d\geq 1 and choose N as in
5.11.
Lemma 5.5.
For every h\in\mathcal{H}_{d}(g) there exist N^{\prime}<N and C_{h}<\infty
such that
S_{h}(R)\leq C_{h}R^{2N_{h}^{\prime}}
for all sufficiently large R.
Proof.
Since N>ad, choose \varepsilon>0 so small that
N^{\prime}:=d(a+\varepsilon)<N.
The conclusion follows from
Lemma 5.1.
∎
Proposition 5.6.
Let R_{0}=R_{0}(N,g) be the radius in
Proposition 5.4. Then, for every
h\in\mathcal{H}_{d}(g) and every integer q\geq 0,
(5.16)
S_{h}(2^{q+1}R_{0})\leq 2^{2N}S_{h}(2^{q}R_{0}).
In particular, the same base radius R_{0} works simultaneously for all
functions in \mathcal{H}_{d}(g).
Proof.
Fix a nonzero h\in\mathcal{H}_{d}(g). We claim that there are arbitrarily large integers Q
for which
(5.17)
S_{h}(2^{Q+1}R_{0})\leq 2^{2N}S_{h}(2^{Q}R_{0}).
In fact, if the reverse strict inequality held for all Q\geq Q_{0}, an iteration would yield
\displaystyle S_{h}(2^{Q}R_{0})\geq 2^{2N(Q-Q_{0})}S_{h}(2^{Q_{0}}R_{0}),
contradicting Lemma 5.5.
Starting from any Q satisfying
(5.17), apply
Proposition 5.4 and we get that 5.16 holds for any 0\leq q\leq Q. Since we proved that 5.17 holds for arbitrarily large integers Q, the proof is complete.
∎
Corollary 5.7.
For every fixed T\geq 1, there exists C_{T}<\infty, depending only on
T,N, and g, such that,
for every nonzero h\in\mathcal{H}_{d}(g) and every dyadic radius
R=2^{q}R_{0},
(5.18)
\frac{S_{h}(TR)}{S_{h}(R)}\leq C_{T}
Moreover, for every integer s\geq 0,
(5.19)
\frac{S_{h}(2^{s}R)}{S_{h}(R)}\leq 2^{2Ns}.
Proof.
The second assertion follows by iterating
(5.16). For the first assertion, let
k\geq 0 be the integer satisfying 2^{k-1}<T\leq 2^{k}. Since the unnormalized integral of
h^{2} is monotone under inclusion of balls,
\displaystyle S_{h}(TR)
\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu_{g}(B_{2^{k}R})}{\mu_{g}(B_{TR})}S_{h}(2^{k}R).
By the change of variables used in 5.6,
\frac{\mu_{g}(B_{2^{k}R})}{\mu_{g}(B_{TR})}=\frac{\nu_{R}(B_{2^{k}})}{\nu_{R}(B_{T})}.
4.5 and 4.7 show that this ratio is bounded for all R\geq R_{2^{k}} by a constant depending
only on T and g. Enlarging the constant to cover the finitely many
remaining dyadic radii and using
(5.19) proves
(5.18).

5.5. Simultaneous blow-down and the dimension estimate
We can now complete the proof of Theorem 1.2. For reader’s convenience, we restate it as follows.
Theorem 5.8.
Let g=e^{2w}g_{0} be a complete non-flat conformal metric on
\mathbb{R}^{n}, n\geq 3, satisfying \operatorname{Ric}_{g}\geq 0 and
\beta\in(0,1). Then, for every integer d\geq 1,
(5.20)
\dim\mathcal{H}_{d}(g)\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{d-1}d_{\ell}=\dim\mathcal{H}_{d-1}(g_{0})<\dim\mathcal{H}_{d}(g_{0}),
where d_{\ell} is the dimension of the space of spherical harmonics of degree \ell
on \mathbb{S}^{n-1}.
Proof.
Let V\subset\mathcal{H}_{d}(g) be an arbitrary finite-dimensional subspace,
and set
k:=\dim V.
Choose N as in 5.11, let R_{0} be the
radius from Proposition 5.4, and set
R_{j}:=2^{j}R_{0}.
For each j, define an inner product on V by
\langle h_{1},h_{2}\rangle_{R_{j}}:=\frac{1}{\mu_{g}(B_{R_{j}})}\int_{B_{R_{j}}}h_{1}h_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{g}.
It is positive definite: if \langle h,h\rangle_{R_{j}}=0, then the smooth
function h vanishes on the open ball B_{R_{j}}, and unique continuation
implies h\equiv 0.
Choose an \langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{R_{j}}-orthonormal basis
h_{1,j},\ldots,h_{k,j}
of V, and define
u_{p,j}(y):=h_{p,j}(R_{j}y),\qquad 1\leq p\leq k.
The normalization gives
(5.21)
\operatorname{Av}_{R_{j},1}(u_{p,j}u_{q,j})=\langle h_{p,j},h_{q,j}\rangle_{R_{j}}=\delta_{pq}.
In particular,
S_{h_{p,j}}(R_{j})=\langle h_{p,j},h_{p,j}\rangle_{R_{j}}=1.
For each fixed T\geq 1, Corollary 5.7 gives
(5.22)
\operatorname{Av}_{R_{j},T}(u_{p,j}^{2})=\frac{S_{h_{p,j}}(TR_{j})}{S_{h_{p,j}}(R_{j})}\leq C_{T},
where C_{T} is independent of p and j. By 5.22, we could apply
Theorem 4.2 simultaneously to the finite
family \{u_{p,j}\}_{p=1}^{k} on B_{T}, first for each integer
T>1 and then by a diagonal argument. After passing to a common
subsequence, we obtain globally defined cone-harmonic functions
u_{1,\infty},\ldots,u_{k,\infty}
on \left((0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1},d\rho^{2}+a^{2}\rho^{2}g_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\right). Then 4.23 and 5.21 give
\operatorname{Av}_{\infty,1}(u_{p,\infty}u_{q,\infty})=\delta_{pq}.
Hence the limiting functions are linearly independent.
The sharper dyadic estimate
(5.19) gives, for every integer s\geq 0,
\operatorname{Av}_{R_{j},2^{s}}(u_{p,j}^{2})\leq 2^{2Ns}.
Passing to the limit in a ball of radius larger than 2^{s} yields
(5.23)
\operatorname{Av}_{\infty,2^{s}}(u_{p,\infty}^{2})\leq 2^{2Ns}.
Expand u_{p,\infty} as in
Proposition 5.2.
By 5.9, 5.23,
and the choice of N (see 5.11), every u_{p,\infty} belongs to
\bigoplus_{\sigma_{\ell}<N}\left\{r^{\sigma_{\ell}}Y:Y\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}\right\}.
By Lemma 5.3, the dimension of this space is
\sum_{\ell=0}^{d-1}d_{\ell}.
Since the k functions u_{1,\infty},\ldots,u_{k,\infty} are linearly independent and the subspace V\subset\mathcal{H}_{d}(g) is arbitrary, we get:
\dim\mathcal{H}_{d}(g)\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{d-1}d_{\ell}=\dim\mathcal{H}_{d-1}(g_{0}),
and the strict inequality in (5.20) follows from
d_{d}>0.
∎
6. Completion of the proof of the Main theorem
We now pass from the globally conformal case treated in the preceding
sections to an arbitrary complete locally conformally flat manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature. The remaining cases are elementary. We recall that the classification theorems of Zhu [30] and of
Carron–Herzlich [4] imply that a connected complete locally conformally flat
manifold (M^{n},g), n\geq 3, with \operatorname{Ric}_{g}\geq 0 belongs to
one of the following classes:
(1)
M is non-flat and globally conformally equivalent to \mathbb{R}^{n};
(2)
M is globally conformally equivalent to a spherical space form;
(3)
M is locally isometric to the round cylinder
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1};
(4)
M is a complete flat manifold.
We treat the four alternatives
separately and thereby complete the proof of the main theorem. For reader’s convenience, we recall it here.
Theorem 6.1.
Let (M^{n},g), n\geq 3, be a connected complete locally conformally flat
manifold satisfying \operatorname{Ric}_{g}\geq 0. Then, for every integer
d\geq 0,
(6.1)
h_{d}(M)\leq h_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).
Moreover, if equality holds for some integer d\geq 1, then (M,g) is
isometric to the Euclidean space.
6.1. The globally conformal non-flat case
Suppose first that M belongs to class (1). Thus
(M,g)\cong(\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{2w}g_{0})
with g non-flat. Let \beta denote its asymptotic volume ratio.
If \beta=0, Theorem 1.1 gives
\mathcal{H}_{d}(M)=\mathbb{R}
for every finite d. Hence
h_{d}(M)=1\leq h_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),
and the inequality is strict whenever d\geq 1.
If \beta>0, Theorem 1.2 gives, for every
integer d\geq 1,
h_{d}(M)\leq h_{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})<h_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).
For d=0, both spaces consist of constants. Thus the theorem holds in
this class, and equality for a positive degree is impossible.
6.2. The spherical case
Suppose that M belongs to class (2). A spherical space form is compact,
and global conformal equivalence preserves compactness. Hence M is
compact. Every harmonic function on a connected compact manifold is
constant, so
\mathcal{H}_{d}(M)=\mathbb{R}\qquad\text{for every }d\geq 0.
Consequently,
h_{d}(M)=1\leq h_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),
with strict inequality for every d\geq 1.
6.3. The cylindrical case
We next consider class (3). The following elementary lemma gives a little
more information than is required for the dimension comparison.
Lemma 6.2.
Let
C:=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
be equipped with the product metric dt^{2}+g_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}. Every
harmonic function on C having polynomial growth is of the form
(6.2)
u(t,\theta)=A+Bt.
In particular,
\dim\mathcal{H}_{d}(C)=\begin{cases}1,&0\leq d<1,\\
2,&d\geq 1.\end{cases}
Proof.
Let \{Y_{\ell,\alpha}\} be an orthonormal basis of spherical harmonics,
normalized so that
-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}Y_{\ell,\alpha}=\lambda_{\ell}Y_{\ell,\alpha},\qquad\lambda_{\ell}=\ell(\ell+n-2).
For a harmonic function u on C, set
a_{\ell,\alpha}(t):=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}u(t,\theta)Y_{\ell,\alpha}(\theta)\,d\theta.
Since
\Delta_{C}=\partial_{t}^{2}+\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}},
each coefficient satisfies
(6.3)
a_{\ell,\alpha}^{\prime\prime}-\lambda_{\ell}a_{\ell,\alpha}=0.
The polynomial-growth assumption on u implies the same polynomial bound
for every coefficient a_{\ell,\alpha} as t\to\pm\infty.
For \ell=0, equation (6.3) gives
a_{0}^{\prime\prime}=0,
so a_{0}(t)=A+Bt. If \ell\geq 1, then
a_{\ell,\alpha}(t)=c_{\ell,\alpha}^{+}e^{\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}t}+c_{\ell,\alpha}^{-}e^{-\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}t}.
Polynomial growth as t\to+\infty forces
c_{\ell,\alpha}^{+}=0, while polynomial growth as t\to-\infty forces
c_{\ell,\alpha}^{-}=0. Thus every positive spherical mode vanishes, and
(6.2) follows. The final dimension assertion is
immediate.
∎
Now let M be complete and locally isometric to the round cylinder. Since
n\geq 3, the cylinder \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1} is simply
connected and is therefore the Riemannian universal cover of M. Let
\pi:C\longrightarrow M
be the covering map. If u\in\mathcal{H}_{d}(M), then
\widetilde{u}:=u\circ\pi is harmonic on C. Moreover, for fixed base
points \widetilde{p}\in C and p=\pi(\widetilde{p}),
d_{M}\bigl(p,\pi(\widetilde{x})\bigr)\leq d_{C}(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{x}),
so \widetilde{u} has polynomial growth of degree at most d on C.
The lifting map
\mathcal{H}_{d}(M)\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{d}(C),\qquad u\longmapsto u\circ\pi,
is injective. Lemma 6.2 therefore yields
h_{d}(M)\leq\begin{cases}1,&0\leq d<1,\\
2,&d\geq 1.\end{cases}
For an integer d\geq 1,
h_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\geq h_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=n+1>2,
because n\geq 3. Thus the Euclidean comparison is strict in the
cylindrical case for every positive integer degree.
6.4. The flat case
It remains to treat class (4). Let M be a complete flat manifold, then its universal cover is the standard Euclidean space, and M=\mathbb{R}^{n}/\Gamma.
Let
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to M
be the covering map. If u\in\mathcal{H}_{d}(M), then
\widetilde{u}:=u\circ\pi is harmonic on \mathbb{R}^{n}. Moreover, for fixed base
points \widetilde{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n} and p=\pi(\widetilde{p}),
d_{M}\bigl(p,\pi(\widetilde{x})\bigr)\leq d_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{x}),
so \widetilde{u} has polynomial growth of degree at most d on \mathbb{R}^{n}.
The lifting map
\mathcal{H}_{d}(M)\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\qquad u\longmapsto u\circ\pi,
is injective.
Suppose h_{d}(M)=h_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n}) for some integer d\geq 1, by injectivity, it is necessary to have h_{1}(M)=h_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}). Note each harmonic lift must be \Gamma-equivariant, i.e.,
\tilde{u}(x)=\tilde{u}(\gamma x),\quad\forall\gamma\in\Gamma.
In particular, for i=1,2,\cdots,n, x_{i}=x_{i}\circ\gamma. The only possibility is \Gamma=\{e\}, which means M is isometric to \mathbb{R}^{n}. This completes the proof of Theorem 6.1.
Remark 6.3.
We conclude this paper with the remark that (6.1) also holds for non-integers.
Given d, which is not necessarily an integer, by (5.20), we have
h_{d}(M)\leq h_{\lfloor d\rfloor+1}(M)\leq h_{\lfloor d\rfloor}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=h_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).
However, the rigidity statement does not hold for non-integer d.
For example, for every p\in(1,2), one can construct a smooth, complete, nonflat conformal
metric g on \mathbb{R}^{n} such that
\operatorname{Ric}_{g}\geq 0,\qquad\beta_{g}>0,
and
\dim\mathcal{H}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n},g)=\dim\mathcal{H}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n},g_{0})=n+1.
To see this, fix a\in(0,1) sufficiently close to 1, and choose a
smooth function \psi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty) satisfying
\psi(\rho)=\rho\quad\text{for }\rho\leq 1,\qquad\psi(\rho)=a\rho+b\quad\text{for }\rho\geq 2,
for some b>0, together with
0<\psi^{\prime}(\rho)\leq 1,\qquad\psi^{\prime\prime}(\rho)\leq 0.
Consider the rotationally symmetric metric
g=d\rho^{2}+\psi(\rho)^{2}g_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}.
It extends smoothly across the origin and is complete. The standard
curvature formulas for warped products give
\operatorname{Ric}_{g}(\partial_{\rho},\partial_{\rho})=-(n-1)\frac{\psi^{\prime\prime}}{\psi}\geq 0
and
\operatorname{Ric}_{g}(e,e)=-\frac{\psi^{\prime\prime}}{\psi}+(n-2)\frac{1-(\psi^{\prime})^{2}}{\psi^{2}}\geq 0
for every unit vector e tangent to the geodesic spheres. Moreover,
\beta_{g}=a^{n-1}>0.
After introducing a Euclidean radial coordinate r by
\frac{dr}{r}=\frac{d\rho}{\psi(\rho)},
the metric takes the form
g=e^{2w(r)}g_{0},
so this is indeed a conformal metric on \mathbb{R}^{n}. Its tangent cone
at infinity is
\left((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1},\,d\rho^{2}+a^{2}\rho^{2}g_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\right).
Let Y_{\ell,\alpha} be a spherical harmonic of degree \ell, and
consider a harmonic function on (\mathbb{R}^{n},g) with separated variables
u(\rho,\theta)=v_{\ell}(\rho)Y_{\ell,\alpha}(\theta).
The radial factor satisfies
v_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}+(n-1)\frac{\psi^{\prime}}{\psi}v_{\ell}^{\prime}-\frac{\ell(\ell+n-2)}{\psi^{2}}v_{\ell}=0.
The solution that is smooth at the origin is uniquely determined, up to
multiplication by a constant, by
v_{\ell}(\rho)=\rho^{\ell}+O(\rho^{\ell+2}),\quad\text{as }\rho\to 0.
Since \psi(\rho)=a\rho+b outside a compact set, this solution has
asymptotic growth
v_{\ell}(\rho)\sim C_{\ell}\rho^{\gamma_{\ell}},\quad\text{as }\rho\to+\infty,
where \gamma_{\ell} is the positive root of
\gamma_{\ell}(\gamma_{\ell}+n-2)=\frac{\ell(\ell+n-2)}{a^{2}}.
In particular,
\gamma_{1}=\frac{-(n-2)+\sqrt{(n-2)^{2}+4(n-1)a^{-2}}}{2}.
Since \gamma_{1}\to 1 as a\to 1, we may choose a<1 so close to 1
that
\gamma_{1}<p.
The degree-one spherical harmonics Y_{1,1},\ldots,Y_{1,n} therefore produce n linearly
independent harmonic functions on (\mathbb{R}^{n},g) of growth at most p. Together with
the constant functions, this gives
\dim\mathcal{H}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n},g)\geq n+1.
On the other hand, note that \gamma_{\ell}>\ell for every \ell\geq 1 because
a<1. Hence, for every \ell\geq 2,
\gamma_{\ell}\geq\gamma_{2}>2>p.
A spherical harmonic decomposition like 5.8 then shows that no mode of degree
at least two can occur in a harmonic function of growth at most p.
Consequently
\mathcal{H}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n},g)=\operatorname{span}\left\{1,\,v_{1}(\rho)Y_{1,1}(\theta),\ldots,v_{1}(\rho)Y_{1,n}(\theta)\right\}.
Thus
\dim\mathcal{H}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n},g)=n+1.
Remark 6.4.
A similar calculation as above yields an explicit value of the dimension of polynomial-growth harmonic functions:
\displaystyle h_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n},g)=\sum_{i=0}^{I_{n,d}}d_{i}=h_{I_{n,d}}(\mathbb{R}^{n},g_{0}),
where I_{n,d}=\lfloor\frac{-(n-2)+\sqrt{(n-2)^{2}+4a^{2}d(d+n-2)}}{2}\rfloor is an integer less than d whenever a<1, and
\displaystyle d_{i}=\binom{n+i-1}{i}-\binom{n+i-3}{i-2}
is the dimension of the space of spherical harmonics of degree i
on \mathbb{S}^{n-1}.
