                                                   Abstract. We study necessary conditions for local maximizers of the isotropic con-
                                                   stant that are related to notions of decomposability. Our main result asserts that the
                                                   polar body of a local maximizer of the isotropic constant can only have few Minkowski
                                                   summands; more precisely, its dimension of decomposability is at most 12 (n2 + 3n).

                                                   RS-decomposability is extended to a larger class of shadow systems. We discuss the
                                                   polytopal case, which turns out to have connections to (affine) rigidity theory, and inves-
                                                   tigate how the bound on the maximal number of irredundant summands can be improved
                                                   if we restrict our attention to convex bodies with certain symmetries.

   A natural follow-up question is to ask for a sharp upper bound on LK or, equivalently,
for the global maximizers of K 7→ LK in a given dimension n ∈ N. With regard to this
problem, a non-asymptotic version of the isotropic constant conjecture asserts that every
convex body K ∈ Knn satisfies

where ∆n is an n-dimensional simplex. Since L∆n ≤ C, this statement is sometimes called
the strong isotropic constant conjecture. As shown by Klartag [24], the strong isotropic
constant conjecture implies the asymmetric version of the well-known Mahler conjecture.
   In dimension n = 2, (1.2) follows from a result by Campi, Colesanti and Gronchi [5]
and it is known that triangles are the only local maximizers of K 7→ LK , as shown by
Saroglou [41]. The affirmative resolution of the slicing problem notwithstanding, not much
is known about local maximizers of K 7→ LK in dimensions n ≥ 3. In the following, we
collect some known facts about such local maximizers.
   Campi, Colesanti and Gronchi [5] proved that a convex body K ∈ Knn whose boundary
contains an open set U which is C 2 with positive principal curvatures cannot be a local
maximizer of the isotropic constant. Meyer and Reisner [35] strengthened this result,
showing the following.
Theorem 1.1 (Meyer-Reisner). If the boundary of K ∈ Kon contains a relatively open set
U that is strictly convex, then K cannot be a local maximizer of the isotropic constant.
  Actually, Meyer and Reisner showed a much stronger statement in [35], namely, that
even a single point x ∈ bd K of strict convexity and with positive generalized Gauss
curvature suffices to rule out that K is a local maximizer. With regard to the polytopal
case, Rademacher proved in [38] that a simplicial polytope P that maximizes the isotropic
constant has to be a simplex. In [22], this statement was strengthened as follows.
Theorem 1.2. A polytope P has to be a simplex if it is a local maximizer of K 7→ LK
and it has a simplicial vertex, i.e., a vertex v such that every facet F ⊂ P with v ∈ F is
a simplex.
   Moreover, it was shown in [22] that a zonotope that maximizes the isotropic constant
in the space of centrally symmetric convex bodies and that has a cubical zone (a zone that
contains only parallelepipeds) must be a cube [22, Thm. 1.4].
   Intuitively speaking, a common theme of the aforementioned results is that a local
maximizer of K 7→ LK cannot be too “flexible” or, equivalently, has to be sufficiently
“rigid” to rule out modifications that provably increase the isotropic constant. A goal of
the present article is to substantiate this intuition.
   The overarching theme of our investigation will be notions of decomposability of a
convex body. In this article, a notion of decomposability corresponds to an embedding of
a class of convex bodies A ⊂ Kn into a function space V , which assigns to each convex
body K ∈ A a function fK (e.g., its support function hK or its gauge function ∥ · ∥K ). In

the cases under consideration, the image of the embedding A := {fK | K ∈ A} will be
convex. Given fK , we consider the lineality space of the support cone of A at fK , which
is given by
                     L± (fK , A) := {g ∈ V | ∃ε > 0 : fK + εg, fK − εg ∈ A}.
The dimension of the vector space L± (fK , A) can be interpreted as a quantitative measure
of the decomposability of K, i.e., the “number of substantially different ways” in which
we can write fK as a convex combination fK = 21 (fL + fM ) with L, M ∈ A. Our goal is
to prove necessary conditions for local maximizers of K 7→ LK of the form:
        If dim L± (fK , A) is “too large”, then K cannot be a local maximizer of the
        isotropic constant.

Essentially as a byproduct of our new proof for Theorem 1.4, we obtain a new necessary

generalized RS-movements. Because the definition of generalized RS-movements and the
corresponding necessary condition for local maximizers are somewhat technical, we defer
them to Section 3.

  The second notion of decomposability that we study results from identifying convex
bodies with their gauge functions or, equivalently, with the support functions of their
polar bodies. We recall that a function h : Rn → R is called positively homogeneous if we
have h(λx) = λh(x) for all λ ≥ 0, x ∈ Rn . A function is called sublinear if it is positively
homogeneous and convex. Every sublinear function is continuous. We denote the convex
cone of all sublinear functions on Rn by Subl(Rn ). Every convex body K ∈ Kn induces a
sublinear function hK via
                              hK (x) = sup ⟨x, y⟩   for x ∈ Rn .
                                        y∈K

The function hK is called the support function of K. Since every sublinear function R → R
is the support function of a unique convex body, there is a one-to-one correspondence
between convex bodies and sublinear functions [43, Sect. 1.7]. Support functions are the
natural functional representatives of convex bodies in the context of the Brunn-Minkowski
theory because the vector addition given by the embedding Kn → Subl(Rn ), K 7→ hK is
the Minkowski sum K + L := {x + y | x ∈ K, y ∈ L}. A convex body L ∈ Kn is called a
Minkowski summand of K ∈ Kn if there exists a decomposition
                                        K =L+M
with M ∈ Kn . We denote the convex cone of convex bodies that are homothetic to (i.e.,
result from scaling and translating) a Minkowski summand of K by S(K) and call the
dimension of this cone the dimension of decomposability of K.
   Now let K ∈ Kon . The gauge function of K is the sublinear function
                              ∥x∥K := min{λ ≥ 0 | x ∈ λK}.
Equivalently, ∥ · ∥K is the unique asymmetric norm on Rn whose unit ball is K. As a
sublinear function, ∥ · ∥K is also the support function of a convex body K ◦ , which is called
the polar body of K. The map K 7→ K ◦ defines an involution on Kon . If we identify
convex bodies with their gauge functions, the analogue of an RS-movement is a linear
interpolant of gauge functions, i.e., a family (Kt )t∈[0,1] given by an equation of the form
∥ · ∥Kt = (1 − t)∥ · ∥K0 + t∥ · ∥K1 .
    In Section 4, we prove our main result, which asserts that the polar body K ◦ of a local
maximizer K of the isotropic constant has few Minkowski summands.
Theorem 1.5. Let K ∈ Kon be a local maximizer of the isotropic constant. Then the
dimension of decomposability of K ◦ satisfies

   Recalling Theorem 1.1 and Theorem 1.2 and the related results from the beginning of
this section, let us shortly discuss how Theorem 1.5 illustrates a common underlying geo-
metric phenomenon of these facts. We first note that if the boundary of a local maximizer
K contains a strictly convex open set U , then [35, Lem. 5] in combination with a result
by Sallee [40, Lem. 4.1] implies that dim S(K ◦ ) = ∞. In this sense, our result provides
an alternative proof for Theorem 1.1. With regard to Theorem 1.2, it is known that
if P is a polytope, then dim S(P ◦ ) can be expressed in terms of the affine dependences
of the vertices on each facet F ⊂ P . If we partition the boundary complex of P into
connected components by removing all facets of P that are simplices (but keeping their
vertices), then Theorem 1.5 implies that there cannot be “too many” of these connected
components. This phenomenon will be discussed in more detail in Section 5, where we
analyze the polytopal case of Theorem 1.5. Concerning the class of zonotopes, we note in
passing that dim S(Z ◦ ) is related to the number of cubical zones of a zonotope Z ∈ Kon
(this follows from the correspondence between Minkowski summands of the polar body
and facewise affine maps, which is discussed in Section 5).

  In Section 6, we consider classes of convex bodies with certain symmetries. In particular,
we consider centrally symmetric, unconditional and 1-symmetric convex bodies. It turns
out that if we restrict our attention to convex bodies with symmetries, then the upper
bound on the dimension of decomposability of the polar body can be improved.
  The article ends with some concluding remarks in Section 7, where we discuss whether
the bound in Theorem 1.5 can be improved in general.

                3. Shadow systems and generalized RS-movements
   Let K ∈ Knn and u ∈ Sn−1 . A shadow system is a one-parameter family of convex bodies
of the form
                      Kt = conv{x + tβ(x)u | x ∈ K}                               for t ∈ [t1 , t2 ],
where β : K → R is an arbitrary function. Motivated by certain isoperimetric-type in-
equalities, shadow systems were first studied by Rogers and Shephard [39], who showed
that the volume vol(Kt ) of a shadow system is a convex function in t. Shadow systems
have also turned out to be a useful tool in the study of the Mahler volume [6, 34, 11, 42,
1, 33].
   In the introduction, we have already encountered a special class of shadow systems,
namely, RS-movements. An RS-movement can be defined as a shadow system with the
additional requirements that
10                                           CHRISTIAN KIPP

     (i) the speed function β is constant on each chord K ∩ [x + span{u}] for x ∈ K, and
    (ii) the set {x + t · β(x)u | x ∈ K} is already convex for all t ∈ [t1 , t2 ].
We note that the speed function β of an RS-movement can be affine (on K). In this
case, Kt is an affine image of K for all t ∈ [t1 , t2 ]. Because such RS-movements provide
no information about extremizers of affinely invariant functionals, the definition of RS-
decomposability (Definition 1.3) requires β to be non-affine.
  RS-movements were introduced by Campi, Colesanti and Gronchi [5] to study local
maximizers of Sylvester-type functionals. Theorem 1.4 is a special case of [5, Thm. 3.1],
corresponding to the exponent r = 2. The latter result is stated in the setting of Sylvester’s
problem and addresses arbitrary exponents. For the connection between Sylvester’s prob-
lem and isotropic constants, we refer to [21].
  In this section, we study a class of shadow systems that is situated between RS-
movements and arbitrary shadow systems. Relaxing condition (i), we allow that the speed
function β is affine (rather than constant) on each chord K ∩ [x + span{u}] that intersects
the hyperplane u⊥ . The purpose of this somewhat technical condition will become clear
below.

                                                 5. The polytopal case
    In this section, we study the polytopal case of Theorem 1.5. Reconsidering the proof of
Theorem 1.5, we encounter the notion of facewise affine maps and discuss their relation
to Minkowski decomposability. At the end of the section, we compare Theorem 1.5 with
Corollary 3.7.
    Let P ∈ Kon be a polytope and F ⊂ P a facet. What can be said about the structure
of an arbitrary function g ∈ L± (∥ · ∥P )? By definition, there exists an ε > 0 such that
∥ · ∥P + εg and ∥ · ∥P − εg are both sublinear. Since ∥ · ∥P is affine on pos F , it follows that
εg|pos F and −εg|pos F are both convex. In other words, g|pos F has to be affine. This leads
us to the following definition. Here and in the following, we write Φn−1 (P ) for the set of
facets of P .
Definition 5.1. Let P ⊂ Rn be a polytope. A map f : bd P → R is called facewise affine
if f |F is affine for each F ∈ Φn−1 (P ). We denote the vector space of facewise affine maps
on bd P by F(P ).
    The considerations above show that the restriction g|bd P of a function g ∈ L± (∥ · ∥P )
is facewise affine. The converse statement is also true, i.e., if P ∈ Kon , g̃ ∈ F(P ) and
g : Rn → R is the unique positively homogeneous extension of g̃, then g is an element
of L± (∥ · ∥P ). To see this, we observe that a sufficiently small radial perturbation leaves
the vertices of P in convex position. It follows that L± (∥ · ∥P ) is isomorphic to F(P ), and
              SHADOW SYSTEMS, DECOMPOSABILITY AND ISOTROPIC CONSTANTS                             21

hence, a solid grasp of Theorem 1.5 is tantamount to a good understanding of the vector
space F(P ).
   In light of the considerations above, it is no surprise that the space F(P ) appears
in the study of Minkowski decomposability, notably in a paper by Smilansky [45]. Let
V := (v1 , v2 , . . . , vm ) be an m-tuple of vectors in Rn . Following [45], we write
                                             Pm                 Pm
                        D(V ) := {x ∈ Rn |     i=1 xi vi = 0,    i=1 xi = 0}
and D(P ) := D(vert P ) for a polytope P . If F ⊂ P is a face, then D(F ) is construed as
a subspace of D(P ). Clearly, a facewise affine map f ∈ F(P ) can be identified with its
restriction f |vert P . The defining constraints of being facewise affine are that the values
f (v1 ), . . . , f (vm ) of vertices v1 , . . . , vm on a single facet are compatible with the affine
dependences of v1 , . . . , vm . Combining this insight with the fact that F(P ) and span S(P ◦ )
are isomorphic, we recover a result by Smilansky [45, Thm. 4.3] (see also [31, Thm. 11]),

context of affine rigidity [48, 15], i.e., the study of transformations of a point set V ⊂ Rn

overview is given by Schneider [43, Sect. 3.2]. Some necessary conditions for equality
in the lower bound can be stated in combinatorial terms (for example, it is known that
n + 1 = dim S(P ◦ ) holds if P is simple), but in general, decomposability is not a combi-
natorial invariant. This was shown by Kallay [20], who also showed that dim S(P ) is a
projective invariant [19]. Therefore, dim S(P ◦ ) is a projective invariant as well.

Remark 5.3.
   (i) Interpreting the facets of a polytope P ∈ Kon as hyperedges of a hypergraph, we find
       ourselves precisely in the setting of the affine rigidity problem as considered in [15].
       In [15], it was shown that affine rigidity is a generic property of a hypergraph H,
       i.e., it is independent of the embedding of H into Rn as long as the coordinates of

   To conclude this section, we analyze how Theorem 1.5 relates to Corollary 3.7. For this,
let P ∈ Kon be a polytope and let β be the speed function of a generalized RS-movement
of P in direction u ∈ Sn−1 . Let F ⊂ P be a facet. We say that F is vertical with respect
to u if the outer unit normal vector v of F is orthogonal to u. Let F be non-vertical
with respect to u with, say, ⟨v, u⟩ > 0. Because β is the speed function of a generalized
RS-movement, there exists an ε > 0 such that gP,u + εβ + and gP,u − εβ + are both convex
on πu⊥ (F ). Since gP,u |πu⊥ (F ) is affine, it follows that β + |πu⊥ (F ) is affine as well. This
shows that the vector space G(P, u) from Definition 3.6 is closely related to F(P ). To
compare the logical strengths of Theorem 1.5 and Corollary 3.7, we have to compare the
dimensions of G(P, u) and F(P ). Here we have to distinguish four cases.
     (i) P does not have vertical facets with respect to u.
          (a) We have πu⊥ (P ) = P ∩ u⊥ . In this case, the map G(P, u) → F(P ), β 7→ β|bd P
              is an isomorphism, and we have dim G(P, u) = dim S(P ◦ ).
         (b) We have πu⊥ (P ) ̸= P ∩ u⊥ . In this case, the map G(P, u) → F(P ), β 7→
              β|bd P is well-defined, but not surjective. Therefore, we have dim G(P, u) <
              dim S(P ◦ ).

   (ii) P has at least one vertical facet with respect to u.
         (a) We have πu⊥ (P ) = P ∩ u⊥ . Here, the map F(P ) → G(P, u) that interpolates
             affinely on each chord P ∩(x+span{u}), is well-defined and linear, but possibly
             not surjective. It follows that dim G(P, u) ≥ dim S(P ◦ ).
        (b) We have πu⊥ (P ) ̸= P ∩ u⊥ . In this case, there exist facewise affine maps that

             are not facewise affine. The situation is indeterminate.

                            6. Symmetric convex bodies
   In this section, we discuss a variant of Theorem 1.5 for classes of convex bodies whose
symmetry group contains a given subgroup of O(n). In particular, we discuss the special
cases of centrally symmetric, unconditional and 1-symmetric convex bodies.
   Let K ⊂ Rn be a convex body. An isometry U : Rn → Rn is called a symmetry of K if it
satisfies U (K) = K. The symmetries of K form a group, we call it the symmetry group of
K and denote it by G(K). We note in passing that some authors use the term “symmetry
group” for the group of orientation-preserving symmetries of K and call the group G(K)
the “full symmetry group” of K. Every U ∈ G(K) has cK as a fixed point. In particular,
if K is centered, then all symmetries of K are linear and G(K) is a subgroup of O(n). For
a function f : Rn → R, we define a symmetry of f to be an isometry U : Rn → Rn with
f ◦ U = f . As in the case of a convex body, the symmetries of a function form a group,
which we call the symmetry group of f and denote by G(f ). Clearly, the symmetry group
of a convex body K coincides with the symmetry groups of its characteristic function 1K ,
its support function hK and (if K ∈ Kon ) its gauge function ∥ · ∥K .
Definition 6.1. Let G be a subgroup of O(n). We say that a convex body K ⊂ Rn or
a function f : Rn → R is G-symmetric if its symmetry group contains G. We denote by
KGn ⊂ Kn the space of full-dimensional G-symmetric convex bodies. For K ∈ Kn , we write

                                7. Concluding remarks
   Considering our main result Theorem 1.5, it is natural to ask whether the bound on
dim S(K ◦ ) can be improved. As discussed in the introduction, not much is known about
local maximizers of the isotropic constant. The only well-understood setting seems to be
the planar case. Here it is known that triangles are the only local maximizers, as shown
by Saroglou [41]. In other words, every local maximizer K ∈ Ko2 of K 7→ LK satisfies

We note, however, that the failure of K 7→ LK ◦ to be quasi-convex does not imply the
existence of decomposable local maximizers – indeed, such local maximizers do not exist
in the planar case even though K 7→ LK ◦ fails to be quasi-convex in this setting. In higher
dimensions, it is of course conceivable that there exist decomposable local maximizers –
for all we know, such local maximizers might even have dimensions of decomposability of
order Ω(n2 ).
   Aside from quantitative improvements to Theorem 1.5, a natural line of further thought
is to look for analogues in terms of other notions of decomposability, the most obvious
candidate being the Minkowski decomposability of K. It is by no means clear how the
proof strategy from Section 4 might be adapted to this problem, and the representation in
terms of support functions seems to be less suited for the task of computing the integrals
at hand. As in the polar case, it is natural to ask whether K 7→ LK might be quasi-convex
with respect to Minkowski addition, and again, the answer is negative, as witnessed by
the example of two non-homothetic ellipsoids E1 , E2 . Nevertheless, it is an interesting
question whether local maximizers of K 7→ LK can be Minkowski decomposable, and if
so, what the maximal dimension of decomposability might be.
