Abstract.
For every smooth Riemannian metric on the two sphere whose Gaussian curvature is bounded below by one, we prove that each positive Laplace eigenvalue, counted with multiplicity, is no smaller than the corresponding eigenvalue of the unit round sphere. Equality at any positive position in the ordered spectrum forces the metric to be isometric to the unit round metric.
We further establish a sharp finite spectral counting comparison for Alexandrov two spheres with curvature bounded below by one. At every positive spectral threshold of the unit round sphere, the number of Laplace eigenvalues below or at that threshold, counted with multiplicity, does not exceed the corresponding number for the round sphere. Equality at any such threshold forces the Alexandrov sphere to be isometric to the unit round sphere.
As an application, we obtain the sharp Euclidean dimension bound for spaces of polynomial growth harmonic functions on complete three dimensional manifolds with nonnegative sectional curvature and positive asymptotic volume ratio, together with rigidity in the equality case.

1. Introduction
Throughout the paper we use the conventions
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}^{+}=\{1,2,\ldots\},\qquad\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}.
For a nonnegative self-adjoint operator P with compact resolvent and eigenvalues
\lambda_{0}(P)\leq\lambda_{1}(P)\leq\cdots, counted with multiplicity, we use the operator-subscript notation
\displaystyle N_{P}^{<}(\Lambda)
\displaystyle:=\#\{j\geq 0:\lambda_{j}(P)<\Lambda\},
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{P}(\Lambda)
\displaystyle:=\#\{j\geq 0:\lambda_{j}(P)\leq\Lambda\}.
Notation 1.1 (Spectral conventions).
We use
\Delta_{g}=\operatorname{div}_{g}\nabla,\qquad-\Delta_{g}\geq 0.
For a compact Riemannian manifold (M,g), define
\lambda_{j}(M,g):=\lambda_{j}(-\Delta_{g}),\qquad j\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.
For a compact Alexandrov space X carrying its canonical
nonnegative Laplacian, define
\lambda_{j}(X):=\lambda_{j}(-\Delta_{X}).
All eigenvalues are counted with multiplicity and indexed from 0.
Let (M^{n},g) be a complete Riemannian manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature. For d\geq 0, denote by \mathscr{H}_{d}(M^{n}) the vector space of harmonic functions on M^{n} with polynomial growth of degree at most d. Yau’s original finite-dimensionality question [37, Problem 48, pp. 275–319] asks whether
\displaystyle\dim\mathscr{H}_{d}(M^{n})<\infty
for every fixed growth degree d\geq 0. That formulation also asks whether \mathbb{R}^{n} has the maximal dimension of polynomial-growth harmonic functions.
In 1998, Colding and Minicozzi proved the following Weyl-type bound [11].
Theorem 1.2 (Colding–Minicozzi).
If (M^{n},g) is complete and \Rc\geq 0, then there exists C(n)>0 such that, for every d\geq 1,
\displaystyle\dim\mathscr{H}_{d}(M^{n})\leq C(n)d^{n-1}.
The same circle of ideas includes Colding–Minicozzi’s work on harmonic functions with polynomial growth and their work on harmonic sections [9, 8, 10], also see [21]. Together with Theorem 1.2, these results give the correct Weyl growth exponent in the following sense.
Here “Weyl growth exponent” means only the power-law exponent predicted by Weyl’s law, not the sharp leading Weyl constant. Let (N^{n-1},h) be a closed Riemannian manifold with Riemannian metric h and suppose \Rc(h)\geq(n-2)h. On the punctured metric cone
\displaystyle C(N)=(0,\infty)\times N,\qquad h_{C}=dr^{2}+r^{2}h,
the Ricci tensor is nonnegative.
If -\Delta_{h}\varphi_{i}=\lambda_{i}(-\Delta_{h})\varphi_{i}, then a separated function r^{\alpha}\varphi_{i}(\theta) is harmonic on the cone if and only if
(1.1)
\displaystyle\alpha(\alpha+n-2)=\lambda_{i}(-\Delta_{h}).
By (1.1), we have
(1.2)
\displaystyle\dim\mathscr{H}_{k}(C(N))=\mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{h}}\big(k(k+n-2)\big).
Note Weyl’s law gives
\displaystyle\lim_{\Lambda\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{h}}(\Lambda)}{\Lambda^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}=\frac{\omega_{n-1}\operatorname{Vol}(N)}{(2\pi)^{n-1}},
where \omega_{n-1} is the Euclidean volume of the unit ball in \mathbb{R}^{n-1}. Thus the Weyl order of the counting function is \Lambda^{\frac{n-1}{2}}.
In the cone discussion above, evaluating at the round thresholds \Lambda=k(k+n-2)\sim k^{2} gives the order k^{n-1} for \mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{h}}(k(k+n-2)), equivalently for the cone dimension in (1.2). Therefore Theorem 1.2 has the sharp Weyl growth exponent d^{n-1}.
The sharp comparison part of Yau’s question asks whether the Euclidean model gives the sharp upper bound at the integer degrees.
Question 1.3 (Yau’s sharp dimension question).
Let (M^{n},g) be complete with \Rc\geq 0. Is it true that
(1.3)
\displaystyle\dim\mathscr{H}_{d}(M^{n})\leq\dim\mathscr{H}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\quad\quad\forall d\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}?
Moreover, if equality holds for some d\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}, must M^{n} be isometric to \mathbb{R}^{n}?
Remark 1.4.
When n=2, Li–Tam give an affirmative answer to the corresponding surface question [19, Theorem 4.2]. For d=1 and general n, the inequality follows from Li–Tam [18], and the rigidity is due to Cheeger–Colding–Minicozzi [6].
Donnelly [12] showed, for some \epsilon>0 there is a complete manifold (M^{5},g) with \Rc\geq 0 such that
\displaystyle\dim\mathscr{H}_{2-\epsilon}(M^{5})>\dim\mathscr{H}_{2-\epsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{5}).
This does not disprove the integer-degree version (1.3), and the integer-degree problem remains a natural sharp form of the question.
For the Euclidean cone \mathbb{R}^{n}=C(\mathbb{S}^{n-1}), (1.2) gives the usual dimension of harmonic polynomials of degree at most k. Hence the sharp integer-degree form of Yau’s question suggests the following question, which can be viewed as a compact cross-section version of Question 1.3.
Question 1.5.
Assume (N^{n-1},h) has \Rc(h)\geq(n-2)h, do we have
(1.4)
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{h}}\big(k(k+n-2)\big)\leq\mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}}\big(k(k+n-2)\big),\qquad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}?
And if the equality in (1.4) holds for some k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}, must N^{n-1} be isometric to \mathbb{S}^{n-1}?
Remark 1.6.
This point of view is already present in [11, pp. 260–262]. Colding and Minicozzi [11, Page 261] (also see [24, Conjecture 3]) pointed out that
“it has been a long standing open problem whether for a closed manifold N^{n-1} with Ricci curvature greater or equal to n-2,
(1.5)
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})\leq\lambda_{i}(N^{n-1}),\qquad i\geq 1,
with equality for some positive integer i=\max\{j\geq i|\lambda_{j}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})=\lambda_{i}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})\} if and only if N^{n-1} is isometric to \mathbb{S}^{n-1}.”
Aryan’s recent work gives counterexamples to (1.5) in the conjecture for n\geq 5 [2]. We show that (1.5) does not hold for n=4 in Section 16.
Note that the rigidity part of the above open problem is equivalent to the rigidity part of Question 1.5, and Question 1.5 remains open for n\geq 3. In this paper, we answer Question 1.5 affirmatively in the case n=3 and prove (1.5) in this dimension.
This inequality (1.4) should be understood as a spectral counting rigidity statement. It compares entire eigenvalue distributions rather than individual eigenvalues.
The estimate (1.4) is also a Pólya-type spectral comparison. Pólya’s classical conjecture asks that the Dirichlet and Neumann eigenvalue counting functions of a Euclidean domain be bounded from above and from below, respectively, by the leading Weyl term [28, 29]; here the comparison model is the round sphere rather than a Euclidean domain.
Thus, in the present paper the phrase “Pólya-type” is meant in this precise finite-counting sense: one asks for a non-asymptotic comparison at the exact round thresholds k(k+n-2), not merely for this Weyl-order growth exponent.
Remark 1.7.
Weyl’s law and Bishop–Gromov volume comparison imply that a non-round metric with the relevant Ricci lower bound has fewer eigenvalues than the round sphere at sufficiently high energy, but this asymptotic information does not decide the exact finite thresholds in (1.4). The difficulty is precisely finite-index spectral comparison.
One main result of this paper is the full finite-index comparison when the cross-section dimension is two.
Theorem 1.8.
Let (S^{2},g) be a smooth Riemannian manifold with sectional curvature K_{g}\geq 1. Then
(1.6)
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(S^{2},g)\geq\lambda_{i}(\mathbb{S}^{2},g_{\mathrm{round}}),\qquad i\geq 1.
Moreover, if equality holds in (1.6) for some i_{0}\geq 1, then (S^{2},g) is isometric to (\mathbb{S}^{2},g_{\mathrm{round}}).
One corollary of the above theorem is the following counting comparison result, which answers Question 1.5 affirmatively.
Corollary 1.9.
Let (S^{2},g) be a smooth Riemannian manifold with sectional curvature K_{g}\geq 1. Then
(1.7)
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{g}}\big(k(k+1)\big)\leq\mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}}\big(k(k+1)\big),\qquad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.
And if the equality in (1.7) holds for some k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}, then (S^{2},g) is isometric to (\mathbb{S}^{2},g_{\mathrm{round}}).
To study Question 1.3 for (M^{3},g), we establish the following strengthening of Corollary 1.9.
Theorem 1.10.
Let X be one Alexandrov two-sphere with curvature \geq 1. Then
(1.8)
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{X}}\big(l(l+1)\big)\leq\mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}}\big(l(l+1)\big),\qquad\forall l\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.
Moreover, if equality holds in (1.8) for some l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}, then X is isometric to (\mathbb{S}^{2},g_{\mathrm{round}}).
Remark 1.11.
Well-known example 17.3 shows that a nonround Alexandrov two-sphere X with curvature \geq 1 may still satisfy \lambda_{1}(X)=\lambda_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2})=2. Thus the rigidity part in Theorem 1.8 does not hold for Alexandrov spheres; the correct hypothesis is equality of the whole finite counting function at a round cluster threshold.
As a consequence of Theorem 1.10, we obtain the following sharp dimension estimate for polynomial growth harmonic functions on three-dimensional manifolds with nonnegative sectional curvature and positive asymptotic volume ratio.
Theorem 1.12.
Let (M^{3},g) be a complete Riemannian manifold with K_{g}\geq 0 and \operatorname{AVR}(M)>0. Then
(1.9)
\displaystyle\dim\mathscr{H}_{d}(M)\leq\dim\mathscr{H}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{3})=(d+1)^{2},\qquad d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.
Moreover, if equality holds in (1.9) for some d\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}, then (M^{3},g) is isometric to \mathbb{R}^{3}.
Remark 1.13.
From [36], we know \dim\mathscr{H}_{d}(M^{3})\geq 2, i.e. there exists a non-constant harmonic function of polynomial growth on such (M^{3},g).
The paper is written deliberately as a progression of languages. The underlying algebra is always the same: one constructs a first-order operator, compares the two partner operators
\displaystyle T^{*}T+c\quad\text{and}\quad TT^{*}+c,
uses curvature to obtain a one-sided inequality, and then converts the kernel dimension of T into a finite counting recursion by min–max. What changes from one stage to the next is not the formal spectral mechanism, but the language needed to make the objects canonical. This general mechanism is discussed in Section 2.
Part 1
. It contains section 3--6, and it deals with rotationally symmetric metrics on S^{n-1}.
For a rotationally symmetric metric, spherical-harmonic decomposition reduces the Laplacian to a family of one-dimensional radial operators. A first-order factorization, together with the curvature inequalities, the identity
\mathrm{dim}(\ker\mathbf{B}_{m})=1,\qquad\ker\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}=\{0\},
and min–max yields a counting recursion in the angular degree. Summing over the spherical-harmonic multiplicities gives the sharp comparison with the round sphere, as well as the corresponding rigidity statement. When n=3, this is precisely the usual Fourier-mode decomposition on circles.
Section 3 sets up the rotationally symmetric model and fixes the real Hilbert-space conventions. Sections 4–6 prove the rotationally symmetric model. Section 4 constructs the separated radial operators and the radial ladder identity. Section 5 proves the one-dimensional kernel identity \dim_{\mathbb{R}}\ker\mathbf{B}_{m}=1 and the adjoint-kernel vanishing \ker\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}=\{0\}. Section 6 applies min–max, sums the angular multiplicities, and proves the rotational comparison and rigidity theorem.
Part 2
. It contains
Sections 7–10
and treats arbitrary smooth metrics on S^{2}.
Without rotational symmetry, the angular modes are replaced by the line
bundles
E_{m}=(T^{1,0}S^{2})^{\otimes m}.
The associated first-order operators
B_{m}:L^{2}(E_{m})\longrightarrow L^{2}(E_{m+1})
satisfy a global curvature-dependent ladder identity. The kernel formulas
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker B_{m}=2m+1,\qquad\ker B_{m}^{*}=\{0\},
then give, through spectral pairing and min–max, a recursion for
A_{m}=B_{m}^{*}B_{m}+m(m+1)I.
Iteration yields the sharp finite counting inequalities, and the equality
case is handled by a strict min–max argument.
Section 7 constructs the global
two-dimensional ladder on the bundles E_{m}, defines B_{m} and its
formal adjoint, identifies B_{0}^{*}B_{0} with -\Delta_{g}, and proves the
Riemannian ladder identity. Section 8 proves
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker B_{m}=2m+1,\qquad\ker B_{m}^{*}=\{0\}.
Section 9 applies the abstract counting
mechanism to the shifted operators A_{m}, proves the ordered eigenvalue
comparison, and obtains smooth rigidity from the strict form of the ladder
inequality.
Section 10 reformulates the same construction
on the Riemann surface (S^{2},J). The bundles are identified with powers
of the anticanonical bundle, and the operators B_{m} become Dolbeault
operators through
\Lambda^{0,1}T^{*}S^{2}\otimes K^{-m}\simeq K^{-m-1}.
Riemann–Roch and Serre duality give the kernel dimensions, while the
Bochner–Kodaira identity supplies the curvature-dependent ladder
inequality. This provides a shorter structural explanation of the smooth
counting argument and, more importantly, introduces the language that
continues to make sense for singular conformal metrics.

After fixing a conformal identification of the Alexandrov sphere with
\mathbb{CP}^{1}, the smooth Gaussian curvature is replaced by the

\nu_{X}:=\omega_{X}-dA_{X}\geq 0.
The singular argument separates the comparison statement from the equality

F:\mathbb{CP}^{1}\longrightarrow X,

g_{X}=e^{2u}g_{0},

\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{c}_{m}^{X})\subset\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{a}_{m+1}^{X}),\qquad\mathfrak{c}_{m}^{X}\geq\mathfrak{a}_{m+1}^{X}.
The same section proves the holomorphic kernel bound
\ker B_{m}^{X}\subset H^{0}(\mathbb{CP}^{1},K_{\mathbb{CP}^{1}}^{-m}),\qquad\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker B_{m}^{X}\leq 2m+1,
and establishes the compact-resolvent properties required by the abstract
counting recursion.
Section 13 analyzes the atomic case.
Equality in a round counting threshold first saturates every rung of the
singular ladder and produces a top-rung space of dimension 2l+1. If
the curvature-defect measure had an atom at p, the maximal adjoint-domain
condition would force every section in that top-rung space to vanish at
p. The resulting loss of one holomorphic degree of freedom contradicts
the saturated dimension. Consequently, equality at a round counting
threshold forces \nu_{X} to be atomless.
Section 14 treats the complementary case.
When the curvature measure is atomless, the weak form inequality can be
sharpened on the saturated top rung to the exact defect identity
\mathfrak{c}_{l-1}^{X}(s,s)-\mathfrak{a}_{l}^{X}(s,s)=2l\int_{X}|s|_{h_{l,X}}^{2}\,d\nu_{X},\qquad s\in V_{l}^{X}.
The proof recovers the curvature term directly from the maximal adjoint
domain, using the Poincaré–Lelong formula, truncation, and the vanishing of
the boundary fluxes around the zeros of the holomorphic section.
Section 15 assembles these ingredients.
Smooth approximation gives the ordered eigenvalue comparison, while the
singular ladder gives the finite counting inequalities at the round
thresholds. In the equality case,
Section 13 first eliminates atoms.
The exact identity from
Section 14, together with saturation of
the holomorphic top rung, then forces
\nu_{X}=0.
Gauss–Bonnet and the equality case of Alexandrov volume comparison finally
show that X is isometric to the unit round sphere.
Part 4
. It contains Sections 16 and
17 and describes both the higher-dimensional
limitation of the spectral comparison and its three-dimensional
harmonic-growth application.
Section 16 shows that the two-dimensional ordered
eigenvalue comparison does not extend directly to higher-dimensional
cross-sections. It constructs an explicit smooth conformal perturbation of
the round metric on S^{3} satisfying
\operatorname{Ric}_{g}\geq 2g
for which a sufficiently high eigenvalue lies strictly below the
corresponding round eigenvalue. Thus the success of the line-bundle ladder
on S^{2} reflects a genuinely two-dimensional structure rather than a
general consequence of a Ricci lower bound.
Section 17 applies the Alexandrov counting
theorem to complete three-manifolds with nonnegative sectional curvature
and positive asymptotic volume ratio. It first obtains the unique tangent
cone at infinity
C(X),
shows that the cross-section X is an Alexandrov two-sphere with curvature at
least 1, and applies the finite-dimensional comparison theorem for
polynomial-growth harmonic functions together with the counting estimate
of Section 15. This gives
\dim\mathscr{H}_{d}(M)\leq\mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{X}}\bigl(d(d+1)\bigr)\leq(d+1)^{2}=\dim\mathscr{H}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).
If equality holds at a positive integer degree, Alexandrov counting
rigidity makes X the unit round sphere. The tangent cone is therefore
\mathbb{R}^{3}, and the equality case of Bishop–Gromov comparison forces
M itself to be isometric to \mathbb{R}^{3}. The section also explains,
through the spherical-football example, why equality of a single
Alexandrov eigenvalue is insufficient for rigidity, and concludes with a
discussion of the collapsed regime \operatorname{AVR}(M)=0.
Thus the paper proceeds from the abstract ladder-counting mechanism to a
rotational model, then to an invariant smooth construction and its
complex-geometric reformulation, and finally to the singular Alexandrov
setting required by the tangent-cone application. The last part records
both the sharp three-dimensional consequence and the obstruction to a
straightforward higher-dimensional extension.

2. The abstract ladder-counting mechanism
The purpose of this section is to isolate the functional-analytic mechanism that is common to all the geometric realizations used below. The geometry determines the Hilbert spaces, the closed first-order operators, their domains, their kernel dimensions, and the form inequality between consecutive levels. Once those facts have been verified, the remaining spectral argument is independent of whether the operators arise from a rotational decomposition, a smooth Riemannian bundle, a Dolbeault complex, or a singular Alexandrov metric.
The common mechanism has six steps.
(1)
The positive spectra of the partner operators T^{*}T and TT^{*} agree, including multiplicity.
(2)
The space \ker T gives an unpaired block at the bottom of T^{*}T and therefore produces an index shift.
(3)
The condition \ker T^{*}=\{0\} removes the corresponding unpaired block on the TT^{*} side.
(4)
A quadratic-form inequality compares the TT^{*} partner at one level with the T^{*}T operator at the next level.
(5)
The min–max principle converts this comparison into a recursion for strict and closed counting functions.
(6)
The recursion terminates when the shift at the final level reaches the prescribed round threshold.
This is precisely the spectral mechanism behind the different geometric stages of the proof.
All Hilbert spaces in this section may be real or complex. We write \mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\} for the ground field, and all dimensions and spectral multiplicities are taken over \mathbb{F}.
Notation 2.1.
Let \{H_{m}\}_{m\geq 0} be Hilbert spaces, and let
\displaystyle B_{m}:\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m})\subset H_{m}\longrightarrow H_{m+1},\qquad m\geq 0,
be closed densely defined operators. Let
\displaystyle 0\leq\alpha_{0}<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\cdots.
Define the closed quadratic forms
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}_{m}(u,v)
\displaystyle:=\langle B_{m}u,B_{m}v\rangle_{H_{m+1}}+\alpha_{m}\langle u,v\rangle_{H_{m}},
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{a}_{m})
\displaystyle=\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m}),
\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{m}(s,t)
\displaystyle:=\langle B_{m}^{*}s,B_{m}^{*}t\rangle_{H_{m}}+\alpha_{m}\langle s,t\rangle_{H_{m+1}},
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{c}_{m})
\displaystyle=\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m}^{*}).
Let A_{m} and C_{m} be the associated self-adjoint operators. Equivalently,
\displaystyle A_{m}=B_{m}^{*}B_{m}+\alpha_{m}I\quad\text{in }H_{m},\qquad C_{m}=B_{m}B_{m}^{*}+\alpha_{m}I\quad\text{in }H_{m+1}.
We assume that, for every m\geq 0, both A_{m} and C_{m}
have compact resolvent. Since A_{m},C_{m}\geq 0, this is equivalent
to requiring the bounded operators
\displaystyle(A_{m}+I)^{-1}:H_{m}\longrightarrow H_{m},\qquad(C_{m}+I)^{-1}:H_{m+1}\longrightarrow H_{m+1}
to be compact.
Set
\displaystyle r_{m}:=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\ker B_{m},\qquad s_{m}:=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\ker B_{m}^{*}.
These dimensions are finite because the eigenspaces of A_{m} and C_{m} at \alpha_{m} are finite dimensional.
Lemma 2.2.
In the setting of Notation 2.1, the positive spectra of B_{m}^{*}B_{m} and B_{m}B_{m}^{*} agree, including multiplicity. More precisely, for every \lambda>0, the map B_{m} gives an isomorphism
\displaystyle B_{m}:\ker(B_{m}^{*}B_{m}-\lambda I)\longrightarrow\ker(B_{m}B_{m}^{*}-\lambda I),
whose inverse is \lambda^{-1}B_{m}^{*}. Consequently,
(2.1)
\displaystyle\lambda_{j+r_{m}}(A_{m})=\lambda_{j+s_{m}}(C_{m}),\qquad j=0,1,2,\ldots,
\displaystyle N_{A_{m}}^{<}(\Lambda)-N_{C_{m}}^{<}(\Lambda)=r_{m}-s_{m},\quad\quad\forall\Lambda>\alpha_{m},
(2.2)
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{A_{m}}(\Lambda)-\mathcal{N}_{C_{m}}(\Lambda)=r_{m}-s_{m},\quad\quad\forall\Lambda\geq\alpha_{m},.
Proof.
Suppose that u\in\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m}^{*}B_{m}) and
\displaystyle B_{m}^{*}B_{m}u=\lambda u,\qquad\lambda>0.
Then B_{m}u\neq 0. Moreover, B_{m}u\in\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m}^{*}), and
B_{m}^{*}(B_{m}u)=\lambda u\in\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m}). Hence
\displaystyle B_{m}B_{m}^{*}(B_{m}u)=\lambda B_{m}u.
Conversely, if v\in\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m}B_{m}^{*}) and
\displaystyle B_{m}B_{m}^{*}v=\lambda v,\qquad\lambda>0,
then B_{m}^{*}v\neq 0 and
\displaystyle B_{m}^{*}B_{m}(B_{m}^{*}v)=\lambda B_{m}^{*}v.
The two maps are inverse after multiplication by \lambda^{-1}. Thus the positive eigenspaces have the same dimensions.
The eigenspace of A_{m} at \alpha_{m} is \ker B_{m}, while the eigenspace of C_{m} at \alpha_{m} is \ker B_{m}^{*}. Removing these two bottom eigenspaces leaves the same ordered sequence of shifted positive eigenvalues. This proves (2.1). The two counting identities in (2.2) follow by counting the bottom eigenspaces and the paired positive spectrum separately.
∎
The quadratic-form order C_{m}\geq A_{m+1} will always mean both the
form-domain inclusion and the inequality displayed below. On a
singular space, the formal commutator identity on smooth sections of the
regular set does not by itself determine the closed realizations of the two
operators. Thus both the domain inclusion and the form inequality must be
established at the level of the closed quadratic forms.
Lemma 2.3.
Fix m\geq 0. Assume that the consecutive forms satisfy
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{c}_{m})
\displaystyle\subset\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{a}_{m+1}),
(2.3)
\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{m}(v,v)
\displaystyle\geq\mathfrak{a}_{m+1}(v,v),\qquad v\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{c}_{m}).
Then \ker B_{m}^{*}=\{0\}.
Proof.
Let v\in\ker B_{m}^{*}. Then v\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{c}_{m}), so (2.3) gives v\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{a}_{m+1}) and
\displaystyle\alpha_{m+1}\|v\|_{H_{m+1}}^{2}
\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{a}_{m+1}(v,v)\leq\mathfrak{c}_{m}(v,v)=\alpha_{m}\|v\|_{H_{m+1}}^{2}.
Since \alpha_{m+1}>\alpha_{m}, one has v=0.
∎
Proposition 2.4.
Fix m\geq 0 and assume (2.3). Then, for every j\geq 0,
(2.4)
\displaystyle\lambda_{j+r_{m}}(A_{m})=\lambda_{j}(C_{m})\geq\lambda_{j}(A_{m+1}).
Consequently, for every \Lambda\in\mathbb{R},
(2.5)
\displaystyle N_{A_{m}}^{<}(\Lambda)
\displaystyle\leq r_{m}+N_{A_{m+1}}^{<}(\Lambda),\quad\quad\mathcal{N}_{A_{m}}(\Lambda)
\displaystyle\leq r_{m}+\mathcal{N}_{A_{m+1}}(\Lambda).
Proof.
Lemma 2.3 gives \ker B_{m}^{*}=\{0\}. Therefore Lemma 2.2 gives the equality in (2.4).
The form inequality (2.3) and the min–max principle give
\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(C_{m})\geq\lambda_{j}(A_{m+1}),\qquad j\geq 0.
This proves (2.4).
Equivalently, the number of eigenvalues of C_{m} below, or below and at, a fixed threshold does not exceed the corresponding number for A_{m+1}, this proves (2.5).
∎
Proposition 2.5.
Let 0\leq q\leq l, and assume that (2.3) holds for m=q,q+1,\ldots,l-1. Then
\displaystyle N_{A_{q}}^{<}(\alpha_{l})
\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=q}^{l-1}r_{m},
(2.6)
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{A_{q}}(\alpha_{l})
\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=q}^{l}r_{m}.
Here the first sum is understood to be zero when q=l.
Proof.
Since
\displaystyle A_{l}=B_{l}^{*}B_{l}+\alpha_{l}I\geq\alpha_{l}I,
one has
\displaystyle N_{A_{l}}^{<}(\alpha_{l})=0,\qquad\mathcal{N}_{A_{l}}(\alpha_{l})=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\ker B_{l}=r_{l}.
Iterating the two inequalities in (2.5) from level q to level l gives (2.6).
∎
Proposition 2.6.
Let l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}, and assume that (2.3) holds for m=0,1,\ldots,l-1. Suppose that
\displaystyle r_{m}\leq\rho_{m},\qquad 0\leq m\leq l,
(2.7)
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{A_{0}}(\alpha_{l})=\sum_{m=0}^{l}\rho_{m}.
Then
(2.8)
\displaystyle r_{m}=\rho_{m},\qquad 0\leq m\leq l,
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{A_{m}}(\alpha_{l})
\displaystyle=r_{m}+\mathcal{N}_{A_{m+1}}(\alpha_{l}),
(2.9)
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{C_{m}}(\alpha_{l})
\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{A_{m+1}}(\alpha_{l}),\qquad 0\leq m\leq l-1.
Define
\displaystyle E_{l}
\displaystyle:=\ker(C_{l-1}-\alpha_{l}I),\quad\quad V_{l}:=\ker B_{l}\cap\operatorname{Dom}(B_{l-1}^{*}).
Then
(2.10)
\displaystyle E_{l}=V_{l}=\ker B_{l},\qquad\dim_{\mathbb{F}}E_{l}=\rho_{l},
(2.11)
\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{l-1}(s,s)=\mathfrak{a}_{l}(s,s)=\alpha_{l}\|s\|_{H_{l}}^{2},\quad\quad\forall s\in E_{l}.
Proof.
Set
\displaystyle n_{m}:=\mathcal{N}_{A_{m}}(\alpha_{l}),\qquad q_{m}:=\mathcal{N}_{C_{m}}(\alpha_{l}).
By Lemma 2.3, \ker B_{m}^{*}=\{0\} for 0\leq m\leq l-1. Since \alpha_{l}\geq\alpha_{m}, Lemma 2.2 gives
\displaystyle n_{m}=r_{m}+q_{m},\qquad 0\leq m\leq l-1.
The form inequality gives q_{m}\leq n_{m+1}, while
\displaystyle n_{l}=\mathcal{N}_{A_{l}}(\alpha_{l})=r_{l}.
Therefore
\displaystyle n_{0}
\displaystyle\leq r_{0}+n_{1}\leq r_{0}+r_{1}+n_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\sum_{m=0}^{l}r_{m}\leq\sum_{m=0}^{l}\rho_{m}.
The first and last terms are equal by (2.7). Hence equality holds at every stage. This proves (2.8) and (2.9).
At the last step,
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{C_{l-1}}(\alpha_{l})=\mathcal{N}_{A_{l}}(\alpha_{l})=r_{l}=\rho_{l}.
Moreover,
\displaystyle C_{l-1}\geq A_{l}\geq\alpha_{l}I
in the quadratic-form sense. Thus every eigenvalue of C_{l-1} counted by \mathcal{N}_{C_{l-1}}(\alpha_{l}) is equal to \alpha_{l}, and consequently
\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{F}}E_{l}=r_{l}.
Let s\in E_{l}. Then s\in\operatorname{Dom}(C_{l-1})\subset\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{c}_{l-1}) and
\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{l-1}(s,s)=\alpha_{l}\|s\|_{H_{l}}^{2}.
Using the form inequality and the definition of \mathfrak{a}_{l}, we obtain
\displaystyle\alpha_{l}\|s\|_{H_{l}}^{2}
\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{a}_{l}(s,s)\leq\mathfrak{c}_{l-1}(s,s)=\alpha_{l}\|s\|_{H_{l}}^{2}.
Hence B_{l}s=0, and s\in\operatorname{Dom}(B_{l-1}^{*}). Therefore
\displaystyle E_{l}\subset V_{l}\subset\ker B_{l}.
The first and last spaces have the same dimension r_{l}, so all three spaces coincide. This proves (2.10), and the same argument gives (2.11).
∎
Corollary 2.7.
Assume that
\displaystyle\alpha_{m}=m(m+1),\qquad r_{m}\leq 2m+1,\qquad m\geq 0,
and that (2.3) holds at every level. Then, for every l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},
(2.12)
\displaystyle N_{A_{0}}^{<}\bigl(l(l+1)\bigr)\leq l^{2},\quad\quad\mathcal{N}_{A_{0}}\bigl(l(l+1)\bigr)\leq(l+1)^{2}.
If equality holds in the closed-counting estimate in (2.12), then all conclusions of Proposition 2.6 hold with
\displaystyle\rho_{m}=2m+1,\qquad 0\leq m\leq l.
Proof.
Proposition 2.5 and the identities
\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{l-1}(2m+1)=l^{2},\qquad\sum_{m=0}^{l}(2m+1)=(l+1)^{2}
give (2.12). The eigenvalue statement is the strict-counting formulation of the first inequality. The equality assertion follows from Proposition 2.6.
∎
Remark 2.8 (Dictionary for the four geometric realizations).
The abstract results above will be used through the following identifications.
(1)
In the rotationally symmetric proof, \mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}, the spaces H_{m} are the radial Hilbert spaces, B_{m}=\mathbf{B}_{m}, and \alpha_{m}=m(m+n-2). The geometric work is the identification of the correct pole-compatible domains, the one-dimensional kernel computation r_{m}=1, and the radial curvature form inequality. Proposition 2.5 then supplies the spectral recursion.
(2)
In the smooth Riemannian proof, \mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}, H_{m}=L^{2}(E_{m}), B_{m} is the closed bundle operator, and \alpha_{m}=m(m+1). The bundle construction, the curvature identity, and the kernel dimension r_{m}=2m+1 are the geometric inputs. Corollary 2.7 supplies the counting argument.
(3)
In the complex-geometric proof, the same ladder is written on the anticanonical powers K^{-m}. Riemann–Roch and Serre duality provide the kernel information, while the Bochner–Kodaira identity provides the form inequality. The Hilbert-space conclusion is again Corollary 2.7.
(4)
In the Alexandrov proof, H_{m}=H_{m}^{X} and B_{m}=B_{m}^{X} are the singular closed operators with their maximal Hilbert-space domains. The singular analysis establishes compactness, the weak form inequality, and the cohomological estimate r_{m}\leq 2m+1. The closed-counting part of Corollary 2.7 gives the finite bound, while Proposition 2.6 gives the full top-rung equality space when the round count is saturated.
Thus the later sections need only verify the geometric and domain-theoretic hypotheses appropriate to their setting; the partner-spectrum algebra, the index shift, the min–max recursion, and the equality propagation will not be repeated.
Part I Rotationally symmetric case
3. Rotationally symmetric model and notation
This part deals with the rotationally symmetric model in arbitrary dimension. Its purpose is not to reduce the general theorem to a symmetric case, but to display the geometric input to the abstract ladder mechanism of Section 2 in the elementary language of separated variables.
Definition 3.1.
Let n\geq 3. A smooth metric g on S^{n-1} is called rotationally symmetric if
\displaystyle g=dr^{2}+f(r)^{2}g_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}},\qquad 0\leq r\leq a,
where g_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}} is the unit round metric. Smoothness at the two poles means that f is smooth on [0,a], positive on (0,a), and
\displaystyle f(0)=f(a)=0,\qquad f^{\prime}(0)=1,\qquad f^{\prime}(a)=-1,
together with the usual even-order compatibility conditions at the poles.
Notation 3.2.
Throughout this part,
\displaystyle g=dr^{2}+f(r)^{2}g_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}.
All Hilbert spaces, inner products, forms, and dimensions are taken over the real field. For m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}, let \mathcal{H}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1}) denote the restrictions to \mathbb{S}^{n-2} of the homogeneous harmonic polynomials of degree m on \mathbb{R}^{n-1}, and set
\displaystyle d_{n-2,m}
\displaystyle:=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{H}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1}),
\displaystyle\lambda_{m}
\displaystyle:=m(m+n-3).
Thus \lambda_{m} is the Laplace eigenvalue corresponding to the space of degree-m spherical harmonics on \mathbb{S}^{n-2}.
We define
\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{m}:=\Bigl\{u\in C^{\infty}([0,a]):\
\displaystyle u(r)=r^{m}\psi(r^{2})=(a-r)^{m}\varphi\bigl((a-r)^{2}\bigr)
\displaystyle\text{for some }\psi,\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\Bigr\}.
The radial Hilbert space is
(3.1)
\displaystyle H:=L^{2}\bigl((0,a),f^{n-2}\,dr;\mathbb{R}\bigr),\qquad\langle u,v\rangle_{H}:=\int_{0}^{a}uvf^{n-2}\,dr.
For later use, write
\displaystyle K_{\mathrm{rad}}:=-\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f},\qquad K_{\mathrm{tan}}:=\frac{1-(f^{\prime})^{2}}{f^{2}}\qquad\text{on }(0,a).
When n\geq 4, these are respectively the radial and tangential sectional curvatures. When n=3, K_{\mathrm{tan}} is the auxiliary radial quantity which occurs in the separated form comparison. The pole compatibility of f implies that both quantities extend smoothly to the poles.
Definition 3.3.
For m\geq 0, define on \mathscr{D}_{m} the densely defined nonnegative symmetric form
\displaystyle\mathfrak{l}_{m}[u,v]:=\int_{0}^{a}\left(u^{\prime}v^{\prime}+\frac{\lambda_{m}}{f^{2}}uv\right)f^{n-2}\,dr.
For m=0, the angular term is identically zero; in particular, this definition does not involve a product of zero with a potentially divergent integral.
Lemma 4.3 below shows that this form is closable in H. We denote its closure, again, by \mathfrak{l}_{m}, and \mathbf{L}_{m} denotes the nonnegative self-adjoint operator associated with the closed form \mathfrak{l}_{m}. Thus
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(\mathbf{L}_{m}):=\Bigl\{u\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{l}_{m}):\
\displaystyle\text{there exists }w\in H\text{ such that}
(3.2)
\displaystyle\mathfrak{l}_{m}[u,v]=\langle w,v\rangle_{H}\text{ for every }v\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{l}_{m})\Bigr\},
and \mathbf{L}_{m}u=w. On the smooth core its formal expression is
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{m}u:=-u^{\prime\prime}-(n-2)\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}u^{\prime}+\frac{m(m+n-3)}{f^{2}}u.
Definition 3.4.
For m\geq 0, define
(3.3)
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{m}u:=u^{\prime}-m\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}u,\qquad u\in\mathscr{D}_{m}.
We first regard this as the densely defined operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{m}:\mathscr{D}_{m}\subset H\longrightarrow H.
Its formal adjoint expression with respect to (3.1) is
(3.4)
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{m}^{\dagger}v:=-v^{\prime}-(m+n-2)\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}v.
For v\in\mathscr{D}_{m+1}, the pole conditions imply that \mathcal{B}_{m}^{\dagger}v\in H. Moreover, for u\in\mathscr{D}_{m} and v\in\mathscr{D}_{m+1}, integration by parts gives
\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{B}_{m}u,v\rangle_{H}=\langle u,\mathcal{B}_{m}^{\dagger}v\rangle_{H}.
Since \mathscr{D}_{m+1} is dense in H, \mathcal{B}_{m} is closable. We set
\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{m}:=\overline{\mathcal{B}_{m}}:\operatorname{Dom}(\mathbf{B}_{m})\subset H\longrightarrow H,
where
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(\mathbf{B}_{m})=\overline{\mathscr{D}_{m}}^{\,\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{B}_{m}}},\qquad\|u\|_{\mathbf{B}_{m}}^{2}:=\|u\|_{H}^{2}+\|\mathcal{B}_{m}u\|_{H}^{2}.
Its Hilbert-space adjoint is denoted by
\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}:\operatorname{Dom}(\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*})\subset H\longrightarrow H.
Thus v\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}) exactly when there exists w\in H such that
\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{B}_{m}u,v\rangle_{H}=\langle u,w\rangle_{H},\qquad u\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathbf{B}_{m}),
and then \mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}v=w. On \mathscr{D}_{m+1}, \mathbf{B}_{m}^{*} agrees with the expression in (3.4).
Definition 3.5.
For m\geq 0, define the closed forms
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}_{m}[u,v]
\displaystyle:=\langle\mathbf{B}_{m}u,\mathbf{B}_{m}v\rangle_{H}+m(m+n-2)\langle u,v\rangle_{H},
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{a}_{m})
\displaystyle:=\operatorname{Dom}(\mathbf{B}_{m}),
\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{m}[v,w]
\displaystyle:=\langle\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}v,\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}w\rangle_{H}+m(m+n-2)\langle v,w\rangle_{H},
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{c}_{m})
\displaystyle:=\operatorname{Dom}(\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}).
Let \mathbf{A}_{m} and \mathbf{C}_{m} be their associated self-adjoint operators. Equivalently,
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(\mathbf{A}_{m})
\displaystyle=\{u\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathbf{B}_{m}):\mathbf{B}_{m}u\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*})\},
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{m}u
\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}\mathbf{B}_{m}u+m(m+n-2)u,
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(\mathbf{C}_{m})
\displaystyle=\{v\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}):\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}v\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathbf{B}_{m})\},
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{m}v
\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}_{m}\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}v+m(m+n-2)v.
4. Frequency decomposition and the radial ladder
Theorem 4.1.
Let d\geq 2. For j\geq 0, let \mathcal{P}_{j}(\mathbb{R}^{d}) be the homogeneous polynomials of degree j, and let
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{j}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\{P\in\mathcal{P}_{j}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}P=0\}.
Every P_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{j}(\mathbb{R}^{d}) has a unique decomposition
\displaystyle P_{j}(x)=H_{j}(x)+|x|^{2}H_{j-2}(x)+\cdots+|x|^{2q}H_{j-2q}(x),\qquad q=\lfloor j/2\rfloor,
where H_{j-2\ell}\in\mathcal{H}_{j-2\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d}). Consequently,
(4.1)
\displaystyle P_{j}|_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\in\mathcal{H}_{j}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\oplus\mathcal{H}_{j-2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\oplus\cdots.
In particular, if Y\in\mathcal{H}_{m}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}), then
(4.2)
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}P_{j}(\theta)Y(\theta)\,dV_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}(\theta)=0
unless j=m+2\ell for some \ell\geq 0.
Proof.
The decomposition is the standard Fischer decomposition; see [3, Proposition 5.5 and Theorem 5.7, pp. 76–77]. Restriction to the unit sphere and orthogonality of spherical harmonics of different degrees give (4.1) and (4.2).
∎
Lemma 4.2.
Let w\in C^{\infty}(S^{n-1};\mathbb{R}), let m\geq 0, and let \varphi\in\mathcal{H}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})|_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}. Then
\displaystyle u(r):=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}w(r,\theta)\varphi(\theta)\,dV_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}\in\mathscr{D}_{m}.
Proof.
Smoothness on (0,a) follows by differentiation under the integral sign. Near r=0, use smooth normal coordinates x=r\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1} and write w(r,\theta)=W_{0}(r\theta) with W_{0} smooth. For every N,
\displaystyle W_{0}(r\theta)=\sum_{j=0}^{m+2N+1}r^{j}P_{j}(\theta)+O(r^{m+2N+2}),
where P_{j} is homogeneous of degree j. By Theorem 4.1, the integral of P_{j} against the degree-m harmonic \varphi vanishes unless j=m+2\ell. Hence
\displaystyle u(r)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}c_{\ell}r^{m+2\ell}+O(r^{m+2N+2})
for every N. Therefore r^{-m}u(r) extends smoothly across r=0 and has only even Taylor coefficients. It follows that, near r=0,
\displaystyle u(r)=r^{m}\psi_{0}(r^{2})
for a smooth function \psi_{0}.
The same argument in normal coordinates at the other pole gives
\displaystyle u(r)=(a-r)^{m}\varphi_{0}\bigl((a-r)^{2}\bigr)
near r=a. The functions r^{-m}u(r) and (a-r)^{-m}u(r), viewed respectively as functions of r^{2} and (a-r)^{2}, are smooth on their compact half-intervals and therefore admit smooth extensions to \mathbb{R}. This is exactly the definition of u\in\mathscr{D}_{m}.
∎
Lemma 4.3.
For every m\geq 0:
(1)
the form \mathfrak{l}_{m} initially defined on \mathscr{D}_{m} is closable, and, with \mathfrak{l}_{m} denoting its closure, the embedding
(4.3)
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{l}_{m})\hookrightarrow H
is compact;
(2)
\mathbf{L}_{m} is nonnegative, self-adjoint, and has compact resolvent;
(3)
every eigenfunction of \mathbf{L}_{m} belongs to \mathscr{D}_{m}.
Proof.
Fix a real L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n-2})-normalized spherical harmonic Y_{m} of degree m and define
\displaystyle J_{m}:H\longrightarrow L^{2}(S^{n-1},g;\mathbb{R}),\qquad J_{m}u(r,\theta):=u(r)Y_{m}(\theta).
Then J_{m} is an isometry and, for u,v\in\mathscr{D}_{m},
\displaystyle\langle J_{m}u,J_{m}v\rangle_{L^{2}(S^{n-1},g)}
\displaystyle=\langle u,v\rangle_{H},
(4.4)
\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}\langle\nabla(J_{m}u),\nabla(J_{m}v)\rangle_{g}\,dV_{g}
\displaystyle=\mathfrak{l}_{m}[u,v].
The pole conditions defining \mathscr{D}_{m} imply J_{m}\mathscr{D}_{m}\subset C^{\infty}(S^{n-1}). Thus
(4.5)
\displaystyle\|J_{m}u\|_{H^{1}(S^{n-1},g)}^{2}=\|u\|_{H}^{2}+\mathfrak{l}_{m}[u,u],\qquad u\in\mathscr{D}_{m}.
If u_{j}\to 0 in H and \mathfrak{l}_{m}[u_{j}-u_{k},u_{j}-u_{k}]\to 0, then J_{m}u_{j} converges in H^{1} and converges to 0 in L^{2}; hence its H^{1} limit is 0. This proves closability. After taking closures, (4.5) identifies \operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{l}_{m}) isometrically with a closed subspace of H^{1}(S^{n-1},g). Rellich compactness on the closed manifold therefore gives (4.3).
The representation theorem for closed nonnegative forms gives the nonnegative self-adjoint operator \mathbf{L}_{m} characterized by (3.2); see [26, Proposition 3.1.7, p. 25]. The compactness of the form-domain embedding implies that (I+\mathbf{L}_{m})^{-1} is compact.
It remains to prove the last assertion. Let \mathbf{L}_{m}u=\lambda u. For \Phi\in C^{\infty}(S^{n-1}), set
\displaystyle v_{\Phi}(r):=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}\Phi(r,\theta)Y_{m}(\theta)\,d\theta.
Lemma 4.2 gives v_{\Phi}\in\mathscr{D}_{m}. Approximating u by elements of \mathscr{D}_{m} in the form norm and using (4.4), we obtain
\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}\langle\nabla(J_{m}u),\nabla\Phi\rangle_{g}\,dV_{g}=\mathfrak{l}_{m}[u,v_{\Phi}]=\lambda\langle u,v_{\Phi}\rangle_{H}=\lambda\int_{S^{n-1}}J_{m}u\,\Phi\,dV_{g}.
Thus J_{m}u is a weak eigenfunction of -\Delta_{g}. Elliptic regularity gives
\displaystyle J_{m}u\in C^{\infty}(S^{n-1});
see [5, Theorem 44, p. 68]. Projecting onto Y_{m} and applying Lemma 4.2 yields u\in\mathscr{D}_{m}.
∎
Lemma 4.4.
For every \Lambda\in\mathbb{R},
\displaystyle N_{-\Delta_{g}}^{<}(\Lambda)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}d_{n-2,m}N_{\mathbf{L}_{m}}^{<}(\Lambda).
The same identity holds with N^{<} replaced by the closed counting function \mathcal{N}.
Proof.
For each m, choose a real orthonormal basis of \mathcal{H}_{m}(\mathbb{S}^{n-2}), denoted by
\displaystyle\{\varphi_{m,\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{d_{n-2,m}}.
The spherical-harmonic decomposition
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n-2};\mathbb{R})=\widehat{\bigoplus}_{m=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{m}(\mathbb{S}^{n-2})
induces the unitary decomposition
\displaystyle L^{2}(S^{n-1},g;\mathbb{R})\cong\widehat{\bigoplus}_{m=0}^{\infty}\widehat{\bigoplus}_{\alpha=1}^{d_{n-2,m}}H,\qquad\{u_{m,\alpha}\}\longmapsto\sum_{m,\alpha}u_{m,\alpha}(r)\varphi_{m,\alpha}(\theta).
For a finite family with u_{m,\alpha}\in\mathscr{D}_{m}, orthogonality and integration by parts on \mathbb{S}^{n-2} give
\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}|\nabla w|_{g}^{2}\,dV_{g}=\sum_{m,\alpha}\mathfrak{l}_{m}[u_{m,\alpha},u_{m,\alpha}].
Conversely, Lemma 4.2 shows that the angular coefficients of every smooth function lie in the corresponding \mathscr{D}_{m}. Finite spherical-harmonic truncations of smooth functions converge in H^{1}, so closure of the preceding identity gives an orthogonal direct-sum decomposition of the Dirichlet form. Hence
\displaystyle-\Delta_{g}\cong\widehat{\bigoplus}_{m=0}^{\infty}\widehat{\bigoplus}_{\alpha=1}^{d_{n-2,m}}\mathbf{L}_{m}.
The strict and closed counting identities follow, including multiplicities. The sums are finite at each fixed threshold because
\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{m}\geq\frac{m(m+n-3)}{\max_{[0,a]}f^{2}}\,I,
so only finitely many angular degrees can contribute below a prescribed number.
∎
Lemma 4.5.
For every m\geq 0,
(4.6)
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{m}\mathscr{D}_{m}\subset\mathscr{D}_{m+1}.
Proof.
Near r=0, smoothness of the warping function gives
\displaystyle f(r)=rF(r^{2}),\qquad\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}=\frac{1}{r}+rH_{0}(r^{2}),
with F smooth and positive and H_{0} smooth. If u(r)=r^{m}\psi(r^{2}), then
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{m}u=r^{m+1}\bigl(2\psi^{\prime}(r^{2})-mH_{0}(r^{2})\psi(r^{2})\bigr).
The analogous calculation at r=a gives the required (a-r)^{m+1} expansion. Hence (4.6) holds.
∎
Lemma 4.6.
For every m\geq 0,
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*})=\overline{\mathscr{D}_{m+1}}^{\,\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}}},\qquad\|v\|_{\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}}^{2}:=\|v\|_{H}^{2}+\|\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}v\|_{H}^{2}.
Proof.
Let v\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}) and set w=\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}v. On compact subintervals of (0,a),
\displaystyle-v^{\prime}-(m+n-2)\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}v=w
holds distributionally, so v\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,a) and
(4.7)
\displaystyle\bigl(f^{m+n-2}v\bigr)^{\prime}=-f^{m+n-2}w.
The integration constant at either endpoint is zero: otherwise v\sim cf^{-(m+n-2)}, which is not in L^{2}((0,a),f^{n-2}dr) for n\geq 3.
Near r=0, (4.7) and Cauchy–Schwarz imply
(4.8)
\displaystyle\int_{\varepsilon}^{2\varepsilon}|v|^{2}f^{n-2}\,dr=o(\varepsilon^{2})\qquad\text{as }\varepsilon\downarrow 0.
The same estimate holds near r=a. Choose a cutoff \chi_{\varepsilon} which vanishes in the two \varepsilon-neighborhoods of the endpoints, equals one outside the two 2\varepsilon-neighborhoods, and satisfies |\chi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}|\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}. Then \chi_{\varepsilon}v\to v in H and, by (4.8),
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{m}^{\dagger}(\chi_{\varepsilon}v)=\chi_{\varepsilon}w-\chi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}v\longrightarrow w\qquad\text{in }H.
After cutting off, ordinary mollification on compact subintervals produces a sequence in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,a)\subset\mathscr{D}_{m+1} converging to v in the graph norm of \mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}.
∎
Lemma 4.7.
For every m\geq 0,
(4.9)
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(\mathbf{B}_{m})=\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{l}_{m}).
Moreover, the graph norm of \mathcal{B}_{m} and the \mathfrak{l}_{m}-form norm are equivalent on \mathscr{D}_{m}.
Proof.
For u\in\mathscr{D}_{m}, expanding the square and integrating the mixed term by parts gives
(4.10)
\displaystyle\|\mathcal{B}_{m}u\|_{H}^{2}-\mathfrak{l}_{m}[u,u]=m\int_{0}^{a}u^{2}f^{n-2}\left[\frac{(m+n-3)((f^{\prime})^{2}-1)+ff^{\prime\prime}}{f^{2}}\right]dr.
The pole compatibility conditions make the coefficient in brackets smooth at both endpoints: its numerator is O(f^{2}). Hence the right-hand side of (4.10) is bounded in absolute value by a constant times \|u\|_{H}^{2}. Therefore
\displaystyle\|u\|_{H}^{2}+\|\mathcal{B}_{m}u\|_{H}^{2}\asymp\|u\|_{H}^{2}+\mathfrak{l}_{m}[u,u]\qquad\text{on }\mathscr{D}_{m}.
The two completions of \mathscr{D}_{m} inside H consequently coincide, proving (4.9).
∎
Lemma 4.8 (Radial ladder identity).
For every m\geq 0,
(4.11)
\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{0}
\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{B}_{0}\qquad\text{on }\mathscr{D}_{0},
(4.12)
\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{m}\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}-\mathbf{B}_{m+1}^{*}\mathbf{B}_{m+1}
\displaystyle=(2m+n-1)K_{\mathrm{rad}}\qquad\text{on }\mathscr{D}_{m+1}.
Proof.
Using (3.3) and (3.4), one obtains on the indicated cores
\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}\mathbf{B}_{m}
\displaystyle=-\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}-(n-2)\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}\frac{d}{dr}+m(m+n-3)\frac{(f^{\prime})^{2}}{f^{2}}+m\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f},
\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{m}\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}
\displaystyle=-\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}-(n-2)\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}\frac{d}{dr}-(m+n-2)\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f}+(m+1)(m+n-2)\frac{(f^{\prime})^{2}}{f^{2}}.
The first formula with m=0 gives (4.11); subtraction gives (4.12).
∎
5. Radial curvature and application of the abstract recursion
Lemma 5.1.
Assume \Rc_{g}\geq(n-2)g. Then, on (0,a),
(5.1)
\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}+f
\displaystyle\leq 0,
(5.2)
\displaystyle f^{2}+(f^{\prime})^{2}
\displaystyle\leq 1.
Consequently,
\displaystyle K_{\mathrm{rad}}\geq 1,\qquad K_{\mathrm{tan}}\geq 1.
Moreover, K_{\mathrm{rad}}\equiv 1 if and only if (S^{n-1},g) is the unit round sphere.
Proof.
For a unit vector X tangent to a principal orbit,
\displaystyle\Rc_{g}(\partial_{r},\partial_{r})
\displaystyle=(n-2)K_{\mathrm{rad}},
\displaystyle\Rc_{g}(X,X)
\displaystyle=K_{\mathrm{rad}}+(n-3)K_{\mathrm{tan}}.
The radial component of the Ricci lower bound gives K_{\mathrm{rad}}\geq 1, equivalently (5.1).
Since f^{\prime\prime}\leq-f<0, f^{\prime} decreases from 1 to -1 and vanishes at a unique point. For
\displaystyle E(r):=f(r)^{2}+f^{\prime}(r)^{2},
one has
\displaystyle E^{\prime}(r)=2f^{\prime}(r)(f(r)+f^{\prime\prime}(r)).
Thus E decreases before the zero of f^{\prime} and increases after it. Since E(0)=E(a)=1, one obtains E\leq 1, which is (5.2) and gives K_{\mathrm{tan}}\geq 1.
If K_{\mathrm{rad}}\equiv 1, then f^{\prime\prime}+f=0, f(0)=0, and f^{\prime}(0)=1, so f(r)=\sin r. The conditions at the second pole force a=\pi. The converse is immediate.
∎
Lemma 5.2.
For every m\geq 0,
(5.3)
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{a}_{m})
\displaystyle=\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{l}_{m}),
(5.4)
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{c}_{m})
\displaystyle=\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{a}_{m+1})=\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{l}_{m+1}).
On this common domain,
(5.5)
\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{m}[v,v]-\mathfrak{a}_{m+1}[v,v]=(2m+n-1)\int_{0}^{a}(K_{\mathrm{rad}}-1)v^{2}f^{n-2}\,dr.
The common form domains in (5.3) and (5.4) embed compactly into H when equipped with any of the indicated form norms. Consequently, the operators \mathbf{A}_{m} and \mathbf{C}_{m} have compact resolvent. In addition, every eigenfunction of \mathbf{C}_{m} belongs to \mathscr{D}_{m+1}.
Proof.
The first domain identity is Lemma 4.7. On \mathscr{D}_{m+1}, the radial ladder identity (4.12), together with
\displaystyle(m+1)(m+n-1)-m(m+n-2)=2m+n-1,
gives (5.5). Since K_{\mathrm{rad}} is bounded on [0,a], the \mathfrak{c}_{m}- and \mathfrak{a}_{m+1}-form norms are equivalent on \mathscr{D}_{m+1}. Lemma 4.6 identifies the first completion with \operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{c}_{m}), while the definition of \mathbf{B}_{m+1} identifies the second with \operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{a}_{m+1}). This proves (5.4) and extends (5.5) to the closed domains.
By Lemma 4.7 and Definition 3.5, the \mathfrak{a}_{m}-form norm is equivalent to the \mathfrak{l}_{m}-form norm on their common domain. The bounded-potential identity (5.5) likewise makes the \mathfrak{c}_{m}- and \mathfrak{a}_{m+1}-form norms equivalent. Therefore (4.3), applied at levels m and m+1, gives the asserted compact embeddings. The standard closed-form resolvent argument then gives compact resolvent for both \mathbf{A}_{m} and \mathbf{C}_{m}.
For the regularity assertion, polarizing the calculation in Lemma 4.7 gives
(5.6)
\displaystyle\mathfrak{l}_{m+1}[v,\phi]-\mathfrak{c}_{m}[v,\phi]=(m+n-2)\int_{0}^{a}\bigl((m+1)(K_{\mathrm{tan}}-1)-(K_{\mathrm{rad}}-1)\bigr)v\phi f^{n-2}\,dr.
The coefficient is smooth and bounded on [0,a]. If \mathbf{C}_{m}v=\gamma v, then (5.6) shows that v\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathbf{L}_{m+1}) and that J_{m+1}v satisfies a second-order elliptic equation with smooth coefficients on S^{n-1}. Elliptic regularity and Lemma 4.2 give v\in\mathscr{D}_{m+1}.
∎
Lemma 5.3 (Comparison with the separated Laplacian).
Assume \Rc_{g}\geq(n-2)g. Then
\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{m}\geq\mathbf{A}_{m}
in the quadratic-form sense. Consequently, for every j\geq 0 and every \Lambda\in\mathbb{R},
(5.7)
\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{L}_{m})
\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{A}_{m}),
(5.8)
\displaystyle N_{\mathbf{L}_{m}}^{<}(\Lambda)
\displaystyle\leq N_{\mathbf{A}_{m}}^{<}(\Lambda).
Proof.
By Lemma 4.7, equivalently by (5.3), the closed forms \mathfrak{l}_{m} and \mathfrak{a}_{m} have the same domain. For u\in\mathscr{D}_{m}, direct calculation gives
(5.9)
\displaystyle\mathfrak{l}_{m}[u,u]-\mathfrak{a}_{m}[u,u]=m\int_{0}^{a}\bigl((m+n-3)(K_{\mathrm{tan}}-1)+(K_{\mathrm{rad}}-1)\bigr)u^{2}f^{n-2}\,dr.
The coefficient is bounded, so this identity extends to every
u\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{l}_{m})=\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{a}_{m}); it also shows that the two form norms on the common domain are equivalent. Lemma 5.1 makes the right-hand side nonnegative. The min–max principle for the self-adjoint operators associated with \mathfrak{l}_{m} and \mathfrak{a}_{m}, namely \mathbf{L}_{m} and \mathbf{A}_{m}, gives (5.7), and the counting inequality is equivalent to it.
∎
Lemma 5.4.
Assume \Rc_{g}\geq(n-2)g. Then, for every m\geq 0,
\displaystyle\ker\mathbf{B}_{m}=\mathbb{R}f^{m},\qquad\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\ker\mathbf{B}_{m}=1,\qquad\ker\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}=\{0\}.
In particular,
(5.10)
\displaystyle\lambda_{0}(\mathbf{A}_{m})=m(m+n-2),
and this eigenvalue is simple.
Proof.
If u\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathbf{B}_{m}) and \mathbf{B}_{m}u=0, then on (0,a)
\displaystyle u^{\prime}=m\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}u
in the distributional, hence classical, sense. Thus (f^{-m}u)^{\prime}=0, so u=cf^{m}. Conversely, the pole expansions of f show that f^{m}\in\mathscr{D}_{m}, and (3.3) gives \mathbf{B}_{m}f^{m}=0.
It remains to prove the adjoint-kernel assertion. Apply Lemma 2.3 to the following concrete realization of Notation 2.1:
\displaystyle H_{m}=H_{m+1}=H,\qquad B_{m}=\mathbf{B}_{m},
\displaystyle\alpha_{m}=m(m+n-2),\qquad\alpha_{m+1}=(m+1)(m+n-1).
The required domain inclusion and form inequality (2.3) are precisely (5.4) and (5.5), together with K_{\mathrm{rad}}\geq 1 from Lemma 5.1. Since \alpha_{m+1}-\alpha_{m}=2m+n-1>0, Lemma 2.3 yields \ker\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}=\{0\}. Formula (5.10) follows from the definition of \mathbf{A}_{m}.
∎
The preceding lemmas verify all hypotheses needed to use the separated radial result from Section 2. Explicitly, for every ladder level m we take
(5.11)
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R},\qquad H_{m}=H,\qquad B_{m}=\mathbf{B}_{m},\qquad\alpha_{m}=m(m+n-2).
The operators are closed and densely defined by Definition 3.4; the partner operators have compact resolvent by Lemma 5.2; their kernel dimensions are r_{m}=1 by Lemma 5.4; and the consecutive form order (2.3) follows from (5.4), (5.5), and Lemma 5.1. Thus the partner-spectrum algebra and the counting iteration need not be reproved in the radial setting.
Proposition 5.5.
Assume \Rc_{g}\geq(n-2)g. For every l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},
\displaystyle N_{\mathbf{L}_{m}}^{<}\bigl(l(l+n-2)\bigr)\leq l-m,\qquad 0\leq m<l,
\displaystyle N_{\mathbf{L}_{m}}^{<}\bigl(l(l+n-2)\bigr)=0,\qquad m\geq l.
Proof.
Apply Proposition 2.5 to the Hilbert spaces, operators, and shifts in (5.11), note r_{m}=1. The verification of its hypotheses was given immediately above; we get
\displaystyle N_{\mathbf{A}_{m}}^{<}\bigl(l(l+n-2)\bigr)\leq l-m\qquad(0\leq m<l),
and gives zero for m\geq l. The comparison (5.8) transfers these bounds from \mathbf{A}_{m} to \mathbf{L}_{m}.
∎
6. The rotational comparison theorem and rigidity
Lemma 6.1.
For every l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},
\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{l-1}d_{n-2,m}(l-m)=N_{-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}}^{<}\bigl(l(l+n-2)\bigr).
Proof.
The standard dimension formula is
(6.1)
\displaystyle d_{n-2,m}=\binom{n+m-2}{n-2}-\binom{n+m-4}{n-2},
with the convention that the binomial coefficient vanishes when the upper index is smaller than n-2. The branching identity
\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{m}d_{n-2,s}=d_{n-1,m}
follows by summing (6.1). Therefore
\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{l-1}d_{n-2,m}(l-m)
\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}\sum_{m=0}^{k}d_{n-2,m}=\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}d_{n-1,k}=N_{-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}}^{<}\bigl(l(l+n-2)\bigr).
∎
Proposition 6.2.
Let n\geq 3, and let (S^{n-1},g) be rotationally symmetric with \Rc_{g}\geq(n-2)g. Then, for every l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},
\displaystyle N_{-\Delta_{g}}^{<}\bigl(l(l+n-2)\bigr)\leq N_{-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}}^{<}\bigl(l(l+n-2)\bigr).
Proof.
By Lemma 4.4 and Proposition 5.5,
\displaystyle N_{-\Delta_{g}}^{<}\bigl(l(l+n-2)\bigr)
\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}d_{n-2,m}N_{\mathbf{L}_{m}}^{<}\bigl(l(l+n-2)\bigr)\leq\sum_{m=0}^{l-1}d_{n-2,m}(l-m).
Lemma 6.1 identifies the last expression with the round strict counting function.
∎
Lemma 6.3.
Let \mathfrak{s} and \mathfrak{t} be lower-bounded closed forms in the radial Hilbert space H defined in (3.1), with common form domain \mathcal{V}:=\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{s})=\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{t}).
Let S and T be the self-adjoint operators associated with \mathfrak{s} and \mathfrak{t}, respectively. Suppose that
\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}[v,v]=\mathfrak{t}[v,v]+\int_{0}^{a}P|v|^{2}f^{n-2},\qquad v\in\mathcal{V},
for a bounded function P\geq 0. Assume that the inclusion \mathcal{V}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{H} is compact and that P is positive on a nonempty open set U\subseteq(0,a). Assume also that, for every j\geq 0, no nonzero element of the span of the first j+1 eigenfunctions of S vanishes identically on U. Then
(6.2)
\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(S)>\lambda_{j}(T),\qquad j=0,1,2,\ldots.
Proof.
Let F_{j} be the span of the first j+1 eigenfunctions of S, counted with multiplicity. For every 0\neq v\in F_{j},
\displaystyle\frac{\mathfrak{s}[v,v]}{\|v\|^{2}}\leq\lambda_{j}(S),
and the spectral-subspace hypothesis gives\int_{0}^{a}P|v|^{2}f^{n-2}>0.
Hence
\displaystyle\frac{\mathfrak{t}[v,v]}{\|v\|^{2}}<\lambda_{j}(S)\qquad(0\neq v\in F_{j}).
The unit sphere of F_{j} is compact, so the supremum of the left-hand side over F_{j}\setminus\{0\} is still strictly smaller than \lambda_{j}(S). The min–max principle for T gives (6.2).
∎
In the radial applications below, the spectral-subspace nonvanishing hypothesis follows from Lemmas 4.3 and 5.2. Indeed, after decomposing a finite spectral sum into its distinct eigenvalue components by polynomial spectral projections, each component satisfies a second-order ordinary differential equation with smooth coefficients on (0,a); vanishing on an open interval therefore forces that component to vanish identically. The same argument, with elliptic unique continuation, applies to the smooth bundle operators used later in the paper.
Lemma 6.4.
Assume that (S^{n-1},g) is rotationally symmetric, \Rc_{g}\geq(n-2)g, and g is not round. Then
(6.3)
\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{L}_{m})>(m+j)(m+j+n-2)\qquad\text{whenever }(m,j)\neq(0,0).
Proof.
Step (1). By Lemma 5.1, nonroundness implies that there is a nonempty open interval U\subset(0,a) on which
\displaystyle K_{\mathrm{rad}}>1.
We first compare the two consecutive partner operators. Apply Lemma 6.3 in the Hilbert space H with
\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}=\mathfrak{c}_{m},\qquad\mathfrak{t}=\mathfrak{a}_{m+1},\qquad S=\mathbf{C}_{m},\qquad T=\mathbf{A}_{m+1}.
The operators \mathbf{C}_{m} and \mathbf{A}_{m+1} are, by Definition 3.5, the self-adjoint operators associated with \mathfrak{c}_{m} and \mathfrak{a}_{m+1}, respectively. By (5.4), their forms have the common domain
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{c}_{m})=\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{a}_{m+1})=\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{l}_{m+1}).
The identity (5.5) holds on the common closed form domain and has the bounded potential
\displaystyle P=(2m+n-1)(K_{\mathrm{rad}}-1).
Hence (4.3), applied at level m+1, shows that the common form domain embeds compactly into H. The displayed potential P is nonnegative everywhere and positive on U.
Finally, the regularity assertion in Lemma 5.2, followed by the ordinary-differential-equation argument stated after Lemma 6.3, verifies that no nonzero element of a finite \mathbf{C}_{m}-spectral subspace can vanish identically on U.
Thus all hypotheses of Lemma 6.3 are satisfied, and
(6.4)
\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{C}_{m})>\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{A}_{m+1}),\qquad m,j\geq 0.
We next use the exact partner shift from Section 2. Apply Lemma 2.2 with H_{m}=H, B_{m}=\mathbf{B}_{m}, and \alpha_{m}=m(m+n-2). Lemma 5.4 gives r_{m}=1 and s_{m}=0, so (2.1) becomes
\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{A}_{m})=\lambda_{j-1}(\mathbf{C}_{m}),\qquad j\geq 1.
Combining this with (6.4) and iterating yields
\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{A}_{m})
\displaystyle>\lambda_{j-1}(\mathbf{A}_{m+1})>\cdots>\lambda_{0}(\mathbf{A}_{m+j})
(6.5)
\displaystyle=(m+j)(m+j+n-2),\qquad j\geq 1,
where the last equality is (5.10).
Step (2). For m=0, (5.3) and (5.9) show that the closed forms \mathfrak{l}_{0} and \mathfrak{a}_{0} coincide. Since \mathbf{L}_{0} and \mathbf{A}_{0} are their associated self-adjoint operators, respectively, one has \mathbf{L}_{0}=\mathbf{A}_{0}, and (6.5) proves the assertion for j\geq 1.
Step (3). Assume now that m\geq 1. We again apply Lemma 6.3 in H, this time with
\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}=\mathfrak{l}_{m},\qquad\mathfrak{t}=\mathfrak{a}_{m},\qquad S=\mathbf{L}_{m},\qquad T=\mathbf{A}_{m}.
By Definition 3.3, \mathbf{L}_{m} is the self-adjoint operator associated with \mathfrak{l}_{m}, while Definition 3.5 identifies \mathbf{A}_{m} as the self-adjoint operator associated with \mathfrak{a}_{m}. The form-domain identity (5.3) gives
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{l}_{m})=\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{a}_{m}),
On this common closed form domain, (5.9) reads
\displaystyle\mathfrak{l}_{m}[u,u]
\displaystyle=\mathfrak{a}_{m}[u,u]
\displaystyle\quad+m\int_{0}^{a}\bigl((m+n-3)(K_{\mathrm{tan}}-1)+(K_{\mathrm{rad}}-1)\bigr)u^{2}f^{n-2}\,dr.
The coefficient is bounded, so the \mathfrak{l}_{m}- and \mathfrak{a}_{m}-form norms are equivalent.
Consequently, (4.3), applied at level m, shows that their common form domain embeds compactly into H. Lemma 5.1 makes the potential nonnegative, and, because m\geq 1, it is strictly positive on U.
By Lemma 4.3 and the ordinary-differential-equation argument stated after Lemma 6.3, no nonzero element of a finite \mathbf{L}_{m}-spectral subspace vanishes identically on U.
Hence Lemma 6.3 gives
\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{L}_{m})>\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{A}_{m}),\qquad j\geq 0.
Together with (5.10) and (6.5), this proves (6.3) in all cases except (m,j)=(0,0), where both sides are zero.
∎
Theorem 6.5.
Let n\geq 3, and let (S^{n-1},g) be a smooth rotationally symmetric Riemannian manifold with \Rc_{g}\geq(n-2)g. Then
(6.6)
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(S^{n-1},g)\geq\lambda_{i}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1},g_{\mathrm{round}}),\qquad i\geq 1.
Consequently,
(6.7)
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{g}}\bigl(l(l+n-2)\bigr)\leq\mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}}\bigl(l(l+n-2)\bigr),\qquad l\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.
Moreover, if equality holds in (6.6) for some i\geq 1, or if equality holds in (6.7) for some l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}, then (S^{n-1},g) is isometric to the unit round sphere.
Proof.
Step (1). Fix i\geq 1, and choose the unique l\geq 1 such that the i-th round eigenvalue is l(l+n-2). Equivalently,
\displaystyle N_{-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}}^{<}\bigl(l(l+n-2)\bigr)\leq i<N_{-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}}^{<}\bigl((l+1)(l+n-1)\bigr).
Proposition 6.2 gives
\displaystyle\lambda_{N_{-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}}^{<}(l(l+n-2))}(S^{n-1},g)\geq l(l+n-2).
Since the eigenvalues are nondecreasing and i is not smaller than this index,
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(S^{n-1},g)\geq l(l+n-2)=\lambda_{i}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1},g_{\mathrm{round}}).
This proves (6.6). The closed-counting comparison (6.7) follows immediately; for l=0, both connected manifolds have exactly one eigenvalue at zero.
Step (2). For rigidity, suppose that g is not round. If
\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{L}_{m})\leq l(l+n-2),
then Lemma 6.4 implies m+j\leq l-1. Hence Lemmas 4.4 and 6.1 give, for every l\geq 1,
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{g}}\bigl(l(l+n-2)\bigr)
\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}d_{n-2,m}\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{L}_{m}}\bigl(l(l+n-2)\bigr)\leq\sum_{m=0}^{l-1}d_{n-2,m}(l-m)
(6.8)
\displaystyle=N_{-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}}^{<}\bigl(l(l+n-2)\bigr)
\displaystyle<\mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}}\bigl(l(l+n-2)\bigr).
Thus equality in (6.7) is impossible for a nonround metric.
Step (3). The same estimate also makes every positive ordered comparison strict. Indeed, if the i-th round eigenvalue equals l(l+n-2), then
\displaystyle i\geq N_{-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}}^{<}\bigl(l(l+n-2)\bigr),
whereas (6.8) shows that at most that many eigenvalues of -\Delta_{g} are less than or equal to l(l+n-2). Therefore
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(S^{n-1},g)>l(l+n-2)=\lambda_{i}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1},g_{\mathrm{round}}).
Equality in (6.6) is likewise impossible unless g is round.
∎
Part II Smooth Riemannian 2-Spheres with K\geq 1
Throughout this part, m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}. The basic objects are the
line bundles E_{m}=(T^{1,0}S^{2})^{\otimes m}, the Hilbert spaces
H_{m}=L^{2}(E_{m}), and the closed first-order operators
B_{m}:H_{m}\supset\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m})\to H_{m+1}. They are constructed
in Section 7. The shifted forms
\mathfrak{a}_{m},\mathfrak{c}_{m} and their operators A_{m},C_{m} are fixed at
the beginning of Section 9.
In this part, we always assume that (S^{2},g) is a smooth Riemannian sphere satisfying K_{g}\geq 1 unless otherwise mentioned.
7. Riemannian ladder identity
The purpose of this section is to remove the artificial symmetry from Section 3 for S^{2}. In the rotational case, m measured angular frequency. For a general metric there is no distinguished angular coordinate, but there is still a canonical rotation group acting on oriented orthonormal frames.
Fix the standard orientation on S^{2}. The metric and the orientation define the bundle map J:TS^{2}\longrightarrow TS^{2} by the rule that, for every local oriented orthonormal frame e_{1},e_{2} with respect to g,
\displaystyle Je_{1}=e_{2},\qquad Je_{2}=-e_{1}.
Thus J^{2}=-\Id. Let
\displaystyle T_{\mathbb{C}}S^{2}:=TS^{2}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}.
Since S^{2} is oriented, let dA_{g} denote the Riemannian area form of g.
Definition 7.1.
Extend \mathbb{C}-linearly J:TS^{2}\longrightarrow TS^{2} to T_{\mathbb{C}}S^{2}, we define the +i-eigenbundle of J by
\displaystyle T^{1,0}S^{2}:=\ker(J-i\Id)=\{W\in T_{\mathbb{C}}S^{2}:JW=iW\}.
For each integer m\geq 0, define
\displaystyle E_{m}:=(T^{1,0}S^{2})^{\otimes m},\qquad E_{0}:=S^{2}\times\mathbb{C}.
The replacement of the m-th Fourier mode is realized geometrically as the line bundle E_{m}=(T^{1,0}S^{2})^{\otimes m}. This is the first conceptual upgrade in the paper.
In a local oriented orthonormal frame, set
\displaystyle Z:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e_{1}-ie_{2}).
If another local oriented orthonormal frame is written as
\displaystyle e_{1}^{\prime}=\cos\alpha\,e_{1}+\sin\alpha\,e_{2},\qquad e_{2}^{\prime}=-\sin\alpha\,e_{1}+\cos\alpha\,e_{2},
then
(7.1)
\displaystyle Z^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e_{1}^{\prime}-ie_{2}^{\prime})=e^{i\alpha}Z.
In an oriented orthonormal frame, set
\displaystyle Z^{\otimes 0}:=1,\qquad Z^{\otimes m}:=Z\otimes\cdots\otimes Z\quad\text{with }m\text{ tensor factors, for }m\geq 1.
Under the frame change (7.1), the local tensors satisfy
\displaystyle(Z^{\prime})^{\otimes m}=e^{im\alpha}Z^{\otimes m}.
Thus a local section s of E_{m} is written as s=u\cdot Z^{\otimes m}, where u is a complex-valued function, and on an overlap the coefficient changes by
\displaystyle u^{\prime}=e^{-im\alpha}u.
This gives a globally defined rank-one complex vector bundle.
The Hermitian metric h_{m} on E_{m} is defined by declaring each frame Z^{\otimes m} to be unit:
\displaystyle|u\cdot Z^{\otimes m}|^{2}=|u|^{2}.
Since |e^{im\alpha}|=1, this definition is independent of the chosen oriented orthonormal frame.
Notation 7.2.
Let \pi_{m}:E_{m}\to S^{2} denote the bundle projection. We use the following spaces of sections:
\displaystyle C^{\infty}(E_{m})
\displaystyle:=\{s:S^{2}\to E_{m}:\ s\text{ is smooth and }\pi_{m}\circ s=\operatorname{id}_{S^{2}}\},
\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathrm{meas}}(E_{m})
\displaystyle:=\{s:S^{2}\to E_{m}:\ s\text{ is Borel measurable and }\pi_{m}\circ s=\operatorname{id}_{S^{2}}\ \text{a.e.}\}/\sim,
where \sim means equality dA_{g}-almost everywhere. The Hilbert space L^{2}(E_{m}) is
\displaystyle L^{2}(E_{m}):=\left\{s\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{meas}}(E_{m}):\int_{S^{2}}|s|_{h_{m}}^{2}\,dA_{g}<\infty\right\}.
Its Hermitian inner product is
\displaystyle\langle s,t\rangle_{L^{2}(E_{m})}:=\int_{S^{2}}h_{m}(s,t)\,dA_{g}.
We use the convention that the inner product is linear in the first variable and conjugate-linear in the second variable. Since S^{2} is compact and E_{m} is smooth, C^{\infty}(E_{m}) is dense in L^{2}(E_{m}).
Let \omega be the Levi-Civita connection one-form in the oriented orthonormal frame e_{1},e_{2}, defined by
(7.2)
\displaystyle\nabla e_{1}=\omega\otimes e_{2},\qquad\nabla e_{2}=-\omega\otimes e_{1}.
Then \nabla Z=i\omega\otimes Z.
With the sign convention in (7.2), the curvature equation is
\displaystyle d\omega=-K_{g}\,dA_{g}.
For instance, on the unit round sphere \mathbb{S}^{2} with e_{1}=\partial_{r} and e_{2}=(\sin r)^{-1}\partial_{\theta}, one has \omega=\cos r\,d\theta, hence d\omega=-\sin r\,dr\wedge d\theta=-dA.
The connection induced on E_{m} is defined as follows:
\displaystyle\nabla^{(m)}(u\cdot Z^{\otimes m})=(du+im\omega u)\otimes Z^{\otimes m}.
For k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}, define H^{k}(E_{m}) to be the Sobolev space of sections obtained by completing C^{\infty}(E_{m}) for the norm
\displaystyle\|s\|_{H^{k}(E_{m})}^{2}:=\sum_{r=0}^{k}\int_{S^{2}}\left|\big(\nabla^{(m)}\big)^{r}s\right|^{2}\,dA_{g}.
In particular, H^{0}(E_{m})=L^{2}(E_{m}).
Notation 7.3.
For a vector field X on (S^{2},g) and any complex-valued function u defined on (S^{2},g), define
\displaystyle D_{X}^{(m)}u:=Xu+im\omega(X)u,\quad\quad\forall m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.
Definition 7.4.
The first-order differential expression is
(7.3)
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{m}:C^{\infty}(E_{m})\longrightarrow C^{\infty}(E_{m+1}),\quad\quad\mathcal{B}_{m}(u\cdot Z^{\otimes m}):=\bigl(D_{e_{1}}^{(m)}u+iD_{e_{2}}^{(m)}u\bigr)Z^{\otimes(m+1)}.
It defines an initial densely defined operator:
\displaystyle B_{m}^{(0)}:\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m}^{(0)})=C^{\infty}(E_{m})\subset L^{2}(E_{m})\longrightarrow L^{2}(E_{m+1}),\qquad B_{m}^{(0)}s:=\mathcal{B}_{m}s.
The operator B_{m}^{(0)} is closable because its formal adjoint is also a first-order differential operator defined on the dense subspace C^{\infty}(E_{m+1}). We write
\displaystyle B_{m}:=\overline{B_{m}^{(0)}}:\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m})\subset L^{2}(E_{m})\longrightarrow L^{2}(E_{m+1})
for its closure.
Standard elliptic theory identifies \operatorname{Dom}(B_{m}) with H^{1}(E_{m}). Thus
\displaystyle B_{m}:\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m})=H^{1}(E_{m})\subset L^{2}(E_{m})\longrightarrow L^{2}(E_{m+1}).
It is a closed unbounded operator in the Hilbert-space sense; it is not an everywhere-defined map on L^{2}(E_{m}). When acting on smooth sections, we keep the notation B_{m} for the differential expression \mathcal{B}_{m}.
This is a global operator. Indeed, under the frame change (7.1), the connection form changes by
\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}=\omega+d\alpha.
Using u^{\prime}=e^{-im\alpha}u, e_{1}^{\prime}=\cos\alpha\,e_{1}+\sin\alpha\,e_{2}, and e_{2}^{\prime}=-\sin\alpha\,e_{1}+\cos\alpha\,e_{2}, one obtains
\displaystyle D_{e_{1}^{\prime}}^{\prime(m)}u^{\prime}+iD_{e_{2}^{\prime}}^{\prime(m)}u^{\prime}=e^{-i(m+1)\alpha}\bigl(D_{e_{1}}^{(m)}u+iD_{e_{2}}^{(m)}u\bigr).
Multiplying by (Z^{\prime})^{\otimes(m+1)}=e^{i(m+1)\alpha}Z^{\otimes(m+1)} gives the same section of E_{m+1}.
Definition 7.5.
The Hilbert-space adjoint of the closed densely defined operator
\displaystyle B_{m}:\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m})=H^{1}(E_{m})\subset L^{2}(E_{m})\longrightarrow L^{2}(E_{m+1})
is denoted by B_{m}^{*}. Thus
\displaystyle B_{m}^{*}:\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m}^{*})\subset L^{2}(E_{m+1})\longrightarrow L^{2}(E_{m})
is the closed densely defined operator characterized by
\displaystyle\langle B_{m}s,t\rangle_{L^{2}(E_{m+1})}=\langle s,B_{m}^{*}t\rangle_{L^{2}(E_{m})},\qquad s\in\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m}),\quad t\in\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m}^{*}).
On smooth sections it agrees with the formal L^{2}-adjoint. More precisely, C^{\infty}(E_{m+1})\subset\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m}^{*}), and if t=vZ^{\otimes(m+1)}, then
(7.4)
\displaystyle B_{m}^{*}(vZ^{\otimes(m+1)})=-\bigl(D_{e_{1}}^{(m+1)}v-iD_{e_{2}}^{(m+1)}v\bigr)Z^{\otimes m}.
Again \operatorname{Dom}(B_{m}^{*})=H^{1}(E_{m+1}) by elliptic theory on the closed surface.
Remark 7.6 (The rotationally symmetric ladder as a symmetry reduction).
The intrinsic construction above contains the rotationally symmetric
two-sphere as a special case. More precisely, this literal identification
concerns the case n=3 in the rotationally symmetric part, beginning
with Section 3.
Let
\displaystyle g=dr^{2}+f(r)^{2}\,d\theta^{2}
be a rotationally symmetric metric on S^{2}, and use the adapted
oriented orthonormal frame
\displaystyle e_{1}=\partial_{r},\qquad e_{2}=\frac{1}{f(r)}\partial_{\theta}.
Its Levi-Civita connection form is
\displaystyle\omega=f^{\prime}(r)\,d\theta,\qquad\omega(e_{1})=0,\qquad\omega(e_{2})=\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}.
Consequently, for a local section
s=u(r,\theta)Z^{\otimes m} of E_{m}, the general operator B_{m}
takes the form
(7.5)
\displaystyle B_{m}s=\left(\partial_{r}u+\frac{i}{f}\partial_{\theta}u-m\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}u\right)Z^{\otimes(m+1)}.
In the adapted moving frame Z^{\otimes m}, the m-th rotational
weight is carried by the frame itself. Hence the complexification of the
m-th Fourier sector is represented by sections whose coefficient is
radial. Restricting (7.5) to u=u(r)
gives
\displaystyle B_{m}\bigl(uZ^{\otimes m}\bigr)
\displaystyle=\left(u^{\prime}-m\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}u\right)Z^{\otimes(m+1)}=(\mathbf{B}_{m}u)Z^{\otimes(m+1)},
where \mathbf{B}_{m} is exactly the radial operator in
(3.3).
On the pole-compatible radial graph core \left\{vZ^{\otimes(m+1)}:v\in\mathscr{D}_{m+1}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}\right\}, the adjoint satisfies
\displaystyle B_{m}^{*}\bigl(vZ^{\otimes(m+1)}\bigr)
\displaystyle=\left(-v^{\prime}-(m+1)\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}v\right)Z^{\otimes m}=(\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}v)Z^{\otimes m},
which agrees with the formal adjoint expression
(3.4) when n=3.
The Hilbert-space structures also agree. For a radial coefficient,
\displaystyle\bigl\|uZ^{\otimes m}\bigr\|_{L^{2}(E_{m})}^{2}=2\pi\int_{0}^{a}|u(r)|^{2}f(r)\,dr.
Thus, up to the harmless factor 2\pi, this is the complexification of
the radial space H from Section 3. Moreover,
smooth extension across the two poles imposes precisely the complexified
pole conditions
\displaystyle u\in\mathscr{D}_{m}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}.
Taking graph closures therefore identifies the restriction of the closed
bundle operator B_{m} with the complexification of the closed radial
operator \mathbf{B}_{m}, and similarly for their adjoints.
Finally, since
\displaystyle K_{g}=-\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f}=K_{\mathrm{rad}},
the Riemannian ladder identity
(7.7), when restricted to these
radial-coefficient subspaces, becomes
\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{m}\mathbf{B}_{m}^{*}-\mathbf{B}_{m+1}^{*}\mathbf{B}_{m+1}=2(m+1)K_{\mathrm{rad}},
which is exactly the radial ladder identity
(4.12) for n=3.
Thus the rotationally symmetric proof on S^{2} is the
separated-variable, symmetry-reduced realization of the intrinsic
Riemannian construction. For a general metric there is no global angular
coordinate, and the bundles E_{m} provide the invariant replacement for
the Fourier-mode decomposition.
The key identity is the following Weitzenbock-type formula.
Lemma 7.7 (Riemannian ladder identity).
On C^{\infty}(S^{2};\mathbb{C}),
(7.6)
\displaystyle B_{0}^{*}B_{0}=-\Delta_{g}
For every m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},
(7.7)
\displaystyle B_{m}B_{m}^{*}-B_{m+1}^{*}B_{m+1}=2(m+1)K_{g}
as identities of differential expressions on C^{\infty}(E_{m+1}). The right-hand side means multiplication by the scalar function 2(m+1)K_{g}.
Proof.
Step (1). We prove B_{0}^{*}B_{0}=-\Delta_{g}. For m=0,
\displaystyle B_{0}u=(e_{1}u+ie_{2}u)Z.
Using the local formula for the adjoint,
\displaystyle B_{0}^{*}(vZ)=-\left(D_{e_{1}}^{(1)}v-iD_{e_{2}}^{(1)}v\right),
we get
\displaystyle B_{0}^{*}B_{0}u=-\left(D_{e_{1}}^{(1)}-iD_{e_{2}}^{(1)}\right)(e_{1}u+ie_{2}u).
Since
\displaystyle D_{e_{1}}^{(1)}=e_{1}+i\omega_{1},\qquad D_{e_{2}}^{(1)}=e_{2}+i\omega_{2},
where \omega_{j}=\omega(e_{j}), this becomes
\displaystyle B_{0}^{*}B_{0}u=-\left(e_{1}-ie_{2}+i\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}\right)(e_{1}u+ie_{2}u).
Expanding,
\displaystyle(e_{1}-ie_{2})(e_{1}u+ie_{2}u)=e_{1}e_{1}u+e_{2}e_{2}u+i[e_{1},e_{2}]u.
Since the Levi-Civita connection is torsion-free,
\displaystyle[e_{1},e_{2}]=\nabla_{e_{1}}e_{2}-\nabla_{e_{2}}e_{1}=-\omega_{1}e_{1}-\omega_{2}e_{2}.
Hence
\displaystyle i[e_{1},e_{2}]u=-i\omega_{1}e_{1}u-i\omega_{2}e_{2}u.
Therefore
\displaystyle\left(e_{1}-ie_{2}+i\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}\right)(e_{1}u+ie_{2}u)=e_{1}e_{1}u+e_{2}e_{2}u+\omega_{2}e_{1}u-\omega_{1}e_{2}u.
On the other hand,
\displaystyle\operatorname{div}e_{1}=\omega_{2},\qquad\operatorname{div}e_{2}=-\omega_{1}.
Thus
\displaystyle\Delta_{g}u=e_{1}e_{1}u+e_{2}e_{2}u+(\operatorname{div}e_{1})e_{1}u+(\operatorname{div}e_{2})e_{2}u=e_{1}e_{1}u+e_{2}e_{2}u+\omega_{2}e_{1}u-\omega_{1}e_{2}u.
Consequently,
\displaystyle B_{0}^{*}B_{0}u=-\Delta_{g}u.
Step (2). The assertion is local. Fix a point p\in S^{2}, and choose the oriented orthonormal frame so that
\displaystyle\nabla e_{1}(p)=0,\qquad\nabla e_{2}(p)=0.
Then \omega_{1}(p)=\omega_{2}(p)=0, but the first derivatives of \omega at p remain. Since [e_{1},e_{2}](p)=0, we have
(7.8)
\displaystyle-K_{g}(p)=d\omega(e_{1},e_{2})(p)=e_{1}\omega_{2}(p)-e_{2}\omega_{1}(p).
Let s=u\cdot Z^{\otimes m} be a local section of E_{m}. At the point p, formulas (7.3) and (7.4) give
\displaystyle B_{m}^{*}B_{m}(u\cdot Z^{\otimes m})
\displaystyle=-\bigl(D_{e_{1}}^{(m+1)}-iD_{e_{2}}^{(m+1)}\bigr)\bigl(D_{e_{1}}^{(m)}+iD_{e_{2}}^{(m)}\bigr)u\,Z^{\otimes m}.
Expanding the right-hand side at p, and using \omega_{1}(p)=\omega_{2}(p)=0, (7.8) gives
\displaystyle B_{m}^{*}B_{m}(u\cdot Z^{\otimes m})
\displaystyle=\bigl[-e_{1}e_{1}u-e_{2}e_{2}u-im(e_{1}\omega_{1}+e_{2}\omega_{2})u+m(e_{1}\omega_{2}-e_{2}\omega_{1})u\bigr]Z^{\otimes m}
(7.9)
\displaystyle=\bigl[-e_{1}e_{1}u-e_{2}e_{2}u-im(e_{1}\omega_{1}+e_{2}\omega_{2})u-mK_{g}u\bigr]Z^{\otimes m}.
Step (3). Next let s=u\cdot Z^{\otimes m} be a local section of E_{m}, now viewed as the target bundle of B_{m-1}. From (7.3) and (7.4),
\displaystyle B_{m-1}B_{m-1}^{*}(u\cdot Z^{\otimes m})
\displaystyle=-\bigl(D_{e_{1}}^{(m-1)}+iD_{e_{2}}^{(m-1)}\bigr)\bigl(D_{e_{1}}^{(m)}-iD_{e_{2}}^{(m)}\bigr)u\,Z^{\otimes m}.
The same expansion gives
\displaystyle B_{m-1}B_{m-1}^{*}(u\cdot Z^{\otimes m})
\displaystyle=\bigl[-e_{1}e_{1}u-e_{2}e_{2}u-im(e_{1}\omega_{1}+e_{2}\omega_{2})u-m(e_{1}\omega_{2}-e_{2}\omega_{1})u\bigr]Z^{\otimes m}
(7.10)
\displaystyle=\bigl[-e_{1}e_{1}u-e_{2}e_{2}u-im(e_{1}\omega_{1}+e_{2}\omega_{2})u+mK_{g}u\bigr]Z^{\otimes m}.
Subtracting (7.9) from (7.10) gives
\displaystyle B_{m-1}B_{m-1}^{*}-B_{m}^{*}B_{m}=2mK_{g}\qquad\text{on }E_{m}.
Replacing m by m+1 proves (7.7).
∎
8. The Vanishing Theorem
The ladder identity alone is not enough for a finite eigenvalue comparison. The counting recursion also needs the exact size of the unpaired kernel and the absence of an adjoint kernel. In the rotational proof, these facts were endpoint facts for the one-dimensional equation B_{m}u=0. Globally, endpoint analysis is replaced by the geometry of holomorphic sections.
This section is therefore the bridge between the Riemannian ladder and complex analysis. We need two facts:
\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker B_{m}=2m+1,\qquad\ker B_{m}^{*}=\{0\}.
The identity \dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker B_{m}=2m+1 is first proved in an elementary way: conformal invariance reduces the problem to the round conformal sphere, and stereographic coordinates identify the kernel with polynomials of degree at most 2m.
The adjoint vanishing \ker B_{m}^{*}=0 is a Bochner consequence of the curvature term in the ladder identity.
Lemma 8.1.
Let \widehat{g}=e^{2\varphi}g, where \varphi\in C^{\infty}(S^{2}). Define \widehat{B}_{m} from \widehat{g} in the same way as B_{m} is defined from g. Then
\displaystyle\ker\widehat{B}_{m}=\ker B_{m}.
Proof.
Let e_{1},e_{2} be a local oriented g-orthonormal frame and put
\displaystyle\widehat{e}_{j}=e^{-\varphi}e_{j},\qquad j=1,2.
Then \widehat{e}_{1},\widehat{e}_{2} is an oriented \widehat{g}-orthonormal frame, and \widehat{Z}=e^{-\varphi}Z.
If s=u\cdot Z^{\otimes m}=\widehat{u}\widehat{Z}^{\otimes m}, then
(8.1)
\displaystyle\widehat{u}=e^{m\varphi}u.
The connection one-form changes by
(8.2)
\displaystyle\widehat{\omega}=\omega-d\varphi\circ J.
In components, if \varphi_{j}=e_{j}\varphi, then
\displaystyle\widehat{\omega}(e_{1})=\omega(e_{1})-\varphi_{2},\qquad\widehat{\omega}(e_{2})=\omega(e_{2})+\varphi_{1}.
Using (8.1) and (8.2), we compute
\displaystyle\widehat{D}_{\widehat{e}_{1}}^{(m)}\widehat{u}+i\widehat{D}_{\widehat{e}_{2}}^{(m)}\widehat{u}
\displaystyle=e^{(m-1)\varphi}\bigl(D_{e_{1}}^{(m)}u+iD_{e_{2}}^{(m)}u\bigr).
Therefore
\displaystyle\widehat{B}_{m}s
\displaystyle=e^{(m-1)\varphi}\bigl(D_{e_{1}}^{(m)}u+iD_{e_{2}}^{(m)}u\bigr)\widehat{Z}^{\otimes(m+1)}
\displaystyle=e^{(m-1)\varphi}\bigl(D_{e_{1}}^{(m)}u+iD_{e_{2}}^{(m)}u\bigr)e^{-(m+1)\varphi}Z^{\otimes(m+1)}=e^{-2\varphi}B_{m}s.
This implies the conclusion.
∎
Lemma 8.2.
For every m\geq 0, \dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker B_{m}=2m+1.
Proof.
By the uniformization theorem, every smooth metric on S^{2} is conformal, after an orientation-preserving diffeomorphism, to the round metric [15].
The construction of E_{m} and B_{m} is natural under diffeomorphisms, and Lemma 8.1 shows that \ker B_{m} is unchanged by a conformal change of metric.
Hence it is enough to compute \ker B_{m} for the round metric.
Let z=x+iy be the stereographic coordinate on \mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\{\infty\}. The round metric is
\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{round}}=e^{2\psi}(dx^{2}+dy^{2}),\qquad e^{\psi}=\frac{2}{1+|z|^{2}}.
Take
\displaystyle e_{1}=e^{-\psi}\partial_{x},\qquad e_{2}=e^{-\psi}\partial_{y}.
Then
(8.3)
\displaystyle\omega=-\psi_{y}\,dx+\psi_{x}\,dy.
Let s=u\cdot Z^{\otimes m} be a section on \mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\{\infty\}. Since
\displaystyle\partial_{\bar{z}}:=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}+i\partial_{y}),
formulas (7.3) and (8.3) give
\displaystyle B_{m}s=0
\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\partial_{\bar{z}}u-m(\partial_{\bar{z}}\psi)u=0\Longleftrightarrow\partial_{\bar{z}}(e^{-m\psi}u)=0.
Thus P(z):=e^{-m\psi}u(z) is an entire complex analytic function on \mathbb{C}.
Now write the section itself in the coordinate vector frame. Since
\displaystyle Z=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e_{1}-ie_{2})=\sqrt{2}\,e^{-\psi}\partial_{z},\qquad\partial_{z}:=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}-i\partial_{y}),
we have
(8.4)
\displaystyle s=u\cdot Z^{\otimes m}=e^{m\psi}P(z)\bigl(\sqrt{2}\,e^{-\psi}\partial_{z}\bigr)^{\otimes m}=2^{m/2}P(z)(\partial_{z})^{\otimes m}.
Near infinity, use the coordinate w=1/z. Then
\displaystyle\partial_{z}=-w^{2}\partial_{w}.
Therefore (8.4) becomes
\displaystyle s=2^{m/2}(-1)^{m}w^{2m}P(1/w)(\partial_{w})^{\otimes m}.
The section s is smooth at w=0 if and only if the coefficient
\displaystyle w^{2m}P(1/w)
has no negative powers in its Laurent expansion at w=0. Since P is entire, this is equivalent to saying that P is a polynomial of degree at most 2m:
\displaystyle P(z)=a_{0}+a_{1}z+\cdots+a_{2m}z^{2m}.
Conversely, every such polynomial gives a smooth global section satisfying B_{m}s=0. Hence
\displaystyle\ker B_{m}=\{2^{m/2}P(z)(\partial_{z})^{\otimes m}:\ P\text{ is a polynomial of degree }\leq 2m\}.
The complex dimension is therefore 2m+1.
∎
Lemma 8.3.
For every m\geq 0,
\displaystyle\ker B_{m}^{*}=\{0\}.
Proof.
We apply Lemma 2.3 to the concrete Hilbert
spaces
\displaystyle H_{m}=L^{2}(E_{m}),\qquad H_{m+1}=L^{2}(E_{m+1}),
the concrete closed operator
B_{m}:\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m})=H^{1}(E_{m})\to L^{2}(E_{m+1}), and the shifts
\alpha_{m}=m(m+1). Indeed, Lemma 7.7 and K_{g}\geq 1
give, first on smooth sections and then on all of H^{1}(E_{m+1}) by
density,
\displaystyle\|B_{m}^{*}t\|_{L^{2}(E_{m})}^{2}+m(m+1)\|t\|_{L^{2}(E_{m+1})}^{2}\geq\|B_{m+1}t\|_{L^{2}(E_{m+2})}^{2}+(m+1)(m+2)\|t\|_{L^{2}(E_{m+1})}^{2}.
Here both form domains are H^{1}(E_{m+1}), so the domain inclusion required
in Lemma 2.3 is automatic. Since
(m+1)(m+2)>m(m+1), that lemma yields \ker B_{m}^{*}=\{0\}.
∎
9. The comparison theorem and rigidity
For the remainder of Part II we use the following notation. For every
m\geq 0, let
\displaystyle H_{m}:=L^{2}(E_{m}),\qquad\alpha_{m}:=m(m+1),
and define the closed forms
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}_{m}(s,t)
\displaystyle:=\langle B_{m}s,B_{m}t\rangle_{H_{m+1}}+m(m+1)\langle s,t\rangle_{H_{m}},
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{a}_{m})
\displaystyle=H^{1}(E_{m}),
\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{m}(s,t)
\displaystyle:=\langle B_{m}^{*}s,B_{m}^{*}t\rangle_{H_{m}}+m(m+1)\langle s,t\rangle_{H_{m+1}},
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{c}_{m})
\displaystyle=H^{1}(E_{m+1}).
Let A_{m} and C_{m} be their associated self-adjoint operators. Thus
\displaystyle A_{m}
\displaystyle=B_{m}^{*}B_{m}+m(m+1)I\quad\text{in }H_{m},
\displaystyle C_{m}
\displaystyle=B_{m}B_{m}^{*}+m(m+1)I\quad\text{in }H_{m+1}.
Elliptic regularity and Rellich compactness on the closed surface show that
both operators have compact resolvent. Their eigenvalues are counted with
complex multiplicity and are denoted by \lambda_{j}(A_{m}) and \lambda_{j}(C_{m}),
starting at j=0.
The geometric content of the comparison is the following exact form identity:
for every t\in H^{1}(E_{m+1}),
(9.1)
\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{m}(t,t)-\mathfrak{a}_{m+1}(t,t)=2(m+1)\int_{S^{2}}(K_{g}-1)|t|^{2}\,dA_{g}.
It follows from Lemma 7.7 on smooth sections and extends to
H^{1}(E_{m+1}) by density. In particular,
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{c}_{m})
\displaystyle=\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{a}_{m+1}),
(9.2)
\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{m}(t,t)
\displaystyle\geq\mathfrak{a}_{m+1}(t,t)\qquad\text{when }K_{g}\geq 1.
Proposition 9.1.
For every m,j\geq 0,
(9.3)
\displaystyle\lambda_{j+2m+1}(A_{m})=\lambda_{j}(C_{m})\geq\lambda_{j}(A_{m+1}).
Consequently, for every l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},
\displaystyle N_{A_{m}}^{<}\bigl(l(l+1)\bigr)\leq(2m+1)+N_{A_{m+1}}^{<}\bigl(l(l+1)\bigr).
The same assertion holds for the closed counting functions \mathcal{N}.
Proof.
Apply Proposition 2.4 to the concrete spaces
H_{m}=L^{2}(E_{m}), the closed operator B_{m}, and the shift
\alpha_{m}=m(m+1). The compact-resolvent assumption in
Notation 2.1 was verified above. The required
form-domain inclusion and form order are exactly
(9.2). Finally,
Lemma 8.2 gives
r_{m}=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker B_{m}=2m+1. Formula
(9.3) and both counting recursions are therefore the
specialization of (2.4) and
(2.5).
∎
Theorem 9.2.
Let (S^{2},g) be a smooth Riemannian two-sphere with K_{g}\geq 1. Then,
for every l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},
(9.4)
\displaystyle N_{-\Delta_{g}}^{<}\bigl(l(l+1)\bigr)
\displaystyle\leq l^{2},
(9.5)
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{g}}\bigl(l(l+1)\bigr)
\displaystyle\leq(l+1)^{2}.
Equivalently,
(9.6)
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(S^{2},g)\geq\lambda_{i}(\mathbb{S}^{2},g_{\mathrm{round}}),\qquad i\geq 1.
Proof.
We apply Corollary 2.7 to
\displaystyle H_{m}=L^{2}(E_{m}),\qquad B_{m},\qquad\alpha_{m}=m(m+1).
The form order is (9.2), compact resolvent was recorded
above, and Lemma 8.2 gives the required exact
kernel dimensions 2m+1. Lemma 7.7 gives
A_{0}=B_{0}^{*}B_{0}=-\Delta_{g}. Hence
(2.12) is precisely
(9.4)–
(9.5).
For (9.6), choose l\geq 1 so that
l^{2}\leq i\leq l^{2}+2l. The round eigenvalue at every index in this
cluster is l(l+1), while the strict-counting estimate gives
\lambda_{l^{2}}(g)\geq l(l+1). Monotonicity of the ordered spectrum gives the
claim.
∎
Lemma 9.3.
Assume K_{g}\geq 1 and K_{g}\not\equiv 1. Then, for every m,j\geq 0,
\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(C_{m})>\lambda_{j}(A_{m+1}).
Proof.
Fix m and set
\displaystyle V:=2(m+1)(K_{g}-1).
By (9.1), C_{m}=A_{m+1}+V in the
quadratic-form sense. The smooth function V is nonnegative and is
positive on a nonempty open set \Omega.
Let S_{j} be the direct sum of the eigenspaces of C_{m} belonging to its
first j+1 eigenvalues, counted with multiplicity. We claim that
\displaystyle\int_{S^{2}}V|s|^{2}\,dA_{g}>0\qquad\text{for every }0\neq s\in S_{j}.
Otherwise s vanishes on \Omega. Decompose s into the finite sum
of its components corresponding to the distinct eigenvalues of C_{m}.
Because s vanishes on \Omega, so do C_{m}^{q}s for all
q\geq 0. A Vandermonde argument therefore shows that each individual eigencomponent vanishes on \Omega.
The unique-continuation theorem for
second-order elliptic operators with smooth coefficients forces each such
component to vanish identically, a contradiction.
The unit sphere of the finite-dimensional space S_{j} is compact, so there
is \delta_{j}>0 such that
\displaystyle\int_{S^{2}}V|s|^{2}\,dA_{g}\geq\delta_{j}\|s\|^{2}\qquad(s\in S_{j}).
Consequently,
\displaystyle\sup_{0\neq s\in S_{j}}\frac{\mathfrak{a}_{m+1}(s,s)}{\|s\|^{2}}\leq\lambda_{j}(C_{m})-\delta_{j}.
The min–max principle for A_{m+1} now gives
\lambda_{j}(A_{m+1})<\lambda_{j}(C_{m}).
∎
Theorem 9.4.
Let (S^{2},g) be a smooth Riemannian two-sphere with K_{g}\geq 1. If
(9.7)
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(S^{2},g)=\lambda_{i}(\mathbb{S}^{2},g_{\mathrm{round}})
for some i\geq 1, or if
(9.8)
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{g}}\bigl(l(l+1)\bigr)=(l+1)^{2}
holds for some l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}, then (S^{2},g) is isometric to the
unit round sphere.
Proof.
Suppose first that K_{g}\not\equiv 1. Combining
Proposition 9.1 with
Lemma 9.3 gives
\displaystyle\lambda_{j+2m+1}(A_{m})>\lambda_{j}(A_{m+1})\qquad(m,j\geq 0).
For fixed l\geq 1, iterate this inequality for
m=0,1,\ldots,l-1. Since
\sum_{m=0}^{l-1}(2m+1)=l^{2}, one obtains
\displaystyle\lambda_{l^{2}}(g)=\lambda_{l^{2}}(A_{0})>\lambda_{0}(A_{l})=l(l+1);
the last equality uses any nonzero element of \ker B_{l}, whose existence
follows from Lemma 8.2. If
(9.7) holds and
l^{2}\leq i\leq l^{2}+2l, then
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(g)\geq\lambda_{l^{2}}(g)>l(l+1)=\lambda_{i}(\mathbb{S}^{2},g_{\mathrm{round}}),
a contradiction. Thus individual eigenvalue equality forces
K_{g}\equiv 1.
Now assume (9.8), and set i=(l+1)^{2}-1=l^{2}+2l. The counting equality implies \lambda_{i}(S^{2},g)\leq l(l+1), whereas the ordered comparison (9.6) gives
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(S^{2},g)\geq\lambda_{i}(\mathbb{S}^{2},g_{\mathrm{round}})=l(l+1).
Thus (9.7) holds at the index i, and the preceding argument gives K_{g}\equiv 1.
In either case, a complete simply connected surface of constant curvature
1 is the unit round sphere. Hence (S^{2},g) is round.

Theorems 9.2 and
9.4 prove the smooth comparison and rigidity assertions
of Theorem 1.8. The role of

supplies the bundle operator, the curvature form order, and the kernel
dimension; Section 2 supplies the partner
spectra, index shifts, counting recursion, and equality propagation.
The proof is genuinely two-dimensional. Section 16

10. A complex-geometric proof
This section gives a complex-geometric proof of Theorem 1.8. The point is that the bundles appearing in the ladder are powers of the anticanonical bundle on the Riemann surface underlying the oriented Riemannian sphere, and the first-order operators are Dolbeault operators after a metric identification.
We use Griffiths–Harris as the standard reference for the complex-geometric background: Dolbeault’s theorem, the correspondence
between divisor classes and holomorphic line bundles on compact Riemann surfaces, Riemann–Roch for compact Riemann surfaces, global duality, Hermitian holomorphic bundles, Chern connections, curvature; see [13, Ch. 0, §3, p. 45; Ch. 0, §5, pp. 66–79; Ch. 0, §7, pp. 106–122; Ch. 1, §1, pp. 129–139; Ch. 2, §3, pp. 240–246; Ch. 5, §4, pp. 705–707].
The one-dimensional Bochner–Kodaira formula needed below is proved explicitly for the reader’s convenience.
If L\to\Sigma is a holomorphic line bundle over a compact Riemann surface, let \mathcal{O}(L) denote the sheaf of holomorphic sections of L, and set
\displaystyle h^{q}(L):=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H^{q}(\Sigma,\mathcal{O}(L)),\qquad q=0,1.
Let K_{\Sigma}:=\Lambda^{1,0}T^{*}\Sigma be the canonical holomorphic line bundle, then K_{\Sigma}^{-1}=T^{1,0}\Sigma.
For each integer m\geq 0, define
\displaystyle L_{m}:=K_{\Sigma}^{-m}=(T^{1,0}\Sigma)^{\otimes m},\qquad L_{0}:=\Sigma\times\mathbb{C}.
Thus L_{m} is the same smooth complex line bundle as the bundle E_{m} used in Sections 7–9, now equipped with its natural holomorphic structure.
Theorem 10.1 (Dolbeault, Riemann–Roch, Serre duality).
Let \Sigma be a compact Riemann surface of genus \mathfrak{g}, and let L\to\Sigma be a holomorphic line bundle. Then
\displaystyle H^{0,q}_{\bar{\partial}}(\Sigma,L)\simeq H^{q}(\Sigma,\mathcal{O}(L)),\qquad q=0,1.
\displaystyle h^{0}(L)-h^{1}(L)=\deg L+1-\mathfrak{g},
\displaystyle H^{1}(\Sigma,\mathcal{O}(L))\simeq H^{0}(\Sigma,\mathcal{O}(K_{\Sigma}\otimes L^{-1}))^{*}.
Proof.
These are standard results. For Dolbeault’s theorem, see Griffiths–Harris [13, Ch. 0, §3, p. 45]. For Riemann–Roch on compact Riemann surfaces, see [13, Ch. 2, §3, pp. 240–246]. The divisor–line-bundle correspondence used to write the statement for holomorphic line bundles is recalled in [13, Ch. 1, §1, pp. 129–139]. The Serre-duality statement used here is the one-dimensional line-bundle case of global duality; see [13, Ch. 5, §4, pp. 705–707].
∎
Lemma 10.2 (Anticanonical powers on the conformal sphere).
Let \Sigma be a compact Riemann surface biholomorphic to \mathbb{CP}^{1}. Then
(10.1)
\displaystyle h^{0}(L_{m})=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H^{0}(\Sigma,L_{m})=2m+1,
and
(10.2)
\displaystyle H^{1}(\Sigma,\mathcal{O}(L_{m}))=0.
Finally, for every p\in\Sigma, the evaluation map
\displaystyle\operatorname{ev}_{p}:H^{0}(\Sigma,L_{m})\longrightarrow(L_{m})_{p}
is surjective. Consequently,
(10.3)
\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\{s\in H^{0}(\Sigma,L_{m}):s(p)=0\}=2m.
Proof.
Step (1). Equivalently, \Sigma has genus \mathfrak{g}=0. The canonical bundle
of a compact Riemann surface of genus \mathfrak{g} has degree
\deg K_{\Sigma}=2\mathfrak{g}-2;
see [15, §5.4, Lemma 5.4.4, p. 215]. Hence, by additivity
of degree under tensor products and dualization,
\displaystyle\deg L_{m}
\displaystyle=\deg\bigl(K_{\Sigma}^{-m}\bigr)=-m\,\deg K_{\Sigma}=2m.
Theorem 10.1 gives
(10.4)
\displaystyle h^{0}(L_{m})-h^{0}(K_{\Sigma}\otimes L_{m}^{-1})=2m+1.
But
\displaystyle K_{\Sigma}\otimes L_{m}^{-1}=K_{\Sigma}^{m+1},\qquad\deg(K_{\Sigma}^{m+1})=-2(m+1)<0.
A holomorphic line bundle of negative degree has no nonzero holomorphic section: if s\not\equiv 0 is holomorphic, then its zero divisor is effective and has degree equal to the degree of the line bundle, which is impossible when the degree is negative. Thus
\displaystyle H^{0}(\Sigma,K_{\Sigma}^{m+1})=0.
Consequently, (10.4) gives (10.1). Serre duality gives
\displaystyle H^{1}(\Sigma,\mathcal{O}(L_{m}))\simeq H^{0}(\Sigma,K_{\Sigma}\otimes L_{m}^{-1})^{*}=H^{0}(\Sigma,K_{\Sigma}^{m+1})^{*}=0,
which proves (10.2).
Step (2). Since
\displaystyle K_{\mathbb{CP}^{1}}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{CP}^{1}}(-2),
one has
(10.5)
\displaystyle L_{m}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{CP}^{1}}(2m).
Moreover,
By (10.5), L_{m} is identified with \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{CP}^{1}}(2m), and \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{CP}^{1}}(2m) is globally generated for m\geq 0.
Therefore for every p\in\Sigma there exists a holomorphic section of L_{m} which does not vanish at p. Since the target (L_{m})_{p} is one-dimensional, \operatorname{ev}_{p} is surjective. Its kernel has codimension one in H^{0}(\Sigma,L_{m}), and (10.1) gives (10.3).

Let (S^{2},g) be oriented. The orientation and metric define the complex structure
\displaystyle J:TS^{2}\longrightarrow TS^{2},\qquad Je_{1}=e_{2},\qquad Je_{2}=-e_{1}
for every local oriented g-orthonormal frame e_{1},e_{2}. Denote the resulting compact Riemann surface by
\displaystyle\Sigma:=(S^{2},J).
For a holomorphic line bundle L\to\Sigma, write
\displaystyle\Omega^{0,q}(\Sigma,L):=C^{\infty}\bigl(\Lambda^{0,q}T^{*}\Sigma\otimes L\bigr),\qquad q=0,1.
The Dolbeault operator is
\displaystyle\bar{\partial}_{L}:\Omega^{0,0}(\Sigma,L)\longrightarrow\Omega^{0,1}(\Sigma,L),
and for L=L_{m} we write \bar{\partial}_{m}:=\bar{\partial}_{L_{m}}.
Let e_{1},e_{2} be a local oriented orthonormal frame, with dual coframe \theta^{1},\theta^{2}. Set
\displaystyle Z:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e_{1}-ie_{2}),\qquad\zeta:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\theta^{1}+i\theta^{2}),\qquad\bar{\zeta}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\theta^{1}-i\theta^{2}).
Then Z is a local unitary frame of T^{1,0}\Sigma=K_{\Sigma}^{-1}, \zeta is the dual local unitary frame of K_{\Sigma}, and \bar{\zeta} is a local unitary frame of \Lambda^{0,1}T^{*}\Sigma.
The metric gives a unitary bundle isomorphism
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{m}:\Lambda^{0,1}T^{*}\Sigma\otimes L_{m}\longrightarrow L_{m+1},\quad\quad\mathcal{I}_{m}(\bar{\zeta}\otimes Z^{\otimes m})=Z^{\otimes(m+1)}.
This definition is independent of the oriented orthonormal frame: if Z^{\prime}=e^{i\alpha}Z, then \bar{\zeta}^{\prime}=e^{i\alpha}\bar{\zeta}, and both sides acquire the same factor e^{i(m+1)\alpha}. In particular, \mathcal{I}_{m}^{*}=\mathcal{I}_{m}^{-1}.
Define
(10.6)
\displaystyle B_{m}^{\mathrm{Dol}}:=\sqrt{2}\,\mathcal{I}_{m}\circ\bar{\partial}_{m}:C^{\infty}(L_{m})\longrightarrow C^{\infty}(L_{m+1}).
Let \omega be the Levi-Civita connection one-form defined by
\displaystyle\nabla e_{1}=\omega\otimes e_{2},\qquad\nabla e_{2}=-\omega\otimes e_{1}.
The induced Chern connection on L_{m} is
\displaystyle\nabla^{(m)}(u\cdot Z^{\otimes m})=(du+i\cdot mu\omega)\otimes Z^{\otimes m}.
The complexified cotangent bundle splits as
\displaystyle T^{*}\Sigma\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}=\Lambda^{1,0}T^{*}\Sigma\oplus\Lambda^{0,1}T^{*}\Sigma.
Let
\displaystyle\Pi^{1,0}:T^{*}\Sigma\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}\to\Lambda^{1,0}T^{*}\Sigma,\qquad\Pi^{0,1}:T^{*}\Sigma\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}\to\Lambda^{0,1}T^{*}\Sigma
be the corresponding projections. Equivalently, for a complex-valued one-form \alpha,
\displaystyle\Pi^{1,0}\alpha=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\alpha-i\,\alpha\circ J\bigr),\qquad\Pi^{0,1}\alpha=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\alpha+i\,\alpha\circ J\bigr),
where (\alpha\circ J)(X)=\alpha(JX).
We define
\displaystyle(\nabla^{(m)})^{\prime}:=(\Pi^{1,0}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{L_{m}})\circ\nabla^{(m)},\qquad(\nabla^{(m)})^{\prime\prime}:=(\Pi^{0,1}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{L_{m}})\circ\nabla^{(m)}.
Thus
\displaystyle\nabla^{(m)}=(\nabla^{(m)})^{\prime}+(\nabla^{(m)})^{\prime\prime},
with
\displaystyle(\nabla^{(m)})^{\prime}:C^{\infty}(L_{m})\to\Omega^{1,0}(\Sigma,L_{m}),\qquad(\nabla^{(m)})^{\prime\prime}:C^{\infty}(L_{m})\to\Omega^{0,1}(\Sigma,L_{m}).
Writing
\displaystyle D^{(m)}_{e_{j}}u:=e_{j}u+imu\omega(e_{j}),\qquad j=1,2,
One obtains
(10.7)
\displaystyle\bar{\partial}_{m}(u\cdot Z^{\otimes m})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(D_{e_{1}}^{(m)}u+iD_{e_{2}}^{(m)}u\bigr)\bar{\zeta}\otimes Z^{\otimes m}.
Combining (10.6) and (10.7),
\displaystyle B_{m}^{\mathrm{Dol}}(u\cdot Z^{\otimes m})=\bigl(D_{e_{1}}^{(m)}u+iD_{e_{2}}^{(m)}u\bigr)Z^{\otimes(m+1)},
which is exactly the operator B_{m} from (7.3). Hence we write simply
\displaystyle B_{m}=B_{m}^{\mathrm{Dol}}.
As before, B_{m} denotes the closed operator initially defined on C^{\infty}(L_{m}):
\displaystyle B_{m}:\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m})=H^{1}(L_{m})\subset L^{2}(L_{m})\longrightarrow L^{2}(L_{m+1}),
and B_{m}^{*} denotes its Hilbert-space adjoint.
For m=0, L_{0} is trivial, and B_{0}^{*}B_{0}=-\Delta_{g} is exactly (7.6) from Lemma 7.7.
We now apply the metric-independent cohomology from Section 10. Since \Sigma is a compact Riemann surface of genus zero, \Sigma\simeq\mathbb{CP}^{1}. By construction,
\displaystyle\ker B_{m}=\ker\bar{\partial}_{m}=H^{0}(\Sigma,L_{m}).
Applying Lemma 10.2 gives
(10.8)
\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker B_{m}=2m+1.
For the adjoint-kernel vanishing, the same lemma gives
\displaystyle H^{1}(\Sigma,\mathcal{O}(L_{m}))=0.
By Dolbeault’s theorem, this is equivalent to
(10.9)
\displaystyle H^{0,1}_{\bar{\partial}}(\Sigma,L_{m})=0.
Let \Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m}^{*}) satisfy B_{m}^{*}\Psi=0. By elliptic regularity for \bar{\partial}_{m}^{*}, it is enough to argue in the smooth category. Put
\displaystyle\eta:=\mathcal{I}_{m}^{-1}\Psi\in\Omega^{0,1}(\Sigma,L_{m}).
Since B_{m}=\sqrt{2}\,\mathcal{I}_{m}\bar{\partial}_{m}, the equation B_{m}^{*}\Psi=0 is equivalent to
\displaystyle\bar{\partial}_{m}^{*}\eta=0.
Because \Sigma has complex dimension one, there are no (0,2)-forms, so automatically
\displaystyle\bar{\partial}_{m}\eta=0.
Thus \eta defines a Dolbeault cohomology class in H^{0,1}_{\bar{\partial}}(\Sigma,L_{m}). By (10.9), this class is zero, so there exists s\in\Omega^{0,0}(\Sigma,L_{m}) such that
\displaystyle\eta=\bar{\partial}_{m}s.
Then
\displaystyle\|\eta\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\langle\eta,\bar{\partial}_{m}s\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle\bar{\partial}_{m}^{*}\eta,s\rangle_{L^{2}}=0.
Hence \eta=0, and therefore \Psi=0. We have proved
(10.10)
\displaystyle\ker B_{m}^{*}=\{0\}.
This is the complex-geometric replacement for Lemma 8.3.
Remark 10.3.
The proof of (10.8) and (10.10) uses only the complex geometry of \mathbb{CP}^{1}, together with the smooth Hilbert adjoint after the metric is chosen. It does not use the curvature lower bound K_{g}\geq 1.
It remains to recover the curvature ladder. This is the one place where the Hermitian metric and the above Chern connections \nabla^{(m)} are essential.
Let \omega_{\Sigma}:=dA_{g} be the Kähler form of (\Sigma,g,J). For m\geq 0, set
\displaystyle\Theta_{m}:=(\nabla^{(m)})^{2}.
and on L_{m}-valued zero-forms define
\displaystyle\Delta_{m}^{\prime}=((\nabla^{(m)})^{\prime})^{*}(\nabla^{(m)})^{\prime},\qquad\Delta_{m}^{\prime\prime}=((\nabla^{(m)})^{\prime\prime})^{*}(\nabla^{(m)})^{\prime\prime}.
By (10.7),
(10.11)
\displaystyle(\nabla^{(m)})^{\prime\prime}=\bar{\partial}_{m}.
Theorem 10.4 (One-dimensional Bochner–Kodaira identity).
For every m\geq 0, on smooth sections of L_{m},
(10.12)
\displaystyle\Delta_{m}^{\prime}-\Delta_{m}^{\prime\prime}=\Lambda_{\omega_{\Sigma}}(i\Theta_{m}).
Equivalently, if i\Theta_{m}=f\omega_{\Sigma}, then
\displaystyle\Delta_{m}^{\prime}-\Delta_{m}^{\prime\prime}=f.
Remark 10.5.
This is the one-dimensional line-bundle case of the classical
Bochner–Kodaira identity, specialized here to the bundles L_{m} and to the Chern connections \nabla^{(m)} already introduced above. The differential-geometric method goes back to Bochner and Kodaira; see Kodaira’s 1953 paper
[17]. The vector-bundle form is
closely related to the Akizuki–Nakano/Nakano identities
[1, 25].
We include the proof in this special case to fix the sign convention.
Proof.
The identity is local. Fix p\in\Sigma, and choose a local oriented orthonormal frame so that the associated unitary (1,0)-coframe \zeta, the dual vector Z, and the unitary frame Z^{\otimes m} of L_{m} are normal at p. Thus
\displaystyle\omega_{\Sigma}=i\zeta\wedge\bar{\zeta}
at p, the first-order frame-connection terms vanish at p, and [Z,\bar{Z}](p)=0. For a smooth section s of L_{m},
\displaystyle(\nabla^{(m)})^{\prime}s=(\nabla^{(m)}_{Z}s)\zeta,\qquad(\nabla^{(m)})^{\prime\prime}s=(\nabla^{(m)}_{\bar{Z}}s)\bar{\zeta}.
At p, the formal adjoints satisfy
\displaystyle((\nabla^{(m)})^{\prime})^{*}(a\zeta)=-\nabla^{(m)}_{\bar{Z}}a,\qquad((\nabla^{(m)})^{\prime\prime})^{*}(b\bar{\zeta})=-\nabla^{(m)}_{Z}b.
Therefore
\displaystyle\Delta_{m}^{\prime}s=-\nabla^{(m)}_{\bar{Z}}\nabla^{(m)}_{Z}s,\qquad\Delta_{m}^{\prime\prime}s=-\nabla^{(m)}_{Z}\nabla^{(m)}_{\bar{Z}}s.
Subtracting gives
\displaystyle(\Delta_{m}^{\prime}-\Delta_{m}^{\prime\prime})s=(\nabla^{(m)}_{Z}\nabla^{(m)}_{\bar{Z}}-\nabla^{(m)}_{\bar{Z}}\nabla^{(m)}_{Z})s=\Theta_{m}(Z,\bar{Z})s.
Write i\Theta_{m}=f\omega_{\Sigma}. Since \omega_{\Sigma}(Z,\bar{Z})=i, one has
\displaystyle\Theta_{m}(Z,\bar{Z})=f.
Thus
\displaystyle(\Delta_{m}^{\prime}-\Delta_{m}^{\prime\prime})s=fs=\Lambda_{\omega_{\Sigma}}(i\Theta_{m})s.
Since p was arbitrary, the identity holds globally.
∎
The next identity is not a new curvature formula; it is the preceding
one-dimensional Bochner–Kodaira identity rewritten in the ladder notation of this paper.
Lemma 10.6 (Dolbeault curvature ladder).
For every m\geq 0, as operators on C^{\infty}(L_{m+1}),
\displaystyle B_{m}B_{m}^{*}-B_{m+1}^{*}B_{m+1}=2(m+1)K_{g}.
Proof.
By (10.11) and the definition of \Delta_{m+1}^{\prime\prime},
\displaystyle\Delta_{m+1}^{\prime\prime}=\bar{\partial}_{m+1}^{*}\bar{\partial}_{m+1}.
By (10.6) and the unitarity of \mathcal{I}_{m+1},
(10.13)
\displaystyle B_{m+1}^{*}B_{m+1}=2\bar{\partial}_{m+1}^{*}\bar{\partial}_{m+1}=2\Delta_{m+1}^{\prime\prime}\qquad\text{on }C^{\infty}(L_{m+1}).
For the (\nabla^{(m+1)})^{\prime}-part, define the unitary metric identification
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{m}:\Lambda^{1,0}T^{*}\Sigma\otimes L_{m+1}\longrightarrow L_{m},\qquad\mathcal{J}_{m}(\zeta\otimes Z^{\otimes(m+1)})=Z^{\otimes(m)}.
The local formula for \nabla^{(m+1)} gives
\displaystyle(\nabla^{(m+1)})^{\prime}(u\cdot Z^{\otimes(m+1)})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(D_{e_{1}}^{(m+1)}u-iD_{e_{2}}^{(m+1)}u\bigr)\zeta\otimes Z^{\otimes(m+1)}.
The formal adjoint of B_{m} is locally
\displaystyle B_{m}^{*}(u\cdot Z^{\otimes(m+1)})=-\bigl(D_{e_{1}}^{(m+1)}u-iD_{e_{2}}^{(m+1)}u\bigr)Z^{\otimes(m)}.
Hence
\displaystyle B_{m}^{*}=-\sqrt{2}\,\mathcal{J}_{m}(\nabla^{(m+1)})^{\prime}.
Since \mathcal{J}_{m} is unitary,
(10.14)
\displaystyle B_{m}B_{m}^{*}=2((\nabla^{(m+1)})^{\prime})^{*}(\nabla^{(m+1)})^{\prime}=2\Delta_{m+1}^{\prime}\qquad\text{on }C^{\infty}(L_{m+1}).
Combining (10.13) and (10.14),
(10.15)
\displaystyle B_{m}B_{m}^{*}-B_{m+1}^{*}B_{m+1}=2(\Delta_{m+1}^{\prime}-\Delta_{m+1}^{\prime\prime}).
It remains to evaluate \Theta_{m+1}. We use the curvature convention
\displaystyle d\omega=-K_{g}\,dA_{g}.
By the local formula for \nabla^{(m+1)},
\displaystyle\Theta_{m+1}=(\nabla^{(m+1)})^{2}=i(m+1)\,d\omega=-i(m+1)K_{g}\omega_{\Sigma},\qquad i\Theta_{m+1}=(m+1)K_{g}\omega_{\Sigma}.
Therefore
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\omega_{\Sigma}}(i\Theta_{m+1})=(m+1)K_{g}.
By (10.12),
\displaystyle\Delta_{m+1}^{\prime}-\Delta_{m+1}^{\prime\prime}=(m+1)K_{g}.
Substituting this into (10.15) gives
\displaystyle B_{m}B_{m}^{*}-B_{m+1}^{*}B_{m+1}=2(m+1)K_{g}\qquad\text{on }C^{\infty}(L_{m+1}).
∎

12. The weak Bochner–Kodaira inequality
For Y=X or Y=g_{\tau}, Notation 11.4
gives the unitary identification
\displaystyle J_{m,Y}:H_{m}^{Y}\longrightarrow H_{m}^{0},\qquad J_{m,Y}s=e^{(m+1)u_{Y}}s.
Indeed, (11.10) gives
\|J_{m,Y}s\|_{H_{m}^{0}}^{2}=\|s\|_{H_{m}^{Y}}^{2}.
Definition 12.1.
Assume V_{i},V are closed subspaces a Hilbert space H, we say V_{i} converge in the Mosco sense to V, if weak limits of sequences v_{i}\in V_{i} belong to V, and every
v\in V is a strong limit of some v_{i}\in V_{i}.
Define
\displaystyle\widehat{B}_{m}^{Y}:=J_{m+1,Y}B_{m}^{Y}J_{m,Y}^{-1}:H_{m}^{0}\supset\operatorname{Dom}(\widehat{B}_{m}^{Y})\longrightarrow H_{m+1}^{0}.
For Y=X or Y=g_{\tau}, define
\displaystyle\operatorname{graph}(\widehat{B}_{m}^{Y}):=\left\{\bigl(v,\widehat{B}_{m}^{Y}v\bigr):v\in\operatorname{Dom}(\widehat{B}_{m}^{Y})\right\}\subset H_{m}^{0}\oplus H_{m+1}^{0}.
Since \widehat{B}_{m}^{Y} is closed, its graph is a closed linear subspace of
H_{m}^{0}\oplus H_{m+1}^{0}.
Lemma 12.2.
For every fixed m\geq 0 and every \tau_{i}\downarrow 0,
\displaystyle\operatorname{graph}(\widehat{B}_{m}^{g_{\tau_{i}}})\xrightarrow{\mathrm{Mosco}}\operatorname{graph}(\widehat{B}_{m}^{X})
in H_{m}^{0}\oplus H_{m+1}^{0}.
Proof.
Step (1). Write
\displaystyle u_{i}:=u_{\tau_{i}},\qquad D_{m}:=B_{m}^{g_{0}}:C^{\infty}(L_{m})\longrightarrow C^{\infty}(L_{m+1}),
and let D_{m}^{\dagger} denote the formal adjoint of D_{m} with
respect to the fixed round Hermitian metrics and dA_{0}.
For Y=X or Y=g_{\tau_{i}}, let u_{Y}=u or u_{Y}=u_{i},
respectively. We claim that
(12.1)
\displaystyle(F,G)\in\operatorname{graph}(\widehat{B}_{m}^{Y})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D_{m}\bigl(e^{-(m+1)u_{Y}}F\bigr)=e^{-mu_{Y}}G\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{CP}^{1},L_{m+1}).
For Y=g_{\tau_{i}}, the conformal covariance of the ladder operator gives
\displaystyle B_{m}^{g_{\tau_{i}}}=e^{-2u_{i}}D_{m}.
Hence, if F=J_{m,g_{\tau_{i}}}s=e^{(m+1)u_{i}}s, then
\displaystyle\widehat{B}_{m}^{g_{\tau_{i}}}F
\displaystyle=J_{m+1,g_{\tau_{i}}}B_{m}^{g_{\tau_{i}}}J_{m,g_{\tau_{i}}}^{-1}F=e^{(m+2)u_{i}}e^{-2u_{i}}D_{m}\bigl(e^{-(m+1)u_{i}}F\bigr)=e^{mu_{i}}D_{m}\bigl(e^{-(m+1)u_{i}}F\bigr),
which is equivalent to (12.1).
For Y=X, the same equation is precisely the maximal
distributional definition of B_{m}^{X}.
Step (2)
Suppose (F_{i},G_{i})\in\operatorname{graph}(\widehat{B}_{m}^{g_{\tau_{i}}}) and
(12.2)
\displaystyle(F_{i},G_{i})\rightharpoonup(F,G)\quad\text{weakly in }H_{m}^{0}\oplus H_{m+1}^{0}.

(12.3)
\displaystyle D_{m}\bigl(e^{-(m+1)u_{i}}F_{i}\bigr)=e^{-mu_{i}}G_{i}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{CP}^{1},L_{m+1}).

(12.4)
\displaystyle\left\langle G_{i},e^{-mu_{i}}\Phi\right\rangle_{H_{m+1}^{0}}=\left\langle F_{i},e^{-(m+1)u_{i}}D_{m}^{\dagger}\Phi\right\rangle_{H_{m}^{0}}.

(11.26) gives
\displaystyle e^{-mu_{i}}\Phi
\displaystyle\longrightarrow e^{-mu}\Phi
\displaystyle\text{strongly in }H_{m+1}^{0},
(12.5)
\displaystyle e^{-(m+1)u_{i}}D_{m}^{\dagger}\Phi
\displaystyle\longrightarrow e^{-(m+1)u}D_{m}^{\dagger}\Phi
\displaystyle\text{strongly in }H_{m}^{0}.
Indeed, this follows directly from the pointwise bundle norm, the

Passing to the limit in
(12.4), using the weak convergence

(12.6)
\displaystyle\left\langle G,e^{-mu}\Phi\right\rangle_{H_{m+1}^{0}}=\left\langle F,e^{-(m+1)u}D_{m}^{\dagger}\Phi\right\rangle_{H_{m}^{0}}.

\displaystyle D_{m}\bigl(e^{-(m+1)u}F\bigr)=e^{-mu}G\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{CP}^{1},L_{m+1}).

\displaystyle(F,G)\in\operatorname{graph}(\widehat{B}_{m}^{X}).

Step (3).

\displaystyle(F,G)\in\operatorname{graph}(\widehat{B}_{m}^{X}).

(12.7)
\displaystyle s:=e^{-(m+1)u}F,\qquad h:=D_{m}s=e^{-mu}G.

\displaystyle F_{i}:=e^{(m+1)u_{i}}s,\qquad G_{i}:=e^{mu_{i}}h.

\displaystyle e^{-(m+1)u_{i}}F_{i}=s,\qquad D_{m}s=h=e^{-mu_{i}}G_{i}.

(12.9)
\displaystyle D_{m}\bigl(e^{-(m+1)u_{i}}F_{i}\bigr)=e^{-mu_{i}}G_{i}\quad\text{distributionally}.

bundle-valued form of (11.25) and the
positive-weight counterpart of Step (2). There the lower bound
u_{i}\geq-C controls the negative weights multiplying smooth test

while F\in H_{m}^{0} and G\in H_{m+1}^{0} provide the required global

u_{i}\longrightarrow u\qquad dA_{0}\text{-almost everywhere},

\displaystyle G_{i}

(12.10)
\displaystyle F_{i}\longrightarrow F\quad\text{in }H_{m}^{0},\qquad G_{i}\longrightarrow G\quad\text{in }H_{m+1}^{0}.
It follows from
(12.9) and
(12.1) that
\displaystyle(F_{i},G_{i})\in\operatorname{graph}(\widehat{B}_{m}^{g_{\tau_{i}}}).
Together with (12.10), this proves the
strong recovery condition and therefore the Mosco convergence of the

\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\alpha}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{Mosco}}\mathfrak{q}^{\alpha}\qquad\text{as closed nonnegative forms on }H_{0}.
Proof.
Step (1). We first verify that the forms appearing in the statement are indeed
densely defined closed nonnegative forms on H_{0}.
Since T_{i} is densely defined,
\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\alpha})=\operatorname{Dom}(T_{i}) is dense in H_{0}. Since T_{i} is closed,
its domain is complete with respect to the graph norm
\displaystyle\|v\|_{\operatorname{graph}(T_{i})}^{2}:=\|v\|_{H_{0}}^{2}+\|T_{i}v\|_{H_{1}}^{2}.
The form norm associated with \mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\alpha} is
\displaystyle\|v\|_{\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\alpha}}^{2}
\displaystyle:=\|v\|_{H_{0}}^{2}+\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\alpha}[v]=(\alpha+1)\|v\|_{H_{0}}^{2}+\|T_{i}v\|_{H_{1}}^{2}.
For v\in\operatorname{Dom}(T_{i}),
\displaystyle\|v\|_{\operatorname{graph}(T_{i})}^{2}\leq\|v\|_{\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\alpha}}^{2}\leq(\alpha+1)\|v\|_{\operatorname{graph}(T_{i})}^{2}.
Thus the form norm is equivalent to the graph norm. Consequently
\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\alpha}) is complete in the form
norm, so \mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\alpha} is closed. The same argument applies
to T and \mathfrak{q}^{\alpha}.
Step (2). We now prove the two Mosco conditions.
Let
\displaystyle v_{i}\rightharpoonup v\qquad\text{weakly in }H_{0}.
We must prove
(12.11)
\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}^{\alpha}[v]\leq\varliminf_{i\to\infty}\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\alpha}[v_{i}].
Put
\displaystyle L:=\varliminf_{i\to\infty}\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\alpha}[v_{i}].
If L=+\infty, then
(12.11) is automatic. Suppose therefore that
L<+\infty. Choose a subsequence, denoted by i_{k}, such that
(12.12)
\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}_{i_{k}}^{\alpha}[v_{i_{k}}]\longrightarrow L.
After discarding finitely many terms, all the quantities in
(12.12) are finite, and hence
\displaystyle v_{i_{k}}\in\operatorname{Dom}(T_{i_{k}}).
Moreover,
\displaystyle\|T_{i_{k}}v_{i_{k}}\|_{H_{1}}^{2}\leq\mathfrak{q}_{i_{k}}^{\alpha}[v_{i_{k}}],
so the sequence T_{i_{k}}v_{i_{k}} is bounded in H_{1}. By weak
compactness of bounded subsets of a Hilbert space, after passing to a
further subsequence, which we do not relabel, there is w\in H_{1}
such that
\displaystyle T_{i_{k}}v_{i_{k}}\rightharpoonup w\qquad\text{weakly in }H_{1}.
Therefore
\displaystyle\bigl(v_{i_{k}},T_{i_{k}}v_{i_{k}}\bigr)\rightharpoonup(v,w)\qquad\text{weakly in }H_{0}\oplus H_{1}.
For every k,
\displaystyle\bigl(v_{i_{k}},T_{i_{k}}v_{i_{k}}\bigr)\in\operatorname{graph}(T_{i_{k}}).
The weak-limit condition in the Mosco convergence of the graphs therefore
implies (v,w)\in\operatorname{graph}(T). Thus
(12.13)
\displaystyle v\in\operatorname{Dom}(T),\qquad w=Tv.
We have weak convergence
\displaystyle\bigl(T_{i_{k}}v_{i_{k}},\sqrt{\alpha}\,v_{i_{k}}\bigr)\rightharpoonup\bigl(Tv,\sqrt{\alpha}\,v\bigr)\qquad\text{in }H_{1}\oplus H_{0}.
Weak lower semicontinuity of the squared norm in H_{1}\oplus H_{0}
now gives
\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}^{\alpha}[v]
\displaystyle=\|Tv\|_{H_{1}}^{2}+\alpha\|v\|_{H_{0}}^{2}=\left\|\bigl(Tv,\sqrt{\alpha}\,v\bigr)\right\|_{H_{1}\oplus H_{0}}^{2}\leq\varliminf_{k\to\infty}\left\|\bigl(T_{i_{k}}v_{i_{k}},\sqrt{\alpha}\,v_{i_{k}}\bigr)\right\|_{H_{1}\oplus H_{0}}^{2}
\displaystyle=\varliminf_{k\to\infty}\mathfrak{q}_{i_{k}}^{\alpha}[v_{i_{k}}]=L.
By the definition of L, this is precisely
(12.11).
Step (3).
Let
\displaystyle v\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{q}^{\alpha})=\operatorname{Dom}(T).
Then
\displaystyle(v,Tv)\in\operatorname{graph}(T).
The strong recovery condition in the Mosco convergence of the graphs
provides pairs
\displaystyle(v_{i},w_{i})\in\operatorname{graph}(T_{i})
such that
(12.14)
\displaystyle(v_{i},w_{i})\longrightarrow(v,Tv)\qquad\text{strongly in }H_{0}\oplus H_{1}.
Since (v_{i},w_{i})\in\operatorname{graph}(T_{i}), we have
\displaystyle v_{i}\in\operatorname{Dom}(T_{i}),\qquad w_{i}=T_{i}v_{i}.
The strong convergence in
(12.14) therefore means
\displaystyle v_{i}\longrightarrow v\quad\text{strongly in }H_{0},\qquad T_{i}v_{i}\longrightarrow Tv\quad\text{strongly in }H_{1}.
Consequently,
\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\alpha}[v_{i}]
\displaystyle=\|T_{i}v_{i}\|_{H_{1}}^{2}+\alpha\|v_{i}\|_{H_{0}}^{2}\longrightarrow\|Tv\|_{H_{1}}^{2}+\alpha\|v\|_{H_{0}}^{2}=\mathfrak{q}^{\alpha}[v].
Thus v_{i} is a recovery sequence for v. This proves the strong
recovery condition and completes the proof of Mosco convergence.
∎

Lemma 12.8.

Thus
\displaystyle s\in\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m}^{X})\subset H_{m}^{X},\qquad B_{m}^{X}s=0.
We first compare the singular H_{m}^{X}-norm with the fixed smooth round

\displaystyle g_{X}=e^{2u}g_{0},
the Hermitian metrics induced on
\displaystyle L_{m}=K_{\mathbb{CP}^{1}}^{-m}=(T^{1,0}\mathbb{CP}^{1})^{\otimes m}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{CP}^{1}}|s|_{h_{m,0}}^{2}e^{2(m+1)u}\,dA_{0}.

\displaystyle|(\partial_{z})^{\otimes m}|_{h_{m,0}}^{2}=2^{-m}e^{2m\psi},\qquad dA_{0}=e^{2\psi}\,dx\,dy.
Hence, for every D^{\prime}\Subset D,
(12.19)
\displaystyle\int_{D^{\prime}}|s|_{h_{m,0}}^{2}\,dA_{0}=2^{-m}\int_{D^{\prime}}|f|^{2}e^{2(m+1)\psi}\,dx\,dy.
Because \psi is smooth, the function
e^{2(m+1)\psi} is bounded above and bounded away from zero on
\overline{D^{\prime}}. From
(12.18) and
(12.19), it follows that
\displaystyle f\in L^{2}(D^{\prime},dx\,dy).
Since D^{\prime} has finite Euclidean measure,
\displaystyle f\in L^{1}(D^{\prime},dx\,dy).
As D^{\prime}\Subset D was arbitrary,
(12.20)
\displaystyle f\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(D)\subset L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(D).

the distribution \bar{\partial}_{m}s is represented by a measurable

\displaystyle B_{m}^{X}s=\sqrt{2}\,\mathcal{I}_{m,X}\eta.

\displaystyle\partial_{\bar{z}}f=0\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(D).

holomorphic on D.

Lemma 12.9.
For every m\geq 0, the embedding of
\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m}^{X}), equipped with its graph norm, into H_{m}^{X}
is compact. Consequently A_{m}^{X}, C_{m}^{X} have compact resolvent.
Proof.
Step (1). Put
\displaystyle\alpha_{r}:=r(r+1),\qquad r\geq 0.
We prove first that the essential spectrum of every operator is empty.
Lemma 12.8 gives
(12.22)
\displaystyle\ker B_{r}^{X}
\displaystyle\subset H^{0}(\mathbb{CP}^{1},K_{\mathbb{CP}^{1}}^{-r}),
\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker B_{r}^{X}
\displaystyle\leq 2r+1.
Second,
(12.23)
\displaystyle\ker(B_{r}^{X})^{*}=\{0\}.
Indeed, if t\in\ker(B_{r}^{X})^{*}, then
t\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{c}_{r}^{X}). By
Proposition 12.7,
t\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{a}_{r+1}^{X}) and
\displaystyle\alpha_{r+1}\|t\|_{H_{r+1}^{X}}^{2}
\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{a}_{r+1}^{X}(t,t)\leq\mathfrak{c}_{r}^{X}(t,t)=\alpha_{r}\|t\|_{H_{r+1}^{X}}^{2}.
Since \alpha_{r+1}-\alpha_{r}=2(r+1)>0, one has t=0.
We next compare the essential spectra of the two partners at one
level. Let
\displaystyle B_{r}^{X}=U_{r}|B_{r}^{X}|
be the polar decomposition. By
(12.23),
\displaystyle\overline{\operatorname{Ran}B_{r}^{X}}=\bigl(\ker(B_{r}^{X})^{*}\bigr)^{\perp}=H_{r+1}^{X}.
Hence the partial isometry U_{r} restricts to a unitary map
\displaystyle U_{r}:(\ker B_{r}^{X})^{\perp}\longrightarrow H_{r+1}^{X}.
Set
\displaystyle T_{r}:=(B_{r}^{X})^{*}B_{r}^{X}\big|_{(\ker B_{r}^{X})^{\perp}}.
The polar-decomposition functional calculus gives
\displaystyle B_{r}^{X}(B_{r}^{X})^{*}=U_{r}T_{r}U_{r}^{*}.
Moreover, (\ker B_{r}^{X})^{\perp} is a reducing subspace for
(B_{r}^{X})^{*}B_{r}^{X}, and therefore
\displaystyle A_{r}^{X}
\displaystyle=\alpha_{r}I_{\ker B_{r}^{X}}\oplus(T_{r}+\alpha_{r}I),
\displaystyle C_{r}^{X}
\displaystyle=U_{r}(T_{r}+\alpha_{r}I)U_{r}^{*}.
The first summand of A_{r}^{X} acts on the finite-dimensional space
\ker B_{r}^{X} by (12.22). A
finite-dimensional direct summand has empty essential spectrum, while
unitary equivalence preserves essential spectrum. Hence
(12.24)
\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(A_{r}^{X})=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(C_{r}^{X}).
For a self-adjoint operator T bounded from below on a Hilbert space
H, define
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathrm{ess}}(T):=\begin{cases}\inf\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T),&\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T)\neq\varnothing,\\
+\infty,&\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T)=\varnothing.\end{cases}
If \mathfrak{q}_{T} is the closed form of T, the max–min principle
and its form-domain version imply
(12.25)
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathrm{ess}}(T)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}L\subset H\\
\dim L<\infty\end{subarray}}\ \inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{q}_{T})\cap L^{\perp}\\
\|v\|_{H}=1\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{q}_{T}(v,v).
Indeed, for each fixed value of \dim L, the inner variational
quantity is the corresponding max–min value; taking the supremum over
all finite dimensions gives the bottom of the essential spectrum.
See
[27, Theorem 8.1.1 and Proposition 8.1.3,
pp. 125–127].
It follows in particular that quadratic-form order is monotone at the
bottom of the essential spectrum: if S\geq T in the form sense, then
(12.26)
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathrm{ess}}(S)\geq\Lambda_{\mathrm{ess}}(T).
For completeness, fix a finite-dimensional L\subset H. The form
order means
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{q}_{S})
\displaystyle\subset\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{q}_{T}),
\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}_{S}(v,v)
\displaystyle\geq\mathfrak{q}_{T}(v,v)\quad(v\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{q}_{S})).
Consequently,
\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{q}_{S})\cap L^{\perp}\\
\|v\|=1\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{q}_{S}(v,v)\geq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{q}_{S})\cap L^{\perp}\\
\|v\|=1\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{q}_{T}(v,v)\geq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{q}_{T})\cap L^{\perp}\\
\|v\|=1\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{q}_{T}(v,v).
Taking the supremum over L proves
(12.26).
Apply (12.26) to the form order
\displaystyle C_{r}^{X}\geq A_{r+1}^{X}
from Proposition 12.7, and then use
(12.24). This yields
(12.27)
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathrm{ess}}(A_{r}^{X})=\Lambda_{\mathrm{ess}}(C_{r}^{X})\geq\Lambda_{\mathrm{ess}}(A_{r+1}^{X})\qquad(r\geq 0).
On the other hand,
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}_{N}^{X}(s,s)
\displaystyle=\|B_{N}^{X}s\|_{H_{N+1}^{X}}^{2}+\alpha_{N}\|s\|_{H_{N}^{X}}^{2}\geq\alpha_{N}\|s\|_{H_{N}^{X}}^{2},\qquad s\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{a}_{N}^{X}).
Thus A_{N}^{X}\geq\alpha_{N}I as a self-adjoint operator, so
\sigma(A_{N}^{X})\subset[\alpha_{N},+\infty)
and therefore
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathrm{ess}}(A_{N}^{X})\geq\alpha_{N}=N(N+1).
Iterating (12.27), for every N>m we
obtain
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathrm{ess}}(A_{m}^{X})
\displaystyle\geq\Lambda_{\mathrm{ess}}(A_{m+1}^{X})\geq\cdots\geq\Lambda_{\mathrm{ess}}(A_{N}^{X})
\displaystyle\geq N(N+1).
Since this holds for every N>m, it follows that
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathrm{ess}}(A_{m}^{X})=+\infty,\qquad\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(A_{m}^{X})=\varnothing.
A self-adjoint operator has empty essential spectrum if and only if it
has compact resolvent; see
[27, Proposition 7.2.4, p. 117].
Thus A_{m}^{X} has compact resolvent. Equation
(12.24) also gives
\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(C_{m}^{X})=\varnothing,
so C_{m}^{X} has compact resolvent as well.
Step (2). It remains to prove the compact graph embedding in the statement. By
the representation theorem for the closed form \mathfrak{a}_{m}^{X},
\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}\bigl((A_{m}^{X}+I)^{1/2}\bigr)=\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{a}_{m}^{X})=\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m}^{X}),
and, for s\in\operatorname{Dom}(B_{m}^{X}),
\displaystyle\bigl\|(A_{m}^{X}+I)^{1/2}s\bigr\|_{H_{m}^{X}}^{2}
\displaystyle=\mathfrak{a}_{m}^{X}(s,s)+\|s\|_{H_{m}^{X}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|B_{m}^{X}s\|_{H_{m+1}^{X}}^{2}+(\alpha_{m}+1)\|s\|_{H_{m}^{X}}^{2}.
The norm in (12.28) is equivalent

Now let (s_{j}) be bounded in the graph norm of B_{m}^{X}, and set
\displaystyle f_{j}:=(A_{m}^{X}+I)^{1/2}s_{j}.
By (12.28), (f_{j}) is bounded in

\displaystyle s_{j}=(A_{m}^{X}+I)^{-1/2}f_{j}.

c_{0}>0 such that
\displaystyle|f(re^{i\theta})|^{2}\geq c_{0}r^{2k}.
By Jensen’s inequality and Lemma 13.3, for every
\varepsilon>0 and all sufficiently small r,
\displaystyle I(r)
\displaystyle\geq c_{0}r^{2k}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{2lU(re^{i\theta})}\,d\theta\geq c_{1}r^{2k}\exp\bigl(2l\overline{U}(r)\bigr)\geq c_{2}r^{2k-2l\gamma+2l\varepsilon}.
Equivalently, with r=e^{-t},
(13.7)
\displaystyle I(e^{-t})^{1/2}\geq c_{3}\exp\bigl((l\gamma-k-l\varepsilon)t\bigr)\qquad\text{for }t\gg 1.
Put J(t):=I(e^{-t})^{1/2}. For a.e. r, the distributional identity
(13.5) gives
(13.8)
\displaystyle\frac{d}{dr}I(r)=2\Re\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i\theta}D_{l}f(re^{i\theta})\overline{f(re^{i\theta})}e^{2lU(re^{i\theta})}\,d\theta.
Here is the precise meaning of this identity. Put
Q:=|f|^{2}e^{2lU}. Since
(13.5) says distributionally that
e^{2lU}D_{l}f=\partial_{z}(e^{2lU}f), and since f is
holomorphic, one has
\displaystyle\partial_{z}Q=e^{2lU}D_{l}f\,\overline{f}
in the sense of distributions on punctured coordinate annuli. Hence, using
\partial_{r}=e^{i\theta}\partial_{z}+e^{-i\theta}\partial_{\bar{z}},
\displaystyle\partial_{r}Q=2\Re\bigl(e^{i\theta}D_{l}f\,\overline{f}\,e^{2lU}\bigr)
distributionally on each such annulus. The two local L^{2}-bounds above imply,
by Cauchy–Schwarz, that the right-hand side is locally integrable. Therefore
the standard slicing theorem for weak derivatives gives that I(r) is locally
absolutely continuous in r and that (13.8) holds for
a.e. r.
By Cauchy’s inequality,
\displaystyle|I^{\prime}(r)|\leq 2\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}|D_{l}f(re^{i\theta})|^{2}e^{2lU(re^{i\theta})}\,d\theta\right)^{1/2}I(r)^{1/2}.
Consequently, for a.e. t with J(t)>0,
(13.9)
\displaystyle|J^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\leq e^{-2t}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|D_{l}f(e^{-t}e^{i\theta})|^{2}e^{2lU(e^{-t}e^{i\theta})}\,d\theta.
Integrating
(13.9) and using (13.6), we obtain
\displaystyle\int_{T}^{\infty}|J^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\,dt<\infty
for every sufficiently large T. Choose T such that J(T)<\infty.
Then, for t\geq T,
(13.10)
\displaystyle J(t)\leq J(T)+\left(\int_{T}^{t}|J^{\prime}(\tau)|^{2}\,d\tau\right)^{1/2}(t-T)^{1/2}\leq C(1+t^{1/2}).
Thus the adjoint-domain condition implies at most polynomial growth of
J(t) as t\to\infty.
Suppose now that k<l\gamma. Choose \varepsilon>0 so small that
\displaystyle l\gamma-k-l\varepsilon>0.
Then (13.7) says that J(t) grows at least
exponentially, while (13.10) says that it grows at
most like t^{1/2}.
This contradiction proves k\geq l\gamma>0. So s(p)=0.
∎
Proposition 13.5.
If
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{X}}\bigl(l(l+1)\bigr)=(l+1)^{2}
for some l\geq 1, then \nu_{X} is atomless.
Proof.
By Lemma 13.2,
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}V_{l}^{X}=2l+1. If p were an atom of \nu_{X},
Lemma 13.4 would imply that every
section in V_{l}^{X} vanishes at p. Lemma 12.8
then gives
\displaystyle V_{l}^{X}\subset\{s\in H^{0}(\mathbb{CP}^{1},K_{\mathbb{CP}^{1}}^{-l}):s(p)=0\}.
By the evaluation statement in
Lemma 10.2, the space on the right has
complex dimension 2l, a contradiction.
∎
14. The atomless Bochner–Kodaira defect identity
The weak form order of Proposition 12.7 retains
only nonnegativity of the limiting defect. Rigidity needs the defect measure
itself. Under atomlessness, the missing term is recovered directly from the maximal adjoint domain.
Lemma 14.1.
Assume \omega_{X} is atomless. Let l\geq 1 and
s\in V_{l}^{X}. Set q:=|s|_{h_{l,X}}^{2}, then
(14.1)
\displaystyle\|(B_{l-1}^{X})^{*}s\|_{H_{l-1}^{X}}^{2}=2l\int_{X}q\,d\omega_{X}.
In particular, the integral on the right is finite.
Proof.
Step (1). By Lemma 12.8, s is holomorphic. In a
holomorphic coordinate with g_{X}=e^{2U}|dz|^{2}, write
\displaystyle s=f(z)(\partial_{z})^{\otimes l},\qquad q=2^{-l}|f|^{2}e^{2lU}.
We use the conventions
\displaystyle\partial_{z}:=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}-i\partial_{y}),\qquad\partial_{\bar{z}}:=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}+i\partial_{y}).
For g_{X}=e^{2U}|dz|^{2}, the oriented orthonormal frame and the associated unitary frames are
\displaystyle e_{1}
\displaystyle=e^{-U}\partial_{x},
\displaystyle e_{2}
\displaystyle=e^{-U}\partial_{y},
(14.2)
\displaystyle Z_{X}
\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e_{1}-ie_{2})=\sqrt{2}\,e^{-U}\partial_{z},
\displaystyle\bar{\zeta}_{X}
\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\theta^{1}-i\theta^{2})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}e^{U}d\bar{z}.
Consequently,
\displaystyle\partial_{z}=2^{-1/2}e^{U}Z_{X},\qquad d\bar{z}=\sqrt{2}\,e^{-U}\bar{\zeta}_{X}.
For every r\geq 0 and every measurable coefficient v, we therefore
have the exact local norm formula
(14.3)
\displaystyle\left|v(\partial_{z})^{\otimes r}\right|_{g_{X}}^{2}\,dA_{X}=2^{-r}|v|^{2}e^{2(r+1)U}\,dx\,dy.
We next record the exact coordinate formula for B_{m}^{X}. If
\displaystyle\phi=a(\partial_{z})^{\otimes m},
then
\displaystyle\bar{\partial}_{m}\phi
\displaystyle=(\partial_{\bar{z}}a)\,d\bar{z}\otimes(\partial_{z})^{\otimes m}
\displaystyle=2^{(1-m)/2}e^{(m-1)U}(\partial_{\bar{z}}a)\,\bar{\zeta}_{X}\otimes Z_{X}^{\otimes m}.
Since
\displaystyle B_{m}^{X}=\sqrt{2}\,\mathcal{I}_{m,X}\bar{\partial}_{m},\qquad\mathcal{I}_{m,X}\bigl(\bar{\zeta}_{X}\otimes Z_{X}^{\otimes m}\bigr)=Z_{X}^{\otimes(m+1)},
and
\displaystyle Z_{X}^{\otimes(m+1)}=2^{(m+1)/2}e^{-(m+1)U}(\partial_{z})^{\otimes(m+1)},
it follows that
(14.4)
\displaystyle B_{m}^{X}\bigl(a(\partial_{z})^{\otimes m}\bigr)=2\sqrt{2}\,e^{-2U}(\partial_{\bar{z}}a)(\partial_{z})^{\otimes(m+1)}.
Put
\displaystyle w:=(B_{l-1}^{X})^{*}s,\qquad w=b(\partial_{z})^{\otimes(l-1)}
on D. Let a\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D), and set
\displaystyle\phi:=a(\partial_{z})^{\otimes(l-1)}.
Using (14.3),
(14.4), and the convention that the
Hermitian inner product is linear in its first variable, we obtain
(14.5)
\displaystyle\left\langle B_{l-1}^{X}\phi,s\right\rangle_{H_{l}^{X}}
\displaystyle=2^{3/2-l}\int_{D}(\partial_{\bar{z}}a)\,\overline{f}\,e^{2lU}\,dx\,dy,
(14.6)
\displaystyle\left\langle\phi,w\right\rangle_{H_{l-1}^{X}}
\displaystyle=2^{1-l}\int_{D}a\,\overline{b}\,e^{2lU}\,dx\,dy.
The defining relation for the Hilbert adjoint says that
(14.5) and
(14.6) are equal for every
a\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D). Distributional integration by parts therefore gives
\displaystyle-\sqrt{2}\,\partial_{\bar{z}}\bigl(e^{2lU}\overline{f}\bigr)=e^{2lU}\overline{b}\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(D).
After complex conjugation,
(14.7)
\displaystyle\partial_{z}\bigl(e^{2lU}f\bigr)=-2^{-1/2}e^{2lU}b\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(D).
Thus the distribution on the left of
(14.7) is represented by a measurable
function, and we may define
(14.8)
\displaystyle D_{l}f:=e^{-2lU}\partial_{z}\bigl(e^{2lU}f\bigr)=-2^{-1/2}b.
Equivalently,
(14.9)
\displaystyle(B_{l-1}^{X})^{*}s=-\sqrt{2}\,D_{l}f\,(\partial_{z})^{\otimes(l-1)}.
When U is smooth, and distributionally in the present sense,
\displaystyle D_{l}f=\partial_{z}f+2lU_{z}f.
Formula (14.3), applied with r=l-1,
now gives
(14.10)
\displaystyle\left|(B_{l-1}^{X})^{*}s\right|_{g_{X}}^{2}\,dA_{X}
\displaystyle=2^{1-l}\left|\sqrt{2}\,D_{l}f\right|^{2}e^{2lU}\,dx\,dy=2^{2-l}|D_{l}f|^{2}e^{2lU}\,dx\,dy.
In particular D_{l}f\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}\bigl(D,e^{2lU}dx\,dy\bigr).
Step (2). We next compute the weak derivative of q. By the local boundedness
of the holomorphic function f, and
(14.10), all the products in the
following calculation are locally integrable.
Since
\partial_{z}\overline{f}=0, we obtain distributionally
(14.11)
\displaystyle\partial_{z}q
\displaystyle=2^{-l}\partial_{z}\bigl(e^{2lU}f\overline{f}\bigr)=2^{-l}\partial_{z}\bigl(e^{2lU}f\bigr)\overline{f}=2^{-l}e^{2lU}D_{l}f\,\overline{f}.
The right-hand side belongs to L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}} by
Cauchy–Schwarz. Since q is real-valued, this also identifies
\partial_{\bar{z}}q, and hence
\displaystyle q\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(D).
On the set where q>0, formula
(14.11) gives
(14.12)
\displaystyle\frac{4|\partial_{z}q|^{2}}{q}
\displaystyle=4\frac{2^{-2l}e^{4lU}|D_{l}f|^{2}|f|^{2}}{2^{-l}e^{2lU}|f|^{2}}=2^{2-l}|D_{l}f|^{2}e^{2lU}.
Finally, write the fixed round metric in this coordinate as
\displaystyle g_{0}=e^{2\psi}|dz|^{2}.
For the weak gradient of the real-valued function q,
\displaystyle|\nabla_{0}q|^{2}=e^{-2\psi}\left(|\partial_{x}q|^{2}+|\partial_{y}q|^{2}\right)=4e^{-2\psi}|\partial_{z}q|^{2},
while
\displaystyle dA_{0}=e^{2\psi}\,dx\,dy.
Therefore
(14.13)
\displaystyle\frac{|\nabla_{0}q|^{2}}{q}\,dA_{0}
\displaystyle=\frac{4|\partial_{z}q|^{2}}{q}\,dx\,dy=2^{2-l}|D_{l}f|^{2}e^{2lU}\,dx\,dy=\left|(B_{l-1}^{X})^{*}s\right|_{g_{X}}^{2}\,dA_{X}.
If s\not\equiv 0, its holomorphic coefficient has only finitely many
zeros. Thus \{q=0\} has dA_{0}-measure zero. We define
|\nabla_{0}q|^{2}/q to be zero on this set; this does not change the
integral. If s\equiv 0, both sides below vanish identically.
Since both sides of (14.13) are globally defined
densities, the local identities agree on coordinate overlaps. Integrating
over X gives
(14.14)
\displaystyle\left\|(B_{l-1}^{X})^{*}s\right\|_{H_{l-1}^{X}}^{2}=\int_{X}\frac{|\nabla_{0}q|^{2}}{q}\,dA_{0}.
Step (2). If Z(s) is the finite zero divisor of s, then the Poincaré–Lelong formula
[13, p. 388]
gives
(14.15)
\displaystyle\Delta_{0}\log|f|=2\pi\sum_{p\in Z(s)}\operatorname{ord}_{p}(s)\,\delta_{p}
as distributions. Here we use the convention
\Delta_{0}=\operatorname{div}_{g_{0}}\nabla_{0}, so that
-\Delta_{0} is nonnegative and, in a Euclidean coordinate,
\Delta_{\mathrm{euc}}\log|z|=2\pi\delta_{0}.
Since
\displaystyle\log q=-l\log 2+2\log|f|+2lU,\quad\quad-\Delta_{0}U=\omega_{X},
we obtain
(14.16)
\displaystyle-\Delta_{0}\log q=2l\,\omega_{X}-4\pi\sum_{p\in Z(s)}\operatorname{ord}_{p}(s)\,\delta_{p}
as measure-valued distributions.
Set
(14.17)
\displaystyle E(q):=\int_{X}\frac{|\nabla_{0}q|^{2}}{q}\,dA_{0}<\infty,
where the integrand is defined to be zero on \{q=0\}.
Because q\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(dA_{0}) and
(14.17) is finite, one has
\displaystyle\sqrt{q}\in W^{1,2}_{\mathrm{loc}},\qquad|\nabla_{0}\sqrt{q}|^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\frac{|\nabla_{0}q|^{2}}{q}\quad\text{a.e. on }\{q>0\}.
For R>0, put
\displaystyle\phi_{R}(x)\vcentcolon=\min\{q(x),R\},\quad\quad\forall x\in X.
It follows that
\phi_{R}\in W^{1,2}_{\mathrm{loc}}\cap L^{\infty}, and the
Sobolev chain rule gives
(14.18)
\displaystyle\nabla_{0}\phi_{R}=\mathbf{1}_{\{q<R\}}\nabla_{0}q\qquad\text{a.e.}
Write
\displaystyle Z(s)=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{N}\}.
Choose mutually disjoint holomorphic coordinate discs
D_{\rho}(p_{\alpha}), 1\leq\alpha\leq N, containing no zeros
other than their centers. For 0<\varepsilon<\rho, set
\displaystyle\Omega_{\varepsilon}:=X\setminus\bigcup_{\alpha=1}^{N}\overline{D_{\varepsilon}(p_{\alpha})}.
Let n_{\varepsilon} be the outward unit normal of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}. On an inner boundary circle this normal
points into the deleted disc. We also write
\nu_{\alpha} for the unit normal to
\partial D_{\varepsilon}(p_{\alpha}) pointing away from p_{\alpha};
thus
\displaystyle n_{\varepsilon}=-\nu_{\alpha}\qquad\text{on }\partial D_{\varepsilon}(p_{\alpha}).
On \Omega_{\varepsilon}, the divisor terms in
(14.16) are absent, and hence
(14.19)
\displaystyle-\Delta_{0}\log q=2l\,\omega_{X}\qquad\text{on }\Omega_{\varepsilon}
in the distributional sense.
Testing
(14.19) by \phi_{R} and using the weak
Green formula gives, for almost every admissible \varepsilon,
\displaystyle 2l\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\phi_{R}\,d\omega_{X}
\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\left\langle\nabla_{0}\log q,\nabla_{0}\phi_{R}\right\rangle\,dA_{0}-\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\phi_{R}\,\partial_{n_{\varepsilon}}\log q\,ds_{0}
(14.20)
\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap\{0<q<R\}}\frac{|\nabla_{0}q|^{2}}{q}\,dA_{0}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\int_{\partial D_{\varepsilon}(p_{\alpha})}\phi_{R}\,\partial_{\nu_{\alpha}}\log q\,ds_{0}.
Indeed, away from Z(s), \nabla_{0}\log q=\frac{\nabla_{0}q}{q}, and therefore (14.18) gives
(14.21)
\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla_{0}\log q,\nabla_{0}\phi_{R}\right\rangle=\mathbf{1}_{\{0<q<R\}}\frac{|\nabla_{0}q|^{2}}{q}\qquad\text{a.e.}
It remains to show that the boundary fluxes in
(14.20) vanish along a sequence
\varepsilon_{j}\downarrow 0.
For almost every r\in(0,\rho), set
\displaystyle e_{\alpha}(r):=\int_{\partial D_{r}(p_{\alpha})}\frac{|\nabla_{\mathrm{euc}}q|^{2}}{q}\,ds_{\mathrm{euc}}.
The coarea formula and
(14.17) give
(14.22)
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\rho}e_{\alpha}(r)\,dr=\int_{D_{\rho}(p_{\alpha})}\frac{|\nabla_{\mathrm{euc}}q|^{2}}{q}\,dx\,dy<\infty.
Step (3). Put
\displaystyle e(r):=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}e_{\alpha}(r).
Then e\in L^{1}(0,\rho).
We claim that, for every \delta,\eta>0, the set
(14.23)
\displaystyle\left\{r\in(0,\delta):re(r)<\eta\right\}
has positive Lebesgue measure. Otherwise, one would have
\displaystyle e(r)\geq\frac{\eta}{r}\qquad\text{for a.e. }r\in(0,\delta),
and hence
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\delta}e(r)\,dr\geq\eta\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{dr}{r}=+\infty,
contrary to e\in L^{1}(0,\rho).
Set \varepsilon_{0}:=\rho. Inductively, having chosen
\varepsilon_{j-1}, put
\displaystyle\delta_{j}:=\min\left\{\frac{\rho}{2^{j}},\frac{\varepsilon_{j-1}}{2}\right\}.
By (14.23), we may choose \varepsilon_{j}\in\cap(0,\delta_{j})
such that \varepsilon_{j}e(\varepsilon_{j})<\frac{1}{j}. Then
(14.24)
\displaystyle\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\varepsilon_{j}e(\varepsilon_{j})=0.
Step (4). In the holomorphic coordinate z centered at p_{\alpha}, write
g_{0}=e^{2\psi_{\alpha}}|dz|^{2}.
Let \nu_{\alpha,0} and
\nu_{\alpha,\mathrm{euc}} denote the unit normals to
\partial D_{r}(p_{\alpha}), with respect to g_{0} and the Euclidean
metric, respectively, both pointing away from p_{\alpha}. Then
\nu_{\alpha,0}=e^{-\psi_{\alpha}}\nu_{\alpha,\mathrm{euc}},\qquad ds_{0}=e^{\psi_{\alpha}}ds_{\mathrm{euc}},
and hence
\partial_{\nu_{\alpha,0}}f\,ds_{0}=\partial_{\nu_{\alpha,\mathrm{euc}}}f\,ds_{\mathrm{euc}}.
In particular,
\int_{\partial D_{r}(p_{\alpha})}\phi_{R}\,\partial_{\nu_{\alpha,0}}\log q\,ds_{0}=\int_{\partial D_{r}(p_{\alpha})}\phi_{R}\,\partial_{\nu_{\alpha,\mathrm{euc}}}\log q\,ds_{\mathrm{euc}}.
For q>0, since \phi_{R}=\min\{q,R\}, one has \frac{\phi_{R}^{2}}{q}\leq R. Consequently, by Cauchy–Schwarz,
\displaystyle\left|\int_{\partial D_{\varepsilon_{j}}(p_{\alpha})}\phi_{R}\,\partial_{\nu_{\alpha,0}}\log q\,ds_{0}\right|
\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\partial D_{\varepsilon_{j}}(p_{\alpha})}\frac{\phi_{R}}{q}\,\partial_{\nu_{\alpha,\mathrm{euc}}}q\,ds_{\mathrm{euc}}\right|
\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\partial D_{\varepsilon_{j}}(p_{\alpha})}\frac{|\partial_{\nu_{\alpha,\mathrm{euc}}}q|^{2}}{q}\,ds_{\mathrm{euc}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\partial D_{\varepsilon_{j}}(p_{\alpha})}\frac{\phi_{R}^{2}}{q}\,ds_{\mathrm{euc}}\right)^{1/2}
(14.25)
\displaystyle\leq\bigl(2\pi R\varepsilon_{j}e_{\alpha}(\varepsilon_{j})\bigr)^{1/2}.
Summing over \alpha and using Cauchy–Schwarz in
\mathbb{R}^{N},
(14.26)
\displaystyle\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\left|\int_{\partial D_{\varepsilon_{j}}(p_{\alpha})}\phi_{R}\,\partial_{\nu_{\alpha}}\log q\,ds_{0}\right|\leq\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\bigl(2\pi NR\varepsilon_{j}e(\varepsilon_{j})\bigr)^{1/2}=0
by (14.24).
We now let j\to\infty in
(14.20). Since the integrand in
(14.21) is dominated by the integrable
function \frac{|\nabla_{0}q|^{2}}{q}, we have
(14.27)
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon_{j}}\cap\{0<q<R\}}\frac{|\nabla_{0}q|^{2}}{q}\,dA_{0}\longrightarrow\int_{\{0<q<R\}}\frac{|\nabla_{0}q|^{2}}{q}\,dA_{0}.
Moreover, \omega_{X} is atomless and \phi_{R}\leq R, so
(14.28)
\displaystyle\left|\int_{X}\phi_{R}\,d\omega_{X}-\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon_{j}}}\phi_{R}\,d\omega_{X}\right|
\displaystyle\leq R\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\omega_{X}\bigl(\overline{D_{\varepsilon_{j}}(p_{\alpha})}\bigr)\longrightarrow 0.
Here we used
\displaystyle\omega_{X}\bigl(\overline{D_{\varepsilon_{j}}(p_{\alpha})}\bigr)\longrightarrow\omega_{X}(\{p_{\alpha}\})=0.
Combining
(14.20),
(14.26),
(14.27), and
(14.28), we obtain
(14.29)
\displaystyle\int_{\{0<q<R\}}\frac{|\nabla_{0}q|^{2}}{q}\,dA_{0}=2l\int_{X}\phi_{R}\,d\omega_{X}.
Since the integrand is defined to be zero on \{q=0\}, the
left-hand side may equivalently be written as \int_{\{q<R\}}\frac{|\nabla_{0}q|^{2}}{q}\,dA_{0}.
Finally, as R\to\infty,
\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\{q<R\}}\frac{|\nabla_{0}q|^{2}}{q}\uparrow\frac{|\nabla_{0}q|^{2}}{q}\quad\text{a.e.},\qquad\phi_{R}(x)\uparrow q(x).
Monotone convergence in
(14.29) therefore yields
\displaystyle\int_{X}\frac{|\nabla_{0}q|^{2}}{q}\,dA_{0}=2l\int_{X}q\,d\omega_{X},
which, together with (14.14), proves
(14.1).
∎
Proposition 14.2.
Assume \nu_{X} is atomless. Then, for every l\geq 1 ,
\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{l-1}^{X}(s,s)-\mathfrak{a}_{l}^{X}(s,s)=2l\int_{X}|s|_{h_{l,X}}^{2}\,d\nu_{X},\quad\quad\forall s\in V_{l}^{X}.
Proof.
Because B_{l}^{X}s=0, the definitions of the two shifted forms give
\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{l-1}^{X}(s,s)-\mathfrak{a}_{l}^{X}(s,s)=\|(B_{l-1}^{X})^{*}s\|^{2}-2l\|s\|_{H_{l}^{X}}^{2}.
Apply Lemma 14.1 and use
\|s\|_{H_{l}^{X}}^{2}=\int|s|^{2}dA_{X} and
\nu_{X}=\omega_{X}-dA_{X}.
∎

Lemma 15.3.
If \nu_{X}=0, then X is isometric to the unit round sphere.
Proof.
The Alexandrov Bonnet–Myers diameter theorem
[4, Theorem 3.6]
gives
\operatorname{diam}X\leq\pi.
Fix p\in X and choose R>\pi. Then B_{R}(p)=X, while the
radius-R ball in the simply connected two-dimensional model of
constant curvature 1 is the whole unit sphere.
By (11.4),
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{2}(X)=\int_{X}1dA_{X}=\omega_{X}(X)=4\pi.
Since both balls have two-dimensional Hausdorff measure 4\pi, the equality case of
the Alexandrov volume-comparison theorem
[4, Theorem 10.2]
shows that X is isometric to the unit round sphere.
∎
Theorem 15.4 (Alexandrov counting rigidity).
Let X be a compact Alexandrov two-sphere with curvature at least 1.
If, for some l\geq 1,
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{X}}\bigl(l(l+1)\bigr)=(l+1)^{2},
then X is isometric to the unit round sphere.
Proof.
By Proposition 13.5, the defect
measure \nu_{X} is atomless. By
Lemma 13.2, the concrete application of
Proposition 2.6 gives
\displaystyle V_{l}^{X}=\ker B_{l}^{X},\qquad\dim_{\mathbb{C}}V_{l}^{X}=2l+1,
and
\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{l-1}^{X}(s,s)=\mathfrak{a}_{l}^{X}(s,s)\qquad(s\in V_{l}^{X}).
Proposition 14.2 therefore yields
(15.2)
\displaystyle\int_{X}|s|_{h_{l,X}}^{2}\,d\nu_{X}=0\qquad\text{for every }s\in V_{l}^{X}.
By Lemma 12.8 and the dimension equality,
\displaystyle V_{l}^{X}=H^{0}(\mathbb{CP}^{1},K_{\mathbb{CP}^{1}}^{-l}).
For every p\in\mathbb{CP}^{1}, the evaluation map is surjective by
Lemma 10.2; hence some
s_{p}\in V_{l}^{X} satisfies s_{p}(p)\neq 0.
In a coordinate centered at
p, its holomorphic coefficient is therefore bounded away from zero on a
smaller neighborhood. The local conformal factor is bounded from below, so
|s_{p}|_{h_{l,X}}^{2}\geq c_{p}>0 almost everywhere on that neighborhood.
Equation (15.2) then implies that \nu_{X}
vanishes there.
Compactness gives finitely many such neighborhoods, and hence
\nu_{X}=0.
Lemma 15.3 now shows that X is round.

Part IV Eigenvalues comparison and dimension of harmonic functions
16. A counterexample in dimension three
This section explains why the two-dimensional ladder should not be regarded as the shadow of a straightforward higher-dimensional theorem. The obstruction is not only technical. In dimension two, the scalar spherical harmonic multiplicities are reproduced by the line-bundle dimensions \dim H^{0}(\mathbb{CP}^{1},K^{-m})=2m+1, and the curvature commutator has the correct sign under K\geq 1. In dimension three, a Ricci lower bound does not supply an analogous first-order ladder with the round multiplicities, and the exact eigenvalue comparison is in fact false.
The example below is included to mark the boundary of the method. It gives a completely explicit counterexample to the statement that \Ric_{g}\geq 2g on S^{3} should imply
\lambda_{i}(S^{3},g)\geq\lambda_{i}(\mathbb{S}^{3},g_{\mathrm{round}}) for every i\geq 1.
Theorem 16.1 (An explicit conformal counterexample on S^{3}).
Let g_{0}=g_{\mathrm{round}} be the unit round metric on
S^{3}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}, where
S^{3}=\{(z,w)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:|z|^{2}+|w|^{2}=1\}. Define
\displaystyle q:=|z|^{2},\qquad\psi(q):=\frac{49}{50}-31q+\frac{75}{4}q^{2}-6q^{3}.

satisfies
\displaystyle\Ric_{\widehat{g}_{\varepsilon}}\geq 2\widehat{g}_{\varepsilon},
but
\displaystyle\lambda_{7714}(S^{3},\widehat{g}_{\varepsilon})<840=\lambda_{7714}(\mathbb{S}^{3},g_{0}),
where eigenvalues are counted with the convention \lambda_{0}=0.
Proof.

\displaystyle z=e^{i\theta}\sin t,\qquad w=e^{i\phi}\cos t,\qquad 0<t<\frac{\pi}{2},
so that q=\sin^{2}t and
\displaystyle g_{0}=dt^{2}+\sin^{2}t\,d\theta^{2}+\cos^{2}t\,d\phi^{2}.

\displaystyle\Ric_{\widehat{g}}=\Ric_{g}-\nabla^{2}u-(\Delta u)g+du\otimes du-|\nabla u|^{2}g.
Taking u=\varepsilon\psi and using \Ric_{g_{0}}=2g_{0}, we get
\displaystyle\Ric_{\widehat{g}_{\varepsilon}}-2\widehat{g}_{\varepsilon}
\displaystyle=2(1-e^{2\varepsilon\psi})g_{0}-\varepsilon\nabla^{2}\psi-\varepsilon(\Delta\psi)g_{0}+\varepsilon^{2}(d\psi\otimes d\psi-|\nabla\psi|^{2}g_{0})
\displaystyle=\varepsilon T+O(\varepsilon^{2}),
where the remainder is uniform on S^{3} and
\displaystyle T:=-\nabla^{2}\psi-(\Delta\psi)g_{0}-4\psi g_{0}.
Let
\displaystyle e_{t}:=\partial_{t},\qquad e_{\theta}:=(\sin t)^{-1}\partial_{\theta},\qquad e_{\phi}:=(\cos t)^{-1}\partial_{\phi}.

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}F(e_{t},e_{t})
\displaystyle=4q(1-q)F^{\prime\prime}+2(1-2q)F^{\prime},
\displaystyle\nabla^{2}F(e_{\theta},e_{\theta})
\displaystyle=2(1-q)F^{\prime},
\displaystyle\nabla^{2}F(e_{\phi},e_{\phi})
\displaystyle=-2qF^{\prime},
\displaystyle\Delta F
\displaystyle=4q(1-q)F^{\prime\prime}+4(1-2q)F^{\prime}.

\displaystyle\psi(q)=\frac{49}{50}-31q+\frac{75}{4}q^{2}-6q^{3}
gives

\displaystyle\Ric_{\widehat{g}_{\varepsilon}}-2\widehat{g}_{\varepsilon}\geq 0.
It remains to prove that an eigenvalue drops. For k\geq 1, consider the round spherical harmonic
\displaystyle f_{k}(z,w):=\operatorname{Re}(w^{k})=(1-q)^{k/2}\cos(k\phi).
Let \mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon} be the Rayleigh quotient of the positive operator -\Delta_{\widehat{g}_{\varepsilon}}. Since
\displaystyle|\nabla f_{k}|_{\widehat{g}_{\varepsilon}}^{2}\,dV_{\widehat{g}_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=e^{3\varepsilon\psi}f_{k}^{2}\,dV_{g_{0}},

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bigl(q\cos^{2}(k\phi)+\sin^{2}(k\phi)\bigr)\,d\theta\,d\phi
\displaystyle=2\pi^{2}(1+q),
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos^{2}(k\phi)\,d\theta\,d\phi

\displaystyle=k^{2}\frac{A_{k}(\varepsilon)}{B_{k}(\varepsilon)},

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}(1-q)^{k-1}(1+q)e^{\varepsilon\psi(q)}\,dq,
\displaystyle B_{k}(\varepsilon)
\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}(1-q)^{k}e^{3\varepsilon\psi(q)}\,dq.

Remark 16.2.
The numerical choices are made only to keep the verification short. The curvature check reduces to three quadratics with negative discriminant, and the eigenvalue drop is detected by the single degree 28 spherical harmonic f_{28}=\operatorname{Re}(w^{28}).
17. The dimension of polynomial growth harmonic functions
This section returns to the original motivation. The eigenvalue comparison on a two-sphere is not merely an isolated compact result; it is the cross-section statement predicted by the sharp dimension problem for polynomial-growth harmonic functions. For a cone C(X), homogeneous harmonic functions are controlled by the spectrum of the cross-section X. Thus a finite counting comparison on X becomes a dimension comparison for harmonic functions on the cone, and, through tangent-cone-at-infinity arguments, for noncompact manifolds.
The smooth theorem proves the needed comparison when the cross-section is a smooth positively curved two-sphere. For a three-manifold with nonnegative sectional curvature and positive asymptotic volume ratio, however, the cross-section at infinity is naturally an Alexandrov two-sphere. Hence the equality case forces us to ask for a singular version of the same finite counting rigidity. This is the point where the complex-geometric language becomes more than a simplification: it provides the right framework for curvature measures, singular Hermitian metrics, and holomorphic sections on the conformal sphere.
We now isolate the part of the sharp Yau dimension problem which is genuinely suggested by the two-dimensional eigenvalue comparison proved above.
For k\geq 0, put \rho(x):=d_{g}(p,x) and define
\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{k}(M):=\{u\in C^{\infty}(M):\Delta_{g}u=0,\ |u(x)|\leq C(\rho(x)^{k}+1)\text{ for some }C>0\},
\displaystyle h_{k}(M):=\dim\mathscr{H}_{k}(M).
In this section (M^{3},g) is complete with sectional curvature K_{g}\geq 0 and
(17.1)
\displaystyle\operatorname{AVR}(M):=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{\operatorname{vol}B_{p}(r)}{r^{3}}>0.
The positivity condition (17.1) is what we call maximal volume growth.
For the Euclidean model,
(17.2)
\displaystyle h_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{3})=\sum_{j=0}^{d}(2j+1)=(d+1)^{2},\qquad d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.
Earlier work of Peter Li–Jiaping Wang [20] gave the corresponding asymptotic upper estimates under nonnegative sectional curvature.
Xian-Tao Huang’s theorem upgrades the asymptotic-cone philosophy to the finite counting inequality under maximal volume growth and uniqueness of the tangent cone.
Theorem 17.1.
Let (M^{n},g) be a complete noncompact Riemannian manifold with \Ric_{g}\geq 0 and maximal volume growth. Assume that M has a unique tangent cone at infinity, denoted by
\displaystyle(C(X),d_{C(X)},m_{C(X)}),
where C(X) is the metric cone over the cross-section (X,d_{X},m_{X}). Then, for every k>0,
(17.3)
\displaystyle h_{k}(M^{n})\leq\mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{X}}\bigl(k(k+n-2)\bigr).
Proof.
This is [14, Theorem 1.6].
∎
We now prove Theorem 1.12 in the notation of this section.
Proof of Theorem 1.12.
Step (1) For k=0, the Cheng–Yau gradient estimate for bounded harmonic functions under \operatorname{Ric}_{g}\geq 0 [7] gives h_{0}(M)=1=h_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{3}). Since K_{g}\geq 0, the metric space (M,d_{g}) is a three-dimensional Alexandrov space with curvature bounded below by 0.
By Shioya’s structure theorem for the limit cone of a noncompact Alexandrov space with nonnegative curvature [32, Proposition 1.1, pp. 209–211], the blow-downs converge, without passing to a subsequence, to the unique tangent cone at infinity:
(17.4)
\displaystyle(M,r_{i}^{-2}g,p)\longrightarrow C(X),\qquad r_{i}\to\infty.
Here X=M(\infty) is the ideal boundary of M, and C(X) is the Euclidean cone over X. Since (17.1) is positive, the convergence in (17.4) is noncollapsed and the limit cone has Hausdorff dimension 3. Hence X has Hausdorff dimension 2, and the standard cone criterion gives Alexandrov curvature at least 1 on X [4, Proposition 4.2.3, pp. 13–14].
Step (2). The pointed form of Perelman’s stability theorem implies that C(X) is a topological three-manifold near its vertex o; see [16, Theorem 7.11, pp. 125–126]. Moreover,
\displaystyle B_{o}(\varepsilon)\setminus\{o\}\cong(0,\varepsilon)\times X
for every \varepsilon>0. With integer coefficients, local homology at the vertex therefore satisfies
\displaystyle H_{j}(C(X),C(X)\setminus\{o\};\mathbb{Z})\cong H_{j}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\};\mathbb{Z}).
Because C(X) is contractible and C(X)\setminus\{o\} deformation retracts onto X, the long exact sequence of the pair also gives
\displaystyle H_{j}(C(X),C(X)\setminus\{o\};\mathbb{Z})\cong\widetilde{H}_{j-1}(X;\mathbb{Z}).
Thus X has the reduced integral homology of S^{2}. By [4, §12.9.3, p. 52], every compact two-dimensional Alexandrov space is a compact topological surface, possibly with boundary. The identity H_{2}(X;\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z} rules out boundary and gives orientability, while H_{1}(X;\mathbb{Z})=0. The classification of compact connected surfaces now shows that X is homeomorphic to S^{2}.
Step (3) For k\geq 1, (17.3) with n=3 and Corollary 15.2 give
\displaystyle h_{k}(M)\leq\mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{X}}\bigl(k(k+1)\bigr)\leq(k+1)^{2}=h_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).
Together with the case k=0 and (17.2), this proves (1.9).
Step (4) Suppose that h_{k}(M)=(k+1)^{2} for some k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}. Equality in the preceding chain forces
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{-\Delta_{X}}\bigl(k(k+1)\bigr)=(k+1)^{2}.
The Alexandrov counting-rigidity theorem, Theorem 15.4, makes X the unit round sphere. Hence the tangent cone in (17.4) is \mathbb{R}^{3} and \operatorname{AVR}(M)=\omega_{3}. Bishop–Gromov monotonicity says that
\displaystyle r\longmapsto\frac{\operatorname{vol}B_{p}(r)}{\omega_{3}r^{3}}
is nonincreasing. Its limit as r\downarrow 0 is 1, and its limit as r\to\infty is \operatorname{AVR}(M)/\omega_{3}=1; it is therefore identically one. The equality case of Bishop–Gromov implies that (M^{3},g) is isometric to \mathbb{R}^{3}.
∎
Remark 17.2 (Why a single-eigenvalue Obata statement is insufficient).
Theorem 15.4 cannot be replaced by a naive Obata-type statement of the form
\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(X)=\lambda_{j}(\mathbb{S}^{2},g_{\rm round})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad X\text{ is isometric to }(\mathbb{S}^{2},g_{\rm round}).
Indeed, to disprove such a statement it is enough to find one Alexandrov two-sphere X with curvature \geq 1, not isometric to the round unit sphere, but satisfying \lambda_{1}(X)=2. The football metrics in Example 17.3 give exactly such examples. Thus the rigidity statement needed in Theorem 15.4 must be formulated as a counting equality at a whole round threshold, not as equality of one eigenvalue.
Example 17.3 (A nonround Alexandrov sphere with \lambda_{1}=2).
Let 0<c<1. On (0,\pi)\times(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}), set
\displaystyle g_{c}=dr^{2}+c^{2}\sin^{2}r\,d\theta^{2}.
Let X_{c} be the metric completion obtained by adding the two endpoints N=\{r=0\} and S=\{r=\pi\}. This is the standard spherical football. The original two-cone classification and construction of spherical metrics of constant curvature on S^{2} with two conical singularities is due to Troyanov [34, pp. 296–306]. The explicit Friedrichs spectrum of the spherical football is computed in Mazzeo–Zhu [23, Lemmas 2–3, arXiv version, pp. 24–25]. The metric completion and the eigenfunction responsible for the equality \lambda_{1}(X_{c})=2 are shown in Figure 6.

Figure 6. The football metric g_{c}=dr^{2}+c^{2}\sin^{2}r\,d\theta^{2}. The cone angle at each added endpoint is 2\pi c, and the axial mode u=\cos r gives the eigenvalue 2 independently of c.
Near r=0, since \sin r=r+O(r^{3}), one has
\displaystyle g_{c}=dr^{2}+c^{2}r^{2}d\theta^{2}+O(r^{4})d\theta^{2}.
The same expansion holds near r=\pi. Hence N and S are conical points with cone angle 2\pi c. Since c<1, these cone angles are strictly smaller than 2\pi. On the regular part X_{c}^{\rm reg}:=X_{c}\setminus\{N,S\}, the Gaussian curvature is
\displaystyle K_{g_{c}}=-\frac{(c\sin r)^{\prime\prime}}{c\sin r}=1.
The curvature measure is therefore
\displaystyle\omega_{X_{c}}=dA_{g_{c}}+2\pi(1-c)\delta_{N}+2\pi(1-c)\delta_{S}\geq dA_{g_{c}}.
Thus X_{c} is an Alexandrov two-sphere with curvature at least 1. It is not isometric to the round unit sphere because it has two conical points.
By the spectrum formula in [23, Lemmas 2–3, arXiv version, pp. 24–25],
\displaystyle\operatorname{Spec}(-\Delta_{X_{c}})=\left\{\left(\frac{j}{c}+\ell\right)\left(\frac{j}{c}+\ell+1\right):j,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\right\},
with multiplicity one for j=0 and multiplicity two for j>0. The low-lying part of this spectrum is represented in Figure 7; it separates the meridian mode at eigenvalue 2 from all angular modes when 0<c<1.
j\ell0123401234\lambda_{0,0}=0\lambda_{0,1}=2j\geq 1 modeshave \lambda_{j,\ell}>2j=0,\ \ell\geq 2gives \lambda\geq 6low modes
Figure 7. The lattice of spectral modes for the spherical football. Only (j,\ell)=(0,0) and (0,1) lie at or below the threshold 2; all other modes are strictly above 2 when 0<c<1.
The mode j=0, \ell=1 gives the eigenfunction
\displaystyle u(r,\theta)=\cos r,\qquad-\Delta_{X_{c}}u=2u.
Since 0<c<1, every mode with j\geq 1 has eigenvalue
\displaystyle\frac{j}{c}\left(\frac{j}{c}+1\right)>2.
Also the modes j=0, \ell\geq 2 have eigenvalues at least 6. Therefore the only eigenvalues not exceeding 2 are 0 and 2, and hence
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(X_{c})=2=\lambda_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2},g_{\rm round}).
This gives a nonround Alexandrov two-sphere with curvature \geq 1 and first positive eigenvalue equal to the first positive round eigenvalue. Thus single ordered-eigenvalue equality is not a valid Alexandrov rigidity hypothesis.
