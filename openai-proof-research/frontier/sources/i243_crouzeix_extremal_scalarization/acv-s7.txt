7. Complete bounds and equality in dimension three




We first transfer the square functions from the uniform algebra to its
quotient.




Lemma 7.1.



Let A\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C}). Suppose that
X=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{q})^{\mathsf{T}}\in M_{q,1}(\mathfrak{A}_{A}) satisfies
\left\lVert X\right\rVert\leq 1, and put T_{j}=\theta_{A}(x_{j}). Then, for every
0<\tau<1,




(7.1)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\tau^{2k}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{W}_{k}}T_{\alpha}^{*}T_{\alpha}\preceq\frac{1}{1-\tau}I.



The analogous row estimate holds for every
X\in M_{1,q}(\mathfrak{A}_{A}) with \left\lVert X\right\rVert\leq 1.





Proof.



Fix \varepsilon>0. Lemma 2.2 gives a lift
F=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{q})^{\mathsf{T}} with





\max_{z\in W(A)}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{q}\left\lvert f_{j}(z)\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq 1+\varepsilon.



The operators f_{j}(A) are the fixed operators T_{j}. Apply
Theorem 1.3 to f_{j}/(1+\varepsilon). For every
finite partial sum, let \varepsilon\to 0^{+}. This gives
(7.1). The row assertion is proved in the
same way.
∎





Proof of Theorem 1.1.



Let A\in M_{3}(\mathbb{C}) and put C=\psi_{\mathrm{cb}}(A). By
Proposition 2.1,
C=\left\lVert\theta_{A}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{cb}}. If C\leq 1, there is nothing to
prove. Suppose that C>1. Theorem 5.3
gives positive integers r,s and
X\in M_{s,r}(\mathfrak{A}_{A}) such that





r+s\leq 3,\qquad\left\lVert X\right\rVert\leq 1,\qquad\left\lVert\theta_{A}^{(s,r)}(X)\right\rVert=C.



The possible pairs are





(r,s)=(1,1),\qquad(1,2),\qquad(2,1).



They are respectively a scalar, a two-component column, and a
two-component row. Lemma 7.1, at \tau=1/2 and
after retaining the terms of degrees zero and one, gives C\leq 2 in
each case. This proves (1.3).




The same proof applies in dimension two. For the lower estimate, let





J=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.



Then W(J)=\overline{\mathbb{D}} and \left\lVert J\right\rVert=2. Direct sums with a zero block
give the same lower estimate in dimension three. Hence
Q_{\mathrm{cb}}(2)=Q_{\mathrm{cb}}(3)=2.




More generally, if \psi_{\mathrm{cb}}(A)>2, a minimal extremal cannot
have r=1 or s=1, by Lemma 7.1. Thus
r,s\geq 2 and r+s\leq d. When d=4, this leaves only
(r,s)=(2,2).
∎





We now identify equality in dimension three.




Proof of Theorem 1.4.



The implication from right to left follows from





\psi(A)\leq\psi_{\mathrm{cb}}(A)\leq 2.



Suppose that \psi_{\mathrm{cb}}(A)=2 and \psi(A)<2. Choose a
minimal rectangular extremal as in
Theorem 5.3. The scalar pair
(1,1) cannot attain the completely bounded norm. Hence the pair is
either (1,2) or (2,1).




Suppose first that the pair is (1,2). Write





X=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad T_{j}=\theta_{A}(x_{j}).



There is a unit vector \xi\in\mathbb{C}^{3} such that




(7.2)

\left\lVert T_{1}\xi\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert T_{2}\xi\right\rVert^{2}=4.



At \tau=1/2, Lemma 7.1 gives





I+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{2}T_{j}^{*}T_{j}+\frac{1}{16}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}(T_{i}T_{j})^{*}(T_{i}T_{j})+\cdots\preceq 2I.



Taking the quadratic form at \xi and using
(7.2), the first two displayed terms already
have value 2. Hence




(7.3)

T_{i}T_{j}\xi=0\qquad(1\leq i,j\leq 2).






Let





\eta=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}T_{1}\xi\\
T_{2}\xi\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{3}.



The minimality assertion in
Theorem 5.3 says that \eta has
Schmidt rank two. Thus T_{1}\xi and T_{2}\xi are linearly independent.
The orthogonality relation (5.5) gives




(7.4)

\xi\perp T_{1}\xi,\qquad\xi\perp T_{2}\xi.



Since \dim\mathbb{C}^{3}=3, the two vectors T_{1}\xi,T_{2}\xi span
\xi^{\perp}. It follows from (7.3) that each
T_{i} vanishes on \xi^{\perp}. Therefore




(7.5)

T_{i}T_{j}=0\qquad(1\leq i,j\leq 2).






The space L=\operatorname{span}\{T_{1},T_{2}\} is two-dimensional and
L^{2}=\{0\}. It is contained in \alg(A). Since I\notin L and
\dim\alg(A)\leq 3, one has





\alg(A)=\mathbb{C}I\oplus L.



Thus A=aI+N for some N\in L. By (7.5),
N^{2}=0, and consequently





\alg(A)=\operatorname{span}\{I,N\}.



This contradicts \dim(\mathbb{C}I\oplus L)=3.




If the minimal pair is (2,1), apply the preceding argument to A^{*}
and the adjoint column. Lemma 2.3 preserves both
the scalar and completely bounded ratios. This completes the proof.
∎





We next prove the quantitative estimate for diagonalizable matrices.




Proof of Theorem 1.5.



Put \kappa=\cond(V) and C=\psi_{\mathrm{cb}}(A). If C=1, the
conclusion is immediate. Otherwise, choose a minimal rectangular
extremal X\in M_{s,r}(\mathfrak{A}_{A}). As before,
(r,s) is one of (1,1),(1,2),(2,1).




Consider first a column X=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{q})^{\mathsf{T}}, where
q=1 or q=2, and put T_{j}=\theta_{A}(x_{j}). The class x_{j} has
well-defined values on the eigenvalues of A. Indeed, if
g\in\mathcal{I}_{A}, then





0=V^{-1}g(A)V=g(\Lambda),



so g vanishes at every diagonal entry of \Lambda. Let





N_{j}=V^{-1}T_{j}V.



The matrices N_{j} are diagonal. Lemma 2.2 gives,
for every \varepsilon>0, a column lift whose pointwise norm is at
most 1+\varepsilon. Evaluation at the eigenvalues and passage to the
limit give





\sum_{j=1}^{q}N_{j}^{*}N_{j}\preceq I.



Lemma 7.1 gives the required column square
function for the tuple (T_{j}). Theorem 4.4
therefore gives





\left\lVert(T_{1},\ldots,T_{q})^{\mathsf{T}}\right\rVert\leq\min\{\kappa,\Phi(\kappa)\}.



The row case follows by taking adjoints. Since the chosen extremal has
norm C, the displayed estimate proves
(1.12). The formula
(1.11) gives \Phi(\kappa)<2 for every finite
\kappa.
∎
