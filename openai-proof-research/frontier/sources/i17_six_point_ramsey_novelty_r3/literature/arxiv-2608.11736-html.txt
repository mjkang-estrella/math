One-point extensions of Euclidean Ramsey sets
Mostafa Mirabi
Abstract
Let XX be a finite Euclidean Ramsey set. We prove that adjoining any point outside the affine hull of XX gives another Euclidean Ramsey set, answering a conjecture of Ivan, Leader, and Walters. We first give an elementary product proof under the additional assumptions that the orthogonal projection of the new point lies in conv⁡(X)\operatorname{conv}(X) and that its distance from aff⁡(X)\operatorname{aff}(X) is sufficiently large. We then prove the general case by combining the product theorem for EE-Ramsey configurations and Kříž’s orbit-gluing theorem with a cyclic construction. No transitivity assumption on XX is needed.
1 Introduction
A finite set XX in a Euclidean space is called Ramsey if, for every positive integer kk, there is an integer NN such that every kk-colouring of ℝN\mathbb{R}^{N} contains a monochromatic isometric copy of XX. The problem of determining which finite Euclidean sets are Ramsey goes back to Erdős, Graham, Montgomery, Rothschild, Spencer and Straus [1].
Ivan, Leader and Walters [2] recently studied generalized prisms and obtained a number of new constructions of Ramsey sets. One consequence of their main result is that if XX is subsoluble, then adding a point outside the hyperplane containing XX again gives a Ramsey set. They asked whether subsolubility can be replaced simply by the assumption that XX is Ramsey. This is their Conjecture 8.
We prove the conjecture.
Theorem 1.1.
Let XX be a finite Ramsey set in a Euclidean space, and let zz be a point outside aff⁡(X)\operatorname{aff}(X). Then X∪{z}X\cup\{z\} is Ramsey.
The argument developed in two steps. The first is an elementary diagonal product construction. If the orthogonal projection of the new point lies in conv⁡(X)\operatorname{conv}(X), it proves the result once the height is sufficiently large. This is Proposition 2.1. The same argument also shows why the height cannot in general be made arbitrarily small by that particular construction.
The second step is the one that removes the restriction. We use Kříž’s language of EE-Ramsey configurations [3]. A cyclic product construction gives one-point extensions at heights
∥y−x0∥n,\frac{\lVert y-x_{0}\rVert}{\sqrt{n}},
which tend to zero, and Kříž’s orbit-gluing theorem makes the two required classes monochromatic. The usual product theorem then increases the height to any prescribed nonzero value.
Note. The argument here was obtained independently of the recent preprint of Moore [4]. The partial result in Proposition 2.1 and the proof of Theorem 1.1 were circulated privately to Ivan, Leader and Walters in June 2026. Moore’s preprint appeared on arXiv on August 10, 2026. His proof is different from the one given here.
2 A first product argument
We use the standard facts that the Ramsey property is invariant under nonzero scaling, is inherited by subsets, and is closed under finite Cartesian products [1].
Let
X={x1,…,xm}⊆ℝdX=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be Ramsey, and let y∈conv⁡(X)y\in\operatorname{conv}(X). Define
ρX(y)2=min{∑i=1mpi∥xi−y∥2:pi≥0,∑i=1mpi=1,∑i=1mpixi=y}.\rho_{X}(y)^{2}=\min\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\lVert x_{i}-y\rVert^{2}:p_{i}\geq 0,\ \sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}=1,\ \sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}x_{i}=y\right\}.
The admissible set is nonempty and compact, so the minimum exists.
Proposition 2.1.
Let X={x1,…,xm}⊆ℝdX=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d} be a finite Ramsey set, and let y∈conv⁡(X)y\in\operatorname{conv}(X). If λ≠0\lambda\neq 0 and
|λ|≥ρX​(y),|\lambda|\geq\rho_{X}(y),
then
(X×{0})∪{(y,λ)}⊆ℝd+1(X\times\{0\})\cup\{(y,\lambda)\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
is Ramsey.
Proof.
By reflecting in the last coordinate, it is enough to consider λ>0\lambda>0. Choose admissible weights p1,…,pmp_{1},\ldots,p_{m} such that
σ2=∑i=1mpi​∥xi−y∥2≤λ2.\sigma^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\lVert x_{i}-y\rVert^{2}\leq\lambda^{2}.
For each ii, the set pi​X\sqrt{p_{i}}X is Ramsey when pi>0p_{i}>0, while for pi=0p_{i}=0 it is the one-point set {0}\{0\}. Hence
Y=∏i=1mpi​XY=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\sqrt{p_{i}}X
is Ramsey.
Inside YY, define
D⁡(x)=(p1​x,…,pm​x),x∈X,D(x)=(\sqrt{p_{1}}x,\ldots,\sqrt{p_{m}}x),\qquad x\in X,
and
w=(p1​x1,…,pm​xm).w=(\sqrt{p_{1}}x_{1},\ldots,\sqrt{p_{m}}x_{m}).
For x,x′∈Xx,x^{\prime}\in X,
∥D⁡(x)−D⁡(x′)∥2=∑i=1mpi​∥x−x′∥2=∥x−x′∥2.\lVert D(x)-D(x^{\prime})\rVert^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\lVert x-x^{\prime}\rVert^{2}=\lVert x-x^{\prime}\rVert^{2}.
Thus D⁡(X)D(X) is an isometric copy of XX.
Also, for x∈Xx\in X,
∥D⁡(x)−w∥2\displaystyle\lVert D(x)-w\rVert^{2}
=∑i=1mpi​∥x−xi∥2\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\lVert x-x_{i}\rVert^{2}
=∑i=1mpi​∥(x−y)+(y−xi)∥2\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\lVert(x-y)+(y-x_{i})\rVert^{2}
=∥x−y∥2+2​⟨x−y,∑i=1mpi​(y−xi)⟩+∑i=1mpi​∥xi−y∥2\displaystyle=\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}+2\left\langle x-y,\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}(y-x_{i})\right\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\lVert x_{i}-y\rVert^{2}
=∥x−y∥2+σ2,\displaystyle=\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}+\sigma^{2},
since ∑ipi​xi=y\sum_{i}p_{i}x_{i}=y. Therefore
D⁡(X)∪{w}D(X)\cup\{w\}
is isometric to
(X×{0})∪{(y,σ)}.(X\times\{0\})\cup\{(y,\sigma)\}.
As a subset of the Ramsey set YY, it is Ramsey.
If λ=σ\lambda=\sigma, we are done. If λ>σ\lambda>\sigma, put
a=λ2−σ2.a=\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-\sigma^{2}}.
The two-point set {0,a}\{0,a\} is Ramsey, so Y×{0,a}Y\times\{0,a\} is Ramsey. It contains
{(D⁡(x),0):x∈X}∪{(w,a)},\{(D(x),0):x\in X\}\cup\{(w,a)\},
and for x∈Xx\in X,
∥(D⁡(x),0)−(w,a)∥2=∥x−y∥2+σ2+a2=∥x−y∥2+λ2.\lVert(D(x),0)-(w,a)\rVert^{2}=\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}+\sigma^{2}+a^{2}=\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}+\lambda^{2}.
Hence this subset is isometric to
(X×{0})∪{(y,λ)}.(X\times\{0\})\cup\{(y,\lambda)\}.
The case λ<0\lambda<0 follows by reflection.
∎
Remark 2.2.
The proposition does not by itself give arbitrary height. If y∈conv⁡(X)∖Xy\in\operatorname{conv}(X)\setminus X, then ρX​(y)>0\rho_{X}(y)>0. In fact, in any diagonal construction of the form used above, the barycentre condition forces the extra squared height to be
∑ipi​∥xi−y∥2.\sum_{i}p_{i}\lVert x_{i}-y\rVert^{2}.
Thus a different idea is needed to produce heights tending to zero.
3 EE-Ramsey configurations and the full theorem
We recall the part of Kříž’s machinery that we need. A finite subset of a Euclidean space will be called a configuration. Let FF be a configuration and let EE be an equivalence relation on FF. We say that FF is EE-Ramsey if, for every positive integer kk, there is an integer NN such that every kk-colouring of ℝN\mathbb{R}^{N} admits an isometric embedding φ:F→ℝN\varphi:F\to\mathbb{R}^{N} with
x​E​y⟹col⁡(φ⁡(x))=col⁡(φ⁡(y)).xEy\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\operatorname{col}(\varphi(x))=\operatorname{col}(\varphi(y)).
Ordinary Ramsey-ness is the special case in which EE has one equivalence class.
We use two results of Kříž [3, Theorems 3.2 and 4.1].
•
If F1F_{1} is E1E_{1}-Ramsey and F2F_{2} is E2E_{2}-Ramsey, then F1×F2F_{1}\times F_{2} is (E1×E2)(E_{1}\times E_{2})-Ramsey. The same holds for finite products.
•
Suppose FF is EE-Ramsey and b:F→Fb:F\to F is an isometry respecting EE. For z∈Fz\in F and r≥1r\geq 1, let U⁡(E,z,b,r)U(E;z,b,r) be the smallest equivalence relation containing EE in which
z,b​z,…,br−1​zz,bz,\ldots,b^{r-1}z
are equivalent. Then FF is U⁡(E,z,b,r)U(E;z,b,r)-Ramsey.
The next simple observation lets us add auxiliary points without imposing any colour condition on them.
Lemma 3.1.
Let X⊆ℝdX\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d} be a nonempty finite Ramsey set, and let C⊆ℝdC\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d} be finite with X⊆CX\subseteq C. Let ECE_{C} be the equivalence relation on CC whose equivalence classes are XX and the singleton sets {c}\{c\} for c∈C∖Xc\in C\setminus X. Then CC is ECE_{C}-Ramsey.
Proof.
Fix kk. Choose N0N_{0} such that every kk-colouring of ℝN0\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}} contains a monochromatic copy of XX. We claim that ℝN0+d\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}+d} witnesses that CC is ECE_{C}-Ramsey.
Take a kk-colouring of ℝN0+d\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}+d} and restrict it to the coordinate subspace ℝN0×{0}\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}}\times\{0\}. There is a monochromatic isometric copy φ⁡(X)\varphi(X) in this subspace. Fix x∗∈Xx_{*}\in X, and put
V=span⁡(X−x∗).V=\operatorname{span}(X-x_{*}).
Let
V′=span⁡(φ⁡(X)−φ⁡(x∗)).V^{\prime}=\operatorname{span}(\varphi(X)-\varphi(x_{*})).
The map φ\varphi extends to an affine isometry
T:x∗+V⟶φ⁡(x∗)+V′.T:x_{*}+V\longrightarrow\varphi(x_{*})+V^{\prime}.
Every c∈Cc\in C can be written uniquely as
c=x∗+p⁡(c)+q⁡(c),c=x_{*}+p(c)+q(c),
with p⁡(c)∈Vp(c)\in V and q⁡(c)∈V⟂q(c)\in V^{\perp}.
Since dimV′=dimV\dim V^{\prime}=\dim V, the space (V′)⟂⊆ℝN0+d(V^{\prime})^{\perp}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}+d} has dimension at least d−dimVd-\dim V. We may therefore choose a linear isometric embedding
J:V⟂⟶(V′)⟂.J:V^{\perp}\longrightarrow(V^{\prime})^{\perp}.
Define
Φ⁡(c)=T⁡(x∗+p⁡(c))+J⁡(q⁡(c)).\Phi(c)=T(x_{*}+p(c))+J(q(c)).
Differences coming from the two terms are orthogonal, so Φ\Phi is an isometric embedding of CC. For x∈Xx\in X we have q⁡(x)=0q(x)=0, hence Φ⁡(x)=φ⁡(x)\Phi(x)=\varphi(x). Thus the whole ECE_{C}-class XX is monochromatic, while the remaining classes are singletons. This proves the lemma.
∎
We now prove the coordinate form of Theorem 1.1.
Theorem 3.2.
Let X⊆ℝdX\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d} be a nonempty finite Ramsey set. Let y∈ℝdy\in\mathbb{R}^{d} and let λ∈ℝ∖{0}\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}. Then
Z=(X×{0})∪{(y,λ)}⊆ℝd+1Z=(X\times\{0\})\cup\{(y,\lambda)\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
is Ramsey.
Proof.
By reflection in the last coordinate, it is enough to treat λ>0\lambda>0.
First suppose that y∈Xy\in X. Then ZZ is a subset of
X×{0,λ}.X\times\{0,\lambda\}.
The two-point set {0,λ}\{0,\lambda\} is Ramsey, so the product theorem shows that X×{0,λ}X\times\{0,\lambda\} is Ramsey. Hence ZZ is Ramsey.
Now assume y∉Xy\notin X, and fix x0∈Xx_{0}\in X. For an integer n≥1n\geq 1, define
ai=(1−in)x0+iny,i=0,1,…,n.a_{i}=\left(1-\frac{i}{n}\right)x_{0}+\frac{i}{n}y,\qquad i=0,1,\ldots,n.
Thus a0=x0a_{0}=x_{0} and an=ya_{n}=y. Put
Cn=X∪{a0,a1,…,an},C_{n}=X\cup\{a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\},
and let EnE_{n} be the equivalence relation on CnC_{n} whose only non-singleton class is XX. By Lemma 3.1, CnC_{n} is EnE_{n}-Ramsey. The product theorem therefore gives that
Cnn+1C_{n}^{n+1}
is Enn+1E_{n}^{n+1}-Ramsey, where the equivalence relation is taken coordinatewise.
Inside Cnn+1C_{n}^{n+1}, define
D⁡(x)=(x,a1,a2,…,an),D(x)=(x,a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}), for x∈X.x\in X.
Let bb be the cyclic coordinate shift
b⁡(u0,u1,…,un)=(un,u0,u1,…,un−1),b(u_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})=(u_{n},u_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{n-1}),
and set
Fn=⋃r=0nbr​D​(X).F_{n}=\bigcup_{r=0}^{n}b^{r}D(X).
Let EE be the restriction of Enn+1E_{n}^{n+1} to FnF_{n}. Since the EE-Ramsey property is inherited by subsets, FnF_{n} is EE-Ramsey. The map bb restricts to an isometry of FnF_{n} and respects EE.
Set
z0=D⁡(x0)=(x0,a1,…,an).z_{0}=D(x_{0})=(x_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}).
By Kříž’s orbit-gluing theorem with r=2r=2, the configuration FnF_{n} is U⁡(E,z0,b,2)U(E;z_{0},b,2)-Ramsey. The EE-class of z0z_{0} contains
D⁡(X)={(x,a1,…,an):x∈X},D(X)=\{(x,a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}):x\in X\},
and
b​z0=(an,a0,a1,…,an−1)=(y,x0,a1,…,an−1).bz_{0}=(a_{n},a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1})=(y,x_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}).
The relation U⁡(E,z0,b,2)U(E;z_{0},b,2) merges the EE-classes of z0z_{0} and b​z0bz_{0}. It follows that
Sn=D⁡(X)∪{b​z0}S_{n}=D(X)\cup\{bz_{0}\}
is Ramsey.
It remains only to compute the geometry of SnS_{n}. For x,x′∈Xx,x^{\prime}\in X,
∥D⁡(x)−D⁡(x′)∥2=∥x−x′∥2.\lVert D(x)-D(x^{\prime})\rVert^{2}=\lVert x-x^{\prime}\rVert^{2}.
Also, for x∈Xx\in X,
∥D⁡(x)−b​z0∥2\displaystyle\lVert D(x)-bz_{0}\rVert^{2}
=∥x−y∥2+∑i=1n∥ai−ai−1∥2.\displaystyle=\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert a_{i}-a_{i-1}\rVert^{2}.
But
ai−ai−1=y−x0n,a_{i}-a_{i-1}=\frac{y-x_{0}}{n},
so
∑i=1n∥ai−ai−1∥2=∥y−x0∥2n.\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert a_{i}-a_{i-1}\rVert^{2}=\frac{\lVert y-x_{0}\rVert^{2}}{n}.
Therefore SnS_{n} is isometric to
(X×{0})∪{(y,∥y−x0∥n)}.(X\times\{0\})\cup\left\{\left(y,\frac{\lVert y-x_{0}\rVert}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right\}.
Write
hn=∥y−x0∥n.h_{n}=\frac{\lVert y-x_{0}\rVert}{\sqrt{n}}.
Then hn→0h_{n}\to 0. Choose nn so large that hn≤λh_{n}\leq\lambda. If hn=λh_{n}=\lambda, we are done. Otherwise let
t=λ2−hn2.t=\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-h_{n}^{2}}.
The product of the Ramsey set
(X×{0})∪{(y,hn)}(X\times\{0\})\cup\{(y,h_{n})\}
with the Ramsey two-point set {0,t}\{0,t\} is Ramsey. It contains
{(x,0,0):x∈X}∪{(y,hn,t)},\{(x,0,0):x\in X\}\cup\{(y,h_{n},t)\},
which is isometric to
(X×{0})∪{(y,λ)}(X\times\{0\})\cup\{(y,\lambda)\}
because hn2+t2=λ2h_{n}^{2}+t^{2}=\lambda^{2}. This finishes the proof.
∎
Proof of Theorem 1.1.
Work in the affine span of X∪{z}X\cup\{z\}. After an isometry, we may identify aff⁡(X)\operatorname{aff}(X) with ℝd×{0}\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{0\} and write
z=(y,λ)z=(y,\lambda)
with y∈ℝdy\in\mathbb{R}^{d} and λ≠0\lambda\neq 0. The result is then exactly Theorem 3.2.
∎
Remark 3.3.
The role of Kříž’s theorem is quite specific. The cyclic construction by itself produces several copies of XX arranged around a product, but there is no transitive group available because XX is assumed only to be Ramsey. The EE-Ramsey formulation lets us keep XX as one colour class, leave all auxiliary points unconstrained, and then glue two consecutive classes along the cyclic shift. This replaces the transitivity assumption used in the generalized-prism construction of [2].
