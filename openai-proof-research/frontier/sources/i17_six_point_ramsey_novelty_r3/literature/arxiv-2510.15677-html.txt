Nearly all known Euclidean Ramsey sets are subsoluble
Natalie Behague
Abstract
A finite set XX in a Euclidean space ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} is called Ramsey if for every kk there exists an integer nn such that whenever ℝn\mathbb{R}^{n} is coloured with kk colours, there is a monochromatic copy of XX. Graham conjectured that all spherical sets are Ramsey, but progress on this conjecture has been slow. A key result of Kříž is that all sets that embed in sets that are acted on transitively by a soluble group are Ramsey. We show that for nearly all known examples of Ramsey sets the converse is true, with only two possible exceptions.
1 Introduction
A finite set XX in a Euclidean space ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} is called Ramsey if for every kk there exists an integer nn such that whenever ℝn\mathbb{R}^{n} is coloured with kk colours, there is a monochromatic isometric copy of XX. An early paper [3] in the study of Euclidean Ramsey theory proved that every Ramsey set is spherical, that is, it lies on the surface of some dd-dimensional sphere. Graham conjectured that the converse holds.
Conjecture 1.1 ([6]).
Every spherical set is Ramsey.
This conjecture is considered the most important in Euclidean Ramsey theory and yet progress has been slow. It is known that rectangular parallelepipeds (a.k.a. bricks) [3], triangles [5], (non-degenerate) simplices [4], isosceles trapezia (a.k.a. isosceles trapezoids)11
1
an isosceles trapezium, also called an isosceles trapezoid, is a quadrilateral with one pair of parallel sides and the other pair of sides equal in length. [10] and all regular polytopes [9, 2] are Ramsey.
A key breakthrough, which was critical in proving that all regular polytopes are Ramsey, is the following result of Kříž.
Theorem 1.2 ([9]).
Let X⊂ℝdX\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} be a finite transitive set. If there is a soluble group GG that acts transitively on XX then XX is Ramsey.
(Kříž actually also proved the stronger result that if there is a soluble group GG that acts on XX with at most two orbits, then XX is Ramsey – we will return to this point at the end of the paper.)
Inspired by Theorem 1.2, we make the following definitions.
Definition 1.
We say a finite set X⊂ℝdX\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} is
1.
soluble if there is a soluble group that acts transitively on XX, and
2.
subsoluble if there is a soluble set YY containing a copy of XX.
Note that YY can lie in a higher-dimensional space than XX.
By Theorem 1.2, we know that all soluble sets are Ramsey, and as a consequence all subsoluble sets are Ramsey.
In this paper we will explore the converse, and ask whether there are any Ramsey sets that are not subsoluble. For example, it is easy to observe that rectangular parallelepipeds are soluble (see Section 2.2). Karamanlis [8] generalised the proof of Frankl and Rödl that all simplices are Ramsey to obtain the following result.
Theorem 1.3 ([8]).
Every (non-degenerate) simplex embeds into a regular polygonal torus, and is therefore subsoluble.
Inspired by the known proofs in Euclidean Ramsey theory, Leader, Russell and Walters [11] proposed a ‘rival’ conjecture to Graham’s.
Conjecture 1.4 ([11]).
A set X⊂ℝdX\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} is Ramsey if and only if it is (isometric to) a subset of a finite transitive set.
Leader, Russell and Walters [11] showed that there exist cyclic quadrilaterals that are spherical but not a subset of any finite transitive set, and therefore the two conjectures are distinct.
To make progress on Conjecture 1.4, Leader, Russell and Walters [11] gave a slightly stronger but purely combinatorial reframing called the Block Sets Conjecture which has a similar statement to the famous Hales-Jewett theorem. They proved the first non-trivial case of the Block Sets conjecture and noted that as a consequence, for any α,β,γ∈ℝ\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R} and i,j∈ℕi,j\in\mathbb{N}, the set X⊂ℝi+j+1X\subset\mathbb{R}^{i+j+1} consisting of permutations of the coordinates (α,…,α⏟i,β,…,β⏟j,γ)(\underbrace{\alpha,\ldots,\alpha}_{i},\underbrace{\beta,\ldots,\beta}_{j},\gamma) is Ramsey. They observe that if α,β,γ\alpha,\beta,\gamma are algebraically independent, these sets are not themselves soluble and speculate that they may therefore be genuinely new Ramsey sets that cannot be found as a consequence of Theorem 1.2.
Perhaps surprisingly, we show that these sets, while not themselves soluble, do embed into soluble sets. Our methods actually extend to almost every known Ramsey set, with at most two exceptions.
Theorem 1.5.
The following sets are subsoluble:
1.
The set of permutations of (α,…,α⏟i,β,…,β⏟j,γ)(\underbrace{\alpha,\ldots,\alpha}_{i},\underbrace{\beta,\ldots,\beta}_{j},\gamma)
for any α,β,γ∈ℝ\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R} and i,j≥0i,j\geq 0,
2.
all regular polytopes except for possibly the 120-cell and 600-cell, and
3.
isosceles trapezia.
In case (1) we can also prove a more general result. A proof of the next two open cases of the Block Sets Conjecture would show that the set of permutations of the coordinates (α,…,α⏟i,β,…,β⏟j,γ,γ)(\underbrace{\alpha,\ldots,\alpha}_{i},\underbrace{\beta,\ldots,\beta}_{j},\gamma,\gamma) and the set of permutations of (α,…,α⏟i,β,…,β⏟j,γ,δ)(\underbrace{\alpha,\ldots,\alpha}_{i},\underbrace{\beta,\ldots,\beta}_{j},\gamma,\delta) are both Ramsey for all α,β,γ,δ∈ℝ\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in\mathbb{R}, i,j≥0i,j\geq 0. These sets were not previously known to be Ramsey, but we are able to prove that they are in fact subsoluble.
Theorem 1.6.
Let α,β,γ,δ∈ℝ\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in\mathbb{R} (not necessarily distinct), and let i,j≥0i,j\geq 0. The following sets are subsoluble and therefore Ramsey:
{permutations of ​(α,…,α⏟i,β,…,β⏟j)},\displaystyle\{\text{permutations of }(\underbrace{\alpha,\ldots,\alpha}_{i},\underbrace{\beta,\ldots,\beta}_{j})\},~~
{permutations of ​(α,…,α⏟i,β,…,β⏟j,γ)},\displaystyle\{\text{permutations of }(\underbrace{\alpha,\ldots,\alpha}_{i},\underbrace{\beta,\ldots,\beta}_{j},\gamma)\},
{permutations of α,…,α⏟i,β,…,β⏟j,γ,γ)},\displaystyle\{\text{permutations of }\underbrace{\alpha,\ldots,\alpha}_{i},\underbrace{\beta,\ldots,\beta}_{j},\gamma,\gamma)\},~~
{permutations of ​(α,…,α⏟i,β,…,β⏟j,γ,δ)}.\displaystyle\{\text{permutations of }(\underbrace{\alpha,\ldots,\alpha}_{i},\underbrace{\beta,\ldots,\beta}_{j},\gamma,\delta)\}.
Unfortunately these results do not go backwards to prove the corresponding cases of the Block Sets Conjecture.
1.1 Paper Outline
We begin with some basic preliminaries in Section 2. We then prove Theorem 1.6 in Section 3.
Section 4 contains the proof that nearly all regular polytopes are subsoluble, bar two exceptions. It is easy to check that regular kk-gons and dd-dimensional regular simplices, cubes and orthoplexes are soluble (acted on by CkC_{k}, CdC_{d}, C2dC_{2}^{d} and C2×CdC_{2}\times C_{d} respectively). The non-trivial cases are the icosahedron and dodecahedron with the dodecahedron being the most difficult.
We give the construction of a soluble set containing an arbitrary isosceles trapezium in Section 5.
Finally, we finish with several open questions in Section 6.

2.2 Direct products are subsoluble
The following simple lemma will be useful later.
Lemma 2.1.
If X,YX,Y are subsoluble then so is the direct product X×YX\times Y.
Proof.
As XX is subsoluble, there exists a finite set X′X^{\prime} containing XX with soluble transitive group action GG. Similarly, there exists a finite set Y′Y^{\prime} containing YY with soluble transitive group action HH. Clearly X′×Y′X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime} is a finite set containing X×YX\times Y and G×HG\times H is soluble. Moreover, G×HG\times H acts transitively on X′×Y′X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime} via the natural action (g,h)​(x,y)=(g⁡(x),h⁡(y))(g,h)(x,y)=(g(x),h(y)) for g∈Gg\in G, h∈Hh\in H, x∈X′x\in X^{\prime} and y∈Y′y\in Y^{\prime}.
∎
Note that as an immediate corollary we have that rectangular parallelepipeds are subsoluble.

4 Nearly all regular polytopes are subsoluble
In this section we prove that all regular polytopes are subsoluble except for possibly two.
Theorem 4.1.
The vertex sets of all regular polytopes are subsoluble, except for possibly the 120120-cell and the 600600-cell.
In particular, the following regular polytopes have soluble symmetry groups:
•
In 22 dimensions: all regular polygons,
•
In 33 dimensions: the tetrahedron, cube and octahedron,
•
In 44 dimensions: the 8-cell (a.k.a. 4-cube), the 16-cell (a.k.a. 4-orthoplex) and the 24-cell;
there is a transitive soluble group action on the vertex sets of the following remaining regular polytopes:
•
In 33 dimensions: the icosahedron,
•
In 44 dimensions: the 5-cell (a.k.a. regular 4-simplex),
•
In d≥5d\geq 5 dimensions: the regular dd-simplex, the dd-cube and the dd-orthoplex;
and finally, there is a soluble set YY containing a copy of the vertex set of the dodecahedron.

Now consider the icosahedron. Colour 8 of the faces of the icosahedron red such that the centres of those faces form a cube, as in Figure 1. We will consider the symmetries of the icosahedron with respect to this colouring: that is, those symmetries that map red faces to red faces. This is the set of symmetries of the pyritohedral icosahedron, which is the pyritohedral group Th≅A4×C2T_{h}\cong A_{4}\times C_{2}. This group is clearly soluble. It is straightforward to check that the icosahedron is vertex-transitive under these symmetries. Each vertex is on the boundary of a red face. A series of reflections can send each red face to any other red face, and rotations by π/3\pi/3 or 2​π/32\pi/3 about the line through the centre of a red face will send a vertex to the other two vertices of the face.

5 Isosceles trapezia are subsoluble
In this section we prove that isosceles trapezia are subsoluble. This proof is inspired by an observation of Leader, Russell and Walters [11] that an arbitrary triangle lies in the vertex set of some twisted prism with a kk-gon base. Since such a twisted prism is acted on transitively by the soluble group D2​k×C2D_{2k}\times C_{2} and all triangles are therefore subsoluble. This same proof was also explained in greater detail in [7].
We show that a similar property holds for isosceles trapezia.
Theorem 5.1.
All isosceles trapezia are subsoluble.
Proof.
Let A​B​C​DABCD be an isosceles trapezium such that the edges A​DAD and B​CBC are parallel, and the edges A​BAB and C​DCD are the same length. Note that all isosceles trapezia are cyclic quadrilaterals. Let 𝒞\mathcal{C} be the circle circumscribing A​B​C​DABCD with centre OO.
Claim 1.
Suppose angle ∠​A​O​B\angle AOB is equal to 2​π/k2\pi/k for some integer kk. Then there exists a finite set Y⊆𝒞Y\subseteq\mathcal{C} containing the vertices A,B,C,DA,B,C,D such that there is a soluble transitive group action on YY.
Proof of Claim.
Since ∠​A​O​B\angle AOB is equal to 2​π/k2\pi/k for some integer kk, there exists a regular kk-gon X⊆𝒞X\subseteq\mathcal{C} containing AA and BB. Reflect XX in the perpendicular bisector of A​DAD to get a regular kk-gon X′X^{\prime} containing CC and DD. Note that the perpendicular bisector of A​DAD passes through OO and so X′⊆𝒞X^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{C}. Let Y=X∪X′Y=X\cup X^{\prime}. Clearly by construction YY contains A,B,C,DA,B,C,D.
Let GG be the group generated by a rotation rr about OO by 2​π/k2\pi/k and the reflection ss in the perpendicular bisector of A​DAD. Clearly GG acts transitively on YY. As GG is isomorphic to the dihedral group D2​kD_{2k}, it is also soluble.
∎
Now suppose that angle ∠​A​O​B\angle AOB is not equal to 2​π/k2\pi/k for any integer kk. Call the line through BB perpendicular to A​DAD the altitude from BB, and similarly for CC.
Consider the isosceles trapezia obtained by moving BB and CC towards A​DAD along their respective altitudes, calling the resulting vertices B′,C′B^{\prime},C^{\prime}. At the limit, as B,CB,C get arbitrarily close to A​DAD, the angle ∠​A​O′​B′\angle AO^{\prime}B^{\prime} gets arbitrarily small. Therefore there must be some choice of B′,C′B^{\prime},C^{\prime} where ∠​A​O′​B′\angle AO^{\prime}B^{\prime} is 2​π/k2\pi/k for some integer kk. Fix this choice of B′B^{\prime} and C′C^{\prime}.
By the claim, there is a soluble set YY containing A​B′​C′​DAB^{\prime}C^{\prime}D that is contained in the circle 𝒞′\mathcal{C}^{\prime} containing A​B′​C′​DAB^{\prime}C^{\prime}D. Working now in 33 dimensions, consider the set Z=Y×{−x,x}Z=Y\times\{-x,x\}, where xx satisfies (2​x)2+|A​B′|2=|A​B|2(2x)^{2}+|AB^{\prime}|^{2}=|AB|^{2}. Note that since YY contains A​B′​C′​DAB^{\prime}C^{\prime}D then the set ZZ contains an isometric copy of A​B​C​DABCD. Moreover, letting GG be the soluble group that acts transitively on YY, then extending GG by the reflection in the x​yxy-axis gives a soluble group that acts transitively on ZZ.
∎
6 Open questions
By Kříž’s arguments, we know that if a set XX is a subset of a transitive set YY with a soluble group action then XX is Ramsey. We have observed that in nearly all known examples of Ramsey sets, the converse is true. We ask if the converse holds in general.
Question 6.1.
Let X⊆ℝdX\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d} be Ramsey. Must there exist a soluble set YY containing a copy of XX?
Kříž in fact proved the stronger result that if XX is a finite set with some soluble group acting on XX with at most two orbits, then XX is Ramsey.
The 120120-cell and 600600-cell were shown to be Ramsey by repeated application of this stronger result [2]. In particular, answering the following question would resolve Question 6.1 for all sets currently known to be Ramsey.
Question 6.2.
Let XX be a transitive set and suppose that some soluble group acts on XX with two orbits. Does there exist a soluble set YY containing XX?
We have shown in Theorem 4.1 that nearly all regular polytopes are subsoluble. It would be particularly interesting to determine whether the two remaining regular polytopes are subsoluble.
Question 6.3.
Does the vertex set of a regular polytope always embed within some soluble set YY? In particular, does this hold for the 120120-cell and the 600600-cell?
Since the vertex set of the 600600-cell is contained within the vertex set of the 120120-cell, it would be enough to answer Question 6.3 for the 120120-cell. A first attempt (for either case) is to try to duplicate the proof that the dodecahedron is subsoluble: that is, to fix a particular inscribed 4-dimensional regular polytope QQ and consider the group GG of automorphisms of XX that map QQ to itself. However, in all cases one of the following conditions necessary to apply Lemma 4.2 fails to hold: either GG is not soluble, the action of GG on XX has more than two orbits, or |A​u​t​(X)|​|Q||G|​|X|>2\frac{|Aut(X)||Q|}{|G||X|}>2. Thus a new idea is required to answer Question 6.3.
We showed in Theorem 1.6 that sets containing all permutations of certain sets of coordinates are subsoluble. We wonder whether it is possible to extend this result further.
Question 6.4.
Let α1,α2,…,αs∈ℝ\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{s}\in\mathbb{R} (not necessarily distinct), and let i1,i2,…,is≥0i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{s}\geq 0.
Let X⁡(i1,i2,…,is)⊂ℝi1+i2+…+isX(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{s})\subset\mathbb{R}^{i_{1}+i_{2}+\ldots+i_{s}} be the set containing permutations of the coordinates
(α1,…,α1⏟i1,α2,…,α2⏟i2,…,αs,…,αs⏟is).(\underbrace{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{1}}_{i_{1}},\underbrace{\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{2}}_{i_{2}},\ldots,\underbrace{\alpha_{s},\ldots,\alpha_{s}}_{i_{s}}).
For which values of i1,…,isi_{1},\ldots,i_{s} is the set X⁡(i1,…,is)X(i_{1},\ldots,i_{s}) subsoluble for any choice of α1,…,αs\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{s}?
In particular, is X⁡(1,1,1,1,1)X(1,1,1,1,1) subsoluble for all α1,α2,…,α5\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{5}?
These sets arise from block sets in a natural way, and a positive answer to the Block Set Conjecture [11] would imply that X⁡(i1,i2,…,is)X(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{s}) is always Ramsey. However, neither result seems to directly imply the other - we do not know whether being Ramsey implies subsoluble (this is Question 6.1), and although we have proved that X⁡(i,j,1,1)X(i,j,1,1) is always Ramsey we have been unable to deduce anything about the corresponding block sets.
Our approach in proving Theorem 1.6 cannot be directly extended to any other patterns without a new idea. We use A​G​L​(1,p)AGL(1,p) in a crucial way within the proof as a group that is both soluble and is 22-transitive on ℤp\mathbb{Z}_{p}: that is, any pair of elements of ℤp\mathbb{Z}_{p} can be sent to any other under the action of A​G​L​(1,p)AGL(1,p). To extend the proof to new X⁡(i1,i2,…,is)X(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{s}) we would need to find a soluble group acting on {1,2,…,n}\{1,2,\ldots,n\} for some n≥5n\geq 5 whose action is 33-transitive or higher. However, one can use the Classification of Finite Simple Groups to show that such groups do not exist. A list of all multiply transitive groups can be found in Tables 7.3 and 7.4 of [1], and it is a matter of routine to check that among these there is no soluble group which acts 33-transitively on a set of size ≥5\geq 5.
Clearly, a positive answer to Question 6.1 would immediately imply one direction of Conjecture 1.4 that every Ramsey set is a subset of a transitive set. To determine whether a negative answer
to Question 6.1 would contradict the conjecture, we would need an answer to
the following question.
Question 6.5.
Is every transitive set subsoluble?
