Abstract
Let P⊂ℝdP\subset\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}^{d} be a finite spherical set.
We prove that if PP admits a solvable group of isometries acting transitively on it, then every rr-coloring of a sufficiently high-dimensional sphere of radius slightly larger than the circumradius of PP contains a monochromatic congruent copy of PP.
Our proof builds on the group-theoretic argument of Kříž and combines it with a topological method that may be of independent interest.
Keywords.
Euclidean Ramsey theory; transitive configuration; solvable group; spherical Ramsey theorem; nearcircumsphere-Ramsey; ncs-Ramsey; Kneser-shift graph; Tucker lemma.

1 Introduction
Euclidean Ramsey theory was started by Erdős, Graham, Montgomery, Rothschild, Spencer, and Straus in a series of three papers just over half a century ago [7, 8, 9].
Its central question is to determine for which point sets it is true that every finite coloring of a sufficiently high-dimensional Euclidean space contains a monochromatic (congruent) copy of the set.
More precisely, a finite set of points P⊂ℝdP\subset\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}^{d} is called Ramsey if for every r∈ℕr\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$} there is a dimension nn such that every rr-coloring of ℝn\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}^{n} contains a monochromatic set congruent to PP, and PP is called spherical if there is a sphere that contains PP.
They proved that every Ramsey set is spherical and conjectured that this necessary condition is also sufficient.
Using a product theorem, they showed that several classes of spherical sets, including all bricks, are Ramsey.
Later, Frankl and Rödl [15, 16] established that every simplex is also Ramsey.
For more results, see Graham’s survey [23]; here we will only focus on results that are directly related to the topic of our paper.
Call P⊂ℝdP\subset\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}^{d} transitive if its isometry group acts on it transitively, i.e., for any p,q∈Pp,q\in P there is an isometry of ℝd\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}^{d} that takes PP to PP and pp to qq.
Moreover, PP is solvable transitive if there is a solvable group of isometries of ℝd\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}^{d} that preserves PP and acts transitively on it.
For example, all regular polygons are solvable transitive, and so are all regular simplices, as some cyclic group acts on them transitively, while the vertex set of the regular dodecahedron is transitive but not solvable transitive.
Every transitive set is spherical, but the converse is not true in general [32, 33].
Kříž [31] showed that solvable transitive sets are even Ramsey; in fact, he proved the stronger statement that it is enough for a set to admit a solvable isometry group with at most two orbits, which is the case for the dodecahedron.
Therefore, he established that the vertex sets of all regular nn-gons and Platonic solids are Ramsey; later Cantwell [4] proved the same for every regular polytope.
A set P⊂ℝdP\subset\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}^{d} is (solvable) subtransitive if P⊂P′P\subset P^{\prime} for some (solvable) transitive set P′P^{\prime} in a possibly higher-dimensional space.
For example, any acute triangle is solvable subtransitive because it embeds in a three-dimensional brick.
As every subset of a Ramsey set is Ramsey, all solvable subtransitive sets are Ramsey, and Leader, Russell, and Walters [33] conjectured that Ramsey sets are precisely the subtransitive sets.
Behague [3] recently showed that, with only two possible exceptions, all previously known Ramsey configurations are solvable subtransitive in this sense.
For a spherical set P⊂ℝdP\subset\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}^{d}, there is a unique point o∈aff⁡Po\in\operatorname{aff}P and a unique ρ≥0\rho\geq 0 such that ‖p−o‖=ρ\|p-o\|=\rho for every p∈Pp\in P.
We call oo the circumcenter, ρ\rho the circumradius, and 𝕊ρd−1​(o)={x∈ℝd:‖x−o‖=ρ}\mbox{$\mathbb{S}$}_{\rho}^{d-1}(o)=\{x\in\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}^{d}:\|x-o\|=\rho\} the circumsphere of PP.
Without loss of generality, we will assume that oo is the origin and use the notation 𝕊Rn={x∈ℝn+1:‖x‖=R}\mbox{$\mathbb{S}$}_{R}^{n}=\{x\in\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}^{n+1}:\|x\|=R\}.
Now we will define various versions of sphere-Ramseyness.
Definition 1.
Let PP be a spherical set with circumradius ρ\rho.
PP is sphere-Ramsey if for every r∈ℕr\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$} there are an R>0R>0 and an n∈ℕn\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$} such that every rr-coloring of 𝕊Rn\mbox{$\mathbb{S}$}_{R}^{n} contains a monochromatic copy of PP.
PP is circumsphere-Ramsey if for every r∈ℕr\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$} there is an n∈ℕn\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$} such that every rr-coloring of 𝕊ρn\mbox{$\mathbb{S}$}_{\rho}^{n} contains a monochromatic copy of PP.
PP is nearcircumsphere-Ramsey, abbreviated ncs-Ramsey, if for every r∈ℕr\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$} and every ε>0\varepsilon>0 there is an n∈ℕn\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$} such that every rr-coloring of 𝕊ρ+εn\mbox{$\mathbb{S}$}_{\rho+\varepsilon}^{n} contains a monochromatic copy of PP.
The first version of these definitions appeared in Graham [20], who used the term sphere-Ramsey for what we call circumsphere-Ramsey and pioneered its study [21, 22].
The terminology later shifted, and sphere-Ramsey came to mean the weaker property defined above, leading to some confusion; the distinction is blurred even in Graham’s last survey [23].
By embedding a sphere as a subsphere of a larger sphere of one higher dimension, we obtain the chain of implications
circumsphere-Ramsey⟹ncs-Ramsey⟹sphere-Ramsey⟹Ramsey\text{circumsphere-Ramsey}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{ncs-Ramsey}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{sphere-Ramsey}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{Ramsey}
Some of these implications may also hold in the reverse direction.
In fact, it is possible that the last three notions are all equivalent; according to Reiher [45], Graham conjectured that all spherical sets are ncs-Ramsey.
We have not found this conjecture stated in Graham’s papers, except for the special case of bricks, which was later settled by Frankl and Rödl [16].
However, circumsphere-Ramseyness is substantially different: a pair of antipodal points is not circumsphere-Ramsey.
A necessary condition for circumsphere-Ramseyness, based on Rado’s theorem about partition regularity over the nonzero reals [43], was proved by Graham [20].
Graham [20] and Lovász [35] independently proved that every two-point set is ncs-Ramsey.
Equivalently, every pair of points at distance less than 22 is Ramsey on a sufficiently high-dimensional unit sphere.
Raigorodskii [44], apparently aware only of Lovász’s topological proof, later rediscovered Graham’s linear algebra argument based on the Frankl–Wilson theorem [17].
Frankl and Rödl [16] also used linear algebra to prove that every simplex is sphere-Ramsey.
Matoušek and Rödl [36], using a Banach-space argument based on Krivine’s theorem, strengthened this by proving that every simplex is ncs-Ramsey.
Several of these papers establish still stronger conclusions, but we keep the presentation minimal and do not introduce notions such as super-Ramsey, hyper-Ramsey, or PP-Ramsey.
One more, geometric strengthening is the notion of a diameter-Ramsey configuration, for which the finite Ramsey witness is required to have the same diameter as the target; see Frankl, Pach, Reiher, and Rödl [14].
The proof of Kříž [31] also implies that all solvable transitive sets are sphere-Ramsey.
The main result of our paper is to improve this to ncs-Ramsey, making an important step towards what Reiher called “Graham’s radius conjecture”.
Theorem 2.
If PP is solvable transitive, then PP is nearcircumsphere-Ramsey.
More explicitly, if PP is a finite spherical set with circumradius ρ\rho and admits a solvable group of isometries that acts transitively on PP, then for every r∈ℕr\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$} and every ε>0\varepsilon>0 there is a dimension nn such that every rr-coloring of 𝕊ρ+εn\mbox{$\mathbb{S}$}_{\rho+\varepsilon}^{n} contains a monochromatic congruent copy of PP.
Note that Theorem 2 does not automatically imply the same conclusion for solvable subtransitive sets PP, because this would require a solvable transitive set containing PP with circumradius only slightly larger than ρ\rho.
This was implicitly established for simplices by Matoušek and Rödl [36], and for triangles an explicit, short proof can be found in Leader, Russell, and Walters [33], but we leave it as an open problem whether such a (solvable) transitive set exists for every (solvable) subtransitive set.
Apart from the trivial one-point case, the conclusion of Theorem 2 cannot be strengthened by replacing ρ+ε\rho+\varepsilon with ρ\rho: no transitive spherical configuration of circumradius ρ\rho is circumsphere-Ramsey.
Indeed, color every sphere by the sign of the first nonzero coordinate.
The sum of finitely many vectors of either color has the same lexicographic sign, so neither color class contains a finite set with barycenter zero.
On the other hand, the barycenter of a transitive configuration is its circumcenter.
If a copy QQ lies on a sphere of radius ρ\rho centered at the origin and has barycenter bb, then
ρ2=1|Q|​∑q∈Q‖q−b‖2=1|Q|​∑q∈Q‖q‖2−‖b‖2=ρ2−‖b‖2,\rho^{2}=\frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{q\in Q}\|q-b\|^{2}=\frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{q\in Q}\|q\|^{2}-\|b\|^{2}=\rho^{2}-\|b\|^{2},
so b=0b=0, a contradiction.
Thus Theorem 2 is sharp in the radius for solvable transitive configurations: every strictly larger prescribed radius works, while the circumradius itself does not.
Theorem 2 also implies the following special case.
Corollary 3.
Every regular polygon is ncs-Ramsey.
Our proof combines topological methods with the original argument of Kříž, though most of our notation is closer to that used in later works [28, 33].
We use the topological machinery to establish a combinatorial result that can be of independent interest, giving a common generalization to shift graphs and Kneser graphs.
Now we turn our attention to this topic.
The vertices of a shift graph Shp​(n)\mathrm{Sh}_{p}(n), defined by Erdős and Hajnal [10], are the increasing (p−1)(p-1)-tuples of [n][n], and its edges are (v1,…,vp−1)​(v2,…,vp)(v_{1},\ldots,v_{p-1})(v_{2},\ldots,v_{p}) for every v1<⋯<vpv_{1}<\cdots<v_{p}.
They showed that shift graphs have unbounded chromatic number: χ​(Shp​(n))→∞\chi(\mathrm{Sh}_{p}(n))\to\infty as n→∞n\to\infty for every fixed pp, roughly as a (p−2)(p-2)-times iterated logarithm of nn.
The vertices of a Kneser graph KG⁡(n,k)\mathrm{KG}(n,k) are the kk-subsets of [n][n], denoted by ([n]k)\binom{[n]}{k}, and two vertices A,BA,B are adjacent when A∩B=∅A\cap B=\emptyset.
Lovász [34] famously proved, using topological methods, Kneser’s conjecture, that χ⁡(KG⁡(n,k))=n−2​k+2\chi(\mathrm{KG}(n,k))=n-2k+2 for all n≥2​kn\geq 2k.
In particular, χ⁡(KG⁡(n,k))→∞\chi(\mathrm{KG}(n,k))\to\infty as n−2​k→∞n-2k\to\infty.

Interestingly, a slightly weaker version of this statement was proved earlier by Szemerédi using a theorem of Kleitman [30]; he showed that χ⁡(KG⁡(⌈(2+ε)​k⌉,k))→∞\chi\bigl(\mathrm{KG}(\lceil(2+\varepsilon)k\rceil,k)\bigr)\to\infty as k→∞k\to\infty for any ε>0\varepsilon>0 [11, Lemma 4].

The vertices of a pp-uniform Kneser hypergraph KG(p)​(n,k)\mathrm{KG}^{(p)}(n,k) are also ([n]k)\binom{[n]}{k}, and its hyperedges are the sets {A1,…,Ap}\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{p}\} of pairwise disjoint vertices.
Alon, Frankl, and Lovász [1] generalized Lovász’s theorem by proving χ⁡(KG(p)​(n,k))=⌈n−p⁡(k−1)p−1⌉\chi(\mathrm{KG}^{(p)}(n,k))=\left\lceil\frac{n-p(k-1)}{p-1}\right\rceil for every n≥p​kn\geq pk.
Instead of the ℤ2\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}_{2}-equivariant Borsuk–Ulam theorem used originally by Lovász, they used the ℤp\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}_{p}-equivariant Bárány–Shlosman–Szűcs theorem [2] for prime pp, and a simple product argument for composite pp.
Now we introduce a new family, Kneser-shift graphs.
The vertices of the Kneser-shift graph KShp​(n,k)\mathrm{KSh}_{p}(n,k) are the ordered (p−1)(p-1)-tuples (A1,…,Ap−1)(A_{1},\ldots,A_{p-1}) of pairwise disjoint sets from ([n]k)\binom{[n]}{k}, and there is an edge between (A1,…,Ap−1)(A_{1},\ldots,A_{p-1}) and (A2,…,Ap)(A_{2},\ldots,A_{p}) whenever A1A_{1} and ApA_{p} are also pairwise disjoint.
Our second main result is the following.
Theorem 4 (Kneser-shift theorem).
χ⁡(KShp​(n,k))→∞\chi(\mathrm{KSh}_{p}(n,k))\to\infty as n−p​k→∞n-pk\to\infty for every prime pp.
In other words, for every prime pp and every r∈ℕr\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$} there is a (least) constant C⁡(p,r)C(p,r), depending only on pp and rr, such that whenever n−p​kn-pk is at least C⁡(p,r)C(p,r), every rr-coloring of the vertices of KShp​(n,k)\mathrm{KSh}_{p}(n,k) admits pairwise disjoint A1,…,Ap∈([n]k)A_{1},\ldots,A_{p}\in\binom{[n]}{k} for which c⁡(A1,…,Ap−1)=c⁡(A2,…,Ap)c(A_{1},\ldots,A_{p-1})=c(A_{2},\ldots,A_{p}).
Quantitative upper and lower bounds for C⁡(p,r)C(p,r) with the same tower height are given in Proposition 17 after the proof of the theorem.
I do not know whether the primality of pp is necessary in this theorem.
For k=1k=1, the subgraph induced by the increasing tuples is precisely the shift graph Shp​(n)\mathrm{Sh}_{p}(n); the additional vertices record all other orderings.
In fact, for every larger kk, our graph KShp​(n,k)\mathrm{KSh}_{p}(n,k) also contains as a subgraph a shift graph Shp​(⌊n/k⌋)\mathrm{Sh}_{p}(\lfloor n/k\rfloor), by partitioning an initial segment of [n][n] into consecutive atoms of size kk.
This already implies that χ⁡(KShp​(n,k))→∞\chi(\mathrm{KSh}_{p}(n,k))\to\infty as n→∞n\to\infty for any fixed pp and kk, but we need the statement when n−p​k→∞n-pk\to\infty.
Indeed, if we also imposed the restriction A1<⋯<ApA_{1}<\cdots<A_{p} on the edges, meaning that each element of AiA_{i} needs to be smaller than each element of AjA_{j} for all i<ji<j, we would only get iterated-logarithmic growth; more precisely,
χ⁡(Shp​(⌊n/k⌋))≤χ⁡(restricted graph of ​KShp​(n,k))≤χ⁡(Shp​(n))≤χ⁡(KShp​(n,k)).\chi\!\left(\mathrm{Sh}_{p}(\lfloor n/k\rfloor)\right)\leq\chi(\text{restricted graph of }\mathrm{KSh}_{p}(n,k))\leq\chi(\mathrm{Sh}_{p}(n))\leq\chi(\mathrm{KSh}_{p}(n,k)).
For p=2p=2, we get back exactly the Kneser graph: KSh2​(n,k)=KG⁡(n,k)\mathrm{KSh}_{2}(n,k)=\mathrm{KG}(n,k).
In the first new case, p=3p=3, the vertices of KSh3​(n,k)\mathrm{KSh}_{3}(n,k) are oriented edges of the ordinary Kneser graph, and we have an edge between (A,B)(A,B) and (B,C)(B,C) when A,BA,B, and CC are pairwise disjoint, i.e., when these three vertices form a hyperedge in the 33-uniform Kneser hypergraph KG(3)​(n,k)\mathrm{KG}^{(3)}(n,k), or equivalently, a triangle in the Kneser graph KG⁡(n,k)\mathrm{KG}(n,k).
A somewhat related result is due to Poljak and Rödl [41], who studied the so-called arc-chromatic number of symmetric digraphs, defined as the minimum number of colors needed to color the directed edges so that no path of length two is monochromatic, and proved that it is exactly min⁡{r:χ⁡(G)≤(r⌊r/2⌋)}\min\left\{r:\chi(G)\leq\binom{r}{\lfloor r/2\rfloor}\right\}.
This theorem does not imply Theorem 4 for p=3p=3, since adjacency in KSh3​(n,k)\mathrm{KSh}_{3}(n,k) imposes the additional condition A∩C=∅A\cap C=\emptyset.
Further iterations of the arc-graph construction, which are related to the cases p>3p>3 of our theorem, were also studied [40, 48].
Also note that a proper rr-coloring cc of KG(p)​(n,k)\mathrm{KG}^{(p)}(n,k) gives a proper rp−1r^{p-1}-coloring c′c^{\prime} of KShp​(n,k)\mathrm{KSh}_{p}(n,k) defined by
c′​(A1,…,Ap−1)=(c⁡(Ai))i=1p−1c^{\prime}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{p-1})=(c(A_{i}))_{i=1}^{p-1}.
Consequently, χ⁡(KShp​(n,k))≤χ​(KG(p)​(n,k))p−1\chi(\mathrm{KSh}_{p}(n,k))\leq\chi(\mathrm{KG}^{(p)}(n,k))^{p-1}, whereas there is no obvious implication in the other direction.
Nevertheless, a recursive history construction will allow us to derive Theorem 4 from the same ℤp\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}_{p}-equivariant topological machinery that underlies the Kneser hypergraph theorem of Alon, Frankl, and Lovász [1].
More precisely, we use Lemma 14, a straightforward special case of Ziegler’s ℤp\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}_{p}-Tucker lemma [53].

2 Main ideas presented for the equilateral triangle
We use the equilateral triangle to showcase the main new ideas used in the proof of Theorem 2.
So our goal in this section is to establish that an equilateral triangle is ncs-Ramsey.
Note that this already follows from Matoušek and Rödl [36], but our proof is entirely different and generalizes in a straightforward manner to other cyclic transitive sets, and, by basic group theory, to solvable transitive sets.
We start with the following special case of Theorem 4.
The proof below shows that we may take C⁡(r)=3⋅2r−2C(r)=3\cdot 2^{r}-2; by definition, the optimal threshold satisfies C⁡(3,r)≤C⁡(r)C(3,r)\leq C(r).

For the three words in Theorem 6, there is one variable and its block is divided into three equal parts labeled by the three elements of C3C_{3}.
No equidistribution of the labels is required in the general definition; only the common block size matters for distances.

If a finite solvable group GG acts on a finite set XX, then DB⁡(G↷X)\mathrm{DB}(G\curvearrowright X) holds.

4 Proof of the Kneser-shift theorem
We prove Theorem 4.
The successive-shift conclusion, rather than only the usual Kneser-hypergraph theorem, is central to the later atom construction.
We use Ziegler’s ℤp\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}_{p}-Tucker lemma as our sole topological black box, isolate the lifting step already used in the triangle case, and then generalize the rank-sum history labeling from that proof.
We first quote the one external lemma used in this section.
It is Ziegler’s prime-cyclic extension of Tucker’s original combinatorial lemma [52].
We state precisely the special case of [53, Lemma 5.3] used below.
Throughout this section, cyclic coordinates are indexed by [p][p]; when we identify them with ℤp\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}_{p}, the index pp represents zero and all subscripts are reduced cyclically.
Let 𝒳p​(n)\mathcal{X}_{p}(n) be the poset of tuples
X=(X1,…,Xp)X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})
of pairwise disjoint subsets of [n][n], not all empty, ordered by coordinatewise inclusion.
Let
σ⁡(X1,…,Xp)=(Xp,X1,…,Xp−1).\sigma(X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})=(X_{p},X_{1},\ldots,X_{p-1}).

Lemma 14 (ℤp\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}_{p}-Tucker lemma; Ziegler [53]).
Let p≥2p\geq 2 be prime and let n,m≥1n,m\geq 1.
If

The form above already has the signed-integer target needed for the lifting argument.
We derive from it the exact lifting statement needed later.
For p≥2p\geq 2, let 𝒫p​(n,k)\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$}_{p}(n,k) consist of tuples
𝒜=(𝒜1,…,𝒜p),\mathcal{A}=(\mathcal{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{p}),
where the 𝒜i\mathcal{A}_{i} are families of kk-subsets of [n][n], not all empty, and every member of one coordinate family is disjoint from every member of any other.
We order these tuples by coordinatewise inclusion, and ℤp\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}_{p} acts by a cyclic shift.
This action is free for every pp.
Indeed, a tuple fixed by a nonidentity shift would repeat every nonempty coordinate family in at least two distinct coordinates, forcing each of its members to be disjoint from itself.
Unlike the action on arbitrary nonconstant pp-tuples used later, this action remains free when pp is composite.
Lemma 15 (Kneser–Tucker lemma).
Let pp be prime and let m≥1m\geq 1.
Suppose that

μ2​(𝒜)=μ2​(ℬ)⟹μ1​(𝒜)=μ1​(ℬ).\mu_{2}(\mathcal{A})=\mu_{2}(\mathcal{B})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mu_{1}(\mathcal{A})=\mu_{1}(\mathcal{B}).
(3)
Then n−p​k<m−1n-pk<m-1.
Equivalently, no such labeling exists if n−p​k≥m−1n-pk\geq m-1.
Proof.
We apply Lemma 14 to 𝒳p​(n)\mathcal{X}_{p}(n).
Put ℓ0=p⁡(k−1)\ell_{0}=p(k-1).
We construct the Tucker labeling λ\lambda in parallel with the proof for p=3p=3.
If |Xi|<k|X_{i}|<k for every ii, set
ℓ⁡(X)=∑i=1p|Xi|.\ell(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}|X_{i}|.
Let λ1​(X)\lambda_{1}(X) be the index in ℤp\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}_{p} of the unique coordinate containing the least element of ⋃i=1pXi\bigcup_{i=1}^{p}X_{i}.
This is equivariant under cyclic shifts of the coordinates.

It remains to check that its conclusion is impossible.
Consider the pp-element chain supplied by Lemma 14, whose signs are pairwise distinct.
Two low-case members have distinct ℓ\ell-values because their total sizes strictly increase along the chain.
A low-case value is at most ℓ0\ell_{0}, whereas a high-case value is at least ℓ0+1\ell_{0}+1.
Finally, if X<YX<Y are two high-case members with ℓ⁡(X)=ℓ⁡(Y)\ell(X)=\ell(Y), then 𝒜⁡(X)≤𝒜⁡(Y)\mathcal{A}(X)\leq\mathcal{A}(Y) and μ2​(𝒜⁡(X))=μ2​(𝒜⁡(Y))\mu_{2}(\mathcal{A}(X))=\mu_{2}(\mathcal{A}(Y)).
The tie condition (3) would then give λ1​(X)=λ1​(Y)\lambda_{1}(X)=\lambda_{1}(Y), contrary to the distinct signs on the Tucker chain.
Thus the chain has pp distinct ℓ\ell-values.
But all these values lie in one fiber of t↦⌈t/(p−1)⌉t\mapsto\lceil t/(p-1)\rceil, and every such fiber contains at most p−1p-1 positive integers.
This is a contradiction.
∎
The primality hypothesis is deliberate.
Ziegler proves Lemma 14 for prime pp and obtains the ordinary composite-uniformity Kneser theorem separately, by induction on the prime factors [53, Section 5].
This does not show that the Kneser-shift conclusion is false for composite uniformities; it only shows that the present prime-cyclic Tucker argument does not establish it.
For us, no prime-power variant will be needed.
If a cyclic quotient has order pap^{a}, its subgroup chain refines that extension into aa extensions with quotient CpC_{p}.
More generally, the solvable-group argument proceeds through a composition series whose factors all have prime order.
This refinement is carried out in Propositions 23 and 24; see the proof of Theorem 11 for the iteration along a composition series.
The labeling used next has close precedents in work on monochromatic monotone paths.
Fox, Pach, Sudakov, and Suk assign to an ordered (k−1)(k-1)-tuple the lengths of the longest monochromatic tight paths ending there; comparison of successive tuples drives their uniformity-reduction recurrence [13, Theorem 2.1].
Moshkovitz and Shapira develop the predecessor-downset mechanism in every uniformity.
In the 33-uniform base case—the one directly connected with cups and caps—they map a vertex to the downset generated by its predecessor pair-labels [39, Lemma 2.2]; for general uniformity they recursively iterate the same construction [39, Lemma 3.2].
Our histories use an analogous iteration in a cyclic equivariant poset of disjoint set systems.
We use neither result as a black box.
We now turn a hypothetical proper coloring into the labeling required by Lemma 15.
At the top level, properness makes the colors of successive shifts incomparable.
Repeatedly taking downsets of predecessor histories propagates this nondomination down to one-set histories.
The resulting cyclic vector of histories is nonconstant and equivariant, and, just as in the triangle proof, we use the sum of the cardinalities of its coordinates as an invariant integer rank.
For h≥1h\geq 1, let th​(r)t_{h}(r) denote the cardinality of the poset obtained from the rr-element antichain by h−1h-1 successive applications of 𝒟\mathcal{D}.

7 Concluding remarks
Several questions remain open; I mention a few that fascinate me most.
(1)
Does Theorem 2 remain true without the solvability assumption?
Since every subset of a Ramsey set is Ramsey, an affirmative answer would prove the “if” direction of the conjecture of Leader, Russell, and Walters [33] that the Ramsey sets are precisely the subtransitive sets.
A first step could be to prove the same conclusion for every finite spherical set admitting a solvable group of isometries with at most two orbits, in parallel with Kříž’s ordinary Ramsey theorem [31, Theorem 4.4].
A stronger combinatorial question is whether DB⁡(G↷X)\mathrm{DB}(G\curvearrowright X) holds for every finite group action.
(2)
Is every solvable subtransitive set ncs-Ramsey?
A stronger, purely geometric statement would imply this: if a finite set PP of circumradius ρ\rho embeds in a solvable transitive set, must it, for every ε>0\varepsilon>0, embed in a solvable transitive set of circumradius less than ρ+ε\rho+\varepsilon?
One may ask the same two questions without the word solvable.
We mention that Moore [38] and, independently, Mirabi [37] recently proved the related ordinary Ramsey statement that adjoining any point outside the affine hull of a Ramsey set preserves Ramseyness, but their methods do not seem to imply anything about our question.
(3)
Does Theorem 4 remain valid for composite pp?
The prime-factor reduction used for ordinary Kneser hypergraphs does not apply directly here, because the colors live on ordered (p−1)(p-1)-tuples and the desired equality concerns a shift by one set.
Moreover, for composite pp the cyclic action on nonconstant history vectors need not be free: for example, (E,F,E,F)(E,F,E,F) is fixed by a half-turn when p=4p=4.
Thus the equivariant sign assignment used in the present proof genuinely breaks down.
(4)
Determine, or estimate, χ​(KShp​(n,k))\chi(\mathrm{KSh}_{p}(n,k)).
In particular, what is the order of growth of the optimal threshold C⁡(p,r)C(p,r) as r→∞r\to\infty for fixed prime p≥3p\geq 3?
The bounds in Proposition 17 have the same tower height.
For p=3p=3, they determine C⁡(3,r)C(3,r) within a factor of O⁡(r)O(\sqrt{r}), but its precise asymptotic order remains open.
(5)
Ivan, Leader, and Walters [27] conjecture that for every fixed template the block degree can be chosen independently of the number of colors, while the ambient word length may still grow with it.
Our density requirement necessarily forces the block size to grow with the number of colors.
Indeed, let GG act transitively on XX, put q=|X|≥2q=|X|\geq 2 and ℓ=⌊logq⁡r⌋\ell=\lfloor\log_{q}r\rfloor, and color a word by its first min⁡{ℓ,n}\min\{\ell,n\} coordinates.
This coloring uses at most rr colors; injectivity rules out n≤ℓn\leq\ell, while for n>ℓn>\ell orbit-insensitivity forces all first ℓ\ell coordinates to be unused.
Consequently
m​kn≤m​km​k+ℓ,\frac{mk}{n}\leq\frac{mk}{mk+\ell},
so an active proportion of at least 1−η1-\eta requires
k≥1−ηm​η​⌊logq⁡r⌋.k\geq\frac{1-\eta}{m\eta}\lfloor\log_{q}r\rfloor.
How close is this elementary logarithmic lower bound to the truth?
More generally, what is the optimal tradeoff between the block size, the number of colors, and the unused proportion in Theorem 11?
(6)
Is there a density version of Theorem 2?
More precisely, if PP is solvable transitive with circumradius ρ\rho, is it true that for every ε,δ>0\varepsilon,\delta>0 there are an nn and a finite set X⊆𝕊ρ+εnX\subseteq\mbox{$\mathbb{S}$}_{\rho+\varepsilon}^{n} such that every Y⊆XY\subseteq X with |Y|≥δ​|X||Y|\geq\delta|X| contains a congruent copy of PP?
Frankl and Rödl [16] proved the corresponding finite-witness density statement for simplices when XX may be an arbitrary finite subset of Euclidean space.
The distinction between ordinary Ramsey and finite-density properties is studied further by Reiher, Rödl, and Sales [46] and by Rödl and Sales [47].
A related but formally different version replaces the finite set XX by the whole sphere and relative cardinality by normalized surface measure.
Guruswami and Li [25] recently proved results of this measurable spherical type for a broad class of inductive configurations, including regular simplices.
The present Kneser-shift input does not directly yield the desired finite-witness statement: for every p≥3p\geq 3, the graph KShp​(n,k)\mathrm{KSh}_{p}(n,k) has an independent set containing at least one quarter of its vertices.
Indeed, randomly 22-color ([n]k)\binom{[n]}{k} and retain the tuples (A1,…,Ap−1)(A_{1},\ldots,A_{p-1}) for which A1A_{1} has color 00 and A2A_{2} has color 11; the expected size of the retained set is one quarter of all vertices, and it is independent because a retained tuple and its shifted successor would require A2A_{2} to have both colors.
(7)
Is there a canonical version of Theorem 2?
More precisely, if PP has circumradius ρ\rho and admits a solvable transitive group of isometries, is it true that for every ε>0\varepsilon>0 there is an nn such that every coloring of 𝕊ρ+εn\mbox{$\mathbb{S}$}_{\rho+\varepsilon}^{n}, with an arbitrary palette, contains either a monochromatic or a rainbow copy of PP?
Our proof methods seem useless for this version.
Recent progress in canonical Euclidean Ramsey theory includes acute triangles and hypercubes [18], all triangles and rectangles [12], all simplices [19], all products of simplices [49], and regular polygons of prime order [50].
These results do not impose the near-circumradius condition above.

A.1 Compressed two-orbit supports and small constant-weight layers
We identify a binary word of length tt with its support, and write
J⁡(t,λ)={A⊆[t]:|A|=λ}.J(t,\lambda)=\{A\subseteq[t]:|A|=\lambda\}.
We first record the closure notion used below.
Definition 25 (iterated solvable coordinate scheme).
We define the class of finite word families having an iterated
solvable coordinate scheme by structural recursion.
At a direct step, a family 𝒲⊆As\mathcal{W}\subseteq A^{s} has a scheme if it is
contained in a supertemplate 𝒞⊆As\mathcal{C}\subseteq A^{s} which is one orbit of
a solvable group L≤SsL\leq S_{s}.
At a recursive step, choose an LL-invariant supertemplate
𝒞⊇𝒲\mathcal{C}\supseteq\mathcal{W}, where L≤SsL\leq S_{s} is solvable, and
permutations π1,…,πq∈Ss\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{q}\in S_{s} preserving 𝒞\mathcal{C}. Put
Λ⁡(w)=(L​π1​w,…,L​πq​w)∈(L\𝒞)q.\Lambda(w)=\bigl(L\pi_{1}w,\ldots,L\pi_{q}w\bigr)\in(L\backslash\mathcal{C})^{q}.
If Λ⁡(𝒲)\Lambda(\mathcal{W}) has a scheme, then so does 𝒲\mathcal{W}.
At a substitution step, let 𝒜⊆2X\mathcal{A}\subseteq 2^{X} be a nonempty
constant-weight family and let ℬ⊆2Y\mathcal{B}\subseteq 2^{Y} be a
constant-weight family of positive weight, both having schemes. Write
𝒜⁡[ℬ]⊆2X×Y\mathcal{A}[\mathcal{B}]\subseteq 2^{X\times Y} for the family whose set of
nonempty YY-fibres belongs to 𝒜\mathcal{A}, whose nonempty fibres belong to
ℬ\mathcal{B}, and whose remaining fibres are empty. Every subfamily of
𝒜⁡[ℬ]\mathcal{A}[\mathcal{B}] has a scheme.
Finally, the property is preserved by a bijective relabelling of the
alphabet. Equivalently, the families having schemes form the least class
closed under these four rules.
The requirement πi​𝒞=𝒞\pi_{i}\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C} is what makes the recursion geometric rather than merely formal.
It ensures that every πi\pi_{i} lifts to an orthogonal coordinate permutation of the same restricted product.
Lemma 26 (iterated orbit design).
Let P⊆ρ​𝕊​(V)P\subseteq\rho\mbox{$\mathbb{S}$}(V) be a finite spherical configuration whose intrinsic circumcenter is the origin and whose intrinsic circumradius is ρ\rho, and let a solvable group G≤Iso⁡(P)G\leq\operatorname{Iso}(P) have orbits O1,…,OuO_{1},\ldots,O_{u}.
Suppose that h1,…,hs∈Iso⁡(P)h_{1},\ldots,h_{s}\in\operatorname{Iso}(P) and that the orbit-type family
𝒲={(j⁡(h1​x),…,j⁡(hs​x)):x∈P}⊆[u]s,hi​x∈Oj⁡(hi​x),\mathcal{W}=\left\{\bigl(j(h_{1}x),\ldots,j(h_{s}x)\bigr):x\in P\right\}\subseteq[u]^{s},\qquad h_{i}x\in O_{j(h_{i}x)},
has an iterated solvable coordinate scheme.
Then PP embeds isometrically in a finite solvable transitive configuration of circumradius exactly ρ\rho.
Consequently PP is ncs-Ramsey.
Proof.
We argue by structural induction on a scheme certificate, using a relative
induction invariant. Simultaneously, we prove the following pointed form of
the assertion: if 𝒰⊆Dm\mathcal{U}\subseteq D^{m} has a scheme and
a∈Da\in D with am∉𝒰a^{m}\notin\mathcal{U}, then its certificate can be run
on 𝒰∪{am}\mathcal{U}\cup\{a^{m}\} with ama^{m} retained as a separate orbit throughout
and with 𝒰\mathcal{U} fused as prescribed. At later stages the distinguished
word may have a different alphabet letter, but it remains
coordinate-constant.
At any stage we have an isometric embedding
e:P↪Z⊆ρ​𝕊​(W),e:P\hookrightarrow Z\subseteq\rho\mbox{$\mathbb{S}$}(W),
a solvable group AA preserving ZZ, and a list of isometries of ZZ whose orbit-type words are governed by the remainder of the coordinate scheme.
Initially Z=PZ=P, A=GA=G, and the isometries are the given hih_{i}.
For a direct step whose current word length is mm and whose supertemplate 𝒞\mathcal{C} is transitive, form
Y={m−1/2(y1,…,ym):yi∈Z,(Ay1,…,Aym)∈𝒞}.Y=\left\{m^{-1/2}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}):y_{i}\in Z,\ (Ay_{1},\ldots,Ay_{m})\in\mathcal{C}\right\}.
The appropriate wreath product Am⋊LA^{m}\rtimes L is solvable and transitive on YY, while the diagonal map obtained from the current isometries embeds PP isometrically.
Indeed, for the initial stage this map is

and the same calculation applies at every later stage.

For the pointed form, replace 𝒞\mathcal{C} by
𝒞∪{am}\mathcal{C}\cup\{a^{m}\}. Coordinate permutations fix ama^{m}; moreover, the
actual product points having this constant orbit type form one orbit under
the base direct power AmA^{m}. Thus they remain a single orbit separate from
the 𝒞\mathcal{C}-orbit.
At a recursive step, use the full invariant supertemplate 𝒞\mathcal{C} in the same construction, but do not yet require transitivity.
The As⋊LA^{s}\rtimes L-orbits of the resulting restricted product are exactly the LL-orbits on 𝒞\mathcal{C}.
Each πi\pi_{i} induces an isometry of that restricted product because it preserves 𝒞\mathcal{C}, so these induced maps and the new restricted product satisfy the same relative invariant for the labelled orbit words.
For the pointed form, use 𝒞∪{as}\mathcal{C}\cup\{a^{s}\}; both LL and every
πi\pi_{i} fix asa^{s}, and its labelled-orbit image is again
coordinate-constant. Thus the pointed induction hypothesis applies as well.
The induction hypothesis applied to Λ⁡(𝒲)\Lambda(\mathcal{W}) completes the
recursive case.
At a substitution step, index the current maps by X×YX\times Y and suppose
that their type family is contained in 𝒜⁡[ℬ]\mathcal{A}[\mathcal{B}].
Run identical copies of the construction for ℬ\mathcal{B} independently in
the YY-fibres over all x∈Xx\in X, retaining the all-zero fibre as a separate
orbit at every stage. This is possible because coordinate permutations fix
the zero word, while the positive weight of ℬ\mathcal{B} prevents an active
fibre from entering its orbit. Once the inner constructions terminate, the
two relevant fibre types are the empty fibre and the single fused positive
type. Apply the construction for 𝒜\mathcal{A} to the resulting word of
fibre types, using its pointed form whenever the whole substitution occurs
inside a still larger inactive fibre. All acting groups are subgroups of
iterated wreath products of
the solvable groups in the two certificates and are therefore solvable.
Associativity of normalized Cartesian products preserves both the isometric
embedding and the exact radius. Alphabet relabelling merely renames orbit
types, transports the distinguished constant word, and requires no
construction.
Structural induction now proves the first assertion.
The final host is solvable transitive and has the same circumradius as PP, so the main theorem applied to the host proves the last assertion.

Appendix B Exact-radius solvable extensions of regular polytopes

B.4 All regular polytopes

The next statements explain why two tempting simplifications do not settle

Let ℋ~α\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha} be the 33-uniform hypergraph on
oriented unit vectors in the same Hilbert space, whose
edges are the triples satisfying (54).
Proposition 72.
The chromatic numbers of the restrictions of
ℋ~α\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha} to finite-dimensional subspaces are bounded
uniformly in the ambient dimension.
Proof.
The three vectors form a fixed triangle on the unit sphere, and their unique
linear dependence has coefficient vector
(1,1,−2​|a|).(1,1,-2|a|).
No nonempty subsum of these coefficients vanishes, because aa is
transcendental.
Graham’s Rado-theoretic necessary condition for a set to be
circumsphere-Ramsey [20, Theorem 1] therefore gives a fixed finite
coloring, in every dimension, with no monochromatic orthogonal image of
this triangle.
This is precisely a proper coloring of ℋ~α\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha}.
∎
The projective hypergraph used in the possible positive route is different:
its vertices are axes, and an edge only has to admit some choice of signs for
which the displayed relation holds.
A coloring of axes must be invariant under v↦−vv\mapsto-v, whereas the
coloring supplied by the preceding Rado argument is not required to be
antipodally invariant.
Thus a proof that the projective hypergraph has infinite chromatic number,
if one exists, must genuinely exploit the unoriented sign choices; it cannot
be inherited from the oriented relation.
There is a second obstruction.
Call a finite subhypergraph of ℋα\mathcal{H}_{\alpha} coherently
orientable if one can designate on every edge one vertex as its center and
the other two as its endpoints, and choose one unit representative of every
axis, so that (54) holds simultaneously with
those designations on every edge.
Proposition 73.
Every finite coherently orientable subhypergraph of
ℋα\mathcal{H}_{\alpha} is 22-colorable.
Proof.
It is enough to prove the assertion hereditarily for every induced
subhypergraph.
For a vertex set SS, let MS​(t)M_{S}(t) be the matrix whose columns are indexed
by SS and whose rows are indexed by the edges contained in SS, with
coefficients 1,1,−t1,1,-t at the two endpoints and the center of each edge.
The realized vectors give
rank⁡MS​(2​α)<|S|.\operatorname{rank}M_{S}(2\alpha)<|S|.
Every |S|×|S||S|\times|S| minor (if any) is an integral polynomial in tt.
Since α\alpha is transcendental, all these minors vanish identically, and
hence MS​(−2)M_{S}(-2) has a nonzero kernel vector vv.
Every row of MS​(−2)M_{S}(-2) has coefficients 1,1,21,1,2, all positive.
Color the positive and negative coordinates of vv differently and recurse
on its zero set.
An edge exposed at the first nonzero stage contains both signs, so the
resulting two-coloring is proper.
∎
Thus only sign-inconsistent finite subhypergraphs can have chromatic number
greater than two.
The proposition gives no coloring of such subhypergraphs and therefore does
not decide the projective chromatic question.
