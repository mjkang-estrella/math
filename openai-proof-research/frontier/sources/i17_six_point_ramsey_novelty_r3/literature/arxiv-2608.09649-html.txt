A pyramid with a Ramsey base is Ramsey
Kenneth Moore
††thanks: Rényi Institute, 1053 Budapest, Reáltanoda u. 13-15, Hungary. Supported by ERC Advanced Grants 882971 “GeoScape” and “ERCiD.” Email:

Abstract
A finite subset XX of Euclidean space is called Ramsey if for any number of colours kk there exists a dimension nn such that whenever ℝn\mathbb{R}^{n} is kk-coloured there exists a monochromatic congruent copy of XX. The classification of Ramsey sets is one of the major unsolved problems in the field of Euclidean Ramsey theory. Towards this, Ivan, Leader and Walters recently asked whether adding a point to a Ramsey set outside of its affine hull necessarily produces another Ramsey set. In this note, we answer their question in the affirmative.
1 Introduction
A finite set TT in a Euclidean space is called Ramsey if, for every integer r≥1r\geq 1, there is an integer nn such that every rr-colouring of ℝn\mathbb{R}^{n} contains a monochromatic congruent copy of TT. This notion was introduced by Erdős, Graham, Montgomery, Rothschild, Spencer and Straus in three foundational papers [3, 4, 5], where they established the field of Euclidean Ramsey theory.
One of the most important open problems in Euclidean Ramsey theory is to classify the Ramsey sets. Interestingly, it is known that all Ramsey sets must be spherical, meaning that they are contained in the surface of a sphere in some dimension. However, all of the known constructions of Ramsey sets have an even stronger property: subtransitivity. A transitive set is a set whose group of symmetries acts transitively, and a subtransitive set is a subset of a transitive set. On this problem, we have
Conjecture 1.1.
We give two competing statements.
A.
The Ramsey sets are the spherical sets.
B.
The Ramsey sets are the subtransitive sets.
Conjecture 1.1(A) was one of the main problems in the field for many years, originating from one of the three aforementioned papers of Erdős et al. But a more recent work of Leader, Russell, and Walters [9] explains why Conjecture 1.1(B) may be much more natural. See also [8, 2] for more on these conjectures.
In their recent paper, Ivan, Leader, and Walters [7] prove several results on prism and pyramid constructions that have symmetry properties. In particular, their Corollary 4 shows that a one-point extension of a transitive configuration produces a subtransitive configuration; under the corresponding solubility hypothesis, the resulting configuration is Ramsey. This gave a new method for constructing Ramsey configurations from subsoluble ones.
They then ask if the assumptions on the base can be replaced by the bare assumption that the base is Ramsey. In this note, we answer their question affirmatively with
Theorem 1.2 (Conjecture 8 in [7]).
Let BB be a finite Ramsey set in a Euclidean space and let zz be a vector outside of the affine hull of BB. Then B∪{z}B\cup\{z\} is Ramsey.
This theorem would also follow from either of the competing conjectures in 1.1, hence it does not support either conjecture. In [7, Section 4] they note this fact, and also pose questions about other simple configurations that could produce evidence for one conjecture or the other.
The proof uses three classical results: the product theorem of Erdős et al., according to which a Cartesian product of Ramsey configurations is Ramsey [3], the theorem of Frankl and Rödl that every nondegenerate simplex is Ramsey [6], and a compactness consequence of the de Bruijn–Erdős theorem. Apart from these inputs, the argument is elementary.
2 Preliminaries
For a set SS in a Euclidean space, we use aff⁡(S)\operatorname{aff}(S) to denote the affine hull of SS, which is the set of all affine combinations of vectors in SS. A congruent copy of SS is another set S′S^{\prime} in a Euclidean space such that there exists a congruence, which is a bijective map φ:S⟶S′\varphi:S\longrightarrow S^{\prime} that preserves all pairwise distances. Note that if S,S′⊆ℝNS,S^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}, φ\varphi may be extended to an isometry of ℝN\mathbb{R}^{N}. Given a second Euclidean set TT, we employ the standard notation
S→𝑟TS\xrightarrow{r}T
if every rr-colouring of SS contains a monochromatic congruent copy of TT. Typically, this notation is used only when TT is a finite set. We will need two classical Euclidean Ramsey theorems, starting with
Theorem 2.1 (Product theorem [3]).
If AA and BB are Ramsey configurations, then their Cartesian product
A×B={(a,b):a∈A,b∈B}A\times B=\{(a,b):a\in A,\ b\in B\}
is Ramsey.
Theorem 2.2 (Simplex theorem [6]).
Every finite affinely independent Euclidean configuration is Ramsey.
We also use an implication of the standard hypergraph form of the de Bruijn–Erdős theorem [1]. This is essentially Proposition 4 in [3], which is also described at the top of Section 2 in [9]. We state it as
Lemma 2.3.
For any finite configuration TT, if ℝn→𝑟T,\mathbb{R}^{n}\xrightarrow{r}T, then there exists a finite set A⊆ℝnA\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n} such that A→𝑟T.A\xrightarrow{r}T.
3 Proof of Theorem 1.2
Denote the target set by X=B∪{z}.X=B\cup\{z\}. We prove by induction on r≥1r\geq 1 that XX is Ramsey for rr colours. The case when r=1r=1 is not difficult.
We first give a brief intuitive explanation of the argument. We are going to find a finite configuration CC such that every (r−1)(r-1)-colouring of CC contains a monochromatic copy of XX. We next create a set PP as a product of the base BB with a simplex, which is a Ramsey set. The simplex is chosen carefully, to make every copy of BB ‘aim’ at a different point of a particular copy of CC, such that adding that point to BB would create a copy of XX.
We choose the ambient dimension sufficiently large so that we can find a monochromatic (say, red) copy of PP. Now, if any point cc of that nearby copy of CC is red, there is a subset of PP congruent to BB which combines with cc to create a red XX, and we are done. Otherwise, no point of the copy of CC is red, and in particular, only r−1r-1 colours are used. Thus we can find a monochromatic copy of XX by induction.
Figure 1: The pyramid set up Y=B∪{z}Y=B\cup\{z\}
We now proceed with the proof proper. Fix r≥2r\geq 2 and assume that XX is known to be Ramsey for r−1r-1 colours; so there is a dimension d2{d_{2}} such that
ℝd2→r−1X.\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\xrightarrow{r-1}X.
By Lemma 2.3, there is a finite configuration
C={c1,…,cd3}⊆ℝd2C=\{c_{1},\dots,c_{d_{3}}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
for some integer d3{d_{3}} satisfying
C→r−1X.C\xrightarrow{r-1}X.
(1)
After applying an isometry if necessary, we may assume aff⁡(B)\operatorname{aff}(B) is the Euclidean space ℝd1\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}, and that we can decompose zz into its orthogonal projection u∈ℝd1u\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}} and its perpendicular component h∈ℝh\in\mathbb{R}, with h>0h>0. So z=(u,h)∈ℝd1+1,z=(u,h)\in\mathbb{R}^{{d_{1}}+1}, and h>0h>0 (as in Figure 1). Consequently, for all b∈Bb\in B,
‖(b,0)−z‖2=‖b−u‖2+h2.\|(b,0)-z\|^{2}=\|b-u\|^{2}+h^{2}.
(2)
Let e1,…,ed3e_{1},\dots,e_{d_{3}} be an orthonormal basis of ℝd3\mathbb{R}^{d_{3}}. Define
si=(ci,h​ei)∈ℝd2+d3for1≤i≤d3,s_{i}=(c_{i},he_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{{d_{2}}+{d_{3}}}\qquad\text{for}\qquad 1\leq i\leq{d_{3}},
and let
S={s1,…,sd3}.S=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{d_{3}}\}.
SS is clearly affinely independent, since the h​eihe_{i}-coordinates are formed by linearly independent vectors. Therefore, Theorem 2.2 implies that SS is Ramsey.
Theorem 2.1 now shows that the product set
P:=B×S={(b,ci,hei):b∈B, 1≤i≤d3}⊆ℝd1+d2+d3P:=B\times S=\{(b,c_{i},he_{i}):b\in B,\ 1\leq i\leq{d_{3}}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{{d_{1}}+{d_{2}}+{d_{3}}}
is Ramsey. For each ii, introduce the corresponding apex point
ai=(u,ci,0)∈ℝd1+d2+d3,a_{i}=(u,c_{i},0)\in\mathbb{R}^{{d_{1}}+{d_{2}}+{d_{3}}},
and let
A={a1,…,ad3}.A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{d_{3}}\}.
Since uu is constant, the configuration AA is congruent to CC. For each fixed ii, we define the fibre
Pi:=B×{si}={(b,ci,h​ei):b∈B}P_{i}:=B\times\{s_{i}\}=\{(b,c_{i},he_{i}):b\in B\}
which is congruent to BB. Further, for every b∈Bb\in B,
‖(b,ci,h​ei)−ai‖2=‖b−u‖2+‖h​ei‖2=‖b−u‖2+h2.\begin{split}\|(b,c_{i},he_{i})-a_{i}\|^{2}&=\|b-u\|^{2}+\|he_{i}\|^{2}\\
&=\|b-u\|^{2}+h^{2}.\end{split}
(3)
Comparing (3) with (2), we obtain
Pi∪{ai}≅X(1≤i≤d3).P_{i}\cup\{a_{i}\}\mathrel{\cong}X\qquad(1\leq i\leq{d_{3}}).
(4)
Because PP is Ramsey, there is a dimension n0n_{0} such that ℝn0→𝑟P.\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}\xrightarrow{r}P. Let
N=max⁡{n0,d1+d2+d3}N=\max\{n_{0},{d_{1}}+{d_{2}}+{d_{3}}\}
and consider PP and CC to be subsets of ℝN\mathbb{R}^{N}. For an arbitrary rr-colouring of ℝN\mathbb{R}^{N}, there is a monochromatic copy P′⊂ℝNP^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N} of PP. Call its colour red, and let
φ:P⟶P′\varphi:P\longrightarrow P^{\prime}
be the corresponding congruence. We extend φ\varphi to an isometry φ~\widetilde{\varphi} on ℝN\mathbb{R}^{N}, and write
ai′=φ~​(ai).a_{i}^{\prime}=\widetilde{\varphi}(a_{i}).
Then every point of every fibre φ~​(Pi)\widetilde{\varphi}(P_{i}) is red.
There are two cases. If ai′a_{i}^{\prime} is red for some ii, then
φ~​(Pi∪{ai})\widetilde{\varphi}(P_{i}\cup\{a_{i}\})
is a red copy of XX by (4). Otherwise none of
a1′,…,ad3′a_{1}^{\prime},\dots,a_{d_{3}}^{\prime} is red. These points therefore use at most the remaining
r−1r-1 colours. Since φ~\widetilde{\varphi} is an isometry, they form a congruent copy of CC; by (1), they contain a monochromatic copy of XX. This completes the proof of Theorem 1.2.
