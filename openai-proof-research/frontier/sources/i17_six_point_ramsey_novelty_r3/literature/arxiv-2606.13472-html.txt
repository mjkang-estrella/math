GENERALISED PRISMS AND EUCLIDEAN RAMSEY THEORY
MARIA-ROMINA IVAN
IMRE LEADER AND MARK WALTERS
Abstract
A finite subset XX of ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} is called Ramsey if for every kk there exists an nn such that whenever ℝn\mathbb{R}^{n} is kk-coloured there exists a monochromatic congruent copy of XX. Kříž showed that if there is a soluble group of symmetries of XX that acts transitively on XX, then XX is Ramsey. Determining which sets are Ramsey is a major unsolved problem.
In this paper we show that if there is a finite group of isometries of ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} that acts transitively on a set XX, and also on a set YY, then the ‘prism’ formed by XX and YY in
ℝd+1\mathbb{R}^{d+1} (meaning the set XX together with a translate of YY in the direction perpendicular to
ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d}) is itself contained in a finite set on which a group of isometries acts transitively. Moreover, if the initial group of isometries is soluble then so is the final group. This provides a new tool for generating Ramsey sets.
1 Introduction
A finite subset XX of some Euclidean space ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} is called Euclidean Ramsey or simplex Ramsey if for every kk there is an nn such that whenever ℝn\mathbb{R}^{n} is kk-coloured there is a monochromatic copy of XX. Here a ‘copy’ means a congruent copy (also called an isometric copy). Erdős, Graham, Montgomery, Rothschild, Spencer and Straus [4] introduced this concept in the 1970s. They showed that if a set is not spherical (meaning that it is contained in a sphere) then it cannot be Ramsey. They conjectured that the converse holds, in other words that a set is Ramsey if and only if it is spherical. There is a ‘rival’ conjecture by Leader, Russell and Walters [10], stating that a set is Ramsey if and only if it is a subset of some finite transitive set YY (meaning a set whose symmetry group acts transitively). We stress that YY may live in a higher-dimensional space than XX itself. It turns out that these are
distinct conjectures, although this is not immediately obvious (see [10]).
The best positive result to date is by Kříž [8]. He showed that, if a set XX has a subgroup GG of its symmetry group that is soluble and acts transitively on XX, then XX is Ramsey. Of course, it follows that subsets of such a set are also Ramsey. Often subsets of such a set (called a soluble set) are called subsoluble, while subsets of a transitive set are called subtransitive.
There are many examples of Ramsey sets now known. Often these did not originally come from the result of Kříž above, but a remarkable recent paper of Behague [1] shows that, with one possible exception, every
set that is known to be Ramsey is actually subsoluble. (The exception is the 4-dimensional regular polytope known as the 120-cell.) We mention also an attractive prior result of Karamanlis [7], who showed that every simplex, in any dimension, is subsoluble – indeed, this is needed for Behague’s result. (The fact that simplices are Ramsey was originally proved by Frankl and Rödl [5].)
Our aim in this paper is to introduce some new examples of sets that are subsoluble, and hence are Ramsey. Note that in general it may be hard to determine whether or not a set does embed into a soluble set – indeed, no algorithm is known for answering this (or indeed for determining whether or not a given set is subtransitive).
Our sets are ‘generalised prisms’, defined in the following way. We have two sets XX and YY
in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d}; a prism on XX and YY is then the subset of ℝd+1\mathbb{R}^{d+1}, viewed as ℝd×ℝ\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}, given by (X×{0})∪(Y×{λ})(X\times\{0\})\cup(Y\times\{\lambda\}) for some non-zero λ\lambda. Note that if XX and YY coincide then this is just the usual notion of a prism.
Our main result is as follows. Suppose that the sets XX and YY in
ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} are each transitive. Moreover, they are ‘simultaneously transitive’, in the sense that the transitive symmetry groups of XX and YY may be taken to be equal. In other words, there exists a finite group GG of isometries of ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} that acts transitively on XX and also on YY.
Then any prism on XX and YY is subtransitive. Moreover, if GG is soluble then the prism is in fact subsoluble – in particular, any prism on XX and YY is Ramsey.
For example, if XX is a rectangle, and YY is a congruent rectangle rotated about its centre by some rational angle, then the prism on XX and YY is Ramsey. Perhaps a more interesting consequence is the following. Let XX be any subsoluble set in ℝd⊂ℝd+1\mathbb{R}^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}. Then if we add any one point to XX, not lying in the hyperplane of XX, then the resulting set is Ramsey.
The plan of the paper is as follows. We prove the above result in Section 2.
There is also an abstract version of the conjecture that all transitive sets are Ramsey: this is called the block sets conjecture [10]. We give the relevant definitions in Section 3, and then go on to
consider the corresponding solubility barrier. In particular, we give an example of a template that has no soluble transitive subgroup of its symmetry group, and yet embeds into a template that does.
Finally, in Section 4 we present some open problems.
Through the paper our notation is standard. We denote by D2​nD_{2n} the dihedral group of size 2​n2n, i.e. the group of symmetries of a regular nn-gon, by CkC_{k} the cyclic group of order kk, and by SlS_{l} the group of all permutations of ll distinct elements.
2 New subtransitive sets from old
The main result of this section is Theorem 1. We show that given two sets with a common symmetry group acting transitively on each on them, ‘lifting’ just one of them above, by any amount, results in a subtransitive set. Furthermore, if the symmetry group is soluble, then the resulting set is also subsoluble, and in particular Ramsey. We make this precise below.
Theorem 1.
Let XX and YY be two finite sets in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d}, and GG a finite group of isometries of ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} that acts transitively on both XX and YY. Then, for any λ≠0\lambda\neq 0 the set Z=(X,0)∪(Y,λ)⊂ℝd+1Z=(X,0)\cup(Y,\lambda)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1} is a subset of a finite transitive set WW in ℝm\mathbb{R}^{m} for some mm. Moreover, if GG is soluble then the group acting transitively on WW may be taken to be soluble as well, so that in particular ZZ is Ramsey.
It is worth mentioning that the result is not true when λ=0\lambda=0. For example, if XX is an equilateral triangle and YY is its centre, then X∪YX\cup Y cannot be Ramsey (or subtransitive), as it is not even spherical.
For a very simple example, consider the sets X={−a,+a}X=\{-a,+a\} and Y={−b,+b}Y=\{-b,+b\} for some positive real numbers a,ba,b. Of course, the ambient starting space here is ℝ\mathbb{R}, i.e. d=1d=1. The reflection in the origin in ℝ\mathbb{R} acts transitively on both XX and YY, thus the soluble group C2C_{2} acts transitively on both XX and YY. Now, given λ>0\lambda>0, the set (X,0)∪(Y,λ)(X,0)\cup(Y,\lambda) is a trapezium. Since any isosceles trapezium can be obtained by choosing a,ba,b and λ\lambda appropriately, Theorem 1 shows that any isosceles trapezium is subsoluble, and consequently Ramsey.
(The fact that all isosceles trapezia are Ramsey was proved by Kříž [9], and the fact that they are subsoluble was proved by Behague [1].)
As another example, consider XX to be an equilateral triangle centred at the origin and YY the equilateral triangle formed by its midpoints. Then XX, YY and G=D6G=D_{6} satisfy the conditions of Theorem 1, hence, for any λ≠0\lambda\neq 0, the set (X,0)∪(Y,λ)(X,0)\cup(Y,\lambda) is subsoluble, and consequently Ramsey. The above result provides us with a tool for generating Ramsey sets. The following corollary is an example.
Corollary 4.
Let XX be a finite transitive set in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} and let zz be a point in ℝd+1\mathbb{R}^{d+1} that does not belong to the hyperplane containing XX. Then the pyramid with base XX and apex zz (in other words, the point set X∪{z}X\cup\{z\}) is subtransitive. Moreover, if XX is subsoluble, then so is the pyramid, which implies that it is Ramsey too.
Proof.
Let GG be a finite transitive group of isometries that acts on XX. As before, we may assume without loss of generality that GG fixes the origin. Let z=(y,λ)z=(y,\lambda), where λ≠0\lambda\neq 0, and define the transitive set Y={g⁡(y):g∈G}Y=\{g(y):g\in G\}. Then GG acts transitively on both XX and YY, and as such, Theorem 1 applies. Therefore, (X,0)∪((Y,λ)CLOSE(X,0)\cup((Y,\lambda) is subtransitive, and if GG is soluble, then it is in fact subsoluble. However, (X,0)∪((Y,λ)CLOSE(X,0)\cup((Y,\lambda) contains (X,0)∪{(y,λ)}(X,0)\cup\{(y,\lambda)\}, the desired pyramid, which finishes the proof.

3 The block sets conjecture and soluble subtransitive sets
The block sets conjecture, which first appears in [10], is a purely combinatorial statement which implies that every subtransitive set is
Ramsey. In fact, it is equivalent to the assertion that, for
any transitive set XX, not only is XX Ramsey but moreover for any kk there is some power XnX^{n} such that whenever XnX^{n} is kk-coloured there is a monochromatic
copy of XX blown up by a fixed factor α\alpha – in other words, for any kk there exist nn and α\alpha such that (1/α)​Xn(1/\alpha)X^{n} is ‘kk-Ramsey for XX’.
In order to arrive at the abstract form in which the block sets conjecture is stated, we first introduce some terminology.
We define a template over [m][m] to be a non-decreasing word T∈[m]lT\in[m]^{l} for some ll. Next, given a template TT, we define what we mean by a block set with template TT.
Let SS be the set of all words on alphabet [m][m] of length ll that are permutations of the
word TT. A block set with template TT in [m]n[m]^{n} is a set BB of words on alphabet [m][m] of length nn, formed in the following way. First, select pairwise disjoint subsets I1,…​Il⊂[n]I_{1},\dots I_{l}\subset[n] all of the same size dd say, and elements aia_{i} for each i∉⋃jIji\notin\bigcup_{j}I_{j}. In other words, we select the ‘block’ positions for all letters of TT, counted with multiplicity, as well as the fixed ‘reference’ word outside them.
And now a word ww is in BB if and only if wi=aiw_{i}=a_{i} for all i∉∪jIji\notin\cup_{j}I_{j} and there exists v∈Sv\in S such that wi=vjw_{i}=v_{j} if i∈Iji\in I_{j}. We say that this set BB is a block set with block size or simply degree dd.
We are now ready to state the block sets conjecture.
Conjecture 6 ([10]).
Let mm and kk be positive integers and let TT be a template over [m][m]. Then there exist positive integers nn and dd such that whenever [m]n[m]^{n} is kk-coloured there exist a monochromatic block set of degree dd with template TT.
The block sets conjecture has been verified for a few templates, most notably 1​2​…​2⏟s​3​…​3⏟t1\underbrace{2\dots 2}_{s}\underbrace{3\dots 3}_{t} for any s,t∈ℕs,t\in\mathbb{N} [10], and 12341234 (a direct consequence of Kříž’s result and the fact that S4S_{4} is soluble). We remark that blocks of size 1 are in general insufficient – for example for the template 123. (It was shown in [6] that in fact blocks of size 2 always suffice for this template, for any number of colours.)
Given a template T∈[m]lT\in[m]^{l}, the geometric sets that are associated with it are the following. Let α1,…​αn∈ℝ\alpha_{1},\dots\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{R}. Then the set of all points in ℝl\mathbb{R}^{l} whose coordinates have as many αi\alpha_{i} as TT has ii, for all i∈[m]i\in[m], corresponds to the template TT. In other words, if the block sets conjecture is true for TT, then this set is Ramsey. Moreover, if α1,…,αn\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n} are algebraically independent, then the only symmetries of XX are the ones given by permuting the ll coordinates.
Therefore, we define the symmetry group of a template T∈[m]lT\in[m]^{l} to be SlS_{l}, which acts on the set of all the permutations of TT (by permuting the coordinates). Thus, in what follows, a subgroup of the symmetry group of a template T∈[m]lT\in[m]^{l} is equivalent to a subgroup of SlS_{l} (with the same action).
Note that if a template TT has a soluble group of symmetries that acts transitively then the corresponding geometric set XX (for any choice of the αi\alpha_{i}) has the property, by
Kříž’s result, that, for every kk, there is some blowup of XnX^{n} for some nn, that is
kk-Ramsey for XX. And then it follows, by considering the case when the αi\alpha_{i} are algebraically independent, that the block sets conjecture holds for that template.
Now, for several templates TT that do not have such a subgroup of symmetries, the result of Behague shows that the corresponding geometric sets do embed into soluble sets. Hence those geometric sets are Ramsey, but now we unfortunately cannot apply the ‘backwards’ direction and deduce that the template satisfies the block sets conjecture. So it is natural to ask if there can ever be a template TT that does not have a soluble transitive subgroup of its symmetries, but that embeds into a template that does have one – it would then follow that TT satisfies the block sets conjecture.
Our aim now is to show that this can indeed happen. This is perhaps quite surprising; we do not know if this is an isolated case or the start of a more general pattern.
Theorem 7.
The template 12233331223333 does not have a transitive soluble symmetry group, but it embeds in the template 1223333312233333, which has a transitive soluble symmetry group.
Proof.
We first note that if a subgroup of S7S_{7} acts transitively on the set of all permutations of 12233331223333, then, by tracking the position of ‘1’, we see that it must be a transitive subgroup of S7S_{7}.
To show that 12233331223333 does not have a transitive soluble symmetry group, we show that S7S_{7} does not have a soluble subgroup that acts transitively on the set of all permutations of 12233331223333. There are 7!2!​4!=105\frac{7!}{2!4!}=105 permutations of 12233331223333, thus if a group GG acts transitively on them it must have order at least 105105 (fix xx a permutation; then the set {g.x:g∈G}\{g.x:g\in G\} must have all 105105 permutations).
However, it is well known that the only transitive subgroups of S7S_{7}, up to conjugacy, of at least this size are S7,A7S_{7},A_{7}, and L⁡(3,2)L(3,2) [2, Appendix A], neither of which is soluble (see for example [3]). This finishes the first claim, that 12233331223333 does not have a transitive soluble symmetry group.
Therefore, it remains to show that 1223333312233333 has a transitive soluble symmetry group. As before, we are searching for a group that acts transitively on a set of size 8!2!​5!=168\frac{8!}{2!5!}=168, therefore its order must be at least 168168.

Moreover, we know that 𝔽8∖{0F}\mathbb{F}_{8}\setminus\{0_{F}\} equipped with multiplication is a cyclic of order 7. Let α\alpha be a generator. Furthermore, let GG be the group of affine semilinear transformation on 𝔽8\mathbb{F}_{8}, denoted in literature by A​Γ​L1​(𝔽8)A\Gamma L_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{8}). 4 Open Problems
Perhaps the most intriguing open problem is to determine whether or not Corollary 4 extends to the setting of general Ramsey sets. In other words, if we assume merely that XX is Ramsey
(whether or not it is subtransitive), does it follow that X∪{z}X\cup\{z\} is Ramsey?
Conjecture 8.
Let XX be a Ramsey set in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} and let zz be a point in ℝd+1\mathbb{R}^{d+1} that does not belong to the hyperplane containing XX. Then the set X∪{z}X\cup\{z\} is Ramsey.
What is intriguing about this conjecture is that it would follow from either of the two competing conjectures about Ramsey sets: that they are the spherical sets or that they are the subtransitive sets. Indeed, apart from statements of the form ‘the transitive set SS is Ramsey’ (which of course would follow from either conjecture), we do not know of any other conjectured statements with this
property.
We mention that if zz is far enough away from XX then X∪{z}X\cup\{z\} must at least be 2-Ramsey.
To see this, suppose that the perpendicular distance tt from zz to the plane of XX is greater than the
circumradius of XX. Given a red-blue colouring of ℝ2​d+1\mathbb{R}^{2d+1}, we may find a monochromatic copy of XX: say that this copy is red. But then, unless we have a monochromatic copy of X∪{z}X\cup\{z\}, it follows that there is a sphere of radius tt in ℝd+1\mathbb{R}^{d+1} that is entirely blue. Now, each copy of XX living on this sphere forces two points to be red (the two possible corresponding positions of zz), and putting these together as the copy of XX varies we obtain a larger sphere that is entirely blue. If we keep going, we eventually obtain a monochromatic sphere that is large enough to contain a copy of X∪{z}X\cup\{z\}, as required.
There are some very natural prism-type configurations that are not covered by our main result. One intriguing example is as follows: the set in ℝ3\mathbb{R}^{3} consisting of a rectangle and, above it, the same rectangle rotated by an irrational multiple of π\pi (about its centre), as illustrated below.
The point is that, while each rectangle is certainly transitive, the actual group of isometries that acts transitively is not the same in the two cases – the two groups are isomorphic, but they are not equal. Another example, also in ℝ3\mathbb{R}^{3}, consists of a rectangle together with, above it, two points at the same height whose midpoint does not lie above the centre of the rectangle but with the vector from the midpoint to the centre of the rectangle being perpendicular to the line segment (this is to ensure that the set is spherical), and with the angle between the line segment and the rectangle not being a rational multiple of π\pi. See the picture below. It would be very interesting to know if these configurations are Ramsey.
