L93: ## 1. Introduction
L94: Throughout, $\mathbb{C}$ and $\mathbb{R}$ denote the complex and real fields, ${M}_{N}\left(\mathbb{C}\right)$ the algebra of $N\times N$ complex matrices, and I the identity matrix of the size dictated by context. For a matrix B, the symbols ${B}^{*}$, $spec\left(B\right)$, and $spr\left(B\right)$ denote its conjugate transpose, spectrum, and spectral radius.
L95: The notation $\parallel ·\parallel $ denotes the Hilbert-space norm on vectors and the induced operator norm on bounded linear maps; on ${\mathbb{C}}^{N}$ these are the Euclidean and induced Euclidean norms. We put
L96: $$ReB={\displaystyle \frac{B+{B}^{*}}{2}},\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}ImB={\displaystyle \frac{B-{B}^{*}}{2i}}.$$
L97: For scalars and functions, Re and Im have their usual pointwise meanings. For Hermitian matrices $B,C$, the relation $B⪰C$ means that $B-C$ is positive semidefinite. We write $intE$ for the interior of a planar set E and $\mathbb{D}=\{z\in \mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}$ for the open unit disk. Scalar matrix coefficients are written as ${x}^{*}By=\langle x,By\rangle $, where inner products are linear in the second argument.
L98: For a bounded scalar-valued function g on a set E, write ${\parallel g\parallel }_{E}={sup}_{z\in E}\left|g\left(z\right)\right|$.
L99: For $A\in {M}_{N}\left(\mathbb{C}\right)$, set
L100: 
L101: $$W\left(A\right)=\{{u}^{*}Au:u\in {\mathbb{C}}^{N},\phantom{\rule{4pt}{0ex}}\parallel u\parallel =1\}.$$
L102: 
L103: For a polynomial p, put
L104: 
L105: $$\psi \left(A\right)=sup\left\{\parallel p\left(A\right)\parallel :p\in {\mathbb{C}\left[z\right],\parallel p\parallel }_{W\left(A\right)}\le 1\right\}.$$
L106: A compact convex body is a compact convex subset of $\mathbb{C}$ with nonempty interior. For such a body K, let ${C}^{0}\left(K\right)$ denote the complex-valued continuous functions on K, and write
L107: 
L108: $$\mathcal{A}\left(K\right)=\{f\in {C}^{0}\left(K\right):f{|}_{intK}\phantom{\rule{4.pt}{0ex}}\mathrm{is}\phantom{\rule{4.pt}{0ex}}\mathrm{holomorphic}\}.$$
L109: The space ${H}^{\infty }(intK)$ consists of the bounded holomorphic functions on $intK$ and carries the norm ${\parallel ·\parallel }_{intK}$. Whenever $spec\left(A\right)\subset intK$, we define $f\left(A\right)$ by Hermite interpolation. If ${m}_{\lambda }$ is the algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda $ in the characteristic polynomial of A, let q be the polynomial of degree less than N satisfying
L110: $${q}^{\left(j\right)}\left(\lambda \right)={f}^{\left(j\right)}\left(\lambda \right)\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}(\lambda \in spec\left(A\right),\phantom{\rule{4pt}{0ex}}0\le j<{m}_{\lambda }),$$
L111: and put $f\left(A\right)=q\left(A\right)$. These spectral jets determine the usual holomorphic functional calculus, since each Jordan block at $\lambda $ has size at most ${m}_{\lambda }$. The constant $\psi \left(A\right)$ was introduced by Crouzeix [cite9†1 ], who proved $\psi \left(A\right)\le 2$ for $2\times 2$ matrices and conjectured the same bound in every dimension. He subsequently obtained the first dimension-free bound [cite10†2 ].
L112: The disk case is the classical Okubo–Ando theorem [cite11†3 ], while the positive operator-valued double-layer representation behind the general convex-domain estimates goes back to Delyon and Delyon [cite12†4 ]. Crouzeix and Palencia reduced the universal constant to $1+\sqrt{2}$ [cite13†5 ].
L113: Jin [cite14†6 ] and, independently, Lorist and Schwenninger [cite15†7 ] proved the scalar conjecture. The latter proof applies an abstract 2-dilation perturbation lemma simultaneously to all iterates in the double-layer representation. Åhag, Czyż, and Virtanen established sharp square-function inequalities and the complete conjecture in dimension three, together with equality, stability, and representing-measure results [cite16†8 ].
L114: Their equality theory identifies a two-dimensional reducing nilpotent block in the extremal matrix value $f\left(A\right)$.
L115: This paper develops the equality data into an all-dimensional geometric rigidity theorem. Its central steps are rational collapse of the extremizer, identification of the full lemniscate with the numerical-range boundary, and a hyperbolic projective-dual contact count.
L116: 
L117: Theorem 1.
L118: 
L119: For every $N\ge 1$ and $A\in {M}_{N}\left(\mathbb{C}\right)$,
L120: 
L121: $$\psi \left(A\right)=2\phantom{\rule{1.em}{0ex}}⟹\phantom{\rule{1.em}{0ex}}W\left(A\right)=\{z\in \mathbb{C}:|z-\gamma |\le \rho \}$$
L122: for some $\gamma \in \mathbb{C}$ and some $\rho >0$.
L123: The proof has four steps. An equality analysis of the sharp bound produces two extremal vectors and a pointwise boundary-kernel identity. A curved exposed arc then turns that identity into a rational formula for the extremizer. A direct resolvent argument identifies its full unit lemniscate with $\partial W\left(A\right)$. Finally, the Kippenhahn determinant contains the dual curve as a hyperbolic factor.
L124: A generic contact count makes the dual a conic, and the circular points at infinity then force $\partial W\left(A\right)$ to be a circle. A separate ingredient, strict domain monotonicity of the matrix ${H}^{\infty }$ functional-calculus norm, rules out a proper disk produced by the boundary-spectrum induction.
L125: Extensive numerical optimization by Greenbaum and Overton [cite17†9 ] repeatedly found stationary configurations with ratio 2 whose numerical ranges were disks; the theorem explains that pattern at exact equality.
L126: cite18†Section 9 passes from matrix rigidity to the scalar domain constants. Compactness in the least extremal dimension yields disk rigidity and uniform near-equality rigidity for proper convex domains, including a uniform gap for unbounded domains in each fixed finite dimension.
L127: ## 2. Numerical-Range Preliminaries
L128: 
L129: Here and below, $convE$ denotes the convex hull of E. For a Hermitian matrix B, ${\lambda }_{max}\left(B\right)$ denotes its largest eigenvalue. At a differentiability point $\sigma $ of a convex boundary, $\mathfrak{n}\left(\sigma \right)$ denotes its outward unit normal; $\nu $, with or without a subscript, denotes an arbitrary supporting unit normal.
L130: 
L131: Lemma 1
L132: 
L133: (Numerical-range geometry). The following facts hold.
L134: 
L135: 1.
L136: $W\left(A\right)$ is nonempty, compact, and convex.
L137: 
L138: 2.
L139: 
L140: 
L141: If $W\left(A\right)$ has empty interior, then A is normal and $\psi \left(A\right)=1$.
L142: 
L143: 3.
L144: 
L145: 
L146: For $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}\setminus \left\{0\right\}$, $\beta \in \mathbb{C}$, and unitary $\mathcal{U}$,
L147: 
L148: $$W(\alpha A+\beta I)=\alpha W\left(A\right)+\beta ,\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}\psi (\alpha A+\beta I)=\psi \left(A\right),\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}\psi \left({\mathcal{U}}^{*}A\mathcal{U}\right)=\psi \left(A\right).$$
L149: Also $W\left({A}^{*}\right)=\overline{W\left(A\right)}$ and $\psi \left({A}^{*}\right)=\psi \left(A\right)$.
L150: 
L151: 4.
L152: 
L153: 
L154: For a finite nonempty direct sum,
L155: 
L156: $$W\left(\underset{j}{⨁}{A}_{j}\right)=conv\left(\bigcup _{j}W\left({A}_{j}\right)\right),\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}\psi \left(\underset{j}{⨁}{A}_{j}\right)\le \underset{j}{max}\psi \left({A}_{j}\right).$$
L157: 
L158: 5.
L159: 
L160: 
L161: The support function of $W\left(A\right)$ is
L162: $${h}_{A}\left(\theta \right)={\lambda }_{max}\phantom{\rule{-0.166667em}{0ex}}\left(Re\left({e}^{-i\theta }A\right)\right).$$
L163: 
L164: Proof.
L165: The unit sphere is compact and $u\mapsto {u}^{*}Au$ is continuous, proving nonemptiness and compactness. To prove convexity, take two points of $W\left(A\right)$ and compress A to the span of corresponding unit vectors. If the points are distinct, those vectors are linearly independent; if they coincide, there is no segment to prove. It remains to prove convexity in size two. For a unit vector $u=({u}_{1},{u}_{2})\in {\mathbb{C}}^{2}$, put
L166: $$r\left(u\right)=\left(2Re\left(\overline{{u}_{1}}{u}_{2}\right),\phantom{\rule{0.166667em}{0ex}}2Im\left(\overline{{u}_{1}}{u}_{2}\right),\phantom{\rule{0.166667em}{0ex}}|{u}_{1}{|}^{2}-{\left|{u}_{2}\right|}^{2}\right).$$
L167: 
L168: These vectors fill the unit sphere of ${\mathbb{R}}^{3}$: the formula gives $\left|r\right(u\left)\right|=1$, and the parametrization $u=(cost,{e}^{i\phi }sint)$ gives every point of that sphere. For any $2\times 2$ matrix B, expansion of ${u}^{*}Bu$ gives
L169: $${u}^{*}Bu={\textstyle \frac{1}{2}}trB+{L}_{B}\left(r\left(u\right)\right)$$
L170: 
L171: for a real-linear map ${L}_{B}:{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\to \mathbb{C}$. The image of the unit sphere under ${L}_{B}$ equals the image of the closed unit ball. Indeed, project a point of the ball onto ${(ker{L}_{B})}^{\perp }$ and add a vector in the nonzero kernel to bring its norm to one. The ball image is convex. Thus $W\left(B\right)$ is convex, and the two-dimensional compression proves convexity of $W\left(A\right)$.
L172: If $W\left(A\right)$ has empty interior, its convexity places it in an affine line. If $W\left(A\right)=\left\{\gamma \right\}$, then ${x}^{*}(A-\gamma I)x=0$ for all x. The complex polarization identity makes every matrix coefficient of $A-\gamma I$ zero. If $W\left(A\right)$ lies in a line, a translation and a nonzero complex rotation make all quadratic forms of A real. The skew-Hermitian part then has zero quadratic form and vanishes by polarization, so the transformed matrix is Hermitian.
L173: A normal matrix satisfies
L174: $$\parallel p\left(A\right)\parallel ={\parallel p\parallel }_{spec\left(A\right)}\le {\parallel p\parallel }_{W\left(A\right)}.$$
L175: 
L176: The constant polynomial $p\equiv 1$ gives the reverse inequality.
L177: The affine and unitary formulas follow by changing variables in polynomials. For adjoints use $\tilde{p}\left(z\right)=\overline{p\left(\overline{z}\right)}$, for which $\tilde{p}\left({A}^{*}\right)=p{\left(A\right)}^{*}$. For a direct sum, a unit vector decomposes into block components and its numerical value is a convex combination of block numerical values. Also $p\left({⨁}_{j}{A}_{j}\right)={⨁}_{j}p\left({A}_{j}\right)$; the supremum norm on the convex hull dominates that on every block. Finally,
L178: $$\underset{z\in W\left(A\right)}{max}Re\left({e}^{-i\theta }z\right)=\underset{\parallel u\parallel =1}{max}{u}^{*}Re\left({e}^{-i\theta }A\right)u,$$
L179: 
L180: which is the largest eigenvalue of the displayed Hermitian matrix. □
L181: ## 3. The Sharp Bound and Equality Conditions
L182: 
L183: We prove the Lorist–Schwenninger iterate estimate [cite15†7 ] and determine the equality conditions needed below.
L184: 
L185: Lemma 2
L186: 
L187: (An abstract dilation estimate). Let T act on a nonzero finite-dimensional Hilbert space. Suppose that $\mathcal{M}$ is a contraction on another Hilbert space, $\mathcal{V}$ is an isometry, and
L188: 
L189: $${E}_{n}=2{\mathcal{V}}^{*}{\mathcal{M}}^{*n}\mathcal{V}-{T}^{*n}\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}(n\ge 1)$$
L190: are uniformly bounded and commute with T. Then $\parallel T\parallel \le 2$.
L191: 
L192: If additionally $\parallel T\parallel =2$, $spr\left(T\right)<1$, ${T}^{*}Tx=4x$, $\parallel x\parallel =1$, and $y=Tx/2$, then
L193: 
L194: $${T}^{*}x=0,\phantom{\rule{1.em}{0ex}}{T}^{*}y=2x,\phantom{\rule{1.em}{0ex}}\mathcal{M}\mathcal{V}x=\mathcal{V}y,\phantom{\rule{1.em}{0ex}}{\mathcal{M}}^{*}\mathcal{V}y=\mathcal{V}x.$$
L195: 
L196: Proof.
L197: Put $\kappa =\parallel T\parallel $. If $\kappa \le 1$, the conclusion is immediate; assume $\kappa >1$. Choose a unit vector x with ${T}^{*}Tx={\kappa }^{2}x$, and set
L198: 
L199: $${m}_{n}=Re\left({x}^{*}{E}_{n}{T}^{n}x\right),\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}\omega ={\mathcal{M}}^{*}\mathcal{V}Tx-\kappa \mathcal{V}x.$$
L200: The defining identity gives $\parallel {T}^{*n}\parallel \le 2+{sup}_{k}\parallel {E}_{k}\parallel $, so the powers of T, and hence $\left({m}_{n}\right)$, are bounded. Put ${Q}_{n}={\mathcal{V}}^{*}{\mathcal{M}}^{*n}\mathcal{V}$ and ${q}_{n}={T}^{*n}x$. Commutativity of ${E}_{n}$ with T gives
L201: 
L202: $$2({Q}_{n+1}T-T{Q}_{n+1})={T}^{*(n+1)}T-T{T}^{*(n+1)}.$$
L203: Moreover, ${m}_{n}=2Re\left({q}_{n}^{*}{Q}_{n}x\right)-{\parallel {q}_{n}\parallel }^{2}$ and ${\mathcal{V}}^{*}{\mathcal{M}}^{*n}\omega ={Q}_{n+1}Tx-\kappa {Q}_{n}x.$ Multiplying the displayed commutator identity on the left by ${q}_{n}^{*}$, using ${T}^{*}Tx={\kappa }^{2}x$, and taking real parts now gives
L204: $$\begin{array}{cc}\hfill \kappa {m}_{n}-{m}_{n+1}\phantom{\rule{1.em}{0ex}}& =\kappa (\kappa -1)\parallel {T}^{*n}{x\parallel }^{2}-2Re\left({\left({\mathcal{V}}^{*}{\mathcal{M}}^{*n}\omega \right)}^{*}{T}^{*n}x\right)\hfill \\ \hfill \phantom{\rule{1.em}{0ex}}& =\kappa (\kappa -1){∥{T}^{*n}x-{\displaystyle \frac{{\mathcal{V}}^{*}{\mathcal{M}}^{*n}\omega }{\kappa (\kappa -1)}}∥}^{2}-{\displaystyle \frac{\parallel {\mathcal{V}}^{*}{\mathcal{M}}^{*n}{\omega \parallel }^{2}}{\kappa (\kappa -1)}}\hfill \\ \hfill \phantom{\rule{1.em}{0ex}}& \ge -{\displaystyle \frac{{\parallel \omega \parallel }^{2}}{\kappa (\kappa -1)}}.\hfill \end{array}$$
L205: Telescoping, with the boundedness of ${m}_{n}$, yields
L206: 
L207: $$\kappa {m}_{1}=\sum _{n\ge 1}{\kappa }^{-n+1}(\kappa {m}_{n}-{m}_{n+1})\ge -{\displaystyle \frac{{\parallel \omega \parallel }^{2}}{{(\kappa -1)}^{2}}}.$$
L208: 
L209: (1)
L210: 
L211: On the other hand, using that $\mathcal{V}$ is isometric and $\mathcal{M}$ contractive,
L212: $$\begin{array}{cc}\hfill {\parallel \omega \parallel }^{2}\phantom{\rule{1.em}{0ex}}& \le 2{\kappa }^{2}-2\kappa Re\left({x}^{*}{\mathcal{V}}^{*}{\mathcal{M}}^{*}\mathcal{V}Tx\right)\hfill \\ \hfill \phantom{\rule{1.em}{0ex}}& =2{\kappa }^{2}-\kappa {m}_{1}-{\kappa }^{3}.\hfill \end{array}$$
L213: 
L214: (2)
L215: 
L216: Combining (cite19†1 ) and (cite20†2 ) gives
L217: 
L218: $${\parallel \omega \parallel }^{2}\left(1-{\displaystyle \frac{1}{{(\kappa -1)}^{2}}}\right)\le {\kappa }^{2}(2-\kappa ),$$
L219: 
L220: which is impossible for $\kappa >2$.
L221: Now let $\kappa =2$. Equations (cite19†1 )–(cite20†2 ) become $2{m}_{1}\ge -{\parallel \omega \parallel }^{2}$ and ${\parallel \omega \parallel }^{2}\le -2{m}_{1}$, so equality holds in both. For each n, the slack in the estimate preceding (cite19†1 ) is
L222: 
L223: $$2{∥{T}^{*n}x-{\textstyle \frac{1}{2}}{\mathcal{V}}^{*}{\mathcal{M}}^{*n}\omega ∥}^{2}+{\textstyle \frac{1}{2}}\left({\parallel \omega \parallel }^{2}-{\parallel {\mathcal{V}}^{*}{\mathcal{M}}^{*n}\omega \parallel }^{2}\right).$$
L224: Their positively weighted sum is zero. Thus, for every n,
L225: 
L226: $${\mathcal{V}}^{*}{\mathcal{M}}^{*n}\omega =2{T}^{*n}x,\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}\parallel {\mathcal{V}}^{*}{\mathcal{M}}^{*n}\omega \parallel =\parallel \omega \parallel .$$
L227: Since $spr\left(T\right)<1$, ${T}^{*n}x\to 0$, and these two equalities force $\omega =0$. Their first instance then gives ${T}^{*}x=0$. With $y=Tx/2$, ${\mathcal{M}}^{*}\mathcal{V}y=\mathcal{V}x$. Both $\mathcal{V}x$ and $\mathcal{V}y$ are unit vectors, so equality in Cauchy–Schwarz for $\mathcal{M}$ gives $\mathcal{M}\mathcal{V}x=\mathcal{V}y$. Finally ${T}^{*}y={T}^{*}Tx/2=2x$. □

L229: Proposition 1
L230: (The sharp numerical-range estimate). For every $N\ge 1$, every $A\in {M}_{N}\left(\mathbb{C}\right)$, and every polynomial p,
L231: 
L232: $$\parallel p\left(A\right)\parallel \le {2\parallel p\parallel }_{W\left(A\right)}.$$
L233: 
L234: (3)
L235: 
L236: More generally, if K is a compact convex body, $W\left(A\right)\subset K$, $spec\left(A\right)\subset intK$, and $f\in \mathcal{A}\left(K\right)$, then
L237: 
L238: $$\parallel f\left(A\right)\parallel \le {2\parallel f\parallel }_{K}.$$
L239: 
L240: (4)
L241: For $f\in {H}^{\infty }(intK)$, the corresponding estimate is
L242: 
L243: $$\parallel f\left(A\right)\parallel \le {2\parallel f\parallel }_{intK}.$$
L244: 
L245: Proof.
L246: Normalize ${\parallel f\parallel }_{K}\le 1$. Cauchy’s boundary formula below also holds when f is only in $\mathcal{A}\left(K\right)$: for ${z}_{0}\in intK$, the functions $f({z}_{0}+r(z-{z}_{0}))$, $r<1$, are holomorphic on a neighborhood of K and converge uniformly to f; apply the usual formula to them and pass to the limit.
L247: The positive operator-valued double-layer kernel used below originates in Delyon and Delyon [cite12†4 ] and appears in the Cauchy-transform form of Crouzeix and Palencia [cite13†5 ]; see also the recent double-layer and configuration-constant treatments in [cite21†10 ,cite22†11 ]. Parametrize the rectifiable convex Jordan curve $\mathsf{\Gamma }=\partial K$ counterclockwise by arclength. Its outward unit normal $\mathfrak{n}\left(\sigma \right)$ exists almost everywhere and has a measurable representative.
L248: All boundary identities below are understood arclength-almost everywhere. Put ${R}_{\sigma }={(\sigma I-A)}^{-1}$ and
L249: $$\mathcal{P}\left(\sigma \right)={\displaystyle \frac{1}{\pi }}Re\left(\mathfrak{n}\left(\sigma \right){R}_{\sigma }\right).$$
L250: 
L251: At a point with a normal, the supporting-half-plane property gives
L252: 
L253: $${\Delta }_{\sigma }:=Re\left(\overline{\mathfrak{n}\left(\sigma \right)}(\sigma I-A)\right)⪰0.$$
L254: 
L255: Multiplication by the two resolvents shows
L256: 
L257: $$Re\left(\mathfrak{n}\left(\sigma \right){R}_{\sigma }\right)={R}_{\sigma }^{*}{\Delta }_{\sigma }{R}_{\sigma }⪰0.$$
L258: 
L259: (5)
L260: Since $d\sigma =i\mathfrak{n}\left(\sigma \right)\phantom{\rule{0.166667em}{0ex}}ds$ and $d\overline{\sigma }=-i\overline{\mathfrak{n}\left(\sigma \right)}\phantom{\rule{0.166667em}{0ex}}ds$,
L261: 
L262: $$\mathcal{P}\left(\sigma \right)\phantom{\rule{0.166667em}{0ex}}ds={\displaystyle \frac{1}{2\pi i}}{R}_{\sigma }\phantom{\rule{0.166667em}{0ex}}d\sigma -{\displaystyle \frac{1}{2\pi i}}{R}_{\sigma }^{*}\phantom{\rule{0.166667em}{0ex}}d\overline{\sigma }.$$
L263: Cauchy’s formula gives ${\int }_{\mathsf{\Gamma }}{R}_{\sigma }\phantom{\rule{0.166667em}{0ex}}d\sigma =2\pi iI$; taking adjoints gives ${\int }_{\mathsf{\Gamma }}{R}_{\sigma }^{*}\phantom{\rule{0.166667em}{0ex}}d\overline{\sigma }=-2\pi iI$. Therefore
L264: 
L265: $${\int }_{\mathsf{\Gamma }}\mathcal{P}\left(\sigma \right)\phantom{\rule{0.166667em}{0ex}}ds=2I.$$
L266: 
L267: (6)
L268: The matrix field $\mathcal{P}$ is measurable and positive semidefinite almost everywhere. Since the positive-square-root map is continuous on the cone of positive semidefinite matrices, $\sigma \mapsto \mathcal{P}{\left(\sigma \right)}^{1/2}$ is measurable, taking its value to be zero on the exceptional null set. Consequently
L269: 
L270: $$\left(\mathcal{V}x\right)\left(\sigma \right)={2}^{-1/2}\mathcal{P}{\left(\sigma \right)}^{1/2}x$$
L271: defines an isometry into ${L}^{2}(\mathsf{\Gamma };{\mathbb{C}}^{N})$. Let ${M}_{f}$ be multiplication by f, and set $T=f\left(A\right)$. For every $n\ge 1$, Cauchy’s formula and the preceding decomposition give
L272: 
L273: $$2{\mathcal{V}}^{*}{M}_{f}^{*n}\mathcal{V}={\displaystyle \frac{1}{2\pi i}}{\int }_{\mathsf{\Gamma }}\overline{f{\left(\sigma \right)}^{n}}{R}_{\sigma }\phantom{\rule{0.166667em}{0ex}}d\sigma +{T}^{*n},$$
L274: 
L275: because
L276: $${\int }_{\mathsf{\Gamma }}\overline{f{\left(\sigma \right)}^{n}}{R}_{\sigma }^{*}\phantom{\rule{0.166667em}{0ex}}d\overline{\sigma }={\left({\int }_{\mathsf{\Gamma }}f{\left(\sigma \right)}^{n}{R}_{\sigma }\phantom{\rule{0.166667em}{0ex}}d\sigma \right)}^{*}=-2\pi i{T}^{*n}.$$
L277: 
L278: Thus
L279: 
L280: $${E}_{n}:=2{\mathcal{V}}^{*}{M}_{f}^{*n}\mathcal{V}-{T}^{*n}={\displaystyle \frac{1}{2\pi i}}{\int }_{\mathsf{\Gamma }}\overline{f{\left(\sigma \right)}^{n}}{R}_{\sigma }\phantom{\rule{0.166667em}{0ex}}d\sigma .$$
L281: 
L282: (7)
L283: The right side commutes with T, because every resolvent does. Also
L284: 
L285: $$\underset{n}{sup}\parallel {E}_{n}\parallel \le {\displaystyle \frac{length\left(\mathsf{\Gamma }\right)}{2\pi }}\underset{\sigma \in \mathsf{\Gamma }}{max}\parallel {R}_{\sigma }\parallel <\infty .$$
L286: 
L287: Lemma 2 proves (cite23†4 ).
L288: 
L289: For $f\in {H}^{\infty }(intK)$, normalize ${\parallel f\parallel }_{intK}\le 1$, fix ${z}_{0}\in intK$, and put
L290: 
L291: $${f}_{r}\left(z\right)=f({z}_{0}+r(z-{z}_{0})),\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}0<r<1.$$
L292: Convexity makes ${f}_{r}$ holomorphic on a neighborhood of K, with $\parallel {f}_{r}{\parallel }_{K}\le 1$. As $r\uparrow 1$, these functions converge locally uniformly on $intK$, with all derivatives. L299: Proposition 2
L300: (Equality data). Let $K=W\left(A\right)$ have nonempty interior, let $spec\left(A\right)\subset \mathsf{\Omega }:=intK$, and let $f\in \mathcal{A}\left(K\right)$ be nonconstant with

L318: Lemma 3
L319: 
L320: (Boundary eigenvalues reduce). If $Ax=\lambda x$, $\parallel x\parallel =1$, and $\lambda \in \partial W\left(A\right)$, then ${A}^{*}x=\overline{\lambda }x$. Consequently, with ≃ denoting unitary similarity,
L321:

L575: 
L576: Now $\mathsf{\Omega }\subset \mathcal{O}$, while $\partial K$ is disjoint from $\mathcal{O}$. Hence the connected set $\mathsf{\Omega }$ is both open and closed relative to $\mathcal{O}$, and is one of its components.
L577: Every component ${\mathcal{O}}_{0}$ is bounded because $f(\infty )=\infty $, and it contains a zero of f. Otherwise its compact closure would avoid all zeros of f: any zero in the closure lies in $\mathcal{O}$ and hence in ${\mathcal{O}}_{0}$. Thus $1/f$ would be holomorphic near $\overline{{\mathcal{O}}_{0}}$, have modulus one on the boundary by (cite44†25 ), and have modulus greater than one inside, contrary to the maximum-modulus principle.
L578: Lemma 10 puts every zero in $\mathsf{\Omega }$, so $\mathcal{O}$ has no other component. This proves the first identity in (cite45†24 ).
L579: Conversely, if $V\left({z}_{0}\right)\ne 0$ and $\left|f\right({z}_{0}\left)\right|=1$, the open mapping theorem says that every neighborhood of ${z}_{0}$ contains points where $\left|f\right|<1$. Thus ${z}_{0}\in \partial \mathcal{O}$, and (cite44†25 ) proves the second identity in (cite45†24 ).
L580: 
L581: Suppose that ${f}^{\prime }\left({z}_{0}\right)=0$ at a level point. Then, in a neighborhood of ${z}_{0}$,
L582: $$f\left(z\right)-f\left({z}_{0}\right)={(z-{z}_{0})}^{k}q\left(z\right),\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}k\ge 2,\phantom{\rule{1.em}{0ex}}q\left({z}_{0}\right)\ne 0.$$
L583: 
L584: Choose $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$ so that $Re\left(\overline{f\left({z}_{0}\right)}{\alpha }^{k}q\left({z}_{0}\right)\right)<0$, and let $\zeta ={e}^{2\pi i/k}$. For all sufficiently small $t>0$, the points ${z}_{j}={z}_{0}+t\alpha {\zeta }^{j}$, $0\le j<k$, are not poles and satisfy
L585: $$|f\left({z}_{j}\right){|}^{2}=1+2{t}^{k}Re\left(\overline{f\left({z}_{0}\right)}{\alpha }^{k}q\left({z}_{0}\right)\right)+O\left({t}^{k+1}\right)<1.$$
L586: 
L587: The first identity in (cite45†24 ) puts every ${z}_{j}$ in $\mathsf{\Omega }$. But
L588: 
L589: $${\displaystyle \frac{1}{k}}{\displaystyle \sum _{j=0}^{k-1}}{z}_{j}={z}_{0}$$
L590: because ${\sum }_{j=0}^{k-1}{\zeta }^{j}=0$. Convexity of $\mathsf{\Omega }$ would then put the boundary point ${z}_{0}$ in $\mathsf{\Omega }$, a contradiction. Hence ${f}^{\prime }\ne 0$ on the level, and the implicit-function theorem makes the boundary real analytic and smooth.
L591: 
L592: Finally suppose that the boundary contained a nontrivial segment of a real line $z={z}_{0}+t\xi $, $t\in \mathbb{R}$, $\xi \ne 0$. The expression
L593: 
L594: $$|U({z}_{0}+t\xi ){|}^{2}-{\left|V({z}_{0}+t\xi )\right|}^{2}$$
L595: is a polynomial in the real variable t. Its vanishing on an interval would make it vanish for every real t. If $V({z}_{0}+t\xi )=0$ for some real t, the same identity would force $U({z}_{0}+t\xi )=0$, contrary to coprimality. The full-level identity would therefore put the entire unbounded line in $\partial K$, contradicting compactness. Thus the boundary has no nontrivial segment, which for a compact convex body is exactly strict convexity. □
L596: ## 7. The Algebraic Tangent Argument
L597: 
L598: The Hermitian determinant below is the line-coordinate polynomial introduced by Kippenhahn [cite46†17 ]; modern projective-dual accounts appear in [cite35†16 ]. Throughout this section, primal projective coordinates are ordered as $[Z:X:Y]$, dual coordinates as $[s:u:v]$, and incidence is
L599: 
L600: $$sZ+uX+vY=0.$$
L601: 
L602: Thus the affine point $z=X+iY$ is $[1:X:Y]$.
L603: For a homogeneous polynomial $\mathcal{P}$, write $\nabla \mathcal{P}$ for its gradient in the displayed coordinate order. We use the following polynomial form of projective contact recovery; for the general duality framework, see [cite47†18 ].
L604: 
L605: Proposition 5
L606: (Generic hyperbolic contact count). Let $\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}$ be real homogeneous polynomials, with $\mathcal{Q}$ irreducible of positive degree m. Suppose that the real projective zero set of $\mathcal{P}$ is precisely a smooth strictly convex affine oval $\partial K$, with $\nabla \mathcal{P}\ne 0$ there, and that
L607: 
L608: $$\mathcal{P}\mid \mathcal{Q}(\nabla \mathcal{P}),\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}\mathcal{Q}\mid \mathcal{P}(\nabla \mathcal{Q}).$$
L609: 
L610: (26)
L611: Assume $\mathcal{Q}(1,0,0)\ne 0$ and that $s\mapsto \mathcal{Q}(s,t,1)$ has only real zeros for every real t. Finally, put
L612: 
L613: $$\tau ={(-{\mathcal{Q}}_{u},{\mathcal{Q}}_{s},0)}^{\mathsf{T}},\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}\mathcal{R}=\nabla \mathcal{Q}\times \left(\left({\nabla }^{2}\mathcal{Q}\right)\tau \right),$$
L614: 
L615: where × is the ordinary cross product in three coordinates. Suppose one component of $\mathcal{R}$ is nonzero at some real point $[s:t:1]$ of $\mathcal{Q}=0$. Then $m=2$.
L616: 
L617: Proof.
L618: The polynomial $q(s,t)=\mathcal{Q}(s,t,1)$ is irreducible and has degree m in s, with constant leading coefficient $\mathcal{Q}(1,0,0)$. Over the field $\mathbb{R}\left(t\right)$, it is relatively prime both to ${q}_{s}$, by characteristic zero, and to the chosen component of $\mathcal{R}(s,t,1)$, by the nonzero value in the hypothesis. Their resultants in s are therefore nonzero polynomials in t. Choose a real t avoiding their finitely many zeros.
L619: Then $q(\phantom{\rule{0.166667em}{0ex}}·\phantom{\rule{0.166667em}{0ex}},t)$ has m distinct real roots, and at each corresponding $\ell ={(s,t,1)}^{\mathsf{T}}$,
L620: $$\mathbf{z}=\nabla \mathcal{Q}(\ell ),\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}\mathbf{w}=\left({\nabla }^{2}\mathcal{Q}\right)(\ell )\tau (\ell )$$
L621: 
L622: are linearly independent.
L623: 
L624: The second divisibility in (cite48†26 ) gives $\mathcal{P}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)=0$. Euler’s homogeneous identity and its derivative in the tangent direction $\tau $ give
L625: 
L626: $${\ell }^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{z}=0,\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}{\ell }^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{w}=0.$$
L627: Likewise, Euler’s identity for $\mathcal{P}$ and differentiation of $\mathcal{Q}\mid \mathcal{P}(\nabla \mathcal{Q})$ along $\tau $ give
L628: 
L629: $$\nabla \mathcal{P}{\left(\mathbf{z}\right)}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{z}=0,\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}\nabla \mathcal{P}{\left(\mathbf{z}\right)}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{w}=0.$$
L630: Two independent vectors in ${\mathbb{R}}^{3}$ have a one-dimensional common annihilator. Thus $\nabla \mathcal{P}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)$ is proportional to ℓ. The real-locus hypothesis puts $\left[\mathbf{z}\right]$ on $\partial K$, where $\nabla \mathcal{P}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)\ne 0$. Consequently $s+tX+Y=0$ is its tangent and hence a support line of K.
L631: For the fixed normal $(t,1)$, the two support offsets are the minimum and maximum of $tX+Y$ on K, and they are distinct. Every root just found gives one of these two lines. Conversely, the first divisibility in (cite48†26 ) puts both support lines on $\mathcal{Q}=0$. Therefore the polynomial has exactly two distinct roots, and $m=2$. □
L632: 
L633: Proposition 6
L634: 
L635: (Circle criterion). Let K be a compact convex body whose boundary is the full level set
L636: $$\partial K=\{z\in \mathbb{C}:V(z)\ne 0,\phantom{\rule{4pt}{0ex}}|U\left(z\right)/V\left(z\right)|=1\},$$
L637: 
L638: (27)
L639: 
L640: where $U,V$ are coprime polynomials, $degU>degV$, K is strictly convex, and ${(U/V)}^{\prime }$ does not vanish on $\partial K$. Writing $z=X+iY$, suppose there are Hermitian matrices $\mathsf{H},\mathsf{J}$ such that, in the real dual coordinates $[s:u:v]$ associated with the line $s+uX+vY=0$, every tangent line to an open boundary arc belongs to the projective curve
L641: $$det(sI+u\mathsf{H}+v\mathsf{J})=0.$$
L642: 
L643: (28)
L644: 
L645: Then $\partial K$ is a circle.
L646: 
L647: Proof.
L648: Put $f=U/V$. Write $z=X+iY$, $w=X-iY$, and ${S}^{\#}\left(w\right)=\overline{S\left(\overline{w}\right)}$ for a polynomial S. For a homogenization ${S}_{h}(w,Z)$, the notation ${S}_{h}^{\#}$ means coefficientwise conjugation, with $w,Z$ left unchanged. Put $\mathbf{z}={(Z,X,Y)}^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $\ell ={(s,u,v)}^{\mathsf{T}}$, in the coordinate order fixed above. Let $d=degU$, $e=degV<d$, and homogenize the real lemniscate polynomial to degree $2d$:
L649: $$\mathcal{F}(Z,X,Y)={U}_{h}(X+iY,Z){U}_{h}^{\#}(X-iY,Z)-{Z}^{2(d-e)}{V}_{h}(X+iY,Z){V}_{h}^{\#}(X-iY,Z).$$
L650: 
L651: (29)
L652: 
L653: Here ${U}_{h},{V}_{h}$ are the usual homogenizations to their own degrees. At $Z=1$, use the Wirtinger derivative ${\partial }_{z}={\textstyle \frac{1}{2}}({\partial }_{X}-i{\partial }_{Y})$. Then
L654: $$\mathcal{F}={\left|V\right|}^{2}{\left(\right|f|}^{2}-1),\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}{\partial }_{z}\mathcal{F}={\left|V\right|}^{2}{f}^{\prime }\overline{f}\phantom{\rule{1.em}{0ex}}\mathrm{on}\phantom{\rule{4.pt}{0ex}}\left|f\right|=1.$$
L655: 
L656: Here $V\ne 0$ on the level and ${f}^{\prime }\ne 0$ there by hypothesis, so the boundary lies in the regular locus of $\mathcal{F}=0$.
L657: Selection of the real component. Factor $\mathcal{F}$ into homogeneous irreducible factors over $\mathbb{C}$. At a regular zero exactly one irreducible factor occurrence vanishes: two vanishing factors, or a repeated vanishing factor, would make every first derivative of $\mathcal{F}$ zero. The choice of that factor is locally constant along the boundary, because all other factors remain nonzero nearby.
L658: The boundary is connected, so one factor $\mathcal{P}$ vanishes on all of it; let $\mathcal{C}=V\left(\mathcal{P}\right)$. Since $\mathcal{F}$ has real coefficients, the coefficientwise conjugate $\overline{\mathcal{P}}$ is also a factor and vanishes at each real boundary point. Local uniqueness makes $\overline{\mathcal{P}}$ an associate of $\mathcal{P}$; rescaling $\mathcal{P}$ therefore gives real coefficients.
L659: Thus $\mathcal{C}$ is an irreducible real projective curve, and it is smooth along the boundary.
L660: The entire real locus. Every real affine point of $\mathcal{C}$ is a real zero of (cite49†29 ). If $V\left(z\right)=0$ there, (cite49†29 ) also forces $U\left(z\right)=0$, contradicting coprimality. Hence $V\left(z\right)\ne 0$, and (cite50†27 ) puts the point on $\partial K$. The reverse inclusion holds by construction of $\mathcal{C}$, so
L661: 
L662: $$\mathcal{C}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\cap \{Z\ne 0\}=\left\{\right[1:X:Y]:X+iY\in \partial K\}.$$
L663: There are no additional real points at infinity. Indeed, if $lc\left(U\right)$ denotes the nonzero leading coefficient of U,
L664: 
L665: $$\mathcal{F}(0,X,Y)={|lc\left(U\right)|}^{2}{({X}^{2}+{Y}^{2})}^{d},$$
L666: 
L667: (30)
L668: 
L669: and ${X}^{2}+{Y}^{2}>0$ for every nonzero real projective pair $[X:Y]$. Therefore
L670: 
L671: $$\mathcal{C}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)=\left\{\right[1:X:Y]:X+iY\in \partial K\},$$
L672: 
L673: exactly one smooth strictly convex affine oval.
L674: Shrink the given boundary arc so that its tangent normal has nonzero last coordinate and nonconstant slope. This is possible because a constant slope would give a line segment. Analyticity then supplies a subarc on which the slope has nonzero derivative. Using the slope as parameter, write the tangent and its contact point as
L675: $$\ell \left(t\right)={(a\left(t\right),t,1)}^{\mathsf{T}},\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}\mathbf{z}\left(t\right)={(1,b\left(t\right),c\left(t\right))}^{\mathsf{T}},\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime }\left(t\right)\ne 0.$$
L676: Both maps are real analytic. Factor the determinant in (cite51†28 ) into real irreducible homogeneous factors. Since its restriction to this arc is identically zero, one factor $\mathcal{Q}$ vanishes identically there. Since the determinant is one at $(1,0,0)$, $\mathcal{Q}(1,0,0)\ne 0$. It has a regular point on the arc: after setting $v=1$, a nonzero resultant of $\mathcal{Q}$ and ${\mathcal{Q}}_{s}$ excludes singular points for all but finitely many slope parameters.
L677: A real irreducible polynomial with a regular real zero is irreducible over $\mathbb{C}$. Indeed, otherwise conjugate complex factors would both vanish at that point and make its gradient zero. We have obtained
L678: $$\mathcal{Q}\mid det(sI+u\mathsf{H}+v\mathsf{J}).$$
L679: 
L680: (31)
L681: We record how this arc determines both contact identities. A polynomial vanishing on an open analytic arc of an irreducible plane curve is divisible by its equation. To see this in an affine graph chart, take the resultant with that equation: a nonzero resultant would restrict the graph parameter to finitely many values. Homogenization gives the same assertion for homogeneous polynomials.
L682: Since $\nabla \mathcal{P}\left(\mathbf{z}\right(t\left)\right)$ is proportional to $\ell \left(t\right)$, this observation gives $\mathcal{P}\mid \mathcal{Q}(\nabla \mathcal{P})$. For the reverse identity, tangency and differentiated incidence give
L683: $${\ell }^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{z}=0,\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}{\ell }^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime }=0,\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}{\left({\ell }^{\prime }\right)}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{z}=0.$$
L684: 
L685: At a regular point of $\mathcal{Q}$, its gradient is orthogonal to ℓ, by Euler’s identity, and to ${\ell }^{\prime }$, by differentiation. These two vectors are independent, so
L686: $$\nabla \mathcal{Q}(\ell (t\left)\right)=\alpha \left(t\right)\mathbf{z}\left(t\right),\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}\alpha \left(t\right)\ne 0.$$
L687: 
L688: Hence $\mathcal{Q}\mid \mathcal{P}(\nabla \mathcal{Q})$, again by the resultant argument. In particular, $\mathcal{Q}$ defines the dual curve: the closure of the tangent lines to $\mathcal{C}$.
L689: 
L690: Differentiating the last gradient identity gives
L691: $$\left({\nabla }^{2}\mathcal{Q}\right)(\ell ){\ell }^{\prime }={\alpha }^{\prime }\mathbf{z}+\alpha {\mathbf{z}}^{\prime }.$$
L692: The vectors $\mathbf{z},{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime }$ are independent because their first coordinates are $1,0$ and ${\mathbf{z}}^{\prime }\ne 0$. Thus the gradient and its derivative are independent. On the regular dual graph, ${\mathcal{Q}}_{s}\ne 0$: otherwise differentiating $\mathcal{Q}\left(a\right(t),t,1)=0$ and using Euler’s identity would make all three partial derivatives zero. Also $\tau ={\mathcal{Q}}_{s}{\ell }^{\prime }$. Therefore the cross product in Proposition 5 is nonzero at such a point.
L693: For real t, every root of $\mathcal{Q}(s,t,1)$ is a root of $det(sI+t\mathsf{H}+\mathsf{J})$, hence is real by the spectral theorem. This is hyperbolicity in the real-rootedness sense [cite52†19 ,cite53†20 ]. Proposition 5 gives $deg\mathcal{Q}=2$.
L694: Write $\mathcal{Q}(\ell )={\ell }^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma \ell $, with $\Sigma $ real symmetric. Irreducibility over $\mathbb{C}$ makes $\Sigma $ invertible, since a singular ternary quadratic factors into linear forms. The invertible substitution $\mathbf{z}=2\Sigma \ell $ in $\mathcal{Q}\mid \mathcal{P}(\nabla \mathcal{Q})$ shows that
L695: 
L696: $${\mathbf{z}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\Sigma }^{-1}\mathbf{z}\mid \mathcal{P}\left(\mathbf{z}\right).$$
L697: The polynomial on the left is a nonconstant quadratic. Irreducibility of $\mathcal{P}$ makes the two polynomials scalar multiples. Thus $\mathcal{C}$ is the conic ${\mathbf{z}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\Sigma }^{-1}\mathbf{z}=0$.
L698: 
L699: Its restriction to $Z=0$ is a nonzero homogeneous binary quadratic, so it has a complex projective zero. Since $\mathcal{P}\mid \mathcal{F}$, equation (cite54†30 ) puts every such zero at one of the circular points
L700: 
L701: $$[0:1:i],\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}[0:1:-i].$$
L702: Real coefficients give both points by conjugation. The quadratic part is therefore a nonzero multiple of ${X}^{2}+{Y}^{2}$, and the conic equation has the form
L703: 
L704: $${q}_{0}({X}^{2}+{Y}^{2})+{q}_{1}ZX+{q}_{2}ZY+{q}_{3}{Z}^{2}=0,\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}{q}_{0}\ne 0.$$
L705: 
L706: Completing squares shows that its nonempty compact real oval is a circle. □
L707: ## 8. Proof of the Main Theorem
L708: 
L709: Proof
L710: 
L711: (Proof of Theorem 1). We use induction on N. For $N=1$, and more generally whenever the numerical range has empty interior, Lemma 1 gives $\psi \left(A\right)=1$, so the hypothesis never occurs.
L712: Assume the theorem holds for all sizes smaller than N, and let $A\in {M}_{N}\left(\mathbb{C}\right)$ satisfy $\psi \left(A\right)=2$. Put $K=W\left(A\right)$. It has nonempty interior. By Lemma 6, either K is already a nondegenerate disk or $spec\left(A\right)\subset \mathsf{\Omega }=intK$. Consider the second case.
L713: Choose $f\in \mathcal{A}\left(K\right)$ as in Lemma 7, and let $x,y$ be the equality vectors from Proposition 2. Since an interior-spectrum numerical range cannot be a polygon, Lemma 8 provides a curved analytic boundary arc. On that arc, Proposition 3 yields
L714: 
L715: $$f\left(z\right)={\displaystyle \frac{U\left(z\right)}{V\left(z\right)}},\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}degU>degV,$$
L716: 
L717: after cancellation of the meromorphic transfer identity (cite42†18 ).
L718: Lemma 10 puts every zero of U in $\mathsf{\Omega }$, every pole outside K, and gives $f(\infty )=\infty $. Proposition 4 then gives
L719: 
L720: $$\{z\in \mathbb{C}:V(z)\ne 0,\phantom{\rule{4pt}{0ex}}|U\left(z\right)/V\left(z\right)|<1\}=\mathsf{\Omega },\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}\{z\in \mathbb{C}:V(z)\ne 0,\phantom{\rule{4pt}{0ex}}|U\left(z\right)/V\left(z\right)|=1\}=\partial K,$$
L721: 
L722: and shows that this boundary is a smooth strictly convex rational lemniscate.
L723: Put ${H}_{A}=ReA$ and ${J}_{A}=ImA$. Writing $z=X+iY$, every supporting line $s+uX+vY=0$, with $s,u,v\in \mathbb{R}$, can be oriented so that $sI+u{H}_{A}+v{J}_{A}⪰0$. A unit vector attaining the point of support has zero quadratic form for this positive semidefinite matrix and therefore lies in its kernel. Hence
L724: 
L725: $$det(sI+u{H}_{A}+v{J}_{A})=0.$$
L726: All hypotheses of Proposition 6 now hold. It follows that $\partial K$ is a circle. Compactness and convexity make K the filled closed disk bounded by that circle.
L727: 
L728: The disk has positive radius because K has nonempty planar interior. Writing its center and radius as $\gamma $ and $\rho $ gives
L729: 
L730: $$W\left(A\right)=\{z:|z-\gamma |\le \rho \},\phantom{\rule{2.em}{0ex}}\rho >0,$$
L731: 
L732: which completes the induction and the proof. □
