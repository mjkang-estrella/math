32: \begin{abstract}
33: The product of two ten-dimensional simplices has larger normalized
34: projection-body volume than a twenty-dimensional simplex. This gives
35: a counterexample to Brannen's proposed simplex maximum.
36: \end{abstract}
37: 
38: \section{Introduction}
39: 
40: Throughout, a convex body in \(\R^d\) is compact and convex with nonempty
41: interior, and \(d\ge2\). Write \(|K|\) for its \(d\)-dimensional
42: volume and \(h_C(x)=\max_{y\in C}\langle x,y\rangle\) for the support
43: function of a compact convex set \(C\). The projection body
44: \(\Pi_d K\), also written \(\Pi K\) when the dimension is evident, is
45: defined by
46: \[
47:  h_{\Pi K}(u)=\vol_{d-1}\bigl(\proj_{u^\perp}K\bigr),
48:  \qquad u\in S^{d-1},
49: \]
50: extended homogeneously to all of \(\R^d\). Here \(S^{d-1}\) is the
51: Euclidean unit sphere and \(\proj_{u^\perp}\) is orthogonal projection.
52: We write \(B_2^j\) for the Euclidean unit ball in \(\R^j\) and
53: \(\kappa_j=|B_2^j|\).
54: The functional under consideration is
55: \begin{equation}\label{eq:functional}
56:  R_d(K)=\frac{|\Pi K|}{|K|^{d-1}},
57:  \qquad c_d=\frac{(d+1)d^d}{d!}.
58: \end{equation}
59: 
60: The extremal values of this affine-invariant functional are classical
61: questions in convex geometry. Petty established the affine covariance of
62: the projection-body operator~\cite{Petty}; see also
63: \cite[Equation~(1)]{Ludwig}. Brannen conjectured in 1996 that simplices
64: maximize \(R_d\) among all convex bodies~\cite{Brannen}.
65: The simplex value is \(c_d\), as we verify below. Thus the proposed
66: simplex upper bound is
67: \begin{equation}\label{eq:proposed-bound}
68:  |\Pi K|\le c_d|K|^{d-1}.
69: \end{equation}
70: It is the volume of \(\Pi K\) itself that occurs here, not the volume of
71: its polar: the classical projection inequalities involving the polar
72: concern a different functional. For \(d\ge3\), Petty's separate minimum
73: problem asks whether ellipsoids are the only minimizers of the same
74: functional \(R_d\); see \cite[Section~1]{Saroglou} for these distinctions.
75: 
76: In dimension three, Chen, Feng, Li, Xi, and Xu settle the minimum problem
77: and also prove
78: the bound \(R_3(K)\le18=c_3\) for all
79: three-dimensional convex bodies, with tetrahedra attaining
80: equality~\cite[Theorems~1.1 and~1.2]{ChenEtAl}. Feng, Hu, Liu, and Xu give
81: counterexamples to \eqref{eq:proposed-bound} in every dimension
82: \(d\ge9\), using polytopes with at most \(d+2\)
83: facets~\cite[Theorem~1.3]{FengEtAl}. Their result already gives a
84: negative answer to the unrestricted-dimensional simplex-maximum
85: question. Their construction uses perturbed projections of a cube and
86: Minkowski's existence theorem~\cite[Section~4]{FengEtAl}. The present
87: paper supplies a direct Cartesian-product construction and its exact
88: value: two ten-dimensional simplices suffice.
89: 
90: \begin{theorem}\label{thm:counterexample}
91: Let \(T_{10}=\conv(0,e_1,\ldots,e_{10})\subset\R^{10}\), where the
92: \(e_i\) are the coordinate unit vectors, and let
93: \(K=T_{10}\times T_{10}\subset\R^{20}\). Then
94: \[
95:  \frac{R_{20}(K)}{c_{20}}
96:  =\frac{121\binom{20}{10}}{21\cdot2^{20}}
97:  =\frac{22\,355\,476}{22\,020\,096}>1.
98: \]
99: Thus \(K\) violates \eqref{eq:proposed-bound} in dimension twenty.
100: \end{theorem}
101: 
102: The exact witness in Theorem~\ref{thm:counterexample} provides an
103: elementary illustration of the failure of the simplex maximum. The
104: optimal upper constant and its maximizing bodies remain separate
105: questions.
106: 
107: The useful mechanism is a product test: for full-dimensional polytopes
108: \(A\subset\R^r\) and \(B\subset\R^s\), with \(r,s\ge2\),
109: \[
110:  R_{r+s}(A\times B)=R_r(A)R_s(B).
111: \]
112: Consequently, the product \(c_rc_s\) of the simplex values is a necessary
113: lower bound for any universal upper constant in dimension \(r+s\). The
114: dimension-twenty counterexample comes from comparing two copies of the
115: ten-dimensional simplex value with the twenty-dimensional simplex value.
116: All identities used in that comparison are proved below.
117: 
118: The growth of upper constants with dimension is a further quantitative
119: question. For centrally symmetric bodies, Henk gives the lower bound
120: \(2^d(9/8)^{\lfloor d/3\rfloor}\) for the optimal upper
121: constant~\cite{Henk}. Iterating our product identity gives an exponential
122: excess over the simplex benchmark: for some fixed \(\lambda>1\) and
123: every sufficiently large \(n\), a product of simplices
124: \(K_n\subset\R^n\) satisfies \(R_n(K_n)\ge\lambda^n c_n\).
125: Corollary~\ref{cor:exponential-excess} proves this consequence by
126: repeating ten-dimensional factors and using one additional simplex to
127: reach each dimension.
128: 
129: Section~\ref{sec:facets} derives the facet formula, the product identity,
130: and affine covariance. Section~\ref{sec:simplex} computes the simplex
131: value by expressing its projection body as a cube plus one segment.
132: Section~\ref{sec:counterexample} combines the identities and gives the
133: exact integer comparison, then proves the exponential excess in all
134: sufficiently large dimensions.

136: \section{Facet vectors and Cartesian products}\label{sec:facets}
137: 
138: We first express projection bodies of polytopes as sums of segments.
139: The facets of a Cartesian product then separate into two families,
140: which will make its normalized projection-body volume multiplicative.
141: The facet formula is the polytopal case of Cauchy's projection formula;
142: see \cite[Section~1]{Saroglou}. We include its proof
143: to fix the factor \(1/2\) on which the subsequent constants depend.
144: 
145: We use Minkowski addition \(C+D=\{c+d:c\in C,\ d\in D\}\).
146: Support functions add under this operation and determine compact convex
147: sets uniquely. For the latter assertion, if \(x\notin C\) and \(y\in C\)
148: is nearest to \(x\), minimization along each segment \([y,z]\subset C\)
149: gives \(\langle x-y,z-y\rangle\le0\) for every \(z\in C\). The linear
150: functional with vector \(x-y\) therefore separates \(x\) from \(C\).
151: 
152: \begin{samepage}
153: \begin{lemma}\label{lem:facets}
154: Let \(P\subset\R^d\), \(d\ge2\), be a full-dimensional convex polytope. For each facet
155: \(F\), let \(s_F\) be its \((d-1)\)-dimensional volume and \(\nu_F\) its
156: outward unit normal. Then
157: \begin{equation}\label{eq:facet}
158:  h_{\Pi P}(u)=\frac12\sum_Fs_F|\langle\nu_F,u\rangle|,
159:  \qquad u\in\R^d,
160: \end{equation}
161: and therefore
162: \begin{equation}\label{eq:zonotope}
163:  \Pi P=\sum_F
164:  \left[-\frac{s_F\nu_F}{2},\frac{s_F\nu_F}{2}\right].
165: \end{equation}
166: \end{lemma}
167: \end{samepage}
168: 
169: \begin{proof}
170: First take \(u\) to be a unit vector. Orthogonal projection from the
171: hyperplane of \(F\) to \(u^\perp\) has absolute Jacobian
172: \(|\langle\nu_F,u\rangle|\). Indeed, for an orthonormal tangent basis
173: \(v_1,\ldots,v_{d-1}\), that Jacobian is
174: \(|\det(v_1,\ldots,v_{d-1},u)|\), which equals the stated inner product
175: in absolute value.
176: 
177: Apart from a set of \((d-1)\)-dimensional measure zero, a point in the
178: projection of \(P\) lies on the projections of exactly two facet
179: interiors: the entry and exit points of its line parallel to \(u\).
180: To see that the exceptions are null, discard the boundary of the
181: projection and the projections of all faces of dimension at most
182: \(d-2\). Also discard projections of facets whose hyperplanes are parallel to
183: \(u\); these projections have dimension at most \(d-2\).
184: Every remaining line meets \(P\) in a nondegenerate interval with its
185: two endpoints in facet interiors. Integrating this multiplicity gives
186: \eqref{eq:facet} for unit \(u\), and homogeneity gives the general case.
187: 
188: The support function of \([-v/2,v/2]\) is
189: \(\tfrac12|\langle v,u\rangle|\). Adding these support functions proves
190: \eqref{eq:zonotope}.
191: \end{proof}
192: 
193: \begin{proposition}\label{prop:product}
194: Let \(A\subset\R^r\) and \(B\subset\R^s\) be full-dimensional convex
195: polytopes, where \(r,s\ge2\). In the orthogonal product space
196: \(\R^{r+s}=\R^r\times\R^s\),
197: \begin{equation}\label{eq:product-body}
198:  \Pi(A\times B)=(|B|\Pi A)\times(|A|\Pi B).
199: \end{equation}
200: In particular,
201: \begin{equation}\label{eq:multiplicative}
202:  R_{r+s}(A\times B)=R_r(A)R_s(B).
203: \end{equation}
204: \end{proposition}
205: 
206: \begin{proof}
207: The face exposed by a linear functional \((u,v)\) on \(A\times B\)
208: is the product of the faces exposed by \(u\) and \(v\).
209: Dimensions add under Cartesian products. A nonzero linear functional
210: exposes a proper face of a full-dimensional body, so if both \(u\) and
211: \(v\) are nonzero, the product face has codimension at least two.
212: A facet therefore has one facet factor and one whole factor.
213: Thus the facets are \(F\times B\)
214: and \(A\times G\), with area-normal vectors
215: \[
216:  (|B|s_F\nu_F,0)
217:  \quad\hbox{and}\quad
218:  (0,|A|s_G\nu_G),
219: \]
220: respectively. The area factors are products because the two coordinate
221: spaces are orthogonal. Lemma~\ref{lem:facets} now gives
222: \[
223:  h_{\Pi(A\times B)}(u,v)
224:  =|B|h_{\Pi A}(u)+|A|h_{\Pi B}(v).
225: \]
226: This is the support function of the right side of
227: \eqref{eq:product-body}, proving that identity. Hence
228: \[
229:  \begin{split}
230:  R_{r+s}(A\times B)
231:  &=\frac{|B|^r|A|^s|\Pi A||\Pi B|}
232:          {(|A||B|)^{r+s-1}}\\
233:  &=\frac{|\Pi A|}{|A|^{r-1}}
234:    \frac{|\Pi B|}{|B|^{s-1}},
235:  \end{split}
236: \]
237: as required.
238: \end{proof}
239: 
240: The product identity reduces our comparison to the values of its factors.
241: To use simplices as a dimension-dependent benchmark, we also need to
242: know that all full-dimensional simplices of a fixed dimension have the
243: same value. The following affine covariance formula supplies that fact.
244: 
245: \begin{lemma}\label{lem:affine}
246: Let \(P\subset\R^d\), \(d\ge2\), be a convex body. For every
247: invertible linear map \(L\colon\R^d\to\R^d\),
248: \[
249:  \Pi(LP)=|\det L|L^{-t}\Pi P,\qquad R_d(LP)=R_d(P),
250: \]
251: where \(L^{-t}\) denotes the inverse transpose. Translations also
252: preserve \(R_d\).
253: \end{lemma}
254: 
255: \begin{proof}
256: First suppose that \(P\) is a polytope.
257: The transformed outward unit normal of \(F\) is
258: \(L^{-t}\nu_F/\|L^{-t}\nu_F\|\). Its area is
259: \[
260:  s_{LF}=|\det L|\,\|L^{-t}\nu_F\|\,s_F.
261: \]
262: For the area formula, take a prism of height one over \(F\), extending
263: in direction \(\nu_F\). Its image has volume \(|\det L|s_F\) and height
264: \(1/\|L^{-t}\nu_F\|\) over \(LF\); dividing volume by height gives the
265: formula. Thus each area-normal vector transforms by
266: \(|\det L|L^{-t}\), including when \(\det L<0\).
267: Apply \eqref{eq:zonotope}. The absolute determinant of this map is
268: \(|\det L|^{d-1}\), exactly the scaling of \(|LP|^{d-1}\).
269: Translation invariance follows directly from projection volumes.
270: 
271: For completeness, we justify the passage to an arbitrary convex body
272: and the existence of its projection body. Translate an interior point
273: of \(P\) to the origin, choose \(R>0\) with \(P\subset RB_2^d\), and
274: take increasing inscribed polytopes \(P_m\) containing a fixed ball
275: \(aB_2^d\), \(a>0\), such that
276: \[
277:  P_m\subset P\subset P_m+\varepsilon_mB_2^d
278:  \subset(1+\varepsilon_m/a)P_m,
279:  \qquad \varepsilon_m\longrightarrow0.
280: \]
281: Take, for example, convex hulls of cumulative finite nets in \(P\) and
282: a fixed inscribed polytope containing \(aB_2^d\). Put
283: \(\lambda_m=1+\varepsilon_m/a\), and for unit \(u\) write
284: \(F(u)=\vol_{d-1}(\proj_{u^\perp}P)\) and
285: \(f_m(u)=h_{\Pi P_m}(u)\). Projection and volume monotonicity give
286: \[
287:  0\le F(u)-f_m(u)
288:  \le (\lambda_m^{d-1}-1)\kappa_{d-1}R^{d-1}.
289: \]
290: Thus \(f_m\to F\) uniformly. The increasing bodies \(\Pi P_m\) lie
291: in a fixed ball and contain \(\kappa_{d-1}a^{d-1}B_2^d\), so their
292: union closure is a convex body with support function \(F\), namely
293: \(\Pi P\). The support inequalities also give
294: \[
295:  \Pi P_m\subset\Pi P\subset\lambda_m^{d-1}\Pi P_m,
296:  \qquad
297:  |\Pi P_m|\le|\Pi P|\le\lambda_m^{d(d-1)}|\Pi P_m|.
298: \]
299: Likewise \(|P_m|\le|P|\le\lambda_m^d|P_m|\). Applying \(L\) to
300: the original sandwich gives the same convergence for \(LP_m\).
301: Passing to the limit in the polytope identity proves covariance and,
302: by these volume inequalities, the asserted invariance of \(R_d\).
303: \end{proof}

305: \section{The simplex value}\label{sec:simplex}
306: 
307: We now compute the value to which the product will be compared.
308: Only one extra segment beyond a coordinate cube is needed to describe
309: the projection body of a simplex.
310: 
311: \begin{proposition}\label{prop:simplex}
312: For \(d\ge2\), the standard simplex
313: \(T_d=\conv(0,e_1,\ldots,e_d)\subset\R^d\) satisfies
314: \[
315:  |T_d|=\frac1{d!},\qquad
316:  |\Pi T_d|=\frac{d+1}{((d-1)!)^d},\qquad
317:  R_d(T_d)=c_d.
318: \]
319: Every full-dimensional \(d\)-simplex has the same value \(c_d\).
320: \end{proposition}
321: 
322: \begin{proof}
323: The simplex volume follows by iterated base and height. Put
324: \(w=e_1+\cdots+e_d\). The coordinate facets have area-normal vectors
325: \(-e_i/(d-1)!\). The remaining facet has unit normal \(w/\sqrt d\).
326: Dropping its last coordinate projects it onto the standard
327: \((d-1)\)-simplex with Jacobian \(1/\sqrt d\); its area-normal vector is
328: therefore \(w/(d-1)!\). In the centered segments of
329: Lemma~\ref{lem:facets}, changing the sign of a generator changes no
330: segment. Translating each segment to start at the origin therefore shows
331: that \(\Pi T_d\) is a translate of
332: \[
333:  \frac{[0,1]^d+[0,w]}{(d-1)!}.
334: \]
335: 
336: To compute the numerator's volume, let \(Q=[0,1]^d\).
337: Every nonempty fiber of \(Q\) parallel to \(w\) is an interval, and
338: adding \([0,w]\) increases its length by \(\|w\|\).
339: The base projection is unchanged. Fubini therefore gives
340: \[
341:  |Q+[0,w]|
342:  =|Q|+\|w\|\vol_{d-1}(\proj_{w^\perp}Q)
343:  =1+h_{\Pi Q}(w).
344: \]
345: The cube has two facets of area one in each coordinate direction,
346: so \eqref{eq:facet} gives \(h_{\Pi Q}(w)=d\).
347: Thus \(|Q+[0,w]|=d+1\); Figure~\ref{fig:extrusion} illustrates the
348: two-dimensional case. Dilation by \(1/(d-1)!\) and division by
349: \(|T_d|^{d-1}\) now yield
350: \[
351:  R_d(T_d)=\frac{(d+1)(d!)^{d-1}}{((d-1)!)^d}
352:          =\frac{(d+1)d^d}{d!}.
353: \]
354: Every full-dimensional simplex is an invertible affine image of
355: \(T_d\), so Lemma~\ref{lem:affine} proves the final assertion.
356: \end{proof}

383: \section{The counterexample and exponential excess}\label{sec:counterexample}
384: 
385: The geometric work is complete. The product formula turns the comparison
386: in dimension twenty into a calculation with two simplex constants.
387: 
388: \begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:counterexample}]
389: The product \(K=T_{10}\times T_{10}\) is compact and convex with
390: nonempty interior in \(\R^{20}\). Propositions~\ref{prop:product}
391: and~\ref{prop:simplex} give
392: \[
393:  R_{20}(K)=R_{10}(T_{10})^2
394:          =\left(\frac{11\cdot10^{10}}{10!}\right)^2.
395: \]
396: Consequently,
397: \[
398:  \frac{R_{20}(K)}{c_{20}}
399:  =\frac{121\cdot10^{20}\cdot20!}
400:         {(10!)^2\cdot21\cdot20^{20}}
401:  =\frac{121\binom{20}{10}}{21\cdot2^{20}}
402:  =\frac{22\,355\,476}{22\,020\,096}>1.
403: \]
404: Here \(\binom{20}{10}=184\,756\), and the exact integer certificate is
405: \[
406:  121\cdot184\,756-21\cdot1\,048\,576=335\,380>0.
407: \]
408: This is an exact strict violation of \eqref{eq:proposed-bound}.
409: \end{proof}
410: 
411: More generally, for \(r,s\ge2\), the same two propositions give the
412: explicit product test
413: \[
414:  \frac{R_{r+s}(T_r\times T_s)}{c_{r+s}}
415:  =\frac{(r+1)(s+1)}{r+s+1}
416:    \binom{r+s}{r}\frac{r^rs^s}{(r+s)^{r+s}}.
417: \]
418: Thus any universal upper constant in dimension \(r+s\) must be at least
419: \(c_rc_s\).
420: 
421: Iterating the product test gives the following quantitative consequence.
422: 
423: \begin{corollary}\label{cor:exponential-excess}
424: There exist a constant \(\lambda>1\) and an integer \(N\ge20\) such that, for every
425: integer \(n\ge N\), some full-dimensional convex polytope
426: \(K_n\subset\R^n\) satisfies
427: \[
428:  \frac{R_n(K_n)}{R_n(T_n)}\ge\lambda^n.
429: \]
430: The polytopes \(K_n\) may be chosen as Cartesian products of simplices.
431: \end{corollary}
432: 
433: \begin{proof}
434: We use repeated ten-dimensional factors to obtain exponential growth,
435: and one factor of dimension between ten and nineteen to reach every
436: sufficiently large dimension. For \(r\in\{0,\ldots,9\}\), put
437: \(d_r=10+r\). Every integer \(n\ge20\) has a unique representation
438: \(n=10k+d_r\) with \(k\ge1\). In the orthogonal coordinate product
439: space, take
440: \[
441:  K_n=T_{10}^{\,k}\times T_{d_r}.
442: \]
443: Here \(T_{10}^{\,k}\) denotes the \(k\)-fold Cartesian product. All
444: factors and their successive products are full-dimensional convex
445: polytopes, with each factor dimension at least ten. Thus
446: Propositions~\ref{prop:product} and~\ref{prop:simplex} apply iteratively
447: and give
448: \[
449:  R_n(K_n)=c_{10}^k c_{d_r}.
450: \]
451: 
452: To compare this value with \(c_n\), we bound the simplex constants by
453: \(n+1\) times a fixed exponential, whose rate is smaller than that
454: supplied by the ten-dimensional factors. Put \(\beta=11/4\).
455: The exponential series gives
456: \[
457:  e=\frac52+\sum_{j=3}^{\infty}\frac1{j!}
458:  <\frac52+\frac16\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}3^{-j}
459:  =\beta,
460: \]
461: since the successive ratios in the tail are strictly less than \(1/3\).
462: The positive term \(n^n/n!\) in the series for \(e^n\) is strictly
463: smaller than the full sum, so
464: \[
465:  c_n<(n+1)e^n<(n+1)\beta^n\qquad(n\ge2).
466: \]
467: For the fixed ten-dimensional block, exact arithmetic yields
468: \[
469:  c_{10}=\frac{17\,187\,500}{567},\qquad
470:  A:=\frac{c_{10}}{\beta^{10}}
471:  =\frac{1\,638\,400\,000\,000}{1\,336\,956\,340\,797}>1.
472: \]
473: Choose a fixed \(\lambda\) with \(1<\lambda<A^{1/10}\). Combining
474: these estimates with the formula for \(R_n(K_n)\) gives
475: \[
476:  \frac{R_n(K_n)}{R_n(T_n)\lambda^n}
477:  >c_{d_r}\beta^{-d_r}\lambda^{-d_r}
478:    \frac{(A/\lambda^{10})^k}{10k+d_r+1}.
479: \]
480: For each fixed \(r\), the right side tends to infinity as \(k\to\infty\),
481: because \(A/\lambda^{10}>1\). A threshold can therefore be chosen in
482: each of the ten residue classes; their maximum, enlarged to be at least
483: twenty, gives one \(N\) for all \(n\ge N\).
484: \end{proof}
