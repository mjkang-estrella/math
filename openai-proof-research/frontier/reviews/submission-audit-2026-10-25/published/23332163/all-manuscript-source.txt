\begin{abstract}
Assume the complete Crouzeix bound and the analytic Faber coefficient
estimate stated below. Let $A$ be a complex matrix of order at most
four, and let $P$ be a matrix-valued polynomial of arbitrary finite
degree and coefficient size. If $\|P[A]\|=2\max_{W(A)}\|P\|>0$,
we prove that there are constant unit coefficient vectors $u,v$ for
which $p=v^*Pu$ attains the same equality. Jin's scalar rigidity
theorem then implies that $W(A)$ is a disk, and the extracted scalar
polynomial is a monomial after centering and scaling that disk.
The main step converts zero slack in a positive boundary-field
inequality into a rank-one condition on the coefficient factors of
extremal singular vectors. An elementary Hermitian-pencil argument
supplies the required regular boundary arc in dimension four.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and analytic inputs}
For $A\in M_n(\C)$, write
\[
 W(A)=\{x^*Ax:x\in\C^n,\ \|x\|=1\}.
\]
All norms without a subscript are Euclidean operator norms. If
$P(z)=\sum_{k=0}^N z^k C_k$ with $C_k\in M_m(\C)$, use the
base-first evaluation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:evaluation}
 P[A]=\sum_{k=0}^N A^k\otimes C_k.
\end{equation}
The coefficient size $m$ and degree $N$ are finite but unrestricted.

The complete Crouzeix inequality bounds \eqref{eq:evaluation} by twice
the supremum norm of $P$ on $W(A)$. Here we study an individual
polynomial attaining this bound. Our analytic inputs are the following
statements from the direct proof in \cite{Direct}. Stating them
explicitly separates the equality argument from the complete bound.

\begin{assumption}\label{ass:analytic}
The following two estimates hold.
\begin{enumerate}
\item For every finite $d,m$, every $B\in M_d(\C)$, and every
$M_m(\C)$-valued polynomial $F$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:complete}
 \|F[B]\|\le2\max_{z\in W(B)}\|F(z)\|.
\end{equation}
\item Let $\Omega$ be a bounded convex domain with regular real-analytic
Jordan boundary, and let $h$ be its normalized exterior conformal map,
\[
 h(z)=cz+c_0+O(z^{-1}),\qquad c>0.
\]
Define the Faber polynomials $b_k$ by the expansion at infinity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:faber}
 \frac{zh'(z)}{h(z)-w}=\sum_{k\ge0}b_k(w)z^{-k}.
\end{equation}
For every finite matrix polynomial $G=\sum_k b_k C_k$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coefficient}
 \sum_k\HS{C_k}^2
 \le\int_\T\HS{G(h(z))}^2\,d\tau(z)
 \le\HS{C_0}^2+2\sum_{k\ge1}\HS{C_k}^2,
\end{equation}
where $d\tau(e^{it})=dt/(2\pi)$ and the Hilbert--Schmidt norm is
unnormalized.
\end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

These are Theorem~1.1 and Proposition~2.2 of \cite{Direct}, at the
revision specified in the bibliography. We also use the analytic
convex outer approximation supplied by its Lemma~4.1. Every compact
convex set has bounded analytic convex outer neighborhoods converging
to it in Hausdorff distance. The remaining exterior-map estimates,
residual identities, and rank deductions are proved below.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Assume Assumption~\ref{ass:analytic}. Let $1\le n\le4$,
$A\in M_n(\C)$, and $P\in M_m(\C)[z]$, with $m<\infty$. Put
\[
 M=\max_{z\in W(A)}\|P(z)\|>0.
\]
If $\|P[A]\|=2M$, there are unit vectors $u,v\in\C^m$ such that
the scalar polynomial $p(z)=v^*P(z)u$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scalar}
 \|p(A)\|=2M,\qquad\max_{z\in W(A)}|p(z)|=M.
\end{equation}
Using the scalar rigidity theorem and finite interpolation lemma of
Jin \cite[Theorem~1 and Lemma~4]{Jin}, one further has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:disk}
 W(A)=a+\rho\overline\D,\qquad
 p(a+\rho z)=M\eta z^k,
\end{equation}
where $a\in\C$, $\rho>0$, $|\eta|=1$, and
$1\le k\le\deg P$.
\end{theorem}

The conclusion extracts a scalar polynomial from the given
matrix-valued polynomial by constant coefficient vectors. It does not
classify the evaluated operator $P[A]$. The theorem concerns attained
polynomial equality; it makes no assertion about a supremum approached
only by a sequence of changing witnesses.

\AA hag, Czy\.{z}, and Virtanen \cite{ACV} prove complete equality
results through base dimension three. Their finite-level reduction
leaves two orthogonal coefficient supports of dimensions $(2,2)$ as
the remaining possibility in dimension four. Their scalar polynomial
classification applies in every finite dimension. In the current
version, these results appear in Theorem~1.6 and Section~12.
The distinction is necessary: scalar equality theory does not by itself
produce a scalar compression of a specified matrix-valued extremizer.

Our argument uses the positive resolvent field for the original matrix.
Zero slack makes its range totally isotropic for the difference of two
singular-vector Gram matrices. At a simple support eigenvalue this
field has corank one, forcing both coefficient supports to have
dimension one. The needed simple support direction exists for every
unitarily irreducible matrix of order at most four. A boundary limit
allows us to use this direction on the numerical range itself.

\section{Exterior maps and a strong boundary limit}
We first justify the limiting step needed at equality. The numerical
range need not have a globally smooth boundary.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:limit}
Let $K\subset\C$ be a compact convex set with nonempty interior.
Let $\Omega_j$ be analytic convex outer neighborhoods converging to
$K$, and let $h_j$ be their exterior conformal maps with positive
leading coefficients. After passage to a subsequence, $h_j$ converges
uniformly on $\T$ to the exterior map $h$ of $K$, and
$q_j(z)=zh_j'(z)$ converges strongly in $L^2(\T)$ to $q(z)=zh'(z)$.
If $\sigma(B)\subset\operatorname{int}K$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Rlimit}
 R_j=q_j(h_jI-B)^{-1}\longrightarrow
 R=q(hI-B)^{-1}\quad\text{strongly in }L^2(\T).
\end{equation}
The fields $R_j$ are uniformly bounded in $L^\infty$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Translate so that $r\overline\D\subset K$ for some $r>0$, and
choose $R_0$ such that $\Omega_j\subset R_0\D$. Write
$h_j(z)=c_jz+c_{0,j}+O(z^{-1})$ and $\Phi_j=h_j^{-1}$.
Schwarz's lemma applied to $r/h_j(1/w)$ and to
$1/\Phi_j(R_0/w)$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:capacity}
 r\le c_j\le R_0.
\end{equation}
Both functions have removable values at $w=0$.

The tangent to the boundary is $izh_j'(z)$, with positive orientation.
Convexity gives
\[
 \Re\left(1+\frac{zh_j''(z)}{h_j'(z)}\right)\ge0
 \quad(|z|\ge1).
\]
The exterior Herglotz representation therefore supplies a probability
measure $\mu_j$ on $\T$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:logderivative}
 L_j(z):=\log\frac{h_j'(z)}{c_j}
       =2\int_\T\log(1-\xi/z)\,d\mu_j(\xi).
\end{equation}
The logarithms are normalized to vanish at infinity. One verifies
the sign by differentiating: $z\,\partial_z\log(1-\xi/z)
=\xi/(z-\xi)$. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:derivativebound}
 \Re L_j\le\log4,\qquad |h_j'|\le4c_j\le4R_0.
\end{equation}

The boundary maps are uniformly bounded and equi-Lipschitz. Moreover,
$|h_j(z)-c_jz|\le2R_0$ on the closed exterior, by the maximum
principle. Normal families and boundary compactness give a subsequence
converging locally uniformly outside the disk and uniformly on its
boundary. Its nonzero limiting leading coefficient makes the limit
nonconstant and univalent. This limit is the exterior map of $K$.
For completeness, every point of $K$ is omitted by the limit, by
Hurwitz's theorem. On compact subsets of $\C\setminus K$, the
inverse maps are eventually defined and satisfy
\[
 |\Phi_j(w)|\le\frac{|w|+2R_0}{r}.
\]
Their limits are nonconstant, as follows from
$|w-c_j\Phi_j(w)|\le2R_0$ for large $w$. The minimum-modulus
principle gives $|\Phi(w)|>1$, and passage to the limit in both
compositions proves that $\Phi$ and $h$ are inverses.
Uniform boundary convergence and Hausdorff convergence identify
$h(\T)=\partial K$. Since $\partial K$ is a Jordan curve, this
boundary extension is a homeomorphism.

Pass also to $c_j\to c$ and weak convergence $\mu_j\to\mu$.
The negative Fourier coefficients of $L_j$ are
\[
 -\frac2k\int_\T\xi^k\,d\mu_j(\xi),\qquad k\ge1.
\]
Each coefficient converges. The squared $L^2$ tail past $N$ is at
most $4\sum_{k>N}k^{-2}$, uniformly in $j$. Hence $L_j$ converges
strongly in $L^2(\T)$. On the half-plane $\Re z\le\log4$,
\[
 |e^s-e^t|\le4|s-t|,
\]
by integration along the segment from $s$ to $t$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:logderivative} now gives strong $L^2$ convergence
of $h_j'$ and $q_j$. Local uniform convergence in the exterior
identifies the limit with the boundary derivative of $h$, for example
by comparison of Laurent coefficients.

Finally, the resolvent of the fixed matrix $B$ is uniformly bounded
on a compact neighborhood of $\partial K$ disjoint from its
spectrum. Uniform convergence of $h_j$ gives uniform convergence of
the resolvents. Combining it with \eqref{eq:derivativebound} and
the strong convergence of $q_j$ proves \eqref{eq:Rlimit}.
\end{proof}

For $K=W(B)$, define $H_j=R_j+R_j^*$ and $H=R+R^*$.
Direct multiplication gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:congruence}
 (h_jI-B)^*H_j(h_jI-B)
       =2\Re\bigl(\overline{q_j}(h_jI-B)\bigr)\succeq0.
\end{equation}
Here $q_j$ is an outward normal and $W(B)\subset\Omega_j$.
Thus $H_j\succeq0$, and the strong limit satisfies $H\succeq0$
almost everywhere. On a regular real-analytic boundary arc of $K$,
the exterior map and its inverse extend by local Schwarz reflection.
Consequently $q$ is a nonzero normal on that arc, and
\eqref{eq:congruence} holds there with the subscripts removed.

\section{The complete residual and its equality case}
In this section, fix $B\in M_d(\C)$ with
$\sigma(B)\subset\operatorname{int}W(B)$. Normalize a polynomial
$P\in M_m(\C)[z]$ so that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalized}
 \max_{W(B)}\|P\|=1,\qquad\|P[B]\|=2.
\end{equation}
Choose any unit maximal singular pair $x,y$ satisfying
$P[B]x=2y$ and $P[B]^*y=2x$. Write
\[
 x=\vecop(X)=\sum_{i=1}^m Xe_i\otimes e_i,\qquad
 y=\vecop(Y),\qquad \HS X=\HS Y=1,
\]
where $X,Y$ are $d$-by-$m$ matrices.

Let $\Omega_j,h_j,q_j,R_j,H_j$ be as in Lemma~\ref{lem:limit}.
Set $M_j=\max_{\overline\Omega_j}\|P\|$, $F_j=P/M_j$, and
$\gamma_j=2/M_j$. Then $M_j\to1$, and the same pair $x,y$
is a maximal singular pair for $F_j[B]$ with singular value
$\gamma_j\to2$.

The Laurent expansion of $R_j$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:resolventseries}
 R_j(z)=\sum_{k\ge0}b_{k,j}(B)z^{-k},\qquad b_{0,j}=1.
\end{equation}
For fixed $j$, analytic boundary continuation and spectral separation
give a collar in which this resolvent is holomorphic. Thus the
coefficients decay geometrically and all the following Parseval
identities are justified. Put
\begin{align*}
 a_j^2&=\sum_{k\ge0}\HS{Y^*b_{k,j}(B)X}^2
             =\|Y^*R_jX\|_{L^2(\mathrm{HS})}^2,\\
 D_{X,j}&=\|X^*H_jX\|_{L^2(\mathrm{HS})}^2,
 &D_{Y,j}&=\|Y^*H_jY\|_{L^2(\mathrm{HS})}^2,\\
 C_j&=\|Y^*H_jX\|_{L^2(\mathrm{HS})}^2.
\end{align*}
Lemma~\ref{lem:limit} implies that $a_j$ is bounded.

We record the residual from the ordered multiplier argument of
\cite[Section~3]{Direct}, including its derivation to specify which
slack is used.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:residual}
Define
\begin{align*}
 T_j&=3\HS{Y^*X}^2+\sum_{k\ge1}\HS{X^*b_{k,j}(B)Y}^2,\\
 E_{X,j}&=4a_j^2/\gamma_j^2-D_{X,j},\qquad
 E_{Y,j}=4a_j^2/\gamma_j^2-D_{Y,j},\\
 E_{B,j}&=D_{X,j}+D_{Y,j}-2C_j.
\end{align*}
These quantities are nonnegative and satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:residual}
 E_{X,j}+E_{Y,j}+E_{B,j}+2T_j
       =2(4/\gamma_j^2-1)a_j^2.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Suppress $j$. For a finite Faber expansion $G=\sum_k b_k C_k$,
base-first evaluation gives the bilinear entry pairing
\[
 y^*G[B]x=\sum_{k,i,l}(Y^*b_k(B)X)_{il}(C_k)_{il}.
\]
Cauchy--Schwarz bounds its modulus by
$a(\sum_k\HS{C_k}^2)^{1/2}$. Evaluation preserves multiplication
in its given order. The singular-vector identities therefore give
\[
 y^*(FG)[B]x=\gamma x^*G[B]x,\qquad
 y^*(GF)[B]x=\gamma y^*G[B]y.
\]
Apply the preceding coefficient functional to the full expansions
of $FG$ and $GF$. The lower bound in \eqref{eq:coefficient} and
$\|F\|_{\partial\Omega}\le1$ bound each by
$a\|G\circ h\|_{L^2(\mathrm{HS})}$.
The upper bound in \eqref{eq:coefficient} then yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ordered}
 \max\{|x^*G[B]x|,|y^*G[B]y|\}
 \le\frac a\gamma
       \left(\HS{C_0}^2+2\sum_{k\ge1}\HS{C_k}^2\right)^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
Duality of this weighted coefficient norm gives
\[
 \HS{X^*X}^2+\frac12\sum_{k\ge1}\HS{X^*b_k(B)X}^2
       \le a^2/\gamma^2,
\]
and the same estimate with $Y$. This can also be seen by testing
\eqref{eq:ordered} with the entrywise conjugate coefficient arrays,
with weight $1/2$ on each positive index, and then taking finite
truncation limits. Parseval gives $D_X,D_Y\le4a^2/\gamma^2$.

The constant coefficient of $Y^*HX$ is $2Y^*X$. Its negative
coefficients are $Y^*b_k(B)X$, and its positive coefficients are
the adjoints of $X^*b_k(B)Y$. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cross}
 C=4\HS{Y^*X}^2+
       \sum_{k\ge1}\bigl(\HS{Y^*b_k(B)X}^2+
                         \HS{X^*b_k(B)Y}^2\bigr)=a^2+T.
\end{equation}
The block matrix $\Delta=[X\ Y]^*H[X\ Y]$ is positive.
For $J=\diag(I_m,-I_m)$, positivity of $J\Delta J$ implies
\[
 0\le\tr(\Delta J\Delta J)
   =\HS{X^*HX}^2+\HS{Y^*HY}^2-2\HS{Y^*HX}^2.
\]
Its integral is $E_B$, proving nonnegativity of the last residual.
Substitution of \eqref{eq:cross} proves \eqref{eq:residual}.
\end{proof}

Since $a_j$ is bounded and $\gamma_j\to2$, every term on the left
of \eqref{eq:residual} tends to zero. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:orthogonal}
 X^*Y=0,\qquad E_{B,j}\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
Put $D=XX^*-YY^*$. The pointwise block slack is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:isotropyintegrand}
 \tr(\Delta_jJ\Delta_jJ)=\tr(H_jDH_jD)
                 =\HS{H_j^{1/2}DH_j^{1/2}}^2.
\end{equation}
Strong $L^2$ convergence of $H_j$ gives $L^1$ convergence of
$\tr(H_jDH_jD)$ to $\tr(HDHD)$. Indeed, expand the difference
as $(H_j-H)DH_jD+HD(H_j-H)D$ and use Cauchy--Schwarz.
The limiting integrand is nonnegative and its integral is zero by
\eqref{eq:orthogonal}. Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:isotropy}
 H(t)^{1/2}DH(t)^{1/2}=0\quad\text{for almost every }t.
\end{equation}
Thus $\ran H(t)$ is totally isotropic for the Hermitian form $D$.
This conclusion uses a strong boundary limit; a weak limit of the
fields would not justify the quadratic passage.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:scalarize}
In the setting of \eqref{eq:normalized}, suppose that $W(B)$ has
a regular real-analytic boundary arc on which its supporting
Hermitian matrix has a simple largest eigenvalue. Then every maximal
singular pair as above satisfies $\rank X=\rank Y=1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
On this arc, put $\nu=q/|q|$. The limiting congruence is
\[
 (hI-B)^*H(hI-B)
   =2|q|\bigl(\Re(\overline\nu h)I-\Re(\overline\nu B)\bigr).
\]
The bracket has nullity one. Since the resolvent is invertible and
$q\ne0$, we have $\rank H=d-1$ there. The arc has an open circle
interval as its conformal preimage, so \eqref{eq:isotropy} holds
at some point of the arc.

Let $p=\rank X$ and $s=\rank Y$. Orthogonality
\eqref{eq:orthogonal} gives inertia $(p,s,d-p-s)$ for $D$.
A totally isotropic subspace for this form has dimension at most
\[
 d-p-s+\min(p,s)=d-\max(p,s).
\]
To see this, quotient by its intersection with $\ker D$ and project
to the positive and negative spectral subspaces. Each projection is
injective, so the quotient has dimension at most $\min(p,s)$.
Applying this bound to $\ran H$ gives $\max(p,s)\le1$.
Both $X$ and $Y$ have Hilbert--Schmidt norm one, so $p=s=1$.
\end{proof}

The field $H$ here is the mass-two resolvent field for $B$, not the
mass-one representing density of a similar matrix in
\cite[Proposition~3.1 and Section~4]{Structural}. Its congruence with
the supporting Hermitian pencil is the reason its rank carries the
needed geometric information.

\section{A simple support direction in dimension four}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:arc}
Every unitarily irreducible $B\in M_d(\C)$ with $2\le d\le4$
has interior spectrum and a regular real-analytic boundary arc of
$W(B)$ with simple largest support eigenvalue.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $W(B)$ had empty interior, a translation and rotation would make
$B$ Hermitian, contradicting irreducibility. If $Bv=\lambda v$
with $\lambda\in\partial W(B)$, choose a supporting normal $\nu$.
The positive matrix
$\Re(\overline\nu\lambda)I-\Re(\overline\nu B)$ has zero
quadratic form at $v$, so it annihilates $v$. Combining this with
the eigenvector equation gives $B^*v=\overline\lambda v$.
The line $\C v$ would reduce $B$. Hence
$\sigma(B)\subset\operatorname{int}W(B)$.

Suppose that $H_\theta=\Re(e^{-i\theta}B)$ never has a simple
largest eigenvalue. Replacing $\theta$ by $\theta+\pi$ shows that
its smallest eigenvalue is also always multiple. For $d=2,3$, this
forces every $H_\theta$ to be scalar, a contradiction.

For $d=4$, subtract $\tr(B)I/4$. Each $H_\theta$ now has trace
zero and is either zero or has eigenvalues $s_\theta,-s_\theta$,
each of multiplicity two. Thus $H_\theta^2$ is scalar for every
$\theta$. Writing $B=H_0+iK_0$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:clifford}
 H_0^2=\alpha I,\qquad K_0^2=\beta I,\qquad
 H_0K_0+K_0H_0=2cI,
\end{equation}
with $\alpha,\beta\ge0$ and $c\in\R$.
If $\alpha=0$, then $H_0=0$ and $B$ is normal. Otherwise put
$K'=K_0-(c/\alpha)H_0$. It anticommutes with $H_0$ and satisfies
$(K')^2=\delta I$, where $\delta=\beta-c^2/\alpha\ge0$.
In the two eigenspaces of $H_0$,
\[
 H_0=\sqrt\alpha\begin{pmatrix}I_2&0\\0&-I_2\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad K'=\begin{pmatrix}0&C\\C^*&0\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad CC^*=C^*C=\delta I_2.
\]
If $\delta=0$, then $K'=0$ and $B$ is normal. If $\delta>0$,
separate unitary changes on the two eigenspaces make
$C=\sqrt\delta I_2$. Reordering the coordinates then splits both
$H_0$ and $K_0$ into two identical two-dimensional blocks.
This too contradicts irreducibility. A simple largest support
eigenvalue must exist.

Simplicity persists on an interval. Let $\lambda(\theta)$ denote
that eigenvalue, let $v$ be a unit eigenvector, and complete it to an
orthonormal eigenbasis $v,v_j$ with eigenvalues $\lambda,\lambda_j$.
Differentiating the eigenvalue equation, using
$H_\theta''=-H_\theta$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:curvature}
 \lambda+\lambda''
       =2\sum_j\frac{|v_j^*H_\theta'v|^2}{\lambda-\lambda_j}>0.
\end{equation}
The denominators are positive. Equality would make $v$ a common
eigenvector of $H_\theta$ and $H_\theta'$, and hence a reducing
eigenvector for $B$. Thus the exposed point
\[
 z(\theta)=e^{i\theta}(\lambda+i\lambda'),\qquad
 z'(\theta)=ie^{i\theta}(\lambda+\lambda'')
\]
parametrizes the required regular analytic arc.
\end{proof}

\section{Scalar compression and the full numerical range}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Divide $P$ by $M$, so $M=1$. Decompose $A$ unitarily into
irreducible reducing blocks. Their polynomial evaluations form an
orthogonal direct sum. Some block $B$ therefore satisfies
$\|P[B]\|=2$. A scalar block has evaluation norm at most one,
so $2\le d=\dim B\le4$. By \eqref{eq:complete},
\[
 2=\|P[B]\|\le2\max_{W(B)}\|P\|\le2.
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:arc} supplies interior spectrum and the required arc.
Proposition~\ref{prop:scalarize} implies that the coefficient
matrices of a maximal singular pair have rank one. In the stated
vectorization convention, write
\[
 X=\xi u^T,\qquad Y=\zeta v^T,
\]
with all four vectors unit. Thus $x=\xi\otimes u$ and
$y=\zeta\otimes v$. For $p=v^*Pu$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pairscalar}
 \zeta^*p(B)\xi=y^*P[B]x=2,
 \qquad |p(z)|\le\|P(z)\|\quad(z\in\C).
\end{equation}
The scalar case of \eqref{eq:complete} now gives
$\|p(B)\|=2$ and $\max_{W(B)}|p|=1$. Since $B$ is a reducing
block of $A$, the same bounds yield
$\|p(A)\|=2$ and $\max_{W(A)}|p|=1$.
This proves the scalar-compression conclusion without an external
scalar equality theorem.

To identify the geometry, define the scalar polynomial constant
\[
 \psi(B)=\sup\{\|f(B)\|:f\in\C[z],\ \max_{W(B)}|f|\le1\}.
\]
The complete bound and \eqref{eq:pairscalar} give $\psi(B)=2$.
Jin's scalar rigidity theorem \cite[Theorem~1]{Jin} implies
$W(B)=a+\rho\overline\D$ for some $\rho>0$.
It remains to control the other reducing blocks of $A$.

Put $T=(B-aI)/\rho$ and $g(z)=p(a+\rho z)$. Then
$\sigma(T)\subset\D$, $\|g\|_{\overline\D}=1$, and
$\|g(T)\|=2$. Prescribe at each eigenvalue of $T$ the contiguous
jet of $g$ through one less than the largest corresponding Jordan
block size. These finitely many data determine $g(T)$.
Their least possible $H^\infty(\D)$ interpolation norm is one.
Indeed, if an interpolant $f$ had norm $c<1$, then for $0<r<1$
the function $f_r(z)=f(rz)$ is holomorphic near $\overline\D$
and has norm at most $c$. Taylor approximation and the scalar case
of \eqref{eq:complete} give $\|f_r(T)\|\le2c$.
As $r\uparrow1$, the interior spectral jets converge, so
$\|f(T)\|\le2c<2$, contradicting $f(T)=g(T)$.

The finite interpolation lemma \cite[Lemma~4]{Jin} says that such
minimal-norm-one data have a unique Schur-class interpolant and
that it is a finite Blaschke product. Hence $g$ is a finite Blaschke
product. Since $g$ is a polynomial, all its Blaschke zeros are zero:
a nonzero zero $\alpha\in\D$ would give a pole at
$1/\overline\alpha$, which cannot cancel a numerator zero inside
the disk. Therefore $g(z)=\eta z^k$ with $|\eta|=1$ and
$1\le k\le\deg P$. The exponent is positive because a constant
cannot evaluate with norm two.

This polynomial identity holds globally. The bound $|p|\le1$ on
$W(A)$ therefore forces $W(A)\subset a+\rho\overline\D$.
The reverse inclusion follows from $W(B)\subset W(A)$.
Restoring $M$ proves \eqref{eq:disk}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
Under the analytic and scalar inputs of Theorem~\ref{thm:main},
if $A$ has order at most four and its numerical range is not a
nondegenerate disk, then every finite matrix polynomial $P$ with
$\max_{W(A)}\|P\|>0$ satisfies
\[
 \|P[A]\|<2\max_{W(A)}\|P\|.
\]
\end{corollary}

This is strictness for each polynomial separately. The proof does not
give a uniform gap as the polynomial or coefficient size varies.
It also gives no quantitative near-equality estimate: the boundary
arc, support gap, and spectral separation used above can degenerate
along a sequence approaching a reducible matrix.

\begin{remark}
Scalar input compression should be distinguished from scalar output
structure. Let
\[
 A=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 P(z)=\begin{pmatrix}0&z\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Here $W(A)=\overline\D$, $\max_{\overline\D}\|P\|=1$, and
$\|P[A]\|=2$. After permuting the tensor factors, $P[A]$ is the
weighted shift with weights $(1,2,1)$. Its unique maximal right
singular vector has nonzero second iterate, so it cannot belong to a
reducing two-dimensional nilpotent block of norm two. Nevertheless,
$e_1^*P(z)e_2=z$ is exactly the scalar compression required by the
theorem.
\end{remark}
