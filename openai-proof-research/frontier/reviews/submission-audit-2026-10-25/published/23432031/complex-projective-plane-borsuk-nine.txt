          Let X = {uu : u ∈ C , kuk = 1} be the rank-one projector realization of the complex
                                3

                                                        √ of trace-one Hermitian matrices, with
      the Frobenius metric. We prove that diam X = 2 and that its Borsuk number is exactly
      nine. The lower bound applies to covers by arbitrary subsets and follows from a Hermitian
      version of a known spectral odd-map construction. For the upper bound, we perturb the
      nine rays of the Hesse symmetric informationally complete conﬁguration. A cyclic assign-

      action. A single covariant tangent, together with a uniform two-trio estimate in full complex
      neighborhoods, removes all equality holes and excludes displaced ones. The resulting nine
      maximum-overlap cells are compact and have strictly smaller diameter. The same construc-
      tion gives nine Fubini–Study balls of radius strictly below π/4 covering CP2 . The result
      attains the bound d + 1 in dimension eight.


1    Statement and relation to earlier methods
For a bounded metric space Y of positive diameter, its Borsuk number b(Y ) is the least number
of subsets of Y , each of diameter strictly smaller than diam Y , whose union is Y . No regularity
is imposed on the subsets. We use the Hermitian inner product hu, vi = u∗ v, conjugate-linear
in its ﬁrst argument, and put

             T = {A ∈ Herm3 : tr A = 1},           X = {Pu = uu∗ : u ∈ C3 , kuk = 1}.

The real vector space Herm3 has dimension nine, and T has dimension eight. Its metric is
induced by the Frobenius norm kAk2F = tr(A∗ A).

The distinction between a strict uniform bound in (1.1) and a pairwise packing bound is essen-
tial.
    The lower-bound mechanism is adapted from the real-projector construction of OpenAI [1]:
enlarge a strict-gap cover, extend a homogeneous partition to positive semideﬁnite matrices,

and subtract the extensions on the positive and negative spectral parts. We give the complete
Hermitian argument. No real equality-case obstruction or source theorem is needed as a premise.
The Hesse conﬁguration, Weyl–Heisenberg action, and twelve mutually unbiased normals are
standard antecedents; see Dang, Blanchﬁeld, Bengtsson and Appleby [2] and Hughston and
Salamon [3]. Their geometry is recalled explicitly below.
    The additional operation is the uniform removal of the Hesse equality holes by a non-SIC
covariant perturbation. A positive derivative at each old hole alone would not control new
nearby minimizers. Lemma 4.2 supplies that missing neighborhood estimate. Compactness and
the conversion to strict smaller-diameter cells are then standard. Section 6 and Appendix A
distinguish this operation from speciﬁc earlier constructions. This is a bounded comparison,
not a claim to have classiﬁed all nine-ray coverings or established worldwide priority.

6    Scope of the construction and comparison
The Hesse SIC itself has α = 1/2 by Lemma 3.4. Its unperturbed maximum-overlap cells contain
orthogonal pairs; neither its optimal separation nor its incidence data makes their diameters
strictly smaller. The perturbation deliberately leaves the SIC locus. If p, q are the two nonzero
squared coordinate moduli of fε , then

                 1       ε                   1       ε                                 1     ε2
            p=     +√            ,      q=     −√            ,                  pq =     −           .
                 2    2(1 + ε2 )             2    2(1 + ε2 )                           4 2(1 + ε2 )2

Within a coordinate-zero group, distinct orbit rays have squared overlap 1 − 3pq > 1/4; between
groups it is pq < 1/4. Thus the positive-ε conﬁguration is not a unitary relabeling of a SIC or
of a conﬁguration having twenty-seven overlaps exactly equal to 1/4.
    Hughston–Salamon classify qutrit SICs without assuming covariance [3]. In their midpoint
representation all nonzero coordinates of a SIC ray have equal modulus. A coordinate ray
therefore has maximum score 1/2, so that classiﬁed family does not by itself give the strict
cap cover here. Their explicit non-SIC examples require a separate comparison: Appendix A
gives exact witnesses below 1/2 for the three-basis WH example and for the particular quartic
example in their Section 10. This does not exclude every choice in their general polynomial
family or every non-SIC WH orbit.
    A diﬀerent useful distinction is provided by Mondal–Heath [4, Theorem 1 and Appendix A].
Their covering-codebook criterion identiﬁes projection covering radius strictly less than one with
covering by complementary subspaces. For rays, this gives α > 0; the requirement here is α >
1/2. Compactness supplies a positive margin from pointwise positivity, but does not prescribe
that margin to exceed 1/2. Their coordinate antenna codebook has α = 1/3.   √ Likewise, for any
codebook whose normalized vectors have all three coordinate moduli 1/ 3, every coordinate
ray has score 1/3 against every center. A packing assertion about such a codebook cannot give
the present cover.

displacement D, up to an irrelevant scalar phase. Its three eigenvalues, after a common phase
change, are 1, ω, ω 2 , all simple. If pj are the squared moduli of f in that eigenbasis, then

                          p0 + p1 + p2 = 1,      p0 + ωp1 + ω 2 p2 = 0.

Taking real and imaginary parts gives p0 = p1 = p2 = 1/3. Conjugation of D by any WH
displacement multiplies it by a scalar, so the same reasoning applies to every orbit vector. Each
D-eigenvector consequently has squared overlap 1/3 with all nine rays.

    Hughston–Salamon’s Example 6.4 [3] is a WH orbit forming three pairwise mutually unbi-
ased orthonormal bases. Its twenty-seven nonzero pair scores are 1/3 and its other nine pairs
are orthogonal. Proposition A.1 excludes the strict cap-cover property for that example without
assuming that an arbitrary MUB triple extends to a fourth basis.
