\begin{abstract}
For every convex polytope $K\subset\R^4$ with nonempty interior and at
most six actual facets, we prove
$V_4(\Pi K)\le (160/3)V_4(K)^3$, with equality precisely for simplices.
Here $h_{\Pi K}(u)=V_3(K\mid u^\perp)$ for unit $u$.
An attained simplex-section maximum reduces the six-facet case to a
signed sum of four independent exponential variables. A classical
log-concavity and Laplace comparison, followed by a two-bracket
identity, reduces the estimate to ten polynomial certificates.
We give all their coefficients and positive endpoint terms, so repeated
knots, zero coefficients and nonsimple polytopes are included.
This is a bounded-class result; it gives no theorem for seven or more
facets, general four-dimensional bodies, or dimensions five through eight.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textit{Review status.} The full proof and exact finite support are
presented for independent manuscript review. Independently reviewed Lean
support covers only the deterministic algebra of Proposition B; the full
geometric theorem is unformalized. This implementation confers no acceptance
or release. Original prose: CC BY 4.0; original code: MIT.

\section{Statement, context and proof structure}
A convex body is compact and convex with nonempty interior. A facet always
means an actual irredundant face of codimension one, not a listed inequality
in a redundant description. Write $V_j$ for $j$-dimensional Euclidean
volume, and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ratio}
 \PP_n(K)=\frac{V_n(\Pi K)}{V_n(K)^{n-1}},\qquad
 c_n=\frac{(n+1)n^n}{n!}.
\end{equation}
The projection body is unpolarized: its support function on the sphere is
$h_{\Pi K}(u)=V_{n-1}(K\mid u^\perp)$, extended homogeneously elsewhere.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Every convex polytope $K\subset\R^4$ with nonempty interior and at most
six actual irredundant facets satisfies
\[
 V_4(\Pi K)\le \frac{160}{3}V_4(K)^3.
\]
Equality holds if and only if $K$ is a simplex. In particular, every
exactly-six-facet case is strict, including nonsimple polytopes and all
repeated, zero and boundary Gale-coordinate cases.
\end{theorem}

The simplex upper-bound question originates with Brannen; our historical
attribution is through the primary texts actually read, principally
Feng--Hu--Liu--Xu (FHLX)~\cite{FHLX} and Saroglou~\cite{Sar11,Sar15}.
FHLX v1, Theorem 1.3 and Section 4, constructs counterexamples in every
$n\ge9$ using at most $n+2$ facets, and its introduction leaves dimensions
$4$ through $8$ unresolved. This is a statement about that version's
reported context, not a fresh exhaustive open-status survey.
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} treats only the stated four-dimensional class.
It is not a solution of the general Brannen problem.

The pinned OpenAI Source P~\cite{SourceP}, at revision
\texttt{fd4aeeb2ee4fc729c18d98444fed42fd0529eeeb}, supplies the facet,
affine and simplex interfaces (\texttt{lem:facets}, \texttt{lem:affine},
\texttt{prop:simplex}). Its product construction is not a proof for all
six-facet polytopes. The pinned theorems and their operative proofs are
stipulated true in this project; this manuscript does not audit them.
The companion Petty manuscript~\cite{PettyComp} concerns the lower bound
and supplies no upper-bound premise here.

The exact section reduction in Sections~\ref{sec:geometry}--\ref{sec:normal}
credits FHLX Section 4, especially Propositions 4.1--4.2, and the earlier
project round r132, R1--R4 and its normal form $(\star)$~\cite{r132}.
The latter was a prefrozen exploration with the global estimate unpaid.
The contribution assessed in the project is the joint assembly of the
conditional-parts Laplace comparison, two-bracket separation and complete
four-variable certificate, with geometric strictness. Log-concavity,
reliability comparisons, partial fractions and coefficient positivity
are not individually claimed as new. In particular the HNBUE and
Laplace-transform literature includes Klefsj\"o~\cite{K82,K83}; the
needed estimate is proved below, without treating those unread bodies
as operative premises.

The novelty language is limited: the result is new to this project, and
no antecedent was found in the recorded bounded searches. We make no
first-result or worldwide-priority claim. The original author and
contribution searches were not exhaustive; their exact reading limits
are described in Appendix~\ref{app:provenance}. No optimal auxiliary
constant or all-dimensions positive threshold is claimed.

Here is the chain of the proof. Actual facet data give an attained
maximum of sections of a simplex. Complementary minors, linear coarea
and the Gamma--Dirichlet change of variables identify it with a density
at zero. Integrating two extreme exponential summands leaves four middle
coefficients. Conditional log-concavity bounds the resulting Laplace
expectation. A deterministic identity reduces its strict upper bound to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:S4}
 D(b)\Prob(W_b>0)^2\le5\E W_b^+,
 \quad W_b=\sum_{j=1}^4 b_jE_j,\quad
 D(b)=\sum_{i<j}|b_i-b_j|,
\end{equation}
for $b$ and $-b$, with strictness for mixed signs. The $E_j$ are independent
exponentials of mean one; $w^+=\max(w,0)$ and $w^-=\max(-w,0)$.
All ten sign patterns are proved by the explicit appendix certificates.

\section{Facet geometry and an attained maximum}\label{sec:geometry}
We first work in dimension $n\ge2$. Let $K$ have $m$ actual facets,
unit outward normals $u_i$, positive areas $f_i$, and supports
$h_i=h_K(u_i)$. Translate an interior point to zero, so $h_i>0$.
Put $w_i=f_i u_i$ and let $W$ have these columns. The divergence theorem,
applied to constant vector fields and to $x/n$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:balance}
 \sum_i w_i=0,\qquad \sum_i f_i h_i=nV_n(K).
\end{equation}
The normals span $\R^n$: otherwise a nonzero orthogonal vector would be
a lineality direction of the intersection of all facet halfspaces,
contradicting compactness. Thus $\rank W=n$.

For almost every line parallel to a unit vector $u$ over the projection
of $K$, its two boundary points lie in facet interiors. The exceptional
projections of lower-dimensional faces and facets parallel to $u$ have
measure zero. Projection of the $i$th facet has Jacobian
$|\langle u_i,u\rangle|$. Counting the two endpoints proves Cauchy's formula
and fixes the normalization:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zonotope}
 h_{\Pi K}(u)=\tfrac12\sum_i f_i|\langle u_i,u\rangle|,
 \qquad \Pi K=\sum_i[-w_i/2,w_i/2].
\end{equation}
This is also Source P, \texttt{lem:facets}.
The segment $[-w_i/2,w_i/2]$ is a translate of $[0,w_i]$; no extra power
of two appears in the zonotope volume formula
\begin{equation}\label{eq:minors}
 V_n(\Pi K)=\sum_{|J|=n}|\det W_J|.
\end{equation}
To justify it, adding $[0,v]$ extends each nonempty fiber parallel to
$v$ by $|v|$, without changing its projection. Fubini gives
$V_n(Z+[0,v])=V_n(Z)+|v|V_{n-1}(Z\mid v^\perp)$, also when $Z$ has
lower dimension. Induction on generators and dimension proves
\eqref{eq:minors}: each projected determinant times $|v|$ is the
absolute determinant with $v$ adjoined. Every unordered $n$-subset
occurs once and dependent subsets contribute zero.

We use the following precise form of Minkowski's first inequality:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:minkowski}
 V_n(K)^{(n-1)/n}V_n(L)^{1/n}
 \le\frac1n\sum_i f_i h_L(u_i)
\end{equation}
for compact convex $K,L$ with interiors, with $K$ a polytope. No condition
that $L$ contain zero is needed. For clarity, its classical foundation
can be reduced to Pr\'ekopa's integral inequality~\cite[Theorem 3]{Prekopa}.
Applied to indicators of convex sets it gives the multiplicative
Brunn--Minkowski inequality. If $a=V(A)^{1/n}$ and $b=V(B)^{1/n}$,
apply that inequality to the unit-volume sets $A/a,B/b$ at weights
$a/(a+b),b/(a+b)$, then dilate by $a+b$. This proves
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BM}
 V(A+B)^{1/n}\ge V(A)^{1/n}+V(B)^{1/n}.
\end{equation}
The first variation at a full-dimensional polytope is
\[
 \left.\frac{d}{dt}V_n(K+tL)\right|_{0+}=\sum_i f_i h_L(u_i).
\]
Here is a local justification. Translate $L$ to contain zero and bound
it in a fixed ball. Away from an $O(t)$ neighborhood of the finitely
many facet boundaries, an exposed point of $L$ in direction $u_i$
and convexity fill the normal layer of thickness $t h_L(u_i)$.
Conversely every point of $K+tL$ satisfies the displaced supporting
inequalities. The remaining regions lie in $O(t)$ neighborhoods of
faces of codimension at least two, with volume $O(t^2)$; the facet
boundary strips also have area $O(t)$ and thickness $O(t)$.
This proves the derivative. Translating back changes the sum by zero
by \eqref{eq:balance}. Differentiating \eqref{eq:BM} with $B=tL$
now gives \eqref{eq:minkowski}. These arguments require neither
simplicity nor an equality classification for Minkowski's inequality.

Let $R$ be the row space of $W$ and $L_0=\ker W=R^\perp$.
Define
\[
 H=\{s\in\R^m:\textstyle\sum_i s_i=1\},\quad
 \Delta=H\cap[0,\infty)^m,\quad
 S_\lambda=(\lambda+R)\cap\Delta\quad(\lambda\in H).
\]
By \eqref{eq:balance}, $\one\in L_0$, so $R\subset\one^\perp$.

\begin{lemma}[Exact section maximum]\label{lem:maximum}
With intrinsic $n$-dimensional section measure,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:maximum}
 V_n(K)^{1-n}=
 \frac{n^n}{\sqrt{\det WW^T}}\max_{\lambda\in H}\HH^n(S_\lambda).
\end{equation}
The maximum is attained at
$\lambda_i^*=f_i h_i/(nV_n(K))$, and every $\lambda_i^*>0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Set $K_\lambda=\{x:\langle w_i,x\rangle\le\lambda_i\ \forall i\}$.
The injective affine map $x\mapsto\lambda-W^Tx$ maps this set exactly
onto $S_\lambda$ and has $n$-Jacobian $\sqrt{\det WW^T}$.
Its inverse on the image is continuous, so $K_\lambda$ is compact when
nonempty. Empty or lower-dimensional sections have zero $n$-volume.
Otherwise $h_{K_\lambda}(u_i)\le\lambda_i/f_i$, and
\eqref{eq:minkowski} gives
\[
 V_n(K)^{(n-1)/n}V_n(K_\lambda)^{1/n}
 \le\tfrac1n\sum_i\lambda_i=\tfrac1n.
\]
This applies even to negative trial supports $\lambda_i$.
Thus $n^nV_n(K_\lambda)\le V_n(K)^{1-n}$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:balance} puts $\lambda^*$ in $H$, with positive
coordinates, and $K_{\lambda^*}=K/(nV_n(K))$. Its volume realizes
the bound, proving both equality and attainment.
\end{proof}

\section{Gale coordinates, coarea and exponential normalization}
Assume now $m=n+2$. The kernel is two-dimensional, so
$L_0=\operatorname{span}\{\one,a\}$ for a nonzero $a\perp\one$.
Let $I=(\min_i a_i,\max_i a_i)$; it is nonempty.
For any vector $c$, write $D(c)=\sum_{i<j}|c_i-c_j|$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:gale}
$V_n(\Pi K)/\sqrt{\det WW^T}=D(a)/(\sqrt m\,|a|)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose an $n\times m$ matrix $U$ with orthonormal rows spanning $R$.
Then $W=MU$ with $M=WU^T$ invertible and
$\sqrt{\det WW^T}=|\det M|$.
Complete $U$ to an orthogonal matrix $Q$ by the rows
$\one/\sqrt m$ and $a/|a|$. The complementary-minor identity for
an orthogonal matrix gives, for $J^c=\{i,j\}$,
\[
 |\det U_J|=\left|\det
 \begin{pmatrix}1/\sqrt m&1/\sqrt m\\ a_i/|a|&a_j/|a|\end{pmatrix}\right|
 =\frac{|a_i-a_j|}{\sqrt m\,|a|}.
\]
One may obtain the identity by taking exterior powers of $Q$:
Hodge duality sends the wedge on $J$ to the complementary wedge, and
an orthogonal map commutes with this operation up to its determinant
$\pm1$. Taking absolute values removes that sign. Equivalently it is
Jacobi's cofactor identity using $Q^{-1}=Q^T$.
Now \eqref{eq:minors} and $\det W_J=\det M\det U_J$ give the claim.
No coordinate differences have been divided out.
\end{proof}

Let $s$ be uniform in $\Delta$ with respect to intrinsic volume,
$X=\langle a,s\rangle$, and
$\sigma(t)=\HH^n(\Delta\cap\{\langle a,s\rangle=t\})$.
The edge Gram matrix of $\Delta=\conv(e_1,\ldots,e_m)$ is
$I+\one\one^T$ of determinant $m$, so
$\HH^{m-1}(\Delta)=\sqrt m/(m-1)!$.
The gradient of $\langle a,s\rangle$ in $H$ is $a$. Resolving Euclidean
measure in the direction $a/|a|$ gives the linear coarea density
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coarea}
 \phi_X(t)=\frac{(m-1)!}{\sqrt m\,|a|}\sigma(t),\qquad t\in I.
\end{equation}
It is zero off the closed interval, with arbitrary endpoint values.
Every interior $t$ has a barycentric representative with all coordinates
positive, and hence a section with positive $n$-volume. Translate parallel
sections into a common $n$-space. Their convex combinations are contained
in the intermediate section; \eqref{eq:BM} makes $\sigma^{1/n}$ concave.
It is continuous on $I$. Section volumes are bounded, since the sections
all lie in a fixed ball. Thus we may use a bounded density $\phi_X$
continuous throughout $I$, regardless of repeated knots or endpoint
section volumes.

For independent $E_i\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1)$ set $S=\sum_iE_i$.
In the affine coordinates $s_1,\ldots,s_{m-1}$, $E_i=rs_i$ has absolute
Jacobian $r^{m-1}$. The joint density becomes $e^{-r}r^{m-1}$,
independent of $s$ on the simplex. Its normalization proves that $s=E/S$
is uniform, independent of $S$, with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gamma}
 S\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(m,1),\quad
 \gamma_m(r)=\frac{r^{m-1}e^{-r}}{(m-1)!},\quad
 \E S^{-1}=\frac{\Gamma(m-1)}{\Gamma(m)}=\frac1{m-1}.
\end{equation}
For $t\in I$ define $Y_t=\sum_i(a_i-t)E_i=S(X-t)$.
At least one coefficient is positive and one negative. Their two-term
sum has a bounded continuous asymmetric-Laplace density; convolution
with the remaining law gives a bounded continuous density $f_{Y_t}$.
Independently the product representation has density
\[
 \psi_t(y)=\int_0^\infty\phi_X(t+y/r)\,\frac{\gamma_m(r)}r\,dr.
\]
Boundedness and continuity of $\phi_X$ at $t$, together with
\eqref{eq:gamma}, give continuity of $\psi_t$ at zero by dominated
convergence. The two densities agree almost everywhere and are both
continuous there, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:section-density}
 f_{Y_t}(0)=\frac{\phi_X(t)}{m-1},\qquad
 \sigma(t)=\frac{\sqrt m\,|a|}{(m-2)!}f_{Y_t}(0).
\end{equation}
This fixes every factorial and density version used below.

\begin{proposition}[Attained density formula, Proposition A]\label{prop:A}
For an actual $(n+2)$-facet polytope,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:A}
 \frac{\PP_n(K)}{c_n}
 =\max_{t\in I}\frac{D(a)}{n+1}f_{Y_t}(0).
\end{equation}
The maximum is attained at $t^*=\langle a,\lambda^*\rangle\in I$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $R=\{\one,a\}^\perp$,
$\lambda+R=\{s\in H:\langle a,s\rangle=\langle a,\lambda\rangle\}$.
Every real level is represented by a $\lambda\in H$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:maximum} gives a global maximum at $\lambda^*$, whose
strictly positive weights put $t^*$ strictly between the extreme knots.
Thus the maximum over the open interval $I$ equals the global maximum;
no assumption that boundary sections vanish is needed.
Multiplying \eqref{eq:maximum}, Lemma~\ref{lem:gale} and
\eqref{eq:section-density}, with $m-2=n$, gives
\[
 \PP_n(K)=\frac{n^n}{n!}D(a)f_{Y_{t^*}}(0).
\]
Division by $c_n$ proves the assertion.
\end{proof}

\section{The normal form and distinct actual facets}\label{sec:normal}
Fix $t\in I$ and select one index of each extreme value of $a$.
Set
\[
 \mu=\frac2{\max a-\min a},\quad
 \alpha=\mu(t-\min a),\quad \beta=\mu(\max a-t)=2-\alpha.
\]
Both $\alpha,\beta$ are positive. The \emph{centered coefficient vector}
$c=a-t\one$ is replaced by $\mu c$; we are not holding a second copy of
$t$ fixed in a redefined $Y_t$. Its extreme coordinates are $-\alpha$
and $\beta$, and its remaining $n$ coordinates form
$b\in[-\alpha,\beta]^n$. Pairwise distances are unchanged by centering;
positive scaling multiplies $D$ by $\mu$ and divides a density at zero
by $\mu$.

For $U=\beta E'-\alpha E''$ direct convolution yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:laplace-density}
 g(u)=\frac1{\alpha+\beta}
 \begin{cases}e^{-u/\beta},&u\ge0,\\e^{u/\alpha},&u\le0.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
For example, at $u>0$ integrate
$\beta^{-1}e^{-(u+\alpha r)/\beta}e^{-r}$ over $r\ge0$.
The expressions meet continuously at zero. Independence gives
$f_{\mu Y_t}(0)=\E g(-W_b)$, where $W_b=\sum b_jE_j$.
The two outer coordinates contribute $2$ to $D$, and each middle
coordinate contributes $|\beta-b_j|+|b_j+\alpha|=2$.
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normal}
 \frac{D(a)f_{Y_t}(0)}{n+1}
 =\Psi(\alpha,b):=
 \left(1+\frac{D(b)}{2(n+1)}\right)
 \E\exp\left(-\frac{W_b^+}{\alpha}-\frac{W_b^-}{\beta}\right).
\end{equation}
In particular the positive part is divided by $\alpha$, since the
convolution evaluates $g$ at $-W_b$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:nonzero}
For an actual $(n+2)$-facet polytope, the middle vector $b$ above is
nonzero for every $t\in I$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $b=0$, then $v=a-t\one\in\ker W$ is supported on the two chosen
extreme indices, with $v_+>0>v_-$. Thus
$v_+w_++v_-w_-=0$ implies $w_+=(-v_-/v_+)w_-$.
The areas are positive, so the outer unit normals coincide. But the
exposed face in a specified direction is unique; two distinct actual
facets cannot have the same outer unit normal. This contradiction
also covers repeated extreme knots: any unselected nonzero extreme
would already have made $b\ne0$.
\end{proof}

\section{Conditional log-concavity and the Laplace estimate}
For $b\ne0$ in $\R^n$ write
\[
 p=\Prob(W_b>0),\qquad P=\E W_b^+,\qquad N=\E W_b^-.
\]
Each nonzero signed exponential summand has a log-concave density on
its half-line, extended by zero. Zero summands are omitted. Pr\'ekopa's
marginal theorem~\cite[Theorems 6--8]{Prekopa}, applied to
$f(x-y)g(y)$, shows that their convolution is log-concave. At least one
summand has a density, so $W_b$ is absolutely continuous and
$\Prob(W_b=0)=0$. Also $\E|W_b|\le\sum|b_j|<\infty$.
When both signs occur the law has positive probability on each
half-line (take the positive summands large and negative ones small,
or reverse these choices). Thus $0<p<1$ exactly in the mixed case;
$p=1$ or $0$ in the positive-only or negative-only case.

Restriction to a half-line, reflection and positive normalization
preserve log-concavity. Hence, when defined, $X_+=(W_b\mid W_b>0)$
and $X_-=(-W_b\mid W_b<0)$ have log-concave densities on $(0,\infty)$,
no atom at zero, and finite strictly positive means. The same holds for
the unconditioned positive variable in a one-sided case.

\begin{lemma}[Classical HNBUE--Laplace comparison]\label{lem:HNBUE}
If $X\ge0$ has a log-concave probability density, no atom at zero, and
finite mean $0<\eta<\infty$, then for $s\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:HNBUE}
 \E e^{-sX}\le\frac1{1+s\eta}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $H(x)=\Prob(X>x)$ and $G(x)=\int_x^\infty H(r)\,dr$, for $x\ge0$.
The survival function is log-concave: apply Pr\'ekopa's marginal theorem
to $f(x+r)\mathbf1_{r\ge0}$. Its finite marginal is allowed to have a
convex half-line support; smoothness and strict log-concavity are not
required. We have continuous $H$, $H(0)=1$, $G(0)=\eta$, and $G'=-H$.
Concavity of $\log H$ makes $H(x+r)/H(x)$ nonincreasing in $x$
where $H(x)>0$, for fixed $r\ge0$. Therefore
\[
 \frac{G(x)}{H(x)}=\int_0^\infty\frac{H(x+r)}{H(x)}\,dr
 \le\int_0^\infty H(r)\,dr=\eta.
\]
If $H(x)=0$, then $G(x)=0$ too. It follows that
$G'\le-G/\eta$ and $G(x)\le\eta e^{-x/\eta}$, the HNBUE bound.
On $[0,R]$, integration by parts gives the exact identity
\[
 \int_0^R e^{-sx}H(x)\,dx
 =\eta-e^{-sR}G(R)-s\int_0^R e^{-sx}G(x)\,dx.
\]
The boundary term tends to zero, since $G(R)\to0$; the exponential
bound also ensures integrability. Thus the left side at infinity is
at least $\eta-s\eta/(s+1/\eta)=\eta/(1+s\eta)$.
Tonelli applied to $1-e^{-sX}=\int_0^X se^{-sx}\,dx$ now yields
\[
 \E e^{-sX}=1-s\int_0^\infty e^{-sx}H(x)\,dx
 \le1-\frac{s\eta}{1+s\eta}.
\]
At $s=0$ the assertion is equality. Finite support endpoints cause no
extra term, since both the survival function and its tail integral
then vanish.
\end{proof}

\section{The universal two-bracket reduction}
The following is the original deterministic reduction, together with
its probability comparison. Its proof does not require the box constraint.
\begin{proposition}[Proposition B]\label{prop:B}
Let $n\ge1$, $\alpha,\beta>0$, $\alpha+\beta=2$, and $b\ne0$.
Assume
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Sn}
 D(b)p^2\le(n+1)P,\qquad D(b)(1-p)^2\le(n+1)N,
\end{equation}
and that the first inequality is strict when $0<p<1$.
Then $\Psi(\alpha,b)<1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Put $d=D(b)/(2(n+1))\ge0$. If $p=1$, then $P>0$ and
$d\le P/2<P/\alpha$, since $\alpha<2$. Lemma~\ref{lem:HNBUE} gives
$\Psi\le(1+d)/(1+P/\alpha)<1$. If $p=0$, use $N>0$ and
$d\le N/2<N/\beta$ instead. These cases include $d=0$.

For $0<p<1$ set $A=P/p>0$, $B=N/(1-p)>0$.
Conditioning on the two half-lines and applying Lemma~\ref{lem:HNBUE}
gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixture}
 \E e^{-W_b^+/\alpha-W_b^-/\beta}
 \le \frac{p\alpha}{\alpha+A}+\frac{(1-p)\beta}{\beta+B}.
\end{equation}
Let $Q$ be one minus $(1+d)$ times the right side. Direct expansion gives
\begin{align}\label{eq:two-brackets}
 \frac{(\alpha+A)(\beta+B)}{\alpha\beta}Q
 &=\left[\frac P\alpha+\frac N\beta-d\right]\\
 &\quad+\frac{AB}{\alpha\beta}
 \left[1-d\left(\frac{p^2\alpha}P+
                 \frac{(1-p)^2\beta}N\right)\right].\notag
\end{align}
For completeness, before dividing by $\alpha\beta$ the numerator is
\[
 pA\beta+(1-p)B\alpha-d\alpha\beta
 +AB-d[p\alpha B+(1-p)\beta A].
\]
Substitution of $P=pA$ and $N=(1-p)B$ gives
\eqref{eq:two-brackets}, with no use of a numerical test.
The two assumptions say $2dp^2\le P$ and $2d(1-p)^2\le N$.
The second bracket is nonnegative because its subtracted terms are
at most $\alpha/2$ and $\beta/2$; it is strictly positive because the
first bound is strict and $\alpha>0$. This remains valid at $d=0$.
For the first bracket,
\[
 \sqrt{2d}\le\sqrt P+\sqrt N,\qquad
 (\sqrt P+\sqrt N)^2
 \le(\alpha+\beta)(P/\alpha+N/\beta)
 =2(P/\alpha+N/\beta).
\]
The first inequality adds the two square-root consequences of
\eqref{eq:Sn}; the second is weighted Cauchy--Schwarz.
Thus that bracket is nonnegative. All prefactors and denominators are
positive, so $Q>0$. Equation~\eqref{eq:mixture} proves the result.
\end{proof}

\section{Partial fractions and the exact four-variable estimate}\label{sec:certificate}
We prove \eqref{eq:S4} with a fully explicit finite certificate.
\begin{lemma}[Signed partial fractions]\label{lem:pf}
For distinct nonzero real $c_1,\ldots,c_r$, set
$A_j=\prod_{i\ne j}c_j/(c_j-c_i)$ and $W=\sum_jc_jE_j$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pf}
 \Prob(W>0)=\sum_{c_j>0}A_j,\qquad
 \E W^+=\sum_{c_j>0}A_jc_j.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The integrable signed density
$g(x)=\sum_j A_j|c_j|^{-1}e^{-x/c_j}\mathbf1_{xc_j>0}$ has bilateral
Laplace transform $\sum_j A_j/(1+sc_j)$ in the strip
$|\Re s|<\min_j1/|c_j|$. The same formula for a negative $c_j$ follows
by putting $x=-r$ in the integral on the negative half-line.
Multiplying the product $\prod_i(1+sc_i)^{-1}$ by $1+sc_j$ at
$s=-1/c_j$ gives exactly $A_j$. The partial fractions have the same
simple poles; their difference has none and vanishes at infinity,
so the transforms agree. Both signed measures have exponential moments
in a smaller strip. Their transforms are holomorphic there, so equality
on a real interval extends to the imaginary axis. Fourier uniqueness
for finite signed measures identifies the laws. For example, that
uniqueness follows by convolution with Gaussian approximate identities,
Fourier inversion for each convolution and weak convergence back to
the measure. Integrating the positive-side density and its first moment
gives \eqref{eq:pf}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Proposition C]\label{prop:C}
For every $b\in\R^4$, $D(b)\Prob(W_b>0)^2\le5\E W_b^+$.
The inequality is strict whenever $b$ has a positive entry, in
particular whenever it has entries of both signs.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
If $b$ has no positive entry, $p=P=0$, including $b=0$.
Otherwise, by permutation write its entries as $k$ positives
$u_1\ge\cdots\ge u_k>0$, $l$ negatives $-v_1\le\cdots\le-v_l<0$,
and $z=4-k-l$ zeros. Use ordered gaps
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gaps}
 u_i=\sum_{a=i}^k x_a,\quad v_j=\sum_{b=j}^l y_b,
 \quad x_a,y_b\ge0,\quad x_k>0,
\end{equation}
and impose $y_l>0$ only when $l>0$. If $l=0$ there are no $y$
variables or negative endpoints and all products over that list are $1$.
Set
\[
 \Den=\prod_{i=1}^k\prod_{j=1}^l(u_i+v_j)>0,\qquad
 \Vand=\prod_{a<b}(u_a-u_b).
\]
For $e=0,1$ define the alternating polynomial
\begin{equation}\label{eq:alternating}
 \widetilde N_e=\sum_{i=1}^k(-1)^{i-1}u_i^{k-1+l+e}
 \Vand(u_1,\ldots,\widehat u_i,\ldots,u_k)
 \prod_{a\ne i}\prod_{j=1}^l(u_a+v_j).
\end{equation}
The hat means omission. Each alternating polynomial vanishes when
$u_a=u_b$, hence is divisible by $u_a-u_b$. These distinct linear
factors are relatively prime in $\mathbb Q[u,v]$, so their product
$\Vand$ divides it. Let $N_p=\widetilde N_0/\Vand$ and
$N_P=\widetilde N_1/\Vand$, as polynomials, and then substitute
\eqref{eq:gaps}.
On the dense locus of all positive gaps, \eqref{eq:pf} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quotients}
 p=N_p/\Den,\qquad P=N_P/\Den.
\end{equation}
Indeed zero coordinates are omitted from the pole list, and
\[
 \Vand=(-1)^{i-1}\prod_{a\ne i}(u_i-u_a)
 \Vand(u_1,\ldots,\widehat u_i,\ldots,u_k),
\]
which fixes both the exponent $k-1+l$ and its alternating sign.

These identities extend to all the closed gap strata with positive
surviving endpoints. To see this without a genericity assumption,
couple $W_{b^{(r)}}$ and $W_b$ using the same exponentials when
$b^{(r)}\to b\ne0$. They converge almost surely; the no-atom property
at zero gives convergence of the sign indicators almost surely and
hence of $p$. In a bounded coefficient neighborhood,
$|W_{b^{(r)}}|\le C\sum E_i$, so dominated convergence gives continuity
of $P$. The polynomial quotients in \eqref{eq:quotients} are continuous
because $\Den>0$, even for repeated same-sign knots. This proves
\eqref{eq:quotients} on the whole stratum.

If $s_1\le\cdots\le s_4$ are the sorted entries of $b$, then
$D(b)=\sum_{i=1}^4(2i-5)s_i$: $s_i$ occurs positively $i-1$ times
and negatively $4-i$ times in the sum of differences. Thus $D$ is
linear in the gaps, with zeros still counted in the ranks. We have the
exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:T}
 5P-D(b)p^2=\frac{T_{klz}}{\Den^2},\qquad
 T_{klz}=5N_P\Den-DN_p^2.
\end{equation}
Appendix~\ref{app:witness} lists every nonzero coefficient of each of
the ten possible $T_{klz}$ and derives a division-free recurrence that
also reconstructs them. All listed coefficients are positive and
all omitted coefficients are zero. Each polynomial contains
\begin{equation}\label{eq:endpoint}
 k(k+1)x_k^{2kl+1}
\end{equation}
with positive coefficient. Since $x_k>0$, this term survives every
zero interior gap, regardless of the negative gaps. Thus $T_{klz}>0$
on its entire stratum. This proves the proposition.

If an endpoint tends to zero, reclassify the limiting vector by its
actual positive, negative and zero entries; its own listed certificate
applies. Strictness is supplied by its own endpoint monomial, not
inferred from continuity. No continuity at $b=0$ is asserted or needed.
\end{proof}

\section{Minimum facets, affine invariance and equality}
A full-dimensional $n$-polytope has at least $n+1$ facets. Indeed, after
putting zero in its interior, write its irredundant inequalities as
$\langle q_i,x\rangle\le1$. Its polar is $\conv(q_i)$: the inclusion
in the polar is immediate, and separation gives the reverse using
boundedness and the interior point. Irredundancy makes each $q_i$ a
vertex (a nonvertex inequality would follow from the others). The polar
is full-dimensional and needs at least $n+1$ vertices. With exactly
$n+1$ vertices they are affinely independent, so it is a simplex.
Its polar is also a simplex: intersecting every choice of $n$ of its
facet hyperplanes gives the $n+1$ dual vertices, as follows directly
from affine barycentric coordinates and the strictly positive
barycentric coordinates of zero. Thus $K$ with $n+1$ facets is a simplex.

For an invertible linear map $A$, the transformed facet normal is
$A^{-T}u_i/|A^{-T}u_i|$ and its area is
$|\det A|\,|A^{-T}u_i|f_i$. For example, map a unit-height prism over
the facet: its volume scales by $|\det A|$, while its new height is
$1/|A^{-T}u_i|$. Hence area vectors transform by $|\det A|A^{-T}$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:zonotope} proves
\[
 \Pi(AK)=|\det A|A^{-T}\Pi K.
\]
The absolute determinant of this map is $|\det A|^{n-1}$, which is
also the factor in $V_n(AK)^{n-1}$. Translations preserve projection
volumes. Thus $\PP_n$ is affine invariant, including orientation
reversals, agreeing with Source P, \texttt{lem:affine}.

The standard simplex $T_n=\conv(0,e_1,\ldots,e_n)$ has volume $1/n!$.
Its area vectors are $-e_i/(n-1)!$ and
$(e_1+\cdots+e_n)/(n-1)!$. For the last facet this follows by projecting
onto a coordinate hyperplane, with projection Jacobian $1/\sqrt n$.
Every one of its $n+1$ full-size minors has absolute determinant
$1/((n-1)!)^n$. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:simplex}
 V_n(\Pi T_n)=\frac{n+1}{((n-1)!)^n},\qquad
 \PP_n(T_n)=\frac{(n+1)n^n}{n!}=c_n.
\end{equation}
Affine invariance proves this for every simplex; this is the interface
of Source P, \texttt{prop:simplex}, with its constants shown explicitly.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
The only facet counts are five and six. With five, $K$ is a simplex,
and \eqref{eq:simplex} gives $c_4=5\cdot4^4/4!=160/3$.
With six, Proposition~\ref{prop:A} evaluates $\PP_4(K)/c_4$ at an
attained $t^*\in I$. Equation~\eqref{eq:normal} gives
$\Psi(\alpha,b)$ there, with $\alpha,\beta>0$ and $\alpha+\beta=2$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:nonzero} gives $b\ne0$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:C} applies to $b$ and $-b$, and is strict in the
mixed case. These are precisely the hypotheses of
Proposition~\ref{prop:B}, so $\PP_4(K)/c_4<1$.
Attainment is essential: pointwise strictness would not by itself
exclude a supremum equal to one. The exhibited maximum supplies it.
No simple-polytope or positive-interior-gap assumption has been used.
\end{proof}

\section{Limits of the auxiliary method}\label{sec:limits}
A negative coefficient in $T$ rejects that nonnegative-coefficient
certificate; it does not refute the underlying inequality.
There is a separate exact reason the auxiliary inequality
$D(b)p^2\le(n+1)P$ fails for $n\ge5$.
Put $r=n-1$, $b=(-1,\varepsilon,\ldots,\varepsilon)$, with
$\varepsilon>0$, and write $W=\varepsilon A-Z$, where
$A\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(r,1)$ and $Z\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1)$
are independent. Conditional on $A$, direct integration gives
\[
 \Prob(Z<\varepsilon A)=1-e^{-\varepsilon A},\qquad
 \E[(\varepsilon A-Z)^+\mid A]=\varepsilon A-1+e^{-\varepsilon A}.
\]
Since $\E e^{-\varepsilon A}=(1+\varepsilon)^{-r}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:corner}
 p=1-(1+\varepsilon)^{-r},\quad
 P=r\varepsilon-1+(1+\varepsilon)^{-r},\quad
 D=r(1+\varepsilon).
\end{equation}
Taylor expansion at zero gives
$p=r\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^2)$,
$P=r(r+1)\varepsilon^2/2+O(\varepsilon^3)$, and hence
\[
 \lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}\frac{Dp^2}{(n+1)P}
 =\frac{2(n-1)^2}{n(n+1)}>1\quad(n\ge5).
\]
For the last inequality, the numerator minus denominator is
$n^2-5n+2$, positive at $5$ and increasing thereafter.
Thus small positive $\varepsilon$ supplies actual auxiliary failures.
This concerns the separated sufficient condition only. It has no
implication that the geometric inequality fails in dimensions $5$--$8$.
The unseparated normal form with its geometric box constraint remains
unproved in those dimensions in this work. We make no claim of an
optimal auxiliary constant $5$, no positive all-$n$ threshold theorem,
and no result for four-polytopes with at least seven facets.

$q_{ij}=\Prob(U_i>V_j)$ and $R_{ij}=\E(U_i-V_j)^+$.

$v_j/(u_i+v_j)$; the first $V$ phase wins with probability
$u_i/(u_i+v_j)$. These follow by integrating the two exponential
densities. Conditional on either outcome, memorylessness leaves the
unfinished phase independent with its original exponential law.

difference. Thus both $q$ and $R$ obey the same two-branch recurrence.

\begin{align}\label{eq:race}
 N_{ij}={}&v_j\prod_{b=j+1}^{l-1}(u_i+v_b)N_{i+1,j}\\
 &+u_i\prod_{a=i+1}^{k-1}(u_a+v_j)N_{i,j+1}.\notag
\end{align}
The boundary numerators equal the boundary values, with denominator one.
This terminates as $i+j$ increases, uses only integer polynomial
