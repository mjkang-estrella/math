\begin{abstract}
We prove, relative to two explicitly stated results of the pinned OpenAI
manuscript on Seymour's conjecture, that every nonempty finite sink-free
oriented graph has two distinct vertices whose exact second out-neighborhood
is at least as large as their first. This is Conjecture~2 of
Dara, Francis, Jacob and Narayanan. The inputs are the ordinary one-vertex
Seymour theorem and a finite-relation pruning lemma; their truth is stipulated
here. A minimum counterexample has a unique good vertex with one successor.
Deleting that vertex leaves weak subset deficits together with a strict
inequality for subsets that do not reach the successor in one or two steps.
In a transitive blowup, compatibility with the diagonal selects many fibers
with this strict inequality. Their total gain dominates the possible losses
of all remaining fibers, allowing the established pruning objective to close.
All deductions from the two inputs are proved below. Finite diagnostics and
formal supporting lemmas are distinguished from the full graph theorem.
\end{abstract}

\section{Statement, notation and dependencies}
An \emph{oriented graph} is a finite directed graph without loops, parallel
arcs or opposite pairs of arcs. For a vertex $v$ of $D$, let $N_{1,D}(v)$ be
its set of out-neighbors and let $N_{2,D}(v)$ consist of the vertices at
\emph{directed distance exactly two} from $v$. In particular, a first neighbor
is not counted again in $N_2$. A vertex is \emph{good} if
$\abs{N_{2,D}(v)}\geq\abs{N_{1,D}(v)}$. A sink has no outgoing arc.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Assume Premises~\ref{prem:snc} and~\ref{prem:pruning}. Every nonempty finite
oriented graph with no sink has at least two distinct good vertices.
\end{theorem}
There is no strong-connectivity, degree, missing-edge or size restriction in
this conclusion. Sink-freeness is essential: a transitive tournament has only
one good vertex, its sink. The statement is the already formulated
Conjecture~2 of Dara et al.\ \cite{Dara}, not a new conjecture.

For any such graph $K$ and vertex subset $S$, define the outgoing image and
its iterates by
\[
 F_K(S)=\bigcup_{v\in S}N_{1,K}(v),\qquad F_K^2(S)=F_K(F_K(S)).
\]
We do not remove $S$ from either image. Put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:deficit}
 \delta_K(S)=\abs{F_K^2(S)\dif F_K(S)}-\abs{F_K(S)\dif S}.
\end{equation}
For a singleton this is $\abs{N_{2,K}(v)}-\abs{N_{1,K}(v)}$:
a length-two return to $v$ would require opposite arcs, and loops are absent.
For larger sets, the image notation in~\eqref{eq:deficit} is the definition;
no distance interpretation for a set is needed.

Our only imported mathematical inputs are the following two statements from
OpenAI~\cite{OpenAI}, at the immutable revision recorded in Appendix~\ref{app:source}.
They are stipulated true for this deduction; their proofs are not part of
this paper's claim of independent verification.

\begin{premise}[Ordinary Seymour theorem]\label{prem:snc}
Every nonempty finite oriented graph has at least one good vertex.
\end{premise}
This is \texttt{thm:main} in the source's \texttt{00-introduction.tex}.
We next give the full pruning input in notation convenient for its later use.

\begin{premise}[Finite-relation pruning]\label{prem:pruning}
Let $X$ be any finite set equipped with a binary relation $\to$; loops or
opposite pairs are allowed for this premise. Let $R,C\subseteq X\times X$
be two separately counted families. A left member $(p,j)\in R$ and a right
member $(i,s)\in C$ conflict precisely when $p\to i$ and $s\to j$.
Let
\begin{align*}
 Z&=\set{(i,j):\exists(p,j)\in R,\ (i,s)\in C,\ p\to i,\ s\to j},\\
 B&=\set{(p,s):\exists(p,j)\in R,\ (i,s)\in C,\ p\to i,\ s\to j}.
\end{align*}
A left member $(p,j)$ covers $(i,j)$ if $p\to i$, and a right member
$(i,s)$ covers $(i,j)$ if $s\to j$; these are the only covering incidences.
There exist subfamilies $R'\subseteq R$ and $C'\subseteq C$ with no
remaining conflict, retaining every member that had no conflict initially,
and satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pruning}
 \abs{R\dif R'}+\abs{C\dif C'}
 \leq \abs B+\abs{\set{u\in Z:u\text{ is not covered by }R'\text{ or }C'}}.
\end{equation}
A member appearing on both sides is counted separately on each side.
\end{premise}
This is \texttt{lem:pruning} in \texttt{02-pruning.tex}; its initial charge
set is called $H$ there and $B$ here to avoid confusion with a graph below.
The premise applies to every finite binary relation, with no additional
orientation, connectivity or nonemptiness hypothesis.

\section{A minimum counterexample and protected subsets}
Suppose the conclusion of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is false. Choose a
sink-free counterexample $D$ minimizing its number of vertices and, subject
to that, its number of arcs. Premise~\ref{prem:snc} implies that $D$ has
exactly one good vertex, denoted by $z$.

The graph $D$ is strongly connected. Otherwise, a terminal strongly connected
component $C$ is proper. It cannot be a singleton, because $D$ is sink-free;
its induced graph is sink-free. Both out-neighborhoods of each vertex in
$C$ stay inside $C$, so minimum order gives two good vertices of $D$,
a contradiction.

Every vertex $x\ne z$ has outdegree at least two. Indeed, outdegree zero is
excluded. If $N_{1,D}(x)=\set{y}$, then
$N_{2,D}(x)=N_{1,D}(y)$: loops and opposite arcs exclude both $x$ and $y$
from the set on the right. It is nonempty, so $x$ would be good.
On the other hand, $z$ has outdegree exactly one. If it had at least two
outgoing arcs, deleting one would leave a sink-free graph with fewer arcs.
At any other vertex the first neighborhood would be unchanged and the
second could not enlarge. All vertices other than $z$ would remain bad,
contradicting arc minimality. Write $z\to w$ for its unique outgoing arc.

\begin{lemma}[Protected strict deficit]\label{lem:protected}
For every nonempty $S\subseteq V(D)\dif\set{z}$, one has $\delta_D(S)<0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Fix $S$, and write $I=F_D(S)\cap S$ and $E=F_D(S)\dif S$.
Strong connectivity and the fact that $S$ is proper imply $E\ne\varnothing$.
For $T\subseteq E$ put $g(T)=\abs{F_D(T)\dif(I\cup T)}$.

For each proper $T\subsetneq E$, delete all arcs from $S$ to $E\dif T$,
and call the resulting graph $D_T$. At least one arc is removed. If $D_T$
has a sink, this sink lies in $S$ and is newly good. If $D_T$ is sink-free,
minimality gives at least two good vertices in it. Outside $S$, first
neighborhoods are unchanged and second neighborhoods cannot improve, so
only $z$ can be good there. In either case there is a newly good vertex
$v\in S$. Set
\[
 Q=N_{1,D}(v)\cap(E\dif T).
\]
This is precisely the set of first neighbors lost by $v$. It is nonempty,
since otherwise $v$ could not improve. Put $d_i=\abs{N_{i,D}(v)}$.
Since $v\ne z$, $d_2\leq d_1-1$.

The surviving first neighbors of $v$ lie in $I\cup T$. A two-step path
through $I\subseteq S$ ends in $I\cup T$, and one through $T$ ends in
$F_D(T)$. Thus all surviving two-step endpoints lie in
$I\cup T\cup F_D(T)$. A newly counted exact second neighbor must be a
lost first neighbor, and therefore lies in $Q\cap F_D(T)$. Conversely, every
member of
\[
 L=F_D(Q)\dif\bigl(I\cup T\cup Q\cup F_D(T)\bigr)
\]
was formerly an exact second neighbor and is no longer reachable in two
steps. Such a member cannot equal $v$, since that would give opposite arcs
between $v$ and a member of $Q$. Goodness in $D_T$ consequently gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss}
 d_1-\abs Q\leq\abs{N_{2,D_T}(v)}
 \leq d_2-\abs L+\abs{Q\cap F_D(T)}.
\end{equation}
On adjoining $Q$ to $T$, exactly $Q\cap F_D(T)$ is removed from the set
counted by $g(T)$, and exactly $L$ is added. Hence
\[
 g(T\cup Q)-g(T)=\abs L-\abs{Q\cap F_D(T)}\leq\abs Q-1.
\]
Starting at $T=\varnothing$, repeat this construction, always forming $D_T$
afresh from $D$. Each nonempty $Q$ is disjoint from the current $T$, so
finitely many steps reach $T=E$. There is at least one step, and $g(\varnothing)=0$;
thus $g(E)\leq\abs E-1$. Finally $I\subseteq S$ implies
$F_D(I)\subseteq F_D(S)$, whence
\[
 F_D^2(S)\dif F_D(S)=F_D(E)\dif(I\cup E).
\]
Its cardinality is $g(E)$, proving the lemma.
\end{proof}
This argument is an adaptation of the source's \texttt{lem:subset-deficit},
with the unique exceptional vertex protected. It does not assert a strict
deficit for sets containing $z$.

\section{Retaining the exceptional boundary}
Let $H=D-z$, and write $F=F_H$. Every remaining vertex had at least two
outgoing arcs in $D$ and loses at most one, so $H$ is sink-free.
For a nonempty $S\subseteq V(H)$ write $A=F(S)$, $A_2=F^2(S)$ and set
\[
 a=\one_{\{z\in F_D(S)\}},\quad
 q=\one_{\{a=1,\ w\notin A\cup A_2\}},\quad
 b=\one_{\{a=0,\ z\in F_D(A)\}}.
\]
The first image in $D$ is $A$ with $z$ adjoined exactly when $a=1$.
The $H$-part of the second image is $A_2$ with $w$ adjoined exactly when
$a=1$. Its possible $z$-part is removed by the first image when $a=1$.
These observations give the exact identities
\begin{align}
 \abs{F_D^2(S)\dif F_D(S)}&=\abs{A_2\dif A}+q+b,\label{eq:boundary1}\\
 \abs{F_D(S)\dif S}&=\abs{A\dif S}+a.\label{eq:boundary2}
\end{align}
Lemma~\ref{lem:protected}, including integrality of cardinalities, now yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weak}
 \delta_H(S)\leq-1+a-q-b\leq0.
\end{equation}
There is extra information beyond this weak bound:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:anchorbase}
 w\notin F(S)\cup F^2(S)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\delta_H(S)\leq-1.
\end{equation}
Indeed, if $a=1$, then $q=1$; if $a=0$, the first bound in~\eqref{eq:weak}
is already at most $-1$.

The graph $H$ is strongly connected as well. Choose a terminal strongly
connected component $C$ in it. Sink-freeness makes $C$ nontrivial, and every
vertex in it has an internal predecessor. Hence $F(C)=C=F^2(C)$.
Strong connectivity of $D$ forces an arc from $C$ to $z$, since there is
no other possible outgoing destination outside $C$. Thus $a=1$ for $S=C$.
As $\delta_H(C)=0$,~\eqref{eq:weak} forces $q=0$, so $w\in C$.
Then $C\cup\set z$ is outgoing-closed in $D$, which by strong connectivity
implies $C\cup\set z=V(D)$. Consequently $C=V(H)$.
In particular, every vertex of $H$ has positive indegree. Write $n=\abs{V(H)}$.

\section{A transitive blowup and its fiber estimates}
Set $m=n^2+2$. Replace each vertex $p$ of $H$ by a transitive tournament
on labels $1,\ldots,m$, with arcs from smaller to larger labels.
For every base arc $p\to q$, put all arcs from the $p$-block to the $q$-block.
Denote this oriented graph by $G$, its vertex set by $X$, and its outgoing
image operator by $J$. Thus $\abs X=nm$, and all vertices of $G$ have
positive indegree. For $U\subseteq X$ define
\[
 c(U)=\abs U+\abs{J^2(U)}-2\abs{J(U)}.
\]

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:blowup}
Let $U$ be a nonempty proper subset of $X$, and let
$S=\set{p\in V(H):U\text{ meets the }p\text{-block}}$.
If $S\ne V(H)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:blowup}
 c(U)\leq m\delta_H(S)+\abs{S\dif F(S)}\leq m\delta_H(S)+n.
\end{equation}
If $S=V(H)$, then $c(U)=\abs U-\abs X<0$.
In all cases $c(U)\leq n$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $T_p$ be the labels selected by $U$ in block $p$, and let $f$ be the
internal image operator of the transitive tournament. If a nonempty label
set $T$ has least element $t$, then $f(T)=\set{t+1,\ldots,m}$.
For $t<m$, $f^2(T)=\set{t+2,\ldots,m}$ and
\[
 \abs T-2\abs{f(T)}+\abs{f^2(T)}=\abs T-(m-t)-1\leq0.
\]
For $t=m$, the expression is $1$. In particular it is always at most $1$.

Put $W=F(S)\cup F^2(S)$. The first image fills every $F(S)$-block and
has label set $f(T_p)$ in a block $p\in S\dif F(S)$.
A two-step path with two external arcs ends in $F^2(S)$, one with exactly
one external arc ends in $F(S)$, and a path wholly internal contributes
$f^2(T_p)$. Allowing complete $W$-blocks therefore gives an upper bound:
\begin{align*}
 c(U)\leq{}&m\bigl(\abs W-2\abs{F(S)}\bigr)+\sum_{p\in S}\abs{T_p}\\
 &-2\sum_{p\in S\dif F(S)}\abs{f(T_p)}
   +\sum_{p\in S\dif W}\abs{f^2(T_p)}.
\end{align*}
Extend the last sum to $S\dif F(S)$; this only increases it. Bound
$\abs{T_p}\leq m$ on $S\cap F(S)$ and apply the internal inequality to
all remaining blocks. The result is
\[
 c(U)\leq m\bigl(\abs{F^2(S)\dif F(S)}-\abs{F(S)\dif S}\bigr)
              +\abs{S\dif F(S)},
\]
which proves~\eqref{eq:blowup}. If $S=V(H)$, positive indegrees in $H$
give $F(S)=V(H)$, hence $J(U)=X$ and $J^2(U)=X$. This proves the
full-support assertion. Finally use~\eqref{eq:weak} for the bound $c(U)\leq n$.
\end{proof}

\section{Strictness after summing compatible fibers}
For a family $P\subseteq X\times X$, define its two coordinate images by
\begin{align*}
 J_1(P)&=\set{(i,j):\exists(p,j)\in P,\ p\to i},\\
 J_2(P)&=\set{(i,j):\exists(i,s)\in P,\ s\to j}.
\end{align*}
Call $P,Q$ \emph{compatible} when $J_1(P)\cap J_2(Q)=\varnothing$.
The diagonal $\Delta=\set{(x,x):x\in X}$ is compatible with itself because
$G$ has no opposite arcs. In this section let $P,Q$ be compatible and
contain $\Delta$.

For $j,i\in X$, respectively write
$P_j=\set{p:(p,j)\in P}$ and $Q_i=\set{s:(i,s)\in Q}$.
Each column $P_j$ and row $Q_i$ is nonempty and proper. The diagonal gives
nonemptiness. If $P_j=X$, positive indegrees imply
$X\times\set j\subseteq J_1(P)$. An in-neighbor $t\to j$ gives
$(t,j)\in J_2(\Delta)$, a conflict. If $Q_i=X$, then
$\set i\times X\subseteq J_2(Q)$, and an in-neighbor $t\to i$ gives
$(i,t)\in J_1(\Delta)$, again a conflict.

Fix $j=(w,a)$ with $2\leq a\leq m$. No member $x\in P_j$ can have a
two-step walk $x\to t\to j$: the left member $(x,j)$ and right diagonal
member $(t,t)$ would conflict. Consequently the support $S$ of $P_j$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:support}
 w\notin F(S)\cup F^2(S).
\end{equation}
For the first image, a base arc $p\to w$ from a supported block lifts from
any chosen $p$-vertex through $(w,a-1)$ to $(w,a)$. For the second image,
a base walk $p\to q\to w$ lifts through any vertex in the $q$-block.
Either would contradict the exclusion just proved. The support is nonempty,
and it is proper since it omits every base in-neighbor of $w$, of which
there is at least one. Equations~\eqref{eq:anchorbase} and~\eqref{eq:blowup}
therefore give
\[
 c(P_{(w,a)})\leq-m+n\qquad(2\leq a\leq m).
\]
For the right row anchored at $i=(w,a)$, a walk $s\to t\to i$ would make
the right member $(i,s)$ conflict with the left diagonal member $(t,t)$:
both coordinate images contain $(i,t)$. The same lifts then prove
\eqref{eq:support} for the row support and give $c(Q_{(w,a)})\leq-m+n$.

There are $nm$ fibers on each side. Lemma~\ref{lem:blowup} bounds each by
$n$, while the $m-1$ specified anchors improve this bound by $m$. Thus
\begin{align}
 \sum_{j\in X}c(P_j)&\leq n^2m-m(m-1)=-m<0,\label{eq:sumleft}\\
 \sum_{i\in X}c(Q_i)&\leq n^2m-m(m-1)=-m<0.\label{eq:sumright}
\end{align}
Coordinate images act separately on the relevant fibers, so these imply
\begin{equation}\label{eq:strictfamilies}
 \abs P+\abs{J_1^2(P)}<2\abs{J_1(P)},\qquad
 \abs Q+\abs{J_2^2(Q)}<2\abs{J_2(Q)}.
\end{equation}
This is a total-family conclusion. It requires no assertion that every
individual nonempty proper subset of $G$ has negative $c$.

\section{The extremal pruning contradiction}
There are finitely many compatible pairs containing $\Delta$, and at least
one exists. Choose such a pair $P,Q$ maximizing $M+d$, where
\[
 M=\abs P+\abs Q,\qquad
 d=\abs X^2-\abs{J_1(P)}-\abs{J_2(Q)}.
\]
Here and below $\abs X^2$ means $(\abs X)^2=\abs{X\times X}$.
Compatibility makes $d$ exactly the number of points uncovered by the two
coordinate images. Form the complementary families
\[
 R=(X\times X)\dif J_2^2(Q),\qquad
 C=(X\times X)\dif J_1^2(P).
\]
Adding~\eqref{eq:strictfamilies} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gain}
 \abs R+\abs C>M+2d.
\end{equation}
Both complements contain $\Delta$. For example, if
$(v,v)\in J_2^2(Q)$, then some $(v,s)\in J_2(Q)$ has $s\to v$.
But $(s,s)\in P$ then puts $(v,s)$ in $J_1(P)$, a conflict.
For the other complement, if $(v,v)\in J_1^2(P)$, then some
$(s,v)\in J_1(P)$ has $s\to v$, while $(s,s)\in Q$ puts $(s,v)$
in $J_2(Q)$.

Moreover, every diagonal member in these new families is conflict-free.
A left diagonal member $(v,v)\in R$ conflicting with $(i,s)\in C$ would
give $s\to v\to i$. Since $(s,s)\in P$, this puts $(i,s)\in J_1^2(P)$,
contrary to its membership in $C$. A right diagonal member $(v,v)\in C$
conflicting with $(p,j)\in R$ gives $p\to v\to j$; from $(p,p)\in Q$
we obtain $(p,j)\in J_2^2(Q)$, again a contradiction.

Apply Premise~\ref{prem:pruning} to $R,C$. For any conflict
$(p,j)\in R$, $(i,s)\in C$, where $p\to i$ and $s\to j$, its initial
charge $(p,s)$ lies outside $J_1(P)\cup J_2(Q)$. Indeed, membership in
$J_1(P)$ would give $(u,s)\in P$ with $u\to p$, hence
$(i,s)\in J_1^2(P)$. Membership in $J_2(Q)$ would give $(p,t)\in Q$
with $t\to s$, hence $(p,j)\in J_2^2(Q)$. Both are impossible.
Thus the initial charge set $B$ in the premise has size at most $d$.

The premise gives compatible retained families $P'\subseteq R$ and
$Q'\subseteq C$, retaining the entire diagonal. Put
\[
 d'=\abs X^2-\abs{J_1(P')}-\abs{J_2(Q')}.
\]
Every point of the premise's set $Z$ left uncovered by the retained members
is outside $J_1(P')\cup J_2(Q')$. Their number is therefore at most $d'$.
Consequently at most $d+d'$ members were deleted, with the two sides
counted separately. Combining this with~\eqref{eq:gain} gives
\[
 \abs{P'}+\abs{Q'}+d'
 \geq\abs R+\abs C-(d+d')+d'>M+d.
\]
The retained pair is admissible for the same maximum, a contradiction.
This rules out the assumed counterexample and proves
Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.\hfill$\square$

\section{Attribution and the scope of the contribution}
Dara et al.\ \cite{Dara} state exactly our conclusion as Conjecture~2 and
its unique-good/sink version as Conjecture~3. Their Lemma~18 describes the
singleton boundary after deleting a vertex: a good vertex that becomes bad
on reinsertion must point to the deleted vertex and see its successors in
its first two neighborhoods. Their Theorem~6 proves a deletion-survival
statement when the deleted graph is a tournament missing a matching, using
safe completions, modules and median-order arguments; Corollary~5 supplies
two good vertices in that restricted class. Their Proposition~8 uses
minimality and arc deletion in comparing the conjectures. These are
antecedents, not new constructions claimed here.

Seacrest's subset-deletion method~\cite[Lemma~4]{Seacrest} is an antecedent
of the protected-subset argument. Brantner et al.\ \cite[Theorems~4.1--4.3]{Brantner}
give minimum-counterexample properties and lexicographic amplification.
OpenAI~\cite{OpenAI} develops the strict subset-deficit reduction,
transitive blowup estimate, compatible-pair objective, complement step and
pruning closure used here. The source's route begins with strict deficits
for every relevant subset of an all-bad counterexample and makes each
individual blown-up subset strictly concave.

The added operation here is the conjunction of
\eqref{eq:boundary1}--\eqref{eq:anchorbase} with
\eqref{eq:support}--\eqref{eq:sumright}. A deleted exceptional vertex leaves
only weak deficits globally, but also a distinguished successor. Compatibility
selects $m-1$ anchor fibers in its block; their combined gain $m(m-1)$
dominates the residual $n^2m$. Strictness is obtained after summing the
constrained fibers. Neither standard minimality nor the final imported
pruning operation receives separate novelty credit.

For additional comparison, Guo, Kang and Zwaneveld~\cite{Tight} study
pointwise tightness under products and module replacements; these are
product precedents rather than the exceptional-anchor selection above.
Tahara~\cite{Tahara} studies excess margins and restricted constructions.
An excess bound and a bound on the number of good vertices are different:
one good vertex can contribute several units of excess. The literature
comparison covers the named primary bodies and is bounded. It is not a
certification of worldwide priority. The theorem is presented relative to
its stipulated inputs, not as a wholly independent proof of ordinary SNC.

\section{Validation and formalization boundary}\label{sec:validation}
The universal proof is Sections~2--6. Computation is not used to infer the
theorem. Two independently written finite diagnostics accompany it. The
author's bitmask checker verifies 352,224 deletion-boundary identities across
23,664 small orientations and verifies a six-vertex counterexample to a
discarded shortcut. An independent reviewer's set-based checker verifies
15,129 deletion endpoint and increment cases, 198,936 blowup subset cases,
and 151,362 anchor exclusion cases. The latter enumerates orientations on
three and four base vertices, with internal block sizes three and two.
These finite tests detect local errors of signs, endpoints or directions;
they do not verify the unrestricted assertion by enumeration.

For clarity, the discarded shortcut asserted that strong connectivity plus
$\delta_H(S)\leq0$ for all nonempty $S$ forces zero singleton deficits.
It is false: with vertices $0,\ldots,5$ and outgoing bitmasks
$(36,25,10,17,37,10)$, all 63 nonempty subset deficits are nonpositive but
the singleton deficits are $(0,-1,0,0,-1,0)$. The proof above uses the
stronger exceptional-successor information~\eqref{eq:anchorbase}, not this
shortcut. The counterexample is not an S2 counterexample.
