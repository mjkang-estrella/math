     EXACT DISCRETE LOGARITHMS FROM A FLAGGED POLYGONAL
                         TRANSFORM

                                             MYEONGJIN KANG


       Abstract. Assume the ﬂagged polygonal-transform and exact-factoring interfaces speciﬁed below.
       We construct a polynomial-time uniform quantum circuit family over one ﬁxed ﬁnite gate set that
       computes discrete logarithms in prime ﬁelds with probability one and polynomial worst-case re-
       sources. For prime-order inputs, each veriﬁed Fourier-bin label has probability at least 1/(81q).
       Given its veriﬁed logarithm, that probability is computable exactly by a bounded number of lattice-
       point counts in dimension at most thirteen. Dyadic completion then produces a preparation with
       success probability exactly one quarter, followed by one exact ampliﬁcation step. All sampled
       probabilities are dyadic; no input-dependent rotation or postselection is used.




                            1. Statement and computational inputs
   Exact quantum algorithms for discrete logarithms predate this work. Mosca and Zalka [4] obtain
exactness through an exact Fourier transform, with computed rotations. Our question concerns
a prescribed ﬁnite gate set, uniform polynomial worst-case resources, and probability-one output.
The construction below uses the polygonal transform and probability-completion methods of [5].
The additional step is an exact evaluation of a two-register selected-bin probability, together with a
uniform lower bound on it. The gate model is ideal and noiseless. Cai and Young [3] study noise in
the controlled rotations of Shor-type discrete-logarithm circuits. Their result is not a lower bound
for every circuit family; we make no claim of robustness to gate noise.
   Fix the gate set G consisting of NOT, CNOT, Toﬀoli, Hadamard H, S = diag(1, i), their inverses,
and their singly controlled versions. This is one ﬁnite set of bounded-arity gates. Let g0 be the
continuous, period-one function given by
                    g0 ((s + t)/4) = (−i)s {1 + (−i − 1)t},           s ∈ Z,    0 ≤ t ≤ 1.                   (1.1)
We isolate the two upstream interfaces used in the argument.
Assumption 1.1. The following interfaces hold over G.
   (1) For n ≥ 128 and Q = 216n , a polynomial-time uniform circuit of size polynomial in n has a
       designated ancillary success outcome whose unnormalized data-register operator is
                             AQ (k, x) = Q−1/2 g0 (kx/Q),          0 ≤ k, x < Q.                             (1.2)
       On the success outcome every ancillary register of this circuit is in a ﬁxed state independent
       of x.
   (2) Integers admit probability-one factorization by polynomial-time uniform G circuits with
       polynomial worst-case gate and qubit counts.
   The ﬁrst item is [5, Lemma 3.2] with its stated choice Q = 216n . Uniform generation and the
ﬁxed ancillary success state follow from the explicit circuit in its proof: Hadamard preparation,
O(n2 ) controlled constant shifts with scratch uncomputed, and an all-zero success test. The second
item is [5, Theorem 1.1]. The prime-order proposition below uses only the ﬁrst item. The full
prime-ﬁeld theorem also uses the second. We do not treat the unnormalized operator in (1.2) as a
unitary or as an outcome that may be enforced.

Theorem 1.2. Under Assumption 1.1, there is a polynomial-time uniform family of circuits over
G with the following property. For every prime p and nonzero g, h (mod p) with h ∈ ⟨g⟩, the output
is the unique a ∈ [0, ordp (g)) satisfying g a = h, with probability one. Gate count, qubit count, and
worst-case running time are polynomial in log p.
  We also use the classical ﬁxed-dimensional lattice-point counting theorem [1, Theorem 4.4 and
Algorithm 5.2]: for each ﬁxed dimension, the number of integer points in a bounded rational
polytope is computable in deterministic polynomial time in its binary encoding. Equalities can
be written as pairs of inequalities. The weighted variant needed here is reduced explicitly to this
unweighted statement.

The binomial coeﬃcient counts integer tuples 0 ≤ t1 < · · · < ts < v, each described by linear

calculation does not enumerate the q − 1 labels.

dresses in the polynomial workspace. Higher controls are formed by reversible conjunctions. These
operations cost polynomial overhead. The ﬁxed 1024 repetitions, histories, inverse, and reﬂections
preserve that bound. This proves Proposition 2.1.


                             6. Arbitrary order in a prime field
   If g = 1, return zero. Otherwise use Assumption 1.1(2) to factor p − 1. Starting from m = p − 1,
