\begin{abstract}
Assume the flagged polygonal-transform and exact-factoring interfaces
specified below. We construct a polynomial-time uniform quantum circuit
family over one fixed finite gate set that computes discrete logarithms
in prime fields with probability one and polynomial worst-case resources.
For prime-order inputs, each verified Fourier-bin label has probability
at least $1/(81q)$. Given its verified logarithm, that probability is
computable exactly by a bounded number of lattice-point counts in
dimension at most thirteen. Dyadic completion then produces a preparation
with success probability exactly one quarter, followed by one exact
amplification step. All sampled probabilities are dyadic; no
input-dependent rotation or postselection is used.
\end{abstract}
\maketitle

\section{Statement and computational inputs}
Exact quantum algorithms for discrete logarithms predate this work.
Mosca and Zalka \cite{MZ} obtain exactness through an exact Fourier
transform, with computed rotations. Our question concerns a prescribed
finite gate set, uniform polynomial worst-case resources, and
probability-one output. The construction below uses the polygonal
transform and probability-completion methods of \cite{OpenAI}.
The additional step is an exact evaluation of a two-register
selected-bin probability, together with a uniform lower bound on it.
The gate model is ideal and noiseless. Cai and Young \cite{CY} study
noise in the controlled rotations of Shor-type discrete-logarithm
circuits. Their result is not a lower bound for every circuit family;
we make no claim of robustness to gate noise.

Fix the gate set $\mathcal G$ consisting of NOT, CNOT, Toffoli,
Hadamard $H$, $S=\diag(1,i)$, their inverses, and their singly
controlled versions. This is one finite set of bounded-arity gates.
Let $g_0$ be the continuous, period-one function given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phase}
 g_0((s+t)/4)=(-i)^s\{1+(-i-1)t\},\qquad
 s\in\Z,\quad 0\le t\le1.
\end{equation}
We isolate the two upstream interfaces used in the argument.

\begin{assumption}\label{ass:source}
The following interfaces hold over $\mathcal G$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item For $n\ge128$ and $Q=2^{16n}$, a polynomial-time uniform circuit of size polynomial
in $n$ has a designated ancillary success outcome whose unnormalized
data-register operator is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transform}
 A_Q(k,x)=Q^{-1/2}g_0(kx/Q),\qquad 0\le k,x<Q.
\end{equation}
On the success outcome every ancillary register of this circuit is in a
fixed state independent of $x$.
\item Integers admit probability-one factorization by polynomial-time uniform
$\mathcal G$ circuits with polynomial worst-case gate and qubit counts.
\end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}
The first item is \cite[Lemma 3.2]{OpenAI} with its stated choice
$Q=2^{16n}$. Uniform generation and the fixed ancillary success state
follow from the explicit circuit in its proof: Hadamard preparation,
$O(n^2)$ controlled constant shifts with scratch uncomputed, and an
all-zero success test. The second item is \cite[Theorem 1.1]{OpenAI}. The prime-order proposition below uses only
the first item. The full prime-field theorem also uses the second.
We do not treat the unnormalized operator in \eqref{eq:transform} as a
unitary or as an outcome that may be enforced.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:source}, there is a polynomial-time uniform
family of circuits over $\mathcal G$ with the following property.
For every prime $p$ and nonzero $g,h\pmod p$ with
$h\in\langle g\rangle$, the output is the unique
$a\in[0,\ord_p(g))$ satisfying $g^a=h$, with probability one.
Gate count, qubit count, and worst-case running time are polynomial
in $\log p$.
\end{theorem}

We also use the classical fixed-dimensional lattice-point counting
theorem \cite[Theorem 4.4 and Algorithm 5.2]{BP}: for each fixed
dimension, the number of integer points in a bounded rational polytope
is computable in deterministic polynomial time in its binary encoding.
Equalities can be written as pairs of inequalities. The weighted
variant needed here is reduced explicitly to this unweighted statement.

\section{The prime-order trial}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:prime}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:source}(1), the conclusion of
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} holds when the prime order $q$ of $g$ is supplied
as an additional input.
\end{proposition}
Suppose that the prime order $q$ of $g$ is supplied. The cases $q=2$
and $h=1$ are solved by direct testing. Hence assume $q\ge3$ and
$h\ne1$, and write $h=g^a$ with $1\le a<q$ for analysis.
Set
\[
 b=\lceil\log_2q\rceil,\quad B=2^b,\quad
 n=\max\{128,\lfloor\log_2p\rfloor+1\},\quad Q=2^{16n},
 \quad k_q(j)=\left\lfloor\frac{jQ}{q}+\frac12\right\rfloor.
\]
Since $q\le p-1<2^n$, we have $Q/q>2^{15n}$. In particular,
$Q\ge216q$, $Q/(2q)\ge2$, and $q<Q$. Prepare
\begin{equation}\label{eq:input}
 \frac1Q\sum_{0\le x,y<Q}\ket{x,y}\ket{g^xh^y\bmod p}.
\end{equation}
Reversible modular powering clears its temporary scratch registers.
In particular, no copies of $x,y$ remain outside the two input registers
before the required interference. Apply \eqref{eq:transform} to each
input register. Record the two ancillary success tests and the output
integers $k,l$.

To check whether $k=k_q(j)$, round $kq/Q$ to the nearest integer, require
$0\le j<q$, and test the displayed formula for $k_q(j)$ exactly.
A genuine bin satisfies $|kq/Q-j|\le q/(2Q)<1/2$, so this decoder is
unique. Decode $l$ similarly as $k_q(j')$. Require $j\ne0$, compute
$a'=j'j^{-1}\pmod q$ in $[0,q)$, and verify $g^{a'}=h$.
Success requires both transform flags and all these tests.
For each $1\le j<q$, let $p_j$ denote the actual probability of success
with label $j$. Its event is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:event}
 k=k_q(j),\qquad l=k_q(aj\bmod q),\qquad
 \text{both ancillary tests succeed}.
\end{equation}
We prove both a lower bound for $p_j$ and an exact algorithm to evaluate
it from the verified triple $(q,j,a)$.

\section{A lower bound for each accepted label}
The Fourier series of \eqref{eq:phase} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fourier}
 g_0(t)=\sum_{m\equiv1\ (4)}c_m e^{-2\pi i mt},\qquad
 c_m=\frac8{\pi^2m^2},\qquad \sum_m c_m=1.
\end{equation}
For completeness, periodize the triangle $T(t)=\max(1-4|t|,0)$.
Then $g_0(t)=\sum_{s=0}^3(-i)^sT(t-s/4)$.
The Fourier coefficient of $T$ is
$\frac14(\sin(\pi m/4)/(\pi m/4))^2$.
The translate sum vanishes unless $m\equiv1\pmod4$, when it is four.
This gives \eqref{eq:fourier}. The series is absolutely convergent,
and evaluation at zero gives the sum of coefficients.

For $1\le t<q$, write $k=k_q(t)$, $\eta=k-tQ/q$, and
\[
 S(k,t)=\sum_{x=0}^{Q-1}g_0(kx/Q)e^{2\pi itx/q},\qquad
 D_Q(v)=\sum_{x=0}^{Q-1}e^{-2\pi ivx}.
\]
Thus $|\eta|\le1/2$ and
\[
 S(k,t)=\sum_{m\equiv1\ (4)}c_m
 D_Q\bigl((m-1)t/q+m\eta/Q\bigr).
\]
The $m=1$ term has magnitude at least
$16Q/\pi^3>Q/2$, since $|D_Q(\eta/Q)|\ge2Q/\pi$.
This also holds when $\eta=0$ by continuity.
We bound the remaining terms in three classes.

If $q\mid m-1$, write $m=1+4qz$, $z\ne0$.
Since $|1+4qz|\ge3q|z|$, their coefficient sum is at most
\[
 \frac8{\pi^2}\sum_{z\ne0}\frac1{9q^2z^2}
 =\frac8{27q^2}<\frac1{24}.
\]
Their contribution is therefore at most $Q/24$.
For nonaliases with $|m|\le M:=Q/(2q)$, primality of $q$ gives
distance at least $1/q$ of $(m-1)t/q$ from the integers. The perturbation
has absolute value at most $1/(4q)$, leaving distance at least
$3/(4q)$ from $\mathbb Z$. Since
$|\sin\pi v|\ge2\,\mathrm{dist}(v,\mathbb Z)$, the geometric-sum
bound $|D_Q(v)|\le1/|\sin\pi v|$ gives $|D_Q(v)|\le2q/3$.
Their total contribution is at most $2q/3$.
Finally, since $M\ge2$ and $8/\pi^2<1$,
\[
 \sum_{|m|>M}c_m\le2\sum_{n>\lfloor M\rfloor}n^{-2}
 \le\frac2{\lfloor M\rfloor}\le\frac4M.
\]
Their contribution is at most $8q$. Double counting aliases in this
last upper bound only increases the error estimate. Since $Q\ge216q$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:S}
 |S(k,t)|\ge\frac Q2-\frac Q{24}-9q
 \ge\frac{5Q}{12}>\frac Q3.
\end{equation}

For the event \eqref{eq:event}, the unnormalized group-register vector is
\[
 v_{k,l}=Q^{-2}\sum_{x,y<Q}g_0(kx/Q)g_0(ly/Q)\ket{g^{x+ay}}.
\]
For analysis only, take the unit vector
$\chi_j=q^{-1/2}\sum_{z=0}^{q-1}e^{-2\pi ijz/q}\ket{g^z}$.
The group elements in this sum are distinct, and
\[
 \langle\chi_j,v_{k,l}\rangle
 =\frac{S(k,j)S(l,aj\bmod q)}{Q^2\sqrt q}.
\]
Both frequencies are nonzero. Equation~\eqref{eq:S} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pjlower}
 p_j=\|v_{k,l}\|^2\ge\frac1{81q}.
\end{equation}
The circuit never prepares or measures $\chi_j$.

\section{Exact evaluation by lattice-point counting}
Given a verified $(q,j,a)$, compute $k=k_q(j)$ and $l=k_q(aj\bmod q)$.
Taking the squared norm gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:count}
 p_j=Q^{-4}\!\sum_{\substack{0\le x,y,x',y'<Q\\
 x+ay-x'-ay'=qz,\ z\in\Z}}
 g_0(kx/Q)g_0(ly/Q)
 \overline{g_0(kx'/Q)g_0(ly'/Q)}.
\end{equation}
It suffices to impose $-Q\le z\le Q$, because
$|x+ay-x'-ay'|\le q(Q-1)$. The coefficient $a$ is now a supplied
integer. Thus the constraint is linear, not a bilinear oracle query.

For each of the four arguments $cv/Q$, where $c$ is $k$ or $l$,
introduce integers $n_v$ and $\sigma_v\in\{0,1,2,3\}$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cell}
 Q(4n_v+\sigma_v)\le4cv
 \le Q(4n_v+\sigma_v+1)-1,\qquad 0\le n_v\le Q.
\end{equation}
Every original tuple has exactly one such choice, and
\[
 g_0(cv/Q)=(-i)^{\sigma_v}
 \left[1+(-i-1)\left(\frac{4cv}{Q}-4n_v-\sigma_v\right)\right].
\]
For each of the $4^4$ residue choices, the summation domain is a bounded
rational polytope in the nine integer variables
$x,y,x',y',n_x,n_y,n_{x'},n_{y'},z$.
The weight is a Gaussian-rational polynomial of total degree at most
four, with denominator dividing $Q^4$. Its eight weighted variables
are nonnegative; the possibly negative variable $z$ is unweighted.
All coefficients have $O(\log Q+\log q)$ bits.

To reduce polynomial weights to counts, expand into monomials and use
\[
 v^d=\sum_{s=0}^d\left\{\begin{matrix}d\\s\end{matrix}\right\}
 s!\binom vs\qquad(v\ge0).
\]
The binomial coefficient counts integer tuples
$0\le t_1<\cdots<t_s<v$, each described by linear inequalities.
A monomial of total degree at most four therefore needs at most four
additional variables. Signed real and imaginary rational coefficients
are applied outside the resulting unweighted counts. The total number
of counts is bounded independently of the input, and each dimension is
at most thirteen. The theorem of \cite{BP} consequently evaluates
\eqref{eq:count} in deterministic polynomial bit time.

Interchanging primed and unprimed variables conjugates the weight and
preserves the domain, so the result is real. Its nonnegativity follows
from its squared-norm origin. More precisely, $p_j=N_j/Q^8$ for an
integer $0\le N_j\le Q^8$. It is therefore a dyadic with polynomial
bit length. This calculation does not enumerate the $q-1$ labels.

\section{Dyadic completion and exact amplification}
A polynomial-bit dyadic $A/2^T$ in $[0,1]$ can be sampled exactly by
preparing $T$ fair bits and testing whether their integer value is less
than $A$. Keeping these bits gives a coherent implementation over
$\mathcal G$. All retention tests below mean appended success flags;
they do not discard and renormalize branches.

Set $\tau=2^{-b-8}$ and $J=2b+4$. For a verified pair $(j,a)$ define
\[
 C_j=2^{-J}\left\lfloor2^J\frac{2\tau}{p_j}\right\rfloor,
 \qquad e_j=\tau-\frac{p_jC_j}{2}.
\]
Equation~\eqref{eq:pjlower} implies $\tau\le p_j/2$, whence
\[
 0\le C_j\le1,\qquad 0\le e_j<p_j2^{-J-1}.
\]
With probability one half run the ordinary trial and retain a verified
pair with probability $C_j$. With probability one half guess two
independent uniform $b$-bit integers $(j,a')$, require $1\le j<q$,
$0\le a'<q$, and $g^{a'}=h$, and retain with probability
$2^{2b+1}e_j$. The latter is less than $1/16$.
All divisions compute scaled integer floors, not nondyadic coins.
Reject invalid candidates before divisions, using fixed positive dummy
denominators on rejected branches.

For each $j$, the ordinary branch has mass $p_jC_j/2$ and the guessing
branch has mass $e_j$. Their sum is $\tau$. Exactly $q-1$ pairs verify,
and they all give the same logarithm. Thus the completed preparation
has the initially known dyadic success probability
\[
 P_0=(q-1)\tau\ge1/512.
\]
Here $q>B/2$ implies $q-1\ge B/2$. Run 1024 independent copies on
fresh registers and keep the first retained success. Its probability is
\[
 P=1-(1-P_0)^{1024}\ge3/4.
\]
Indeed, $(1-1/512)^{512}\le1/2$, by the binomial lower bound for
$(1+1/512)^{512}$. The representation of $P$ has polynomial bit length.

We include the final correction, following \cite[Lemma 5.3]{OpenAI}.
Put $L=b$, $J_*=L+2$, and
\[
 C=2^{-J_*}\left\lfloor\frac{2^{J_*}}{2P}\right\rfloor,
 \qquad e=\frac14-\frac{PC}{2}.
\]
Then $0\le C\le1$ and $0\le e<P2^{-J_*-1}$.
With probability one half run the repeated preparation and retain its
success with probability $C$. Otherwise guess a uniform $L$-bit
integer $a'$, require $a'<q$ and $g^{a'}=h$, and retain with
probability $2^{L+1}e<P/4$. Exactly one guess, $a'=a$, passes these
tests. The resulting success mass is exactly
\[
 \frac{PC}{2}+\frac12\,2^{-L}2^{L+1}e=\frac14.
\]
Every successful branch contains the same canonical logarithm.

Replace each computational-basis measurement by a copy into a fresh
history register. Later operations use that record only as a classical
control. Distinct histories stay orthogonal, so their probabilities
agree with the measured procedure. Copy no input before its intended
interference. Unroll the classical controller with a polynomial clock,
retain its workspace, and use reversible Boolean circuits for all
arithmetic. This is the retained-record construction of
\cite[Lemma 2.2]{OpenAI}; it gives a uniform unitary $V$ and its inverse
over $\mathcal G$.

Write $\psi=V\ket0$ and let $\Pi$ project onto success. Since
$\|\Pi\psi\|^2=1/4$,
\[
 (2\ket\psi\bra\psi-I)(I-2\Pi)\psi=2\Pi\psi.
\]
The success sign and the all-zero sign are exact Boolean-controlled
phases, and the reflection $2\ket\psi\bra\psi-I$ equals
$V(2\ket0\bra0-I)V^{-1}$. This is the one-step exact-amplification
case of \cite{BHMT}. It acts on all retained histories and yields the
correct logarithm with certainty.

All arithmetic and counting computations have polynomial worst-case
clocks and bit lengths. A bounded-arity instruction with runtime
addresses is implemented by testing each tuple of addresses in the
polynomial workspace. Higher controls are formed by reversible
conjunctions. These operations cost polynomial overhead. The fixed
1024 repetitions, histories, inverse, and reflections preserve that
bound. This proves Proposition~\ref{prop:prime}.

\section{Arbitrary order in a prime field}
If $g=1$, return zero. Otherwise use Assumption~\ref{ass:source}(2) to
factor $p-1$. Starting from $m=p-1$, divide by a prime $\ell$ whenever
$g^{m/\ell}=1$. The invariant $\ord_p(g)\mid m$ shows that the final
$m$ is exactly $\ord_p(g)$. There are $O(\log p)$ modular-power tests,
and the factorization of $m$ is inherited from that of $p-1$.

For each $\ell^e\mid m$ with $\ell^{e+1}\nmid m$, put
\[
 g_\ell=g^{m/\ell^e},\quad h_\ell=h^{m/\ell^e},\quad
 \gamma=g_\ell^{\ell^{e-1}}.
\]
Starting with $x_0=0$, solve the supplied-prime-order logarithm
\[
 \gamma^{d_k}=(h_\ell g_\ell^{-x_k})^{\ell^{e-1-k}},
 \qquad 0\le d_k<\ell,
\]
and set $x_{k+1}=x_k+d_k\ell^k$ for $0\le k<e$.
If $x_k\equiv a\pmod{\ell^k}$, the displayed right side is
$\gamma^{(a-x_k)/\ell^k}$, so the invariant improves by one digit.
Chinese remaindering the $x_e$ gives the unique $a\in[0,m)$.
This is the classical prime-power reduction; no novelty is claimed for
it. There are at most $\log_2m$ prime-order calls. Fresh workspace and
retained old histories preserve certainty and polynomial resources.
The promise in Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is used throughout.
