                                            DYNAMICAL MODELS FOR LIOUVILLE AND OBSTRUCTIONS TO
                                                    FURTHER PROGRESS ON SIGN PATTERNS

                                                                                       WILL SAWIN




arXiv:1809.03280v1 [math.NT] 10 Sep 2018
                                                  Abstract. We deﬁne a class of dynamical systems by modifying a construction due
                                                  to Tao, which includes certain Furstenburg limits arising from the Liouville function.
                                                  Most recent progress on the Chowla conjectures and sign patterns of the Möbius and
                                                  Liouville functions uses methods that apply to any dynamical system in this class. Hence
                                                  dynamical systems in this class with anomalous local behavior present obstructions to
                                                  further progress on these problems by the same techniques. We construct straightforward
                                                  examples of dynamical systems in this class based on polynomial phases and calculate
                                                  the resulting obstruction. This requires explicit bounds for the number of sign patterns
                                                  arising in a certain way from polynomials, which is elementary but not completely trivial.


                                                                                   1. Introduction
                                              Matomäki and Radziwill proved that the Liouville function cancels in almost intervals
                                           of a given length, as long as the length increases to inﬁnity [3, Theorem 1]. Using this,
                                           Matomäki, Radziwill, and Tao showed certain results about sign patterns of the Liouville
                                           function [4]. Here a sign pattern is an element of {−1, 1}k and we say it occurs at
                                           a point n if it equals (λ(n), . . . , λ(n + k − 1)). They show that every three-term sign
                                           pattern occurs with positive density and give a lower bound for the number of k-term sign
                                           patterns that occur with positive density as a function of k. These two papers have a very
                                           diﬀerent ﬂavor. [3] uses the multiplicativity of Liouville at large primes, making it closer to
                                           traditional analytic number theory, and is more conceptual. [4] uses only multiplicativity
                                           at small primes, is more combinatorial, and uses ad-hoc arguments that involve checking
                                           diﬀerent cases. Later work of Tao [5] and Tao and Teräväinen [8] improves on [4] by using
                                           information-theoretic methods to prove the logarithmically averaged Chowla’s conjecture
                                           for all correlations of the Liouville function over two or an odd number of points, which
                                           among other applications gives further results on sign patterns.
                                              To make further progress using the methods of [3], [5], and [8], new ideas are needed,
                                           but [4] gives the impression that it’s possible that even more complicated arguments,
                                           perhaps too complicated to be found by humans or computers, could prove new cases
                                           with longer sign patterns. It would be desirable to show that this is not entirely true, and
                                           that progress beyond a certain point on the sign pattern problem requires progress on the
                                           analytic side. The idea by Tao [7] of studying the Furstenburg limits of Liouville gives a
                                           way to make this a well-deﬁned mathematical problem, which we will solve in this paper.
                                              Given a sequence λ(n) of signs, one can ﬁnd a sequence Ni of natural numbers such
                                           that for each k, and for each sign pattern of length k, the fraction of numbers from 1 to
                                           Ni where that sign pattern occurs converges as i goes to ∞. Such a sequence determines a

does not use the ﬁrst axiom of [7, Proposition 1], which states that the dynamical system
actually arises as a limit of Liouville, except to apply the main theorem of [3]. The same
is true for the combinatorial arguments of [4]. Hence we can test the boundaries of these
methods by replacing that axiom by a weaker one, which encapsulates what has been
proven on the analytic side (or a potential future analytic result). In this paper we will
show concrete limits on what can be proven in these weaker axiom systems by explicit
examples.
   In fact we will use a slightly diﬀerent set of axioms, which turns out to lead to simpler
constructions of dynamical systems. As we will see below, dynamical systems satisfying
all of Tao’s axioms but the ﬁrst can be constructed from dynamical models for λ in our
sense by an inverse limit construction.
Definition 1.1. Let λ : N → {±1} be a multiplicative function. Deﬁne a dynami-
cal model for λ to be a measurable space X with probability measure µ, a measurable
function F : X → ±1 a measure-preserving transformation: T : X → X, a measure
preserving transformation M : X → Ẑ, and for each natural number n a transformation
In : M −1 (nẐ) → X, such that that for any x ∈ X outside a set of measure 0:
    (1) For any n ∈ N with M(x) ∈ nẐ, nM(In (x)) = M(x).
    (2) M(T (x)) = M(x) + 1.
    (3) For any n ∈ N with M(x) ∈ nẐ, T (In (x)) = In (T n (x)).
    (4) For any n, m ∈ N with M(x) ∈ nmẐ, Im (In (x)) = Inm (x) (where the composition
        is well-deﬁned by axiom (1)).
    (5) For any n ∈ N with M(x) ∈ nẐ, F (In (x)) = λ(n)F (x).
   and such that the pushforward of the measure µ along In (after restriction to M −1 (nZ))
is µ/n.
Remark 1.2. Here we think of X as the natural numbers, µ as the uniform measure on
some long interval, F as λ, T as increment, M as the natural map N → Ẑ, and In as the
division by n map from multiples of n to all numbers.
   We can make this more precise by explaining how some dynamical models for λ arise
from Furstenburg limits of λ. We can take X = (±1)N0 × Ẑ. For each point n ∈ N we
deﬁne a point (h 7→ λ(n + h), h) of X. By logarithmically averaging the point measures
associated to numbers n in the interval [1, . . . , N], we can deﬁne a measure on X. Any
sequence of Ns admits a subsequence on which these measures converge to some limit,
which we will take to be µ. One takes F (f, m) = f (0), T ((f, m)) = (h 7→ f (h + 1), m + 1),
In (f, m)) = (h 7→ λ(n)f (nh), m/n) and veriﬁes that identities (1-5) hold and that the
measure associated to N is approximately preserved by T , M, and In , with the last after
multiplication by 1/2, so the limit is exactly preserved by this transformation. Removing

Definition 1.3. The sign pattern ǫ1 , . . . , ǫk ∈ {−1, 1}k appears in (X, µ, F, T, M, In ) if

d − 1-step nilsystem. For this reason, we focus our attention on the following deﬁnition.

                             2. Polynomial Sign Patterns
   View Rd+1 as the space of polynomials in one variable of degree d.
   Let sgn : Rd+1 → {+1, −1}k be the function that sends a polynomial f to the k-tuple
of signs whose nth element is (−1)⌊f (n)⌋ .
   The goal of this section is to bound explicitly the cardinality of the image of sgn from
the space of all degree d polynomials.
Definition 2.1. Deﬁne a pre-cell to be a closed convex subset of Rd+1 of the form {f ∈
Rd+1 |vn ≤ f (n) ≤ vn + 1 for all n from 1 to k} for a k-tuple of integers v1 , . . . , vk . Say
that it is a cell if it has nonempty interior.
  Say that two nontrivial cells C1 , C2 are equivalent if C2 is equal to the translation of
C1 by a polynomial f ∈ Rd+1 such that f (n) ∈ 2Z for all n in Z.
Lemma 2.2. A polynomial f lies in the interior of a precell if and only if it lies in the
precell and f (n) is not an integer for any n from 1 to k.
Proof. If f lies in the precell {f ∈ Rd+1 |vn ≤ f (n) ≤ vn + 1 for all n from 1 to k} and
takes non-integer values, then f lies in {f ∈ Rd+1 |vn < f (n) < vn +1 for all n from 1 to k}
which is open and is contained in the precell, and hence f lies in the interior.
   If f takes some integer value, then for some n ∈ {1, . . . , k}, f (n) = vn or f (n) = vn+1 .
In the ﬁrst case f −ǫ is not in the precell for any positive ǫ, and in the second case f (n)+ǫ
is not in the precell for any positive ǫ, so in neither case is f in the interior.           
Lemma 2.3. Every sign pattern in the image of sgn is attained for some polynomial
whose coefficients lie in the interior of some cell.
Proof. Let g(x) be a polynomial of degree d and let v(n) = ⌊g(n)⌋. Consider the precell
{f ∈ Rd+1 |vn ≤ f (n) ≤ vn + 1 for all n from 1 to k}.
   For ǫ > 0 suﬃciently small, we have g(n) + ǫ ∈ (⌊g(n)⌋, ⌊g(n)⌋ + 1).
   Hence ⌊g(n) + ǫ⌋ = ⌊g(n)⌋, so sgn(g(n) + ǫ) = sgn(g(n), and g + ǫ ∈ [vn , vn+1 ], so g + ǫ
lies in this precell, and ﬁnally g(n) + ǫ takes noninteger values, so it lies in the interior of
the precell by Lemma 2.2, and thus the precell is in fact a cell.                             
Lemma 2.4. sgn is constant on the interior of each cell.
Proof. The function (−1)⌊f (n)⌋ is locally constant everywhere that f (n) is not an integer.
For n from 1 to k, f (n) is not an integer on the interior of each cell by Lemma 2.2.

Because cells are convex bodies, their interiors are connected, so this function is constant
on the interior of each cell. Hence sgn is constant on the interior of each cell.         
Lemma 2.5. On two equivalent cells, sgn(f ) takes the same value.
Proof. sgn(f ) is invariant under translation by even-integer-valued polynomials, so it takes
the same value on two sets where one is a translation of another by such a polynomial. 
Lemma 2.6. The cardinality of the image of sgn(f ) is at most the number of equivalence
classes of nontrivial cells.
Proof. Every sign pattern in the image of f is attained in the interior of some nontrivial
cell by Lemma 2.3. Because the sign pattern is constant on each cell by Lemma 2.4, and
equal for equivalent cells by Lemma 2.5, there is at most one sign pattern appearing in
each equivalence class of cells.                                                        

Remark 3.5. The relation of dynamical models for λ to the axioms of [7, Proposition 1]
