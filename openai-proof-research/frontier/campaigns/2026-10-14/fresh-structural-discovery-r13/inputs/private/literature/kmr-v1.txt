                                           0. Introduction
                                           A system of equations {wi = 1 | i ∈ I} with coefficients from a group G, where wi are words in the
                                                                                                                                                       e
                                           alphabet G ⊔ X ±1 , and X is a set (the set of unknowns), is called solvable over G, if there exist a group G
                                           containing G as a subgroup and a retraction of the free product G   e ∗ F (X) onto G
                                                                                                                              e containing all elements
                                           wi in its kernel (henceforth, F (X) is the free group with basis X). If the solution group G e can be chosen
                                           from a class K, then we say that the system is solvable in K.
                                                The study of solvability of equations over groups has a long history: see, e.g., [GR62], [Le62], [Ly80],
                                           [How81], [B84], [EH91], [How91], [K93], [KP95], [FeR96], [K97], [K99], [CG00], [EdJu00], [IK00], [Juhá03],
                                           [K06], [P08] [BK12], [KL12], [KT17], [Ro17], [BE18], [ABA21], [EH21], [NT22], [KM22], and references
                                           therein; see also surveys [Ro12], [NRR79], and book [LS80].
                                                A system of equations (possibly infinite and with, possibly, infinitely many unknowns) over a group is
                                           called nonsingular if the rows composed of the exponent-sums of unknowns in each equation are linearly
                                           independent over Q. If these row are linearly independent over the p-element field Fp = Z/pZ for each
                                           prime p, then we call the system of equations unimodular. In particular, one equation with one unknown is
                                              – nonsingular if the exponent sum of the unknown in this equation is nonzero;
                                              – unimodular if this sum is ±1.
                                           Unimodular equations behave better than arbitrary nonsingular ones: e.g, in [K93] (see also [FeR96]), it was
                                           proven that

                                                           any unimodular equation over a torsion-free group is solvable over this group;

                                           it is unknown whether a similar statement is valid for arbitrary nonsingular equations.
                                                 For nilpotent groups, everything is simple: Shmel’kin’s theorem [Sh67] says (in particular) that
                                            any finite nonsingular system of equations over a nilpotent torsion-free group G has a (unique) solution in
                                            a nilpotent group Ge ⊇ G of the same nilpotency class; namely, G  e is the completion of G (if the system is
                                            unimodular, then the torsion-free condition can be dropped, and the unique solution exists in G itself).*)

                                           For solvable groups, the situation is more complicated: Section 1 contains examples showing (in particular)
                                           that
                                                   there exists a unimodular equation with one unknown over a metabelian group (that can be chosen
                                                   finite or, on the contrary, torsion-free), which is not solvable in any larger metabelian group.

                                           (An example on this subject in [Ro17] is incorrect.)

     It is unknown whether this is true or false, but there are many partial results on this subject (see
references above). We suggest to call a class K of groups a Howie class if any nonsingular system of equations
over each group from K has a solution in a larger group from K.
     The quasivariety generated by a Howie class is a Howie class too (see Section 2). Therefore, we are
mainly interested in Howie quasivarieties (i.e. Howie classes which are quasivarieties). Examples of such
quasivarieties are
   – the variety of all abelian groups, and also the quasivariety of all abelian torsion-free groups (an easy
     exercise);
   – the quasivariety generated by all finite groups ([GR62] + Malcev theorem on local residual finiteness
     of linear groups [Ma40]).
Examples from Section 1 mentioned above show that the variety of all metabelian groups is not a Howie
class. Nevertheless, we prove the following theorem in Section 3.
Main theorem. The class consisting of all extensions of groups from a Howie quasivariety by abelian
torsion-free groups is a Howie quasivariety too.
     In particular, since abelian groups form a Howie quasivariety, an obvious induction leads us to the
following fact on solvable groups:
                   for each positive integer n, the class of groups G admitting a subnor-
                   mal series G = G1 ⊲ . . . ⊲ Gn+1 = {1} whose all quotients Gi /Gi+1
                   are abelian, and all, except maybe the last one, are torsion-free is a
                   Howie quasivariety, i.e. any nonsingular system of equations over any
                   group from this class has a solution in a larger group from this class.
(We can drop the words “except maybe the last one”  and obtain another valid fact; indeed, we can start
with a simple observation: the trivial quasivariety {1} is a Howie class, and then use a similar induction
argument.) Note, however, that the minimum length of such a series can be larger than the derived length;
see Section 1.

2. Quasivarieties and systems of equations
Recall that a quasivariety of groups is the class of all groups satisfying a given (finite or infinite) set of
quasi-identities, i.e. sentences of the form
                      ∀x, y, . . . v1 (x, y, . . .) = 1 & . . . & vk (x, y, . . .) = 1 =⇒ w(x, y, . . .) = 1,

where vi and w are some elements of the free group F (x, y, . . .). For example, the quasivariety of abelian
torsion-free groups can be defined by the following quasi-identities (the last one is, in fact, an identity):
∀x x2 = 1 =⇒ x = 1, ∀x x3 = 1 =⇒ x = 1, . . . , ∀x, y 1 = 1 =⇒ [x, y] = 1. Another (equivalent)
definition of the notion of quasivariety can found below, in the beginning of the proof of Proposition 2. For
more details about quasivarieties, see books [Bu02] and [Go99]; we recall only that
             the class consisting of all possible extensions of groups from a quasivariety by groups from
             another quasivariety is a quasivariety [Ma67] (called the product of two quasivarieties).
Therefore, to prove the main theorem, it suffices to show that the class of extensions (in that theorem) is a
Howie class.

quasivariety K, and the quotient group G/B = A is abelian and torsion-free. Then any finite nonsingular
system of equations {wi = 1 | i ∈ I} (where wi ∈ G ∗ F (X) and I is a finite set) has a solution in some
group Ge ⊇ G, which is also an extension of a group from K by an abelian torsion-free group.
