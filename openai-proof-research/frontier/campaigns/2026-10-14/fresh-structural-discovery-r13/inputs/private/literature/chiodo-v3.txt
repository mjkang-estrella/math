                                             A UNIVERSAL FINITELY PRESENTED TORSION-FREE
                                                                GROUP

                                                                             MAURICE CHIODO




arXiv:1107.1489v3 [math.GR] 8 Mar 2013
                                                 Abstract. We give a uniform construction that, on input of a recursive
                                                 presentation P of a group, outputs a recursive presentation P tor-free of a
                                                 torsion-free group, isomorphic to P whenever P is itself torsion-free. We use
                                                 this to form a universal finitely presented torsion-free group; one into which
                                                 all finitely presented torsion-free groups embed. We apply our techniques
                                                 to extend a result by Lempp that recognising torsion-freeness of finitely
                                                 presented groups is Π02 -complete; we show that recognising embeddability
                                                 of finitely presented groups is Π02 -hard, Σ02 -hard, and lies in Σ03 . We also
                                                 show that the sets of orders of torsion elements of finitely presented groups
                                                 are precisely the Σ02 sets which are factor-complete (i.e. closed under taking
                                                 factors); by a simple numbering trick any Σ02 set is thus one-one equivalent
                                                 to the set of orders of torsion elements of a finitely presented group.




                                                                            1. Introduction
                                            A finite presentation ⟨X∣R⟩ of a group is a finite collection of generators
                                         X, together with a finite set R of defining relations. A recursive presentation
                                         ⟨X∣R⟩ of a group is a finite collection of generators X, together with a recursive
                                         enumeration of a (possibly infinite) set R of defining relations. The Higman
                                         embedding theorem [14] shows that every recursively presented group embeds
                                         into a finitely presented group. Moreover, this embedding can be made uniform;
                                         there is an algorithm that takes any recursive presentation P and outputs a
                                         finite presentation Q and an explicit embedding φ ∶ P ↪ Q.
                                            This embedding theorem was used by Higman to show the existence of a
                                         universal finitely presented group; one into which all finitely presented groups
                                         embed. By analysing Higman’s embedding theorem, we make the following re-
                                         markable extension of this fact.

                                         Theorem 4.8. There is a universal finitely presented torsion-free group G.
                                         That is, G is torsion-free, and for any finitely presented group H we have that
                                         H ↪ G if (and only if ) H is torsion-free.

                                           Moreover, this G can be made to have an embedded copy of every recursively
                                         presented torsion-free group, and still be finitely presented (theorem 4.9).

  Our work begins with the following important technical observation: that the
Higman embedding theorem can strictly preserve the orders of torsion elements.

Theorem 2.2. There is a uniform algorithm that, on input of a countably
generated recursive presentation P , constructs a finite presentation T(P ) such
that the group P embeds in the group T(P ), and an explicit embedding φ ∶ P ↪
T(P ). Moreover, for each k ∈ N, the group P has an element of order k if and
only if the group T(P ) has an element of order k.

   We note that Collins [12], extending the work of Clapham [10, 11], has shown
that the Higman embedding theorem can be made to simultaneously preserve
the Turing degrees of the word, order, and power problems (see [23] for an
introduction to Turing degrees). However, this does not immediately imply
that the orders of torsion elements are preserved under the embedding.
   Every group G has a unique torsion-free quotient through which all other
torsion-free quotients factor; we call this the torsion-free universal quotient
Gtor-free . By carefully considering the behaviour of recursively presented torsion-
free groups, we give the following construction:

Theorem 3.14. There is a uniform algorithm that, on input of a countably
generated recursive presentation P = ⟨X∣R⟩ of a group, outputs a countably
generated recursive presentation P tor-free = ⟨X∣R′ ⟩ (on the same generating set
X, and with R ⊆ R′ as sets) of the torsion-free universal quotient of P .

   Our main result, theorem 4.8, then follows by combining theorem 2.2 and
theorem 3.14, in a way similar to Higman’s original construction of a universal
finitely presented group.
   The independent work of Boone, Britton and Novikov [1, 5, 20] gave the first
examples of constructions which realise incomputable sets in finitely presented
groups. This was achieved by incorporating the action of a Turing machine into
a finite presentation. However, these early constructions showed only that cer-
tain problems in group theory were incomputable (more specifically, Σ01 -hard),
but not any higher level of complexity in the arithmetic hierarchy (see [23],
or the introduction to [17], for a description of Σ0n sets, Π0n sets, and Kleene’s
arithmetic hierarchy). It is known [19] that the problem of recognising each
of the following properties amongst finitely presented groups is Σ01 -complete:
trivial, abelian, finite, free, nilpotent, polycyclic, automatic. Boone and Rogers
[3] showed that having solvable word problem is Σ03 -complete. More recently,
Papasoglu [22] has shown that being word hyperbolic is Σ01 -complete.
   In [17] it was shown by Lempp that the problem of recognising torsion-
freeness for finitely presented groups is Π02 -complete. In [7] we showed that
there exists a finitely presented group whose finitely presented subgroups are
not recursively enumerable (i.e. not Σ01 ). Applying the techniques and results
from this paper, we extend the result by Lempp and show the following:
            A UNIVERSAL FINITELY PRESENTED TORSION-FREE GROUP                      3


Theorem 5.6. Take an enumeration P1 , P2 , . . . of all finite presentations of
groups, and use ⟨i, j⟩ to denote Cantor’s pairing function; a computable bijection
from N × N to N. Then the set K = {⟨i, j⟩ ∈ N ∣ Pi embeds in Pj , as groups} is
Σ02 -hard, Π02 -hard, and has a Σ03 description (we conjecture it is Σ03 -complete).

   We write TorOrd(G) to denote the orders of torsion elements of a group G,
and say a set X ⊆ N is factor-complete if it is closed under taking multiplicative
factors (excluding 1). Extending our techniques, and using an idea by Dorais
[30], we give the following complete characterisation of sets which can occur as
TorOrd(G) for G a finitely presented group:

Theorem 6.3. Let X ⊆ N. Then the following are equivalent:
1. X = TorOrd(G) for some finitely presented group G.
2. X is factor-complete and has a Σ02 description.

  By a simple prime numbering trick (if A ⊆ N then Aprime ∶= {pi ∣ i ∈ A}), we
show that we can realise any Σ02 set, up to one-one equivalence:

Theorem 6.6. Given any Σ02 set A, the set Aprime is one-one equivalent to
A, and can be realised as the set of orders of torsion elements of some finitely
presented group G.

                               2. Preliminaries
2.1. Notation.
We take ϕm to be the mth partial recursive function ϕm ∶ N → N, and the mth
partial recursive set (r.e. set) Wm as the domain of ϕm . If P = ⟨X∣R⟩ is a
group presentation with generating set X and relators R, then we denote by P
the group presented by P . A presentation P = ⟨X∣R⟩ is said to be a recursive
presentation if X is a finite set and R is a recursive enumeration of relators;
P is said to be a countably generated recursive presentation if instead X is
a recursive enumeration of generators. A group G is said to be finitely (resp.
recursively) presentable if G ≅ P for some finite (resp. recursive) presentation P .
If P, Q are group presentations then we denote their free product presentation
by P ∗ Q, given by taking the disjoint union of their generators and relators;
this extends to the free product of arbitrary collections of presentations. If
X is a set, we write X ∗ for the set of finite words on X ∪ X −1 , including the
4                                 MAURICE CHIODO


empty word ∅. If φ ∶ X → Y ∗ is a set map, then we write φ ∶ X ∗ → Y ∗ for
the extension of φ. If g1 , . . . , gn are a collection of elements of a group G, then
we write ⟨g1 , . . . , gn ⟩G for the subgroup in G generated by these elements, and
⟪g1 , . . . , gn ⟫G for the normal closure of these elements in G. If ρ is an algebraic
property of groups, then we write ρ–grp for the class of groups with property
ρ, and we write G ∈ ρ–grp to mean that G has property ρ. Cantor’s pairing
function is given by ⟨., .⟩ ∶ N × N → N, ⟨x, y⟩ ∶= 12 (x + y)(x + y + 1) + y which is a
computable bijection.
2.2. Embedding theorems.
Definition 2.1. Let G be a group. We let o(g) denote the order of a group
element g, and say g is torsion if 1 ≤ o(g) < ∞. We then define the sets
                          Tor(G) ∶= {g ∈ G ∣ g is torsion}
             TorOrd(G) ∶= {n ∈ N ∣ ∃g ∈ Tor(G) with o(g) = n ≥ 2}
   Thus Tor(G) is the set of torsion elements of G, and TorOrd(G) is the set of
orders of non-trivial torsion elements of G (never containing 0 or 1). We note
that in [21] Osin uses the notation π(G) to denote TorOrd(G); there does not
seem to be any consistent notation in the literature for this.
   In [7, Lemma 6.9 and Theorem 6.10] we made the following observation about
the Higman embedding theorem, which we state here for later use.
Theorem 2.2 (Higman). There is a uniform algorithm that, on input of a
countably generated recursive presentation P = ⟨X∣R⟩, constructs a finite pre-
sentation T(P ) such that P ↪ T(P ) and TorOrd(P ) = TorOrd(T(P )), along
with an explicit map φ which extends to an embedding φ ∶ P ↪ T(P ).
  Though we rely heavily on theorem 2.2 later on, we note that is was originally
used in [7] to show the following:
Theorem 2.3 (Chiodo [7, Lemma 6.11]). There is a uniform algorithm that, on
input of any n ∈ N, constructs a finite presentation Qn such that TorOrd(Qn )
is one-one equivalent to N ∖ Wn . Taking n′ with Wn′ non-recursive thus gives
that TorOrd(Qn′ ) is not recursively enumerable; thus the finitely presentable
subgroups of Qn′ are not recursively enumerable.
    Osin [21] has proved the following related embedding result.
Theorem 2.4 (Osin [21, Theorem 1.1]). Any countable group G can be embed-
ded into a 2-generator group C such that any two elements in C of the same
order are conjugate in C, and TorOrd(C) = TorOrd(G).
   We note that C so constructed above cannot always be made to be recursively
presentable, even on input of a finitely presentable group (as pointed out to the
author by Denis Osin). For suppose it could. Then take a finitely presentable
torsion-free group G with unsolvable word problem ([19, Theorem 2.3]), and
suppose there was a C as above which was recursively presentable. Then C
will have precisely 2 conjugacy classes, and so will be a recursively presentable
simple group, and hence have solvable word problem ([19, Theorem 6.2]). But
G is a subgroup of C with unsolvable word problem, a contradiction.

                             4. Universal groups
Definition 4.1. For ρ an algebraic property of groups, we say a finitely pre-
sentable group G is a universal ρ group if both of the following occur:
1. G ∈ ρ–grp.
2. Every finitely presentable group H ∈ ρ–grp embeds in G.
  The motivation for such a definition comes from the following famous result
by Higman. We outline a proof (based around the Higman embedding theorem:
theorem 2.2), as we will generalise it later.
Theorem 4.2 (Higman, [19, Theorem 2.5]). There is a universal finitely pre-
sentable group. That is, a finitely presentable group into which every finitely
presentable group embeds.
Proof. Take an enumeration P1 , P2 , . . . of all finite presentations of groups, and
form the countably generated recursive presentation P ∶= P1 ∗ P2 ∗ . . . Now use
theorem 2.2 to embed P into a finitely presentable group T(P ). By construc-
tion, T(P ) has an embedded copy of every finitely presentable group, since P
did.                                                                                
   The above theorem is not merely existential. Using the uniformity of Hig-
man’s construction, Valiev [28] produced an explicit finite presentation of a
universal finitely presentable group using 14 generators and 42 relators, which
he subsequently reduced to 21 relators in [29].
   It seems reasonable to ask for which (if any) algebraic properties of groups
ρ there exists a universal ρ group. An easy example is free groups.
            A UNIVERSAL FINITELY PRESENTED TORSION-FREE GROUP                        9


Lemma 4.3. There is a universal free group (namely, F2 ).
Proof. Take the standard presentation P ∶= ⟨a, b∣−⟩ of F2 . Then, for any n ∈ N,
the set {b−i abi ∣1 ≤ i ≤ n} freely generates Fn in P , which follows easily from the
normal form theorem for free products (see [18, §IV Theorem 1.2]).                 
  We now use some abstract formalisation to prove that certain well-known
group properties do not admit a universal group.
Definition 4.4. An algebraic property of groups ρ is said to be strongly held
under subgroups if there is some function fρ ∶ {finitely presentable groups} → N
satisfying the following two conditions:
1. If G ∈ ρ–grp and H ≤ G, with G, H both finitely presentable, then H ∈ ρ–grp
and fρ (H) ≤ fρ (G).
2. For each n ∈ N there is some finitely presentable G ∈ ρ–grp with fρ (G) > n.
Lemma 4.5. The following are some examples of group properties which are
strongly held under subgroups: finite, abelian, solvable, nilpotent.
Proof. For ρ being finite (resp. abelian, nilpotent, solvable), we simply take
fρ (G) to be the cardinality of G (resp. free abelian rank, nilpotency class,
derived length). All properties are then immediate.                         
Proposition 4.6. Let ρ be a group property which is strongly held under sub-
groups. Then there is no universal (finitely presentable) ρ group.
Proof. Assume there exists a universal ρ group G. Then fρ (G) = M ∈ N. Since
ρ is strongly held under subgroups, there is some finitely presentable H ∈ ρ–
grp with fρ (H) > M . But G is a universal ρ group, so H ≤ G. But since ρ
is strongly held under subgroups, we must have that fρ (H) ≤ fρ (G) = M , a
contradiction since H was taken with fρ (H) > M .                          
Corollary 4.7. None of the following group properties admit a universal (finitely
presentable) group: finite, abelian, solvable, nilpotent.
   Moreover, Miller [19, Corollary 3.14], extending a result of Boone and Rogers
[3, Theorem 2], showed there is no universal solvable word problem group.
   Combining several of our previous observations, we now give a key result of
this work.
Theorem 4.8. There is a universal (finitely presentable) torsion-free group G.
That is, G is finitely presentable, and for any finite presentation P we have that
P ↪ G if (and only if ) P is torsion-free.
Proof. Take an enumeration P1 , P2 , . . . of all finite presentations of groups, and
construct the countably generated recursive presentation Q ∶= P1tor-free ∗P2tor-free ∗
. . .; this is the countably infinite free product of the universal torsion-free quo-
tient of all finitely presentable groups (with some repetition). As each Pitor-free
is uniformly constructible from Pi (by theorem 3.14), we have that our construc-
tion of Q is indeed effective, and hence Q is a countably generated recursive
presentation. Also, theorem 3.14 shows that Q is a torsion-free group, as we
have successfully annihilated all the torsion in the free product factors, and the
10                               MAURICE CHIODO


free product of torsion-free groups is again torsion-free. Moreover, Q contains
an embedded copy of every torsion-free finitely presentable group, as proposi-
tion 3.2 shows that the universal torsion-free quotient of a torsion-free group is
itself. Now use theorem 2.2 to embed Q into a finitely presentable group T(Q).
By construction, ∅ = TorOrd(Q) = TorOrd(T(Q)), so T(Q) is torsion-free.
Finally, T(Q) has an embedded copy of every finitely presentable torsion-free
group, since Q did. Taking G to be T(Q) completes the proof.                    

Note. One may ask why theorem 4.8 does not follow immediately from Higman’s
embedding theorem by taking the free product of all finite presentations of
torsion-free groups, and using the fact that Higman’s theorem preserves orders
of torsion elements. This cannot work, as we later shown in theorem 5.2 that the
set of finite presentations of torsion-free groups is not recursively enumerable.

     We can generalise theorem 4.8 slightly, to the following extent:

Theorem 4.9. There is a finitely presentable group G which is torsion free, and
contains an embedded copy of every countably generated recursively presentable
torsion-free group.

Proof. By starting with an enumeration P1 , P2 , . . . of all countably generated
recursive presentations of groups in the proof of theorem 4.8, constructing their
torsion-free universal quotients Pitor-free, and following the rest of the proof
there, we can construct a finite presentation P of a group that has an embedded
copy of every countably generated recursively presentable torsion-free group.
                                                                                

  Following the methods of Valiev in [28, 29], one might attempt to construct
explicit finite presentations of the groups described in theorems 4.8 and 4.9.
  An important question is whether there exists a universal simple group. We
use the rest of this section to draw a connection between such a group and the
Boone-Higman theorem, originally proved in [2, Theorem 1] and strengthened
by Thompson as follows:

Theorem 4.10 (Thompson [27, Main Theorem]). A finitely presentable group
G has solvable word problem if and only if G embeds into a finitely generated
simple group S which in turn embeds into a finitely presentable group H.

   It is a long-standing open problem as to whether a finitely presentable group
G has solvable word problem if and only if G embeds into some finitely pre-
sentable simple group S.
   As finitely presentable simple groups have solvable word problem, so do their
finitely presentable subgroups. Existence of a universal simple group would
show that not every finitely presentable group with solvable word problem
embeds in some finitely presentable simple group. For if so, then all finitely
presentable groups with solvable word problem would embed into a universal
simple group, which would then be a universal solvable word problem group,
contradicting Miller’s result that no such group exists [19, Corollary 3.14].

                        5. Complexity of embeddings
   Using the machinery described in section 2, we can encode the following

Lemma 5.1 ([23, §13.3 Theorem VIII]). The set {n ∈ N ∣ Wn = N} is Π02 -

   Our existing observations immediately recover the following result, first proved
in [17, Main Theorem] by Lempp.
Theorem 5.2. The set of finite presentations of torsion-free groups is Π02 -
complete.
Proof. Given n ∈ N, we use theorem 2.3 to construct a finite presentation Qn

if and only if Wn = N. From lemma 5.1, {n ∈ N ∣ Wn = N} is Π02 -complete, so
the set of torsion-free finite presentations is at least Π02 -hard. But this set has
the following Π02 description (taken from [17]):
       G is torsion-free if and only if ∀w ∈ G∀n > 0(wn ≠G e or w =G e)
and hence is Π02 -complete.                                                       

Corollary 5.3. There is a finitely presented group whose finitely presentable
subgroups form a Π02 -complete set.
   Before proceeding, we show that given an index n with Wn finite, we can
recursively construct an index n′ with Wn′ = {0, . . . , ∣Wn ∣ − 1}.
Lemma 5.4. There is a recursive function h ∶ N → N satisfying the following:
1. If Wn = ∅ then Wh(n) = ∅.
2. If 1 ≤ ∣Wn ∣ < ∞ then Wh(n) = {0, . . . , ∣Wn ∣ − 1}.
3. If ∣Wn ∣ = ∞ then Wh(n) = N.
Proof. Given n, we begin an enumeration of Wn . For each element enumerated
into Wn we increase the size of Wh(n) by 1, by adding the next smallest integer
not already in Wh(n) . If Wn = ∅ then Wh(n) = ∅. If 1 ≤ ∣Wn ∣ < ∞, then
Wh(n) = {0, . . . , ∣Wn ∣ − 1}. If ∣Wn ∣ = ∞ then we will continue to sequentially
enumerate elements of N into Wh(n) , so Wh(n) = N. As this is an effective
description of Wh(n) , we have that h is recursive.                             

                         6. Complexity of TorOrd(G)
  We now apply our techniques to investigate the complexity of TorOrd(G) for
G a finitely presented group.
Definition 6.1. If n ∈ N then we denote by π(n) the set of all non-trivial
factors of n, including n but excluding 1. Call a set X ⊆ N factor-complete if it
is closed under taking non-trivial factors. That is, n ∈ X ⇒ π(n) ⊆ X.
   Elementary group theory shows that TorOrd(G) is factor-complete for ev-
ery group G. Fortunately, we are able to give an analysis of precisely which
factor-complete sets can appear as TorOrd(G) for G finitely presented. We first
presented the following theorem and subsequent proof in [8].

Theorem 6.3. Let X ⊆ N. Then the following are equivalent:
1. X = TorOrd(G) for some finitely presented group G.
2. X is factor-complete and has a Σ02 description.

Note. In this section, all our constructions for finitely presented groups ‘pass
through’ finitely generated recursively presented groups, via theorem 2.2. More-
over, TorOrd(G) has a Σ02 description for any such group. Thus, all the results in
this section retract back to finitely generated recursively presented groups. That
is, there is no additional computational ‘freedom’ in considering TorOrd for
finitely generated recursively presented groups, over finitely presented groups.

                                 7. Further work
     This paper invites research into several questions. We mention some here.
Further research problem 1. The positions of the following properties in
the arithmetic hierarchy have not been fully determined. Techniques such as
those we have covered here may be of use in locating them.
1. Solvable: Known to have a Σ03 description.
2. Residually finite: Known to have a Π02 description.
3. Simple: Known to have a Π02 description.
4. Orderable: Known to have a Π03 description (the Ohnishi condition).
5. Right orderable: Known to have a Π03 description (the Ohnishi condition).
Points 1–3 are mentioned in [19, p. 20], while points 4–5 appear in [13, Lemma
2.2.1 and Lemma 2.2.2]. We note that it may very well be the case that some
of these are neither Π0n -complete nor Σ0n -complete, for any n.
Further research problem 2. Given the existence of a universal torsion-free
group (theorem 4.8), and the constructions of Valiev [28, 29] of finite presen-
tations of universal finitely presented groups, one could perhaps combine these
techniques to produce an explicit finite presentation of a universal torsion-free
group.
