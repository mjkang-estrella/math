Quantitative stability of optimal transport
via regularization



Songbo Wang

Affiliation: CEREMADE, Université Paris-Dauphine, Université PSL, Paris, France

  
Xiaozhen Wang

Affiliation: CMAP, École polytechnique, Institut polytechnique de Paris, Palaiseau, France




Abstract
    

We prove quantitative stability estimates for quadratic optimal transport
maps by regularizing a Brenier potential and estimating the error in the
duality gap. The source has an L^{p} density, p>1, relative to a
probability measure with a BV density. When one target lies in a fixed
ball, we obtain the W_{2} Hölder exponent (p-1)/(4p-2). The constant
depends on the dimension through three explicit source quantities: the
variance, the total variation of the reference density and the L^{p}
norm of the weight. The proof uses a second-order bound on the averaged
regularized Fenchel gap. By restricting the slopes of the potential,
we also treat targets with a bounded moment of any order greater than
two. In both estimates, the condition is imposed on one target, while
the other can be any probability measure with finite second moment.
For Gaussian and isotropic log-concave sources, the constants grow
polynomially with the dimension. For L^{p} changes of Gaussian measure,
the factor in the bounded-target estimate is d^{(p-1)/(4p-2)}.
For each p, a fixed source in two dimensions proves optimality of both
stability exponents in this source class. A Gaussian construction
provides polynomial lower bounds on the dimension dependence.
  



keywordsoptimal transport, quantitative stability, regularization, convex duality, bounded variation




1 Introduction and main results




Let \rho\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{d}) be absolutely continuous. For
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{d}), denote by T_{\mu}=\nabla\phi_{\mu} the Brenier map from
\rho to \mu, for the quadratic cost. Quantitative stability asks how
\lVert T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\rVert_{L^{2}(\rho)} depends on a Wasserstein distance between
\mu and \nu. We allow discrete targets and impose a support or
higher-moment bound on one target. All norms are Euclidean, and
W_{r} denotes the usual r-Wasserstein distance.




Stability under regularity assumptions goes back to an argument of
Ambrosio, reported by
Gigli [11, Proposition 3.3 and Corollary 3.4].
If the optimal map is Lipschitz, the conjugate potential is strongly
convex, and the duality gap controls the squared distance
to the optimal graph. Li and Nochetto [14, Proposition 3.1]
formulate this coercivity for transport plans and use it to prove
stability under perturbations of both marginals. Their
Theorem 3.5 treats arbitrary \mathcal{P}_{2} perturbations when the reference
optimal map is Lipschitz.




For compact targets, Berman [3] proved stability
estimates with dimension-dependent exponents under upper and lower
bounds on a source density over a convex body. Mérigot, Delalande and
Chazal [16, Theorem 3.1] proved the dimension-independent
exponent 2/15 for uniform sources on convex bodies.
Delalande and Mérigot [7, Theorem 4.2]
improved this to
W_{1}^{1/6} for source densities bounded above and away from zero on a
convex body. They also treated unbounded targets under moment
assumptions [7, Corollary 4.4].
Letrouit and Mérigot [13]
used gluing arguments to treat more general sources, including
log-concave densities with full support in \mathds{R}^{d}.
Mérigot [15] proved the sharp W_{1}^{1/4}
estimate for bounded, compactly supported source densities, using
quantitative regularity estimates for convex functions due to Carlier,
Delalande and Mérigot [5].
Ford [10] extended this approach to upper Ahlfors
regular sources
and to stability of plans under changes in both marginals.




The OpenAI preprint [18, Theorem 1.1]
proves the sharp W_{2}^{1/3} estimate for uniform
sources on compact convex bodies in dimensions d\geqslant 2, with both
targets in a fixed compact set.




In this paper, we consider sources given by L^{p} changes of BV reference
densities, with possibly unbounded support. We prove optimal Hölder
exponents for W_{2} stability under a support or higher-moment bound on
one target, with explicit constants. The examples proving optimality use a fixed source
with unbounded, disconnected support for each p.




We regularize the Brenier potential to obtain a Lipschitz gradient and
apply the coercivity argument above. It then remains to estimate the
regularization error. We use the Moreau–Yosida envelope
[17, 1], whose conjugacy identity
\phi_{t}^{*}=\phi^{*}+t\lvert\cdot\rvert^{2}\!/2 makes the first-order changes of
the two potentials cancel after integration against the optimal plan.
A BV integration-by-parts estimate bounds the error by O(t^{2}).
Interpolation extends this estimate to L^{p} changes of density.
Quantitative Fenchel–Young inequalities involving proximal maps have
also been used in optimal transport by Carlier [4].




The constants depend on the dimension through the source parameter
\mathsf{S}_{p} in (2). For log-concave sources, this dependence
is polynomial. For an L^{p} change of Gaussian measure with a fixed
bound on the weight, the factor in the bounded-target estimate is
d^{\alpha_{p}}.




In both estimates, the second target ranges over all of \mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{d}).
For bounded, compactly supported source densities, a one-sided support
condition follows from
Mérigot [15, Theorem 3.4] and the coupling argument in
Lemma 8; see Remark 9.
Our moment estimate assumes a bound of any order q>2 on one target.
The moment estimates of
Delalande–Mérigot [7, Theorem 4.3 and
Corollary 4.4]
assume higher-moment bounds on both targets.




AI use




The use of potential regularization to prove stability arose in a
dialogue between the authors and OpenAI Codex (GPT-6). The AI first
proved a W_{2}^{1/4} stability estimate for compactly supported targets
using heat regularization. The authors then asked it to determine
whether this exponent was optimal, extend the result to L^{p} source
densities and noncompact targets, and compare regularization schemes.
This comparison led to the Moreau–Yosida argument, which improves the
dimension dependence for p<\infty. The AI wrote the entire manuscript,
and the authors checked it.

1.1 Main results




Fix p\in(1,\infty]. We assume that the source and reference measures
satisfy





\displaystyle\rho_{0}
\displaystyle=f_{0}\,\mathrm{d}x\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{d}),
\displaystyle\qquad f_{0}
\displaystyle\in\mathrm{BV}(\mathds{R}^{d}),

(1)



\displaystyle\rho
\displaystyle=h\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{d}),
\displaystyle\qquad h
\displaystyle\in L^{p}(\rho_{0}).



Here h is nonnegative and normalized by \int h\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}=1.




Write





\Lambda=\lVert h\rVert_{L^{p}(\rho_{0})},\qquad B_{0}=|Df_{0}|(\mathds{R}^{d}).



The variation B_{0} includes boundary contributions when f_{0} jumps
across the boundary of its support. For example,
f_{0}=|K|^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{K} gives B_{0}=\operatorname{Per}(K)/|K|.
Let m_{\rho} be the source mean and M_{\rho} the root-mean-square
distance from this mean:





m_{\rho}=\int x\,\mathrm{d}\rho(x),\qquad M_{\rho}=\biggl(\int|x-m_{\rho}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\rho(x)\biggr)^{\!1/2}.



Let p^{\prime}=p/(p-1), with p^{\prime}=1 when p=\infty.
Define





\mathsf{S}_{p}=M_{\rho}B_{0}\Lambda^{p^{\prime}},\qquad\alpha_{p}=\frac{1}{2(p^{\prime}+1)}.

(2)


The quantity \mathsf{S}_{p} is invariant under a common translation or scalar
dilation of the source and reference measures. If \rho itself has a
BV density, we may take \rho_{0}=\rho, h=1 and p=\infty;
then \mathsf{S}_{\infty}=M_{\rho}B_{0} and \alpha_{\infty}=1/4.




Theorem 1 (One bounded target).



Assume (1). Let \mu be supported in a ball of radius
R>0, and let \nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{d}) be arbitrary. Then, with
\delta=W_{2}(\mu,\nu),





\lVert T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\rVert_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leqslant\delta+2\sqrt{2}\,R^{1-\alpha_{p}}(\mathsf{S}_{p}\delta)^{\alpha_{p}}.

(3)


In particular, \alpha_{p}=(p-1)/(4p-2) and \alpha_{\infty}=1/4.





Theorem 2 (One target with a bounded moment).



Assume (1). Let \nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{d}) be arbitrary, and
suppose that, for some q>2 and A>0,





\int|y|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\leqslant A^{q}.



Set





\beta_{p,q}=\frac{\alpha_{p}(q-2)}{q-2\alpha_{p}}=\frac{q-2}{2q(p^{\prime}+1)-2}.

(4)


Then, with \delta=W_{2}(\mu,\nu),





\lVert T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\rVert_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leqslant\delta+10A^{1-\beta_{p,q}}(\mathsf{S}_{p}\delta)^{\beta_{p,q}}.

(5)


For p=\infty, the exponent is (q-2)/(4q-2).





The constants are uniform over \nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{d}), including measures
with infinite q-th moment in Theorem 2.
The linear term \delta accounts for large target distances.
By symmetry, either target may play the role of \mu.




Remark 3 (Compact sources).



Under (1), if \operatorname{supp}\rho\subset\overline{B}(x_{0},L) and
both targets lie in a common ball of radius R, then, with
w=W_{1}(\mu,\nu),





\lVert T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\rVert_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leqslant(2Rw)^{1/2}+2\sqrt{2}\,R^{1-\alpha_{p}}\biggl(\frac{L}{M_{\rho}}\mathsf{S}_{p}w\biggr)^{\!\alpha_{p}}.

(6)


See Remark 10 for the derivation. For bounded, compactly
supported source densities, this recovers the known W_{1}^{1/4} estimate,
whose sharpness in dimensions d\geqslant 2 is due to
Letrouit [12, Theorem 2]; see
Ford [10, Proposition 5.2] for an extension of that construction.





Proposition 4 (Optimality of the exponents).



For every p\in(1,\infty], there is a fixed source on \mathds{R}^{2} satisfying
(1) for which the following hold.


1. 



There are targets \mu_{n}, \nu_{n} in the unit ball with
\delta_{n}=W_{2}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\to 0 such that, for every a>\alpha_{p},





\frac{\lVert T_{\mu_{n}}-T_{\nu_{n}}\rVert_{L^{2}(\rho)}}{\delta_{n}^{a}}\longrightarrow\infty.




2. 



For each q>2, there are targets with both q-moments at most one
and \delta_{n}=W_{2}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\to 0 such that, for every b>\beta_{p,q},





\frac{\lVert T_{\mu_{n}}-T_{\nu_{n}}\rVert_{L^{2}(\rho)}}{\delta_{n}^{b}}\longrightarrow\infty.





For p=\infty, the source itself has a bounded BV density.





We prove Proposition 4 in Section 4.
Thus 1/4 is the optimal W_{2} exponent over the class of BV sources
with finite second moment and bounded targets.

For the Gaussian, B_{0}=\E[|G_{d}|] with G_{d}\sim N(0,I_{d}). The bound B_{0}\leqslant Cd for
isotropic log-concave densities goes back to Eldan and
Klartag [8, Lemma 11], who proved
\int|\nabla f_{0}|\,\mathrm{d}x\leqslant Cd for smooth densities.
Gaussian smoothing, rescaling to isotropic position and lower
semicontinuity extend the bound to BV densities, including boundary
contributions. For a different proof and a
surface-area formulation, see Eskenazis, Giannopoulos and
Tziotziou [9, Theorems 1.3 and 1.4].
In the last line, V\in C^{2}(\mathds{R}^{d})
and 0<\kappa\leqslant K. By the Poincaré inequality,
M_{\rho}^{2}\leqslant d/\kappa, and by integration by parts,
B_{0}^{2}\leqslant\int|\nabla V|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\rho=\int\Delta V\,\mathrm{d}\rho\leqslant Kd.




For targets in a ball of radius R, Theorem 1 becomes





\lVert T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\rVert_{2}\leqslant CR\biggl(\frac{\delta}{R}\biggr)^{\!1/4}\begin{cases}d^{1/4},&\rho=\gamma_{d},\\
d^{3/8},&\rho\text{ isotropic log-concave},\\
d^{1/4}(K/\kappa)^{1/8},&\kappa I\leqslant D^{2}V\leqslant KI.\end{cases}

(7)


We absorbed the linear term using \delta\leqslant 2R.
Under the common moment bound of Theorem 2, the factor
CR(\delta/R)^{1/4} becomes CA(\delta/A)^{\beta_{\infty,q}}.
The three dimension factors become
d^{\beta_{\infty,q}}, d^{3\beta_{\infty,q}/2} and
d^{\beta_{\infty,q}}(K/\kappa)^{\beta_{\infty,q}/2}, respectively.




For \rho=h\gamma_{d} with \lVert h\rVert_{L^{p}(\gamma_{d})}=\Lambda, Hölder’s
inequality and Gaussian moment bounds imply





M_{\rho}\leqslant C_{p}\Lambda^{1/2}\sqrt{d},\qquad\mathsf{S}_{p}\leqslant C_{p}\Lambda^{p^{\prime}+1/2}d.



Thus, for fixed p>1 and both targets in a common ball of radius R,





\lVert T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\rVert_{2}\leqslant C_{p}\Lambda^{\frac{2p^{\prime}+1}{4(p^{\prime}+1)}}d^{\alpha_{p}}R^{1-\alpha_{p}}\delta^{\alpha_{p}}.

(8)


Under a common q-moment bound on the targets, the same computation gives





\lVert T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\rVert_{2}\leqslant C_{p,q}\Lambda^{(p^{\prime}+1/2)\beta_{p,q}}d^{\beta_{p,q}}A^{1-\beta_{p,q}}\delta^{\beta_{p,q}}.



Here \delta\leqslant 2A absorbs the linear term. For the uniform source on
B(0,L), LB_{0}=d and (6) gives
\lVert T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\rVert_{2}\leqslant CR^{3/4}d^{1/4}W_{1}(\mu,\nu)^{1/4}.




For Gaussian sources, the next construction gives a polynomial lower
bound on the dimension dependence in Theorems 1
and 2.




Proposition 5 (Dimension dependence for Gaussian sources).



There are universal c,C>0 such that, in every sufficiently large
dimension d, the source \rho=\gamma_{d}=N(0,I_{d}) admits targets
\mu_{d}, \nu_{d} in the unit ball satisfying





W_{2}(\mu_{d},\nu_{d})\leqslant Cd^{-1/2},\qquad\lVert T_{\mu_{d}}-T_{\nu_{d}}\rVert_{L^{2}(\gamma_{d})}\geqslant c.



Consequently, a uniform estimate \lVert T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\rVert_{2}\leqslant C_{d}W_{2}(\mu,\nu)^{a}
with a>0 requires C_{d}\geqslant c_{a}d^{a/2}.





Proof.



First let d=2n. Choose a binary linear code \mathcal{C}\subset\{0,1\}^{n}
of dimension k=\lfloor n/4\rfloor whose nonzero words have weight
greater than n/8. Such codes exist for large n: for a random linear
map from \{0,1\}^{k} to \{0,1\}^{n}, a union bound gives a failure
probability at most 2^{k-n(1-H_{2}(1/8))}, which tends to zero; here
H_{2} is the binary entropy and 1-H_{2}(1/8)>1/4.




Write the associated sign vectors as
v_{i}=n^{-1/2}((-1)^{c_{j}})_{j=1}^{n}, 1\leqslant i\leqslant N=2^{k}.
They have unit norm, satisfy |v_{i}-v_{j}|^{2}\geqslant 1/2 for i\neq j,
and are permuted transitively by orthogonal coordinate sign changes.
Put





r=\frac{\log N-1}{\log N+1},\qquad a_{i}=(v_{i},0),\qquad b_{i}=(rv_{i},\sqrt{1-r^{2}}\,v_{i}),



and let \mu_{d}, \nu_{d} be uniform on the a_{i}, b_{i}, respectively.
For independent standard Gaussian vectors X,Z\in\mathds{R}^{n}, set
Y=rX+\sqrt{1-r^{2}}Z and I(x)=\arg\max_{i}\langle v_{i},x\rangle.
Ties have probability zero, and transitivity implies
\PP[I(X)=i]=1/N. The gradients of the two maxima of linear functions
are therefore the Brenier maps:





T_{\mu_{d}}(X,Z)=a_{I(X)},\qquad T_{\nu_{d}}(X,Z)=b_{I(Y)}.



Nelson’s Gaussian hypercontractivity theorem
(see [6, Theorem 2.1]) implies, for the cells
A_{i}=\{I=i\},





\PP[I(X)=I(Y)]\leqslant\sum_{i}\gamma_{n}(A_{i})^{2/(1+r)}=N^{-(1-r)/(1+r)}=e^{-1}.



Since |a_{i}-b_{j}|^{2}=2(1-r)+r|v_{i}-v_{j}|^{2}, diagonal matching is optimal
between the two uniform targets. Therefore





W_{2}(\mu_{d},\nu_{d})^{2}=\frac{4}{\log N+1},\qquad\lVert T_{\mu_{d}}-T_{\nu_{d}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\geqslant\frac{r}{2}(1-e^{-1}).



For odd dimensions, add an independent Gaussian coordinate on which
both maps are constant.
∎





These targets have moments of every order bounded by one, so the lower
bound applies to both main theorems. For exponent 1/4, the constant is
bounded below by cd^{1/8} and above by Cd^{1/4}. For the moment
estimate with exponent \beta_{\infty,q}, the bounds are
cd^{\beta_{\infty,q}/2} and Cd^{\beta_{\infty,q}}, respectively.
The optimal power of the dimension between these bounds is open.






2 One bounded target




For a proper lower semicontinuous convex function u, write
u^{*}(y)=\sup_{x}\{\langle x,y\rangle-u(x)\} and
g_{u}(x,y)=u(x)+u^{*}(y)-\langle x,y\rangle for its Fenchel gap.
Let \phi be a Brenier potential from \rho to \mu, and set
\sigma_{*}=(\mathrm{Id},T_{\mu})_{\#}\rho. For \sigma\in\Pi(\rho,\mu), define





\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\sigma)
\displaystyle=\int g_{\phi}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

(9)




\displaystyle=\int\langle x,T_{\mu}(x)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\rho(x)-\int\langle x,y\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(x,y)\geqslant 0.



The second equality follows from Fenchel equality on \sigma_{*}.
Thus \mathcal{G}(\sigma) is the difference between the optimal correlation
and the correlation under \sigma, and \mathcal{G}(\sigma_{*})=0.




When \operatorname{supp}\mu\subset\overline{B}(0,R), we choose \phi globally convex
and R-Lipschitz by taking the supremum of its supporting affine
functions with slopes in this ball. Define its Moreau–Yosida
regularization and the corresponding gap by





\phi_{t}(x)=\inf_{z}{\biggl\{\phi(z)+\frac{|x-z|^{2}}{2t}\biggr\}},\qquad\mathcal{G}_{t}(\sigma)=\int g_{\phi_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma,\quad t>0.

(10)


The envelope is convex and continuously differentiable, and





\phi_{t}^{*}(y)=\phi^{*}(y)+\frac{t}{2}|y|^{2}.

(11)


For the properties of the envelope, see
Moreau [17] and
Bauschke–Combettes [1, Chapter 12].
The proof has three steps: estimate the regularization error, use
coercivity of the regularized gap, and bound the original gap by the
target distance through a suitable coupling.




Lemma 6 (Stability of the duality gap).



Assume (1), and set H_{p}=\Lambda B_{0}^{1/p^{\prime}}.
For any convex R-Lipschitz function \phi, define almost everywhere





a_{t}=\phi_{t}-\phi+\frac{t}{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2},\qquad A_{t}=\int a_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\rho.

(12)


Then





A_{t}\leqslant\frac{H_{p}}{2\,3^{1/p^{\prime}}}R^{2+1/p^{\prime}}t^{1+1/p^{\prime}}.

(13)


If \nabla\phi pushes \rho to \mu, then for every
\sigma\in\Pi(\rho,\mu),





0\leqslant\mathcal{G}_{t}(\sigma)-\mathcal{G}(\sigma)=\mathcal{G}_{t}(\sigma_{*})=A_{t}.

(14)






Proof.



By (11) and Fenchel equality,
a_{t}(x)=g_{\phi_{t}}(x,\nabla\phi(x))\geqslant 0 almost everywhere.
Since \phi_{t}\leqslant\phi and |\nabla\phi|\leqslant R, we also have
0\leqslant a_{t}\leqslant tR^{2}/2. If (\nabla\phi)_{\#}\rho=\mu, the
common marginals and (11) give





\mathcal{G}_{t}(\sigma)-\mathcal{G}(\sigma)=\int(\phi_{t}-\phi)\,\mathrm{d}\rho+\frac{t}{2}\int|y|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=A_{t}.



Evaluating at \sigma_{*} proves (14).
All integrals are finite: the potentials have linear growth and
\phi^{*} is integrable against \mu by Fenchel equality on \sigma_{*}.




We next prove the second-order estimate





\int a_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}\leqslant\frac{R^{3}B_{0}}{6}t^{2}.

(15)


First take \rho=\rho_{0} and suppose that \phi is smooth.
The Moreau envelope satisfies
the Hamilton–Jacobi equation





\partial_{s}\phi_{s}+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\phi_{s}|^{2}=0,\qquad\phi_{0}=\phi.



Differentiating in time, we find A_{0}=A^{\prime}_{0}=0 and





A^{\prime\prime}_{s}=\int\langle\nabla\phi_{s},D^{2}\phi_{s}\nabla\phi_{s}\rangle\,f_{0}\,\mathrm{d}x.



Since D^{2}\phi_{s}\geqslant 0, we have





\operatorname{div}(|\nabla\phi_{s}|^{2}\nabla\phi_{s})\geqslant 3\langle\nabla\phi_{s},D^{2}\phi_{s}\nabla\phi_{s}\rangle.



Integration by parts and the bound |\nabla\phi_{s}|\leqslant R imply





0\leqslant A^{\prime\prime}_{s}\leqslant-\frac{1}{3}\int|\nabla\phi_{s}|^{2}\nabla\phi_{s}\cdot\,\mathrm{d}Df_{0}\leqslant\frac{R^{3}B_{0}}{3}.

(16)


Integrating twice in time, we obtain





A_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)A^{\prime\prime}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s\leqslant\frac{R^{3}B_{0}}{6}t^{2}.



In the integration by parts, cutoffs on balls of radius a
produce an error bounded by CR^{3}/a, which tends to zero.
For a general convex R-Lipschitz \phi, apply the estimate to smooth
convolutions. As the mollification parameter vanishes, the potentials
and their envelopes converge uniformly, while the gradients of the initial
potentials converge almost everywhere to \nabla\phi and remain
bounded by R. Dominated convergence proves (15) for
the original convex Lipschitz potential.




For \rho=h\rho_{0} and 1<p<\infty, Hölder’s inequality gives





A_{t}=\int a_{t}h\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}\leqslant\Lambda\biggl(\int a_{t}^{p^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}\biggr)^{\!1/p^{\prime}}.



Using 0\leqslant a_{t}\leqslant tR^{2}/2 and (15), we have





\int a_{t}^{p^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}\leqslant\biggl(\frac{tR^{2}}{2}\biggr)^{\!p^{\prime}-1}\int a_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}\leqslant\biggl(\frac{tR^{2}}{2}\biggr)^{\!p^{\prime}-1}\frac{R^{3}B_{0}t^{2}}{6}.



Taking the 1/p^{\prime} power and using (p^{\prime}-1)/p^{\prime}=1/p, we conclude that





A_{t}\leqslant\Lambda\biggl(\frac{tR^{2}}{2}\biggr)^{\!1/p}\biggl(\frac{R^{3}B_{0}t^{2}}{6}\biggr)^{\!1/p^{\prime}}=\frac{H_{p}}{2\,3^{1/p^{\prime}}}R^{2+1/p^{\prime}}t^{1+1/p^{\prime}}.



For p=\infty, the same bound follows directly from
A_{t}\leqslant\Lambda\int a_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{0} and (15), with p^{\prime}=1.
∎





Lemma 7 (Coercivity of the regularized gap).



For t>0, the regularized potential satisfies





\lVert\nabla\phi_{t}\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant R,\qquad\operatorname{Lip}(\nabla\phi_{t})\leqslant\frac{1}{t}=:L_{t}.

(17)


For every x,y\in\mathds{R}^{d},





g_{\phi_{t}}(x,y)\geqslant\frac{t}{2}|y-\nabla\phi_{t}(x)|^{2}.

(18)


Integrating against any \sigma\in\Pi(\rho,\mu), we obtain





\int|y-\nabla\phi_{t}(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(x,y)\leqslant\frac{2}{t}\mathcal{G}_{t}(\sigma).

(19)






Proof.



Let z_{t}(x) be the unique minimizer in (10).
The optimality condition gives





\nabla\phi_{t}(x)=\frac{x-z_{t}(x)}{t}\in\partial\phi(z_{t}(x)),



so its norm is at most R. Monotonicity of \partial\phi implies





t|\nabla\phi_{t}(x)-\nabla\phi_{t}(x^{\prime})|^{2}\leqslant\langle\nabla\phi_{t}(x)-\nabla\phi_{t}(x^{\prime}),x-x^{\prime}\rangle,



which proves the Lipschitz bound. In the definition of \phi_{t}^{*}(y),
use the inequality





\phi_{t}(z)\leqslant\phi_{t}(x)+\langle\nabla\phi_{t}(x),z-x\rangle+\frac{1}{2t}|z-x|^{2}



at z=x+t(y-\nabla\phi_{t}(x)) to obtain (18).
Integration proves (19). This is the
smoothness–strong-convexity argument used by Li and
Nochetto [14, Lemma 2.2 and Proposition 3.1].
For proximal versions of the Fenchel–Young inequality with a remainder,
see Carlier [4].
∎





Lemma 8 (The target distance controls the gap).



Let \mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{d}) and \delta=W_{2}(\mu,\nu).
There exist jointly distributed (X_{\nu},Y_{\nu},Y_{\mu}) with
X_{\nu}\sim\rho, Y_{\nu}=T_{\nu}(X_{\nu}) and (Y_{\mu},Y_{\nu}) an optimal
coupling of the targets, such that \sigma=\operatorname{Law}(X_{\nu},Y_{\mu})
satisfies





\mathcal{G}(\sigma)\leqslant 2M_{\rho}\delta.

(20)


Here \mathcal{G} is defined by (9).





Proof.



Glue a W_{2}-optimal target coupling to the two optimal graph plans to
obtain (X_{\mu},X_{\nu},Y_{\mu},Y_{\nu}) with X_{\eta}\sim\rho and
Y_{\eta}=T_{\eta}(X_{\eta}) for \eta\in\{\mu,\nu\}.
Optimality of T_{\nu} gives
\E[\langle X_{\mu},Y_{\nu}\rangle]\leqslant\E[\langle X_{\nu},Y_{\nu}\rangle]. Hence





\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\sigma)
\displaystyle=\E[\langle X_{\mu}-X_{\nu},Y_{\mu}\rangle]





\displaystyle\leqslant\E[\langle X_{\mu}-X_{\nu},Y_{\mu}-Y_{\nu}\rangle]





\displaystyle\leqslant\lVert X_{\mu}-X_{\nu}\rVert_{2}\lVert Y_{\mu}-Y_{\nu}\rVert_{2}\leqslant 2M_{\rho}\delta.



The last bound uses
\lVert X_{\mu}-X_{\nu}\rVert_{2}\leqslant\lVert X_{\mu}-m_{\rho}\rVert_{2}+\lVert X_{\nu}-m_{\rho}\rVert_{2}.
∎





Remark 9 (A one-sided support condition).



For bounded, compactly supported source densities and bounded \mu,
Mérigot’s coercivity estimate [15, Theorem 3.4]
combined with Lemma 8 gives
\lVert T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\rVert_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leqslant\delta+C\delta^{1/4} for arbitrary
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{d}), where \delta=W_{2}(\mu,\nu).





Proof of Theorem 1.



Translate both targets to arrange \operatorname{supp}\mu\subset\overline{B}(0,R).
Choose the coupling \sigma from Lemma 8.
Applying Lemma 7 to \sigma and \sigma_{*} gives





\displaystyle\int|T_{\mu}(x)-y|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(x,y)
\displaystyle\leqslant 4L_{t}\bigl(\mathcal{G}_{t}(\sigma_{*})+\mathcal{G}_{t}(\sigma)\bigr)





\displaystyle\leqslant 4L_{t}\mathcal{G}(\sigma)+8L_{t}A_{t},

(21)


where the second inequality is Lemma 6.
Since Y_{\nu}=T_{\nu}(X_{\nu}) and
\lVert Y_{\mu}-Y_{\nu}\rVert_{2}=\delta, the L^{2} triangle inequality implies,
with D=\lVert T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\rVert_{L^{2}(\rho)},





(D-\delta)_{+}^{2}\leqslant\frac{8M_{\rho}\delta}{t}+\frac{4}{3^{1/p^{\prime}}}H_{p}R^{2+1/p^{\prime}}t^{1/p^{\prime}}.

(22)


For \delta>0, choose





s=\biggl(\frac{M_{\rho}\delta}{H_{p}R^{2+1/p^{\prime}}}\biggr)^{\!p^{\prime}/(p^{\prime}+1)},\qquad t=2s.



Since (2/3)^{1/p^{\prime}}\leqslant 1, the right-hand side of
(22) is at most 8M_{\rho}\delta/s.
Using M_{\rho}H_{p}^{p^{\prime}}=\mathsf{S}_{p} and taking square roots gives
(3). When \delta=0, the two maps agree by uniqueness.
∎





Remark 10 (W_{1} stability for compact sources).



To prove (6), write w=W_{1}(\mu,\nu) and use the gluing
construction of Lemma 8 with a W_{1}-optimal target
coupling. Since |X_{\mu}-X_{\nu}|\leqslant 2L, the same gap estimate gives





\mathcal{G}(\sigma)\leqslant 2L\E[|Y_{\mu}-Y_{\nu}|]=2Lw.



The common target ball also gives
\lVert Y_{\mu}-Y_{\nu}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leqslant 2R\E[|Y_{\mu}-Y_{\nu}|]=2Rw.
Thus the proof of Theorem 1 applies with
M_{\rho}\delta replaced by Lw in (22) and the initial
term \delta replaced by (2Rw)^{1/2}. Balancing the two terms in t
gives (6).




Every bounded probability density f supported in B(x_{0},L) satisfies
(1) with p=\infty: take \rho_{0} uniform on this ball
and h=|B(x_{0},L)|f. Thus (6) applies to every such density
with exponent 1/4.





Remark 11 (Heat regularization).



The proof also applies to the heat regularization
P_{t}\phi, where P_{t}=e^{t\Delta}. Convexity gives P_{t}\phi\geqslant\phi
and (P_{t}\phi)^{*}\leqslant\phi^{*}, so the change in the duality gap is
bounded by \int(P_{t}\phi-\phi)\,\mathrm{d}\rho.
Set \kappa_{d}=\sqrt{2}\,\E[|G_{d}|] for G_{d}\sim N(0,I_{d}).
The pointwise bound 0\leqslant P_{t}\phi-\phi\leqslant R\kappa_{d}\sqrt{t}
and the BV estimate
\int(P_{t}\phi-\phi)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}\leqslant RB_{0}t, combined with
Hölder’s inequality, imply





\int(P_{t}\phi-\phi)\,\mathrm{d}\rho\leqslant R\Lambda\kappa_{d}^{1/p}B_{0}^{1/p^{\prime}}t^{(1+1/p^{\prime})/2}.



The regularized gradient satisfies





\operatorname{Lip}(\nabla P_{t}\phi)\leqslant\frac{R}{\sqrt{\pi t}}.



The same coercivity and balancing argument proves a bound of the form
(3), with a universal numerical constant and source
parameter \mathsf{S}_{p}\kappa_{d}^{p^{\prime}-1}. The Hölder exponent is \alpha_{p}.
Since \kappa_{d} is of order \sqrt{d}, the extra factor contributes to
the dimension dependence when 1<p<\infty and equals one when
p=\infty.






3 One target with a bounded moment




For \mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{d}), define





\tau_{\mu}(R)=\biggl(\int_{|y|>R}|y|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\biggr)^{\!1/2}.



We extend the bounded-target estimate by adding an error term involving
the tail of \mu.




Proposition 12 (Stability with a target-tail error).



Assume (1), and let \mu, \nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{d}).
For every R>0 such that \mu(\overline{B}(0,R))>0, with
\delta=W_{2}(\mu,\nu), one has





\lVert T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\rVert_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leqslant\delta+2\sqrt{2}\,R^{1-\alpha_{p}}(\mathsf{S}_{p}\delta)^{\alpha_{p}}+3\sqrt{2}\,\tau_{\mu}(R).

(23)






Proof.



The case \delta=0 follows from uniqueness, so assume \delta>0.
Let \phi be a proper lower semicontinuous Brenier potential from
\rho to \mu. Define the slope truncation





\phi_{R}(x)=(\phi^{*}+\iota_{\overline{B}(0,R)})^{*}(x)=\sup_{|y|\leqslant R}{\{\langle x,y\rangle-\phi^{*}(y)\}},

(24)


where \iota_{E} is zero on E and +\infty outside E.
Since \phi^{*} is finite \mu-almost everywhere and
\mu(\overline{B}(0,R))>0, the supremum includes a finite affine function.
Lower semicontinuity on the compact ball bounds \phi^{*} from below
there. Hence \phi_{R} is finite, convex and R-Lipschitz, with





\phi_{R}\leqslant\phi,\qquad\phi_{R}^{*}=\phi^{*}+\iota_{\overline{B}(0,R)}.

(25)


This is the Pasch–Hausdorff Lipschitz regularization,





\phi_{R}(x)=\inf_{z}{\{\phi(z)+R|x-z|\}};



see
Bauschke and Combettes [1, Proposition 12.17]
for the envelope properties and Beck and
Teboulle [2, Lemma 2.1] for its dual representation.




Write T=\nabla\phi, T_{R}=\nabla\phi_{R} and let
\phi_{R,t} be the Moreau–Yosida regularization of \phi_{R}.
Set





a_{R,t}=\phi_{R,t}-\phi_{R}+\frac{t}{2}|T_{R}|^{2},\qquad A_{R,t}=\int a_{R,t}\,\mathrm{d}\rho.



Applying Lemma 6 to \phi_{R}, we obtain





A_{R,t}\leqslant\frac{H_{p}}{2\,3^{1/p^{\prime}}}R^{2+1/p^{\prime}}t^{1+1/p^{\prime}}.

(26)


Also |T_{R}|, |\nabla\phi_{R,t}|\leqslant R. Wherever |T(x)|\leqslant R,
Fenchel equality gives \phi_{R}(x)=\phi(x), hence T_{R}(x)=T(x)
almost everywhere on that set. Consequently,





0\leqslant\int\bigl(|T|^{2}-|T_{R}|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\rho\leqslant\tau_{\mu}(R)^{2}.

(27)





For |y|\leqslant R, the conjugacy identities
(25) and (11) imply





g_{\phi_{R,t}}(x,y)\leqslant g_{\phi}(x,y)+a_{R,t}(x)+\frac{t}{2}\bigl(|y|^{2}-|T_{R}(x)|^{2}\bigr).

(28)


For any \sigma\in\Pi(\rho,\mu), the function
|y|^{2}-|T_{R}(x)|^{2} is nonnegative on \{|y|>R\}.
Thus its integral over \{|y|\leqslant R\} is bounded
above by its integral over all pairs, which is at most
\tau_{\mu}(R)^{2} by (27).
Since g_{\phi} and a_{R,t} are nonnegative, coercivity gives





\int_{|y|\leqslant R}|\nabla\phi_{R,t}(x)-y|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\leqslant\frac{2}{t}\bigl(\mathcal{G}(\sigma)+A_{R,t}\bigr)+\tau_{\mu}(R)^{2}.



On \{|y|>R\}, use
|\nabla\phi_{R,t}(x)-y|^{2}\leqslant 4|y|^{2} instead. We obtain





\int|\nabla\phi_{R,t}(x)-y|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\leqslant\frac{2}{t}\bigl(\mathcal{G}(\sigma)+A_{R,t}\bigr)+5\tau_{\mu}(R)^{2}.

(29)


Here \mathcal{G} is the gap for the original potential \phi as in
(9). Its potential terms are integrable: affine lower
bounds for \phi and \phi^{*} control their negative parts, while
Fenchel equality on the optimal graph and the second moments control
their positive parts.




On the optimal graph, T_{R}=T when |T|\leqslant R, so the moment correction
vanishes on this set:





\int|\nabla\phi_{R,t}-T|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\rho\leqslant\frac{2}{t}A_{R,t}+4\tau_{\mu}(R)^{2}.



Combining this with (29) gives





\int|T(x)-y|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\leqslant\frac{4}{t}\mathcal{G}(\sigma)+\frac{8}{t}A_{R,t}+18\tau_{\mu}(R)^{2}.



Choose the coupling from Lemma 8 and use
(26). We bound the first two terms as
in (22). The same choice of t, followed by
\sqrt{a+b}\leqslant\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b} and the target-coupling triangle
inequality, proves (23).
∎





Remark 13.



The estimate (28) bounds the regularized gap by the
original Fenchel gap, the regularization error and a moment correction.
Lemma 8 therefore applies with the original targets
\mu and \nu, while
(27) bounds the correction by the tail of \mu.
Delalande–Mérigot [7, Theorem 4.3 and
Corollary 4.4] treat unbounded targets
using moment estimates and local control of potentials.





Proof of Theorem 2.



Write \alpha=\alpha_{p}, \beta=\beta_{p,q} and
z=\mathsf{S}_{p}\delta/A. The moment bound gives





\tau_{\mu}(R)\leqslant A^{q/2}R^{-(q-2)/2}.

(30)


If 0<z\leqslant 1, choose





R=2Az^{-1/[q(p^{\prime}+1)-1]}.



Then R\geqslant 2A, so \mu(\overline{B}(0,R))>0.
The two error terms in Proposition 12 have the
same power z^{\beta}, because





\alpha-\frac{1-\alpha}{q(p^{\prime}+1)-1}=\frac{q-2}{2[q(p^{\prime}+1)-1]}=\beta.



Their sum is bounded by





\bigl(2\sqrt{2}\,2^{1-\alpha}+3\sqrt{2}\,2^{-(q-2)/2}\bigr)Az^{\beta}\leqslant 7\sqrt{2}\,Az^{\beta}<10Az^{\beta}.



If z\geqslant 1, a W_{2}-optimal target coupling and Minkowski’s inequality give





\lVert T_{\nu}\rVert_{L^{2}(\rho)}=\biggl(\int|y|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)\biggr)^{\!1/2}\leqslant A+\delta.



Hence \lVert T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\rVert_{2}\leqslant 2A+\delta\leqslant\delta+10Az^{\beta}.
The case \delta=0 follows from uniqueness.
∎






4 Optimality of the exponents




Proof of Proposition 4.



Fix p\in(1,\infty] and its conjugate p^{\prime}. We construct one source
whose mass is concentrated on pairs of thin rectangles escaping to
infinity. For integers n\geqslant N, with N sufficiently large, set





\begin{gathered}m_{n}=e^{-n^{2}},\qquad b_{n}=n^{2(p^{\prime}-1)}m_{n}^{p^{\prime}},\qquad\ell_{n}=n^{2}b_{n},\\
L_{n}=\frac{1}{n\sqrt{m_{n}}},\qquad c_{n}=1-2^{-n}.\end{gathered}

(31)


Let u_{n}^{\pm} be the uniform probability measures on the rectangles





Q_{n}^{\pm}=\biggl[c_{n}\pm\ell_{n}-\frac{\ell_{n}}{4},c_{n}\pm\ell_{n}+\frac{\ell_{n}}{4}\biggr]\times[\mp L_{n}-1,\mp L_{n}+1],



and let u_{0} be uniform on [-2,-1]\times[-1,1]. Define





\displaystyle\rho
\displaystyle=\biggl(1-\sum_{n\geqslant N}m_{n}\biggr)u_{0}+\sum_{n\geqslant N}\frac{m_{n}}{2}(u_{n}^{+}+u_{n}^{-}),




\displaystyle\rho_{0}
\displaystyle=\biggl(1-\sum_{n\geqslant N}b_{n}\biggr)u_{0}+\sum_{n\geqslant N}\frac{b_{n}}{2}(u_{n}^{+}+u_{n}^{-}).



These are probabilities on pairwise disjoint rectangles. Since
b_{n}\leqslant m_{n} and m_{n}L_{n}^{2}=n^{-2}, both measures have finite second
moments. Each Q_{n}^{\pm} has area \ell_{n} and perimeter 4+\ell_{n},
so the density f_{0} of \rho_{0} satisfies





|Df_{0}|(\mathds{R}^{2})\leqslant C+\sum_{n\geqslant N}b_{n}\frac{4+\ell_{n}}{\ell_{n}}<\infty.



For p<\infty, the density h=\,\mathrm{d}\rho/\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{0} obeys





\int h^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}=C_{p}+\sum_{n\geqslant N}\frac{m_{n}^{p}}{b_{n}^{p-1}}=C_{p}+\sum_{n\geqslant N}\frac{1}{n^{2}}<\infty.



For p=\infty, b_{n}=m_{n}, so \rho=\rho_{0} and h=1.
The density is then bounded, since its value on Q_{n}^{\pm} is
m_{n}/(2\ell_{n})=1/(2n^{2}).




Put s_{n}=\sum_{j>n}m_{j} and r_{n}=m_{n}/2+s_{n}, so that
r_{n}\sim m_{n}/2 and s_{n}<m_{n}/4. The half-space \{x_{1}>c_{n}\}
contains Q_{n}^{+} and all rectangles with index j>n, hence has
\rho-mass r_{n}. Set





\eta_{n}=\frac{4\ell_{n}}{L_{n}}=4n^{2p^{\prime}+1}m_{n}^{p^{\prime}+1/2},\qquad v_{n}=\frac{e_{1}+\eta_{n}e_{2}}{\sqrt{1+\eta_{n}^{2}}}.



Under the projection F_{n}(x)=x_{1}+\eta_{n}x_{2}, the two rectangles
exchange order:





F_{n}(Q_{n}^{+})\subset\biggl[c_{n}-\frac{7}{2}\ell_{n},c_{n}-\frac{5}{2}\ell_{n}\biggr],\qquad F_{n}(Q_{n}^{-})\subset\biggl[c_{n}+\frac{5}{2}\ell_{n},c_{n}+\frac{7}{2}\ell_{n}\biggr].



Their centers project to c_{n}\mp 3\ell_{n} and their projected
half-width is at most \ell_{n}/4+\eta_{n}\leqslant\ell_{n}/2.
Every rectangle with index j<n projects below
c_{n}-4\ell_{n}. For N large, the right edges of the rectangles
increase with their index and L_{n-1}+1\leqslant L_{n}, so their projections
are at most





c_{n-1}+\frac{5}{4}\ell_{n-1}+4\ell_{n}<c_{n}-4\ell_{n}.



The last inequality follows from \ell_{n}\leqslant n^{2}e^{-n^{2}} and
c_{n}-c_{n-1}=2^{-n}. The base rectangle satisfies the same bound
directly.




Choose t_{n} so that \rho(F_{n}>t_{n})=r_{n}; the projected distribution
has no atoms. The two current rectangles lie above all earlier ones
and have mass m_{n}>r_{n}. Since the later rectangles have total mass
s_{n}, the level set must take at least r_{n}-s_{n}=m_{n}/2 from the current
pair, starting with Q_{n}^{-}. It therefore contains all of Q_{n}^{-} and
at most mass s_{n} from Q_{n}^{+}.




For any amplitude a_{n}>0, consider the two-point targets





\mu_{n}=(1-r_{n})\delta_{0}+r_{n}\delta_{a_{n}e_{1}},\qquad\nu_{n}=(1-r_{n})\delta_{0}+r_{n}\delta_{a_{n}v_{n}}.



Their Brenier maps are





T_{\mu_{n}}=a_{n}e_{1}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}>c_{n}\}},\qquad T_{\nu_{n}}=a_{n}v_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{F_{n}>t_{n}\}},



as gradients of scaled positive parts of affine functions.
On mass at least m_{n}-s_{n}, exactly one of these maps vanishes.
Matching corresponding target atoms, we obtain





\displaystyle\lVert T_{\mu_{n}}-T_{\nu_{n}}\rVert_{2}
\displaystyle\geqslant a_{n}\sqrt{m_{n}-s_{n}},

(32)



\displaystyle\delta_{n}
\displaystyle\leqslant a_{n}\sqrt{r_{n}}\,|e_{1}-v_{n}|\leqslant Ca_{n}n^{2p^{\prime}+1}m_{n}^{p^{\prime}+1}.



Taking a_{n}=1 gives targets in the unit ball. Since m_{n}=e^{-n^{2}},
(32) rules out every exponent larger than
1/[2(p^{\prime}+1)]=\alpha_{p}.




For q>2, take a_{n}=r_{n}^{-1/q}. Both targets then
have q-moment exactly one, while





\lVert T_{\mu_{n}}-T_{\nu_{n}}\rVert_{2}\geqslant cm_{n}^{1/2-1/q},\qquad\delta_{n}\leqslant Cn^{2p^{\prime}+1}m_{n}^{p^{\prime}+1-1/q}.



Every exponent larger than
(1/2-1/q)/(p^{\prime}+1-1/q)=\beta_{p,q} is therefore impossible.
∎
