Sharp conditioning and quantitative stability of optimal transport



Yuanlong Ruan

Address: Beihang University, Beijing, China


Date: October 6, 2026



Abstract.
    

When a feasible plan is obtained whose quadratic transport cost is known to be close to the optimal cost, we try to determine how close the feasible plan is to the true optimal map under mild density and moment conditions. The source and target may be unbounded or have non-compact supports. We show that for a source with density in L^{p} and an n-th moment, let
s=1-1/p and \eta=sn/[n+(d-1)s].
Under the target constraint
\int|y|^{m}[\log(e+|y|)]^{\beta}\,d\nu\leqslant 1,
the worst-case class-uniform conditioning has a sharp modulus comparable to
    





t^{\eta(m-2)/[m(1+\eta)-\eta]}[\log(e/t)]^{-\beta(2+\eta)/[m(1+\eta)-\eta]},


    

whenever t>0 is small. This holds for m\geqslant 2 and \beta\geqslant 0. When m=2, every \beta>0 gives a sharp logarithmic modulus, whereas the m=2,\,\beta=0 extreme has no
vanishing modulus. The matching lower bound is verified for both the squared map error and barycentric projection error. The sharp map error bounds allow us to directly derive quantitative stabilities of common source Brenier maps without passing through potential estimates, thereby avoiding loss of information. These stabilities strictly improve the corresponding results of Delalande-Merigot [13] and Letrouit-Mérigot [19] under identical or weaker settings, particularly no convexity of the source support or bounds of the source density are assumed.
  




Key words and phrases: optimal transport, quantitative stability, power-logarithm conditioning, sharp error bound, non-compact support

1. Introduction and main results




Quantitative stability of optimal transport has attracted a substantial effort
in the literature with seminal works
[6, 22, 13, 10, 23]
and in Wasserstein barycenter stability
[9, 11]. The settings are
mostly for compactly supported source, convex source support or a combination
of the two. Unbounded source support is considered recently, e.g.,
[20], which also extends the strong convexity theory of
[13] to more general domains. Sources are treated
through variance inequalities and gluing. Extension to general cost
[24] is also studied. If more regularities are
assumed, stronger stabilities are anticipated. For example
[18] assumes strong c-concavity with general costs,
[4] treats smooth map stability and statistical
consequences, [8] proves Lipschitz stability for
nondegenerate Hölder densities on smooth uniformly convex domains, to name
a few.




Our focus in this article is to provide a matched conditioning law under
simultaneous density and moment constraints and its power-logarithm case,
including rough and singlar targets. It is designed to answer the question: if
an approximate feasible plan is obtained whose transport cost is known to be
close to the ground true within a given tolerance and an approximate optimal
map is constructed, then what we can say about how close the constructed map
is to the true optimal map? The answer to this question will have its own
numerical benefits, but it will also allow us to treat the quantitative
stability of optimal transport maps without touching the potentials. As
implied by the recent work [23], we believe going from
potential to map could cause loss of information if the goal is stability of
Brenier map itself.




1.1. Problem setting




Let \rho, \nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right). When
\rho has a density, write T_{\nu} for the unqiue Brenier map for the
quadratic optimal transport. For a feasible plan \sigma\in\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right), let




(1.1)

\mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)=\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-T_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|^{2}d\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right),






(1.2)

\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-y}}\right|^{2}d\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}W_{2}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right).



The first quantity \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:Eintro}}}\right), the squared map
error, measures the error of the plan \sigma relative to the map T_{\nu}
at the same source point. The second quantity \mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right) is the objective/cost gap, the difference between
the cost of an approximate plan \sigma and the optimal cost \frac{1}{2}W_{2}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right).




If \sigma=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\operatorname{Id},S}}\right)_{\#}\rho, not necessarily in
\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right), then




(1.3)

\mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)=\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|S\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)-T_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|^{2}d\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|S-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{L^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)}^{2}.



A general plan \sigma may split mass. Barycentric projection
[3, Definition 5.4.2][25] is
the usual way to reconstruct an approximate map. Write \sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dx,dy}}\right)=\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dx}}\right)\sigma_{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dy}}\right), where
\sigma_{x} is the conditional kernel. The barycentric projection is defined
for \rho-a.e. x,





\bar{T}_{\sigma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\int yd\sigma_{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y}}\right).



If we write





V_{\sigma}=\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-\bar{T}_{\sigma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|^{2}d\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right),



then




(1.4)

\mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\bar{T}_{\sigma}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{L^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)}^{2}+V_{\sigma}.



The cross term vanishes because \int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y-\bar{T}_{\sigma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right)d\sigma_{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y}}\right)=0. The barycentric map
\bar{T}_{\sigma} needs not push \rho to \nu.




We ask how accurately a small objective gap determines the optimal map,
uniformly over a specified class of marginals. This is a quantitative
stability question in practice: does a nearly optimal feasible plan
approximate the optimal map, and at what rate?




There are two aspects of this question. The first is conditioning
which views the question as the conversion of objective gap into squared map
error, i.e. a bound of the form





\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)\leqslant t\implies\mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)\leqslant\omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right),



where \omega is a conditioning modulus. Its size measures how much map error
a given objective tolerance permits under required assumptions. It provides a
sufficient upper error modulus. For a fixed class \mathcal{C} of probability
marginals, define




(1.5)

\Omega_{\mathcal{C}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right):\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right)\in\mathcal{C},\text{ }\sigma\in\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right),\text{ }\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)\leqslant t}}\right\}.



This is the smallest class-uniform conditioning modulus. An upper bound
\Omega_{\mathcal{C}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\leqslant C\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
answers how small a cost tolerance is sufficient for desired solution
accuracy. A matching lower bound \Omega_{\mathcal{C}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\geqslant c\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right) answers whether a uniformly better
conversion can follow from the same requirement. Together they describe the
intrinsic worst-case information provided by the cost gap. This is a
class-uniform stability, \Omega_{\mathcal{C}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\rightarrow 0 as t\rightarrow 0. By \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:variance-decomposition}}}\right), it also justifies the barycentric reconstruction.




The other aspect is data perturbation: how does the optimal map change when a
marginal changes? The two aspects are closedly related but not the same.
Uniform stability implies indivisual stability, but the converse does not
hold. Failure of uniform stability does not rule out stability for a fixed
pair of marginals.





1.2. Main results and organization




The structural distribution-adaptive estimate. Under the
Brenier setting of Assumption 1, Section section 5
proves the structural estimate for an arbitrary plan \sigma\in\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right),




(1.6)

\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\sigma)\leqslant\inf_{b\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,R,h>0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{4R\frac{\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(\sigma)}{h}+18R^{2}\mathfrak{A}_{\rho}(h)+8\tau_{2,\nu,b}(R)}}\right\}.



Here \mathfrak{A}_{\rho} measures squared subgradient oscillation of convex
1-Lipschitz functions and \tau_{2,\nu,b} is the ordinary squared target
tail. The information of the source measure enters only \mathfrak{A}_{\rho}
and is processed through the elegent geometric estimates from
[12, 9]. The estimate is also
inspired by the recent work [23]. One advantage of the
structural estimate is it allows us to treat source and target separately,
giving distribution-adaptive error bound. Moreover, since we directly addresss
the stability of the maps, more information is retained. Definitions and
concrete controls appear in Sections section 3 -
section 6.




Source estimates and power laws. Under appropriate source density
conditions, \rho=fdx, f\in L^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right),
1<p\leqslant\infty and s=1-1/p, Theorem Theorem 2 and
Theorem Theorem 3 combine subgradient oscillation estimates
with an m-th target moment, m>2 to build power laws for \mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\sigma). For bounded densities and compact source and target,
Corollary Corollary 1 gives





\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\sigma)\leqslant\operatorname*{Const}\cdot\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(\sigma)^{s/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+s}}\right)},\text{ }0<s\leqslant 1.



When p=\infty, s=1, this is the squared map error estimate of Mérigot
[23, Theorem 3.4 (3.15)], for an arbitrary feasible plan
with the same marginals as the reference optimal map. The correlation gap
there equals \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:Dintro}}}\right). The constructions in
Section section 9 confirms that a matching class-uniform modulus is
not available in the extreme cases, including the unbounded density and m=2
cases. For a prescribed L^{1} density the source profile still vanishes.




The power-logarithm conditioning law. The pure power law is
established in Theorem Theorem 3 with a matching lower
bound verified in Theorem Theorem 6, and consolidated in
Theorem Theorem 7 when m\geqslant 2, \beta=0. With





\eta=\frac{sn}{n+(d-1)s},\text{ }H=m(1+\eta)-\eta,



the pure power law is, for small t>0,





\Omega_{\mathcal{C}_{m,0}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\simeq t^{\eta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-1}}\right)/H},



for the fixed class \mathcal{C}_{m,0} defined in Section section 10. Theorem Theorem 7 in Section section 10 extends it to the
logarithmically weighted target moment class \mathcal{C}_{m,\beta}, giving
the power-logarithm law





\Omega_{\mathcal{C}_{m,\beta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\simeq t^{\eta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-1}}\right)/H}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e/t}}\right)}}\right]^{-\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2+\eta}}\right)/H}.






Brenier map stabilities with common source. Section section 11
introduces a repairing process that constructs an optimal plan through a
coupling of two targets. The objective gap of the repaired plan is then
controlled by centered source moments or integrated source tails.




For compact source and target, Theorem Theorem 8 gives





\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leqslant\operatorname*{Const}\cdot W_{1}(\mu,\nu)^{s/[2(1+s)]}.



At p=\infty, s=1, this recovers Mérigot’s 1/4 power [23, Theorem
3.1]. It is also the common source specialization of
[17, Theorem 4.6]. Here the error is an L^{2} norm,
not its square, and the input is the distance between two targets, not a
feasible plan’s cost gap. The passage between these problems requires the
repairing argument in the proof of Theorem Theorem 8, not just
taking a square root.




Theorem Theorem 11 gives the corresponding map bound for
unbounded non-compact sources and targets under its stated moment assumptions,





\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leqslant\operatorname*{Const}\cdot W_{1}(\mu,\nu)^{\alpha\Theta_{\eta,m}/2}.



And in Section subsection 12.1, we discuss the sharpness of the exponent.




The flexible structural estimate \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:error-intro}}}\right)
permits the combinations of various source and target assumptions, each
combination may lead to a dedicated quantiative stability which exploits
specific properties of a given family of marginals thus gives stronger
stability than the general theorem would provide, and the sharp conditioning
can also give refined stability. But we have refrained from exhausting the
combinations and focus instead on combinations of moments and compactness of supports.




Section Appendix A, Section Appendix B,
Section Appendix C are devoted to comparisons, particularly
showing dedicated quantiative stabilities for specific probability classes.
These sections may be read as applications of the main results. The main part
of the article does not depend on these materials.




Further comparisons with prior works and strict improvements. For
targets in a fixed ball and \delta=W_{1}(\mu,\nu), we prove in
Section subsection B.1, for source with Gaussian upper envelope
(Theorem Theorem 14), such as the log-concave family, that





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leqslant\operatorname*{Const}\cdot\delta^{1/4}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c/\delta}}\right)}}\right]^{1/8},\text{ \ if }f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\leqslant C_{0}e^{-\kappa\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-a}}\right|^{2}/2},



and for source with polynomial upper envelope (Theorem Theorem 15), that





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leqslant\operatorname*{Const}\cdot\delta^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\beta-d-1}}\right)/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(4\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\beta-d}}\right)}}\right)},\text{ \ if }f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\leqslant C_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-a}}\right|^{2}}}\right)^{-\beta/2},\text{
}\beta>d+2.



On common hypotheses these improve the map exponents in [19, Theorems 1.4 and
1.12]. The first also avoids an upper Hessian bound, and
the second avoids a density lower bound. Precise comparisons are given after
the corresponding proofs.




Section Appendix A gives detailed comparison with [13, Section
4] on Brenier map stability between compact source
and noncompact target. Section Appendix B is devoted to
comparison with the corresponding results of [19] on
compact source and target under various source density assumptions. Our
Brenier map stability results give strict improvements in all these
comparisons under identical or weaker assumptions.




From objective gap to reconstructed maps.
Section Appendix C is for applications. It treats the
verification of solution accuracy from primal-dual gap, and gives barycentric
map error bounds under explicit accuracy requirements. It is worth noting in
passing that the map error bound and the conditioning law could also be used
in regularized settings, such as verification of the accuracy of entropic map
[26], or barycentric map constructed from
divergence-regularized optimal transport plan [14].
The theory will tell us when to stop a solver to reach a desired solution accuracy.




Remark 1.



Throughout the article, when compactly supported probability measures are
defined, we use closed balls such as B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,R_{X}}}\right), B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b,R_{Y}}}\right) to indicate where their supports belong. But no convexity of
these underlying sets are needed.







2. Preliminaries and assumptions




2.1. Notations




We use \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\cdot}}\right| to denote the Euclidean norm, and
B(a,R) is the closed ball with center a and radius R.
\omega_{d} is the volume of the unit ball in \mathbb{R}^{d},
d\geqslant 1.




\mathcal{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right) denotes probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}. \mathcal{P}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right) denotes
probability measures with finite second moment. If a probability has a
density, then we write e.g. \rho=fdx or \rho\ll dx. For \mu\in\mathcal{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right), r>0, b\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
define the r-th probability norm with center b and its minimized form





M_{\mu,r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b}}\right|^{r}d\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y}}\right)}}\right)^{1/r},\text{ }M_{\mu,r}=\inf_{b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}M_{\mu,r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b}}\right).



For R>0, k\geqslant 0, define the k-th order tail integral





\tau_{k,\mu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)=\int_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b}}\right|>R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b}}\right|^{k}d\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y}}\right).



In particular, we write \tau_{\mu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)=\tau_{0,\mu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)=\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b}}\right|>R}}\right) for the 0-th
tail integral.




For convex u, \operatorname{dom}u=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x:u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)<+\infty}}\right\}, \operatorname{dom}\partial u=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x:\partial u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\text{ is non-empty}}}\right\}. For a set C\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}, I_{C} denotes its indicator, I_{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=0 on
C, while I_{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=+\infty otherwise.




We write \log for the natural logarithm.





2.2. Transport, potentials and integrability




The following is the standing assumption that holds throughout the paper.




Assumption 1 (Brenier setting).



The Borel probability measures \rho, \nu belong to
\mathcal{P}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right). There is a proper lower
semicontinuous convex function u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\infty,+\infty}}\right] such that the unique optimal plan from \rho to \nu is
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\operatorname{Id},T}}\right)_{\#}\rho, where T=\nabla u, \rho-a.e. The potential is chosen so that





u\in L^{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right),\text{ }u^{\ast}\in L^{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nu}}\right).







If \rho\ll dx, this assumption follows from Brenier’s theorem and quadratic
Kantorovich duality
[7, 30, 29]. Some singular
sources are also admissible if they do not give mass to small sets of
Hausdorff dimension at most d-1, but we retain Assumption 1
whenever singular sources are discussed [21].




In general for a proper convex u, \operatorname{dom}\partial u\subset\operatorname{dom}u [27, Theorems 23.4]. Assumption
1 implies that




(2.1)

\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x:\partial u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\nabla u}}\right\}}}\right)=\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\operatorname{dom}\partial u}}\right)=\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\operatorname{dom}u}}\right)=1.



For a lower semicontinuous convex v:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\infty,+\infty}}\right], its conjugate and subdifferential are





v^{\ast}(p)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x\cdot p-v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right\},







\partial v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right)\geqslant v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)+p\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z-x}}\right),\ \forall z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right\}.



Following [12], for a proper lower semicontinuous convex
v, we define its Fenchel gap by




(2.2)

g_{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,p}}\right)\triangleq v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)+v^{\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)-x\cdot p\geqslant 0.



The equality holds if and only if p\in\partial v(x).





2.3. Lifting a feasible plan




Lemma 1 (Self-coupling lift).



Assume Assumption 1 and
let \sigma\in\Pi(\rho,\nu). There is a self-coupling q\in\Pi(\rho,\rho)
such that




(2.3)

\mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)=\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)-T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|^{2}dq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,x^{\prime}}}\right),






(2.4)

\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)=\int x\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)-T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right)dq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,x^{\prime}}}\right),






(2.5)

\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)=\int g_{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right)dq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,x^{\prime}}}\right).







Proof.



By disintegration [3, Theorem 5.3.1], there is a
measurable probability kernel y\mapsto\kappa_{y} which disintegrates \rho
w.r.t. the Brenier map T,





\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)=\int\kappa_{y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)d\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y}}\right),



and




(2.6)

\kappa_{y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x^{\prime}:T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)=y}}\right\}}}\right)=1\text{, }\nu\text{-a.e. }y.



Define





\Lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dx,dy,dx^{\prime}}}\right)=\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dx,dy}}\right)\kappa_{y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dx^{\prime}}}\right).



and let q be the \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,x^{\prime}}}\right)-marginal of \Lambda. The
first marginal of q is the first marginal of \sigma, namely \rho, and
its second marginal is





\int\kappa_{y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dx^{\prime}}}\right)d\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y}}\right)=\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dx^{\prime}}}\right).



Thus q\in\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\rho}}\right). Since T(x^{\prime})=y for
\Lambda-a.e. triple \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y,x^{\prime}}}\right) by \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:Ky1}}}\right), the map error can be rewritten as





\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|^{2}d\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)=\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|^{2}d\Lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y,x^{\prime}}}\right)=\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)-T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|^{2}dq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,x^{\prime}}}\right).



This proves \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:E-lift}}}\right).




Expanding the difference \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:Dintro}}}\right) of costs gives





\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)
\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-y}}\right|^{2}d\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}W_{2}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right)





\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-y}}\right|^{2}d\Lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y,x^{\prime}}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|^{2}d\Lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y,x^{\prime}}}\right)





\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-T(x^{\prime})}}\right|^{2}d\Lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y,x^{\prime}}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|^{2}d\Lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y,x^{\prime}}}\right)





\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-T(x^{\prime})}}\right|^{2}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|^{2}}}\right]dq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,x^{\prime}}}\right)





\displaystyle=\int x\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)-T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right)dq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,x^{\prime}}}\right)+\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right|^{2}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|^{2}}}\right]dq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,x^{\prime}}}\right).



The last integral is zero because both coordinates of q have law \rho.
Hence \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:D-corr}}}\right) follows.




Since T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)\in\partial u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right),





u^{\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right)=x^{\prime}\cdot T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)-u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right).



All terms are integrable under Assumption 1. Hence





\displaystyle\int g_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right)dq
\displaystyle=\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)-u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x-x^{\prime}}}\right)\cdot T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right]dq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,x^{\prime}}}\right)





\displaystyle=\int x^{\prime}\cdot T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)dq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,x^{\prime}}}\right)-\int x\cdot T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)dq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,x^{\prime}}}\right)





\displaystyle=\int x\cdot T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)d\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)-\int x\cdot T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)dq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,x^{\prime}}}\right)





\displaystyle=\int x\cdot T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)dq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,x^{\prime}}}\right)-\int x\cdot T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)dq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,x^{\prime}}}\right)



where the second line uses equality of the two marginals of q and the third
renames the integration variable. The result is \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:D-corr}}}\right). Therefore \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:D-gap}}}\right) is proved.
∎







3. Intrinsic source and target profiles




Let v:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R} be convex. For h>0, define its
subgradient oscillation around a point x by




(3.1)

\omega_{v,h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\operatorname{diam}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bigcup_{z\in B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,h}}\right)}\partial v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right)}}\right).



If v is L-Lipschitz, all subgradient norms are bounded by L, so




(3.2)

0\leqslant\omega_{v,h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\leqslant 2L\text{ for all }x.



The union in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:omega}}}\right) is non-empty and compact.
Indeed, the subdifferential has closed graph and is locally bounded
[27, Theorems 24.4 and 24.7]. The correspondence
x\twoheadrightarrow\bigcup_{z\in B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,h}}\right)}\partial v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right) is upper hemicontinuous. Thus by Berge’s maximum theorem
[1, Lemma 17.30], the maximum of \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|p-p^{\prime}}}\right| over the compact set of the pairs \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z,p}}\right), \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z^{\prime},p^{\prime}}}\right) with z, z^{\prime}\in B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,h}}\right) is upper semicontinuous in x. In particular it
is Borel measurable.




Definition 1 (Source oscillation profile).



Let \rho\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right). Define




(3.3)

\mathfrak{A}_{\rho}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right)=\sup_{v}\int\omega_{v,h}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)d\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right),



where the supremum is over all convex 1-Lipschitz functions v:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}. An immediate bound from its definition is




(3.4)

0\leqslant\mathfrak{A}_{\rho}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right)\leqslant 4.







If v is convex and L-Lipschitz, then




(3.5)

\int\omega_{v,h}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)d\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\leqslant L^{2}\mathfrak{A}_{\rho}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right)\leqslant 4L^{2}.






Definition 2 (Target tail profiles).



For \nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right), b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}, R>0 and t>0, define




(3.6)

\tau_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)=\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b}}\right|>R}}\right),






(3.7)

\tau_{2,\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)=\int_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b}}\right|>R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b}}\right|^{2}\,d\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y}}\right),



and




(3.8)

\mathfrak{T}_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)=4\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\tau_{2,\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)+U_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)}}\right)}}\right],



where for t\geqslant 0,




(3.9)

U_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=\sup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b}}\right|^{2}\vartheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y}}\right)d\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y}}\right):0\leqslant\vartheta\leqslant 1,\ \int\vartheta d\nu\leqslant t}}\right\}.







Remark 2.



Throughout the article we introduce centers to both source or target measures.
These centers help optimize the coefficients appearing in various estimates in
a distribution-adaptive manner.





The function \vartheta in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:U}}}\right) acts as a selector
and is required for general t when \nu has atoms. In \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:Tdef}}}\right) the mass allowance of U_{\nu,b} corresponds to
t=\tau_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right). In this case the tail profile is
simplified,




(3.10)

U_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)}}\right)=\tau_{2,\nu,b}(R),\text{ }\mathfrak{T}_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)=8\tau_{2,\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right).



Indeed, let Z=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b}}\right|^{2}, A=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b}}\right|>R}}\right\}=\{Z>R^{2}\} and t=\nu(A). For every \vartheta
with 0\leqslant\vartheta\leqslant 1,





\displaystyle\int Z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vartheta-1_{A}}}\right)d\nu
\displaystyle=\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Z-R^{2}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vartheta-1_{A}}}\right)d\nu+R^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int\vartheta d\nu-t}}\right)





\displaystyle=\int_{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Z-R^{2}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vartheta-1_{A}}}\right)d\nu+\int_{A^{c}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Z-R^{2}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vartheta-1_{A}}}\right)d\nu+R^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int\vartheta d\nu-t}}\right).



Observe that all terms are nonpositive, and equal to zero if \vartheta=1_{A}. Thus U_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)}}\right) is attained
by \vartheta=1_{A} and \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:tail-exact}}}\right) follows.




Lemma 2.



If \nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right), then





\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\mathfrak{T}_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)=0.







Proof.



Since the function y\mapsto\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b}}\right|^{2} is \nu-integrable, the dominated convergence theorem gives \tau_{2,\nu,b}(R)\rightarrow 0. The limit follows from the identity \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:tail-exact}}}\right): \mathfrak{T}_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)=8\tau_{2,\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right).
∎






4. The proximal Fenchel estimate




In the proof of Theorem Theorem 1 we will need a local slope with
required closeness to a given subgradient. The proximal map produces such a
slope. For \lambda>0, define





\operatorname{prox}_{\lambda u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\xi}}\right)\triangleq\arg\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right)+\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z-\xi}}\right|^{2}}{2\lambda}}}\right\}.



The proximal operator is well-defined for proper lower semicontinuous convex
u with unique minimizer for each \xi. The first order condition of the
minimization gives





0\in\partial\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right)+\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z-\xi}}\right|^{2}}{2\lambda}}}\right].



The two terms have non-empty intersection of domain relative interiors, since
the quadratic term has a domain relative interior of \mathbb{R}^{d}. So





0\in\partial u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right)+\frac{z-\xi}{\lambda}\text{ or equivalently
}\frac{\xi-z}{\lambda}\in\partial u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right).






We recall a useful property of the proximal map \operatorname{prox}_{\lambda u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cdot}}\right) that it is nonexpansive. Indeed, if z_{i}=\operatorname{prox}_{\lambda u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\xi_{i}}}\right), i=1,2, set





q_{i}=\frac{\xi_{i}-z_{i}}{\lambda}\in\partial u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z_{i}}}\right).



Then by the cyclic monotonicity of the subdifferential we have \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1}-q_{2}}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z_{1}-z_{2}}}\right)\geqslant 0, and hence





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z_{1}-z_{2}}}\right|^{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z_{1}-z_{2}}}\right)-\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1}-q_{2}}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z_{1}-z_{2}}}\right)\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}}}\right|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z_{1}-z_{2}}}\right|.



If \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z_{1}-z_{2}}}\right| is not zero, we obtain





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z_{1}-z_{2}}}\right|\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}}}\right|.



When \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z_{1}-z_{2}}}\right|=0, the inequality is trivial.
Therefore nonexpansiveness follows.




The next construction gives the local slope that we need later. The estimate
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:prox-gap}}}\right), which is a kind of coercivity of the
Fenchel gap \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:prox-gap}}}\right), is proved in [12, Lemma
1.1].




Lemma 3 (Pointwise proximal estimate).



Let u be proper lower
semicontinuous and convex. For fixed x, x^{\prime}\in\operatorname*{dom}\partial u, suppose that p\in\partial u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right), r\in\partial u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right), and \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|p}}\right|, \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|r}}\right|\leqslant R. For \lambda>0, define




(4.1)

z=\operatorname{prox}_{\lambda u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x+\lambda r}}\right),\text{
}v=r+\frac{x-z}{\lambda}.



Then




(4.2)

v\in\partial u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right),






(4.3)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z-x}}\right|\leqslant 2R\lambda,






(4.4)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|v}}\right|\leqslant 3R,






(4.5)

g_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,r}}\right)\geqslant\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|v-r}}\right|^{2}.







Proof.



The inclusion \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:prox-subgrad}}}\right) follows from the
first-order condition





0\in\partial u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right)+\frac{z-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x+\lambda r}}\right)}{\lambda}\text{ or }\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x+\lambda r}}\right)-z}{\lambda}\in\partial u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right).



Let \xi_{1}=x+\lambda r and \xi_{2}=x+\lambda p. The definition \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:prox-v}}}\right) gives





z=\operatorname{prox}_{\lambda u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x+\lambda r}}\right)=\operatorname{prox}_{\lambda u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\xi_{1}}}\right).



Observe that the condition p\in\partial u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right) is equivalent to





x=\operatorname{prox}_{\lambda u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x+\lambda p}}\right)=\operatorname{prox}_{\lambda u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\xi_{2}}}\right).



Applying the nonexpansiveness of the proximal map, we get





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z-x}}\right|\leqslant\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|r-p}}\right|\leqslant 2R\lambda.



This proves \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:prox-distance}}}\right). So it follows that





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|v}}\right|\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|r}}\right|+\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-z}}\right|}{\lambda}\leqslant 3R,



which proves \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:v-bound}}}\right). Using the subgradient
inequality at \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z,v}}\right) and the definition of the conjugate we
have





u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\geqslant u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right)+v\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x-z}}\right)\text{ and }u^{\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r}}\right)\geqslant r\cdot z-u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right).



Adding them up gives





u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)+u^{\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r}}\right)\geqslant u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right)+v\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x-z}}\right)+r\cdot z-u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right).



Subtracting x\cdot r from both sides and using that x-z=\lambda(v-r) we
obtain





g_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,r}}\right)\geqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v-r}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x-z}}\right)=\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|v-r}}\right|^{2}.



Therefore the proof is complete.
∎





The next is a simple way to truncate long-distance transports which we call a
slope-clipping principle. For convex functions, it has appeared in
[10, 23]. See also
[15] for general handling.




Lemma 4 (Slope-clipping principle).



Under the hypotheses of
Lemma Lemma 3, define





u_{R}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[u^{\ast}+I_{B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,3R}}\right)}}}\right]^{\ast}.



The closed ball B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,3R}}\right) contains a point where u^{\ast} is
finite so that u_{R} is convex and 3R-Lipschitz. Moreover,





p\in\partial u_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right),\text{ }v\in\partial u_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right).



and





u_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right),\text{ }u_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right)=u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right).







Proof.



1. According to the hypotheses of Lemma Lemma 3,
x\in\operatorname*{dom}u, p\in\partial u(x), \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|p}}\right|\leqslant R. Hence u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right) is finite. Since the subgradient
condition gives u^{\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=x\cdot p-u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right), so





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|u^{\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}\right|\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x}}\right|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|p}}\right|+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|<\infty.



This says that B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,3R}}\right) contains point p where u^{\ast} is
finite. By the definition of conjugate





u_{R}(\xi)=\sup_{s\in B(0,3R)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\xi\cdot s-u^{\ast}(s)}}\right].



It is a supremum of affine functions with slopes of norm at most 3R, and is
therefore convex and 3R-Lipschitz wherever it it finite. The finiteness of
u^{\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)\ gives





u_{R}(\xi)\geqslant\xi\cdot p-u^{\ast}(p)>-\infty,\text{ }\forall\xi,



showing that u_{R} cannot take -\infty anywhere. The finiteness of
u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right) on the other hand gives a lower bound for u^{\ast} on
the closed ball B(0,3R),





u^{\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)\geqslant x\cdot s-u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\geqslant 3R\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x}}\right|-u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right),\text{ }\forall s\in B(0,3R).



Therefore u_{R}(\xi) is bounded above pointwise. These combined tell us that
the truncated conjugate u_{R} must be finite everywhere.




2. Fix w\in\operatorname{dom}\partial u. If s\in\partial u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right) and \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|s}}\right|\leqslant 3R, the Fenchel equality
gives





u_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)\geqslant w\cdot s-u^{\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)=u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right).



The inequality has used the definition of u_{R} and the fact that s\in B(0,3R). The restricted supremum u_{R} is bounded above by u^{\ast\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right) which equals u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right) because u is lower
semicontinuous convex. Hence for such w we have u_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)=u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right). So for every \xi,





u_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\xi}}\right)\geqslant\xi\cdot s-u^{\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)=u_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)+s\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\xi-w}}\right),



indicating s\in\partial u_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right). With the assumptions on p
and \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:v-bound}}}\right), we apply the preceding to \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w,s}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,p}}\right) and \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w,s}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z,v}}\right) to conclude that p\in\partial u_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right),
v\in\partial u_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right).
∎






5. The distribution-adaptive map error bound




The next theorem is the structural result of the paper. It turns the Fenchel
gap into a map error bound by separating three contributions: the objective
gap, the source mass near convex singularities and target tail.




Theorem 1 (Map error bound).



Assume Assumption 1
and let \sigma\in\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right). Then




(5.1)

\mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)\leqslant\inf_{b\in\mathbb{R}^{d},R,h>0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{4R}{h}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)+18R^{2}\mathfrak{A}_{\rho}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right)+\mathfrak{T}_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)}}\right\}.







Proof.



1. Let q\in\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\rho}}\right) be the self-coupling from
Lemma Lemma 1. Fix b\in\mathbb{R}^{d} and define





\bar{T}=T-b,\text{ }\bar{u}=u-b\cdot x.



Note that \partial\bar{u}=\partial u-b and hence \bar{T}\in\partial\bar{u}, \rho-a.e. Since for x^{\prime}\in\operatorname{dom}\partial\bar{u},





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bar{u}}}\right]^{\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right)=u^{\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)+b}}\right)=u^{\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right),



the definition gives





\displaystyle g_{\bar{u}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,\bar{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right)
\displaystyle=\bar{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bar{u}}}\right]^{\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right)-x\cdot\bar{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)





\displaystyle=u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)-b\cdot x+u^{\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right)-x\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)-b}}\right].



Hence




(5.2)

g_{\bar{u}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,\bar{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right)=g_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right).



Fix R, h>0. Define





G_{b,R}=\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,x^{\prime}}}\right):\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\bar{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|\leqslant R,\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\bar{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right|\leqslant R\}.



Note that





G_{b,R}\subset\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\operatorname{dom}\partial\bar{u}}}\right)^{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\operatorname{dom}\partial u}}\right)^{2}\subset\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\operatorname{dom}u}}\right)^{2}.






2. We claim that




(5.3)

\int_{G_{b,R}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\bar{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)-\bar{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)\,}}\right|^{2}dq\leqslant\frac{4R}{h}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)+18R^{2}\mathfrak{A}_{\rho}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right).



Since the inequality already holds if q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G_{b,R}}}\right)=0, we assume
q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G_{b,R}}}\right)>0 in the following. So G_{b,R} is non-empty.
For q-a.e. \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,x^{\prime}}}\right)\in G_{b,R}, let





p=\bar{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right),\text{ }r=\bar{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right),



and choose





\lambda=\frac{h}{2R}.



Applying Lemma Lemma 3 to \bar{u} yields z, v such that





z\in B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,h}}\right),\text{ }v\in\partial\bar{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right),\text{ }\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|v}}\right|\leqslant 3R,\text{ }g_{\bar{u}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,r}}\right)\geqslant\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|v-r}}\right|^{2}.



Since p\in B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,R}}\right) provides one point where \bar{u}^{\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=x\cdot p-u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right) is finite, the truncated
potential





\bar{u}_{R}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bar{u}^{\ast}+I_{B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,3R}}\right)}}}\right]^{\ast}



is well-defined by Lemma Lemma 4 and





p\in\partial\bar{u}_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right),\text{ }v\in\partial\bar{u}_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right).



Since z\in B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,h}}\right), the definition in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:omega}}}\right) gives





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|v-p}}\right|\leqslant\omega_{\bar{u}_{R},h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right).



Writing r-p=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r-v}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v-p}}\right), we obtain




(5.4)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|r-p}}\right|^{2}\leqslant 2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|r-v}}\right|^{2}+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|v-p}}\right|^{2}\leqslant\frac{2}{\lambda}g_{\bar{u}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,r}}\right)+2\omega_{\bar{u}_{R},h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)^{2}.



The Fenchel gap is nonnegative, so \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:D-gap}}}\right) and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:same-gap}}}\right) imply





\int_{G_{b,R}}g_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right)dq=\int_{G_{b,R}}g_{\bar{u}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,\bar{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right)dq\leqslant\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right).



The first marginal of q|_{G_{b,R}} is dominated by \rho. Since \bar{u}_{R} convex and 3R-Lipschitz, we have by \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:A-scale}}}\right)





\int_{G_{b,R}}\omega_{\bar{u}_{R},h}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)dq\leqslant 9R^{2}\mathfrak{A}_{\rho}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right).



Thus \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:good-bound}}}\right) follows by integrating \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:good-point}}}\right) on G_{b,R} and using 2/\lambda=4R/h.




3. Now we return to the original transport map. Since translation
does not change differences of transports, we may replace \bar{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)-\bar{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right) with T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)-T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right) in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:good-bound}}}\right) and
let \pi=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T,T}}\right)_{\#}q\in\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nu,\nu}}\right), with
coordinates y, y^{\prime}. Let A=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b}}\right|>R}}\right\}. Then





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G_{b,R}}}\right)^{c}=\{y\in A\}\cup\{y^{\prime}\in A\}.



Since for all y, y^{\prime},





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-y^{\prime}}}\right|^{2}\leqslant 2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b}}\right|^{2}+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y^{\prime}-b}}\right|^{2},



we have





\int_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G_{b,R}}}\right)^{c}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-y^{\prime}}}\right|^{2}d\pi\leqslant 2\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b}}\right|^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y\in A}}\right\}}+1_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y^{\prime}\in A}}\right\}}}}\right)d\pi+2\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y^{\prime}-b}}\right|^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y\in A}}\right\}}+1_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y^{\prime}\in A}}\right\}}}}\right)d\pi.



Each diagonal term equals \tau_{2,\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right). For a cross
term, say the first, the y-marginal of the submeasure 1_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y^{\prime}\in A}}\right\}}\pi has the form \vartheta\nu, where
0\leqslant\vartheta\leqslant 1 and \int\vartheta d\nu=\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)=\tau_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right). Hence each cross term is at most U_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)}}\right). It follows that




(5.5)

\int_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G_{b,R}}}\right)^{c}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)-T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)\,}}\right|^{2}dq\leqslant\mathfrak{T}_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right).



Adding \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:good-bound}}}\right), \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:bad-bound}}}\right) and using \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:E-lift}}}\right), we obtain





\mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)\leqslant\frac{4R}{h}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)+18R^{2}\mathfrak{A}_{\rho}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right)+\mathfrak{T}_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right).



The proof is therefore completed by taking the infimum over R, h>0 and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.
∎





Remark 3.



Note that the compactness of the source is not used in the proof. It enters
only in some estimates of \mathfrak{A}_{\rho}, thus allowing us to treat
source and target tails separately.






6. Estimates of the source profile




6.1. Geometric estimate and the tailored oscillation integral




Let v be L-Lipschitz convex, a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}, h>0, and \omega_{d}
be the volume of the unit ball. [10, Theorem 2.1]
gives a covering of oscillation in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:omega}}}\right) by balls
of radius 8h. Hence using \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S+4h}}\right)^{d-1}\leqslant 5^{d-1}S^{d-1} for 0<h\leqslant S we get




(6.1)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x\in B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,S}}\right):\omega_{v,h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)>\alpha}}\right\}}}\right|\leqslant K_{d}^{\mathrm{vol}}\frac{LS^{d-1}h}{\alpha}\text{ for }0<h\leqslant S,\ \alpha>0,



where the bound has been scaled by the volume \omega_{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(8h}}\right)^{d} and




(6.2)

K_{d}^{\mathrm{vol}}=48d^{2}\omega_{d}8^{d}5^{d-1}.






The following is a variant of [10, Corollary 2.2]
tailored to our required specifications.




Lemma 5 (Oscillation integral).



For r>1, define




(6.3)

K_{d,r}^{\mathrm{osc}}=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{r2^{r-1}}{r-1}K_{d}^{\mathrm{vol}},\,2^{r}\omega_{d}}}\right\}.



Then, for h, S>0,




(6.4)

\int_{B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,S}}\right)}\omega_{v,h}^{r}dx\leqslant K_{d,r}^{\mathrm{osc}}S^{d-1}L^{r}h.



The constant in the bound is independent of a.





Proof.



If L=0, the function is constant and its oscillation vanishes. Assume L>0.
If 0<h\leqslant S, using \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:volume}}}\right) and that
\omega_{v,h}\leqslant 2L yield





\displaystyle\int_{B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,S}}\right)}\omega_{v,h}^{r}dx
\displaystyle=r\int_{0}^{2L}\alpha^{r-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x\in B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,S}}\right):\omega_{v,h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)>\alpha}}\right\}}}\right|d\alpha





\displaystyle\leqslant rK_{d}^{\mathrm{vol}}LS^{d-1}h\int_{0}^{2L}\alpha^{r-2}\,d\alpha=\frac{r2^{r-1}}{r-1}K_{d}^{\mathrm{vol}}S^{d-1}L^{r}h.



Since r>1, the integral is finite. If h>S, we use \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:A-scale}}}\right) to obtain





\int_{B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,S}}\right)}\omega_{v,h}^{r}dx\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2L}}\right)^{r}\omega_{d}S^{d}\leqslant 2^{r}\omega_{d}S^{d-1}L^{r}h.



Combining the two cases we see that \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:osc-int}}}\right) holds
for all h, S>0.
∎






6.2. Source with compact support




Proposition 1.



Let \rho=fdx\in\mathcal{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right) be supported in B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,R_{X}}}\right), R_{X}>0, f\in L^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right),1<p\leqslant\infty and s=1-1/p. For
every h>0,




(6.5)

\mathfrak{A}_{\rho}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right)\leqslant K_{d,\rho,p}R_{X}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s}h^{s}.



where




(6.6)

K_{d,p}^{L^{p}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K_{d,2/s}^{\mathrm{osc}}}}\right)^{s}\text{ and
}K_{d,\rho,p}=K_{d,p}^{L^{p}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f}}\right\|_{L^{p}}.







Proof.



Let v be 1-Lipschitz convex. For 1<p\leqslant\infty, using Hölder’s
inequality with exponents p, 1/s and the oscillation estimate \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:osc-int}}}\right) with L=1, r=2/s, S=R_{X} we obtain





\int\omega_{v,h}^{2}fdx\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f}}\right\|_{L^{p}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,R_{X}}}\right)}\omega_{v,h}^{2/s}dx}}\right)^{s}\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K_{d,2/s}^{\mathrm{osc}}}}\right)^{s}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f}}\right\|_{L^{p}}R_{X}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s}h^{s}.



The proof is completed by taking the supremum in v.
∎





The following small-set control weakens the assumption of Proposition
1. It will compensate the need to deal with critical
L^{p}-norms without sending limits.




Proposition 2 (Small-set control).



Let \rho=fdx\in\mathcal{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right) be supported in B(a,R_{X}), R_{X}>0. Suppose that
there is C_{V}>0 such that for each Borel E\subset B(a,R_{X}),




(6.7)

\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E}}\right)\leqslant C_{V}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|E}}\right|^{s},\text{
}0<s\leqslant 1,



where \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|E}}\right| is Lebesgue measure. Then for h>0,




(6.8)

\mathfrak{A}_{\rho}(h)\leqslant B_{s}R_{X}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s}h^{s},



where




(6.9)

B_{s}=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{2^{3-s}}{2-s}C_{V}(K_{d}^{\mathrm{vol}})^{s},\,4R_{X}^{-ds}}}\right\}.







Proof.



First assume 0<h\leqslant R_{X}. Take a 1-Lipschitz convex v and t>0.
From the oscillation estimate \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:volume}}}\right) and the
small-set condition \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:small-set-density}}}\right), we have





\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\omega_{v,h}>t}}\right)\leqslant C_{V}(K_{d}^{\mathrm{vol}}R_{X}^{d-1})^{s}h^{s}t^{-s}.



It follows that





\int\omega_{v,h}^{2}d\rho=2\int_{0}^{2}t\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\omega_{v,h}>t}}\right)dt\leqslant 2C_{V}(K_{d}^{\mathrm{vol}}R_{X}^{d-1})^{s}h^{s}\int_{0}^{2}t^{1-s}dt=\frac{2^{3-s}}{2-s}C_{V}(K_{d}^{\mathrm{vol}}R_{X}^{d-1})^{s}h^{s}.



The integral is finite for all 0<s\leqslant 1. Since this holds for all
1-Lipschitz convex v, by taking the supremum in v we get \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:small-set-A}}}\right) for the range 0<h\leqslant R_{X}. For
h>R_{X}, we get by the universal bound \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:A-univ}}}\right)
that \mathfrak{A}_{\rho}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right)\leqslant 4\leqslant 4R_{X}^{-s}h^{s}. Combining the estimates yields \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:small-set-A}}}\right).
∎





Remark 4.



If f\in L^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right), then the small-set condition
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:small-set-density}}}\right) holds with s=1-1/p. On the
other hand, if we write s=1-1/p in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:small-set-density}}}\right), then the small-set condition implies that f\in L^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right) for 0<t<p, but it does not necessarily mean f\in L^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right). As its name indicated, small-set
condition control behaviours on small scales.






6.3. Source with nth-moment and integrable density




Define for a\in\mathbb{R}^{d},





M_{\rho,n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-a}}\right|^{n}f(x)dx}}\right)^{1/n}.






Proposition 3.



Let \rho=fdx\in\mathcal{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right), f\in L^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right), 1<p\leqslant\infty, s=1-1/p, a\in\mathbb{R}^{d} and suppose M_{\rho,n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a}}\right)<\infty for some n\geqslant 2. Let




(6.10)

\eta=\frac{sn}{n+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s},



Then for S, h>0,




(6.11)

\mathfrak{A}_{\rho}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right)\leqslant K_{d,\rho,p}S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s}h^{s}+4M_{\rho,n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a}}\right)^{n}S^{-n}.



where K_{d,\rho,p} is in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:K-d-rho-p}}}\right).
Consequently, for every h>0,




(6.12)

\mathfrak{A}_{\rho}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right)\leqslant B_{d,\rho,p,n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a}}\right)h^{\eta},



where




(6.13)

B_{d,\rho,p,n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}c_{r,n}K_{d,\rho,p}^{n/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n+r}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[4M_{\rho,n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a}}\right)^{n}}}\right]^{r/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n+r}}\right)},&d>1,\\
K_{d,\rho,p},&d=1,\end{array}}}\right.



the exponent r=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s and c_{r,n} is from \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:two-power-constant}}}\right).





Proof.



Let v be 1-Lipschitz convex. On B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,S}}\right), the calculation
in the proof of Proposition 1 shows





\int_{B(a,S)}\omega_{v,h}^{2}f\,dx\leqslant K_{d,\rho,p}S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s}h^{s}.



On B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,S}}\right)^{c}, we use the universal bound \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:A-univ}}}\right) and Markov’s inequality,





\int_{B(a,S)^{c}}\omega_{v,h}^{2}f\,dx\leqslant 4\int_{B(a,S)^{c}}fdx\leqslant 4M_{\rho,n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a}}\right)^{n}S^{-n}.



Hence we get \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:A-Lp-moment}}}\right) by combining the two
estimates and taking supremum in v. Since\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:A-Lp-moment}}}\right) holds for every S>0, we can choose S that minimizes its RHS.
For d>1, this is done by invoking Lemma Lemma 7. For d=1, we
send S\rightarrow\infty in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:A-Lp-moment}}}\right).
Therefore the desired inequality \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:A-Lp-moment-Opt}}}\right)
follows by substituting the chosen S.
∎






6.4. Source on concentric annuli




Proposition 4.



Let \rho=fdx\in\mathcal{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right), a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}, 1<p\leqslant\infty and s=1-1/p. Set





A_{0}=B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,2}}\right),\text{ }A_{k}=B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,2^{k+1}}}\right)\setminus B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,2^{k}}}\right),\text{ }k\geqslant 1,



and define





W_{p,a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f}}\right\|_{L^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{0}}}\right)}+\sum_{k\geqslant 1}2^{k\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f}}\right\|_{L^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{k}}}\right)}.



If W_{p,a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f}}\right)<\infty, then for h>0,




(6.14)

\mathfrak{A}_{\rho}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right)\leqslant K_{d,p}^{\mathrm{ann}}W_{p,a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f}}\right)h^{s},



where K_{d,p}^{\mathrm{ann}}=2^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s}K_{d,p}^{L^{p}}.





Proof.



On each shell A_{k}, as in the proof of Proposition 1 we
can write, for k\geqslant 0,





\int_{A_{k}}\omega_{v,h}^{2}fdx\leqslant K_{d,p}^{L^{p}}2^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k+1}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f}}\right\|_{L^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{k}}}\right)}h^{s}.



We now sum the integrals over k\geqslant 0 and get





\int\omega_{v,h}^{2}fdx\leqslant K_{d,p}^{L^{p}}h^{s}\sum_{k\geqslant 0}2^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k+1}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f}}\right\|_{L^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{k}}}\right)}=2^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s}K_{d,p}^{L^{p}}h^{s}\sum_{k\geqslant 0}2^{k\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f}}\right\|_{L^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{k}}}\right)}.



The RHS is independent of v. Therefore taking the supremum in v we get
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:A-annuli}}}\right).
∎





Remark 5.



For fast decaying source measures, both Proposition 3 and
Proposition 4 apply, but the latter is more dedicated and
gives stronger exponent: \eta\leqslant s with strict inequality when d>1.







7. Estimates of the target profile




Let m>2 and b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}, define





M_{\nu,m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b}}\right|^{m}d\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y}}\right)}}\right)^{1/m},\text{ }M_{\nu,m}=\inf_{b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}M_{\nu,m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b}}\right).



Using the triangle inequality in L^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nu}}\right) we have





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|M_{\nu,m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b_{1}}}\right)-M_{\nu,m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b_{2}}}\right)}}\right|\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|b_{1}-b_{2}}}\right|\text{ for }b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{.}



Thus, whenever M_{\nu,m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b_{0}}}\right)<\infty for some b_{0}, the
function b\mapsto M_{\nu,m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b}}\right)^{m} is continuous and
coercive. Hence its infimum is attained. For m>1 it is strictly convex and
the minimizer is unique. We retain a specified b when geometric information
gives a natural center and use M_{\nu,m} when the best estimate is desired.




Proposition 5.



Let m>2 and b\in\mathbb{R}^{d} satisfy
M_{\nu,m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b}}\right)<\infty. Then for R>0,




(7.1)

\mathfrak{T}_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)\leqslant 8M_{\nu,m}^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b}}\right)R^{2-m}.



Consequently,





\inf_{b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathfrak{T}_{\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)\leqslant 8M_{\nu,m}^{m}R^{2-m}.







Proof.



Since m>2, we have \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b}}\right|^{2}\leqslant R^{2-m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b}}\right|^{m} whenever \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b}}\right|>R. Thus





\tau_{2,\nu,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)\leqslant R^{2-m}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b}}\right|^{m}d\nu=M_{\nu,m}^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b}}\right)R^{2-m}.



Hence \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:T-moment}}}\right) follows from the exact calculation
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:tail-exact}}}\right).
∎






8. Profile optimization and the error bound




This section will see the conversion of profile estimates into explicit power
laws for the error bound.




Theorem 2 (Compact-moment).



Assume Assumption 1. Let
\rho=fdx\in\mathcal{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right) be supported in
B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,R_{X}}}\right), R_{X}>0, 1<p\leqslant\infty, f\in L^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right) and the target v satisfy M_{\nu,m}<\infty for m>2. Let





s=1-\frac{1}{p},\text{ }H_{s,m}=m\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+s}}\right)-s.



Then for \sigma\in\Pi(\rho,\nu),




(8.1)

\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\sigma)\leqslant Q_{d,\rho,p,\nu,m}R_{X}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-2}}\right)/H_{s,m}}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(\sigma)^{s(m-2)/H_{s,m}},



where




(8.2)

a_{s}=\frac{2+s}{1+s},\text{ \ }B_{d,\rho,p}=c_{s,1}18^{1/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+s}}\right)}4^{s/(1+s)}K_{d,\rho,p}^{1/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+s}}\right)},







Q_{d,\rho,p,\nu,m}=c_{a_{s},m-2}8^{a_{s}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{s}+m-2}}\right)}B_{d,\rho,p}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-2}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+s}}\right)/H_{s,m}}M_{\nu,m}^{m(2+s)/H_{s,m}},



and K_{d,\rho,p} is given in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:K-d-rho-p}}}\right) and
c_{a_{s},m-2}, c_{s,1} are from \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:two-power-constant}}}\right). The objective gap exponent is




(8.3)

\theta_{s,m}=\frac{s\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-2}}\right)}{H_{s,m}}=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p-1}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-2}}\right)}{2mp-m-p+1}.



The constant in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:power-full}}}\right) is independent of the
source center.





Proof.



Let b_{m} be the minimizer in the definition of M_{\nu,m}. Applying
Proposition 1 to the source profile,
Proposition 5 to the target profile, and then
Theorem Theorem 1 with center b_{m}, we obtain for R, h>0,




(8.4)

\mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)\leqslant 4R\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)h^{-1}+18K_{d,p}^{L^{p}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f}}\right\|_{L^{p}}R_{X}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s}R^{2}h^{s}+8M_{\nu,m}^{m}R^{2-m}.



If \mathcal{D}_{\nu}(\sigma)=0, letting h\rightarrow 0 then R\rightarrow\infty yields that \mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\sigma)=0. So \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:power-full}}}\right) holds trivially. If M_{\nu,m}=0, then
\nu=\delta_{b_{m}}, and every feasible plan is supported on \mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{b_{m}\}, hence again \mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\sigma) is zero and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:power-full}}}\right) holds. Assume henceforth that
\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(\sigma)>0 and M_{\nu,m}>0. For fixed R>0. Applying
Lemma Lemma 7 we find the minimum of the sum of the first two
terms in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:pre-opt}}}\right),






\displaystyle c_{s,1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[18K_{d,\rho,p}R_{X}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s}R^{2}}}\right]^{1/(1+s)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[4R\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(\sigma)}}\right]^{s/(1+s)}





\displaystyle=c_{s,1}18^{1/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+s}}\right)}4^{s/(1+s)}K_{d,\rho,p}^{1/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+s}}\right)}R_{X}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+s}}\right)}R^{(2+s)/(1+s)}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(\sigma)^{s/(1+s)}





\displaystyle=B_{d,\rho,p}R_{X}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+s}}\right)}R^{(2+s)/(1+s)}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(\sigma)^{s/(1+s)}.



Substituting it into \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:pre-opt}}}\right) we get




(8.5)

\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\sigma)\leqslant B_{d,\rho,p}R_{X}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+s}}\right)}R^{(2+s)/(1+s)}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(\sigma)^{s/(1+s)}+8M_{\nu,m}^{m}R^{2-m}.



We now unfreeze R and apply Lemma Lemma 7 to the sum in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:after-h}}}\right) to find the minimum





c_{a_{s},m-2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[B_{d,\rho,p}R_{X}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+s}}\right)}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(\sigma)^{s/(1+s)}}}\right]^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-2}}\right)/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{s}+m-2}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[8M_{\nu,m}^{m}}}\right]^{a_{s}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{s}+m-2}}\right)}.



To work out the exponents we calculate





a_{s}+m-2=\frac{2+s+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-2}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+s}}\right)}{1+s}=\frac{m\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+s}}\right)-s}{1+s}=\frac{H_{s,m}}{1+s},







m\frac{a_{s}}{a_{s}+m-2}=\frac{m(2+s)}{H_{s,m}},\text{ \ \ \ \ \ }\frac{s}{1+s}\frac{m-2}{a_{s}+m-2}=\frac{s\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-2}}\right)}{H_{s,m}}.



Substituting the calculations into \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:after-h}}}\right) we
obtain





\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\sigma)\leqslant Q_{d,\rho,p,\nu,m}R_{X}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-2}}\right)/H_{s,m}}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(\sigma)^{s(m-2)/H_{s,m}}.



Therefore the desired bound \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:power-full}}}\right) is proved.
∎

∎

9. Sharpness and extreme cases




The section tries to determine the sharpness of the exponents of the error
bound obtained in the preceding section. Here the sharpness means with a given
class of probability marginals, the exponent cannot be increased uniformly for
all pairs of marginals in that class.




9.1. Sharpness of Theorem Theorem 2




The next theorem establishes sharpness of the map error exponent in
Theorem Theorem 2. The example below does not uniformly satisfy the
strong assumptions of Corollary Corollary 1, there is no common
bounded target support as h\rightarrow 0.




Theorem 4.



Fix d\geqslant 1, 1<p\leqslant\infty, m>2 and s=1-1/p.
There are \rho_{h}=f_{h}dx supported in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]^{d} for all
small h>0, target \nu_{h} and plans \sigma_{h}\in\Pi(\rho_{h},\nu_{h})
such that





\sup_{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f_{h}}}\right\|_{L^{p}}<\infty,\text{ }\sup_{h}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y}}\right|^{m}d\nu_{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y}}\right)<\infty,



and




(9.1)

\mathcal{E}_{\nu_{h}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{h}}}\right)=2\mathcal{D}_{\nu_{h}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{h}}}\right)^{\theta_{s,m}}.



Here \theta_{s,m} is exactly the exponent in Theorem Theorem 2,
defined by \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:theta}}}\right). Thus the exponent there is sharp
and uniform in the given class of marginals.





Proof.



First consider d=1. Let h>0 small enough so that h<1/4 and q_{h}=h^{s}<1/2. Let





I_{-}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1-2h,1-h}}\right],\text{ }I_{+}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1-h,1}}\right],



and define on [0,1]





f_{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-2q_{h}}}\right)/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-2h}}\right),&x\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1-2h}}\right).\\
q_{h}/h,&x\in I_{-}\cup I_{+},\end{array}}}\right.



Each of I_{-}, I_{+} has mass q_{h}, while the remaining interval has
mass 1-2q_{h}. Thus f_{h}\geqslant 0 and \int_{0}^{1}f_{h}\,dx=1. We now
verify that \sup_{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f_{h}}}\right\|_{L^{p}}<\infty. For
p=\infty, s=1, q_{h}=h, so f_{h}\equiv 1 and \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f_{h}}}\right\|_{L^{p}}=1. For p<\infty, we have on I_{-}\cup I_{+},





2h\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{h}/h}}\right)^{p}=2h\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{s-1}}}\right)^{p}=2hh^{-1}=2,



On the remaining interval,





0\leqslant\frac{1-2q_{h}}{1-2h}\leqslant\frac{1}{1-2h}\leqslant 2.



Hence \sup_{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f_{h}}}\right\|_{L^{p}}<\infty.




Set R_{h}=q_{h}^{-1/m} and define





T_{h}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1-h}}\right),\\
R_{h},&x\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1-h,1}}\right].\end{cases}



Up to a null set, T_{h} equals the derivative the convex function





u_{h}(x)=R_{h}\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,x-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-h}}\right)}}\right\}.



The pushforward of \rho_{h} under T_{h} is





\nu_{h}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-q_{h}}}\right)\delta_{0}+q_{h}\delta_{R_{h}}.



By uniqueness of Brenier map, T_{h} is the optimal map between \rho_{h}
and \nu_{h}. The moments of \nu_{h} are uniformly bounded,





\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y}}\right|^{m}d\nu_{h}=q_{h}R_{h}^{m}=1.



So the construction satisfies all requirements.




Let S_{h} be the map that swaps I_{-} and I_{+} by translations of
length h and leaves the remaining unchanged. Since the densities on I_{-}
and I_{+} are equal, we have \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{h}}}\right)_{\#}\rho_{h}=\rho_{h}.
Thus \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\operatorname{Id},S_{h}}}\right)_{\#}\rho_{h}\in\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho_{h},\rho_{h}}}\right). Define \sigma_{h}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\operatorname{Id},T_{h}\circ S_{h}}}\right)_{\#}\rho_{h}, where T_{h}\circ S_{h} is the
composed transport map





T_{h}\circ S_{h}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1-2h}}\right),\\
R_{h}&x\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1-2h,1-h}}\right),\\
0,&x\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1-h,1}}\right],\end{cases}



Substituting T_{h} and \sigma_{h} into \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:Eintro-map}}}\right) we get




(9.2)

\mathcal{E}_{\nu_{h}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{h}}}\right)=2q_{h}R_{h}^{2}=2h^{s\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-2/m}}\right)}.



By \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:D-corr}}}\right) in Lemma Lemma 1 with
q=(\operatorname{Id},S_{h})_{\#}\rho_{h},




(9.3)

\mathcal{D}_{\nu_{h}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{h}}}\right)=R_{h}\frac{q_{h}}{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{I_{+}}xdx-\int_{I_{-}}xdx}}\right)=R_{h}\frac{q_{h}}{h}h^{2}=h^{1+s-s/m}.



Since





s(1-\frac{2}{m})=\frac{s\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-2}}\right)}{m},\text{ }1+s-\frac{s}{m}=\frac{m(1+s)-s}{m},



so \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:sharp-relation}}}\right) is proved in dimension one.




For d>1, define





\bar{\rho}_{h}=\rho_{h}\otimes\mathcal{L}^{d-1}|_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]^{d-1}},\text{ }\bar{T}_{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},x^{\prime}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T_{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1}}}\right),0}}\right).



Up to a null set, the map is the gradient of the convex function





\bar{u}_{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},x^{\prime}}}\right)=\int_{0}^{x_{1}}T_{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)dt,



which depends only on x_{1}. Swapping I_{-}\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]^{d-1} and I_{+}\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]^{d-1} does not change
x^{\prime}-coordinates, so the calculations in the one-dimensional case are
unchanged, particularly the L^{p} norms and the quantities in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:E-sharp}}}\right), \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:D-sharp}}}\right) are unchanged.
Therefore the relation \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:sharp-relation}}}\right) holds in
general dimension.
∎





The next examples have no class uniform modulus. Their objective gaps
converges to zero while their map errors stay bounded away from zero.




Theorem 5.



If p=1 or m=2, there is no class uniform modulus for
which the bound of the form \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:power-full}}}\right) in Theorem
Theorem 2 holds, even for compact targets.





Proof.



The examples run parallel to Theorem Theorem 4. Restrict first to d=1
and extend to higher dimension by tensorizing. For the extreme case p=1. Let
q_{h} to a fixed q_{0}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1/2}}\right) and take R_{h}=1. The
constructions of \rho_{h}=f_{h}dx, \nu_{h}, T_{h}, \sigma_{h} and
calculations in Theorem Theorem 4 remain valid except that we need to
replace q_{h} with q_{0}. In particular, each \rho_{h} is supported in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right] and each target \nu_{h} in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}.
Therefore all source and target moments, of any finite order, are bounded
uniformly. Hence we get from \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:E-sharp}}}\right) and \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:D-sharp}}}\right) that





\mathcal{E}_{\nu_{h}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{h}}}\right)=2q_{0},\text{ }\mathcal{D}_{\nu_{h}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{h}}}\right)=R_{h}\frac{q_{0}}{h}h^{2}=q_{0}h\rightarrow 0.



This proves that boundedness of L^{1}-norm of the source measure alone
cannot provide a class-uniform vanishing modulus, even if the source has
densities and targets have uniformly bounded supports.




For extreme case m=2. The constructions and calculations in Theorem
Theorem 4 continue to hold if we set p=\infty. Therefore s=1,
q_{h}=h and \rho_{h} is uniform on \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]. Set
R_{h}=q_{h}^{-1/2}. Then we see that the targets have uniformly bounded
second moments,





\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y}}\right|^{2}d\nu_{h}=q_{h}R_{h}^{2}=1.



However,





\mathcal{E}_{\nu_{h}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{h}}}\right)=2,\text{ }\mathcal{D}_{\nu_{h}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{h}}}\right)=q_{h}^{1/2}h\rightarrow 0.






∎






9.2. Sharpness of Theorem Theorem 3




Theorem 6.



Fix d\geqslant 1, 1<p\leqslant\infty,
n\geqslant 2 and m>2. Let





s=1-\frac{1}{p},\text{ }\eta=\frac{sn}{n+(d-1)s},\text{ }\Theta=\frac{\eta(m-2)}{m(1+\eta)-\eta}.



There exist \rho_{h}=f_{h}dx, targets \nu_{h} and deterministic feasible
plans \sigma_{h} for all small h>0, such that





\sup_{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f_{h}}}\right\|_{p}<\infty,\text{ }\sup_{h}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x}}\right|^{n}\,d\rho_{h}<\infty,\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y}}\right|^{m}\,d\nu_{h}=1,\text{ }\mathcal{E}_{\nu_{h}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{h}}}\right)=2\mathcal{D}_{\nu_{h}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{h}}}\right)^{\Theta}.



The objective gaps converge to zero, and all pairs satisfy
Assumption 1. Consequently the exponent in
Theorem Theorem 3 is sharp for classes with uniform bounds and
no common source support radius. For d>1, the source profile exponent \eta
defined in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:A-eta}}}\right), which is part of the exponent in
Theorem Theorem 3, is also sharp for this class.





Proof.



We use the construction from Step 5 in the proof of
Theorem Theorem 7. That construction is proved directly from the
error bound and source estimates and does not use the present theorem. With
\alpha=\xi=K=1 and \beta=0,





S=q^{-1/n},\text{ }h=q^{1/\eta},\text{ }q=R^{-m},\text{ }u_{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=R\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,x_{1}}}\right\}.



We now use the formulas from \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:log-lower-E}}}\right) and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:log-lower-D}}}\right) to compute the exact error and
objective gap,





\mathcal{E}_{\nu_{h}}(\sigma_{h})=2qR^{2}=2h^{\eta(1-2/m)},\text{ }\mathcal{D}_{\nu_{h}}(\sigma_{h})=hqR=h^{1+\eta-\eta/m}.



Since





\frac{\eta(1-2/m)}{1+\eta-\eta/m}=\frac{\eta(m-2)}{m(1+\eta)-\eta}=\Theta,



so the construction satisfies all requirements and saturates the exponent in
Theorem Theorem 3.




For the sharpness of exponent \eta defined in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:A-eta}}}\right), consider the convex 1-Lipschitz function v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x_{1}}}\right| which has \omega_{v,h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=2 on
A_{-}\cup A_{+}. Each ball with radius h that is admissible to the
defintion of \omega_{v,h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right) meets x_{1}=0, at which the
subdifferential of v contains e_{1} and -e_{1}. A_{-}\cup A_{+} has
total source mass 2q, so





\mathfrak{A}_{\rho_{h}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right)\geqslant 4\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2q}}\right)=8h^{\eta}.



This together with the uniform upper estimate \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:A-Lp-moment-Opt}}}\right) therefore gives the matching order.
∎







10. Sharp conditioning with logarithmic moments




Throughout this section, we fix d\geqslant 1, 1<p\leqslant\infty, and
n\geqslant 2. Define





s=1-1/p,\text{ }\eta=\frac{sn}{n+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s},\text{ }F_{p}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}3^{1/p},&p<\infty,\\
1,&p=\infty,\end{array}}}\right.,\text{ }M_{d,n}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d+3}}\right)^{n/2}+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(4d-3}}\right)^{n/2}.



Fix m\geqslant 2, \beta\geqslant 0 and scaling constants \alpha\geqslant 1,
\xi\geqslant 1, K>0. Let \mathcal{C}_{m,\beta}=\mathcal{C}_{m,\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha,\xi,K}}\right) be the class consisting of probability pairs
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right) satisfying




(10.1)

\rho=fdx,\text{ }\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f}}\right\|_{p}\leqslant\alpha F_{p},\text{
}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x}}\right|^{n}d\rho\leqslant\xi M_{d,n},\text{ }\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y}}\right|^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y}}\right|}}\right)}}\right]^{\beta}d\nu\leqslant K.






Note that each pair \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right) in the class has \rho,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}. The class \mathcal{C}_{m,\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha,\xi,K}}\right) is non-empty for all scaling choice \alpha\geqslant 1,
\xi\geqslant 1, K>0. Indeed, let \rho=dx on





F=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[-2,-1}}\right]\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]^{d-1}.



It has L^{p}-norm one and n-th moment at most \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d+3}}\right)^{n/2}. Let \nu=\delta_{0}. Then \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right)\in\mathcal{C}_{m,\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,1,1}}\right).




Define for a class \mathcal{C} of probaility pairs, t>0,




(10.2)

\Omega_{\mathcal{C}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right):\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right)\in\mathcal{C},\text{ }\sigma\in\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right),\text{ }\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)\leqslant t}}\right\}.






Theorem 7 (Sharp power-logarithm conditioning law).



For the fixed
non-empty class in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:normalized-class}}}\right), let





H=m(1+\eta)-\eta,\text{ }\Theta=\frac{\eta(m-2)}{H},\text{ }\Lambda=\frac{\beta(2+\eta)}{H}.



Define




(10.3)

\varphi(t)=t^{\Theta}[\log(e/t)]^{-\Lambda},\text{ }0<t<1.



Then the following hold.




\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right) There exist c, C, t_{0}>0, depending only on d,

exactly t.




\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(ii}}\right) The same matching bounds \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:sharp-class-law}}}\right) hold when the map error \mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right) is replaced in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:class-modulus}}}\right) by barycentric map error \|\bar{T}_{\sigma}-T_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}.

(10.8)

L_{z}\geqslant 4,\text{ }\sqrt{L_{z}}\geqslant 4\frac{b_{\ell}}{a_{\ell}}.

(10.9)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:log-scale}}}\right) into the upper bound of \mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)/K. For the first term, the substitution is





\displaystyle B_{\eta}z^{\eta/(1+\eta)}R^{(2+\eta)/(1+\eta)}

-\frac{b_{\ell}(2+\eta)}{1+\eta}=-\frac{\beta(1+\eta)}{H}\frac{2+\eta}{1+\eta}=-\frac{\beta(2+\eta)}{H}=-\Lambda.



Thus the first substitution is exactly B_{\eta}z^{\Theta}L_{z}^{-\Lambda}.
For the second term,





R^{2-m}=(z^{-a_{\ell}}L_{z}^{-b_{\ell}})^{2-m}=z^{a_{\ell}(m-2)}L_{z}^{b_{\ell}(m-2)}.



Since \beta\geqslant 0 and both logarithms in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:explicit-log-comparison}}}\right) are positive,





[\log(e+R)]^{-\beta}\leqslant(a_{\ell}/2)^{-\beta}L_{z}^{-\beta}.



Multipliplying the inequality by the previous equation we have





8R^{2-m}[\log(e+R)]^{-\beta}\leqslant 8(a_{\ell}/2)^{-\beta}z^{a_{\ell}(m-2)}L_{z}^{b_{\ell}(m-2)-\beta}.



The exponents are simplified as,





a_{\ell}(m-2)=\frac{\eta(m-2)}{H}=\Theta,







\displaystyle b_{\ell}(m-2)-\beta

\displaystyle=-\frac{\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m(1+\eta)-\eta-(m-2)(1+\eta)}}\right]}{H}=-\frac{\beta(2+\eta)}{H}=-\Lambda.



Putting the above substitutions back into the upper bound of \mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\sigma), we obtain





\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\sigma)\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[B_{\eta}+8(a_{\ell}/2)^{-\beta}}}\right]K\varphi(t/\kappa).



To return to the original modulus \varphi(t), we first compute





1-(1+1/\eta)\Theta=1-\frac{(1+\eta)(m-2)}{H}=\frac{2+\eta}{H}.



Hence recalling \kappa=K^{1+1/\eta},





K\varphi(t/\kappa)=K^{(2+\eta)/H}t^{\Theta}[\log(e\kappa/t)]^{-\Lambda}.



Let L_{t}=\log(e/t). The second condition in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:explicit-threshold}}}\right) gives L_{t}\geqslant 2|\log\kappa|,
hence





\frac{1}{2}\leqslant\frac{\log(e\kappa/t)}{L_{t}}=1+\frac{\log\kappa}{L_{t}}\leqslant\frac{3}{2}.



Because \Lambda\geqslant 0,




(10.10)

(3/2)^{-\Lambda}K^{(2+\eta)/H}\varphi(t)\leqslant K\varphi(t/\kappa)\leqslant 2^{\Lambda}K^{(2+\eta)/H}\varphi(t).



Taking the supremum over all admissible pairs and plans we have




(10.11)

\Omega_{\mathcal{C}_{m,\beta}}(t)\leqslant C_{\mathrm{up}}\varphi(t),\text{
}0<t\leqslant t_{\mathrm{up}}.



where

Note that so far no division by m-2 has occurred, so the computation covers
m=2.




5. Construct matching lower example. Set





R_{K}=\max\{4,(16K)^{1/m}\}.



For R\geqslant R_{K}, let





S=q^{-1/n},\text{ }h=q^{1/\eta},\text{ }q=KR^{-m}L_{R}^{-\beta},\text{ }



and




(10.12)

L_{R}=\log(e+R).



Since L_{R}\geqslant 1 and KR^{-m}\leqslant 1/16, these definitions yield
that 0<q\leqslant 1/16, 0<h<1 and S\geqslant 1. For d>1, define





I_{-}=[-h,0],\text{ }I_{+}=[0,h]



and





F=[-2,-1]\times[0,1]^{d-1},\text{ }A_{-}=I_{-}\times[S,2S]^{d-1},\text{ }A_{+}=I_{+}\times[S,2S]^{d-1}.



For d=1, F=[-2,-1], A_{-}=I_{-}, A_{+}=I_{+}. The sets are disjoint up
to null boundaries. Define \rho_{R}=f_{R}dx with

\|f_{R}\|_{\infty}=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-2q}}\right),1}}\right\}\leqslant 1\leqslant\alpha F_{\infty}.



On F, \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x}}\right|^{2}\leqslant d+3. On A_{-}\cup A_{+},

The latter also holds when d=1, because h\leqslant 1\leqslant S.
Consequently, using qS^{n}=1 we get





\displaystyle\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x}}\right|^{n}d\rho_{R}
\displaystyle\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-2q}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d+3}}\right)^{n/2}+2q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(4d-3}}\right)^{n/2}S^{n}





\displaystyle\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d+3}}\right)^{n/2}+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(4d-3}}\right)^{n/2}=M_{d,n}\leqslant\xi M_{d,n}.



Therefore \rho_{R} satisfies all requirements of \mathcal{C}_{m,\beta}.

u_{R}(x)=R\max\{0,x_{1}\},



and T_{R}=\nabla u_{R} up to a null set. Thus T_{R}=0 on F\cup A_{-} and

\nu_{R}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T_{R}}}\right)_{\#}\rho_{R}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-q}}\right)\delta_{0}+q\delta_{Re_{1}}.



So T_{R} is the unique Brenier map between \rho_{R} and \nu_{R}.
Moreover,





\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y}}\right|^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y}}\right|}}\right)}}\right]^{\beta}d\nu_{R}=qR^{m}L_{R}^{\beta}=K.



Therefore the construction verifies all three requirements in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:normalized-class}}}\right).

(\mathcal{S}_{R})_{\#}\rho_{R}=\rho_{R}. Hence

is a deterministic feasible plan. Thus




(10.13)

E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)\triangleq\mathcal{E}_{\nu_{R}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{R}}}\right)=2qR^{2}=2KR^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-2}}\right)}L_{R}^{-\beta}.



The correlation identity \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:D-corr}}}\right) gives,




(10.14)

\displaystyle D\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)
\displaystyle\triangleq\mathcal{D}_{\nu_{R}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{R}}}\right)=R\frac{q}{hS^{d-1}}S^{d-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{h}x_{1}\,dx_{1}-\int_{-h}^{0}x_{1}\,dx_{1}}}\right)





\displaystyle=R\frac{q}{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{h^{2}}{2}+\frac{h^{2}}{2}}}\right)=hqR=\kappa R^{-H/\eta}L_{R}^{-\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\eta}}\right)/\eta}.



In the last equality, we have used m\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+1/\eta}}\right)-1=H/\eta and
\kappa=K^{1+1/\eta}. Define





\Delta_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)=R^{-H/\eta}L_{R}^{-\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\eta}}\right)/\eta}\text{ and }D\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)=\kappa\Delta_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right).






6. Derive exact moduli in the small objective gap regime.
The function D\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right) is continuous and strictly decreasing on
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[R_{K},\infty}}\right), with limit zero as R\rightarrow\infty. Thus
for every 0<t\leqslant D\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{K}}}\right) there is a unique R\geqslant R_{K} with D\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)=t. Let





t_{0}\triangleq\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t_{\mathrm{up}},D\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{K}}}\right)}}\right\}.



Then for 0<t<t_{0}, there is a unique R with D\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)=t. For
such R, \log R\leqslant L_{R}\leqslant 2\log R. The latter inequality
follows from e+R\leqslant R^{2} for R\geqslant 4. Hence





\frac{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e/\Delta_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)}}\right)}{L_{R}}=\frac{1+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(H/\eta}}\right)\log R+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\eta}}\right)/\eta}}\right]\log L_{R}}{L_{R}}\geqslant\frac{H}{2\eta}.



We now substitute E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right) and D\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right) into the

\frac{E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)}{K\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(D\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)/\kappa}}\right)}=\frac{E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e/\Delta_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)}}\right)}}\right]^{\Lambda}}{K\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Delta_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)}}\right]^{\Theta}}=\frac{2KR^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-2}}\right)}L_{R}^{-\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e/\Delta_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)}}\right)}}\right]^{\Lambda}}{K\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[R^{-H/\eta}L_{R}^{-\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\eta}}\right)/\eta}}}\right]^{\Theta}}.



To identify the powers we calculate





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(H/\eta}}\right)\Theta=m-2,







\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\eta}}\right)/\eta}}\right]\Theta+\Lambda=\frac{\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\eta}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-2}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2+\eta}}\right)}}\right]}{H}=\beta.

\frac{E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)}{K\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(D\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)/\kappa}}\right)}=2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e/\Delta_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)}}\right)}{L_{R}}}}\right]^{\Lambda}\geqslant 2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{H}{2\eta}}}\right)^{\Lambda}\triangleq c_{0}.

Thus in the desired inequality \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:sharp-class-law}}}\right), we
can choose the constants,





c=2[H/(3\eta)]^{\Lambda}K^{(2+\eta)/H},\text{ }C=C_{\mathrm{up}}\text{ and
}t_{0}=\min\{t_{\mathrm{up}},D(R_{K})\}.



Note that by the definition \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:explicit-threshold}}}\right), we
have t_{\mathrm{up}}<1, so t_{0}<1. The constructed coupling \sigma_{R}
together with its marginals satisfy all requirements of the class
\mathcal{C}_{m,\beta} including the exact objective gap D\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R}}\right)=t, so E(R)\leqslant\Omega_{\mathcal{C}_{m,\beta}}(t). Therefore the
required lower bound for 0<t<t_{0} is proved.




7. Derive mathcing barycentric error bounds. Let \sigma\in\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right) be an arbitrary feasible plan for an
arbitrary pair (\rho,\nu) in \mathcal{C}_{m,\beta}. Then using \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:variance-decomposition}}}\right) and \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:explicit-upper-constant}}}\right) we get





\|\bar{T}_{\sigma}-T_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\leqslant\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\sigma)\leqslant C_{\mathrm{up}}\varphi(t).



By contrast the particular deterministic plan \sigma_{R} constructed in Step
5 with marginals (\rho_{R},\nu_{R}) is concentrated on a map, so





\|\bar{T}_{\sigma_{R}}-T_{R}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{R})}^{2}=\mathcal{E}_{\nu_{R}}(\sigma_{R})=E(R).



Recall that in Step 6 we have chosen R with D(R)=t for t in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,t_{0}}}\right). So these equations together with \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:lower-matching-E}}}\right) yield the desired matching modulus for
barycentric map error.




8. Matching bounds for the extreme cases. In the extreme
case m=2, we have H=2+\eta, \Theta=0, and \Lambda=\beta. For \beta>0, the modulus is therefore comparable to [\log(e/t)]^{-\beta}, which
tends to zero. However if m=2 and \beta=0 happen at the same time, the
example in Step 5 and \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:log-lower-E}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:log-lower-D}}}\right) indicate that





q=KR^{-2},\text{ }E(R)=2qR^{2}=2K,\text{ }D(R)=K^{1+1/\eta}R^{-(2+\eta)/\eta}\rightarrow 0.



On the other hand, for each pair \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right)\in\mathcal{C}_{2,0} and each feasible plan \sigma\in\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right) we have
that





\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\sigma)\leqslant 2\int|y|^{2}d\nu+2\int|T_{\nu}(x)|^{2}d\rho=4\int|y|^{2}d\nu\leqslant 4K.



The equality uses (T_{\nu})_{\#}\rho=\nu. Note that the above does not need
any objective gap restriction to hold. Consequently 2K\leqslant\Omega_{\mathcal{C}_{2,0}}(t)\leqslant 4K for small t>0. So there is no
vanishing modulus for the extreme case m=2, \beta=0. Finally the exteme
case m>2, \beta=0 corresponds to the pure power law of Theorem
Theorem 3.

11. From arbitrary plans to two Brenier maps




Throughout this section \rho=fdx\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}) and
\mu, \nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right). Denote the
Brenier maps from \rho by T_{\mu}, T_{\nu}. The section intends to
convert the arbitrary plan error bound into a comparison of T_{\mu},T_{\nu}.
The conversion requires an auxiliary plan constructed through a
repairing process.




Definition 3 (Plan repaired through a target coupling).



Let
\gamma\in\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\nu}}\right). By disintegration, there is a
measurable probability kernel y\mapsto k_{y} such that





\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dy,dz}}\right)=\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dy}}\right)k_{y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dz}}\right).



Define a probability measure on triples by




(11.1)

\Lambda_{T_{\mu},\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dx,dy,dz}}\right)=\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dx}}\right)\delta_{T_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dy}}\right)k_{y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dz}}\right),



and define the \gamma-repaired plan by




(11.2)

\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,z}}\right)_{\#}\Lambda_{T_{\mu},\gamma}.







The x-marginal of \Lambda_{T_{\mu},\gamma} is \rho, its \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y,z}}\right)-marginal is \gamma, and therefore the z-marginal is \nu.
Hence \sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}\in\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right). Up to a
\Lambda_{T_{\mu},\gamma}-null set, y=T_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right). So under
\Lambda_{T_{\mu},\gamma},





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|T_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)-T_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|^{2}\leqslant 2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-z}}\right|^{2}+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z-T_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|^{2}.



Integrating w.r.t. \Lambda_{T_{\mu},\gamma}, and using its \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y,z}}\right)-marginal \gamma and its \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,z}}\right)-marginal
\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}, yields




(11.3)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\leqslant 2\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-z}}\right|^{2}d\gamma+2\mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}}}\right).



We have proved in Section section 8 that, under mild
conditions, \mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}}}\right) is
controlled by a modulus of \mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}}}\right). So in order to get the stability of Brenier map, it remains to
control \mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}}}\right).




11.1. Maximal correlation and translation invariance




For the source measure, recall that for a\in\mathbb{R}^{d},





M_{\rho,r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-a}}\right|^{r}f(x)dx}}\right)^{1/r},\text{ }M_{\rho,r}=\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}M_{\rho,r}(a).



Define for a\in\mathbb{R}^{d},





F_{a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta}}\right)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\eta}}\right)}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x-a}}\right)\cdot yd\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right).



For \pi\in\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\eta}}\right),





\frac{1}{2}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-y}}\right|^{2}d\pi=\frac{1}{2}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x}}\right|^{2}d\rho+\frac{1}{2}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y}}\right|^{2}d\eta-\int x\cdot yd\pi.



The first two terms are fixed by the marginals, so minimizing the quadratic
cost is equivalent to maximizing the correlation. Subtracting a\int yd\eta,
which is also fixed by the marginals, does not change the maximizers.
Therefore, if \pi_{\nu}\in\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right) is optimal for the
quadratic cost, then





F_{a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nu}}\right)=\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x-a}}\right)\cdot zd\pi_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,z}}\right).



Substituting \sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma} constructed in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:repaired-plan}}}\right) and \pi_{\nu} into the definition \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:Dintro}}}\right) we have





\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}}}\right)
\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-z}}\right|^{2}d\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,z}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}W_{2}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right)





\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-z}}\right|^{2}d\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,z}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-z}}\right|^{2}d\pi_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,z}}\right)





\displaystyle=\int x\cdot zd\pi_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,z}}\right)-\int x\cdot zd\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,z}}\right).



Hence




(11.4)

\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}}}\right)=F_{a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nu}}\right)-\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x-a}}\right)\cdot z\,d\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,z}}\right).



This expression is independent of a. Indeed, let a^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}. Since each coupling in \Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right) has
fixed y-marginal \nu,





F_{a^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nu}}\right)=F_{a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nu}}\right)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a^{\prime}-a}}\right)\int zd\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right).



Since the z-marginal of \sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma} is \nu, so





\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x-a^{\prime}}}\right)\cdot zd\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}=\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x-a}}\right)\cdot zd\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a^{\prime}-a}}\right)\int zd\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right).



Subtracting it from the previous equation, we see that \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:D-F}}}\right) is independent of a. Thus a affects only the quality of the
moment or tail estimate, not the objective gap itself.




Lemma 6.



For \gamma\in\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\nu}}\right), define




(11.5)

\mathcal{C}_{\rho,a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)=\sup_{\Lambda_{\gamma}}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-a}}\right|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z-y}}\right|d\Lambda_{\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y,z}}\right),



where the supremum is over all probabilistic triple law \Lambda_{\gamma}
whose x-marginal is \rho and whose \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y,z}}\right)-marginal is
\gamma. Under the construction in Definition Definition 3,




(11.6)

\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}}}\right)\leqslant 2\mathcal{C}_{\rho,a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right).







Proof.



Let \Lambda_{T_{\mu},\gamma} be the triple law in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:repaired-triple}}}\right). Its \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)-marginal is the
optimal map from \rho to \mu, hence





\int(x-a)\cdot yd\Lambda_{T_{\mu},\gamma}=F_{a}(\mu).



Its \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,z}}\right)-marginal is \sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}.
Consequently,





F_{a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu}}\right)-\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x-a}}\right)\cdot zd\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}=\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x-a}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y-z}}\right)d\Lambda_{T_{\mu},\gamma}\leqslant\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-a}}\right|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z-y}}\right|d\Lambda_{T_{\mu},\gamma}\leqslant\mathcal{C}_{\rho,a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right).



For the converse comparison, let \pi_{\nu}\in\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right)
attain F_{a}(\nu). We disintegrate \gamma with respect to its
z-marginal,





\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dy,dz}}\right)=\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dz}}\right)\ell_{z}(dy),



and then set





\bar{\Lambda}_{\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dx,dy,dz}}\right)=\pi_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dx,dz}}\right)\ell_{z}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dy}}\right).



This law has x-marginal \rho and \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y,z}}\right)-marginal \gamma,
so it is admissible in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:D-product}}}\right). Since the
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)-marginal of \bar{\Lambda}_{\gamma} is a coupling of
\rho and \mu,





\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x-a}}\right)\cdot yd\bar{\Lambda}_{\gamma}\leqslant F_{a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu}}\right).



Using \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:D-F}}}\right) and that \bar{\Lambda}_{\gamma} has
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,z}}\right)-marginal \pi_{\nu}, we obtain





F_{a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nu}}\right)-F_{a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu}}\right)\leqslant\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x-a}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z-y}}\right)d\bar{\Lambda}_{\gamma}\leqslant\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-a}}\right|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z-y}}\right|d\bar{\Lambda}_{\gamma}\leqslant\mathcal{C}_{\rho,a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right).



Adding the previous inequalities we get \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:D-C}}}\right). Note
that the supremum in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:D-product}}}\right) is essential
because \Lambda_{\gamma} and \bar{\Lambda}_{\gamma} need not coincide.
∎






11.2. Objective gap of repaired plan




This section provides control of the objective gap \mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}}}\right) by the distance between targets under
various source and target assumptions.




Proposition 6 (W_{r}-repair).



Let 1<r\leqslant 2, r^{\prime}=r/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r-1}}\right) and M_{\rho,r^{\prime}}<\infty. If \gamma is W_{r}-optimal, then




(11.7)

\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}}}\right)\leqslant 2M_{\rho,r^{\prime}}W_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\nu}}\right).







Proof.



Applying Hölder’s inequality to each law admitted in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:D-product}}}\right), we have





\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-a}}\right|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z-y}}\right|d\Lambda_{\gamma}\leqslant M_{\rho,r^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z-y}}\right|^{r}d\gamma}}\right)^{1/r}.



The last factor equals W_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\nu}}\right). Thus,





\mathcal{C}_{\rho,a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)\leqslant M_{\rho,r^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a}}\right)W_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\nu}}\right)



for all a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}. Using \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:D-C}}}\right) and taking
the infimum over a we obtain \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:Wr-ctrl}}}\right).
∎





Proposition 7 (W_{1}-repair-moment-moment).



Let n\geqslant 2, m>2
and define




(11.8)

M_{\mu,\nu,m}=\inf_{b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{M_{\mu,m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b}}\right),M_{\nu,m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b}}\right)}}\right\}.



Assume M_{\rho,n}<\infty and M_{\mu,\nu,m}<\infty. Let





q=\frac{n}{n-1},\text{ }\alpha=\frac{m-q}{q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-1}}\right)}=\frac{m\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n-1}}\right)-n}{n\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-1}}\right)}.



If \gamma is optimal for W_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\nu}}\right), then with
\delta=W_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\nu}}\right),




(11.9)

\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-z}}\right|^{2}d\gamma\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2M_{\mu,\nu,m}}}\right)^{m/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-1}}\right)}\delta^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-2}}\right)/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-1}}\right)},






(11.10)

\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}}}\right)\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2M_{\rho,n}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2M_{\mu,\nu,m}}}\right)^{1-\alpha}\delta^{\alpha}.







Proof.



Since n\geqslant 2, 1<q\leqslant 2<m,





q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-1}}\right)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-q}}\right)=m\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q-1}}\right)>0.



So \alpha\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right). Since





m\alpha=\frac{m\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-q}}\right)}{q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-1}}\right)},\text{ }1-\alpha=\frac{m\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q-1}}\right)}{q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-1}}\right)},



hence





\alpha+\frac{1-\alpha}{m}=\frac{1}{q}.



Let b_{\ast} minimize the definition of M_{\mu,\nu,m} (see the discussion
at the beginning of Section section 7). By the definition of
M_{\mu,\nu,m} we have




(11.11)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|z-y}}\right\|_{L^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|z-b_{\ast}}}\right\|_{L^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nu}}\right)}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|y-b_{\ast}}}\right\|_{L^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu}}\right)}\leqslant 2M_{\mu,\nu,m},



Let r_{1}=1/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha q}}\right) and r_{2}=m/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\alpha}}\right)q}}\right). Both exponents exceed one and 1/r_{1}+1/r_{2}=\alpha q+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\alpha}}\right)q/m=1. Hence by Hölder
interpolation we obtain





\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z-y}}\right|^{q}d\gamma=\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z-y}}\right|^{\alpha q}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z-y}}\right|^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\alpha}}\right)q}d\gamma\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z-y}}\right|d\gamma}}\right)^{\alpha q}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z-y}}\right|^{m}d\gamma}}\right)^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\alpha}}\right)q/m}.



Taking the q-th root and using \int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z-y}}\right|d\gamma=\delta we get





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|z-y}}\right\|_{L^{q}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}\leqslant\delta^{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2M_{\mu,\nu,m}}}\right)^{1-\alpha}.



For each admissible \Lambda_{\gamma} in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:D-product}}}\right), Hölder’s inequality with exponents n, q therefore gives





\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-a}}\right|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z-y}}\right|d\Lambda_{\gamma}\leqslant M_{\rho,n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a}}\right)\delta^{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2M_{\mu,\nu,m}}}\right)^{1-\alpha}.



Taking the supremum over \Lambda_{\gamma}, using \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:D-C}}}\right), and then taking the infimum over a, we obtain \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:W1-D}}}\right). For the direct coupling estimate \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:W1-direct}}}\right), Hölder interpolation with the relation





\frac{1}{2}=\frac{m-2}{2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-1}}\right)}+\frac{m}{2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-1}}\right)}\frac{1}{m}



gives





\|z-y\|_{L^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}^{2}=\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-z}}\right|^{2}d\gamma\leqslant\|z-y\|_{L^{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}^{(m-2)/(m-1)}\|z-y\|_{L^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}^{m/(m-1)}.



Substituting \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:repair-nm-Lm}}}\right) into the RHS yields

∎





Proposition 8 (W_{1}-repair-moment-compact).

Assume that M_{\rho,n}<\infty, the targets \mu,\nu are supported in a

Theorem 8 (Stability-compact-compact).



If \rho is suppored in
B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,R_{X}}}\right), \rho=fdx with f\in L^{p}, p>1, s=1-1/p

Proof.

where C_{1}=2^{s/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+s}}\right)}B_{d,\rho,p}R_{X}^{ds/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+s}}\right)}R_{Y}^{(2+s)/(1+s)}, B_{d,\rho,p} is defined in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:source-const}}}\right). Substituting the above estimates into
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:map-graph}}}\right) yields, with \delta=W_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\nu}}\right),





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{L^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)}^{2}\leqslant 4R_{Y}\delta+2C_{1}\delta^{s/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+s}}\right)}.



For 0<\delta\leqslant 1, s/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+s}}\right)<1, so \delta\leqslant\delta^{s/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+s}}\right)}. Hence





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{L^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)}\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(4R_{Y}+2C_{1}}}\right)^{1/2}\delta^{s/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+s}}\right)}}\right]}.



The case \delta=0 follows from uniqueness, and for the range \delta>1,
use





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{L^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)}\leqslant 2R_{Y}\leqslant 2R_{Y}\delta^{s/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+s}}\right)}}\right]}.



Thus the constant valid for all \delta\geqslant 0 is \max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(4R_{Y}+2C_{1}}}\right)^{1/2},2R_{Y}}}\right\}.
∎





Theorem 9 (Stability-moment-compact).



Assume Assumption 1, let \rho=fdx,
f\in L^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right), 1<p\leqslant\infty, s=1-1/p,
M_{\rho,n}(a)<\infty for some a\in \mathbb{R}^{d}, n\geqslant 2 and the
targets \mu, \nu are supported in B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b,R_{Y}}}\right). Let





\eta=\frac{sn}{n+(d-1)s},\text{ }\lambda=\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}







\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leqslant CW_{1}(\mu,\nu)^{\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n-1}}\right)/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2n}}\right)},



where the constant C>0 depends only on d,s,p,R_{Y}, not on a,b or the targets.





Proof.



Let a_{\ast} attains the minimum in the definition of M_{\rho,n}\leqslant M_{\rho,n}(a)<\infty. Let \gamma be optimal for W_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\nu}}\right) and let \sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma} be the repaired plan from
Definition Definition 3. Applying Corollary
Corollary 2 with source center a_{\ast} gives





\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma})\leqslant C_{1}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma})^{\eta/(1+\eta)}=C_{1}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma})^{\lambda},



where C_{1}=B_{d,\rho,p,n,c}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{\ast}}}\right)R_{Y}^{(2+\eta)/(1+\eta)}. With \delta=W_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\nu}}\right),





\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-z}}\right|^{2}d\gamma\leqslant 2R_{Y}\delta.



Using \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:D-nth-compact}}}\right), we also have





\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}}}\right)\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2M_{\rho,n}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2R_{Y}}}\right)^{1/n}\delta^{1-1/n}.



So combining these estimates yields





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{L^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)}^{2}\leqslant 4R_{Y}\delta+2C_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2M_{\rho,n}}}\right)^{\lambda}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2R_{Y}}}\right)^{\lambda/n}\delta^{\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n-1}}\right)/n}.



For 0<\delta\leqslant 1, the slower exponent is \lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n-1}}\right)/n. Hence





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{L^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)}\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(4R_{Y}+2C_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2M_{\rho,n}}}\right)^{\lambda}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2R_{Y}}}\right)^{\lambda/n}}}\right)^{1/2}\delta^{\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n-1}}\right)/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2n}}\right)}.



For \delta>1,





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{L^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)}\leqslant 2R_{Y}\leqslant 2R_{Y}\delta^{\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n-1}}\right)/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2n}}\right)}.



Hence we can take C=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(4R_{Y}+2C_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2M_{\rho,n}}}\right)^{\lambda}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2R_{Y}}}\right)^{\lambda/n}}}\right)^{1/2},2R_{Y}}}\right\}.
∎





We now provide the stability of Brenier map for compact source and target with
moment. Since it will used later, we will give a version that slightly weakens
the target moment condition to squared tail control. The tail radius cutoff is
set to 1 to only look at the the tail for measures that might have
singularities near the origin. A probability measure with \beta-th moment
implies the tail bound, but not the reverse. The tail bound controls behaviour
near infinity.




Theorem 10 (Stability-compact-tail).



Assume \rho=fdx is a
probability supported in B(a,R_{X}), R_{X}>0, f\in L^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right), 1<p\leqslant\infty, s=1-1/p and the targets
\mu, \nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right) satisfy the tail
control: for some \beta>0, J\geqslant 0, and a common center b, that for
R\geqslant 1,




(12.3)

\tau_{2,\mu,b}(R)\leqslant JR^{-\beta},\text{ }\tau_{2,\nu,b}(R)\leqslant JR^{-\beta}.



Let





H_{s,\beta}=\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s+1}}\right)+s+2.



Then with \delta=W_{1}(\mu,\nu),





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leqslant C\delta^{\beta s/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2H_{s,\beta}}}\right)}.



The constant C depends only on d,p,R_{X},J, but not the center b.





Proof.



Proposition 1 gives, for every h>0,




(12.4)

\mathfrak{A}_{\rho}(h)\leqslant Bh^{s}\text{ with }B=K_{d,\rho,p}R_{X}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)s}>0.






First assume 0<\delta\leqslant 1. Choose a optimal for W_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\nu}}\right) \gamma and the repaired plan \sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}. For
every triple law in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:D-product}}}\right),





\int|x-a||z-y|d\Lambda\leqslant R_{X}\int|z-y|d\gamma=R_{X}\delta.



Thus \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:D-C}}}\right) yields





\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma})\leqslant 2R_{X}\delta.



To bound the direct coupling term, write Z=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-z}}\right| and
take R>0. On \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{Z>2R}}\right\}, Z^{2}\leqslant 4\max\{|y-b|^{2},|z-b|^{2}\}. Hence,





Z^{2}1_{\{Z>2R\}}\leqslant 4|y-b|^{2}1_{\{|y-b|>R\}}+4|z-b|^{2}1_{\{|z-b|>R\}}.



On \{Z\leqslant 2R\}, Z^{2}\leqslant 2RZ. Integrating the two parts gives
for R\geqslant 1,





\int Z^{2}d\gamma\leqslant 2R\delta+4\tau_{2,\mu,b}(R)+4\tau_{2,\nu,b}(R)\leqslant 2R\delta+8JR^{-\beta}.



Choosing R=\delta^{-1/(\beta+1)}\geqslant 1 gives





\int Z^{2}d\gamma\leqslant(2+8J)\delta^{\beta/(\beta+1)}.



Theorem Theorem 1 combined with the source oscillation bound
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:compact-Lp}}}\right) and the target tail bound \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:source-2tail}}}\right) gives





\mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}}}\right)\leqslant 8R_{X}\delta Rh^{-1}+18R^{2}Bh^{s}+8JR^{-\beta},\text{ }R\geqslant 1,\ h>0.



For fixed R, choosing h to minimizes the sum of the first two terms
yields, for R\geqslant 1,





\mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}}}\right)\leqslant C_{1}\delta^{s/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s+1}}\right)}R^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s+2}}\right)/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s+1}}\right)}+8JR^{-\beta},



where C_{1}=c_{s,1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(8R_{X}}}\right)^{s/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s+1}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(18B}}\right)^{1/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s+1}}\right)}. Now choose R=\delta^{-s/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s+1}}\right)+s+2}}\right]}\geqslant 1 to balance the power.
Substitution gives





\mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}}}\right)\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C_{1}+8J}}\right)\delta^{\beta s/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s+1}}\right)+s+2}}\right]}.



\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:map-graph}}}\right) now gives





\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(4+16J}}\right)\delta^{\beta/(\beta+1)}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2C_{1}+16J}}\right)\delta^{\beta s/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s+1}}\right)+s+2}}\right]}.



The latter term has a slower exponent. Hence





\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((4+2C_{1}+32J}}\right)^{1/2}\delta^{\beta s/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s+1}}\right)+s+2}}\right]}}\right]}.



Taking square roots proves the theorem in this range. For \delta=0,
uniqueness gives equal maps. For \delta>1, the tail bound condition gives





\int|y-b|^{2}d\mu,\text{ }\int|z-b|^{2}d\nu\leqslant 1+J.



Thus





\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\leqslant 2\int|y-b|^{2}d\gamma+2\int|z-b|^{2}d\gamma\leqslant 4\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+J}}\right),



which after taking square root is bounded by the same \delta-power term,





\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leqslant 2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+J}}\right)^{1/2}\delta^{\beta s/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s+1}}\right)+s+2}}\right]}}\right]}.



This proves the theorem with constant C=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((4+2C_{1}+32J}}\right)^{1/2},2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+J}}\right)^{1/2}}}\right\}.
∎





Theorem 11 (Stability-moment-moment).



Assume
Assumption 1, let \rho=fdx, f\in L^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right), 1<p\leqslant\infty, s=1-1/p, M_{\rho,n}(a)<\infty for some a\in \mathbb{R}^{d}, n\geqslant 2 and the targets
\mu, \nu have M_{\mu,\nu,m}<\infty. Let





\alpha=\frac{m(n-1)-n}{n(m-1)},



and let \eta and \Theta_{\eta,m} be defined as in
Theorem Theorem 3. Then




(12.5)

\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leqslant CW_{1}(\mu,\nu)^{\alpha\Theta_{\eta,m}/2},



The constant C depends only on d,p,n,m,M_{\rho,n},M_{\mu,\nu,m}.





Proof.



Choose a W_{1}-optimal coupling \gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu), form the triple law
\Lambda_{T_{\mu},\gamma} in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:repaired-triple}}}\right), and
let \sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}\in\Pi(\rho,\nu) be its repaired (x,z)-marginal. With \delta=W_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\nu}}\right), \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:W1-D}}}\right) gives





\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}}}\right)\leqslant C_{1}\delta^{\alpha},



where C_{1}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2M_{\rho,n}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2M_{\mu,\nu,m}}}\right)^{1-\alpha}. Now choosing a_{\ast} that attains the infimum in the
definition of M_{\rho,n} and applying \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:unbounded-E}}}\right) with this center a_{\ast} give





\mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}}}\right)\leqslant C_{2}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma}}}\right)^{\Theta_{\eta,m}}\leqslant C_{2}C_{1}^{\Theta_{\eta,m}}\delta^{\alpha\Theta_{\eta,m}},



where C_{2}=Q_{d,\rho,p,n,\nu,m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{\ast}}}\right). The \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y,z}}\right)-coupling term is controlled by \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:W1-direct}}}\right),





\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-z}}\right|^{2}d\gamma\leqslant C_{3}\delta^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-2}}\right)/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-1}}\right)},



where C_{3}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2M_{\mu,\nu,m}}}\right)^{m/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-1}}\right)}.
Substitution in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:map-graph}}}\right) yields





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\leqslant 2C_{3}\delta^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-2}}\right)/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-1}}\right)}+2C_{2}C_{1}^{\Theta_{\eta,m}}\delta^{\alpha\Theta_{\eta,m}}.



It remains to identify the slower power of \delta. Since m>2,





0<\alpha=\frac{m(n-1)-n}{n(m-1)}<1,\text{ }\Theta_{\eta,m}=\frac{\eta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-2}}\right)}{m(1+\eta)-\eta}<\frac{m-2}{m-1}.



Thus, for 0<\delta\leqslant 1,





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2C_{3}+2C_{2}C_{1}^{\Theta_{\eta,m}}}}\right)\delta^{\alpha\Theta_{\eta,m}}.



Taking a square root proves \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:both-unbounded}}}\right).  If
\delta=0, then \mu=\nu and uniqueness of the Brenier map gives T_{\mu}=T_{\nu}, \rho-a.e.. For \delta>1, for every b,





\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{L^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)}^{2}
\displaystyle\leqslant 2\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|T_{\mu}-b}}\right|^{2}d\rho+2\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|T_{\nu}-b}}\right|^{2}d\rho





\displaystyle=2\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b}}\right|^{2}d\mu+2\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z-b}}\right|^{2}d\nu\leqslant 4\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{M_{\mu,m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b}}\right),M_{\nu,m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b}}\right)}}\right\}^{2}.



The last step uses monotonicity of moment norm and that m>2. Taking the
infimum over b gives





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{L^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)}\leqslant 2M_{\mu,\nu,m}\leqslant 2M_{\mu,\nu,m}\delta^{\alpha\Theta_{\eta,m}/2}.



Thus one constant valid for all \delta\geqslant 0 is \max\{(2C_{3}+2C_{2}C_{1}^{\Theta_{\eta,m}})^{1/2},2M_{\mu,\nu,m}\}.
∎





12.1. Sharpness of Theorem Theorem 11 in dimension one




The sharp error bound exponent in Theorem Theorem 3 does not
necessarily make the exponent for the Brenier map bound \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:both-unbounded}}}\right) of Theorem Theorem 11 sharp,
although the latter is indeed sharp for compact source and target proved in
Theorem Theorem 8. In dimension one the exponent is in fact
strictly suboptimal, as the following shows.




Let d=1. Recall that for any probability measure \eta on \mathbb{R}, the
distribution function and its quantile function are given by





F_{\eta}(x)=\eta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(({-\infty},x]}}\right),\text{ }G_{\eta}(t)=\inf\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}:F_{\eta}(x)\geqslant t}}\right\},\text{ }0<t<1.



Observe that G_{\eta}(t)\leqslant x if and only if F_{\eta}(x)\geqslant t.
Hence





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t:G_{\eta}(t)>x}}\right\}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F_{\eta}(x),1}}\right).






Theorem 12.



Let \rho, \mu, \nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}}}\right). Suppose that \rho\ll dx and M_{\mu,\nu,m}<\infty for
m>2. Then with \delta=W_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\nu}}\right),




(12.6)

\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leqslant(2M_{\mu,\nu,m})^{m/[2(m-1)]}\delta^{(m-2)/[2(m-1)]}.



The exponent is sharp. Note that the constant does not depend on the source
parameters. Note that if M_{\mu,\nu,m}=0, then both targets are forced to be
the same dirac measure on the point that attains M_{\mu,\nu,m}.





Proof.



1. Let X\sim\rho, then the absolute continuity of \rho ensures
that F_{\rho}(X) is uniform on \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right) and




(12.7)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t:G_{\mu}(t)>x}}\right\}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F_{\mu}(x),1}}\right),\text{
\ }\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t:G_{\nu}(t)>x}}\right\}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F_{\nu}(x),1}}\right).



Moreover the non-decreasing maps G_{\mu}(F_{\rho}(x)), G_{\nu}(F_{\rho}(x)) pushes \rho to the targets \mu, \nu, and they are gradient of
convex functions. By uniqueness they equal T_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right),
T_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right) for \rho-a.e. x.




2. Let \gamma_{G}=(G_{\mu},G_{\nu})_{\#}dt, here dt denotes
uniform measure on \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right). We claim that the joint law
\gamma_{G} is optimal for W_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\nu}}\right). Indeed, for any
\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu), we have by Tonelli’s theorem,





\displaystyle\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-z}}\right|d\gamma(y,z)
\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|1_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y>r}}\right\}}-1_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{z>r}}\right\}}}}\right|d\gamma(y,z)dr\geqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y>r}}\right\}}-1_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{z>r}}\right\}}}}\right)d\gamma(y,z)}}\right|dr





\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mu((r,\infty))-\nu((r,\infty))|dr.



The lower bound already equals W_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\nu}}\right) by
[28, Proposition 2.17]. It remains to show that the
inequality is attained by \gamma_{G}. Note that \gamma_{G}\in\Pi(\mu,\nu).
In \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:distribution-inverse}}}\right), one of those terminal
intervals contains the other, so their symmetric difference is the interval
between the two left endpoints. It follows that





\int_{0}^{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|1_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{G_{\mu}(t)>r}}\right\}}-1_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{G_{\nu}(t)>r}}\right\}}}}\right|dt=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|F_{\mu}(r)-F_{\nu}(r)}}\right|.



For real y\geqslant z, the indicators 1_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y>r}}\right\}} and
1_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{z>r}}\right\}} differ exactly on [z,y), whose length is y-z.
So by symmetry we have




(12.8)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-z}}\right|=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|1_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y>r}}\right\}}-1_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{z>r}}\right\}}}}\right|dr.



Using the definition of \gamma_{G} and Tonelli’s theorem, we therefore have





\displaystyle\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-z}}\right|d\gamma_{G}(y,z)
\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|G_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)-G_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}}\right|dt=\int_{0}^{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|1_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{G_{\mu}(t)>r}}\right\}}-1_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{G_{\nu}(t)>r}}\right\}}}}\right|dr}}\right)dt





\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|1_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{G_{\mu}(t)>r}}\right\}}-1_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{G_{\nu}(t)>r}}\right\}}}}\right|dtdr=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|F_{\mu}(r)-F_{\nu}(r)}}\right|dr





\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mu((r,\infty))-\nu((r,\infty))|dr.



The second equality uses \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{\autoref{eq:indicators-difference}}}}\right). This shows the inequality is attained by \gamma_{G}. Note that
the same calculation applies to squared distance. Since U=F_{\rho}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right) is uniform, so with Z=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|G_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)-G_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)}}\right| we have





\mathbb{E}Z=\delta,\text{ }\mathbb{E}Z^{2}=\int_{0}^{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|G_{\mu}(t)-G_{\nu}(t)}}\right|^{2}dt=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{L^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)}^{2}.






3. Recall M_{\mu,\nu,m} from \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:M-mu-nu-pair}}}\right). The discussion at the beginning of Section section 7 shows
\max\{M_{\mu,m}(b),M_{\nu,m}(b)\} is continuous in b and coercive. So it
attains its infimum at a center b_{\ast}. Let \eta\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mu,\nu}}\right\}. By the quantile pushforward,





\int_{0}^{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|G_{\eta}(t)-b_{\ast}}}\right|^{m}dt=\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b_{\ast}}}\right|^{m}d\eta(y)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M_{\eta,m}(b_{\ast})}}\right]^{m}.



Write G_{\mu}-G_{\nu}=(G_{\mu}-b_{\ast})-(G_{\nu}-b_{\ast}). By Minkowski’s
inequality we have





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|Z}}\right\|_{m}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|G_{\mu}(t)-G_{\nu}(t)}}\right|^{m}dt}}\right)^{1/m}\leqslant M_{\mu,m}(b_{\ast})+M_{\nu,m}(b_{\ast})\leqslant 2M_{\mu,\nu,m}.



Set r_{1}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-2}}\right)/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-1}}\right), r_{2}=1/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-1}}\right). Then r_{1}+r_{2}=1, r_{1}+mr_{2}=2 and both r_{1},
r_{2} are in (0,1). Therefore we can use Hölder’s inequality with
exponents 1/r_{1}, 1/r_{2} to obtain





\mathbb{E}Z^{2}=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z^{r_{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Z^{m}}}\right)^{r_{2}}}}\right]\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}Z}}\right)^{r_{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}Z^{m}}}\right)^{r_{2}}\leqslant\delta^{(m-2)/(m-1)}(2M_{\mu,\nu,m})^{m/(m-1)}.



Taking square root yields the desired stability.




4. We now prove the sharpness of the exponent by constructing
probability marginals that saturate the exponent. In the following we fix
\rho=dx as uniform on (0,1), so the density f is constant 1. For
0<q<1, we let R=q^{-1/m} and define the targets





\mu_{q}=\delta_{0},\text{ \ \ }\nu_{q}=(1-q)\delta_{0}+q\delta_{R}.



The source has \|f\|_{p}=1 for all p>0, and \int_{0}^{1}x^{n}dx=1/(n+1). Since qR^{m}=1, both targets have bounded centralized
m-th moment. Their Brenier maps are





T_{\mu_{q}}(x)=0,\text{ \ \ }T_{\nu_{q}}(x)=R1_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x>1-q}}\right\}}.



The W_{1}-distance and the squared error are





\delta=W_{1}(\mu_{q},\nu_{q})=qR=q^{(m-1)/m},







\|T_{\mu_{q}}-T_{\nu_{q}}\|_{2}^{2}=qR^{2}=q^{(m-2)/m}=\delta^{(m-2)/(m-1)}.



For sufficiently small \delta>0, \|T_{\mu_{q}}-T_{\nu_{q}}\|_{2}=\delta^{(m-2)/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[2(m-1)}}\right]} is attained by choosing
q=\delta^{m/(m-1)}. Therefore the proof for sharpness is complete.
∎





Improvement discussion. Let k_{0} be the exponent in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:both-unbounded}}}\right). Recall that in the proof of Theorem
Theorem 11, we already know that the \alpha in k_{0} is in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right). Hence





\frac{k_{0}}{(m-2)/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[2(m-1)}}\right]}=\frac{\alpha\eta(m-1)}{m+\eta(m-1)}<1.



So the one-dimensional exponent is sharper than the general exponent given by
Theorem Theorem 11.

We compare [13, Section 4]. Its Theorem 4.3,
Remark 4.2, and Corollary 4.4 allow noncompact target supports. Write m for
the target moment order called p in Corollary 4.4, to distinguish it from
our source density exponent. All comparisons concern the L^{2}(\rho) norm of
the map difference. Constants are uniform with the stated source and target
parameters fixed. We compare orders, not optimal constants.




A.1. Corollary 4.4 with moment bounds alone




Let \rho=fdx be supported in B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,R_{X}}}\right), f\in L^{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right) and the targets \mu, \nu have
M_{m,\mu,\nu}\leqslant K_{m} for some K_{m}>0 and m>2. See \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:M-mu-nu-pair}}}\right) for the definion of M_{\mu,\nu,m} and let
b_{\ast}attain the infimum of M_{\mu,\nu,m}. Let \eta\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mu,\text{ }\nu}}\right\}. On \{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b_{\ast}}}\right|>R\},
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b_{\ast}}}\right|^{2}\leqslant R^{2-m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-b_{\ast}}}\right|^{m} gives




(A.1)

\tau_{2,\eta,b_{\ast}}(R)\leqslant K_{m}R^{2-m}.



Hence Theorem Theorem 10 applies with s=1, \beta=m-2,
J=K_{m}, and gives for some C>0,




(A.2)

\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\|_{2}\leqslant C\delta^{k_{m}}\text{\ where }k_{m}=\frac{m-2}{4m-2}.



Here we only used a bounded containing source ball, an upper density bound and
a target with moment m>2. Convexity, a positive density lower bound, and
potential regularity were not used. The constant C is uniform for fixed d,
\|f\|_{\infty}, R_{X}, m, K_{m}, independently of the target centers.




In [13, Corollary 4.4], the source instead has
two-sided positive density bounds on a compact convex set, and the target
moment order satisfies m\geqslant 4, m>d. Its map norm exponent is
m/(6m+16d). On this common class the difference of exponents is,





k_{m}-\frac{m}{6m+16d}=\frac{m^{2}+(8d-5)m-16d}{(2m-1)(6m+16d)}.



The numerator is strictly positive,





m^{2}+(8d-5)m-16d=(m-4)^{2}+(8d+3)(m-4)+16d-4>0.



Thus the map upper bound is recovered and strictly improved in small-distance
order. Our moment-only bound \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:moment-only-bound}}}\right)
also allows 2<m<4 and m\leqslant d, and drops the two source density bounds.




Corollary 4.4 in the cited paper can be strengthened. Indeed, Proposition 3.3
there (or [5, Proposition 2.22]) gives the potential
exponent \alpha=1-d/m when m>d. Substituting q=m/2 into Remark 4.2
yields





\frac{q-1}{2(q(7-4\alpha)-3)}=\frac{m-2}{2(m(7-4\alpha)-6)}=\frac{m-2}{6m+8d-12}\geqslant\frac{m}{6m+16d}\text{.}



The inequality is valid under Corollary 4.4’s m\geqslant 4. So this gives a
stronger refinement. For every m>\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2,d}}\right\}, our new
exponent k_{m} still strictly exceeds this refinement,





k_{m}-\frac{m-2}{6m+8d-12}=\frac{m-2}{4m-2}-\frac{m-2}{6m+8d-12}=\frac{(m-2)(2m+8d-10)}{(4m-2)(6m+8d-12)}>0.







A.2. Theorem 4.3 and Remark 4.2 with potential regularity




Since this might be useful beyond the comparison, we state it as a theorem.
Recall that our moment order m is 2q there.




Theorem 13.



Suppose now that \rho=fdx is supported on a compact convex body
\mathcal{X}, f is bounded f\leqslant M_{\rho} on \mathcal{X} for some
M_{\rho}>0, and the targets \mu,\nu have M_{\mu,\nu,m}\leqslant K_{m}<\infty for some m\geqslant 2 and K_{m}>0. Suppose that both Brenier
potentials u_{\eta}, \eta\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mu,\text{ }\nu}}\right\} satisfy,
for fixed H_{\alpha}>0 and 0<\alpha<1,




(A.3)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|u_{\eta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)-u_{\eta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right|\leqslant H_{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-x^{\prime}}}\right|^{\alpha},\text{ }\forall x,\text{ }x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}.



Let





\kappa=\frac{1}{1-\alpha},\text{ }\ell=\max\{m,\kappa\}.



Then for m>2,




(A.4)

\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\|_{2}\leqslant C\delta^{k_{\ell}}\text{ where
}k_{\ell}=\frac{\ell-2}{4\ell-2}.



The stability continues to hold at m=2 if \alpha>1/2. The constant C>0
depends only on m,K_{m},\alpha,H_{\alpha}, the source geometry, and the
density upper bound. No target support bound is imposed.





Remark 7.



Theorem 4.3 in the cited paper assumes in addition a finite 4-th moment and
obtains the exponent 1/[2(11-8\alpha)]. If we do not use the Hölder
bound and only use its finite 4-th moment assumption for the targets, then
the moment-only exponent \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:moment-only-bound}}}\right),
k_{4}=1/7, dominate the exponent there only when \alpha<15/16. The two
exponents equal at \alpha=15/16.





Proof.



1. The case m>2. If \kappa\leqslant m, then \ell=m. We
use the moment bound condition. Since M_{\mu,\nu,m}\leqslant K_{m} implies
that the tail bound \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:moment-tail-bound}}}\right) holds. Now
using Theorem Theorem 10 with s=1, \beta=\ell-2=m-2 and
J=K_{m} or simply using \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:moment-only-bound}}}\right), we
obtain the desired stability \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:higher-regularity-bound}}}\right) for \kappa\leqslant m. If instead \kappa>m, then \ell=\kappa. First we
derive a target tail bound from the Hölder assumption. At an interior
differentiable point x, let y=\nabla u_{\eta}(x). If y\neq 0, the point
x+r\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)y/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y}}\right| belongs to \mathcal{X}.
The subgradient inequality and the Hölder continuity \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:Holder-potential}}}\right) give





r\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y}}\right|\leqslant u_{\eta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x+r\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)y/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y}}\right|}}\right)-u_{\eta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\leqslant H_{\alpha}r\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)^{\alpha}.



Thus




(A.5)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y}}\right|\leqslant H_{\alpha}r(x)^{\alpha-1}.



It also holds for p=0. This is the erosion mechanism of [13, Propositions
4.5–4.6]. The calculation here records the
constants needed for our tail estimation. Since a compact convex domain is
Lipschitz, the geometric layer estimate \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:LM-boundary-layer}}}\right) holds with a domain-dependent constant K_{\mathcal{X}}>0. The
constant may not be optimal for convex domain, but it is sufficient for our
uses. The layer estimate also gives \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\partial\mathcal{X}}}\right|=0 by letting t\rightarrow 0, so the preceding argument covers \rho-a.e.
x. Let C_{H}=M_{\rho}K_{\mathcal{X}}H_{\alpha}^{\kappa}. Since (\nabla u_{\eta})_{\#}\rho=\eta, by \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:y-rx}}}\right) we have for
R>0 that





\eta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y}}\right|>R}}\right)=\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\nabla u_{\eta}}}\right|>R}}\right)\leqslant\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)<\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(H_{\alpha}/R}}\right)^{\kappa}}}\right)\leqslant C_{H}R^{-\kappa}.



Since m>2, so \kappa>2. Applying Tonelli’s theorem to





|y|^{2}1_{\{|y|>R\}}=R^{2}1_{\{|y|>R\}}+\int_{R}^{\infty}2t1_{\{|y|>t\}}dt



we get




(A.6)

\displaystyle\tau_{2,\eta,0}(R)
\displaystyle=R^{2}\eta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y}}\right|>R}}\right)+\int_{R}^{\infty}2t\eta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y}}\right|>t}}\right)dt





\displaystyle\leqslant C_{H}R^{2-\kappa}+2C_{H}\int_{R}^{\infty}t^{1-\kappa}dt=C_{H}\frac{\kappa}{\kappa-2}R^{-(\kappa-2)}.



This is a tail bound at the exponent \kappa-2. Using Theorem
Theorem 10 with s=1, \beta=\kappa-2=\ell-2, J=C_{H}\kappa/(\kappa-2), we therefore get the stability \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:higher-regularity-bound}}}\right) for \kappa>m.




2. The case m=2. If \alpha>1/2, then \kappa>2=m, so the
tail bound \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:squared-tail-bound}}}\right) we just derived
continues to hold and yields the same stability bound with strictly positive
exponent k_{\ell}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\kappa-2}}\right)/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(4\kappa-2}}\right)=(2\alpha-1)/[2(1+\alpha)]>0.
∎





Improvement discussion. We now check explicitly that \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:higher-regularity-bound}}}\right) improves the cited Remark 4.2
exponent





k_{\mathrm{DM}}(m,\alpha)=\frac{m-2}{2(m(7-4\alpha)-6)}.



First we compare the case m>2. The denominator of k_{\mathrm{DM}} is
positive, since 7-4\alpha>3 and m>2. If \kappa\leqslant m, then
\ell=m. We form the difference





k_{\ell}-k_{\mathrm{DM}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{m-2}{4m-2}-\frac{m-2}{2(m(7-4\alpha)-6)}}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m-2}}\right)\cdot\frac{2(m(7-4\alpha)-6)-(4m-2)}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(4m-2}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[2(m(7-4\alpha)-6)}}\right]}.



By the definition of \kappa and that \kappa\leqslant m we have
\alpha\leqslant 1-1/m. So the numerator is positive,





\displaystyle 2(m(7-4\alpha)-6)-(4m-2)
\displaystyle=2[m(5-4\alpha)-5]





\displaystyle\geqslant 2[m(1+4/m)-5]=2(m-1)>0.



Thus the new exponent k_{\ell} is strictly larger than k_{\mathrm{DM}}. If
\kappa>m, then \ell=\kappa. Substituting \alpha=1-1/\kappa yields





k_{\ell}-k_{\mathrm{DM}}=\frac{\kappa-2}{4\kappa-2}-\frac{m-2}{2(3m+4m/\kappa-6)}=\frac{(\kappa-2)(3m+4m/\kappa-6)-(m-2)(2\kappa-1)}{2(2\kappa-1)(3m+4m/\kappa-6)}.



Since m>2, the numerator is strictly positive,





(\kappa-2)(3m+4m/\kappa-6)-(m-2)(2\kappa-1)=(m-1)(m-2)+(\kappa-m)(m-2+8/\kappa)>0.



The common denominator is also strictly positive, since \kappa>m>2. This
shows strict improvement in both cases. In particular, under Theorem 4.3’s
4th-moment assumption, m=4. The equation \kappa=m=4 then gives
\alpha=3/4. So our stability exponent is





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}1/7,&0<\alpha\leqslant 3/4,\\
(2\alpha-1)/[2(1+\alpha)],&3/4<\alpha<1.\end{array}}}\right.



In the second line, we have substituted \kappa=1/(1-\alpha) into k_{\ell}=k_{\kappa}=(\kappa-2)/(4\kappa-2). The two expressions agree at
\alpha=3/4. The preceding comparison with m=4 proves that they strictly
improve 1/[2(11-8\alpha)] throughout 0<\alpha<1. When m=2, this is
Remark 4.2 at q=1, the exponent there is zero. If \alpha>1/2, then
\kappa>2=m, our exponent k_{\ell}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\kappa-2}}\right)/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(4\kappa-2}}\right)=(2\alpha-1)/[2(1+\alpha)] is still strictly positive.






Appendix B Comparisons with Letrouit-Mérigot [19]




We now compare with the Brenier map stability estimates [19, Theorem 1.4
(1.7), Theorem 1.7 (1.10), Theorem 1.10 (1.13), Theorem 1.11 (1.15) and
Theorem 1.12 (1.17)]. As already mentioned in its Remark
1.5, these stabilities are extendible to non-compact targets under additional
moments via the route of [13, Section 4]. For
comparison purpose we only treat compact targets, non-compact targets can be
treated as in Theorem Theorem 10 or Theorem
Theorem 11. Therefore we assume that throughout this section,
the targets \mu, \nu lie in one fixed common ball B(b,R_{Y}), R_{Y}>0,
and \delta=W_{1}(\mu,\nu).




B.1. Source-tail envelopes




For rapidly decaying source density, the annular estimate \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:A-annuli}}}\right) with p=\infty retains the linear bound,





\mathfrak{A}_{\rho}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right)\leqslant Bh,\text{ where }B=K_{d,\infty}^{\mathrm{ann}}W_{p,a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f}}\right)



With target measures supported in B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b,R_{Y}}}\right), R_{Y}>0, the
error bound Theorem Theorem 1 therefore gives, for every feasible
\sigma\in\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho,\nu}}\right),





\mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)\leqslant 4R_{Y}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)/h+18BR_{Y}^{2}h,\text{ }h>0.



Choosing the minimizing h gives




(B.1)

\mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)\leqslant C_{E}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)^{1/2},\text{ where }C_{E}=12\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2}}\right)^{1/2}B^{1/2}R_{Y}^{3/2}.






Theorem 14 (Gaussian upper envelope).



Let \rho=fdx and suppose, for
some a\in\mathbb{R}^{d} and C_{0}, \kappa>0, that





0\leqslant f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\leqslant C_{0}e^{-\kappa\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-a}}\right|^{2}/2},\text{ for almost every }x.



Let \mu, \nu be supported in B(b,R_{Y}), R_{Y}>0. Then, with
\delta=W_{1}(\mu,\nu),




(B.2)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{L^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)}\leqslant C\delta^{1/4}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c/\delta}}\right)}}\right]^{1/8}.



Constants C>0, c\geqslant e depend only on d, C_{0}, \kappa, R_{Y}, not on the center a or on the two target measures.





Proof.



The envelope implies finite moments of every order. For the annuli in
Proposition 4 with p=\infty,





W_{\infty,a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f}}\right)\leqslant C_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\sum_{k\geqslant 1}2^{k\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)}e^{-\kappa 4^{k}/2}}}\right)\triangleq W<\infty.



The sum converges by ratio test. Thus the preceding estimate \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:E-sqrtD}}}\right) applies with B=K_{d,\infty}^{\mathrm{ann}}W.
First compute, for S\geqslant 0,





\displaystyle\tau_{1,\rho,a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)
\displaystyle=\int_{|u|>S}|u|f(a+u)du\leqslant C_{0}d\omega_{d}\int_{S}^{\infty}r^{d}e^{-\kappa r^{2}/2}dr





\displaystyle\leqslant C_{0}d\omega_{d}e^{-\kappa S^{2}/4}\int_{S}^{\infty}r^{d}e^{-\kappa r^{2}/4}dr\text{ (write }e^{-\kappa r^{2}/2}=e^{-\kappa r^{2}/4}\cdot e^{-\kappa r^{2}/4}\text{)}





\displaystyle\leqslant C_{0}d\omega_{d}e^{-\kappa S^{2}/4}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d}e^{-\kappa r^{2}/4}dr\text{ }





\displaystyle=C_{0}d\omega_{d}e^{-\kappa S^{2}/4}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1/2}}\right)(4/\kappa)^{(d+1)/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}w^{(d-1)/2}e^{-w}dw\text{ (let }w=\kappa r^{2}/4\text{)}





\displaystyle=C_{0}d\omega_{d}e^{-\kappa S^{2}/4}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1/2}}\right)(4/\kappa)^{(d+1)/2}\Gamma((d+1)/2).



Consequently





\tau_{1,\rho,a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)\leqslant C_{1}e^{-c_{1}S^{2}},



where c_{1}=\kappa/4, C_{1}=C_{0}d\omega_{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1/2}}\right)(4/\kappa)^{(d+1)/2}\Gamma((d+1)/2). Let now \gamma be optimal for
W_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\nu}}\right) and construct \sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma} as in
Definition Definition 3. For 0<\delta\leqslant 1, let





S=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ell/c_{1}}}\right)^{1/2},\text{ }\ell=\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c/\delta}}\right),\text{ }c=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,2R_{Y}}}\right\},C_{1}}}\right\}.



Since \delta cannot exceed 2R_{Y}, we have \ell\geqslant 1 and





\tau_{1,\rho,a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)\leqslant C_{1}e^{-\ell}=(C_{1}/c)\delta\leqslant\delta.



Proposition 9 therefore yields





\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma})\leqslant 2\delta(S+2R_{Y})=2\delta(c_{1}^{-1/2}\ell^{1/2}+2R_{Y})\leqslant C_{D}\delta\ell^{1/2},



where C_{D}=2(c_{1}^{-1/2}+2R_{Y}). Since





\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-z}}\right|^{2}d\gamma\leqslant 2R_{Y}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|y-z}}\right|d\gamma=2R_{Y}\delta,



\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:map-graph}}}\right) and \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:E-sqrtD}}}\right)
imply





\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\leqslant 4R_{Y}\delta+2C_{E}C_{D}^{1/2}\delta^{1/2}\ell^{1/4}\leqslant(4R_{Y}+2C_{E}C_{D}^{1/2})\delta^{1/2}\ell^{1/4}.



Taking square roots yields





\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leqslant(4R_{Y}+2C_{E}C_{D}^{1/2})^{1/2}\delta^{1/4}\ell^{1/8}.



For \delta=0, \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:logconcave-map}}}\right) is trivial. For
1<\delta\leqslant 2R_{Y}, the chosen c still gives \ell\geqslant 1. So





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leqslant 2R_{Y}\leqslant 2R_{Y}\delta^{1/4}\ell^{1/8}.



Thus choosing C=\max\{(4R_{Y}+2C_{E}C_{D}^{1/2})^{1/2},\,2R_{Y}\} proves
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:logconcave-map}}}\right).
∎





Corollary 3 (Bounded perturbations of strongly log-concave densities).



Let \rho=fdx and suppose that the density
f=e^{-U-F} with U\in C^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right), D^{2}U\geqslant\kappa I_{d}, \kappa>0 and F\in L^{\infty}. Let \mu, \nu be
supported in B(b,R_{Y}). Then \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:logconcave-map}}}\right)
holds with C_{0}=e^{-U\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a}}\right)+\|F\|_{\infty}}, where a
is the unique minimizer of U. No upper Hessian bound is required.





Proof.



The lower Hessian bound implies, for every x,





U(x)\geqslant U(z)+\nabla U(z)\cdot(x-z)+\frac{\kappa}{2}|x-z|^{2},



where z is the unique minimizer of U. Using -F(x)\leqslant\|F\|_{\infty}, we obtain





f(x)=e^{-U(x)-F(x)}\leqslant e^{-U(a)+\|F\|_{\infty}}e^{-\kappa|x-a|^{2}/2},\text{ for almost every }x.



This is the Gaussian upper envelope with the stated C_{0}, therefore
Theorem Theorem 14 applies.
∎





Improvement discussion. Under these strong log-concavity and bounded
perturbation assumptions, [19, Theorem 1.4 (1.7)]
additionally assumes D^{2}U\leqslant\kappa^{\prime}I_{d} for its map
estimate and obtains





q_{\mathrm{LM}}=\frac{\kappa}{2\kappa^{\prime}+7\kappa}\leqslant\frac{1}{9}.



The accompanying refinement there also allows any positive exponent
q<\kappa/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\kappa^{\prime}+6\kappa}}\right)\leqslant 1/8. For either
choice, q<1/4. Thus \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:logconcave-map}}}\right) improves
these map exponents in the small \delta range.




Theorem 15 (Polynomial upper envelope).



Let \rho=fdx and suppose for
some a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}, \beta>d+2, C_{0}>0 that





0\leqslant f(x)\leqslant C_{0}(1+|x-a|^{2})^{-\beta/2},\text{ for almost every
}x.



Let \mu, \nu be supported in B(b,R_{Y}), R_{Y}>0. Then with
\delta=W_{1}(\mu,\nu),




(B.3)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leqslant C\delta^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\beta-d-1}}\right)/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(4\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\beta-d}}\right)}}\right)}.



The constant depends only on d, \beta, C_{0}, R_{Y}. No density lower
bound is assumed.





Proof.



Write t=\beta-d>2. The envelope implies \rho\in\mathcal{P}_{2} and





W_{\infty,a}(f)\leqslant C_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\sum_{k\geqslant 1}2^{k(d-1-\beta)}}}\right)\triangleq W.



The geometric series converges, so \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:E-sqrtD}}}\right) applies
with B=K_{d,\infty}^{\mathrm{ann}}W. For S\geqslant 1,





\displaystyle\tau_{1,\rho,a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)
\displaystyle=\int_{|u|>S}|u|f(a+u)\,du\leqslant C_{0}d\omega_{d}\int_{S}^{\infty}r^{d}(1+r^{2})^{-\beta/2}\,dr





\displaystyle\leqslant C_{0}d\omega_{d}\int_{S}^{\infty}r^{d-\beta}\,dr=C_{0}d\omega_{d}\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\frac{R^{d-\beta+1}-S^{d-\beta+1}}{d-\beta+1}





\displaystyle=\frac{C_{0}d\omega_{d}}{\beta-d-1}S^{-(\beta-d-1)}=C_{\tau}S^{-(t-1)}.



where C_{\tau}=C_{0}d\omega_{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t-1}}\right)^{-1}. Let \gamma be
optimal for W_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\nu}}\right) and construct \sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma} as in Definition Definition 3. For 0<\delta\leqslant 1,
choose S=\delta^{-1/t}\geqslant 1. Then





\delta S=\delta^{(t-1)/t},\text{ }S^{-(t-1)}=\delta^{(t-1)/t}.



Thus Proposition 9 gives





\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(\sigma_{T_{\mu},\gamma})\leqslant 2\{\delta S+2R_{Y}C_{\tau}S^{-(t-1)}\}\leqslant C_{D}\delta^{(t-1)/t},



where C_{D}=2(1+2R_{Y}C_{\tau}). Using \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:map-graph}}}\right)
and \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:E-sqrtD}}}\right) gives





\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\leqslant 4R_{Y}\delta+2C_{E}C_{D}^{1/2}\delta^{(t-1)/(2t)}\leqslant(4R_{Y}+2C_{E}C_{D}^{1/2})\delta^{(t-1)/(2t)}.



Here 0<(t-1)/(2t)<1 and \delta\leqslant 1. Taking square roots gives the
exponent





\frac{t-1}{4t}=\frac{\beta-d-1}{4(\beta-d)}.



At \delta=0, uniqueness gives equality of the maps. For \delta>1, use
\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leqslant 2R_{Y}\leqslant 2R_{Y}\delta. Therefore C=\max\{(4R_{Y}+2C_{E}C_{D}^{1/2})^{1/2},2R_{Y}\} works for
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:cauchy-map}}}\right).
∎





Improvement discussion. Under two-sided comparability with
(1+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x}}\right|^{2})^{-\beta/2}, [19, Theorem 1.12
(1.17)] gives the compact target map exponent \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\beta-d-2}}\right)/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(8\beta-2d-4}}\right). For t=\beta-d>2, the
improvement in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:cauchy-map}}}\right) is strict,





\frac{t-1}{4t}-\frac{t-2}{8t+6d-4}=\frac{4t^{2}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(6d-4}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t-1}}\right)}{4t\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(8t+6d-4}}\right)}>0.



Our theorem also removes the density lower bound.





B.2. John domains




In [19, Theorem 1.7], the source \rho has a density f
bounded above and below by positive constants on a bounded John domain
\mathcal{X}. The map stability conclusion there additionally assumes
rectifiable boundary of finite \mathcal{H}^{d-1} measure and has
L^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)-norm exponent 1/6. For this setting, the
small-set condition is satisfied, since for every Borel E, it holds





\rho(E)=\int_{E\cap\mathcal{X}}fdx\leqslant\|f\|_{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|E}}\right|.



So Theorem Theorem 8 applies with s=1, C_{V}=\|f\|_{\infty} and gives




(B.4)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{2}\leqslant C\delta^{1/4}.



Thus the map estimate [19, Theorem 1.7 (1.10)] is
strictly improved in the small \delta order. Neither the source density
lower bound, the John domain condition, connectedness, nor boundary
rectifiability is used in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:LM-John-improved}}}\right), only a
ball containing the source and an upper density bound are used. In particular
the same comparison applies to the finite union extension in [19, Section
4.3]. Note that the improvement in this setting has
already been made recently in [23, Theorem 3.1] as remarked
in Theorem Theorem 8.





B.3. Boundary powers




To avoid conflicting notation, we write \zeta for the boundary exponent
called \delta in [19, Theorem 1.10 (1.13)], while
keeping \delta for W_{1}(\mu,\nu) here. Let \mathcal{X} be a bounded
Lipschitz domain, r(x)=\operatorname{dist}(x,\partial\mathcal{X}), and
suppose the probability density, extended by zero outside \mathcal{X},
satisfies for \zeta>-1,




(B.5)

c_{1}r(x)^{\zeta}\leqslant f(x)\leqslant c_{2}r(x)^{\zeta}\text{, }x\in\mathcal{X}.



Note that only the upper bound will be used below. The map exponent in
[19, Theorem 1.10] is




(B.6)

q_{\mathrm{LM}}(\zeta)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}(1+\zeta)/6,&-1<\zeta\leqslant 0,\\
(2+\zeta)/[12(1+\zeta)],&\zeta\geqslant 0.\end{array}}}\right.



The two expressions agree at zero.




Nonnegative boundary powers. Assume \zeta\geqslant 0. Let
D_{\mathcal{X}}=\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{X})>0. Since r(x)\leqslant D_{\mathcal{X}}, the density is bounded by c_{2}D_{\mathcal{X}}^{\zeta}.
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:LM-John-improved}}}\right) therefore applies without using
the lower bound or the Lipschitz condition. The exact exponent difference is




(B.7)

\frac{1}{4}-\frac{2+\zeta}{12(1+\zeta)}=\frac{1+2\zeta}{12(1+\zeta)}>0.






Negative boundary powers and the endpoint small-set estimate. Assume
-1<\zeta<0. Recall the boundary layer geometric estimate from [16, 3.2.37] or [2, Theorem 2.106]: there
are t_{0}>0 and K_{\mathcal{X}}>0 depending only on the domain, such that
for 0<t\leqslant t_{0},




(B.8)

V(t)\triangleq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}:r\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)<t}}\right\}}}\right|\leqslant K_{\mathcal{X}}t.



After replacing K_{\mathcal{X}} with a larger constant, e.g., \max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{K_{\mathcal{X}},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{X}}}\right|/t_{0}}}\right\}, we may
assume that the estimate holds for all t>0. For 0<r<t, the identity





r^{\zeta}=t^{\zeta}+(-\zeta)\int_{r}^{t}u^{\zeta-1}\,du



has nonnegative terms on the right. Since r(x)>0 for x\in\mathcal{X},
multiplying the identity by the layer indicator gives the pointwise identity
on \mathcal{X}





1_{\{r(x)<t\}}r(x)^{\zeta}=t^{\zeta}1_{\{r(x)<t\}}+(-\zeta)\int_{0}^{t}1_{\{r(x)<u\}}u^{\zeta-1}\,du.



Integrate the two terms over \mathcal{X}. Tonelli’s theorem allows the
interchange of integtation in the second term





\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{t}1_{\{r(x)<u\}}u^{\zeta-1}dudx=\int_{0}^{t}u^{\zeta-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{X}}1_{\{r(x)<u\}}dx}}\right)du=\int_{0}^{t}u^{\zeta-1}V(u)du.



The first term integrates to t^{\zeta}V(t). \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:LM-boundary-layer}}}\right) consequently gives




(B.9)

\displaystyle\int_{\{r<t\}}r(x)^{\zeta}dx
\displaystyle=t^{\zeta}V(t)+(-\zeta)\int_{0}^{t}u^{\zeta-1}V(u)du





\displaystyle\leqslant K_{\mathcal{X}}t^{1+\zeta}+\frac{-\zeta}{1+\zeta}K_{\mathcal{X}}t^{1+\zeta}=\frac{K_{\mathcal{X}}}{1+\zeta}t^{1+\zeta}.



The integral at t=0 is finite because 1+\zeta>0. Fix a Borel set E. If
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|E}}\right|>0, choose t=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|E}}\right|/K_{\mathcal{X}}. Split E\cap\mathcal{X} at distance t from the boundary.
On \{r\geqslant t\}, r^{\zeta}\leqslant t^{\zeta}. On \{r<t\}, \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:LM-weighted-layer}}}\right) applies. Hence




(B.10)

\rho(E)\leqslant c_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{K_{\mathcal{X}}}{1+\zeta}t^{1+\zeta}+t^{\zeta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|E}}\right|}}\right\}=c_{2}\frac{2+\zeta}{1+\zeta}K_{\mathcal{X}}^{-\zeta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|E}}\right|^{1+\zeta}.



For \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|E}}\right|=0, absolute continuity gives \rho(E)=0. Thus
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:small-set-density}}}\right) holds with 0<s=1+\zeta<1 and
C_{V}=c_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2+\zeta}}\right)K_{\mathcal{X}}^{-\zeta}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\zeta}}\right). So Theorem Theorem 8 gives,




(B.11)

\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\|_{2}\leqslant C\delta^{(1+\zeta)/[2(2+\zeta)]},\text{ }-1<\zeta<0.



This exponent exceeds q_{\mathrm{LM}} in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:LM-boundary-old}}}\right), since





\frac{1+\zeta}{2(2+\zeta)}-\frac{1+\zeta}{6}=\frac{(1+\zeta)(1-\zeta)}{6(2+\zeta)}>0.



The constant C of \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:LM-boundary-improved}}}\right) are
controlled by the upper density coefficient, \zeta, \mathcal{X}, d and
the target radius R_{Y}. The lower bound of \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:boundary-powers}}}\right) is not used. We may also replace Lipschitz
geometry by the explicit layer estimate \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:LM-boundary-layer}}}\right).





B.4. Spherical uniform density




The source in [19, Theorem 1.11 (1.15)] is




(B.12)

f(x)=c_{d}|x|^{1-d}1_{\{0<|x|\leqslant 1\}},



where c_{d}=(d\omega_{d})^{-1}. By polar coordinates, for 0\leqslant r\leqslant 1,




(B.13)

\int_{B(0,r)}f(x)dx=c_{d}d\omega_{d}\int_{0}^{r}t^{1-d}t^{d-1}dt=r.



The exponent of [19, Theorem 1.11 (1.15)] is 1/(6d),
which depends on the dimension. We provide two versions of improvement of the
exponent, one depends on the dimension and the other is independent of the dimension.




L^{p} bound alone gives improved dimension-dependent exponent. For
d\geqslant 2, using polar integration we have





\|f\|_{p}^{p}=c_{d}^{p}d\omega_{d}\int_{0}^{1}r^{d-1-p(d-1)}dr=\frac{c_{d}^{p}d\omega_{d}}{d-p(d-1)},\text{ if }1<p<\frac{d}{d-1}.



The integral diverges at p=d/(d-1). Hence s=1-1/p ranges over (0,1/d).
So the bound of L^{p}-norm of f depends on the dimension. Applying
Theorem Theorem 8, we get a stability with the norm exponent
s/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s+1}}\right)}}\right]\triangleq q ranges over
0<q<1/[2(d+1)]. Clearly the exponent obtained this way depends on the
dimension. Since





\frac{1}{2(d+1)}-\frac{1}{6d}=\frac{2d-1}{6d(d+1)}>0,



hence any exponent q strictly between these two numbers give rise to an
improve of [19, Theorem 1.11 (1.15)]. Next we show that
how to obtain a dimension-free exponent.




A stronger estimate from shrinking shells. The integrated oscillation
estimate \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:osc-int}}}\right) retains more information than the
L^{p} bound. Let d\geqslant 1, 0<h\leqslant 1 and set





N=\lfloor\log_{2}(1/h)\rfloor\text{ and }r_{j}=2^{-j},\text{ }j\geqslant 0.



Since \log_{2}(1/h)-1<N\leqslant\log_{2}(1/h), we have h\leqslant r_{N}<2h. Let A_{j}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{r_{j+1}<\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x}}\right|\leqslant r_{j}}}\right\}, then A_{j}\subset B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,r_{j}}}\right) and





B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right)=B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,r_{N}}}\right)\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bigcup_{0\leqslant j<N}A_{j}}}\right).



For each 1-Lipschitz convex v, we use \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:omega-2L}}}\right)
and \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:LM-f-spherical-integral}}}\right) to get the integrated
oscillation on B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,r_{N}}}\right),





\int_{B(0,r_{N})}\omega_{v,h}^{2}fdx\leqslant 4r_{N}\leqslant 8h.



On each A_{j} with 0\leqslant j<N, the density definition yields
f\leqslant c_{d}r_{j+1}^{1-d}. So using \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:osc-int}}}\right)
with r=2 and S=r_{j}, we obtain





\int_{A_{j}}\omega_{v,h}^{2}fdx\leqslant c_{d}r_{j+1}^{1-d}\int_{B(0,r_{j})}\omega_{v,h}^{2}dx\leqslant c_{d}K_{d,2}^{\mathrm{osc}}r_{j+1}^{1-d}r_{j}^{d-1}h=c_{d}2^{d-1}K_{d,2}^{\mathrm{osc}}h.



Taking the supremum over v, we obtain for 0<h\leqslant 1,




(B.14)

\mathfrak{A}_{\rho}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right)\leqslant 8h+Nc_{d}2^{d-1}K_{d,2}^{\mathrm{osc}}h\leqslant B_{d}h\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e/h}}\right)\leqslant B_{d}h\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c/h}}\right),



where B_{d}=8+c_{d}2^{d-1}K_{d,2}^{\mathrm{osc}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log 2}}\right)^{-1}
and c=e\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,2R_{Y}}}\right\}. To prove the map stability, let
\gamma be optimal for W_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\nu}}\right). The source is support
in B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,R_{X}}}\right) with R_{X}=1, so \mathcal{D}_{\nu}(\sigma_{\gamma})\leqslant 2\delta. As in the proof of Corollary
Corollary 1 the tail term there vanishes for compact target with
the choice R=R_{Y}. Substituting \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:LM-spherical-A}}}\right)
into \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:error-bound}}}\right) and substituting the resulting
inequality into \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:map-graph}}}\right), we get for
0<h\leqslant 1,




(B.15)

\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant 4R_{Y}\delta+16R_{Y}\delta/h+36R_{Y}^{2}B_{d}h\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c/h}}\right).



For 0<\delta\leqslant 1, let \ell=\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c/\delta}}\right). Then
\ell>1 and \ell>\delta. We now choose h=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\delta/\ell}}\right)^{1/2}, this h belongs to (0,1]. By direct calculations we have





\log(c/h)=\frac{2\log c+\log\ell-\log\delta}{2}=\frac{2\log c+\log\ell-\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c/e^{\ell}}}\right)}{2}=\frac{\ell+\log c+\log\ell}{2}\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+e^{-1}}}\right)\ell,



where the inequality uses \log\ell\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+e^{-1}}}\right)\ell-\log c. We also have





\delta\leqslant\sqrt{\delta\ell},\text{ }\delta/h=\sqrt{\delta\ell},\text{
}h\log(c/h)\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+e^{-1}}}\right)h\ell=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+e^{-1}}}\right)\sqrt{\delta\ell}.



Substituting this h into \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:LM-T2-bound-pre}}}\right) therefore gives,




(B.16)

\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\|_{2}\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+e^{-1}}}\right)^{1/2}(20R_{Y}+36R_{Y}^{2}B_{d})^{1/2}\delta^{1/4}[\log(c/\delta)]^{1/4}.



For \delta=0, the map error is zero. For 1<\delta\leqslant 2R_{Y} (\delta
cannot exceed 2R_{Y}), the chosen c has \ell\geqslant 1. So





\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\|_{2}\leqslant 2R_{Y}\leqslant 2R_{Y}\delta^{1/4}[\log(c/\delta)]^{1/4}.



Therefore for all admissible \delta\geqslant 0, we have the modulus
\delta^{1/4}[\log(c/\delta)]^{1/4}. Since 1/4-1/(6d)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(3d-2}}\right)/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(12d}}\right)>0 for all d\geqslant 1, so the modulus of \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:LM-spherical-map}}}\right) improves the cited exponent 1/(6d) for
all d\geqslant 1 in the small \delta range.




The even stronger one-dimensional case. When d=1, \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:LM-spherical-density}}}\right) is uniform on (-1,1) with bounded
density 1/2, and \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:LM-John-improved}}}\right) gives 1/4
without a logarithm. In fact all three comparisons from Section
subsection B.2, Section subsection B.3 to Section
subsection B.4 have a stronger one-dimensional conclusion. As proved
in Section subsection 12.1, the common source increasing maps induce the
monotone W_{1}-optimal target coupling. For targets in B(b,R_{Y}),





\|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}\|_{2}^{2}=\int|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}|^{2}d\rho\leqslant 2R_{Y}\int|T_{\mu}-T_{\nu}|\,d\rho=2R_{Y}\delta.



Thus the norm exponent is 1/2 in dimension one, for any absolutely
continuous source and compactly supported targets.






Appendix C From objective gap to map accuracy




Dual-based algorithms for optimal transport usually use primal-dual gap as
stopping criterion. An approximate optimal map is then constructed, e.g.,
through barycentric projection. The purpose of this section is to demonstrate
turning a primal-dual gap into an error bound for barycentric projection map.
Let \Psi_{\rho,\nu}(z) denote the infimum on the right of \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:error-bound}}}\right), with objective gap replaced by z\geqslant 0.
It is nondecreasing, since every expression being minimized is nondecreasing.




Corollary 4.



Fix \rho,\nu satisfying Assumption 1,
a plan \sigma\in\Pi(\rho,\nu), and integrable functions \phi\in L^{1}(\rho), \psi\in L^{1}(\nu) such that \phi(x)+\psi(y)\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x-y}}\right|^{2}/2 for every x,y. Let





\epsilon=\frac{1}{2}\int|x-y|^{2}\,d\sigma-\int\phi\,d\rho-\int\psi\,d\nu



be the primal-dual gap. Then 0\leqslant\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(\sigma)\leqslant\epsilon and





\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\bar{T}_{\sigma}-T_{\nu}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+V_{\sigma}=\mathcal{E}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)\leqslant\Psi_{\rho,\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\epsilon}}\right).







Proof.



It is a result of Theorem Theorem 1, the monotonicity of
\Psi_{\rho,\nu} and \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:variance-decomposition}}}\right).
∎





Corollary 5.



Suppose the setting of
Corollary Corollary 4 holds for a family \sigma_{t}\in\Pi(\rho,\nu) of plans with the corresponding primal-dual gaps \epsilon_{t}\leqslant C_{0}t^{\zeta}, where C_{0},\zeta>0. If \Psi_{\rho,\nu}(z)\leqslant Kz^{\Theta} for z\geqslant 0, then





\|\bar{T}_{\sigma_{t}}-T_{\nu}\|_{2}\leqslant K^{1/2}C_{0}^{\Theta/2}t^{\zeta\Theta/2}\text{ and }V_{\sigma_{t}}\leqslant KC_{0}^{\Theta}t^{\zeta\Theta}.



For example, under Theorem Theorem 3 take \Theta=\Theta_{\eta,m} and K=Q_{d,\rho,p,n}. For the logarithmic class, replace
Kz^{\Theta} by C_{\mathrm{up}}\varphi(z) on 0<z\leqslant t_{\mathrm{up}}, as in \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:explicit-upper-constant}}}\right).





Proof.



The bounds follow from the proof of Theorem Theorem 3 and
Section section 10.
∎






Appendix D An optimization of sum of powers




Lemma 7.



Let A,B,\alpha,\beta>0. Then




(D.1)

\inf_{t>0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{At^{\alpha}+Bt^{-\beta}}}\right\}=c_{\alpha,\beta}A^{\beta/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha+\beta}}\right)}B^{\alpha/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha+\beta}}\right)},






(D.2)

c_{\alpha,\beta}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}}\right)^{\alpha/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha+\beta}}\right)}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}}\right)^{\beta/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha+\beta}}\right)}.



The unique minimizer is




(D.3)

t_{\ast}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta B}{\alpha A}}}\right)^{1/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha+\beta}}\right)}.







Proof.



For \Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=At^{\alpha}+Bt^{-\beta},





\Phi^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=\alpha At^{\alpha-1}-\beta Bt^{-\beta-1}.



Thus \Phi^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=0 if and only if





\alpha At^{\alpha+\beta}=\beta B,



which gives \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:two-power-minimizer}}}\right). Since
\Phi(t)\rightarrow+\infty at both endpoints 0 and +\infty, this critical
point is the global minimizer. Substitution gives





\displaystyle At_{\ast}^{\alpha}
\displaystyle=A\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta B}{\alpha A}}}\right)^{\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}}\right)^{\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)}A^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)}B^{\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)},




\displaystyle Bt_{\ast}^{-\beta}
\displaystyle=B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta B}{\alpha A}}}\right)^{-\beta/(\alpha+\beta)}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}}\right)^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)}A^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)}B^{\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)}.



Adding these two identities proves \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\autoref{eq:two-power}}}\right).
∎
