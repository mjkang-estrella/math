Quantitative Stability, Coercivity and Uniqueness of Optimal Transport PlansThanks: 1CMAP École Polytechnique, Palaiseau, France. Email: william.ford@polytechnique.edu



William Ford1




Abstract.
    

For probability measures on \mathbb{R}^{d} supported in fixed compact sets, we prove that quadratic optimal transport plans are quantitatively stable in Wasserstein distance under perturbation of both marginal measures, assuming that one of the initial measures satisfies an upper Ahlfors regularity condition with exponent strictly greater than d-1. Under the same assumption, we also prove novel quantitative stability results for optimal transport maps.
    

Furthermore, we prove a coercivity theorem which states that any probability measure on the product space must be quantitatively close to the set of optimal plans, if it has similar marginals and a similar transport cost to the optimal value.
    

Finally, we prove a quantitative uniqueness theorem which acts as a quantitative counterpart to Brenier’s theorem. The Wasserstein diameter of the set of optimal plans is controlled by the distance of one marginal measure to a regular measure for which uniqueness holds. In this way, “almost uniqueness” of optimal plans is quantified by the source measure being “almost regular”.
    

Examples are provided which prove that the exponents of all estimates are sharp.
    

Keywords: optimal transport, quantitative stability, uniqueness, coercivity, Brenier theorem

1. Introduction




Given two probability measures \rho and \mu on \mathbb{R}^{d}, the quadratic optimal transport problem between \rho and \mu is the minimisation problem




(1.1)

\min_{\gamma\in\Pi(\rho,\mu)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-y\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y),



where \Pi(\rho,\mu) denotes the set of all transport plans between \rho and \mu, i.e. probability measures on \mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d} with first marginal \rho and second marginal \mu. In this paper we study the following questions:


(I) 



When are optimal plans quantitatively stable under perturbation of \rho and \mu?


(II) 



When are plans with a nearly optimal cost quantitatively close to the set of optimisers?


(III) 



When is the set of optimal plans quantitatively close to a singleton, i.e. when does it have small diameter?



These questions are crucial to the many applications of optimal transport, for example in machine learning and computer vision [15, 30, 5], statistics [18, 10, 16] and numerical analysis [9, 22], where one would like to quantify the errors introduced by various approximations and estimations. To respond quantitatively to the above questions we require a distance in which we will measure the perturbations of the marginals, as well as another to compare transport plans. For r\in[1,\infty], the r-Wasserstein distance between \rho and \mu is given by




(1.2)

W_{r}(\rho,\mu)=\min_{\gamma\in\Pi(\rho,\mu)}\|x-y\|_{L^{r}(\gamma)},



with problem (1.1) corresponding to the case r=2. We will measure perturbations in the marginal measures using Wasserstein distances on \mathbb{R}^{d}. Solutions to (1.1) are also probability measures, and will be compared instead using Wasserstein distances on \mathbb{R}^{2d}.




1.1. Contributions




For a subset E of Euclidean space, denote by \mathcal{P}(E) the set of probability measures on E. Throughout this paper we assume d\geq 2. For marginals \rho and \mu, we denote by \Gamma(\rho,\mu) the set of all solutions to problem (1.1), and \gamma_{\rho\to\mu} will denote any optimiser to (1.1).




The four main results of this paper are stated below: stability of optimal plans under perturbation of both marginals and optimal maps with fixed source, coercivity of the objective with respect to transport plans on \mathbb{R}^{2d} with arbitrary marginals, and quantitative uniqueness of solutions near a regular source measure. All the theorems are proven in the text for W_{q} distances between plans and L^{q} between maps, for any q\geq 1. In the introduction we present only the case q=2 for readability. The principal assumption in all theorems is that one of the measures satisfies the following regularity condition.




Assumption 1.1.



Assume \rho\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}) such that for some M>0 and \theta\in(0,1],

1.2. Existing results




Simple examples in [19] involving discrete measures show that without any regularity assumptions, problem (1.1) is not quantitatively stable. OT stability literature can be divided into two categories of assumptions: local stability in the neighbourhood of a transport problem with a regular map/potential, and local stability in the neighbourhood of a transport problem with at least one regular marginal measure. All stability results in this paper are of the second type.




For the first type of result, in [23] it is proved that with fixed probability density \rho and some target \mu, if T_{\rho\to\mu} is Lipschitz, then OT maps are locally quantitatively stable in L^{2}(\rho) with respect to W_{1} perturbations of the target \mu. This is generalised to bi-marginal plan stability under the same assumption in [29]. Assuming the transport from \rho_{0} to \mu_{0} has a class \mathcal{C}^{1,1} Brenier potential (see (1.7) below for the definition), Li and Nochetto establish the control





\forall\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\forall\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}),\quad W_{2}(\gamma_{\rho_{0}\to\mu_{0}},\gamma_{\rho_{1}\to\mu_{1}})\lesssim\Big(W_{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})+W_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}},



for a constant depending on \mathcal{X}, \mathcal{Y}, and the Lipschitz constant of \nabla\phi_{\rho_{0}\to\mu_{0}}. We also mention [21] which adapts this assumption to a larger class of transport costs, as well as [31] which derives statistical results under related regularity assumptions. Unfortunately, in applications, such regularity assumptions can be difficult to verify and need not hold. OT maps are often not even continuous, let alone Lipschitz, even between regular densities.




Assuming instead that one marginal measure is regular is often more relevant to applications. Much progress has been made recently in this direction, in particular for stability of OT maps with a regular fixed source \rho, under perturbation of the target \mu. We mention [6] for the first such result, followed by [17, 26] in which a fixed-source stability theory is developed directly for Brenier potentials – and consequently maps – using tools from functional inequalities. For \mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} a bounded Lipschitz domain and \rho a probability density on \mathcal{X} bounded above and below by positive constants, they prove that for all \mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}),





\|\phi_{\rho\to\mu_{0}}-\phi_{\rho\to\mu_{1}}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\lesssim W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})^{\frac{1}{2}}\quad\;\text{ and }\quad\;\|T_{\rho\to\mu_{0}}-T_{\rho\to\mu_{1}}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\lesssim W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})^{\frac{1}{6}},



where the potentials are normalised by \int_{\mathcal{X}}\phi_{\rho\to\mu_{i}}\mathrm{d}\rho=0, and the constants depend on \mathcal{X}, \mathcal{Y} and the upper and lower density bounds for \rho. We refer the reader to the lecture notes [27] for more details on these methods. The current state of the art papers [33, 14] prove OT map and plan stability for a larger class of source measures. Notably, the support of the regular measure need not be connected and the density can vanish arbitrarily fast. In [33], Mérigot proves that for \rho a bounded, compactly supported probability density on \mathbb{R}^{d},





\forall\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}),\quad\|T_{\rho\to\mu_{0}}-T_{\rho\to\mu_{1}}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\lesssim W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})^{\frac{1}{4}},



for a constant depending on \mathcal{Y} and \rho. Due to the example in [28], the exponent 1/4 is sharp, matching the special case of our Theorem 4.6 and Proposition 5.2 when \theta=1 and q=2. In [14], Cazelles, Pauwels and Portales prove that for \rho_{0} a bounded probability density on \mathcal{X}, for all \rho_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}) and all \mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}),





W_{2}(\gamma_{\rho_{0}\to\mu_{0}},\gamma_{\rho_{1}\to\mu_{1}})\lesssim\Big(W_{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})+W_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\Big)^{\frac{1}{8}},



for a constant depending on \diam(\mathcal{X}), \diam(\mathcal{Y}) and \rho_{0}.
Here their exponent is not sharp. Using our Theorem 4.8 with \theta=1 and q=2 improves their exponent from 1/8 to 1/4, since by Jensen’s inequality W_{1}\leq W_{2}. Our result is still not equal to the theoretical upper bound of 1/3 given by Proposition 5.2. The above results are consequences of the paper [12] on the quantitative regularity of convex functions. Our techniques in this paper extend the quantitative regularity theory to allow for our new parameters \theta and q.




Coercivity of problem (1.1) has also received some study. In [29], Li and Nochetto prove that any transport problem satisfying the same \mathcal{C}^{1,1} regularity assumption on a Brenier potential is coercive in a similar sense to our Theorem 4.2 presented above.
In [33], Mérigot proves two coercivity results for plans and maps with respect to both dual and primal suboptimality with fixed marginals. These form the basis for our Theorem 4.5 and Theorem 4.4. We also mention [13] which proves coercivity of the distance of a plan to the subgradient of the Brenier potential with respect to the primal suboptimality gap, as well as [11, 19] which prove a strong convexity inequality for the dual problem which acts as a dual coercivity result for Brenier potentials.




The current literature on uniqueness of the primal problem is, to the best of our knowledge, only qualitative. The foundational results of Brenier and McCann [8, 32] state that if \rho vanishes on all sets of Hausdorff dimension d-1, then solutions to (1.1) are unique for any \mu, and the optimal plan is induced by a transport map. Our assumption 
                  
                    
                      A
                      
                        
                          
                          
                          
                        
                        
                          (
                          M
                          ,
                          θ
                          )
                        
                      
                    
                  
                 can be in some sense seen as a quantitative strengthening of this Brenier-McCann hypothesis. The sharp hypotheses for qualitative uniqueness have since been identified as slightly weaker. To guarantee that the optimal transport is unique and map induced for any \mu, it is necessary and sufficient that \rho vanishes on any hypersurface which can be locally written as the graph of a difference of convex functions; see [24, 34]. For the dual OT problem, quantitative uniqueness has been recently investigated by the author, in [20]. Here almost connectedness of one marginal measure’s support implies a quantitative bound on the diameter of the set of optimal dual potentials.





1.3. Idea of the proofs




Since





\frac{1}{2}W_{2}^{2}(\rho,\mu)=\frac{1}{2}\int\|x\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho(x)+\frac{1}{2}\int\|y\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(y)-\sup_{\gamma\in\Pi(\rho,\mu)}\int\langle x,y\rangle\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y),



problem (1.1) is equivalent to the maximum correlation problem. Its dual formulation is




(1.7)

\sup_{\gamma\in\Pi(\rho,\mu)}\int\langle x,y\rangle\mathrm{d}\gamma=\inf_{\phi\text{ convex}}\int\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\rho(x)+\int\phi^{*}(y)\mathrm{d}\mu(y),



where \phi^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle x,y\rangle-\phi(x) denotes the Legendre transform. We call any minimiser \phi a Brenier potential. Primal and dual optimality imply that for \phi a Brenier potential and \gamma an optimal plan,





\spt\gamma\subset\graph(\partial\phi).



The main tool used for all the results in this paper is Theorem 2.2, which proves that for q\geq 1, a measure \tilde{\rho} satisfying 
                  
                    
                      A
                      
                        
                          
                          
                          
                        
                        
                          (
                          M
                          ,
                          θ
                          )
                        
                      
                    
                  
                , and a convex Lipschitz function \phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},




(1.8)

\forall\eta\geq 0,\quad\int_{\mathcal{X}}\diam\partial\phi(\overline{B}_{\eta}(x))^{q}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}(x)\lesssim\eta^{\theta},



where here





\partial\phi(\overline{B}_{\eta}(x))=\bigcup_{z\in\overline{B}_{\eta}(x)}\partial\phi(z).



This extends the result of [12], which proves (1.8) when q>1 and \rho is the Lebesgue measure and consequently for all bounded densities, i.e. \rho satisfying 
                  
                    
                      A
                      
                        
                          
                          
                          
                        
                        
                          (
                          M
                          ,
                          θ
                          )
                        
                      
                    
                  
                 with \theta=1. This result should be seen as a “quantitative regularity” result for convex functions, with the integral quantity representing average local oscillation at scale \eta.




Once we have (1.8), then the proof of Theorem 3.1, quantitative uniqueness, is direct. We sketch the argument for the W_{1} plan - W_{1} marginal bound here. Fix any two optimal plans \gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}\in\Gamma(\rho,\mu) and a Brenier potential \phi, and let \alpha\in\Pi(\tilde{\rho},\rho) be an optimal coupling for W_{1}(\tilde{\rho},\rho). Then, for any \eta>0, by choosing a coupling \pi\in\Pi(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}) which couples mass according to the identity for the first marginal \rho of each plan (see (2.8) with \beta\in\Pi(\rho,\rho) concentrated along the diagonal),





\displaystyle W_{1}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\leq
\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}\diam\partial\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\rho(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}\diam\partial\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\alpha(\tilde{x},x)




\displaystyle\leq
\displaystyle\int_{\{\|\tilde{x}-x\|\leq\eta\}}\diam\partial\phi(\overline{B}_{\eta}(\tilde{x}))\mathrm{d}\alpha(\tilde{x},x)+D_{\mathcal{Y}}\alpha(\|x-\tilde{x}\|>\eta)




\displaystyle\lesssim
\displaystyle\eta^{\theta}+\frac{W_{1}(\tilde{\rho},\rho)}{\eta}.



The bound follows by choosing \eta=W_{1}(\tilde{\rho},\rho)^{1/(1+\theta)}.




The stability and coercivity theorems are less direct, as we must compare two plans which do not have the same marginals, and are not, in general, concentrated on the graph of the subgradient of the same convex function. For these bounds, we also couple transport plans in a manner which is optimal for the first marginal. The main term we are left to bound in this case is a quantitative measure of the suboptimality of one plan to being in the subgradient of the other problem’s Brenier potential, of the form





\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}G_{\phi_{0}}(x,y)\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}(x,y),



where G_{\phi}(x,y)=\phi(x)+\phi^{*}(y)-\langle x,y\rangle\geq 0 is the slackness in the Fenchel–Young inequality at (x,y) for a convex function \phi. In the coercivity theorem, this quantity is bounded by the difference in transport costs between \gamma_{0} and \gamma_{1}, plus the sum of W_{1} differences between their marginals. In the stability theorem, this quantity is bounded directly by the W_{1} differences between their marginals.






2. Quantitative regularity: coupling transport plans




In the majority of settings considered in this paper, objects are invariant under translations of \mathcal{X} and \mathcal{Y}, so we may assume without loss of generality that 0 belongs to both sets. It will also cost us no loss of generality to assume they are convex since we never ask measures to have full support. We denote the radii and diameters of each set by





R_{\mathcal{X}}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\|x\|,\quad R_{\mathcal{Y}}=\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\|y\|,\quad D_{\mathcal{X}}=\diam(\mathcal{X}),\quad D_{\mathcal{Y}}=\diam(\mathcal{Y}).



When 0\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{Y}, we have R_{\mathcal{X}}\leq D_{\mathcal{X}} and R_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq D_{\mathcal{Y}}. In general, we will always assume that a pair of Brenier and dual (\phi,\psi) potentials is of the form




(2.1)

\phi(x)=\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\langle x,y\rangle-\psi(y)\quad\text{ and }\quad\psi(y)=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\langle x,y\rangle-\phi(x),



rather than taking the Legendre transform with respect to all \mathbb{R}^{d}. Every optimal potential admits \rho and \mu a.e. representatives of this form; see [20, Appendix B] for a rigorous justification of this assumption. Consequently, \phi and \psi are R_{\mathcal{Y}} and R_{\mathcal{X}} Lipschitz respectively, as envelopes of uniformly Lipschitz functions.




2.1. An integral bound




For a convex function \phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R} and \eta\geq 0, denote




(2.2)

D_{\eta}\phi(x):=\diam\partial\phi(\overline{B}_{\eta}(x)).



The following theorem generalises [12, Cor. 2.2] to allow the exponent q=1 and measures satisfying 
                  
                    
                      A
                      
                        
                          
                          
                          
                        
                        
                          (
                          M
                          ,
                          θ
                          )
                        
                      
                    
                  
                . These bounds build on the previous works [2, 3], which study the size of singular sets of convex functions, rather than the average local oscillation quantity considered here. We first prove a lemma which gives an interior L^{\infty} estimate for gradients of convex functions, as a consequence of monotonicity. Similar estimates exist in the literature, for example [25, 7].




Lemma 2.1.



Let \phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R} be a convex function. Then for any x,c\in\mathbb{R}^{d} and any \eta>0,





D_{\eta}\phi(x)\leq\frac{2(d+1)}{\beta_{d-1}\eta^{d}}\int_{B_{2\eta}(x)}\|\nabla\phi(z)-c\|\mathrm{d}z,



where \beta_{d}:=|B_{1}| is the volume of the d-dimensional unit ball.





Proof.



Subtracting \langle\cdot,c\rangle from \phi, it suffices to consider c=0. Choose z\in\overline{B}_{\eta}(x) and y\in\partial\phi(z). For all z^{\prime}\in B_{\eta}(z) and y^{\prime}\in\partial\phi(z^{\prime}), monotonicity implies





\langle z^{\prime}-z,y\rangle\leq\langle z^{\prime}-z,y^{\prime}\rangle\leq\eta\|y^{\prime}\|.



Integrating in z^{\prime} over the half ball A=\{z^{\prime}\in B_{\eta}(z):\langle z^{\prime}-z,y\rangle\geq 0\}\subset B_{2\eta}(x) gives




(2.3)

\frac{\beta_{d-1}}{d+1}\eta^{d+1}\|y\|\leq\eta\int_{B_{2\eta}(x)}\|\nabla\phi(z^{\prime})\|\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}.



Thus, for every z\in\overline{B}_{\eta}(x) and y\in\partial\phi(z),




(2.4)

\|y\|\leq\frac{d+1}{\beta_{d-1}\eta^{d}}\int_{B_{2\eta}(x)}\|\nabla\phi(z^{\prime})\|\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}.



Passing to the supremum over such y gives the result.
∎





Theorem 2.2.



Let d\geq 2, and let \phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R} be convex, with \partial\phi(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset\mathcal{Y} for some compact convex \mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}. Let R>0, and \tilde{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}(\overline{B}_{R}) satisfy 
                  
                    
                      A
                      
                        
                          
                          
                          
                        
                        
                          (
                          M
                          ,
                          θ
                          )
                        
                      
                    
                  
                . Then, for every q\in[1,\infty),




(2.5)

\forall\eta\geq 0,\qquad\int_{\overline{B}_{R}}D_{\eta}\phi(x)^{q}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}(x)\leq c_{d}MR^{d-1+\theta}D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}\left(\frac{\eta}{R}\right)^{\theta},



where





c_{d}=\frac{4}{3}\frac{6^{d}d^{2}(d+1)\beta_{d}}{\beta_{d-1}}.







Proof.



If D_{\mathcal{Y}}=0, then \phi is affine and the result is immediate. Otherwise, fix x\in\mathbb{R}^{d} and \eta>0, and set





c_{x}=\frac{1}{|B_{2\eta}(x)|}\int_{B_{2\eta}(x)}\nabla\phi(z)\mathrm{d}z.



Applying Lemma 2.1,





D_{\eta}\phi(x)\leq\frac{2(d+1)}{\beta_{d-1}\eta^{d}}\|\nabla\phi-c_{x}\|_{L^{1}(B_{2\eta}(x))}.



The BV Poincaré inequality [4, Theorem 3.44], applied to each component with the diameter bound and sharp universal coefficient 1/2 [1], gives





\|\nabla\phi-c_{x}\|_{L^{1}(B_{2\eta}(x))}\leq 2\eta\|D^{2}\phi\|_{1,1}(B_{2\eta}(x)),



where D^{2}\phi (and later \Delta\phi) is a Radon measure and





A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\quad\|A\|_{1,1}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|A_{ij}|



denotes the entry-wise l^{1} norm of a matrix. For a symmetric positive semi-definite matrix A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}, by the inequality |A_{ij}|\leq\frac{A_{ii}+A_{jj}}{2}, it holds that \|D^{2}\phi\|_{1,1}\leq d\Delta\phi in the sense of measures. Thus




(2.6)

D_{\eta}\phi(x)\leq\frac{4(d+1)}{\beta_{d-1}\eta^{d-1}}\|D^{2}\phi\|_{1,1}(B_{2\eta}(x))\leq\frac{4d(d+1)}{\beta_{d-1}\eta^{d-1}}\Delta\phi(B_{2\eta}(x)).



Integrating (2.6) in x and using Fubini’s theorem gives





\displaystyle\int_{\overline{B}_{R}}D_{\eta}\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}(x)
\displaystyle\leq\frac{4d(d+1)}{\beta_{d-1}\eta^{d-1}}\int_{\overline{B}_{R}}\int_{B_{2\eta}(x)}\mathrm{d}\Delta\phi(z)\mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}(x)





\displaystyle=\frac{4d(d+1)}{\beta_{d-1}\eta^{d-1}}\int_{B_{R+2\eta}}\tilde{\rho}(\overline{B}_{R}\cap B_{2\eta}(z))\mathrm{d}\Delta\phi(z)





\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{d+2}d(d+1)}{\beta_{d-1}}M\Delta\phi(B_{R+2\eta})\eta^{\theta}.



Let \chi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(B_{1}) be a non-negative mollifier with \int\chi=1, set \chi_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-d}\chi(x/\varepsilon), and define \phi_{\varepsilon}=\phi*\chi_{\varepsilon}. If \phi is convex and L-Lipschitz, integration by parts yields





\Delta\phi_{\varepsilon}(B_{R+2\eta})\leq\lip(\phi_{\varepsilon})\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial B_{R+2\eta})\leq d\beta_{d}L(R+2\eta)^{d-1}.



Passing to the limit \varepsilon\to 0,





\Delta\phi(B_{R+2\eta})\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\Delta\phi_{\varepsilon}(B_{R+2\eta})\leq d\beta_{d}L(R+2\eta)^{d-1},



by the lower-semicontinuity of the measure of an open set for the convergence \Delta\phi_{\varepsilon}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\Delta\phi. Noting that \Delta\phi(A)=\Delta(\phi+\langle\cdot,c\rangle)(A) for any constant c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}, L can be replaced by D_{\mathcal{Y}}. By assumption, for any \eta>0, D_{\eta}\phi\leq D_{\mathcal{Y}}, and hence





D_{\eta}\phi(x)^{q}\leq D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q-1}D_{\eta}\phi(x),



which proves that for all \eta>0,




(2.7)

\int_{\overline{B}_{R}}D_{\eta}\phi(x)^{q}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}(x)\leq 3^{1-d}c_{d}M(R+2\eta)^{d-1}D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}\eta^{\theta}.



If \eta\leq R, the stated bound is proven using (R+2\eta)\leq 3R; otherwise if \eta\geq R the bound holds trivially since 1=\tilde{\rho}(\overline{B}_{R})\leq MR^{d-1+\theta} and D_{\eta}\phi\leq D_{\mathcal{Y}} for all \eta. Finally, letting \eta\downarrow 0 proves the case \eta=0.
∎





Remark 2.3 (Sharpness of the exponent).



The exponent of \eta is sharp in general, as the following example demonstrates. Let \phi(x)=|x_{1}| and \rho(x)=c_{\theta}|x_{1}|^{\theta-1}\chi_{[-1,1]^{d}}(x) for c_{\theta}=\theta 2^{-d} a normalising constant. Then





D_{\eta}\phi(x)=\begin{cases}2&|x_{1}|\leq\eta\\
0&|x_{1}|>\eta,\end{cases}



so that for small \eta,





\int_{[-1,1]^{d}}D_{\eta}\phi(x)^{q}\mathrm{d}\rho(x)=2^{q}\rho(|x_{1}|\leq\eta)=2^{q}\eta^{\theta}.








2.2. The Fenchel gap




For (x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d} and a convex function \phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}, denote the remainder in the Fenchel-Young inequality by





G_{\phi}(x,y)=\phi(x)+\phi^{*}(y)-\langle x,y\rangle\geq 0,



where here \phi^{*}(y)=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle z,y\rangle-\phi(z) is the convex conjugate. Equality G_{\phi}(x,y)=0 is equivalent to the pair (x,y) belonging to the graph of the subgradient of \phi, i.e. y\in\partial\phi(x). In [13, Lemma 1.1], Carlier proves a quantitative bound





G_{\phi}(x,y)\geq\frac{1}{2}\dist((x,y),\graph(\partial\phi))^{2},



i.e. points which almost attain equality in Fenchel-Young are quantitatively close to the set \graph(\partial\phi).
The following lemma controls the distance of a specific y to elements of \partial\phi(x), rather than a pair (x,y) to \graph(\partial\phi).




Lemma 2.4.



Let \phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R} be convex. Then for any (x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}, any g\in\partial\phi(x), and any \eta>0,





\|y-g\|\leq D_{\eta}\phi(x)+\frac{G_{\phi}(x,y)}{\eta}.







Proof.



Write (x_{0},y_{0})=(x,y). The conclusion is immediate if G_{\phi}(x_{0},y_{0})=+\infty. Otherwise, for any (x_{1},y_{1}) with \phi^{*}(y_{1})<+\infty, Fenchel-Young inequalities give





\phi(x_{0})\geq\langle x_{0},y_{1}\rangle-\phi^{*}(y_{1}),\quad\phi^{*}(y_{0})\geq\langle x_{1},y_{0}\rangle-\phi(x_{1}),



so that





\displaystyle G_{\phi}(x_{0},y_{0})\geq
\displaystyle\langle x_{0},y_{1}\rangle-\phi^{*}(y_{1})+\langle x_{1},y_{0}\rangle-\phi(x_{1})-\langle x_{0},y_{0}\rangle




\displaystyle=
\displaystyle-G_{\phi}(x_{1},y_{1})-\langle x_{1}-x_{0},y_{1}-y_{0}\rangle.



In particular, when one takes (x_{1},y_{1})\in\graph(\partial\phi), G_{\phi}(x_{1},y_{1})=0 so that the first term on the right vanishes. Fix any g_{0}\in\partial\phi(x_{0}). If y_{0}=g_{0}, the result is immediate. Otherwise choose





x_{1}=x_{0}+\eta\frac{y_{0}-g_{0}}{\|y_{0}-g_{0}\|},



and also choose any y_{1}\in\partial\phi(x_{1}). Then





\displaystyle G_{\phi}(x_{0},y_{0})\geq
\displaystyle\langle x_{1}-x_{0},y_{0}-y_{1}\rangle=\eta\Big\langle\frac{y_{0}-g_{0}}{\|y_{0}-g_{0}\|},y_{0}-g_{0}+g_{0}-y_{1}\Big\rangle




\displaystyle\geq
\displaystyle\eta\left(\|y_{0}-g_{0}\|-\|g_{0}-y_{1}\|\right).



Since x_{1}\in\overline{B}_{\eta}(x_{0}), rearranging,





\|y_{0}-g_{0}\|\leq D_{\eta}\phi(x_{0})+\frac{G_{\phi}(x_{0},y_{0})}{\eta}.



∎






2.3. Coupling transport plans




To compare probability measures \gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}), we use the q-Wasserstein distance on \mathbb{R}^{2d} with the norm





\|(x,y)\|=\left(\|x\|^{q}+\|y\|^{q}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}},



so that





W_{q}^{q}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4d}}\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{q}+\|y_{0}-y_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\pi(x_{0},y_{0},x_{1},y_{1}).



Since all norms on finite dimensional spaces are equivalent, this distance is equivalent to the Wasserstein distance induced by the Euclidean norm on \mathbb{R}^{2d}, up to multiplicative constants. All our bounds hold for any q\in[1,\infty), with an exponent that degenerates as q\to\infty. The examples given in the sharpness section indicate that in general, one cannot hope for any of our bounds to hold for q=\infty.




For \rho_{0},\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}) and \mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}), two plans \gamma_{i}\in\Pi(\rho_{i},\mu_{i}) and a coupling \beta\in\Pi(\rho_{0},\rho_{1}), we denote by \pi_{\beta}\in\Pi(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}) the coupling given by




(2.8)

\pi_{\beta}(\mathrm{d}x_{0},\mathrm{d}y_{0},\mathrm{d}x_{1},\mathrm{d}y_{1})=\beta(\mathrm{d}x_{0},\mathrm{d}x_{1})\gamma_{0}(\mathrm{d}y_{0}\mid x_{0})\gamma_{1}(\mathrm{d}y_{1}\mid x_{1}),



where \gamma_{i}(\cdot\mid x_{i}) denotes the disintegration of \gamma_{i} at x_{i}. We will use the coupling \pi_{\beta}\in\Pi(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}) as a competitor for the minimisation problem W_{q}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}), controlling its transport cost in terms of our objects of interest. In particular,





\displaystyle W_{q}^{q}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\leq
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4d}}\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{q}+\|y_{0}-y_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}




\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\beta+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4d}}\|y_{0}-y_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}.



Choosing \beta\in\Pi(\rho_{0},\rho_{1}) optimal for some r-Wasserstein distance, the first term is easily controllable in terms of W_{r}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1}). The majority of our work will be spent controlling the second term. Observe that when \rho=\rho_{0}=\rho_{1} and the \gamma_{i} are induced by maps T_{i}, then for \beta the identity coupling,





\pi_{\beta}=(\id,T_{0},\id,T_{1})_{\#}\rho,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4d}}\|y_{0}-y_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}=\|T_{0}-T_{1}\|_{L^{q}(\rho)}^{q}.



The below lemma provides the general framework for all the bounds in this paper. The first bound considers when both plans are concentrated on the same subgradient, and the second when only one is.




Lemma 2.5 (Coupling transport plans).



Let q\in[1,+\infty), \mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} be compact and convex, and let \phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R} be a proper convex function with \partial\phi(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset\mathcal{Y}. Let \tilde{\rho},\rho_{0},\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}) and \mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}), and suppose for i=0,1 that \gamma_{i}\in\Pi(\rho_{i},\mu_{i}) satisfy \spt\gamma_{i}\subset\graph(\partial\phi). Then for any \alpha\in\Pi(\tilde{\rho},\rho_{0}) and \beta\in\Pi(\rho_{0},\rho_{1}) and \eta,s\geq 0,





\displaystyle\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{2}}\|y_{0}-y_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}\leq
\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}D_{s+\eta}\phi(\tilde{x})^{q}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}(\tilde{x})





\displaystyle+D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}\alpha(\|\tilde{x}-x_{0}\|>\eta)+D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}\beta(\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|>s).



Alternatively, if we do not have the hypothesis \spt\gamma_{1}\subset\graph(\partial\phi), then for s>0 instead





\displaystyle\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{2}}\|y_{0}-y_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}\leq
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q-1}\int_{\mathcal{X}}D_{s+\eta}\phi(\tilde{x})\mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}(\tilde{x})





\displaystyle+\frac{D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q-1}}{s}\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{2}}G_{\phi}(x_{0},y_{1})\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}+D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}\alpha(\|\tilde{x}-x_{0}\|>\eta).







Proof.



Partitioning, for s\geq 0, over \|x_{0}-x_{1}\|>s and \|x_{0}-x_{1}\|\leq s,





\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{2}}\|y_{0}-y_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}D_{s}\phi(x_{0})^{q}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(x_{0})+D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}\beta(\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|>s).



The integral term is controlled by partitioning for \eta\geq 0, on \|\tilde{x}-x_{0}\|\leq\eta or >\eta,





\int_{\mathcal{X}}D_{s}\phi(x_{0})^{q}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(x_{0})\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}D_{s+\eta}\phi(\tilde{x})^{q}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}(\tilde{x})+D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}\alpha(\|x_{0}-\tilde{x}\|>\eta),



which completes the proof of the first statement. For the second, supposing instead we do not know that \spt\gamma_{1}\subset\graph(\partial\phi), we instead apply Lemma 2.4, to deduce





\displaystyle\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{2}}\|y_{0}-y_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}\leq
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q-1}\left(\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{2}}D_{s}\phi(x_{0})\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}+\frac{1}{s}\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{2}}G_{\phi}(x_{0},y_{1})\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}\right).



The first term is controlled as above, giving





\displaystyle\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{2}}D_{s}\phi(x_{0})\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}D_{s+\eta}\phi(\tilde{x})\mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}(\tilde{x})+D_{\mathcal{Y}}\alpha(\|x_{0}-\tilde{x}\|>\eta),



which completes the proof.
∎







3. Quantitative uniqueness: plans on a common subgradient




In this section we prove two theorems regarding the comparison of transport plans concentrated on the subgradient of the same convex function. The first, quantitative uniqueness, compares plans with the same marginals, when one marginal is close to regular. The second compares a plan with a regular first marginal to another plan with potentially different marginals. Although, by the triangle inequality, the second implies the first (with a larger constant), we prefer to prove the two results separately to isolate the proof mechanisms involved.




3.1. Quantitative uniqueness




Theorem 3.1 (Quantitative uniqueness).



Let q\in[1,\infty) and r\in[1,\infty], and let \mathcal{X} and \mathcal{Y} be compact and convex subsets of \mathbb{R}^{d} for d\geq 2. Let \tilde{\rho} be a probability measure on \mathcal{X} satisfying 
                  
                    
                      A
                      
                        
                          
                          
                          
                        
                        
                          (
                          M
                          ,
                          θ
                          )
                        
                      
                    
                  
                . Then for all \rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}) and \mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}),




(3.1)

\diam_{W_{q}}\Gamma(\rho,\mu)\leq CW_{r}(\rho,\tilde{\rho})^{\nu},



where \nu\in(0,1] is given by





\nu=\begin{cases}\frac{r\theta}{q(r+\theta)}&r<\infty\\
\frac{\theta}{q}&r=\infty,\end{cases}



and C=D_{\mathcal{Y}}\left(c_{d}MD_{\mathcal{X}}^{d-1+\theta}+1\right)^{1/q}D_{\mathcal{X}}^{-\nu}>0.





Proof.



Fix two optimal plans \gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}\in\Gamma(\rho,\mu). If W_{r}(\rho,\tilde{\rho})=0 then \rho=\tilde{\rho} and the result holds by the Brenier-McCann theorem, so assume that W_{r}(\rho,\tilde{\rho})>0, with \alpha the optimal coupling between them. Fix a Brenier potential \phi for the transport from \rho to \mu. We apply Lemma 2.5 with \beta the identity coupling and s=0:





\int\|y_{0}-y_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}\leq D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q-1}\int_{\mathcal{X}}D_{\eta}\phi(\tilde{x})\mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}(\tilde{x})+D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}\alpha(\|\tilde{x}-x_{0}\|>\eta).



We apply Theorem 2.2 and a Markov bound. For \eta>0,





\int\|y_{0}-y_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}\leq D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}\left(c_{d}MD_{\mathcal{X}}^{d-1+\theta}\left(\frac{\eta}{D_{\mathcal{X}}}\right)^{\theta}+\frac{W_{r}^{r}(\rho,\tilde{\rho})}{\eta^{r}}\right).



We balance the terms containing \eta, choosing





\left(\frac{\eta}{D_{\mathcal{X}}}\right)^{\theta}=\frac{W_{r}^{r}(\rho,\tilde{\rho})}{\eta^{r}},\quad\text{ so that }\quad\eta=\left(W_{r}^{r}(\rho,\tilde{\rho})D_{\mathcal{X}}^{\theta}\right)^{1/(r+\theta)}.



Consequently,




(3.2)

\int\|y_{0}-y_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}\leq D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}\left(c_{d}MD_{\mathcal{X}}^{d-1+\theta}+1\right)D_{\mathcal{X}}^{-\frac{r\theta}{r+\theta}}W_{r}(\rho,\tilde{\rho})^{\frac{r\theta}{r+\theta}}.



This is the claimed bound. For r=+\infty, the proof is the same, choosing \eta=W_{\infty}(\rho,\tilde{\rho}) without needing any Markov bound.
∎





Remark 3.2 (Uniformity of constant in q and r and \theta).



The statements of the theorems in the introduction claim the existence of a constant depending on only \mathcal{X}, \mathcal{Y} and M, and a priori, the above constant





C=D_{\mathcal{Y}}\left(c_{d}MD_{\mathcal{X}}^{d-1+\theta}+1\right)^{1/q}D_{\mathcal{X}}^{-\nu}



does not satisfy this. Since any constant which holds for all measures on \mathcal{X}^{\prime} also holds for all measures on \mathcal{X}\subset\mathcal{X}^{\prime}, with D_{\mathcal{X}}\leq D_{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}, we can freely replace instances of D_{\mathcal{X}} by \max(1,D_{\mathcal{X}}); then \max(1,D_{\mathcal{X}})^{-\nu}\leq 1 and \max(1,D_{\mathcal{X}})^{d-1+\theta}\leq\max(1,D_{\mathcal{X}})^{d}. Furthermore, since the expression inside the qth root is larger than 1, we can bound this by the same term without the qth root. Thus a choice of constant depending only on \mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y} and M is given by





C=D_{\mathcal{Y}}(1+c_{d}M\max(1,D_{\mathcal{X}})^{d}).



The same principle applies to all the other theorems using similar calculations.






3.2. Plans on the same subgradient with different marginals




The above result uses the \alpha coupling in Lemma 2.5. If instead we use the \beta coupling, we can arrive at the following theorem of independent interest, which generalises a result of [14] to compare transport plans in the same subgradient with different marginals. We improve their exponent and give a general W_{q}-W_{r} bound rather than W_{2}-W_{2}. Our exponent is proven sharp in Proposition 5.1. We use this theorem in the Appendix to give an alternative proof of stability for the optimal transport plans.




Theorem 3.3 (Same subgradient plan control).



Let q\geq 1 and r\in[1,\infty], let \mathcal{X} and \mathcal{Y} be compact and convex subsets of \mathbb{R}^{d} for d\geq 2, and let \rho_{0} be a probability measure on \mathcal{X} satisfying 
                  
                    
                      A
                      
                        
                          
                          
                          
                        
                        
                          (
                          M
                          ,
                          θ
                          )
                        
                      
                    
                  
                . Let \phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R} be convex with \partial\phi(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset\mathcal{Y}, and set \gamma_{0}=(\id,\nabla\phi)_{\#}\rho_{0}. Then for any \gamma_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}) satisfying \spt\gamma_{1}\subset\graph(\partial\phi) with first marginal \rho_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}),





W_{q}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\leq CW_{r}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})^{\nu},



where \nu\in(0,1] is given by





\nu=\begin{cases}\frac{r\theta}{q(r+\theta)}&r<\infty\\
\frac{\theta}{q}&r=\infty,\end{cases}



and C^{q}=D_{\mathcal{X}}^{q(1-\nu)}+D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}(1+c_{d}MD_{\mathcal{X}}^{d-1+\theta})D_{\mathcal{X}}^{-q\nu}.





Proof.



If W_{r}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})=0, then \rho_{0}=\rho_{1}, and differentiability of \phi \rho_{0}-almost everywhere implies \gamma_{0}=\gamma_{1}, so assume that W_{r}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})>0.




We apply Lemma 2.5 with \tilde{\rho}=\rho_{0}, \rho_{0}=\rho_{0}, \rho_{1}=\rho_{1}, \alpha\in\Pi(\rho_{0},\rho_{0}) the identity plan, \beta optimal for W_{r}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1}), and \eta=0. Then for all s\geq 0,





\displaystyle W_{q}^{q}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\leq
\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\beta(x_{0},x_{1})





\displaystyle+\int_{\mathcal{X}}D_{s}^{q}\phi(x_{0})\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(x_{0})+D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}\beta(\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|>s).



For the last two terms, Theorem 2.2 and Markov’s inequality give, for finite r and 0<s,





\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}D_{s}\phi(x_{0})^{q}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(x_{0})+D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}\beta(\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|>s)




\displaystyle\leq D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}\left(c_{d}MD_{\mathcal{X}}^{d-1}s^{\theta}+\frac{W_{r}^{r}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})}{s^{r}}\right).



As in the quantitative uniqueness theorem, we choose





\left(\frac{s}{D_{\mathcal{X}}}\right)^{\theta}=\frac{W_{r}^{r}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})}{s^{r}},\quad\text{ so that }\quad s=\left(W_{r}^{r}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})D_{\mathcal{X}}^{\theta}\right)^{1/(r+\theta)},



which, substituting, gives




(3.3)

W_{q}^{q}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\leq\int\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\beta+D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}(1+c_{d}MD_{\mathcal{X}}^{d-1+\theta})D_{\mathcal{X}}^{-q\nu}W_{r}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})^{q\nu}.



The same bound holds in the case r=\infty by directly choosing s=W_{\infty}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1}) without need of the Markov bound. To control the \beta term,




(3.4)

\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\beta\leq\begin{cases}D_{\mathcal{X}}^{q-r}W_{r}^{r}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})&1\leq r\leq q<\infty\\
W_{r}^{q}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})&1\leq q\leq r\leq\infty.\end{cases}



Here the first case follows from \|x_{0}-x_{1}\|\leq D_{\mathcal{X}} and the second by Jensen’s inequality if r<\infty or \beta almost everywhere if r=\infty. Thus for q\geq 1, r\in[1,\infty],





\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\beta\leq D_{\mathcal{X}}^{q(1-\nu)}W_{r}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})^{q\nu},



which, after combining with (3.3), gives the result.
∎







4. Coercivity and stability: plans close to a common subgradient




In this section, we prove two coercivity theorems for plans, showcasing two different types of bound. In principle, one could create a combined coercivity theorem containing both, but for readability, we prefer to present each idea separately. We then prove a coercivity theorem for maps with respect to dual suboptimality, followed by the two main stability theorems for maps and plans.




4.1. Coercivity for plans with arbitrary marginals




We note that unlike every other theorem in this paper, the constants in the following theorem are not translation invariant with respect to the marginals, as the suboptimality gap between plans with different marginals is not translation invariant.
Indeed, for any a\in\mathbb{R}^{d} and \gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}), define





\gamma_{i}^{a}=(x+a,y)_{\#}\gamma_{i}\in\mathcal{P}((\mathcal{X}+a)\times\mathcal{Y}).



Then





\displaystyle\int_{(\mathcal{X}+a)\times\mathcal{Y}}\langle x,y\rangle\mathrm{d}(\gamma_{1}^{a}-\gamma_{0}^{a})=
\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\langle x+a,y\rangle\mathrm{d}(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{0})




\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\langle x,y\rangle\mathrm{d}(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{0})+\big\langle a,\int_{\mathcal{Y}}y\mathrm{d}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{0})\big\rangle,



which can be made to take any value by varying a, if \mu_{1} and \mu_{0} do not have the same barycentre. Thus some constants here will be given in terms of R_{\mathcal{X}} and R_{\mathcal{Y}}. We will control the mixed Fenchel deficit appearing in Lemma 2.5 using the following lemma.




Lemma 4.1.



Let \mathcal{X} and \mathcal{Y} be compact convex subsets of \mathbb{R}^{d} for d\geq 2, and let \rho_{0},\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}) and \mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}). Fix \phi a Brenier potential between \rho_{0} and \mu_{0}. Then for any \gamma_{0}\in\Gamma(\rho_{0},\mu_{0}) optimal and any \gamma_{1}\in\Pi(\rho_{1},\mu_{1}),





\displaystyle\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{2}}G_{\phi}(x_{0},y_{1})\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}\leq
\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\langle x,y\rangle\mathrm{d}(\gamma_{0}-\gamma_{1})\right|+R_{\mathcal{Y}}W_{1}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})+R_{\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}),



where \pi_{\beta} is the coupling defined in (2.8), with \beta chosen optimal for W_{1}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1}).





Proof.



Denote \psi=\phi^{*}. We have





\displaystyle\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{2}}G_{\phi}(x_{0},y_{1})\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}=
\displaystyle\int\phi\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}+\int\psi\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}-\int\langle x_{0},y_{1}\rangle\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}




\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\int\phi\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}+\int\psi\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}-\int\langle x_{1},y_{1}\rangle\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}





\displaystyle+\int\langle x_{1}-x_{0},y_{1}\rangle\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}+\int\psi\mathrm{d}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{0}).



By optimality, \int\phi\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}+\int\psi\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}=\int\langle x,y\rangle\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}. By the Cauchy-Schwarz and Kantorovich-Rubinstein inequalities,





\int\langle x_{1}-x_{0},y_{1}\rangle\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}\leq R_{\mathcal{Y}}\int\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|\mathrm{d}\beta,\qquad\int\psi\mathrm{d}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{0})\leq R_{\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}),



which completes the proof.
∎





Theorem 4.2 (Arbitrary marginal coercivity).



Let q\geq 1, and let \mathcal{X} and \mathcal{Y} be compact and convex subsets of \mathbb{R}^{d} for d\geq 2. Let \rho_{0} be a probability measure on \mathcal{X} satisfying 
                  
                    
                      A
                      
                        
                          
                          
                          
                        
                        
                          (
                          M
                          ,
                          θ
                          )
                        
                      
                    
                  
                , let \mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}), and denote by \gamma_{0} the unique optimal plan between them. Then for any \rho_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}), \mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}) and any plan \gamma_{1}\in\Pi(\rho_{1},\mu_{1}),





W_{q}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\leq C\left(\left|\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\langle x,y\rangle\mathrm{d}(\gamma_{0}-\gamma_{1})\right|+R_{\mathcal{Y}}W_{1}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})+R_{\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\right)^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\theta)}}



where C^{q}=D_{\mathcal{X}}^{q-1+1/(1+\theta)}R_{\mathcal{Y}}^{-\theta/(1+\theta)}+D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}(1+c_{d}MR_{\mathcal{X}}^{d-1+\theta})(D_{\mathcal{Y}}R_{\mathcal{X}})^{-\theta/((1+\theta))}.





Proof.



Let \phi be a Brenier potential for the transport between \rho_{0} and \mu_{0}. We apply Lemma 2.5 with \tilde{\rho}=\rho_{0}, \rho_{0}=\rho_{0}, \rho_{1}=\rho_{1}, \alpha the identity coupling, and \beta\in\Pi(\rho_{0},\rho_{1}) optimal for W_{1}. Then





\displaystyle W_{q}^{q}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\leq
\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\beta+\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{2}}\|y_{0}-y_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}




\displaystyle\leq
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{X}}^{q-1}W_{1}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})+D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q-1}\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{2}}\|y_{0}-y_{1}\|\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}.



Applying Lemma 2.4 with x=x_{0}, y=y_{1} and g=y_{0}, followed by Theorem 2.2, for s>0,




(4.1)

\displaystyle\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{2}}\|y_{0}-y_{1}\|\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}\leq D_{\mathcal{Y}}c_{d}MR_{\mathcal{X}}^{d-1}s^{\theta}+\frac{\Delta}{s},



where





\Delta=\int G_{\phi}(x_{0},y_{1})\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}.



If \Delta=0, passing s\downarrow 0 gives the result. Otherwise for \Delta>0 we balance the terms in s on the unitless length scale, choosing





\left(\frac{s}{R_{\mathcal{X}}}\right)^{\theta}=\frac{\Delta}{D_{\mathcal{Y}}s}\implies s^{\theta+1}=\frac{\Delta R_{\mathcal{X}}^{\theta}}{D_{\mathcal{Y}}},



so that





\frac{\Delta}{s}=R_{\mathcal{X}}^{-\theta/(1+\theta)}D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{1/(1+\theta)}\Delta^{\theta/(1+\theta)},\qquad R_{\mathcal{X}}^{d-1}s^{\theta}=R_{\mathcal{X}}^{d-1+\theta}R_{\mathcal{X}}^{-\theta/(1+\theta)}D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{-\theta/(1+\theta)}\Delta^{\theta/(1+\theta)}.



Substituting gives





\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{2}}\|y_{0}-y_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}\leq D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}(1+c_{d}MR_{\mathcal{X}}^{d-1+\theta})(D_{\mathcal{Y}}R_{\mathcal{X}})^{-\theta/(1+\theta)}\Delta^{\theta/(1+\theta)}.



The proof is completed by applying Lemma 4.1 above, and absorbing the W_{1} term into the constant via





D_{\mathcal{X}}^{q-1}W_{1}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\leq D_{\mathcal{X}}^{q-1+1/(1+\theta)}R_{\mathcal{Y}}^{-\theta/(1+\theta)}\Big(R_{\mathcal{Y}}W_{1}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\Big)^{\theta/(1+\theta)}.



∎





Remark 4.3 (Recovering the version in the introduction).



Since we are comparing measures with different marginals, the max correlation suboptimality gap above and the quadratic cost optimality gap are not equal. We nonetheless have





\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}2\langle x,y\rangle\mathrm{d}(\gamma_{0}-\gamma_{1})=
\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\|x-y\|^{2}\mathrm{d}(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{0})





\displaystyle+\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|x\|^{2}\mathrm{d}(\rho_{0}-\rho_{1})+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\|y\|^{2}\mathrm{d}(\mu_{0}-\mu_{1})




\displaystyle\leq
\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\|x-y\|^{2}\mathrm{d}(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{0})





\displaystyle+2R_{\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})+2R_{\mathcal{Y}}W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}).



After doing the same bound for \gamma_{1}-\gamma_{0}, we can control the absolute value, and thus recover the version presented in the introduction, up to a different constant.






4.2. Approximate coercivity with an almost regular source measure




The second plan coercivity theorem concerns the transport problem where one marginal \rho satisfies

In this case, we prove that for any \mu, the \rho\to\mu transport problem is coercive up to an error given in terms of W_{r}(\rho,\tilde{\rho}). While for r=1 this theorem is implied by the above coercivity theorem combined with the stability theorem, for other r the error exponent here is sharper. Since we only treat the fixed marginal case, we are once again in the setting where constants are translation invariant, so can be stated in terms of the diameters.




Theorem 4.4 (Coercivity with almost regular source).



Let q\geq 1 and r\in[1,\infty], and let \mathcal{X} and \mathcal{Y} be compact and convex subsets of \mathbb{R}^{d} for d\geq 2. Fix \tilde{\rho} a probability measure on \mathcal{X} satisfying

                          M

                          )
                        
                      
                    
                  
                . Then for any \rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}) and \mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}) and any optimal plan \gamma_{\rho\to\mu}\in\Gamma(\rho,\mu),





\forall\gamma\in\Pi(\rho,\mu),\quad W_{q}(\gamma_{\rho\to\mu},\gamma)\leq C_{0}\left(\int_{{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}}\|x-y\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\gamma-W_{2}^{2}(\rho,\mu)\right)^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\theta)}}+C_{1}W_{r}(\rho,\tilde{\rho})^{\nu},



where \nu\in(0,1] is given by





\nu=\begin{cases}\frac{r\theta}{q(r+\theta)}&r<\infty\\
\frac{\theta}{q}&r=\infty,\end{cases}



where C_{0}^{q}=D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}(1+c_{d}MD_{\mathcal{X}}^{d-1+\theta})(D_{\mathcal{Y}}D_{\mathcal{X}})^{-\theta/(1+\theta)} and C_{1}>0 is the constant from Theorem 3.1.





Proof.



Let \phi be a Brenier potential for the transport \rho\to\mu. We apply Lemma 2.5 with \tilde{\rho}=\tilde{\rho}, \rho_{0}=\rho_{1}=\rho, \alpha optimal for W_{r}(\rho,\tilde{\rho}), and \beta the identity coupling. Then





W_{q}^{q}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\leq\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{2}}\|y_{0}-y_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}\leq D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q-1}\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{2}}\|y_{0}-y_{1}\|\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}.



For r<\infty, applying Lemma 2.4 followed by Theorem 2.2, the subadditivity of (\cdot)^{\theta}, and a Markov bound,





\displaystyle\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{2}}\|y_{0}-y_{1}\|\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}\leq
\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}D_{\eta+s}\phi(\tilde{x})\mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}(\tilde{x})+\frac{\Delta}{s}+D_{\mathcal{Y}}\alpha(\|\tilde{x}-x\|>\eta)




\displaystyle\leq
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{Y}}c_{d}MD_{\mathcal{X}}^{d-1}(s^{\theta}+\eta^{\theta})+\frac{\Delta}{s}+D_{\mathcal{Y}}\frac{W_{r}^{r}(\rho,\tilde{\rho})}{\eta^{r}}



where as before





\Delta=\int G_{\phi}(x_{0},y_{1})\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}.

W_{q}^{q}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\leq C_{0}^{q}\Delta^{\theta/(1+\theta)}+C_{1}^{q}W_{r}(\rho,\tilde{\rho})^{q\nu}



for the constants given in the statement of the theorem. We take the qth root then use subadditivity, before applying Lemma 4.1 to complete the proof when r<\infty. Here we recover the quadratic cost gap directly since the plans have the same marginals, so that





\int\langle x,y\rangle\mathrm{d}(\gamma_{0}-\gamma_{1})=\frac{1}{2}\int\|x-y\|^{2}\mathrm{d}(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{0}).



When r=\infty, the same bounds hold directly, taking the same choice of s and instead \eta=W_{\infty}(\rho,\tilde{\rho}) without needing any Markov bound.
∎






4.3. Coercivity of maps with respect to dual suboptimality




For compact sets \mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}, we denote the maximum correlation semidual functional by \mathcal{K}_{\rho,\mu}:\mathcal{C}^{0}(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathbb{R},




(4.2)

\mathcal{K}_{\rho,\mu}(\psi)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi^{*}\mathrm{d}\rho+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\psi\mathrm{d}\mu,



where for \psi\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(\mathcal{Y}), \psi^{*}(x)=\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\langle x,y\rangle-\psi(y) denotes the Legendre transform of \psi extended to +\infty outside of \mathcal{Y}. The following theorem provides a hybrid dual to primal coercivity theorem. It serves to directly extend [33, Theorem 3.2], which corresponds to the case q=2 and \theta=1.




Theorem 4.5 (Map coercivity with respect to dual suboptimality).



Let q\in[1,+\infty), and let \mathcal{X} and \mathcal{Y} be compact and convex subsets of \mathbb{R}^{d} for d\geq 2. Let \rho be a probability measure on \mathcal{X} satisfying 
                  
                    
                      A
                      
                        
                          
                          
                          
                        
                        
                          (
                          M
                          ,
                          θ
                          )
                        
                      
                    
                  
                , and \mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}). Then





\forall\psi\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(\mathcal{Y}),\quad\|\nabla\psi^{*}-T_{\rho\to\mu}\|_{L^{q}(\rho)}^{q}\leq C\left(\mathcal{K}_{\rho,\mu}(\psi)-\min_{\mathcal{C}^{0}(\mathcal{Y})}\mathcal{K}_{\rho,\mu}\right)^{\frac{\theta}{1+\theta}},



where C=D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}(1+c_{d}MD_{\mathcal{X}}^{d-1+\theta})(D_{\mathcal{Y}}D_{\mathcal{X}})^{-\theta/(1+\theta)}.





Proof.



Denote T=T_{\rho\to\mu}. Since \partial\psi^{*}(\mathcal{X})\subset\mathcal{Y}, we apply Lemma 2.5 with \rho_{0}=\rho_{1}=\tilde{\rho}=\rho, \gamma_{0}=(\id,\nabla\psi^{*})_{\#}\rho, \gamma_{1}=(\id,T)_{\#}\rho, \phi=\psi^{*}, \alpha,\beta the identity couplings, and \eta=0. For this choice of coupling,





\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{2}}\|y_{0}-y_{1}\|^{q}\mathrm{d}\pi_{\beta}=\|\nabla\psi^{*}-T\|_{L^{q}(\rho)}^{q}.



The lemma implies that for all s>0





\|\nabla\psi^{*}-T\|_{L^{q}(\rho)}^{q}\leq D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q-1}\left(\int_{\mathcal{X}}D_{s}\psi^{*}(x)\mathrm{d}\rho(x)+\frac{\Delta}{s}\right),



where





\Delta=\int_{\mathcal{X}}G_{\psi^{*}}(x,T(x))\mathrm{d}\rho(x).



Applying Theorem 2.2, for all s>0,





\|\nabla\psi^{*}-T\|_{L^{q}(\rho)}^{q}\leq D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q-1}\left(D_{\mathcal{Y}}c_{d}MD_{\mathcal{X}}^{d-1}s^{\theta}+\frac{\Delta}{s}\right).



If \Delta=0, letting s\downarrow 0 completes the proof. Otherwise, balancing the terms in s as in previous theorems, we choose





\left(\frac{s}{D_{\mathcal{X}}}\right)^{\theta}=\frac{\Delta}{D_{\mathcal{Y}}s}\implies s=\left(\frac{\Delta D_{\mathcal{X}}^{\theta}}{D_{\mathcal{Y}}}\right)^{1/(1+\theta)}.



Substituting this value gives





\|\nabla\psi^{*}-T\|_{L^{q}(\rho)}^{q}\leq D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}(1+c_{d}MD_{\mathcal{X}}^{d-1+\theta})(D_{\mathcal{Y}}D_{\mathcal{X}})^{-\theta/(1+\theta)}\Delta^{\theta/(1+\theta)}.



The proof is completed by observing that \Delta is a lower bound for the dual suboptimality gap. Since \psi^{**}\leq\psi,





\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}G_{\psi^{*}}(x,T(x))\mathrm{d}\rho(x)\leq
\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi^{*}(x)+\psi(T(x))-\langle x,T(x)\rangle\mathrm{d}\rho(x)




\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\rho,\mu}(\psi)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\langle x,T(x)\rangle\mathrm{d}\rho(x)




\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\rho,\mu}(\psi)-\min_{\mathcal{C}^{0}(\mathcal{Y})}\mathcal{K}_{\rho,\mu},



by optimality of T and strong duality.
∎






4.4. Stability of optimal maps




The following theorem provides a stability bound for optimal transport maps with respect to perturbation of both marginals, again extending a result [33, Theorem 3.3] to general exponents q and \theta, as well as improving the numerical constant.




A priori, there are some issues with regard to what one means by a bi-marginal stability result for optimal transport maps. In general, as soon as both marginals are perturbed, a Brenier map T_{\rho_{1}\to\mu_{1}} need not exist, since \partial\phi_{\rho_{1}\to\mu_{1}} may be multivalued on a set of positive \rho_{1} measure. Here we compare the gradients of the Brenier potentials with respect to L^{q}(\rho_{0}), using that the gradient of any convex function is well defined \rho_{0} almost everywhere.




Nonetheless, given a Brenier potential for the transport from \rho_{1} to \mu_{1}, there is no canonical choice for what values it should take outside the support of \rho_{1}, and so the gradients of two different Brenier potentials, although they are both well defined, might differ on a set of positive \rho_{0} measure. The below theorem applies to any Brenier potential which satisfies (2.1).




Theorem 4.6 (Bi-marginal Brenier gradient stability).



Let q\in[1,+\infty), let \mathcal{X} and \mathcal{Y} be compact subsets of \mathbb{R}^{d}, and let \rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}) satisfy 
                  
                    
                      A
                      
                        
                          
                          
                          
                        
                        
                          (
                          M
                          ,
                          θ
                          )
                        
                      
                    
                  
                . Then for all \rho_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}) and \mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}), and Brenier potentials \phi_{\rho_{i}\to\mu_{i}}





\|\nabla\phi_{\rho_{0}\to\mu_{0}}-\nabla\phi_{\rho_{1}\to\mu_{1}}\|_{L^{q}(\rho_{0})}^{q}\leq C\Big(D_{\mathcal{Y}}W_{1}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})+D_{\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\Big)^{\frac{\theta}{1+\theta}}



where C is the constant in Theorem 4.5.





Proof.



Let (\phi_{0},\psi_{0}) and (\phi_{1},\psi_{1}) be pairs of optimal potentials for the transport between \rho_{i} and \mu_{i} respectively. We apply the dual coercivity Theorem 4.5 to the \rho_{0},\mu_{0} problem, using that (\phi_{1},\psi_{1}) is a competitor to the dual \mathcal{K}_{\rho_{0},\mu_{0}}. Thus




(4.3)

\|\nabla\phi_{\rho_{0}\to\mu_{0}}-\nabla\phi_{\rho_{1}\to\mu_{1}}\|_{L^{q}(\rho_{0})}^{q}\leq C\left(\int\phi_{1}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}+\int\psi_{1}\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}-\int\phi_{0}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}-\int\psi_{0}\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}\right)^{\frac{\theta}{1+\theta}}.



Adding the second dual suboptimality gap





\int\phi_{0}\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}+\int\psi_{0}\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}-\int\phi_{1}\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}-\int\psi_{1}\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}\geq 0



to the right hand side of (4.3) inside the bracket,





\|\nabla\phi_{\rho_{0}\to\mu_{0}}-\nabla\phi_{\rho_{1}\to\mu_{1}}\|_{L^{q}(\rho_{0})}^{q}\leq C\left(\int(\phi_{1}-\phi_{0})\mathrm{d}(\rho_{0}-\rho_{1})+\int(\psi_{1}-\psi_{0})\mathrm{d}(\mu_{0}-\mu_{1})\right)^{\frac{\theta}{1+\theta}}.



Since \nabla\phi_{i}\subset\mathcal{Y} and \nabla\psi_{i}\subset\mathcal{X} for i=0,1, then the functions \phi_{1}-\phi_{0} and \psi_{1}-\psi_{0} are D_{\mathcal{Y}} and D_{\mathcal{X}} Lipschitz respectively, so the result follows by Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality.
∎





Remark 4.7 (Interpretation for statistical estimation of transport maps).



The theorem has the following implications for approximation of transport maps. It says that if one can solve the dual problem for \rho_{1},\mu_{1} to get some Brenier potential \phi_{\rho_{1}\to\mu_{1}} defined on all \mathcal{X}, then \nabla\phi_{\rho_{1}\to\mu_{1}} is a good approximator in L^{q}(\rho_{0}) for the transport map T_{\rho_{0}\to\mu_{0}}=\nabla\phi_{\rho_{0}\to\mu_{0}}. This means that a map constructed out of the gradient of the Brenier potential \phi_{\rho_{1}\to\mu_{1}} will, on average, send samples from \rho_{0} to a point near the image of the true optimal map T_{\rho_{0}\to\mu_{0}}.





The fixed source stability of optimal transport maps presented in the introduction is a direct consequence of the above version by taking \rho_{0}=\rho_{1}, since T_{\rho\to\mu_{i}}=\nabla\phi_{\rho\to\mu_{i}}.





4.5. Stability of optimal plans




We now prove the bi-marginal stability of optimal transport plans with respect to perturbation of both marginals. This is a relatively direct consequence of Theorem 4.2. In the appendix, we present a second proof of this theorem using Theorem 4.6 and Theorem 3.3.




Theorem 4.8 (Bi-marginal stability of optimal plans).



Let q\in[1,+\infty), and let \mathcal{X} and \mathcal{Y} be compact convex subsets of \mathbb{R}^{d} for d\geq 2. Let \rho_{0} be a probability measure on \mathcal{X} satisfying 
                  
                    
                      A
                      
                        
                          
                          
                          
                        
                        
                          (
                          M
                          ,
                          θ
                          )
                        
                      
                    
                  
                , let \mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}), and let \gamma_{\rho_{0}\to\mu_{0}}\in\Gamma(\rho_{0},\mu_{0}) be the unique optimal plan between them. Then for all \rho_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}), all \mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}) and any optimal plan \gamma_{\rho_{1}\to\mu_{1}}\in\Gamma(\rho_{1},\mu_{1}),





W_{q}(\gamma_{\rho_{0}\to\mu_{0}},\gamma_{\rho_{1}\to\mu_{1}})\leq C\Big(W_{1}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})+W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\Big)^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\theta)}},



where C=2\max(D_{\mathcal{X}},D_{\mathcal{Y}})^{\theta/(q(1+\theta))}C^{\prime} for C^{\prime} the constant in Theorem 4.2, with R_{\mathcal{X}} and R_{\mathcal{Y}} replaced by D_{\mathcal{X}} and D_{\mathcal{Y}}.





Proof.



Since all the quantities in the theorem are invariant under translation of source and target domains, we can assume without loss of generality that 0\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{Y}. We apply the arbitrary marginal coercivity theorem, Theorem 4.2, with \gamma_{i}=\gamma_{\rho_{i}\to\mu_{i}}, so that




(4.4)

W_{q}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\leq C^{\prime}\left(\left|\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\langle x,y\rangle\mathrm{d}(\gamma_{0}-\gamma_{1})\right|+D_{\mathcal{Y}}W_{1}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})+D_{\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\right)^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\theta)}},



and we replaced the instances of R_{\mathcal{X}} and R_{\mathcal{Y}} by D_{\mathcal{X}} and D_{\mathcal{Y}}, since 0\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{Y}. Let (\phi_{0},\psi_{0}) and (\phi_{1},\psi_{1}) be pairs of optimal maximum correlation potentials for the respective dual problems from \rho_{i} to \mu_{i}. Write \gamma_{i}=\gamma_{\rho_{i}\to\mu_{i}}. The compatibility condition for each of the two transport problems implies





\displaystyle\int\langle x,y\rangle\mathrm{d}(\gamma_{0}-\gamma_{1})=
\displaystyle\int\phi_{0}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}+\int\psi_{0}\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}-\int\phi_{1}\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}-\int\psi_{1}\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}




\displaystyle\leq
\displaystyle\int\phi_{1}\mathrm{d}(\rho_{0}-\rho_{1})+\int\psi_{1}\mathrm{d}(\mu_{0}-\mu_{1})




\displaystyle\leq
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{Y}}W_{1}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})+D_{\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}),



where we used the suboptimality of (\phi_{1},\psi_{1}) in the \rho_{0} to \mu_{0} dual problem, followed by Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality. Interchanging \gamma_{0} and \gamma_{1} and applying a symmetric bound controls the absolute value \left|\int\langle x,y\rangle\mathrm{d}(\gamma_{0}-\gamma_{1})\right|. Thus the gap





\Delta=\left|\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\langle x,y\rangle\mathrm{d}(\gamma_{0}-\gamma_{1})\right|+D_{\mathcal{Y}}W_{1}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})+D_{\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})



in Theorem 4.2 satisfies





\Delta\leq 2D_{\mathcal{Y}}W_{1}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})+2D_{\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\leq 2\max(D_{\mathcal{X}},D_{\mathcal{Y}})(W_{1}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})+W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})).



Substituting this bound into (4.4) completes the proof.
∎







5. Sharpness




In this section we provide two examples which prove that the four main theorems are sharp in exponent, in the sense that the same bound cannot hold with a better exponent. The first example provides sharpness for quantitative uniqueness as well as the same subgradient plan control, while the second provides sharpness for the stability and coercivity of plans, as well as stability of maps. The second example is heavily based on the example given in [28], which corresponds to the case \theta=1.




5.1. Sharpness for uniqueness




In general, it is challenging to ascertain a lower bound on W_{q}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}), since often the optimal coupling between the plans is not clear. This is in contrast to lower bounding a distance between maps \|T_{0}-T_{1}\|_{L^{q}(\rho)}, where as soon as the optimal map is known explicitly, the distance can be calculated. For this first example, it is sufficient for us to use the crude bound





W_{q}^{q}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\geq\int\dist((x,y),\spt\gamma_{0})^{q}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}(x,y),



as the expression on the right is more easily calculable. This bound holds since, at best, every point in \spt\gamma_{1} is coupled with its closest point to \spt\gamma_{0}.

5.2. Sharpness for stability of maps and plans




In our second example, we cannot use the crude support distance lower bound, and W_{q}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}) is instead lower bounded by a form of measure theoretic pigeonhole principle. Roughly speaking, we show that although for every point in \spt\gamma_{0} there is a nearby point in \spt\gamma_{1}, the mass given locally to certain regions of the product space by each plan is different, and so any coupling between the two plans must couple a certain amount of mass across a long distance.




The example shows that even for the fixed source estimate, the plan stability exponent is optimal. This automatically implies the same upper bound on the exponent for the distance between maps, since W_{q}(\gamma_{\rho\to\mu_{0}},\gamma_{\rho\to\mu_{1}})\leq\|T_{\rho\to\mu_{0}}-T_{\rho\to\mu_{1}}\|_{L^{q}(\rho)}. Thus although we do not explicitly calculate the map distance, this example also gives sharpness of the map stability, Theorem 4.6.

\frac{\theta}{q}-\frac{\alpha(r+\theta)}{r}\geq 0\implies\alpha\leq\frac{r\theta}{q(r+\theta)}.



When r=\infty, the bound W_{\infty}(\mu_{0},\mu_{i})\leq h_{i} gives





\frac{W_{q}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{i})}{W_{\infty}(\mu_{0},\mu_{i})^{\alpha}}\geq 2c_{\rho,\theta}^{1/q}a_{i}l_{i}^{1/q}h_{i}^{\theta/q-\alpha}\longrightarrow\infty\quad\text{if }\alpha>\theta/q.



Consequently, conclusions of Theorems 4.6 and 4.8 cannot hold with any exponent larger than the one proven.
∎






5.3. Sharpness for coercivity




The above example can be adapted to show that the coercivity theorems, Theorems 4.2 and 4.4, are also sharp in exponent. The general form of this is already sharp as a consequence of the sharpness for stability with r=1. The example below uses fixed marginals, establishing that the exponent in terms of the suboptimality deficit is also sharp.




Proposition 5.3.



Let d\geq 2 and \theta\in(0,1]. Let \mathcal{X} and \mathcal{Y} be compact convex sets in \mathbb{R}^{d} with nonempty interior. There exists a probability measure \rho on \mathcal{X} satisfying 
                  
                    
                      A
                      
                        
                          
                          
                          
                        
                        
                          (
                          M
                          ,
                          θ
                          )
                        
                      
                    
                  
                 for some M>0, a measure \mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}), and transport plans (\gamma_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in\Pi(\rho,\mu) such that for any q\in[1,+\infty) and





\alpha>\frac{\theta}{q(1+\theta)},



it holds that

\limsup_{i\to\infty}\frac{W_{q}(\gamma_{\rho\to\mu},\gamma_{i})}{\left(\int\langle x,y\rangle\mathrm{d}(\gamma_{\rho\to\mu}-\gamma_{i})\right)^{\alpha}}=\infty.

Proof.



We use the same construction as for the stability, taking the same \rho, and \mu=\mu_{0}. The optimal plan is induced by the map T_{0} already calculated. For a candidate suboptimal plan, we choose \gamma_{i}\in\Pi(\rho,\mu) induced by the (non-optimal) map





T_{i}(x)=\begin{cases}T_{0}(x)&x\notin R_{i}^{+}\cup R_{i}^{-}\\
y_{i}^{+}&x\in R_{i}^{+}\\
y_{i}^{-}&x\in R_{i}^{-}.\end{cases}



Using the same pigeonhole argument lower bound as for the stability, we deduce





W_{q}(\gamma_{\rho\to\mu},\gamma_{i})=2a_{i}\sigma_{i}^{1/q},



where the equality is a consequence of the upper bound by \|T_{0}-T_{i}\|_{L^{q}(\rho)}=2a_{i}\sigma_{i}^{1/q}. To compute the excess transport cost, we compute





\Delta_{i}:=\int\|T_{i}(x)-x\|^{2}-\|T_{0}(x)-x\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho(x)=\int\|x-y\|^{2}\mathrm{d}(\gamma_{i}-\gamma_{\rho\to\mu})=2\int\langle x,y\rangle\mathrm{d}(\gamma_{\rho\to\mu}-\gamma_{i}),



since the marginals are equal. The maps are equal on R_{i}^{+}\cap\{x_{2}\geq 0\} and R_{i}^{-}\cap\{x_{2}\leq 0\}, and for x\in R_{i}^{+}\cap\{x_{2}\leq 0\} we have





\displaystyle\|T_{i}(x)-x\|^{2}-\|T_{0}(x)-x\|^{2}=
\displaystyle\|x-y_{i}^{+}\|^{2}-\|x-y_{i}^{-}\|^{2}




\displaystyle=
\displaystyle(a_{i}-x_{2})^{2}-(a_{i}+x_{2})^{2}=4a_{i}|x_{2}|



with the value on R_{i}^{-}\cap\{x_{2}\geq 0\} equal by symmetry. Consequently,





\Delta_{i}=2a_{i}\int_{R_{i}^{+}\cup R_{i}^{-}}|x_{2}|\mathrm{d}\rho(x)=\frac{4a_{i}l_{i}}{Z_{\theta}}\int_{-h_{i}/2}^{h_{i}/2}|x_{2}|^{\theta}\mathrm{d}x_{2}=\frac{4}{Z_{\theta}(1+\theta)2^{\theta}}a_{i}l_{i}h_{i}^{1+\theta}.



Finally, for \alpha>0 and the same sequences as the previous proposition,





\frac{W_{q}(\gamma_{\rho\to\mu},\gamma_{i})}{\Delta_{i}^{\alpha}}=C_{\rho,\theta,q,\alpha}a_{i}^{\left(\frac{q+1}{q}-2\alpha\right)}h_{i}^{\left(\frac{\theta}{q}-\alpha(1+\theta)\right)}



so that the sequence only remains bounded as i\to\infty when





\frac{\theta}{q}-\alpha(1+\theta)\geq 0\implies\alpha\leq\frac{\theta}{q(1+\theta)}.



Thus Theorems 4.2 and 4.4 are also sharp in exponent when comparing plans with fixed marginals as required.
∎

\|\nabla\psi_{0}^{*}-\nabla\psi^{*}_{1}\|_{L^{q}(\rho)}^{\frac{q(1+\theta)}{\theta}}\leq C^{-1}J_{\rho}(\psi_{0},\psi_{1})



where





C=\theta(1+\theta)^{-\frac{1+\theta}{\theta}}4^{-\frac{1+\theta}{\theta}}\left(k_{q}D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q+\theta(q-1)}c_{d}MD_{\mathcal{X}}^{d-1}\right)^{-\frac{1}{\theta}}



and k_{q} is defined in the proof.





Proof.



For i=0,1, \phi_{i}=\psi_{i}^{*} is differentiable \rho-almost everywhere. Set u(x)=\nabla\phi_{1}(x)-\nabla\phi_{0}(x) and A=\|u\|_{L^{q}(\rho)}. If A=0, the result follows from convexity of \mathcal{K}_{\rho}, so assume A>0. By definition,




(A.2)

J_{\rho}(\psi_{0},\psi_{1})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}j(x)\mathrm{d}\rho(x)



where





j(x)=\frac{\phi_{0}(x)+\phi_{1}(x)}{2}-\left(\frac{\psi_{0}+\psi_{1}}{2}\right)^{*}(x).



For every h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}, the definition of the conjugate gives





\left(\frac{\psi_{0}+\psi_{1}}{2}\right)^{*}(x)\leq\frac{\phi_{0}(x+h)+\phi_{1}(x-h)}{2}.



Fix a point x where both \phi_{i} are differentiable. For some h\in\mathbb{R}^{d} to be fixed later, choose y_{h}^{0}\in\partial\phi_{0}(x+h) and y_{h}^{1}\in\partial\phi_{1}(x-h). Subgradient inequalities give





\phi_{0}(x)-\phi_{0}(x+h)\geq\langle y_{h}^{0},-h\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad\phi_{1}(x)-\phi_{1}(x-h)\geq\langle y_{h}^{1},h\rangle



so that





\displaystyle j(x)\geq
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\langle y_{h}^{1}-y_{h}^{0},h\rangle




\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\langle u(x),h\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle y_{h}^{1}-\nabla\phi_{1}(x),h\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla\phi_{0}(x)-y_{h}^{0},h\rangle.



We choose, for some \lambda>0 to be fixed later,





h_{\lambda}(x)=\begin{cases}\lambda\|u(x)\|^{q-2}u(x)&u(x)\neq 0,\\
0&u(x)=0.\end{cases}



Set \eta_{\lambda}:=D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q-1}\lambda, so that \|h_{\lambda}(x)\|\leq\eta_{\lambda}. Set k_{1}=1 and for q>1,





k_{q}=\frac{4^{q-1}(q-1)^{q-1}}{q^{q}}\geq\frac{3}{4}.



For q>1, Young’s inequality a^{q-1}b\leq a^{q}/4+k_{q}b^{q} implies




(A.3)

j(x)\geq\frac{\lambda}{4}\|\nabla\phi_{1}(x)-\nabla\phi_{0}(x)\|^{q}-\frac{k_{q}\lambda}{2}\left(\big(D_{\eta_{\lambda}}\phi_{0}(x)\big)^{q}+\big(D_{\eta_{\lambda}}\phi_{1}(x)\big)^{q}\right),



and when q=1 the same inequality holds directly since \|h_{\lambda}(x)\|\in\{0,\lambda\}. For any \lambda\geq 0, Theorem 2.2 gives





\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}k_{q}\big(D_{\eta_{\lambda}}\phi_{i}(x)\big)^{q}\mathrm{d}\rho(x)\leq
\displaystyle k_{q}D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q}c_{d}MD_{\mathcal{X}}^{d-1}\eta_{\lambda}^{\theta}




\displaystyle=
\displaystyle k_{q}D_{\mathcal{Y}}^{q+\theta(q-1)}c_{d}MD_{\mathcal{X}}^{d-1}\lambda^{\theta}=\overline{C}\lambda^{\theta}.



Thus integrating (A.3) with respect to \rho,




(A.4)

J_{\rho}(\psi_{0},\psi_{1})\geq\frac{\lambda}{4}A^{q}-\overline{C}\lambda^{1+\theta}.



We choose





\lambda^{\theta}=\frac{A^{q}}{4(1+\theta)\overline{C}},



so that substituting the chosen value of \lambda into (A.4) gives





J_{\rho}(\psi_{0},\psi_{1})\geq CA^{\frac{q(1+\theta)}{\theta}}



for the C given in the statement of the theorem, so the proof is complete.
∎
