# Two usable predecessors for partitions with large top four rows

Author: Myeongjin Kang. Parent-side mathematical development for T205M, not an independent review or an accepted result. Original prose CC BY 4.0; accompanying original code MIT.

This proves the high-capacity alternative in the active author's narrow-partition induction. The full multiplicity target remains separate: the low-capacity Kostka estimate and the representation-theoretic recursion must also be justified.

## Statement

Let m≥7, h=m−2, N=m(m+1)/2 and n=h(h+1)/2. Let λ be a partition of N with λ₁≤m. Set C=λ₁+λ₂+λ₃+λ₄, padding by zeros, and suppose C≥2m+1. Then there exist at least two distinct partitions ν of n such that

    λᵢ ≥ νᵢ ≥ λᵢ₊₄  for every i,

and neither belongs to

    E_h = {(n), (1^n), (h,1^(n−h)), (h,2,1^(n−h−2))}.

All four exceptional shapes are partitions at the stated h≥5. No transpose is applied to ν.

## The interval as an ideal lattice

Put μ=(λ₅,λ₆,…) and S=λ/μ, with the coordinate order on cells: (i,j)≤(i′,j′) if i≤i′ and j≤j′. Partitions μ⊆ν⊆λ correspond exactly to order ideals of S. A coordinate interval between comparable cells lies inside the skew diagram, so this order is the usual order generated by the row and column adjacencies.

The desired ideals have size

    k=n−|μ|=C−(2m−1).

Thus k≥2 and C−k=2m−1≥13. If H_j=λᵗ_j is a column height, column j of S comprises rows

    L_j=max(1,H_j−3), …, H_j.

Both L_j and H_j are nonincreasing in j, and each column has at most four cells. Write w=λ₁ for the number of columns.

## Every interior rank has at least two ideals

For this S, every rank r with 2≤r≤C−2 has at least two ideals. To prove this, suppose A is the unique ideal of rank r and put B=S\A. Every a∈A and b∈B must satisfy a≤b. Indeed b<a is precluded by the ideal property. If a and b were incomparable, adding the order relation b<a would leave an acyclic poset, which has a linear extension. The initial r cells of that extension form another ideal of S and cannot equal A, since A contains a but not b.

Let c be the greatest column meeting A and d the least column meeting B. The cross-comparability forces c≤d. If c<d, column 1 lies wholly in A and column w wholly in B, so H₁≤L_w. But H₁≥H_w≥L_w; equality implies H_w=1, hence H₁=1. Then C=w≤m, contrary to C≥2m+1.

If c=d is strictly between 1 and w, the same argument applies. If c=d=1, all of A is a prefix in column 1, while column w lies wholly in B. Hence L₁+r−1≤L_w≤L₁, giving r≤1. If c=d=w, all of B is a suffix in column w, while column 1 lies wholly in A. Hence H_w−|B|+1≥H₁≥H_w, giving |B|≤1. The possibility w=1 is excluded by C≤4. These contradictions prove the assertion.

Applying it at rank k already proves the theorem whenever none of the exceptional shapes is an interval predecessor. It remains to account for exceptions rather than merely count all predecessors.

## When an exception fits, the first column separates

The one-row exception (n) cannot fit because n>m≥λ₁ for m≥7. Denote the other three shapes by S_h, H_h and J_h, in their displayed order above.

If S_h fits, the lower containment μ⊆S_h implies λ₅≤1. If H_h fits, it implies λ₆≤1; if J_h fits, it implies λ₇≤1. Consequently, whenever any exception fits, every column beyond the first has height at most six. Also the length of λ is at least the smallest exceptional length,

    n−h=(m−2)(m−3)/2≥10.

The four first-column cells of S therefore have row indices at least seven. Every other cell has row index at most six and column index at least two. They are incomparable. Thus S is the disjoint union, as a poset, of a chain of four cells and a poset S′ of C−4 cells.

Every finite poset has an ideal of each size between zero and its cardinality, by taking prefixes of a linear extension. Since k=C−(2m−1)≤C−4, for every a=0,…,min(4,k) we may combine a first-column prefix of size a with an ideal of S′ of size k−a. These give min(4,k)+1 distinct predecessors.

At most two exceptional shapes can fit simultaneously. Their lengths are n, n−h+1 and n−h. Every interval predecessor has length between H₁−4 and H₁, while the first and last exceptional lengths differ by h≥5. Thus all three cannot fit.

For k≥3 there are at least four predecessors and at most two exceptions, proving the required conclusion.

## The rank two boundary

It remains to treat k=2, equivalently C=2m+1. The disjoint decomposition supplies at least three predecessors. For any rank-two ideal the number a of its first-column cells lies between zero and two, so any two corresponding predecessor lengths differ by at most two. S_h therefore cannot coexist with H_h or J_h, whose length differences from S_h are h−1≥4 and h≥5.

Suppose instead that H_h and J_h both fit. The containment μ⊆H_h implies λ₆≤1. Since λ has length at least ten, λ₅≥1. Hence the number of cells of μ outside its first column is λ₅−1. The numbers of cells of H_h and J_h outside their first columns are h−1 and h, respectively. Thus J_h\μ has h−λ₅+1 cells outside the first column. Its total size is k=2, so λ₅≥h−1=m−3. But then

    C=λ₁+λ₂+λ₃+λ₄ ≥4λ₅≥4(m−3)>2m+1,

where the last inequality holds for every m≥7. This contradicts C=2m+1. At most one exceptional predecessor fits at rank two, leaving at least two usable predecessors among the three constructed ones. This completes the proof.
