                                                             Weak Linear Mappings – A Survey
                                                                                 Hans Havlicek

                                         There are various concepts of structure preserving mappings in geometry. It is the aim
                                         of the present paper to give a survey on geometrical characterizations of some of those
                                         mappings. We discuss the results for projective spaces in some detail and report on
                                         generalizations to other spaces.
                                             We shall come across partially defined mappings. The notation ϕ : P ≻→ P ′ is used in
                                         order to point out that ϕ is a mapping with domain dom ϕ ⊂ P and image set im ϕ ⊂ P ′ .
                                         The exceptional set of ϕ (with respect to P) is given as ex ϕ := P \ dom ϕ. The mapping
                                         ϕ is said to be globally defined (with respect to P) provided that ex ϕ is empty. The
                                         notation ϕ : P → P ′ is maintained for globally defined mappings only.
                                             If we are given any subset M ⊂ P, then {X ϕ | X ∈ M ∩ dom ϕ} is a well–defined
                                         set. By abuse of notation, it is written as Mϕ . Hence Mϕ = ∅ exactly for M ⊂ ex ϕ.

1.2    Definition and Examples of Linear Mappings
In the sequel (P, L) and (P ′ , L′ ) denote arbitrary projective spaces. Property (3) is
adopted in the following purely geometric definition:
   A linear mapping 2 of projective spaces is a mapping ϕ : P ≻→ P ′ (defined on some
subset of P) satisfying the following conditions:

(L1) ({X} ∨ {Y })ϕ = {X}ϕ ∨ {Y }ϕ for all X, Y ∈ P, X 6= Y .
(L2) If X ϕ = Y ϕ for distinct points X, Y ∈ dom ϕ, then there is at least one exceptional
     point on the line {X} ∨ {Y }.

Condition (L2) is only used to rule out the possibility that all points of a line are mapped
to the same point. There are three possibilities for the image of a line l ∈ L:
    #(l ∩ ex ϕ) ≥ 2: Then l ⊂ ex ϕ, i.e. we have an exceptional line.
    #(l ∩ ex ϕ) = 1: Then all points of l ∩ dom ϕ are mapped to the same point.
    #(l ∩ ex ϕ) = 0: Then ϕ | l is injective and lϕ ∈ L′ .

We give some examples of linear mappings:
  1. The mapping (1) is linear, if f : V → V′ is semilinear.
  2. Let S ⊂ P be a subspace. Write P/S for the set of all subspaces of the form
     S ∨ {X}, where X ∈ P \ S. Then P/S is the set of points of the quotient space of
     (P, L) modulo S. The canonical projection

                        ψS : P ≻→ P/S, X 7→ S ∨ {X} for all X ∈ P \ S

      is a surjective linear mapping with ex ψS = S.
   3. With S as above, the canonical injection ιS : S → P, X 7→ X is a globally defined
      linear mapping of the projective space on S in P.
   4. A projection is based on two complementary subspaces of S, T of (P, L) as follows:

            π : P ≻→ T , X 7→ X π with {X π } := (S ∨ {X}) ∩ T for all X ∈ P \ S.

      This π is a surjective linear mapping with ex π = S.
   5. Any collineation of projective spaces is a globally defined bijective linear mapping.

1.3   Brauner’s Theorem
Theorem 1 (Brauner [7]) Let ϕ : P ≻→ P ′ be a mapping of projective spaces.
  1. If ϕ satisfies (L1), then the exceptional subset ex ϕ ⊂ P and the image set im ϕ ⊂ P ′
     are subspaces.
  2. If ϕ satisfies (L1) and #im ϕ 6= 1, then ϕ is linear.
  3. Each linear mapping ϕ is decomposable into the canonical projection P ≻→ P/ex ϕ,
     a collineation of this quotient space onto the subspace im ϕ, and the canonical in-
     jection of im ϕ in P ′ .

As a matter of fact Brauner did not discuss mappings ϕ satisfying (L1) and #im ϕ = 1.
It is immediate that here ex ϕ can be any subspace of (P, L) other than P.
    By the second fundamental theorem of projective geometry we obtain:

Corollary 1 Each linear mapping ϕ : P(V) ≻→ P(V′ ) such that im ϕ contains a tri-
angle is induced by a semilinear mapping f : V → V′ which is determined to within a
non–zero factor in K ′ .

The existence of a triangle in im ϕ is needed in Corollary 1 to avoid “degenerate” cases:
   1. If im ϕ is a line, then such an f needs not exist, since a collineation of 1–dimensional
      projective spaces is just a bijection of their point sets. However, when #K ∈
      {2, 3, 4}, then ϕ is nevertheless induced by a semilinear mapping.
   2. If im ϕ is a singleton or empty, then K and K ′ need not be isomorphic. If K and
      K ′ are assumed to be isomorphic, then ϕ can be induced by a semilinear mapping.
We remark that under the assumptions of Corollary 1 the transpose of the semilinear
mapping f induces the transpose of ϕ.
    A geometric characterization of linear mappings of real projective spaces is due to
Rehbock [48], [49]. Timmermann [54] characterizes the projections in projective spaces
by conditions similar to our (L1). [9, 4.5] and a paper by Faure and Frölicher [21]
contain proofs of Brauner’s Theorem. A characterization of a linear mapping ϕ of a
projective space in its dual space in terms of two mappings is given by Faure and Frö-
licher [23]. The two mappings are ϕ and ϕT |P, where P is identified with a subspace
of the bidual projective space. When these two mappings coincide, then one obtains a
possibly degenerate quasipolarity. See also Lenz [34], [35].

1.4   Definition and Examples of Weak Linear Mappings
Formula (2) motivates the following definition: A weak linear mapping of projective
spaces is a mapping ϕ : P ≻→ P ′ (defined on some subset of P) satisfying

(WL1) ({X} ∨ {Y })ϕ ⊂ {X}ϕ ∨ {Y }ϕ for all X, Y ∈ P, X 6= Y .

By (WL1), there are four possibilities for the image of a line l ∈ L:
    #(l ∩ ex ϕ) ≥ 2: Then l ⊂ ex ϕ, i.e. we have an exceptional line.
    #(l ∩ ex ϕ) = 1: Then all points of l ∩ dom ϕ are mapped to the same point.
    #(l ∩ ex ϕ) = 0 and all points of l are mapped to the same point.
    #(l ∩ ex ϕ) = 0 and ϕ | l is injective, but lϕ is not necessarily a line.
Thus now, in contrast to a linear mapping, we do not rule out the possibility that all
points of a line are mapped onto the same point.
    Each mapping ϕ : P → P ′ , which is globally defined, injective, and collinearity–
preserving, is a weak linear mapping. If non–collinearity of points is also preserved,
then ϕ is called an embedding. An embedding is called strong, if independent points in
(P, L) always go over to independent points in (P ′ , L′ ).

We give some examples of weak linear mappings:
  1. Formula (1) yields a weak linear mapping.
  2. Each linear mapping is also a weak linear mapping.
  3. Let L/K be a field extension and let V be a vector space over K. Then

                           ϕ : P(V) → P(V ⊗K L), aK 7→ (a ⊗ 1)L

     is a strong embedding. This ϕ is a collineation if, and only if, K = L or dim V ≤ 1.
  4. The following embedding is not strong (Brezuleanu and Rădulescu [11]): Let
     L/K be a field extension such that there exist elements 1, y0 , y1 , y2 ∈ L which are
     linearly independent in the left vector space L over K. Then define

       ϕ : P(K 4 ) → P(L3 ), (a0 , a1 , a2 , a3 )K 7→ (a0 + y0 a3 , a1 + y1 a3 , a2 + y2 a3 )L.

  5. Each product of weak linear mappings is a weak linear mapping.

1.5   A Fundamental Theorem for Weak Linear Mappings
Theorem 2 (Faure and Frölicher [22], Havlicek [31]) Each weak linear mapping
ϕ : P(V) ≻→ P(V′ ) such that im ϕ contains a triangle is induced by a weak semilinear
mapping f : V → V′ which is determined to within a non–zero factor in K ′ .

We infer from the decomposition of a weak semilinear mapping in 1.1 the following

Corollary 2 Each weak linear mapping ϕ : P ≻→ P ′ of desarguesian projective spaces
such that im ϕ contains a triangle can be decomposed into a strong embedding P → P ′′
in some projective space (P ′′ , L′′ ) and a linear mapping P ′′ ≻→ P ′ .

Corollary 2 does not remain true if the hypothesis on im ϕ is dropped: If im ϕ does not
contain a triangle of (P ′ , L′ ), then ex ϕ is still a subspace. For each point Y ∈ im ϕ
the extended preimage ex ϕ ∪ {Y }ϕ is a subspace. Thus P/ex ϕ is partitioned into

subspaces. However, those subspaces are not necessarily isomorphic. Conversely, any
subspace S ⊂ P and any partition of P/S into subspaces determines a weak linear
mapping in some projective line.
Corollary 3 Let ϕ : P(V) ≻→ P(V′ ) be given as in Theorem 2. Furthermore, assume
that each monomorphism K → K ′ is surjective. Then ϕ is a linear mapping.
For example, each monomorphism of R is surjective [1, p. 88], whereas C admits non–
surjective monomorphisms [1, p. 114].
    A recent result due to Kreuzer [33] says that each pappian projective space is
embeddable in a pappian projective plane. Other results on embeddings and specific
examples are due to Benz [1, p. 109], Brezuleanu and Rădulescu [11], Brown [13],
Carter and Vogt [16], [17], Havlicek [30], and Limbos [37], [39], [40].
    Another special class of weak linear mappings are semicollineations of projective
spaces, i.e. globally defined bijective mappings which preserve the collinearity, but not
necessarily the non–collinearity of points. Ceccherini [18] has given an example of a
semicollineation of a 4–dimensional projective space onto a non–desarguesian projective
plane. Cf. also Bernardi and Torre [2], and Maroscia [43]. It seems to be an open
problem, if each semicollineation of desarguesian projective spaces with dimensions ≥ 2
is a collineation.

1.6   Local Characterizations
The definition of a linear mapping ϕ uses all points of P. The following “local” results
characterize a linear mapping only in terms of its domain or in terms of a subset of its
domain.
   Firstly, we state the following variant of axiom (L1):
(L1’) ({X} ∨ {Y })ϕ = {X}ϕ ∨ {Y }ϕ for all X, Y ∈ dom ϕ, X 6= Y .

Theorem 3 (Sörensen [52]) Let ϕ : P ≻→ P ′ be a mapping of projective spaces sat-
isfying (L1’). Then the following conditions hold true:
   1. The image set im ϕ ⊂ P ′ is a subspace.
   2. If span (ex ϕ) 6= P and if #im ϕ ≥ 2, then the exceptional subset ex ϕ ⊂ P is a
      subspace.
   3. If span (ex ϕ) 6= P and if im ϕ contains a triangle, then ϕ is a linear mapping.

The subsequent examples illustrate that the assumptions in Theorem 3 are essential:
  1. Let ε : P ′ → P be a non–surjective strong embedding with P ′ 6= ∅, e.g., the canon-
     ical injection of a proper subspace P ′ ⊂ P. Define ϕ : P ≻→ P ′ as inverse mapping
     of ε with domain P ′ε . This ϕ satisfies (L1’), but ex ϕ ⊂ P is not a subspace.
  2. Let #P > 1 and #P ′ = 1. There exists an M ⊂ P other than a subspace. Define
     ϕ : P ≻→ P ′ by ex ϕ = M. Hence #im ϕ = 1 and (L1’) is trivially true.

                                           5
    3. Let (P, L) be a projective space containing a line t1 with more than three points.
       Choose a complement S1 of t1 and let S be a hyperplane containing S1 . Write
       ϕ1 : P ≻→ t for the projection onto t with exceptional subspace S1 and put t :=
       t1 \S. The mapping ϕ : P \ S → t, X 7→ X ϕ1 satisfies (L1’) with (P ′ , L′ ) = (t, {t}),
       but clearly (L1) is violated. Thus ϕ is not a linear mapping [52].

Secondly, axioms (L1) and (L2) are modified as follows:

(L1”) X, Y, Z collinear implies X ϕ , Y ϕ , Z ϕ collinear, for all X, Y, Z ∈ dom ϕ.
(L2”) If X ϕ = Y ϕ for distinct points X, Y ∈ dom ϕ, then ϕ | (({X} ∨ {Y }) ∩ dom ϕ) is
     a constant mapping.

Moreover, we need some topological tools: A projective space (P, L) is said to carry a
linear topology, if each line x ∈ L is endowed with a non–trivial3 topology Tx such that
all perspectivities between intersecting lines are continuous. A subset O of P is called
linearly open, if O ∩ x is open in the topological space (x, Tx ) for all lines x ∈ L [3]. If
(P, L) is a topological projective space [14, Ch. 23], then the induced topologies on the
lines yield a linear topology. Each open set M ⊂ P is also linearly open. However, there
are linear topologies that do not arise in this way. An example is given by the cofinite
topology in a projective space with infinite order: A subset m of a line a is defined to be
open, if a \ m is finite.

Theorem 4 (Frank [24]) Let (P, L) and (P ′ , L′ ) be projective spaces satisfying the
minor axiom of Desargues. Suppose that ϕ : P ≻→ P ′ is a mapping satisfying (L1”) and
(L2”) such that im ϕ contains a triangle. Each of the following conditions is sufficient
for the existence of a unique linear mapping ϕ : P ≻→ P ′ extending ϕ:
  (a) (P, L) admits a linear topology such that dom ϕ is linearly open. Moreover, there
      exists a line l ∈ L such that (dom ϕ ∩ l)ϕ contains non–empty open set with respect
      to some linear topology of (P ′ , L′ ).
  (b) (P, L) and (P ′ , L′ ) are spaces of the same finite order N . Moreover, x ∩ dom ϕ 6= ∅
      implies #(x ∩ dom ϕ) > 32 N for all x ∈ L.

Theorem 4 generalizes a result of Lenz [36] for real projective spaces. There are also sev-
eral theorems in the literature giving local characterizations of embeddings of projective
planes and spaces. Refer to Bognár and Kertész [6], Brezuleanu and Rădulescu
[12], and the references given in [24].


2        Other Spaces
2.1        Partial Linear Spaces and Linear Spaces
The geometric conditions (L1), (L2), (L1’) etc. still do make sense in a wider context.
    Let (P, L) be a pair consisting of a point set P and a set L ⊂ 2P whose elements are
called lines. If any two distinct points are on at most one common line, and if each line

contains at least two points, then (P, L) is called a partial linear space. A linear space is
a partial linear space such that any two distinct points are on a common line.
    Sörensen [52] has discussed mappings between linear spaces (P, L) and (P ′ , L′ )
satisfying (L1). Given M ⊂ P the trace space (M, LM ), which is defined by putting

                          LM := {l ∩ M | l ∈ L, #(l ∩ M) ≥ 2},

is also a linear space. Thus, if a linear mapping is not globally defined, one can go over
to the trace space on its domain. Hence it means no loss of generality, that all mappings
in [52] are assumed to be globally defined.
    Examples of semicollineations of linear spaces and necessary conditions for a semi-
collineation of linear spaces to be a collineation are due to Kreuzer [32]. There is a
widespread literature on embeddings of linear spaces in projective spaces [14, Ch. 6].

2.2   Affine Spaces
The following seems to be part of the folklore: Suppose that a globally defined mapping
ϕ of the affine space on V (over K with #K > 2) in the affine space on V′ is satisfying
(L1). If im ϕ contains a triangle, then ϕ − 0ϕ is a semilinear mapping V → V′ .
     This result follows from Theorem 4 by going over to the projective closures and, for
infinite order, by applying their cofinite topology. See Sörensen [52] for a direct proof.
     Globally defined mappings of affine spaces with property (WL1) have been discussed
by Zick [58], [59]. Since such a mapping needs not preserve parallelity of lines, fractional
weak semilinear mappings have been introduced to obtain an algebraic description.
     Even an embedding ϕ of the affine space on V in the affine space on V′ has in general
not the property that ϕ−0ϕ is a weak semilinear mapping. One class of examples is given
by spaces over GF(2). A more interesting example is the embedding of an affine plane
over GF(3) in the complex affine plane which is based on the nine inflections of an elliptic
cubic curve, another example is due to Benz [1, p. 113]. See Bichara and Korchmáros
[4], Carter and Vogt [16], [17], Limbos [37], [38], [39], [40], Ostrom and Sherk [44],
Rigby [50], Thas [53]. Schaeffer [51] has given a local characterization of some
embeddings which can also be found in [1, 3.2].

2.3   Grassmann Spaces
Write d P for the set of all d–flats (i.e. d–dimensional subspaces) of an n–dimensional
projective space (P, L) (1 ≤ d ≤ n − 2) and d L for the set of all pencils of d–flats.
Then (d P, d L) is a partial linear space, called Grassmann space. Two distinct “points”
d X, d Y ∈ d P are “collinear” if, and only if, d X ∩ d Y is a (d − 1)–flat. The Grassmann

space (d P, d L) is covered by one–dimensional projective spaces, namely the pencils of
d–flats.
    A mapping ϕ : d P ≻→ P ′ in the point set of a projective space (P ′ , L′ ) is called
linear, if the restriction of ϕ to each pencil is a linear mapping of projective spaces [26].
    If (P, L) is pappian, then each linear mapping ϕ : d P ≻→ P ′ determines a mapping
ϕb : Gn,d ≻→ P ′ of the associated Grassmann variety Gn,d (cf. Burau [15]) such that the

restriction to each line l ⊂ Gn,d is linear. By Havlicek [27], this ϕ

linear mapping of the ambient space of Gn,d . A weaker result is due to Wells [55].
    If (P, L) is not pappian, then Grassmann varieties are not available due to a non–
existence theorem [27], and little seems to be known here.
    A geometric hyperplane of (d P, d L) is a subset of d P which intersects each pencil
of d–flats in exactly one or in all elements. The geometric hyperplanes are exactly the
exceptional sets of linear mappings ϕ : d P ≻→ P ′ with #im ϕ = 1. Hence in the pappian
case all geometric hyperplanes arise as hyperplane sections of the associated Grassmann
variety [27, 4.5]. All geometric hyperplanes in the non–pappian case have been descibed
by Hall and Shult [25].
    It seems that Eckhart [19] and Rehbock [48], [49] have been the first geometers to
discuss linear mappings of the Grassmann space formed by the lines of the real projective
3–space. See Brauner [7], [8], [10], Havlicek [28], and Lübbert [41] for further refer-
ences and kinematic line mappings. Zanella [56] has investigated linear mappings of
Grassmann spaces which are globally defined and injective. He has given sufficient condi-
tions for the image of such a mapping to be projectively equivalent to the corresponding
Grassmann variety and rather sophisticated examples where this is not the case. Cf. also
Bichara and Zanella [5].

2.4   Product Spaces
Another partial linear space is the product space of two projective spaces. If both spaces
have isomorphic commutative ground fields, then their product space can be represented
as a Segre variety. Linear mappings of a product space in a projective space may be
defined as for Grassmann spaces. A thorough discussion of globally defined injective
linear mappings can be found in a paper by Zanella [57]. Unfortunately, we cannot
give the details here due to the lack of space. Let us just remark that the situation is
more complicated than for Grassmann spaces. Cf. also Bichara and Zanella [5].

2.5   Lattice Geometries
A significant generalization of linear mappings to projective and affine lattice geometries
has been given by Pfeiffer [45], [46], and Pfeiffer and Schmidt [47]. See furthermore
Faigle [20].
