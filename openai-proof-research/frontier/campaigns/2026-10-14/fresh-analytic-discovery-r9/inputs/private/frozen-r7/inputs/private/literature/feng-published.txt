Abstract
We consider infinite random planar maps decorated by the critical Fortuin-Kasteleyn
model with parameter q > 4. The paper demonstrates that when appropriately
rescaled, these maps converge in law to the infinite continuum random tree as pointed
metric-measure spaces, that is, with respect to the local Gromov-Hausdorff-Prokhorov
topology. Furthermore, we also show that these maps do not admit any Fortuin-
Kasteleyn loops with a macroscopic graph distance diameter. Our proof is based on
Scott Sheffield’s hamburger-cheeseburger bijection.

1 Introduction

In the past few decades, there has been extensive research concerning random planar
maps. A planar map is a graph embedded into the Riemann sphere in such a way that no
two edges cross viewed modulo orientation-preserving homeomorphisms. Self-loops
and multiple edges are allowed. The study of planar maps dates back to the 1960s with
Tutte’s attempts at solving the four-color problem [42–45].

   One of the motivations for studying random planar maps comes from statistical
mechanics. Certain random planar maps are discrete analogs of γ -Liouville quan-
tum gravity (LQG) surfaces with γ ∈ (0, 2], which are random topological surfaces
equipped with a measure, a metric, and a conformal structure. Such surfaces play a
vital role in many physics models, starting from the foundational work of Polyakov
[36]. The earliest mathematical studies include [37] and [21]. We will not use the
mathematical theory of LQG in this paper. For more background on LQG, readers can
refer to [15, 29, 40].
   This paper will focus on the random planar map decorated by the critical Fortuin-
Kasteleyn (FK) model with parameter q > 0 [22, 39]. This model can be thought of
as a discrete version of 2D gravity in which the geometry of the space interacts with
the matter. The critical FK model has a deep relationship with the critical q-state Potts
model. In fact, their partition functions are equal, see e.g. [8].
   For a planar map M, a spanning subgraph G is formed by all the vertices of M and
a subset of the edges of M. The dual map M ∗ and dual spanning subgraph G ∗ are
defined in the following way:
 1. The vertices of M ∗ are the faces of M:For each face f of M (including the external
    face), we assign a vertex f ∗ on it, which will be the vertex of M ∗ . See the blue
    vertices in the top right of Figure 1).
 2. The edges of M ∗ are the dual edges of edges in M: When M’s faces f 1 and f 2
    share a common edge e, we draw an edge e∗ crossing e and connecting f 1∗ and
     f 2∗ .
 3. The vertices of G ∗ are the vertices of M ∗ .
 4. As a subgraph of M ∗ , G ∗ takes the edge e∗ if and only if G does not take e.
   Next, we draw lines from each f ∗ ∈ G ∗ to each corner of f , connecting the vertices
of f in G. See the bottom left of Figure 1. These edges we draw are called Tutte edges.
See the bottom left of Figure 1, Tutte edges are in gray. We get a triangulation in this
step.
   Note G and G ∗ are both connected only if G is a spanning tree. And their clusters
are distributed alternately: means that the outer boundary of a G cluster is an inner
boundary of G ∗ , and vice versa. We draw the interfaces between these clusters. They
are loops crossing over the Tutte edges, and are called FK loops. See the bottom right
of Figure 1, the FK loops are brown circles.
   We say a map M is rooted if it is decorated by a distinct Tutte edge. Note this Tutte
edge has exactly one endpoint in primal map M, we will treat this endpoint as the root.

case has been studied in [14] and [26–28], which computed exponents associated with
the length and the loop area, etc. and described the scaling limits of some of these
quantities.

Remark 1.5 Another topology with respect to the convergence of random planar maps
has been studied in [39]. In short, we can glue together, in a certain sense, a pair of
discrete plane trees associated with a certain pair of random walks in Z, to encode
maps {Mi } (see Section 2 for details). When the walks converge to correlated contin-
uous functions which encode the map M, we say Mi → M in the mating of trees
topology. In [39], it is proved that the random walks which encode FK maps will
converge to the correlated Brownian motion in distribution when q < 4, and converge
to identical Brownian motion when q ≥ 4. The trees that Brownian motion associated
with are CRTs. Gluing together two identical CRT just gives back to the original CRT.
We also know from the monumental paper [20] that we can glue together the CRTs
associated with two correlated Brownian motions to get an LQG surface. Thus, paper
[39] proves the conjecture in mating of trees sense when q = 4. However, mating
of trees convergence is far from being equivalent to GHP convergence. For example,
when q = 4, the mating of trees convergence says they should converge to CRT. But
in GHP sense, the conjecture is that q-FK planar maps converge to critical LQG.
Remark 1.6 With more work, we can also prove (M, εad, ε2 bμv , o) converges to the
CRT, where μv is the counting measure on vertices. Here, b is the expectation of
the root’s degree in M. The degree of the root is proven to have an exponential tail
distribution by [18, Proposition 6], hence b is finite.

Remark 1.8 For many random planar map models, the planar maps are expected or
proven to converge to the CRT when the parameter values are in the “degenerate”
regime. Some proven examples include, for instance, increasing triangulations [6],
maps with a boundary much larger than the volume [12], outerplanar maps [17], limits
of Lévy maps [32], block-weighted maps [23][3]. Also, for the maps that belong to the
universality class of supercritical LQG, they are believed to converge to CRT when
conditioned to be finite. See [13] for an example and [24][7][4] for some discussions.
   In recent years, Stufler has developed a robust machinery [41] for the convergence
of maps which can be decomposed along a tree-like structure. The current paper shares
the same spirit as some of the ideas in this survey (as well as in [23][3]): we first couple
the original map with a tree. When there are infinitely many cut points, the geodesics
in both the map and the tree are forced to pass through these cut points. This allows
us to apply the law of large numbers to show that the distance function is close to the
tree distance multiplied by a constant. Then it suffices to prove that the tree converges
to the CRT. However, in that framework, the tree needs to be simply generated (e.g.,
Galton–Watson trees), which is not the case in our setting: We use the spanning tree
as an intermediate object, but it is not a simply generated tree.

2 Hamburger-Cheeseburger bijection

In this section, we will describe a bijection from random words to planar maps dec-
orated by the FK model, which is known as the Hamburger-Cheeseburger bijection
[39]. We will mainly describe the correspondences in the infinite case. They are similar
to the finite case which has been discussed in [39].
   Recall the definitions above Figure 1, we can see the Tutte edges in fact form a
quadrangulation2 , which we denote by Q. Note for each face of Q, exactly one of its
diagonals appears in either G or G ∗ . From this point of view, we see T := Q ∪ G ∪ G ∗
as a canonical triangulation of (M, G), and call it the Tutte triangulation of (M, G).
Note that (M, G) and T uniquely determine each other.
   Next, we introduce the inventory accumulation model in [39]. Imagine in a busy
restaurant, the kitchen produces hamburgers and cheeseburgers, while the customers
