Two Seymour vertices in every sink-free oriented graph
An exceptional-boundary and aggregate-ﬁber argument
Myeongjin Kang
October 16, 2026
Abstract
We prove, relative to two explicitly stated results of the pinned OpenAI manuscript on Seymour’s conjecture, that every nonempty ﬁnite sink-free oriented graph has two distinct vertices
whose exact second out-neighborhood is at least as large as their ﬁrst. This is Conjecture 2 of
Dara, Francis, Jacob and Narayanan. The inputs are the ordinary one-vertex Seymour theorem
and a ﬁnite-relation pruning lemma; their truth is stipulated here. A minimum counterexample
has a unique good vertex with one successor. Deleting that vertex leaves weak subset deﬁcits
together with a strict inequality for subsets that do not reach the successor in one or two steps.
In a transitive blowup, compatibility with the diagonal selects many ﬁbers with this strict inequality. Their total gain dominates the possible losses of all remaining ﬁbers, allowing the
established pruning objective to close. All deductions from the two inputs are proved below.
Finite diagnostics and formal supporting lemmas are distinguished from the full graph theorem.

1

Statement, notation and dependencies

An oriented graph is a ﬁnite directed graph without loops, parallel arcs or opposite pairs of arcs.
For a vertex v of D, let N1,D (v) be its set of out-neighbors and let N2,D (v) consist of the vertices
at directed distance exactly two from v. In particular, a ﬁrst neighbor is not counted again in N2 .
A vertex is good if |N2,D (v)| ≥ |N1,D (v)|. A sink has no outgoing arc.
Theorem 1. Assume Premises 1 and 2. Every nonempty ﬁnite oriented graph with no sink has at
least two distinct good vertices.
There is no strong-connectivity, degree, missing-edge or size restriction in this conclusion. Sinkfreeness is essential: a transitive tournament has only one good vertex, its sink. The statement is
the already formulated Conjecture 2 of Dara et al. [2], not a new conjecture.

A minimum counterexample and protected subsets

Suppose the conclusion of Theorem 1 is false. Choose a sink-free counterexample D minimizing its
number of vertices and, subject to that, its number of arcs. Premise 1 implies that D has exactly
one good vertex, denoted by z.
The graph D is strongly connected. Otherwise, a terminal strongly connected component C
is proper. It cannot be a singleton, because D is sink-free; its induced graph is sink-free. Both
out-neighborhoods of each vertex in C stay inside C, so minimum order gives two good vertices of
D, a contradiction.
Every vertex x ̸= z has outdegree at least two. Indeed, outdegree zero is excluded. If N1,D (x) =
{y}, then N2,D (x) = N1,D (y): loops and opposite arcs exclude both x and y from the set on the
right. It is nonempty, so x would be good. On the other hand, z has outdegree exactly one. If
it had at least two outgoing arcs, deleting one would leave a sink-free graph with fewer arcs. At
any other vertex the ﬁrst neighborhood would be unchanged and the second could not enlarge. All
vertices other than z would remain bad, contradicting arc minimality. Write z → w for its unique
outgoing arc.
Lemma 2 (Protected strict deﬁcit). For every nonempty S ⊆ V (D) \ {z}, one has δD (S) < 0.
Proof. Fix S, and write I = FD (S) ∩ S and E = FD (S) \ S. Strong connectivity and the fact that
S is proper imply E ̸= ∅. For T ⊆ E put g(T ) = |FD (T ) \ (I ∪ T )|.
For each proper T ⊊ E, delete all arcs from S to E \ T , and call the resulting graph DT . At
least one arc is removed. If DT has a sink, this sink lies in S and is newly good. If DT is sink-free,
minimality gives at least two good vertices in it. Outside S, ﬁrst neighborhoods are unchanged and

initial charge (p, s) lies outside J1 (P ) ∪ J2 (Q). Indeed, membership in J1 (P ) would give (u, s) ∈ P
with u → p, hence (i, s) ∈ J12 (P ). Membership in J2 (Q) would give (p, t) ∈ Q with t → s, hence
(p, j) ∈ J22 (Q). Both are impossible. Thus the initial charge set B in the premise has size at most
d.

Attribution and the scope of the contribution

Dara et al. [2] state exactly our conclusion as Conjecture 2 and its unique-good/sink version as
Conjecture 3. Their Lemma 18 describes the singleton boundary after deleting a vertex: a good
vertex that becomes bad on reinsertion must point to the deleted vertex and see its successors
in its ﬁrst two neighborhoods. Their Theorem 6 proves a deletion-survival statement when the
deleted graph is a tournament missing a matching, using safe completions, modules and medianorder arguments; Corollary 5 supplies two good vertices in that restricted class. Their Proposition 8
uses minimality and arc deletion in comparing the conjectures. These are antecedents, not new
constructions claimed here.
Seacrest’s subset-deletion method [3, Lemma 4] is an antecedent of the protected-subset argument. Brantner et al. [4, Theorems 4.1–4.3] give minimum-counterexample properties and lexicographic ampliﬁcation. OpenAI [1] develops the strict subset-deﬁcit reduction, transitive blowup
estimate, compatible-pair objective, complement step and pruning closure used here. The source’s
route begins with strict deﬁcits for every relevant subset of an all-bad counterexample and makes
each individual blown-up subset strictly concave.
6

The added operation here is the conjunction of (4)–(7) with (9)–(11). A deleted exceptional
vertex leaves only weak deﬁcits globally, but also a distinguished successor. Compatibility selects
m − 1 anchor ﬁbers in its block; their combined gain m(m − 1) dominates the residual n2 m.
Strictness is obtained after summing the constrained ﬁbers. Neither standard minimality nor the
ﬁnal imported pruning operation receives separate novelty credit.
For additional comparison, Guo, Kang and Zwaneveld [5] study pointwise tightness under products and module replacements; these are product precedents rather than the exceptional-anchor
selection above. Tahara [6] studies excess margins and restricted constructions. An excess bound
and a bound on the number of good vertices are diﬀerent: one good vertex can contribute several
units of excess. The literature comparison covers the named primary bodies and is bounded. It is
not a certiﬁcation of worldwide priority. The theorem is presented relative to its stipulated inputs,
not as a wholly independent proof of ordinary SNC.

Validation and formalization boundary

The universal proof is Sections 2–6. Computation is not used to infer the theorem. Two independently written ﬁnite diagnostics accompany it. The author’s bitmask checker veriﬁes 352,224
deletion-boundary identities across 23,664 small orientations and veriﬁes a six-vertex counterexample to a discarded shortcut. An independent reviewer’s set-based checker veriﬁes 15,129 deletion
endpoint and increment cases, 198,936 blowup subset cases, and 151,362 anchor exclusion cases.
The latter enumerates orientations on three and four base vertices, with internal block sizes three
and two. These ﬁnite tests detect local errors of signs, endpoints or directions; they do not verify
the unrestricted assertion by enumeration.
For clarity, the discarded shortcut asserted that strong connectivity plus δH (S) ≤ 0 for all
nonempty S forces zero singleton deﬁcits. It is false: with vertices 0, . . . , 5 and outgoing bitmasks
(36, 25, 10, 17, 37, 10), all 63 nonempty subset deﬁcits are nonpositive but the singleton deﬁcits are
(0, −1, 0, 0, −1, 0). The proof above uses the stronger exceptional-successor information (7), not
this shortcut. The counterexample is not an S2 counterexample.
