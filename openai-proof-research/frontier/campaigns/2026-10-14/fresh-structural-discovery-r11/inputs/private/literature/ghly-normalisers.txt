  NORMALISERS OF PARABOLIC SUBGROUPS OF ARTIN–TITS GROUPS
                AND TITS CONE INTERSECTIONS

              OWEN GARNIER, EDMUND HENG, ANTHONY LICATA, AND ODED YACOBI


        Abstract. Let (W, S) be a Coxeter system and let J ⊆ S. Motivated by 3-fold flops, Iyama
        and Wemyss study the hyperplane arrangement in the Tits cone intersection of J, which is a J-
        relative generalisation of the classical Coxeter arrangement. For Γ of spherical type, we show that
        the complexified complement of this J-relative hyperplane arrangement is a K(π, 1) space for the
        normaliser (quotient) of the standard parabolic subgroup attached to J in the Artin–Tits group.
        For general Γ we show that the Brink–Howlett groupoid, which describes normalisers of parabolic
        subgroups of the Coxeter groups, has its universal cover described by the wall-and-chamber structure
        of the Tits cone intersection. We use this to show that wall crossing sequences satisfy an “atomic
        Matsumoto relation”, generalising a theorem of Ko and answering questions raised by Iyama and
        Wemyss.




                                              1. Introduction
1.1. General overview. Let (W, S) be a Coxeter system and consider the associated hyperplane
arrangement (Cone◦ , H). Here Cone◦ denotes the interior of the Tits cone and H is the set of
reflecting hyperplanes. Much of the structure of W is reflected in the geometry of the arrangement
(Cone◦ , H), and this geometry also plays an important role in the study of the Artin–Tits group
A of W . Indeed, the complexified hyperplane complement X C associated to (Cone◦ , H) (see (2.2))
carries a free and properly discontinuous action of W , and the quotient X C → X C /W is a normal
covering inducing the short exact sequence:
                                1 → π1 (X C , x) → π1 (X C /W, x) → W → 1.                                     (1.1)

We then have isomorphisms A ∼   = π1 (X C /W, x) and P ∼ = π1 (X C , x) (P is the pure Artin–Tits group).
Conjecturally, X /W is a classifying space for A. This statement – known as the K(π, 1) conjecture
                  C

– is proven for spherical types [9], FC-types [7], affine types [27], and more [11, 19, 23], but remains
open in general.
   Now let J ⊆ S. The main goal of this paper is to generalise and study J-relative versions of
the groups and arrangements associated to (W, S). In this relative setting, the groups W and
A are replaced by normaliser quotients of the standard parabolic subgroups associated to J, and
the Coxeter arrangement (Cone◦ , H) is replaced by an arrangement restricted to the Tits cone
intersection of J introduced by Iyama–Wemyss [24].

1.1.1. Normaliser quotients of parabolics. The choice J defines standard parabolic subgroups WJ ⊆
W and AJ ⊆ A, normaliser subgroups NormW (WJ ), NormA (AJ ) and normaliser quotients:
                 N (W, J) – NormW (WJ )/WJ              and     N (A, J) – NormA (AJ )/AJ .                    (1.2)
There is significant literature investigating these groups: the papers [3, 22, 26] initiate the study of
normaliser quotients of Coxeter groups. In particular, Brink and Howlett introduce a groupoid BH
in [5] (see Section 4) and use it to present and study N (W, J). For Artin–Tits groups, normaliser
quotients were studied in [14, 17, 28] for spherical types, [15] for FC type, [16] for two-dimensional
type, and [18] for affine types Ar and rC.

1.1.2. Tits cone intersection. On the geometric side, the Tits cone intersection of J is a convex
cone Cone(J) ⊆ Cone, and we can consider the restriction HJ of H to this subspace; see Sec-
tion 3.1 for the precise definitions. As in the classical case, the hyperplane arrangement in the
interior (Cone(J)◦ , HJ ) is locally finite (see Proposition 3.6), and when J = ∅ the arrangement
(Cone(J)◦ , HJ ) recovers the classical Coxeter arrangement (Cone◦ , H).
   The hyperplane arrangement (Cone(J)◦ , HJ ) plays an important role in the homological min-
imal model program and the theory of 3-fold flops [32]. Here, each chamber of (Cone(J)◦ , HJ )
labels the derived category of a minimal model of a 3-fold flopping contraction; or algebraically,
its corresponding non-commutative crepant resolution. Adjacent chambers are related by flopping
equivalences and geodesic paths with the same source and target give rise to compositions of flop-
ping functors that are isomorphic. As such, the fundamental group of the complexified hyperplane
complement XJC (see (3.26)) acts on the derived categories via composition of these flopping equiv-
alences. For more details, we refer the reader to [12, 20, 21] for the geometric setting, and [1, 24]
for the algebraic setting. Motivated by this, Iyama and Wemyss classify these arrangements in low
ranks [24], noting that they are not in general Coxeter arrangements (see Figure 1 in §7).

1.2. Main results. Just as the Coxeter arrangement (Cone◦ , H) controls the Coxeter group W
and Artin–Tits group A, our results show that the Tits cone intersection (Cone(J)◦ , HJ ) controls
the normaliser quotients:
     • In arbitrary type, we show that the groupoid BH can be obtained geometrically from the
       arrangement (Cone(J)◦ , HJ ). We also prove an atomic Matsumoto theorem for BH .
     • In spherical type, we show that the N (W, J)-quotient of the complexified hyperplane com-
       plement XJC associated to (Cone(J)◦ , HJ ) is a K(π, 1) space for N (A, J).
We now state the precise theorems.

formations, etc. (see Section 2.4). In particular, any connected groupoid G admits a universal
groupoid covering Gr → G , whose deck transformation group is the vertex group of G .

                                          2. Background
2.1. Hyperplane arrangements. Let V be a real vector space. A hyperplane will always refer
to a linear hyperplane (so all hyperplanes pass through the origin). Throughout this paper, a
hyperplane arrangement consists of a convex subset T ⊆ V and a (possibly infinite) set H of
hyperplanes in V such that H ∩ T ̸= ∅ for all H ∈ H. We denote a hyperplane arrangement by the
pair (T, H). Given a hyperplane arrangement (T, H) and a convex subset T ′ ⊆ T , we can define
a restricted hyperplane arrangement in T ′ by removing from H all the hyperplanes that do not
intersect with T ′ . For notation simplicity, we simply denote this arrangement by (T ′ , H), with the
understanding that only hyperplanes in H that intersect T ′ are considered. Finally, we say that a
hyperplane arrangement is locally finite if for all x ∈ T , there exists an open neighbourhood Ux of
x in T such that Ux only intersects finitely many hyperplanes in H.               S
   Given a hyperplane arrangement (T, H), the connected components of T \ H∈H H are called
chambers. Following [24, Definition 1.27], a wall of a chamber C is the intersection of C with
a hyperplane H ∈ H that has codimension 1. (Note that the support of a face is called a wall in
[4, 9, 29], which we have reserved for a different usage above.)

   To do so we need the following lemma, which is a special case of [31, Lemma 9]. We included a
proof in our setting for the convenience of the reader. Note that we say a hyperplane H separates

Proposition 3.6. Every point u ∈ Cone(J)◦ is contained in an open ball in Cone(J) that intersects
only finitely many hyperplanes in HJ . In particular, (Cone(J)◦ , HJ ) is a locally-finite hyperplane
arrangement.
Proof. Firstly, note that the interior of a convex space in a finite dimensional vector space is convex,
so Cone(J)◦ is convex.
   Let u be an element of Cone(J)◦ . Seeing u as an element of Cone, we can consider a neighbour-
hood of u in Cone which intersects only the hyperplanes in H containing u. For instance, take the
union of the strata of the natural stratification of Cone (from (3.4) with J = ∅) whose closure
contains u. We can thus consider a small enough open ball B centred at u so that B ∩ Cone only
intersects the hyperplanes in H containing u. Since u lies in the interior of Cone(J) in ΘJ , we can
also assume (by shrinking B if necessary) that B ∩ ΘJ is an open ball in Cone(J) that contains u.
   We will show that only finitely many hyperplanes in HJ intersect B ∩ ΘJ by constructing two
points v and v ′ in B ∩ Θ(J) such that (i) they touch no hyperplanes in HJ , and (ii) their line
segment [v, v ′ ] contains u as an interior point. From this, it follows that any H ∈ H satisfying
H ∩ ΘJ ∈ HJ and u ∈ H has to separate v and v ′ in Cone. Since all hyperplanes in HJ are of
the form H ∩ ΘJ , it follows from Lemma 3.5 that only finitely many hyperplanes in HJ intersect u
(even though there may be infinitely many hyperplanes in H intersecting u). By the construction
of B, all hyperplanes in H intersecting B must intersect u, so we get that B ∩ ΘJ only intersects
finitely many hyperplanes in HJ as desired.
   We now provide the construction of such v and v ′ . By looking at the stratification (3.4), we see
that the complement of all the hyperplanes in HJ in Cone(J) is dense in Cone(J). This allows us
to choose some point v in the open ball B ∩ ΘJ which lies on no hyperplane in HJ . Being an open
ball centred at u, B ∩ ΘJ contains the symmetric of v relative to u defined by v ′ – u + (u − v). Note
that if v ′ is contained in some hyperplane H ∩ ΘJ ∈ HJ , then the hyperplane H intersects B and
hence contains u (by construction of B). We then have that H ∩ ΘJ also contains v = u + (u − v ′ ),
which contradicts our choice of v. As such, both v and v ′ are points in B ∩ ΘJ which lie on no
hyperplane of HJ . It is clear from our construction that the segment [v, v ′ ] passes through u, hence
v and v ′ satisfy both of the desired properties, which completes the proof.                          □

3.5. The Deligne groupoid. The Deligne groupoid was introduced in [9] to study the topology
of finite simplicial arrangements (and prove the K(π, 1) conjecture for finite real reflection groups).
Although our setting is more general than that of Deligne, the construction of the Deligne groupoid
can be carried out similarly. However, the key features proven by Deligne, namely that the Deligne
groupoid is a Garside groupoid equivalent to the fundamental groupoid of the complexified com-
plement, only hold (to our knowledge) in the setting of finite simplicial arrangements.
   To the locally-finite arrangement (Cone(J)◦ , HJ ), we associate an oriented graph on the set
of vertices Cham(J). To every pair of adjacent chambers we associate a 2-cycle, i.e. given two
adjacent chambers (x, I) and (y, K) there is one arrow (x, I) → (y, K) and another opposite arrow
(y, K) → (x, I). We continue to use the notation ωa,I as above to label these arrows.
   A path in this graph from (x, I) to (y, K) is a geodesic if it is a path of minimal length between
(x, I) and (y, K). Equivalently, it crosses every hyperplane H ∈ HJ at most once.
   Define D + := D + (J) as the category with:
       • objects given by Cham(J); and
       • morphisms HomD + ((x, I), (y, K)) are paths from (x, I) to (y, K) modulo the relation gen-
         erated by the identification of any two geodesic paths with the same source and target.
The arrangement groupoid D := D(J) associated to (Cone(J)◦ , HJ ) is defined as the groupoid
completion of D + . If Γ is of spherical-type, then (Cone(J)◦ , HJ ) = (ΘJ , HJ ) is a finite simplicial

                     4. The geometry of the Brink–Howlett groupoid
   In [5] Brink and Howlett introduced a groupoid in order to study the normaliser of a standard
parabolic subgroup in a Coxeter group. In this section, we give an explicit realisation of its universal
cover in terms of the geometry of the associated Tits cone intersection. In Corollary 4.13 we use
this to give a new proof to parts of one of Brink and Howlett’s main results (Theorem A in [5]).
We then explain in Section 4.2 how to use the connection between Brink and Howlett’s work and
the Tits cone intersection to answer questions of Iyama and Wemyss about “atomic Matsumoto
relations”.
