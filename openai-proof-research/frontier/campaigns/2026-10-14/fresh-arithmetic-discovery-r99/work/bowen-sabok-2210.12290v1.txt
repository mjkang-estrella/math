                                                   Abstract. We show that for every coloring of the rationals into finitely
                                                   many colors, one of the colors contains a set of the form {x, y, xy, x + y}
                                                   for some nonzero x and y.

                                                                            1. Introduction
                                              The classical theorem of Schur [Sch16] states that for any finite coloring
                                           of the naturals, one of the colors contains a subset of the form {x, y, x + y}
                                           for some nonzero x, y. This result has had quite a few generalizations. For
                                           instance, Rado [Rad33] generalized Schur’s theorem to more general families
                                           of linear equations and the Folkman theorem [GRS90, Theorem 11] says that
                                           for every r we can find a monochromatic set consisting of all sums of subsets
                                           of an r-element set.
                                              In [Hin79] Hindman famously asked (see also the textbook of Graham,
                                           Rothschild and Spencer [GRS90]) whether any finite coloring of N contains
                                           a monochromatic set of the form {x, y, xy, x + y} for some nonzero x, y. Our
                                           main result is as follows.
                                           Theorem 1.1. For any coloring of the rationals into finitely many col-
                                           ors there exists a monochromatic set of the form {x, y, xy, x + y} for some
                                           nonzero x, y.
                                              In fact, in Theorem 4.3, we prove an extension of the above result in-
                                           volving arithmetic progressions and several variables. In particular, we get
                                           monochromatic sets of the form {x, y, xy, x + iy : i ≤ k} for any k ∈ N.
                                              Recently, there has been quite a bit of progress on Hindman’s conjecture.
                                           Green and Sanders [GS16], building on earlier work of Shkredov [Shk10]
                                           and Cilleruelo [Cil12], showed that for any number n of colors there exists
                                           a constant cn > 0 such that in any n-coloring of Fp there are at least cn p2
                                           monochromatic tuples of the form {x, y, xy, x + y}. In particular, the result
                                           of Green and Sanders implies that for any number n there exists a prime pn
                                           such that if p > pn , then in any coloring of Fp into n colors there exists at

giving the following.
Corollary 1.2. For every n there exists a prime p such that whenever a

monochromatic set of the form {x, y, xy, x + y} for some nonzero x and y.
   In the case of two colors much more was known. Graham showed that
any 2-coloring of {1, . . . , 252} contains a monochromatic configuration the
form {x, y, xy, x + y}, and Hindman showed the same for any 2-coloring
of {2, . . . , 990} [Hin79]. It is worth noting that these proofs were based
on a computer search and only recently the first author [Bow22] gave a
mathematical proof of the fact that any 2-coloring of the naturals contains
a monochromatic configuration the form {x, y, xy, x + y}.
   In [Hin74] Hindman proved that for any coloring of the naturals into
finitely many colors, one of the colors contains all sums of finite subsets of
an infinite set. A very elegant proof of the latter theorem was given by
Galvin and Glazer and their method has since been used to prove a number
of other strong combinatorial results (see, e.g., the textbook of Hindman–
Strauss [HS12] or of Todorčević [Tod10]). For instance, Bergelson, Hindman
and Leader [BHL99] showed how such methods can be used to show that in
any measurable coloring of the reals there exists an infinite set all of whose
finite products and sums are monochromatic.
   The classical van der Warden theorem [vdW27] says that for any coloring
of the naturals into a finite number of colors, one of those colors contains
arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions. This has been famously general-
ized by the Szeméredi theorem [Sze75] which ensures that arbitrarily long
arithmetic progressions can be found in any subset of the naturals of posi-
tive density. Since the ergodic-theoretic proof of the Szemerédi theorem by
Furstenberg [Fur77], there has been quite a few advances in the field, and
many generalizations have been proved using methods coming from ergodic
theory (see, e.g., the textbook of McCutcheon [McC99]).
   Our proof of Theorem 1.1 uses two main new ingredients. The first ingre-
dient is a result due to Bergelson and Glasscock [BG20] that is a quantitative
version of the Szemerédi theorem and is based on the density version of the
Hales–Jewett theorem [FK91]. The second ingredient seeks to localize cer-
tain thick sets with respect to a given finite coloring of the rationals. This
ingredient is stated in purely combinatorial terms, in order to make the pa-
per accessible to a wider audience. However, our original motivation was

   In this section we recall several notions of size that will be useful in the
structure of the additive group (Q, +) throughout the remainder of this
paper.

Definition 2.1.
     • A subset of Q is IP if it contains a set of the form FS(A) for some
       infinite sequence A of elements of Q.
     • Given r ∈ N, a subset of Q is IPr if it contains a set of the form

   Recall that an invariant mean on a commutative semigroup S is a pos-
itive linear functional d of norm 1 which is translation invariant on the
space of all bounded, real-valued functions on S with the k · k∞ norm. Any
commutative semigroup admits an invariant mean.
   The Szemerédi theorem [Sze75] states that a subset of (N, +) of positive
density contains artitrarily long arithmetic progressions. The set of possi-
ble differences of such arithmetic progressions is an IP∗ set, as proved by
Furstenberg and Katznelson [FK85]
   The following result of Bergelson and Glasscock [BG20, Theorem 7.5] is
a quantitative strengthening of Szemerédi’s theorem that we will use in the
inductive steps of our construction.

Theorem 2.2 (Bergelson, Glasscock, [BG20] Theorem 7.5). Let s ∈ N and
α > 0 be given. There exists r ∈ N and α′ > 0 for which the following holds.
For any commutative semigroup C and homomorphisms ϕ1 , . . . , ϕs : C → C,
for any invariant mean d on C and A ⊆ C with d(A) > α the set

           {c ∈ C : d(A − ϕ1 (c) ∩ . . . ∩ A − ϕs (c)) > α′ }   is IP∗r .

   We will apply the above result in case C is the additive group (Q, +) and
the homomorphisms ϕ1 , . . . , ϕs are of the form ϕi (c) = qi · c for some qi ∈ Q.
The key point in our application is that the numbers r and α′ depend only on
s and α, and not on the particular choice of the homomorphisms ϕ1 , . . . , ϕs .

Definition 3.1.
     • A set T ⊆ Q \ {0} is (multiplicatively) thick if for any finite F ⊆
       Q \ {0} there is an a ∈ Q with a · F ⊆ T ,
     • A set S ⊆ Q \ {0} is (multiplicatively) syndetic if there is a finite
       F ⊆ Q \ {0} so that Q \ {0} = F · S.

intervals, and it is syndetic if it has bounded (multiplicative) gaps. Note
that a set is thick if and only if its complement in Q \ {0} is not syndetic.

rather than (Q, ·) is because we want to obtain non-zero elements in our
main result. We will sometimes abuse the notation slightly and say that
a subset of Q is thick or syndetic if its intersection with Q \ {0} has this
property.

                                   6. Open problems
  Our main result was a common extension of Hindman’s conjecture and the
van der Waerden theorem, so it is natural to wonder if our approach might
extend to generalize the polynomial van der Waerden theorem as well.
Question 6.1. Let P be a finite set of integral polynomials. Does any finite
coloring of Q contain a monochromatic set of the form {x, y, xy, x + p(y) :
p ∈ P }?
  This is known to hold even for N if the y term is dropped [Mor17]. Our
approach would immediately extend to prove this if the IP∗r polynomial
Szemerédi theorem were known to be true, but perhaps finding the right
quantitative version of the polynomial van der Waerden theorem will suffice.
  Another potential direction is to extend our results to generalize the geo-
metric van der Waerden theorem as well. Currently even the following is
open.
Question 6.2. Does every finite coloring of Q contain a monochromatic set
of the form {xy, xy 2 , x + y}?
   Finally, let us mention that Hindman also conjectured stronger state-
ments, namely that any finite coloring of N contains monochromatic sets of
the form F S(A) ∪ F P (A) for A ⊆ N arbitrarily large. Our approach can
deal with some subsets of these configurations in Q, but the problem still
seems difficult even for |A| = 3 in Q.
