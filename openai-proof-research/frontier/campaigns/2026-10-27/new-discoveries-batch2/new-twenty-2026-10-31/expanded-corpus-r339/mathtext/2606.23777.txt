Abstract
Quantum tomography and quantum retrodiction are traditionally viewed as separate inference tasks. We show that the two are manifestations of the same underlying principle. We prove that the Petz recovery map of a measurement channel is a gradient of the log-likelihood used in maximum-likelihood tomography, and we derive its extension to arbitrary quantum channels. Consequently, for measurement channels, repeated applications of the Petz map monotonically increase the likelihood, naturally yielding a method for quantum tomography.
Quantum tomography and quantum retrodiction are very similar in spirit but differ in their mathematical formulations. Quantum tomography seeks to identify the quantum state that most likely generated a set of measurement outcomes and to design measurements that enable this reconstruction as efficiently as possible. Quantum retrodiction, by contrast, seeks to invert a generally non-invertible quantum channel by inferring the input state that would have produced a given observed output state.
In quantum tomography, the task is performed by making measurements and observing either individual outcomes, probability distributions, or expectation values of observables to probe the space of density matrices. Since no single projective measurement can uniquely determine a quantum state, this typically requires measurements in multiple bases. The state reconstruction methods may differ in practice but should converge to the same solution for complete and noise-free data. The two prominent methods are linear inversion [106, 59, 47, 31], which seeks to solve for the quantum state as a solution to a set of linear equations, and maximum-likelihood estimation [43, 51, 69, 78, 92, 12, 38, 14, 6, 3], which aims to find the state that most-likely generated the data. Other methods include Bayesian state [13, 45, 54, 39, 97, 66, 20], shadow [4, 44, 98, 91], least squares [68], gentle or weak measurements [11, 109], compressed-sensing [40, 35], matrix product states [27, 57], self-guided [34, 21], and neural network tomography [65, 102, 110, 18, 77].
Quantum retrodiction, on the other hand, assumes knowledge of the full output quantum state, rather than only measurement outcomes or a probability distribution, together with the channel that produced it. Its task is then to infer a possible input state that could have led to the observed output. For a unitary evolution, retrodiction is trivial and amounts to evolving the observed state backward in time. For non-invertible channels, however, some information about the input is inevitably lost, so the retrodicted state cannot be uniquely determined. How to assign this missing information remains an open question, with the Petz recovery maps representing two prominent mathematically motivated approaches. Their appeal stems from the properties of exact recoverability [84, 85, 88], where the Petz map recovers the original state exactly when equality in the data-processing inequality is achieved, and approximate recoverability [76, 33, 107, 29, 28, 101, 50], where an averaged rotated Petz map recovers a state close to the original one when the loss under data processing is small. Moreover, in the commuting case, the Petz map reduces to classical Bayesian updating motivating its interpretation as a quantum analogue of Bayes’ theorem [58, 103, 100, 79, 80, 32, 8, 94, 36, 63, 62, 64].
Both quantum tomography and quantum retrodiction have critical applications in quantum technologies: quantum tomography remains the go-to method for full quantum diagnostics, enabling the characterization and verification of quantum states and processes in quantum computers, sensors, and communication devices [24, 90, 67, 30]. It provides information about state-preparation fidelity, gate errors, noise sources, and the security of quantum communication protocols [47, 95]. Quantum retrodiction plays a central role in quantum error correction and channel reversal. The Petz map is optimal for perfectly correctable codes and remains near-optimal when the code is only approximately correctable [9, 73, 37, 111, 23], as supported by recent experimental demonstrations [60, 89, 70, 96]. It has also found applications across quantum information theory [10, 52], quantum thermodynamics [5, 56, 7, 16, 32, 17, 72], and quantum gravity [26, 22].
In this paper, we mathematically connect these two frameworks by extending quantum retrodiction to the multi-channel setting required for tomography and extending quantum tomography beyond measurement channels to arbitrary quantum channels. We show that the gradient of the log-likelihood is represented by a quantum channel, which we call the Kubo–Mori (KM) update. It shares the Bayesian interpretation with the Petz map while being derived directly from the underlying inference problem. For the measurement channel, when the predicted output commutes with the observed evidence, the Petz map and the KM update coincide. The Petz map itself then becomes a gradient of the standard log-likelihood. We show that the KM update increases the log-likelihood locally, prove the global monotonicity for the commuting-output case, and provide evidence for the general case. Finally, we use these results to develop and implement tomographic protocols based on iterating the Petz recovery map and the KM update for standard and general-channel quantum tomography, respectively.
Mathematical preliminaries.
We consider the setting of quantum state inference. The goal is to infer an unknown input state {\rho} from measurement data obtained through a known quantum channel \mathcal{E} . Since {\rho} is not directly accessible, only its image under \mathcal{E} can be estimated from data. We therefore write
\displaystyle X\geqslant 0,\qquad Y\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathcal{E}(\sigma)>0,\quad\Tr[X]=\Tr[Y]=1,
(1)
where X denotes the evidence and Y the prediction corresponding to a candidate input state \sigma . The evidence may be the mean state of a classical register, an output state estimate obtained from a preceding tomographic procedure, or a state constructed from incomplete measurement data supplemented by additional physical constraints or inference principles, such as maximum or minimum entropy, entanglement, or a prescribed mean energy. Y>0 ensures that \ln Y is well-defined.
We assume that conditions (1) hold throughout the paper.
We consider the problem of finding the state \sigma that best explains the
evidence X , by maximizing
\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\Tr\!\big[X\ln\mathcal{E}(\sigma)\big],
(2)
which we refer to as the log-likelihood functional. Equivalently, this minimizes the Umegaki relative entropy
D(X\|\mathcal{E}(\sigma)) , which has an operational meaning through
quantum Stein’s lemma as the asymptotic rate governing optimal discrimination
between the two states [104, 41, 75, 74].
For the quantum-to-classical (i.e., measurement) channel,
{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma)=\sum\nolimits_{i}\Tr[\Pi_{i}\sigma]\outerproduct{i}{i},
(3)
which maps a quantum state to measurement outcomes encoded in a classical register, the log-likelihood functional (2) reduces to the standard form,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma)=\sum\nolimits_{i}\hat{p}_{i}\ln\Tr[\Pi_{i}\sigma],
(4)
used in maximum-likelihood quantum tomography, where \hat{p}_{i}=N_{i}/N are relative measured frequencies, \{\Pi_{i}\} is a set of POVM elements, and \{\ket{i}\} is an orthonormal basis of the classical register.
KM update as a gradient of the log-likelihood.
A gradient of the log-likelihood (2), with an appropriate choice of the input metric, defines a quantum channel,
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}}(X)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sqrt{\sigma}\,\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\!\big[\Omega_{Y}^{-1}\!(X)\big]\sqrt{\sigma},
(5)
which maps the evidence state with a candidate input state into an updated input state \sigma_{+} . We call this map the KM update. \Omega_{Y}^{-1} denotes the inverse KM superoperator. See the End Matter for details.
Relation between the KM update and the Petz recovery map.
The Petz recovery map is defined as [85],
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}}(X)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sqrt{\sigma}\,\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\!\big[Y^{-1/2}XY^{-1/2}\big]\sqrt{\sigma},
(6)
with X and Y as in Eq. (1).
We have the following relation.
Theorem 1.
The KM update can be expressed as,
\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}}(X)=\sqrt{\sigma}\,\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\!\Big[k(K_{Y})\big[Y^{-1/2}XY^{-1/2}\big]\Big]\sqrt{\sigma},
(7)
where K_{Y}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=[\ln Y,\cdot] encodes the noncommutativity between the evidence X and the prediction Y ,
and
k(\omega)=\frac{\omega}{2\sinh(\omega/2)}=1-\frac{\omega^{2}}{24}+\mathcal{O}(\omega^{4}).
(8)
Thus, the Petz map is the zeroth-order approximation to the KM update. Since k is an even function, the leading noncommutative correction is quadratic in K_{Y} . This is why, for weak noncommutativity, the Petz map and the KM update nearly coincide. Although all these maps reduce to the ordinary Petz map in their respective commuting limits, the KM update is distinct both from the map proposed in Ref. [36], which is derived from a fidelity criterion for matching forward and reverse processes, and from the weighted rotated Petz map [28], despite using the same weights \beta_{0} (see Eq. (17)). Additional properties of the KM update, together with all proofs, are provided in the Supplementary Material (SM) [2]. The underlying geometric intuition is illustrated in Fig. 1.
Figure 1: Schematic representation of the KM update. The current estimate \sigma predicts Y=\mathcal{E}(\sigma) , while the data define an output-space evidence operator X ; ideally, X=\mathcal{E}(\rho) . The inverse KM action at Y gives the corrected evidence \Omega_{Y}^{-1}\!(X) , which is pulled back through \mathcal{E}^{\dagger} to the Hilbert–Schmidt gradient {G_{\!X}}(\sigma) . Conjugation by \sqrt{\sigma} produces the positive state update \sigma_{+}=\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}}(X) , with updated prediction Y_{+}=\mathcal{E}(\sigma_{+}) .
Monotonicity.
We define a line-search update using a Riemannian gradient \Delta ,
\sigma_{\eta}=\sigma+\eta\Delta=(1-\eta)\,\sigma+\eta\,\sigma_{+},
(9)
where \sigma_{+}=\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}}(X) corresponds to the full step, \eta=1 . This update in the density matrix is in the direction of the maximum log-likelihood increase (see the End Matter), which guarantees that the log-likelihood will increase locally. We derive an exact quantitative bound showing this.
Theorem 2.
The minimal and maximal guaranteed increase in the log-likelihood is given by,
-\ln\!\big(1\!-\!\eta\textsl{g}_{\sigma}\!(\Delta_{\sigma_{\eta}}^{\sigma},\Delta)\big)\leqslant\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma_{\eta})\!-\!\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma)\leqslant\eta\textsl{g}_{\sigma}(\Delta,\Delta),
(10)
where \!\Delta_{\sigma_{\eta}}^{\sigma}\!\!\!\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\!\!\!G_{\!X}^{\sigma}(\sigma_{\eta})\!\!-\!\!\sigma , \!\Delta\!\!\!=\!\!\!\Delta_{\sigma}^{\sigma} , and \!G_{\!X}^{\sigma}(\sigma_{\eta})\!\!=\!\!\sqrt{\sigma}\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}[\Omega_{\mathcal{E}(\sigma_{\eta})}^{-1}\!(X)]\sqrt{\sigma} . For non-invertible \sigma , the expressions are evaluated by replacing \sigma^{1/2}\sigma^{-1/2}\!\to\!\mathds{1} , which leads to -\ln\Tr\!\big[X\Omega_{\mathcal{E}(\sigma_{\eta})}^{-1}\!(Y)\big] and \eta\!\Tr\!\big[\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}[\Omega_{\mathcal{E}(\sigma)}^{-1}(X)]\Delta] on the left and right-hand sides, respectively.
This theorem shows that, unless the Riemannian gradient \Delta=0 (i.e., \sigma is a fixed point of the KM update; a stationary point of the log-likelihood for an invertible \sigma ; see the End Matter), there always exists a sufficiently small \eta>0 such that the argument of the logarithm is less than one. Consequently, the log-likelihood increases strictly. Inspired by this, we formulate the following conjecture: the KM update increases the log-likelihood globally.
Conjecture 3.
The KM update can only increase the log-likelihood,
\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma_{+})\geqslant\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma),
(11)
where \sigma_{+}=\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}}(X) .
Equality holds iff \sigma is the fixed point of the KM update, \sigma_{+}=\sigma .
Numerical tests on 10^{8} random instances in dimensions 2\!-\!5 and Kraus ranks 2\!-\!5 , supplemented by 40 independent simulated-annealing runs, revealed no numerically significant violation of \Delta_{\mathrm{KM}}\geqslant 0 , with the smallest value reaching zero within machine precision; see the SM for details [2]. We prove a weaker version of the conjecture for quantum-to-classical channels. For such channels, X and Y commute, and the KM update reduces to the Petz map.
Figure 2:
Hilbert–Schmidt gradient flow (black) vs. full-step KM iteration (purple). (a) Starting sufficiently close to the maximum leads to convergence. (b) When the unconstrained maximum lies outside the physical state space, the KM trajectory may approach a nearby boundary point. (c) Even when the maximum lies inside the physical state space, KM iterations starting outside its basin of attraction can converge to a nonoptimal boundary state. See Theorems 4 and 6.
Theorem 4 (Petz monotonicity for quantum-to-classical likelihood).
Consider the quantum-to-classical channel (3). For any state {\sigma} ,
the Petz update is given by,
\displaystyle\sigma_{+}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathcal{R}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}_{\text{qc}}}(X)=\sum\nolimits_{i}\,\frac{\hat{p}_{i}}{\Tr[\Pi_{i}\sigma]}\,\sqrt{\sigma}\;\Pi_{i}\,\sqrt{\sigma}.
(12)
Then the standard tomographic log-likelihood satisfies
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma_{+})\geqslant\mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma).
(13)
Equality holds iff \sigma is a fixed point of the Petz map, \sigma_{+}=\sigma .
Along with the Petz recovery map [36], the KM update is a possible generalization of Jeffrey’s rule to quantum physics. The above theorem and the previous conjecture imply that the application of this version of Jeffrey’s rule always increases the log-likelihood. This generalizes the classical result of Jacobs [46], see SM [2].
Note that replacing the KM update with the Petz recovery map in Conjecture 3 does not satisfy the desired inequality: since the Petz map is not the exact gradient of the log-likelihood (2), applying it at a maximizer for noncommuting outputs with overcomplete basis and imperfect data can decrease it. A concrete example exhibiting a large violation is provided in the SM [2].
KM update and Petz recovery as quantum tomography methods.
The following observation, which is a rephrasing of the discussion below Eq. (23) combined with concavity of the log-likelihood (see SM [2]), provides the basis for the KM update as a tomography method.
Theorem 5.
Every full-rank fixed point \sigma of the KM update is a maximizer of the log-likelihood (MLE).
Theorem 2 guarantees that a line-search implementation can choose a step that increases the log-likelihood whenever the current state is not stationary. By Theorem 5, any full-rank fixed point reached by the iteration is a maximizer.
For the full step \eta=1 in the Petz case, we prove monotonicity and subsequential fixed-point convergence; under uniqueness of the relevant fixed point, the whole sequence converges. We conjecture that the analogous full-step statement extends to the KM update.
Theorem 6 (Convergence of the Petz iteration).
Suppose that \{\Pi_{i}\} is a tomographically complete measurement with all observed probabilities \hat{p}_{i} positive. We denote the unique maximizer of the log-likelihood \mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}} as \hat{\sigma}_{\text{MLE}} , and the iterated Petz update as \sigma_{k}=(\sigma_{k-1})_{+} , for some initial prior \sigma_{0} . Then \sigma_{k}\to\hat{\sigma}_{\text{MLE}} or \det(\sigma_k)\to 0 . If the upper level set \{\sigma:\mathcal{L}(\sigma)\geqslant\mathcal{L}(\sigma_{0})\} contains only invertible states, then \sigma_{k}\to\hat{\sigma}_{\text{MLE}}.
While the above results apply to a single measurement, we extend them to any number of measurements by mapping them onto a single effective POVM. This yields a general method for quantum tomography as follows. Consider a set \{\mathcal{E}_{j}\} of channels with corresponding estimated outputs X_{j} . Define the weighted log-likelihood as
\sum\nolimits_{j}w_{j}\mathcal{L}_{X_{j}}(\sigma),\quad\mathcal{L}_{X_{j}}(\sigma)=\Tr\bigl[X_{j}\ln\mathcal{E}_{j}(\sigma)\bigr],
(14)
which generalizes the standard maximum-likelihood formulation. The weights \{w_{j}\} represent the relative importance of each dataset and, in analogy with the usual log-likelihood, are typically chosen as empirical frequencies w_{j}=N_{j}/N of channel executions yielding data X_{j} .
Due to the linearity of the Fréchet derivative, we can define the weighted KM update as
\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,\{\mathcal{E}_{j}\}}(\{X_{j}\})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum\nolimits_{j}w_{j}\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}_{j}}(X_{j}),
(15)
which corresponds to the gradient of the weighted log-likelihood in the inverse-square-root metric (19). Equivalently, this is the ordinary KM update for the block channel \mathcal{E}_{\oplus}=\bigoplus_{j}w_{j}\mathcal{E}_{j} with data X_{\oplus}=\bigoplus_{j}w_{j}X_{j} , up to an irrelevant additive constant in the likelihood, see SM [2].
Figure 3: Left: Maximum-likelihood quantum tomography based on iterative application of the weighted Petz map over Pauli bases with full 3^{6} measurement settings, with the prior initialized as the maximally mixed state. Ten Wishart-random [112] six-qubit density matrices of various ranks were sampled and regularized to make them full-rank, {\rho}=(1-\epsilon){\rho}+\epsilon\,I/d , \epsilon=10^{-2} , to put the initial prior into the basin of attraction (see Fig. 2). Diluted \mathrm{R}\rho\mathrm{R} shows comparable performance while being slightly faster, with a KM-to-diluted \mathrm{R}\rho\mathrm{R} wall-clock time ratio of 1.23\pm 0.13 . Right: Multi-view KM reconstruction of a global four-qubit state consistent with six supplied two-qubit reduced density matrices. Ten test states were sampled as in the previous example and regularized with \epsilon=0.1 . Both examples operate with exact probabilities.
The channel set may be incomplete, overcomplete, or informationally complete. Quantum tomography is performed by iterating the KM update from an initial prior {\sigma}_{0} . The prior and weights influence the trajectory and its limit, which matters especially for noisy or incomplete data. Figure 2 illustrates both convergence to an MLE and approach to a nonoptimal boundary state, which can occur even for compatible data. In the overcomplete case, the weights also encode the relative importance of different channels, prioritizing those with more reliable data.
We illustrate the method in Fig. 3 on the standard tomography setup using Pauli measurements on six qubits and on the following noncommutative KM example with incomplete information, inspired by local quantum tomography [102]: Consider an experimenter who performed full tomography on every pair of qubits of a four-qubit system, determining the six reduced density matrices, X_{AB},X_{AC},X_{AD},X_{BC},X_{BD},X_{CD} ,
This corresponds to the six quantum channels,
\mathcal{E}_{AB}(\rho)=\Tr_{CD}[\rho],\ \dots
(16)
We seek which global state \sigma best reproduces the available local data. Since the six reduced density matrices do not uniquely determine the global state, we infer a single \sigma by iterating the KM update from the maximally mixed prior. We quantify convergence by the average quantum relative entropy
\frac{1}{6}\sum_{ij}D\!\left(X_{ij}\middle\|\mathcal{E}_{ij}(\sigma)\right) ,
which vanishes when all six reconstructed marginals reproduce the measured reduced states. Because the two-qubit reductions overlap, X_{ij} and \mathcal{E}_{ij}(\sigma) generally do not commute during the iteration, providing a genuinely noncommutative application of the KM update. An additional thermodynamic example combining measured and inferred data is given in the SM.
Discussion and conclusions.
In this paper, we studied the exact gradient of the log-likelihood functional and found that it leads to an update rule that we call the KM update. Together with the Petz recovery map and the rotated Petz recovery map, the KM update may be viewed as a candidate quantum analogue of Bayes’ and Jeffrey’s rule. All three maps reduce to the classical Bayes update in the commuting case. Unlike the Petz map, which arises naturally in the characterization of equality in the data-processing inequality [84, 88], or the rotated Petz map, which strengthens this connection through fidelity-based recovery bounds [28, 101], the KM update arises from the maximum-likelihood inference problem, namely the task of finding the reference state whose predicted output best explains the observed data.
Specifically, if closeness is quantified by the Umegaki quantum relative entropy (a choice supported by its uniqueness under four natural axioms [108]), then the KM update is its gradient with respect to the reference state and therefore follows the direction of steepest descent. Conjecture 3 and Theorem 4 further suggest that it monotonically decreases the relative entropy, extending Jacobs’ monotonicity result for Jeffrey’s rule and the Kullback–Leibler divergence [46]. In contrast, the Petz recovery map violates the analogous monotonicity property.
Since the KM update points in the direction of the likelihoods’ maximal increase, it satisfies local monotonicity, which guarantees that the limit exists. Theorems 2 and 5 establish sufficient conditions for this limit to be the MLE. In the commuting-output setting, the KM iteration reduces to the Petz iteration, for which we establish the stronger properties of full-step monotonicity and convergence to the maximum-likelihood estimator in Theorems 4 and 6.
This allows us to design and implement a tomographic protocol that is a first-order gradient-ascent method, applicable both to standard quantum tomography via the quantum-to-classical channel and to a multi-view reconstruction using arbitrary quantum channels. This is realized by formulating the weighted KM update (15), which enables us to treat incompatible measurements as well as undercomplete, informationally complete, overcomplete, and imperfect data within a unified framework.
For quantum-to-classical channels, the Petz update becomes \sigma_{+}=\sqrt{\sigma}R\sqrt{\sigma} , where R=\sum_{i}\frac{\hat{p}_{i}}{\Tr[\Pi_{i}\sigma]}\Pi_{i} , and can be compared with the standard and diluted R\rho R iterations, \sigma_{+}^{R\rho R}=\mathcal{N}(R\sigma R) and \sigma_{+}^{R\rho R(\eta)}=\mathcal{N}(R_{\eta}\sigma R_{\eta}) , with R_{\eta}=(R+\eta I)/(1+\eta) . For commuting R and \sigma , the methods reduce to \sigma_{+}=\sigma R and \sigma_{+}^{R\rho R}=\sigma R^{2}/\Tr[\sigma R^{2}] . Monotonicity guarantees the convergence of the Petz update, to a MLE if the limit point is full-rank, whereas R \rho R requires adaptive- \eta schemes for convergence guaranties [92, 38]. The Petz update may nevertheless converge to a non-MLE boundary point. Unlike R \rho R, the Petz update is a CPTP map on the evidence state. The Petz update has a slightly higher per-iteration computational cost due to the additional evaluation of \sqrt{\sigma} .
Another interesting comparison can be made with Bayesian quantum tomography, which applies the classical Bayes theorem to infer a posterior distribution over quantum states. A common point estimate is the Bayesian mean estimator,
\hat{\sigma}_{\text{BME}}=\int\sigma\,p(\sigma|\text{data})\,d\sigma,
obtained by averaging over the posterior distribution [13, 45, 54, 39, 97, 66, 20]. In contrast to the present approach, this integral is generally not available in closed form and is typically approximated using methods such as sequential Monte Carlo or Metropolis–Hastings sampling [99].
Here, we find the KM update to generalize Bayes’ and Jeffrey’s rules, while being formulated entirely in terms of quantum states rather than probability distributions. Starting from the search for a fully quantum analogue of Bayesian inference, we therefore arrived at a somewhat surprising conclusion: the resulting update naturally leads to maximum-likelihood estimation. This raises the intriguing possibility that maximum-likelihood estimation is, in fact, the natural form of Bayesian inference in quantum theory.
The remaining open problems are to establish the global monotonicity of the undamped KM update, derive general convergence guaranties, characterize its limiting points even in undercomplete settings, and develop faster and higher-order tomographic protocols inspired by the present approach, which will be reported in a separate work [1].
Finally, the results presented here reveal a geometric structure underlying maximum-likelihood inference, arising from the interplay between Kubo–Mori geometry at the output and inverse-square-root geometry at the input, connecting quantum tomography, recovery maps, and quantum Bayesian retrodiction within a unified framework.

Log-likelihood gradient.
The KM superoperator and its inverse are defined as [53, 71, 83, 86, 87]
\begin{split}\Omega_{Y}(X)&=\int_{0}^{1}Y^{t}X\,Y^{1-t}\,dt,\\
\Omega_{Y}^{-1}\!(X)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\beta_{0}(t)\,Y^{-(1+it)/2}X\,Y^{-(1-it)/2}\,dt,\end{split}
(17)
where \beta_{0}(t)=\pi/[2(\cosh(\pi t)+1)] .
To understand how small changes in \sigma affect the log-likelihood functional (2), we consider its Fréchet derivative,
D\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma)[\Delta]=\Tr\!\big[X\,\Omega^{-1}_{Y}\!\big(\mathcal{E}(\Delta)\big)\big].
(18)
It can be interpreted as the directional derivative of \mathcal{L}_{X} at \sigma in the direction \Delta . The Fréchet derivative of the matrix logarithm at Y is the inverse KM action \Omega_{Y}^{-1} 11
1
Equivalently, if f(A)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\ln A , then the Fréchet derivative of f at Y is the linear map Df(Y)=\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\Omega^{-1}_{Y} , i.e., Df(Y)[X]=\Omega^{-1}_{Y}\!(X) [42]..
The gradient is the Riesz representation of the Fréchet derivative with respect to a chosen inner product [25]. We consider two inner products: the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product and, for a full-rank state \sigma , the inverse-square-root metric
\textsl{g}_{\sigma}(A,B)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\Tr\!\big[\sigma^{-1/2}A^{\dagger}\sigma^{-1/2}B\big],
(19)
which is a monotone metric corresponding to f=\sqrt{\cdot} [87] and a metric dual to the KMS metric [49, 105]. In the commuting case, the latter reduces to the classical Fisher metric \textsl{g}_{p}(a,b)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{i}\frac{a_{i}b_{i}}{p_{i}} . We choose this metric because the corresponding gradient defines a CPTP map related to the Petz map. Since \Omega^{-1}_{Y} is self-adjoint with respect to the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product, and \mathcal{E}^{\dagger} is the Hilbert–Schmidt adjoint of \mathcal{E} , we have
D\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma)[\Delta]=\Tr\left[{G_{\!X}}(\sigma)\,\Delta\right]=\textsl{g}_{\sigma}\!\,\big(G_{\!X}^{\sigma}(\sigma),\Delta\big),
(20)
where the two gradients with respect to \sigma for a fixed output operator X are defined as
{G_{\!X}}(\sigma)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\!\big[\Omega^{-1}_{\mathcal{E}(\sigma)}\!(X)\big],\quad G_{\!X}^{\sigma}(\sigma)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sqrt{\sigma}\,{G_{\!X}}(\sigma)\sqrt{\sigma}.
(21)
A full-rank stationary point of \mathcal{L}_{X} on the trace-one manifold, such as a maximizer of the log-likelihood, satisfies
D\mathcal{L}_{X}({\sigma})[\Delta]=0,\quad\text{ for all }\;\;\Delta=\Delta^{\dagger},\;\Tr[\Delta]=0.
(22)
Using (18), this is equivalent to
{G_{\!X}}({\sigma})=\lambda\mathds{1},
(23)
for some Lagrange multiplier \lambda\in\mathbb{R} . Thus (23) is the Euler–Lagrange equation for the trace-one constrained optimization problem. Using that both X and \sigma are density matrices implies \lambda=1 : \Tr[{G_{\!X}}({\sigma}){\sigma}]=\Tr[\Omega_{Y}^{-1}\!(X)Y]=\Tr[X] . If \sigma is full rank, then Eq. (23) is equivalent to G_{\!X}^{\sigma}(\sigma)=\sigma . This can be proven by multiplying the equation by \sqrt{\sigma} and \sigma^{-1/2} from both sides to get the right and left implications, respectively.
KM update as the direction of maximal log-likelihood increase.
Let us define the line-search update function
\sigma_{\eta}=\sigma+\eta\Delta,\qquad f(\eta)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma_{\eta}).
(24)
From the definition of the Fréchet derivative, we have
f^{\prime}(0)=D\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma)[\Delta]=\textsl{g}_{\sigma}\big(G_{\!X}^{\sigma}(\sigma)-\sigma,\Delta\big),
(25)
where \Tr[\Delta]=0 allows us to make the first argument tangent by adding a -\sigma term.
In the inverse-square-root metric (19), the steepest-ascent direction on the trace-one manifold is given by the Riemannian gradient,
\Delta=G_{\!X}^{\sigma}(\sigma)-\sigma.
(26)
Substituting this choice into (25) gives
f^{\prime}(0)=\textsl{g}_{\sigma}(\Delta,\Delta)=\|\Delta\|_{\textsl{g}_{\sigma}}^{2}\geqslant 0.
(27)
For full-rank \sigma , equality holds iff \Delta=0 , i.e., iff the stationary condition (23) is satisfied with \lambda=1 .
Using (26), the line search is given by
\sigma_{\eta}=(1-\eta)\,\sigma+\eta\,\sigma_{+},
(28)
where \sigma_{+}=G_{\!X}^{\sigma}(\sigma) denotes the updated density matrix corresponding to \eta=1 , in which case the update becomes purely multiplicative.
The prescription X\to\sigma_{+} defines a quantum channel,
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}}(X)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=G_{\!X}^{\sigma}(\sigma)=\sqrt{\sigma}\,\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\!\big[\Omega_{Y}^{-1}\!(X)\big]\sqrt{\sigma}.
(29)
We call this map the KM update.

Modular-frequency and integral forms of the inverse KM action
Let Y\!=\!\sum_{i}y_{i}\outerproduct{i}{i} , y_{i}\!>\!0 . Then each matrix unit \outerproduct{i}{j} is an eigenoperator of the modular generator K_{Y}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=[\ln Y,\cdot]
with eigenvalue
K_{Y}(\outerproduct{i}{j})=(\ln y_{i}-\ln y_{j})\outerproduct{i}{j}=\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\omega_{ij}\outerproduct{i}{j}.
(30)
In the same basis, the inverse KM action is diagonal,
\big(\Omega_{Y}^{-1}\!(X)\big)_{ij}=\frac{\ln y_{i}-\ln y_{j}}{y_{i}-y_{j}}\,X_{ij},
(31)
with the diagonal case understood by continuity. Since
y_{i}-y_{j}=2\,\sqrt{y_{i}y_{j}}\,\sinh(\omega_{ij}/2),
(32)
we obtain
\big(\Omega_{Y}^{-1}\!(X)\big)_{ij}=k(\omega_{ij})\,\frac{X_{ij}}{\sqrt{y_{i}y_{j}}},\quad k(\omega)=\frac{\omega}{2\sinh(\omega/2)}.
(33)
Equivalently,
\Omega_{Y}^{-1}\!(X)=k(K_{Y})\!\big[Y^{-1/2}XY^{-1/2}\big].
(34)
This proves the modular-frequency filter form in Theorem 1.
The same inverse KM action admits the integral representation
\Omega_{Y}^{-1}\!(X)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\beta_{0}(t)\,Y^{-(1+it)/2}X\,Y^{-(1-it)/2}\,dt,
(35)
where \beta_{0}(t)=\pi/[2(\cosh(\pi t)+1)]
is normalized such that
k(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\beta_{0}(t)e^{-it\omega/2}\,dt.
(36)
The same probability density \beta_{0} appears in Ref. [50] as the weight in the averaged rotated-Petz recovery map. Here, however, it represents the inverse KM operator itself rather than a CPTP average of recovery channels.
Properties of the KM update
1.
Linearity in \bm{X}
\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}}(aX_{1}+bX_{2})=a\,\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}}(X_{1})+b\,\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}}(X_{2}).
(37)
2.
Positivity
If X\geqslant 0 , then
\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}}(X)\geqslant 0,
(38)
because \Omega^{-1}_{Y} is positive, \mathcal{E}^{\dagger} is positive, and conjugation by \sqrt{{\sigma}} preserves positivity.
3.
Trace preservation in the argument
\displaystyle\Tr\!\big[\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}}(X)\big]
\displaystyle=\Tr\!\big[\sigma\,\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}[\Omega_{Y}^{-1}\!(X)]\big]=\Tr\!\big[\mathcal{E}(\sigma)\,\Omega_{Y}^{-1}\!(X)\big]
\displaystyle=\Tr\!\big[Y\,\Omega^{-1}_{Y}\!(X)\big]=\Tr[X].
(39)
In particular, if X is a density operator, then \mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{{\sigma},\mathcal{E}}(X) is again a density operator.
4.
Exact recovery of the reference output
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{{\sigma},\mathcal{E}}\big(\mathcal{E}({\sigma})\big)={\sigma},
(40)
since \Omega^{-1}_{Y}(Y)=\mathds{1} and \mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(\mathds{1})=\mathds{1} .
5.
Reduction to the Petz term in the commuting limit
If [X,Y]=0 , then
\displaystyle\Omega^{-1}_{Y}\!(X)=Y^{-1/2}XY^{-1/2},
(41)
and therefore
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{{\sigma},\mathcal{E}}(X)=\sqrt{\sigma}\;\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\!\big[Y^{-1/2}XY^{-1/2}\big]\,\sqrt{\sigma},
(42)
which is the ordinary Petz recovery form.
6.
Sum representation
The KM update can be written as the sum
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{{\sigma},\mathcal{E}}(X)={\sigma}+\Delta^{\text{KM}}_{{\sigma},\mathcal{E}}(X),
(43)
with \Delta^{\text{KM}}_{{\sigma},\mathcal{E}}(X)\equiv G_{\!X}^{\sigma}(\sigma)-\sigma , cf. Eqs. (26) and (29).
7.
Channel representation
Introducing
\displaystyle M_{\sigma}(A)
\displaystyle\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathcal{E}\big[\sqrt{\sigma}\,\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(A)\,\sqrt{\sigma}\big],
(44)
\displaystyle\Phi_{Y}(A)
\displaystyle\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=Y^{-1/2}\,M_{\sigma}(A)\;Y^{-1/2},
(45)
the predicted output after the update Y_{+}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathcal{E}[\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}}(X)] factorizes as
Y_{+}=M_{\sigma}[\Omega^{-1}_{Y}\!(X)]=\Phi_{Y,*}(\widehat{X}_{Y}),
(46)
where \Phi_{Y,*} denotes the Hilbert–Schmidt adjoint of \Phi_{Y} and \widehat{X}_{Y}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sqrt{Y}\,\Omega^{-1}_{Y}\!(X)\sqrt{Y} . Since \Phi_{Y} is unital CP, \Phi_{Y,*} is CPTP. Moreover, it preserves Y , \Phi_{Y,*}(Y)=Y . This is not the physical measurement channel itself, but an auxiliary CPTP map determined by Y , {\sigma} , and \mathcal{E} . The data processing inequality can be written in CPTP channel form as
D(Y_{+}\|Y)=D\big(\Phi_{Y,*}\big(\widehat{X}_{Y}\big)\big\|\Phi_{Y,*}(Y)\big)\leqslant D\big(\widehat{X}_{Y}\|Y\big).
(47)
Concavity of the log-likelihood and uniqueness of the maximizer
For fixed X\geqslant 0 , the map
\sigma\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma)=\Tr[X\ln\mathcal{E}(\sigma)]
(48)
is concave on the domain where \mathcal{E}(\sigma)>0 . Indeed, for 0\leqslant t\leqslant 1 ,
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{X}(t\sigma_{1}+(1-t)\sigma_{0})
\displaystyle=\Tr\!\big[X\ln\!\big(t\mathcal{E}(\sigma_{1})+(1-t)\mathcal{E}(\sigma_{0})\big)\big]
\displaystyle\geqslant t\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma_{1})+(1-t)\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma_{0}),
(49)
by operator concavity of the logarithm and positivity of the trace pairing with X . Consequently, for any two admissible states,
\mathcal{L}_{X}(\rho)\leqslant\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma)+D\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma)[\rho-\sigma].
(50)
In the quantum-to-classical case, write p_{i}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\hat{p}_{i} and set
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
\displaystyle\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\{\sigma\geqslant 0:\Tr[\sigma]=1\},
(51)
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{+}
\displaystyle\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}:\Tr[\Pi_{i}\sigma]>0\ \;\forall\,i\}.
(52)
On states in \mathcal{S}\!\setminus\!\mathcal{D}_{+} , the likelihood \mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}} is extended by setting \mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma)=-\infty .
Lemma 7.
Suppose \{\Pi_{i}\} is a finite informationally complete POVM with nonzero effects and p_{i}\!>\!0\;\forall\,i . Then \mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}} is strongly concave on \mathcal{D}_{+} and has a unique maximizer over the state space \mathcal{S} .
Proof.
Let \mathbb{H}_{0} denote the space of traceless Hermitian matrices and set
p_{\min}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\min_{i}p_{i},\quad c\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\!\!\min\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Delta\in\mathbb{H}_{0}\\
\|\Delta\|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{i}\;[\Tr(\Pi_i\Delta)]^{2}.
(53)
Since \{\Pi_{i}\} is informationally complete, the map \Delta\!\mapsto\!(\Tr[\Pi_{i}\Delta])_{i} is injective on \mathbb{H}_{0} . Hence c\!>\!0 , because the function being minimized is continuous and strictly positive on the compact unit sphere in \mathbb{H}_{0} . For \sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{+} and \Delta\in\mathbb{H}_{0} ,
\displaystyle\begin{aligned} -D^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma)[\Delta,\Delta]&=\sum_{i}\frac{p_{i}}{[\Tr(\Pi_i\sigma)]^{2}}[\Tr(\Pi_i\Delta)]^{2}\\
&\geqslant\sum_{i}\,p_{i}[\Tr(\Pi_i\Delta)]^{2}\\
&\geqslant p_{\min}\,c\,\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2},\end{aligned}
(54)
where we used \Tr[\Pi_{i}\sigma]\leqslant 1 due to 0\leqslant\Pi_{i}\leqslant\mathds{1} and \Tr[\sigma]=1 . Thus \mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}} is strongly concave on \mathcal{D}_{+} .
It remains to prove existence. Since each \Pi_{i} is a nonzero POVM effect, the maximally mixed state \mathds{1}/d belongs to \mathcal{D}_{+} , and thus \mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}}(\mathds{1}/d)\!>\!-\infty . With the boundary convention specified above, the map \sigma\!\mapsto\!\mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma) is upper semicontinuous on \mathcal{S} , i.e.,
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\;\mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma_{n})\leqslant\mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma)
(55)
whenever \sigma_{n}\!\in\!\mathcal{S} and \sigma_{n}\!\to\!\sigma\!\in\!\mathcal{S} . Since \mathcal{S} is compact, \mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}} attains a maximum on \mathcal{S} . Moreover, since the maximum value is at least \mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}}(\mathds{1}/d)\!>\!-\infty , any maximizer must lie in \mathcal{D}_{+} .
Finally, if \sigma_{1} and \sigma_{2} were two distinct maximizers, then for 0<\theta<1 strong concavity would give
\mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}}\big((1-\theta)\sigma_{1}+\theta\sigma_{2}\big)>(1-\theta)\mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma_{1})+\theta\mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma_{2}),
(56)
contradicting maximality. Hence the maximizer is unique.
∎
Proof of Theorem 2
Lower bound.
Assume that Y>0 and Y_{\eta}>0 , such that the logarithms below are ordinary matrix logarithms. We have
\Tr[X\ln Y_{\eta}-X\ln Y]=D(X||Y)-D(X||Y_{\eta}),
(57)
where Y=\mathcal{E}(\sigma) and Y_{\eta}=\mathcal{E}(\sigma_{\eta}) . The quantum relative entropy, defined as [104, 74],
D(X||Y)=\Tr[X(\ln X-\ln Y)],
(58)
can be expressed through the following optimization identity [93]:
D(X||Y)=\sup_{H=H^{\dagger}}\big\{\!\Tr[XH]-\ln\Tr[e^{H+\ln Y}\big]\big\}.
(59)
This implies
\displaystyle\Tr[X\ln Y_{\eta}-X\ln Y]
\displaystyle=\sup_{H=H^{\dagger}}\big\{\!\Tr[XH]-\ln\Tr[e^{H+\ln Y}]\big\}
\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt-\sup_{\tilde{H}=\tilde{H}^{\dagger}}\big\{\!\Tr\!\big[X\tilde{H}\big]-\ln\Tr\!\big[e^{\tilde{H}+\ln Y_{\eta}}\big]\big\}
\displaystyle\hskip-34.1433pt\geqslant-\ln\Tr[e^{\ln X-\ln Y_{\eta}+\ln Y}]+\ln\Tr[e^{\ln X}]
\displaystyle\hskip-34.1433pt=-\ln\Tr[e^{\ln X-\ln Y_{\eta}+\ln Y}],
(60)
where we picked H=\tilde{H}=\ln X-\ln Y_{\eta} , which maximizes the second supremum and gives a lower bound on the first.
We use Lieb’s triple-matrix inequality [61, 93]: for any positive definite operators R,S,T>0 ,
\Tr\!\left[e^{\ln R-\ln S+\ln T}\right]\leqslant\Tr\!\left[R\,\Omega_{S}^{-1}\!(T)\right],
(61)
where
\Omega_{S}^{-1}\!(T)=\int_{0}^{\infty}(S+u\mathds{1})^{-1}\,T\,(S+u\mathds{1})^{-1}\,du.
(62)
Using the theorem, we thus have
\Tr[X\ln Y_{\eta}-X\ln Y]\geqslant-\ln\Tr[X\,\Omega_{Y_{\eta}}^{-1}\!(Y)].
(63)
Next, we derive,
\displaystyle 1-\Tr[X\,\Omega_{Y_{\eta}}^{-1}\!(Y)]
\displaystyle=\Tr[\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\big(\Omega_{Y_{\eta}}^{-1}\!(X)\big)(\sigma_{\eta}-\sigma)]
(64)
\displaystyle=\eta\Tr[\big[\mathcal{E}^\dagger\big(\Omega_{Y_\eta}^{-1}\!(X)\big) - \mathds{1}\big](\sigma_+ - \sigma)]
\displaystyle=\eta\Tr[\big[\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\!\big(\Omega_{Y_{\eta}}^{-1}\!(X)\big)-\mathds{1}\big]\big(\sqrt{\sigma}\,\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\!\big(\Omega_{Y}^{-1}\!(X)\big)\sqrt{\sigma}-\sigma\big)]
\displaystyle=\eta\textsl{g}_{\sigma}\Big(\sqrt{\sigma}\big[\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\!\big(\Omega_{Y_{\eta}}^{-1}\!(X)\big)-\mathds{1}\big]\sqrt{\sigma},\sqrt{\sigma}\big[\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\!\big(\Omega_{Y}^{-1}\!(X)\big)-\mathds{1}\big]\sqrt{\sigma}\Big)
\displaystyle=\eta\textsl{g}_{\sigma}(\Delta_{\sigma_{\eta}}^{\sigma},\Delta_{\sigma}^{\sigma}).
This provides the desired inequality,
\hskip-2.13394pt\Tr[X\ln Y_{\eta}-X\ln Y]\geqslant-\ln\!\big[1-\eta\,\textsl{g}_{\sigma}(\Delta_{\sigma_{\eta}}^{\sigma},\Delta)\big],
(65)
where we have set \Delta\equiv\Delta_{\sigma}^{\sigma} .
Using the inequality -\ln(1-x)\geqslant x , valid whenever x<1 , gives the looser inequality
\Tr[X\ln Y_{\eta}\!-\!X\ln Y]\geqslant 1\!-\!\Tr[X\,\Omega_{Y_{\eta}}^{-1}\!(Y)\big]\!=\!\eta\textsl{g}_{\sigma}(\Delta_{\sigma_{\eta}}^{\sigma},\Delta).
(66)
This can be independently derived using concavity of the log-likelihood (50), and extends the inequality to semidefinite X for which the explicit optimizer H=\ln X-\ln Y_{\eta} has to be interpreted by approximation.
Upper bound.
Using Eq. (50), we have
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma_{\eta})-\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma)
\displaystyle\leqslant D\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma)[\sigma_{\eta}-\sigma]
(67)
\displaystyle=\eta\,\Tr\!\left[\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\!\big(\Omega_{Y}^{-1}\!(X)\big)\,\Delta\right]
\displaystyle=\eta\,\Tr\!\left[\left(\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\!\big(\Omega_{Y}^{-1}\!(X)\big)-\mathds{1}\right)\Delta\right]
\displaystyle=\eta\,\textsl{g}_{\sigma}(\Delta,\Delta),
where Y=\mathcal{E}(\sigma) and \Delta=\sigma_{+}-\sigma=(\sigma_{\eta}-\sigma)/\eta .
Evidence for Conjecture 3
We tested the conjectured monotonicity of the KM update by evaluating
\Delta_{\mathrm{KM}}\equiv\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma_{+})-\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma)
(68)
for randomly generated states and quantum channels.
For the broad random search, the states X and \sigma were sampled independently from a regularized Hilbert–Schmidt ensemble,
\rho=\frac{AA^{\dagger}+\epsilon I}{\Tr[AA^{\dagger}+\epsilon I]},\qquad\epsilon=10^{-8},
(69)
where A is a complex Ginibre matrix. The channels were generated from
independent complex Ginibre Kraus operators L_{a} , normalized according to
K_{a}=L_{a}\left(\sum_{b}L_{b}^{\dagger}L_{b}\right)^{-1/2},
(70)
which guarantees \sum_{a}K_{a}^{\dagger}K_{a}=I . We tested dimensions d=2,\ldots,5 and Kraus ranks 2,\ldots,5 , for a total of 10^{8} independent random instances. No violation of \Delta_{\mathrm{KM}}\geq 0 was observed. The smallest values occurred predominantly for Kraus-rank-two channels.
Because uniform random sampling may fail to detect a small region in parameter space where \Delta_{\mathrm{KM}}<0 , we supplemented this test by a targeted optimization. Since the smallest margins in the random search occurred for Kraus-rank-two channels, we concentrated this search on d=2 and Kraus rank two. We first generated 10^{4} independent random triples (\sigma,X,\mathcal{E}) from the same regularized ensemble and selected the one with the smallest \Delta_{\mathrm{KM}} as the initial point. In this initial sample the minimum was
\Delta_{\mathrm{KM}}\simeq 1.05\times 10^{-4}.
(71)
Starting from this candidate, we performed a multistart simulated-annealing search directly minimizing \Delta_{\mathrm{KM}} . A trial move consisted of a stochastic perturbation of either \sigma , X , the Kraus operators, or two of these objects simultaneously, with respective probabilities 0.35 , 0.35 , 0.20 , and 0.10 . After every perturbation, the states were renormalized to density matrices and the Kraus operators were renormalized to satisfy the trace-preserving condition. During this stage the density-matrix parametrization was regularized by adding 10^{-10}I before normalization.
The perturbation amplitudes for \sigma , X , and the Kraus operators were all decreased geometrically from 5\times 10^{-2} to 5\times 10^{-3} over the course of each run. To combine local exploration with occasional larger moves, the perturbation amplitude was additionally multiplied by 0.3 , 1 , or 3 , chosen with probabilities 0.70 , 0.25 , and 0.05 , respectively.
Moves that decreased \Delta_{\mathrm{KM}} were always accepted. Moves that increased it by \delta were accepted with the simulated-annealing probability
p_{\mathrm{acc}}=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\delta}{T}\right),
(72)
where the effective temperature T was decreased geometrically from 10^{-2} to 10^{-5} . This allows a trajectory to leave a local minimum during the early part of the search. If no improvement of the best value was found for 5000 consecutive steps, the current point was restarted by a larger perturbation around the best point found so far.
We performed 40 independent simulated-annealing runs, each containing 2\times 10^{5} trial moves and using an independent random seed. The smallest value obtained over all 40 runs was
\Delta_{\mathrm{KM}}\simeq 3.10\times 10^{-10}.
(73)
Finally, we subjected the best annealing result to a zero-temperature local refinement. In this stage, only moves decreasing \Delta_{\mathrm{KM}} were accepted. The perturbation scales were decreased geometrically from 5\times 10^{-3} to 5\times 10^{-4} , and the refinement was run for 10^{5} steps. The relative probabilities of perturbing \sigma , X , the channel, or two quantities simultaneously were 0.40 , 0.40 , 0.15 , and 0.05 , respectively, with a restart after 10^{4} steps without improvement.
The local refinement reached
\Delta_{\mathrm{KM}}\simeq-2.22\times 10^{-16}.
(74)
This value is at the scale of double-precision machine accuracy and is therefore numerically indistinguishable from zero. The corresponding state was close to the boundary of state space, with smallest eigenvalue
approximately 1.9\times 10^{-10} , and was nearly a fixed point of the KM iteration, \sigma_{+}\simeq\sigma . We therefore interpret the residual negative value as floating-point roundoff rather than evidence of a violation.
For numerical stability, eigenvalues entering logarithms and inverse matrix functions were clipped below at 10^{-15} . The logarithmic mean appearing in \Omega^{-1} was evaluated with a relative near-degeneracy criterion of 100\,\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}} , where \epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}\simeq 2.22\times 10^{-16} . These numerical safeguards are distinct from the 10^{-8} and 10^{-10} state regularizations used in the random and annealing ensembles, respectively.
Thus, neither the broad search over 10^{8} random instances nor the targeted multistart optimization produced a numerically significant value \Delta_{\mathrm{KM}}<0 .
Proof of Theorem 5
We will prove that every full-rank fixed point \sigma of the KM update is a maximizer of the log-likelihood. Since \sigma is full rank, the fixed-point equation \mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}}(X)=\sigma is equivalent to {G_{\!X}}(\sigma)=\mathds{1} . Conversely, stationarity on the trace-one manifold gives {G_{\!X}}(\sigma)=\lambda\mathds{1} , and taking the trace against \sigma gives \lambda=\Tr[X]=1 . Thus being a full-rank fixed point is equivalent to being a stationary point of the log-likelihood. In other words, as we have shown in the End Matter, the following statements are equivalent,
\displaystyle\begin{aligned} D\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma)[\Delta^{\prime}]=0\quad\forall\;\Delta^{\prime}=\Delta^{\prime\dagger},\;\Tr[\Delta^{\prime}]=0\\
\Longleftrightarrow\,{G_{\!X}}(\sigma)=\mathds{1}\,\Longleftrightarrow\,\Delta=0\,\Longleftrightarrow\,\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}}(X)=\sigma.\end{aligned}
(75)
This follows from Eqs. (26) and (29). Using concavity of the log-likelihood (50), for any other state \sigma^{\prime} , we have
\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma^{\prime})\leqslant\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma)+D\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma)[\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma]=\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma),
(76)
where we selected \Delta^{\prime}=\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma for the first stationarity condition. Thus, the log-likelihood of any other state is upper-bounded by that at the stationary point; hence every full-rank stationary point is a maximizer of the log-likelihood.

Lemma 8.
If D is a diagonal matrix with diagonal entries d_{1},\dots,d_{m}\geqslant 0 , then \sum_{i}d_{i}q_{i}\leqslant\rho(DC) .

Lemma 9.
Let A be a square matrix such that A_{ij}\geqslant 0 , and let x be a vector such that x_{i}>0 . If Ax\leqslant\lambda x with \lambda>0 , then \rho(A)\leqslant\lambda . In particular, if Ax=\lambda x , then \rho(A)=\lambda .
Proof.

Lemma 10 (Jacobs-type inequality for the quantum-to-classical Petz update).
\Tr[{G_{\!X}}(\sigma_{+})\,\sigma]\leqslant 1 .
Proof.
The proof adapts the matrix argument behind Jacobs’ Proposition 2 on the classical Jeffrey-rule contraction [46] to the quantum-to-classical Petz update. The new ingredient is the Gram matrix (78) built from the noncommuting POVM elements and the current state \sigma .
Let D be the diagonal matrix defined by the entries
d_{i}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\begin{cases}p_{i}/[(Cp)_{i}],&p_{i}>0,\\
0,&p_{i}=0.\end{cases}
(86)

(Cp)_{i}\geqslant C_{ii}p_{i}=\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\left\|\Pi_{i}^{1/2}\sqrt{\sigma}\,\Pi_{i}^{1/2}\right\|^{2}>0,
(87)
and we have
\sum_{j:p_{j}>0}d_{i}C_{ij}p_{j}=d_{i}\sum_{j}C_{ij}p_{j}=d_{i}(Cp)_{i}=p_{i}.
(88)
Equivalently, the submatrix of DC obtained by retaining the rows and columns with p_{i}\!>\!0 satisfies

(89)

DC=\begin{pmatrix}(DC)_{p_{i}>0,p_{j}>0}&(DC)_{p_{i}>0,p_{j}=0}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},
(90)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i:p_{i}>0}\frac{p_{i}q_{i}}{\Tr[\Pi_{i}\sigma_{+}]}

Weighted channel data as a single block channel
A weighted likelihood for several channels can be written as a single-channel likelihood on a direct-sum output space. Define
\mathcal{E}_{\oplus}(\sigma)=\bigoplus_{j}w_{j}\,\mathcal{E}_{j}(\sigma),\quad X_{\oplus}=\bigoplus_{j}w_{j}\,X_{j},\quad\sum_{j}w_{j}=1,
(106)
and w_{j}\geqslant 0 .
Then, up to the constant \sum_{j}w_{j}\Tr[X_{j}]\ln w_{j} ,
\Tr[X_{\oplus}\ln\mathcal{E}_{\oplus}(\sigma)]=\sum_{j}w_{j}\Tr[X_{j}\ln\mathcal{E}_{j}(\sigma)].
(107)
Moreover, since \Omega^{-1}_{w_{j}Y_{j}}(w_{j}X_{j})=\Omega^{-1}_{Y_{j}}(X_{j}) , the KM update for \mathcal{E}_{\oplus} is exactly
\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}_{\oplus}}(X_{\oplus})=\sum_{j}w_{j}\,\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}_{j}}(X_{j}).
(108)
Example: quantum-to-classical channel.
For \mathcal{E}_{j}(\sigma)=\sum_{i}\Tr[\Pi_{i}^{(j)}\sigma\big]\outerproduct{j,i}{j,i} , the block channel \mathcal{E}_{\oplus} is the quantum-to-classical channel associated with the single POVM \{w_{j}\Pi_{i}^{(j)}\}_{i,j} and the block data
X_{\oplus}=\sum_{j,i}w_{j}\hat{p}_{i}^{(j)}\outerproduct{j,i}{j,i}.
(109)
Thus the weighted Petz update is the ordinary Petz update for this unified POVM, and the weighted KM identity above reduces to the same construction in the commuting case.
Classical and quantum Bayes’ theorem and monotonicity violation by the Petz map
Let X and Y be random variables taking values from finite sets \mathcal{X} and \mathcal{Y} respectively. We view the random variable X as representing the state of a classical system, while Y is the result of an observation on the system. The observation can be modeled as a “forward” process \mathcal{E} corresponding to the stochastic matrix with entries given by the conditional probabilities p(y|x) for x\!\in\!\mathcal{X} and y\!\in\!\mathcal{Y} . Given a prior distribution \sigma(x) representing an agent’s belief about X , we apply the forwards process to obtain the distribution
\mathcal{E}(\sigma)(y)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p(y|x)\,\sigma(x).
(110)
If an observation y is made, Bayes’ rule prescribes the following update to the prior:
\sigma_{y}(x)=\frac{p(y|x)\,\sigma(x)}{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\,p(y|x)\,\sigma(x)}.
(111)
Jeffrey’s rule extends Bayes’ to situations with “soft evidence”, where the observation is a distribution \tau(y) . In this case, the updated state is
\sigma_{+}(x)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\sigma_{y}(x)\,\tau(y).
(112)
The expression above has the form \sigma_{+}=\mathcal{R}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}}(\tau) , where \mathcal{R}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}} is the Bayesian reverse process determined by the prior \sigma and the forward channel \mathcal{E} . Bayes’ rule (111) is recovered when \tau is a delta distribution. Jeffrey’s rule follows from Jeffrey’s probability kinematics [48], is equivalent to Pearl’s virtual-evidence update [82, 19], and can also be derived from a minimum-change principle [36].
Viewing quantum mechanics as the noncommutative generalization of classical probability theory, one naturally asks for a quantum Bayes rule: a CPTP map \mathcal{R} extending (112) to quantum states and channels. Several inequivalent proposals exist [81, 100, 79, 36], reflecting the fact that no single quantum extension has the same universal status as classical Bayes’ rule. In the relative-entropy setting, the canonical object is the Petz recovery map (6), which characterizes equality in the data-processing inequality [84, 85, 88]
D(\mathcal{E}(\rho)\|\mathcal{E}(\sigma))\leqslant D(\rho\|\sigma).
(113)
For faithful \sigma and compatible supports, equality holds precisely when \rho is recovered from its channel output by the Petz map, \mathcal{R}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{E}(\rho))=\rho .
Jacobs [46] showed that Jeffrey’s update moves the predicted observation toward the soft evidence in a precise relative-entropy sense: for the Kullback–Leibler divergence [55]
D_{\text{KL}}(p\|q)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{z}p(z)[\ln p(z)-\ln q(z)],
(114)
one has
D_{\text{KL}}(\tau\|\mathcal{E}(\sigma_{+}))\leqslant D_{\text{KL}}(\tau\|\mathcal{E}(\sigma)),
(115)
where \tau , \mathcal{E}(\sigma_{+}) , and \mathcal{E}(\sigma) are classical distributions.
Theorem 4 in the main text generalizes the Petz–Jeffrey monotonicity statement for quantum-to-classical channels (3), whose outputs are mutually commuting, even though their quantum inputs need not be. This Theorem can be rewritten as,
D\!\left(X\,\middle\|\,\mathcal{E}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma_{+})\right)\leqslant D\!\left(X\,\middle\|\,\mathcal{E}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma)\right),
(116)
with equality iff \sigma_{+}=\sigma , and where \sigma_{+}=\mathcal{R}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}_{\text{qc}}}(X) .
However, as mentioned in the main text, the Petz recovery map does not satisfy such a monotonicity theorem in general. The obstruction is the noncommutative output differential: when [\mathcal{E}(\sigma),X]\neq 0 , one generally has
\Omega_{\mathcal{E}(\sigma)}^{-1}\!(X)\neq\mathcal{E}(\sigma)^{-1/2}X\,\mathcal{E}(\sigma)^{-1/2}.
(117)
A concrete example which exhibits violation of this inequality is
\displaystyle\sigma
\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{2}{3}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{3}\end{pmatrix}\!,\ X=\begin{pmatrix}0.99&0\\
0&0.01\end{pmatrix}\!,\ \mathcal{E}(\sigma)=\sum_{j=1}^{2}K_{j}\sigma K_{j}^{\dagger},
\displaystyle K_{1}
\displaystyle\!=\!\begin{pmatrix}-0.251899\!+\!0.447484\,i&\;\;0.284823\!-\!0.031868\,i\\
0.308714\!-\!0.635360\,i&-0.388139\!+\!0.067281\,i\end{pmatrix}\!,
\displaystyle K_{2}
\displaystyle\!=\!\begin{pmatrix}-0.215046\!+\!0.189193\,i&-0.500736\!-\!0.114805\,i\\
0.279643\!-\!0.277632\,i&0.696647\!+\!0.115962\,i\end{pmatrix}\!.
(118)
For the Petz recovery map \mathcal{R}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}} , we obtain
D\!\left(X\,\middle\|\,\mathcal{E}(\sigma)\right)-D\!\left(X\,\middle\|\,\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}}(X))\right)=-2.022036,
(119)
while for the KM update \mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}} , we have
D\!\left(X\,\middle\|\,\mathcal{E}(\sigma)\right)-D\!\left(X\,\middle\|\,\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,\mathcal{E}}(X))\right)=0.069089.
(120)
Notice that in this case X and Y=\mathcal{E}(\sigma) are highly non-commuting; the Frobenius norm is equal to \norm{[X,Y]}_{F}=0.659206 , which is close to the maximum given by 1/\sqrt{2} . This example was found numerically using random instances further refined by simulated annealing.
This is consistent with Theorem 1 of Ref. [36], where the Petz map is recovered as the solution of the minimum-change problem in the output-commuting case [\mathcal{E}(\sigma),X]=0 . The corresponding noncommutative likelihood-gradient correction is the inverse KM action derived in the End Matter.
Expressions for the quantum-to-classical channel
The quantum-to-classical channel is defined as
{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma)=\sum_{i}\Tr[\Pi_{i}\sigma]\outerproduct{i}{i}.
(121)
Using \Pi_{i}=K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{i} , we can rewrite it in terms of its Kraus decomposition as
{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma)=\sum_{i,j}\outerproduct{i}{j}K_{i}\sigma K_{i}^{\dagger}\outerproduct{j}{i},
(122)
with adjoint
{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{qc}}^{\dagger}(Z)=\sum_{i,j}K_{i}^{\dagger}\outerproduct{j}{i}Z\outerproduct{i}{j}K_{i}.
(123)
The inverse KM superoperator is
\Omega_{{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma)}^{-1}\!(X)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\beta_{0}(t)\,{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma)^{-(1+it)/2}X\,{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma)^{-(1-it)/2}\,dt.
(124)
If X is diagonal in the \{\ket{i}\} basis, e.g., because it has been produced by the quantum-to-classical channel, then X=\sum_{i}\hat{p}_{i}\outerproduct{i}{i} , {\mathcal{E}}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma) and X commute, and
\Omega_{{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma)}^{-1}\!(X)={\mathcal{E}}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma)^{-1/2}X\,{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma)^{-1/2}=\sum_{i}\frac{\hat{p}_{i}}{\Tr[\Pi_{i}\sigma]}\outerproduct{i}{i}.
(125)
This means that the Hilbert-Schmidt gradient is
\displaystyle{G_{\!X}}(\sigma)
\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\!\big[\Omega_{{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma)}^{-1}\!(X)\big]=\sum_{i,j}K_{i}^{\dagger}\ket{j}\frac{\hat{p}_{i}}{\Tr[\Pi_{i}\sigma]}\bra{j}K_{i}
\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\frac{\hat{p}_{i}}{\Tr[\Pi_{i}\sigma]}\Pi_{i},
(126)
and the gradient in the inverse square-root metric (19) is
G_{\!X}^{\sigma}(\sigma)=\sqrt{\sigma}\,{G_{\!X}}(\sigma)\sqrt{\sigma}=\sum_{i}\frac{\hat{p}_{i}}{\Tr[\Pi_{i}\sigma]}\sqrt{\sigma}\,\Pi_{i}\sqrt{\sigma}.
(127)
This leads to the Riemannian gradient,
\Delta=G_{\!X}^{\sigma}(\sigma)-\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma}\Big(\sum_{i}\frac{\hat{p}_{i}}{\Tr[\Pi_{i}\sigma]}\Pi_{i}-I\Big)\sqrt{\sigma}.
(128)
If X={\mathcal{E}}_{\text{qc}}(\rho) has been computed directly as an output of the channel for a state \rho , then \hat{p}_{i}\equiv p_{i}=\Tr[\Pi_{i}\rho] , and the KM update applied to {\mathcal{E}}_{\text{qc}}(\rho) equals the Petz recovery map,
\mathcal{R}^{\text{KM}}_{\sigma,{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{qc}}}({\mathcal{E}}_{\text{qc}}(\rho))=\mathcal{R}_{\sigma,{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{qc}}}({\mathcal{E}}_{\text{qc}}(\rho))=\sum_{i}\frac{p_{i}}{\Tr[\Pi_{i}\sigma]}\sqrt{\sigma}\,\Pi_{i}\sqrt{\sigma}.
(129)
Inserting the quantum-to-classical channel and X into the generalized log-likelihood, we obtain
\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma)&=\Tr\!\big[X\ln{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{qc}}(\sigma)\big]\\
&=\Tr\!\bigg[\sum_{i}\hat{p}_{i}\outerproduct{i}{i}\ln\Big(\sum_i \Tr[\Pi_i \sigma] \ketbra{i}{i}\Big)\bigg]\\
&=\sum_{i}\hat{p}_{i}\ln\Tr[\Pi_{i}\sigma].\end{split}
(130)
This is the standard log-likelihood.
For a line search update
\sigma_{\eta}=\sigma+\eta\,\Delta,\qquad f(\eta)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma_{\eta}),
(131)
the quantum-to-classical channel gives
\displaystyle\begin{aligned} f^{\prime}(0)&=\textsl{g}_{\sigma}(\Delta,\Delta)=\|\Delta\|_{\textsl{g}_{\sigma}}^{2}\\
&=\Tr[\left(\bigg(\sum_{i}\frac{\hat{p}_{i}}{\Tr[\Pi_{i}\sigma]}\Pi_{i}-I\bigg)\sqrt{\sigma}\right)^{2}].\end{aligned}
(132)
This means an approximate increase in the log-likelihood when performing a line search update,
\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma_{\eta})\approx\mathcal{L}_{X}(\sigma)+\eta\,f^{\prime}(0).
(133)
Thermodynamically-motivated KM update example
Figure 4: KM reconstruction for a qubit undergoing thermal Lindblad evolution for t=0.5\,\gamma_{\downarrow}^{-1} , with \beta\omega=1 and \gamma_{\uparrow}/\gamma_{\downarrow}=e^{-\beta\omega} . Only \langle\sigma_{x}\rangle and \langle\sigma_{y}\rangle of the evolved state are assumed to be measured, while the missing diagonal is inferred from the mean energy fixed by the bath temperature, \Tr[HX]=\Tr[H\tau_{\beta}] . The KM iteration starts from the thermal state \tau_{\beta} and reconstructs the initial state. Curves show the infidelity with the analytic inverse for ten random initial states with the same mean energy and different coherences. The color indicates their purity.
We consider a qubit with Hamiltonian
H=\omega|1\rangle\langle 1|
(134)
coupled to a thermal bath at inverse temperature \beta . Its dynamics is described by the master equation [15],
\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]+\gamma_{\downarrow}\mathcal{D}[\sigma_{-}]\rho+\gamma_{\uparrow}\mathcal{D}[\sigma_{+}]\rho,
(135)
with detailed balance
\frac{\gamma_{\uparrow}}{\gamma_{\downarrow}}=e^{-\beta\omega}.
(136)
The corresponding stationary state is the Gibbs state
\tau_{\beta}=\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{\Tr[e^{-\beta H}]}=\begin{pmatrix}1-p_{\beta}&0\\
0&p_{\beta}\end{pmatrix},\qquad p_{\beta}=\frac{1}{1+e^{\beta\omega}}.
(137)
We generate initial states with the same mean energy as the bath,
\Tr[H\rho]=\Tr[H\tau_{\beta}]=\omega p_{\beta},
(138)
but with different coherences, and evolve them for a fixed time t under the channel \mathcal{E}_{t}=e^{t\mathcal{L}} . At the output we assume that only \langle\sigma_{x}\rangle and \langle\sigma_{y}\rangle are measured, determining the off-diagonal matrix element of the state X . The missing diagonal is completed using the known bath temperature through the constraint
\Tr[HX]=\omega p_{\beta}.
(139)
Thus
X=\begin{pmatrix}1-p_{\beta}&(x-iy)/2\\
(x+iy)/2&p_{\beta}\end{pmatrix},
(140)
where x=\langle\sigma_{x}\rangle and y=\langle\sigma_{y}\rangle .
We then infer the initial state by iterating the KM update for the channel \mathcal{E}_{t} , see Fig. 4, starting from the thermal prior
\sigma_{0}=\tau_{\beta}.
(141)
Since X generally contains coherence while \mathcal{E}_{t}(\tau_{\beta})=\tau_{\beta} is diagonal, [X,\mathcal{E}_{t}(\sigma_{0})]\neq 0 , so the iteration is genuinely non-commutative. The reconstructed state is compared with the analytic inverse of the thermal channel using the fidelity.
