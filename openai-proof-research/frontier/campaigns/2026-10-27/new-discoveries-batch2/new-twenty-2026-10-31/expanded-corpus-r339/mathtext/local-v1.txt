Abstract.
A graph is locally chordal if, locally at each vertex, it is chordal, i.e. composed of cliques glued along a tree.
In addition to extending many classic characterizations of chordal graphs, we show that locally chordal graphs are precisely those graphs G which can be decomposed into cliques arranged in the shape of a high-girth graph. The decomposition in the shape of a high-girth graph simultaneously witnesses that G is, locally at each vertex, composed of cliques glued along a tree. We also show that these decompositions can be computed efficiently. Our analysis of locally chordal graphs is inspired by a recent framework to study local vs. global structure in graphs [DJKK22].
Key words and phrases: Locally chordal, chordal, graph-decomposition, local separator, clique, local covering

1. Introduction
Consider a graph G which is obtained by gluing cliques in the shape of a high-girth graph H in such a way that, locally at each vertex, the graph G looks as if it were composed of cliques glued together along a tree.
An illustration is given in Figure 1.
Graphs that are composed of cliques glued together along a tree are the well-known chordal11
1
Chordal graphs are also referred to as rigid circuit [D61] or triangulated [R70] graphs. graphs.
The global composition of the graph G along H described above ensures by definition that locally G is chordal.
Figure 1. A locally chordal graph G , depicted in black, obtained by gluing cliques in the shape of H , depicted in blue.
In this context, one of the greatest hopes is that the converse from local to global also holds: Does a locally chordal graph G admit a global decomposition which simultaneously witnesses that G is locally chordal everywhere?
Although locally at each vertex v the graph G by definition consists of cliques glued along a tree T^{v} , it is generally not at all obvious that the local descriptions over the trees T^{v} agree in such a way that they can be combined into a common description over a common graph H .
What is surprising, however, is that our main result confirms the local-to-global hope in the affirmative.
Furthermore, our results are the first to make use of a new general theory designed to facilitate local-to-global analysis by constructing global decompositions from the local structure [DJKK22]. Our results therefore provide a possible roadmap for using the framework to study local-to-global structures more generally.
To state our results more precisely, we give some definitions. A graph is chordal if each of its cycles of length at least four has a chord. For an integer r\geqslant 0 , the given degree of locality, a graph is r -chordal if it each of its cycles of length at least four but at most r has a chord, and a graph is r -locally chordal if each of its balls of radius r/2 is chordal.22
2
For a vertex v of a graph G , we define the ball of radius r/2 around v , denoted B_{G}(v,r/2) or, for short, B_{r/2}(v) , as the subgraph of G given by the set of all vertices of distance at most r/2 from v and the set of all edges of G for which the sum of the endpoints’ distances to v is strictly less than r , see Section 2.2 for a comparison to the depth- d closed neighborhood.
Our main result not only shows that r -locally chordal graphs G admit a global decomposition which simultaneously witnesses that G is r -locally chordal, but indeed admit many different characterizations in terms of forbidden induced subgraphs, separators and intersection graphs.
Characterization.
Let G be a locally finite graph and r\geqslant 3 an integer. The following are equivalent.
(i)
G is r -locally chordal.
(ii)
G is r -chordal and wheel-free (Theorem 1).
(iii)
Every minimal r -local separator of G is a clique of G (Theorem 9).
(iv)
G is an r -acyclic region intersection graph (Theorem 4).
(v)
G admits a graph-decomposition into cliques which trivially induces r -local separations of G (Theorem 7).
Next, we describe in more detail our characterizations of locally chordal graphs and also their relation to chordal graphs.
1.1. When the degree of locality goes to infinity
Since trees are chordal and every ball of radius r/2 for r\leqslant 2 is a tree (specifically, a star), it follows that every graph is 0 -, 1 -, and 2 -locally chordal. From the definition, every graph which is r -locally chordal is r^{\prime} -locally chordal for all 0\leqslant r^{\prime}\leqslant r . Moreover, the graphs which are r -locally chordal for every r\in\mathbb{N} are exactly the chordal graphs. Therefore, the sequence of r -locally chordal graphs for r\in\mathbb{N} is an inclusion-wise decreasing sequence of graph classes, starting from the class of all graphs and ending with the class of chordal graphs.
When r goes to infinity, our characterization of locally chordal graphs indeed recovers the well-known characterizations
of chordal graphs [D61, G74, HL18].
Well-known characterizations of chordal graphs.
Let G be a locally finite graph. The following are equivalent:
(1)
G is chordal.
(2)
G has no induced cycle of length at least four.
(3)
Every minimal separator of G is a clique of G .
(4)
G is a subtree intersection graph.
(5)
G admits a tree-decomposition into cliques.
Chordal graphs are widely studied and have many theoretical and algorithmic applications.
One reason for this is that chordal graphs have so many different characterizations.
A disadvantage of this strong structure is that only few graphs are chordal graphs.
Although locally chordal graphs are a much larger class of graphs, for a given r\geqslant 0 they range from all graphs to wheel-free graphs to chordal graphs,
our characterization ensures that these classes still retain many of the properties that make chordal graphs so nice.
1.2. Characterization via induced subgraphs
On first glance it might seem that r -chordal implies r -locally chordal but each wheel with a rim of length at least 4 is not chordal but contained in a 3/2 -ball around its hub and thus not r -locally chordal for r\geqslant 3 .
Formally, a wheel W_{n} for n\geqslant 4 consists of a cycle of length n and an additional vertex complete to the cycle. A graph is wheel-free if it does not contain a wheel W_{n} for n\geqslant 4 as an induced subgraph.
Surprisingly, characterization Item (ii) states that induced wheels are the only obstruction.
When r goes to infinity, r -chordal yields that every cycle of length at least four has a chord, so characterization Item (ii) recovers Item (2).
1.3. Characterization via coverings
Recently, Diestel, Jacobs, Knappe and Kurkofka [DJKK22] introduced a new framework to capture the local structure of a graph.
Given a (finite) graph G and an parameter r\geqslant 0 , they construct a (possibly infinite) graph G_{r} , called the r -local cover of G , that represents the local structure of G . The construction of G_{r} is based on the simple heuristic that short cycles, of length at most r , are local, and that the “local structure” of a graph consists of exactly the structure “generated” by the local cycles. This is made precise using the notion of covering maps from topology. The graph G_{r} is equipped with a surjective homomorphism p\colon G_{r}\mapsto G , its covering map, which satisfies the property that it preserves r/2 -balls, i.e. B_{G_{r}}(\hat{v},r/2)) is isomorphic to B_{G}(v,r/2) for every vertex v of \hat{G} and v=p(\hat{v}) of G . The r -local cover G_{r} can be defined as the cover which is universal among those whose covering is r/2 -ball-preserving. See Section 2.3 for a precise definition of the local cover.
In the local cover perspective, if the r -local cover G_{r} of a graph G is chordal, then the r -local structure of G is chordal. Since G_{r} preserves r/2 -balls, every r/2 -ball of G appears as an r/2 -ball of G_{r} . Since r/2 -balls of chordal graphs are chordal (Lemma 2.2), every graph whose r -local cover G_{r} is chordal is r -locally chordal. It turns out that the converse is also true: for every r\in\mathbb{N} , a graph G is r -locally chordal if and only if G_{r} is chordal (Theorem 1).
The fact that r -locally chordal graphs are precisely the graphs whose r -local covers are chordal allows us to make use of local covers to study locally chordal graphs. Local covers are used to prove several of our results: specifically, that r -locally chordal graphs are precisely those graphs that are r -chordal and wheel-free (Theorem 1) and to prove all our Characterization Item (iv) and Item (v) not only for finite but also locally finite graphs. Moreover, the framework introduced in [DJKK22, JKK25]also yields for every integer r\geqslant 0 a canonical graph-decomposition \mathcal{H}_{r}(G) of G (see Section 14, and Section 13, for a recap). We prove that when r -locally chordal graphs are precisely those for which the decomposition \mathcal{H}_{r}(G) is into cliques (Theorem 14).
Moreover, \mathcal{H}_{r}(G) is used in the construction of Item (iv) and Item (v) (Proof of Theorem 5). Through the r -local cover perspective, our results are connected to recent works on local structure, such as [DJKK22, JKK25].
1.4. Characterization via local separators
Local components at arbitrarily sized vertex-subset X of a graph G were introduced in [JKK25] as a local analog to componental cuts, i.e. the sets of edges between a component of G-X and X .
Based on this notion, they introduced (tight) r -local separators, a local analog of separators.
When r goes to infinity, these are precisely the usual (tight) separators.
Unfortunately, tight local separators of locally chordal graphs are not in general cliques (Example 9.1).
This is due to the fact that, in contrast to the usual separators, tight local separators are not necessarily minimal local separators, which we introduce as a local analogue to minimal separators in this paper.
When r goes to infinity, minimal r -local separators are precisely the usual minimal (equivalently: tight) separators.
Following this, when r goes to infinity, Characterization Item (iii) yields Dirac’s characterization of chordal graphs as those graphs whose minimal separators are cliques [D61, Theorem 1].
1.5. Characterization via region representations
Let’s next discuss the characterization Item (iv) in some detail. A region of a graph H is a connected subgraph of H .
An assignment v\mapsto H_{v} of the vertices v of a graph G to regions H_{v} of a graph H represents G when an edge uv is present in G if and only if H_{u} and H_{v} intersect.
A region representation v\mapsto H_{v} is r -acyclic if for every set X\subseteq V(G) of at most r vertices, the union \bigcup_{x\in X}H_{x} is an acyclic subgraph of H .
In this context, we then call G an r -acyclic region intersection graph.
Every graph is a 2-acyclic region intersection graph; see Example 4.4 for a proof. But it’s already interesting to ask which graphs are 3-acyclic region intersection graphs. Wheels, and all graphs containing wheels as induced subgraphs, are not 3-acyclic region intersection graphs (Lemma 5.5), so 3-acyclic region intersection graphs must exclude wheels. In fact, Gavril [G96] characterized the graphs which are 3-acyclic region intersection graphs as precisely the wheel-free graphs (see Section 4.2).
Wheel-free graphs are also precisely the 3-locally chordal graphs. On the other end, \infty -acyclic region intersection graphs are equivalent to intersection graphs of subtrees of trees, which are well-known to be exactly the chordal graphs. Characterization Item (iv) now fills in the spectrum for all other values of r , giving a characterization of r -acyclic region intersection graphs for all r\in\mathbb{N} as exactly the r -locally chordal graphs.
1.6. Characterization via graph-decompositions
Let’s next turn to the Characterization Item (v). A graph-decomposition of a graph G , as introduced in [DK05, DJKK22], consists of a decomposition graph H and an assignment h\mapsto V_{h} of a vertex-subset V_{h} of G to each node h of H satisfying
conditions that formalize the idea that the graph-decomposition arranges the graph G into the shape of H . Specifically: (H1) the union of the subgraphs G[V_{h}] is G , and (H2) the co-part
H_{v} of each vertex v of G in H is connected, where the co-part of a vertex v is the subgraph H_{v} of H induced by the nodes h for which V_{h} contains v (Section 3.1).
A graph-decomposition is into cliques if each set V_{h} is a clique of G .
It turns out that graph-decompositions of a graph G into cliques precisely correspond to its region representations (Section 3).
More specifically, a graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) into cliques induces a region representation by assigning each vertex v of G its co-part H_{v} . On the other hand, a region representation v\mapsto H_{v} induces a graph-decomposition by assigning every node h of H the vertex-subset V_{h}\coloneqq\{v\in V(G)\mid h\in V(H_{v})\} .
In fact, r -acyclic region representations (equivalently: graph-decompositions into cliques) from Item (iv) can be equivalently described by those graph-decompositions which “trivially induce” r -local separations.
Local separations were introduced in [JKK25] simultaneously with local separators as a local analog to separations, which, when r goes to infinity, are precisely the usual separations.
Following this, when r goes to infinity, characterization Item (v) gives that chordal graphs are exactly the graph that admit tree-decomposition into cliques.
We can now talk about what kinds of graph-decompositions (H,\mathcal{V}) we obtain from characterization Item (v) (equivalently: Item (iv)) for r -locally chordal graphs G . Specifically, the r -acyclic condition from Item (iv) guarantees that the decomposition graph H has girth greater than r (Lemma 8.1).
This means that we can bound the number of edges of H by a subquadratic function in the vertices of H , |E(H)|\leqslant|V(H)|^{1+\frac{2}{r-2}} [B78, §III], so the decomposition graphs H are sparse. Moreover, the bound on the number of edges decreases with increasing r and approaches linear when r goes to infinity.
For r=\infty , the decomposition graphs H are precisely trees (more formally: acyclic graphs), since the girth approaches infinity.
Therefore, a qualitative way of describing characterization Item (v) is that r -locally chordal graphs admit “well-structured” decompositions into cliques modeled on “ r -sparse” graphs H .
Furthermore, we can choose the graph-decomposition from Item (v) into maximal cliques, i.e. the sets V_{h} are in one-to-one correspondence with the maximal cliques of G (Theorem 7 Item (ii)). There are at most |V(G)|^{2} maximal cliques in a finite wheel-free (equivalently: 3 -locally chordal) graph G (Section 12.1), which now bounds the number of vertices of the chosen decomposition graph H .
We conjecture that the number of maximal cliques in finite r -locally chordal graphs G approaches O(|V(G)|) as r goes to infinity (Conjecture 12.1).
1.7. Further results: Locally deriving tree-decompositions
Recall that we started with the idea of gluing cliques together in the shape of a high-girth graph such that locally at each vertex the graph looks like it is composed of cliques glued together along a tree. Correspondingly, the graphs we study in this paper, locally chordal graphs, are precisely those graphs which, locally around each vertex, admit tree-decompositions into cliques. It turns out that the graph-decompositions from Characterization Item (v) Item (iv) (equivalently: Item (v)) witness this property. Let G be an r -locally chordal graph and (H,\mathcal{V}) be an r -acyclic graph-decomposition of G as in Characterization Item (v) Item (iv). Now, roughly speaking, if we restrict the decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) to just a ball of radius r/2 in G , we obtain a tree-decomposition into cliques. Essentially, this allows us to start from a collection of |V(G)| tree-decompositions into cliques, one for each ball of radius r/2 of G , and find a single graph-decomposition of G which “puts them together” – in other words, which simultaneously witnesses each local tree-decomposition in a single structure. This property holds for every r -acyclic graph-decomposition of locally chordal graphs into cliques (Theorem 8).
1.8. Further results: Computing region representations
Given an r -locally chordal graph G on n vertices, we give an algorithm (Algorithm 11) to compute the r -acyclic region representation from Item (iv).
The algorithm is not only efficient but conceptually simple: for every vertex v of G , it runs Kruskal’s algorithm to compute a maximum-weight spanning tree of the clique intersection graph33
3
See Section 3.2 for a definition. of B_{3/2}(v) , and returns the union of the outputs.
The (parallel) running time is at most O(\Delta^{2}\log\Delta) on n processors, where \Delta is the maximum degree of G , and the algorithm does not take r as an input (Theorem 10).
In fact, given a wheel-free graph G , our algorithm returns an r -acyclic region representation for the maximum value of r such that G is r -locally chordal.
We remark that the r -acyclic region representations computed by Algorithm 11 correspond to graph-decompositions from Item (v) into maximal cliques. In the extremal case that the graph G is chordal, the computed region representation is over a tree recovering the familiar notion of clique trees. Clique trees are known to be precisely the maximum-weight spanning trees of the clique intersection graphs of chordal graphs. Similarly, we prove that r -acyclic region representations corresponding to graph-decompositions into maximal cliques are precisely maximum-weight r -acyclic spanning subgraphs of the clique intersection graphs of r -locally chordal graphs (Theorem 12).
Since r -acyclic region representations can be efficiently computed, they can be used in algorithms.
Clique trees are powerful algorithmic tools (see e.g. [WJ08]), and our algorithm to compute r -acyclic region representations of locally chordal graphs is an important step toward extending the algorithmic power of chordal graphs to locally chordal graphs.
We outline a specific potential application, to the maximum independent set problem, in Section 12.2.
1.9. Further results: New characterizations of chordal graphs
In the course of studying locally chordal graphs, we also obtained several new characterizations of chordal graphs.
First, we characterized chordal graphs as those graphs which are wheel-free and whose cycles are symmetric differences of generated by triangles (Corollary 2.7).
In fact, r -locally chordal graphs are precisely those wheel-free graphs whose cycles of length at most r are symmetric differences of triangles (Theorem 2).
Second, every locally finite connected chordal graph G admits a representation as subtree intersection graph which is canonical, i.e. which commutes with the automorphism of G (Theorem 3), which is a different kind of subtree representation of chordal graphs than the ones previously known (see Example 3.8).
In fact, every locally finite r -locally chordal graph G admits a canonical r -acyclic region representation (Theorem 5).
Third, we characterize chordal graphs as exactly those graphs G which are 3-acyclic region intersection graphs and such that every 3-acyclic region representation of G is in fact acyclic (Theorem 6.1).
In fact, r -locally chordal graphs are precisely those graphs G which are 3 -acyclic region intersection graphs and such that every 3 -acyclic region representation of G is r -acyclic (Theorem 6).
1.10. An application: Locally interval graphs are circular-arc graphs
With Characterization Item (iv) in hand, we can ask which graphs have r -acyclic region representations over cycles. The graphs with 1 -acyclic region representations over cycles form the well-known class of circular-arc graphs. By Item (iv), r -acyclic circular-arc graphs are a subclass of r -locally chordal graphs. Intuitively, zooming in around a vertex on a cycle yields a path, so we might guess that the chordal r/2 -balls in this case have not only tree-decompositions but even path-decompositions into cliques. The subclass of chordal graphs which admit path-decompositions into cliques are the well-studied interval graphs. A correspondence between circular-arc graphs and locally interval graphs seems natural. Indeed, Rzążewski conjectured in private communication that locally interval graphs are precisely circular-arc graphs (Conjecture 11.3).
A graph is r -locally interval if each of its r/2 -balls is interval. We prove that for r\geqslant 4 , the r -locally interval graphs are exactly the r -acyclic circular-arc graphs (Theorem 13). The proof relies on the characterization of 3 -acyclic circular arc graphs via forbidden induced subgraphs [CGS17] by Cao, Grippo and Safe. We also obtain a forbidden induced subgraph characterization of r -locally interval graphs for r\geqslant 3 (Proposition 11.5 and Theorem 13).

2.2. Chordal graphs are locally chordal.
Our first result of the section is the following.
Lemma 2.2.
Let G be a (possibly infinite) chordal graph and let r\geqslant 3 be an integer.
If G is chordal, then G is r -locally chordal.
We begin with some definitions.
Let G be a graph.
For a vertex v of G and an integer d\geqslant 0 , the depth- d closed neighborhood N_{G}^{d}[v] is the set of all vertices of G that have distance at most d to v , and the depth- d (open) neighborhood N_{G}^{d}(v) is the set of all vertices of G that have distance precisely d to v . Note that, if r=2d+1 , then B_{G}(v,r/2)=G[N^{d}[v]] .
Otherwise, if r=2d , then B_{G}(v,r/2) is obtained from G[N^{d}[v]] by deleting the edges between vertices of distance precisely r/2 to v .
Let X and Y be two vertex-subsets of G .
An X – Y path is a path such that only its first vertex is in X and only its last vertex is in Y .
A path P in G is through X if all internal vertices of P are in X and P has at least one internal vertex.
Lemma 2.3.
Let d=\lfloor r/2\rfloor with r\geqslant 0 an integer.
Let v be a vertex of a (possibly infinite) graph G such that B_{G}(v,r/2) is chordal.
Let u,w be two distinct vertices in N^{d-1}(v) .
If there is a u – w path in B_{G}(v,r/2) through N^{d}(v) , then uw is an edge of B_{G}(v,r/2) .
Proof.
For clarity in the proof, we remark that the assumption that N^{d-1}(v) contains two distinct vertices u,w yields d\geqslant 2 , i.e. r\geqslant 4 .
The assumption on u,w guarantees that there is a u – w path P through N^{d}(v) in B_{G}(v,r/2) .
Fix a vertex z of P in N^{d}(v) .
Note that N^{d-1}(v) is a v – z separator in G and thus also in B_{G}(v,r/2) .
Let S\subseteq N^{d-1}(v) be an inclusion-minimal v – z separator.
The u – w path P through N^{d}(v) together with S\subseteq N^{d-1}(v) yields that u,w\in S .
By Theorem 2.1, the minimal separator S of the chordal graph B_{G}(v,r/2) is a clique; in particular, uw is an edge of B_{G}(v,r/2) .
∎
Now we prove Lemma 2.2.
Proof of Lemma 2.2.
We want to show that every r/2 -ball of G is chordal. If r is odd,
then the r/2 -balls in G are induced subgraphs and thus chordal, as G is chordal.
So assume that r is even.
Let O be a cycle of length at least 4 in an r/2 -ball B around a vertex v in G .
Since r-1 is odd, the (r-1)/2 -ball around v in G is chordal.
Thus, O has a chord or contains a vertex z of distance r/2=:d to v .
If O contains a chord, then we are done, so we may assume that O contains a vertex z of distance d to v .
Let u,w be the two (distinct) neighbors of z on the cycle O .
Since r is even, it follows that N^{d}(v) is independent in B_{G}(v,r/2) , so every neighbor of z in B_{G}(v,r/2) is in N^{d-1}(v) . Now, u,w\in N^{d-1}(v) and uzw is a u – w path through N^{d}(v) , so by Lemma 2.3, uw\in E(G) . It follows that O has a chord. Since O and B were chosen arbitrarily, we conclude that G is r -locally chordal.

2.3. Background: local cover & cliques
Our major tool to study the local structure of graphs involves coverings from topology.
Let G be a graph.
A covering of G is a surjective homomorphism p\colon\hat{G}\to G such that p restricts to a bijection from the edges incident to any given vertex \hat{v} of \hat{G} to the edges incident to its projection v\coloneqq p_{r}(\hat{v}) .
In this paper, we restrict our view to those coverings p of graphs G whose preimage p^{-1}(C) of a component C of G is connected.
Let r\in\mathbb{N} .
A homomorphism p\colon\hat{G}\to G is r/2 -ball-preserving if p restricts to an isomorphism from B_{\hat{G}}(\hat{v},r/2) to B_{G}(v,r/2)) for every vertex \hat{v} of \hat{G} and v\coloneqq p_{r}(\hat{v}) .
Thus, a surjective homomorphism p\colon\hat{G}\to G is a covering of G if and only if p is 1 -ball preserving.
The r -local covering p_{r}\colon G_{r}\to G of G is the universal r/2 -ball-preserving covering of G , i.e. for every r/2 -ball-preserving covering p\colon\hat{G}\to G there exists a covering q\colon G_{r}\to\hat{G} such that p_{r}=p\circ q .
We refer to G_{r} as the r -local cover of G .
Note that the 0 -, 1 - and 2 -local covers of a connected, simple graph G are trees. For experts, we remark that the 0 -, 1 - and 2 -local coverings are all isomorphic to the universal covering of G .
A deck transformation of a covering p\colon\hat{G}\to G is an automorphism \gamma of \hat{G} that commutes with p , i.e. p=p\circ\gamma .
We denote the group of deck transformations of a covering p by \Gamma(p) .
Local covers of graphs interact well with cliques, as stated in the following lemma.
Let G be a graph.
Recall that a clique X in G is a vertex-set which induces a complete subgraph of G , and X is a maximal clique in G if it is inclusion-maximal among the cliques.
For a vertex v of a graph G , we denote the set of all maximal cliques in G containing v by K_{G}(v) .
Moreover, for a vertex-subset X of G , we define K_{G}(X) to be the set \bigcup_{x\in X}K_{G}(x) of all cliques meeting X .
For a set \mathcal{X} of sets, we denote \bigcup_{X\in\mathcal{X}}X by \bigcup\mathcal{X} .
Lemma 2.4 ([JKK25], Lemma 5.15).
Let G be a (possibly infinite) connected graph and r\geqslant 3 an integer.
Then:
(i)
For every clique \hat{X} of G_{r} , its projection p_{r}(\hat{X}) is a clique of G , and p_{r} restricts to a bijection from \hat{X} to p_{r}(\hat{X}) .
(ii)
For every clique X of G , there exists a clique \hat{X} of G_{r} such that p_{r} restricts to a bijection from \hat{X} to X .
(iii)
(\bigcup K_{G_{r}}(\hat{X}))\cap(\bigcup K_{G_{r}}(\gamma(\hat{X})))=\emptyset for every clique \hat{X} of G_{r} and every \gamma\in\Gamma(p_{r})\smallsetminus\{\text{id}_{G_{r}}\} .
For a given clique X of G , we refer to all cliques \hat{X} of G_{r} such that p_{r} restricts to a bijection from \hat{X} to X as lifts of X to G_{r} . Lemma 2.4 ensures that every clique of G_{r} is a lift of a clique in G , lifts of cliques of G exist, and two distinct lifts of a common clique are disjoint.
2.4. Binary cycle space of chordal graphs
Let \mathcal{E}(G) be the \mathbb{Z}_{2} -vector space whose elements are the edge-subsets of G and whose vector addition is the symmetric difference \triangle .
An element C of \mathcal{E}(G) is generated by a subset \mathscr{C} of \mathcal{E}(G) if there is \mathscr{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathscr{C} such that the symmetric difference of all the elements of \mathscr{C}^{\prime} is C .
If all elements of a subset \mathscr{D} of \mathcal{E}(G) are generated by a set \mathscr{C} , the set \mathscr{C} generates \mathscr{D} .
The binary cycle space \mathcal{Z}(G) of a graph G is the subspace of \mathcal{E}(G) that consists of all elements generated by the set of (the edge sets of) cycles in G .
Binary cycle spaces of local covers are already generated by the short cycles:
Lemma 2.5.
[DJKK22, Lemma 4.6]
The binary cycle space of the r -local cover G_{r} of a (possibly infinite) graph G for r\in\mathbb{N} is generated by its cycles of length \leqslant r .
The short cycles in r -chordal graphs are generated by triangles.
A triangle in a graph G is a cycle of length 3 (equivalently: clique of size 3 ) in G .
Lemma 2.6.
Let r and \ell be integers with 3\leqslant\ell\leqslant r . Every cycle O of length \ell in a (possibly infinite) r -chordal graph G is the symmetric difference of \ell-2 triangles of G whose vertex sets are included in V(O) .
Proof.
Let O be a cycle in G of length \ell .
We proceed by induction on \ell . If O has length three, then O is a triangle and so trivially the symmetric difference of |O|-2=1 triangles.
Now assume that O has length \ell>3 (and at most r ) and that the statement holds for all cycles of length less than \ell .
Since G is r -chordal, O has a chord e in G .
Thus, O+e contains two cycles O_{1} and O_{2} whose intersection is exactly e and whose union covers O .
In particular, O_{1} and O_{2} are both shorter than O , the sum |O_{1}|+|O_{2}| of their sizes is |O|+2 , and their symmetric difference O_{1}\triangle O_{2} is O .
Now, we may apply the inductive hypothesis to obtain sets \mathscr{C}_{1} of size |O_{1}|-2 and \mathscr{C}_{2} of size |O_{2}|-2 triangles whose respective symmetric differences are O_{1} and O_{2} , and whose vertex sets are included in the respective V(O_{i})\subseteq V(O) .
It follows that the symmetric difference of all triangles in \mathscr{C}\coloneqq\mathscr{C}_{1}\triangle\mathscr{C}_{2}=(\mathscr{C}_{1}\smallsetminus\mathscr{C}_{2})\cup(\mathscr{C}_{2}\smallsetminus\mathscr{C}_{1}) is O_{1}\triangle O_{2}=O and
|\mathscr{C}|=|\mathscr{C}_{1}|+|\mathscr{C}_{2}|=(|O_{1}|-2)+(|O_{2}|-2)=|O_{1}|+|O_{2}|-4=|O|-2=\ell-2,
where the first equality holds, since no triangle in \mathscr{C}_{1} is in \mathscr{C}_{2} and vice versa, as they are contained in their respective V(O_{i}) but O_{1} and O_{2} intersect only in the edge e .
Therefore, \mathscr{C} is the desired set of triangles for O .
∎
If a graph G is wheel-free, then the converse of Lemma 2.6 also holds.
Theorem 2.
Let G be a (possibly infinite) wheel-free graph and let r be a positive integer. Then G is r -chordal if and only if all its cycles of length at most r are generated by triangles.
Proof.
If G is r -chordal, then Lemma 2.6 ensures that all its cycles of length at most r are generated by triangles.
Conversely, assume that all cycles of G of length at most r are generated by triangles. Consider a cycle O in G of length at least 4 and at most r .
Since the cycles in G of length at most r are generated by triangles, there is a finite set of triangles whose symmetric difference is O .
Fix \mathscr{C} to be a smallest such set.
If all three vertices of some triangle in \mathscr{C} are in O , then O has a chord, as desired.
Suppose for a contradiction that this is not the case.
Let T be a triangle in \mathscr{C} .
By assumption, there is a vertex c of T that is not on O .
Let e_{1},\ldots,e_{q} be the edges of \bigcup_{C\in\mathscr{C}}C incident with c , with endpoints c and v_{1},\ldots,v_{q} , respectively. Let H be the subgraph of G with vertex set \{v_{1},\ldots,v_{q}\} and edge set consisting of edges e with both endpoints in \{v_{1},\ldots,v_{q}\} such that e is an edge of a triangle in \mathscr{C} containing c . Since the symmetric difference of the triangles in \mathscr{C} is exactly O and c is not in O , it follows that every edge e_{1},\ldots,e_{q} disappears in the symmetric difference of \mathscr{C} . Therefore, each edge e_{i} is contained in at least two triangles of \mathscr{C} . Since c is an end of each edge e_{i} , it follows that every triangle containing e_{i} is contained in the neighborhood of c , and in particular uses an edge from H . Therefore, H has minimum degree at least two, and so H contains a cycle C .
We may assume by reordering that C is the symmetric difference of the \ell triangles C_{i} formed by the edges e_{i},e_{i+1},v_{i}v_{i+1} , where e_{\ell+1}=e_{1} and v_{\ell+1}=v_{1} .
If G[V(C)] contains an induced cycle of length at least four, then G[V(C)\cup\{c\}] contains a wheel with hub c as an induced subgraph, a contradiction, so G[V(C)] is chordal.
Now, Lemma 2.6 gives a set \mathscr{C}^{*} of \ell-2 triangles in G[V(C)] whose symmetric difference is C , and \mathscr{C}^{\prime}\coloneqq(\mathscr{C}\smallsetminus\{C_{1},\dots,C_{\ell}\})\triangle(\mathscr{C}^{*}\cup\{C\}) contradicts the choice of \mathscr{C} , because \mathscr{C}^{\prime} is smaller than \mathscr{C} .
∎

A (possibly infinite) graph G is chordal if and only if G is wheel-free and its binary cycle space is generated by triangles. ∎

3. Equivalence: Graph-decompositions & region representations
In this section, we introduce our major tools to describe the structure of locally chordal graphs, graph-decompositions and region intersection graphs. We begin with several definitions.
3.1. Graph-decompositions & region intersection graphs
Let G be a graph.
A graph-decomposition of G is a pair \mathcal{H}=(H,\mathcal{G}) consisting of a graph H and a family \mathcal{G} of subgraphs G_{h} of G indexed by the nodes h of H which satisfies
(H1)
G=\bigcup_{h\in H}G_{h} , and
(H2)
H_{v}\coloneqq H[\{h\in V(H)\mid v\in V(G_{h})\}] is connected for every v\in V(G) .
We refer to H as the decomposition graph, and also say that (H,\mathcal{G}) is a decomposition over H .
The induced subgraphs G_{h} are the parts of \mathcal{H} .
The vertex-sets V_{h}\coloneqq V(G_{h}) are called the bags of \mathcal{H} .
A tree-decomposition (T,\mathcal{V}) is precisely a graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{G}) whose parts G_{h}\coloneqq G[V_{h}] are induced subgraphs of G and whose decomposition graph H is the tree T .
In this paper, we will always assume that the parts G_{h} of a graph-decomposition are subgraphs of G induced on their corresponding bags V_{h}=V(G_{h}) .
Thus, we introduce and refer to graph-decompositions by (H,\mathcal{V}) , where \mathcal{V}=(V_{h}\mid h\in V(H)) is the family of its bags.
Therefore, in this paper, a graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) is a tree-decomposition if and only if H is a tree.
We remark that a bag V_{h} of a graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of G is precisely \{v\in V(G)\mid h\in V(H_{v})\} .
Thus, one may equivalently axiomatise graph-decompositions as follows.
A graph-decomposition of G is a map that assigns to each vertex v of G a subgraph H_{v} of H such that
(H1V)
H_{v} is nonempty for every vertex v of G ,
(H1E)
H_{u}\cap H_{v} is nonempty for every edge uv of G , and
(H2)
H_{v} is connected for every vertex v of G .
The regions of a graph are its (nonempty) connected subgraphs.
In this language, a graph-decomposition over H assigns to each vertex v a region H_{v} of H such that H_{u}\cap H_{v}\neq\emptyset if uv\in E(G) . If the map also satisfies the converse of this condition, i.e. H_{u}\cap H_{v}\neq\emptyset if and only if uv\in E(G) , then we obtain a concept called region representations:
A region representation v\mapsto H_{v} of G over a graph H is a map that assigns to each vertex v of G a region H_{v} of H such that uv is an edge of G if and only if H_{u} and H_{v} have nonempty intersection. If such a region representation of G over H exists, then G is a region intersection graph over H .
For a vertex-subset X of G , we write H_{X} for the union \bigcup_{x\in X}H_{x} of the region corresponding to the elements of X .
Given a class \mathcal{H} of graphs, a graph G is a region intersection graph over \mathcal{H} if G is a region intersection graph over some H\in\mathcal{H} .
We remark that a region representation of G restricts to a region representation of each of its induced subgraphs.
Moreover, an induced subgraph G^{\prime} of G is connected if and only if the union H_{V(G^{\prime})} of its corresponding regions is connected.
A region representation v\mapsto T_{v} of G over a tree T is better known as a subtree representation of G over the tree T .
Similarly, if G has a subtree representation over a tree T , then G is a subtree intersection graph over T .
The following is now immediate from the definitions, as the converse of (H1E) holds if and only if every bag V_{h} is a clique.
A graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of G is into cliques if each bag V_{h} of (H,\mathcal{V}) is a clique of G .
Lemma 3.1.
Let G and H be (possibly infinite) graphs.
(i)
If v\mapsto H_{v} is a region representation of G over H , then (H,\mathcal{V}) with V_{h}\coloneqq\{v\in V(G)\mid h\in V(H_{v})\} is a graph-decomposition of G into cliques.
(ii)
If (H,\mathcal{V}) is a graph-decomposition of G into cliques, then v\mapsto H_{v}\coloneqq\{h\in V(H)\mid v\in V_{h}\} is a region representation of G over H . ∎
We remark that Lemma 3.1
yields a 1 -to- 1 correspondence between region representations of a graph and its graph-decompositions into cliques, which allows us to discuss the graph-decomposition corresponding to a given region representation and vice versa.
Let G be a graph, and let \Gamma be a subgroup of the group \Aut(G) of automorphisms of G .
A graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of G is \Gamma -canonical if there exists an action of \Gamma on H which commutes with h\mapsto V_{h} , i.e. \gamma(V_{h})=V_{\gamma\cdot h} .
Equivalently, considering the graph-decomposition presented as v\mapsto H_{v} , the graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) is \Gamma -canonical if v\mapsto H_{v} commutes with the action of \Gamma on H , i.e. \gamma(H_{v})=H_{\gamma(v)} .
Moreover, (H,\mathcal{V}) is canonical if it is \Aut(G) -canonical.
A region representation v\mapsto H_{v} of G over a graph H is \Gamma -canonical or canonical if its corresponding graph-decomposition is \Gamma -canonical or canonical, respectively.
3.2. Clique graphs & graph-decompositions into maximal cliques
Let G be a graph.
We denote the set of all maximal cliques of G by K(G) .
A graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of G is into maximal cliques if h\mapsto V_{h} is a bijection from the nodes of H to the set K(G) of maximal cliques of G .
The corresponding concept for region representations is clique graphs.
The clique intersection graph of a graph G is the graph \mathbf{K}(G) whose vertex set is K(G) and where two maximal cliques K_{1} and K_{2} of G are adjacent in \mathbf{K}(G) when K_{1}\cap K_{2}\neq\emptyset .
A clique graph of G is a spanning subgraph H of \mathbf{K}(G) such that v\mapsto H_{v}\coloneqq H[K_{G}(v)] is a region representation of G over H .
A clique tree66

The following is immediate from the definitions.
Lemma 3.2.
Let G be a (possibly infinite) graph. Suppose H is a spanning subgraph of \mathbf{K}(G) , and set H_{v}\coloneqq H[K_{G}(v)] for every vertex v of G .
Then K_{G}(v)=K(B_{3/2}(v)) for every vertex v of G .

For the curious reader, we remark that one may generalise K_{G}(v)=K(B_{3/2}(v)) to \bigcup_{v\in N^{d-1}[v]}K_{G}(v)=K(B_{r/2}(v)) for r -locally chordal graphs with r=2d+1 with d a positive integer.
A clique graph H of G is \Gamma -canonical for a subgroup \Gamma of \Aut(G) or canonical if its corresponding region representation of G is \Gamma -canonical or canonical, respectively.
The automorphisms \gamma of G induce an action on the maximal cliques, and thus on \mathbf{K}(G) , by K\mapsto\gamma(K) .
Note that a clique graph H is \Gamma -canonical if and only if H is \Gamma -invariant, i.e. \gamma(H)=H for every \gamma\in\Gamma .
The following is immediate from the definitions and Lemma 3.1.
Lemma 3.3.
Let G be a (possibly infinite) graph.
(i)
If H is a clique graph of G , then assigning to each node K of H (where K\in K(G) ) the bag V_{K}\coloneqq K yields a graph-decomposition of G into maximal cliques.
(ii)
If (H,\mathcal{V}) is a graph-decomposition of G into maximal cliques, then the map h\mapsto V_{h} embeds H onto a clique graph of G . ∎
We remark that Lemma 3.3 yields a 1 -to- 1 correspondence between clique graphs of a graph and its graph-decompositions (H,\mathcal{V}) into maximal cliques (up to the images of H under h\mapsto V_{h} in \mathbf{K}(G) ), which allows us to dicuss the graph-decomposition (into maximal cliques) corresponding to a given clique graph and vice versa.
3.3. Canonical graph-decompositions via coverings
We conclude this section by discussing the relationship between graph-decompositions, region representations, and local covers. The main result here is a construction from [DJKK22] that folds each given canonical tree-decompositions of the r -local cover G_{r} of a graph G to a graph-decompositions of the graph G .
Construction 3.4 ([DJKK22], Construction 3.8 and Lemma 3.9).
Let G be a (possibly infinite) connected graph and r\in\mathbb{N} .
Let (T,\hat{\mathcal{V}}) be a \Gamma -canonical tree-decomposition of G_{r} , where \Gamma\coloneqq\Gamma(p_{r}) is the group of deck transformations of p_{r} .
We define the graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) obtained from (T,\hat{\mathcal{V}}) by folding via p_{r} as follows.
The decomposition graph H is the orbit-graph of T under the action of \Gamma , i.e. H=T/\Gamma . The bag V_{h} corresponding to a node h of H is p_{r}(\hat{V}_{t}) for a (equivalently: every) t in the \Gamma -orbit h .
Consider the equivalent description of the tree-decomposition (T,\hat{\mathcal{V}}) as v\mapsto T_{v} .
Denote the quotient map from T to the orbit-graph H=T/\Gamma also by p_{r} .
Then the equivalent description of the graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) is given by v\mapsto H_{v}=p_{r}(T_{\hat{v}}) for a (equivalently: every) \hat{v}\in p_{r}^{-1}(v) .
It turns out that certain nice properties of tree-decompostions of G_{r} transfer to the graph-decompositions of G obtained by folding via p_{r} . First, canonical tree-decompositions yield canonical graph-decompositions:
Lemma 3.5 ([DJKK22], Lemma 3.12).
Let \mathcal{H} be a graph-decomposition of a (possibly infinite) graph G obtained from a \Gamma -canonical tree-decomposition \mathcal{T} of G_{r} by folding via p_{r} . Then \mathcal{H} is canonical if \mathcal{T} is canonical.
Next, tree-decompositions of G_{r} into cliques yield graph-decompositions of G into cliques:
Lemma 3.6.
Let G be a (possibly infinite) graph and r\geqslant 3 an integer.
Suppose that \mathcal{T} is
a \Gamma(p_{r}) -canonical tree-decomposition of G_{r} and let \mathcal{H} be the graph-decomposition \mathcal{H} obtained from \mathcal{T} by folding via p_{r} .
Then \mathcal{T} is into cliques if and only if \mathcal{H} is into cliques.
Equivalently,
the map v\mapsto T_{v} corresponding to the tree-decomposition \mathcal{T} is a subtree representation of G_{r} if and only if the map v\mapsto H_{v} corresponding to the graph-decomposition \mathcal{H} is a region representation of G .
In the context of Lemma 3.6, we now refer to the map v\mapsto H_{v} as the region representation of G obtained from the subtree representation v\mapsto T_{v} by folding via p_{r} .
Proof.
If the tree-decomposition \mathcal{T} is into cliques, then Lemma 2.4 (i) yields the graph-decomposition \mathcal{H}(G) is into cliques.
The converse follows from Lemma 2.4 (ii).
∎
Now, Lemma 3.6 suggests a proof outline to obtain nice graph-decompositions and region representations of locally chordal graphs. By Theorem 1, a graph G is r -locally chordal if and only if its r -local cover G_{r} is chordal. Therefore, if we could obtain a \Gamma -canonical tree-decomposition of G_{r} into cliques, then the graph-decomposition of G obtained by folding via p_{r} is also into cliques and corresponds to a region representation of G .
It turns out that we can obtain canonical tree-decompositions of locally finite chordal graphs into cliques:
Theorem 3.
Let G be a locally finite, connected chordal graph. Then G admits a canonical tree-decomposition into cliques (equivalently: G has a canonical subtree representation).
If we ask only for \Gamma(p_{r}) -canonical tree-decompositions rather than canonical tree-decompositions, we can even obtain such tree-decompositions into maximal cliques:
Theorem 3.7.
Let G be a connected, locally finite graph and p\colon\hat{G}\to G a normal covering.
If \hat{G} is chordal, then \hat{G} admits a \Gamma(p) -canonical tree-decomposition into maximal cliques (equivalently: \hat{G} has a \Gamma(p) -canonical clique tree).
The proofs of Theorem 3 and Theorem 3.7 are in Section 13. In Section 5, Theorem 3 and Theorem 3.7 are used to construct nice graph-decompositions of 3-locally-chordal graphs into cliques.
In general, it is not possible to find a tree-decomposition which is both canonical and into maximal cliques.
Example 3.8.
The stars K_{1,t} with t\geqslant 3 leaves are chordal graphs which do not admit a canonical tree-decomposition into maximal cliques (equivalently: not have a canonical clique tree).
Proof.
Consider the chordal graph K_{1,t} , the star with t leaves, for t\geqslant 3 . For every tree-decomposition (T,\mathcal{V}) of K_{1,t} into maximal cliques, the bags \mathcal{V} are exactly the set of edges of K_{1,t} . For any pair of edges e,f of K_{1,t} , there is an automorphism of G mapping e to f , so if (T,\mathcal{V}) is canonical, then T must be vertex-transitive. But a finite vertex-transitive tree has at most two vertices, since every finite tree with at least three vertices contains a vertex of degree one and a vertex of degree greater than one. Therefore, as t\geqslant 3 , the graph K_{1,t} does not admit a canonical tree-decomposition into maximal cliques.
∎
4. Overview: Region representations of (locally) chordal graphs
In this section, we further discuss why region intersection graphs (equivalently: graph-decompositions into cliques) are the right tools to describe the structure of locally chordal graphs. First, we review the classic characterizations of chordal graphs as those graphs which have subtree representations (equivalently: admit tree-decompositions into cliques). Then, we explain how these characterizations generalize to the locally chordal case.
It is well-known77
7
This statement is immediate from the first paragraph of the proof presented in [G74, Theorem 3]. that subtree intersection graphs are chordal.
Proposition 4.1 (Folklore).
Every subtree intersection graph, finite or infinite, is chordal.
Equivalently, every graph that admits a tree-decomposition into cliques is chordal.
For finite graphs, it is well-known that the converse holds as well, see e.g. Gavril [G74, Theorem 2 and 3]: the connected chordal graphs are precisely the subtree intersection graphs (equivalently: those graphs which admits a tree-decomposition into cliques), or even more strictly those graphs that have a clique tree (equivalently: admit a tree-decomposition into maximal cliques).
Hofer-Temmel and Lehner extended this result to locally finite graphs:
Theorem 4.2 (Hofer-Temmel & Lehner [HL18], Theorem 3).
Let G be a locally finite, connected graph.
Then G has a clique tree (equivalently: G admits tree-decomposition into maximal cliques).
We remark that one may have derived Theorem 4.2 already from Diestel’s more general forbidden simplicial minors characterization of (possibly infinite) countable subtree intersection graphs [D88, Theorem 1].
But, in contrast to Diestel, Hofer-Temmel and Lehner extended the techniques designed for finite graphs to locally finite graphs.
More generally, a region representation v\mapsto H_{v} of a graph G over a graph H is acyclic if H_{V(G)} is acyclic.
A clique graph H of G is acyclic if its corresponding region representation is acyclic (equivalently: H is acyclic).
Thus, the following is obtained from Proposition 4.1 and from applying Theorem 4.2 to the components of the graph G .
Theorem 4.3.
Let G be a locally finite (not necessarily connected) graph.
Then the following are equivalent:
(i)
G is chordal.
(ii)
G has an acyclic clique graph.
(iii)
G has an acyclic region representation. ∎
As cycles are finite, a region representation v\mapsto H_{v} of G over a graph H is acyclic if and only if only if for every finite vertex-subset X of G the union H_{X} is acyclic.
More generally, let H be a graph and let \mathbf{H} be a family of subgraphs of H .
Then, \mathbf{H} is r -acyclic for an integer r\geqslant 0 if every union of at most r elements in \mathbf{H} is acyclic.
A region representation v\mapsto H_{v} of a graph G over H is r -acyclic if (H_{v}\mid v\in V(G)) is r -acyclic.
A clique graph of G is r -acyclic
if its
corresponding region representation is r -acyclic.
In light of the notion r -acyclic, we may refer to acyclic region-representations, families of subgraphs or clique graphs as being \infty -acyclic.
Theorem 4.3 says that chordal graphs are exactly the graphs which have \infty -acyclic region representations. On the other end of the spectrum, we observe the following:
Example 4.4.
Every graph G has a 2 -acyclic region representation v\mapsto H_{v} over some graph H such that the bag V_{h}=\{v\in V(G)\mid h\in V(H_{v})\} is a clique of size at most 2 for every node h of H .
Proof.
Let G be a graph and let H be the graph formed from G by subdividing each edge e once by a vertex v_{e} .
Then assign each vertex v of G the star S_{v} in H formed by v together with v_{e} for all edges e incident to v in G .
Now v\mapsto H_{v} is a 2 -acyclic region representation of G .
It is immediate from the definition that V_{v_{e}}=\{u,v\} for every edge e=uv of G and V_{v}=\{v\} for every vertex v of G .
∎
Given Theorem 4.3 and Example 4.4, it is natural to ask whether there is a nice characterization of graphs which have r -acyclic region representations for general values of r . The class of graphs with r -acyclic region representations is (inclusion-wise) monotone decreasing as r goes to infinity, eventually approaching the class of chordal graphs. It turns out that r -locally chordal graphs are exactly the right characterization here.
Theorem 4.
Let G be a locally finite graph and r\geqslant 3 an integer.
Then the following are equivalent:
(i)
G is r -locally chordal.
(ii)
G has an r -acyclic clique graph.
(iii)
G has an r -acyclic canonical region representation.
(iv)
G has an r -acyclic region representation. ∎
4.1. Proof of Theorem 4
The proof of Theorem 4 involves two steps. First, we prove that Theorem 4 holds for r=3 :
Theorem 5.
Let G be a locally finite graph. The following are equivalent:

(ii)
G has a 3-acyclic clique graph.
(iii)
G has a 3-acyclic canonical region representation.
(iv)
G has a 3-acyclic region representation.

4.2. Comparison to Gavril’s work

To state his result, let us first introduce Helly.
Let H be a graph and let \mathbf{H} be a family of subgraphs of H . Then, \mathbf{H} is Helly if every finite set of pairwise intersecting elements of \mathbf{H} has nonempty intersection.
A region representation v\mapsto H_{v} of a graph G over H is Helly

if its corresponding region representation is.

Let G be a finite graph. The following are equivalent:
(i)

(ii)
G has a Helly, 2 -acyclic clique graph.
(iii)

We show in this section that Theorem 4.5 is the same as Theorem 4 (without (iii)) for r=3 . In particular, we prove that a region representation is 3 -acyclic if and only if it is Helly and 2 -acyclic.
Lemma 4.6.
Let \mathbf{H} be a family of regions of a (possibly infinite) graph H . Then \mathbf{H} is 3 -acylic if and only if \mathbf{H} is 2 -acyclic and Helly.
We remark that, by Lemma 4.6, the region representations in Theorem 4 are Helly, since they are in particular 3-acyclic.
To prove Lemma 4.6 not only for finite but also infinite graphs H , we first extend two statements of Gavril on finite graphs H to infinite graphs H .
Lemma 4.7 (Gavril [G96], Lemma 1 and Theorem 2).
Let \mathbf{H} be a 2-acyclic family of regions of a (possibly infinite) graph H .
(i)
If the trees in \mathbf{H} have an element in common, then the union of \mathbf{H} is a subtree of H .
(ii)
\mathbf{H} is Helly if and only if the intersection of every three pairwise intersecting elements of \mathbf{H} is nonempty.
Proof.
Gavril [G96, Lemma 1 and Theorem 2] proved Item (i) and Item (ii) for finite graph H , respectively.
In the following, we reduce the case that H is infinite to the case that H is finite.
Claim 4.7.1.
Item (i) holds for infinite H , as well.
Proof.
The union \bigcup\mathbf{H} is connected, as the elements of \mathbf{H} are connected and all share a node, say t .
Suppose for a contradiction that \bigcup\mathbf{H} contains a cycle C .
Then there is a finite subfamily \mathbf{H}^{*}=(H_{i}\mid i\in[n]) of \mathbf{H} such that C\subseteq\bigcup\mathbf{H}^{*} .
For each H_{i} in \mathbf{H}^{*} , we may choose a finite connected subgraph H^{\prime}_{i} of the connected graph H_{i} that contains t and the finite subgraph C\cap H_{i} of H_{i} .
Now, their union H^{\prime}\coloneqq\bigcup_{i\in[n]}H_{i}^{\prime} is a finite graph.
As H_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq H_{i} and \mathbf{H} is 2 -acyclic, the family \mathbf{H}^{\prime} consisting of the regions H_{i}^{\prime} of H^{\prime} is 2 -acyclic.
The choice of the H_{i}^{\prime} ensures that t is a common node of the H_{i}^{\prime} .
Applying Gavril’s Item (i) for finite graphs to the family \mathbf{H}^{\prime} of regions in the finite graph H^{\prime} yields that \bigcup\mathbf{H}^{\prime} is a tree.
This contradicts that the choice of the H_{i}^{\prime} yields that \bigcup\mathbf{H}^{\prime} contains the cycle C .
∎
Claim 4.7.2.
Item (ii) holds for infinite H , as well.
Proof.
Let \mathbf{H}^{*}=(H_{i}\mid i\in[n]) be a finite subfamily of pairwise intersecting elements of \mathbf{H} .
For each two H_{i},H_{j} in \mathbf{H}^{*} , fix a node h_{i,j}=h_{j,i} in their intersection H_{i}\cap H_{j} .
Now, for each H_{i} in \mathbf{H}^{*} , we may choose a finite connected subgraph H_{i}^{\prime} of the connected graph H that contains h_{i,j} for every H_{j} in \mathbf{H}^{*} , since \mathbf{H}^{*} is finite and H_{i} is connected.
Now, their union H^{\prime}\coloneqq\bigcup_{i\in[n]}H_{i}^{\prime} is a finite graph.
As H^{\prime}_{i}\subseteq H_{i} and \mathbf{H} is 2 -acyclic, the family \mathbf{H}^{\prime}\coloneqq(H_{i}^{\prime}\mid i\in[n]) is 2 -acyclic.
The choice of the H_{i}^{\prime} ensures that each two elements H_{i}^{\prime},H_{j}^{\prime} of \mathbf{H}^{\prime} still intersect in h_{i,j}=h_{j,i} .
Now, applying Gavril’s Item (ii) for finite graphs to the family of regions in the finite graph H^{\prime} yields that \bigcap\mathbf{H}^{\prime} is nonempty.
Since \bigcap\mathbf{H}^{\prime}\subseteq\bigcap\mathbf{H} , also \bigcap\mathbf{H} is nonempty.
∎
This completes the proof.

Additionally, we need the following lemma to deduce that a Helly and 2 -acyclic family is 3 -acyclic.
Lemma 4.8.
Let \mathbf{H} be a 2 -acyclic family of regions of a (possibly infinite) graph H .
If the union of three elements of \mathbf{H} contains a cycle, then each two of these regions intersect.
Proof.
Let T_{1},T_{2},T_{3} be three regions in \mathbf{H} whose union contains a cycle C .
Note that the T_{i} are trees, as \mathbf{H} is 1 -acyclic.
Moreover, since \mathbf{H} is 2 -acyclic, the cycle C contains an edge f_{i} of T_{i} which is not contained in any other T_{j} with j\neq i .
Consider the path P_{i} in C which contains f_{i} and such that the intersection of P_{i} with \bigcup_{j\neq i}T_{j} is exactly the ends of P_{i} ; in particular, P_{i}\subseteq T_{i} .
To ensure that T_{i} intersects both T_{j} with j\neq i , it suffices to show that the ends of P_{i} are not both in the same T_{j} for a j\neq i .
So suppose for a contradiction that the ends of P_{i} are contained in a common T_{j} for a j\neq i .
Consider the path Q_{i} in T_{j} joining the two ends of P_{i} .
Because f_{i} is not in T_{j} but in P_{i}\subseteq T_{i} , the paths Q_{i} and P_{i} are distinct.
As they join the same vertices, P_{i}\cup Q_{i}\subseteq T_{i}\cup T_{j} contains a cycle, which contradicts that \mathbf{H} is 2 -acyclic.
∎
Corollary 4.9.
Let v\mapsto H_{v} be a region representation of a graph G over a (possibly infinite) graph H .
Suppose that a vertex-subset X of G is of minimum size such that H_{X} contains a cycle.
If X has size at most 3 , then X is a clique of G .

The following well-known fact about trees will be frequently used in the rest of the paper. We include a proof for completeness.
Lemma 4.10.
Let T and T^{\prime} be two trees.
Then T\cup T^{\prime} is a tree if and only if T\cap T^{\prime} is a tree.
Proof.
Assume that T\cup T^{\prime} is a tree.
As T\cap T^{\prime}\subseteq T\cup T^{\prime} , it remains to show that T\cap T^{\prime} is connected.
For every two given nodes s,t of T\cap T^{\prime} , their unique s – t path P in T\cup T^{\prime} is the unique s – t path in T and also in T^{\prime} .
Hence, P is the unique s – t path in T\cap T^{\prime} . Thus, T\cap T^{\prime} is a tree.
Conversely, assume that T\cap T^{\prime} is a tree.
In particular, T\cap T^{\prime} is nonempty.
Thus, T\cup T^{\prime} is connected, as T and T^{\prime} are trees.
It remains to show that T\cup T^{\prime} is acyclic.
Suppose for a contradiction that T\cup T^{\prime} is not acyclic.
As T,T^{\prime} are trees, we may pick cycle C in T\cup T^{\prime} such that P\coloneqq T\cap C and Q\coloneqq T^{\prime}\cap C are paths.
But as the endvertices of P (equivalently: Q ) are in T\cap T^{\prime} , and thus there is a path R in the tree T\cap T^{\prime} joining them.
Now R is either distinct from P or from Q .
Hence R\cup P\subseteq T or R\cup Q\subseteq T^{\prime} contains a cycle, which is a contradiction to T,T^{\prime} being trees.
∎
We are now ready to prove Lemma 4.6.
Proof of Lemma 4.6.
Let \mathbf{H} be a family of regions of a graph H .
Claim 4.10.1.
If \mathbf{H} is Helly and 2 -acyclic, then \mathbf{H} is 3 -acyclic.
Proof.
Consider a three-element subset \mathbf{H}^{\prime} of \mathbf{H} .
Suppose for a contradiction that their union contains a cycle.
Note that \mathbf{H}^{\prime} is inclusion-minimal with this property, as \mathbf{H} is 2 -acyclic.
Hence, Lemma 4.8 yields that they are pairwise intersecting.
Thus, since \mathbf{H} is Helly, the trees in \mathbf{H}^{\prime} have an element in common. By Lemma 4.7 (i), it follows that the union of \mathbf{H}^{\prime} is a tree, and thus acyclic.
∎
Claim 4.10.2.
If \mathbf{H} is 3 -acyclic, then \mathbf{H} is Helly and 2 -acyclic.
Proof.
By Lemma 4.7 (ii), it suffices to consider three pairwise intersecting elements T_{1},T_{2},T_{3} of \mathbf{H} and show that their intersection is nonempty.
Since \mathbf{H} is in particular 2 -acyclic, T_{1}\cup T_{2} is acyclic.
So, since their intersection S\coloneqq T_{1}\cap T_{2} is nonempty, it is a tree by Lemma 4.10.
As we are done otherwise, we assume that T_{3} does not intersect S .
Fix x_{i}\in T_{i}\cap T_{3} for i=1,2 witnessing that T_{i} and T_{3} intersect.
In particular, x_{i}\notin S , and thus x_{i}\not\in T_{3-i} by assumption.
Let P be the path from x_{1} to x_{2} in T_{3} , and let Q be the path from x_{1} to x_{2} in T_{1}\cup T_{2} .
In particular, P and Q are distinct because P\subseteq T_{3} avoids S and, since x_{i}\in T_{i}\smallsetminus T_{3-i} for i=1,2 , the path Q\subseteq T_{1}\cup T_{2} meets T_{1}\cap T_{2}=S .
Now, P\cup Q\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{3}T_{i} contains a cycle, contradicting that \mathbf{H} is 3-acyclic. Therefore, \mathbf{H} is Helly.
∎
Therefore, being 3-acyclic is equivalent to being 2-acyclic and Helly.
∎

Before we are ready to prove Theorem 5, we need two lemmas regarding the interaction between cliques and local coverings. These are tools to help us project the structure from the r -local cover G_{r} down to G .
Lemma 5.1.
Let G be a (possibly infinite) connected graph and r\geqslant 3 an integer.
Let \hat{v}\mapsto T_{\hat{v}} be a \Gamma(p_{r}) -canonical subtree representation of G_{r} over a tree T and let v\mapsto H_{v} be the region representation of G over H=T/\Gamma(p_{r}) obtained from v\mapsto T_{v} by folding via p_{r} .
Then, for every clique X of G and lift \hat{X} of X to G_{r} , the map p_{r} restricts to an isomorphism from T_{\hat{X}} to H_{X} .
Proof.
Fix a clique X of G and a lift \hat{X} of X to G_{r} . Since H_{X}=\bigcup_{\hat{x}\in\hat{X}}p_{r}(T_{\hat{x}}) , the map p_{r} restricts to a surjection from T_{\hat{X}} to H_{X} . Next, we show that p_{r} restricts to an injection from T_{\hat{X}} to H_{X} . First, we need the following observation. Let t be a node of T and define V_{t}\coloneqq\{\hat{v}\in V(G_{r})\mid t\in V(T_{\hat{v}})\} . Since \hat{v}\mapsto T_{\hat{v}} is a subtree representation, it follows that V_{t} is a clique of G_{r} . Therefore, for every \hat{v}\in V_{t} , it holds that V_{t}\subseteq\bigcup K_{G_{r}}(\hat{v}) .
By the above observation, \bigcup_{t\in V(T_{\hat{x}})}V_{t}\subseteq\bigcup K_{G_{r}}(\hat{x}) for every \hat{x}\in\hat{X} , and thus \bigcup_{t\in V(T_{\hat{X}})}V_{t}\subseteq\bigcup K_{G_{r}}(\hat{X}) . Similarly, for all \gamma\in\Gamma(p_{r}) , it holds that \bigcup_{t\in V(T_{\gamma(\hat{X})})}V_{t}\subseteq\bigcup K_{G_{r}}(\gamma(\hat{X})) . By Lemma 2.4 Item (iii), \bigcup K_{G_{r}}(\hat{X}) is disjoint from \bigcup K_{G_{r}}(\gamma(\hat{X})) for all \gamma\in\Gamma(p_{r}) distinct from the identity. Therefore, T_{\hat{X}} is disjoint from T_{\gamma(\hat{X})} for all \gamma\in\Gamma(p_{r}) distinct from the identity. We conclude that p_{r}(T_{\hat{X}}) is isomorphic to T_{\hat{X}} , and so p_{r} restricts to an isomorphism from T_{\hat{X}} to H_{X} .

7. Graph-decompositions of locally chordal graphs
In view of our characterization of locally chordal graphs using region representations and the equivalence between region representations and graph-decompositions, we have already the tools to prove a characterization of locally chordal graphs in terms of their graph-decompositions. (iii)

(iv)

However, in the context of graph-decompositions, we would like to say something structurally stronger than the characterization in Theorem 7.1.
A well-known key property of a tree-decomposition (T,\mathcal{V}) of a graph G is that every edge f of the decomposition tree T induces a separation s^{f} of G whose separator is the adhesion set V_{f} corresponding to f , see e.g. [D24, Lemma 12.3.1]. Formally:
(T2’)
For every edge f=t_{1}t_{2} of T and for i=1,2 , set A^{f}_{i}\coloneqq\bigcup_{t\in V(T_{i})}V_{t} and T_{i} is the component of T-f which contains t_{i} . Then {\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}{{s^{f}}}}\coloneqq\{A^{f}_{1},A^{f}_{2}\} is a separation of G and its separator A^{f}_{1}\cap A^{f}_{2} is the adhesion set V_{f}=V_{t_{1}}\cap V_{t_{2}} corresponding to f .
A bond of a graph is an inclusion-minimal cut of the graph.
Note that the bonds of a tree are precisely the set consisting of single edges.
Analogous to Item (T2’), one may prove along the lines of the proof of [DJKK22, Lemma 3.4] that every bond c of the decomposition graph H of a graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of G induces a separation s^{c} of G :
(H2’)
For every bond c=\{A_{1},A_{2}\} of H and for i=1,2 , set A^{c}_{i}\coloneqq\bigcup_{h\in A_{i}}V_{h} .
Then {\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}{{s^{c}}}}\coloneqq\{A^{c}_{1},A^{c}_{2}\} is a separation of G and its separator A^{c}_{1}\cap A^{c}_{2} is precisely the union \bigcup_{f\in E_{H}(A_{1},A_{2})}V_{f} of the adhesion sets in the cut.
In view of Item (H2’), a graph-decomposition represents the structure of the underlying graph.
Indeed, one could show that an equivalent definition of graph-decomposition is that a pair (H,\mathcal{V}) is a graph-decomposition if it satisfies Item (H1) and Item (H2’).
Given a graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of a graph G , the edges of H do not satisfy a property similar to Item (T2’) in general; if the decomposition graph is not a tree, there is no reason to hope that already single edges of H correspond to separations of G .
However, they do sometimes correspond to a local analog of separations. A very recent paper [JKK25] investigates the relationship between graph-decompositions and local separations.
It turns out that, since the graph-decompositions in our context are very well-structured, we can also prove that they interact nicely with local separations. The goal of this section is to make this interaction precise. We will at the end of the section be able to prove with Theorem 7 that r -locally chordal graphs are precisely those graphs which admit graph-decompositions whose edges induce r -local separation in an obvious way.

7.3. Equivalence: Characterization Item (iv) and Characterization Item (v)
Our main tool to prove Theorem 7 is a theorem that proves an equivalence between r -acyclic region representations and graph-decompositions satisfying the properties we want.
Theorem 7.7.
Let G be a (possibly infinite) graph and r\geqslant 3 an integer.
A region representation v\mapsto H_{v} of G over a graph H is r -acyclic if and only if its corresponding graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of G into cliques trivially induces r -local separations.
Proof.
First, suppose that G has an r -acyclic region representation v\mapsto H_{v} over H . Let (H,\mathcal{V}) be the graph decomposition into cliques induced by v\mapsto H_{v} .
As v\mapsto H_{v} is r -acyclic for r\geqslant 2 , the induced graph-decomposition is 2 -acyclic, so Lemma 7.2 ensures that (H,\mathcal{V}) satisfies Item (H2T) and Item (H3).
It remains to show that every edge of H induces an r -local separation of G .
Let f=h_{0}h_{1} be an edge of H . We claim that f induces an r -local separation of G . Let \{E_{0},V_{f},E_{1}\} be the pre-separation of G induced by f .
Suppose for a contradiction that there is an r -local V_{f} -walk W=v_{0}e_{1}v_{1}\dots e_{\ell}v_{\ell} in G whose first edge e_{1} is in E_{0} and whose last edge e_{\ell} is in E_{1} .
We may choose W shortest among these.
Since (H,\mathcal{G}) is into cliques, V_{f} is a clique.
Hence, depending on whether v_{0} equals v_{\ell} or not, v_{0}v_{1}\dots v_{\ell} or v_{0}v_{1}\dots v_{\ell}v_{0} is a cycle C of length at most r in G because W is r -local.
We claim C is an induced cycle.
Indeed, whenever there is a chord e=v_{i}v_{j} of C with 0<i<j<\ell , then the walk v_{0}v_{1}\dots v_{i}v_{j}v_{j+1}\dots v_{\ell} is shorter r -local V_{f} -walk, which contradicts the choice of W .
By symmetry, it remains to consider a chord e=v_{0}v_{j} with j=2,\dots,\ell-1 .
As W is an V_{f} -walk, e\in\partial V_{f} , and thus there is j\in\{0,1\} with e\in E_{j} .
If j=1 , then v_{0}v_{j}v_{j+1}\dots v_{\ell} contradicts the choice of W .
If j=2 , then v_{0}v_{1}\dots v_{j}v_{0} contradicts the choice of W .
Set X\coloneqq\{v_{0},\dots,v_{\ell}\} .
Since W is r -local, the size of X is at most r .
As C is an induced cycle of G , we may apply Theorem 6.2 to obtain that H_{X}=\bigcup_{x\in X}H_{x} contains a cycle, which contradicts that v\mapsto H_{v} is r -acyclic.
Therefore, every edge of H induces an r -local separation of G .
Conversely, suppose G admits a graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) into cliques which trivially induces r -local separations.
Let v\mapsto H_{v} be the region representation of G over H induced by (H,\mathcal{V}) .
It remains to show that it is r -acyclic.
Suppose for a contradiction that v\mapsto H_{v} is not r -acyclic.
Then there is a set X of at most r vertices such that H_{X}=\bigcup_{x\in X}H_{x} contains a cycle.
We may choose X to be inclusion-minimal.
By Lemma 7.6, there is an edge f in a cycle O of H_{x} with its induced pre-separation \{E_{0},V_{f},E_{1}\} such that the induced cycle C\coloneqq G[X] meets X in a single vertex y and its two incident edges e_{0},e_{1} on C satisfy e_{i}\in E_{i} for i=1,2 .
Since X meets V_{f} only in y and |X|\leqslant r , a closed walk W once around C based at y is an r -local V_{f} -walk.
Then the two edges e_{0},e_{1} incident to y on C are contained in the same E_{i} of the r -local separation \{E_{0},V_{f},E_{1}\} , which is a contradiction.
∎
7.4. Proof of Theorem 7
We can now prove the main result of this section.
Proof of Theorem 7.
By Theorem 4, G is r -locally chordal if and only if G has an r -acyclic region representation v\mapsto H_{v} . By Theorem 7.7, the graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of G into cliques corresponding to v\mapsto H_{v} trivially induces r -local separations. By the definition, (H,\mathcal{V}) is canonical if and only if v\mapsto H_{v} is canonical. By Lemma 3.3, (H,\mathcal{V}) is into maximal cliques if and only if v\mapsto H_{v} is a clique graph. By Theorem 4, each of these is also equivalent to G being r -locally chordal. This completes the proof.
∎
8. Characterization via graph-decompositions that
locally derive tree-decompositions
Recall that each r/2 -ball B_{G}(v,r/2) of a locally chordal graph is chordal, and thus admits a tree-decompositon (T^{v},\mathcal{V}^{v}) into cliques (Theorem 4.2).
In this section, we show that indeed r -acyclic graph-decompositions of an r -locally chordal graph form a common description of some choice of such tree-decompositions of the r/2 -balls into cliques.
Our first observation is that honest r -acyclic graph-decompositions always have high-girth model graphs.
A graph-decomposition is honest if all its adhesion sets are non-empty.
Lemma 8.1.
Let r\geqslant 3 be an integer, let G be a graph, and let (H,\mathcal{V}) be an r -acyclic graph-decomposition of G which is honest. Then H has girth greater than r .
Proof.
Let C be a cycle of H of length \ell\geqslant 3 . Since (H,\mathcal{V}) is honest, we may choose for each edge f of C a vertex v_{f} of G in its adhesion set V_{f} . Now, C\subseteq\bigcup_{f\in E(C)}v_{f} . Since (H,\mathcal{V}) is r -acyclic, it follows that \ell>r . Therefore, H has no cycle of length at most r , i.e. H has girth greater than r .
∎
Consider a graph G and an r -acyclic graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of G . Since H is of high girth, it follows that locally, H is a tree. Could it also be the case that locally, (H,\mathcal{V}) is a tree-decomposition of the r/2 -balls of G ? In the case that G is r -locally chordal and (H,\mathcal{V}) is into cliques, this perfectly corresponds to the fact that chordal graphs are precisely those with tree-decompositions into cliques.
A reasonable guess, therefore, is that a graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) is r -acyclic if and only if (H,\mathcal{V}) induces a tree-decomposition into cliques on each ball B_{G}(v,r/2) for v\in V(G) .
This doesn’t work exactly, since the r/2 -balls for even r are not necessarily induced subgraphs of G , and thus the induced decomposition is in general not even into cliques. Unfortunately, it is sometimes not even a tree-decomposition.
However, we can circumvent these problems for even r , as follows.
First, we make precise what is meant by ’inducing a decomposition’.
Let (H,\mathcal{V}) be a graph-decomposition of a graph G .
Consider an arbitary subgraph G[U] induced on a vertex-subset U of G .
Then assigning each node h of H^{U}\coloneqq H_{U} the bag V^{U}_{h}\coloneqq V_{h}\cap U forms a graph-decomposition of G[U] .
We refer to (H^{U},\mathcal{V}^{U}) as the graph-decomposition of G[U] induced by (H,\mathcal{V}) .
Note that, when the graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of G is into cliques, so is its induced graph-decomposition (H^{U},\mathcal{V}^{U}) of the induced subgraph G[U] of G .
Assume now that (H,\mathcal{V}) is a decomposition of G into cliques.
Now let r\geqslant 0 be an integer.
Assume that r is odd.
Set d\coloneqq(r-1)/2 , i.e. B_{r/2}(v)=G[N^{d}[v]] .
We say that (H,\mathcal{V}) derives a tree-decomposition of B_{r/2}(v) into cliques if its induced graph-decomposition of G[N^{d}[v]]=B_{r/2}(v) is a tree-decomposition into cliques (equivalently: the decomposition graph H^{N^{d}[v]} of the induced graph-decomposition of G[N^{d}[v]]=B_{r/2}(v) is a tree).
Assume that r is even.
Since B_{r/2}(v) is not an induced subgraph of G , we have to be more careful to make sure that the derived decomposition (H^{v},\mathcal{V}^{v}) of B_{r/2}(v) is not only along a tree but also into cliques.
Set d\coloneqq r/2-1 ; in particular, for each vertex u\in N^{r/2}(v) and U^{v}_{u}\coloneqq N^{d}[v]\cup\{u\} , G[U^{v}_{u}]\subseteq B_{r/2}(v) .
We say that (H,\mathcal{V}) derives a tree-decomposition of B_{r/2}(v) into cliques if, for each vertex u\in N^{r/2}(v) , its induced graph-decomposition of G[U^{v}_{u}] is a tree-decomposition (equivalently: the decomposition graph H^{U^{v}_{u}} of the induced graph-decomposition of G[U^{v}_{u}] is a tree).
Why does this property indeed derive a tree-decomposition of the entire ball into cliques when r is even?
For every vertex u\in N^{r/2}(v) , let h_{u} be a node of H^{N^{d}[v]} such that V_{h_{u}} contains u .
Now we set H^{\prime v} to be the disjoint union of H^{N^{d}[v]} and the H_{u} with u\in N^{r/2}(v) where we add an edge between h_{u} and its copy h_{u}^{\prime} in H_{u} .
Assign each node h in H^{\prime v} the bag V^{v}_{h}=V^{N^{d}[v]}_{h} if h is in H^{N^{d}[v]} , the bag V^{v}_{h}=V^{U^{v}_{u}}_{h} if h is in H_{u} .
This forms a graph-decomposition of B_{r/2}(v) .
Moreover, since the decomposition graph H^{U^{v}_{u}} of the induced graph-decomposition of G[U^{v}_{u}] is a tree for each u\in N^{r/2}(v) , the decomposition (H^{v},\mathcal{V}^{v}) is indeed a tree-decomposition of B_{r/2}(v) .
Moreover, it has the additional property that (H^{v},\mathcal{V}^{v}) is into cliques, when (H,\mathcal{V}) is into cliques, as no two vertices in N^{r/2}(v) are in the same bag.
Theorem 8.
Let G be a (possibly infinite) graph, and let r\geqslant 3 be an integer.
Then a graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of G into cliques is r -acyclic if and only if it derives a tree-decomposition into cliques of B_{r/2}(v) for each vertex v of G .
Proof.
First, we assume that (H,\mathcal{V}) is r -acyclic. If r is odd, then B_{r/2}(v) is an induced chordal subgraph of G , and thus, as r\geqslant 3 , the r -acyclic decomposition of B_{r/2}(v) induced by (H,\mathcal{V}) is a tree-decomposition into cliques by Theorem 6.1.
Let us now assume that r is even and set d\coloneqq r/2-1 .
Then the same argument applies to U^{v}_{u}=G[N^{d}[v]\cup\{u\}] for each u\in N^{r/2}(v) because U^{v}_{u} is chordal as induced subgraph of the chordal graph B_{r/2}(v) but also U^{v}_{u} is an induced subgraph of G .
Conversely, assume that (H,\mathcal{V}) derives a tree-decomposition into cliques of B_{r/2}(v) for each vertex v of G .
Let X be a vertex-subset of G of minimum size such that H_{X} contains a cycle; in particular, G[X] is connected.
If |X|\leqslant 3 , then G[X]\subseteq B_{3/2}(v) for some v\in X , and thus decomposition tree H_{N[v]} induced on B_{3/2}(v) contains H_{X} , which contains a cycle - a contradiction.
Thus, |X|\geqslant 4 ; in particular, (H,\mathcal{V}) is 3 -acyclic.
By Theorem 6.2, it suffices to show that G has no induced cycle of length at least 4 but at most r .
Indeed, every cycle in G of length at most r lives in some B_{r/2}(v) if r is odd or in some U^{v}_{u} if r is even. Note that in the respective cases B_{r/2}(v) and U^{v}_{u} are chordal graphs, as witnessed by the induced tree-decomposition of B_{r/2}(v) and U^{v}_{u} , respectively, into cliques. Thus, the cycles in G of length at least 4 but at most r are not induced, as desired.
∎
9. Characterization via minimal local separators
Recall that chordal graphs can also be characterized by the structure of their minimal separators.
See 2.1
How might Theorem 2.1 be generalized to locally chordal graphs? As we have seen in Theorem 7, the local analog of a separation is an r -local separation.
In [JKK25], the authors also introduced a local analog of (tight) separator.
To define it here, we first need to introduce their local analog of components.
Let X be a vertex-subset of a graph G .
The r -local components at X in G are the equivalence classes of the transitive closure of the relation on \partial X defined by letting
e\sim_{r}f at X in G :\Leftrightarrow e and f are the first and last edge on the same r -local X -walk in G , or e=f .
Now X is an r -local separator of G if there are at least two r -local components at X .
An r -local component F at X is tight at X if every vertex in X is incident to some edge in F .
Moreover, an r -local separator X of G is tight if there are at least two tight r -local components at X in G .
We remark that if one would set r\coloneqq\infty , the \infty -local components at X precisely correspond to the componental cuts at X , i.e. the sets E_{G}(C,X) consisting of the edges between X and a fixed component C of G-X .
Moreover, the tight \infty -local components are precisely the componental cuts given by the components of G-X that are tight at X .
Now, one might hope that r -locally chordal graphs are precisely those graphs whose tight r -local separators are cliques.
Unfortunately, not all tight r -local separators of an r -locally chordal graphs are cliques:
Example 9.1.
The graph depicted in Figure 3 is r -locally chordal graph for r=3 and has a tight r -local separator that is not a clique.
Figure 3. A tight r -local separator X , formed by the yellow vertices, in an r -locally chordal graph that is not a clique for r=3 . The two r -local components at X are indicated in red and blue, respectively.
Proof.
Let G be the graph depicted in Figure 3.
Each 3/2 -ball of G is chordal, so G is 3 -locally chordal.
The red and blue edges, respectively, form tight 3 -local components at the set X of yellow vertices, but X is not a clique in G .
∎
Recall that a set X is a tight separator of a graph G if and only if X is a minimal (vertex) separator, i.e. there are two vertices u,w such that X is an inclusion-minimal u – w separator.
The example depicted in Figure 3 suggests that this equivalence does not hold for local separators. To put this on a formal basis, we define the following.
Let u,w be two vertices of a graph G of distance 2 . Fix a vertex v of G with uv,vw\in E(G) .
A vertex-subset X of G r -locally separates u and w in G with respect to v , or is an r -local u – w separator in G with respect to v , if X avoids u,w , contains v , and uv and vw are in distinct r -local components of X .
When r\geqslant 4 or when r\geqslant 3 but X is a clique, we remark that this definition is independent of the choice of the vertex v .
Note that X\coloneqq V(G)\smallsetminus\{u,w\} always forms an r -local u – w -separator of G (with respect to v ).
A vertex-subset X of G is a minimal r -local (vertex) separator in G if there are two vertices u,w of distance 2 in G such that X is an inclusion-minimal r -local u – w separator in G with respect to some vertex v with uv,vw\in E(G) .
Note that the tight r -local separator depicted in Figure 3 is indeed not a minimal r -local separator.
With this definition, Theorem 2.1 extends to locally chordal graphs:
Theorem 9.
For an integer r\geqslant 3 , a (possibly infinite) graph G is r -locally chordal if and only if every minimal r -local separator in G is a clique.
Proof.
We show the two directions separately.
Claim 9.1.1.
If every minimal r -local separator in a graph G is a clique, then G is r -locally chordal.
Proof.
By Theorem 1, it suffices to prove that G neither contains a wheel W_{n} with n\geqslant 4 nor a cycle of length at least four and at most r as an induced subgraph.
Suppose for a contradiction that G contains a cycle O of length at least 4 but at most r (analogously: contains a wheel W_{n} with n\geqslant 4 , rim O ) as an induced subgraph.
Let u,v,w be three consecutive vertices on O (analogously: let u,w be two non-adjacent vertices on the rim O and let v be the hub of wheel). In particular, u,w have distance 2 and uv,vw\in E(G) .
Let X be an inclusion-minimal r -local u – w separator in G .
Since uv and vw are in distinct r -local components at X , the short cycle O meets X in at least one vertex x other than v (analogously: each P_{i} of the two u – w paths P_{1},P_{2} in the rim O meets X in at least one vertex x_{i} ).
Thus, X is a minimal r -local separator in G , which contains two non-adjacent vertices v,x (analogously: x_{1},x_{2} ) of G .
This is a contradiction, since X is a clique by assumption.
∎
Claim 9.1.2.
If G is r -locally chordal, then every minimal r -local separator in G is a clique.
Proof.
By Theorem 7.1, the r -locally chordal graph G admits an r -acyclic graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) into cliques.
Let X be a minimal r -local separator, i.e. there are three vertices u,v,w with uv,vw\in E(G) such that X is an inclusion-minimal u – w separator in G with respect to v .
Thus by definition, X avoids u,w , contains v , and the r -local components F_{u},F_{w} at X which contain uv and vw , respectively, are distinct.
Consider the sets N_{u} and N_{w} of neighbors of v in G outside X that are incident to edges in the r -local component F_{u} and F_{w} , respectively. Set N^{\prime}\coloneqq N_{G}(x)\smallsetminus(X\cup N_{u}\cup N_{w}) .
Since F_{u} and F_{w} are distinct r -local components, N_{u} and N_{w} are disjoint and, as r\geqslant 3 , the three sets N_{u} , N_{w} , N^{\prime} are pairwise anticomplete.
Hence, H_{N_{u}} , H_{N_{w}} , and H_{N^{\prime}} are pairwise disjoint because (H,\mathcal{V}) is into cliques.
As u,w are neighbors of x in G , H_{x} meets H_{u} and H_{w} .
Since H_{x} is connected, there is an H_{u} – H_{w} path P in H_{x} . As H_{N_{u}}\supseteq H_{u} and H_{N_{w}}\supseteq H_{w} are disjoint, there is a last edge on P leaving H_{N_{u}} ; in particular, f has precisely one endnode in H_{N_{u}} . Hence, since H_{N_{u}} , H_{N_{w}} , and H_{N^{\prime}} are pairwise disjoint, f is contained in neither of them, so V_{f}\subseteq N_{G}(x)\smallsetminus(N_{u}\cup N_{w}\cup N^{\prime})=X because x\in V_{f} and (H,\mathcal{V}) is into cliques.
By Theorem 7.7, the edge f trivially induces the pre-separation \{E_{0},V_{f},E_{1}\} and \{E_{0},V_{f},E_{1}\} is an r -local separation of G .
The path P ensures that uv\in E_{0}^{v} and vw\in E_{1}^{v} , where we refer to the definition of \{E_{0},V_{f},E_{1}\} introduced in Lemma 7.5.
Hence, V_{f}\subseteq X r -locally separates u,w in G with respect to v .
By the inclusion-wise minimality of X , we have V_{f}=X , so, X is a clique as desired.
∎
This completes the proof.
∎

10. Computing clique graphs
In Theorem 4, we prove that G is r -locally chordal if and only if G has an r -acyclic clique graph. In this section, we provide a simple and efficient algorithm to compute r -acyclic clique graphs of finite r -locally chordal graphs. This algorithm is a generalization of a well-known algorithm to compute clique trees of chordal graphs.
The algorithm in particular finds a spanning subgraph of the clique intersection graph \mathbf{K}(G) . For the rest of the paper, we equip the edges of clique intersection graphs \mathbf{K}(G) with weights according to the number of vertices in the intersection they represent; i.e. the weight of an edge K_{1}K_{2} is |K_{1}\cap K_{2}| .
Let \mathbf{K} be an edge-weighted graph.
The total weight w_{\mathbf{K}}(H) of a subgraph H of \mathbf{K} is the sum of the weights of the edges in H .
A maximum weight spanning tree of \mathbf{K} is a spanning tree of \mathbf{K} which has maximum total weight among all spanning trees of \mathbf{K} .
Maximum weight spanning trees of clique graphs are related to chordal graphs through the following theorem.
Theorem 10.1 ([G87], Theorem 1).
Let G be a finite connected chordal graph.
Clique trees of G are precisely the maximum weight spanning trees of \mathbf{K}(G) .
Theorem 10.1 is especially useful since there is an efficient algorithm to compute maximum weight spanning trees:
Theorem 10.2 (Kruskal [K56]).
There is an algorithm, Algorithm 10.3, to compute a maximum weight spanning tree of every given finite connected edge-weighted graph G that runs in time O(m\log n) , where m\coloneqq|E(G)| and n\coloneqq|V(G)| .
Algorithm 10.3 (Kruskal [K56]).
Let H be a finite connected edge-weighted input graph.
(1)
Order the edges of H in order e_{1},\dots,e_{m} of decreasing weight.
(2)
Let T_{0} be the edgeless graph with vertex set V(H) .
(3)
For i from 1 to m , let T_{i}\coloneqq T_{i-1}+e_{i} if T_{i}+e_{i} is acyclic. Else, let T_{i}\coloneqq T_{i-1}
(4)
After step m , set T\coloneqq T_{m} .
In view of the previous three results, computing a clique tree of a chordal graph is equivalent to computing a maximum weight spanning tree of \mathbf{K}(G) . In order to assess the running time of such an algorithm, we need to understand the size of \mathbf{K}(G) .
Proposition 10.4 (Folklore, e.g. [G72, R70]).
A chordal graph G on n vertices has at most n maximal cliques. Thus, \mathbf{K}(G) has at most \binom{n}{2}\leqslant n^{2} edges.
All in all, ones derives the following from Theorems 10.1, 10.2 and 10.4.
Theorem 10.5.
Given a finite connected chordal graph G on n vertices, computing the clique intersection graph \mathbf{K}(G) and running Algorithm 10.3 on \mathbf{K}(G) yields a clique tree of G in running time O(n^{2}\log n) .
In this section, we provide a parallel algorithm that, when G is a finite r -locally chordal (equivalently: wheel-free) graph, computes an r -acyclic clique graph of G .
Theorem 10.
There is a parallel algorithm, Algorithm 11, to compute an r -acyclic clique graph of any given finite r -locally chordal graph G that runs on n=|V(G)| processors in time O(\Delta^{2}\log\Delta) , where \Delta is the maximum degree of G .
We remark that Gavril’s proof [G96, Theorem 5] of Theorem 5 (i) \to (ii) is an algorithm with running time O(n^{3}\cdot\log n) .1010
10
In fact, Gavril claims that his algorithm has running time O(n^{3}) . As far as we understand his argument, it yields O(n^{3}\cdot\log n) .
By Theorem 6, it suffices to consider the case r=3 .
Inspired by Gavril’s work, our parallel algorithm works as follows.
Algorithm 11.
Let G be a 3 -locally chordal (equivalently: wheel-free) input graph on [n] .
Run on processor v\in[n] the following:
(1)
Compute the clique intersection graph \mathbf{K}(B_{3/2}(v)) .
(2)
Order the edges of \mathbf{K}(B_{3/2}(v)) in order e^{v}_{1},\dots,e^{v}_{m(v)} of decreasing weight; decide ties of two edges by the lexicographic order on the union of their respective ends.
(3)
Let H^{v}_{0} be the edgeless graph with vertex set V(\mathbf{K}(B_{3/2}(v)) .
(4)
For i from 1 to m(v) , let H^{v}_{i}\coloneqq H^{v}_{i-1}+e^{v}_{i} if H^{v}_{i-1}+e^{v}_{i} is acyclic. Else, let H^{v}_{i}\coloneqq H^{v}_{i-1} .
(5)
After step m(v) , set H^{v}\coloneqq H^{v}_{m(v)} .
After all n processor are finished, set H\coloneqq\bigcup_{v\in V(G)}H^{v} .
10.1. Analysis of the algorithm
Algorithm 11 essentially works by running Kruskal’s algorithm to find a clique tree of B_{3/2}(v) for every v\in V(G) simultaneously. In this section we prove that this algorithm indeed returns an r -acyclic clique graph when the input graph is r -locally chordal.
For this, by Theorem 6, we only need to ensure that the spanning subgraph H of \mathbf{K}(G) returned by Algorithm 11 is a 3 -acyclic clique graph.
Indeed, it suffices to show that H is a 2 -acyclic clique graph due to the following lemma.
Lemma 10.6.
Let G be a (possibly infinite) graph.
Then every 2 -acyclic clique graph H of G is Helly, and thus 3 -acyclic.
Proof.
Let H be a 2 -acyclic clique graph of G , and let v\mapsto H_{v}\coloneqq H[K_{G}(v)] be its corresponding region representation of G .
By Lemma 4.6, the 2 -acyclic clique graph H is 3 -acyclic, if H is Helly.
To show that H is Helly, let
X be a finite vertex-subset of G such that each two H_{x},H_{y} with x,y\in X intersect.
In particular, X is a clique of G .
Let K be a maximal clique of G containing X , which is thus a node of every H_{x} with x\in X .
Hence, Lemma 4.7 (i) yields that H_{X} is a tree.
Therefore, x\mapsto H_{x} is a subtree representation of the complete graph G[X] over the tree H_{X} .
By Theorem 6.3, \bigcap_{x\in X}H_{x} is nonempty.
All in all, the 2 -acyclic clique graph H is Helly, and thus 3 -acyclic.
∎
We note that we cannot push this further to 1 -acyclic due to the following example.
Example 10.7.
Consider the wheel W_{n} with n\geqslant 4 whose hub is v and whose rim is the cycle v_{1}v_{2}\dots v_{n}v_{1} .
Let G be the graph obtained from W_{n} by adding a vertex x and the edges xv_{i} for i=1,2 .
Now, G has a 1 -acyclic clique graph but not a 2 -acyclic clique graph. However, its induced subgraph W_{n} does not even have a 1 -acyclic clique graph.
Proof.
Claim 10.7.1.
The wheel W_{n} with n\geqslant 4 has no 1 -acyclic clique graph.
Proof.
The maximal cliques of W_{n} are D_{i}\coloneqq\{v,v_{i},v_{i+1}\} for i\in[n] , where v_{n+1}=v_{1} , so the clique intersection graph \mathbf{K}(W_{n}) is the cycle D_{1}D_{2}\dots D_{n}D_{1} .
Now consider a clique graph H of W_{n} .
Let i\in[n] . Consider D_{0} to be D_{n} .
Since H_{v_{i}}=H[K_{W_{n}}(v_{i})]=G[\{D_{i-1},D_{i}\}] is connected, the edge D_{i-1}D_{i} is in H .
Thus, H is the whole cycle \mathbf{K}(W_{n}) .
Since H_{v}=H[K_{W_{n}}(v)]=H , the clique graph H is not 1 -acyclic.
∎
Claim 10.7.2.
G has a 1 -acyclic clique graph.
Proof.
The maximal cliques of G are D_{1},\dots,D_{n} and also D\coloneqq\{x,v_{1},v_{2}\} .
Hence, the clique graph \mathbf{K}(G) is obtained from \mathbf{K}(W_{n}) by adding D and the edges DD_{n},DD_{1},D_{2} .
Let H be the spanning cycle DD_{1}D_{2}\dots D_{n}D .
We claim that H is a 1 -acyclic clique graph of G .
For i\in[n]\smallsetminus\{1,2\} , H_{v_{i}} consists of the single edge D_{i-1}D_{i} .
H_{v_{1}} is the path D_{n}DD_{1} , H_{v_{2}} is the path DD_{1}D_{2} , and H_{x} is the graph on the single vertex D .
Moreover, H_{v} is the path D_{1}D_{2}\dots D_{n} .
All in all, G is a 1 -acyclic clique graph.
∎
To see that G has no 2 -acyclic clique graph, one follows the argument presented in the proof of Claim 10.7.1 to show that a 1 -acyclic clique graph H of G contains all but one edge of \mathbf{K}(W_{n}) .
Thus, the union of H_{v} and either H_{v_{1}} or H_{v_{2}} forms a cycle, and thus H is not 2 -acyclic.
∎
The next lemma yields that the spanning subgraph H of \mathbf{K}(G) returned by Algorithm 11 is 2 -acylic, if the parallel runs of the algorithm make the same decision about whether to contain an edge.
Lemma 10.8.
Let G be a (possibly infinite) graph and
let H be a spanning subgraph of \mathbf{K}(G) .
Then H is a 2 -acyclic clique graph of G if and only if H[K_{G}(v)] is a clique tree of B_{3/2}(v) for all vertices v of G .
Proof.
Set H_{v}\coloneqq H[K_{G}(v)] for every vertex v of G .
By Lemma 3.2, H_{u}\cap H_{v}=H_{v}[K_{B_{3/2}(v)}(u)] for every two vertices u,v of G .
First, assume that H is a 2 -acyclic clique graph.
Fix a vertex v of G .
Consider a vertex u of B_{3/2}(v) .
It remains to show that H_{v}[K_{B_{3/2}(v)}(u)] is connected.
Since u is a vertex of B_{3/2}(v) , there is a maximal clique of G containing u and v .
Thus, by Lemma 7.3, H_{u}\cap H_{v}=H_{v}[K_{B_{3/2}(v)}(u)] is connected.
Conversely, assume that H_{v} is a clique tree of B_{3/2}(v) for all vertices v of G .
In particular, H_{v} is connected for all v\in V(G) .
Therefore, the spanning subgraph H of \mathbf{K}(G) is a clique graph.
It remains to show that H is 2 -acyclic.

12. Future work
In this section, we detail several directions for future work about locally chordal graphs.
12.1. Bounding the number of maximal cliques
One property of finite chordal graphs is that they have at most n maximal cliques (Proposition 10.4), where n is the number of their vertices. We can thus easily deduce that every r -locally chordal graph G for r\geqslant 3 has at most n^{2} maximal cliques by observing that B_{3/2}(v) is chordal for every v\in V(G) and that every maximal clique of G lives in B_{3/2}(v) for some v . However, we suspect that a better bound should hold for the number of maximal cliques in r -locally chordal graphs, where the bound depends on r and approaches n as r tends to infinity. Specifically, we conjecture the following:
Conjecture 12.1.
For every \epsilon>0 , there exists an integer r\geqslant 0 such that the number of maximal cliques in finite r -locally chordal graphs G is O(|V(G)|^{1+\varepsilon}) .
An extremal case of r -locally chordal graphs are those whose r/2 -balls are tree, i.e. precisely the graphs of girth >r .
Then Conjecture 12.1 holds, as the number of maximal cliques coincides with the number of edges in connected graphs G of girth >r , which is at most n^{1+\frac{2}{r-2}} if the graph G has n vertices and at least 2n edges [B78, §III].
12.2. Algorithmic applications
One benefit of structural decompositions like tree-decompositions is that they enable the design of algorithms for graphs with “good” decompositions. Indeed, tree-decompositions play a large role in algorithmic graph theory. In Algorithm 11, we give an efficient algorithm to compute r -acyclic graph-decompositions into cliques (equivalently: r -acyclic clique graphs) of r -locally chordal graphs, which are structural descriptions that generalize tree-decomposition into cliques (equivalently: clique trees). Therefore, a very interesting follow-up question is whether these representations can be used in the design of efficient algorithms for locally chordal graphs.
One of the most successful algorithmic applications of chordal graphs is to the maximum independent set problem. The Maximum Independent Set problem asks, given a graph G (and possibly a weight function w:V(G)\to\mathbb{N} of the vertices), to return a maximum size (or maximum weight) independent set of G . Maximum Independent Set is a classic NP-hard problem in general [K72], and is even hard to approximate in general [H99]. However, there is a linear-time algorithm to solve Maximum independent set in chordal graphs [G72]. One can also solve MIS in chordal graphs using standard 1dynamic programming on a tree-decomposition into cliques, which does not require that the chordal graphs have bounded treewidth, because independent sets intersect cliques, and thus bags of tree-decompositions, in at most one vertex. The nice interplay between chordal graphs and the Maximum Independent Set problem has inspired several other approaches to and perspectives on the Maximum Independent Set problem, for example the PMC method [LVV14, FV10]and the width-parameter tree-independence number [DMŠ24].
Since chordal graphs and Maximum Independent Set are such a fruitful pair, the Maximum Independent Set problem is a promising potential algorithmic application of locally chordal graphs. Our decompositions likewise have the property that they are into cliques, so a solution to Maximum independent set intersects each bag in at most one vertex. Since r -locally chordal graphs include, in particular, all graphs of girth greater than r , the class of graphs of high girth witnesses that Maximum Independent Set is NP-hard even in r -locally chordal graphs [P74]. However, we hope for an affirmative answer to the following.
Question 12.2.
Is there a good approximation algorithm for Maximum Independent Set in r -locally chordal graphs?
There are decent approximation algorithms for Maximum Independent Set in high-girth graphs, e.g. [M92]. We hope that these algorithms can be modified to give approximation algorithms in locally chordal graphs given their r -acyclic graph-decompositions (H,\mathcal{V}) into cliques, by using the high-girth model graph H (on which the high-girth algorithms can be leveraged) and the fact that all bags are cliques (which intersect independent sets in at most one vertex).
12.3. Stronger Helly properties
Let H be a graph and let \mathbf{H} be a family of subgraphs of H . The family \mathbf{H} has the Helly-Erdős-Pósa property if, for every k\geqslant 2 , every finite subfamily \mathbf{H}^{\prime} of \mathbf{H} contains either k pairwise disjoint sets or there is a vertex-subset of H of size at most k-1 that meets every subgraph of \mathbf{H}^{\prime} . Observe that when k=2 this corresponds to the Helly property defined in this paper, so the Helly-Erdős-Pósa property can be viewed as a generalization of the Helly property.
It is known that if T is a tree and \mathbf{T} is a family of subtrees of T , then \mathbf{T} satisfies the Helly-Erdős-Pósa property, see [GL70]. Therefore, if G is chordal, then the families corresponding to acyclic region representations of G (i.e. if G is connected, the subtree representations of G ) satisfy the Helly-Erdős-Pósa property.
For r -locally chordal graphs, we proved that the families corresponding to their r -acyclic region representations satisfy the Helly property. A natural extension of this result is thus to ask whether locally chordal graphs, like chordal graphs, also satisfy the Helly-Erdős-Pósa property:
Question 12.3.
Let r\geqslant 3 be an integer and G an r -locally chordal graph. For an r -acyclic region representation v\mapsto H_{v} of G , does the family (H_{v}\mid v\in V(G)) satisfy the Helly-Erdős-Pósa property?
We thank Piotr Micek for suggesting Question 12.3 in private communication.
12.4. Generalizing treewidth
Following the correspondence of the tree-decompositions of a graph and its chordal completions,
a final intriguing direction for further study is to exploit a similiar correspondence between r -acyclic graph-decompositions and and r -locally chordal completions to generalize the notion of treewidth. Inspired by our work on locally chordal graphs, two of the authors introduce together with Diestel a new (parameterized by r , as in r -locally chordal) width parameter called r -acyclic width. Let us begin by reviewing relevant background information.
13.1. Background: Separations
Let us recall some definitions regarding separations.
Let G be a graph.
A separation \{A,B\} of G is proper if both A\smallsetminus B and B\smallsetminus A are non-empty.
A separation \{A,B\} of G is tight if each proper side contains the vertex set of a tight component of G-(A\cap B) .
We remark that a separation \{A,B\} is tight if and only if its separator A\cap B is an inclusion-minimal a – b separator for some a\in A\smallsetminus B and b\in B\smallsetminus A .
The order of \{A,B\} is the size k\coloneqq|A\cap B| of its separator.
We also say that \{A,B\} is a k -separation of G .
Note that the automorphisms \varphi of G naturally act on the separations s=\{A,B\} of G by mapping them to \varphi(s)\coloneqq\{\varphi(A),\varphi(B)\} .
For a subgroup \Gamma of \Aut(G) , a set S of separations of G is \Gamma -invariant if \varphi(s)\in S for every s\in S and every \varphi\in\Gamma .
13.2. Background: Interplay of tree-decompositions and separations
We refer to (A,B) and (B,A) as the orientations of the separation \{A,B\} or just as oriented separations of G .
Two separations \{A,B\} and \{C,D\} are nested if there are orientations of \{A,B\} and \{C,D\} , say (A,B) and (C,D) , such that A\subseteq C and B\supseteq D .
A set of separations is nested if its elements are pairwise nested.
Let (T,\mathcal{V}) be a tree-decomposition of a graph G .
Recall that, by Item (T2’), every edge f of the decomposition tree T induces a separation s^{f} of G .
A tree-decomposition is regular if all its induced separations are proper.
Lemma 13.1 ([DJKK22], Lemma 3.7).
Let (T,\mathcal{V}) be a tree-decomposition of a graph G .
If (T,\mathcal{V}) is regular, then for every automorphism \gamma of G there exists at most one automorphism \varphi of T such that \gamma(V_{t})=V_{\varphi(t)} .
In particular, if additionally (T,\mathcal{V}) is \Gamma -canonical for a subgroup \Gamma of \Aut(G) , then there is a unique action of \Gamma on T which commutes with t\mapsto V_{t} .
Let G be a graph.
A tree-decomposition (T,\mathcal{V}) of G is point-finite if T_{v} is finite for every vertex v of G .
A set S of separations of G is point-finite if every vertex of G appears in the separator of at most finitely many separations in S .
Given a nested set N of proper separations of a graph, let \mathcal{T}(N) be defined as in [JKK25, Construction 2.3].
Lemma 13.2 ([EKT22], Lemma 2.7).
Let N be a nested set of proper separations of a connected graph G . If N is point-finite, then there is a tree decomposition \mathcal{T}(N) of G such that f\mapsto s^{f} is a bijection from the edges of T to N .
In particular, \mathcal{T}(N) is regular and point-finite.
Moreover, if N is \Gamma(G) -invariant, then \mathcal{T}(N) is \Gamma -canonical.
13.3. Canonical tree-decompositions into cliques
Given two vertex-subsets X,Y of a graph G , a separation \{A,B\} of G distinguishes X and Y if, up to renaming sides, X\subseteq A , X meets A\smallsetminus B , Y\subseteq B and Y meets B\smallsetminus A .
If \{A,B\} has minimum order among the separations of G that separate X and Y , then \{A,B\} distinguishes X and Y efficiently.
A set S of separations of G efficiently distinguishes a set \mathcal{X} of vertex-subsets of G if every two elements of \mathcal{X} are efficiently distinguished by some s\in S .
Lemma 13.3.
Let X and Y be two distinct maximal cliques in a locally finite, chordal graph G .
Then X and Y are distinguished by a separation of order less than the sizes of X and Y .
Proof.
If X lies in another component C of G than Y , then \{C,V(G)\smallsetminus C\} is as desired.
So we assume otherwise; in particular, we may assume without loss of generality that G is connected.
By Theorem 4.2, the chordal graph G admits a tree-decomposition (T,\mathcal{V}) into maximal cliques.
Consider the bags V_{s},V_{t} with V_{s}=X and V_{t}=Y .
Let f=ss^{\prime} be the first edge of the unique s – t path in T .
Since (T,\mathcal{V}) is into maximal cliques, V_{f}=V_{s}\cap V_{s^{\prime}} is a proper subset of X=V_{s} and Y=V_{t} .
Thus, the separation s^{f} of G corresponding to f distinguishes X and Y and has order less than |X| and |Y| .
∎
For a given locally finite, connected graph G , let N(G) be defined as in [JKK25, Construction 3.6].
Lemma 13.4.
Let G be a locally finite, connected chordal graph. Then N(G) is an \Aut(G) -invariant nested set of tight separations of G that efficiently distinguishes every two distinct maximal cliques of G .
Proof.
The following proof uses the notion of ’bottleneck in G ’ from [JKK25, § 3.1] as black box, in that it is only relevant to the proof that bottlenecks are sets of tight separations of G and, by Lemma 13.3, the set \beta(K,K^{\prime}) of separations which efficiently distinguish some two distinct maximal cliques K,K^{\prime} in G is a so-called bottleneck in G [JKK25, Example 3.4].
Then [JKK25, Theorem 3.7] yields that the nested set N(G) of separations of G meets in particular every \beta(K,K^{\prime}) .
Hence, N(G) efficiently distinguishes the set K(G) of all maximal cliques.
The construction of N(G) [JKK25, Construction 3.6] is invariant under isomorphisms of the underlying graph and thus N(G) is \Aut(G) -invariant.
Moreover, all elements of N(G) are tight, as they are contained in some so-called bottleneck, which by definition only contains tight separations.
∎
For experts, we remark that one may alternatively obtain a nested set like N(G) by [EKT22, Theorem 1.3], as every maximal clique in a chordal graph yields a ’robust regular profile’ and by invoking Lemma 13.3 one may prove that each two of them are ’distinguishable’. Unfortunately, the nested set from [DJKK22, Definition 5.2] does not work in general, as the ’tangles’ induced by cliques K have in general only order 2/3\cdot|K| , which as a consequence may make the two tangles induced by two distinct maximal cliques indistinguishable.
Lemma 13.5.
Let G be a locally finite, connected chordal graph.
Assume that N is a nested set of tight separations of G that efficiently distinguishes every two distinct maximal cliques in G .
Then all separators of separations in N are cliques and N is point-finite.
Proof.
Since all separations in N are tight, so are their separators, which are hence cliques by Theorem 2.1.
Since G is locally finite, every vertex of G is contained in only finitely many cliques, and since G is connected, there are only finitely many separations with the same finite separator.
Thus, every vertex of G is contained in only finitely many separators of separations in N , so N is point-finite.
∎
Lemma 13.6.
[HL18, Lemma 14]
If X is a tight separator of a (possibly infinite) chordal graph G , then there are two distinct vertices u,v\in V(G)\smallsetminus X such that X is a u – v separator and both u and v are complete to X .
Lemma 13.7.
Let G be a connected, locally finite, chordal graph. Assume that N is a nested set of tight separations of G that efficiently distinguishes every two distinct maximal cliques.
Then \mathcal{T}(N) is a regular, point-finite tree-decomposition of G into cliques.
Moreover, \mathcal{T}(N) is canonical if N is \Aut(G) -invariant.
Proof.
By Lemma 13.5, N is point-finite and the separators of separations in N are cliques.
As all separations in N are tight, they are also proper.
So Lemma 13.2 ensures that \mathcal{T}\coloneqq\mathcal{T}(N) is a tree-decomposition (T,\mathcal{V}) such that the map f\mapsto s^{f} is a bijection from E(T) to N ; in particular, N=\{s^{f}\mid f\in E(T)\} , and thus all adhesion sets of (T,\mathcal{V}) are cliques of G .
The latter ensures that every part G_{t}=G[V_{t}] of \mathcal{T} is connected, since G is connected.
Note that, by Lemma 13.2, \mathcal{T} is regular and point-finite, and also \mathcal{T} is canonical, if N is \Aut(G) -invariant.
We claim that \mathcal{T} is into cliques.
Suppose for a contradiction that some V_{t} is not a clique of G , i.e. there are two non-adjacent vertices u,v\in V_{t} .
In particular, V_{t}\smallsetminus\{u,v\} separates u and v in the part G_{t}=G[V_{t}] of (T,\mathcal{V}) .
Now let X be an inclusion-minimal u – v separator in G_{t} .
Note that X is nonempty, as the part G_{t} of \mathcal{T} is connected.
As G is chordal, so is its induced subgraph G[V_{t}]=G_{t} .
Thus, Theorem 2.1 implies that X is a clique, and Lemma 13.6 ensures that there are two vertices u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in V_{t}\smallsetminus X such that X is a u^{\prime} – v^{\prime} separator in G_{t} and both u^{\prime} and v^{\prime} are complete to X .
Let K_{1} and K_{2} be two maximal cliques of G that contain X\cup\{u^{\prime}\} and X\cup\{v^{\prime}\} , respectively.
As the adhesion sets of (T,\mathcal{V}) are cliques, X is not only a u^{\prime} – v^{\prime} separator in the part G_{t} but also in G .
In particular, X separates the cliques K_{1}\ni u^{\prime} and K_{2}\ni v^{\prime} in G .
Note that every K_{1} – K_{2} separator in G must contain their intersection K_{1}\cap K_{2} , which contains at least X .
Hence, the only K_{1} – K_{2} separator of minimum size in G is indeed X .
Since N=\{s^{f}\mid f\in E(T)\} efficiently distinguishes every two distinct maximal cliques such as K_{1} and K_{2} by assumption, some separation s^{f} corresponding to an edge f of T separates K_{1} and K_{2} efficiently.
Then its separator is X following the above analysis; in particular, s^{f} separates u^{\prime}\in K_{1}\smallsetminus X and v^{\prime}\in K_{2}\smallsetminus X .
But u^{\prime},v^{\prime} are in the common bag V_{t} and thus cannot be separated by a separation s^{f} corresponding to an edge f of T by the definition of s^{f} , which is a contradiction.
∎
Theorem 13.8.
Let G be a locally finite, connected chordal graph.
Then \mathcal{T}(N(G)) is a point-finite, regular, canonical tree-decomposition of G into cliques.
Proof.
By Lemma 13.4, N(G) is an \Aut(G) -invariant, nested set N of tight separations of G that efficiently distinguishes every two distinct maximal cliques of G .
Then Lemma 13.7 yields that \mathcal{T}(N(G)) is a canonical tree-decomposition of G into cliques, which is regular and point-finite.
∎
Proof of Theorem 3.
This follows immediately from Theorem 13.8 by choosing the tree-decomposition \mathcal{T} to be \mathcal{T}(N(G)) .
∎
13.4. Tree-decompositions of coverings into maximal cliques
Next we prove Theorem 3.7.
In view of Example 3.8, it is unfortunately not the case that every (locally) finite chordal graph admits a canonical tree-decomposition into maximal cliques. For our purposes, however, it is enough that the tree-decomposition be \Gamma(p) -canonical, and this turns out to be true. We need one more lemma before we are ready for the proof.
Recall that a covering p\colon\hat{G}\to G is normal if the group \Gamma(p) of deck transformations of p acts transitively on the fibres p^{-1}(v) of p .
Indeed, the desired result holds for all normal coverings of a graph, since the normal coverings yet satisfy the following weak version of Lemma 2.4 (iii).
Lemma 13.9.
Let G be a connected, locally finite graph and p\colon\hat{G}\to G a normal covering, e.g. the r -local covering.
Then for every clique K , the intersection K\cap\gamma(K) is empty whenever \gamma\in\Gamma(p)\smallsetminus\{\text{id}_{\hat{G}}\} .
Proof.
As G is simple, it has no loops.
Thus, the fibres of p are independent sets in \hat{G} .
In particular, every two distinct vertices in a clique K of \hat{G} are in distinct fibres of p .
Thus, the desired statement follows, as \Gamma(p) always acts freely and, since P is normal, also transitively on the fibres of p .
∎
Now we can prove Theorem 3.7.
Proof of Theorem 3.7.
Set \Gamma\coloneqq\Gamma(p) .
Theorem 13.8 yields a tree-decomposition of G that is a point-finite, regular, \Gamma -canonical and also into cliques.
From now on let (T,\mathcal{V}) be any tree-decomposition of \hat{G} with these properties.
We claim that if there is no edge f=st of T such that V_{s}\subseteq V_{t} , then (T,\mathcal{V}) is into maximal cliques, i.e.
(i)
for every maximal clique K of \hat{G} , there is a unique node t(K) of T with K=V_{t(K)} , and
(ii)
every V_{t} is a maximal clique.
Item (i):
First, we show that t(K) exists.
By Lemma 3.1, we may consider the subtree representation v\mapsto T_{v} of G corresponding to (T,\mathcal{V}) .
Thus, \bigcap_{v\in K}T_{v} is non-empty, since the T_{v} with v\in K are pairwise intersecting because K is a clique and v\mapsto T_{v} is Helly by Theorem 6.3.
Let t be some node in the intersection \bigcap_{v\in K}T_{v} , i.e. K\subseteq V_{t} .
As (T,\mathcal{V}) is into cliques and K is a maximal clique, V_{t} is indeed equal to K .
Secondly, we show that t(K) is unique.
Suppose for a contradiction that there are two distinct nodes t,s of T with V_{t},V_{s}=K .
Then (T2) ensures that all vertices t^{\prime} on the t – s path in T satisfy X\subseteq V_{t^{\prime}} .
As (T,\mathcal{V}) is into cliques and X is a maximal clique, we have X=V_{t^{\prime}} .
Thus, every edge f=t^{\prime}s^{\prime} on the t – s path in T contradicts the assumption, as V_{t^{\prime}}=X=V_{s^{\prime}} .
Item (ii):
Suppose for a contradiction that V_{t} is not a maximal clique.
Let K be a maximal clique that contains V_{t} .
Let f=ts be the first edge on the t – t(K) path in T .
Then (T2) ensures that V_{t}\subseteq V_{s} , which contradicts our assumption.
Since (T,\mathcal{V}) is \Gamma -canonical and regular, the deck transformations of p act by Lemma 13.1 uniquely on T such that this action commutes with t\mapsto V_{t} .
We claim that T is countable.
Indeed, since (T,\mathcal{V}) is point-finite and the connected graph G is locally finite, T is the union \bigcup_{v\in V(G)}T_{v} of countably many finite graphs, and thus countable.
Hence, E(T) countably many \Gamma -orbits M_{1},M_{2},\dots .
Set I_{0}\coloneqq\emptyset .
We iteratively construct an inclusion-wise increasing sequence of I_{i}\subseteq[i] such that the tree-decomposition (T^{i},\mathcal{V}^{i}) of \hat{G} obtained by contracting M^{i}\coloneqq\bigcup_{j\in I_{i}}M_{j} in T , i.e. set T^{i}\coloneqq T/M^{i} , set V_{t}^{i}\coloneqq\bigcup_{s\in C}V_{s} if t is the contraction vertex given by some component C of (V(T),M^{i}) , and else V^{i}_{t}\coloneqq V_{t} , is into cliques, and we have V^{i}_{s}\nsubseteq V^{i}_{t} and V^{i}_{s}\nsupseteq V^{i}_{t} for every edge f=st\in\bigcup_{j\in[i]\smallsetminus I_{i}}M_{j} .
It is immediate from the definition that the tree-decompositions (T^{i},\mathcal{V}^{i}) are point-finite, regular, \Gamma -canonical, and the \Gamma -orbits of E(T^{i}) are the M_{j} with j\in[i]\smallsetminus I_{i} and the M_{i},M_{i+1},\dots .
Assume that I_{i-1} is defined for some integer i\geqslant 1 .
Let f_{i}=st be an edge in M_{i} .
If V_{s}\nsubseteq V_{t} and V_{s}\nsupseteq V_{t} , then I_{i}\coloneqq I_{i-1} is as desired.
Otherwise, we claim that I_{i}\coloneqq I_{i-1}\cup\{i\} is as desired.
Observe that the \Gamma -orbit M_{i} of f_{i} in E(T^{i-1}) is a matching in T^{i-1} by Lemma 13.9.
The tree-decomposition (T^{i},\mathcal{V}^{i}) of \hat{G} is (canonically isomorphic to) the one obtained from (T^{i-1},\mathcal{V}^{i-1}) by contracting M_{i} in T^{i-1} .
As only independent edges f=st with, say, V^{i-1}_{s}\subseteq V^{i-1}_{t} have been contracted, (T^{i},\mathcal{V}^{i}) is still into cliques.
Set I\coloneqq\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}I_{i} , and let (T^{\prime},\mathcal{V}^{\prime}) be the tree-decomposition of \hat{G} obtained from (T,\mathcal{V}) by contracting \bigcup_{j\in I}M_{j} in T .
Again, (T^{\prime},\mathcal{V}^{\prime}) is by definition point-finite, regular, \Gamma -canonical, and we have V^{\prime}_{s}\nsubseteq V^{\prime}_{t} and V^{\prime}_{s}\nsupseteq V^{\prime}_{t} for every edge f=st\in E(T^{\prime}) .
We note that every bag V^{\prime}_{t} is a clique of G , as otherwise there would have been a bag V^{i}_{t^{\prime}}\subseteq V^{\prime}_{t} which was not a clique as well, which contradicts the recursive construction.
∎
Corollary 13.10.
Let G be a connected, locally finite graph and p\colon\hat{G}\to G a normal covering, e.g. the r -local covering.
If \hat{G} is chordal, then its group \Gamma(p) of deck transformations is free.
Proof.
Set \Gamma\coloneqq\Gamma(p) .
By Theorem 3, \hat{G} admits a \Gamma -canonical tree-decomposition (T,(V_{t})_{t\in T}) into cliques.
Lemma 13.9 ensures that \Gamma acts freely on the decomposition tree T .
Hence, it follows from [L17, Theorem 4.2.1] that \Gamma is free.
∎
14. Comparison: Canonical graph-decomposition via coverings
We conclude by highlighting the relationship between the graph-decompositions obtained in this paper and the graph-decompositions developed and studied in [JKK25], which is a slight adaptation of the one from [DJKK22, Theorem 1], as discussed in [JKK25]. We warn the reader that this section assumes some familiarity with [JKK25].
We start by reviewing the graph-decompositions from [JKK25].
Let G be a locally finite, connected graph and r a positive integer.
For k\in\mathbb{N} , we denote by N^{\leqslant k}(G) the subset of N(G) of all separations of order at most k .
Then \mathcal{T}^{\leqslant k}\coloneqq\mathcal{T}(N^{\leqslant k}(G_{r})) is a tree-decomposition by [JKK25, Theorem 3.12].
Let \mathcal{H}_{r}^{\leqslant k}\coloneqq{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}{{\mathcal{H}_{r}^{\leqslant k}(G)}}} be the graph-decomposition of G obtained from \mathcal{T}(N^{\leqslant k}(G_{r})) by folding via p_{r} .
In [JKK25, Theorem 1], they discuss the computability of these graph-decompositions \mathcal{H}_{r}^{\leqslant k} for finite graphs G .
Note that, for k\leqslant k^{\prime} , one may obtain \mathcal{H}_{r}^{\leqslant k} from \mathcal{H}_{r}^{\leqslant k^{\prime}} by contracting all edges hh^{\prime} of the decomposition graph H_{r}^{\leqslant k^{\prime}} of \mathcal{H}_{r}^{\leqslant k^{\prime}} which are the orbit of an edge st of the decomposition tree T^{\leqslant k^{\prime}} of \mathcal{T}^{\leqslant k^{\prime}} that induce a separation of G of order >k and merge the bags assigned to h and h^{\prime} .
Thus, we say that \mathcal{H}_{r}^{\leqslant k^{\prime}} refines \mathcal{H}_{r}^{\leqslant k} .
If also {\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}{{\mathcal{T}(G_{r})}}}\coloneqq\mathcal{T}(N(G_{r})) is a tree-decomposition then the graph-decomposition {\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}{{\mathcal{H}_{r}(G)}}} obtained from \mathcal{T}(G_{r}) by folding via p_{r} is their limit.
Theorem 14.
Let G be a connected, locally finite graph and r\geqslant 3 an integer.
The following are equivalent:
(i)
G is r -locally chordal.
(ii)
The graph-decomposition \mathcal{H}_{r}(G) obtained from the canonical tree-decomposition \mathcal{T}(G_{r}) by folding via p_{r} is well-defined and into cliques.
Proof.
Assume that G is r -locally chordal.
Then its r -local cover G_{r} is chordal by Theorem 1.
Thus, \mathcal{T}(N(G)) is into cliques by Theorem 13.8.
Therefore, Lemma 3.6 ensures that \mathcal{H}_{r}(G) is into cliques.
Conversely, assume that \mathcal{H}_{r}(G) is well-defined and into cliques.
Then Lemma 3.6 yields that \mathcal{T} is into cliques.
So, the r -local cover G_{r} of G is chordal by Proposition 4.1.
Therefore, Theorem 1 ensures that G is r -locally chordal.
∎
