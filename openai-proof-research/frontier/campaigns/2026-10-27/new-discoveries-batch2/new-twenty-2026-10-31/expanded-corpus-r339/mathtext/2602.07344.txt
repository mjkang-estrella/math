The complexity of pinning simple multiloops
Eric Seo
Christopher-Lloyd Simon
Ben Stucky
August 24, 2026
Abstract
A multiloop with s\in\mathbb{N} strands is a generic immersion \gamma\colon\sqcup_{1}^{s}\mathbb{S}^{1}\looparrowright\Sigma of the union of s circles into a surface \Sigma , considered up to homeomorphisms.
A pinning set of \gamma is a set of points P\subset\Sigma\setminus\operatorname{im}(\gamma) , such that in the punctured surface \Sigma\setminus P , the immersion \gamma has the minimal number of double points in its homotopy class.
Its pinning number \varpi(\gamma) is the minimum cardinal of its pinning sets.
In any fixed orientable surface \Sigma , the pinning problem which given a multiloop \gamma and k\in\mathbb{N} decides whether \varpi(\gamma)\leq k has been show to be NP-complete, even in restrictions to loops (with s=1 strand).
In this work we study the complexity of the pinning problem in restriction to multiloops whose strands are simple (embedded circles).
We show that in any fixed oriented surface \Sigma , the problem is in P when s\leq 3 and NP-complete when s\geq 20 , and present some follow-up questions and conjectures.

0 Introduction
0.1 Key definitions and previous work
Definition 0.1 (multiloop, simple multiloop, pinning set, pinning number).
A multiloop with s\in\mathbb{N} strands in a smooth surface \Sigma is a generic immersion \gamma\colon\sqcup_{1}^{s}\mathbb{S}^{1}\looparrowright\Sigma of the union of s circles into \Sigma , considered up to diffeomorphisms of the source and the target.
In particular, it has a finite number of multiple points and those are all transverse double-points: we denote that number of double-points by \#\gamma . A multiloop is called simple when each of its strands is embedded in \Sigma .
The connected components of \Sigma\setminus\operatorname{im}(\gamma) will be called regions of \gamma and we denote by \mathcal{R}(\gamma) the set of regions.
For a subset of regions P\subset\mathcal{R}(\gamma) , we define the self-intersection number \si_{P}(\gamma) of \gamma in \Sigma\setminus P as the minimal number of double-points of multiloops in \Sigma\setminus P homotopic to \gamma .
We say that P is a pinning set for \gamma when \#\gamma=\si_{P}(\gamma) , namely when \gamma is taut in \Sigma\setminus P .
The collection of pinning sets of \gamma forms the pinning ideal \mathcal{PI}(\gamma) , that is a poset under inclusion which is absorbing under union.
Among the minimal pinning sets, some are optimal in the sense that they minimize the cardinal function: this minimum cardinal is called the pinning number \varpi(\gamma) .
Remark 0.2 (no orientations).
By definition, a multiloop has non-oriented strands and its ambient surface is not endowed with a preferred orientation (even when it is orientable), since the diffeomorphisms may reverse any of these orientations.
Example 0.3 (empirical structure of pinning ideals).
A multiloop may have minimal pinning sets of different cardinalities as demonstrated in Figure 2. The second and third author used the results of [25] and some functionalities from plantri [4] and SnapPy [6] to enumerate the smallest multiloops in \mathbb{S}^{2} and compute and plot their pinning ideals. In particular we did this for the smallest simple multiloops. The full results are available in the LooPindex online catalog [24].

The pinning problem \mathsf{Pin}(\Sigma,s) has
Instance:
A combinatorial encoding of a multiloop \gamma\colon\sqcup_{i=1}^{s}\mathbb{S}^{1}\looparrowright\Sigma (see below) and k\in\mathbb{Z} .

Remark 0.5 (filling multiloops).
A multiloop \gamma is filling when all of its regions \mathcal{R}(\gamma) have genus zero (namely are homeomorphic to disks with a certain number of punctures). It is enough to restrict to multiloops that are filling when studying the complexity of \mathsf{Pin}(\Sigma,s) because any region with positive genus may be replaced by a disk with a puncture.
Definition 0.6 (combinatorial encodings of multiloops).
Our complexity results depend on the existence of combinatorial encodings of multiloops in surfaces.
There are several ways to do this which lead to equivalent notions of complexity.
For filling multiloops, one may do this using maps and their encodings as permutations as in [25].
The size of a \mathsf{Pin}(\Sigma,s) problem instance (\gamma,k) in memory (that is, the size of the input) is then proportional to \#\gamma+\lvert k\rvert .
The second and third author show in [25] that for every fixed orientable \Sigma and s\geq 1 , the pinning problem \mathsf{Pin}(\Sigma,s) is NP-complete, by a reduction from the graph-theoretic decision problem Planar Vertex Cover (see Definition 1.17).
0.2 Summary of results
In this work we consider the restriction of the pinning problem to the class of simple multiloops, and will show that its complexity exhibits a “phase transition” as the number of strands increases.
Definition 0.7 ( \mathsf{SimplePin} ).
For a surface \Sigma and s\in\mathbb{N} , the decision problem \mathsf{SimplePin}(\Sigma,s) has
Instance:
A simple multiloop \gamma\colon\sqcup_{i=1}^{s}\mathbb{S}^{1}\looparrowright\Sigma and k\in\mathbb{Z} .

Recalling the formal language definition, we also let \mathsf{SimplePin}(\Sigma)=\cup_{s=1}^{\infty}\mathsf{SimplePin}(\Sigma,s) .
A simple loop is trivially pinned by the empty set.
For simple multiloops with 2 strands, it is an exercise (as we will see) to show that there is a unique minimal pinning set (hence a unique optimal pinning set) and it is computable in polynomial time, so the problem is in P.
Our first main result is that the pinning problem remains easy for simple multiloops with s\leq 3 strands.

Remark 0.10 (restricting to \mathbb{R}^{2} to establish hardness).
If \Sigma_{1} is homotopic to a subsurface of an orientable surface \Sigma_{2} , then a hardness result for a pinning problem in \Sigma_{1} implies the same hardness result for that pinning problem in \Sigma_{2} . So to prove that a pinning problem is NP-hard, it is enough to prove it for \Sigma=\mathbb{R}^{2} .
However, for easyness results (such as proving that a pinning problem is in P or NP), we must deal with all surfaces.
0.3 Further directions of research
0.3.1 Hardness threshold
Theorems 0.8 and 0.9 raise the question: For a fixed surface \Sigma , what is the largest s\in\mathbb{N} such that \mathsf{SimplePin}(\Sigma,s) is P?
We suspect that the answer to this question is 3 , and provide some rationale for the following conjecture in Section 2.3.
Conjecture 0.11 (hardness starts at s=4 ).
For a fixed orientable surface \Sigma , the problem \mathsf{SimplePin}(\Sigma,4) is NP-complete.
0.3.2 Non-orientable surfaces
Let \Sigma be a surface which is not necessarily orientable. Our results depend on theorems of Hass and Scott presented in Section 1 which apply only to orientable surfaces.
Conjecture 0.12 (non-orientable surfaces).
For a non-orientable surface \Sigma and s\in\mathbb{N} , what are the complexities of \mathsf{Pin}(\Sigma,s) and \mathsf{SimplePin}(\Sigma,s) ?
We conjecture that both are in P when s\leq 3 and NP-complete when s\geq 4 .
0.3.3 SAT attacks
The observations in Section 1.2 show that when \Sigma is orientable there is a polynomial time algorithm which converts a loop or simple multiloop to a positive boolean formula in conjunctive normal form (CNF) called its mobidisc formula \Phi(\gamma) (Definition 1.10), and that the pinning sets of \gamma correspond to the satisfying assignments of \Phi(\gamma) . The problem of computing all minimal pinning sets of a multiloop is thus reduced to the problem of dualizing \Phi(\gamma) by converting it from a CNF to a logically equivalent positive disjunctive normal form (DNF).
Thus, one may ask whether SAT-solvers suited for such positive CNFs may be used to compute the minimum cardinal of their solutions more efficiently than expected. In practice, the current SAT-solvers for these problems are surprisingly fast (see for instance [18, 20]).
Question 0.13 (SAT-solvers heuristics).
The positive CNFs arising from multiloops are not arbitrary as they come from loops in surfaces: this adds constraints given by (the genus of) the surface. Can one formulate a precise structural result in this direction?
Can one tune a SAT-solver to find heuristics exploiting that surface structure of these motonone CNFs to solve them faster?
0.3.4 Random models: average parameters and complexity
Question 0.14 (average complexity).
Given a random model of multiloops in surfaces, what is the “average” complexity of the pinning problems as measured by the aforementioned SAT solvers?
Let us sketch a few random models of simple multiloops in a fixed or variable surface.
The random state model.
Our first random model constructs, given a surface \Sigma and integers p,l\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1} , a random simple multiloop of length (in a sense that will be clear) bounded by l .
For simplicity assume that \Sigma is orientable and add p punctures to obtain a new surface \Sigma^{\prime} whose fundamental group is free (a sphere with \geq 3 punctures, a torus with \geq 1 puncture or any surface of genus \geq 2 ), and choose a negatively curved (say hyperbolic) metric on \Sigma^{\prime} .
Choose a free presentation of its fundamental group, and choose a random word of length l in the generators: there is a unique closed geodesic for the associated hyperbolic geodesic for any chosen hyperbolic metric on \Sigma^{\prime} .
Finally, label the intersections and resolve them according to the following procedure so as to obtain a union of simple loops: while some strands have some self-intersections, resolve their self-intersection with the smallest label in the unique way that increases the number of strands.
The random spin model.
Our second random model first constructs for given a size parameter n\in\mathbb{N} , a random surface \Sigma of size n\in\mathbb{N} : choose a random 4 -valent multigraph with n vertices and a random cyclic order on the vertices yielding a banded surface to which you may glue discs to obtain a closed surface.
Then construct a simple multiloop by choosing a random Euler multicycle of the embedded multigraph (for instance by taking a random walk on the edges and burning edges as they are used), and finally resolve self-intersections between strands as in the previous model.
The random braid model.
Our third model constructs in any surface \Sigma , for any parameter s\in\mathbb{N} , a random simple multiloop with s strands using the mapping class group action.
First choose a simple loop in \Sigma , then add several punctures in each region and apply a random element of the pure braid group of the punctured surface. Continue the process s times, starting with the initial simple loop with different punctures and applying a random pure braid. Finally erase all punctures.
0.3.5 Pinning games
One may devise and study various multiplayer games related to pinning multiloops. In what follows we define just one and reflect on the first combinatorial game-theoretic questions that come to mind.
Definition 0.15 (unpinning avoidance game).
To a multiloop \gamma\colon\sqcup_{1}^{s}S^{1}\looparrowright\Sigma we associate the following impartial combinatorial game for 2 players called the unpinning avoidance game.
The initial configuration has a pin in each region; two players take turns removing pins so as to leave a pinning set after their turn; the first player with no legal move loses. Note that the states of the game are the vertices of the pinning ideal \mathcal{PI}(\gamma) .
We denote by \mathsf{Unpin_{AVOID}}(\Sigma,s) the decision problem which has

A multiloop \gamma\colon\sqcup_{i=1}^{s}\mathbb{S}^{1}\looparrowright\Sigma .
Question:
Does Player 1 have a winning strategy in the unpinning avoidance game for \gamma ?
We denote the restriction of \mathsf{Unpin_{AVOID}}(\Sigma,s) to simple multiloops by \mathsf{SimpleUnpin_{AVOID}}(\Sigma,s) .
Question 0.16 (nimbers).
The Sprague–Grundy theorem [26, 13] applies to unpinning avoidance games. Can we construct infinite families of multiloops yielding increasingly hard unpinning avoidance games (for instance whose Grundy numbers grow fast with the number of double points)? What are the Grundy numbers associated to multiloops with s strands in genus g ? How does restricting to simple multiloops affect the answers to these questions?
Remark 0.17 (node kayles).
The unpinning avoidance game appears related (via the mobidisc formula) to a game that some authors call node kayles. Node kayles is played on a graph: Two players start with a vertex cover consisting of all vertices and take turns removing vertices from the cover so as to leave a vertex cover after each turn; the last player who can make a legal move wins. We denote by Node Kayles the decision problem concerned with deciding winning strategies in node kayles. It turns out that Node Kayles (along with other closely related games such as \mathsf{G_{AVOID}(POS\text{ }DNF)}) is PSPACE-complete [23].
Conjecture 0.18 (PSPACE-complete).
The problem \mathsf{Unpin_{AVOID}}(\Sigma,s) is PSPACE-complete for any s and \Sigma , and so is \mathsf{SimpleUnpin_{AVOID}}(\Sigma,s) for any s\geq 4 .
Remark 0.19 ( \mathsf{Unpin_{AVOID}}(\Sigma,s) versus \mathsf{Pin}(\Sigma,s) ).
It appears that Node Kayles is to Vertex Cover as \mathsf{Unpin_{AVOID}}(\Sigma,s) is to \mathsf{Pin}(\Sigma,s) . In particular, the complexity of \mathsf{Pin}(\Sigma,s) for a given class of multiloops has limited direct bearing on the complexity of \mathsf{Unpin_{AVOID}}(\Sigma,s) for that same class. One difficulty is that naïve reductions from Node Kayles would require limiting mobidisc gadgets to have 2 regions each. Another is that we do not know if Node Kayles remains PSPACE-complete in restriction to planar graphs and/or graphs with a max-degree bound. Additionally, Node Kayles in restriction to max-degree 3 trees (the complexity of which appears to be open, see for instance [2, 19]) seems likely to reduce to \mathsf{SimpleUnpin_{AVOID}}(\Sigma,3) , so determining the complexity of the latter is probably a difficult task for the “meta” reason that it would break new ground on a longstanding open question.
1 General results for simple multiloops
In this section, we first recall some terminology about Boolean formulae in conjunctive normal form that are positive.
This will serve to set up a correspondence between the pinning sets of loops or simple multiloops and the satisfying assignments of such a formula.
After reviewing the Vertex Cover problem and a few of its variants, we use these ideas to prove a preliminary result (Theorem 1.19) that pinning simple multiloops with arbitrary many strands is NP-complete.
1.1 Boolean formulae in conjunctive normal form
Definition 1.1 (formulae and satisfying assignments).
Consider a set of variables x=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\} which may take boolean values \{\true,\false\} .
A formula \phi is an expression in the variables x involving negations \neg , disjunctions \vee , and conjunctions \wedge (and parentheses).
Evaluating the variables x at \xi\in\{\true,\false\}^{r} yields a value \phi(\xi)\in\{\true,\false\} for the formula \phi .
Such an assignment \xi of the variables x is said to satisfy \phi when \phi(\xi)=\true .
Two formulae \phi,\phi^{\prime} on the set of variables x are called logically equivalent when their evaluations on every assignment \xi of the variables x are equal \phi(\xi)=\phi^{\prime}(\xi) .
It is a classical observation that every formula is equivalent to a conjunctive normal form, which we now define.
Definition 1.2 (positive conjunctive normal forms).
Consider a set of boolean variables x=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\} .
A clause is a formula of the form l_{1}\vee\dots\vee l_{k} where each l_{j} is a literal, namely a variable x_{t} or its negation \neg x_{i} .
A conjunctive normal form (abbreviated CNF) is a formula which is conjunction c_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge c_{m} of clauses c_{j} .
A conjunctive normal form is called positive (or monotone) when none of the clauses feature negated variables.
Definition 1.3 (solution ideal).
Consider a positive conjunctive normal form \phi over a set of boolean variables x=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\} .
For an assignment \xi of x satisfying \phi , we call \{x_{i}\colon\xi_{i}=\true\}\in\Parts(x) a solution of \phi .
The solutions of \phi form an ideal \mathcal{S}(\phi)\subset\Parts(x) : a sub-poset which is absorbing under union, and it contains the whole set of all elements \{x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\} .
Remark 1.4 (pruning).
By definition if two positive CNF are equivalent then their solution ideals are isomorphic.
Thus to compute the solution ideal, we may first “prune” it by retaining only those innermost clauses which are implied by no other clause.
Remark 1.5 (hypergraph vertex cover).
The solutions of a positive conjunctive normal form correspond to the vertex covers of the hypergraph whose vertices are indexed by the variables and hyperedges correspond to the clauses.
1.2 Pinning simple multiloops is NP
Throughout this subsection, we fix an oriented surface \Sigma .
Before dealing with simple multiloops, let first recall from [25, Section 2] how the problem of pinning loops reduces to a problem regarding satisfying assignments of its mobidisc formula, a positive CNF.
Hass and Scott [14] characterize taut loops in terms of the absence of singular monogons and bigons, which we define below for multiloops and subsequently use for simple multiloops.
Definition 1.6 (singular, embedded, and regional monorbigons).
Consider a multiloop \gamma\colon\sqcup_{i=1}^{s}\mathbb{S}^{1}\looparrowright\Sigma .
A strand of \gamma obtained by restriction to one of the circles will be denoted \gamma_{i}\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}_{i}\looparrowright\Sigma .
A singular monogon of the strand \gamma_{i} is a non-trivial closed interval I\subset\mathbb{S}^{1}_{i} such that \gamma(\partial I)=\{x\} is a double point of \gamma and \gamma(I) is null-homotopic.
This singular monogon is embedded when \gamma is injective on the interior of I , and regional when \gamma(I) bounds a region of \gamma .
A singular bigon between strands \gamma_{i},\gamma_{j} (which may coincide) is a disjoint union of non-trivial closed intervals I\sqcup J where I\subset\mathbb{S}^{1}_{i} and J\subset\mathbb{S}^{1}_{j} such that \gamma(\partial I)=\{x,y\}=\gamma(\partial J) for distinct double points x,y of \gamma and \gamma(I\sqcup J)\subset\Sigma is null-homotopic.
This singular bigon is embedded when \gamma is injective on the interior of I\sqcup J , and regional when \gamma(I\sqcup J) bounds a region of \gamma .
A singular monorbigon K refers to a singular monogon I or singular bigon I\sqcup J .
The restriction \gamma(K) is a subloop of \gamma and we call \gamma(\partial K) its marked points.

Definition 1.7 (immersed monorbigons and mobidiscs).
For a multiloop \gamma\colon\sqcup_{i=1}^{s}\mathbb{S}^{1}\looparrowright\Sigma , a singular monorbigon K of \gamma is called immersed when there exists an immersion \iota\colon\Disc\looparrowright\Sigma such that the restriction \gamma\colon K\looparrowright\Sigma factors through \iota\colon\partial\Disc\looparrowright\Sigma .
The image \iota(\Disc) of such an immersion covers a set of regions of \gamma , thus defining an element in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{R}(\gamma)) , called a mobidisc.
The set of all mobidiscs \MoB(\gamma)\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{R}) defines the hyperedges of a hypergraph with vertices \mathcal{R} .
An immersed monorbigon or its associated mobidisc is called innermost when the associated mobidisc contains no other mobidiscs of \gamma . In particular, a regional monorbigon gives rise to an innermost mobidisc with exactly one region.

Figure 3 illustrates monorbigons with increasing restrictions from (non-singular to) singular to immersed to embedded to regional. Most important to this work is the difference between embedded and regional bigons.

Theorem 1.9 (pinning loops with mobidiscs).
For a loop \gamma\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\looparrowright\Sigma , a subset of regions P\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{R}(\gamma)) is pinning \gamma if and only if every mobidisc D\in\MoB(\gamma) has D\cap P\neq\emptyset .
Definition 1.10 (mobidisc formula).
To a multiloop \gamma\colon\sqcup_{i=1}^{s}\mathbb{S}^{1}\looparrowright\Sigma we associate its mobidisc formula denoted by \Phi(\gamma) : it is the positive boolean formula in conjunctive normal form on the set of boolean variables indexed by \mathcal{R}(\gamma) whose clauses correspond to \MoB(\gamma)\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{R}) .
The following is a reformulation of Theorem 1.9.
Corollary 1.11 (pinning a loop = satisfying its mobidisc formula).
The pinning sets of a loop correspond to the solutions of its mobidisc formula.
In particular, the pinning ideal of a loop \gamma is isomorphic to the solution ideal of \Phi(\gamma) .
In this work we are interested in multiloops, however the following Remark 1.12 shows that Corollary 1.11 does not generalize so immediately.

1.3 Pinning simple multiloops is NP-complete
This subsection first defines the Vertex Cover problem and the variants that we will need for the purposes of this work.
Then a preliminary result is proved: the complexity of pinning simple multiloops with no restriction on the number of strands is NP-complete.
Definition 1.17 (Vertex Cover and variants).
The Vertex Cover (VC) problem has:
Instance:
A simple graph G=(V,E) and an integer k .
Question:
Is there U\subset V with \Card(U)\leq k such that for all \{v_{1},v_{2}\}\in E , at least one of v_{1} and v_{2} belongs to U ?
We also define the following variants of VC:
•
Planar Vertex Cover (PVC) is the restriction of VC to input graphs which are planar.
•
3-Connected Cubic Planar Vertex Cover (3C3PVC) is the restriction of PVC to 3 -connected cubic graphs. Here, 3 -connected means that the graph is connected upon removal of any set of at most 2 vertices, and cubic means that every vertex has degree 3 .
•
Bipartite Vertex Cover (BVC) is the restriction of VC to input graphs which are bipartite.
Lemma 1.18 (NP-complete Vertex Cover variants).
The problems VC and PVC are NP-complete.
Proof.
It is well-known (and straightforward) that VC is in NP, and thus so is the restriction PVC. It is also classical that VC is NP-hard [17], and that PVC is NP-hard [11][Theorem 2.7].
∎
We will show that BVC is in P in Lemma 2.1, and that 3C3PVC is NP-complete in Lemma 3.1.
Theorem 1.19 (pinning simple multiloops with no strand restriction is NP-hard).
For a fixed orientable surface \Sigma , the problem \mathsf{SimplePin}(\Sigma) is NP-hard.
Proof.
In light of Remark 0.10, it is enough to prove this when \Sigma=\mathbb{R}^{2} .
One may show this using a simplified adaption of the reduction from PVC described in [25, Section 2.2]. It also follows from the proof of Theorem 0.9 that we will give in Section 3.
Let us sketch yet another reduction from 3C3PVC (depending on Lemma 3.1) which we find to be elegant and which is similar to constructions in the next section.
Consider an instance (G,k) of 3C3PVC.
Note that since G is 3 -connected, it is bridgeless ( 2 -edge connected).
Embed G in \mathbb{R}^{2} in linear time with straight-line edges (using for instance [15]).
Since G is bridgeless, every edge bounds different faces on either side, hence the boundary of each face defines a cycle of edges and vertices in which each one appears exactly once. To each face of G associate a loop surrounding only its edges and vertices, so that their union is a simple multiloop whose regions correspond to the vertices and faces of G . The innermost bigons correspond to the edges of G .
This defines a polynomial time reduction from an instance of \mathsf{3C3PVC} to \mathsf{SimplePin}(\Sigma) . See Figure 5 for an example.

We must now show that \gamma can be pinned with at most k pins if an only G has a vertex cover of size at most k .
This will follow from Corollary 1.14, after making some observations about the mobidisc formula and its optimal satisfying assigements.
Every innermost bigon of \gamma consist of exactly three regions (two triangular regions corresponding to vertices of G and one quadrangular regions corresponding to the edge between them), hence the mobidiscs of \gamma are in one-to-one correspondence with the edges of G . Moreover, any two mobidiscs of \gamma intersect in at most one triangular region corresponding to the vertex of G in that region. Hence the problem of choosing which of the two vertices adjacent to an edge of G belongs to a vertex cover is equivalent to the problem of choosing which of the two triangular regions of the associated mobidisc of \gamma receives a pin (note that optimal pinning sets avoid the quadrangular regions of each mobidisc).
∎
The proof of Theorem 1.19 reveals an interesting connection to the 4 -color theorem [1], to which we will refer again in Section 2.3 in relation to Conjecture 0.11.
Remark 1.20 ( 4 -color theorem).
The construction in the proof of Theorem 1.19 gives a way to 4 -color the strands of a \gamma in such a way that no two strands of the same color intersect each other. Indeed, after 4 -coloring the faces of G we may color each strand of \gamma according to the color of the face it surrounds. In fact this may be done in quadratic time [22].

2.3 Pinning 4 -strand simple multiloops
It is natural to ask whether these ideas can be extended to simple multiloops with 4 strands. We restate Conjecture 0.11.
Conjecture 2.3.
For every surface \Sigma , the problem \mathsf{SimplePin}(\Sigma,4) is NP-complete.
One may hope to prove this conjecture by reduction from 3C3PVC as hinted in Theorem 1.19 and Remark 1.20. Given a bridgeless cubic planar graph G and a valid 4 -coloring of the dual graph of G , one may obtain a simple multiloop \gamma whose strands are 4 -colored according to the faces of G they surround (in quadratic time). One could then imagine performing surgeries to connect strands of \gamma of the same color, without damaging the mobidisc structure of \gamma too much.
For some additional rationale for this conjecture, note that the combinatorial complexity of mobidiscs for multiloops with 4 strands is evidently more complex than the 3 -strand case; in particular there is no clear reduction to Vertex Cover. The clause structure of a mobidisc CNF of a 3 -strand simple multiloop is predictable and controlled: after ignoring rectangular regions, the disjunctions may be thought of as having at most 2 variables each, and the fact that the associated graph is bipartite is equivalent to all cycles having even length. In an earlier attempt at proving Theorem 0.8, we also noticed that the graph G produced by the reduction has \dim(H_{1}(G;\mathbb{Z}))\leq 5 , and we believe that Vertex Cover for graphs of bounded homological rank is in P.
Thus, while we believe that the conjecture is true, one may hope to disprove it by bounding the homological rank or finding certain parity structures on appropriate “hypergraph complexes” associated to 4 -strand simple multiloops.
3 Simple multiloops with many strands
In this section, we demonstrate the hardness of pinning simple multiloops with a fixed sufficiently large number of strands, namely we show that for s\geq 20 the \mathsf{SimplePin}(\Sigma,s) problem is NP-hard.
Thus (under the assumption that P \neq NP) there is no polynomial-time algorithm to compute minimal pinning sets for simple multiloops with many strands.
We prove NP-hardness by reduction from an appropriate variant of the Vertex Cover problem, namely 3-Connected Cubic Planar Vertex Cover (3C3PVC in Definition 1.17).
3.1 The 3-Connected Cubic Planar Vertex Cover problem
We now establish that 3C3PVC is NP-hard. This was essentially shown in work of Uehara [27], but we add some details for completeness.
Lemma 3.1 (hardness of 3C3PVC).
The problem 3C3PVC is NP-complete.
Proof.
Note that the problem is in NP as a special case of Vertex Cover.
We show that is NP-hard by reduction from Max-degree 3 Planar Vertex Cover (the restriction of Vertex Cover to planar graphs of max-degree 3 ), which problem is shown to be NP-hard in [12, Lemma 1].
Let (G,k) be an instance of Max-degree 3 Planar Vertex Cover (so G is a planar graph of max-degree 3 and k\in\mathbb{Z} ).
Passing to a planar graph of max-degree 3 and min-degree 2 .
We first construct a graph G^{\prime} from G which is planar of max-degree 3 and min-degree 2 . We do this by iteratively deleting a subgraph of G induced by an edge (v,w) where v has degree 1 . The reader may check that each time this procedure is performed, the size of the vertex cover must be decreased by 1 . In other words, if this procedure is performed n times before it terminates in a planar graph G^{\prime} with max-degree 3 and min-degree 2 , then G has a vertex cover of size at most k if and only if G^{\prime} has a vertex cover of size at most k^{\prime}=k-n .
Passing to a connected planar graph of max-degree 3 and min-degree 2 .
Suppose now that G^{\prime} has n components. We next construct a connected graph G^{\prime\prime} which has a vertex cover of size at most k^{\prime\prime}=k^{\prime}+3n if and only if G^{\prime} has a vertex cover of size at most k^{\prime} .
We may assume that every connected component of G^{\prime} has at least one edge (since an isolated vertex of G^{\prime} never belongs to a minimal vertex cover, so discarding them yields a graph with the same minimal vertex covers as G^{\prime} ), and we may also assume n>0 .
We construct G^{\prime\prime} from G^{\prime} as follows (see Figure 9).
For each connected component G_{i} of G^{\prime} , choose an edge e_{i} and let v_{i},w_{i} be its endpoints. Subdivide e_{i} by adding two vertices x_{i},y_{i} , and add another vertex z_{i} connected to x_{i} and y_{i} to form a triangle. Let C_{2n} be a cycle of length 2n with vertices labeled b_{1},c_{1},\ldots b_{n},c_{n} in this cyclic order.
Add edges connecting each z_{i} to b_{i} . Finally, subdivide each edge connecting z_{i} to b_{i} by adding a vertex a_{i} .
This achieves the construction of G^{\prime\prime} . The reader may check that it is planar and connected with max-degree 3 and min-degree 2 , and it contains all vertices of G^{\prime} .

3.2 Planar graph embeddings with few slopes
To prove Theorem 0.9, we will reduce from 3C3PVC.
The first step of the reduction is critically important to our construction and relies on the following lemma.
Lemma 3.2 (embedding 3 -connected cubic planar graphs with exactly 6 slopes).
Every 3 -connected cubic planar graph G has a straight-line planar embedding, computable in linear time, in which the union of edge slopes is a 6 -element set \{\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{4},\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3}\} for some \phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3}\in[0,\pi) , and each of \phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3} is realized exactly once by a single edge on the outer face of G . In particular one can specify the outer face in advance.
Proof.
This lemma is proved in [8] without an explicit complexity analysis. The first step is to compute a so-called canonical decomposition of G based on an initial planar embedding, which may be done in linear time [16][Theorem 3.2] (an initial planar embedding may be computed in linear time by [15]). A careful reading of the recursive construction in the proof of

In the very last step, it is sometimes possible that we may be able finish the drawing so that fewer than 6 slopes are used, but vertically perturbing the final vertex allows us to realize exactly 6 slopes. This last step also shows that the 3 edges with slopes \phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3} occur in succession on the outside face.

Definition 3.4 (edge gadget bundle).
The edge gadget bundle with parameter \epsilon>0 is a planar configuration associated to a set of n\geq 1 collinear edges in \mathbb{B}(0,\frac{1}{2}) having slope m . It consists of 2n piecewise linear paths with labels among \alpha_{m}^{+},\alpha_{m}^{-},\beta_{m}^{+},\beta_{m}^{-} (respectively colored orange, purple, green, blue), the unit circle \mu , and another strand \nu which is within a closed \delta=\epsilon\tan\epsilon -neighborhood of \mu . Construct edge gadgets with parameter \epsilon around each edge, alternating between types \alpha and \beta , starting with an \alpha edge gadget at the bottom (see Figure 14 at left). 1.

2.
paths of \alpha edges join monotonously the top-left to the bottom-right,
paths of \beta edges join monotonously the top-right to the bottom-left, and
3.
all vertical \beta segments are closer than the \alpha segments to L .

Repeat this construction for downward-pointing vertical paths of edge gadgets after rotating the picture by \pi radians.

Note that the union of all of these bigons approaches L as \epsilon\to 0 and that there will be no paths with \beta labels if n=1 .

3.3.3 Connecting paths of edge gadget bundles with a given slope to form 4 strands
In this subsection, we explain how to connect the paths with labels \alpha_{m}^{+},\alpha_{m}^{-},\beta_{m}^{+},\beta_{m}^{-} from all edge gadget bundles with a given slope m\in[0,\pi) to form a simple multiloop with 4 strands.
The connections will be made outside of the unit circle, around which there will be 2 additional strands (which will be the same for all slopes).
The description of the construction will span several pages. Schematic examples of the end results are shown in Figures 20, 21, 22, 23.
The resulting construction (a multiloop \gamma_{m} with 6 strands) will be referred to as the bundle plumbing or slope m plumbing sub-multiloop.
First, we orient all edges in the same direction and rotate the plane so that m is vertical and the edges point upward.
The n edges are partitioned into a set of collinearity classes which define parallel lines. For each collinearity class, construct an edge gadget bundle as in Definition 3.4 with parameter \epsilon . Assume that \epsilon is small enough so that the non-vertical paths with labels among \{\alpha_{m}^{+},\alpha_{m}^{-},\beta_{m}^{+},\beta_{m}^{-}\} (respectively colored orange, purple, green, and blue) associated to each edge gadget bundle are contiguous. Suppose that there are n_{\alpha} edges with \alpha labels and n_{\beta} edges with \beta labels so that n=n_{\alpha}+n_{\beta} . Note that n_{\alpha}\geq 1 and n_{\beta}\geq 0 .
Parity balancing procedure.
We must ensure that the parity of each label among \{\alpha_{m}^{+},\alpha_{m}^{-},\beta_{m}^{+},\beta_{m}^{-}\} is even and each label occurs at least once by adding either 2 or 4 or 6 more vertical segments which extend to a distance of \frac{1}{2} above and below the unit circle \mu at incremental distances \delta=\epsilon\tan\epsilon to the left of the leftmost edge gadget bundle, and give them labels from among \{\alpha_{m}^{+},\alpha_{m}^{-},\beta_{m}^{+},\beta_{m}^{-}\} (still colored orange, purple, green, blue) from left to right, according to the following rules.
1.
If n_{\alpha} is odd and n_{\beta} is even, add six new line segments with labels/colors

3.

Upper and lower terminal pairings.
The endpoints of paths which terminate at distance \frac{1}{2} outside of \mu have labels among \alpha_{m}^{+},\alpha_{m}^{-},\beta_{m}^{+},\beta_{m}^{-} which we call terminals, and by reading them from left to right above and below \mu we obtain two terminal sequences: the upper terminal sequence above \mu and the lower terminal sequence below \mu . Moreover, each sequence begins with (\beta_{m}^{-},\beta_{m}^{+},\ldots) (including the case where n_{\beta}=0 ).
In every terminal sequence, for each color \sigma_{m}^{\star}\in\{\alpha_{m}^{+},\alpha_{m}^{-},\beta_{m}^{+},\beta_{m}^{-}\} , the parity balancing procedure ensures that there is a positive even number of occurrences of \sigma_{m}^{\star} in the subsequence. Enumerate the positions of the subsequence \rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{2n(\sigma_{m}^{\star})} from left to right, where 2n(\sigma_{m}^{\star}) is the number of terminals with that label.
Pair the terms in the subsequence as follows, for each i\bmod{2n(\sigma_{m}^{\star})} :
•
In each upper terminal sequence, pair \rho_{2i} with \rho_{2i+1} .
•
In each lower terminal sequence, pair \rho_{2i-1} with \rho_{2i} .
In particular \rho_{1} is paired with \rho_{2n(\sigma_{m}^{\star})} .
Note that connecting all terminals according to their pairings yields a closed loop and therefore a strand associated to \sigma_{m}^{\star}\in\{\alpha_{m}^{+},\alpha_{m}^{-},\beta_{m}^{+},\beta_{m}^{-}\} . The plumbing we will construct will realize this pairing in the plane while avoiding self intersections within each strand and accidental bigons between distinct strands.
Blocks and pairing types.
We partition each terminal subsequence into blocks: there is a block for each maximal subsequence of consecutive labels within the same edge gadget, and there is one additional block for all those coming from the parity balancing procedure. With this terminology in place, the terminal pairings may be one of the following pairing types.
•
The pairing between \rho_{1} and \rho_{2n(\sigma_{m}^{\star})} is called a cnidarian head pairing.
•
We refer to a terminal pairing as an inter-bundle pairing when the terminals are not \{\rho_{1},\rho_{2n(\sigma_{m}^{\star})}\} and come from different blocks in the terminal subsequence.
•
We refer to a terminal pairing as an intra-bundle pairing when the terminals come from the same block in the terminal subsequence.

3.3.4 Innermost bigon structure of bundle plumbings
The following lemma will be used to show that in a slope m plumbing sub-multiloop \gamma_{m} , there is no innermost embedded bigon of \gamma_{m} both of whose marked points lie along a connecting arc of the plumbing.
Lemma 3.5 (connecting arcs are staggered).
Let m\in[0,\pi) and \lambda_{m} and \lambda^{\prime}_{m} be connecting arcs in a slope m plumbing sub-multiloop \gamma_{m} that are both in the upper levels or both in the lower levels. Let r and r^{\prime} be their associated radii, respectively. Let a,b be the left and right x -coordinates of the connection points of \lambda_{m} (relative to the vertical orientation of slope m ), and let a^{\prime} and b^{\prime} be the left and right x -coordinates of the connection points of \lambda^{\prime}_{m} . If r>r^{\prime} , then a<a^{\prime} or b>b^{\prime} .
Proof.
Suppose r>r^{\prime} and consider all possible combinations for the levels of \lambda_{m} and \lambda^{\prime}_{m} . We will consider the case where \lambda_{m} and \lambda^{\prime}_{m} are in the upper levels \{U1,U2,U3\} . The following arguments are immediately adapted for the case where \lambda_{m} and \lambda^{\prime}_{m} are in the lower levels \{L1,L2\} .
1.
U3 vs \{U1,U2,U3\} .
•
Suppose \lambda_{m}\in U3 and \lambda^{\prime}_{m}\in U1 or \lambda_{m}\in U3 and \lambda^{\prime}_{m}\in U2 . Here a<a^{\prime} since a is in the parity balancing block and a^{\prime} is not.
•
Suppose \lambda_{m},\lambda^{\prime}_{m}\in U3 . If both \lambda_{m} and \lambda^{\prime}_{m} belong to \alpha strands, then \lambda_{m} belongs to a minus strand and \lambda^{\prime}_{m} belongs to a plus strand, so a<a^{\prime} since a+\delta=a^{\prime} . The same argument applies if both \lambda_{m} and \lambda^{\prime}_{m} are \beta strands. On the other hand if \lambda_{m} is a \beta strand and \lambda^{\prime}_{m} is an \alpha strand, then a<a^{\prime} since a+\delta\leq a^{\prime} .
2.
U2 vs \{U1,U2\} .
•
Suppose \lambda_{m}\in U2 and \lambda^{\prime}_{m}\in U1 .
In this case, \lambda_{m} comes from an intra-bundle pairing and \lambda^{\prime}_{m} comes from an inter-bundle pairing. Thus b^{\prime}-a^{\prime}=2\delta and b-a>2\delta , so a<a^{\prime} or b>b^{\prime} .
•
Suppose \lambda_{m},\lambda^{\prime}_{m}\in U2 . In this case, both \lambda_{m} and \lambda^{\prime}_{m} come from an inter-bundle pairings. If both \lambda_{m} and \lambda^{\prime}_{m} belong to \alpha strands, then \lambda_{m} belongs to a minus strand and \lambda^{\prime}_{m} belongs to a plus strand. Then either \lambda_{m} and \lambda^{\prime}_{m} do not connect the same two blocks (in which case [a,b]\cap[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]=\emptyset ), or they do connect the same two blocks, and then a<a^{\prime} since a+\delta=a^{\prime} . The same argument applies if both \lambda_{m} and \lambda^{\prime}_{m} belong to \beta strands. On the other hand if \lambda_{m} belongs to a \beta strand and \lambda^{\prime}_{m} belongs to an \alpha strand, we argue as follows. Since the edge gadget bundle associate to b has at least one \alpha edge, there is an upper terminal in that same bundle with an \alpha label whose x -coordinate lies in [a,b] . This terminal cannot be skipped by \lambda^{\prime}_{m} . Thus either [a,b]\cap[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]=\emptyset or one of a^{\prime},b^{\prime} lies in [a,b] .
3.
U1 vs U1 .
•
Suppose \lambda_{m},\lambda^{\prime}_{m}\in U1 . In this case, both \lambda_{m} and \lambda^{\prime}_{m} come from intra-bundle pairings. If both \lambda_{m} and \lambda^{\prime}_{m} belong to \alpha strands, then \lambda_{m} belongs to a minus strand and \lambda^{\prime}_{m} belongs to a plus strand, so either [a,b]\cap[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]=\emptyset or a+\delta=a^{\prime} . The same argument applies if both \lambda_{m} and \lambda^{\prime}_{m} belong to \beta strands. On the other hand, if one of \lambda_{m} and \lambda^{\prime}_{m} belongs to an \alpha strand and the other belongs to a \beta strand, then [a,b]\cap[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]=\emptyset .
This completes the proof of the lemma. ∎
Lemma 3.6 (no accidental innermost bigons within a bundle plumbing).
Fix a slope m\in[0,\pi) and consider a slope m plumbing sub-multiloop \gamma_{m} with 6 strands as defined in Section 3.3.3. For \epsilon>0 sufficiently small (which we can compute in polynomial time), the only innermost embedded monorbigons of \gamma_{m} are bigons M_{e} around edges e (as in Figure 13) and bigons between \mu and \nu (as in Figure 14).
Proof.
Let B be an innermost bigon of \gamma_{m} with marked points x,y bounded by paths \lambda,\lambda^{\prime} .
Suppose first that \lambda or \lambda^{\prime} is a subarc of \mu or \nu . In this case both x and y lie along \mu or \nu , and B contains a tooth bigon or subdivided tooth bigon. Thus we may assume that \lambda and \lambda^{\prime} have labels among \{\alpha_{m}^{+},\alpha_{m}^{-},\beta_{m}^{+},\beta_{m}^{-}\} .
Suppose now that \lambda or \lambda^{\prime} intersects \mu or \nu . Then it intersects \mu=\partial\Ball(0,1) and there are teeth bigons to the left and right of this intersection points along \mu , one of which must be contained in B . Thus we may assume that B lies entirely inside \Ball(0,1) , or outside it.
If B lies entirely inside \Ball(0,1) , then a choice of \epsilon sufficiently small ensures that all strands of \{\alpha_{m}^{+},\alpha_{m}^{-},\beta_{m}^{+},\beta_{m}^{-}\} are arbitrarily close to parallel to the slope m . This implies that each arc of \alpha_{m}^{\pm}\cap\Ball(0,1) intersects each arc of \beta_{m}^{\pm}\cap\Ball(0,1) at most once as in Figure 14 at left. Thus B must be an M_{e} bigon.
Thus we may assume that B lies entirely outside of \Ball(0,1) , and there are two cases according to whether \lambda and \lambda^{\prime} have subarcs among the upper levels \{U_{1},U_{2},U_{3}\} or lower levels \{L_{1},L_{2}\} (having subarcs among both is impossible since neither \lambda nor \lambda^{\prime} intersect \mu ).
Our construction implies that \lambda and \lambda^{\prime} have at most two corners. Specifically, each has one of the following types:
•
Type 0 (no corners): It is a subarc of a connecting arc,
•
Type 1 (one corner): It consists of a one vertical arc and one subarc of a connecting arc (whose marked point on the vertical arc lies at a smaller radius than that on the connecting arc),
•
Type 2 (two corners): It consists of two vertical arcs with an entire connecting arc in between them, with corners at the connection points.
Note first that Type 1 is in fact impossible, because if without loss of generality \lambda is Type 1, there is no available type for \lambda^{\prime} . It is also impossible for both \lambda,\lambda^{\prime} to be of Type 0, and for both of \lambda,\lambda^{\prime} to be of Type 2. In fact the only way for \lambda and \lambda^{\prime} to form a bigon is for \lambda to be of Type 2 and \lambda^{\prime} to be of Type 0 . But then the connecting arc associated to \lambda would be at a larger radius than that of \lambda^{\prime} , contradicting Lemma 3.5. ∎
3.3.5 Proof of Theorem 0.9
We are ready to prove the main theorem by reducing from 3C3PVC to \mathsf{SimplePin}(\Sigma,s) using this construction. We restate Theorem 0.9.
Theorem 3.7.
For any fixed orientable surface \Sigma and s\geq 20 , \mathsf{SimplePin}(\Sigma,s) is NP-complete.
Proof.
First note that that \mathsf{SimplePin}(\Sigma,s) is in NP. Indeed, one may consult [25] or appeal to Corollary 1.16.
To demonstrate that the problem is NP-hard, let (G=(V,E),k) be an instance of 3C3PVC, where k\in\mathbb{Z} . We will construct an instance (\gamma,k^{\prime}) of \mathsf{SimplePin}(\Sigma,s) , where k^{\prime}=8\lvert E\rvert+f+k for some 24\leq f\leq 72 which depends on an embedding of G in a way that will be described below. We will do this in polynomial time, and show that they are equivalent problems. An application of Theorem 3.1 will finish the proof.
We may assume that k>0 and that \Sigma=\mathbb{R}^{2} by Remark 0.10.
Construct an embedding with six slopes.
From Lemma 3.2, we construct a straight-line embedding of G in \mathbb{R}^{2} in linear time in which the union of edge slopes is a 6 -element set \{\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{4},\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3}\} for some \phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3}\in[0,\pi) , and each of \phi_{1} , \phi_{2} and \phi_{3} is realized exactly once by a single edge on the outer face of G .
For each edge e\in G , let L_{e} be the line containing e . Scale the embedding so that G\subset\mathbb{B}(0,\frac{1}{2}) and additionally that all intersection points between distinct lines L_{e} and L_{e^{\prime}} are contained inside \mathbb{B}(0,\frac{1}{2}) .
Construct the multiloop via bundle plumbings.
Next, we apply the construction in Section 3.3.3 once for each slope m\in\{\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{4},\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3}\} to build a simple multiloop \gamma with 4*6+2=26 strands, choosing the same \epsilon>0 for all of them. We choose \epsilon small enough so that all intersection points between distinct plumbing sub-multiloops \gamma_{m} and \gamma_{m^{\prime}} lie inside \Ball(0,\frac{1}{2}) . Additionally, we claim that we can compute \epsilon in polynomial time small enough so that the following properties hold. Proofs will follow.
1.
Deformation retraction condition. Let M_{e} denote the bigon around edge e . For all \{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset E ,
\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}M_{e_{i}}\quad\text{retracts by deformation onto}\quad\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}.
This property ensures that the topology of gadget bigons mimics the topology of G .
2.
Angle condition. Let \sigma_{m},\sigma_{m^{\prime}}\in\{\alpha_{m}^{+},\alpha_{m}^{-},\beta_{m}^{+},\beta_{m}^{-}\} be any two strands associated to distinct slopes m\neq m^{\prime} . Let \lambda_{m} and \lambda_{m^{\prime}} be any two maximal paths in \sigma_{m}\cap\mathbb{B}(0,\frac{1}{2}) and \sigma_{m^{\prime}}\cap\mathbb{B}(0,\frac{1}{2}) , respectively. Then \lambda_{m}\cap\lambda_{m^{\prime}} is a single point.
This property ensures that all intersections of \gamma are transverse multiple points, and also helps control the mobidisc structure.
Proofs of claims.
1.
Proof of deformation retraction condition. To achieve this, we must control both vertical and horizontal dimensions of gadget bigons.
•
Vertical separation. For an edge e of G , let \ell(e) denote the length of the edge. We choose \epsilon<\frac{1}{5}\min_{e\in E}\ell(e) .
•
Horizontal separation. Consider the set of edges with slope m . These edges are partitioned into a set \mathcal{C}_{m} of collinearity classes which define parallel lines. For distinct collinearity classes C,C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{m} , let \#C denote the number of edges in the collinearity class and let d(C,C^{\prime}) denote the distance between these lines. Since the angles in Figure 13 tend to 0 with \epsilon , we may choose \epsilon small enough so that denoting d_{m}=\min\{d(C,C^{\prime})\colon C,C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{m},C\neq C^{\prime}\} and c_{m}=5\times\max\{\#C\colon C\in\mathcal{C}_{m}\} we have
\epsilon\tan\epsilon<\min\left\{d_{m}/c_{m}\colon m\in\{\pi/4,\pi/2,3\pi/4,\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3}\}\right\}.
2.
Proof of angle condition.
The paths \lambda_{m} and \lambda_{m^{\prime}} intersect at least once since their endpoints are linked on \mathbb{B}(0,\frac{1}{2}) . Since the angles in Figure 13 tend to 0 with \epsilon , we can make all segments of \lambda_{m} and \lambda_{m^{\prime}} arbitrarily close to slopes m and m^{\prime} , respectively, so that \lambda_{m} and \lambda_{m^{\prime}} intersect transversely exactly once (for instance, by making \max_{e\in E}\max_{i\in\{1,2,3,4\}}\theta_{i}<\frac{1}{3}\min\{\lvert m-m^{\prime}\rvert\colon m\neq m^{\prime}\in\{\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{4},\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3}\}\} ).
Resolve multiple points.
Now we deal with isolated multiple points. We compute all intersection points of the multiloop in polynomial time, as well as the minimum distance \epsilon^{\prime} between them, and arbitrarily resolve multiple points locally (inside balls of radius \epsilon^{\prime}/2 , say) into sets of transverse double points.
Prune extra strands.
We now have a multiloop with exactly 26 strands. To reach s\geq 20 strands, we proceed as follows. Since the three slopes \phi_{1} , \phi_{2} , and \phi_{3} correspond to a unique edge on the outer face of G , we have n_{\alpha}=1 and n_{\beta}=0 for these slopes. In particular there are guaranteed to be no \beta bigons for these slopes. We now remove the \beta strands corresponding to these edges and the tooth bigons between \mu and \nu associated to them. This yields a multiloops with exactly 20 strands.
Now we add s-20 pairwise disjoint closed loops away from \gamma and redefine \gamma to be the resulting multiloop.
Compute the number of forced pins.
Recall that every edge of G gives rise to 8 bigons between \mu and \nu , so that we will need to add 8\lvert E\rvert to our potential pinning set of \gamma .
We now define the number f , which corresponds to the number of forced pins induced by the parity balancing procedure. Recall that for each slope m\in\{\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{4}\} , there are 4 ways that the parity balancing procedure may have been performed for the slope m plumbing subloop. We count the number of extra bigons between \mu and \nu in each case, which is 4 times the number of extra vertical arcs needed on the leftmost side of the plumbing construction.
•
If n_{\alpha} is odd and n_{\beta} is even, there are 6 extra vertical arcs yielding f_{m}=24 extra bigons between \mu and \nu (see Figure 15).
•
If n_{\alpha} is odd and n_{\beta} is odd, there are 4 extra vertical arcs yielding f_{m}=16 extra bigons between \mu and \nu (see Figure 16).
•
If n_{\alpha} is even and n_{\beta} is odd, there are 2 extra vertical arcs yielding f_{m}=8 extra bigons between \mu and \nu (see Figure 17).
•
If n_{\alpha} is even and n_{\beta} is even, there are 4 extra vertical arcs yielding f_{m}=16 extra bigons between \mu and \nu (see Figure 18).
Now let f=f_{\frac{\pi}{4}}+f_{\frac{\pi}{2}}+f_{\frac{3\pi}{4}} . Note that f\equiv 0\mod 8 and 24\leq f\leq 72 .
Define the problem instance and bound its size in memory.
Finally, let k^{\prime}=8\lvert E\rvert+f+k . This completes the polynomial-time construction of the \mathsf{SimplePin}(\mathbb{R}^{2},s) problem instance, (\gamma,k^{\prime}) .
Note that the size of \gamma in memory is quadratic in the size of G . Indeed, \gamma may be viewed as a union of 2\lvert E\rvert+\frac{f}{4} paths almost parallel to edges of G , plus \lvert E\rvert+\frac{f}{8} connecting arcs. They all intersect each other pairwise no more than 2 times each, and each of the 2\lvert E\rvert+\frac{f}{4} paths almost parallel to edges of G gives rise to 4 double points involving \mu and \nu . Thus a crude bound is
\#{\gamma}\leq 4(2\lvert E\rvert+f/4)+2\binom{(2\lvert E\rvert+f/4)+(\lvert E\rvert+f/8)}{2}.
Argue that the problems are equivalent.
It remains to argue that the graph G has a vertex cover of size at most k if and only if the simple multiloop \gamma has a pinning set of size at most k^{\prime} .
From pinning set to vertex cover.
Suppose \gamma has a pinning set of size at most k^{\prime} , and choose one such. Note that each of the 4\lvert E\rvert+\frac{f}{2} regional teeth bigons must have a pin, and another 4\lvert E\rvert+\frac{f}{2} pins are used for subdivided teeth. This accounts for at least 8\lvert E\rvert+f pins. We construct a map from the remaining \leq k pins to vertices of G as follows: If the pin is in no M_{e} , then choose any vertex; if the pin is only in one M_{e} , then choose either vertex associated to that bigon; if it is in more than one M_{e} , these intersect in a nonempty region containing a unique vertex of G by the deformation retraction condition (1), which is the chosen vertex. Since every M_{e} contains at least one pin by Corollary 1.14, this yields a vertex cover of the graph using at most k vertices.
From vertex cover to pinning set.
Suppose the graph G has a vertex cover of size at most k , and choose one such. We claim that every innermost bigon of \gamma is either one of the bigons between \mu and \nu , or one of the M_{e} bigons. Indeed, Lemma 3.6 implies that the only innermost bigons within each plumbing subloop are of this form, and since the slopes of strands of each plumbing subloop \gamma_{m} are very close to m within \Ball(0,\frac{1}{2}) , the angle condition (2) and abundance of regional teeth bigons imply that there are no innermost bigons using two strands from two different plumbing subloops. Now pin \gamma as follows. Each of the 8\lvert E\rvert+f bigons between \mu and \nu receives a pin (choosing arbitrarily for subdivided teeth). For each vertex v of the cover, we place a pin in the region M_{e_{1}}\cap M_{e_{2}}\cap M_{e_{3}} where e_{1} , e_{2} and e_{3} are the 3 edges connected to v . This region is nonempty and simply connected by the deformation retraction condition (1). Since we started with a vertex cover, all M_{e} bigons are pinned with at most k pins, so that \gamma is pinned with at most k^{\prime} pins by Corollary 1.14. ∎
3.3.6 Concluding remarks
Recall the question raised by Theorems 0.8 and 0.9: For a fixed surface \Sigma , what is the hardness threshold s_{\textsf{mP}}=\max\{s\in\mathbb{N}\colon\mathsf{SimplePin}(\Sigma,s)\in\mathsf{P}\} ? Our theorems show (assuming P \neq NP) that 3\leq s_{\textsf{mP}}\leq 19 and we conjecture that s_{\textsf{mP}}=3 in Section 2.3.
We conclude with a comment on the possibility of lowering the upper bound on s_{\textsf{mP}} by lowering the value of s appearing in the statement of Theorem 0.9.
Remark 3.8 (improving the upper bound on the number of strands).
As mentioned in the introduction, the proof of Theorem 0.9 uses 20 strands for the following reason. For a 6 slope drawing of a 3 -connected cubic planar graph G , it uses 4 strands for each slope in \{\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{4}\} , 2 strands for each of the slopes among \{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{e}\} occurring exactly once on the outside face, and 2 additional strands around the unit circle to anchor the construction. It is probably possible to use similar ideas as those we presented (at the cost of a more delicate construction or proof) to reduce the number of strands as low as s\geq 12 (using only the 12 strands corresponding to the slopes \{\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{4}\} ), thereby showing s_{\textsf{mP}}\leq 11 . This is probably as far as one could hope to push the bound without significantly modifying our approach. Note that Uehara’s construction in the proof of Lemma 3.1 (originally given in [27]) may be used to produce a 3 -connected cubic planar graph which also has girth at least 4 (namely is triangle-free); this additional restriction may be helpful for such endeavors.
