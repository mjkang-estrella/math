Abstract.
A graph is locally chordal if each of its small-radius balls is chordal. In an earlier work [1], the authors and Kobler proved that locally chordal graphs can be characterized by having chordal local covers, by forbidding short cycles and wheels as induced subgraphs, and by the property that each of their minimal local separators is a clique. In this paper, we address the global structure of locally chordal graphs. The global structure of chordal graphs is given by the following characterizations: a graph is chordal if and only if it is the intersection graph of subtrees of a tree, if and only if it admits a tree-decomposition into cliques. We prove a local analog of this characterization, which essentially says that a graph is locally chordal if and only if it is the intersection graph of special subtrees of a high-girth graph, if and only if it admits a special graph-decomposition over a high-girth graph into cliques. We also prove that these global representations of locally chordal graphs can be efficiently computed.
This paper has two major contributions. The first is to exhibit for locally chordal graphs an ideal “local to global” analysis: given a graph class defined by restricted local structure, we fully describe the global structure of graphs in the class. The second is to develop the theory of graph-decompositions. Much of the work in this paper is devoted to properties of graph-decompositions that represent the global structure of graphs. This theory will be useful to find global decompositions for graph classes beyond locally chordal graphs.
Key words and phrases: Locally chordal, chordal, graph-decomposition, local separator, clique, local covering
1. Introduction
Consider a graph G which is obtained by gluing cliques in the shape of a high-girth graph H in such a way that, locally at each vertex, the graph G looks as if it were composed of cliques glued together along a tree.
An illustration is given in Figure 1.
Graphs that are composed of cliques glued together along a tree are the well-known chordal11
1
Chordal graphs are also referred to as rigid circuit [9] or triangulated [27] graphs. graphs.
The global composition of the graph G along H described above ensures that G exhibits such a tree-shape locally: that G is locally chordal.
Figure 1. A locally chordal graph G , depicted in black, obtained by gluing cliques in the shape of H , depicted in blue.
One way to phrase the above observation is that a global decomposition of G into cliques along a high-girth graph witnesses that G is locally chordal, so knowledge of the global structure allows us to deduce the local structure. One of the great hopes of graph theory research is to be able to do the converse: given knowledge of local structure, deduce (something about) the global structure. We aim for something even stronger: to characterize locally chordal graphs by their global structure. Specifically, we want to show that every locally chordal graph G admits a global decomposition which certifies that G is locally, around each vertex, chordal.
By assumption, a locally chordal graph G looks in the small-radius ball around each vertex like it is composed of cliques glued together along a tree. However, it is not at all obvious that these trees at each vertex can be combined into a single graph H that simultaneously witnesses the local tree-likeness everywhere. Surprisingly, we prove that this is indeed the case: every locally chordal graph G admits a global decomposition that witnesses that G is locally chordal.
To state our results more precisely, we give some definitions. A graph is chordal if each of its cycles of length at least four has a chord.
For a vertex v of a graph G , we define the ball of radius r/2 around v , denoted B_{G}(v,r/2) or, for short, B_{r/2}(v) , as the subgraph of G given by the set of all vertices of distance at most r/2 from v and the set of all edges of G for which the sum of the endpoints’ distances to v is strictly less than r .
For an integer r\geqslant 0 , the given degree of locality, a graph is
r -locally chordal if each of its balls of radius r/2 is chordal.
Our main result shows that r -locally chordal graphs G admit a global decomposition which simultaneously witnesses that G is r -locally chordal:
Theorem 1.
Let G be a locally finite22

Recall that a graph is locally finite if each vertex has finite degree. In particular, finite graphs are locally finite. graph and r\geqslant 3 an integer. The following are equivalent:
(i)
G is r -locally chordal.
(ii)
G is an r -acyclic region intersection graph (Theorem 3).
(iii)
G admits an r -acyclic graph-decomposition into cliques (Theorem 3).
(iv)
G admits a graph-decomposition into cliques which r -locally induces tree-decompositions (Theorem 6).
(v)
G admits a graph-decomposition into cliques which induces r -local separations of G (Theorem 7).
In what follows, we introduce the relevant terms and intuitions.
Our results here draw on our first paper about locally chordal graphs [1]. In that paper, we prove that locally chordal graphs can be characterized by forbidden induced subgraphs, by minimal local separators (a local analog of minimal separators), and by their local covers. We now state the main theorem of [1]. A graph is r -chordal if each of its cycles of length at least four and at most r has a chord. A wheel W_{n} for n\geqslant 4 consists of a cycle of length n , its rim, and a vertex v complete to the rim, its hub.
Theorem 1.1 ([1], Theorem 1).
Let G be a (possibly infinite) graph and let r\geqslant 3 be an integer. The following are equivalent:
(i)
G is r -locally chordal.
(ii)
G is r -chordal and wheel-free.
(iii)
Every minimal r -local separator of G is a clique.
(iv)
The r -local cover G_{r} of G is chordal.
The equivalence between Theorem 1.1 (i) and (iv) is used heavily in this paper. (We also use the equivalence of (i) and (ii), but we don’t use the equivalence to Item (iii) in this paper.) The local cover is introduced in [7] as a graph that represents the local structure of G . The local cover is part of a new general theory designed to facilitate local-to-global analysis by constructing global decompositions from local structure. Our results are the first to apply this framework to characterize a locally-defined graph class. We therefore provide a possible roadmap to use this framework for local-to-global analysis more generally.
Next, we describe our results in more detail.
1.1. Characterization via region representations
First, let us discuss Theorem 1 Item (ii). A region of a graph H is a connected subgraph of H .
A map v\mapsto H_{v} assigning to the vertices v of a graph G regions H_{v} of a graph H represents G if an edge uv is present in G if and only if the regions H_{u} and H_{v} intersect in at least one vertex.
A region representation v\mapsto H_{v} is r -acyclic if for every set X\subseteq V(G) of at most r vertices, the union \bigcup_{x\in X}H_{x} is an acyclic subgraph of H .
If G has such a representation, we then call it an r -acyclic region intersection graph.
Every graph is a 2-acyclic region intersection graph; see Example 3.4 for a proof. But it is already interesting to ask which graphs are 3-acyclic region intersection graphs.
Wheels, and all graphs containing wheels as induced subgraphs, are not 3-acyclic region intersection graphs (Lemma 4.2), so 3-acyclic region intersection graphs must exclude wheels. In fact, Gavril [14] characterized the graphs which are 3-acyclic region intersection graphs as precisely the wheel-free graphs (see Section 3.2).
Wheel-free graphs, those graphs with no wheel as an induced subgraph, are also precisely the 3-locally chordal graphs. At the other extreme, \infty -acyclic region intersection graphs are precisely the intersection graphs of subtrees of forest, which are well known to be exactly the chordal graphs. Theorem 1 Item (ii) now fills in the spectrum for all other values of r , giving a characterization of r -acyclic region intersection graphs for all r\in\mathbb{N} as exactly the r -locally chordal graphs.
1.2. Characterization via graph-decompositions
Let us now turn to Theorem 1 Item (iii).
A graph-decomposition of a graph G , as introduced in [7], consists of a model graph H and an assignment h\mapsto V_{h} of a vertex-subset V_{h} of G to each node h of H such that the V_{h} decompose G , i.e. (H1) the union of the subgraphs G[V_{h}] is G , and this arranges G in the shape of H , i.e. (H2) for each vertex v of G the subgraph of H induced by the set W_{v} of nodes h whose V_{h} contains v is connected (Section 2.1).
A graph-decomposition is into cliques if every set V_{h} is a clique of G .
It turns out that graph-decompositions into cliques correspond to region representations.
More specifically, a graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of G into cliques induces a region representation of G by mapping each vertex v of G to a connected, spanning subgraph of H[W_{v}] , the induced subgraph from (H2). Conversely, a region representation v\mapsto H_{v} induces a graph-decomposition by assigning to each node h of H the vertex-subset V_{h}\coloneqq\{v\in V(G)\mid h\in V(H_{v})\} .
Notice that these two constructions are inverse to each other (see Section 2 for details).
1.3. Locally inducing tree-decompositions
Recall that we started with the idea of gluing cliques together in the shape of a high-girth graph so that locally at each vertex the graph looks like being composed of cliques glued together along a tree.
Graph-decompositions from Theorem 1 Item (iv) formalize this idea. Indeed, let G be an r -locally chordal graph and (H,\mathcal{V}) be an r -acyclic graph-decomposition of G as in Theorem 1 Item (iv). Now, roughly speaking, if we restrict the decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) to just a ball of radius r/2 in G , we obtain a tree-decomposition into cliques (see Section 6 for more details). Essentially, this means that there is a collection of |V(G)| tree-decompositions into cliques, one for each ball of radius r/2 of G , such that one can find a single graph-decomposition of G which “puts them together” – in other words, which simultaneously witnesses each local tree-decomposition in a single structure. This property holds for every r -acyclic graph-decomposition of locally chordal graphs into cliques (Theorem 6).
1.4. Inducing local separations
The r -acyclic region representations (equivalently: graph-decompositions into cliques), from Theorem 1 Item (ii), can be characterized as those graph-decompositions which induce r -local separations (see Section 7 for details).
Local separations were introduced in [5] together with local separators as a local analog to separations, which, when r goes to infinity, are precisely the usual separations. Tree-decompositions are well-known to have a special relationship with the usual separations.
Thus, when r goes to infinity, Theorem 1 Item (v) gives that connected, chordal graphs are exactly the graph that admit tree-decompositions into cliques.
1.5. Further results: Computing region representations
Our proofs of Theorem 1 are not computable; indeed, they rely on the local cover, which is often an infinite graph. Nevertheless, we give an efficient and conceptually simple algorithm (Algorithm 9) which, when given an r -locally chordal graph G , computes an r -acyclic region representation of G . The algorithm in fact gives an independent proof of the main decomposition theorem Theorem 3, except the existence of a canonical decomposition, for finite locally chordal graphs. Our algorithm is essentially a parallelization of the algorithm given by Gavril [11] to compute 3-acyclic region representations of 3-locally-chordal graphs.
The algorithm does not depend on r .
The (parallel) running time is at most O(\Delta^{2}\log\Delta) on n=|V(G)| processors, where \Delta is the maximum degree of G . Furthermore, the algorithm does not require r as an input (Theorem 8):
given a wheel-free graph G , it returns an r -acyclic region representation of G where r is the maximum integer such that G is r -locally chordal.
In the extreme case that the graph G is chordal, the computed region representation is \infty -acyclic and thus the graph H is a forest, recovering the familiar notion of intersection graphs of subtrees of a tree (if G is additionally connected).
More generally,
a graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of a graph G into the maximal cliques of G corresponds to a spanning subgraph of the intersection graph \mathbf{K}(G) of the maximal cliques in G , by identifying the model graph H with the one obtained from the map h\mapsto V_{h} .
Note that, for any fixed vertex v of G , the subgraph of H induced on the maximal cliques of G containing v is precisely H[W_{v}] , and thus connected.
Conversely, any given spanning subgraph H of \mathbf{K}(G) whose subgraphs H_{v} induced on the maximal cliques containing any fixed vertex v of G are connected yields a region representation of G over H by mapping a vertex v to H_{v} , which then corresponds to a graph-decomposition of G into the maximal cliques of G .
We call such an H a clique graph of G , and it is r -acylic if its corresponding region representation is.
If H is a tree, this recovers the notion of clique trees. The algorithm above does indeed return a clique graph of G .
Clique trees of a chordal graph G are known to be precisely the maximum-weight spanning trees of \mathbf{K}(G) , where we weight each edge by the size of the corresponding intersection of maximal cliques. We generalize this by proving that r -acyclic clique graphs of r -locally chordal graphs G are precisely the maximum-weight r -acyclic spanning subgraphs of \mathbf{K}(G) (Theorem 10).
Since r -acyclic clique graphs can be efficiently computed, they can be used in algorithms.
Clique trees are powerful algorithmic tools (see e.g. [28]), and our algorithm to compute r -acyclic region representations of locally chordal graphs is an important step toward extending the algorithmic power of chordal graphs to locally chordal graphs.
We outline a specific potential application, to the maximum independent set problem, in Section 9.2.

1.7. Notation & conventions
We follow standard graph theory notation and definitions from [8]. We also refer to
[19] for terminology on topology.
In this paper we deal with simple graphs, i.e. graphs with neither loops nor parallel edges. Often, we will restrict our view to locally finite graphs; a graph is locally finite if each vertex has finite degree. We specify when we deal with finite graphs, with locally finite, or with general (possibly infinite) graphs.
Let G be a graph, and let X and Y be two vertex-subsets of G . The induced subgraph of G on vertex-set X is denoted G[X] .
The vertex-subset X is connected in G if its induced subgraph G[X] is.
An X – Y path is a path whose first vertex is in X , last vertex is in Y , and which is otherwise disjoint from X\cup Y .
An X -path is a non-trivial path whose first and last vertex is in X and which is otherwise disjoint from X .
A path P is through X if all internal vertices of P are in X and P has at least one internal vertex.
Let d\geqslant 0 be a real number.
The depth- d closed neighborhood N_{G}^{d}[X] is the set of all vertices of G that have distance at most d to X , and the depth- d (open) neighborhood N_{G}^{d}(X) is the set of all vertices of G that have distance precisely d to X .
If the ambient graph G is clear from context, we also write N^{d}[X] and N^{d}(X) .
Note that the depth- d open neighborhoods are always empty when d is not an integer.
For any vertex v of G , we use N_{G}^{d}[v] and N_{G}^{d}(v) as a shorthand for N_{G}^{d}[\{v\}] and N_{G}^{d}(\{v\}) .
Also we use N_{G}[X] as a shorthand for N^{1}_{G}[X] .
Finally, we will need the following lemma.
Lemma 1.2 ([1], Lemma 2.4).
Let r\geqslant 4 be an integer and set d:=\lfloor r/2\rfloor .
Let X be a connected vertex-subset of a (possibly infinite) chordal graph G .
If, for any two distinct vertices u,w\in N^{d-1}(X) , there is a u – w path in B_{r/2}(X) through N^{d}(X) , then uw is an edge of B_{r/2}(X) .

2. Equivalence: Graph-decompositions & region representations
In this section we introduce our central objects to describe the structure of locally chordal graphs, graph-decompositions and region intersection graphs, and explore the relationship between them.
2.1. Graph-decompositions & region intersection graphs
Graph-decompositions, introduced in [7], generalize the notion of tree-decompositions by allowing the bags of the decompositions to be arranged along general graphs instead of just trees.
Let G be a graph.
A graph-decomposition of G is a pair \mathcal{H}=(H,\mathcal{V}) consisting of a graph H and a family \mathcal{V} of vertex-subsets V_{h} of G indexed by the nodes h of H which satisfies
(H1)
G=\bigcup_{h\in H}G[V_{h}] , and
(H2)
for every vertex v of G , the subgraph H[W_{v}] of H induced on W_{v}:=\{h\in V(H)\mid v\in V_{h}\} is connected.
We refer to H as the model graph, and also say that (H,\mathcal{V}) is a graph-decomposition of G over, or modeled on, H .
The vertex-subsets V_{h} are called the bags of \mathcal{H} , and the W_{v} are called the co-bags of \mathcal{H} .
We can flesh out the bags and co-bags by choosing spanning subgraphs G_{h} of G[V_{h}] with G=\bigcup_{h\in H}G_{h} and connected, spanning subgraphs H_{v} of H[W_{v}] .
Then the G_{h} are the parts and the H_{v} are the co-parts of the decomposition \mathcal{H} .
In this paper, we will always consider the induced subgraphs G_{h}:=G[V_{h}] of G from Item (H1) as the parts.
Whenever we introduce a graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) without a specific choice of co-parts in this paper, we tacitly assume that we have chosen any valid family of co-parts H_{v} .
A graph-decomposition is honest if every bag is nonempty and the adhesion sets V_{h_{1}}\cap V_{h_{2}} corresponding to the edges f=h_{1}h_{2} of H are nonempty.
Note that a graph-decomposition is honest if and only if H=\bigcup_{v\in G}H_{v} . In this paper, we call a pair \mathcal{H}=(H,\mathcal{V}) a decomposition if it consists of a graph H and a family \mathcal{V} of vertex-subsets V_{h} of G indexed by the nodes h of H which satisfies Item (H1).
A tree-decomposition (T,\mathcal{V}) is precisely a graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) whose model graph H is the tree T .
In this case, the only valid choice of co-parts T_{v} of a tree-decomposition (T,\mathcal{V}) are the induced subgraphs T[W_{v}] , since the T[W_{v}] is a tree and co-parts must be connected. Indeed, most of our graph-decomposition only admit one valid choice of co-parts (cf. Lemma 4.11).
The bags V_{h} and co-bags W_{v} of any graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of G satisfy the duality
v\in V_{h}\Longleftrightarrow h\in W_{v}.
Given this, we can cryptomorphically axiomatize graph-decompositions (H,\mathcal{V}) (whose parts G_{h} are the induced subgraphs G[V_{h}] and whose co-parts H_{v} are any valid choice), as follows.
A graph-decomposition of G over a graph H is a map v\mapsto H_{v} that assigns to each vertex v of G a subgraph H_{v} of H such that
(H1V)
H_{v} is nonempty for every vertex v of G ,
(H1E)
H_{u}\cap H_{v} is nonempty for every edge uv of G , and
(H2)
H_{v} is connected for every vertex v of G .
Given a graph G , a graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of G , and a vertex-subset X of G , we set {\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}{{H_{X}}}}\coloneqq\bigcup_{x\in X}H_{x} . Observe that the graph-decomposition axioms and the definition of co-part imply the following:
Lemma 2.1.
Let (H,\mathcal{V}) be a graph-decomposition of a graph G .
If a vertex-subset U of G is connected, then H_{U} is connected. ∎
The regions of a graph are its (nonempty) connected subgraphs.
In this language, a graph-decomposition over H assigns to each vertex v a region H_{v} of H such that H_{u}\cap H_{v}\neq\emptyset if uv\in E(G) . If the map also satisfies the converse of this condition, i.e. H_{u}\cap H_{v}\neq\emptyset if and only if uv\in E(G) , then we obtain a concept called region representations:
A region representation v\mapsto H_{v} of G over a graph H is a map that assigns to each vertex v of G a region H_{v} of H such that uv is an edge of G if and only if H_{u} and H_{v} have nonempty intersection. If such a region representation of G over H exists, then G is a region intersection graph over H .
For a vertex-subset X of G , we write H_{X} for the union \bigcup_{x\in X}H_{x} of the regions corresponding to the elements of X .
Given a class \mathcal{H} of graphs, a graph G is a region intersection graph over \mathcal{H} if G is a region intersection graph over some H\in\mathcal{H} .
We remark that, by simply restricting the map, a region representation of G induces a region representation of each induced subgraph of G .
Then the following is a simple observation similiar to Lemma 2.1:
Lemma 2.2.
Let v\mapsto H_{v} be a region representation of a graph G .
Then an induced subgraph G^{\prime} of G is connected if and only if the union H_{V(G^{\prime})} of its corresponding regions is connected. ∎
A region representation v\mapsto T_{v} of G over a tree T is better known as a subtree representation of G over the tree T .
Similarly, if G has a subtree representation over a tree T , then G is a subtree intersection graph over T .
A clique X in G is a vertex-subset which induces a complete subgraph of G .
It is well-known that, for a tree-decomposition (T,\mathcal{V}) of a graph G into cliques, the map v\mapsto T_{v} assigning a vertex to its co-part is a subtree representation and vice versa.
More generally, it is now immediate from the definitions (since the converse of (H1E) holds) that region representation correspond precisely to the graph-decompositions (H,\mathcal{V}) of G that are decompositions into cliques, i.e. all its bag V_{h} are cliques of G :
Theorem 2.
Let G and H be (possibly infinite) graphs.
(i)
If v\mapsto H_{v} is a region representation of G over H , then (H,\mathcal{V}) with V_{h}\coloneqq\{v\in V(G)\mid h\in V(H_{v})\} is a graph-decomposition of G into cliques, for which the family (H_{v})_{v\in G} is a valid choice of co-parts.
(ii)
If (H,\mathcal{V}) is a graph-decomposition of G into cliques and (H_{v})_{v\in G} is a valid choice of co-parts, then v\mapsto H_{v} is a region representation of G over H . ∎
We remark that Theorem 2
yields a one-to-one correspondence between region representations of a graph and its graph-decompositions into cliques, which allows us to discuss the graph-decomposition (into cliques) corresponding to a given region representation and vice versa.
2.2. Canonical graph-decompositions & region representations
Let G be a graph and let \Gamma be a subgroup of the group \Aut(G) of automorphisms of G .
A graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of G is \Gamma -canonical if there exists an action of \Gamma on H which commutes with the assignment h\mapsto V_{h} of the bags, i.e. \gamma(V_{h})=V_{\gamma\cdot h} for every \gamma\in\Gamma .
Equivalently, the action of \Gamma on H commutes with the assignment v\mapsto W_{v} of the co-bags, i.e. W_{\gamma(v)}=\gamma\cdot W_{v} for every \gamma\in\Gamma .
If parts G_{h} or co-parts H_{v} are specified, then we require the respective assignments h\mapsto G_{h} or v\mapsto H_{v} to commute with the action of \Gamma on H .
Moreover, (H,\mathcal{V}) is canonical if it is \Aut(G) -canonical.
As discussed in Section 2.1, we can consider a graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) with a family (H_{v})_{v\in G} of specified co-parts to be presented by the assignment v\mapsto H_{v} of co-parts.
Then the graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) is \Gamma -canonical if and only if v\mapsto H_{v} commutes with the action of \Gamma on H , i.e. \gamma\cdot H_{v}=H_{\gamma(v)} .
Thus, we say that a region representation v\mapsto H_{v} of G over a graph H is \Gamma -canonical or canonical if its corresponding graph-decomposition with specified co-parts H_{v} is \Gamma -canonical or canonical, respectively.
2.3. Clique graphs & graph-decompositions into maximal cliques
Let G be a graph.
We denote the set of all (inclusion-wise) maximal cliques of G by K(G) .
By \mathbf{K}(G) we denote the intersection graph of the maximal cliques of G , i.e. the graph \mathbf{K}(G) whose vertex set is K(G) and where two maximal cliques K_{1} and K_{2} of G are adjacent in \mathbf{K}(G) when K_{1}\cap K_{2}\neq\emptyset .
The set of maximal cliques of G containing a fixed vertex v of G we denote by K_{G}(v) .
The map v\mapsto\mathbf{K}(G)[K_{G}(v)] always forms a graph-decomposition of G over \mathbf{K}(G) .
A naive attempt to flesh out the \mathbf{K}(G)[K_{G}(v)] by a choice of co-parts H_{v} , i.e. connected, spanning subgraphs, is to choose a spanning subgraph H of \mathbf{K}(G) and consider the induced subgraph H[K_{G}(v)] as co-parts H_{v} .
This then yields a valid choice of co-parts for v\mapsto\mathbf{K}(G)[K_{G}(v)] if and only if the H[K_{G}(v)] are all connected.
More formally, given any spanning subgraph H of \mathbf{K}(G) , the assignment v\mapsto H_{v}\coloneqq H[K_{G}(v)] will always satisfy H_{u}\cap H_{v}\neq\emptyset if and only if uv\in E(G) (and also (H1V) and (H1E) are satisfied).

3. Overview: Region representations of (locally) chordal graphs
In this section, we discuss why region representations (equivalently: graph-decompositions into cliques) are the right tools to describe the structure of locally chordal graphs.
First, we review the classic characterizations of chordal graphs as those graphs which have subtree representations (equivalently: admit tree-decompositions into cliques).
Then, we explain how these characterizations generalize to the locally chordal case.
It is a simple observation55
5
This statement is immediate from the first paragraph of the proof presented in [12, Theorem 3]. that subtree intersection graphs are chordal:
Proposition 3.1 (Folklore).
Every (possibly infinite) subtree intersection graph is chordal
(equivalently: every graph that admits a tree-decomposition into cliques is chordal).
Conversely, Gavril in the finite case [12] and Halin [16, 10.2’] based on his zweiter Zerlegungssatz [17, Satz 5] showed that in particular every locally finite, connected, chordal graph is a subtree intersection graph (equivalently: admits a tree-decomposition into cliques).
There are two stronger variants of subtree representations (equivalently: tree-decomposition into cliques): clique trees and canonical ones.
Indeed, Halin showed in [16] that every locally finite, connected, chordal graph admits a clique tree.66
6
Halin actually showed this for connected, chordal graphs that have no infinite maximal clique. Later, Hofer-Temmel and Lehner [20, Theorem 3] extended the techniques from finite graphs to locally finite graphs to obtain another proof of this result in the case of locally finite graphs.
Jacobs and Knappe [21, Theorem 2] showed that every such graph also admits a canonical tree-decomposition into cliques.
The following characterization of chordal graphs is immediate by applying the above-mentioned results to the components of the considered graph.
A region representation v\mapsto H_{v} of a graph G over a graph H is acyclic if H_{V(G)} is acyclic.
A clique graph H of G is acyclic if its corresponding region representation is acyclic (equivalently: H is acyclic).
A forest-decomposition of a graph G is a graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) where H is a forest.
Theorem 3.2.
Let G be a locally finite (not necessarily connected) graph.
Then the following are equivalent:
(i)
G is chordal.
(ii)
G has an acyclic clique graph (equivalently: G admits a forest-decomposition into maximal cliques).
(iii)
G has a canonical acyclic region representation (equivalently: G admits a canonical forest-decomposition into cliques).
(iv)
G has an acyclic region representation (equivalently: G admits a forest-decomposition into cliques).
In contrast, every graph G is a region intersection graph, since the intersection graph \mathbf{K}(G) of maximal cliques itself is already a clique graph of G .
Thus, we need to require additional properties to use region representations to characterize locally chordal graphs.
As cycles are finite, a region representation v\mapsto H_{v} of G over a graph H is acyclic if and only if only if for every finite vertex-subset X of G the union H_{X} is acyclic.
More generally, let H be a graph and let \mathbf{H} be a family of subgraphs of H .
Then, \mathbf{H} is r -acyclic for an integer r\geqslant 0 if every union of at most r elements in \mathbf{H} is acyclic.
A region representation v\mapsto H_{v} of a graph G over H is r -acyclic if the family (H_{v}\mid v\in V(G)) is r -acyclic.
A clique graph of G is r -acyclic
if its
corresponding region representation is r -acyclic.
If a graph G has an r -acyclic region representation, then G is an r -acyclic region intersection graph.
A graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) with a fixed family (H_{v})_{v\in G} of co-parts is r -acyclic if the family (H_{v})_{v\in G} is r -acyclic.
Let us observe that honest r -acyclic graph-decompositions always have high-girth models, i.e. the models are locally trees.
Lemma 3.3.
Let r\geqslant 3 be an integer, let G be a (possibly infinite) graph, and let (H,\mathcal{V}) be an honest, r -acyclic graph-decomposition of G . Then H has girth greater than r .
Proof.
Let C be a cycle of H of length \ell\geqslant 3 . Since (H,\mathcal{V}) is honest, we may choose for each edge f of C a vertex v_{f} of G in its adhesion set V_{f} . Now, C\subseteq\bigcup_{f\in E(C)}H_{v_{f}} . Since (H,\mathcal{V}) is r -acyclic, it follows that \ell>r . Therefore, H has no cycle of length at most r , i.e. H has girth greater than r .
∎
In light of the notion of r -acyclic, we may refer to acyclic region-representations, families of subgraphs, or clique graphs as being \infty -acyclic.
Then Theorem 3.2 says that chordal graphs are exactly the graphs which have \infty -acyclic region representations. On the other end of the spectrum, every graph has not only a region representation but indeed even a (g(G)-1) -acyclic region representation, where {\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}{{g(G)}}}\geqslant 3 is the girth of G , i.e. the length of a shortest cycle in G .
Example 3.4.
Every graph G has a (g(G)-1) -acyclic region representation v\mapsto H_{v} over some graph H such that the bag V_{h}=\{v\in V(G)\mid h\in V(H_{v})\} is a clique of size at most 2 for every node h of H .
Proof.
Let G be a graph and let H be the graph formed from G by subdividing each edge e once by a vertex v_{e} .
Then assign each vertex v of G the star S_{v} in H formed by v together with v_{e} for all edges e incident to v in G .
Note that the stars S_{v} are edge-disjoint, and every cycle in the subdivision H of G meets every S_{v} in precisely 0 or 2 edges.
Thus, v\mapsto H_{v} is a (g(G)-1) -acyclic region representation of G , the bag V_{v_{e}} is \{u,v\} for every edge e=uv of G , and the bag V_{v} is \{v\} for every vertex v of G .
∎
Given Theorem 3.2 and Example 3.4, it is natural to ask whether there is a nice characterization r -acyclic region intersection graphs for general values of r . The class of r -acyclic region intersection graphs is (inclusion-wise) monotone decreasing as r goes to infinity, eventually approaching the class of chordal graphs.
It turns out that r -locally chordal graphs are exactly those graphs.
Theorem 3.
Let G be a locally finite graph and r\geqslant 3 an integer.
Then the following are equivalent:
(i)
G is r -locally chordal.
(ii)
G has an r -acyclic clique graph (equivalently: G admits an r -acyclic graph-decomposition into maximal cliques).
(iii)
G has an r -acyclic canonical region representation (equivalently: G admits a canonical r -acyclic graph-decomposition into cliques).
(iv)
G has an r -acyclic region representation (equivalently: G admits an r -acyclic graph-decomposition into cliques).
3.1. Proof of Theorem 3
The proof of Theorem 3 involves two steps. First, we prove that Theorem 3 holds for r=3 :
Theorem 4.
Let G be a locally finite graph. The following are equivalent:
(i)
G is 3 -locally chordal (equivalently: wheel-free).
(ii)
G has a 3-acyclic clique graph.
(iii)
G has a 3-acyclic canonical region representation.
(iv)
G has a 3-acyclic region representation.
Next, we prove that for r>3 , the 3 -acyclic region representations of r -locally chordal graphs are already the r -acyclic region representations:
Theorem 5.
Let v\mapsto H_{v} be a 3 -acyclic region representation of a (possibly infinite) graph G over a graph H , and let r>3 be an integer.
Then G is r -locally chordal if and only if v\mapsto H_{v} is r -acyclic.
The proof of Theorem 4 appears in Section 4 and the proof of Theorem 5 appears in Section 5. Before proceeding to the proofs, we remark that 3-locally-chordal graphs have been analyzed through a similar view in the past. Specifically, in the nineties, Gavril [14] studied (finite) 3-locally-chordal graphs, which he called neighborhood chordal, and obtained a characterization similar to that in Theorem 4. Next, we discuss his result and how it relates to ours.
3.2. Comparison to Gavril’s work

Lemma 3.7 (Gavril [14], Lemma 1 and Theorem 2).
Let \mathbf{H} be a 2-acyclic family of regions of a (possibly infinite) graph H .
(i)
If the elements in \mathbf{H} have a node in common, then the union \bigcup\mathbf{H} of all elements in \mathbf{H} is a tree.
(ii)

Proof.
Gavril [14, Lemma 1 and Theorem 2] proved Item (i) and Item (ii) for finite graphs H , respectively.
In the following, we reduce the case that H is infinite to the case that H is finite.
Claim 1.
Item (i) holds for infinite H , as well.
Proof.
The union \bigcup\mathbf{H} is connected, as the elements of \mathbf{H} are connected and all share a node, say t .
Suppose for a contradiction that \bigcup\mathbf{H} contains a cycle C .
Then there is a finite subfamily \mathbf{H}^{*}=(H_{i}\mid i\in[n]) of \mathbf{H} such that C\subseteq\bigcup\mathbf{H}^{*} .
For each H_{i} , we may choose a finite connected subgraph H^{\prime}_{i} of the connected graph H_{i} that contains t and the finite subgraph C\cap H_{i} of H_{i} .
As H_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq H_{i} and \mathbf{H} is 2 -acyclic, the family \mathbf{H}^{\prime} consisting of the regions H_{i}^{\prime} of H^{\prime} is 2 -acyclic.
Their union H^{\prime}\coloneqq\bigcup\mathbf{H}^{\prime} is a finite graph.
The choice of the H_{i}^{\prime} ensures that t is a common node of the H_{i}^{\prime} .
Applying Gavril’s Item (i) for finite graphs to the finite family \mathbf{H}^{\prime} of finite regions in the finite graph H^{\prime} yields that \bigcup\mathbf{H}^{\prime} is a tree.
This contradicts that, by the choice of the H_{i}^{\prime} , their union \bigcup\mathbf{H}^{\prime} contains the cycle C .

Claim 2.
Item (ii) holds for infinite H , as well.

Now, for each H_{i} in \mathbf{H}^{*} , we may choose a finite connected subgraph H_{i}^{\prime} of the connected graph H that contains h_{i,j} for every H_{j} in \mathbf{H}^{*} , since \mathbf{H}^{*} is finite and H_{i} is connected.

Lemma 3.8.
Let \mathbf{H} be a 2 -acyclic family of regions of a (possibly infinite) graph H .
If the union of three elements of \mathbf{H} contains a cycle, then each two of these regions intersect.

Let H_{1},H_{2},H_{3} be three regions in \mathbf{H} whose union contains a cycle C .
Note that the H_{i} are trees, as \mathbf{H} is 1 -acyclic.
Moreover, since \mathbf{H} is 2 -acyclic, the cycle C contains an edge f_{i} of H_{i} which is not contained in any other H_{j} with j\neq i .
Consider the path P_{i} in C which contains f_{i} and such that the intersection of P_{i} with \bigcup_{j\neq i}H_{j} is exactly the ends of P_{i} ; in particular, P_{i}\subseteq H_{i} .
Since \mathbf{H} is 2 -acyclic and P_{i} contains the edge f_{i} that is not in H_{j} with j\neq i , the ends of P_{i} are not both in the same H_{j} for a j\neq i .
The ends of the P_{i} thus witness that the desired intersections are nonempty.

Let v\mapsto H_{v} be a region representation of a graph G over a (possibly infinite) graph H .
Assume that X is a vertex-subset of G of minimum size such that H_{X} contains a cycle.
If X has size at most 3 , then X is a clique of G .

Proof.

The following well-known fact about trees will be frequently used in the rest of the paper. We include a proof for completeness.
Lemma 3.10.
Let T and T^{\prime} be two trees.
Then the following three statements are equivalent:
(i)
T\cup T^{\prime} is a tree.
(ii)
T\cap T^{\prime} is connected.
(iii)
T\cap T^{\prime} is a tree.
Proof.
We prove Item (i) \to Item (ii) \to Item (iii) \to Item (i).
Claim 1.
Item (i) implies Item (ii).
Proof.
Assume Item (i), i.e. T\cup T^{\prime} is a tree.
For every two given vertices s,t of T\cap T^{\prime} , their unique s – t path P in T\cup T^{\prime} is the unique s – t path in T and also in T^{\prime} .
Hence, P is an s – t path in T\cap T^{\prime} .
Thus, T\cap T^{\prime} is connected, i.e. Item (ii) holds.
∎
Claim 2.
Item (ii) implies Item (iii).
Proof.
Any connected subgraph of a tree is a tree.
Thus, the, by Item (ii), connected subgraph T\cap T^{\prime} of the tree T is a tree, as desired.
∎
Claim 3.
Item (iii) implies Item (i).
Proof.
Assume Item (iii), i.e. T\cap T^{\prime} is a tree.
In particular, T\cap T^{\prime} is nonempty.
Thus, T\cup T^{\prime} is connected, as T and T^{\prime} are trees.
To show Item (i), it remains to show that T\cup T^{\prime} is acyclic.
Suppose for a contradiction that T\cup T^{\prime} is not acyclic.
As T,T^{\prime} are trees, we may pick cycle C in T\cup T^{\prime} such that P\coloneqq T\cap C and Q\coloneqq T^{\prime}\cap C are paths.
But as the endvertices of P (equivalently: Q ) are in T\cap T^{\prime} , and thus there is a path R in the tree T\cap T^{\prime} joining them.
Now R is either distinct from P or from Q .
Hence R\cup P\subseteq T or R\cup Q\subseteq T^{\prime} contains a cycle, which is a contradiction to T,T^{\prime} being trees.
∎
This completes the proof.

Proof of Lemma 3.6.
Let \mathbf{H} be a family of regions of a graph H .
Claim 1.
If \mathbf{H} is Helly and 2 -acyclic, then \mathbf{H} is 3 -acyclic.
Proof.
Consider a three-element subset \mathbf{H}^{\prime} of \mathbf{H} .
Suppose for a contradiction that their union contains a cycle.
Then Lemma 3.8 yields that they are pairwise intersecting.
Thus, since \mathbf{H} is Helly, the elements of \mathbf{H}^{\prime} have a node in common. By Lemma 3.7 (i), it follows that the union of \mathbf{H}^{\prime} is a tree, and thus acyclic.
∎
Claim 2.
If \mathbf{H} is 3 -acyclic, then \mathbf{H} is Helly and 2 -acyclic.
Proof.
Since \mathbf{H} is 3 -acyclic, \mathbf{H} is also 2 -acyclic.
It remains to show that \mathbf{H} is Helly.
By Lemma 3.7 (ii), it suffices to consider three pairwise intersecting elements H_{1},H_{2},H_{3} of \mathbf{H} and show that their intersection is nonempty.
Since \mathbf{H} is 2 -acyclic, H_{1}\cup H_{2} is acyclic.
So, since their intersection S\coloneqq H_{1}\cap H_{2} is nonempty but H_{1} and H_{2} are connected, H_{1}\cup H_{2} is connected, and thus a tree.
If H_{3} meets the intersection S=H_{1}\cap H_{2} , then we are done.
Thus, we assume that H_{3} does not intersect S .
Fix x_{i}\in H_{i}\cap H_{3} for i=1,2 witnessing that H_{i} and H_{3} intersect.
In particular, x_{i}\notin S by assumption, so x_{i}\not\in H_{3-i} .
Let P be the path from x_{1} to x_{2} in H_{3} , and let Q be the path from x_{1} to x_{2} in H_{1}\cup H_{2} .
In particular, P and Q are distinct because P\subseteq H_{3} avoids S and, since x_{i}\in H_{i}\smallsetminus H_{3-i} for i=1,2 , the path Q\subseteq H_{1}\cup H_{2} meets H_{1}\cap H_{2}=S .
Hence, P\cup Q\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{3}H_{i} contains a cycle, contradicting that \mathbf{H} is 3-acyclic. Therefore, \mathbf{H} is Helly.
∎
Therefore, 3-acyclic is equivalent to 2-acyclic and Helly for region representations.

4.2. Background: Local and normal coverings
We remind the reader that the graphs we consider in this paper are always simple, i.e. they neither have loops nor parallel edges.
A covering of a (loopless) graph G is a surjective homomorphism p\colon\hat{G}\to G such that p restricts to an isomorphism from the edges incident to any given vertex \hat{v} of \hat{G} to the edges incident to its projection v\coloneqq p_{r}(\hat{v}) .
In this paper, we restrict our view to those coverings p of graphs G whose preimage p^{-1}(C) of any component C of G is connected. Typically, we consider only coverings p:\hat{G}\to G where both G and \hat{G} are connected.
Let r\in\mathbb{N} .
A (graph) homomorphism p\colon\hat{G}\to G is r/2 -ball-preserving if p restricts to an isomorphism from B_{\hat{G}}(\hat{v},r/2) to B_{G}(v,r/2) for every vertex \hat{v} of \hat{G} and v\coloneqq p_{r}(\hat{v}) .
Thus, a surjective homomorphism p\colon\hat{G}\to G is a covering of a (simple) graph G if and only if p is 2/2 -ball preserving.
In [7] the r -local covering p_{r}\colon G_{r}\to G of a connected graph G is introduced as the covering of G whose characteristic subgroup is the r -local subgroup of the fundamental group of G .
Since the formal definition is not relevant to this paper, we refer the reader to [7, §4] for details.
In this paper, we will often use that the r -local covering preserves the r/2 -balls.
Indeed, the r -local covering is the universal r/2 -ball-preserving covering of G [7, Lemma 4.2, 4.3 & 4.4], i.e. for every r/2 -ball-preserving covering p\colon\hat{G}\to G there exists a covering q\colon G_{r}\to\hat{G} such that p_{r}=p\circ q .
Following this equivalent description of the r -local covering, it is also defined for non-connected graphs G .
We refer to the graph G_{r} as the r -local cover of G .
Note that the 0 -, 1 - and 2 -local covers of a (simple) graph G are forests. For the reader who is familiar with coverings, we remark that the 0 -, 1 - and 2 -local coverings are all the universal covering of G .
A deck transformation of a covering p\colon\hat{G}\to G is an automorphism \gamma of \hat{G} that commutes with p , i.e. p=p\circ\gamma .
We denote the group of deck transformations of a covering p by \Gamma(p) . A covering p:\hat{G}\to G is normal if for every vertex v of G and every two vertices \hat{v},\hat{v}^{\prime}\in p^{-1}(v) , there is a deck transformation f\in\Gamma(p) such that f(\hat{v})=\hat{v}^{\prime} .
4.3. Background: Canonical graph-decompositions via coverings

Denote the quotient map from T to the orbit-graph H=T/\Gamma also by p .
The bag V_{h} corresponding to a node h of H is p(\hat{V}_{t}) for a vertex (equivalently: every vertex) t in the \Gamma -orbit h .88

4.4. Interplay: Local covering & cliques
Let us collect some lemmas regarding the interplay of cliques and the local coverings.
Local covers of graphs interact well with cliques, made formal in the following lemma:
Lemma 4.5 ([5], Lemma 5.15).
Let G be a (possibly infinite) graph and let r\geqslant 3 an integer.
(i)
For every clique \hat{X} of G_{r} , its projection p_{r}(\hat{X}) is a clique of G , and p_{r} restricts to a bijection from \hat{X} to p_{r}(\hat{X}) .
(ii)
For every clique X of G , there exists a clique \hat{X} of G_{r} such that p_{r} restricts to a bijection from \hat{X} to X .
(iii)
N_{G_{r}}[\hat{X}]\cap N_{G_{r}}[\gamma(\hat{X})]=\emptyset for every clique \hat{X} of G_{r} and every \gamma\in\Gamma(p_{r})\smallsetminus\{\text{id}_{G_{r}}\} .
For a given clique X of G , we refer to all cliques \hat{X} of G_{r} such that p_{r} restricts to a bijection from \hat{X} to X as lifts of the clique X to G_{r} .
Lemma 4.5 ensures that every clique \hat{X} of G_{r} projects to a clique X:=p_{r}(\hat{X}) of G and \hat{X} is indeed a lift of the clique X in G , every clique of G has a lift to G_{r} , and not only are two distinct lifts of any clique disjoint, but their closed neighborhoods are also disjoint.
The intersection graph \mathbf{K}(G_{r}) of the maximal cliques of G_{r} covers the intersection graph \mathbf{K}(G) of the maximal cliques of G via p_{r} :
Lemma 4.6.
Let G be a (possibly infinite) graph and r\geqslant 3 an integer.
Then p_{r} induces a covering map from \mathbf{K}(G_{r}) to \mathbf{K}(G) via \hat{X}\mapsto p_{r}(\hat{X}) which restricts to an isomorphism from \mathbf{K}(G_{r})[K_{G_{r}}(\hat{X})] to \mathbf{K}(G)[K_{G}(X)] for every clique X of G and each of its lifts \hat{X} to G_{r} .
In particular, the maximal cliques of G_{r} are precisely the lifts of the maximal cliques of G .
In the context of Lemma 4.6, we denote the covering map from \mathbf{K}(G_{r}) to \mathbf{K}(G) induced by p_{r} again by p_{r} .
Proof.
First, we show that V(\mathbf{K}(G_{r})) is the set K(G) of lifts of the maximal cliques of G .
Let \hat{X} be a maximal clique of G_{r} .
Then Lemma 4.5 (i) ensures that X\coloneqq p_{r}(\hat{X}) is a clique of G .
Let Y be a maximal clique of G containing X .
By Lemma 4.5 (ii), there is a lift \hat{Y} of Y to G_{r} .
By potentially shifting \hat{Y} with a deck transformation of p_{r} , we assume that \hat{Y} meets \hat{X} .
Since distinct lifts of the clique X are disjoint by Lemma 4.5 (iii), \hat{Y} must contain the whole lift \hat{X} of X .
In particular, since \hat{X} is a maximal clique, \hat{Y}=\hat{X} , and thus X=Y is a maximal clique of G .
We can similarly prove that every maximal clique of G_{r} projects to a maximal clique of G .
All in all, this shows that the maximal cliques of G_{r} are precisely the lifts of the maximal cliques of G and that p_{r} induces a surjection from V(\mathbf{K}(G_{r})) to V(\mathbf{K}(G)) .
Lemma 4.5 (iii) ensures that, for every clique X and each of its lifts \hat{X} to G_{r} , the map induced by p_{r} restricts to a bijection from K_{G_{r}}(\hat{X}) to K_{G}(X) . Moreover, for every two cliques Y,Z\in K_{G}(X) and their respective lifts \hat{Y},\hat{Z}\in K_{G_{r}}(\hat{X}) , the map p_{r} restricts to a bijection from \hat{Y}\cap\hat{Z} to Y\cap Z .
Thus, p_{r} induces an isomorphism from \mathbf{K}(G_{r})[K_{G_{r}}(\hat{X})] to \mathbf{K}(G)[K_{G}(X)] .
In particular, p_{r} induces a covering map from \mathbf{K}(G_{r}) to \mathbf{K}(G) .

A separator X is a minimal (vertex) separator of G if there are two vertices u,v of G such that X is inclusion-minimal among the u – v separators in G .

In particular, u,v are separated by Y\smallsetminus\{u,v\} in G[Y] .
Pick an inclusion-minimal u – v separator S in G[Y] .
Fix a separation \{A,B\} of G[Y] with separator A\cap B=S such that u\in A and v\in B .
As every maximal clique K of G[Y] satisfies either K\subseteq A or K\subseteq B , both subgraphs G[A],G[B] induced on the sides of \{A,B\} have fewer maximal cliques than G[Y] .
Since G[Y] is chordal, S is a clique by Theorem 5.5.
Thus, the subgraphs G[A],G[B] are connected.
As induced subgraphs of a chordal graph, both G[A],G[B] are chordal.
The induction assumption applied to A and B yields that both H_{A} and H_{B} are trees.
Since \{A,B\} is a separation and v\mapsto H_{v} represents G , their intersection H_{A}\cap H_{B} is precisely H_{S} , which is also a tree by the induction assumption.
Thus, H_{Y}=H_{A}\cup H_{B} is a tree by Lemma 3.10.
∎
Now, we prove the converse of Theorem 5.2 in slightly more detail.
Lemma 5.6.
Let v\mapsto H_{v} be a 2 -acyclic region representation of a (possibly infinite) graph G over a graph H .
If X is an inclusion-minimal vertex-subset of G such that H_{X} contains a cycle, then G[X] is an induced cycle.
Proof.
Assume that X is inclusion-minimal so that H_{X} contains a cycle.
Since v\mapsto H_{v} is 2 -acyclic, X has size at least 3 .
If X has size 3 , then G[X] is an induced cycle by Corollary 3.9.
Thus, we assume that X has size at least 4 .
As X is inclusion-minimal with the property that H_{X} contains a cycle and X has size at least 4 , the restriction of v\mapsto H_{v} to X is a 3 -acyclic region representation of G[X] .
Therefore, by applying Theorem 5.1 to G[Y] with Y\subseteq X and its 3 -acyclic region representation, the restriction of v\mapsto H_{v} to Y , we deduce that X is inclusion-minimal such that G[X] is not chordal, i.e. G[X] is an induced cycle of length at least 4 , as desired.
∎
Proof of Theorem 5.2.
The backwards direction follows immediately from Lemma 5.6.
For the forwards direction, assume that G[X] is an induced cycle of length at least 4 .
In particular, G[X] is connected but not chordal.
Thus, Theorem 5.1 applied to v\mapsto H_{v} restricted to X , which is a 3 -acyclic region representation of G[X] , yields that H_{X} is not acyclic, i.e. H_{X} contains a cycle.
∎
Proof of Theorem 5.
Since G admits a 3 -acyclic region representation v\mapsto H_{v} , no wheel W_{n} is an induced subgraph of G for n\geqslant 4 by Lemma 4.2.
Theorem 5.2 yields that G has no induced cycles of length at least 4 and at most r if and only if v\mapsto H_{v} is r -acyclic.
The former is, for wheel-free graphs G , equivalent to being r -locally chordal by Theorem 1.1, as desired.
∎
6. Characterization via graph-decompositions that
locally induce tree-decompositions
By the definition, each r/2 -ball B_{r/2}(v) of an r -locally chordal graph is chordal, and thus admits a tree-decomposition (T^{v},\mathcal{V}^{v}) into cliques (Theorem 3.2).
In this section, we revisit the statement from the introduction that r -locally chordal graphs admit decompositions which look like a single decomposition formed by “putting together” the tree-decompositions of the r/2 -balls. Specifically, we show that r -acyclic graph-decompositions of an r -locally chordal graph form a common description of some choice of tree-decompositions (T^{v},\mathcal{V}^{v}) of the r/2 -balls into cliques (Theorem 6 and Theorem 6.6).
First, we formalize what the locally induced decompositions are.
Let (H,\mathcal{V}) be a decomposition of a graph G .
The decompositions which (H,\mathcal{V}) r -locally induces in G are the families (H^{v,r},\mathcal{V}^{v,r}) where H^{v,r}:=\bigcup\{H_{v}\mid d_{G}(u,v)<r/2\} and V^{v,r}_{h}:=V_{h}\cap N^{r/2}[v] for each node h of H^{v,r} .
For a given vertex v of G , we assign a vertex u\in N^{r/2}[v] the co-part H^{v,r}_{u} of u in the decomposition (H^{v,r},\mathcal{V}^{v,r}) , where H^{v,r}_{u}:=H_{u} if d_{G}(u,v)<r/2 and H^{v,r}_{u}:=H_{u}\cap H^{v,r} if d_{G}(u,v)=r/2 .
We can now state the main result of this section.
Theorem 6.
Let G be a locally finite graph and r\geqslant 3 an integer. The following are equivalent:
(i)
G is r -locally chordal.
(ii)
G admits a graph-decomposition into its maximal cliques which r -locally induces tree-decompositions.
(iii)
G admits a canonical graph-decomposition into cliques which r -locally induces tree-decompositions.
(iv)
G admits a graph-decomposition into cliques which r -locally induces tree-decompositions.
In general, the r -locally induced families (H^{v,r},\mathcal{V}^{v,r}) satisfy Item (H1).
For odd r , they even satisfy Item (H2), and thus are graph-decompositions.
But, for even r , the subgraphs of H^{v,r} induced on the co-bag of a vertex u\in N^{r/2}(v) are not necessarily connected.
Thus, the decomposition (H^{v,r},\mathcal{V}^{v,r}) does not in general satisfy Item (H2), and hence need not be graph-decompositions.
We show that every r -acyclic decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) r -locally induces tree-decompositions. In fact, something even stronger is true. A decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) is connected- r -acyclic if, for every connected vertex-subset X of G of at most r vertices, the union H_{X} is acyclic.
From the definition, every r -acyclic graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) is connected- r -acyclic. If (H,\mathcal{V}) is into cliques, then the converse holds as well:
Lemma 6.1.
Let (H,\mathcal{V}) be a graph-decomposition of a (possibly infinite) graph G into cliques, and let r\geqslant 1 be an integer.
Then (H,\mathcal{V}) is connected- r -acyclic if and only if (H,\mathcal{V}) is r -acyclic.
Proof.
It follows from the definition that every r -acyclic decomposition is connected- r -acyclic.
Conversely, assume that (H,\mathcal{V}) is connected- r -acyclic. Suppose that X is a vertex-subset of G such that H_{X} contains a cycle O .
By Lemma 2.2, the connected subgraph O of H is contained in H_{X^{\prime}} for some component G[X^{\prime}] of G[X] with X^{\prime}\subseteq X . Therefore, (H,\mathcal{V}) is r -acyclic if it is connected- r -acyclic.
∎
It turns out that connected- r -acyclic decompositions are precisely the ones that r -locally induce tree-decompositions:
Theorem 6.2.
Let (H,\mathcal{V}) be a graph-decomposition of a (possibly infinite) graph G and let r\geqslant 1 be an integer.
Then (H,\mathcal{V}) is connected- r -acyclic if and only if the decompositions it r -locally induces are tree-decompositions.
First, we characterize those graph-decomposition whose locally induced decompositions are tree-decompositions:
Lemma 6.3.
Let (H,\mathcal{V}) be a graph-decomposition of a (possibly infinite) graph G , and let r\geqslant 1 be an integer.
Then the decompositions that are r -locally induced by (H,\mathcal{V}) are tree-decompositions if and only if
•
r is odd and H_{N^{r/2}[v]} is a tree, or
•
r is even and H_{N^{r/2-1}[v]\cup\{u\}} is a tree for every u\in N^{r/2}(v) .
Proof.
Let v be a vertex of G , set B:=B_{r/2}(v) , and let (T,\mathcal{X}):=(H^{v,r},\mathcal{V}^{v,r}) be the decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) r -locally induces.
We warn the reader that we use terms such as bag, part and co-part in the context of the pair (T,\mathcal{X}) before knowing that (T,\mathcal{X}) is a graph-decomposition of B .
Let us make the following observations about the definition of (T,\mathcal{X}) , without assuming anything about (H,\mathcal{V}) or (T,\mathcal{X}) .
Since Item (H1) holds for (H,\mathcal{V}) , Item (H1) also holds for the pair (T,\mathcal{X}) with respect to B .
As (H,\mathcal{V}) r -locally induces (T,\mathcal{X}) ,
•
if r is odd, then the co-part T_{u} of a vertex u\in B_{r/2}(v) in (T,\mathcal{X}) is the co-part H_{u} of u in (H,\mathcal{V}) , and
•
if r is even, then the co-part T_{u} of a vertex u\in B_{r/2}(v) in (T,\mathcal{X}) is either H_{u} if u\in N^{r/2-1}[v] or H_{u}\cap T if u\in N^{r/2}(v) .
Hence, since Item (H2) holds for (H,\mathcal{V}) , to show Item (H2) for (T,\mathcal{X}) it suffices to prove that H_{u}\cap T is connected if r is even and u\in N^{r/2}(v) .
So let us assume that r is even; in particular, T=H_{N^{r/2-1}[v]} .
No matter which of the two assumptions about the decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) we make, the graph T=H_{N^{r/2-1}[v]} is a tree and every H_{u} is a tree for every vertex u of G .
Thus, consider u\in N^{r/2}(v) . By Lemma 3.10, H_{u}\cup T is a tree if and only if H_{u}\cap T is connected.
The latter is precisely Item (H2) of (T,\mathcal{X}) .
The former is the property described in the statement of Lemma 6.3.
Hence, the desired statement holds.
∎
Before we prove our next lemma, we give some notation around paths. For a path P , we denote the number of vertices of P by |P| and the number of edges of P by ||P|| . The length of P is the number of its edges, so if P has length \ell then |P|=\ell+1 . For a fixed vertex v of P , we denote by Pv the subpath of P starting at the first vertex of P and ending at v , and by vP the subpath of P starting at v and ending at the last vertex of P .
Lemma 6.4.
Let G be a (possibly infinite) graph, let r\geqslant 1 be an integer, and let U be a connected set of r vertices of G .
Then some vertex v\in U has distance at most r/2 from every u\in U and at most one u\in U has distance precisely r/2 from v .
In particular, if r is odd, then some vertex v\in U has distance less than r/2 from every u\in U .
Proof.
Let P be a longest induced path in the induced subgraph G[U] .
Let v be one of the ‘central’ vertices of P :
If ||P|| is even, then let v be the vertex such that ||vP||=||Pv|| .
Otherwise, ||P|| is odd, and we let v be the vertex such that ||vP||-1=||Pv|| .
In both cases, \ell:=||vP||\geqslant||Pv|| .
Let T be a spanning tree of G[U] containing P .
If some inclusion-maximal path Q in T that ends in v is longer than \ell , then at least one of QvP or PvQ is a path which is longer than P .
Since this contradicts the choice of P , we may assume that all leaves of T have distance at most \ell from v in T .
Hence, if \ell<r/2 , then the tree T witnesses that every u\in U has distance at most \ell<r/2 from v , as desired.
Thus, we assume ||vP||=\ell=r/2 ; in particular, r is even, since \ell is an integer.
Since P\subseteq G[U] , it follows that P has at most r vertices, so ||P||\leqslant r-1=r/2+(r/2-1) .
Hence, the choice of v yields ||Pv||=(r/2-1) , so V(P)=U .
Thus, the path P=T shows that the statement holds.
The in-particular part follows from the fact that distances in graphs are always integers.
∎
Proof of Theorem 6.2.
Assume that the decompositions r -locally induced by (H,\mathcal{V}) are tree-decompositions.
We claim that (H,\mathcal{V}) is connected- r -acyclic.
Indeed, let U be a connected set of at most r vertices of G .
Since U is connected in G , Lemma 6.4 yields some vertex v\in U that has distance at most r/2 from every u\in U but at most one of them has distance precisely r/2 .
Hence, H_{U} is acyclic by Lemma 6.3.
To prove the converse, assume that (H,\mathcal{V}) is connected- r -acyclic.
By induction on r\geqslant 1 , we show that the decompositions r -locally induced by (H,\mathcal{V}) are tree-decompositions by checking the equivalent properties described in Lemma 6.3.
Let v be a vertex of G .
The base case r=1 follows immediately from (H,\mathcal{V}) being connected- 1 -acyclic, as N^{1/2}[v]=\{v\} is connected.
For the induction step, we assume that r\geqslant 2 and the decompositions (r-1) -locally induced by (H,\mathcal{G}) are tree-decompositions.
Set d:=\lfloor r/2\rfloor , and d^{\prime}:=d-1 .
Set N^{\prime}:=N^{d^{\prime}}[v] .
By the induction assumption, H_{N^{\prime}} is a tree.
If r is even, then we set W:=\emptyset , N^{\ast}:=N^{\prime} , and H^{\ast}:=H_{N^{\ast}} .
If r is odd, then let W be any subset of N^{d}(v) such that, for N^{\ast}:=N^{\prime}\cup W , the union H^{\ast}:=H_{N^{\ast}} is a tree.
Let u\in N^{d}(v)\smallsetminus W be arbitrary.
To conclude the induction step, it suffices to show that H^{\ast}\cup H_{u} is acyclic, and thus a tree.
Suppose for a contradiction that H^{\ast}\cup H_{u} contains a cycle, i.e. H_{u} contains an H^{*} -path Q with ends h_{1},h_{2} .
Since u\in N^{d}(v) , there is u^{\prime}\in N^{\prime} so that uu^{\prime} is an edge of G , so H_{u}\cup H_{u^{\prime}} is a tree because (H,\mathcal{V}) is connected- r -acyclic for r\geqslant 2 .
Thus, the H^{*} -path Q in H_{u} yields that H_{u^{\prime}}\subseteq H^{\ast} avoids h_{i} for some i=1,2 .
As H_{u}\cup H_{u^{\prime}} is a tree, there is a Q – H_{u^{\prime}} path Q^{\prime} in H_{u} .
If H_{u^{\prime}} contains the other h_{j} with j=3-i , then we choose Q^{\prime} as the trivial path h_{j} .
The choice of Q^{\prime} guarantees that some h_{k} with k=1,2 , say h_{1} , is neither in H_{u^{\prime}} nor in Q^{\prime} .
Let v_{1}\in N^{*} with h_{1}\in H_{v_{1}} and fix a shortest v – v_{1} path P_{1} in G .
Fix a shortest v – u^{\prime} path P in G .
Since u^{\prime}\in N^{\prime} , it follows that |P|\leqslant d^{\prime}+1=d .
Similarly, since v_{1}\in N^{*} , it follows that |P_{1}|\leqslant d+1 if r is odd and |P_{1}|\leqslant d if r is even.
Since uu^{\prime} is an edge of G and V(P_{1})\cup V(P) is connected in G , the set U:=V(P_{1})\cup V(P)\cup\{u\} is also connected in G and has size at most
|V(P_{1})\cup V(P)\cup\{u\}|\leqslant|V(P_{1})|+|V(P)|-|\{v\}|+|\{u\}|\leqslant r.
As U\smallsetminus\{u\}=V(P_{1})\cup V(P) is connected in G , Lemma 2.1 yields that H_{U\smallsetminus\{u\}} is connected.
Let Q^{*} be the path in Q\cup Q^{\prime} that starts in h_{1} follows Q until it meets Q^{\prime} and then follows Q^{\prime} until it meets H_{U\smallsetminus\{u\}} .
Note that this will happen at latest at the end of Q^{\prime} in H_{u^{\prime}} , which is in H_{U\smallsetminus\{u\}} .
The choice of Q^{\prime} guarantees that Q^{*} is non-trivial, as Q^{\prime} does not contain h_{1} .
Hence, Q^{*} is an H_{U\smallsetminus\{u\}} -path in H_{u} , so Q^{*} closes to a cycle in H_{U} , which contradicts that (H,\mathcal{V}) is connected- r -acyclic.
∎
Proof of Theorem 6.
Let G be a graph and let (H,\mathcal{V}) be a graph-decomposition of G into cliques. By Lemma 6.1, (H,\mathcal{V}) is connected- r -acyclic if and only if it is r -acyclic. Furthermore, by Theorem 6.2, (H,\mathcal{V}) r -locally induces tree-decompositions if and only if it is r -acyclic. Now, Theorem 6 follows from Theorem 3.
∎
6.1. Locally deriving tree-decompositions into cliques
Since every r/2 -ball is chordal in an r -locally chordal graph, it is natural to hope that the tree-decompositions r -locally induced by r -acyclic graph-decompositions into cliques are themselves tree-decompositions into cliques, especially given the characterization in Lemma 6.3. If r is odd, then the r -locally induced decompositions are indeed into cliques if and only if (H,\mathcal{V}) is into cliques, since the (r/2) -balls are then induced subgraphs.
Unfortunately, if r is even, even though the decompositions r -locally induced by an r -acyclic graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) into cliques are tree-decompositions, they might not be into cliques.
But we can derive from (H,\mathcal{V}) tree-decompositions of its r/2 -balls into cliques, as follows.
First, let us observe the following corollary of Lemma 1.2:
Corollary 6.5.
Let v be a vertex in an (possibly infinite) r -locally chordal graph G , and let r\geqslant 2 be an even integer.
Then, for every vertex u\in N^{r/2}(v) , its neighborhood N(u)\cap N^{r/2-1}(v) in B_{r/2}(v) forms a clique. ∎
Let G be an r -locally chordal graph and let (H,\mathcal{V}) be an r -acyclic decomposition of G for r\geqslant 3 .
Assume now that r is even.
Fix a vertex v of G .
Obtain H^{v,r,*} from the disjoint union of H^{v,r-1} and one single node h_{u} for each u\in N^{r/2}(v) by adding a single edge f_{u}=h_{u}h_{u}^{\prime} where h_{u}^{\prime} is a node of H^{v,r-1} whose bag V^{v,r-1}_{h_{u}^{\prime}} contains the neighborhood N(u)\cap N^{r/2-1}(v) , which exists by Lemma 4.1, as N(u)\cap N^{r/2-1}(v) is a clique by Corollary 6.5.
Consider a node h of H^{v,r,*} .
If h is a node of H^{v,r-1} , then V^{v,r,*}_{h}:=V^{v,r-1}_{h} .
Otherwise, there exists a unique u\in N^{r/2}(v) such that h=h_{u} .
Then V^{v,r,*}_{h}:=\{u\}\cup(N(u)\cap N^{r/2-1}(v)) .
We refer to the decomposition (H^{v,r,*},\mathcal{V}^{v,r,*}) as a decomposition r -locally derived by (H,\mathcal{V}) .
If r is odd, then we refer to the decomposition r -locally induced by (H,\mathcal{V}) to a decomposition r -locally derived by (H,\mathcal{V}) .
Theorem 6.6.
Let (H,\mathcal{V}) be an r -acyclic graph-decomposition of a (possibly infinite) graph G into cliques with r\geqslant 3 .
Then the decompositions r -locally derived by (H,\mathcal{V}) are tree-decompositions into cliques.
Proof.
The decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) witnesses that G is r -locally chordal by Theorem 3.
Fix a vertex v of G .
If r is odd, then this follows from Theorem 6 and the definition of the decompositions r -locally induced by (H,\mathcal{V}) , as B_{r/2}(v) is an induced subgraph of G .
Thus, assume that r is even.
Since r-1 is odd, the decomposition (H^{v,r-1},\mathcal{V}^{v,r-1}) (r-1) -locally induced by (H,\mathcal{V}) is a tree-decomposition of B_{(r-1)/2}(v) into cliques.
It is immediate from the above construction of the decomposition (H^{v,r,*},\mathcal{V}^{v,r,*}) r -locally derived at v that it is a tree-decomposition of B_{r/2}(v) into cliques.
∎
7. Characterization via graph-decompositions that
locally induce local separations
A well-known key property of a tree-decomposition (T,\mathcal{V}) of a graph G is that every edge f of the decomposition tree T induces a separation s^{f} of G whose separator is the adhesion set V_{f} corresponding to f , see e.g. [8, Lemma 12.3.1]. Formally:
(T2′)
For every edge f=t_{1}t_{2} of T and for i=1,2 , set A^{f}_{i}\coloneqq\bigcup_{t\in V(T_{i})}V_{t} , where T_{i} is the component of T-f which contains t_{i} . Then {\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}{{s^{f}}}}\coloneqq\{A^{f}_{1},A^{f}_{2}\} is a separation of G and its separator A^{f}_{1}\cap A^{f}_{2} is the adhesion set V_{f}=V_{t_{1}}\cap V_{t_{2}} corresponding to f .
Indeed, it is well-known that a pair (T,\mathcal{V}) is a tree-decomposition if and only if it satisfies Item (H1) and Item (T2′).
A bond of a graph is an inclusion-minimal cut of the graph.
Note that the bonds of a tree T are precisely the edge-subsets of T consisting of single edges.
Analogous to Item (T2′), one may prove along the lines of the proof of [7, Lemma 3.4] that every bond F of the model graph H of a graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of G induces a separation s^{F} of G :
(H2′)
For every bond F=E_{G}(A_{1},A_{2}) of H and for i=1,2 , set A^{F}_{i}\coloneqq\bigcup_{h\in A_{i}}V_{h} .
Then {\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}{{s^{F}}}}\coloneqq\{A^{F}_{1},A^{F}_{2}\} is a separation of G and its separator A^{F}_{1}\cap A^{F}_{2} is precisely the union \bigcup_{f\in F}V_{f} of the adhesion sets V_{f}=V_{h_{1}}\cap V_{h_{2}} corresponding to edges f=h_{1}h_{2} in the bond F .
In view of Item (H2′), a graph-decomposition represents the structure of the underlying graph.
Indeed, a pair (H,\mathcal{V}) is a graph-decomposition if and only if it satisfies Item (H1) and Item (H2′) (for more details see [2]).
Given a graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of a graph G , the edges of H do not satisfy a property similar to Item (T2′) in general; if the model graph is not a tree, there is no reason to hope that single edges of H correspond to separations of G .
However, they do sometimes correspond to a local analog of separations. A recent paper [5] investigates the relationship between graph-decompositions and local separations.
It turns out that, since the graph-decompositions in our context are very well-structured, we can also prove that they interact nicely with local separations. The goal of this section is to make this interaction precise. We will at the end of the section be able to prove in Theorem 7 that r -locally chordal graphs are precisely those graphs which admit graph-decompositions whose edges induce r -local separations in an obvious way.
7.1. Additional properties of graph-decompositions.
Let (H,\mathcal{V}) be a graph-decomposition of G .
Note that, for a vertex-subset X of G , the graph H_{X}=\bigcup_{x\in X}H_{x} is the subgraph of H formed by those nodes h and those edges f=h_{0}h_{1} whose corresponding bags V_{h} and adhesion sets V_{f}=V_{h_{0}}\cap V_{h_{1}} meet X .
In the following, we consider a graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) which additionally satisfy
(H2T)
H_{v} is a tree for every vertex v of G , and
(H3)
H_{u}\cap H_{v}=H[\{h\in V(H)\mid u,v\in V_{h}\}] is connected for every edge uv of G .
We remark that both Item (H2T) and Item (H3) hold for every tree-decomposition. Item (H3) also holds for a graph-decomposition obtained from a tree-decomposition of G_{r} by folding via p_{r} [7, Paragraph after Lemma 3.9].
It turns out that the properties Item (H2T) and Item (H3) are equivalent to being connected-2-acyclic.
Lemma 7.1.
A graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of a (possibly infinite) graph G satisfies Item (H2T) and Item (H3) if and only if it is connected- 2 -acyclic.
Proof.
Item (H2T) says that the regions H_{v} are trees. Therefore, the equivalence follows from Lemma 3.10.
∎
Note that, for an honest graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) satisfying Item (H2T) and Item (H3), the only valid choice of co-parts H_{v} is the subgraphs H[W_{v}] induced by the co-bags W_{v} by Lemma 4.11.
7.2. Interplay: Graph-decompositions & local separations
We now formally introduce what a local separation from [5] is.
For a vertex-subset X of G , we denote the set of edges of G with exactly one end in X by \partial_{G}X .
A pre-separation of G is a set \{E_{0},X,E_{1}\} with X\subseteq V(G) and disjoint E_{0},E_{1} whose union is \partial_{G}X .
We remark that every separation \{A,B\} of G induces the pre-separation {\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}{{\pre(\{A,B\})}}}\coloneqq\{\partial_{G}(A\smallsetminus B),A\cap B,\partial_{G}(B\smallsetminus A)\} , see Figure 2a.
(a) A pre-separation induced by a separation.
(b) A local separation not induced by a separation.

Let X be a vertex-subset of G .
A walk which starts and ends in X but internally avoids X is an X -walk.
An r -local X -walk in G is an X -walk that is contained in a cycle of length at most r in G or consists of a single edge with both ends in X .
We remark that every reduced non-trivial r -local X -walk is either an X -path or a walk once around a cycle of length at most r that meets X precisely once.
A pre-separation \{E_{0},X,E_{1}\} of G is an r -local separation of G if there is no r -local X -walk which traverses an edge of E_{0} and an edge of E_{1} .1111
11
It is easy to see that this definition is equivalent to the one given in [5] but avoids the need of introducing r -local components. See Figure 2 for examples.
The pre-separations \pre(s) induced by separations s of G are r -local separations. We also recall the following useful fact about r -local separations.
Lemma 7.2.
Let G be a (possibly infinite) graph, r a positive integer, and \{E_{0},X,E_{1}\} an r -local separation of G . Then, a cycle of length at most r meets each of E_{0} and E_{1} an even number of times.
Proof.
Let O be a cycle in G of length at most r .
By symmetry, it suffices to show that O meets E_{1} an even number of times.
If O does not intersect X , then O does not meet E_{1} , as desired.
Otherwise, O meets X .
Then, the reduced non-trivial r -local X -walks in O partition the edge set of O .
As each of them meets E_{1} exactly zero or two times, O meets E_{1} an even number of times.
∎
Graph-decompositions are related to pre-separations in the following way.
Lemma 7.3.
Let (H,\mathcal{V}) be graph-decomposition of a (possibly infinite) graph G which satisfies Item (H2T) and Item (H3).
Fix an edge f=h_{0}h_{1} of H .
For every vertex v\in V_{f} , let H^{v}_{0},H^{v}_{1} be the components of H_{v}-f containing h_{0} and h_{1} , respectively, and set E^{v}_{i}\coloneqq\{vw\in\partial_{G}V_{f}\mid\exists h\in V(H^{v}_{i})\colon w\in V_{h}\} for i=0,1 .
Then E_{i}\coloneqq\bigcup_{v\in V_{f}}E_{i}^{v} forms a pre-separation \{E_{0},V_{f},E_{1}\} of G .
We think that it may be possible that satisfying Item (H2T) and Item (H3) is not the only way to guarantee that every edge of the model graph induces a pre-separation which has the potential to be a local separation.
However, in this paper, our constructed graph-decompositions always satisfy Item (H2T) and Item (H3).
Thus,
in the context of Lemma 7.3, the edge f induces the pre-separation \{E_{0},V_{f},E_{1}\} of G .
If \{E_{0},V_{f},E_{1}\} is an r -local separation of G , then f induces an r -local separation.
Proof.
Fix v\in V_{f} .
By Item (H1), every edge vw in \partial_{G}V_{f} is contained in the union of the two sets E^{v}_{0} and E^{v}_{1} .
It remains to show that E^{v}_{0} and E^{v}_{1} are disjoint.
Since H_{v} is a tree by Item (H2T), the two components H_{0}^{v} and H_{1}^{v} are distinct.
Consider an edge vw\in\partial_{G}V_{f} .
Since vw\in\partial_{G}V_{f} , it follows that w\not\in V_{f} , and thus that f is not in the tree H_{w} . Since (H,\mathcal{V}) satisfies Item (H3), we know that H_{w}\cap H_{v} is connected, so H_{w} meets exactly one of H_{1}^{v} and H_{0}^{v} . As edge vw was chosen arbitrarily, this proves that E_{0} and E_{1} are disjoint, and thus that \{E_{0},V_{f},E_{1}\} is a pre-separation of G .
∎
We are now ready to state the main result of this section. A graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of G induces r -local separations if (H,\mathcal{V}) satisfies Item (H2T) and Item (H3) and the pre-separation induced by each edge of H is an r -local separation of G .
Theorem 7.
Let G be a locally finite graph and r\geqslant 3 an integer.
The following are equivalent:
(i)
G is r -locally chordal.
(ii)
G admits a graph-decomposition into its maximal cliques which induces r -local separations.
(iii)
G admits a canonical graph-decomposition into cliques which induces r -local separations.
(iv)
G admits a graph-decomposition into cliques which induces r -local separations.
We conclude this subsection with a more precise statement regarding the structure of pre-separations in graphs with graph-decompositions into cliques which we will need later:
Lemma 7.4.
Let (H,\mathcal{V}) be a graph-decomposition of a (possibly infinite) graph G into cliques which satisfies Item (H2T) and Item (H3).
Suppose X\subseteq V(G) is inclusion-minimal such that H_{X} contains a cycle.
Then, G[X] is an induced cycle, and there exists an edge f in a cycle C in H_{X} with induced pre-separation \{E_{0},V_{f},E_{1}\} such that G[X] meets V_{f} in a single vertex whose two incident edges in G[X] are contained in distinct E_{i} .
Proof.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that H=H_{V(G)} , i.e. the decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) is honest, and thus the only valid choice for the co-parts are the induced subgraph H[W_{v}] by Lemma 4.11.
By Lemma 7.1, (H,\mathcal{V}) is connected-2-acyclic.
Let X\subseteq V(G) be inclusion-minimal such that H_{X} contains a cycle C .
By Lemma 5.6 (via Theorem 2), G[X] is an induced cycle of G .
Fix v\in X . Since X is inclusion-minimal with the property that H_{X} contains C , there is an edge f of C such that v is the unique vertex of X whose co-part H_{v} contains f , i.e. f\in E(C\cap H_{v})\smallsetminus E(H_{X-v}) , where X-v:=X\smallsetminus\{v\} . Consider the pre-separation \{E_{0},V_{f},E_{1}\} of G induced by f . Since f\not\in H_{x} for any x\in X-v , it follows that V_{f}\cap X=\{v\} , as the H_{x} are the induced subgraphs H[W_{v}] .
Let P be an inclusion-maximal connected subgraph of (C\cap H_{v})-E(H_{X-v}) containing f .
Since P\subseteq H_{v} and (H,\mathcal{V}) is connected-2-acyclic, it follows that P\neq C , so P is a subpath of C .
Let h_{0} and h_{1} be the ends of P .
By the choice of P , there are x_{0},x_{1}\neq v in X such that h_{i} is a node of H_{x_{i}} for i=0,1 .
Since (H,\mathcal{V}) is 2 -acyclic, x_{0}\neq x_{1} .
Now, up to symmetry, vx_{0}\in E_{0} and vx_{1}\in E_{1} . This completes the proof.
∎
7.3. Equivalence: r -acyclic & inducing local separations
Our main tool to prove Theorem 7 is an equivalence between being r -acyclic and inducing r -local separations for graph-decompositions into cliques. We begin by showing that every graph-decomposition which is connected- r -acyclic induces r -local separations.
Theorem 7.5.
Let G be a (possibly infinite) graph and r\geqslant 3 an integer. Suppose (H,\mathcal{V}) is a connected- r -acyclic graph-decomposition of G . Then (H,\mathcal{V}) induces r -local separations.
Proof.
Since r\geqslant 2 , not only is H_{v} a tree for every vertex v of G , i.e. (H,\mathcal{V}) satisfies Item (H2T), but also H_{u}\cup H_{v} is a tree for every edge uv of G , so (H,\mathcal{G}) satisfies Item (H3) by Lemma 3.10.
Let f=h_{1}h_{2} be an edge of H , and let \{E_{1},V_{f},E_{2}\} be the pre-separation induced by f from Lemma 7.3.
Consider an r -local V_{f} -walk W in G which contains at least one internal vertex.
Let v_{1},v_{2} be its ends, and let U be the set of vertices visited by W .
In particular, U is a connected set of at most r vertices.
Thus, since (H,\mathcal{V}) is r -connected-acyclic, H_{U} is a tree.
Let H_{i} be the component of H_{U}-f containing h_{i} for i=1,2 .
As U^{\prime}:=U\smallsetminus\{v_{1},v_{2}\} is connected in G , Lemma 2.1 yields that H_{U^{\prime}} is connected.
Since every u\in U^{\prime} is not in V_{f} , the edge f is not in its corresponding co-part H_{u} .
Hence, H_{U^{\prime}} meets precisely one of H_{1} and H_{2} .
In particular, all H_{u} with u\in U^{\prime} meet the same H_{i} .
Thus, as H_{i}^{v_{j}} (defined as in Lemma 7.3) is a subgraph of H_{i} for every j=1,2 by definition, the first and last edge of W are in the same E_{i} .
It follows that \{E_{1},V_{f},E_{2}\} is an r -local separation of G , as desired.
∎
Next, we show that the converse also holds when (H,\mathcal{V}) is into cliques.
Theorem 7.6.
Let G be a (possibly infinite) graph and r\geqslant 3 an integer.
A graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of G into cliques is r -acyclic if and only if it induces r -local separations.
Proof.
Let (H,\mathcal{V}) be a graph-decomposition of G into cliques.
Assume that (H,\mathcal{V}) is r -acyclic.
By Theorem 7.5, (H,\mathcal{V}) induces r -local separations.
Conversely, assume that (H,\mathcal{V}) induces r -local separations.
Suppose for a contradiction that (H,\mathcal{V}) is not r -acyclic.
Then there is a set X of at most r vertices such that H_{X}=\bigcup_{x\in X}H_{x} contains a cycle.
We may choose X to be inclusion-minimal.
By Lemma 7.4, there is an edge f in a cycle O of H_{X} with its induced pre-separation \{E_{0},V_{f},E_{1}\} such that the induced cycle C\coloneqq G[X] meets V_{f} in a single vertex y and its two incident edges e_{0},e_{1} on C satisfy e_{i}\in E_{i} for i=0,1 .
Since X meets V_{f} only in y and |X|\leqslant r , a closed walk W once around C based at y is an r -local V_{f} -walk.
Then the two edges e_{0},e_{1} incident to y on C are contained in the same E_{i} of the r -local separation \{E_{0},V_{f},E_{1}\} , which is a contradiction.
∎
7.4. Proof of Theorem 7
We can now prove the main result of this section.
Proof of Theorem 7.
By Theorem 3, G is r -locally chordal if and only if G admits an r -acyclic graph-decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) into cliques. By Theorem 7.6, (H,\mathcal{V}) induces r -local separations. By the definition, (H,\mathcal{V}) is canonical if and only if v\mapsto H_{v} is canonical. By Theorem 3, we may additionally assume that (H,\mathcal{V}) is either canonical or into maximal cliques. This completes the proof.
∎
8. Computing clique graphs
In Theorem 3, we prove that a locally finite graph G is r -locally chordal if and only if G has an r -acyclic clique graph. In this section, we provide a simple and efficient algorithm to compute r -acyclic clique graphs of finite r -locally chordal graphs. This algorithm is a generalization of a well-known algorithm to compute clique trees of chordal graphs and a parallelization of Gavril’s construction to characterize wheel-free graphs [11].
The algorithm in particular finds a spanning subgraph of the intersection graph \mathbf{K}(G) of the maximal cliques of G . For the rest of the paper, we equip the edges of \mathbf{K}(G) with weights according to the number of vertices in the intersection they represent; i.e. the weight of an edge K_{1}K_{2} is |K_{1}\cap K_{2}| .
Let \mathbf{K} be an edge-weighted graph.
The total weight w_{\mathbf{K}}(H) of a subgraph H of \mathbf{K} is the sum of the weights of the edges in H .
A maximum weight spanning tree of \mathbf{K} is a spanning tree of \mathbf{K} which has maximum total weight among all spanning trees of \mathbf{K} .
Maximum weight spanning trees of clique graphs are related to chordal graphs through the following theorem.
Theorem 8.1 ([13], Theorem 1).
Let G be a finite connected chordal graph.
Clique trees of G are precisely the maximum weight spanning trees of \mathbf{K}(G) .
Theorem 8.1 is especially useful since there is an efficient algorithm to compute maximum weight spanning trees:
Theorem 8.2 (Kruskal [23]).
There is an algorithm, Algorithm 8.3, to compute a maximum weight spanning tree of every given finite connected edge-weighted graph G on n vertices with m edges that runs in time O(m\log n) .
Algorithm 8.3 (Kruskal [23]).
Let H be a finite connected edge-weighted input graph.
(1)
Order the edges of H in order e_{1},\dots,e_{m} of decreasing weight.
(2)
Let T_{0} be the edgeless graph with vertex set V(H) .
(3)
For i from 1 to m , let T_{i}\coloneqq T_{i-1}+e_{i} if T_{i}+e_{i} is acyclic. Else, let T_{i}\coloneqq T_{i-1}
(4)
After step m , set T\coloneqq T_{m} .
In view of the previous three results, computing a clique tree of a chordal graph is equivalent to computing a maximum weight spanning tree of \mathbf{K}(G) . In order to assess the running time of such an algorithm, we need to understand the size of \mathbf{K}(G) .
Proposition 8.4 (Folklore, e.g. [11, 27]).
A chordal graph G on n vertices has at most n maximal cliques. Thus, \mathbf{K}(G) has at most \binom{n}{2}\leqslant n^{2} edges.
All in all, one derives the following from Theorem 8.1, 8.2 and Proposition 8.4.
Theorem 8.5.
Given a finite connected chordal graph G on n vertices, computing \mathbf{K}(G) and running Algorithm 8.3 on \mathbf{K}(G) yields a clique tree of G in running time O(n^{2}\log n) .
In this section, we provide a parallel algorithm that, when G is a finite r -locally chordal graph, computes an r -acyclic clique graph of G .
Theorem 8.
There is a parallel algorithm, Algorithm 9, to compute an r -acyclic clique graph of every given finite r -locally chordal graph G that runs on n=|V(G)| processors in time O(\Delta^{2}\log\Delta) , where \Delta is the maximum degree of G .
We remark that Gavril’s proof [14, Theorem 5] of Theorem 4 (i) \to (ii) is an algorithm with running time O(n^{3}\cdot\log n) .1212
12
In fact, Gavril claims that his algorithm has running time O(n^{3}) . As far as we understand his argument, it only yields O(n^{3}\cdot\log n) .
By Theorem 5, it suffices to consider the case r=3 .
Our algorithm is essentially a parallel version of Gavril’s.
It works as follows.
Algorithm 9.
Let G be a 3 -locally chordal (equivalently: wheel-free) input graph on [n] .
Run on processor v\in[n] the following:
(1)
Compute the intersection graph \mathbf{K}(B_{3/2}(v)) of the maximal cliques of B_{3/2}(v) .
(2)
Order the edges of \mathbf{K}(B_{3/2}(v)) in order e^{v}_{1},\dots,e^{v}_{m(v)} of decreasing weight; decide ties of two edges by the lexicographic order on the union of their respective ends.
(3)
Let H^{v}_{0} be the edgeless graph with vertex set V(\mathbf{K}(B_{3/2}(v)) .
(4)
For i from 1 to m(v) , let H^{v}_{i}\coloneqq H^{v}_{i-1}+e^{v}_{i} if H^{v}_{i-1}+e^{v}_{i} is acyclic. Else, let H^{v}_{i}\coloneqq H^{v}_{i-1} .
(5)
After step m(v) , set H^{v}\coloneqq H^{v}_{m(v)} .
After all n processor are finished, set H\coloneqq\bigcup_{v\in V(G)}H^{v} .
8.1. Analysis of the algorithm
Algorithm 9 essentially works by running Kruskal’s algorithm to find a clique tree of B_{3/2}(v) for every v\in V(G) simultaneously. In this section we prove that this algorithm indeed returns an r -acyclic clique graph when the input graph is r -locally chordal.
For this, by Theorem 5, we only need to ensure that the spanning subgraph H of \mathbf{K}(G) returned by Algorithm 9 is a 3 -acyclic clique graph.
Indeed, it suffices to show that H is a 2 -acyclic clique graph due to the following lemma.
Lemma 8.6.
Let G be a (possibly infinite) graph.
Then every 2 -acyclic clique graph H of G is Helly, and thus 3 -acyclic.
Proof.
Let H be a 2 -acyclic clique graph of G , and let v\mapsto H_{v}\coloneqq H[K_{G}(v)] be its corresponding region representation of G .
By Lemma 3.6, the 2 -acyclic clique graph H is 3 -acyclic if H is Helly.
To show that H is Helly, let
X be a finite vertex-subset of G such that each two H_{x},H_{y} with x,y\in X intersect.
In particular, X is a clique of G .
Let K be a maximal clique of G containing X , which is thus a node of every H_{x} with x\in X .
Hence, Lemma 3.7 (i) yields that H_{X} is a tree.
Therefore, x\mapsto H_{x} is a subtree representation of the complete graph G[X] over the tree H_{X} .
By Theorem 5.3, \bigcap_{x\in X}H_{x} is nonempty.
Therefore, the 2 -acyclic clique graph H is Helly, and thus 3 -acyclic.
∎
We note that we cannot push this further to 1 -acyclic due to the following example.
Example 8.7.
Consider the wheel W_{n} with n\geqslant 4 whose hub is v and whose rim is the cycle v_{1}v_{2}\dots v_{n}v_{1} .
Let G be the graph obtained from W_{n} by adding a vertex x and the edges xv_{i} for i=1,2 .
Now, G has a 1 -acyclic clique graph but not a 2 -acyclic clique graph. However, its induced subgraph W_{n} does not even have a 1 -acyclic clique graph.
Proof.
Claim 1.
The wheel W_{n} with n\geqslant 4 has no 1 -acyclic clique graph.
Proof.
The maximal cliques of W_{n} are D_{i}\coloneqq\{v,v_{i},v_{i+1}\} for i\in[n] , where v_{n+1}=v_{1} , so \mathbf{K}(W_{n}) is the cycle D_{1}D_{2}\dots D_{n}D_{1} .
Now consider a clique graph H of W_{n} .
Let i\in[n] . Consider D_{0} to be D_{n} .
Since H_{v_{i}}=H[K_{W_{n}}(v_{i})]=G[\{D_{i-1},D_{i}\}] is connected, the edge D_{i-1}D_{i} is in H .
Thus, H is the whole cycle \mathbf{K}(W_{n}) .
Since H_{v}=H[K_{W_{n}}(v)]=H , the clique graph H is not 1 -acyclic.
∎
Claim 2.
G has a 1 -acyclic clique graph.
Proof.
The maximal cliques of G are D_{1},\dots,D_{n} and also D\coloneqq\{x,v_{1},v_{2}\} .
Hence, the clique graph \mathbf{K}(G) is obtained from \mathbf{K}(W_{n}) by adding D and the edges DD_{n},DD_{1},D_{2} .
Let H be the spanning cycle DD_{1}D_{2}\dots D_{n}D .
We claim that H is a 1 -acyclic clique graph of G .
For i\in[n]\smallsetminus\{1,2\} , H_{v_{i}} consists of the single edge D_{i-1}D_{i} .
H_{v_{1}} is the path D_{n}DD_{1} , H_{v_{2}} is the path DD_{1}D_{2} , and H_{x} is the graph on the single vertex D .
Moreover, H_{v} is the path D_{1}D_{2}\dots D_{n} .
All in all, G is a 1 -acyclic clique graph.
∎
To see that G has no 2 -acyclic clique graph, one follows the argument presented in the proof of Claim 1 to show that a 1 -acyclic clique graph H of G contains all but one edge of \mathbf{K}(W_{n}) .
Thus, the union of H_{v} and either H_{v_{1}} or H_{v_{2}} forms a cycle, and thus H is not 2 -acyclic.
∎
The next lemma yields that the spanning subgraph H of \mathbf{K}(G) returned by Algorithm 9 is 2 -acylic, if the parallel runs of the algorithm make the same decision about whether to contain an edge.
Lemma 8.8.
Let G be a (possibly infinite) graph and
let H be a spanning subgraph of \mathbf{K}(G) .
Then H is a 2 -acyclic clique graph of G if and only if H[K_{G}(v)] is a clique tree of B_{3/2}(v) for all vertices v of G .
Proof.
Set H_{v}\coloneqq H[K_{G}(v)] for every vertex v of G .
By Lemma 2.4, H_{u}\cap H_{v}=H_{v}[K_{B_{3/2}(v)}(u)] for every two vertices u,v of G .
Fix a vertex v of G . The vertex set of K_{G}(v) is exactly the set of maximal cliques containing v , which is also exactly the set of maximal cliques of B_{3/2}(v) . Therefore, H[K_{G}(v)] is a clique tree of B_{3/2}(v) if and only if H_{v} is a tree and H_{v}[K_{B_{3/2}(v)}(u)]=H_{u}\cap H_{v} is connected for every u\in B_{3/2}(v) . Since this holds for every vertex v of G , this is equivalent to requiring that H_{v} is a tree for every vertex v of G , i.e. H satisfies Item (H2T), and H_{v}\cap H_{u} is connected for every edge uv of G , i.e. H satisfies Item (H3). By Lemma 7.1, H is connected-2-acyclic if and only if H satisfies Item (H2T) and Item (H3). Since H_{u} and H_{v} intersect in a clique graph of G if and only if uv is an edge of G , it follows that a clique graph is 2-acyclic if and only if it is connected-2-acyclic. This completes the proof.
∎
Now, we combine the above observations to prove Theorem 8.
Proof of Theorem 8.
We claim that the spanning subgraph H outputted by Algorithm 9 is indeed an r -acyclic clique graph of G . Afterwards we discuss its running time.
Correctness.
As K_{G}(v)=K(B_{3/2}(v)) by Lemma 2.4 and thus \mathbf{K}(G)[K_{G}(v)]=\mathbf{K}(B_{3/2}(v)) , the graph H=\bigcup_{v\in V(G)}H^{v} is a spanning subgraph of \mathbf{K}(G)=\bigcup_{v\in V(G)}\mathbf{K}(G)[K_{G}(v)]=\bigcup_{v\in V(G)}\mathbf{K}(B_{3/2}(v)) .
Note that H^{v} is a spanning tree of H_{v}\coloneqq H[K_{G}(v)] , as H^{v} is obtained by running Kruskal’s algorithm to obtain a maximum weight spanning tree of \mathbf{K}(B_{3/2}(v)) .
In particular, H_{v} is connected.
Therefore, H is a clique graph.
It remains to show that H is r -acyclic.
As G is r -locally chordal, it suffices to show that H is 3 -acyclic by Theorem 5.
As, by Lemma 8.6, a clique graph is 2 -acyclic if and only if it is 3 -acyclic, Lemma 8.8 ensures that it suffices that H_{v}=H[K_{G}(v)] is a clique tree of B_{3/2}(v) .
As H^{v} is obtained by running Kruskal’s algorithm on \mathbf{K}(B_{3/2}(v)) , H^{v} is a clique tree of B_{3/2}(v) by Theorem 8.5.
Thus, we need to show that the subgraph H_{v} is indeed H^{v} .
This follows from the following.
Claim 1.
For every two vertices u,v of G , the graphs H^{u} and H^{v} agree on K_{G}(u)\cap K_{G}(v) .
Proof.
Order the edges of \mathbf{K}(G) in order e_{1},\dots,e_{m} of decreasing weight where we decide ties of two edges by the lexicographic order on the union of their respective ends.
Note that, for every vertex v of G , the order e_{1}^{v},\dots,e_{m(v)}^{v} is the order of the edges of \mathbf{K}(B_{3/2}(v))=\mathbf{K}(G)[K_{G}(v)] induced by e_{1},\dots,e_{m} .
For a vertex v of G and an index i=1,\dots,m , let I(i,v) be the maximum j=1,\dots,m(v) such that e_{1}^{v},\dots,e_{j}^{v} is a subsequence of e_{1},\dots,e_{i} .
Let u and v be two vertices of G .
If K_{G}(u)\cap K_{G}(v) is empty, then the claim holds.
Hence, we may assume that K_{G}(u) and K_{G}(v) meet.
We proceed by induction on i=0,1,\dots,m to show that H^{u}_{I(i,u)} and H^{v}_{I(i,v)} agree on K_{G}(u)\cap K_{G}(v) .
The base case i=0 holds by definition of H^{u}_{0} and H^{v}_{0} .
Suppose that H^{u}_{I(i,u)} and H^{v}_{I(i,v)} agree on K_{G}(u)\cap K_{G}(v) for some i=0,\dots,m-1 .
As we are otherwise done, we assume that e_{i+1} is an edge of \mathbf{K}(G)[K_{G}(u)\cap K_{G}(v)] , i.e. e_{i+1}=K_{1}K_{2} with u,v\in K_{j} for j=1,2 .
By symmetry, it suffices to show that if H^{u}_{I(i,u)}+e_{i+1} contains a cycle C , then H^{v}_{I(i,v)}+e_{i+1} contains the cycle C , as well.
Note that the K_{1} – K_{2} path P\coloneqq C-e_{i+1} is in H^{u}_{I(i,u)} .
If P\subseteq\mathbf{K}(G)[K_{G}(u)\cap K_{G}(v)] , then P is in H^{v}_{I(i,v)} by induction assumption, and thus C\subseteq H^{v}_{I(i,v)}+e_{i+1} .
In the following we show that P\subseteq\mathbf{K}(G)[K_{G}(u)\cap K_{G}(v)] .
Since H^{u}_{I(i,u)} is by definition a subgraph of the tree H^{u} , the unique K_{1} – K_{2} path in the spanning tree H^{u} of \mathbf{K}(G)[K_{G}(u)] is P .
Recall that H^{u} is even a clique tree of B_{3/2}(u) by Theorem 8.5.
Therefore,
H^{u}_{v}\coloneqq H^{u}[K_{B_{3/2}(u)}(v)]=H^{u}[K_{G}(u)\cap K_{G}(v)]\subseteq\mathbf{K}(G)[K_{G}(u)\cap K_{G}(v)]
is a subtree of H^{u} , which contains the nodes K_{1} and K_{2} .
In particular, the unique K_{1} – K_{2} path in H^{u}_{v} agrees with the unique K_{1} – K_{2} path P in H^{u} .
So, P\subseteq\mathbf{K}(G)[K_{G}(u)\cap K_{G}(v)] , as desired.
∎
Running time.
Let v be a vertex of G .
As B_{3/2}(v) contains d(v)+1 many vertices and d(v) is at most the maximum degree \Delta , Theorem 8.5 yields that the algorithm on processor v has running time O(\Delta^{2}\log\Delta) .

8.2. Clique graphs as maximum weight spanning subgraphs
Let G be a graph.
A spanning subgraph H of \mathbf{K}(G) is r -acyclic if (H_{v}\mid v\in V(G)) for H_{v}\coloneqq H[K_{G}(v)] is an r -acyclic family of subgraphs of H .
A maximum weight r -acyclic spanning subgraph of \mathbf{K}(G) is an r -acyclic spanning subgraph H of \mathbf{K}(G) that has maximum total weight among all r -acyclic spanning subgraphs of \mathbf{K}(G) . It turns out that, just as clique trees are precisely maximum weight spanning trees of \mathbf{K}(G) (Theorem 8.1), r -acyclic clique graphs are precisely the maximum weight r -acyclic spanning subgraphs of \mathbf{K}(G) . Specifically, we prove:
Theorem 10.
Let G be a finite r -locally chordal graph for an integer r\geqslant 3 . Let H be a subgraph of \mathbf{K}(G) .
Then the following are equivalent:
(i)
H is an r -acyclic clique graph of G .
(ii)
H is a maximum weight 2 -acyclic spanning subgraph of \mathbf{K}(G) .
(iii)
H is a 2 -acyclic spanning subgraph of \mathbf{K}(G) of weight W(G)\coloneqq(\sum_{K\in K(G)}|K|)-|V(G)| .
First, we need the following lemma.
Lemma 8.9.
Let G be a finite graph. Suppose that H is a 1 -acyclic spanning subgraph of \mathbf{K}(G) .
Then the total weight of H is at most W(G)(\coloneqq(\sum_{K\in K(G)}|K|)-|V(G)|) .
Moreover, equality holds if and only if H is a clique graph of G .
Proof.
Recall that an acyclic graph T on n vertices has at most n-1 edges.
Using this, the desired inequality follows from double counting the total weight w(H) :
\displaystyle\sum_{K_{1}K_{2}\in E(H)}|K_{1}\cap K_{2}|
\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V(G)}|E(H[K_{G}(v)])|
\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{v\in V(G)}(|K_{G}(v)|-1)
\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{K\in K(G)}|K|\right)-|V(G)|=W(G).
Recall that a spanning subgraph H of \mathbf{K}(G) is a clique graph of G if and only if all H[K_{G}(v)] are connected
Moreover, it is well-known that an acyclic graph T on n vertices is connected if and only if T has n-1 edges (see e.g. [8, Corollary 1.5.2]).
Thus, equality holds if and only if H[K_{G}(v)] is connected for every vertex v of G .
∎
Proof of Theorem 10.
First, consider an r -acyclic clique graph H of G .
By Lemma 8.8, the induced subgraph H[K_{G}(v)] are in particular trees for every vertex v of G .
Thus, H has total weight W(G) by Lemma 8.9.
Now, let H be a maximum weight 2 -acyclic spanning H subgraph of \mathbf{K}(G) .
As G is r -locally chordal, G has r -acyclic clique graphs by Theorem 3.
Thus, the above shows that the weight of H is at least W(G) .
As H is in particular 1 -acyclic, Lemma 8.9 yields that the weight of H is at most W(G) , and thus precisely W(G) .
Then the moreover-part of Lemma 8.9 yields that H is a clique graph.
So, H is a 2 -acylic clique graph, and thus H is 3 -acyclic by Lemma 8.6.

9. Future work
In this section, we detail several directions for future work about locally chordal graphs.
9.1. Bounding the number of maximal cliques
One property of finite chordal graphs is that they have at most n maximal cliques (Proposition 8.4), where n is the number of their vertices. We can thus easily deduce that every r -locally chordal graph G for r\geqslant 3 has at most n^{2} maximal cliques by observing that B_{3/2}(v) is chordal for every v\in V(G) and that every maximal clique of G lives in B_{3/2}(v) for some v . However, we suspect that a better bound should hold for the number of maximal cliques of r -locally chordal graphs, where the bound depends on r and approaches n as r tends to infinity. Specifically, we conjecture the following:
Conjecture 9.1.
For every \varepsilon>0 , there exists an integer r\geqslant 0 such that the number of maximal cliques in finite r -locally chordal graphs G is O(|V(G)|^{1+\varepsilon}) .
An extremal case of r -locally chordal graphs are those whose r/2 -balls are trees, i.e. precisely the graphs of girth >r .
Then Conjecture 9.1 holds, as the number of maximal cliques coincides with the number of edges in connected graphs G of girth >r , which is at most n^{1+\frac{2}{r-2}} if the graph G has n vertices and at least 2n edges [4, §III].
9.2. Algorithmic applications
One benefit of structural decompositions like tree-decompositions is that they enable the design of algorithms for graphs with “good” decompositions. Indeed, tree-decompositions play a large role in algorithmic graph theory. In Algorithm 9, we give an efficient algorithm to compute r -acyclic graph-decompositions into the maximal cliques (equivalently: r -acyclic clique graphs) of r -locally chordal graphs, which are structural descriptions that generalize tree-decomposition into the maximal cliques (equivalently: clique trees). Therefore, a very interesting follow-up question is whether these representations can be used in the design of efficient algorithms for locally chordal graphs.
One of the most successful algorithmic applications of chordal graphs is to the maximum independent set (MIS) problem. The MIS problem asks, given a graph G (and possibly a weight function w:V(G)\to\mathbb{N} of the vertices), to return a maximum-size (or maximum-weight) independent set of G . MIS is a classic NP-hard problem in general [22], and is even hard to approximate in general [18]. However, there is a linear-time algorithm to solve MIS in chordal graphs [11]. One can also solve MIS in chordal graphs using standard dynamic programming on a tree-decomposition into cliques. This does not require that the chordal graphs have bounded treewidth, because independent sets intersect cliques, and thus bags of tree-decompositions, in at most one vertex. The nice interplay between chordal graphs and the MIS problem has inspired several other approaches to and perspectives on the MIS problem, for example the PMC method [24, 10]and the width-parameter tree-independence number [6].
Since chordal graphs and MIS are such a fruitful pair, the MIS problem is a promising potential algorithmic application of locally chordal graphs. Our decompositions likewise have the property that they are into cliques, so a solution to MIS intersects each bag in at most one vertex. Since r -locally chordal graphs include, in particular, all graphs of girth greater than r , the class of graphs of high girth witnesses that MIS is NP-hard even in r -locally chordal graphs [26]. However, we hope for an affirmative answer to the following.
Question 9.2.
Is there a good approximation algorithm for Maximum Independent Set in r -locally chordal graphs?
There are decent approximation algorithms for MIS in high-girth graphs, e.g. [25]. We hope that these algorithms can be modified to give approximation algorithms in locally chordal graphs given their r -acyclic graph-decompositions (H,\mathcal{V}) into cliques, by using the high-girth model graph H (on which the high-girth algorithms can be leveraged) and the fact that all bags are cliques (which intersect independent sets in at most one vertex).
9.3. Stronger Helly properties
Let H be a graph and let \mathbf{H} be a family of subgraphs of H . The family \mathbf{H} has the Helly-Erdős-Pósa property if, for every k\geqslant 2 , every finite subfamily \mathbf{H}^{\prime} of \mathbf{H} contains either k pairwise disjoint sets or there is a vertex-subset of H of size at most k-1 that meets every subgraph of \mathbf{H}^{\prime} . Observe that when k=2 this corresponds to the Helly property defined in this paper, so the Helly-Erdős-Pósa property can be viewed as a generalization of the Helly property.
It is known that if T is a tree and \mathbf{T} is a family of subtrees of T , then \mathbf{T} satisfies the Helly-Erdős-Pósa property, see [15]. Therefore, if G is chordal, then the families corresponding to acyclic region representations of G (i.e. if G is connected, the subtree representations of G ) satisfy the Helly-Erdős-Pósa property.
For r -locally chordal graphs, we proved that the families corresponding to their r -acyclic region representations satisfy the Helly property. A natural extension of this result is thus to ask whether locally chordal graphs, like chordal graphs, also satisfy the Helly-Erdős-Pósa property:
Question 9.3.
Let r\geqslant 3 be an integer and G an r -locally chordal graph. For an r -acyclic region representation v\mapsto H_{v} of G , does the family (H_{v}\mid v\in V(G)) satisfy the Helly-Erdős-Pósa property?
We thank Piotr Micek for suggesting Question 9.3 in private communication.
9.4. Local-global width parameters
Chordal graphs can be used to define treewidth: the treewidth of a graph G is (one less than) the minimum clique number of a chordal supergraph of G . Can we use r -locally chordal graphs in a similar way as inspiration to define some width parameter?
A large part of the contribution of this paper is studying the various properties and behavior of specific graph-decompositions. We identified four key properties of graph-decompositions: r -acyclic, connected- r -acyclic, r -locally acyclic, and inducing r -local separations. From this paper, we know the following relationships between these properties:
Theorem 9.4.
Let (H,\mathcal{V}) be a graph-decomposition of a graph G , and let r\geqslant 1 be an integer. Then the following statements hold:
(i)
If (H,\mathcal{V}) is r -acyclic, then (H,\mathcal{V}) is connected- r -acyclic.
(ii)
(H,\mathcal{V}) is connected- r -acyclic if and only if (H,\mathcal{V}) is r -locally acyclic.
(iii)
If (H,\mathcal{V}) is connected- r -acyclic and r\geqslant 2 , then (H,\mathcal{V}) induces r -local separations.
Proof.
Item (i) is a consequence of the definitions. Item (ii) is Theorem 6.2. Item (iii) is Theorem 7.5.
∎
We can use these properties to introduce three new graph width parameters. Let G be a graph. The width of a decomposition (H,\mathcal{V}) of G is the maximum size of its bags. Now:
(i)
The r -acyclic width of G , denoted \hbox{\rm aw}_{r}(G) , is the minimum width of an r -acyclic graph-decomposition of G .
(ii)
The connected- r -acyclic width of G , denoted \hbox{\rm caw}_{r}(G) , is the minimum width of a connected- r -acyclic graph-decomposition of G .
(iii)
The r -local-separation width of G , denoted \hbox{\rm lsw}_{r}(G) , is the minimum width of a graph-decomposition of G that induces r -local separations.
We also define the r -local treewidth of G , denoted \hbox{\rm ltw}_{r}(G) , as the maximum treewidth of an r/2 -ball of G plus one. Together, we refer to these parameters as the local-global width parameters.
The following relationship between the local-global width parameters is immediate from the definitions.
Theorem 9.5.
For every graph G and r\geqslant 3 , we have:
\tw(G)\geqslant\hbox{\rm aw}_{r}(G)\geqslant\hbox{\rm caw}_{r}(G)\geqslant\hbox{\rm lsw}_{r}(G),\hbox{\rm ltw}_{r}(G).
Studying the behavior of these width parameters on different graph classes and for different values of r presents an intriguing research direction. To our knowledge, the local-global width parameters are the first width parameter to be based on graph-decompositions more general than tree-decompositions. This is a promising development: since graph-decompositions are structurally different than tree-decompositions, we can go beyond the tree-based width parameters (which are often closely related to treewidth), but graph-decompositions still offer a decomposition framework, which is useful for studying graph structure.
The local-global width parameters in particular have the potential to interact in interesting ways with the local complexity of a graph. For example, to what extent is the following statement true: the local-global width parameters are large for a given graph class if and only if the graphs in that class have dense local structure? We can also ask about how the local-global width parameters interact with other measures of the local complexity of graph structure. A good starting place for any of these ideas is the following:
Question 9.6.
Which natural graph classes have bounded (or unbounded) local-global width parameters?
