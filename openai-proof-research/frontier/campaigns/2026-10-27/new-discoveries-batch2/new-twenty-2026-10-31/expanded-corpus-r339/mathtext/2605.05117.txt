On immanants of Cayley tables
Xuan Wang, Hanbin Zhang
Address: School of Mathematics (Zhuhai), Sun Yat-sen University, Zhuhai 519082, Guangdong, P.R. China
Email address: wangx728@mail2.sysu.edu.cn, zhanghb68@mail.sysu.edu.cn
Abstract.
Let G be a finite abelian group of order n and let \mathcal{M}_{G}=(x_{a+b})_{a,b\in G}
be the Cayley table of G . Let \operatorname{imm}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{M}_{G}) be the immanant of \mathcal{M}_{G} with respect to a partition \lambda and \mathcal{I}_{\lambda}(G) be the number of formally different monomials occurring in \operatorname{imm}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{M}_{G}) (in particular, we denote by \mathcal{P}(G) (resp. \mathcal{D}(G) ) for the corresponding quantity for \operatorname{per}(\mathcal{M}_{G}) (resp. \det(\mathcal{M}_{G}) ) for simplicity). The study of \mathcal{P}(G) and \mathcal{D}(G) lies at the intersection of algebraic combinatorics and additive combinatorics. In this paper, we prove the following results.
(1)
If |G| is a prime power, then
\mathcal{P}(G)=\mathcal{D}(G).
(2)
If |G| is odd, then
\mathcal{I}_{(n-1,1)}(G)=\mathcal{I}_{(2,1^{n-2})}(G)=0,
and that if |G|\equiv 2\pmod{4} , then
\mathcal{I}_{(n-1,1)}(G)=\mathcal{P}(G)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{I}_{(2,1^{n-2})}(G)=\mathcal{D}(G).
(3)
If |G| is odd and |G|\geq 7 , then
\operatorname{imm}_{(4,1^{n-4})}(\mathcal{M}_{G})=\operatorname{imm}_{(2,2,2,1^{n-6})}(\mathcal{M}_{G}).
Key words and phrases: immanants, Cayley table, finite abelian group, zero-sum sequence, Specht module, Hall fiber, group determinant
2020 Mathematics Subject Classification05E10, 15A15, 05A15, 20K01, 11B75
1. Introduction
For an n\times n matrix \mathcal{M}=(m_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq n} , its immanant with respect to a partition \lambda is defined as
\mathsf{imm}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{M})=\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\chi^{\lambda}(\tau)\prod_{i=1}^{n}m_{i,\tau(i)}.
Note that, determinant (resp. permanent) is the immanant corresponding to the sign character (resp. the trivial character):
\operatorname{imm}_{(1^{n})}(\mathcal{M})=\det(\mathcal{M}),\qquad\operatorname{imm}_{(n)}(\mathcal{M})=\operatorname{per}(\mathcal{M}).
The prehistory of immanants goes back to Schur’s work on Hermitian forms and characters, he considered generalized matrix functions associated with group characters and proved fundamental inequalities for positive semidefinite Hermitian matrices; see [33, 24]. The word and systematic study of immanants entered the literature through Littlewood and Richardson’s work [20, 21], their papers placed immanants in the representation theory of symmetric groups and in the emerging language of symmetric functions. Classical works on immanants developed along several directions: matrix inequalities and the permanental-dominance program [23, 19, 25, 27, 38]; combinatorial and symmetric-function formulas for Jacobi–Trudi and related matrices [7, 8, 9, 35]; and positivity phenomena on totally positive or totally nonnegative matrices [37, 30, 28, 17].
Subsequent work connected immanants with Hecke algebras, Kazhdan–Lusztig theory, Temperley–Lieb quotients, and quantum matrix identities [10, 29, 30, 31, 15]; with probabilistic models such as finite point processes [5]; and with computational complexity, where determinant and permanent sit at opposite ends of a sharp complexity landscape for immanant families [12, 4]. Recent contributions continue to enlarge this circle of ideas, for instance through new hook-shape character formulas and quasisymmetric refinements [16, 2]. In short, immanants present a rich interplay between representation theory, algebraic combinatorics, matrix theory, probability, and complexity.
In this paper, we study a highly structured specialization of the immanant problem, namely the Cayley table of a finite abelian group. Let G be a finite abelian group of order n , written additively, and put
\mathcal{M}_{G}=(x_{a+b})_{a,b\in G},
where x_{g} (g\in G) are commuting indeterminates. For a partition \lambda\vdash n , let \chi^{\lambda} denote the irreducible character of \mathfrak{S}_{n} indexed by \lambda . After identifying the rows and columns with G , we may write
\operatorname{imm}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{M}_{G})=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(G)}\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\prod_{a\in G}x_{a+\sigma(a)}.
All characters are understood through the natural identification of \mathfrak{S}(G) with \mathfrak{S}_{n} ; character values therefore depend only on cycle type.
The study of \det(\mathcal{M}_{G}) can be traced back to seminal works of Dedekind and Frobenius, which led to the foundation of representation theory of groups; see [14].
In 1952, with an elegant constructive approach, Hall [11] studied \operatorname{per}(\mathcal{M}_{G}) and proved the following interesting result.
Theorem 1.1.
([11])
Let G=\{g_{0},\cdots,g_{n-1}\} be an abelian group of order n with g_{0} the identity. Then the monomial x_{g_{0}}^{\lambda_{0}}x_{g_{1}}^{\lambda_{1}}\cdots x_{g_{n-1}}^{\lambda_{n-1}} occurs in \operatorname{per}(\mathcal{M}_{G}) if and only if
\lambda_{0}+\cdots+\lambda_{n-1}=n\quad\text{and}\quad{\lambda_{0}}g_{0}+{\lambda_{1}}g_{1}+\cdots+{\lambda_{n-1}}g_{n-1}=g_{0},
where, for any 0\leq j\leq n-1 , \lambda_{j}g_{j}\coloneqq g_{j}+\cdots+g_{j} ( \lambda_{j} times).
Note that, a sequence S=g_{0}^{\lambda_{0}}g_{1}^{\lambda_{1}}\cdots g_{n-1}^{\lambda_{n-1}} satisfying {\lambda_{0}}g_{0}+{\lambda_{1}}g_{1}+\cdots+{\lambda_{n-1}}g_{n-1}=g_{0} is called a zero-sum sequence over G of length n , which is an important object in additive combinatorics.
Let \mathcal{I}_{\lambda}(G) be the number of formally different monomials occurring in \operatorname{imm}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{M}_{G}) . In particular, we denote by \mathcal{P}(G) (resp. \mathcal{D}(G) ) for the corresponding quantity for \operatorname{per}(\mathcal{M}_{G}) (resp. \det(\mathcal{M}_{G}) ) for simplicity. For example, for
\mathcal{M}_{C_{3}}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{0}&x_{1}&x_{2}\\
x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{0}\\
x_{2}&x_{0}&x_{1}\end{pmatrix},
we have \mathsf{det}(\mathcal{M}_{C_{3}})=-x_{0}^{3}-x_{1}^{3}-x_{2}^{3}+3x_{0}x_{1}x_{2} and \mathsf{per}(\mathcal{M}_{C_{3}})=x_{0}^{3}+x_{1}^{3}+x_{2}^{3}+3x_{0}x_{1}x_{2} , which implies that \mathcal{P}(C_{3})=\mathcal{D}(C_{3})=4 . Due to possible cancellation, we have \mathcal{D}(G)\leq\mathcal{P}(G) . Li and Zhang [18] proved that, for finite abelian groups G and H , \mathcal{P}(G)=\mathcal{P}(H) if and only if G\cong H . Using symmetric functions, Thomas [39] proved that, if n is a prime power, \mathcal{D}(C_{n})=\mathcal{P}(C_{n}) . Later, Colarte, Mezzetti, Miró-Roig, and Salat proved the converse statement, i.e., \mathcal{D}(C_{n})=\mathcal{P}(C_{n}) only if n is a prime power; see [3]. Later Panyushev [26] proposed the following problem.
Problem 1.2.
What are necessary/sufficient conditions on a finite abelian group G for the equality \mathcal{D}(G)=\mathcal{P}(G) ? Specifically, is it still true that the condition ‘ |G| is a
prime power’ is sufficient?
Our first main result is the following.
Theorem 1.3.
Let G be a finite abelian group and |G| is a prime power, then we have
\mathcal{P}(G)=\mathcal{D}(G).
Recently, Wang, Zhang, and Zhang [40] initiated a systematic study of general immanants of \mathcal{M}_{G} and formulated several conjectures for cyclic groups:
Conjecture 1.4.
(1)
For any n\geq 3 , if n is odd, then \mathcal{I}_{(n-1,1)}(C_{n})=\mathcal{I}_{(2,1^{n-2})}(C_{n})=0 .
(2)
For any n\geq 2 , if n\equiv 2\pmod{4} , then \mathcal{I}_{(n-1,1)}(C_{n})=\mathcal{P}(C_{n}) and \mathcal{I}_{(2,1^{n-2})}(C_{n})=\mathcal{D}(C_{n}) .
(3)
For any n\geq 7 , if n is odd, then \mathcal{I}_{(n-2,1,1)}(C_{n})=\mathcal{P}(C_{n}) .
Our second main result proves (1) and (2) of Conjecture 1.4 in a generalized version.
Theorem 1.5.
Let G be a finite abelian group.
(1)
If |G| is odd, then
\mathcal{I}_{(n-1,1)}(G)=\mathcal{I}_{(2,1^{n-2})}(G)=0.
(2)
If |G|\equiv 2\pmod{4} , then
\mathcal{I}_{(n-1,1)}(G)=\mathcal{P}(G)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{I}_{(2,1^{n-2})}(G)=\mathcal{D}(G).
Based on some numerical experiments via SageMath, we observed several interesting phenomena concerning \operatorname{imm}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{M}_{G}) for general \lambda and we shall prove the following neat result, which shows that two seemingly unrelated immanants (with respect to non-conjugate partitions) are identical.
Theorem 1.6.
Let G be a finite abelian group with |G| odd and |G|\geq 7 , then
\operatorname{imm}_{(4,1^{n-4})}(\mathcal{M}_{G})=\operatorname{imm}_{(2,2,2,1^{n-6})}(\mathcal{M}_{G}).
The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we study \mathcal{P}(G) and \mathcal{D}(G) . In Section 3, we consider \operatorname{imm}_{(n-1,1)}(\mathcal{M}_{G}) and \operatorname{imm}_{(2,1^{n-2})}(\mathcal{M}_{G}) . In Section 4, we prove Theorem 1.6. We end this paper with some concluding remarks.
2. Determinant and permanent supports
In this section, we consider the relation between \mathcal{D}(G) and \mathcal{P}(G) , and provide the proof of Theorem 1.3.
It is convenient to use the Toeplitz group matrix
A_{G}=(x_{a-b})_{a,b\in G}.
The matrices A_{G} and \mathcal{M}_{G} differ by the column permutation b\mapsto-b , so they have the same determinant and permanent supports.
Let S=g_{1}\cdots g_{n} be a sequence over G of length n . If \alpha_{g} is the multiplicity of g in S , define the labelled determinant coefficient
\widetilde{c}_{G}(S)=\left(\prod_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}!\right)[x_{g_{1}}\cdots x_{g_{n}}]\det(A_{G}).
Let \Pi_{0}(S) be the set of set partitions \pi of \{1,\ldots,n\} such that every block is zero-sum:
\sum_{i\in B}g_{i}=0\qquad(B\in\pi).
Theorem 2.1.
For every sequence S over G of length n ,
(2.1)
\widetilde{c}_{G}(S)=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{0}(S)}(-1)^{n-|\pi|}n^{|\pi|}\prod_{B\in\pi}(|B|-1)!.
Proof.
The abelian group determinant factorization gives
\det(A_{G})=\prod_{\chi\in\widehat{G}}\left(\sum_{g\in G}\chi(g)x_{g}\right).
Therefore
\widetilde{c}_{G}(S)=\sum_{\theta:\{1,\ldots,n\}\to\widehat{G}\,\text{bijection}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\theta(i)(g_{i}).
Impose bijectivity by Möbius inversion on the partition lattice \Pi_{n} . The Möbius function from the discrete partition \hat{0} (the minimal element) to \pi is
\mu(\hat{0},\pi)=(-1)^{n-|\pi|}\prod_{B\in\pi}(|B|-1)!.
Thus
\widetilde{c}_{G}(S)=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{n}}\mu(\hat{0},\pi)\prod_{B\in\pi}\left(\sum_{\chi\in\widehat{G}}\chi\left(\sum_{i\in B}g_{i}\right)\right).
By the orthogonality of characters, the inner sum is n if the block sum is zero and 0 otherwise. Hence precisely the partitions in \Pi_{0}(S) survive, giving (2.1).
∎
Proof of Theorem 1.3.
Let n=|G|=p^{r} and let S=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}) be a zero-sum sequence. We show \widetilde{c}_{G}(S)\neq 0 . In (2.1), the one-block partition belongs to \Pi_{0}(S) and contributes
T_{1}=(-1)^{n-1}n(n-1)!.
Its p -adic valuation is r+v_{p}((n-1)!) .
Consider another zero-sum partition with k\geq 2 blocks of sizes b_{1},\ldots,b_{k} . Its term has valuation
kr+\sum_{j=1}^{k}v_{p}((b_{j}-1)!).
It suffices to prove
(2.2)
v_{p}\left(\frac{(n-1)!}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}(b_{j}-1)!}\right)<(k-1)r.
Put m_{j}=b_{j}-1 , so \sum_{j}m_{j}=n-k . Then
\frac{(n-1)!}{\prod_{j}(b_{j}-1)!}=(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-k+1)\binom{n-k}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}.
Since n=p^{r} and 1\leq i\leq k-1<n , we have v_{p}(n-i)=v_{p}(i) , so the first factor has valuation v_{p}((k-1)!) . Also n-k<n=p^{r} , and repeated use of v_{p}\binom{M}{d}<r for M<p^{r} gives
v_{p}\binom{n-k}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}\leq(k-1)(r-1).
Therefore the valuation in (2.2) is at most
v_{p}((k-1)!)+(k-1)(r-1)<(k-1)r.
Thus the one-block term is the unique term of minimal p -adic valuation in (2.1). The sum cannot vanish.
∎
3. On \operatorname{imm}_{(n-1,1)}(\mathcal{M}_{G}) and \operatorname{imm}_{(2,1^{n-2})}(\mathcal{M}_{G})
In this section, we consider the immanants \operatorname{imm}_{(n-1,1)}(\mathcal{M}_{G}) and \operatorname{imm}_{(2,1^{n-2})}(\mathcal{M}_{G}) , and then prove Theorem 1.5.
For a monomial m=\prod_{a\in G}x_{a}^{\lambda_{a}} of degree n , define
\mathcal{P}(m):=\Bigl\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(G):\prod_{u\in G}x_{u+\sigma(u)}=m\Bigr\}.
Then p_{m}=|\mathcal{P}(m)| , while
d_{m}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(m)}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma).
For a\in G , set
\mathcal{P}_{a}(m):=\{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(m):\sigma(0)=a\}.
The proof of the following lemma employ ideas from [40].
Lemma 3.1.
For each \gamma\in G , define a permutation action on \mathfrak{S}(G) by
(\gamma\star\sigma)(u)=\sigma(u-\gamma)-\gamma.
Then \operatorname{sgn}(\gamma\star\sigma)=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) and
\prod_{u\in G}x_{u+(\gamma\star\sigma)(u)}=\prod_{u\in G}x_{u+\sigma(u)}.
In particular, \gamma\star\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(m) whenever \sigma\in\mathcal{P}(m) .
Proof.
Let \tau_{\gamma}(u)=u+\gamma be translation by \gamma . Then
\gamma\star\sigma=\tau_{-\gamma}\circ\sigma\circ\tau_{-\gamma},
so
\operatorname{sgn}(\gamma\star\sigma)=\operatorname{sgn}(\tau_{-\gamma})^{2}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma).
Also,
\prod_{u\in G}x_{u+(\gamma\star\sigma)(u)}=\prod_{u\in G}x_{u+\sigma(u-\gamma)-\gamma}=\prod_{v\in G}x_{v+\sigma(v)},
after the change of variables v=u-\gamma .
∎
Lemma 3.2.
For every a\in G ,
|\mathcal{P}_{a}(m)|=\frac{\lambda_{a}}{n}\,p_{m},
and
\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{a}(m)}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)=\frac{\lambda_{a}}{n}\,d_{m}.
Proof.
Consider the set
X_{a}(m):=\{(\sigma,u)\in\mathcal{P}(m)\times G:u+\sigma(u)=a\}.
If \sigma\in\mathcal{P}(m) , then the symbol x_{a} occurs exactly \lambda_{a} times in the product \prod_{u\in G}x_{u+\sigma(u)} , so there are exactly \lambda_{a} choices of u with u+\sigma(u)=a . Hence
|X_{a}(m)|=\lambda_{a}\,|\mathcal{P}(m)|=\lambda_{a}p_{m}.
Now define
\Phi:X_{a}(m)\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}_{a}(m)\times G,\qquad\Phi(\sigma,u)=((-u)\star\sigma,u).
If u+\sigma(u)=a , then
((-u)\star\sigma)(0)=\sigma(u)+u=a,
so (-u)\star\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{a}(m) . Conversely, given (\pi,u)\in\mathcal{P}_{a}(m)\times G , let \sigma=u\star\pi . Then
\sigma(u)=\pi(0)-u=a-u,
so (\sigma,u)\in X_{a}(m) and \Phi(\sigma,u)=(\pi,u) . Thus \Phi is a bijection, and therefore
\lambda_{a}p_{m}=|X_{a}(m)|=n\,|\mathcal{P}_{a}(m)|.
This proves the first identity.
For the signed identity, weight every pair (\sigma,u)\in X_{a}(m) by \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) . Because the action in Lemma 3.1 preserves sign, the same bijection gives
\sum_{(\sigma,u)\in X_{a}(m)}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)=n\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{a}(m)}\operatorname{sgn}(\pi).
On the other hand, each \sigma\in\mathcal{P}(m) contributes \lambda_{a} times, so the left-hand side equals \lambda_{a}d_{m} . The result follows.
∎
Recall the characters
(3.1)
\chi^{(n-1,1)}(\sigma)=\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma)-1,\qquad\chi^{(2,1^{n-2})}(\sigma)=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)(\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma)-1).
Theorem 3.3.
Let G be a finite abelian group of order n , and let
m=\prod_{a\in G}x_{a}^{\lambda_{a}}
be a monomial of degree n . Define
r(a):=|\{g\in G:2g=a\}|.
Then
(3.2)
[m]\operatorname{imm}_{(n-1,1)}(\mathcal{M}_{G})=\frac{\sum_{a\in G}r(a)\lambda_{a}-n}{n}\,p_{m},
and
(3.3)
[m]\operatorname{imm}_{(2,1^{n-2})}(\mathcal{M}_{G})=\frac{\sum_{a\in G}r(a)\lambda_{a}-n}{n}\,d_{m}.
Proof.
By (3.1),
[m]\operatorname{imm}_{(n-1,1)}(\mathcal{M}_{G})=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(m)}(\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma)-1).
Now
\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(m)}\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma)=\sum_{u\in G}|\{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(m):\sigma(u)=u\}|.
Fix u\in G . The condition \sigma(u)=u is equivalent to u+\sigma(u)=2u , and by the same translation argument as in Lemma 3.2, the number of such permutations equals |\mathcal{P}_{2u}(m)| . Hence
\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(m)}\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma)=\sum_{u\in G}|\mathcal{P}_{2u}(m)|=\sum_{a\in G}r(a)|\mathcal{P}_{a}(m)|.
By Lemma 3.2, this is
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{a\in G}r(a)\lambda_{a}\,p_{m}.
Subtracting p_{m}=|\mathcal{P}(m)| gives (3.2).
For the second identity, by (3.1),
[m]\operatorname{imm}_{(2,1^{n-2})}(\mathcal{M}_{G})=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(m)}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)(\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma)-1).
The same computation as above, but weighted by sign, gives
\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(m)}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma)=\sum_{a\in G}r(a)\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{a}(m)}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{a\in G}r(a)\lambda_{a}\,d_{m},
again by Lemma 3.2. Subtracting d_{m} yields (3.3).
∎
Proof of Theorem 1.5.(1).
If n is odd, the doubling map on G is bijective, so r(a)=1 for every a\in G . Since \sum_{a\in G}\lambda_{a}=n for every monomial of degree n , the scalar factor in (3.2) and (3.3) is always zero. Hence every coefficient vanishes.
∎
Proof of Theorem 1.5.(2).
Write n=2m with m odd. Since the Sylow 2 -subgroup of G has order 2 , the subgroup 2G has index 2 , so G/2G\cong C_{2} . Moreover,
r(a)=\begin{cases}2,&a\in 2G,\\
0,&a\notin 2G.\end{cases}
For a monomial m=\prod_{a\in G}x_{a}^{\lambda_{a}} , let
E=\sum_{a\in 2G}\lambda_{a},\qquad O=\sum_{a\notin 2G}\lambda_{a}.
Then E+O=n , and the scalar factor in Theorem 3.3 is
\frac{2E-n}{n}=\frac{E-O}{n}.
Assume first that p_{m}>0 . By Theorem 1.1, the multiset corresponding to m is a zero-sum sequence of length n over G . Passing to the quotient G/2G\cong C_{2} , we see that the number of terms outside 2G must be even; in other words, O is even. But n/2=m is odd, so E=O=n/2 is impossible. Hence E-O\neq 0 , and therefore the factor in (3.2) is nonzero. It follows that
[m]\operatorname{imm}_{(n-1,1)}(\mathcal{M}_{G})\neq 0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad p_{m}\neq 0.
This proves the first statement.
For the second statement, if d_{m}\neq 0 then certainly p_{m}>0 , so the same scalar factor is again nonzero. Therefore (3.3) implies
[m]\operatorname{imm}_{(2,1^{n-2})}(\mathcal{M}_{G})\neq 0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad d_{m}\neq 0.
This completes the proof.
∎
4. The odd-order immanant twin
In this section, we consider a general case and prove Theorem 1.6.
For a permutation \sigma , let c_{i}(\sigma) be the number of i -cycles of \sigma . It is easy to see that
(4.1)
\chi^{(n-3,1^{3})}=\binom{c_{1}-1}{3}-(c_{1}-1)c_{2}+c_{3}.
and
(4.2)
\chi^{(n-3,3)}=\binom{c_{1}}{3}-\binom{c_{1}}{2}+(c_{1}-1)c_{2}+c_{3}.
Since (4,1^{n-4})=(n-3,1^{3})^{\prime} and (2,2,2,1^{n-6})=(n-3,3)^{\prime} , subtracting (4.2) from (4.1) after sign twist gives
(4.3)
\chi^{(4,1^{n-4})}(\sigma)-\chi^{(2,2,2,1^{n-6})}(\sigma)=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)(c_{1}(\sigma)-1)(1-2c_{2}(\sigma)).
Let A=(a_{ij}) be an arbitrary n\times n matrix. Write A(S|S) for the principal matrix obtained by deleting rows and columns indexed by S . Define
F_{1}(A)=\sum_{i}a_{ii}\det A(i|i),
T_{2}(A)=\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}a_{ji}\det A(i,j|i,j),
and
T_{12}(A)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\notin\{j,k\}\\
j<k\end{subarray}}a_{ii}a_{jk}a_{kj}\det A(i,j,k|i,j,k).
Proposition 4.1 (principal-minor reduction).
For every n\times n matrix A ,
(4.4)
\operatorname{imm}_{(4,1^{n-4})}(A)-\operatorname{imm}_{(2,2,2,1^{n-6})}(A)=F_{1}(A)-\det A+2(T_{12}(A)-T_{2}(A)).
Proof.
By (4.3), the difference of immanants is
\sum_{\sigma}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)(c_{1}-1)(1-2c_{2})\prod_{i}a_{i,\sigma(i)}.
The determinant sum with one fixed point marked is F_{1}(A) , so the contribution of c_{1}-1 is F_{1}(A)-\det A . The determinant sum with one transposition marked has an extra negative sign from the transposition. Thus the contribution of (c_{1}-1)c_{2} is -T_{12}(A)+T_{2}(A) . Multiplying by -2 gives (4.4).
∎
We apply this with A=\mathcal{M}_{G} . Work over the rational function field K=\mathbb{C}(x_{g}:g\in G) . The determinant of \mathcal{M}_{G} is not the zero polynomial: specializing x_{0}=1 and x_{g}=0 for g\neq 0 gives the permutation matrix of a\mapsto-a . Hence \mathcal{M}_{G} is invertible over K . Its inverse again has group-Hankel form
\mathcal{M}_{G}^{-1}=(y_{a+b})_{a,b\in G}
for rational functions y_{g} . Equivalently, the functions x_{g} and y_{g} satisfy the convolution equations
(4.5)
\sum_{r\in G}x_{r}y_{r+s}=\delta_{s,0}\qquad(s\in G).
This follows, for instance, from diagonalizing the group matrix by the characters of G .
Let \Delta=\det\mathcal{M}_{G} . Jacobi’s complementary minor theorem gives
(4.6)
\det\mathcal{M}_{G}(i|i)=\Delta y_{2i},
(4.7)
\det\mathcal{M}_{G}(i,j|i,j)=\Delta(y_{2i}y_{2j}-y_{i+j}^{2}),
and
(4.8)
\det\mathcal{M}_{G}(i,j,k|i,j,k)=\Delta\,\Gamma(i,j,k),
where
\displaystyle\Gamma(i,j,k)={}
\displaystyle y_{2i}y_{2j}y_{2k}+2y_{i+j}y_{i+k}y_{j+k}
\displaystyle-y_{2i}y_{j+k}^{2}-y_{2j}y_{i+k}^{2}-y_{2k}y_{i+j}^{2}.
Lemma 4.2.
Let G be a finite abelian group of odd order, then F_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{G})=\det\mathcal{M}_{G} .
Proof.
Using (4.6),
F_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{G})=\Delta\sum_{i}x_{2i}y_{2i}=\Delta\sum_{r}x_{r}y_{r}=\Delta,
because doubling is a bijection and (4.5) with s=0 gives \sum_{r}x_{r}y_{r}=1 .
∎
Lemma 4.3.
If G has odd order, then T_{12}(\mathcal{M}_{G})=T_{2}(\mathcal{M}_{G}) .
Proof.
Set
C=\sum_{s\in G}x_{s}^{2}\sum_{t\in G}y_{t}y_{2s-t},\qquad S=\sum_{s\in G}x_{s}^{2}y_{s}^{2}.
By (4.7), extending the symmetric sum over i<j to an ordered sum gives
\displaystyle\frac{2T_{2}(\mathcal{M}_{G})}{\Delta}
\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}x_{i+j}^{2}(y_{2i}y_{2j}-y_{i+j}^{2})
(4.9)
\displaystyle=C-nS.
Indeed, the map (i,j)\mapsto(s,t)=(i+j,2i) is a bijection of G^{2} , and for fixed s=i+j there are n ordered pairs (i,j) .
Similarly, because \Gamma is symmetric and vanishes when two indices coincide,
(4.10)
\frac{2T_{12}(\mathcal{M}_{G})}{\Delta}=\sum_{i,j,k}x_{2i}x_{j+k}^{2}\Gamma(i,j,k).
Split the right-hand side according to the five terms in \Gamma as
B_{1}+2B_{2}-B_{3}-B_{4}-B_{5}.
The first and third terms are
B_{1}=C,\qquad B_{3}=nS,
using (4.5) and the same reindexing as above.
For B_{2} , use the bijection
(i,j,k)\longmapsto(u,v,s)=(i+j,i+k,j+k),
whose inverse exists because 2 is invertible in G . Then
B_{2}=\sum_{u,v,s}x_{u+v-s}x_{s}^{2}y_{u}y_{v}y_{s}=\sum_{v,s}x_{s}^{2}y_{v}y_{s}\sum_{u}x_{u+v-s}y_{u}.
By (4.5), the inner sum is \delta_{v,s} ; hence B_{2}=S .
For B_{4} , use the bijection
(i,j,k)\longmapsto(t,v,s)=(i-j,2j,j+k).
Then
B_{4}=\sum_{t,v,s}x_{v+2t}x_{s}^{2}y_{v}y_{s+t}^{2}=\sum_{s,t}x_{s}^{2}y_{s+t}^{2}\sum_{v}x_{v+2t}y_{v}.
The inner sum is \delta_{2t,0}=\delta_{t,0} by (4.5) and oddness. Therefore B_{4}=S . By symmetry B_{5}=S . Substituting into (4.10) gives
\frac{2T_{12}(\mathcal{M}_{G})}{\Delta}=C+2S-nS-S-S=C-nS,
which agrees with (4.9).
∎
Proof of Theorem 1.6.
Combining Proposition 4.1 and Lemmas 4.2, 4.3, we obtain the desired result.
∎
Proposition 4.4.
For G=C_{n} with n\geq 6 even,
[x_{0}^{n}]\left(\operatorname{imm}_{(4,1^{n-4})}(\mathcal{M}_{G})-\operatorname{imm}_{(2,2,2,1^{n-6})}(\mathcal{M}_{G})\right)=(3-n)(-1)^{(n-2)/2}.
In particular the two immanants are not equal.
Proof.
The monomial x_{0}^{n} comes only from \sigma(i)=-i . For even n this permutation has two fixed points and (n-2)/2 transpositions. Substituting c_{1}=2 and c_{2}=(n-2)/2 into (4.3) gives the desired result.
∎
5. Concluding remarks
In this paper, we proved several results concerning the immanants \operatorname{imm}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{M}_{G}) of a Cayley table. It is interesting to study the converse of Theorem 1.3. It is known that \mathcal{D}(G)<\mathcal{P}(G) when G is a non-cyclic abelian group of order 12. We have some partial results (as well as numerical results) strongly suggest that \mathcal{D}(G)=\mathcal{P}(G) only if |G| is a prime power. Theorem 1.6 suggests that it is worthy to study the immanants \operatorname{imm}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{M}_{G}) for general partition \lambda and find more similar relations. Meanwhile, we also have some partial results (as well as numerical results) supporting Conjecture 1.4.(3) and it is could be interesting to find other partitions \lambda with \mathcal{I}_{\lambda}(G)=\mathcal{P}(G) or \mathcal{D}(G) . These problems are our next targets to study.
