Abstract
In 1983, A. Bouchet extended W.T. Tutte’s notion of nowhere-zero flows to signed graphs, and conjectured that every flow-admissible signed graph has a nowhere-zero 6-flow. In this paper we prove that every flow-admissible signed graph that is 3-edge-connected has a nowhere-zero 8-flow. This is a continuation of a previous paper where we proved the same conclusion under stronger assumptions.
1 Introduction
Motivated by proper colourings of planar graphs, W.T. Tutte introduced the notion of a nowhere-zero flow in the 1950’s. He made three profound flow conjectures: Tutte’s 3-flow conjecture (1972) is a generalization of Grotzsch’s theorem that triangle-free planar graph are 3-colourable. It was proved to hold for 6-edge-connected graphs (2013) by Lovász, Thomassen, Wu, and Zhang [9]. Tutte’s 4-flow conjecture (1966) is a generalization of the 4-colour theorem. It was shown (1997-2016), and widely accepted, although not completely published, that the 4-flow conjecture holds for cubic graphs [11, 8]. Tutte’s 5-flow conjecture (1954) is generally considered the most important of these conjectures.
Conjecture 1 (Tutte).
Every flow-admissible graph has a nowhere-zero 5-flow.
Seymour proved that every flow-admissible graph has a nowhere-zero 6-flow [12] in 1981. Two years later, Bouchet noticed that Tutte’s flow-colouring duality extends to general surfaces. Interestingly, however, this requires flows on signed graphs. Bouchet made the following conjecture, which parallels Tutte’s 5-flow conjecture in the broader setting of signed graphs [2].
Conjecture 2 (Bouchet).
Every flow-admissible signed graph has a nowhere-zero 6-flow.
Like the 5-flow conjecture, Bouchet’s 6-flow conjecture is best-possible due to (a signed version of) the Petersen graph [2]. Bouchet proved that his conjecture holds with 6 replaced by 216. This has been further improved by numerous authors and the best current result, published in 2021, is that every flow-admissible signed graph has a nowhere-zero 11-flow [4].
The present paper is a continuation of [5], where we proved that every flow-admissible signed graph that is cyclically 5-edge-connected and cubic has a nowhere-zero  8 -flow. Here, we prove the same conclusion under weaker assumptions.
Theorem 3.
Every flow-admissible, 3-edge-connected signed graph has a nowhere-zero 8-flow.
Throughout, we consider (signed) graphs which are finite, but may have multiple edges or loops. Our methods here are based on those in [5], and we use notation defined there and in [7]. In particular, expanded definitions about signed graphs such as signature, switching, balance, etc., and flows on those graphs can be found in [5]. We briefly review them now.
A signed graph is a graph  G  equipped with a bipartition of its edges  E(G)  into negative and positive, which we call a signature. An oriented signed graph, also called a bidirected graph, is a signed graph equipped with exactly two directions for every edge, one assigned to each of its ends (both directions of a loop-edge are assigned to its end). This orientation must respect the signature of  G : A positive edge is oriented so that one of its directions is towards and the other is away. A negative edge is oriented so that both of its directions are the same (both towards or both away). These directions are represented by arrowheads in Figure 1.
Figure 1: Orientations of signed edges. Positive edges on left, negative edges on right.
We allow two operations on an oriented signed graph  G : reversing edges, and switching at vertices. To reverse an edge  e\in E(G)  is to reverse both of the directions of  e  (from away to towards and/or from towards to away). This has no effect on the signature of  G . To switch at a vertex  v\in V(G)  means to reverse every edge-direction that is assigned to  v . This changes the signature of  G , so that positive non-loop edges incident to  v  become negative, and negative non-loop edges incident to  v  become positive.
Let  G  be an oriented signed graph, and  A  an abelian group. For  v\in V(G) , denote by  \partial^{+}(v)  the set of edges directed away from  v  and denote by  \partial^{-}(v)  the set of edges directed toward  v . With apologies, we include a negative loop-edge incident to  v  twice in one of these sets (depending on its orientation).
A function  f:E(G)\to A  is an  A -flow whenever  \partial f(v):=\sum_{e\in\partial^{+}(v)}f(e)-\sum_{e\in\partial^{-}(v)}f(e)=0  at every vertex  v . An integer-flow  f  is called a nowhere-zero  k -flow whenever the range of  f  is in  \{\pm 1,\pm 2,\dots,\pm(k-1)\} .
It follows from the above discussion that reversing an edge preserves the existence of a flow in a signed graph  G : when  G  has a flow  f , if we reverse an edge  e  and replace  f(e)  with its inverse, then  f  remains a flow.
It also follows that switching at a vertex preserves the existence of a flow. Hence, we say two signed graphs  G  and  G^{\prime}  are equivalent if  G^{\prime}  can be obtained from  G  by a sequence of switching operations.
Therefore, to demonstrate the existence of a nowhere-zero  k -flow in a signed graph  G  it suffices to demonstrate a nowhere-zero  k  flow in any orientation of  G  and with any equivalent signature of  G .
A signed graph is  k -unbalanced whenever each of its equivalent signatures has at least  k  negative edges. A  1 -unbalanced signed graph is simply unbalanced.
A signed graph is flow-admissible whenever it admits a nowhere-zero integer flow. A 2-edge-connected, unbalanced signed graph is flow-admissible if and only if it is 2-unbalanced [2].
This paper proceeds as follows. In Section 2, we reduce proving Theorem 3 to proving a result about cubic graphs with an additional connectivity property. After this, Sections 3 to 6 parallel those sections in [5], where we build our list of cycles and establish a preflow. Because of our weaker assumptions, the cycle selection process here is considerably more involved. Once our list of cycles is built, however, with slight modification, we are able to use the machinery already established in [5].
In Section 3, we show that we can always find a usable cycle for our process – this time the reduced connectivity assumptions force us to look in a leaf-block of our graph. In Section 4, we establish Lemma 20 to ensure the number of vertices in our subdivided graph is even (so that we may find a perfect matching in our graph). Our weaker connectivity assumptions force us to allow an exception graph which we call fish. In Section 5, we describe our cycle-selection process – this time via an algorithm. Here we are forced to allow weaker connectivity with the positive cycles than we did in [5].
Finally, using the machinery established in the last three sections of [5], we put everything together to prove our main result in Section 7.
2 Reducing the main theorem
In this section we show that the main theorem can be reduced to proving a result for cubic graphs with an additional connectivity property. To achieve this, we will consider a counterexample graph  G  for which the parameter  3|E(G)|-4|V(G)|  is minimum. Let us note here that for every graph  G  with minimum degree at least 3 we have
 3|E(G)|-4|V(G)|=\tfrac{3}{2}\sum_{v\in V(G)}\mathrm{deg}(v)-4|V(G)|\geq\tfrac{1}{2}|V(G)|, 
with equality when  G  is cubic. In particular, this quantity must always be positive for every 3-edge-connected graph.
We require a proposition about uncontracting edges from [3].
Suppose  G  is a signed graph,  v\in V(G)  with  \deg(v)\geq 4 , and  e,f  are distinct edges incident to  v  whose other ends are  v_{e},v_{f}  respectively (we are allowing  e,f  to be loops). We uncontract at  v  with  \{e,f\}  to get a new signed graph by adding new vertex  v^{\prime} , changing the ends of  e,f  to be  v_{e}v^{\prime}  and  v_{f}v^{\prime}  respectively, and adding a positive edge  vv^{\prime} . See Figure 2.
Figure 2: Uncontracting at  v  with  \{e,f\} .
Proposition 4 ([3]).
Let  G  be a 2-unbalanced, 3-edge-connected signed graph on at least two vertices. Let  v\in V(G)  have degree at least four, and  e\in E(G)  be incident to  v . Then there is an edge  e^{\prime}  incident to  v  so that the graph  G^{\prime} , obtained from  G  by uncontracting at  v  with  \{e,e^{\prime}\} , is 2-unbalanced and 3-edge-connected.
Lemma 5.
If  G  is a counterexample to Theorem 3 for which  3|E(G)|-4|V((G)|  is minimum, then  G  is cubic.
Proof.
Suppose (for a contradiction) that  G  is not cubic and choose a vertex  v\in V(G)  with degree greater than 3. By Proposition 4 we may uncontract a positive edge  e  at the vertex  v  so that the resulting signed graph  G^{\prime}  is both flow-admissible and 3-edge-connected. Since  G  is a minimum counterexample and  G^{\prime}  has one more edge and vertex, the theorem applies to  G^{\prime}  and we may choose a nowhere-zero 8-flow for  G^{\prime} . Now contracting  e  gives a nowhere-zero 8-flow of  G , a contradiction.
∎
In preparation for the next reduction, we prove two lemmas concerning flows in ordinary (not signed) graphs with prespecified values on some edges.
Lemma 6.
Let  G  be an oriented 2-edge-connected graph and let  v\in V(G)  be incident with exactly three edges  e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}  all directed away from  v . Let  \Gamma  be an abelian group with  |\Gamma|\geq 6  and let  g_{1},g_{2},g_{3}\in\Gamma\setminus\{0\}  satisfy  g_{1}+g_{2}+g_{3}=0 . Then there exists a nowhere-zero  \Gamma -flow  \phi  of  G  with  \phi(e_{i})=g_{i}  for  i=1,2,3. 
Proof.
This follows from Tutte’s deletion-contraction formula11

              For graph  G  and  e\in E(G) , the number of nowhere-zero  \Gamma -flows in  G  is equal to the number of nowhere-zero  \Gamma -flows in  G  contract  e  ( G/e ) minus the number of nowhere-zero  \Gamma -flows in  G  delete  e  ( G-e ). See, for example, [7] Section 6.3, page 145. for counting flows. Define
 \mathcal{T}=\{(h_{1},h_{2},h_{3})\in(\Gamma\setminus\{0\})^{3}\mid h_{1}+h_{2}+h_{3}=0\}. 
If  G^{\prime}  is a minor of  G  obtained by deleting and contracting edges other than  e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}  and  T=(h_{1},h_{2},h_{3})\in\mathcal{T}  we define  \Gamma_{T}(G^{\prime})  to be the number of nowhere-zero  \Gamma -flows  \phi  of  G^{\prime}  that satisfy  \phi(e_{i})=h_{i} .
Claim:
 \Gamma_{T}(G^{\prime})=\Gamma_{T^{\prime}}(G^{\prime}) 
holds for every  T,T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T} .
We prove this claim by induction on  |E(G^{\prime})| . As a base case, when  G^{\prime}  has just the edges  e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}  the resulting graph has exactly one suitable nowhere-zero flow if  e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}  are parallel, and none otherwise. For the inductive step, choose an edge  f\in E(G)\setminus\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}  and apply the deletion-contraction formula together with the inductive hypothesis as follows:
 \Gamma_{T}(G^{\prime})=\Gamma_{T}(G^{\prime}/f)-\Gamma_{T}(G^{\prime}-f)=\Gamma_{T^{\prime}}(G^{\prime}/f)-\Gamma_{T^{\prime}}(G^{\prime}-f)=\Gamma_{T^{\prime}}(G^{\prime}) 
This completes the proof of claim.
By Seymour’s 6-flow theorem [12], we must have  \Gamma_{T}(G)\neq 0  for some  T\in\mathcal{T}  and then by the above argument, this must hold true for every triple in  \mathcal{T} .
∎
Lemma 7.
Let  G  be an oriented 2-edge-connected graph and and let  v\in V(G)  be incident with exactly three edges  e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}  all directed away from  v . Let  k\geq 6  and let  g_{1},g_{2},g_{3}\in\{-(k-1),\ldots k-1\}\setminus\{0\}  satisfy  g_{1}+g_{2}+g_{3}=0 . Then there exists a nowhere-zero  k -flow  \phi  of  G  satisfying  \phi(e_{i})=g_{i}  for  i=1,2,3 .
Proof.
By the previous lemma, we may choose a nowhere-zero flow  \psi:E(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{k} 
so that  \psi(e_{i})=g_{i}+k\mathbb{Z}  for  i=1,2,3 . By Tutte’s Theorem22
                2
              This is well-known lore. See, for example, the proof of [1, Theorem 21.3]., we may choose a  k -flow  \phi:E(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}  so that  \phi(e)+k\mathbb{Z}=\psi(e)  holds for every  e\in E(G) . Now for  1\leq i\leq 3  the edge  e_{i}  either has the desired value:  \phi(e_{i})=g_{i} , or has  \phi(e_{i})=g_{i}\pm k . Since  \sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi(e_{i})=0=\sum_{i=1}^{3}g_{i}  either all three edges have the desired value and we are done, or by rearranging indices we may assume  \phi(e_{1})=g_{1} ,  \phi(e_{2})=g_{2}+k  and  \phi(e_{3})=g_{3}-k . Note that this implies  \phi(e_{2})>0  and  \phi(e_{3})<0 . Modify  \phi  and the orientation of  G  as follows: for every edge  f\in E(G)\setminus\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}  with  \phi(f)<0 , reverse  f  and multiply  \phi(f)  by  -1 . Note that after this operation  \phi  is still a nowhere-zero  k -flow and  \phi  is positive on all edges apart from  e_{1},e_{2},e_{3} .
Let  e_{i}  have ends  v  and  u_{i}  for  i=1,2,3 . We claim that there is a directed path from  u_{2}  to  u_{3}  in the graph  G-v . Suppose (for a contradiction) that no such path exists. Then there is a partition of  V(G)\setminus\{v\}  into  \{A,B\}  with  u_{2}\in A  and  u_{3}\in B  so that there are no edges with tail in  A  and head in  B . If  u_{1}\in A  then add  v  to the set  A , otherwise  u_{1}\in B  and we add  v  to  B . After this modification, all edges directed from  B  to  A  have positive flow in  \phi  and all edges directed from  A  to  B  have negative flow, contradicting the cut rule for flows. Therefore, we may choose a directed path  P  from  u_{2}  to  u_{3}  in  G-v . Modify the flow  \phi  by subtracting  k  on all edges in  E(P)\cup\{e_{2}\}  and adding  k  on  e_{3} . This results in a new flow that satisfies the lemma.
∎
For a (signed) graph  G=(V,E)  and a set  X\subseteq V(G)  we use  d(X)  to denote the number of edges with one end in  X  and one end in  V\setminus X .
To identify  x,y\in V(G) , set  x=y  (so the cardinality of  V(G)  is decreased by one).
Lemma 8.
If  G  is a counterexample to Theorem 3 for which  3|E(G)|-4|V((G)|  is minimum, then  G  does not have a set  Y\subseteq V  with  |Y|\geq 2  and  d(Y)=3  for which  G[Y]  is balanced.
Proof.
Suppose (for a contradiction) that such a set  Y  exists and let  X=V(G)\setminus X . By Lemma 5 the graph  G  is cubic so the edges in  E(X,Y)  are pairwise nonadjacent. It follows from this and our assumptions that we may modify the signature of  G  so that every edge incident with a vertex in  Y  is positive. Choose an orientation of  G  for which all edges in  E(X,Y)  are directed from a vertex in  X  to a vertex in  Y . Let  G_{x}   (G_{y})  be the oriented signed graph obtained from  G  by identifying  X  to a new vertex  x  (identifying  Y  to a new vertex  y ). By our assumptions,  G_{x}  has all non-loop edges positive (i.e. it is an oriented graph, plus 0 or at least two negative loops incident to  x ) and  G_{y}  is flow-admissible (if  G_{y}  were not flow-admissible, the same would be true of  G ). Note that for any cubic graph  H  we have  3|E(H)|-4|V(H)|=\frac{1}{2}|V(H)| . Using this property and the minimality of our counterexample, we may choose a nowhere-zero 8-flow, say  \phi_{y} , of  G_{y} . By applying Lemma 7 we may choose a nowhere-zero 8-flow, say  \phi_{x}  of  G_{x}  so that  \phi_{x}(e)=\phi_{y}(e)  holds for every  e\in E(X,Y) . Combining  \phi_{x}  and  \phi_{y}  then gives a suitable nowhere-zero 8-flow of  G . This contradiction completes the proof.
∎
3 Finding a usable cycle
In the following definition, we describe the central class of graphs of interest for us in establishing the proof of our main theorem. A subcubic (signed) graph has maximum degree three.
Definition 9.
Let  G  be a signed graph. We say that  G  is well-behaved if it is subcubic and satisfies the following two properties for every non-empty  X\subseteq V(G) 
1. 
 3\leq d(X)+\sum_{x\in X}(3-\mathrm{deg}(x)) 
2. 
If  G[X]  is balanced and  |X|\geq 2  then  4\leq d(X)+\sum_{x\in X}(3-\mathrm{deg}(x)) 
Note that by taking  X=V(G)  in part (i) of the above definition, every well-behaved graph must have some vertices with degree less than 3. Another thing to note is that every induced subgraph of a well-behaved graph is also well-behaved. The next observation illuminates the reason for the above definition. It follows immediately from our definitions together with Lemmas 5 and 8.
Observation 10.
If  G  is a counterexample to Theorem 3 for which  3|E(G)|-4|V((G)|  is minimum, then every proper induced subgraph of  G  is well-behaved.
In the next lemma, we require Corollary 11.1, which is a straightforward consequence of the following theorem found in [6].
If  G  is a signed graph and  e_{1},e_{2}\in E(G) , then we say that  e_{1}  and  e_{2}  are untied if there exists a positive cycle  C^{+}  and a negative cycle  C^{-}  so that  \{e_{1},e_{2}\}\subseteq E(C^{+})\cap E(C^{-}) . Otherwise we say that  e_{1}  and  e_{2}  are tied.
Theorem 11 ([6]).
Let  G  be a 3-connected signed graph and let  e_{1},e_{2}\in E(G)  be distinct and not in parallel with any other edges. Then  e_{1}  and  e_{2}  are tied in  G  if and only if one of the following holds:
1. 
There exists a parallel class  F  containing edges of both signs so that  F^{+}=F\cup\{e_{1},e_{2}\}  is an edge-cut and  G-F^{+}  is balanced,
2. 
 e_{1},e_{2}  are incident with a common vertex  v  and  G-v  is balanced,
3. 
 G-\{e_{1},e_{2}\}  is balanced.
Corollary 11.1.
Let  G  be a 3-connected, cubic signed graph and let  e_{1},e_{2}\in E(G)  be distinct. If  G-\{e_{1},e_{2}\}  is unbalanced, then there exists a positive cycle containing both  e_{1}  and  e_{2} .
We say a generalized cycle (i.e.: a cycle or a single vertex)  C  in a subcubic signed graph is good if  C  is either a vertex of degree 0 or 1, or  C  is a positive cycle containing at least two vertices of degree 2. A generalized cycle is usable if it is either good, or is a negative cycle containing at most one vertex not of degree 2. We use  V_{i}(G)  to denote the set of vertices of degree  i  in  G . A theta graph is a 2-connected subcubic graph containing exactly two vertices of degree three.
Lemma 12.
If  G  is a well-behaved, 2-connected signed graph, then either  G  is a negative cycle, or  G  contains a good cycle.
Proof.
Suppose for contradiction that the lemma does not hold. We proceed in two cases.
In the first case, suppose  G  is not a subdivison of a  3 -connected signed graph. If  G  were itself a cycle then it would be usable, a contradiction, which means there is a  2 -edge-cut  E(A,B)  that separates two degree-three vertices in  G . Because  G  is well-behaved, without loss of generality there exist  \{x,y\}\subseteq V_{2}(G)\cap A , and  z\in V_{2}(G)\cap B . We may further suppose that  E(A,B)  was chosen so that  B  is minimal. By  2 -connectivity,  G  has a subgraph  H  that is either a cycle or a theta with  x,y,z\in V(H) ; and such that if  H  is a theta, then each of  x,y,z  are on different branches of  H . If  H  is a positive cycle or a theta, then  H  contains a good cycle, a contradiction. Hence it must be that  H  is a negative cycle. If  G[B]  is unbalanced, then we may reroute the path of  H  through  B  (possibly missing  z ) so it is a positive cycle. This rerouted cycle contains  x  and  y  and so it is good, again a contradiction. Hence it must be that  G[B]  is balanced. By property (ii) in the definition of well-behaved, there must exist  w\in V_{2}(G)\cap B  with  w\neq z . If there is a cycle containing  w  and  z  in  G[B] , then it is positive and good, a contradiction. Hence it must be that  G[B]  has a cut-edge separating  w  and  z . But this cut-edge contradicts that  B  was chosen to be minimal. This completes the first case.
In the second case, suppose  G  is a subdivision of a  3 -connected graph, say  H . Let  S\subseteq E(H)  be the set of edges that are subdivided to form  G , and notice that by our connectivity assumptions  |S|\geq 2 . Let  e_{1},e_{2}\in S . Since  G  does not have a good cycle, by Corollary 11.1 this means  G-\{e_{1},e_{2} } is balanced. And so, again because  G  does not have a good cycle, we may assume the signature of  H  is such that  e_{1}  is negative while all other edges are positive.
If  |S|\geq 3  then by  3 -connectivity of  H ,  G  has a good cycle, a contradiction. Hence  S=\{e_{1},e_{2}\} . Similarly, if  e_{2}  is subdivided at least two times then  G  has a good cycle, and so it follows that  e_{2}  is subdivided exactly once while  e_{1}  is subdivided at least twice. But now the set  X  consisting of all vertices in  G  except for the degree two vertices on the subdivided edge  e_{1}  contradicts part (ii) of the definition of well-behaved. This completes the second case and the proof.
∎
4 Using an unbalanced theta
The main goal of this section is to prove Lemma 20. That lemma will be used to satisfy a parity requirement on our set of cycles in Section 5. (In that section, as in [5], we will need the number of even-length negative (not special) cycles in our list to be even). A main difference between this section and the parallel section in [5] is that in [5] we always found a pair of cycles satisfying our requirements, whereas a family of exception signed graphs (see Definition 17) is allowed here. We will also call upon a special case of a theorem by Watkins and Mesner [13], while the parallel section in [5] is self-contained.
We say that a pair  (D,Q)  of subgraphs of  G  form a good theta-pair if  D  is a negative cycle,  Q  is a path with both ends in  V(D)  but internally disjoint from  V(D)  and  |V(Q)\cap V_{2}(G)|\geq 2 .
Lemma 13.
If  G  is a well-behaved signed graph with a good theta-pair, then there exist a pair of cycles  C_{0},C_{1}  satisfying the following:
1. 
 C_{1}  is negative and  C_{0}  is good
2. 
 |V_{2}(G)\cap V(C_{1})|\geq 2 
3. 
 G-V(C_{0})  and  G-V(C_{1})  have the same negative cycles.
Proof.
Choose a good theta-pair  (D,Q)  for which  Q  is maximal. Let  x,y  be the ends of  Q  and let  G^{\prime}=G\setminus(V(Q)-\{x,y\}) . Let  H  be the block of  G^{\prime}  containing the cycle  D .
If there exists a negative cycle in  H  not containing  x , this cycle may be extended within  H  to give a good theta-pair with a longer path—a contradiction. Hence every negative cycle in  H  contains  x . Let  C_{0},C_{1}  be the two cycles in the theta graph  D\cup Q  containing  Q  so that  C_{0}  is positive and  C_{1}  is negative. It follows immediately from our construction that  C_{0}  and  C_{1}  satisfy all three required properties above.
∎
Lemma 14.
Let  G  be an unbalanced, 2-connected, well-behaved signed graph with no good theta-pair and let  C_{0}  be a good cycle. Then there exists a negative cycle  C_{1}  with  |V(C_{1})\cap V_{2}(G)|\geq 1  so that every negative cycle of  G-V(C_{1})  is contained in  G-V(C_{0}) .
Proof.
Let  a,b\in V(C_{0})\cap V_{2}(G)  be distinct. Because  G  is 2-connected and unbalanced, there must exist a path  P\subseteq G  that has both ends in  V(C_{0}) , is internally disjoint from  V(C_{0}) , and so that  H=P\cup C_{0}  is an unbalanced theta. Let  x,y\subseteq V(C_{0})  be the ends of  P . Because  G  does not have a good theta-pair it must be that  x,y  interleave  a,b  on  C_{0} , and  V(C_{0})\cap V_{2}(G)=\{a,b\} . Let  Q\subseteq C_{0}  be the  x,y -path in  C_{0}  which contains  a , and suppose we have chosen  P  so that  Q  is minimal. Let  C_{1}  be the negative cycle in  C_{0}\cup P  containing  b .
The vertex  b  witnesses that  |V(C_{1})\cap V_{2}(G)|\geq 1 . To complete the proof, suppose (for contradiction) that  N  is a negative cycle in  G-V(C_{1})  not contained in  G-V(C_{0}) . In this case,  N  must intersect  Q-\{x,y\} . This means there is a path  P^{\prime}\subseteq G  with ends in  Q-\{x,y\}  so that  P^{\prime}\cup C_{0}  makes an unbalanced theta. But this contradicts our choice of  P .
∎
We call a signed subcubic graph  G  fragile if  G  contains an unbalanced theta, but for every good cycle  C  the graph  G-V(C)  has no unbalanced theta.
Lemma 15.
Let  G  be a 2-connected, fragile, well-behaved signed graph with no good theta-pair, and let  C\subseteq G  be a good cycle. Then every negative cycle  N  in  G-V(C)  has  |V_{3}(G)\cap V(N)|=2  and  |V_{2}(G)\cap V(N)|\geq 1 .
Proof.
Let  H  be a component of  G^{\prime}=G-E(C)  with a negative cycle  N . We will consider the block structure of  H . Note that because  G  is fragile,  N  is itself a block of  H . Because  G  is subcubic and  2 -connected, every leaf-block of  H  is a single edge with a vertex in  V(C) . This means that if  |V(N)\cap V_{3}(G)|\geq 3 , then there would be three vertex-disjoint paths from  V(N)  to  V(C)  as in Figure 4. But then using two of those paths, there would be a good theta-pair containing  N  and a path  Q  with two degree-2 vertices on  C , a contradiction. Thus  |V(N)\cap V_{3}(G)|\leq 2 . Since at least two degree-3 vertices are required by 2-connectivity, it must be that  |V(N)\cap V_{3}(G)|=2 . But then because  G  is well-behaved it follows that  |V(N)\cap V_{2}(G)|\geq 1 .
∎
Lemma 16.
If  G  is a  2 -connected, fragile, well-behaved signed graph with no good theta-pair, then for every good cycle  C  the graph  G-V(C)  has at most two negative cycles.
Proof.
Let  G^{\prime}=G-E(C) . By Lemma 15, every negative cycle  D  in  G^{\prime}  has  |V_{3}(G)\cap V(D)|=2  and  |V_{2}(G)\cap V(D)|\geq 1 .
If a component  H  of  G^{\prime}  contained two negative cycles  D,D^{\prime} , then there would be a good theta-pair using  D  and a path  Q  containing a degree-2 vertex from  D^{\prime}  and a degree 2 vertex from  C , a contradiction. Hence every component of  G^{\prime}  has at most one negative cycle.
Now, suppose for contradiction that  G^{\prime}  has three negative cycles, say  D_{1},D_{2},D_{3} . By 2-connectivity of  G , for  1\leq i\leq 3  choose a pair of vertex disjoint paths from  V(D_{i})  to  V(C)  and let  H_{i}  be the subgraph of  G  made by the union of  D_{i}  and the two paths associated with it. Observe that by the previous paragraph,  H_{1},H_{2},H_{3}  are pairwise vertex disjoint. For  1\leq i\leq 3  let  \{x_{i},y_{i}\}=V(C)\cap V(H_{i}) . Note that  H_{i}  contains paths between  x_{i}  and  y_{i}  of both signs, and that at least one of those two paths contains a vertex in  V_{2}(G) .
Now, because  C  is good, there exist  a,b\in V_{2}(G)\cap C . It must be that, for each  1\leq i\leq 3 , the vertices  x_{i},y_{i}  interleave  a,b  on  C , because otherwise there would be a good theta-pair using  D_{i}  and a path  Q\subseteq H_{i}\cup C  which contains  a,b  as in the left side of the above figure. It also must be that  x_{i},y_{i}  do not interleave  x_{j},y_{j}  for  i\neq j , because otherwise there would be a good theta-pair using  D_{i}  and a path  Q\subseteq H_{i}\cup H_{j}\cup C  containing a vertex in  V_{2}(G)\cap V(D_{j}) , and a vertex in  V_{2}(G)\cap V(C) , as in the right side of the above figure.
Hence it follows (wlog) that the vertices  a,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},b,y_{3},y_{2},y_{1}  occur in that cyclic order around  C . But now  H_{1}\cup C  has an unbalanced theta while  H_{2}\cup H_{3}\cup C  has a theta with a degree-2 vertex on each branch ( x_{3}  and  y_{3}  are the vertices of degree 3 in that theta), and so that these subgraphs are disjoint. Since the later subgraph contains a good cycle, this contradicts that  G  is fragile and completes the proof.
∎
For the next lemma, we define the family of exception signed graphs mentioned at the start of this section.
Definition 17.
We say a signed graph  G  is a fish if  G  is formed by taking the union of a theta  H  whose branches have length two, two, and three; and a path  P  of odd-length at least three which is internally disjoint from  H  but whose ends are the adjacent degree-2 vertices of  H . And the signature of  G  is such that  H  is balanced but  H\cup P  is unbalanced. See Figure 3.
Figure 3: A family of fish signed graphs: signed graphs having an equivalent signature with a single negative edge shown in red. The three black dots represent an even number (zero or more) of consecutive degree-two vertices.
We also require the following theorem of Watkins and Mesner [13].
Theorem 18 ([13]).
Let  G  be a  2 -connected subcubic graph and let  x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in V(G)  be distinct. Either there exists a cycle  C\subseteq G  with  x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in V(C)  or there is a partition of  V(G)  into  \{Y_{1},Y_{2},X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}\}  with  x_{i}\in X_{i}  for  1\leq i\leq 3  satisfying
• 
 e(X_{i},Y_{j})=1  for  1\leq i\leq 3  and  1\leq j\leq 2 , and
• 
 e(Y_{1},Y_{2})=0=E(X_{i},X_{j})  for  1\leq i<j\leq 3 .
Lemma 19.
Let  G  be a 2-connected, fragile, well-behaved signed graph with no good theta-pair, and assume that there exists in  G  a good cycle disjoint from some even-length negative cycle. Then either  G  is isomorphic to fish, or there exist cycles  C_{0},C_{1}  satisfying the following:
1. 
 C_{1}  is negative and  C_{0}  is good
2. 
 |V_{2}(G)\cap V(C_{1})|\geq 1 
3. 
 G-V(C_{0})  and  G-V(C_{1})  have the same negative cycles.
Proof.
Choose a good cycle  C  and an even-length negative cycle  N  for which  V(C)\cap V(N)=~\emptyset . By Lemma 15, let  \{x,y\}=V_{3}(G)\cap V(N) . Note, of course, that this means there is a 2-edge cut separating  V(N)  and  V(G)\setminus V(N) . Again by Lemma 15,  |V_{2}(G)\cap V(N)|\geq 1 , but since  N  has even-length it must be that  |V_{2}(G)\cap V(N)|\geq 2 . By 2-connectivity, we may choose two edge-disjoint paths  P_{1},P_{2}  from a vertex in  V_{2}(G)\setminus V(N)  and  x,y .
This means that if there was a degree-2 vertex on both of the  x,y -paths in  N , then  N\cup P_{1}\cup P_{2}  would be a theta which contains a pair of cycles satisfying the lemma. Hence we may assume  x  and  y  are adjacent in  N , and we let  Q\subseteq N  be the longest  x,y -path in  N . Note that  |V(Q)\cap V_{2}(G)|\geq 2 .
If  G-E(Q)  has a negative cycle  N^{\prime} , then by 2-connectivity there is a good theta-pair using  N^{\prime}  and a path containing  Q , a contradiction. Hence  G-E(Q)  is balanced.
In particular,  G  can be signed with a single negative edge contained in  E(Q) .
Now, modify  G  to form  G^{\prime}  by identifying  N  to a new (degree-2) vertex called  x_{1} . Let  x_{2},x_{3}  be distinct degree-two vertices in  V(C) . The graph  G^{\prime}  cannot have a cycle containing  x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}  as this would give a good theta-pair (contradicting our assumption). Therefore, by applying Theorem 18 to the graph  G^{\prime}  we deduce that the original graph  G  has a vertex partition  \{Y_{1},Y_{2},X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}\}  with  N\subseteq G[X_{1}] ,  x_{i}\in X_{i}  for  2\leq i\leq 3 , and so that
 e(X_{i},Y_{j})=1  for  1\leq i\leq 3 ,  1\leq j\leq 2 , and  e(Y_{1},Y_{2})=0=E(X_{i},X_{j})  for  1\leq i<j\leq 3 . Choose this structure so that  |V(Y_{1})\cup V(Y_{2})|  is maximum. This means that each  X_{i}  is 2-connected or a single vertex.
For  i=2,3 , it must be that  |V_{2}(G)\cap X_{i}|=1 , because otherwise there would be a good cycle in  X_{i}  contradicting that  G  is fragile. Because  G  is well-behaved, this means that  X_{2}=\{x_{2}\} , and  X_{3}=\{x_{3}\} . If  N\neq G[X_{1}] , then  X_{1}  contains an unbalanced theta which is disjoint from a good cycle using  x_{2},x_{3} , contradicting that  G  is fragile. Hence  G[X_{1}]=N .
Similarly, for  i=1,2  if  Y_{i}\cap V_{2}(G)\neq\emptyset , then  G  has a good theta-pair using  N  and a path containing a degree-2 vertex in  Y_{i}  and one of the degree-2 vertices in  X_{2}  or  X_{3} . Because  G  is well-behaved this means each  Y_{i}  is also a single vertex. But now the graph  G  is isomorphic to fish, which completes the proof.
∎
With the above lemmas in place, we can now prove the following, which we will use going forward.
Lemma 20.
Let  G  be a 2-connected, fragile, well-behaved signed graph. Then either  G  is isomorphic to fish, or  G  has a pair of cycles  C_{0},C_{1}  satisfying the following:
1. 
 C_{1}  is negative and  C_{0}  is good
2. 
 G-V(C_{1})  has no unbalanced theta
3. 
 G-V(C_{0})  and  G-V(C_{1})  have the same even length negative cycles.
4. 
(a) 
 |V_{2}(G)\cap V(C_{1})|\geq 2  or
(b) 
 |V_{2}(G)\cap V(C_{1})|\geq 1  and the graph  G-V(C_{1})  has at most two negative cycles
Proof.
We proceed in two cases. First, suppose  G  has a good theta-pair. Then by Lemma 13  G  has a negative cycle  C_{1}  and a good cycle  C_{0}  so that  |V_{2}(G)\cap V(C_{1})|\geq 2 , and the graphs  G-V(C_{0})  and  G-V(C_{1})  have the same negative cycles. Clearly requirements 1, 3, and 4 are satisfied in this case. But an unbalanced theta is just a union of two negative cycles, and  G-V(C_{0})  and  G-V(C_{1})  have the same negative cycles. This means that because  G  is fragile (and so  G-V(C_{0})  has no unbalanced theta)  G-V(C_{1})  also does not have an unbalanced theta, and requirement 2 is satisfied.
Now suppose  G  has no good theta-pair. We proceed in two subcases.
In the first subcase, suppose there is a good cycle  C_{0}  so that every negative cycle in  G-V(C_{0})  has odd-length. Then by Lemma 14 there is a negative cycle  C_{1}  with  |V_{2}(G)\cap V(C_{1})|\geq 1  so that every negative cycle of  G-V(C_{1})  is contained in  G-V(C_{0}) . Since by assumption of this subcase  G-V(C_{0})  has no even-length negative cycles, the same is true for  G-V(C_{1}) , Hence  C_{0}  and  C_{1}  satisfy requirement 1, 3, and the first part of 4 (b) of the lemma. As before, because  G  is fragile  G-V(C_{0})  has no unbalanced theta, which means the same is true for  G-V(C_{1}) . Thus  C_{0}  and  C_{1}  also satisfy 2. Finally, Lemma 16 means that  G-V(C_{0})  (and so also  G-V(C_{1}) ) has at most two negative cycles, meaning the final part of 4 (b) is satisfied.
In the second subcase, by Lemma 12 let  C_{0}  be a good cycle in  G . Since we are not in the first subcase, let  N\subseteq G  be an even-length negative cycle disjoint from  C_{0} . If  G  is isomorphic to fish, the lemma is proved. And so we may assume  G  is not isomorphic to fish which means by Lemma 19 there exist a negative cycle  C_{1}  and a good cycle  C_{0}  in  G  so that  |V_{2}(G)\cap V(C_{1})|\geq 1 , and the graphs  G-V(C_{0})  and  G-V(C_{1})  have the same negative cycles. Recalling that  G  is fragile, this means requirements 1, 2, 3, and the first part of 4 (b) of the lemma are satisfied by  C_{0}  and  C_{1} . And, again by Lemma 16 the final part of 4 (b) is satisfied, completing the proof.
∎
5 Constructing a sequence of generalized cycles using the Cycle Selection Algorithm
With the lemmas of the previous two sections in place, we describe an algorithm to select our list of cycles. We will also require the following lemma from [5].
Lemma 21 ([5]).
Let  G  be a 3-connected signed graph that has two vertex-disjoint negative cycles. Then one of the following holds:
1. 
 G  has vertex-disjoint negative cycles  C_{1} ,  C_{2}  so that  V(C_{1})\cup V(C_{2})=V(G) , or
2. 
 G  has a negative cycle  C  so that  G\setminus V(C)  has an unbalanced theta.
Cycle Selection Algorithm
Input: A signed 3-edge-connected cubic graph  G  with two disjoint negative cycles
Output: A non-empty ordered list  \mathcal{C}=C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{t}  of ‘cycles’, where each cycle in  \mathcal{C}  is classified one of four ways: positive, negative ordinary, negative special, or fish
Pre-process step: One of the two cases in Lemma 21 holds for  G . If it is the first case, then let  C_{1},C_{2}  be the two cycles given in that case. Declare both of them to be negative special and return  \mathcal{C}=C_{1},C_{2} .
The second case of Lemma 21 must hold. Let  C_{1}  be the negative cycle supplied in that case, and declare it to be negative special. The remainder of the algorithm is recursive. Begin with  \mathcal{C}=C_{1} .
Recursive step: Given  \mathcal{C}=C_{1},\dots,C_{i-1} . Let  G^{\prime}=G-\cup_{C\in\mathcal{C}}V(C) . Note that our assumptions imply that  G^{\prime}  is well-behaved.
1. 
If  G^{\prime}  is empty return  \mathcal{C} .
2. 
Let  H  be a leaf-block in the block-cut tree of  G^{\prime} , choose  H  so that  G^{\prime}-V(H)  has an unbalanced theta if possible.
(From this point forward,  H  has a usable cycle by Lemma 12 and because a vertex of degree 0 or 1 is usable.)
3. 
If  G^{\prime}  does not have an unbalanced theta, let  C_{i}  be a usable cycle in  H . If  C_{i}  is negative, then declare that  C_{i}  is negative ordinary. Otherwise declare that  C_{i}  is positive. Then, goto Recursive step with  \mathcal{C}\cup\{C_{i}\} .
(From this point forward,  G^{\prime}  has an unbalanced theta.)
4. 
If  H  is a negative cycle, then it is usable. Declare that  C_{i}=H  is negative ordinary, and goto Recursive step with  \mathcal{C}\cup\{C_{i}\} .
(From this point forward  H  has a good cycle by Lemma 12.)
5. 
Let  C_{i}  be a good cycle in  H , choosing so that  G^{\prime}-V(C_{i})  has an unbalanced theta if possible.
6. 
If  G^{\prime}-V(C_{i})  has an unbalanced theta, declare that  C_{i}  is positive and goto Recursive step with  \mathcal{C}\cup\{C_{i}\} .
(From this point forward,  G^{\prime}  is fragile by definition, and  G^{\prime}=H  is 2-connected.)
7. 
Let  k  be the number of even-length negative ordinary cycles in  \mathcal{C} .
8. 
(a) 
If  G^{\prime}  is switching equivalent to a fish graph proceed as follows: If  k  is odd, declare that  C_{i}=G^{\prime}  is fish and goto Recursive step with  \mathcal{C}\cup\{C_{i}\} . If  k  is even, let  C_{i}  be the good cycle in  G^{\prime} , declare that  C_{i}  is positive and goto Recursive step with  \mathcal{C}\cup\{C_{i}\} .
(b) 
 G^{\prime}  is not switching equivalent to a fish graph.
• 
Let  C_{0},C_{1}  be the cycles supplied by Lemma 20.
• 
Let  j  be the number of even-length negative cycles in  G^{\prime}-V(C_{0})  (which is equal to the number of even-length negative cycles in  G^{\prime}-V(C_{1}) ).
If  k+j  is even, let  C_{i}  be  C_{1}  and declare  C_{i}  to be negative special. Otherwise let  C_{i}  be  C_{0}  and declare  C_{i}  to be positive. Then, goto Recursive step with  \mathcal{C}\cup\{C_{i}\} .
The following lemmas describe properties of the Cycle Selection Algorithm.
Lemma 22.
The Cycle Selection Algorithm terminates. The members of the returned list  \mathcal{C}  are vertex-disjoint and  V(G)=\cup_{C\in\mathcal{C}}V(C) .
Proof.
The first sentence is true because an element from a finite set is added to  \mathcal{C}  at each recursive step. The second sentence follows from Lemma 21 for the pre-process step, and for the recursion it follows by definition of  G^{\prime} , and because  G^{\prime}  is empty when the algorithm terminates.
∎
Lemma 23.
The Cycle Selection Algorithm either exits in the pre-process step, or reaches Step 8 exactly once.
Proof.
If the algorithm doesn’t exit in the pre-process step, then it begins the recursion with an unbalanced theta in the graph  G^{\prime} . This unbalanced theta remains in the graph  G^{\prime}  until step 8 is reached, and so step 8 is reached at least once. At step 8, one of steps 8a or 8b is executed and adds a cycle to  \mathcal{C}  which removes all unbalanced thetas from the next graph to be processed. (This is straightforward to see in step 8a, and in step 8b it follows from Lemma 20). Since  G^{\prime}  must have an unbalanced theta to reach step 8, it follows that step 8 can only be reached once.
∎
Lemma 24.
In the Cycle Selection Algorithm, the first cycle  C_{1}  in the returned list  \mathcal{C}  is negative special, and at most one other cycle in  \mathcal{C}  is negative special.
Proof.
The first part is straightforward. By Lemma 23, step 8 is encountered at most once in the algorithm. In particular this means step 8b is executed at most once. Since step 8b is the only recursive step to add a negative special cycle to  \mathcal{C} , the lemma follows.
∎
Lemma 25.
If  \mathcal{C}=C_{1},\dots,C_{t}  is returned by the Cycle Selection Algorithm, and  C_{j}\subseteq\mathcal{C}  is type negative special with  j\neq 1 , then either  e(\cup_{i=1}^{j-1}V(C_{i}),V(C_{j}))\geq 2 , or  e(\cup_{i=1}^{j-1}V(C_{i}),V(C_{j}))\geq 1  and at most two negative cycles come after  C_{j}  in the list  \mathcal{C} .
Proof.
If the algorithm exits in the pre-process step, then  t=2  and the lemma holds. And so we may assume  C_{j}  is added to  \mathcal{C}  in step 8b because that is the only recursive step to add a negative special cycle. But in step 8b, the negative special cycle is supplied by Lemma 20. This lemma follows from Part 4 of Lemma 20 because the input graph  G  is cubic.
∎
Lemma 26.
If  \mathcal{C}=C_{1},\dots,C_{t}  is returned by the Cycle Selection Algorithm, and  C_{j}\subseteq\mathcal{C}  is type fish, then  j=t .
Proof.
Step 8a of the algorithm is the only step to add a fish-type cycle to  \mathcal{C} . At that point, the graph  G^{\prime}  being processed is a fish graph. And so  C_{j}  is the last cycle to be added to  \mathcal{C} .∎
Lemma 27.
In the Cycle Selection Algorithm, every negative ordinary cycle  C_{j}  in the returned list  \mathcal{C}=C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{t}  is induced and satisfies  e(V(C_{j}),\cup_{i=j+1}^{t}V(C_{i}))\leq 1 . (In other words, all but at most one of the edges leaving a negative ordinary cycle go to the cycles earlier in the list).
Proof.
The algorithm adds a negative ordinary cycle  C_{j}  to  \mathcal{C}  only in steps 3 and 4. In both cases,  C_{j}  is a negative usable cycle and so has at most one vertex not in  V_{2}(G^{\prime})  by definition. Because the input graph is cubic the lemma follows.
∎
Lemma 28.
In the Cycle Selection Algorithm, every positive cycle  C_{j}  in the returned list  \mathcal{C}=C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{t}  has two distinct vertices  x,y\in V(C_{j})  so that  x  has a neighbour in  \cup_{i=1}^{j-1}V(C_{i})  and one of the following is true.
1. 
 y  also has a neighbour in  \cup_{i=1}^{j-1}V(C_{i}) .
2. 
 y  is incident with a cut edge in  \cup_{i=j}^{t}V(C_{i}) .
Proof.
The algorithm adds a positive cycle  C_{j}  to  \mathcal{C}  only in steps 3, 6 and 8b. In all cases the cycle added is good. But a good cycle is either a single vertex of degree at most one, or has at least two vertices of degree two, and so the lemma follows because the input graph is cubic.
∎
Lemma 29.
If  \mathcal{C}  is the list returned by the Cycle Selection Algorithm, the number of even-length negative ordinary cycles plus the number of special cycles in  \mathcal{C}  is even.
Proof.
Let  \mathcal{C}=C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{t}  be the list returned by the algorithm on input graph  G .
If the algorithm finishes in the pre-process step, then the lemma clearly holds. And so we may assume that the recursive step is reached. By Lemma 23, step 8 is reached exactly once, and therefore executes exactly one of 8a or 8b. If 8a is executed, then either a fish is added or both a positive cycle and an even-length negative ordinary cycle is added to  \mathcal{C}  (the negative cycle is added on the next step in the recursion). Either way this fixes the number of even-length negative ordinary cycles in  \mathcal{C}  to be odd. Since there is exactly one negative special cycle in  \mathcal{C}  the lemma follows.
If 8b is executed, note that in the following recursive call  G^{\prime}  is a graph with no unbalanced theta, and so every negative cycle is its own block. It follows that every one of those negative cycles will be added to  \mathcal{C} . The choice of adding either a negative special cycle or a positive cycle in that step fixes the parity required for this lemma.
∎
The following lemma will be used to establish sufficient connectivity to find a perfect matching in a certain auxiliary graph later. Let  G=(V,E)  be a connected graph and let  X\subseteq V . Denote by  \delta(X)  the set of edges with one end in  X  and the other in  V\setminus X . As in [5], we say that a cycle  C  straddles the edge-cut  \delta(X)  if  C  contains an edge with both ends in  X  and an edge with both ends in  V\setminus X .
Lemma 30.
Let  G  be a signed 3-edge-connected cubic graph with two disjoint negative cycles, and let  \mathcal{C}=C_{1},\dots,C_{t}  be the list of cycles returned when  G  is input to the Cycle Selection Algorithm. Let  \mathcal{N}  be the set of all but the last two even-length negative ordinary cycles in  \mathcal{C} . Then for every  C_{i},C_{j}\in\mathcal{N}  and every edge cut  S\subseteq E(G) 
1. 
if  C_{i}  straddles  S , then  |S|\neq 3 , and
2. 
if  C_{i}  and  C_{j}  straddle  S , and  i\neq j , then  |S|\neq 4 .
Proof.
Suppose for contradiction part two is not true. Then there exists  S=E(A,B)  with  |S|=4  so that both  C_{i}  and  C_{j}  straddle  S . By Lemma 24  C_{1}\neq C_{i},C_{j}  because  C_{1}  is negative special. This means  E(C_{1})\cap S=\emptyset  and so without loss let  V(C_{1})\subseteq A . Let  k  be the minimum index for which  V(C_{k})\cap B\neq\emptyset  and note that  k\leq\min\{i,j\} . At the point when  C_{k}  is selected, we are operating in the graph  G-\cup_{h=1}^{k-1}V(C_{h})  and in this graph all vertices in  B  have degree 3. Every cycle selected by the algorithm has at least one degree 2 vertex (by Lemma 28 a positive cycle has at least one degree 2 vertex, by Lemma 27 a negative ordinary cycle has at least two degree 2 vertices, and by Lemma 25 a negative special cycle has at least one degree 2 vertex) so it must be that  k=\min\{i,j\} . But then  C_{k}  would be an ordinary negative cycle containing at least two degree 3 vertices, which is a contradiction to Lemma 27. This completes the proof of part two.
Next suppose (for a contradiction) that part one is false and let  S=E(A,B)  be a 3-edge-cut straddled by the negative ordinary cycle  C_{i} . Define  S=\{e,f,f^{\prime}\}  where  S\cap E(C_{i})=\{f,f^{\prime}\}  and note that because  G  is 3-edge-connected and cubic, the edges  e,f,f^{\prime}  are pairwise non-adjacent.
As before, we may assume  V(C_{1})\subseteq A  and we let  k  be the minimum index for which  V(C_{k})\cap B\neq\emptyset  (again note that  k\leq i ). Let  v  be the end of  e  in  B . Note that at the point when  C_{k}  is selected, we are operating in the graph  G-\cup_{h=1}^{k-1}V(C_{h})  and in this graph all vertices in  B  have degree 3 except possibly  v .
First consider the case that  k=i  and let  u,u^{\prime}  be the ends of the edges  f,f^{\prime}  in  B . When  C_{k}  is selected both  u  and  u^{\prime}  are degree 3, but since  C_{k}  is negative ordinary this is a contradiction to Lemma 27. So we must have  k<i  and hence  V(C_{k})\subseteq B . This means that  C_{k}  has at most one vertex of degree 2 when it is selected.
Observe that there are only two types of cycles that can be selected by our process with at most one vertex of degree two: negative special and positive. If  C_{k}  were negative special, it would have at most two negative cycles that follow it by Lemma 25. But this contradicts that  k\leq i  and  C_{i}\in\mathcal{N}  by definition of  \mathcal{N} . And so it must be that  C_{k}  is positive. Because at most one vertex in  V(C_{k})  has a neighbour in  A , by Lemma 28 it must be that there is a vertex  y\in V(C_{k})  incident to a cut-edge in  G[B] . But this contradicts that  G  is 3-edge-connected. And so  C_{k}  cannot be positive, a contradiction. This completes the proof of part one, and the lemma.
∎
6 Forming a  {\mathbb{Z}}_{3} -preflow
As in the parallel section in [5], we will begin our construction of a nowhere-zero 8-flow with a  \mathbb{Z}_{3} -preflow. For a subcubic bidirected graph  G=(V,E)  and abelian group  A , a function  \phi:E\rightarrow A  is a preflow when  \partial\phi(v)\neq 0  if and only if  \mathrm{deg}(v)\in\{1,2\} . We say  \partial\phi(v)  is the boundary of  \phi  at  v .
We will require the following lemma from [5].
Lemma 31 ([5]).
Let  C  be a cycle, and let  b:V(C)\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}_{3} . If  C  is negative, or all edges of  C  are positive and  \sum_{v\in V(C)}b(v)=0 ,
then there exists  \tau:E(C)\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}  so that  \partial\tau=b .
The following lemma is similar to the parallel lemma in [5]; the main differences are that here there is a fish-type ‘cycle’, and positive cycles are no longer guaranteed to have two edges to cycles before them in the list  \mathcal{C} .
We say the unique edge in fish that is not in a 2-edge cut is distinguished (as shown in Figure 4).
Lemma 32.
Let  G  be a signed 3-edge-connected cubic graph with two disjoint negative cycles, and let  \mathcal{C}=C_{1},\dots,C_{t}  be the list of cycles returned when  G  is input to the Cycle Selection Algorithm. Let  G^{*}  be an orientation of the graph obtained from  G  by subdividing one edge on every negative special and even-length negative ordinary cycle in  \mathcal{C} . Then there exists a preflow  \phi:E(G^{*})\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{3}  so that  \phi(e)=0  only when  e  lies in (a subdivision of) a negative special cycle, a positive cycle, or  e  is the distinguished edge in fish.
Proof.
For every  C_{i}\in\mathcal{C}  that is a cycle in  G , let  C_{i}^{*}  be the corresponding cycle in  G^{*}  which is obtained from  C_{i}  by at most one subdivision. Denote the corresponding list of these cycles by  \mathcal{C}^{*} . We will use the same classification: positive, negative ordinary, negative special, or fish for both  C_{i}  and  C_{i}^{*} .
For  1\leq k\leq t  let  U_{k}=\bigcup_{i=k+1}^{t}V(C_{i}^{*})  and  D_{k}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k-1}V(C_{i}^{*})  ( U  for up and  D  for down). Let  F_{k}  denote  E(V(C_{k}^{*}),D_{k})  and  E_{k}  denote  E(V(C_{k}^{*}),U_{k}) .
Before we start, we choose a signature of  G^{*}  obeying certain qualities on the cycles  C_{k}^{*}\in\mathcal{C^{*}} . Note that by switching only on the vertices of  C_{k}^{*} , we may change the sign of the edges in  E(C_{k}^{*})  without affecting the sign of the edges of any other cycle in  \mathcal{C^{*}} . First, if  C_{k}^{*}  is positive, we choose a signature so that every edge of  E(C_{k}^{*})  is positive. Similarly, if  C_{k}^{*}  is negative ordinary, because our subdivisions have ensured that  C_{k}^{*}  has odd length, then we choose a signature so that every edge in  E(C_{k}^{*})  is negative. We make no restriction on the edges of  C_{k}^{*}  if it is negative special, but if  C_{k}^{*}  is fish then we choose a signature with exactly one negative edge (as in Figure 3).
We will construct a preflow  \phi:E(G^{*})\to\mathbb{Z}_{3} . Begin with  \phi  having empty domain, and we will extend the domain of  \phi  in steps, starting with the edges incident to a vertex in  V(C_{t}^{*}) , then  V(C_{t-1}^{*})  and proceeding backwards through the list  \mathcal{C^{*}}  finishing with the edges incident to a vertex in  V(C_{1}^{*})  at which point the domain of  \phi  will be  E(G^{*}) . For  i=t\dots 1 , at each step  i  we will ensure that
• 
every  v\in V(C_{i}^{*})\cup U_{i}  satisfies the boundary requirements of a preflow (that is  \partial\phi(v)=~0  if and only if the degree of  v  is three), and
• 
for each edge  e  with both ends in  [V(C_{i}^{*})\cup U_{i}] ,  \phi(e)=0  only when  e  lies in a negative special cycle, a positive cycle, or is the distinguished edge in fish.
At each step  i  we proceed according to the type of  C_{i}^{*}\in G^{*} .
Case 1:  C_{k}^{*}  is fish.
By Lemma 26,  k=t  and  C_{k}^{*}  is the first graph to be processed. Without loss of generality, let  C_{k}^{*}  be partially oriented as in Figure 4 (the orientation of the distinguished edge is immaterial). For an edge  e\in C_{k}^{*} , set  \phi(e)=0  if  e  is the distinguished edge, and  \phi(e)=1  otherwise. Because  k=t , it follows that  E_{k}=\emptyset . Finally for each  f\in F_{k}  we may choose  \phi(f)=\pm 1  so its end in  V(C_{k}^{*})  has boundary zero. This satisfies the requirements of a preflow because every vertex  v\in C_{k}^{*}  has boundary  \partial\phi=0 .
Figure 4: A fish graph with partial orientation. The distinguished edge is unoriented and highlighted in grey. The signature has exactly one negative edge (with two opposite-pointing arrows).
Case 2:  C_{k}^{*}  is negative ordinary
By Lemma 27,  C_{k}^{*}  has no chords. Because every edge  e\in E(C_{k}^{*})  is negative, we may assume, by possibly reversing orientation of some edges, that every vertex is a source in  C_{k}^{*} . We set  \phi(C_{k}^{*})=\pm 1 , assigning the same value to each edge and choosing so that (by Lemma 27) the at most one vertex  w\in V(C_{k}^{*})  incident to an edge in  E_{k}  has zero boundary (if it exists). Now we assign a value  \phi(f)=\pm 1  to each edge  f\in F_{k}  so that the vertices of degree three on  C_{k}^{*}  have zero boundary. This leaves the vertex of degree two (if it exists) with non-zero boundary as desired.
Case 3:   C_{k}^{*}  is negative special
For every edge  f  that is either a chord of  C_{k}^{*}  or in  F_{k} , set  \phi(f)=1 . Now by Lemma 31, we may choose values  \phi(e)\in\{0,\pm 1\}  for the edges in  V(C_{k}^{*})  so that each vertex of degree three in  V(C_{k}^{*})  has zero boundary and the unique vertex of degree two has non-zero boundary.
Case 4:  C_{k}^{*}  is positive
We set  \phi(e)=1  for every chord edge  e  of  C_{k}^{*} . We proceed in two subcases.
If  |F_{k}|\geq 2 , then we proceed as in the analogous case in [5]. That is, for each  f\in F(C_{k})  we choose  \phi(f)=\pm 1  so that the sum of the boundary of  \phi  on the vertices of  C_{k}^{*}  is zero. This means that by Lemma 31 we may set  \phi(e)\in\{0,\pm 1\}  for every edge  e\in E(C_{k}^{*})  so that  \phi  has zero boundary at every vertex in  V(C_{k}^{*}) .
Otherwise by Lemma 28, it must be that  |F_{k}|=1  and there exists  y\in V(C_{k}^{*})  so that  y  is incident to an edge  e  in  E_{k}  and  e  is a cut-edge in the graph  H^{\prime}=G[U_{k}\cup V(C_{k}^{*})] . Let  H  be the component of  H^{\prime}-y  containing the other end of  e . Let  f  be the (unique) edge in  F_{k} .
Figure 5: A positive cycle  C_{k}  with  |F_{k}|=1 .
We must extend  \phi  to  E(C_{k}^{*})\cup\{f\} . If  s=\sum_{v\in V(C_{k}^{*})}\partial\phi(v)\neq 0 , then we may choose  \phi(f)=\pm 1  to make the sum of  \partial\phi  on the all the vertices of  C_{k}^{*}  equal to zero. If  s=0 , we must first modify the preflow on the edges with an end in  V(H) : for every edge  d  in the domain of  \phi , let  \phi^{\prime}(d)=-\phi(d)  if  d  has an end in  V(H)  and  \phi^{\prime}(d)=\phi(d)  otherwise. Then
 \partial\phi^{\prime}(v)=-\partial\phi(v)  for all vertices  v\in V(H)\cup\{y\} , and  \partial\phi^{\prime}(v)=\partial\phi(v)  for the remaining vertices in  V(H^{\prime}) .
This means  \phi^{\prime}  still satisfies the requirements of the preflow up to this step, but in particular  \partial\phi^{\prime}(y)=-\partial\phi(y)  (which is non-zero) while the boundaries of the remaining vertices in  V(C_{k})  haven’t changed. This means  s^{\prime}=\sum_{v\in V(C_{k}^{*})}\partial\phi^{\prime}(v)\neq 0 . But now letting  \phi=\phi^{\prime}  puts us in the  s\neq 0  case and we choose  \phi(f)=\pm 1  to make the sum of  \partial\phi  on the all the vertices in  C_{k}^{*}  equal to zero. Now by Lemma 31 we set  \phi(e)\in\{0,\pm 1\}  on the edges of  C_{k}^{*}  so that  \phi  has zero boundary at every vertex in  V(C_{k}^{*}) .
∎
7 Proof of the main theorem
In this section we put together our lemmas to complete the proof of our main theorem. We follow the method set out in [5], and use machinery from there. We also call on the following theorem of Lu, Luo, and Zhang [10].
Theorem 33 (Lu et. al.).
If  G  is a flow-admissible signed graph with no two edge-disjoint negative cycles, then  G  has a nowhere-zero  6 -flow.
Next, we state two lemmas and a definition from [5], before we conclude with the proof of our main theorem.
Lemma 34 ([5]).
Let  G  be a subdivision of a 3-connected cubic graph, let  x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in V_{2}(G) , and for  1\leq i\leq k  let  C_{i}  be a cycle of odd length in  G  with  x_{i}\in V(C_{i}) . If the following conditions are satisfied:
• 
 |V_{2}(G)|  is even,
• 
 V(C_{i})\cap V(C_{j})=\emptyset  whenever  i\neq j ,
• 
 |V_{2}(G)\setminus\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}|<6 ,
• 
If  C_{i}  straddles an edge cut  S , then  |S|\neq 3 , and
• 
If  C_{i}  and  C_{j}  straddle an edge cut  S , and  i\neq j , then  |S|\neq 4 ,
then  G  contains a perfect matching.
Definition 35 ([5]).
Let  \phi:E(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{3}  be a pre-flow in an oriented signed graph  G  with all vertices of degree 2 or 3, and assume that  Z:=\{e\in E(G)\mid\phi(e)=0\}  is a matching. We will show how to construct the auxiliary graph associated with  G  and  \phi .
Without loss of generality, we may assume that every edge in  Z  is positive, and  \phi(E(G))\subseteq\{0,1\} . First, modify  G  to form a new bidirected graph  G^{+}  by adding a single new vertex  w  and then for every vertex  v\in V_{2}(G)  we add a positive edge with ends  v,w . We may extend the function  \phi  to a function  \phi^{+}  with domain  E(G^{+})  by assigning each newly added edge  e  a suitable orientation and setting  \phi^{+}(e)=1  so that  \partial\phi^{+}(v)=0  holds for every  v\in V(G) . Let us comment that  w  has only been added to make the graph (without  w ) cubic for the forthcoming modification and will be deleted later.
Figure 6: Auxiliary graph construction
Let  e=uu^{\prime}\in E(G^{+})  have  \phi(e)=0 . Note  w  is not an end of  e . Let  h_{1},h_{2}  and  h_{1}^{\prime},h_{2}^{\prime}  be the other edges incident to  u  and  u^{\prime}  respectively; without loss we may assume their nearest arrows to  u,u^{\prime}  are oriented as in the left side of Figure 6. Modify  G^{+}  by subdividing  h_{1}  and  h_{2}^{\prime} , and adding a new edge in between the two new vertices. Let  H  be the graph (not signed or oriented) obtained by doing this process at every edge where  \phi  is zero, and then deleting the vertex  w . We call  H  the auxiliary graph associated with  G  and  \phi .
Lemma 36 ([5]).
Let  G  be an oriented signed graph with all vertices of degree 2 or 3, let  \phi:E(G)\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}  be a preflow with the property that  Z=\{e\in E(G)\mid\phi(e)=0\}  is a matching. If the auxiliary graph associated with  G  and  \phi  has a perfect matching, then there exists an integer preflow  \psi:E(G)\rightarrow\{0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\pm 3\}  so that  \psi+3\mathbb{Z}=\phi , and  \partial\psi(v)=\pm 1  for every  v\in V_{2}(G) .
Now we may conclude with our proof of the main result.
Proof of Theorem 3.
Suppose for contradiction that the theorem is false. Let  G  be a flow-admissible 3-edge-connected oriented signed graph which is a counterexample to the theorem and for which  3|E(G)|-4|V(G)|  is minimum. Then by Lemma 5,  G  is cubic; and
by Observation 10, every proper induced subgraph of  G  is well-behaved. Since  G  is cubic, if  G  does not have two vertex-disjoint negative cycles, then by Theorem 33  G  has a nowhere-zero 6-flow, which contradicts that  G  is a counterexample. Thus it must be that  G  has two vertex-disjoint negative cycles.
Now, input  G  to the Cycle Selection Algorithm, and let  \mathcal{C}=C_{1},\dots,C_{t}  be the list of cycles returned.
Modify  G  to form  G^{*}  by subdividing one edge from each  C_{i}  that is either negative special or has even-length and is negative ordinary. Let  \mathcal{C}^{*}=C_{1}^{*},\ldots,C_{t}^{*}  be the list of cycles in  G^{*}  corresponding to  \mathcal{C} , and let the cycles in  \mathcal{C}^{*}  inherit the type of their corresponding cycle. By Lemma 32, there is a preflow  \phi:E(G^{*})\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}  so that  \phi(e)=0  only when  e  lies in a negative special cycle, a positive cycle, or  e  is the distinguished edge in fish. Note that this means every vertex in  V(G^{*})  is incident to at least one edge  e  with  \phi(e)\neq 0 . Because  G^{*}  is subcubic, and by the boundary requirements of a preflow, the set  Z\in E(G^{*})  of edges  e  with  \phi(e)=0  is therefore a matching.
Let  H  be the auxiliary graph associated with  G^{*}  and  \phi  (see Definition 35). We will show that each negative ordinary cycle in  C^{*}  has a corresponding cycle in  H  of odd-length. Let  e\in E(G^{*})  be an edge in a negative ordinary cycle in  C^{*} . The structure of  phi  means  e  is not adjacent to an edge in  Z . But every edge which is subdivided in the construction of  H  is adjacent to an edge in  Z . Hence it follows that  e  is not subdivided in the construction of  H . Thus every negative ordinary cycle in  C^{*}  appears unaltered in  H . Because the negative ordinary cycles in  G^{*}  have odd-length, it follows that each has a corresponding cycle in  H  which also has odd-length. We are particularly concerned with those cycles in  H  which correspond to negative ordinary cycles of even-length in  \mathcal{C}  (because they are subdivided in  C^{*}  and so each contains a vertex of degree-two in  H ). Denote the set of those cycles by  \mathcal{C}_{H} .
Let  \mathcal{N}  be the set of all but the last two even-length negative ordinary cycles in  \mathcal{C} , let  \mathcal{N}^{*}  be the set of corresponding cycles in  G^{*} , and let  \mathcal{N}_{H}  be the set of corresponding cycles in  H  (so  \mathcal{N}_{H}  is all but the last two cycles in  \mathcal{C}_{H} ). By Lemma 30, no cycle in  \mathcal{N}  straddles a 3-edge-cut in  G , and no two of these cycles straddle a  4 -edge-cut in  G . By construction, it is straightforward to see that the same is true for  \mathcal{N^{*}}  and  G^{*} . Further, again by construction, the same is true for  H  and  \mathcal{N}_{H} : if there was an edge-cut  S  of size 3 or 4 which violated this requirement, it would have to contain two of the edges in the 4-cycle of a gadget (see Figure 6). But this is not possible because those edges are not in a cycle in  \mathcal{N}_{H} , and all but at most one of the edges in  S  are contained in some cycle in  \mathcal{N}_{H} .
Now, there are at most four degree-2 vertices in  H  which are not contained in a cycle in  \mathcal{N}_{H}  (the degree-2 vertices associated with the at most two negative special cycles in  \mathcal{C} , and the degree-two vertices contained in a cycle in  \mathcal{C}_{H}  but not  \mathcal{N}_{H} ). Also, by Lemma 29 it follows that there are an even number of degree-two vertices in  H . Hence, it follows from Lemma 34 that  H  has a perfect matching. This means that by Lemma 36 (and the structure of  \phi ), there is a integer-preflow  \psi:E(G^{*})\to\{0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\pm 3\}  so that  \psi(e)=0  only if  e  lies on a positive cycle, a negative special cycle, or  e  is the distinguished edge in fish; and  \partial\psi(v)=\pm 1  for every  v\in V_{2}(G^{*}) .
We construct  \mathbb{Z} -preflow  \tau:E(G^{*})\to\{0,\pm 1\}  which will ‘match’  \phi  at the vertices of degree-two (so that when  \phi,\tau  are summed in a certain way, the result is a flow), and so that every edge in  E(G^{*})  is non-zero in at least one of  \phi,\tau .
Beginning with  \tau=0 , we will modify  \tau  so that it is nonzero on exactly the positive cycles, negative special cycles, negative ordinary cycles with a degree-two vertex, and a positive cycle in fish. For every  C_{i}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}^{*}  that is positive, modify  \tau  by assigning  \tau(e)=\pm 1  for every  e\in E(C_{i}^{*})  so that  \partial\tau(v)=0  for every  v\in V(C_{i}^{*}) . For every  C_{i}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}^{*}  that is either negative special, or negative ordinary with a degree-two vertex, let  x_{i}  be the degree-two vertex in  C_{i}^{*} . Modify  \tau  by assigning  \tau(e)=\pm 1  for every  e\in E(C_{i}^{*})  so that  \partial\tau(v)=0  for every  v\in V(C_{i}^{*})\setminus\{x_{i}\}  and so that  \partial\tau(x_{i})=-2\partial\psi(x_{i}) .
Finally, if  C_{t}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}^{*}  is type fish, let  C\subseteq C_{t}^{*}  be a positive cycle containing the distinguished edge of  C_{t}^{*} . Modify  \tau  by assigning  \tau(e)=\pm 1  for every  e\in E(C)  so that  \partial\tau(v)=0  for every  v\in V(C) .
To conclude, notice that  2\psi+\tau  is a nowhere-zero 8-flow in  G^{*} . This gives a nowhere-zero  8 -flow in  G , which contradicts that  G  is a counterexample to the theorem and completes the proof.
∎
