Sums and products in sets of positive density
By  Florian K. Richter
August 24, 2026
Abstract
We develop an analytic approach that draws on tools from Fourier analysis and ergodic theory to study Ramsey-type problems involving sums and products in the integers. Suppose  Q  denotes a polynomial with integer coefficients. We establish two main results. First, we show that if  Q(1)=0 , then any set of natural numbers with positive upper logarithmic density contains a pair of the form  \{x+Q(y),xy\}  for some  x,y\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{1\} .
Second, we prove that if  Q(0)=0 , then any set of natural numbers with positive density relative to a new multiplicative notion of density, which arises naturally in the context of such problems, contains  \{x+Q(y),xy\}  for some  x,y\in\mathbb{N} .
Contents
1.  Introduction
In [Hin79], Hindman posed the following conjecture (see also [HLS03, Question 3] and [Ber96, Question 11]).
Conjecture 1.1 (Hindman’s conjecture).
For any finite coloring of  \mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}  there exist infinitely many  x,y\in\mathbb{N}  such that the set  \{x,y,x+y,xy\}  is monochromatic.
In fact, Hindman also formulated an extension of Conjecture 1.1 in [Hin79] (see also [HS12, Question 17.18], [GRS90, p. 84] and [BR09, Problem 6.5]).
Conjecture 1.2 (Generalized Hindman’s conjecture).
For any  k\in\mathbb{N}  and any finite coloring of  \mathbb{N}  there exist infinitely many  x_{1}<\ldots<x_{k}\in\mathbb{N}  such that all finite sums and all finite products formed using distinct elements from  \{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}  are monochromatic.
Note that Conjecture 1.1 corresponds to the case  k=2  of Conjecture 1.2.
Hindman’s conjectures stand among the most significant touchstone problems in Ramsey theory, exposing the limits of our understanding of the complex interplay between additive and multiplicative structures in the integers.
A major step towards resolving Conjecture 1.1 was taken in [Mor17], where it was shown that any finite coloring of  \mathbb{N}  admits infinitely many  x,y\in\mathbb{N}  such that the set  \{x,x+y,xy\}  is monochromatic.
The special case of Conjecture 1.1 where the coloring consists of two colors was solved recently in [Bow25]. Despite this progress, Conjecture 1.1 remains open. Moreover, Hindman’s conjectures are surrounded by a wide range of other unsolved Ramsey-type problems concerning the joint behavior of sums and products in the integers, see for example [Sah18, Section 7], [BM18, Section 6], [KMRR25, Section 5], [BS24, Section 6], [Alw24, Section 4], [Fra24], and [Bow25, Section 4].
Considerably more is known about questions of this nature when the setting of the integers is replaced by the setting of fields, such as the finite field with  p  elements  \mathbb{F}_{p} , or the rational numbers  \mathbb{Q} .
Shkredov [Shk10] used Fourier analysis to study the pattern  \{x,x+y,xy\}  in  \mathbb{F}_{p} , showing that for any  \delta>0  there is a cofinite set of primes  p  such that any subset of  \mathbb{F}_{p}  with relative density  \geqslant\delta  contains  \{x,x+y,xy\} .
Later, Green and Sanders [GS16] proved that
for any  r\in\mathbb{N}  there is a cofinite set of primes  p  such that any  r -coloring of  \mathbb{F}_{p}  admits a monochromatic quadruple  \{x,y,x+y,xy\} ; this result can be viewed as the natural analogue of Conjecture 1.1 in finite fields.
In [BM17], Bergelson and Moreira used techniques from ergodic theory to study monochromatic sums and products in countably infinite fields, such as  \mathbb{Q} . They proved that any set with positive density (with respect to a double Følner sequence, see [BM17, Definition 1.3]) contains a set of the from  \{x+y,xy\} . In [Kou26], Kousek refined the ergodic approach and extended its scope.
Finally, the analogues of Conjectures 1.1 and 1.2 in  \mathbb{Q}  were solved recently in [BS24] and [Alw23], respectively.
This goes to show that Fourier-analytic and ergodic-theoretic techniques have been used with great success to study the joint behavior of sums and products in fields. Despite this success, the application of these tools has not yet been adapted to the setting of the integers, as the non-amenability of affine integer actions presents serious obstructions. The main purpose of this paper is to use ideas from multiplicative number theory to overcome these obstacles and establish an analytic framework for studying Ramsey-type sum-product problems in  \mathbb{N}  based on methods from Fourier analysis and ergodic theory.
Our work is motivated by two open problems informed by Hindman’s conjectures. The first is a conjecture formulated several years ago by Moreira.
The density of a set of natural numbers  A\subseteq\mathbb{N}  is defined as
 d(A)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{|A\cap\{1,\ldots,N\}|}{N} 
(1.1)
whenever this limit exists.
Conjecture 1.3 (Moreira’s Conjecture).
Any subset of  \mathbb{N}  with positive density contains  \{x+y-1,xy\}  for some  x,y\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{1\} .
Observe that not every subset of  \mathbb{N}  with positive density contains  \{x+y,xy\}  due to simple divisibility constraints; for instance, the set of odd numbers has density  \frac{1}{2}  but does not contain such a configuration.
Moreira’s conjecture elegantly circumvents this issue via a translation of the first component, which dispels all such local obstructions.
The second motivating problem for this paper concerns the general question of which subsets of the integers contain the pattern  \{x+y,xy\} . Very little is currently known about this question, with some conjectures
being proposed in [BM18, Conjectures 6.4 and 6.5].
A natural class to consider are all sets with positive density that exhibit no modular biases. More precisely, we say a set  A\subseteq\mathbb{N}  is evenly distributed across all residue classes if for any  a\in\mathbb{N}  and  b\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}  the density  d(A\cap(a\mathbb{N}+b))  exists and equals  a^{-1}d(A) .
Problem 1.4.
Prove that any subset of  \mathbb{N}  with positive density that is evenly distributed across all residue classes contains  \{x+y,xy\}  for some  x,y\in\mathbb{N} .
While both Conjecture 1.3 and Problem 1.4 have been circulating the field for some time, neither has appeared in print.
However, extensions of both have recently been asked in [KMRR25, Questions 5.4 and 5.2].
1.1.  Main results
Our main theorems resolve Conjecture 1.3 and Problem 1.4. Before formulating them, we first introduce some necessary notation.
Given a finite set  B\subseteq\mathbb{N}  and a function  f\colon B\to\mathbb{C} , the Cesàro average of  f  over  B  and the logarithmic average of  f  over  B  are defined receptively as
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in B}f(n)=\frac{\sum_{n\in B}f(n)}{|B|}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in B}f(n)=\frac{\sum_{n\in B}\frac{f(n)}{n}}{\sum_{n\in B}\frac{1}{n}}. 
Throughout this paper, we use  [N]  to abbreviate the set  \{1,\dots,N\} .
The upper density of a set  A\subseteq\mathbb{N}  is defined as
 \overline{d}(A)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}1_{A}(n), 
and the upper logarithmic density of  A  is
 \overline{\delta}(A)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}1_{A}(n). 
Note that for all  A\subseteq\mathbb{N}  we have  \overline{\delta}(A)\leqslant\overline{d}(A) , and if the density  d(A)  exists (as defined in (1.1)) then
 d(A)=\overline{d}(A)=\overline{\delta}(A). 
(1.2)
As was already observed in the 1930’s in the works of Besicovitch [Bes35] and Davenport-Erdős [DE36], sets with positive upper logarithmic density have far richer multiplicative structure than sets with positive upper density (see also [Erd35, DE51] and [BBHS06, Section 2]).
Our first result reaffirms this heuristic, showing that in Moreira’s conjecture the positive density assumption can be weakened to positive upper logarithmic density. Furthermore, our result provides a natural polynomial extension of Conjecture 1.3.
Theorem 1.5.
Let  Q  be a polynomial with integer coefficients satisfying  Q(1)=0 . Then
any  A\subseteq\mathbb{N}  with  \overline{\delta}(A)>0  contains  \{x+Q(y),xy\}  for some  x,y\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{1\} .
The case  Q(y)=0  of Theorem 1.5 recovers a classical result of Davenport and Erdős [DE36], which asserts that any set of positive upper logarithmic density contains  \{x,xy\}  for some  x,y\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{1\} .
By taking  Q(y)=y-1  and in light of (1.2), we see that Theorem 1.5 implies that Conjecture 1.3 is true.
Our proof of Theorem 1.5 reveals that sets with positive upper logarithmic density admit not only one but many configurations of the from  \{x+Q(y),xy\}  whenever  Q  is an integer polynomial with  Q(1)=0 . In fact, we show that for any  A\subseteq\mathbb{N}  and any  \varepsilon>0  there exist many “almost prime” numbers  y  such that the set  \{x\in\mathbb{N}:x+Q(y),xy\in A\}  has upper logarithmic density at least  \overline{\delta}(A)^{2}-\varepsilon . Loosely speaking, by “almost prime” numbers we mean positive integers that have relatively few prime factors; for the precise statement, see Theorem 1.10.
It is natural to ask wether the condition  \overline{\delta}(A)>0  in Theorem 1.5 can be relaxed to the weaker condition  \overline{d}(A)>0 . The following example shows that this is not possible.
Example 1.6.
The set  A=\bigcup_{n\geqslant 4}[2^{2^{n}},2^{2^{n}+\frac{1}{2}})  satisfies  \overline{d}(A)>0 , but does not contain patterns of the from  \{x+y-1,xy\}  for  x,y\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{1\} .

Theorem 1.7 is a consequence of a more general theorem that we prove, which provides optimal correlation estimates for the pattern  \{x+Q(y),xy\} , see Theorem 1.11 below.
Note that if  A\subseteq\mathbb{N}  has positive density and is evenly distributed across all residue classes, then  \mathrm{d}^{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\rotatebox[origin={c}]{-45.0}{$\rule{0.34422pt}{3.27773pt}\hskip 0.49164pt\rule{0.34422pt}{3.27773pt}$}\cr\rotatebox[origin={c}]{45.0}{$\rule{0.34422pt}{3.27773pt}\hskip 0.49164pt\rule{0.34422pt}{3.27773pt}$}\cr}}}\hskip-0.09995pt(A)>0 
. This means that choosing  Q(y)=y  in Theorem 1.7 resolves Problem 1.4.
Also, the subadditivity property of  \mathrm{d}^{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\rotatebox[origin={c}]{-45.0}{$\rule{0.34422pt}{3.27773pt}\hskip 0.49164pt\rule{0.34422pt}{3.27773pt}$}\cr\rotatebox[origin={c}]{45.0}{$\rule{0.34422pt}{3.27773pt}\hskip 0.49164pt\rule{0.34422pt}{3.27773pt}$}\cr}}}\hskip-0.09995pt(.) 
 implies that if  \mathbb{N}  is finitely colored, say  \mathbb{N}=C_{1}\cup\ldots\cup C_{r} , then at least one of the color classes  C_{i}  satisfies  \mathrm{d}^{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\rotatebox[origin={c}]{-45.0}{$\rule{0.34422pt}{3.27773pt}\hskip 0.49164pt\rule{0.34422pt}{3.27773pt}$}\cr\rotatebox[origin={c}]{45.0}{$\rule{0.34422pt}{3.27773pt}\hskip 0.49164pt\rule{0.34422pt}{3.27773pt}$}\cr}}}\hskip-0.09995pt(C_{i})>0 
. Therefore, for any finite coloring of  \mathbb{N}  and any integer polynomial  Q  with  Q(0)=0  one can find infinitely many  x,y\in\mathbb{N}  such that  \{x+Q(y),xy\}  is monochromatic, recovering a special case of Moreira’s Theorem [Mor17, Theorem 1.4]. This provides the first “analytic” proof that any finite coloring of  \mathbb{N}  admits a monochromatic pair  \{x+y,xy\} .
It is natural to ask whether in (1.3) the restriction to sequences  (N_{s})_{s\in\mathbb{N}}  for which the inside limit exists is necessary, especially since without this restriction one would obtain a more appealing and easier to comprehend notion of density. However, it turns out that this restriction cannot be omitted. Example 1.8 provides the construction of a set  A\subseteq\mathbb{N}  that satisfies
 \liminf_{a\to\infty}\sup_{m\in a\mathbb{N}}\Big(\limsup_{N\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}1_{A}(mn)\Big)\geqslant\frac{1}{8}, 
(1.4)
but for which there exist no  x,y\in\mathbb{N}  such that  \{x+y,xy\}\subseteq A .
Example 1.8.
Let  N_{k,\ell}=2^{5^{k^{2}+\ell}} , define
 A_{k,\ell}=2^{2\ell-1}(2\mathbb{N}+1)\cap\big[N_{k,\ell}^{1/2},N_{k,\ell}\big], 
and take  A=\bigcup_{\ell\leqslant k}A_{k,\ell} .
If  m=2^{2\ell-1}q  for some  q\in 2\mathbb{N}+1  then
a straightforward calculation reveals that
 \limsup_{N\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}1_{A}(mn)\geqslant\lim_{k\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N_{k,\ell}]}1_{A_{k,\ell}}(2^{2\ell-1}qn)=\frac{1}{4}. 
On the other hand, if  m=2^{2\ell}q  for some  q\in 2\mathbb{N}+1  then
 \limsup_{N\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}1_{A}(mn)\geqslant\lim_{k\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N_{k,\ell}]}1_{A_{k,\ell}}(2^{2\ell}qn)=\frac{1}{8}. 
Therefore, the set  A  satisfies (1.4).
Note that for any  (k,\ell),(k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})  with  \ell\leqslant k  and  \ell^{\prime}\leqslant k^{\prime} , if either  k<k^{\prime}  or  \ell<\ell^{\prime}  then we have
 N_{k,\ell}^{4}<N_{k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}} .
This means that
 x+y\in\big[N_{k,\ell}^{1/2},N_{k,\ell}\big],\penalty\ xy\in\big[N_{k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}^{1/2},N_{k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}\big]\implies(k,\ell)=(k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}). 
So if  A  contains  \{x+y,xy\} , then necessarily there exist  (k,\ell)  such that  A_{k,\ell}  contains  \{x+y,xy\} .
But the set  2^{2\ell-1}(2\mathbb{N}+1)  cannot contain the sum and the product of the same two numbers. Indeed, all numbers in  2^{2\ell-1}(2\mathbb{N}+1)  have identical  2 -adic valuation, and this  2 -adic valuation is an odd number. However, if the sum and the product of two given numbers have the same  2 -adic valuation, then this  2 -adic valuation must be even. We conclude that  2^{2\ell-1}(2\mathbb{N}+1)  does not contain  \{x+y,xy\} . Consequently,  A_{k,\ell}  does not contain  \{x+y,xy\} .
1.2.  Statement of main technical results
While Theorems 1.5 and 1.7 are framed as the main results of this paper, we actually derive them from two stronger theorems formulated in this subsection.
These are averaging versions of Theorems 1.5 and 1.7, whose formulations involve considerably more technical terminology, making them harder to state. However, they reveal more about the density aspects of the underlying problem.
In particular, they provide optimal correlation estimates for the patterns  \{x+Q(y),xy\}  with respect to the corresponding averages involved (see Remark 1.12 for more details).
On the integers  \mathbb{Z}=\{\ldots,-2,-1,0,1,2,\ldots\} , we define the shift map  \tau^{b}\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}  for  b\in\mathbb{Z}  and the dilation map  \sigma_{a}\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}  for  a\in\mathbb{N}  as
 \displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\tau^{b}(n)=n+b\hskip 10.00002pt 
 \displaystyle\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\sigma_{a}(n)=an. 
(1.5)
Since  \sigma_{a_{1}a_{2}}=\sigma_{a_{1}}\circ\sigma_{a_{2}}  and  \tau^{b_{1}+b_{2}}=\tau^{b_{1}}\circ\tau^{b_{2}}  for all  a_{1},a_{1}\in\mathbb{N}  and  b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathbb{Z} , the multiplicative semigroup  (\mathbb{N},\cdot)  and the additive semigroup  (\mathbb{Z},+)  naturally act on  \mathbb{Z}  via the representations  a\mapsto\sigma_{a}  and  b\mapsto\tau^{b}  respectively. In light of the distributive law
 \displaystyle\sigma_{a}\circ\tau^{b} 
 \displaystyle\,=\,\tau^{ab}\circ\sigma_{a}, 
(Distributive Law)
the joint action induced by  a\mapsto\sigma_{a}  and  b\mapsto\tau^{b}  corresponds to an action of the affine semigroup, i.e., the semigroup of maps  \{n\mapsto an+b:a\in\mathbb{N},\penalty\ b\in\mathbb{Z}\}  under the operation of composition.
We denote by  \ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})  the Banach space of all bounded complex-valued functions on  \mathbb{Z} , endowed with the supremum norm  \|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|f(n)| .
The action on  \mathbb{Z}  by the affine semigroup introduced above naturally extends to an anti-action on  \ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})  via
 \displaystyle f\mapsto f\circ\sigma_{a}\hskip 10.00002pt 
 \displaystyle\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptf\mapsto f\circ\tau^{b}. 
(1.6)
When endowed with pointwise addition and pointwise multiplication, the Banach space  (\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}),\|.\|_{\infty})  becomes a (unital)  C^{*} -algebra.
A  C^{*} -subalgebra of  \ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}) 
is a subset  \mathcal{A}\subseteq\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}) 
satisfying the following four properties:
– 
 \mathcal{A}  is closed (with respect to  \|.\|_{\infty} );
– 
 1_{\mathbb{Z}}\in\mathcal{A} ;
– 
if  f,g\in\mathcal{A}  and  \lambda,\eta\in\mathbb{C}  then  \lambda f+\eta g\in\mathcal{A} ;
– 
if  f\in\mathcal{A}  then  \overline{f}\in\mathcal{A} .
Note that the second property in this list specifies that  \mathcal{A}  is in fact a unital  C^{*} -subalgebra, as  1_{\mathbb{Z}}  is a multiplicative unit. Since we consider only unital  C^{*} -subalgebras throughout this paper, we omit the word “unital” from the definition. We caution the reader, however, that henceforth all occurrences of “ C^{*} -subalgebra” should be understood to mean “unital  C^{*} -subalgebra.”
We denote by  \mathbb{P}=\{2,3,5,7,11,\ldots\}  the set of prime numbers.
Definition 1.9.
Let  \mathbf{N}=([N_{s}])_{s\in\mathbb{N}} , where  N_{1}<N_{2}<\ldots\in\mathbb{N} , and
let  \mathbf{P}_{W}=(\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{t}])_{t\in\mathbb{N}} , where  M_{1}<M_{2}<\ldots\in\mathbb{N} ,  W\in\mathbb{N} , and define
 \mathbb{P}_{W}=\{p\in\mathbb{P}:p\equiv 1\bmod W\}. 
If  \mathcal{A}\subseteq\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})  is a  C^{*} -subalgebra then we say that  \mathcal{A}   \ldots 
• 
 \ldots  is separable if it contains a countable, dense subset;
• 
 \ldots  is translation invariant if for all  f\in\mathcal{A}  and  b\in\mathbb{Z}  we have  f\circ\tau^{b}\in\mathcal{A} ;
• 
 \ldots  is dilation invariant if for all  f\in\mathcal{A}  and  a\in\mathbb{N}  we have  f\circ\sigma_{a}\in\mathcal{A} ;
• 
 \ldots  is affinely invariant if it is both translation and dilation invariant.
• 
 \ldots  admits logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N}  if for all  f\in\mathcal{A}  the limit
 \lim_{s\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N_{s}]}f(n) 
exists.
In this case, we simply write
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(n)=\lim_{s\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N_{s}]}f(n) 
(1.7)
for all  f\in\mathcal{A} , to streamline notation.
• 
 \ldots  admits iterated affine correlations along  \mathbf{P}_{W}  if for all  f,g\in\mathcal{A} ,  k,a\in\mathbb{N} , and all polynomials  Q  with integer coefficients, the limits
 \lim_{t_{k}\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{k}\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{t_{k}}]}\cdots\lim_{t_{1}\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{t_{1}}]}\Big(\lim_{s\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N_{s}]}f(an+Q(p_{k}\cdots p_{1}))g(p_{k}\cdots p_{1}n)\Big) 
(1.8)
exist. In this case, instead of (1.8) we simply write
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(an+Q(p))g(pn), 
again to streamline notation.
In particular, we use  \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}^{\log}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}  to abbreviate the iterated average  \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}^{\log}_{p_{k}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\cdots\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}^{\log}_{p_{1}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}} .
When  W=1 , we simplify the notation further and write  \mathbf{P}  instead of  \mathbf{P}_{1}  and  \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}^{\log}_{p\in\mathbf{P}^{*k}}  instead of  \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}^{\log}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{1}^{*k}} .
Let us now state our two main technical results, from which Theorems 1.5 and 1.7 can be derived.
Theorem 1.10.
Let  N_{1}<N_{2}<\ldots\in\mathbb{N}  and  M_{1}<M_{2}<\ldots\in\mathbb{N} . Suppose  \mathcal{A}\subseteq\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})  is a separable, affinely invariant  C^{*} -subalgebra that admits logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N}=([N_{s}])_{s\in\mathbb{N}}  and iterated affine correlations along  \mathbf{P}_{W}=(\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{t}])_{t\in\mathbb{N}}  for all  W\in\mathbb{N} .
Let  Q  be a polynomial with integer coefficients satisfying  Q(1)=0 .
Then for any  1_{A}\in\mathcal{A}  and  \varepsilon>0  there exist  k,W\in\mathbb{N}  such that
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{A}(n+Q(p))1_{A}(pn)\geqslant\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{A}(n)\Big)^{2}-\varepsilon. 
(1.9)
Proof that Theorem 1.10 implies Theorem 1.5.
Suppose  A\subseteq\mathbb{N}  with  \overline{\delta}(A)>0  is given. Let  N_{1}<N_{2}<\ldots\in\mathbb{N}  be any sequence such that  \overline{\delta}(A)=\lim_{s\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{s}]}1_{A}(n) .
Let  \mathcal{A}\subseteq\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})  denote the smallest affinely invariant  C^{*} -subalgebra that contains  1_{A} . Since finite linear combinations of products of translations and dilations of  1_{A}  are dense in  \mathcal{A} , we see that  \mathcal{A}  is separable. Using a standard diagonalization argument and replacing  (N_{s})_{s\in\mathbb{N}}  with a subsequence of itself if necessary, we can assume without loss of generality that  \mathcal{A}  admits logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N}=([N_{s}])_{s\in\mathbb{N}} . Let  M_{1}<M_{2}<\ldots\in\mathbb{N}  be any sequence such that  \mathcal{A}  admits iterated affine correlations along  \mathbf{P}_{W}=(\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{t}])_{t\in\mathbb{N}}  for all  W\in\mathbb{N} ; again such a sequence exists because  \mathcal{A}  is separable.
If we now apply Theorem 1.10 then we obtain
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{A}(n+Q(p))1_{A}(pn)\geqslant\big(\overline{\delta}(A)\big)^{2}-\varepsilon.

Taking  x=n  and  y=p_{1}\cdots p_{k}  proves  \{x+Q(y),xy\}\subseteq A  as desired.
∎

2.  Preliminaries
In this section we collect preliminaries and preparatory results from number theory, ergodic theory, and Fourier analysis which are needed for the proofs of Theorems 1.10 and 1.11.
2.1.  Basic properties of logarithmic averages
We use logarithmic averages in the formulations of Theorems 1.10 and 1.11 and, as we have seen in Example 1.6, it is in general not possible to replace them by Cesàro averages.
This naturally prompts the question: which properties of logarithmic averages are necessary for our argument? An answer is given by the following lemma, which identifies a property of logarithmic averages that does not hold for Cesàro averages, and which will be used repeatedly in the proofs of our main results.
Lemma 2.1.
For any  q,N\in\mathbb{N}  and any  1 -bounded  f\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}  we have
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}f(n)=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}q1_{q\mid n}f\big(\tfrac{n}{q}\big)+{\mathrm{O}}\bigg(\frac{\log q}{\log N}\bigg). 
Proof.
We have
 \displaystyle\sum_{n\in[N]}\frac{f(n)}{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in[qN]\\
q\mid n\end{subarray}}\frac{f\big(\tfrac{n}{q}\big)}{\tfrac{n}{q}}=\sum_{n\in[qN]}\frac{q1_{q\mid n}f\big(\tfrac{n}{q}\big)}{n}=\sum_{n\in[N]}\frac{q1_{q\mid n}f\big(\tfrac{n}{q}\big)}{n}+{\mathrm{O}}(\log q). 
Hence,
 \displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}f(n) 
 \displaystyle=\frac{1}{\big(\sum_{n\in[N]}\frac{1}{n}\big)}\Bigg(\sum_{n\in[N]}\frac{f(n)}{n}\Bigg) 
 \displaystyle=\frac{1}{\big(\sum_{n\in[N]}\frac{1}{n}\big)}\Bigg(\sum_{n\in[N]}\frac{q1_{q\mid n}f\big(\tfrac{n}{q}\big)}{n}\Bigg)+{\mathrm{O}}\bigg(\frac{\log q}{\log N}\bigg) 
 \displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}q1_{q\mid n}f\big(\tfrac{n}{q}\big)+{\mathrm{O}}\bigg(\frac{\log q}{\log N}\bigg), 
as desired.
∎
2.2.  Preliminaries from multiplicative number theory
A corollary of the Turán-Kubilius inequality (cf. [Ell79, Chapter 4]) states that for any finite set of primes  P\subseteq\mathbb{P}\cap[N]  one has
 \sum_{n\in[N]}\left(\,\sum_{p\in P}\mathbf{1}_{p\mid n}-\sum_{p\in P}\frac{1}{p}\right)^{2}\leqslant 3N\Bigg(\sum_{p\in P}\frac{1}{p}\Bigg), 
(2.1)
where  \mathbf{1}_{p\mid n}  denotes the function that is  1  if  p  divides  n  and  0  otherwise.
Dividing (2.1) by  N(\sum_{p\in P}1/p)^{2} , we obtain the following equivalent version:
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in P}p\mathbf{1}_{p\mid n}-1\Big)^{2}\leqslant 3\Bigg(\sum_{p\in P}\frac{1}{p}\Bigg)^{-1}. 
(2.2)
Note that in (2.2), it is not possible to replace the logarithmic average in the variable  p  with a Cesàro average.
Since our main results utilize logarithmic averages in all variables, we require a variant of (2.2) that uses logarithmic averages in the variable  n  too.
One way to obtain such a variant would be to simply derive it from (2.2) using partial summation. Another approach – one we take – is to give a short and self-contained proof.
Proposition 2.2 (Logarithmically averaged Turán-Kubilius inequality).
Let  N\in\mathbb{N}  and  P\subseteq\mathbb{P}\cap[N] .
Then
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in P}\big(p1_{p\mid n}-1\big)\Big)^{2}\leqslant 9\Bigg(\sum_{p\in P}\frac{1}{p}\Bigg)^{-1}. 
Proof.
By expanding the square, we obtain
 \displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in P}p1_{p\mid n}-1\Big)^{2} 
 \displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in P}p1_{\mathrm{lcm}(p,q)\mid n}\Big)^{2}-2\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in P}p1_{p\mid n}\Big)+1 
 \displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p,q\in P}pq\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}1_{\mathrm{lcm}(p,q)\mid n}\Big)-2\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in P}p\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}1_{p\mid n}\Big)+1. 
For the first term, we can estimate from above using
 \displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}1_{\mathrm{lcm}(p,q)\mid n} 
 \displaystyle=\frac{1}{\big(\sum_{n\in[N]}\frac{1}{n}\big)}\sum_{n\in[N]}\frac{1_{\mathrm{lcm}(p,q)\mid n}}{n}\leqslant\frac{1}{\mathrm{lcm}(p,q)}. 
For the second term, we can establish a bound from below by
 \displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}1_{p\mid n} 
 \displaystyle=\frac{1}{\big(\sum_{n\in[N]}\frac{1}{n}\big)}\sum_{n\in[N]}\frac{1_{p\mid n}}{n} 
 \displaystyle=\frac{1}{\big(\sum_{n\in[N]}\frac{1}{n}\big)}\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant N/p}\frac{1}{pn} 
 \displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{\big(\sum_{n\in[N]}\frac{1}{n}\big)}\sum_{N/p<n\leqslant N}\frac{1}{pn} 
 \displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{p}-\frac{\log(p)}{p\log(N)}, 
where the last estimate is quickly derived from a standard approximation of the harmonic sum  \frac{1}{2M}-\frac{1}{8M^{2}}\leqslant(\sum_{n\in[M]}\frac{1}{n})-\log(M)-\gamma\leqslant\frac{1}{2M} , where  \gamma  is the Euler-Mascheroni constant.
It follows that
 \displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in P}p1_{p\mid n}-1\Big)^{2} 
 \displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p,q\in P}\frac{pq}{\mathrm{lcm}(p,q)}-1+2\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in P}\frac{\log(p)}{\log(N)}. 
To finish the proof, note that
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p,q\in P}\frac{pq}{\mathrm{lcm}(p,q)}\leqslant 1+\left(\sum_{p\in P}\frac{1}{p}\right)^{-1}, 
and that
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in P}\frac{\log(p)}{\log(N)}=\frac{\big(\sum_{p\in P}\frac{\log(p)}{p}\big)}{\big(\sum_{p\in P}\frac{1}{p}\big)\log(N)}. 
By Mertens’ first theorem and since  P\subseteq\mathbb{P}\cap[N] , we have
 \sum_{p\in P}\frac{\log(p)}{p}\leqslant\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}\cap[N]}\frac{\log(p)}{p}\leqslant\log(N)+2. 
Therefore,
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in P}\frac{\log(p)}{\log(N)}\leqslant 4\left(\sum_{p\in P}\frac{1}{p}\right)^{-1}. 
The claim follows by combining the above estimates.
∎
The way we employ Proposition 2.2 in the subsequent proofs is via the following two corollaries.
Corollary 2.3.
For  W\in\mathbb{N}  and any bounded function  f\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}  we have
 \limsup_{M\to\infty}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}f(n)-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M]}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}f(pn)\Big|=0. 
Proof.
Using Lemma 2.1 and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain
 \displaystyle\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}f(n)-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M]}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}f(pn)\Big| 
 \displaystyle=\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}f(n)-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M]}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}p1_{p\mid n}f(n)\Big| 
 \displaystyle=\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}f(n)\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M]}\big(1-p1_{p\mid n}\big)\Big)\Big| 
 \displaystyle\leqslant\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}|f(n)|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M]}\big(1-p1_{p\mid n}\big)\Big|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}. 
The claim now follows from Proposition 2.2 together with the fact that
 \sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{W}}\frac{1}{p}=\infty, 
which follows from the prime number theorem in arithmetic progressions.
∎
Corollary 2.4.
For  W\in\mathbb{N}  and any bounded function  f\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}  we have for any  k\in\mathbb{N}  that
 \limsup_{M_{k}\to\infty}\cdots\limsup_{M_{1}\to\infty}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}f(n)-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{k}\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{k}]}\cdots\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{1}]}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}f(p_{k}\cdots p_{1}n)\Big|=0. 
Proof.
This follows by iterating Corollary 2.3.
∎
2.3.  Preliminaries concerning exponential sums along primes
Throughout, we use the abbreviation  e(x)=e^{2\pi ix}  for  x\in\mathbb{R} .
It follows from the classical works of Vinogradov [Vin57, Vin58] and Rhin [Rhi73] that for any real polynomial  Q(x)=a_{d}x^{d}+\ldots+a_{1}x+a_{0}  with the property that at least one of the coefficients  a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}  is an irrational number one has
 \lim_{M\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{p\in\mathbb{P}\cap[M]}e(Q(p))=0. 
(2.3)
For the proof of our main results, we require a version of (2.3) using logarithmic averages; we include a proof for completeness.
Lemma 2.5.
For any  a\in\mathbb{N} ,  b\in\mathbb{Z} , any non-constant polynomial with rational coefficients  Q , and any irrational real number  \alpha  we have
 \lim_{M\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbb{P}\cap[M]}1_{a\mathbb{Z}+b}(p)\,e(Q(p)\alpha)=0. 
(2.4)
Proof.
We will make use of the basic identity
 1_{a\mathbb{Z}+b}(n)=\frac{1}{a}\sum_{r=0}^{a-1}e\bigg(\frac{(b-n)r}{a}\bigg). 
Define  Q_{r}(x)=Q(x)\alpha-\frac{xr}{a} .
We thus have
 \displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}\cap[M]}1_{a\mathbb{Z}+b}(p)\,\frac{e(Q(p)\alpha)}{p} 
 \displaystyle=\frac{1}{a}\sum_{r=0}^{a-1}e\bigg(\frac{br}{a}\bigg)\Bigg(\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}\cap[M]}\frac{e(Q_{r}(p))}{p}\Bigg). 
Let  \rho\in(1,2)  and pick  J\in\mathbb{N}  such that  M\in(\rho^{J},\rho^{J+1}] .
By the prime number theorem,  |\mathbb{P}\cap(\rho^{j},\rho^{j+1}]|\sim\frac{\rho^{j}}{j\log(\rho)} .
Hence
 \displaystyle\bigg|\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}\cap[M]} 
 \displaystyle\frac{e(Q_{r}(p))}{p}\bigg| 
 \displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{J}\bigg|\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}\cap(\rho^{j},\rho^{j+1}]}\frac{e(Q_{r}(p))}{p}\bigg|+{\mathrm{O}}(1) 
 \displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{J}\bigg|\frac{1}{\rho^{j}}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}\cap(\rho^{j},\rho^{j+1}]}e(Q_{r}(p))\bigg|+{\mathrm{O}}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{\rho-1}{j\log(\rho)}\bigg) 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{j\log(\rho)}\bigg|\frac{j\log(\rho)}{\rho^{j}}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}\cap(\rho^{j},\rho^{j+1}]}e(Q_{r}(p))\bigg|+{\mathrm{O}}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{\rho-1}{j\log(\rho)}\bigg). 
Note that by (2.3),
 \displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{j\log(\rho)}{\rho^{j}}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}\cap(\rho^{j},\rho^{j+1}]}e(Q_{r}(p))=\lim_{j\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{p\in\mathbb{P}\cap(\rho^{j},\rho^{j+1}]}e(Q_{r}(p))=0, 
because  Q_{r}(x)  is a polynomial with at least one irrational non-constant coefficient. Therefore, we are left with
 \sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{j\log(\rho)}\bigg|\frac{j\log(\rho)}{\rho^{j}}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}\cap(\rho^{j},\rho^{j+1}]}e(Q_{r}(p))\bigg|={\mathrm{o}}_{J\to\infty}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{j\log(\rho)}\bigg). 
Overall, this gives
 \bigg|\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}\cap[M]}1_{a\mathbb{Z}+b}(p)\,\frac{e(Q(p)\alpha)}{p}\bigg|={\mathrm{O}}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{\rho-1}{j\log(\rho)}\bigg). 
(2.5)
Using  M\in(\rho^{J},\rho^{J+1}]  and the prime number theorem, we see that
 \sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}\cap[M]}\frac{1}{p}\sim\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{j\log(\rho)}. 
(2.6)
Combining (2.5) and (2.6), we obtain
 \limsup_{M\to\infty}\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbb{P}\cap[M]}1_{a\mathbb{Z}+b}(p)\,e(Q(p)\alpha)\Big|={\mathrm{O}}\big(\rho-1\big). 
Letting  \rho  approach  1  finishes the proof.
∎
Below, we offer a slight variant of Lemma 2.5, which will be used in Section 5.3. Recall that  \mathbb{P}_{W}=\{p\in\mathbb{P}:p\equiv 1\bmod W\} .
Corollary 2.6.
For any  a,W\in\mathbb{N} ,  b\in\mathbb{Z} , any non-constant polynomial with rational coefficients  Q , and any irrational real number  \alpha  we have
 \lim_{M\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M]}1_{a\mathbb{Z}+b}(p)\,e(Q(p)\alpha)=0. 
(2.7)
Proof.
In light of the prime number theorem in arithmetic progressions, we have for any bounded function  u\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}  that
 \limsup_{M\to\infty}\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M]}u(p)-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbb{P}\cap[M]}W1_{W\mathbb{Z}+1}(p)\,u(p)\Big|=0. 
It is then clear that (2.7) follows from (2.4).
∎
2.4.  Preliminaries from ergodic theory
For the reminder of this section, let  \mathcal{H}  be Hilbert space with inner product  \langle.,.\rangle  and norm  \|.\| . A unitary operator on  \mathcal{H}  is a bounded linear operator  U\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}  which is invertible and preserves the inner product of the Hilbert space, that is, for all  f,g\in\mathcal{H}  we have
 \langle Uf,Ug\rangle=\langle f,g\rangle. 
We say that  \mathcal{H}  is the orthogonal direct sum of  \mathcal{H}_{1}  and  \mathcal{H}_{2} , and write  \mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2} , if  \mathcal{H}_{1}  and  \mathcal{H}_{2}  are closed subspaces of  \mathcal{H}  satisfying:
• 
 \langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle=0  whenever  f_{1}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}  and  f_{2}\in\mathcal{H}_{2} , and
• 
for every  f\in\mathcal{H} , there exist  f_{1}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}  and  f_{2}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}  such that  f=f_{1}+f_{2} .
Note that in this case,  f_{1}  and  f_{2}  are uniquely determined by  f . In fact,  f_{1}  equals the orthogonal projection of  f  onto the subspace  \mathcal{H}_{1} , whereas  f_{2}  is the orthogonal projection of  f  onto  \mathcal{H}_{2} .
Let us now state von Neumann’s mean ergodic theorem in an equivalent form.
Theorem 2.7 (Mean ergodic theorem).
Let  U\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}  be a unitary operator on a Hilbert space  \mathcal{H} . Then  \mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{inv}}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{erg}} , where
 \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{inv}}=\{f\in\mathcal{H}:Uf=f\}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{erg}}=\Big\{f\in\mathcal{H}:\lim_{H\to\infty}\Big\|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{h\in[H]}U^{h}f\Big\|=0\Big\}. 
A proof of Theorem 2.7 can be found in any standard book on ergodic theory, for example [Pet83, Theorem 1.2]. We will use the following well-known corollary of Theorem 2.7.
Corollary 2.8 (cf. [Ber96, p. 14]).
Let  U\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}  be a unitary operator on a Hilbert space  \mathcal{H} . Then
 \mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{rat}}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tot\,erg}} , where
 \displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{rat}} 
 \displaystyle=\overline{\big\{f\in\mathcal{H}:\penalty\ \exists q\in\mathbb{N},\penalty\ U^{q}f=f\big\}}, 
 \displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tot\,erg}} 
 \displaystyle=\Big\{f\in\mathcal{H}:\forall q\in\mathbb{N},\penalty\ \lim_{H\to\infty}\Big\|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{h\in[H]}U^{qh}f\Big\|=0\Big\}. 
Proof.
For every  q\in\mathbb{N} , define
 \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{inv},q}=\{f\in\mathcal{H}:U^{q}f=f\}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{erg},q}=\Big\{f\in\mathcal{H}:\lim_{H\to\infty}\Big\|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{h\in[H]}U^{qh}f\Big\|=0\Big\}. 
By Theorem 2.7, we have  \mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{inv},q}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{erg},q}  for all  q\in\mathbb{N} . Therefore,
 \mathcal{H}=\overline{\bigcup_{q\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{inv},q}}\,\oplus\,\bigcap_{q\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{erg},q}. 
It is now straightforward to verify that  \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{rat}}=\overline{\bigcup_{q\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{inv},q}}  and  \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tot\,erg}}=\bigcap_{q\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{erg},q} , and the proof is complete.
∎
It is worth noting that, in ergodic theory, functions in  \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tot,erg}}  are often referred to as totally ergodic functions with respect to the operator  U .
2.5.  Preliminaries from Fourier analysis
Throughout this paper, we identify the one-dimensional torus  \mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}  with the interval  [0,1) , endowed with addition modulo  1 . This allows us to treat elements of  \mathbb{T}  as real numbers in  [0,1) , which simplifies notation. As above, we use the abbreviation  e(x)=e^{2\pi ix}  for  x\in\mathbb{R} .
Let  \mu  be a finite Borel measure on  \mathbb{T} . The Fourier transform of  \mu  on  \mathbb{T}  is the function  \hat{\mu}\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}  defined as
 \hat{\mu}(m)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}e(mx)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu(x),\hskip 20.00003pt\forall m\in\mathbb{Z}. 
Since  \mu  is a finite measure, we have  |\hat{\mu}(m)|\leqslant\hat{\mu}(0)=\mu(\mathbb{T})<\infty  for all  m\in\mathbb{Z} , and hence  \hat{\mu}  is a bounded function.
It is natural to ask what type of bounded functions on  \mathbb{Z}  correspond to the Fourier transform of a finite Borel measure on  \mathbb{T} . The answer to this question is provided by a classical result in Fourier analysis known as Herglotz’s theorem.
A function  \varphi\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}  is called non-negative definite if for all  M\in\mathbb{N}  and  \lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{M}\in\mathbb{C}  one has
 \sum_{i,j=1}^{M}\lambda_{i}\overline{\lambda_{j}}\varphi(i-j)\geqslant 0. 
Equivalently,  \varphi  is non-negative definite if for any  M\in\mathbb{N}  the matrix  A\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}  defined by  A_{i,j}=\varphi(i-j)  for all  1\leqslant i,j\leqslant M  is a non-negative definite matrix.
Theorem 2.9 (Herglotz’s theorem).
Let  \varphi\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}  be bounded. Then  \varphi  is non-negative definite if and only if there exists a finite Borel measure  \mu  on  \mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}  such that  \varphi=\hat{\mu} , or in other words,
 \varphi(m)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}e(mx)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu(x),\hskip 20.00003pt\forall m\in\mathbb{Z}. 
If  \mathcal{A}\subseteq\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})  is a translation invariant  C^{*} -subalgebra that admits logarithmic averages along an increasing sequence  \mathbf{N}=(N_{s})_{s\in\mathbb{N}}  (see Definition 1.9), then it is straightforward to check that for any  f\in\mathcal{A}  the function
 m\mapsto\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(n)\overline{f(n+m)} 
is non-negative definite. By Herglotz’s theorem, there exists a finite Borel measure  \mu_{f}  on  \mathbb{T}  such that
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(n)\overline{f(n+m)}=\hat{\mu}_{f}(m)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}e(mx)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu_{f}(x). 
(2.8)
We call  \mu_{f}  the spectral measure of  f .
Lemma 2.10.
Let  N_{1}<N_{2}<\ldots\in\mathbb{N} , and let  \mathcal{A}\subseteq\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})  be an affinely invariant  C^{*} -subalgebra that admits logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N}=([N_{s}])_{s\in\mathbb{N}} . Then for any  \ell\in\mathbb{N} ,  m_{1},\ldots,m_{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z} ,  c_{1},\ldots,c_{\ell}\in\mathbb{C} , and  f\in\mathcal{A}  we have
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}c_{i}f(n+m_{i})\bigg|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}c_{i}e(m_{i}x)\bigg|^{2}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu_{f}(x), 
where  \mu_{f}  denotes the spectral measure of  f .
Proof.
By expanding the square and rearranging, we can rewrite the left hand side as
 \displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}c_{i}f(n+m_{i})\bigg|^{2} 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{\ell}c_{i}\overline{c_{j}}\,\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(n+m_{i})\overline{f(n+m_{j})} 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{\ell}c_{i}\overline{c_{j}}\,\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(n)\overline{f(n+m_{j}-m_{i})}. 
Let  \mu_{f}  denote the spectral measure of  f .
If we now use (2.8), reorder, and collapse the resulting expression back into a square, we obtain
 \displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{\ell}c_{i}\overline{c_{j}}\,\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(n)\overline{f(n+m_{j}-m_{i})} 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{\ell}c_{i}\overline{c_{j}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}e((m_{j}-m_{i})x)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu_{f}(x) 
 \displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}c_{i}e(m_{i}x)\bigg|^{2}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu_{f}(x). 
This completes the proof.
∎
3.  The structure theorem
Many proofs in combinatorics depend on some type of structure theorem that allows one to decompose a given objet (such as a set, function, or graph) into two components, a “structured” component that is responsible for the main behavior and can be analyzed using combinatorial methods, and a “random” component that represents noise and can be dealt with using probabilistic techniques; see [Tao07] for a survey.
The proofs of our main theorems also follow this strategy, and the purpose of this section is to introduce the structure theorem on which our arguments rely.
3.1.  Statement of the structure theorem
Definition 3.1.
Let  \mathbf{N}=(N_{s})_{s\in\mathbb{N}}  with  N_{1}<N_{2}<\ldots\in\mathbb{N} , and let  \mathcal{A}\subseteq\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})  be a translation invariant  C^{*} -subalgebra that admits logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N} .
• 
We say  f\in\mathcal{A}  is locally totally ergodic (with respect to logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N} ) if for all  q\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}  we have
 \lim_{H\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}^{\phantom{\log{}}}_{h\in[H]}\,f(n+qh)\Big|^{2}=0. 
• 
We say  f\in\mathcal{A}  is locally rationally almost periodic (with respect to logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N} ) if for all  \varepsilon>0  there exists some  q\in\mathbb{N}  such that
 \sup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}\big|f(n+qm)-f(n)\big|^{2}\Big)\leqslant\varepsilon. 
Note that the collection of all locally totally ergodic elements of  \mathcal{A}  forms a closed subspace of  \mathcal{A}  that is invariant under complex conjugation, whereas the collection of all locally rationally almost periodic elements of  \mathcal{A}  forms a  C^{*} -subalgebra of  \mathcal{A} . Additional convenient properties of locally rationally almost periodic and locally totally ergodic functions are collected in Lemma 3.4 at the end of this subsection, and in Section 5.1.
Theorem 3.2 (Structure Theorem).
Let  \mathbf{N}=(N_{s})_{s\in\mathbb{N}}  with  N_{1}<N_{2}<\ldots\in\mathbb{N} , and let  \mathcal{A}\subseteq\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})  be a separable and translation invariant  C^{*} -subalgebra that admits logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N} .
Then there exists a separable and translation invariant  C^{*} -subalgebra  \mathcal{A}^{\prime}\subseteq\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})  that admits logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N} , contains  \mathcal{A}  as a subset, and such that the following holds:
For all  f\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}  there exist  f_{\mathrm{str}},f_{\mathrm{rnd}}\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}  with
 f=f_{\mathrm{str}}+f_{\mathrm{rnd}}, 
where  f_{\mathrm{str}}  is locally rationally almost periodic with respect to logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N}  and  f_{\mathrm{rnd}}  is locally totally ergodic with respect to logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N} . Moreover, if  f  takes values in a closed interval  [a,b]\subseteq\mathbb{R}  then the same holds for  f_{\mathrm{str}} .
Theorem 3.2 can be viewed as an integer analogue of Corollary 2.8. Similar structure theorems in the integers were obtained in [MRR19].
The proof of Theorem 3.2 is given in Section 4.
Remark 3.3.
It will be clear from the construction used in the proof of Theorem 3.2 that
if  \mathcal{A}  is affinely invariant then the  C^{*} -supalgebra  \mathcal{A}^{\prime}  guaranteed by Theorem 3.2 is also affinely invariant.
Similarly, if  \mathbf{P}_{W}=(\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{t_{k}}])_{t\in\mathbb{N}} , where  M_{1}<M_{2}<\ldots\in\mathbb{N}  and  W\in\mathbb{N} , and  \mathcal{A}  admits iterated affine correlations along  \mathbf{P}_{W}  (see Definition 1.9), then so does  \mathcal{A}^{\prime} .
Lemma 3.4 (Basic properties of locally rationally almost periodic and locally totally ergodic functions).
Let  \mathbf{N}=(N_{s})_{s\in\mathbb{N}}  with  N_{1}<N_{2}<\ldots\in\mathbb{N} , and let  \mathcal{A}\subseteq\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})  be a translation invariant  C^{*} -subalgebra that admits logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N} .
Suppose  f\in\mathcal{A}  is locally rationally almost periodic and  g\in\mathcal{A}  is locally totally ergodic. Then:
(i) 
 f  and  g  are orthogonal, i.e.,  \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}^{\log}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(n)\overline{g(n)}=0 ;
(ii) 
For all  b\in\mathbb{Z} ,  f\circ\tau^{b}  is locally rationally almost periodic and  g\circ\tau^{b}  is locally totally ergodic;
(iii) 
If  \mathcal{A}  is affinely invariant then for all  a\in\mathbb{N} ,  f\circ\sigma_{a}  is locally rationally almost periodic and  g\circ\sigma_{a}  is locally totally ergodic.
Each of the parts (i)–(iii) is straightforward to verify using the definition of locally rationally almost periodic and locally totally ergodic functions, and we therefore omit their proofs.
3.2.  Outlining the proofs of Theorems 1.10 and 1.11
We now explain how the structure theorem provides a useful framework for proving both Theorems 1.10 and 1.11.
To albeit with the hypothesis in Theorems 1.10 and 1.11, let us assume for the reminder of this section that  N_{1}<N_{2}<\ldots\in\mathbb{N}  and  M_{1}<M_{2}<\ldots\in\mathbb{N}  are two increasing integer sequences, and  \mathcal{A}\subseteq\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})  is a separable, affinely invariant  C^{*} -subalgebra that admits logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N}=([N_{s}])_{s\in\mathbb{N}}  and iterated affine correlations along  \mathbf{P}_{W}=(\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{t}])_{t\in\mathbb{N}}  for all  W\in\mathbb{N} .
By replacing  \mathcal{A}  with the  C^{*} -subalgebra  \mathcal{A}^{\prime}\subseteq\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})  guaranteed by Theorem 3.2, we can assume without loss of generality that  1_{A}\in\mathcal{A}  splits into two components,
 1_{A}=f_{\mathrm{str}}+f_{\mathrm{rnd}}, 
(3.1)
where  f_{\mathrm{str}}\in\mathcal{A}  is locally rationally almost periodic and  f_{\mathrm{rnd}}\in\mathcal{A}  is locally totally ergodic. Also, since  1_{A}  is non-negative, it follows that the function  f_{\mathrm{str}}  is non-negative too, which will be important for technical reasons later on.
The next theorem lets us conclude that the random component  f_{\mathrm{rnd}}  in the decomposition  1_{A}=f_{\mathrm{str}}+f_{\mathrm{rnd}}  does not contribute to the averages in (1.9) and (1.10). This will allow us to “ignore”  f_{\mathrm{rnd}}  and focus solely on  f_{\mathrm{str}}  in the proofs of Theorems 1.10 and 1.11.
Theorem 3.5 (Handling the “random” component).
If  f\in\mathcal{A}  is locally totally ergodic with respect to logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N} , then for all  k,W,a\in\mathbb{N}  with  k\geqslant 2 , all  g\in\mathcal{A} , and all non-constant polynomials  Q  with integer coefficients we have
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f\big(an+Q(p)\big)g(pn)=0. 
A proof of Theorem 3.5 is given in Section 5.3.
The next theorem can be viewed as a (somewhat technical) generalization of a
theorem of Ahlswede, Khachatrian, and Sárközy [AKS99], which in turn was a generalization of the aforementioned result of Davenport and Erdős [DE36, DE51].
Theorem 3.6 (Handling the “structured” component).
Suppose  a_{1},a_{2},\ldots\in\mathbb{N}  and  f\in\mathcal{A}  with  f(n)\geqslant 0  for all  n\in\mathbb{N} .
Then for any  \varepsilon>0  and any  W\in\mathbb{N}  there are  k\geqslant 2  and an infinite set  I\subseteq\mathbb{N} , such that for all  i,j\in I  with  i<j  we have
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(a_{i}n)f(a_{j}pn)\geqslant\Big(\limsup_{j\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(a_{j}n)\Big)^{2}-\varepsilon. 
A proof of Theorem 3.6 can be found in Section 6.
We now explain why Theorems 3.5 and 3.6 are enough to derive Theorems 1.10 and 1.11.
Proof of Theorem 1.10 assuming Theorems 3.5 and 3.6.
Fix  \varepsilon>0 . Or goal is to find  W\geqslant 1  and  k\geqslant 2  such that
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{A}(n+Q(p))1_{A}(pn)\geqslant\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{A}(n)\Big)^{2}-\varepsilon. 
(3.2)
If  Q  is a constant polynomial then the condition  Q(1)=0  implies that  Q=0 . In this case,
(3.2) follows from Theorem 3.6 applied with  f(n)=1_{A}(n)  and  a_{1}=a_{2}=\ldots=1 .
If  Q  is non-constant then we proceed as follows.
Using the decomposition (3.1), we can rewrite the left hand side as
 \displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}} 
 \displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{A}(n+Q(p))1_{A}(pn) 
 \displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f_{\mathrm{str}}(n+Q(p))f_{\mathrm{str}}(pn)+\overbrace{\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f_{\mathrm{str}}(n+Q(p))f_{\mathrm{rnd}}(pn)}^{[1]} 
 \displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\underbrace{\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f_{\mathrm{rnd}}(n+Q(p))f_{\mathrm{str}}(pn)}_{[2]}+\underbrace{\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f_{\mathrm{rnd}}(n+Q(p))f_{\mathrm{rnd}}(pn)}_{[3]}. 
By Lemma 3.4, we have for all  p ,
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f_{\mathrm{str}}(n+Q(p))f_{\mathrm{rnd}}(pn)=0\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f_{\mathrm{rnd}}(n+Q(p))f_{\mathrm{str}}(pn)=0. 
This shows that the cross terms [1] and [2] disappear, and only the diagonal terms remain. Moreover, in light of Theorem 3.5 (applied with  f(n)=f_{\mathrm{rnd}}(n) ,  g(n)=f_{\mathrm{rnd}}(n) , and  a=1 ), the final term [3] also does not contribute to the average for any  W,k\in\mathbb{N}  as long as  k\geqslant 2 . This proves that
 \displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{A}(n+Q(p))1_{A}(pn)=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f_{\mathrm{str}}(n+Q(p))f_{\mathrm{str}}(pn). 
Since  f_{\mathrm{str}}  is locally rationally almost periodic, there exists  W\in\mathbb{N}  such that
 \sup_{m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}}\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}\big|f_{\mathrm{str}}(n+Wm^{\prime})-f_{\mathrm{str}}(n)\big|^{2}\Big)\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}. 
Using  Q(1)=0 , we see that for every  m\in\mathbb{N}  there is  m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}  such that  Q(Wm+1)=m^{\prime}W .
Since any  p=p_{1}\cdots p_{k} , where  p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\in\mathbb{P}_{W} , can be written in the form  Wm+1  for some  m\in\mathbb{N} , it follows that
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}\big|f_{\mathrm{str}}(n+Q(p))-f_{\mathrm{str}}(n)\big|^{2}\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}. 
Therefore, using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we get
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{A}(n+Q(p))1_{A}(pn)\geqslant\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f_{\mathrm{str}}(n)f_{\mathrm{str}}(pn)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. 
(3.3)
Finally, note that  \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}^{\log}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f_{\mathrm{rnd}}(n)=0  and hence  \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}^{\log}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f_{\mathrm{str}}(n)=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}^{\log}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{A}(n) .
So it follows from Theorem 3.6, applied with  f(n)=f_{\mathrm{str}}(n)  and  a_{1}=a_{2}=\ldots=1 , that there is some  k\geqslant 2  for which we have
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f_{\mathrm{str}}(n)f_{\mathrm{str}}(pn)\geqslant\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f_{\mathrm{str}}(n)\Big)^{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}=\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{A}(n)\Big)^{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. 
(3.4)
Since (3.3) and (3.4) combined imply (3.2), the proof is finished.

Proof of Theorem 1.11 assuming Theorems 3.5 and 3.6.
We use a similar argument the one used above to show that Theorems 3.5 and 3.6 imply Theorem 1.10.
Fix  \varepsilon>0  and let  a_{1},a_{2},\ldots\in\mathbb{N}  be a divisible sequence. Our goal is to find  k\geqslant 2  and  \{u,vu\}\subseteq\{a_{j}:j\in\mathbb{N}\}  such that
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{A}(un+Q(vp))1_{A}(uvpn)\geqslant\Big(\limsup_{j\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{A}(a_{j}n)\Big)^{2}-\varepsilon. 
(3.5)
By replacing  (a_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}  with a subsequence of itself if necessary, we can assume without loss of generality that  a_{j+1}  is a multiple of  a_{j}  for all  j\in\mathbb{N} . This can be done in a way that leaves  \limsup_{j\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}^{\log}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{A}(a_{j}n)  unchanged.
If  Q  is constant then  Q(0)=0  implies  Q=0 . If this is the case then (3.5) is a consequence of Theorem 3.6, taking  u=a_{i}  and  v=\frac{a_{j}}{a_{i}} .
Therefore, we can assume for the remainder of this proof that  Q  is non-constant.
Arguing as above, we can use (3.1) to split  1_{A}  into a “structured” component  f_{\mathrm{str}}  and a “random” component  f_{\mathrm{rnd}} . Consequently, the left hand side of (3.5) splits into four terms. The cross terms involving one “structured” component  f_{\mathrm{str}}  and one “random” component  f_{\mathrm{rnd}}  are  0  due to orthogonality, see Lemma 3.4. Moreover, the term involving twice the “random” component  f_{\mathrm{rnd}}  is  0  because of Theorem 3.5 (applied with  f(n)=f_{\mathrm{rnd}}(n) ,  g(n)=f_{\mathrm{rnd}}(a_{j}n) ,  W=1 ,  a=a_{i} , and  b=\frac{a_{j}}{a_{i}} ). This gives that
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{A}\big(a_{i}n+Q\big(\tfrac{a_{j}p}{a_{i}}\big)\big)1_{A}(a_{j}pn)=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f_{\mathrm{str}}\big(a_{i}n+Q\big(\tfrac{a_{j}p}{a_{i}}\big)\big)f_{\mathrm{str}}(a_{j}pn). 
(3.6)
Invoking Theorem 3.6 (with  f(n)=f_{\mathrm{str}}(n)  and  W=1 ), we can find  k\geqslant 2  and an infinite set  I\subseteq\mathbb{N} , such that for all  i,j\in I  with  i<j  we have
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f_{\mathrm{str}}\big(a_{i}n\big)f_{\mathrm{str}}(a_{j}pn)\geqslant\Big(\limsup_{j\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f_{\mathrm{str}}(a_{j}n)\Big)^{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. 
Since  \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}^{\log}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f_{\mathrm{str}}(bn)=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}^{\log}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{A}(bn)  for all  b\in\mathbb{N} , this implies that
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f_{\mathrm{str}}\big(a_{i}n\big)f_{\mathrm{str}}(a_{j}pn)\geqslant\Big(\limsup_{j\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{A}(a_{j}n)\Big)^{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. 
(3.7)
Let  i\in I  be fixed for the remainder of this proof.
Using that  f_{\mathrm{str}}  is locally rationally almost periodic, there exists  q\in\mathbb{N}  such that
 \sup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}\big|f_{\mathrm{str}}(a_{i}n+qm)-f_{\mathrm{str}}(a_{i}n)\big|^{2}\Big)\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}. 
Since  (a_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}  is divisible, there exist  j\in I  such that  a_{j}  is a multiple of  qa_{i} .
Since  Q(0)=0 , for any  p  the number  Q(\frac{a_{j}p}{a_{i}})  is a multiple of  q .
It follows that for all  p  we have
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}\big|f_{\mathrm{str}}\big(a_{i}n+Q\big(\tfrac{a_{j}p}{a_{i}}\big)\big)-f_{\mathrm{str}}(a_{i}n)\big|^{2}\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}. 
(3.8)
Applying (3.8) and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to (3.6), we can conclude that
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{A}\big(a_{i}n+Q\big(\tfrac{a_{j}p}{a_{i}}\big)\big)1_{A}(a_{j}pn)\geqslant\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f_{\mathrm{str}}(a_{i}n)f_{\mathrm{str}}(a_{j}pn)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. 
(3.9)
Since (3.7) and (3.9) give (3.5), we can take  u=a_{i}  and  v=\frac{a_{j}}{a_{i}}  and the proof is complete.

4.  Proof of the structure theorem
The purpose of this section is to prove Theorem 3.2.
As noted previously, it is natural to interpret Theorem 3.2 as a discrete analogue of Corollary 2.8, and the main idea behind its proof is to transfer Corollary 2.8 from the setting of unitary operators on Hilbert spaces to the setting of the translation operator acting on bounded functions over the integers.
4.1.  The Hilbert space  \mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N}) 
Let  \mathbf{N}=(N_{s})_{s\in\mathbb{N}}  with  N_{1}<N_{2}<\ldots\in\mathbb{N} , and let  \mathcal{A}\subseteq\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})  be a  C^{*} -subalgebra that admits logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N} .
Consider the space
 \mathscr{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})=\left\{f:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}:\forall\varepsilon>0,\penalty\ \exists g\in\mathcal{A},\limsup_{s\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N_{s}]}|f(n)-g(n)|^{2}<\varepsilon\right\}. 
We can define an equivalence relation on  \mathscr{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})  via
 f\sim g\hskip 10.00002pt\iff\hskip 10.00002pt\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}|f(n)-g(n)|^{2}=0. 
For any function  f\in\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N}) , let  [f]_{\sim}  denote the equivalence class of  f  with respect to  \sim , and let
 \mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})=\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})/\sim 
denote the quotient space of  \mathscr{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})  by  \sim .
Note that pointwise addition, pointwise multiplication, scalar multiplication, and complex conjugation on  \mathscr{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})  factor through to  \mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})  via
 \displaystyle[f]_{\sim}+[g]_{\sim} 
 \displaystyle=[f+g]_{\sim}, 
 \displaystyle[f]_{\sim}\cdot[g]_{\sim} 
 \displaystyle=[f\cdot g]_{\sim}, 
 \displaystyle c[f]_{\sim} 
 \displaystyle=[cf]_{\sim}, 
 \displaystyle\overline{[f]_{\sim}} 
 \displaystyle=\big[\overline{f}\big]_{\sim}, 
for all  f,g\in\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})  and  c\in\mathbb{C} .
This shows that  \mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})  is a vector space over  \mathbb{C} .
If one considers Cesàro averages instead of logarithmic averages, it was shown by Farhangi in [Far24, Theorem 2.1] that  \mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})  is actually a Hilbert space.
It is straightforward to adapt Farhangi’s argument to our situation; for completeness we include a proof below.
Theorem 4.1.
The space  \mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})  is a Hilbert space with inner product
 \langle[f]_{\sim},[g]_{\sim}\rangle=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(n)\overline{g(n)}. 
Proof.
It is clear that  \mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})  is an inner product space; to prove that it is a Hilbert space it therefore remains to verify that it is complete.
For the remainder of this proof we abuse notation and identify each function in  \mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})  with an arbitrarily chosen representative in its equivalency class in  \mathscr{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N}) .
Let  (f_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}  be a Cauchy sequence in  \mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N}) . Choose  \varepsilon_{i}\to 0  such that for every  i,j\in\mathbb{N} ,
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}|f_{j}(n)-f_{i}(n)|^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon_{\min\{i,j\}}. 
This means for any  i,j\in\mathbb{N}  there exists some  s_{i,j}\in\mathbb{N}  such that for every  s\geqslant s_{i,j}  we have
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N_{s}]}|f_{j}(n)-f_{i}(n)|^{2}\leqslant 2\varepsilon_{\min\{i,j\}}. 
Now we define  s_{0}=0  and  s_{j}=\max\{s_{1,j},s_{2,j},\ldots,s_{j,j}\}  for  j\geqslant 1 .
Then for all  i,j\in\mathbb{N}  with  i\leqslant j  and all  s\geqslant s_{j}  we have
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N_{s}]}|f_{j}(n)-f_{i}(n)|^{2}\leqslant 2\varepsilon_{i}. 
(4.1)
By replacing  (s_{j})  with a subsequence of itself if necessary, we can assume that  s_{j}  is sufficiently larger relative to  s_{j-1}  so that
 \Big(\max_{n\in[N_{s_{j-1}}]}\max_{\ell\leqslant j-1}|f_{\ell}(n)|^{2}\Big)\frac{\sum_{n\in[N_{s_{j-1}}]}\frac{1}{n}}{\sum_{n\in[N_{s_{j}}]}\frac{1}{n}}\to 0\penalty\ \text{as}\penalty\ j\to\infty. 
(4.2)
Define
 f(n)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}1_{(N_{s_{i-1}},N_{s_{i}}]}(n)f_{i}(n). 
Then for any  i\in\mathbb{N}  and  s_{j-1}<s\leqslant s_{j}  we have
 \displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N_{s}]}|f(n)-f_{i}(n)|^{2} 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N_{s}]}1_{(N_{s_{\ell-1}},N_{s_{\ell}}]}(n)|f(n)-f_{i}(n)|^{2} 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N_{s}]}1_{(N_{s_{\ell-1}},N_{s_{\ell}}]}(n)|f_{\ell}(n)-f_{i}(n)|^{2} 
 \displaystyle=[1]+[2]+[3], 
where
 \displaystyle[1]=\sum_{\ell=1}^{j-2}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N_{s}]}1_{(N_{s_{\ell-1}},N_{s_{\ell}}]}(n)|f_{\ell}(n)-f_{i}(n)|^{2}, 
 \displaystyle[2]=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N_{s}]}1_{(N_{s_{j-2}},N_{s_{j-1}}]}(n)|f_{j-1}(n)-f_{i}(n)|^{2}, 
 \displaystyle[3]=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N_{s}]}1_{(N_{s_{j-1}},N_{s_{j}}]}(n)|f_{j}(n)-f_{i}(n)|^{2}. 
It follows from (4.1) that  [2]\leqslant 2\varepsilon_{i}  as well as  [3]\leqslant 2\varepsilon_{i} . For  [1] , we have the estimate
 \displaystyle[1] 
 \displaystyle\leqslant 2\Big(\max_{n\in[N_{s_{j-2}}]}\max_{\ell\leqslant j-2}|f_{\ell}(n)|^{2}\Big)\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N_{s}]}1_{[1,N_{s_{j-2}}]}(n), 
 \displaystyle\leqslant 2\Big(\max_{n\in[N_{s_{j-2}}]}\max_{\ell\leqslant j-2}|f_{\ell}(n)|^{2}\Big)\frac{\sum_{n\in[N_{s_{j-2}}]}\frac{1}{n}}{\sum_{n\in[N_{s_{j-1}}]}\frac{1}{n}}. 
Therefore, (4.2) implies that  [1]={\mathrm{o}}_{j\to\infty}(1) . Combining all of the above shows
 \limsup_{s\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N_{s}]}|f(n)-f_{i}(n)|^{2}\leqslant 4\varepsilon_{i}, 
which implies that the Cauchy sequence  (f_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}  converges to  f . This proves that  \mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})  is complete.

4.2.  Proof of Theorem 3.2
Proof of Theorem 3.2.
Let  \mathscr{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})  and  \mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})  be as defined above, and define  \mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})\cap\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}) . Note that if  \mathcal{A}  is separable (resp. translation invariant/dilation invariant/admits iterated affine correlations along  \mathbf{P}_{W} ) then  \mathcal{A}^{\prime}  has the same property. Moreover, due to Theorem 4.1,  \mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})  is a Hilbert space with inner product  \langle[f]_{\sim},[g]_{\sim}\rangle=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}^{\log}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(n)\overline{g(n)}  and norm  \|[f]_{\sim}\|=(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}^{\log}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}|f(n)|^{2})^{1/2} .
Define an operator  U\colon\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})\to\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})  via
 U[f]_{\sim}=[f\circ\tau]_{\sim},\hskip 20.00003pt\forall[f]_{\sim}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N}), 
where  \tau(n)=n+1  is the shift map defined in (1.5).
Since logarithmic averages are shift invariant, we see that  \langle U[f]_{\sim},U[g]_{\sim}\rangle=\langle[f]_{\sim},[g]_{\sim}\rangle . In other words,  U  is a unitary operator on  \mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N}) .
It follows from Corollary 2.8 that
 \mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N})=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{rat}}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tot\,erg}} , where  \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{rat}}  and  \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tot\,erg}}  are as defined in the statement of Corollary 2.8.
It is now easy to check that  [f]_{\sim}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{rat}}  if and only if  f  is locally rationally almost periodic with respect to logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N} , and similarly  [f]_{\sim}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tot\,erg}}  if and only if  f  is locally totally ergodic with respect to logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N} . It follows that for any  f\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}  we can find
 f_{\mathrm{str}},f_{\mathrm{rnd}}\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}  such that
 [f_{\mathrm{str}}]_{\sim}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{rat}} ,  [f_{\mathrm{rnd}}]_{\sim}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tot\,erg}} , and
 f=f_{\mathrm{str}}+f_{\mathrm{rnd}}. 
It remains to show that we can take  f_{\mathrm{str}}  and  f_{\mathrm{rnd}}  to be bounded, or equivalently, that the equivalency classes of  f_{\mathrm{str}}  and  f_{\mathrm{rnd}}  contain representatives that belong to  \ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}) .
Suppose  f  takes values in the closed interval  [a,b]  for some  a\leqslant b\in\mathbb{R} . If we truncate  f_{\mathrm{str}}  above by  a  and below by  b , then the resulting function, let us call if  f_{\mathrm{str}}^{\prime} , also has the property that  [f_{\mathrm{str}}^{\prime}]_{\sim}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{rat}} . Since  [f_{\mathrm{str}}]_{\sim}  coincides with the orthogonal projection of  [f]_{\sim}  onto  \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{rat}} , we know that  [f_{\mathrm{str}}]_{\sim}  is the unique element in  \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{rat}}  that minimizes the distance between  [f]_{\sim}  and  \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{rat}}  within the Hilbert space  \mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathbf{N}) . But the distance between  f_{\mathrm{str}}^{\prime}  and  f  is no larger than the distance between  f_{\mathrm{str}}  and  f . By uniqueness, we conclude that  [f_{\mathrm{str}}^{\prime}]_{\sim}=[f_{\mathrm{str}}]_{\sim} .
This shows that with out loss of generality, we can assume that  f_{\mathrm{str}}  takes values in  [a,b] . In particular, both  f_{\mathrm{str}}  and  f_{\mathrm{rnd}}  are bounded and hence  f_{\mathrm{str}},f_{rnd}\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime} , completing the proof.
∎
5.  Controlling the “random” component
The goal of this section is to provide a proof of Theorem 3.5. This proof has two main ingredients, a spectral characterization of locally totally ergodic functions given in Section 5.1, and a new multiplicative analogue of van der Corput’s inequality proved in Section 5.2.
5.1.  Spectral characterizations of locally rationally almost periodic and locally totally ergodic functions
Recall that we identify the torus  \mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}  with the interval  [0,1)  in the natural way.
In particular, we call an element of  \mathbb{T}  rational if it corresponds to a rational number in  [0,1) , and irrational otherwise.
By abuse of language, we say that a finite Borel measure on  \mathbb{T}  is supported on rational (resp. irrational) numbers if the set of rational (resp. irrational) numbers in  \mathbb{T}  has full measure.
Theorem 5.1.
Let  \mathbf{N}=(N_{s})_{s\in\mathbb{N}}  with  N_{1}<N_{2}<\ldots\in\mathbb{N} , and let  \mathcal{A}\subseteq\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})  be a translation invariant  C^{*} -subalgebra that admits logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N} .
Let  f\in\mathcal{A} . The following hold:
(i) 
if  f  is locally rationally almost periodic then its spectral measure  \mu_{f}  is supported on rational numbers.
(ii) 
if  f  is locally totally ergodic then its spectral measure  \mu_{f}  is supported on irrational numbers.
Proof.
Suppose  f\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}) , and let  \mu_{f}  denote its spectral measure.
Using the dominated convergence theorem, we obtain for every  q\in\mathbb{N}  that
 \displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{m\in[M]}\hat{\mu}_{f}(qm) 
 \displaystyle=\lim_{M\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{m\in[M]}\int_{\mathbb{T}}e(qmx)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu_{f}(x) 
 \displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\bigg(\lim_{M\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{m\in[M]}(e(qmx)\bigg)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu_{f}(x) 
 \displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}1_{\big\{0,\frac{1}{q},\ldots,\frac{q-1}{q}\big\}}(x)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu_{f}(x). 
Therefore, we have for all  q\in\mathbb{N} ,
 \mu_{f}\big(\big\{0,\tfrac{1}{q},\ldots,\tfrac{q-1}{q}\big\}\big)=\lim_{M\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{m\in[M]}\hat{\mu}_{f}(qm). 
(5.1)
We will use (5.1) to prove both (i) and (ii).
Let us first show (i). If  f  is locally rationally almost periodic then, by definition, for all  \varepsilon>0  there exists some  q\in\mathbb{N}  such that
 \sup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}|\hat{\mu}_{f}(qm)-\hat{\mu}_{f}(0)|<\varepsilon. 
This implies that
 \mu_{f}(\mathbb{T})=\hat{\mu}_{F}(0)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}e(qmx)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu_{f}(x)+{\mathrm{O}}(\varepsilon), 
where the error term  {\mathrm{O}}(\varepsilon)  is independent of  m . Averaging over  m  and using (5.1), we obtain
 \displaystyle\mu_{f}(\mathbb{T})=\mu_{f}\big(\big\{0,\tfrac{1}{q},\ldots,\tfrac{q-1}{q}\big\}\big)+{\mathrm{O}}(\varepsilon). 
In other words, up to an  \varepsilon -error, the measure  \mu_{f}  is supported on the set  \big\{0,\frac{1}{q},\ldots,\frac{q-1}{q}\big\} . Letting  \varepsilon  go to  0  proves that  \mu_{f}  is supported on the rationals.
To prove (ii), assume  f  is locally totally ergodic. From the definition, it follows that for all  q\in\mathbb{N}  we have
 \lim_{M\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{m\in[M]}\hat{\mu}_{f}(qm)=0. 
In light of (5.1), this means that  \mu_{f}\big(\big\{0,\frac{1}{q},\ldots,\frac{q-1}{q}\big\}\big)=0  for all  q\in\mathbb{N} . But if this holds for all  q\in\mathbb{N}  then the measure  \mu_{f}  must be supported on the irrationals.
∎
5.2.  A multiplicative analogue of van der Corput’s inequality
Van der Corput’s fundamental inequality (see [KN74, Lemma 3.1]) states that for any  1 -bounded function  f\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}  and any  N,H\in\mathbb{N}  with  1\leqslant H\leqslant N  one has
 \Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{h\in[H]}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}f(n)\Big|^{2}\leqslant{\rm Re}\bigg(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{h\in[H]}\Big(\tfrac{H-h}{H}\Big)\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}f(n)\overline{f(n+h)}\bigg)+{\mathrm{O}}\bigg(\frac{1}{H}+\frac{H}{N}\bigg). 
(5.2)
It is an instrumental inequality used for proving correlation estimates, convergence theorems, and uniform distribution results in number theory, harmonic analysis, and ergodic theory.
A small refinement of the classical formulation of van der Corput’s inequality, and one that is often overlooked, asserts that for any  1 -bounded functions  f,g\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}  and any  N,H\in\mathbb{N}  with  1\leqslant H\leqslant N ,
 \Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{h\in[H]}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}f(n)g(n+h)\Big|^{2}\leqslant{\rm Re}\bigg(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{h\in[H]}\Big(\tfrac{H-h}{H}\Big)\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}f(n)\overline{f(n+h)}\bigg)+{\mathrm{O}}\bigg(\frac{1}{H}+\frac{H}{N}\bigg). 
(5.3)
We remark that the standard proof of (5.2) (such as the one given in [KN74, Lemma 3.1]) with minimal adjustments proves (5.3) as well.
One can also derive a logarithmically averaged version of van der Corput’s inequality: For any
 1 -bounded functions  f,g\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}  and any  N,H\in\mathbb{N}  with  1\leqslant H\leqslant N  we have
 \Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{h\in[H]}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}f(n)g(n+h)\Big|^{2}\leqslant{\rm Re}\bigg(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{h\in[H]}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}f(n)\overline{f(n+h)}\bigg)+{\mathrm{O}}\bigg(\frac{1}{\log(H)}+\frac{\log(H)}{\log(N)}\bigg). 
(5.4)
Since we do not make use of (5.4) in this paper, we omit its proof. It merely serves as motivation for the following novel analogue of (5.4) that uses multiplicative instead of additive differences and plays a crucial role in our argument.
Theorem 5.2.
Let  y\geqslant 1 ,  P\subseteq\mathbb{P}\cap[y] , and suppose  f_{p},g\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}  are  1 -bounded functions for all  p\in P . Then
 \Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in P}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}f_{p}(n)g(pn)\Big|^{2}\,\leqslant\,{\rm Re}\bigg(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p,q\in P}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}f_{p}(qn)\overline{f_{q}(pn)}\bigg)+{\mathrm{O}}\Bigg(\bigg(\sum_{p\in P}\frac{1}{p}\bigg)^{-1}+\frac{\log y}{\log N}\Bigg). 
Remark 5.3.
If  g(n)  is a multiplicative function (i.e.  g(nm)=g(n)g(m)  whenever  \gcd(n,m)=1 ) and  f_{p}(n)=f(n)  for some  f\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}  and all  p\in P , then Theorem 5.2 is closely related to the Daboussi-Delange-Kátai-Bourgain-Sarnak-Ziegler orthogonality criterion [DD82, Kát86, BSZ13].
Proof of Theorem 5.2.
Using Lemma 2.1, we can write
 \displaystyle\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in P}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}f_{p}(n)g(pn)\Big|^{2} 
 \displaystyle=\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in P}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}p1_{p\mid n}f_{p}\big(\tfrac{n}{p}\big)g(n)\Big|^{2}+{\mathrm{O}}\bigg(\frac{\log y}{\log N}\bigg) 
 \displaystyle\leqslant\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in P}p1_{p\mid n}f_{p}\big(\tfrac{n}{p}\big)\Big|^{2}+{\mathrm{O}}\bigg(\frac{\log y}{\log N}\bigg) 
 \displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p,q\in P}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}pq1_{\operatorname{lcm}(p,q)\mid n}f_{p}\big(\tfrac{n}{p}\big)\overline{f_{q}\big(\tfrac{n}{q}\big)}+{\mathrm{O}}\bigg(\frac{\log y}{\log N}\bigg) 
 \displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p,q\in P}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}pq1_{pq\mid n}f_{p}\big(\tfrac{n}{p}\big)\overline{f_{q}\big(\tfrac{n}{q}\big)}+[1]+{\mathrm{O}}\bigg(\frac{\log y}{\log N}\bigg), 
where
 \displaystyle[1] 
 \displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p,q\in P}1_{p=q}\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}p^{2}1_{p\mid n}\big|f_{p}\big(\tfrac{n}{p}\big)\big|^{2}-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}p^{2}1_{p^{2}\mid n}\big|f_{p}\big(\tfrac{n}{p}\big)\big|^{2}\Big) 
 \displaystyle\leqslant\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p,q\in P}1_{p=q}\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}p^{2}1_{p\mid n}\Big) 
 \displaystyle\leqslant\bigg(\sum_{p\in P}\frac{1}{p}\bigg)^{-2}\sum_{p\in P}\frac{1}{p^{2}}\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}\penalty\ p^{2}1_{p\mid n}\Big) 
 \displaystyle\leqslant\bigg(\sum_{p\in P}\frac{1}{p}\bigg)^{-1}. 
Using Lemma 2.1 once more, we see that
 \displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p,q\in P}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}pq1_{pq\mid n}f_{p}\big(\tfrac{n}{p}\big)\overline{f_{q}\big(\tfrac{n}{q}\big)}={\rm Re}\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p,q\in P}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in[N]}f_{p}(qn)\overline{f_{q}(pn)}\Big)+{\mathrm{O}}\bigg(\frac{\log y}{\log N}\bigg). 
Combining all the estimates above completes the proof.

5.3.  Proof of Theorem 3.5
We now have all the necessary ingredients to provide a proof of Theorem 3.5. For the convenience of the reader, let us restate the theorem here.
Theorem 3.5.
Let  N_{1}<N_{2}<\ldots\in\mathbb{N}  and  M_{1}<M_{2}<\ldots\in\mathbb{N} . Suppose  \mathcal{A}\subseteq\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})  is a separable, affinely invariant  C^{*} -subalgebra that admits logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N}=([N_{s}])_{s\in\mathbb{N}}  and iterated affine correlations along  \mathbf{P}_{W}=(\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{t}])_{t\in\mathbb{N}}  for all  W\in\mathbb{N} .
If  f\in\mathcal{A}  is locally totally ergodic with respect to logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N} , then for all  k,W,a\in\mathbb{N}  with  k\geqslant 2 , all  g\in\mathcal{A} , and all non-constant polynomials  Q  with integer coefficients we have
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f\big(an+Q(p)\big)g(pn)=0. 
Proof.
It suffices to prove the case  k=2 .
Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Theorem 5.2, we obtain
 \displaystyle\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{2}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}} 
 \displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(an+Q(p_{2}p_{1}))g(p_{2}p_{1}n)\Big|^{2} 
 \displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{2}\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{t}]}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(an+Q(p_{2}p_{1}))g(p_{2}p_{1}n)\Big|^{2} 
 \displaystyle\leqslant\lim_{t\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{2}\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{t}]}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(an+Q(p_{2}p_{1}))g(p_{2}p_{1}n)\Big|^{2} 
 \displaystyle\leqslant\lim_{t\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\Big({\rm Re}\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{2},p_{3}\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{t}]}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(ap_{3}n+Q(p_{2}p_{1}))\overline{f(ap_{2}n+Q(p_{3}p_{1}))}\Big)\Big) 
 \displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{2},p_{3}\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{t}]}{\rm Re}\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(ap_{3}n+Q(p_{2}p_{1}))\overline{f(ap_{2}n+Q(p_{3}p_{1}))}\Big). 
We claim that whenever  p_{2}\neq p_{3}  then
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(ap_{3}n+Q(p_{2}p_{1}))\overline{f(ap_{2}n+Q(p_{3}p_{1}))}=0. 
(5.5)
Once this claim has been verified, it follows that only the diagonal terms  p_{2}=p_{3}  contribute, which implies that
 \displaystyle\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{2}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}} 
 \displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(an+Q(p_{2}p_{1}))g(p_{2}p_{1}n)\Big|^{2}\leqslant\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}\Big(\lim_{t\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{2},p_{3}\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{t}]}1_{p_{2}=p_{3}}\Big)=0. 
It remains to verify (5.5) under the assumption  p_{2}\neq p_{3} . By symmetry, we can assume without loss of generality that  p_{3}>p_{2} .
Let  q=ap_{2}p_{3} .
First, we split the right hand side of (5.5) into residue classes mod  q .
Observe that for all  m\in\mathbb{Z} ,
 Q(p_{2}(qm+r))\equiv Q(p_{2}r)\bmod q\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptQ(p_{3}(qm+r))\equiv Q(p_{3}r)\bmod q. 
Define
 Q_{2,r}(n)=\frac{Q(p_{2}n)-Q(p_{2}r)}{ap_{3}}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptQ_{3,r}(n)=\frac{Q(p_{3}n)-Q(p_{3}r)}{ap_{2}}. 
Therefore
 \displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(ap_{3}n+Q(p_{2}p_{1}))\overline{f(ap_{2}n+Q(p_{3}p_{1}))} 
 \displaystyle\penalty\ =\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{q\mathbb{Z}+r}(p_{1})\,f(ap_{3}n+Q(p_{2}p_{1}))\overline{f(ap_{2}n+Q(p_{3}p_{1}))} 
 \displaystyle\penalty\ =\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{q\mathbb{Z}+r}(p_{1})\,f\big(ap_{3}\big(n+Q_{2,r}(p_{1})\big)+Q(p_{2}r)\big) 
 \displaystyle\hskip 190.00029pt\overline{f\big(ap_{2}\big(n+Q_{3,r}(p_{1})\big)+Q(p_{3}r)\big)}. 
To simplify notation, let us define, for  r\in\{0,\ldots,q-1\} , the functions
 f_{r,1}(n)=f(ap_{3}n+Q(p_{2}r))\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptf_{r,2}(n)=f(ap_{2}n+Q(p_{3}r)). 
Then we can rewrite the above as
 \displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}} 
 \displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(ap_{3}n+Q(p_{2}p_{1}))\overline{f(ap_{2}n+Q(p_{3}p_{1}))} 
 \displaystyle\penalty\ =\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{q\mathbb{Z}+r}(p_{1})\,f_{r,1}(n+Q_{2,r}(p_{1}))\overline{f_{r,2}(n+Q_{3,r}(p_{1}))} 
 \displaystyle\penalty\ =\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{q\mathbb{Z}+r}(p_{1})\,f_{r,1}(n)\overline{f_{r,2}(n+Q_{3,r}(p_{1})-Q_{2,r}(p_{1}))}. 
After exchanging the order of summation in the variables  n  and  p_{1} , using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and finally expanding the square, we can eliminate the function  f_{r,1}  and obtain a single correlation expression in the function  f_{r,2} :
 \displaystyle\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}} 
 \displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(ap_{3}n+Q(p_{2}p_{1}))\overline{f(ap_{2}n+Q(p_{3}p_{1}))}\Big| 
 \displaystyle\penalty\ =\bigg|\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}\Big(\lim_{t\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{t}]}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}1_{q\mathbb{Z}+r}(p_{1})\,f_{r,1}(n)\overline{f_{r,2}(n+Q_{3,r}(p_{1})-Q_{2,r}(p_{1}))}\Big)\bigg| 
 \displaystyle\penalty\ \leqslant\|f\|_{\infty}\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}\lim_{t\to\infty}\bigg(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{t}]}1_{q\mathbb{Z}+r}(p_{1})\,f_{r,2}(n+Q_{3,r}(p_{1})-Q_{2,r}(p_{1}))\Big|^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}. 
Let  \mu_{f_{r,2}}  be the spectral measure associated to  f_{r,2} , as defined in (2.8).
In light of Lemma 2.10, it follows that
 \displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\bigg(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}} 
 \displaystyle\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{t}]}1_{q\mathbb{Z}+r}(p_{1})\,f_{r,2}(n+Q_{3,r}(p_{1})-Q_{2,r}(p_{1}))\Big|^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}} 
 \displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{t}]}1_{q\mathbb{Z}+r}(p_{1})\,e\big(\big(Q_{3,r}(p_{1})-Q_{2,r}(p_{1})\big)x\big)\Big|^{2}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu_{f_{r,2}}(x)\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}. 
Since  Q  is non-constant and  p_{2}  and  p_{3}  are distinct primes, the polynomial  x\mapsto Q_{3,r}(x)-Q_{2,r}(x)  is non-constant with rational coefficients.
According to Corollary 2.6, if  x  is irrational then
 \lim_{t\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{t}]}1_{q\mathbb{Z}+r}(p_{1})\,e\big(\big(Q_{3,r}(p_{1})-Q_{2,r}(p_{1})\big)x\big)=0. 
By assumption,  f  is locally totally ergodic. Due to Lemma 3.4, this means that  f_{r,2}  is also locally totally ergodic. In view of the spectral characterization given by Theorem 5.1, the spectral measure  \mu_{f_{r,2}}  of  f_{r,2}  is supported on irrational numbers. It thus follows by the Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem that
 \lim_{t\to\infty}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Big|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{t}]}1_{q\mathbb{Z}+r}(p_{1})\,e\big(\big(Q_{3,r}(p_{1})-Q_{2,r}(p_{1})\big)x\big)\Big|^{2}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu_{f_{r,2}}(x)\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}=0. 
This concludes the proof.

6.  Controlling the “structured” component
In this section, we give a proof of Theorem 3.6.
Let us restate the theorem:
Theorem 3.6.
Let  N_{1}<N_{2}<\ldots\in\mathbb{N}  and  M_{1}<M_{2}<\ldots\in\mathbb{N} . Suppose  \mathcal{A}\subseteq\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})  is a separable, affinely invariant  C^{*} -subalgebra that admits logarithmic averages along  \mathbf{N}=([N_{s}])_{s\in\mathbb{N}}  and iterated affine correlations along  \mathbf{P}_{W}=(\mathbb{P}_{W}\cap[M_{t}])_{t\in\mathbb{N}}  for all  W\in\mathbb{N} .
Suppose  a_{1},a_{2},\ldots\in\mathbb{N}  and  f\in\mathcal{A}  with  f(n)\geqslant 0  for all  n\in\mathbb{N} .
Then for any  \varepsilon>0  and any  W\in\mathbb{N}  there are  k\geqslant 2  and an infinite set  I\subseteq\mathbb{N} , such that for all  i,j\in I  with  i<j  we have
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(a_{i}n)f(a_{j}pn)\geqslant\Big(\limsup_{j\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(a_{j}n)\Big)^{2}-\varepsilon. 
(6.1)
Proof.
Let  \varepsilon>0  be given, and define  \delta=\limsup_{j\to\infty}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}^{\log}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(a_{j}n) .
By replacing  (a_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}  with a subsequence of itself, we can assume without loss of generality that
 \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(a_{j}n)\geqslant\delta-\frac{\varepsilon}{3},\hskip 20.00003pt\forall j\in\mathbb{N}. 
(6.2)
Let  K\in\mathbb{N}  be any natural number that satisfies  K>3\varepsilon^{-1} .
We now define a  K  coloring of the set  \{(i,j)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}:i<j\} , and
we represent this coloring as a map  \chi\colon\{(i,j)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}:i<j\}\to\{1,\ldots,K\} .
Given  i,j\in\mathbb{N}  with  i<j , define
 \chi(i,j)=\begin{cases}k,&\text{if $k$ is the smallest number in $\{2,\ldots,K\}$ for which \eqref{eqn_improved_DE} holds;}\\
1,&\text{if \eqref{eqn_improved_DE} doesn't hold for any $k\in\{2,\ldots,K\}$}.\end{cases} 
By Ramsey’s theorem [Ram30, Theorem A], there exists an infinite set  I\subseteq\mathbb{N}  such that  \chi(i,j)  has the same color for all  i,j\in I  with  i<j .
If this color belongs to the set  \{2,\ldots,K\}  then we are done.
So it remains to show that  1  is not an admissible color.
By way of contradiction, suppose  \chi(i,j)=1  for all  i,j\in I  with  i<j .
This implies that all  i,j\in I  with  i<j ,
 \max_{k=2,\ldots,K}\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(a_{i}n)f(a_{j}pn)\Big)<\delta^{2}-\varepsilon. 
Let  i_{2}<\ldots<i_{K}\in I  be arbitrary.
Using (6.2), Corollary 2.4, and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have
 \displaystyle\Big(\delta-\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\Big)^{2} 
 \displaystyle\leqslant\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{k\in\{2,\ldots,K\}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(a_{i_{k}}n)\Big)^{2} 
 \displaystyle=\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{k\in\{2,\ldots,K\}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*k}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(a_{i_{k}}pn)\Big)^{2} 
 \displaystyle=\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{K}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\cdots\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{k\in\{2,\ldots,K\}}f(a_{i_{k}}p_{k}\cdots p_{1}n)\Big)^{2} 
 \displaystyle\leqslant\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{K}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\cdots\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}\Big(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{k\in\{2,\ldots,K\}}f(a_{i_{k}}p_{k}\cdots p_{1}n)\Big)^{2} 
 \displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{k,\ell\in\{2,\ldots,K\}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{K}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\cdots\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(a_{i_{\ell}}p_{\ell}\cdots p_{1}n)f(a_{i_{k}}p_{k}\cdots p_{1}n) 
 \displaystyle\leqslant\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{k,\ell\in\{2,\ldots,K\}}21_{\ell<k}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{K}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\cdots\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(a_{i_{\ell}}p_{\ell}\cdots p_{1}n)f(a_{i_{k}}p_{k}\cdots p_{1}n)+\frac{1}{K} 
 \displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{k,\ell\in\{2,\ldots,K\}}21_{\ell<k}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{K}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\cdots\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p_{1}\in\mathbf{P}_{W}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(a_{i_{\ell}}n)f(a_{i_{k}}p_{k}\cdots p_{k-\ell+1}n)+\frac{1}{K-1} 
 \displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{k,\ell\in\{2,\ldots,K\}}21_{\ell<k}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{p\in\mathbf{P}_{W}^{*\ell}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}^{\log{}}}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}f(a_{i_{\ell}}n)f(a_{i_{k}}pn)+\frac{1}{K-1} 
 \displaystyle<\delta^{2}-\varepsilon+\frac{1}{K-1}. 
This contradicts the assumption that  K>3\varepsilon^{-1} , completing the proof.
∎
7.  Open questions
To conclude, we formulate some open questions and conjectures connected to our results and the broader topic.
We begin by asking for a natural extension of Theorem 1.7.
Question 7.1.
Is there a multiplicatively invariant density on  \mathbb{N}  (similar to  \mathrm{d}^{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\rotatebox[origin={c}]{-45.0}{$\rule{0.34422pt}{3.27773pt}\hskip 0.49164pt\rule{0.34422pt}{3.27773pt}$}\cr\rotatebox[origin={c}]{45.0}{$\rule{0.34422pt}{3.27773pt}\hskip 0.49164pt\rule{0.34422pt}{3.27773pt}$}\cr}}}\hskip-0.09995pt(.) 
) such that any set with positive density with respect to this density notion contains  \{x,x+y,xy\}  for some  x,y\in\mathbb{N} .
The next question seeks a quantitative version of (a special case of) Moreira’s theorem [Mor17].
Given  r\in\mathbb{N} , let  M(r)  denote the smallest positive integer such that for all  N\geqslant M(r)  and all  r -colorings of  [N]  one can find  x,y\in[N]  with  x>y>2  such that  \{x+y,xy\}\subseteq[N]  is monochromatic.
It follows from the compactness principle (see [GRS90, Section 1.5]) applied to Moreira’s theorem that  M(r)  is well defined for every  r\in\mathbb{N} .
Question 7.2.
What lower and upper bounds on  M(r)  can be provided?
Remark 7.3.
After a preprint of this paper appeared on arXiv, it was shown by Green and Sawhney [GS25] that
 r\ll\log(M(r))\ll e^{r^{50}} .
Finally, it is natural to inquire about sums and products in the set  \mathbb{P}-1 .
Conjecture 7.4.
For every  a\in\mathbb{N}  there exist  x,y\in\mathbb{N}  with  x>y>a  such that
 \{x+y,xy\}\subseteq\mathbb{P}-1 .
