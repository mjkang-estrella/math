Introduction
This paper proposes seven combinatorial problems around formulas for the characteristic polynomial and the spectral numbers of a quasihomogeneous singularity. One of them is a new conjecture on the characteristic polynomial. It is an amendment to an old conjecture of Orlik on the integral monodromy of an isolated quasihomogeneous singularity. The search for a combinatorial proof of the new conjecture led us to the seven purely combinatorial problems.
We start with a result on 
\({\mathbb {Z}}\)
-lattices with automorphisms. Then we describe Orlik’s conjecture and our new conjecture. Finally, we give a rough outline of the seven problems.

                
Definition 1.1

                
Let 
\(M\subseteq {\mathbb {N}}=\{1,2,3,\ldots \}\)
 be a finite nonempty subset. Its 
Orlik block
 is a pair 
\((H_M,h_M)\)
 with 
\(H_M\)
 a 
\({\mathbb {Z}}\)
-lattice of rank 
\(\sum _{m\in M}\varphi (m)\)
 and 
\(h_M:H_M\rightarrow H_M\)
 an automorphism with characteristic polynomial 
\(\prod _{m\in M}\Phi _m\)
 (
\(\Phi _m\)
 is the 
m
-th cyclotomic polynomial) and with a cyclic generator 
\(e_1\in H_M\)
, i.e.,
$$\begin{aligned} H_M =\bigoplus _{j=1}^{rk M}{\mathbb {Z}}\cdot h_M^{j-1}(e_1). \end{aligned}$$

                    (1.1)
                
\((H_M,h_M)\)
 is unique up to isomorphism. 
\(\mathrm{Aut}_{S^1}(H_M,h_M)\)
 denotes the group of all automorphisms of 
\(H_M\)
 which commute with 
\(h_M\)
 and which have all eigenvalues in 
\(S^1\)
.

              
Definition 
6.1
 enriches the set 
M
 to a directed graph 
\({\mathscr {G}}(M)\)
. An edge goes from 
\(m_1\in M\)
 to 
\(m_2\in M\)
 if 
\(\frac{m_1}{m_2}\)
 is a power of a prime number 
p
 and if no 
\(m_3\in M{\setminus } \{m_1,m_2\}\)
 with 
\(m_2|m_3|m_1\)
 exists. Then it is called a 
p
-edge. The main result in [
5
] is cited precisely in Theorem 
6.2
. Roughly it is as follows.

                
Theorem 1.2

                
[
5
, Theorem 1.2] Let 
\((H_M,h_M)\)
 be the Orlik block of a finite nonempty subset 
\(M\subseteq {\mathbb {N}}\)
. Then 
\(\mathrm{Aut}_{S^1}(H_M,h_M)=\{\pm h_M^k\, |\, k\in {\mathbb {Z}}\}\)
 if and only if condition (I) or condition (II) in Theorem 
6.2
 is satisfied. They are conditions on the graph 
\({\mathscr {G}}(M)\)
.

              
A weight system 
\(\mathbf{w}=(w_1,\ldots ,w_n)\)
 with 
\(w_i\in {\mathbb {Q}}_{>0}\)
 equips any monomial 
\(\mathbf{x}^\mathbf{j}=x_1^{j_1}...x_n^{j_n}\)
 with a weighted degree 
\(\deg _\mathbf{w}{} \mathbf{x}^\mathbf{j}:=\sum _{i=1}^n w_ij_i\)
. A polynomial 
\(f\in {\mathbb {C}}[x_1,\ldots ,x_n]\)
 is called an 
isolated quasihomogeneous singularity
 if for some weight system 
\(\mathbf{w}\)
 with 
\(w_i\in {\mathbb {Q}}\cap (0,1)\)
 each monomial in 
f
 has weighted degree 1 and if the functions 
\(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1},\ldots ,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\)
 vanish simultaneously only at 
\(0\in {\mathbb {C}}^n\)
. Then the 
Milnor lattice
\(H_\mathrm{Milnor}:=H_{n-1}(f^{-1}(1),{\mathbb {Z}})\)
 is a 
\({\mathbb {Z}}\)
-lattice of some rank 
\(\mu \in {\mathbb {N}}\)
 [
12
], which is called 
Milnor number
. It comes equipped with a natural automorphism 
\(h_\mathrm{mon}:H_\mathrm{Milnor}\rightarrow H_\mathrm{Milnor}\)
 of finite order, the 
monodromy
. Thus its characteristic polynomial has the form
$$\begin{aligned} p_{ch,h_\mathrm{mon}}&= \prod _{m\in M_1}\Phi _m^{\nu (m)} \end{aligned}$$
for a finite subset 
\(M_1\subseteq {\mathbb {N}}\)
 and a function 
\(\nu :M_1\rightarrow {\mathbb {N}}\)
. Denote 
\(\nu _\mathrm{max}:=\max (\nu (m)\, |\, m\in {\mathbb {N}})\)
 and for 
\(j=1,\ldots ,\nu _\mathrm{max}\)
$$\begin{aligned} M_j:=\{m\in M_1\, |\, \nu (m)\ge j\},\quad g_j:=\prod _{m\in M_j} \Phi _m. \end{aligned}$$
Then
$$\begin{aligned} M_1\supset M_2\supset ...\supset M_{\nu _\mathrm{max}}\ne \emptyset \\ \text {and}\qquad p_{ch,h_\mathrm{mon}} =\prod _{j=1}^{\nu _\mathrm{max}} g_j. \end{aligned}$$
The polynomials 
\(g_1,\ldots ,g_{\nu _\mathrm{max}}\)
 are called 
elementary divisors
 of 
\(p_{ch,h_\mathrm{mon}}\)
.

                
Conjecture 1.3

                
(Orlik’s conjecture, [
14
, Conjecture 3.1]) For any isolated quasihomogeneous singularity, there is an isomorphism
$$\begin{aligned} (H_\mathrm{Milnor},h_\mathrm{mon})\cong \bigoplus _{j=1}^{\nu _\mathrm{max}} (H_{M_j},h_{M_j}). \end{aligned}$$

              
The conjecture is known to be true for curve singularities [
11
] and a few other cases, but it is still (after 45 years) open in general. The following conjecture is independent of Orlik’s conjecture, but it is motivated by Orlik’s conjecture, as it concerns the sets 
\(M_1,\ldots ,M_{\nu _\mathrm{max}}\)
.

                
Conjecture 1.4

                
For any isolated quasihomogeneous singularity, each of the sets 
\(M_1\)
,..., 
\(M_{\nu _\mathrm{max}}\)
 satisfies condition (I) in Theorem 
6.2
.

              
If this is true for some singularity, then Theorem 
1.2
 gives for these sets 
\(\mathrm{Aut}_{S^1}(H_{M_j}, h_{M_j}) \cong \{\pm h_{M_j}^k\, |\, k\in {\mathbb {Z}}\}\)
. If also Orlik’s conjecture holds, then this is helpful in determining the automorphisms of the Milnor lattice which respect the monodromy (and intersection form or Seifert form).

                
Examples 1.5

                
(i) Fix 
\(\mu \in {\mathbb {Z}}\)
. The singularity 
\(A_\mu \)
, 
\(f(x_1)=x_1^{\mu +1}\)
, is quasihomogeneous with 
\(w_1=\frac{1}{\mu +1}\)
. Here
$$\begin{aligned} p_{ch,h_\mathrm{mon}}(t)= & {} \frac{t^{\mu +1}-1}{t-1}= \prod _{m\in M}\Phi _m(t),\\ \text {with }\quad M= & {} \{a\in {\mathbb {N}}\, |\, a\ne 1 \text { and } a\text { divides }\mu +1\}. \end{aligned}$$
Here 
\((H_\mathrm{Milnor},h_\mathrm{mon})\cong (H_M,h_M)\)
 is well known, so Orlik’s conjecture holds here. The set 
M
 satisfies condition (I) in Theorem 
6.2
 (because of Theorem 
6.9
), so Conjecture 
1.4
 holds here. Therefore 
\(\mathrm{Aut}_{S^1}(H_\mathrm{Milnor},h_\mathrm{mon})=\{\pm h_\mathrm{mon}^k\, |\, k\in {\mathbb {Z}}\}\)
.

                
(ii) The Thom–Sebastiani sum of two singularities 
f
(
x
) and 
g
(
y
) is 
\(f(x)+g(y)\)
. Then [
20
]
$$\begin{aligned} (H_\mathrm{Milnor},h_\mathrm{mon})(f+g)\cong (H_\mathrm{Milnor},h_\mathrm{mon})(f)\otimes (H_\mathrm{Milnor},h_\mathrm{mon})(g) \end{aligned}$$
But it is unknown whether Orlik’s conjecture holds for 
\(f+g\)
 if it holds for 
f
 and 
g
. Example 
6.5
 (ii) shows that condition (I) in Theorem 
6.2
 does not behave well under tensor product of products of cyclotomic polynomials. This motivates problem 7.

                
(iii) It is not even clear for all Brieskorn–Pham singularities 
\(\sum _{j=1}^n x_j^{a_j}\)
 whether Orlik’s conjecture or Conjecture 
1.4
 hold. Though both conjectures hold if 
\(\gcd (a_i,a_j)=1\)
 for all 
i
 and 
j
 with 
\(i\ne j\)
 [
3
, Proposition 6.3].

              
Orlik’s Conjecture 
1.3
 concerns the Milnor lattice. Any proof requires to go into geometry. But we hope that our Conjecture 
1.4
 is amenable to a combinatorial proof. It just concerns the characteristic polynomial. Milnor and Orlik [
13
] proved a formula which expresses this in terms of the weight system 
\(\mathbf{w}\)
 of the quasihomogeneous singularity. It says 
\(\mathrm{div\, }p_{ch,h_\mathrm{mon}}=D_\mathbf{w}\)
, where 
\(D_\mathbf{w}\)
 is defined in (
3.9
). See Theorem 
3.9
. Therefore we hope that there will be a purely combinatorial Proof of Conjecture 
1.4
 dealing solely with properties of 
\(\mathbf{w}\)
. This is problem 6 below. For most of the other problems, we need two more data.
First, an isolated quasihomogeneous singularity comes also equipped with 
exponents
\(\alpha _1,\ldots , \alpha _\mu \in {\mathbb {Q}}\cap (0,n)\)
. They are slightly finer invariants than 
\(p_{ch,h_\mathrm{mon}}\)
. They satisfy
$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{div\, }p_{ch,h_\mathrm{mon}} =\sum _{j=1}^\mu [ e^{2\pi i\alpha _j}] \end{aligned}$$
and, for any 
\(d\in {\mathbb {N}}\)
 with 
\(v_i:=d\cdot w_i\in {\mathbb {N}}\)
 for all 
\(i\in \{1,\ldots ,n\}\)
,
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{j=1}^\mu t^{d\cdot \alpha _j} = \rho _{(\mathbf{v},d)} \end{aligned}$$
where 
\(\rho _{(\mathbf{v},d)}\)
 is defined in (
3.8
). See Theorem 
3.9
.
Second, the weight systems 
\(\mathbf{w}\)
 for which isolated quasihomogeneous singularities exist, can be characterized by a combinatorial condition (
C
1) (and equivalent combinatorial conditions 
\((C1)'\)
 and (
C
2), see Lemma 
3.3
). This is cited in Theorem 
3.5
. It was proved first by Kouchnirenko [
8
, Remarque 1.13 (i)]. The necessity of (
C
1) had already been seen by K. Saito [
17
], the sufficiency not. A weaker combinatorial property 
\(\overline{(C1)}\)
 is equivalent to 
\(\rho _{(\mathbf{v},d)}\in {\mathbb {Z}}[t]\)
 (
3.16
).
The seven problems are given in detail in the later chapters. Roughly, they are as follows. 
Problem 1:
:

                    
(Remark 
3.8
) Let 
\((\mathbf{v},d)=(v_1,\ldots ,v_n,d)\in {\mathbb {N}}^{n+1}\)
 with 
\(d>\max _i v_i\)
 be given which satisfies 
\(\overline{(C1)}\)
. Write 
\(\rho _{(\mathbf{v},d)}=\sum _{\alpha \in \frac{1}{d}{\mathbb {Z}}}\sigma (\alpha )\cdot t^{d\cdot \alpha }\in {\mathbb {Z}}[t]\)
. Is 
\(D_\mathbf{w}=\sum _{\alpha }\sigma (\alpha )\cdot [ e^{2\pi i\alpha }]\)
?

                  
Problem 2 :
:

                    
(Remark 
3.11
 (ii)) Let 
\((\mathbf{v},d)=(v_1,\ldots ,v_n,d)\in {\mathbb {N}}^{n+1}\)
 with 
\(d>\max _i v_i\)
 be given which satisfies (
C
1). Give combinatorial proofs of the formulas in Theorem 
3.9
 which connect 
\(D_\mathbf{w}\)
 and 
\(\rho _{(\mathbf{v},d)}\)
 with the exponents and with one another.

                  
Problem 3:
:

                    
(Remark 
3.11
 (iii)) Make some good use of the conditions for 
J
 with 
\(|J|\ge 2\)
 in (
C
1).

                  
Problem 4:
:

                    
(Remarks 
5.2
) Find examples different from Ivlev’s example for weight systems 
\(\mathbf{w}\)
 which satisfy 
\(\overline{(C1)}\)
, but not (
C
1).

                  
Problem 5:
:

                    
(Remark 
5.6
) Prove or disprove K. Saito’s Conjecture 
5.4
 that 
\(d_\mathbf{w}\in M_1\)
 or 
\(\frac{d_\mathbf{w}}{2}\in M_1\)
 for 
\(\mathbf{w}\)
 with (
C
1). Here 
\(d_\mathbf{w}:=\mathrm{lcm}(\text {denominator of }w_i\, |\, i\in \{1,\ldots ,n\})\)
.

                  
Problem 6:
:

                    
(Remark 
6.4
 (i)) Prove (or disprove) combinatorially Conjecture 
1.4
.

                  
Problem 7:
:

                    
(Remark 
6.4
 (iii)) Find a natural condition on products 
f
 of cyclotomic polynomials which implies for any elementary divisor of 
f
 condition (I) in Theorem 
6.2
 and which is stable under tensor product. Prove that 
\(D_\mathbf{w}\)
 for 
\(\mathbf{w}\)
 with (
C
1) satisfies it (this would prove Conjecture 
1.4
).

                  
Some comments: The problems 1, 2, 3 and 7 are motivated by problem 6, i.e., the wish to prove 
combinatorially
 Conjecture 
1.4
. If Conjecture 
1.4
 and Orlik’s conjecture hold, we understand the Milnor lattice with monodromy of an isolated quasihomogeneous singularity much better, and we can determine 
\(\mathrm{Aut}_{S^1}(H_\mathrm{Milnor},h_\mathrm{mon})\)
 much easier. This, in turn, will be useful for many problems on singularities, for example, for period maps and Torelli problems. The problems 1 and 2 are closely related. A positive solution to one of them will probably also give a positive solution to the other one. And both will probably be useful for positive solutions of the problems 6 and 7. [
6
] made good use of the conditions for 
\(|J|=1\)
 in (
C
1). They give rise to a graph. But the problems here probably require to involve also the conditions for 
\(|J|\ge 2\)
. Problem 3 is vague, but fundamental. The condition (
C
1) is because of Theorem 
3.5
 central for the classification of (weight systems of) isolated quasihomogeneous singularities. We need to be able to work with the condition (
C
1). Problem 3 addresses this. It looks surprisingly difficult to find solutions for the very concrete problem 4. Though the problems 4 and 5 are less important than the other problems. Problem 5 is motivated by the (more important) problems 6 and 7. They are closely related. A positive solution of problem 6 goes probably via a positive solution of problem 7.
The paper is structured as follows. Section 
2
 gives notations and basic facts around cyclotomic polynomials. Section 
3
 introduces for abstract weight systems 
\(\mathbf{w}\)
 and 
\((\mathbf{v},d)\)
 the objects 
\(D_\mathbf{w}\)
 and 
\(\rho _{(\mathbf{v},d)}\)
 and the conditions 
\(\overline{(C1)}\)
 and (
C
1), and it states elementary facts as well as the formulas and facts which hold for the weight systems 
\(\mathbf{w}\)
 of isolated quasihomogeneous singularities. This is all classical. Section 
4
 gives more explicit formulas in the cases of the quasihomogeneous singularities of cycle type and chain type. This builds on Sect. 
3
 and on [
6
] and is elementary. Section 
5
 presents examples. Especially, it gives counter-examples to the part of K. Saito’s Conjecture 
5.4
 which says that 
\(d_\mathbf{w}\in M_1\)
 in the case of a weight system 
\(\mathbf{w}\)
 with all 
\(w_i<\frac{1}{2}\)
. These counter-examples are interesting also in Sect. 
6
. Section 
6
 formulates in Theorem 
6.2
 the main result from [
5
] on automorphisms of Orlik blocks. It discusses Conjecture 
1.4
, it gives examples, and it proves Conjecture 
1.4
 in special cases, which include the cycle type, the chain type, the cases with 
\(n=2\)
 and many of the cases with 
\(n=3\)
 (Theorem 
6.9
).
2 
Notations around cyclotomic polynomials
This section fixes some notations and recalls some well-known formulas around products of cyclotomic polynomials.
In this paper 
\({\mathbb {N}}=\{1,2,3,\ldots \}\)
 and 
\({\mathbb {N}}_0=\{0,1,2,3,\ldots \}\)
. Whenever a number 
\(n\in {\mathbb {N}}\)
 is fixed then 
\(N:=\{1,\ldots ,n\}\)
.
Denote by 
\(\mu ({\mathbb {C}})\subseteq S^1\)
 the group of all unit roots. Denote by 
\({\mathbb {Q}}[ \mu ({\mathbb {C}})]\)
 and 
\({\mathbb {Z}}[ \mu ({\mathbb {C}})]\)
 the group rings with elements 
\(\sum _{j=1}^l b_j[\zeta _j]\)
 where 
\(b_j\in {\mathbb {Q}}\)
, respectively, 
\(b_j\in {\mathbb {Z}}\)
 and where 
\(\zeta _j\in \mu ({\mathbb {C}})\)
, with multiplication 
\([\zeta _1]\cdot [\zeta _2]=[ \zeta _1\cdot \zeta _2]\)
. The unit element is [1]. The trace of an element 
\(\sum _{j=1}^l b_j[\zeta _j]\)
 is
$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{tr}\left( \sum _{j=1}^l b_j[\zeta _j]\right) := \sum _{j=1}^l b_j\cdot \zeta _j\in {\mathbb {C}}. \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.1)
                
The degree of it is
$$\begin{aligned} \deg \left( \sum _{j=1}^l b_j[\zeta _j]\right) := \sum _{j=1}^l b_j\in {\mathbb {Q}}. \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.2)
                
The trace map 
\(\mathrm{tr}:{\mathbb {Q}}[ \mu ({\mathbb {C}})]\rightarrow {\mathbb {C}}\)
 and the degree map 
\(\deg :{\mathbb {Q}}[ \mu ({\mathbb {C}})]\rightarrow {\mathbb {Q}}\)
 are ring homomorphisms.
The divisor of a unitary polynomial 
\(f=(t-\lambda _1)\cdot ...\cdot (t-\lambda _l)\in {\mathbb {C}}[t]\)
 with 
\(\lambda _j\in \mu ({\mathbb {C}})\)
 is
$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{div\, }f:=[ \lambda _1]+\cdots +[\lambda _l]. \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.3)
                
Of course 
\(\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{div\, }f)=\lambda _1+\cdots +\lambda _l\)
 and 
\(\deg (\mathrm{div\, }f)=\deg f\)
.
For two polynomials, 
f
 as above and 
\(g=(t-\kappa _1)\cdot ...\cdot (t-\kappa _k)\)
 with 
\(\kappa _j\in \mu ({\mathbb {C}})\)
, define the new polynomial 
\(f\otimes g\in {\mathbb {C}}[t]\)
 with zeros in 
\(\mu ({\mathbb {C}})\)
 by
$$\begin{aligned} (f\otimes g)(t):=\prod _{i=1}^k\prod _{j=1}^l(t-\kappa _i\lambda _j). \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.4)
                
Then
$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{div\, }(f\otimes g)= & {} (\mathrm{div\, }f)\cdot (\mathrm{div\, }g), \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.5)
                
$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{div\, }(f\otimes g))= & {} (\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{div\, }f))\cdot (\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{div\, }g)), \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.6)
                
$$\begin{aligned} \deg (f\otimes g)= & {} (\deg f)\cdot (\deg g). \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.7)
                
The order 
\(\mathrm{ord\, }(\zeta )\in {\mathbb {N}}\)
 of a unit root 
\(\zeta \in \mu ({\mathbb {C}})\)
 is the minimal number 
\(m\in {\mathbb {N}}\)
 with 
\(\zeta ^m=1\)
. For 
\(m\in {\mathbb {N}}\)
, the 
m
-th cyclotomic polynomial is
$$\begin{aligned} \Phi _m(t)=\prod _{\zeta :\, \mathrm{ord\, }(\zeta )=m}(t-\zeta )\in {\mathbb {C}}[t]. \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.8)
                
It is in 
\({\mathbb {Z}}[t]\)
, it has degree 
\(\varphi (m)\)
, and it is irreducible in 
\({\mathbb {Z}}[t]\)
 and 
\({\mathbb {Q}}[t]\)
. Denote
$$\begin{aligned} \Lambda _m:=\mathrm{div\, }(t^m-1),\quad \Psi _m:=\mathrm{div\, }\Phi _m,\quad E_m:=\frac{1}{m}\Lambda _m. \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.9)
                
Then 
\(\Lambda _1=[ 1]\)
. Of course
$$\begin{aligned} t^n-1= & {} \prod _{m|n}\Phi _m(t), \Lambda _n=\sum _{m|n}\Psi _m, \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.10)
                
$$\begin{aligned} \Phi _n= & {} \prod _{m|n}(t^m-1)^{\mu _\mathrm{Moeb}(\frac{n}{m})},\quad \Psi _n=\sum _{m|n}\mu _\mathrm{Moeb}\left( \frac{n}{m}\right) \cdot \Lambda _m. \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.11)
                
Here 
\(\mu _\mathrm{Moeb}\)
 is the Möbius function [
1
]
$$\begin{aligned} \mu _\mathrm{Moeb}:{\mathbb {N}}\rightarrow & {} \{0,1,-1\},\nonumber \\ m\mapsto & {} \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} (-1)^r &{} \text {if }m=p_1\cdot ...\cdot p_r\text { with } p_1,\ldots ,p_r\\ &{} \text {different prime numbers},\\ 0&{} \text {else} \end{array}\right. \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.12)
                
(here 
\(r=0\)
 is allowed, so 
\(\mu _\mathrm{Moeb}(1)=1\)
). The traces of 
\(\Lambda _m\)
 and 
\(\Psi _m\)
 are
$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{tr}\Lambda _m= & {} \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} 1&{}\text {if }m=1 \\ 0&{}\text {if }m\ge 2,\end{array}\right. \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.13)
                
$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{tr}\Psi _m= & {} \mu _\mathrm{Moeb}(m). \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.14)
                
It is easy to see that
$$\begin{aligned} \Lambda _a\cdot \Lambda _b= & {} \gcd (a,b)\cdot \Lambda _{\mathrm{lcm}(a,b)},\quad E_a\cdot E_b=E_{\mathrm{lcm}(a,b)}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.15)
                
$$\begin{aligned} {}[ \zeta ]\cdot \Lambda _b= & {} \Lambda _b\qquad \text {if }\mathrm{ord\, }(\zeta )|b, \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.16)
                
$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{div\, }(f)\cdot \Lambda _b= & {} \deg f\cdot \Lambda _b\qquad \text {if }f|(t^b-1). \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.17)
                
Especially
$$\begin{aligned} \Lambda _a\cdot \Lambda _b= & {} a\cdot \Lambda _b\qquad \text {if }a|b. \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.18)
                
It is more difficult to write down formulas for 
\(\Psi _a\cdot \Psi _b\)
. They can be cooked up from the following special cases.
$$\begin{aligned} \Psi _a\cdot \Psi _b= & {} \Psi _{a\cdot b}\qquad \text {if } \gcd (a,b)=1, \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.19)
                
$$\begin{aligned} \Psi _{p^a}\cdot \Psi _{p^b}= & {} \varphi (p^b)\cdot \Psi _{p^a} \qquad \text {if }p \text { is a prime number} \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.20)
                
$$\begin{aligned}&\text {and }a>b\ge 0,\nonumber \\ \Psi _{p^a}\cdot \Psi _{p^a}= & {} \varphi (p^a) \cdot \sum _{j=0}^a\Psi _{p^j}-p^{a-1}\cdot \Psi _{p^a}\nonumber \\&\text {if }p \text { is a prime number and }a>0. \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.21)
                
Especially
$$\begin{aligned} \Psi _{2^a}\cdot \Psi _{2^a}= & {} 2^{a-1} \cdot \sum _{j=0}^{a-1}\Psi _{p^j}. \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.22)
                
Fix a finite set 
\(M\subseteq {\mathbb {N}}\)
 and a map 
\(\nu :{\mathbb {N}}\rightarrow {\mathbb {N}}_0\)
 with support 
M
 (so 
\(M=\{m\in {\mathbb {N}}\, |\, \nu (m)\ne 0\}\)
) and define the unitary polynomial
$$\begin{aligned} \Delta :=\prod _{m\in M}\Phi _m^{\nu (m)}\in {\mathbb {Z}}[t] \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.23)
                
(if 
\(M=\emptyset \)
, then 
\(\Delta =1\)
). Of course, then
$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{div\, }\Delta= & {} \sum _{m\in M}\nu (m)\cdot \Psi _m. \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.24)
                
Define also
$$\begin{aligned} d_M:=\mathrm{lcm}(m\in M\}. \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.25)
                
Then 
\(M\subseteq \{m\in {\mathbb {N}}\, |\, m\text { divides }d_M\}\)
. (
2.11
) and (
2.24
) give a unique function 
\(\chi :{\mathbb {N}}\rightarrow {\mathbb {Z}}\)
 with finite support
$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{supp\, }(\chi )\subseteq \{n\in {\mathbb {N}}\, |\, \exists \ m\in M \text { with }n|m\}\subseteq \{n\in {\mathbb {N}}\, |\, n\text { divides }d_M\} \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.26)
                
and
$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{div\, }\Delta= & {} \sum _{n\in {\mathbb {N}}}\chi (n)\cdot \Lambda _n, \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.27)
                
$$\begin{aligned} \nu (m)= & {} \sum _{n:\, m|n}\chi (n), \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.28)
                
$$\begin{aligned} \chi (n)= & {} \sum _{m:\, n|m} \nu (m)\cdot \mu _\mathrm{Moeb}\left( \frac{m}{n}\right) . \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.29)
                
\(\nu \)
 and 
\(\chi \)
 and the following third function 
\(L:{\mathbb {N}}\rightarrow {\mathbb {Z}}\)
 determine each other. 
L
 does not have finite support. The numbers 
\(L(k)\in {\mathbb {Z}}\)
 are the 
Lefschetz numbers
 of 
\(\Delta \)
. They are defined by
$$\begin{aligned} L(k):= & {} \sum _{j=1}^{\deg \Delta }\lambda _j^k\qquad \text {if } \Delta =\prod _{j=1}^{\deg \Delta }(t-\lambda _j). \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.30)
                
Especially 
\(L(1)=\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{div\, }\Delta )\)
. Observe
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{a=0}^{m-1}[ e^{2\pi i a/m}]= & {} \Lambda _m, \nonumber \\ \sum _{a=0}^{m-1}[ e^{2\pi i ka/m}]= & {} \gcd (k,m)\cdot \Lambda _{m/\gcd (k,m)} \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.31)
                
Thus
$$\begin{aligned} L(k)= & {} \sum _{m\in M}\chi (m)\cdot \mathrm{tr}\Bigl (\gcd (k,m)\Lambda _{m/\gcd (k,m)}\Bigr ) \nonumber \\= & {} \sum _{m:\, m|k}m\cdot \chi (m). \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.32)
                
Möbius inversion [
1
] gives
$$\begin{aligned} m\cdot \chi (m)= & {} \sum _{k|m}\mu _\mathrm{Moeb}(\frac{m}{k})\cdot L(k). \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.33)
                
L
 does not have finite support, but the following extended periodicity property:
$$\begin{aligned} L(k)= & {} L(\gcd (k,d_M)). \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.34)
                
Therefore 
L
 is determined by its values on 
\(\{m\in {\mathbb {N}}\, |\, m|d_M\}\)
. (
2.34
) implies the periodicity
$$\begin{aligned} L(k)=L(k+d_M), \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.35)
                
but it is stronger. In fact, (
2.34
) is equivalent to 
\(\mathrm{supp\, }(\chi )\subseteq \{m\in {\mathbb {N}}\, |\, m|d_M\}\)
 and to 
\(M\subseteq \{m\in {\mathbb {N}}\, |\, m|d_M\}\)
.
All the formulas (
2.24
)–(
2.35
) make also sense if 
\(\mathrm{div\, }\Delta \)
 is replaced by any element of 
\({\mathbb {Q}}[ \mu ({\mathbb {C}})]\)
. Then 
\(\nu ,\chi \)
 and

 have values in 
\({\mathbb {Q}}\)
.
3 
Weight system and characteristic polynomial of an isolated quasihomogeneous singularity
Fix a number 
\(n\in {\mathbb {N}}\)
, and denote 
\(N:=\{1,2,\ldots ,n\}\)
 and 
\(e_i:=(0,..,0,1,0,..,0)\in {\mathbb {N}}_0^n\)
 (with 1 at the 
i
-th place) for 
\(i\in N\)
.

                
Definition 3.1

                
A 
weight system
 is a tuple 
\((v_1,\ldots ,v_n,d)\subseteq ({\mathbb {Q}}_{>0})^{n+1}\)
 with 
\(v_i<d\)
. Another weight system is 
equivalent
 to it, if the second one has the form 
\(q\cdot (v_1,\ldots ,v_n,d)\)
 for some 
\(q\in {\mathbb {Q}}_{>0}\)
. A weight system is 
integer
 if 
\((v_1,\ldots ,v_n,d)\in {\mathbb {N}}^{n+1}\)
. It is 
reduced
 if it is integer and it is minimal with this property, i.e., 
\(\gcd (v_1,\ldots ,v_n,d)=1\)
. It is normalized if 
\(d=1\)
.

              
Any equivalence class contains a unique reduced weight system and a unique normalized weight system. From now on, the letters 
\((v_1,\ldots ,v_n,d)\)
 will be reserved for integer weight systems, and 
\((w_1,\ldots ,w_n,1)\)
 will be the equivalent normalized weight system, i.e., 
\(w_i=\frac{v_i}{d}\)
.
Let 
\((v_1,\ldots ,v_n,d)\)
 be an integer weight system (not necessarily reduced, it does not matter here). For 
\(J\subseteq N\)
 and 
\(k\in {\mathbb {N}}_0\)
 denote
$$\begin{aligned} {\mathbb {Z}}^J:= & {} \{\alpha \in {\mathbb {Z}}^n\ |\ \alpha _i=0\text { for }i\notin J\}, \qquad {\mathbb {N}}_0^J:={\mathbb {Z}}^J\cap {\mathbb {N}}_0^n,\\ ({\mathbb {Z}}^n)_k:= & {} \{\alpha \in {\mathbb {Z}}^n\ |\ \sum _i\alpha _i\cdot v_i=k\}, \qquad ({\mathbb {N}}_0^n)_k:=({\mathbb {Z}}^n)_k\cap {\mathbb {N}}_0^n,\\ ({\mathbb {Z}}^J)_k:= & {} {\mathbb {Z}}^J\cap ({\mathbb {Z}}^n)_k, \qquad ({\mathbb {N}}_0^J)_k:=({\mathbb {Z}}^J)_k\cap {\mathbb {N}}_0^n={\mathbb {N}}_0^J\cap ({\mathbb {N}}_0^n)_k. \end{aligned}$$
So, 
\({\mathbb {Z}}^J\)
 is a coordinate plane in 
\({\mathbb {Z}}^n\)
 where some coordinates are 0, 
\(({\mathbb {Z}}^J)_k\)
 is an affine hyperplane in 
\({\mathbb {Z}}^J\)
, and 
\({\mathbb {N}}_0^J\)
 and 
\(({\mathbb {N}}_0^J)_k\)
 are the intersections of 
\({\mathbb {Z}}^J\)
 and 
\(({\mathbb {Z}}^J)_k\)
 with the 
quadrant
\({\mathbb {N}}_0^n\)
. Of course, 
\({\mathbb {Z}}^{N}={\mathbb {Z}}^n\)
 and 
\({\mathbb {Z}}^\emptyset =\{(0,\ldots ,0)\}\)
.

                
Remark 3.2

                
For 
\(J\subseteq N\)
 with 
\(J\ne \emptyset \)
 define the semigroup
$$\begin{aligned} SG(J):=\sum _{j\in J}{\mathbb {N}}_0\cdot v_j\subseteq {\mathbb {N}}_0 \end{aligned}$$

                    (3.1)
                
and observe
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{j\in J}{\mathbb {Z}}\cdot v_j={\mathbb {Z}}\cdot \gcd (v_j\, |\, j\in J). \end{aligned}$$

                    (3.2)
                
Then
$$\begin{aligned} ({\mathbb {N}}_0^J)_k\ne \emptyset\iff & {} k\in SG(J), \end{aligned}$$

                    (3.3)
                
$$\begin{aligned} ({\mathbb {Z}}^J)_k\ne \emptyset\iff & {} \gcd (v_j\, |\, j\in J) | k. \end{aligned}$$

                    (3.4)
                

              
The following combinatorial lemma is a specialization of [
6
, Lemma 2.1]. It will be useful in Theorem 
3.5
. (The conditions 
\((C2)'\)
 and (
C
3) in [

Lemma 3.3

                
Fix an integer weight system 
\((v_1,\ldots ,v_n,d)\)
. The following three conditions (
C
1), 
\((C1)'\)
 and (
C
2) are equivalent.

                  

                    
(C1):

                    
                      
   
\(\forall \ J\subseteq N\text { with }J\ne \emptyset \quad ({\mathbb {N}}_0^J)_d\ne \emptyset \)
 or 
\(\exists \ K\subseteq N{\setminus } J\)
 with 
\(|K|=|J|\)
 and 
\(\forall \ k\in K\ ({\mathbb {N}}_0^J)_{d-v_k}\ne \emptyset \)
.

                    
                  

                  

                    
(C1)’:

                    
                      
   As (C1), but only for

 with 
\(|J|\le \frac{n+1}{2}\)
.

                    
                  

                  

                    
(C2):

                    
                      
   
\(\forall \ J\subseteq N\text { with }J\ne \emptyset \quad \exists \ K\subseteq N\)
 with 
\(|K|=|J|\)
 and 
\(\forall \ k\in K\ ({\mathbb {N}}_0^J)_{d-v_k}\ne \emptyset \)
.

                    
                  

                

              
                
Proof

                
\((C1)\Rightarrow (C1)'\)
 is trivial. 
\((C1)'\Rightarrow (C1)\)
 and 
\((C2)\Rightarrow (C1)\)
 are easy. See [
6
] for details. The least easy implication is 
\((C1)\Rightarrow (C2)\)
. In [

] it was proved via the condition (

Suppose that (

\(J\subseteq N\)
 with 
\(J\ne \emptyset \)
. We want to find a 
\(K\subseteq N\)
 such that

 and 
K
 satisfy (

2). Define the support of

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{supp\, }(J):=\{j\in J\, |\ \exists \ \alpha \in ({\mathbb {N}}_0^J)_d \text { with }\alpha _j\ne 0\}\subseteq J. \end{aligned}$$
Consider 
\(J_1:=J{\setminus } \mathrm{supp\, }(J)\)
.

                
1st case,
\(J_1=\emptyset \)
: Then 
J
 and 
\(K:=J\)
 satisfy (
C
2).

                
2nd case,
\(J_1\ne \emptyset \)
: The definition of 
\(J_1\)
 implies 
\(({\mathbb {N}}_0^{J_1})_d=\emptyset \)
. Therefore (
C
1) gives the existence of a set 
\(K_1\subseteq N{\setminus } J_1\)
 with 
\(|K_1|=|J_1|\)
 and 
\(\forall \ k\in K_1\quad ({\mathbb {N}}_0^{J_1})_{d-v_k}\ne \emptyset \)
.

                
Because of

 for some

If some

\(\square \)

              
                
Remarks 3.4

                
(i) Denote with a bar the analogous conditions 
\(\overline{(C1)}, \overline{(C1)'}, \overline{(C2)}\)
 where 
\({\mathbb {N}}_0\)
 is replaced by 
\({\mathbb {Z}}\)
. Also these conditions are equivalent to one another. The proof is the same as above.

                
(ii) Recall that a polynomial
$$\begin{aligned} f=\sum _{\alpha \in {\mathbb {N}}_0^n}a_\alpha \cdot x^\alpha \in {\mathbb {C}}[x_1,\ldots ,x_n] \quad \text {where}\quad x^\alpha =x_1^{\alpha _1}...x_n^{\alpha _n} \end{aligned}$$
is quasihomogeneous with respect to a weight system 
\((v_1,\ldots ,v_n,d)\)
 if
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{i=1}^n\alpha _i\cdot v_i=d\quad \text {for all }\alpha \text { with }a_\alpha \ne 0. \end{aligned}$$
Recall that a quasihomogeneous polynomial has an isolated singularity at 0 if the functions 
\(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\)
 vanish simultaneously precisely at 0. The set of all quasihomogeneous polynomials with a given weight system 
\((v_1,\ldots ,v_n,d)\)
 is the (finite dimensional) space 
\(\{\sum _{\alpha \in ({\mathbb {N}}_0^n)_d}a_\alpha \cdot x^\alpha \, |\, a_\alpha \in {\mathbb {C}}\}\)
. A generic quasihomogeneous polynomial with the given weight system is one in a Zariski open subset of this space.

                
(iii) Let 
f
 be a quasihomogeneous polynomial with weight system 
\((v_1,\ldots ,v_n,d)\)
 and an isolated singularity at 0. Then all fibers 
\(f^{-1}(\tau )\subseteq {\mathbb {C}}^n\)
 for 
\(\tau \in {\mathbb {C}}^*\)
 are smooth and diffeomorphic. The Milnor lattice 
\(H_\mathrm{Milnor}:=H_{n-1}^{(red)}(f^{-1}(1),{\mathbb {Z}})\)
 (reduced homology if and only if 
\(n=1\)
) is a 
\({\mathbb {Z}}\)
-lattice of rank 
\(\mu =\prod _{j=1}^n(\frac{d}{v_j}-1)\in {\mathbb {N}}\)
. It comes equipped with a natural automorphism 
\(h_\mathrm{mon}\)
 of finite order, called 
monodromy
. The pair 
\((H_\mathrm{Milnor},h_\mathrm{mon})\)
 depends up to isomorphism only upon the weight system. Theorem 
3.9
 expresses 
\(p_{ch,h_\mathrm{mon}}\)
 in terms of the weight system. Orlik’s conjecture predicts the isomorphism class of the pair 
\((H_\mathrm{Milnor},h_\mathrm{mon})\)
 and uses only the weight system for this.

Theorem 3.5

                
Let 
\((v_1,\ldots ,v_n,d)\in {\mathbb {N}}^{n+1}\)
 be an integer weight system. The following three conditions are equivalent.

                  

                    
(IS3):

                    
                      
There exists a quasihomogeneous polynomial 
f
 with the weight system 
\((v_1,\ldots ,v_n,d)\)
 and an isolated singularity at 0.

                    
                  

                  

                    
(IS3)’:

                    
                      
A generic quasihomogeneous polynomial with the weight system 
\((v_1,\ldots ,v_n,d)\)
 has an isolated singularity at 0.

                    
                  

                  

                    
(C1) to (C2):

                    
                      
The weight system 
\((v_1,\ldots ,v_n, d)\)
 satisfies one of the equivalent conditions 
\((C1),(C1)',(C2)\)
.

                    
                  

                

              
In Definition

Definition 3.6

                
Let 
\((\mathbf{v},d)=(v_1,\ldots ,v_n,d)\in {\mathbb {N}}^{n+1}\)
 be an integer weight system.

                  

                    
(a)

                    
                      
Define unique numbers 
\(s_1,\ldots ,s_n,t_1,\ldots ,t_n\in {\mathbb {N}}\)

 so by reducing the fractions

. The numbers depend only on the normalized weight system 
\(\mathbf{w}=(w_1,\ldots w_n)=(\frac{v_1}{d},\ldots ,\frac{v_n}{d})\)
.

                    
                  

                  

                    
(b)

                    
                      
Define 
$$\begin{aligned} d_\mathbf{w}:=\mathrm{lcm}(t_j\, |\, j\in N). \end{aligned}$$

                    (3.5)
                
 Of course 
\(d_\mathbf{w}|d\)
. If 
\((\mathbf{v},d)\)
 is reduced and 
\(\gcd (v_1,\ldots ,v_n)|d\)
 (which holds, for example, if 
\(\overline{(C2)}\)

\(\gcd (v_1,\ldots ,v_n)=1\)

\(d_\mathbf{w}=d\)

(c)

                    
                      
For

                    (3.7)
                
 (the empty product is by definition 1).

                    
                  

                  

                    
(d)

The last point in this section is a discussion of a well-known fact on the order of the monodromy of a quasihomogeneous singularity. That order is
$$\begin{aligned} d_\mathrm{mon}:=\mathrm{lcm}(m\in {\mathbb {N}}\, |\, \nu (m)>0) \end{aligned}$$
where the numbers 
\(\nu (m)\)
 are determined by 
\(D_\mathbf{w}=\sum _{m\in {\mathbb {N}}}\nu (m)\cdot \Psi _m\)
.

                
Lemma 3.12

                
In the case of an isolated quasihomogeneous singularity with 
\(w_j\le \frac{1}{2}\)
 for all 
\(j\in N\)
, 
\(d_\mathrm{mon}=d_\mathbf{w}\)
 or 
\(d_\mathrm{mon}=\frac{d_\mathbf{w}}{2}\)
. If all 
\(w_j<\frac{1}{2}\)
, then 
\(d_\mathrm{mon}=d_\mathbf{w}\)
.

              
                
Proof

                
Because of the Definition (
3.9
) of 
\(D_\mathbf{w}\)
, 
\(d_\mathrm{mon}\)
 is a divisor of 
\(d_\mathbf{w}\)
. The equalities
$$\begin{aligned} \prod _{j=1}^n\left( \frac{1}{w_j}-1\right)= & {} \mu = \mathrm{tr}\mathrm{id}= \mathrm{tr}(Mon)^{d_\mathrm{mon}} =L(d_\mathrm{mon})\\= & {} \pm \mu (d_\mathrm{mon})=\pm \prod _{j\, :\, t_j|d_\mathrm{mon}}\left( \frac{1}{w_j}-1\right) \end{aligned}$$
show that the second product can miss only indices 
j
 with 
\(w_j=\frac{1}{2}\)
. Therefore 
\(\mathrm{lcm}(t_j\ |\ w_j<\frac{1}{2})\)
 divides 
\(d_\mathrm{mon}\)
. If all 
\(w_j<\frac{1}{2}\)
, then 
\(\mathrm{lcm}(t_j\ |\ w_j<\frac{1}{2})=d_\mathbf{w}\)
. If some 
\(w_j=\frac{1}{2}\)
 then 
\(\mathrm{lcm}(t_j\ |\ w_j<\frac{1}{2})=d_\mathbf{w}\)
 or 
\(\frac{d_\mathbf{w}}{2}\)
. 
\(\square \)

Formulas for isolated quasihomogeneous singularities of cycle type and of chain type
The formulas in this section concern the isolated quasihomogeneous singularities of 
cycle type
,
$$\begin{aligned}&x_nx_1^{a_1}+x_1x_2^{a_2}+x_2x_3^{a_3}+\cdots +x_{n-1}x_n^{a_n},\qquad \text {where }n\in {\mathbb {N}}, \ a_1,\ldots ,a_n\in {\mathbb {N}},\\&\text { and in the case }n\text { even neither }a_j=1\text { for all even }j\text { nor } a_j=1\text { for all odd }j, \end{aligned}$$
and of 
chain type
,
$$\begin{aligned} x_1^{a_1+1}+x_1x_2^{a_2}+x_2x_3^{a_3}+\cdots +x_{n-1}x_n^{a_n}\qquad \text {where }n\in {\mathbb {N}}, \ a_1,\ldots ,a_n\in {\mathbb {N}}. \end{aligned}$$
The formulas start with normalized weight systems 
\((\mathbf{w},1)=(w_1,\ldots ,w_n,1)\)
 which satisfy a part of the conditions (
C
1) and (
C
2) in Lemma 
3.3
, namely that part which concerns subsets 
\(J\subseteq N\)
 with 
\(|J|=1\)
. That part leads to graphs and types of weight systems, see Sect. 3 in [
6
]. As already said in Remark 
3.10
 (iv), it is difficult to make use of the conditions in (
C
1) and (
C
2) for 
J
 with 
\(|J|\ge 2\)
. The formulas here do not make use of these higher conditions.
The formulas extend formulas in [
6
] for parts of the weight system and parts of the Milnor number to formulas for parts of 
\(D_\mathbf{w}\)
. Some calculations already made in [
6
] will be reproduced here for better readability. We start with the cycle type, then consider a generalization of the chain type and finally specialize that to the chain type. The formulas for the generalization of the chain type will allow to glue its root to another graph.
Define the function
$$\begin{aligned} \rho :\bigcup _{k=0}^\infty {\mathbb {Z}}^k\rightarrow & {} {\mathbb {Z}},\nonumber \\ \rho (x_1,\ldots ,x_k):= & {} x_1\ldots x_k-x_2\ldots x_k+\cdots +(-1)^{k-1}x_k+(-1)^k \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.1)
                
(the case 
\(k=0\)
 is 
\(\rho (\emptyset )=1\)
).

                
Lemma 4.1

                
(Partly [
6
, Lemma 3.4 and (4.6)]) Fix 
\(n\in {\mathbb {N}}\)
 and 
n
 numbers 
\(a_1,\ldots ,a_n\in {\mathbb {N}}\)
 such that, if 
n
 is even, neither 
\(a_j=1\)
 for all even 
j
 nor 
\(a_j=1\)
 for all odd 
j
.

                
Then there is a unique normalized weight system 
\((\mathbf{w},1)=(w_1,\ldots ,w_n,1)\)
 with 
\(a_jw_j+w_{j-1}=1\)
 for all 
\(j\in N\)
, where 
\(w_0:=w_n\)
. It is
$$\begin{aligned} w_j= & {} \frac{v_j}{d}\qquad \text {where }\nonumber \\ v_j:= & {} \rho (a_{j-1},a_{j-2},\ldots ,a_2,a_1,a_n,a_{n-1},\ldots ,a_{j+1})\in {\mathbb {N}}, \nonumber \\ d:= & {} \prod _{j=1}^na_j-(-1)^n\in {\mathbb {N}}. \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.2)
                
Define
$$\begin{aligned} \gamma :=\gcd (v_1,d). \end{aligned}$$
Then the unique numbers 
\(s_j,t_j\in {\mathbb {N}}\)
 with 
\(\gcd (s_j,t_j)=1\)
 and 
\(w_j=\frac{s_j}{t_j}\)
 from Definition 
3.6
 are
$$\begin{aligned} s_j= & {} \frac{v_j}{\gamma },\nonumber \\ t_1= & {} ...=t_n=\frac{d}{\gamma }. \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.3)
                
Especially 
\(\gamma =\gcd (v_j,d)\)
 for any 
\(j\in N\)
.

                
The Milnor number is
$$\begin{aligned} \mu= & {} \prod _{j=1}^n a_j. \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.4)
                
The divisor 
\(D_\mathbf{w}\)
 from Definition 
3.6
 is
$$\begin{aligned} D_\mathbf{w}= & {} \gamma \cdot \Lambda _{d/\gamma } + (-1)^n\cdot \Lambda _1. \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.5)
                

              
                
Proof

                
The matrix of the system 
\(a_jw_j+w_{j-1}=1\)
 of linear equations has the determinant
$$\begin{aligned} \det \begin{pmatrix}a_1 &{} &{} &{} 1\\ 1 &{} a_2 &{} &{} \\ &{} \ddots &{} \ddots &{} \\ &{} &{} 1 &{} a_n \end{pmatrix} =\prod _{j=1}^n a_j - (-1)^n=d, \end{aligned}$$
here 
\(d>0\)
 by hypothesis. Therefore it has a unique solution. It is easy to see that this solution is given by (
4.2
). The conditions that in the case 
n
 even neither 
\(a_j=1\)
 for all even 
j
 nor 
\(a_j=1\)
 for all odd 
j
 make sure that the numbers 
\(v_j\)
 and 
d
 and the weights 
\(w_j\)
 are not zero, but positive. The equation 
\(a_jw_j+w_{j-1}=1\)
 implies 
\(w_j<1\)
.

                
By definition 
\(t_1=d/\gamma \)
. The identities (where 
\(w_0=w_n,s_0=s_n,t_0=t_n\)
)
$$\begin{aligned} \left. \begin{array}{r} \frac{s_j}{t_j}=w_j=\frac{1-w_{j-1}}{a_j} =\frac{t_{j-1}-s_{j-1}}{t_{j-1}\cdot a_j}\\ \gcd (t_{j-1},t_{j-1}-s_{j-1})=1 \end{array} \right\} \Rightarrow t_j=t_{j-1}\cdot \frac{a_j}{\gcd (a_j,t_{j-1}-s_{j-1})} \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.6)
                
show 
\(t_{j-1}|t_j\)
. As we have a cycle here, 
\(t_j=d/\gamma \)
 and 
\(\gcd (v_j,d)=\gamma \)
 for any 
\(j\in N\)
.

                
The Milnor number is calculated by (with 
\(v_0=v_n\)
)
$$\begin{aligned} \mu = \prod _{j=1}^n\frac{d-v_{j-1}}{v_{j-1}} =\prod _{j=1}^n \frac{a_j\cdot v_j}{v_{j-1}} =a_1\cdot ...\cdot a_n. \end{aligned}$$
The divisor 
\(D_\mathbf{w}\)
 is defined in (
3.9
). Because of (
2.15
) it has only the two summands 
\(\Lambda _{d/\gamma }\)
 and 
\(\Lambda _1\)
, and the coefficient of 
\(\Lambda _1\)
 is obviously (from (
3.9
)) 
\(\chi (1)=(-1)^n\)
. As 
\(\mu =\deg D_\mathbf{w}= \chi (d/\gamma )\cdot d/\gamma + \chi (1)\)
, the coefficient 
\(\chi (d/\gamma )\)
 of 
\(\Lambda _{d/\gamma }\)
 is 
\(\chi (d/\gamma )=\gamma \)
, so (
4.5
) holds. 
\(\square \)

              
Lemma 
4.2
 is a slight generalization of the chain type. Corollary 
4.3
 specializes it to the chain type.

                
Lemma 4.2

                
(Partly [
6
, (4.10)]) Fix 
\(n\in {\mathbb {N}}\)
, 
n
 numbers 
\(a_1,\ldots ,a_n\in {\mathbb {N}}\)
, two numbers 
\(s_0,t_0\in {\mathbb {N}}\)
 with 
\(s_0<t_0\)
 and 
\(\gcd (s_0,t_0)=1\)
, and define 
\(w_0:=\frac{s_0}{t_0}\)
.

                
Then there are unique weights 
\(w_1,\ldots ,w_n\in {\mathbb {Q}}\cap (0,1)\)
 with 
\(a_jw_j+w_{j-1}=1\)
 for 
\(j=1,\ldots ,n\)
. Write 
\(w_j=\frac{s_j}{t_j}\)
 with 
\(s_j,t_j\in {\mathbb {N}}\)
 and 
\(\gcd (s_j,t_j)=1\)
 and 
\(\beta _j:=\gcd (t_{j-1}-s_{j-1},a_j)\in {\mathbb {N}}\)
 and 
\(\alpha _j:=\frac{a_j}{\beta _j}\in {\mathbb {N}}\)
. Then
$$\begin{aligned} s_j= & {} \frac{\rho (a_{j-1},\ldots ,a_1)\cdot t_0+(-1)^js_0}{\beta _j\cdot ...\cdot \beta _1}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.7)
                
$$\begin{aligned} t_j= & {} \alpha _j\cdot t_{j-1}= \alpha _j\cdot ...\cdot \alpha _1\cdot t_0. \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.8)
                
The partial divisor and the partial Milnor number associated to 
\((w_1,\ldots ,w_n)\)
 are
$$\begin{aligned} \prod _{j=1}^n\left( \frac{1}{s_j}\Lambda _{t_j}-\Lambda _1\right)= & {} (-1)^n\Lambda _1+ \sum _{j=1}^n (-1)^{n-j}\frac{\beta _j...\beta _1}{t_0-s_0}\cdot \Lambda _{t_j} \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.9)
                
$$\begin{aligned}= & {} (-1)^n E_1+\sum _{j=1}^n (-1)^{n-j}\frac{a_j...a_1}{1-w_0} \cdot E_{t_j}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.10)
                
$$\begin{aligned} \prod _{j=1}^n\left( \frac{1}{w_j}-1\right)= & {} \frac{\rho (a_n,a_{n-1},\ldots ,a_1)+(-1)^{n-1}w_0}{1-w_0}. \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.11)
                

              
                
Proof

                
The weights are unique and in 
\({\mathbb {Q}}\cap (0,1)\)
 because they are determined inductively by the equations 
\(a_jw_j+w_{j-1}=1\)
, i.e.,
$$\begin{aligned} w_j= & {} \frac{1-w_{j-1}}{a_j} = \frac{t_{j-1}-s_{j-1}}{a_j\cdot t_{j-1}}\\= & {} \frac{(t_{j-1}-s_{j-1})/\beta _j}{\alpha _j\cdot t_{j-1}}. \end{aligned}$$
As 
\(1=\gcd (s_{j-1},t_{j-1})=\gcd (t_{j-1}-s_{j-1},t_{j-1})\)
, this shows (
4.8
). For 
\(j=1\)
 (
4.7
) is clear. For 
\(j\ge 2\)
 the additional calculation
$$\begin{aligned} \beta _j\cdot s_j= & {} t_{j-1}-s_{j-1}\\= & {} \frac{a_{j-1}\cdot ...\cdot a_1\cdot t_0}{\beta _{j-1}\cdot ...\cdot \beta _1}- \frac{\rho (a_{j-2},\ldots ,a_1)\cdot t_0+(-1)^{j-1}s_0}{\beta _{j-1}\cdot ...\cdot \beta _1}\\= & {} \frac{\rho (a_{j-1},\ldots ,a_1)\cdot t_0+(-1)^js_0}{\beta _{j-1}\cdot ...\cdot \beta _1} \end{aligned}$$
shows (
4.7
). Now the partial divisor is also calculated inductively. The induction uses the partial divisor and the partial Milnor number for 
\(n-1\)
. Also 
\(t_j|t_n\)
 and (
2.18
) (
\(\Lambda _a\Lambda _b=a\Lambda _b\)
 for 
a
|
b
) are used.
$$\begin{aligned}&\left( \prod _{j=1}^{n-1}\left( \frac{1}{s_j}\Lambda _{t_j}-\Lambda _1\right) \right) \cdot \left( \frac{1}{s_n}\Lambda _{t_n}-\Lambda _1\right) \\&\quad = \left( \prod _{j=1}^{n-1} \left( \frac{1}{w_j}-1\right) \right) \cdot \frac{1}{s_n}\Lambda _{t_n} -\prod _{j=1}^{n-1} \left( \frac{1}{s_j}\Lambda _{t_j}-\Lambda _1\right) \\&\quad = \frac{\beta _n...\beta _1}{t_0-s_0}\cdot \Lambda _{t_n}+ (-1)^n+ \sum _{j=1}^{n-1} (-1)^{n-j}\frac{\beta _j...\beta _1}{t_0-s_0}\cdot \Lambda _{t_j}. \end{aligned}$$
This shows (
4.9
) and (
4.10
). The partial Milnor number is the degree of the partial divisor. 
\(\square \)

              
                
Corollary 4.3

                
In the situation of Lemma 
4.2
, suppose 
\(w_0=w_1\)
. Then
$$\begin{aligned} s_0=s_1=1,t_0=t_1=a_1+1,\beta _1=a_1,\alpha _1=1. \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.12)
                
Define
$$\begin{aligned} b_k:= & {} (a_1+1)\cdot a_2\cdot ...\cdot a_k \quad \text {for }k=1,\ldots ,n,\quad b_0:= 1, \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.13)
                
$$\begin{aligned} \mu _k:= & {} \rho (a_k,\ldots ,a_2,a_1+1),\quad \text {for }k=1,\ldots ,n,\quad \mu _0:=1. \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.14)
                
Then
$$\begin{aligned} s_j= & {} \frac{\mu _{j-1}}{\beta _j\cdot ...\cdot \beta _2},\end{aligned}$$

                    (4.15)
                
$$\begin{aligned} t_j= & {} \alpha _j\cdot t_{j-1}= \alpha _j\cdot ...\cdot \alpha _2\cdot (a_1+1) =\frac{b_j}{\beta _j\cdot ...\cdot \beta _2},\end{aligned}$$

                    (4.16)
                
$$\begin{aligned} D_\mathbf{w}= & {} \prod _{j=1}^n\left( \frac{1}{s_j}\Lambda _{t_j}-\Lambda _1\right) \nonumber \\= & {} (-1)^n+ \sum _{j=1}^n (-1)^{n-j}\beta _j...\beta _2\cdot \Lambda _{t_j} \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.17)
                
$$\begin{aligned}= & {} (-1)^n+\sum _{j=1}^n (-1)^{n-j}b_j \cdot E_{t_j}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.18)
                
$$\begin{aligned} \mu _k= & {} \prod _{j=1}^k\left( \frac{1}{w_j}-1\right) =b_k-\mu _{k-1} . \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.19)
                
Furthermore, define
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{j=0}^n(-1)^{n-j}\Lambda _{b_j}=: & {} \sum _{i=1}^\mu [\lambda _i] . \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.20)
                
The definition (
4.20
) makes sense, as obviously for the divisor on the left hand side,

                    (4.21)
                
Then
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{j=0}^n(-1)^{n-j}\Lambda _{b_j}= & {} \prod _{j=1}^n\left( \frac{1}{\mu _{j-1}} \Lambda _{b_j}-\Lambda _1\right) \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.22)
                
$$\begin{aligned} D_\mathbf{w}= & {} \sum _{i=1}^\mu [ \lambda _i^\mu ] . \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.23)
                

              
                
Proof

                
Formula (
4.12
) is trivial. The formulas (
4.15
) to (
4.19
) are immediate consequences of the formulas in Lemma 
4.2
. (
4.22
) is proved inductively by a similar calculation as (
4.9
),
$$\begin{aligned}&\left( \prod _{j=1}^n\left( \frac{1}{\mu _{j-1}}\Lambda _{b_j}-\Lambda _1\right) \right) \cdot \left( \frac{1}{\mu _{n-1}}\Lambda _{b_n}-\Lambda _1\right) \\&\quad = \left( \prod _{j=1}^{n-1} \left( \frac{1}{w_j}-1\right) \right) \cdot \frac{1}{\mu _ {n-1}}\Lambda _{b_n} -\prod _{j=1}^{n-1} \left( \frac{1}{\mu _{j-1}}\Lambda _{b_j}-\Lambda _1\right) \\&\quad = \Lambda _{b_n}+ \sum _{j=0}^{n-1} (-1)^{n-j}\Lambda _{b_j}. \end{aligned}$$
For the final formula (
4.23
), it is in view of (
2.31
) and (
4.18
) enough to show
$$\begin{aligned} \frac{b_j}{\gcd (b_j,\mu )}= & {} t_j\qquad \text {for }j\ge 1. \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.24)
                
But 
\(\mu _k=b_k-\mu _{k-1}\)
 (for 
\(k\ge 1\)
) and 
\(b_k=a_kb_{k-1}\)
 (for 
\(k\ge 2\)
) show
$$\begin{aligned} \gcd (b_j,\mu )= & {} \gcd (b_j,\mu _{n-1})=...= \gcd (b_j,\mu _j)=\gcd (b_j,\mu _{j-1}). \end{aligned}$$
As 
\(w_j=\frac{s_j}{t_j}=\frac{\mu _{j-1}}{b_j}\)
,
$$\begin{aligned} t_j = \frac{b_j}{\gcd (b_j,\mu _{j-1})} =\frac{b_j}{\gcd (b_j,\mu )}. \end{aligned}$$
\(\square \)

              
                
Remark 4.4

                
The last formula (
4.23
) in corollary 
4.3
 fits to a result of Orlik and Randell [
16
, (2.11) theorem]. They showed that the integral monodromy is the 
\(\mu \)
-th power of a cyclic automorphism of the Milnor lattice, whose eigenvalues are given by the divisor in (
4.20
). Formula (
4.23
) just confirms that the divisor 
\(D_\mathbf{w}\)
 has the eigenvalues which fit to this theorem.

                
We made this calculation mainly to see how it works and to get some inspiration for good guesses for other types of weight systems of isolated quasihomogeneous singularities.

A conjecture on the orders of the eigenvalues of the monodromy of an isolated quasihomogeneous singularity
Recall the Definition 
1.1
 of the Orlik block 
\((H_M,h_M)\)
 and of the group 
\(\mathrm{Aut}_{S^1}(H_M,h_M)\)
 for a finite nonempty set 
\(M\subseteq {\mathbb {N}}\)
. The main result in [
5
] characterizes those sets 
M
 for which 
\(\mathrm{Aut}_{S^1}(H_M,h_M)\)

M
. It is recalled below in Theorem 
6.2
. The following definitions are needed.

                
Definition 6.1

                
Let 
\(M\subseteq {\mathbb {N}}\)
 be a finite set of positive integers.

                
(a) A graph 
\({\mathscr {G}}(M)=(M,E(M))\)
 is associated to it as follows. 
M
 itself is the set of vertices. The edges in 
E
(
M
) are directed. The set 
E
(
m
) is defined as follows. From a vertex 
\(m_1\in M\)
 to a vertex 
\(m_2\in M\)
 there is no edge if at least one of the following two conditions holds:

                  

                    
(i)

                    
                      
\(m_1/m_2\)
 is not a power of a prime number.

                    
                  

                  

                    
(ii)

                    
                      
An 
\(m_3\in M{\setminus } \{m_1,m_2\}\)
 with 
\(m_2|m_3|m_1\)
 exists.

                    
                  

                
If 
\(m_1/m_2\)
 is a power 
\(p^k\)
 with 
\(k\in {\mathbb {N}}\)
 of a prime number 
p
 and if no 
\(m_3\in M{\setminus } \{m_1,m_2\}\)
 with 
\(m_2|m_3|m_1\)
 exists, then there is a directed edge from 
\(m_1\)
 to 
\(m_2\)
, which is additionally labeled with 
p
. It is called a 
p
-edge. Together such edges form the set 
E
(
M
) of all edges.

                
(b) For any prime number 
p
 the components of the graph 
\((M,E(M){\setminus } \{p\text {-edges}\})\)
 which is obtained by deleting all 
p
-edges, are called the 
p
-planes of the graph. A 
p
-plane is called a highest 
p
-plane if no 
p
-edge ends at a vertex of the 
p
-plane. A 
p
-edge from 
\(m_1\)
 to 
\(m_2\)
 is called a highest 
p
-edge if no 
p
-edge ends at 
\(m_1\)
.

                
(c) A property 
\((T_p)\)
 for a prime number 
p
 and a property 
\((S_2)\)
 for the prime number 2:
$$\begin{aligned} (T_p)&: \text {The graph }{\mathscr {G}}(M) \text { has only one highest }p\text {-plane.} \end{aligned}$$

                    (6.1)
                
$$\begin{aligned} (S_2)&: \text {The graph } (M,E(M){\setminus } \{\text {highest }2\text {-edges}\}) \nonumber \\&\quad \text { has only 1 or 2 components.} \end{aligned}$$

                    (6.2)
                
(d) The least common multiple of the numbers in 
M

Lemma 6.8

                
Suppose that numbers 
\(k_1,\ldots ,k_l\in {\mathbb {N}}\)
 with 
\(k_{j}|k_{j-1}\)
 for 
\(j=2,\ldots ,l\)
 are given. Then the set 
\(M\subseteq {\mathbb {N}}\)
 which is defined by 
\(\Lambda _{k_1}-\Lambda _{k_2}+\cdots +(-1)^{l-1}\Lambda _{k_l}=\sum _{m\in M}\Psi _m\)
 is either empty or satisfies the conditions in Remark 
6.6
(ii).

              
                
Proof

                
We suppose that the set 
M
 is not empty. If 
\(k_j=k_{j-1}\)
 for some 
\(j\in \{2,3,\ldots ,l\}\)
, we can drop 
\(k_j\)
 and 
\(k_{j-1}\)
. Therefore we can suppose 
\(k_j<k_{j-1}\)
 for 
\(j\in \{2,\ldots ,l\}\)
. We have to prove the following two claims.

              
                
Claim 1

                
The graph 
\({\mathscr {G}}(M)\)
 is a directed graph with root 
\(k_1\)
.

              
                
Claim 2

                
In 
\({\mathscr {G}}(M)\)
 a chain of 2-edges exists which connects all 2-planes.

              
                
Proof of claim 1:

                
The cases 
\(l\in \{1,2\}\)
 are trivial. Suppose 
\(l\ge 3\)
. The proof uses induction over 
l
.

                
Define the sets 
\(M_1\)
 and 
\(M_2\)
 by 
\(\Lambda _{k_1}-\Lambda _{k_2}=\sum _{m\in M_1}\Psi _m\)
 and 
\(\sum _{j=3}^l(-1)^{j-1}\Lambda _{k_j}=\sum _{m\in M_2}\Psi _m\)
, so that 
\(M=M_1\dot{\cup }M_2\)
. The graph 
\({\mathscr {G}}(M_1)\)
 is obviously a directed graph with root 
\(k_1\)
. The graph 
\({\mathscr {G}}(M_2)\)
 is by induction hypothesis a directed graph with root 
\(k_3\)
. For the proof of the claim it is sufficient to show that the graph 
\({\mathscr {G}}(M)\)
 contains a directed edge from a vertex in 
\(M_1\)
 to 
\(k_3\)
. As 
\(k_2<k_1\)
, a prime number 
q
 with 
\(l(k_2,q)<l(k_1,q)\)
 exists. Then the number
$$\begin{aligned} m:= q^{l(k_1,q)}\cdot \prod _{p\ \text {prime number},p\ne q} p^{l(k_3,p)} \end{aligned}$$
is in 
\(M_1\)
, and there is a directed edge from 
m
 to 
\(k_3\)
. 
\(\square \)

              
Useful for the proof of claim 2 will be

                
Claim 3

                
For any prime number 
p
and any
\(r\in {\mathbb {N}}_0\)
, 
the set
\(M(p,r):= \{m\in M\, |\, l(m,p)=r\}\)
is either empty or a single
\(p-plane\)
. 
In the second case its graph is a directed graph with a root.

              
                
Proof of claim 3:

                
\(M(p,r)=\{p^r\cdot m\, |\, m\in {\widetilde{M}}(p,r)\}\)
 where 
\({\widetilde{M}}(p,r)\)
 is the support of the divisor
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{j:\, l(k_j,p)\ge r}(-1)^{j-1}\Lambda _{\widetilde{k_j}} \quad \text {with }\widetilde{k_j}:=p^{-l(k_j,p)}\cdot k_j. \end{aligned}$$
If this divisor is not 0, claim 1 applies and gives claim 3.

              
                
Proof of claim 2:

                
Two cases will be distinguished.

                
1st case
, for any odd 
\(j\in \{1,\ldots ,l-1\}\)
\(\frac{k_j}{k_{j+1}}=2^{l(k_j,2)-l(k_{j+1},2)}\)
: Then
$$\begin{aligned} M(2,r)= & {} \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \emptyset &{} \text { if }r>l(k_1,2),\\ &{} \text { or if }l(k_j,2)\ge r>l(k_{j+1},2) \text { for an even }j,\\ &{} \text { or if }l(k_l,2)\ge r\text { and } l\text { is even.}\\ \{2^r\cdot m &{}| \ m|\widetilde{k_j}\} \text { where }\widetilde{k_j}:=2^{-l(k_j,2)}\cdot k_j\\ &{} \text { if }l(k_j,2)\ge r> l(k_{j+1},2)\text { for an odd } j,\\ &{} \text { or if }l(k_l,2)\ge r\text { and } j=l\text { is odd.} \end{array}\right. \end{aligned}$$
Define
$$\begin{aligned} \widetilde{k_\mathrm{min}}:= \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \widetilde{k_l} &{} \text { if }l\text { is odd,}\\ \widetilde{k_{l-1}} &{} \text { if }l\text { is even.} \end{array}\right. \end{aligned}$$
Then the set 
\(\{2^r\cdot \widetilde{k_\mathrm{min}}\, |\, M(2,r)\ne \emptyset \}\)
 is the set of vertices in 
M
 of a chain of 2-edges which connects all 2-planes.

                
2nd case
, a minimal odd 
\(j\in \{1,2,\ldots ,l-1\}\)
 with 
\(\frac{k_j}{k_{j+1}}\ne 2^{l(k_j,2)-l(k_{j+1},2)}\)
 exists: Then a prime number 
\(p\ge 3\)
 with 
\(l(k_j,p)> l(k_{j+1},p)\)
 exists. And then 
\(\{2^{r-l(k_j,2)}\cdot k_j\, |\, 0\le r\le l(k_j,2)\} \subseteq M\)
. Therefore for 
\(0\le r\le l(k_j,2)\)
 the set 
M
(2, 
r
) is not empty. For 
\(r>l(k_j,2)\)
 the set 
M
(2, 
r
) is as in the 1st case. Thus the set 
\(\{2^{r-l(k_j,2)}\cdot k_j\, |\, M(2,r)\ne \emptyset \}\)
 is the set of vertices in 
M
 of a chain of 2-edges which connects all 2-planes. 
\(\square \)

              
                
Theorem 6.9

                
Conjecture 
6.3
 holds for the weight systems of isolated quasihomogeneous singularities of cycle type and of chain type. It holds for all isolated quasihomogeneous singularities in 
\(n=2\)
 variables. It holds for the isolated quasihomogeneous singularities in 
\(n=3\)
 variables which are of the types III, IV, V, VI and VII in example 3.2 (ii) in [
6
] (see Remark 
6.10
 for the types I and II).

                
In fact, in all these cases the set 
M
 of each elementary divisor satisfies even the stronger conditions in Remark 
6.6
 (ii).

              
                
Proof

                
First consider the cycle type. Recall Lemma 
4.1
, and especially formula (
4.5
) for 
\(D_\mathbf{w}\)
. It implies that all elementary divisors except one have the divisor 
\(\Lambda _{d/\gamma }\)
, and the last one has the divisor 
\(\Lambda _{d/\gamma }-\Lambda _1\)
 if 
n
 is odd, and it has the divisor 
\(\Lambda _1\)
 if 
n
 is even. These divisors satisfy by Lemma 
6.8
 the conditions in Remark 
6.6
 (ii).

                
Next consider the chain type. Recall corollary 
4.3
 and especially formula (
4.17
) for 
\(D_\mathbf{w}\)
. It implies that any elementary divisor satisfies the conditions in Lemma 
6.8
. Therefore it satisfies the conditions in Remark 
6.6
 (ii).

                
Now consider the case 
\(n=2\)
. By example 3.2 (i) in [
6
], there are three types. Type III is a cycle type. Type II is a chain type. They are treated above. Type I is the tensor product of two 
A
-type singularities, it is called Fermat type. In general, the tensor product is difficult to deal with, but this case is fairly easy. Here the weights are 
\((w_1,w_2)=(\frac{1}{t_1},\frac{1}{t_2})\)
, and 
\(D_\mathbf{w}\)
 is
$$\begin{aligned} D_\mathbf{w}= & {} (\Lambda _{t_1}-\Lambda _1)(\Lambda _{t_2}-\Lambda _1)\\= & {} \gcd (t_1,t_2)\Lambda _{\mathrm{lcm}(t_1,t_2)}-\Lambda _{t_1} -\Lambda _{t_2}+\Lambda _1. \end{aligned}$$
The elementary divisors are as follows.
$$\begin{aligned} \text {For }k\le \gcd (t_1,t_2)-2:&\mathrm{div\, }g_k=\Lambda _{\mathrm{lcm}(t_1,t_2)},\\ \text {for }k= \gcd (t_1,t_2)-1:&\mathrm{div\, }g_k=\Lambda _{\mathrm{lcm}(t_1,t_2)} -\Lambda _{\gcd (t_1,t_2)}+\Lambda _1,\\ \text {for }k= \gcd (t_1,t_2):&\mathrm{div\, }g_k=\Lambda _{\mathrm{lcm}(t_1,t_2)} -\Lambda _{t_1}-\Lambda _{t_2}+\Lambda _{\gcd (t_1,t_2)}. \end{aligned}$$
The divisors in the first two cases satisfy the conditions in Lemma 
6.8
 and therefore the conditions in Remark 
6.6
 (ii).

                
Consider the divisor 
\(\mathrm{div\, }g_k\)
 in the third case. Suppose that 
 and 
, because else 
\(\mathrm{div\, }g_k=0\)
. The set 
\(M\subseteq {\mathbb {N}}\)
 with 
\(\mathrm{div\, }g_k=\sum _{m\in M}\Psi _m\)
 is
Obviously, its graph is a directed graph with root 
\(\mathrm{lcm}(t_1,t_2)\)
. This gives the first condition in Remark 
6.6
 (ii). For the second condition, we distinguish the following two cases. Write 
\({\widetilde{t}}_j=2^{-m(t_j,2)}\cdot t_j\)
, so that 
\(t_j=2^{m(t_j,2)}\cdot {\widetilde{t}}_j\)
. Suppose 
\(m(t_1,2)\ge m(t_2,2)\)
. Then 
 and 
\(\mathrm{lcm}(t_1,t_2)=2^{m(t_1,2)}\cdot \mathrm{lcm}({\widetilde{t}}_1,{\widetilde{t}}_2)\)
.

                
1st case, 
: Then the set 
\(\{2^r\cdot \mathrm{lcm}({\widetilde{t}}_1,{\widetilde{t}}_2)\, |\, 0\le r\le m(t_1,2)\}\)
 is a subset of 
M
 and is a chain of 2-edges which connects all 2-planes.

                
2nd case, 
\({\widetilde{t}}_1|{\widetilde{t}}_2\)
: Then 
\(m(t_1,2)>m(t_2,2)\)
. Then the set 
\(\{2^r\cdot {\widetilde{t}}_2\, |\, m(t_2,2)+1\le r\le m(t_1,2)\}\)
 is a subset of 
M
 and is a chain of 2-edges which connects all 2-planes.

                
Now consider the case 
\(n=3\)
. By example 3.2 (ii) in [
6
], there are seven types. Type V is a chain type, and type VII is a cycle type. They are treated above. The types III, IV and VI will be treated in a similar way as the type I for 
\(n=2\)
.

                
Type III for 
\(n=3\)
: weights 
\(\mathbf{w}=(\frac{1}{t_1},\frac{s_2}{t_2},\frac{s_3}{t_3})\)
 with
$$\begin{aligned} w_j=\frac{1-w_1}{a_j}, \quad t_j=t_1\cdot \alpha _j\text { with }\alpha _j=\frac{a_j}{\gcd (a_j,t_1-1)} \quad \text {for }j=2,3, \end{aligned}$$
for some 
\(a_2,a_3\in {\mathbb {N}}\)
. Write 
\({\widetilde{\alpha }}:=\mathrm{lcm}(\alpha _2,\alpha _3)\)
. Then
$$\begin{aligned} D_\mathbf{w}= & {} \left( \frac{1}{s_1}\Lambda _{t_1}-\Lambda _1\right) \left( \frac{1}{s_2}\Lambda _{t_2}-\Lambda _1\right) \left( \frac{1}{s_3}\Lambda _{t_3}-\Lambda _1\right) \\= & {} (\Lambda _{t_1}-\Lambda _1) \left( \frac{t_1\cdot \gcd (\alpha _2,\alpha _3)}{s_2\cdot s_3}\Lambda _{t_1{\widetilde{\alpha }}} -\frac{1}{s_2}\Lambda _{t_2}-\frac{1}{s_3}\Lambda _{t_3}+\Lambda _1\right) \\= & {} r_1\Lambda _{t_1{\widetilde{\alpha }}}-r_2\Lambda _{t_2}-r_3\Lambda _{t_3} +\Lambda _{t_1}-\Lambda _1\\ \text {with} \ r_1= & {} \frac{t_1(t_1-1)\gcd (\alpha _2,\alpha _3)}{s_2s_3},\quad r_2=\frac{t_1-1}{s_2},\quad r_3=\frac{t_1-1}{s_3}. \end{aligned}$$
Suppose (without loss of generality) that 
\(r_2\le r_3\)
. The elementary divisors 
\(g_k\)
 are as follows:
$$\begin{aligned} \text {For }1\le k\le r_1-r_2-r_3:&\mathrm{div\, }g_k =\Lambda _{t_1{\widetilde{\alpha }}},\\ \text {for }k=r_1-r_2-r_3+1:&\mathrm{div\, }g_k = \Lambda _{t_1{\widetilde{\alpha }}} -\Lambda _{t_1\gcd (\alpha _2,\alpha _3)}\\& + \Lambda _{t_1} - \Lambda _1,\\ \text {for }r_1-r_2-r_3+2\le k\le r_1-r_3:&\mathrm{div\, }g_k = \Lambda _{t_1{\widetilde{\alpha }}} - \Lambda _{t_1\gcd (\alpha _2,\alpha _3)},\\ \text {for }r_1-r_3+1\le k\le r_1-r_2:&\mathrm{div\, }g_k = \Lambda _{t_1{\widetilde{\alpha }}} - \Lambda _{t_3},\\ \text {for }r_1-r_2+1\le k \le r_1:&\mathrm{div\, }g_k = \Lambda _{t_1{\widetilde{\alpha }}} -\Lambda _{t_2} - \Lambda _{t_3}\\& + \Lambda _{t_1\gcd (\alpha _2,\alpha _3)}. \end{aligned}$$
The divisors in the first four cases satisfy the conditions in Lemma 
6.8
 and therefore the conditions in Remark 
6.6
 (ii). The divisors in the fifth case are of the same type as the divisor in the third case in type I for 
\(n=2\)
.

                
Type IV for 
\(n=3\)
 is a sum of a 1 variable Fermat type and a 2 variable cycle type. The weights are 
\(\mathbf{w}=(\frac{1}{t_1},\frac{s_2}{t_2},\frac{s_3}{t_3})\)
 with
$$\begin{aligned} \gamma =\gcd (a_2-1,a_2a_3-1)=\gcd (a_3-1,a_2a_3-1),\\ t_2=t_3=\frac{a_2a_3-1}{\gamma },\quad s_2=\frac{a_3-1}{\gamma },\quad s_3=\frac{a_2-1}{\gamma } \end{aligned}$$
for some 
\(a_2,a_3\in {\mathbb {N}}_{\ge 2}\)
, by Lemma 
4.1
. Write 
\({\widetilde{t}}:=\gcd (t_1,t_2)\)
. Again by Lemma 
4.1
, 
\(D_\mathbf{w}\)
 is the product
$$\begin{aligned} D_\mathbf{w}= & {} (\Lambda _{t_1}-\Lambda _1)(\gamma \Lambda _{t_2}+\Lambda _1)\\= & {} \gamma {\widetilde{t}}\Lambda _{\mathrm{lcm}(t_1,t_2)}-\gamma \Lambda _{t_2}+\Lambda _{t_1} -\Lambda _1. \end{aligned}$$
The elementary divisors 
\(g_k\)
 are as follows:
$$\begin{aligned} \text {For }1\le k\le \gamma ({\widetilde{t}}-1):&\mathrm{div\, }g_k =\Lambda _{\mathrm{lcm}(t_1,t_2)},\\ \text {for }k=\gamma ({\widetilde{t}}-1)+1:&\mathrm{div\, }g_k =\Lambda _{\mathrm{lcm}(t_1,t_2)} - \Lambda _{t_2}+\Lambda _{{\widetilde{t}}} -\Lambda _1,\\ \text {for }\gamma ({\widetilde{t}}-1)+2\le k\le \gamma {\widetilde{t}}:&\mathrm{div\, }g_k = \Lambda _{\mathrm{lcm}(t_1,t_2)} - \Lambda _{t_2},\\ \text {for } k= \gamma {\widetilde{t}}+1:&\mathrm{div\, }g_k = \Lambda _{t_1} - \Lambda _{{\widetilde{t}}}. \end{aligned}$$
All these divisors satisfy the conditions in Lemma 
6.8
 and therefore the conditions in Remark 
6.6
 (ii).

                
Type VI for 
\(n=3\)
 consists of a cycle such that one of its vertices is the root of a 2 variable chain. The weights are 
\(\mathbf{w}=(\frac{s_1}{t_1},\frac{s_2}{t_2},\frac{s_3}{t_3})\)
 with
$$\begin{aligned} \gamma= & {} \gcd (a_2-1,a_1a_2-1)=\gcd (a_1-1,a_1a_2-1),\ t_1=t_2=\frac{a_1a_2-1}{\gamma },\\ t_3= & {} t_1\cdot \alpha \text { for some }\alpha \in {\mathbb {N}}, \ s_1=\frac{a_2-1}{\gamma },\ s_2=\frac{a_1-1}{\gamma }, \end{aligned}$$
for some 
\(a_1,a_2\in {\mathbb {N}}_{\ge 2}\)
. By Lemma 
4.1
, 
\(D_\mathbf{w}\)
 is the product
$$\begin{aligned} D_\mathbf{w}= & {} (\gamma \Lambda _{t_1}+\Lambda _1) \left( \frac{1}{s_3}\Lambda _{t_3}-\Lambda _1\right) \\= & {} r\Lambda _{t_3}-\gamma \Lambda _{t_1}-\Lambda _1 \quad \text {with}\quad r=\frac{\gamma t_1+1}{s_3}. \end{aligned}$$
Observe 
\(r\ge \gamma +1\)
, because 
\(r-\gamma -1\)
 is the coefficient of [1] in 
\(D_\mathbf{w}\)
. The elementary divisors 
\(g_k\)
 are as follows:
$$\begin{aligned} \text {For }1\le k\le r-\gamma -1:&\mathrm{div\, }g_k =\Lambda _{t_3},\\ \text {for }k=r-\gamma :&\mathrm{div\, }g_k =\Lambda _{t_3} -\Lambda _1,\\ \text {for }r-\gamma +1\le k\le r:&\mathrm{div\, }g_k = \Lambda _{t_3} - \Lambda _{t_1}. \end{aligned}$$
All these divisors satisfy the conditions in Lemma 
6.8
 and therefore the conditions in Remark 
6.6
 (ii). 
\(\square \)

              
                
Remark 6.10

                
In example 3.2 (ii) in [
6
], i.e., for 
\(n=3\)
, type I is the Fermat type with 
\(\mathbf{w}=(\frac{1}{t_1},\frac{1}{t_2},\frac{1}{t_3}\)
), and type II is the sum of a 1 variable Fermat type and 2 variable chain type, so 
\(\mathbf{w}=(\frac{1}{t_1},\frac{1}{t_2}, \frac{s_3}{t_3})\)
 with 
\(\frac{s_3}{t_3}=\frac{1-w_2}{a_3}\)
 and 
\(a_3\ge 2\)
. In both cases, a similar ansatz as in the Proof of Theorem 
6.9
 leads to an unpleasant multitude of different subcases. Examples 
6.7
 (i)+(ii) show that in special cases of both types, some elementary divisor does not satisfy the conditions in Remark 
6.6
 (ii). It does not seem worth to try to prove Conjecture 
6.3
 in this way.
