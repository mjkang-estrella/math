295: 1.1. Overview
296: 
297: 
298: 
299: 
300: Higher dimensional partitions are classical combinatorial objects introduced by MacMahon over a century ago [32, 33]. For  n,d\in{\mdmathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0} , an  (n-1) -dimensional partition of size  d  can be graphically visualised as an arrangement of  d  boxes in  {\mdmathbb{R}}^{n} . Let  p_{d}^{n}  be the number of such partitions. Based on small experimental data, MacMahon conjectured the elegant formula
301: 
302: 
303: 
304: (1.1)
305: 
306:  \sum_{d\geqslant 0}p_{d}^{n}t^{d}=\prod_{m>0}\left(1-t^{m}\right)^{-\binom{m+n-3}{n-2}}, 
307: 
308: 
309: 
310: which does hold for  n=2,3 . The case  n=2  was known to Euler, whereas for the case  n=3  we refer to MacMahon’s original work [32, 33].
311: The identity (1.1) is, however, only true modulo  t^{6}  as long as  n\geqslant 4  [2]. Computing the numbers  p_{d}^{n}  for  n\geqslant 4  is a major unsolved problem in Combinatorics; quoting the words of Stanley, “almost nothing significant is known” [47, Sec. 7.20, p. 365]. While it was recently proved that the right-hand side of (1.1) enumerates higher dimensional partitions of fixed corner-hook volume [1], a closed formula for its left-hand side is currently out of reach. See Section 6.1 for more on this matter.
312: 
313: 
314: 
315: 
316: It is worth stressing that, despite the validity of Euler’s formula, no closed (nonrecursive) formula is known for  p_{d}^{2} , let alone for  p_{d}^{n} . This problem originally motivated Hardy and Ramanujan, over a century ago, to look for asymptotic formulas for  p_{d}^{2} . In [27] they proved that  \log\,p_{d}^{2}\approx\pi\sqrt{2d/3} . A short while later, they improved this estimate with analytic methods, see [29, 28]. In more recent years it was confirmed that the asymptotic growth of  p_{d}^{n}  is exponentially fast for higher  n  as well [5, 40, 43]; more precisely, one has
317: 
318: 
319: 
320: 
321:  \displaystyle\log\,p_{d}^{n}\approx d^{\frac{n-1}{n}}. 
322: 
323: 
324: 
325: This makes the direct computation of  p_{d}^{n}  cumbersome and, in practice, unfeasible; for instance, the exact values of  p_{d}^{4}  are only known for  d\leqslant 72 , and for a general  n\geqslant 1  the numbers  p_{d}^{n}  were computed for  d\leqslant 26  [16, 17].
326: 
327: 
328: 
329: 
330: In this paper we are interested in the exact values of the numbers  p^{\bullet}_{\bullet} , rather than their asymptotics. Motivated by Govindarajan’s work [17] and by our previous paper [20], we study the distribution of the numbers  p_{d}^{\bullet}  where we fix the size  d  and allow the dimension to grow indefinitely. This means that, instead of working with the series (1.1), we switch perspective and deal with the (rational) generating function
331: 
332: 
333: 
334: (1.2)
335: 
448: 
449: 
450: 
451: It is worth mentioning that [2] introduced some refined numbers  \overline{y}_{\bullet}^{\bullet} , formally extracted by MacMahon’s conjectural formula (1.1) analogously to (1.4) (cf. Section 6) and conjectured the positivity of the discrepancy  e^{k}_{d}=\overline{y}_{d}^{k}-y_{d}^{k} . We propose a conjectural rational formula
452: for the generating series of the numbers  \overline{y}_{\bullet}^{\bullet}  (cf. Conjecture 6.2) and prove that implies the positivity of the discrepancy  e^{k}_{d}  for  d\gg 0  (cf. Proposition 6.5).

457: 1.2.2. No socle in degree 1
458: 
459: 
460: 
461: 
462: Similarly to (1.4), it was already noticed in [17, 20] that the numbers  y^{\bullet}_{\bullet}  admit further refinements via
463: 
464: 
465: 
466: 
467:  \displaystyle y_{k+e+1}^{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{2e}\binom{k}{j}c_{e}^{j}, 
468: 
469: 
470: 
471: where  c_{e}^{j}  is the number of partitions of size  1+j+e , embedding dimension  j  and no socle in degree 1, where the socle of a partition is the collection of its maximal elements according to the standard poset structure (see Equation 2.1 for the precise definition).
573: 
574: 
575: 
576: Excluding from the counting all the higher dimensional partitions with socle in degree 2 dramatically reduces the computational complexity of  p^{\bullet}_{\bullet} . In fact, M-partitions enjoy particularly nice features; for instance, their embedding dimension is always bounded by their number of quadrics, cf. Proposition 4.2. This allowed us to find exact formulas for their generating function in many cases, as we outline in Section 1.3.
577: 
578: 
579: 
580: 
581: In Section 5.5, we devise an algorithmic strategy for computing the numbers  \alpha^{\bullet}_{\bullet,\bullet}  in the general case, which we have implemented in Sage [48], Mathematica [30] and Macaulay2 [21] to collect a large amount of data.
582: 
583: 
584: 
585: 
586: Combining the recursion of Proposition 1.1 with the main Theorems A and B, the exact generating functions in Section 5 and the algorithmic strategy presented in Section 5.5, we were able to compute the number  p_{d}^{n}  of higher dimensional dimensional partitions for all  n\geqslant 1  and  d\leqslant 30 . We remark that already the number  p^{n}_{27}  would be computationally out of reach for  n\geqslant 7  using only the algorithms currently present in the literature, see e.g. [17, Sec. 4.3].
587: 
588: 
589: 
590: 
591: It is worth mentioning that our socle reduction principle could ideally be extended to reduce the general partition enumeration problem to the enumeration problem of partitions with arbitrary lower bound for the socle degree. However, already in the case of constraining the socle to be in degree at least 4, finding a recursive structure analogous to the one of Proposition 1.1 is a highly nontrivial problem, which we leave to future exploration.

597: 1.3. Counting M-partitions
598: 
599:

601: 
602: We introduce in Definition 5.8 a special kind of M-partitions, that we call hydral partitions. These are  (n-1) -dimensional partitions  \lambda  with socle in degree at least 3, and such that  h_{\lambda}(1)=h_{\lambda}(2)=n , i.e. with minimal number of quadrics. They are counted by the integers  \alpha^{n}_{n,\bullet} . The following theorem describes their count in arbitrary dimension, see also Propositions 5.6 and 5.9 for a direct calculation of their generating function in the case  n=2  and  n=3  respectively, and Example 5.11 for the case  n=4 .
