                                                                                         Abstract
                                                    We present two related conjectures, arising in work on i-matchings in random r-regular
                                                bipartite graphs. The conjectures themselves are easily stated and involve only basic proper-
                                                ties of convergent power series. One formulation involves Bell’s polynomials. The conjectures
                                                name was chosen since we earnestly believe only a truly genius mathematician will prove
                                                them. We advise others not to try. A further belief is that the proof will arise from some
                                                deep properties of partitions.
                                               We write the paper in a form so that the conjectures are reached as early as possible. Thus
                                          Section 1 presents the mathematical setting of the conjectures, and Section 2 contains the con-
                                          jectures themselves. One need read only these two sections to see the task before one. Section 3
                                          presents a reformulation of the setting using Bell’s polynomials [1]. This section is entirely the
                                          work of my colleague David Williams. One may only read Section 3 and then Section 2 to arrive
                                          at a full treatment of the conjectures using Bell polynomials.
                                               The final section, Section 4, on background provides the place of the conjectures in the
                                          development of mathematics, how they arose, and what follows from their truth. Thus, this
                                          section contains the contents of usual introductions and conclusions. I only say now they arose
                                          from certain equations of Mario Pernici [2] in his treatment of work of Ian Wanless on i-matchings,
                                          [3].

61                                    2. Nested colourings
62   In this section, we introduce three new concepts: nested colourings, the de-duplicate graph,
63 and the weak duplicate preorder.
64 2.1. Nested colourings & the nested chromatic number.
65   We first define a nested neighbourhood condition on vertices of a finite simple graph.
66 We use NG (u) = {v : {u, v} ∈ E(G)} to denote the open neighbourhood of u in G and
67 NG [u] = NG (u) ·∪ {u} to denote the closed neighbourhood of u in G.
68 Definition 2.1. Let G be a finite simple graph, and let u, v be vertices of G. The vertex u is
69 a weak duplicate of v if NG (u) ⊂ NG (v); if equality holds, then u is a duplicate of v. Further,
70 a duplicate-free graph is a finite simple graph for which no pair of vertices are duplicates.
71 An independent set I of G is nested if the vertices of I can be linearly ordered so that v ≤ u
72 implies u is a weak duplicate of v.
73   The order on the vertices of a nested independent set is unique, up to permutations of
74 duplicates. This will be formalised in Section 2.3.
75   Using this condition on the neighbourhoods, we define a novel proper vertex colouring of
76 a graph.
77 Definition 2.2. Let G be a finite simple graph, and let C be a proper vertex k-colouring
78 C1 ·∪ · · · ·∪ Ck of G. If every colour class of C is nested, then C is a nested colouring of G.
79 The nested chromatic number χN (G) is the least cardinality of a nested colouring of G.
80 Moreover, the graph G is colour-nested if χN (G) = χ(G).

100 of every vertex of a graph. Thus isolated vertices can be put in to any colour class without

102   We also have a pair of immediate bounds on the nested chromatic number.
103 Remark 2.5. Since every nested colouring of a finite simple graph G is a proper colouring
104 of G, we clearly have χ(G) ≤ χN (G). Moreover, χN (G) ≤ #V (G) as the singleton colouring
105 ·∪ v∈V (G) {v} is nested.
106   Graphs with very small or very large chromatic number are colour-nested.
107 Lemma 2.6. Let G be a finite simple graph on n vertices. If χ(G) ∈ {1, n − 1, n}, where
108 n ≥ 2, then χN (G) = χ(G), i.e., G is colour-nested.
109 Proof. Suppose that χ(G) = 1. Hence E(G) = ∅ and every vertex is a duplicate of every
110 other vertex by Remark 2.4. Thus the set V (G) is a nested colouring of G and χN (G) = χ(G).
111   Suppose that χ(G) = n − 1, where n ≥ 2. Thus G is Kn with a nonempty subset of the
112 edges connected to some vertex, say, v, removed. Let u be a vertex nonadjacent to v. Thus
113 NG (u) = V (G) \ {u, v} contains NG (v), and v is a weak duplicate of u. Hence {u, v} is a
114 nested independent set of G and so χN (G) ≤ n − 1. By the preceding remark we thus have
115 χN (G) = χ(G).
116   Suppose that χ(G) = n. By Remark 2.5 we have χ(G) = χN (G) = #V (G).                    

184 2.3. The weak duplicate preorder.
185    We next define a preorder on the vertices of a graph using the concept of weak duplicates.
186 It is particularly important to notice that the preorder is in the reverse order of containment.
187 Definition 2.14. Let G be a finite simple graph. The weak duplicate preorder on V (G) is
188 the preorder defined by v ≤ u if u is a weak duplicate of v.
189    Exchanging a vertex of a clique for a lesser vertex in the preorder generates another clique
190 of the graph.
191 Lemma 2.15. Let G be a finite simple graph, and let C be a clique of G. If u is a vertex of
192 C, and v ≤ u under the weak duplicate preorder on V (G), then (C ·∪ {v}) \ {u} is a clique
193 of G.
194 Proof. Since NG (u) ⊂ NG (v), C ⊂ NG (u) implies C ⊂ NG (v). Thus (C ∪· {v}) \ {u} is a
195 clique of G.                                                                         
196   This gives an alternate condition on a partition of the vertices that is equivalent to being
197 a nested colouring.
198 Proposition 2.16. Let G be a finite simple graph. If C = C1 ·∪ · · · ·∪ Ck is a partition of
199 V (G), then the following conditions are equivalent:
200      (i) C is nested;
201     (ii) there is an ordering on the vertices of each colour class Ci such that if v is less than
202          u in that order, and K is a clique of G containing u, then (K ∪· {v}) \ {u} is a clique
203          of G; and
204    (iii) there is an ordering on the vertices of each colour class Ci such that if v is less than
205          u in that order, and {u, w} is an edge of G, then {v, w} is an edge of G.
206 Proof. Suppose that condition (i) holds. Since each independent set Ci is nested, the vertices
207 of Ci are comparable under the weak duplicate preorder. If we arbitrarily order the duplicates
208 in Ci , then the induced order on Ci is the desired order for condition (ii) by Lemma 2.15.
209    Clearly, condition (ii) implies condition (iii), since edges are cliques of G.
210    Suppose now that condition (iii) holds. Since {u, w} ∈ E(G) implies that {v, w} ∈ E(G),
211 NG (u) is a subset of NG (v). That is, the order on the vertices of Ci respects the weak
212 duplicate preorder, and so Ci is nested. In particular, condition (i) holds.                

227    Dilworth [5, Theorem 1.1] proved that the width (or Dilworth number) of a poset P , i.e.,
228 the maximum cardinality of an antichain of P , is precisely the minimum cardinality of a
229 chain cover of P . Hence the nested chromatic number of a graph is the width of the poset
230 of the de-duplicate of the graph.
231 Corollary 2.19. If G is a finite simple graph, then χN (G) is the width of PG⋆ .
232 Remark 2.20. Let G be a finite simple graph. A vertex v of G dominates a vertex u of G
233 if NG (u) ⊂ NG [v]. Notice the subtle difference between domination and weak duplication,
234 namely, u and v may be adjacent in the former. The Dilworth number of G is the cardinality
235 of the largest set of vertices of G such that no vertex dominates any other in the set.
236    Following Felsner, Raghavan, and Spinrad [9], we partially order the vertices of a duplicate-
237 free graph G by v ≤ u if v dominates u. The width of this partial order is precisely the
238 Dilworth number of the graph G. This partial order is in the reverse order of containment,
239 as in the weak duplicate partial order.
240    The Dilworth number of a graph is not the nested chromatic number of the graph despite
241 the similarities. Recall that threshold graphs are precisely the graphs with Dilworth number
242 1. In Corollary 5.8 we classify the nested chromatic number of threshold graphs as one more
243 than the number of domination steps in the construction of the graph.
244     As a consequence, the nested chromatic number can be computed in polynomial time.
245 Theorem 2.21. The nested chromatic number of a finite simple graph on n vertices can be
246 computed in O(n3 ) time.
247 Proof. Fulkerson [10] proved that computing the width of a poset is equivalent to computing
248 the cardinality of a maximum matching of a related bipartite graph. Hopcroft and Karp [11]
249 proved that computing the latter can be done in O(n5/2 ) time.
250   Computing the relations between the n vertices corresponds to computing n2 subset
251                                                                                     
252 containments, where each subset has size at most O(n). Hence computing the poset structure
253 on PG⋆ takes O(n3 ) time. Thus computing the nested chromatic number of a finite simple
254 graph on n vertices via the width of the weak duplicate partial order takes O(n3 ) time. 

591 4.3. A topological remark.
592    Consider the homomorphism complex Hom(H, G) coming from the homomorphisms from
593 H to G, where G and H are finite simple graphs; see [16, Definition 3.2]. Kozlov showed [16,
594 Theorem 3.3] that Hom(H, G) collapses onto Hom(H, G − u) if u is a weak duplicate of some
595 other vertex v in G. Thus if C1 ∪· · · · ∪· Ck is a nested k-colouring of G, and G′ is an induced
596 subgraph of G with vertex set {v1 , . . . , vk }, where vi is a minimal element of Ci under the
597 weak duplicate preorder, then Hom(H, G) collapses onto Hom(H, G′ ), and so Hom(H, G)
598 and Hom(H, G′ ) have the same simple homotopy type.
599    This is particularly interesting as the neighbourhood complex of G, i.e., the simplicial
600 complex of subsets of V (G) which have a common neighbour, is homotopy equivalent to
601 Hom(K2 , G). Thus Lovász’s lower bound on the chromatic number [17, Theorem 2] can be
602 interpreted as conn Hom(K2 , G) ≤ χ(G)−3, where conn X is the connectivity of the complex
603 X. We note that this lower bound is strict in the case of Kneser graphs.
604    Hence we see that there exists an induced subgraph G′ of G on χN (G) vertices such that
605 Hom(K2 , G′) ≤ χ(G) − 3. Thus if χN (G) < #V (G), then G has more redundancy than
606 necessary for such topological bounds on the chromatic number to be useful. Indeed, it is
607 this redundant and recursive nature that is exploited in the associated algebras studied in [3].
608    Further, recall that the independence complex Ind(G) of a finite simple graph G is the
609 simplicial complex with faces given by the independent sets of G. Engström showed [6,
610 Lemma 3.2] that Ind(G) collapses onto Ind(G − v) if v is weakly duplicated by some other
611 vertex u. More generally, this implies that if C1 ·∪ · · · ·∪ Ck is a nested k-colouring of G, and
612 G′′ is an induced subgraph of G with vertex set {u1 , . . . , uk }, where ui is a maximal element
613 of Ci under the weak duplicate preorder, then Ind(G) collapses onto Ind(G′′ ), and so Ind(G)
614 and Ind(G′′ ) have the same simple homotopy type. Again, this emphasises the redundant
615 structure present in graphs with χN (G) < #V (G).
616    We note that the de-duplicate graph G⋆ of G can be constructed in both of these fashions.
617 In particular, let C1 ∪· · · · ∪· Ck be the nested k-colouring such that each colour-class is an
618 equivalence class of duplicates. Selecting an induced subgraph of G with precisely one
619 element from each colour class generates a graph isomorphic to G⋆ . Hence Hom(H, G) and
620 Hom(H, G⋆ ) have the same simple homotopy type, as do Ind(G) and Ind(G⋆ ).

810    Let L be the set of all colour classes of two vertices. Every nested colouring of G  H
811 consists of subset of L of pairwise disjoint elements together with singleton sets of the
812 remaining vertices. In particular, χN (G  H) is #V (G  H) minus the largest subset of L
813 that consists of pairwise disjoint elements.
814    Since the weak duplicate vertex of each element of L is unique, selection of a subset of L
815 of pairwise disjoint elements depends only on the weakly duplicated vertex of each element
816 of L. In particular, if (g, h) is the weak duplicate vertex of an element of L, then no other
817 element of L with weak duplicate vertex (i, j) such that [g]∼ = [i]∼ and [h]∼ = [j]∼ can be in
818 such a disjoint set. Thus the largest subset of L that consists of pairwise disjoint elements
819 is of size ℓ′ (G) · ℓ′ (H).                                                                 
820 Example 5.15. The cube graph Qn is defined recursively by Q1 = K2 and Qn = Qn−1  K2 ,
821 so Qn has no leaves for n ≥ 2. Hence χN (Qn ) = 2n if n 6= 2 and χN (Q2 ) = 2. This also
822 follows by Proposition 3.4 since Qn is n regular and duplicate-free for n 6= 2 by Corollary 5.13.
823 5.6. Strong product.
824   Let G and H be finite simple graphs. The strong product of G and H is the graph G ⊠ H
825 with vertex set V (G) × V (H), where (g, h) is adjacent to the distinct vertex (g ′, h′ ) if and
826 only if g = g ′ or g is adjacent to g ′ in G, and h = h′ and h is adjacent to h′ in H. In
827 particular, the open neighbourhood of (g, h) in G ⊠ H is
828                              NG⊠H (g, h) = NG [g] × NH [h] \ {(g, h)}.
829   Notice that G⊠K1 is isomorphic to G, so χN (G⊠K1 ) = χN (G). Moreover, (G ·∪ G′ )⊠H =
830 (G ⊠ H) ·∪ (G′ ⊠ H). Thus following Section 5.2, we need only consider finite simple graphs
831 G that are connected and have at least two vertices.
832   With the exception of G ⊠ K1 , the strong product of connected graphs has no weak
833 duplicate vertices.
834 Lemma 5.16. Let G and H be connected finite simple graphs, neither of which is K1 . The
835 vertices (g, h) and (g ′, h′ ) are weak duplicates in G ⊠ H if and only if (g, h) = (g ′ , h′ ).
836 Proof. Since G and H are connected, NG (g) 6= ∅ 6= NH (h).
837   Suppose (g, h) is a weak duplicate of (g ′ , h′ ). This implies that {g}×NH (h) ⊂ NG⊠H (g ′ , h′ ),
838 and so g ∈ NG [g ′], i.e., g ′ ∈ NG [g]. By symmetry, we also have h′ ∈ NH [h]. If (g, h) 6= (g ′ , h′ ),
839 then (g ′ , h′ ) ∈ NG⊠H (g, h) ⊂ NG⊠H (g ′ , h′ ), which is absurd.                                   

841   Thus the nested chromatic number of the strong product is the number of vertices of the
842 product.

848 5.7. Composition.
849   Let G and H be finite simple graphs. The composition (or lexicographic product) of G and
850 H is the graph G[H] with vertex set V (G) × V (H), where (g, h) is adjacent to (g ′ , h′ ) if and
851 only if either g is adjacent to g ′ in G or g = g ′ and h is adjacent to h′ in H. In particular,
852 the open neighbourhood of (g, h) in G[H] is
853                           NG[H] (g, h) = NG (g) × V (H) ·∪ {g} × NH (h).
854 Clearly, composition is non-commutative, in general.
855   The weak duplicates in the composition come from weak duplicates of the operands.
856 Lemma 5.18. Let G and H be finite simple graphs. The vertex (g, h) is a weak duplicate of
857 the distinct vertex (g ′ , h′ ) in G[H] if and only if either g = g ′ and h is a weak duplicate of h′
858 in H or g is a weak duplicate of g ′ in G and h is an isolated vertex in H.
859 Proof. Suppose g = g ′. This implies that (g, h) is a weak duplicate of (g ′, h′ ) if and only if
860 NH (h) ⊂ NH (h′ ), i.e., h is a weak duplicate of h′ in H.
861    Assume g 6= g ′ . Further suppose h is an isolated vertex in H. Thus NG[H] (g, h) =
862 NG (g) × V (H), and so (g, h) is a weak duplicate of (g ′, h′ ) if and only if NG (g) ⊂ NG (g ′ ),
863 i.e., g is a weak duplicate of g ′ in G.
864    Now suppose h is not an isolated vertex in H, and suppose (g, h) is a weak duplicate
865 of (g ′, h′ ). This implies that g is adjacent to g ′; hence {g ′} × V (H) ⊂ NG[H] (g ′, h′ ), i.e.,
866 V (H) ⊂ NH (h′ ), which is absurd.                                                               
867      From this we can derive conditions classifying which compositions are duplicate-free.
868 Corollary 5.19. Let G and H be finite simple graphs. The graph G[H] is duplicate-free if
869 and only if H is duplicate-free and either
870      (i) H has no isolated vertices, or
871     (ii) H has an isolated vertex and G is duplicate-free.
872 Proof. Let (g, h) and (g ′ , h′ ) be distinct vertices of G[H].
873    Suppose g = g ′ . By Lemma 5.18, (g, h) and (g ′, h′ ) are duplicates in G[H] if and only if h
874 and h′ are duplicates in H.
875    Now suppose g 6= g ′. By Lemma 5.18, (g, h) and (g ′ , h′ ) are duplicates in G[H] if and only
876 if h and h′ are isolated vertices in H and g and g ′ are duplicates in G.                      
877   Further, we can bound the nested chromatic number of a graph composition, and equality
878 holds when the secondary graph has no isolated vertices.
879 Proposition 5.20. If G and H are finite simple graphs, then
880                                    χN (G[H]) ≤ #V (G) · χN (H).
881      Moreover, equality holds if H has no isolated vertices.
882 Proof. Let C be a nested colouring C1 ∪· · · · ∪· Ck of H. For each g ∈ V (G) and for 1 ≤ i ≤ k,

886 G[H], then by Lemma 5.18 g1 = · · · = gt and {h1 , . . . , ht } forms a nested independent set of
887 H. Thus any nested colouring of G[H] is of the form described in the first paragraph, and
888 so χN (G[H]) = #V (G) · χN (H).                                                                       
