                                                                                                     Abstract
                                                     The theory of generalized locally Toeplitz (GLT) sequences is a powerful apparatus for computing the asymp-
                                                 totic spectral distribution of matrices An arising from numerical discretizations of differential equations. Indeed,
                                                 when the mesh fineness parameter n tends to infinity, these matrices An give rise to a sequence {An }n , which
                                                 often turns out to be a GLT sequence. In this paper, we extend the theory of GLT sequences in several directions:
                                                 we show that every GLT sequence enjoys a normal form, we identify the spectral symbol of every GLT sequence
                                                 formed by normal matrices, and we prove that, for every GLT sequence {An }n formed by normal matrices and
                                                 every continuous function f : C → C, the sequence {f (An )}n is again a GLT sequence whose spectral symbol
                                                 is f (κ), where κ is the spectral symbol of {An }n . In addition, using the theory of GLT sequences, we prove a
                                                 spectral distribution result for perturbed normal matrices.
                                                 Keywords: generalized locally Toeplitz sequences, spectral distribution, normal form, normal matrices and per-
                                                 turbed normal matrices, matrix functions
                                                 2010 MSC: 15B05, 15A18, 47B06, 47B15, 15A16

                                          of mesh refinement n → ∞. More precisely, it often turns out that, for a large class of test functions F ,

2     Overview of the theory of GLT sequences

2.1    Singular value and spectral distribution of a sequence of matrices
Let µk be the Lebesgue measure in Rk . Throughout this paper, all terminology from measure theory (such as

Remark 2.1. The informal meaning behind the spectral distribution (2.1) is the following [18, p. 46]: assuming

of the informal meaning given here.

2.2    Special matrix-sequences
In what follows, a matrix-sequence is a sequence of the form {An }n , where An ∈ Cn×n for every n. If A ∈ Cn×n
and 1 ≤ p ≤ ∞, we denote by kAkp the Schatten p-norm of A, i.e., the p-norm of the vector (σ1 (A), . . . , σn (A))
formed by the singular values of A. The Schatten 2-norm kAk2 coincides with the Frobenius norm. The Schatten
∞-norm kAk∞ coincides with the classical spectral (or Euclidean) norm and is preferably denoted by kAk. For
more on Schatten p-norms, see [9].

of Xn . Throughout this paper, we use the natural convention 1/∞ = 0.

Theorem 3.2 (spectral distribution of perturbed normal sequences). Let {Xn }n be a normal sequence
such that {Xn }n ∼λ f and let {Yn }n be a matrix-sequence. Suppose that one of the following conditions is met.
1. kYn kp = o(1) for some 1 ≤ p ≤ 2.
2. kYn kp = o(n2/p−1 ) for some 2 ≤ p ≤ ∞.
3. {Yn }n is zero-distributed and the matrices Xn + Yn are normal.
Then {Xn + Yn }n ∼λ f .
   The last two main results of this paper (Theorems 3.3 and 3.4) generalize properties GLT 1 and GLT 4 to the
case where the matrices An are normal.
Theorem 3.3 (spectral distribution of normal GLT sequences). If {An }n ∼GLT κ and the matrices An are
normal then {An }n ∼λ κ.
Theorem 3.4 (functions of normal GLT sequences). If {An }n ∼GLT κ and the matrices An are normal then
{f (An )}n ∼GLT f (κ) for every continuous function f : C → C.
    Note that f (An ) is normal whenever An is normal. Thus, under the hypotheses of Theorem 3.4, we have
{f (An )}n ∼λ f (κ) for every continuous function f : C → C by Theorem 3.3.

4.3     Proof of Theorem 3.3
Theorem 3.3 is a direct consequence of Theorems 3.1 and 3.2.
Proof of Theorem 3.3. Let {An }n ∼GLT κ and assume that the matrices An are normal. By Theorem 3.1, there
exist a unitary sequence {Qn }n and a zero-distributed sequence {Zn }n such that An = Qn Dn Q∗n + Zn , where
{Dn }n is a diagonal sequence and {Dn }n ∼λ κ. The thesis {An }n ∼λ κ now follows from Theorem 3.2 applied with
Xn = Qn Dn Q∗n and Yn = Zn .

4.4     Proof of Theorem 3.4

4.4.1     Matrix functions and matrix polynomials
We first recall the notion of matrix functions in the case of a diagonalizable matrix. For more details on matrix
functions, see [20].
Definition 4.3 (function of a diagonalizable matrix). Let A ∈ Cn×n be a diagonalizable matrix and let
f : Λ(A) → C be a function defined on the spectrum of A, denoted by Λ(A). We define f (A) as the unique matrix
in Cn×n such that f (A)u = f (λ)u whenever Au = λu.

Definition 4.4 (complex bivariate polynomial). A complex bivariate polynomial is a function p(x, y) : C → C
of the form
                                      p(x, y) = a(x, y) + i b(x, y),
where a(x, y), b(x, y) : R2 → R are real bivariate polynomials. Note that we write p(x, y) instead of p(z) for
convenience (x and y are the real part and the imaginary part of z, respectively). Note also that a complex
bivariate polynomial p(x, y) should not be confused with a polynomial q(z) of the variable z, i.e., a (analytic)
function of the form q(z) = a0 + a1 z + a2 z 2 + . . . + am z m .

Definition 4.5 (complex bivariate polynomial of commuting matrices). If p(x, y) : C → C is a complex
bivariate polynomial and A, B are commuting matrices in Cn×n , then we define p(A, B) as the matrix in Cn×n
obtained by replacing x with A and y with B in the expression p(x, y) (with the usual convention that a constant
α independent of x and y in the expression of p(x, y) must be interpreted as αx0 y 0 and replaced with αIn ). Note
that this definition is well-posed because A, B commute and so the resulting matrix p(A, B) does not depend on
the way in which we write the expression p(x, y).
    For example, if p(x, y) = 2 + yx + xy 2 + i(2xy + yx3 ) and A, B are commuting matrices in Cn×n , then p(A, B) =
2In + BA + AB 2 + i(2AB + BA3 ). If we write p(x, y) = 2 + xy + xy 2 + 2 + i(2xy + x3 y), the resulting matrix
p(A, B) = 2In + AB + AB 2 + i(2AB + A3 B) is the same because A and B commute. In what follows, whenever
f (x, y) : C → C is a complex function written as f (x, y) instead of f (z), it is understood that a writing such as
f (z0 ), with a unique argument z0 , must be interpreted as the complex number obtained by replacing x with Re z0
and y with Im z0 in the expression f (x, y).
