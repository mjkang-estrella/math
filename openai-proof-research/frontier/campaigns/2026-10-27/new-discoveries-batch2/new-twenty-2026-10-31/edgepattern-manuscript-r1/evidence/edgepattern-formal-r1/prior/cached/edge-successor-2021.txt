                                                                                      1. Introduction
                                              Given a graph G, a list assignment of G is a map L : V (G) → 2N , and a proper L-colouring
                                           c of G is a map c : V (G) → N such that c(v) ∈ L(v) for every vertex v ∈ V (G), and c(u) 6= c(v)
                                           for every edge uv ∈ E(G). If there exists an L-colouring of G, we say that G is L-colourable.
                                           The list chromatic number of G, denoted χℓ (G), is the minimum k such that G is L-colourable
                                           for every list assignment L with |L(v)| ≥ k for every vertex v ∈ V (G).
                                              In a seminal result, Johansson [11] showed that the list chromatic number of triangle-free
                                           graphs of maximum degree ∆ never exceeds O(∆/ ln ∆) as ∆ → ∞. Two decades later,
                                           Molloy [12] showed with the help of entropy compression that this bound can be tightened
                                           to (1 + o(1))∆/ ln ∆ as ∆ → ∞; Bernshteyn [4] then showed that it was possible to replace
                                           the use of entropy compression with an application of the lopsided Lovász Local Lemma and
                                           obtain a similar result. Following that breakthrough, there has been a lot of interest for
                                           triangle-free graph colourings and extensions [1, 3, 6, 7, 8].
                                              Although quite different, all the proofs in those works rely on a 2-step colouring procedure.
                                           The first step is the one that varies the most among those proofs; given a graph G and a
                                           list assignment L, it consists in constructing a (pseudo-)random partial L-colouring c0 of
                                           G. In the work of Johansson [11], that partial colouring was obtained with an iterative
                                           colouring procedure and analysed with a nibble argument, while more recent proofs rely on
                                           a more straightforward construction of that partial colouring; for instance Bernshteyn picks
                                           a partial colouring uniformly at random in his proof [4]. After that first step, the set V1 of
                                           uncoloured vertices induces a new list assignment L1 : V1 → 2N . After showing that with
                                           positive probability, the maximum colour degree in that instance is sufficiently smaller than
                                           the minimum list size, the second step consists in choosing simultaneously one colour at
                                           random from L1 (v) for each uncoloured vertex v ∈ V1 ; this returns a proper L1 -colouring of
                                           G[V1 ], and hence a proper L-colouring of G, with positive probability as can be shown using
                                           the Lovász Local Lemma. This second step is usually called the Finishing Blow, and follows
                                           from a result of Haxell [10].

  It has been observed in [7] that there exist graphs satisfying the hypothesis of Corollary 1
and with chromatic number ( 21 − o(1))∆/ ln min{f, ∆}. So the bound in Corollary 1 is sharp
up to an asymptotic factor 2. Reducing this gap would constitute a breakthrough in Ram-
sey theory, as this would imply an improvement of the estimate of the off-diagonal Ramsey
numbers R(3, t) for t ∈ N, a long-standing open problem.
   In Section 2, we prove Theorem 1. Our proof relies on the stronger induction hypothesis
that given a uniform random colouring of any induced subgraph H of G, the expected number
of colours available for a vertex v ∈ V (G) \ V (H) is large enough in expectation. This is done
using only the first moment method. We were inspired by a counting argument which was
first used in the context of graph colouring by Rosenfeld [14] (see [17, Section 3.5] for an
introduction to the method), and later devised more generally for hypergraph colouring by
Wanless and Wood [16]. We note however that our use of that counting argument does not
follow the usual guidelines, as we do not introduce any explicit bad events that our colourings
should avoid.
   We note that our result also holds in the context of DP-colouring, an extension of list
colouring introduced by Dvořák and Postle [9]. We have decided to state those results in the
context of list colouring in order to avoid the verbose formalism of DP-colouring, but our
proofs adapt readily to that context.

                                         4. Discussion
4.1. Simplicity of the proof. A classical intuition behind the result of Johansson is the
following. Consider any vertex v ∈ V (G) in a triangle-free graph G, and assume that we
properly colour its neighbourhood randomly. Since N (v) is empty, the colours drawn by
each vertex in N (v) are independent random variables. The solution to the Coupon Collector
Problem hence tells us that if v has k colours (initially) available and degree less than Ω(k ln k),
then with high probability some colours from L(v) do not appear in N (v). This shows
that a random colouring “works” locally. We believe that our proof closely approximates
this intuition. Following Molloy [12], both Bernshteyn [4] and ourselves have relied on the
                                   1
approximation of (1−1/t) by e− t−1 , where t is the order of some list in N (v) given a colouring
of the vertices outside N (v). This allows us to fruitfully exploit the convexity of the latter
and assume that list sizes are evenly distributed across the colourings from which we sample.
However, this approximation is only possible if t > 1 and is wasteful if t is small. Thus
previous proofs introduced a blank colour (which lets them replace t with t + 1) and showed
that however an adversary coloured G \ N (v), a random colouring of N (v) left many colours
available at v with high probability. In particular this required showing that, having fixed any
colouring of G \ N (v), the appearance of colours x and y in N (v) were negatively correlated.
We instead require t to be large enough to ensure the convex approximation is good and we
banish the adversary by sampling uniformly from the set of colourings of G\v (which typically
induce large lists by induction and (1)). Thus the “support” of the convex approximation,
and its power, is enlarged to all colourings of G \ v rather than than just the extensions of a
particular colouring c0 of G \ N (v). Of course, sampling from all colourings simultaneously
comes at a cost... the appearance of colours in N (v) becomes tangled in a complex knot of
correlations. Crucially, we do not attempt to untangle this knot and instead slice through
it by focusing only on the first moment. We believe that our technique could be adapted in
other contexts where complex correlations obstruct intuitive arguments. Can one systemically
replace some family of Rödl nibble arguments using our technique? An affirmative answer
would be extremely exciting.
4.2. A possible tightening of the bound. In a previous version of our work, we have
claimed that the events x ∈ Lc (v) for every colour x ∈ L(v), given a vertex v ∈ V (H) and
8                                         E. HURLEY AND F. PIROT


a uniform random colouring c ∈ C (H), are negatively correlated. As was pointed out to us
by Anton Bernshteyn, this claim is false, although it holds once a colouring of H \ N [v] is
fixed. If one could prove that ℓc (v) is highly concentrated around its mean, then this would
imply a tightening of the bound in Theorem 1. However, it is not clear whether such a high
concentration holds for a uniform random colouring c ∈ C (H).
