20:                                                                                       Abstract
21:                                                      In a graph G, the cardinality of the smallest ordered set of vertices that distin-
22:                                                  guishes every element of V (G) ∪ E(G) is called the mixed metric dimension of G. In
23:                                                  this paper we first establish the exact value of the mixed metric dimension of a uni-
24:                                                  cycic graph G which is derived from the structure of G. We further consider graphs
25:                                                  G with edge disjoint cycles in which a unicyclic restriction Gi is introduced for each
26:                                                  cycle Ci . Applying the result for unicyclic graph to each Gi then yields the exact
27:                                                  value of the mixed metric dimension of such a graph G. The obtained formulas for
28:                                                  the exact value of the mixed metric dimension yield a simple sharp upper bound
29:                                                  on the mixed metric dimension, and we conclude the paper conjecturing that the
30:                                                  analogous bound holds for general graphs with prescribed cyclomatic number.

33:                                            1    Introduction
34:                                            The distance between vertices u and v from a graph G will be denoted by dG (u, v) (or
35:                                            simply d(u, v) if no confusion arises). If there is a vertex s ∈ V (G) such that dG (u, s) 6=
36:                                            dG (v, s), then we say that s distinguishes (or resolves) u and v. If any two vertices u and
37:                                            v from G are distinguished by at least one vertex of a subset S ⊆ V (G), then we say
38:                                            that S is a metric generator for G. The cardinality of the smallest metric generator is
39:                                            called the metric dimension of G, and it is denoted by dim(G). This notion for graphs
40:                                            was independently introduced by [3] and [12], under the names resolving sets and locating
41:                                            sets, respectively. Even before this notion was introduced for the realm of metric spaces
42:                                            [1].
43:                                                 The concept of metric dimension was recently extended from resolving vertices to
44:                                            resolving edges of a graph by Kelenc, Tratnik and Yero [5]. Similarly as above, a vertex
45:                                            s ∈ V (G) distinguishes two edges e, f ∈ E(G) if dG (s, e) 6= dG (s, f ), where dG (e, s) =

49: dG (uv, s) = min{d(u, s), d(v, s)}. A set of vertices S ⊆ V (G) is an edge metric generator
50: for G, if any two edges of G are distinguished by a vertex of S. The cardinality of the
51: smallest edge metric generator is called the edge metric dimension of G, and it is denoted
52: by edim(G). This variation of metric dimension also attracted interest (see [9], [11], [14],
53: [15]).
54:     Very recently the mixed metric dimension was introduced by Kelenc, Kuziak, Tara-
55: nenko, and Yero [6]. Here S is a mixed metric generator, if it distinguishes any two
56: elements from V (G) ∪ E(G), and similarly as above, the size of the smallest such a set S
57: is the mixed metric dimension of G, and it is denoted by mdim(G). The paper contains
58: lower and upper bounds for various graph classes. For a wider and systematic introduc-
59: tion of the topic metric dimension that encapsulates all three above mentioned variations,
60: we recommend the PhD thesis of Kelenc [4].
61:     In this paper, after this introductory section, we will first provide the necessary def-
62: initions and notation in the next section. In the third section the exact value of the
63: mixed metric dimension for unicyclic graphs is obtained, while in the fourth section that
64: result is extended to graphs with more cycles than one, but which cycles are edge disjoint.
65: We conclude the paper with the fifth section in which concluding remarks and possible
66: directions for further research are given.

69: 2    Preliminaries
70: In a graph G, let n denote the number of vertices and m the number of edges. The degree
71: of a vertex v ∈ V (G) is defined as the number of neighbors of v in G and denoted by
72: deg(v). We say that a vertex v ∈ V (G) is a leaf if deg(v) = 1. By L1 (G) we denote the
73: number of leaves in G.
74:     A graph G with edge disjoint cycles is called a cactus. A cactus with only one cycle
75: is also called a unicyclic graph. Let G be a cactus, let C be a cycle in G and let v be a
76: vertex from C. By Tv we will denote the connected component of G − E(C) containing
77: the vertex v. If G is a unicyclic graph then Tv is a tree for every v ∈ V (C), but if G
78: has more than one cycle then Tv need not be a tree, i.e. it may contain cycles. We say
79: that a vertex v from a cycle C is a root vertex on C if Tv is nontrivial, otherwise we say
80: that v is a non-root vertex. The number of all root vertices on a cycle C is denoted by
81: rt(C). Obviously, every vertex on a cycle of the degree ≥ 3 is a root vertex, while all
82: vertices on a cycle which are of degree 2 are non-root vertices. For three vertices u, v
83: and w from a cycle C in a cactus G we say that they form a geodesic triple of vertices if
84: d(u, v) + d(v, w) + d(w, u) = |V (C)| .
85:     The cyclomatic number c(G) of a graph G is defined by c(G) = m − n + 1. Observe
86: that for a cactus graph G the cyclomatic number c(G) equals the number of cycles in G.
87: The (vertex) connectivity κ(G) of a graph G is the minimum size of a vertex cut, i.e. any
88: subset of vertices S ⊆ V (G) such that G − S is disconnected or has only one vertex. We
89: say that a graph G is k-connected if κ(G) ≥ k. Notice that for a connected graph G which
90: has a leaf it obviously holds that κ(G) = 1. Also, notice that for every cactus graph G
91: with at least two cycles it holds that κ(G) = 1, even if G does not contain a leaf.
92:     Let S ⊆ V (G) be a set of vertices in a cactus graph G. We say that a vertex v from a

98: of S-active vertices on C. Any shortest path between two vertices from S is called a
99: S-closed path. Let x and x0 be a pair of elements from the set V (G) ∪ E(G). We say that
100: a pair x and x0 is enclosed by S if there is a S-closed path containing x and x0 . We say
101: that a pair x and x0 is half-enclosed by S if there is a vertex s ∈ S such that a shortest
102: path from s to x contains x0 or a shortest path from s to x0 contains x.

106: x0 are distinguished by S. If x and x0 are half-enclosed by S then x and x0 are distinguished
107: by S in all cases except possibly when x and x0 are a pair consisting of a vertex and an

341:     It is sufficient to prove that S is not a mixed metric generator. In the first case, let e
342: be the edge incident to a leaf v not contained in S and let w be the end-vertex of the edge
343: e distinct from v. Then e and w are not distinguished by S, so S is not a mixed metric
344: generator. In both second and third case, let Ci be a cycle without a geodesic triple of
345: S-active vertices. If there are no S-active vertices on C, then S = φ, so S cannot be a
346: mixed metric generator. If there is only one S-active vertex u on Ci , then neighbors v
347: and w of u on Ci are not distinguished by S. Finally, if there are at least two S-active
348: vertices on Ci , let u and v be two S-active vertices on Ci such that d(u, v) is maximum
349: possible and let w be the neighbor of v on Ci such that d(u, v) ≤ d(u, w). Notice that
350: such a w must exist, as there is no geodesic triple of S-active vertices on C. Then v and
351: vw are not distinguished by S, so S is not a mixed metric generator.

372: has at least one neighboring cycle. Therefore, rt(Ci ) ≥ 1 for every cycle Ci even without
373: leaves present in G.
374: Corollary 9 Let G be a cactus graph with c ≥ 2 cycles and without leaves. Then
375: mdim(G) ≤ 2c and the equality is attained if and only if every cycle in G has exactly
376: one root vertex.
377: Proof. Considering the formula for mdim(G) from Theorem 8, note that L1 (G) = 0
378: since G has no leaves. Also, note that every cycle Ci contributes to mdim(G) by either 2
379: or 1. The contribution of the cycle Ci to mdim(G) equals 2 when rt(G) = 1 (since then
380: max{3−rt(Ci ), 0} = 2 and Ci contributes 0 to ∆). On the other hand, the contribution of
381: Ci to mdim(G) can be 1 in two situations: when rt(G) = 2 (since then max{3−rt(Ci ), 0} =
382: 1 and Ci contributes 0 to ∆) or when rt(Ci ) ≥ 3 and there is no geodesic triple of root
383: vertices on Ci (since then max{3 − rt(Ci ), 0} = 0 and Ci contributes 1 to ∆).
384:      We conclude that mdim(G) will be the greatest when all cycles in G have rt(G) = 1
385: in which case mdim(G) = c · max{3 − rt(Ci ), 0} = 2c (an example of such a graph is
386: illustrated by Figure 3). For any cactus graph the value of mdim(G) can be only smaller,
387: i.e. mdim(G) ≤ 2c, which concludes the proof.

396:     Notice that the presence of leafs in a cactus graph with at least two cycles does not
397: decrease the minimum number of root vertices on every cycle, i.e. even if a cactus graph
398: has a leaf it still holds rt(Ci ) ≥ 1 for every cycle Ci . Also, adding leaves to a leafless
399: cactus graph with at least two cycles does not necessarily increase rt(Ci ) either, since
400: leaves can be added to vertices on cycles which are already root vertices or to the internal
401: vertices of the paths connecting cycles. Therefore, the following corollary also holds.
402: Corollary 10 Let G be a cactus graph with c ≥ 2 cycles. Then mdim(G) ≤ L1 (G) + 2c
403: and the bound is obtained if and only if every cycle in G has exactly one root vertex.

424:    Let us now summarize what has been done so far in order to comment what might be
425: done next. So far we have proven that in the graphs with edge disjoint cycles, i.e. cactus
426: graphs, it holds that mdim(G) ≤ L1 (G) + 2c where c is the number of cycles in G. Notice
427: that this bound was proved for trees and unicyclic graphs, as special subclasses of cactus
428: graphs, in Proposition 2 and Corollary 5 respectively. Finally, the bound was proven in
429: Corollary 10 for general cactuses. Recalling that in graphs with edge disjoint cycles the
430: number of cycles c equals the cyclomatic number c(G), this bound can be generalized as
431: mdim(G) ≤ L1 (G) + 2c(G) and the question posed whether that bound holds for general
432: graphs. We conjecture that the answer to that question is affirmative.
