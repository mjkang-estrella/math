                                                       On the mixed metric dimension of
                                                              2-connected graphs

                                                                                       Abstract
                                                        We show that for a 2-connected graph G which is not a cycle, the mixed
                                                     metric dimension of G is at most 2c(G), where c(G) is the cyclomatic number
                                                     of G. As an immediate application, we prove a conjecture proposed by Sedlar
                                                     and Škrekovski (2021).
                                                     Keywords: Mixed metric dimension; Cyclomatic number; 2-connected graph
                                                     2020 MSC: 05C12; 05C38




                                           1         Introduction
                                           Every graph considered in our paper is finite, simple, and connected. Let G be a
                                           graph. Denote by V (G) and E(G) the vertex set and edge set of G, respectively. Let
                                           u and v be two vertices of G. The distance between u and v is denoted by dG (u, v),
                                           or simply by d(u, v) when no confusion can arise. For distinct x, y ∈ V (G), we say a
                                           vertex v ∈ V (G) distinguishes x and y whenever d(v, x) ̸= d(v, y). A subset S ⊆ V (G)
                                           is called a metric generator of G if for every pair of distinct vertices x, y ∈ V (G), there
                                           exists a vertex in S such that this vertex distinguishes x and y. A metric generator
                                           of G with minimum cardinality is called a metric basis of G, and the cardinality of
                                           a metric basis is referred to as the metric dimension of G. The concept of metric
                                           dimension was independently introduced in the 1970s by Harary and Melter [4] and
                                           Slater [13]. The aim is to distinguish vertices of a graph using distances. The metric

dimension of a graph has a wide range of applications, including robot navigation in
networks [8], chemistry [3], pattern recognition and image processing [10], and the
game of Mastermind [1].
    As a further variation of the classical notion of a metric generator, the concept
of an edge metric generator was introduced in [7] to distinguish the edges of a graph
by means of distances from a prescribed set of vertices. Although a metric basis
of a connected graph G uniquely determines every vertex of G through its distance
representation, it does not necessarily distinguish all edges of G. This observation
motivates the following definitions. For a vertex v ∈ V (G) and an edge e = {u, w} ∈
E(G), the distance between v and e is defined by d(v, e) = min{d(v, u), d(v, w)}. A
vertex x ∈ V (G) is said to distinguish two distinct edges e1 , e2 ∈ E(G) on condition
that d(x, e1 ) ̸= d(x, e2 ). A set S ⊆ V (G) is called an edge metric generator for G
provided that every pair of distinct edges of G is distinguished by some vertex of S.
An edge metric generator of minimum cardinality is called an edge metric basis of G,
and its cardinality is called the edge metric dimension of G.
    Given a graph G, we write

                                 O(G) = V (G) ∪ E(G).

A vertex v ∈ V (G) is said to distinguish two distinct elements x, y ∈ O(G) (vertices
or edges) if d(v, x) ̸= d(v, y). A set S ⊆ V (G) is called a mixed metric generator for G
if for every pair of distinct elements x, y ∈ O(G), there exists a vertex of S such that it
can distinguish x, y. The cardinality of a mixed metric generator with minimum size,
denoted by dimm (G), is called the mixed metric dimension of G. The mixed metric
dimension of a graph was first introduced in [6]. For a comprehensive overview of
the variants of metric dimension and their applications to network detection, graph
isomorphism, coin-weighing problems, and machine learning, we refer the reader to
the two surveys [9, 14].
     In [6], the authors determined the mixed metric dimension of several families of
graphs, including cycles, complete bipartite graphs, trees, and grid graphs. They also
showed that the problem of determining the mixed metric dimension of a graph is
NP-hard in the general case. A leaf of G is a vertex with degree 1. Equivalently,
a leaf of G is a vertex of G that is incident to exactly one edge. Denote by ℓ(G)
the number of all leaves of G. The cyclomatic number of G, denoted by c(G), is the
number of independent cycles of G. Namely

                              c(G) = |E(G)| − |V (G)| + 1.

In [12], Sedlar and Škrekovski obtained the exact value of the mixed metric dimension
of a unicyclic graph and they proposed the following conjecture:

                                            2
Conjecture 1.1 ([12, Conjecture 13]) Let G be a connected graph which is not a
cycle. Then dimm (G) ≤ ℓ(G) + 2c(G).

    Conjecture 1.1 holds for trees and 3-connected graphs, see [12]. In [11], the au-
thors showed that Conjecture 1.1 holds for all Theta graphs. Recently, Chakraborty,
Foucaud, and Hakanen [2] proved that Conjecture 1.1 holds for a connected graph
which is not a tree such that ℓ(G) ≥ 1, and a connected graph G with ℓ(G) = 0 and
has a cut-vertex. In [5], the authors gave some graphs satisfying Conjecture 1.1. In
this paper, we will prove the following theorem:

Theorem 1.2 Let G be a 2-connected graph which is not a cycle. Then

                                  dimm (G) ≤ 2c(G).

As an immediate application of Theorem 1.2, we confirm Conjecture 1.1.


2     Preliminaries
Let G be a graph. A thread in a graph G is a path whose internal vertices all have
degree 2 in G. Let P = (p0 , p1 , . . . , pl ) be a thread of G. We use G − P 0 to denote
the subgraph of G that is obtained by removing the internal vertices of this thread
P and keeping the endpoints p0 and pl . For convenience, throughout this paper, we
denote the vertex pi of P by [i, i], and the edge {pj , pj+1 } of P by [j, j + 1], where
0 ≤ i ≤ l and 0 ≤ j ≤ l − 1. Namely, every element of P can be expressed as

                        [a, b], b − a ∈ {0, 1}, 0 ≤ a ≤ b ≤ l.

If G − P 0 is connected and l < dG−P 0 (p0 , pl ), then P is called a short thread of G.
If deg(p0 ) ≥ 3 and deg(pl ) ≥ 3, then this thread P is called maximal. By [15], a
2-connected graph which is not a cycle can be decomposed into distinct maximal
threads. Write
                            B = {v ∈ V (G) : deg(v) ≥ 3}.

3     Proof of the Main Result
In this section, we will show Theorem 1.2 and confirm Conjecture 1.1. We begin by
citing three known results.

Theorem 3.1 ([2, Corollary 16]) Let G be a connected graph which is not a tree such
that ℓ(G) ≥ 1. Then dimm (G) ≤ ℓ(G) + 2c(G).

Theorem 3.2 ([6, Theorem 4.3]) Let G be a tree. Then dimm (G) = ℓ(G).

Theorem 3.3 ([2, Corollary 12(i)]) Let G be a connected graph with ℓ(G) = 0. If G
has a cut-vertex, then dimm (G) ≤ 2c(G).

    We are now ready to prove Theorem 1.2.
Proof of Theorem 1.2. Note that G is not a cycle and ℓ(G) = 0. Then c(G) ≥ 2. If
every thread of G is short, then by Lemmas 2.3 and 2.5, we have that

                          dimm (G) ≤ |B| ≤ 2c(G) − 2 ≤ 2c(G),

as desired.
    In the following, we may assume that G has a maximal thread P = (p0 , p1 , . . . , pl )
such that dG−P 0 (u, v) ≤ l. Now let H = G − P 0 . Clearly, H is connected and has no
leaves. It follows that

           c(H) = |E(H)| − |V (H)| + 1 = (|E(G)| − l) − (|V (G)| − (l − 1)) + 1,

that is,
                                    c(G) = c(H) + 1.                                   (9)
    Suppose that H is a cycle. Then c(G) = 2. Namely, G is a graph in which p0 and
pl are the only vertices of degree 3, while every other vertex has degree 2. It follows
from [11, Corollaries 8 and 11] that dimm (G) ≤ 2c(G), as required.
    In the following, suppose that H is not a cycle. Assume that H has a cut-vertex.
Theorem 3.3 implies that dimm (H) ≤ 2c(H). Moreover, by Lemma 2.4 and (9), we
deduce that dimm (G) ≤ dimm (H) + 2 ≤ 2c(H) + 2 = 2c(G), as wanted. Thus, next,
we may assume that H has no cut vertices. It follows that H is 2-connected.
    Note that H is a 2-connected graph which is not a cycle. To complete the proof
of this theorem, we now proceed by induction on c(G). If c(G) = 2, then by (9), H
is a cycle, and so the result follows from the above proof.
    Now suppose that c(G) ≥ 3, and assume that the assertion holds for every graph
G satisfying the hypotheses of the theorem with c(G′ ) < c(G). Note that H satisfies
  ′



                                            9
the hypotheses of our theorem. In view of (9), by the induction hypothesis, we have
dimm (H) ≤ 2c(H). Moreover, by Lemma 2.4, we conclude that

                   dimm (G) ≤ dimm (H) + 2 ≤ 2c(H) + 2 = 2c(G).

This completes the induction and the proof.                                         2
    Note that a 2-connected graph has no leaves. Now, combining Theorems 3.1, 3.2,
3.3, and 1.2, we conclude that Conjecture 1.1 holds for any connected graph which is
not a cycle. Namely, Conjecture 1.1 is valid.
