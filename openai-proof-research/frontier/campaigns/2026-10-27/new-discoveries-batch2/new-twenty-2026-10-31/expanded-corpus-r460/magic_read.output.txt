    A magic square of order n is an n × n array with entries 1, 2, . . . , n2 , each appearing once, such
that the sum of each row, column, and both main diagonals is equal to n(n2 + 1)/2. The earliest
known magic square is a 3 × 3 magic square called Lo Shu magic square and can be traced in Chinese
literature as far back as 2800 B.C. Since then, certainly, many people studied magic squares. For
a survey of magic squares, see Chapter 34 in [5]. Magic rectangles are a natural generalization of
magic squares. A magic rectangle MR(a, b) is an a × b array with entries from the set {1, 2, . . . , ab},
each appearing once, with all its row sums equal to a constant δ and with all its column sums equal
to a constant η. The following theorem was proved in [12, 13]:

Theorem 1.2 ([12, 13]) A magic rectangle MR(a, b) exists if and only if a, b > 1, ab > 4, and
a ≡ b (mod 2).

    In [7], Froncek introduced the concept of magic rectangle sets. A magic rectangle set M=
MRS(a, b; c) is a collection of c arrays (a × b) whose entries are elements of {1, 2, . . . , abc}, each
appearing once, with all row sums in every rectangle equal to a constant δ = b(abc + 1)/2. and all
column sums in every rectangle equal to a constant η = a(abc + 1)/2. It was shown the following.

Theorem 1.3 ([8]) For a, b > 1 and ab > 4, a magic rectangle set MRS(a, b; c) exists if and only if
a, b ≡ 0 (mod 2) or abc ≡ 1 (mod 2).

    The following generalization magic rectangle set was introduced ([2]).

Definition 1.4 A Γ-magic rectangle set MRSΓ (a, b; c) on a group Γ of order abc is a collection of
c arrays (a × b) whose entries are elements of group Γ, each appearing once, with all row sums in
every rectangle equal to a constant ω ∈ Γ and all column sums in every rectangle equal to a constant
δ ∈ Γ.

                                           1         Introduction
                                           Let m be a positive integer. A magic square of order m is an m × m array with entries from
                                           the set {1, 2, . . . , m2 }, each appearing exactly once, such that the sum of the m numbers in
                                           each row, each column, and each of the two main diagonals is the same. A magic square of
                                           order m exists for any positive integer m 6= 2. We refer the reader to [12] for a brief history
                                           and constructions for magic squares.
                                               Magic rectangles are a natural generalization of magic squares. Let a and b be positive
                                           integers. A magic rectangle MR(a, b) is an a × b array with entries from the set {1, 2, . . . , ab},
                                           each appearing exactly once, such that the sum of the entries in each row is a constant α, and
                                           the sum of the entries in each column is another constant β. The total sum of all entries in an
                                           MR(a, b) is aα = bβ = 1+2+· · ·+ab = ab(ab+1)/2. Thus α = b(ab+1)/2 and β = a(ab+1)/2.
                                           Since α and β are both integers, a and b must have the same parity. Harmuth [10, 11] settled
                                           the existence problem of magic rectangles as early as 1881.

                                           Theorem 1.1. [10, 11] For a, b > 1, there exists an MR(a, b) if and only if a ≡ b (mod 2) and
                                           (a, b) 6= (2, 2).

                                               Following the work by Bier and Rogers [2], Bier and Kleinschmidt [1] provided a simple
                                           proof for Theorem 1.1 by utilizing centrally symmetric rectangles. Subsequently, Hagedorn [9]
                                           further simplified Bier and Kleinschmidt’s method, resulting in an entirely theoretical proof
                                           that does not depend on any concrete example of magic rectangles given in [1, Section 2.7].

    Motivated by the need to schedule a special kind of incomplete round robin tournaments,
called handicap tournaments, Froncek [6] proposed a generalization of magic rectangles, which
he termed magic rectangle sets. Let a, b and c be positive integers. A magic rectangle set
MRS(a, b; c) is a collection of c arrays of size a × b, whose entries are elements of {1, 2, . . . , abc},
each appearing exactly once, such that the sum of each row in every a × b array equals a
constant b(abc + 1)/2 and the sum of each column in every a × b array equals another constant
a(abc + 1)/2. Froncek [7] settled the existence problem for magic rectangle sets.

Theorem 1.2. [7, Theorem 3.2] For a, b > 1, there exists an MRS(a, b; c) if and only if either
abc ≡ 1 (mod 2) or a ≡ b ≡ 0 (mod 2), and (a, b) 6= (2, 2).

    Let a, b and c be positive integers. Let (G, +) be a finite abelian group of order abc. A
G-magic rectangle set MRSG (a, b; c) is a collection of c arrays of size a × b with entries from
the group G, each appearing exactly once, such that all row sums in every a × b array equal a
constant γ ∈ G and all column sums in every a × b array equal another constant δ ∈ G. When
c = 1, an MRSG (a, b; 1) is called a G-magic rectangle, and is simply written as an MRG (a, b).
    Denote by Zv the additive group of integers modulo v. Evans [5, Theorem 1] investigated
the existence of an MRZv (a, b), which he termed a modular magic rectangle. Cichacz and Hinc
[3] generalized Evans’s result to any finite abelian group.

Theorem 1.3. [3, Theorem 3.11] For any abelian group G of order ab with a, b > 1, an
MRG (a, b) exists if and only if either a ≡ b (mod 2), or the Sylow 2-subgroup of G is noncyclic.

    Furthermore, Cichacz and Hinc [3] examined the existence of a G-magic rectangle set for any
finite abelian group G. They established the following necessary condition for the existence of
an MRSG (a, b; c) (see [3, Observations 1.6, 1.7 and 3.8]). We outline the proof for completeness.

Lemma 1.1. [3] Let G be an abelian group of order abc with a, b > 1. If there exists an
MRSG (a, b; c), then either a ≡ b ≡ 0 (mod 2), or the Sylow 2-subgroup of G is trivial or
noncyclic, and when 2 ∈ {a, b}, ab ≡ 0 (mod 4).

Proof. If the Sylow 2-subgroup of G is cyclic and nontrivial, then G has the unique element
of order 2, denoted by θ. Let M be an MRSG (a, b; c). Let γ and δ be the row sum and the

    Furthermore, consider the case of a = 2 and b = 2l + 1 for some positive integer l. Note

2, then the other cannot be odd.

2     Problem reduction
By Lemmas 1.1 and 1.2, in order to prove Theorem 1.4, it remains to examine the existence of
an MRSG (2l + 1, 2n ; c), where l ⩾ 1 and n ⩾ 2. In this section, we reduce the existence problem
of an MRSG (2l + 1, 2n ; c) to that of an MRSZp ⊕S2 (p, 4, |S2|/4), where p is a prime factor of 2l + 1
and S2 is the Sylow 2-subgroup of G.

Lemma 2.1. Let a ⩾ 3 be an odd integer and n ⩾ 2 be an integer. Let G be an abelian group
of order 2n ac, whose Sylow 2-subgroup S2 is noncyclic. Let p be a prime factor of a. If there
exists an MRSZp ⊕S2 (p, 4; |S2|/4), then there exists an MRSG (a, 2n ; c).

Proof. For n ⩾ 2, if there exists an MRSG (a, 4; 2n−2 c), which consists of 2n−2 c arrays, each
