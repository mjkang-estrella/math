1189: 5.2     Discrete product measures and word maps
1190: Here, we develop the formalism of weighted words over a finite alphabet, and we will ultimately prove
1191: that these suffice to capture all non-colliding product measures. The terminology of “words” is meant to
1192: suggest parallels to combinatorial group theory, but this presentation stands alone without group-theoretical
1193: machinery. One motivation is to show that the locus of product measures Pm is semi-algebraic by describing
1194: it with word maps—mappings from weights to measures for fixed words.
1195: Definition 5.2 (Word maps and weighted words). Consider a finite set of symbols Σ = {a1 , . . . , am }, which
1196: we will think of as an alphabet. A word of length r is a string of r symbols from the alphabet, w ∈ Σr . This
1197: word induces a word map Fw : Rr≥0 → ∆(Sm ) as follows. Let α ∈ Rr≥0 be a weight vector. For a symbol
1198: ai , appearing si times in w, suppose the weights of these occurrences are (ri1 . . . . , risi ), summing to ri . For
1199: each symbol ai , we will select exactly one of its occurrences in the word w, selecting the kth occurrence
1200: with probability rik /ri . This makes Fw (α) into a probability distribution on Sm , where the probability of
1201: σ ∈ Sm is given by the selected symbols coming in the order aσ(1) aσ(2) . . . aσ(m) . We will call the pair (w, α)
1202: a weighted word over the alphabet Σ, and let Wm,r ⊆ Mm denote the measures on Sm given by weighted
1203: words of length ≤ r.

1214: by w = abab and α = (2, 1, 3, 5), which gives weights (2, 3) to copies of a and (1, 5) to copies of b. We now
1215: describe how to interpret this as a probability distribution on the strings {ab, ba}. The word abab contains
1216: both ordered subsequences: ab (achieved three ways as a b a b, a b a b, or a b a b) and ba (achieved one way
1217: as a b a b). The weights tell us that the relative likelihood that the symbols are chosen in each position, so
1218: that for instance b is five times as likely to be chosen in the fourth position as in the second. We calculate

1667: Theorem 5.13 (Dimension upper bound). Write C(m) = k=2 k·(m−k)!               for the number of pure cycles
1668: in Sm , i.e., permutations with exactly one nontrivial cycle and all other points fixed. Then for all m, the
1669: dimension of Pm is at most C(m).
1670: Proof. Consider the set
1671:                            B := {F (π) | π contains at least two nontrivial cycles}.
1672: By Lemma 5.12, each element of B is a scalar multiple of a gradient of Equation (EO) at the uniform
1673: distribution for some collection of at least 2 disjoint events. By Lemma E.1, these gradients are all cotangent
1674: vectors. Since the elements of B are scalar multiples of a subset of the Lie shuffle basis, these gradient
1675: vectors are all linearly independent by Lemma 5.11. Thus, so far we have found a collection of independent
1676: cotangent vectors, one for each permutation with at least two nontrivial cycles. There is one more constraint:
1677: In Pm ⊂ ∆(Sm ), all elements are linear combinations of permutations whose coefficients sum to one. Thus, in
1678: the tangent space, vectors can be regarded as linear combinations of permutations with coefficients summing
1679: to zero. In other words, we have the all-ones vector as an additional vector in the cotangent space, which is
1680: independent of the others. We define this vector to be F of the identity permutation. Hence, the codimension
1681: of the tangent space of Pm at the uniform distribution is at least the number of permutations that have at
1682: least two nontrivial cycles, plus one for the identity permutation (which has zero nontrivial cycles). Since this
1683: holds at the uniform distribution, Lemma E.4 lets us conclude that this holds at all points in Pm . Therefore,
1684: the dimension of Pm is at most the number of permutations with exactly one nontrivial cycle.
1685:    This sequence C(m) appears in OEIS as entry A006231 [17]. We note that C(m) ∼ e(m − 1)!, while
1686: ∆(Sm ) itself has dimension m! − 1, so asymptotically this dimension tends to a fraction e/m of the ambient
1687: dimension.
