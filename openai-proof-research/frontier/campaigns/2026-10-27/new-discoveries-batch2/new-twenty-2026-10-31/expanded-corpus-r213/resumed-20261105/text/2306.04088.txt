                                                   A Comment on Dean’s Construction of Prime
                                                            Labelings on Ladders
                                                                     Stephen J. Curran∗ and M. A. Ollis†

                                                                                        Abstract
                                                      A prime labeling on a graph of order m is an assignment of {1, 2, . . . , m} to the
                                                  vertices of the graph such that each pair of adjacent vertices has coprime labels. The




arXiv:2306.04088v1 [math.CO] 7 Jun 2023
                                                  ladder of order 2n is the 2 × n grid graph graph P2 × Pn . In a recent paper, Dean
                                                  claimed a proof of the Prime Ladder Conjecture that every ladder has a prime
                                                  labeling. We point out a flaw in Dean’s construction, showing that a stronger
                                                  hypothesis is needed for it to hold. We conjecture that this stronger hypothesis
                                                  is true. We also offer an alternative construction inspired by Dean’s approach
                                                  which shows that if the Even Goldbach Conjecture and a particular strengthening
                                                  of Lemoine’s Conjecture are true then the Prime Ladder Conjecture follows.


                                          1       Introduction
                                          Let G be a graph of order m. A prime labeling of G is an assignment of the integers
                                          {1, 2, . . . , m} to the vertices of the graph such that each pair of adjacent vertices has
                                          coprime labels. If G has a prime labeling then it is prime. See Gallian’s dynamic survey
                                          of graph labelings [4] for a comprehensive account of graph labelings in general and prime
                                          labelings in particular.
                                               In this note we are concerned with ladders: the ladder Ln of order 2n is the 2 × n
                                          grid graph P2 × Pn . The Prime Ladder Conjecture, made by Vilfred et al. [9], is that
                                          every ladder is prime. There are several results available about the primality of ladders,
                                          unfortunately not all complete and correct.
                                               Vilfred, Somasundaram and Nicholas show that Ln is prime when n, n + 1, n + 2
                                          or 2n + 1 is prime [9] and Sundaram, Ponraj and Somasundaram show that Ln is prime
                                          when n is the sum of two primes and when n ≤ 116 [8]. Berliner et al show that Ln is
                                          prime when n = (q − p)/2 for some prime q with p either 1 or a prime smaller than 2n + 1
                                          [1].
                                               In [3], Dean claims a full proof of the conjecture. However, the method is flawed.
                                          We explore this in the next section, showing that Dean’s construction requires a stronger
                                          condition than the one given. In [6], a proof that all cubic bipartite graphs other than
                                          K3,3 are prime is claimed, a result that immediately implies the primality of ladders.
                                          Unfortunately, this proof is also incorrect [7].

    In an unpublished work, Ghorbani and Kamali claim a proof that all ladders are
prime [5].
    In this short note our contribution is two-fold. In Section 2 we consider what goes
wrong in Dean’s construction and show how the existence of “strong canonical partitions”
of integers would imply that Dean’s approach is successful. Sadly, Nathaniel Dean died
in 2021 and we were unable to discuss this work with him.
    In Section 3 we offer a new construction, somewhat similar to that of Dean’s. It
already follows from [8] that Goldbach’s Even Conjecture implies that Ln is prime for
even n. Our construction shows that the following strengthening of Lemoine’s Conjecture
implies that Ln is prime for odd n.
Conjecture 1.1 (Strengthened Lemoine’s Conjecture). For any odd integer n ⩾ 7, there
exist primes p and q with p < 2q such that n = 2p + q.
    We have checked with GAP that the Strengthened Lemoine’s Conjecture holds for
n < 107 .
    Related work of the present authors shows that the techniques of Section 3 may be
extended to the 3 × n grid graphs P3 × Pn . In [2] it is shown that Goldbach’s Even
Conjecture and a more restrictive strengthening Lemoine’s Conjecture requiring p < q
are sufficient to show that these graphs are prime for all n.

Theorem 2.1. [3] If n has a strong canonical partition then Ln is prime.
    Dean conjectures that all n ≥ 50 have a canonical partition with at most 3 terms
and confirms this up to n = 5, 000, 000. As a canonical partition with two terms is
automatically strong, Dean’s check guarantees strong canonical partitions for even n in
this range. We have checked that there is a strong canonical partition with three terms
for every odd n in the same range. Therefore, we have prime labelings for P2 × Pn when
50 ≤ n ≤ 5, 000, 000 via Dean’s construction.
    It seems likely that all n ≥ 50 have a strong canonical partition with at most three
terms.

In this section we construct a prime labeling for the ladders Ln , where n = 2p + q for a
prime p and odd prime q with p < 2q. Some notation: for a label ℓ, let N (ℓ) denote the
set of labels that are on adjacent vertices to the vertex labeled ℓ.
    We start with a lemma that gives us a prime labeling for L2p for prime p that has a
useful additional property.

Theorem 3.2. If n = 2p + q where p is prime and q is an odd prime, then Ln is prime.
Proof. Let S1 be the prime labeling on L2p given in Lemma 3.1. Extend the labeling S1
to the labeling S2 on L2p+q by letting f (ui,j ) = 2p + (i − 1)q + j for all 1 ⩽ i ⩽ 2 and
2p + 1 ⩽ j ⩽ 2p + q. Let k = ⌊4p/q⌋ and let column j ∗ be the column with the labels
{(k + 1)q, (k + 2)q}. The internal labels in column j ∗ are the only non-coprime labels
in S2 . We show that we can swap one of the labels in column j ∗ with another to produce
a new labeling S3 that is prime.
    First suppose that either p < q or 21 p < q < 23 p. If 2p < q then 2q is in column j ∗ , if
p < q < 2p then 4q is in column j ∗ and if 21 p < q < 23 p then 8q is in column j ∗ . Therefore,
as all adjacent labels in S2 are of opposite parity, it is sufficient to find a power of 2 that
is not adjacent to a multiple of q to swap with 2p, 4p or 8p and let S3 be the labeling
resulting from this swap.
    If p = 2 then N (8) = {1, 3, 9} which we can use when q ̸= 3. If q = 3 then there is no
suitable power of 2 as 3 is in each of N (2), N (4) and N (8). However, N (12) = {1, 9, 13}
and N (14) = {11, 13} and so we may swap 12 and 14 to obtain a prime labeling.

q = 5 then N (8) = {1, 9, 11} so we may use 8.
    If p = 5 then N (8) = {3, 7, 9} which is suitable for q ̸∈ {3, 7}. If q = 3 then no
suitable power of 2 is available. Instead, swap 7q = 21, which is in column j ∗ and has

then N (4) = {3, 9, 17}.
    If p ≥ 7 then N (2) = {3, p, p + 2} or {3, p + 2, 3p}. As q ̸= 3 and q ̸= p, this is suitable
for all q except q = p + 2. If q = p + 2 then p > 7 and we have N (8) = {7, 9, p + 8}.
    Now suppose that 32 p < q ≤ p. In this range, if 45 p < q then the entries in column j ∗
are {5q, 6q} and if 45 p > q then the entries in column j ∗ are {6q, 7q}. It is sufficient to
find a label of the form 2a 3b with a, b > 0 that is not adjacent to a multiple of q to swap
with 6p and let S3 be the labeling resulting from this swap.
    We cannot have p = 2. If p = 3 then q = 3 and we can use N (6) = {5, 7}. If p = 5
then q ≤ 5 and we can use N (6) = {1, 13, 19}.
    If p = 7 then q ≤ 7 and N (12) = {5, 11, 13}, leaving only q = 5 to consider. There
is no label of the form 2a 3b that we can use in this case. Instead, we switch the other
entry in column j ∗ , which is 5p = 35, with p = 7. We have N (7) = {4, 6, 22} and
N (35) = {30, 34, 36}.
    If p ≥ 11 then q > 32 p ≥ 7 and N (6) = {3, 5, p + 6}. If q | p + 6 then p + 6 must be a
non-trivial odd multiple of q. Hence p + 6 ≥ 3q > 2p and so p < 6, which it is not and
hence gcd(q, p + 6) = 1.
