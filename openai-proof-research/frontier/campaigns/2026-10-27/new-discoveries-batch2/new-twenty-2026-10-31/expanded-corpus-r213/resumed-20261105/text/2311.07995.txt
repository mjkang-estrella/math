                                                                               1. Introduction
                                             Let A and B be finite structures (e.g. graphs, hypergraphs, or metric spaces)
                                          such that A is a substructure of B (all substructures and subgraphs in this note
                                          will be induced). We say that B is an EPPA-witness for A if every isomorphism of
                                          substructures of A (a partial automorphism of A) extends to an automorphism of
                                          B. We say that a class C of finite structures has the extension property for partial
                                          automorphisms (EPPA, also called the Hrushovski property) if for every A ∈ C
                                          there is B ∈ C which is an EPPA-witness for A.
                                             In 1992, Hrushovski [Hru92] established that the class of all finite graphs has
                                          EPPA. This result was used by Hodges, Hodkinson, Lascar, and Shelah to show
                                          the small index property for the random graph [HHLS93]. After this, the quest of
                                          identifying new classes of structures with EPPA continued with a series of papers
                                          including [Her95, Her98, HL00, HO03, Sol05, Ver08, ABWH+ 17, HKN18, Con19,
                                          HJKS19, HKN19, Kon19, EHKN20, Ott20, EHN21, HKN22, HJKS24]. Never-
                                          theless, there are still many classes for which EPPA is open. The most notable
                                          examples are the class of all finite tournaments (see [HL00]), the class of all finite
                                          partial Steiner systems (see [HKN22]), and the class of all finite graphs where au-
                                          tomorphisms can also send edges to non-edges and vice versa (see [EHKN20]). See
                                          also Remark 2.2.

                                  2. Upper bounds
  There are three types of EPPA-witnesses which one should keep in mind:
2.1. Finite homogeneous graphs. A finite graph is homogeneous if it is an
EPPA-witness for itself. In 1976, Gardiner [Gar76] classified all finite homogeneous
graphs. Up to complementation, they are the following:
    (1) C5 , the cycle on five vertices,
    (2) L(K3,3 ), the line graph of the complete bipartite graph with both partitions
        of size 3, (this graph has nine vertices), and
    (3) disjoint unions of complete graphs of the same size.
Clearly, the following holds:
Observation 2.1. A homogeneous graph is an EPPA-witness for all its subgraphs.
   While there are very few homogeneous graphs, one needs to keep them in mind,
as their subgraphs tend to be exceptions in otherwise general lower bounds.
Remark 2.2. In general, a possibly infinite (model-theoretic) structure M is ho-
mogeneous if every isomorphism between finite substructures of M extends to an
automorphism of M. The Cherlin–Lachlan classification programme of homoge-
neous structures (see e.g. [LW80, Lac84b, Lac84a, Che98, CSS99, Che22]) shows
that homogeneous structures are very special; for example, Lachlan and Woodrow
in 1980 proved that the only countably infinite homogeneous graphs are, up to
isomorphism and complementation, the countable random graph (also called the
Rado graph), its Kk -free analogues (called the Henson graphs), and disjoint unions
of complete graphs of the same size.
    Given a countable structure M, we can assume that its vertex set is the set N
of natural numbers, and hence identify the automorphism group Aut(M) with a
subgroup of the symmetric group Sym(N). There is a natural topology of Sym(N)
(the pointwise convergence topology) and it turns out that Γ is a closed subgroup of
Sym(N) if and only if it is the automorphism group of some countable homogeneous
structure.
    It is thus, perhaps, not that surprising that various dynamical properties of
closed subgroups of Sym(N) are equivalent to combinatorial properties of classes
of finite structures. EPPA is an example of this: If a hereditary class C of finite
structures has EPPA and the joint embedding property (for every A, B ∈ C there
is C ∈ C containing both A and B as substructures) then C is the age of some
homogeneous structure M (the class of all finite substructures of M, denoted by
age(M)). Kechris and Rosendal in 2007 proved that if M is locally finite then
Aut(M) can be written as the closure of a union of a chain of compact subgroups
if and only if age(M) has EPPA. [KR07]
    Another example of such a property is the Ramsey property. Kechris, Pestov,
and Todorčević in 2005 proved that Aut(M) is extremely amenable if and only if
age(M) has the Ramsey property, that is, satisfies a structural variant of Ramsey’s
theorem. [KPT05] This can also be seen as a link between the study of EPPA

numbers and the well-developed area of Ramsey numbers (see e.g. [CFS15]). See

                                 3. Lower bounds
   In this section we will present some lower bounds for EPPA numbers. The first
and the only published lower bound has already been proved in Hrushovski’s paper.
The half graph on 2m vertices is the bipartite graph on the vertex set [2m] where
i < j form and edge if and only if j ≥ i + m.
Theorem 3.1 (Hrushovski, 1992 [Hru92]). Every EPPA-witness of the half-graph
on 2m vertices has at least 2m vertices.
   Hrushovski shows that for any non-empty subset of one partition of the half
graph, every EPPA-witness needs to contain a vertex which is connected to the
subset and not connected to the rest of the partition. There are 2m − 1 such
subsets. The following lemma abstracts the key idea from Hrushovski’s proof of
Theorem 3.1 and will be important for the majority of lower bounds in this section:
Lemma 3.2. Let G be a graph on n vertices containing an independent set A of
size m. Let k1 < · · · < kℓ be integers such that for every 1 ≤ i ≤ ℓ there exists
                                                                                
                                                                       P
vi ∈ G \ A which has exactly ki neighbours in A. Then eppa(G) ≥ m + ℓi=1 kmi .
Proof. Let H be an EPPA-witness for G. We will prove that for every X ⊆ A with
|X| = ki for some i, there is a vertex w ∈ H which is connected to every member
of X and no member of A \ X. This clearly implies the conclusion. Given such a
set X, let Yi be the set of neighbours of vi inside A. By definition, |Yi | = |X| = ki ,
and thus there exists a permutation f : A → A such that f (Yi ) = X. As f is a
partial automorphism of G, it extends to an automorphism g : H → H, and g(vi )
is a vertex of H connected to every member of X and no member of A \ X.              

                    4. Further directions and questions
   EPPA-witnesses are very symmetric objects. First, it is easy to see that smallest
EPPA-witnesses are vertex transitive, and smallest EPPA-witnesses which more-
over minimize the number of edges are also edge-transitive. Second, if H is an
EPPA-witness for G then orbits of tuples of vertices G in H are determined by the
isomorphism type of the subgraph induced on the tuple. Thus, in particular, every
vertex of G has the same degree in H, every pair of vertices connected by an edge
has the same cardinality of the intersection of their neighbourhoods, and similarly
for non-edges (cf. strongly regular graphs, see e.g. [GR01]), and so on.
   Thus, the EPPA number can be seen as a measure of asymmetry of a graph – a
very asymmetric graph should be hard to symmetrise and hence have large EPPA
number (although with the current results it is possible that in reality EPPA num-
bers only measure something much simpler such as the maximum degree). Besides
this and it being a nice problem with elegant results, we believe that the study
of EPPA numbers will lead to an improved understanding of structural properties
which make a graph easy or hard for EPPA, as well as to new methods for proving
upper bounds. In turn, these new methods might help solve some important open
problems in the wider area of EPPA for structures, such as the questions presented
in the following subsection:

4.1. Existence of EPPA-witnesses for classes of structures.
Question 4.1 (Herwig–Lascar, 2000 [HL00]). Does the class of all finite tourna-

there is a finite tournament B containing A as a sub-tournament such that every
isomorphism of sub-tournaments of A extends to an automorphism of B?
   This is, perhaps, the biggest open problem of the area. Herwig and Lascar show
that it is equivalent to a statement about the profinite topology (or, more precisely,
odd-adic topology) on free groups [HL00]. One of the reasons why this problem has,
so far, eluded all attempts at solving it, is that all automorphisms of tournaments
have odd order, while the existing methods for proving EPPA for general structures
(see e.g. [HKN22] or [Kon23]) rely heavily on automorphisms of even orders. We
remark that Huang, Pawliuk, Sabok, and Wise [HPSW19] proved that a custom-
tailored class of hypertournaments does not have EPPA by showing a failure of an
equivalent profinite topology statement.

A ∪ C. This was the key observation for our proof: Fixing all members of B gave us

   This is essentially as far as one can get using this simple idea: There are only
2O(n log n) partial automorphisms of any structure on n vertices. Therefore, we pose
the following question:
Question 4.25. Are there 3-uniform hypergraphs on n vertices for arbitrarily large
                                               2
n whose EPPA-witnesses need to have at least cn vertices for some constant c > 1?
   Proving a 2ω(n log(n)) lower bound would already be a significant progress (or
even improving the base of the exponent in Observation 4.24), even for k-uniform
hypergraphs for k not necessarily equal to 3.
