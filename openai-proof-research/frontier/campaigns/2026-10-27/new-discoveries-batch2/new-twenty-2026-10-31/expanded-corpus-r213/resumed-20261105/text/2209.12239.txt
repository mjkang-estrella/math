                                           UNIMODALITY OF RANKS AND A PROOF OF STANTON’S CONJECTURE

                                                                 KATHRIN BRINGMANN, SIU HANG MAN, AND LARRY ROLEN

                                                   Abstract. Recently, much attention has been given to various inequalities among partition func-
                                                   tions. For example, Nicolas, and later DeSavlvo–Pak, proved that p(n) is eventually log-concave,
                                                   and Ji–Zang showed that the cranks are eventually unimodal. This has led to a flurry of recent
                                                   activity generalizing such results in different directions. At the same time, Stanton recently made
                                                   deep conjectures on the positivity of certain polynomials associated to ranks and cranks of parti-
                                                   tions, with the ultimate goal of pointing the way to “deeper” structure refining ranks and cranks.

                                                   These have been shown to be robust in recent works, which have identified further infinite families
                                                   of such conjectures in the case of colored partitions. In this paper, we employ the Circle Method
                                                   to prove unimodality for ranks. As a corollary, we prove Stanton’s original conjecture. This points
                                                   to future study of the positive, integral coefficients Stanton conjectured to exist, hinting at new
                                                   combinatorial structure yet to be uncovered.


                                                                      1. Introduction and statement of results
                                              The study of the sizes and of various inequalities among partitions has a long history, going
                                           back to Hardy and Ramanujan’s original development of the Circle Method to prove their famous
                                           asymptotic for p(n), the number of integer partitions of n. Recently, there has been a reinvigoration
                                           of this subject in several directions. Nicolas proved, and later DeSalvo-Pak independently reproved
                                           [9, 16] that p(n) is log-concave starting at n = 26. That is,
                                                                            p(n)2 ≥ p(n − 1)p(n + 1),          for n ≥ 26.
                                           An infinite family of generalizations of log-concavity was conjectured by Chen–Jia–Wang [7] and
                                           proven by Griffin, Ono, Zagier, and the third author [12]. This was in direct analogy with a
                                           program of Jensen–Polá on the Riemann Hypothesis, and uncovered a general phenomenon which
                                           gave new evidence for it. Another striking multiplicative-additive inequality recently was proven
                                           by Bessenrodt–Ono [5]
                                                                         p(a)p(b) ≥ p(a + b)           for a, b ≥ 2 a + b > 8.
                                              In this paper we prove further inequalities in the world of partitions, namely for rank counting
                                           functions. Although analytic in nature, the motivation behind these inequalities, based on ideas
                                           of Stanton, hints at combinatorial structure “refining” partition ranks. In conjunction with re-
                                           cent works extending Stanton’s conjectures, this suggests that the deeper combinatorial structure
                                           Stanton posited is waiting to be discovered. We highlight this in Question 1 below.
                                              To set up this picture, we recall the classical story of ranks and cranks. Ramanujan [19, 20]
                                           initiated the arithmetic study of p(n) by stating and proving his famous three congruences
                                                 p(5n + 4) ≡ 0 (mod 5) ,            p(7n + 5) ≡ 0 (mod 7) ,             p(11n + 6) ≡ 0 (mod 11) .
                                           His proofs relied on q-series manipulation. Given the combinatorial nature of the statement, it
                                           is natural to ask for a combinatorial proof. In the search for such a combinatorial explanation,
                                           Dyson [10] defined the rank of a partition to be its largest part minus its number of parts, and
                                           conjectured that the partitions of 5n + 4 (resp. 7n + 5) are equidistributed with respect to the rank
                                           modulo 5 (resp. 7). Dyson’s conjecture was proved by Atkin and Swinnerton-Dyer [4]. Dyson also

   The main goal of this paper is to prove Conjecture 1.1. To state our methods, we first recall the
previous proof of part (1), and then outline our methods for proving part (2). It turns out that the
key phenomenon beyond Stanton’s Conjecture is the eventual near-unimodality of ranks and cranks.
In the case of cranks, let M (m, n) denote the number of partitions of n with crank m. Eventual
unimodality in this case was recently proven by Ji and Zang [13] (as M (m, n) = M (−m, n),
unimodality is the same as monotonicity starting at m = 0).

   To prove Stanton’s conjecture for the rank, we need a result analogous to Theorem 1.2 for the
rank, which was conjectured in [6]. The methods used to prove Theorem 1.2 were the judicious
use of clever q-series manipulations and comparisons. These methods do not, however, generalize
to other situations. Here, we give a more general analytic proof based on the Circle Method.
   The monotonicity result we aim to show here is the following, where N (m, n) denote the number
of partitions of n with rank m.
Conjecture 1.4. For n ≥ 39 and 0 ≤ m ≤ n − 3, we have
                                           N (m, n) ≥ N (m + 1, n).
  As shown in [6], this is the key analytic result needed to prove Stanton’s Conjecture.
Theorem 1.5 ([6], Theorem 1.5). Conjecture 1.4 implies Conjecture 1.1 (1).

ferent arguments depending on the size of m, and no explicit bounds are known in the literature.

   We outline the strategy for proving Theorem 1.6. Our proof for m ≪ n 4 is based on Zhou’s
work on the eventual log-concavity of the rank [25], which in turn is based on Wright’s Circle
Method. Typical for such approaches, this only shows that there exists n0 ∈ N (which may be
very large), such that the statement is true for n ≥ n0 . In this paper, we fine-tune the arguments
in [25], establish strong error bounds, so that the constant n0 is sufficiently small to make explicit
                                                       1
checking of the remaining cases feasible. For m ≫ n 2 , we have another argument, which is based
on the convexity of the partition function p(n). Combining these arguments, we find an explicit
n0 such that the statement is true for n ≥ n0 , and check the remaining cases by computer.
   The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we give some preliminaries on asymptotics,
particularly for the reciprocal of (q)∞ , the Euler–Maclaurin summation formula, explicit estimates
for Bessel functions, and resulting bound for partitions and partition differences. Using these
analytic estimates, in Section 3, we set up the Circle Method and bound minor and major arc
contributions. To prove our main result, we require extensive computer computations, which are
summarized in a case-by-case basis in Section 4, culminating in the proof of Theorem 4.1 which
                                                                                   3
proves Theorem 1.6 for small m (specifically, asymptotically up to about n 4 ) for n sufficiently
large. We conclude with the proof of Theorem 1.6, that is, Stanton’s Conjecture, in Section 5.
