                                                          Computing higher graph gonality is hard
                                                                        Ralph Morrison and Lucas Tolley

                                                                                         Abstract
                                                   In the theory of divisors on multigraphs, the rth divisorial gonality of a graph is the
                                               minimum degree of a rank r divisor on that graph. It was proved by Gijswijt et al. that the
                                               first divisorial gonality of a finite graph is NP-hard to compute. We generalize their argument
                                               to prove that it is NP-hard to compute the rth divisorial gonality of a finite graph for all r.




arXiv:2208.03573v1 [math.CO] 6 Aug 2022
                                               We use this result to prove that it is NP-hard to compute rth stable divisorial gonality for a
                                               finite graph, and to compute rth divisorial gonality for a metric graph. We also prove these
                                               problems are APX-hard, and we study the NP-completeness of these problems.


                                          1    Introduction
                                          Divisor theory on graphs provides a combinatorial analog of divisor theory on algebraic curves.
                                          This was introduced on finite graphs in [4] through the lens of chip-firing games, and was extended
                                          to metric graphs in [10, 12] through the language of tropical rational functions. A direct link
                                          between divisor theory on graphs and divisor theory on curves was established in [3], allowing for
                                          algebro-geometric results to be proved through purely combinatorial means, as done in [6].
                                              Of particular interest have been various analogs of the gonality of an algebraic curve. This can
                                          be defined as the minimum degree of a positive rank divisor on the curve, and equals the minimum
                                          degree of a map from the curve to a line. For finite or metric graphs, the divisorial gonality is the
                                          minimum degree of a positive rank divisor. In the case of finite graphs, one can also study the
                                          stable divisorial gonality, the minimum divisorial gonality of any subdivision of the graph. It was
                                          proved in [11] that divisorial gonality and stable divisorial gonality are both NP-hard to compute
                                          for finite graphs. The same result was proved for metric graphs in [8]; as mentioned in [15, Remark
                                          3.6], this result can also be deduced from arguments in [11].
                                              In this paper we consider the computational complexity of higher gonalities of graphs. For a
                                          finite or a metric graph, the rth divisorial gonality is the minimum degree of a divisor of rank r;
                                          and for a finite graph, the rth stable divisorial gonality is the minimum rth divisorial gonality of
                                          any subdivision of the graph.
                                              Throughout, the problems we consider take a graph and an integer k as input, and asks whether
                                          the relevant type of rth gonality is bounded by k. Our first main result is the following, which we
                                          obtain by adapting the construction from [11, §3].
                                          Theorem 1.1. For any positive integer r, the rth Divisorial Gonality problem is NP-hard.
                                             As in [11], our construction behaves nicely under subdivisions, allowing us to obtain the fol-
                                          lowing result.
                                          Theorem 1.2. For any positive integer r, the rth Stable Divisorial Gonality problem is
                                          NP-hard.
                                              From here, we utilize [15, Theorem 1.3] to develop our result for metric graphs. This answers
                                          in the affirmative an open problem posed in [15, Remark 3.6].
                                          Theorem 1.3. For any positive integer r, the rth Metric Divisorial Gonality problem is
                                          NP-hard.
                                             Our paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we present necessary background on divisor
                                          theory on graphs. In Section 3 we prove that rth gonality is NP-hard on finite graphs. This is
                                          extended to rth stable gonality in Section 4, and to metric graphs in Section 5. We prove results
                                          regarding APX-hardness and NP-completeness in Section 6

2    Divisor theory on graphs
In this paper we deal with both finite graphs and metric graphs; if unspecified, a graph is taken
to be finite. Our finite graphs are connected multigraphs G = (V, E), where V = V (G) is a finite
vertex set and E = E(G) is a finite edge multiset. Note that we allow multiple edges connecting
a pair of vertices, but never an edge from a vertex to itself; we denote the collection of edges
connecting v, w ∈ V (G) by E(v, w). The valence val(v) of a vertex v ∈ V (G) is the number of
edges incident to v. We say that a graph H is a subdivision of a graph G if H can be obtained by
iteratively introducing 2-valent vertices in the middle of the edges of G. We say a set S ⊂ V (G)
is an independent set if no two elements of S are connected by an edge in G. The independence
number of G, denoted α(G), is the largest possible size of an independent set for that graph. Given
a subset S ⊂ V (G), we let G[S] denote the subgraph induced by S, whose vertex set is S and whose
edge set is the subset of E with both endpoints in S.
    The divisor group of G, denoted Div(G), is the free Abelian group on the vertex set V (G). An
element D ∈ Div(G) is called a divisor, and can be written

intuitively think of a divisor as a placement of (possibly negative) integer numbers of poker chips
on the vertices of the graph, so that the degree is the total number of chips. If D(v) ≥ 0 for all
v ∈ V (G), we say that D is effective. If D(v) < 0 for some vertex v, we say that v is in debt.
    Order the vertices of G as v1 , . . . , vn , and let L be the Laplacian of G, i.e. the n × n matrix
with diagonal entries Lii = val(vi ) and off-diagonal entries Lij = −|E(vi , vj )|. Treating elements of
Div(G) as integer-valued vectors, we say two divisors D, D0 ∈ Div(G) are equivalent if D0 = D −Lσ
for some σ ∈ Z|V (G)| . We then write D ∼ D0 .
    This equivalence can be phrased more intuitively in the language of chip-firing games. Given a
divisor D, we transform it into a new divisor by “firing” a vertex v, which moves chips from v to
its neighbors (one along each edge). Then, D ∼ D0 if and only if we can obtain D0 from D via a
sequence of chip-firing moves; in particular, if D0 = D − Lσ, then σ encodes the number of times
each vertex should be fired.
    It is useful to think about firing multiple vertices simultaneously. Given a subset U ⊂ V (G), we
can transform a divisor D into an equivalent divisor D0 by firing all the vertices in U simultaneously.
In Laplacian notation, we have D0 = D − L1U , where 1U is the 0-1 vector with 1’s corresponding
to the elements of U . A key fact is that if D ∼ D0 with D and D0 both effective, then there exists a
sequence of subset-firing moves transforming D into D0 such that every intermediate divisor is also
effective [13, Corollary 3.11]. In fact, slightly more is true, which we summarize with the following
lemma.
Lemma 2.1. Suppose D, D0 ∈ Div(G), with D ∼ D0 and D0 effective. Then there exists a
collection of subset-firing moves transforming D to D0 such that no subset-firing move introduces
debt, and no subset-firing move increases the debt on any vertex.
Proof. Consider a firing script σ ∈ Z|V (G)| transforming D to D0 . Since 1V (G) ∈ ker L, we can
scale σ by the all 1’s vector, and so we may assume that σ(i) ≥ 0 for all i, and that σ(i) = 0 for
at least one i. Let k = maxi σ(i), and for 1 ≤ ` ≤ k consider the sets

                                U` = {vi ∈ V (G) | σ(i) ≥ k + 1 − `}.

That is, U` is the set of vertices that are fired at least k + 1 − ` times by σ. We then have that the
sequence of subset-firing moves given by U1 , U2 , . . . , Uk is the same firing script as σ [14, Lemma
2.3]
    Since Ui ⊂ Ui+1 for all i, once a vertex is fired, it is fired as part of every subsequent subset-
firing move, and will never gain more chips. Since D0 is effective, it follows that the only vertices
that can be fired are those that are not in debt. Thus no vertex ever has a debt that increases
from a subset-firing move. Similarly, no vertex can be newly put into debt in a subset-firing move,
since it would never gain more chips after that. This completes the proof.
   Given a divisor D ∈ Div(G), we let |D| denote the set of all effective divisors equivalent to
D. If |D| = ∅, we say D has rank −1, written r(D) = −1. Otherwise, we define the rank of D,


                                                   2
written r(D), to be the maximum value r such that for all effective divisors E of degree r we have
that |D − E| 6= ∅. In the language of chip-firing, the rank of a divisor is the maximum amount of
additional debt the divisor can eliminate, regardless of where that debt is placed. For r ≥ 1, we
then define the rth divisorial gonality dgonr (G) to be the minimum degree of a rank r divisor on
G. From there, the rth stable divisorial gonality sdgonr (G) is the minimum rth divisorial gonality
of any subdivision of G.
    We now briefly describe metric graphs, which will be considered in Section 5. A metric graph Γ
is a topological space arising from a pair (G, l), where G is a finite graph and l is a length function,
assigning to each edge of G a positive real number. Given a finite graph G, we can construct a
metric graph Γ(G) by assigning a length of 1 to each edge in E(G).
    We can study divisor theory on metric graphs, where a divisor is now a Z-linear combination
of all the points on the metric graph, with the requirement that all but finitely many coefficients
are zero; the notions of degree and effectiveness can be defined as before. Equivalence of divisors
is phrased in the language of tropical rational functions, and leads us to notions of the rank of
a divisor; we refer the reader to [10] for more details. For any r ≥ 1, the rth divisorial gonality
dgonr (Γ) is the minimum degree of a rank r divisor on a metric graph Γ.
    For a finite graph G, let σk (G) be the k th uniform subdivision of G, obtained by replacing every
edge in G with a path consisting of k edges. In [15], the authors prove that the gonalities of G and
Γ(G) need not be equal in general, but that

                                dgonr (Γ(G)) = min dgonr (σk (G)).
                                                 k∈Z>0


With this we can readily prove that, given a graph G, the rth metric gonality of Γ(G) falls between
rth divisorial gonality and rth stable divisorial gonality of G.
Proposition 2.2. For a finite graph G and for r ≥ 1, we have

                               sdgonr (G) ≤ dgonr (Γ(G)) ≤ dgonr (G).

Proof. By [15, Theorem 1.3], dgonr (Γ(G)) = mink∈Z>0 dgonr (σk (G)). Since each σk (G) is a sub-
division of G, we have sdgonr (G) ≤ dgonr (Γ(G)). Since G = σ1 (G), we have dgonr (Γ(G)) ≤
dgonr (G).


3    Higher gonality on finite graphs
For any fixed r ≥ 1, we define the rth Divisorial Gonality problem as follows.

      rth Divisorial Gonality
      Input: a graph G = (V, E) and an integer k ≤ r|V |.
      Question: is dgonr (G) ≤ k?

    Given some finite graph G, we will create a new graph whose rth gonality is directly related
to the independence number of G. Because the independence number of a graph is NP-hard to
compute, it will follow that that rth gonality is NP-hard to compute.
Construction 3.1. Given a graph G = (V, E), construct G0r = (V 0 , E 0 ) as follows. Begin with
a single vertex T in G0r . For each vertex v ∈ V , create 3 vertices in G0r : v, v 0 and Tv . Let
M = r(3|V | + 2|E| + 1) + 1. Between each T and Tv , add M parallel edges. Between each v and
v 0 , add M parallel edges. Between v 0 and Tv , add r + 2 edges. For each edge e(u, v) ∈ E, create
2 vertices in G0r : ev and eu . In G0r , connect v to ev with M edges and connect u to eu with M
edges, for each v and ev pair. Between each pair of ev and eu with e(v, u) ∈ E(G), add r edges.
    Note that M was defined to be larger than r times the number of vertices in G0r . See Figure

4     Higher stable divisorial gonality
Recall that the rth stable divisorial gonality of a graph G is the minimum rth divisorial gonality of
any graph which is a subdivision of G. For any fixed r ≥ 1, we define the rth Stable Divisorial
Gonality problem as follows.

      rth Stable Divisorial Gonality
      Input: a graph G = (V, E) and an integer k ≤ r|V |.

r, we will use an additional definition and result from [11]. Given an effective divisor D, define a
D-stopping path as a path where every internal vertex has degree 2 and whose ends are equivalent
under ∼D . Note that if D0 ∼ D then the D-stopping paths are the same as the D0 -stopping paths.

Note that if two or more chips are on a path’s internal vertices, subsets of the path can be fired
to move at least one chip to an endpoint, so there is always an effective D0 ∼ D in which all
D-stopping paths are clean.

Corollary 4.2. The rth Divisorial Gonality and rth Stable Divisorial Gonality remain
NP-hard when we restrict the inputs to bipartite graphs.
Proof. Let G be any finite graph, let G0r be constructed as usual, and consider σ2 (G0r ). Note that
σ2 (G0r ) is a bipartite graph, with partite sets given by the original vertices as one set, and the new
vertices as the other set. Since this graph is a subdivision of G0r , by the previous proof we have

                       dgonr (G0r ) = sdgonr (G0r ) ≥ dgonr (σ2 (G0r )) ≥ dgonr (G0r ),

so dgonr (G0r ) = dgonr (σ2 (G0r )). Moreover, since any subdivision of σ2 (G0r )) is also a subdivision of
G0r , we have sdgonr (G0r ) = sdgonr (σ2 (G0r )). Thus if we can lower bound either dgonr (σ2 (G0r )) or
sdgonr (σ2 (G0r )) efficiently, we can also upper bound α(G) efficiently. It follows that these problems
are NP-hard, even for bipartite graphs.

Theorem 4.3. The rth Divisorial Gonality and rth Stable Divisorial Gonality remain

Proof. Let G be a planar graph, and construct G0r as usual. We remark that G0r is an apex graph,

5    Higher gonality on metric graphs
For any fixed r ≥ 1, we define the rth Metric Divisorial Gonality problem as follows.
      rth Metric Divisorial Gonality
      Input: a metric graph Γ = (G, l) and an integer k ≤ r|V (G)|.
      Question: is dgonr (Γ) ≤ k?
    Given a finite graph G, recall that Γ(G) is the metric graph obtained by assigning edge lengths
of 1 to each edge of G. Recall further that for k ≥ 1, σk (G) denotes the finite graph obtained
by subdividing each edge into k edges. We are now ready to prove that our metric problem is
NP-hard.
Proof of Theorem 1.3. Let G = (V, E) be a finite connected graph, and let G0r be the graph from
Construction 3.1. By Lemma 3.7 and the argument of the proof of Theorem 1.2, we have that
sdgonr (G0r ) and dgonr (G0r ) are both equal to
                                r + (3r + 1)|V | + (2r − 1)|E| − α(G).
By Proposition 2.2, we know
                                sdgonr (G) ≤ dgonr (Γ(G)) ≤ dgonr (G).
Thus dgonr (Γ(G)) is bounded above and below by the same number, and so must be equal to it.
It follows that if we can upper bound the rth divisorial gonality of a metric graph efficiently, we
can efficiently lower bound α(G). We conclude that the problem is NP-hard.


6    Other complexity classes
We close with results pertaining to other computational complexity classes, namely APX-hard
problems and NP-complete problems. We will prove that each variation of higher divisorial gonality
is hard even to approximate; that is, that the rth Divisorial Gonality problem, the rth Stable
Divisorial Gonality, and the rth Metric Divisorial Gonality problem are all APX-hard.
Our proof closely follows those of [11, Theorems 3.10 and 3.11], and relies on showing that being
able to construct a “good” divisor of rank r (that is, one with degree close to the rth gonality)
allows one to quickly construct a “good” independent set (that is, one with a number of elements
close to α(G)). We recall the following result.
Lemma 6.1 (Corollary 3.8 in [11]). Given an effective divisor D on a graph G and any two vertices
u, v ∈ V (G), we can determine in polynomial time if u ∼D v.
