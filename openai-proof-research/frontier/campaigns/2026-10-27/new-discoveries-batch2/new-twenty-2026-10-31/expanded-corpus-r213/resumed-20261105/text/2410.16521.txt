                                                                                         1. Introduction
                                              The foremost computational problem in stable homotopy theory is to determine the stable ho-
                                           motopy groups of spheres, which fit into a graded ring. We will refer to the graded components of
                                           this ring as stable stems.
                                              Beyond applications to problems in homotopy theory itself, knowledge of stable stems can also be
                                           used to solve problems in algebra and geometry. For example, there is a well-known correspondence
                                           between finite-dimensional division algebras over R and the Hopf invariant one elements in the 2-
                                           primary stable stems. Adams [Ada60] computed differentials on the Hopf invariant one elements
                                           and used this correspondence to prove that R, C, H, and O are the only such algebras.
                                              In seemingly unrelated work, Browder [Bro69] reduced the existence of manifolds with Kervaire
                                           invariant one to the existence of elements in the 2-primary stable stems that behave algebraicly as
                                           squares of the Hopf invariant one elements . Using this relationship and techniques from equivariant
                                           and chromatic stable homotopy theory, Hill, Hopkins, and Ravenel [HHR16] showed that no such
                                           manifold exists beyond dimension 126. This is closely related to the work of Kervaire and Milnor
                                           [KM63] on the classification of exotic smooth structures on spheres. In the 61-dimensional case,
                                           Wang and Xu [WX17] used computations in the Adams spectral sequence to show that the 61-
                                           sphere has a unique smooth structure, the last possible case in odd dimension.
                                              In order to study the 2-primary component of the stable homotopy groups of spheres, Adams
                                           introduced a spectral sequence of the form
                                                                             E2s,t = Exts,t                   0
                                                                                        A (F2 , F2 ) ⇒ πt−s (S ) ⊗ Z2 ,
                                           where S 0 denotes the sphere spectrum, Ext∗,∗A denotes Ext in comodules over the dual Steenrod
                                           algebra, and Z2 denotes the 2-adic integers. Henceforth, we work implicitly at the prime 2.

    Turning now to the more general pattern of τ n -torsion on the C-motivic Adams E2 page, in
the computed range of stems in [IWX23a], it seems that higher τ n -torsion appears only in higher
filtration. In fact this holds for all stems:
Theorem 5. If x is a class on the C-motivic Adams E2 page and τ n x = 0 while τ n−1 x ̸= 0, then
x has Adams filtration at least 2n + 2.
   This bound is not tight, and based on the charts of [IWX23a], it seems reasonable to expect that
the 2n + 2 above could be replaced by a better bound:
Conjecture 6. If x is a class on the C-motivic Adams E2 page and τ n x = 0 while τ n−1 x ̸= 0, then
x has Adams filtration at least 3n.
   If it were to hold in general, this bound would be tight, by the existence of the τ 3 -torsion element
h2 g 2 in filtration 9. By Theorem 1, the 3n bound holds through the 5-line. In fact, we can prove a
better bound for τ 2 -torsion elements, even though we do not have enough information to compute
the entire 6-line by our methods:
Theorem 7. Through the 6-line, all C-motivic Adams E2 classes are either τ -free or τ 1 -torsion.
   In particular, all C-motivic Adams E2 classes which are τ 2 -torsion but not τ -torsion can appear
only in the 7-line and up, giving further empirical evidence for the conjectured 3n bound.
   By Theorem 5, there is no τ n -torsion appearing until at least the 4-line. The relations in this range
are, up to powers of τ to account for motivic weight, exactly those found on the classical Adams E2
page. Informally, one may think of the motivic Adams E2 page as being the “τ -adjoined” version
of the classical one in this range. However, by low-dimensional calculations, there is a τ -torsion
element, namely h41 , living on the 4-line. This is closely related to the discussion in Remark 2.
   Classically, Lin [Lin08] and his student Chen [Che11] computed the 4- and 5-lines of the Adams
E2 page using the Lambda algebra. In this method, complete information about the s-line is taken
as input. Calculations are made in low internal degrees on the (s + 1)-line using the Lambda algebra
and this input, and then one uses this to compute one degree higher by relating the Adams E2 page
        ∞
for RP−∞    to that for the sphere spectrum, which is critical to the proof of Lin’s theorem [Lin81].
The combination of these steps allows one to use low-degree calculations inductively to move along
the (s + 1)-line and work out all elements there. The result of Lin and Chen’s work is summarized in
[Che11] and provides an F2 -module presentation for the Adams E2 page for Adams filtration s ≤ 5.
   The approach we follow is the one laid out in Burklund and Xu [BX23] and uses complete
information about the classical Adams E2 page through the s-line to work out the C-motivic Adams
E2 page through the s-line. This approach can be summarized in the following way, where algAHSS
denotes the so-called algebraic Atiyah-Hirzebruch spectral sequence of [BX23] and CESS denotes the
usual Cartan-Eilenberg spectral sequence:
                                              algAHSS E1


                                                CESS E2


                  classical Adams E2                               motivic Adams E2
This diagram will be explained in the following sections. In Section 2 we describe the main properties
of the CESS which we will need for our computations. In particular, we show that there are only a
few differential patterns we need to consider, based on sparsity of the CESS. We also prove Theorem 5
by appealing to a rigidity theorem of [BX23]. In Section 3 we describe in detail the algAHSS, the
spectral sequence in which we will be carrying out the bulk of our computations. We show how

                         2. The Cartan-Eilenberg Spectral Sequence

pattern in the Cartan-Eilenberg spectral sequence. Recall that there is a Hopf algebra extension

                   3. The Algebraic Atiyah-Hirzebruch Spectral Sequence

6.4. Exotic Extensions. Now we will point out another feature of these calculations by first defin-
ing an extension on the C-motivic Adams E2 page to be exotic if it is of the form ab = τ n c for some
n ≥ 1 and with neither of a, b divisible by τ . The first exotic extension appears in stem 3 and is of
the form
                                                 h20 h2 = τ h31 ,
as seen in [IWX23a]. Classically, the corresponding relation is h20 h2 = h31 , but in the C-motivic
setting there is a motivic weight which requires this factor of τ . This exotic extension can be seen
in our low-dimensional charts, specifically in Figure 1, where we see a q0 -extension from q0 · h1 to
h30 . Since the source of the extension lies in CE filtration 1 while the target lies in CE filtration 0,
a multiple of τ is required to label a “mismatch” in filtrations.

this is not quite true. To see this, note that we can view the extension above as an h2 -extension from
q02 , which detects h20 , to h30 , which detects h31 . Since q02 lies in filtration 2 while h30 lies in filtration
0, we see that factors of τ are needed to label the “mismatch” in filtrations. However, we still need
only a single factor, even though the difference in CE filtrations is now 2 instead of 1.
