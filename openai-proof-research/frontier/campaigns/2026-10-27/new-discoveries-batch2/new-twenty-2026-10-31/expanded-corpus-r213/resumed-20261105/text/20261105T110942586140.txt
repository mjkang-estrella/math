                                           1. Introduction
     Let S be a closed oriented surface of genus g ≥ 2, and let RPSLn (R) (S) denote the representation-
variety of PSLn (R)-representations of the surface-group π1 (S), up to conjugation. For n = 2, there
is a component comprising discrete, faithful representations (i.e. Fuchsian representations), which
can be identified with the classical Teichmüller space T (S) since they arise as the holonomy of
hyperbolic structures on S. For n > 2, Hitchin ([Hit92]) showed the existence of a component
that we denote by Hitn (S) in RPSLn (R) (S) diffeomorphic to a ball, that Labourie ([Lab06]) proved
comprises discrete-faithful representations that are Anosov in the sense that the dynamics of a flow
on the associated Rn -bundle on T 1 S is Anosov. In [FG06], Fock-Goncharov introduced the notion
of positivity, and provided an alternative proof of Labourie’s result. The study of this Hitchin
component and exploring the analogy with Teichmüller space is the subject of the active field of
higher Teichmüller theory (see, for example, [Wie19]).
     In this article, we consider complex deformations of Hitchin representations in the variety
RPSLn (C) (S) of representations of the surface-group into PSLn (C), obtained by “bending” a Hitchin
representation along a maximal geodesic lamination λ, and we first prove a domination result for
the length spectra (of closed curves) – see Theorem 1.2. Our preceding paper [BG25] had handled
the case when the surface S has punctures, using Fock-Goncharov coordinates on the correspond-
ing representation-variety RPSLn (C) (S). When S is a closed surface (as in this paper), analogous
coordinates for Hitn (S) were introduced by Bonahon-Dreyer ([BD17]), in which Hitchin represen-
tations form a convex cone in an appropriate space of shear-cocycles (see §2 for a brief account).
Here, we shall use the recent extension of this by Maloni-Martone-Mazzoli-Zhang ([MMMZ26])
that parametrizes a subset R(λ) ⊂ RPSLn (C) (S) of “n-pleated” representations, by “shear-bend”
cocycles (valued in C/2πiZ) transverse to λ. In what follows, we say that ρ ∈ R(λ) is obtained by
bending ρ0 along λ if the corresponding difference of shear-bend cocycles is purely imaginary; the
set of representations is called the bending fiber (see §2 for details).

     The strategy of the proof generalizes that in the preceding article [BG25]; in particular, the
dominating Hitchin representation ρ0 is the one obtained by setting the imaginary part of the
shear-bend cocycle to zero. In case of a punctured-surface, we had taken the modulus of each
Fock-Goncharov parameter; moreover, the “maximal lamination” we had considered there was an
ideal triangulation, with finitely many leaves. Here, in the case of a maximal lamination on a closed
surface, we are forced to consider transverse arcs crossing infinitely many (possibly uncountable)
leaves; we rely on the methods of [MMMZ26] that generalize work of Bonahon (for PSL2 (C)-
representations) and handle the corresponding convergence issues. In particular, we develop a
method to augment the finite product of building blocks used in [BG25] by finite approximants to
the “slithering map” of [MMMZ26]. We then show that these approximants are weight matrices
of weighted planar networks, and are compatible with taking the real part of the cocycle; the
domination argument in [BG25] then applies and passes to the limit.
     A key consequence of the domination result will be an entropy rigidity result for quasi-Hitchin
representations, namely those obtained by bending deformations of Hitchin representations that
are still Borel-Anosov (see Definition 2.5).

                                          2. Preliminaries
2.1. Shear-bend coordinates for PSL2 (C)-representations. It is well-known that the Te-
ichmüller space T (S), comprising Fuchsian representations in PSL2 (R), is diffeomorphic to a ball
6                              PABITRA BARMAN AND SUBHOJOY GUPTA


of dimension 6g − 6, and one of the parametrizations is via shear coordinates with respect to a
maximal lamination on the surface.
      Recall that a geodesic lamination on S, equipped with a choice of a hyperbolic metric, is a
closed set that is a union of disjoint simple complete geodesics (either closed or bi-infinite), and
it is maximal if its complement comprises ideal triangles (necessarily 4g − 4 in number). In the
case that the maximal lamination λ has finitely many leaves, the shear coordinates for a hyperbolic
surface (an element of T (S)) are simple to describe: realizing leaves of λ as geodesics, the two
ideal hyperbolic triangles adjacent to a bi-infinite leaf differ by a real-valued shear, measured by
the distance between the points on the leaf where the altitudes from the two centroids intersect.
(See [BBFS13] for an account.)
      More generally, a maximal lamination λ could have uncountably many leaves, such that a
transverse arc intersects λ in a Cantor set; in this case one can record the mutual shears as a
real-valued transverse cocycle (see [Bon01]), and T (S) is diffeomorphic to a finite-sided cone C(λ)
in the vector space of such cocycles H(λ; R) (see Theorem A in [Bon96]).
      Bonahon extended this to parametrizing subsets of PSL2 (C)-representations obtained by bend-
ing a Fuchsian representation along leaves of λ; in the universal cover H2 of a hyperbolic surface,
this bending can be described in geometric terms, by equivariantly bending a totally-geodesic copy
of H2 in H3 along the leaves of the lift λ̃. The resulting surface is a piecewise totally-geodesic pleated
plane in H3 (see Definition 4.3), equivariant under a new surface-group representation into PSL2 (C);
such surfaces were introduced by Thurston in the context of discrete PSL2 (C)-representations (i.e.
Kleinian groups) and are an important tool in the study of ends of hyperbolic 3-manifolds.
      In particular, Bonahon introduced shear-bend coordinates with respect to λ, for such pleated
surfaces (and their corresponding PSL2 (C)-representations), measured by complex -valued cocycles
in the vector space H(λ; C/2πiZ) of C/2πiZ-valued cocycles. (Note that a 2π-bend does not affect
the representation, which explains quotient by 2πiZ.) He proved:
Theorem 2.1 (Bonahon, [Bon96]). The subset of PSL2 (C)-representations pleated along λ is bi-
holomorphic to
                         C(λ) + iH(λ; R/2πZ) ⊂ H(λ; C/2πiZ)
where C(λ) is the cone parametrizing Teichmüller space T (S).
2.2. Bonahon-Dreyer parametrization. In [BD14], Bonahon-Dreyer gave a parametrization of
the Hitchin component Hitn (S) that generalized the shear parameters of Teichmüller space; before
we state their result, we note two earlier developments alluded to in the Introduction:
       First, after the work of Labourie ([Lab06]), one salient feature of a Hitchin representation to
PSLn (R) has been the associated limit map ξ : ∂∞ S̃ → F(Rn ) from the ideal boundary circle of
the universal cover to the space of complete flags in F(Rn ).
       Second, in [FG06], Fock-Goncharov proved that this limit map ξ has the property that for
any cyclically ordered triple of points x, y, z ∈ ∂∞ S̃, the images ξ(x), ξ(y) and ξ(z) are in general
position, and in fact determine a positive triple of flags in F(Rn ), a notion they introduced inspired
by Lusztig’s notion of total positivity. Indeed, for any such triple of flags, they defined a collection
of 21 (n − 1)(n − 2) positive real numbers that characterized the triple up to the action of PSLn (R).
       Thus, given a maximal lamination λ on S, the ideal vertices of the lift of a complementary ideal
triangle to the universal cover determine a positive triple of flags via the limit map ξ. Bonahon-
Dreyer’s parameters then correspond to the 21 (n − 1)(n − 2) “triangle-invariants” of such a triple for
each complementary ideal triangle, together with a finite-sided cone in a vector space H(λ; Rn−1 )
of Rn−1 -valued shearing cocycles. We record their result as:
                 ENTROPY AND DOMINATION FOR QUASI-HITCHIN REPRESENTATIONS                               7


Theorem 2.2 (Bonahon-Dreyer, Theorem 0.4 of [BD17]). For λ a maximal lamination, Hitn (S)
is homeomorphic to a finite-sided cone C(λ, n) ⊂ RT × H(λ; Rn−1 ), where T is the total number of
triangle invariants.
     The shearing cocycle for a given Hitchin representation can be determined by the triangle-
invariants of triples of flags and “double-invariants” of quadruple of flags (the latter invariant
was introduced by Fock-Goncharov and generalizes the classical notion of cross-ratio). The exact
definition is in terms of the slithering map, as in the work of [MMMZ26] that we shall refer to later.

2.4. Snakes and the monodromy formula. We recall the monodromy formula expressing the
holonomy ρ(γ) of a loop γ in terms of Fock–Goncharov coordinates (i.e. the triangle and edge
invariants) with respect to an ideal triangulation T (see [FG06] and [BG25]). Note that an ideal
triangulation is a special example of a maximal lamination, which has finitely many leaves (the
edges of T ).
     Assume that the oriented loop γ is in general position with respect to T , so that γ crosses edges
of T transversely and avoids the vertices. Lifting γ to the universal cover, we obtain a sequence of
ideal triangles t1 , t2 , . . . , tk and edges e1 , e2 , . . . , ek crossed successively by the lifted path. To each
triangle ti and each edge ei one associates matrices T (ti ), E(ei ) ∈ SLn (C) defined in terms of the
corresponding triangle and edge invariants. These matrices change the projective basis associated
with one side of T to another, where the projective basis associated with a side is the one in which
the two endpoint flags become the standard flag and its opposite flag.
     The monodromy formula then asserts that the holonomy of γ is given, up to conjugation, by
the ordered product
(2.1)                      ρ(γ) = T (t1 )δ1 E(e1 ) T (t2 )δ2 E(e2 ) · · · T (tk )δk E(ek ),
where each δi ∈ {−1, 1} records whether γ turns left/right when passing through the corresponding
ideal triangle. We refer to [BG25] for a more detailed exposition (with examples and figures);
as in that paper, we normalize a matrix in PSLn (C) to obtain a lift in SLn (C) by choosing the
determinant-one representative in the projective class.
Snakes and the triangle matrix. Let P be an ideal triangle with an ordered triple of flags
(A, B, C) at its vertices, and let t denote its 21 (n − 1)(n − 2) triangle invariants. Consider the

2.5. Planar networks for building-blocks.
Definition 2.6. A weighted planar network (Γ, ω) is a finite acyclic directed planar graph Γ, with
n sources on the left boundary and n sinks on the right boundary, and with a weight function
ω : E → C defined on the set of directed edges. The weight matrix W(Γ, ω) of such a network is
the n × n matrix whose (i, j)–entry is the sum of the weights of all directed paths from source i to
sink j, where the weight of a path is the product of the weights of its edges.
     One fundamental result concerning weighted planar networks is the following result attributed
to Lindström ([Lin73], see [CN12, Lemma 7] or [FZ00, Lemma 1] for the proof). In what follows, a
vertex-disjoint family of paths is a collection of paths (in a given planar network), no two of which
have a common vertex; its weight is defined to be the product of the weights of the paths in the
family (for an example see [BG25, Example 6]).
Lemma 2.7 (Lindström’s lemma). For I, J ⊂ {1, 2, , . . . , n} where |I| = |J|, any minor ∆I,J of
the weight matrix of a planar network is equal to the sum of weights of all vertex-disjoint families
of paths from the sources indexed by I to the sinks indexed by J.
    In particular, in the case that all weights are real and positive, the weight matrix is totally
nonnegative (see, for example, [FZ00]).
The key observation in [BG25] is that:

Lemma 2.8 ([BG25]). Each building block in the decomposition (2.1) is a weight matrix of a
weighted planar network. Moreover, the weights are positive Laurent monomials in the Fock-
Goncharov coordinates (i.e., products of some of the coordinates and their reciprocals with positive
coefficients).
Sketch of the proof. Recall that from Fock-Goncharov’s theory of snakes (described in the previous
subsection), each building block appearing in (2.1) is of the form T (t)±1 E(e).
     The case when the building block is T (t)E(e), is simpler: In the decomposition T (t) = M (t)S
(see (2.2)), the matrix M (t) is a product of elementary matrices Fi and Hi (X). Each of these
elementary matrices is the weight matrix of an explicit planar network (see [BG25, Figure 16])
whose weights are exactly the triangle invariants or 1. Concatenating these planar networks yields
a weighted planar network whose weight matrix is M (t).
     Moreover, the matrix S · E(e) is diagonal and is realized by a planar network consisting of a
disjoint union of horizontal edges weighted by its diagonal entries. Since concatenation of planar
networks corresponds to the multiplication of their weight matrices, concatenating the networks
for M (t) and S · E(e) yields the planar network for T (t)E(e). By construction, these individual
networks only use triangle invariants, edge invariants, and 1s as their edge weights. Therefore, the
resulting concatenated network inherently carries weights that are positive Laurent monomials in
the Fock-Goncharov coordinates.
     For the case of the building block T (t)−1 E(e), the relation S 2 = ±I (depending on the parity of
n) is used to write T (t)−1 E(e) = S·M (t)−1 ·S·S·E(e). Constructing the network for M (t)−1 directly
introduces negative entries. To resolve this, M (t)−1 is factored into individual steps corresponding
to elementary snake moves.
     By multiplying each step on both sides by the matrix S, matrices of the form S · Step(k)(t) · S
are obtained, which map to planar networks with strictly positive weights (that are products of
the Fock-Goncharov coordinates and their reciprocals, see [BG25, Lemma 3.9]). Concatenating
these step networks with the network for S · E(e) yields the required weighted planar network for
T (t)−1 E(e) (see Figure 1). Since the weights of the individual step networks are already of the
form asserted, the final concatenated network naturally preserves the property that all weights are
positive Laurent monomials.                                                                          □

                                    3. Proof of the domination result
     The proof of this follows the strategy in our preceding paper [BG25]; however, in our former
setting, when S was a punctured surface, the “maximal lamination” we were considering formed an
ideal triangulation, and therefore had finitely many leaves. As observed before, a general maximal
lamination could have infinitely many leaves, and to extend our previous argument we develop here
a method of approximating monodromy by weight matrices of weighted planar networks, using
some of the technical machinery in [MMMZ26].
     We shall first recall the proof of the domination result in [BG25] for a finite lamination in
§3.1; this uses planar networks and the monodromy formula introduced in the previous section.
Next, in §3.2, we shall describe the analogue of the monodromy formula in the closed surface case
with “generalized building blocks” that involve the slithering map. In §3.3, we shall introduce the
corresponding planar networks, and in §3.4 we shall complete the proof of Theorem 1.2.

3.1. Punctured surface case. In this section assume that S is a punctured surface, and ρ : π1 (S) →

Definition 3.10 (Transparent network). A weighted planar network of order n is said to be
transparent if it contains a family of n distinguished paths hr from source r to sink r for each
