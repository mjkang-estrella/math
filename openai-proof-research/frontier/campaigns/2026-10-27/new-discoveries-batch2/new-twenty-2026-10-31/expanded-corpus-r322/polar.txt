 Abstract

 
 We show that the polar degrees of the coordinate-wise inverse of a linear subspace L ⊆ ℙ n L\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n} are given by the coefficients of a substitution of the reduced characteristic polynomial of the associated matroid M ⁡ ( L ) \Matroid(L) . Our proof connects the geometry of conormal varieties of reciprocal linear spaces to the combinatorial conormal fan of M ⁡ ( L ) \Matroid(L) . As a corollary, we settle two open conjectures regarding matroid discriminants.

 1 Introduction


 
 Let L ⊆ ℙ n L\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n} be a linear subspace of dimension d d not contained in any coordinate hyperplane and consider the rational Cremona map 

 

 
 
 ℙ n ⇢ ℙ n , [ x 0 : … : x n ] ↦ [ x 0 − 1 : … : x n − 1 ] . \mathbb{P}^{n}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n},\qquad[x_{0}:\dots:x_{n}]\mapsto[x_{0}^{-1}:\dots:x_{n}^{-1}]. 
 
 
 
 The closure of the image of L L under this map is denoted L − 1 L^{-1} and is called the reciprocal linear space associated to L L . This a projective variety whose geometric properties are closely related to the realisable matroid M M represented by L L . Reciprocal linear spaces have been widely studied both from the viewpoint of likelihood geometry in algebraic statistics and scattering equations [ 20 , 15 , 9 , 21 ] and from the viewpoint of matroid theory [ 27 , 31 , 19 , 16 ] . In this article we study L − 1 L^{-1} through the lens of projective duality. The central object in our work is the conormal variety Con ⁡ ( L − 1 ) ⊆ ℙ n × ( ℙ n ) ∗ \Con(L^{-1})\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}\times(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{*} whose general points are pairs ( p , H ) (p,H) where p ∈ L − 1 p\in L^{-1} is a smooth point and H ∈ ( ℙ n ) ∗ H\in(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{*} is a hyperplanes tangent to L − 1 L^{-1} at p p . The projectively dual variety ( L − 1 ) ∨ (L^{-1})^{\vee} of L − 1 L^{-1} is the image of Con ⁡ ( L − 1 ) \Con(L^{-1}) under the projection to the second factor ( ℙ n ) ∗ (\mathbb{P}^{n})^{*} .



 
 Our initial motivation for studying these varieties stems from a collection of recent conjectures by Matsubara-Heo and Telen in their work on principal matroid determinants [ 26 ] . They introduce an analogue of the theory of principal A A -determinants for toric varieties by Gelfand, Kapranov and Zelevinsky [ 18 ] to the setting of reciprocal linear spaces, thereby defining the principal matroid determinant E L E_{L} . The key idea is that the stratification of toric varieties into orbits is replaced by the stratification of reciprocal linear spaces into strata corresponding to flats of the corresponding matroid M ≔ M ⁡ ( L ) M\coloneq\Matroid(L) . The main theorem [ 26 , Theorem 1.2] shows how the principal matroid determinant decomposes into a union of dual varieties of reciprocal linear spaces of minors of M M . The following three conjectures about these factors and their multiplicities in E L E_{L} were left open.



 
 1.1 [ 26 , Conjecture 7.2] . 

 
 The reciprocal linear space L − 1 L^{-1} is dual defective, i.e. dim ( L − 1 ) ∨ < n − 1 \dim\,(L^{-1})^{\vee}<n-1 ,
if and only if the matroid M M is not connected.





 
 1.2 [ 26 , Conjecture 7.3] . 

 
 If the matroid M M is connected, then ( L − 1 ) ∨ (L^{-1})^{\vee} is a hypersurface of degree 2 d ​ β ​ ( M ) 2^{d}\beta(M) , where β ⁡ ( M ) \beta(M) is the beta invariant of M M .





 
 1.3 [ 26 , Conjecture 7.1] . 

 
 For each flat F F of M M , such that ( L | F − 1 ) ∨ (L|_{F}^{-1})^{\vee} is a hypersurface, the defining polynomial of that hypersurface appears in the factorization of E L E_{L} with exponent equal to the local multiplicity of L − 2 L^{-2} along the stratum indexed by F F . Here L − 2 L^{-2} denotes the image of L − 1 L^{-1} under the coordinate-wise squaring map.





 
 In this article we shall prove and strengthen the first two conjectures by describing the class of Con ⁡ ( L − 1 ) \Con(L^{-1}) in the Chow ring of ℙ n × ( ℙ n ) ∗ \mathbb{P}^{n}\times(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{*} , in other words, the sequence of polar degrees of L − 1 L^{-1} . Indeed, the dual degree is the last nonzero polar degree and we can read off dual defectiveness from the number of nonzero polar degrees. This motivates our first main theorem, which provides a combinatorial formula for these polar degrees.



 
 To state it, let χ ¯ M ​ ( t ) ∈ ℤ ​ [ t ] \overline{\chi}_{M}(t)\in\mathbb{Z}[t] denote the reduced characteristic polynomial of the matroid M M . We also write μ i ​ ( L − 1 ) \mu_{i}(L^{-1}) for the i i -th polar degree of L − 1 L^{-1} , where the indexing is chosen such that μ 0 ​ ( L − 1 ) = deg ⁡ L − 1 \mu_{0}(L^{-1})=\deg L^{-1} . Using this notation we show the following.



 
 Theorem A . 

 
 Let L ⊆ ℙ n L\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n} be a linear subspace of dimension 0 ≤ d < n 0\leq d<n that is not contained in any coordinate hyperplane. Then 

 

 
 
 ∑ j = 0 d μ j ​ ( L − 1 ) ​ t j = ( − 2 ​ t − 1 ) d ​ χ ¯ M ​ ( 2 ​ t 2 ​ t + 1 ) . \sum_{j=0}^{d}\mu_{j}(L^{-1})t^{j}=(-2t-1)^{d}\,\overline{\chi}_{M}\left(\frac{2t}{2t+1}\right). 
 
 
 
 Equivalently, setting δ i ​ ( L − 1 ) ≔ μ d − i ​ ( L − 1 ) \delta_{i}(L^{-1})\coloneq\mu_{d-i}(L^{-1}) we obtain 

 

 
 
 ∑ i = 0 d δ i ​ ( L − 1 ) ​ t i = ( − t − 2 ) d ​ χ ¯ M ​ ( 2 t + 2 ) . \sum_{i=0}^{d}\delta_{i}(L^{-1})t^{i}=(-t-2)^{d}\,\overline{\chi}_{M}\left(\frac{2}{t+2}\right). 
 
 
 




 
 Corollary 1.4 . 

 
 Conjectures   1.1 and  1.2 hold true. 





 
 From the viewpoint of the principal matroid determinant E L E_{L} , this describes which factors actually appear and what their degrees are in the factorization of E L E_{L} due to [ 26 , Theorem 1.2] . The remaining Conjecture   1.3 concerns the multiplicities of these factors within E L E_{L} and hence would finish the complete description of the factorization of E L E_{L} . The resolution of Conjecture   1.3 is the topic of our future work [ 11 ] .



 
 Especially in recent years, expressing combinatorial invariants as geometric and tropical intersection numbers turned out to be a powerful tool for resolving longstanding conjectures in matroid theory [ 19 , 1 , 10 , 5 ] . The theorem above can be seen as another work in this line of tradition as it expresses the coefficients of a substitution of χ ¯ M \overline{\chi}_{M} as intersection numbers on the conormal variety. Following this theme, Theorem   A could be used to show the log-concavity of the coefficients of ( − 2 ​ t − 1 ) d ​ χ ¯ M ​ ( 2 ​ t 2 ​ t + 1 ) (-2t-1)^{d}\overline{\chi}_{M}\left(\frac{2t}{2t+1}\right) for realizable matroids. We note however that this log-concavity follows more directly from the log-concavity of the coefficients of χ ¯ M \overline{\chi}_{M} which was proven in the groundbreaking work of Huh and Katz for realizable matroids [ 19 ] and Adiprasito, Huh and Katz for all matroids [ 1 ] .



 
 Our proof of Theorem   A uses a rational parametrization of Con ⁡ ( L − 1 ) \Con(L^{-1}) described in [ 26 ] , akin to the Horn–Kapranov uniformization. We resolve the base locus of this map by passing from a product of projective spaces to the bipermutohedral variety . The latter is a smooth projective toric variety whose corresponding fan hosts the conormal fan of every matroid, introduced in [ 5 ] . We prove Theorem   A in three steps that can be summarized as follows:



 
 
 1. 
 
 Using tropical methods we pass from the rational parametrization of Con ⁡ ( L − 1 ) \Con(L^{-1}) to a similar, but slightly simpler map with a different image while tracking how this affects polar degrees ( Section   3.1 ).




 2. 
 
 We show that these new degrees can be computed as intersection numbers on the bipermutohedral variety that involve the class of the conormal fan of M M . From this we derive a first combinatorial formula which sums over all decreasing flags of flats of M M ( Section   3.2 ).




 3. 
 
 Finally, we extract a recursion for the complicated combinatorial formula obtained in the previous step to relate the polar degrees to a substitution of the reduced characteristic polynomial χ ¯ M \overline{\chi}_{M} ( Section   3.3 ).




 


 
 The structure of this article is as follows. Section   2 serves as an introduction to the tools that we will use in proving Theorem   A . We also provide direct proofs of Conjectures   1.1 and  1.2 . Especially the latter showcases our technique in a slightly simpler setting before we apply it to the conormal variety. The detailed proof of Theorem   A is then presented in Section   3 , which is subdivided further into the three parts mentioned above. We finish the article providing several applications of Theorem   A in Section   4 . This section contains examples, a discussion of Chern–Mather degrees of L − 1 L^{-1} and a generalization of Theorem   A to arbitrary negative powers of linear spaces L − m L^{-m} , m ≥ 1 m\geq 1 .

 2 Preliminaries and dual degree


 
 Throughout this article, L ⊆ ℙ n L\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n} is a projective subspace of dimension 0 ≤ d < n 0\leq d<n . We work over an algebraically closed field 𝕂 \mathbb{K} of characteristic 0 see however Example   4.3 for a comment on this assumption. We always fix projective coordinates ℙ n = ℙ ⁡ ( 𝕂 E ) \mathbb{P}^{n}=\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{K}^{E}) , E = { 0 , … , n } E=\{0,\dots,n\} and assume that L L is not contained in a coordinate subspace. Unless otherwise specified, M M is the matroid on the ground set E E associated to L L .



 
 2.1 Matroids


 
 In this section we collect the required combinatorial preliminaries. Namely, we recall how to associate a matroid to a linear space, as well as some important properties of matroids. This construction is what allows us to study the geometric properties of reciprocal linear spaces from a combinatorial point of view.
For a thorough introduction to matroids, we refer to [ 28 ] .



 
 We start by recalling the definition of a matroid.



 
 Definition 2.1 . 

 
 A matroid is a pair M = ( E , ℬ ) M=(E,\mathcal{B}) where E E is a finite set, called the ground set of M M , and ℬ \mathcal{B} is a nonempty family of subsets of E E , called the bases of M M , such that the basis exchange axiom holds: 

 

 
 
 ∀ B 1 , B 2 ∈ ℬ : ∀ e 1 ∈ B 1 ∖ B 2 : ∃ e 2 ∈ B 2 ∖ B 1 : ( B 1 ∖ e 1 ) ∪ { e 2 } ∈ ℬ . \forall B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathcal{B}:\forall e_{1}\in B_{1}\setminus B_{2}:\exists e_{2}\in B_{2}\setminus B_{1}:(B_{1}\setminus e_{1})\cup\{e_{2}\}\in\mathcal{B}. 
 
 
 




 
 We say that a subset S ⊆ E S\subseteq E is independent if it is contained in a basis, otherwise we call S S dependent . A circuit of M M is an independent subset S S which is inclusion-minimal among all independent subsets. We further denote the rank of any subset S ⊆ E S\subseteq E by rk M ⁡ ( S ) ≔ max B ∈ ℬ ⁡ | S ∩ B | \rank_{M}(S)\coloneq\max_{B\in\mathcal{B}}|S\cap B| . For S = E S=E we also simply write rk ⁡ ( M ) ≔ rk M ⁡ ( E ) \rank(M)\coloneq\rank_{M}(E) , which is the cardinality of any basis of M M . By cl M ​ ( S ) ≔ { x ∈ E | rk M ⁡ ( S ∪ { e } ) = rk M ⁡ ( S ) } \text{cl}_{M}(S)\coloneq\set{x\in E}{\rank_{M}(S\cup\{e\})=\rank_{M}(S)} we denote the closure of S S in M M . A subset F ⊆ E F\subseteq E is called a flat of M M if F = cl M ​ ( F ) F=\text{cl}_{M}(F) . The groundset E E is always a flat and cl M ​ ( ∅ ) \text{cl}_{M}(\emptyset) is unique flat of rank zero. Its elements are called loops . The beauty of matroid theory lies in the fact that most of the objects defined in this paragraph (bases, independent sets, circuits, rank function, closure, …) can be used to give “cryptomorphic definitions” of matroids.



 
 Below we also work with flags of flats , these are collections ℱ \mathcal{F} of flats which are pairwise comparable under inclusion ℱ = { F 1 ⊆ ⋯ ⊆ F k } \mathcal{F}=\{F_{1}\subseteq\dots\subseteq F_{k}\} .
The size of such a collection is called the length of ℱ \mathcal{F} and denoted by | ℱ | ≔ k |\mathcal{F|}\coloneq k . When working with loopless matroids we usually assume the flats in a flag to be non-empty.



 
 As the terminology suggests, matroids are inspired by independence of vectors in linear algebra. We now explain how to associate a matroid M ⁡ ( L ) \Matroid(L) to a linear subspace L ⊆ ℙ ⁡ ( 𝕂 E ) L\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{K}^{E}) , E ≔ { 0 , … , n } E\coloneq\{0,\dots,n\} .
Pick a matrix A ∈ 𝕂 ( d + 1 ) × ( n + 1 ) A\in\mathbb{K}^{(d+1)\times(n+1)} such that d = dim L d=\dim L and its projectivized row span is L L , and label the columns of A A by the elements of E E . These correspond to the n + 1 n+1 coordinate linear functions restricted to L ^ \widehat{L} . We declare that a subset of E E is independent if and only if the column vectors (hence the linear forms) it indexes are linearly independent. In particular, the bases of M M correspond to non-vanishing maximal minors of A A .
This construction is independent of the choice of the matrix A A . Indeed, if A ′ ∈ 𝕂 ( d + 1 ) × ( n + 1 ) A^{\prime}\in\mathbb{K}^{(d+1)\times(n+1)} also has row span L L , then there exists B ∈ GL d + 1 ⁡ ( 𝕂 ) B\in\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}(\mathbb{K}) such that A ′ = B ​ A A^{\prime}=BA .



 
 Operations on vector spaces also have matroid analogues. In this paper, we will mostly be concerned with the following operation.
Let L ^ ⊂ 𝕂 n + 1 \widehat{L}\subset\mathbb{K}^{n+1} denote the affine cone over L L , and L ⟂ ≔ ℙ ⁡ ( L ^ ⟂ ) ⊆ ( ℙ n ) ∗ L^{\perp}\coloneq\mathbb{P}(\widehat{L}^{\perp})\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{*} the orthogonal complement of L L . The matroid M ⟂ ≔ M ⁡ ( L ⟂ ) M^{\perp}\coloneq\Matroid(L^{\perp}) is called the dual matroid of M M . More generally, for any matroid M M , its dual M ⟂ M^{\perp} is defined as a matroid with the same ground set as M M , whose bases are the complements of the bases of M M .

 2.2 Polar degrees and conormal varieties


 
 We now switch gears and recall the fundamentals of projective duality, the most important of which are the definition of conormal variety and of polar degrees. For more details on projective duality we refer to [ 18 , 22 ] , for background on Chow groups and (multi)degrees see [ 17 ] .



 
 We start by defining polar classes. Let X ⊂ ℙ n X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n} be a projective variety of dimension d d . Denote by X reg X_{\mathrm{reg}} the dense open set of regular points of X X . Let Λ ⊆ ℙ n \Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n} be a linear subspace and consider the polar variety

 For general ( n − d + k − 2 ) (n-d+k-2) -dimensional subspaces Λ \Lambda , this algebraic subset is empty or equidimensional of the expected codimension codim X ⁡ P Λ ​ ( X ) = k \codim_{X}P_{\Lambda}(X)=k . Its class in the Chow group A d − k ​ ( X ) \mathrm{A}_{d-k}(X) is independent of the general choice of Λ \Lambda .



 
 Definition 2.5 . 

 
 The polar classes of X ⊆ ℙ n X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n} are the classes P k ​ ( X ) = [ P Λ ​ ( X ) ] ∈ A dim X − k ​ ( X ) P_{k}(X)=[P_{\Lambda}(X)]\in\mathrm{A}_{\dim X-k}(X) for general [ Λ ] ∈ 𝔾 ⁡ ( n − d + k − 2 , ℙ n ) [\Lambda]\in\PGr(n-d+k-2,\mathbb{P}^{n}) , k = 0 , … , d k=0,\dots,d . The polar degrees of X X are the degrees μ k ​ ( X ) ≔ deg ℙ n ⁡ P k ​ ( X ) \mu_{k}(X)\coloneq\deg_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}P_{k}(X) . 





 
 For example, P 0 ​ ( X ) = [ X ] ∈ A d ​ ( X ) P_{0}(X)=[X]\in\mathrm{A}_{d}(X) , hence μ 0 ​ ( X ) = deg ⁡ X \mu_{0}(X)=\deg X . Our main result Theorem   A concerns the polar degrees of the variety L − 1 L^{-1} .
For more background on polar degrees and their place in projective geometry, see for example [ 29 , 30 ] .



 
 A useful approach to studying polar degrees of a projective variety X ⊆ ℙ n X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n} is via its conormal variety

 This is an irreducible biprojective variety of dimension n − 1 n-1 .
The dual variety X ∨ X^{\vee} is the image of Con ⁡ ( X ) \Con(X) under the projection pr 2 : Con ⁡ ( X ) → ( ℙ n ) ∗ \operatorname{pr}_{2}\colon\Con(X)\rightarrow(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{*} .
It is the Zariski closure in ( ℙ n ) ∗ (\mathbb{P}^{n})^{*} of the set of hyperplanes of ℙ n \mathbb{P}^{n} that are tangent to X X at a smooth point.

 2.4 The bipermutohedral fan and dual degrees


 
 In this section we recall some of the results from [ 5 ] which will be useful in our setting to compute the polar degrees of reciprocal linear spaces. Here the corresponding combinatorial problem has already been solved in [ 5 ] and hence we obtain a closed formula for the dual degree of L − 1 L^{-1} for any linear space L ⊆ ℙ n L\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n} .  3 Proof of the main theorem


 
 This section contains all details for the proof of our main theorem and it is divided into three parts.



 
 The first part relates the polar degrees of L − 1 L^{-1} to the bidegrees of the class [ ϕ ⁡ ( L × L ⟂ ) ¯ ] [\overline{\phi(L\times L^{\perp})}] . This is precisely the analog of Proposition   2.19 where we did the same already for the dual variety ( L − 1 ) ∨ (L^{-1})^{\vee} . We will again use tropical methods to extract a power of 2 from the polar degrees.



 
 The second part focuses on computing the bidegrees of the class [ ϕ ⁡ ( L × L ⟂ ) ¯ ] [\overline{\phi(L\times L^{\perp})}] using Proposition   2.28 . We will work in the Chow ring A ∙ ​ ( Σ M , M ⟂ ) \mathrm{A}^{\bullet}(\Sigma_{M,M^{\perp}}) of the conormal fan of the matroid M M to obtain a combinatorial formula summing over flags of flats in the matroid M M and involving beta invariants of minors of M M . In the case of the dual ( L − 1 ) ∨ (L^{-1})^{\vee} , this was already done by [ 5 , Proposition 4.9] . We note that the arguments in this subsection work for all matroids M M , irregardless of their representability over 𝕂 \mathbb{K} .



 
 In the final part we rewrite the combinatorial formula obtained in part two in terms of the reduced characteristic polynomial of the matroid M M which allows us to arrive at the clean formula presented in Theorem   A . In this way we conclude the proof of that theorem.



 
 We will assume in Sections   3.1 and  3.2 that M M is coloopless (and loopless). The main theorem does not have this assumption, we will do this reduction in the proof of the theorem.
