 Abstract

 
 We analyze Column-Row Impartial Merge (CRIM), an impartial combinatorial game played on integer partitions.
A move in CRIM consists of removing an arbitrary row or column from the corresponding Young diagram, with the remaining parts reattaching to form a single partition.
We define rectairs — a common generalization of rectangles and staircases — and characterize their 𝒫 / 𝒩 \mathcal{P}/\mathcal{N} -status.
We define the meld operation on partitions and show that the meld of losing rectairs is losing.
We introduce Odds-Are-Even (OAE) and Evens-Are-Odd (EAO) partitions, proving that all OAE partitions are 𝒫 \mathcal{P} -positions and characterizing the losing positions within EAO partitions.
We determine the 𝒫 / 𝒩 \mathcal{P}/\mathcal{N} -status for staircases and for 2 2 - and 3 3 -part partitions.
We evaluate CRIM and its restrictions to certain partition families within the Conway-Gurvich-Ho classification scheme, establishing that CRIM is neither returnable nor domestic.
We conjecture that every losing partition has even rank.

 1 Introduction


 
 Combinatorial games have been studied extensively since the pioneering work of Bouton, Sprague, and Grundy Bouton (1901) ; Sprague (1935) ; Sprague (1937) ; Grundy (1939) . In his book On Numbers and Games Conway (2001) , Conway extended and generalized this work and placed it in a broader theoretical framework.
These foundational results have shaped the study of impartial games, providing tools to determine whether a position is winning or losing (i.e. 𝒫 / 𝒩 \mathcal{P}/\mathcal{N} status), to decompose complex games, and to identify winning strategies.
Some well-known combinatorial games (e.g. Nim , Sato-Welter , and Wythoff ) admit interpretations in which positions are (integer) partitions.



 
 Partitions play important roles in number theory, combinatorics, representation theory, and mathematical physics
 (1986) ; Agarwala and Auluck (1951) ; Sagan (2001) ; Auluck and Kothari (1946) 
and have been studied by mathematicians including Euler Euler (1797) , Erdős Erdös (1946) , Hardy and Ramanujan Hardy and Ramanujan (1918) , Rademacher Rademacher (1938) , and Andrews Andrews (1998) ; Andrews (2018) .
In 2018 and 2021,
Bloom and Saracino Bloom and Saracino (2018) and Bloom and McNew Bloom and McNew (2021) studied column and row deletion of partitions in the context of rook polynomials, rook equivalence, and Wilf equivalence.
In this article, we will study Column-Row Impartial Merge ( CRIM ), another combinatorial game on partitions, in which row and column deletion play an essential role.



 
 1.1 Related work


 
 The study of games played on partitions bridges combinatorial game theory with the elegant theory of partitions, a cornerstone of number theory and enumerative combinatorics.
The first such game that we know of was studied by Sato Sato (1970) , who showed that Welter’s game can be formulated in this way and conjectured that its Sprague-Grundy values relate to the representation theory of the symmetric group.
In 2018, Irie Irie (2018) confirmed Sato’s conjecture.
Subsequent work by Abuku and Tada Abuku and Tada (2023) and Motegi Motegi (2021) extended these results.



 
 Since then, a number of researchers have explored other combinatorial games on partitions: LCTR by several authors Gottlieb et al. (2023) ; Gottlieb et al. (2024b) , CRIM by Bašić Bašić et al. (2022) ; Bašić (2023) , Nim on partitions and hyperrectangles Gottlieb et al. (2025b) , and a suite of chess-inspired impartial games, collectively called Impartial Chess , by Berlekamp Berlekamp (2017) and others Gottlieb et al. (2025a) .
In her honors thesis, Meit Meit (2025) studied CRPM and CRPS , two partizan combinatorial games on partitions.



 
 Conway  Conway (2001) introduced the notion of a tame game. Gurvich and Ho  Gurvich and Ho (2018) extended this idea to a more general classification of games. The Conway-Gurvich-Ho classification of the games Nim , Subtraction , Wythoff , Mark , LCTR , PNim , and Downright are known; see Conway (2001) ; Gottlieb et al. (2024b) ; Gottlieb et al. (2025b) ; Gurvich and Ho (2018) and Fig.   6 .

 2.3 Combinatorial games and their classification 


 
 In this paper we consider finite (short) impartial games, that is, players have the same moves available to them throughout a game, and after finitely many steps the games concludes with a uniquely defined winner.
Throughout the paper all positions in the games are partitions.



 
 A winning position , also known as an 𝒩 \mathcal{N} -position, is a position in which the next player can guarantee a win.
Conversely, a losing position , equivalently a 𝒫 \mathcal{P} -position, is a position from which the previous player can guarantee a win. Whenever a move leads to a 𝒫 \mathcal{P} position, we also say that such a move wins. 
We call a position terminal if no further moves can be made from it.



 
 There are two ways to play a combinatorial game, depending on the winning condition. In normal play , a player who reaches a terminal position loses. In misère play , a player who reaches a terminal position wins.



 
 Given an impartial game G G , let λ \lambda be one of its positions.
For normal-play impartial games, Sprague Sprague (1935) and Grundy Grundy (1939) independently found a way to generalize winning and losing positions, assigning to each position in an impartial combinatorial game a nonnegative integer value, called the Sprague-Grundy value of the position. We will denote the Sprague-Grundy value of a position λ \lambda by 𝒢 G ​ ( λ ) \mathcal{G}_{G}(\lambda) 
and the related minimal excluded value of a finite set of nonnegative integers S S by mex ⁡ ( S ) \mathrm{mex}(S) . For an in-depth treatment of the concepts mentioned in this subsection we refer the reader to Siegel (2013) .



 
 The misère Grundy numbers 𝒢 G − \mathcal{G}_{G}^{-} are defined the same way as the ordinary Sprague-Grundy values, where the recursion end with terminal positions having the misère Grundy value equal to one.
Let 𝒢 G ​ ( λ ) \mathcal{G}_{G}(\lambda) and 𝒢 G − ​ ( λ ) \mathcal{G}_{G}^{-}(\lambda) be its Sprague-Grundy and misère Grundy values, respectively.
Then, we define the Conway pair of λ \lambda to be CP G ​ ( λ ) = ( 𝒢 G ​ ( λ ) , 𝒢 G − ​ ( λ ) ) \mathrm{CP}_{G}(\lambda)=(\mathcal{G}_{G}(\lambda),\mathcal{G}_{G}^{-}(\lambda)) .
We omit the subscript when G G is clear from context.

 3 CRIM 


 
 CRIM is played on partitions. If λ = [ λ 1 , ⋯ , λ r ] \lambda=[\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{r}] then a row move consists of removing the i i th part of λ \lambda for some i i , 1 ≤ i ≤ r 1\leq i\leq r , yielding the partition [ λ 1 , … , λ i − 1 , λ i + 1 , … , λ r ] [\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{i-1},\lambda_{i+1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}] .
