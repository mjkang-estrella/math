                                                                                 1. Introduction

                                              The classical Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem [8] states that if n ⩾ 2k, an intersecting
                                           family of k-subsets of {1, 2, . . . , n} has size at most n−1
                                                                                                            
                                                                                                        k−1
                                                                                                              ; if equality holds, the
                                           family must consist of all k-subsets containing a fixed element. We deal with
                                           analogues of this result for C-linear representations of finite groups.
                                              Let G be a finite group. If α : G → Sym(X) is a transitive permutation
                                           representation, where X is a finite set, we say that two subsets S1 , S2 ⊆ G are
                                           cross-t-intersecting (with respect to α) if |{x ∈ X : α(g1 )(x) = α(g2 )(x)}| ⩾ t for
                                           any (g1 , g2 ) ∈ S1 ×S2 . In particular, S1 is said to be t-intersecting (with respect to
                                           α) if S1 = S2 . Similarly, if β : G → GL(V ) is an irreducible linear representation,
                                           where V is a finite-dimensional vector space, we say that two subsets S1 , S2 ⊆ G
                                           are cross-t-intersecting (with respect to β) if dim {v ∈ V : β(g1 )(v) = β(g2 )(v)} ⩾
                                           t for any (g1 , g2 ) ∈ S1 × S2 . In particular, S1 is said to be t-intersecting (with
                                           respect to β) if S1 = S2 . One can ask, for each representation, what is the
                                           maximum possible product of sizes of a pair of cross-t-intersecting subsets of G.

                                3. Background

3.1. Representation theory. Let G be a finite group. A C-representation of G
is a pair (ρ, V ), where V is a finite-dimensional C-vector space and ρ : G → GL(V )
is a homomorphism. An equivalence between two representations (ρ, V ) and
(ρ′ , V ′ ) is a linear isomorphism ψ : V → V ′ such that ψ(ρ(g)(v)) = ρ′ (g)(ψ(v))
for all g ∈ G and v ∈ V . The representation (ρ, V ) is said to be irreducible if
there is no proper subspace of V which is ρ(g)-invariant for all g ∈ G. There are
only finitely many equivalence classes of irreducible C-representations of G. The
character χρ of ρ is the map defined by χρ (g) = Tr(ρ(g)) where Tr denotes the
trace. For more background, the readers may consult [14, 17, 18].
   GL2 (q) has four types of irreducible C-representations, namely the One-dimensional
representations, the Special representations, the Principal series representations
Vm,n , and the Weil representations. In particular, for Vm,n , the linear action of
GL2 (q) on Vm,n is the right regular representation, namely defined by ⟨g1 , g2 (f )⟩ :=
⟨g1 g2 , f ⟩ where g1 , g2 ∈ GL2 (q) and f ∈ Vm,n . For more background, the readers
may consult [2].

                                    4. Proof of Theorem 1.1

4.1. Strategy. Note that dim {v ∈ V : g1 v = g2 v} = dim v ∈ V : g2−1 g1 v = v ,
                                                               

thus we define η(g) := dim {v ∈ V : gv = v}. With this terminology, two subsets
S1 , S2 ⊆ G are cross-t-intersecting if and only if η(g2−1 g1 ) ⩾ t for any (g1 , g2 ) ∈
S1 ×S2 . If we construct the Cayley graph Cay(G, X) with X = {g ∈ G : η(g) < t},
then for two subsets S1 , S2 ⊆ G, they are cross-t-intersecting if and only if no
edges of Cay(G, X) between S1 and S2 . (For now, let AG denote the weighted
adjacency matrix of Cay(G, X).) This observation allows us, with the help of
Theorem 3.2, to use the eigenvalues of AG to give an upper bound for |S1 | · |S2 |,
and Theorem 3.1 will be useful in calculating the eigenvalues of AG . It is worth
noting that the condition for applying Theorem 3.2 is somewhat strict, namely θ1
and |θ2 | should be distinct. In fact, we can ensure this by letting the multiplicity
of θ1 as an eigenvalue of AG is exactly 1.
   Hence, the proof of Theorem 1.1 is divided into three steps. Step 1: to find the
derangements, which is the generating set X for the Cayley graph Cay(G, X).
Step 2: to use Theorem 3.1 to calculate the eigenvalues of AG . Step 3: to run a
linear programming such that the multiplicity of θ1 is exactly 1.
