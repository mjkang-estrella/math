Our proofs are based on bijections from trees to labeled Motzkin or Schröder paths; these bijections extend the well-known bijection of Françon–Viennot (1979) interpreted in terms of increasing binary trees. This work can also be viewed as a sequel to the recent work of Elvey Price and Sokal (2020), where they provide combinatorial interpretations for Thron-type continued fractions with coefficients that are affine. Towards the end of the paper, we conjecture an equidistribution of vincular patterns on permutations. Our combinatorial interpretations will thus involve several classes of increasing trees, i.e. labeled rooted trees in which the labels increase from parent to child. We will prove each J-fraction or T-fraction by constructing a bijection from the given class of increasing trees to a suitable class of labeled Motzkin or Schröder paths. These bijections will generalize the classical Françon–Viennot [ 21 ] bijection from permutations to labeled Motzkin paths, reformulated [ 19 , 23 ] as a bijection from increasing binary trees to labeled Motzkin paths. Some of our tree constructions have been studied previously by Kuba and Panholzer [ 26 ] , albeit not in the context of continued fractions. A labeled tree with vertex set V V is a tree in which each vertex is assigned a distinct label from V V , such that each element of V V is the label for some vertex of the tree (hence for exactly one vertex). A multilabeled tree with label set ℒ {\mathcal{L}} is a tree in which each vertex v v is assigned a nonempty label set L v ⊆ ℒ L_{v}\subseteq{\mathcal{L}} , such that each label i ∈ ℒ i\in{\mathcal{L}} belongs to exactly one of the sets L v L_{v} . Specific types of multilabeled trees arise by restricting the subsets of ℒ {\mathcal{L}} that are allowed to be vertex label sets L v L_{v} . For instance, in 𝒌 \bm{k} -labeled trees [ 27 , Section 2.6] , all vertex label sets L v L_{v} must have cardinality k k ; in interval-labeled trees , the label set ℒ {\mathcal{L}} is some set of integers (usually an interval), and all vertex label sets L v L_{v} must be intervals. A labeled tree on a partially ordered vertex set V V is called increasing if the label of a child is always greater than the label of its parent. In most applications the vertex set is totally ordered: this is the case for ordinary (unilabeled) increasing trees, in which the vertex set is some set of integers (ordinarily either [ n ] [n] or [ 0 , n ] [0,n] ) and each vertex has a single integer label. However, for multilabeled trees the vertex set V V is some collection of nonempty subsets of the label set ℒ {\mathcal{L}} , equipped with the partial order A < B A<B ⟺ \Longleftrightarrow max ⁡ A < min ⁡ B \max A<\min B . Let σ = ( σ 1 ⋯ σ n ) ∈ 𝔖 n \sigma=(\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{n})\in\mathfrak{S}_{n} be a permutation of [ n ] [n] , which we shall consider principally as a word. We declare σ 0 = σ n + 1 = 0 \sigma_{0}=\sigma_{n+1}=0 . An index i ∈ [ n ] i\in[n] (or a letter σ i ∈ [ n ] \sigma_{i}\in[n] ) is called a • peak (pk) if σ i − 1 < σ i > σ i + 1 \sigma_{i-1}<\sigma_{i}>\sigma_{i+1} , • valley (val) if σ i − 1 > σ i < σ i + 1 \sigma_{i-1}>\sigma_{i}<\sigma_{i+1} , • double ascent (dasc) if σ i − 1 < σ i < σ i + 1 \sigma_{i-1}<\sigma_{i}<\sigma_{i+1} , • double descent (ddes) if σ i − 1 > σ i > σ i + 1 \sigma_{i-1}>\sigma_{i}>\sigma_{i+1} . Clearly every index i i belongs to one of these four types; we refer to this classification as the linear classification . Remark. The boundary condition σ 0 = σ n + 1 = 0 \sigma_{0}=\sigma_{n+1}=0 plays a key role in our definition of the linear classification. Other boundary conditions can also be used: for instance, Han, Mao and Zeng [ 24 ] use σ 0 = 0 \sigma_{0}=0 , σ n + 1 = n + 1 \sigma_{n+1}=n+1 . Different boundary conditions give rise to different linear classifications. ■ \blacksquare Next we introduce certain permutation statistics given by the occurrence of some vincular patterns [ 2 ] (see also the survey article [ 40 ] ). More precisely, we considered a generic quasi-affine T-fraction of the form ( 1.5 ) in which we let each of the variables x , y , u , v , a , b , c , d x,y,u,v,a,b,c,d be either 0 or 1. For each of the 2 8 = 256 2^{8}=256 cases we generated the first 10 terms a n a_{n} using a Mathematica code, and we then searched the OEIS [ 28 ] for the given sequence, deleting the initial term a 0 = 1 a_{0}=1 . Of course, the lack of an OEIS entry does not necessarily mean that the sequence is combinatorially uninteresting; indeed, the sequence ( 1.7 ) is a counterexample. And conversely, an OEIS entry sometimes lacks a combinatorial interpretation. But we figured that the OEIS would be a good place to start. Our idea was that if the OEIS entry for an integer sequence arising from x , y , u , v , a , b , c , d ∈ { 0 , 1 } x,y,u,v,a,b,c,d\in\{0,1\} mentions some combinatorial interpretation, then by refining that combinatorial interpretation we might conjecture (and then prove) a T-fraction in which one or more of the coefficients are variables conjugate to some statistic in the combinatorial model. This strategy turned out to pay off. In reality we imposed some restrictions. To begin with, we imposed x = y = 1 x=y=1 , because taking either x = 0 x=0 or y = 0 y=0 would make α 1 = 0 \alpha_{1}=0 or α 2 = 0 \alpha_{2}=0 , leading to a finite (and quite trivial) continued fraction; this restriction reduces the number of cases to 2 6 = 64 2^{6}=64 . Likewise, we disallowed a = b = c = d = 0 a=b=c=d=0 , since that would give an S-fraction. We excluded those because the main goal of this project was to discover new T-fractions that cannot be represented as an S-fraction or J-fraction. This further brought down the number of cases to 60. We performed an automated search for these 60 sequences on the OEIS. Among the resulting searches, we further omitted the sequences with c = d = 0 c=d=0 , since they correspond to simple linear transforms of S-fractions 7 7 7 See [ 3 , Propositions 3 and 15] for some special cases, and [ 36 ] for the general case. ; this reduced the number of sequences to 48. This approach was very helpful to us in guessing our families of trees, and also in helping us to check the correctness of our T-fractions. Indeed, whenever we conjectured a family of trees to match a given sequence arising from a T-fraction, we checked our conjecture by first writing out a derivative operator to generate that family of trees; this was a quick way of obtaining a weighted count of the trees in our family without having to construct them explicitly. We then checked whether the weighted count of the trees matched the sequence generated by our T-fraction. We applied this approach to increasing binary trees and increasing restricted ternary trees. However, for increasing interval-labeled restricted ternary trees, our approach was different: instead, we generated the first few terms of the ordinary generating function for increasing restricted ternary trees using a derivative operator, and then checked our construction by using the identity ( 6.15 ). In the rest of this section, we write out our grammar rules and derivative operators for binary trees and restricted ternary trees.
