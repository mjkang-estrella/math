                                                                                                 Abstract
                                                     In threshold-linear networks (TLNs), a fixed point is called minimal if no proper subset of its support
                                                 is also a fixed point. Curto et al (Advances in Applied Mathematics, 2024) conjectured that every stable

                                                 fixed point of any TLN must be a minimal fixed point. We provide a counterexample to this conjecture:
                                                 an explicit competitive TLN on 3 neurons that exhibits a stable fixed point whose support is not minimal

                                            1    Introduction
                                            Threshold-linear networks (TLNs) are recurrent neural network models in which each neuron’s activity xi ≥ 0
                                            evolves according to a linear combination of inputs passed through a rectified linear (ReLU) activation
                                            function. TLNs can exhibit multiple stable equilibria (fixed points), which represent memory or activity
                                            patterns stored by the network. Curto et al [8] posed the question of whether stable fixed points must be
                                            minimal in the sense that no smaller subset of neurons could by itself support a (possibly unstable) fixed
                                            point. In other words, if a certain set of neurons σ is active at a stable equilibrium, is it possible that a
                                            strict subset τ ⊊ σ also yields a fixed point of the system? One might suspect that in competitive networks
                                            (with inhibitory interactions between neurons), stable equilibria would tend to be minimal, since superfluous
                                            active neurons might destabilize the state.
                                                Threshold-linear networks (TLNs) have become a central model for understanding the combinatorial
                                            geometry of neural representations, bridging nonlinear dynamics with network topology. Over the past few
                                            decades, a series of works [1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 9, 10] have established that many qualitative properties of TLNs
                                            (existence, multiplicity, and stability of fixed points) can be characterized purely from the signed directed
                                            graph of inhibitory and excitatory connections.
                                                Within this framework, a recurring theme has been the search for minimal supports of fixed points,
                                            viewed as the fundamental “building blocks” of network memory states. The conjecture that stability im-
                                            plies minimality arose naturally from the observation that in several structured families of TLNs, every stable
                                            equilibrium corresponded to a minimal fixed-point support. This suggested a deep principle: that competi-
                                            tion among neurons enforces a type of parsimony, preventing redundant active neurons from coexisting at
                                            equilibrium.
                                                The present note shows that this principle can fail even in small, fully competitive networks. The
                                            counterexample reported here demonstrates that a network may sustain nested stable equilibria, in which
                                            a large stable state contains a smaller stable subset. The existence of such stable non-minimal fixed points
                                            indicates that competitive dynamics can harbor richer attractor hierarchies than previously recognized.
                                                In Section 2, we formally define the TLN model. In Section 3, we present a concrete 3-neuron competitive
                                            TLN with a stable fixed point whose support {1, 2, 3} contains a strictly smaller support {2} that also
                                            supports a stable fixed point. Thus the full-support equilibrium is stable despite not being support-minimal.
                                            In Section 4, we show that it is impossible for a non-degenerate competitive TLN on 2 neurons to have a
                                            stable non-minimal fixed point support. In Section 5, we expand the 3-neuron example to show for any
                                            positive integers i, j with i < j − 1 that there exists a competitive TLN with stable fixed point supports
                                            τ ⊊ σ for which |τ | = i and |σ| = j. In Section 6, we use a different expansion of the 3-neuron example to

show that these phenomena are robust: small changes to the weight matrix (preserving inhibition) do not
destroy the existence or stability of the fixed points or their support relationships. Consequently, there is
a continuum of networks exhibiting stable non-minimal fixed points. In Section 8, we discuss stable fixed

2     Preliminaries: TLN Model and Fixed Points
We consider a competitive TLN defined by a weight matrix W ∈ Rn×n and bias (external drive) vector
b ∈ Rn . The network dynamics are given by
                                             ẋ = −x + [W x + b]+ ,
where [·]+ denotes the componentwise ReLU, i.e. [z]+ = max{z, 0} applied to each coordinate of z. The term
competitive means bi ≥ 0 and Wii = 0 for all i (no self-coupling), and Wij ≤ 0 for all i ̸= j (all connections
between distinct neurons are inhibitory).
   A fixed point x∗ ∈ Rn≥0 is a nonnegative equilibrium satisfying x∗ = [W x∗ + b]+ . Equivalently, letting
σ = {i : x∗i > 0} denote the support (active neuron set) of x∗ , the following conditions must hold [7]:
    • On-neuron equations: (I − Wσ ) x∗σ = bσ , with x∗σ > 0. Here Wσ denotes the |σ| × |σ| principal
      submatrix of W on the indices in σ, and x∗σ , bσ are the restriction of x∗ , b to those coordinates.
    • Off-neuron inequalities: For each j ∈  / σ, bj +(Wj,σ x∗σ ) ≤ 0. In words, every inactive neuron receives
      net input at most zero (so that its output remains at 0 under the ReLU).
We say that a support σ supports a fixed point if there exists some x∗ ≥ 0 with supp(x∗ ) = σ satisfying
these conditions. We denote by FP(W, b) the set of all supports of fixed points of (W, b).
   A fixed point x∗ (with support σ) is stable if all eigenvalues λ of −I + Wσ have ℜ(λ) < 0 [3, 6].
Equivalently, all eigenvalues µ of Wσ satisfy ℜ(µ) < 1. We will be especially interested in stable fixed points
that are non-minimal in the sense that they have a support σ which strictly contains some other support
τ ⊂ σ that also lies in FP(W, b) (i.e. τ also supports a fixed point, stable or unstable).
