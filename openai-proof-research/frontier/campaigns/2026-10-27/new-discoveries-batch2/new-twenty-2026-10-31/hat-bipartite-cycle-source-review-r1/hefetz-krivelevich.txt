                                                       The cycle space of a graph G, denoted C(G), is a vector space over F2 , spanned by all incidence

                                                   conditions for Hamiltonicity and show that an appropriate and fairly mild strengthening of each

                                                   the classical Chvátal-Erdős criterion and prove that (under various additional restrictions) if n

                                                   satisfying αBIP (G) ≤ 2δ(G) − 24, then C2n (G) = C(G).


                                           1       Introduction
                                           Let G = (V, E) be a graph on n vertices. The edge space of G, denoted E(G), is a vector space
                                           over F2 consisting of all incidence vectors of subsets of E. The cycle space of G, denoted C(G), is
                                           the subspace of E(G), spanned by all incidence vectors of cycles of G. For any integer 3 ≤ k ≤ n,
                                           let Ck (G) be the subspace of C(G), spanned by all incidence vectors of cycles of length k in G.
                                           Determining conditions under which Ck (G) = C(G) holds for some 3 ≤ k ≤ n is a well-studied
                                           problem (see, e.g., [4, 7, 10, 18, 27, 28]). In this paper we are interested in the case k = n, that is, in
                                           graphs whose cycle space is spanned by their Hamilton cycles. This problem has been addressed by
                                           various researchers (see, e.g., [2, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23]). Since the symmetric difference of any two even

bipartite or n is odd. Moreover, G must either be acyclic or Hamiltonian.1 Since the former case is
trivial, this study can be viewed as part of the common theme of proving that (possibly somewhat
strengthened) various sufficient conditions for Hamiltonicity in fact ensure stronger properties.
    One of the earliest and most well-known sufficient conditions for Hamiltonicity is Dirac’s Theorem,
stipulating that any graph on n ≥ 3 vertices whose minimum degree is at least n/2 is Hamiltonian;
n-vertex graphs whose minimum degree is at least n/2 are thus dubbed Dirac graphs. Given an
n-vertex graph G, where n is odd, with minimum degree (n + 1)/2 + k, where k ≥ 0 is as small
as possible, we would like to determine whether Cn (G) = C(G) holds. This question was first
considered by Heinig [21] who proved that Cn (G) = C(G) holds whenever G is an n-vertex graph
with minimum degree δ(G) ≥ (1/2 + ε)n, for any constant ε > 0 and sufficiently large odd n. This
was significantly improved by Christoph, Nenadov, and Petrova [8] who proved that δ(G) ≥ n/2 + 37
suffices. Recently, a complete resolution of this problem was announced by Hou and Yin [23] who
proved that δ(G) > n/2 is sufficient (and necessary) for Cn (G) = C(G) to hold.
    Another natural venue for this problem are random and pseudo-random graphs. Indeed, it was
proved by Christoph, Nenadov, and Petrova in [8] that Cn (G) = C(G) holds whenever G is a pseudo-
random graph with certain appropriate properties (see Theorem 1.3 in [8] for details). One immediate
consequence of this result, significantly improving a result of Heinig [22], is that if G ∼ G(n, p), where
n is odd and p ≥ C log n/n for a sufficiently large constant C, then asymptotically almost surely
(a.a.s. for brevity hereafter) Cn (G) = C(G). The exact bound on p has been subsequently attained
by the authors in [19] where it is proved that if p is large enough to ensure that δ(G) ≥ 3 holds a.a.s.,
then it also ensures that a.a.s. Cn (G) = C(G) (the necessity of this condition was observed by Heinig
in [22]). Note that the required value of p is slightly larger than the one required for Hamiltonicity
(recall that the latter coincides with the probability ensuring that δ(G) ≥ 2 holds a.a.s.).
    An additional immediate consequence of the aforementioned result of Christoph, Nenadov, and
Petrova regarding pseudo-random graphs is that if G is an (n, d, λ)-graph, n is odd, d ≥ C log n, and
λ ≤ εd, where C and ε are appropriate constants, then Cn (G) = C(G). This was somewhat improved
by the authors [20] who proved that d ≥ C log n/ log(d/λ) suffices to ensure the same conclusion
(with the other assumptions unchanged). In the most natural special case, where G is a random
d-regular graph G ∼ Gn,d , it is proved in [20] that sufficiently large yet constant d is a.a.s. enough
to ensure that Cn (G) = C(G) holds. Since odd n mandates that d is even, a complementary result,
taking care, in particular, of odd values of d, is also proved in [20]. Namely, it is proved there that
if G ∼ Gn,d , where n is even and d is a sufficiently large constant, then a.a.s. Cn−1 (G) = C(G).
    In light of the aforementioned results for Dirac graphs and for G(n, p), it is natural to consider
this cycle space problem for randomly perturbed dense graphs. This was done by the authors in [20],

1.1   Our results
All results stated in this section show that mild strengthenings of known sufficient conditions for
an n-vertex graph G to be Hamiltonian in fact imply that Cn (G) = C(G); throughout we assume
(explicitly or implicitly) that n is odd or G is bipartite.
    A well-known sufficient condition for Hamiltonicity, due to Chvátal and Erdős [9], stipulates that
highly connected graphs with a small independence number are Hamiltonian. Formally, their result
reads as follows.

Theorem 1.1 ([9]). Let G be a graph on at least 3 vertices. If κ(G) ≥ α(G), then G is Hamiltonian.

    It was conjectured by Jackson and Ordaz [24] that the (needed) slight strengthening κ(G) > α(G)
in fact ensures that G is pancyclic. Following a recent breakthrough by Draganić, Munhá Correia,
and Sudakov [13], who proved an asymptotically optimal version of this conjecture, it has been
completely resolved (for large graphs) by Letzter [26]. It is natural to ask whether the same (or,
possibly, slightly strengthened — in particular, n must be odd) condition also implies Cn (G) = C(G).
We prove that this is indeed the case up to a multiplicative constant factor, provided κ(G) is not
too small as a function of |V (G)|.

Theorem 1.2. There exist a constant c and an integer n0 such that the following holds. Let G be
an n-vertex graph, where n ≥ n0 is odd. If κ(G) ≥ c max{α(G), log n} or κ(G) ≥ cα(G)2 , then
Cn (G) = C(G).

   The condition κ(G) ≥ c log n appearing in the statement of Theorem 1.2 is an artifact of our
proof of that theorem and we do not believe it is necessary; this leads us to the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.3. There exists a constant c such that the following holds. Let G be an n-vertex graph,
where n is odd. If κ(G) ≥ cα(G), then Cn (G) = C(G).

   The following result offers a first step towards the resolution of Conjecture 1.3.

Theorem 1.4. Let G be an n-vertex graph, where n is a sufficiently large odd integer. If δ(G) ≥ δn
for some δ := δ(n) > 0, and κ(G) ≥ max 180α(G), 2000δ −2 , then Cn (G) = C(G).

    It is not hard to see that c = 1 is not sufficient in Conjecture 1.3. That is, similarly to the case
of pancyclicity, κ(G) > α(G) is necessary (though perhaps not sufficient) to ensure Cn (G) = C(G).

Proposition 1.5. For every integer k ≥ 2 and sufficiently large odd n there exists an n-vertex graph
G satisfying κ(G) = α(G) = k and Cn (G) ̸= C(G).

    A connected dominating set (CDS for brevity, hereafter) in a graph G is a set S ⊆ V (G) such
that G[S] is connected and for every vertex v ∈ V (G) \ S there exists a vertex u ∈ S such that
uv ∈ E(G). CDSs form an important concept in graph theory with many practical applications (see,
e.g., the survey [34] and the many references therein). It is easy to see that if G admits t pairwise
disjoint CDSs, then it is t-connected. Therefore, essentially the same arguments we use to prove
Theorem 1.2 can also be used to prove the following result.

Theorem 1.6. Let G be an n-vertex graph, where n is odd, which admits 16α(G) + 12 pairwise
disjoint CDSs. Then, Cn (G) = C(G).

     Note that the same example we present in the proof of Proposition 1.5 shows that Theorem 1.6
is tight up to the constants 16 and 12.

Proposition 1.7. For every integer k ≥ 2 and sufficiently large odd n, there exists an n-vertex graph
G which admits α(G) pairwise disjoint CDSs but satisfies Cn (G) ̸= C(G).

    Another interesting sufficient condition for Hamiltonicity, which extends Dirac’s classical crite-
rion, was proved by McDiarmid and Yolov [29]. In order to state their result, we need the following
definition.

Definition 1.8. Let G be a graph. The bipartite independence number of G, denoted α̃(G), is
the smallest integer k for which there exist positive integers a and b such that a + b = k + 1, and
EG (A, B) ̸= ∅ whenever A ⊆ V (G) and B ⊆ V (G) \ A are sets of sizes |A| = a and |B| = b.

   We may now state the aforementioned result of McDiarmid and Yolov.

Theorem 1.9 ([29]). Let G be a graph on at least 3 vertices. If δ(G) ≥ α̃(G), then G is Hamiltonian.

    Note that, as alluded to above, this is a generalization of Dirac’s Theorem. Indeed, If δ(G) ≥
⌈n/2⌉, then EG (v, A) ̸= ∅ for every v ∈ V (G) and every A ⊂ V (G) \ {v} of size ⌊n/2⌋. Hence,
α̃(G) ≤ ⌈n/2⌉ ≤ δ(G) and thus G is Hamiltonian by Theorem 1.9. This theorem of McDiarmid
and Yolov has been significantly strengthened by Draganić, Munhá Correia, and Sudakov [12] who
proved that the same condition in fact implies that G is pancyclic (with G = Kn/2,n/2 being the sole
exception). Here too we wish to determine whether this condition also implies Cn (G) = C(G). We
prove that this is indeed the case up to a small multiplicative constant factor.

Theorem 1.10. Let G be an n-vertex graph, where n is odd. If δ(G) ≥ max {2α̃(G) + 9, α̃(G) + 18},
then Cn (G) = C(G).

    Note that both the Chvátal-Erdős and the McDiarmid-Yolov sufficient conditions for Hamiltonic-
ity (namely, Theorems 1.1 and 1.9) are trivial for bipartite graphs. Indeed, if G is a bipartite graph
satisfying κ(G) ≥ α(G) or δ(G) ≥ α̃(G), then G is a balanced complete bipartite graph and thus
obviously Hamiltonian. In an attempt to devise an effective variant of the Chvátal-Erdős criterion
for bipartite graphs, the following parameter was defined by Ash [3].

Definition 1.11. Let G = (X ∪ Y, E) be a balanced bipartite graph, i.e. |X| = |Y |. An independent
set A ⊆ X ∪ Y is said to be balanced if |A ∩ X| = |A ∩ Y |. The balanced independence number of G,
denoted αBIP (G), is the largest size of a balanced independent set in G.

   Following results by Ash [3], by Fraisse [17], and by Favaron, Mago, and Ordaz [16], the following
optimal result was proved by Ordaz, Amar, and Raspaud.

Theorem 1.12 ([30]). Let G be a balanced bipartite graph. If αBIP (G) ≤ 2δ(G) − 4, then G is
Hamilton-biconnected.2

    As alluded to above, this result is tight. Indeed, it is shown in [30] that there are infinitely many
values of n for which there exist non-Hamiltoian balanced bipartite graphs G on 2n vertices which
satisfy αBIP (G) = 2δ(G) − 2.
    Since αBIP (G) ≤ α(G) and δ(G) ≥ κ(G) clearly hold, Theorem 1.12 may be viewed as an effective
Chvátal-Erdős criterion for balanced bipartite graphs.
    Our next result asserts that (a minor strengthening of) the condition appearing in Theorem 1.12
in fact ensures that the cycle space of G is spanned by its Hamilton cycles.

Theorem 1.13. Let G be a balanced bipartite graph on 2n vertices. If αBIP (G) ≤ 2δ(G) − 24, then
C2n (G) = C(G).

    Instead of starting with a sufficient condition for Hamiltonicity and show that it can be slightly
strengthened so as to yield stronger properties, one may simply start with a Hamiltonian graph. This
was done, for example, in the context of pancyclicity. Indeed, a well-known problem of Erdős [15]
asks for the smallest function f such that any Hamiltonian n-vertex graph G satisfying n ≥ f (α(G))
is pancyclic (note that, on its own, the condition n ≥ f (α(G)) does not even imply connectivity, so
assuming that G is Hamiltonian is needed). Following a series of results, this problem was recently
solved by Draganić, Munhá Correia, and Sudakov [14] who proved that n ≥ (2 + o(1))α(G)2 is
sufficient (a construction of Erdős shows that this is asymptotically best possible). It is natural to ask
whether the same condition (possibly somewhat strengthened) also implies Cn (G) = C(G) (assuming
of course that n is odd). However, the same example we present in the proof of Propositions 1.5
and 1.7 shows that this is false in a strong sense.

Proposition 1.14. For every integer k ≥ 2, function f , and sufficiently large odd n, there exists an
n-vertex Hamitonian graph G satisfying α(G) = k, n ≥ f (k), and Cn (G) ̸= C(G).

2     Preliminaries and tools
For the sake of simplicity and clarity of presentation, we do not make a particular effort to optimize
some of the constants obtained in our proofs. We also omit floor and ceiling signs whenever these
are not crucial. Throughout this paper, log stands for the natural logarithm, unless explicitly stated
otherwise. Our graph-theoretic notation is standard; in particular, we use the following.
    For a graph G, let V (G) and E(G) denote its sets of vertices and edges respectively, and let
v(G) = |V (G)| and e(G) = |E(G)|. For a set A ⊆ V (G), let EG (A) denote the set of edges
of G with both endpoints in A and let eG (A) = |EG (A)|. For disjoint sets A, B ⊆ V (G), let
EG (A, B) denote the set of edges of G with one endpoint in A and one endpoint in B, and let
eG (A, B) = |EG (A, B)|. For a set S ⊆ V (G), let G[S] denote the subgraph of G induced by the
set S, and let G \ S = G[V (G) \ S]. For a set S ⊆ V (G), let NG (S) = {v ∈ V (G) \ S : ∃u ∈
S such that uv ∈ E(G)} denote the external neighbourhood of S in G. For a vertex u ∈ V (G) we
abbreviate NG ({u}) under NG (u) and let degG (u) = |NG (u)| denote the degree of u in G. The
maximum degree of a graph G is ∆(G) := max{degG (u) : u ∈ V (G)}, and the minimum degree of
a graph G is δ(G) := min{degG (u) : u ∈ V (G)}. For a vertex u ∈ V (G) and a set S ⊆ V (G), let
degG (u, S) = |NG (u) ∩ S|. Given any two (not necessarily distinct) vertices x, y ∈ V (G), the distance
between x and y in G, denoted distG (x, y), is the length of a shortest path between x and y in G,
where the length of a path is the number of its edges (for the sake of formality, we define distG (x, y)
to be ∞ whenever x and y lie in different connected components of G). The diameter of G, denoted
diam(G), is max{distG (x, y) : x, y ∈ V (G)}. A graph G is said to be Hamilton-connected if for every
two vertices x, y ∈ V (G) there is a Hamilton path of G whose endpoints are x and y. A bipartite
graph G = (X ∪ Y, E) is said to be Hamilton-biconnected if for every two vertices x ∈ X and y ∈ Y
there is a Hamilton path of G whose endpoints are x and y.

2.1   The recipe of Christoph, Nenadov, and Petrova
Let n be an odd integer, and let G be an n-vertex Hamiltonian graph. A recipe for proving Cn (G) =
C(G) is presented in [8]; it has since been utilized in [19, 20, 23]. In order to describe it we need
some definitions and results.

Lemma 2.1 ([8]). Let G be an n-vertex Hamiltonian graph, where n is odd or G is bipartite, and
suppose that Cn (G) ̸= C(G). Then, there exists a subgraph R of G such that the following conditions

                                                   6
hold.

(C1) R ̸= G;

(C2) Every Hamilton cycle in G contains an even number of edges from R;

(C3) For every partition V (G) = A ∪ B it holds that eR (A, B) ≥ eG (A, B)/2 and R ̸= G[A, B].

Remark 2.2. In fact, the version of this lemma appearing in [8] is only for odd n, whereas if G is
bipartite and Hamiltonian it has to be balanced, rendering n even. However, it is not hard to see that
its proof in [8] applies essentially verbatim to the bipartite case as well. Indeed, the only place where
the assumption that n is odd is used in [8] is in the proof of Property (C1); if n is odd, then it is an
immediate corollary of Property (C2). On the other hand, if G (and thus also R) is bipartite, then
(C1) is an immediate corollary of Property (C3) from the same lemma.

    The following definition of a so-called parity switcher is central to the method of [8]. It describes
a construction that, given graphs G and R as in Lemma 2.1, aids one in finding a Hamilton cycle of
G with an odd number of edges in R, thus arriving at a contradiction to (C2) above.

Definition 2.3. Given a graph G and a subgraph R ⊆ G, a subgraph W ⊆ G is called an R-parity-
switcher if it consists of an even cycle C = (v1 , v2 , . . . , v2k , v1 ) with an odd number of edges in R,
and vertex-disjoint paths P2 , . . . , Pk such that for every 2 ≤ i ≤ k, the endpoints of Pi are vi and
v2k−i+2 and V (Pi ) ∩ V (C) = {vi , v2k−i+2 }.

   In justification of its title, the parity switcher, as per Definition 2.3, contains two Hamilton paths
whose endpoints are v1 and vk+1 ; one having an odd number of edges of R and the other having an
even number of edges of R (additional details and a figure can be found in [8]).
   We may now specify the recipe from [8]; it consists of the following five steps.

(S1) Let G be an n-vertex Hamiltonian graph, where n is odd or G is bipartite. Suppose it satisfies
     Cn (G) ̸= C(G), and let R ⊆ G be a subgraph as in Lemma 2.1.

(S2) Find in G a (small) R-parity-switcher W , that is,

    (S2a) Find an even (short) cycle C = (v1 , . . . , v2k , v1 ) with an odd number of edges in R.
    (S2b) Find pairwise vertex-disjoint (short) paths P2 , . . . , Pk such that, for every 2 ≤ i ≤ k, the
          endpoints of Pi are vi and v2k−i+2 , and V (Pi ) ∩ V (C) = {vi , v2k−i+2 }.

(S3) Find in (G \ V (W )) ∪ {v1 , vk+1 } a Hamilton path P whose endpoints are v1 and vk+1 .

(S4) If P contains an odd (even) number of edges of R, then choose a Hamilton path P ′ in W whose
     endpoints are v1 and vk+1 with an even (odd) number of edges of R.



                                                     7
(S5) Conclude that the concatenation of P and P ′ yields a Hamilton cycle H ⊆ G with an odd
     number of edges in R, contradicting (C2).

   Note that there is nothing to prove in steps (S4) and (S5). Moreover, whenever we start with a
graph which we know to be Hamiltonian, Step (S1) becomes immediate. The main task is thus to
deal with steps (S2) and (S3).

Remark 2.4. Again, the method of Christoph, Nenadov, and Petrova [8] was only designed for odd
n (see the description of Step (S1)). However, as noted in Remark 2.2, it applies to the bipartite
case as well (the only change is Lemma 2.1).


3    Highly connected graphs with a small independence number
In this section we prove Theorems 1.2, 1.4, and 1.6. We begin by recalling several known results that
facilitate our proofs.
    The first such result, due to Chvátal and Erdős, asserts that a slight strengthening of the condition
appearing in Theorem 1.1 strengthens its conclusion.

Theorem 3.1 ([9]). If G is a graph satisfying κ(G) > α(G), then G is Hamilton-connected.

   Improving earlier results by Robertson and Seymour [31] and by Bollobás and Thomason [6], it
was proved by Thomas and Wollan [32] that highly connected graphs are also highly linked.

Theorem 3.2 ([32]). Let G be a graph and let x1 , y1 , . . . , xr , yr be 2r distinct vertices of G. If
κ(G) ≥ 10r, then G admits pairwise vertex-disjoint paths P1 , . . . , Pr such that, for every i ∈ [r], the
endpoints of Pi are xi and yi .

   A variant of Theorem 3.2 in which the paths span the vertex-set of G was proved by Aigner-Horev
and the authors.

Theorem 3.3 ([1]). Let G = (V, E) be an n-vertex graph and let x1 , y1 , . . . , xr , yr be 2r distinct
vertices of G. There exists a constant c > 0 such that if κ(G) ≥ c max{α(G), log n, r}, then G admits
pairwise vertex-disjoint paths P1 , . . . , Pr such that V (P1 ) ∪ . . . ∪ V (Pr ) = V and, for every i ∈ [r],
the endpoints of Pi are xi and yi .

    The following result allows one to partition a graph into large highly connected subgraphs.

Lemma 3.4 ([5]). Let H = (V, E) be an n-vertex graph with minimum degree k > 0. Then, there
exists a partition V = V1 ∪. . .∪Vt such that, for every i ∈ [t], the subgraph H[Vi ] is k 2 /(16n)-connected
and |Vi | ≥ k/8.

    The following simple observation will be useful in the proof of Theorem 1.4.


                                                      8
Observation 3.5. Let G be a k-edge-connected graph and let V1 ∪ . . . ∪ Vt be a partition of V (G) into
non-empty parts. Let H be the multigraph obtained from G by collapsing each set Vi into a single
vertex, that is, V (H) = {v1 , . . . , vt } and for every 1 ≤ i < j ≤ t there are eG (Vi , Vj ) edges of H
between vi and vj . Then, H is k-edge-connected.

Proof. Fix any 1 ≤ i < j ≤ t, and let x ∈ Vi and y ∈ Vj be arbitrary vertices. Since G is k-edge-
connected, it admits k pairwise edge-disjoint paths between x and y. Upon collapsing G to yield
H, each such path becomes a walk between vi and vj and these walks are pairwise edge-disjoint.
Each such walk contains a path between vi and vj ; clearly these paths are also pairwise edge-disjoint.
Since i and j were arbitrary, it follows that H admits k pairwise edge-disjoint paths between vi and
vj for every 1 ≤ i < j ≤ t, and is thus k-edge-connected.

   The following result is the main technical tool of this section; it handles Step (S2a).

Lemma 3.6. Let G be a graph satisfying κ(G) ≥ 12α(G) + 8. Let R be a subgraph of G as in
Lemma 2.1. Then, there exists a cycle C ⊆ G for which the following two properties hold.

 (a) |C| is even and |E(C) \ E(R)| is odd;

 (b) |C| ≤ 10α(G) + 8.

Proof. Let k = κ(G) and let α = α(G). It follows by Property (C3) from Lemma 2.1 that R is
k/2-edge-connected and, in particular, that δ(R) ≥ k/2. We begin by proving that there exists a
cycle of some length satisfying Property (a); we distinguish between the following two cases.

 (1) R is bipartite. Let A ∪ B be the bipartition of R. Since R ̸= G[A, B] holds by Property (C3)
     from Lemma 2.1, there exist vertices x ∈ A and y ∈ B such that xy ∈ E(G) \ E(R). Since R
     is connected, there is a path P in R between x and y. Since R is bipartite, the length of P is
     odd. Hence, combined with the edge xy, this yields a cycle which satisfies Property (a).

 (2) R is not bipartite. Let C1 = (x1 , . . . , x2t−1 , x1 ) be an odd cycle in R of minimum possible
     length and let U = V (G) \ V (C1 ). Note that we may assume that |V (C1 )| ≤ 2α + 1. Indeed,
     assume that |V (C1 )| ≥ 2α + 3 and let S = {x2i : 1 ≤ i ≤ α + 1}. Since |S| ≥ α + 1, there
     exist 1 ≤ i < j ≤ α + 1 such that x2i x2j ∈ E(G). By the assumed minimality of C1 , it must
     hold that x2i x2j ∈ E(G) \ E(R). In this case, one of the two cycles in C1 ∪ {x2i x2j } which
     contain the edge x2i x2j satisfies Property (a). This case is further divided into the following
     two subcases.

Lemma 5.1. Let G be a balanced bipartite graph satisfying αBIP (G) ≤ 2δ(G) − 6. Let R ̸= G be

admits a path P whose endpoints are x and y. Since R is bipartite, the length of P is odd. Hence,
combined with the edge xy, this yields an even cycle which satisfies Property (b).
