                                               CROSS NUMBER INVARIANTS OF FINITE ABELIAN GROUPS

                                                                                         XIAOYU HE

                                                 Abstract. The cross number of a sequence over a finite abelian group G is the sum of the
                                                 inverse orders of the terms of that sequence. We study two group invariants, the maximal




arXiv:1308.3896v1 [math.NT] 18 Aug 2013
                                                 cross number of a zero-sum free sequence over G, called k(G), introduced by Krause, and the
                                                 maximal cross number of a unique factorization sequence over G, called K1 (G), introduced
                                                 by Gao and Wang. Conjectured formulae for k(G) and K1 (G) are known, but only some
                                                 special cases are proved for either. We show structural results about maximal cross number
                                                 sequences that allow us to prove an inductive theorem giving conditions under which the
                                                 conjectured values of k and K1 must be correct for G ⊕ Cpα if they are correct for a group G.
                                                 As a corollary of this result we prove the conjectured values of k(G) and K1 (G) for cyclic
                                                 groups Cn , given that the prime factors of n are far apart. Our methods also prove the
                                                 K1 (G) conjecture for rank two groups of the form Cn ⊕ Cq , where q is the largest or second
                                                 largest prime dividing n, and the prime factors of n are far apart, and the k(G) conjecture for
                                                 groups of the form Cn ⊕ Hq , where the prime factors of n are far apart, q is the largest prime
                                                 factor of n, and Hq is an arbitrary finite abelian q-group. Finally, we pose a conjecture about
                                                 the structure of maximal-length unique factorization indexed sequences over elementary p-
                                                 groups, which is a major roadblock to extending the K1 conjecture to groups of higher rank,
                                                 and formulate a general question about the structure of extremal zero-sum free and unique
                                                 factorization sequences with respect to arbitrary weighting functions.



                                                                                    1. Introduction
                                             Let G be a finite abelian group written additively, and let G• denote the set of nonzero
                                          elements in G. Define an indexed sequence S, or simply a sequence, over G to be a set
                                          of ordered pairs (g, k), where g ∈ G• and k ∈ N, under the condition that the second
                                          components k are pairwise distinct. Note that it is not important what the second coordinates
                                          of these pairs are, so long as they are pairwise distinct. Unless otherwise stated, it can be
                                          assumed that they are 1, 2, . . . , n in order, where n is the size of S. Two sequences are
                                          considered the same if they are the same as sets.
                                             For a, b ∈ N, interval notation of the forms (a, b), (a, b], [a, b), and [a, b] will denote the
                                          sets of integers {a < m < b}, {a < m ≤ b}, {a ≤ m < b}, {a ≤ m ≤ b}, respectively. Also
                                          for n ∈ N the notation [n] will denote the positive integers up through n.
                                             We write sequences multiplicatively. Thus, the notation S = g1 g2 · · · gn , where gi ∈ G
                                          for each i ∈ [n], stands for the sequence S = {(gi , i) : i ∈ [n]}. We say that another
                                          indexed sequence T is a subsequence of S, or divides S, if as sets T is a subset of S. This
                                          is written T |S. For a set of pairwise disjoint sequences S1 , S2 , . . . , Sm that all divide S, we
                                          write S = S1 S2 · · · Sm if S is the disjoint union of the Si . If T |S then we will write ST −1 for
                                          the subsequence of S consisting of all elements of S not in T . Finally, we will write 1 for the
                                          empty sequence.
                                             An element of a sequence is the first component of some element of that sequence consid-
                                          ered as a set. When we multiply two sequences we relabel their index sets if they overlap.

   A study of sumset sizes of several types of sequences is made in Chapter 5.2 of [12], based

problem, such as counting the number of subsequences of any given sum [4], and restricting

To improve on these theorems, we develop structural results on the extremal sequences of
interest. The following definitions, motivated by an argument of Girard [15], will be helpful
to introduce.
Definition 5. A zero-sum free sequence is dense if it has maximal cross number among all
zero-sum free sequences over the same group, and minimal length among all such maximal
cross number zero-sum free sequences.
  Similarly, UFIS is dense if it has maximal cross number among all UFIS’s over the same
group, and minimal length among such maximal cross number UFIS’s.
   In Section 2, we will show that subject to certain conditions dense sequences of both types
have few elements of each order, and mostly elements of prime power order. In the rest of
this introduction we define the conditions under which these arguments hold and state our
main results.
   For convenience, we will henceforth call terms of prime power order pure terms. Analo-
gously, we will call terms whose order have more than one prime factor cross terms, following
Kriz [17].
   We say that a positive integer n is supported in a set P of primes if all the prime factors
of n lie in P . Let a group G be supported in P if G can be written as a direct sum of cyclic
groups with orders that are powers of primes in P . Equivalently, exp(G) is supported in
P . Correspondingly, the support of n is the set of all prime factors of n, and the support of
a group G is the set of prime factors of exp(G). We may also write supp(n) and supp(G),
respectively.
   We introduce the following definition, which will prove essential in Sections 2 and 3. It
turns out that when the primes in supp(G) are far apart, it is easier to bound the cross terms
in dense sequences over G.

  For the proof of the UFIS case, we will require the following lemma, which combines two
results of Gao and Geroldinger [5]. We reproduce the proof here for convenience.
Lemma 14. If G is a finite abelian group and S is a sequence over G• , then S is a subse-
quence of a UFIS if and only if for any two zero-sum subsequences U and V of S, U ∩ V is
also zero-sum.
Proof. In one direction, suppose S is the subsequence of some UFIS T , and S has two zero-
sum subsequences U and V which have nonzero sum intersection. In particular, there are
some irreducible subsequences U ′ |U and V ′ |V which have nontrivial intersection. For each
of U ′ and V ′ , we can find an irreducible factorization of T containing it by factoring T (U ′ )−1
and T (V ′ )−1 , respectively. Thus T has at least two nonequivalent irreducible factorizations,
contradicting our original assumption.
   In the other direction, suppose that S has no two zero-sum subsequences with nonzero
sum intersection. Then, we claim that either S is zero-sum and thus already a UFIS, or else
S(−σ(S)) is a UFIS.
   In the first case, if S is already zero-sum then it must have unique factorization. For, if
it had two distinct irreducible factorizations S = U1 U2 · · · Um = V1 V2 · · · Vn , then some Vi
intersects U1 but their intersection cannot be zero-sum, contradicting our assumption.
   In the second case, suppose that T = S(−σ(S)) does not have unique factorization. We
can reduce to the first case by showing that no two zero-sum subsequences of T have nonzero
sum intersection. Suppose otherwise; let two zero-sum subsequences of T called U and V
intersect in a nonzero sum sequence. If either of U and V contains −σ(S), we can replace
it by its complement. Thus we have two zero-sum subsequences of T (−σ(S))−1 = S itself
whose intersection is not zero-sum. This is a contradiction, so we’re done.                       

                                3. Proof of Main Theorem
  Combining the structural results of Section 2, we have enough to prove our main theorems.
Let p and G satisfy the conditions of Theorems 7 and 10, so that p ≺ supp(G) or p ≺2
supp(G), respectively. Write p1 = p, and let the elements of supp(G) be p2 , p3 , . . . , pr .
Proof. (of Theorem 7) Let S be a dense zero-sum free sequence over G′ = Cpα1 ⊕ G. By the
Amalgamation Lemma and Lemma 15, we find that S has at exactly p1 − 1 elements of each
order pa1 , where a ∈ [α]. Let H = Cpα1 be the p1 -component of G′ , and let S ′ = S ∩ H. Then
k(S ′ ) = k(H) = k ∗ (H). It is not difficult to show from here that S ′ has full sumset in H.
   But then, we find that S(S ′ )−1 cannot have any subsums lying in H\{0}, or else together
with some subsequence of S ′ one could form a zero-sum subsequence of S. Therefore, we
find that even after projecting G′ → G, the image of the sequence S(S ′ )−1 is still zero-sum
free. As a result, k(S(S ′ )−1 ) ≤ k(G), since projection cannot decrease cross number. We
have,
                             k(S) ≤ k(H) + k(G) = k(Cpα1 ) + k(G),
as desired. It follows that k(G′ ) = k ∗ (G′ ) if k(G) = k ∗ (G), since k ∗ is also additive (over
direct sums).                                                                                   
  The proof of Theorem 10 is slightly more subtle, because Lemma 15 is too weak by itself.
Proof. (of Theorem 10) Let S be a dense UFIS over G′ = Cpα1 ⊕ G. It follows from Lemma 15
that S contains at least p1 − 1 elements of each order pa1 . Let H = Cpα1 be the p1 -component
of G, and let S ′ = S ∩ H. We have already that S ′ is a subsequence of a UFIS over H and
contains at least p1 − 1 elements of each order dividing pα1 . It is not difficult to show that S ′
must have full sumset over H under these conditions.
    Now, we show that in fact S ′ has exactly p1 elements of each of these orders. Suppose this
is not the case, and choose a to be minimal for which S ′ contains only p1 − 1 elements of
order pa1 , forming a subsequence S ′′ |S ′ . We may assume σ(S ′′ ) 6= 0.
    Because S has p1 elements of each order lower than a, if H ′ = Cpa1 then S ∩ H ′ has full
sumset over H ′ . Because the elements of lower order all sum to zero, there can be at most
one zero-sum free subsequence of S\S ∩ H ′ which has sum in H ′ and this sum must be of
order exactly pa1 ; otherwise, S would have two distinct intersecting irreducible subsequences.
    Now, by removing one element a from this zero-sum free subsequence of S\S ∩ H ′ , if it
exists, we are then allowed to insert −σ(S ′′ ) into S, to construct a sequence Sa−1 (−σ(S ′′ ))
which is a subsequence of a UFIS. The element a can be picked to have order divisible by
pa1 , since the sum of the sequence containing it must have order pa1 . But then the cross
number has increased, so it follows that our assumptions were false and S contains exactly
p1 elements of order pa1 .
    We can now follow the same argument as for the proof of 7, showing that k(S) ≤ K1 (Cpα1 )+
K1 (G) and proving the theorem from there.                                                      

                                   5. Concluding Remarks
   The study of unique factorization over elementary p-groups Cpn is particularly important
to Conjecture 2 because every nonzero element of Cpn has order p, so we have k(S) = p1 |S| for
all p. This problem is also interesting because the study of zero-sum sequences over p-groups
lends itself to algebraic techniques from representation theory, see for example Chapter 5.5
of [1] or [20, 21, 22], giving strong results that are not available in the general case.
   Study of the Narkiewicz constant N1 (G) is not limited by the same obstacles as study
of K1 (G) in the case of groups of large support; the N1 conjecture has been shown for all
groups of rank at most 2 [6, 7, 8].
   However, in the rank direction N1 (G) and K1 (G) have proved equally difficult to compute.
More specifically, it suffices to prove the following statement to resolve the elementary p-group
case in both the N1 and K1 conjectures.
Conjecture 21. If G = Cpn , then N1 (G) = np.
  Conjecture 21 has been shown for the following cases.
Proposition 22. [3, 12] If G is one of the following groups then N1 (G) = np.
   (1) G = C2n , C3n .
   (2) G = Cp2 .
   (3) G = C53 , C54 , C73 .

about maximal-length UFIS’s over Cpn .
   Henceforth, let S be a UFIS over G = Cpn that factors into irreducibles as S = U1 U2 · · · Ut .

  We say a Ui is optimal in S if it cannot be replaced by any longer irreducible sequence
while preserving the unique factorization of S. Notice that the only property necessary to
satisfy is the following.
Proposition 23. [3] If U1 and S ′ are an irreducible sequence and a UFIS, respectively, then
U1 S ′ is a UFIS if and only if Σ(U1 ) ∩ Σ(S ′ ) = {0}.
   Thus Ui is optimal in S if and only if it is the longest irreducible sequence over G which
has sumset equal to {0} ∪ (G\Σ(S ′ )), where S ′ = SUi −1 .
   Note that if |Ui | ≤ p for all i, then |S| ≤ np by the concavity of the logarithm and (5.1).
Henceforth a zero-sum sequence is called short if it has length at most p and long otherwise.
A zero-sum free sequence is called short if it has length less than p and long otherwise. The
following conjecture would resolve the case of longer Ui .
Conjecture 24. If T is a zero-sum free sequence over G = Cpk , then there exists a subse-
quence T0 of T with length less than p such that no other subsequence of T has the same sum
as T0 .
  Note that the statement is trivial for |T | < p, since we can just take T0 = T , and the
sum is unique because T is zero-sum free. Also, it suffices to show that T has a proper
subsequence T0 (not necessarily with length less than p) satisfying this property, since we
can apply the result inductively. To see why this conjecture implies Conjecture 21, we claim
that any maximal UFIS over G must have only short irreducible subsequences. Otherwise,
remove one element from a long Ui and apply Conjecture 24 to the remaining zero-sum free
subsequence to find that Ui can be replaced by a pair of short irreducible sequences of longer
total length.
  Next, we offer the following strengthening of Conjecture 24.
Conjecture 25. If T is a zero-sum free sequence over G = Cpk , then there exists a cyclic
subgroup H of G such that H ∩ T 6= ∅ and H ∩ Σ(T \H) = ∅.
  Here T \H = T (T ∩ H)−1 . To see that Conjecture 25 implies Conjecture 24, take T0 =
H ∩ T.
  For the study of the group invariants k, K, and K1 over groups of large support, our
methods rely on conditions such as wideness and 2-wideness. We are hopeful that a fully
general solution removing these conditions, or improvements on the inequalities in the wide
and 2-wide conditions, can be made.

2-wideness conditions. Define weighting function f to be the totally multiplicative function
