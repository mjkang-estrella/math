                                                                                          1. Introduction
                                           1.1. Background. The chromatic number of a graph G, denoted χ(G), is the minimum number
                                           of colours needed to colour the vertices of G such that adjacent vertices always receive distinct
                                           colours. The clique number of a graph G, denoted ω(G), is the size of a maximum clique in G. If
                                           A ⊆ V (G), then G[A] denotes the subgraph of G induced by A and if H is a subgraph of G, then
                                           we write G[H] to mean G[V (H)]. Let r(G) be the maximum chromatic number of any subgraph
                                           G[P ] of G, where P is a path in the graph G.
                                              Obviously, ω(G) ≤ r(G) ≤ χ(G). However, it is unclear whether there exists a function h such
                                           that χ(G) ≤ h(r(G)). In fact, the authors are unaware of any graphs G appearing in the literature
                                           that have been shown to satisfy χ(G) > r(G) + 1. It is natural to ask, do such graphs exist? To
                                           see that χ(G) = r(G) + 1 is achievable for all r ≥ 3 one can take a sufficiently large (r + 1)-critical
                                           Gallai graph, defined in [8]. Two graphs with r = 3 and chromatic number 4 are shown in Figure 1.
                                           Trivially, χ(G) = r(G) for all graphs with r(G) ≤ 2.
                                              Determining if there exists a function h, such that for all graphs G, χ(G) ≤ h(r(G)) is a relaxed
                                           version of the following problem: Is there a function f such that if each odd cycle of a graph spans
                                           a subgraph with chromatic number at most r, then the chromatic number the graph is at most
                                           f (r)? This problem was published in 1997 by Gyárfás [11] where it is attributed to Hajnal and
                                           Erdős.

   We now take a diversion to mention the related problem of χ-boundedness. Here, χ-boundedness
refers to the study of which graph classes G admit functions f such that for all G ∈ G, χ(G) ≤
f (ω(G)). Motivated by the study of perfect-graphs (i.e. graphs G where for every induced subgraph
H of G, χ(H) = ω(H)) Gyárfás began the study of χ-boundedness in [9]. Significantly, the famous
result by Erdős [7] that there exists graphs with large girth and arbitrarily large chromatic number
implies that many graph classes are not χ-bounded. In particular, H-free graphs are not χ-bounded
for any choice of H that contains a cycle. This leads to the famous Gyárfás-Sumner conjecture
[9, 17] which states that the class of H-free graphs are χ-bounded if and only if H is a forest. For
more on χ-boundedness we refer the reader to Scott and Seymour’s survey [16] and to Char and
Karthick’s very recent survey for subfamilies of Pt -free graphs [2].
   Significantly, one can view the connection between chromatic number and r as a generalization
of χ-boundedness. It is obvious that if a graph class G is χ-bounded, then there exists a function
f where for all G ∈ G, χ(G) ≤ f (r(G)). However, we have no evidence that the converse is true.
In fact, Conjecture 1.1 is an explicit statement to the contrary.
   If one is to believe that there exists a function h, such that χ(G) ≤ h(r(G)) for all graphs G,
then this difference becomes even more extreme. Of course, the same can be said for considering
bounds on chromatic number in terms of the maximum chromatic number of subgraphs spanned by
odd cycles, as proposed by Hajnal and Erdős. Our goal here is to take some first steps to exploring
this exciting, hard to tackle, area.

1.3. Notation and preliminaries. We refer the readers to [18] for most standard graph theory
notation, but we will now briefly list some definitions and notation to avoid any confusion. All
graphs considered in this paper are undirected, finite, simple, and loopless. Even though all graphs
are undirected, we will sometimes use an ordered pair to denote an edge. Every colouring in this
paper is assumed to be a proper colouring. For example, we may write G − (u, v) to denote the
graph obtained be removing the edge between u and v. For a graph G we let χ(G), κ(G), α(G),
ω(G), and δ(G) denote its chromatic number, vertex-connectivity, independence number, clique
number, and minimum degree respectively. We say that a graph G is H-free if it does not contain
the graph H as an induced subgraph. For a graph G and two subsets S and T of V (G), we
use E(S, T ) to denote the set of edges with one endpoint in S and the other in T . We let G[S]
denote the subgraph of G induced by S. We let N [S] denote the closed neighbourhood of the set S
consisting of all neighbours of S together with the set S and we let N (S) = N [S] \ S be the open
neighbourhood. When S = {v}, we simply write N [v] and N (v). We say a graph is traceable if it
has a Hamiltonian path. A graph G is vertex-critical if χ(G − v) < χ(G) for all v ∈ V (G). The
disjoint union of two graphs is denoted G + H. The the disjoint union of ℓ copies of the same graph
G is denoted ℓG. For a positive integer k, we let [k] denote the set {1, 2, . . . , k}.
   We additionally use some notation from order theory. We assume the reader knows the definition
of a partially ordered set (poset). Given a poset (P, ⪯), x is said to cover y if x ≺ y and for no
other z ∈ P do we have x ≺ z ≺ y. A covered chain u1 ≺ u2 ≺ · · · ≺ uℓ is a chain such that ui
covers ui+1 for each i ∈ {1, . . . ℓ − 1}. Also, an element m ∈ P is said to be minimal with respect
to ⪯ if there is no element p ∈ P \ {m} such that p ⪯ m.

                                       2. K1,t -free Graphs
   In this section we will prove upper bounds for the chromatic number of K1,t -free graphs G in
terms of r(G). Since K1,t -free graphs are χ-bounded, as proven by Gyárfás [10], there trivially
exists such bounds. Our focus then is to prove good upper bounds, rather than the existence of
upper bounds, for chromatic number.
   Of course, if G is Hamiltonian, then χ(G) = r(G), since G is a subgraph of itself and G is
spanned by a path. We will use this observation to great effect in this section. Specifically, we
leverage the well known Chvátal-Erdős condition for a graph to be Hamiltonian.
Theorem 2.1 (Theorem 1 [5]). If G is a graph with at least 3 vertices and κ(G) ≥ α(G), then G
is Hamiltonian.

dence number.
Lemma 2.2. If G is a graph with α(G) ≤ 3, then G is path-perfect.
Proof. Let G be a graph with α(G) ≤ 3. If G has at most 2 vertices the result is trivial. Suppose
G is a smallest graph where α(G) ≤ 3 and χ(G) > r(G). Then G has at least 3 vertices, G is
connected, and G does not contain a Hamilton path.
                                                  4
  If κ(G) ≥ 3, then Theorem 2.1 implies G is Hamiltonian, implying χ(G) = r(G). Otherwise,
1 ≤ κ(G) ≤ 2 since G is connected.
  Let S be a vertex-cut of order κ(G) in G. Then the vertices of G can be partitioned into non-
empty sets A, B, S where E(A, B) = ∅ such that each vertex v ∈ S has neighbours in A and B.
Since E(A, B) = ∅, it must be the case that α(G[A]) + α(G[B]) ≤ α(G) ≤ 3. As A and B are both
non-empty, we have α(G[A]), α(G[B]) ≥ 1. So without loss of generality suppose that α(G[A]) = 1
and α(G[B]) ≤ 2.
Claim: If G[B] is connected, then there exists a vertex w ∈ B ∩ N (S) and a Hamilton path P in
G[B] where w is an endpoint of P .
   Suppose G[B] is connected. If κ(G[B]) ≥ 2, then Theorem 2.1 implies there is a Hamilton cycle
in G[B]. This implies the claim immediately.
   Otherwise, κ(G[B]) = 1. In this case there exists a cut vertex x in G[B]. Let B1 , B2 be the
connected components of G[B] − x. Then, α(B1 ) + α(B2 ) ≤ α(G[B]) ≤ 2. Hence, B1 and B2 induce
cliques.
   If B1 ∩ N (S) = ∅, then G is the 1-clique sum of G[V (G) \ B1 ] and G[B1 ∪ {x}] at x. Trivially,
this contradicts G being a smallest counter-example. Suppose then that B1 ∩ N (S) ̸= ∅. By the
same argument if N (S) ∩ B1 = N (x) ∩ B1 = {y} and |B1 | > 1, then G is the 1-clique sum of
G[(V (G) \ B1 ) ∪ {y}] and G[B1 ] at y. This also contradicts G being a smallest counter-example.
Suppose then that either N (S) ∩ B1 = N (x) ∩ B1 = {y} or |B1 | > 1 is false.
   If |B1 | = 1 then the vertex y ∈ B1 must have a neighbour in S. Let w = y. Since B2 induces
a clique, there is a path P ′ which visits exactly the vertices of B2 , with an endpoint that is a
neighbour of x. Letting P ′ be such a path, the path P = P ′ , x, w satisfies all requirements.
   Suppose then that |B1 | > 1. Then N (S) ∩ B1 = N (x) ∩ B1 = {y} is false. Hence, there exists a
vertex w ∈ B1 ∩ N (S) such that w is not the unique neighbour of x in B1 . Let w be such a vertex
and let y be a neighbour of x in B1 distinct from w. Since B1 and B2 induce cliques, there are
paths P1 , P2 such that P2 visits exactly the vertices of B2 , with an endpoint that is a neighbour of
x, and P1 visits exactly the vertices of B1 and the endpoints of P1 are w and y. Letting P1 , P2 be
such paths, the path P = P1 , x, P2 satisfies all requirements.                                     ⋄
   If G[B] is connected, then let w and P be a vertex and path as described in the claim. Since,
α(G[A]) induces a clique, and since S is a vertex-cut of smallest size it is trivial to verify that P
can be extended to a Hamilton path.
   Suppose then that G[B] is disconnected. Then G[B] has two components B1 and B2 both of
which induce cliques, since α(B1 ) + α(B2 ) ≤ α(G[B]) ≤ 2. If |S| = κ(G) = 2 it is trivial that G
has a Hamilton path. Otherwise, |S| = κ(G) = 1. In this case let S = {v}. Then, G is the 1-sum
of G[A ∪ {v}], G[B1 ∪ {v}], and G[B2 ∪ {v}] all at v. This contradicts the fact G is a smallest
counter-example.
   Therefore, we have demonstrated that G being a smallest counter-example leads to a contradic-
tion. This completes the proof.                                                                     □

Lemma 3.1. If G is a connected (K2 + 2K1 )-free and P is a longest path in G, then V (G) \ V (P )
is an independent set.
Proof. Let G be a (K2 + 2K1 )-free and P = v1 , . . . , vk is a longest path in G. If P is a Hamiltonian
path or a path of length n − 1, then the results is trivial. Suppose then that there exists a vertices
u, w ∈ V (G) \ V (P ), and for contradiction suppose (u, w) ∈ E(G). Since P is a longest path, v1
and vk are not adjacent to u or w.
   As G is (K2 + 2K1 )-free, G[{u, w, v1 , vk }] cannot induce a graph isomorphic to K2 + 2K1 . Hence,
(v1 , vk ) ∈ E(G). So the vertices of V (P ) are spanned by a cycle. Since G is connected, either u or
w is adjacent to some vertex on P , or there exits an internal vertex z on a path P ′ which connects
V (P ) and u, such that no internal vertex of P ′ is on P . But this contradicts the fact that P is
longest.                                                                                               □

Proof of Theorem 1.5. Let H be a fixed forest with at most 4 vertices other than K1,3 . If H ∈
{K1 , K2 , 2K1 , P3 , K2 + K1 , P4 }, then every H-free graph is perfect. Hence, for such an H, every

                                            7. Future Work
  To conclude the paper we propose some conjectures and open problems for future work. Of
course, the principle problem in this area remains to resolve Conjecture 1.1, however this seems
out of reach. Instead we focus on introducing problems that seem more approachable, although in
the same vein.
  Extending our work from Section 2 and Section 3:
Problem 7.1. What is the least integer t such that there exists a K1,t -free graph G where χ(G) >
r(G)?
   Phrased differently, is every claw-free graph path-perfect? If yes, then what is the greatest integer
ℓ such that every K1,ℓ -free graph is path-perfect? Notice that the left graph in Figure 1 has χ > r
and is K1,6 -free.
   Similarly, given Lemma 2.2, one can ask if graphs with independence number 4 are path-perfect.
Problem 7.2. Is every graph G with α(G) = 4 path-perfect?
  We also conjecture that Theorem 1.5 can be strengthened.
Conjecture 7.3. If G is 2K2 -free or (K2 + 2K1 )-free, then G is path-perfect.
                                                         28
  Moreover, we believe that an even stronger statement could be true.
Conjecture 7.4. If G is P5 -free graph, then G is path-perfect.
   Of course one can also ask, what is the least integer t, such that there exists a Pt -free graph G
with χ(G) > r(G)? In this case, it is worth noting that the left graph in Figure 1 has χ > r and is
P10 -free.
   Our final problem relating to graphs with a forbidden induced subgraph, is a further weakening
of Hajnal and Erdős’ problem. This weakening is of interest, since it marks a stark difference
χ-boundedness and bounding chromatic number by a function of r. It is not obvious to the authors
what a good candidate for the graph H should be.
Conjecture 7.5. There exists a graph H and a function h = hH such that H contains a cycle and
if G is H-free, then χ(G) ≤ h(r(G)).
  To conclude we conjecture that our main result, Theorem 1.2, can be improved as follows.
Conjecture 7.6. For every constant c > 0 there exists a graph G with
                                                χ(G) > c · r(G).
