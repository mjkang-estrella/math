1.1    Terminology
A sequence s contains a sequence u if some subsequence of s can be changed
into u by a one-to-one renaming of its letters. If s does not contain u, then
s avoids u. The sequence s is called r-sparse if any r consecutive letters in
s are pairwise different. If u is a sequence with r distinct letters, then the
extremal function Ex(u, n) is the maximum length of any r-sparse sequence
with n distinct letters which avoids u.
     An (r, s)-formation is a concatenation of s permutations of r distinct let-
ters. For example abcddcbaadbc is a (4, 3)-formation. Let Fr,s (n) be the max-
imum length of any r-sparse sequence with n distinct letters which avoids all
(r, s)-formations. Klazar [5] proved Fr,2 (n) = O(n) and Fr,3 (n) = O(n) for
every r. Agarwal, Sharir, and Shor [2] proved the lower bound and Nivasch
                                                              1    t        t−1
[6] proved the upper bound to show that Fr,s (n) = n2 t! α(n) ±O(α(n) ) for
all r ≥ 2 and odd s ≥ 5 with t = s−3    2 .
     Nivasch [6] used bounds on formation-free sequences to obtain bounds
on Ex(u, n) by proving that Ex(u, n) ≤ Fr,s−r+1 (n) for any sequence u
with r distinct letters and length s. Let f w(u), the formation width of
u, be the minimum value of s such that there exists an r for which every
(r, s)-formation contains u, and let f l(u), the formation length of u, be the
minimum r such that every (r, f w(u))-formation contains u. By Nivasch’s
proof, f w(u) ≤ s − r + 1 for any sequence u with r distinct letters and length
s.
     If at is an alternation of length t, then f w(at ) ≤ t−1 since every (r, t−1)-
formation contains at for r ≥ 2. Any (r, t − 2)-formation in which order of

    Pettie [7] used the fact that every (4, 4)-formation contains abcacbc to

with order reversing in adjacent permutations would avoid abcacbc, then
f w(abcacbc) = 4. Similarly f w(abcadcbd) = 4.
    Call an (r, s)-formation f binary if there exists a permutation p on r
letters such that every permutation in f is either the same as p or the
reverse of p. Let Ic be the increasing sequence 12 . . . c on c letters. Dc is the
decreasing sequence c(c − 1) . . . 1 on c letters. Given a permutation π ∈ Sc ,
let the sequences Iπ and Dπ be π(1)π(2) . . . π(c) and π(c)π(c − 1) . . . π(1)
respectively.

t permutations, starting with Il and alternating between Il and Dl . For
example, up(3, 3) = 123123123 and alt(3, 3) = 123321123.

3     Binary formations
We determine the minimum function γ(r, s) such that every (γ(r, s), s)-
formation contains a binary (r, s)-formation. Then we use binary formations
to compute f w(u) for every sequence u with two distinct letters. We also
apply binary formations to find every sequence u for which f w(u) ≤ 3. All
sequences u with f w(u) ≤ 3 satisfy Ff l(u),f w(u) (n) = O(n) since Fr,3 (n) =
O(n) for every r, so Ex(u, n) = O(n) by Lemma 2.2.

3.2   Using binary formations to compute f w
If u has one distinct letter, then f w(u) is the length of u. If u has two
distinct letters, then f w(u) also depends only on the length of u.

Lemma 3.5. If a sequence u has two distinct letters and length t, then
f w(u) = t − 1.

the first t − 2 permutations of f (u) avoids u.

second letters, the first permutation of f (u) is ab. If the first i permutations
of f (u) are defined for i < t − 1, then permutation i + 1 of f (u) is the same

denote the sequence obtained by deleting the last letter of u and suppose u

the last letter of the copy of u is the last letter of f (u) by induction on the

Lemma 4.1. If lπ (Ic ) = a and lπ (Dc ) = b, then a + b = c + 1.
Proof. Represent the permutation π by the set of points (i, π(i)). Con-
necting consecutive sections of points with increasing heights partitions the
points into a connected sections. Connecting consecutive sections of points
with decreasing heights partitions the points into b connected sections.
    We count the total number of endpoints of connected sections of points
in two ways so that each connected section of points is considered to have
two endpoints, even when the section consists of a single point. Since every
connected section has two endpoints, then there are a total of 2(a + b)
endpoints. Alternatively every point besides the first and last contributes
exactly two endpoints, while the first and last points each contribute exactly
three endpoints. Thus there are a total of 2c + 2 endpoints, so a + b =
c + 1.                                                                      

4.3    Further bounds on f w
For c ≥ 2 the bounds on l(u) imply (c + 1)k ≤ f w(alt(c, 2k)) ≤ 2(c + 1)k − 1

Theorem 4.7. If c ≥ 2, then f w(Ic Dc Ic ) = c + 3.
Proof. First we prove for every permutation π ∈ Sc that Iπ Dπ Iπ is not a
subsequence of the binary (c, c + 2)-formation Icc Dc2 . Assume for contradic-
tion that Icc Dc2 has the subsequence Iπ Dπ Iπ for some permutation π ∈ Sc .
Since l(Ic Dc ) = c + 1, then the last letter of Dπ must be in the first Dc in
Icc Dc2 . However, the first letter of Iπ is the same as the last letter of Dπ ,
so the first letter of the last Iπ in Iπ Dπ Iπ must be in the last Dc in Icc Dc2 .
Then Iπ = Dc , so the last letter of Dπ is c. This would imply Icc c has the
subsequence Iπ Dπ . Since the last letter of Icc is c, then Iπ Dπ would be a
subsequence of Icc , a contradiction. Thus Icc Dc2 does not have Iπ Dπ Iπ as a
subsequence for any permutation π ∈ Sc .
     Now we show why every binary (c, c + 3)-formation has a subsequence
Iπ Dπ Iπ for some permutation π ∈ Sc . Without loss of generality suppose
the first permutation is Ic . If the binary (c, c + 3)-formation is Icc+3 , then it
has Ic Dc Ic as a subsequence. If the formation has an alternation of Ic and

Dc terms of length at least 3, then also it must have Ic Dc Ic as a subsequence.
Otherwise it has the form Ica Dcb with a+b = c+3. If a ≤ 2, then f has Ic Dc Ic
as a subsequence. If b ≤ 2, then f has Dc Ic Dc as a subsequence. Otherwise f
has the subsequence Iπ Dπ Iπ , such that Iπ is the sequence 1 . . . (b−2)c . . . (b−
1) obtained by reversing the last a − 1 letters of Ic .                            

Lemma 4.8. If c ≥ 2 and π ∈ Sc , then Iπ Dπ is a subsequence of Icc Dc if
and only if π(1) < π(2).

Proof. Let π ∈ Sc and suppose Iπ Dπ is a subsequence of Icc Dc . Then the last
letter of Iπ Dπ , namely π(1), occurs in the last Dc of Icc Dc . If it is not the
only letter of Iπ Dπ occurring in the last Dc , then π(2)π(1) is a subsequence
of Dc . This is possible only if π(1) < π(2).
     If the final Dc contains no letters in Iπ Dπ besides π(1), then the last π(2)
in Iπ Dπ occurs in some Ic . If π(1) > π(2), then the last π(1) in Iπ Dπ can
be replaced with the π(1) in the same permutation as the last π(2) in Iπ Dπ .
This would imply that Iπ Dπ is a subsequence of Icc , which is impossible since
l(Ic Dc ) = c + 1. Thus π(1) < π(2).
     For the other direction suppose π(1) < π(2). Then Iπ Dπ is a subsequence
of Icc+1 with exactly one letter in the last permutation of Icc+1 . Thus Iπ Dπ
is a subsequence of Icc Dc .                                                    

Corollary 4.9. If c ≥ 2 and π ∈ Sc , then Iπ Dπ is a subsequence of Dc Icc if
and only if π(2) < π(1).

Proof. By reflection, Iπ Dπ is a subsequence of Dc Icc if and only if Iπ Dπ
is a subsequence of Dcc Ic . Moreover Iπ Dπ is a subsequence of Dcc Ic if and
only if πr (Iπ Dπ ) is a subsequence of Icc Dc . By Lemma 4.8, πr (Iπ Dπ ) is a
subsequence of Icc Dc if and only if πr (π(1)) < πr (π(2)). Since πr (π(1)) <
πr (π(2)) if and only if π(2) < π(1), then Iπ Dπ is a subsequence of Dc Icc if
and only if π(2) < π(1).                                                     
