University of Sheffield, Sheffield S3 7HF, UK Abstract The bipartite edge frustration of a graph G G , φ ⁡ ( G ) \varphi(G) , is the minimum
number of edges that must be removed from G G to obtain a graph that is
bipartite. Core graphs are singular graphs on 2 2 or more vertices, for which
the kernel of the adjacency matrix contains a full eigenvector. A nut
graph is a core graph of nullity one. Nut graphs G G are non-bipartite. It is
proved here that φ ⁡ ( G ) ≥ 2 \varphi(G)\geq 2 if G G is a nut graph, i.e. every nut
graph requires deletion of at least two edges to produce a bipartite graph. In
addition, we give a constructive proof that for every integer k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 there
exist infinitely many chemical nut graphs G G with φ ⁡ ( G ) = k \varphi(G)=k .
Furthermore, for 3 ≤ d ≤ 8 3\leq d\leq 8 , the values k k , for which a d d -regular nut
graph G G with φ ⁡ ( G ) = k \varphi(G)=k exists, are characterised. It is shown here that
for every integer k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 there exist infinitely many cubic polyhedral nut
graphs G G with φ ⁡ ( G ) = k \varphi(G)=k . Two ways of producing larger nut graphs from
smaller are described: the cut and frustration constructions. One
straightforward consequence is that total double subdivision of a nut graph
(i.e. simultaneous insertion of two degree- 2 2 vertices into every edge) yields
a nut graph. Keywords: Nut graph, core graph, nullity, regular graphs,

the adjacency matrix contains an eigenvector without zero entries (i.e. a full
vector). Nut graphs are defined to have 2 2 or more vertices, nullity one, and
the property that they have no zero entry in their non-trivial kernel
eigenvector [ 54 ] . In other words, nut graphs are precisely the core
graphs with nullity one. They are of interest for several applications in
chemistry and
physics [ 33 , 34 , 42 , 51 , 52 , 53 ] .
The graph K 1 K_{1} is bipartite, but excluded from the definition of core and nut
graphs [ 54 ] . Core graphs may be bipartite or not, but nut graphs
are non-bipartite [ 54 ] . This prompts the question: if nut graphs
are not bipartite, how ‘far from bipartite’ are they? A natural approach to quantifying this notion of bipartivity is based on
counting those edges that break the defining rule of bipartite graphs,
i.e. edges with both endpoints in the same class of a bipartition of V ⁡ ( G ) V(G) . An
edge e ∈ E ⁡ ( G ) e\in E(G) is a frustrated edge with respect to a given
bipartition V ⁡ ( G ) = V 1 ⊔ V 2 V(G)=V_{1}\sqcup V_{2} if both endpoints of e e are in the same
class of the bipartition. The bipartite edge frustration of a graph G G ,
denoted φ ⁡ ( G ) \varphi(G) , is the minimum number of frustrated edges over all
possible bipartitions of the vertex set V ⁡ ( G ) V(G) [ 22 ] . In other
words, φ ⁡ ( G ) \varphi(G) is the smallest number of edges that must be deleted from G G in order to obtain a bipartite spanning subgraph. In these terms, the bipartivity of a graph G G with m m edges is the fraction 1 − φ ⁡ ( G ) / m 1-\varphi(G)/m [ 38 ] . Note that φ ⁡ ( G ) \varphi(G) is a graph invariant,
but that minimum edge count is typically achieved with more than one selection
of frustrated edges. Figure 1 shows examples of nut graphs
with small values of the parameter φ ⁡ ( G ) \varphi(G) . We will present stratified
tabulations of φ \varphi for various classes of nut graph. A large literature in mathematics and computer science deals with versions of
this
problem [ 3 , 4 , 5 , 12 , 23 , 46 , 47 , 55 ] ,
as bipartite edge frustration has been studied under different names. Some
authors denote it by τ odd ​ ( G ) \tau_{\text{odd}}(G) and call it the smallest size of an
odd-cycle edge transversal [ 40 ] . Many authors have studied the
maximum cut (max-cut) problem. For example, in [ 4 ] the notation f ⁡ ( G ) f(G) is used to denote the maximum number of edges of G G crossing between the
two parts of a bipartition, taken over all possible bipartitions of the vertex

and note that κ ⁡ ( G ) + φ ⁡ ( G ) = | E ⁡ ( G ) | \kappa(G)+\varphi(G)=|E(G)| . It is known that determining bipartite edge frustration of a graph, φ ⁡ ( G ) \varphi(G) , is NP-hard in general [ 59 , 60 ] . The
