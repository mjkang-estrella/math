3    Longest perfect matching having no crossings
It is well known that a perfect matching of a set of 2m points that minimizes the sum of the
lengths of its edges, has no crossing edges. This might leads us to think that a longest matching,
i.e. the one that maximizes the sum of the lengths of its edges has many crossings; we will show
now that that is not necessarily the case. We prove the following result.

Theorem 9. There exist point sets S on the plane whose longest perfect matching has no cross-
ings.

Proof. To obtain S, we will use a set of segments obtained by Villanger as presented in a paper
by Tverberg [15]. We describe the point set as follows.
   Consider m straight line segments s1 , . . . , sm such that for each k ≥ 3, sk intersects the
convex hull of si ∪ sj , 1 ≤ i < j < k ≤ m, see Figure 8.

                                 Figure 8: Villanger’s configuration


    Let S(si ) denote the slope of the segment si , 1 ≤ i ≤ m. The set of segments is constructed
in such a way that 0 = S(s1 ) < S(s2 ) < · · · < S(sm ) < π/2 and, given ε > 0, the left endpoint
of each segment si is at distance less than ε from the left endpoint of s1 .
    For each segment si , 1 ≤ i ≤ m, let ai be its left endpoint and bi be its right endpoint. Let
A = {a1 , . . . , am }, B = {b1 , . . . , bm }, and S = A ∪ B. The segments are such that the distance
between any point in A and any point in B is greater than the distance between any pair of
points in A or the distance between any pair of points in B. This forces the longest matching to
contain only edges joining points in A to points in B. The points of B are constructed in such a
way that they all lie roughly on the same vertical line.
    Let ap and aq be two points in A, and br be a point in B. Let Hp,q,r denote the hyperbola
that passes through br and whose foci are ap and aq , see Figure 9. The point set S is constructed
in such a way that, for 2 ≤ s ≤ m, the point bs lies above the right branch of all hyperbolas
Hp,q,r , where p < q ≤ s and r ≤ s − 1.
    Let us observe an implication of the last condition: Take two points ap and aq in A with
p < q, and two points br and bs in B with r < s. Let d(a, b) denote the euclidean distance between
points a and b. Then, d(bs , ap ) − d(bs , aq ) < d(br , ap ) − d(br , aq ) because bs is above the right
branch of hyperbola Hp,q,r , see Figure 9. Then d(bs , ap ) + d(br , aq ) < d(bs , aq ) + d(br , ap ), and
thus, the matching {ap bs , aq br } is shorter than the matching {ap br , aq bs } on these four points.
    We now prove that the matching {s1 , . . . , sm } is in fact the longest perfect matching on S.
We refer to a perfect matching in which each of its segments joins a point in A to a point in B as
a potential matching. As noted before, the longest matching must be a potential matching and
for the proof we consider only potential matchings.
    We define a bijection between the set of potential matchings and the set of all permutations
on [m] = {1, . . . , m} as follows. To the potential matching {a1 bi1 , . . . , am bim } corresponds the
permutation {i1 , . . . , im }. In this manner, if the segment ai bj belongs to a potential matching,
then the number j is in the position i in the corresponding permutation. Therefore, we will
prove that the permutation corresponding to the longest matching is the identity permutation
Id = {1, . . . , m}. To do this, we consider transpositions in which the matching corresponding
to the permutation before the transposition is longer than the matching corresponding to the
permutation after the transposition. We say that such transpositions reduce the length of the
matching.

the longest one in this point set.
