The colors are governed by coloring axioms, which describe which colors a vertex is allowed to
have depending on how the vertices within distance two are structured and whether they are
dominated. Some of these axioms are permanent in the sense that they are to be maintained
throughout the whole game, while other, temporary axioms will be introduced and dropped
at the start and end of different phases of the game. Of course, it is integral to the validity
of the proof that any axiom holds at the time it is introduced. The permanent axioms are as
follows.
 (P1) If a vertex is not yet dominated, it is always white.
 (P2) Only unplayable vertices may be colored red.
 (P3) A vertex adjacent to an undominated leaf or an undominated parent can only be
      white, yellow or green.
 (P4) Once a vertex has been colored yellow, it can only be recolored if it is not adjacent to
      an undominated parent.
 (P5) An orange vertex can have at most one undominated neighbor.
 (P6) No vertex is colored yellow before the start of Phase 5 and no vertex is colored blue
      or orange before the start of Phase 8.
 (P7) Once a leaf has been given a color other than white, it may not be colored white again.
      Furthermore, once a parent has been given a color other than white, it may only be
      colored white again if its leaf is not white anymore.

    As mentioned in §1, each Latin square has six conjugate squares obtained by uniformly per-
muting the co-ordinates within its triples. These conjugates can be labelled by a permutation
giving the new order of the co-ordinates, relative to the former order of (1, 2, 3). For example,
the (1, 2, 3)-conjugate is the square itself and the (2, 1, 3)-conjugate is its matrix transpose. The
(1, 3, 2)-conjugate is found by interchanging columns and symbols, which is another way of saying
that each row, when thought of as a permutation, is replaced by its inverse. A consequence first
noted in [24] is this:
Lemma 2.1. A Latin square is row-Hamiltonian if and only if its (1, 3, 2)-conjugate is row-
Hamiltonian. Hence, ν(L) ∈ {0, 2, 4, 6} for any Latin square L.
    Column permutations and symbol permutations can be defined similarly to row permutations,
and the operations of conjugacy interchange these objects. In particular, the row permutations of
L correspond to column permutations of the (2, 1, 3)-conjugate of L, and to symbol permutations
of the (3, 2, 1)-conjugate of L. This also leads to notions of column-Hamiltonian and symbol-
Hamiltonian squares where all the column and symbol permutations, respectively, are single cycles.
Another view of Theorem 1.1 is that we are showing that any two of the three related notions (row-
Hamiltonian, column-Hamiltonian and symbol-Hamiltonian) can be achieved without achieving the
third property.
    The case when all three properties hold has been studied in some detail. If ν(L) = 6 for a
Latin square L, then L is said to be atomic because it has an indivisible structure mimicking the
structure of cyclic groups of prime order. The papers [4, 17, 19, 22, 24] construct atomic Latin
squares, including several infinite families. An infinite family of Latin squares for which ν = 2 is
constructed in [3]. It is easy to construct Latin squares with ν = 0. In particular, any Latin square
L of order n ⩾ 4 satisfying L0,0 = L1,1 = 0 and L0,1 = L1,0 = 1 will have ν = 0. By a result of
Ryser [21], such Latin squares exist of all orders n ⩾ 4. An explicit example of an infinite family
of Latin squares with ν = 0 is the family of addition tables of the cyclic groups of composite order.
