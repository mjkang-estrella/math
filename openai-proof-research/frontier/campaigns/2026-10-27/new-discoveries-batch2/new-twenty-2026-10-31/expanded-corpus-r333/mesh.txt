Affiliation: Reykjavik University Email: henningu@ru.is August 24, 2026 Abstract Theorems relating permutations with objects in other fields of mathematics are often stated in terms of avoided patterns. Examples include various classes of Schubert varieties from algebraic geometry (Billey and Abe 2013), commuting functions in analysis (Baxter 1964), beta-shifts in dynamical systems (Elizalde 2011) and homology of representations (Sundaram 1994). We present a new algorithm, BiSC, that, given any set of permutations, outputs a conjecture for describing the set in terms of avoided patterns. The algorithm automatically conjectures the statements of known theorems such as the descriptions of smooth (Lakshmibai and Sandhya 1990) and forest-like permutations (Bousquet-Mélou and Butler 2007), Baxter permutations (Chung et al. 1978), stack-sortable (Knuth 1975) and West-2-stack-sortable permutations (West 1990). The algorithm has also been used to discover new theorems and conjectures related to the dihedral and alternating subgroups of the symmetric group, Young tableaux, Wilf-equivalences, and sorting devices. 1 Introduction Permutation patterns have established connections between certain subsets of permutations and objects in other fields, including algebraic geometry (Billey and Abe [AB]), analysis (Chung, Graham, Hoggatt and Kleiman [CGHK78], Graham [Bax64]), dynamical systems (Elizalde [Eli11]), algorithm analysis in computer science (Knuth [Knu75]) and homology (Sundaram [Sun94]). A permutation of length $n$ is a bijection from the set $\{1,\dotsc,n\}$ to itself. We write permutations in one-line notation, where $\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\dotsm\pi_{n}$ is the permutation that sends $i$ to $\pi_{i}$ . The empty permutation is the unique permutation of length $0$ , and is denoted $\epsilon$ . If $w=w_{1}w_{2}\dotsm w_{k}$ is a word of distinct integers, $\mathrm{fl}(w)$ is the permutation obtained by replacing the $i$ th smallest letter in $w$ with $i$ . This is called the flattening of $w$ . A permutation $\pi$ of length $n$ contains a permutation $p$ of length $k$ if there exist indices $1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\dotsm<j_{k}\leq n$ such that $\mathrm{fl}(\pi_{j_{1}}\pi_{j_{2}}\dotsm\pi_{j_{k}})=p$ . In this context $p$ is called a (classical) pattern in $\pi$ and the values $\pi_{j_{1}}\pi_{j_{2}}\dotsm\pi_{j_{k}}$ are an occurrence of $p$ in $\pi$ . Consider, for example, the permutation $\pi=47318265$ . This permutation contains the pattern $231$ since the subword $482$ is an occurrence of $231$ . If $\pi$ does not contain $p$ then it avoids $p$ . The permutation $\pi=47318265$ from above avoids the pattern $4321$ . We let $S$ denote the set of all permutations and $\mathrm{Av}(P)$ denote the permutations that avoid all the patterns in a set $P$ . If $A$ is any set of permutations let $A_{\leq n}$ be the subset of permutations of length at most $n$ , and $A_{n}$ be the subset of permutations with length exactly $n$ . Property of perms. The library contains a tutorial on how to use the algorithms. 2 Learning patterns Given a set of permutations $A$ , we call a set of patterns $P$ a basis for $A$ if $A=\mathrm{Av}(P)$ , and no subset of $P$ has this property. The set $A$ is called a permutation class if $P$ only contains classical patterns. There is a well-known algorithm whose input is a permutation class (known to have a basis with patterns of length at most $m$ ) presented as a list of permutations in length order that can find the basis. Assume we input the permutations below. $\displaystyle 1,12,21,123,132,213,312,321,1234,1243,1324,1423,1432,2134,2143,3124,$ $\displaystyle 3214,4123,4132,4213,4321.$ The first missing permutation is $231$ , so we add it to our potential basis $P=\{231\}$ . When we reach the permutations of length $4$ we see that $1342$ , $2314$ , $2341$ , $2413$ , $2431$ , $3142$ , $3241$ , $3412$ , $3421$ , $4231$ and $4312$ are all missing. All of these, except the last one, contain the pattern $231$ , which is already in the basis, so we should expect them to be missing. The last one, the permutation $4312$ , does not contain the pattern $231$ , so we extend the basis to $P=\{231,4312\}$ . If the input had permutations of length $5$ , we would continue in the same manner: checking whether the missing permutations contain a previously forbidden pattern, and if not, extending the basis by adding new classical patterns. This process continues until we reach $m$ , the length of the longest basis elements. Classical patterns are sufficient to describe some sets of permutations. However, in other cases, they do not suffice. Consider for example the West- $2$ -stack-sortable permutations [Wes90]: $\displaystyle 1,12,21,123,132,213,231,312,321,1234,1243,1324,1342,1423,1432,2134,$ $\displaystyle 2143,2314,2413,2431,3124,3142,3214,3412,3421,4123,4132,4213,4231,$ $\displaystyle 4312,4321,\dotsc,35241,\dotsc.$ The first missing permutations are $2341$ and $3241$ so we would initially suspect $\{2341,3241\}\subseteq P$ . There are many missing permutations of length $5$ , and all are consequences of $2341$ being forbidden, e.g., $34152$ is not in the input since the subword $3452$ is an occurrence of $2341$ . But one of the input permutations is $35241$ , which contains the previously forbidden pattern $3241$ . It seems that the presence of the $5$ inside the occurrence of $3241$ in $35241$ is important. This inspired West [Wes90] to define barred patterns, but we will use the more expressive mesh patterns of Brändén and Claesson [BC11], which allow us to forbid letters from occupying certain regions in a pattern. In the language of mesh patterns, we must find a shading $R$ such that the mesh pattern $(3241,R)$ is contained in the permutation $3241$ but avoided by $35241$ . In this case, it is easy to guess $R=\{(1,4)\}$ (the top-most square between $3$ and $2$ ), which in fact is the correct choice: the basis $P=\{2341,(3241,\{(1,4)\})\}$ exactly describes the West- $2$ -stack-sortable permutations, as was first shown by West [Wes90], although his notation was in terms of the previously mentioned barred patterns. The only permutation of length $3$ in the input is $321$ , which is consistent with our current basis. We see that every permutation of length four is missing until the permutation $2341$ , which makes sense since each contains $(12,\emptyset)$ . But $2341$ also contains $12$ ; in fact it contains several occurrences: $23$ , $24$ and $34$ . These occurrences tell us that the following mesh patterns are actually allowed. The following section presents an algorithmic solution to this problem. 2.1 The $\mathsf{BiSC}$ algorithm In the example with West- $2$ -stack-sortable permutations, we saw the mesh pattern $(3241,\{(1,4)\})$ was added to the basis by considering a permutation in the input of length $5$ . This motivates the first step in the high-level description of $\mathsf{BiSC}$ : (1) $\mathsf{Mine}$ : Record mesh patterns that are contained in permutations in the input In the second step: (2) $\mathsf{Gen}$ : Infer the forbidden patterns from the allowed patterns found in step (1) More precisely, step (1) is accomplished with Algorithm 1 below. Algorithm 1 $\mathsf{Mine}$ : The first step in $\mathsf{BiSC}$ finds all allowed mesh patterns Input: $A$ , a finite set of permutations; and $m$ , an upper bound on the length of the patterns to search for Output: A list, $S$ , consisting of tuples $(p,\mathrm{sh}_{p})$ for all classical patterns, $p$ , of length at most $m$ . For each pattern $p$ , $\mathrm{sh}_{p}$ contains the maximal shadings $R$ of $p$ such that $(p,R)$ has an occurrence in some input permutation 1 Initialize $S=\{(p,\emptyset)\colon p\textrm{ classical pattern of length at most }m\}$ 2 for $\pi\in A$ do 3 for $s\in\mathrm{subwords}_{\leq m}(\pi)$ do 4 Let $p=\mathrm{fl}(s)$ 5 Let $R$ be the maximal shading of $p$ for the occurrence $s$ in $\pi$ 6 if $R\nsubseteq T$ for all shadings $T\in\mathrm{sh}_{p}$ then 7 Add $R$ to $\mathrm{sh}_{p}$ and remove subsets of $R$ In line 1, $\mathrm{subwords}_{\leq m}(\pi)$ consists of subwords of length at most $m$ in the permutation $\pi$ , so for example, $\mathrm{subwords}_{\leq 2}(1324)=\{13,12,14,32,34,24,1,3,2,4,\epsilon\}$ . In line 1, we let $R$ be the maximal shading that can be applied to the classical pattern $p$ while ensuring that $s$ is still an occurrence of $(p,R)$ , see Fig. 1. Figure 1: Applying the maximal shading to the classical pattern $12$ so that $s=24$ is still an occurrence in $1324$ In lines 1-1, $\mathrm{sh}_{p}$ is a set we maintain of maximal shadings of $p$ . For example if we see that is an allowed shading, and then later come across we will only store the second shading since the first has now become redundant. We note that classical patterns $p$ that have no occurrence in any permutation $\pi$ in the input $A$ will appear in the output as $(p,\emptyset)$ . Theorems 2.3 and 2.4 below show that $\mathsf{BiSC}$ will never produce patterns contained in the input and, in some cases, a correct description of the input permutations. These theorems rely on the following two lemmas. Lemma 2.1. If $(p,R)$ is any mesh pattern, of length at most $m$ , that occurs in a permutation in $A$ then there exists a mesh pattern $(p,R^{\prime})$ in the output of $\mathsf{Mine}(A,m)$ such that $R\subseteq R^{\prime}$ . Proof. Let $(p,R)$ be a mesh pattern of length at most $m$ that occurs in the permutation $\pi$ in $A$ . Let $w$ be the subword of $\pi$ which is an occurrence of $(p,R)$ . At some stage in the for-loop in Algorithm 1, we consider the permutation $\pi$ and the subword $w$ . Either $R$ is maximal or is a subset of the maximal shading, $R^{\prime}$ of $s$ in $\pi$ . Furthermore, either of these shadings will be added to $\mathrm{sh}_{p}$ , unless there is already a shading in $\mathrm{sh}_{p}$ that is a superset. As the for-loop continues, the maximal shading of $s$ will never be removed from $\mathrm{sh}_{p}$ unless it is replaced with a superset, which proves the lemma. ∎ Lemma 2.2. If a set of permutations $A$ is defined by the avoidance of a (possibly infinite) list of classical patterns $P$ and $(q,\mathrm{sh}_{q})$ with $\mathrm{sh}_{q}\neq\emptyset$ is in the output of $\mathsf{Mine}(A_{\leq n},m)$ then $q$ is not in the list $P$ and $\mathrm{sh}_{q}$ only contains the full shading of $q$ . Proof. If $\mathrm{sh}_{q}$ does not contain the complete shading then $q$ is not in $A$ , but appears as a classical pattern in some larger permutation $\pi$ in $A$ . Since $q$ is not in $A$ it must contain one of the forbidden patterns $p$ in $P$ , which would imply that $p$ also occurs in $\pi$ , contradicting the fact that $\pi$ is in $A$ . ∎ Step (2) in the high-level description of the algorithm is implemented in Algorithm 2, where we generate the forbidden patterns from the allowed patterns found in step (1). Algorithm 2 $\mathsf{Gen}$ : The second step in $\mathsf{BiSC}$ generates the forbidden patterns Input: $S$ , a list of classical patterns along with shadings $\mathrm{sh}_{p}$ , ordered by the length of the patterns Output: A list consisting of $(p,\mathrm{forb}_{p})$ where $p$ is a classical pattern and $\mathrm{forb}_{p}$ is a set of minimal forbidden shadings 1 for $(p,\mathrm{sh}_{p})\in S$ do 2 Let $\mathrm{forb}_{p}$ be the minimal shadings of $p$ that are not contained in any member of $\mathrm{sh}_{p}$ 3 for $R\in\mathrm{forb}_{p}$ do 4 if $R$ is a consequence of some shading in $\mathrm{forb}_{q}$ for a pattern $q$ contained in $p$ then 5 Remove $R$ from $\mathrm{forb}_{p}$ To explain lines 2–2 in the algorithm assume $p=1243$ and the shading, $R$ , on the left in Fig. 2 is one of the shadings in $\mathrm{forb}_{p}$ on line 2. Assume also that earlier in the outer-most for-loop, we generated $q=12$ with the shading $R^{\prime}$ on the right in Fig. 2. Then the shading $R$ is removed from $\mathrm{forb}_{p}$ on line 2 since any permutation containing $(p,R)$ , also contains $(q,R^{\prime})$ . Figure 2: The mesh patterns $(p,R)$ and $(q,R^{\prime})$ We now define the full algorithm as $\mathsf{BiSC}(A,m)=\mathsf{Gen}(\mathsf{Mine}(A,m))$ . The next two theorems show that the output of $\mathsf{BiSC}$ is correct, in the sense that it will never output patterns contained in a permutation in the input, and can give a proof of the description if a bound is known on the length of the patterns. Theorem 2.3. Let $A$ be any set of permutations. Then for all positive integers $N\geq n$ and $m$ $A_{\leq n}\subseteq\mathrm{Av}(\mathsf{BiSC}(A_{\leq N},m))_{\leq n}.$ Furthermore, if the set $A$ is defined in terms of a finite list of patterns (classical or not) whose longest pattern has length $k$ , and if $N\geq n\geq k$ , $m\geq k$ , then there is equality between the two sets above. Proof. To prove the subset relation let $\pi$ be a permutation in $A_{\leq n}$ and $(p,R)$ be a mesh pattern of length at most $m$ contained in $\pi$ . By Lemma 2.1 there is a pattern $(p,R^{\prime})$ in the output from $\mathsf{Mine}(A_{\leq N},m)$ such that $R\subseteq R^{\prime}$ . This implies that $R$ is not one of the shadings in $\mathrm{forb}_{p}$ and therefore $(p,R)$ is not in the output of $\mathsf{BiSC}(A_{\leq n},m)$ , and therefore $\pi$ is in $\mathrm{Av}(\mathsf{BiSC}(A_{\leq N},m))_{\leq n}$ . To prove the equality of the sets, let $\pi$ be a permutation that is not in the set on the left because it contains a mesh pattern $(p,R)$ from the list defining $A$ . If $p$ never occurs as a classical pattern in a permutation in $A_{\leq N}$ then $(p,\emptyset)$ is in the output of $\mathsf{Mine}(A_{\leq N},m)$ which implies that $(p,\emptyset)$ is in the output of $\mathsf{BiSC}(A_{\leq N},m)$ . This implies that $\pi$ is not in the set on the right. If, however, $p$ occurs in some permutations in $A_{\leq N}$ , then every occurrence of a mesh pattern $(p,R^{\prime})$ must satisfy $R\nsubseteq R^{\prime}$ . This implies that when $\mathrm{forb}_{p}$ is created in line 2 in Algorithm 2, it contains $R$ or a non-empty subset, $R^{\prime\prime}$ , of it. Without loss of generality, we assume that $R^{\prime\prime}$ is not removed due to redundancy in line 2. This implies that $(p,R^{\prime\prime})$ is in the output of $\mathsf{BiSC}(A_{\leq N},m)$ , so $\pi$ is not in the set on the right. ∎ We can strengthen the previous theorem if the input $A$ to $\mathsf{BiSC}$ is defined in terms of classical patterns. In cases where the human mathematician (or a future algorithm) has verified the output of $\mathsf{BiSC}$ , a natural follow-up question is to try to enumerate the permutations in the set by length.
