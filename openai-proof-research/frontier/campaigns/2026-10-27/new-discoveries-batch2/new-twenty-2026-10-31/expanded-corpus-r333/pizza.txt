Date: August 24, 2026. Abstract. We are interested in conjecturing the sign of the pizza quantity $P(\mathcal{H},\mathbb{B}(a,R))$ for the irreducible Coxeter arrangements $\mathcal{H}$ of type $A_{n}$ , where $n\equiv 2$ or $3\bmod 4$ , and type $D_{n}$ , where $n$ is odd. Our approach is to express the pizza quantity in terms of the pizza quantity of subarrangements known as $2$ -structures, and we obtain the first non-zero term in the multivariate Taylor expansion. Key words and phrases: Coxeter arrangements, $2$ -structures, Pizza Theorem, reflection groups 2020 Mathematics Subject ClassificationPrimary 20F55, 51F15; Secondary 51M20, 51M25. 1. Introduction For an hyperplane arrangement $\mathcal{H}$ in an inner product space $V$ let $\mathscr{T}$ denote the set of chambers of the arrangement $\mathcal{H}$ . Let $T_{0}$ be a base chamber and let the sign $(-1)^{T}$ be $-1$ to the number of hyperplanes that separate the chamber $T$ from the base chamber $T_{0}$ . The pizza quantity for a measurable set $K$ in the space $V$ is defined in [5] to be the alternating sum (1.1) $\displaystyle P(\mathcal{H},K)$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathscr{T}}(-1)^{T}\cdot\Vol(K\cap T).$ The notion to have in mind is that two friends are sharing a pizza. They cut the pizza with the hyperplanes in $\mathcal{H}$ and then alternate taking slices. The pizza quantity measures the difference in the amount of pizza they each receive. Recall that a Coxeter arrangement is an hyperplane arrangement which is preserved under reflections in each of its hyperplanes, and the group generated is finite. For balls and for Coxeter arrangements satisfying the parity condition, the following result states that the two friends can share the pizza equally as long as the ball contains the origin. Theorem 1.1 (Ehrenborg–Morel–Readdy [5]). Let $\mathcal{H}$ be a Coxeter arrangement on a finite-dimensional inner product space $V$ such that $|\mathcal{H}|\geq\dim(V)$ . Assume that the ball $\mathbb{B}(a,R)$ contains the origin, that is, $R\geq\|a\|$ . (i) If the number of hyperplanes is strictly greater than the dimension of $V$ and has the same parity as that dimension then the pizza quantity $P(\mathcal{H},\mathbb{B}(a,R))$ vanishes. (ii) If the number of hyperplanes is equal to the dimension of $V$ then $P(\mathcal{H},\mathbb{B}(a,R))$ is independent of the radius $R$ . This result was known in a few cases: For the type $B_{2}$ it was stated as a problem by Upton [11]. For the dihedral arrangement $I_{2}(2k)$ , where $k\geq 2$ , this result was first proved by Goldberg [8]. For a product arrangement of type $A_{1}\times I_{2}(2k)$ , again $k\geq 2$ , it was proved by Frederickson [7]. Furthermore, Brailov had proved the result independently for an arrangement of type $B_{n}$ in [2]. Note that Theorem 1.1 does not yield a satisfactory answer for the arrangements where the parities of the number of hyperplanes and the dimension differ. The irreducible types of Coxeter arrangements that do not satisfy the parity condition are $I_{2}(2k+1)$ where $k\geq 1$ , $A_{n}$ where $n\equiv 2$ or $3\bmod 4$ , and $D_{n}$ where $n$ is odd. For the two-dimensional dihedral arrangements Mabry and Deiermann [10] obtained the following result. Theorem 1.2 (Mabry–Deiermann [10]). Let $\mathcal{H}$ be an arrangement of type $I_{2}(m)$ where $m\geq 3$ and $m$ is odd. Let $a$ be a point in the interior of a chamber $T$ of the arrangement such that $\|a\|\leq R$ . Then the sign of the pizza quantity is given by $\displaystyle(-1)^{(m-3)/2}\cdot(-1)^{T}\cdot P(\mathcal{H},\mathbb{B}(a,R))$ $\displaystyle>0.$ In terms of pizza, assume that you and your friend are sharing a two-dimensional pizza and you would like to maximize the amount of pizza you receive. If you cut the pizza with $m\equiv 3\bmod 4$ lines in a dihedral arrangement you should make sure you take the slice containing the center of the pizza. On the other hand, if $m\equiv 1\bmod 4$ you should avoid the slice containing the center. Hence it is natural to conjecture the following: Conjecture 1.3 (Ehrenborg–Morel–Readdy [5]). Let $\mathcal{H}$ be a Coxeter arrangement and let $a\in V$ such that $\|a\|\leq R$ . Assume that the number of hyperplanes of $\mathcal{H}$ is greater than the dimension of $V$ and that these two quantities differ in their parities. Then the pizza quantity $P(\mathcal{H},\mathbb{B}(a,R))$ is zero if and only if the center $a$ lies on one of the hyperplanes of $\mathcal{H}$ . This conjecture is basically Conjecture 9.5 in [5]. Our purpose in this paper is to generate data such that we can predict the sign of the pizza quantity given the sign of the open chamber that contains the center $a$ , in the case when the Coxeter arrangement has type $A_{n}$ or $D_{n}$ . Our approach for obtaining data follows the idea of [6] of expressing the pizza quantity in terms of pizza quantities of smaller subarrangements, known as $2$ -structures. These subarrangements were introduced by Herb in the study of the characters of discrete series representations [9]. In the cases we are interested in, the $2$ -structures all have the type $A_{1}^{k}$ . For the type $A_{1}^{k}$ we obtain the multivariate Taylor series for the pizza quantity, yielding the Taylor series for the desired pizza quantity of types $A_{n}$ and $D_{n}$ . We were able to do the (computer) calculations up to and including dimension $7$ , before the polynomials became too unwieldy. Lastly, we return to type $D_{n}$ , where we make a conjecture that would imply the sign for arrangements of type $D_{n}$ where $n$ is odd. 2. Coxeter arrangements, pseudo-root systems and $2$ -structures Given two hyperplane arrangements $\mathcal{H}_{1}$ and $\mathcal{H}_{2}$ in the two spaces $V_{1}$ and $V_{2}$ , respectively. The product arrangement $\mathcal{H}_{1}\times\mathcal{H}_{2}$ in $V_{1}\times V_{2}$ is defined to be $\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}\times\mathcal{H}_{2}$ $\displaystyle=\{H\times V_{2}:H\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\}\cup\{V_{1}\times H:H\in\mathcal{H}_{2}\}.$ A Coxeter arrangement $\mathcal{H}$ is a finite set of hyperplanes such that the arrangement is invariant under the orthogonal reflections in any of its hyperplanes, and the group (known as the Coxeter group) generated by these reflections is finite. Note that the class of Coxeter arrangements is closed under Cartesian products. Furthermore, call an arrangement irreducible if it cannot be written as a Cartesian product. The irreducible Coxeter arrangements have been classified; see for instance [1]. Their types are $A_{n}$ for $n\geq 1$ ; $B_{n}$ for $n\geq 2$ (also known as type $B/C$ ); $D_{n}$ for $n\geq 3$ (note that $D_{3}$ is isomorphic to $A_{3}$ ); $E_{6}$ , $E_{7}$ and $E_{8}$ ; $F_{4}$ ; $H_{3}$ and $H_{4}$ ; and the dihedral arrangement $I_{2}(m)$ where $m\geq 2$ (observe that $I_{2}(2)\cong A_{1}^{2}$ , $I_{2}(3)\cong A_{2}$ and $I_{2}(4)\cong B_{2}$ ). Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional real vector space with an inner product $(\cdot,\cdot)$ . For every non-zero vector $\alpha\in V$ , let $H_{\alpha}=\{x\in V:(\alpha,x)=0\}$ denote the hyperplane orthogonal to $\alpha$ , and let $s_{\alpha}$ be the orthogonal reflection in the hyperplane $H_{\alpha}$ . Definition 2.1. A subset $\Phi$ of $V$ is a pseudo-root system if: (a) $\Phi$ is a finite set of unit vectors; (b) for all $\alpha,\beta\in\Phi$ , we have $s_{\beta}(\alpha)\in\Phi$ . Note that a consequence of condition (b) is that $\alpha\in\Phi$ implies $-\alpha\in\Phi$ by setting $\alpha=\beta$ . Elements of $\Phi$ are called pseudo-roots. Let the Coxeter group $W$ be the group generated by all the reflections $s_{\alpha}$ for $\alpha$ in the pseudo-root system $\Phi$ . For a group element $w$ in $W$ the sign of the element $w$ is the sign of the chamber $w(T_{0})$ , that is, $(-1)^{w}=(-1)^{w(T_{0})}$ . Note that this definition is same as defining the sign of $w$ as $(-1)^{\ell(w)}$ where $\ell$ is the length function in the Coxeter system. The elements of a pseudo-root system $\Phi$ are naturally divided into positive and negative roots, that is, the following disjoint union holds: $\Phi=\Phi^{+}\sqcup\Phi^{-}$ . We use the characterization given in [4, Lemma B.1.9]. Define the lexicographic order on $\mathbb{R}^{r}$ as follows: $(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})<_{\text{lex}}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{r})$ if there is an index $i$ such that $x_{1}=y_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1}=y_{i-1}$ and $x_{i}<y_{i}$ . Then Lemma B.1.9 is the following statement. Lemma 2.2. Let $\Phi$ be a pseudo-root system in $V$ and assume that $v_{1}$ through $v_{r}$ are linearly independent elements of $V$ such that no pseudo-root of $\Phi$ is orthogonal to every $v_{i}$ . Then the set $\Phi^{+}$ defined by $\displaystyle\Phi^{+}$ $\displaystyle=\{\alpha\in\Phi:((\alpha,v_{1}),\ldots,(\alpha,v_{r}))>_{\text{lex}}(0,\ldots,0)\}$ is a system of positive pseudo-roots. Given a pseudo-root system $\Phi$ . We obtain a Coxeter arrangement by considering the collection $\mathcal{H}_{\Phi}=\{H_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\}$ . Moreover, the base chamber $T_{0}$ is on the positive side on each hyperplane, that is, $T_{0}=\bigcap_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\{x\in V:(\alpha,x)\geq 0\}$ . Similarly, given a Coxeter arrangement we obtain a pseudo-root system by considering the unit vectors orthogonal to the hyperplanes. The positive pseudo-roots are on the same side as the base chamber, that is, $\Phi^{+}=\{\alpha\in\Phi:\forall x\in T_{0}\>\>(\alpha,x)\geq 0\}$ . For a root system $\Phi$ with positive roots $\Phi^{+}$ we define the Jacobian to be the product $\displaystyle J(a)$ $\displaystyle=\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}(\alpha,a).$ Note that the Jacobian is the polynomial which is zero on all the hyperplanes in the arrangement associated with $\Phi$ . Moreover, any skew symmetric polynomial on $V$ factors as the Jacobian times a polynomial invariant under $W$ . The two types of irreducible Coxeter arrangements, equivalently pseudo-root systems, we consider in this paper are the types $A_{n}$ and $D_{n}$ . For each type we present the positive pseudo-roots and the Jacobian. $(A_{n})$ Let $V$ be the $n$ -dimensional space $\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n+1}=0\}$ . The positive pseudo-roots are $\displaystyle\Phi^{+}$ $\displaystyle=\{(e_{i}-e_{j})/\sqrt{2}:1\leq i<j\leq n+1\}.$ In this type the Jacobian is the Vandermonde product up to a scalar $\displaystyle J(a)$ $\displaystyle={2}^{-(n+1)n/4}\cdot\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n+1}(a_{i}-a_{j}).$ $(D_{n})$ Let $V$ be $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ . The positive pseudo-roots are $\displaystyle\Phi^{+}$ $\displaystyle=\{(e_{i}\pm e_{j})/\sqrt{2}:1\leq i<j\leq n\}.$ The Jacobian in this type is given by $\displaystyle J(a)$ $\displaystyle={2}^{-n\cdot(n-1)/2}\cdot\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(a_{i}-a_{j})\cdot(a_{i}+a_{j})$ $\displaystyle={2}^{-n\cdot(n-1)/2}\cdot\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(a_{i}^{2}-a_{j}^{2}),$ that is, the Vandermonde product in the squares $a_{1}^{2},\ldots,a_{n}^{2}$ . We now present $2$ -structures and their basic properties. Herb introduced them for crystaligraphic root systems in order to study the characters of discrete series representations [9]. We use Definition B.2.1 of [4], which works for pseudo-root systems. Definition 2.3. Let $\Phi$ be a pseudo-root system with Coxeter group $W$ . A $2$ -structure for $\Phi$ is a subset $\varphi$ of $\Phi$ satisfying the following properties: (a) The subset $\varphi$ is a disjoint union $\varphi=\varphi_{1}\sqcup\varphi_{2}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\varphi_{r}$ , where the $\varphi_{i}$ are pairwise orthogonal subsets of $\varphi$ and each of them is an irreducible pseudo-root system of type $A_{1}$ , $B_{2}$ or $I_{2}(2^{k})$ for $k\geq 3$ . (b) Let $\varphi^{+}=\varphi\cap\Phi^{+}$ . If $w$ is an element in $W$ such that $w(\varphi^{+})=\varphi^{+}$ then the sign of $w$ is positive, that is, $(-1)^{w}=1$ . We denote by $\mathcal{T}(\Phi)$ the set of $2$ -structures for $\Phi$ . Furthermore, to each $2$ -structure $\varphi$ , we can associate a sign $\epsilon(\varphi)$ ; see [4, Section 3 and Appendix B] . Two essential properties of the sign are described as follows. Lemma 2.4. (a) Let $w$ be in the Coxeter group such that $w(\varphi^{+})\subseteq\Phi^{+}$ . Then the following identity holds: $\epsilon(w(\varphi))=(-1)^{w}\cdot\epsilon(\varphi)$ . (b) The alternating sum of all the signs of $2$ -structures is equal to $1$ , that is, $\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{T}(\Phi)}\epsilon(\varphi)$ $\displaystyle=1.$ For part (a) see Lemma B.2.10 in [4] and for part (b) Proposition 3.1.1 in the same reference. Next we need Theorem 3.2.1 in [4]. However, we only need it in the case when the valuation is the volume. Hence we can consider the more straightforward case: when the valuation vanishes on lower dimensional cones. Let $\mathcal{C}(V)$ be the collection of cones in the vector space $V$ and let $\mathcal{B}(V)$ be the smallest Boolean algebra that contains $\mathcal{C}(V)$ and is closed under unions and intersections. A valuation $v$ on $\mathcal{B}(V)$ is a function such that $v(\emptyset)=0$ and $v(S)+v(T)=v(S\cap T)+v(S\cup T)$ for all $S,T\in\mathcal{B}(V)$ . For a hyperplane arrangement $\mathcal{H}$ and a valuation $v$ define the pizza quantity with respect to the valuation $v$ as the alternating sum $\displaystyle\Pi(\mathcal{H},v)$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathscr{T}}(-1)^{T}\cdot v(T).$ Theorem 3.2.1 in [4] implies the following statement. For a pseudo-root system of type $A_{n}$ or of type $D_{n}$ , each 2-structure’s index set is a set of maximal matchings on a set $\{1,2,\ldots,r\}$ . Let the matching $M$ be given by (2.1) $\displaystyle M$ $\displaystyle=\{(i_{1}<j_{1}),(i_{2}<j_{2}),\ldots,(i_{m}<j_{m})\},$ where $m=\lfloor r/2\rfloor$ . The $2$ -structure $\varphi$ indexed by the matching $M$ in equation (2.1) is described by $\displaystyle\varphi$ $\displaystyle=\{\pm(e_{i_{1}}-e_{j_{1}})/\sqrt{2},\pm(e_{i_{2}}-e_{j_{2}})/\sqrt{2},\ldots,\pm(e_{i_{m}}-e_{j_{m}})/\sqrt{2}\}.$ Note that $\varphi$ has type $A_{1}^{m}$ . $(D_{n})$ The $2$ -structures of a pseudo-root system of type $D_{n}$ have the type $A_{1}^{2m}$ where $m=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ . In both type $A_{n}$ and type $D_{n}$ the sign $\epsilon(\varphi)$ of the $2$ -structure $\varphi$ is given by the sign of the matching $M$ , that is, $\epsilon(\varphi)=(-1)^{M}$ . Multivariate Taylor expansion of the pizza quantity We begin to consider the pizza quantity of an arrangement of type $A_{1}^{k}$ in an $n$ -dimensional space. Let $\beta_{m}$ be the volume of the $m$ -dimensional unit ball, that is, $\displaystyle\beta_{m}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{m/2}}{\Gamma(m/2+1)}.$ Lemma 3.1. Let $V$ be an $n$ -dimensional space and let $\mathcal{H}$ be the Coxeter arrangement of type $A_{1}^{k}$ . That is, $\mathcal{H}=\{H_{f}\}_{f\in E}$ where $E=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\}$ is a set of $k$ orthogonal unit vectors. The base chamber $T_{0}$ is the cone generated by the set $E$ and all vectors orthogonal to the set $E$ . Let $a$ be a point in $V$ such that $0\in\mathbb{B}(a,1)$ . Then the pizza quantity is given by the $k$ -dimensional integral $\displaystyle P(\mathcal{H},\mathbb{B}(a,1))$ $\displaystyle=2^{k}\cdot\beta_{n-k}\cdot\int_{0}^{(f_{1},a)}\cdots\int_{0}^{(f_{k},a)}\left(1-t_{1}^{2}-\cdots-t_{k}^{2}\right)^{(n-k)/2}\>dt_{1}\cdots dt_{k}.$ If $n$ and $k$ have the same parity, then the pizza quantity is a polynomial in $a$ of degree at most $n$ . Proof. Assume that the point $a$ lies in the base chamber $T_{0}$ , that is, $(f_{i},a)\geq 0$ for all $i$ . The arrangement $\mathcal{H}$ consists of the $k$ hyperplanes $H_{i}=\{x\in V:(f_{i},x)=0\}$ , $1\leq i\leq k$ , and cuts the ball $\mathbb{B}(a,1)$ into $2^{k}$ pieces. Furthermore, cut the ball with the affine hyperplanes $\{x\in V:(f_{i},x)=2\cdot(f_{i},a)\}$ , yielding $3^{k}$ pieces. We index these pieces by a sign vector $(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})\in\{0,\pm 1\}^{k}$ . Let $\displaystyle H^{+1}_{i}$ $\displaystyle=\{x\in V:2\cdot(f_{i},a)\leq(f_{i},x)\},$ $\displaystyle H^{0}_{i}$ $\displaystyle=\{x\in V:0\leq(f_{i},x)\leq 2\cdot(f_{i},a)\},$ $\displaystyle H^{-1}_{i}$ $\displaystyle=\{x\in V:(f_{i},x)\leq 0\},$ and finally let $P(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})=\mathbb{B}(a,1)\cap H^{c_{1}}_{1}\cap\cdots\cap H^{c_{k}}_{k}$ . Note that the two pieces $\displaystyle P(0,\ldots,0,+1,c_{i+1},\ldots,c_{k})\text{ and }P(0,\ldots,0,-1,c_{i+1},\ldots,c_{k})$ are reflections of each other in the affine hyperplane $\{x\in V:(f_{i},x)=(f_{i},a)\}$ and hence they have the same volume. But their volumes cancel in the pizza quantity. By this sign-reversing involution, the only remaining piece is $P(0,\ldots,0)$ , hence $P(\mathcal{H},\mathbb{B}(a,1))=\Vol(P(0,\ldots,0))$ . Pick $t_{1}$ through $t_{k}$ such that $-(f_{i},a)\leq t_{i}\leq(f_{i},a)$ . These values describe a point $\displaystyle x$ $\displaystyle=a+t_{1}\cdot f_{1}+\cdots+t_{k}\cdot f_{k},$ that lies in the intersection $P(0,\ldots,0)\cap(\Span(E)+a)$ . The cross section of $P(0,\ldots,0)$ parallel to $E^{\perp}$ containing the point $x$ is an $(n-k)$ -dimensional ball of radius $\sqrt{1-t_{1}^{2}-\cdots-t_{k}^{2}}$ . Hence we conclude $\displaystyle P(\mathcal{H},\mathbb{B}(a,1))$ $\displaystyle=\beta_{n-k}\cdot\int_{-(f_{1},a)}^{(f_{1},a)}\cdots\int_{-(f_{k},a)}^{(f_{k},a)}\left(1-t_{1}^{2}-\cdots-t_{k}^{2}\right)^{(n-k)/2}\>dt_{1}\cdots dt_{k}$ $\displaystyle=2^{k}\cdot\beta_{n-k}\cdot\int_{0}^{(f_{1},a)}\cdots\int_{0}^{(f_{k},a)}\left(1-t_{1}^{2}-\cdots-t_{k}^{2}\right)^{(n-k)/2}\>dt_{1}\cdots dt_{k}.$ Lifting the restriction that the point $a$ lies in the base chamber $T_{0}$ , the above argument holds with volume replaced with signed volume. Note that the integrand is a polynomial when $n-k$ is even. In this case, the pizza quantity is a polynomial in $a$ of degree at most $2\cdot(n-k)/2+k=n$ . ∎ We now briefly consider the case where the $2$ -structures of the Coxeter arrangement have type $A_{1}^{k}$ and $k$ has the same parity as the dimension $n$ of the space $V$ . Proposition 3.2. Let $V$ be an $n$ -dimensional space. Let $\Phi$ be a pseudo-root system in $V$ of type different than $A_{1}^{n}$ . Let $a$ be a point such that $\|a\|\leq 1$ . Assume that the type of the $2$ -structures of $\Phi$ are $A_{1}^{k}$ . If $n$ and $k$ have the same parity then the pizza quantity $P(\mathcal{H}_{\Phi},\mathbb{B}(a,1))$ vanishes. Proof. Let the valuation $v$ be given by $v(S)=\Vol(\mathbb{B}(a,1)\cap S)$ . By Theorem 2.5 we express the pizza quantity as the sum $\displaystyle P(\mathcal{H},\mathbb{B}(a,1))$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{T}(\Phi)}\epsilon(\varphi)\cdot P(\mathcal{H}_{\varphi},\mathbb{B}(a,1)),$ where $\varphi$ ranges over all $2$ -structures of the pseudo-root system $\Phi$ . Since $n$ and $k$ have the same parity, each summand is a polynomial of degree at most $n$ ; hence the pizza quantity is a polynomial of degree at most $n$ in the variable $a$ . Note that the zeros of this polynomial include the hyperplanes of the arrangement $\mathcal{H}$ , and there are more than $n$ hyperplanes. Hence the polynomial is the zero polynomial. ∎ Note that this result is not as strong as Theorem 1.1 in [5]. However, this result lets us concentrate on the case when the parities of $n$ and $k$ differ. Through the remainder of the paper we have this assumption. Let $c_{m}$ be the coefficient $(-1)^{m}\cdot\binom{(n-k)/2}{m}$ where the binomial coefficient is given by $\binom{\alpha}{m}=\alpha\cdot(\alpha-1)\cdots(\alpha-m+1)/m!$ . Note that the polynomial $T_{d}$ is symmetric in the variables $x_{1}$ through $x_{k}$ . Moreover, $T_{d}$ is an odd function in each variable. Lemma 3.3. Given the arrangement $\mathcal{H}=(H_{e_{i}})_{1\leq i\leq k}$ of type $A_{1}^{k}$ and a point $x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})$ such that $\|x\|\leq 1$ . Proof. Note that Taylor’s theorem implies $\displaystyle(1-x)^{(n-k)/2}$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geq 0}c_{m}\cdot x^{m},$ which converges for $-1\leq x\leq 1$ . Letting $x$ be the sum $t_{1}^{2}+\cdots+t_{k}^{2}$ in this series yields $\displaystyle(1-t_{1}^{2}-\cdots-t_{k}^{2})^{(n-k)/2}$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geq 0}c_{m}\cdot(t_{1}^{2}+\cdots+t_{k}^{2})^{m}$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geq 0}c_{m}\cdot\sum_{r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}=m}\binom{m}{r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}}\cdot t_{1}^{2r_{1}}\cdots t_{k}^{2r_{k}}.$ Note that this power series converges for $t_{1}^{2}+\cdots+t_{k}^{2}\leq 1$ . Now the result follows by integrating each variable $t_{i}$ from $0$ to $x_{i}$ . Furthermore, it converges when $x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{k}^{2}\leq 1$ which contains the ball $\|x\|\leq 1$ . ∎ For $\psi$ a set of $k$ orthogonal unit vectors in the space $V$ , that is, $\psi=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\}$ , define $T_{d}(\psi;a)$ to be the polynomial $\displaystyle T_{d}(\psi;a)$ $\displaystyle=T_{d}((f_{1},a),\ldots,(f_{k},a)).$ Note that $T_{d}(\psi;a)$ is a polynomial in $a$ of degree $d$ . Furthermore, the polynomial is skew-symmetric for $s_{\alpha}$ where $\alpha\in\psi$ , that is, $T_{d}(s_{\alpha}(\psi);a)=-T_{d}(\psi;a)=T_{d}(\psi;s_{\alpha}(a))$ . Lastly, it also satisfies $T_{d}(w(\psi);w(a))=T_{d}(\psi;a)$ for all $w\in W$ . Since the Coxeter group $W$ acts on the set of $2$ -structures $\mathcal{T}(\Phi)$ we can rewrite the sum as $\displaystyle Z_{d}(\Phi;w(a))$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{w(\varphi)\in\mathcal{T}(\Phi)}\epsilon(w(\varphi))\cdot T_{d}((w(\varphi))^{+};w(a)).$ Note that $(w(\varphi))^{+}=w(\varphi)\cap\Phi^{+}$ consists of positive pseudo-roots. However there exists an element $v$ in the Coxeter group $W$ of the $2$ -system $w(\varphi)$ such that $v(w(\varphi^{+}))=(w(\varphi))^{+}$ . That is, $v$ is the commutative product of the reflections $s_{\alpha}$ where the pseudo-roots $\alpha$ are the negative roots in $w(\varphi^{+})$ , that is, $\alpha\in w(\varphi^{+})\cap\Phi^{-}=w(\varphi^{+})\cap(\Phi-\Phi^{+})$ . Using that the polynomial $T_{d}$ is skew-symmetric, we have that $T_{d}(v(w(\varphi^{+}));w(a))=(-1)^{v}\cdot T_{d}(w(\varphi^{+});w(a))$ . Hence the above sum is given by $\displaystyle\sum_{w(\varphi)\in\mathcal{T}(\Phi)}\epsilon(w(\varphi))\cdot T_{d}(v(w(\varphi^{+}));w(a))$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{w(\varphi)\in\mathcal{T}(\Phi)}\epsilon(w(\varphi))\cdot(-1)^{v}\cdot T_{d}(w(\varphi^{+});w(a))$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{w(\varphi)\in\mathcal{T}(\Phi)}\epsilon(w(\varphi))\cdot(-1)^{v}\cdot T_{d}(\varphi^{+};a).$ But note that $w(\varphi)=v(w(\varphi))$ . Since $v(w(\varphi^{+}))\subseteq\Phi^{+}$ , Lemma 2.4 part (a) implies that the signs satisfy $\epsilon(w(\varphi))=\epsilon(v(w(\varphi)))=(-1)^{vw}\cdot\epsilon(\varphi)=(-1)^{w}\cdot\epsilon(\varphi)\cdot(-1)^{v}$ . Hence the sum is given by $\displaystyle(-1)^{w}\cdot\sum_{w(\varphi)\in\mathcal{T}(\Phi)}\epsilon(\varphi)\cdot T_{d}(\varphi^{+};a).$ Finally by replacing $w(\varphi)\in\mathcal{T}(\Phi)$ with $\varphi\in\mathcal{T}(\Phi)$ the result follows. ∎ Theorem 3.5. Let $\mathcal{H}$ be a Coxeter arrangement in a space $V$ of dimension $n$ with positive pseudo-roots $\Phi^{+}$ . Lemma 5.1. The polynomial $Z_{d}(\Phi;a)$ , which is homogenous of degree $d$ , satisfies the following properties: (a) It is invariant under the reflection $s_{i}$ , that is, $Z_{d}(\Phi;s_{i}(a))=Z_{d}(\Phi;a)$ . In other words, $Z_{d}(\Phi;a)$ is a polynomial in the variables $a_{1}^{2},\ldots,a_{n}^{2}$ . (b) The polynomial $Z_{d}(\Phi;a)$ contains no monomial where all the exponents are positive. (c) The polynomial $Z_{d}(\Phi;a)$ contains no monomial where two exponents are equal. Proof. To prove part (a) it is enough to verify that $T_{d}(\varphi^{+};s_{i}(a))=T_{d}(\varphi^{+};a)$ for a $2$ -structure $\varphi$ . Let $M=\{(i_{1}<j_{1}),\ldots,(i_{m}<j_{m})\}$ be a maximal matching on the set $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ . The positive roots of the associated $2$ -structure are given by $\displaystyle\varphi^{+}$ $\displaystyle=\left\{f^{+}_{1},f^{-}_{1},f^{+}_{2},f^{-}_{2},\ldots,f^{+}_{k/2},f^{-}_{k/2}\right\},$ where $f^{+}_{r}=(e_{i_{r}}+e_{j_{r}})/\sqrt{2}$ and $f^{-}_{r}=(e_{i_{r}}-e_{j_{r}})/\sqrt{2}$ . If $i$ is the isolated vertex of matching $M$ then each vector of $\varphi^{+}$ is invariant under the reflection $s_{i}$ and since $s_{i}$ is self-adjoint we have $\displaystyle T_{d}(\varphi^{+};s_{i}(a))$ $\displaystyle=T_{d}((f^{+}_{1},s_{i}(a)),\ldots,(f^{-}_{k/2},s_{i}(a)))=T_{d}((s_{i}(f^{+}_{1}),a),\ldots,(s_{i}(f^{-}_{k/2}),a))$ $\displaystyle=T_{d}((f^{+}_{1},a),\ldots,(f^{-}_{k/2},a))=T_{d}(\varphi^{+};a).$ If $i$ is equal to $j_{r}$ for some index $r$ then again the vectors of $\varphi^{+}$ are invariant under $s_{i}$ except for $s(f^{+}_{r})=f^{-}_{r}$ and $s(f^{-}_{r})=f^{+}_{r}$ . Since the polynomial $T_{d}$ is symmetric the calculation carries through. Finally, if $i$ is equal to $i_{r}$ for some index $r$ then the only difference is that for $s(f^{+}_{r})=-f^{-}_{r}$ and $s(f^{-}_{r})=-f^{+}_{r}$ . Since $T_{d}$ is odd in each variable, the two negative signs will cancel in the calculation. Let $p$ be the isolated vertex of a matching $M$ . Then for the associated $2$ -structure $\varphi$ the expression $T_{d}(\varphi^{+};a)$ is independent of the variable $a_{r}$ . From this observation part (b) follows. Concluding remarks The other pizza theorem proved in [5] states that for a Coxeter arrangement $\mathcal{H}$ , different from $A_{1}^{n}$ , such that the negative of the identity map belongs to the associated Coxeter group $W$ , and for a measurable set $K$ , stable under the action of $W$ , such that the convex hull of $\{w(a):w\in W\}$ is contained in $K$ , then the pizza quantity vanishes, that is, $P(\mathcal{H},K+a)=0$ ; see [5, Theorem 1.2]. For the case when the Coxeter group $W$ does not contain the negative map, can we obtain a multivariate Taylor series for the pizza quantity? In the irreducible types, these cases would be $A_{n}$ for $n\geq 2$ , $D_{n}$ for $n$ odd, the sporadic type $E_{6}$ and the dihedral type $I_{2}(m)$ where $m$ is odd. Note that Proposition 3.2 uses analysis in its proof. There are two pizza theorems in [5, Theorems 1.1 and 1.2]. The first result is Theorem 1.1 and the second one is discussed in the previous paragraph. The paper [6] provides a dissection proof of the second theorem. Hence it would be interesting if the usage of analysis could be removed from the proof of Proposition 3.2 such that we have a completely non-analytic proof of the result. Question 6.1. Can the following linear map be described in a more concise way? Given a root system $\Phi$ whose $2$ -structures have type $A_{1}^{k}$ and a symmetric polynomial $p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})$ in $k$ variables. Note that the polynomial $p(x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{k}^{2})$ is stable under the action of type $A_{1}^{k}$ , that is, $p(x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{k}^{2})$ is invariant under the substitution $x_{i}\longmapsto-x_{i}$ . Let $q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=x_{1}\cdots x_{k}\cdot p(x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{k}^{2})$ , that is, this polynomial is skew-symmetric with respect to $A_{1}^{k}$ . For a $2$ -structure $\varphi$ of $\Phi$ let $\displaystyle q(\varphi;a)$ $\displaystyle=q((e_{1},a),\ldots,(e_{k},a)),$ where $\varphi^{+}=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}$ . Finally, define $r(a)$ to be the sum $\displaystyle r(a)$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{\varphi}\epsilon(\varphi)\cdot q(\varphi;a).$ Observe that the polynomial $r(a)$ is divisible by the Jacobian of $\Phi$ . Note that the map sending $p$ to the quotient $r/J$ is linear. Hence, is there a better way to understand the linear map $p\longmapsto r/J$ ?
