365: 5     The consumption ordering of partitions
366: 5.1     Introduction
367: Let Γ act on X. Then given any labeling φ of X, we can define the type of φ to be the partition
368: of |X| with ith part (in decreasing order of size) the number of elements of X given the ith most
369: common label. Then given a labeling φ of type λ, the permutations of X that preserve the labelings
370: are given by Pφ = Sλ1 × Sλ2 × · · · × Sλk , where Sλi acts on the elements. The intersection of the
371: image of Γ in Sym(X) and Pφ is the subgroup of Γ that preserves the labelings. Henceforth for
372: convenience, we will use Γ to also represent its image in Sym(X) if Γ acts on X.
373:     The distinguishing number of Γ, then, is simply the shortest length of a partition λ such that
374: there is a labeling φ of X with type λ such that Γ ∩ Pφ = {e}.
375:     While the distinguishing number gives information on the number of labels necessary to distin-
376: guish a group action, it doesn’t give any more information about the partition of the labels itself.
377: Studying these partitions themselves is a natural extension.
378: Definition 5.1 (distinguishing partition). Let H be a subgroup of Sn . A set partition [n] =
379: A1 ∪ · · · ∪ Ak is said to distinguish H if the only element of H that preserves each of the subsets
380: in the set partition is the identity. The set partition itself is called a distinguishing set partition,
381: while a distinguishing partition refers to a partition of n for which there exists a distinguishing set
382: partition.
383: 
384: 
385: 
386:                                                         8
387: 
388: Definition 5.2 (consumes). A subgroup H < Sn consumes a partition λ ⊢ n if there is a set
389: partition of [n] with weight λ that distinguishes H.
390:    Note that the previous definition simply says that if there is a distinguishing labeling corre-
391: sponding to a partition λ ⊢ n with respect to the action of H on [n], then H consumes λ. This
392: induces a poset structure on partitions of n with the following ordering relation: λ ≥c µ if every
393: H that consumes λ also consumes µ. We call this ordering the consumption ordering. It is not
394: immediate that this defines a partial ordering, for it is conceivable that two different partitions are
395: consumed by the same subgroups of Sn . This would violate the condition of antisymmetry. We
396: show that this does not happen in the next theorem.
397: 
398: 5.2    Structure of the poset
399: Recall the dominance ordering on partitions:
400: Definition 5.3 (dominance order). Let λ and µ be two partitions of n with parts in decreasing
401: order. Under the dominance ordering, λ ≥ µ if and only if ki=1 λi ≥ ki=1 µi for all i where the
402:                                                          P         P
403: expressions are defined.
404:    We show that the consumption ordering is indeed a partial ordering by showing it is consistent
405: with the dominance ordering. We use ≥c to denote the consumption ordering and ≥ to denote the
406: dominance ordering.
407: Theorem 5.4. If λ ≥c µ, then λ ≥ µ.
408: Proof. Assume otherwise. Then let λ and µ be different partitions of n such that λ ≥c µ and
409: λ 6≥ µ. Let λ⊺ = (a1 , a2 , . . . , ak ) and take a labeling X of a Young diagram of λ. Then let
410: H = Sa1 × · · · × Sak where each Sai acts on the ith column of the labelled Young diagram. Then
411: this consumes λ because the only intersection of H with the subgroup that permutes the rows is
412: the identity element.
413:     We claim that H does not consume µ. Take any labeling Y of µ. If H consumes µ under this
414: labeling, then all the the elements of every column in the labeling X of λ must be in different
415: columns of Y . Taking the transpose, this tells us that µ⊺ ≥ λ⊺ ⇒ λ ≥ µ, a contradiction. Thus if
416: every subgroup that consumes λ also consumes µ, then λ ≥ µ, as desired.
417: Corollary 5.5. The consumption ordering is a well-defined poset.
418: Proof. Reflexivity and transitivity are clear. For antisymmetry, note that if λ ≥c µ and µ ≥c λ, by
419: Theorem 5.4 we have λ ≥ µ and µ ≥ λ, so λ = µ as desired.
420:    By Corollary 5.5 we may use the symbol >c rather than ≥c to denote this ordering relation.

18:                                                      of the vertices of G in which each vertex of the graph is adjacent to every vertex
19:                                                      of some color class. The total dominator chromatic (TDC) number χtd (G) of G,

21:                                                      neighbourhood corona of two graphs G1 and G2 is denoted by G1 ⋆ G2 and is the

25:                                                      of two graphs and investigate the total dominator chromatic number of r-gluing
26:                                                      of two graphs. Stability (bondage number) of total dominator chromatic number
27:                                                      of G is the minimum number of vertices (edges) of G whose removal changes the
28:                                                      TDC-number of G. We study the stability and bondage number of certatin graphs.

34:                                            1        Introduction
35:                                            In this paper, we are concerned with simple finite graphs. Let G = (V, E) be such a
36:                                            graph and λ ∈ N. A mapping f : V (G) −→ {1, 2, ..., λ} is called a λ-proper coloring
37:                                            of G, if f (u) 6= f (v) whenever the vertices u and v are adjacent in G. A color class of
38:                                            this coloring, is a set consisting of all those vertices assigned the same color. If f is a
39:                                            proper coloring of G with the coloring classes V1 , V2 , ..., Vλ such that every vertex in Vi
40:                                            has color i, then sometimes write simply f = (V1 , V2 , ..., Vλ ). The chromatic number
41:                                            χ(G) of G is the minimum number of colors needed in a proper coloring of a graph.
42:                                            The concept of a graph coloring and chromatic number is very well-studied in graph
43:                                            theory.

52:     Vijayalekshmi and Kazemi [9, 10, 12, 13] studied the total dominator coloring,
53: abbreviated TD-coloring. Let G be a graph with no isolated vertex, the total dominator
54: coloring is a proper coloring of G in which each vertex of the graph is adjacent to every
55: vertex of some (other) color class. The total dominator chromatic number, abbreviated
56: TDC-number, χtd (G) of G is the minimum number of color classes in a TD-coloring of G.
57: Computation of the TDC-number is NP-complete ([9]). The TD-chromatic number of
58: some graphs, such as paths, cycles, wheels and the complement of paths and cycles has
59: computed in [9]. Also Henning in [8] established the lower and the upper bounds on the
60: TDC-number of a graph in terms of its total domination number γt (G). He has shown
61: that, every graph G with no isolated vertex satisfies γt (G) ≤ χtd (G) ≤ γt (G) + χ(G).
62: The properties of TD-colorings in trees has studied in [8, 9]. Trees T with γt (T ) = χtd (T )
63: has characterized in [8]. We have examined the effects on χtd (G) when G is modified
64: by operations on vertex and edge of G in [5].
65:     The corona of two graphs G and H which denoted by G ◦ H is defined in [7] and
66: there have been some results on the corona of two graphs [4]. In [1] we have studied the
67: total dominator chromatic number of corona of two graphs. In this paper we consider
68: another variation of corona of two graphs and study its total dominator chromatic
69: number. Given simple graphs G1 and G2 , the neighbourhood corona of G1 and G2 ,
70: denoted by G1 ⋆ G2 and is the graph obtained by taking one copy of G1 and |V (G1 )|
71: copies of G2 and joining the neighbours of the ith vertex of G1 to every vertex in the
72: ith copy of G2 ([6]). Figure 1 shows P4 ⋆ P3 , where Pn is the path of order n. Liu and
73: Zhu in [11] determined the adjacency spectrum of G1 ⋆ G2 for arbitrary G1 and G2 and
74: the Laplacian spectrum and signless Laplacian spectrum of G1 ⋆ G2 for regular G1 and
75: arbitrary G2 , in terms of the corresponding spectrum of G1 and G2 . Also Gopalapillai
76: in [6] has studied the eigenvalues and spectrum of G1 ⋆ G2 , when G2 is regular.
77:     A domination-critical (domination-super critical, respectively) vertex in a graph G,
78: is a vertex whose removal decreases (increases, respectively) the domination number.
79: Bauer et al. [3] introduced the concept of domination stability in graphs. The dom-
80: ination stability, or just γ-stability, of a graph G is the minimum number of vertices
81: whose removal changes the domination number. Motivated by domination stability, we
82: introduce the total dominator chromatic (TDC)-stability (TDC-bondage number) of a
83: graph.
84: 
85: Definition 1.1 The total dominator chromatic (TDC)-stability (TDC-bondage num-
86: ber) of graph G, is the minimum number of vertices (edges) of G, whose removal changes
87: the TDC-number of G.

10:                                                    Two boards are rook equivalent if they have the same number of non-attacking rook placements for
11:                                                any number of rooks. Define a rook equivalence graph on an equivalence class of Ferrers boards by
12:                                                specifying that two boards are connected by an edge if you can obtain one of the boards by moving
13:                                                squares in the other board out of one column and into a single other column. Given such a graph, we
14:                                                characterize which boards will yield connected graphs. We also provide some cases where common graphs
15:                                                will or will not be the graph for some set of rook equivalent Ferrers boards. Finally, we extend this graph
16:                                                definition to the m-level rook placement generalization developed by Briggs and Remmel. This yields a
17:                                                graph on the set of rook equivalent singleton boards, and we characterize which singleton boards give
18:                                                rise to a connected graph.

21:                                           1    Introduction
22:                                           Broadly speaking, rook theory is the study of how many ways a number of rooks may be placed on a board,
23:                                           which is a collection of square cells grouped into a grid of rows and columns. The formal study of rook
24:                                           theory began with Kaplansky and Riordan [KR46], who connected rook placements with elements of the
25:                                           symmetric groups, Sn , and used rook placements to study permutations with various restrictions.
26:                                               Two boards are equivalent if there are the same number of ways to place k rooks on both boards, for
27:                                           any non-negative integer k. The number of ways to place k rooks on a board is also called the k-th rook
28:                                           number of the board. Foata and Schützenberger [FS70] demonstrated that for Ferrers boards, a convenient
29:                                           subset of boards, any rook equivalence class would contain a unique element of a specified type. They also
30:                                           defined explicit bijections between rook placements of k rooks on any two rook equivalent Ferrers boards.
31:                                           Their result was primarily geometric, and involved transposing specified subboards of a Ferrers board.
32:                                               Goldman, Joichi, and White [GJW75] connected the results of Foata and Schützenberger to algebraic
33:                                           combinatorics. They did so by slightly adjusting the existing definition of the rook polynomial, a generating
34:                                           function for the rook numbers. By redefining it to be in the falling factorial basis of polynomials, rather
35:                                           than the standard basis, they were able to factor the rook polynomial of a Ferrers board. This led them to
36:                                           a much simpler proof that Foata and Schützenberger’s unique element existed in any equivalence class, as
37:                                           well as allowing them to enumerate the size of the rook equivalence classes of Ferrers boards.
38:                                               Briggs and Remmel [BR06] gave a generalization of rook placements by associating sets of m consecutive
39:                                           rows into a single level. By doing so, they created rook placements that correspond to the wreath product
40:                                           Cm ≀ Sn , where Cm is the cyclic group with m elements, in the way that ordinary rook placements correspond
41:                                           to Sn . Working on a subset of Ferrers boards that behave nicely with their m-level rook placements, called
42:                                           singleton boards, Briggs and Remmel were able to extend the factorization theorem of Goldman, Joichi, and
43:                                           White to their generalization.
44:                                               Through a collaboration between Loehr, Remmel, Sagan, and myself [BLRS13], we factored the m-level
45:                                           rook polynomial it a couple different ways. One method considered the heights of the different columns,
46:                                           similar to what Goldman, Joichi, and White and Briggs and Remmel have done. The other method focused
47:                                           on the number of cells in each level. In a second paper [BLRS16] we were able to extend the bijections that
48:                                           Foata and Schützenberger developed to m-level rook equivalence classes of singleton boards.
49:                                               In this paper we connect rook theory and graph theory by defining a graph whose vertices are the Ferrers
50:                                           boards in a rook equivalence class, and whose edges are simple geometric alterations that transform one
51:                                           board to another in the equivalence class. In order to keep this paper self contained, Section 2 provides a

121: 3    Rook Placements
122: In this section we provide an introduction to the definitions and concepts central to rook theory. There is
123: a decided emphasis on the algebraic methods pioneered by Goldman, Joichi, and White, but they should
124: also be understood in the context of the more geometric reasoning of Foata and Schützenberger, and the
125: geometric interpretations which they have in their own right.
126:     Begin by tiling the first quadrant with 1 by 1 square cells. A board is any finite subset of this tiling.
127: Like Foata and Schützenberger we will restrict our attention to a convenient subset of boards, called Ferrers
128: boards. A Ferrers board is a board consisting of connected columns, each beginning in the bottom row of
129: the first quadrant, where the column heights are non-decreasing from left to right. Consider Figure 2 for
130: an example of a Ferrers board. We index Ferrers boards by their column heights, so the Ferrers board in
131: Figure 2 is B = (1, 2, 2, 4). Note that the shape of a Ferrers board is unchanged if we add columns of height
132: zero to the left side of the board, for example, B = (1, 2, 2, 4) = (0, 0, 1, 2, 2, 4). Henceforth, any board
133: we refer to should be assumed to be a Ferrers board and we will only specify Ferrers boards in Theorem
134: statements as a reminder of this condition.
135:     Given a board, a rook placement of k rooks on that board is a selection of k squares from that board,
136: so that no two squares are in the same row or column. This corresponds to putting k rook chess pieces, on
137: the board so that no two rooks are attacking each other, since each rook attacks its own row and its own
138: column. Figure 2 contains an example of a rook placement of 2 rooks on the board. The rooks are in squares
139: (2, 1) and (4, 3); note that we are using Cartesian coordinates to label the squares.
140:     The kth rook number of a board B is the number of rook placements of k rooks on that board and is
141: denoted rk (B). For any board, r0 (B) = 1 and r1 (B) = |B|, the number of squares that B contains. We call
142: two boards rook equivalent if they have the same rook numbers for all non-negative integers n, we denote this
143: B1 ≡ B2 . In order to determine if two boards are rook equivalent, we define a generating function for the
144: rook numbers of a board. However, we do so in the falling factorial basis for the vector space of polynomials,
145: which we define as follows. Given a non-negative integer n, we define kth falling factorial of x to be
146: 
147:                                       x↓k = x(x − 1) · · · (x − (k − 1)).
148: 
149: As with the traditional factorial, x↓0 = 1. Thus {x↓k | k ≥ 0} is a basis for the vector space of polynomials.

53: 1     Introduction
54: All graphs considered in this paper are simple, finite, and undirected. We follow the
55: terminology and notation of Bondy and Murty in [4] for those not defined here.
56:     Consider an edge-colored graph where adjacent edges may have the same color. A
57: path of the graph is called a rainbow path if no two edges of the path have the same
58: color. The graph is called rainbow connected if for any two distinct vertices of the
59: graph, there is a rainbow path connecting them. For a connected graph G, the rainbow
60: connection number of G, denoted by rc(G), is defined as the minimum number of colors
61: that are required to make G rainbow connected. The concept of rainbow connection of
62: graphs was proposed by Chartrand et al. in [5], and has been well-studied since then.
63: For further details, we refer the reader to the book [13].
64:     In 2014, Liu et al. [14] introduced the concept of total rainbow connection, a gener-
65: alization of rainbow connection. Consider a total-colored graph, i.e, all its edges and
66: vertices are colored. A path in the graph is called a total-rainbow path if all the edges
67: and inner vertices of the path are assigned distinct colors. The total-colored graph
68: is total-rainbow connected (with respect to a total-coloring c) if every pair of distinct
69: vertices in the graph are connected by a total-rainbow path. In this case, the total-
70: coloring c is called a total-rainbow connection coloring (T RC-coloring, for short). For
71: a connected graph G, the total-rainbow connection number of G, denoted by trc(G), is
72: the minimum number of colors that are required to make G total-rainbow connected.
73: The following observations are immediate.
74: 
75: Proposition 1.1. Let G be a connected graph. Then we have
76:      (i) trc(G) = 1 if and only if G is complete;
77:     (ii) trc(G) ≥ 3 if G is noncomplete;
78:     (iii) trc(G) ≥ 2diam(G) − 1, where diam(G) is the diameter of G.
79: 
80:     A Nordhaus-Gaddum-type result is a (tight) lower or upper bound on the sum or
81: product of the values of a graph parameter for a graph and its complement. The name
82: “Nordhaus-Gaddum-type” is given because Nordhaus and Gaddum [16] first estab-
83: lished the following type of inequalities for chromatic number of graphs in 1956. They
84: proved that if G and G are complementary graphs √ on n vertices whose chromatic num-
85: bers are χ(G) and χ(G), respectively, then 2 n ≤ χ(G) + χ(G) ≤ n + 1. Since then,
86: many analogous inequalities of other graph parameters have been considered, such as
87: diameter [8], domination number [7], rainbow connection number [6], (total) proper
88: connection number [9] ([11]), and so on [1, 2, 3, 12]. Both Ma [15] and Sun [17] stud-
89: ied the Nordhaus-Gaddum-type lower bound of the total-rainbow connection number.
90: In [15], Ma proposed the following conjecture.
91: 
92: Conjecture 1. Let G and G be complementary connected graphs with n vertices. Does
93: there exist two constants C1 and C2 such that trc(G) + trc(G) ≤ C1 n + C2 , where this
94: upper bound is tight.

100: n ≥ 6, if both G and G are connected, then
101:                                   trc(G) + trc(G) ≤ 2n.
