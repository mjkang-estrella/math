87: Presented in this section is a short introduction to the concepts of graph theory needed to keep this paper
88: internally complete. Anyone already familiar with graph theory can probably skip this section.
89:      A graph is a pair of sets, a set of vertices V = {v1 , . . . , vn } and a set of edges E = {e1 , . . . , vM }, where
90: each edge is an unordered pair of distinct vertices, e = {vi , vj }. Visually, graphs are usually represented by
91: assigning vertices to unique points in space, and then drawing a line between vertices vi and vj if and only
92: if e = {vi , vj } is an element of the edge set. A graph is called connected if, given any two vertices vi , vj ∈ V ,
93: there exists a sequence of edges e1 , e2 , . . . , ep ∈ E such that vi ∈ e1 , vj ∈ ep and en ∩ en+1 6= ∅ for all
94: 1 ≤ n < p. Figure 1 contains three examples of graphs, the first is not connected, but the other two are.
95:      Some graphs of particular note are the complete graphs and the complete bipartite        graphs. A complete
96: graph on n vertices, denoted Kn , has a vertex set of size n and edge set of size n2 , requiring that every
97: vertex be connected to every other vertex. A graph is bipartite if its vertex set can be decomposed into
98: two disjoint subsets V = V1 ⊔ V2 such that every edge in the edge set contains one vertex from V1 and one
99: vertex from V2 . A complete bipartite graph, denoted Kn,m , is a graph where V = V1 ⊔ V2 such that |V1 | = n,
100: |V2 | = m, and the edge set is of size n · m, containing every possible edge with one vertex from V1 and one
101: vertex from V2 . The middle graph of Figure 1 is a complete graph and the right graph is a complete bipartite
102: graphs.
103:      Two graphs, G1 = {V1 , E1 } and G2 = {V2 , E2 } are called isomorphic if there is a bijection f between V1
104: and V2 such that {vi , vj } ∈ E1 if and only if {f (vi ), f (vj )} ∈ E2 . In other words, two graphs are isomorphic
105: if they have the same number of vertices and their vertices are connected in the same way by edges.

121: 3    Rook Placements
122: In this section we provide an introduction to the definitions and concepts central to rook theory. There is
123: a decided emphasis on the algebraic methods pioneered by Goldman, Joichi, and White, but they should
124: also be understood in the context of the more geometric reasoning of Foata and Schützenberger, and the
125: geometric interpretations which they have in their own right.
126:     Begin by tiling the first quadrant with 1 by 1 square cells. A board is any finite subset of this tiling.
127: Like Foata and Schützenberger we will restrict our attention to a convenient subset of boards, called Ferrers
128: boards. A Ferrers board is a board consisting of connected columns, each beginning in the bottom row of
129: the first quadrant, where the column heights are non-decreasing from left to right. Consider Figure 2 for
130: an example of a Ferrers board. We index Ferrers boards by their column heights, so the Ferrers board in
131: Figure 2 is B = (1, 2, 2, 4). Note that the shape of a Ferrers board is unchanged if we add columns of height
132: zero to the left side of the board, for example, B = (1, 2, 2, 4) = (0, 0, 1, 2, 2, 4). Henceforth, any board
133: we refer to should be assumed to be a Ferrers board and we will only specify Ferrers boards in Theorem
134: statements as a reminder of this condition.
135:     Given a board, a rook placement of k rooks on that board is a selection of k squares from that board,
136: so that no two squares are in the same row or column. This corresponds to putting k rook chess pieces, on
137: the board so that no two rooks are attacking each other, since each rook attacks its own row and its own
138: column. Figure 2 contains an example of a rook placement of 2 rooks on the board. The rooks are in squares
139: (2, 1) and (4, 3); note that we are using Cartesian coordinates to label the squares.
140:     The kth rook number of a board B is the number of rook placements of k rooks on that board and is
141: denoted rk (B). For any board, r0 (B) = 1 and r1 (B) = |B|, the number of squares that B contains. We call
142: two boards rook equivalent if they have the same rook numbers for all non-negative integers n, we denote this
143: B1 ≡ B2 . In order to determine if two boards are rook equivalent, we define a generating function for the
144: rook numbers of a board. However, we do so in the falling factorial basis for the vector space of polynomials,
145: which we define as follows. Given a non-negative integer n, we define kth falling factorial of x to be
146:

235: 4      Graph Theory of Rook Placements
236: This section defines the rook equivalence graph motivated by geometric manipulation of the shape of rook
237: equivalent Ferrers boards. We then segue into considering not the geometry of the boards, but their algebra,
238: in the form of their root vectors. This enables us to develop a criterion for when a rook equivalence graph
239: is connected.
240:     In order to connect rook theory to graph theory, we define the rook equivalence graph as follows. Given
241: a board B, the vertex set V will be the set of all boards rook equivalent to B, including B itself. The edge
242: set consists of pairs of boards {B1 , B2 } such that B1 and B2 differ in only two columns i and j where B1
243: has k more squares in column i than B2 and k fewer squares in column j than B2 . Graphically, we can
244: consider transforming B1 into B2 by taking k squares from the top of column i and moving them to the top
245: of column j. Figure 3 illustrates this with a pair of boards connected by an edge. Both root vectors are
246: shown to demonstrate that B1 ≡ B2 . We denote the root equivalence graph of the rook equivalence class
247: containing B by G(B).
248:     Consider that B1 and B2 in Figure 3 differ only by moving squares, the one shaded square in this case,
249: from a column of B1 to another column in B2 . Remember that we must consider B2 as having four columns,
250: in order to compare it to B1 which also has four columns. Finally, examine how the two root vectors differ
251: only in positions 1 and 3. where the values have been swapped, this motivates the next theorem.
