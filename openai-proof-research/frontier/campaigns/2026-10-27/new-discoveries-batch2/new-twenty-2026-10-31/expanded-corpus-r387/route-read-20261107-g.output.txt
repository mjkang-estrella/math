52:     Vijayalekshmi and Kazemi [9, 10, 12, 13] studied the total dominator coloring,
53: abbreviated TD-coloring. Let G be a graph with no isolated vertex, the total dominator
54: coloring is a proper coloring of G in which each vertex of the graph is adjacent to every
55: vertex of some (other) color class. The total dominator chromatic number, abbreviated
56: TDC-number, χtd (G) of G is the minimum number of color classes in a TD-coloring of G.
57: Computation of the TDC-number is NP-complete ([9]). The TD-chromatic number of
58: some graphs, such as paths, cycles, wheels and the complement of paths and cycles has
59: computed in [9]. Also Henning in [8] established the lower and the upper bounds on the
60: TDC-number of a graph in terms of its total domination number γt (G). He has shown
61: that, every graph G with no isolated vertex satisfies γt (G) ≤ χtd (G) ≤ γt (G) + χ(G).
62: The properties of TD-colorings in trees has studied in [8, 9]. Trees T with γt (T ) = χtd (T )
63: has characterized in [8]. We have examined the effects on χtd (G) when G is modified
64: by operations on vertex and edge of G in [5].
65:     The corona of two graphs G and H which denoted by G ◦ H is defined in [7] and
66: there have been some results on the corona of two graphs [4]. In [1] we have studied the
67: total dominator chromatic number of corona of two graphs. In this paper we consider
68: another variation of corona of two graphs and study its total dominator chromatic
69: number. Given simple graphs G1 and G2 , the neighbourhood corona of G1 and G2 ,
70: denoted by G1 ⋆ G2 and is the graph obtained by taking one copy of G1 and |V (G1 )|
71: copies of G2 and joining the neighbours of the ith vertex of G1 to every vertex in the
72: ith copy of G2 ([6]). Figure 1 shows P4 ⋆ P3 , where Pn is the path of order n. Liu and
73: Zhu in [11] determined the adjacency spectrum of G1 ⋆ G2 for arbitrary G1 and G2 and
74: the Laplacian spectrum and signless Laplacian spectrum of G1 ⋆ G2 for regular G1 and
75: arbitrary G2 , in terms of the corresponding spectrum of G1 and G2 . Also Gopalapillai
76: in [6] has studied the eigenvalues and spectrum of G1 ⋆ G2 , when G2 is regular.
77:     A domination-critical (domination-super critical, respectively) vertex in a graph G,
78: is a vertex whose removal decreases (increases, respectively) the domination number.
79: Bauer et al. [3] introduced the concept of domination stability in graphs. The dom-
80: ination stability, or just γ-stability, of a graph G is the minimum number of vertices
81: whose removal changes the domination number. Motivated by domination stability, we
82: introduce the total dominator chromatic (TDC)-stability (TDC-bondage number) of a
83: graph.

86: ber) of graph G, is the minimum number of vertices (edges) of G, whose removal changes
87: the TDC-number of G.

475: Having defined the rook equivalence graph and presented some of its properties, it is worth considering which
476: graphs are actually realized as rook equivalence graphs. In this section we demonstrate that the complete
477: graphs are a subset of rook equivalence graphs. We also present some notable absences in the form of small,
478: complete bipartite graphs.
479: Theorem 11. For any integer n > 0, Kn , the complete graph on n vertices, is isomorphic to G(B) where
480: B = (0, . . . , n − 1, n − 1).
481: Proof. Consider the root vector of B = (0, . . . , n − 1, n − 1). Since ξ(B) = h0, . . . , 0, 1i must begin with
482: an entry of value 0, the single entry of value 1 cannot occupy this position. However, since the root vector
483: consists exclusively of elements of value 0 or 1, there is no way for any rearrangement of the root vector to
484: have values that increase by more than 1 from one element to the next. Thus, the element of value 1 can
485: appear in any of the other n positions, including index n + 1 where it begins. If the 1 is in position i > 1, it
486: can be moved to position j > 1 simply by swapping the elements in positions i and j. Thus every one of the
487: n vertices of G(B) is connected by an edge to every single other vertex of G(B), and G(B) is isomorphic to
488: Kn .

423: 4.2    C2,1 (n, k)
424: In this section, we are concerned with computing the number of conjugacy classes of maximal parabolic
425: subalgebras of sl(n) with index k, which we denote C2,1 (n, k). The conjugacy classes of such subalgebras are
426: in one-to-one correspondence with seaweeds of the form a|b
427:                                                          n . The subscripts in our notation for C2,1 (n, k) are
428: suggestive of the number of parts in the top and bottom compositions of the conjugacy class representatives
429: that we will be counting.

431: 
432: Theorem 9. If n = (k + 1)t for some integer t, then C2,1 (n, k) = ϕ(t), where ϕ is Euler’s totient function.
433: Otherwise, C2,1 (n, k) = 0.
434: 
435: Proof. For a given positive integer n, there are n − 1 seaweeds of the form a|bn . The index of such a seaweed
436: is equal to gcd(a, b) − 1 (see [7]). Since gcd(a, b) = gcd(a, a + b), our goal is to find the cardinality of the
437: following set
438:                                 {a ∈ Z | 1 ≤ a ≤ n − 1 and gcd(a, n) = k + 1}.
439: Thus, n = (k + 1)t for some integer t, otherwise C2,1 (n, k) = 0. Since k + 1 must also divide a, we can
440: express each element of this set as a = (k + 1)s for some integer s. To ensure that gcd(a, n) = k + 1, s must
441: be relatively prime to t, and furthermore s must be less than t to ensure that a ≤ n − 1. It follows that
442: C2,1 (n, k) = ϕ(t).

547: 5     Afterword
548: This initial investigation makes use of recent Lie algebraic technology (meanders, homotopy types, and gcd
549: index formulas) to enumerate composition types associated with seaweed subalgebras in Type A. By doing
550: so, it provides the framework for similar investigations in the other classical families, which likewise require
551: analogous technologies – recently advanced by several investigative groups as follows.
552:     In [13], Panyushev extended the Lie theoretic definition of seaweed subalgebras to the reductive algebras.
553: If p and p′ are parabolic subalgebras of a reductive Lie algebra g such that p + p′ = g, then p ∩ p′ is called a
554: seaweed subalgebra of g or simply seaweed when g is understood. For this reason, Joseph has elsewhere [10]
555: called seaweed algebras, biparabolic. One can show that Type-C and Type-B seaweeds, in their standard
556: representation, can be parametrized by a pair of partial compositions of n. Indeed, in [4], Coll et al
557: have topically extended the Type-A work of Dergachev and A. Kirillov to the Type-B and Type-C cases,
558: providing analogous definitions of meanders and index formulas; see also [15], where Type-C meanders were
559: independently developed – absent the index formulas based on the compositions which define the seaweed.
560: The homotopy types for Types B and C have also been classified by the second and fourth authors who have
561: additionally shown in unpublished work that, at least in the Type C case, the homotopy type is a conjugation
562: invariant. Preliminary results in Types B and C suggest that the generating functions for C(n, n − k), i.e.,
563: P                                      f (x)                                                               f (x)
564:    n>0 C(n, n − k), are of the form (1−2x)k+1 much like that of Type-A, which seem to be of the form (1−2x)k
565: where f (x) is a polynomial with integer coefficients and has the same degree in both cases. In Types A, B,
566: and, C, precise enumerative formulas for the number of maximal parabolic seaweeds are made possible by
567: linear gcd index formulas. The complete compliment of such formulas has been developed in [5] for Type-A
568: and in [4], for Types B and C.

570: [1] have completed the classification of linear greatest common divisor formulas for the classical families by
