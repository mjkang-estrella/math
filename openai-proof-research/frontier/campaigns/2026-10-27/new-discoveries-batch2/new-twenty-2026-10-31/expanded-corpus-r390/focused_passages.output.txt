52: 1. Introduction
53: 
54: In the past few decades the direction of the examination of combinatorial games
55: has slightly changed. As a great mathematical invention of the 20th century the
56: theory of “misère” and “partizan” type games were developed. In these games the
57: last move decides the outcome of the game. The books and monographs (e.g., [1],
58: [2]) on this subject have became a part of the classical mathematical knowledge.
59: However, there are some other types of games that were not examined so extensively.
60: These are the so-called “scoring games” in which the players aim to collect the most
61: points. Although the first few papers about scoring games were published in the
62: middle of the last century ([8], [4]), no deep theory was developed for them.
63:    After several attempts to classify the scoring games, the two classes of “well tem-
64: pered” and “dicot” games were introduced [9]. A theory of well-tempered scoring
65: games was developed in [6]. There, algebraic and topological (ordering) aspects
66: were investigated and applied to several games, like knot games [5]. In [3] a real-
67: valued metric was defined for positional games, and it was proved that a particular
68: class of games is a topological semigroup. Then a separation property was defined
69: that connects these games to closely related Conway games.
70:    Another attempt to generalize the previous works of Berlekamp, Conway and
71: Guy ([1], [2]) was made to the theory for scoring play games [11]. Their types of
72: games show nice behavior for the usual game operations, but the theory is fairly
73: restrictive. It was shown that scoring play games do not form a group, there is
74: no non-trivial identity among them, and that the comparison of two games in the
75: usual sense is impossible. However, the paper also states that scoring play games
76: are ordered under the disjunctive sum, and we can form equivalence classes among
77: them using a canonical form. Unfortunately, this theory does not involve several
78: types of games, as in the case of the Pirates and Treasure game.
79:    The combinatorial game Pirates and Treasure was introduced by F. Stewart
80: in [10]. The game is played between two players, Left and Right, on a finite,
81: simple, undirected graph, where each vertex has a weight. The vertices of the
82: graph correspond to islands, and a weight function on the vertices indicates the
83: amount of treasure the island has. Left player has n ships, Right player has m
84: ships, in pre-defined vertices. In each turn, the current player moves one of his
85: ships into an adjacent, non-visited vertex, and the weight of the vertex is added to
86: the current points of the player. If a player in his turn cannot move any of his ships
87: to an adjacent, non-visited vertex, the game ends. Left moves first. The player who
88: collects more treasures (points) than his opponent, wins the game. In this paper we
89: will always assume that n = m = 1. All of our results apply easily for the general
90: case as well.
91:    In [10] it is shown that it is NP-hard to determine which player wins the game. It
92: was also conjectured that this problem is, in fact, PSPACE-complete. In this paper
93:
