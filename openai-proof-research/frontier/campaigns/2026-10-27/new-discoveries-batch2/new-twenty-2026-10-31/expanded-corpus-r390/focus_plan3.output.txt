991: Definition 4.7. An SE(s) graph is a weighted graph G with all edge weights in the interval [0, 1]
992: and with vertices u and v such that:
993:    • the edge weight between u and any vertex in V (G) \ {u, v} is 0,
994:    • the edge weight between v and any vertex in V (G) \ {u, v} is 1, and
995: 
996:    • the edge weight between u and v is s ∈ (0, 1).
997:     Observe that the complement of an SE(s) graph is an SE(t) graph, for s + t = 1, with the
998: roles of u and v exchanged. The SE(s) graphs are, in retrospect, a natural family of weighted
999: graphs to consider when trying to disconnect, as much as possible, both the weighted graph and its
1000: complement. A simple graph G is most easily disconnected by having any one vertex of degree 0;
1001: symmetrically, it is easiest to disconnect Gc by having any one vertex with degree n − 1 in G. A
1002: graph and its complement cannot be simultaneously disconnected, of course, but they could be if it
1003: were possible to have both a vertex u of degree 0 and a vertex v of degree n − 1. The impediment
1004: to simultaneous disconnection comes from the fact that u and v cannot agree on the number of
1005: edges between them, a number that cannot be simultaneously 0 and 1. In the class of weighted
1006: graphs, u and v can compromise on this number using any edge weight wuv = s in the range [0, 1]
1007: (and edge weight t = 1 − s in the complement), giving a one-parameter family of graphs that are
1008: poorly connected simultaneously with their complements. In fact we will show the full SE(s) family
1009: consists of a great many such one-parameter families, because the non-parametrized weight of any
1010: edge involving neither u nor v is arbitrary within its legal range of [0, 1].

20:                                               Since the publications of Duncan and Steingrímsson [15], Corteel, Martinez, Savage and
21:                                            Weselcouch [14] and Mansour and Shattuck [24], there has been increasing interest in
22:                                            counting pattern avoiding ascent/inversion sequences [1, 2, 6, 8, 10–12, 19–23, 25, 28, 29]. In
23:                                            particular, motivated by the study of generalized patterns in permutations [4,13], Yan and
24:                                            the first named author [23] carried out the systematic study of ascent/inversion sequences
25:                                            avoiding vincular patterns of length 3. They reported many nice connections with familiar
26:                                            combinatorial families and posed several challenging enumeration conjectures. The ob-
27:                                            jective of this paper is to address two of their conjectures concerning the pattern 120 in
28:                                            ascent/inversion sequences. It turns out that 120-avoiding ascent and inversion sequences
29:                                            possess attractive enumeration results albeit having elusive structure.
30:                                               Before stating our results, we need to review some definitions on inversion sequences.
31:                                            An integer sequence e = e1 e2 . . . en of length n is an inversion sequence if 0 ≤ ei < i for
32:                                            all 1 ≤ i ≤ n. Inversion sequences of length n, denoted In , are in natural bijection with
33:                                            permutations Sn of [n] := {1, 2, . . . , n} via the famous Lehmer code (see [14, 20]). The set
34:                                            An of ascent sequences of length n consists of e ∈ In such that
35:                                                                                  ei+1 ≤ asc(e1 e2 . . . ei ) + 1
36:                                            for all 1 ≤ i < n, where asc(e1 e2 . . . ei ) := |{ℓ ∈ [i − 1] : eℓ < eℓ+1 }| is the number of ascents
37:                                            of e1 e2 . . . ei . As one of the most important subsets of inversion sequences, ascent sequences
38:                                            were introduced by Bousquet-Mélou, Claesson, Dukes and Kitaev [9] to encode the (2+2)-
39:                                            free posets. Many remarkable connections between inversion (resp. ascent) sequences and
40:                                            permutations (resp. restricted permutations) with unexpected applications have been found
41:                                            in the literature; see [17, 20, 27] and the references therein.

50:    Permutations and inversion sequences can both be viewed as words on N. A word
51: w = w1 w2 · · · wn contains a pattern p = p1 p2 · · · pk if there exists i1 < i2 < · · · < ik such that
52: the subword wi1 wi2 · · · wik of w is order isomorphic to p. In addition, if some consecutive
53: letters in a pattern p are underlined, then we further require that in any occurrence of p, the
54: letters corresponding to these underlined letters be adjacent in w. Such generalized patterns
55: are known as vincular patterns (cf. Kitaev’s book [18, pp.2]), which were introduced in the
56: classification of Mahonian statistics by Babson and Steingrímsson [7]. If a word w does not
57: contain an occurrence of a vincular pattern p, then w is said to avoid the pattern p. For
58: example, an inversion sequence e ∈ In is 120-avoiding if there does not exist indices i and
59: j, 2 ≤ i < j ≤ n, such that ej < ei−1 < ei . For a set W of words, the set of p-avoiding
60: words in W is denoted by W (p).
