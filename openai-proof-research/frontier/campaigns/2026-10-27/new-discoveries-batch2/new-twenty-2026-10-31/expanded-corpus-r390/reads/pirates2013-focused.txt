50:    Our game is played on an undirected graph, where players have multiple coins
51: placed on various vertices of the graph. These coins represent their pirate ships. The
52: remaining vertices are given a numerical value, and players gather points (treasure)
53: by moving onto those vertices. The player who gathers the most points (treasure)
54: wins.
55:    The formal rules are given as follows:
56: 
57:   1. The game is played on a finite simple graph, defined arbitrarily before the
58:      game begins. Left has n ships, and Right has m ships.
59:   2. Each ship has a pre-defined starting vertex.
60:   3. Every node is numbered to indicate how much treasure there is at that node,
61:      the players’ starting vertices are not numbered.
62:   4. On a player’s turn he moves to an adjacent, unvisited vertex. The number of
63:      points he gets, corresponds to the number on that vertex. A player may not
64:      move a previously visited vertex, including the starting vertices.
65:   5. The game ends when it is a player’s turn, and he is not adjacent to an unvisited
66:      vertex.
67:   6. The player who gathers the most treasure wins.
68: 
69:    In this paper, first we will be examining the complexity of this game, and then
70: showing that there are variations of it which are comparable with normal and misère
71: play.

713: 4. Conclusion
714: 
715: We have introduced a new game, and shown that there are variations of this game
716: that exhibit behavior that is very similar to normal play, and variations that exhibit
717: behaviour that is very similar to misère play. This means that there are non-trivial
718: subsets of scoring play games that have this same behaviour. The question is
719: whether we can define these subsets precisely, and determine which scoring games
720: lie in them, and which do not? We hope that we have convinced the reader that
721: this is an interesting problem to pursue.
