 Route Proposed approach For an exceptional ordered pair $e=(a,b)$, put $P_e={x:f_1(x,b)\ne0}$. Let $h_e(w)$ count the occurrences of $a$ in the maximal initial $P_e$-segment of $w$ when the next letter is $b$, and be zero otherwise. The proof establishes barrier laws from coefficient comparisons in commutativity and associativity. These laws imply a conservation identity for every nonzero recursion path. The word factor is 
 $$q(w)=\prod_{e=(a,b)\in E}f_1(a,b)^{-h_e(w)}.$$ 
 Only pairs of letters occurring in $w$ contribute, so this product is finite even for an uncountable alphabet. The linear map $\Phi(w)=q(w)w$ has inverse given by reciprocal factors. The conserved path exponent proves the multiplicative intertwining identity after summing paths and extending bilinearly.

The kernel proof covers every characteristic-zero field and alphabet in  claim ; a finite numerical test is not its replacement.

WORD FULL RELEVANT
                                                                SQUARE-FREE EXTENSIONS OF WORDS

                                                  JAROSLAW GRYTCZUK, HUBERT KORDULEWSKI, AND BARTLOMIEJ PAWLIK

                                                 Abstract. A word is square-free if it does not contain nonempty factors of the form XX. In




arXiv:2104.04841v3 [math.CO] 1 May 2021
                                                 1906 Thue proved that there exist arbitrarily long square-free words over a 3-letter alphabet.
                                                 It was proved recently [8] that among these words there are inﬁnitely many extremal ones,
                                                 that is, having a square in every single-letter extension.
                                                    We study diverse problems concerning extensions of words preserving the property of
                                                 avoiding squares. Our main motivation is the conjecture stating that there are no extremal
                                                 words over a 4-letter alphabet. We also investigate a natural recursive procedure of gen-
                                                 erating square-free words by a single-letter right-most extension. We present the results of
                                                 computer experiments supporting a supposition that this procedure gives an inﬁnite square-
                                                 free word over any alphabet of size at least three.




                                                                                     1. Introduction

                                            A square is a finite nonempty word of the form XX. For instance, the word hotshots is
                                          a square with X = hots. A word W contains a square if it can be written as W = UXXV
                                          for some words U, V , and a nonempty word X. A word is square-free if it does not contain
                                          any squares. For instance, the word repetition contains the square titi, while recreation
                                          is square-free.
                                            It is easy to check that there are no binary square-free words of length greater than 3.
                                          However, there exist ternary square-free words of any length, as proved by Thue in [15]
                                          (see [3]). This result is the starting point of Combinatorics on Words, a wide discipline with
                                          many exciting problems, deep results, and important applications (see [1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 10, 11]).
                                            In this paper we study problems concerning square-free extensions of words, a new concept
                                          introduced recently in [8]. Let A be a fixed alphabet and let W be a finite word over A.
                                          The set of all finite words over A is denoted by A⋆ . An extension of W over A is any word of
                                          the form W ′ xW ′′ , where x ∈ A and W = W ′ W ′′ . For instance, the word bear is an extension
                                          of the word bar by inserting the letter e between letters b and a. A square-free word W is
                                          called extremal over A if there is no square-free extension of W . For instance, the word

    A natural intuition is that extremal words should be rare or even should have bounded
length. However, in the case of a 3-letter alphabet, the said intuition turned out to be untrue.

Theorem 1 (Grytczuk, Kordulewski, Niewiadomski [8]). There exist infinitely many ex-
tremal words over a 3-letter alphabet.

    The proof of this theorem is by a recursive construction whose validity is partially based
on computer verifications. In [12] Mol and Rampersad determined all positive integers k for
which there exist extremal ternary words of length exactly k. In particular, they proved that
for every k ⩾ 87 there exists an extremal ternary word of length k.
    One may naturally wonder what the case is for larger alphabets. Actually, we do not know
if there are any extremal words over a 4-letter alphabet. The following conjecture was stated
in [8].

Conjecture 1. Every square-free word over a 4-letter alphabet can be extended to a square-
free word.

    Actually, we do not even know if there exist any constants n and N such that every
square-free word over a n-letter alphabet of length at least N can be extended.
    In the forthcoming sections we shall present some observations and results of computer
experiments inspired by the conjecture above.

                                   2. Nonchalant words

2.1. The main conjectures. The problem of extremal square-free words is connected to
the following recursive construction.
    Given a fixed ordered alphabet A, we start with the first letter from A and continue by
inserting the earliest possible letter at the rightmost position of the actual word so that
the new word is square-free. For instance, for the alphabet {1, 2, 3} this greedy procedure
starts with the following sequence of square-free words:

                      1, 12, 121, 1213, 12131, 121312, 1213121, 12131231.

The last word was obtained by inserting 3 at the penultimate position of the previous word.
    We conjecture that the aforementioned procedure never stops. To state it formally, let us
define recursively a sequence of nonchalant words Ni over the alphabet An = {1, 2, . . . , n} by
putting N1 = 1, and letting Ni+1 = Ni′ xNi′′ to be a square-free extension of Ni such that Ni′′
is the shortest possible suffix of Ni and x ∈ An is the earliest possible letter.

Conjecture 2. The sequence of nonchalant words over An is infinite for every n ⩾ 3.

Remark 3.2. Note that given k and ℓ, there are only finitely many structural equivalence classes of
matrices in Rk×ℓ .

    We will be interested in matrices A in [kℓ]k×ℓ such that |E(A)| = kℓ. In particular, this means that each
value from [kℓ] appears exactly once in A. We denote this set of matrices by R(k, ℓ) and refer to these
as patterns. So, the right way of thinking of a pattern is that it is a matrix with a unique smallest entry,
a unique second smallest entry etc. By structural equivalence we do not really worry about the actual
values, but the relative position of those entries. Given A ∈ R(k, ℓ) and B ∈ [0, 1]k×ℓ such that B ≡ A, we
call B a realisation of A.
