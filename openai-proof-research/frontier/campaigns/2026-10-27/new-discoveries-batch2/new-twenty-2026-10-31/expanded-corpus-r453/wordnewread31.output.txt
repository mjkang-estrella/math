                                            No extremal square-free words over alphabets of size
                                                                 at least 5
                                                                       Eng Keat Hng∗                Silas Rathke†


                                                                                        Abstract
                                                       A word over an alphabet A contains a square if it has a subword of the form XX
                                                   where X is a word. A word W is extremal square-free if it does not contain a square,




arXiv:2608.10591v1 [math.CO] 11 Aug 2026
                                                   but it contains a square as soon as any letter of A is inserted at any position of W .
                                                   Grytczuk, Kordulewski, and Niewiadomski conjectured that there are no extremal
                                                   square-free words over alphabets of size at least 4. We prove this for alphabets of
                                                   size at least 5. Our proof also implies that the sequence of nonchalant words defined
                                                   by Grytczuk, Kordulewski, and Niewiadomski is infinite and converges to an infinite
                                                   word for all alphabets of size at least 5.

                                           1       Introduction
                                           An alphabet is a finite set of symbols which are called letters, and a word is a finite sequence
                                           of letters drawn from an alphabet. A square is a nonempty word of the form XX, where X
                                           represents a nonempty word. A word is square-free if it does not contain a square as a
                                           factor, that is, a subword consisting of consecutive letters. It is easy to check that there
                                           are no binary square-free words with more than three letters. However, Thue [7] showed
                                           in 1906 that there are arbitrarily long ternary square-free words (see [1]). His work [7, 8]
                                           is considered to be the starting point of research in combinatorics on words [2].
                                               Recently, Grytczuk, Kordulewski, and Niewiadomski [3] initiated the systematic study
                                           of extremal square-free words. We give a formal definition below.

                                           Definition 1.1. Let W be a word over an alphabet A. An extension of W is a word of
                                           the form W1 xW2 where W1 and W2 are (possibly empty) words such that W = W1 W2
                                           and x ∈ A is a single letter. An extremal square-free word is a square-free word such that
                                           none of its extensions are square-free.

    The only binary extremal square-free words are 010 and 101. Grytczuk, Kordulewski
and Niewiadomski [3] showed that there are infinitely many ternary extremal square-
free words. Mol and Rampersad [6] concretely determined all possible lengths of ternary
extremal square-free words. The following conjecture was posed in [3, 6].
Conjecture 1.2 ([3, 6]). There is no extremal square-free word over an alphabet of size
at least 4.
     The notion of nonchalant words was introduced and discussed in [3, 4] as a concept
closely related to extremal square-free words. The sequence (N0 , N1 , . . . ) of nonchalant
words over a linearly ordered alphabet (A, ≺) is generated as follows. Let N0 be the
empty word, and for each integer i ∈ N we define Ni to be the square-free extension P xS
of Ni−1 = P S such that the suffix S of Ni−1 is shortest possible and the letter x ∈ A is
earliest possible with respect to ≺. If no such S and x exist, then the algorithm terminates,
i.e. the sequence is finite. Note that this can happen only if Ni−1 is extremal.
     Even though it is known that there are infinitely many extremal words over alphabets
of size 3, it is conjectured that the sequence of nonchalant words is infinite.
Conjecture 1.3 ([3, 4]). For any alphabet A of size k ≥ 3 with a linear ordering ≺, the
sequence of nonchalant words over (A, ≺) is infinite.
    Even if the sequence of nonchalant words is infinite, it still might not converge to an
infinite word. Therefore, the following conjecture is even stronger than the previous one.
Conjecture 1.4 ([3, 4]). For any alphabet A of size k ≥ 3 with a linear ordering ≺, the
sequence of nonchalant words over (A, ≺) converges to an infinite word.
    Hong and Zhang [5] confirmed Conjectures 1.2 and 1.4 for alphabets of size at least 17.
Theorem 1.5 ([5]). For any integer k ≥ 17, there is no extremal square-free word over
an alphabet of size k.
Theorem 1.6 ([5]). For any alphabet A of size k ≥ 17 with a linear ordering ≺, the
sequence of nonchalant words over (A, ≺) converges to an infinite word.
    Our main results improve on the results of Hong and Zhang and reduce the lower bound
on the alphabet size from 17 to 5; this confirms Conjectures 1.2 and 1.4 for alphabets of
size at least 5.
Theorem 1.7. For any integer k ≥ 5, there is no extremal square-free word over an
alphabet of size k.
Theorem 1.8. For any alphabet A of size k ≥ 5 with a linear ordering ≺, the sequence
of nonchalant words over (A, ≺) converges to an infinite word.


2     The proof
We first introduce the notation and terminology we need, which are mostly identical to
those in [5]. Write N for the set of positive integers and N0 for the set N∪{0}. For m ∈ N0 ,
write [m] for the set {1, . . . , m} and [m]0 for the set [m]∪{0}. Let W be a word of length n.

                                              2
Enumerate its letters from left to right as w1 , . . . , wn . For i ∈ [n], let W [i] denote the
letter wi . Given integers 1 ≤ a < b ≤ n + 1, let W [a, b) denote the factor wa . . . wb−1
of W . For i ∈ [n − 1], we call the space between letters wi and wi+1 gap i; we call the
spaces to the left of w1 and to the right of wn gap 0 and gap n respectively.

Definition 2.1 ([5]). Let W be a word of length n over an alphabet A. For b ∈ [n]0
and c ∈ A, let W +b c be the word obtained by inserting the letter c at gap b in W .
For a ∈ [n], ℓ ∈ N, b ∈ [n]0 and c ∈ A, the quadruple (a, ℓ, b, c) is square-completing
in W if (W +b c)[a, a + ℓ) = (W +b c)[a + ℓ, a + 2ℓ). The sign of a square-completing
quadruple (a, ℓ, b, c) in W is 1 if b ≤ a + ℓ − 2 and −1 otherwise; this indicates whether
the letter c we inserted at gap b appears in (W +b c)[a, a + ℓ) or (W +b c)[a + ℓ, a + 2ℓ).

   We introduce the key new concept of a minimal square-completing quadruple. This
helps us achieve better bounds in the proof and attain the value 5 in Theorem 1.7.

Definition 2.2. A square-completing quadruple q = (a, ℓ, b, c) in a word W is mini-
mal if for every square-completing quadruple q′ = (a′ , ℓ′ , b, c) in W one of the following
statements holds.

(M1) ℓ′ > ℓ.

(M2) ℓ′ = ℓ and q and q′ have different signs in W .

(M3) ℓ′ = ℓ, both q and q′ have sign 1 in W and a′ ≤ a.

(M4) ℓ′ = ℓ, both q and q′ have sign −1 in W and a′ ≥ a.

Remark 2.3. The definition quickly implies the following: if W is a word of length n over
an alphabet A and (b, c) ∈ [n]0 × A is fixed, then if there is a square-completing quadruple
of the form (a, ℓ, b, c), then there is also a minimal square-completing quadruple with b
and c as the third and fourth entries. Furthermore, there are at most two minimal square-
completing quadruples with b and c as the third and fourth entries, and if there are two,
then both have the same second entry ℓ and different signs.

    Let W be a word. Write A(W ) for the set of all minimal square-completing quadruples
in W . For ℓ ∈ N, let Aℓ (W ) be the set of all elements of A(W ) with second entry ℓ;

2.1     Proof of Lemma 2.4
In this subsection we prove Lemma 2.4. Our proof approach, which is motivated by the
ideas of [5], involves grouping up and analysing the interactions between pairs of minimal
square-completing quadruples of the same sign. This motivates the following definition.
Definition 2.6. For positive integers ℓ, ℓ′ ∈ N let φ(ℓ, ℓ′ ) ∈ N0 ∪ {∞} be the least d
such that there is an alphabet A and a square-free word W over A with distinct minimal
square-completing quadruples of sign 1 of the forms (a, ℓ, b, c) and (a + d, ℓ′ , b′ , c′ ).
   The following lemma gives a simple upper bound on φ(ℓ, ℓ′ ).
Lemma 2.7. For all ℓ, ℓ′ ∈ N we have φ(ℓ, ℓ′ ) ≤ ℓ.
Proof. We distinguish three cases.
    Case 1: ℓ ≤ ℓ′ . Then define A := [ℓ′ + 1], P := 2 · · · ℓ, Q := (ℓ + 1) · · · ℓ′ , and consider
the word W := P 1P Q(ℓ′ + 1)P Q. First, we show that it is square-free. Indeed, as the
letters 1 and ℓ′ + 1 only appear once in W , any square of W must be a square in P or P Q.
But in both of them, no letter appears more than once, so W is square-free. Furthermore,
W has distinct minimal square-completing quadruples (1, ℓ, 0, 1) and (ℓ + 1, ℓ′ , ℓ, ℓ′ + 1) of
sign 1, so φ(ℓ, ℓ′ ) ≤ ℓ.
    Case 2: ℓ′ < ℓ ≤ 2ℓ′ . Then set A := [ℓ′ + 1], R := 2 · · · ℓ′ , S := 2 · · · (ℓ − ℓ′ ), and
consider the word W := R(ℓ′ + 1)S1R(ℓ′ + 1)R. Again, we claim that W is square-
free. Indeed, as the letter 1 appears only once in W , any square of W must be a square
in R(ℓ′ + 1)S or R(ℓ′ + 1)R. But in both of them, ℓ′ + 1 only appears once because
ℓ − ℓ′ < ℓ′ + 1, so each square of W must be a square in R or S. As both R and S have no
square, W is square-free as well. Furthermore, W has distinct minimal square-completing
quadruples (1, ℓ, 0, 1) and (ℓ + 1, ℓ′ , ℓ, ℓ′ + 1) of sign 1, so φ(ℓ, ℓ′ ) ≤ ℓ.
    Case 3: 2ℓ′ < ℓ. Set A := [ℓ − ℓ′ + 1], T := 2 · · · ℓ′ , U := (ℓ′ + 2) · · · (ℓ − ℓ′ + 1),
and consider the word W := T (ℓ′ + 1)T U 1T (ℓ′ + 1)T U . Again, we claim that W is
square-free. Indeed, first the letter 1 is unique and then the letter (ℓ′ + 1) is unique
in both of the resulting factors. Thus, any square of W must be a square in T or T U
which is impossible. Thus, W is square-free. Furthermore, it has distinct minimal square-
completing quadruples (1, ℓ, 0, 1) and (ℓ + 1, ℓ′ , ℓ, ℓ′ + 1) of sign 1, so φ(ℓ, ℓ′ ) ≤ ℓ.

of Lemma 2.4. Its proof, which entails a highly technical and careful analysis, is deferred
