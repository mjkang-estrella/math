                                                                                         Abstract
                                                         A finite subset M ⊂ Rd is basic, if for any function f : M → R there
                                                     exists a collection of functions f1 , . . . , fd : R → R such that for each
                                                     element (x1 , . . . , xd ) ∈ M we have f (x1 , . . . , xd ) = f1 (x1 )+. . .+fd (xd ).
                                                     For certain finite sets, we prove a criterion for a set to be basic, and
                                                     we show that it cannot be extended to the general case. In addition,
                                                     we interpret the above criterion in terms of doubly-weighted graphs
                                                     and give an estimation for the number of elements in certain basic and
                                                     non-basic subsets.
                                                         Key words: finite basic subsets, doubly-weighted graphs.


                                           1         Introduction
                                               The concept of basic subsets arises in connection with Hilbert’s thirteenth
                                           problem on the superposition of continuous functions. The first time it was
                                           introduced in an explicit form by Sternfeld in 1989 [5]. He called a subset
                                           M ⊂ Rd (continuously) basic, if for any continuous function f : M → R there
                                           exists a collection of continuous functions f1 , . . . , fd : R → R such that

                                                                       f (x1 , . . . , xd ) = f1 (x1 ) + . . . + fd (xd )                     (1)

                                           for each element (x1 , . . . , xd ) ∈ M. The origin of this concept goes back to
                                           1958 [1] when Arnold raised the question equivalent to the following: what

    In this paper, we focus on finite subsets of Rd , where d ⩾ 2. In this case,
the condition of continuity can be omitted. Without loss of generality, we
identify finite subsets of Rd with integer points inside d-dimensional cube [n]d ,
where [n] = {1, . . . , n}. We establish the following estimation for the number
of elements in basic subsets.
Theorem 1.1. If M ⊂ [n]d is a basic subset, then
                              |M| ⩽ dn − (d − 1).
This boundary cannot be improved.
   On the other hand, we estimate the number of elements in non-basic
subsets that are minimal in the sense of inclusion (to be precise, we call a non-
basic subset M ⊂ [n]d minimal, if any proper subset K ⊂ M is basic). In
order to obtain non-trivial results, we suppose that each layer of [n]d contains
an element of a subset, where by layers we mean hyperplanes orthogonal to
the coordinate axes, so that each layer can be determined by the equation
xi = j, where i ∈ [d] and j ∈ [n].
Theorem 1.2. If M ⊂ [n]d is a minimal non-basic subset such that every
layer of [n]d has a non-empty intersection with M, then
                           2n ⩽ |M| ⩽ dn − (d − 2).
     The condition for a subset M ⊂ [n]d to be basic is equivalent to the
consistency of the corresponding system of linear equations with integer co-
efficients. In particular, there exists an algorithm, polynomial in |M|, for
determining whether the subset M is basic. For d = 2, however, one can
indicate a simpler algorithm based on the following criterion: a finite subset
M ⊂ [n]2 is basic if and only if M does not contain a closed array, that is,
a non-trivial finite array with the coinciding first and last points [4]. The
reader can think about this criterion in the following way. Let us color the
points of a closed array in two colors, say, color odd points in red and even
points in blue respectively. Then every layer contains the same number of red
and blue points. Extending the idea of coloring onto Rd , we get the following
theorem.

                                        2

PAGE 3 Theorem 1.3. Let M ⊂ [n]d be a subset containing two or zero elements
in every layer. Then M is non-basic if and only if there exists a non-empty
subset K ⊂ M and a coloring of K in two colors such that every layer
contains the same number of elements of each color.
   It turns out that the same ideas can be used for studying doubly-weighted
graphs as well. In particular, we establish the following result which is of
independent interest.
Theorem 1.4. A graph G = (V, E) does not contain a bipartite connected
component if and only if for any vertex weight function wV : V → R there
exists an edge weight function wE : E → R such that the weight of any vertex
is equal to the sum of weights of the edges incident to this vertex:

    Assigning colors to elements of a finite subset M ⊂ [n]d is a particular case
of weight functions M → Z. Identifying red and blue colors with values of the
set {−1, 1}, one can notice that if a non-basic subset M satisfies Theorem 1.3,
then, for a subset K ⊂ M, the sum of the weight function values taken over
a fixed layer is zero. This observation leads us to the following definition.
We will call f : M → Z annihilation function of M, if for each layer L:
                                 X
                                       f (X) = 0.
                               X∈L∩M

It turns out that basic and minimal non-basic subsets admit the following
descriptions in terms of their annihilation functions.
Lemma 1.5. A subset M ⊂ [n]d is non-basic if and only if there exists
a non-trivial annihilation function of M.
Lemma 1.6. If a non-basic subset M ⊂ [n]d is minimal, then the annihila-
tion function is unique up to multiplying by a constant.
    As we have just seen, when a non-basic subset M satisfies conditions of
Theorem 1.3, one can choose an annihilation function of M whose values are
in {−1, 0, 1}. In general, however, the values are not bounded. More pre-
cisely, consider the annihilation function of a minimal non-basic subset M
whose values are setwise coprime integers (we will call such annihilation func-
tions irreducible). Then the following result takes place.

                                       3

PAGE 4 Theorem 1.7. For any positive integer m there exist a positive integer n
and a minimal non-basic subset M ⊂ [n]3 whose irreducible annihilation
function f has the value f (X) = m for some X ∈ M.

    Since the coefficients of irreducible annihilation functions are unbounded,
one cannot expect to have a criterion similar to Theorem 1.3 in the general
case. Nevertheless, one can obtain a simplification by considering an annihi-
lation function f of a subset M ⊂ [n]d as a function f : [n]d → Z such that
f (X) = 0 as long as X ∈    / M. The simplest non-trivial annihilation func-
tions of [n]d correspond to the simplest closed arrays, i.e. rectangles (we call
such annihilation functions simple, see Definition 5.2). It turns out that any
annihilation function can be generated as a sum of simple annihilation func-
tions. This observation gives us a rather simple way to construct non-trivial
non-basic subsets of [n]d as domains of annihilation functions of [n]d .

Theorem 1.8. Every annihilation function of [n]d can be decomposed into
a finite sum of simple annihilation functions.

    The structure of the paper is the following. In Section 2, we translate the
concept of finite basic subsets into the language of systems of linear equations
and study their properties. Section 3, Section 4 and Section 5 are devoted
to the proofs of Theorems 1.1 and 1.2, Theorems 1.3 and 1.4, Theorems 1.7
and 1.8 respectively. We end the paper by Section 6 discussing possible
directions for further research.

Proof of Lemma 1.5. A subset M ⊂ [n]d is non-basic if and only if the rows
of the matrix AM of system (2) are linearly dependent. In other words, there
exists a non-trivial linear combination of rows which equals zero. Since the
rows and columns of AM correspond to the elements of M and the layers
respectively, coefficients of this linear combination are the values of annihi-
lation function. Note, that the entries of AM are zeroes and ones, therefore,
it is possible to choose a linear combination with integer coefficients.
Proof of Lemma 1.6. The existence of two different annihilation functions
corresponds to the existence of two different linear combinations of rows
of AM that equal zero. The latter implies that we can construct a non-trivial
linear combination of rows such that at least one of its coefficients is zero.
Hence, there is a proper subset K ⊂ M which is non-basic.
Remark 2.3. The converse of Lemma 1.6 does not hold. For example, let
              M = {(1, 1, 1), (2, 1, 1), (1, 2, 1), (1, 1, 2), (2, 2, 1)}.

plane array (see Fig. 1). Nevertheless, any annihilation function of M is
a product of

and some constant.
   We can see that in this example f (1, 1, 2) = 0. In fact, the uniqueness of
the annihilation function implies minimality of a non-basic subset M, if we
additionally require that f (X) 6= 0 for any X ∈ M.

Lemma 2.4. A subset M ⊂ [n]d is basic, if for any non-empty subset K ⊂ M
there is a layer containing only one element from K.

Proof. In terms of matrix AM , the conditions of Lemma 2.4 mean that any
collection of rows of AM generates a submatrix having a column with the
unique non-zero entry. Hence, there is no non-trivial linear combination of
rows of AM which equals zero. This implies that M is basic.


3     Estimations for basic and non-basic subsets
    The main goal of this section is to prove Theorems 1.1 and 1.2.

Lemma 3.1. Let n ∈ N and Yij be coordinates in Rdn , where i ∈ [d] and
j ∈ [n]. If the entries of each of m vectors from Rdn satisfy (d − 1) equations

                      Y11 + . . . + Y1n = . . . = Yd1 + . . . + Ydn                        (3)

and m > dn − (d − 1), then these m vectors are linearly dependent.

Proof. Each vector is contained in the subspace V ⊂ Rdn determined by
condition (3). Hence, dim V = dn − (d − 1) < m, which implies that any
m vectors in V are linearly dependent.
Proof of Theorem 1.1. Let us suppose that |M| > dn − (d − 1). Then the
rows of the matrix AM satisfy conditions of Lemma 3.1 for m = |M|. Hence,
they are linearly dependent, meaning, that M is not basic.
   To show that it is impossible to improve the inequality, it is sufficient to
present an appropriate example. For this purpose, set M to be as follows:

         M = {(k, 1, . . . , 1), (1, k, . . . , 1), . . . , (1, 1, . . . , k) | k ∈ [n]}

(Figure 2 illustrates the set M in the case d = 3, n = 4). Then M is basic
by Lemma 2.4 and |M| = dn − (d − 1).

4     Criterion for certain subsets to be basic
    The main goal of this section is to prove Theorem 1.3 and Theorem 1.4.
The second part of the section assumes that the reader is familiar with the
basics of hypergraph theory. For an extensive account of this topic, we refer,
for example, to [2].
Proof of Theorem 1.3. Let us assume that there is an appropriate coloring
of a subset K ⊂ M. Then this coloring corresponds to the annihilation
function f : K → {±1}. Extending f to the set M by defining f (X) = 0 for
X ∈ M \ K, we obtain an annihilation function of M. Hence, by Lemma 1.5,
M is non-basic.

   A subset M ⊂ [n]d can be naturally considered as a hypergraph whose
vertices are elements of M and hyperedges are subsets of elements of M
that lie in the same layer. We denote this hypergraph G(M). If every layer
contains two or zero elements of M, then the hypergraph becomes a graph,
possibly with multiple edges.
   The notion of finite basic subsets can be naturally extended to hyper-
graphs as follows.
