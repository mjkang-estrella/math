253: Motivated by relativistic materials,
254: we develop a numerical scheme to support existing
255: or state new conjectures in the spectral optimisation
256: of eigenvalues of the Dirac operator,
257: subject to infinite-mass boundary conditions.
258: We study the optimality
259: of the regular polygon (respectively, disk)
260: among all polygons of a given number of sides
261: (respectively, arbitrary sets),
262: subject to area or perimeter constraints.
263: We consider the three lowest positive eigenvalues and their ratios.
264: Roughly, we find results analogous
265: to known or expected for the Dirichlet Laplacian,
266: except for the third eigenvalue which does not need
267: to be minimised by the regular polygon
268: (respectively, the disk) for all masses.
269: In addition to the numerical results,
270: a new, mass-dependent upper bound to the lowest
271: eigenvalue in rectangles is proved
272: and its extension to arbitrary quadrilaterals is conjectured.

286: The ancient Greeks had no doubt that the disk has the largest
287: area among all domains of fixed perimeter.
288: Simply because they could have checked it by measuring
289: on a huge number of samples.
290: The conviction is reflected in the celebrated account
291: of the legendary Dido founding Carthage almost a millennium BC.
292: Moreover, the Greeks pretty much solved the isoperimetric problem,
293: by their standards,
294: when Zenodorus proved that the disk has greater area than any
295: polygon with the same perimeter.
296: However, a rigorous proof for all domains
297: was done only by Steiner, by his standards,
298: and completed by Weierstrass in 1879.
299: The almost three millennia were necessary to develop
300: new tools in mathematics to prove

1887: To mitigate potential dependencies on the initial shape which could influence the optimal domain, several additional simulations were carried out using different starting domains and collocation/source points for the Method of Fundamental Solutions. The domain presented not only exhibited the lowest second eigenvalue but also shared a very similar shape to the other domains identified in various simulations. Unfortunately, it is very hard to impose topological conditions on the optimisation algorithm and the simulations show that the method tries to close the gap between the cusps in Figures 19 and 20, resulting in a disjoint domain. However, since we are working with only one connected domain, this forces the domain to auto-intersect, creating ties which are not addressed by the MFS. In the same vein as in the relativistic analogue of the Faber–Krahn inequality, and given the resemblance of the optimal found shape with two disjoint disks with the same area, we also conjecture that a relativistic analogue of the Krahn–Szegö inequality also holds for the Dirac operator with infinite mass boundary conditions. For future reference, the first and second eigenvalues of two disjoint disks with unitary total area for  m=1  are  \lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}\approx 4.16799436 . These results suggest that an analogous result to the Krahn inequality for the classical Laplacian [30] shall hold for Dirac eigenvalues.
