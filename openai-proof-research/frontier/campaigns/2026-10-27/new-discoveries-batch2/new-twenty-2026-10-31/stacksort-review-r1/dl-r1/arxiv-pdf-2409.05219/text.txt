                                                  Abstract. Defant found that the relationship between a sequence of (univariate) classical cumu-
                                                  lants and the corresponding sequence of (univariate) free cumulants can be described combinatori-
                                                  ally in terms of families of binary plane trees called troupes. Using a generalization of troupes that

                                                  we call weighted troupes, we generalize this result to allow for multivariate cumulants. Our result

                                                  whose cumulants correspond to some specific weighted troupes.




                                                                                          1. Introduction
                                             Let A be a unital associative algebra over a commutative ring K, and let φ : A → K be a
                                          unital linear functional. The pair (A, φ) is called a noncommutative probability space (over K),
                                          and the functional φ is called a noncommutative expectation. Let (X1 , . . . , Xn ) be a tuple of
                                          elements of A. We associate to this tuple three important quantities: the classical cumulant
                                          Kn (X1 , . . . , Xn ), the free cumulant Rn (X1 , . . . , Xn ), and the Boolean cumulant Bn (X1 , . . . , Xn ).
                                          The classical cumulant Kn (X1 , . . . , Xn ) is informally a measure of the dependence of X1 , . . . , Xn ,
                                          whereas the free cumulant Rn (X1 , . . . , Xn ) is an analogue used in the free probability theory of
                                          Voiculescu [MS17,Voi85,Voi91,Voi94,VDN92]. Classical, free, and Boolean cumulants are all related
                                          by formulas involving sums over particular sets of partitions of the set [n] := {1, . . . , n} [AHLV15].
                                             If the variables X1 , . . . , Xn are all equal, then more is known. In 2013, Josuat-Vergès found
                                          a combinatorial expansion of the negative classical cumulant −Kn (X, . . . , X) into negative free
                                          cumulants −Ri (X, . . . , X) for i ≤ n [JV13]. In 2022, Defant proved that the coefficients of Josuat-
                                          Vergès’s expansion count objects called valid hook configurations, which also appear in the study
                                          of West’s stack-sorting map [Def22]. This connection allowed Defant to prove several results about
                                          the stack-sorting map.
                                             Defant also found that the relationship between free and classical cumulants is encoded by special
                                          families of binary plane trees called troupes. Our main theorem (Theorem 4.1) generalizes this result
                                          in three ways. First, our theorem deals with a new generalization of troupes that we call weighted
                                          troupes. Second, our theorem does not require the elements X1 , . . . , Xn ∈ A to be all equal. Third,
                                          our theorem handles Boolean cumulants in addition to classical and free cumulants.
                                             A troupe is a family of binary plane trees satisfying certain conditions related to an operation
                                          called insertion. A branch is a binary plane tree in which no vertex has more than one child.
                                          In [Def22], Defant proved that each troupe is uniquely determined by the set of branches that
                                          it contains and that every set of branches generates a unique troupe. He then defined the troupe
                                          transform to be the transform that takes as input a sequence (ωn )n≥1 enumerating a set of branches
                                          and outputs the sequence (q      ωn )n≥1 enumerating the troupe generated by that set of branches. He

2.1. Partitions. Let Y be a finite set. A partition of Y is a collection of nonempty disjoint sets,
called blocks, whose union is Y . For any partition π of Y , we define an equivalence relation ∼π on
Y , where i ∼π j if and only if i and j are in the same block of π. In this way, partitions of Y are
in bijective correspondence with equivalence relations on Y .
   Suppose that Y is totally ordered. We say that a partition π of Y is an interval partition if there
do not exist i, j, k ∈ Y with i < j < k such that i ∼π k ̸∼π j. We say that π is noncrossing if there
do not exist i, j, k, ℓ ∈ Y with i < j < k < ℓ such that i ∼π k, j ∼π ℓ, and i ̸∼π j. We say that a
noncrossing partition π of Y is irreducible if min Y ∼π max Y . We write Π(n), Int(n), NC(n), and
NCirr (n) for the set of partitions of [n], the set of interval partitions of [n], the set of noncrossing
partitions of [n], and the set of irreducible noncrossing partitions of [n], respectively.
   Let Sn denote the symmetric group whose elements are permutations of [n]. The descent
set of a permutation σ = σ(1) · · · σ(n) ∈ Sn is Des(w) = {i ∈ [n − 1] : σ(i) > σ(i + 1)}. Let
des(σ) = |Des(σ)|. The descending runs of σ are the maximal consecutive decreasing subsequences
of σ; note that σ has n − des(σ) descending runs. Let druns(σ) be the partition
                                  {D1 (σ), . . . , Dn−des(σ) (σ)} ∈ Π(n),
where Dj (σ) is the set of numbers appearing in the jth descending run of σ. For example,
                     druns(854791632) = {{4, 5, 8}, {7}, {1, 9}, {2, 3, 6}} ∈ Π(9).

2.3. Binary Plane Trees. A binary plane tree is a rooted tree T in which each child of a vertex
is designated as a left child or a right child and no vertex can have more than one left child or
more than one right child. The empty binary plane tree is denoted ∅. When there is no risk of
confusion, we also use the symbol T to denote the set of vertices of the tree T . The size of T ,
denoted |T |, is the number of vertices in T . Let BPT denote the set of all binary plane trees (up
to isomorphism), and let BPTn denote the set        of all binary plane trees of size n. It is a classical
                                              1  2n
result that |BPTn | = Cn , where Cn = n+1         n denotes the nth Catalan number.
    The inorder on a binary plane tree T is the unique total order on the vertices of T such that for
each vertex v of T , all the vertices in the left subtree of v precede v, which in turn precedes all the
vertices in the right subtree of v. The postorder on a binary plane tree T is the unique total order
on the vertices of T such that for each vertex v of T , all the vertices in the left subtree of v precede
all the vertices in the right subtree of v, which in turn precede v.
    A labeling of a binary plane tree T is a function L : T → N. The label of a vertex v under a
labeling L is the integer L(v). We say that a labeling of T is standard if it uses each of the labels
1, . . . , |T | exactly once. A labeling of a binary plane tree is decreasing if the label of each vertex is
greater than the labels of its children.
    Given a binary plane tree T , we will now describe two important standard labelings of T . The
inorder labeling of T , denoted IT , assigns the vertices of T the labels 1, . . . , n in inorder. The
postorder labeling of T , denoted PT , assigns the vertices of T the labels 1, . . . , n in postorder. Note
that the postorder labeling of T is decreasing. See Figure 1.
    Let DBPT denote the set of all trees T ∈ BPT that are equipped with a standard decreasing
labeling L. Formally, DBPT is the set of all pairs (T, L), where T ∈ BPT and L is a standard
decreasing labeling of T . However, we will usually refer to an element of DBPT using just the
letter T and leave the labeling L implicit. We call the elements of DBPT decreasing binary plane
trees. Let DBPTn denote the set of all decreasing binary plane trees of size n. There is an injection
ι : BPT → DBPT that equips each binary plane tree T with its postorder labeling.
    A branch is a nonempty binary plane tree in which every vertex has at most one child. Let
Branch denote the set of all branches, and let Branchn denote the set of all branches of size n.
Clearly, we have |Branchn | = 2n−1 for n ≥ 1.
    We now define functions α, β : DBPTn → Sn . Given (T, L) ∈ DBPTn and a label k ∈ [n], let
                      α(T, L)(k) = L(IT−1 (k))        and β(T, L)(k) = L(PT−1 (k)).
In other words, α(T ) is the permutation formed by reading the labels of the vertices of T in inorder,
while β(T ) is the permutation formed by reading the labels of the vertices of T in postorder.

   Fix an index set I (possibly infinite). An I-coloring of a binary plane tree T is a function
χ : T ⊔ {⊟} → I, where ⊟ is a special symbol that denotes an element not in T . Let BPT(I) denote
the set of all I-colored binary plane trees, let Branch(I) denote the set of all I-colored branches,
and let DBPT(I) denote the set of all I-colored binary plane trees equipped with a decreasing
labeling. We have an injection ι : BPT(I) → DBPT(I) that assigns each I-colored binary plane tree
the postorder labeling.
   Much of the flavor of our results is retained if I = {⋆} is a singleton, so the reader may find it
helpful to keep that special case in mind. In that case, BPT(I), Branch(I), and DBPT(I) are in
obvious bijection with BPT, Branch, and DBPT, respectively. When there is no risk of confusion, we
will sometimes use the notations BPT, Branch, and DBPT as shorthand for BPT({⋆}), Branch({⋆}),
and DBPT({⋆}), respectively.
   Given i1 , . . . , in ∈ I, define DBPT(i1 , . . . , in ) to be the set of all T ∈ DBPT(I) such that χ(⊟) = in
and such that χ(v) = iL(v) for all v ∈ T .
   Define BPT(i1 , . . . , in ) = ι−1 (DBPT(i1 , . . . , in )). This is the set of all I-colored binary plane
trees such that the colors of the vertices form the sequence i1 , . . . , in−1 when read in postorder and
such that the color of ⊟ is in .
   Define Branch(i1 , . . . , in ) = Branch(I) ∩ BPT(i1 , . . . , in ).
   Given a tree T ∈ DBPT and a vertex v ∈ DBPT with exactly one child, we can swing T at v by
changing the subtree of v from a left subtree to a right subtree or vice versa. In a similar way, we
may swing trees in BPT, Branch, BPT(I), Branch(I), DBPT(I), BPT(i1 , . . . , in ), Branch(i1 , . . . , in ),
and DBPT(i1 , . . . , in ).

   Let T1 , T2 ∈ BPT(I) \ {∅}, and let v be a vertex of T1 . We will now define a new I-colored
tree ∇v (T1 , T2 ) ∈ BPT(I) \ {∅}, called the insertion of T2 into T1 at v. First, form a new tree T1v
from T1 by extending v into a left edge; identify v with the bottom vertex of this left edge, and
call the upper vertex v ∗ . Next, attach T2 as the right subtree of v ∗ . To define the I-coloring χ
of ∇v (T1 , T2 ), let χ1 and χ2 be the I-colorings of T1 and T2 , respectively. For u ∈ T1 ⊔ {⊟}, let
χ(u) = χ1 . For u′ ∈ T2 , let χ(u′ ) = χ2 (u′ ). Finally, let χ(v ∗ ) = χ2 (⊟).

Remark 3.4. Let us make an analogy between binary plane trees and classical algebraic structures
(such as modules over a ring). One should view I-colored branches as the “indecomposable” objects
of BPT(I) \ {∅}. Each tree T ∈ BPT(I) \ {∅} can be obtained from a sequence of I-colored
branches via iterated insertions. Such a sequence of I-colored branches is not necessarily unique.
However, Proposition 3.3 implies that the multiset of I-colored branches appearing in the sequence
is uniquely determined by T (it is IF(T )). Thus, one can view Proposition 3.3 as an analogue of
the Jordan–Hölder theorem for binary plane trees.
