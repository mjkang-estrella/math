9:                                                                                         Abstract
10:                                                     In this paper we introduce a product-like operation that generalizes the construc-
11:                                                 tion of generalized Sierpiński graphs. Let G, H be graphs and let f : V (G) → V (H)
12:                                                 be a function. Then the Sierpiński product of G and H with respect to f is defined
13:                                                 as a pair (K, ϕ), where K is a graph on the vertex set V (G) × V (H) with two types
14:                                                 of edges:
15:                                                    • {(g, h), (g, h′ )} is an edge in K for every g ∈ V (G) and every {h, h′ } ∈ E(H),
16:                                                    • {(g, f (g′ ), (g′ , f (g))} is an edge in K for every edge {g, g ′ } ∈ E(G);
17:                                                 and ϕ : V (G) → V (K) is a function that maps every vertex g ∈ V (G) to the vertex
18:                                                 (g, f (g)) ∈ V (K). Graph K will be denoted by G ⊗f H. Function ϕ is needed to
19:                                                 define the product of more than two factors. By applying this operation n times to
20:                                                 the same graph we obtain the n-th generalized Sierpiński graph.
21:                                                     Some basic properties of the Sierpiński product are presented. In particular, we
22:                                                 show that G ⊗f H is connected if and only if both G and H are connected and we
23:                                                 present some necessary and sufficient conditions that G, H must fulfill in order for
24:                                                 G⊗f H to be planar. As for symmetry properties, we show which automorphisms of
25:                                                 G and H extend to automorphisms of G ⊗f H. In many cases we can also describe
26:                                                 the whole automorphism group of G ⊗f H.

32:                                           1     Introduction
33:                                           The family of Sierpiński graphs Spn was first introduced by Klavžar and Milutinović in [13]
34:                                           as a variant of the Tower of Hanoi problem. They can be defined recursively as follows:
35:                                           Sp1 is isomorphic to the complete graph Kp and Spn+1 is constructed from p copies of Spn by
36:                                           adding exactly one edge between every pair of copies of Spn+1 . Sierpiński graphs S31 , S32 ,
37:                                           and S33 are depicted in Figure 1. In the “classical” case, when p = 3, the Sierpiński graphs
38:                                           are isomorphic to Hanoi graphs. More about Sierpiński graphs and their connections to
39:                                           the Hanoi graphs can be found in the recent second edition of the book about the Tower
40:                                           of Hanoi puzzle by Hinz et al. [7].
41:                                                Sierpiński graphs have been extensively studied in most graph-theoretical aspects as
42:                                           well as in other areas of mathematics and even psychology. Some notable papers are
43:                                           [8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 19, 23, 24]. An extensive summary of topics studied on and around

48: that paper the authors introduced Sierpiński-type graphs as graphs that are derived from
49: or lead to the Sierpiński triangle fractal.

56:     Recently these families of graphs have been generalized by Gravier, Kovše and Parreau
57: to a family called generalized Sierpiński graphs [5]. Instead of iterating a complete graph
58: they start with an arbitrary graph G and form a self-similar graph in the same way as
59: Sierpiński graphs are derived from a complete graph. See Figure 2 for an example of the
60: second iteration of a generalized Sierpiński graph, where the base graph is a house. For a
61: given graph G, SGn denotes the n-th iteration generalized Sierpiński graph.

68:     The generalized Sierpiński graphs have been extensively studied in the past few years.
69: A few years after they were introduced in 2011 the first two papers appeared at the
70: same time. Geetha and Somasundaram [4] studied their total chromatic number while
71: Rodrı́guez-Velázquez and Tomás-Andreu [22] examined their Randić index. Shortly after-
72: wards several papers followed on similar topics, but also on the chromatic number, vertex
73: cover number, clique number, and domination number, see [21].
74:     Metric properties have always presented intriguing problems in the family of Sierpiński-
75: type graphs mostly due to their connection to the Hanoi graphs. Namely a solution to
76: the Tower of Hanoi problem may be modelled as a shortest path problem on the corre-
77: sponding Hanoi graph. Therefore it is not surprising that metric properties of generalized
78: Sierpiński graphs have been studied as well. In [3] Estrada-Moreno, Rodrı́guez-Bazan
79: and Rodrı́guez-Velázquez investigate distances and present, among other results, an algo-
80: rithm for determining the distance between an extreme vertex and an arbitrary vertex of
81: a generalized Sierpiński graph. In the recent paper [1] Alizadeh et al. investigate metric
82: properties for generalized Sierpiński graphs where the base graph is a star graph.

86:     At this point we would like to mention another approach towards the Sierpiński graphs.
87: The graphs S3n appear naturally locally by applying a series of truncations of maps; see
88: Pisanski and Tucker [20] and Alspach and Dobson [2]. For a cubic graph G this is
89: equivalent to applying a series of truncations to G, where the truncation of G is the line
90: graph of the subdivision graph of G. For any graph and the neighbourhood of vertex of
91: valence d the repeated truncation looks like Sdn . A related construction, called the clone
92: cover, is considered by Malnič, Pisanski and Žitnik in [17].
93:     In this paper we generalize the generalized Sierpiński graphs even further. Instead of
94: taking just one graph, we take two (or multiple) graphs and present the operation that
95: yields SGn from SGn−1 and G as a product. If we take two graphs G and H, the resulting
96: product locally has the structure of H, but globally it is similar to G. We call such a
97: product operation the Sierpiński product.
98:     The Sierpiński product shows some features of classical graph products [6], the most
99: important being that the vertex set of the Sierpiński product of graphs G and H is
100: V (G) × V (H). However, one needs some extra information outside G and H to complete
101: the definition of the Sierpiński product of graphs G and H. This information can be
102: encoded as a function f : V (G) → V (H). Furthermore, the product is defined so that we
103: can extend it to multiple factors. We will see that by definition the Sierpiński product of
104: two graphs is always a subgraph of their lexicographic product.
105:     The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we give a formal definition of the
106: Sierpiński product of graphs G and H with respect to f : V (G) → V (H), this product is
107: denoted by G ⊗f H. We explore some graph-theoretical properties such as connectedness
108: and planarity of the Sierpiński product. In particular, we show that G ⊗f H is connected
109: if and only if both G and H are connected and we present some necessary and sufficient
110: conditions that G, H must fulfill in order for G ⊗f H to be planar. In Section 3 we study
111: symmetries of the Sierpiński product of two graphs. We focus on the automorphisms of
112: G ⊗f H that arise from the automorphisms of its factors and study the group, generated
113: by these automorphisms. In many cases we can also describe the whole automorphism
114: group of G ⊗f H. Finally in Section 4 we consider the Sierpiński product of more than
115: two graphs. In the special case when we have n equal factors, say equal to G, and
116: f : V (G) → V (G) is the identity function, their Sierpiński product is equal to SGn .

119: 2     Definition of the Sierpiński product and basic prop-
120:       erties
121: Let us first review some necessary notions. All the graphs we consider are undirected
122: and simple. Let G be a graph and x be a vertex of G. By N(x) we denote the set of
123: vertices of G that are adjacent to x, i.e., the neighborhood of x. Vertices in this paper will
124: usually be tuples, but instead of writing them in vector form (xm , . . . , x1 ), we will usually
125: write them as words xm . . . x1 . More precisely, vertices (0, 0, 0) or (0, (0, 0)) will simply
126: be denoted by 000, except in the case when we will emphasize their origins. The number
127: of vertices of a graph G, i.e., the order of G, will be denoted by |G|, and the number of
128: edges of G, i.e., the size of G, will be denoted by ||G||.

130: Definition 2.1. Let G, H be graphs and let f : V (G) → V (H) be a function. Then the
131: Sierpiński product of G and H with respect to f is defined as a pair (K, ϕ), where K is
132: 
133: 
134:                                                3
135: 
136: a graph on the vertex set V (K) = V (G) × V (H) with two types of edges:
137:    • {(g, h), (g, h′)} is an edge in K for every vertex g ∈ V (G) and every edge {h, h′ } ∈
138:      E(H),
139: 
140:    • {(g, f (g ′), (g ′, f (g))} is an edge in K for every edge {g, g ′} ∈ E(G);
141: and ϕ : V (G) → V (K) is a function that maps every vertex g ∈ V (G) to the vertex
142: (g, f (g)) ∈ V (K). We will denote such Sierpiński product by G ⊗f H.
143:     Often when we will have only two factors, we will be interested only in the graph
144: K and not the embedding ϕ of G into K. In such cases we will use the notation K =
145: G ⊗f H. If V (G) ⊆ V (H) and f is the identity function on its domain, we will skip the
146: index f and denote the Sierpiński product of G and H simply by G ⊗ H. The role of
147: function ϕ will become clear in Section 4. Note that there are no restrictions on function
148: f : V (G) → V (H). However, sometimes it is convenient that for every g, g1 , g2 ∈ V (G)
149: the following property holds: if g1 , g2 ∈ N(g), then f (g1) 6= f (g2). In this case we say
150: that f is locally injective.
151:     The Sierpiński product can be defined in a similar way also for graphs with loops
152: and multiple edges. In this case, a loop in G, say {g, g}, would correspond to a loop
153: {(g, f (g)), (g, f (g))} in G ⊗f H and a multiple edge in G would correspond to a multiple
154: edge in G ⊗f H, but all our graphs will be simple.

202:     We say that the subgraph of G ⊗f H from Lemma 2.4 (i) is associated with g and
203: denote it by gH. We may view G ⊗f H as obtained from identical copies of H, one for
204: each vertex of G, and attaching for every edge {g, g ′} ∈ E(G) the corresponding vertex
205: f (g) in g ′ H to the vertex f (g ′) in gH. The edges of G ⊗f H naturally fall into two classes.
206: All edges connecting different copies gH are called connecting edges, while the edges inside
207: some subgraph gH are called inner edges.

209: Lemma 2.5. Let G, H be graphs and let f : V (G) → V (H) be a function. Then G ⊗f H
210: has |G| · |H| vertices and ||H|| · |G| + ||G|| edges. In particular, G ⊗f H has ||H|| · |G|
211: inner edges and ||G|| connecting edges.

213: Lemma 2.6. Let G and H be graphs and let f : V (G) → V (H) be any mapping. Then
214: the following holds.
215: 
216:   (i) There is at most one edge connecting gH and g ′ H for every g, g ′ ∈ V (G).
217: 
218:  (ii) Suppose that f is locally injective. Then any vertex of G ⊗f H is an end-vertex of
219:       at most one connecting edge.

221: Proof. (i) Only {(g, f (g ′)), (g ′, f (g))} can connect gH and g ′ H and since G is simple there
222: is only one such edge.
223: (ii) Let (g, h) be a vertex of G ⊗f H. Since f is locally injective, there exists at most one
224: vertex g ′ ∈ N(g) such that h = f (g ′). If such a vertex exists, then (g, h) is an end-vertex
225: of the edge {(g, h), (g ′, f (g)}, otherwise it is not contained in any connecting edge.
226:     The lexicographic product of graphs G and H is a graph G ◦ H with vertex set
227: V (G) × V (H) and two vertices (g, h) and (g ′, h′ ) are adjacent in G ◦ H if and only if either
228: g is adjacent with g ′ in G or g = g ′ and h is adjacent with h′ in H. In other words, G ◦ H
229: consists of |G| copies of H and for every edge {g, g ′} in G, every vertex of gH is connected
230: to every vertex in g ′H. Therefore the next result follows straight from Definition 2.1.

240: Theorem 2.8. Let G, H be graphs and let f : V (G) → V (H) be a function. Let α ∈
241: Aut(G), β ∈ Aut(H) and f ′ = β ◦ f ◦ α. Then G ⊗f H is isomorphic to G ⊗f ′ H.
242: Proof. Define a function γ : V (G ⊗f H) → V (G ⊗′f H) by γ(g, h) = (α−1 (g), β(h)) for
243: g ∈ V (G) and h ∈ V (h). Since α, β are bijections, also γ is a bijection. Since β is an
244: automorphism, γ maps inner edges to inner edges.
245:     Take a connecting edge in G ⊗f H, say {(g, f (g ′)), (g ′, f (g))}, where {g, g ′} ∈ E(G).
246: Then γ(g, f (g ′)) = (α−1 (g), β(f (g ′)) and γ(g ′ , f (g)) = (α−1 (g ′ ), β(f (g)). Since f ′ (α−1 (g)) =
247: β(f (α(α−1(g)))) = β(f (g)) and f ′ (α−1 (g ′ )) = β(f (α(α−1(g ′ )))) = β(f (g ′)), we see that γ
248: also maps a connecting edge to a connecting edge. Therefore γ is an isomorphism.
249: Corollary 2.9. Let G be a graph and let f ∈ Aut(G). Then G ⊗ G is isomorphic to
250: G ⊗f G.
251:     In the remainder of this section we consider two other basic graph-theoretic properties
252: of the Sierpiński product with respect to its factors: connectedness and planarity.
253: Proposition 2.10. Let G and H be graphs and let f : V (G) → V (H) be a function.
254: Then G ⊗f H is connected if and only if G and H are connected.
255: Proof.       Suppose G and H are connected. Pick two vertices (g, h), (g ′, h′ ) in G ⊗f H.
256: Then there exists a path from g to g ′ in G, say g = g0 , g1 , g2 , . . . , gk−1, gk = g ′ . We
257: construct a path from (g, h) to (g ′, h′ ) in G ⊗f H, passing through g1 H, g2 H, . . . , gk−1H
258: in that order as follows. Let P0 be a path from (g0 , h) to (g0 , f (g1)) in g0 H, let Pi be a
259: path from (gi , f (gi−1)) to (gi , f (gi+1 )) in gi H for i = 1, . . . k − 1 and let Pk be a path from
260: (gk , f (gk−1)) to (gk , h′ ) in gk H. Such paths exist since every subgraph gi H is connected.
261: Then P0 P1 . . . Pk is a path between (g, h), (g ′, h′ ) in G ⊗f H.
262:      Conversely, suppose G ⊗f H is connected. Pick two vertices g and g ′ from G. Then
263: a path from gH to g ′ H in G ⊗f H corresponds to a path in G from g to g ′. Therefore
264: also G is connected. Suppose now that H is not connected. We will show that in this
265: case G ⊗f H is not connected. Denote by H1 a connected component of H such that
266: V1 = {g ∈ G| f (g) ∈ V (H1 )} is nonempty. For g ∈ V1 and h ∈ V (H1 ), all the neighbours
267: of (g, h) belong to gH1 or to g ′ H1 for some g ′ ∈ V1 . Therefore there are no edges between
268: the set of vertices {(g, h) ∈ G ⊗f H| g ∈ V1 and h ∈ V (H1 )} and the rest of the vertices
269: of G ⊗f H. So G ⊗f H is not connected. This finishes the proof.                                     ✷

274:     The next Theorem characterises when a Sierpiński product G ⊗f H is planar.
275: Theorem 2.11. Let G, H be connected graphs and let f : V (G) → V (H) be any mapping.
276: Then G ⊗f H is planar if and only the following three conditions are fulfilled:
277:   (i) graph G is planar,
278:  (ii) for every g ∈ V (G) the graph H + g is planar,
279:  (iii) there exists an embedding of G in the plane with the following property: for every
280:        g ∈ V (G), with g1 , g2 , . . . , gk being the cyclic order of vertices around g, there exists
281:        an embedding of H + g in the plane such that the cyclic order of vertices around gH
282:        in H + g is f (gk ), f (gk−1), . . . , f (g1 ).
283: 
284:                                                    6
285: 
286: Proof. All three conditions are necessary. Suppose G ⊗f H is embedded in the plane.
287: Then a planar embedding of G is obtained by contracting gH to a single vertex for every
288: g ∈ V (G). Hence G must be planar. Suppose G is embedded in the plane as above. Let
289: g ∈ V (G) and let g1 , g2 , . . . , gk , be the cyclic order of vertices around g in this embedding.
290: Let N(gH) = {g ′H|g ′ ∈ N(g)} denote the collection of graphs g ′ H that are adjacent to
291: gH for some g ∈ V (g). We contract every member g ′ H from N(gH) to a single vertex.
292: Then we keep gH, all the new vertices all the new edges and delete the rest of the graph.
293: The graph obtained in this way is still embedded in the plane. Now we identify all the
294: new vertices; we call the vertex obtained in this way gH for convenience. We obtain a
295: graph that is isomorphic to H + g. The obtained graph gH + g is planar. Moreover,
296: the cyclic order of vertices around gH in gH + g is (g, f (gk )), (g, f (gk−1)), . . . , (g, f (g1)).
297: Therefore the embedding of G, obtained from G ⊗f H by contracting every copy of H,
298: fulfills (iii).
299:     The converse goes by construction. We first embed G in the plane as in (iii) and then
300: expand every vertex g of G to gH, embedded in the plane as in (iii). By (iii) it is possible
301: to connect the copies of H such that the resulting graph is a plane embedding of G ⊗f H.

338:   2. If there are between two and four vertices in some gH, then we need at least four
339:      edges connecting gH to other copies of H in K. This is not possible, since maximal
340:      degree in G is at most three.
341: 
342:   3. There are five vertices in some gH and one vertex in some g ′ H for g 6= g ′. Since H
343:      is outerplanar, gH cannot contain a subdivision of K2,3 . Therefore we need at least
344:      two edges going out of gH to obtain a subdivision of K2,3 from the five vertices in
345:      gH. We also need three edges going out of gH to connect gH to the vertex of K3,3
346:      in g ′H. This is again not possible, since the maximal degree of G is at most 3.

349:      Since H is outerplanar, there can be at most seven edges (or paths) between pairs
350:      of vertices of K3,3 in gH. The remaining two paths must go through other copies
351:      of H, which means that we again need at least four edges connecting gH to other
352:      copies of H in K. A contradiction.

355: subdivision of K5 . There are three cases to consider, depending on how many vertices of
356: degree 4 of the subdivision of K5 are in the same copy of H.

359:      vertex for every g ∈ G we see that K5 is a minor in G, so G is not planar.
360:

362:      edges connecting gH to other copies of H in K. This is not possible, since maximal
363:      degree in G is at most three.

366:      H. Since H is outerplanar, it doesn’t contain a subdivision of K4 or K2,3 . Therefore

369:      through other copies of H, which means that we need at least four edges connecting
370:      gH to other copies of H in K. A contradiction.

391: 3     Symmetry
392: Throughout this section let G, H be connected graphs and let f : V (G) → V (H) be any
393: mapping. Recall that the edge set of G⊗f H can be naturally partitioned into two subsets:
394:     • inner edges {(g, h), (g, h′)} for every vertex g ∈ V (G) and every edge {h, h′ } ∈
395:       E(H), and
396: 
397:     • connecting edges {(g, f (g ′), (g ′ , f (g))} for every edge {g, g ′} ∈ E(G).
398: We call this partition of the edge set the fundamental edge partition. We will say that
399: an automorphism of G ⊗f H respects the fundamental edge partition if it takes inner
400: edges to inner edges, and connecting edges to connecting edges. We denote the set of all
401: automorphisms of G ⊗f H that respect the fundamental edge partition by Ã(G, H, f ). It
402: is easy to see that this set is a subgroup of the whole automorphism group of G ⊗f H.
403: If G, H are connected graphs, the automorphisms that respect the fundamental edge
404: partition have the following useful property.
405: Proposition 3.1. Let G and H be connected graphs. Then every automorphism γ̃ ∈
406: Ã(G, H, f ) permutes the subgraphs gH, g ∈ G. In particular, the restriction γ̃|V (gH) :
407: V (gH) → V (g ′H), where g ′ ∈ V (G), is a graph isomorphism.
408:    In this section we first show that any automorphism of G ⊗f H that respects the
409: fundamental edge partition induces automorphisms of G and H. And conversely, we define
410: two families of automorphisms of G⊗f H that respect the fundamental edge partition using
411: automorphisms of G and H. Then we show that in many cases all the automorphisms
412: of G ⊗f H respect the fundamental edge partition. Finally, we focus on the case when
413: G = H and f is an automorphism. In this case we can completely describe the group of
414: automorphisms that respect the fundamental edge partition.
415: 
416: 3.1     Automorphisms that respect the fundamental edge parti-
417:         tion
418: Let γ̃ be an automorphism of G ⊗f H that respects the fundamental edge partition.
419: Then it permutes the subgraphs gH, g ∈ G. Define a mapping γ such that γ(g) = g ′
420: if γ̃ maps gH to g ′ H. Obviously, γ is a bijection. Let {g, g1} be an edge of G. Then
421: {(g, f (g1)), (g1 , f (g))} is a connecting edge of G ⊗f H. Since γ̃ respects the fundamental
422: edge partition, it maps this edge to another connecting edge, say {(g ′ , f (g1′ )), (g1′ , f (g ′))},
423: where g ′ and g1′ are adjacent in G. But then γ maps the edge {g, g1} to an edge (i.e.
424: to {g ′, g1′ }) and γ is an automorphism. We will say that γ is the projection of γ̃ on G.
425: Conversely, γ̃ is a lift of γ. Note that projection of γ̃ ∈ Aut(G ⊗f H) on G is uniquely
426: defined. However, given an automorphism of G, it can have a unique lift, more than one
427: lift or none at all.
428:      On the other hand, the action of γ̃ on every copy of gH in G ⊗f H induces an
429: automorphism γg of H, defined by γg (h) = h′ if γ̃ sends (g, h) to (g1 , h′ ) for some g1 ∈ V (G)
430: and h′ ∈ V (H).
431:      We will now introduce two families of automorphism of G ⊗f H that can be obtained
432: from automorphisms of G and H. All such automorphisms respect the fundamental edge
433: partition.

452: Proposition 3.4. The mapping Ψ(α, B) is always a bijection.
453: 
454: Proof. It is enough to prove that Ψ(α, B) is injective. This is straightforward since α and
455: βg , g ∈ V (G), are all injective.

457: Proposition 3.5. The mapping Ψ(α, B) is an automorphism if and only if for every
458: g ∈ V (G) we have f ◦ α = βg ◦ f on N(g). Moreover, in this case Ψ(α, B) respects the
459: fundamental edge partition.
460: 
461: Proof. We first show that Ψ(α, B) always maps an inner edge to an inner edge. To see
462: this, let e = {(g, h1 ), (g, h2)} be an inner edge. Then Ψ(α, B) maps edge e to edge
463: {(α(g), βg (h1 )),(α(g), βg (h2 ))}, which is an inner edge since βg is an automorphism of H.
464:     Suppose now that Ψ(α, B) is an automorphism. Since Ψ(α, B) maps inner edges to in-
465: ner edges, it must map connecting edges to connecting edges. Let e = {(g, f (g1)), (g1, f (g))}
466: be a connecting edge. Then Ψ(α, B)(e) = {α(g), βg (f (g1 )), (α(g1), βg1 (f (g))} is also a con-
467: necting edge. Therefore f (α(g1)) = βg (f (g1 )). Since g1 can be any neighbour of g in G,
468: we have f ◦ α = βg ◦ f on N(g).
469:     Conversely, let f ◦α = βg ◦f on N(g) for every g ∈ V (G). Let e = {(g, f (g1)), (g1 , f (g))}
470: be a connecting edge in G ⊗f H. Then Ψ(α, B)(e) = {α(g), βg (f (g1)), (α(g1 ), βg1 (f (g))}.
471: Since f (α(g)) = βg1 (f (g)) and f (α(g1)) = βg (f (g1 )), Ψ(α, B)(e) is a connecting edge.
472: Therefore Ψ(α, B) is an automorphism.

475: 
476: Proof. Let f ◦ α = β ◦ f on N(G) for every g ∈ V (G). Since G is connected it has no
477: isolated points and so f ◦ α = β ◦ f on V (G). The claim then follows from Proposition
478: 3.5.

750: 3.3    Group of automorphisms of G ⊗f G
751: We now consider the group of automorphisms that respect the fundamental edge partition
752: in the special case when G = H and f : V (G) → V (G) is an automorphism. Since in this
753: case G ⊗f G is isomorphic to G ⊗ G we could restrict ourselves to the case where f is the
754: identity. Note that in that case the structure of the automorphism group was sketched in
755: the paper [5], but the proofs were never published.
756:     Recall that by Corollary 3.7, every automorphism α of G has a lift, Ψ(α, f ◦ α ◦ f −1 ).
757: We call this automorphism the diagonal automorphism of G ⊗f G corresponding to α and
758: denote it by ᾱ. Denote by Ā(G, f ) the set of all diagonal automorphisms. The following
759: proposition is straightforward to prove.
760: Proposition 3.24. The set Ā(G, f ) is a subgroup of Ã(G, G, f ), isomorphic to Aut(G).
761:     To determine the structure of the group Ã(G, G, f ), we first show that every element
762: of Ã(G, G, f ) can be written as a product of an element from B̂(G, G, f ) and an element
763: of Ā(G, f ). Furthermore, we show that B̂(G, G, f ) is normal in Ã(G, G, f ).
764: Theorem 3.25. Let G be a connected graph and let f : V (G) → V (G) be an auto-
765: morphism. Let γ̃ be an automorphism of G ⊗f G. Then        Q there exist α ∈  Aut(G) and
766: βg ∈ Aut(G)f (N (g)) for every g ∈ V (G) such that γ̃ = ᾱ   g∈V (G) Φ(g, βg ) .
767: 
768: Proof. Let α be the projection of γ̃ to Aut(G). Then ᾱ = Ψ(α, f ◦ α ◦ f −1 ) permutes
769: the copies gG in the right way. Observe that ᾱ already agrees with γ̃ on the endvertices
770: of all the connecting edges. To obtain γ̃ from ᾱ, we only need to adjust the action of
771: ᾱ on the vertices that are not endvertices of connecting edges. We can do this on every
772: copy gG separately, by acting with Φ(g, βg ), where βg ∈ Aut(G) is induced by ᾱ−1 γ̃.
773: Also βg ∈ Aut(G)f (N (g)) since the vertices f (N(g)) have the right image already and are
774: fixed.
775: Theorem 3.26. Let f : V (G) → V (G) be an automorphism. Then B̂(G, G, f ) is a
776: normal subgroup of group Ã(G, G, f )
777: Proof. Observe that the mapping λ : Ã(G, G, f ) → Ã(G, G, f ) defined by λ : Ψ(α, B) →
778: Ψ(α, f ◦ α ◦ f −1) is a homomorphism of groups with B̂(G, G, f ) being its kernel. Therefore
779: B̂(G, G, f ) is a normal subgroup of Ã(G, G, f ).
780: Theorem 3.27. Let G be a connected graph and let f : V (G) → V (G) be an automor-
781: phism. Then the group Ã(G, G, f ) is a semidirect product,
782:                            Ã(G, G, f ) = Ā(G, f ) ⋉ B̂(G, G, f ).
783: Proof. Group B̂(G, G, f ) is a normal subgroup of Ã(G, G, f ) by Theorem 3.26. By The-
784: orem 3.25, every element of Ã(G, G, f ) can be written as a product of a diagonal auto-
785: morphism and an element from B̂(G, G, f ). Moreover, only identity is in both Ā(G, f )
786: and B̂(G, G, f ). This proves that Ã(G, G, f ) is a semidirect product of Ā(G, f ) and
787: B̂(G, G, f ).
788:    Note that in general not every automorphism of G has a lift. Diagonal mappings
789: are well defined only if f is a bijection. Define Ā(G, H, f ) to be the set of all diagonal
790: mappings that are also automorphisms. This is a subgroup of Ã(G, H, f ). We believe
791: that the following conjecture holds.

801: 4     Sierpiński product with multiple factors
802: When extending the Sierpiński product to more than two factors we first need to specify
803: how the graph G embeds into the product G ⊗f H in order to be able to multiply it with
804: the next graph. This is exactly the role of the function ϕ from Definition 2.1. Let G1 , G2
805: and G3 be graphs and f : V (G2 ) → V (G1 ), f ′ : V (G3 ) → V (G2 ) be functions. Then the
806: Sierpiński product of these graphs is constructed so that we first build (K, ϕ) = G2 ⊗f G1
807: and then form the product (K ′ , ϕ′ ) = G3 ⊗ϕ◦f ′ K. Note that with given functions f
808: and f ′ we cannot form this product in any other way, therefore Sierpiński product is not
809: associative. We will denote such Sierpiński product by G3 ⊗f ′ G2 ⊗f G1 .
810:     In Figure 8 it is shown how the product C3 ⊗f ′ C4 ⊗f C3 is formed in two steps (with
811: f : V (C4 ) → V (C3 ), f : i 7→ i (mod 3) and f ′ : V (C3 ) → V (C4 ) being the identity
812: function on its domain).
813:     It is now easy to see that Sierpiński products possess a nice recursive structure, similar
814: to Sierpiński graphs and generalized Sierpiński graphs. By the same reasoning as above,
815: the product Gm ⊗fm−1 · · · ⊗f2 G2 ⊗f1 G1 , where V (Gℓ ) = {0, 1, . . . , |Gℓ | − 1}, and fℓ :
816: V (Gℓ+1 ) → V (Gℓ ), ℓ = 1, . . . , m − 1, are arbitrary functions, can be constructed as
817: follows.
818: 
819:     • First, take |G2 | copies of the graph G1 and label them iG1 , i ∈ {0, . . . , |G2| − 1}.
820:       Vertices of these graphs have labels g2 g1 .
821:     • Connect any two copies iG1 and jG1 if there is an edge {i, j} in G2 . More precisely, if
822:       {i, j} ∈ E(G2 ), we add an edge {if1 (j), jf1 (i)} between iG1 and jG1 . The resulting
823:       graph is then indeed the Sierpiński product G2 ⊗f1 G1 and the corresponding function
824:       ϕ1 : V (G2 ) → V (G2 ⊗ G1 ) maps i to if1 (i) for every i ∈ {0, . . . , |G2 | − 1}.
825:     • Next we form the Sierpiński product of graphs G3 and K(2) := G2 ⊗f1 G1 . To do
826:       so we take |G3 | copies of graph K(2), label them iK(2), i ∈ {0, . . . , |G3 | − 1}, and
827:       connect iK(2) and jK(2) whenever {i, j} is an edge in G3 . Such an edge then has
828:       the form {if2 (j)f1 (f2 (j)), jf2(i)f1 (f2 (i))}.
829:     • The final step is to form the Sierpiński product of graphs Gm and K(m − 1) in the
830:       same way as we formed all the products so far: make |Gm | copies of K(m − 1) and
831:       label them iK(m − 1); then for every edge {i, j} in Gm we add an edge between
832:       copies iK(m − 1) and jK(m − 1). Such an edge has then the following form
833: 
834:            {ifm−1 (j) . . . f1 (f2 . . . (fm−1 (j)) . . . ) , jfm−1 (i) . . . f1 (f2 . . . (fm−1 (i)) . . . )}.
835: 
836:       The resulting graph is the product Gm ⊗fm−1 · · · ⊗f2 G2 ⊗f1 G1 .

1007: 5     Conclusion
1008: This paper generalizes Sierpiński graphs even further than generalized Sierpiński graphs,
1009: where the whole structure is based only on one graph. Here we create a product like
1010: structure of two (or more) factors. Some basic graph theoretical properties are studied
1011: 
1012:                                               20
1013: 
1014: in detail, and planar Sierpiński products are completely characterized. Apart from this
1015: the symmetries of Sierpiński products are studied as well. In general, these are not fully
1016: understood. In many cases we are able to determine the automorphism group of Sierpiński
1017: product of two graphs exactly.
1018:       In [11] an algorithm is given for recognizing generalized Sierpiński graphs. Given a
1019: graph it is also natural to ask whether it can be represented as a Sierpiński product of
1020: two or more graphs. Moreover, one can ask if such a representation is unique. The latter
1021: question has a negative answer. Consider the Sierpiński product of C4 and 2K3 + e with
1022: function f as in Figure 7. It can be easily verified that it is isomorphic to C8 ⊗f ′ C3
1023: where f ′ : V (C8 ) → V (C3 ) is defined by f ′ (1) = f ′ (2) = f ′ (5) = f ′ (6) = 1 and f ′ (3) =
1024: f ′ (4) = f ′ (7) = f ′ (8) = 2. However, in this case not all the factors are prime with respect
1025: to the Sierpiński product: C8 can be represented as a Sierpiński product of C4 and K2
1026: while 2K3 + e can be represented as a Sierpiński product of K2 and C3 . It would be
1027: interesting to see whether there exist prime graphs with respect to the Sierpiński product
1028: G, H, G′ , H ′ and functions f : V (G) → V (H), f ′ : V (G′ ) → V (H ′ ) such that G, H are
1029: not isomorphic to G′ , H ′ while G ⊗f H is isomorphic to G′ ⊗f ′ H ′ .
