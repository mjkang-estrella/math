   We next turn into results concerned with the existence of matchings or acyclic
matchings. As mentioned in the introduction, it is established in [26] that an
abelian group has the matching property if and only if it is torsion-free or cyclic
of prime order. The proof therein utilizes Hall’s marriage theorem and a result of
Kneser on the size of sumsets in abelian groups.
Theorem 2.4 ([26]). Let G be an abelian group which is either torsion-free or
cyclic of prime order. Suppose A and B are two finite subsets of G which are of
the same size and 0 ∈/ B. Then there exists a matching f : A → B.
   The preceding theorem indicates that an abelian group which is either torsion-
free or of prime order has the matching property. As for the stronger acyclic
matching property, torsion-free abelian groups admit the latter property as es-
tablished in [26] whereas there exist infinitely many primes p for which Z/pZ does
not possess the acyclic matching property.
Theorem 2.5 ([1]). There are infinitely many primes p for which Z/pZ does not
satisfy the acyclic matching property.
Indeed, it is proved in [1] that if p ≡ −1 (mod 8) or if the multiplicative order
of 2 modulo p is odd4 then the acyclic matching property fails for Z/pZ. The
basic idea is that if a matching f : A → A is acyclic, then f and f −1 coincide
since obviously mf = mf −1 . But then, assuming that #A is odd, f must have
a fixed point a ∈ A which requires a + f (a) = 2a to be outside A. But one
may choose a subset A ⊂ (Z/pZ)× with odd cardinality which is invariant under
multiplication by 2, e.g. the set of quadratic residues when p ≡ −1 (mod 8), or
the multiplicative subgroup generated by 2 when ordp 2 is odd.
   We next turn into Question 1.4 regarding the existence of acyclic matching
from A onto B whenever all bijections A → B are matchings. Proposition below
establishes this under the assumption that B, following the terminology of [7], is
a Sidon set.
Proposition 2.6. Let A, B be subsets of an abelian group G. Suppose A and B
are of the same size satisfying A ∩ (A + B) = ∅. Then there exists an acyclic
matching f : A → B if we assume the equation x + y = z + w has no solution in
B with {x, y} ∩ {z, w} = ∅.
Proof. Aiming for a contradiction, let k be the smallest cardinality for which
the proposition is false. Label elements of A and B as A = {a1 , . . . , ak } and
B = {b1 , . . . , bk }. Pick indices i, j ∈ {1, . . . , k} arbitrarily. Bijections f : A → B
with ai 7→ bj are all matchings and are in a one-to-one correspondence with the
bijections A \ {ai } → B \ {bj }. No such f is acyclic, so there exists another

such a g with g(ai ) 6= bj as otherwise no bijection (matching) A \ {ai } → B \ {bj }

   In the case of symmetric matchings where A = B, Theorem 1.2 establishes the
existence of an acyclic matching in the absence of the Sidon condition imposed in
Proposition 2.6, but at the expense of limiting the cardinality of A.

Proof of Theorem 1.2. Aiming for a contradiction, take A to be a subset with
the smallest possible size for which the identity map id : A → A – which is a
matching due to A ∩ 2A = ∅ – is not acyclic. Therefore, writing A as {a1 , . . . , ak }
where k := #A, there should be a bijection ai 7→ aσ(i) with σ ∈ Sk \ {id} which
is a matching and has the same multiplicity function. In other words, {2ai }1≤i≤k
and {ai + aσ(i) }1≤i≤k coincide as multi-sets. Hence there must exist a second
permutation λ ∈ Sk which yields the multi-set {2ai }1≤i≤k as a re-ordering of the
multi-set {ai + aσ(i) }1≤i≤k , i.e.
                       aλ(i) + aσ(λ(i)) = 2ai for all i ∈ {1, . . . , k}.                      (4)
The equation above and σ 6= id clearly imply that both permutations λ and
σ ◦ λ are different from the identity. Moreover, no proper non-empty subset of
{1, . . . , k} is preserved by both σ and σ ◦ λ (and hence λ) because for a proper
subset of A, the identity map should provide an acyclic self-matching. Equation
(4) can be rephrased in terms of permutation matrices: Denoting the permutation
matrix corresponding to a permutation ν ∈ Sk with Pν = [pij := δiν(i) ]1≤i,j≤k ,
the vector [a1 . . . ak ]T – whose entries are distinct – should lie in the null space of
2Ik − Pλ − Pσ◦λ . We claim that the hypothesis k.2k−1 < p of the theorem implies
that if permutations α, β ∈ Sk \ {id} generate a transitive subgroup of Sk , then
no vector of Fkp with distinct entries lies in the null space of 2Ik − Pα − Pβ . The
resulting contradiction will then conclude the proof.
    Notice that the vector [1 . . . 1]T is in the kernel of 2Ik − Pα − Pβ . Our approach
is to show that, over Fp , the nullity of this matrix is one. The key idea is to first
prove this in characteristic zero: The matrix is real; so it suffices to show that
the entries of any real vector in its kernel must be identical. This easily follows
from the fact that R is ordered: if the entries of a real vector [x1 . . . xk ]T satisfy
2xi = xα(i) + xβ(i) for all i, then {i ∈ {1, . . . , k} : xi ≥ xj ∀j} is invariant under
both α and β, and thus must coincide with {1, . . . , k}. Now the characteristic
polynomial q(t) ∈ Z[t] of 2Ik − Pα − Pβ is monic with constant term 0. We
claim that its coefficient of t is non-zero; namely, the algebraic multiplicity of 0
as an eigenvalue of 2Ik − Pα − Pβ is one as well. This is due to the fact that the
