Let $f: V(G)\cup E(G)\rightarrow \{1,2,\dots,k\}$ be a non-proper total $k$-coloring of $G$. Define a weight function on total coloring as $$\phi(x)=f(x)+\sum\limits_{e\ni x}f(e)+\sum\limits_{y\in N(x)}f(y),$$ where $N(x)=\{y\in V(G)|xy\in E(G)\}$. If $\phi(x)\neq \phi(y)$ for any edge $xy\in E(G)$, then $f$ is called a neighbor full sum distinguishing total $k$-coloring of $G$. The smallest value $k$ for which $G$ has such a coloring is called the neighbor full sum distinguishing total chromatic number of $G$ and denoted by fgndi$_{\sum}(G)$. We prove that the conjecture is true for (i) paths and cycles; (ii) 3-regular graphs and (iii) stars, complete graphs, trees, hypercubes, bipartite graphs and complete $r$-partite graphs. **Abstract:** A neighbor full sum distinguishing total coloring of a graph G is a proper total-coloring such that no two adjacent vertices meet the same full sum, where the full sum of a vertex consists of all colors assigned on the vertex and its incident elements, the minimum number of the colors required is called the neighbor full sum distinguishing total chromatic number, denoted by ftdniΣ(G). Let mad(G) and Δ(G) denote the maximum average degree and the maximum degree of graph G, respectively. In this paper, we prove the following results. (i) If G is a graph with Δ(G)=3 and mad(G)<12/5, then ftdniΣ(G)≤6. (ii) If G is a graph with Δ(G)=4 and mad(G)<11/5, then ftdniΣ(G)≤8. (iii) If G is a graph with Δ(G)≥5 and mad(G)<7/3, then ftdniΣ(G)≤max{9, Δ(G)+2}. By the way, it further follows that the conjecture (Cui et al., 2023) of neighbor full sum distinguishing total coloring holds on graphs with Δ(G)≥7 and mad(G)<7/3.

**Key words:** proper total coloring; neighbor full sum distinguishing total coloring; maximum average degree; combinatorial nullstellensatz

## Abstract

For a connected graph G, the average hitting time \alpha \left(G\right) and the Kemeny’s constant \kappa \left(G\right) are two similar quantities, both measuring the time for the random walk on G to travel between two randomly chosen vertices. We prove that, among all weighted trees whose edge weights form a fixed multiset, \alpha is maximized by a special type of “polarized” paths and is minimized by a unique weighted star graph. We also obtain a similar characterization of the \kappa -maximizing and \kappa -minimizing elements among such a collection of weighted trees. Our proofs are based on the forest formulas for \alpha and \kappa .

## Introduction

In this paper, we consider random walks on graphs whose edges are assigned positive weights. When the random walk on a connected weighted graph G=(V,E) is at vertex u, it will go to vertex v with probability proportional to the weight of the edge uv (see Section 1.4 in [10]). The hitting time {H}_{G}(u,v) is the expected number of steps to walk from vertex u to vertex v. We note that {H}_{G}(v,v)=0 trivially for all vertex v. We shall study the average hitting time, defined as the arithmetic mean of all hitting times of G, \alpha \left(G\right)≔\frac{1}{{\left|V\right|}^{2}}\sum _{u\in V}\sum _{v\in V}{H}_{G}(u,v).

The stationary distribution of G is a unique probability vector {\pi }_{G} satisfying {\pi }_{G}^{T}={\pi }_{G}^{T}{P}_{G}, where {P}_{G} is the transition probability matrix for the random walk on G (see Exercise 2.1 in [10]). The Kemeny’s constant of G, similar to \alpha \left(G\right) but more complicated, is defined as \kappa \left(G\right)≔\sum _{v\in V}{H}_{G}(u,v){\pi }_{G}\left(v\right).In words, \kappa \left(G\right) is the expected time to travel from a fixed starting vertex u to a random destination sampled from the stationary distribution. Formally, this definition of \kappa \left(G\right) depends on the choice of u, but Kemeny and Snell [6] observed the surprising fact that the value \kappa \left(G\right) is independent of u (see Exercise 2.48 in [10]). The Kemeny’s constant has applications in network theory [8], robotics [11] and chemistry [9].

The original motivation of this paper is to prove the following.

Theorem 1.1

Among all unweighted trees of a fixed size, the path has the largest \alpha and \kappa , and the star graph has the smallest \alpha and \kappa .

We note that, among all unweighted trees of a fixed size, Ciardo et al. [4] previously proved the star graph minimizes \kappa ; and recently, Faught et al. [5] proved the path maximizes \kappa . Our approach will be somewhat different from theirs, and it generalizes to a weighted setting: For a multiset (i.e. set with multiplicities) W of positive numbers, we let {\mathcal{T}}_{W} be the collection of all weighted trees (up to isomorphism) T such that W equals \left\{\omega \left(e\right)\right|e\in E\left(T\right)\} as a multiset. Here, \omega \left(e\right) denotes the weight of the edge e. See Fig. 1 for examples of elements in {\mathcal{T}}_{W}. Notice that when W only consists of multiple 1’s, {\mathcal{T}}_{W} is the collection of all unweighted trees of a fixed size. We will investigate which elements in {\mathcal{T}}_{W} maximize or minimize \alpha and \kappa for a general W.

Clearly, there is a unique element {S}_{W} in {\mathcal{T}}_{W} whose underlying graph structure is a star graph. On the other hand, there are many weighted paths in {\mathcal{T}}_{W} due to various ways to assign the edge weights. We care about a special type of “polarized” paths: For a weighted path P={v}_{1}{v}_{2}\dots {v}_{n}, we denote its edges as {e}_{i}≔{v}_{i}{v}_{i+1} and define c\left({e}_{i}\right)≔min\{i,n-i\} for i=1,\dots ,n-1, then we say P is polarized provided \omega \left({e}_{i}\right)\ge \omega \left({e}_{j}\right) whenever c\left({e}_{i}\right)\le c\left({e}_{j}\right).

Intuitively, c\left({e}_{i}\right) measures how centrally {e}_{i} is located in P: We regard {e}_{1} and {e}_{n-1}, the edges containing the leaves of P, as the least central edges hence they have the smallest c\left({e}_{1}\right)=c\left({e}_{n-1}\right)=1; We regard {e}_{2} and {e}_{n-2} as the second least central edges hence they have the second smallest c\left({e}_{2}\right)=c\left({e}_{n-2}\right)=2 and so on; We regard the middle edge {e}_{\lceil \frac{n-1}{2}\rceil } (as well as {e}_{\lceil \frac{n-1}{2}\rceil +1} if n is odd) as the most central edge hence it has the largest c\left({e}_{\lceil \frac{n-1}{2}\rceil }\right)=\lceil \frac{n-1}{2}\rceil . So, in words, a weighted path is polarized if edges located more centrally are assigned smaller weights. See Fig. 1 for an illustration.

Theorem 1.2

In {\mathcal{T}}_{W}, where W is a multiset of positive numbers, the polarized paths are the elements that maximize \alpha ; and {S}_{W} is the unique element that minimizes \alpha .

By a slight modification of the proof of Theorem 1.2, we obtain a similar result for the Kemeny’s constant.

Theorem 1.3

In {\mathcal{T}}_{W}, where W is a multiset of positive numbers, the elements that maximize \kappa are exactly the paths P={v}_{1}{v}_{2}\dots {v}_{n} whose edge weight assignment maximizes \sum _{i=1}^{n-1}\sum _{j=1}^{i-1}\sum _{k=i+1}^{n-1}\frac{\omega \left({e}_{j}\right)\omega \left({e}_{k}\right)}{\omega \left({e}_{i}\right)},where {e}_{i}≔{v}_{i}{v}_{i+1} for i=1,\dots ,n-1; and {S}_{W} is the unique element that minimizes \kappa .

The characterization in Theorem 1.3 is not as clean as that in Theorem 1.2. The major contribution in the proof of Theorem 1.3 is to show that a \kappa -maximizing element in {\mathcal{T}}_{W} has to be a path. Nevertheless, there is only one unweighted path with a given size, so Theorem 1.1 is implied by Theorem 1.2, Theorem 1.3.

Inside a general {\mathcal{T}}_{W}, the \alpha -maximizing elements are quite different from the \kappa -maximizing elements. For example, when W=\{10,8,1,1,0.1\}, we can check through Theorem 1.3 that \kappa is maximized by a path with edge weight assignment {\omega }_{1}=10, {\omega }_{2}=0.1, {\omega }_{3}=1, {\omega }_{4}=1 and {\omega }_{5}=8, which is not polarized, hence not \alpha -maximizing by Theorem 1.2.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 presents definitions, notations, and the forest formulas for \alpha and \kappa ; Sections 3 Proof of, 4 Proof of are devoted to the proof of Theorem 1.2, Theorem 1.3 respectively; Section 5 lists some remarks.
