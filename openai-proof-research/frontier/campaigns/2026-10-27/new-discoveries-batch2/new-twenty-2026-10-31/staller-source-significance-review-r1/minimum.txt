                                                  The Maker Breaker Domination Game is a two player game played on a graph G
                                               in which the players take turns to claim a vertex from the graph. The aim of the
                                               Dominator is to claim the vertices of a dominating set, and the aim of the Staller is
                                               to prevent this. In this paper, we consider the following problem: for a given integer
                                               d, what is the size of the smallest (with respect to the number of vertices) graph
                                               with minimum degree d such that the Dominator loses going first? We write β(d)
                                               to denote the answer to this question. We determine the precise value of β(d) for
                                               d ≤ 3. For general d it was known that 2d+1 ≤ β(d) ≤ 2d+1 + 2d; the upper bound is
                                               due to a construction communicated to us by Valentin Gledel, while the lower bound
                                               follows from a simple application of the Erdős-Selfridge Theorem. We improve the
                                               lower bound to β(d) ≥ 2d+1 + 2.


                                          1 Introduction

                                          A Maker-Breaker game is a two-player positional game. The rules of the game are as follows.
                                          We are given a ground set X and a family F ⊂ P(X) of winning sets. The players take turns
                                          to move, and each move consists of the player claiming an element of X. Once an element has
                                          been claimed by a player, it cannot be claimed again. The goal of the Maker is to claim all
                                          of the elements of a winning set F ∈ F, while the goal of the Breaker is to prevent this from
                                          happening. One can consider the situations where the Maker or the Breaker has the first move,
                                          and moving this advantage from one player to another has the potential to significantly change
                                          the outcome of the game. See [7] for more background on Maker-Breaker games, as well as the
                                          more general topic of Positional Games.
                                          In this paper, we study the Maker-Breaker Domination game on a finite graph G = (V, E). This
                                          game was introduced by Duchêne, Gledel, Parreau and Renault [4], based closely on earlier work
                                          of Brešar, Klavžar and Rall [3]. Recall that U ⊂ V is a dominating set if

                                                                      ∀ v ∈ V \ U, ∃ u ∈ U such that uv ∈ E.



                                                                                         1
The two players in the Maker-Breaker Domination game are called the Dominator and the
Staller, and the players take turns to choose a previously unclaimed vertex from V . The goal
of the Dominator is to claim all of the elements of a dominating set, and the goal of the Staller
is to stop the Dominator from achieving this. One typically thinks of the Dominator as the
Maker and the Staller as the Breaker, in which case, the family of winning sets is the family
F consisting of all dominating sets. It is also possible to view the Staller as the Maker by
adjusting the definition of F suitably, as we see later in this paper. The game ends when either
the Dominator has claimed a dominating set (in which case the Dominator wins), or when all
vertices have been claimed and the Dominator has not claimed a dominating set (in which case
the Staller wins).
In particular, each game has exactly one winner. The following table is used to introduce the
shorthand notation that we frequently use to describe the outcome on a given graph G.


       Notation       Meaning

       o(G) = D       The Dominator has a strategy which ensures victory going second.
       o(G) = N       The Dominator has a strategy which ensures victory going first,
                      and the Staller has a strategy which ensures victory going first.
       o(G) = S       The Staller has a strategy which ensures victory going second.

It was shown in [4] that each graph G has exactly one of these three possible outcome statuses.
For ease of language and explanation, we shorten the phrasing above. For example, we say that
the dominator wins going second if o(G) = D. We use the notation o(G) ≥ N to say that the
Dominator wins going first (while giving no information about who wins when the Dominator
has the second move) and the notation o(G) ≤ N is used to describe the case when the Staller
wins going first (while giving no information about who wins when the Staller has the second
move).
For example, it is not difficult to show that o(Cn ) ≥ N for all n ≥ 3. Indeed, one can show this
with a pairing strategy (see also [4]). If n is even, then the Dominator can pair off neighbouring
vertices of the cycle and proceed to ensure that at least one element from each pair is claimed.
If n is odd, then the Dominator chooses a first vertex arbitrarily and then has a similar pairing
strategy for the remaining vertices (see Figure 1). Moreover, it is not much more difficult to
check that the Dominator wins on Cn even going second (see also [4]).
On the other hand, consider the graph G = P3 ∪ P3 formed by two disjoint copies of P3 . The
Staller has a winning strategy for this graph going second (see Figure 2). We therefore write
o(P3 ∪ P3 ) = S.
A natural and general problem is to classify the graphs which have each possible outcome.
However, obtaining such a classification appears to be a very difficult problem; determining the
outcome of the game on a given graph is PSPACE-complete (see [4]). The examples above give
some indication that the outcome of the Maker-Breaker Domination game is heavily dependent
on the structure (i.e. the edge distribution) of the graph G. In particular, it appears that
well-connected graphs offer an advantage to the Dominator. Meanwhile, low degree vertices
are helpful for the Staller. The main aim of this paper is to study the relationship between
the minimum degree of a graph G and the outcome of the game on G. Let δ(G) denote the
minimum degree of G.

A naive first question to ask is: do there exist graphs G with arbitrarily large minimum degree d
such that o(G) = S? The answer to this question is yes, and a beautiful construction of Gledel
(private communication) shows that, for every d ∈ N, there exists a graph G with 2d+1 + 2d
vertices such that the Staller wins going second. See Section 3 for more details concerning this
construction. On the other hand, it was observed in [4] that the Erdős-Selfridge Theorem (see
Corollary 8) implies that the Dominator wins going first on any graph with minimum degree d
and fewer than 2d+1 vertices.
The question that we seek to answer in this paper is the following; what is the size (in terms of
the number of vertices) of the smallest graph with minimum degree d such that the Staller wins
going first. Let β(d) denote the answer to this question, that is,

               β(d) := min{n ∈ N : ∃ G = (V, E), |V | = n, δ(G) = d, o(G) = S}.

The previous paragraph gives
                                    2d+1 ≤ β(d) ≤ 2d+1 + 2d.                                  (1)
The aim of this paper is to determine the precise value of β(d) for some small values of d. Our
main result shows that the upper bound in (1) is optimal for 0 ≤ d ≤ 3.

Theorem 1. β(0) = 2, β(1) = 6, β(2) = 12, β(3) = 22.




                                                3
The proofs that β(0) = 2 and β(1) = 6 are straightforward and can be done by hand. Proving
that β(2) = 12 could also still be done by hand, however, the computational tools we develop in
order to determine β(3) deal with β(2) quite straightforwardly, and the manual proof requires
quite some work, so we give only the computer assisted proof that β(2) = 12.
We develop some theory in this paper which gives some structural information about graphs for
which o(G) = S, namely that such graphs have what we call an RSI decomposition, see Section 5.
The proof of β(3) = 22 does then heavily rely on computer-aided computations. Breaking up
graphs into its RSI decomposition allows us to greatly reduce the required computing resources.
One may notice that all values of β(d) that are determined by Theorem 1 align with the upper
bound from (1), which does not rule out the possibility that Gledel’s construction gives the best
possible bound for all d. On the other hand, we are able to give a very small improvement to
the trivial lower bound from (1). The lower bound β(d) ≥ 2d+1 + 1 was implicit in the work of
[2], and this bound can be nudged forward slightly.

Theorem 2. For all d ∈ N, β(d) ≥ 2d+1 + 2.

We also study the same problem for the case when the Staller has the first move. To this end,
define
              β ′ (d) := min{n ∈ N : ∃ G = (V, E), |V | = n, δ(G) = d, o(G) ≤ N }.

It turns out β ′ (d + 1) can be easily calculated using β(d). We prove the following result.

Theorem 3. For all integers d ≥ 0,

                                         β ′ (d + 1) = β(d) + 1.


2 Notation and Preliminaries

In this paper all graphs are finite and simple, that is, they are undirected and have no multiple
edges or loops. For a graph G = (V, E), we denote by V (G) := V and E(G) := E the
vertex set and edge set of G, respectively. For two distinct vertices a, b ∈ V (G), we denote by
ab = ba := {a, b} the edge between a and b, and furthermore, if A, B ⊆ V (G), then EG (A, B) =
{ab ∈ E(G) : a ∈ A, b ∈ B} and eG (A, B) = |EG (A, B)|. In case A = B, we use the shorthand
notation EG (A) = EG (A, A) and eG (A) = eG (A, A). In case G is known from context, we drop
the subscript G. We write G = (A, B, E) to mean that the graph G = (A∪B, E) is bipartite with
bipartition (A, B), that is, A, B ⊆ V (G) partition the vertex set V (G) and E = E(G) = E(A, B).
For a vertex v ∈ V (G), denote by degG (v), NG (v), and NG [v] = NG (v) ∪ {v} the degree,
the neighbourhood, and the closed neighbourhood of v, respectively. In case G is clear from
context, we drop the corresponding subscript in these notations. If V (G) = {v1 , . . . , vn } with
n = |V (G)| such that deg(v1 ) ≤ · · · ≤ deg(vn ), then (deg(v1 ), . . . , deg(vn )) is the degree sequence
of G. If deg(v1 ) = deg(vn ) = d, then the graph G is called d-regular. The minimum degree
of G is denoted by mindeg(G) = deg(v1 ). A vertex of degree zero is called isolated, and the
set of all isolated vertices of a graph G is denoted by Iso(G). Again, we use the lower case
iso(G) = | Iso(G)| to denote the number of isolated vertices in G.
A graph G′ = (V ′ , E ′ ) is a subgraph of the graph G = (V, E) if V ′ ⊆ V and E ′ ⊆ E. In case
V ′ = V , we say that G′ is a spanning subgraph of G. A connected component of G is a maximal



                                                    4
set C ⊆ V where every pair a, b ∈ C of vertices is connected by a path. For a subset A ⊆ G,
the induced subgraph of G on the set A is the subgraph of G with vertex set A and maximal
edge set. It is denoted by G[A], and in case A = V (G) \ {v} for some v ∈ V (G), we also write
G − v = G[A]. If G and H are graphs with disjoint vertex sets, then the disjoint union of G and
H, denoted by G ∪ H, is the graph on V (G ∪ H) = V (G) ∪ V (H) with E(G ∪ H) = E(G) ∪ E(H).
For positive integers s, t, we use the usual notation Ks , Ks,t , and Cs for a complete graph on s
vertices, a complete bipartite graph with parts of sizes s and t, and a cycle graph on k vertices.
The graph K1,t is also called star, and a maximum degree vertex of a star is called centre of the
star.
For a graph G and disjoint subsets D, S ⊆ V (G), we call (G, D, S) a game state on G and
think of the vertices in D and S to be the vertices claimed by the Dominator and the Staller,
respectively. In particular, it makes sense to talk about the MBD-game on a game state. We
already saw an informal definition of the MBD-game in Section 1, but especially for the later
Algorithm 1, it is insightful to have a more formal recursive definition. Let us use D and S as
synonyms for the players Dominator and Staller and define the order ≤ on {D, S} by S ≤ D.

Definition 4. Let (G, D, S) be a game state, and let p ∈ {D, S}. We define the outcome
op (G, D, S) ∈ {D, S} of the game state (G, D, S) and the first player p as follows. If D ∪ S =
V (G), then op (G, D, S) = S if and only if there is a vertex v ∈ V (G) with NG [v] ⊆ S. On the
other hand, if D ∪ S ̸= V (G), then

               oD (G, D, S) = max{oS (G, D ∪ {v}, S) : v ∈ V (G) \ (D ∪ S)} and
               oS (G, D, S) = min{oD (G, D, S ∪ {v}) : v ∈ V (G) \ (D ∪ S)}.

Here min and max are with respect to the order ≤ on {D, S}. In case D = S = ∅, we drop them
in the notation and write op (G) = op (G, D, S).

We shall also use the more common notion of outcome o(G) ∈ {D, N , S} defined for a graph G
as follows.

                           o(G) = D ⇔ oS (G) = D
                           o(G) = N ⇔ oD (G) = D and oS (G) = S
                            o(G) = S ⇔ oD (G) = S

If one of the players wins on a graph going second, they also win going first (cf. [7, Proposition
2.1.6]). This means that this notion of outcome of a graph is well-defined. As before, it is
convenient to introduce the linear ordering S ≤ N ≤ D on {S, N , D} so that we can, for
example, write o(G) ≥ N to mean that the Dominator has a winning strategy on G as the first
player.

3 An upper bound construction

In this section, we derive the upper bound for β(d). This is given by a general graph construction
introduced to us by Valentin Gledel.

Theorem 5. For every d ∈ N, there exists a graph G with 2d+1 +2d vertices such that o(G) = S.

Proof. We construct a bipartite graph G = (X, Y, E) with

                                     |X| = 2d, |Y | = 2d+1

such that the Staller wins the game on G going second. The elements of X and Y each come in
pairs; let a, b, A and B be symbols for denoting vertices and let

                                    X = {a1 , b1 , . . . , ad , bd },

and let

Vertices in Y are indexed by binary sequence s ∈ {a, b}d , so we can define the edge set E as
follows: for each sequence s, we draw an edge between the corresponding elements of X and
both vertices As and Bs . For example, the sequence s = (a, a, . . . , a) gives rise to a complete
bipartite subgraph Kd,2 between the vertex sets {a1 , . . . , ad } and {As , Bs }.
A pairing strategy is used to show that the Staller wins. The Staller decomposes the vertex set
into disjoint pairs
                        (a1 , b1 ), . . . , (ad , bd ), (As1 , Bs1 ), . . . , (As2d , Bs2d ).
After each move of the Dominator, the Staller responds by choosing the other vertex in the
pairing. At the end of the game, there is some sequence s ∈ {a, b}d such that the Staller has
chosen all from X corresponding to this sequence, and the Staller has also claimed a vertex
x ∈ {As , Bs }. It follows that the Staller has claimed x and all of its neighbours, and thus this
pairing strategy guarantees that the Staller wins. See Figure 3 for an example.

Corollary 6. β(d) ≤ 2d+1 + 2d.

Putting things together, we see that if pot(G, D, S) < 1 and the Dominator sticks to his strategy
as first player, he can guarantee that the potential is always less than 1. At the end of the game,
all the vertices are claimed, and the potential has to be a non-negative integer. The potential
at the end of the game is thus 0, which means that the Dominator won. If pot(G, D, S) < 21
and the Staller is the first player, she can at most double the potential in her first move. After
that we are again in the case of pot(G, D, S) < 1 and the Dominator being the first player.

In Section 6 and for computing β(d) and β ′ (d + 1) for d ∈ {1, 2} (see the forthcoming Propo-
sition 13), we use computers to check all relevant graphs using the following Algorithm 1. At
each game state, it first checks two winning conditions: It checks whether the Staller has al-
ready claimed a winning set, and it checks whether the potential is small enough, such that it
is guaranteed the Dominator has a winning strategy. In all other cases, it recursively goes over
all continuations of the game and decides if there is a winning move for the player who moves
next.
Generally, deciding the outcome of a graph is PSPACE-complete (cf. [4]), and in fact, for our
purposes, this approach only works for very small graphs. The implementation of Algorithm 1
we use can be found in game_state.cpp. There we additionally use the heuristic that claiming
vertices v with high potential pot(G,D,S) (v), as defined in the proof of Theorem 9, is typically

In Section 6 and for Theorem 2, we need a slight modification of a partition strategy from [2].

graph Cn with n ≥ 3. The Dominator wins on K2 and on cycle graphs Cn going second. This

Proposition 14 ([2, Lemma 3.1]). Let G be a graph. If G admits a perfect [1, 2]-factor, then
o(G) = D.

Much like Tutte’s condition for a graph to admit a perfect matching [9], there is a necessary and
sufficient condition for a graph to have a perfect [1, 2]-factor. For a proof of the next theorem,
we refer the reader to [1, Theorem 7.2].

Theorem 15 (Tutte’s condition for perfect [1, 2]-factors, [10]). A graph G admits a perfect
[1, 2]-factor if and only if iso(G − S) ≤ |S| for all S ⊆ V (G).

While the Erdős-Selfridge Theorem applies to graphs on few vertices with high degrees, the
winning condition for the Dominator coming from having a perfect [1, 2]-factor is particularly
useful for almost regular graphs. The Dominator, for example, wins on regular graphs.

Corollary 16 ([2, Corollary 3.5]). Regular graphs have perfect [1, 2]-factors. In particular, the
Dominator wins on regular graphs going second.

Proof. Let G be a regular graph of degree d, and let S ⊆ V (G). Then

                               iso(G − S)d = e(S, Iso(G − S)) ≤ d|S|,

so that G has a perfect [1, 2]-factor by Theorem 15.

The modification of perfect [1, 2]-factors we need takes into account the extra first move the
Dominator has as first player. Instead of requiring every component of the spanning subgraph
F to be an edge or a cycle, we may also allow a single star to be among the components of F .

Definition 17 (v-factor). Let G be a graph, and let F be a spanning subgraph of G. Assume
there is a connected component F1 of F that is isomorphic to a star K1,k with k ≥ 0, and let v
be the centre of the star F1 . If F − V (F1 ) is a perfect [1, 2]-factor of G − V (F1 ), then F is called
v-factor of G.

It follows from Proposition 14 that if G admits a v-factor for a vertex v ∈ V (G), then the
Dominator wins on (G, {v}, ∅) going second and thus on G going first.



                                                   12
The next proposition, Proposition 19, should be seen as an analogue to Theorem 15 for v-
factors. For a graph G to have a perfect [1, 2]-factor, we have to forbid subsets S ⊆ V (G) with
iso(G − S) > |S|. It turns out that for G to have a v-factor, we have to forbid the following
structure.

Definition 18 (RSI-decomposition). Let G be a graph, let v be a vertex of G, and let R, S, I
be a partition of V (G). If v ∈ R, I = iso(G − S) \ {v}, and |I| > |S|, then the triple (R, S, I) is
called an RSI-decomposition of G separating v. Furthermore, an RSI-decomposition (R, S, I) is
called proper if every s ∈ S has at least two distinct neighbours in I.

The vertex v is usually a fixed vertex of maximum degree, so we often omit explicitly mentioning
v and just speak of an RSI-decomposition of G.

has a v-factor if and only if G′ has a perfect [1, 2]-factor.

Often it is useful to require an RSI-decomposition to be proper. This is, in fact, no restriction.

Lemma 20. Let G be a graph, v be a vertex of G, and assume that there is an RSI-decomposition
of G separating v. Then there is a proper RSI-decomposition of G separating v.

Proof. Let (R, S, I) be an RSI-decomposition of G separating v, such that |S| is minimal, and
assume there was an s with |N (s) ∩ I| ≤ 1. Consider the triple (R1 , S1 , I1 ) with R1 = R ∪ {s} ∪
(N (s) ∩ I), S1 = S \ {s}, and I1 = I \ (N (s) ∩ I). It is easy to check that this triple again forms
an RSI-decomposition of G separating v, but with |S1 | < |S|, contradicting the minimality of
|S|.

The form in which we use Proposition 19 most often is as follows.

Corollary 21. Let G be a graph with o(G) = S, and let v ∈ V (G). Then there is a proper
RSI-decomposition of G separating v.

Proof. Since o(G) = S, there is no v-factor of G. So there has to be an RSI-decomposition of G
separating v by Proposition 19. By Lemma 20, there now is a proper RSI-decomposition of G
separating v.

7 High Surplus: Completing Cores

In this section we finish the proof that β(3) = 22. Note that, together with Theorem 25 and
Lemma 24, the following theorem immediately implies this result.

Theorem 33. Let G be a reduced graph on at most 21 vertices and mindeg(G) = 3, and assume
that sur(G) ≥ 9. Then o(G) ≥ N .

Throughout this section G is a graph of minimum degree d, and we assume that G is reduced.
Recall that reduced means that no vertices of degree greater than d are adjacent. Then the core
of G is the bipartite spanning subgraph C of G with edge set

                     E(C) = {uv ∈ E(G) : degG (u) = d and degG (v) > d}.

If P1 and P2 are spanning subgraphs of G with

                                E(C) ⊆ E(P1 ) ⊆ E(P2 ) ⊆ E(G),

then P1 and P2 are called partially completed cores, or PCCs for short, and furthermore we say
that P2 is a partial completion of P1 . In case P2 has minimum degree d, which happens if and
only if P2 = G, we say that P2 is a completion of P1 . In the remainder of the section, all reduced

The rough structure of the proof of Theorem 33 is as follows. First, we use Algorithm 2 to
generate all degree sequences of reduced graphs on at most 21 vertices of minimum degree 3
and surplus at least 9, which are not ruled out by Lemma 34, an easy application of the Erdős-
Selfridge theorem. Using Nauty, we then generate the cores of the reduced graphs having one of
these degree sequences. Since cores are bipartite and we have quite restrictive degree conditions,
generating the cores is much easier than generating the reduced graphs themselves. Finally, we
use Algorithm 4 to check whether the Dominator wins on all completions of these cores going
first.

of reduced graphs. Instead of bounding the potential after one step into the game, we bound it
after two steps.

Proof of correctness of Algorithm 2. First observe that whenever the sequence (d1 , . . . , dN ) is
defined, it is non-decreasing with dN ≤ N . Indeed, (d1 , . . . , dN ) is only ever updated in Line 9
and Line 12, both updates preserve monotonicity, and dN only increases in Algorithm 2, in which

sequences (d1 , . . . , dN ) in lexicographic order; that is, if in some iteration of the loop the sequence
is (s1 , . . . , sN ) and in the next iteration it is (s′1 , . . . , s′N ) then (s1 , . . . , sN ) <lex (s′1 , . . . , s′N ). Here
(s1 , . . . , sN ) <lex (s′1 , . . . , s′N ) if there is k ∈ [N ] with si = s′i for i < k and sk < s′k . In particular,
the while loop terminates as there are only finitely many non-decreasing sequences (d1 , . . . , dN )
with dN ≤ N .

are satisfied for s: the first one follows from sur(G) ≥ s, the second one from the handshaking

The second algorithm we need for the proof of Theorem 33 is Algorithm 4. Given a core, or
more generally a PCC P , and a first player p, it decides whether the Dominator wins on all
completions of P when p starts the game. Roughly speaking, the algorithm proceeds by inserting
edges to P one-by-one and after each insertion checking if the Dominator already wins on all
the completions using Algorithm 3. For the latter algorithm, we need the notion of a game state
on a PCC. Let P be a PCC and let

                                  D, S ⊆ {v ∈ V (P ) : degP (v) ≥ d}                                   (8)

be disjoint. Then the triple (P, D, S) is called partially completed core game state on P or PCC
game state for short. Note, that in the supporting code, we use PCCGS. The game state degree of
a vertex of a PCC game state and the potential of a PCC game state are defined via the game
state degree and the potential of its completions. Let G be a completion of the PCC P and let
v be a vertex of P . Then the game state degree of v with respect to (P, D, S) and the potential
of the PCC game state (P, D, S) are defined by

              psdeg(P,D,S) (v) = sdeg(G,D,S) (v) and pccpot(P, D, S) = pot(G, D, S).

Because of (8), D and S only contain vertices v with NP (v) = NG (v), so that both quantities
are well-defined as they do not depend on the completion G. Similarly, as for PCCs if (P1 , D, S)
and (P2 , D, S) are PCC game states such that P2 is a (partial) completion of P1 , then (P2 , D, S)
is a (partial) completion of (P1 , D, S).
Before stating Algorithm 3 and proving its correctness, note that the main structure closely
follows that of Algorithm 1. Let us informally discuss some differences between these algorithms.
The main difference is that we are given a PCC game state (P, D, S) instead of a game state. In
other words, some edges between vertices v ∈ V (P ) with degP (v) < d are “hidden”. Accordingly,
we cannot always guarantee to decide whether the Dominator wins on all completions of (P, D, S)
without making assumptions about these hidden edges, in which case the algorithm returns
Unsure. It also has as a consequence that the Dominator is only allowed to pick vertices v
of degree degP (v) ≥ d, since these are vertices whose neighbourhood NP (v) is the same in all
completions of P . Finally, in the case of the Staller claiming a vertex v ∈ V (P ) with degP (v) < d,
the Erdős-Selfridge potential in completions of (P, D, S) may depend on how we complete the

assuming the potential-wise worst hidden neighbours of v.
