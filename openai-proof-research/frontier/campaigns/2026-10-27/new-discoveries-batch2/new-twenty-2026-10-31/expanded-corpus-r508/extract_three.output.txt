                                            Cake-Cutting with Different Entitlements: How Many Cuts are Needed?

                                                                                             Erel Segal-Halevi
                                                                            Ariel University, Ariel 40700, Israel. erelsgl@gmail.com

                                          Abstract
                                          A cake has to be divided fairly among n agents. When all agents have equal entitlements, it is known that
                                          such a division can be implemented with n − 1 cuts. When agents may have different entitlements, the paper
                                          shows that at least 2n − 2 cuts may be necessary, and O(n log n) cuts are always sufficient.
                                             JEL classification: D63
                                          Keywords: Fair cake-cutting, Unequal shares, Asymmetric agents, Connected components, Land division


                                          1. Introduction

                                              The fair cake-cutting problem involves a continuous resource dubbed “cake” that has to be divided among
                                          several agents with different value measures on the cake. The problem was introduced by Steinhaus [20] and
                                          inspired hundreds of research papers and several books [3, 6, 12, 15]. See [5, 7, 10, 13, 16] for recent surveys.
                                              An important requirement in dividing a cake is to minimize the number of cuts used to implement the
                                          division(see [18] and the references therein). When a cake is divided among n people, it would be ideal to
                                          make n − 1 cuts, such that each person receives a single connected piece. Making too many cuts might
                                          result in each person receiving a bunch of crumbs. Connectivity is even more important when the “cake”
                                          represents a resource such as land or time. When a land-estate is divided among heirs, a plot made of
                                          many disconnected pieces cannot be efficiently built upon or cultivated. Similarly, when the usage-time of a
                                          vacation-house is divided among its owners, having many disconnected time-intervals is practically useless.
                                              In the simplest variant of the cake-cutting problem, all agents have equal entitlements. The common
                                          fairness criterion in this case is that each agent should receive a piece that is worth for him/her at least 1/n
                                          of the total cake value (a requirement called proportionality or fair-share guarantee). Steinhaus [20] already
                                          proved that a proportional cake division can be implemented using n− 1 cuts, which is obviously the smallest
                                          number of cuts needed for making n pieces.
                                              However, in many division problems, agents have different entitlements. As an example, consider a land-
                                          estate owned by a partnership in which Alice’s share is 2/5 and George’s share is 3/5. If the partnership is
                                          dissolved, then Alice is entitled to 2/5 and George is entitled to 3/5 of the land-estate. As another example,
                                          consider a vacation-house bought by several friends, each of whom invested a different sum of money towards
                                          the purchase. The time in which the house is used should be divided among the friends in proportion to
                                          their investment.
                                              In general, in the cake-cutting with entitlements problem, each agent i has an entitlement ti such that
                                          the sum of all entitlements is 1. A division is called t-proportional if each agent i values his/her share as at
                                          least a fraction ti of the total cake value. Proportionality is the special case of t-proportionality in which
                                          ti = 1/n for every agent i. The present paper studies the following question:

                                                How many cuts are required to implement a t-proportional allocation among n agents?

                                              A first solution that comes to mind is agent cloning. In the example above, create 2 clones of Alice and 3
                                          clones of George and find a proportional division among the clones. Since each clone receives at least 1/5 of
                                          the total cake value, each original agent receives at least his/her entitlement. However, this solution might
                                          result in a large number of cuts. In particular, if the common denominator of all agents’ entitlements is D
                                          then agent-cloning might require up to D − 1 cuts.
                                              Several authors have tried to improve on the agent-cloning method with respect to computational complex-
                                          ity — the number of evaluation queries and intermediate cut-marks required to find a proportional division.

2. Definitions

    There is a cake C, represented by an interval (a connected subset of R). There is a set N of n agents,
represented by n nonatomic value-measures (Vi )i∈N on C. There is a vector tP of length n where each element
ti represents the entitlement of agent i. The vector is normalized such that i∈N ti = 1.
    The goal is to find a partition of C among the agents, (Xi )i∈N , where the Xi are pairwise-disjoint and
∪i∈N Xi = C. The partition should be t-proportional, i.e, for every agent i ∈ N :

                                                                                    Abstract
                                                     The Steklov eigenvalue problem was introduced over a century ago, and its
                                                 discrete form attracted interest recently. Let D and δΩ be the maximum vertex
                                                 degree and the set of vertices of degree one in a graph G respectively. Let λ2
                                                 be the first (non-trivial) Steklov eigenvalue of (G, δΩ). In this paper, using the
                                                 circle packing theorem and conformal mapping, we first show that λ2 ≤ 8D/|δΩ|
                                                 for planar graphs. This can be seen as a discrete analogue of Kokarev’s bound,
                                                 that is, λ2 < 8π/|∂Ω| for compact surfaces with boundary of genus 0. Let B and
                                                 L be the maximum block size and the diameter of a block graph G respectively.
                                                 Secondly, we prove that λ2 ≤ 4(B−1)(D−1)/|δΩ| and λ2 ≤ B/L for block graphs,
                                                 which extend the results on trees by He and Hua. In the end, for trees with fixed
                                                 leaf number and maximum degree, candidates that achieve the maximum first
                                                 Steklov eigenvalue are given.

                                                  Abstract. For a set of permutations (patterns) Π in Sk , consider the set of permutations in Sn
                                                  that avoid all patterns in Π. In current algebraic combinatorics, a significant problem is to identify
                                                  pattern sets Π for which the corresponding quasisymmetric function is symmetric for all n. Recently,
                                                  Bloom and Sagan proved that unless Π ⊆ {12 . . . k, k . . . 21}, the size of such Π must be at least 3
                                                  for any k ≥ 4. They also posed a general lower bound conjecture.

                                                  size of such Π is exactly k − 1. The proof relies on a novel generalization of Bose’s theorem in
                                                  extremal combinatorics, utilizing the multilinear polynomial approach introduced by Alon, Babai,
                                                  and Suzuki.

Otherwise (or if k > n), we say that σ avoids π. For instance, consider the permutation σ =
72681543 ∈ S8 . It contains the pattern 2413 ∈ S4 due to the subsequence 2614. The set of
permutations in Sn that avoid every π ∈ Π is denoted Sn (Π).
   The study of pattern avoidance in permutations has a rich history. A notable early result
in this area is the Erdős and Szekeres’ theorem [7], which states (as a special case) that every
σ ∈ Sa2 +1 contains a monotone subsequence of length a + 1. In other words, for all n ≥ a2 + 1, we
have Sn ({ιa+1 , δa+1 }) = ∅. Enumerative problems related to pattern avoidance have also received
significant attention. For instance, MacMahon [13] showed that |Sn ({123})| = |Sn ({321})| is equal
to the Catalan number, and Knuth [12] extended this result to |Sn ({π})| for any π ∈ S3 .
   A set B ⊆ Sn is referred to as symmetric or Schur-positive if its quasisymmetric generating
function, as defined in Section 2, exhibits symmetry or Schur-positivity, respectively. The study of
symmetric functions is partially motivated by the correspondence between symmetric functions of
degree n and class functions on Sn , which is established through the Frobenius characteristic map
described in [15, Chapter 4.7]. A symmetric function is Schur-positive if its corresponding class
function is a proper character (see [1] for a detailed explanation). This perspective provides a rich
algebraic framework for studying symmetric and Schur-positive sets. The investigation of such sets
was initiated in the seminal works of Gessel [8] on Knuth-classes and Gessel—Reutenauer [9] on
conjugacy classes.
   In an effort to establish a connection between pattern avoidance and algebraic structures asso-
ciated with Schur-positivity, the search for symmetric pattern-avoiding sets of permutations has
gained interest. One notable example of such a Schur-positive set is the set of arc permutations,
introduced by Elizalde and Roichman [6]. This set consists of permutations σ ∈ Sn that avoid all
patterns π ∈ S4 with |π(1) − π(2)| = 2.
   To further explore the concept, we introduce the notion of symmetrically avoided sets:
Definition 1.1. Given Π ⊆ Sk , we say that Π is symmetrically avoided if the pattern-avoiding set
Sn (Π) is symmetric for all n.
  Elizalde and Roichman’s result on arc permutations mentioned above can be equivalently stated
by noting that the set of permutations {π ∈ S4 | |π(1) − π(2)| = 2} is symmetrically avoided.
Building upon this result, Sagan and Woo posed the following problem:
Question 1.2 (Sagan and Woo [16]). Which subsets Π ⊆ Sk are symmetrically avoided?
   Sagan and coauthors [16, 10, 3] initiated the study of small symmetrically avoided sets. Using
the Robinson—Schensted correspondence, it can be shown that the singleton sets Π = {ιk } and
Π = {δk }, which consist of the increasing and decreasing elements, respectively, are symmetrically
avoided (see [10, Section 2] for more details). Furthermore, it can be shown that Sn (Π) is Schur-
positive for these singleton sets. Bloom and Sagan [3, Theorem 2.3] proved that these are the only
symmetrically avoided singleton sets.
   By combining the results of Hamaker—Pawlowski—Sagan [10, Theorem 1.2] and Bloom—Sagan
[3, Theorem 2.6], a complete classification of 2-element symmetrically avoided sets can be ob-
tained. In particular, it turns out that for every k ≥ 4 and every Π ⊆ Sk with two elements, Π is
symmetrically avoided if and only if Π = {ιk , δk }.
   Based on these findings, Bloom and Sagan formulated the following conjecture:
Conjecture 1.3 ([3, Conjecture 2.7]). For each p ≥ 3, there exists a K = K(p), such that if |Π| = p
and Π ⊆ Sk for some k ≥ K, then Π is not symmetrically avoided.
    We prove that the conjecture holds with K(p) = p + 2:
Theorem 1.4. Let p ≥ 3 and k ≥ p + 2 be positive integers, and let Π ⊆ Sk with |Π| = p. Then Π
is not symmetrically avoided.

   The bound is tight: For every p ≥ 3, there exists a symmetrically avoided set Π ⊆ Sp+1 with
|Π| = p. Such a set was discovered by Hamaker, Pawlowski and Sagan [10, Section 5] and is
discussed in Corollary 5.5.
   The proof utilizes a new generalization of a theorem by Bose [4] in extremal combinatorics, which
bounds the size of an intersecting family. Before we proceed, let us define an intersecting family:
Definition 1.5. Let F = {A1 , . . . , Am } be a family of m sets.
   (1) We say that F is k-uniform if for all i, |Ai | = k.
   (2) We say that F is ℓ-intersecting if for all i ̸= j, |Ai ∩ Aj | = ℓ.
   (3) We say that F is (ℓ1 , ℓ2 )-intersecting if the following holds for all i ̸= j:
        • If |i − j| = 1, then |Ai ∩ Aj | = ℓ1 .
        • If |i − j| ≥ 2, then |Ai ∩ Aj | = ℓ2 .
  In 1949, Bose presented an influential theorem that addressed the size of intersecting families:
Theorem 1.6 (Bose [4]). Let F = {A1 , . . . , Am } be a k-uniform family consisting of m distinct
subsets of [n]. If F is ℓ-intersecting for some ℓ ≥ 0, then m ≤ n.
  Ray-Chaudhuri and Wilson later generalized this theorem in 1975 [14]. Another proof of the
Ray-Chaudhuri—Wilson theorem was presented by Alon, Babai, and Suzuki in 1991 [2, Section 2],
where they employed multilinear polynomials and analyzed their linear structure.
  In this paper, we extend the scope of Bose’s theorem in a different direction:
Theorem 1.7. Let F = {A1 , . . . , Am } be a k-uniform family consisting of m distinct subsets of [n].
If F is (ℓ1 , ℓ2 )-intersecting for some ℓ1 , ℓ2 ≥ 0, then m ≤ n.
   We present two proofs of Theorem 1.7. The first proof applies the multilinear polynomial method
introduced by Alon, Babai and Suzuki, and the second applies matrix theory.
   The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 provides the necessary background
information, while Section 3 presents the proofs of Theorem 1.7. In Section 4, we apply our
generalized theorem to address the problem of symmetric pattern-avoiding sets. Finally, in Section 5
we conclude with further research.
