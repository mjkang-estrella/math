                                          1     Introduction
                                          All graphs considered in this paper are finite, simple and connected. Denote by Pn , In and Kn the path, the
                                          independent set and the complete graph on n vertices, respectively. Denote by Kn − e, Kn − 2e, Kn − P2 and
                                          Kn − △ the graph obtained from Kn by removing an edge, removing two nonadjacent edges, removing two
                                          adjacent edges, and removing three edges which induce a triangle, respectively. For any two vertex-disjoint
                                          graphs G and K, denote by G ∨ K the graph obtained from G and K by connecting each vertex of G to each
                                          vertex of H. Let V1 and V2 be two disjoint set of vertices of a graph G. Denote by EG (V1 , V2 ) the set of edges
                                          connecting a vertex of V1 to a vertex of V2 . When G is clear from the context, we use E(V1 , V2 ) for short.


                                          1.1    Parity signed graphs
                                          Signed graph is a kind of extension of graph, and the research on signed graph is one of the hot issues in graph
                                          theory in recent years, such as flow of signed graphs (eg. [3, 11]), homomorphism of signed graphs (eg. [9, 10]),
                                          coloring of signed graphs (eg. [5, 6, 8, 12]), circuit cover of signed graphs (eg. [7]) and so on. The concept
                                          of signed graph was first proposed by Harary [4] in 1953. A signed graph (G, σ) is a graph G together with a
                                          mapping σ : E(G) → {1, −1}. We call G the underlying graph and σ a signature of G. An edge e is positive if
                                          σ(e) = 1, and negative otherwise. Denote by (G, +) the signed graph (G, σ) with σ(e) = 1 for each e ∈ E(G).
                                          Let S = (G, σ) be a signed graph. The set of all negative edges of S is denoted by E − (S). Let v ∈ V (S). By

    Parity signed graph is a particular class of signed graphs. The concept of parity signed graphs was introduced
very recently by Acharya and Kureethara [1]. A signed graph S = (G, σ) is called a parity signed graph if there
exists a bijection f : V (G) → {1, 2, · · · , n} such that for each edge e = uv of G, f (u) and f (v) have the same
parity if σ(e) = 1, and opposite parities if σ(e) = −1. Such an f (resp., σ) is called a parity-labeling (resp.,
parity-signature) of S, or of G. In the paper [2], Acharya, Kureethara and Zaslavsky characterized parity
signed graphs as follows: A signed graph S is a parity signed graph if and only if its vertex set V (S) can be
bipartitioned into two sets V1 (S) and V2 (S) such that ||V1 (S)| − |V2 (S)|| ≤ 1 and that any two adjacent vertices
u and v of S belong to the same set if and only if the edge uv is positive. In this paper, such a bipartition is
called a parity-partition, and V1 (S) and V2 (S) are correspondingly called parity sets. The characterization above
can be easily observed by taking V1 (S) = {v ∈ V (G) : f (v) is odd} and V2 (S) = {v ∈ V (G) : f (v) is even}. In
what follows, we will use the language of parity-partition very often for parity signed graphs.


1.2    The rna number σ − (G)
In the paper [1], the authors introduced the concept of the rna number of a parity signed graph. However,
according to its definition, it is more reasonable to say the rna number of a graph. Let G be a graph. Denote by
Σ− (G) the set of the number of negative edges of a parity signed graph (G, σ) over all possible parity-signatures
σ. The rna number σ − (G) of G is defined as the minimum number of negative edges of a parity signed graph
(G, σ) over all possible parity-signatures σ, i.e., σ − (G) = min Σ− (G).
    In the paper [1], Acharya and Kureethara put forward the following conjecture on the rna number and
verified it for the class of trees.

Conjecture 1.1 ([1], Conjecture 1). Let G be a parity signed graph. Then σ − (G) = |E − (G)| if and only if G
is either K1,n , n odd or Kn .

   This conjecture is formulated a little bit weird since under the assumption that “Let G be a parity signed
graph”, we can not say “G is either K1,n , n odd or Kn ”, for which the parity signature of G has not been given.
We can check that the argument for the verification of this conjecture for trees ([1], Theorem 10) is actually a
proof of the following conjecture for trees, which is formulated slightly different from Conjecture 1.1.

Conjecture 1.2. Let G be a graph on n vertices. Then Σ− (G) = {σ − (G)} if and only if G is K1,n−1 with n
even or Kn .

    We will confirm the truth of Conjecture 1.2 in Section 2.3. There are two crucial ideas for the proof:
the application of a graph operation called “parity-switch”, and the characterization of parity signed graphs
that are “degree-balanced”. We will introduce and study the concept of parity-switch in Section 2.1, and
we will introduce the concept of degree-balance and address a complete list of parity signed graphs that are
degree-balanced in Section 2.2.
    Moreover, we study upper bounds for the rna number in Section 2.4. A result of the paper [2] (Theorem
3.4) states that for any natural number k, there is a graph G with σ − (G) = k. Hence, there exist no constant
upper bounds for σ − in general. It is known that σ − (Kn ) = ⌈ n2 ⌉⌊ n2 ⌋ ([1], Proposition 9). Actually, ⌈ n2 ⌉⌊ n2 ⌋ is
a trivial upper bound for the rna number of graphs on n vertices. In Section 2.4, we will prove a much better
upper bound than the trivial one. More precisely, we prove that for any graph G on m edges and n (n ≥ 4)

2.1    Parity-switches
Switch is an important tool for the study of signed graphs. Let S = (G, σ) be a signed graph and v ∈ V (G).
Denote by E(v) the set of edges incident with v. Switching at v gives a new signed graph S ′ = (G, σ ′ ) defined
as σ ′ (e) = −σ(e) if e ∈ E(v); and σ ′ (e) = σ(e) otherwise. Two signed graphs are called switch-equivalent if one
can be obtained from the other by a sequence of switches.
    For a parity signed graph, a switch at a pair of vertices with opposite parity labels (equivalently, a pair of
vertices locating in different parity sets) is called a parity-switch. In this paper, parity-switch plays a crucial
role in the proof of our results.
    The following proposition, which follows directly from definition, gives a characterization of parity signed
graphs from the perspective of switch. Hence, we can easily list all the parity signed graphs for any given
underlyling graph.

Proposition 2.1. A signed graph (G, σ) on n vertices is a parity signed graph if and only if it can be obtained
from (G, +) by switching at a set of vertices of cardinality ⌊ n2 ⌋.

   From any given parity signed graph, the following proposition provides a way to generate new parity signed
graphs whose underlying graph is same as before.

Proposition 2.2. Given a parity signed graph S = (G, σ), the graph S ′ = (G, σ ′ ) obtained from S by a
parity-switch is also a parity signed graph.

Proof. Exchange the labels of the two vertices we switch at. It is easy to verify by definition that the resulting
labeling is a parity-labeling of S ′ .

   Combining the previous two propositions, one can easily deduce the following proposition, which shows that
parity-switch is the exact way to describe the transformation between any two parity signed graphs having the
same underlying graph.

Proposition 2.3. Let (G, σ) be a parity signed graph. Then a signed graph (G, σ ′ ) is a parity signed graph if
and only if it can be obtained from (G, σ) by a sequence of parity-switches.

Proof. The sufficiency follows by applying Proposition 2.2 multiple times. For the necessity, by Proposition
2.1, both (G, σ) and (G, σ ′ ) can be obtained from (G, +) by switching at some set of vertices of cardinality
⌊ |V (G)|
     2    ⌋, say V1 and V2 , respectively. So, on one hand, (G, σ ′ ) can be obtained from (G, σ) by switching at
(V1 − V1 ∩ V2 ) ∪ (V2 − V1 ∩ V2 ). On the other hand, the two sets V1 − V1 ∩ V2 and V2 − V1 ∩ V2 have the same
cardinality and different parities under the parity labeling of (G, σ). Therefore, we can conclude that the switch
from (G, σ) to (G, σ ′ ) above consists of a sequence of parity-switches, which completes the proof.

2.3    The proof of Conjecture 1.2
Proof. Sufficiency has already been proved (see [1], Proposition 9 and Theorem 10).
    Necessity. Take any parity signature σ of G and let S = (G, σ). Let V1 and V2 be the two parity sets of S.
By Proposition 2.2, switching at a vertex of V1 (S) and a vertex of V2 (S) (i.e., a parity-switch) in S results into
another parity signed graph. As Σ− (G) = {σ − (G)}, this switch will not change the number of negative edges
of the whole graph. Thus, S is degree-balanced. By Lemma 2.5, G must be K1,n−1 or Kn when n is even; and
G must be P2 ∨ In−2 or Kn when n is odd. Moreover, it is easy to compute that Σ− (P2 ∨ In−2 ) = {n − 1, n + 1}.
Hence, G can not be P2 ∨ In−2 since the assumption that Σ− (G) = {σ − (G)}. This completes the proof of the
conjecture.


2.4    Upper bounds for the rna number
The following theorem gives a trivial upper bound for the rna number as well as a complete characterization
of graphs whose rna number is very close to this bound.

Theorem 2.6. Let G be a graph on n vertices. Then σ − (G) ≤ ⌈ n2 ⌉⌊ n2 ⌋ and the following statements hold:

  1. σ − (G) = ⌈ n2 ⌉⌊ n2 ⌋ if and only if G = Kn ;
  2. σ − (G) = ⌈ n2 ⌉⌊ n2 ⌋ − 1 if and only if G = Kn − e;
  3. σ − (G) = ⌈ n2 ⌉⌊ n2 ⌋ − 2 if and only if G ∈ {Kn − △, Kn − 2e, Kn − P2 }.

Proof. Let S = (G, σ) be a parity signed graph with minimum |E − (S)|, and let V1 and V2 be the two parity
sets of S. Then σ − (G) ≤ |E − (S)| = |E(V1 , V2 )| ≤ ⌈ n2 ⌉⌊ n2 ⌋.
     For the three statements, let us first prove the sufficiency. For G = Kn , there are precisely ⌈ n2 ⌉⌊ n2 ⌋ edge
between V1 and V2 Hence, σ − (Kn ) = ⌈ n2 ⌉⌊ n2 ⌋. For G = Kn − e, we have |E − (S)| = ⌈ n2 ⌉⌊ n2 ⌋ if e ∈ E(V1 , V2 );
and |E − (S)| = ⌈ n2 ⌉⌊ n2 ⌋ − 1 otherwise. Hence, σ − (Kn − e) = ⌈ n2 ⌉⌊ n2 ⌋ − 1. Similarly, we can prove that
σ − (Kn − 2e) = σ − (Kn − P2 ) = ⌈ n2 ⌉⌊ n2 ⌋ − 2. For G = Kn − △, we have |E(△) ∩ E(V1 , V2 )| ∈ {0, 2}, which
implies that |E − (S)| ∈ {⌈ n2 ⌉⌊ n2 ⌋ − 2, ⌈ n2 ⌉⌊ n2 ⌋}. Hence, σ − (Kn − △) = ⌈ n2 ⌉⌊ n2 ⌋ − 2.
     It remains to prove the necessity. For the first statement, suppose to the contrary that G 6= Kn . Let
xy 6= E(G). Take a choice of V1 and V2 such that x ∈ V1 and y ∈ V2 . So, σ − (G) ≤ |E − (S)| ≤ ⌈ n2 ⌉⌊ n2 ⌋ − 1, a
contradiction. For the second statement, suppose to the contrary that G 6= Kn − e. Since the truth of the first
statement, G 6= Kn . Hence, we may let e1 and e2 be two edges (may have a common end) not in G. Take a
choice of V1 and V2 such that e1 , e2 ∈ E(V1 , V2 ). So, σ − (G) ≤ |E − (S)| ≤ ⌈ n2 ⌉⌊ n2 ⌋ − 2, a contradiction. For the
third statement, suppose to the contrary that G 6= {Kn − 2e, Kn − P2 , Kn − △}. Since the statements 1 and
2, we may let e1 , e2 and e3 be three edges not in G which do not induce a triangle. Then we can take a choice
of V1 and V2 such that e1 , . . . , e3 ∈ E(V1 , V2 ). So, σ − (G) ≤ |E − (S)| ≤ ⌈ n2 ⌉⌊ n2 ⌋ − 3, a contradiction.

2.5    Parity complement
Denote by G the complement of a graph G. The parity complement S p of a parity signed graph S is the parity
signed graph whose underlying graph is the complement of the one of S and whose parity sets are same as the
one of S. This definition is formulated slightly different from but equivalent to the one in [2].
    In this section, we answer the following question on the rna number of the parity complement of a parity
signed graph, proposed in [2].

Question 2.8. For any parity signed graph S, what is the relation between σ − (S) + σ − (S p ) and σ − (S ∪ S p )?

   Since the rna number of a parity signed graph is actually defined for its underlying graph, this question can
be equivalently formulated as: for any graph G, what is the relation between σ − (G) + σ − (G) and σ − (G ∪ G)?
We answer this question by the following theorem.

Theorem 2.9. For any graph G, σ − (G) + σ − (G) ≤ σ − (G ∪ G). The equality holds if and only if G is K1,n−1
with n even or Kn .

Proof. Let n = |V (G)|. Clearly, σ − (G ∪ G) = σ − (Kn ) = ⌊ n2 ⌋⌈ n2 ⌉. Let S be any parity signed graph whose
underlying graph is G. By the definition of parity complement, |E − (S)| + |E − (S p )| = ⌊ n2 ⌋⌈ n2 ⌉. Since the
arbitrariness, it follows that max Σ− (G) + min Σ− (G) = ⌊ n2 ⌋⌈ n2 ⌉. Therefore, σ − (G) + σ − (G) ≤ max Σ− (G) +
min Σ− (G) = σ − (G∪G). Clearly, the equality holds if and only if σ − (G) = max Σ− (G), i.e., Σ− (G) = {σ − (G)}.
By the truth of Conjecture 1.2, this is equivalent to that G is K1,n−1 with n even or Kn .

1     Background
In 2010, Mark Conger [3] proved that a permutation with k descents has an
expected first letter of π(1) = k + 1, independent of n. This paper has the same
premise, but with a different permutation statistic: the number of k-cycles of a
permutation.
    This section, (Section 1) provides an overview of where we’re headed, and
includes an critical example that will hopefully spark the reader’s curiosity and
motivate the remainder of the paper.
    Section 2 establishes some recurrence relations for the number of permuta-
tions in Sn with a given number of k-cycles. It also contains a theorem that
gives an explicit way to compute the expected value of the first letter based on
these counts.
    Section 3 describes an explicit correspondence between k-cycles of permu-
tations in Skn and fixed points of elements of the generalized symmetric group
(Z/kZ) ≀ Sn . Using generating functions and results from the previous section,
this shows that the expected value of π(1) of a permutation with a given num-
ber of k-cycles is intimately connected to the number of derangements of a
generalized symmetric group.
    While Section 3 emphasizes the case of Skn , Section 4 looks at SN where
k ∤ N . Here, the expected value of π(1) is simply (N + 1)/2, which agrees with
the expected value of the first letter of a uniformly chosen N -letter permuta-
tion with no additional restrictions. This fact together with the main theorem
from Section 2 implies the existence of a bijection ϕk : SN −1 × [N ] → SN that
preserves the number of k-cycles whenever k ∤ N . Section 4 constructs such a
bijection explicitly, and proves that it has the desired properties.

3.1     Derangements of the generalized symmetric group
Definition 3.1. The generalized symmetric group S(k, n) is the wreath
product (Z/kZ) ≀ Sn , which in turn is a semidirect product (Z/kZ)n ⋊ Sn .
    A natural way of thinking about the symmetric group Sn is by considering
how the elements act on length-n sequences by permuting the indices. Infor-

tially similar way: each element consists of an ordered pair in (Z/kZ)n ⋊ Sn ,
where (Z/kZ)n gives information about what to add componentwise, and Sn
gives information about how to rearrange afterward.

4       A k-cycle preserving bijection
Motivated by Equation 2.2a, this section describes a family of bijections,

                             φk : Sn−1 × [n] → Sn ,

each of which preserves the number of k-cycles when k ∤ n. Of course, there
is no map that preserves the number of k-cycles when k | n. For example, a
permutation in Sn consisting entirely of k-cycles contains n/k k-cycles, while a
permutation in Sn−1 can contain at most n/k − 1 k-cycles by the pigeonhole
principle.

in canonical cycle notation, incrementing all letters in σ that are greater than
or equal to x ∈ [n], inserting x into the rightmost cycle, and then recursively


                                         14
moving letters into or out of subsequent cycles, whenever a k-cycle is turned
into a (k + 1)-cycle or a (k − 1)-cycle is turned into a k-cycle.

5     Further directions
In the introduction, we mentioned Conger’s paper which analyzed how the num-
ber of descents of a permutation affects the expected value of the first letter of
the permutation. And similarly in the following sections, we looked at how the
number of k-cycles affects the expected value of the first letter of the permu-
tation. This section will principally look at the obvious generalization: given
some permutation statistic stat : Sn → Z, does the map
                     f (n, m) = E[π(i) | π ∈ Sn , stat(π) = m]                 (5.1)
have any interesting structure?
   But notice that the first letter of a permutation is itself a statistic, so we can
play a more general game. Given pairs of statistics (stat1 , stat2 ), does the map
                  g(n, m) = E[stat1 (π) | π ∈ Sn , stat2 (π) = m]              (5.2)
have any interesting structure?

5.1    FindStat database
The result by Conger gives the expected value of π(1) given des(π), and this
paper gave the expected value of π(1) given the number of k-cycles of π. Of
course, it would be interesting to do analogous analysis with other permutations.
In particular, the FindStat permutation statistics database [7] contains over
370 different permutation statistics, and many of these appear to have some
structure with respect to the expected value of the first letter of a permutation.

5.3    An elusive bijection
Let Fk (n, m) be the number of elements of the generalized symmetric group
S(k, n) = (Z/kZ)≀Sn with m fixed points, and recall that Ck (n, m) is the number
of elements of Skn with m k-cycles. Then for each pair of nonnegative integers
(α, β) with α, β ≤ n, then as Lemma 3.7 suggests, there exists a bijection of
sets
                  Ck (n, α) × Fk (n, β) → Ck (n, β) × Fk (n, α).            (5.7)
This bijection has proven to be elusive to construct outside of the special cases
where n = 1 or k = 1. Note that, the map cannot be a group automorphism of



                                       20
Skn × S(k, n), because the identity of this group is in Ck (n, 0) × Fk (n, n), so it
cannot be preserved under this map.
    It would be especially interesting if there’s a way to use the embedding of
(Z/kZ) ≀ Sn into Skn as the centralizer of an element that is the product of n
disjoint k cycles.

                                           Abstract
                                           In this paper, pursuing the same line of ideas in the proof of an old longstanding

                                           the interlacing lemma which indicates a necessary condition for having a real root
                                           for sums of polynomials with (at least) one real root. Then, as an immediate
                                           application of this simple but potentially useful lemma we characterize several class

                                           1   Introduction and Motivation

                                           The property of having only real roots for graphs polynomials of some special
                                           classes of graphs is of special interest for algebraic graph theorists in recent
                                           years. The reason is that having only real roots for a graph polynomial
                                           results in detecting many combinatorial features for the given graph.
                                           One of the most interesting breaking through in mathematical sciences in
                                           recent years was the solution of the well-known Kadsion-Singer conjecture
                                           which was open for more than 50 years. The main idea behind the proof
                                           was introducing an interlacing family of polynomials (see [1] and reference
                                           therein for more details).
                                           We are not really aware of the origin of interlacing idea, but it seems that
                                           one of the early motivations comes from the famous Rolle’s theorem in
                                           the calculus of one-variable functions. An immediate corollary of Rolle’s
                                           theorem simply states that for a real polynomial f (x), roots of f ′ (x) interlace
                                           those of f (x).
                                           It seems that by using similar line of proofs for the Rolle’s theorem, one can
                                           get the following simple but interesting lemma.

2   Basic Definitions and Notations

Throughout this paper, we will assume that our graphs are all finite, simple
and undirected. For the definitions which are not appear here, one may refer
to [3].
A subset of vertices of a graph G that are pairwise adjacent is called a com-
plete subgraph or a clique of G. A clique with k vertices is called a k-clique.
The number of k-cliques will be denoted by ck (G). For a subset S of vertices,

G − v. In a similar way, a subgraph obtained by only removing an edge e
from G is denoted by G − e.
A chord of a cycle is an edge connecting two non-adjacent vertices in the
cycle. A chordal graph is a graph that any cycle of length greater than three
has a chord.
