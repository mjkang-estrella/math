1.1    Notation and preliminaries
Let D = (V (D), A(D)) be a digraph. A vertex u is an in-neighbor of v if (u, v) ∈
A(D) and an out-neighbor of v if (v, u) ∈ A(D). The open out-neighborhood of v is
the set of out-neighbors of v and is denoted by ND+ (v). The closed out-neighborhood
of v is the set ND+ [v] defined by ND+ [v] = ND+ (v) ∪ {v}. In a similar manner one
defines the open in-neighborhood ND− (v) of v and the closed in-neighborhood ND− [v]
of v. The in-degree of v is the number |ND− (v)| and the out-degree of v is |ND+ (v)|.
The subgraph of D induced by a set S ⊆ V (D) is denoted by D[S]. For k ≥ 1
an integer, we let [k] denote the set {1, . . . , k}. If the digraph D is clear from the
context, then we may omit the subscript D from the above notations.
    A vertex x ∈ V (D) dominates a vertex y if y ∈ ND+ (x), and we then also say
that y is dominated by x. If S ⊆ V (D), then y ∈ V (D) is dominated by S if
there exists x ∈ S that dominates y. A set S is a total dominating set (or shortly,
a TD-set) of D if every vertex in D is dominated by S. Note that a digraph D has
a total dominating set if and only if |ND− (v)| ≥ 1 for any v ∈ V (D). The minimum
cardinality of a TD-set of D is the total domination number of D, denoted γt (D).
A total dominating set S of D with |S| = γt (D) will be called a γt -set of D.
    For a set S ⊆ V (D) and a vertex v ∈ S, the open S-private neighborhood of v,
denoted by pn(v, S), is the set of vertices that are in the open out-neighborhood of
v but not in the open out-neighborhood of the set S \ {v}. Equivalently, pn(v, S) =
{w ∈ V (D) : N − (w) ∩ S = {v}}. If S is a γt -set of a digraph D, then pn(v, S) 6= ∅
holds for any v ∈ S.

of G is defined by a mapping f : E(G) → V (G) × V (G), such that if uv ∈ E(G),
then f (uv) ∈ {(u, v), (v, u)}. We denote this orientation of G by Gf , while we refer

                                                                                     1. I NTRODUCTION
                                            Since the time of Young it has been known that partitions index the irreducible repre-
                                         sentations of the symmetric groups. Young and mathematicians of his time also knew that
                                         a partition could be encoded in a convenient way — via what is now known as a Young
                                         diagram — and that flipping this diagram about a natural diagonal amounted to tensoring
                                         the corresponding irreducible representation with the sign character. Hence it was de-
                                         duced that the Young diagrams invariant under this flip corresponded to those irreducible
                                         representations that split upon restriction to the alternating subgroup.

   Some time later, it was discovered by Frame-Robinson-Thrall [6] that the hook lengths of
a Young diagram determine the dimension of the corresponding irreducible representation
(over C). It followed that the study of partitions with hook lengths indivisible by a given
integer t — so-called t-core partitions — was connected to modular representation theory.
   In this paper we study self-conjugate t-core partitions, asymptotically resolving all but
two conjectures posed in the paper of Hanusa and Nath [10] on counting self-conjugate
t-core partitions. In all but two cases the implied constants are effective, so in principle this
reduces many of these conjectures to a finite amount of computation. The ineffective cases
are due to the ineffectivity of a result of Duke–Schulze-Pillot [5] on integers represented by
forms in a given spinor genus, which arises due to the Landau-Siegel phenomenon.

1. Introduction

    Let G = (V , E) denote an undirected graph with vertex set V and edge set E. The graphs in this paper are finite (i.e. |V |
is finite) simple (without loops or multiple edges) graphs. The (open) neighborhood N(v ) of a vertex v is the set of vertices
adjacent to v , i.e. N(v ) = {u | uv ∈ E }. The vertices in N(v ) are the neighbors of v . The closed neighborhood of a vertex v
is the set defined by N [v] := N(v ) ∪ {v}. The degree d(v ) of v is defined as the number of neighbors of v . The vertex v is
isolated if d(v ) = 0, and full if every other vertex is a neighbor of v , i.e. d(v ) = |V | − 1. A graph is isolate-free if there are
no isolated vertices in it. The minimum degree and the maximum degree of G are denoted by δ (G) and ∆(G), respectively.
A vertex u ∈ V dominates v ∈ V if uv ∈ E, or in other words if v ∈ N(u). Then v is dominated by u. A vertex subset
S ⊆ V dominates T ⊆ V if and only if T ⊆ ∪v∈S N(v ). A vertex subset S ⊆ V is a dominating set of G if each vertex in
V \ S is dominated by a vertex in S, or equivalently if V \ S ⊆ ∪v∈S N(v ). A dominating set that does not contain any
other dominating set as a proper subset is a minimal dominating set. The domination number of G, denoted by γ (G), is the
minimum cardinality of a dominating set of G.
    A set S ⊆ V is a total dominating set of G if every vertex in V is dominated by a vertex from S, i.e. ∪v∈S N(v ) = V . Note
that a graph G has a total dominating set if and only if it is isolate-free. A total dominating set is a minimal total dominating
set if it does not contain any total dominating set as a proper subset. The total domination number of G, denoted by γt (G),
is the minimum cardinality of a total dominating set of G. Since total domination is in the main focus of this paper, let
us suppose that every graph is isolate-free in the sequel. For X ⊆ V , let G − X denote the undirected graph that we get
from G by deleting the vertices of X .

    Let G = (V , E) be an undirected graph. An orientation o of G gives rise to a directed graph, denoted by Go , which has
the same vertex set as G and every edge from G is oriented in one of the two possible ways. We call an orientation valid
if the in-degree of every vertex is at least 1, thus the notion of a total dominating set can be interpreted and always
exists. What kind of graphs admit a valid orientation? It is straightforward to see that every connected component of G
must have at least as many edges as vertices because otherwise the non-zero in-degree condition would fail. Thus every
connected component must contain a cycle. However in each connected component, if we fix a spanning tree T and add
another edge e from this component then it will introduce a cycle C in T ∪ {e}. If we orient the edges of C to be a circuit
and the rest of the edges of T ∪ {e} are oriented outwards from the cycle then every vertex of this component will have
at least one in-neighbor. Hence those graphs that admit a valid orientation have a complete characterization, namely in
each of their connected components they must contain at least one cycle. Let us denote the class of these graphs with C .
In the sequel, we will always consider undirected graphs which belong to C .
    There are recent papers [1,3] that are focusing on the orientable domination number of an undirected graph G from class
C . The orientable domination number is denoted by DOM(G) = max{γ (Go ) : o is an orientation of G}. The analogous
concept was introduced earlier for total domination in [4,9]. The (upper) orientable total domination number is defined
to be DOMt (G) = max{γt (Go ) : o is a valid orientation of G}, and the (lower) orientable total domination number is
defined to be domt (G) = min{γt (Go ) : o is a valid orientation of G}. It was also shown in those early papers that
DOMt (G) = |V (G)| = domt (G) if and only if G is a cycle. Recently, the upper and lower orientable total domination
numbers were investigated by Anderson et al. [2], and they characterized those graphs of girth g(G) ≥ 7 for which
DOMt (G) = domt (G) = g(G), and also characterized those graphs for which domt (G) = |V (G)| − 1. They introduced a
family of graphs, denoted by F , that is the collection of those graphs that can be obtained from a cycle Ck , k ≥ 3, and a
path Pℓ , ℓ ≥ 2, by identifying one of the leaves of the path with an arbitrary vertex of the cycle.
