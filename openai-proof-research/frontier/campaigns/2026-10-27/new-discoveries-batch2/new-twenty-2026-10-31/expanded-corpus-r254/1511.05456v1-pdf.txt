                                           Abstract. Zubieta conjectured a formula to enumerate the corners in symmetric tree-like
                                           tableaux. By exploring the relationship among symmetric tree-like tableaux, symmetric alter-
                                           native tableaux, permutation tableaux of type B and signed permutations, we reduce Zubieta’s
                                           conjecture to counting the number of signed descents and ascents in signed permutation, and
                                           thus confirm this conjecture.
                                           AMS Classification 2010: 05A05, 05A19
                                           Keywords: Signed Permutations; Permutation tableaux of type B; Symmetric Tree-like tableaux;
                                           Symmetric Alternative tableaux.


                                           1    Introduction

                                           The main objective of this paper is to prove Zubieta’s conjecture on the enumeration of corners
                                           in symmetric tree-like tableaux. Tree-like tableaux were introduced by Aval, Boussicault and
                                           Nadeau in [1], and they are related to many combinatorial objects such as permutations, per-
                                           mutation tableaux, non-ambiguous trees, alternative tableaux, Dyck tableaux and so on. For
                                           more information, we refer the reader to [2, 3, 1, 11, 12, 13].
                                              Zubieta [14] introduced a kind of minor structure called corner in a symmetric tree-like
                                           tableau and he studied the number of occupied corners in symmetric tree-like tableaux and
                                           obtained the following result.
                                           Theorem 1.1 ([14]). The number of occupied corners in the set of symmetric tree-like tableaux
                                           of size 2n + 1 are 2n n!.

                                              Moreover, Zubieta also considered the number of corners in symmetric tree-like tableaux and
                                           proposed the following conjecture.
                                           Conjecture 1.2. ([14, Conjecture 4.2]) The number of corners in the set of symmetric tree-like
                                           tableaux of size 2n + 1 is 2n × n! × 4n+13
                                                                                  12 .

                                           definition of corner in [14]. To our surprise, on the basis of the bijection established by Corteel

2     Definitions and notations

First of all, to be self-contained in this paper, let us recall some necessary basic notions and
introduce some notations in this section.


2.1   (k, n)-diagrams and Tableaux

For two nonnegative integers n, k with n ≥ k, a (k, n)-diagram D is a left-justified diagram of
boxes in a k × (n − k) rectangle with λi boxes in the i-th row, where λ1 ≥ λ2 ≥ · · · ≥ λk ≥ 0,
cf. See the left of Figure 1 as an example. The length of a (k, n)-diagram D is defined to be n,
i.e., the number of its rows plus the number of its columns.
    Given a (k, n)-diagram D, a shifted (k, n)−diagram denoted by D ∗ , is a (k, n)−diagram
together with the stair-shaped array of boxes added above, where the j-th column(from the left)
has (n − j + 1) additional boxes for j ∈ [n − k]. In terms of the definitions in [9], we call the
unique (k, n)-diagram in a shifted (k, n)-diagram the (k, n)-subdiagram, and the (n − k) topmost
boxes in a shifted (k, n)-diagram diagonals. The length of a shifted (k, n)-diagram is defined
to be the length of its (k, n)-subdiagram, which is n. For example, a (4, 8)-diagram(left) and a
shifted (4, 8)-diagram(middle) are shown in Figure 1.

    Note that a (k, n)-diagram is uniquely determined by its southeast border which is the lattice
path labeled orderly by [n] from the top right corner of the shape to its bottom left corner; it
is the path labeled by the integers 1, 2, · · · , 8 on the left of Figure 1. That is to say, a shifted
(k, n)-diagram is uniquely determined by the southeast border of its (k, n)-subdiagram . Thus,
given a shifted (k, n)-diagram D ∗ , we call an edge a border edge if it lies on the southeast border
of its (k, n)-subdiagram D. A border edge is called a south(resp. west) step if it goes down(resp.
left) along the southeast border.
    Let D ∗ be a shifted (k, n)-diagram, rows and columns of D ∗ will be labeled by integers in
the following manner: Since the border edges of D ∗ are labeled by [n], if a south step is labeled
by i, then the corresponding row is labeled with i and if a west step is labeled by j, then the
corresponding column is labeled with j. And then labeling the added rows as follows. If the
diagonal cell of an added row is in column i, then the row is labeled by −i. We will say row i
or column j when we actually refer to the row with the label i or the column with the label j.
Then the cell locating at the intersection of row i and column j can be denoted by (i, j). Note
that we will actually write all the column(resp. row) labels on the top(resp. left side) of the
corresponding columns(resp. rows) for an improved readability, as shown in the right of Figure
1.

   In what follows, we shall introduce three kinds of tableaux, which are main combinatorial
objects of our work. They are symmetric tree-like tableaux, symmetric alternative tableaux and
permutation tableaux of type B.
Definition 2.3. A symmetric tree-like tableau is a filling of a (k, n)−diagram(without empty
rows or empty columns in this case) with points inside some cells, such that the resulting diagram
satisfies the following three rules,

 (1) the top left cell of the diagram contains a point, called the root point;

 (2) for every non-root pointed cell c, there exists either a pointed cell above c in the same
     column, or a pointed cell to its left in the same column, but not both;

 (3) every column and every row possess at least one pointed cell;

 (4) the tableaux are invariant with respect to the reflection across the main diagonal of its
     underlying diagram.

     Let D be a (k, n)-diagram, by the main diagonal of D we mean the line going south east
from the top left corner of D. Symmetric tree-like tableau was first introduced by Aval et al
[1]. The size of a symmetric tree-like tableau is defined to be its number of points. It is not
difficult to see that the length of a tree-like tableau equals to its size plus one [1]. The size of

2.2    Signed Permutations

A signed permutation(also called permutation of type B) σ of length n is a bijection on [±n]
satisfying σ(i) = σi and σ(−i) = −σ(i) for i ∈ [n]. The group of type B permutations is denoted
by SB n.
   Following Corteel and Nadeau’s terminologies in [6], we need to define some statistics on
permutations of type B.
Definition 2.7. Let σ = (σ1 , σ2 , · · · , σn ) ∈ SB
                                                   n with σn+1 = n. For i ∈ [n],

    • σi is a signed descent if σi < 0 or σi > |σi+1 | , otherwise we call σi is a signed ascent if
      0 < σi < |σi+1 |.

    • A set consisting of all the signed descents in a permutation π is called a descent set, denoted
      by D(π).

3.1.1    A Bijection between Symmetric Tree-like Tableaux and Symmetric Alterna-
         tive Tableaux

We first exhibit a bijection α between the set of tree-like tableaux of size n and the set of
alternative tableaux of size n − 1, which was given by Aval et al [1]. Here, we denote by tree-like
tableaux the tableaux that satisfy the first three items in definition 2.3 and by Tn the set of such
tableaux of size n. Moreover, denote by alternative tableaux the tableaux that satisfy the first
item in definition 2.4 and by AT n the set of such tableaux of size n.

Theorem 3.1 ([1]). There is a bijection α : Tn → AT n−1 such that for any T ∈ Tn , the
underlying diagram of α(T ) is obtained from D by deleting the first row and column of D, where
D is the underlying diagram of T .

   First of all, we give a description of the bijection α and its inverse α−1 in detail without
proof. Given a tree-like tableau T of size n, replace every non-root point p with a left arrow
“←” if there is no point left of p and an up arrow “↑” if there is no point above of p. Then
simply deleting the topmost row and the leftmost column. One can verify that the obtained
tableau, denoted by α(T ), is alternative tableau of length n − 1.
     As to α−1 , given an alternative tableau T ′ of length n − 1 with underlying diagram D ′ .
Suppose D ′ has k rows and n − 1 − k columns. We first add k cells at the left of the leftmost
column of D ′ and n−i−k cells above the topmost row of D ′ . Then adding one cell at the top left
corner of D ′ . Now we denote the resulting diagram by D. Note that D ′ is a (k, n − 1)-diagram
and D is a (k + 1, n + 1)-diagram. Next, for any cell c = (i, j) in D ′ , if there is an up arrow ” ↑ ”
in c, add a point in the cell (i + 1, 1) and (i + 1, j + 1) in D. If there is a left arrow ” ← ” in c,
add a point in the cell (1, j + 1) and (i + 1, j + 1) in D. Lastly, add a point in (1, 1) and (1, j) or
(i, 1) in D if column j is an empty column or row i is an empty row in D ′ , the resulting tableau
is a tree-like tableau of size n, denoted by T = α−1 (T ′ ).
    As a matter of fact, the bijection α can extend to symmetric tree-like tableaux and symmetric
alternative tableaux.
