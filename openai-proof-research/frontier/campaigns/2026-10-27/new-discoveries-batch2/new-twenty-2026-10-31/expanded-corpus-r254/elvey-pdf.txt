                                           Permutations sortable by deques and two stacks in parallel
                                                         share the same growth rate.




arXiv:1912.00056v1 [math.CO] 29 Nov 2019
                                                                                   Andrew Elvey Price1

                                                                        LaBRI, Université de Bordeaux, France.
                                                                                          Abstract
                                                         Recently Albert and Bousquet-Mélou obtained the solution to the long-standing
                                                     problem of the enumeration of permutations sortable by two stacks in parallel (2sip).
                                                     Their solution was expressed in terms of functional equations. E.P. and Guttmann
                                                     then showed that the equally long-standing problem of the number of permutations
                                                     sortable by a double ended queue (deque) can be simply related to the solution of
                                                     the same functional equations. They then conjectured that the radius of convergence
                                                     of both generating functions is the same, and reduced this conjecture to a series of
                                                     conjectures of Albert and Bousquet-Mélou regarding a generating function for quarter-
                                                     plane loops. In this note we prove that the two growth rates are equal, using a
                                                     combinatorial argument on certain lattice paths which are in bijection with the two
                                                     classes. As a corollary we prove that the generating function P (t) for permutations
                                                     sortable by two stacks in parallel satisfies an inequality which was conjectured by
                                                     Albert and Bousquet-Mélou.



                                           1        Introduction
                                           In his seminal book the art of computer programming [8] Knuth considered a number
                                           of classical data structures from the perspective of the permutations which they could
                                           produce. Equivalently one can ask which permutations can be sorted by these machines
                                           as these will be exactly the inverses of the producible permutations. The first non-trivial
                                           machine that Knuth considered in this context is a single stack. In this case, starting with
                                           the identity permutation 12 · · · n in the input, two operations are permitted as shown in
                                           Figure 1:

                                                   • The input operation I, which moves the next element from the input onto the top
                                                     of the stack.

                                                   • The output operation O, which moves the top element of the stack to the output.

Further to this, Knuth characterised the set of achievable permutations, showing that they
are exactly the permutations which are now called 312-avoiding permutations. This is one
of the first results in the field of pattern avoiding permutations. Knuth then asked the
same question for each of the following three more complicated data structures, shown in
Figures 2, 3 and 4:

   • two stacks in parallel (2sip).

   • A double ended queue (deque).

   • two stacks in series (2sis).

For each of these machines, the set of sortable permutations has been shown to be a
permutation class with infinite basis [9, 11]. In other words, the set of sortable permuta-
tions cannot be defined as those permutations which avoid some finite set of permutation
patterns. For all three problems a polynomial time algorithm is known for determining
whether a permutation is sortable [6, 10].
    We will focus on the problem of enumerating permutations sortable by one of these
machines. Albert, Atkinson and Linton considered all three enumeration problems in [1],
where they derived upper and lower bounds on the growth rates µp , µs and µd for 2sip-
sortable, 2sis-sortable and deque-sortable permutations. They were also the first to suggest
that the growth rates µd and µp may be equal based on the similarities in the bounds that

generating function P (t) of these permutations. From an asymptotic perspective, these
equations are surprisingly impenetrable, in that we are still unable to determine either
the growth rate or the sub-exponential behaviour of the sequence exactly. In 2017, E.P.
and Guttmann solved the problem for permutations sortable by a deque by demonstrating
the following simple algebraic relationship between their generating function D(t) and the

They then reduced the problem of showing that µd = µp to three conjectures of Albert
and Bousquet-Mélou regarding quarter-plane walks. Moreover, they used the equations

conjecture that these growth rates are equal. This is the conjecture that we prove in this
article:
Theorem 2.11. The growth rate µd of deque-sortable permutations is equal to the growth
rate µp of 2sip-sortable permutations.
    We were not able to prove the three conjectures of Albert and Bousquet-Mélou about
quarter-plane walks. Instead our proof of this theorem uses descriptions of D(t) and P (t)
as generating functions for certain explicit classes of walks given in [2] and [4].
    The outline of this paper is as follows: In Section 2 we will prove Theorem 2.11. In
Section 3 we will use this result in conjunction with (1) to prove the inequality

for t ∈ [0, 1/µp ], which was conjectured in [2]. Finally, in the Section 4 we will describe a
new rigorous lower bound on the shared growth rate µd = µp . To the author’s knowledge,
this is now also the strongest known lower bound for the growth rate of permutations
sortable by two stacks in series.


2    The growth rates of deque-sortable and 2sip-sortable per-
     mutations
In this section we prove Theorem 2.11. First we will describe a class of lattice walks
counted by each of the two generating functions P (t) and D(t). These were essentially
described in [2] and [4], respectively, albeit in the language of operation sequences rather
than walks. The correspondence can be seen by replacing each operation I1 , I2 , O1 , O2
with the step N, E, S, W , respectively. In each case the walks start from the origin and
have steps from the set {N, E, S, W }. In the case of P (t), the walks are restricted to the
quarter-plane {(x, y)|x, y ≥ 0} while for D(t), the walks are only restricted to the diagonal
half-plane {(x, y)|x + y ≥ 0}. Before defining the two types of walks, we need to define
three properties of these walks.
Definition 2.1. A walk is called eager if it contains no ES or N W corners.
Definition 2.2. A QP-subloop of a walk is a subsection of the walk which starts and
ends at the same point (p, q) and only visits points (x, y) satisfying x ≥ p and y ≥ q. In
other words, a QP-subloop is a translate of a quarter-plane excursion. A walk w is called
standard if every QP-subloop of w begins with an N step.
Definition 2.3. A walk is vertical-happy if each step from the lines x+y = 0 and x+y = 1
is either N or S.
    In the language of [2] and [4] an eager walk corresponds to an operation sequence
that outputs eagerly, a standard walk corresponds to a standard operation sequence, a
QP-subloop corresponds to a tsip-subword of the corresponding operation sequence and a
verticle-happy walk corresponds to a top-happy operation sequence. Note that the terms
top-happy and tsip-subword were only used in [4] while the terms standard and outputs
eagerly were introduced in [2] and used in both articles.
    Now we are ready to define the walks which are counted by P (t) and D(t), which we
will call 2sip-walks and deque-walks respectively.

                                              4
Definition 2.4. A 2sip-walk is a walk confined to the quarter-plane, which starts and
ends at the point (0, 0) and which is standard and eager.

Definition 2.5. A deque-walk is a walk confined to the diagonal half-plane {(x, y)|x + y ≥
0}, which starts at the point (0, 0), ends at some point (x, −x) and which is standard,
eager and vertical happy.

    The following two propositions state the desired results that 2sip-walks correspond
bijectively with 2sip-sortable permutations and deque-walks correspond bijectively with
deque-sortable permutations. It follows immediately from these propositions that the
series P (t) counts 2sip-walks by half-length while the series D(t) counts deque-walks by
half-length. These are essentially [2, Prop. 6] and [4, Prop. 2.5] respectively, so we omit
the proofs here.

Proposition 2.6. For each integer n ≥ 0, each 2sip-walk of length 2n corresponds to a
unique 2sip-sortable permutation of length n. In particular, the number of these walks is
equal to pn , the number of 2sip-sortable permutations of length n.

Proposition 2.7. For each integer n ≥ 0, each deque-walk of length 2n corresponds to a
unique deque-sortable permutation of length n. In particular, the number of these walks is
equal to dn , the number of deque-sortable permutations of length n.

    It will be convenient to define a third type of walk, which simultaneously generalises
2sip-walks and deque-walks:

Definition 2.8. A big-walk is a walk confined to the diagonal half-plane {(x, y)|x+y ≥ 0},
which starts at the point (0, 0), ends at some point (x, −x) and which is standard and
eager. Let bn be the number of these walks of length 2n and let

Definition 2.9. A left-walk is a big-walk that ends at some point (x, −x) satisfying x ≤ 0.
Conversely, a right-walk is a big-walk that ends at some point (x, −x) satisfying x ≥ 0.

    The following lemma states that there is a bijection between left-walks and right-walks
of the same length. This justifies our introduction of big-walks, as the analogous symmetry
does not hold for deque-walks.

Lemma 2.10. For each n, the number of left-walks of length 2n is equal to the number of
right-walks of length 2n.

Proof. We will describe a procedure which bijectively converts left-walks wl into a right-
walks wr . Starting with a left-walk wl , let wl0 be the reflection of wl in the line x = y. Now
each 2sip-loop in wl0 begins with E. For each such 2sip-loop which is not contained in any
other 2sip-loop, replace each N , E, S and W step with E, N , W and S respectively. Call
the resulting path wr . Then each 2sip-loop in wr will start with N , so wr is a big path.
Moreover, the endpoint of wr will be the same as that of wl0 , so wr is a right-walk. We can
then apply the exact same transformation to wr to reproduce wl , so this transformation
is bijective. Hence the number of left-walks is equal to the number of right-walks.

    Let b̂n be the number of left-walks of length 2n, which, as we have just seen, is also the
number of right-walks of length 2n. Since each big-walk is either a right-walk or left-walk
(or both), we have the inequality 2b̂n ≥ bn . In particular this means that the growth rate

corners, so it is eager. Finally we will show that the path is standard. Suppose for the
sake of contradiction that the path is not standard, so it contains a QP-subloop ` starting
with E. Without loss of generality, ` does not return to its starting point until it ends,
as otherwise it would contain a shorter QP-subloop, starting with the same step E. The
QP-subloop ` cannot start before the first big-path, as the only E steps in stage (1) are at
height y = 2m + 1, but the path goes to the point (2m, 2m) before it could return to the
starting point. Since there are no E steps in stage (3), the QP-subloop ` must start in one
of the big-walks, hence it must start and end at some point (x` , y` ) satisfying x` +y` ≥ 4m.

of `. Since ` is a QP-subloop, we have x` ≤ (2m + x) and y` ≤ (2m − x). Moreover, these
cannot both be equalities as we assumed that ` does not contain its starting point except
at its ends. Hence x` + y` < (2m + x) + (2m − x) = 4m. This is a contradiction, so in fact
the entire path is standard. Hence the path is a 2sip-walk.

4    Bounds on the growth rate
In this section we derive a new lower bound of 8.21927 on the growth rate µ of permu-
tations sortable by two stacks in parallel and permutations sortable by a deque. This
improves on the bounds shown by Albert, Atkinson and Linton [1] of 7.535 for 2sip-
sortable permutations and 7.890 for deque-sortable permutations. Indeed, this even im-
proves on their lower bound 8.156 for the growth rate of permutations sortable by two
stacks in series, since any permutation which can be sorted by two stacks in parallel
can be sorted by two stacks in series. To derive our new bounds, we use the fact that
the class of deque-sortable permutations is sum-closed, that is, if τ = τ1 τ2 · · · τm and
π = π1 π2 · · · πn are deque-sortable permutations (written in series form), then so is their
direct sum τ ⊕ π = τ1 τ2 · · · τm (π1 + m)(π2 + m) · · · (πn + m). Equivalently, concatenating
two deque-walks yields another deque-walk. It follows that we can write
