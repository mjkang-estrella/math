                                                                                        1. I NTRODUCTION
                                             When studying walks on Z2 , it is natural to restrict oneself to walks which project vertically on a
                                          fixed vertical path P1 and horizontally on a fixed horizontal path P2 , where P1 (resp. P2 ) comprises
                                          N and S steps (resp. E and W steps) [1]. Such paths      correspond with shuffles, or interleavings, of
                                                                                            j+k
                                          P1 and P2 . If |P1 | = j and |P2 | = k, there are k such shuffles.
                                             Given a path, several natural statistics arise. In this paper, we focus on variants of L-count,
                                          which counts the number of NW and ES corners. Equivalently, L-count counts occurrences of ES
                                          and NW in a shuffle of P1 and P2 . Albert and Bousquet-Melou introduced L-count in order to study
                                          the asymptotic number of permutations sortable by two stacks in parallel [1]. They also proved
                                          that L-count has the same distribution over over shuffles as do several other statistics (Proposition
                                          14 of [1]).
                                             Albert and Bousquet-Melou studied several generating functions involving L-count of planar
                                          walks in depth, but very little is known in the case where the path’s horizontal and vertical projec-
                                          tions are fixed [1]. Albert and Bousquet-Melou conjecture the following.
                                          Conjecture 1.1 (Albert, Bousquet-Melou [1]). Let w (resp. v) be a path beginning and ending at
                                          the origin of half-length i (resp. j) on the alphabet {E,W } (resp. {N, S}). Then the polynomial
                                          that counts walks    of the shuffle class of vw according to the number of NW and ES corners takes
                                                      i+ j
                                          the value i at −1.
                                             Albert and Bosquet-Melou note that, among other things, proving the conjecture would elimi-
                                          nate the need for the somewhat lengthy proof of their Proposition 15 [1].
                                             We prove Conjecture 1.1 and extend it to the case where w and v are arbitrary words on the
                                          alphabets {E,W } and {N, S} respectively. This corresponds with considering arbitrary walks on
                                          the plane, rather than only planar loops.
                                             In order to study L-count modulo 2, it is sufficient to study what we call signed L-count, which
                                          is the number of NW corners minus the number of ES corners. While signed L-count and L-count
                                          are guaranteed to share parity, it turns out that signed L-count exhibits nice behavior.

   In Section 4, we pose conjectures reducing the study of (x + 1)-positivity of L-count to the
study of (x + 1)-positivity of absolute value of signed L-count. With this as motivation, in Section
3, we prove a stronger version of Conjecture 1.3 for signed L-count rather than for L-count. In
particular, we prove that the polynomial counting loops according to the absolute value of their
signed L-count is (x + 1)-positive, even when we restrict ourselves to loops with fixed horizontal
and vertical projections.
   One approach to proving a polynomial is (x + 1)-positive is to evaluate the polynomial at (x − 1)
and then to prove that the coefficients of x are non-negative. For absolute value of signed L-
count, however, this approach does not easily yield results. Instead, we introduce (x + 1)-positive
building-block polynomials out of which we construct the polynomial counting shuffles by abso-
lute value of signed L-count.

                               2. E NUMERATING S IGNED L- COUNT
  In this section, we derive a formula for the number of shuffles with a given signed L-count. To
accomplish this, we introduce a subtly related statistic called N-count. We begin by introducing
some conventions.
  For the rest of this section, let a, b, c, d be non-negative integers satisfying a + c > 0 and b + d >
0. Let P1 (resp. P2 ) be a word comprising a ones (resp. c twos) and b negative ones (resp. d
negative twos). In particular, 1, 2, −1, −2 correspond with N, E, S,W respectively, but are more
convenient to discuss since they already have pre-defined parity and sign.
Definition 2.1. A shuffle of P1 and P2 is an intertwining of the letters in P1 and P2 . In addition, a
shuffle has the letter − inf in its zero-th position.
Remark 2.2. The convention of − inf preceding the first letter is non-standard. But it will facilitate
the definition of a statistic called N-count.
  We will use S to denote the set of shuffles of P1 and P2 .
Example 2.3. If P1 = 1, 1 and P2 = 2, −2, then S contains:

Definition 2.4. For s ∈ S, we use #(A, B)s to denote the number of occurrences in s of a value in
set A followed immediately by a value in set B. If A or B contains only a single element, we omit
set brackets around that element. For example, A = {1} is written as A = 1.
Definition 2.5. We use the following short-hands for certain sets: Odd = {1, −1}, Even = {2, −2},
Pos = {1, 2}, Neg = {−1, −2, − inf}.
Example 2.6. The value #(Neg, 2)s counts instances of a negative value followed by a 2. For
example, if s = − inf, 2, −1, −2, 1, −2, 2, 2, then #(Neg, 2)s = 2 and the instances are over-lined.
Definition 2.7. The signed L-count of s ∈ S is #(2, −1)s − #(1, −2)s .

Definition 2.11. A run in s ∈ S is a (non-empty) maximal contiguous subsequence of positive

Proposition 2.14. Suppose the final letter of P2 is −2. Then for all s ∈ S the shifted N-count of
f (s) is the same as the signed L-count of s.
Proof. Since the final letter of P2 is −2, if s ends with a run, then that run must consist only of
1’s. Thus every run in s containing at least one 2 is preceded and proceeded by a negative letter.
Consequently, #(Neg, 2) f (s) = #(2, Neg)s , since flipping a shuffle reverses the order of every run.
Additionally, it is easy to see that #(Pos, −2) f (s) = #(Pos, −2)s .

   In this section, we state conjectures relating L-count and signed L-count. In addition, we provide
directions for future work. Our main conjecture, Conjecture 4.2, reduces a conjecture of [1] to
Theorem 3.11.
   We call a path vertical (resp. horizontal) if it comprises N and S (resp. E and W ) steps. A path
is quarter-planar if it resides in the quarter plane {(x, y)|x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0}. A quarter-planar vertical or
horizontal path is referred to as Dyck.
   Fix v to be a vertical Dyck path with a N’s and b S’s. Let M be the set of quarter-planar paths
of length n with c E’s, d W ’s, and vertical projection v. Let G1 be the generating function for
L-count over M and G2 be the generating function for signed L-count over M. We will state our
conjectures in terms of G1 and G2 . Additionally, computations indicate that all of our conjectures
are still true when we modify M to consider planar paths rather than quarter-planar paths and v to
be any vertical path rather than necessarily a Dyck one1.
   We begin by re-stating a conjecture of [1]. Conjecture 4.1 implies several weaker conjectures
of [1], including their Conjecture 10 on which their asymptotic analysis of two-stack sortable
permutations relies.
Conjecture 4.1 (Albert, Bousquet-Melou [1]). If a = b and c = d, then G1 is (x + 1)-positive.
   Our main conjecture, Conjecture 4.2, states a surprising equivalence between (x + 1)-positivity
of G1 and G2 (tested for a, b, c, d ≤ 5). In particular, given Conjecture 4.2, our analysis of (x + 1)-
positivity of signed L-count in Theorem 3.11 implies Conjecture 4.1.
Conjecture 4.2. The polynomial G1 is (x + 1)-positive if and only if G2 is (x + 1)-positive.
   Since L-count and signed L-count are of equal parity, the constant coefficients of G1 and G2 as
polynomials on (x + 1) are equal. Computations show, however, that (x + 1)-positivity of G1 and
G2 does not depend only on this coefficient.
   By Theorem 2.16, G2 depends only on a, b, c, d. Thus Conjecture 4.2 implies the following.
Conjecture 4.3. The (x + 1)-positivity of G1 depends only on a, b, c, d.
   This conjecture is particularly surprising given that G1 itself does not depend only on a, b, c, d.
Can one characterize for which a, b, c, d G1 and G2 are (x + 1)-positive? Proposition 3.12 shows
that (x + 1)-positivity of G2 is unchanged by swapping b and c or a and d.

   Our proof of Theorem 3.11 suggests several additional questions. In the proof of Theorem 3.11,
we discuss F(m, m, n, n) in terms of the absolute even count of binary words. Assuming the final
letter of P2 is −2, however, then the proof of Proposition 2.15 provides a straightforward bijection
between binary words in Wnm with absolute even count l and shuffles of P1 and P2 with shifted
N-count l. Furthermore, if we apply Proposition 2.14, then we obtain a bijection between binary
words in Wnm with absolute even count l and shuffles of P1 and P2 with signed L-count l. This leads
us to pose several open problems:
     (1) When i-toggling a binary word changes its even count by one, it also changes the signed
         L-count of the corresponding shuffle by one. Computer computations indicate that, in fact,
         when i-toggling changes signed L-count by one, it often also only changes L-count by one.
         If one could characterize when this is the case, then toggling could be a useful tool for
         proving the Conjectures 4.1 and 4.2.
     (2) Can the toggling argument in the proof of 3.11 be extended to F(m, n, m, n) without using
         the scaling trick from the proof of Proposition 3.12? In particular, such an extension might
         yield a combinatorial interpretation for the super Catalan number. Indeed, the shuffles in
         toggle-equivalence classes of size one are exactly those counted by the constant term of
         F(a, b, a, b), of which there are a super Catalan number by Corollary 2.17.
     (3) Suppose P1 is of the form
                                 1, 1, 1, · · · , 1, −1, . . . , −1, −1, −1,
       and P2 is of the form
                                 2, 2, 2, . . . , 2, −2, . . . , −2, −2, −2,
       each with the same number of positive letters as negative letters. Then any shuffle of P1
       and P2 cannot contain both (1, −2) and (2, −1) pairs. Thus the absolute value of the signed
       L-count is equal to the L-count of such shuffles. Consequently, Theorem 3.11 proves the
       (x + 1)-positivity of the generating function counting these shuffles by their L-count.
          However, I have been unable to find a simple combinatorial description for the action of
       i-toggling on these shuffles (i.e., the result of i-toggling the corresponding binary word).
