## 1. Statement and conventions

For formal power series over the integers put

    C(x) = (1−x)^(−1) ∏_{i≥0}(1−x^{2^i})^(−1),
    c_m(n) = [x^n] C(x)^m,       Δ_m(n) = c_m(4n) − c_m(n).

Throughout the theorem m and n are positive integers. Part 1 has 2m colours and every part 2^i, i ≥ 1, has m colours: there are two factors of (1−x)^(−1) in C. This convention is essential. It is not the product with m colours at every binary part size. Write ν₂(a) for the ordinary 2-adic valuation of a nonzero integer; for coefficient estimates only, extend it by ν₂(0)=∞. We will prove Δ_m(n)>0 before using valuations of the differences.

Ulas and Żmija [1, Section 6] report computations for m ∈ {2,3,…,10} and n ≤ 10^5. The m=1 case is already classical: c₁(n)=b(2n), where b is the ordinary binary partition function. They attribute the relevant congruences to Churchhouse [2] and their proofs to Rødseth [3] and Gupta [4]. The requested summary m ≤ 10 includes this separately known case; the reported new numerical range begins at 2.

Here is Conjecture 6.1 verbatim in wording, with the mathematical typography normalized from the cached source:

“For k ∈ ℕ_+ and each n ∈ ℕ_+, we have:

    ν₂(c_{2k}(4n) − c_{2k}(n)) = ν₂(n) + 2ν₂(k) + 3.

Moreover, for k ∈ ℕ and n ∈ ℕ_+ the following inequalities hold

    ν₂(c_{4k+1}(4n) − c_{4k+1}(n)) ≥ ν₂(n) + 3,
    ν₂(c_{4k+3}(4n) − c_{4k+3}(n)) ≥ ν₂(n) + 6.

In each case the equality holds for infinitely many n ∈ ℕ.”

The final sentence admits two scopes. P1 requires infinitely many equality indices for each eligible k in each case. P2 keeps all universal formulas and bounds but asks for some eligible k in each sharpness clause. We treat both explicitly. Including 0 in the last infinite sets changes at most one point and cannot affect infinitude. No value of ν₂(0) is needed in the ordinary statement.

## 2. Full theorem and consequences

Theorem. For every n ≥ 1 all the following statements hold.

• For each k ≥ 1, Δ_{2k}(n)>0 and

    ν₂(Δ_{2k}(n)) = ν₂(n) + 2ν₂(k) + 3.

• For each m ≡ 1 (mod 4), Δ_m(n)>0 and ν₂(Δ_m(n)) ≥ ν₂(n)+3. Equality holds at every odd n.

• For each m ≡ 3 (mod 4), Δ_m(n)>0 and ν₂(Δ_m(n)) ≥ ν₂(n)+6. If m ≡ 3 or 7 (mod 16), equality holds at every odd n. If m ≡ 11 (mod 16), equality holds at every n ≡ 2 (mod 4).

• If m ≡ 15 (mod 16), then ν₂(Δ_m(n)) ≥ ν₂(n)+7 for every positive n. Consequently equality in the preceding +6 bound never holds in this family.

The displayed equality families are sufficient infinite sets, not descriptions by “if and only if”. For example Δ₃(2)=4224=2^7·33 gives equality at an even index. The never-equality assertion for the fifteen family is universal.

Corollary. P1 is false, and P2 is true. Indeed take k=3 in the 4k+3 clause of P1; then m=15 and its equality set is empty. For P2 choose even k=1 and odd k=0 in both odd clauses. All positive indices in the even case and all positive odd indices in the latter cases give infinite sets. A single exceptional value such as Δ₁₅(1) is not by itself a refutation of infinitude: the universal +7 theorem supplies the refutation.

## 3. The even-section transfer and integrality

Set y=x/(1−x), w=(1+x)/(1−x)=1+2y. Let E be the even-section operator: E(Σa_n x^n)=Σa_{2n}x^n. Cancellation of the two products proves

    C(x)=w(x)C(x²),       E(C^m F)=C^m T(F),
    T(F)=E(w^m F).

The second identity follows coefficientwise from E(g(x)h(x²))=h(x)E(g(x)). All these equalities are formal; no analytic convergence is involved. For a fixed coefficient only finitely many product factors matter.

Use t_N and u_N for Chebyshev polynomials of first and second kind, defined by t₀=1, t₁=z and u₀=1, u₁=2z, with recurrence P_{N+1}=2zP_N−P_{N−1}; set u_{−1}=0. The substitution x↦−x sends w to w^(−1), so

    E(P(w))(x²) = (P(w)+P(w^(−1)))/2,
    E(w^N)=t_N(w).

To distinguish variables let s=(w+w^(−1))/2 and Y=(s−1)/2. Then y²=wY. For e ≥ 0 the old-variable identities y^{2e}=w^eY^e and y^{2e+1}=w^eY^e(w−1)/2 give

    T(y^{2e}) = y^e t_{m+e}(1+2y),
    T(y^{2e+1}) = y^{e+1} V_{m+e}(1+2y),
    V_N=u_N+u_{N−1}.

For the odd rule use t_{N+1}(z)−t_N(z)=(z−1)V_N(z), verified from the recurrence and its initial cases. Thus T maps ℤ[y] into ℤ[y], despite the division by 2 in the symmetric expression. This supplies the integrality missing from the earliest write-up.

Put A_j=T^j(1) and d_j=A_{j+2}−A_j. Iteration of the transfer identity yields

    E^j(Σ Δ_m(n)x^n)=C^m d_j,
    Δ_m(2^j o)=[x^o]C^m d_j.                         (1)

Evaluation at y=0 gives T(F)(0)=F(0). Evaluation at y=−1 (w=−1) gives T(F)(−1)=(−1)^m F(−1); this follows from the two monomial rules. Hence d_j vanishes at both 0 and −1. Since y(1+y) is monic, there is an integral polynomial r_j with

    d_j=y(1+y)r_j.

Finally C≡C(x²) (mod 2), because w≡1. Strong induction on positive coefficient indices, halving even indices and annihilating odd indices, proves C≡1 (mod 2). Consequently C^m≡1 (mod 2).


## 4. Lemma R: the reduced operator

Lemma R. There is an integral linear operator L on ℤ[y] such that T(y(1+y)R)=y(1+y)L(R). Its monomial rules are

    L(y^{2e}) = 2y^e u_{m+e}(1+2y)
              = 2y^e Σ_i 4^i binom(m+e+i+1,2i+1)y^i,
    L(y^{2e+1}) = y^e(u_{m+e+1}−u_{m+e})(1+2y)
              = y^e Σ_i 4^i binom(m+e+i+1,2i)y^i.    (2)

All sums terminate. In particular r_{j+1}=L(r_j) and

    [y^0]L(R)=2(m+1)R₀+R₁.                          (3)

Proof. In the old variable, y(1+y)=w(w−w^(−1))/4. Write G=w^{m+1}R. Taking the symmetric part gives

    E(w^m y(1+y)R)(x²)
       =(w−w^(−1))(G(w)−G(w^(−1)))/8
       =(s²−1) Ψ(G)/2,
    Ψ(G)=(G(w)−G(w^(−1)))/(w−w^(−1)).

For w^N the quotient Ψ equals u_{N−1}(s), as follows from the same second-order recurrence and the initial values. It is linear over symmetric Laurent functions. Substitute y^{2e}=w^eY^e and y^{2e+1}=w^eY^e(w−1)/2. Since s²−1=4Y(1+Y), division by Y(1+Y) gives exactly the two rules in (2). The coefficient expansions

    u_N(1+2y)=Σ_i 4^i binom(N+i+1,2i+1)y^i,
    (u_{N+1}−u_N)(1+2y)=Σ_i 4^i binom(N+i+1,2i)y^i

follow from Pascal's identity and the initial values. They prove integrality directly and yield (3). This completes the proof.

## 5. Lemma W: a weight adapted to halving

Let ℓ(0)=0 and ℓ(E)=⌊log₂ E⌋+1 for E>0. For R=ΣR_Ey^E define ω(R)=min_E(ν₂(R_E)−ℓ(E)), ignoring zero coefficients; ω(0)=∞. Equivalently ω(R)≥v means 2^{v+ℓ(E)} divides R_E whenever the exponent is nonnegative. We only use nonnegative v below.

Lemma W. If ω(R)≥v, then

    L(R)=Σ_e R_{2e+1}y^e+2(m+1)R₀+H,
    ω(H)≥v+2,        ω(L(R))≥v+1.                  (4)

The next page gives the per-monomial proof, including the zero-index cases needed for the odd initialization. The exact formula (3) shows H₀=0; treating it merely as highly divisible is harmless.


## 6. Weight estimates, readout, and nonvanishing

Proof of Lemma W. In (2) a contribution of R_{2e} to degree e+i has valuation at least v+ℓ(2e)+1+2i. If e≥1, its weight is at least v+2: for i=0 use ℓ(2e)=ℓ(e)+1, and for i≥1 use ℓ(e+i)≤ℓ(e)+ℓ(i) and 2i−ℓ(i)≥1. If e=0 and i≥1 its weight is at least v+1+2i−ℓ(i)≥v+2. The remaining even term, e=i=0, is 2(m+1)R₀.

For an odd input degree 2e+1 and i≥1 the weight is at least v+ℓ(2e+1)+2i−ℓ(e+i)≥v+2. For e≥1 this uses ℓ(2e+1)=ℓ(e)+1 and subadditivity; for e=0 it uses 1+2i−ℓ(i)≥2. The i=0 term is R_{2e+1}y^e. It gains at least one unit of weight, also when e=0. The remaining even constant gains at least one. This proves (4).

The same proof gives the more precise input-by-input gains: odd positive degrees gain at least 1, positive even degrees gain at least 2, and degree 0 gains at least min(1+ν₂(m+1),2). In particular the constant gain is 2 when m is odd. These per-monomial estimates, rather than only the uniform bound in (4), will be used for m≡3 (mod 4).

Readout lemma. If every coefficient of r_j is divisible by 2^a, (1) implies 2^a divides Δ_m(2^j o). If moreover ν₂(r_{j,0})=a and every positive-degree coefficient is divisible by 2^{a+1}, then for odd o

    Δ_m(2^j o) ≡ r_{j,0}[x^o]y(1+y)
                = r_{j,0}o              (mod 2^{a+1}).

Indeed C^m≡1 (mod 2), and y(1+y)=x/(1−x)² has coefficient o at x^o. Thus the exact valuation is a.

Strict increase. All coefficients of C are positive, directly from its product with nonnegative coefficients and its factor (1−x)^(−2). The functional equation also gives

    (1−x)C(x)=(1+x)C(x²).

The coefficient at x^n on the right is c₁(⌊n/2⌋)>0. Therefore c₁(n)−c₁(n−1)>0 for n≥1. For m≥1,

    (1−x)C^m=((1−x)C)C^{m−1}

has positive coefficients: both factors are nonnegative and the second has constant coefficient 1. Consequently c_m is strictly increasing and Δ_m(n)>0 for all n≥1. This converts every divisibility estimate below to the stated ordinary valuation bound. In Lean, padicValInt 2 0=0; the nonzero clauses are therefore substantive, not cosmetic.


## 7. Even initialization: Pell and Kummer

Let m=2k with k≥1 and set a=3+2ν₂(k). The Chebyshev Pell identity t_k(z)²−(z²−1)u_{k−1}(z)²=1 follows by induction from the recurrence (or multiplication of the pairs representing z±√(z²−1)). Together with t_{2k}=2t_k²−1 it gives

    T(1)−1=y(1+y)ρ,       ρ=8u_{k−1}(1+2y)².

Since d₀=(T+1)(T(1)−1), Lemma R gives r₀=ρ+L(ρ).

Write u_{k−1}(1+2y)=Σ_b u_b y^b. Formula (2) gives u_b=4^b binom(k+b,2b+1). For 0≤b<k the factorial identity

    binom(k+b,2b+1)(2b+1)binom(2b,b)
       =k binom(k+b,b)binom(k−1,b)

and ν₂(binom(2b,b))=s₂(b) imply ν₂(u_b)≥ν₂(k)+2b−s₂(b)≥ν₂(k)+b. Here s₂ is binary digit sum; the central-binomial valuation follows from the factorial digit-sum formula, equivalently Kummer's carry count. For b≥k the coefficient is zero. Convolution therefore gives ν₂(ρ_E)≥a+E. In particular ω(ρ)≥a, since ℓ(E)≤E.

Define I(R,v) by ω(R)≥v, ν₂(R₀)=v, and ν₂(R_{2^i−1})≥v+i+1 for every i≥1. The constant ρ₀=8k² has valuation a. At degree 1, the two equal convolution terms supply an extra factor 2, so ν₂(ρ₁)≥a+2. For i≥2, E=2^i−1≥i+1 and the general bound a+E suffices. Thus I(ρ,a) holds. This elementary argument avoids any extra no-carries assertion for these exceptional degrees.

If m is even and I(R,v) holds, then I(L(R),v+1) holds. Lemma W proves the weight assertion. In (3), 2(m+1)R₀ has valuation v+1 and R₁ has valuation at least v+2, so the constant has valuation exactly v+1. At E=2^i−1>0, (4) gives the coefficient R_{2^{i+1}−1}+H_E. Both summands have valuation at least v+i+2: the former by I, the latter by ω(H)≥v+2 and ℓ(E)=i. This proves the invariant step.

Now I(Lρ,a+1) and I(ρ,a) imply I(r₀,a) by addition: the constant cannot cancel and the exceptional divisibilities persist. Iteration gives I(r_j,a+j). Its weight condition makes every positive coefficient divisible by 2^{a+j+1}. Write n=2^j o with o odd and apply the exact readout. This proves ν₂(Δ_{2k}(n))=ν₂(n)+2ν₂(k)+3, with nonzero difference by Section 6.


## 8. Odd initialization and exact low coefficients

Let m=2h+1 and W_N=u_N−u_{N−1}. The half-angle identity

    t_m(w)+1=(w+1)W_h(w)²

is a polynomial identity: it follows from the Chebyshev recurrence or from cos((2h+1)θ)/cos θ=W_h(cos 2θ), followed by squaring. Thus d₀=(T−1)(T(1)+1). Define T′ by T(2(1+y)H)=2(1+y)T′(H). The same symmetric calculation as in Section 3 gives

    T′(y^{2e})=y^e W_{m+e}(1+2y),
    T′(y^{2e+1})=2y^{e+1}u_{m+e}(1+2y),
    W_N(1+2y)=Σ_i4^i binom(N+i,2i)y^i.

Set G=W_h(1+2y)². The constant of T′H equals that of H, and therefore

    r₀=2(T′G−G)/y ∈ ℤ[y].

If G=Σγ_E y^E, then 4^E divides γ_E. To contribute to degree F+1, an even input E=2e in T′ has i=F+1−e, yielding valuation at least 4e+2i=2F+2+2e. An odd input E=2e+1 has i=F−e and valuation at least 4e+2+1+2i=2F+3+2e. The subtraction term has valuation at least 2F+2. Multiplication by 2 proves

    ν₂(r_{0,F})≥2F+3.                               (5)

The low terms, valid for every positive m, satisfy r_{0,0}=(2/3)m²(m+1)(m+5) and the following exact identities. Write P₁=315r_{0,1}, P₂=467775r_{0,2}; the denominators are odd.

    P₁=2m⁸+56m⁷+476m⁶+1400m⁵+938m⁴−1456m³−1416m²,
    P₂=4m¹²+264m¹¹+6380m¹^0+71280m⁹+379302m⁸
        +820512m⁷−183040m⁶−3127080m⁵−2604206m⁴
        +2235024m³+2401560m².                        (6)

These are finite symbolic identities, not interpolation assumptions. One direct derivation uses τ_{m,i}=[y^i]t_m(1+2y). For i≥1,

    τ_{m,i}=4^i m² ∏_{a=1}^{i−1}(m²−a²)/(2i)!;
    τ_{m,0}=1.

This follows by inserting the Chebyshev recurrence, with the initial values. Compute [y^E]A₂ by summing τ_{m,i}[y^E]T(y^i) for i≤2E, using the two monomial rules in Section 3. Terms with i>2E vanish, including the odd boundary term. Divide A₂−1 by y(1+y), so r_{0,F} is the alternating sum of [y^E]A₂ for 1≤E≤F+1. For F=0,1,2 these operations give (6). The dossier script repeats exactly this rational-polynomial calculation and checks the identities, without Lean.


## 9. Odd bounds and the equality families

For clarity, the finite divisibility certificate needed from (6) is as follows. Substitute m=qt+s in P₁ and P₂ and expand in ordinary integer powers of t. Exact polynomial arithmetic gives these coefficient divisibilities (the dossier records every coefficient):

    (q,s)=(4,3):   every coefficient of P₁,P₂ divisible by 128;
    (q,s)=(16,15): every coefficient of P₁,P₂ divisible by 256;
    (q,s)=(16,11): P₁(16t+11) ≡ 128 (mod 256).

In the last line the constant is 128 modulo 256 and all positive-degree coefficients are 0 modulo 256. Because the denominators in (6) are odd, the first two lines give valuation at least 7 and 8 respectively for r_{0,1},r_{0,2}; the last gives valuation exactly 7 for r_{0,1}. These are identities in ℤ[t], so they hold for all t≥0. No experimental degree bound or interpolation is used. Only ≥8 is used in the fifteen family; the stronger ≥11 reported in the later formal packet is unnecessary here.

If m≡1 (mod 4), the constant formula gives ν₂(r_{0,0})=1+1+1=3. Formula (5) gives valuation ≥5 for every positive degree and ω(r₀)≥3. Lemma W gives ω(r_j)≥3+j; the divisibility readout proves the bound, and the exact readout at j=0 proves equality at every odd n.

If m≡3 (mod 4), exactly one of m+1,m+5 has valuation 2 and the other at least 3. Thus ν₂(r_{0,0})=3+max(ν₂(m+1),ν₂(m+5))≥6. The degree-1 and degree-2 terms have valuation ≥7; degrees F≥3 have valuation ≥2F+3. Hence r₀ is divisible by 64. Their respective weights are at least 6,6,5, and 7 for F=0,1,2, and F≥3. The per-monomial gains in Lemma W are respectively 2,1,2, and at least 1. Thus ω(r₁)≥7 and ω(r_j)≥6+j for j≥1. Together with the j=0 divisibility, this proves the +6 bound.

For m≡3 or 7 (mod 16), the constant has valuation exactly 6 and every positive coefficient has valuation ≥7, so odd n give equality. For m≡11 (mod 16), equation (3) gives r_{1,0}=2(m+1)r_{0,0}+r_{0,1}. The first term has valuation at least 10, and the second exactly 7. Since ω(r₁)≥7, all its positive coefficients have valuation ≥8. The exact readout at j=1 gives equality for n≡2 (mod 4).

Finally let m≡15 (mod 16). Then ν₂(r_{0,0})=3+ν₂(m+1)≥7. The two low terms have valuation ≥8, and (5) covers all higher degrees. Thus r₀ is divisible by 128. Its weights at degrees 0,1,2 are at least 7,7,6; higher weights are at least 7. The same per-monomial gains yield ω(r₁)≥8 and then ω(r_j)≥7+j. Readout proves the universal +7 bound and therefore never-equality in the +6 bound. Section 6 supplies positivity throughout, completing the theorem.
