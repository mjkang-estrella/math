56: 1 Introduction
57: The symmetric inverse semigroup IS n , also known as the rook monoid, consists
58: of the partial injective transformations of {1, 2, . . . , n} [1, 2]. Any element of
59: IS n can be represented as an n × n matrix whose entries are 0 or 1, with at
60: most one 1 in every row and every column.
61:     In a previous paper [3], we introduced a submonoid Mn of IS n and studied
62: its properties. The monoid Mn consists of the zero matrix together with those
63: matrices of IS n whose 1s lie on a single diagonal and form an uninterrupted
64: block (i.e., no 0 lies between any two 1s). Let d be the said diagonal (d =
65: −n + 1, . . . , n − 1, with d = 0 being the main diagonal), let k be the row of the
66: northwestern 1, and let m be the row of the southeastern 1. The study of [3] was
67: facilitated by representing the elements of Mn as triplets ⟨d, k, m⟩ ∈ Z3 (d, k,
68: and m are appropriately restricted), and developing a closed-form expression
69: representing the product of two elements.
70:     This short note is an extension that allows ⟨d, k, m⟩ ∈ R3 ; the restrictions
71: on the parameters d, k, m, as well as the product formula, remain unaltered.
72: We thus study a new monoid M n , of which the Mn of [3] is a submonoid. For
73: reasons of symmetry, we switch the order of the first two arguments and use
74: the notation ≺k, d, m≻ for an x ∈ M n , so that
75: 
76:                     x =≺k, d, m≻= ⟨d, k, m⟩,       k, d, m ∈ Z.                 (1)
77: 
78: To facilitate comparisons with “the integer case,” however, we maintain much
79: of the notation of [3]. For example, we retain the symbol xT for the semigroup
80: inverse; the underlying reason is that inverting x ∈ Mn amounts to transposing
81: the matrix represented by x. As in [3], 0 and 1 denote monoid zero and identity,
82: and ideal means two-sided ideal. We use the traditional notations for Green’s
83: relations, associated equivalence classes, and principal ideals, as well as the
84: usual notations r(x) and d(x) [4, 5] for xxT and xT x, respectively. A jth root
85: of x ∈ M n is a y ∈ M n such that x = y j (j ∈ N).

1331: 7. Summary and future work
1332: In the present work, we give a complete Coxeter spectral classification of connected non-negative
1333: posets I of Dynkin type DynI = A|I|−crkI . A complete description and classification of Dynkin
1334: type Dn posets is unknown (partial results are given in [9, 15, 16]). Therefore, the following problem
1335: remains open.
1336: 
1337: Problem 7.1. Give a structural description and Coxeter spectral classification of Dn Dynkin type non-
1338: negative connected posets.
1339:     In the case of Am type posets, we show in the present work that there are exactly ⌊ n2 ⌋ Coxeter
1340: types:
1341:     • coxI (t) = tn + tn−1 + . . . + t + 1 ∈ Z[t] if I is positive (crkI = 0),
1342: 
1343:     • coxI (t) = tn − tp − tn−p + 1 ∈ Z[t], where n2 ≤ p ≤ n − 2, if I is principal (crkI = 1).
1344: Moreover, we prove that, given a pair of non-negative posets I and J of Dynkin type DynI = DynJ =
1345: A|I|−crkI , the incidence matrices CI and CJ are Z-congruent if and only if speccI = speccJ .
1346: It is known that the assumption that Dynkin types coincide cannot be omitted in general (see [9,
1347: Example 4.1]). Our experiments show that this is possible for small posets.
