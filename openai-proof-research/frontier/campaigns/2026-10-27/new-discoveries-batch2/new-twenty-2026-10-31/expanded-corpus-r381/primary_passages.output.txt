22:                                                                                 Abstract
23:                                                 We define a subsemigroup Sn of the rook monoid Rn and investigate its
24:                                                 properties. To do this, we represent the nonzero elements of Sn (which
25:                                                 are n × n matrices) via certain triplets of integers, and develop a closed-
26:                                                 form expression representing the product of two elements; these tools
27:                                                 facilitate straightforward deductions of a great number of properties. For
28:                                                 example, we show that Sn consists solely of idempotents and nilpotents,
29:                                                 find the numbers of idempotents and nilpotents, compute nilpotency
30:                                                 indexes, determine Green’s relations and ideals, and come up with a
31:                                                 minimal generating set. Furthermore, we give a necessary and sufficient
32:                                                 condition for the jth root of a nonzero element to exist in Sn , show that
33:                                                 existence implies uniqueness, and compute the said root explicitly. We
34:                                                 also point to several combinatorial aspects; describe a number of sub-
35:                                                 semigroups of Sn (some of which are familiar from previous studies);
36:                                                 and, using rook n-diagrams, graphically interpret many of our results.
37: 
38:                                                 Keywords: rook monoid, symmetric inverse semigroup, rook n-diagrams,
39:                                                 inverse semigroups, orthodox semigroups, combinatorial semigroups

173:     The elements of our semigroups are matrices whose entries are 0 and 1,
174: called zero and one, respectively. Z is the set of integers, N is the set of positive
175: integers, and N0 is the set of nonnegative integers. An infinite matrix is one
176: whose entries are xi,j with i, j ∈ N. We use δij (i, j ∈ N0 ) to denote the
177: Kronecker delta. dxe stands for the ceiling of x ∈ R.
178:     We often assume that elements of isomorphic semigroups coincide. For
179: example, our Mn is a monoid of n × n matrices. After establishing a
180: multiplication-preserving, one-to-one correspondence between the nonzero
181: matrices of Mn and certain triplets of integers, we make no distinction between
182: a matrix and its representing triplet. As another example, any multiplicative
183: semigroup consisting of n × n matrices is isomorphic to a semigroup consist-
184: ing of square matrices whose dimension is larger than n, including infinite
185: matrices: We can regard each n × n matrix as being a submatrix of the larger
186: one, with the remaining entries of the larger matrix all equal to zero. It is in
187: this sense that—in Section 4—we define Mn as a subsemigroup of a certain
188: semigroup S∞ , whose elements are infinite matrices.

1306: 12 Summary
1307: In this paper, we defined a noncommutative, inverse monoid Mn ; showed that
1308: the nonzero elements of Mn can be represented as hd, k, mi, where d, k, and m
1309: are properly defined integers; and used this “triplet representation” to study
1310: numerous properties of Mn . Both Mn and the set Sn = Mn \ {1} (where
1311: 1 is the monoid identity, corresponding to the n × n identity matrix) are
1312: inverse subsemigroups of the well-known rook monoid Rn , also known as the
1313: symmetric inverse semigroup. The monoid Mn consists of those elements of
1314:

1320: Rn whose ones lie on a single diagonal and form an uninterrupted block (i.e.,
1321: no zero lies between any two ones). Equivalently, the rook-n diagram of any
1322: monoid element has the form shown in Figure 5.
1323:     In Sections 3 and 4, we regarded Mn as a subsemigroup of a certain
1324: semigroup S∞ of countably infinite order; this helped us find the triplet rep-
1325: resentation of the product of two monoid elements (Theorem 3). Our results
1326: on Mn include:
1327: • A simple formula for powers (Corollary 2).
1328: • A proof that any element is idempotent or nilpotent (Theorem 4). Proposi-
1329:   tion 9 further gives the numbers of idempotents and nilpotents.
1330: • A formula (in Theorem 4) for the index ` of any nonzero nilpotent in terms
1331:   of d, k, and m.
1332: • A proof that the jth root of any given nonzero element either does not exist;
1333:   or does exist, is unique, and has the triplet representation given explicitly
1334:   in Theorem 6.
1335: • Green’s relations (Theorem 12), enabling a determination of all ideals
1336:   (Theorem 13).
1337: • An investigation of the (n-dependent) number of nonzero products in
1338:   Sn \ {0}; this number is 50% of the total number of products when n = 2,
1339:   and appears to be slightly larger than 50% for all other n (Conditional
1340:   Proposition 1 and Figure 4).
1341: • The use of rook-n diagrams to give graphical interpretations of many of our
1342:   results (Section 11).
1343: • An investigation of some subsemigroups of Mn (Section 9). Two of
1344:   these are the familiar monogenic subsemigroups generated by the well-
1345:   known backward and forward shift matrices (Proposition 16). For another
1346:   subsemigroup—denoted by SU Tn and consisting of all strictly upper tri-
1347:   angular matrices of Mn —we found a minimal generating set An , gave a
1348:   (non-unique) explicit formula for any x ∈ SU Tn , and computed the rank of
1349:   SU Tn (Theorem 17).

839: 5. Algorithms
840: Since isomorphic posets are strongly Gram Z-congruent (2.10), we can view Z-congruence as a gen-
841: eralization of the notion of isomorphism. Similar to the isomorphism case [28], we have two general
842: strategies for devising algorithms that construct a matrix B ∈ Gl(n; Z) that defines strong Gram
843: Z-congruence between two non-negative posets I and J of Dynkin type DynI = DynJ = Am :
844:                                    B
845:  ( i ) compute such a B that I ≈Z J directly;
846:                                                                             BI                  BJ
847:  (ii) compute a canonical representative R and Z-congruences I ≈ Z R and J ≈ Z R, then calculate
848:       B as B := BI · BJ−1 .
849: 
850:     In the first strategy, we are given two posets, encoded in, e.g., incidence matrix form (2.2), and
851: our aim is to construct such a matrix B ∈ Gl(n; Z), that B tr · CI · B = CJ . The reader is referred to
852: [13, 15, 29] for discussion of some of the algorithms of this type.
853:     The main advantage of the second strategy is its efficiency in a general classification setting defined
854: as follows: divide a (possibly large) list of posets into sublists of Z-congruent ones. Since two posets
855: I and J are Z-congruent if and only if their canonical representatives are equal, it is more efficient to
856: compute representatives first and then use a sorting, hashing, or balanced tree algorithm to efficiently
857: group the input list into desired sublists. This methodology is analogous to canonical labeling in a
858: graph isomorphism setting, see [28]. It is the strategy we follow in the current work.

1331: 7. Summary and future work
1332: In the present work, we give a complete Coxeter spectral classification of connected non-negative
1333: posets I of Dynkin type DynI = A|I|−crkI . A complete description and classification of Dynkin
1334: type Dn posets is unknown (partial results are given in [9, 15, 16]). Therefore, the following problem
1335: remains open.
1336: 
1337: Problem 7.1. Give a structural description and Coxeter spectral classification of Dn Dynkin type non-
1338: negative connected posets.
1339:     In the case of Am type posets, we show in the present work that there are exactly ⌊ n2 ⌋ Coxeter
1340: types:
1341:     • coxI (t) = tn + tn−1 + . . . + t + 1 ∈ Z[t] if I is positive (crkI = 0),
1342: 
1343:     • coxI (t) = tn − tp − tn−p + 1 ∈ Z[t], where n2 ≤ p ≤ n − 2, if I is principal (crkI = 1).
1344: Moreover, we prove that, given a pair of non-negative posets I and J of Dynkin type DynI = DynJ =
1345: A|I|−crkI , the incidence matrices CI and CJ are Z-congruent if and only if speccI = speccJ .
1346: It is known that the assumption that Dynkin types coincide cannot be omitted in general (see [9,
1347: Example 4.1]). Our experiments show that this is possible for small posets.

411:    It would be nice to have examples of graphs other than forests where toric promotion displays
412: interesting behavior. It could also be interesting to find toric analogues of other appearances of
413: promotion in the literature. For example, one might consider toric analogues of the promotion
414: Markov chains from [2, 15].

418: 12 · · · (n − h)n(n − 1) · · · (n − h + 1). In particular, ζ (1) is the identity permutation 12 · · · n. One can
419: show that if π ∈ Sn , then TProπ is conjugate in the symmetric group on ΛG to an operator of the

27:                                           Let G be a simple graph on n vertices. The adjacency matrix A = (aij ) of G
28:                                           is a 0-1 square matrix of order n with rows and columns indexed by the vertex
29:                                           set V (G) of G such that for two vertices i, j ∈ V (G), aij = 1 if and only if
30:                                           i, j are adjacent in G. The spectral radius ρ(G), or ρ(A), of G is the largest
31:                                           eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix A of G. In 1976, Brauldi and Hoffman
32:                                           proposed the problem [1, p.438] of finding the maximum spectral radius of a
33:                                           graph with exactly e edges. One decade later in 1985, they gave the Brauldi-
34:                                           Hoffman conjecture in [3] stating that the maximum spectral radius of a graph
35:                                           with e edges is attained by taking a complete graph and adding a new vertex
36:                                           which is adjacent to a corresponding number of vertices in the complete graph.
37:                                           This conjecture was proved in 1988 by Rowlinson [11]. See [7, 12] for the proof
