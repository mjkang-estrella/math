50: 1    Introduction
51: Graphs considered in this paper are finite, undirected and do not contain any loops. Note
52: that graphs may contain parallel edges, and when we want to emphasise that a graph does
53: or does not admit some parallel edges, we refer to it as a multigraph or a simple graph,
54: respectively. The vertex set and the edge set of a graph G are respectively denoted by
55: V (G) and E(G). Let U ⊆ V (G). The set consisting of all the edges having exactly one
56: endvertex in U is denoted by ∂G U , and when it is obvious which graph G we are referring
57: to we just write ∂U . When U consists of only one vertex, say u, we write ∂u, instead of
58: ∂{u}, for simplicity. Let H be an arbitrary graph: an H-colouring of G is a proper edge-
59: colouring f : E(G) → E(H) of G with edges of H, such that for each vertex u ∈ V (G),
60: there exists a vertex v ∈ V (H) with f (∂G u) = ∂H v. If there is no pair of distinct vertices
61: v and w of H such that ∂H w = ∂H v, then an H-colouring f (of G) naturally induces the
62: map fV : V (G) → V (H) defined for every vertex u of V (G) as fV (u) = v, where v
63: is the unique vertex of H such that f (∂G u) = ∂H v. If G admits an H-colouring, then
64: we write H ≺ G and we say that the graph H colours the graph G. Let P denote the
65: well-known Petersen graph. One of the most important conjectures in graph theory is the
66: Petersen Colouring Conjecture by Jaeger.
67: Conjecture 1.1 (Petersen Colouring Conjecture—Jaeger, 1988 [6]). For any bridgeless
68: cubic graph G, P ≺ G.
