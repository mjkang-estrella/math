2063:    Our approach for proving Theorem 12.1 has been analytic. The formulas that we just discussed,
2064: in particular the formulas (14.3)–(14.5) for An (q), Bn (q) and Cn (q), are unsuitable for asymptotic
2065: approximation. The reason is that each dominating term in the sums (14.3)–(14.5) has order O(4n /n),

192: In the previous section, we isolated three key observations about spherical codes. The least
193: rigorous aspect of these observations is the word “complexity.” Intuitively, the complexity
194: of an object corresponds to how difficult it is to express. In this section, we leverage ideas
195: from computational complexity to make this notion rigorous. In doing so, we obtain a notion
196: of complexity for sequences of objects. Specifically, a sequence of objects is considered to

284: from removing a point from an orthoplex. Interestingly, there are many settings in which

289: pose a general conjecture in terms of partial symmetries of the code C, that is, nontrivial
290: members g of the isometry group for which |gC ∩ C| is large. First, we identify what it
291: means for this intersection to be large:
292: 
293: Definition 6. The symmetry strength of a metric space (M, d) with isometry group G
294: is the supremum of t such that for every size-t subset T ⊆ M , there exists g ∈ G \ {id} such
295: that gT = T . Here, we adopt the convention that g∅ = ∅ so that symmetry strength is
296: nonnegative whenever the isometry group is nontrivial.
297: 
298:     For example, consider the Euclidean plane. In this case, the isometry group is the
299: Euclidean group E(2). For every set T = {x} of size 1, one may nontrivially rotate about
300: x, and so the symmetry strength is at least 1. In fact, for every set T = {x, y} of size 2,
301: one may reflect about the affine line containing x and y, and so the symmetry strength is at
307: that g = id. Overall, the symmetry strength of the Euclidean plane is exactly 2. Similarly,
308: the symmetry strength of the k-dimensional Euclidean space is exactly k.
309: 
310: Conjecture B. Let (M, d) be a unicorn space with unicorn sequence {Ci }, nontrivial isome-
311: try group G, and symmetry strength t ∈ [0, ∞). There exists an i0 such that for every i > i0 ,
312: if C is an optimal code in (M, d) of size |Ci | − 1, then there exists g ∈ G \ {id} for which
313: |gC ∩ C| > t.

587:    Another method is based on the theory of elliptic curves. It is commonly known as the ECPP
588: algorithm, which means Elliptic Curve Primality Proving. The names we must mention here are
589: Sh. Goldwasser, J. Kilian, A. Atkin and F. Morain. This algorithm is probabilistic; however, it is
590: not of the “Monte-Carlo type” but of the “Las Vegas type”. The latter means that it always gives
591: the correct answer; it is the computation time which is random. It is polynomial on average if
592: certain as yet unproved conjectures are true. Beside the correct answer, this algorithm also creates
593: a primality certificate. A certificate is “something” which may be difficult to find but, once found,
594: allows one to make a verification easily.
595:    In [5], the following example is given. Consider the number m = 44052638 + 26384405 . It
596: has 15 071 digits. The proof of its primality by the ECPP algorithm was achieved in 5.1 GHz-
597: years. This is a truly remarkable result if we compare it with other error-free algorithms. Note,
598: however, that the Rabin–Miller algorithm gives the correct (though unproved) answer in less than
599: two minutes.

1161:      We finish our paper with a list of possible follow-up ideas based on our work.
1162: 
1163:   FRACTIONAL PARTITIONS AND CONJECTURES OF CHERN–FU–TANG AND HEIM–NEUHAUSER 19
1164: 
1165: (1) Use explicit bounds and convolution of series to prove log-concavity of other infinite
1166:     families of sequences. The fact that the proof of Conjecture 1 can be reduced to a finite
1167:     check (instead of simply a finite check for each value of k) is surprising and may be able
1168:     to be used to prove similar results.
1169: (2) Find other interesting inequalities satisfied by the k-colored partition functions. As men-
1170:     tioned in Section 1, there are a number of papers studying analogues of the Bessenrodt–
1171:     Ono inequality in various settings. There are likely a number of other inequalities to
1172:     consider for k-colored partitions.
1173: (3) Prove Conjecture 2 for intervals of α. Using Proposition 4.3, Corollary 1.4 could be
1174:     extended to infinitely many other values of α (for example, by doing a computer check
1175:     if α = 3.5). However, the methods in this paper only appear to allow us to prove the
1176:     conjecture for discrete sets of α via computer calculations.
1177: (4) Give a combinatorial proof of Conjecture 1.
1178: (5) Find explicit bounds for when the higher order Turán inequalities hold for pα (n). Chen,
1179:     Jia, and Wang [6] conjectured that inequalities beyond log-concavity eventually hold for
1180:     the partition function, which was proven in [13]. This paper also tells us that these
1181:     inequalities eventually hold for pα (n). However, one could make these bounds explicit
1182:     similar to how we have done here, which may show when exactly the inequalities begin

119:      Motivated by the problem of proving the Schur positivity of the modified Mac-
120: donald polynomials, Garsia and Haiman introduced the Sn -module of diagonal
121: harmonics, and they conjectured that its Frobenius characteristic is ∇en , where ∇
122: is the so called nabla operator introduced by Bergeron and Garsia [4]. For any
123: symmetric function f , a so called Delta operator ∆f (and its sibling ∆′f ) is defined.
124: These operators have been introduced by Bergeron, Garsia, Haiman and Tesler in
125: [5], and it turns out that the nabla operator restricted to homogeneous symmetric
126: functions of degree n coincides with the Delta operator ∆en (and with ∆′en−1 ).
127:      In [24] a combinatorial formula for ∇en has been conjectured, in terms of
128: labelled Dyck paths (i.e. parking functions). This formula has been known as the
129: shuffle conjecture, and it has been recently proved by Carlsson and Mellit in [9],
130: where they actually proved a refinement of it, known as the compositional shuffle
131: conjecture, proposed in [25].
132:      A generalization of the shuffle conjecture, known as the Delta conjecture, has
133: been stated in [26]. This predicts a combinatorial interpretation for ∆′en−k−1 en in
134: terms of decorated labelled Dyck paths (for k = 0 it reduces to the case of no dec-
135: orations, i.e. to the shuffle conjecture). More recently in [45] Zabrocki constructed
136: bigraded Sn -modules which extend the diagonal harmonics, and he conjectured
137: that their Frobenius characteristic is given by ∆′en−k−1 en , completing in this way
138: the extension of the all framework of the shuffle conjecture.
139:      Some special cases and consequences of the Delta conjecture have been proved:
140: see Section 2.1 for the precise statement and the state of the art of this conjecture.
141: To this date, the general problem remains open.
142:      In the same paper [26], Haglund, Remmel and Wilson proposed a general-
143: ization of the Delta conjecture, which predicts a combinatorial interpretation of
144: ∆hm ∆′en−k−1 en in terms of decorated partially labelled Dyck paths: see Section 2.2
145: for the precise statement and the state of the art of this conjecture.
