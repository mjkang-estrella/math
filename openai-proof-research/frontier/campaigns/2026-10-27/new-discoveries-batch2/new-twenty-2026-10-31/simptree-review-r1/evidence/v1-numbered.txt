15:                                                                                               Abstract
16:                                                        This paper extends the concept of trees in graphs to the context of pure n-simplicial com-
17:                                                    plexes by generalising the notion of paths and cycles (in graphs). We introduce and study
18:                                                    these higher-dimensional analogues of trees known as pure n-simplicial trees. Our main result is
19:                                                    to establish the equivalence between pure n-simplicial trees and the (m, n)-trees introduced by
20:                                                    Dewdney in 1974 when m = n−1, thereby extending and improving his original characterisation.
21:                                                    We also disprove two conjectures proposed by Dewdney in 1974 by providing counterexamples,
22:                                                    and we present a refined version of the conjecture with an added condition, along with a rigorous
23:                                                    proof. Our results contribute to the theoretical knowledge of higher-dimensional trees and their
24:                                                    structural properties.

29:                                            1       Introduction
30:                                            Various generalisations of graph-theoretic trees to simplicial complexes have been developed and
31:                                            studied in the literature [4, 6, 1, 8]. Beineke and Pippert [4] introduced the concept of “n-trees” in
32:                                            1969, which was the first generalisation of trees. In [6], Beineke and Pippert studied the structural
33:                                            properties of 2-trees and generalised the following graph-theoretic results to 2-trees [5]:
34: 
35:                                                1. A tree with p vertices has p − 1 edges.
36: 
37:                                                2. A connected graph with p vertices and p − 1 edges is a tree.
38: 
39:                                                3. An acyclic graph with p vertices and p − 1 edges is a tree.
40:

42:                                            a further generalisation of trees to higher dimensions within pure n-simplicial complexes. Dewdney
43:                                            aimed to generalise the fundamental properties of trees to higher-dimensional settings. One of the
44:                                            main results presented in his paper is stated below.

82:    Later, in 2002, Faridi [8] introduced the notion of simplicial trees as a generalisation of graph-
83: theoretic trees to simplicial complexes (not necessarily pure n-simplicial complexes), focusing
84: mainly on their associated facet ideals and algebraic properties, such as sequential Cohen–Macaulayness.
85: Algorithms for detecting these simplicial tree structures were developed in [2]. However, these
86: approaches focus largely on algebraic and general structural aspects, without exploring classical
87: graph-theoretic characterisations of trees, such as conditions involving the number of vertices and
88: edges or the existence of unique paths between vertices.
89:    In this work, we develop and extend Dewdney’s ideas by restricting to pure n-dimensional
90: simplicial complexes, and systematically study properties such as acyclicity and connectivity, in
91: order to generalise key graph-theoretic tree results to this setting. The main contributions of this
92: work are as follows:
93:    • We introduce a notion of pure n-simplicial trees based on restricted versions of the (m, n)-
94:      paths and (m, n)-circuits introduced by Dewdney [1].
95:    • We generalise the classical result [5]: “A connected graph G is a tree if and only if there exists
96:      a unique path between any pair of vertices,” to the setting of pure n-simplicial trees.
97:    • We establish an equivalence between pure n-simplicial trees and (n − 1, n)-trees, which leads
98:      to an improved bound for k in Theorem 1.1.
99:    • We disprove Conjecture 1.2 and Conjecture 1.3 for the case m = n − 1 by constructing
100:      counterexamples. We then propose and prove a refined version of the conjecture with some
101:      additional conditions.
102:     The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section 2, we present preliminary definitions and
103: notations that will be used throughout the paper. In Section 3, we introduce the notion of a
104: reduced simplicial path in simplicial complexes, followed by a discussion of different types of reduced
105: simplicial paths and the definition of connected simplicial complexes. We define simplicial cycles
106: and study their characteristics in Section 4. In Section 5, we define simplicial trees and study their
107: characteristics, which form the core of this article. Section 6 concludes the paper with a summary
108: of results and the formulation of a new conjecture.

111: 2     Preliminaries
112: In this section, we first review the basic notions related to combinatorial simplicial complexes,
113: followed by a detailed description of Dewdney’s (m, n)-tree framework [1], including concepts such
114: as (m, n)-connectedness and (m, n)-circuits. These definitions are essential for understanding the
115: results and conjectures discussed in Section 1, and form the foundation for the extensions developed
116: in this work.
117: 
118: 2.1   Simplicial Complexes
119: A simplicial complex K is a collection of non-empty subsets of a finite vertex set V , satisfying
120: the condition that every singleton subset is included in K, and that every non-empty subset of
121: an element of K is also an element of K. The elements of a simplicial complex are referred to as
122: simplices, and the dimension of a simplex is defined as one less than the number of its vertices;
123: in particular, a k-simplex is a simplex of dimension k and hence consists of exactly k + 1 vertices.
124: Accordingly, the dimension of a simplicial complex is the maximum dimension among its simplices.
125: We denote by K k the set of all k-dimensional simplices in a complex K, and by αk (K) the number
126: of such simplices. Given two simplices σ and τ in a simplicial complex K, τ is a face of σ if every
127: vertex of τ is also a vertex of σ. A simplicial complex K is called a pure n-simplicial complex
128: if every simplex in K is a face of at least one n-simplex of K. In particular, a pure 1-simplicial
129: complex K with vertex set V can be viewed as a simple, undirected graph G(W, E) with no isolated
130: vertices, where W = V and E = K 1 . A subset L ⊆ K of simplices is termed a subcomplex of K if
131: it satisfies the following condition: for every simplex σ in L, if τ is a face of σ in K, then τ must
132: also be an element of L. These definitions are standard and can be found in foundational texts on
133: combinatorial algebraic topology [3, 7].

135: 2.2   Dewdney’s Framework
136: Definition 2.1. Let S be a subset of a simplicial complex K. The closure of S in K is the smallest
137: subcomplex S of K such that S ⊆ S, that is,
138:                           S = {τ ∈ K : τ is a face of σ for some σ ∈ S}.
139: Definition 2.2. The attachment of an n-simplex ηi in a pure n-simplicial complex K is the sub-
140: complex denoted by A(ηi , K) and is defined as
141:               A(ηi , K) := {σ ⊂ ηi : σ ⊂ ηj for some n-simplex ηj , such that ηj ̸= ηi }.
142: Definition 2.3. A simplicial complex K with p + 1 vertices is called m-complete if the dimension
143: of K is m, and every subset of the vertex set of K with m + 1 elements is a simplex in K. In
144: particular, if m = p, then K is called a complete simplicial complex.
145: Definition 2.4. A pure n-simplicial complex K is said to have an m-ordering for some 0 ≤ m ≤
146: n − 1 if there exists a distinct sequence of n-simplices η1 , . . . , ηt , where t = αn (K), such that
147: A(ηi , Ki ) is an m-dimensional simplicial complex for each 2 ≤ i ≤ t, where Ki = {η1 , . . . , ηi }.
148: Furthermore, K is said to have an m-complete ordering if A(ηi , Ki ) forms a complete simplicial
149: complex with m + 1 vertices for each 2 ≤ i ≤ t.
150:    Note: Here we have adopted simplified notions and notations for the concepts originally defined
151: by Dewdney [1]. For example, an m-complete ordering corresponds to a Km+1  m -ordering, and an
152:                                 m
153: m-ordering corresponds to the S≥1 -ordering in Dewdney’s work. This avoids notational complexity
154: while preserving the original meaning.

157: Definition 2.5. A pure n-simplicial complex K is called an (m, n)-tree if K has an m-complete
158: ordering.
159:    To further characterise (m, n)-trees, Dewdney [1] introduced the notions of (m, n)-walks, (m, n)-
160: paths, (m, n)-connectedness and (m, n)-circuits, which we now recall.
161: Definition 2.6. In a pure n-dimensional simplicial complex K, an (m, n)-walk sequence between
162: two m-simplices σi and σj , for some 0 ≤ m ≤ n − 1, is an alternating sequence of m-simplices and
163: n-simplices, given by
164:                                 σi = σ1 , η1 , σ2 , . . . , σr , ηr , σr+1 = σj ,
165: such that for each 1 ≤ k ≤ r, the simplices σk and σk+1 are distinct and both are faces of ηk . The
166: integer r is called the length of the (m, n)-walk sequence.
167: Definition 2.7. An (m, n)-walk sequence σi = σ1 , η1 , σ2 , . . . , σr , ηr , σr+1 = σj between two m-
168: simplices σi and σj in a pure n-dimensional simplicial complex K is called an (m, n)-path sequence
169: if all simplices in the sequence are distinct.
170: Definition 2.8. A pure n-simplicial complex K is (m, n)-connected if there exists an (m, n)-path
171: sequence between every pair of m-simplices in K.
172: Definition 2.9. An (m, n)-walk sequence σi = σ1 , η1 , σ2 , . . . , σr , ηr , σr+1 = σj in a pure n-simplicial
173: complex K is called an (m, n)-circuit sequence if it satisfies the following conditions:
174:     1. σi = σj .
175: 
176:     2. For all 1 ≤ p ̸= q ≤ r, we have σp ̸= σq and ηp ̸= ηq .

180: Building upon the notion of an (m, n)-path sequence introduced by Dewdney [1], we now define a
181: more restrictive structure called a reduced (m, n)-path sequence.
182: Definition 3.1. An (m, n)-path sequence σi = σ1 , η1 , σ2 , . . . , σr , ηr , σr+1 = σj in a pure n-simplicial
183: complex K is called a reduced (m, n)-path sequence if the following conditions hold:
184:     1. σi is a face only of η1 among the n-simplices in the sequence, and σj is a face only of ηr .
185: 
186:     2. For each 2 ≤ z ≤ r, σz is a face of an (n − 1)-simplex σz′ of K, such that σz′ is a face of ηz−1
187:        and ηz , and σx′ ̸= σy′ for all 2 ≤ x ̸= y ≤ r.
188: 
189: The subcomplex P = {η1 , ..., ηr } of K is called a reduced simplicial path.

751:      From Theorem 5.4 and Definition 2.5, we observe that the notion of a pure n-simplicial tree is
752: equivalent to the notion of an (n − 1, n)-tree introduced by Dewdney [1].
753:      In Theorem 5.4, we observe that a pure n-simplicial complex K is a simplicial tree with αn (K) =
754: t if and only if there exists a sequence of distinct n-simplices η1 , . . . , ηt such that, for all 2 ≤ p ≤ t,
755: there exists 1 ≤ i ≤ p − 1 and a vertex vp of ηp such that ηp and ηi share a common (n − 1)-face,
756: and vp is not a vertex of ηj for each 1 ≤ j ≤ p − 1. Therefore, pure n-simplicial trees can be defined
757: inductively as: A simplex of dimension n is a simplicial tree, and a simplicial tree with p vertices,
758: where p > n + 1, is formed by adding an n-simplex to a simplicial tree with p − 1 vertices, sharing
759: exactly one (n − 1)-face.
760:      Beineke and Pippert, in [4], introduced the concept of an “n-tree” inductively as follows: A set
761: of n mutually adjacent vertices forms an n-tree. An n-tree with p vertices, where p > n, is obtained
762: 
763: 
764:                                                              15
765: by adding a p-th vertex adjacent to each of the n already mutually adjacent vertices in an n-tree
766: with p − 1 vertices.
767:    To establish a relation between pure n-simplicial trees and n-trees, we first recall the following
768: standard definitions [3].
769: 
770: Definition 5.5. In a simplicial complex K, the k-skeleton is the collection of all simplices in K
771: with dimensions less than or equal to k, where 0 ≤ k ≤ dim(K).
772: 
773: Definition 5.6. Clique complex Cl(G) of a graph G(V, E) is a simplicial complex whose set of
774: vertices is V , and whose simplices are all subsets of V that form a clique in G.
775: 
776:     From Definition 5.5 and Definition 5.6, we observe that the edges and vertices of the graph
777: G form 1-simplices and 0-simplices, respectively, in the clique complex Cl(G). Therefore, the 1-
778: skeleton of Cl(G) is the graph G itself. Also, observe that cliques with k+1 vertices in G correspond
779: to k-simplices in Cl(G).
780:     Therefore, the clique complex of an n-tree with at least n + 1 vertices is a simplicial tree of
781: dimension n, and the 1-skeleton of a simplicial tree of dimension n is an n-tree. Therefore, the
782: number of k-simplices in a simplicial tree for any 0 ≤ k ≤ n is equal to the number of cliques
783: consisting of k + 1 vertices in the corresponding n-tree, and vice versa.

955: 6       Conclusions
956: In this work, we studied tree-like structures in pure n-dimensional simplicial complexes by building
957: on Dewdney’s theory of (m, n)-trees. We introduced the notion of simplicial trees based on restricted
958: forms of (m, n)-path sequences and (m, n)-circuits, and extended several classical graph-theoretic
959: characterisations of trees, such as:
960:        • A connected graph with p vertices and p − 1 edges is a tree.
961: 
962:        • A connected graph is a tree if and only if there exists a unique path between any pair of
963:          vertices.
964: 
965:        • An acyclic graph with p vertices and p − 1 edges is a tree.
966:     We observed that if the condition (2) is omitted from Theorem 5.12, then the converse is invalid,
967: as illustrated in Fig. 4(b). However, a natural question arises: can condition (2) of Theorem 5.12
968: be relaxed by replacing it with the knowledge of αk (K) for some 1 ≤ k ≤ n − 1? Our current
969: framework does not provide a proof for this, nor have we been able to construct a counterexample.
970: This leads us to the following conjecture.
971: 
972:                                                          19
973: Conjecture 6.1. A pure n-simplicial complex K with p vertices is a simplicial tree if and only if
974: it satisfies the following three properties:
975: 
976:    1. K has no (m, n)-simplicial cycle sequences for some 0 ≤ m ≤ n − 1.
977: 
978:    2. αk (K) = (p − n) nk + k+1
979:                               n
