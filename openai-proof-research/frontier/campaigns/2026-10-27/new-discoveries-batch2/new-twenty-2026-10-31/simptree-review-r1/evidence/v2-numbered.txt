12:                                                                                               Abstract
13:                                                        We study higher-dimensional analogues of graph-theoretic trees within the class of pure n-
14:                                                    simplicial complexes. Focusing on the case m = n − 1 in Dewdney’s (m, n)-tree framework, we
15:                                                    introduce refined notions of path and circuit sequences that overcome the structural limitations
16:                                                    of existing definitions. Using these refinements, we establish higher-dimensional analogues of
17:                                                    the classical characterisations of trees in graphs, including equivalences based on connectivity,
18:                                                    acyclicity, path uniqueness, and enumerative constraints. We further disprove two conjectures
19:                                                    posed by Dewdney by constructing explicit counterexamples, and we formulate corrected ver-
20:                                                    sions that hold under additional necessary conditions in the case m = n − 1. These results
21:                                                    provide a structurally complete theory of (n − 1, n)-trees, parallel to the classical theory of
22:                                                    graph-theoretic trees.

27:                                            1       Introduction
28:                                            Various generalisations of graph-theoretic trees to simplicial complexes have been developed and
29:                                            studied in the literature [4, 6, 1]. In graph theory, trees admit several equivalent characterisations.
30:                                            In particular, for a finite graph G with p vertices, the following statements are equivalent [5]:
31: 
32:                                                1. G is a tree;
33: 
34:                                                2. G is connected and has p − 1 edges;
35: 
36:                                                3. G is acyclic and has p − 1 edges;
37: 
38:                                                4. G is connected and acyclic;
39: 
40:                                                5. there exists a unique path between any pair of vertices of G.
41: 
42:                                                Beineke and Pippert [4] introduced the notion of n-trees as the first higher-dimensional gen-
43:                                            eralisation of graph-theoretic trees. In the special case of dimension two, Beineke and Pippert [6]
44:                                            studied the structural properties of 2-trees and showed that several classical graph-theoretic char-
45:                                            acterisations of trees admit natural analogues in this setting.

56:     Subsequently, Dewdney [1] introduced the notion of an (m, n)-tree, where n ≥ 1 and 0 ≤ m ≤
57: n−1, as a generalisation of trees to pure n-simplicial complexes. Dewdney’s framework successfully
58: generalises several enumerative properties of trees, including higher-dimensional analogues of the
59: statements that a tree with p vertices has p − 1 edges, and that a connected graph with p vertices
60: and p − 1 edges is a tree. However, this framework does not provide higher-dimensional analogues
61: of all classical structural characterisations of trees. In particular, no analogue is obtained in which
62: tree structure is characterised solely in terms of connectedness and an intrinsic notion of acyclicity.
63: Moreover, Dewdney’s framework does not recover the graph-theoretic characterisations based on
64: uniqueness of paths or on the equivalence between acyclicity together with a p − 1 edge count and
65: tree structure.
66:     In this work, we address this structural gap for the case of (n − 1, n)-trees, which is the setting
67: considered throughout the paper. To do this, we begin by examining Dewdney’s notions of (m, n)-
68: paths and (m, n)-circuits. Using these notions alone, classical structural characterisations of trees do
69: not extend to higher dimensions, and explicit counterexamples can be constructed. This shows that
70: direct generalisations based solely on Dewdney’s original path and circuit definitions are insufficient.
71:     Motivated by these observations, we introduce restricted (m, n)-path sequences, obtained by
72: imposing additional conditions on Dewdney’s (m, n)-paths. These conditions eliminate redundan-
73: cies inherent in general path sequences and are necessary to exclude the counterexamples arising
74: from Dewdney’s original definitions. Using these restricted paths, we obtain a higher-dimensional
75: analogue of the classical result that a connected graph is a tree if and only if there exists a unique
76: path between any pair of vertices.
77:     We next examine the problem of generalising the characterisation that a graph is a tree if and
78: only if it is connected and acyclic. Defining acyclicity in a pure n-simplicial complex as the absence
79: of (m, n)-circuits is insufficient, as such a condition admits counterexamples. To overcome this, we
80: introduce a refined notion of cyclicity, based on a subcomplex of circuits called simplicial cycles,
81: which capture the appropriate higher-dimensional obstruction to the (n − 1, n)-tree.

138: 2     Preliminaries
139: In this section, we review the basic notions related to combinatorial simplicial complexes and intro-
140: duce the terminology required for the study of (m, n)-trees. These definitions form the foundation
141: for the refinements and extensions developed in this work.
142: 
143: 2.1    Simplicial complexes
144: A simplicial complex K is a collection of non-empty subsets of a finite vertex set V , satisfying
145: the condition that every singleton subset is included in K, and that every non-empty subset of
146: an element of K is also an element of K. The elements of a simplicial complex are referred to as
147: simplices, and the dimension of a simplex is defined as one less than the number of its vertices;
148: in particular, a k-simplex is a simplex of dimension k and hence consists of exactly k + 1 vertices.
149: Accordingly, the dimension of a simplicial complex is the maximum dimension among its simplices.
150: We denote by K k the set of all k-dimensional simplices in a complex K, and by αk (K) the number
151: of such simplices. Given two simplices σ and τ in a simplicial complex K, τ is a face of σ if every
152: vertex of τ is also a vertex of σ. A simplicial complex K is called a pure n-simplicial complex
153: if every simplex in K is a face of at least one n-simplex of K. In particular, a pure 1-simplicial
154: complex K with vertex set V can be viewed as a simple, undirected graph G(W, E) with no isolated
155: vertices, where W = V and E = K 1 . A subset L ⊆ K of simplices is termed a subcomplex of K if
156: it satisfies the following condition: for every simplex σ in L, if τ is a face of σ in K, then τ must
157: also be an element of L. These definitions are standard and can be found in foundational texts on
158: combinatorial algebraic topology [3, 7].
159: 
160: 2.2    Dewdney’s framework
161: Dewdney’s (m, n)-tree framework [1] provides one of the earliest higher-dimensional analogues
162: of graph-theoretic trees. The theory is based on an inductive construction of pure n-simplicial

167: alternating sequences of simplices. Throughout this subsection, we assume 0 ≤ m ≤ n − 1 unless
168: otherwise stated.
169: 
170: Definition 2.1. Let S be a subset of a simplicial complex K. The closure of S in K is the smallest
171: subcomplex S of K such that S ⊆ S, that is,
172: 
173:                             S = {τ ∈ K : τ is a face of σ for some σ ∈ S}.
174: 
175: Definition 2.2. The attachment of an n-simplex ηi in a pure n-simplicial complex K is the sub-
176: complex denoted by A(ηi , K) and is defined as
177: 
178:                       A(ηi , K) = {σ ⊂ ηi | ∃ ηj ∈ K n \ {ηi } such that σ ⊂ ηj } .
179: 
180:      In other words, A(ηi , K) consists of all faces of ηi that are shared with some other n-simplex of
181: K.
182: 
183: Definition 2.3. A simplicial complex K with p + 1 vertices is called m-complete if the dimension
184: of K is m, and every subset of the vertex set of K with m + 1 elements is a simplex in K. In
185: particular, if m = p, then K is called a complete simplicial complex.
186: 
187: Definition 2.4. A pure n-simplicial complex K is said to have an m-ordering if there exists a se-
188: quence of distinct n-simplices η1 , . . . , ηt , where t = αn (K), such that A(ηi , Ki ) is an m-dimensional
189: simplicial complex for all 2 ≤ i ≤ t, where Ki = {η1 , . . . , ηi }. Furthermore, K is said to have an
190: m-complete ordering if A(ηi , Ki ) forms a complete simplicial complex with m + 1 vertices for all
191: 2 ≤ i ≤ t.

237: 3.1     Connectivity and components

240: decomposition of a pure n-simplicial complex into components. This provides a stable foundation
241: for the structural developments that follow.

369: 3.2    Reduced simplicial paths
370: We now examine the limitations of Dewdney’s notion of an (m, n)-path sequence. Unlike the
371: graph-theoretic setting, such path sequences need not be unique in (n − 1, n)-trees. This failure
372: of uniqueness indicates that Dewdney’s definition is too permissive to support higher-dimensional
373: analogues of classical path-based characterisations of trees.
374:     The source of this non-uniqueness arises from a local configuration of simplices. Let K be an
375: (n − 1, n)-tree with at least two n-simplices and n ≥ 2. Then there exists an (n − 1)-simplex σk that
376: is a face of two distinct n-simplices ηi and ηj . For any 0 ≤ m ≤ n−2, any two distinct m-faces of σk
377: admit two distinct (m, n)-simplicial path sequences of length one between them, passing through ηi
378: and ηj , respectively. Consequently, path non-uniqueness arises whenever the endpoints lie within
379: such a simplex. To isolate this obstruction, we introduce the following notion.
380: Definition 3.4. An m-simplex of a pure n-simplicial complex K is called a joint m-simplex if it
381: is a face of at least two distinct n-simplices.
382:     Joint simplices therefore account for one fundamental source of path non-uniqueness. Specif-
383: ically, whenever two m-simplices lie in a common joint (n − 1)-simplex, multiple (m, n)-path se-
384: quences necessarily exist between them. Excluding such pairs is thus a necessary step toward
385: recovering path uniqueness. However, as we show below, this exclusion alone is not sufficient. Even
386: when the endpoints are not faces of a common joint (n − 1)-simplex, non-uniqueness may still arise
387: due to ambiguity in how paths traverse successive n-simplices.
388:     This phenomenon is illustrated by a simple example. The simplicial complex

393: ordering of S1 . Here, there exist two distinct (0, 2)-path sequences between the 0-simplices {1} and
394: {5}, namely,

511: 3.3   Simplicial cycles
512: In graph theory, a classical result states that a graph is a tree if and only if it is connected and
513: acyclic. In the setting of pure n-simplicial complexes, a notion of connectivity based on (n − 1, n)-
514: paths has already been established. To obtain a higher-dimensional analogue of the graph-theoretic
515: characterisation of trees, it therefore remains to identify an appropriate notion of acyclicity.
516:    A first natural attempt would be to define a pure n-simplicial complex to be acyclic if it
517: admits no (m, n)-circuit sequences. However, this requirement is too restrictive and excludes some
518: simplicial complexes that are (n − 1, n)-trees. For example, in the (1, 2)-tree
519: 
520:                              T1 = {{1, 2, 3}, {1, 3, 5}, {1, 5, 6}, {3, 4, 5}},
521: 
522: the sequence
523:                             {1}, {1, 2, 3}, {3}, {3, 4, 5}, {5}, {1, 5, 6}, {1}
524: forms a (0, 2)-circuit sequence. Thus, forbidding all (m, n)-circuit sequences rules out complexes
525: that are otherwise tree-like and cannot serve as a suitable notion of acyclicity.
526:     The failure in the previous example arises because successive n-simplices in a circuit may be
527: linked through lower-dimensional faces without sufficient restriction. One might therefore attempt
528: to refine the notion of a circuit by requiring that each transition between consecutive n-simplices
529: occur through a common (n − 1)-face. However, this condition alone does not eliminate circuit
530: sequences. Indeed, in the (1, 2)-tree
531: 
532:                                   T2 = {{1, 2, 3}, {2, 3, 4}, {2, 4, 5}},
533: 
534: the sequence
535:                             {2}, {1, 2, 3}, {3}, {2, 3, 4}, {4}, {2, 4, 5}, {2}
536: is a (0, 2)-circuit sequence even though each pair of consecutive 2-simplices intersects along a com-
537: mon 1-face. This shows that adjacency via (n − 1)-faces alone does not prevent cyclic behaviour. In
538: this case, the obstruction appears to arise from endpoint ambiguity, as the initial 0-simplex reap-
539: pears as a face of an intermediate 2-simplex. Thus, additional restrictions are required to control
540: the behaviour of the initial simplex in a circuit sequence.
541:     Even after imposing the above restrictions, short circuit sequences may still occur in an (n−1, n)-
542: tree. For example, in the (1, 2)-tree
543: 
544:                                        T3 = {{1, 2, 3}, {2, 3, 4}},
545: 
546: the sequence
547:                                     {2}, {1, 2, 3}, {3}, {2, 3, 4}, {2}
548: forms a (0, 2)-circuit sequence satisfying all previously imposed conditions. To rule out such trivial
549: cases, it is therefore necessary to require that a circuit sequence involve at least three distinct
550: n-simplices.
551:     Taken together, the above examples show that a suitable notion of acyclicity must simultane-
552: ously control adjacency between n-simplices, restrict endpoint behaviour, and exclude short circuit
553: sequences. Motivated by these observations, we now introduce a definition of simplicial cycles that
554: incorporates all of these requirements.
555: 
556: Definition 3.8. An (m, n)-circuit sequence σi = σ1 , η1 , σ2 , . . . , σr , ηr , σr+1 = σi in a pure n-
557: simplicial complex K is called an (m, n)-simplicial cycle sequence if the following conditions hold:
558:
