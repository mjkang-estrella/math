## Nim routes: 330357 and 330359

Primary 1504.06926v2 Section3 uses exactly H+={{0},{1},{2},{0,1},{0,2}}: one may reduce the auxiliary pile, either one ordinary pile, or auxiliary and one ordinary pile, never both ordinary piles. Zero piles cannot be strictly reduced. All variables are nonnegative integers. SG is mex of all legal followers. The literal XOR lower bound and sum upper bound match source Conjectures1 and3. The latest v2 still conjectures these formulas; its general n>=3 theorem does not apply to these two ordinary piles. This failure of the easy extension shortcut is structural.

For fixed b,c, write N(a,b,c) and U=a+b+c. Every follower of (a-1,b,c), and that position itself, is reachable from (a,b,c), because an auxiliary reduction can be combined with the single ordinary-pile reduction in such a follower. Hence N(a,b,c)>N(a-1,b,c). Also N<=U by induction on total tokens. Once N(a0,b,c)=a0+b+c, these two facts force N(a,b,c)=U for every a>=a0. Let A(b,c) be the least such a when it exists. This is not monotonicity in b or c; assuming that would invalidate the intended induction.

Route A, power-of-two formula (330357). Actual 2020 Integers20 #G4, Beideman–Bowen–Muyesser, pp10–15, Definitions2–3, Lemmas5–8 and Theorem5, was acquired and its statements and relevant proof bodies read. Its game is the same selective compound *a boxplus(*b xor *c); matching the five allowed coordinate sets proves the bridge, not just similarity of names. The paper's introduction refers to an older numbering of Boros et al.; this is not taken as proof of exact scope.

Put B=2^k, c>=B. If c's B-bit is zero, b=B has no common set bit with c. Thus b xor c=b+c, the a=0 lower and upper bounds coincide, and strict auxiliary monotonicity proves N(a,B,c)=U for every a. This covers every even B-block, without another theorem.

Otherwise c=(2j+1)B+r, 0<=r<B. The public Theorem5 says for a<B-r that N is the (a+1)st gap in B xor c. Here B xor c=2jB+r. For n between this value and 2jB+B-1, its first different bit from the XOR is a low bit where B and c both have zero; these and the XOR itself are gaps. All n below the XOR are not gaps by its first differing bit, and crossing the B-bit is not a gap because both operands have B-bit1. Consequently the first B-r gaps are consecutive and the (a+1)st is 2jB+r+a, exactly a+(B xor c). This pays every low-branch parameter, including a=0 and r=0.

For a>=B-r: when j=0, the same-highest-bit threshold in public Lemma10 gives A(B,B+r)=B-r (including equal-pile base via Theorem5). For j>=1, public Lemma8 applies because c's highest bit is above B's and c contains B's sole set bit. The bound counts gaps below c OR (B+bitwiseComplement(B)), with complements taken within the finite significant-bit width of B, so B+complement(B)=2B-1. That endpoint is 2jB+2B-1. Below it the only gaps are 2jB+r,...,2jB+B-1: a first difference above B cannot be a gap as B has0 and c's changed1 would be decreased; the B-bit difference cannot be a gap; and within the zero-B-bit slab the first low difference gives the stated interval. Hence the count is B-r, giving A<=B-r. Auxiliary monotonicity pays all larger a. This completes the parameter bridge to the exact two-branch literal, but it is subsumed by that actual 2020 theorem/lemma combination. Strongest contrary significance case: the target is an infinite exact SG formula and the original paper leaves it open. The complete bridge above, rather than shortness, prevents new credit. No formal execution is appropriate.

For the opposite side c=jB+t, if b and t have disjoint binary supports, b xor c=b+c and the same a=0 argument again suffices. These cases are genuine parts of the literal, not its complete solution.

## Box-move routes: 330210

Original v1 is titled Two Partial Orders for Littlewood–Richardson Tableaux; 2019 final is Standard Young Tableaux. Both actual bodies were read at the relevant definitions and algorithm sections. Original v1 Section2 defines rook strip, lattice words and dominance. Section3 standardizes the top-right-to-bottom-left word to a permutation, numbering repeated letters in increasing occurrence order. The second algorithm reads the opposite direction omega, left-to-right bottom-up. Reversing conventions without translating inequalities reverses the intended move.

In omega notation let target word z and current word x first differ at k, with z_k=s<x_k. Let m>k be the FIRST occurrence of s in current x; let t be the least entry >s among positions k,...,m-1; and ell be the LAST occurrence of t in that interval. Algorithm2 swaps ell,m. Prefix cumulative counts of letters<=u change by +1 exactly when ell<=c<m and s<=u<t. Original Lemma4.2 expresses dominance by these counts. On this rectangle, counts of target minus current are positive: at k the target has an s while current has a larger letter; before m the current has no s and no letter in (s,t), so for every u in [s,t) its cumulative count cannot catch the target. Subtracting the rectangle leaves all counts nonnegative. This proves the proposed move remains above the target in dominance, but does not identify Algorithm1's choice. The lattice condition is separate and handled in the source's proof; it cannot be inferred solely from dominance.

Route1: uniqueness of a cover in the Bruhat interval. Fails: intervals generally have several covers, and different choices of ell before fixing the last occurrence already produce different allowed moves in the source example omega(target)=132211, omega(current)=232111. The source's example illustrates the two allowed 2 positions. Requiring ell maximal removes this particular tie; it does not prove that the subword algorithm chooses it.

Route2: expose the first omitted reflection in a bubble-sort reduced word. Algorithm1 takes a reduced expression of the upper permutation and a reduced subword for the lower permutation whose first omitted index is maximal. Restore that reflection. If its prefix is p, the selected reflection is conjugated to p s_i p^{-1}, a transposition. Thus equality of the two algorithms reduces to proving that this transposition swaps precisely the standardized positions corresponding to ell,m above, for every pair of comparable lattice words of fixed content. This is a precise unpaid combinatorial lemma, including repeated-letter standardization and opposite reading directions. Maximizing the first omitted position does not by itself describe all later omissions; a greedy induction must preserve feasibility of completing the remaining subword. Bruhat order inequalities guarantee existence, not this explicit choice.
