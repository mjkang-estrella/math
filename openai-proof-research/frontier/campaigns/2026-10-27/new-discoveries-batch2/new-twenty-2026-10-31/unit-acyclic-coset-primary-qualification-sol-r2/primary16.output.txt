   THE WEAK ACYCLIC MATCHING PROPERTY IN ABELIAN
                      GROUPS

                          MOHSEN ALIABADI1 AND PETER TAYLOR2

         Abstract. A matching from a finite subset A ⊂ Zn to another subset B ⊂ Zn is
         a bijection f : A → B with the property that a + f (a) never lies in A. A matching
         is called acyclic if it is uniquely determined by its multiplicity function. Alon et
         al. established the acyclic matching property for Zn , which was later extended to
         all abelian torsion-free groups. In a prior work, the authors of this paper settled
         the acyclic matching property for all abelian groups. The objective of this note is
         to explore a related concept, known as the weak acyclic matching property, within
         the context of abelian groups.



                                          1. Introduction
  Let (G, +) denote an abelian group. Consider nonempty finite subsets A, B ⊆ G.
A matching from A to B is defined as a bijection f : A → B satisfying the condition
a + f (a) ∈
          / A for every a ∈ A. When such a matching exists from A to B, we say
that A is matched to B. This concept, introduced by Fan and Losonczy in [8], serves
as a tool in exploring Wakeford’s classical problem concerning canonical forms for
symmetric tensors [14]. It is evident that the existence of a matching from A to B
requires |A| = |B| and 0 ∈/ B.
  An abelian group G possesses the matching property if the aforementioned con-
ditions on A and B suffice to ensure the existence of a matching from A to B.
Losonczy addressed the question of which groups possess this property in [13], giv-
ing the following criterion.
Theorem 1.1. An abelian group G possesses the matching property if and only if
G is torsion-free or cyclic of prime order.
  For a matching f : A → B, the associated multiplicity function mf : G → Z≥0 is
defined as follows:
                      for all x ∈ G, mf (x) = |{a ∈ A : a + f (a) = x}|.

implies f = g. If an acyclic matching from A to B exists, we say that A is acyclically
matched to B. A group G exhibits the acyclic matching property if for any pair of
subsets A and B in G with |A| = |B| and 0 ∈/ B, there is at least one acyclic matching

of matching theory are developed in [7], while a matroidal version is presented in

viewpoint of additive number theory, connections to matching theory can also be
found in [10].
   Acyclic matchings for specific sets: When discussing matchings between two
sets A and B within G, two scenarios represent the extremes of the spectrum in
terms of the number of matchings:
    (1) A + B = A: In this case A is not matched to B. This case is treated in [3,
        Lemma 2.3] where it is proved that in this situation B must be a subgroup
        of G, and A is a coset of B.
    (2) A ∩ (A + B) = ∅: In this case, not only is A matched to B but every bijection
        from A to B constitutes a matching, resulting in the maximum number of
        matchings.
Motivated by the preceding discussion, the acyclic matching problem arises for sets
A and B where A ∩ (A + B) = ∅. Intuitively, the situation might be complex when
A ∩ (A + B) = ∅ because there could be as many as |A|! matchings, increasing
the probability of at least one acyclic matching existing. On the other hand, the
number of matchings may grow faster than the number of multiplicity functions, so
the average number of matchings per multiplicity function may be large reducing
the probability of at least one acyclic matching existing. The following special cases
of acyclic matching were proved in [1]:
Theorem 1.3. Let A be a subset of the cyclic group Z/pZ where p is a prime
number. Suppose A satisfies A ∩ 2A = ∅ where 2A = {2a | a ∈ A}and A is of size
k with k · 2k−1 < p. Then A is acyclically matched to itself via the identity map.
Theorem 1.4. Let A and B be subsets of an abelian group G. Suppose A and B are
of the same size satisfying A ∩ (A + B) = ∅. Then there exists an acyclic matching
f : A → B if B is a Sidon set. (That is, the equation x + y = z + w has no solution
in B with {x, y} ∩ {z, w} = ∅.)

  Following [2], we define an abelian group G to possess the weak acyclic matching
property if, for every finite nonempty subsets A and B of G with |A| = |B| and
A ∩ (A + B) = ∅, A is acyclically matched to B. In [2], the following conjecture
concerning the weak acyclic matching property was proposed.
Conjecture 1.5. There are infinitely many n for which Z/nZ has the weak acyclic
matching property.
  The following question was also raised in [1].
Question 1.6. Does Z/pZ possess the weak acyclic matching property for every
prime p?
  In this note, our main result, stated as Theorem 2.3, provides an affirmative
answer to Conjecture 1.5 and thoroughly addresses Question 1.6. Furthermore,
Theorem 2.3 classifies all abelian groups with respect to their adherence to the weak
acyclic matching property.
Remark 1.7. It is worth noting that while the matching theory developed in this
paper is related to the classical theory introduced by Philip Hall in 1935, the two
are conceptually distinct. In particular, to address a linear algebra problem posed
by Wakeford [14], Fan [8] constructed a bipartite graph G = (V (G), E(G)) based on
two sets A, B ⊂ Zn , which may overlap. Each element a ∈ A is assigned a symbol
xa , and each b ∈ B a symbol yb , forming vertex sets
                    X = {xa : a ∈ A} and Y = {yb : b ∈ B}.
The graph has vertex set V (G) = X ∪ Y , with an edge between xa ∈ X and yb ∈ Y
if and only if a + b ∈
                     / A.
   In graph-theoretic terms, a matching is a set of edges with no shared endpoints,
and a perfect matching is one that covers all vertices. In the bipartite graph asso-
ciated with A and B, perfect matchings correspond to bijections f : A → B such
that a + f (a) ∈/ A for all a ∈ A. Wakeford’s question thus reduces to determining
the existence of such perfect matchings, known as acyclic matchings from A to B,
discussed earlier.

                                 2. Main results
   Multiplicity functions in a more general setting: We begin with a few
definitions. Let A, B and C be three nonempty sets. An operator ⊕ : A × B → C
is said to possess the left cancellation property provided:
                           a ⊕ b1 = a ⊕ b2 ⇐⇒ b1 = b2 ,
for every a ∈ A and b1 , b2 ∈ B.
It will be convenient to define also the operator ⊖ : C × A → B as an inverse
operation:
                              c ⊖ a = b ⇐⇒ a ⊕ b = c,
where a ∈ A, b ∈ B and c ∈ C.

    In the following lemma, we obtain a bijection with a unique multiplicity function.
Lemma 2.1. Let A, B be finite sets of the same cardinality. Let C be a set. Let
⊕ : A × B → C be an operator with the left cancellation property and also the
operator ⊖ as an inverse operation. Consider the set F of bijections f : A → B
with corresponding multiplicity functions mf (c) = |{a ∈ A : a ⊕ f (a) = c}|. Then
there exists an element f0 ∈ F which has a unique mf0 . That is, if g ∈ F and
mg = mf0 then g = f0 .
Proof. The proof begins by constructing such an f0 . Let C ′ = {a⊕b : (a, b) ∈ A×B}
be the image of ⊕. Since A and B are finite, C ′ is also finite and we can label its
elements c1 , . . . , ck where k = |C ′ |.
   Let A1 = A, B1 = B. For each 1 ≤ j ≤ k, let
                   A′j = {a ∈ Aj : there exists b ∈ Bj with a ⊕ b = cj }.
For each a ∈ A′j we assign f0 (a) = cj ⊖ a. For the next iteration we define Aj+1 =
Aj \ A′j and Bj+1 = Bj \ {cj ⊖ a : a ∈ A′j }.
   Since the paired elements of A and B are removed from the active subsets Aj+1 ,
Bj+1 it is evident that no element is paired twice. Since |A| = |B| and |A′j | = |Bj′ |,
by induction we always have |Aj | = |Bj |. We claim that |Ak+1 | = 0. Suppose
for contradiction that |Ak+1 | > 0. Then, we can arbitrarily choose a′ ∈ Ak+1 and
b′ ∈ Bk+1 . Since there exists j ≤ k for which a′ ⊕ b′ = cj , at least one of them should
have been removed no later than at step j, which leads to a contradiction. Hence
|Ak+1 | = 0. Therefore f0 is a bijection A → B, as claimed.
   We show the uniqueness of mf0 by assuming that g : A → B is a bijection and
proving the property

    (mg (ci ) = mf0 (ci ), for all i and j with 1 ≤ i < j)
         implies    (g(a) = f0 (a), for all a ∈ A′i , and for all i and j with 1 ≤ i < j)
by induction on j.
   When j = 1 the right-hand side of the implication is trivially true, giving the base
case.
   For the inductive step we note that, by construction, f0 maximizes mf0 (j) subject
to the assignments made in steps 1 to j − 1. Therefore if g agrees with f0 on
all the assignments made in those steps and mg (cj ) = mf0 (cj ), g must make the
same assignments as those made for f0 in step j. It cannot assign an a which is
not in Aj because it is already assigned; the values in Aj that can potentially be
assigned to yield cj are precisely those in A′j ; and each has a unique b ∈ Bj (and,
indeed, b ∈ B) to which it can be assigned in order to yield cj , because of the left

  In the following theorem, we will establish that all abelian groups possess the

Theorem 2.3. Every abelian group possesses the weak acyclic matching property.
Proof. Let G be an abelian group. Let A and B be nonempty finite subsets of G
with |A| = |B| and A ∩ (A + B) = ∅. Since the bijections A → B are precisely
the matchings and the group operation is cancellative, we can apply Lemma 2.1 to
obtain an acyclic matching from A to B, completing the proof.                 
Remark 2.4. In the case that A ⊆ C and B ⊆ C, any operation ⊕ : A × B → C
for which (C, ⊕) is a quasigroup has the left cancellation property. The definition
of matching generalizes straightforwardly to quasigroups, and so does the proof of
Theorem 2.3. With that in mind, one might explore the properties of matchings in
broader contexts beyond abelian groups, such as quasigroups. Delving further into
this line of research could prove to be worthwhile.

   The concept of matching was introduced by Fan and Losonczy in [7] as a
tool to investigate Wakeford’s classical problem regarding canonical forms for
symmetric tensors [12]. In particular, let V be a q-dimensional complex vector
space, S p (V ) be the symmetric tensor of degree p and A be a fixed set con-
sisting of q(q − 1) monomials in S p (V ), where ik > 0 for all k. It is proved
in [7] that the generic element of S p (V ) can be conjugated, via an appropriate
linear transformation, to an element whose support lies outside of A. The main
tool to prove this is by establishing the existence of an acyclic matching in a
specific weighted bipartite graph. A linear analogue of matchings in vector sub-
spaces over field extensions was introduced in [6]. The matroidal counterpart
of matchings was explored in [4].

   In this paper, our objective is to establish a necessary and sufficient condition
for an abelian group to possess the acyclic matching property. In view of
Theorems 1.1 and 1.2, it naturally prompts an inquiry into the acyclic matching
property in Z/pZ. This problem was initially explored in [2], where it was
proved that infinitely many primes p exist for which Z/pZ lacks the acyclic
matching property. Particularly, it was shown that for the following two families
of primes, Z/pZ does not exhibit the acyclic matching property:
      • p ≡ −1 (mod 8)
      • The multiplicative order of 2 modulo p is an odd number.
Considering the latter condition along with a result from [8] concerning the
parity of the multiplicative order of 2 modulo p, it is observed that primes
p where Z/pZ lacks the acyclic matching property have a density of at least
 7
24 . Additionally, employing a rectification principle [9], which posits that a
suitably small subset of Z/pZ can be embedded in integers while preserving
certain additive properties, it is proved in [1] that for A, B p ⊂ Z/pZ, A is
acyclically matched to B provided that 0 ∈ / B and |A| = |B| ≤ log2 p − 1.

that are not acyclically matched. However, Example 2.2 in [1] demonstrates
that in an abelian group G if |A| = |B| = |G| − 1 or |A| = |B| = |G| − 2, A must
be acyclically matched with B. Therefore, A and B in Lemma 2.6 represent
the largest possible sets that fail to exhibit acyclic matchings.
   In the following theorem, we classify all cyclic groups of prime order with
respect to the acyclic matching property. This lemma gives a negative answer to
Question 2.4 in [2], and serves as the “finite cyclic group” analogue of Theorem
1 in [5] and Theorem 4.1 in [11].
Theorem 2.8. If p > 5 is prime, then Z/pZ does not possess the acyclic
matching property.
Proof. The proof is immediate from Lemma 2.6.
                                                                                 
Remark 2.9. It is worth mentioning that Lemma 2.6 and Theorem 2.8 are
applicable in a more general setting. In essence, we can assert that for n > 5,
Z/nZ lacks the acyclic matching property. The case where n is prime follows
directly from Theorem 2.8. For non-prime n, choose a such that gcd(a, n) 6= 1.
Choose x ∈ Z/nZ \ hai and set A := hai and B := (hai ∪ {x}) \ {0}. Then
A is not matched to B, let alone acyclically matched. Therefore, Z/nZ does
not possess the acyclic matching property. With all this, we are primarily
interested in the scenario where n is prime, in view of Theorem 1.1.
   We are now ready to provide a necessary and sufficient condition for abelian
groups to possess the acyclic matching property. This theorem serves as the
“acyclic matching property” analogue of Theorem 1.1.
Theorem 2.10. An abelian group G possesses the acyclic matching property
if and only if either G is torsion-free or G = Z/pZ, where p ∈ {2, 3, 5}.
Proof. Suppose G possesses the acyclic matching property. Then it also sat-
isfies the matching property. From Theorem 1.1, it follows that G is either
torsion-free or cyclic of prime order. However, according to Lemma 2.8, if G is
cyclic of prime order with acyclic matching property, then p ≤ 5, as claimed.
Conversely, it follows from Theorem 1.2 that torsion-free groups possess the
acyclic matching property. Also, the simulation results in [3, page 153, Algo-
rithm 3] demonstrate that Z/pZ for p = 2, 3, 5 exhibits the acyclic matching
property, thereby completing the proof.                                      
