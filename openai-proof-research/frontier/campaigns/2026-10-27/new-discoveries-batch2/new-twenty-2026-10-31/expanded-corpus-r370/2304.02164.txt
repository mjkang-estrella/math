                                                                              John Byrne ∗                  Michael Tait †

                                                                                                 Abstract
                                                      We study the function Hn (C2k ), the maximum number of Hamilton paths such that the union
                                                  of any pair of them contains C2k as a subgraph. We give upper bounds on this quantity for k ≥ 3,
                                                  improving results of Harcos and Soltész, and we show that if a conjecture of Ustimenko is true then
                                                  one additionally obtains improved upper bounds for all k ≥ 6. We also give bounds on Hn (K2,3 )
                                                  and Hn (K2,4 ). In order to prove our results, we extend a theorem of Krivelevich which counts
                                                  Hamilton cycles in (n, d, λ)-graphs to bipartite or irregular graphs, and then apply these results
                                                  to generalized polygons and the constructions of Lubotzky-Phillips-Sarnak and Füredi.

    This result can be interpreted as follows: the number of Hamilton cycles in a regular pseudorandom

will use the K2,t −free graphs of Füredi [11]. These constructions are all irregular or bipartite, so we
will prove versions of Theorem 5 which hold for irregular or bipartite graphs.

    Following [23], we use the following notation and conventions for a given graph G: n is the number
of vertices; δ, d and ∆ are the minimum, average, and maximum degrees respectively; A is the adjacency
matrix; and λ1 ≥ · · · ≥ λn are the adjacency eigenvalues, which satisfy max{|λi | : i 6= 1} ≤ λ. For a
general symmetric n × n matrix M , we also use λ1 (M ) ≥ · · · ≥ λn (M ) to denote the eigenvalues of M .
A 2-factor in G is a collection of vertex-disjoint cycles which covers G, where a single edge counts as
a cycle. If F is a 2-factor in G then c(F ) is the number of cycles in F of length at least 3; for s ∈ N,
f (G, s) is the number of 2-factors in G with exactly s cycles; f (G) is the total number of 2-factors in
G; for an integer 2 ≤ k ≤ n, φ(G, k) is the maximum number of 2-factors in an induced subgraph of G
on k vertices. For U, W ⊆ V , e(U, W ) := {(u, w) : u ∈ U, w ∈ W, u ∼ w}; e(U ) is the number of edges
with both endpoints in U ; and N (U ) is the set of vertices not in U which have a neighbor in U . We
use the notation f ≪ g to mean f = o(g).
    This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we collect several tools that we will need including
an irregular expander mixing lemma, the lemmas we require to find Hamilton cycles, and results on
permanents. In Section 3, we prove Theorem 6 and Theorem 7. In Section 4, we prove our main results
Theorem 2, Theorem 3, and Theorem 4. In Section 5 we present a lower bound on K2,3 . Finally, in
Section 6, we give concluding remarks and show that if a conjecture of Ustimenko holds, then the
upper bound from Theorem 1 can be improved for all k > 2.

3     Hamilton cycles
We now prove Theorem 6 and Theorem 7. Our argument follows [23], but we must take into account
the fact that the graph is irregular or bipartite. For the upper and lower bounds, we utilize the
connection between the permanent of A and 2-factors in the graph. The upper bound follows quickly
using Lemma 6. A high-level outline of the proof of the lower bound is as follows:

    • Using Lemma 7 and the connection between the permanent of A and 2-factors in the graph, we
      have a lower bound on a weighted count of the 2-factors in G.
    • We show that the number of 2-factors with “many” distinct cycles is negligible compared to the
      total.
    • For each 2-factor with “few” distinct cycles, Lemma 4 or Lemma 5 is used to convert it to a
      Hamilton cycle with few rotations.
    • For any given Hamilton cycle, the number of ways that a 2-factor can be converted to it by few
      rotations is bounded above.
    • Combining all of the bounds, we obtain a lower bound on the number of distinct Hamilton cycles
      in the graph.

    We now proceed with the details.

Using the corollaries of the bipartite expander mixing lemma included in the appendix, one can follow

6     Concluding remarks
In Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 we improved the best known upper bounds on Hn (C2k ) for every k ≥ 3.
Here we discuss a possible approach to obtaining a further improvement for every k ≥ 6. In Theorem 3
we use the graphs of Lubotzky, Phillips and Sarnak because they are dense graphs with high girth, but a
denser construction is known: these are the graphs CD(k, q) of Lazebnik, Ustimenko, and Woldar [26].

the bound also holds for CD(6, q).

from Lemma 1 are asymptotically sharp, even if it is applied to optimal G−free graphs. Moreover, it is
not clear whether the best construction for a lower bound on Hn (G) is obtained by taking all Hamilton
cycles in a G−free graph; Harcos and Soltész noted in [15] that this is the case if G = C3 , C4 , C5 , but
not if G = K3,3 . We were unable to find a construction to show this is not the case for K2,3 and K2,4 .
Rather than determine the asymptotics of Hn (C2k ) for every k, a more modest question is perhaps:
does this quantity increase or decrease with k?
    In the case of Hn (K2,4 ) we displayed the exponential factor in Theorem 4 because existing bounds
on Hn (K1,4 ) already imply that Hn (K2,4 ) ≤ nn/2+o(n) . The latter quantity has been determined up
to an exponential factor:
                              1                                 1
                            n 2 n 2−1.47n−o(n) ≤ Hn (K1,4 ) ≤ n 2 n 2−0.72n+o(n)

(see [19] for the lower bound and [20] for the upper bound). Our upper bound on Hn (K2,4 ) is a slight
improvement for K2,4 since 3−n/2 < 2−0.79n . However we expect that the true value of Hn (K2,4 ) is
much smaller, given the following conjecture of Harcos and Soltész:
Conjecture 2 ([15]). There is a constant C > 0 such that Hn (K2,4 ) ≤ C n .
     In [15] it was shown that Conjecture 2 is equivalent to the reversing permutations conjecture. Two
permutations of [n] are called reversing if there are two coordinates where they have the same entries
but in the opposite order, and the reversing permutations conjecture states that the maximum size
of a family of pairwise reversing permutations of n, denoted RP (n), is at most C n for some constant
C > 0. The best lower bound for Hn (K2,4 ) we are aware of comes from this connection and it shows
that H4n (K2,4 ) ≥ RP (n) ≥ 1.587n−2 using a construction also given in [15].
     It is perhaps more promising to attempt to improve on the construction given in Section 5 for
Hn (K2,3 ). That construction is only a slight improvement over the one implied by the lower bound
for Hn (K2,4 ), since the base is increased from 1.5871/4 to 1.6181/4.
     We now consider some possible improvements of our results counting Hamilton cycles, focusing on
Theorem 6. A common problem in graph theory is to obtain the weakest possible assumptions on a
graph that guarantee the existence of a Hamilton cycle. A longstanding conjecture from [25] is that
there is a constant C such that any (n, d, λ)−graph with d/λ > C is Hamiltonian. Recently, Glock,
Correia, and Sudakov in [12] proved that the conjecture holds for graphs where d is polynomial in n, and
that for general (n, d, λ)−graphs the condition d/λ ≥ C(log n)1/3 guarantees Hamiltonicity. In [12],
Lemma 4 (Lemma 3.1 of [23]) is improved, and it would be interesting to see if combining the methods in
this paper with [12] could give any improvements. When we care about the number of Hamilton cycles,
there are several ways one could formulate an analogous question. Perhaps most naturally, we would
ask how much we can weaken conditions (1)-(3) such that the formula h(G) = n!(d/n)n (1 + o(1))n
still holds, since this represents similar behavior to the Erdős-Rényi random graph G(n, d/n). We
suspect that it is possible to decrease the right-hand side of (2) to (log n)1+ε for an arbitrary constant
ε > 0, which is the corresponding condition appearing in [23]. The only place we needed the increased
exponent 2 is in equation (2) (and there we could have instead used d/λ ≫ (log ∆)2 ).
     Another natural question is whether the condition (1) on the degrees in the graph can be relaxed,
perhaps to a condition of the form ∆/δ ≤ 1 + ε for some positive ε > 0, such that the conclusion
h(G) = n!(d/n)n (1 + o(1))n still holds. We believe that the techniques in this paper still can be used to
prove nontrivial bounds on h(G) under weaker assumptions about the degrees, perhaps using stronger


                                                    15
pseudorandomness conditions. However, the resulting lower bound may be less than n!(d/n)n (1 +
o(1))n , and when the degrees may differ by a constant factor, it is unclear what the desired formula
for h(G) should be. We note some places in our proof where we have used bounds on the degrees:
    • in Lemma 2, to bound the degree variance;
    • in Corollary 4;
    • in the bounds on g(n/2, z) in Corollary 1;
    • in equation (2);
    • in estimating the average degree of G[V − V0 ] in section 3.
    For irregular graphs one often considers the eigenvalues of the combinatorial Laplacian L rather
than adjacency matrix. In [5], Butler and Chung built on [25] and gave a mild condition on the
spectral gap of L which guarantees Hamiltonicity. It would be interesting to prove a counting version
of [5], though again without some condition on the degrees of the graph it is not even clear how many
Hamilton cycles one should expect.
