                                                                                  Abstract
                                                       Using a unified method of identifying a telescoping function for each
                                                   statistic-action pair, we prove conjectures of La Croix and Roby, and
                                                   of Sheridan-Rossi that various permutation statistics, such as the num-
                                                   ber of fixed points, are homomesic under Foatic actions: compositions
                                                   of the Foata bijection and dihedral symmetries of one-line notation.
                                                   Underlying these results is a family of bijective identities relating per-
                                                   mutation statistics across the Foata bijection; one of them — an unex-
                                                   pected invariance of the number of right-to-left minima under reverse-
                                                   complement, proved by an explicit bijection — may be of independent
                                                   interest. The telescoping functions also turn out to be shared among
                                                   some Foatic actions, implying that mixing certain actions still yields a
                                                   constant average on closed walks.
                                                       Keywords. Homomesy, dynamical algebraic combinatorics, Foata
                                                   bijection, Rényi–Foata transform, Foatic actions, permutation statis-
                                                   tics, telescoping function, symmetric group.

                                                   05A05, 05A19 (secondary)

Lemma 5.2. For every π ∈ Sn , anxc(π) = des(F(π)).
Proof. In the CCD of π, each antiexcedance i corresponds to a shape of ei-
ther (. . . , i, π(i), . . . ) where i and π(i) are in the main body of the cycle, or
(π(i), . . . , i) where i and π(i) are at the ends of the cycle. The second case
is impossible in CCD, since we know that π(i) < i while by definition of the
CCD each cycle must start with its largest element, a contradiction. Hence,
each antiexcedance i must appear in a (. . . , i, π(i), . . . ) shape in the CCD of
π. Hence, when we remove the parentheses and read the resulting string as
OLN (applying F), each antiexcedance becomes a descent of F(π).
    Conversely, any descent of F(π) comes from an antiexcedance of π. Indeed,
a descent of F(π) must be a j ∈ [n − 1] such that F(π)(j) > F(π)(j + 1).
If F(π)(j) and F(π)(j + 1) belong to the same cycle in the CCD of π, then
F(π)(j + 1) follows F(π)(j) on this cycle, and thus F(π)(j) > F(π)(j + 1)
shows that F(π)(j) is an antiexcedance of π. If they don’t, then F(π)(j) must
be the last element of a cycle, while F(π)(j + 1) starts the next cycle; hence,
F(π)(j + 1) must be a left-to-right maximum of F(π) (by the construction of
F −1 ). But this is impossible, since F(π)(j) > F(π)(j + 1). So they do belong
to the same cycle, and thus come from an antiexcedance of π.
    Thus we have a bijection between the antiexcedances of π and the descents
of F(π), implying that
                                     anxc(π) = des(F(π)).

     1.1. Preliminaries. A (simple) graph G consists of a set of vertices V (G) and a set of edges E(G)
such that each edge e ∈ E(G) is a subset of two vertices. Define n = |V (G)| and m = |E(G)|. We will
only consider simple graphs without multiedges or loops. For ease of notation, ij will be used to denote
the edge {i, j}. We also use i ∼ j to denote that i and j share an edge. The set of all vertices j such that
ij ∈ E(G) is called the neighborhood of the vertex i. The degree of a vertex i, denoted deg(i), is the size of
its neighborhood. For a graph G and a set S ⊆ V (G), let G\S denote the graph G with the vertices in S
(and all their associated edges) removed. A walk is a traversal of the edges of a graph and a non-backtracking
walk is a walk in which the edges that are traversed cannot immediately be repeated. All graphs we consider
are connected, or have a walk between any two vertices. A graph is bipartite if there exists a partition
V (G) = A ∪ B such that every edge ij ∈ E(G) i ∈ A and j ∈ B. A graph is unicyclic if the graph contains
only one cycle as a subgraph. For more information regarding graph theory, see [20].

     For a vector v, we will let vi denote the ith entry of the vector. The all ones vector of dimension n
will be denoted 1n (or just 1 when the dimension is clear) and the identity matrix of dimension n will be
denoted In (or just I when the dimension is clear). The ith standard basis vector will be denoted ei .

    For a matrix M , an eigenvalue λ is defective if its geometric multiplicity is strictly less than its algebraic
multiplicity. A matrix has no defective eigenvalues if and only if it has n linearly independent eigenvectors.
Equivalently, a matrix has no defective eigenvalues if and only if it is diagonalizable. For matrices that are
not diagonalizable, it is useful to consider their Jordan Canonical Form instead.

k. Thus, the existence of a Jordan chain of length k ≥ 2 corresponding to eigenvalue λ for M is sufficient
to show that λ is a defective eigenvalue of M . For more information regarding the Jordan Canonical Form,
see [9].

    The non-backtracking matrix has many eigenvalues and corresponding eigenspaces we understand well;
these are λ = ±1 (see [8, 7, 16, 10]). Therefore, the spectral interest of this matrix tends to be on the
remaining eigenvalues and corresponding eigenspaces. A useful tool for prioritizing these eigenvalues is
Ihara’s theorem.
    Theorem 1.1 (Ihara’s Theorem [11]). For a graph G with n vertices and m edges, let B be the non-
backtracking matrix of G. Let A be the adjacency matrix of G and D the degree diagonal matrix. Then

                                       det(I − uB) = (1 − u2 )m−n det(u2 (D − I) − uA + I).

    Note that this does not directly give us the eigenvalues of B, since the eigenvalues are the solutions to
det(λI − B). Therefore, for the solutions to det(I − uB), it follows that u = 1/λ. The matrix polynomial
