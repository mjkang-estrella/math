    Our ultimate goal is to provide a complete probability distribution for the
piercing number as a function of the number of voters, n, and the approval set
length, p. In other words, we hope to answer the question below.
Question 4.2. Given a random fixed-length circular society S with n voters and
approval sets of length p ∈ (0, 1), and a positive integer k, what is the probability
that τ (S) = k?
    For arbitrary n, we are able to answer this question only if the approval set
length p is sufficiently large or sufficiently small. Theorem 3.1 tells us that if
p ≥ Q(n, 1) = n−1n , then the piercing number must be 1. If p is sufficiently small,
then the approval sets may be pairwise disjoint, making it possible for the piercing
number to equal the number of voters (i.e., k = n). The following theorem from
Solomon [9, Equation (4.5)] gives us the probability that the piercing number equals
the number of voters when p < n1 .
    Theorem 4.3 (Solomon). The probability that n arcs of length p ∈ [0, 1/n),
randomly placed on the circle, are pairwise disjoint is (1 − np)n−1 .

                                                                  1
with distinct approval sets A1 , . . . , An of length p < 2k        . We follow Definition 4.1,
denoting Ai = [`i , ri ], where ri = `i + p.
     First, suppose there exists a unique collection of k pairwise-disjoint approval
sets A1 , . . . , Ak whose union contains the left endpoints of all other approval sets.
This means that each of the remaining n − k approval sets contains one of the right
endpoints of A1 , . . . , Ak . Thus, the set of the right endpoints of A1 , . . . , Ak is a
piercing set of size k. Since A1 , . . . , Ak are pairwise disjoint, a minimum piercing
set of S must contain at least k points. Therefore, τ (S) = k.
     Conversely, suppose τ (S) = k. Then, there is a minimum piercing set W =
{x1 , . . . , xk } of S. For each i = 1, . . . , k, let Si denote the collection of all approval
sets pierced by xi . Let Li denote the leftmost left endpoint among sets in Si
(i = 1, . . . , k) and let Ri denote the rightmost right endpoint among sets in Si .
Since each set in Si is pierced by xi , then Li and Ri are each within distance p
from xi . Therefore, Ri − Li ≤ 2p < k1 . So, the union of all approval sets in each Si
is a closed interval of length strictly less than k1 .
     This means that the union of all sets in S is a union of closed intervals whose
total length is strictly less than k k1 = 1, which implies that not all points on the
circle are covered by the approval sets of S. Therefore, S is essentially a linear
society: We can choose a point x not contained in any approval set; then, we can
“cut” the spectrum at x and “unroll” it to produce a linear society. Note that
choosing a different point x may have the effect of a cyclic permutation of the
approval sets in the S, but does not affect the results of this theorem. We treat S
as a linear society in the remainder of the proof.
     For general collections of arbitrary sets, the task of finding minimum pierc-
ing sets is computationally hard. However, for linear societies, there is a simple
linear-time algorithm that finds a minimum piercing set [7, Section 1.4]: Start by
ordering the approval sets by their right endpoints. Find the approval set with the
leftmost right endpoint, r, and add r into the piercing set; then, remove all approval
sets pierced by r. Repeat with the remaining sets until all sets are pierced. The
collection of all right endpoints chosen in this way is a minimum piercing set. In
addition, the approval sets that correspond to these right endpoints are pairwise
disjoint.

4     The telescope method
Our proofs of the above claims are based on a common set of basic methods
set out by the following lemmas. Based on the coboundary condition given
by Propp in [Pro21, Proposition 2.4] for 0-mesic statistics, we give a simple
generalization to handle the c-mesic situation with no conditions on our func-
tion f (Propp uses g for the same purpose). A similar technique is employed
by Defant, Hopkins, Poznanović, and Propp in [DHPP23] to prove homomesy
results where they note the use of a coboundary condition which does not ex-
actly match the one given by Propp. We formalize the way this comes about
in Remark 4.2.
