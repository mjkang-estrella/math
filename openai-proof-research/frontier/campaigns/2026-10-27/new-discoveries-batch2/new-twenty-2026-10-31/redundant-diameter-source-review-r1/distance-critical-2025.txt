Abstract
In 1971, Graham and Pollak provided a formula for the determinant of the distance
matrix of any tree on n vertices. Yan and Yeh reproved this by exploiting the fact that
pendant vertices can be deleted from trees without changing the remaining entries
of the distance matrix. Considering failures of their argument to generalize invites
the question: which graphs have the property that deleting any one vertex results in a
change to some pairwise distance? Such worst-case graphs are known as “distance crit-
ical”, and were first studied by Erdős and Howorka. This work explores the structural
properties of distance critical graphs, preservation of distance-criticality by products,
and the nature of extremal distance critical graphs. We end with a few open questions.

Keywords Distance Critical · Graphs · Determining Pair · Distance Matrices


1 Introduction

Graham and Pollak ( [1]) proved the following remarkable result. Given a graph G,
define its “distance matrix” D(G) to be the n ×n matrix whose (v, w) ∈ V (T )× V (T )
entry is the ordinary graph distance between v and w in G.
Theorem 1 For a tree T , det(D(T )) = −(n − 1)(−2)n−2 .
   In particular, they showed that the determinant of a tree’s distance matrix depends
only on its number of vertices. This result spawned several generalizations and new
directions in subsequent years; see [2] for an extensive survey on the topic.
   Some of this research is concerned with finding new proofs of the Graham-Pollak
Theorem. One such example that largely inspired the present work is [3], providing
an elegant reproof that relies on the fact that deleting pendant vertices from a tree
causes all remaining pairwise distances to remain unchanged. In order to consider the
limits of their methods, it is natural to ask which graphs have the property that no

2 Preliminaries

We begin with several useful definitions.

Definition 1 Given a graph G = (V (G), E(G)), the distance dG (x, y) between two
vertices x, y ∈ V (G) is the length of the shortest path whose endvertices are x and y,
or ∞ if there is no such path. A shortest path between two vertices is called a geodesic
path.

Definition 2 A graph G is distance critical if, for each v ∈ V (G), there exist x, y ∈
G − v so that dG (x, y) = dG−v (x, y).

   Distance-critical graphs are those in which every vertex is on the unique geodesic
determined by some pair of vertices not containing it. Each vertex v is “between” some
other pair of vertices in a strong sense: every shortest path between the pair passes
through v. Thus, this definition is closely related to (open) geodomination and graph
convexity; see [5] for an extensive exploration of these topics.
   For an example of a distance critical graph, see Fig. 1. Below, we present a useful
reformulation of distance criticality that draws on the notion of “determining pairs”.

4 Graph Products

In this section, we investigate the effect of graph products on distance-criticality. The
following three types of products are standard.

Definition 4 Let G and H be graphs.
1. The Cartesian product GH is a graph such that V (GH ) = V (G) × V (H ).
   Two vertices (x, y) and (x , y ) are adjacent in GH if and only if either
     • x = x and y ∼ y or
     • y = y and x ∼ x .
2. The tensor product G × H is a graph such that V (G × H ) = V (G) × V (H ),
   with two vertices (x, y) and (x , y ) adjacent in G × H if and only if x ∼ x and
   y∼y.
3. The strong product G  H is a graph such that V (G  H ) = V (G) × V (H ), with
   two distinct vertices (x, y) and (x , y ) adjacent in G  H if and only if
     • x = x or x ∼ x and
     • y = y or y ∼ y .

Proposition 5 Let G and H be graphs. Then
1. GH is distance critical if G or H is distance critical.
2. G × H is distance critical if both G and H are distance critical.
3. G  H is distance critical if both G and H are distance critical.

(a, c) is nonadjacent to (b, d) because a and b are nonadjacent in G.

6 Open Questions

Open questions abound. We end by presenting a few particularly interesting ones.
   First, although Theorem 12 can be used to obtain a lower bound on the number of
distance critical graphs, we were unable to determine if this bound is tight.

Question 1 hat fraction of graphs are distance critical?

   Next, a vertex set S of G is said to be “convex” if, for all v, w ∈ S, every u − v
geodesic is contained in S. Cliques in a graph are convex, but the converse is not
true. We may define the convexity number con(G) to be the maximum cardinality of
a proper convex subset of V (G). Clearly, ω(G) ≤ con(G) ≤ n − 1, and it is not hard
to see that any distance critical graph has con(G) ≤ n − 2. (See [5] for more on this
parameter.) In analogy to Theorem 11, we may ask the following.

Question 2 hat is the maximum value of con(G) over all distance-critical graphs G on
n vertices?

   Finally, the property of distance criticality is easily generalized to finite metric
spaces, but the present work did not investigate which of the above results translate
easily to that setting, or which ones fail to generalize. So, we offer the following
open-ended question as well.

Question 3 et (X , ρ) be a finite metric space with the property that the induced metric
space on X − v for any v ∈ X disagrees with ρ on at least one pair of points. What
can be said about such spaces?
