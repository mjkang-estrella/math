17:                                                                                    Abstract
18:                                                        We study a variation of Nim-type subtraction games, called Cu-
19:                                                    mulative Subtraction (CS). Two players alternate in removing pebbles
20:                                                    out of a joint pile, and their actions add or remove points to a common
21:                                                    score. We prove that the zero-sum outcome in optimal play of a CS
22:                                                    with a finite number of possible actions is eventually periodic, with
23:                                                    period 2s, where s is the size of the largest available action. This set-
24:                                                    tles a conjecture by Stewart in his Ph.D. thesis (2011). Specifically, we
25:                                                    find a quadratic bound, in the size of s, on when the outcome function
26:                                                    must have become periodic. In case of exactly two possible actions,
27:                                                    we give an explicit description of optimal play.

49:     In this paper we study generalizations of this game, called cumulative
50: subtraction (CS). CS is related to the famous game of nim. It has similar
51: type of moves, but a different winning condition. We restrict attention to
52: games with a single heap and a common finite action set of size at least 2.
53: Definition 1 (cumulative subtraction). An instance of cumulative
54: subtraction (CS), (S, x, p), is composed of a finite action set S, where
55: |S| ≥ 2, a heap of x ∈ Z≥0 pebbles, and a current score p. In case p = 0, the
56: game is also denoted by (S, x), and when the heap size is generic, the game
57: is simply called S (e.g. S is viewed as a ruleset). It is a two player game,
58: and the two players Positive and Negative take turns moving. A position is
59: denoted by (x, p). A Positive’s move is of the form (x, p) 7→ (x − s, p + s),
60: for some s ∈ S, provided that x − s ≥ 0. A Negative’s move is of the form
61: (x, p) 7→ (x − s, p − s), for some s ∈ S, provided that x − s ≥ 0. A position
62: (t, pt ) is terminal if t < min S. The result of a game is the terminal score pt .
63:     We are interested in optimal play of CS, which is a zero-sum game, where
64: Positive is the ‘maximizer’ and Negative is the ‘minimizer’. Optimal play is
65: reflected in the outcome function.

97:     A sequence (xi ) is periodic if there is a p such that, for all i, xi = xi+p . If
98: p is the smallest such number, then the sequence is periodic with period p.
99: 
100: Definition 5 (Eventual periodicity). A function g : Z≥0 → Z is eventually
101: periodic, if there is a p ∈ Z>0 , such that g(x) = g(x + p), for all sufficiently
102: large x ∈ Z. It is eventually periodic with period p0 , if p0 is the smallest p
103: such that g(x) = g(x + p0 ), for all sufficiently large x ∈ Z.

105:     Because of our convention that Positive starts, the relative number of
106: actions that players will have throughout the game is: either Positive and
107: Negative play the same number of actions, or Positive has an extra turn.

109: Definition 6 (Greedy action and sacrifice). Consider a game (S, x). A
110: greedy action is max{s ∈ S | s ≤ x}, and a sacrifice is any action that
111: is not greedy.

114: in the first paragraph, the answer is no.

116: Example 1. Consider the game (S = {2, 3}, x = 7). The optimal action is

118: That is, Positive’s optimal strategy is a sacrifice, taking 2 on her first action.

132: Our main result (see Section 3) is that all CS games converge (to the max-
133: imum action), and thus the outcome of any CS is eventually periodic. The
134: results are
135: 
136:     1. In Theorem 3 we give an upper bound on the convergence of any CS
137:        game. The bound is quadratic in max S.
138: 
139:     2. In Corollary 5 we prove that any game S, is eventually periodic with
140:        period 2 max S. That is, o(x + 2 max S) = o(x), for any large enough
141:        position x. This is a proof of a conjecture by Stewart from [4].
142: 
143:     3. In Theorem 6, we fully solve the case where all actions up to max S are
144:        permitted (the case of full support).
145: 
146:     4. In Theorem 12 we describe explicitly the optimal play for the class of
147:        games with exactly two actions, and in Corollary 13 we specify the
148:        corresponding outcome. In Corollary 14 we give an explicit formula for
149:        convergence.
150: 
151:     5. Section 6 concerns so-called truncated games (small actions are cut
152:        out). We were not able to solve the whole class, but we guide the
153:        readers towards a thrilling conjecture, Conjecture 16.

18:                                                                                1. Introduction
19:                                                We assume our matroids are finite throughout the paper. A matroid is modular
20:                                            if every pair of flats of the matroid is modular, and hypermodular if every pair
21:                                            of maximal proper flats is modular. Modularity and hypermodularity of a matroid
22:                                            both are equal in rank 3, but different in higher ranks. Kantor made a conjecture
23:                                            about the modular extensions of hypermodular matroids [Kan74], and Bonin later
24:                                            rephrased it [Bon11].
25:                                            Conjecture 1.1 (Kantor’s Conjecture 1974). Every matroid that is hypermodular
26:                                            is strongly embeddable in a modular matroid.
27:                                            Conjecture 1.2 (Hypermodular Matroid Conjecture 2011). Every hypermodular
28:                                            matroid is a restriction of a modular matroid of the same rank.
29:                                               A matroid M is called sticky if for every two matroids M1 and M2 with M =
30:                                            M1 |E(M) = M2 |E(M) there is a matroid M̃ with M1 = M̃ |E(M1 ) and M2 = M̃ |E(M2 )
31:                                            where E(N ) denotes the ground set of N . In 1982, Poljak and Turzík [PT82] showed
32:                                            that every modular matroid is a sticky matroid and conjectured the converse holds.
33:                                            Conjecture 1.3 (Sticky Matroid Conjecture 1982). Stickiness of a matroid implies
34:                                            modularity. Thus, stickiness is equivalent to modularity.
35:                                              If Kantor’s conjecture holds in rank 4, the sticky matroid conjecture holds due to
36:                                            an argument of Bachem, Kern, and Bonin, [BK88, Bon11]. Recently, Hochstättler
37:                                            and Wilhelmi [HW19] showed the sticky matroid conjecture and Kantor’s conjecture
38:                                            both are equivalent. In this paper, we prove Kantor’s conjecture in rank 4 and hence
39:                                            both the sticky matroid conjecture and the whole Kantor’s conjecture.
40:                                            Notations. For a matroid M , unless otherwise stated, r denotes its rank function
41:                                            while r(M ) denotes its rank. For a positive integer n, we denote [n] := {1, . . . , n}.
42:                                            We denote by A ⊔ B the union of two disjoint sets A and B.
