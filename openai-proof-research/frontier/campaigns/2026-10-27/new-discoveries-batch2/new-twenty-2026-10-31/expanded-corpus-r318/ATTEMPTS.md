## 357394: reversal, two failed direct inductions, then an exact public proof

Dekking's sequence x is indexed by nonnegative integers and satisfies x(3n)=x(2n), x(3n+1)=1-x(2n), x(3n+2)=x(2n). A literal finite factor is a consecutive interval of x. The target is closure of the language under reversal, with no bounded-length restriction.

First route: extend a factor to complete length-three blocks, desubstitute to consecutive length-two blocks, and reverse its preimage. This fails algebraically: kappa(01)=010 but kappa(reverse(01))=101, whereas reverse(kappa(01))=010. Thus complement closure, which commutes with kappa, supplies no reversal identity. This is a failure of the proposed induction, not a counterexample to the target.

Second route: the outputs a(1-a)a are palindromes. Reverse the sequence of output blocks and seek it as another output segment. The controlling input symbols are x(2j), not arbitrary consecutive x(j). Reversal of a consecutive preimage does not preserve its selection at even positions. Need a parity-sensitive occurrence theorem or an explicit symbolic conjugacy; neither follows merely from complement closure or occurrence somewhere in x. This precisely identifies the gap hidden by a naive palindromic-block argument.

The current companion closes this gap publicly. In arXiv:2602.21895v1 (25 February 2026), Section1.4 defines t-prime by exactly the unnormalized digit-sum rule, and Lemma4 proves x=t-prime=phi(t), phi(0)=010, phi(1)=101. Here t is the DIFFERENT normalized-base sibling, so a scope bridge is indispensable. Read Lemma4's block regrouping proof: g0(a)=a(1-a)a, g1(a)=empty; regrouping length-three output blocks produces f0(a)=aa and f1(a)=1-a on the sibling. The source's Proposition14 supplies both-parity occurrences; Proposition17's full first proof reverses alternating morphism prefixes using that occurrence theorem. The source also gives a numeration-tree proof; its initial explanation and Figure6 textual account were inspected, but no image was rendered.

The complete final reduction is short: any factor w of x lies in phi(u) for a finite factor u of t. Proposition17 says reverse(u) occurs in t. Since both images under phi are palindromes, reverse(phi(u))=phi(reverse(u)), which occurs in x. Reverse(w), a factor of this reversed block, therefore occurs. All lengths and occurrence positions are covered; no frequency or uniform-recurrence assertion is needed for this final bridge. This is a public solution, zero credit. No formalization plan is appropriate.

## 362481: path extremality in the cordiality game

For a completed Admirable vertex set A, put b(A)=number of edges crossing A and its complement. Since e1=b(A) and e0=m-b(A), terminal payoff is |2b(A)-m|. Admirable minimizes, Impish maximizes, alternating legal vertex selections, with Admirable starting for c_g. This payoff is not the signed balance-game value. Negating labels preserves the completed payoff but does not silently change who moves first.

Route one was branch removal: follow an optimal strategy on a smaller tree, answering an opponent's move within the same even branch. The source's Theorem3.1 addresses pass/turn-order issues and gives n/2. To prove comparison with P_n, a recursive bound on the ACTUAL path minimax value is needed, not its displayed upper bound. From c_g(T)<=n/2 and c_g(P_n)<=approximately n/3 one cannot infer either ordering. A path upper bound cannot be used as a lower bound.

Route two was to strengthen the induction by signed branch discrepancy and boundary labels. Joining a branch to a remainder changes the signed discrepancy by the bridge contribution, +1 or -1. Taking absolute values before joining loses cancellation. Therefore a state would need both attainable signed intervals, the attachment label, starting player, remaining numbers of each label, and any allowed pass. The source's triangle-inequality estimate is insufficient to transfer the full state to a path of equal order. No such simulation is proved.

As a hand calculation check, a star K_(1,n-1) has a terminal cut size equal to the number of leaves opposite its center. For even n=2r both label classes have size r; either center label gives b=r and payoff1. For odd n=2r+1 the center label gives b=r or r+1 and payoff0 or2; Admirable can take the center initially for payoff0. Thus a high degree alone does not generate the required counterexample.

## 365686: cycle-neighborhood coloring and planar extension

Write D_i for color class i in a four-color map on vertices; source use of arbitrary maps permits empty classes. D_i is cycle isolating iff every cycle Q has a vertex in N[D_i]. By symmetry of undirected adjacency, this is equivalent to N[V(Q)] intersecting D_i. Thus the exact goal is a coloring such that the closed neighborhood of EVERY cycle contains every color. This includes non-induced cycles; restricting checks to facial cycles is unjustified.

First route: proper four-coloring. It does not imply the needed property. Color a four-cycle alternately1,2, and attach a path of four new vertices at a color1 vertex, colored2,1,3,4 in order away from the cycle. This is a proper four-coloring of a connected planar graph using all colors, yet the closed neighborhood of the original cycle uses only1,2. This refutes only the proof mechanism. Another coloring of that graph can work.

Second route: leaf and block induction. If a leaf v is added to an already four-cycle-isolating colored H, any color at v works: all cycles are in H and their closed neighborhoods only increase. If G is obtained by gluing graphs G_j at a single common vertex u, each G_j already admits such a coloring, and no edges connect distinct G_j-u, permute palettes so that all colorings agree at u. Every simple cycle is in one G_j, where all four required neighborhood colors remain present. Hence the glued coloring works. This does not handle a triangle block: K3 itself cannot expose four colors on its closed neighborhood. In the full graph an external neighbor may supply the fourth color, but that is a boundary constraint, not an application of the gluing lemma. Consequently this argument does not give all connected planar graphs.

Third route: extend across a low-degree vertex v. Given D_i in H=G-v, F_i=H-N_H[D_i] is a forest. If i is neither v's color nor a color present among neighbors of v, then v survives in G-N_G[D_i]. To avoid creating a cycle, its surviving neighbors must lie in distinct components of F_i: if two lie in one component, the unique forest path plus their edges to v creates a cycle; conversely any new cycle must arise this way. If a neighbor has color i, v is removed and no new cycle using it arises; if v itself has color i, it is also removed. Thus each color absent at N(v) with a repeated residual component is a constraint that v itself must receive that color. Two distinct such constraints cannot be solved just by coloring v. This is an exact obstruction to a greedy extension, including at degree at most5. A simultaneous recoloring lemma or reducible-configuration discharging proof is missing.

The source's Lemma3.4 and minimal-counterexample proof use claw-free subcubic structure to dispose of crossings and special cycles. Their hypotheses cannot be dropped because the graph is planar. Planar degeneracy alone does not settle the residual-forest constraints. No global extension theorem or counterexample is proved.

## 364981: exact prism reduction, without an unjustified exclusion

For any finite simple H, H square K2 is interval colorable iff H has a near-interval coloring, where at each vertex the incident colors form an interval or an interval with one missing integer. Here degree-zero spectra can be handled separately and shifts make all colors positive.

Forward: restrict an interval coloring of the prism to one H-layer. Exactly one incident rung edge is removed at each vertex. Deleting one color from an interval leaves an interval (if an endpoint was removed) or an interval with a single internal gap. Properness remains.

Backward: use the same near-interval coloring in both copies of H. For each vertex v with an internal missing color, color its rung by that integer. If its spectrum is already an interval, color the rung by one above its maximum. The two copies have identical spectra at v; the added rung completes or extends each to an interval. Distinct rungs have no common endpoint and cause no conflicts. Isolated vertices may receive a fresh positive rung color. Shift all colors if needed. If the construction leaves global unused integer gaps, compress the ordered used colors: incident intervals remain intervals since any missing global color belonged to no vertex spectrum. The result is an interval coloring.

This proof identifies an honest remaining ordinary target: all outerplanar graphs must have near-interval edge colorings. The source proves relevant special cases but explicitly leaves this question. The both-large-factors theorem is not the complete conjecture, and the prism equivalence supplies no universal outerplanar coloring algorithm. Nothing is excluded for being short or classical-looking.

## Public cube counterexample 357858

Example4.2 of the June2024 source already constructs the family. Attach2305 leaves to a vertex of Q9. Leaves preserve medianity: paths from a leaf to the old graph pass through its attachment, while the unique median for old triples is unchanged. No induced cube of dimension>=2 contains a degree-one vertex. Thus the cube polynomial is (x+2)^9+2305(x+1). Its first coefficients are2817,4609,4608,5376.
