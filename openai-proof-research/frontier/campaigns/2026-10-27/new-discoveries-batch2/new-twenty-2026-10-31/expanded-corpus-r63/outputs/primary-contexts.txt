315: Conditional mutual information (CMI) has recently attracted significant attention as a key quantity for characterizing quantum correlations in many-body systems. While it is conjectured that CMI decays rapidly in finite-temperature Gibbs states, a complete and general proof remains elusive.
316: In this work, we introduce a new formulation of the problem based on the belief propagation (BP) channel, namely a completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) map that realizes local perturbations of the Hamiltonian.
317: Within this framework, we prove that establishing the quasi-locality of BP channels implies the decay of CMI, thereby reducing the original conjecture to a more tractable problem.
318: We show that such quasi-local BP channels can be constructed under natural physical conditions, such as uniform rapid mixing or uniform clustering.
319: Under these assumptions, we obtain conditional proofs of CMI decay valid at all temperatures. Moreover, because these assumptions are automatically satisfied at high temperatures, our results in that regime yield unconditional proofs of CMI decay.
320: At the same time, in order to better understand the high-temperature behavior of Gibbs states, we revisit the cluster expansion method.
321: Contrary to common intuition, we demonstrate that when multipartite correlations such as CMI are considered, the cluster expansion suffers from intrinsic divergence problems rooted in the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula, revealing fundamental limitations of this traditional approach.

2582: 4 How to prove Conjecture 2?
2583: 
2584: 
2585: 
2586: 
2587: It is easy to see that Conjecture 2 is a strengthening of the Four Color Theorem (indeed, it is well-known and straightforward to prove
2588: that a minimal counterexample to the Four Color Theorem would have minimum degree five and no separating triangles). Up to some minor variations, we know
2589: essentially only one proof of the Four Color Theorem: Using Kempe chains, prove that many configurations in planar graphs are reducible, i.e.,
2590: cannot appear in a minimal counterexample, then use discharging to show that at least one of them appears in every planar graph.

If there is a mapping  \phi:E(G)\rightarrow E(H) , such that for each  v\in V(G)  there is  w\in V(H)  such that  \phi(\partial_{G}(v))=\partial_{H}(w) , then  \phi  is called an  H -coloring of  G . If  G  admits an  H -coloring, then we will write  H\prec G . It can be easily seen that if  H\prec G  and  K\prec H , then  K\prec G . In other words,  \prec  is a transitive relation defined on the set of cubic graphs.

234: The four-color theorem has been the driving force behind most developments in graph theory. It is simple, beautiful, and can be stated in numerous ways, each of which offers a different insight and a different direction of study. One of the earliest reformulation, proposed by P.G. Tait in 1884 [10], led to the development of theory of (nowhere zero) flows and established connection with the Hamiltonicity of cubic bridgeless planar graphs. More precisely, Tait conjectured that every  3 -connected cubic planar graph is Hamiltonian, noting that such cubic graphs are, in particular,  3 -edge-colorable. Prior to the conjecture, he had presented the first reformulation of the four-color conjecture: every cubic bridgeless planar graph is  3 -edge-colorable.
237: 
238: 
239: It took about six decades until W. Tutte [11] disproved this strong conjecture by building a  3 -connected cubic planar graph which is not Hamiltonian. Tutte’s example was composed of three identical pieces, which are now called Tutte’s fragment.
240: 
241: 
242: 
243: 
244: The dual of Tutte’s fragment is known as Wenger’s graph and is used by various authors to disprove various potential extensions or relaxations of the four-color theorem. Wenger, in [12], used this gadget to build a planar graph which cannot be partitioned into a bipartite graph and a forest. In this work we similarly use Wenger’s gadget to build some examples of planar (signed) graphs whose fractional balanced chromatic number and whose fractional arboricity is strictly larger than 2. To that end we should first introduce these two notions.

286: Many deep problems in graph theory, including
287: celebrated long-standing conjectures about cycles, perfect
288: matchings, and flows, can be reduced to cubic graphs. The
289: validity of these conjectures often depends upon a fairly
290: narrow class of  2 -connected cubic graphs that do not admit a
291: proper  3 -edge-colouring, the snarks. In this paper, we
292: focus on properties of snarks that can be expressed in terms of
293: perfect matchings and cycles. In particular, we deal with two
294: important conjectures, the Berge conjecture about the size of a
295: perfect-matching cover and the  7/5 -conjecture about the
296: length of a shortest cycle cover.

301: Berge’s conjecture. According to a stronger form of
302: Petersen’s perfect matching theorem every edge of a bridgeless cubic graph
303: belongs to a perfect matching. It follows that the edges of any
304: bridgeless cubic graph  G  can be covered with a collection of
305: perfect matchings. The minimum number of perfect matchings
306: needed to cover the edges of  G  is called the perfect
307: matching index of  G , denoted by  \pi(G) . Berge’s conjecture
308: (circa 1970) suggests that  \pi(G)\leq 5  for every bridgeless
309: cubic graph  G . However, neither a constant upper bound for
310:  \pi(G)  nor an example with  \pi(G)>5  are currently known.
311:

314: 
315: The 7/5-conjecture. In 1985, Alon and Tarsi [1],
316: and independently Jaeger [25], conjectured that
317: every bridgeless graph can have its edges covered with a
318: collection of cycles of total length at most  7/5\cdot m  where
319:  m  is the number of edges. This conjecture is surprisingly
320: strong because, if true, it would imply the validity of the
321: cycle double cover conjecture (Jamshy and Tarsi [13]). The
322: conjecture is best possible because it is attained for the
323: Petersen graph. Fleischner’s splitting lemma
324: [10, Lemma III.26] implies that the  7/5 -conjecture
325: reduces to bridgeless subcubic graphs. However, as with other
326: conjectures concerning cycles and flows, the case of cubic
327: graphs appears to be crucial. Observe that every cycle cover of
328: a cubic graph must have length at least  4/3\cdot m , because
329: every vertex is incident with at least one doubly covered edge.
330: This lower bound is reached by  3 -edge-colourable cubic
331: graphs, where any two bi-coloured  2 -factors provide a cycle
332: cover of length  4/3\cdot m . For snarks, the conjecture
333: remains widely open. It is known, however, that every snark
334: with perfect matching index  4  admits a cycle cover of length
335:  4/3\cdot m  (see Steffen [27, Theorem 3.1]). Potential
336: counterexamples to the  7/5 -conjecture may thus occur only
337: among snarks with perfect matching index at least  5 .
