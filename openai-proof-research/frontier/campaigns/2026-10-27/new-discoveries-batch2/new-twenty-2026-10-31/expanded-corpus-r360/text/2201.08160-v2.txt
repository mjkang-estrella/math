Abstract
A mixed graph is called second kind hermitian integral(or HS-integral) if the eigenvalues of its Hermitian-adjacency matrix of second kind are integers. A mixed graph is called Eisenstein integral if the eigenvalues of its (0, 1)-adjacency matrix are Eisenstein integers. A mixed Cayley graph Cay(\Gamma,S) is called normal if S is the union of some conjugacy classes of the group \Gamma. We characterize the set S for which a normal mixed Cayley graph \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) is HS-integral. We also show that that every Eisenstein integral normal mixed Cayley graph is an HS-integral. We purpose a conjecture to prove its converse. In short, this paper extends almost all the results of Kadyan-Bhattarcharjya to normal mixed Cayley graphs.
Keywords. integral graphs; HS-integral mixed graph; Eisenstein integral mixed graph; normal mixed Cayley graph. 
Mathematics Subject Classifications: 05C50, 05C25
1 Introduction
A mixed graph G is a pair (V(G),E(G)), where V(G) and E(G) are the vertex set and the edge set of G, respectively. Here E(G)\subseteq V(G)\times V(G)\setminus\{(u,u)~|~u\in V(G)\}. If G is a mixed graph, then (u,v)\in E(G) need not imply that (v,u)\in E(G). An edge (u,v) of a mixed graph G is called undirected if both (u,v) and (v,u) belong to E(G). An edge (u,v) of a mixed graph G is called directed if (u,v)\in E(G) but (v,u)\notin E(G). A mixed graph can have both undirected and directed edges. A mixed graph G is said to be a simple graph if all the edges of G are undirected. A mixed graph G is said to be an oriented graph if all the edges of G are directed.
For a mixed graph G on n vertices, its (0, 1)-adjacency matrix and Hermitian-adjacency matrix of the second kind are denoted by \mathcal{A}(G)=(a_{uv})_{n\times n} and \mathcal{H}(G)=(h_{uv})_{n\times n}, respectively, where
a_{uv}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}1&\mbox{ if }(u,v)\in E\\
0&\textnormal{ otherwise,}\end{array}\right.~~~~~\text{ and }~~~~~~h_{uv}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1&\mbox{ if }(u,v)\in E\textnormal{ and }(v,u)\in E\\
\frac{1+i\sqrt{3}}{2}&\mbox{ if }(u,v)\in E\textnormal{ and }(v,u)\not\in E\\
\frac{1-i\sqrt{3}}{2}&\mbox{ if }(u,v)\not\in E\textnormal{ and }(v,u)\in E\\
0&\textnormal{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.
The Hermitian-adjacency matrix of the second kind was introduced by Bojan Mohar [26].
Let G be a mixed graph. By an HS-eigenvalue of G, we mean an eigenvalue of \mathcal{H}(G). By an eigenvalue of G, we mean an eigenvalue of \mathcal{A}(G). Similarly, the HS-spectrum of G, denoted Sp_{H}(G), is the multi-set of the HS-eigenvalues of G, and the spectrum of G, denoted Sp_{A}(G), is the multi-set of the eigenvalues of G. Note that the Hermitian-adjacency matrix of the second kind of a mixed graph is a Hermitian matrix, and so its HS-eigenvalues are real numbers. However, if a mixed graph G contains at least one directed edge, then \mathcal{A}(G) is non-symmetric. Accordingly, the eigenvalues of G need not be real numbers. The matrix obtained by replacing \frac{1+i\sqrt{3}}{2} and \frac{1-i\sqrt{3}}{2} by i and -i, respectively, in \mathcal{H}(G), is called the Hermitian adjacency matrix of G. Hermitian adjacency matrix of mixed graphs was introduced in [13, 24].
A mixed graph is called H-integral if the eigenvalues of its Hermitian adjacency matrix are integers. A mixed graph G is said to be HS-integral if all the HS-eigenvalues of G are integers. A mixed graph G is said to be Eisenstein integral if all the eigenvalues of G are Eisenstein integers. Note that complex numbers of the form a+b\omega_{3}, where a,b\in\mathbb{Z},\omega_{3}=\frac{-1+i\sqrt{3}}{2}, are called Eisenstein integers. An HS-integral simple graph is called an integral graph. Note that \mathcal{A}(G)=\mathcal{H}(G) for a simple graph G. Therefore in case of a simple graph G, the terms HS-eigenvalue, HS-spectrum and HS-integrality of G are the same with that of eigenvalue, spectrum and integrality of G, respectively.
Integrality of simple graphs have been extensively studied in the past. Integral graphs were first defined by Harary and Schwenk [14] in 1974 and proposed a classification of integral graphs. See [4] for a survey on integral graphs. Watanabe and Schwenk [32, 33] proved several interesting results on integral trees in 1979. Csikvari [10] constructed integral trees with arbitrary large diameters in 2010. Further research on integral trees can be found in [7, 6, 30, 31]. In 2009, Ahmadi et al. [2] proved that only a fraction of 2^{-\Omega(n)} of the graphs on n vertices have an integral spectrum. Bussemaker et al. [8] proved that there are exactly 13 connected cubic integral graphs. Stevanović [29] studied the 4-regular integral graphs avoiding \pm 3 in the spectrum, and Lepović et al. [22] proved that there are 93 non-regular, bipartite integral graphs with maximum degree four. In 2017, Guo et. al. [13] found all possible mixed graphs on n vertices with spectrum \{-n+1,-1,-1,...,-1\}.
Throughout the paper, we consider \Gamma to be a finite group. Let S be a subset, not containing the identity element, of \Gamma. The set S is said to be symmetric (resp. skew-symmetric) if S is closed under inverse (resp. a^{-1}\not\in S for all a\in S). Define \overline{S}=\{u\in S:u^{-1}\not\in S\}. Clearly, S\setminus\overline{S} is symmetric and \overline{S} is skew-symmetric. The mixed Cayley graph G=Cay(\Gamma,S) is a mixed graph, where V(G)=\Gamma and E(G)=\{(a,b):a,b\in\Gamma,ba^{-1}\in S\}. If S is symmetric then G is a simple Cayley graph. If S is skew-symmetric then G is an oriented Cayley graph. A mixed Cayley graph Cay(\Gamma,S) is called normal if S is the union of some conjugacy classes of the group \Gamma.
If every element of a group is conjugate to its inverse, the group is said to be ambivalent. All symmetric groups S_{n} are ambivalent, as is widely known.
The alternating groups A_{n} are ambivalent only for n\in\{1,2,5,6,10,14\}. It is clear that all normal mixed Cayley graphs over an ambivalent group are undirected. Thus, in order to examine the integrality of normal mixed Cayley graph with at least one directed edge, we must look at non-ambivalent groups. A basic example of a non-ambivalent group is an abelian group.
In 1982, Bridge and Mena [5] introduced a characterization of integral Cayley graphs over abelian groups. Later on, same characterization was rediscovered by Wasin So [27] for cyclic groups in 2005. In 2009, Abdollahi and Vatandoost [1] proved that there are exactly seven connected cubic integral Cayley graphs. On the same year, Klotz and Sander [20] proved that if Cayley graph Cay(\Gamma,S) over an abelian group \Gamma is integral then S belongs to the Boolean algebra \mathbb{B}(\Gamma) generated by the subgroups of \Gamma. Moreover, they conjectured that the converse is also true, which was proved by Alperin and Peterson [3]. In 2014, Cheng et al. [21] proved that normal Cayley graphs over symmetric groups are integral. In 2017, Lu et al. [25] gave necessary and sufficient condition for the integrality of Cayley graphs over the dihedral group D_{n}=\langle a,b|a^{n}=b^{2}=1,bab=a^{-1}\rangle. In particular, they completely determined all integral Cayley graphs over the dihedral group D_{p} for a prime p. In 2019, Cheng et al.[9] obtained several simple sufficient conditions for the integrality of Cayley graphs over the dicyclic group T_{4n}=\langle a,b|a^{2n}=1,a^{n}=b^{2},b^{-1}ab=a^{-1}\rangle. In particular, they also completely determined all integral Cayley graphs over the dicyclic group T_{4p} for a prime p.
H-integrality of mixed Cayley graphs over abelian groups have been discussed in [18] and [19]. HS-integrability and Eisenstein integrability of mixed Cayley graphs over abelian groups have been discussed in [16] and [17]. In 2014, Godsil et al. [12] characterised integral normal Cayley graphs. In this paper, we characterise the HS-integrality and Eisenstein integrality of normal mixed Cayley graphs in terms of their connection set.
The following is how this document is structured. Some preliminary notions and results are addressed in Section 2. We express the HS-eigenvalues of a normal mixed Cayley graph as the sum of the HS-eigenvalues of a simple Cayley graph and an oriented Cayley graph. In Section 3, we characterise the HS-integrality of normal oriented Cayley graphs in terms of symbol set. In Section 4, we extend this characterisation to normal mixed Cayley graphs. In the last section, we discuss the Eisenstein integrality of normal mixed Cayley graphs.
2 Preliminaries
Let \Gamma be a finite group and g,h\in\Gamma. If g=x^{-1}hx for some x\in\Gamma then we say that h is a conjugate of g. The set of all conjugates of g in \Gamma is called the conjugacy class of g and is denoted by Cl(g). Define C_{\Gamma}(g) to be the set of all elements of \Gamma that commute with g. We denote the group algebra of \Gamma over the field \mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3}) by \mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3})\Gamma. That is, \mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3})\Gamma is the set of all sums \sum\limits_{g\in\Gamma}c_{g}g, where c_{g}\in\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3}) and we assume 1.g=g to have \Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3})\Gamma.
A representation of a finite group \Gamma is a homomorphism \rho:\Gamma\to GL(V) for some finite-dimensional vector space V over the complex field \mathbb{C}, where GL(V) denotes the group of automorphisms of V. The dimension of V is called the degree of \rho. Two representations \rho_{1} and \rho_{2} of \Gamma on V_{1} and V_{2}, respectively are equivalent if there is an isomorphism T:V_{1}\to V_{2} such that T\rho_{1}(g)=\rho_{2}(g)T for all g\in\Gamma.
Let \rho:\Gamma\to GL(V) be a representation. The character \chi_{\rho}:\Gamma\to\mathbb{C} of \rho is defined by setting \chi_{\rho}(g)=Tr(\rho(g)) for g\in\Gamma, where Tr(\rho(g)) is the trace of the matrix of \rho(g) with respect to a basis of V. By the degree of \chi_{\rho}, we mean the degree of \rho, which is simply \chi_{\rho}(1). If W is a \rho(g)-invariant subspace of V for each g\in\Gamma, then we call W a \rho(\Gamma)-invariant subspace of V. If the \rho(\Gamma)-invariant subspaces of V are \{0\} and V only, then we call \rho an irreducible representation of \Gamma, and the corresponding character \chi_{\rho} an irreducible character of \Gamma.
For a group \Gamma, we denote by \text{IRR}(\Gamma) and \text{Irr}(\Gamma) the complete set of pairwise non-equivalent irreducible representations of \Gamma and the complete set of pairwise non-equivalent irreducible characters of \Gamma, respectively. Note that \text{IRR}(\Gamma) is not necessarily unique, whereas \text{Irr}(\Gamma) is unique.
For a representation \rho:\Gamma\to GL(V) of a finite group \Gamma, define \overline{\rho}:\Gamma\to GL(V) by \overline{\rho}(g)=\overline{\rho(g)}, where \overline{\rho(g)} is the matrix whose entries are the complex conjugates of the corresponding entries of \rho(g). Note that if \rho is irreducible, then \overline{\rho} is also irreducible. Hence we have the following lemma. See Proposition 9.1.1 and Corollary 9.1.2 in [28] for details.
Lemma 2.1.
Let \Gamma be a finite group, \text{Irr}(\Gamma)=\{\chi_{1},...,\chi_{h}\} and j\in\{1,...,h\}. Then there exists a k\in\{1,...,h\} such that \overline{\chi}_{k}=\chi_{j}, where \overline{\chi}_{k}:\Gamma\to\mathbb{C} such that \overline{\chi}_{k}(x)=\overline{\chi_{k}(x)} for all x\in\Gamma.
Let \Gamma be a finite group and \alpha:\Gamma\to\mathbb{C} be a function. The Cayley color digraph of \Gamma with connection function \alpha, denoted by \text{Cay}(\Gamma,\alpha), is defined to be the graph (with loops) having vertex set \Gamma and edge set \{(x,y):x,y\in\Gamma\} such that each edge (x,y) is colored by \alpha(yx^{-1}). The adjacency matrix of \text{Cay}(\Gamma,\alpha) is defined to be the matrix whose rows and columns are indexed by the elements of \Gamma, and the (x,y)-entry is equal to \alpha(yx^{-1}). The eigenvalues of \text{Cay}(\Gamma,\alpha) are simply the eigenvalues of its adjacency matrix.
Let w_{m}=\exp(\frac{2\pi i}{m}) be a primitive m-th root of unity. In the special case when \alpha:\Gamma\to\{0,1,\omega_{6},\omega_{6}^{5}\} with \alpha(g)=\overline{\alpha(g^{-1})} and the set S=\{g:\alpha(g)=1\text{ or }\omega_{6}\} satisfying 1\not\in S are chosen, the adjacency matrix of \text{Cay}(\Gamma,\alpha) is equal to the Hermitian-adjacency matrix of the second kind of the mixed graph \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S). Similarly, if \alpha:\Gamma\to\{0,1\} and the set S=\{g:\alpha(g)=1\} satisfying 1\not\in S are chosen, then the adjacency matrix of \text{Cay}(\Gamma,\alpha) is equal to the (0, 1)-adjacency matrix of the mixed graph \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S).
Theorem 2.2.
[11]
Let \alpha:\Gamma\to\mathbb{C} be a class function and \text{Irr}(\Gamma)=\{\chi_{1},...,\chi_{h}\}. Then the spectrum of the Cayley color digraph \text{Cay}(\Gamma,\alpha) can be arranged as \{[\lambda_{1}]^{d_{1}^{2}},...,[\lambda_{h}]^{d_{h}^{2}}\}, where
\lambda_{j}=\frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum_{x\in\Gamma}\alpha(x)\chi_{j}(x)\textnormal{ and }d_{j}=\chi_{j}(1)\textnormal{ for all }j=1,...,h.
Lemma 2.3.
Let \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) be a normal mixed Cayley graph and \text{Irr}(\Gamma)=\{\chi_{1},...,\chi_{h}\}. Then the HS-spectrum of \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) can be arranged as \{[\gamma_{1}]^{d_{1}^{2}},...,[\gamma_{h}]^{d_{h}^{2}}\}, where \gamma_{j}=\lambda_{j}+\mu_{j},
\lambda_{j}=\frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum_{s\in S\setminus\overline{S}}\chi_{j}(s),\hskip 5.69046pt\mu_{j}=\frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum_{s\in\overline{S}}(\omega_{6}\chi_{j}(s)+\omega_{6}^{5}\chi_{j}(s^{-1}))\textnormal{ and }d_{j}=\chi_{j}(1)\textnormal{ for all }j=1,...,h.
Proof.
Let \alpha:\Gamma\to\{0,1,\omega_{6},\omega_{6}^{5}\} be the function such that
\alpha(s)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}1&\mbox{if }s\in S\setminus\overline{S}\\
\omega_{6}&\mbox{if }s\in\overline{S}\\
\omega_{6}^{5}&\mbox{if }s\in\overline{S}^{-1}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.
Since S is a union of some conjugacy classes of \Gamma, \alpha is a class function. By Theorem 2.2,
\gamma_{j}=\frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\bigg(\sum_{s\in S\setminus\overline{S}}\chi_{j}(s)+\sum_{s\in\overline{S}}\omega_{6}\chi_{j}(s)+\sum_{s\in\overline{S}^{-1}}\omega_{6}^{5}\chi_{j}(s)\bigg),
and the result follows.
∎
As special cases of Lemma 2.3, we have the following two corollaries.
Corollary 2.3.1.
Let \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) be a normal simple Cayley graph and \text{Irr}(\Gamma)=\{\chi_{1},...,\chi_{h}\}. Then the spectrum (or HS-spectrum) of \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) can be arranged as \{[\lambda_{1}]^{d_{1}^{2}},...,[\lambda_{h}]^{d_{h}^{2}}\}, where
\lambda_{j}=\frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum_{s\in S}\chi_{j}(s)\textnormal{ and }d_{j}=\chi_{j}(1)\hskip 5.69046pt\textnormal{ for all }j=1,...,h.
Corollary 2.3.2.
Let \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) be a normal oriented Cayley graph and \text{Irr}(\Gamma)=\{\chi_{1},...,\chi_{h}\}. Then the HS-spectrum of \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) can be arranged as \{[\mu_{1}]^{d_{1}^{2}},...,[\mu_{h}]^{d_{h}^{2}}\}, where
\mu_{j}=\frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum_{s\in S}(\omega_{6}\chi_{j}(s)+\omega_{6}^{5}\chi_{j}(s^{-1}))\textnormal{ and }d_{j}=\chi_{j}(1)\hskip 5.69046pt\textnormal{ for all }j=1,...,h.
Let n\geq 2 be a positive integer. For a divisor d of n, define G_{n}(d)=\{k:1\leq k\leq n-1,\gcd(k,n)=d\}. It is clear that G_{n}(d)=dG_{\frac{n}{d}}(1).
Alperin and Peterson [3] considered the Boolean algebra generated by the class of subgroups of a group in order to determine the integrality of simple Cayley graphs over abelian groups. Suppose \mathcal{F}_{\Gamma} is the family of all subgroups of \Gamma. The Boolean algebra \mathbb{B}(\Gamma) generated by \mathcal{F}_{\Gamma} is the set whose elements are obtained by arbitrary finite intersections, unions, and complements of the elements in the family \mathcal{F}_{\Gamma}. The minimal non-empty elements of this algebra are called atoms. Thus each element of \mathbb{B}(\Gamma) is the union of some atoms.
Consider the equivalence relation \sim on \Gamma such that x\sim y if and only if y=x^{k} for some k\in G_{m}(1), where m=\text{ord}(x).
Lemma 2.4.
[3] The equivalence classes of \sim are the atoms of \mathbb{B}(\Gamma).
For x\in\Gamma, let [x] denote the equivalence class of x with respect to the relation \sim. Also, let \langle x\rangle denote the cyclic subgroup generated by x.
Lemma 2.5.
[3] The atoms of the Boolean algebra \mathbb{B}(\Gamma) are the sets [x]=\{y:\langle y\rangle=\langle x\rangle\}.
By Lemma 2.5, each element of \mathbb{B}(\Gamma) is a union of some sets of the form [x]=\{y:\langle y\rangle=\langle x\rangle\}. Thus for all S\in\mathbb{B}(\Gamma), we have S=[x_{1}]\cup...\cup[x_{k}] for some x_{1},...,x_{k}\in\Gamma.
Theorem 2.6.
[12]
Let \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) be a normal simple Cayley graph. Then \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) is integral if and only if S\in\mathbb{B}(\Gamma).
Let n\equiv 0\pmod{3}. For a divisor d of \frac{n}{3} and r\in\{1,2\}, define
G_{n,3}^{r}(d)=\{dk:k\equiv r\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3),\gcd(dk,n)=d\}.
It is easy to see that G_{n}(d)=G_{n,3}^{1}(d)\cup G_{n,3}^{2}(d) is a disjoint union and G_{n,3}^{r}(d)=dG_{\frac{n}{d}}^{r}(1) for r=1,2.
Define \Gamma(3) to be the set of all x\in\Gamma satisfying \text{ord}(x)\equiv 0\pmod{3}. Define an equivalence relation \approx on \Gamma(3) such that x\approx y if and only if y=x^{k} for some k\in G_{m,3}^{1}(1), where m=\text{ord}(x). Observe that if x,y\in\Gamma(3) and x\approx y then x\sim y, but the converse need not be true. For example, consider x=5\pmod{12}, y=7\pmod{12} in \mathbb{Z}_{12}. Here x,y\in\mathbb{Z}_{12}(3) and x\sim y but x\not\approx y. For x\in\Gamma(3), let \langle\!\langle x\rangle\!\rangle denote the equivalence class of x with respect to the relation \approx.
3 HS-integral normal oriented Cayley graphs
Let \text{Irr}(\Gamma)=\{\chi_{1},...,\chi_{h}\}. Let E be the matrix of size h\times n, whose rows are indexed by 1,...,h and columns are indexed by the elements of \Gamma such that E_{j,g}=\chi_{j}(g). Note that EE^{*}=nI_{h} and the rank of E is h, where E^{*} is the conjugate transpose of E.
It is well known that the Galois group Gal(\mathbb{Q}(w_{m})/\mathbb{Q})=\{\sigma_{r}:r\in G_{m}(1)\}, where \sigma_{r}(w_{m})=w_{m}^{r}. Assume that m\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3). Since \mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3},w_{m})=\mathbb{Q}(w_{m}), the Galois group Gal(\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3},w_{m})/\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3})) is a subgroup of Gal(\mathbb{Q}(w_{m})/\mathbb{Q}). Therefore, Gal(\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3},w_{m})/\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3})) contains all those automorphisms from Gal(\mathbb{Q}(w_{m})/\mathbb{Q}) that fixes \omega_{3}. Note that G_{m}(1)=G_{m,3}^{1}(1)\cup G_{m,3}^{2}(1) is a disjoint union. Using \sigma_{r}(\omega_{3})=\omega_{3} for all r\in G_{m,3}^{1}(1) and \sigma_{r}(\omega_{3})=\omega_{3}^{2} for all r\in G_{m,3}^{2}(1), we get
Gal(\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3},w_{m})/\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3}))=Gal(\mathbb{Q}(w_{m})/\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3}))=\{\sigma_{r}:r\in G_{m,3}^{1}(1)\}.
Now assume that m\not\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3). Then
[\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3},w_{m}):\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3})]=\frac{[\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3},w_{m}):\mathbb{Q}(w_{m})]\times[\mathbb{Q}(w_{m}):\mathbb{Q}]}{[\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3}):\mathbb{Q}]}=[\mathbb{Q}(w_{m}):\mathbb{Q}]=\varphi(m).
Thus the field \mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3},w_{m}) is a Galois extension of \mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3}) of degree \varphi(m). Here \varphi denotes the Euler \varphi-function. Any automorphism of the field \mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3},w_{m}) is uniquely determined by its action on w_{m}. Hence Gal(\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3},w_{m})/\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3}))=\{\tau_{r}:r\in G_{m}(1)\}, where \tau_{r}(w_{m})=w_{m}^{r}\text{ and }\tau_{r}(\omega_{3})=\omega_{3}. For z\in\mathbb{C}, let \overline{z} denote the complex conjugate of z and \Re(z) (resp. \Im(z)) denote the real part (resp. imaginary part) of the complex number z.
Theorem 3.1.
Let \Gamma be a finite group, \text{Irr}(\Gamma)=\{\chi_{1},...,\chi_{h}\}, and x=\sum\limits_{g\in\Gamma}c_{g}g an element in \mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3})\Gamma. Then \chi_{j}(x) is rational for each 1\leq j\leq h if and only if the following conditions hold:
(i) 
\sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g)}c_{s}=\sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g^{-1})}\overline{c_{s}}   for all g\in\Gamma,
(ii) 
\sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g_{1})}c_{s}=\sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g_{2})}c_{s}   for all g_{1},g_{2}\in\Gamma(3) and g_{1}\approx g_{2},
(iii) 
\Re\left(\sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g)}c_{s}\right)=\Re\left(\sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g^{-1})}c_{s}\right)   for all g\in\Gamma\setminus\Gamma(3), and
(iv) 
\Im\left(\sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g)}c_{s}\right)=0   for all g\in\Gamma\setminus\Gamma(3).
Proof.
Let L be a set of representatives of the conjugacy classes in \Gamma. Since characters are class functions, we have
\displaystyle\chi_{j}(x)=\sum_{g\in L}\bigg(\sum_{s\in Cl(g)}c_{s}\bigg)\chi_{j}(g)\textnormal{ for all }1\leq j\leq h.
(1)
Assume that \chi_{j}(x) is rational for each 1\leq j\leq h.
Let g_{1},g_{2}\in\Gamma(3), g_{1}\approx g_{2} and m=\text{ord}(g_{1}). Then there is r\in G_{m,3}^{1}(1) and \sigma_{r}\in Gal(\mathbb{Q}(w_{m})/\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3})) such that g_{2}=g_{1}^{r} and \sigma_{r}(w_{m})=w_{m}^{r}. Note that \sigma_{r}(\chi_{j}(g_{1}))=\chi_{j}(g_{1}^{r}) for all 1\leq j\leq h. For t\in\Gamma, let \theta_{t}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{h}\chi_{j}(t)\overline{\chi}_{j}, where \overline{\chi}_{j}(g)=\overline{\chi_{j}(g)} for all g\in\Gamma. By orthogonality of characters, we have
\displaystyle\theta_{t}(u)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}|C_{\Gamma}(t)|&\mbox{ if $u$ and $t$ are conjugates to each other}\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise}.\end{array}\right.
So \theta_{t}(x)=|C_{\Gamma}(t)|\sum\limits_{s\in Cl(t)}c_{s}\in\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3}) implies that \sigma_{r}(\theta_{t}(x))=\theta_{t}(x). By assumption, \sigma_{r}(\chi_{j}(x))=\chi_{j}(x) for all j=1,...,h. Thus
\displaystyle|C_{\Gamma}(g_{1})|\sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g_{1})}c_{s}=\theta_{g_{1}}(x)=\sigma_{r}(\theta_{g_{1}}(x))
\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{h}\sigma_{r}(\chi_{j}(g_{1}))\sigma_{r}(\overline{\chi}_{j}(x))
\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{h}\chi_{j}(g_{1}^{r})\overline{\chi}_{j}(x)
\displaystyle=\theta_{g_{1}^{r}}(x)=\theta_{g_{2}}(x)=|C_{\Gamma}(g_{2})|\sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g_{2})}c_{s}.
(4)
Since g_{1}\approx g_{2}, we have C_{\Gamma}(g_{1})=C_{\Gamma}(g_{2}). So Equation (\ref{ConjEqThetaEqua}) implies that \sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g_{1})}c_{s}=\sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g_{2})}c_{s}. Hence condition (ii) holds.
Again
\displaystyle 0=\chi_{j}(x)-\overline{\chi_{j}(x)}
\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in L}\bigg(\sum_{s\in Cl(g)}c_{s}\bigg)\chi_{j}(g)-\sum_{g\in L}\bigg(\sum_{s\in Cl(g)}\overline{c_{s}}\bigg)\overline{\chi_{j}(g)}
\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in L}\bigg(\sum_{s\in Cl(g)}c_{s}\bigg)\chi_{j}(g)-\sum_{g\in L}\bigg(\sum_{s\in Cl(g)}\overline{c_{s}}\bigg)\chi_{j}(g^{-1})
\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in L}\bigg(\sum_{s\in Cl(g)}c_{s}-\sum_{s\in Cl(g^{-1})}\overline{c_{s}}\bigg)\chi_{j}(g),
and so
\displaystyle\sum_{g\in L}\bigg(\sum_{s\in Cl(g)}c_{s}-\sum_{s\in Cl(g^{-1})}\overline{c_{s}}\bigg)\begin{bmatrix}\chi_{1}(g)\\
\chi_{2}(g)\\
\vdots\\
\chi_{h}(g)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\

0\end{bmatrix}.
(5)
Since characters are class functions and the rank of E is h, the columns of E corresponding to the elements of L are linearly independent. Thus by Equation (\ref{eqLIsumZero}),
\bigg(\sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g)}c_{s}-\sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g^{-1})}\overline{c_{s}}\bigg)=0 for all g\in L, and so condition (i) holds.
Let g\in\Gamma\setminus\Gamma(3) and m=\text{ord}(g). Then there exists \tau_{m-1}\in Gal(\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3},w_{m})/\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3})) such that \tau_{m-1}(w_{m})=w_{m}^{m-1}. Note that \tau_{m-1}(\chi_{j}(g))=\chi_{j}(g^{m-1}) for all 1\leq j\leq h. Now
\displaystyle|C_{\Gamma}(g)|\sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g)}c_{s}=\theta_{g}(x)=\tau_{m-1}(\theta_{g}(x))
\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{h}\tau_{m-1}(\chi_{j}(g))\tau_{m-1}(\overline{\chi}_{j}(x))
\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{h}\chi_{j}(g^{m-1})\overline{\chi}_{j}(x)
\displaystyle=\theta_{g^{m-1}}(x)=\theta_{g^{-1}}(x)=|C_{\Gamma}(g^{-1})|\sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g^{-1})}c_{s}.
(6)
Since C_{\Gamma}(g)=C_{\Gamma}(g^{-1}), Equation (\ref{ConjEqThetaEqua2}) implies that \sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g)}c_{s}=\sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g^{-1})}c_{s}. This, together with condition (i), imply that condition (iii) and (iv) hold.
Conversely, assume that all the given conditions hold. Let n=\text{ord}(\Gamma). We have the following two cases.
Case 1. Assume that n\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3).
Let \sigma_{k}\in Gal(\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3},w_{n})/\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3})). Then \sigma_{k}(w_{n})=w_{n}^{k} and k\in G_{n,3}^{1}(1), and so \sigma_{k}(\chi_{j}(g))=\chi_{j}(g^{k}) for all 1\leq j\leq h. Thus
\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(\chi_{j}(x))
\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in L}\bigg(\sum_{s\in Cl(g)}c_{s}\bigg)\sigma_{k}(\chi_{j}(g))
\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in L(3)}\bigg(\sum_{s\in Cl(g)}c_{s}\bigg)\chi_{j}(g^{k}).
(7)
Since g\approx g^{k}, by condition (ii) we have \sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g)}c_{s}=\sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g^{k})}c_{s}. From Equation (\ref{ConjEqThetaEqua3}), we get
\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(\chi_{j}(x))
\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in L}\bigg(\sum_{s\in Cl(g^{k})}c_{s}\bigg)\chi_{j}(g^{k})=\chi_{j}(x).
(8)
The second equality in (\ref{neweq}) holds, because \{g^{k}:g\in L\} is also a set of representatives of conjugacy classes of \Gamma. Now \sigma_{k}(\chi_{j}(x))=\chi_{j}(x) for all k\in G_{n,3}^{1}(1) implies that \chi_{j}(x)\in\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3}).
Case 2. Assume that n\not\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3). As in Case 1, we get \tau_{r}(\chi_{j}(x))=\chi_{j}(x) for all r\in G_{n}(1), where \tau_{r}\in Gal(\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3},w_{m})/\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3})). Thus \chi_{j}(x)\in\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3}).
In both cases, we get \chi_{j}(x)\in\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3}). Taking complex conjugates in Equation (\ref{ConjEqThetaEqua5}),
\displaystyle\overline{\chi_{j}(x)}=\sum_{g\in L}\bigg(\sum_{s\in Cl(g)}\overline{c_{s}}\bigg)\overline{\chi_{j}(g)}
\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in L}\bigg(\sum_{s\in Cl(g)}\overline{c_{s}}\bigg)\chi_{j}(g^{-1})
\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in L}\bigg(\sum_{s\in Cl(g^{-1})}c_{s}\bigg)\chi_{j}(g^{-1})
\displaystyle=\chi_{j}(x).
(9)
Equation (\ref{ConjEqThetaEqua4}) implies that \chi_{j}(x)\in\mathbb{Q}.
∎

Theorem 3.2.

Proof.

Lemma 4.1.
Let S be a skew-symmetric subset a finite group \Gamma and that S is the union of some conjugacy classes of \Gamma. Let \text{Irr}(\Gamma)=\{\chi_{1},...,\chi_{h}\} and t(\neq 0)\in\mathbb{Q}. If \frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum\limits_{s\in S}t\left(i\sqrt{3}\chi_{j}(s)-i\sqrt{3}\chi_{j}(s^{-1})\right) is an integer for each 1\leq j\leq h then S\in\mathbb{E}(\Gamma)
Proof.
Let x=\sum\limits_{g\in\Gamma}c_{g}g\in\mathbb{Q}(\omega_{3})\Gamma, where
c_{g}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}it\sqrt{3}&\mbox{if }g\in S\\
-it\sqrt{3}&\mbox{if }g\in S^{-1}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.
Assume that \frac{\chi_{j}(x)}{\chi_{j}(1)}=\frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum\limits_{s\in S}t\left(i\sqrt{3}\chi_{j}(s)-i\sqrt{3}\chi_{j}(s^{-1})\right) is an integer for each 1\leq j\leq h. Then all the four conditions of Theorem 3.1 hold. By the fourth condition of Theorem 3.1, we get \Im\left(\sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g)}c_{s}\right)=0 for all g\in\Gamma\setminus\Gamma(3), and so we must have S\cup S^{-1}\subseteq\Gamma(3). Again, let g_{1}\in S, g_{2}\in\Gamma(3) and g_{1}\approx g_{2}. The second condition of Theorem 3.1 gives \sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g_{1})}c_{s}=\sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g_{2})}c_{s}. Note that \sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g_{1})}c_{s}=it\sqrt{3}|Cl(g_{1})|. Therefore \sum\limits_{s\in Cl(g_{2})}c_{s}=it\sqrt{3}|Cl(g_{1})|, and so g_{2}\in S. Thus g_{1}\in S implies \langle\!\langle g_{1}\rangle\!\rangle\subseteq S. Hence S\in\mathbb{E}(\Gamma).
∎
Lemma 4.2.
Let S be a skew-symmetric subset a finite group \Gamma and that S is the union of some conjugacy classes of \Gamma. Let \text{Irr}(\Gamma)=\{\chi_{1},...,\chi_{h}\} and t(\neq 0)\in\mathbb{Q}. If \frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum\limits_{s\in S}t\left(i\sqrt{3}\chi_{j}(s)-i\sqrt{3}\chi_{j}(s^{-1})\right) is an integer for each 1\leq j\leq h then \frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum\limits_{s\in S\cup S^{-1}}\chi_{j}(s) is also an integer for each 1\leq j\leq h.
Proof.
Assume that \frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum\limits_{s\in S}t\left(i\sqrt{3}\chi_{j}(s)-i\sqrt{3}\chi_{j}(s^{-1})\right) is an integer for each 1\leq j\leq h. By Lemma 4.1 we have S\in\mathbb{E}(\Gamma), and so S=\langle\!\langle x_{1}\rangle\!\rangle\cup...\cup\langle\!\langle x_{k}\rangle\!\rangle for some x_{1},...,x_{k}\in\Gamma(3). Therefore, we get
S\cup S^{-1}=\left(\langle\!\langle x_{1}\rangle\!\rangle\cup...\cup\langle\!\langle x_{k}\rangle\!\rangle\right)\cup\left(\langle\!\langle x_{1}^{-1}\rangle\!\rangle\cup...\cup\langle\!\langle x_{k}^{-1}\rangle\!\rangle\right)=[x_{1}]\cup...\cup[x_{k}]\in\mathbb{B}(\Gamma).
Thus by Theorem 2.6, \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S\cup S^{-1}) is integral, that is, \frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum\limits_{s\in S\cup S^{-1}}\chi_{j}(s) is an integer for each 1\leq j\leq h.
∎

Lemma 4.3.
Let \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) be a normal mixed Cayley graph. Then \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) is HS-integral if and only if \text{Cay}(\Gamma,{S\setminus\overline{S}}) and \text{Cay}(\Gamma,{\overline{S}}) are HS-integral.
Proof.
Let \text{Irr}(\Gamma)=\{\chi_{1},...,\chi_{h}\}. By Lemma 2.3, the HS-spectrum of \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) is \{[\gamma_{1}]^{d_{1}^{2}},...,[\gamma_{h}]^{d_{h}^{2}}\}, where \gamma_{j}=\lambda_{j}+\mu_{j},
\lambda_{j}=\frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum_{s\in S\setminus\overline{S}}\chi_{j}(s),\hskip 5.69046pt\mu_{j}=\frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum_{s\in\overline{S}}(\omega_{6}\chi_{j}(s)+\omega_{6}^{5}\chi_{j}(s^{-1}))\textnormal{ and }d_{j}=\chi_{j}(1)\textnormal{ for all }j=1,...,h.
Note that \{[\lambda_{1}]^{d_{1}^{2}},...,[\lambda_{h}]^{d_{h}^{2}}\} is the spectrum of \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S\setminus\overline{S}) and \{[\mu_{1}]^{d_{1}^{2}},...,[\mu_{h}]^{d_{h}^{2}}\} is the HS-spectrum of \text{Cay}(\Gamma,\overline{S}).
Assume that the mixed Cayley graph \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) is HS-integral. Let j\in\{1,...,h\}. By Lemma 2.1, there exists k\in\{1,...,h\} such that \chi_{k}=\overline{\chi}_{j}. Then \lambda_{j}=\lambda_{k} and \chi_{j}(1)=\chi_{k}(1). Thus
\displaystyle\gamma_{j}-\gamma_{k}
\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum_{s\in\overline{S}}\left(\omega_{6}\chi_{j}(s)+\omega_{6}^{5}\chi_{j}(s^{-1})\right)-\frac{1}{\chi_{k}(1)}\sum_{s\in\overline{S}}\left(\omega_{6}\chi_{k}(s)+\omega_{6}^{5}\chi_{k}(s^{-1})\right)
\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum_{s\in\overline{S}}\left(\omega_{6}\chi_{j}(s)+\omega_{6}^{5}\chi_{j}(s^{-1})\right)-\frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum_{s\in\overline{S}}\left(\omega_{6}\overline{\chi_{j}(s)}+\omega_{6}^{5}\overline{\chi_{j}(s^{-1})}\right)
\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum_{s\in\overline{S}}\left(\omega_{6}\chi_{j}(s)+\omega_{6}^{5}\chi_{j}(s^{-1})\right)-\frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum_{s\in\overline{S}}\left(\omega_{6}\chi_{j}(s^{-1})+\omega_{6}^{5}\chi_{j}(s)\right)
\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum_{s\in\overline{S}}(\omega_{6}-\omega_{6}^{5})\chi_{j}(s)+(\omega_{6}^{5}-\omega_{6})\chi_{j}(s^{-1})
\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum_{s\in\overline{S}}\left(i\sqrt{3}\chi_{j}(s)-i\sqrt{3}\chi_{j}(s^{-1})\right).
By assumption \gamma_{j},\gamma_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}, and so \frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum\limits_{s\in\overline{S}}\left(i\sqrt{3}\chi_{j}(s)-i\sqrt{3}\chi_{j}(s^{-1})\right)\in\mathbb{Z} for all 1\leq j\leq h. By Lemma 4.2, we get \frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum\limits_{s\in\overline{S}\cup\overline{S}^{-1}}\chi_{j}(s)\in\mathbb{Z} for all 1\leq j\leq h. Since
\displaystyle\mu_{j}=\frac{1}{2\chi_{j}(1)}\sum\limits_{s\in\overline{S}\cup\overline{S}^{-1}}\chi_{j}(s)+\frac{1}{2\chi_{j}(1)}\sum\limits_{s\in\overline{S}}\left(i\sqrt{3}\chi_{j}(s)-i\sqrt{3}\chi_{j}(s^{-1})\right),
\mu_{j} is a rational algebraic integer, and hence it is an integer for each 1\leq j\leq h. Thus \text{Cay}(\Gamma,\overline{S}) is HS-integral. Now we have \gamma_{j},\mu_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}, and so \lambda_{j}=\gamma_{j}-\mu_{j}\in\mathbb{Z} for each 1\leq j\leq h. Hence \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S\setminus\overline{S}) is also HS-integral.
Conversely, assume that \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S\setminus\overline{S}) and \text{Cay}(\Gamma,\overline{S}) are HS-integral. Then Lemma 2.3 implies that \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) is integral.
∎

Assume S is the union of some conjugacy classes from the finite group \Gamma that doesn’t contain 1 and Irr(\Gamma)=\{\chi_{1},...,\chi_{h}\}. Consider the function \alpha:\Gamma\rightarrow\{0,1\} defined by

For each j\in\{1,...,h\}, define

Lemma 5.1.
The normal mixed Cayley graph \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) is Eisenstein integral if and only if 2f_{j}(S) and 2g_{j}(S) are integer with same parity for each 1\leq j\leq h.
Proof.

Then f_{j}(S)+g_{j}(S) and g_{j}(S)-g_{k}(S)=\frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum\limits_{s\in\overline{S}}\frac{-1}{3}(\sqrt{3}i\chi_{j}(s)-\sqrt{3}i\chi_{j}(s^{-1})) are integers for each 1\leq j\leq h, where \chi_{k}=\overline{\chi}_{j}. we find that 2g_{j}(S) is an integer. Therefore, 2f_{j}(S)=2(f_{j}(S)+g_{j}(S))-2g_{j}(S) is also integer with the same parity with 2g_{j}(S).

Lemma 5.2.
The normal mixed Cayley graph \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) is Eisenstein integral if and only if f_{j}(S) and g_{j}(S) are integers for each 1\leq j\leq h.
Proof.
Let 1\leq j\leq h. According to Lemma 5.1, it is sufficient to prove that 2f_{j}(S) and 2g_{j}(S) are integers with the same parity if and only if f_{j}(S) and g_{j}(S) are integers. If f_{j}(S) and g_{j}(S) are integers, then clearly 2f_{j}(S) and 2g_{j}(S) are even integers. Conversly, assume that 2f_{j}(S) and 2g_{j}(S) are integers of the same parity. Since f_{j}(S) is an algebraic integer, then the integrality of 2f_{j}(S) implies that f_{j}(S) is integer. Thus 2f_{j}(S) is even integer, and so by assumption 2g_{j}(S) is also even. Hence g_{j}(S) is an integer.
∎
Theorem 5.3.
Let \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) be a normal mixed Cayley graph. If \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) is Eisenstein integral then \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) is HS-integral.
Proof.
Assume that \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) is Eisenstein integral. By Lemma 5.2, we get f_{j}(S) and g_{j}(S) are integers for each 1\leq j\leq h. It is important to note that f_{j}(S) is an eigenvalue of the normal simple Cayley graph \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S\setminus\overline{S}). By Theorem 2.6, f_{j}(S) is an integer for each 1\leq j\leq h if and only if S\setminus\overline{S}\in\mathbb{B}(\Gamma). We know \frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum\limits_{s\in\overline{S}}\frac{-i\sqrt{3}}{3}(\chi_{j}(s)-\chi_{j}(s^{-1}))=g_{j}(S)-g_{k}(S) is an integer for each 1\leq j\leq h, where \chi_{k}=\overline{\chi}_{j}. Using Lemma 4.1, we see that \overline{S}\in\mathbb{E}(\Gamma). Hence by Theorem 4.4, \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) is HS-integral.
∎
For x\in\Gamma and 1\leq j\leq h, define
C_{x}(j)=\frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum_{s\in S^{1}_{x}}\chi_{j}(s)
where S^{1}_{x} is the smallest symmetric subset of \Gamma containg x that is closed under both conjugate and equivalence relation \sim. It is worthy to note that C_{x}(j) is an eigenvalue of the normal simple(mixed) Cayley graph \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S^{1}_{x}). As a consequence, by Theorem 2.6, C_{x}(j) is an integer for each x\in\Gamma and 1\leq j\leq h.
For y\in\Gamma(3), define S^{2}_{y} to be the smallest skew-symmetric subset of \Gamma containg y that is closed under conjugate as well as equivalence relation \approx. If \Gamma is an abelian group then S_{y}^{2}=\langle\!\langle y\rangle\!\rangle for all y\in\Gamma(3). If \Gamma is an ambivalent group then S_{y}^{2} does not exist for all y\in\Gamma(3). Therefore, if y\in\Gamma(3) and S_{y}^{2} exists, then for 1\leq j\leq h, define
T_{y}(j)=\frac{1}{\chi_{j}(1)}\sum_{s\in S^{2}_{y}}\sqrt{3}i(\chi_{j}(s)-\chi_{j}(s^{-1})).
Let j\in\{1,...,h\}. Using S^{1}_{y}=S^{2}_{y}\cup(S^{2}_{y})^{-1}, we get \frac{C_{y}(j)+T_{y}(j)}{2} is an HS-eigenvalue of the normal oriented Cayley graph \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S^{2}_{y}), for each 1\leq j\leq h. Therefore, by Theorem 3.2, \frac{C_{y}(j)+T_{y}(j)}{2} is an integer. Similarly, by Theorem 2.6, C_{y}(j) is also an integer. Which inplies that T_{y}(j) is an integer.
Let \Gamma is an abelian group or order n, y\in\Gamma(3) and Irr(\Gamma)=\{\chi_{1},...,\chi_{n}\}. In [16], the auther have proved that \frac{T_{y}(j)}{3} is integer for each 1\leq j\leq n.
Consider a alternating group A_{4} and y=(1,2,3)\in A_{4}(3). Then S_{y}^{2}=\langle\!\langle(1,2,3)\rangle\!\rangle\cup\langle\!\langle(4,2,1)\rangle\!\rangle\cup\langle\!\langle(2,4,3)\rangle\!\rangle\cup\langle\!\langle(3,4,1)\rangle\!\rangle=Cl((1,2,3)), and so T_{y}(1)=T_{y}(4)=0, T_{y}(2)=-12 and T_{y}(3)=12. Thus \frac{T_{y}(j)}{3} is integer for each 1\leq j\leq 4. Similarly, for z=(1,3,2) also, \frac{T_{z}(j)}{3} is integer for each 1\leq j\leq 4.
Let \Gamma be a finite group and y\in\Gamma(3) with S_{y}^{2} exists. We have seen that T_{y}(j) is an integer for each 1\leq j\leq h. However, if \Gamma is an abelian group or \Gamma=A_{4}, then \frac{T_{y}(j)}{3} is an integer for each 1\leq j\leq h. In the next result, we introduce a conjecture stating that it is also true in general.
Conjecture 5.4.
Let \Gamma be a finite group and y\in\Gamma(3) with S_{y}^{2} exists. Then \frac{T_{y}(j)}{3} is an integer for each 1\leq j\leq h.
In the following result, we prove the converse of Theorem 5.3 by combining the Conjecture 5.4 into a sufficient condition.
Theorem 5.5.
Let \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) be a normal mixed Cayley graph and \frac{T_{y}(j)}{3} is integer whenever y\in\overline{S}\subseteq\Gamma(3) with S_{y}^{2} exists, for each 1\leq j\leq h. If \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) is HS-integral, then \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) is Eisenstein integral.
Proof.
Assume that \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) is HS-integral and \frac{T_{y}(j)}{3} is integer whenever y\in\Gamma(3) with S_{y}^{2} exists for each 1\leq j\leq h. Then \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S\setminus\overline{S}) is integral, and hence f_{j}(S) is an integer for each 1\leq j\leq h. By Theorem 4.4, we have \overline{S}\in\mathbb{E}(\Gamma), and so \overline{S}=S_{y_{1}}^{2}\cup...\cup S_{y_{k}}^{2}, for some y_{1},...,y_{k}\in\Gamma(3).
\begin{split}g_{j}(S)&=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{s\in\overline{S}\cup\overline{S}^{-1}}\chi_{j}(s)-\frac{1}{6}\sum\limits_{s\in\overline{S}}i\sqrt{3}\left(\chi_{j}(s)-\chi_{j}(s^{-1})\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{k}\sum\limits_{s\in S_{y_{l}}^{2}\cup(S_{y_{l}}^{2})^{-1}}\chi_{j}(s)-\frac{1}{6}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{k}\sum\limits_{s\in S_{y_{l}}^{2}}i\sqrt{3}\left(\chi_{j}(s)-\chi_{j}(s^{-1})\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{k}C_{y_{l}}(\alpha)-\frac{1}{6}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{k}T_{y_{l}}(\alpha)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{k}\left(C_{y_{l}}(j)-\frac{1}{3}T_{y_{l}}(j)\right).\end{split}
(11)
Let 1\leq l\leq k. Since, \frac{C_{y_{l}}(j)+T_{y_{l}}(j)}{2} is an HS-eigenvalue of the normal oriented Cayley graph \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S^{2}_{y_{l}}), therefore, C_{y_{l}}(j) and T_{y_{l}}(j) are integers of the same parity. Given that \frac{T_{y_{l}}(j)}{3} is an integer, and so C_{y_{l}}(j) and \frac{T_{y_{l}}(j)}{3} are also integers of the same parity for each 1\leq j\leq h. We have C_{y_{l}}(j)-\frac{1}{3}T_{y_{l}}(j) is an even integer for each 1\leq j\leq h. Then by equation 11, g_{j}(S) is an integer for each 1\leq j\leq h. Hence by Lemma 5.2, \text{Cay}(\Gamma,S) is Eisenstein integral.
∎
Consider the normal mixed Cayley graph \text{Cay}(A_{4},S) of example 4.1. By Theorem 5.5, \text{Cay}(A_{4},S) is Eisenstein integral. Further, using Theorem 2.2 and Equation 10, we will caluclate the spectrum of \text{Cay}(A_{4},S). Let y=(1,2,3)\in\overline{S}. Then S_{y}^{1}=S^{2}_{y}\cup(S^{2}_{y})^{-1}=\overline{S}\cup\overline{S}^{-1}, and so C_{y}(1)=8,C_{y}(2)=-4,C_{y}(3)=-4,C_{y}(4)=0. Using equation 11, we get g_{1}(S)=4,g_{2}(S)=0,g_{3}(S)=-4 and g_{4}(S)=0. Similarly, f_{1}(S)=3,f_{2}(S)=3,f_{3}(S)=3 and f_{4}(S)=-1. Thus by Theorem 2.2 and Equation 10, the spectrum of \text{Cay}(A_{4},S) is \{[7]^{1},[3+4\omega_{3}]^{1},[-1-4\omega_{3}]^{1},[-1]^{9}\}.
