## 388685: complete classical reduction, zero credit

For the standard finite family of nonempty closed axis-parallel rectangles, and positive integer m, the exact extremal answer is f(m)=2(m-1). Here f is the worst-case minimum number of lines over all families whose independent-set number is less than m, not the number needed for each individual family. The original TOPP page uses the less precise bound 2m. Its 2012 update points explicitly to Kaiser, Theorem 1.4. The following resolves the off-by-one convention without producing a new mathematical result.

Given R=I_R x J_R, form the separated 2-interval H_R=(I_R x {0}) union (J_R x {1}) on two abstract tracks. Two H_R are disjoint exactly when both corresponding projections are disjoint. A point (t,0) in a transversal corresponds to the vertical line x=t, and a point (s,1) to the horizontal line y=s. This correspondence preserves covering and cardinality after redundant duplicates are removed. Thus the packing number equals the independent-set number, and the transversal number equals the slicing number. Kaiser, published pp.196-197, Theorems 1.1 and 1.4, gives tau <= 2 nu. Therefore nu <= m-1 implies slicing number <= 2(m-1). The theorem is already attributed to Tardos (1995), with sharpness attributed to Gyarfas-Lehel (1970).

For an explicit lower bound, take A=[0,2]x[0,1], B=[1,3]x[3,4], C=[4,5]x[0,4]. No two are independent: A and B share x-projection; C shares y-projection with each. No single vertical line intersects all three, because their x-projections have empty common intersection. No single horizontal line does either, because A and B have disjoint y-projections. Two lines suffice (for instance x=3/2 and x=9/2), so nu=1 and tau=2. For k=m-1, translate k copies by (10j,10j), 0<=j<k. Projection ranges of distinct copies are disjoint in both coordinates. A maximal independent set chooses exactly one rectangle per copy, giving nu=k. Any horizontal or vertical line meets rectangles in at most one copy. At least two lines per copy are necessary and sufficient, giving tau=2k. For m=1 the family is empty and zero lines suffice.

Routes tested: (1) direct greedy selection of one independent rectangle and two stabbing lines does not control rectangles whose x or y projections overlap its projections without containing the chosen coordinates; maximality only gives overlap, not piercing by fixed endpoints. (2) projection-track transformation closes the full upper bound immediately via the actual classical theorem. (3) the explicit three-rectangle gadget closes sharpness. A proposed new slicing theorem would therefore duplicate the classical family; there is no candidate and no dormant Pi plan. Open/degenerate/interior-only or arbitrary noncompact collection interpretations are not used to manufacture a domain correction.

Actual proof-body depth: Kaiser's definitions and Theorems 1.1/1.4, pp.196-197, and Section 3 proof pp.201-203 read, with preceding fractional-matching argument pp.199-200. The proof uses a product-of-spheres zero theorem to make an auxiliary bipartite graph weight-regular; if nonempty, its fractional matching number is at least n+1 and its ordinary matching number at most k, contradicting n=k via Konig. This is a classical topological proof, not an elementary lemma that may be silently imported into a novel Lean claim. External Ramos/Borsuk-Ulam dependencies were not independently audited. Figure 1 is a homogeneous, one-track example with tau=3, and is not the two-track construction above; its caption was read, its graphic was not inspected. No figure-dependent novelty claim is made.

## 358364: full packing/MFMC equivalence, park

A finite clutter is an inclusion-antichain of subsets of a finite vertex set. Deletion removes edges containing a deleted vertex; contraction removes that vertex from edges and retains inclusion-minimal sets. Packing means cover number equals maximum disjoint-edge matching number in EVERY minor, including the clutter itself. With vertex-by-edge incidence matrix A, MFMC asks, for EVERY integral vector w>=0, that the dual LPs min w.x subject to x>=0, xA>=1 and max 1.y subject to y>=0, Ay<=w have integral optimizers. This is an unbounded family of weights and clutters, not a single finite packing test.

The record's references say 'solved' but its statement says unsolved. All four arXiv versions retain the unsolved conjecture: v1-v3 Conjecture 11.19; v4 Conjecture 11.25, pp.38-40. The nearby 'This conjecture was proved' sentence concerns the graph edge-ideal normality/Hochster-configuration theorem (v4 Theorem 6.10), not packing. The source dates the packing problem to Conforti-Cornuejols 1993; 2023 is the survey date, not first statement. Historical campaign records 325241 and 373722 are equivalent algebraic formulations previously parked; no new credit can be obtained by renaming them. Source v4 Theorems 11.12/11.21 and Conjectures 11.27/11.28 identify equality of all symbolic/ordinary powers and normality of the Rees algebra as the remaining obstruction.

Route 1, idealness plus LP duality: the packing property implies an integral covering polyhedron (v4 Theorem 11.23). This supplies integral primal optima, but equality of primal and dual REAL optimum does not force an integral dual optimum. The missing lemma is integral generation of each relevant tight incidence cone (Hilbert-basis/TDI property); replacing it by 'integral polyhedron' is invalid. Q6 in Example 11.19 shows that idealness alone is insufficient even for unweighted packing, and Q6 is explicitly not Konig, so cannot be a packing-property counterexample.

Route 2, weight induction/replication: replace each vertex by w_v clones. A disjoint matching in the replicated clutter encodes a capacitated packing. If replication preserved packing, the desired equality would follow by unweighted packing in the expanded clutter. But replication is not deletion/contraction, so the hypothesis does not establish this preservation. Preservation under one replication is precisely the replication conjecture; assuming it is circular, not a proof. Zero weights correspond to deletion; weights above one are the obstruction. A one-step weight decrement likewise lacks a guaranteed chosen edge meeting all tight residual capacities without destroying the required residual packing invariant.

Route 3, symbolic-power saturation: reducing to normality of S[Iz] makes the missing assertion explicit rather than proving it. No argument that the integer semigroup is saturated follows from the all-minors unit-weight hypothesis. Restricting to graphs, balanced clutters or (n-2)-uniform clutters only proves known proper subclasses and does not answer this record.

Current successor body: Abdi-Schwarcz arXiv:2606.16543v2, July 9/10 2026, Introduction pp.1-5 and Theorems 1-3 read. It still states the full assertion as Conjecture 1, explains the idealness/TDI gap, and reports verification for clutters on at most 10 elements and cuboids of degree <=9. This rules out treating small test objects as a credible shortcut here; no SAT verification was run or independently validated. No complete all-size route survives. All-version primary proof audit and correction/citation-family exhaustion remain incomplete, so novelty is not cleared even apart from the proof gap.

## 349766: signed nonzero real three-lists, park

Literal endpoint: for every finite simple nice graph (no component K2), and every assignment of three DISTINCT NONZERO REAL numbers to each edge, choose one label per edge so adjacent vertices have different products of their incident labels. Isolated vertices impose no adjacent constraint. The star on ch*_Pi removes zero; it does not restrict to positive numbers. The source's Observation 2.1 relates the zero-allowed parameter by adding one. K2, duplicate entries, zero lists, and assertions about only {1,2,3} are therefore invalid counterexamples or incomplete proxies.

Route 1, logarithms, sustained to an exact equivalence: on positive lists, taking logarithms preserves three distinct labels and transforms product inequality into additive sum inequality. Conversely, exponentiating ANY additive three-real lists transforms them into positive multiplicative lists and converts any product-distinguishing solution back into a sum-distinguishing one. Thus even the positive-list subproblem is EXACTLY equivalent to the full additive List 1-2-3 problem. For signed lists, logarithms of absolute values may collapse three labels to two, e.g. {-a,a,b}. The paper's Theorem 2.5 requires 2k-1 labels to guarantee k distinct absolute values: five, not three, for additive k=3. Distinguishing magnitudes for every edge would need a stronger two-choice additive theorem, which is false in general. Negative signs can repair some conflicts but need a new global compatibility argument. Original non-list 1-2-3 results supply no edge-dependent-list bridge.

Route 2, polynomial certificate: cancel the common nonzero label on each tested edge uv and use factors F_uv=product_{e incident u,e!=uv} x_e - product_{e incident v,e!=uv} x_e. A successful labeling is exactly a nonzero evaluation of product F_uv on the three-point edge grids. The direct maximum-degree Nullstellensatz shortcut would require a nonzero top-degree monomial with EACH exponent <=2. On an r-regular simple nice graph, every factor has degree r-1 and the nonzero product is homogeneous of total degree |E|(r-1). For r>=4 this exceeds 2|E|, so no such monomial exists. This disproves the shortcut, not the conjecture. Reducing modulo the degree-three grid vanishing polynomials could lower degree, but there is no proof that a nonzero residue survives for every graph and every list. That universal nonvanishing assertion is the precise missing lemma.

Route 3, greedy extension and forcing gadgets: changing an edge xy multiplies both endpoint products by its new nonzero label. Each already fixed neighbor of x or y can forbid one value, with up to d(x)+d(y)-2 constraints, exceeding three. The x-y comparison itself cancels the changed label and cannot be repaired by that choice if partial products coincide. A pendant path of length two forces its inner label to differ from 1, but three-lists then still have at least two choices. The paper's two-list forcing trees do not force a unique value for a three-list, so adjoining them does not produce a counterexample. Sign-parity obstructions from {-1,1} similarly apply to TWO-lists; a third distinct nonzero real cannot have the same absolute value and supplies an escape. No exact in-scope three-list obstruction was obtained.
