                                                                                             Abstract
                                                         We consider two recent conjectures of Harrington, Henninger-Voss, Karhadkar, Robinson
                                                     and Wong concerning relationships between the sum index, difference index and exclusive
                                                     sum number of graphs. One conjecture posits an exact relationship between the first two
                                                     invariants; we show that in fact the predicted value may be arbitrarily far from the truth
                                                     in either direction. In the process we establish some new bounds on both the sum and
                                                     difference index. The other conjecture, that the exclusive sum number can exceed the sum
                                                     index by an arbitrarily large amount, follows from known, but non-constructive, results; we
                                                     give an explicit construction demonstrating it.


                                           1        Introduction
                                           Let G be a finite simple graph and let f : V (G) → Z be an injective labelling. We may derive
                                           two natural edge labellings f+ : E(G) → Z and f− : E(G) → Z from f as follows.

                                                                                     f+ : uv 7→ f (u) + f (v);
                                                                                     f− : uv 7→ |f (u) − f (v)|.

                                           We consider two parameters of G based on these labellings. The sum index, sum(G), introduced
                                           by Harrington and Wong [7], is the minimum number of different values taken by f+ for any
                                           choice of f , and the difference index, dif(G), is defined likewise with respect to f− . In a
                                           recent preprint [6], Harrington, Henninger-Voss, Karhadkar, Robinson and Wong introduced
                                           the difference index and made the following bold conjecture, based on computations of exact
                                           values for several families of graphs.

                                           Conjecture 1.1 ([6, Conjecture 4.2]). Every nonempty simple graph G satisfies

                                                                                      dif(G) = ⌈sum(G)/2⌉.

                                               They further supported Conjecture 1.1 by proving a one-directional version for bipartite
                                           graphs: if G is bipartite then dif(G) ≥ ⌈sum(G)/2⌉.
                                               We show in Sections 2 and 3 that Conjecture 1.1 is false in general, and in fact the conjectured
                                           value can be arbitrarily far from the truth in either direction. In one direction, we prove this in
                                           the strongest possible form: we show that the sum index cannot be bounded by any function
                                           of the difference index, and in fact even for difference index 2 there are connected graphs with

may be arbitrarily large, it is small relative to the two indices. We give some new bounds on the

     We also consider the exclusive sum number of a graph, which closely related to the well-
studied sum number, introduced by Harary [5]. Harary defined a graph G to be a sum graph if
there is an injective function f : V (G) → Z+ such that for any distinct vertices u, v ∈ V (G) we
have uv ∈ E(G) if and only if f (u) + f (v) ∈ f (V (G)); this f is a sum labelling of G. Necessarily
any sum graph has at least one isolated vertex, since the vertex v maximising f (v) cannot have
any neighbours; the sum number, σ(G), of a graph G is the smallest number of isolated vertices
which can be added to create a sum graph. Harary asked for bounds on σ(G), and conjectured
that σ(T ) = 1 for every tree T on more than one vertex. Bounds on σ(G) in terms of an
optimal clique cover for G were given by Gould and Rödl [4], and Harary’s conjecture for trees
was proved by Ellingham [1]. There has since been a great deal of work on the sum number and
variations; see [3, Section 7.1] for (much) more detail, including on the exclusive sum number
defined below.
     The exclusive sum number was introduced by Miller, Patel, Ryan, Sugeng, Slamin and Tuga
[9] (although the definition appears in earlier work of Nagamochi, Miller and Slamin [11]). Given
a sum labelling f of a graph, a vertex w is a working vertex if f (w) = f (u) + f (v) for some edge
uv. If G is a graph with sum number r and f is a sum labelling of G ∪ K r , then necessarily all
vertices not in G are working vertices, since otherwise one of them could be omitted to obtain
a sum labelling of G ∪ K r−1 . A sum labelling f is an exclusive sum labelling if these are the
only working vertices, and the exclusive sum number, ǫ(G), of G to be the minimum r for which
G ∪ K r has an exclusive sum labelling. (We will generally assume G to be a connected graph
on more than one vertex, so that it will be clear which vertices may be working.) We give an
equivalent, but perhaps more natural, definition of the exclusive sum number in Section 4. For
further results on this parameter, see [12, 10].
     Harrington et al. [6, Theorem 2.5] observed that the exclusive sum number gives an upper
bound for the sum index, and proved that there is strict inequality for a specific graph. (There
are also graphs for which equality occurs; for example sum(Kn ) = ǫ(Kn ).) Motivated by this,
they conjecture that the difference can be arbitrarily large.

Conjecture 1.2. For any positive integer N there exists a graph G with ǫ(G) − sum(G) > N .

    In fact Conjecture 1.2 is true in a strong sense, and this follows immediately from known
results on the sum number. Clearly ǫ(G) ≥ σ(G), and it was shown by Gould and Rödl
[4] that there exist graphs with σ(G) = Θ(|G|2 ), and by Nagamochi, Miller and Slamin [11]
that such graphs are quite common. Since trivially sum(G) = O(|G|), such graphs satisfy
sum(G) = o(ǫ(G)). However, both proofs referred to are non-constructive, and in fact no
specific construction is known for which σ(G) = Ω(|G|) (see [9, 3]). In Section 4 we give a
constructive proof of Conjecture 1.2 by giving an explicit sequence of graphs with unbounded
exclusive sum index for which the two parameters differ by a constant multiplicative factor.
    We conclude by giving some natural open questions suggested by this work. In what follows,
we often use the notation [m] := {1, . . . , m} for m ∈ Z+ .

3    Graphs with difference index larger than conjectured

observed that dif(G) ≥ δ(G). We give a strengthening of this fact, and establish a similar
bound for the sum index. Write δk (G) for the kth smallest degree.

4    Graphs with sum index far from exclusive sum number
In this section we give a constructive proof of Conjecture 1.2 by giving explicit graphs GN
satisfying ǫ(GN ) − sum(GN ) ≥ N for any fixed N ; in fact |GN | = O(N ) so the two invariants
differ by a constant proportion. Recall, however, that a much stronger form of Conjecture 1.2
follows from known, but non-constructive, results.
    Our proof will use a stability result from additive combinatorics. For sets of integers (all
sets we consider will be finite and nonempty) A and B we write A + B for the sumset {a + b |
a ∈ A, b ∈ B}. It is easy to verify that |A + B| ≥ |A| + |B| − 1, with equality if and only if
A and B are arithmetic progressions with a common difference. A natural question is whether
this structural constraint is robust when replacing equality with near-equality. Stanchescu [13],
building on earlier work by Freı̆man [2] and by Lev and Smeliansky [8], showed that it is robust
in the following sense. For a nonempty finite set X of integers, write ℓ(X) = max(X) − min(X).
Given two such sets A and B, let d(A, B) be the greatest common divisor of {a − a′ | a, a′ ∈
A} ∪ {b − b′ | b, b′ ∈ B}. We write δij to mean the Kronecker delta.

Theorem 4.1 ([13, Corollary 2]). Let A and B be two finite sets of integers. If |A + B| <
|A| + |B| + min(|A|, |B|) − 2 − δℓ(A)ℓ(B) then ℓ(A)/d(A, B) ≤ |A + B| − |B| and ℓ(B)/d(A, B) ≤
|A + B| − |A|.

    Note that A is contained in an arithmetic progression A′ of order 1 + ℓ(A)/d with difference
d, and likewise for B, so Theorem 4.1 implies that if A + B is close to minimal size then these
arithmetic progressions are also not much larger than A and B.

more natural, definition of the exclusive sum number. For two finite sets of positive integers
S, T we define G+ (S, T ) to be the graph on vertex set S, with two vertices being adjacent if
and only if their sum is in T .

5    Open questions
These results raise several natural questions. Notice that the counterexamples we obtain to
Conjecture 1.1 are all non-bipartite. For Section 2 this is necessarily the case, since dif(G) ≥
⌈sum(G)/2⌉ for bipartite graphs [6]. For Section 3, however, a bipartite counterexample may
be possible; conversely, Conjecture 1.1 might in fact be true for bipartite graphs.

Question 5.1. Is there a bipartite graph G with dif(G) > ⌈sum(G)/2⌉?

    We would also be interested to know by how far dif(G) can exceed ⌈sum(G)/2⌉. In Section
3 we showed that the absolute discrepancy can be arbitrarily large, but the relative discrepancy
of our examples is small. Is it possible to improve our results to give a constant relative
discrepancy?

Question 5.2. Is there a constant β > 1 for which there are graphs with arbitrarily large sum
index satisfying dif(G) ≥ (1 + β)⌈sum(G)/2⌉?

   We conjecture that, even if the answer to Question 5.2 were positive, the results of Section
3 could not be improved any further than that. Recall that Harrington et al. proved that no
bipartite graph has larger difference index than sum index [6]; we expect this relationship to
hold more generally.
