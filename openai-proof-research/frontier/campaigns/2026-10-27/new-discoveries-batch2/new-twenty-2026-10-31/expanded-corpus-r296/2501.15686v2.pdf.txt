                                                                                                   Abstract
                                                     Given a graph F , a graph G is weakly F -saturated if all non-edges of G can be added in some order
                                                 so that each new edge introduces a copy of F . The weak saturation number wsat(n, F ) is the minimum
                                                 number of edges in a weakly F -saturated graph on n vertices. Bollobás initiated the study of weak
                                                 saturation in 1968 to study percolation processes, which originated in biology and have applications in
                                                 physics and computer science. It was shown by Alon that for each F , there is a constant wF such that
                                                 wsat(n, F ) = wF n + o(n). We characterize all possible rational values of wF , proving in particular that
                                                 wF can equal any rational number at least 32 .


                                          1      Introduction
                                          Given a (finite, simple) graph F , an F -bootstrap percolation process on a graph G is a sequence G = G0 ⊆
                                          G1 ⊆ . . . ⊆ Gm where Gi is obtained from Gi−1 by adding an edge which creates a new copy of F .
                                          Bollobás initiated the study of F -bootstrap percolation processes in 1968 [3] as a graph-theoretic analogue
                                          of self-reproducing cellular automata from biology [13] and percolation models prevalent in physics, see e.g.
                                          [1]. Monotone cellular automata such as bootstrap percolation have received significant attention in both
                                          mathematics and statistical physics with the resolution of the so-called universality conjecture [5].
                                              A graph G on n vertices is weakly F -saturated (or just weakly saturated if F is clear from context) if
                                          there exists an F -bootstrap percolation process G = G0 ⊆ G1 ⊆ . . . ⊆ Gm = Kn . For given F and n ≥ 1,
                                          the weak saturation number wsat(n, F ) is the minimum number of edges in a weakly saturated graph on n
                                          vertices. We denote by wSAT(n, F ) the set of all weakly saturated graphs on n vertices, and by wSAT(n, F )
                                          the subset of wSAT(n, F ) of minimum weakly saturated graphs, i.e. those with exactly wsat(n, F ) edges.

                 {0} ∪ {1 − 1/k : k ≥ 3} ∪ {1} ∪ {3/2 − 1/k : k ≥ 4 even} ∪ Q ∩ [3/2, ∞) .

    Surprisingly, any rational number at least 3/2 is attainable by wF , while below 3/2, the situation is more
discrete, with only a single accumulation point at 1. Theorem 1.1 breaks naturally into two cases, which
together answer the question of Terekhov and Zhukovkii for rational values.

Theorem 1.2 (Sparse Regime). Suppose δ ≥ 2 and w < δ/2. There exists a graph F with δF = δ and
wF = w if and only if w = δ/2 − 1/k where k is the number of vertices in some δ-regular graph, i.e. k ≥ δ + 1
and k must be even if δ is odd.

   For w ≥ δ/2, the behavior is radically different.

Theorem 1.3 (Dense Regime). For any integer δ ≥ 2 and any rational w ∈ [δ/2, δ − 1], there exists a graph
F with δF = δ and wF = w.

    By (1), we have wF = 0 if δ = 1, so Theorem 1.1 follows immediately from Theorem 1.2 and Theorem 1.3.
Roughly speaking, Theorem 1.2 and Theorem 1.3 together prove the existence of extremal bootstrap perco-
lation processes of every possible “rational” or “periodic” behavior, and Theorem 1.2 additionally disallows
the possibility of “aperiodic” behaviors in the sparse regime. It would be extremely interesting to prove
that all extremal processes are periodic in the dense regime as well; see Section 5 for more discussion of this
problem.
    Alon’s [2] subadditivity argument showed that for any graph F , we have wsat(n, F ) = wF n + o(n). In
1992, Tuza conjectured that the lower order term is in fact O(1), which is of the same spirit as the conjecture
that all extremal processes are periodic.

Conjecture 1.4 ([19]). For any graph F , there exists cF such that wF n − cF ≤ wsat(n, F ) ≤ wF n + cF for
all large enough n.

    The lower inequality is easy to show; see [17] for a short proof, as well as a discussion of for which classes
of graphs the conjecture is known. We establish Tuza’s conjecture when wF in the “sparse regime.”

Theorem 1.5. For any graph F , if wF < δF /2, then wsat(n, F ) = wF n + O(1).

    This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we prove our main technical lemma, inspired by [17], for
constructing F with a target value of wF . In Section 3, we prove Theorem 1.2, where the main difficulty is
showing that no small values of wF are possible other than the specific family described. Theorem 1.5 follows
as an easy consequence of that proof. In Section 4, we prove Theorem 1.3 by showing that any rational value
in the target interval can be achieved; the construction is obtained by carefully modifying a Cayley graph
and attaching it to a large clique. We conclude the paper in Section 5 with a few of the fascinating open
problems that remain in this area.

2     Preliminaries
In this section we prove our main technical tool for constructing F with a given weak saturation limit. We
begin with the following definitions, slightly modified from [17].
Definition 2.1. For a subset S of V (F ), let mF (S) denote the number of edges in F with at least one
endpoint in S. Given a nonempty subset S ⊆ V (F ), define γF (S) = (mF (S) − 1)/|S|, and let γF =
min∅⊊S⊆V (F ) γF (S).
    Terekhov and Zhukovskii related wF to γF as follows.
Lemma 2.2 ([17, Theorem 2.1 and Section 3.1]). For any graph F , wF ≥ γF .
   Although wF and γF are not always equal, our key lemma states that every value attained by γ is attained
by w.
Lemma 2.3. Given a graph F , there exists a graph F̃ with δF̃ = δF and wF̃ = γF .
Proof. Let ∅ ⊊ S ⊆ V (F ) be the vertex subset achieving γF = γF (S). We may assume that there exists
f0 ∈ V (F ) \ S which is not adjacent to any vertex in S. To accomplish this, we can for example modify F
by taking its disjoint union with a very large clique.
   Let F̃ be the disjoint union of all spanning supergraphs of F , including F itself. Clearly, δF̃ = δF . It
remains to show that wF̃ = γF , which we break into two claims.
Claim. With F̃ as above, wF̃ ≥ γF .

Proof. By Lemma 2.2, it suffices to show that γF̃ = γF . We first reduce to the case that T lies in a
single spanning supergraph of F . If T intersects multiple connected components of F̃ , then by the formula
γF̃ (T ) = (e(T ) − 1)/|T |, if we restrict to the sparsest component of T , γF̃ (T ) can only decrease. Now, if
T ⊆ V (F̃ ) lies inside one of the supergraphs of F in F̃ , then to minimize γF̃ (T ), we should choose the
corresponding subset inside F itself; but then, T = S is the γF̃ -minimizing choice by definition of S, as
desired.

Claim. With F̃ as above, wF̃ ≤ γF .

Proof. Define weakly F̃ -saturated graphs Gi as follows. Let K be a clique of size |V (F̃ )| + 1, and choose
an arbitrary set U ⊆ V (K) of size |V (F ) \ S|. Also, fix an arbitrary edge e∗ incident to S. Let V (Gi ) =
K ⊔ S1 ⊔ · · · ⊔ Si where each |Si | = |S|. The edges of Gi are such that K forms a clique, and for each
1 ≤ j ≤ i, the induced subgraph Gi [U ∪ Sj ] is an isomorphic copy of F − e∗ where the isomorphism identifies
Sj with S, except that the subgraph on U is replaced by a clique. There are no edges between two different
Sj .

3     The Sparse Regime
In this section we prove Theorem 1.2. We begin by showing all the values described in the theorem are
achievable.
Lemma 3.1. For any δ ≥ 2, if F is a 2-edge-connected δ-regular graph, then γF = δ/2 − 1/|V (F )|.
Proof. For every ∅ ⊊ S ⊊ V (F ), since F is 2-edge-connected, S must have at least two edges to V (F ) \ S,
so mF (S) ≥ δ|S|/2 + 1. Thus, γF (S) ≥ δ/2 > γF (V (F )) = δ/2 − 1/|V (F )|, as desired.

   Observe that if a δ-regular graph exists on k vertices, then a 2-edge-connected one exists as well, since we
can always force the graph to have a Hamilton cycle. The if direction of Theorem 1.2 thus follows from the
above lemma and Lemma 2.3. It remains to show the only if direction, which states that the only possible
values of wF smaller than δ/2 are those achieved above.
   The intuition for this proof is that when wF is less than δ/2, essentially the only way to form a large
minimum weakly F -saturated graph is to repeatedly copy a sparse connected component. Such a component
may not always be present in an arbitrary member G of wSAT(n, F ), but as we show below, there is a
“rotation” operation we can apply to any such G to obtain a G′ ∈ wSAT(n, F ) with a sparse component.
   In the remainder of this section, F is a fixed graph of minimum degree δ with wF < δ/2, and G ∈
wSAT(n0 , F ) is a minimum weakly F -saturated graph on n0 vertices, where n0 is very large (so that
e(G)/|V (G)| is very close to wF ).
Definition 3.2. Fix an F -bootstrap percolation process on G. Let all vertices of G start out inactive.
When an edge e ̸∈ E(G) is restored in the process, choose a new copy of F formed using e (ignoring any
other new copies of F if there are more) and let a(e) ⊆ V (G) be the set of inactive vertices used in that new
copy. Those vertices are activated by e and are considered active for the rest of the process. We call e the
activating edge of a(e), and we call edges that activate a nonzero number of vertices activating edges.
    Observe that the sets {a(e) : e ̸∈ E(G), a(e) ̸= ∅} partition V (G). (Indeed, if a vertex u is never activated,
then u is complete to the rest of G and is never required for new copies of F in the percolation process,
contradicting that G ∈ wSAT(n0 , F ).) Call this partition the activation partition of G, and denote it by A.
Say that a part a(e) ∈ A owns edges of G with at least one end in a(e) that are used in the new copy of F
formed by e. Edges of G not owned by any a ∈ A are called free.
    The definition of ownership above has several important consequences.
Proposition 3.3. For each edge e ∈ E(G), the following hold.
    1. If there is a part a ∈ A which owns e, then a contains at least one end of e.

    2. At most one part in A owns e.
Proof. The first statement follows directly from the definition. For the second statement, suppose that an
edge e′ ∈ E(G) has one end in each of a(e1 ) and a(e2 ), where e1 is restored before e2 in the percolation
process. We claim that e′ cannot be owned by a(e1 ). Indeed, if a(e1 ) owns e′ , then e′ is used in the new
copy of F formed by e1 , so its end in a(e2 ) is activated by e1 and lies in a(e1 ) as well, a contradiction.

Definition 3.4 (A-matching, rotation). Let Ĝ denote the graph obtained from G by adding the activating
edge of each part in A. The graph Ĝ has the same vertex set, activation partition, and ownership of edges
as G. Additionally, for Ĝ, if an activating edge has at least one end in the part it activates, then it is owned
by that part; otherwise, it is free.
   Define an A-matching to be a subset M ⊆ E(Ĝ) consisting of exactly one edge owned by each part of A.
A graph G′ is called a rotation of G if it is obtained from Ĝ by removing an A-matching.

   We remark that Proposition 3.3 holds for Ĝ as well as for G.

Lemma 3.5. Let G′ be a rotation of G ∈ wSAT(n0 , F ). Then, G′ ∈ wSAT(n0 , F ).

Proof. The graphs G, Ĝ, and G′ have the same vertex set. In the F -bootstrap percolation process on G,
say that the parts a1 , . . . , ak of A were activated in that order by edges e1 , . . . , ek . Label the edges of the
A-matching removed from Ĝ to form G′ as e′1 , . . . , e′k where part ai owns edge e′i . Define an F -bootstrap
percolation process on G′ by restoring edges in the same order as in G, but when an edge ei is restored in
G, instead restore e′i in G′ .
   We see that this is a valid percolation process from the following observation, which can be proved by
induction running the percolation processes for G and G′ in synchrony. At every point in time, if A is the
current set of activated vertices, then the induced subgraphs G[A] and G′ [A] are the same.
   Thus G′ ∈ wsat(n0 , F ), and the fact that G′ ∈ wSAT(n0 , F ) follows from e(G′ ) = e(G).

   The next definition applies to any partition of V (G), not necessarily the activation partition.

Definition 3.6 (Density). Let P be any partition of V (G) and suppose each edge of G is owned by exactly
one part in P. Denote |p| to be the number of vertices in the part p ∈ P and m∗ (p) to be the number of
edges owned by p. Then, the density of p is given by d(p) = m∗ (p)/|p|.

    We emphasize that to talk about the density of a part in a partition P, we require that ownership is
defined for all edges of G.

Definition 3.7. Recall that a mixed multigraph M is one with both directed and undirected edges, and
its underlying undirected graph is the graph on the same vertex set obtained by placing an edge between
two vertices if and only if there is at least one edge between them in M (undirected or directed in either
direction).

   Let M be a mixed multigraph with a distinguished vertex v such that there are no out-edges from v and
no undirected edges incident to v. Say that M is out-connected if after deleting any one out-edge from each
vertex of M , any two vertices other than v are still connected in the underlying undirected graph.

Proposition 3.8. If a mixed multigraph M with distinguished vertex v is out-connected and has d0 directed
edges not to v, d1 directed edges to v, e undirected edges, and s vertices besides v, then at least one of the
following inequalities hold: 12 d0 + e ≥ s − 1, or 12 (d0 + d1 ) + e ≥ s.

Proof. We proceed by induction on s. The base case s = 1 is trivial, so assume s > 1 and that the statement
is true for smaller s. For each vertex m ∈ V (M ) except for v, either (i) an undirected edge has m as one
end and some m′ ̸= m as the other, (ii) there are two out-edges from m (possibly to v), or else (iii) there are
two out-edges from some vertex m′ ̸= m to m. Indeed, if none of these occur, then by deleting an out-edge
from each vertex of M , we can disconnect m from the rest of the graph: we simply delete the (at most one)
out-edge from m and the (at most one) out-edge from each m′ ̸= m to m.
    First suppose case (i) or (iii) holds for some pair m, m′ . Note that by assumption, neither m nor m′
is v. Form a mixed multigraph M̃ by contracting the undirected edge or the two directed edges (in case
(i) or (iii) respectively). That is, remove all edges between m and m′ and replace those two vertices by a
single vertex m̃; edges with an end in m or m′ now have an end in m̃, with directions inherited from their
directions in M . No other edges are destroyed by contraction as we allow parallel edges. Our assumptions on
v clearly hold for M̃ . Now M̃ is out-connected since M is: if by removing an out-edge from each vertex of M̃
we could disconnect two non-v vertices of M̃ , then removing the corresponding edges of M disconnects the
corresponding non-v vertices of M , a contradiction. Say M̃ has d′0 directed edges not to v, d′1 directed edges
to v, and e′ undirected edges. The induction hypothesis applied to M̃ tells us that either 12 d′0 +e′ ≥ (s−1)−1
or 12 (d′0 + d′1 ) + e′ ≥ s − 1. Uncontracting the undirected edge or two directed edges, we have e = e′ + 1 or
d0 = d′0 + 2; in either case, 12 d0 + e = 12 d′0 + e′ + 1, and furthermore, d1 = d′1 . Thus either 21 d0 + e ≥ s − 1
or 12 (d0 + d1 ) + e ≥ s, as desired.
    Thus we may assume cases (i) and (iii) do not hold for any pair m, m′ , so in particular e = 0. We now
show that d0 + d1 ≥ 2s (the second inequality). If not, then since d0 + d1 counts the total out-degree of all
vertices of M , some vertex m ̸= v of M has at most one out-edge. But then none of (i), (ii), or (iii) hold for
m, a contradiction which finishes the proof.

5     Concluding remarks
5.1    Tuza’s conjecture and a question of Terekhov and Zhukovskii
We first return to the question of Tuza’s Conjecture 1.4, which is still open when wF ∈ [δF /2, δF − 1]. We
remark that for all of the graphs F we construct in the proofs in this paper, we indeed have wsat(n, F ) =
wF n + O(1). This is easy to see since the proof of our main tool for constructions, Lemma 2.3, already shows
that the graph F̃ guaranteed by that lemma has wsat(n, F̃ ) = γF n + O(1).
    Terekhov and Zhukovskii [17] gave some intuition for Tuza’s conjecture by remarking that for many
graphs F , an asymptotically minimum sequence of weakly F -saturated graphs can be formed by repeatedly

taking the disjoint union of H, K7 , and K100 , and then placing one edge from each of two distinct vertices
of the K7 to a vertex of the K100 (see Figure 3). We claim that wF = 15/7, which is clearly not attained by
any γF (S).

    To prove wF ≥ 15/7, first observe that each component of our graph F is two-edge-connected, so if a
graph G has multiple connected components, it cannot be weakly F -saturated as it is impossible to restore

Claim. For any ∅ ⊊ S ⊆ V (F ) such that S does not contain H, we have γF (S) > 15/7.

Proof. First recall that if S has vertices in both connected components, then restricting to just one of the
two results in a lower value of γF (S), so we can assume S ⊊ H or S ⊆ F \ H. In the former case, easy
casework by checking |S| = 1, 2, . . . , 6 directly shows that mF (S) > 15/7. In the latter case, since all vertices
in F \ S have degree at least 6, we have mF (S) ≥ 3|S|. If |S| ≤ 2, then we always have γF (S) ≥ 6, while
otherwise, (mF (S) − 1)/|S| ≥ 3 − 1/|S| > 15/7.

    Now, let G ∈ wSAT(n, F ). Consider the activation partition A and edge ownership of G (see Section 3).
Recall that when a new edge e is restored, a new copy F ′ of F is formed; the inactive vertices of G which
are used in F ′ are activated and we call those vertices a(e) ∈ A, and the edges of G incident to a(e) which
are used in F ′ are owned by a(e). We modify the definition of ownership slightly: if a(e) contains all seven
vertices of H in F ′ , then a(e) additionally owns an edge between one of those vertices and a vertex in F ′ \ H.
Such an edge, which is not part of F ′ , must exist in G because G is connected.

activation partition (see Proposition 3.3). Indeed, if a part a(e) is given ownership of an edge e′ which is
not part of the copy F ′ formed by e, then all the vertices of H in F ′ are in a(e), and e′ is incident to one of
those vertices. This means that e′ could not be owned by a previously activated part; if it were, then one of
the vertices of H in F ′ would be in that part of the activation partition instead of a(e).

5.2    Other open questions
Theorem 1.1 raises the natural question of whether weak saturation limits must be rational. We conjecture
that the answer is yes.

Conjecture 5.1. For any graph F , wF is rational.

   This would also follow from the stronger statement that once n is large enough, minimum weakly F -
saturated graphs on n vertices can be formed by copying a fixed subgraph many times. Note that Theorem 1.2
implies that if wF < δF /2, then wF is rational; however, there could be irrational values of wF between δF /2
and δF − 1.
   We close by discussing an analog of weak saturation limits for hypergraphs. Recently, Shapira and Ty-
omkyn [15] proved that for any r-uniform hypergraph H, the limit CH := limn→∞ wsat(n, H)/ns−1 exists,
where s = s(H) is the minimum size of a vertex subset contained in exactly one edge of H. Another proof
was later provided by Terekhov [16]. It would be interesting to characterize the possible values of CH for
any given uniformity r ≥ 3.
