                                                                                              Abstract
                                                     For a graph G, and a nonnegative integer g, let ag (G) be the number of 2-cell embeddings
                                                 of G in an orientable surface of genus g (counted up to the combinatorial homeomorphism
                                                 equivalence). In 1989, Gross, Robbins, and Tucker [21] proposed a conjecture that the sequence
                                                 a0 (G), a1 (G), a2 (G), . . . is log-concave for every graph G. This conjecture is reminiscent to the
                                                 Heron–Rota–Welsh Log Concavity Conjecture that was recently resolved in the affirmative by
                                                 June Huh et al. [24, 1], except that it is closer to the notion of ∆-matroids than to the usual
                                                 matroids. In this short paper, we disprove the Log Concavity Conjecture of Gross, Robbins,
                                                 and Tucker by providing examples that show strong deviation from log-concavity at multiple
                                                 terms of their genus sequences.


                                           1    Introduction
                                           The genus sequence (also called the genus distribution) of a graph G is the sequence (ag (G))g≥0 ,
                                           whose term ag (G) gives the number of 2-cell embeddings of G in the orientable surface of genus g
                                           (up to ambient homeomorphisms). Due to a variety of applications within mathematics, theoretical
                                           physics and theoretical computer science (see, e.g., [29]), there is abundant literature on the genus
                                           distribution of graphs. The earlier papers were mainly concerned with the average genus compu-
                                           tations [40, 2]. One of the initial motivations for considering the variety of all 2-cell embeddings of
                                           a graph was the possibility of performing local optimization for the minimum genus problem, with
                                           the hope to obtain good approximations (see, e.g., [17] or [48, 10]). Later it became evident that
                                           genus distributions are intimately related to the enumeration of rooted maps on surfaces [32]. We
                                           refer to [29] for extensive treatment of map enumeration and its applications in different areas of
                                           mathematics, such as theoretical physics (quantum field theory, string theory, Feynman diagrams,
                                           the KdV equation), matrix integrals, algebraic number theory (algebraic curves, Galois theory via
                                           Grothendiek’s dessins d’enfants, geometry of moduli spaces of complex algebraic curves), singu-
                                           larity theory. Even the very special case of one-vertex graphs (bouquet of loops) bears interesting
                                           applications, in particular the relationship to the Hopf algebra of chord diagrams and algebraic
                                           invariants of knots and links [29]. The one-vertex maps are isomorphic, via surface duality, to
                                           unicellular maps that have received a lot of attention, see, e.g. [3, 7, 8].
                                                Embedding distributions for specific families have been the subject of extensive research. Fam-
                                           ilies containing vertices of arbitrarily large degrees include bouquets of loops [21, 9, 28], dipoles

structure resembles a long path (see, e.g., [16, 41, 42, 43, 30, 32, 22, 46]) or a long cycle ([38, 49]);
in such structures, the vertices of a path or a cycle are replaced by copies of a small graph.
    While considering embedding distributions of various infinite families, Gross, Robbins, and
Tucker [21] proposed what became known as the Log-Concavity Genus Distribution Conjec-
ture (shortly LCGD Conjecture):

Conjecture 1 (Gross, Robbins, and Tucker, 1989). The genus distribution of every graph is log-
concave.

    The conjecture has been confirmed for many graph families, including bouquets of loops [21],
dipoles [27, 45], graphs constructed by vertex and edge-amalgamations [19], linear sequences of
graphs [20, 13, 12], ring-like families [18, 11], Halin graphs [18], and others.
    The LCGD Conjecture is reminiscent on another famous problem, the Heron–Rota–Welsh log-
concavity conjecture [23, 37, 47], which asserts that the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial
of a matroid form a log-concave sequence. A weaker conjecture that the coefficients are unimodal
has been proposed by Read. In 2012, Huh used algebraic geometry to prove Read’s unimodality
conjecture [24] for graphs, and the more general Heron–Rota–Welsh conjecture for matroids repre-
sented over a field of characteristic 0. The case of matroids representable over a field of a non-zero
characteristic was resolved a bit later by Huh and Katz [25]. Finally, in 2018 the Heron–Rota–Welsh
conjecture was proved in full generality by Adiprasito, Huh, and Katz [1]. All these exciting de-
velopments gave hope to resolve the LCGD Conjecture since the variety of 2-cell embeddings is
related to the notion of ∆-matroids [5, 6, 33, 14] and to the Bollobás-Riordan polynomial [4], both
of which deal with graphs on surfaces and involve contraction-deletion recurrences.
    The main results of this paper are Theorems 3 and 9 that refute the LCGD Conjecture in a
rather spectacular (and unexpected) way. Let us state it first in a weaker form.

Theorem 2. For every k ≥ 1 there is a 4-connected graph whose genus sequence has at least k
terms at which the log-concavity is violated.

    We use standard graph theory language [15] and follow the usual terminology of topological
graph theory [35]. Throughout the paper, G will always be a connected simple graph, and by a
surface we will refer to a closed orientable surface. Let us observe that the analogue of our main
result holds for nonorientable surfaces as well.


2    Multiple log-convexity of genus sequences is viable
Let (an )n∈N be a sequence of non-negative numbers. The kth term ak (k ≥ 1) of this sequence is
said to be log-concave if a2k ≥ ak−1 ak+1 , and it is log-convex if a2k ≤ ak−1 ak+1 . If the corresponding
inequality is strict, we say that the term is strictly log-concave or strictly log-convex, respectively.
The sequence is log-concave if all of its terms are log-concave. These definitions extend to any finite
sequence, which can be viewed as an infinite sequence with zeros appended at the end to make it
infinite.
    The afore-mentioned LCGD Conjecture 1 of Gross, Robbins, and Tucker [21] states that the
genus sequence of every graph is log-concave. In this section we refute this long-standing open
problem by proving that there are graphs whose genus sequence has many strictly log-convex
terms.

Theorem 3. For every integer g ≥ 0 and every k ≥ 2 there is a 4-connected graph Gg,k of genus
g such that the terms ag+1 , ag+3 , ag+5 , . . . , ag+2k−1 of its genus sequence are all strictly log-convex.

    In the proof of this result we will need several properties about combinatorics of 2-cell embed-
dings.1 Suppose that a graph G is 2-cell embedded in a closed (orientable) surface S of genus g.
A closed walk or a cycle in G that bounds a face in this embedding is said to be a facial walk or
facial cycle. A cycle C of G is contractible if it is contractible viewed as a curve on the surface.
This is the same as saying that C splits the surface into two components, one of which is a disk.
More generally, C is surface separating if it splits the surface into two components.
    Combinatorially, C is a non-separating cycle of the graph G if the subgraph G − V (C) is
connected; the cycle C is an induced cycle of G if it is induced as the subgraph of G, i.e. it has no
chords in G. Realizing that facial cycles of 3-connected plane graphs (i.e. graphs of 3-dimensional
convex polyhedra) are induced and non-separating, Tutte [44] called such cycles peripheral. Due to
this relationship, we say that a 2-cell embedding of a graph in a surface is a polyhedral embedding
if every face is bounded by a peripheral cycle.
    If a peripheral cycle in a 2-cell embedded graph is surface separating, then it must be facial,
see [35] for more details. For further use we state this simple fact as a lemma.

Lemma 4. Suppose that C is a peripheral cycle of a graph G that is 2-cell embedded in a surface
S. If C is not facial, then it is surface non-separating.

    More generally, we say that a family C of peripheral cycles in G is peripheral if any two cycles
in the family are disjoint and nonadjacent (i.e., no two vertices on distinct cycles are adjacent) and
G − ∪{V (C) | C ∈ C} is connected.
    The following result is a simple generalization of Lemma 4.

Lemma 5. Suppose that C is a peripheral family of cycles of a graph G that is 2-cell embedded in
a surface S of genus g. If none of the cycles in C is facial, then |C| ≤ g.

Proof. Each cycle in C is surface nonseparating and has a neighbor on each side (otherwise it would
be facial). Those neighbors are not on any of the other cycles in the family. This implies that the
union of all cycles in C is surface non-separating. Consequently, repeatedly cutting the surface along
any cycle in C decreases the genus by 1 (easily seen by using Euler characteristic computation),
and hence the number of cycles is at most g.

    Two facial walks W1 , W2 are said to be cofacial if there is a facial walk W that contains a vertex
in W1 and a vertex in W2 . In particular, two facial walks that intersect are cofacial. The facial
walk W is said to be a cofacial connector for W1 (and at the same time also for W2 ). Given a set
C of facial cycles, and C ∈ C, we denote by ∆(C) the number of different cofacial connectors for C.
The set C is sparse if ∆(C) ≤ 1 for each C ∈ C.

none of the cycles in C is facial. Then the genus of the embedding Π is at least |C|.

3    Many consecutive log-convex terms
One obvious question is whether having only every second term being strongly log-convex is nec-
essary. Our examples can be generalized to obtain the following result.
Theorem 9. For every g ≥ 0 and k ≥ 1, there exists a 4-connected graph of genus g, whose genus
distribution sequence has k consecutive terms that are strongly log-convex.
Proof. The proof is essentially the same as that of Theorem 3 and we do not give all details here.
However, we provide the basic idea.
    The graphs for this theorem are essentially the same as those in the proof of Theorem 3. The
graphs Gg,k have k connecting parts, in each of which three connectors join one stacked antiprism
with another one. We replace the jth triple of connectors with k + j connectors (1 ≤ j ≤ k). We
also make their lengths slightly different, the lengths increasing with j (this will make the proof
work). For some very large d, we let the length of all these q = k + j connectors be cq d, where
                                          q−1   q
                                   cq =       + 2,              k ≤ q ≤ 2k.
                                           q   4k

Note that ck = 1 − 4k < ck+1 < · · · < c2k = 1 and that the function r(q) = q.cq is strictly convex:

                                    2qcq < (q − 1)cq−1 + (q + 1)cq+1 .                                       (4)

Details are left to the reader.
   The genus coefficients ag , ag+1 , . . . , ag+k−1 grow “roughly” with the factor of 2(1+od (1))d , i.e.

                                            ag+t = 2(1+od (1))dt

for 1 ≤ t < k. This growth is realized by joining t + 1 large faces of the last connectors where we
have 2k + 1 connectors of height c2k d = d. However, ag+k , ag+k+1 , . . . , ag+2k grow exponentially
faster. We claim that
                                 ag+k+t = 2ck+t+1 d(k+t+1)(1+od (1))                              (5)
for 0 ≤ t ≤ k. To see that ag+k+t ≥ 2ck+t+1 d(k+t+1) , we use a similar proof as for Claim 1. With
k + t + 1 connectors of height ck+t+1 d we increase genus g to g + k + t to obtain one face where
the sides of these k + t + 1 connectors appear as in the mentioned proof, yielding 2ck+t+1 d(k+t+1)
different embeddings of the same genus.

    To prove that the right hand side of (5) is an upper bound, we have to prove that using the
increase of the genus by k + t as in the lower bound construction above is best possible. To show
this we have to argue that merging k + t + 1 big faces with the highest connectors of height ck+t+1 d
gives much smaller numbers, and that using the gain where we merge k + s faces of height ck+s d
and adding the rest of the genus by merging t − s + 1 faces with the connectors of height c2k d also
gives lower numbers. This comparison reduces to the following two inequalities:

                   ck+t+1 (k + t + 1) > c2k (k + t)      and
                   ck+t+1 (k + t + 1) > ck+s+1 (k + s) + c2k (t − s),     0 ≤ s < t.

Using the fact that c2k = 1, they are both easy to verify and details are left to the reader.
   Finally, (5) implies that for every 0 ≤ t < k the following holds

              2 log2 ag+k+t   ≈   2ck+t+1 d(k + t + 1)
                              ≪ ck+t d(k + t) + ck+t+2 d(k + t + 2)
                              ≈   log2 ag+k+t−1 (1 + od (1)) + log2 ag+k+t+1 (1 + od (1)),

where ≈ means equality up to a factor 1 + o(1), and a ≪ b means that a/b < 1 − ε for some ε > 0.
This harsh inequality follows from (4). This completes the proof.


4    In conclusion
Let us end with a more positive viewpoint. Our counterexamples to the LCGD Conjecture are still
unimodal. As far as the author can tell, the unimodal weakening of the LCGD Conjecture could
(and actually should) still be true.

Conjecture 10. The genus distribution of every graph G is unimodal.

    The following also looks plausible.

Conjecture 11. Every graph that triangulates some surface has log-concave genus distribution.
