            Three spectral invariants do not determine
                  geodesic growth of tree-based RACGs
                                Research draft for MathDB 330264

        31 October 2026 campaign; round 2 dossier prepared 4 November 2026 UTC


                                                Abstract
         We give two explicit trees on 19 vertices with equal adjacency, complement and line-graph
     characteristic polynomials whose right-angled Coxeter groups have different geodesic growth
     with respect to their vertex generators. At length 18 the numbers of individual shortest words are
     respectively 37769749324097905710910 and 37769749324097905710906. A complete local Lean
     certificate proves the negation of the universal finite-tree implication in Ciobanu–Kolpakov,
     arXiv:1504.02774v3, Conjecture 1.3. The formal chain includes the presented-group normal
     form, automaton recognition, and a bridge to cardinalities of individual words. This draft makes
     no minimality, infinite-family, new-normal-form or global-priority claim. The post-publication
     arXiv revision retains the conjecture; the 2016 journal body remains unread, a qualified source-
     version limitation.


1   Statement and scope
For a finite simple graph T on V , put

                    WT = ⟨sv (v ∈ V ) | s2v = 1,     su sv = sv su if {u, v} ∈ E(T )⟩.

Let gT (k) count words of length k in these generators that have minimum length among all words
representing the same element of WT . Different words representing the same element are counted
separately. Let χT (x) = det(xI − AT ); write T for the simple complement and L(T ) for the graph
on unordered edges, adjacent when they share a vertex.

Theorem 1. There are trees G, H on {0, . . . , 18} such that

              χG = χH ,       χ G = χH ,       χL(G) = χL(H) ,       gG (18) − gH (18) = 4.

In particular the three spectral invariants jointly fail to determine the full geodesic-growth series
even among trees.

    Theorem 1 refutes the universal implication in the inspected arXiv source [1, Conjecture 1.3],
as recorded in MathDB 330264. A single unequal coeﬀicient is enough; no asymptotic growth-
rate assertion is involved. The journal bibliographic identity is J. Algebraic Combinatorics 44(2),
249–264 (2016), DOI 10.1007/s10801-016-0667-9. The 2020 arXiv revision is not a 2020 journal
publication. The claim concerns the authenticated arXiv conjecture. The author-submitted v3 (17
January 2020), after the 2016 publication, retains its hypotheses and conclusion. Root has resolved
the source hold as a qualified limitation; the version of record body remains unread.

3   Shortest words in the actual group
Call a word clean when it has no two occurrences of the same letter a with every intervening letter
adjacent to a. The intervening word may be empty.

Lemma 1. A word is shortest in WT if and only if it is clean.

Proof. An offending pair can be brought together by commuting the first occurrence across the
intervening letters; the pair then cancels, shortening the word by two. For the converse, consider
clean words modulo adjacent commuting swaps. These swaps preserve length. An occurrence
of a is movable to the front if every letter before it commutes with a. Define a toggle: delete
that occurrence if it exists, and otherwise prepend a. On clean words there is at most one such
occurrence. The resulting word is clean: a newly offending pair after deletion would already have
been offending, unless blocked by the deleted a; but any letter of a pair straddling the deletion lies
before that occurrence and commutes with a, so it was not blocked by it. In the prepend case the
only new offending pair would make a movable, contrary to the case assumption.
    The toggle is well-defined on swap classes. Moving one commuting pair does not change the
relative order of any noncommuting letters, so deletion of a movable occurrence gives the same swap
class. A second toggle restores the original class: after deletion no other a can become movable,
by cleanliness, and prepending the deleted letter recovers its original position by swaps. Toggles
for adjacent distinct letters commute. Indeed, inserting or deleting one cannot block the other’s
movability, since they commute. Thus the four insertion/deletion cases yield identical classes after
the two operations, with a possible swap of the inserted letters.
    Consequently the toggles define involutive permutations satisfying the presentation relations,
and hence an action of the actual presented group WT on these classes. Each generator changes
length by one. A clean word acting on the empty class gives its own class: read the product action
from right to left, and prepend its successive letters; cleanliness precludes a deletion. If another
word of length r represents the same group element, its action on the empty class reaches that
same class, whose length is at most r. The clean word is therefore shortest.

    In Lean these arguments are separated into NormalForm.lean and TraceAction.lean. Swap
equivalence has no cancellation constructor. Length descends to its quotient, the toggles satisfy the
Coxeter relations, and Mathlib’s presentation universal property constructs the action. The group
length is Mathlib’s minimum generator-word length, not a language-based replacement definition.
This formalization of standard graph-product normal-form reasoning supplies a semantic bridge
and is not claimed as a new normal-form theorem.

4    Recognition and exact individual-word counts
For a clean word w, let S(w) be the letters that can be moved to the end using commuting swaps.
Any two distinct letters in S(w) commute: in their original order the later occurrence must commute
with the earlier one to move that earlier occurrence to the end. Thus S(w) is a clique. Appending
v fails cleanliness exactly when v ∈ S(w). Otherwise

                                    S(wv) = {v} ∪ (S(w) ∩ N (v)).

Indeed, an old letter remains movable exactly if it was movable before and commutes with the
appended letter. Lemma 1 now proves recognition of shortest words by the partial automaton
                                       {
                                         failure,           v ∈ S,
                             δ(S, v) =
                                         {v} ∪ (S ∩ N (v)), v ∈
                                                              / S.

The initial state is empty and every nonfailure state accepts. For a tree, its cliques are empty,
single vertices, or edges; our two machines each have 1 + 19 + 18 = 38 states and 19 input letters.
Failure may equivalently be a nonaccepting sink.
    Here is a direct finite recurrence that makes the counting semantics explicit. For every accepting
state S, set b0 (S) = 1, and put
                                                  ∑
                                    bk+1 (S) =           bk (δ(S, v)).
                                                   v∈V
                                              δ(S,v)̸=failure

Splitting a word into its first letter and its remaining k letters is a bijection onto this disjoint union
of continuation sets. Induction proves that bk (S) counts individual accepted input words of length
k from S. Summation is over letters, including multiplicities when distinct letters have the same
target. Recognition then gives gT (k) = bk (∅).
    ConcreteMachine.lean contains all 722 transition entries for each graph, the state-meaning
specification, and all 19 vectors b0 , . . . , b18 , each with 38 entries. Every transition and arithmetic
recurrence is checked in the kernel. The two final empty-state entries are

                                 bG
                                  18 (∅) = 37769749324097905710910,
                                 bH
                                  18 (∅) = 37769749324097905710906.

This proves Theorem 1. The equality of the earlier coeﬀicients through 17 is recorded computa-
tionally in the independent review and is used only for the source-family comparison below; it is
not a global optimality assertion.
    AutomatonSemantics.lean proves recognition, including reversal to account for the imple-
mentation’s reading convention. WordCounting.lean uses subtypes of functions Fin(k) → V ,
establishes the split equivalence, and proves equality with actual shortest-word cardinality for ev-
ery length. No quotient by commutation or group equality is used in the counted word type.
MachineInvariant.lean identifies the successful state with the exact suﬀix state. The closed
endpoint is MathDB330264.conjecture_false.

5   Comparison with inspected constructions
The mathematical separation is from the joint three invariants, including the additional line-
spectrum protection in [1, Conjecture 1.3]. It is one conjecture refutation. The following com-
parisons incorporate the independent significance review; they do not establish worldwide priority.
    McKay [2, Lemma 4.1 and Theorem 4.2, pp. 223–225] proves bordered matrix identities and
coalesces specified rooted 16-vertex limbs with a common rooted tree. Incidence identities transfer
the construction to line spectra. Ciobanu–Kolpakov [1, Theorem 3.2 and Lemma 3.3] prove equal
geodesic growth for that source family. It cannot by that theorem alone give the unequal-growth
conclusion here. McKay’s Theorem 5.3 reconstructs a tree from a substantially larger collection of
noncommutative adjacency/degree spectral data; it supplies no implication that the growth series
distinguishes all nonisomorphic trees.
    Godsil [3] is a slide deck dated 9 November 2007. Relevant labels are “Spectrum of a 0-Sum”
(PDF p. 22), “Corollary” (p. 24), “Another Pair” (p. 27), and “A Hint” (p. 28), rather than
invented theorem numbers. Ciobanu–Kolpakov [1, Theorem 5.1 and Lemma 5.2] use that rooted
construction to obtain unequal growth but explicitly allow different line spectra. Their proof gives
a nonzero discrepancy at length 10 for every nontrivial common rooted extension: its determining
rooted counts are 8919523 and 8919522, multiplied by twice the positive attachment-root degree.
The present pair agrees at length 10. That is an isomorphism-invariant obstruction to identifying
this pair with that exact family, not an assertion that no composition of known constructions could
refute the conjecture.
    Godsil–McKay [4, Sections 2.1–2.7] give orthogonal switching and attachment-matrix construc-
tions. Their proofs protect adjacency spectra, and in the specified switching setting complement
spectra; they do not generally protect line spectra or establish the required geodesic inequality. We
do not claim that the underlying graphs here are new.
    The automaton is existing machinery [1, Definition 2.2; 5, Proposition 3.2; 7, Theorem 4.9].
Later equality formulas in [5, Theorem 3.7], [6, Theorems 1.1 and 2.2] and [7, Theorem 5.14] assume
link-regularity. Our trees have degrees 1, 2 and 3, violating that condition already on singleton
cliques. Those results do not directly supply this endpoint. The independent review read the
indicated proofs, with the exception that [7, Theorem 6.4] was checked only at statement level and
is not relied upon here.
    The bounded searches and inspected primary bodies identified no complete prior theorem-to-
refutation bridge. Search misses and the historical MathDB open label are not positive novelty
evidence. Registry screening used the dated OpenAI/math head and complete preprints-subtree
receipts described in the review; the root tree was truncated and screening was by paths, not all
bodies. MathDB/Palomar access was denied and not retried. This manuscript inherits those limits
rather than claiming fresh exhaustive clearance.
