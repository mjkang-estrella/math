INTEGER AND MODULAR FLOW RECONFIGURATION: THE MODULI
          UP TO SIX, CHORDS, AND LARGE MODULI

                                               DAVE GOEL

                                      Version 1.20, October 23, 2026.


       Abstract. For a finite multigraph G and an integer k ≥ 2, let Mk (G) be the graph on
       the nowhere-zero Zk -flows of G, and Ik (G) the graph on the integer flows F of G with
       1 ≤ |F (e)| ≤ k − 1 on every edge; a step adds a nonzero constant along one cycle. The
       Esperet–Hendrey–Lagoutte–Marseloo–Norin–Steiner (2025) lifting question asks for a proof
       that Ik (G) is connected whenever Mk (G) is connected; its authors write that they do not
       know how to prove this even for k = 4. We prove it for k = 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and every graph,
       in a stronger form: reduction modulo k induces a bijection between the components of
       Ik (G) and of Mk (G). We prove the same for every k on the graphs in which every cycle
       with more than nine edges has a chord between two vertices that are not neighbors on the
       cycle. We also prove that Ik (G) is connected for every bridgeless multigraph G and every
       k ≥ mc(G) + 2, where mc(G) is the largest number of edges of a cut of G; and that scaling
       by m carries the components of Mk (G) into components of Imk (G). With a theorem of
       Esperet et al. on planar graphs, the first result settles the question for every planar graph
       and every k. The bijection of the components for k ≤ 6 holds more generally for the integer
       and modular kernel vectors of every totally unimodular matrix, with the circuit vectors as
       steps, and in this generality it fails at every prime p ≥ 7, for the tensions of a graph on
       2p vertices; this does not answer the question for the flows of graphs. For the six moduli
       k = 8, 9, 12, 16, 18, 36 we prove the bijection of the components for every graph also; this is
       a short deduction from a lemma on liftable steps of a companion paper. For the other k ≥ 7
       this paper does not settle the question on arbitrary graphs.




                                           1. Introduction
   Graphs are finite multigraphs; loops and parallel edges are allowed. Let k ≥ 2 be an integer.
A modular flow is a nowhere-zero Zk -flow of G, and an integer k-flow is an integer circulation
F with 1 ≤ |F (e)| ≤ k − 1 on every edge. A step adds a nonzero constant along one cycle, and
it is legal if the result is again a flow of the same kind. Mk (G) is the graph on the modular
flows and Ik (G) the graph on the integer k-flows, with the legal steps as edges. These are the
graphs F(G, Zk ) and F(G, k) of Esperet, Hendrey, Lagoutte, Marseloo, Norin and Steiner [1].
The exact definitions are in Section 2, for the second part of the paper in Section 7, and for
matrices in Section 12.

The lifting question. Reduction modulo k maps integer k-flows onto modular flows, and
an integer step to a modular step or to equality. So, if Ik (G) is connected, then Mk (G) is
connected; this is Observation 2.5 of [1]. The converse is the Esperet–Hendrey–Lagoutte–
Marseloo–Norin–Steiner (2025) lifting question, their Problem 2.6:
       “Let G be a graph and k be an integer. Prove that if F(G, Zk ) is connected,
       then F(G, k) is also connected.”

They write directly after it: “We do not know how to prove the result even in the case k = 4.”
Cranston, Li, Su, Wang and Xu state the equivalence of the two connectivity properties as a
conjecture [2, Conjecture A].

The results.
Theorem 1.1. Let G be a finite multigraph, and let k ≥ 2 be an integer.
    (a) If k ≤ 6, then reduction modulo k induces a bijection between the connected components
        of Ik (G) and of Mk (G).
    (b) Suppose that every cycle of G with more than nine edges has an edge outside the cycle
        joining two distinct vertices that are not neighbors on the cycle. Then the conclusion
        of (a) holds for every k ≥ 2. For k = 7 it holds with nine replaced by twenty-one.
    (c) If G is bridgeless and k ≥ mc(G) + 2, where mc(G) is the largest number of edges of
        a cut of G, then Ik (G) is connected.
   Part (a) is Theorem 2.1, part (b) is Theorem 2.2, and part (c) is Theorem 7.1. In the
cases (a) and (b), Ik (G) is connected if and only if Mk (G) is connected; so the implication
of the lifting question holds there, for k = 4 in particular. The statement on the components
says more: Mk (G) need not be connected (for six parallel edges and k = 3 it is not [2,
Theorem 2.1]), and then Ik (G) has exactly as many components, and reduction modulo k
matches them. In case (c) the graph Mk (G) is connected as well, by reduction and lifting
(Lemma 3.2); so for these pairs (G, k) both graphs are connected. For simple graphs the
hypothesis of (b) says that no induced cycle has more than nine edges.
   For planar graphs, part (a) and a theorem of [1] leave no modulus open.
Corollary 1.2. Let G be a planar multigraph, and let k ≥ 2 be an integer. Then Ik (G) is
connected if and only if Mk (G) is connected.
Proof. For k ≤ 6 this follows from Theorem 1.1 (a). Let k ≥ 7; we show that both graphs are
connected. As in Section 2, a reconfiguration graph with at most one vertex is regarded as
connected. If G has a bridge, then G has no flow of either kind, and both graphs are empty.
Suppose that G is bridgeless. A loop is a cycle, and a step on it changes the value of this
loop only, to any other allowed value; and every cycle that is not a loop lies in one component
of G. So each of Ik (G) and Mk (G) is the Cartesian product of the corresponding graphs
of the components of the graph G without its loops, and of one complete graph for each
loop. A component without an edge has one flow of each kind, the empty map. Every other
component is a 2-edge-connected loopless planar graph, and its two reconfiguration graphs are
connected by [1, Corollary 5.9]. A finite product of nonempty connected graphs is connected,
and a product with an empty factor is empty.                                                 □

   For k ≥ 7 we prove the statement of the lifting question on arbitrary graphs only at the six
moduli 8, 9, 12, 16, 18 and 36 (Theorem 18.1; see “Six more moduli” below). For the other
k ≥ 7 this paper does not settle it on arbitrary graphs; by Corollary 1.2, a graph for which it
is not settled here is not planar. The smallest such modulus is k = 7: by Corollary 4.2 and
Theorem 2.3, the only modular steps that are not treated there have size 3 or 4 and lie on
cycles with at least twenty-two edges. That this paper does not settle a modulus does not
mean that it is open: at k = 40, at k = 48 and at every multiple k of 4 with k ≥ 52 the earlier
paper [4] proves that both graphs of flows are connected for every graph with no bridge and
no loop (see “What was known, and two companion papers” below).

Six more moduli. For k = 8, 9, 12, 16, 18, 36 the conclusion of part (a) of Theorem 1.1 holds
for every graph: this is Theorem 18.1. Its proof, in Sections 18 to 20, is a short deduction from
a lemma of the earlier paper [4] on the steps that can be lifted (its Lemma 1.12): at each of
the six moduli, every element other than 0 is one of the elements that the lemma names. The
six moduli were found in the work on that paper and are not stated in it. Section 17 names
the four statements of [4, Section 1] that the proof uses; their proofs are there. Corollary 18.2
applies the theorem, with two results of the companion paper [5] on the Zk -flows, to the
graphs in which every connected component has at most eight, at most twelve, or at most
twenty-four vertices of odd degree. The theorem and the corollary make no statement on any
other modulus k ≥ 7, and we give no classification of the moduli for which this deduction
works; the modulus 7 is not one of them (Remark 20.1).

Totally unimodular matrices. Part (a) of Theorem 1.1 is not special to graphs. Let A be a
finite integer matrix with column set E that is totally unimodular : each square subdeterminant
is 0, 1 or −1. An integer k-flow of A is a vector F ∈ ZE with AF = 0 and 1 ≤ |Fe | ≤ k − 1 for
every e, and a modular k-flow of A is a vector f ∈ (Zk )E with Af = 0 in Zk and fe ̸= 0 for
every e. A circuit vector is an integer vector in the kernel of A whose support is not empty
and is minimal among the supports of the nonzero real kernel vectors, and whose nonzero
entries have greatest common divisor one; its entries are 0, 1 and −1 (Lemma 13.2). A step
adds a nonzero multiple of a circuit vector. Ik (A) and Mk (A) are the graphs on the integer
and on the modular k-flows of A, with the legal steps as edges. The exact definitions are in
Section 12.
Theorem 1.3. The following two statements hold.
  (a) For every totally unimodular matrix A and every k ∈ {2, 3, 4, 5, 6}, reduction modulo
      k induces a bijection between the connected components of Ik (A) and of Mk (A).
  (b) For every prime p ≥ 7 there is a totally unimodular matrix A, representing the ten-
      sion space of a finite simple graph, for which reduction modulo p does not induce an
      injection on connected components: a component of Mp (A) with two vertices has lifts
      in at least two different components of Ip (A).
   Part (a) is Theorem 12.1, and part (b) is Theorem 12.2. The integer kernels of totally
unimodular matrices are regular chain groups in the sense of Tutte [6]. If A is the oriented
incidence matrix of a graph G, then the flows of A are the flows of G and the circuit vectors
are the signed indicator vectors of the cycles of G; so for graphs part (a) is Theorem 1.1 (a)
again. Its proof in Sections 12 to 14 is shorter than the proof in Sections 3 to 6: it rounds a
real vector directly and uses no cut condition and no count of intervals. This shorter proof is
not new for graphs: it is the proof by the lemmas of [4], carried over to matrices (see “Relation
to our earlier paper” below).
   Part (b) refutes the bijection of the components in the wider setting; it does not refute
the implication of the lifting question, and it is not a statement on the flows of a graph. Its
matrices represent tensions, the vectors of the differences of a vertex potential along the edges,
of a graph Hp that consists of two complete graphs on p vertices and of p(p − 2) edges between
them. This graph contains K5 , so its tension space is not the flow space of a graph (see the
remark at the end of Section 16); and the graph Mp (A) of the example is not connected, so
the example does not even contradict the statement “if Mp (A) is connected, then Ip (A) is
connected”. What it shows is that a proof of the bijection of the components for the flows of
all graphs at a prime p ≥ 7 cannot be a proof for all totally unimodular matrices. Theorem 1.3
claims nothing for composite moduli above six, and nothing for the flows of graphs at k = 7.

What was known, and two companion papers. Esperet et al. [1, Theorem 7.1] prove
that F(G, k) is connected at some modulus k for each loopless 2-edge-connected graph G; the
modulus chosen in their proof is 36|E(G)|(|V (G)| + 1). For planar graphs they prove more
[1, Corollary 5.9]: for every 2-edge-connected planar graph G, every integer k ≥ 7 and every
abelian group A with at least seven elements, the graphs F(G, k) and F(G, A) are connected.
So the lifting question was answered for these pairs (G, k); their Corollaries 5.10 to 5.12 lower
the bound 7 for planar graphs of higher edge-connectivity and for outerplanar graphs. The
graphs of [1] may have multiple edges, but no loop.
   The paper [4] proves that Mk (G) and Ik (G) are both connected for every finite bridgeless
loopless multigraph G if k = 40, if k = 48, or if k is a multiple of 4 with k ≥ 52 [4,
Theorem 9.2]. For one fixed graph G it gives every modulus from a bound on: every k ≥
max(48, c(G)+4), where c(G) is the number of edges of a longest cycle of G, and every k ≥ 40
if c(G) ≤ 39 [4, Corollary 10.6]; every k ≥ 40 if G has at most 189 edges [4, Corollary 10.12]
or at most 149 vertices [4, Corollary 10.16]. It claims nothing for a modulus below 40 on all
graphs, and nothing for a prime modulus on all graphs; but some statements of the present
paper follow from its lemmas, and we say which under “Relation to our earlier paper” below.
The paper [5] proves the Cranston–Li–Su–Wang–Xu (2026) conjecture [2, Conjecture 4.1] for
the integer flows: both graphs are connected for every graph with all degrees even and every
k ≥ 4; and that the implication of the lifting question holds for these graphs for every k ≥ 2.
   Part (c) of Theorem 1.1 is a bound of the same kind as the bound max(48, c(G) + 4) of [4],
with another parameter, and neither contains the other. For the complete graph K4 we have
mc(K4 ) = 4, so (c) gives every k ≥ 6, and [4] gives every k ≥ 40. For the complete bipartite
graph K3,n with n ≥ 3 we have c = 6 and mc = 3n, so [4] gives every k ≥ 40, and (c)
gives every k ≥ 3n + 2. The proof of (c) uses no base modulus: it uses Hoffman’s circulation
theorem and a walk through the regions of a hyperplane arrangement.

The proofs in a few lines. Small moduli and chords (Sections 2 to 6 ). Every modular flow
has integer lifts, and all of them lie in one component of Ik (G) (Lemma 3.2). So it is enough
to show, for one legal modular step f → f + cχC , that some lift of f and some lift of f + cχC
lie in one component. A pair of adjacent lifts whose difference lies on C exists if and only
if one of two cut conditions of Hoffman type holds (Lemma 3.3). A cut that violates such a
condition meets the cycle C in many intervals, and this needs many edges of C (Lemma 4.1).
So steps of size ±1, ±2 always have adjacent lifts, and so has every step on a short cycle. For
k ≤ 5 there is no other size. For k = 6 the size 3 is treated by a path through other modular
flows (Lemma 5.1). A chord between two vertices that are not neighbors on C splits a step on
C into two steps of the same size on shorter cycles, unless 2c = 0 modulo k (Lemma 6.1); the
case 2c = 0 is the step of size k/2 of Lemma 5.1. This gives (b) by induction on the length of
the cycle.
   Large moduli (Sections 7 to 10 ). All integer k-flows that are positive on the same orien-
tation lie in one component (Lemma 8.1). If two strong orientations differ on one edge and
k ≥ mc(G) + 2, then Hoffman’s theorem gives two adjacent integer k-flows, one for each ori-
entation (Lemma 8.2). In a 3-edge-connected graph the strong orientations are the regions of
the arrangement of the hyperplanes z(e) = 0 in the space of real circulations, and a segment
in general position joins two regions by crossing one hyperplane at a time (Lemma 9.1). Loops
and cuts with two edges are removed first (Lemma 10.1).
   Scaling (Section 11 ). For a modular step f → f + cχC with 1 ≤ c ≤ k − 1, the maps
mf + sχC are nowhere zero modulo mk for s = 0, for s = mc, and for every s between them
that m does not divide. Consecutive ones differ by a step of size 1 or 2, and such steps have
adjacent integer lifts (Lemma 11.4).
   Totally unimodular matrices (Sections 12 to 16 ). If x ∈ [0, 1]E and Ax is integral, then
there is z ∈ {0, 1}E with Az = Ax that agrees with x wherever x is integral (Lemma 13.1).
This gives the integer lifts of a modular flow, and a lift that takes a given step of size ±1

                              2. Definitions and statements
   Graphs are finite multigraphs, with loops and parallel edges allowed. A cycle is a connected
2-regular subgraph, including a loop or two parallel edges. Give each edge a reference direction.
For a cycle C with a direction of traversal, χC is 1 on edges traversed forward, −1 on edges
traversed backward, and 0 elsewhere. The divergence of a map on edges is its outgoing sum
minus its incoming sum at each vertex; a loop cancels in this sum.
   For k ≥ 2, a Zk -flow is an edge map with divergence zero in Zk ; a modular flow will mean
a nowhere-zero Zk -flow. A circulation has divergence zero at every vertex. An integer k-flow
is an integer circulation F with 1 ≤ |F (e)| ≤ k − 1 on every edge. Their reconfiguration
graphs are Mk (G) and Ik (G): a step adds a nonzero constant times χC , and both endpoints
must be flows of the specified kind. An integer lift of a modular flow has the same edge
values modulo k. Reduction modulo k maps an integer step to a modular step or to equality.
A reconfiguration graph with at most one vertex is regarded as connected. Our Ik (G) and
Mk (G) are the graphs F(G, k) and F(G, Zk ) of [1]. That paper treats loopless graphs; here
loops are allowed.
   The lifting question of Esperet et al. [1, Problem 2.6] asks for a proof that connectivity of
Mk (G) implies connectivity of Ik (G). They state their belief in this implication and write
immediately after the question: “We do not know how to prove the result even in the case
k = 4.” Cranston et al. state as a conjecture [2, Conjecture A] the equivalence of the two
connectivity properties; the reverse implication follows by reduction and lifting. We prove the
stronger component correspondence for small moduli on all graphs, and for every modulus on
the following structural classes.
   A direct integer lift of a modular step f → f + cχC is a pair of adjacent integer lifts, one
of f and one of f + cχC , whose difference is supported on C. We say that a lift F accepts

an integer increment a on C if F + aχC is an integer k-flow. A c-step means a modular step
with increment c.
Theorem 2.1. For every finite multigraph G and every k ∈ {2, 3, 4, 5, 6}, reduction modulo
k induces a bijection between the connected components of Ik (G) and Mk (G).
Theorem 2.2. Suppose every cycle of G with more than nine edges has an edge outside
the cycle joining two distinct vertices that are not neighbors on the cycle. Then, for every
k ≥ 2, reduction modulo k induces a bijection between the connected components of Ik (G) and
Mk (G). For k = 7, the same conclusion holds with nine replaced by twenty-one.
   For simple graphs the hypothesis says that no induced cycle is longer than the stated bound:
a cycle is induced exactly when it has no edge joining two of its nonneighboring vertices. The
explicit nonneighbor-chord condition also applies to multigraphs. An additional edge parallel
to a cycle edge does not provide the strict shortening used below.
Theorem 2.3. Every legal modular step on a cycle of length at most nine has two adjacent
integer lifts, for every k ≥ 3. For k = 7, the same holds for length at most twenty-one.
  Our proof for k = 6 treats increment 3 by a path between the lifting fibres, without requiring
a direct integer lift of that step. For k ≥ 7, the results of Sections 2 to 6 do not assert the
conjecture for arbitrary graphs; the six composite moduli 8, 9, 12, 16, 18, 36 are treated in
Theorem 18.1.

                         3. Lifting facts and prescribed signs
  We include proofs of the lifting facts used below. A possibly-zero Zk -flow may be lifted
with its zero edges kept zero.
Lemma 3.1 (rounding). If x : E(G) → [0, 1] has integral divergence, there is z : E(G) →
{0, 1} with the same divergence and with z(e) = x(e) wherever x(e) is integral.
Proof. The fractional edges, if nonempty, contain a cycle. A fractional loop is already a
cycle; otherwise a vertex cannot have just one incident fractional edge, by integral divergence.
Choose a cycle of fractional edges and one direction of traversal. Add a positive multiple
of its vector χC , as far as possible while staying in [0, 1]. At least one fractional coordinate
becomes integral, and no integral coordinate changes. Repeat until none is fractional.         □
   The lifting and connected-fibre assertions are also given in [1, Theorem 2.3 and Lemma
5.5], respectively; the numbering is that of its fourth version.
Lemma 3.2. Every modular flow has an integer lift, and all its integer lifts lie in one compo-
nent of Ik (G). A possibly-zero Zk -flow has an integer lift with absolute values at most k − 1
and exactly the same zero edges.
Proof. Represent the modular values by integers r(e) ∈ {0, . . . , k − 1}. The vector r/k has
integral divergence. Round it to z by Lemma 3.1; then F = r −kz is a circulation. If r(e) = 0,
rounding fixes z(e) = 0. Otherwise F (e) is r(e) or r(e) − k, both nonzero and of absolute
value at most k − 1.
   For two lifts F, F ′ of a nowhere-zero flow, (F ′ − F )/k is an integer circulation valued
in {−1, 0, 1}. Orient its nonzero edges positively and decompose them into edge-disjoint
directed cycles. Adding k successively on those cycles gives intermediate edge values that are
individually old or final, hence are valid integer k-flow values.                           □

                              7. Statement and definitions
   Graphs are finite multigraphs; loops and parallel edges are allowed. A cycle is a connected
2-regular subgraph, including a loop or two parallel edges. Choose a reference direction for
every edge. For a cycle C with a direction of traversal, χC is 1 on edges traversed forward, −1
on edges traversed backward, and 0 elsewhere. The divergence of an edge map is its outgoing
sum minus its incoming sum at each vertex. A loop cancels in this difference. A circulation
has divergence zero at every vertex.
   For an integer k ≥ 2, an integer k-flow is an integer circulation F with 1 ≤ |F (e)| ≤ k−1 on
every edge. The graph F(G, k) has these flows as vertices; two are adjacent if their difference
is aχC for an integer a ̸= 0 and a cycle C with a direction of traversal. Thus both endpoints
of a step must be integer k-flows. All flows use the same reference directions. These flows
are the nowhere-zero k-flows of [1], whose step definition agrees: a circulation supported on
one cycle is constant along its traversal. A reconfiguration graph with at most one vertex is
regarded as connected; the convention for the empty case agrees with [1].
   Write δG (S) for the edges with exactly one endpoint in S ⊆ V (G), and set
                                   mc(G) = max |δG (S)|.
                                             S⊆V (G)

Loops belong to no cut. A graph is bridgeless if no edge is a bridge; it need not be connected.
A graph is 3-edge-connected if it is connected, has at least two vertices, and every nonempty
proper vertex set has a cut of size at least three.
Theorem 7.1. Let G be a finite bridgeless multigraph. For every integer k ≥ mc(G) + 2, the

                               12. Setting and statements
   Let A be a finite integer matrix with column set E. It is totally unimodular if each square
subdeterminant is 0, 1, or −1. An integer k-flow of A, for k ≥ 2, is a vector F ∈ ZE with
AF = 0 and 1 ≤ |Fe | ≤ k − 1 for every e ∈ E. A modular k-flow is a vector f ∈ (Zk )E
with Af = 0 in Zk and fe ̸= 0 for every e. A circuit vector is a primitive integer vector in
ker A with nonempty support minimal under inclusion among supports of nonzero real kernel
vectors. Primitivity means that its nonzero coordinates have greatest common divisor one.
Such a vector is determined up to sign by its support. For a totally unimodular matrix its
coordinates belong to {−1, 0, 1}, as proved below.
   Let Ik (A) and Mk (A) have the integer and modular k-flows, respectively, as vertices. Two
vertices are adjacent when their difference is a nonzero scalar multiple of a circuit vector, with
the scalar taken over Z or Zk , respectively. A step is legal only if both endpoints are vertices.
Reduction modulo k sends an integer step to a modular step or to equality. A graph with at
most one vertex is regarded as connected. When E is empty, the empty vector is the unique
flow of either kind.
Theorem 12.1. For every totally unimodular matrix A and every k ∈ {2, 3, 4, 5, 6}, reduction
modulo k induces a bijection between the connected components of Ik (A) and Mk (A).
Theorem 12.2. For every prime p ≥ 7 there is a totally unimodular matrix A representing
the tension space of a finite simple graph for which reduction modulo p does not induce an
injection on connected components. In fact a component of Mp (A) with two vertices has lifts
in at least two different components of Ip (A).
16                                          DAVE GOEL

   The integer kernels considered here are regular chain groups in Tutte’s sense [6]: every min-
imal nonzero support carries a kernel vector whose nonzero coefficients are 1 or −1. Oriented
incidence matrices give the graphic flow setting; our integer k-flows are the nowhere-zero k-
flows of Esperet et al. [1]. For these matrices, Theorem 12.1 recovers the graph component
correspondence through six, with a shorter proof using rounding directly instead of a cut and
interval argument. The implication from modular connectedness to integer connectedness for
graph flows is the lifting question of Esperet et al. [1, Problem 2.6]. Theorem 12.1 gives a
stronger statement in a broader setting at these small moduli. Theorem 12.2 does not refute
that question for graphs: its examples are tension spaces, and their modular reconfiguration
graphs are themselves disconnected.

                           13. Rounding and conformal circuits
   Negating a column of A and the corresponding flow coordinate preserves total unimodu-
larity and both reconfiguration graphs. We use these coordinate reorientations freely.
Lemma 13.1 (Rounding). Suppose A is totally unimodular, x ∈ [0, 1]E , and Ax is integral.
There is z ∈ {0, 1}E with Az = Ax and ze = xe on every coordinate where xe is integral.
Proof. The nonempty bounded polytope defined by these equalities and 0 ≤ z ≤ 1 has
a vertex. Its constraint matrix consists of rows of A, their negatives, and signed coordinate
rows, and is totally unimodular. At a vertex a full-rank square subsystem of active constraints
determines the coordinates. Its determinant is 1 or −1 and its right-hand side is integral, so
that vertex is integral. The assertion is immediate for the empty coordinate set.            □
     A real vector u is conformal to Y if supp(u) ⊆ supp(Y ) and ue Ye > 0 for every e ∈ supp(u).
Lemma 13.2. Every circuit vector of a totally unimodular matrix has nonzero coordinates in
{−1, 1}. Every integer vector Y ∈ ker A is a sum of circuit vectors, all supported on supp(Y )
and conformal to Y .
Proof. The kernel restricted to a circuit support is one-dimensional: two independent vectors
on that support could be linearly combined to cancel one coordinate without cancelling them
all, contrary to minimality. Thus on a circuit support of size s, the columns have rank
s − 1. Choose s − 1 independent rows of this restricted matrix. Every remaining row is a
linear combination of them, so their kernel is the kernel of the full restricted matrix. The
cofactor formula gives a nonzero kernel vector whose coordinates are its signed maximal
minors. Minimality of the circuit implies that none of these coordinates vanishes. Total
unimodularity therefore makes them all 1 or −1. For a singleton circuit the column is zero
and the assertion is immediate.
   For the decomposition, restrict to supp(Y ) and reorient so that Y is positive there. Choose
a nonzero nonnegative real kernel vector with minimal support. Its support is a circuit:
otherwise a kernel direction on a proper subset of that support could be added or subtracted
until a coordinate vanishes, leaving a nonzero nonnegative vector of smaller support. The
corresponding positive circuit vector has value 1 on its support. Subtract it from Y . The
result is nonnegative and integral, and its coordinate sum strictly decreases. Repetition
terminates at zero.                                                                          □
Lemma 13.3 (Lifts and fibres). Every modular k-flow has an integer k-lift, and all its integer
lifts are in one component of Ik (A). More generally a possibly-zero modular kernel vector has
an integer lift of absolute values at most k − 1 with exactly the same zero coordinates.

                       14. Small steps and half-modulus steps
Lemma 14.1. For k ≥ 3, the two endpoints of every legal modular step of increment 1, −1, 2,
or −2 have adjacent integer lifts.

bound. Any integer lift works.

adjacent integer lifts. Reverse the circuit vector for the negative increments.            □
Lemma 14.2. Let k = 2d with d ≥ 2. For every legal modular step f → f + dγ, all integer
lifts of its two endpoints belong to one component of I2d (A).

with exactly that zero set by Lemma 13.3. No coordinate of supp(γ) lies in E0 : there the only

                     15. Tensions as totally unimodular kernels
   For a connected loopless graph H, an integer tension is the vector of oriented differences
of an integer vertex potential, taken up to a constant. The modular definition uses vertex
potentials in Zk . A nowhere-zero modular tension is therefore a proper k-colouring up to a
constant. The integer bound requires all edge differences to have absolute values in {1, . . . , k −
1}.
Lemma 15.1. The integer and modular tension spaces of a connected loopless graph with at
least two vertices are kernels of one totally unimodular integer matrix. Their circuit steps are
exactly shifts by a constant on one side of a bond.
Proof. Let D be the oriented incidence matrix of H with one row deleted, and partition its
columns into a spanning tree T and the remaining edges N . The square matrix DT has
determinant ±1, by leaf elimination. Put B = DT−1 DN . An edge vector v is a tension exactly
when vN = B T vT , over both the integers and Zk , because DT−1 is integral. Hence the tension
matrix is [−B T I].
   The incidence matrix is totally unimodular: in any square submatrix a column with at
most one nonzero permits determinant expansion, while if every column has two nonzeros
their signs are opposite and the row sum is zero. For equally sized row and column index sets
I, J of B, replace the tree columns in positions I of DT by the columns of DN indexed by J,
obtaining R. The matrix DT−1 R is an identity matrix with those columns replaced by columns
of B. Expanding along its unchanged identity columns gives det R/ det DT = ± det B[I, J].
Since R consists of columns of D, this determinant is 0 or ±1. Thus B is totally unimodular;
transposition and adjoining identity columns show the same for [−B T I].
   The minimal nonzero tension supports are bonds. Indeed, a nonconstant real potential has
a nonempty level cut contained in its tension support, so minimal support must be a minimal
nonempty cut. In a connected graph a nonempty cut is inclusion-minimal exactly when both
induced sides are connected; such a cut is a bond. Conversely, when both sides of a cut are
connected, any potential with differences supported on that cut is constant on each side. Its
primitive tension vector is the signed indicator of the bond. Its scalar multiples shift one side
by a constant.                                                                                 □

                        16. Failure at each prime from seven on
Proof of Theorem 12.2. Fix a prime p ≥ 7. Let S = {si : i ∈ Zp } and T = {ti : i ∈ Zp } each
induce a complete graph. Insert a cross edge si tj exactly when 1 − j + i ∈    / {0, 3} modulo p.
This defines a connected simple graph Hp with 2p vertices and p(p − 1) + p(p − 2) = 2p2 − 3p
edges. By Lemma 15.1 its tension space has a totally unimodular kernel representation A.
   Define colours c(si ) = −i and c(tj ) = 1 − j in Zp . The cross-edge differences c(tj ) − c(si )
exhaust all residues except 0 and 3. Consequently c and c′ = c + 31S are proper colourings,
and shifting S by 3 is a legal bond move.
   We first show that their modular tension component has exactly two vertices. In any proper
colouring of a Kp block, the colours exhaust Zp . Suppose a legal move by a ̸= 0 modulo p
shifts a subset of that block whose colour set is U . Avoiding collisions with unchanged vertices
                       INTEGER AND MODULAR FLOW RECONFIGURATION                                    19

requires U + a ⊆ U , hence U + a = U . Primality implies U is empty or all of Zp . Thus each
block is shifted wholly or not at all. Modulo a global constant, every nontrivial move shifts S
only. Relative to c, such a shift is proper only at offsets 0 and 3. These are distinct tensions,
so the component is exactly {c, c′ }.
   We show that no integer move joins a lift of c to a lift of c′ . Its reduction is a nontrivial
modular move, since c ̸= c′ . An integer bond step and its nontrivial reduction have exactly
the same support. The block argument therefore makes its side S or T . Shifting T by an
integer is, up to a global constant, shifting S by its negative. Thus the move shifts S by an
integer b ≡ 3 (mod p). A crossing edge changes its difference by b; both endpoint differences
have absolute value at most p − 1, so |b| ≤ 2p − 2. Therefore
                                  b ∈ {3, 3 − p, 3 + p, 3 − 2p}.
For a lift with vertex heights h, an initial cross difference of residue ρ ∈ {1, . . . , p − 1} \ {3}
is ρ or ρ − p; after the move it decreases by b.
   If b = 3, the edges of residues 1 and 2 must use their positive representatives. Thus
                           h(ti ) = h(si ) + 1,    h(ti−1 ) = h(si ) + 2,
giving h(ti−1 ) = h(ti ) + 1 for every index. Summing around Zp is impossible.
   If b = 3 − p, edges of residues 4 and 5 must use their negative representatives. Both classes
exist because p ≥ 7. They give
                      h(ti−3 ) = h(si ) + 4 − p,     h(ti−4 ) = h(si ) + 5 − p.
Again successive t-heights increase by 1 cyclically, a contradiction.
  If b = 3 + p, every initial cross difference would have to be at least 4; a residue-1 edge
cannot have that value. If b = 3 − 2p, every initial cross difference would have to be at most
2 − p; a residue-(p − 1) edge, whose possibilities are p − 1 and −1, cannot have that value.
This excludes all four shifts.
  Both modular tensions have integer lifts: choose their height representatives in {0, . . . , p −
1}. Any integer path between the fibres would reduce to a modular walk inside {c, c′ } and
would contain a first step crossing between these fibres, which has just been excluded. Hence
the component map is not injective.                                                              □
Remark 16.1 (Scope of the obstruction). The modular tension graph in this example is not
connected as a whole. For example, negate all the colours of c. The differences inside either
clique are then negated and are not those of c or c′ , since p is odd. This gives a modular tension
outside their two-vertex component. Thus the example does not disprove the weaker assertion
that modular connectedness implies integer connectedness for tensions. It does show that a
universal proof of the stronger component correspondence at 7 cannot apply to every totally
unimodular kernel. These examples are not graphic: Hp contains K5 , so its cycle matroid has
an M (K5 ) minor and is not cographic by [7, Corollary 10.3.5]; equivalently, its bond matroid
is not graphic. A proof for graphic flows by the stronger component-correspondence route
must exclude these examples. Theorems 12.1 and 12.2 give correspondence through six and
failure at every prime from seven on. In particular, six is the largest endpoint of a universal
initial interval of moduli; no claim is made about all composite moduli above six.

                  18. Six composite moduli: statements and credit
Conventions. All graphs are finite; loops and parallel edges are allowed. Let k ≥ 3 be an
integer, and fix a reference direction of every edge of a graph G. A nowhere-zero Zk -flow
gives every edge a value other than 0 in Zk such that at every vertex the sum of the values
that leave is equal to the sum of the values that enter. An integer k-flow gives every edge an
integer F (e) with 1 ≤ |F (e)| ≤ k − 1, with the same condition at every vertex. A cycle is a
connected subgraph in which every vertex has degree two; a loop is a cycle. A step adds a
constant other than 0 along one direction of one cycle (it is subtracted on the edges whose
reference direction is against this direction); the result must be a flow of the same kind. The
graph F(G, Zk ) has the nowhere-zero Zk -flows of G as its vertices, and the graph F(G, k)
has the integer k-flows of G as its vertices; in both, two flows are adjacent if one comes from
the other by a step. These are the graphs F(G, Zk ) and F(G, k) of [1], with a loop counted
as a cycle, and the graphs Mk (G) and Ik (G) of the other sections of this paper and of [4].
A graph of flows with no vertex or with one vertex counts as connected. The graph G is
Zk -flow-connected if F(G, Zk ) is connected, and k-flow-connected if F(G, k) is connected.
The reduction of an integer k-flow is the nowhere-zero Zk -flow of its values modulo k, and an
integer lift of a nowhere-zero Zk -flow f is an integer k-flow whose reduction is f .
The problem and the credit. Reduction modulo k maps the integer k-flows onto the
nowhere-zero Zk -flows, by the lifting theorem of Tutte [1, Theorem 2.3], and the reduction of
a step of F(G, k) is a step of F(G, Zk ) or changes nothing. So, if F(G, k) is connected, then
F(G, Zk ) is connected [1, Observation 2.5]. The lifting question [1, Problem 2.6] asks for a
proof of the converse, and [2, Conjecture A] states the equivalence. Part (a) of Theorem 1.1
proves the equivalence, in the form of a bijection of the components, for k ≤ 6 and every
graph, and Section 1 notes that for the graphs with no loop and for 3 ≤ k ≤ 6 this follows
from parts (a) to (d) of the lemma on liftable steps [4, Lemma 1.12]. Part (e) of that lemma
is not used there. With part (e) the same deduction works at six more moduli; this is the
content of these sections. All tools are from [4]: its Definition 1.7 and its Lemma 1.12, whose
part (e) rests on its Lemma 1.11 and its Theorem 1.5. The six moduli were found in the work
on that paper and are not stated in it; these sections write the deduction out. The deduction
is short, and all its tools are in that paper; Section 17 names them.
Theorem 18.1. Let k be one of 8, 9, 12, 16, 18, 36, and let G be a graph. Then reduction
modulo k induces a bijection between the connected components of F(G, k) and the connected
components of F(G, Zk ). In particular F(G, k) is connected if and only if F(G, Zk ) is con-
nected.
Corollary 18.2. Let t and k be integers, and let G be a graph in which every connected
component has at most t vertices of odd degree. Then G is k-flow-connected in each of these
cases:
    (a) t = 8 and k is 8, 9 or 12;
                        INTEGER AND MODULAR FLOW RECONFIGURATION                                    21

   (b) t = 12 and k is 16 or 18;
   (c) t = 24 and k = 36.
   Theorem 18.1 and Corollary 18.2 say nothing on any other modulus: not that the statement
of the theorem holds there, and not that it fails; for k ≤ 6 the statement of the theorem is
part (a) of Theorem 1.1. We give no classification of the moduli for which this proof works.
No statement is made on a finite abelian group that is not cyclic.

component of F(G, k).

      integer lifts lie in one connected component of F(G, k).
  (b) 1 and −1 are liftable.
  (c) 2 and −2 are liftable.
  (d) q and −q are liftable for every divisor q of k with 1 ≤ q < k.
  (e) Let k = mq with integers m, q ≥ 2, and let c be an integer that is invertible modulo k,

there. The last sentence of part (e) of [4, Lemma 1.12], a special case for the moduli 4p with

               20. The proofs of the theorem and of the corollary
Proof of Theorem 18.1. First let G have no loop. Reduction modulo k maps an integer k-flow
to a nowhere-zero Zk -flow, and it maps a step of F(G, k) to a step of F(G, Zk ) or to equality.
So it maps each connected component of F(G, k) into one connected component of F(G, Zk ).
By Lemma 19.1(a) every nowhere-zero Zk -flow has an integer lift; so every component of
F(G, Zk ) contains the reduction of an integer k-flow. Let F and F ′ be integer k-flows whose
reductions f and f ′ lie in one component of F(G, Zk ), and let f = f0 , f1 , . . . , fr = f ′ be a
path of F(G, Zk ). If r = 0, then F and F ′ are integer lifts of one flow, and they lie in one
component of F(G, k) by Lemma 19.1(a). Let r ≥ 1. For every i the step from fi−1 to fi adds
an element z ̸= 0 of Zk along a direction of a cycle, and z is liftable by Proposition 19.2. So
all integer lifts of fi−1 and all integer lifts of fi lie in one component of F(G, k). So all integer
lifts of f0 , . . . , fr lie in one component, and F and F ′ are among them. So two components of
F(G, k) with reductions in the same component of F(G, Zk ) are equal. This is the bijection.
    Now let G have loops, and let G◦ be G without its loops. A loop adds its value to both
sums at its vertex; so the flows of G of either kind are the flows of G◦ of that kind together
with one allowed value on each loop: one of the 2(k − 1) integers j with 1 ≤ |j| ≤ k − 1, or
one of the k − 1 elements other than 0 of Zk . A loop is a cycle, and no other cycle contains a
loop. A step on a loop changes the value of this loop only, to any other allowed value; every
other step is a step of G◦ and keeps the values of the loops. So the connected components
of F(G, k) are the sets of all flows of G whose part on G◦ lies in one given component of
F(G◦ , k), and the same holds for F(G, Zk ) and F(G◦ , Zk ). Reduction modulo k commutes
with the passage to the part on G◦ . So the bijection for G◦ gives the bijection for G.
    The last sentence of the theorem follows: a graph is connected if and only if it has at most
one connected component.                                                                           □
Proof of Corollary 18.2. (a) By [5, Corollary 86.2] the graph F(G, Zk ) is connected for every
k ≥ 6. (b) and (c) By [5, Theorem 82.1], for integers r ≥ 4 and k ≥ 3r − 2, a graph in which
every connected component has at most 2r vertices of odd degree is Zk -flow-connected; use
r = 6, where 3r − 2 = 16, for k = 16 and k = 18, and r = 12, where 3r − 2 = 34, for k = 36.
In each case Theorem 18.1 gives that F(G, k) is connected.                                  □
Remark 20.1. The modulus 7 is not among the six. The number 7 is a prime; so part (d)
of Lemma 19.1 names only q = 1 there, and part (e) has no case. So parts (b) to (e) name
only ±1 and ±2 modulo 7. This says nothing on the truth of the statement of Theorem 18.1
for k = 7.
