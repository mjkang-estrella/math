vertical partner. Let X be the indicator that at least one bottom arc from R0(k) to (j,0) is retained, and let Y be the
indicator that at least one top arc from R1(k) to (j,1) is retained. These two families contain exactly a and b arcs re-
spectively, they are disjoint because they lie in different layers, and all their tokens are independent and fair. Hence

Write q = 2ˆ(-a) and r = 2ˆ(-b). The four possible increments have probabilities

P(U=1,V=0) = (1-q) r / 2,

P(U=1,V=1) = 1 - (q+r)/2.

3.3 Reﬂection and reﬂected mass dominance. Let ρ(a,b) = (b,a) be reﬂection in the diagonal. The increment ker-
nel K satisﬁes K(ρ s, ρ t) = K(s,t): swapping X and Y and keeping Z exchanges the two middle probabilities and
ﬁxes the other two. Also U and V are bits, so the difference a-b changes by at most one in a single step. Therefore a
one-step transition from a > b cannot end at a < b, and a one-step transition from a < b cannot end at a > b. This is
the no-crossing property.

Let µ(k) be the law of (A(k),B(k)). For a ﬁnitely supported probability µ on pairs (a,b), deﬁne
∆µ(a,b) = µ(a,b) - µ(b,a).
We claim that ∆µ(k)(a,b) ≥ 0 whenever a > b. Initially µ(u) is 1/2 at (1,0) and 1/2 at (1,1); for (1,0) we have ∆ = 1/2

with a > b. Then
∆(µK)(t) = Σ_s µ(s) [K(s,t) - K(s,ρ t)]
       = Σ_{s above} ∆µ(s) [K(s,t) - K(s,ρ t)]

The second equality pairs each state s above the diagonal with its reﬂection ρ s; diagonal states cancel because re-

We do not claim that A(k) ≥ B(k) holds pathwise; only the signed mass inequality is asserted.

If v < u, both events are impossible by index monotonicity, so both probabilities are zero. If v = u, the zero-length

3.6 Where transitivity is used. Transitivity enters at two points. First, arcs strictly increase the index, so no path
returns to a smaller index; this makes preﬁx reachability ﬁnal and permits the column-by-column revelation. Sec-
ond, and indispensably, every pair i < j is an arc, so every reachable lower copy contributes exactly one candidate
arc into the new copy. This completeness is what makes the candidate counts equal to a and b, and hence what
makes the counts a sufﬁcient statistic.

A general acyclic digraph has the ﬁrst property but not the second. There, the number of candidate arcs into a new
vertex is the number of its reachable predecessors, which depends on which vertices are reachable, not merely on
how many. For instance, if a later vertex j has only one predecessor x, then a preﬁx that reaches x0 but not y0 and a
preﬁx that reaches y0 but not x0 have the same counts, yet different entry probabilities. The count process is then
not Markov in (a,b), and the paired-sum induction has no analogue. Przybylowski’s acyclic counterexample shows
that the statement itself fails in that generality. Adding the missing arcs to obtain a tournament changes the connec-
tion probabilities, and no monotonicity of their difference under edge addition is available.
