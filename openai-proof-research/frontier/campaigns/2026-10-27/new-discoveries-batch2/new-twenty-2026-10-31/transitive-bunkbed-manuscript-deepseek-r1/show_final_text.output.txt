Abstract
We prove the bunkbed conjecture in Hollom’s E7 model for the transitive tournament T(n): for every n and all ver-
tices u, v, the probability that (u,0) reaches (v,0) is at least the probability that (u,0) reaches (v,1). The proof reveals
the random tokens column by column, compresses the revealed preﬁx to the pair of reachable-copy counts, and pre-
serves a reﬂected mass dominance by a paired-sum induction. Transitivity is used through the total order, which
prevents a path from returning to a smaller index, and through completeness, which makes every earlier vertex a
predecessor of the new one. The endpoint is kernel checked in Lean 4.34.1 with mathlib revision
d13f23b723b8a846827a245b89c10fc7d3f11612; the reported axioms are exactly propext, Classical.choice and
Quot.sound. No acceptance, peer review, priority or worldwide novelty is claimed.
1. Introduction
The bunkbed conjecture concerns two copies, called layers, of a graph, joined by vertical edges. In the undirected
form, with independent retention of edges, it asserts that for all vertices u and v the probability that the layer-0
copies of u and v are connected is at least the probability that the layer-0 copy of u is connected to the layer-1 copy
of v. The conjecture was introduced by Kasteleyn in 1985 and is now known to be false in general.
Hollom studied directed versions. In the model E7 of the arXiv version, renamed D7 in the published journal ver-
sion at the same numbered locations, a directed graph is doubled; each horizontal arc is retained independently in
each layer, while each vertex carries one vertical token that enables both directions at once. Hollom’s Theorem 1.6
shows that BBC(D7(D)) fails for suitable directed multigraphs. He then asks, in Question 6.3, whether
BBC(D7(D)) holds for all acyclic digraphs, and writes that it "seems unclear ... whether the answer ... should be
positive or negative", adding that the problem is "far more amenable to inductive techniques than the case of graphs
with cycles, but actually performing this induction seems far from trivial".
Conjecture 6.4 then singles out the transitive tournament: it asserts that BBC(D7(T(n))) holds for T(n), the transitive
tournament on n vertices. Hollom calls this "one case that does seem approachable, though was found by the author
to be more difﬁcult than expected", and remarks that the techniques used for the undirected complete graph "do not
directly apply, in short because Tn does not have the same symmetries as Kn". The undirected complete graph case
is due to van Hintum and Lammers.
The general acyclic question was later answered negatively by Przybylowski: he constructs a simple acyclic di-
rected graph with distinguished vertices u and v such that P(u- → v-) < P(u- → v+) (Theorem 1.2), resolving the
conjectures of Leander and Hollom. His graph has missing adjacencies and is not a tournament, so it does not con-
tradict Conjecture 6.4. Tournaments are singled out because the order is total: every earlier vertex is a predecessor
of every later one. In the proof below, this completeness is exactly what makes the pair of reachable-copy counts a
sufﬁcient statistic. The MathDB record 382297 states the same conjecture for T(n) in the E7 model and records it as
open.

2. Model and theorem
Let T(n) be the transitive tournament with vertex set {0,...,n-1}, in which i → j is an arc exactly when i < j. The
bunkbed has vertices (i,l) with i in {0,...,n-1} and layer l in {0,1}. There are two kinds of random token:
     • For every arc i → j and every layer l there is one fair horizontal token h(i,j,l). If h(i,j,l) is retained, the arc (i,l)
     → (j,l) is present.
     • For every index i there is one fair vertical token z(i). If z(i) is retained, both arcs (i,0) → (i,1) and (i,1) → (i,0)
     are present. The two directions are enabled together by the single token.
An outcome is an assignment of 0 or 1 to every token, and all 2ˆ(n(n-1)+n) = 2ˆ(n*n) outcomes are equally likely.
Reachability is the existence of a directed path; formally it is the reﬂexive-transitive closure of the enabled-arc rela-
tion, so the zero-length path counts and every vertex reaches itself. We write (u,0) → (v,s) when (v,s) is reachable
from (u,0).
Theorem 2.1. For every n ≥ 0 and all u, v in {0,...,n-1},
P((u,0) → (v,0)) ≥ P((u,0) → (v,1)).
Equivalently, in the ﬁnite uniform model,
|{ω : Reach n ω (u,false) (v,true)}| ≤ |{ω : Reach n ω (u,false) (v,false)}|,
where both sets are counted among the 2ˆ(n*n) outcomes; the common denominator cancels. For n = 0 there are no
vertices u and v, so the statement is vacuous.
3. Proof
Fix u. Since every arc weakly increases the index, a directed path from (u,0) never visits an index below u; in partic-
ular, if v < u then both events are impossible. We therefore reveal the tokens in increasing index order and track
reachability of the revealed preﬁx.
3.1 Preﬁxes and counts. For k ≥ u, let P(k) be the set of tokens whose largest touched index is at most k, that is,
the horizontal tokens of arcs into indices at most k together with the vertical tokens of indices at most k. Reveal
P(u), P(u+1), ... in order. Let R0(k) and R1(k) be the sets of indices in {u,...,k} whose layer-0 and layer-1 copies are
reachable from (u,0) using only the tokens of P(k), and put
A(k) = |R0(k)|,    B(k) = |R1(k)|.
Reachability of a vertex of index at most k is the same whether we use P(k) or all tokens: any directed path to such
a vertex has nondecreasing indices, so it stays in {u,...,k} and uses only tokens whose largest touched index is at
most k. Thus the sets R0(k) and R1(k) are ﬁnal for their indices; later tokens cannot change them. This is the no-re-
turn property, and it uses only acyclicity.
At k = u the preﬁx P(u) contains the vertical token at u and the horizontal tokens into u from lower indices. The lat-
ter cannot be used, because no index below u is reachable from (u,0). The zero-length path makes u belong to R0(u)
always, and the copy (u,1) is reachable exactly when z(u) is retained. Hence
(A(u),B(u)) = (1,1) with probability 1/2, and (1,0) with probability 1/2.
3.2 The increment law. Condition on the whole revealed preﬁx P(k), and suppose its counts are (a,b). The new in-
dex is j = k+1. A path ﬁrst enters index j horizontally from a previously reachable copy at some i < j, because no
vertex of index j is reachable before index j is entered; after the ﬁrst entry, the only further vertex at index j is the
vertical partner. Let X be the indicator that at least one bottom arc from R0(k) to (j,0) is retained, and let Y be the
indicator that at least one top arc from R1(k) to (j,1) is retained. These two families contain exactly a and b arcs re-
spectively, they are disjoint because they lie in different layers, and all their tokens are independent and fair. Hence
X and Y are independent with
P(X = 0) = 2ˆ(-a),    P(Y = 0) = 2ˆ(-b).

Let Z be the vertical token at j, independent and fair. The new reachable indicators are
U = X or (Z and Y) for layer 0,
V = Y or (Z and X) for layer 1,
and the counts become (a+U, b+V). This is exact: if X = 0 and ZY = 0 there is no entry into (j,0), and if X = 1 there
is one; if X = 0 but Y = 1 and Z = 1, the vertical token carries reachability from (j,1) to (j,0). The same reasoning
gives V. There is no other route into index j, because arcs enter j only from lower indices or vertically from the part-
ner at j, and no path returns to the old preﬁx.
The law of (X,Y,Z) depends on the preﬁx only through a and b. This is the point at which completeness in the order
is used: every index i < j is a predecessor of j, so R0(k) contributes exactly a candidate bottom arcs and R1(k) ex-
actly b candidate top arcs, no matter which indices belong to these sets. All these arcs are present independently
with probability 1/2, and the tokens in the two layers are distinct. If some pairs i < j were missing, the number of
candidate arcs into j would depend on the identity of R0(k), not merely on its size.
Write q = 2ˆ(-a) and r = 2ˆ(-b). The four possible increments have probabilities
P(U=0,V=0) = q r,
P(U=1,V=0) = (1-q) r / 2,
P(U=0,V=1) = q (1-r) / 2,
P(U=1,V=1) = 1 - (q+r)/2.
They sum to one. For example, (U,V) = (1,0) means that a bottom entry exists, no top entry exists, and Z = 0, which
has probability (1-q)r/2; the case (1,1) is the complement of the other three.
3.3 Reﬂection and reﬂected mass dominance. Let ρ(a,b) = (b,a) be reﬂection in the diagonal. The increment ker-
nel K satisﬁes K(ρ s, ρ t) = K(s,t): swapping X and Y and keeping Z exchanges the two middle probabilities and
ﬁxes the other two. Also U and V are bits, so the difference a-b changes by at most one in a single step. Therefore a
one-step transition from a > b cannot end at a < b, and a one-step transition from a < b cannot end at a > b. This is
the no-crossing property.
Let µ(k) be the law of (A(k),B(k)). For a ﬁnitely supported probability µ on pairs (a,b), deﬁne
∆µ(a,b) = µ(a,b) - µ(b,a).
We claim that ∆µ(k)(a,b) ≥ 0 whenever a > b. Initially µ(u) is 1/2 at (1,0) and 1/2 at (1,1); for (1,0) we have ∆ = 1/2
- 0 = 1/2 ≥ 0, and (1,1) is diagonal. For the inductive step, let µ be a law with ∆µ(a,b) ≥ 0 for a > b, and let t = (a,b)
with a > b. Then
∆(µK)(t) = Σ_s µ(s) [K(s,t) - K(s,ρ t)]
       = Σ_{s above} ∆µ(s) [K(s,t) - K(s,ρ t)]
       = Σ_{s above} ∆µ(s) K(s,t) ≥ 0.
The second equality pairs each state s above the diagonal with its reﬂection ρ s; diagonal states cancel because re-
ﬂection ﬁxes them and K(s,ρ t) = K(s,t) when ρ s = s. The third equality uses no crossing: s is above the diagonal
and ρ t is below it, so K(s,ρ t) = 0. All sums are ﬁnite. Thus reﬂected mass dominance is preserved at every preﬁx.
We do not claim that A(k) ≥ B(k) holds pathwise; only the signed mass inequality is asserted.
3.4 The payoff. Conditionally on (a,b), we have U - V = (1-Z)(X-Y), hence
g(a,b) := E[U-V | a,b] = (1/2)(2ˆ(-b) - 2ˆ(-a)).
The function g is antisymmetric, g(b,a) = -g(a,b); it vanishes on the diagonal and is nonnegative above it, because a
> b gives 2ˆ(-a) < 2ˆ(-b). For any law µ with ∆µ(a,b) ≥ 0 for a > b,
E_{µ}[U-V] = Σ_s µ(s) g(s) = Σ_{a>b} ∆µ(a,b) g(a,b) ≥ 0,
by pairing s with ρ s and using g(ρ s) = -g(s) and g = 0 on the diagonal.

3.5 The endpoint. Fix v > u. The law µ(v-1) of (A(v-1),B(v-1)) satisﬁes reﬂected mass dominance by Section 3.3.
At the step into index v, the indicators U and V are exactly the indicators that v belongs to R0(v) and R1(v). There-
fore
P((u,0) → (v,0)) - P((u,0) → (v,1))
 = E[U-V] = E_{µ(v-1)}[g(A,B)] ≥ 0.
The equality with the full connection probabilities holds because reachability of index v is decided by the preﬁx
through v: every path to v has nondecreasing indices and ends at v, so it uses only tokens whose largest touched in-
dex is at most v.
If v < u, both events are impossible by index monotonicity, so both probabilities are zero. If v = u, the zero-length
path gives probability 1 for (u,0) → (u,0), while (u,0) → (u,1) requires the vertical token at u and has probability
1/2; thus 1 ≥ 1/2. Together with the vacuous case n = 0, this covers all n and all u, v, proving Theorem 2.1.
3.6 Where transitivity is used. Transitivity enters at two points. First, arcs strictly increase the index, so no path
returns to a smaller index; this makes preﬁx reachability ﬁnal and permits the column-by-column revelation. Sec-
ond, and indispensably, every pair i < j is an arc, so every reachable lower copy contributes exactly one candidate
arc into the new copy. This completeness is what makes the candidate counts equal to a and b, and hence what
makes the counts a sufﬁcient statistic.
A general acyclic digraph has the ﬁrst property but not the second. There, the number of candidate arcs into a new
vertex is the number of its reachable predecessors, which depends on which vertices are reachable, not merely on
how many. For instance, if a later vertex j has only one predecessor x, then a preﬁx that reaches x0 but not y0 and a
preﬁx that reaches y0 but not x0 have the same counts, yet different entry probabilities. The count process is then
not Markov in (a,b), and the paired-sum induction has no analogue. Przybylowski’s acyclic counterexample shows
that the statement itself fails in that generality. Adding the missing arcs to obtain a tournament changes the connec-
tion probabilities, and no monotonicity of their difference under edge addition is available.
4. Remarks
4.1 Column-homogeneous probabilities. The same proof works under a broader law, which we record as an im-
mediate extension and not as a novelty claim. Fix any set T of posts, and for each column j let the horizontal tokens
of all arcs i → j in both layers be retained independently with a common probability p(j) in [0,1]. Let Z(j) = 1 if j is
in T; the vertical token at j is retained exactly when Z(j) = 1. Then the failure probabilities at a preﬁx with counts
(a,b) are q(j)ˆa and q(j)ˆb, where q(j) = 1 - p(j), and the conditional payoff is
g(j)(a,b) = (1 - Z(j)) (q(j)ˆb - q(j)ˆa) / 2.
The kernel is time-inhomogeneous, but reﬂection equivariance and no crossing still hold at every step, and the ini-
tial law is unchanged. The paired-sum induction therefore gives the same inequality. Conditioning on an arbitrary
random post set is also allowed; taking each vertex independently in T with probability 1/2 recovers E7, with p(j) =
1/2 for all j. Columns with p(j) = 0, which have no incoming horizontal arc, are included. This extension does not
cover arbitrary posets, interval orders or general acyclic digraphs, for the reason given in Section 3.6.
4.2 Reﬂection until meeting. The paired-sum induction is a reﬂection-until-meeting argument, and reﬂection until
meeting is an old technique (Aldous and Fill, Chapter 12, Section 12.1.8). We make no novelty claim for it. The
process here is two-dimensional, with state-dependent transition probabilities and a terminal antisymmetric payoff;
it is not the cycle walk treated in that reference.
