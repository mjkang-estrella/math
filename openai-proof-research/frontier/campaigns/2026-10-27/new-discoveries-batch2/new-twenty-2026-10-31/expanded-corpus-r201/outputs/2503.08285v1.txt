                                                                                           Abstract
                                                     We introduce a new sorting device for permutations which makes use of a pop stack
                                                 augmented with a bypass operation. This results in a sorting machine, which is more

                                                 powerful than the usual Popstacksort algorithm and seems to have never been investigated
                                                 previously.
                                                     In the present paper, we give a characterization of sortable permutations in terms of for-
                                                 bidden patterns and reinterpret the resulting enumerating sequence using a class of restricted
                                                 Motzkin paths. Moreover, we describe an algorithm to compute the set of all preimages of
                                                 a given permutation, thanks to which we characterize permutations having a small number
                                                 of preimages. Finally, we provide a full description of the preimages of principal classes of
                                                 permutations, and we discuss the device consisting of two pop stacks in parallel, again with
                                                 a bypass operation.


                                         1       Introduction
                                             Sorting disciplines for permutations constitute a flourishing research area in contemporary
                                         combinatorics. Starting from the stacksorting procedure introduced by Knuth [K], dozens of
                                         articles (many of which are very recent) have explored the subject. For example, machines
                                         making use of various types of containers have been studied, as well as networks of the corre-
                                         sponding devices. Typical containers that are considered in this context include stacks, queues,
                                         and deques, as well as their “pop” versions. In particular, a pop stack is a stack whose push
                                         and pop operations are similar to the usual ones for stacks. The distinction is that a pop
                                         operation extracts all the elements from the pop stack, rather than just the element on the
                                         top. The sorting power of a pop stack was originally studied by Avis and Newborn [AN], who
                                         also considered pop stacks in parallel. Concerning pop stacks in series, we mention that the
                                         right-greedy version of pop stacks in series was introduced by Pudwell and Smith [PS] and was
                                         successively studied also by Claesson and Gukmundsson [CG].

                                             When dealing with a classical stack, the algorithm Stacksort introduced by Knuth uses
                                         only two operations, namely the push operation (which pushes the current element of the input
                                         into the stack) and the pop operation (which pops the stack by moving the top of the stack
                                         into the output). Recall that permutations sortable using Stacksort are characterized as those
                                         avoiding the pattern 231. In this case, a bypass operation, which sends the current element of
                                         the input directly into the output, is clearly redundant, since it can be simulated by means of
                                         a push followed by a pop. This is clearly false if we replace the stack with a queue: without

whereas adding a bypass gives rise to a much more interesting device. In the latter case, an
optimal sorting algorithm, called Queuesort, is described in [M] and further studied in [CF1],
and sortable permutations can be characterized as those avoiding the pattern 321.
    When we move to the “pop” versions, again in the case of a pop queue a bypass operation
is necessary in order to have nontrivial algorithms [CF2]. On the other hand, in the case of a
pop stack, having or not having a bypass operation gives rise to two different (and nontrivial)
sorting devices (unlike what happens for a classical stack). Curiously, there is no evidence in
the literature of papers investigating the sorting properties of a pop stack with a bypass. This
paper aims to fill this gap. Clearly, adding a bypass to a pop stack slightly increases the sorting
power of the device and poses interesting combinatorial questions on its properties, which we
analyze in some detail. More specifically, the content of our paper is the following.
    In Section 3 we characterize the set of sortable permutations in terms of two forbidden
patterns; the enumeration of the resulting class of pattern avoiding permutations was already
known (odd-indexed Fibonacci numbers, sequence A001519 in [Sl]); however, in Section 4 we give
an independent proof of this enumerative result by describing a bijective link with a restricted
class of Motzkin paths. In Section 5 we describe an algorithm to compute the preimage of a
given permutation and use it to characterize and enumerate permutations having 0,1, and 2
preimages. Section 6 contains a complete description of the preimages of principal classes of
permutations, by determining in which cases we obtain classes (and in such cases by determining
the basis of the resulting classes). Section 7 is devoted to the characterization and (partly)
enumeration of the sets of sortable permutations for the compositions of our sorting algorithm
with other classical sorting algorithms (the characterization being given in terms of forbidden
patterns). Finally, in Section 8 we consider the device consisting of two pop stacks in parallel
with a bypass, and we determine the basis of the associated set of sortable permutations, and
in Section 9 we give some hints for further work.


2    Preliminaries on permutations and sorting algorithms
    Let [1, n] = {1, . . . , n}. A permutation π of size n is a bijection from [1, n] to [1, n]. It can
be represented in linear form as π = π1 · · · πn , where πi = π(i), for all 1 ≤ i ≤ n. The identity
permutation of size n S   is 12 · · · n, and is denoted idn . We denote by Sn the set of all permutations
of size n and by S = n∈N Sn the set of all permutations.
    For a given permutation π, we say that two elements are adjacent when their positions are
consecutive integers, whereas we say that they are consecutive when their values are consecutive
integers. For instance, if π = 3645712, the elements 1 and 7 are adjacent, the elements 5 and
6 are consecutive, and the elements 4 and 5 are both adjacent and consecutive. We warn the
reader that we will strictly adhere to this terminology regarding the terms “consecutive” and
“adjacent” throughout the paper.
    Given π = π1 · · · πn ∈ Sn , an element πi is called a left-to-right maximum when it is larger
than all the elements to its left (that is, πi = max{πj | j ≤ i}). We denote by LT R(π) the set of
left-to-right maxima of π. Therefore, the set of left-to-right maxima of the above permutation
is LT R(3645712) = {3, 6, 7}. Moreover, the reverse of π is the permutation π r = πn · · · π1 .
    Given π ∈ Sn and τ = τ1 · · · τm ∈ Sm , the direct sum of π and τ is the permutation π ⊕ τ
obtained by concatenating π with the sequence (τ1 + n) · · · (τm + n) (obtained from τ by adding
n to each of its elements). For instance, the direct sum of the permutations 3142 and 42315 is
314286759.
    We note that any sequence of n distinct integers can be regarded as a permutation by appro-
priately rescaling its elements to [1, n]. Indeed, two sequences are said to be order-isomorphic if

                                                   2
their elements are in the same relative order. This notion is useful in the definition of a pattern
that we give next.
    Let π, ρ be two permutations of size n and k, respectively. Then ρ is a pattern of π (or π
contains the pattern ρ) when there exists a subsequence πi1 · · · πim of π, with i1 ≤ · · · ≤ im ,
which is order-isomorphic to ρ. Such a subsequence is also called an occurrence of the pattern
ρ. Conversely, if π does not contain ρ, then we say that π avoids ρ. The set of all permutations
avoiding a pattern ρ is denoted Av(ρ), and similarly, for T ⊆ S, we write Av(T ) for the set
of permutations avoiding each pattern of T . We also use the notations Avn (ρ) and Avn (T ) to
denote the sets of permutations of size n avoiding ρ and T , respectively. As an example, the
permutation 35142 contains the patterns 213 and 321 (among others), but avoids the pattern
123. In particular, an occurrence of the pattern 21 is called an inversion.
    The notion of pattern defines a partial order ≤ on S, by declaring ρ ≤ π whenever ρ is a
pattern of π. The resulting poset is called the permutation pattern poset, and its down-sets1
are usually called classes. It is not difficult to see that any nonempty class of the permutation
pattern poset can be defined in terms of the avoidance of a set of patterns. More formally,
given nonempty D ⊆ S, D is a class if and only if there exists an antichain T ⊆ S such that
D = Av(T ). In this case, we say that T is the basis of the class D.
    There are also several variations of the above notion of pattern. One that is relevant for
our paper (specifically in Proposition 7.1) is that of a barred pattern. A barred pattern is a
permutation in which some of the entries are barred. For a permutation π to avoid the barred
pattern ρ means that every set of entries of π which form a copy of the nonbarred entries of ρ
can be extended to form a copy of all entries of ρ. For example, the permutation 462351 avoids
the barred pattern 35̄241, as it has two occurrences of 3241 (namely 4251 and 4351), which can
both be extended to an occurrence of 35241.
    To conclude this section on preliminaries, we briefly recall a few sorting algorithms for per-
mutations that will be used in our paper. We will not give formal definitions of the algorithms,
instead settling for an informal description of each of them.
    The classical Stacksort algorithm was first introduced by Knuth [K], and tries to sort a
permutation by making use of a stack as follows: the entries of the input permutation are
processed from left to right and, when the current entry is smaller than the top of the stack (or
the stack is empty), then such entry is pushed into the stack; otherwise the stack is popped, and
the top of the stack is moved in the next available position of the output (i.e. to the immediate
right of the elements already in the output). When all entries for the input permutations have
been processed, the content of the stack is poured into the output. As is well known, the set of
sortable permutations for Stacksort is the set of 231-avoiding permutations, which are counted
by Catalan numbers. A similar sorting algorithm is Queuesort [M], where the stack is replaced
with a queue and a bypass operation is also allowed. In this case, the algorithm proceeds by
inserting the current entry into the queue if it is larger than the element at the back of the
queue; otherwise, the current entry is compared with the element in front of the queue, and
the smaller one is output. The set of sortable permutations using Queuesort is known to be
the set of 321-avoiding permutations, which is again counted by Catalan numbers. Finally, we
mention the very classical sorting algorithm Bubblesort, whose description can be found in
most standard algorithm textbooks. In fact, our version of Bubblesort consists of a single pass
of the algorithm reported in textbooks. In terms of Stacksort and Queuesort, Bubblesort
can be interpreted as a special case of any of the two in which the container has depth 1.
Permutations sortable through Bubblesort are precisely those that avoid the two patterns 231
and 321, and are counted by the sequence of the powers of two [AABCD].

   3    An optimal algorithm to sort permutations using a pop stack
        with a bypass
       A pop stack is a container in which elements can be stacked on top of each other, on which
   two operations are defined: the PUSH operation, which inserts an element into the stack, and
   the POP operation, which extracts all the elements from the stack. The difference between a
   pop stack and a (classical) stack lies therefore in the way the elements are removed from the
   stack.
       When using a stack or a pop stack to sort a permutation, the elements of the permutations
   are usually processed from left to right, and either the current element of the permutation
   is pushed into the stack, or the topmost element (or all the content) of the stack is popped
   into the output, again from left to right. We now introduce a new kind of pop stack, by
   allowing one more operation: this is the BYPASS operation, which takes the current element
   of the input permutation and moves it directly into the output, in the next available position.
   More formally, given a permutation π = π1 π2 . . . πn , a pop stack with bypass has the following
   allowed operations:
 PUSH: insert the current element of the input into the pop stack, on top of all the other elements
       (if there are any);

   POP: remove all the elements in the pop stack, from top to bottom, sending them into the
        output;

BYPASS: output the current element of the input.

        Our goal is to use a pop stack with bypass in order to sort permutations. It is not too
   difficult to realize that not every permutation can be sorted.

   Proposition 3.1. Let π be a permutation such that 231 ≤ π or 4213 ≤ π. Then π cannot be
   sorted using a pop stack with bypass.

   Proof. Suppose that π contains the pattern 231, and let bca be an occurrence of 231 in π. We
   analyze all possible configurations that can occur during the sorting process. In order to obtain
   the identity permutation, neither b nor c can reach the output before a, therefore they must
   enter the pop stack and wait until a is the current element of the input. However, this implies
   that b and c are inserted into the pop stack in this order, so they will necessarily appear in the
   output in reverse order, which means that the output will be unsorted.
      Now suppose that π contains the pattern 4213, and let dbac be an occurrence of 4213 in π.
   Using a similar argument as before, we observe that, in order to sort the output, both d and b
   must enter the pop stack and wait for a to be the current element of the input. Since the POP
   operation removes all the elements currently in the pop stack, b and d will reach the output
   before or after c (that is, the output will contain either the subsequence bdc or the subsequence
   cbd). This implies that the output will not be the identity permutation.

       We will now prove the converse of the above proposition, by introducing an optimal sorting
   algorithm, that we call PSB (an acronym for “PopStacksort with Bypass”), by which we can
   sort all permutations in the class Av(231, 4213).
       The algorithm PSB maintains elements in the pop stack only when they are consecutive in
   value (and increasing from top to bottom), which ensures at least that a POP operation is not
   directly responsible for the possible failure of the sorting procedure. An example of how PSB
   works is shown in Figure 1, where the input permutation (on the right of the pop stack) is not
   sortable.

   To prove that PSB actually sorts every permutation in Av(231, 4213) we need some prelimi-
nary lemmas. In what follows, we will denote by PSB(π) the output of PSB on the permutation
π.
Lemma 3.2. Let a, b be two positive integers such that a < b, and let π be a permutation. If
the pair (a, b) is a noninversion in π, then it remains a noninversion in PSB(π) (that is, PSB
does not create new inversions).
Proof. Let a < b, and suppose that a appears to the left of b in π. Consider the configuration in
which a is the current element of the input, and suppose that a enters the pop stack (otherwise
there is nothing to prove). If a is still in the pop stack when b is processed, then b is certainly
larger than the top of the pop stack, and so a POP operation is performed, which is enough to
ensure that a is also to the left of b in PSB(π).

   We also propose a (rather technical) generalization of the previous lemma, which is not
actually needed in the proof of the next Proposition 3.5, but which instead will be extremely
useful in Section 6.
Lemma 3.3. Given a permutation π, suppose that PSB(π) contains the subsequence mβ, where
m ∈ N is larger than each element of the sequence β. Then mβ is also a subsequence of π.
Proof. The lemma is obvious when β is empty, so suppose that β contains at least one element.
Thanks to the previous lemma, π contains the subsequence mβ̃, for some rearrangement of the

                                                        5
elements of β. Since m reaches the output before the elements of β, there must be an element
M > m before β̃ in π. As a consequence, we observe that, when an element of β̃ is the current
element of the input, the top of the pop stack is certainly strictly larger than m (which is in
turn larger than all elements of β̃), and therefore the output will also contain the sequence β̃.
This means that actually β̃ = β.

Lemma 3.4. Suppose that a is an element of a permutation π that enters the pop stack during
the execution of PSB. Then there exists a (necessarily unique) nonnegative integer k ≥ 0 such
that π contains the decreasing subsequence of (not necessarily adjacent) consecutive elements
a + k, a + k − 1, . . . , a and a + k is the rightmost left-to-right maximum of π (weakly) to the
left of a. In particular, when k = 0, we see that the left-to-right maxima of π are precisely the
elements that are at the bottom of the pop stack at some point during the sorting procedure.

Proof. We argue by induction on the position of the element a in π.
    If a is the first element of π, then a is a left-to-right maximum and enters the pop stack.
    Now suppose that a is the i-th element of π and that a enters the pop stack. This may
happen in two distinct ways. The first case is when a is larger than the current top of the
pop stack (and so it induces a POP operation). In such a case, we see that a is larger than
every element inside the pop stack (since the elements in the pop stack are consecutive), and
so in particular it is larger than the current element lying at the bottom of the pop stack,
which (by the induction hypothesis) is a left-to-right maximum of π. Therefore, a is a left-to-
right maximum of π. The second case occurs when a is pushed into the pop stack on top of
a nonempty sequence of elements. In such a case, the elements in the pop stack (from top to
bottom) are a + 1, a + 2, . . . , a + k (for some k ≥ 1). This means that π contains the subsequence
a + k, . . . , a + 1, a, and that a + k is a left-to-right maximum (by the induction hypothesis).
Moreover, there cannot be elements larger than a + k between a + k and a in π, otherwise any
such element would have induced a POP operation, and so a + k could not be inside the pop
stack when a is processed. This means that a + k is the rightmost left-to-right maximum to the
left of a.

Proposition 3.5. Let π ∈ Av(231, 4213). Then PSB(π) is an identity permutation.

Proof. Let π be a permutation such that PSB(π) is not an identity permutation. This means
that there exist two elements a, b such that a < b and where b appears to the left of a in
PSB(π). We want to prove that π contains 231 or 4213.
    By Lemma 3.2, the element b must occur to the left of a in π as well. We now focus on
the configuration when b is sent to the output. This may happen by means of either a POP
operation or a BYPASS operation.
    In the former case, the element b is sent to the output from the pop stack; therefore, the
current element of the input, say c, must be larger than TOP(S). Since the content of the pop
stack consists of consecutive elements by construction, then b < c, and the elements b, c, a form
an occurrence of the pattern 231 in π.
    In the latter case, let c =TOP(S). This implies b < c − 1. The element c − 1 must appear
somewhere in π. If it appears to the left of c, then (c − 1)cb forms an occurrence of the pattern
231 in π. Clearly c − 1 cannot occur between c and b, otherwise it would be in the pop stack
by Lemma 3.4. If c − 1 is between b and a, then b(c − 1)a forms an occurrence of the pattern
231 in π. Finally, if c − 1 appears to the right of a in π, then cba(c − 1) forms an occurrence of
the pattern 4213 in π.
    Therefore, if π ∈ Av(231, 4213), then PSB(π) is an identity permutation.

4     A bijective link with a class of restricted Motzkin paths
    The coefficients (|Avn (231, 4213)|)n are the odd-indexed Fibonacci numbers, that is, the
sequence A001519 in [Sl], as shown in [A]. For our purposes, Fibonacci numbers are precisely
sequence A000045 in [Sl], and so they are defined by the usual recurrence Fn = Fn−1 + Fn−2
(for n ≥ 2), with initial conditions F0 = 0 and F1 = 1.
    It is possible to give a bijective proof of this enumerative result by providing a link with a
class of restricted Motzkin paths, whose enumeration can be easily carried out using standard
techniques.

    To be more precise, there is a very nice bijection between the permutations sortable by PSB
and a subset of ternary words that are counted by the odd-indexed Fibonacci numbers. The
bijection is to take the operation(s) that each entry of each sortable permutation utilizes in PSB
and convert it to a letter of the word.
    For each permutation π, the sorting word of π will be defined entry-wise using the operation
of PSB used when the entry is the next in the input to be acted on as follows:

      PUSH: 0

      BYPASS: 1

      POP, then PUSH: 2

Note that since each entry of the permutation will either be pushed into the pop stack or bypass
the pop stack exactly once, the sorting word will have the same size as the sortable permutation.
    For example, the permutation π = 3127465 has sorting word 0102100 as shown in Figure 3.

Proposition 4.1.       The sorting words of permutations sortable by PSB are exactly the ternary
words of W .

Proof. To see that these conditions are necessary is a straightforward application of the algo-
rithm PSB which prioritizes pushing entries into the pop stack over bypassing when appropriate
for sorting. The first entry of the permutation must be pushed into the pop stack, so every
sorting word begins with 0. The last entry cannot bypass the stack as if this would allow for
sorting, the last entry must be one less than the current top entry of the pop stack or the pop
stack must be empty, so the sorting word cannot end with a 1. Finally, the consecutive pattern
12 would indicate a bypass followed by a pop, which again means that the entry that bypassed
the stack could have been pushed to the top of the pop stack and then popped with the rest
of those entries for the same output. This proves that the set of sorting words of sortable
permutations is contained in W .
    Conversely, given w ∈ W , we now show that w is the sorting word of a sortable permutation
by explicitly constructing the unique sortable permutation π having w as sorting word. To do
so, we start by observing that w can be factorized into subwords as w = w1 w2 · · · wk such that,
for all i ≥ 2, each wi starts with 2 and (if it has size strictly greater than 1) the remaining
letters are 0’s and 1’s, with a 0 at the end; the factor w1 has exactly the same form, except
that it starts with 0. The form of this factorization depends on the defining properties of W , in
particular on the fact that there are no consecutive occurrences of 12. Denote with ℓi the size
of wi , for each i.
    The permutation π that we are going to construct can be factorized in terms of its left-
to-right maxima as π = α1 α2 · · · αk where, for each i, αi starts with a left-to-right maximum.
Since every left-to-right maximum (except for the first one) pops the stack, the output of PSB
on π is PSB(π) = PSB(α1 )PSB(α2 ) · · · PSB(αk ). In view of Lemma 3.4, the set containing
the initial letter 0 and each letter 2 in a sorting word correspond to the left-to-right maxima
of the permutation associated with that sorting word. Thus for w to be the sorting word of π,
then αi need to have size ℓi , for each i. Moreover, since we require π to be sortable, each αi
must contain all the integers from ℓi−1 + 1 to ℓi .
    The above discussion allows us to finally describe a procedure to construct π starting from w.
We successively replace the letters of w with positive integers, starting from 1 and increasingly,
as follows. For i running from 1 to k, scan wi from left to right and replace each 1 with an integer
(increasingly from left to right); then replace with integers (increasingly from right to left) the
remaining letters of wi . As a consequence of the above discussion, the resulting permutation π
is sortable and has w as its sorting word.

   The permutations sortable by PSB can similarly be placed in bijection with a restricted class
of Motzkin paths. Recall that a Motzkin path is a lattice path from (0, 0) to (0, n) that never
goes below the x -axis which takes steps of the following types:

   • Up (NE): (1, 1)

   • Down (SE): (1, −1)

   • Horizontal (E): (1, 0)

    For each permutation π, the sorting path of π will be defined entry-wise. Specifically, for
each entry πi , use the operation of PSB utilized when πi is the next in the input to be acted on
as follows:

      PUSH: Up step

      BYPASS: Horizontal step

      POP, then PUSH: Down steps until reaching the x-axis, followed by an Up Step

      Completion: Down steps until the x-axis is reached.

Note that a Motzkin path corresponding to a sorting word of size n will have exactly n total
Up and Horizontal steps combined.

5    Preimages under PSB
    In this section we investigate the preimages of a generic permutation σ under PSB. More
precisely, since the algorithm PSB induces a map PSB from the set of all permutations to itself,
we may ask to determine the set of permutations PSB−1 (σ) of all permutations whose output
under PSB is σ. This kind of investigation is somehow classical for Stacksort [B-M, D1], and
has been recently carried out also for Queuesort [CF1] and Bubblesort [BCF].
    We start by providing an alternative description of PSB that focuses on the left-to-right
maxima of the input permutation. To this aim, we will use the notion of shuffle, which we
define next.
    Let ρ, σ be two sequences of distinct integers. We say that a sequence τ is a shuffle of ρ and
σ if τ contains both ρ and σ as subsequences and contains no elements other than those of ρ
                                                                            
and σ. We denote the set of all possible shuffles of ρ and σ with ρ σ. As an example, if ρ = 31
                                                             
and σ = 24, then the set of all shuffles of ρ and σ is ρ σ = {3124, 3214, 3241, 2314, 2341, 2431}.

      We can now give an alternative description of PSB.
      Let π ∈ Sn , and let m1 , . . . , mk be its left-to-right maxima. For every i = 1, . . . , k, suppose
that mi (mi −1) · · · (mi −ji ) is the longest decreasing subsequence of consecutive elements starting
with mi and entirely to the left of mi+1 (if it exists). Then we can write π as π = m1 A1 · · · mk Ak ,
                                       
with Ai ∈ ((mi − 1) · · · (mi − ji )) Pi , where the Pi ’s collect all the remaining elements of π.
Using Lemma 3.4 and the description of PSB, we obtain PSB(π) = P1 (m1 − j1 ) · · · m1 P2 (m2 −
j2 ) · · · m2 · · · Pk (mk − jk ) · · · mk .
      For example, for the permutation π = 635247198, we see that m1 = 6, m2 = 7, m3 = 9,
j1 = 2, j2 = 0, and j3 = 1. Moreover, P1 = 32 and P2 = 1. Furthermore, P3 is empty,
hence we obtain PSB(π) = 324561789. Notice that 3524 is one of the possible shuffles of 54
and 32, but there exist others, such as 3542. We remark that any shuffle of 54 and 32 (in the
input permutation π) will give the same output. For instance, setting σ = 635427198, we get
PSB(σ) = 324561789 = PSB(π).
      The description above allows us to understand the relation between the left-to-right maxima
of a permutation and those of its preimages.

Proposition 5.1. Let π, σ be permutations such that PSB(π) = σ. If m is a left-to-right
maximum of π, then m is a left-to-right maximum of σ as well.

Proof. The proof is an immediate consequence of the description of PSB given above. In
fact, setting π = m1 A1 · · · mk Ak with the same notation as above, we see that, for every
i = 1, . . . , k, the element mi is greater than all the elements of Ai . Since σ = PSB(π) =
P1 (m1 − j1 ) · · · m1 P2 (m2 − j2 ) · · · m2 · · · Pk (mk − jk ) · · · mk , then mi is clearly a left-to-right
maximum also of σ.

    We now introduce a recursive algorithm that generates all preimages of a given permutation
σ. We start by giving an informal description of the algorithm, in order to better understand
how it works. Notice that our algorithm is actually defined on sequences of distinct integers (not
necessarily permutations): this allows us to recursively execute it on subsequences of elements
of a permutation.
    Suppose that σ = σ1 σ2 · · · σn = αµk , where µk is the maximum suffix of consecutive left-

ways, according to the following rules:
   • the elements are reinserted in decreasing order;

   • the maximum (i.e. n) is inserted to the immediate right of one of the remaining left-to-

     of σ;

   • the minimum (i.e. m) is inserted somewhere to the right of m − 1.

Proposition 5.2. The above algorithm computes all the preimages of a given permutation.
Proof. (Skecth.) Let σ ∈ Sn . It is easy to realize that all the permutations produced by the
above described algorithm are indeed preimages of σ under PSB.
    Conversely, let π ∈ PSB−1 (σ). If we decompose π as π = LnR, we observe that the
elements in L are processed by PSB independently from the rest of π, hence PSB(L) is a
prefix of σ. When n is pushed into the pop stack (clearly at the bottom of it), each of the
remaining elements of π (i.e., the elements of R) is either pushed into the pop stack or bypasses
it. After all elements of π are processed, the elements in the pop stack are poured into the
output. From the definition of PSB it then follows that the elements appearing in the pop stack
after n is processed constitute the maximal decreasing subsequence of π made of consecutive
elements starting with n (call it ρ). Notice that the elements of ρ need not be adjacent in π.
Notice that all the remaining elements of R appear in σ in the same order as they appear in
π. In particular, as a consequence of the maximality of ρ, the maximum of the elements of R
bypassing the pop stack necessarily appears to the left of the minimum of ρ in R. Collecting all
the above information tells us that π must have precisely the form prescribed by our algorithm
for preimages.

6    Preimages of classes
    The investigation of the preimages of a sorting algorithm is not limited to preimages of a
single permutation. A common task in this framework is to study the preimages of sets of
permutations, typically classes of permutations (i.e. sets of permutations defined in terms of
the avoidance of certain patterns). Clear evidence for this approach exists in the literature, for
instance in the cases of Stacksort [D] (and references therein), Queuesort [M] and Bubblesort
[AABCD]. When the preimages of permutation classes turn out to be classes themselves, the
best way to describe them is to find an explicit basis. In case this is not possible, some kind of


                                                  15
algorithmic procedure to determine the elements of the preimage is a notable alternative.
    In the present section, we consider the preimages of principal classes under PSB, and we
completely determine the permutations ρ for which PSB−1 (Av(ρ)) is a class. Moreover, in all
cases, we explicitly describe the basis of the resulting class. Our results will be useful in the
next section, where we will compose PSB with other sorting algorithms.

    Our first result concerns the principal classes whose basis is a permutation that begins with
its maximum.

    The patterns ρ of the form nα and (n − 1)αn are the only ones for which PSB−1 (Av(ρ)) is a
class. The next three propositions are devoted to show that this is indeed the case. In all cases,
in order to prove that PSB−1 (Av(ρ)) is not a permutation class, we exhibit two permutations
σ, π such that σ ≤ π and ρ ≤ PSB(σ), but ρ  PSB(π).

Proposition 6.3. Let ρ = αnβ ∈ Sn , for some nonempty sequences α, β. Then PSB−1 (Av(ρ))
is not a permutation class.

Proof. Let σ = (n + 1)α(n + 2)βn and π = (n + 1)(n + 3)α(n + 2)βn. Then σ ≤ π, because we
obtain σ by removing n + 3 from π. Also, PSB(σ) = α(n + 1)βn(n + 2), which contains the
occurrence α(n + 1)β of αnβ. On the other hand, PSB(π) = (n + 1)αβn(n + 2)(n + 3), and we
now show that such a permutation does not contain ρ. Indeed, since α and β are not empty, ρ
neither begins nor ends at its maximum. Thus n + 3 cannot be part of an occurrence of ρ in
PSB(π). For the same reason, neither n + 2 nor n + 1 can. Therefore, an occurrence of ρ in
PSB(π) should be an occurrence of ρ in αβn, which is impossible since ρ and αβn have the
same length and are different. Therefore ρ  PSB(π).

Proposition 6.4. Let ρ = α(n − 1)βn ∈ Sn , for some nonempty sequences α, β. Then
PSB−1 (Av(ρ)) is not a permutation class.

Proof. Let σ = nα(n + 1)β(n − 1) and π = n(n + 2)α(n + 1)β(n − 1). Then σ ≤ π, because we
obtain σ by removing n + 2 from π. Also, PSB(σ) = αnβ(n − 1)(n + 1), which contains the
occurrence αnβ(n + 1) of ρ. On the other hand, PSB(π) = nαβ(n − 1)(n + 1)(n + 2), and we
now show that such a permutation does not contain ρ. Indeed, if we remove both n+1 and n+2
from PSB(π), the resulting permutation (of length n) is different from ρ, therefore, to have an
occurrence of ρ in PSB(π), at least one of n+1 and n+2 must be involved. Actually, we cannot
choose both, since the maximum and the second maximum are not adjacent in ρ. Therefore
PSB(π) contains an occurrence of ρ if and only if π ′ = nαβ(n − 1) contains an occurrence of
ρ′ = α(n − 1)β. Since α and β are not empty, ρ′ neither begins nor ends with its maximum
n − 1. Hence n and n − 1 cannot be part of an occurrence of ρ′ in π ′ , but ρ′ has length n − 1
and π ′ has length n. Therefore, there are no occurrences of ρ′ in π ′ , that is, ρ  PSB(π).

Proposition 6.5. Let ρ = α(n − 1)n ∈ Sn , with α ∈ Sn−2 and n ≥ 4. Then PSB−1 (Av(ρ)) is
not a permutation class.

Proof. Let σ = (n − 1)(n + 1)nα and π = (n − 1)(n + 1)(n + 2)nα. Then σ ≤ π because we
obtain σ by removing n + 2 from π. Also, PSB(σ) = (n − 1)αn(n + 1), which contains the
occurrence αn(n + 1) of ρ. On the other hand, PSB(π) = (n − 1)(n + 1)nα(n + 2), and we now
show that such a permutation does not contain ρ. Indeed, since α has at least two elements and
(n − 1)(n + 1)n(n + 2) cannot be an occurrence of ρ, then a possible occurrence of ρ in PSB(π)
should contain at least one element of α. Moreover, since ρ ends with its maximum, n+2 should
belong to such an occurrence. Finally, since ρ has n elements, at least one element amongst
n − 1, n + 1 and n should be selected to form our occurrence. However, this is impossible,
because the three latter elements are all greater than each element of α, and ρ ends with its
two largest elements. Therefore, ρ  PSB(π).

   We remark that, if ρ = 123, we cannot use the same argument as in the above proof to

two permutations σ = 321 and π = 3421.

7    Compositions with other sorting algorithms
    If we execute a sorting algorithm X on a permutation and then execute another sorting
algorithm Y on the output permutation, we get a new sorting algorithm, whose associated map
is the composition of the maps associated with X and Y.
    In this section, we investigate the permutations that can be sorted using a composition of
PSB with another sorting algorithm. Specifically, we will consider the algorithms Queuesort,
Stacksort and Bubblesort, whose associated maps will be denoted Q, S and B, respectively. In
several cases, we obtain potential matches with sequences in [Sl], which we propose as conjectures
for possible further work.

    We begin by considering compositions in which PSB is performed first.
Proposition 7.1. Let π ∈ Sn . Then S ◦ PSB(π) = idn if and only if π ∈ Av(2341, 25314, 52314,
45231, 42531, 35̄241).
Proof. The algorithm Stacksort sorts a permutation if and only if it belongs to the permutation
class Av(231). Therefore, π is not sortable by the composition S ◦ PSB if and only if PSB(π)
contains the pattern 231.
       We start by supposing that π is a permutation which is not sortable by S ◦ PSB. Then
there exist three elements a, b, c, with a < b < c, such that PSB(π) contains such elements
in the order b, c, a. By Lemma 3.2, we have that π contains the elements a, b, c, either in the
order b, c, a or in the order c, b, a.
       Suppose that π contains the elements b, c, a in this order. We know that, when we apply
the algorithm PSB, these elements remain in the same order. Suppose that c is pushed into
the pop stack during the sorting process. Then c must reach the output before a is the current
element of the input, otherwise a would bypass, thus reaching the output before c. Therefore
there must be an element d between c and a in π that forces the pop stack to be emptied. Since
this happens only when the current element of the input is larger than the content of the pop
stack, we have d > c, hence π contains the pattern 2341 (given by the elements b, c, d, a). On
the other hand, suppose that c is not pushed into the pop stack during the sorting process.
Then it is not a left-to-right maximum, and there exists at least one element larger than c and
to the left of c in π. Let e be the rightmost of such elements. Now consider the instant in which
c is the current element of the input. By construction, e is in the pop stack. Since c does not
enter the pop stack by hypothesis, then π does not contain the decreasing sequence e, e − 1,
. . . , c + 1, c. Therefore, either there exists an element d, with c < d < e, which is not between
e and c in π, or there exist two elements d1 d2 , with c < d1 < d2 < e, that appear in π in the
order e, d1 , d2 , c. The first case gives one of the patterns 2341, 25314, 42531, 45231 or 52314,
depending on the positions of d and e with respect to b, c, and a. The second case gives one of
the patterns 2341, 45231 or 42531, depending on the positions of d1 and d2 with respect to b,
c, and a.
       On the other hand, suppose that π contains the elements c, b, a in this order, and during
the sorting process b reaches the output before c. For this to happen, c must be pushed into the
pop stack and stay there until b becomes the current element. Moreover, the pop stack must
be emptied before a becomes the current element. This can occur only if there is an element d
greater than c between b and a in π. Note that the elements c, b, d, a form an occurrence of
the pattern 3241 in π. Since c must remain in the pop stack until b is the current element of
the input, there cannot be any element larger than c between c and b in π. This implies that
π must avoid the patterns 35̄241 and 34̄251. However, we can ignore the pattern 34̄251, since
an occurrence of the pattern 34251 would contain an occurrence of the pattern 2341, which is
prohibited.

8    Pop stacks in parallel with a bypass
    As with other sorting devices, we can connect multiple pop stacks with a bypass option in
different ways to create more robust sorting machines. In this section, we consider one of the
most natural such options by putting pop stacks in parallel where for each entry of the input,
there is still an option to bypass the pop stacks and go directly to the output. Pop stacks in
parallel were originally studied by Atkinson and Sack [AS] who determined a recurrence relation
for the enumeration of the sortable permutations in the case of two pop stacks in parallel; they
also proved that the set of forbidden patterns that characterize the sortable permutations for
any number k of pop stacks in parallel is finite. Atkinson and Sack [AS] also conjectured that
the generating function for the enumeration of sortable permutations for any value k is rational,
which was proved by the last author and Vatter [SV].
    As before, we begin with the necessary condition that sortable permutations must avoid a
certain class of permutations.
Proposition 8.1. Let π be a permutation that contains one of the following permutations:

                 2341, 25314, 42513, 42531, 45213, 45231, 52314, 642135, 642153.

Then π cannot be sorted by two pop stacks in parallel with a bypass operation.
Proof. With each of the patterns 2341, 25314, 42513, 42531, 45213, 45231, 52314, 642135, 642153,
consider what must be done to sort a permutation that contains them. Recall that, when sorting
a permutation, the entries of a pop stack must be in increasing order from top to bottom and
also be such that each entry πi is exactly one less than the entry immediately below πi in the
pop stack.
    Consider an attempt to sort any of the patterns by two pop stacks in parallel with a
bypass. Notice that, for any of the patterns 2341, 25314, 42513, 42531, 45213, 45231, 52314,
642135, 642153, at least the first three entries must enter a pop stack before any can be popped,
as there is a later entry in the pattern that is smaller than all three. However, we see that the
second entry, say πα2 , of each pattern must enter a different pop stack from that entered by
the first entry, say πα1 , as either πα2 > πα1 (2341, 25314, 45213, 45231) or there is both a later
entry, call it παk , such that πα2 < παk < πα1 and so πα2 cannot be one less than the current top
element of the pop stack πα1 entered (42513, 42531, 52314, 642135, 642153). Finally, the third
entry, say πα3 of each pattern, can now not enter either of the pop stacks for the same reasons
that πα2 could not enter the same pop stack as πα1 .
    Hence, any algorithm will not be able to sort a permutation containing any of the patterns
2341, 25314, 42513, 42531, 45213, 45231, 52314, 642135, 642153.

to push an entry into a pop stack instead of bypassing the stacks if both moves are an option
to sort a permutation.

   Once again, the inverse of the above class has a regular insertion encoding [V], thus it is
possible to automatically deduce its rational generating function [ABCNPU, BEMNPU], which
is
                                    (1 − x)(1 − 2x)(1 − 4x)
                                                               .                          (2)
                                  1 − 8x + 20x2 − 18x3 + 3x4
    The sequence of coefficients has now been recorded as A374165 in [Sl].
    In fact, we can show that any machine made up of k pop stacks in parallel with a bypass
option will sort a class of permutations characterized by the avoidance of a finite set of patterns.
This is an extension of work of Atkinson and Sack [AS, Theorem 2] showing that a machine
made up of k pop stacks in parallel has sortable permutations being exactly a permutation class
with a finite basis. We can even utilize their constructive technique which is not dissimilar
to specific case shown in Proposition 8.2. As there is a small error in the original published
construction, we include the full proof of Theorem 8.3 here. We also note that the basis given
following Atkinson and Sack [AS, Theorem 2] for the k = 2 case is actually the complement
of the correct basis. Indeed, the set of permutations sortable by two pop stacks in parallel
(without a bypass) is Av(2341, 3412, 25314, 42531, 52314, 53124, 53142).
    Before stating Theorem 8.3, we will describe an algorithm that will sort a sortable permu-
tation π by k pop stacks in parallel with a bypass where we do not concern ourselves with
the image of any non-sortable permutation. At each stage, the process (in priority order) is as
follows:

  1. If the next entry of the input πj is the next needed entry for the output, πj goes directly
     to the output via the bypass operation.

  2. If the top entry of any pop stack Si is the next needed entry for the output, pop Si .

  3. If the next entry of the input πj is exactly one less than the top entry ti of pop stack Si ,
     push πj on top of ti .

  4. If the next entry of the input πj is not exactly one less than the top entry of any of the
     pop stacks, push πj into an empty pop stack.

  5. If none of these moves are possible, π is not sortable.

   With this set of legal moves in mind, we can now prove that the basis for the class of
permutations sortable by k pop stacks in parallel with a bypass is finite.



                                                25
Theorem 8.3. There is a finite set of permutations Bk such that a permutation π is sortable
by k pop stacks in parallel with a bypass if and only if π ∈ Av(Bk ).

Proof. Consider a permutation π that cannot be sorted by k pop stacks in parallel with a bypass
option by any algorithm. Specifically, there must be a point of failure in any process where no
legal move can be made. At this stage,

    1. every pop stack Si contains a nonempty consecutive sequence of entries increasing from
       top to bottom with top entry ti and bottom entry bi ,

    2. the last entry of the output is c (where c = 0 if the output is currently empty),

    3. the current input entry is πj

    4. ti > c + 1 for all i,

    5. For each i, we have either πj > ti or πj < ti − 1, and

    6. πj > c + 1.

     Notice that each entry bi , if bi 6= n, must have entered the stacks prior to bi + 1, as otherwise
bi would have been placed on top of bi + 1 instead of being put at the bottom of an empty stack.
Further, the entry c + 1 is not in the stacks, so must appear after πj in π. Finally, πj cannot
enter any stack, so if πj 6= n, then πj + 1 must appear after πj .
     However, conversely, suppose we have any set of (not necessarily distinct) entries
{b1 , b2 , . . . , bk , b1 + 1, b2 + 1, . . . , bk + 1, πj , πj + 1, c + 1} (where we eliminate the largest entry)
such that

    1. bi appears before bi + 1,

    2. πj appears after bi for all i

    3. πj appears before πj + 1, and

    4. c + 1 appears after πj .

Then each bi needs to be pushed into a different pop stack, none of the pop stacks can be
popped, and πj has no legal move (push or bypass). Thus we have a finite set of permutations
that can minimally fulfill the constraints forcing a failure. Therefore the basis of permutations
that can be sorted by a machine consisting of k pop stacks in parallel with a bypass is finite.

    It is a straightforward extension of the proof of [SV, Theorem 1] to show that the generating
function for the enumeration of permutations sortable by k pop stacks in parallel with a bypass
is rational for any k, so we include the following theorem without further justification.

Theorem 8.4. For any positive integer k, the set of permutations sortable by k pop stacks in
parallel with a bypass has a rational generating function.


9     Further work
9.1     An alternate algorithm and sorting words
   While PSB is an ideal algorithm to handle permutations when sorting with pop stacks with
a bypass, the priority of push over bypass can be a weakness when trying to modify to sort
words. When stack sorting, the patterns that must be avoided to be sortable are the same for

                                                       26
permutations and words. However, the same is not true for pop stack sorting where, in addition
to avoiding 213 and 312, words must also avoid 1010, as shown for Cayley words by Cerbai [Ce]
and later expanded to all words in [MSS]. The same additional restriction of avoiding 1010 for
words applies to PSB.
    If we have a regular word with the same number of copies of each letter, for example Stirling
permutations, or if we want to check the count of each letter prior to sorting, we can remove the
need to avoid 1010 by prioritizing the bypass operation more. It can be shown using techniques
similar to those given in Section 3 that Algorithm 3, which we call PSBW(k), gives the procedure
for k regular words with the smallest letter 0. This family of algorithms could also be expanded
to keep track of individual repetitions to properly prioritize the bypass when sorting other types
of words.

 1 S := ∅;
 2 i := 1;
 3 j := 0;
 4 while i ≤ kn do
 5   if πi ≤ ⌊ kj ⌋ then
 6       BYPASS;
 7           j := j + 1;
 8     else if πi ≤ TOP(S) then
 9        PUSH;
10     else
11        POP;
12        j := i − 1;
13        if πi ≤ ⌊ kj ⌋ then
14            BYPASS;
15            j := j + 1 ;
16           else
17              PUSH;

18     i := i + 1;
19 POP;

 Algorithm 3: PSBW(k) (S is the pop stack; TOP(S) is the current top element of the
 pop stack; π = π1 · · · πkn is the input k-regular word (for a permutation, k = 1); j is the
 number of entries in the output; TOP(S) at the top of pop stack.



9.2   Conjecture on the enumeration of simple permutations sortable by two
      pop stacks in series
    We have already noted that the terms of the generating function for the sequence corre-
sponding to the permutations of size n sortable by k = 2 pop stacks in parallel now appear
in [Sl] as A374165. However, there is also a potential nice closed form for the simple sortable
permutations that we boldly put here as a conjecture:

Conjecture. Let an be the number of simple permutations of size n that can be sorted by a
machine consisting of two pop stacks in parallel where entries are allowed to bypass the pop

stacks. Then a0 = a1 = 1, a2 = 2, an = F2n−5 − 1 if n ≥ 3 is odd, and an = F2n−5 if n > 3 is
even (where Fn is the n-th Fibonacci number).
