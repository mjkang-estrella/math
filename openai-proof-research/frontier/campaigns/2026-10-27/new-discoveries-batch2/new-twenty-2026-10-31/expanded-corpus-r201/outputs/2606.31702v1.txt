                                                                                           Abstract
                                                    List-coloring, introduced independently by Vizing and by Erdős, Rubin, and Taylor in the
                                                1970s, generalizes ordinary vertex coloring by assigning to each vertex its own set of admissible
                                                colors. A graph is chromatic-choosable if its list chromatic number equals its chromatic number.
                                                The previous survey on list-coloring by D R Woodall (2001), emphasized defective choosability,
                                                the list-coloring conjectures, and different methods used for list-coloring. This survey reviews
                                                major developments on list-coloring and chromatic-choosability, with emphasis on graph classes
                                                for which equality is known, graph classes exhibiting a nontrivial gap, and the principal methods
                                                used to prove such results. The survey covers embedded graphs, perfect graphs, complete bipar-
                                                tite and multipartite graphs, claw-free graphs, line graphs, powers of graphs, graph products,
                                                and selected variants of list-coloring.


                                           Keywords: List-coloring, Chromatic-choosability.

                                           Mathematics Subject Classification: 05C15


                                           1    Introduction
                                               The concept of graph list-coloring was introduced independently by Vizing [4] in 1976 and Erdős,
                                           Rubin, and Taylor [7] in 1979. Many foundational results can be found in the seminal paper by Erdös
                                           et al. [7]. Since list coloring generalizes ordinary vertex coloring, we begin by recalling the basic
                                           terminology of vertex coloring.

                                               The proper vertex coloring or simply vertex coloring of a graph is the coloring of its vertices
                                           in such a way that no two adjacent vertices receive the same color. We say that a given graph is
                                           k-colorable if the graph admits a proper coloring using k colors. The least k for which a graph G is
                                           k-colorable is called the chromatic number of G, denoted by χ(G).

                                              In ordinary vertex coloring, all vertices are colored from a common set of colors.
                                              A list assignment for a graph G is a function L that assigns to each vertex v its palette of colors
                                           L(v), called the list of v. The graph G is said to be L-colorable if there exists a proper coloring c of
                                           G such that c(v) ∈ L(v) for every v ∈ V (G).

                                               A list assignment L is called a k-list assignment if |L(v)| = k for every v ∈ V (G). A graph G is
                                           k-choosable or k-list-colorable if G admits an L-coloring for every k-list assignment L. The least k
                                           for which a graph G is k-choosable is called the list chromatic number, choice number, or choosability
                                           of G, denoted by χL (G), χℓ (G) or ch(G). Throughout this paper, we use the notation χℓ (G).

                                              Every k-choosable graph is k-colorable: take the same k-element list at every vertex. However,
                                           any k-colorable graph need not be k-choosable. For example, any graph G is not (χ(G)−1)-choosable

Several major conjectures ask whether broader graph classes are chromatic-choosable; these are dis-
cussed in later sections.

example. We know χ(K2,4 ) = 2. Assign the lists {{a, b}, {c, d}} and {{a, c}, {a, d}, {b, c}, {b, d}} for

2     Upper bounds for χℓ (G)
2.1    In terms of ∆(G)
    The classical greedy bound gives χ(G) ≤ ∆(G) + 1. Brooks’ theorem improves this to χ(G) ≤
∆(G) for every connected graph that is neither complete nor an odd cycle. Erdős et al. [7] proved
the corresponding list-coloring analogue: any connected graph G that is neither a complete graph
nor an odd cycle, is ∆(G)-choosable. The greedy coloring argument also extends to list-coloring,
yielding ∆(G) + 1 as an upper bound for the list chromatic number for all graphs.

2.2    The coloring number
    Another relevant upper bound is the coloring number. The coloring number of a graph G,
denoted by col(G), is given by col(G) = 1 + max{δ(G′ ) : G′ ⊆ G}. Equivalently, col(G), is the
least integer d for which G admits an ordering v1 , v2 , ..., vn such that each vertex vi has at most
d − 1 neighbors among v1 , v2 , ..., vi−1 . Thus col(G) is one more than the degeneracy of G, and in
particular col(G) ≤ ∆(G) + 1.

an algebraic formulation via the graph polynomial and a combinatorial formulation via orientations.

   The first viewpoint is algebraic and uses the graph polynomial associated with the given graph
G. This is done by giving a linear order for the vertices and then associating with each vertex z, a
variable xz . The graph polynomial of G is defined by,

    Despite its strength, the Alon–Tarsi method can be difficult to apply directly. On the algebraic
side, dense graphs lead to graph polynomials with many factors and complicated coefficient struc-
ture. On the orientation side, the number of relevant orientations grows rapidly with the number of
edges. Consequently, the method is most effective when additional structure restricts the possible
orientations or simplifies the polynomial.

for any graph G, the upper bounds mentioned above occur in the following order:

3     Planar graphs
    A k-degenerate graph is (k +1)-choosable. Planar graphs being 5-degenerate are 6-choosable. We
start with the conjecture posed by Erdős et al. [7] which was the first conjecture on the list-coloring
of planar graphs.
Conjecture 3.1 ([7]). Every planar graph is 5-choosable.


                                                  3
    Thomassen [20] proved this conjecture by strengthening the induction hypothesis in a highly
effective way. He showed that a near-triangulation remains list-colorable when two adjacent vertices
on the outer face are precolored, the other boundary vertices have lists of size at least three, and the
interior vertices have lists of size at least five. This carefully designed rigid boundary condition is
the key mechanism that allows the induction step to carry through smoothly by carefully peeling a
vertex off the boundary and updating the lists of its internal neighbors without dropping their sizes
below the required thresholds. Later, the result was broadened to K5 -minor-free graphs [36].

   By the four-color theorem, every planar graph is 4-colorable. Hence it is natural to ask whether
every planar graph is also 4-choosable. Erdős, Rubin, and Taylor conjectured that this is not the
case.

Conjecture 3.2 ([7]). There exists a planar graph which is not 4-choosable.
   This conjecture was proved true by Voigt [18] by giving a non-4-choosable planar graph with 238
vertices. Later, Gutner [26] improved this result by giving a smaller graph with 75 vertices that is
not 4-choosable. Both of these graphs are 4-colorable but not 4-choosable. Mirzakhani [29] subse-
quently constructed an example of a 3-colorable planar graph with 63 vertices that is not 4-choosable.

    We next survey the sufficient conditions under which planar graphs are 4-choosable. The initial
set of results used forbidden cycles. Lam et al. [39] proved the 4-choosability of C4 -free planar
graphs. Fijavž et al. [47] extended the result to C5 -free planar graphs and C6 -free planar graphs by
showing that they are 3-degenerate. They immediately conjectured the 4-list-colorability of planar
graphs without C7 . Farzad [79] proved this conjecture and extended the result to planar graphs
without C7 by using the discharge method, another method for the list-coloring of planar graphs.
Consequently, it may be concluded that planar graphs that are Ck -free for any k ∈ {3, 4, 5, 6, 7} are
4-choosable.

    In addition to these, the other conditions involved for being 4-choosable are the absence of inter-
secting triangles [51], absence of C4 adjacent to C3 [67], or every C5 is not simultaneously adjacent to
C3 or C4 [111]. Although the proof for 4-choosability of planar graphs without intersecting triangles
uses both the boundary method and the discharge method, the result for planar graphs with C5 not
simultaneously adjacent to C3 or C4 is restricted to the discharge method.

    The study of 3-choosability for planar graphs is subtler, since planar graphs are not 3-choosable
in general. In [7], Erdős et al. questioned the existence of a non-3-choosable planar bipartite graph.
Although they identified a non-3-choosable planar graph, it was not bipartite. Alon [13] answered
this question by proving that every planar bipartite graph is 3-choosable which was a consequence
of the finding that Alon-Tarsi number of such graphs is 3. The example of a non-3-choosable planar
graph without 4-cycles, 5-cycles, and intersecting triangles appears in [63].

    The girth of a planar graph plays an important role when it comes to list-coloring. Thomassen
[23] proved that every planar graph of girth at least 5 is 3-choosable. This can be seen as a list-
coloring analogue, with a slightly stronger girth requirement, of Grötzsch’s theorem, which states
that every planar graph of girth at least 4 is 3-colorable. Li [82] extended Thomassen’s result for
planar graphs of girth at least 4 that has no C4 that shares a vertex with another C4 or C5 . Later,
Guo and Wang [87] proved a result that strengthened Li’s statement. They showed that planar
graphs of girth 4 that have no C4 that shares a vertex with another C4 or C5 are 3-choosable.

     It is known that planar graphs with girth 4 are 4-choosable because they are 3-degenerate. Voigt
[24] and Gutner [26] constructed planar graphs of girth 4 that are not 3-choosable, with 166 vertices
and 164 vertices, respectively. For a planar graph of girth 4, 3-choosability is also guaranteed when
it is either {C5 , C6 }-free [58] or {C7 , C8 }-free [78].

    When there are no girth constraints, the results depend on forbidden cycles and often on the
distance between the triangles. The distance between two triangles, say T and T ′ , d(T, T ′ ) =
min{d(u, v) : u ∈ T, v ∈ T ′ }. Montassier et al. [63] proved that a {C4 , C5 }-free planar graph is
3-choosable if d(T, T ′ ) ≥ 4 for any T, T ′ or is C6 -free with d(T, T ′ ) ≥ 3 for any T, T ′ . Zhang and Sun
[74] proved that a {C5 , C6 }-free planar graph is 3-choosable if either it is C7 -free with d(T, T ′ ) ≥ 3
for any T, T ′ or C8 -free with d(T, T ′ ) ≥ 2 for any T, T ′ . Wang et al. [73] summarized the results
in [57, 60, 65] and generalized it by stating that any planar graph without C4 , Ci , Cj and C9 where

4    Toroidal graphs
    Toroidal graphs, the graphs that can be embedded on a torus, exhibit properties similar to pla-
nar graphs when it comes to coloring. By the Heawood bound for the torus, every toroidal graph
is 7-colorable, and this bound is sharp since K7 embeds on the torus. Toroidal graphs, being 6-
degenerate, are 7-choosable. Böhme et al. [37] gave the necessary and sufficient condition for a
toroidal graph to have list chromatic number 7. For a toroidal graph G, χℓ (G) = 7 if and only if K7
is an induced subgraph of G.

    Cai et al. [85] gave the list-coloring of the toroidal graphs excluding cycles of fixed length k.
Their results showed that a toroidal graph is 4-choosable if k = 3, 4, 5, 5-choosable if k = 6 and
6-choosable if k = 7. Furthermore, they also proved that χℓ (G) = 6 if and only if K6 is an induced
subgraph of G. Based on this finding and the result given by Böhme et al.[37], they conjectured that
χℓ (G) = 5 if and only if K5 is an induced subgraph of G. Choi [107] disproved this conjecture by
giving an infinite family of graphs containing neither a K5 nor a C6 which is not 4-colorable, hence
not 4-choosable. Alternatively, Choi [107] proved that a toroidal graph containing neither a K5 − e
nor a C6 is 4-choosable.

   While most of the results for planar graphs cannot be extended to toroidal graphs, there are
some exceptions. The 4-choosability of toroidal graphs without intersecting triangles [66] and the
4-choosability of toroidal graphs for which every C5 is not adjacent simultaneously to C3 or C4 [113]
are two of such results.

   The Cartesian product of two paths Pm and Pn is often called a grid and is denoted by Lm,n .
Similarly, the Cartesian product of two cycles Cm , Cn is often called a toroidal grid and is denoted
by Tm,n , as it is a grid that can be embedded on a torus. Li et al. [127] proved that for any m, n ∈ N
the toroidal grid Tm,n is 4-choosable. This provides another natural link between toroidal graphs
and graph products.


5    Perfect graphs
    A graph G is perfect if χ(H) = ω(H) for every induced subgraph H of G. Perfect graphs form a
broad and structurally rich class. Among the earliest list-coloring results relevant to perfect graphs
is the characterization of 2-choosable graphs by Erdős, Rubin, and Taylor [7], which implies that
even cycles are chromatic-choosable. The same paper also treats odd-cycles, showing that every
cycle is chromatic-choosable.

    The next chromatic-choosable class of perfect graphs was chordal graphs [31]. Chordal graphs
are characterized by simplicial elimination ordering i.e., for any chordal graph with n vertices, there
exists a vertex ordering v1 , v2 , . . . , vn such that NGi (v) induces a clique in Gi , where Gi is the sub-
graph induced by v1 , v2 , . . . , vi . The chromatic-choosability was immediate from this vertex ordering
of the chordal graphs.

    Hall’s theorem on systems of distinct representatives (SDR) is one of the oldest and most com-
mon techniques used in list-coloring. An SDR for a family of sets S1 , S2 , . . . , Sk is a sequence
(x1 , x2 , . . . , xk ) such that xi ∈ Si for each i, and xi ̸= xj whenever i ̸= j. We may treat the lists
assigned for the vertices as the family of sets and a proper list-coloring as an SDR keeping the adja-
cency constraints. Using this idea, {K3 , P4 }-free graphs have been proved to be chromatic-choosable
[45].

   Interval graphs are yet another important class of perfect graphs. As a consequence of the Alon-
Tarsi method, if the given graph G has an acyclic orientation and a list assignment L satisfying the


                                                     5
condition that the outdegree of each vertex is at most one less than the size of list assigned to it,
then G is L-colorable. Woodall [45] observed that any interval graph G has an acyclic orientation
with d+ (v) ≤ ω(G) − 1. Hence, χℓ (G) = χ(G) = ω(G).

    An alternate proof for some classes of graphs like block graphs, k-trees and threshold graphs
can be found in [133]. Several important subclasses of perfect graphs — including bipartite graphs,
complete multipartite graphs, and claw-free perfect graphs — have developed substantial list-coloring
literatures. These classes are therefore treated separately in the following sections.


6     Bipartite graphs
    This section focuses primarily on complete bipartite graphs, for which many exact and asymp-
totic results are known. Graphs with chromatic number at most two are precisely bipartite graphs;
among graphs with at least one edge, the 2-chromatic graphs are exactly the bipartite graphs. This
does not guarantee 2-choosability. The characterization for being 2-choosable uses the concept of
m-core of a graph G, denoted by Gˆm . It is the graph obtained by deleting the vertices in G of
degree less than m successively until there are no more vertices of degree less than m. Specifically,
a graph G is 2-choosable if and only if Ĝ2 is of the form K1 , C2m+2 or θ2,2,2m for m ≥ 1, where
θ2,2,2m is a graph consisting of two vertices x and y with three vertex disjoint x, y-paths each of
length 2, 2 and 2m [7]. Recently, a generalization of this result was established in [120] that a graph
G is k-choosable if and only if Gˆk is k-choosable.

    We first consider balanced complete bipartite graphs Km,m . Erdős et al. [7] showed that if

6.1    K1,q and K2,q
   Using the characterization of 2-choosable graphs, it is evident that the only 2-choosable graphs
among the complete bipartite graph family are K1,n for n ∈ N, K2,2 and K2,3 , and all others fail
to be chromatic-choosable. In their fundamental paper on list-coloring, Erdős et al. [7] showed
that any Kp,q with p ≤ q is (p + 1)-choosable and precisely p-choosable if and only if q < pp . An
immediate consequence of these results gives χℓ (K2,q ) = 3 for q > 3.

6.2    K3,q
    Another immediate consequence of the result in [7] gives χℓ (K3,q ) = 3 for q < 27. Mahadev
et al. [11] extensively investigated the 3-choosability of bipartite graphs. They relied solely on the
concept of the System of Distinct Representatives (SDR) to derive the results. They showed that
K3,q for q ≥ 27 is not 3-choosable. But incorporating a later result that, χℓ (Kp,q ) = p + 1 when
q ≥ pp [116], we can conclude that χℓ (K3,q ) = 4 for q ≥ 27.

6.3    K4,q
   The other details in [11] include the set of 3-choosable and non-3-choosable complete bipartite
graphs. For K4,q , there is a boundary value of 18 for q, less than or equal to which the graph
becomes 3-choosable and greater than which the graph is no longer 3-choosable. A consequence of
the result given by Hoffman and Johnson was used to investigate the result for a larger range of q.
As χℓ (Kp,q ) = p when (p − 1)p−1 − (p − 2)p−1 < q < pp [17], χℓ (Kp,q ) = 4 when 19 ≤ q ≤ 255.

6.4    K5,q
    Mahadev et al. [11] showed that K5,q is 3-choosable for q ≤ 8 and is not 3-choosable for q ≥ 15,
leaving the cases 9 ≤ q ≤ 14 unresolved. Shende and Tesman [22] partially filled the gap by showing

6.5    K6,q
    Additionally, in [11] they added that K6,q for q ≥ 11 is not 3-choosable. Although the 3-list-
coloring for q < 11 is not known, combining it with the result given by Hanson et al. [27] that
χℓ (G) ≤ 3 for every bipartite graph with at most 13 vertices, we have χℓ (K6,6 ) = χℓ (K6,7 ) = 3. In
his preprint, O’Donnell established that χℓ (K6,11 ) = 4.

    Hoffman and Johnson introduced a concept called thwart number in their paper [19]. The thwart
number of a graph G, thw(G) is the smallest k for which G is not list-colorable when the lists assigned
are k subsets of {1, 2, . . . , χℓ (G) − 1}. Subsequently, for any graph G, χℓ (G) ≤ thw(G). In [28], the
same authors obtained that thw(K6,q ) = 3 for q = 8, 9, 10 and hence their list chromatic number of
these graphs is also 3.

6.6    K7,q
   In [11], they also established that K7,q is not 3-choosable. The result in [7] given by χℓ (K7,7 ) ≥ 4
supports this finding. The non-3-choosability of K7,7 was given using the Fano plane configuration.
The equality was established later.

8     Claw-free graphs
    Claw-free graphs are K1,3 -free graphs. Gravier and Maffray [33] initiated the systematic study
of chromatic-choosability for claw-free graphs and formulated the following conjecture.
Conjecture 8.1 (LCC for claw-free graphs [33]). For every claw-free graph G, χℓ (G) = χ(G).
    Gravier and Maffray [33] proved that any k-colorable claw-free graph with at most 2k + 2 vertices
is k-choosable [35]. Chudnovsky and Seymour [86] gave a slightly better bound for the list chromatic
number of claw-free graph as the maximum among χ(G) and n(G)      2 . They showed that for claw-free
graphs G with χℓ (G − {v}) ≤ 2ω(G − {v}), for some vertex v in G whose neighborhood is the union
of two cliques, then χℓ (G) ≤ 2ω(G). In the same paper, the authors showed that twice the clique
number bounds the chromatic number of a claw-free graph with independence number at least three.
As a consequence of this bound, a {claw, K4 }-free graph is 6-colorable and a {claw, K5 }-free graph
is 8-colorable. Esperet et al. [95] studied the corresponding list-coloring questions and proved that
{claw, K4 }-free graphs are 4-choosable and {claw, K5 }-free graphs are 7-choosable.

   It is well known that any graph is (∆(G) + 1)-colorable. Borodin and Kostochka [5] had conjec-
tured that every graph G with ∆(G) ≥ 9 and that has no clique of size ∆(G) is (∆(G) − 1)-colorable.
Cranston and Rabern [109] proved that the conjecture is true for claw-free graphs. In the same pa-
per by these authors, we can find the list-coloring analogue of the conjecture given by Borodin and
Kostochka.
Conjecture 8.2 ([109]). Every graph G with ∆(G) ≥ 9 and has no clique of size ∆(G) is (∆(G)−1)-
choosable.
   In a later paper, the same authors have shown that if G is a claw-free graph with ∆(G) ≥ 69
and has no clique of size ∆(G) is (∆(G) − 1)-choosable. For the graphs for which 9 ≤ ∆(G) ≤ 68
and the same condition, the conjecture is still open.

   Although chromatic-choosability remains largely open for claw-free graphs in general, stronger
results are known for claw-free perfect graphs.

8.1    Claw-free perfect graphs
   We first discuss elementary graphs, a subclass of claw-free perfect graphs. A graph G is said
to be an elementary graph if its edges can be bicolored such that every induced P3 is bicolored.
Gravier and Maffray [33] proved that any elementary graph G with clique number at most 3 is
chromatic-choosable.

    Another subclass of claw-free perfect graphs is peculiar graphs. Rather than a definition, we
have a construction for peculiar graph which is as follows. Let Kn be a complete graph whose set of
vertices is split into six pairwise disjoint nonempty sets A1 , B1 , A2 , B2 , A3 , B3 . For each i = 1, 2, 3,
remove at least one edge with one endpoint in Ai and the other endpoint in Bi+1 (subscript is modulo
3). Finally, add three pairwise disjoint nonempty cliques Q1 , Q2 , Q3 , and, for each i = 1, 2, 3, provide
adjacencies between every vertex in Qi and Kn − (Ai ∪ Bi ). Gravier and Maffray [35] established
the chromatic-choosability of peculiar graphs.

    The classification of claw-free perfect graphs according to Chvátal [9] states that every claw-free
perfect graph that has no clique cutset is either elementary or peculiar. A graph G is said to have a
clique cutset if it has a vertex cut that induces a clique. Giving an extension to 3-chromatic claw-
free perfect graphs with clique cutset, Gravier and Maffray [55] established that every 3-chromatic
claw-free perfect graph is 3-choosable.

    After nearly two decades, Gravier et al. [105] contributed further to the chromatic-choosability

The authors formally stated the chromatic-choosability of claw-free perfect graphs as a conjecture

Conjecture 8.3 (LCC for claw-free perfect graphs [105]). For every claw-free perfect graph G,

    The conjecture for claw-free perfect graphs is a natural intermediate problem toward the broader
list-coloring conjecture for claw-free graphs. Using the classification of claw-free perfect graphs,
we can say that the conjecture remains open for non-peculiar claw-free perfect graphs with clique

8.2    Line graphs
   We considered different versions of the list-coloring conjecture. We now discuss two more such
conjectures. The first one was stated independently by Vizing, by Gupta, and by Albertson and
Harris. The second is its formulation for line graphs by Häggkvist and Chetwynd [15].

    The edge coloring of a graph is the coloring of its edges in such a way that no two incident edges
receive the same color. We say that a given graph is k-edge colorable if the graph admits an edge
coloring using k colors. The least k for which a graph G is k-edge colorable is called the chromatic
index of G, denoted by χ′ (G).

    Let L be a list assignment on the edge set of G. If G can be edge colored from their corresponding
lists, we say that G is L-edge colorable. If all lists are of the same size, say k and G is L-edge colorable
for every k-list assignment L on G, then G is k-edge choosable. The least k for which a graph G is
k-edge choosable is called the list chromatic index of G, denoted by χ′ℓ (G) or ch′ (G).
Conjecture 8.4 (List Edge Coloring Conjecture (LECC)). For any graph G, χ′ℓ (G) = χ′ (G).
   Corresponding to any given graph G, there is a graph associated with it, called the line graph
of G. The line graph of G, denoted by L(G) is the graph with V (L(G)) = E(G) and any pair of
vertices in L(G) are adjacent if the corresponding pair of edges share a common endpoint. Line
graphs form a subclass of claw-free graphs.

   Observe that the edge coloring of a graph is equivalent to the vertex coloring of its line graph.
Similarly, the list edge coloring of a graph is equivalent to the list-coloring of its line graph. Thus,
Häggkvist and Chetwynd rephrased the LECC as follows.
Conjecture 8.5 (LCC for Line Graphs [15]). For any graph G, χℓ (L(G)) = χ(L(G)).
    Several results have been proved in favour of the conjecture. Snarks [8], K3,3 [10], K4,4 [13], K6,6
[13], bipartite graphs [21], 2-connected regular planar graphs [25], d-regular multiplanar graphs [25],
K2n+1 [34], line-perfect graphs [40], series-parallel graphs [38], multicircuits [42], multigraphs with
a vertex whose deletion makes the graph bipartite [41], graphs with no circuit of size 4 or more [80].

    We highlight Galvin’s proof for the bipartite graphs [21] as it introduced one of the most impor-
tant applications of the kernel method to list coloring. A kernel in a digraph D is an independent
set S ⊆ V (D) such that every vertex in V (D) \ S has an outgoing edge to a vertex of S. A digraph
is kernel-perfect if every induced subdigraph has a kernel. The kernel lemma states that if a graph
G has a kernel-perfect orientation D satisfying d+  D (v) ≤ |L(v)| − 1 for every vertex v, then G is
L-colorable.

    Let H be a bipartite multigraph with bipartition (X, Y ), and let G = L(H). The vertices of
G are the edges of H. For x ∈ X, the set of edges incident with x may be viewed as a row, and
for y ∈ Y , the set of edges incident with y may be viewed as a column. Starting from a proper


                                                    10
n-edge-coloring f : E(H) → {1, . . . , n}, where n = ∆(H), Galvin defines an orientation of L(H) as
follows: if two adjacent edges u, v lie in the same row, orient u → v whenever f (u) > f (v); if they
lie in the same column, orient u → v whenever f (u) < f (v). Thus, the orientation is determined by
the edge-coloring together with the bipartition of H.

   This orientation has maximum outdegree at most n − 1. Moreover, it is a normal orientation of
the line graph, and Galvin uses the stable marriage theorem, in Maffray’s formulation, to show that
every induced subdigraph has a kernel. The kernel lemma then implies that L(H) is n-choosable.
Since the chromatic number of L(H) is the chromatic index of H, and a bipartite multigraph has
chromatic index equal to its maximum degree, it follows that:

                                        χℓ (L(H)) = χ(L(H)).

Equivalently, the list chromatic index of every bipartite multigraph equals its chromatic index.


9    Square of graphs
   The square of a given graph G, denoted by G2 is the graph with V (G2 ) = V (G) and E(G2 )={uv :
u and v are of distance at most 2 in G}. This section surveys list-coloring results for squares of
graphs. We begin with total coloring, since total coloring can be naturally reformulated as a coloring
problem for squares of subdivision graphs.

    The total coloring of a graph is the coloring of its vertices and edges in such a way that no two
adjacent vertices, no two incident edges and, no incident vertex-edge pair receive the same color. We
say that a given graph is k-total colorable if the graph admits a total coloring using k colors. The
least k for which a graph G is k-total colorable is called the total chromatic number of G, denoted
by χ′′ (G).

    Let L be a list assignment on the vertex set and edge set of G. If G can be totally colored from
their corresponding lists, we say that G is L-totally colorable. If all lists are of the same size, say k
and G is L-totally colorable for every k-list assignment L on G, then G is k-totally choosable. The
least k for which a graph G is k-totally choosable is called the list total chromatic number or total
choosability of G, denoted by χ′′ℓ (G) or ch′′ (G).

   Borodin et al. [32] stated a conjecture on the coincidence of total chromatic number and list
total chromatic number in 1996. This is called the list total coloring conjecture.
Conjecture 9.1 (List Total Coloring Conjecture (LTCC) [32]). For any multigraph G, χ′′ℓ (G) =
χ′′ (G).
     Corresponding to any given graph G, there is a graph associated with it, called the total graph of
G. The total graph of G, denoted by T (G) is the graph with V (T (G)) = V (G)∪E(G) and any pair of
vertices in T (G) are adjacent if the corresponding pair of vertices are adjacent or the corresponding
pair of edges are incident or the corresponding edge is incident on the corresponding vertex. Note
that the total coloring of a graph is equivalent to the coloring of its total graph. Analogously, the
list total coloring of a graph is equivalent to the list-coloring of its total graph. Thus, LTCC can be
rephrased as χℓ (T (G)) = χ(T (G)).

   We do not discuss LTCC in detail, but we recall several classes of graphs for which LTCC holds.
Kostochka and Woodall showed that for some graphs like multigraphs whose underlying simple
graph is a forest, multigraphs whose underlying simple graph is a circuit and bipartite graphs with
total chromatic number ∆ + 2, LTCC holds. See [44, 48, 49] for details. While working with these
graphs, they observed that for any multigraph G, T (G) = H 2 where H is the graph obtained by
subdividing every edge of G exactly once. At this point, Kostochka and Woodall [44] conjectured
the chromatic-choosability of squares of graphs.
Conjecture 9.2 (List Square Coloring Conjecture (LSCC) [44]). For any graph G, χℓ (G2 ) = χ(G2 ).
   It can be seen that LSCC implies LTCC. Since T (G) = H 2 , where H is obtained from G by
subdividing every edge exactly once, the List Total Coloring Conjecture is a special case of the List
Square Coloring Conjecture on bipartite graphs of this type. Hence, LSCC is true for all graphs for
which LTCC holds. A look-alike of this conjecture and a small variant of this question is raised in

Question [100]: If G is a bipartite graph such that every vertex of one partite set has degree at
most 2, then is it true that χℓ (G2 ) = χ(G2 )?

Question [100]: If the above question is true, then what is the largest k such that G2 is chromatic-
choosable for every bipartite graph G with a partite set in which each vertex has degree at most k?

    Later, the conjecture was disproved by Kim and Park [101]. For each prime p > 2, they con-

9.2    Squares of subcubic planar graphs
Some cubic planar graphs provide counterexamples to the list square coloring conjecture for planar
graphs. We now focus on results in this class. A graph with ∆ ≤ 3 is called a subcubic graph.
Thomassen [114] showed that the square of a subcubic planar graph is 7-colorable. This naturally
raises the question whether the square of a subcubic planar graph is 7-choosable. As in the planar
case, we can see that girth is an important factor for the results involving squares of subcubic planar
graphs.

    In [69], Cranston and Kim established that for every subcubic graph G except the Petersen
graph, G2 is 8-choosable. This bound is tight, as the square of the graph obtained by deleting an
edge from the Petersen graph is K8 . They also proved that the square of every subcubic planar
graph of girth at least 7 is 7-choosable, and that the square of every subcubic planar graph of girth
at least 9 is 6-choosable. An improvement to this result was given by Kim and Lian in [129] that
the square of a subcubic planar graph of girth at least 6 is 7-choosable. Dvořák et al. [70] extended
these results to the squares of a subcubic planar graph of girth at least 14 and at least 24 are 5-
choosable and 4-choosable respectively. Havet [81] made an improvement to one of the results given
by Dvořák et al. and thus establishing that the square of a subcubic planar graph of girth at least
13 is 5-choosable. It can be observed that as girth of a subcubic planar graph G is increased, the
bound on χℓ (G2 ) is tightened gradually.

11     Graph operations
   This section surveys list-coloring results for standard graph operations, including joins, Cartesian
products, and lexicographic products.

11.1    Join of graphs
    The join of two graphs G and H, denoted by G ∨ H, is the graph with vertex set V (G ∨ H) =
V (G) ∪ V (H) and E(G ∨ H) = E(G) ∪ E(H) ∪ {uv : u ∈ V (G), v ∈ V (H)}. It is known that
χ(G ∨ H) = χ(G) + χ(H). But this need not necessarily hold for the list chromatic number. If
Sk denotes a stable set of size k, we have Kn,nn = Sn ∨ Snn . When χℓ (Sn ∨ Snn ) = n + 1,
χℓ (Sn ) + χℓ (Snn ) = 2. Replacing Sn with any graph G with n vertices, we can deduce that G ∨ Snn
is not n-choosable.

    Ohba [50] gave a condition under which the join
                                                 p of two graphs is chromatic-choosable. He proved
that whenever n(G) + n(H) ≤ (χ(G) + χ(H)) + 2 (χ(G) + χ(H)), then χℓ (G ∨ H) = χ(G ∨ H). He
also proved that for any graph G, there exists a non-negative integer n0 such that for any integer
n ≥ n0 , G ∨ Kn is chromatic-choosable. Moreover, χℓ (G ∨ K1 ) ≤ χℓ (G) + 1, and the equality need
not hold always.

    Gravier et al. [52] showed that for a graph G, there exists a function f (G) defined as the small-
est integer k such that G ∨ Sk is not n(G)-choosable. Using this result, it follows that f (Kn ) = 1
and f (Sn ) = nn . Thus, for any graph with n vertices, 1 ≤ f (G) ≤ nn . They have also given the
value of f for some graphs, such as complete graphs, graphs that are disjoint unions of cliques, and
triangle-free graphs.

Open Problem (Allagan from Open Problem Garden): Given a, b ≥ 2, what is the smallest
t ≥ 0 such that χℓ (Ka,b ∨ Kt ) ≤ χ(Ka,b ∨ Kt )?

11.2    Cartesian product
    The Cartesian product of two graphs G = (V (G), E(G)) and H = (V (H), E(H)), denoted by
G□H, is the graph with vertex set V (G) × V (H) and any two vertices (u, v), (u′ , v ′ ) are adjacent
if and only if either u = u′ and vv ′ ∈ E(H) or v = v ′ and uu′ ∈ E(G). Sabidussi [1] proved that
χ(G□H) = max{χ(G), χ(H)}. Hence one possible question was χℓ (G□H) = max{χℓ (G), χℓ (H)},
which was proved false by Borowiecki and Jozef [61].

   As Km □Kn ∼ = L(Km,n ), it was trivial from Galvin’s result that [21], χℓ (Km □Kn ) = χ(Km □Kn ) =
max{m, n}. One of the first contributions to list-coloring of products of graphs was given by
Borowiecki and Jozef [61] in the form of an upper bound for the Cartesian product of two graphs as

Conjecture 11.3 ([127]). χℓ (C2m+1 □C2n+1 ) = 3.
   The most recent work is given by Li et al.[132]. They have succeeded in finding the Alon-Tarsi

pointed to the fact that in either case, the list chromatic number is strictly less than the Alon-Tarsi
number. Finding the list chromatic number of these products remains open. The authors have given
an open question at the end of the paper.

Open problem [132]: For a given pair of graphs G and H with some conditions, if χℓ (G) = AT (G)
and χℓ (H) = AT (H), is χℓ (G□H) = AT (G□H)?

11.3     Lexicographic product
    The lexicographic product of two graphs G = (V (G), E(G)) and H = (V (H), E(H)), denoted
by G[H], is the graph with vertex set V (G) × V (H) and any two vertices (u, v), (u′ , v ′ ) are adjacent
if and only if uu′ ∈ E(G) or u = u′ and vv ′ ∈ E(H). As with the other products, Hammack et al.
[88] proved that χ(G[H]) ≤ χ(G)χ(H).

    Balázs and Xuding [110] gave a bound for the lexicographic product of two graphs of the same
order. Let G and H be graphs with n vertices. Then χℓ (G[H]) ≤ (4∆(G) + 2)(χℓ (H) + log n). They
also posed a question on a slightly different bound for lexicographic product of two graphs of the
same order.

Open problem [99] : Does there exist a constant c such that, for any graph G and any graph H
on n vertices, χℓ (G[H]) ≤ cχℓ (G)(χℓ (H) + log n)?

    Let G be a graph with V (G) = {u1 , u2 , . . . , un }. A graph G′ is the inflation of G if V (G′ ) is the
disjoint union of n sets V1 , V2 , . . . , Vn such that each Vk is a clique and x ∈ Vi , y ∈ Vj are adjacent in
G′ if and only if vi , vj is adjacent in G. Kostochka and Woodall [44] had proved that the inflation of
a graph with at most five vertices and the inflation of square of a graph with at most seven vertices
are chromatic-choosable. The method employed for the proof is Hall’s theorem.

    Let G′ be an inflation of the graph G. If all the Vk ’s are of same size, say t, it is called uniform
inflation and is denoted by (G)t . It can be observed that this uniform inflation is the same as the
lexicographic product G[Kt ]. Prowse and Woodall [53] showed that the uniform inflation of power
of a cycle is chromatic-choosable.i.e., χℓ ((Cnp )t ) = χ((Cnp )t ). Since it is uniform inflation, we may
restate that the lexicographic product Cnp [Kt ] is chromatic-choosable.


12      Some regular graphs
   Fleischner and Stiebitz [14] proved that for a 4-regular graph G on 3n vertices whose edges can
be decomposed into a Hamiltonian circuit and n pairwise vertex-disjoint triangles, AT (G) = 3. As
χ(G) = 3, we have, any graph satisfying these conditions are chromatic-choosable. Later, Gutner
and Tarsi [80] gave a generalization of this result for any (3k + 1)-regular graph on 3kn vertices
whose edges can be decomposed into a Hamiltonian circuit and n pairwise vertex-disjoint 3k-cliques.
They showed that for such a graph G, χℓ (G) = χ(G) = 3k.


13      Some variants of list-coloring
13.1     Paintability
    The concept of paintability was introduced in 2009 by Schauz[83]. Let us look at a game on a
given graph G. Paintability is defined through a two-player game on a graph G, played by Lister
and Painter. Let f : V (G) → N. Initially, no vertex is colored, and each vertex v has f (v) tokens.
In each round, Lister chooses a set S of uncolored vertices and removes one token from every vertex
in S. Painter then colors an independent subset of S. Lister wins if some uncolored vertex has no
tokens remaining; Painter wins if all vertices are colored. The graph G is f -paintable if Painter
has a winning strategy for the function f , and G is k-paintable if it is f -paintable for the constant
function f (v) = k. The paint number χp (G) is the least k for which G is k-paintable.
    It can be seen that χℓ (G) ≤ χp (G) and the difference between can be arbitrarily large [98].

13.2     DP coloring
    Dvořák and Postle [112] introduced a generalization of list-coloring called correspondence color-
ing, which was later renamed as DP-coloring.

    Let G be a graph. A cover of G is defined to be a pair (L, H), where L is a pairwise disjoint list
assignment of G and H is a graph with V (H) = ∪v∈V (G) L(v) with the following conditions:
    (a) For each v ∈ V (G), H[L(v)] is a complete graph.
    (b) For each uv ∈ E(G), the edge set between L(u) and L(v) is a matching.
    (c) For each uv ∈
                    / E(G), the edge set between L(u) and L(v) is empty.
An (L, H)-coloring of G is an independent set I of V (H) of size n(G). And G is (L, H)-colorable
if G has an (L, H)-coloring. The DP-chromatic number of a graph G, denoted by χDP (G), is the
least k for which G is (L, H)-colorable for every cover (L, H), with L being a k-list assignment.

   Since DP-coloring generalizes list-coloring, we have χℓ (G) ≤ χDP (G).

13.3     (a : b)-choosability
   Another early variant introduced by Erdős, Rubin, and Taylor [7], in the same paper in which
they introduced list-coloring is (a : b)-choosability.
   Let L be an a-list assignment on G. If there exists a b-list assignment L′ on G in such a way that


                                                      16
   (a) L′ (v) ⊆ L(v), for all v ∈ V (G)
   (b) L′ (x) ∩ L′ (y) = ϕ whenever xy ∈ E(G)
then we say that G is (a : b)-choosable. The k th choice number of G, denoted by chk (G), is the
smallest n for which G is (n : k)-choosable.

    It can be seen that (a : 1)-choosable is nothing but a-choosable. Hence χℓ (G) = ch1 (G). Prowse
and Woodall [53] inspected the equivalence of (a : b)-choosable and a-choosable, and conjectured as
follows.
Conjecture 13.1 ((a : b)-Choosability Equivalence Conjecture [53]). For all a, b ∈ N, a graph G is
(a : b)-choosable if and only if (G)b is a-choosable.

Conjecture 13.2 (Weak (a : b)-Choosability Conjecture [53]). For all a, b, k ∈ N, if a graph G is
(a : b)-choosable, then G is (ka : kb)-choosable.
    We can find a few examples for which the conjecture holds. Tuza and Voigt [30] proved that
every 2-choosable graph is (2m : m)-choosable for every m ∈ N. Gutner and Tarsi [80] proved that
every planar bipartite graph is (3m : m)-choosable for every m ∈ N and every d-degenerate graph is
((d + 1)m : m)-choosable for every m ∈ N. The conjecture was disproved by Dvořák et al.[115] by
producing a 4-choosable graph which is not (8:2)-choosable.

13.4     Fractional coloring
    The fractional coloring of a graph G was introduced by Hilton et al.[3] in the name multicoloring.
It is a variant of (a : b)-choosability.

   Let L = {1, 2, . . . , a} be the set of colors available for G. If there exists a b-list assignment L′ on
G in such a way that
   (a) L′ (v) ⊆ L(v), for all v ∈ V (G)
   (b) L′ (x) ∩ L′ (y) = ϕ whenever xy ∈ E(G)
then we say that G has a fractional (a : b)-coloring. The fractional chromatic number of G, denoted
by χf (G), is the infimum of the fractions ab .

   It can be observed that the fractional (a, b)-coloring on a graph is merely (a : b)-choosability
with the additional condition that the a-list assignment is identical for all vertices.


14     Conclusion
   Through the survey, we have observed how list-coloring has evolved over the years and how it
has developed for different classes of graphs and different operations on graphs.

    First we considered the embedded graphs, we restricted our analysis to planar graphs and toroidal
graphs. All planar graphs are 5-choosable and all toroidal graphs are 7-choosable. Any further
tightening of the bound for list-coloring is influenced by forbidding few structures like cycles and
intersecting or adjacent cycles. When there are conditions under which both planar and toroidal
graphs behave alike while list-coloring, there are even more factors like girth and distance between
triangles that play a crucial role for planar graphs. We have seen that the list-coloring of planar
graphs have witnessed some good constructions of counterexamples - two different 4-colorable non-4-
choosable planar graphs, a 3-colorable non-4-choosable planar graph, non-3-choosable planar graphs
without C4 , C5 and intersecting triangles and two different non-3-choosable planar graphs with girth
4.

    List-coloring of complete bipartite and multipartite graphs have been studied in the literature.
The list chromatic number is found for Kp,q for p = 1, 2, 3, 4 and q ≥ p. Whereas χℓ (Kp,q ) is not
known for a certain range of q for p = 5, 6, 7, it is unknown for p > 7 and p ≤ q ≤ (p−1)p−1 −(p−2)p−1
except that they are p-choosable. Even though the chromatic-choosable complete multipartite graphs
have not been characterized, the Ohba conjecture, being proved true, implies that if the total number
of vertices is at most one more than its list-coloring. Also the list chromatic number of a few other
complete multipartite graphs are shown.




                                                    17
    Claw-free graphs, another important class, have also gained importance in the problem of list-
coloring. There have been few results on the list-coloring of claw-free graphs with small ∆ and
claw-free perfect graphs with small clique number. As the coloring of line graph of a graph can be
equalized by edge coloring the graph itself, we had to deviate into list edge coloring and the results
on it.

   The list-coloring of powers of graphs has emerged as an important problem in chromatic-choosability.
Considerable progress has been made for squares of planar graphs, especially for subcubic planar
graphs. One recurring theme is that, under suitable restrictions on girth and maximum degree,
the list chromatic number of the square of a planar graph becomes more tractable and, in many
cases, admits sharp or near-sharp bounds. Although maximum degree is fixed for subcubic planar
graph, girth still plays a role. Among the basic graph classes, paths and cycles remain the principal
examples whose powers are known to be chromatic-choosable for every power.

    Finding the coloring parameter for different graph operations in terms of the parameters of the
component graphs is one classic challenge. Ohba introduced the condition under which the join of
two graphs becomes chromatic-choosable. Analyzing the list-coloring problem for the product, the
significant progress is limited to the Cartesian product and the Lexicographic product. When the
bound for the list chromatic number of the Cartesian product of k graphs is found, for the Lexico-
graphic product, the bound is only for the product of two graphs of same order.

    The results surveyed here show that chromatic choosability is governed by a diverse range of
structural and methodological phenomena. Degeneracy and Hall-type arguments provide some of
the earliest tools, while the Alon–Tarsi method, kernel-perfect orientations, boundary-list induction,
and discharging play central roles in more refined results. Several major conjectures, such as the List
Square Coloring Conjecture and its planar version, have been disproved by explicit counterexamples,
while others remain open or are known only under additional structural assumptions. The remaining
problems suggest several promising directions, including claw-free graphs, line graphs, graph powers,
complete multipartite graphs, and the behavior of list coloring under graph operations.
