Remark 1.10. Since gM (t) is covaluative [47], it follows that N◦1 (M ) := (−1)c(M ) N1 (M ) ∈
Z is a valuative matroid invariant. It can take negative values, e.g. N◦1 (V8 ) = −4, but our
results above show that for all graphic or cographic matroids M , its value

                                         N◦1 (M ) = c(M ) − 1 ≥ 0

is non-negative. The study of similar valuative constraints on subclasses of matroids was
proposed in [20], where a polytope of all matroids is defined. The hyperplane {N◦1 = 0}
cuts this polytope such that all (co)graphic matroids lie on the side N◦1 ≥ 0. On the
other side (N◦1 < 0), we find, for example, all non-(co)graphic uniform matroids Unr with
2 ≤ r ≤ n − 2. For indeed, we saw in Example 1.7 that then

                         N◦1 (Unr ) = gU′ nr (−1) − 1 = −(r − 1)(n − r − 1) < 0.

Remark 1.11. Following [20, §7], we ask: Which matroids minimize or maximize N◦1 (M )
at a given rank and size? We did not study this question closely, but neither (co)graphic
nor uniform matroids are candidates: [19, Table 5.1] has an example with N◦1 (M ) = 20 at
rank 5 (larger than the (co)graphic matroids), and calculations for projective geometries
indicate that the negative values for uniform matroids are not minimal.

Conjecture 1.13. If a 3-connected graph G is planar, then N2 (G) = 1.
  This condition can detect non-planarity of some graphs, for example one computes
that N2 (K5 ) = N2 (K3,3 ) = 0 for the Kuratowski graphs K5 and K3,3 . From (1.6) we see
that the invariant N◦2 (M ) := (−1)c(M )−1 N2 (M ) satisfies N◦2 (G) ≥ 0 for all planar graphs
(not necessarily 3-connected). Similarly to Remark 1.10, we may thus view N◦2 (M ) ≥ 0
as a valuative constraint that is necessary for a matroid M to be (graphic and) planar.
Remark 1.14. With N2 (G) becoming fixed, it might be interesting to study and interpret
the next invariant N3 (G) especially for planar graphs.
Conjecture 1.15. Let G1 and G2 denote two 3-connected graphs, each having a triangle
Ti ⊂ Gi , and let G1 ⊕3 G2 denote a corresponding 3-sum. Let pi = |π0 (Gi \ V (Ti ))| be
the number of connected components left over when the triangle vertices are deleted. Then

                            N2 (G1 ⊕3 G2 ) = N2 (G1 ) + N2 (G2 ) − p1 · p2 .

  A 3-sum [37, §9.3] of two graphs Gi along triangles Ti ⊂ Gi (see fig. 2) is a graph G
obtained from gluing G1 to G2 by identifying T1 with T2 and then deleting the triangle
edges.3 In a 3-sum, the identified vertices S ⊂ V (G) form a 3-vertex cut, such that the
two parts of the edge partition E(G) = A1 ⊔ A2 into Ai = E(Gi ) \ E(Ti ) meet only in
S. Conversely, given any bipartition E(G) = A1 ⊔ A2 such that both parts meet only in
a 3-vertex cut S, we can realize G = G1 ⊕3 G2 as    a 3-sum of the graphs Gi = Ai ⊔ T

Example 1.16. Every 3-valent vertex v in a graph G gives rise to a 3-sum representation
G = K4 ⊕3 Gv , where Gv = (G \ {v}) ⊔ T is the star-triangle (or Y –∆) transform of G.
Let S denote the 3 neighbours of v. Since N2 (K4 ) = 1, Conjecture 1.15 specializes to

   Taken together, the series-parallel invariance, the identity N2 (G) = 1 for planar graphs,
the 3-sum (e.g. star-triangle) reduction, and the twist identity, constitute a stringent set
of constraints that determines a huge number of relations between the invariants N2 (G)
of different graphs. We hope that its intriguing properties will stimulate further research
and lead to a better understanding of N2 (G) and its role in graph theory.

3. Speyer’s invariant from Ferroni’s algorithm
We use a combinatorial characterization of the polynomial gM (t), which was proposed
in [47] and confirmed in [21, Theorem 9.15] and [19, Definition 1.2]. Namely, Speyer’s
polynomial is the unique covaluative matroid invariant M 7→ gM (t) ∈ [t] such that:     Z
  1. If M has a loop or coloop, then gM (t) = 0.

  2. If M is series-parallel, then gM (t) = t.

  3. If M = M1 ⊕ M2 is a direct sum, then gM (t) = gM1 (t) · gM2 (t).



                                                        18
A loop of M is an edge (element of the ground set) of rank zero; in a graphic matroid
this is an edge with both endpoints at the same vertex (“self-loop”). A coloop is an edge
e with rk(M \ e) = rk(M ) − 1, i.e. an edge contained in every basis—also called a bridge
in a graphic matroid. We call a matroid series-parallel if it can be obtained from the
2-cycle M ∼ = U21 by a sequence of series extensions M 7→ M ⊕2 U32 (subdivide an edge)
and parallel extensions M 7→ M ⊕2 U31 (add an edge parallel to an existing edge).
  The covaluative property states that gM can be computed from any decomposition of
the matroid polytope of M into other matroid polytopes; see [19, §2.3]. The algorithm
for computing gM elaborated in [19] exploits this in two steps:
   1. Decompose M into Schubert matroids, following [29] using the lattice of cyclic flats.
   2. Decompose Schubert matroids into direct sums of series-parallel matroids, using a
      description in terms of lattice paths.
In the sequel, we discuss both of these steps, in order to develop formulas that are explicit
and efficient to evaluate. We apply these to prove Proposition 1.5 from the introduction,
and in Section 3.4 we present our algorithm for efficient computation of gM (t).

3.1. Cyclic flats, chains, and the Möbius function
A subset A ⊆ M of a matroid is called a flat if rk(A ∪ {e}) = rk(A) + 1 for every edge
e∈/ A. Dually, A is called cyclic if rk(A \ {e}) = rk(A) for every e ∈ A. Any given A
contains a unique largest cyclic subset cyc(A), and any given A is contained in a unique
smallest flat cl(A). In the case of a graphic matroid, a subset A ⊆ E(G) is cyclic if each
e ∈ A is contained in a cycle that is contained in A, and A is a flat if all edges e ∈     /A
have their endpoints in two different connected components of A (that is, each connected
component of A is an induced subgraph of G). The operation A 7→ cyc(A) removes all
bridges from A, and the closure A 7→ cl(A) adds all edges e ∈ A whose endpoints are in
the same connected component of A.
  The lattice Z of cyclic flats as studied in [5] consists of all A ⊆ M that are simultaneously
cyclic and flat. Cyclic flats form a poset under the inclusion relation A ≤ B ⇔ A ⊆ B,
and in fact they form a lattice: every pair A, B ∈ Z has a join (least upper bound)
A ∨ B ∈ Z and a meet (greatest lower bound) A ∧ B ∈ Z. Concretely, these are

                     A ∨ B = cl(A ∪ B)      and    A ∧ B = cyc(A ∩ B).

The minimum 0Z = cl(∅) of Z is the set of all loops and the maximum 1Z = cyc(M ) is
the complement of the coloops. Since gM (t) is only non-zero for matroids without loops
or coloops, in fact we will have 0Z = ∅ and 1Z = M . Figure 6 shows the Hasse diagrams
of the lattices of cyclic flats of several graphs.

3.2. Lattice paths
To compute the Speyer polynomial of a Schubert matroid, [19] exploits a representation
of Schubert matroids in terms of lattice paths. We review this description below, and
then suggest an efficient method (Algorithm 1) to compute Speyer’s polynomial for any
Schubert matroid.
   A word P ∈ {N, E}× in two letters N (north) and E (east) encodes a lattice path
starting at the origin (0, 0) with steps N = (0, 1) and E = (1, 0). If P has r letters N and
n letters in total, then this path ends at (n − r, r). All such paths are determined by the
positions I = {i1 < . . . < ir } of the letters N, such that P = P (I) where
                       P (I) := Ei1 −1 NEi2 −i1 −1 N . . . Eir −ir−1 −1 NEn−ir .
This gives a bijection I 7→ P (I) from subsets I of {1, . . . , n} of size r, to lattice paths
of length n with r letters N. Given another such subset J = {j1 < . . . < jr }, we write
P (J) ≤ P (I) if and only if jk ≥ ik for all 1 ≤ k ≤ r. Visually, this condition dictates that
the path P (J) stays on or below the path P (I), but never protrudes above it; see fig. 8.
Definition 3.8. The lattice path matroid LM (P ) for P = P (I) ∈ {N, E}n with r letters
N is the matroid of rank r on the ground set {1, . . . , n} such that J = {j1 , . . . , jr } is a
basis of LM (P ) if and only if P (J) ≤ P (I).
Example 3.9. The path P = Nr En−r = P (I) for thus subset I = {1, . . . , r} bounds
(from above) the rectangle [0, n − r] × [0, r]. Therefore, P (J) ≤ P (I) is true for every
subset J of {1, . . . , n} with size r. Therefore, LM (Nr En−r ) ∼
                                                                 = Unr is the uniform matroid.

consecutive letters, any lattice path P can be written uniquely in the form

4.3. Planarity
Our data set of small graphs (table 6) contains 208 151 graphs that are planar and
3-connected. They all have N2 (G) = 1, supporting Conjecture 1.13. Further evidence
comes from Conjecture 1.15 (about the 3-sum), because already its special case (1.7) (the
star-triangle identity) would imply Conjecture 1.13. This can be seen as follows:
   The proof of Steinitz’s theorem given in [27, §13.1] shows that every 3-connected
planar simple graph G0 can be reduced to Gn = K4 by a suitable sequence of moves
G0 7→ G1 7→ G2 7→ . . . where each move consists of either a star-triangle operation
followed by simplification of any parallel edges thus created (if any), or of a triangle-star



                                             39
operation followed by series reduction of any 2-valent vertices thus created (if any). The
construction ensures that each Gi along the sequence is itself a 3-connected, planar, simple
graph. Since N2 is invariant under the series and parallel simplifications, N2 (G0 ) = 1 thus
follows inductively from N2 (K4 ) = 1 and the star-triangle identities N2 (Gi ) = N2 (Gi+1 ).
The latter are implied by (1.7) because for any 3-vertex cut S of Gi we must have
|π0 (Gi \ S)| = 2. For if Gi \ S = C1 ⊔ . . . ⊔ Cr would have r ≥ 3 connected components,
then contracting each of these components to a single vertex produces a complete bipartite
graph K3,r is a minor of Gi —which would contradict planarity.8
   The next invariant N3 (G) is not trivial for 3-connected planar graphs. For the 208 151
such graphs in our data set, it takes only few values: N3 (G) ∈ {−4, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2}.

Conjecture 4.10. If G is planar and 4-connected, then N3 (G) = 0.

   The support for this conjecture from our data set of small graphs (Table 6) alone
is weak, because this set contains merely 26 planar graphs that are 4-connected. To
seriously probe Conjecture 4.10, we therefore used the program plantri [7] to generate
all 4-connected planar graphs with 13 vertices or less. There are 21 078 such graphs, and
for all of them, we found N3 (G) = 0.

4.4. Vertex cuts
For each of the small graphs (table 6), we computed all 3-sum decompositions and all
twists. In each case, Conjecture 1.15 and Conjecture 1.18 are satisfied. Let us note two
consequences of these conjectures:

 (1) Let G be a 3-connected graph and S a 3-vertex cut of G. Then N2 (G) = N2 (G \ H)
     where H ⊂ E(G) denotes all edges of G with both endpoints in S.

 (2) Let G be a 3-connected graph and S a minimal 4-vertex cut of G. Suppose that
     G has an edge e = {v1 , v3 } with both endpoints in S = {v1 , v2 , v3 , v4 }, and set
     e′ = {v2 , v4 }. Then N2 (G) = N2 ((G \ {e}) ⊔ {e′ }). In particular, if e′ was already
     present in G, then N2 (G) = N2 (G \ {e}).

because adding H amounts to adding edges in parallel to the edges of the gluing triangle
(recall that N2 is invariant under parallel operations). For example, this explains why

4.8. Connection to Feynman integrals
In perturbative quantum field theory, scattering amplitudes of particles are computed as

B.2. Implementation details
Our implementation is tailored towards graphs, but the code can be extended to other
matroids by replacing/extending the function CyclicFlats. Repeated calculations of
the same quantity are avoided by storing intermediate results in tables for subsequent
                                        <k
lookup from memory. Thus storing the gA    (t) in memory for all cyclic flats A ∈ ZM , the
primary driver of the runtime of Algorithm 2 are:

  (I) computing the lattice Z, i.e. its elements and covering relations,

 (II) computing the Möbius function µZ (A, B) for all pairs A < B,

(III) computing for each B and k the sum over A < B and k ′ .

A direct implementation of Algorithm 2 would use O( {(A, B) ∈ ZM      2 : A ≤ B} · rk(M )2 )

polynomial additions and multiplications for (III), but the sum over k ′ does not depend
on the rank of A. Hence we first sum µZ (A, B) · gRecursive(A, k ′ ) over all A, grouped
by the pairs (k ′ , ℓ(A)). Then for each B, there are at most rk(B) values of k ′ and ℓ(B)
values of ℓ(A) to consider. Due to this implementation, we reduced the complexity for
(III) to O( {(A, B) ∈ ZM   2 : A ≤ B} · rk(M ) + |Z | · rk(M ) · ℓ(M )).
                                                   M
   Asymptotically, the most time consuming parts of our implementation are therefore
steps (I) and (II). We implemented (II) by computing once, for each pair A < B, the
defininig recursion µ(A, B) = − A≤C<B µ(A, C). Hence we can estimate the time for
                                    P

step (II) by the number

                                           3
                             {(A, C, B) ∈ ZM : A < C < B}

of 2-simplices in the order complex of Z. Asymptotic improvements would thus require
a faster method for Möbius inversion. However, the integer additions for µ are much
quicker than the calculations with polynomials gRecursive(A,k ′ ) in step (III), such that
in practice we often find that the time spent on steps (II) and (III) is comparable.
   For very large graphs, also step (I) takes significant time, because our implementation
effectively enumerates the lattice of all flats (and then filters out the cyclic ones), which
can be exponentially larger than the lattice of only cyclic flats. In comparison, however,
for the largest graphs we calculated, the runtime is dominated by step (II).
