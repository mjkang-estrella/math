                                             Abstract. We exploit the resemblance between a theorem of Hassler Whitney
                                          and the construction of Thompson’s group F to take an algebraic approach to the
                                          problem of finding region 4-colorings of the class W of planar maps described by
                                          the conclusion of Whitney’s theorem. The class W is the set of connected, cubic
                                          maps whose dual has a Hamiltonian circuit. We find that expressions of elements
                                          of F as words in a certain generating set for F correspond to attempts to color
                                          the planar maps in W, and we develop an algorithmic criterion for the success of
                                          such at attempt. We find, for the maps in W, that an extension of F seems to
                                          parametrize all the region 4-colorings that use all four colors.

                                  1. Introduction
    This paper combines algebraic structures (that of a specific finitely presented
group) with the problem of coloring planar maps. This paper can be viewed either
as a search for a shorter proof of the Four Color Theorem or as an exploration of the
colorings that must exist given the truth of the theorem. We present a mechanism
that seems to find all colorings. We describe the mechanism, present some of its
properties, and discuss questions of enumeration that it leads to.
    In this introduction, Section 1.1 gives enough background to tie the items in the
title of the paper together, Section 1.2 paraphrases our main results, and Section
1.3 lists the topics in the order that they are developed in the paper.

1.1. Background. The classical Four Color Theorem (4CT) states that any planar
map can be region 4-colored. A typical reduction says that the 4CT holds if it holds
for every cubic map (a map whose underlying graph is cubic) whose dual graph has
no loops or parallel edges. These reductions often include arguments based on
shrinking edges. See for example Section 3-2 of [8].
    Loosely stated, another approach starts with cubic maps and reduces by cutting
along certain circles. If a finiteness argument forces the cutting to stop, then
the remaining pieces are called irreducible. A standard way to do this arrives at
irreducibles that are cubic and whose dual graphs have no loops, no parallel edges
and no circuits of length three that fail to bound a complementary region of the
dual graph (no false triangles). It is a standard argument that the 4CT follows if
it holds for all irreducibles. See, for example, the arguments in Theorem 5-2 of [8].
    The benefits of dealing with such irreducibles is that they satisfy the hypotheses
of a theorem of Whitney [14] which guarantees a Hamiltonian circuit in the dual.
A consequence of this is that the underlying graph of every irreducible is built from
two binary trees that are glued together along their leaves. This has been used by
Whitney and others to reformulate the 4CT into equivalent statements that sound
nothing like the 4CT. See Section 6.4.
    This paper starts from the conclusion of Whitney’s theorem. We deal with the
class W of maps whose underlying graphs are unions of two binary trees glued
together along their leaves. The main topic of this paper is a mechanism that finds
region 4-colorings of maps in W. The mechanism seems to find every region 4-
coloring for maps in W that are “true” 4-colorings in that they use all 4 colors. The
special situation of region 3-colorings is already well understood, and its relationship
to the mechanism will be separately discussed.
    The mechanism cooperates well with a reduction process that is looser than
the reduction to irreducibles, and following Loday in [4], we call the looser pieces
primes. It is easy to build examples of maps in W that are not irreducible, and
we will partly reduce such maps. Loops in the dual are already impossible, we do
not worry about the presence of false triangles in the dual, and we cut to eliminate
parallel edges in the dual. Our primes will be the result. The importance of primes
is that when a single application of our mechanism succeeds in finding a coloring for
a prime, it finds exactly one coloring for that prime. Other colorings of that prime
will arise from separate applications of the mechanism. This was partly discovered
by a fair amount of (computer assisted) counting.
    Colorings of non-primes are easily determined from colorings of primes, and it
would be expected that a single application of the mechanism would not lead to
                            COLORING PLANAR GRAPHS                                 3


single colorings of non-primes. There are situations where this does not hold (the
mechanism seems unaware of the limited role it is supposed to play) and these
situations are only partly understood.
   This paper was motivated by the resemblance of the conclusions of Whitney’s
theorem to a group known as Thompson’s group F . The elements of F are built
from pairs of binary trees that can be glued together along their leaves to create a
cubic, planar map. The group F has been studied extensively since its introduction
by R. J. Thompson around 1970.
   The obvious resemblance was first noticed on the appearance in 1998 of the
expository article [13] by Robin Thomas. It was felt at the time that the proof
of the 4CT, based as it was on hundreds of cases that needed to be checked by
computer, could benefit from a little extra organization, and that this organization
might just be supplied by the group structure of F . Unfortunately, first looks by
several people found that the multiplication on the group and the colorings of the
maps seemed to have absolutely nothing to do with one another.
   In June of 2011, the authors of the current paper decided to take another look and
found (with the help of the computer) that while the multiplication and colorings
did not cooperate, there is a strong relationship between a presentation of F by
generators and relations on the one hand, and machinery that attempts to create
4-colorings of maps in W on the other hand.
   Binary trees are parametrized by polyhedra known as the associahedra in that
the vertices of the associahedron Ad of dimension d are trees with d + 3 leaves (or
d + 2 leaves depending on how you are counting). Thus each element of F can be
represented by a pair of vertices on some Ad . There is a generating set for F with
the property that if an element of F is represented by the pair (T, S) of vertices
on some Ad , then each path from T to S along the edges of Ad corresponds to an
expression of the element as a product of the generators. A single application of
our machinery consists of dragging the possible colorings of the tree T along a path
to see if any survive the journey as far as S.

1.2. Main results. We can summarize our main observations.
   (1) It is possible to define “having a coloring” for elements of F independent of
the machinery, and it is a trivial consequence of Whitney’s theorem [14] that the
4CT is equivalent to the statement that every element of F has a coloring.
   (2) The machinery does not discover region 3-colorings. The distinction between
region 3-colorings and “true” region 4-colorings (those colorings that use all four
colors) is sharp and region 3-colorings are confined to an easily described subgroup
of F denoted F4 corresponding to the well known characterization of the region
3-colorable cubic maps as those whose regions all have an even number of edges
in their boundaries. This is discussed in Section 10. Note that every map with 4
or more regions that has a region 3-coloring also has a “true” region 4-coloring, so
avoiding region 3-colorings is not a problem.
   (3) Not every edge path succeeds in finding a coloring. While the truth of the
4CT implies that every element of F has a coloring, it does not follow that every
element of F can be colored by some edge path. Computer evidence (maps with
up to 12 regions have been checked) is that every element can, and we conjecture
that this is so.
   (4) Deciding which edge paths are successful in finding a region 4-coloring is
algorithmic and comes down to a question of whether a certain signed graph is
                             COLORING PLANAR GRAPHS                                  4


balanced. Components of this graph correspond to splittings of the map being
colored along certain circuits in the dual graph. Maps that cannot be cut non-
trivially in this way are called “prime” by Loday in [4], and a signed graph for a
prime map completely determines a coloring for the map. Colorings for “compound”
maps are combinations of colorings of the “prime factors.”
   (5) Some maps have many “true” region 4-colorings and the truth of the 4CT
also does not imply that every coloring can be discovered by an edge path. Again
computer evidence is that this happens, and we conjecture that this is so.
   (6) From the previous item, we have that different edge paths for a given element
of F can produce different colorings. It appears that a smaller set of relations than
the ones that define F capture the classes of edge paths that lead to similar colorings
and distinguish those that lead to different colorings. The smaller set of relations
defines an extension of F that we call E. Some elements of E (the unsuccessful)
lead to no coloring. This corresponds to the existence of unsuccessful edge paths.
With less computer evidence than the previous two items, we conjecture that the
successful elements of E parametrize, in a one-to-one and onto fashion, the “true”
region 4-colorings of maps built from pairs of trees.
   (7) Our use of the computer let us also count things. Certain patterns emerged
and we verified some and have questions about others. One of our questions has
since been answered. See the note after Question 20.8.

1.3. In the paper. We describe the flow of the paper and add a bit of detail to
the above list.
   Section 2 gives a brief description of the setting of the paper. One class of
maps that we consider is discussed and the breaking of maps into simpler pieces is
reviewed.
   Section 3 covers the immediate consequences of Whitney’s theorem and brings
in the pairs of trees. We also describe the dual view which replaces pairs of trees
by pairs of triangulated polygons. The dual view has its advantages and will be
used in one or two places.
   Trees are the central objects of the paper and all definitions of trees and related
structures are given in Section 4. The machinery needed to define the faces of the
associahedra are introduced and one use is made of the dual view mentioned above.
   The associahedra are introduced in Section 5. Edges of the associahedra are
important to us and they are studied in detail. A move along an edge can be
thought of as a modification of the tree at one end of the edge to make it look like
the tree at the other end. This is called a rotation (by many) and is defined in this
section.
   Color (not mentioned in the sections before) is added in Section 6. Since we
view maps as made of pairs of trees, there is extensive discussion of colorings of
trees and tree pairs. The easy consequence of Whitney’s theorem that the 4CT is
equivalent to the colorability of pairs of trees is pointed out. The orientation of
colors used around a single vertex (called its sign) is introduced and discussed.
   Section 7 combines color with rotations. The use of paths along edges to find
colorings is introduced and we state the first of our conjectures: that every pair of
vertices in an associahedron has an edge path between them that succeeds in carry-
ing a coloring from the first vertex to the second. This conjecture implies the 4CT
and might or might not be stronger depending on the status of a conjecture given
in Section 10. We also introduce the notion of a color graph on an associahedron.
                             COLORING PLANAR GRAPHS                                  5


Such a graph has as vertices all trees colored by a single “vector” of colors and the
edges are all the rotations between the vertices that stay within the colorings given
by the vector. The conjecture in Section 7 might be thought of as implying that
the color graphs are connected. Sometimes they are not and this will be covered
shortly when we describe Section 10 below.
   Thompson’s group F and its extension E are described in Section 8. Algebra is
combined with rotations to represent elements of F as edge paths in an associahe-
dron.
   Section 9 goes as far as it can combining color with the multiplication on Thomp-
son’s group. The equivalence of the 4CT to the colorability of tree pairs is given
the immediate translation into the equivalence of the 4CT to the colorability of
elements of F . Since F is finitely generated, this can be reformulated as an equiv-
alence, Theorem 9.2, that sounds simpler but that adds little true knowledge. A
compatibility lemma is given that finds cooperation between the multiplication and
colorings in certain situations and is exploited to show that all elements in a certain
submonoid of F have colorings and that some of them have unique colorings. These
results have other proofs.
   Section 10 covers thoroughly the uncooperative region 3-colorings. The thor-
oughness is for two reasons. The first is that it is a surprisingly pretty topic. The
second is that the thorough analysis lets us package the situation nicely enough
to supply convenient wording for avoiding it in statements. We use this conve-
nient wording to state a refinement of our first conjecture. As mentioned above,
the first conjecture hints that color graphs might be connected. Region 3-colorings
correspond to color graphs with no edges. Our refined conjecture states that color
graphs are either connected or contain no edges. The truth of this conjecture would
imply that our conjecture of Section 7 is equivalent to the 4CT.
   Section 11 further considers color graphs and also looks at their complements.
We show that an easy sounding conjecture about the complement is not so easy,
and we show that color graphs (which live in the graph of edges of associahedra)
can be more contorted in appearance than the full edge graph of the associahedron
that they live in.
   Section 12 develops the criterion that detects the ability of an edge path to
successfully drag a coloring from one tree to another. The test for success builds,
from the edge path, a signed graph which will be balanced if and only the edge
path gives a coloring. Prime maps have connected signed graphs and colorings of
non-prime graphs are combinations of colorings of prime graphs. Surprisingly, the
combination of a true region 4-coloring on one prime factor with a region 3-coloring
on another prime factor seems to result in a coloring that can still be created by
an edge path in spite of the fact that the region 3-coloring could not have been
so created on its own. When this happens, the signed graph constructed from the
path has components that correspond to more than one prime factor. Restatements
of earlier conjectures are given and the discussion is given that brings the group E
into the picture. Conjectures about the relevance of the group E are given.
   The remaining sections cover material that is not part of the main flow of the
paper.
   Section 13 separates those color arrangements that color no trees from those that
color some trees by characterizing the latter. (Except for miniscule trees, there are
no arrangements that color all trees.)

   In the discussion about region 3-colorings, a pattern for coloring all trees comes
up. This does not automatically color tree pairs, since a coloring of a tree pair has a
condition to be met (colors must agree on the leaves of the two trees). The pattern
considered in Section 10 produces region 3-colorings when a match occurs. This
pattern also leads to the subgroup F4 of F that we mentioned above. Section 14
briefly looks, in some generality, at conditions that make a pattern lead to subgroups
of F . Section 15 looks at a particularly simple pattern that seems to lead to a not
so simple subgroup of F . Section 16 demonstrates the limit of usefulness of the
pattern of Section 15 to the 4CT.
   Sections 17, 18 and 19 take a brief look at standard examples of maps on the
torus and projective plane from the point of view of the current paper and relate the
examples to another of Thompson’s groups known as V . As expected, the situation
is quite different. In particular, the obvious generalization to V of the statement
that all elements of F have a coloring is false.
   Section 20 gathers all our results and questions about counts. Formulas are given
that count the colorings that are valid for at least one tree, colorings that lead to
region 3-colorings of maps, and colorings that lead to “true” region 4-colorings

more colorings than others. One map in particular (the biwheel from the dual
point of view) seemed to have approximately twice as many as any other. We
raise the question of which maps of a given size have the most colorings and have
candidates for the top four counts of colorings for a given size. Our candidate for
the top count and the fact that it corresponds precisely to the biwheel has been
since proven correct by P. D. Seymour [9]. The section ends with other questions
of enumeration motivated by other parts of the paper.

                                      2. Maps
   This section and Section 3 below set the stage for the entire paper. Certain
terms are defined carefully. However, others are to be interpreted intuitively by
the reader during these two sections and they will be defined more carefully in the
remaining sections of the paper.

2.1. Classes of maps. Planar maps are connected graphs that are embedded in
the 2-sphere. The graph is referred to as the underlying graph of the map. It will
eventually be clear why we use the 2-sphere and not the plane. The regions are the
complementary domains of the graph and a k-coloring of a map is a function from
the regions of the map to a set of k colors so that two different regions that share
an edge are not sent to (assigned) the same color.
   The 4CT says that every planar map can be 4-colored. There are many re-
ductions of this statement to similar or related statements about classes of maps
smaller than “all planar maps.” See for example Section 3-2 of [8].
   We will partly restrict ourselves to a certain class M of maps. We say partly
because once we make the transition to maps built from the group F , we will be
dealing with a larger class than M. A map is in M if its underlying graph is cubic
(every vertex has valence 3), is connected, and if it has additional properties best
                            COLORING PLANAR GRAPHS                                  7


expressed in terms of its dual graph. The dual graph will also be viewed as also
embedded in the 2-sphere and have its own associated regions. The dual graph
shall be assumed to have
    (1) no loops and no parallel edges (the graph is simple), and
    (2) no circuit consisting of three edges unless that circuit is the boundary of a
         region of the dual graph.
   It is standard that the 4CT follows if all maps in M have a 4-coloring. The
arguments for this reduction are given in the proof of Theorem 5-2 of [8]. We
review them briefly to introduce terminology.

2.2. Map building blocks. We assume that our maps are cubic. The arguments
that we can do this are given in Section 3-2 of [8]. We always keep the requirement
that the dual graph of a cubic map have no loops, for otherwise a region shares an
edge with itself. Were this to happen, a decision must be made as to exactly what
“two regions meeting along an edge” means. Assuming no loops in the dual graph
avoids having to make this decision.
   We consider the failure of the rest of (1) and (2) above.
   If the dual graph has parallel edges, they do not bound a region since all regions
bounded by the dual graph have three edges. It follows that the circuit created
by two parallel edges cuts the map into two parts each of which has at least two
vertices. If each of these two parts has a region 4-coloring (regarding the cutting
circle as shrunk to a point), then the colorings can be glued together by permuting
the colors of one part to create a match where the gluing takes place. Since the
gluing has to match only two colors, there are two permutations that will work.
   If the dual graph violates (2) above, then there is a circuit of length three that
cuts the original map into two parts, each of which contains at least two vertices.
If each of the parts has a coloring, then once again, a permutation of the colors of
one part will allow the colorings to be glued together. However, now three colors
have to match and there is only one permutation that will work.
   After Loday in [4], we call cubic maps whose dual graphs have no parallel edges
prime. From the arguments we have given, if a map has k prime factors and the
number of colorings of the i-th factor is ni , then the number of colorings of the map
is the product of the ni times 2k−1 .
    We do not have a similar discussion for maps that satisfy (2) above. The main
reason is that (2) is there mostly to obtain Whitney’s theorem which is more of a
motivating factor than a key ingredient. This is discussed in Section 3 below. A
secondary reason is that cutting along curves that arise from a violation of (2) does
not correspond to any of the structures developed in the rest of the paper.

                3. Whitney’s theorem and its consequences
3.1. The equator. The main theorem of Whitney’s paper [14] says that if M is a
map in M, then the dual of the underlying graph of M has a Hamiltonian circuit.
This fact is the main motivation for this paper. A restatement of Whitney’s theorem
is given as Theorem 4-7 in [8].
   An immediate consequence of Whitney’s theorem is that given an M ∈ M there
is a (non-unique) simple closed curve E in S 2 that is transverse to the edges of M ,
that intersects each region of M in exactly a single, non-degenerate arc, and that
does not pass through any vertices of M .
                             COLORING PLANAR GRAPHS                                   8


   We regard S 2 as the set {(x, y, z) | x2 + y 2 + z 2 = 1} in R3 . We let the northern
hemisphere in S 2 be all the points in S 2 with z ≥ 0, we let the southern hemisphere
be the points in S 2 with z ≤ 0, and we refer to the set points in S 2 with z = 0 as
the equator. We denote by W the set of cubic maps M in S 2 in so that

    (1) the equator is transverse to M , contains no vertex of M , and intersects
        each region of M in a single non-degenerate arc,
    (2) some point of the intersection of M with the equator is the point (0, 1, 0),
        and
    (3) the intersection of each of the northern and southern hemisphere of S 2 with
        the graph of M is a connected tree.



3.2. The trees. Given any M ∈ M with simple closed curve E as guaranteed by
Whitney’s theorem, we can compose the embedding of M with a self homeomor-
phism of the 2-sphere so that the new embedding satisfies (1) and (2) above. To
see that it also satisfies (3), we consider an intersection of M with one of the hemi-
spheres and note that if the intersection is not connected, then some region hits
the equator twice, and if the intersection is not simply connected, then some region
does not hit the equator at all. Thus every M ∈ M leads to a map in W. From
now on we work with W.
   For an M ∈ W, we will usually use D to denote the intersection of M with
the northern hemisphere, and R to denote the intersection of M with the southern
hemisphere. The reason for these letters will be clarified in Section 8.1.



3.3. Rooting and orienting the trees. Given M ∈ W with D and R as above,
we declare that the common root of D and R is the point (0, 1, 0). The leaves,
which are common to both D and R, are all other points on the equator. Note that
in each of D and R, the root has degree 1 and the root and leaves together account
for all the vertices of degree 1.
   We then embed each of D and R (separately) in the xy-plane by vertical projec-
tion. If the fact that the two images intersect is bothersome, then D can be moved
two units in the negative x direction, and R can be moved two units in the positive
x direction. The purpose of the projection is to discuss orientation in a well defined
way.
   With D and R projected to the xy-plane, we associate to each vertex v of degree
3 a cyclic order on the edges that impinge on v by using the counterclockwise order
on the edges as viewed from above the plane. We also get a linear order on the
leaves. The order is obtained from a counterclockwise walk (as viewed from above)
that starts and ends at the root around the circle of radius 1 that contains the root
and leaves.
   Since the vertices that are neither root nor leaf are all of degree 3, we can call
each of D and R a binary tree. Thus we have a process that takes each M ∈ W
to a pair (D, R) of finite, rooted, oriented, binary trees that have the same number
of leaves. We will later be very careful with definitions, but the reader can infer
enough definitions at this point for us to continue.

We will refer to these correspondences often.

3.4. Reversing the process. Conversely, we can take a pair (D, R) of finite,

W, but such maps will not always be in the class M.

definitions made later will fill in details.

(all leaves and the root), which becomes a linear order if the root is declared to
come first.

to the point (0, 1, 0), so that the leaves of D occupy the same points as the leaves
of R, and so that a counterclockwise walk (viewed from above) around the equator
starting at the root visits the leaves of the two trees in an order that agrees with
the order induced from the orientations.
   Note that the number of regions is equal to the number of vertices on either D
or R of degree 1.

theorem.

3.5. Triangulations. There is a dual view to the above. The equator for a map
M in W gives a Hamiltonian circuit in the dual graph ∆ of M . We focus on ∆ and
note that it is a triangulation (all faces are triangles) since M is cubic. If we use
E to denote the Hamiltonian circuit in ∆, then E breaks ∆ into two triangulated
polygons whose common boundary is E.
   The dual view works with pairs (P1 , P2 ) for which the Pi are triangulated poly-
gons with the same number of boundary edges. Each Pi is triangulated using no
vertices other than the vertices of the polygon, and there is a cyclic order preserv-
ing bijection between the vertices of the two polygons. The number of edges in the
boundary of each Pi is the number of regions of the map M .
   We make only small use of this view because we wish to introduce certain groups
which are traditionally given as pairs of trees, and because the tree view offers
certain technical efficiencies. See, for example, Section 8.5. However, the dual view
is more efficient for certain observations. For example it is obvious from the dual
view is that the dihedral group of order 2n acts on the set of maps in W with n
regions. See Section 4.8.

                                      4. Trees
   We do a lot with trees, and we use this section to define the terms and structures
that we need. Some of the oddness in our definitions is motivated by the discussion
in Section 3.

4.1. General trees. A tree is a connected graph with no circuits. A finite tree is
a tree with finitely many vertices. A locally finite tree is a tree in which the degree
of every vertex is finite.
   Every tree must have a root, and we will be very restrictive about where the
root can be. For us, the root of a tree must have degree 1. We will emphasize this
by saying that such a tree is rooted.
   Each vertex in a tree has a distance to the root defined as the length (in edges)
of the unique simple walk from the vertex to the root. If v is a vertex in a tree with
a root, then the children of v are those vertices adjacent to v that are farther from
the root than v is. The vertex adjacent to the root is the only child of the root,
and we insist that the root have a child. The leaves of a tree are the vertices with
no children. The trivial tree is the unique tree with one leaf.
   The inverse of “child” is “parent.” Every vertex except the root has a unique
parent, and the root has no parent. The transitive closure of “child” is “descendant”
and the transitive closure of “parent” is “ancestor.” The root is an ancestor of all
other vertices in a tree.
                             COLORING PLANAR GRAPHS                                  11


   In a binary tree every vertex that is neither root nor leaf has degree 3.
   If T is a tree, then the internal vertices of T are the vertices of degree greater
than 1. The internal edges are the edges whose endpoints are both internal vertices.
While not standard terminology, we need to call the edges that are not internal
edges the external edges. It will be convenient to separate the external edges into
the root edge, the edge that impinges on the root, and the leaf edges, those edges
that impinge on leaves.
   The root and the root edge are motivated by Section 3.3, and are there for
sometimes technical, sometimes formal, and sometimes practical reasons. Since the
root and root edge must always be there, it adds no information in a drawing to
include them. So the occasional drawing of a tree with no root and root edge shown
will be assumed to be “augmented” by these items.
   The root edge will be inconvenient at times. However it would be inconvenient
more often if it were omitted.


4.2. Orders in trees. A tree is locally ordered if for every vertex v there is a cyclic
order defined on the set of edges that impinge on v. The cyclic order on the edges
gives a cyclic order on the vertices adjacent to v. For any vertex v other than the
root, this leads to a linear order on the children of v by starting the linear order
with the vertex that follows the parent in the cyclic order. The children of the root
have a linear order since the number of children of the root is 1.
   If a tree T is embedded in the plane, then the embedding induces a local order
by using the cyclic order of the edges that impinge on a vertex v that comes from
counterclockwise walk (as seen from above the plane) around a small circle that
surrounds v and no other vertex and which intersects each edge impinging on v
exactly once.
   Conversely, every finite, ordered tree can be embedded in the plane so that the
local order induced from the embedding agrees with the given order.
   An embedding can be referred to as a drawing. We will draw trees with the
roots at the top. See the paragraphs at the bottom of Page 8 and top of Page 9 in
[5] for a discussion of this choice.
   An isomorphism of locally ordered trees is required to respect both root and
local order. It follows that the only automorphism of a locally ordered tree is the
identity.
   A locally ordered tree can be given the prefix total order on all the vertices. This
is the unique linear order that restricts to the local linear order on the children of
each vertex and that for two children v and w of a single vertex, if v < w, then
the descendants of v precede w which in turn precedes the descendants of w. The
restriction of this total order to the leaves gives a total order on the leaves.
   In a locally ordered binary tree, every vertex with two children has the first child
designated as the left child and the other child is the right child. A locally ordered
binary tree can also be given the infix total order. This is the unique linear order
that restricts to the local linear on the children and that places each vertex after
its left child and the descendants of the left child and before its right child and the
descendants of the right child. The restriction of the infix order to the leaves is the
same as the restriction of the prefix order to the leaves.
   The induced order on the leaves (from prefix or infix order) will usually be
referred to as the left-right order on the leaves since, with the tree drawn with the
                              COLORING PLANAR GRAPHS                                   12


root at the top, visiting the leaves moving left to right in the drawing follows the
order.
   We will often have need to refer to the i-th leaf of a finite, locally ordered tree,
and this will refer to the i-th leaf in the left-right order of the leaves, with the count
starting from 1.
4.3. Standing assumptions on trees. In the rest of this paper, all trees will
be locally finite, rooted, locally ordered, and no vertex will have degree 2. These
assumptions will not be repeated in statements.
4.4. Subtrees. A subtree S of a tree T is a subgraph that is also a tree and that
satisfies the extra condition that if v is a vertex of S other than the root of S, then
either all the children of v in T are in S, or none of the children of v in T are in S.
The root of S must have only one child.
   A local order on a tree is inherited by a subtree.
   Since we assume T has a root, there is a unique vertex in S that is closest to the
root of T and we will insist that this vertex be the root of S.
4.5. Standard model for binary trees. Binary trees can be given a very stan-
dard structure that makes them all subtrees of a common structure. This will be
convenient in many places.
   The vertex set of the infinite, locally ordered, binary tree T consists of a single
special element that we will denote ∗, together with the set {0, 1}∗ of all finite
words (including the empty word ∅) in the alphabet 0 and 1. In {0, 1}∗, we take
concatenation, prefix, suffix to have their usual meanings. Concatenation will be
denoted by juxtaposition. The left child of a vertex u in {0, 1}∗ will be u0 and
the right child of u will be u1. The only child of ∗ will be ∅. Words like parent,
ancestor, child, descendant will have their usual meanings. We order the children
of u ∈ {0, 1}∗ by u0 < u1. The edges of T are all connections from parent to child.
Declaring ∗ to be the root makes T a rooted, locally ordered, binary tree. The infix
order will be used on T at times.
   Throughout the paper we will need to discuss words. We will use notation similar
to regular expressions and write 0n for a string of n zeros, 0+ for a non-empty finite
string of zeros and 0∗ for a possibly empty finite string of zeros. Thus 1+ 0+ 1∗
represents at least one 1 followed by at least one zero followed by zero or more
ones.
   Recall that all trees are are locally ordered. It is standard that any finite, binary
tree can be realized uniquely as a finite subtree of T so that its root is ∗, so that
its vertex set is closed under the taking of prefixes and for which u0 is in T if and
only if u1 is in T . It will be convenient to view all finite, binary trees this way.
   Each v ∈ {0, 1}∗ is a vertex of T and of any tree in T that contains v. As a
word, it also contains the information on how to get to v as a walk from ∅. As such
it can be thought of as an “address.” This makes for the odd statement that the
word v is the address for the vertex v, but at times this will be convenient.
   Subtrees are defined as for subtrees of general trees. There are some special
notations for certain subtrees.
   If T is a binary tree and v is a vertex in T with parent w, then Tv will denote
the subtree {vu | vu ∈ T } ∪ {w}. The root of Tv is w and every leaf of Tv is a leaf
of T . If T is a binary tree, then T0 will be referred to as the left subtree of T and T1
will be referred to as the right subtree of T . We will be consistent with the words
                                                   COLORING PLANAR GRAPHS                                                     13


“tree” and “subtree” and insist that a finite, binary tree in T have ∗ as its root,
while subtrees can have any vertex in T as its root.
   The advantage of putting finite binary trees in T is that if T and S are binary
trees, then so are S ∩ T and S ∪ T . Recall that trees are rooted at ∗.

4.6. Inducting with trees. We will frequently appeal to either of two inductive
arguments without giving details.
    Finite trees are well founded (every set has a minimal element where vertices
of a tree are partially ordered by declaring descendants less than ancestors) and
as such can have constructions or statements about them argued by well founded
(Noetherian) induction which lets a statement or construction be applied to a vertex
if it applies to all of its descendants. We will often simply say that something can
be defined or argued inductively. For example, height can be defined inductively
by declaring the leaves of a finite tree to have height zero, and any vertex whose
descendants all have defined heights is defined to have height one greater than the
largest height of its children.
    Another argument is based on a binary operation on binary trees. Given two
binary trees, S and T , there is a unique binary tree S b T whose left subtree is
isomorphic to S and whose right subtree is isomorphic to T . Every binary tree that
has more than one leaf is uniquely of the form S b T . Since S b T has more vertices
than either of S or T , we can induct on the size of a binary tree.

4.7. Projections. Since we will study pairs of binary trees, we will be comparing
structures of trees. The following will help organize trees into related groups of
various sizes.
    If T is a finite, binary tree then a projection of T can be defined based on certain
data. Here is one place where the root edge annoys. We say that two subtrees S1
and S2 of T are edge disjoint if removing the root and root edge from each of S1
and S2 leaves them with no edge in common.
    The data for the projection consists of a finite set T1 , . . . , Tn of subtrees in T ,
which are pairwise edge disjoint. To avoid trivialities, we require that each Ti have
at least three leaves.
    We form the projection determined by T1 , . . . , Tn as follows. For each i with
1 ≤ i ≤ n, let wi be the child of the root of Ti . We remove from T all the non-root
edges of Ti and all the internal vertices of Ti except for wi . Then for each leaf of
Ti , we add an edge that connects that leaf to wi .

                                    5. The associahedra
   The associahedra are cellular complexes whose vertices are finite, binary trees.
There is one associahedron in each dimension. The vertices of the d-dimensional
associahedron are all the trees with d internal edges, d + 1 internal nodes, d + 2
leaves and pairs of such vertices (trees) create maps with d + 3 regions. Thus there
are several integer values associated to one associahedron and will require care to
keep track of. The books [10] and [5] refer to the d-dimensional associahedron by
Kd+2 so their notational parameter (subscript) is the number of leaves.
   We will denote the d-dimensional associahedron by Ad .

which is

associahedron Ad by root shifts and reflections on the individual trees. See Section
4.8. That the action preserves the edges of Ad is easy to see from the fact that

                                                               6. Color
6.1. The colors. All our region and edge colors will come from Z2 × Z2 . We use
Z2 × Z2 since addition and subtraction are identical in this group. Notations such
as (0, 0) and (0, 1) and so forth are cumbersome, and we will “code” the elements
of Z2 × Z2 by the mapping (0, 0) → 0, (0, 1) → 1, (1, 0) → 2 and (1, 1) → 3. In
other words, the 0 and 1 in each (x, y) is thought of as a binary digit. In spite of
                              COLORING PLANAR GRAPHS                                   19


this coding, the view that the colors reside in Z2 × Z2 will be retained since we
will use the arithmetic in Z2 × Z2 to add colors. Thus 0 is the identity, we have
1 + 2 = 3, 1 + 3 = 2, 2 + 3 = 1, and we have n + n = 0 for any n ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3}.

6.2. Region and edge colorings. It is standard that for planar cubic maps, a
region 4-coloring exists if and only if there is a corresponding edge 3-coloring. This
observation goes back to Tait [12]. One uses colors from a group of four elements
(such as Z2 ×Z2 ). Given a region 4-coloring, one colors the edges with the difference
of the colors of the two regions that impinge on the edge. The identity is never the
color of an edge. That the result is an edge 3-coloring (no two edges impinging on
a common vertex have the same color) is a trivial exercise. The reverse direction
depends on the planarity of the map. See Theorem 4-3 of [8]. The planarity is
essential. Figure 2-16 of [8] is a map on a torus that is edge 3-colorable (exercise)
but which cannot be region 4-colored. This is discussed in more detail in Section
17.
   Unfortunately, we do not pass over to edge 3-colorings and leave region 4-
colorings behind forever. This means we have to carry around the extra words
“edge” and “region” every time we mention a coloring. The reason for this is that
region 3-colorings, while special cases of region 4-colorings, are special enough to
require separate treatment when we get down to details. Theorem 2-5 of [8] charac-
terizes maps with region 3-colorings as those whose regions all have an even number
of edges. However, such maps also have region 4-colorings that use all 4 colors, and
these colorings will be considered as well. Later in the paper we develop a criterion
that distinguishes those edge 3-colorings coming from a region 3-coloring from those
that come from a region coloring that uses all four colors. However, it is not clear
that this is an easy criterion to apply. We do not know of any criterion that could
be described as easy.
   Now let M be in W with (D, R) the associated pair of trees. Recall the one-to-
one correspondence from the vertices of degree 1 in D to the similar set in R. Since
we are going to discuss edges, we can also think of this correspondence as between
the set of external edges in D to the set of external edges in R.
   If M has an edge 3-coloring, then the coloring restricts to an edge 3-coloring of
both D and R. The important feature of these colorings of D and R is that they
give the same color to corresponding external edges of D and R. This leads to the
next discussion.

6.3. Coloring binary trees. Let T be a finite, binary tree with n leaves. A color
vector for T is an n-tuple c = (c1 , . . . , cn ) with values in Z2 × Z2 . The vector c is
thought of as an assignment of colors to the edges impinging on the leaves of T .
The left-right order of the leaves of T is used to number the leaves from left to right
starting at 1, and the color ci is assigned to the edge impinging on the i-th leaf of
T.
   We also think of the color ci as being assigned to the i-th leaf itself as well as
the edge impinging on it. In fact, it will often be convenient to think of a color
assigned to an edge in a tree as also being assigned to the vertex at the lower end
of the edge. To be special, a color assigned to the root edge is not only thought of
as assigned to ∅ but also to ∗. We call this color the root color.
   One inductively argues the following.
                              COLORING PLANAR GRAPHS                                    20


Lemma 6.1. Given a finite, binary tree T and color vector c for T , there is a
unique coloring of the edges of T from Z2 × Z2 so that at every vertex v of degree
3 in T the sum of the colors of the edges impinging on v is zero.
   We refer to the coloring of T given in Lemma 6.1 as the coloring determined by
the color vector c.
   Note that the coloring in Lemma 6.1 is not guaranteed to be an edge 3-coloring
for several reasons. First, we did not restrict the colors in c to the non identity
elements of Z2 × Z2 . Second, even if we made such a restriction, there is nothing to
prevent the identity from showing up as the computed color of an edge not ending
at a leaf. Of course if this happens, then the colors leading to the computed identity
will also be identical.
   If the identity is used in c, and the tree has more than one leaf, then at least
one vertex will not have three different colors for the edges ending at that vertex.
A last comment is that if no edge impinging on a given vertex v of degree 3 has
color 0, then because of the arithmetic in Z2 × Z2 , the three edges impinging on v
have different colors. We have argued the following.
Lemma 6.2. If T is a finite, binary tree with at least two leaves and c is a color
vector for T , then the coloring of T determined by c will be an edge 3-coloring of
T if and only if the identity in Z2 × Z2 is not used in the coloring of T determined
by c.
  We say that a color vector for a binary tree is valid if the coloring determined
by the vector is an edge 3-coloring. There is no color vector valid for all trees. In
particular, we have the following which is only a check of small number of cases.
Lemma 6.3. There are five finite, binary trees with four leaves and there is no
color vector that is valid for all five.
   There are color vectors that are valid for no trees. We say that a color vector is
acceptable if there is a finite, binary tree for which it is valid. In Section 13 we will
prove that a color vector is acceptable if and only if the vector is not a constant
vector and does not sum to zero.
   A finite, binary tree T with n leaves gives a way to parenthesize a sum of n
variables. A leaf corresponds to a single (unparenthesized) variable. If T = A b B
where A and B have j and k leaves respectively, then j + k = n, the tree A
parenthesizes the sum of the first j variables to give an expression EA , the tree B
parenthesizes the sum of the last k variables to give an expression EB and we define
T to correspond to the expression (EA + EB ). The first sentence of the following
is argued inductively and the second follows from the associativity of the addition
in Z2 × Z2 .
Lemma 6.4. If c is a color vector for finite, binary tree T , then the root color
determined by c is simply the sum of the colors in c. In particular this color
depends only on c and not on the structure of T .
   We now turn to pairs of trees.
6.4. Coloring binary tree pairs. Recall that given a pair (D, R) of finite trees
it is always assumed that the trees have the same number of leaves.
    If (D, R) is a pair of finite, binary trees with n leaves each, and c is a color vector
for both D and R, then we say that c is valid for or is a coloring of (D, R) if it
                             COLORING PLANAR GRAPHS                                   21


is valid for both D and R. If there is a color vector valid for (D, R), we say that
(D, R) has a coloring.
    We now can get a statement that is equivalent to the 4CT. This observation is
just a variation on statements whose equivalence to the 4CT follows from Whitney’s
theorem [14]. The earliest are by Whitney [15] (with two from that paper repeated
as C19 and C20 in Section 5.5 of [8]), and others by Mycielski (C21 in [8]), Kauffman
[3], and Loday [4].
Proposition 6.5. The four color theorem is equivalent to the statement that every
pair of finite, binary trees with the same number of leaves has a coloring.
Proof. If (D, R) is the pair of trees associated to M ∈ W, then a color vector for
the pair determines the same root color for both D and R by Lemma 6.4. Thus
the edge colorings of the two trees will match when the map is reconstructed. The
other direction is immediate from the construction in Section 3.4.              
   Using arguments that are essentially in [4], we get the following. The term prime
is defined in Section 3.4.
Proposition 6.6. The four color theorem is equivalent to the statement that every
prime pair of finite, binary trees with the same number of leaves has a coloring.
Proof. If a pair is not prime, then the map created by the construction in Section
3.4 will have parallel edges in its dual. The circuits formed from these parallel
edges cut the map in two points each. Let us regard the two points on each such
circuit as associated. If we cut the map at these points, then each component can
be used to build a new map by joining each pair of associated points with an edge.
These maps break into tree pairs that are now prime. Recovering a coloring of the
original map is straightforward from colorings of the prime pairs. The coloring of
each prime pair may have to be altered by a permutation of the colors to make the
colorings fit together.                                                          
6.5. Signs. Let T be a finite, binary tree. It will be convenient to view T as drawn
in the plane in a way that agrees with the ordering. Assume that T is given an edge
3-coloring from the non-zero elements of Z2 × Z2 . Taking into account the cyclic
ordering of the edges around a vertex v of valence three and the fact that one edge
(the edge to the parent) is distinguished, there are six possible valid colorings of
the edges that meet at v. If the colors 1, 2 and 3 are assigned so that the numbers
increase by one as the intervals are visited in the cyclic order, then v is said to have
positive sign (or color). Otherwise it is said to have negative sign (or color).
   In case our definition of positive and negative was not clear, we show the positive
arrangements

6.7. Permuting colors. Two 3-colorings (of anything, a tree, an M ∈ M), that
differ but can be made the same by permuting the colors of one of the colorings are
not different in an interesting way. If a finite, binary tree T has an edge 3-coloring
and its derived sign assignment, then permuting the colors will either preserve the
sign assignment (if the permutation is even) or negate it (if the permutation is
odd). Conversely a sign assignment on T together with the negative of the sign
assignment will produce all six permutations of an edge 3-coloring of T by running
through the three starting colors for a particular edge for both the sign assignment
and its negative.
   We can try to “normalize” colorings on a finite, binary tree T by insisting that
the color of the root edge be 1, and that the sign of the unique child of the root be
positive. However, Lemma 6.8 shows that we might need to consider colorings that
violate this normalization if we are to look for color vectors that are simultaneously
valid for pairs of trees.
                                      COLORING PLANAR GRAPHS                                     23


   Thus if the coloring of a finite, binary tree assigns the color 1 to the root edge, we
say it is positive normal if the sign of the child of the root is positive and negative
normal if the sign of the child of the root is negative.
   Note that there is a one-to-one correspondence between sign assignments of a
finite, binary tree and edge 3-colorings of that tree that use the color 1 for the
root edge. The normalization and this one-to-one correspondence will allow us to
discuss sign assignments as if they are edge 3-colorings and vice versa.
   We apply this to counting colorings of pairs of finite, binary trees. Given a
coloring of a pair (D, R) of finite, binary trees, there is a unique way to permute
the colors so that the coloring of D is positive normal. Thus the number of colorings
of (D, R) modulo the action of S3 on the colors is the number of colorings in which
the coloring of D is positive normal. When we count colorings of a pair of finite,
binary trees, we will always be counting modulo the action of S3 on the colors.
Statements that colorings are unique will be made with this convention.

provisions of Lemma 6.8 that if there is a signed rotation between two trees, then
there is a color vector valid for the two trees.

7.3. Colored associahedra and color graphs. If Ad is the d-dimensional asso-
ciahedron, then its vertices are trees with d + 2 leaves. A color vector c with d + 2
entries induces a coloring on all the vertices of Ad , although for some of the trees
the induced coloring from c may have at least one zero on an edge and be invalid.
   We will call the pair (Ad , c) a colored associahedron. The subset of vertices
of Ad consisting of trees for which c is valid will be called the colored vertices of
(Ad , c). The set of colored vertices will be empty if the vector is not acceptable, so
we restrict this discussion to acceptable color vectors.
                              COLORING PLANAR GRAPHS                                    25


   The subgraph of the 1-skeleton of Ad spanned by the colored vertices will be
called the color graph of (Ad , c). Since the number of entries in c specifies the
dimension of the associahedron that it colors, it makes sense to refer to the color
graph of (Ad , c) as the color graph of c. Note that from Lemma 7.1, the edges
of the color graph of c correspond exactly to the rotations that are valid for trees
validly colored by c.
   Note that if c is valid for some pair (D, R) of trees with d + 2 leaves each, then
D and R are vertices in the color graph of c. It is natural to ask if the color graph
of every acceptable color vector is connected. The answer is no, but in a very
spectacular way. We discuss this in Section 10 and then state Conjecture 10.11
that takes this phenomenon into account.

                                        8. Groups
   We introduce two groups. One is widely known as Thompson’s group F , and
the other, while known and related to F , is less well known. Their relevance is that
one way to define their elements is as equivalence classes of pairs of binary trees.

8.1. Thompson’s group F . Thompson’s group F is a finitely presented, infi-
nite group with many interesting properties. It has many closely related faithful
representations and we will add to that list here. While coming up with a new
representation of F is almost never interesting or deep, it is often useful. We will
give what information we need to proceed and will give references where necessary.
    A standard reference for the group is [1].
    Recall that given a pair (D, R) of finite trees it is always assumed that the trees
have the same number of leaves.
    We start with one of the most common representations. The group F can be
defined as the group of self homeomorphisms of the unit interval [0, 1] that are
defined by pairs (D, R) of finite, binary trees. We will show how to do this.
    We associate to each word w in {0, 1}∗ an interval i(w) in [0, 1]. We let i(∅) =

map x 7→ x/2 and i(1w) be the image of i(w) under the map x 7→ (x + 1)/2. It will
be convenient to let m(w) be the midpoint of i(w).

inductively that i(w0) is the left half of i(w) and i(w1) is the right half of i(w).

8.2. Carets. It is convenient to call a graph looking like ⑧⑧ ❄ a caret. To make it a
formal tree, a root and root edge must be added so that it looks like ⑧⑧❄❄. We will
do so because then binary trees become unions of carets.
   In a binary tree containing vertices w, u, u0 and u1 with w the parent of u, the
four element set {w, u, u0, u1} forms a caret. We will sometimes refer to u as the
center vertex of the caret and say that the caret is centered at u. In a finite binary
tree, the number of carets is the number of internal vertices. Every finite, binary
tree is now a union of a finite number of carets if we agree that the trivial tree is a
union of zero carets.
   We can give words to the construction A b i and say that A b i is obtained from
A by “adding a caret to the i-th leaf of A.” We can describe (A, B) → (A′ , B ′ ) by
saying that we have added a caret to the i-th leaves of both A and B.

8.3. Reduced pairs. A pair (D, R) of finite, binary trees representing an element
of F is said to be reduced if there is no pair (A, B) with (A, B) → (D, R). Should
such a pair (A, B) exist, then passing from (D, R) to (A, B) is called a reduction.
   Reductions are findable. In a finite, binary tree, let us call a caret whose two
leaves are both leaves of the tree an exposed caret. If (A, B) → (D, R), then there
will be an exposed caret in D whose leaves occupy the same positions in the left-
right order of the leaves of D as the leaves of an exposed caret in R. Conversely, if
such “matching” exposed carets exist in a pair of trees (D, R), then there is a pair
(A, B) such that (A, B) → (D, R).
   Each element of F is represented by a unique reduced pair (shown following
Lemma 2.2 in [1]) and any pair will be reduced to a reduced pair in the same
                             COLORING PLANAR GRAPHS                                   27


equivalence class by any random sequence of reductions taken as far as possible
(clear).

8.5. Unions, intersections and differences of trees. We elaborate on remarks
made at the end of Section 4.5.
   If B and C are finite, binary trees in T , then it is easier to work with expressions
such as B − C and C − B if we think of B and C as unions of carets. The unions of
carets point of view makes no change when discussing B ∪ C and B ∩ C, so there
is no disadvantage to this view.
   We say that two carets are adjacent if they share an edge. If two carets are
adjacent, there will be one shared edge and it will be a root edge of one caret and
a leaf edge of the other. This allows us to talk about a component of a set of carets
as the union of a set of carets whose adjacency graph is maximally connected.

breaks into components with one component for each leaf of B ∩ C that is not a
leaf of C. If v is such a leaf, it is a leaf of B and the corresponding component of
C − B will be the subtree Cv . The subtree Cv will share its root edge with the leaf
edge of B (and of B ∩ C) that impinges on v.

8.6. Another representation. Let D = Z[ 12 ] ∩ (0, 1), the set of dyadic rationals
between 0 and 1. Each x ∈ D has a last “1” in its base 2 representation. The word
w in {0, 1}∗ in the base 2 representation of x from the binary point up to but not
including the last 1 has m(w) = x. Thus m : {0, 1}∗ → D is a bijection. If we
regard {0, 1}∗ as the non-root leaves of T , if T is given the infix order, and if D is
given the usual order in [0, 1], then m preserves order.
   Since D is dense in [0, 1] and an element h(D, R) in F preserves D, the element
h(D, R) is determined by its action on D and thus is also determined by the corre-
sponding action on the non-root vertices of T . We can extend the action to be the
identity on the root of T so that we can stop saying “non-root vertex.” Note that
these actions are not tree homomorphisms. We denote the action given by a pair
(D, R) by t(D, R).
   The “other” representation of F is as the set of permutations t(D, R) on the
vertices of T as (D, R) runs over all pairs of finite, binary trees. This will be our
working version of F .
   Since this is the version that we work with and since it is traditional to write
elements of F as pairs of binary trees, we will drop the t and write (D, R) instead
of t(D, R). This starts immediately.

the homomorphism from the group in [1] to our representation is an isomorphism.

8.11. Sizes of trees in tree pairs. The notion of size is slippery in F . Since an
element of F is an equivalence class of pairs of trees, an obvious candidate is some
measure of tree pairs that is minimized over all pairs that represent an element.
A typical measure is the number of carets (which equals the number of internal
                             COLORING PLANAR GRAPHS                                  31


vertices) used in each tree of the tree pair. This will be minimized by the unique
reduced pair representing an element.
   This measure is only moderately well behaved. In general it is complicated
to predict the size of the result of a multiplication without actually doing the
multiplication. In particular it is complicated to predict the size of the result of
multiplying an element by a rotation (one of the generators used above) without
actually doing the multiplication.
   We deal with this by partly ignoring it. We will not be concerned with minimal
representatives of elements of F and only concerned with the size of the represen-
tative that we have in hand. A single pair (D, R) is a pair of vertices on a specific
associahedron Ad . Edges in the associahedron Ad are rotations that apply to trees
of the size of D and R. Only finitely many of the generators in {⌈u⌋ | u ∈ {0, 1}∗}
are available among the edges of Ad . If a generator outside this finite set is to be
used, then a different (larger) representative will need to be chosen (a move to a
larger associahedron takes place) and this will be duly noted. These considerations
will appear below in Section 9.5.

8.12. The group E. We now describe the other group we consider.
   The relations in (F1) and (F2) involve four generators each and will be referred
to as the “square relations,” while the relations in (F3) which involve five generators
will be referred to as the “pentagonal relations.” We will see that the pentagonal
relations behave very differently from the square relations when the generators are
interpreted as signed rotations.
   We define the group E as the group presented by the same generating set as F
and with the relations given by (F1) and (F2). The group E is finitely presented,
but that fact is neither surprising nor important here. The group E is an extension
of F . We use the letter E since it is the first letter of “extension,” since it is next
to F in the alphabet, and since the other neighbor of F in the alphabet has already
been used for groups closely related to F .
   The group E has been mentioned in conversations by several people familiar
with F but there has never been any compelling reason to consider it. Part of the
charm of Thompson’s groups or its close relatives is that they are either simple or
close to it. Groups that are farther from simple are often not considered.
   We will bring the group E into the discussion when we investigate valid paths
of signed rotations in Section 12.

9.2. Extending the set of colored elements. In Corollary 2.6 of [1] it is shown
that ⌈∅⌋ and ⌈1⌋ generate F . We know that the set of elements of F with colorings
is non-empty. The identity certainly has a coloring. If a non-identity element is
sought, then Lemma 6.8 says that ⌈∅⌋ has a coloring. It is trivial to show that
⌈1⌋ has a coloring. Since every element of F is a product of copies of ⌈∅⌋, ⌈1⌋ and
their inverses, and a coloring of an element is also a coloring of the inverse of that
element, we have the following.
Theorem 9.2. The four color theorem is equivalent to the statement that the set
of elements of F with colorings is closed under right multiplication by ⌈∅⌋, ⌈∅⌋, ⌈1⌋
and ⌈1⌋.
   We will see later that this theorem has limited effect.
   We note that the set of elements of F with colorings is clearly closed under
inversion (independent of the 4CT). The dihedral group of order 2(d + 3) acts on
Ad and thus on pairs of its vertices (elements of F ). The set of elements of F
with colorings is also closed under this action (independent of the 4CT). We have
not explored how much this extends the set of colored elements of F , but there
are reasons for not expecting much from such extension. See the remarks after (8)
below.
9.3. The compatibility lemma. Consider a product (A, B)(C, D) of elements of
F . Assume that a is a color vector valid for (A, B) and that c is a color vector valid
for (C, D). The vector a determines an edge 3-coloring of B, and c determines an
edge 3-coloring of C. We say that a and c are compatible on B ∩ C if the colorings
they determine on B and C, respectively, agree on B ∩ C.
Lemma 9.3 (Compatibility). If the vector a is valid for the pair (A, B), the vector
c is valid for (C, D), and the vectors are compatible on B ∩ C, then there is a
coloring of (A, B)(C, D).
Proof. We will refer to color vectors as indexed by leaves rather than indexed by
integers. This suffices to specify the vector.
   We let the vector d indexed over the leaves of B ∪ C have the color of a on a
leaf of B ∪ C that is a leaf of B but not a leaf of C and have the color of c on the
                              COLORING PLANAR GRAPHS                                   33


remaining leaves. Note that the second choice agrees with the color from a on the
leaves of B ∪ C that are leaves of both B and C since such leaves are also leaves of
B ∩ C.
   The discussion that follows is based on the discussion in Section 8.5. We view
the product (A, B)(C, D) as computed by (A′ , B ∪ C)(B ∪ C, D′ ) as describe in that
section.
   For a component Cv of C − B that is attached to B ∩ C at leaf v of B, the colors
of d on those leaves are those of c. Thus the root color of Cv from d is the root
color of Cv from c. But the root edge of Cv is the leaf edge of B ∩ C impinging
on v. By assumption, this edge gets the same color from a and c. This common
color is also the color that a assigns to the leaf edge in A for the leaf v ′ that has
the same left-right position in A as v has in B. But A′ has an isomorphic copy of
Cv attached to A at v ′ and the color derived from d at the leaf edge to v ′ will agree
with the color assigned there by a. It follows that the edge coloring on A′ derived
from d will agree with the color on A derived from a and agree with the colors on
components of A′ − A derived from c. Thus the color vector d will be valid for A.
Similar remarks apply to D′ .                                                        

9.4. Vines. The trees in this section are all finite, and binary.
   A vine is a tree with exactly one exposed caret. Note that the adjacency graph
of the carets in a vine will have no branches.
   If T is a tree and V is a vine, then T ∩ V and V − T are vines. In particular,
V − T is connected.
   The right vine is a vine where all the internal vertices are of the form 1∗ . The
reader can define the left vine, but we will rarely refer to it.
   If v is a vertex in T , then there is a unique vine Vv in T which has v as an
internal vertex and for which the children of v are the leaves of Vv . Note that with
this notation, we have that the rotation ⌈u⌋ is given by the pair (Vu0 , Vu1 ). Note
also that Vu0 ∩ Vu1 = Vu .

                                 10. Rigid colorings
10.1. Introduction. If c is a color vector with d + 2 entries, then it has a color
graph in Ad . For the color graph of c to have an edge that impinges on a vertex
T of Ad , there has to be a pair of adjacent internal vertices in T that have equal
signs under c. If no such pair exists, then T is an isolated vertex in the color graph
of c. We then call c a rigid coloring for T . Otherwise we call the coloring flexible.
    From the connectedness of a tree and the absence of loops it is immediate that
given an assignment of a sign to a single vertex, there is a unique rigid sign assign-
ment that agrees with that assignment. Thus every tree has two rigid colorings:
one with the vertex ∅ positive, and one with the vertex ∅ negative. We refer to the
first as the positive rigid coloring and the latter as the negative rigid coloring.
    It would appear that the terms “rigid,” “positive rigid,” “negative rigid” and
“flexible” apply to pairs (T, c) where T is some tree and c is a color vector of the
right length for T . It turns out that the words can be applied directly to a color
vector c.
    In Section 10.8, we will show that if there is one isolated vertex in the color
graph of c (i.e., is rigidly colored by c), then all vertices in the color graph of c are
isolated. Even further, if c gives the positive rigid coloring to one vertex T in the
color graph of c, then c gives the positive rigid coloring to every other vertex in
the color graph of c. It follows that a similar statement holds for the negative rigid
coloring.
    Given the above claims, we say that a color vector c is positive rigid if there is a
tree to which it gives the positive rigid coloring, and give a corresponding definition
to the term negative rigid. A color vector is rigid if it is positive or negative rigid.
A color vector that is not rigid is flexible.
    We also show that the set of all tree pairs that have rigid colorings forms a
subgroup of F and that this subgroup is easy to describe. We then reveal that rigid
colorings of tree pairs correspond precisely to the maps that have region 3-colorings.
Maps that have region 3-colorings are well understood. See Theorem 2-5 in [8].
    It is clear that the positive, rigid coloring for a tree is just the restriction to that
tree of the unique, positive, rigid coloring of T . It is also clear that the positive,

10.2. Basic facts. We list some items that can be proven as exercises. To do so,
some terminology will help.
    In T , a set of vertices in a line “down to the right” or “down to the left” forms a
line. To be technical. a line is of the form w0n where w is fixed and is either empty
or ends in 1 and n varies, or w1n where w is fixed and is either empty or ends in
0 and n varies. The lines of the form w1n are right lines and the lines of the form
w0n are left lines. Note that every vertex that is not the topmost vertex of a right
line is a topmost vertex of a left line and similarly with right and left reversed.
   The positive rigid sign assignment of T has the sign of w as (−1)|w| where |w|
is the length of w. It is the unique sign assignment with + as the sign of ∅ and no
two adjacent vertices having the same sign. The negative sign assignment gives w
the sign −(−1)|w|.
   We can also refer to |w| as the level or depth of w.
   The following are easy to demonstrate for the rigid sign assignments.
     (1) If the colors of the edges impinging on u are a, b and c as one goes counter-
          clockwise starting at the edge ascending from u, then the left line below u
          alternates b and a going down and the right line below u alternates c and
          a going down.
     (2) If T is a finite, binary tree with the positive rigid coloring, if u is a leaf of T
          and v is the leaf of T immediately following u is the left-right order then the
          color of v equals the color of u if |u| and |v| have different parities, and the
          color of v equals (−1)|u|+1 plus the color of u if |u| and |v| have the same
          parity. Recall that the color of a leaf is the color of the edge impinging on
          it.
     (3) If all the leaves of a finite, binary tree T with the positive rigid coloring
          have the same depth, then the colors of the leaves read in left-right order
          are repetitions of 132 if the depth is even and are repetitions of 231 if the
          depth is odd.
     (4) If color vector c gives both trees of the pair (D, R) the positive rigid col-
          oring, then for each i, the i-th leaves of D and R in the left-right order
          have depths of the same parity. We refer to the conclusion of the previous
          sentence as the parity condition.
     (5) Conversely, if a tree pair (D, R) satisfies the parity condition, then there is
          a color vector that gives the pair the positive rigid coloring.

10.3. 3-colorings. It is clear that a pair (D, R) that satisfies the parity condition
yields a map in S 2 in which each region is bounded by an even number of edges.
From Theorem 2-5 of [8], we know that this map has a region 3-coloring. In fact
we have the following.
Proposition 10.1. If a color vector c gives both trees of the pair (D, R) the positive
rigid coloring, then any region coloring of the corresponding map consistent with
the edge colorings of D and R is a region 3-coloring.
Proof. If (D, R) → (D′ , R′ ) and c gives (D, R) the positive rigid coloring, then
because of the parity condition, there is a positive rigid coloring of (D′ , R′ ) that
agrees with c on the leaves of D′ and R′ that are also leaves of D and R. In
particular, we can add carets to D and R to give a pair (D′′ , R′′ ) so that one (say
D′′ ) has all its leaves on one level. To simplify, we assume they are all on an even
level. The partial sums (starting from the left) of repetitions of 132 are repetitions
of 120. Thus if the region to the left of the leftmost leaf is given a color from
Z2 × Z2 , the regions encountered as the equator is traversed counterclockwise will
repeat in a pattern of three. Thus the map created from (D′′ , R′′ ) will have a
region 3-coloring. The map created from (D, R) is obtained from that of (D′′ , R′′ )
by eliminating a succession of regions and parallel edges while keeping the colors
for the remaining regions.                                                          

  We have a converse.
Lemma 10.2. If the map corresponding to the tree pair (D, R) has a region 3-
coloring, then the corresponding edge coloring is rigid.
Proof. The corresponding edge coloring gives to an edge the non-zero color that is
not the color of either region impinging on the edge. It follows that all the edges
that impinge on vertices in the boundary of a region R but are not edges in that
boundary are given the color of the region R. From that it follows that no two
adjacent vertices have the same sign.                                            

10.4. Negative, rigid colorings. The rigid coloring where the top internal vertex
is negative is the negative, rigid coloring. All that was said about the positive rigid
coloring applies to the negative rigid coloring with minor modifications to (2) and
(3). In particular the edge coloring of T under the negative, rigid coloring is the
mirror image (left-to-right reflection) of the edge coloring under the positive, rigid
coloring and the leaves on a common level are repetitions of 123 on an even level
and 321 of on an odd level of the negative, rigid coloring.

we count rigid color vectors.
Proposition 10.3. The positive rigid color vectors (modulo the action of S3 on
the colors) are the non-constant vectors c that sum to 1 and for which no prefix
sums to 3.
Proof. With the convention that 1 is the root edge color, we have that the sum
of any vector that we discuss is 1. The assumption that the top internal vertex is
positive takes care of the rest of the action by S3 .

   We now make use of the relationship between the edge colors and region colors

10.6. The group F4 . Here we identify the subgroup of F that has a rigid coloring.
We start by pointing out the following.
Proposition 10.4. The set of elements of F that have a rigid coloring forms a
subgroup of F .
Proof. If (A, B) and (C, D) have rigid colorings, then each pair satisfies the parity
condition and we may assume that both are positive, rigid. We can form the
product from (A′ , B ∪ C)(B ∪ C, D′ ) which is obtained by attaching components
of B − C to C and D to obtain (B ∪ C, D′ ) and attaching components of C − B
to A and B to obtain (A′ , B ∪ C). Each component of B − C has its root edge
at a certain level which is in turn the level of a leaf edge of a leaf of C which in
turn has the same parity as the level of the corresponding leaf of D. Thus the
positive rigid sign pattern of T will be extended in the construction of D′ from D.
Similar remarks apply to the construction of A′ from A. Now the details of the
Compatibility lemma (Lemma 9.3) give that the resulting color vectors on A′ and
D′ will be identical.                                                              

Proof. This is an immediate consequence of the fact that a pair has a rigid coloring

    11. Color graphs, zero sets, shadow patterns, and long paths
   We return to color graphs but from a complementary view. The set of vertices
in a colored associahedron (Ad , c) for which c is not valid will be called the zero set
of (Ad , c). As before it makes sense to refer to the zero set of (Ad , c) as the zero
set of c. Conjecture 10.11 is equivalent to the following.
Conjecture 11.1 (Connectivity′ ). For each d > 1, no zero set of any (Ad , c) with
c acceptable and flexible separates the 1-skeleton of Ad .
    We now discuss zero sets of acceptable color vectors.
    Let c be an acceptable color vector with d+2 entries, and let T be a vertex in Ad .
If c is not valid for T , then some edge e evaluates to zero when c is applied to the
leaves of T . This explains the use of the term “zero set.” If v is the lower endpoint
of e, then Tv has edge e as its root edge and the leaves of Tv form an interval in the
totally ordered set consisting of the leaves of T . That is, for some interval [i, j] in
[1, d + 2], the leaves of Tv are the i-th through the j-th in the left-right order. The
color zero assigned to e is a result of the fact that the sum of the colors in c from
the i-th through the j-th is zero.
    This leads to the next definition. If T is a finite, binary tree with d + 2 leaves,
then for each internal vertex v of T , the leaves of Tv form the shadow of v, and the
interval in [1, d + 2] that contains the numbers in left-right order of the leaves of Tv
is the shadow interval of v. Note that different internal vertices of T yield different
shadow intervals.
    The set of shadow intervals of all internal vertices of T except for the vertex ∅
forms the shadow pattern of T . The shadow interval of ∅ includes all of [1, d+ 2] and
gives no information. It is an exercise that T is determined by its shadow pattern.
We will see examples shortly.
    Applying this to zero sets, we see that if c is not valid for T , then a shadow
interval [m, n] for T for which the sum of the ci with i ∈ [m, n] is zero. Thus if Zc
is the set of those intervals J for which the sum of the ci with i ∈ J is zero, then a
tree T is in the zero set of c if and only if a shadow interval for T is in Zc .
    Given any interval J of length k in [1, d + 2], the set of trees with J in their
shadow patterns is a codimension-1 face of Ad of the form Ak−2 × Ad−k+1 . From
this it follows that the zero set of a given color vector is a union of codimension-1
faces. One might ask as an alternative to Conjecture 11.1 if it is possible for a
union of codimension-1 faces can separate the 1-skeleton of an associahedron. It
turns out that this is possible and we give an example in the next section. The
union of codimension-1 faces that we give will not be a zero set however.

generators. Let this word be w = ⌈v1 ⌋⌈v2 ⌋ · · · ⌈vk ⌋. Thinking of each ⌈vi ⌋ and
(D, R) as elements of F , lets us write

However, there are other paths that represent the same element of F . These paths
either have the same endpoints but get from one to the other by a different route,
or the paths read as the same string of symmetric generators but connect different
pairs of vertices, or both. For example, in the drawing (5) of A3 there are four
different paths that read as ⌈u⌋⌈u1⌋. Our definition of Σ(w) will depend on the
word in the symmetric generators and not on the particular start and end vertices.

in the path w. The graph Σ(w) may have parallel edges but will have no loops.
The vertex set will be the set of vertices in T so we will simply use T to denote
the vertex set. This is an infinite set and is more than needed. After proving a key
lemma, we will cut down the set of vertices.
   We define Σ(w) inductively and if w = ⌈v1 ⌋⌈v2 ⌋ · · · ⌈vk ⌋, then we will need pi =
⌈v1 ⌋⌈v2 ⌋ · · · ⌈vi ⌋ where 1 ≤ i ≤ k and we will need p0 to be the identity in F . We
start with Σ0 = Σ(p0 ) as the graph on T with no edges.

need to pick the endpoints of ei and its sign. Let x and y be the pivot vertices of

   Rather than explain this definition, we will state and prove the following. A
signed graph is balanced if every closed walk traverses an even number of negative
edges.

Theorem 12.1. Let w be an edge path in an associahedron that starts at a vertex
D. Then
    (1) all of the endpoints of edges in Σ(w) are internal vertices of D, and
    (2) there is a sign assignment for D for which w is valid if and only if Σ(w) is
        balanced.

Proof. If the pi are as defined above, then Dpi is the i-th vertex visited by w with
D as the 0-th. Lemma 8.1 makes pi a bijection from the internal vertices of D to
the internal vertices of Dpi . Item (1) follows immediately.
   If there is a sign assignment for D for which w is valid, then to show that Σ(w)
is balanced it is sufficient to show that each edge in Σ(w) is positive if and only if
it connects two vertices in D with the same sign.
   The edge ei in the construction above connects x and y where xpi−1 and ypi−1
are the pivot vertices of ⌈vi ⌋. But the sign of xpi−1 in the sign assignment of Dpi−1
is the sign of x in D as modified by pi−1 = ⌈v1 ⌋⌈v2 ⌋ · · · ⌈vi−1 ⌋. The modification
consists one negation for each xpj with 1 ≤ j < i for which xpj is a pivot vertex of
⌈vj+1 ⌋. But this is exactly the degree of the vertex x in Σi−1 . Similarly the sign
of ypi−1 in Dpi−1 is the sign of y negated a number of times which is the degree of
the vertex y in Σi−1 . Thus the sign of ei is positive if and only if the signs xpi−1
and ypi−1 in Dpi−1 both agree or both disagree, respectively, with the signs of x
and y in D. The validity of w says that the signs of xpi−1 and ypi−1 must be equal.
Thus the sign of ei is positive if and only if the signs of x and y in D are equal.
This proves the desired condition needed to show that Σ(w) is balanced.
   If it is known that Σ(w) is balanced, then there is a sign assignment of the
internal vertices of D with the property that each edge in Σ(w) is positive if and
only if it connects two vertices in D with the same sign. Considerations almost
identical to the previous paragraph show that w is valid for this sign assignment
on D.                                                                                

   The details in the above proof give us the following. If Σ(w) is balanced (contin-
uing the notation of this section), then a sign assignment of the internal vertices of
D is compatible with Σ(w) if an edge in Σ(w) is positive if and only if it connects
edges with the same sign. We say that a coloring of the pair (D, Dw) is compat-
ible with Σ(w) if the associated sign assignment on the internal vertices of D is
compatible with Σ(w).

12.3. The vertex set of a sign structure. Giving the set of vertices of T to Σ(w)
is clearly excessive, and also unrevealing. Item (1) of Theorem 12.1 hints that if w
is a path starting at a tree D, then the internal vertices of D might make a good
vertex set for Σ(w). However, we have pointed out that different paths can read
as the same string of rotation symbols, and the edges of Σ(w) really only depend
on the string of symbols. Another obvious choice would be the set of endpoints of
the edges of Σ(w). For reasons that we hope will be made clear, we will adopt a
compromise.
   Let w be a path in some Ad , and let S be the set of endpoints of Σ(w). There is
a smallest tree T containing all of S among the internal vertices of T . We let the
internal vertices of T be the set of vertices of Σ(w).
   The reason for our choice of vertex set is the next result.
Theorem 12.5. Let w be an edge path in an associahedron, and let T be the tree
whose internal vertices are the vertices of Σ(w). If the pair (T, T w) is prime, then
Σ(w) is connected.
Proof. The proof will be inductive, and we will have to prove more than the state-
ment of the theorem.
   Let w have length n, and let pi be the prefix of length i of w, 0 ≤ i < n.
Obviously, p0 is the empty word. Recall that we can think of w and all the pi as
functions, where the special case of i = 0 has p0 as the identity.
   We need a preliminary discussion before giving the statements that we will prove.
If S is a tree and S ′ and S ′′ are subtrees of a tree S having disjoint non-empty sets
of internal vertices and there is an edge from an internal vertex of S ′ to an internal
vertex of S ′′ , then either a leaf of S ′ is an internal vertex of S ′′ or the reverse. The
cases are mutually exclusive. In both cases, we say that S ′ and S ′′ are adjacent.
In the case that a leaf of S ′ is an internal vertex of S ′′ , we say that S ′ is above S ′′ .
Otherwise we say that S ′′ is above S ′ .
   Consider the following statements where 1 ≤ i ≤ n.
                              COLORING PLANAR GRAPHS                                   48


   αi : If C is a component of Σ(pi ) and V (C) is its vertex set, then for each j with
0 ≤ j ≤ i, there is a subtree TC(j) of T pj whose internal vertices are exactly the
vertices (V (C))pj .
   βi : Let C and D be components of Σ(pi ). If TC(0) and TD(0) are adjacent with
TC(0) above TD(0) , then for each j with 0 ≤ j ≤ i, the subtrees TC(j) and TD(j) are
adjacent with TC(j) above TD(j) . If TC(0) and TD(0) are not adjacent, then for each
j with 0 ≤ j ≤ i, the subtrees TC(j) and TD(j) are not adjacent.
   We have that α1 is trivial, and we have that β1 follows from the nature of a
single rotation.
   We assume αk and βk hold for some k < n. Let ⌈u⌋ = wk+1 . There are two
cases to consider.
   Case I: Both pivot vertices of ⌈u⌋ are internal vertices of a single TC(k) . In
this case, the edge added to Σ(pk ) to create Σ(pk+1 ) has both of endpoints in the
vertices of a single component C of Σ(pk ). Thus the components of Σ(pk ) and
Σ(pk+1 ) have identical vertex sets and αk+1 holds for 1 ≤ j ≤ k. To get αk+1 for
j = k + 1, we note that the vertex set (V (C))pk+1 = ((V (C))pk )⌈u⌋ is the image
under ⌈u⌋ of the set of internal vertices of the subtree TC(k) . From the illustration
in (3), we see that this forms the set of internal vertices of a subtree of T pk . All
other subtrees corresponding to components of the sign structure are carried to
T pk+1 isomorphically. This proves αk+1 .
   The statement βk+1 clearly holds for 1 ≤ j ≤ k. The β family of statements says
that the relation “adjacent to” and the relation “over” among the relevant subtrees
are preserved by the prefixes of w. The illustration in (3) also shows that these
relations are preserved by a single rotation. Thus βk+1 also holds for j = k + 1.
   Case II: The pivot vertices of ⌈u⌋ are internal vertices of two different TC(k) and
TD(k) . Since the pivot vertices are endpoints of an edge, we have that TC(k) and
TD(k) are adjacent, and we can assume that TC(k) is over TD(k) . Since βk says
that the relation “adjacent to” and the relation “over” are preserved by the pj
with 1 ≤ j ≤ k, we know that TC and TD are adjacent with TC over TD . Similar
statements apply to the TC(j) and TD(j) for 1 ≤ j < k, while for j = k it is our
hypothesis. It is now seen that for 0 ≤ j ≤ k, the union of TC(j) with TD(j) is a
subtree TA(j) of T pj whose set of internal vertices is the vertex set of a component
of (Σ(pk+1 ))pj . Thus if we replace TC(0) and TD(0) by TA(0) in T and keep all the
other subtrees the same, then we now have the desired one-to-one correspondence
between subtree and component of Σ(pk+1 ). This is carried in the correct way
to each T pj by prefixes pj for 1 ≤ j ≤ k, and the argument that this works for
j = k + 1 follows because we are now in the situation of Case I and the argument
for j = k + 1 for both α and β applies here.
   We now assume that Σ(w) = Σ(pn ) is not connected. Let C be a component
of Σ(w) so that TC is minimal with respect to the “over” relation. This will make
every leaf of TC a leaf of T . Thus every leaf of TC(n) is a leaf of T w. Since rotations,
and thus chains of rotations, preserve prefix order and thus the left-right order of
the leaves, the leaves of the subtrees TC and TC(n) define exactly the same intervals
in the leaf numberings of T and T w. Since Σ(w) is not connected, this interval is
not the entire interval of leaf numbers. Thus (T, T w) is not prime.                    

12.4. Relations among the paths. This section contains a set of related obser-
vations about the sign structures. We give them to explain the wording of some
conjectures that we raise about colorings that arise from sign structure considera-
tions.
12.4.1. Moving paths across faces. If w and w′ are two edge paths in some Ad with
the same endpoints, then as words in the symmetric generators, they represent the
same element of F . It follows from the presentation discussed in Section 8.6 that as
words, we can alter w using a sequence of “square” or “pentagonal” relations. We
will call these alterations “square” or “pentagonal” moves. These moves, together
with cancellation or introduction of consecutive inverse pairs, can be applied to w
so that the result equals w′ . From the nature of the square and pentagonal moves,
it is clear that these moves can be realized as moves across 2-dimensional faces in
Ad . We look at the two kinds of alterations.
12.4.2. Moving paths across pentagons. Let D and R be two non-adjacent vertices
in A2 . See (4). There are two simple paths between them—one of length three
and one of length two. The path of length three is always inconsistent and the
path of length two is always consistent. This is easy enough to check by hand. For
example, ⌈u0⌋⌈u⌋⌈u1⌋ is inconsistent, but ⌈u0⌋⌈u⌋ is consistent. Because of the
action of the dihedral group of order ten on A2 , it is only necessary to check one of
each length. Thus a pentagonal move is capable of changing a path from consistent
to inconsistent or vice versa.
12.4.3. Adding or removing canceling pairs. It is also clear that a consistent path
can be “ruined” by inserting consecutive canceling pairs. The consistent path
⌈u0⌋⌈u⌋ represents the same element of F as the inconsistent path ⌈u0⌋⌈u⌋⌈u1⌋⌈u1⌋.
We will comment again on the problems introduced by increasing the length of a
path.
12.4.4. Moving paths across squares. There are two cases to consider.
   In the simpler case, a two-edge subpath along part of a square is replaced by the
other two-edge subpath along the other part of the square. If the various square
relations are investigated, it is found that the first two-edge subpath introduces
two edges in Σ of the path that have disjoint endpoints and that the other two-
edge subpath introduces the same two edges in Σ in the other order. This is in
spite of the fact that the four edges around the square correspond to three different
symmetric generators. When two edges with disjoint end points are introduced in
succession into Σ, reversing the order does not change any of the sign assignments
in Σ. Thus a two-edge to two-edge move across a square preserves sign consistency.
   In the more complicated case, a three-edge subpath is replaced by a one-edge
subpath. The three edge path introduces three edges into Σ in which two are
parallel and one has endpoints disjoint from the parallel edges. The two parallel
edges acquire the same signs as each other. The shorter path leaves out the two
                             COLORING PLANAR GRAPHS                                  50


parallel edges. Removing a pair of parallel edges does not alter the parities of
any vertex. Thus it is seen that a three-edge to one-edge move across a square
does not destroy sign consistency, but it might convert a sign inconsistent path
into a sign consistent path by removing a damaging term. For example w1 =
⌈u0⌋⌈u⌋⌈u1⌋⌈u111⌋⌈u1⌋ is sign inconsistent. But this represents the same element
of F and of E as w2 = ⌈u0⌋⌈u⌋⌈u11⌋ which is sign consistent.
   Since the passage from w2 to w1 takes a shorter consistent path to a longer
inconsistent path, we see again that increasing the length of a path can cause
problems with consistency.

12.5. Two conjectures. From the remarks in Section 12.4, moves across single
pentagons have drastic consequences. However, it is possible to find moves across
pairs of pentagons that take sign consistent paths to sign consistent paths. Experi-
ment hints that the resulting pairs of paths have drastically different sign structures,
leading to different colorings of the two trees at the endpoints.
   Moves across squares tend to be less drastic, although careless applications can
introduce inconsistencies in the paths. If we restrict moves to square moves, then
we create square path classes, by declaring that two paths are square equivalent if
one can be carried to the other (without changing the endpoints) by only making
square moves. The square path classes all lie within a single element of E.
   We now give two conjectures and reinterpret a third.
Conjecture 12.6 (Well definedness). Let (T, S) be prime and let w and w′ be sign
consistent paths from T to S that are square equivalent with no shorter path in the
square equivalence class that is also sign consistent. Then Σ(w) = Σ(w′ ).
Conjecture 12.7 (Injectivity). Let (T, S) be prime and let w and w′ be sign con-
sistent paths from T to S with no shorter path in their respective square equivalence
class that is also sign consistent. If Σ(w) = Σ(w′ ), then w and w′ represent the
same element of E.
   If the first conjecture above holds, then each element of E with a sign consistent
representative corresponds to a unique sign structure. If the image of E in F is
prime, then this gives a single well defined coloring of the reduced pair representing
the element of F . If the second conjecture above holds, then no two elements of E
that give colorings (have sign consistent representatives) can give the same coloring.
We can ask if every coloring comes from an element of E and refer to this as a
“surjectivity conjecture.” However, this is logically equivalent to the Connectivity
Conjectures 10.11 and 11.1 that have already been stated.

12.6. On the injectivity conjecture. What would be desirable now would be for
elements of E expressed as words in the symmetric generators to have a nice normal
form. The elements of F expressed as words in the generating set {1n | n ≥ 0}
have a nice normal form. See [1]. Were elements of E to have a nice normal form,
then two elements of E reduced to a normal form and still different might be in
good enough form to be able to show that where they differ forces a difference
in their respective sign structures. Alas, the relations in E among the symmetric
generators allow for a lot of moving around and a nice normal form seems elusive.
This approach to the injectivity conjectures still seems to have promise.

12.6.1. Comment. It is possible to show that if Σ(w) is not connected, then w is
square equivalent to a path that breaks into a finite number of subpaths in one-to-
one correspondence with the components of Σ(w) where each subpath generates its
corresponding component.

   This section and the sections that follow gather observations that either fill

   After Lemma 6.2, we announced that a color vector is acceptable (valid for some
tree) if and only if the vector is not a constant and does not sum to zero. Here we
prove that claim.
   A color vector that is constant produces a zero wherever an exposed caret exists,
and a vector that sums to zero produces a zero at the root edge. Thus the conditions
are necessary. We give the result after a sequence of lemmas.
   We will use v to denote a color vector that is not a constant and does not sum to
zero. We will represent the contents of a vector as strings and combine vectors by
concatenation. Letters x, y and z will represent unspecified colors that are assumed
to be different if the letters are different.
Lemma 13.1. A constant vector xi sums to zero if i is even and x if i is odd.
Lemma 13.2. The vectors xn y and xy n are always acceptable.
Proof. The first is valid for a right vine (see Section 9.4), and the second is valid
for a left vine.                                                                   

Lemma 13.3. If v = ps and both p and s are acceptable, then v is acceptable.
Proof. Since the sum of v is not zero, the sums of p and s are not equal. If p is
valid for A and s is valid for B, then ps is valid for A b B (see Section 4.6). 

Lemma 13.4. If v = xi s or v = sxi , i ≥ 1 and s is acceptable with sum different
from x, then v is acceptable.
Proof. If s is valid for A, then xi s is valid for the tree formed by identifying the
root edge of A with the rightmost leaf edge of the right vine Vi (right vine with i
internal vertices). A similar construction with a left vine handles sxi .          

  The next lemma is not used, but is too cute to omit.
Lemma 13.5. If v = xi y j , 1 ≥ 1, j ≥ 1, then v is acceptable.
Proof. The sum of v is not zero, so at least one of i or j is odd. Assume i is odd.
From Lemma 13.2, we can assume j > 1. The sum of xi y is z, and by Lemma
13.4, v = (xi y)y j−1 is acceptable. If j is odd, we assume i > 1 and a similar proof

   In the argument below, regular expression syntax is used. We use [xyz] to denote
an unspecified choice of one of x, y or z. The expression [xyz]n denotes a string of
n such choices rather than one choice repeated n times.
Proposition 13.6. A color vector of length at least 2 is acceptable if and only if
it is non-constant and has a non-zero sum.
Proof. We only need to argue one direction and we assume v is not constant and

   We know v = xi y[xyz]n−i with i ≥ 1. If the sum of v is y or z, then v = xs

   If the sum of v is x and the last color in v is not x, then v = p[yz] and where

                                   14. Patterns
   This section generalizes some of the observations in the proof of Proposition
10.4 which found a group arising from a certain sign assignment on T . However,
we have not explored the generalization that we are about to present and it is not
clear that it opens up much in the way of examples. There is one example that is
rather trivial to establish and that will be given in the next section. Even though
it is trivial to establish, it is not trivial in structure. However, the structure has
not yet been explored.
   A pattern P is a sign assignment on T . Any finite, binary tree inherits a sign
assignment from P , and the convention that the root edge be colored 1 gives each
finite binary tree an edge coloring derived from P . If the vector of the colors of the
leaf edges of two trees turn out to be identical under the coloring derived from P ,
then the common color vector is valid for the pair. In such cases, we will say that
the pair is P -compatible.
   An example of a pattern is the positive rigid pattern that we denote P r . It
was defined as P r (v) = (−1)|v| . The negative rigid pattern −P r was also defined.
Proposition 10.4 shows that the P r -compatible pairs form a subgroup of F . We
discuss conditions under which an arbitrary pattern P leads to a subgroup.
   To help with the discussion, we look at subpatterns. If P is a pattern and u is
a vertex in T , then Pu will be defined by Pu (v) = P (uv). If we recall that Tu is
defined by Tu = {uv | v ∈ T }, then we see that Pu is the composition of the obvious
isomorphism v 7→ uv from T to Tu with the restriction of P to Tu .
   If we now have a pattern P and a pair of P -compatible pairs (A, B) and (C, D),
then (A, B)(C, D) has a coloring since for trivial reasons the coloring of (A, B) and
(C, D) induce the same coloring on B ∩ C and the Compatibility Lemma (Lemma
9.3) applies. However, the coloring on the product might not be derived from P .
   We say that the pair of pairs ((A, B), (C, D)) is P -compatible if both of the
following hold.
    (1) For every leaf v of B ∩ C that is a leaf of C and not a leaf of B and leaf w
        of D that occupies the same position in the left-right order of the leaves of
        D as v does in the leaves of C, then Pv = Pw .
    (2) For every leaf v of B ∩ C that is a leaf of B and not a leaf of C and leaf w
        of A that occupies the same position in the left-right order of the leaves of
        A as v does in the leaves of B, then Pv = Pw .

   The group F has a property shared by F . If T is a finite binary tree, if (A, B)
is a pair in F + , and v is a leaf of T , then we can form a new element of F + . We do

that A′ is obtained by identifying the root edge of A with the leaf edge of T that
                                COLORING PLANAR GRAPHS                               56


impinges on v. A more technical definition is A′ = T ∪ vA where the vertices of vA
are those in {vu | u ∈ A}. The new element of F + is (A′ , B ′ ).

on the particular T that has v as a leaf. Following [6], we refer to (A′ , B ′ ) as the
deferment of (A, B) to v. We thus have that, like F , the group F + is closed under
deferment.

from the leaves of D to the leaves of R. See [1] for descriptions of the equivalence
relation on the triples used to define the elements of V and the multiplication on
the triples. These are easy enough to guess correctly.
   We give an example to show that not every element of V has a valid edge 3-
coloring. That is, there is a triple (D, σ, R) where D and R have n leaves each so
that no color vector c = (c1 , . . . , cn ) of length n exists that is valid for both D and
R in the following sense. If v1 , . . . , vn are the leaves of D in left-right order then
for each i with 1 ≤ i ≤ n the color ci is applied to leaf vi of D and leaf σ(vi ) of R.
   The smallest examples (by computer search) have 6 leaves for each tree, and our
example is of this size. Examples are not rare. Of 13,800 triples involving trees
with 6 leaves, there are 3,584 triples with no edge 3-coloring.

20.2. Colorings of a single tree. If T is a tree with n internal vertices, then it
has 2n sign assignments. We count colorings modulo the action of S3 on the colors,
so we assume that the root color is 1 and that the top internal vertex is positive.
Thus we get that the number of colorings of T modulo the action of S3 on the
colors is 2n−1 . This is equivalent to the formula for δn at the top of Page 212 in
[15] where the action of S3 is not taken into account.

values in {1, 2, 3} ignoring validity. This latter number is 3n+1 if the action of S3 is
ignored, or approximately 12 (3n ) modulo the action of S3 . We say “approximately”
since not every vector uses all three colors.
   Note also that these numbers are smaller than 4n .

20.4. Acceptable colorings. Proposition 13.6 characterizes acceptable colorings
of length at least two as those that are not constant and do not sum to zero. Every
non-constant color vector of length n + 1 is of the form 1i 2[123]n−i if we mod out
by the action of S 3 on the colors. We compute c(n), the number of these that do
not sum to zero.
   We have c(0) = 0 and c(1) = 1.
   We calculate c(n + 1) by breaking the vectors of length n + 2 into three classes.
   (I) There is one vector of the form 1i+1 2.
   (II) There are 3c(n) vectors of the form (1i 2[123]n−i)x, x ∈ {1, 2, 3}, where the
sum of (1i 2[123]n−i ) is not zero. But only two values of x give the whole vector a
non-zero sum, so this contributes 2c(n) to c(n + 1).
   (III) This leaves vectors of the form (1i 2[123]n−i)x where the sum of (1i 2[123]n−i )
is zero. But (1i 2[123]n−i) has the form py where p must have sum y. Now
(1i 2[123]n−i ) cannot have the form 1n 2 since 1n 2 does not have sum zero. Thus p
has the form p = (1i 2[123]n−i−1) and there are c(n − 1) of these. Since we assume
py has sum zero, there is only one choice for y given p. Since the sum of py is zero,
all three values of x will give pyx a non-zero sum. So this contributes 3c(n − 1) to
c(n + 1).

Proposition 20.3. Modulo the action of S3 on the colors, there are c(n) acceptable
color vectors for the set of trees with n carets, where c(0) = 0, c(1) = 1, and
c(n + 1) = 2c(n) + 3c(n − 1) + 1.

20.5. Rigid colorings. To count rigid colorings, we count the color vectors that
are described in Proposition 10.3. These are (modulo permutations of the colors)
the non-constant vectors that sum to 1 with no prefix that sums to 3.
Proposition 20.4. Let r(n) be the number (modulo the action of S3 on the colors)
of positive, rigid color vectors valid for trees with n carets. Then r(1) = 1, r(2) = 2
and r(n + 1) = r(n) + 2r(n − 1) + 1.
Proof. The cases n = 1 and n = 2 are done by direct checking.
   Consider the set S of vectors c that are color vectors that each positively and
rigidly color some tree with n + 1 carets with n ≥ 2.
   We break our set into three disjoint sets. The first consists of all vectors in S of
the form 2s, the second consists of all vectors in S of the form 13s and the third
consists of all vectors in S of the form 11s. In these, s is the remainder of the color
vector. In the first two cases, the sum of s is 3, and in the third case the sum of s
is 1.
   We first assume that s is a constant sequence. If we have the form 11s, the sum
of the full vector is 0 if s has even length, so it must have odd length. But then
the sum is 1 and s is the constant vector 1 making the full vector constant. So the
form 11s cannot have s constant.
   In the other two cases, the sum of s is 3 and must be the constant sequence 3
of odd length. We get one valid, positive, rigid vector with s constant from the
form 2s if the length of the full vector is even, and we get one valid, positive, rigid
vector with s constant from the form 13s if the length of the full vector is odd.
This contributes 1 to the number of valid vectors no matter what the parity of the
length of the full vector is.
   From now on we assume that s is not constant.
   In form 2s, we know that s is non-constant, sums to 3 and cannot have a prefix
summing to 1. This is the criterion for Proposition 10.3 if we interchange the roles
of 1 and 3. Thus the number of such s is equal to r(n).
   In form 13s, we know that s is non-constant, sums to 3 and cannot have a prefix
summing to 1. Once again, we refer to Proposition 10.3 with the roles of 1 and 3
interchanged and we have that the number of such s is r(n − 1).
   In form 11s, we know that s is non-constant, sums to 1 and cannot have a prefix
summing to 3. From Proposition 10.3, the number of such s is r(n − 1).
   Thus we have r(n + 1) = r(n) + 2r(n − 1) + 1.                                     

   From Lemma 20.1, this is the sequence of partial sums of the Jacobsthal numbers.
Note that in this case, the value of p in Lemma 20.2 is 1. So the values of the r(n)
are never far from the values of the Jacobsthal numbers.

20.7.3. The counts. We are interested in the number of vertex 4-colorings of Wn ,
Θn , Ξn , Yn and ∇n . Available to us is the chromatic polynomial P (Γ, t) of a finite
graph Γ and the standard techniques for calculating the polynomial. We are thus
interested in P (Γ, 4) for Γ one of the five graphs listed. Note that we have been
counting colorings modulo the permutations of the colors while P (Γ, t) considers
colorings different even if one is obtained from the other by permuting the colors.
This is not a problem since none of our graphs has a 2-coloring. It follows from
this that the number we want is 1/24 of the number that the chromatic polynomial
gives.

20.8. Zero sets and color graphs. This section also raises more questions than
it answers.
    If c is a color vector of length n + 1, then it has a zero set and complementary
color graph on Ad with d = n − 1. Thus small zero sets should go with large color
graphs and vice versa. We can measure zero sets directly by the number of vertices
of Ad that are in it and indirectly by the number of codimension-1 faces that are in
it. We have few examples from calculations, but we have observed that diameter as
well as size enters into this discussion. The color graphs of highest diameter we have
seen are the color graphs of Section 11.2. It turns out that that the complementary
zero sets of these color graphs are the smallest we have seen measured in terms of
codimension-1 faces. On the other hand, color graphs of smallest diameter have
complementary zero sets that have the largest number of codimension-1 faces. We
now describe some specifics.
    We get two numbers to consider from Section 11. Let n = d + 1. If c is a color
vector of length n + 1, then it has a zero set which is determined by certain set Zc
of intervals in [0, n]. An interval J is in Zc if the sum of the ci for i ∈ J is zero.
Each such J ∈ Zc determines a codimension-1 face of Ad all of whose vertices are
trees for which c is not valid. We define u(n) to be the smallest integer and l(n) to
be the largest integer so that for any acceptable color vector c of length n + 1, we
have l(n) ≤ |Zc | ≤ u(n).

rior to the color graph of a color vector c are those trees that are either completely
positively signed by c or are completely negatively signed by c. If we accept that

                                      21. The end
  This section represents the ill defined frontier of this paper. It gathers questions
that fit nowhere else.
Question 21.1. Given vertices u and v in a color graph Γ in Ad , is there a pair
of codimension-1 faces in Ad that contains a path in Γ between u and v? Can the
path and faces be chosen so that there is an intermediate vertex w in the path with
the path from u to w in one face and the path from w to v in the other?
   Recall from Section 11 that for a color vector c with n + 1 entries, the set Zc
is the set of intervals [m, n] in [0, n] for which the sum of the ci with i ∈ [m, n] is
zero.
Question 21.2. Is there a way to characterize those sets of intervals in [0, n] that
are of the form Zc for some acceptable color vector c with n + 1 entries?
Question 21.3. Is there any significance to the inconsistent elements of E?
    It is not clear what is meant by this last question. Perhaps there are imaginary
colorings of some tree pairs.
    Another question is motivated by our construction of Σ(w). Its relevance to the
paper is highly questionable. Let Γ be a finite graph. If the edges of Γ are numbered
e1 , e2 , . . . , en with n = |E(Γ)|, then we turn Γ into a signed graph much as we do
for Σ(w). We let Γi be the subgraph of Γ having exactly the edges {ek | 1 ≤ k ≤ i}.
We say that edge ei+1 with endpoints ai and bi is positive if the parities in Γi of ai
and bi agree, and is negative otherwise. Obviously, the signing of the edges depends
on the numbering of the edges. We are interested in whether the signed graph Γ is
balanced. The question we raise is as follows.
Question 21.4. Which connected, finite graphs become balanced for every number-
ing of their edges, which connected, finite graphs become balanced for no numbering
of their edges, and which connected, finite graphs are not in the two classes just
described?
   Of course trees, having no interesting closed walks, are in the first class. Elim-
inating trees, a minor amount of experimentation hints that in the first class are
the boundaries of polygons with an even number of edges, in the second class are
the boundaries of polygons with an odd number of edges, and in the third class is
everything else.
