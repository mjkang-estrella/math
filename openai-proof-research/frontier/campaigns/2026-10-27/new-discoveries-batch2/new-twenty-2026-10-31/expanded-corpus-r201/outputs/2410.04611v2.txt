                                                                                             Abstract
                                                   In this research, we introduce an algorithm that produces what appears to be a new mathematical
                                               object as a consequence of projecting the n-dimensional Z-curve onto an n-dimensional sphere. The first
                                               part presents the algorithm that enables this transformation, and the second part focuses on studying
                                               its properties.
                                         Keywords: Morton Coordinates, Space Filling Curves, Discrete Mathematics, Group Theory, Graph The-
                                         ory, Representation Theory


                                         1    Introduction
                                         Space-filling curves (SPCs), such as the Peano Curve [6], Hilbert Curve [3], and the Z-Curve [5], are discrete
                                         fractals formed by a continuous line that visits every cell in an n-dimensional unit cube exactly once. Each
                                         cell is assigned a unique number, and when connected sequentially by this line, a self-repeating pattern
                                         emerges that covers the entire space without intersecting itself. Initially, the hypercube contains 2D cells.
                                         Each cell is subdivided into 2D new cells, resulting in a total of 2D×K smaller cells, where D is the number
                                         of dimensions and K represents the number of subdivision levels (referred to in this research as the scaling
                                         factor). Each new cell is assigned a unique number from 0 to 2D×K − 1, and we denote this range of numbers
                                         by XD K . Each type of space-filling curve—whether Peano, Hilbert, or Z-Curve—has its own method for
                                         assigning numbers to the initial cells and the cells resulting from subsequent subdivisions. These numbering
                                         methods determine the specific connection patterns within the hypercube when the cells are connected in
                                         sequence.

                                             The SP C this research focuses on is the Z-Curve, also known as the Morton Curve or Lebesgue Curve.
                                         In this curve, we assign specific directions to the bits, which determine the value of each cell, and there
                                         is always one bit per dimension. In 2D, the initial four cells correspond to the smallest number of space-
                                         directional divisions—up-right, up-left, down-right, and down-left, so we only need 2 bits to define those
                                         positions, one for the X axis and the other for the Y axis. The bits 0 and 1 specify the directions for
                                         up/down and left/right in both dimensions, and the curve moves according to how these bits assign the
                                         directions through the different dimensions. When the parameter K increases, continuing with the 2D case,
                                         each portion of space or cell is subdivided into four smaller cells, with each new cell receiving directions based
                                         on the original directions assigned to the bits. For instance, a cell initially oriented up-right subdivides into
                                         four new cells: up-right, up-left, down-right, and down-left. To calculate the number for each new cell, the
                                         bits corresponding to the new directions are added to the right of the binary representation of the previous
                                         cell. For example, if the original cell value was 01, after subdivision, the new cells would be assigned values
                                         0111, 0101, 0110, and 0100, respectively. The resulting string of bits is then converted to a decimal number.
                                         By connecting these decimal numbers in sequence with a continuous thread, we can observe the Z-pattern

    As we continue fragmenting the space, we increase the precision in defining regions, starting with the
larger area, like up-right, and then subdividing within that, for example, focusing on bottom-left within
the up-right region. This process works similarly to a coordinate system. In fact, the Z-Curve integrates
its own coordinate system, known as Morton coordinates [5]. Morton coordinates encode multi-dimensional
points into a single scalar value by interleaving the binary representations of each coordinate. This binary
representation relies on two parameters: the dimension D and the expansion factor K, which together result
in D × K bits. If the number has fewer than D × K bits, this chain of bits will add leading zeros to reach
the desired length. After obtaining the final binary representation, we split the chain of bits into blocks of
size D, with each bit corresponding to a specific dimension. The dimensions follow the order [X, Y, Z, . . . ],
and we enclose each block in square brackets for clarity:

2    Methodology
This section has two parts. First, we will explain the concept of BitDistance and its relationship with
the Z-Curve. Next, we will use the Morton coordinates and BitDistance to project the numbers from an
N -dimensional Morton curve onto an N -dimensional sphere.

2.1    BitDistance
Definition 2.1 (BitDistance). The BitDistance (BD) represents the distance a dimensional component of
a number moves, determined by the directions and weights assigned to its bits.
    In Morton coordinates, each binary number contains K strings of bits, each of length D bits. The
BitDistance is calculated in each dimension, resulting in D BitDistances. The position of each bit in the
sequence for each dimension determines the magnitude of movement, with the leftmost bits contributing
more heavily to the distance. These contributions are weighted according to the inverse powers of 2. Each
bit’s weight can be positive or negative, depending on the assigned direction, with the values 1 and 0 being
arbitrarily assigned positive or negative weights. The directions assigned to the bits in up to 3D space are
as follows:

   • Bit = 1: Positive weights, { Right, Up, Forward }.
   • Bit = 0: Negative weights, { Left, Down, Backward }.

    Given a chain of bits b0 , b1 , b2 , . . . , bK−1 representing the Morton coordinates in the dimension D, we
calculate the BitDistance for that dimension using the following expression:

2.2    Projecting onto an N -Dimensional Sphere
The Z-curve forms a fractal, exhibiting a self-repeating structure. A point or number on the fractal takes
different relative positions depending on the zoom level. For example, a point may appear in the upper-right
at one scale but shift to the bottom-left when zooming in. Zooming in focuses on numbers that share the
same leftmost D bits as the target number. Each zoom step ignores or removes 1 bit per dimension, halving
the space size in each dimension. Consequently, the new space includes only the numbers that match the
same D × Zoom bits as the target number. Therefore, when calculating the position of a number after
zooming in using the BD, we ignore the bits already excluded during the zoom process.

   The absolute position of a point, represented by the BD calculated taking into consideration all the bits,
and the relative position, determined by the BD after ignoring at least one bit per dimension, are indepen-
dent. We will specify the number of bits ignored in the calculation to distinguish between the BitDistance
(BD) calculated using all the bits and the BitDistance that ignores D × n bits. Each time we reference a
BitDistance that ignores certain bits, we denote it as BDn , where n indicates the number of bits excluded
per dimension, with n ranging up to K − 1. For instance, when ignoring the first D bits in the calculation,
we label it as BD1 .

each position. To address this, we will :

3.1.3   Equivalent Position and the Minimum Number of Knowable Subsets
Estimating the number of subsets of different sizes within a set XDK using only the parameters D and K is
challenging. Specifically, we cannot predict whether there are n subsets of size 2D × A or m subsets of size
2D × B after applying the circular projection to the set XD K . However, we observe that certain regions in
the Z-curve consistently form subsets with the same number of elements, even after subdividing each cell
into 2D smaller cells K times, regardless of the numerical values within each cell. A prominent example is
the values located at the corners of the Z-curve, which always include the zero. Independently of the value
of K, these regions consistently constitute a subset with exactly 2D elements. Initially, this suggests that
when a parent cell divides into 2D new child cells, one of them will always preserve its equivalent position,
maintaining a subset size consistent with its parent.

its own parent before subdivision. In other words, it is the child cell that occupies the same proportional
location in the subdivided space as the parent cell did in the original space.

3.3    Internal Structure of T
In the previous subsections, we have observed that subsets T ⊂ XD   K exhibit intriguing properties, including
consistent patterns, symmetries, and underlying rules. This suggests that all subsets share an abstract
internal structure that serves as a foundation for these properties. Such internal structures are linked to
the size of the subset in a given dimension and remain invariant with respect to the scaling factor K or the
specific numbers contained within the subset. In this subsection, we aim to uncover this internal structure.
    To identify the internal structure of a subset T, we proceed as follows:

  1. Sorting and Decomposition: Sort the elements of T in ascending order, from the smallest to the
     largest. Then, represent each number in the subset using its Morton coordinates, separating them into
     their dimensional components.
  2. Identifying Transition Chains: Starting with the dimensional components of the smallest number,
     identify the chain of bits that, for each dimensional component of the first number, starting with the
     dimension X subjected to the XOR operation, will generate the corresponding dimensional component
     of the next number. Repeat this step for each consecutive pair of numbers in T. Upon reaching the
     last element, compute the chain of bits that produces the first element.
  3. Constructing the Dictionary: Each time a chain of bits is identified, register it in a shared dic-
     tionary, associating it with a letter variable starting from A. This dictionary is common to all
     dimensional components. For example, if the entry A = 1011 appears in the dimensional component
     X, other dimensional components can also utilize the variable A.
      Before including any new chains of bits, the initial dictionary includes the variables =, representing a
      chain of K zeros, and X, representing a chain of K ones. The = operation indicates that the specific
      chain remains unchanged, while the X operation indicates that the entire sequence of bits is inverted
      (logical NOT operation).

Definition 3.9 (Subset Generator). We define the Subset Generator (SG) as the sequence of variables
across all dimensional components in a subset, regardless of the specific values assigned to the variables in
the dictionary.
   The sequence of variables in a single dimension D is called PathD . We use the term SG because we
can generate every element in the subset using the sequence of variables, the corresponding values stored in
the dictionary, and the Morton Coordinates of the first number in the subset. We observe that all the SG,
regardless of the dimension or subset size, share the following properties:

 (a) Unique Paths: Each path has a distinct sequence of variables, meaning no two paths can share the
     same variables sequence.

 (b) Cyclic Structure: When the last element of the subset is reached, the sequence of variables that
     connects it to the first element always is X, common to all paths, creating a cyclic structure. These
     are referred to as trivial variables, as they are common to all SG and are sometimes omitted. When
     not omitted, they are conventionally placed at the beginning of the SG.

 (c) Symmetric Structure: In the middle of every path sequence, the variable X appears consistently
     across all paths. The variables that follow this point mirror the variables that preceded it.

5     Contributions, Future Work and Conclusion
5.1     Contributions
This research, primarily focused on 2D and 3D, can be divided into three main contributions:

    1. Development of a projection algorithm: First, we developed an algorithm to project the elements
       in XD
           K onto an n-dimensional sphere.

    2. Analysis of subset structures and properties: We focused on subsets as our primary object of
       study, observing that, for any D and K, the sum of elements in a subset is always ΨD  K or a multiple
       of it. Furthermore, we observed that subset sizes corresponding to elements with the same radius on
       an n-dimensional sphere are consistently 2D or a multiple of 2D . We introduced the ϕ function, which
                                                              D
       projects a point in XSD to its equivalent position in XB . Additionally, through group actions using
       the matrix representation of both group actions and Morton coordinates, we demonstrated that each
       point in a subset could be derived from a single element via symmetries and rotations. This rotation
       operation, together with previous findings, enabled us to reinterpret each subset as a collection of
       orthotopes, O, where each O contributes to a sum of ΨD K.

    3. Identification of structural patterns and isomorphisms: Finally, to understand the origins of
       these patterns, we observed that all subsets share the same internal structure, which we call SG.
       Additionally, the dictionary associated with any SG exhibits structural patterns as shown in the
       canonical graphs. We conclude by hypothesizing that all subsets using the same number of variables,
       regardless of subset size or dimension, are isomorphic.

5.2     Future Work
Key questions for future work include why all the SG with the same number of variables, regardless of
dimension and subset size, appear to be isomorphic, why certain subsets, such as the subset with 40 elements,
do not exist in dimension 3 while the subset with 48 elements does, or why the sum of all subset elements is
ΨDK or a multiple of it. Another potential direction is to pursue formal mathematical proofs for the observed
patterns, which have so far been based on empirical results without rigorous mathematical justification.
Lastly, expanding this research to include higher dimensions, beyond the current focus on dimensions 2 and
3, offers another direction for future study.

5.3     Conclusion
To conclude, we recognize that numerous facets of the circular projection of the n-dimensional Z-curve
remain to be explored. While this work lays only a foundational piece, it serves as a stepping stone for
further research. Refinements in the current methodology will be essential for building upon this base to
enhance our knowledge of this object and its potential implications in other areas.
