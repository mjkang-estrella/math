                                                 Abstract. Given a group G acting faithfully on a set S, one gets a simple group denoted
                                                 SVG , called a twisted Brin–Thompson group. In this paper we drop the faithfulness as-
                                                 sumption, and get an abstract version of a twisted Brin–Thompson group SVG . While the

                                                 resulting group is not simple, since SVG surjects onto SVG/ ker(G↷S) , we prove that every
                                                 proper normal subgroup of SVG lies in the kernel of this surjection, so SVG is “relatively
                                                 simple”. The advantage is that now we can prove that every finitely presented simple
                                                 group embeds in a finitely presented abstract twisted Brin–Thompson group intersecting
                                                 this kernel trivially. In particular, if the Boone–Higman conjecture is true, then so is a
                                                 related conjectural characterization of groups with solvable word problem, arising purely
                                                 in the world of twisted Brin–Thompson groups. We also prove a variety of additional
                                                 results about abstract twisted Brin–Thompson groups, some of which are new even in
                                                 the faithful case: they are all uniformly perfect, have property NL and property FW∞ ,
                                                 are boundedly acyclic and ℓ2 -invisible, and are C ∗ -simple as soon as they have trivial
                                                 amenable radical. Along the way we formulate a new general criterion for ℓ2 -invisibility
                                                 that is interesting in its own right.




                                                                                  1. Introduction
                                             Twisted Brin–Thompson groups, introduced by Belk and the third author [BZ22] fol-
                                          lowing the non-twisted case due to Brin [Bri04], are a family of simple groups that have
                                          emerged recently as having outsized importance. For example, with Belk and Hyde the first
                                          and third authors proved that every finitely presented simple group that is mixed identity-
                                          free embeds in a finitely presented twisted Brin–Thompson group [BFFHZ]. Also, twisted
                                          Brin–Thompson groups reveal that every finitely generated group quasi-isometrically em-
                                          beds in a finitely generated simple group [BZ22, Corollary C].
                                             The input to constructing a twisted Brin–Thompson group SVG is a faithful action of
                                          a group G on a set S. If C denotes the binary Cantor set with the defining action of
                                          Thompson’s group V , there is a natural action of the permutational wreath product V ≀S G
                                          on C S . Then SVG can be defined as the topological full group of this action. Despite this
                                          definition as a group of homeomorphisms, a large portion of the toolbox to study twisted
                                          Brin–Thompson groups is purely combinatorial. Over the years, it has become clear that,
                                          from this combinatorial point of view, the requirement of faithfulness is both frustrating
                                          and also not entirely necessary for some purposes.

   The goal of this paper is therefore to introduce “abstract” twisted Brin–Thompson
groups SVG for G ↷ S not necessarily faithful (Definition 2.12), analyze the properties
that remain true in the non-faithful case, and deduce some relations to the Boone–Higman
conjecture. In case S is a point, this recovers the labeled Thompson group V (G), which
was essentially introduced by Thompson in [Tho80], see also [WWZZ25, Subsection 1.5]
for a more modern treatment.
1.1. Relative simplicity. While our abstract twisted Brin–Thompson groups are not
simple, outside the faithful case, they are what we call relatively simple, as we now explain.
Definition 1.1 (Normal pair). By a normal pair (G, N ) we mean a group G together with
a normal subgroup N ≤ G. Call (G, N ) proper if N is proper in G. Call (G, N ) finitely
generated/presented if G is, with no assumption on N .
Definition 1.2 (Relatively simple). Call a proper normal pair (G, N ) relatively simple if
every proper normal subgroup of G is contained in N . Call the group G relatively simple
if (G, N ) is relatively simple for some proper normal subgroup N , and in this case call N
the largest normal subgroup of G.
   If (G, N ) is relatively simple then G/N is simple. In fact, a finitely generated pair (G, N )
is relatively simple if and only if N is the unique normal subgroup of G such that G/N is
simple (Lemma 3.2).
   Our first main result is that abstract twisted Brin–Thompson groups are always relatively
simple.
Theorem A (Theorem 3.6). Every abstract twisted Brin–Thompson group is relatively
simple. More precisely, every proper normal subgroup of the abstract twisted Brin–Thompson
group SVG lies in the kernel of the map SVG → SVG/ ker(G↷S) .
    The canonical kernel in SVG is defined to be
                             SKG := ker(SVG → SVG/ ker(G↷S) ),
where (G/ ker(G ↷ S)) ↷ S is the action induced by G ↷ S. Thus, Theorem A says that
the normal pair (SVG , SKG ) is relatively simple.

1.2. Finiteness properties. A group is of type F n if it has a classifying space with finite
n-skeleton, and of type F ∞ if it is of type F n for all n. All groups are of type F 0 , type F 1
is equivalent to finite generation, and type F 2 is equivalent to finite presentability. There
has been much interest in studying finiteness properties of Thompson-like groups over the
years [BG84, Bro87, BFM+ 16, SWZ19, LISW], and here we inspect finiteness properties
of abstract twisted Brin–Thompson groups. First let us define the following family of
properties of group actions.
Definition 1.3. Let G be a group acting on a non-empty set S. For n ∈ N, we say the
action is of type [An ] if the following hold:
     (i) G is of type F n .
    (ii) For any finite T ⊆ S, StabG (T ) is of type F n−|T | .
                        ABSTRACT TWISTED BRIN–THOMPSON GROUPS                                    3

   (iii) The diagonal action of G on S n has finitely many orbits.
The action is of type [A∞ ] if it is of type [An ] for all n ∈ N. If an action of type [An ] (with
n ∈ N ∪ {∞}) is moreover faithful, we say that it is of type (An ).
  The use of brackets versus parentheses is inspired by the notation of Bader–Sauer for
higher Kazhdan properties [BS]. Note that the condition in the second item is trivially
satisfied for any T ⊆ S with |T | ≥ n. Actions of type [An ] have an established tight
connection to finiteness properties, thanks to permutational wreath products. Indeed, for
an infinite group B of type F n , the permutational wreath product B ≀S G is of type F n if
and only if the action G ↷ S is of type [An ] [dC06, BdCK15, FFKLZ25].
  It was shown in [Zar24] that for G acting faithfully on S, the twisted Brin–Thompson
group SVG is finitely presented if and only if the (faithful) action G ↷ S is of type (A2 ).
(This was mostly called type (A) in [Zar24] and in subsequent work, where the focus
was only on finite presentability, though “type (An )” was also introduced in [Zar24, Re-
mark 2.8].) Moreover, SVG is finitely generated if and only if G ↷ S is of type (A1 ), and
type F ∞ if and only if G ↷ S is of type (A∞ ) [BZ22, Zar24]. We prove that these same
results hold in the non-faithful case:
Theorem B (Theorem 4.1). Let G be a group acting on a non-empty set S. Then SVG
is finitely generated if and only if G ↷ S is of type [A1 ], finitely presented if and only if
G ↷ S is of type [A2 ], and type F ∞ if and only if G ↷ S is of type [A∞ ].
   An interesting consequence is that if SVG is finitely presented, then its simple quotient
SVG/ ker(G↷S) is finitely presented if and only if G/ ker(G ↷ S) is (Corollary 4.2).
   As for the higher finiteness properties, we conjecture that SVG is of type F n if and only
if G ↷ S is of type [An ]. This is even open for G ↷ S faithful [BZ22, Conjecture H]
(outside the n = 1, 2, ∞ cases). In the faithful case, [Zar24, Section 4] makes clear that
one can achieve SVG being of type F n with stronger hypotheses than G ↷ S being of
type (An ). For example, for each n there exists k ≥ n such that if G and StabG (S ′ ) are of
type F n for all S ′ ⊆ S with |S ′ | ≤ k and the diagonal action of G on S k has finitely many
orbits, then SVG is of type F n . This is likely true in the non-faithful case as well, but we
will not pursue this here, since it would be better to be able to weaken the hypotheses to
G ↷ S being of type [An ].
1.3. Embeddings. Recall the well-known Boone–Higman conjecture first posed in [BH74],
and the related permutational Boone–Higman conjecture, posed for example in [Zar24]:
Conjecture 1.4 (Boone–Higman (BH)). Every finitely generated group with solvable word
problem embeds in a finitely presented simple group.
Conjecture 1.5 (Permutational Boone–Higman (PBH)). Every finitely generated group
with solvable word problem embeds in a group admitting an action of type (A2 ) (equivalently
embeds in a finitely presented simple twisted Brin–Thompson group).
  Here the “equivalently” in the parenthetical is thanks to [Zar24, BFFHZ].
  One of the main interests in the Boone–Higman conjecture is that it would provide
an elegant algebraic characterization for solvability of the word problem: indeed, an
4                    F. FOURNIER-FACIO, X. WU, AND M. C. B. ZAREMSKY

easy algorithm that goes back to Kuznetsov [Kuz58] shows that subgroups of finitely
presented simple groups have solvable word problem. On the other hand, the permu-
tational Boone–Higman conjecture proposes to replace the algebraic property of being
simple with the more geometric property of the existence of a certain permutation rep-
resentation, and also highlights the outsized importance of Thompson-like groups among
finitely presented simple groups. Note that (PBH)⇒(BH) for any given group. In addition
to the question of whether either of these conjectures is true, it is also a major question
whether (PBH)⇔(BH), or equivalently whether twisted Brin–Thompson groups are univer-
sal among finitely presented simple groups. By now the Boone–Higman conjecture is known
for many families of groups, e.g., Q-linear groups [Sco84, Zar25a], Aut(Fn ) [BFFHZ], hy-
perbolic groups [BBMZ23a], Baumslag–Solitar and free-by-cyclic groups [BLIW], and more
[BBMZ23b]; in all these cases, the groups not only satisfy (BH) but even (PBH).
  Here we formulate “relative” versions of these conjectures and prove a surprising con-
nection with the classical conjectures (Theorem C).
Definition 1.6 (Embeds, sharply embeds). Given two normal pairs (G, N ) and (G′ , N ′ ),
we say (G, N ) embeds in (G′ , N ′ ) if there exists an injective homomorphism ι : G ,→ G′
such that ι(G) ∩ N ′ = ι(N ). We say a group Γ sharply embeds in a normal pair (G, N ) if
the normal pair (Γ, {1}) embeds in (G, N ). We say a group Γ sharply embeds in a relatively
simple group G if Γ sharply embeds in the normal pair (G, N ) for N the largest normal
subgroup of G.
Conjecture 1.7 (Relative Boone–Higman (relBH)). Let Γ be a finitely generated group
with solvable word problem. Then Γ sharply embeds in a finitely presented relatively simple
group.
Conjecture 1.8 (Relative permutational Boone–Higman (relPBH)). Let Γ be a finitely
generated group with solvable word problem. Then there exists a group G with a type [A2 ]
action on a set S such that Γ sharply embeds in (G, ker(G ↷ S)) (and hence Γ sharply
embeds in the finitely presented relatively simple group SVG ).
   Here the “hence” in the parenthetical follows from Theorems A and B and Lemma 5.2. It
would be interesting to know whether the converse of this parenthetical is true, i.e., whether
every finitely presented abstract twisted Brin–Thompson group sharply embeds in a normal
pair of the form (G, ker(G ↷ S)) for some G ↷ S of type [A2 ] (see Question 5.7).
   As in the case of the classical Boone–Higman conjecture, these conjectures would also
provide an elegant characterization of solvability of the word problem, thanks to essentially
the same algorithm; in fact the relative Boone–Higman conjecture, the weakest of the four,
is a natural strengthening of a characterization of groups with solvable word problem that
follows from the Boone–Higman–Thompson theorem, and that we present in Appendix A
(see Proposition A.4). In particular the converses of these conjectures are true. We should
emphasize that the desired embeddings in these relative conjectures need to be sharp.
Indeed, every finitely presented group G (regardless of the word problem) embeds non-
sharply in a group admitting a type [A2 ] action, namely G acting trivially on a point.
   Clearly we have the following implications for a given group:
                      ABSTRACT TWISTED BRIN–THOMPSON GROUPS                                5

                                    (PBH) ⇒ (BH)
                                       ⇓         ⇓
                                   (relPBH)⇒ (relBH)

  Our main result in this vein is that in fact (BH)⇒(relPBH).
Theorem C (Theorem 5.1). If a group satisfies the Boone–Higman conjecture then it
satisfies the relative permutational Boone–Higman conjecture.
   We emphasize that the relative permutational Boone–Higman conjecture is a statement
entirely about embedding in a group admitting a certain action, with no a priori reference
to (relative) simplicity or Thompson-like groups. Theorem C and Lemma 5.2 imply that
if a given group satisfies the Boone–Higman conjecture, meaning it embeds in a finitely
presented simple group that a priori has nothing to do with Thompson groups, then in fact
it sharply embeds in a finitely presented relatively simple group that is definitively from
the Thompson world, namely an abstract twisted Brin–Thompson group.
   Since it is a major question whether (BH)⇒(PBH), an obvious followup question to The-
orem C is whether (relPBH)⇒(PBH), and relatedly whether (relBH)⇒(BH). See Ques-
tion 5.9 for a more precise formulation.
1.4. Other properties. Finally, we investigate a number of other properties of abstract
twisted Brin–Thompson groups. These have all already been studied to some extent in the
special cases of faithful twisted Brin–Thompson groups and/or labeled Thompson groups,
though some of our results here are new even in those cases.
   First, we prove that abstract twisted Brin–Thompson groups are always uniformly per-
fect (Theorem 6.2) and have property NL (Theorem 6.4), meaning that they cannot act
on a hyperbolic space with a loxodromic element. Uniform perfectness was previously
known for faithful twisted Brin–Thompson groups [BFFG25] and labeled Thompson groups
[WWZZ25]. Property NL was known in the faithful case [BFFG25], but to the best of our
knowledge is even new for labeled Thompson groups. Thanks to a criterion of Genevois
[Gen19], we deduce that abstract twisted Brin–Thompson groups have property FW∞ ,
meaning that every action on a finite-dimensional CAT(0) cube complex fixes a point.
   Next we look at homological properties, and prove that all abstract twisted Brin–
Thompson groups are boundedly acyclic (Theorem 7.9) and ℓ2 -invisible (Corollary 7.15).
Bounded acyclicity was already known in the faithful case and for labeled Thompson groups
[WWZZ25], while ℓ2 -invisibility was only previously known for labeled Thompson groups
with non-amenable label group [WWZZ25] and for (non-twisted) Brin–Thompson groups
[ST14, Thu16]. The key is a new criterion for ℓ2 -invisibility (Theorem 7.13) that is inter-
esting in its own right. Let us state it in the special case of homeomorphism groups, in
which case it parallels a criterion for C ∗ -simplicity by Le Boudec–Matte Bon [LBMB18].
Theorem D (Corollary 7.16). Let Γ be a group acting faithfully by homeomorphisms on
a Hausdorff space X. Suppose that for every non-empty open set U , the rigid stabilizer
RStabΓ (U ) is non-amenable. Then Γ is ℓ2 -invisible.

ℓ -invisible (Corollary 7.17).
   Finally, we characterize precisely when an abstract twisted Brin–Thompson group SVG
is C ∗ -simple. The answer (Theorem 8.1) is that it happens if and only if SVG has trivial
amenable radical, which is equivalent to just requiring ker(G ↷ S) to have no amenable
normal subgroups. It is curious that neither G nor ker(G ↷ S) need to be C ∗ -simple, for
SVG to be C ∗ -simple.

Outline. This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we define abstract twisted Brin–
Thompson groups. In Section 3 we prove Theorem A, that they are relatively simple. In
Section 4 we prove Theorem B regarding finiteness properties. In Section 5 we investigate
embedding properties and prove Theorem C. In Section 6 we investigate geometric prop-
erties, including actions on hyperbolic spaces. In Section 7 we look at homological prop-
erties, and prove that every abstract twisted Brin–Thompson group is boundedly acyclic
and ℓ2 -invisible. Finally, in Section 8 we characterize precisely which abstract twisted
Brin–Thompson groups are C ∗ -simple. In Appendix A we prove a strengthening of the
Boone–Higman–Thompson theorem (Proposition A.4) that has an interesting connection
to the relative Boone–Higman conjecture.

                           2. Construction of the groups
  In this section we construct our abstract twisted Brin–Thompson groups. This will
proceed in stages.

2.1. Brin–Thompson groups. First let us recall the construction of the (non-twisted)
Brin–Thompson group SV , for S a non-empty set. We will use the setup and notation from
[BZ22], where the twisted case was introduced; see also [Zar25b] for a shorter introduction.
Let C = {0, 1}N be the Cantor set and C S the space of functions from S to C with the
usual product topology. Let {0, 1}∗ be the set of finite words in {0, 1}. Given a function
ψ : S → {0, 1}∗ such that ψ(s) = ∅ (the empty word) for all but finitely many s ∈ S,
denote by B(ψ) the basic open set
                 B(ψ) := {κ ∈ C S | ψ(s) is a prefix of κ(s) for all s ∈ S}.
Call any such B(ψ) a dyadic brick. For any such ψ, the canonical homeomorphism
                                      hψ : C S → B(ψ)
is defined by
                                  hψ (κ)(s) := ψ(s) · κ(s),
                        ABSTRACT TWISTED BRIN–THOMPSON GROUPS                                   7

where · denotes concatenation. Given two such ψ and φ, we get the canonical homeomor-
phism
                           hψ→φ := hφ ◦ h−1
                                         ψ : B(ψ) → B(φ).

Definition 2.1 (Brin–Thompson group). The Brin–Thompson group SV is the group of
all homeomorphisms h of C S constructed as follows. Partition the domain C S into finitely
many dyadic bricks B(ψ1 ), . . . , B(ψn ), partition the range C S into the same number of
dyadic bricks B(φ1 ), . . . , B(φn ), and define h to map each B(ψi ) to B(φi ) via the canonical
homeomorphism hψi →φi .
  Intuitively, SV is the group of all “piecewise prefix replacements” of C S .
2.2. Brin–Thompson groupoids. It is often convenient to work with the Brin–Thompson
groupoid SV, which we recall now. For m ∈ N let C S (m) be the disjoint union of m copies
of C S .
Definition 2.2 (Brin–Thompson groupoid, rank, corank). The Brin–Thompson groupoid
SV is the groupoid of all homeomorphisms from C S (m) to C S (n) (m, n ∈ N) obtained
by partitioning the domain and range into the same number of dyadic bricks (meaning
dyadic bricks within each copy of C S ) and mapping the domain ones to the range ones
via canonical homeomorphisms. An element with this domain and range has rank n and
corank m.
  In particular, SV is the subgroup of SV consisting of elements with rank and corank 1.
Let us recall some basic elements and operations on SV.
Definition 2.3 (Simple split). For s ∈ S, the simple split xs is the element of SV with
corank 1 and rank 2 given by partitioning C S into B(ψ0s ) and B(ψ1s ), where ψis (i = 0, 1)
sends s to i and s′ ̸= s to ∅, and mapping C S to C S (2) by sending B(ψ0s ) to the first copy
of C S and B(ψ1s ) to the second copy, both via canonical homeomorphisms.
Definition 2.4 (Permutation). For m ∈ N and σ ∈ Σm , the permutation pσ ∈ SV is the
element with rank and corank m that permutes the copies of C S in C S (m) via σ. Write
S(m) for the copy of Σm in SV consisting of all the pσ for σ ∈ Σm .
Definition 2.5 (Direct sum). Given h, h′ ∈ SV, say h has corank m and rank n and h′
has corank m′ and rank n′ , the direct sum h ⊕ h′ is the element of SV with corank m + m′
and rank n + n′ that sends the first m copies of C S in the domain to the first n copies
of C S in the range via h, and sends the last m′ copies of C S in the domain to the last n′
copies of C S in the range via h′ .
  Certain partitions into dyadic bricks are of particular interest, namely those that are
encoded into the leaves of a multicolored tree. Let us explain all this.
Definition 2.6 (Multicolored forest/tree). Consider the subset of SV that contains all
simple splits and is closed under compositions and direct sums. The elements of this
subset are called multicolored forests, and the elements of corank 1 are called multicolored
trees. When we want to encode the dependence on S we may say S-multicolored. The roots

of a multicolored forest are the copies of C S in its domain, and its leaves are the copies of
C S in its range.
   This definition is slightly different than the one in [BZ22]. There, permutations were
also allowed. For our purposes it is more convenient to define multicolored forests this way.

C S (n).

Lemma 2.11. Given two multicolored forests F, F ′ with the same corank, there exist mul-
ticolored forests E, E ′ and a permutation σ such that E ◦ F = pσ ◦ E ′ ◦ F ′ .

                                     3. Relative simplicity
  Recall from the introduction that a group is relatively simple if it has a proper normal
subgroup, called the largest, containing every proper normal subgroup. In this section we
look at some examples and non-examples, and then prove Theorem A.
                      ABSTRACT TWISTED BRIN–THOMPSON GROUPS                               13

3.1. Examples and non-examples. The first example of relatively simple groups is sim-
ple groups, with largest normal subgroup the trivial one. Beyond simple groups, there are
many existing examples of groups from the Thompson world that are relatively simple
but not simple. For example, in the braided Thompson group bV [Bri07, Deh06] and the
labeled Thompson groups V (G) [Tho80], every proper normal subgroup lies in the kernel
of the canonical map to V [Zar18a, WWZZ25].
   Central extensions of simple groups are often relatively simple. For example, for even
n ≥ 2 and most fields K, the simple [Rot95, Theorems 8.14 and 8.23] group P SLn (K) has
as a central extension SLn (K), which is relatively simple with largest normal subgroup
{±I}. In a different context, in the lift T of Thompson’s group T to the line [GS87],
every proper normal subgroup lies in the kernel of the canonical map to T [FFLZ24b,
Lemma 3.1]; other related examples are discussed further in [FFLZ24b].
   Examples also arise from the world of lamplighter groups, or permutational wreath
products.

  Before turning to non-examples, let us give a characterization of relative simplicity.
(Here we continue to follow the convention that the trivial group is not simple.)
Lemma 3.2. A finitely generated group G is relatively simple if and only if G has a unique
normal subgroup with simple quotient.
Proof. Suppose that (G, N ) is a finitely generated relatively simple pair. Because there is
no intermediate normal subgroup N < M < G, the group G/N is simple. Moreover, if
G/M is another simple quotient of G, then M is contained in N , hence G/M surjects onto
G/N , so by simplicity M = N .
   Next, suppose that N < G is the unique normal subgroup such that G/N is simple, and
let M < G be a proper normal subgroup of G. Recall that every finitely generated group
14                     F. FOURNIER-FACIO, X. WU, AND M. C. B. ZAREMSKY

has a simple quotient; this is obvious if G has a finite quotient, and otherwise it follows
from a Zorn’s lemma argument that goes back at least to Higman [Hig51]. In particular
G/M has a simple quotient, which is then also a simple quotient of G, hence it must be
G/N . This shows that M < N and concludes the proof.                                     □
Remark 3.3. We only used the assumption that G is finitely generated in the second part,
so it is still true that a relatively simple group has a unique simple quotient. The converse
is not true however: the group Q has no simple quotient, which shows that e.g., Q × Z/2 is
a group with a unique normal subgroup with simple quotient (namely Q × {0}), but which
is not relatively simple.
   We should also mention another prominent class of simple groups, which likely have rela-
tively simple generalizations, namely commutator subgroups of Scott–Röver–Nekrashevych
groups, first developed by Scott in [Sco84], and brought to prominence by Röver [Röv99]
and Nekrashevych [Nek04]. Here the input is a faithful self-similar action of a group G
on a rooted d-regular tree, and the output is a group denoted Vd (G), whose commutator
subgroup is always simple. If we relax the assumption that the self-similar action of G be
faithful, then we still get a group Vd (G), and presumably now its commutator subgroup is
relatively simple instead of simple. It is not clear to us however what sort of utility these
groups have for investigating any Boone–Higman-related questions, so we will not work
out any details here.
     Let us also mention some broad classes of non-examples of relative simplicity.
Example 3.4. If G is a group surjecting onto Z, then it is never relatively simple, because
it has distinct simple quotients: Z/2 and Z/3, for example. Similarly a group that surjects
onto a finite abelian group that is not cyclic of prime power order, is not relatively simple
(note that cyclic groups of prime power order are relatively simple).
   Hence the abelianization of a finitely generated relatively simple group must be cyclic
of prime power order. Note that it does not need to be trivial, as witnessed by some
Higman–Thompson groups Vn [Hig74].
Example 3.5. If G is acylindrically hyperbolic, then G admits an infinite simple quotient,
and in fact we can prescribe the quotient to be injective on any given finite set, after
modding out the finite radical [CFF, Theorem 3.5]. This implies that G has infinitely
many simple quotients with distinct kernel, hence G is not relatively simple. In particular
this applies to non-elementary hyperbolic and relatively hyperbolic groups, mapping class
groups of non-exceptional surfaces, and outer automorphism groups of finitely generated
non-abelian free groups [Osi18].

                               4. Finiteness properties
   In this section we establish when an abstract twisted Brin–Thompson group SVG is
finitely generated, finitely presented, and type F ∞ . These were all previously worked out
for faithful twisted Brin–Thompson groups in [BZ22, Zar24]; the main result of this section
is that the analogous results hold in the non-faithful case.
Theorem 4.1 (Theorem B). Let G be a group acting on a non-empty set S. Then:
    (i) SVG is finitely generated if and only if G ↷ S is of type [A1 ].
   (ii) SVG is finitely presented if and only if G ↷ S is of type [A2 ].
  (iii) SVG is of type F ∞ if and only if G ↷ S is of type [A∞ ].
  The most interesting and important of these is item (ii), as Section 5 will make clear.
The rest of this section is devoted to proving Theorem 4.1. First let us record the following
easy consequence of item (ii).
Corollary 4.2. Let G be a group acting on a non-empty set S. Let K = ker(G ↷ S). If
SVG is finitely presented, then the simple group SVG/K is finitely presented if and only if
G/K is.
Proof. Suppose SVG is finitely presented, so the action G ↷ S is of type [A2 ]. Hence G is
finitely presented, each StabG (s) is finitely generated, and G ↷ S × S has finitely many
orbits. Passing to the quotient G/K acting on S, we have that the point stabilizers are
still finitely generated and there are still finitely many orbits in S × S, so G/K ↷ S is of
type (A2 ) if and only if G/K is finitely presented. But SVG/K being finitely presented is
also equivalent to G/K ↷ S being of type (A2 ) (by Theorem 4.1(ii), or by [Zar24]), and
we are done.                                                                              □

Definition 4.6 (Elementary, spectrum). We define what it means for a multicolored forest
to be elementary, and what its spectrum is, as follows. The identity 1m is elementary for
all m, with spectrum ∅. Now given a multicolored forest F with spectrum S ′ ⊆ S, say
F has corank m ≥ 2 and rank n, and given an element s ∈ S \ S ′ , we declare that the
multicolored forest F ◦ (1i ⊕ xs ⊕ 1m−i−2 ) (0 ≤ i ≤ m − 2) is elementary with spectrum
S ′ ∪ {s}. Call an elementary multicolored forest k-elementary if its spectrum has size at
most k.
  Intuitively, a multicolored forest is elementary if, viewed as a homeomorphism, each
input is “sent through” at most one simple split xs of each color s ∈ S. The spectrum is
the set of colors that are actually used. For example, returning to Figures 1 and 2, the
multicolored tree in Figure 1 is not elementary since some inputs are sent through two
copies of xr , and the multicolored tree in Figure 2 is elementary with spectrum {r, b}.
Definition 4.7 (Stein complex, short/long edge). Let G be a group acting faithfully on a
set S. The Stein complex SXG is the flag simplicial complex constructed as follows. The
vertices are the cosets W(m)h for h ∈ SVG an element with corank 1 and rank m. There is
an edge from W(m)h to W(n)(F ◦ h) for each elementary multicolored forest with corank
m and rank n. The complex SXG is now the flag complex with this 1-skeleton. Call an
edge short if the ranks of its endpoints differ by 1, and long otherwise. Let SXG (k) be
the flag subcomplex of SXG with the same vertex set but only including the edges from
W(m)h to W(n)(F ◦ h) where F is k-elementary. Finally, let SXGn and SXGn (k) be the
                       ABSTRACT TWISTED BRIN–THOMPSON GROUPS                               19

full subcomplexes of SXG and SXG (k), respectively, spanned by all vertices with rank at
most n.
   There is a (right) action of SVG on SXG via right multiplication in SVG . This action
stabilizes each of the subcomplexes defined by restricting the ranks of vertices and/or sizes
of spectra of multicolored forests being used to define edges.
   For G ↷ S faithful, in [BZ22, Zar24] some results were obtained on the connectivity
of these complexes. The following collects together these results, phrased in a convenient
way.
Proposition 4.8 (Connectivity). Assume G ↷ S is faithful. Then the following hold.
     (i) The complex SXG is contractible.
    (ii) For all n ∈ N there exists M ∈ N such that for all m ≥ M the complex SXGm is
         (n − 1)-connected.
   (iii) For all k ≥ n the complex SXG (k) is (n − 1)-connected.
   (iv) For all k ≥ n and all m, if SXGm is (n − 1)-connected then so is SXGm (k).
Proof. Item (i) is [BZ22, Proposition 5.6]. Item (ii) follows by combining [BZ22, Proposi-

(iv) are [Zar24, Proposition 4.4].                                                        □

                                   5. Embedding results
  In this section we connect abstract twisted Brin–Thompson groups to the Boone–Higman
conjecture.
Theorem 5.1 (Theorem C). Let Γ be a finitely presented simple group. Then there exists a
group G with a type [A2 ] action on a set S such that Γ sharply embeds in (G, ker(G ↷ S)).
Hence Γ sharply embeds in the finitely presented (relatively simple) abstract twisted Brin–
Thompson group SVG .
     First let us record the following easy lemma:
Lemma 5.2. Let G ↷ S be a group action with kernel K. Then the normal pair (G, K)
sharply embeds in SVG .
Proof. Send g ∈ G to [·, id, g, ·], where · is the trivial multicolored tree. To see that this
gives the desired embedding, we just have to prove that if [·, id, g, ·] ∈ SKG then g ∈ K.
Indeed, if g ̸∈ K then the action of g on C S by permuting coordinates is non-trivial. □
Proof of Theorem 5.1. We follow the strategy from [BFFHZ]. Let G = AutΓ (Γ ∗ Fn ) be
the group of automorphisms of the free product Γ ∗ Fn , for Fn the free group of rank
n ≥ 2, that restrict to the identity on the Γ factor. Let S = HomΓ (Γ ∗ Fn , Γ) be the set
of homomorphisms Γ ∗ Fn → Γ that restrict to the identity on the Γ factor. We have an
action of G on S by precomposition. Since Γ is simple, this action is highly transitive
[BFFHZ, Proposition 2.3], hence G ↷ S × S has finitely many orbits. Since Γ is finitely
generated, each StabG (s) for s ∈ S is finitely generated by [BFFHZ, Proposition 2.6].
Since Γ is finitely presented and has trivial center, G is finitely presented by [BFFHZ,
Proposition 1.1]. Thus G ↷ S is of type [A2 ]. The group Γ embeds into G as the Γ-
translations of some fixed generator of Fn . The only remaining thing we have to prove is
                       ABSTRACT TWISTED BRIN–THOMPSON GROUPS                                25

that the image of this embedding intersects the kernel of the action trivially, i.e., that the
restriction of G ↷ S to Γ is faithful. In fact it is free, as we will show. Let ϕ ∈ S send
our fixed generator of Fn to γ0 . Now for any γ ∈ Γ, viewed in G as above, we have that
ϕ ◦ γ sends our fixed generator of Fn to γ0 γ. Thus ϕ ◦ γ cannot equal ϕ unless γ = 1,
so we conclude that the action of Γ on S is free. The last statement is immediate from
Theorem 4.1(ii) and Lemma 5.2.                                                              □
Remark 5.3. The action G ↷ S constructed in the proof above is of type [A2 ] but not of
type [A∞ ] in general, for example when Γ is a (finitely presented, simple) Burger–Mozes
group [FFKLZ25, Corollary 1.6].
   We should emphasize that one advantage of allowing non-faithful actions is that, a
priori, the simple quotient obtained by taking SVG modulo its largest subgroup is not
necessarily finitely presented, and so even though sharpness of the embedding induces an
embedding into the quotient, this quotient is not necessarily useful for Boone–Higman-
related purposes. (That being said, we do not know an explicit example where it fails, see
Question 5.8.) This is in contrast to other examples of relatively simple groups, for instance
in the braided Thompson group or labeled Thompson groups the quotient by the largest
subgroup is V , which is finitely presented. Also, for the relatively simple lamplighters in
Lemma 3.1, if the wreath product is finitely presented then so is the quotient by the largest
normal subgroup, since the quotient splits.
   It is not difficult to see that the converse of the (relative) (permutational) Boone–Higman
conjecture is true, in fact the relative Boone–Higman conjecture is a natural strengthening
of a characterization of groups with solvable word problem that follows from the Boone–
Higman–Thompson theorem, and that we present in the appendix (Proposition A.4). Note
that a finitely presented relatively simple group itself may have unsolvable word problem,
for example if |S| = 1 and G has unsolvable word problem, then SVG has unsolvable word
problem (but any group sharply embedding in it has solvable word problem). As a remark,
the braided Thompson group bV is an example of a finitely presented relatively simple
group with solvable word problem for which the Boone–Higman conjecture (and even the
relative Boone–Higman conjecture) remains open.
  Let us record a few observations about the relative permutational Boone–Higman (relPBH)
conjecture, and actions of type [A2 ].
Observation 5.4. If a group admits an action of type [An ] then it admits a transitive
action of type [An ].
Proof. The restriction of the action to a single orbit still has finitely many orbits of n-
tuples.                                                                                  □
   Interestingly, trying to use the analogous proof for type (An ) does not always work, since
the restriction of a faithful action to a single orbit need not be faithful.
   Recall that the bi-index of a subgroup H of a group G is the cardinality of the set
of double cosets HgH (g ∈ G). The following gives a purely group theoretic equivalent
condition to satisfying the relative permutational Boone–Higman conjecture.
26                        F. FOURNIER-FACIO, X. WU, AND M. C. B. ZAREMSKY

Lemma 5.5. The following are equivalent for a group Γ:
       (i) Γ satisfies the relative permutational Boone–Higman conjecture, i.e., there is a
           group G with a type [A2 ] action on a set S such that Γ sharply embeds in the
           normal pair (G, ker(G ↷ S)).
      (ii) The same as item (i) but moreover the action is transitive.
     (iii) There exists a finitely presented group G and a finitely generated subgroup H ≤ G
           with finite bi-index, such that Γ embeds in G with every non-trivial element of Γ
           conjugate in G to an element of G \ H.

Proof. First assume (i) holds, so G ↷ S is of type [A2 ] and Γ sharply embeds in (G, ker(G ↷
S)), say via ι : Γ → G. Note that by Observation 5.4, we could assume the action
is transitive by just restricting to a single orbit, but then the embedding of Γ might
no longer be sharp, so more work is needed. Let S1 , . . . , Sn ⊆ S be the G-orbits of
G ↷ S. Consider the action of the finitely presented group Gn on S1 × · · · × Sn via
(g1 , . . . , gn ).(s1 , . . . , sn ) := (g1 .s1 , . . . , gn .sn ), so this action is transitive. Since G ↷ S has
finitely many orbits of pairs, the same is true of this action of Gn on S1 × · · · × Sn . Since
point stabilizers for G ↷ S are finitely generated, the same is true for the new action.
Finally, embed Γ into Gn diagonally via ι in each factor, and notice that since each non-
trivial element of Γ must act non-trivially on some Si , the embedding is sharp relative the
kernel of the action of Gn . This shows (ii).
   Now assume (ii) holds, so G ↷ S is of type [A2 ] and transitive, and Γ sharply embeds
in (G, ker(G ↷ S)). Choose s ∈ S and let H = StabG (s), so H is finitely generated and
has finite bi-index. Since every non-trivial element g of Γ acts non-trivially on S, g can be
conjugated out of H, which confirms (iii).
   Finally, given the assumptions of (iii), let S be the set of cosets G/H with the G-action
by left translation, so the assumptions ensure this action is of type [A2 ]. The kernel of
this action is the intersection of all conjugates of H, so Γ intersects this trivially. Thus, Γ
sharply embeds in (G, ker(G ↷ S)).                                                                              □

   In words, if one wishes to prove (relPBH) for a given group Γ, one should hunt for a
finitely presented group G and a finitely generated subgroup H ≤ G of finite bi-index,
such that Γ admits an “H-escapable” embedding into G. (Here H-escapable means any
non-trivial element of Γ can be conjugated out of H.)

Remark 5.6. The Boone–Higman conjecture ties together two classical results: the Boone–
Higman–Thompson theorem [BH74, Tho80], and Higman’s embedding theorem [Hig61].
Restricting to always talking about finitely generated groups for conciseness, the former
says that every group with solvable word problem embeds in a recursively presented sim-
ple group, and the latter says that every recursively presented group embeds in a finitely
presented group. Thus, one can work out that the Boone–Higman conjecture is equivalent
to a version of the Boone–Higman–Thompson theorem that preserves finite presentability,
and also to a version of the Higman embedding theorem that preserves simplicity (here
one needs the fact, due to Clapham [Cla67], that every group with solvable word problem
                       ABSTRACT TWISTED BRIN–THOMPSON GROUPS                                27

embeds in a finitely presented group with solvable word problem). In fact, twisted Brin–
Thompson groups provide a stronger version of the Boone–Higman–Thompson theorem,
since a finitely generated group G has solvable word problem if and only if the finitely
generated simple group GVG is recursively presented [BBMZ23b, Corollary 4.14], so the
Boone–Higman conjecture is equivalent to always being able to embed such a GVG into a
finitely presented simple group.
  Let us conclude this section by collecting some of the relevant questions that have come
up.
Question 5.7. For SVG a finitely presented abstract twisted Brin–Thompson group, does
the normal pair (SVG , SKG ) sharply embed in a normal pair of the form (G′ , ker(G′ ↷ S ′ ))
for some G′ ↷ S ′ of type [A2 ]?
Question 5.8. Does there exist an example of a group action G ↷ S that is of type [A2 ]
such that G/ ker(G ↷ S) is not finitely presented? (This is equivalent to the induced action
of this quotient on S being not of type (A2 ).)
Question 5.9. For G ↷ S an action of type [A2 ] with kernel K, does there always exist
an action G′ ↷ S ′ of type [A2 ] with kernel K ′ finitely normally generated, such that (G, K)
sharply embeds in (G′ , K ′ )? Does every finitely presented relatively simple (G, N ) sharply
embed in a finitely presented relatively simple (G′ , N ′ ) such that N ′ is finitely normally
generated in G′ ?
  Note that a “yes” answer to the first part of Question 5.9 would tell us that (relPBH)
implies (PBH), and the second part would tell us that (relBH) implies (BH). By Theorem C
the first part would imply that (BH), (relPBH), and (PBH) are all equivalent to each other,
and the second part would imply that (BH), (relPBH), and (relBH) are all equivalent to
each other.

6.2. Actions on hyperbolic spaces. Next, we prove that all abstract twisted Brin–
Thompson groups SVG have property NL, as defined in [BFFG25]. This extends the
faithful case [BFFG25, Proposition 5.23]. To the best of our knowledge, this is new also
for V (G).
   We start with preliminary definitions that are also needed for the statement. Let X be
a (Gromov-)hyperbolic space, let ∂X denote its boundary, and consider the compact space
X ∪ ∂X endowed with its visual topology. If g is an isometry of X, we say that g is
       • elliptic if it has bounded orbits;
       • hyperbolic if {g n x}n∈Z defines a quasi-isometric embedding of Z into X, for some
         (equivalently every) point x ∈ X;
       • parabolic otherwise.
   Given a group G acting on X by isometries, the limit set is
                                     Λ(G) := Gx ∩ ∂X,
where x ∈ X is any point; the definition is independent of this choice. Now g is elliptic
if Λ(⟨g⟩) = ∅, hyperbolic if Λ(⟨g⟩) = {g ±∞ } consists of two points, and parabolic if
Λ(⟨g⟩) = {g ∞ } consists of a single point. These points at infinity are called limit points of
g.
   More generally, group actions on hyperbolic spaces can be classified in terms of their
limit sets. We say that the action of G on X is:
       • elliptic if Λ(G) = ∅, equivalently if G has bounded orbits;
       • horocyclic (or parabolic) if |Λ(G)| = 1, equivalently if G fixes a unique point on
         ∂X and contains no loxodromic element;
       • lineal if |Λ(G)| = 2, equivalently if G contains a loxodromic element and any two
         loxodromic elements have the same limit points; in this case we moreover say that
         the action is oriented if the two points in the limit set are fixed by all of G, and
         non-oriented otherwise;
       • focal (or quasi-parabolic) if |Λ(G)| = ∞ and G has a global fixed point on ∂X,
         equivalently if any two loxodromic elements share a common limit point, but there
         exist pairs of loxodromic elements not sharing both limit points;
       • general type if |Λ(G)| = ∞ and G has no global fixed point on ∂X, equivalently if
         G contains two loxodromic elements with disjoint sets of limit points.
   Any action is of one of these forms, see [Gro87, Section 8.2]. Every group admits an
elliptic action on a point, and every countably infinite group admits a parabolic action
on a hyperbolic space (for finitely generated groups, on combinatorial horoballs on Cayley
graphs [GM08], and for infinitely generated groups, on trees [Ser80, Chapter 6]). All other
actions involve loxodromic elements, which motivates the following definition.
Definition 6.3. A group has Property NL, short for No Loxodromics, if every action on a
hyperbolic space is either elliptic or parabolic.
 Many interesting groups have property NL, such high rank lattices [Hae20, BCFS25] and
many Thompson-like groups [Gen19, BFFG25] (although F -like groups do not [BFFZ], nor
30                    F. FOURNIER-FACIO, X. WU, AND M. C. B. ZAREMSKY

do braided Thompson groups [FFLZ24a]). In particular SVG has property NL when G ↷ S
is faithful [BFFG25, Proposition 5.23]. The faithfulness is important in that proof, since
it is an application of a general criterion that gives property NL for a class of groups of
homeomorphisms of compact Hausdorff spaces [BFFG25, Theorem 5.1]. Nevertheless, we
now prove:
Theorem 6.4. Let G be a group acting on a non-empty set S. Then SVG has property
NL.

6.3. Actions on finite-dimensional CAT(0) cube complexes. We say that a group
has property FW∞ if every action on a finite-dimensional CAT(0) cube complex has a
global fixed point. This was introduced by Barnhill and Chatterji in [BC08] with the
name “property FW”, however since then the name has been used to encompass also
actions on infinite-dimensional CAT(0) cube complexes [Cor], so we use the “property
FW∞ ” convention of [Gen19]. Thanks to a criterion of Genevois [Gen19, Theorem 5.1],
our results on property NL immediately imply:
Corollary 6.6. Let G be a group acting on a non-empty set S. Then SVG has property
FW∞ .
Proof. This follows directly from [Gen19, Theorem 5.1], together with the fact that SVG
has no proper finite index subgroups, by Theorem 3.6; see [BFFG25, Proposition 6.2]. □
Remark 6.7. This invites the question of whether SVG can act on an infinite dimensional
CAT(0) cube complex without a global fixed point, see [Cor] for several equivalent defini-
tions. When |S| = 1, so SVG = V (G), the Stein complex SXG = X(G) (Definition 4.7)
admits a coarser, cubical structure, with respect to which it is a CAT(0) cube complex.
This is often called the Stein–Farley complex (following Farley’s work in [Far03]), and the
action of V (G) on X(G) has unbounded orbits (see the proof of [WWZZ25, Corollary 3.17]).
When G is finite the action is even proper, revealing that V (G) has the Haagerup property
for finite G.
   When |S| ≥ 2 the picture is different. It turns out 2V , hence every SVG for |S| ≥ 2,
contains distorted elements [CS24]. By [Hag23], this means SVG cannot act properly on a
CAT(0) cube complex when |S| ≥ 2. To the best of our knowledge it is open whether 2V
(more generally SVG for |S| ≥ 2) can act with unbounded orbits on an infinite dimensional
CAT(0) cube complex. Relatedly, as far as we know, the Haagerup and Kazhdan properties
are open for 2V .

[Lüc02, Chapter 12]. In this direction, Sauer–Thumann proved that there exist ℓ2 -invisible
groups of type F ∞ , such as Thompson’s group V [ST14]. This was then generalized by
Thumann to a larger class of Thompson-like groups, which includes the Brin–Thompson
groups nV [Thu16].
    In this subsection we prove that all abstract twisted Brin–Thompson groups SVG are
ℓ2 -invisible. This was known in a particularly easy case, namely when G is non-amenable
and S is a point [WWZZ25, Theorem 7.1]; it is new and interesting even in the faithful
case. To do this, we introduce a new dynamical criterion (Theorem 7.13) for a group to
be ℓ2 -invisible.
Definition 7.11 (Deferment system). Let Γ be a group acting by homeomorphisms on a
space X. For each open subset U ⊆ X, let ∆(U ) ≤ Γ be a subgroup. Call ∆ : U 7→ ∆(U )

      • The support of ∆(U ) is contained in U for each U .
      • If U ⊆ U ′ then ∆(U ) ≤ ∆(U ′ ).

      • ⟨∆(U1 ), . . . , ∆(Uk )⟩ is a direct product ∆(U1 ) × · · · × ∆(Uk ) whenever U1 , . . . , Uk
        are pairwise disjoint.

some element of the subgroup. Note that if X is Hausdorff (and not a single point) then
necessarily ∆(∅) = {1}, since ∆(∅) ≤ ∆(U ) ∩ ∆(U ′ ) = {1} for disjoint U, U ′ ̸= ∅. Thus
even though Γ ↷ X need not be faithful, the kernel is not a major player in deferment
systems. On the other hand, note that if g acts trivially on X then g must normalize every
∆(U ), so the kernel is not completely invisible to deferment systems.

                                       8. C ∗ -simplicity
   In this section we prove that an abstract twisted Brin–Thompson group SVG is C ∗ -simple
if and only if it has trivial amenable radical, and characterize precisely when this happens
in terms of the action G ↷ S:
Theorem 8.1. Let G be a group acting on a non-empty set S with kernel K. The following
are equivalent.
      (i) SVG is C ∗ -simple;
     (ii) SVG has trivial amenable radical;
    (iii) There is no non-trivial amenable normal subgroup of G contained in K;
    (iv) K has trivial amenable radical.
                        ABSTRACT TWISTED BRIN–THOMPSON GROUPS                                  39

   Recall that a (discrete) group is called C ∗ -simple if its reduced C ∗ -algebra is simple. We
will not introduce this analytic point of view, and rather use the following purely group-
theoretic characterization. Here a subgroup Λ of a group Γ is called confined if there exists
a finite subset F ⊂ Γ \ {1} such that gF g −1 ∩ Λ ̸= ∅ for every g ∈ Γ. Note that the trivial
subgroup is not confined.
Citation 8.2. [Ken20] A group Γ is C ∗ -simple if and only if it has no confined amenable
subgroups.
  The original reference [Ken20] calls confined subgroups residually normal, although the
name confined had been established much earlier [HZ97] and is now the standard term for
this notion, see, e.g., [LBMB22].
  If Γ has some non-trivial amenable normal subgroup, equivalently if Γ has non-trivial
amenable radical (defined to be the unique maximal amenable normal subgroup), then of
course it cannot be C ∗ -simple. On the other hand, there exist finitely generated groups
with trivial amenable radical that are not C ∗ -simple [LB17]. Thus, Theorem 8.1 says that
such examples cannot happen among abstract twisted Brin–Thompson groups.
  Let us spell out the following easy rephrasing of the relevant definitions; for a subset
F ⊆ Γ \ {1} and subgroup Λ ≤ Γ, say that F is displaceable from Λ if there exists g ∈ Γ
such that gF g −1 ∩ Λ = ∅.
Observation 8.3. Let Γ be a group. Then:
    • Γ is C ∗ -simple if and only if every finite F ⊆ Γ \ {1} is displaceable from every
      amenable Λ ≤ Γ.
    • Γ has trivial amenable radical if and only if every singleton {f } ⊆ Γ \ {1} is
      displaceable from every amenable Λ ≤ Γ.
Proof. The first item is just Citation 8.2 rephrased. For the second item, if Γ has non-trivial
amenable radical then a non-trivial element of the amenable radical cannot be displaced
from the amenable radical, and conversely if some non-trivial element cannot be displaced
from some amenable subgroup, then the normal core of that subgroup is a non-trivial
amenable normal subgroup.                                                                    □

  We first focus on the faithful case.
Lemma 8.4. Let G ↷ S be a faithful action on a non-empty set, and consider the induced
action of SVG on C S . Then, for every non-empty open subset U ⊂ C S , the rigid stabilizer
RStabSVG (U ) is non-amenable.
Proof. In the faithful case, the deferment system from the proof of Corollary 7.15 coincides
with that of rigid stabilizers from Example 7.12. Non-amenability was shown in the proof
of Corollary 7.15.                                                                        □

 Appendix A. A strengthening of the Boone–Higman–Thompson theorem
   Recall from Remark 5.6 the Boone–Higman–Thompson theorem, that a finitely gener-
ated group has solvable word problem if and only if it embeds in a finitely generated sim-
ple group that embeds in a finitely presented group [BH74, Tho80]. In this appendix we
slightly strengthen this theorem, and also discuss connections to twisted Brin–Thompson
groups and the Boone–Higman conjecture. The statement can be phrased in a number of
equivalent ways: the following is left to the reader.
Lemma A.1. Let Γ ≤ P be groups. Then the following are equivalent.
   • Every non-identity element of Γ normally generates P .
   • Every proper normal subgroup of P intersects Γ trivially.
   • Every non-trivial quotient of P is injective on Γ.                                            □
  We will need the following lemma.
Lemma A.2. Let Γ be a finitely presented group and 1 ̸= g ∈ Γ. Then there exists a
finitely presented group P containing Γ, such that g normally generates P .
Proof. First embed Γ into a finitely generated simple group G, for instance the twisted
Brin–Thompson group ΓVΓ . We claim that there exists a finitely presented cover P → G
satisfying the hypotheses. Indeed, since Γ is finitely presented, only finitely many relations
from G suffice to ensure that Γ embeds. Moreover, g normally generates G since G is
simple, that is, every generator of G is a product of conjugates of powers of g, and this
can again be expressed by only finitely many relations.                                     □
Remark A.3. Recently, Chatterji–Kassabov proved that P can moreover be chosen to
have property (T) [CK], hence property (T) can be assumed of the container group in
Proposition A.4. In fact, via an easy construction using small cancellation theory over
acylindrically hyperbolic groups [Hul16], the container group P may be chosen to be a
quotient of any given finitely presented acylindrically hyperbolic group. Since these ad-
ditional properties are not relevant to us, we only need Lemma A.2, for which we could
present a self-contained elementary proof.
  The main result of this appendix is the following.
Proposition A.4. Let Γ be a finitely generated group. Then Γ has solvable word problem
if and only if there exists a finitely presented group P such that Γ embeds as a subgroup of
P trivially intersecting every proper normal subgroup of P .
42                    F. FOURNIER-FACIO, X. WU, AND M. C. B. ZAREMSKY

Proof. First suppose Γ has solvable word problem, so by the Boone–Higman–Thompson
theorem there exist a finitely generated simple group G and a finitely presented group P
such that Γ ≤ G ≤ P . By Lemma A.2, there exists a finitely presented group Q containing
P such that an element 1 ̸= g ∈ G normally generates Q. Since G is simple, it follows
that every non-identity element in G, hence in particular in Γ, normally generates Q. We
conclude by Lemma A.1.
   Now suppose Γ is a subgroup of a finitely presented group P such that Γ trivially
intersects every non-trivial normal subgroup of P . We must show that Γ has solvable word
problem.
   We follow the classical Kuznetsov algorithm [Kuz58], which treated the case when P
is simple. Fix a finite presentation ⟨S | R⟩ for P , and fix a word w in the alphabet S
representing an element of Γ. We run a “day and night” algorithm, which returns “yes” if
w represents the identity, and “no” otherwise.
   During the day, we enumerate all relations in the presentation ⟨S | R⟩, and check whether
w appears as one of them. If it does, then the algorithm stops and returns “yes”.
   During the night, we enumerate all relations in the presentation ⟨S | R∪{w}⟩, and check
whether each generator in S appears as a relation. If they all do, then the algorithm stops
and returns “no”.
   We claim that this algorithm terminates. Indeed, if w represents the identity, then it
must eventually appear in an enumeration of all the relations. Otherwise, w represents an
element in Γ \ {1}, hence a normal generator of P , by Lemma A.1. Therefore ⟨S | R ∪ {w}⟩
is a presentation for the trivial group. Thus, all the generators in S must eventually appear
in an enumeration of all the relations of this new presentation, and we are done.          □
  This immediately gives us the following strengthening of the Boone–Higman–Thompson
theorem:
Corollary A.5. A finitely generated group Γ has solvable word problem if and only if it
embeds in a finitely generated simple group G that embeds in a finitely presented group P ,
such that G normally generates P .                                                      □
   The relative Boone–Higman conjecture amounts to saying that the group P in Proposi-
tion A.4 can always be taken to be relatively simple, so conjecturally the statement about
trivially intersecting every proper normal subgroup boils down to just trivially intersecting
one proper normal subgroup (the largest). Note that if Γ is infinite, then since it embeds
in every non-trivial quotient of P by Lemma A.1, it follows that P has no non-trivial finite
quotients. Thus a Zorn’s lemma argument going back to Higman [Hig51] shows that P
admits a (finitely generated) simple quotient. If one could show that, in this situation,
P admits a finitely presented simple quotient, then this would prove the Boone–Higman
conjecture.
