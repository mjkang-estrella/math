                                             COMBINATORIAL PROOF OF A KOSTKA ANALOGUE OF THE
                                                          K-SATURATION THEOREM

                                                                               PER ALEXANDERSSON


                                                  Abstract. We give a combinatorial proof of the skew Kostka analogue of the K-
                                                  saturation theorem. More precisely, for any positive integer k, we give an explicit
                                                  injection from the set of skew semistandard Young tableaux with skew shape kλ/kµ
                                                  and weight kw to the set of skew semistandard Young tableaux of shape λ/µ and
                                                  weight w.




arXiv:1307.3999v1 [math.CO] 15 Jul 2013
                                                     Based on this method, we pose some natural conjectural refinements on related
                                                  problems.

                                            The main result of this paper is a combinatorial proof of the following theorem:
                                          Theorem 1. For fixed partitions λ, µ and w, the following holds for any integer k ≥ 1 :
                                          (1)                              Kkλ/kµ,kw > 0 ⇔ Kλ/µ,w > 0.
                                             Here, multiplication of a partition by a scalar is done as if it were a vector in Zn , and
                                          is commonly referred to as stretching.
                                             The identity (1) is known as Fulton’s K-saturation conjecture, and an indirect proof
                                          of this was recently found by A. Knutson and T. Tao, [6, 1]. They give a proof of a
                                          stronger result, namely
                                          Theorem 2. For fixed partitions λ, µ and w, the following holds for any integer k ≥ 1 :
                                          (2)                               ckλ/kµ,kw > 0 ⇔ cλ/µ,w > 0
                                          where the cλ/µ,w denotes the Littlewood-Richardson coefficients.

   There is a parametrization of the Littewood-Richardson coefficients which gives the
skew Kostka coefficients. Using this, (2) therefore implies (1). The explicit parametriza-
tion can be found in [4].

   A corollary of (1) is the following statement, where only non-skew tableaux are con-
sidered:

(3)                  Kkλ,kw > 0 ⇔ Kλ,w > 0 for each integer k ≥ 1.

This statement can be proved directly by using the fact that Kλ,w > 0 if and only of
λ D w, together with the fact that kλ D kw if and only if λ D w.
   Theorem (1) therefore lies somewhere in between (2) and (3) in difficulty. The fact that
there is no simple characterization to determine whenever Kλ/µ,w is non-zero strongly
indicates that proving (3) is non-trivial.
   The proof of (2) is rather intricate, which motivates a more direct proof of (3) which
is presented here.
   Another motivation of this paper is that the methods used may be used to give some
insight in some conjectures related to certain polynomials obtained from stretching. It
has recently been showed [2, 11], that k 7→ ckλ/kµ,kw is polynomial for all fixed λ/µ and
w which therefore implies polynomiality of stretched (skew) Kostka coefficients.
   In [4], King, Tollu and Toumazet gave the following conjecture:

Conjecture 3. For any fixed λ/µ and w, the polynomial P (k) = Kkλ,kw has non-
negative coefficients.

   As a consequence of this paper, we get a natural refinement of this conjecture, which
may give some insight in the above conjecture.
   Before concluding the introduction, we show the easy direction of the equivalence in
(1):

Lemma 4. For fixed partitions λ, µ and w, the following holds for any integer k ≥ 1 :

(4)                    Kλ/µ,w > 0 ⇒ Kkλ/kµ,kw > 0 for all k > 0.

Proof. There is a simple map that establishes this result: Given any tableau of shape
λ/µ and weight w, let every box in the tableaux be replaced by a row of k identical
boxes, see Figure 1. It is clear that this gives a tableau counted by Kkλ/kµ,kw .  

   We will prove (1) by working with skew GT-patterns. GT-patterns were introduced
in [3], and there is a bijection between GT-patterns and semistandard Young tableaux.
Since we work with skew tableaux, we need a generalization of GT-patterns introduced
in [8], called skew GT-patterns. These objects are in bijection with skew semistandard
Young tableaux and are for our purposes easier to work with than the tableaux them-
selves. There is also a bijection to non-intersecting lattice paths, where an analogous
method to our proof can be carried out.

2.1. A bijection between skew Young tableaux and skew GT-patterns. A skew
GT-patten consists of non-negative integers (xij )1≤i≤n,1≤j≤m arranged in a parallelo-
gram, satisfying the conditions xi+1,j ≥ xij and xij ≥ xi+1,j+1 for all values of i, j where
the indexing is defined. This can be realized as in (5), and the inequality conditions sim-
ply states that horizontal rows are weakly decreasing, down-right diagonals are weakly
decreasing, and down-left diagonals are weakly increasing.

2.2. Properties of GT-patterns. We will now state some general facts about skew
GT-patterns. These are immediate consequences of the bijection above.
Lemma 6. Let G ∈ GTλ/µ,w . Then the following statements holds:
(a) The partition in the top row in G is equal to λ.
(b) The partition in the bottom row in G is equal to µ.
(c) If |Ri | denotes the sum of the entries in row i, then |Ri+1 | − |Ri | = wi .
   Let kGTλ/µ,w ⊆ GTkλ/kµ,kw denote the subset of GT-patterns where each element
is a multiple of k. Then the map τk : GTλ/µ,w → kGTλ/µ,w defined as elementwise
multiplication with k is a bijection, and this map corresponds to the map given in Lemma
4. As an immediate consequence of Lemma 6, we also have the following:
Lemma 7. Let G ∈ GTkλ/kµ,kw . Then the sum of the entries in any row in G is a
multiple of k.
Proof. Combining the second and third property in Lemma 6 immediately gives that the
sum of the entries in row i in G is given by |kµ| + kw1 + kw2 + · · · + kwi−1 .   
2.3. Snakes in GT-patterns. We will now closely examine GT-patterns in GTkλ/kµ,kw .
The goal is to define an operation on such patterns, such that repeated application of
this operation results in an element in kGTλ/µ,w .
Definition 8. Let G ∈ GTkλ/kµ,kw . The snake partition (of G) is a partition of the
elements in G not divisible by k, into disjoint sets s1 , s2 , . . . , sm , called snakes.
(a) No two elements from the same row in G belongs to the same snake
(b) All elements in any snake has the same value.
(c) If two elements in a snake are from adjacent rows, then the topmost element is either
    immediately top-right or top-left of the other element.
(d) If a snake sa contains an element from row i1 and an element from row i2 , then sa
    contains one element from every row between i1 and i2 .
(e) If xij ∈ sa and xi,j+1 ∈ sb and xij = xi,j+1 then 1 xi±1,j ∈ sa .
    The first four conditions defines what a snake is, and essentially says that a snake is
a maximal-length zig-zag pattern in G consisting of elements with the same value. The
fifth condition says that whenever there is an ambiguity, make the leftmost snake longer,
in order to make the snake-partition well-defined. This technical condition is used later.
    If xij is the element in a snake sa with largest i, then we say that sa ends at row i.
Similarly, if xij is the element in a snake sa with smallest i, then we say that sa starts
at row i − 1.

Corollary 11. If a snake starts (ends) at some row j, at least one other snake starts or
ends at the same row.
  In Example 9, snake (1) and (3) starts at the same row, snake (2) and (3) ends at the
same row, snake (1) ends where (4) starts, and snake (4) ends where (2) starts.
   Given G ∈ GTkλ/kµ,ww we construct an undirected graph ΓG (with possible multiple
edges) as follows: The vertices are the starting and ending indices of the snakes in G
and every snake starting at row a and ending at row b corresponds to an edge between
a and b in ΓG . Corollary 11 implies that the degree of each vertex in ΓG is at least two,
and the graph is loop-free. It is easy to show that any such graph contains at least one
simple cycle.
   Given a set of edges C in ΓG that constitute such a cycle, we assign a direction to each
edge in C so that the cycle becomes directed. Each directed edge a → b in C corresponds
to a snake s in G. We assign the sign sgn(b − a) to every such snake s, and all other
snakes in G are assigned the sign 0.
   Note that since C is a cycle, it follows that, for each row R in G, the number of
elements in R belonging to a snake with positive sign, is equal to the number of elements
in R belonging to a snake with negative sign.
   For example, in Figure 4, a set of snakes corresponding to a circuit in the associated
graph, have been assigned non-zero signs, such that the total sum of the signs in each
row is zero. We have now reached the key step in the procedure:

   In conclusion, these are the steps we perform in order to get G0 from G :
    (1) Find the snake partition.
    (2) Find a simple cycle of snakes to obtain signed snakes.
    (3) Move signed snakes.
    (4) Increase or decrease values in signed snakes according to sign.
   We need to be a bit more precise in order for this procedure to be well-defined; In step
2 above, we have a subset S of snakes belonging to at least one cycle. Choose any cycle
containing the snake in S with highest end index, and if there are more than one such
snake, choose the leftmost one and call this s. There are always two ways to assign signs
to the snakes, corresponding to the two orientations of the cycle. Choose the orientation
that gives s a positive sign. These choices ensures that in every iteration that changes

G to G0 , the topmost row that changed, decreases in the lexicographical ordering. This
guarantees that the process terminates, and when it does, there are no more snakes.
  In conclusion, the algorithm projects G ∈ GTkλ/kµ,kw onto some G0 ∈ kGTλ/µ,w .
Performing elementwise division in G0 by k gives a G00 ∈ GTλ/µ,w . This defines the map
π+ : GTkλ/kµ,kw → GTλ/µ,w , which implies Theorem 1.
   If we modify the procedure above, but in each step select the opposite orientation of
the cycle, we get a different projection π− . Some properties of π+ and π− is given in the
following lemma:
Lemma 13. Let ≤lex define a total order on GTλ/µ,w , by lexicographically comparing
the word given by concatenation of the rows, starting with the topmost row. Then, for
G ∈ GTkλ/kµ,kw we have π− (G) ≤lex G ≤lex π+ (G) with equality if and only if G ∈
kGTλ/µ,w .
Proof. If G ∈ kGTλ/µ,w , then there are no snakes in G and thus π+ (G) = G = π+ (G).
If not, then π+ (π− ) will perform at least one operation with snakes, and thereby strictly
increase (decrease) the lexicographic order of G.                                        
Corollary 14. The smallest (largest) element in the lexicographic ordering of all elements
in GTkλ/kµ,kw is also an element in kGTλ/µ,w .
  We can use this to give a short proof of the following, which is a generalization of
K-Fultons Conjecture:
Proposition 15. For fixed partitions λ, µ and w, the following holds for any integer
k≥1:
                              Kkλ/kµ,kw = 1 ⇔ Kλ/µ,w = 1.
Proof. Let G ∈ GTkλ/kµ,kw . By Corollary 14, there exist a smallest and a largest GT-
pattern Gmin , Gmax ∈ kGTλ/µ,w satisfying Gmin ≤lex G ≤lex Gmax .
  Now notice that Gmin = Gmax is equivalent to both the left hand side, and the right
hand side above.                                                                   
   The non-skew version of this is known as the K-Fulton conjecture, and this also follows
from the Littlewood-Richardson version, which is proved in [7].

              3. Final remarks and refinements on open questions

kGTλ/µ,w satisfying G0 ≤lex G. Denote the map that projects G on G0 by φ.
  Using the bijection τk : GTλ/µ,w → kGTλ/µ,w , define ψk := τk−1 ◦ φ.
