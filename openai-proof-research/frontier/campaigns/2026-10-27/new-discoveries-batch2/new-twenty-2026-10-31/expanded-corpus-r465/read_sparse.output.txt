                                                                         ON THE “SECOND” KAHN–KALAI CONJECTURE


                                                                             QUENTIN DUBROFF, JEFF KAHN, AND JINYOUNG PARK


                                                    A BSTRACT. We make progress on a conjecture of Kahn and Kalai, the original (stronger but less general) version
                                                   of what became known as the “Kahn-Kalai Conjecture” (KKC; now a theorem of Park and Pham). This “sec-
                                                   ond” KKC concerns the threshold, pc (H), for Gn,p to contain a copy of a given graph H, predicting pc (H) =
                                                   O(pE (H) log n), where pE is an easy lower bound on pc . What we actually show is p∗E (H) = O(pE (H) log2 n),




arXiv:2508.14269v1 [math.CO] 19 Aug 2025
                                                   where p∗E , the fractional expectation threshold, is a larger lower bound suggested by Talagrand. When combined
                                                   with Talagrand’s fractional relaxation of the KKC (now a theorem of Frankston, Kahn, Narayanan and Park), this
                                                   gives pc (H) = O(pE (H) log3 n). (The second KKC would follow similarly if one could remove the log factors
                                                   from the above bound on p∗E .)




                                                                                               1. I NTRODUCTION

                                             For graphs G and H, a copy of H in G is an (unlabeled) subgraph of G isomorphic to H. We use N (G, H)
                                           for the number of such copies. As usual, Gn,p is the Erdős-Rényi random graph; that is, it has vertex set
                                           [n] := {1, . . . , n}, and contains each edge of Kn with probability p, independent of other choices. (We will,
                                           a little abusively, use “G ⊇ H” to mean G contains a copy of H.) Additional notation is given at the end of
                                           this section.
                                             Define the threshold for H-containment, pc (H) (= pc (n, H), but we suppress the n), to be the unique p for
                                           which P(Gn,p ⊇ H) = 1/2. This is finer than the original notion of Erdős and Rényi [3], which defines
                                           p0 = p0 (n) to be a threshold function for the property {Gn,p ⊇ H} if
                                                                                             (              )

                                                                            P(Gn,p ⊇ H) →                        as n → ∞.

                                           That pc (H) is always such a threshold function follows from [1].
                                             For a graph J, set Ep XJ = EN (Gn,p , J). In [5], Kalai and the second author considered a naive lower
                                           bound on pc (H) that one might now call the graphic expectation threshold for H:

                                                                            pE (H) = pE (n, H) = min{p : Ep XI ≥ 1/2 ∀I ⊆ H}.

                                           (This is a lower bound on pc (H) since, for any I ⊆ H, P(Gn,p ⊇ H) ≤ P(Gn,p ⊇ I) ≤ Ep XI . Strictly
                                           speaking, [5] defines pE with 1 in place of 1/2. It also calls pE the expectation threshold, and gives no name to
                                           what has been called the “expectation threshold” since [4].)
                                             Our focus in the present work is on the following suggestion from [5] to the effect that pc (H) and pE (H)
                                           are never very far apart; this conjecture, called the “second Kahn–Kalai Conjecture” in [7], was in fact the
                                           starting point for [5]. (We use vH and eH for |V (H)| and |E(H)|.)

                                           Conjecture 1.1. [5, Conjecture 2.1] There is a fixed K such that for any graph H,

In the limited graphic setting to which it applies, Conjecture 1.1 is considerably stronger than the main
conjecture of [5] (called the “Kahn–Kalai Conjecture” since [11]), which was recently proved by Pham and
the third author [8]. Here we show that this stronger version isn’t too far from correct, in that it is never off
by more than a log2 n factor, and that it does hold in a sufficiently sparse regime:

Theorem 1.2. There is a fixed K such that for any graph H,

                                          pc (H) ≤ KpE (H) log vH log2 n.

Theorem 1.3. There is a fixed K such that if pE (H) < 1/(3n), then

                                             pc (H) < KpE (H) log vH .

(While it’s hard to judge past efforts on Conjecture 1.1 absent published progress, a comment from [6] is
suggestive: “but this [conjecture] is widely open, even if log n is replaced by no(1) .”)

      What we actually show, strengthening Theorems 1.2 and 1.3, is a connection between pE and another
lower bound, the fractional expectation threshold for H, which in our context may be defined as

(1)                               p∗E (H) = min{p : Ep XI ≥ N (H, I)/2 ∀I ⊆ H}.

(Note “⊆ H” is superfluous.) In this case, p∗E (H) ≤ pc (H) is given by Markov’s Inequality: for any I,
P(Gn,p ⊇ H) ≤ P(N (Gn,p , I) ≥ N (H, I)) ≤ Ep XI /N (H, I).
      We omit the usual definition of fractional expectation threshold, due to Talagrand [10, 11] (but named
in [4]), which applies to general increasing properties. It is not hard to see that in the present context it
agrees with the definition in (1) (briefly: the fractional expectation threshold is the maximum of a collec-
tion of lower bounds on pc , which maximum is, in the case of graph containment, attained by the bound
corresponding to one of the constraints in (1); see also [7]).

      Trivially,
                                                  pE (H) ≤ p∗E (H),
and it was shown in [4] that Conjecture 1.1 holds with pE replaced by p∗E :

Theorem 1.4. [4, Theorem 1.1] There is a fixed K such that for any graph H,

                                             pc (H) < Kp∗E (H) log eH .

(Here we could replace eH by vH to agree with Conjecture 1.1. The present version, which corresponds to
what’s in [4], is formally stronger since log eH ≤ 2 log vH , but easily seen to be equivalent.)

      In view of Theorem 1.4, Theorems 1.2 and 1.3 are implied by the following assertion. The first part of this
should be considered the main point of the present work, but we would also like to stress the second part,
as a small success with Conjecture 1.6 below, which is our ultimate goal (see also the remark following the
conjecture).

Theorem 1.5. There is a fixed K such that for any graph H,

                                             p∗E (H) < KpE (H) log2 n.

Furthermore, there is a fixed α > 0 such that if pE (H) < 1/(3n), then p∗E (H) < α−1 pE (H).

Our present belief is that the extra log factors are never necessary; that is, that the following strengthening
of Conjecture 1.1 holds.

Conjecture 1.6. There is a fixed K such that for any graph H, p∗E (H) < KpE (H).

(This is stronger than a positive answer to [5, Question 2.2], which asks whether the “expectation threshold”
of [4], which is never more than p∗E (H), is O(pE (H)).)

      For the proof of Theorem 1.5, we work with a reformulation. Given q ∈ [0, 1], say a graph J is q-sparse if

                                                Eq XI ≥ 1 ∀I ⊆ J,

and note that, apart from the irrelevant switch from 1/2 to 1 (which we abusively ignore), this is just
another way of saying pE (H) ≤ q. We then have the following appealing restatement of Conjecture 1.6, and
the promised reformulation of Theorem 1.5.

Conjecture 1.7. There is a fixed K such that if H is q-sparse and p = Kq, then

                                           N (H, F ) < Ep XF    ∀F ⊆ H.

(Again “⊆ H” is unnecessary.)

Remark. Conjecture 1.7 is challenging even for simple classes of F ’s. In a separate paper [2] we prove it
for a few such classes, namely (a) cliques, (b) bounded degree forests, and (c) cycles, which turn out to be
(a) not hard (in retrospect), (b) quite hard (as in, hard to find, though some of the details are also not easy),
and (c) rather hard, even given the result for paths in (b). Ideas developed to prove these results played an
important role in the discovery of the proof of Theorem 1.8.

Theorem 1.8. There is a fixed K such that if H is q-sparse and p = Kq log2 n, then

(2)                                        N (H, F ) < Ep XF    ∀F ⊆ H.

Furthermore, there is a fixed α > 0 such that (2) holds if H is q-sparse with q = αp < 1/(3n).

(Eventually α ≈ e−2 is enough; see the end of Section 5.)

Remark. The proof of Theorem 1.8 also shows that if |E(F )| < C, then (2) holds for p = KC q log n.

      The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 includes definitions and a few easy points, and
sketches the main line of argument for Theorem 1.8. This is implemented in Sections 3 and 4, and the quite
different argument for smaller q is given in Section 5.

Usage. For a graph J and U ⊆ V (J), the neighborhood, N (U ) = NJ (U ), is the set of vertices with neighbors
in U , and J[U ] is the subgraph of J induced by U . As above, the sizes of V (J) and E(J) are denoted vJ and
eJ (or v(J) and e(J)). We use [a] = {1, 2, . . . , a} and (n)a = n(n − 1) · · · (n − a + 1) (for positive integer a),
and, throughout the paper, take log to mean log2 . We make no effort to optimize constant factors, and, in
line with common practice, pretend all large numbers are integers.

                                     2. P ROOF PREVIEW AND PRELIMINARIES

      Here we introduce a few basic notions that provide the framework for most of what follows. With these
in hand, we then sketch a portion of the proof of Theorem 1.8, both to give a general idea of strategy and
to point to sources of the “extra” log factors that Conjecture 1.7 says should be unnecessary. In closing
the section we note some easy points, in particular slightly simplifying our assignment by showing that in
proving Theorem 1.8 it is enough to consider connected F .

Extensions and leading decomposition. For W ⊊ Z ⊆ V (J), the subgraph of J on Z rooted at W , denoted
J[W, Z], has vertex set Z and edge set E(J[Z]) \ E(J[W ]). When the identity of J is either clear or irrelevant
(e.g. for the rest of the present discussion), we usually abbreviate J[W, Z] = [W, Z]. (Any J used without
qualification is a general graph.) We use e(W, Z) for the number of edges of [W, Z] and v(W, Z) for |Z \ W |.
We will also use [B, A] for [B, B ∪ A], and similarly for v(B, A) and e(B, A) (note this includes edges con-
tained in A). The set of automorphisms of J is Aut(J), and Aut(W, Z) is the set of automorphisms of [W, Z]
that fix W pointwise. We will always use aut(·) for |Aut(·)|.
      In what follows we will have: J, W, Z as above; a graph G (usually H) and U ⊆ V (G) of size |W |; and
orderings (w1 , . . . , ws ) and (u1 , . . . , us ) of W and U , which will be left implicit. A [W, Z]-extension on U (in G
if the identity of G is not clear) is then an injection ψ : Z → V (G) that extends the map wi 7→ ui (i ∈ [s]) and
is edge-preserving (i.e. {x, x′ } ∈ E(J) ⇒ {ψ(x), ψ(x′ )} ∈ E(G)). When W = ∅, such an extension is a labeled
copy of J[Z] in G. Rooted graphs [W, Z] and [U, V ] with implicit orderings (w1 , . . . , ws ) and (u1 , . . . , us ) of
W and U are isomorphic if there is an isomorphism of Z to V extending the map wi 7→ ui (i ∈ [s]).
      Extensions ψ and ψ ′ are equivalent if ψ = ψ ′ ◦ γ for some γ ∈ Aut(W, Z), and a copy of [W, Z] on U is an
equivalence class of such extensions (so an “unlabeled” extension, but note we continue to label W and U ).

      We use τ̃[W,Z] (U, G) and τ[W,Z] (U, G) for the numbers of [W, Z]-extensions and copies of [W, Z] on U in G
(so τ̃[W,Z] (U, G) = τ[W,Z] (U, G) · aut(W, Z)), and set

      The next notion is key.

Definition 2.1. A rooted graph [W, Z] is p-leading if µp (Y, Z) < 1 for all Y satisfying W ⊊ Y ⊊ Z.

Proposition 2.2. If 0 ≤ q ≤ p ≤ 1 and J is q-sparse, then there is a sequence

                                          ∅ = W0 ⊊ W1 ⊊ · · · ⊊ Wk = V (J)

such that J[Wi−1 , Wi ] is p-leading and µp (Wi−1 , Wi ) ≥ 1 for i ∈ [k].

Proof. Note that if J is q-sparse, then (i) J is q ′ -sparse for every q ′ > q, and (ii) every subgraph of J is q-
sparse. By (i), we just need to prove the proposition when p = q. We proceed by induction on vJ ≥ 1 (with
base case vacuous). Since q-sparseness gives µq (∅, V (J)) ≥ Eq XJ ≥ 1, we may choose Y ⊊ V (J) maximal
with µq (Y, V (J)) ≥ 1. By maximality, [Y, V (J)] is q-leading; so either Y = ∅ and we are done, or, since J[Y ]
is q-sparse (by (ii)), induction gives a sequence ∅ = W0 ⊊ W1 ⊊ · · · ⊊ Wk−1 = Y as in the proposition, and
appending Wk := V (J) completes the proof.                                                                              ■

Sketch. Here we try to give some idea of what will happen in the main line of argument for Theorem 1.8.
For this informal discussion we call labeled copies embeddings. We fix a decomposition

                                        ∅ = W0 ⊊ W1 ⊊ · · · ⊊ Wk = V (F )

as in Proposition 2.2, with p replaced by q ′ ≈ q log n, and, for each i ∈ [k], bound the number of ways an
embedding of F [Wi−1 ] in H can be extended to an embedding of F [Wi ]. (One of the extra log factors in
Theorem 1.8 can be blamed on our use of this worst case bound for quantities that should usually be much
smaller.)
   For the bound, we note that the extensions of a given embedding divide into “components,” and show,
using the sparseness of H, that (i) the number of components is not too large, and (ii) no component contains
too many extensions. These assertions are shown in Lemmas 3.1 and 3.2, with the first easy and the second
the main part of our argument.
   The main point for Lemma 3.2 is Lemma 3.4, which says, roughly, that a union, T , of many overlapping
extensions of a given F [Wi−1 ] has small Eq XT , so cannot appear in a suitably sparse H. Crucial here is
limiting the numbers of automorphisms of our extensions: the contribution to our bound on Eq XT (which
is really a bound on the number of labeled copies) of an extension of “type” [Y, Z] with Y ̸= Wi−1 is es-
sentially µ̃q (Y, Z) = µq (Y, Z) · aut(Y, Z), and µq (Y, Z) is small because [W, Z] is leading; so the aut(Y, Z)’s
become the issue. (Note that this gets us only so far, since such “local” symmetries may not be reflected in
aut(T ); see following Lemma 3.4 for a possible improvement that would eliminate the second “extra” log
in Theorem 1.8.)
   Control of automorphism counts is mainly based on Lemmas 4.2 and 4.3, the more important of which,
Lemma 4.3, says that a graph with many automorphisms must contain a large “sunflower” (see Defini-
tion 4.1). Concretely, we show that for k > 4 log n, a graph (or rooted graph) J with n vertices and no
k-sunflower has at most O(k)eJ automorphisms. We conjecture this holds for all k, which seems to us both
interesting in itself and a probably necessary step toward the above-mentioned improvement of Lemma 3.4.
