                                           1     Introduction
                                           Let G be a finite abelian group, written additively, and let S ⊆ G \ {0} have size n. An ordering
                                           s1 , . . . , sn of S is valid if its nonempty partial sums s1 , s1 + s2 , . . . , s1 + · · · + sn are pairwise distinct.
                                           We call S rearrangeable if it admits a valid ordering. In 1971, Graham [33] raised the following
                                           conjecture, which was later reiterated by Erdős and Graham [30].
                                           Conjecture 1.1 (Graham). If p is prime, then every S ⊆ Zp \ {0} is rearrangeable.
                                               In his original paper, Graham [33] posed the rearrangement problem alongside several questions
                                           about sumset structures, a central topic in additive combinatorics. Later, he suggested that the
                                           problem was also motivated by applications to juggling [16, 19, 34]; also see [15].
                                               Questions about distinct partial sums first arose in the construction of combinatorial designs.
                                           Motivated by constructions of complete Latin squares, Gordon [32] asked in 1961 which finite groups
                                           admit an ordering of all their elements with distinct partial products, and characterized the abelian
                                           groups with this property. Such an ordering gives a complete Latin square by permuting the rows
                                           and columns of the multiplication table. A Latin square is complete if every ordered pair of distinct
                                           symbols occurs exactly once in horizontally adjacent cells and exactly once in vertically adjacent
                                           cells. For 1 ≤ i < j ≤ n, the partial sums s1 + · · · + si and s1 + · · · + sj coincide precisely when
                                           si+1 + · · · + sj = 0. Thus, rearrangeability asks for an ordering in which every consecutive interval
                                           starting after the first term has nonzero sum. This viewpoint connects rearrangement problems
                                           with classical questions in additive combinatorics. For example, the Erdős–Ginzburg–Ziv theorem
                                           [29] states that every sequence of 2m − 1 elements of Zm contains m terms whose sum is zero.
                                               Moving beyond prime orders, Archdeacon, Dinitz, Mattern and Stinson [8] conjectured the
                                           following.

   A slightly stronger conjecture is due to Alspach and Liversidge [7] (see also [11, 15, 20, 23, 24,

    Most previous work towards Conjecture 1.1 focused primarily on the two extreme regimes where
|S| is either very small or very large. An approach based on the Combinatorial Nullstellensatz [2]
established the conjecture for |S| ≤ 10 [39] and |S| ≤ 12 [26]. Direct constructive arguments settled
the nearly full cases |S| ≥ p − 3 with nonzero sum [39]. Later, as a consequence of their resolution
of the random Hall–Paige conjecture, Müyesser and Pokrovskiy [49] established that every subset
S ⊆ G \ {id} of size |S| ≥ (1 − o(1))|G| is rearrangeable in any finite group G. This was subsequently
extended to |S| ≥ |G|1−c for an absolute constant c > 0 by Bedert, Bucić, Kravitz, Montgomery
and Müyesser [11]. For small sets, Kravitz [41] established the conjecture for |S| ≤ log p/ log log p
using rectification, also see Sawin [59]. In a major breakthrough, Bedert and Kravitz [12] overcame
this rectification barrier in [41, 59], extending the range to |S| ≤ exp(c(log p)1/4 ) for every fixed
c > 0 and sufficiently large p.
    Very recently, Pham and Sauermann [53] bridged the intermediate regime by proving Conjec-
ture 1.1 for Cα ≤ |S| ≤ p1−α , where α ∈ (0, 1) is arbitrary and Cα > 0 is a constant depending only
on α. Together with the dense bound of Bedert, Bucić, Kravitz, Montgomery and Müyesser [11] (by
setting α = c) and the small-set bounds for |S| ≤ Cα , their result shows a complete resolution of
Graham’s rearrangement conjecture for all sufficiently large primes p.
    Nevertheless, proving rearrangeability for general finite abelian groups is significantly more
difficult. Our understanding beyond these remains very limited. Bedert, Bucić, Kravitz, Montgomery
and Müyesser [11] proved that every sufficiently large subset of Fr2 \ {0} is rearrangeable. For further
partial results in cyclic groups or other groups, see [21, 22, 23].
1.1    Rainbow subgraphs
Finding rainbow subgraphs is an important theme in extremal graph theory, with connections to
additive combinatorics and combinatorial designs [66]. In this paper, we study Conjecture 1.3 via
rainbow subgraphs. In fact, Conjecture 1.3 can equivalently be stated in terms of rainbow paths in
Cayley digraphs. The colored Cayley digraph Cay(G, S) has vertex set G and an edge x → x + s
of color s for each x ∈ G and s ∈ S. A path or cycle is rainbow if its edges have distinct colors.
If a directed walk x0 x1 · · · xk has successive edge colors s1 , . . . , sk , then xi = x0 + s1 + · · · + si for
1 ≤ i ≤ k. Thus Conjecture 1.3 is equivalent to the existence of a rainbow directed path of length
|S| − 1 in every such Cayley digraph (see [15], and also Lemma 2.1 in this paper). Schrijver [63]
asked the following problem for properly edge-colored regular graphs.
Problem 1.4 (Schrijver [63]). Does every d-regular graph with a proper edge-coloring using d colors
contain a rainbow path of length d − 1?
   Bucić, Frederickson, Müyesser, Pokrovskiy and Yepremyan [15] asked the following analogue in
directed graphs. An edge-coloring of a digraph is proper if edges with a common initial vertex, or a
common terminal vertex, have distinct colors.
Problem 1.5 (Bucić, Frederickson, Müyesser, Pokrovskiy and Yepremyan [15]). Let H be a digraph
in which every vertex has in-degree and out-degree d, with a proper edge-coloring using d colors.
Does H contain a rainbow directed path of length d − 1?

    An affirmative answer to Problem 1.5 would imply the rearrangement conjecture for all finite
groups. Bucić, Frederickson, Müyesser, Pokrovskiy and Yepremyan [15] proved approximate results
for rainbow paths and showed that every finite subset S of a finite group has an ordering in which
all but o(|S|) partial products are distinct. Wilson [70] subsequently proved that the digraphs in
Problem 1.5 contain rainbow directed paths of length d − o(d), and deduced that every finite subset
S of a finite group contains a rearrangeable subset of size |S| − o(|S|).
    These questions belong to a broader study of rainbow embeddings. Alon, Jiang, Miller and
Pritikin [4] studied rainbow subgraphs under local coloring constraints. Montgomery [46] proved
that every Latin square of sufficiently large order m contains a partial transversal of size m − 1. Alon,
Pokrovskiy and Sudakov [5] found rainbow cycles of length m−O(m3/4 ) in properly edge-colored Km ,
and recently, Bowtell, Montgomery, Müyesser and Pokrovskiy [14] proved Andersen’s conjecture for
large m by finding a rainbow path on m − 1 vertices. Benzing, Pokrovskiy and Sudakov [13] proved
that every properly edge-colored complete symmetric digraph on m vertices contains a rainbow
directed cycle of length m − O(m4/5 ). Related embedding and absorption methods also underlie the
work of Montgomery, Pokrovskiy and Sudakov on rainbow trees and Ringel’s conjecture [47, 48].
1.2    Main results via dissociated sets
In this paper, we solve Conjecture 1.3 (also Conjectures 1.1 and 1.2) for sufficiently large sets.

Theorem 1.6. There exists an absolute integer n0 such that the following holds. Let G be a finite
abelian group and let S ⊆ G \ {0} have size n ≥ n0 . Then S is rearrangeable.

    By Lemma 2.1, this also answers Problem 1.5 for the canonical colorings of Cayley digraphs over
finite abelian groups whenever d is sufficiently large.
    We first give a sufficient condition for rearrangeability in terms of dissociated sets, an additive
analogue of linearly independent sets.

    Dissociated sets play a role analogous to that of linearly independent sets in the study of additive
structure [54, 67]. The maximum size of a dissociated subset of a set is called its additive dimension,
and controlling this parameter is an important topic in additive combinatorics [62, 65]. For example,
Chang’s theorem bounds the additive dimension of large Fourier spectra [18], which are used in
arguments based on density increments, including Sanders’s work on Roth’s theorem [56]. For
integer intervals, Erdős [28, Section 6] asked whether dimdiss ({1, . . . , N }) ≤ log2 N + O(1). Dubroff,
Fox and Xu [27] presented two short proofs yielding the weaker upper bound dimdiss ({1, . . . , N }) ≤
log2 N + 12 log2 log2 N + O(1).
    Dissociated sets also give properly edge-colored graphs with no rainbow cycle [3, 42]. Given
D ⊆ G, let X and Y be two disjoint copies of G. Join x ∈ X to y ∈ Y whenever y − x ∈ D, and color
the edge xy with y − x. This defines a properly edge-colored bipartite graph. A rainbow cycle with
successive edge colors d1 , . . . , d2ℓ yields d1 − d2 + · · · + d2ℓ−1 − d2ℓ = 0, a contradiction. A set D ⊆ G
is called a Sidon set if every equality a + b = c + d with a, b, c, d ∈ D implies {a, b} = {c, d} [31].
When D is Sidon, the same construction contains no rainbow 4-cycle, since such a cycle would yield
d1 + d3 = d2 + d4 with four distinct elements of D.

1.3.2     Theorem 1.6
By Theorem 1.8, it suffices to consider dimdiss (S) = O((log n)2 ). By Theorem 1.9, almost all
elements of S lie in a coset a + H, while S ∩ (a + H) has many elements outside every coset
of a proper subgroup of H. We distinguish three cases according to |H| and ordG/H (a + H).
The following informal statements correspond to these cases; their precise statements appear in
Lemma 6.3, Theorem 7.1 and Theorem 8.1, respectively.

                                                             5
Lemma 1.10 (A large direction subgroup, informal). Suppose that dimdiss (S) = O((log n)2 ) and
all but a sufficiently small power of n elements of S lie in a coset a + H. If |H| ≥ n1+δ for some
fixed δ > 0 and S ∩ (a + H) has at least a fixed positive power of n elements outside every proper
subcoset, then S is rearrangeable.

Lemma 1.11 (A coset of large order, informal). Suppose that all but a small power of n elements
of S lie in a coset a + H. If the order of a + H in G/H is at least a larger fixed power of n, then S
is rearrangeable.

Lemma 1.12 (A dense subgroup core, informal). Let Q ≤ G be a sufficiently large subgroup. If
S ∩Q is sufficiently dense in Q and |S \Q| is sufficiently small relative to |Q|, then S is rearrangeable.

   The first two cases, covered by Lemmas 1.10 and 1.11, use local repair, adapting the methods of
Pham and Sauermann [53] and Costa, Della Fiore, Feng and Liu [22]. In the first case, the bounds
outside proper subcosets yield anticoncentration estimates for random subset sums. In the second
case, the large order of a + H restricts the possible lengths of zero-sum intervals in an ordering of
S ∩ (a + H). These provide two different ways to control the events arising in local repair. In the
third case, both |H| and q = ordG/H (a + H) are small, so Q = H + ⟨a⟩ has order q|H| and contains
almost all of S. The set S ∩ Q is then sufficiently dense for Lemma 1.12. Here we combine the
expansion approach of Bucić, Frederickson, Müyesser, Pokrovskiy and Yepremyan [15] with the
absorption method of Bedert, Bucić, Kravitz, Montgomery and Müyesser [11]. The full deduction,
including the choice of constants, appears in Section 8.

Organization. Section 2 introduces notation and some basic results. In Section 3, we show
estimates for subset sums arising from random orderings of dissociated sets. We use these estimates in
Section 4 to prove Theorem 1.8. Section 5 develops the coset concentration and slice anticoncentration
estimates. Sections 6 and 7 handle the cases of a large direction subgroup and a coset of large
order in the quotient, respectively. In Section 8, we settle the remaining case using expansion and
absorption and complete the proof of Theorem 1.6. The proof of Lemma 6.1 appears in Appendix A.

We use P and E for probability and expectation, respectively. And we write 1P for the indicator
function of a set P , and 1E for the indicator random variable of an event E. For 0 ≤ p ≤ 1, a
p-random subset of a finite set is obtained by including each element independently with probability
p. We shall use the following standard inequalities below.

Lemma 2.2 (Chernoff’s inequality [6]). Let X be a sum of independent Bernoulli random variables,
with µ = EX > 0. Then, for every t > 0,
   (i) P(X ≤ µ − t) ≤ exp(−t2 /(2µ));
  (ii) P(X ≥ µ + t) ≤ exp(−t2 /(2µ + t)).

    The following lemma was proved by Hoeffding [40].

Lemma 2.3 (Chernoff’s inequality for the hypergeometric distribution [40]). Let Ω be a set of size
N ≥ 1, let A ⊆ Ω have size K, and let R be a uniformly random m-subset of Ω. Write X = |R ∩ A|
and µ = EX = mK/N > 0. Then, for 0 < δ < 1,
   (i) P(X ≤ (1 − δ)µ) ≤ exp(−δ 2 µ/2);
  (ii) P(X ≥ (1 + δ)µ) ≤ exp(−δ 2 µ/3).

6    A large direction subgroup
In this section, we use the local-repair argument of Pham and Sauermann [53, Section 5], also see
Costa, Della Fiore, Feng and Liu [22]. Starting from a random ordering, the argument removes

right to left. We combine this criterion with the slice anticoncentration estimate from Section 5 to
handle the case in which the direction subgroup of the concentrated coset is large. Related switching
arguments have been used in graph packing [58] and in the generation of random regular graphs [43]
and Latin rectangles [44]. For random permutations, Harris and Srinivasan [36, 37] developed a
general swapping algorithm.

8.3   Constructing the rainbow path
We adapt the dense-case rainbow-path construction of Bedert, Bucić, Kravitz, Montgomery and
Müyesser [11, Theorem 9.11], which builds on the expansion-based approach of Bucić, Frederickson,
Müyesser, Pokrovskiy and Yepremyan [15]. The main difference is that our dense color set lies
in a subgroup Q ≤ G, while a small number of colors may lie outside Q. We use a Cayley graph
diameter argument to pass to an expanding subgroup and adapt the switching and absorption steps
to the relevant cosets.
