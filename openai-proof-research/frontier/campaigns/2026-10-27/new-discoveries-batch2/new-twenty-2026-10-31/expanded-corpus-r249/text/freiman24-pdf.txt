arXiv:2408.00183v2 [math.NT] 30 Sep 2024
                                                 Abstract. — Freiman’s 3k − 4 Theorem states that if a subset A of k integers has
                                                 a Minkowski sum A + A of size at most 3k − 4, then it must be contained in a short
                                                 arithmetic progression. We prove a function field analogue that is also a generalisation:
                                                 it states that if K is a perfect field and if S ⊃ K is a vector space of dimension k inside
                                                 an extension F/K in which K is algebraically closed, and if the K-vector space generated
                                                 by all products of pairs of elements of S has dimension at most 3k − 4, then K(S) is a
                                                 function field of small genus, and S is of small codimension inside a Riemann-Roch space
                                                 of K(S).




                                                                                   1. Introduction
                                              We are interested in analogues of addition theorems that occur in field extensions
                                           F/K of a base field K. If S and T are finite-dimensional K-vector subspaces of F , we
                                           denote by ST the K-linear span of the set of all products st, s ∈ S, t ∈ T . The general
                                           purpose of this area of research is to characterise subspaces S and T whose product ST
                                           has unusually small dimension: it is naturally inspired by one of the goals of additive
                                           combinatorics which is to characterise subsets A, B of a group that have sumsets A + B
                                           of small cardinality, where A + B denotes the set of elements a + b, a ∈ A, b ∈ B.
                                              This line of research was arguably triggered by Hou, Leung and Xiang when they
                                           proved [HLX02] an extension field analogue of Kneser’s classical addition Theorem for
                                           Abelian groups [Kne56]. The extension field version essentially states that, given a field
                                           extension F/K, if S and T are K-linear spaces of finite dimension contained in F , then
                                           whenever dimK ST < dimK S +dimK T −1, it must be the case that the product space ST
                                           is stabilised by a non-trivial (different from K) subfield of F . This theorem was initially
                                           proved for separable extensions in [HLX02], and was shown to be true for all extensions
                                           in [BSZ18]. Versions of Kneser’s Theorem for products of vector spaces were also shown
                                           to hold in other algebras than field extensions [BL17, MZ15]. Many applications of the
                                           theory of products of spaces in the algebra Fnq with componentwise multiplication are
                                           discussed in [Ran15] : the latter theory is concerned in particular with the existence
                                           and the construction of products of linear codes of small dimension, and since many of
                                           these codes are constructed by evaluating functions drawn from a function field over Fq ,
                    FREIMAN’S 3k − 4 THEOREM FOR FUNCTION FIELDS                        2



this makes the study of the dimension of products of spaces in function fields relevant to
this topic as well.
   If F/K does not contain a finite extension of K, and if S, T are K-subspaces of F
such that ST 6= F , then Kneser’s Theorem for extension fields implies that dimK ST ⩾
dimK S + dimK T − 1. A study of the limiting case when dimK ST = dimK S + dimK T − 1
was undertaken in [BSZ17]. A particularly simple case occurs when K is algebraically
closed. One can then easily uncover the structure of the spaces S and T choosing a
discrete valuation v on F , considering the sets of integers v(S) and v(T ) and arguing
that their Minkowski sum v(S) + v(T ) must satisfy |v(S) + v(T )| = |v(S)| + |v(T )| − 1,
from which addition in Z tells us that v(S) and v(T ) must be arithmetic progressions
with the same difference: It can then be inferred that the spaces S and T must have
bases in geometric progression. Switching to the symmetric case S = T for the sake of
simplicity, it becomes then tempting to ask for the structure of spaces S such that
(1)                           dimK S 2 = 2 dimK S − 1 + γ
for increasing values of γ. It was suggested in [BCZ18] that one should draw inspiration
from what is often known as Freiman’s “3k−4” Theorem [Fre73], (called Freiman’s 3k−3
Theorem in [TV06, Thm. 5.11]). Recall that it states that, if A is a set of integers,
(2)                              |A + A| = 2|A| − 1 + γ
implies, when γ ⩽ |A| − 3, that A is included in an arithmetic progression of length
|A| + γ. Natural analogues of such sets A are spaces S that are of codimension at most
γ inside a space with a basis in geometric progression. However, the point was made in
[BCZ18] that other examples of spaces satisfying (1) are Riemann-Roch spaces L(D) of
an algebraic curve of genus γ. Note that a space with a basis in geometric progression is
a particular instance of a Riemann-Roch space in a genus 0 function field. In the present
article we show that Riemann-Roch spaces play the role of arithmetic progressions in
the extension field setting and that the following analogue of Freiman’s 3k − 4 Theorem
holds:
Theorem 1. — Let K be a perfect field and F be an extension field of K in which K
is algebraically closed (i.e. any element of F that is algebraic over K is in K). Let S be
a K-subspace of finite dimension in F such that K ⊂ S and F = K(S). If
               dimK S 2 = 2 dimK S − 1 + γ    with   0 ⩽ γ ⩽ dimK S − 3,
then F has transcendence degree 1 over K and its genus g satisfies g ⩽ γ. Furthermore,
there exists a Riemann-Roch space L(D) of F that contains S and such that dimK L(D)−
dimK S ⩽ γ − g.
   Theorem 1 was first conjectured in [BCZ18] where the statement was proved for
γ ⩽ 1. Moreover, it was also proved that, under the conditions of the theorem, F must
always be of transcendence degree 1 over K. Later on, the conjecture was proved for
γ = 2 under the additional assumption that F = K(x) [Wes24].
   The integer γ in (1) was called the combinatorial genus of the space S because of the
way it relates to the geometric genus g of the associated function field. We note that
Theorem 1 is not only a linear analogue of Freiman’s 3k − 4 Theorem, but can also be
seen a generalisation since it easily recaptures the original additive statement. Indeed,
                     FREIMAN’S 3k − 4 THEOREM FOR FUNCTION FIELDS                           3



if A is a set of integers satisfying (2) with γ ⩽ |A| − 3, then, supposing without loss
of generality that A ⊂ [0, n = max A] and gcd(A) = 1, we may form the vector space
S generated by xa , a ∈ A, inside the rational function field extension K(x). We have
that S 2 is generated by xa , a ∈ A + A, and Theorem 1 therefore applies. The relevant
Riemann-Roch space L(D) in the conclusion of the Theorem can only be L(nP∞ ), where
P∞ is the place at infinity and n = max A, and that S is of codimension at most γ inside
L(nP∞ ) means exactly that A equals the arithmetic progression [0, n] with at most γ
missing elements.
   The way we prove Theorem 1 borrows from Lev and Smeliansky’s elegant proof of
Freiman’s additive 3k − 4 Theorem [LS95] (see [TV06, Th. 5.11] for a concise descrip-
tion). The idea was to project the set of integers under study onto a finite quotient
Z/nZ and apply Kneser’s Theorem in this finite group. Our starting point will be to
view F = K(S) as a finite extension of a transcendental extension K(x) of K, for a
suitably chosen element x ∈ S. The goal will then be to apply the extension field version
of Kneser’s Theorem to K(S)/K(x) and extract non-trivial information from it: there
will be significant differences with the additive situation however. In the next section we
give an overview of the paper and the structure of the proof of Theorem 1, which will
draw upon a number of algebraic and combinatorial techniques.


                                       2. Overview
2.1. The additive case.
   To describe the main steps of the proof of Theorem 1, it is useful to have in mind the
template for Lev and Smeliansky’s proof of the original 3k − 4 Theorem. One starts with
a set of integers A satisfying (2)
                                  |A + A| = 2|A| − 1 + γ
with min A = 0, max A = n and gcd(A) = 1: the goal is to prove that |A| ⩾ n + 1 − γ.
To this end one reduces the set A to the set Ã of integers modulo n, so that we have
|Ã| = |A| − 1. Since the sets A and A + n have only n as a common element, and since
both reduce to Ã modulo n, we have that
(3)                            |Ã + Ã| ⩽ |Ã| + γ ⩽ 2|Ã| − 2.
Now Kneser’s Theorem tells us that there exists a subgroup H of Z/nZ such that Ã+ Ã =
Ã + Ã + H and |Ã + Ã| ⩾ 2|Ã| − |H| (with (3) therefore implying that |H| > 1). It could
happen that Ã + Ã = Z/nZ, in which case we are done: indeed, the left-hand inequality
of (3) entails precisely that |Ã| ⩾ n − γ and hence |A| ⩾ n + 1 − γ. The rest of the proof
consists of showing that H 6= Z/nZ cannot happen. This is achieved by partitioning A
into sets that represent cosets modulo H: by studying the behaviour of sums of these
sets, one finds elements of A+ A that are equal modulo H and counting them contradicts
the hypothesis γ ⩽ |A| − 3 in (2). The argument is detailed concisely in [TV06].

2.2. The function field setting.
  It is shown in [BCZ18] that under the hypothesis of the Theorem, the field F must
have transcendence degree 1 so that it is a function field in one variable. It is then natural
                    FREIMAN’S 3k − 4 THEOREM FOR FUNCTION FIELDS                          4



enough to consider the smallest Riemann-Roch space L(D) containing S as the analogue
of the interval [0, n] in the additive case. The divisor D is therefore the least sum of all
the places P , counted with suitable multiplicities, for which there exist functions in S
with poles at P . We then identify a function x in S whose pole divisor is exactly D.
Next, we augment the space S by considering its linear span S̃ = K(x)S over K(x). The
choice of x will ensure that S ∩ xS = Kx so that dimK (S + xS) = 2 dim S − 1. This
guarantees that
                       dimK(x) S̃ 2 ⩽ dimK(x) S̃ + γ ⩽ 2 dimK(x) S̃ − 2
similarly to (3). Viewing now F as an algebraic extension of K(x) (rather than K), we
can apply Kneser’s Theorem for field extensions [BSZ18] which says that there exists a
field L, K(x) ⊂ L ⊂ F , such that S̃ 2 = LS̃ 2 and dimK(x) S̃ 2 ⩾ 2 dimK(x) S̃ − dimK(x) L.
Since 2 dimK(x) S̃ − dimK(x) L ⩽ 2 dimK(x) S̃ − 2 we have that the field L must be strictly
larger than K(x). It will not be difficult to obtain that if L = F , then, somewhat
similarly to the additive case, the conclusion of Theorem 1 holds. This is a consequence
of Clifford’s Theorem and is detailed in Section 3.
   It will remain therefore to show that the situation L 6= F cannot occur. At this point,
similarities with the additive case break down rather seriously and we are confronted
with new challenges. Following the additive template would be easier if we could prove
the existence of an L-adapted basis of S, i.e. the existence of elements si ∈ S such that
LS decomposes as the direct sum of the Lsi , and S decomposes as the direct sum of
Si = S ∩ Lsi . We could then study the dimensions of the products Si Sj without fearing
the appearance of unexpected linear relations between these subspaces. We do not know
how to argue for the existence of such a decomposition of S however, but we did manage
to obtain a partial result in this direction. We showed that the dimension over K of L ∩ S
is a non-trivial quantity: this is the object of Section 4. The way this is achieved is by
studying the evaluations of functions of S on suitably chosen places, and showing that
some of these functions must have block-wise constant evaluations, exactly as elements
of L do. Galois Theory arguments then enable us to show that these evaluations must
come from functions of S that also fall into L.
   Section 5 switches from the algebraic techniques developed in Section 4 to a much
more combinatorial approach. It uses the result of Section 4 on S ∩ L to decompose
S into a direct sum of spaces that is much cruder than what would be provided by a
hypothetical L-adapted basis, but which turns out to be sufficient to give an evaluation
of dimK S 2 that only just contradicts the hypothesis (1) of Theorem 1.

2.3. A geometric comment.
   Using K(x)S as we do may not seem like the most natural approach. Since the additive
case resorted to a quotient of the ring of integers, a tempting analogue, that may seem
closer in spirit to the additive proof, would be to consider a subring of F containing S and
reduce it modulo some ideal. In Section 4 this is exactly what we do: we choose a place
O of K(x) which splits totally into a sum Q of places in F and we study S modulo Q.
However, we have not seen how to derive a proof of Theorem 1 using only this approach,
even though there exist Kneser-type theorems for such quotients [BL17, MZ15].
   Nevertheless, we believe that using K(x)S definitely makes sense from a geometric
point of view. Indeed, the extension F/K(x) corresponds to some curve covering Y →
                     FREIMAN’S 3k − 4 THEOREM FOR FUNCTION FIELDS                            5



X , where X , Y have respective function fields K(x) and F . The space K(x)S can be
interpreted as the restriction of S to the generic fibre of the covering Y → X , while
reducing S modulo Q corresponds to a restriction to some special fibre. In the end,
proving the result required us to restrict S both to the generic fibre and to some special
fibre.

2.4. Organisation of the article
   In Section 3 we provide the setup for the proof of Theorem 1, introduce the subexten-
sion fields of K, K(x) ⊂ L ⊂ F , and prove that the conclusion of the Theorem holds if
L = F . In Section 4 we study the dimension over K of S ∩ L. In Section 5 we conclude
the proof by showing that L 6= F cannot happen. Both Sections 4 and 5 rely on the
results of Section 3: Section 5 relies upon the result of Section 4 on the dimension of
S ∩ L, but it is possible to put off its proof and read Section 5 before Section 4, if one so
wishes. The proof of Theorem 1 developed through Sections 3 to 5 uses the hypothesis
that the ground field K is algebraically closed: in Section 6 we show how to extend the
result to perfect fields, yielding Theorem 1 in full generality.

3.1. Background and Notation
3.1.1. The arithmetic of function fields
    Number theoretic background is fairly basic and most of it can be found in Chapters 1
and 3 of [Sti09], from which we borrow notation.
    Given a field K, the function fields over K that we will encounter (see Section 3.2.1
below) will be exclusively function fields in one variable, i.e. finite extensions of the field
K(x) of rational functions.

3.1.2. Products of spaces
   As mentioned in the introduction, when S, T are K-vector subspaces of an extension
field F of K, we denote by ST the K-vector space generated by products st of elements
of S with elements of T . Note that this is simply the set of sums of products of elements
of S and T . In the particular case when T = M is itself a subfield of F , M S is simply
the M -subspace generated by S. Also given an intermediate field K ⊂ M ⊂ L, then
we have M (ST ) = (M S)(M T ) and we denote this space by M ST . More generally, the
product ST of two K–vector spaces can be defined in any K–algebra. In particular, in
Section 4, products of spaces in the product ring K n are involved.
   We assume the setting of Theorem 1. Precisely, let K be a fixed perfect field and F/K
be an extension field such that no element of F \ K is algebraic over K. Let S be a
finite-dimensional K-subspace of F , such that K ⊂ S and F = K(S), i.e. S generates F
as an extension of K. We denote henceforth the dimension dimK S of S by k. Finally,
we suppose that S has combinatorial genus 0 ⩽ γ ⩽ k − 3, where the combinatorial genus
γ is defined to be the integer such that

                                      dim S 2 = 2k − 1 + γ.

(28)             dimK S 2 ⩾ 2k − 1 + (k + τ − 1) − ℓ = 3k − 2 − (ℓ − τ ).
This does not contradict the Freiman hypothesis (23), but it is not too far from it: to
obtain a contradiction, what we need to do is to improve the lower bound (28) by ℓ−τ −1.
  We will improve upon (28) in two ways. We will use valuation arguments to obtain
that (S 2 ∩ LS) is potentially strictly larger than S + xS, yielding a larger term than
2k − 1 in (28). We will also need to improve upon the (κ − 1)ℓ estimate in (27). For this
we will use the Cauchy-Davenport inequality:

Lemma 5.2. — If U and V are K-vector subspaces of F of finite dimension, then
                            dimK U V ⩾ dimK U + dimK V − 1.

Proof. — This is a direct consequence of Theorem 2, since F does not contain finite-
dimensional extensions of K.

   The strategy will be to look for K-subspaces U, V of S for which Lemma 5.2 will yield
a subspace U V of S 2 such that dimK U V > ℓ dimL LU V . Care will be needed to ensure
that these “extra K-dimensions” add up to a sufficiently large improvement of the lower
bound (28).
   Proposition 4.9 allows us to forget how S0 was originally defined in Section 4, and we
may now use the notation S0 simply to refer to S ∩ L.
   Finally, recall from Lemma 3.6 that there exist κ elements s1 = 1, s2 , . . . , sκ of S whose
valuations at Q∞ lie in distinct classes modulo d. We may suppose additionally that each
vQ∞ (si ) is maximal within its class modulo d. Let these elements si of S remain fixed
throughout Section 5.

5.3. Studying S0 S and S0 s ∩ S
   Let us write S0 = S0′ ⊕ S0′′ where S0′ is the subspace of S0 consisting of the elements

                       6. When K is not algebraically closed
  Once Theorem 1 is proved in the special case of an algebraically closed ground field K,
there remains to extend it to the more general case a perfect ground field K. Suppose
that K is perfect and algebraically closed in F . The proof to follow is in the same spirit as
that of [BCZ18, § 9]. Denote by K the algebraic closure of K. Since K is algebraically
                                 def
closed in F , the compositum F ′ = KF is a function field over K. Moreover, from [Sti09,
Prop. 3.6.1(b)], dimK KT = dimK T for any K-subspace T , so that we have
(36)                                dimK KS = dimK S
and also
                             dimK KS 2 = 2 dimK KS − 1 + γ.
Theorem 1 for algebraically closed fields therefore applies, so that F ′ has genus g ⩽ γ
and the least divisor D ′ of F ′ such that KS ⊂ LF ′ (D ′ ) satisfies
(37)                         dimK LF ′ (D ′ ) − dimK S ⩽ γ − g.
   From [Sti09, Thm. 3.6.3(b)], F ′ has the same genus as F which yields the upper
bound on the genus. Let D be the least divisor of F such that S ⊂ LF (D), there remains
to evaluate the codimension of S in LF (D). Following the lines of [BCZ18, Thm. 9.2],
one proves easily that D ′ is nothing but the conorm (also called the pullback ) of D in F ′
(see [Sti09, Def. 3.1.8]) and, from [Sti09, Thm. 3.6.3(d)],
(38)                          dimK LF ′ (D ′ ) = dimK LF (D).
Therefore, (36) and (38) together with (37) permits to conclude.
