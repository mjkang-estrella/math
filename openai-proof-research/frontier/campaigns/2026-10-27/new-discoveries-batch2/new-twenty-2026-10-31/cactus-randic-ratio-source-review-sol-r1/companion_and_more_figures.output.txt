11:                                                    Abstract. We propose a framework for thinking about eccentricity in terms
12:                                                    of blocks. We extend the familiar deﬁnitions of radius and center to blocks and
13:                                                    verify that a central block contains all central points. We classify graphs into
14:                                                    two types depending upon the relationship between block radius and vertex
15:                                                    radius and between central blocks and central vertices; from this we derive a
16:                                                    new lower bound on diameter in terms of the diameter of the central block. We
17:                                                    also identify a subgraph which respects the block structure of the original graph
18:                                                    and realizes the same vertex radius, and we use it to verify that cactus graphs
19:                                                    satisfy a conjectured bound between vertex radius and the Randić index, an
20:                                                    invariant from mathematical chemistry.

25:                                                                                 1. Introduction
26:                                               We employ a modification of a traditional concept in graph theory (the extension
27:                                            of eccentricity to blocks) to derive new results on classic questions regarding the
28:                                            center of a graph and the relationship between radius and diameter.
29:                                            Origins of Centrality. The idea of centrality in a graph traces back to the nine-
30:                                            teenth century and the work of Jordan. To aid in his classification of small con-
31:                                            nected graphs up to isomorphism, including their symmetry groups, he defined the
32:                                            version of centrality that we now call a centroid. Only well into the next century did
33:                                            the concept of centrality begin to be embraced as a way to identify vertices which
34:                                            are somehow medial in the graph, with numerous variations crafted for different
35:                                            types of applications, for example, betweenness centrality as a measure of the influ-
36:                                            ence a node has on information flow in a network, or various forms of eigenvector
37:                                            centrality and their use in internet search algorithms.
38:                                               The structure of the central points is not obvious. Jordan [Jor69, Section 2]
39:                                            showed that a tree’s centroid consists of either one vertex or two adjacent vertices.
40:                                            Some time later, a parallel theorem emerged concerning the center of a tree, and,
41:                                            in 1953, Harary and Norman showed further that the center of any graph lies in a
42:                                            single block [HN53, Lemma 1]. (Note the theorem on the center of a tree was erro-
43:                                            neously attributed to Jordan in his 1869 paper by, e.g., Buckley and Norman [BH90,
44:                                            Theorem 2.1], but Jordan defines “center” the way we now define “centroid”).
45:                                            Eccentricity and Centrality. We refine these results on the relationship between
46:                                            eccentricity and the block structure of a graph. In Section 2, we extend the concept
47:                                            of eccentricity to a block and define block versions of center and radius. In Section 3,

79:                                   2. Definitions
80:    For a graph G, we let V (G) be the vertex set and E(G) the edge set. We write
81: the edge between adjacent vertices u and v as [u, v]. Unless otherwise stated, all
82: graphs are connected.
83: 2.1. Blocks and the BC-tree. A special type of vertex we will use extensively
84: is an articulation point, also known as a separating vertex or cut vertex. This
85: is a vertex whose removal would increase the number of components. A graph
86: without articulation points is nonseparable or 2-connected, and a maximal non-
87: separable subgraph is a block (sometimes called a 2-connected component ); note
88: each articulation point is in all incident blocks. We denote the set of blocks B(G).
89:    To describe the block structure, we may draw a new graph G, called the block-
90: cutpoint tree, often shortened to BC-tree. We assign one vertex a for each articu-
91: lation point a ∈ V (G) and one vertex B for each block B ∈ B(G). If v ∈ B, we
92: further assign an edge [a, B]. If H is a subgraph of G whose BC-tree is H, then H
93: is a subgraph of G, and we call H the shadow of H.
94:    We will regularly use the articulation components or branches at an articulation
95: point a, the maximal subgraphs which do not contain a as an articulation point;
96: equivalently, they are the shadows in G of the connected components of G − a
97: (note that, if we allow disconnected graphs, then it is traditional to require that
98: the articulation point contain a, i.e., it must come from the same component as a).
99: We extend this definition to a block: if B is a block, the articulation components at
100: B are the maximal connected subgraphs which do not contain B and do not have
101: any vertex of B as an articulation point; equivalently, they are the shadows in G
102: of the connected components of G − B.
103:    Note that there may be a complicated relationship between the two types of
104: articulation components. If a is an articulation point on block B, then the set of

501: Proof. We may examine the construction in the BC-tree rather than in G: for a
502: graph of type A (respectively, type B), we pick a vertex v in Gi of maximal distance
503: from a (resp., B), say v is in a block called D, and find a path in G from D to a
504: (resp., B). Alter G one step at a time by deleting leaves and their accompanying
505: pendant edges, reflecting each deletion in G as we go by removing a block. Deleting
506: a leaf D in G will correspond to deleting a block D in G which was connected to
507: a single articulation point aD , which will reduce the size and order of the graph
508: but not disconnect it. That is, G′ is connected, and G′ and G′ are subgraphs of
509: G and G, respectively. Additionally, as long as deg aD ≥ 3, removing D reduces
510: the number of blocks intersecting at aD but leaves it an articulation point, so G′
511: is the BC-tree of G′ . On the other hand, if deg aD = 2, then aD is an articulation
512: point between two blocks, and removing D renders it a non-articulation point and
513: converts aD to a leaf itself, so G′ is not a BC-tree of anything until we also remove
514: aD and its pendant edge.
515:     By construction, all central blocks and so also all central points are retained in
516: G′ . Additionally, any u and v which survive to G′ will have the same distance there
517: which they did in G: removing blocks which correspond to leaves in the BC-tree
518: will not interfere with geodesics between surviving vertices or affect the relative
519: distance between them; in particular, the eccentricity of any vertex or block will
520: not increase from G to G′ . Similarly, the vertex eccentricity of a or the block
521: eccentricity of B with respect to each Gi will remain the same.
522:     We next show that the radius does not change. If G is type A, then the ec-
523: centricity of a will not change: if vi is a vertex in Gi of maximal distance from
524: a, then either vi or some other vertex of equal distance in Gi will survive to G′ .
525: Additionally, if vertex v ∈ Gi survives to G′ , then its eccentricity also does not
526: change by Theorem 3.6 since it is realized by at least one vertex in some other Gj ,
527: and this vertex is separated from v by a, so either it or another vertex of equal
528: distance in Gj will survive.
529:     Similarly, if G is type B, then the eccentricities of all the vertices in B will also
530: remain the same because the distance between vertices within B will be unchanged,
531: and the eccentricity of B and so all its vertices with respect to any given Gi will
532: be the same from G to G′ . Additionally, the eccentricity of no vertex outside B
533: will change: by Theorem 3.6, the eccentricity of a vertex in Gi is attained only by
534: some vertex from which it is separated by ai , and that vertex is either in B (so
535: will survive) or is in some other Gj (in which case it or another vertex of the same
536: distance will survive).
537:     We finally show that the diameter does not change. A type A graph remains
538: type A because its central blocks remain central, and its radius is preserved, so its
539: diameter is also preserved. For a type B graph, let u and v be vertices of maximal
540: distance. Their geodesic must pass through B by Theorem 3.6, and any portion of
541: it in B is preserved. If it contains any portion in some Gi , then it passes through
542: some ai , and, even if that portion does not itself survive, some other geodesic in
543: Gi of the same length will.                                                            

556:            6. Application: The Randić index of a cactus graph
557:    We now turn towards an invariant from mathematical chemistry which is used
558: to study the branching of a graph. It was originally defined by Milan Randić in
559: 1975 in an attempt to mathematically characterize branching in a way consist with
560: boiling point and other structure-related properties such as enthalpy of formation
561: of alkanes and the relationship of vapor pressure to temperature [Ran75]. It has
562: been experimentally verified to be associated to boiling and reactivity of hydro-
563: carbons and has now become a standard tool for evaluating molecular structure
564: in quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) models, that is, regressive
565: models that predict biological activity, physicochemical properties, and toxicolog-
566: ical responses of chemical compounds based on their molecular structure (see, for
567: example, [KH86, Pog00, GDGdJOP08, TC08, KH76]).
