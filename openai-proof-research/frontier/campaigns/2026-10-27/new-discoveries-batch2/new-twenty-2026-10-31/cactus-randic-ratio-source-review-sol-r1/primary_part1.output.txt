11:                                                    Abstract. We study the Randić index for cactus graphs. It is conjectured
12:                                                    to be bounded below by radius (for other than an even path), and it is known
13:                                                    to obey several bounds based on diameter. We study radius and diameter for
14:                                                    cacti then verify the radius bound and strengthen two diameter bounds for
15:                                                    cacti. Along the way, we produce several other bounds for the Randić index
16:                                                    in terms of graph size, order, and valency for several special classes of graphs,
17:                                                    including chemical nontrivial cacti and cacti with starlike BC-trees.

22:                                                                                 1. Introduction
23:                                            Scientific motivation. A number of molecular properties appear to depend on
24:                                            the shape of a molecule and vary even between different arrangements of the same
25:                                            atoms; for example, the boiling points of hexane isomers appear to be correlated
26:                                            to the surface area of the molecular cloud, which reflects the degree and location
27:                                            of branching of the molecule. In 1975, Milan Randić proposed a molecular branch-
28:                                            ing index based entirely on molecular graphs in an attempt to mathematically
29:                                            characterize branching in a way consistent with boiling point and other structure-
30:                                            related properties such as the enthalpy of formation of alkanes and the relationship
31:                                            of vapor pressure to temperature. Besides simplifying study of such properties
32:                                            and allowing their prediction for novel molecules, such a mathematical character-
33:                                            ization also reveals a correlation between preexisting constants such as some of
34:                                            the Antoine coefficients and therefore allows a reduction in the number of con-
35:                                            stants which must be experimentally determined [Ran75]. Since then, Randić’s
36:                                            index has become a standard tool for evaluating molecular structure in quanti-
37:                                            tative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) models, that is, regressive models
38:                                            that predict biological activity, physicochemical properties, and toxicological re-
39:                                            sponses of chemical compounds based on their molecular structure (see, for exam-
40:                                            ple, [KH86, Pog00, GDGdJOP08, TC08, KH76]).
41:                                            Mathematical investigations. As it depends exclusively (at least as phrased
42:                                            initially) on the arrangement of atoms within a molecule, the Randić index is a
43:                                            graph theoretic invariant. Randić’s original formulation was based on the graph
44:                                            adjacency matrix, and the common formula is due to Balaban in 1982 [Bal82]. The
45:                                            Randić index of a graph G is

245: Definitions and notation. Let G = (V, E) be a graph with vertex set V and
246: edge set E. The degree of a vertex v will be denoted dv , and the size of a cycle c
247: will be sc . Graph order is the number of vertices, and graph size is the number of
248: edges. A cut vertex or articulation point is a vertex whose removal increases the
249: number of connected components of a graph, and a cut edge or bridge is an edge
250: whose removal does the same. A leaf is a vertex of degree 1, and a pendant is an
251: edge with a leaf at one end. If required for intelligibility, we will write R(G) for R,
252: dv (G) for dv , and so on.
253:     Common graphs we use are: the path on n vertices, Pn ; the cycle on n vertices,
254: Cn ; the star on n + 1 vertices Sn . A graph is starlike with r > 2 arms if it has one
255: vertex of degree r called its root to which are attached r paths. We may refer to
256: a path or cycle as even (respectively, odd ) when it has an even (respectively, odd )
257: number of vertices. A tree is a connected graph where every vertex is either a leaf
258: or an articulation point; equivalently, every edge is a bridge. It is the connected
259: graph with the fewest edges for a given number of vertices. A cactus is a connected
260: graph where any two cycles intersect in at most one point, and a nontrivial cactus
261: is a cactus without bridges; in other words, a nontrivial cactus may be constructed
262: by iteratively adding cycles to one another (glued together by identifying a vertex
263: from each), and a cactus by adding cycles and bridges.
264:     We may sometimes divide more complicated graphs up into smaller subgraphs
265: for convenience. A block is a maximal biconnected subgraph, that is, a subgraph
266: without any articulation points; equivalently, the articulation points of a graph
267: divide it into connected subgraphs which we call blocks. We may draw a graph of
268: these blocks as in Figure 1, called the block cut tree or BC-tree: assign a vertex in
269: the BC-tree for each articulation point and for each block in the graph, and assign
270: an edge in the BC-tree for each pair of an articulation point and a block which
271: contains it. Note that it is indeed a tree.
272:     We study these graphs by use of two standard invariants, radius and diameter,
273: and a degree-based topological index, the Randić index. The distance dist(u, v)
274: between two vertices is the minimal length of a path between them, and the eccen-
275: tricity ecc(v) of a vertex is the maximum distance from that vertex to any other

423:    We will be studying radius and diameter under the addition of cycles or bridges,
424: so we will need some a few facts for our later study.
425: Lemma 3.1. All centers in a graph are contained in the same block.
426: Proof. If two centers a1 and a2 are not in the same block, then find a shortest path
427: between a1 and a2 ; it must pass through some articulation point a. Then removing
428: this articulation point would separate the graph into components, one containing
429: a1 , one containing a2 , and possibly some additional components. Partition the
430: vertices V − a along these lines: a set V1 for the component of a1 , a set V2 for the
431: component of a2 , and a set V0 (possibly empty) for the remaining vertices. The
432: vertices in V1 set are closer to a than they are to a2 , those in V2 likewise are closer
433: to a than they are to a1 , and the remaining in V0 are closer to a than to either ai ;
434: therefore, a has eccentricity lower than either ai , which is a contradiction.        
435:    Consider a graph and select a special central block : if there are multiple centers,
436: select the block containing them; if there is a a unique center which is not an artic-
437: ulation point, select its block; if there is a the unique center which is an articulation
438: point, select one adjoining block which contains an edge of a shortest path from
439: that center to some vertex of distance r.
