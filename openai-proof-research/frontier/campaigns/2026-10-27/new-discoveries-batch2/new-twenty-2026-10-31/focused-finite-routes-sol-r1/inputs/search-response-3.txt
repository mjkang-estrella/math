# Thesis

## Putting the pieces together: from graph decompositions to Ramsey theory

Abstract:
    This thesis investigates the decomposition of graphs into a small number of well-structured subgraphs and the unavoidable substructures that arise in such decompositions.
A celebrated problem in this area is the Linear Arboricity Conjecture, which states that the edges of every graph with maximum degree $\Delta$ can be decomposed into at most $\lceil (\Delta+1)/2\rceil$ linear forests, where a linear forest is a collection of vertex-disjoint paths. Although this conjecture remains wide open, we prove that every graph with $n$ vertices admits a decomposition into at most $\Delta/2 + \mathcal{O}(\log n)$ linear forests. For $\Delta \ge \Omega(n^\varepsilon)$, this is an exponential improvement over the previous best error term. We also show that every $d$-regular graph has a spanning linear forest with at most $2 \cdot n / (d+1)$ paths and a cycle-factor with at most $\mathcal{O}((n \log d) / d)$ cycles. This confirms a conjecture of Feige and Fuchs, resolves a conjecture of Magnant and Martin up to a factor of two, and yields an efficient algorithm for finding short tours in connected regular graphs.
We then turn to an extremal problem in graph colouring. Here, we disprove a conjecture of Scott and Seymour by constructing graphs of arbitrarily large chromatic number together with tournaments on the same vertex set such that every out-neighbourhood in the tournament induces a bipartite subgraph of the graph.
The thesis concludes with several contributions to Ramsey theory. First, we establish that the $k$-colour Ramsey number of an odd cycle of length $2\ell+1$ is at most $(4\ell)^k \cdot k^{k/\ell}$. This proves a conjecture of Fox and is the first improvement in the exponent beyond an absolute constant factor since the work of Bondy and Erdős. --------------------------------------------------------------------------------
