                                                  Abstract. For digital topological spaces defined using adjacency relations,
                                                  there is an established homotopy equivalence relation which parallels that of
                                                  classical topology. Many classical homotopy equivalence invariants, such as the
                                                  Euler characteristic and the homology groups, do not remain invariants in the
                                                  digital setting. This paper develops two numeric digital homotopy invariants
                                                  and begins to catalog all possible connected digital spaces on a small number
                                                  of points, up to homotopy equivalence.




                                                                              1. Introduction
                                             The field of digital topology has been developed over the past few decades,
                                          motivated by computer graphics, image processing, and other applications. See [13]
                                          for an early foundational work. Three main settings for studying digital spaces have
                                          emerged: the Khalimsky topology on Zn [12, 14], geometric realizations of subsets
                                          of Zn into Rn [4], and graph-like adjacency structures on finite sets [1, 3, 8]. This
                                          paper works in the final setting but de-emphasizes the traditional focus on spaces
                                          given by subsets of Zn with various “rectangular” adjacency grids. Essentially, we
                                          are viewing digital topological spaces as simple graphs.
                                             As in classical topology, invariants are of particular interest in the study of digital
                                          spaces. Homeomorphism invariants, such as the fundamental group, homology
                                          groups, and the Euler characteristic are studied in [1, 3, 8]. But homeomorphisms
                                          of digital spaces are very strict (for example, they must preserve the number of
                                          points), so it is natural to expand this study to homotopy equivalences, which
                                          allow for more freedom. In classical topology, the Euler characteristic and homology
                                          groups are also homotopy equivalence invariants, but a counterexample is given in
                                          [8] showing this does not hold in the digital setting.
                                             Digital homotopy equivalence often behaves counterintuitively especially when
                                          compared to classical topology. For example, we will see that all cycles of fewer
                                          than five points are homotopy equivalent to a point, while any two cycles of dif-
                                          ferent lengths greater than or equal to 5 will not be homotopy equivalent to one
                                          another (this result also appears in [2]). The aim of this paper is to develop tools
                                          and numerical invariants to capture properties which are preserved by homotopy
                                          equivalences and to use these tools to classify digital spaces by their homotopy
                                          types.
                                             Homotopy equivalence of digital spaces is defined in the obvious way [2]. While
                                          the equivalence relation has been mentioned in several papers, no numerical invari-
                                          ants seem to have been developed (other than the number of connected components,
                                          which is easily seen to be an invariant). We note that there is an established no-
                                          tion of homotopy equivalence for graphs described in [5]; however, the homotopy

                                2. Preliminaries
   A digital topological space is a finite set of points, X, with some adjacency re-
lation, κ, which is symmetric and antireflexive. Typically in digital topology the
space X is a subset of Zn and the adjacency relation is based on some notion of
closeness of points in the integer lattice. We will not be considering our spaces as
points in the integer lattice, but allow more general adjacency relations. Digital
spaces not based on the integer lattice can be used (see [9]) to model hexagonal
pixel tilings of R2 or other nonstandard pixel arrangements in Rn .
   The standard notation for such a digital space is (X, κ). With the adjacency
relation giving us a notion of closeness, some familiar constructions from classical
topology can be made in digital spaces.

2.1. Continuous functions and homotopies. We will use the notation x ↔κ y
when x is adjacent to y, and x -κ y when x is adjacent or equal to y. The particular
adjacency relation will usually be clear from context, and in this case we will omit
the subscript.
Definition 2.1. Let (X, κ1 ), (Y, κ2 ) be digital spaces. A function f : (X, κ1 ) →
(Y, κ2 ) is (κ1 , κ2 )-continuous iff whenever x ↔κ1 y ∈ κ1 then f (x) -κ2 f (y) ∈
(Y, κ2 ).
   For simplicity of notation, we will generally not need to reference the adjacency
relation specifically. Thus we typically will denote a digital space simply by X,
and when the appropriate adjacency relations are clear we simply call a function
between digital spaces “continuous”. We will refer to digital topological spaces
as simply “spaces”, and all functions discussed will be assumed to be continuous
unless otherwise noted.
   A digital space X can be viewed naturally as a simple graph, where the vertices
are points of X and an edge connects two vertices x, x′ ∈ X whenever x ↔ x′ . This
viewpoint is helpful for our results, and we will use graph-theoretic terminology
whenever convenient. Usually when describing specific examples of spaces we will
simply draw the graph rather than describing the adjacency relation abstractly.
Definition 2.2. A homeomorphism between two digital spaces X and Y is a con-
tinuous bijection f : X → Y whose inverse is also continuous.
     HOMOTOPY EQUIVALENCE IN GRAPH-LIKE DIGITAL TOPOLOGICAL SPACES                      3


  Unlike in classical topology, a bijective continuous selfmap is always a homeo-
morphism:
Lemma 2.3. If f : X → X is a continuous bijection, then f is a homeomorphism.
Proof. It suffices to show that the inverse function f −1 : X → X is continuous.
Equivalently, we must show that for any x, x′ ∈ X, if f (x) ↔ f (x′ ) then x ↔ x′ .
   Consider the cartesian product X 2 = X × X, and let E ⊂ X 2 be the “edge set”
E = {(x, y) ∈ X 2 | x ↔ y}. Let f 2 : X 2 → X 2 be the map f 2 (x, y) = (f (x), f (y)).
Then f 2 is a bijection, and continuity of f means that f 2 (E) ⊆ E. But since f 2 is
a bijection and X 2 is finite this means that f 2 (E) = E.
   Now if f (x) ↔ f (x′ ) then (f (x), f (x′ )) ∈ E and so (x, x′ ) ∈ E since f 2 is a
bijection on E, and thus x ↔ x′ as desired.                                          
   The authors would like to thank Nathaniel Eldredge for suggesting the above
argument. The lemma fails to be true if f is taken to be a bijection between two
different spaces. For example if X is a disconnected space of 2 points and Y is a
connected space of 2 points, then a bijection from X to Y will be continuous but
its inverse will not be.
   Let [a, b]Z = {z ∈ Z | a ≤ z ≤ b} denote the digital interval from a to b.
The standard adjacency relation for an interval is called 2-adjacency, indicating
that each integer in the interval is adjacent to exactly the number preceding and
following it. A (2, κ)-continuous function p : [0, k]Z → (X, κ) is called a path from
p(0) to p(k).
Definition 2.4. A space X is connected if for every x, y ∈ X there exists a path
p : [0, k]Z → X from x to y. Given x ∈ X, the component of x is the set of all
y ∈ X such that there is a path from x to y.
  Thus in the digital setting, connected and path-connected are equivalent. Ho-
motopies and homotopy equivalence are defined in the natural way.
Definition 2.5. Let X, Y be spaces and let f, g : X → Y be continous functions.
The maps f and g are homotopic if there exists k ∈ Z such that there is a map
H : X × [0, k]Z → Y with H(x, 0) = f (x) and H(x, k) = g(x) for all x ∈ X, for any
fixed t ∈ [0, k]Z , the map H(·, t) : X → Y is continuous, and for any fixed x ∈ X
the map H(x, ·) : [0, k]Z → Y is continuous.
   The map H is called the homotopy, and we write f ≃ g when f is homotopic to
g. As in classical topology, homotopy of maps is an equivalence relation (though the
transitivity will typically require changing the length of the digital interval [0, k]Z ).
In the case where f ≃ g by some homotopy with k = 1, we say that f is homotopic
to g in one step.
   We denote the identity map on X as idX , which is always continuous.
Definition 2.6. The spaces X and Y are homotopy equivalent if there exist two
continuous functions f : X → Y and g : Y → X such that f ◦ g ≃ idY and
g ◦ f ≃ idX .
  It is shown in [1] that homotopy equivalence is an equivalence relation.
Definition 2.7. A space X is reducible when it is homotopy equivalent to a space
of fewer points. Otherwise we say X is irreducible.
  The following is a useful characterization of reducible spaces.

Lemma 2.8. A space X is reducible if and only if idX is homotopic to a nonsur-
jective map.
Proof. If f : X → X be a nonsurjective map homotopic to idX . Let Y be the image
of X under f , so we may consider f as a map f : X → Y . If i : Y → X is the
inclusion mapping, then i ◦ f = idY and f ◦ i = f ≃ idX . Thus X is homotopy
equivalent to Y ( X, making X reducible.
   For the converse implication of the lemma, let X be reducible, then X is homo-
topy equivalent to a space of fewer points Z. Therefore there exists f : X → Z and
g : Z → X such that g ◦ f : X → X is homotopic to idX but since g must map
points in X to the same points in Z, g ◦ f must be a nonsurjective map.          
    In fact a stronger version of the above is also true:
Lemma 2.9. A space X is reducible if and only if idX is homotopic in one step to
a nonsurjective map.
Proof. If idX is homotopic in one step to a nonsurjective map it follows immedatly
from Lemma 2.8 that X is reducible. So we need only show that if X is reducible
then idX is homotopic in one step to a nonsurjection.
   If X is reducible then idX is homotopic to a nonsurjective map f . Let H :
X × [0, k] → X be any valid homotopy between the two functions. Without loss of
generality assume that H(x, t) is surjective for all t < k and not surjective for t = k.
Let g : X → X be the surjective map g(x) = H(x, k − 1). Since g is surjective and
continuous it is a homeomorphism by Lemma 2.3.
   Consider then the homotopy H ′ : X × [0, 1] → X where
                         H ′ (x, 0) = idX   H ′ (g(x), 1) = f (x).
Since g is a homeomorphism the above formula for H ′ (g(x), 1) is well-defined. Since
g is homotopic to f in one step the function H ′ (x, 1) is continuous, and since f is
homotopic to idX in one step H ′ (x, t) is continuous in the t parameter.
   Thus idX is homotopic in one step to the nonsurjective map f , as desired.      
  We can use the above to show that a homotopy equivalence of irreducible spaces
must actually provide a homeomorphism between the spaces.
Theorem 2.10. Let X and Y be irreducible spaces. Then X is homotopy equivalent
to Y if and only if X is homeomorphic to Y .
Proof. It suffices to assume that X and Y are homotopy equivalent, and show that
X and Y are homeomorphic. Let f : X → Y and g : Y → X give a homotopy
equivalence, so g ◦ f ≃ idX and f ◦ g ≃ idY . It will suffice to show that f and g are
bijections, in which case they are homeomorphisms by Lemma 2.3.
   Since X is irreducible and g ◦ f ≃ idX , the map g ◦ f must be surjective by
Lemma 2.8. Since X and Y are finite this means that #Y ≥ #X, where # denotes
cardinality. By the same reasons applied to f ◦ g we have #X ≥ #Y , and so
#X = #Y and f and g are bijections.                                                 
   By Lemma 2.8, reducibility of spaces can be judged by which selfmaps are homo-
topic to the identity. In the most restrictive case the identity map is not homotopic
to any other map.
Definition 2.11. We say a space X is rigid if the only map homotopic to idX is
idX .

2.2. Loops. Examining examples of small digital spaces reveals surprisingly few
to be irreducible. In fact, most spaces seem reducible to a point. The simplest
demonstrably irreducible spaces are cycles of points.
Definition 2.12. An m-gon (a cycle of m points), denoted Cm , is a set of m
distinct points, {c0 , c1 , . . . , cm−1 }, such that ci is adjacent to ci−1 and ci+1 and no
other points, where subscripts are read modulo m.
   When we refer to points in an m-gon, unless otherwise noted we will denote
them as ci ∈ Cm , and we will always read the subscripts modulo m.
   We will see later (Corollary 3.9) that when m ≥ 5, the m-gon Cm is irreducible
but not rigid. When 2 ≤ m ≤ 4, the space Cm is in homotopy equivalent to a point,
which we will see as a consequence of Theorem 4.9.
   When an m-gon with m ≥ 5 appears within a larger space, other edges in the
space may cause it to lose its irreducibility. We would like to investigate which
loops in a space can be reduced or made equal by homotopies. For example in the
space below, the “outer” 6-gon can be shrunk onto the 5-gon with a homotopy.




  The following definitions will formalize our efforts to capture this sort of infor-
mation. We adapt definitions from Boxer in [1].
Definition 2.13. In a space X, an m-loop is a map p : Cm → X. If p is an injection
such that p(ci ) ↔ p(ci±1 ) in X and there are no other adjacencies between points
in the image of Cm , then p is a simple m-loop. The length of an m-loop is m.
   It will sometimes be convenient to discuss simple paths rather than loops. A
simple path in a digital space X is a continuous injection p : [0, k]Z → X such that
p(i) ↔ p(i + 1) and p(i) ↔ p(i − 1) and there are no other adjacencies between the
p(i) for all i ∈ [0, k]Z .
   Though we define paths and loops as maps into X, we will sometimes indicate
them by tuples of points of X, so a path p : [0, k]Z → X would be written as
(p(0), p(1), . . . , p(k)). We may also indicate loops similarly.
   We wish to measure when two simple loops are homotopic, but unfortunately
this requires a slightly different notion than ordinary homotopy of maps. As in the
example above, we will need to allow two loops p : Cm → X and q : Cn → X to be
equivalent in some cases even when m 6= n, in which case it is impossible for p ≃ q
because the maps p and q have different domains. To skirt this technicality we use
the concept of trivial extensions, also adapted from Boxer. Informally speaking, p̄
is a trivial extension of p when p̄ is a path along the same points as p but with
some pauses for rest inserted.
Definition 2.14 (Adapted from [1], Definition 4.6). Given Cm = {c0 , . . . cm−1 },
p : Cm → X a loop, we say p̄ is a trivial extension of p if p̄ : Cn → X, where
Cn = {d0 , . . . dn−1 }, n ≥ m, such that there exists a sequence tk , k ∈ {0, . . . m − 1}

such that t0 = 0, p̄(dtk ) = p(ck ), if dtk < di < dtk+1 , then p̄(di ) = p(ctk ), and
p̄(di ) = p(cm−1 ) for all di > dtm−1 .
   Now we are ready to define our desired homotopy relation for simple loops,
perhaps having different lengths. If p and q are loops in X, then we say p ≈ q if
and only if p̄ ≃ q̄ for some trivial extensions p̄, q̄ of p and q having the same length.
   The appeal to trivial extensions is sometimes necessary even when p and q have
the same length to begin with. That is, it is possible for p ≈ q but p 6≃ q even when
p and q have the same length. Consider the following:




Let p be a simple 5-loop around the “inner” pentagon, and let q be a simple 5-loop
around the “outer” pentagon (with the same orientation as p). Then p ≈ q, but
the equivalence between p and q requires a 6-loop as an intermediate step, and so
p 6≃ q, since a true homotopy of p to q would require all intermediate steps to have
length 5.
Theorem 2.15. The above relation ≈ is an equivalence relation.
Proof. Reflexivity and symmetry are inherited from the homotopy equivalence re-
lation.
   To prove transitivity suppose p, q, r are loops in a space X such that p ≈ q and
q ≈ r. Let p̄, r̄ be trivial extensions with the same length, greater than the length
of q. Choose a trivial extension q̄ of q having length equal to that of p̄ and r̄. Now
since p ≈ q and q ≈ r, we have p̄ ≃ q̄ and r̄ ≃ q̄. So by transitivity of ≃, we have
that p̄ ≃ r̄, and thus, p ≈ r.                                                       
    We say a simple m-loop p is irreducible if there is no n-loop q ≈ p with n < m.

                       3. Two loop-counting invariants
  In this section we define two integer loop-counting invariants for spaces: Lm (X)
and Am (X).
3.1. A counting invariant for homotopy classes of irreducible loops. Our
first invariant simply counts equivalence classes of the above relation ≈.
Definition 3.1. For any positive integer m, let Lm (X) be the number of equiva-
lence classes of simple irreducible m-loops with respect to the equivalence relation
≈ on X.
    The numbers Lm (X) are homotopy equivalence invariants:
Theorem 3.2. If X and Y are homotopy equivalent, then Lm (X) = Lm (Y ) for
all m ∈ Z.
Proof. Let X and Y be homotopy equivalent spaces, and let f : X → Y and
g : Y → X be the homotopy equivalence.
     HOMOTOPY EQUIVALENCE IN GRAPH-LIKE DIGITAL TOPOLOGICAL SPACES                      7


   First we show that if p : Cm → X is irreducible then f ◦ p is also irreducible.
We prove the contrapositive of this statement: assume that f ◦ p is reducible in Y .
Then f ◦ p ≈ q where q has less than m points. Then g ◦ q must have fewer than m
points, but g ◦ q ≈ g ◦ f ◦ p ≃ p since g ◦ f ≃ idX , and thus p is reducible.
   Now to prove the theorem, by way of contradiction assume without loss of gener-
ality that for some m we have Lm (X) > Lm (Y ). Then by the pigeonhole principle
and the above paragraph, there exists two irreducible m-loops, p and q in X, such
that p 6≈ q but f ◦ p ≈ f ◦ q. Because g is continuous, g ◦ f ◦ p ≈ g ◦ f ◦ q. Since
g ◦ f ≃ idX , this implies that p ≈ q, a contradiction. Thus Lm (X) = Lm (Y ).    
   The following two theorems show that, for m ≤ 4, the values of Lm (X) are very
predictable.
Theorem 3.3. For any space, L1 (X) is the number of connected components of
X.
Proof. For 1-loops p and q, an equivalence p ≈ q is equivalent to the existence of a
path from p(c0 ) to q(c0 ). Thus the number of equivalence classes equals the number
of connected components.                                                          
Theorem 3.4. For any space X, we have L2 (X) = L3 (X) = L4 (X) = 0.
Proof. It suffices to show that any m-loop is reducible when m ∈ {2, 3, 4}. First
we consider m ∈ {2, 3}. Let p : Cm → X be a loop with m ∈ {2, 3}, and let
q : Cm → X be the loop given by q(ci ) = p(c0 ) for all i. Then p ≃ q in one step
because p(ci ) - q(ci ) = p(c0 ) for all i, but q is a trivial extension of a 1-loop, so p
is reducible.
    The case m = 4 requires a different homotopy: Let p : C4 → X be a 4-loop and
let q : C4 → X be given by q(c0 ) = q(c1 ) = p(c0 ) and q(c2 ) = q(c3 ) = p(c2 ). Then
it is routine to check that q is continuous and p ≃ q, but q is a trivial extension of
a 2-loop, and so p is reducible.                                                        

Definition 3.6. We say that a simple path p : [0, k]Z → X has no right angles if
for every i ∈ {1, . . . , k − 1}, the two edges from p(i − 1) to p(i) and p(i) to p(i + 1)
do not form two edges of any 4-loop (simple or otherwise). Similarly we say that
a simple loop p : Cm → X has no right angles if no two consecutive (modulo m)
edges of p form two edges of a 4-loop in X.
Lemma 3.7. Let p : [0, k]Z → X be a simple path with no right angles, and let q
be another path homotopic to p in one step with q(1) = p(0). Then q(i) = p(i − 1)
for all i ∈ {1, . . . , k}.
Proof. Our proof is by induction on on i. The base case i = 1 is our hypothesis
that q(1) = p(0). For the inductive step assume that q(i − 1) = p(i − 2), and we
will show that q(i) = p(i − 1).
   Since q is continuous we will have q(i) - q(i − 1) = p(i − 2), and since q is
homotopic to p in one step we have q(i) - p(i). Thus q(i) is adjacent to both of
p(i) and p(i − 2).
   We claim in fact that p(i − 1) is the only point in X adjacent to both of p(i) and
p(i−2). If there were some other such point x, then the path (p(i), p(i−1), p(i−2), x)
would form a 4-loop, two of whose edges are p(i − 2) to p(i − 1) and p(i − 1) to
p(i − 2). This would contradict our hypothesis that p has no right angles. Thus
p(i − 1) is the only point adjacent to both of p(i) and p(i − 2), and so q(i) = p(i − 1)
as desired.                                                                           
   We refer to the above lemma informally as a “path-pulling” lemma because it
states that a right-angle-free path must “pull” along its whole length when its
second point is moved to its first.
   We obtain a result for loops similar to Lemma 3.7 if we additionally assume that
our loops do not pass through 3-loops.
Theorem 3.8. Let p, q : Cm → X be simple m-loops in X with no right angles
and no edge of p or q part of a 3-loop. Then p ≃ q if and only if there is some k
with p(ci ) = q(ci+k ).
Proof. If p(ci ) = q(ci+k ) then p ≃ q by the homotopy H(x, t) = q(ci+t ) for t ∈
{0, . . . , k}, so one implication of the theorem is clear. For the converse, it suffices
to show that if p is homotopic to q in one step, then p(ci ) = q(ci+ǫ ) for some
ǫ ∈ {−1, 0, 1} and all i. Since p is homotopic to q in one step, we have q(c1 ) ∈
{p(c0 ), p(c1 ), p(c2 )}.
   For the case where q(c1 ) = p(c0 ) we will show that q(ci ) = p(ci−1 ) for all i.
Consider the paths p̄, q̄ : [0, m − 1]Z → X given by p̄(i) = p(ci ) and q̄(i) = q(ci ).
These paths have no right angles, are homotopic in 1 step, and q̄(1) = p̄(0). Thus
by Lemma 3.7 we have q̄(i) = p̄(i−1) for all i ∈ {1, . . . m−1}, and so q(ci ) = p(ci−1 )
for all such i. It remains to show that q(c0 ) = p(cm−1 ), but this is seen by applying
the same argument above to paths with domain on the interval [1, m]Z . This shows
that q(cm ) = p(cm−1 ), which gives the desired equality because cm = c0 .
   For the case where q(c1 ) = p(c2 ), consider the paths p̄, q̄ : [0, m − 1]Z → X given
by p̄(i) = p(c2−i ) and q̄(i) = q(c2−i ). Then q̄(1) = q(c1 ) = p(c2 ) = p̄(0). Lemma
3.7 applies to p̄ and q̄, and so by the same argument as the above paragraph we
have q(c2−i ) = p(c2−i−1 ) for all i, which is equivalent to q(ci ) = p(ci+1 ) for all i.

Corollary 3.9. For any m ≥ 5, the space Cm is irreducible but not rigid.

Theorem 3.12. For m ≥ 5 we have L1 (Cm ) = 1 and Lm (Cm ) = 2, and Li (Cm ) =

Proof. The fact that L1 (Cm ) = 1 is by Theorem 3.3, and L2 (Cm ) = 0 by Theorem

q2 (ci ) = cm−i . We will show that all simple m-loops in Cm are homotopic to either
q1 or q2 .

loop p is homotopic to a loop p′ with p′ (c0 ) = c0 . By continuity we have p′ (c1 ) - c0 ,
but since p′ is a bijection (because p is simple) and p′ (c0 ) = c0 we have p′ (c1 ) 6= c0
and so p′ (c1 ) ∈ {c−1 , c1 }. We will see below that both of these values for p′ (c1 ) are
permissible, and they determine completely the other values of p′ .
    In the first case where p′ (c1 ) = c1 , by continuity we have p′ (c2 ) - c1 but p′
is a bijection and p′ (c0 ) = c0 and p′ (c1 ) = c1 , so p′ (c2 ) cannot equal c0 or c1 , so
p′ (c2 ) = c2 . By the same arguments it is easy to see that p′ (ci ) = ci for all i, and
thus p′ = q1 .

that p′ (ci ) = c−i for all i. In other words, p′ (ci ) = cm−i for all i, and thus p′ = q2 .

Definition 3.15. Let p and q be loops in X. We say that p and q are ambiently
equivalent, denoted p ≈A q, if there are trivial extensions p̄ and q̄ of p and q with

this case we will sometimes say that p is ambiently equivalent to q by the map h.

equivalent to a k-loop for k < m. If p is ambiently equivalent to a k-loop for k < m
by some map h, we will sometimes say p is ambiently reducible [to q] by the map
h. We define our new loop-counting invariant exactly as we defined Lm , but using
≈A in place of ≈.
Definition 3.16. For any positive integer m, let Am (X) be the number of equiva-
lence classes of simple ambiently irreducible m-loops with respect to the equivalence
relation ≈A on X.
  Note that since any ambiently irreducible loop is irreducible, and since p ≈A q
implies p ≈ q we will always have Am (X) ≥ Lm (X) for any X and m.

Lemma 4.1. If there exists a complete subgraph S ⊆ X so that all vertices of X
are adjacent to points of S, then X is homotopy equivalent to a point.
Proof. Let f : X → {s} for some s ∈ S be a constant map and g : {s} → X be
the inclusion map. Then f ◦ g : {s} → {s} is the identity map and g ◦ f : X → X

H(x, 0) = x, the map H(x, 1) takes each point to an adjacent point in S, which
must exist by hypothesis, and H(x, 2) = s. This shows that g ◦ f ≃ idX and
therefore X is homotopy equivalent to {s}.                                    

Theorem 4.7. If X has no simple m-loop for any m ≥ 4, then X is homotopy
equivalent to a point.
Proof. Our proof is by induction on n, the number of vertices of X. If n = 1 then
there is nothing to show.
   Viewing X as a graph, our condition that there are no simple m-loops of degree
4 means that X is chordal [6]. A fundamental theorem in the study of chordal
graphs is that a graph is chordal if and only if it admits a perfect elimination
ordering [7, 10]. A perfect elimination ordering is an ordering of the vertices X =
{x1 , . . . , xn } such that for any i, the vertex xi together with any of its neighbors
xj with j > i form a complete subgraph of X.
   Assuming our space X has a perfect elimination ordering in this way, consider in
particular the first point x1 . The condition above means that x1 , together with all
its neighbors, forms a complete subgraph. Thus N (x1 ) is a complete subgraph and
so X is homotopy equivalent to X − {x1 } by Corollary 4.3. By induction X − {x1 }
is homotopy equivalent to a point, and thus by transitivity X is reducible to a
point.                                                                                

a space is rigid.
Definition 4.13. A lasso in X is a simple loop p : Cm → X and a simple path
r : [0, k]Z → X with r(k) = p(c0 ) such that k > 0 and m ≥ 5 and neither of p(c1 )
or p(cm−1 ) are adjacent to r(k − 1).
   We will say such a lasso has no right angles when each of p and r have no right
angles, and when no right angle is formed where r meets p. That is, the final edge
of r, together with either of the edges in p meeting p(x0 ), do not form two edges of
any 4-loop.
  Note that above we require p and r to be simple in themselves and at the the
point r(k), but points of r and p may be adjacent or even equal to one another
away from r(k).
Theorem 4.14. Let X be a space in which, for any two adjacent points x ↔ x′ ∈
X, there is a lasso with no right angles having path r : [0, k]Z → X with r(1) = x
and r(0) = x′ . Then X is rigid.
Proof. It suffices to show that the only map homotopic to idX in one step is idX .
Let f : X → X be homotopic to the identity in one step, and choose some x ∈ X.
For the sake of a contradiction assume that f (x) 6= x.
   Since x ↔ f (x), there is a lasso given by p : Cm → X and r : [0, k]Z → X with
r(1) = x and r(0) = f (x). By Lemma 3.19 applied to r we have f (r(k)) = r(k − 1),
and so f (p(c0 )) = r(k − 1). Let q : [0, 2]Z → X be the path (r(k − 1), r(k) =
p(c0 ), p(c1 )). Since our lasso has no right angles, q has no right angles and thus
Lemma 3.19 again gives f (p(c1 )) = p(c0 ). Similarly applying Lemma 3.19 to the
two-step path from r(k − 1) to p(cm−1 ) gives f (p(cm−1 )) = p(c0 ).

   Now choose some y = p(cj ) with 1 > j > m − 1 (here we use the fact that
m ≥ 5), and consider the arc of p from p(c0 ) to y through p(c1 ). Again we apply
Lemma 3.19 and obtain f (y) = p(cj−1 ). But we can consider the other arc of p
from p(c0 ) to y through p(cm−1 ) and this time Lemma 3.19 gives f (y) = p(cj+1 ),
which is a contradiction.                                                      

      5. Pointed homotopy equivalence and a question of Boxer
   The space X in the proof of Theorem 4.11 provides an answer to a question of
Boxer in [2]. For a pointed space (X, x0 ), and pointed maps f, g : X → X (with
f (x0 ) = x0 and g(x0 ) = x0 ), a pointed homotopy from f to g is a homotopy H
from f to g satsifying H(x0 , t) = x0 for all t. Pointed spaces (X, x0 ) and (Y, y0 )
are pointed homotopy equivalent if there is a homotopy equivalence f : X → Y and
g : Y → X with f ◦ g ≃ idY and g ◦ f ≃ idX by pointed homotopies.
   Boxer asks: if X and Y are homotopy equivalent, must they be pointed ho-
motopy equivalent for any choice of base points x0 and y0 ? The question arises
naturally when studying the digital fundamental group, where the natural cate-
gory of discourse is pointed spaces with pointed homotopies.
   The answer to Boxer’s question is “no”. Consider the space called X in the proof
of Theorem 4.11:

In the proof of Theorem 4.11 we show that X is homotopy equivalent to C5 . We
will show that this equivalence cannot be realized by pointed homotopies.
   We will view X as the pointed space (X, x0 ). It will suffice to show that no
function is pointed homotopic to idX in one step other than idX itself. Let f be
a map with f ≃ idX by a pointed homotopy in one step, and we will show that
f (x) = x for all x ∈ X by path-pulling arguments using Lemma 3.19.
   The right angles in X at x4 and x′4 mean that we cannot apply Lemma 3.19
indiscriminately, but a slight variation of the lemma will hold even for certain paths
through x4 and x′4 . For example if we consider the path (x2 , x3 , x4 , x0 ) and assume
that f (x3 ) = x2 , we can conclude by the proof of Lemma 3.19 that f (x0 ) ∈ {x4 , x′4 }.
   Now we show that f must be the identity. Since f ≃ idX by a pointed homotopy
with base point x0 we will have f (x0 ) = x0 . We will show that f is the identity on
each of the other points individually, starting with x3 .
   Now consider x3 : Since f ≃ idX in one step, f (x3 ) ∈ {x2 , x3 , x4 , x′4 }. If f (x3 ) =
x4 then Lemma 3.19 applied to the path (x4 , x3 , x2 , x1 , x0 ) gives f (x0 ) = x1 which
is a contradiction since f (x0 ) = x0 . Similarly f (x3 ) 6= x′4 . If f (x3 ) = x2 then our
variation of Lemma 3.19 applied to (x2 , x3 , x4 , x0 ) gives f (x0 ) ∈ {x4 , x′4 } which is
again a contradiction. Thus we conclude that f (x3 ) = x3 .
   Now consider x4 : Since f is homotopic to the identity in one step, f (x4 ) ∈
{x0 , x4 , x3 }. As above, if f (x4 ) = x3 then f (x0 ) ∈ {x4 , x′4 } which a contradiction.
Similarly if f (x4 ) = x0 then f (x3 ) ∈ {x4 , x′4 } which contradicts the fact above that
f (x3 ) = x3 . Thus we have f (x4 ) = x4 . For exactly the same reasons we have
f (x′4 ) = x′4 .
   It remains to show that f (x1 ) = x1 and f (x2 ) = x2 . For x1 we have f (x1 ) ∈
{x0 , x1 , x2 }. If f (x1 ) = x2 then Lemma 3.19 applied to (x2 , x1 , x0 ) gives f (x0 ) = x1
which is a contradiction. If f (x1 ) = x0 then Lemma 3.19 applied to the path
(x0 , x1 , x2 , x3 ) gives f (x3 ) = x2 . But we have already shown that f (x3 ) = x3 and
so we conclude that f (x1 ) = x1 . Similar arguments show that f (x2 ) = x2 , and
thus f = idX .
   We have shown there are no maps pointed homotopic to idX other than idX
(one could say that X is “pointed rigid”), and so the pointed space (X, x0 ) is not
pointed homotopy equivalent to (C5 , c) for any c ∈ C5 , even though X is homotopy
equivalent to C5 when we do not require pointed homotopies. Similar arguments
will show that (X, x) is not pointed homotopy equivalent to C5 regardless of which
point x ∈ X is chosen as the base point.
