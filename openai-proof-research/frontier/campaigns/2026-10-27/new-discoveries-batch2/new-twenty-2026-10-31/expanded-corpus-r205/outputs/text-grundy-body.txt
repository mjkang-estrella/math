                                                                     Abstract
                                A sequence of vertices of a graph is legal when every vertex in it dominates some vertex
                            that none of the earlier ones dominated, and the Grundy domination number of the graph
                            is the length of its longest legal sequence. In the strong product of two graphs, two vertices
                            are adjacent when each coordinate is equal or adjacent in its factor. It has been known
                            since 2016 that the Grundy domination number of a strong product is at least the product
                            of the Grundy domination numbers of the two factors, and it was conjectured, by Brešar,
                            Bujtás, Gologranc, Klavžar, Košmrlj, Patkós, Tuza and Vizer, that equality always holds.
                            We show that the conjecture is false, and false by an unbounded factor: for every number
                            C there are graphs whose strong product has Grundy domination number more than C
                            times the product of the two factors. The graphs are built on the five-element subsets of an
                            m-element set, with adjacency decided by the size of the intersection; incidence   matrices
                            over the fields with two and three elements bound the twofactors by m and m     2 , while the
                                                                                       m
                            diagonal of the product is an independent set of size 5 . We also compute the Grundy
                            domination number of the graph on {0, . . . , N − 1} in which u and v are adjacent when
                            |u − v| ≡ 2 (mod 3): it is ⌊(N + 1)/3⌋ + 3 for every N ≥ 6. Its strong product with the
                            octahedron has a legal sequence of length N , so the conjecture already fails for N = 19,
                            on a graph with 114 vertices, and for every N ≥ 21. Every statement is proved by hand.

1        Introduction
Let G be a finite simple graph and write N [v] for the closed neighbourhood of a vertex v,
the set consisting of v and its neighbours. A sequence (v1 , . . . , vk ) of vertices of G is legal if
every vi dominates a vertex that none of its predecessors dominates, that is,
                                                 [
                                     N [vi ] \         N [vj ] ̸= ∅   (1 ≤ i ≤ k);
                                                 j<i

a vertex in this difference is a footprint of vi .1 The Grundy domination number γgr (G) is
the length of a longest legal sequence; a longest legal sequence always dominates G, since
otherwise an undominated vertex could be appended. The parameter was introduced by
Brešar, Gologranc, Milanič, Rall and Rizzi [7], who connected it with the domination game.
    In the strong product G ⊠ H two distinct vertices (g, h) and (g ′ , h′ ) are adjacent when
g ∈ NG [g] and h′ ∈ NH [h]; equivalently NG⊠H [(g, h)] = NG [g] × NH [h]. Taking a Grundy
  ′

dominating sequence of G and one of H and listing their pairs in lexicographic order gives a
legal sequence of G ⊠ H, so

                                         γgr (G ⊠ H) ≥ γgr (G) γgr (H)                                               (1.1)

for all G and H [6, Proposition 21].2 In the same paper the authors conjectured that the
bound is always attained.

Conjecture 1.1 ([6, Conjecture 22]). For all graphs G and H, γgr (G ⊠ H) = γgr (G) γgr (H).

   The conjecture holds when G is a caterpillar [6] and, more generally, when G is a forest,
by the work of Bell, Driscoll, Krop and Wolff [2]. A proof for all graphs was announced by
Herrman and Smith [8] and later withdrawn.3 We show that the conjecture is false and that
no constant multiple of its proposed value is an upper bound.

Theorem 1.2. For every C > 0 there are nonempty finite graphs G, H such that

                                         γgr (G ⊠ H) > C γgr (G)γgr (H).

    The proof is in Sections 3 to 5. After the first vertex of a legal sequence is chosen in a

2       Unbounded product ratios
To obtain unbounded ratios we vary both factors. The factor bounds come from incidence
matrices over different fields, in the manner of the intersection-congruence arguments used by
Alon [1, Section 3] for the Shannon capacity; the application here needs the nonzero entries
to be exactly the closed neighbourhoods, so that every legal sequence, and not only every
independent set, is bounded. No result about the Shannon capacity is used.
Lemma 2.1. Let G be a finite graph and let M be a matrix over a field F, with rows and
columns indexed by V (G). Suppose that

                                    Muv ̸= 0      ⇐⇒         v ∈ NG [u].

Then γgr (G) ≤ rankF M .
Proof. Let (v1 , . . . , vk ) be legal and choose a footprint wi for each vi . The footprints are
distinct, since wj is not dominated by v1 , . . . , vj−1 while wi ∈ N [vi ] for i < j. If i < j, then
wj ∈/ NG [vi ], so Mvi wj = 0, and each Mvi wi is nonzero. The submatrix with rows v1 , . . . , vk
and columns w1 , . . . , wk , in this order, is lower triangular with nonzero diagonal, so its rank
is k, and k ≤ rank M .

   The condition on M cannot be weakened to vanishing on non-edges: the footprint wi
need not equal vi , so the entries Muv with v a neighbour of u must be nonzero as well.4
    4
    For an independent set the diagonal alone would do; this is the difference between bounding the indepen-
dence number, as in the Shannon capacity arguments, and bounding the Grundy domination number.

Lemma 3.1. γgr (K2,2,2 ) = 2.
Proof. The sequence (g0 , x0 ) is legal: x0 footprints itself. Conversely, let (u, v) be legal. If v
is the antipode of u, then N [u] ∪ N [v] is the whole vertex set; otherwise N [u] misses only the
antipode of u, which lies in N [v]. Either way nothing is left for a third vertex to footprint.

    In HN we write ai = 3i, bi = 3i + 1 and ci = 3i + 2. Two vertices of the same residue
class modulo 3 are never adjacent, and for vertices of different classes
              ai ∼ cj ⇐⇒ j ≥ i,            bi ∼ aj ⇐⇒ j > i,                 ci ∼ bj ⇐⇒ j > i,     (3.1)
because cj − ai = 3(j − i) + 2, aj − bi = 3(j − i) − 1 and bj − ci = 3(j − i) − 1, and a difference
3k − 1 with k ≤ 0 has absolute value 3|k| + 1 ≡ 1 (mod 3).


4    A long legal sequence in the product
The construction uses one property of HN : with the colouring v 7→ v mod 3, every edge uv
with u < v satisfies u ≡ v + 1 (mod 3), since v − u ≡ 2. We state it for any graph with this
property.
Proposition 4.1. Let H be a graph with vertices v0 , . . . , vN −1 and a map t : V (H) → Z3
such that t(vi ) ≡ t(vj ) + 1 (mod 3) for every edge vi vj with i < j. Then the sequence

is legal in K2,2,2 ⊠ H, and γgr (K2,2,2 ⊠ H) ≥ N . In particular γgr (K2,2,2 ⊠ HN ) ≥ N for every
N ≥ 1, with t(v) = v mod 3.
Proof. We show that sj footprints fj = (xt(vj )+1 , vj ), the indices of g and x being taken

5      The Grundy domination number of HN
We prove the formula of Theorem 1.3 for every N ≥ 6. A legal sequence in an induced subgraph
remains legal in the larger graph, with the same footprints, since closed neighbourhoods in
the subgraph are the intersections of those in the larger graph with the subgraph. We use
this observation for both bounds.

Lemma 5.1. For every integer q ≥ 1, γgr (H3q+1 ) ≤ q + 3.

Proof. Put n = 3q + 1. For 1 ≤ d < n, the conditions d ≡ 2 (mod 3) and n − d ≡ 2 (mod 3)
are equivalent, because n ≡ 1. So u and v are adjacent in Hn exactly when (u − v) mod n lies
in the set of residues congruent to 2 modulo 3, and translation modulo n preserves adjacency.5
A legal sequence stays legal under a graph automorphism, so we may translate a nonempty
legal sequence so that its first vertex is 0.
    The vertex 0 dominates itself and the vertices Ck = 3k + 2, 0 ≤ k < q. The undominated
vertices are
                        Xi = 3(i + 1),      Yj = 3j + 1    (0 ≤ i, j < q).
There are no edges within either class, and

                                       Xi ∼ Yj        ⇐⇒    i ≥ j,                                   (5.1)

because Xi − Yj = 3(i − j) + 2 when i ≥ j, and Yj − Xi = 3(j − i − 1) + 1 when j > i. Thus
the undominated vertices induce a half graph with the perfect matching Xi Yi (Figure 3).
    Follow the sequence from its second vertex on until either a vertex Ck is selected or the
selected X- and Y -vertices contain an edge for the first time. Before either event, let I and
J be the sets of selected indices on the two sides, and put r = |I|, s = |J|. By (5.1), the
absence of edges means
                                  i<j      (i ∈ I, j ∈ J).                                (5.2)
We treat the three possibilities in turn.

Lemma 5.2. If N ≥ 6 and q = ⌊(N + 1)/3⌋, then γgr (HN ) ≥ q + 3.

Proof. For q = 2, the sequence (1, 3, 5, 0, 2) in H6 has the successive footprints 1, 5, 0, 2, 4
and remains legal in H7 . For q = 3, the sequence (0, 7, 2, 4, 5, 1) in H8 has the successive
footprints 0, 7, 4, 6, 3, 1 and remains legal in H9 and H10 .6
    For q ≥ 4 it suffices to work in H3q−1 , which is an induced subgraph of HN because
3q − 1 ≤ N . Its vertices are ai , bi for 0 ≤ i ≤ q − 1 and ci for 0 ≤ i ≤ q − 2. Take the sequence

                                 a0 , a1 , . . . , aq−3 , bq−1 , cq−3 , c0 , aq−2 , b0 .                          (5.3)

The first q − 2 vertices footprint themselves, since vertices of one class are never adjacent.
The last five have the successive footprints

                                    bq−1 ,    bq−2 ,    bq−3 ,    aq−2 ,     aq−1 ,

by (3.1): bq−1 is adjacent to no ai with i ≤ q − 3, so it is undominated and footprints itself;
bq−2 is adjacent to cq−3 and not to any earlier vertex of the sequence; bq−3 is adjacent to c0 ,
because q − 3 > 0, and not to a0 , . . . , aq−3 , bq−1 or cq−3 ; neither aq−2 nor aq−1 is adjacent
to bq−1 , cq−3 or c0 , so aq−2 footprints itself and b0 footprints aq−1 . The sequence has length
(q − 2) + 5 = q + 3.

Proof of Theorem 1.3. Put q = ⌊(N + 1)/3⌋. Then 3q ≤ N + 1, so N ≤ 3q + 1 and HN is an
induced subgraph of H3q+1 ; Lemma 5.1 gives γgr (HN ) ≤ q + 3, and Lemma 5.2 gives equality.
The product bound is Proposition 4.1.
    At N = 19 the formula gives γgr (H19 ) = 9, and 19 > 2 · 9. For N ≥ 21 write N = 3q − 1,
3q or 3q + 1; the differences N − 2(q + 3) are q − 7, q − 6 and q − 5, and each is positive in its
range (q ≥ 8, q ≥ 7 and q ≥ 7 respectively). Hence N > 2γgr (HN ) = γgr (K2,2,2 )γgr (HN ) for
N = 19 and every N ≥ 21, while γgr (K2,2,2 ⊠ HN ) ≥ N .

Example 5.3. For N = 19 the sequence (5.3) with q = 6, in H17 ⊆ H19 , is (0, 3, 6, 9, 16,
11, 2, 12, 1), of length 9 = γgr (H19 ), and K2,2,2 ⊠ H19 has 114 vertices and a legal sequence

Remark 5.4. Everything above is proved in full. Theorem 1.2 rests on Lemma 2.1 and the
two incidence matrices; Theorem 1.3 on the half-graph count of Lemma 5.1, the explicit
sequences of Lemma 5.2 and Proposition 4.1. The only results quoted from elsewhere are
the inequality (1.1) and the bound γgr (G ⊠ H) ≤ |V (G)|γgr (H), both from [6] with proofs of
a few lines, and neither is needed for the counterexamples themselves. Nothing is claimed
about the exact values of γgr (Am ), γgr (Bm ) or γgr (Am ⊠ Bm ) beyond the three bounds, nor
about the smallest possible counterexample.
