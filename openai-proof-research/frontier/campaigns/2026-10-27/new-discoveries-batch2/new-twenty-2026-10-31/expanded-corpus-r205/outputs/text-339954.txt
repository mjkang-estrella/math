                                                            VIETORIS–RIPS COMPLEXES OF REGULAR POLYGONS


                                                    HENRY ADAMS, SAMIR CHOWDHURY, ADAM QUINN JAFFE, AND BONGINKOSI SIBANDA


                                                    Abstract. Persistent homology has emerged as a novel tool for data analysis in the past two decades.
                                                    However, there are still very few shapes or even manifolds whose persistent homology barcodes (say of
                                                    the Vietoris–Rips complex) are fully known. Towards this direction, let Pn be the boundary of a regular
                                                    polygon in the plane with n sides; we describe the homotopy types of Vietoris–Rips complexes of Pn .
                                                    Indeed, when n = (k + 1)!! is an odd double factorial, we provide a complete characterization of the
                                                    homotopy types and persistent homology of the Vietoris–Rips complexes of Pn up to a scale parameter
                                                    rn , where rn approaches the diameter of Pn as n → ∞. Surprisingly, these homotopy types include
                                                    spheres of all dimensions. Roughly speaking, the number of higher-dimensional spheres appearing is
                                                    linked to the number of equilateral (but not necessarily equiangular) stars that can be inscribed into




arXiv:1807.10971v1 [math.MG] 28 Jul 2018
                                                    Pn . As our main tool we use the recently-developed theory of cyclic graphs and winding fractions.
                                                    Furthermore, we show that the Vietoris–Rips complex of an arbitrarily dense subset of Pn need not
                                                    be homotopy equivalent to the Vietoris–Rips complex of Pn itself, and indeed, these two complexes
                                                    can have different homology groups in arbitrarily high dimensions. As an application of our results, we
                                                    provide a lower bound on the Gromov–Hausdorff distance between Pn and the circle.




                                                                                            1. Introduction
                                              Let S 1 = {(x, y) ∈ R2 | x2 + y 2 = 1} be the unit circle in the plane. Given an integer n ≥ 3, let
                                           Pn ⊆ R2 be the boundary of the regular polygon inscribed in S 1 with n vertices. In other words, Pn is a
                                           piecewise-linear closed curve in the complex plane with vertices the n-th roots of unity. We equip Pn
                                           with the Euclidean metric. What can be said about the homotopy types and the persistent homology of
                                           the Vietoris–Rips simplicial complexes VR(Pn ; r)?
                                              One motivation for such questions is the application of topology to data analysis [20, 10]. Suppose one
                                           is given a finite data set X sampled from some unknown underlying infinite metric space M , and would
                                           like to use X to recover information about M . For example, one can use persistent homology to attempt
                                           to recover the homology groups of M [20, 16]. Though data set X is typically finite, as the density of X
                                           increases, the persistent homology of VR(X; r) converges to the persistent homology of VR(M ; r) [14].
                                           Hence Vietoris–Rips complexes of infinite metric spaces are important, as they are the limiting objects of
                                           Vietoris–Rips complexes of finite data sets. Nevertheless, extremely little is known about the persistent
                                           homology of Vietoris–Rips complexes of basic shapes. In this paper, we develop the tools necessary to
                                           describe the homotopy types and persistent homology of Vietoris–Rips complexes of regular polygons.
                                              Another motivation for such questions is the application of topology to machine learning. One reason
                                           for incorporating more mathematics in machine learning is to try to improve not only the predictive
                                           power of machine learning algorithms, but also their interpretability. There are by now a wide variety of
                                           ways to turn the output from persistent homology into feature vectors for a machine learning task; see
                                           for example [6, 8, 11, 15, 18, 19, 24, 25, 33, 34]. Nevertheless, the interpretation of persistent homology
                                           barcodes (or equivalently diagrams, see [13]) at larger scale parameters is still under development, even
                                           though we expect to be able to extract more geometric information about a space by considering its
                                           persistence not only at small scales but also at large scales [36, Section 12]. As a starting example, in
                                           this paper we detail the precise geometric information that higher-dimensional persisistent homology
                                           measures in a regular polygon.
                                              Our main tool will be the structure of cyclic graphs and their clique complexes. Roughly speaking,
                                           a cyclic graph is a directed graph in which the vertex set is equipped with a cyclic order, such that
                                           whenever there is a directed edge u → v, then there is also a directed edge u → w → v for all vertices w
                                           cyclically between u and v. The clique complex (of the underlying undirected graph) is the simplicial
                                           complex on the same vertex set, with faces given by the cliques (complete subgraphs) of the undirected

   Let S 1 be the unit circle equipped with the Euclidean metric. As n → ∞, the metric polygons
Pn converge in the Gromov-Hausdorff distance to the circle S 1 . It follows from stability [14] that the
persistent homology of VR(Pn ; r) converges to that of VR(S 1 ; r). The persistent homology of VR(S 1 ; r)
is known: as r increases, VR(S 1 ; r) obtains the homotopy types of S 1 , S 3 , S 5 , S 7 , . . . , until finally it
is contractible [2]. In this paper we study the topological features present in the persistent homology
of VR(Pn ; r) before achieving convergence. One interesting observation is that even though VR(S 1 ; r)
contains an i-dimensional persistent homology interval of positive length if and only if i is odd, the
polygon shapes Pn provide examples of arbitrarily close metric spaces homeomorphic to S 1 which can
contain nontrivial persistent i-dimensional homology even for i even and arbitrarily large.
   The stability of persistent homology guarantees that the Gromov-Hausdorff distance between two
compact metric spaces is bounded below by half the bottleneck distance between the i-dimensional
persistent homology barcodes of the Vietoris-Rips complexes of the two spaces [14]. The Gromov-
Hausdorff distance is in general NP-hard to compute [28], whereas both the persistent homology and
bottleneck distance computations can be carried out in polynomial time [21, 38, 30]. Therefore, knowledge
of the persistent homology of VR(Pn ; r) enables us to provide a lower bound on the Gromov-Hausdorff
distance between S 1 and Pn (Section 8).
   As a secondary result, we study finite subsets of the regular polygons. This work is related to Latschev’s
Theorem [27, Theorem 1.1], which states that if M is a Riemannian manifold and scale r is sufficiently
small, then for any sufficiently dense X ⊆ M we have a homotopy equivalence VR< (X; r) ≃ M . By
Hausmann’s Theorem [23] we also have M ≃ VR< (M ; r), giving VR< (X; r) ≃ VR< (M ; r) for r
sufficiently small and X sufficiently dense. In Question 9.3 we ask: for M a Riemannian manifold, is it
also true at larger scale parameters r that VR< (X; r) ≃ VR< (M ; r) for X sufficiently dense depending
on r? This is known to be true in the case when M = S 1 is the circle [2], but to our knowledge this
question is unknown for a general Riemannian manifold. Our Theorem 7.2(ii) shows that Question 9.3
has a negative answer when M is not Riemannian, for example if M = Pn is a regular polygon equipped
with the Euclidean metric.

 the theory of infinite cyclic graphs in the metric setting. Metric cyclic graphs are more broadly applicable

 notation, and in Section 3 we review cyclic graphs. In Section 4 we introduce our main tool, metric cyclic

Graphs and directed graphs. A directed graph is a pair G = (V, E) with V the set of vertices and
E ⊆ V × V the set of directed edges, where we require that there are no loops and that no edges are
oriented in both directions. We also denote the set of vertices by V (G), and the directed edge (u, w) will
also be denoted u → w. A homomorphism of directed graphs h : G → G̃ is a vertex map such that for
every edge u → w in G, either h(u) = h(w) or there is an edge h(u) → h(w) in G̃. For a vertex u ∈ V we
define the out- and in-neighborhoods
                          N + (G, u) = {w | u → w},          N − (G, u) = {w | w → u},
as well as their respective closed versions
                          N + [G, u] = N + (G, u) ∪ u,       N − [G, u] = N − (G, u) ∪ u.
An undirected graph is a graph in which the orientations on the edges are omitted. Given a graph
G = (V, E) (directed or undirected) and a subset S ⊆ V , we write G[S] to denote the induced subgraph
with vertex set S.

equivalent topological spaces, then we write X ≃ Z. The i-fold wedge sum of a space X with itself

Simplicial complexes. A geometric k-simplex is the convex hull of k + 1 affinely independent points in
Euclidean space. A simplicial complex is a collection of geometric simplices such that
      • Every face of a simplex in K also belongs to K.

between an abstract simplicial complex and its geometric realization.
   For G an undirected graph, the clique complex Cl(G) is the simplicial complex with vertex set V (G)
and with faces determined by all cliques (complete subgraphs) of G.

Persistent homology. Given an increasing sequence (i.e. a filtration) of topological spaces
                                                  X0 ,→ X1 ,→ . . . ,→ Xk−1 ,→ Xk ,
one can apply the i-dimensional homology functor with coefficients in a field F to obtain a sequence of
vector spaces and linear maps
                       Hi (X0 ; F) → Hi (X1 ; F) → . . . → Hi (Xk−1 ; F) → Hi (Xk ; F).
The persistent homology of this sequence will be a multiset of intervals in which the start and end of
each interval parametrizes, roughly speaking, the birth and death time of a topological feature in this
filtered topological space [20].
    For example, let X be a metric space, and consider the Vietoris–Rips complexes VR(X; r) over a
finite set of r-values r0 < r1 < . . . < rk−1 < rk . We obtain an increasing sequence of topological spaces
                    VR(X; r0 ) ,→ VR(X; r1 ) ,→ . . . ,→ VR(X; rk−1 ) ,→ VR(X; rk ),
whose persistent homology describes how the shape of VR(X; r) changes as r increases. If X is a sample
from some unknown underling metric space M , then the persistent homological features in VR(X; r) are
often taken as an estimate of the homology of M , whereas the remaining short-lived features are often
regarded as noise [10, 16].
   Instead of selecting a finite number of scale parameters r0 < r1 < . . . < rk−1 < rk , one can instead
vary r over the entire interval (0, ∞). Though it is more subtle, the theory of persistent homology over
the reals also exists (see for example [13]). The persistent homology diagrams of VR(Pn ; r) which we
describe, for example in Corollary 6.4, are over all real scale parameters r ∈ (0, ∞).

                                                                        3. Cyclic graphs
   In this section we review the basic theory of cyclic graphs. Cyclic graphs are of central importance
to our results—we have a clear understanding of the homotopy type of simplicial complex VR(Pn ; r)
whenever VR(Pn ; r) is a cyclic graph. Specifically, the Vietoris-Rips complex will be homotopy equivalent
to either an odd sphere or a wedge sum of even spheres of the same dimension. This theory in this section
was developed and used in [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 32].
Definition 3.1. Let G = (V, E) be a directed graph, where V is a equipped with a fixed injective map
into S 1 . Therefore V inherits a counterclockwise ordering from S 1 , along with the subspace topology.
Then G is said to be cyclic if whenever there is a directed edge u0 → u1 , there are also edges u0 → w → u1
for all u0 ≺ w ≺ u1 ≺ u0 .
   The Vietoris–Rips graph of a circle, of an ellipse of small eccentricity, or of a regular regular polygon
Pn (so long as the scale is sufficiently small) are examples of cyclic graphs with an infinite number of
vertices. However, we will introduce the theory in the (simpler) finite case first.

3.1. Finite cyclic graphs. We call a cyclic graph G finite if its underlying vertex set V is finite, and

Lemma 3.4. Let G = (V, E) be a finite cyclic graph. Then all the periodic orbits of G have the same

Proof of Lemma 3.4. First note that for any u, w ∈ V ,
            u ≺ w  f (u) ≺ u implies u ≺ w  f (u)  f (w)  f 2 (u) ≺ f (u).                          (monotonicity)
To see this, let u ≺ w  f (u) ≺ u in V . Towards a contradiction, suppose f (w) ≺ f (u)  f 2 (u)  f (w).
But then we have u ≺ w  f (w) ≺ f (u)  u. Because G is cyclic, the presence of the edge u → f (u)
would imply the presence of the edge w → f (u). This contradicts the strict relation f (w) 6= f (u) in the
ordering f (w) ≺ f (u)  f 2 (u)  f (w).
    Next let A = {u, f (u), f 2 (u), . . . , f ` (u)} be a periodic orbit of length `. Let B be a distinct periodic orbit.
We claim that by relabeling terms if necessary, we can pick a vertex w ∈ B such that u ≺ w ≺ f (u)  u.
To see this, suppose towards a contradiction that no such w ∈ B exists. Then there must be some w ∈ B
such that w ≺ u  f (u) ≺ f (w) ≺ w, where the strict relation w 6= u holds because B is distinct from A.
But because G is cyclic, this contradicts monotonicity above.
    Let w ∈ B be such that u ≺ w ≺ f (u)  u. By the monotonicity observation, we have f (u) ≺ f (w) ≺
f 2 (u)  f (u). Here the strict relations hold because B and A are distinct orbits. By induction, we get
that u = f ` (u) ≺ f ` (w) ≺ f `+1 (u) = f (u)  u.
    Now there are three cases: either f ` (w) = w, or u ≺ f ` (w) ≺ w ≺ f (u)  u, or u ≺ w ≺ f ` (w) ≺
f (u)  u. We show that the last two cases cannot occur.
    Towards a contradiction, suppose u ≺ f ` (w) ≺ w ≺ f (u)  u. Then by repeated application of f ` , we
get the following sequence of inequalities: u  . . .  f 3` (w)  f 2` (w)  f ` (w)  w ≺ u. Because V is
finite and the sequence is “bounded below” by u (which is not in the orbit of w), there must exist some
nonnegative integer k such that f k` (w) = f (k+1)` (w). But then {f k` (w), f k`+1 (w), . . . , f k`+` (w)} forms

that u ≺ w ≺ f ` (w) ≺ f (u)  u also cannot occur, and so we must have f ` (w) = w. Thus the orbit of w
also has length `.

follows that they have the same winding number.                                                                        

                                         4. Metric cyclic graphs
   In this section we study the properties of cyclic graphs which are also equipped with a compatible
metric structure. Many of the properties in this section are generalizations of [5, Section 6], in which the
cyclic graph has as its vertex set an ellipse of small eccentricity with the Euclidean metric.
Definition 4.1. A cyclic graph G is metric when its vertex set V = V (G) is equipped with a metric dV
such that:
    (1) the topology induced by dV is the same as the subspace topology V inherits from its fixed injection
        into S 1 , and
    (2) for all directed paths u0 → u1 → u2 in G, we have dV (u0 , u1 ) < dV (u0 , u2 ).
   It is important to remark that the metric dV on V need not be the same as the geodesic metric on S 1 .
Indeed, the example of interest in this paper is when V is a subset of the regular polygon Pn equipped
with the Euclidean metric. We identify V as a subset of S 1 via the fixed injective map from Pn into S 1
as described in Section 2. However, the metric dV on V is the restriction of the Euclidean metric on
Pn ⊆ R2 , which is different from the geodesic metric on S 1 .

                                                     9. Conclusion
   We end with some open questions motivated by this work.
Question 9.1. When the graph VR(Pn ; r) is not cyclic, what can be said about the homotopy type of
the simplicial complex VR(Pn ; r)?
Question 9.2. For l > 1 and n not a multiple of Pn , what is an analytic formula for sn,l and tn,l ? This
question is related to proving Conjecture 5.10 in the cases where it remains open.
   Let V = S 1 be the circle of unit radius, equipped with the Euclidean metric. It is necessary that
sn,l → sV,l and tn,l → tV,l as n → ∞, by virtue of the fact that Pn converges to S 1 as n → ∞ (for
example in the Hausdorff distance).
   As explained in the introduction, we think of the following question as an analogue of Latschev’s
Theorem [27] at higher scale parameters.
Question 9.3. Let M be a Riemannian manifold, and let scale r > 0 be arbitrary. Is it true that for
X ⊆ M sufficiently dense (depending on M and r), we have a homotopy equivalence VR< (X; r) ≃
VR< (M ; r)?
                                                              24
   Latschev’s Theorem provides a positive answer when r is sufficiently small depending on M , and [2]
provides a positive answer at all scales r when M = S 1 is the circle. Related questions for the fundamental
group are considered in [37]. Our Theorem 7.2(ii) provides a negative answer to Question 9.3 if the
assumption that M is a Riemmanian manifold is removed.

the previous work and the assumption that s2l+1 does not have a local minimum at x0 , we know that

direction, S2l+1 (x) becomes either more parallel or less parallel to 4IJK. Thus our previous work shows
that s2l+1 (x) varies strictly monotonically. But this contradicts the assumption that s2l+1 has a local
maximum at x0 . It follows that s2l+1 has a local minimum at x0 , and moreover that 4P QR is parallel
to 4IJK.

Corollary B.4 that s2l+1 (x) is strictly increasing until x reaches a vertex crossing x1 . At this point there
are multiple triangles (each with its own Napoleon triangle) inside which S2l+1 (x1 ) can be inscribed. By
symmetry and Lemma 5.9, we know that s2l+1 (x) decreases again after x passes x1 . In particular, s2l+1
has a local maximum at the vertex crossing x1 .

crossing. Towards a contradiction, suppose x2 is another vertex crossing. Since x1 was a local maximizer

and s2l+1 is strictly monotonic, we know that s2l+1 (x) strictly decreases as x approaches x2 . But the

It follows that x2 is a midpoint crossing.
