                                                                SETS IN Rd DETERMINING k TAXICAB DISTANCES


                                                      VAJRESH BALAJI,       OLIVIA EDWARDS, ANNE MARIE LOFTIN, SOLOMON MCHARO,
                                                                           LO PHILLIPS, ALEX RICE, BINEYAM TSEGAYE


                                                   Abstract. We address an analog of a problem introduced by Erdős and Fishburn, itself an inverse formu-
                                                   lation of the famous Erdős distance problem, in which the usual Euclidean distance is replaced with the




arXiv:1911.08067v2 [math.CO] 20 Nov 2019
                                                   metric induced by the `1 -norm, commonly referred to as the taxicab metric. Specifically, we investigate the
                                                   following question: given d, k ∈ N, what is the maximum size of a subset of Rd that determines at most
                                                   k distinct taxicab distances, and can all such optimal arrangements be classified? We completely resolve
                                                   the question in dimension d = 2, as well as the k = 1 case in dimension d = 3, and we also provide a full
                                                   resolution in the general case under an additional hypothesis.




                                                                                              1. Introduction

                                              In 1946, Erdős [2] asked a now famous question: given n ∈ N, what is the minimum number of distinct
                                           distances determined by n points in√a plane? Denoting this minimum by f (n), he proved via an elementary
                                                                                                                                           √
                                           counting argument
                                                          √ that √ f (n) = Ω( n), and he conjectured that the correct order of growth is n/ log n, as
                                           attained by a n × n integer grid. After decades of incremental progress, this conjecture was effectively
                                           resolved in a celebrated result of Guth and Katz [5], who established that f (n) = Ω(n/ log n).
                                              50 years after Erdős’s original paper, Erdős and Fishburn [3] addressed the same question from the inverse
                                           perspective, and aspired to precise results in fixed cases rather than general asymptotic results. Specifically,
                                           they investigated the following: given k ∈ N, what is the maximum number of points in a plane that
                                           determine at most k distinct distances, and can such optimal arrangements be classified? This question,
                                           which we refer to as the Erdős-Fishburn problem, was fully resolved by Erdős and Fishburn for 1 ≤ k ≤ 4,
                                           then by Shinahara [8] for k = 5, and Wei [9] for k = 6, while it remains open for k ≥ 7. By convention, in
                                           the quoted results and throughout this paper, 0 is not counted as a distance determined by a set of points.
                                             These questions can also be adapted to higher dimensions, and to alternative notions of distance. Here
                                           we focus on a particular, well-known alternative metric.
                                           Definition 1.1. For d ∈ N and x = (x1 , . . . , xd ) ∈ Rd , we define the `1 -norm of x by
                                                                                          kxk1 = |x1 | + · · · + |xd |,
                                           which in particular satisfies the triangle inequality kx + yk1 ≤ kxk1 + kyk1 . Like every norm, the `1 -norm
                                           induces a metric on Rd by defining kx − yk1 to be the `1 -distance between x, y ∈ Rd .

                                               The metric induced by the `1 -norm is commonly referred to as the taxicab metric, because it measures
                                           the length of the shortest path between two points in space, under the restriction that one can only travel
                                           in directions parallel to the coordinate axes, as if in a taxicab on a grid of city streets. For example, if two
                                           people at city intersections are separated by 3 blocks horizontally and 4 blocks vertically, then, as the crow
                                           flies, they are 5 blocks apart by the Pythagorean theorem. However, to actually make the journey without
                                           cutting through buildings, they must walk 7 blocks, which is the `1 -distance.
                                              As noted in Chapters 0 and 1 of [4], one can show that the minimum number of `1 -distances determined
                                           by n points in Rd is Ω(n1/d ), and this order of growth is attained by {1, 2, 3, . . . , dn1/d e}d . Therefore, in
                                           the case of the taxicab metric, the big picture asymptotic question is immediately resolved, which begs the
                                           question of whether this case can be analyzed more precisely. To begin this journey, we first consider the
                                           Erdős-Fishburn problem in the plane with k = 1.

   We fix two points U, V ∈ R2 , say U = (−1, 0) and V = (1, 0). With the usual notion of distance, if any
additional points can be added without determining an additional distance, those points necessarily lie on
the circles of radius 2 centered at √U and V , respectively.
                                                        √     Those two circles intersect in only two points,
and we find that they are Q = (0, 3) and R = (0, − 3). Since the distance between Q and R is greater
than 2, only one of the two can be added to {U, V } while maintaining only a single distance. In summary,
a set P ⊆ R2 determining a single distance satisfies |P | ≤ 3, and equality holds if and only if P is the set of
vertices of an equilateral triangle.
  However, even in this simplest case, the taxicab metric case diverges from that of the usual distance.
With the taxicab metric, the “circle” (which we refer to as an `1 -circle) of radius 2 centered at U is in fact a
square, rotated 45◦ from axis-parallel, with the four sides connecting the points (−3, 0), (−1, 2), (1, 0), and
(−1, −2). Similarly, the `1 -circle of radius 2 centered at V is a square with sides connecting (3, 0), (1, −2),
(−1, 0), and (1, 2). Like the usual distance case, these two circles intersect in exactly two points, this time
Q = (0, 1) and R = (0, −1). The difference is that here Q and R are indeed separated by `1 -distance 2, and
hence the four-point configuration {U, V, Q, R} determines a single `1 -distance.

Detailed analysis of Λd (k) is perhaps of independent interest, but to make headway toward our goal, we
need to address the important questions: does Λd (k) have maximal size amongst subsets of Rd determining
at most k distinct `1 -distances? If so, is Λd (k) the only such optimal arrangement? In anticipation of the
latter question, we observe that any optimal arrangement can undergo any scaling, or any transformation
that preserves the `1 -norm, and remain optimal, leading to the following definition.
Definition 2.4. For d ∈ N, we say that two subsets of Rd are `1 -similar if one can be mapped to the
other via a composition of translations, reflections about coordinate hyperplanes, dilations, and coordinate
permutations, as these transformations either preserve or uniformly scale collections of `1 -distances.

We note that the list of transformations in Definition 2.4 does not include rotations, because, unlike the usual
Euclidean metric, the taxicab metric is not invariant under rotation, unless the rotation can alternatively be
obtained through reflection about coordinate hyperplanes and permutation of coordinates. This fact rears
its head in our exploration of the taxicab metric in higher dimensions, and plays a key role in our discussions
in Section 6. For now, though, the following result established in Section 4 completely resolves the taxicab
analog of the Erdős-Fishburn problem in the plane.
Theorem 2.5. If k ∈ N and P ⊆ R2 determines at most k distinct `1 -distances, then |P | ≤ (k + 1)2 .
Further, |P | = (k + 1)2 if and only if P is `1 -similar to Λ2 (k).

As we discuss in Section 4, the d = 2 case is simplified by the fact that, for the purposes of analyzing
distance sets, the `1 -norm in R2 is effectively the same as the `∞ -norm defined by k(x, y)k∞ = max{|x|, |y|}.

However, this equivalence does not persist in dimension d ≥ 3, and for this reason, our proof strategy does not
immediately generalize to higher dimensions. (Although, for the interested reader, the proof does generalize
to show that if P ⊆ Rd determines at most k distinct `∞ -distances, then |P | ≤ (k + 1)d , and equality holds
if and only if P is `1 -similar to {0, 1, 2, . . . , k}d .)
With considerable additional effort, we successfully get our foot into the higher-dimensional door in Section
5, which assures us that the unique optimality of Λd (k) is not completely dependent on a connection to the

Theorem 2.6. If P ⊆ R3 determines a single `1 -distance, then |P | ≤ 6. Further, |P | = 6 if and only if P
is `1 -similar to Λ3 (1).

Remark on previous work for k = 1. After the initial posting of this paper to the arxiv server, we
were alerted to previous work done in the k = 1 case (referred to as equilateral sets) in a variety of metric
spaces, including Rd with the taxicab metric (referred to as rectilinear space). Specifically, Theorem 2.6
above follows from Corollary 4.2 of [1], due to Bandelt, Chepoi, and Laurent, while Koolen, Laurent, and
Schrijver [7] showed that if P ⊆ R4 determines a single `1 -distance, then |P | ≤ 8 = |Λ4 (1)|. This partially
settles a question of Kusner (see Problem 0 in [6]), who asked if |P | ≤ 2d = |Λ1 (d)| holds for subsets of Rd
determining a single `1 -distance, and this remains open for d ≥ 5. Conjecture 2.7 below can be thought of
as a precise, multi-distance generalization of Kusner’s question. While the conclusion of Theorem 2.6 was
known previously, we believe our alternative, elementary proof given in Section 5 remains of interest.
In Section 6, we explore the question of what additional hypotheses are required to prove the optimality of
Λ3 (k) for all k ∈ N, or even Λd (k) in full generality. We find that the proof of Theorem 2.5 can be fully
adapted with a seemingly mild additional assumption, leading us to make the following general conjecture.
Conjecture 2.7. If d, k ∈ N and P ⊆ Rd determines at most k distinct `1 -distances, then |P | ≤ |Λd (k)|.
Further, |P | = |Λd (k)| if and only if P is `1 -similar to Λd (k).

                       4. Optimality in Two Dimensions: Proof of Theorem 2.5

   In this section, we prove the unique optimality of Λ2 (k), in that it is the unique subset of R2 , up to
` -similarity, of maximal size amongst sets determining at most k distinct `1 -distances. As referenced in
 1

Section 2, the proof is in part enabled by an equivalence between the `1 -norm and the `∞ -norm on R2 . For
the sake of exposition, we frame our discussion entirely in the context of the `1 -norm, but the connection is
implicit in our proof, particularly the following lemma.
Lemma 4.1. Let v1 = (1, 1) and v2 = (−1, 1). If x ∈ R2 with x = c1 v1 + c2 v2 , then
                                              kxk1 = 2 max{|c1 |, |c2 |}.

Proof. Let v1 = (1, 1), v2 = (−1, 1), fix x ∈ R2 , and write x uniquely as x = c1 v1 + c2 v2 = (c1 − c2 , c1 + c2 ).
By potentially reflecting over the diagonal x1 = x2 and/or replacing x by −x, both of which preserve the
`1 -norm, we can assume without loss of generality that |c1 | ≥ |c2 | and c1 ≥ 0. In this case,
                    kxk1 = |c1 − c2 | + |c1 + c2 | = c1 − c2 + c1 + c2 = 2c1 = 2 max{|c1 |, |c2 |}.
                                                                                                                  

Our main strategy for proving Theorem 2.5 is inspired by Erdős and Fishburn [3]. Specifically, we suppose
that P ⊆ R2 determines at most k distinct `1 -distances, and we seek an upper bound on the number of
points we must remove from P in order to eliminate the largest `1 -distance, hence reducing to the case of
k − 1 distinct `1 -distances and allowing us to invoke an inductive hypothesis. The following sequence of
lemmas formalizes this strategy. Here we define an `1 -ball in the expected way, as the region bounded by an
`1 -sphere, which for d = 2 is an `1 -circle.


Lemma 4.2. Suppose P ⊆ R2 is finite. If D is the largest `1 -distance determined by P , then P is contained
in a closed `1 -ball of diameter D.

Proof. Suppose P ⊆ R2 is finite. Let v1 = (1, 1) and v2 = (−1, 1). Since {v1 , v2 } forms a basis for R2 , every
x ∈ P can be written uniquely as x = c1 v1 + c2 v2 . Choose x1 , x2 , x3 , x4 ∈ P such that x1 maximizes c1 ,
x2 minimizes c1 , x3 maximizes c2 , and x4 minimizes c2 . Call these values c1,max , c1,min , c2,max , and c2,min ,
respectively. These choices contain P inside of a rectangle R, rotated 45◦ from axis parallel, determined by
the inequalities c1,min ≤ c1 ≤ c1,max and c2,min ≤ c2 ≤ c2,max .
                                                          6
Let w1 = c1,max − c1,min and w2 = c2,max − c2,min , and assume without loss of generality that w1 ≥ w2 . By
Lemma 4.1, we have that kx1 − x2 k1 = 2w1 and kp1 − p2 k1 ≤ 2w1 for all p1 , p2 ∈ R, so D = 2w1 is the
largest `1 -distance determined by P . Let c2,new = c2,max − w1 ≤ c2,min , and let B ⊇ R ⊇ P be defined by
the inequalities c1,min ≤ c1 ≤ c1,max and c2,new ≤ c2 ≤ c2,max . B is a square rotated 45◦ from axis parallel,
or in other words a closed `1 -ball, of diameter D, as required.                                           



Lemma 4.3. If P ⊆ R2 is contained in a closed `1 -ball B of diameter D, then the `1 -distance D can be
eliminated from P by removing the points of P contained in any two adjacent sides of the boundary of B.

Proof. Suppose P ⊆ R2 is contained in a closed `1 -ball B of diameter D.
Let a1 , a2 be the left and right vertices of B, respectively, so in particular ka1 − a2 k1 = D. Let U denote the
closed (including a1 , a2 ) upper `1 -semicircle connecting a1 and a2 , and let L denote the open (not including
a1 , a2 ) lower `1 -semicircle connecting a1 and a2 . Since the `1 -norm is invariant under 90◦ rotation, it suffices
to establish the conclusion of the lemma for removing the points of P lying in U . Suppose x1 , x2 ∈ P \ U .
Case 1: At least one of x1 , x2 lies in B \ (U ∪ L), which is an open `1 -ball of radius D/2.
Assume without loss of generality that x1 ∈ B \ (U ∪ L), and let c be the center of B. Therefore, kx1 − ck1 <
D/2 and kx2 − ck1 ≤ D/2. By the triangle inequality, kx1 − x2 k1 ≤ kx1 − ck1 + kc − x2 k1 < D/2 + D/2 = D.
Case 2: x1 , x2 ∈ L. After possibly reflecting, assume without loss of generality that x1 is to the left of x2
and kx1 − a1 k1 ≤ kx2 − a2 k1 , so x1 is positioned at least as high as x2 , By replacing x1 with a1 , we move
up and to the left, so both the horizontal and vertical components of the `1 -distance to x2 get larger, hence
                                          kx1 − x2 k1 < ka1 − x2 k1 = D.

In both cases, all distances amongst points in P \ U are strictly less than D, and the lemma follows.              



Lemma 4.4. If P ⊆ Rd is contained in a line and determines at most k distinct `1 -distances, then |P | ≤ k+1.
Further, if |P | = k + 1, then P is an arithmetic progression, meaning the `1 -distances are λ, 2λ, . . . , kλ for
some λ > 0.

Proof. Since `1 -distance along a straight line in Rd is just a constant multiple, depending on the direction
of the line, times the standard Euclidean distance, it suffices to establish the lemma with d = 1, for which
we induct on k.
The base case k = 1 is trivial, as three points x1 < x2 < x3 in R automatically determine two distances
x2 − x1 < x3 − x1 , and any two points form an arithmetic progression.
Now, fix k ≥ 2, and assume that if Q ⊆ R determines at most k − 1 distances, then |Q| ≤ k, and further, if
|Q| = k, then Q is an arithmetic progression. Now suppose P ⊆ R determines at most k distances.
Let P = {x1 < x2 < · · · < xn }. The n − 1 distances x2 − x1 < x3 − x1 < · · · < xn − x1 are all distinct, hence
n − 1 ≤ k, or in other words n ≤ k + 1. Further, suppose n = k + 1. By removing xk+1 , we also remove
the longest distance xk+1 − x1 , so the set Q = {x1 , . . . , xk } determines k − 1 distances. By our inductive
hypothesis, Q must be an arithmetic progression, in other words Q = {x1 , x1 + λ, x1 + 2λ, . . . x1 + (k − 1)λ}.
If xk+1 < x1 + kλ, then both xk+1 − x1 > (k − 1)λ and xk+1 − xk < λ are new distances not determined by
Q. If xk+1 > x1 + kλ, then both xk+1 − x1 > kλ and xk+1 − x2 > (k − 1)λ are new distances not determined
by Q. In either case, P determines at least k + 1 distinct distances, contradicting the assumption that it
determines at most k distances. Therefore, xk+1 must be x1 + kλ, and the lemma follows.                 

Proof. Suppose S ⊆ R2 is contained in two adjacent sides of an `1 -circle and determines at most k distinct

Further, if |S| ≥ 2k + 1, then there are exactly k + 1 points on one side, assume the left, and at least k points

least the `1 -distance from the leftmost point to the shared vertex. In particular, if the shared vertex is not
included amongst the k + 1 points on the left side, then at least k + 1 distinct `1 -distances occur from the
leftmost point, contradicting our assumption.
Therefore, if |S| ≥ 2k + 1, it must be the case that there are exactly k + 1 points on both the left and right
sides, including the shared vertex, meaning in fact |S| = 2k + 1. Finally, by Lemma 4.4, we know that the
k + 1 points on each side must form an arithmetic progression.                                              

Proof of Theorem 2.5. We induct on k. For our base case, consider k = 0. In order for a set to determine

                               6. Conditional Results in Higher Dimensions

   In the remainder of our discussion, we use the terms `1 -sphere and `1 -ball as before, defined analogously to
regular spheres and balls in Rd , with the usual distance replaced by `1 -distance. As noted at the beginning of
Section 5, our proof of Theorem 2.5 does not naturally generalize to higher dimensions, because in dimension
d ≥ 3, it is not necessarily the case that if the largest `1 -distance determined by a finite set P ⊆ Rd is λ,
then P is contained in a closed `1 -ball of diameter λ.
However, consider the case where the largest `1 -distance determined by P is λ and (0, 0, . . . , −λ/2),
(0, 0, . . . , λ/2) ∈ P. In this case, P is contained in the intersection of the closed `1 -balls of radius λ centered
at (0, 0, . . . , −λ/2) and (0, 0, . . . , λ/2) ∈ P , respectively, which happens to be the closed `1 -ball of radius λ/2,
hence diameter λ, centered at the origin. Using our intuition from the usual Euclidean metric, it is tempting
to say that we can reduce to this case by translating and rotating our original set P , but we must contend
with the fact that the taxicab metric is not invariant under rotation. The following definition codifies when
we can reduce to this model case.

Definition 6.1. Given P ⊆ Rd and an `1 -distance λ > 0, we say that λ occurs in an axis parallel direction
if there exist x ∈ P and 1 ≤ i ≤ d such that x + λei ∈ P , where ei is the i-th standard basis vector.

The following definition introduces notation intended to streamline the induction arguments that follow.

Definition 6.2. For a fixed d, k ∈ N, we let Opt(d, k) denote the proposition that Λd (k) is the unique (up
to `1 -similarity) maximal subset of Rd determining at most k distinct `1 -distances. For example, we know
that Opt(3, 1) and Opt(2, k) for all k ∈ N hold by Theorems 2.6 and 2.5, respectively, while Conjecture 2.7
says precisely that Opt(d, k) holds for all d, k ∈ N.
