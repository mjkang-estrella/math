                                          1     Introduction
                                          Throughout the paper, we denote [n] := {1, 2, . . . , n}, and 2[n] := {X : X ⊂
                                          [n]}. A family F ⊂ 2[n] is called intersecting if any two of its elements
                                          intersect. The degree δ(x) of x ∈ [n] is defined to be the number of members
                                          of F that contain x, and F is called regular if all of its elements have the
                                          same degree. If F is regular, we also denote σ(F ) the degree of elements of F .
                                          Given an intersecting family F , the diversity of F is defined to be div(F ) :=
                                          |F | − maxx∈[n] |F (x)|, where F (x) = {F : x ∈ F ∈ F }. Let n = 2k + 1 and
                                          Qk = {A : A ⊂ [2k + 1], |A| ≥ k + 1}. About the maximal diversity among
                                          families included in 2[n] , Huang[1] gave the following conjecture.

                                          Conjecture 1.1. For n = 2k + 1, suppose F ⊂ 2[n] is intersecting. Then

The circular shift operation σ on [n] is defined as

              σ(i) ≡ i + 1 mod n, for all entries i = 1, 2, . . . , n,

and we denote σ(A) = {σ(i) : i ∈ A} for A ⊂ [n].
    Let L3 = {1, 2, 4} and Lk = {1, 2, 4} ∪ {7, 9, . . . , 2k − 1} for k ≥ 4. For
k ≥ 3, we define Fk to be the family obtained by repeatedly applying σ to
Lk , i.e.
                      Fk = {σ i (Lk ) : i = 0, 1, . . . , n − 1}
For example, F3 consists of the 7 lines of the Fano plane.

Lemma 2.1. For k ≥ 3 and n = 2k + 1, Fk ⊂ 2[n] is a regular intersecting
family with degree equal to k.

Proof. F3 consists of the 7 lines of the Fano plane, and thus is a regular
intersecting family with degree 3. For k > 3, it is clear that Fk is regular
with degree |Fk | ∗ k/n = k. To prove that Fk is intersecting, we just need to
show that Lk and σ i (Lk )(1 ≤ i ≤ n − 1) intersect, since σ n−i (σ i (Lk )) = Lk
and σ n−i (σ j (Lk )) = σ j−i (Lk ) for 1 ≤ i < j ≤ n − 1. Assume that Lk and
σ i (Lk )(1 ≤ i ≤ n − 1) do not intersect for some i ∈ [n − 1], then we must
have σ i (1) = 5 or σ i (1) = 2k. In the first case, we obtain σ i (2k − 1) = 2, and
σ i (4) = 2 in the second one. Both of the two cases contradict the assumption,
thus Fk is intersecting.
    Given a set A ⊂ [n], We denote Ā the complement of A in [n], and denote
F̄ := {Ā : A ∈ F } for F ⊂ 2[n] . The family Pk is defined as

                              Pk = Fk ∪ (Qk \ F̄k )

theorem 2.2. For n = 2k + 1 with k ≥ 3, Pk is a regular intersecting family
and div(Pk ) = div(Qk ) + 1.

Proof. By Lemma 2.1, the family Fk is intersecting. If Pk is not intersecting,
then there exist disjoint sets F and Q with F ∈ Fk and Q ∈ Qk \ F̄k . Since
F ∩ Q = ∅ and |Q| ≥ |F̄ | = k + 1, we must have Q = F̄ , which contradicts
the choice of F and Q. Therefore Pk is intersecting.
