583: 1.2     Introducing Combinatorial Game Theory
584: 
585: 
586: In this section, we will give an overview of the main ideas of combinatorial game theory.
587: This is merely to give the reader a quick introduction. Full details are available from the
588: following sources: [1, 5, 7].
589: 
590:       Most readers are familiar with the idea of a game; some familiar ones are tic-tac-toe,
591: poker, chess, and monopoly. This thesis concerns itself with combinatorial games, which
592: are games satisfying the following conditions:
593: 
594: 
595:    1. There are two players, generally denoted as Left and Right. These two players have
596:         genders assigned to them. In this thesis, Left will be female, while Right is male. This
597:         agrees with the Louise/Left and Richard/Right convention of [5]. It was also chosen
598:         since the author of this thesis is both female and left-handed.
599: 
600:    2. There is a clearly defined rule set which states which moves are legal moves and which
601:         moves are not.
602: 
603:    3. There is complete information. That is, all information is available to both Left and
604:         Right at all points during the game.
605: 
606:    4. There are no elements of chance which affect the outcome of the game. That is, for
607:         example, there are no dice, spinners, or revealing of cards.

665: 
666:    In game theory, we often also refer to a position in a certain game as a game. For example,
667: we would say the game of nim when talking about nim and its rule set in general. However,
668: we may also say that two heaps of size three and a heap of size six is a game where we are
669: playing nim on those heaps. This can often be confusing to a non-game theorist. This thesis
670: endeavours to avoid this confusion by using game to denote a rule-set (in our example, nim)
671: and a position to be a position in a game (in our example, the two heaps of size three and
672: a heap of size six where we are to play nim on those heaps). We have tried to ensure that
673: this convention is followed throughout the thesis.
674: 
675:    With the exception of the named positions 0, 1, 1, and ∗, this thesis denotes positions
676: by lower-case Greek letters. This is to ensure that there is no confusion between positions
677: in a game and elements of a misère monoid, for which we generally use Roman letters.
678: 
679:    We now continue our game theory definitions.
680: 
681: Definition 1.2.3. Suppose we are given a position ξ. A left option of ξ is a new position
682: which arises after one move from Left. Similarly, a right option of ξ is a new position
683: which arises after one move from Right. The set of options of a position is the union
684: of the left options and the right options. We let ξ L denote the set of left options of ξ and ξ R
685: denote the set of right options of ξ.
686: 
687: 
688:    In an abuse of notation, for a position ξ, we often use ξ L or ξ R to denote an element of
689: the set of left or right options respectively. However, it is always clear from context whether
690: we mean an element of the set or the set itself.
691: 
692:    Given a set of left options, ξ L , we can again, for example, determine the left options
693: of ξ L , which we denote by ξ LL . For those starting their investigations into combinatorial
694: game theory, this feels decidedly strange, as earlier we stated that the players Left and Right
695: alternate moves and ξ LL records the results of Left moving twice in the position ξ. However,
696: it is important to keep track of such things. Suppose, for example, that we have the position
697: ξ + γ, for some other position γ. Moreover, suppose Left moves first to ξ L + γ, to which
698: Right responds with ξ L + γ R . Then Left can move to ξ LL + γ R , and it becomes clear why we
699: would need to record those occurrences where one player moves multiple times in the same
700: component.

771:    In Section 1.1, we introduced impartial and partizan. We now have the tools to define
772: these terms formally.
773: 
774: Definition 1.2.9. A position ξ is impartial if the left options and right options of ξ are
775: equal as sets. Otherwise the position is partizan
776: 
777:    A game is impartial if every position in the game is impartial. Otherwise the game is
778: partizan.
779: 
780: 
781:    When we are considering whether a game is impartial or partizan, we must consider all
782: positions, including such positions as ξ LLL which may never occur during allowed play.
783: 
784:    We again return to our three example positions.
785: 
786: Example 1.2.10. In 0 and ∗, Left and Right have the same options. Thus 0 and ∗ are
787: impartial positions. This is not true for 1, and so 1 is a partizan position.

792: Example 1.2.11. The game nim (Example 1.2.2) is an impartial game. If we modified the
793: rules of nim so that Left’s moves are to take an even number of tokens, while Right’s moves
794: are to take an odd number of tokens, then this modified game is partizan.

796: 
797:    We also classify games based on whether Left and Right can both move from any position,
798: even if they do not necessarily have the same moves from every position.
799: 
800: Definition 1.2.12. A position ξ is all-small if Left can move if and only if Right can, and
801: every option of ξ is all-small.
802: 
803:    A game is all-small if every position in the game is all-small.
804: 
805: Example 1.2.13. All impartial games are all-small. The position ∗ is all-small.

808:    We often wish to play sums of positions. The following definitions clarifies what we mean
809: by a sum of positions.
810: 
811: Definition 1.2.14. Given positions ξ1 , ξ2 , . . ., ξm , the disjunctive sum is the position
812: ξ1 + ξ2 + · · · + ξm where on a given players turn, that player picks a position ξi and plays in
813: it according to the rules of ξi . In misère play, when a player has no moves in every position
814: in the disjunctive sum, that player wins. In normal play, when a player has no moves in

815: every position in the disjunctive sum, that player loses.
816: 
817: 
818:    Notice that the ξi need not all be from the same game. For example, ξ1 could be a nim
819: position, ξ2 could be a domineering position (for the rules of domineering, see [1]), ξ3
820: could be an amazons position (for the rules of amazons, see [1]), etc.
821: 
822:    To represent the disjunctive sum of two positions ξ and κ in terms of its options, we write
823: the following:
824:                           ξ + κ = {ξ L + κ, ξ + κL | ξ R + κ, ξ + κR },
825: 
826: where the commas in the expressions denote union.
827: 
828:    Disjunctive sum is not the only way we can play in a set of positions. In Chapter 6, we
829: look at some other types of sums on positions.

834:    In our definition of combinatorial game, we stated that in each game there is always a
835: winner, and hence, at least one of the players has a winning strategy. The following definition
836: separates positions based on who has the winning strategy.
837: 
838: Definition 1.2.15. Let ξ be a position of a combinatorial game. Then ξ belongs to an
839: outcome class which specifies which player(s) has a winning strategy in ξ. They are
840: 
841: 
842:   1. L if Left has a winning strategy in ξ regardless of moving first or second.
843: 
844:   2. R if Right has a winning strategy in ξ regardless of moving first or second.
845: 
846:   3. N if the next player to move has a winning strategy in ξ.
847: 
848:   4. P if the next player moving does not have a winning strategy (i.e. the next player to
849:         move will lose) in ξ. The P stands for previous, as if the next player loses, the player
850:         who would have played previous will win.
851: 
852: 
853:    The Fundamental Theorem of Combinatorial Games states that every position belongs
854: to precisely one of the four outcome classes listed above [1].
855: 
856:    We need to be careful when we are considering the outcome class of a position. The
857: definition is framed as to who has the winning strategy, but the concept of winning differs
858: between normal play and misère play. As such, we need some further definition.
859: 
860: Definition 1.2.16. For a position ξ, we let o+ (ξ) denote the normal play outcome of ξ while
861: o− (ξ) denotes the misère play outcome.
862: 
863: Notation 1.2.17. In considering outcomes, ∪ denotes or.
864: 
865: Example 1.2.18. If o− (ξ) = N ∪ P, this means that the misère outcome of ξ is either N
866: or P.
867: 
868: 
869:    Knowing the outcomes of the options of a position allows us to determine what the
870: outcome of the position itself is, irrespective of whether we are playing under the normal
871: play or misère play convention. Table 1.2.1 [1] shows how the outcome class of a position
872: can be determined from the outcome classes of its options.
873: 
874:                                                                                           10
875: 
876:                                          some ξ R ∈ R ∪ P   all ξ R ∈ L ∪ N
877:                    some ξ L ∈ L ∪ P              N                  L
878:                      all ξ L ∈ R ∪ N             R                  P
879: 
880: 
881: Table 1.2.1: Determining the outcome class of a position ξ given information about the
882: outcome classes of ξ’s options.
883: 
884:    We can also sometimes glean information about the outcomes of the options of a position
885: if we know the outcome of the position itself. The most important instance of this is if ξ is
886: a P position, then no option of ξ is also a P position, otherwise one of the players would
887: have moved to this position and won, meaning our initial position could not have been a P
888: position.
