23:                                                                                1. Introduction
24: 
25:                                              In 1933, Borsuk [5] made the following conjecture.
26:                                           Conjecture (Borsuk). Every set of diameter d > 0 in the Euclidean n-space Rn
27:                                           is the union of n + 1 sets of diameters less than d.
28: 
29:                                              From the 1930s, this conjecture has attracted a wide interest among geometers.
30:                                           The frequent attempts to prove it led to results in a number of special cases: for
31:                                           sets in R2 and R3 , for smooth bodies or sets with certain symmetries, etc., but the
32:                                           conjecture in general remained open till 1993, when it was disproved by Kahn and
33:                                           Kalai [17]. Their result did not mean that research on this problem stopped: the
34:                                           investigation of the so-called Borsuk number of a bounded set; that is, the minimum
35:                                           number of pieces of smaller diameters that it can be partitioned into, is still one of
36:                                           the fundamental problems of discrete geometry.
37:                                              Since 1933, a large number of generalizations of Borsuk’s problem has been
38:                                           introduced. Without completeness, we list only a few. The generalized Borsuk
39:                                           problem asks to find, for a fixed value of 0 < r < 1, the minimum number m such
40:                                           that any set of diameter one in Rn can be partitioned into m pieces of diameters at
41:                                           most r (cf., for example, [10]). The cylindrical Borsuk problem makes restrictions
42:                                           on the method of partition (cf. [15]). Clearly, the original problem is meaningful
43:                                           for sets in any metric space, for instance, for finite dimensional normed spaces (cf.
44:                                           [3]) or for binary codes equipped with Hamming distance. The latter one is called
45:                                           the (0, 1)-Borsuk problem, and is investigated, for example, in [21], [33] and [22].
46:                                           For more information on this problem and its generalizations, the reader is referred
47:                                           to the survey [24].

18:                                              A choice function for a family of sets F = (F1 , . . . , Fm ) is a choice of elements f1 ∈ F1 , . . . , fm ∈ Fm .
19:                                           It is also called a system of representatives (SR) for F . Many combinatorial questions can be formulated
20:                                           in terms of SRs that satisfy yet another condition. For example, in Hall’s theorem [13] the extra condition
21:                                           is injectivity. In Rado’s theorem [17] the condition is injectivity, plus the demand that the range of the
22:                                           choice function belongs to Sa given matroid. In the most general setting, a simplicial complex (closed down
23:                                           hypergraph) C is given on Fi , and the condition is that the range of the function belongs to C. The SR is
24:                                           then called a C-SR. (Even injectivity can be formulated in the terminology of C-SRs, using a trick of making
25:                                           many copies of each element.)
26:                                              Given a graph G, we denote by I(G) the set of independent sets in G. An I(G)-SR is also called an ISR
27:                                           (independent system of representatives).
28:                                              We shall focus our attention on one special case, in which the sets Fi are hypergraphs, and the extra
29:                                           condition is that the edges chosen are disjoint. In this case, the system of representatives is called a rainbow
30:                                                                                                                                                          S

33:                                              The conjecture that motivates our inquiry is the following conjecture of Aharoni and Berger (not published

35:                                           Conjecture 1.1. k matchings of size k + 1 in a bipartite graph possess a (full) rainbow matching.
36:                                              This conjecture strengthens a conjecture of Brualdi and Stein, on Latin squares. A Latin square of order
37:                                           n is an n × n matrix whose entries are the symbols 1, . . . , n, each appearing once in every row and once in
38:                                           every column. A (partial) transversal in a Latin square is a set of entries, each in a distinct row and a distinct
39:                                           column, containing distinct symbols. A transversal of size n is said to be full. A well known conjecture of
40:                                           Ryser [18] is that for n odd every n × n Latin square contains a full transversal. For even n this is false, and
41:                                           Brualdi and Stein [10, 20] raised independently the following natural conjecture:
42:                                           Conjecture 1.2. In a Latin square of order n there exists a partial transversal of size n − 1.
43:                                              A Latin square can be viewed as a 3-partite hypergraph, with sides (R=set of rows, C=set of columns,
44:                                           S=set of symbols), in which every entry e corresponds to the edge (row(e), column(e), symbol(e)). The
45:                                           3-partite hypergraph corresponding to a Latin square satisfies stringent conditions - every pair of vertices
46:                                           in two different sides belongs to precisely one 3-edge. In particular, this means that for every vertex v, the
47:                                           set of 2-edges complementing v to a 3-edge is a matching in a bipartite graph. Hence the Brualdi-Stein
48:                                           conjecture would follow from:

51:                                           size k to matchings of size k + 1, adding the same edge to all.

50: 1      Introduction
51:     Circular planar electrical networks are a natural generalization of an idea from classical physics:
52: that any electrical resistor network with two vertices connected to a battery behaves like one with
53: a single resistor. When we embed a resistor network in a disk and allow arbitrarily many boundary
54: vertices connected to batteries, the situation becomes more interesting. An inverse boundary
55: problem for these electrical networks was studied in detail by de Verdière-Gitler-Vertigan [dVGV]
56: and Curtis-Ingerman-Morrow [CIM]: given the response matrix of a network, that is, information
57: about how the network responds to voltages applied at the boundary vertices, can the network be
58: recovered?
59:     In general, the answer is “no,” though much can be said about the information that can be
60: recovered. If, for example, the underlying graph of the electrical network is known and is critical, the
61: conductances (equivalently, resistances) can be uniquely recovered [CIM, Theorem 2]. Moreover,
62: any two networks which produce the same response matrix can be related by a certain class of
63: combinatorial transformations, the local equivalences [dVGV, Théorème 4].
64:     The goal of this paper is to study more closely the rich theory of circular planar electrical
65: networks. Specifically, we define a poset EPn of circular planar graphs, under the operations of
66: contraction and deletion of edges, and investigate its properties.
67:     For instance, the poset EPn has an intricate topological structure. By [CIM, Theorem 4] and

13:                                                     Given a group Γ acting on a set X, a k-coloring φ : X → {1, . . . , k} of X is distinguishing
14:                                                 with respect to Γ if the only γ ∈ Γ that fixes φ is the identity action. The distinguishing
15:                                                 number of the action Γ, denoted DΓ (X), is then the smallest positive integer k such that
16:                                                 there is a distinguishing k-coloring of X with respect to Γ. This notion has been studied in a
17:                                                 number of settings, but by far the largest body of work has been concerned with finding the
18:                                                 distinguishing number of the action of the automorphism group of a graph G upon its vertex
19:                                                 set, which is referred to as the distinguishing number of G.
20:                                                     The distinguishing number of a group action is a measure of how difficult it is to “break” all
21:                                                 of the permutations arising from that action. In this paper, we aim to further differentiate the
22:                                                 resilience of group actions with the same distinguishing number. In particular, we introduce
23:                                                 a precoloring extension framework to address this issue. A set S ⊆ X is a fixing set for Γ
24:                                                 if for every non-identity element γ ∈ Γ there is an element s ∈ S such that γ(s) 6= s. The
25:                                                 distinguishing extension number extD (X, Γ; k) is the minimum number m such that for all
26:                                                 fixing sets W ⊆ X with |W | ≥ m, every k-coloring c : X \ W → [k] can be extended to a
27:                                                 k-coloring that distinguishes X.
28:                                                     In this paper, we prove that extD (R, Aut(R), 2) = 4, where Aut(R) is comprised of compo-
29:                                                 sitions of translations and reflections. We also consider the distinguishing extension number
30:                                                 of the circle and (finite) cycles, obtaining several exact results and bounds.
60:     The notion of distinguishing a general group action was introduced by Tymoczko in [35], where
61: a number of results on actions of Sn appear, and was also addressed in [16, 17]. In [28], the
62: distinguishing number of GLn (K) over a field K acting on the vector space K n was completely
63: determined. We are most frequently concerned with the case where Γ is the action of a symmetry
64: group on some geometric or combinatorial object. In particular, the overwhelming body of work on
65: this problem is concerned with determining the distinguishing number of a graph G, first introduced
66: by Albertson and Collins [4] in 1996.
67:     Specifically, a vertex coloring of a graph G, c : V (G) → {1, . . . , k}, is said to be distinguishing if
68: the only automorphism of G that preserves all of the vertex colors is the identity. The distinguishing

74: all of the permutations arising from that action; the more colors required, the more resilient the
75: action. Almost all graphs have trivial automorphism group (see [12]), and hence have distinguishing
76: number 1. Many other familiar graph classes have distinguishing number 2 (see [2, 4, 11, 22, 25,

110: coloring the m uncolored elements using colors from {1, . . . , k}. The distinguishing extension
111: number extD (X, Γ; k) is then the minimum m such that the Hero has a guaranteed win.

70: 1.1. Historical background. Topological graph theory dates back to Heawood (1890)
71: [29] and Heffter (1891) [30], who transformed the four-color map problem for the plane,
72: into the Heawood problem, which specifies the maximum number of map colors needed
73: for the surfaces of higher genus and for non-orientable surfaces as well. The solution of
74: this problem by Ringel and Youngs (1968) (see [54]) led to development of the study
75: of embeddings of various families of graphs in higher genus surfaces and to simulta-
76: neous study of all the possible maps on a given surface. There have subsequently
77: evolved substantial enumerative branches of graph embeddings, according to genus,
78: and maps, according to numbers of edges, with frequent interplay between topological
79: graph theory and combinatorics, as in the present paper.
80:    For instance, a combinatorial formula of Jackson [33] (1987), based on group char-
81: acter theory, is critical to the calculation of the genus distributions of bouquets [26]
82: (bouquets are graphs with one vertex and any number of self-loops). Recent calcu-
83: lations of genus distributions of star-ladders [12] and of embedding distributions of
84: circular ladders [13] use Chebyshev polynomials and the overlap matrix of Mohar [46].
85: The log-concavity problem for genus distributions, our general target here, is presently
86: one of the oldest unsolved oft-cited problems in topological graph theory.
87:    There have always been two approaches to graph embeddings: fix the graph and
88: vary the surface, or fix the surface and vary the graph. The Heawood Map Theorem
89: can be viewed either way: find the largest complete graph embeddable in the surface
90: of genus g, or find the lowest genus surface in which a given complete graph can be
91: embedded. Genus distribution, along with minimum and maximum genus, are exam-
92: ples of fix-the-graph. Robertson and Seymour’s [55] Kuratowski theorem for general
93: surfaces, which solves a problem of Erdös and König [40], is an example of fix-the-
94: surface. Another example is the theory of maps, where the graph embedding itself is
95: fixed and its automorphisms are investigated. For example, until very recently, noth-
96: ing was known about the regular maps (maps having full rotational symmetry) in a
97: surface of given genus g > 5; a full classification of such maps when g = p + 1 for p
98: prime is given in [16]. Methods there are entirely algebraic, even involving parts of the
99: classification of finite simple groups.
