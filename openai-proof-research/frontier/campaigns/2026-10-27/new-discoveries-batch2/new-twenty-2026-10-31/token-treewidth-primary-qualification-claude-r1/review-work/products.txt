                                                        Treewidth of Products of Graphs
                                                             with High Treewidth

                                                                                        Raj Kaul

                                                                                         Abstract

                                                  Treewidth is the standard measure for how “tree-like” a graph is. This paper
                                                  studies how the treewidth of a product graph depends on the treewidth of
                                                  its factors. Kozawa, Otachi, and Yamazaki [2014] and Hickingbotham and
                                                  Wood [2025] independently showed that tw(G ⊠ H) ⩾ (tw(G) + 1) had(H) − 1




arXiv:2607.16778v1 [math.CO] 18 Jul 2026
                                                  for all graphs G and H, where had(H) is the Hadwiger number of H. We
                                                  improve this bound to tw(G ⊠ H) ⩾ (tw(G) + 1)(tw(H) + 1) − 1, thereby solving
                                                  an open problem of Hickingbotham and Wood. We also prove analogous
                                                  product inequalities for pathwidth, Cartesian products, and strict bramble
                                                  number, which is a parameter that is tied to treewidth. As an application of
                                                  our results, we show that products of expanders have large subgraphs that
                                                  are expanders.



                                           1     Introduction
                                           Treewidth1 is a foundational parameter in structural and algorithmic graph the-
                                           ory [4, 9, 17] that measures the structural complexity of a graph. Informally,
                                           graphs with small treewidth are more “tree-like”. Grids are the canonical graphs
                                           with large treewidth, since the n × n-grid has treewidth n and, by the Grid-
                                           Minor Theorem of Robertson and Seymour [18], every graph with large enough
                                           treewidth contains the n × n-grid as a minor.2 Inspired by the fact that grids are
                                           Cartesian products of paths, this paper studies the treewidth of various products
                                           of general graphs.

    Our first contribution, proven in Section 2, is a lower bound on the treewidth
of a strong product. The strong product of graphs G and H, denoted by G ⊠ H,
is the graph with vertex set V (G) × V (H), where (g1 , h1 )(g2 , h2 ) ∈ E(G ⊠ H) if and
only if g1 = g2 and h1 h2 ∈ E(H); or g1 g2 ∈ E(G) and h1 = h2 ; or g1 g2 ∈ E(G) and
h1 h2 ∈ E(H).
Theorem 1. For all graphs G and H, tw(G ⊠ H) ⩾ (tw(G) + 1)(tw(H) + 1) − 1.

    Theorem 1 improves upon the bound tw(G⊠H) ⩾ (tw(G)+1) had(H)−1, where
had(H) denotes the Hadwiger number of H,3 which was shown independently by
Kozawa, Otachi, and Yamazaki [12] and Hickingbotham and Wood [11]. Further-
more, Theorem 1 solves an open problem of Hickingbotham and Wood [11], who
asked whether there exists a constant c > 0 such that tw(G ⊠ H) ⩾ c tw(G) tw(H)
for all graphs G and H. Theorem 1 says the answer is ‘yes’ with c = 1.
   Our second contribution, proven in Section 3, is that Theorem 1 holds when
treewidth is replaced by pathwidth:
Theorem 2. For all graphs G and H, pw(G ⊠ H) ⩾ (pw(G) + 1)(pw(H) + 1) − 1.

   Theorems 1 and 2 are optimal since tw(Kn ) = pw(Kn ) = n−1 and tw(Km ⊠Kn ) =
tw(Kmn ) = mn − 1 = (tw(Km ) + 1)(tw(Kn ) + 1) − 1.
    In addition to the strong product, this paper also studies Cartesian products
and lexicographic products, which we now introduce. The Cartesian product of
graphs G and H, denoted by G □ H, is the graph with vertex set V (G) × V (H),
where (g1 , h1 )(g2 , h2 ) ∈ E(G □ H) if and only if g1 = g2 and h1 h2 ∈ E(H); or g1 g2 ∈
E(G) and h1 = h2 . The lexicographic product of graphs G with H, denoted by G ◦ H,
is the graph with vertex set V (G) × V (H), where (g1 , h1 )(g2 , h2 ) ∈ E(G ◦ H) if and
only if g1 g2 ∈ E(G); or g1 = g2 and h1 h2 ∈ E(H).
    Both □ and ⊠ are commutative (through isomorphism). On the other hand, ◦
is not commutative. As illustrated in Figure 1, the basic relationship between the
different products is G □ H ⊆ G ⊠ H ⊆ G ◦ H.

   The constant 21 in Theorem 3 is best possible since Harvey and Wood [10]

    A key tool in the proof of Theorem 1 is the following dual notion to treewidth
introduced by Seymour and Thomas [20] called the bramble number. Two con-
nected subsets A and B of V (G) touch if G[A ∪ B] is connected. A bramble in G
is a collection B of non-empty connected subsets of V (G) that pairwise touch. A
hitting set for B is a set S ⊆ V (G) that intersects every element of B. The order
of B, denoted by ord(B), is the minimum size of a hitting set for B. The bramble
number of G, denoted by bn(G), is the maximum order of a bramble in G.
Theorem 4 ([20]). For every graph G, tw(G) + 1 = bn(G).
   As an aside, Bellenbaum and Diestel [2] and Mazoit [15] each gave a short
proof of Theorem 4.
   Brambles are useful for lower bounding the treewidth. For example, recall
that the n × n-grid has treewidth n. The lower bound of n follows from The-
orem 4 by considering the bramble consisting of the top row, the last column
minus the top vertex, and the crosses in the bottom-left (n − 1) × (n − 1)-grid. A
well-known generalisation of this lower bound says that tw(G □ H) ⩾ n for all
connected graphs G and H with at least n ⩾ 2 vertices (see [7] for a proof). Wood
[21] improved this bound by taking into account the connectivity of G and H.4
Theorem 5 ([21]). For all k-connected graphs G and H with at least n ⩾ 2 vertices,
                                  tw(G □ H) ⩾ k(n − 2k + 2).

    Qualitatively, the bound in Theorem 5 is of a very different character from
the bounds in Theorems 1 and 3, which are solely in terms of treewidth. This is
because treewidth is a global structural parameter, unlike connectivity, and the
number of vertices in a graph can be much larger than its treewidth. Quantita-
tively, the bound in Theorem 5 is better when the factors have treewidth com-
parable to their connectivity. On the other hand, Theorems 1 and 3 are superior
when the factors have large treewidth and small connectivity, such as random d-
regular graphs. An important consequence of Theorems 1 and 3, which is made
precise in Section 4, is that products of expanders have large subgraphs that are
expanders.
   Since the treewidth of a graph is at least its connectivity [6, Exercise 12.26],
Theorem 5 inspires the question: Can the bound tw(G □ H) ∈ Ω(tw(G) tw(H)) in
Theorem 3 be improved to tw(G □ H) ∈ Ω(tw(G)|V (H)|)? The answer is ‘no’ since
Hickingbotham and Wood [11, Proposition 21] showed that for all n ⩾ k +1, there
exists an n-vertex treewidth k graph Gk,n such that tw(Gk,n ⊠ Gk,n ) ∈ O(n + k 2 ).
   On a related note, Hickingbotham and Wood [11] asked whether tw(G ⊠ H) ∈
O(tw(G □ H))? Our next theorem shows that the analogous relationship fails for
◦ and ⊠, that is tw(G ◦ H) < O(tw(G ⊠ H)). More strongly, in Section 5 we show
that tw(G ⊠ H) and tw(G ◦ H) are not tied.
   4 Theorem 5 is stated in [21] as tw(G □ H) ⩾ k(n − 2k + 2) − 1, however it is implicit in the proof

   Guided by the role of brambles in our proof of Theorem 1, we next consider
the strict bramble number. A bramble in a graph G is strict if its elements are
pairwise intersecting. The strict bramble number of G, denoted by sbn(G), is the
maximum order of a strict bramble in G.
   The next two theorems give strict bramble analogues of our earlier product
inequalities. The first is proven in Section 2:
Theorem 7. For all graphs G and H, tw(G □ H) ⩾ sbn(G)(tw(H) + 1) − 1.

    Theorem 7 strengthens the bound tw(G □ H) ⩾ sbn(G)(had(H) + 1) − 1 by
Kozawa, Otachi, and Yamazaki [12], who introduced the notion of strict bram-
bles under the name “PI family”.5 They also showed that strict bramble number
is tied to treewidth, in particular, sbn(G) ⩾ 21 (tw(G) + 1). Therefore Theorem 7
implies Theorem 3.
   A duality result (Theorem 19 below) for strict brambles analogous to Theo-
rem 4 was proved by Lardas, Protopapas, Thilikos, and Zoros [13]. In Section 6,
we use their duality result to obtain the following lower bound on the strict bram-
ble number of Cartesian products:
Theorem 8. For all graphs G and H, sbn(G □ H) ⩾ sbn(G) sbn(H).

    We now highlight parallels between Theorems 1 and 8. By Theorem 4, we
may rewrite Theorem 1 as bn(G ⊠H) ⩾ bn(G) bn(H). Therefore Theorems 1 and 8
motivate viewing (⊠, bn) and (□, sbn) as natural ‘product-parameter pairings’,
in the sense that the parameter respects the product via a multiplicative lower
bound. Theorem 8 pertains to the more restrictive product, which necessitates a
more restrictive parameter. Indeed, the proofs of Theorems 1 and 8 are nearly
identical, differing only in the modifications required to accommodate the more
restrictive setting. The pairing is also supported by the optimality of Theorem 3,
specifically, if bn is used in place of sbn in Theorem 8, then a factor of 12 must be
introduced.

3      Pathwidth of a Strong Product
This section proves Theorem 2, which states that for all graphs G and H

                         pw(G ⊠ H) ⩾ (pw(G) + 1)(pw(H) + 1) − 1.

   In their proof of the Tree-Minor Theorem,6 Bienstock, Robertson, Seymour,
and Thomas [3] introduced a dual parameter for pathwidth, called blockages.
The first step in their proof is a duality result (Theorem 12 below) that is analo-
gous to Theorem 4. We use their duality result in our proof of Theorem 2.
    Let G be a graph and let k be a non-negative integer. For each set X ⊆ V (G),
let ∂X be the set of vertices in X that have a neighbour in V (G) \ X. A k-cut in G
is a pair (A, B) of subsets of V (G) such that |A ∩ B| ⩽ k and G[A] ∪ G[B] = G. A cut
is a k-cut for some k. A blockage of order k in G is a set B ⊆ 2V (G) that satisfies the
following blockage axioms:
     • if X ∈ B, then |∂X| ⩽ k;

   • if Y ⊆ X ∈ B and |∂Y | ⩽ k, then Y ∈ B;
   • if (X, Y ) is a k-cut in G, then B contains exactly one of X or Y .
Theorem 12 ([3]). G has a blockage of order k if and only if pw(G) ⩾ k.

   Subsequent proofs of the Tree-Minor Theorem by Diestel [5] and Sey-
mour [19] avoid pathwidth-blockage duality by employing a shorter and more
direct approach.
   For convenience, our proof of Theorem 2 uses the notion of stoppages, which
were also introduced by Bienstock et al. [3, §4] and are essentially equivalent to
blockages. Let G be a graph and let k be a non-negative integer. A stoppage of
order k in G is a set Y of k-cuts in G that satisfies the following stoppage axioms:
   • if (A, B) is a k-cut in G, then (A, B) ∈ Y or (B, A) ∈ Y;
   • if (A, B) ∈ Y and (C, D) ∈ Y, then G[A] ∪ G[C] , G.
   Bienstock et al. [3] stated the relationship between blockages and stoppages
but left the details to the reader. We provide the details for completeness.
Lemma 13 ([3, 4.1 (reworded)]). G has a blockage of order k if and only if G has a
stoppage of order k.

Proof. ( =⇒ ) Suppose B is a blockage of order k in G. Let Y be the set of all k-
cuts (A, B) with A ∈ B. We show that Y is a stoppage of order k in G. The first
stoppage axiom follows from the third blockage axiom. For the second stoppage
axiom, suppose there exists (A, B) ∈ Y and (C, D) ∈ Y with G[A] ∪ G[C] = G. Let X
be the set of vertices in A ∩ B that have no neighbour in B \ A, and let B′ B B \ X.
Then (A, B′ ) is a k-cut. Since A ∈ B, the third blockage axiom implies B′ < B. Note
that B′ \ A ⊆ C because G[A] ∪ G[C] = G. Moreover, each vertex u ∈ A ∩ B′ has a
neighbour v ∈ B′ \ A. Then since uv < E(G[A]), uv ∈ E(G[C]), thus u ∈ C. Hence
B′ ⊆ C, so the second blockage axiom implies B′ ∈ B, contradiction.
    ( ⇐= ) Suppose Y is a stoppage of order k in G. Let B B {A : (A, B) ∈ Y}. We
show that B is a blockage of order k in G. Since every element of Y is a k-cut, the
first blockage axiom holds. For the second blockage axiom, suppose Y ⊆ X ∈ B
and |∂Y | ⩽ k. Then there exists W ⊆ V (G) such that (X, W ) ∈ Y. Furthermore, by
letting Z B (V (G) \ Y ) ∪ ∂Y , we have that (Y , Z) is a k-cut in G. Since Y ⊆ X, it
follows that (V (G) \ X) ∪ ∂X ⊆ (V (G) \ Y ) ∪ ∂Y , thus G[X] ∪ G[Z] = G. Hence by
the second stoppage axiom, (Z, Y ) < Y, thus (Y , Z) ∈ Y by the first stoppage axiom,
so Y ∈ B. Next we prove the third blockage axiom. Suppose that (X, Y ) is a k-cut
in G. Then the first stoppage axiom implies X ∈ B or Y ∈ B. If X ∈ B and Y ∈ B,
then (X, W ) ∈ Y and (Y , Z) ∈ Y for some W , Z ⊆ V (G). However since (X, Y ) is a
cut, G[X] ∪ G[Y ] = G, which contradicts the second stoppage axiom.

ℓ(t) are called bags. The width of (T , ℓ) is maxt∈V (T ) |ℓ(t)|. Note that a lenient tree-
decomposition may not be a tree-decomposition.
