                                                                     1. Introduction
             Let G be an abelian group of order n. The Davenport constant of G, denoted by D(G), is the
             maximal integer k such that there is a sequence of elements of G with length k − 1 having no
             non-empty zero-sum subsequence. The investigation of zero-sum subsequences with length n
             starts with a result of Erdős, Ginzburg and Ziv [2], stating that every sequence of elements of G
             with length ⩾ 2n − 1 has a zero-sum subsequence of length n. A result of Gao [4], stating that
             every sequence of elements of G with length ⩾ n + D(G) − 1 has a zero-sum subsequence of
             length n, unifies these two areas of zero-sum problems. For more details on these questions and
             some of their applications, the reader may refer to the book of Geroldinger and Halter-Koch [6]
             and to the survey paper of Caro [1].
                Attempts were made to generalize zero-sum problems to the weighted case. Examples of such
             results may be found in the survey paper of Caro [1], a paper by the author [9] and two more
             recent papers by Gao and Jin [5] and Grynkiewicz [7].
                Let x = (x1 , . . . , xm ) ∈ E m , where E is a set. As usual one may consider x as a map from [1, n]
             into E. We shall put ρ(x) = max{|x−1 (a)|; a ∈ E}. Notice that ρ(x) is the maximal repetition of
             the sequence x.

                  Theorem A (Scherk [11]). Let A and B be non-empty finite subsets of an abelian group G. If
                  |A ∩ (−B)| = {0}, then |A + B| ⩾ |A| + |B| − 1.

             Theorem B (folklore). Let G be a finite abelian group of order n. Let x = (x1 , . . . , xn ) ∈ Gn be
             a sequence of elements of G with a maximal repetition ⩽ k. Then x has a non-empty zero-sum
             subsequence with length ⩽ k.

                Theorem B is now a standard tool in zero-sum theory [6]. A proof of Theorem B requires an
             addition theorem. The oldest such result easily implying Theorem B is Shepherdson’s theorem
             [12]. An almost identical proof follows by Scherck’s theorem [11]. When G is cyclic, Theorem B
             is a special case of Conjecture 4 of Erdős and Heilbronn [3]. In a note added in proof, Erdős and
             Heilbronn [3] mentioned that Flor proved this conjecture. A generalization of Theorem B to (not
             necessarily) abelian groups is proved in [8] using a result of Kemperman [10].
                In order to make the present work self-complete, we give short proofs of Theorems B and A
             in the Appendix.

                                                   3. Proofs of Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 1.2
                  Proof of Theorem 1.1.
                  Claim 1. For any two subsets A, B ⊂ [1, m] such that |B| ⩾ |A|, there exist R ⊂ A and an
                  injection g : R → B such that (R, g) is D-shellable and |R| ⩾ |A| − D + 1.
                     Take a maximal subset R ⊂ A and an injection g : R → B such that (R, g) is D-shellable
                  (recall that the empty map is injective and hence (∅, ∅) is -shellable). We must have |R| ⩾
                  |A| − D + 1. Suppose that |R| ⩽ |A| − D. Take a D-subset of A ⊂ (A \ R) and D-subset B ⊂
                  (B \ g(R)). Let h be an arbitrary bijection from R onto S . By the definition of the Davenport
                  constant there is a non-empty subset R ⊂ A such that R h = 0. We now extend g to R ∪ R by
                  taking g(x) = h(x) for all x ∈ R . Now (R ∪ R ) is (D, g)-shellable, contradicting the maximality
                  of R.
                     Assume first D ⩽ . Since m ⩾ n − 1, we may take any subset S0 of [1, m] with cardinality
                  n − 1. By Claim 1, there exists S ⊂ S0 with n − 1 ⩾ |S| ⩾ n − D + 1 and an injection g : S →
                  [1, m] such that S g = 0. The result holds in this case.
                     Now assume  < D. Thus m = n −  + D − 1.
                  Claim 2. There is a subset T ⊂ [1, m] such that |T | ⩾ D −  and an injection g : T → [1, m]
                  such that (T , g) is -shellable. Take a maximal subset R ⊂ [1, m] and an injection g : R →
                  [1, m] such that (R, g) is -shellable (recall that the empty map is injective and hence (∅, ∅) is
                  -shellable). We must have |R| ⩾ D − , since otherwise m − |R| ⩾ n −  + D − 1 − (D −
                   − 1) ⩾ n. By the lemma there is a non-empty subset R ⊂ [1, m] \ R with |R | ⩽  and an
                  injection h : R → [1, m] \ g(R) such that R h = 0. We now extend g to R ∪ R by taking
                  g(x) = h(x) for all x ∈ R . Now (R ∪ R , g) is -shellable, contradicting the maximality of R.
                     Put C = [1, m] \ T . By Claim 1, applied to C, there are a subset E ⊂ C and injective map
                  f : E → [1, m] \ g(R), such that n − 1 ⩾ |C| ⩾ |E| ⩾ |C| − D + 1 and E f = 0. Now n − 1 −
                  |E| ⩽ n − 1 − |C| + D − 1 = (m +  − D − 1) − |C| + 1 = |T | +  − D < |T |. By Lemma 2.1
                  there is a subset F ⊂ T such that F f = 0 and n − 1 − |E| ⩾ |F| ⩾ n − 1 − |E| −  + 1. In
                  particular, n − 1 ⩾ |S| ⩾ n − , where S = E ∪ F. We extend f to S, by taking f(i) = g(i) for
                  every i ∈ F. Clearly f is injective on S and S f = E f + F f = 0.

                                           Appendix: Proofs of Theorem A and Theorem B
             Proof of Theorem A. The proof is by induction on |B|, the result being obvious for |B| = 1.
             Since 0 ∈ A ∩ B, we have |A + B| ⩾ |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|. Therefore we may assume
             that there exists b ∈ A ∩ B, with b = 0.
                Put B = B ∩ (B − b). We have B − b = B, since otherwise we would have b ∈ A ∩ −B, a
             contradiction. Hence 0 ∈ B and |B | < |B|.
                Put A = A ∪ A + b. We have A ∩ −B = (A ∩ −B) ∩ (−B + b) ∪ A + b ∩ (−B + b)∩
             (−B) = {0}. The result follows by induction.
             Proof of Theorem B. One may partition the sequence x as X1 ∪ · · · Xk , where Xi is a set.
             Suppose that x has no non-empty zero-sum subsequence. We prove by induction that |X1 +
             · · · + Xj | ⩾ |X1 | + · · · + |Xj |, for every j ⩽ k. This is true for j = 1. Suppose it is true for j < k.
             Put A = X1 + · · · + Xj ∪ {0} and B = Xj+1 ∪ {0}. Suppose that x has no non-empty zero-sum
             subsequence: we have |A| ⩾ |X1 + · · · + Xj | + 1 and B = |Xj+1 | + 1. By Theorem A,
                            |X1 + · · · + Xj+1 | ⩾ |A + B| − 1 ⩾ |A| + |B| − 2 ⩾ |X1 | + · · · + |Xj+1 |.
             Then |X1 + · · · + Xk | ⩾ |X1 | + · · · + |Xk | = |G|, and x then has a zero-sum subsequence, a
             contradiction.
