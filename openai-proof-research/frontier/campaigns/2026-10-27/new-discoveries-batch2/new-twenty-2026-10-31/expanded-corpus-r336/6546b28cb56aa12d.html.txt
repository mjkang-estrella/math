Abstract
    

Given a graph drawn in the plane, the degenerate crossing number of the drawing is the number of points in the plane which are contained in the relative interior of at least two edges, where each edge is required to be drawn as a simple arc. The degenerate crossing number of a graph is the minimum degenerate crossing number among all its drawings.
    

Given a drawing, cutting a neighborhood of the surface around each crossing and pasting a Möbius band gives a non-orientable surface, on which the drawing of the graph can be extended to an embedding. From this observation, Mohar derived that the degenerate crossing number of a graph is at most its non-orientable genus, and conjectured that these quantities are equal for every graph. He also made a stronger conjecture for loopless pseudo-triangulations with a fixed embedding scheme.
    

In this paper, we prove a structure theorem that allows to understand when the degenerate crossing number and non-orientable genus coincide in a large class of loopless bipartite embedding schemes. In particular, we provide a counterexample to Mohar’s stronger conjecture, but show that in the vast majority of the 2-vertex cases, as well as for many bipartite graphs, Mohar’s conjecture is satisfied.
    

The reversal distance between two signed permutations is the minimum number of reversals that transform one permutation to the other one.
If we represent the trajectory of each element of a signed permutation under successive reversals by a simple arc, we obtain a drawing of a 2-vertex embedding scheme with degenerate crossings.
Our main result is proved by leveraging this connection and a classical result in genome rearrangement (the Hannenhalli–Pevzner algorithm) and can also be understood as an extension of this algorithm when the reversals do not necessarily happen in a monotone order.

An even stronger conjecture was hinted at in [14, Paragraph following Conjecture 3.4], suggesting that one could possibly remove the loopless assumption if one forbids separating loops. This strengthening was disproved by Schaefer and Štefankovič [16, Theorem 7].




In addition to their motivation from crossing number theory, these conjectures would also shed light on the difficult task of visualizing high genus embedded graphs, providing an alternate approach to that of Duncan, Goodrich and Kobourov [7], who rely on canonical polygonal schemes [12].




A big step towards both these conjectures was achieved by Schaefer and Štefankovič, who proved [16, Theorem 10] that
any multi-graph embedded on a non-orientable surface of genus  g gg has a cross-cap drawing with  g gg cross-caps, in which each edge enters each cross-cap at most twice.
The theorem of Schaefer and Štefankovič applies in particular to one-vertex embedded multi-graphs,
and thus suggests a natural approach towards proving Conjectures 1.1 and 1.2.
First contract a spanning tree to obtain a one-vertex graph and apply this theorem.
Then, edges might enter cross-caps twice, but since the initial graph is loopless, one could hope to uncontract some edges so as to spread these two cross-caps on two edges, thus obtaining a perfect cross-cap drawing.
Our first result shows that this approach cannot work, as some loopless  2 22-vertex graph embeddings do not admit perfect cross-cap drawings.

2 Preliminaries




2.1 Embedding schemes




We assume that the reader is familiar with basic concepts on the topology of surfaces (classification, genus, orientability, etc.) and refer to Mohar and Thomassen [13] or Stillwell [17] for background on these topics.




In this article, we work with multi-graphs, possibly with loops and multiple edges. To every edge of a multi-graph we associate two half-edges, one incident to each endpoint of the edge: the main function of these half-edges is to differentiate the two incidences of a loop with a vertex. An embedding of a graph  G GG on a surface  S SS is a continuous injective map  \phi:G\rightarrow S ϕ:G→S\phi:G\rightarrow S. We consider two embeddings on a surface  S SS to be equivalent if there exists a homeomorphism of  S SS sending the image of one embedding to the other one. The faces of an embedded graph are the connected components of  S\setminus\phi(G) S∖ϕ⁡(G)S\setminus\phi(G).



Important remark.



From now on, all the graphs that we consider are always embedded, and therefore we apply the common abuse of language to identify a graph  G GG with its embedding  \phi ϕ\phi.





An embedding is cellular if its faces are homeomorphic to topological disks. The Euler genus,  eg(G) e​g​(G)eg(G), of a cellular embedding of a graph  G GG is the quantity  2-v+e-f 2−v+e−f2-v+e-f, where  v vv,  e ee and  f ff denote respectively the number of vertices, edges and faces of the embedding of  G GG. If the surface  S SS that  G GG is embedded on is non-orientable and the embedding is cellular, the Euler genus  eg(G) e​g​(G)eg(G) equals the non-orientable genus of the surface that  G GG is embedded on, denoted by  g(G) g⁡(G)g(G).




A cyclic permutation is a permutation consisting of a single cycle. If  S SS is orientable, a cellular embedding on  S SS can be described combinatorially by a rotation system, that is, a set of cyclic permutations encoding the order of the half-edges around each vertex. When  S SS is non-orientable, which will almost always be the case in this paper, some additional data is required to encode a cellular embedding, which is encompassed in the concept of an embedding scheme. We introduce the main definitions and refer to Mohar and Thomassen [13, Section 3.3] for extensive background.

Then, pasting a topological disk on each facial cycle yields a cellular embedding of  G GG on a surface  S SS. Given an embedding scheme  (G,\rho,\lambda) (G,ρ,λ)(G,\rho,\lambda), a flip at a vertex  v vv is a transformation that yields another embedding scheme of the same graph, in which we reverse the order of the edges incident to  v vv and invert the signature of those edges incident to  v vv that are not loops. We say that two embedding schemes  (G,\rho,\lambda) (G,ρ,λ)(G,\rho,\lambda) and  (G,\rho^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}) (G,ρ′,λ′)(G,\rho^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}) are equivalent if one can go from one to the other one by a sequence of flips. Two embedding schemes are equivalent if and only if they induce equivalent cellular embeddings [13, Theorem 3.3.1]. This justifies that equivalence classes of embedding schemes and embedded graphs can be considered as being two representations of the same objects, and we switch freely between the two points of view in this article, often using the shorthand  G GG to denote  (G,\rho,\lambda) (G,ρ,λ)(G,\rho,\lambda).




A cycle in a graph is a closed walk without repeated vertices or edges, and a cycle in an embedding scheme is a cycle on the edges of the underlying graph. A cycle in an embedding scheme is one-sided (resp. two-sided) if and only if the signatures of its edges multiply to  -1 −1-1 (resp.  +1 +1+1). An embedding scheme is called orientable if it only contains two-sided cycles; otherwise it is called non-orientable. A cycle  \gamma γ\gamma in a non-orientable embedding scheme is separating if  S\setminus\gamma S∖γS\setminus\gamma has two connected components. A cycle  \gamma γ\gamma in a non-orientable embedding scheme is orienting [9, 16] if  S\setminus\gamma S∖γS\setminus\gamma is a connected orientable surface.




We use cycle notation to describe cyclic permutations, and thus for example  (1,4,3,2) (1,4,3,2)(1,4,3,2) and  (4,3,2,1) (4,3,2,1)(4,3,2,1) denote the same permutation. 2.2 2-vertex loopless embedding schemes and signed cyclic permutations




This paper almost exclusively deals with loopless graphs with two vertices. In that setting, without loss of generality, one can number the edges so that the cyclic permutation around one of the vertices is the cyclic permutation  (1,2,\ldots,n) (1,2,…,n)(1,2,\ldots,n), which we denote by  I II. Then the data of the embedding scheme just consists of the cyclic permutation around the other vertex, and the signature of the edges, and thus this amounts to a signed cyclic permutation: a cyclic permutation where each number is additionally endowed with a  + ++ or  - −- sign, which we denote using the overline notation  \overline{i} i¯\overline{i}.



Important remark.



Throughout the rest of the article, we freely identify a signed cyclic permutation  \pi π\pi and the 2-vertex loopless embedding scheme where:

• 



the cyclic permutation around one vertex is  I II,

• 



the cyclic permutation around the other vertex is the mirror of  \pi π\pi, and

• 



the signatures of the edges are indicated by the signs of the elements in  \pi π\pi.



There is a mirror here in order to be coherent with signed reversal distance: this ensures that the base case  \pi=I π=I\pi=I corresponds to an orientable, planar (genus zero) embedding scheme. Similarly, the following lemma allows us to recognize separating cycles combinatorially.



Lemma 2.3.



For two edges  a aa and  b bb in a loopless 2-vertex embedding scheme, the cycle  a\cdot b a⋅ba\cdot b is separating if and only if  a aa and  b bb have the same signature and  \omega_{a,b} ωa,b\omega_{a,b} encloses all the edges.




Proof.



We contract  a aa. If  a aa is positive, then  b bb has the same signature and this is maintained after contraction. If  a aa is negative, then  b bb is also negative but this is reversed by the contraction. In both cases, after the contraction, the loop  b bb has positive signature in  G^{\prime} G′G^{\prime}. Furthermore, the half-edges in  \omega_{a,b} ωa,b\omega_{a,b} in  G GG correspond to one of the sets of half-edges divided by  b bb in  G^{\prime} G′G^{\prime} (in the case where  a aa is negative, the change in the definition of wedge and the mirroring in the contraction cancel out). Therefore, for every loop in  G^{\prime} G′G^{\prime}, the two halves are contained on the same side of  b bb. Since  b bb is positive, it is two-sided, and thus it partitions the set of faces into two-nonempty subsets. Therefore  b bb is separating. Conversely if the halves of some edge alternate with  b bb, this contradicts the fact that  b bb is separating since one can use that edge to connect its two sides. Uncontracting  a aa yields the proof of the lemma.
∎

Blocks, homotopies and portions




For an sc-permutation  \pi π\pi which we interpret as a 2-vertex embedding scheme, applying a flip at one of the vertices amounts to flipping  \pi π\pi, i.e., mirroring the sc-permutation and changing the sign of all its elements. We say that two sc-permutations are equivalent if they differ by such a flip.




A positive block in an sc-permutation  \pi π\pi is an interval  I=(\pi_{i},\pi_{j}) I=(πi,πj)I=(\pi_{i},\pi_{j}) where all the elements are positive, and  I II contains exactly all the elements in  [\pi_{i},\pi_{j}] [πi,πj][\pi_{i},\pi_{j}]. For example the interval  (6,3)=\{6,2,1,3\} (6,3)={6,2,1,3}(6,3)=\{6,2,1,3\} is a positive block in the sc-permutation  (2,1,3,\bar{5},4,6) (2,1,3,5¯,4,6)(2,1,3,\bar{5},4,6). A negative block in an sc-permutation is an interval  I=(\pi_{i},\pi_{j}) I=(πi,πj)I=(\pi_{i},\pi_{j}) where all the elements are negative, and  I II contains exactly all the elements in  [\pi_{j},\pi_{i}] [πj,πi][\pi_{j},\pi_{i}], for example  (\bar{6},\bar{3}) (6¯,3¯)(\bar{6},\bar{3}) is a negative block in the sc-permutation  (2,1,\bar{6},\bar{4},\bar{5},\bar{3},7) (2,1,6¯,4¯,5¯,3¯,7)(2,1,\bar{6},\bar{4},\bar{5},\bar{3},7). A positive (respectively a negative) block is trivial if it is sorted in increasing (respectively decreasing) order, i.e., if it is equal to  (\pi_{i},\pi_{i}+1,\ldots,\pi_{j}-1,\pi_{j}) (πi,πi+1,…,πj−1,πj)(\pi_{i},\pi_{i}+1,\ldots,\pi_{j}-1,\pi_{j}) or to  (\overline{\pi_{i}},\overline{\pi_{i}-1},\ldots,\overline{\pi_{j}+1},\overline{\pi_{j}}) (πi¯,πi−1¯,…,πj+1¯,πj¯)(\overline{\pi_{i}},\overline{\pi_{i}-1},\ldots,\overline{\pi_{j}+1},\overline{\pi_{j}}).
In these examples and also in non trivial blocks we call  \pi_{i} πi\pi_{i} and  \pi_{j} πj\pi_{j} the frames of the block.
Two edges are homotopic if they belong to a common trivial block (note that this matches the definition given in the introduction). A block is (inclusionwise) minimal if it does not contain any block except itself. We remark that the notion of blocks is similar to the notions of hurdles of [10], and of unoriented components in [2].




From the point of view of embedding schemes, a block corresponds to a collection of edges defining an orientable subscheme, as defined in the introduction. Indeed, by Lemma 2.3 the condition of containing exactly all integers between the positive integer values of the two frames  \pi_{i} πi\pi_{i} and  \pi_{j} πj\pi_{j}, together with the frames having the same sign, corresponds to the closed curve  \pi_{i}\cdot\pi_{j} πi⋅πj\pi_{i}\cdot\pi_{j} being a separating curve. The condition on all the edges in the block having the same sign implies that all closed curves in the block are two-sided and therefore the frames separate an orientable portion.




Recall from the introduction that portions are defined by (i) cutting a  2 22-vertex embedding scheme  G GG along all edges belonging to separating curves, yielding connected components of the surface, (ii) merging together adjacent non-orientable components and (iii) capping off boundaries with disks. These portions are naturally arranged in a tree.




To describe the portions tree, we first consider the connected components before the merging at step (ii) takes place. Define a relation between edges,  e\sim e^{\prime} e∼e′e\sim e^{\prime} if  e\cdot e^{\prime} e⋅e′e\cdot e^{\prime} is a separating curve. It follows from Lemma 2.3 that this is an equivalence relation. Moreover, by the same lemma, for each of these equivalence classes, the restrictions of the sc-permutation  \pi π\pi and the sc-permutation  I II to elements of that class are identical.




We now define a bipartite graph  T^{\prime} T′T^{\prime}, that has one node for each connected component appearing after step (i) above, and one node for each equivalence class described in the previous paragraph. A node in  T^{\prime} T′T^{\prime} corresponding to a connected component  C CC is connected to a node in  T^{\prime} T′T^{\prime} corresponding to an equivalence class  Q QQ if a pair of edges on the boundary of  C CC forming a separating curve belongs to  Q QQ. Finally we define the portions tree  T TT to be a quotient of  T^{\prime} T′T^{\prime}: we merge any two nodes of  T^{\prime} T′T^{\prime} corresponding to non-orientable connected components that share an edge. This implies that they are at distance two in  T^{\prime} T′T^{\prime}, and thus this merging preserves the tree structure. Furthermore, if  v vv is a node corresponding to an equivalence class that has degree  2 22 in  T^{\prime} T′T^{\prime} after this merging, connecting a node  u uu to a node  w ww, we erase  v vv and connect directly  u uu to  w ww. We say that an edge of  G GG is a boundary edge if it is involved in an edge of the portions tree, i.e., either if it is contained in a separating curve between two different vertices of the portions tree, or if its equivalence class is present in the portion tree. 2.3 Cross-cap drawings




Recall from the introduction that a cross-cap drawing of an embedded graph  G GG is a map  \phi^{\prime}:G\rightarrow(S^{2}\setminus g\tiny{\bigotimes})/\sim ϕ′:G→(S2∖g⨂)/∼\phi^{\prime}:G\rightarrow(S^{2}\setminus g\tiny{\bigotimes})/\sim such that there exists a homeomorphism  f\colon N_{g}\to(S^{2}\setminus g\tiny{\bigotimes})/\sim f:Ng→(S2∖g⨂)/∼f\colon N_{g}\to(S^{2}\setminus g\tiny{\bigotimes})/\sim, such that  f(\phi(G))=\phi^{\prime}(G) f⁡(ϕ⁡(G))=ϕ′​(G)f(\phi(G))=\phi^{\prime}(G), where  \phi ϕ\phi is the embedding of  G GG. The next lemma allows us to recognize types of cycles in a cross-cap drawing.



Lemma 2.5.



For any cross-cap drawing of a non-orientable embedding scheme:




1. 



A cycle is one-sided (two-sided) if and only if it enters an odd (even) number of cross-caps.

2. 



A cycle is orienting (separating) if and only if it enters each cross-cap an odd (even) number of times.







We refer to [16, Lemma 3] and [16, Lemma 4] for proofs.




The definition of cross-cap drawing above requires the underlying surface to be orientable and thus does not apply to orientable embedding schemes. We extend it to orientable embedding schemes of positive Euler genus as follows. A non-orientable extension of an orientable embedding scheme  G GG is a (non-cellular!) embedding obtained by adding exactly one cross-cap in one of the faces of  G GG. This choice is in general not unique. A cross-cap drawing of an orientable embedding  G GG is a cross-cap drawing of one of its non-orientable extensions. Such a cross-cap drawing has  eg(G)+1 e​g​(G)+1eg(G)+1 cross-caps.




Accordingly, when  G GG is an orientable embedding scheme with non-zero Euler genus, we define its non-orientable genus to be  g(G):=eg(G)+1 g⁡(G):=e​g​(G)+1g(G):=eg(G)+1. As for the Euler genus, this definition and notation is also used for sc-permutations. As in the non-orientable case, a cross-cap drawing is perfect if every edge enters every cross-cap at most once.

4.1 The Bafna-Pevzner inequality from Euler’s formula




Let  \pi π\pi be an sc-permutation, which, as explained in Section 2, we think of as a 2-vertex embedding scheme, which requires  g(\pi) g⁡(π)g(\pi) cross-caps to be drawn.  2-eg(\pi)=2-e(\pi)+f(\pi) 2−e​g​(π)=2−e⁡(π)+f⁡(π)2-eg(\pi)=2-e(\pi)+f(\pi) which simplifies to
 eg(\pi)=e(\pi)-f(\pi) e​g​(π)=e⁡(π)−f⁡(π)eg(\pi)=e(\pi)-f(\pi). By the construction in Section 2.4, we thus have  d^{c}(\pi,id)\geq e(\pi)-f(\pi) dc​(π,i​d)≥e⁡(π)−f⁡(π)d^{c}(\pi,id)\geq e(\pi)-f(\pi).




A very similar inequality was first discovered by Bafna and Pevzner [1, Theorem 2] without reference to embeddings. This inequality is sometimes strict, this is for instance the case for the embedding schemes of Theorem 3 and the example in Figure 3.




The starting idea of the Hannenhalli–Pevzner (HP) algorithm is to identify intervals in an sc-permutation where applying an sc-reversal is clearly making progress. Given an sc-permutation  \pi π\pi, we call a pair of consecutive integers  i ii and  i+1 i+1i+1 (modulo  n nn) reversible if they have opposite signs in  \pi π\pi (this is called an oriented pair in [2]). For a given reversible pair there exist two sc-reversals  \sigma,\sigma^{\prime} σ,σ′\sigma,\sigma^{\prime} such that  i ii and  i+1 i+1i+1 are homotopic in  \pi\cdot\sigma π⋅σ\pi\cdot\sigma and in  \pi\cdot\sigma^{\prime} π⋅σ′\pi\cdot\sigma^{\prime}. These two reversals are equivalent in the sense that the two sc-permutations that they yield differ by a flip. For example consider the sc-permutation  (1,3,\bar{2},4) (1,3,2¯,4)(1,3,\bar{2},4). The integers  1 11 and  2 22 are reversible. To make  1 11 and  2 22 homotopic we can reverse the interval  (3,\bar{2}) (3,2¯)(3,\bar{2}) or the interval  (4,1) (4,1)(4,1). Applying these reversals we obtain  (1,2,\bar{3},4) (1,2,3¯,4)(1,2,\bar{3},4) and  (\bar{4},3,\bar{2},\bar{1}) (4¯,3,2¯,1¯)(\bar{4},3,\bar{2},\bar{1}), respectively, in which  1 11 and  2 22 are homotopic.




The following lemma follows from an Euler characteristic argument.



Lemma 4.1.



If  (i,i+1) (i,i+1)(i,i+1) is a reversible pair in the sc-permutation  \pi π\pi and  \sigma σ\sigma is an sc-reversal that turns them into homotopic edges in  \pi\cdot\sigma π⋅σ\pi\cdot\sigma, then  eg(\pi\cdot\sigma)=eg(\pi)-1 e​g​(π⋅σ)=e​g​(π)−1eg(\pi\cdot\sigma)=eg(\pi)-1.




Proof.



Since  e(\pi)=e(\pi\cdot\sigma) e⁡(π)=e⁡(π⋅σ)e(\pi)=e(\pi\cdot\sigma), we need to show that  f(\pi\cdot\sigma)=f(\pi)+1 f⁡(π⋅σ)=f⁡(π)+1f(\pi\cdot\sigma)=f(\pi)+1. Since the edges  i ii and  i+1 i+1i+1 are consecutive in  I II, the edges  i ii and  i+1 i+1i+1 are incident to a face  a aa in  \pi π\pi. The faces of  \pi\cdot\sigma π⋅σ\pi\cdot\sigma can be computed by following the edges and switching sides depending on the signature. In doing so, one sees that the faces of  \pi\cdot\sigma π⋅σ\pi\cdot\sigma are the same as the faces of  \pi π\pi except that the face  a aa is subdivided into two faces: one is the degree two face incident to  i ii and  i+1 i+1i+1 and the other face is incident to the other edges that were incident to  a aa. No other face is disrupted by the sc-reversal, and thus this finishes the proof.
∎






4.2 The HP algorithm for sc-permutations




It is immediate to see that blocks do not contain reversible pairs of edges. More subtly, this is also the case for orientable portions, which generalize (minimal) blocks. These orientable portions will constitute the natural obstruction to applying the sc-reversals described above. Following the terminology for embedding schemes, we say that an sc-permutation is non-orientable if it contains two elements of different signs, otherwise it is orientable. Similarly, it is reduced if the associated embedding scheme is. Cleaning an sc-permutation means identifying its homotopic elements, yielding a cleaned permutation.




Given a reversible pair  (i,i+1) (i,i+1)(i,i+1) in the sc-permutation  \pi π\pi, let  \sigma σ\sigma be an sc-reversal that turns  i ii and  i+1 i+1i+1 into homotopic edges. The score of  (i,i+1) (i,i+1)(i,i+1) is the number of reversible pairs in  \pi\cdot\sigma π⋅σ\pi\cdot\sigma. Note that the score of  (i,i+1) (i,i+1)(i,i+1) is independent of the choice of sc-reversal, since the two possible sc-reversals are equivalent. The basis of the HP algorithm, following the presentation of Bergeron [3] adapted to the cyclic case, is as follows.



        



HP algorithm:

        



Input: A reduced two-vertex non-orientable embedding scheme  G GG.

Output: A perfect cross-cap drawing of  G GG.

Algorithm: While there is a reversible pair, apply an sc-reversal on a pair of maximal score.

      




Then the main theorem summarizing the properties of the HP algorithm is the following.



Theorem 4.2.



If an sc-permutation  \pi π\pi is non-orientable and reduced, then  d^{c}(\pi,I)=eg(\pi) dc​(π,I)=e​g​(π)d^{c}(\pi,I)=eg(\pi), and the HP algorithm gives a sequence of sc-reversals of this optimal length.





This theorem follows at once from the following lemma. Recall that an sc-permutation is reduced if its component tree has only one connected component and to clean an sc-permutation we identify homotopic edges.



Lemma 4.3.



Let  \pi π\pi be a non-orientable and reduced sc-permutation, let  (i,i+1) (i,i+1)(i,i+1) be a reversible pair of maximal score, and let  \sigma σ\sigma be an sc-reversal that makes  i ii and  i+1 i+1i+1 homotopic, then either

• 



 \pi\cdot\sigma π⋅σ\pi\cdot\sigma is either  I II or its flip, or

• 



 \pi\cdot\sigma π⋅σ\pi\cdot\sigma is non-orientable and is reduced once it is cleaned.







We first show how to prove Theorem 4.2 assuming the lemma, and postpone the proof of the lemma to the Section 4.4.



Proof of Theorem 4.2.



The proof is by induction on  eg(\pi) e​g​(π)eg(\pi). Since by assumption  \pi π\pi is non-orientable,  eg(\pi)\geq 1 e​g​(π)≥1eg(\pi)\geq 1.




For the initialization step, if  eg(\pi)=1 e​g​(π)=1eg(\pi)=1, by Lemma 4.1 a single sc-reversal transforms  \pi π\pi into an sc-permutation of Euler genus zero, i.e., the sc-permutation  I II or its flip.

4.3 The interleaving graph

6 From  2 22-vertex graphs to bipartite graphs




As we announced in the introduction, the case of bipartite embedding schemes reduces to the case of 2-vertex embedding schemes. This is summarized in the next two lemmas, and completes the proof of the general case of Theorem 1.5.




Recall that an allowable contraction of  G GG is the result of adding an edge in a face of  G GG between two non-adjacent vertices on the same side of the bipartition and contracting it. Note that the embedding scheme we obtain after an allowable contraction of  G GG has the same genus as  G GG.

Lemma 6.2.



For any connected bipartite embedding scheme  G GG, there exists an embedding scheme  H HH that is obtained after a sequence of allowable contractions of  G GG such that  H HH has only two vertices and does not contain any loop.
