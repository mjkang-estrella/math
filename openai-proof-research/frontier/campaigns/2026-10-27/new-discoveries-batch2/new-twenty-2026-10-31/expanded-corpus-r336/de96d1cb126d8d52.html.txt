Abstract.
    

 \Delta Δ\Delta-spaces have been defined by a natural generalization of a classical notion of  \Delta Δ\Delta-sets of reals to
Tychonoff topological spaces; moreover, the class  \Delta Δ\Delta of all  \Delta Δ\Delta-spaces consists precisely of those  X XX for which the locally convex space  C_{p}(X) Cp​(X)C_{p}(X) is distinguished [25].
    

The aim of this article is to better understand the boundaries of the class  \Delta Δ\Delta,
by presenting new examples and counter-examples.
    

1) We examine when trees considered as topological spaces equipped with the interval topology belong to  \Delta Δ\Delta.
In particular, we prove that no Souslin tree is a  \Delta Δ\Delta-space.
Other main results are connected with the study of
2)  \Psi Ψ\Psi-spaces built on maximal almost disjoint families of countable sets; and 3) Ladder system spaces.
    

It is consistent with CH that all ladder system spaces on  \omega_{1} ω1\omega_{1} are in  \Delta Δ\Delta.
We show that in forcing extension of ZFC obtained by adding one Cohen real, there is a ladder system space on  \omega_{1} ω1\omega_{1} which is not in  \Delta Δ\Delta.
    

We resolve several open problems posed in [12], [25], [31], [32].
  



Key words and phrases:  \Delta Δ\Delta-set, countably metacompact space,  \omega_{1} ω1\omega_{1}-tree,  \Psi Ψ\Psi-space, ladder system space

1. Introduction




The reader is referred to consequent sections for an unexplained terminology.
Throughout the article, all topological spaces are assumed to be Tychonoff and infinite.
By  C_{p}(X) Cp​(X)C_{p}(X) we mean the space  C(X) C⁡(X)C(X) of real-valued continuous functions defined on  X XX
equipped with the pointwise convergence topology.
In this paper we investigate the class of topological spaces  X XX
such that the locally convex space  C_{p}(X) Cp​(X)C_{p}(X) is distinguished.
Distinguished locally convex (metrizable) spaces were introduced by J. Dieudonné and L. Schwartz
and the reader may consult [13] (and references therein) for an introduction to and a brief history of the main advances
in the area of general distinguished locally convex spaces.




An intrinsic description of  X XX such that  C_{p}(X) Cp​(X)C_{p}(X) is distinguished
has been found recently in [25] (see also [12]).



Theorem 1.1.



([25])
For a Tychonoff space  X XX, the following conditions are equivalent:

(1) 



 C_{p}(X) Cp​(X)C_{p}(X) is distinguished.

(2) 



Any countable disjoint collection of subsets of  X XX admits a point-finite open expansion in  X XX.

(3) 



 X XX is a  \Delta Δ\Delta-space.






Definition 1.2.



([25], [27])
A topological space  X XX is said to be a  \Delta Δ\Delta-space if for every decreasing sequence  \{D_{n}:n\in\omega\} {Dn:n∈ω}\{D_{n}:n\in\omega\}
of subsets of  X XX with empty intersection, there is a decreasing sequence  \{V_{n}:n\in\omega\} {Vn:n∈ω}\{V_{n}:n\in\omega\} consisting of open
subsets of  X XX, also with empty intersection, and such that  D_{n}\subseteq V_{n} Dn⊆VnD_{n}\subseteq V_{n} for every  n\in\omega n∈ωn\in\omega.




The class of all  \Delta Δ\Delta-spaces in the paper is denoted by  \Delta Δ\Delta. Also, any uncountable subset of the real line  {\mathbb{R}} ℝ{\mathbb{R}} that is a  \Delta Δ\Delta-space is called a  \Delta Δ\Delta-set.





The original definition of a  \Delta Δ\Delta-set is due to G. M. Reed and E. K. van Douwen (see [39]) and the formulation of and
the interest in  \Delta Δ\Delta-sets followed from the study of the dividing line between the class of normal spaces and the class of countably paracompact spaces.
Research on countably paracompact spaces has a long history in set-theoretic topology related to both the Normal Moore Space Conjecture
and the study of preservation of normality in products.




C. H. Dowker proved that for a normal space  X XX the product  X\times[0,1] X×[0,1]X\times[0,1] is normal if and only if  X XX
is also countably paracompact (equivalently, countably metacompact).
M. E. Rudin [43] gave the first ZFC example of a Dowker space of size  (\aleph_{\omega})^{\aleph_{0}} (ℵω)ℵ0(\aleph_{\omega})^{\aleph_{0}}, but
Z. Balogh [2] constructed the first ZFC Dowker space with cardinality of the continuum  \mathfrak{c} 𝔠\mathfrak{c}.
Using PCF theory, M. Kojman and S. Shelah [28] constructed another ZFC Dowker space of cardinality  \aleph_{\omega+1} ℵω+1\aleph_{\omega+1}.




So, when a normal space is countably paracompact is an important question still attracting attention to this day, and M. E. Rudin’s problem whether
there is a normal not countably paracompact space of size  \omega_{1} ω1\omega_{1} in ZFC is still open (see [22]).
Conversely, countably paracompactness also does not imply normality but distinguishing between these properties is often delicate.




An uncountable set  X\subset{\mathbb{R}} X⊂ℝX\subset{\mathbb{R}} is called a  Q QQ-set if every subset of  X XX is relative  G_{\delta} GδG_{\delta} (see e.g. [16]).
It is straightforward to show that every  Q QQ-set is a  \Delta Δ\Delta-set.
The question whether there can be a  Q QQ-set goes back to F. Hausdorff and W. Sierpiński in the 1930’s ([20], [45])
and renewed interest in  Q QQ-sets arose in connection to constructions of interesting counterexamples in the theory of normal spaces.
F. B. Jones proved that well-known construction of the tangent disc topology over a set of reals  X XX is normal and non-metrizable if and only if  X XX is a  Q QQ-set.
As well, the same construction is countably paracompact if and only if  X XX is a  \Delta Δ\Delta-set.
Furthermore, T. C. Przymusiński
showed that the existence of a  \Delta Δ\Delta-set is actually equivalent to the existence of a separable countably paracompact non-metrizable Moore space [38, Theorem 7].




It is not difficult to show that the existence of a  Q QQ-set implies  2^{\aleph_{0}}=2^{\aleph_{1}} 2ℵ0=2ℵ12^{\aleph_{0}}=2^{\aleph_{1}} [42],
while it is consistent that there are no  \Delta Δ\Delta-sets (e.g. assuming the Continuum Hypothesis (CH)).
On the other hand, under Martin’s Axiom (MA) with the negation of  CH C​HCH,
every uncountable subset of  {\mathbb{R}} ℝ{\mathbb{R}} of cardinality less than  \mathfrak{c} 𝔠\mathfrak{c} is a  Q QQ-set (see [16]).




Whether it is consistent that there is a  \Delta Δ\Delta-set which is not a  Q QQ-set seems to be still not well understood.
The proof appearing in [27] is not clear and we are unaware of any expert in the field who understands it.
Let us state as an open and challenging problem to find an alternative proof.
The following question also seems to be open until now.



Problem 1.3.



([40]) 
Does the existence of a  \Delta Δ\Delta-set imply  2^{\aleph_{0}}=2^{\aleph_{1}} 2ℵ0=2ℵ12^{\aleph_{0}}=2^{\aleph_{1}}?





Most of the above facts about  Q QQ-sets and  \Delta Δ\Delta-sets can be found in [35], [39].




To distinguish between sets of reals and general topological spaces, we define a topological space  X XX to be a  Q QQ-space if every subset of  X XX is a  G_{\delta} GδG_{\delta}-set
(equivalently, every subset of  X XX is a  F_{\sigma} FσF_{\sigma}-set).  X XX is called a  \sigma σ\sigma-closed discrete space if  X XX is a countable union of closed discrete subspaces.
We mention the following simple facts about  Q QQ-spaces:




• 



 X XX is a  Q QQ-space implies that  X XX is a  \Delta Δ\Delta-space (the proof is the same as for sets of reals, see e.g. [25, Proposition 4.1]).

• 



If  X XX is a  \sigma σ\sigma-closed discrete then  X XX is a  Q QQ-space, therefore also a  \Delta Δ\Delta-space.

• 



If  X XX is a  Q QQ-space then  X XX is a  \sigma σ\sigma-discrete iff  X XX is  \sigma σ\sigma-closed discrete.






Whether there is a  Q QQ-space that is not  \sigma σ\sigma-discrete is quite non-trivial.
This question was first asked by H. Junnila [24] and led Z. Balogh to define a topological space  X XX to be a  Q QQ-set space if  X XX is a  Q QQ-space and
 X XX is not  \sigma σ\sigma-discrete [1]. In that paper he gave a beautiful ZFC construction of a  Q QQ-set space and later improved this result to obtain
a hereditarily paracompact, perfectly normal  Q QQ-set space  X XX such that  |X|=\mathfrak{c} |X|=𝔠|X|=\mathfrak{c} [3]. It is worth remarking that the techniques used in constructing
 Q QQ-set spaces Z. Balogh later adapted to construct several Dowker spaces in ZFC including the aforementioned example of size  \mathfrak{c} 𝔠\mathfrak{c}.




A systematic study of the class  \Delta Δ\Delta was originated in the paper [25] and continued in [26].
Among other results, it was proved that a  \Delta Δ\Delta-space  X XX must be scattered if  X XX is
locally compact [25] or countably compact [26].
It has been shown that the ordered space of ordinals  [0,\omega_{1}] [0,ω1][0,\omega_{1}] provides
an example of a scattered compact space which is not in  \Delta Δ\Delta [25].




However, a collection of compact  \Delta Δ\Delta-spaces is quite rich and includes both scattered Eberlein compact spaces and
one-point compactifications of Isbell-Mrówka  \Psi Ψ\Psi-spaces (for details see [25]).




The class  \Delta Δ\Delta is preserved under closed continuous images and it is invariant under the operation of taking countable unions of closed subspaces [26].
In particular, any topological space which can be represented as a countable union of scattered Eberlein compact spaces is in  \Delta Δ\Delta.




It is worth mentioning also that the class  \Delta Δ\Delta is invariant under taking arbitrary subspaces, and adding a finite set
to a  \Delta Δ\Delta-space does not destroy the property of being a  \Delta Δ\Delta-space [25].




The aim of this article is to better understand the boundaries of the class  \Delta Δ\Delta,
by presenting new examples and counter-examples.




We focus on the three very well known set-theoretical constructions producing locally compact first-countable topological spaces:
 \omega_{1} ω1\omega_{1}-trees equipped with the interval topology (Section 3);
 \Psi(D,{\mathcal{A}}) Ψ⁡(D,𝒜)\Psi(D,{\mathcal{A}}) spaces, where  {\mathcal{A}} 𝒜{\mathcal{A}} is an almost disjoint family of countable subsets of an infinite set  D DD (Section 4);
and the ladder system spaces  X_{L} XLX_{L} (Section 5).




Summing up several known facts, we note that every almost Souslin tree is countably metacompact (Fact 3.5), and we observe that
consistently there is an almost Souslin Aronszajn tree that is a  Q QQ-space (Fact 3.2).
The following statement proved in Section 3 is one of the main results of our paper:
no Souslin tree is a  \Delta Δ\Delta-space (Theorem 3.6).




In Section 4 we give the negative answer to [25, Problem 5.11]:
there is a locally compact  \Psi Ψ\Psi-space  X XX such that its one-point compactification is a scattered compact space with a finite Cantor-Bendixson rank,
but  X\notin\Delta X∉ΔX\notin\Delta (Corollary 4.6).
Also, we demonstrate that there exists an Isbell-Mrówka  \Psi Ψ\Psi-space  X XX such that one-point extension  X_{p}=X\cup\{p\} Xp=X∪{p}X_{p}=X\cup\{p\} of  X XX has uncountable
tightness at the point  p pp, for some  p\in\beta(X)\setminus X p∈β⁡(X)∖Xp\in\beta(X)\setminus X (Theorem 4.10).
This result resolves [31, Problem 2.15] and [32, Question 4.1].




It is consistent with CH that all ladder system spaces on  \omega_{1} ω1\omega_{1} are in  \Delta Δ\Delta.
The main result presented in Section 5 says that
in forcing extension obtained by adding one Cohen real, there is a ladder system  L LL on  \omega_{1} ω1\omega_{1} whose corresponding space  X_{L} XLX_{L}
is not countably metacompact and hence  X_{L}\notin\Delta XL∉ΔX_{L}\notin\Delta (Theorem 5.3).
Also, assuming  MA(\omega_{1}) M​A​(ω1)MA(\omega_{1}), we prove that for any pair of ladder systems,  L_{1} L1L_{1} and  L_{2} L2L_{2}, the product  X_{L_{1}}\times X_{L_{2}} XL1×XL2X_{L_{1}}\times X_{L_{2}} is hereditarily normal
(Theorem 5.7).




In the last Section 6 we notably generalize several results from [13] and also we
provide an example of a Lindelöf  P PP-space  X\notin\Delta X∉ΔX\notin\Delta answering negatively [12, Problem 24].

3. Trees




A tree is a well-founded partially ordered set such the set of predecessors of any element is well-ordered.
In the article,  \text{Lev}_{\alpha}(T) Levα​(T)\text{Lev}_{\alpha}(T) denotes the  \alpha^{\text{th}} αth\alpha^{\text{th}} level of a tree  T TT and  T_{\alpha}=\bigcup_{\beta<\alpha}\text{Lev}_{\beta}(T) Tα=⋃β<αLevβ​(T)T_{\alpha}=\bigcup_{\beta<\alpha}\text{Lev}_{\beta}(T).
An Aronszajn tree is an  \omega_{1} ω1\omega_{1}-tree with no uncountable branches. A Souslin tree is an  \omega_{1} ω1\omega_{1}-tree such that
every branch and every antichain is at most countable.
An  \omega_{1} ω1\omega_{1}-tree  T TT is said to be almost Souslin if whenever  A AA is an antichain of  T TT then
 \{\alpha\in\omega_{1}:A\cap\text{Lev}_{\alpha}(T)\neq\emptyset\} {α∈ω1:A∩Levα​(T)≠∅}\{\alpha\in\omega_{1}:A\cap\text{Lev}_{\alpha}(T)\neq\emptyset\} is not stationary.
A tree is called special if it can be represented as a countable union of antichains.




While there are a number of interesting topologies that can be considered on a tree, we consider trees as topological spaces equipped with the interval topology.
The interval topology on a tree  T TT is generated by the open base consisting of all intervals of the form
 (s,t]=\{x\in T:s<x\leq t\} (s,t]={x∈T:s<x≤t}(s,t]=\{x\in T:s<x\leq t\}, together with all singletons  \{t\} {t}\{t\}, where  t tt is a minimal element of  T TT.
