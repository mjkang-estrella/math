10:                                                   Abstract. We consider the weighted version of the Tron game on graphs
11:                                                   where two players, Alice and Bob, each build their own path by claiming one
12:                                                   vertex at a time, starting with Alice. The vertices carry non-negative weights
13:                                                   that sum up to 1 and either player tries to claim a path with larger total weight
14:                                                   than the opponent. We show that if the graph is a tree then Alice can always
15:                                                   ensure to get at most 1/5 less than Bob, and that there exist trees where Bob
16:                                                   can ensure to get at least 1/5 more than Alice.

19:                                                                                1. Introduction
20:                                              Mathematical games have been a frequent subject of study (see the 1700+ ar-
21:                                           ticles recently collected by Fraenkel [1]) for more than 60 years by now; not only
22:                                           because they are entertaining in nature, but also because of their relevance in
23:                                           practice, such as their close relation to diverse fields like biology, economics, and
24:                                           psychology. Moreover, the analysis of mathematical games often reveals funda-
25:                                           mental combinatorial structures and sometimes leads to the development of entire
26:                                           branches in mathematics and computer science.
27:                                              In 1990 Bodlaender and Kloks [2] introduced the following impartial game with
28:                                           perfect information called Tron: Given a graph, two players Alice and Bob choose
29:                                           distinct start vertices, first Alice then Bob. Afterwards Alice and Bob take turns,
30:                                           each time claiming a new vertex adjacent to his/her previously claimed vertex and
31:                                           not claimed by anybody so far. Thus either player builds a path with one end being
32:                                           its start vertex such that throughout the two paths are vertex disjoint. A player
33:                                           loses when no vertex can extend the corresponding path.
34:                                              Tron is inspired by the famous light cycle scene from the 1982 movie with the
35:                                           same name, which also gave rise to several computer games. The combinatorial
36:                                           game Tron belongs to the class of subgraph building games, where we essentially
37:                                           ask which of Alice and Bob can build a larger subgraph of a given graph according
38:                                           to certain building rules. In the weighted version, the game is played on instances
39:                                           (G, w), where G is a graph and w : V (G) → R⩾0 are non-negative vertex weights,
40:                                           and one asks whether Alice or Bob can build the heavier subgraph, that is, one
41:                                           with larger total weight. In this setting, it makes sense to allow either player to
42:                                           continue building his subgraph even if the other player already has no available
43:                                           move any more. (Note that a player might win even by claiming significantly
44:                                           fewer vertices than the opponent.) For convenience, instances are normalized (by
45:                                           scaling
46:                                           P        the weights) so that the total sum of weights in the graph equals 1, i.e.,
47:                                              v∈V (G) w(v) = 1. The outcome of a game is the difference between the final
48:                                           weight of Bob’s subgraph and Alice’s subgraph. So Bob tries to maximize the

91: Related work. A central question in combinatorial game theory is to determine
92: the computational complexity of deciding which player has a winning strategy, i.e.,
93: whether for a given instance (G, w) we have ∆(G, w) ⩽ 0 or ∆(G, w) ⩾ 0. For Tron,
94: Bodlaender and Kloks [2] present a polynomial time algorithm when the graph is
95: a tree, whereas Miltzow [3] proves PSPACE-hardness for general graphs.
96:    Related path building games include Geography where both players build the
97: same path, always extending at the same end. This variant, introduced by Fraenkel
98: and Simonson in 1993 [4], resembles the children’s game in which two players al-
99: ternatingly have to find a country whose first letter matches the last letter of the
100: previously country and that has not been taken yet. For a summary of such path
101: building games and their computational complexity we refer to the survey of Bod-
102: laender [5].
103:    In 2008 Peter Winkler stated the so-called Pizza Game in which the given graph
104: is a vertex weighted cycle and both players build the same path which in each step
105: may be extended on either end. Again, if all the weight lies on one vertex, this
106: is arbitrarily bad for Bob. But Winkler asked whether Alice can always ensure

96: 1    Definitions and notations
97: Graphical models (Lauritzen 1996; Pearl 1988) are a class of statistical models which
98: combine the rigour of a probabilistic approach with the intuitive representation of re-
99: 
100: Marco Scutari                                                                            3
101: 
102: lationships given by graphs. They are composed by a set X “ tX1 , . . . , Xn u of random
103: variables describing the data D and a graph G “ pV, Eq in which each vertex or node
104: v P V is associated with one of the random variables in X. Nodes and the corresponding
105: variables are usually referred to interchangeably. The edges e P E are used to express
106: the dependence relationships among the variables in X. Different classes of graphs ex-
107: press these relationships with different semantics, having in common the principle that
108: graphical separation of two vertices implies the conditional independence of the corre-
109: sponding random variables (Pearl 1988). The two examples most commonly found in
110: literature are Markov networks (Whittaker 1990; Edwards 2000), which use undirected
111: graphs (UGs, see Diestel 2005), and Bayesian networks (Neapolitan 2003; Korb and
112: Nicholson 2010), which use directed acyclic graphs (DAGs, see Bang-Jensen and Gutin
113: 2009). In the context of Bayesian networks, edges are often called arcs and denoted
114: with a P A; we will adopt this notation as well.
115:     The structure of G (that is, the pattern of the nodes and the edges) determines
116: the probabilistic properties of a graphical model. The most important, and the most
117: used, is the factorisation of the global distribution (the joint distribution of X) into a
118: set of lower-dimensional local distributions. In Markov networks, local distributions are
119: associated with cliques (maximal subsets of nodes in which each element is adjacent
120: to all the others); in Bayesian networks, each local distribution is associated with one
121: node conditional on its parents (nodes linked by an incoming arc). In Markov networks
122: the factorisation is unique; different graph structures correspond to different probability
123: distributions. This is not so in Bayesian networks, where DAGs can be grouped into
124: equivalence classes which are statistically indistinguishable. Each such class is uniquely
125: identified by the underlying UG (i.e. in which arc directions are disregarded, also
126: known as skeleton) and by the set of v-structures (i.e. converging connections of the
127: form vi Ñ vj Ð vk , i ‰ j ‰ k, in which vi and vk are not connected by an arc) common
128: to all elements of the class.
129:    As for the global and the local distributions, there are many possible choices depend-
130: ing on the nature of the data and the aims of the analysis. However, literature have
131: focused mostly on two cases: the discrete case (Whittaker 1990; Heckerman et al. 1995),
132: in which both the global and the local distributions are multinomial random variables,
133: and the continuous case (Whittaker 1990; Geiger and Heckerman 1994), in which the
134: global distribution is multivariate normal and the local distributions are univariate (in
135: Bayesian networks) or multivariate (in Markov networks) normal random variables. In
136: the former, the parameters of interest Θ are the conditional probabilities associated with
137: each variable, usually represented as conditional probability tables. In the latter, the
138: parameters of interest Θ are the partial correlation coefficients between each variable
139: and its neighbours in G. Conjugate distributions (Dirichlet and Wishart, respectively)
140: are then used for learning and inference in a Bayesian setting.
