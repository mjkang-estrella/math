   A composition of n ≥ 1 is a tuple (x1 , . . . , xm ) of positive integers such that m ≥ 1 and
x1 +. . .+xm = n. We denote by Fpn the set of compositions of n such that for all i, 1 ≤ xi ≤ p.
Fpn is enumerated by the p-generalized Fibonacci sequence (Fnp )n≥0 , defined for every p ≥ 2
by
                                         p        p              p
                                Fnp = Fn−1  + Fn−2    + . . . + Fn−p
with initial conditions Fnp = 0 if n < 0 and F0p = 1 (see [10]). The dominance order on the
set of compositions of n is defined for two compositions x = (x1 , . . . , xm ) and y = (y1 , . . . , yℓ )
by
                                                                 X k      X k
                   x ≤ y ⇐⇒ for all 1 ≤ k ≤ min(m, ℓ),               xi ≤     yi ,
                                                                   i=1        i=1
see [9]. This order has mainly been studied on integer partitions, particularly for its connec-
tion to the representations of the symmetric group, see for instance [6, 16, 17]. Sapounakis,
Tasoulas and Tsikouras also investigated the dominance order on Dyck paths [15]. It is
proved in [2] that the poset Fpn equipped with the dominance order forms a distributive lat-
tice, i.e. each pair of compositions x, y ∈ Fpn admits a meet (greatest lower bound) x ∧ y, and
a join (least upper bound) x ∨ y, and each of the two operations distributes over the other.
If x, y ∈ Fpn , we say that y covers x if x < y and for all z ∈ Fpn , x ≤ z ≤ y ⇒ z ∈ {x, y}. We
say equivalently that y is an upper cover of x, or x is a lower cover of y. An element x ∈ Fpn
is meet-irreducible if for all y, z ∈ Fpn , x = y ∧ z ⇒ y = x or z = x. Dually, an element
x ∈ Fpn is join-irreducible if for all y, z ∈ Fpn , x = y ∨ z ⇒ y = x or z = x. Since Fpn is a finite
lattice, x is meet-irreducible (resp. join-irreducible) if and only if x has exactly one upper
cover (resp. lower cover). See for instance [18] for the standard definitions of lattice theory.
Let mipn (resp. jipn ) be the set of meet-irreducible (resp. join-irreducible) elements in Fpn . See
Figure 2 for an example of Fpn and mipn with (n, p) = (5, 3).
   Other posets on integer compositions are studied in the literature, for instance the sub-
word order [3, 14, 8, 4]. However much less can be found on posets on restricted sets of
compositions. In this respect, the study of Fpn is natural, since its elements are enumerated
by the famous p-generalized Fibonacci sequence, and it turns out that it presents fascinating
enumerative properties. Indeed, in [2] the authors gave the enumeration of many character-
istic elements in Fpn , and they proved in particular that mipn is surprisingly enumerated by
ap (n). As a consequence of Birkhoff’s representation theorem, since Fpn is a finite distributive
lattice, |jipn | = |mipn | = ap (n). The unexpected appearance of those numbers, well-known in
extremal graph theory, in a context of order theory, motivates the study of the links between
the corresponding objects in those two fields. In Section 2, we build an explicit bijection
between E(Tnp ) and mipn , and then we show how this bijection can be adapted to jipn . In
Section 3 we use this bijection to compute asymptotically the average value of three statistics
on mipn .

Lemma 2.1. The number of upper covers of a composition x ∈ Fpn is the number of occur-
rences ab in x with 1 ≤ a ≤ p − 1 and 2 ≤ b ≤ p, plus one if x has suffix i1 for 1 ≤ i ≤ p − 1.

Proof. Suppose that x ∈ Fpn satisfies x = (x1 , . . . , xj , a, b, xj+1 , . . . , xm ) with 1 ≤ a ≤ p − 1
and 2 ≤ b ≤ p. Then z := (x1 , . . . , xj , a + 1, b − 1, xj+1 , . . . , xm ) is an upper cover of x,
and we say that z has the form (⋆). Similarly, if x = (x1 , . . . , xm , i, 1) with 1 ≤ i ≤ p − 1,
then z := (x1 . . . , xm , i + 1) is an upper cover of x, and we say that z has the form (⋆⋆).
Conversely, suppose x = (x1 , . . . , xm ) ∈ Fpn , and let y = (y1 , . . . , yℓ ) ∈ Fpn be such that x < y.
To end the proof it suffices to prove that there exists z ∈ Fpn with the form (⋆) or (⋆⋆) such
that x < z ≤ y. Let j be the smallest integer such that xj < yj . Then we have xj < p.
Case 1: Suppose that xj+1 > 1. Then we set zj = xj + 1 and zj+1 = xj+1 − 1, and zi = xi
for i ̸∈ {j, j + 1}. Then z has the form (⋆), and x < z ≤ y.
Case 2: Suppose that xj+1 = xj+2 = . . . = xm = 1. Then we set zi = xi if i ≤ m − 2,
and zm−1 = xm−1 + 1. Note that we may have m = j + 1. Then z has the form (⋆⋆), and
x < z ≤ y.
Case 3: Suppose that xj+1 = 1 and there exists j + 1 < k ≤ m such that xk > 1. We
consider the smallest such k, and we set zk−1 = xk−1 + 1 and zk = xk − 1, and zi = xi for
i ̸∈ {k − 1, k}. Then z has the form (⋆), and x < z ≤ y.
    These three cases prove the converse and hence the lemma.                          □

number of occurrences ab in x with 2 ≤ a ≤ p and 1 ≤ b ≤ p − 1, plus one if x does not end
in 1.
