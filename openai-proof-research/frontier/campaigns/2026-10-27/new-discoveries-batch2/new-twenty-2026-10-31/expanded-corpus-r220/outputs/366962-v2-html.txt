Abstract
Quantum error correction is vital for fault-tolerant quantum computation, with deep connections to entanglement, magic, and uncertainty relations. Entanglement, for instance, has driven key advances like surface codes and has deepened our understanding of quantum gravity through holographic quantum codes. While these connections are well-explored, the role of contextuality, a fundamental nonclassical feature of quantum theory, remains unexplored. Notably, Bell nonlocality is a special case of contextuality, and prior works have established contextuality as a key resource for quantum computational advantage.
In this work, we establish the first direct link between contextuality and quantum error-correcting codes. Using a sheaf-theoretic framework, we define contextuality for such codes and prove key results on its manifestation. Specifically, we prove the equivalence of definitions of contextuality from Abramsky–Brandenburger’s sheaf-theoretic framework and Kirby–Love’s tree-based approach for the partial closure of Pauli measurement sets. We present several findings, including the proof of a recent conjecture posed by Kim and Abramsky. Working within the partial closure, we further show that subsystem stabilizer codes with two or more gauge qubits are strongly contextual, while others are noncontextual. As a consequence, we highlight that several protocols for code-switching between stabilizer codes, which admit a universal transversal gate set, are strongly contextual. Our findings reveal a direct connection between contextuality and quantum error correction, offering new insights into the nonclassical resources enabling fault-tolerant quantum computation.
I Introduction
Quantum error correction plays a crucial role in realizing fault-tolerant quantum computation by protecting fragile quantum states from noise [shor1995scheme, gottesman1997stabilizer, dennis2002topological, kitaev2003fault, lidar2013quantum, terhal2015quantum]. This field has seen remarkable progress [ryan2021realization, krinner2022realizing, sivak2023real, google2023suppressing, bluvstein2024logical, gupta2024encoding, da2024demonstration, acharya2024quantum], driven by the understanding of its deep interplay with other fundamental aspects of quantum theory, such as entanglement [bennett1996mixed, brun2006correcting, dur2007entanglement, bravyi2024much], magic [bravyi2005universal, veitch2014resource, gidney2024magic], and uncertainty relations [steane1996error, renes2016uncertainty, coles2017entropic]. Quantum error correction and entanglement theory, in particular, are two active and intimately related areas of quantum research [lidar2013quantum].
The interplay between quantum error correction and entanglement theory has led to significant advancements [kitaev2003fault, anshu2023nlts, bravyi2024much]. Many-body entangled states with topological quantum order [wen1990ground] have inspired the development of fault-tolerant architectures, such as the surface code [kitaev2003fault, dennis2002topological]. At the same time, concepts from quantum error correction have advanced our understanding of entanglement in many-body quantum systems [freedman2013quantum, anshu2023nlts], as well as the intricate role of entanglement in quantum gravity [pastawski2015holographic, almheiri2015bulk].
Beyond entanglement, other nonclassical features of quantum theory, such as magic [bravyi2005universal, veitch2014resource, gidney2024magic], also play an essential role in quantum error correction. Magic is intimately connected to the Eastin–Knill theorem [eastin2009restrictions], which states that no quantum error-correcting code can transversely implement a universal gate set. Overcoming this limitation has led to the development of magic state distillation [bravyi2005universal], a cornerstone of fault-tolerant quantum computation. Interestingly, magic has also been linked to quantum contextuality [howard2014contextuality], a fundamental feature of quantum mechanics thought to underlie the power of quantum computation [Koc, Specker1960-bg, budroni2022kochen, klyachko2008simple, howard2014contextuality, raussendorf2013contextuality]. Specifically, it has been shown that violating a noncontextuality inequality, a generalization of Bell inequalities, is necessary for achieving a quantum computational speedup through magic state distillation [howard2014contextuality]. Moreover, contextuality, which generalizes the concept of commutation and captures the nonclassical behavior of quantum systems [lapkiewicz2011experimental, um2013experimental, jerger2016contextuality, zhan2017experimental, malinowski2018probing, leupold2018sustained, zhang2019experimental, um2020randomness, wang2022significant, hu2023self, liu2023experimental, bharti2019robust, bharti2019local, xu2024certifying, saha2020sum, budroni2022kochen, wang2022significant, hu2023self, liu2023experimental, liu2023exploring, xu2024certifying, arora2024computational], has indirect ties to error correction via uncertainty relations [steane1996error, renes2016uncertainty, sun2022disturbance, coles2017entropic]. Bell nonlocality [bell1964einstein, brunner2014bell], a special case of contextuality [abramsky2011sheaf, budroni2022kochen, arora2024computational], can be used to witness entanglement in quantum systems [brunner2014bell], reinforcing the profound interplay between these concepts.
Despite several indirect connections, a direct link between quantum error correction and contextuality has not yet been established.
In this work, we take the first step in addressing this gap by studying the contextuality of quantum error-correcting codes. We formulate the definition of contextuality as applied to quantum error-correcting codes and prove several results on the contextuality of these codes. We also establish, under certain conditions, the equivalence of different definitions of quantum contextuality, including the sheaf-theoretic ones (both probabilistic and strong) introduced by Abramsky–Brandenburger in [abramsky2011sheaf] and the tree-based one introduced by Kirby–Love in [kirby2019contextuality]. In doing so, we resolve a recent conjecture posed by Kim and Abramsky in [kim2023stateindependentallversusnothingarguments]. A brief informal overview of the main definitions and results is given below.
For any set  $S$  of quantum measurements, we may informally define the partial closure  $\bar{S}$  of  $S$  to be the set of all quantum measurements whose outcomes can be determined based on an assignment of outcomes to each measurement in  $S$ . In other words, if we iteratively enlarge the set  $S$  by including products of commuting measurements in  $S$ , we eventually end up with the partial closure  $\bar{S}$  of  $S$  (c.f., Remark ). (See Definition  in section V for a formal definition.)
We show that for the partial closure  $\bar{X}$  of any set  $X$  of Pauli measurements (e.g., of a quantum error-correcting code), Abramsky--Brandenburger’s definitions of probabilistic and strong contextuality11

This allows us to say that a quantum error-correcting code is either noncontextual (even) in its partial closure or both probabilistically and strongly contextual in its partial closure. We also give a characterization of the subsystem qubit stabilizer codes which are contextual in a partial closure. More precisely, any subsystem qubit stabilizer code with at least two gauge qubits is (strongly) contextual in its partial closure; otherwise, it is noncontextual (Corollary 1).
We then apply this result to the code-switching protocol between the  $\llbracket 7,1,3\rrbracket$  color code and the  $\llbracket 15,1,3\rrbracket$  color code from [butt2024fault], which admits a universal transversal gateset, and show that this protocol is strongly contextual in a partial closure when viewed through the lens of the subsystem code formalism (Corollary ). We use the same reasoning for two other code-switching protocols (Remark  and Remark ), both also with universal transversal gate sets, to show that they exhibit strong contextuality in a partial closure as well.
I.1 Comparison with the Prior Art
In this section, we situate our work within the broader landscape of research on quantum nonclassicality, highlighting both foundational results and recent developments related to the contextuality inherent in quantum error-correcting codes.
Foundational studies on the nonclassical features of quantum mechanics—such as Bell’s seminal work [bell1964einstein] and the Kochen–Specker theorem [Koc]—established that no local hidden-variable model can fully account for quantum predictions. The Greenberger–Horne–Zeilinger (GHZ) argument [greenberger1989going, greenberger1990bell], later refined by Mermin [mermin1990extreme], demonstrated that certain quantum states give rise to “all-or-nothing” (AvN) contradictions with local realism. Furthermore, investigations like those in [divincenzo1997quantum, cabello2008mermin] leveraged the structure of quantum codes and multipartite correlations to reveal nonlocal features.
Building on these early insights, subsequent work has advanced abstract frameworks to formalize contextuality. For example, the sheaf-theoretic approach introduced in [abramsky2011sheaf] and extended via cohomological methods in [abramsky2015contextuality] offers a unifying language in which the failure of local sections to glue into a global assignment signals contextuality. Additionally, [abramsky2017complete] provides a detailed account of AvN contradictions arising from stabilizer states, while [choudhary2025contextuality] examines cyclic arrangements of observables to establish sufficient conditions for contextuality in these settings. Collectively, these works illustrate how the algebraic structure of quantum observables can lead to irreconcilable contradictions under any classical assignment.
Contextuality has also emerged as a resource for quantum computation. Studies such as [howard2014contextuality] highlight its role in enabling quantum speedup, and application-oriented research has demonstrated its presence in variational quantum algorithms [kirby2021contextual, kirby2019contextuality]. Moreover, recent work has explored contextuality in topological settings [hart2024playing, hart2024braiding] and its connection with classical coding theory [waegell2022golay].
Our work fills an important gap in the literature by extending these established frameworks of contextuality to the realm of quantum error-correcting codes. In contrast to previous studies that have primarily focused on stabilizer states to demonstrate nonlocality and AvN contradictions [greenberger1989going, greenberger1990bell, mermin1990extreme, divincenzo1997quantum, cabello2008mermin], our approach systematically applies the sheaf-theoretic framework—and complementary graph- and tree-based methods—directly to the check measurements defining these codes. This novel perspective enables us to rigorously characterize the conditions under which subsystem stabilizer codes exhibit strong contextuality, a property intimately linked to their ability to protect quantum information against errors.

Next, we recall three notions of contextuality: a sheaf-theoretic formulation of contextuality in section II.2, a graph-based contextuality in section II.3, and the all-versus-nothing argument in section II.4.
In section III we begin by discussing what it means for a quantum error-correcting code to exhibit contextuality in an operational sense and give a precise definition based on sheaf-theoretic contextuality.
We then present a characterization of whether a subsystem qubit stabilizer code is contextual by using a graph-based contextuality characterization and argue for its equivalence to the sheaf-theoretic characterization.
This equivalence is proven later in Corollary  in section V using the relationships between the Kirby–Love property (graph-based contextuality) and the sheaf-theoretic contextuality of an empirical model (see Theorems 4 and 5) established in section IV.
In section V, we present the rest of the argument used to prove the equivalences in Corollary , in which we use other notions of contextuality in the literature to obtain a chain of relationships between different notions of contextuality that all turn out to be equivalent under partial closure. Lastly in section , we discuss an application of our result on the strong contextuality of subsystem codes (Corollary 1) to three code-switching protocols, each admitting a universal transversal gate set, which we argue to be strongly contextual in a partial closure.
This includes the code-switching protocol between the  $\llbracket 7,1,3\rrbracket$  color code and  $\llbracket 15,1,3\rrbracket$  color code described in [butt2024fault], which we discuss in detail.
II Preliminaries
Throughout this paper we focus on codes in the stabilizer formalism.
We denote the  $n$ -qubit Pauli group by  $G_{n}=\langle I,X,Y,Z\rangle^{\otimes n}$ , where  $\langle a,b,c,\dots\rangle$  denotes the group generated by group elements  $a,b,c,\dots$ , and write  $X_{i}$  (resp.  $Y_{i},Z_{i}$ ) for the  $n$ -qubit Pauli operator that is the tensor product of  $X$  (resp.  $Y,Z$ ) in the  $i$ th component and  $I$  in all other components. (All tensor products are taken over  ${\mathbb{C}}$ .)
Also we let  $\mathcal{P}_{n}\subseteq G_{n}$  be the set of all  $n$ -qubit Pauli operators with real global phase, i.e.,  $\mathcal{P}_{n}=\{\pm 1\}\times\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}$ , for convenience when working with stabilizer codes.
Finally, we denote the weight of  $n$ -qubit Pauli operator  $p\in G_{n}$  by  ${\textrm{wt}\,}p$ , which is the number of nonidentity terms among the tensor factors of  $p$ .
For example,  $p=I\otimes Z\otimes X=Z_{2}X_{3}$  has a weight of  ${\textrm{wt}\,}p=2$ .
II.1 Quantum Error-Correcting Codes
In general, a quantum error-correcting code is defined by a subspace  $\mathcal{Q}$  of a quantum system described by a  $d$ -dimensional complex vector space  ${\mathbb{C}}^{d}$ .
A quantum state  $|\psi\rangle$  that belongs to  $\mathcal{Q}$  can be recovered after being inflicted by an error  $E$  correctable by  $\mathcal{Q}$ .
This is generally done by first performing a set of check measurements  $\mathcal{C}$  on the erroneous state  $E|\psi\rangle$  which gives a set of error syndromes  $o=\{o_{1},\dots,o_{s}\}$ , followed by a correcting operation  $T_{o}$  depending on the error syndromes to recover the initial quantum state as  $T_{o}E|\psi\rangle\propto|\psi\rangle$ .
An important and perhaps most studied family of quantum error-correcting codes is the stabilizer codes [gottesman1997stabilizer, gottesman2016surviving].
A stabilizer code  $\mathcal{Q}$  is defined by an abelian subgroup  $\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{n}$  of the Pauli group  $G_{n}$ , which allows convenient group-theoretic analysis for its properties mainly due to its check measurements  $\mathcal{C}$  being those that generate the group  $\mathcal{S}$ .
In this work, we focus on quantum error-correcting codes within the formalism of stabilizer codes.
Below, we formally define a stabilizer code.
Definition 1.
Let  $\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{n}$  be an abelian subgroup of  $n$ -qubit Pauli group  $G_{n}$ , with  $-I\not\in\mathcal{S}$ .
The stabilizer code  $\mathcal{Q}$  fixed by  $\mathcal{S}$  is a subspace of  ${\mathbb{C}}^{2^{n}}$  defined by
 $\mathcal{Q}=\textrm{span}\big\{|\psi\rangle:\forall p\in\mathcal{S},\,p|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle\big\}\;.$ 
(1)
In words,  $\mathcal{Q}$  consists of quantum states in the  $+1$  eigenspace of all  $p\in\mathcal{S}$ , where  $\mathcal{S}$  is known as a stabilizer group and an element  $s$  of the stabilizer group  $\mathcal{S}$  is known as a stabilizer of  $\mathcal{Q}$ .
A set of check measurements  $\mathcal{C}$  of a stabilizer code  $\mathcal{Q}$  can be taken as the set of independent generators  $p_{1},\dots,p_{s}$  of stabilizer group  $\mathcal{S}$ .
An  $n$ -qubit stabilizer code  $\mathcal{Q}$  is an  $\llbracket n,k,d\rrbracket$  code whenever22

Stabilizer codes belong to the family of subspace quantum error-correcting codes where the entire subspace  $\mathcal{Q}$  is being used to store logical information.
A more general family of codes called the subsystem codes [kribs2005operator, kribs2005unified] offers more flexibility where the subspace  $\mathcal{Q}$  of  $d$ -dimensional complex vector space  ${\mathbb{C}}^{d}$  is further partitioned into two subsystems: (1) a code system  $\mathcal{A}$  where logical quantum information is encoded in and (2) a gauge system  $\mathcal{B}$ .

When the subsystem  $\mathcal{B}$  is one-dimensional, this gives us a subspace quantum error-correcting code.
In this work we focus on a class of subsystem codes, the stabilizer subsystem codes [poulin2005stabilizer] which uses the stabilizer formalism to construct a subsystem code.
As opposed to the stabilizer code (Definition 1) which is defined only by the abelian stabilizer group  $\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{n}$ , a subsystem stabilizer code is defined by both an abelian stabilizer group  $\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{n}$  and a gauge group  $\mathcal{G}\subseteq G_{n}$  (which is not necessarily abelian) such that  $Z(\mathcal{G})=\langle\mathcal{S},iI\rangle$ , where  $Z(\mathcal{G})$  is the center of  $\mathcal{G}$ . From  $\mathcal{S}$  and  $\mathcal{G}$ , we can define the corresponding codespace  $\mathcal{Q}$  fixed by  $\mathcal{S}$  and a corresponding decomposition  $\mathcal{Q}\cong\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}$  such that, under this isomorphism, each  $g\in\mathcal{G}$  acts as  $I_{\mathcal{A}}\otimes g_{\mathcal{B}}$ , where  $I_{\mathcal{A}}$  is the identity operator on  $\mathcal{A}$  and  $g_{\mathcal{B}}$  is some operator on  $\mathcal{B}$ .
A subsystem stabilizer code is formally defined as follows.

The associated subsystem qubit stabilizer code  $\mathcal{Q}\cong\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}$  is a subspace of  ${\mathbb{C}}^{2^{n}}$  defined by
 $\mathcal{Q}=\textrm{span}\big\{|\varphi\rangle:\forall p\in\mathcal{S},\,p|\varphi\rangle=|\varphi\rangle\big\}\;,$

along with the data of  $\mathcal{A}$  and  $\mathcal{B}$  as described in [poulin2005stabilizer].
The check measurements  $\mathcal{C}$  of subsystem stabilizer code  $\mathcal{Q}$  form a minimal set of generators  $\{p_{1},\dots,p_{l}\}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{n}$  that, together with  $iI$ , generate  $\mathcal{G}$ .
The subsystem stabilizer code  $\mathcal{Q}$  fixed by  $\mathcal{G}$  is an  $\llbracket n,k,g,d\rrbracket$  code whenever  $g=(\log|\mathcal{G}/\langle\mathcal{S},iI\rangle|)/2$  and  $k=n-g-\log|\mathcal{S}|$  and  $d=\min_{p\in N(\mathcal{S})\setminus\mathcal{G}}{\textrm{wt}\,}p$ .
As shown in [poulin2005stabilizer], given a subsystem qubit stabilizer code, one can construct  $n$  pairs of “virtual” Pauli  $X$  and  $Z$  operators  $\{X_{j}^{\prime},Z_{j}^{\prime}\}_{1\leq j\leq n}$  from  $G_{n}$  satisfying:
1. 
 $[X_{j}^{\prime},X_{k}^{\prime}]=[Z_{j}^{\prime},Z_{k}^{\prime}]=[X_{j}^{\prime},Z_{k}^{\prime}]=0$  for all distinct  $j,k\in\{1,\cdots,n\}$ ,
2. 
 $X_{j}^{\prime}Z_{j}^{\prime}=-Z_{j}^{\prime}X_{j}^{\prime}$  for all  $j\in\{1,\cdots,n\}$ , and
3. 
 $(X_{j}^{\prime})^{2}=(Z_{j}^{\prime})^{2}=I$  for all  $j\in\{1,\cdots,n\}$ .
The stabilizer group  $\mathcal{S}$  is given by  $\mathcal{S}=\langle Z_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,Z_{s}^{\prime}\rangle$  for some  $s\geq 0$ , and its normalizer in  $G_{n}$  is  $N(\mathcal{S})=\langle iI,Z_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,Z_{n}^{\prime},X_{s+1}^{\prime},\cdots,X_{n}^{\prime}\rangle$ . We can now define the set of check measurements to be  $\mathcal{C}=\{Z_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,Z_{s}^{\prime},X_{s+1}^{\prime},Z_{s+1}^{\prime},\cdots,X_{s+g}^{\prime},Z_{s+g}^{\prime}\}$  for some  $g\geq 0$  with  $s+g\leq n$ . Together with  $iI$ , these generate the gauge group  $\mathcal{G}=\langle\mathcal{C},iI\rangle$ .
II.2 Sheaf-Theoretic Contextuality Framework
We recall the sheaf-theoretic definition of contextuality from [abramsky2011sheaf, abramsky2015contextuality, kim2023stateindependentallversusnothingarguments].
Definition 3.
A quantum measurement scenario (also known as a measurement scenario with a quantum representation in [abramsky2011sheaf]), is a triple  $\langle X,\mathcal{M},O\rangle$ , where  $X$  is a nonempty, finite set of observables on some Hilbert space,  $\mathcal{M}$  is the set of maximally commuting subsets of  $X$ , and  $O$  is a nonempty, finite set of outcomes for the measurement of each observable. The set  $\mathcal{M}$  is also known as the measurement cover, and each element in  $\mathcal{M}$  is a measurement context.
Definition 4.
For a quantum measurement scenario  $\langle X,\mathcal{M},O\rangle$ , we define the event sheaf  $\mathcal{E}$  on  $X$  (as a discrete topological space) by taking the sections to be  $\mathcal{E}(U)=O^{U}$  for all  $U\subseteq X$  and the restriction maps to be  $s\mapsto s|_{U}$  for all  $U\subseteq U^{\prime}\subseteq X$  and  $s\in\mathcal{E}(U^{\prime})$ .  $d\mapsto\left(y\mapsto\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(\{y\})}d(x)\right).$ 
A (no-signalling)  $R$ -valued empirical model on  $\langle X,\mathcal{M},O\rangle$  is defined to be a family  $e=\{e_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{M}}$  with  $e_{C}\in\mathcal{D}_{R}\mathcal{E}(C)$  such that  $e_{C}|_{C\cap C^{\prime}}=e_{C^{\prime}}|_{C\cap C^{\prime}}$  for all  $C,C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}$ . A possibilistic empirical model on  $\langle X,\mathcal{M},O\rangle$  is defined to be a subpresheaf  $\mathcal{S}$  of  $\mathcal{E}$  satisfying the following properties:
(E1) 
 $\mathcal{S}(C)\neq\emptyset$  for all  $C\in\mathcal{M}$ ,
(E2) 
 $\mathcal{S}(U^{\prime})\to\mathcal{S}(U)$  is surjective for all  $C\in\mathcal{M}$  and  $U\subseteq U^{\prime}\subseteq C$ ,
(E3) 
for any family  $\{s_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{M}}$  with  $s_{C}\in\mathcal{S}(C)$  satisfying  $s_{C}|_{C\cap C^{\prime}}=s_{C^{\prime}}|_{C\cap C^{\prime}}$  for all  $C,C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}$ , there is a global section  $s\in\mathcal{S}(X)$  satisfying  $s|_{C}=s_{C}$  for all  $C\in\mathcal{M}$ . (This section is unique because  $\mathcal{E}$  and hence  $\mathcal{S}$  are separated.)
In the case where the semiring  $R$  is the two-element boolean semiring  ${\mathbb{B}}=\{0,1\}$  with  $1+1=1$ , there is a bijective correspondence between  ${\mathbb{B}}$ -valued empirical models and possibilistic empirical models. Given a  ${\mathbb{B}}$ -valued empirical model  $\{e_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{M}}$ , we obtain a possibilistic empirical model  $\mathcal{S}$  defined by setting, for each  $U\subseteq X$ ,
 $\mathcal{S}(U)\coloneqq\{s\in O^{U}\mid e_{C}|_{U\cap C}(s|_{U\cap C})=1\text{ for all }C\in\mathcal{M}\}.$ 
Conversely, given a possibilistic empirical model  $\mathcal{S}$ , the family  $\{\chi_{\mathcal{S}(C)}\}_{C\in\mathcal{M}}$  forms a  ${\mathbb{B}}$ -valued empirical model, where we use  $\chi_{A}$  to denote the indicator function of the set  $A$ . These two operations are inverse to each other.
Given a unital additive map  $f\colon R\to S$  between semirings  $R$  and  $S$  (i.e.,  $f$  satisfies  $f(1)=1$  and  $f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)$  for all  $x,y\in R$ ), there is a natural transformation of functors from  $\mathcal{D}_{R}$  to  $\mathcal{D}_{S}$  given by sending each  $d\in\mathcal{D}_{R}(X)$  to  $f\circ d\in\mathcal{D}_{S}(X)$ , and thus any  $R$ -valued empirical model  $\{e_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{M}}$  gives rise to an  $S$ -valued empirical model  $\{f\circ e_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{M}}$ .
For any semiring  $R$ , we can define its support map  $f\colon R\to{\mathbb{B}}$  by setting  $f(r)=0$  if and only if  $r=0$ . This map is unital and additive if and only if  $R$  is a nonzero zerosumfree semiring (i.e., a nonzero semiring in which the sum of any two nonzero elements is nonzero). In this case, we get a  ${\mathbb{B}}$ -valued empirical model, or equivalently a possibilistic empirical model, associated to any  $R$ -valued empirical model in the manner described above. We formalize this in the definition below.
Definition 5.
Let  $R$  be a nonzero zerosumfree semiring, and  $f\colon R\to{\mathbb{B}}$  be its support map. The possibilistic empirical model  $\mathcal{S}$  associated to an  $R$ -valued empirical model  $e=\{e_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{M}}$  on  $\langle X,\mathcal{M},O\rangle$  is the one corresponding to (under the bijective correspondence described earlier) the  ${\mathbb{B}}$ -valued empirical model  $\{f\circ e_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{M}}$ . Equivalently, it is defined by setting, for each  $U\subseteq X$ ,
 $\mathcal{S}(U)\coloneqq\{s\in O^{U}\mid s|_{U\cap C}\in\mathrm{supp}(e_{C}|_{U\cap C})\text{ for all }C\in\mathcal{M}\}.$ 
An example of a nonzero zerosumfree semiring is  ${\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}$ . In this case,  ${\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}$ -distributions on a finite set  $U$  are precisely probability distributions on  $U$ .
Definition 6.
Given a quantum measurement scenario  $\langle X,\mathcal{M},O\rangle$  on a Hilbert space  $\mathcal{H}$  as well as a quantum state  $\rho$  on  $\mathcal{H}$ , the empirical model defined by  $\rho$  is the  ${\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}$ -valued empirical model  $e$  on  $\langle X,\mathcal{M},O\rangle$  obtained by setting each  $e_{C}$  to be the probability distribution on  $O^{C}$  of the measurement outcomes from making the measurements in  $C$  on the state  $\rho$ . The possibilistic empirical model defined by  $\rho$  is the possibilistic empirical model associated to the empirical model defined by  $\rho$ .
Definition 7.
We say a possibilistic empirical model  $\mathcal{S}$  on a quantum measurement scenario  $\langle X,\mathcal{M},O\rangle$  is strongly contextual if it has no global section, i.e., if  $\mathcal{S}(X)=\emptyset$ . We say an  $R$ -valued empirical model  $e=\{e_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{M}}$  on  $\langle X,\mathcal{M},O\rangle$  is contextual if there is no global section  $d\in\mathcal{D}_{R}\mathcal{E}(X)$  satisfying  $d|_{C}=e_{C}$  for all  $C\in\mathcal{M}$ , and strongly contextual if the possibilistic empirical model associated to  $e$  is strongly contextual. If  $e$  is not contextual, we say it is noncontextual.
Remark 1.
In [abramsky2011sheaf], contextuality of an  ${\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}$ -valued empirical model is known as probabilistic nonextendability, while contextuality of a  ${\mathbb{B}}$ -valued empirical model is known as possibilistic nonextendability. In [abramsky2015contextuality], contextuality of the  ${\mathbb{B}}$ -valued empirical model associated to a possibilistic empirical model is known as logical contextuality.
As observed in [abramsky2011sheaf, Sections 4 and 6], strongly contextuality of an  ${\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}$ -valued empirical model implies contextuality of its corresponding  ${\mathbb{B}}$ -valued empirical model, and this in turn implies contextuality of the  ${\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}$ -valued empirical model. (It was also noted that the converse to either of these implications may not be true.) More generally, we have the following.
Theorem 1.
Let  $R$  be a nonzero zerosumfree semiring. Let  $e$  be an  $R$ -valued empirical model on  $\langle X,\mathcal{M},O\rangle$ , with  $e^{\prime}$  its corresponding  ${\mathbb{B}}$ -valued empirical model. We then have the following.
1. 
If  $e$  is strongly contextual,  $e^{\prime}$  is contextual.
2. 
If  $e^{\prime}$  is contextual,  $e$  is also contextual.
Therefore,  $e$  is contextual if it is strongly contextual.
Proof.
The final statement follows from the first two.
For the first statement, suppose that  $e^{\prime}$  is not contextual. We need to show that  $e$  is not strongly contextual, i.e., the possibilistic empirical model  $\mathcal{S}$  associated to  $e$  (and equivalently, to  $e^{\prime}$ ) is not strongly contextual. Since  $e^{\prime}=\{\chi_{\mathcal{S}(C)}\}_{C\in\mathcal{M}}$  is not contextual, there is some  $d\in\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{B}}}\mathcal{E}(X)$  such that  $d|_{C}=\chi_{\mathcal{S}(C)}$  for all  $C\in\mathcal{M}$ . Since  $d\in\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{B}}}\mathcal{E}(X)$ , there is some  $s\in\mathcal{E}(X)$  for which  $d(s)=1$ . It follows that  $\chi_{\mathcal{S}(C)}(s|_{C})=d|_{C}(s|_{C})=1$  and thus  $s|_{C}\in\mathcal{S}(C)$  for all  $C\in\mathcal{M}$ . Hence,  $s\in\mathcal{S}(X)$  by condition (E3) and separatedness of  $\mathcal{S}$ , proving that  $\mathcal{S}(X)\neq\emptyset$ , as desired.
The second statement follows immediately from the definition of contextuality and the fact that the map  $\mathcal{D}_{R}\to\mathcal{D}_{B}$  inducing the map from  $e$  to  $e^{\prime}$  is a natural transformation. More precisely, if  $f:R\to{\mathbb{B}}$  is the support map and  $e=\{e_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{M}}$  is such that there is some  $d\in\mathcal{D}_{R}\mathcal{E}(X)$  such that  $d|_{C}=e_{C}$  for all  $C\in\mathcal{M}$  (i.e.,  $e$  is noncontextual), then  $f\circ d\in\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{B}}}\mathcal{E}(X)$  satisfies  $(f\circ d)|_{C}=f\circ e_{C}$  for all  $C\in\mathcal{M}$  (so  $e^{\prime}$  is also noncontextual).
∎

II.4 AvN property of empirical models
In [abramsky2015contextuality], the authors pointed out that strong contextuality can be established for a large family of quantum states using “all-versus-nothing” arguments like the one introduced in [mermin1990extreme], and proposed the following definitions to capture the essence of such arguments.
Definition 10.
Let  $\mathcal{S}$  be a possibilistic empirical model on a quantum measurement scenario  $\langle X,\mathcal{M},R\rangle$  (with event sheaf  $\mathcal{E}$ ), where  $R$  is a ring.
An  $R$ -linear equation is a triple  $\phi=\langle C,a,b\rangle$  with  $C\in\mathcal{M}$ ,  $a\colon C\to R$  (representing the coefficients) and  $b\in R$  (representing the constant), and we write  $V_{\phi}\coloneqq C$ . For each  $C\in\mathcal{M}$ , we say a section  $s\in\mathcal{E}(C)$  satisfies  $\phi=\langle C,a,b\rangle$ , written  $s\models\phi$ , if
 $\sum_{m\in C}a(m)s(m)=b.$ 
The  $R$ -linear theory of  $\mathcal{S}$  is the set  $\mathbb{T}_{R}(\mathcal{S})$  of all  $R$ -linear equations  $\phi$  such that every  $s\in\mathcal{S}(V_{\phi})$  satisfies  $\phi$ . We say a section  $s\in\mathcal{S}(X)$  satisfies  $\mathbb{T}_{R}(\mathcal{S})$  if  $s|_{V_{\phi}}$  satisfies  $\phi$  for all  $\phi\in\mathbb{T}_{R}(\mathcal{S})$ , and say  $\mathcal{S}$  is AvN if  $\mathbb{T}_{R}(\mathcal{S})$  is inconsistent, i.e., there is no section  $s\in\mathcal{S}(X)$  that satisfies  $\mathbb{T}_{R}(\mathcal{S})$ . We will also say an empirical model is AvN if the associated possibilistic empirical model is AvN.
The following is proven in [abramsky2015contextuality, Proposition 7].
Fact 1.
A possibilistic empirical model on a quantum measurement scenario which is AvN is also strongly contextual.
We now specialize to the quantum measurement scenario  $\langle X,\mathcal{M},R\rangle$  where  $R={\mathbb{Z}}_{2}=\{0,1\}$  is the ring of integers modulo  $2$  and the observables  $x$  in  $X$  only have eigenvalues  $1$  and/or  $-1$  (or equivalently, satisfy  $x^{2}=I$  since  $x$  is diagonalizable), corresponding to the measurement outcomes  $0$  and  $1$  respectively. (In other words, each outcome  $b\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}$  corresponds to the eigenvalue  $(-1)^{b}$ .)
Given a multiplicative relation55

The linear theory of  $X$  is the set  $\mathbb{T}_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(X)$  of all  $\phi=\langle C,a,b\rangle$  (for  $C\in\mathcal{M}$ ,  $a\colon C\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}$ ,  $b\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}$ ) such that the following relation holds in the space of observables on the Hilbert space:
 $\prod_{x\in C}x^{a(x)}=(-1)^{b}I.$

The next result is proven in [kim2023stateindependentallversusnothingarguments] for the case where  $X$  is a subset of the set of observables in the Pauli  $n$ -group (which are self-inverse), but the same proof also applies to the more general result below, so we omit its proof.
Theorem 2.
Suppose  $\langle X,\mathcal{M},{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\rangle$  is a quantum measurement scenario, where the operators in  $X$  have eigenvalues  $1$  and/or  $-1$  corresponding to the measurement outcomes  $0$  and  $1$  respectively. If  $X$  is state-independently AvN, then any possibilistic empirical model on  $\langle X,\mathcal{M},{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\rangle$  that is defined by some quantum state is AvN.
III Contextuality of Quantum Error-Correcting Codes
The primary operations performed on a quantum system used for a quantum error-correcting code  $\mathcal{Q}$  are the check measurements  $\mathcal{C}$  whose outcomes give some information about errors to perform an appropriate correction operation.
In the case of a subspace stabilizer code, the check measurements  $\mathcal{C}$  consist of repeated measurements of the Pauli observables that generate its stabilizer group  $\mathcal{S}$  (see e.g., section IV of [dennis2002topological]).
In contrast, for a subsystem stabilizer code, the check measurements  $\mathcal{C}$  consist of measurements of the Pauli observables that generate its gauge group  $\mathcal{G}$  in a particular sequence such that the eigenvalues of its stabilizers can be inferred from the check outcomes (see e.g., [suchara2011constructions, higgott2021subsystem]).
As in other tasks in quantum information and computation, the statistics of the outcomes from the check measurements  $\mathcal{C}$  of a quantum error-correcting code is where contextuality can manifest.
Thus we see that a quantum error-correcting code exhibits contextuality whenever its corresponding check measurements  $\mathcal{C}$  exhibit contextual behaviors, as defined in the sheaf-theoretic framework of contextuality in section II.2.
In particular, we characterize the contextuality of quantum error-correcting codes in the stabilizer formalism (see section II.1) by its check measurements  $\mathcal{C}$  consisting of Pauli observables.
Our results regarding contextuality of quantum error-correcting codes in this section revolves around the partial closure of the check measurements  $\mathcal{C}$  of that code (see Definition  in section V).
We explain why considering the partial closure of check measurements is operationally justified for quantum error-correcting codes in Remark 2.
III.1 Contextuality of subsystem qubit stabilizer codes
We now discuss contextuality in the context of quantum error-correcting codes whose check measurements lie in  $\mathcal{P}_{n}$ .
Definition 12.
Let  $\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{n}$  be the set of check measurements of a quantum error-correcting code, and  $\mathcal{M}$  (resp.  $\bar{\mathcal{M}}$ ) be the set of maximally commuting subsets of  $\mathcal{C}$  (resp.  $\bar{\mathcal{C}}$ , where  $\bar{\mathcal{C}}$  is the partial closure of  $\mathcal{C}$  in  $\mathcal{P}_{n}$ ). We say this quantum error-correcting code is contextual (resp. contextual in a partial closure) if the empirical model on  $\langle\mathcal{C},\mathcal{M},{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\rangle$  (resp.  $\langle\bar{\mathcal{C}},\bar{\mathcal{M}},{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\rangle$ ) defined by some quantum state on the system is contextual. We can also say this code is strongly contextual (resp. strongly contextual in a partial closure) if the empirical model on  $\langle\mathcal{C},\mathcal{M},{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\rangle$  (resp.  $\langle\bar{\mathcal{C}},\bar{\mathcal{M}},{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\rangle$ ) defined by some quantum state on the system is strongly contextual.
Remark 2.
Performing any two commuting Pauli measurements  $p$  and  $q$  consecutively in any order is equivalent to performing the Pauli measurement  $pq$  (or  $qp$ ), in that we can infer the outcome from measuring  $pq$  by measuring  $p$  and then  $q$  (or measuring  $q$  and then  $p$ ).
Hence, if commuting measurements  $p$  and  $q$  are in the set of checks  $\mathcal{C}$ , we effectively also obtain the statistics of  $pq$  from measuring all checks in  $\mathcal{C}$ .
Thus, in determining which observables should be considered in a contextuality scenario for a given code with checks  $\mathcal{C}$ , it is natural to also include the product of any two commuting Pauli observables.
This leads to the notion of the partial closure  $\bar{\mathcal{C}}$  of check measurements  $\mathcal{C}$  which contains all Pauli observables whose values can be inferred from  $\mathcal{C}$  (by iteratively taking the product of any pair of commuting observables, starting from those in  $\mathcal{C}$ ).
The partial closure for Pauli measurements is defined formally and discussed in Definition  in section V.
Recall from section II.1 that for an  $\llbracket n,k,g,d\rrbracket$  subsystem qubit stabilizer code, its gauge group  $\mathcal{G}$ , stabilizer group  $\mathcal{S}$ , and its normalizer  $N(\mathcal{S})$  can be described in terms of virtual Pauli operators  $\{X_{j}^{\prime},Z_{j}^{\prime}\}_{1\leq j\leq n}$  from  $G_{n}$ .
Its check measurements  $\mathcal{C}$  can therefore be identified by the independent generators of  $\mathcal{G}$ , i.e.,  $\mathcal{C}=\{Z_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,Z_{s}^{\prime},X_{s+1}^{\prime},Z_{s+1}^{\prime},\cdots,X_{s+g}^{\prime},Z_{s+g}^{\prime}\}$  for some  $g\geq 0$  with  $s+g\leq n$ .
Lemma 1.
The compatibility graph of the set  $\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{n}$  of check measurements in the subsystem qubit stabilizer code has the Kirby–Love property if and only if  $g\geq 2$ .
Proof.
If  $g=0$ , all the operators in  $\mathcal{C}$  commute with one another, so the compatibility graph clearly does not have the Kirby–Love property. If  $g=1$ , there is only one pair of anticommuting operators in  $\mathcal{C}$ , namely  $X_{s+1}^{\prime}$  and  $Z_{s+1}^{\prime}$ , and so the compatibility graph again does not have the Kirby–Love property. If  $g\geq 2$ , it is straightforward to verify that the condition for Kirby–Love property of the compatibility graph of  $\mathcal{C}$  in Definition 9 is met by taking  $a=X_{s+1}^{\prime},b=X_{s+2}^{\prime},c=Z_{s+2}^{\prime},d=Z_{s+1}^{\prime}$  (see Fig. 1(c)).
∎
Theorem 3.
Let  $\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{n}$  be the set of check measurements in the subsystem qubit stabilizer code, and  $\bar{\mathcal{M}}$  be the set of maximally commuting subsets of  $\bar{\mathcal{C}}$ , where  $\bar{\mathcal{C}}$  is the partial closure of  $\mathcal{C}$  in  $\mathcal{P}_{n}$ . Let  $\mathcal{S}_{\rho}$  be the empirical model on  $\langle\bar{\mathcal{C}},\bar{\mathcal{M}},{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\rangle$  defined by some quantum state  $\rho$ . Then,  $\mathcal{S}_{\rho}$  is noncontextual if  $g<2$ , while  $\mathcal{S}_{\rho}$  is strongly contextual if  $g\geq 2$ .
Proof.
This follows from Lemma 1 and the equivalence between the Kirby–Love property, (probabilistic) contextuality, and strong contextuality in a partial closure, which will be established later in Corollary .

Corollary 1.
The subsystem qubit stabilizer code is strongly contextual in a partial closure if and only if there are at least  $2$  gauge qubits. It is noncontextual if and only if there are less than  $2$  gauge qubits.
Proof.
This simply follows from Theorem 3 and the definition of contextuality of a quantum error-correcting code.

Stabilizer codes are noncontextual.
Proof.

IV Characterization of the Kirby–Love property
We now give an alternative characterization of the Kirby–Love property. (This is related to the property described in [kirby2019contextuality, Theorem 3], but our proof is purely graph-theoretic and does not depend on contextuality arguments.)
We then use this characterization to establish that the graph corresponding to a measurement scenario possesses the Kirby–Love property whenever the empirical model on this scenario is strongly contextual (Theorem 4) or even merely contextual (Theorem 5).
Lemma 2.
Let  $G$  be a graph. Then the maximal cliques of  $G$  are disjoint if and only if  $G$  does not contain vertices  $a,b,c$  such that  $\{a,b\}$  and  $\{a,c\}$  are edges but  $\{b,c\}$  is not an edge.
Proof.
Assume first that the maximal cliques of  $G$  are disjoint, and let  $a,b,c$  be any vertices such that  $\{a,b\}$  and  $\{a,c\}$  are edges. Then each of  $\{a,b\}$  and  $\{a,c\}$  belongs to a maximal clique, which must be the same one since they are not disjoint. In particular,  $\{b,c\}$  is also an edge. This proves the “only if” direction.
Assume instead that  $G$  does not contain vertices  $a,b,c$  such that  $\{a,b\}$  and  $\{a,c\}$  are edges but  $\{b,c\}$  is not an edge. It follows that the neighborhood  $N(a)$  of any vertex  $a$  in  $G$  must form a clique. Consider any two maximal cliques  $C_{1},C_{2}$  of  $G$  that are not disjoint, i.e., they share some vertex  $a$ . Then the clique  $N(a)$  contains  $(C_{1}\cup C_{2})\setminus\{a\}$ , which must also thus be a clique. Then  $C_{1}\cup C_{2}$  is a clique containing both  $C_{1}$  and  $C_{2}$ . By maximality,  $C_{1}=C_{2}$ , as desired.
∎
Lemma 3.
Let  $G$  be a graph with vertex set  $V$ , and  $U$  be the set of universal vertices in  $G$ , i.e., vertices in  $G$  which are adjacent to all other vertices. Then  $G$  has the Kirby–Love property if and only if the maximal cliques of the induced subgraph of  $V\setminus U$  are not disjoint.
Proof.
Assume first that  $G$  does not have the Kirby–Love property. We need to show that the maximal cliques of the induced subgraph of  $V\setminus U$  are disjoint, or equivalently by Lemma 2 that it does not contain vertices  $a,b,c$  such that  $\{a,b\}$  and  $\{a,c\}$  are edges but  $\{b,c\}$  is not an edge. Indeed, suppose on the contrary there were three such vertices. Since  $a\not\in U$ , then there must be some vertex  $d$  such that  $\{a,d\}$  is not an edge. This means  $G$  has the Kirby–Love property, a contradiction.
Assume instead that  $G$  has the Kirby–Love property, i.e., there are vertices  $\{a,b,c,d\}$  such that  $\{a,b\}$  and  $\{a,c\}$  are edges but  $\{a,d\}$  and  $\{b,c\}$  are not edges. The latter condition implies that  $a,b,c\in V\setminus U$ . It then follows from Lemma 2 that the maximal cliques of the induced subgraph of  $V\setminus U$  are not disjoint.
∎
The following lemma simplifies the calculation of the maximal cliques of a graph.
Lemma 4.
Let  $G$  be a graph with vertex set  $V$ , and  $U$  be the set of universal vertices in  $G$ . Let  $\mathcal{M}$  be the set of maximal cliques of the induced subgraph of  $V\setminus U$ . Then the maximal cliques of  $G$  are exactly the sets  $U\cup C$  for  $C\in\mathcal{M}$ .
Proof.
It is clear that  $U\cup C$  is a clique for each  $C\in\mathcal{M}$ , since each vertex in  $U$  is universal and  $C$  is a clique. Each such  $U\cup C$  is also maximal, because any vertex outside of it must lie in some  $C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}$  different from  $C$  and so must not be adjacent to some vertex in  $C$  by maximality of the clique  $C$  in the induced subgraph of  $V\setminus U$ .
It remains to prove that these are all the maximal cliques in  $G$ . Let  $K$  be any maximal clique in  $G$ . Then  $K\setminus U$  is a clique in the induced subgraph of  $V\setminus U$ , so is contained in some  $C$ . Thus  $K\subseteq U\cup C$ , and equality holds by maximality of  $K$ .
∎
Finally, the theorem below proves that strong contextuality implies the Kirby–Love property of the compatibility graph.
Theorem 4.
The compatibility graph of any strongly contextual possibilistic empirical model on a quantum measurement scenario has the Kirby–Love property.
Proof.
Let  $\mathcal{S}$  be a possibilistic empirical model on a quantum measurement scenario  $\langle X,\mathcal{M},O\rangle$  whose compatibility graph  $G$  does not have the Kirby–Love property. We need to show that  $\mathcal{S}$  is not strongly contextual, i.e.,  $\mathcal{S}(X)\neq\emptyset$ .
Let  $V$  be the vertex set of  $G$ , and  $U$  be the set of universal vertices in  $G$ . Taking  $\mathcal{M}^{\prime}$  to be the (necessarily nonempty66
                6
              If  $V=U$ , then  $\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=\{\emptyset\}$ .) set of maximal cliques of the induced subgraph of  $V\setminus U$ , we have
 $\mathcal{M}=\{U\cup C:C\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\},$ 
by Lemma 4. As  $G$  does not have the Kirby–Love property, Lemma 3 says that the sets  $C\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}$  are disjoint and so the intersection of any two distinct maximal contexts is  $U$ . (See Fig. 2 for a diagram of the structure of  $G$ .)
Fix any  $C_{0}\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset$ . Since  $\mathcal{S}(U\cup C_{0})\neq\emptyset$  by property (E1), we may choose some  $s_{C_{0}}\in\mathcal{S}(U\cup C_{0})$ . This restricts to  $s_{C_{0}}|_{U}\in\mathcal{S}(U)$ . For each  $C\in\mathcal{M}$  distinct from  $C_{0}$ , we may choose some  $s_{C}\in\mathcal{S}(U\cup C)$  such that  $s_{C}|_{U}=s_{C_{0}}|_{U}$ , by property (E2) which says that  $\mathcal{S}(U\cup C)\to\mathcal{S}(U)$  is surjective. Thus, for any distinct  $C,C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}$ ,
 $s_{C}|_{(U\cup C)\cap(U\cup C^{\prime})}=s_{C}|_{U}=s_{C_{0}}|_{U}=s_{C^{\prime}}|_{U}=s_{C^{\prime}}|_{(U\cup C)\cap(U\cup C^{\prime})}.$ 
The equality between the first and last terms is also (trivially) true if  $C=C^{\prime}$ . Property (E3) then ensures the existence of some section  $s\in\mathcal{S}(X)$ , as desired.
∎

Theorem 5.
If  $R$  is a zerosumfree semifield, the compatibility graph of any contextual  $R$ -valued empirical model on a quantum measurement scenario has the Kirby–Love property. In particular, this applies to any contextual empirical model defined by some quantum state  $\rho$ .
Proof.
The second part of the theorem follows from the first, because the semiring  ${\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}$  is a zerosumfree semifield. We now prove the first part of the theorem.
Let  $R$  be a zerosumfree semifield and  $e=\{e_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{M}}$  be an  $R$ -valued empirical model on a quantum measurement scenario  $\langle X,\mathcal{M},O\rangle$  whose compatibility graph  $G$  does not have the Kirby–Love property. We need to show that  $e$  is noncontextual, i.e., that there is some global section  $d\in\mathcal{D}_{R}\mathcal{E}(X)$  satisfying  $d|_{C}=e_{C}$  for all  $C\in\mathcal{M}$ .
As in the proof of Theorem 4, we take  $V$  to be the vertex set of  $G$ ,  $U$  to be the set of universal vertices in  $G$ , and  $\mathcal{M}^{\prime}$  to be the set of maximal cliques of the induced subgraph of  $V\setminus U$ , giving us
 $\mathcal{M}=\{U\cup C^{\prime}:C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\},$ 
with the sets  $C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}$  disjoint and so the intersection of any two distinct maximal contexts is  $U$ . For each  $x\in O^{U}$  and  $y_{C}\in O^{C^{\prime}}$ , we will also use  $(x,y_{C})\in O^{C}$  to denote the unique element  $g\in O^{C}$  such that  $g|_{U}=x$  and  $g|_{C^{\prime}}=y_{C}$ , and  $(x,\{y_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{M}})$  to denote the unique element  $h\in O^{X}$  such that  $h|_{U}=x$  and  $h|_{C^{\prime}}=y_{C}$  for all  $C\in\mathcal{M}$ .
For distinct  $C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{M}$  and  $x\in O^{U}$ , the condition that  $e_{C_{1}}|_{C_{1}\cap C_{2}}=e_{C_{2}}|_{C_{1}\cap C_{2}}$  translates to saying
 $\sum_{y\in O^{C_{1}^{\prime}}}e_{C_{1}}(x,y)=\sum_{y\in O^{C_{2}^{\prime}}}e_{C_{2}}(x,y).$

Remark 3.
Theorem 5 actually holds for all semifields (including fields) and rings. To see this for general semifields, we may modify the proof above by defining  $d(x,\{y_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{M}})$  differently if  $d_{x}=0$ . Instead of setting its value to zero, we can fix distinguished elements  $y_{C}^{\ast}\in O^{C^{\prime}}$  and define  $d(x,\{y_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{M}})$  to be:
• 
 $0$ , if  $y_{C}\neq y_{C}^{\ast}$  for at least two distinct  $C\in\mathcal{M}$ ,
• 
 $e_{C_{0}}(x,y_{C_{0}})$ , if  $y_{C_{0}}\neq y_{C_{0}}^{\ast}$  but  $y_{C}=y_{C}^{\ast}$  for all  $C\neq C_{0}$  (for some  $C_{0}\in\mathcal{M}$ ), and
• 
 $\sum_{C\in\mathcal{M}}e_{C}(x,y_{C}^{\ast})$ , if  $y_{C}=y_{C}^{\ast}$  for all  $C\in\mathcal{M}$ .
(This definition coincides with the one in the proof above, if  $R$  is a zerosumfree semifield.) It is straightforward to check that equation (3) continues to hold under this definition.
For general rings, we can modify the proof by using this modified definition of  $d(x,\{y_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{M}})$  for any  $x\in O^{U}$  (not just the ones with  $d_{x}=0$ ), except that we set
 $d(x,\{y_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{M}})=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{M}}e_{C}(x,y_{C}^{\ast})-(|\mathcal{M}|-1)d_{x}$ 
(instead of  $\sum_{C\in\mathcal{M}}e_{C}(x,y_{C}^{\ast})$ ) in the case where  $y_{C}=y_{C}^{\ast}$  for all  $C\in\mathcal{M}$ . Again, a similar check would show that equation (3) holds.
