Abstract
Berlekamp proposed a class of impartial combinatorial games based on the moves of chess pieces on rectangular boards.
We generalize impartial chess games by playing them on Young diagrams and obtain results about winning and losing positions and Sprague-Grundy values for all chess pieces.
We classify these games, and their restrictions to sets of partitions known as rectangles, staircases, and general staircases, according to the approach of Conway, later extended by Gurvich and Ho.
The games Rook and Queen restricted to rectangles are known to have the same game tree as  $2$ -pile Nim and Wythoff, respectively, so our work generalizes these well-known games.

impartial game , chess , Young diagram , integer partition , combinatorial game

1 Introduction
In a series of YouTube videos, Berlekamp [1, 2] introduced Impartial chess, a family of impartial combinatorial games based on the moves of various chess pieces that are played on rectangular boards. In this paper, we formalize and extend the study of these games by playing them on Young diagrams of integer partitions. The extension to non-rectangular boards is inspired by previous work on LCTR [3], the misère form of which, known as Downright, is similar in spirit to Berlekamp’s impartial chess.
Conway [4] classified impartial games using certain properties that depend on the interaction of normal and misere play. Gurvich and Ho [5] subsequently undertook a similar effort and showed some of their categories to be equivalent to those of Conway. We refer to the unification of these schemes as the Conway-Gurvich-Ho (CGH) classification.
The CGH classification of Nim, Subtraction, Wythoff, Mark, LCTR, and Downright are established in [3, 4, 5].
We determine Sprague-Grundy values or winning and losing positions for impartial chess games of all chess pieces on the sets of partitions known as rectangles, staircases, and general staircases.
Some of the games we study occupy the same region of the CGH classification scheme as one of the above games.
Played on a particular set of partitions, we show that Rook occupies a region that was not previously known to contain any game, while Queen occupies a region that was only known to contain a single small artificial game.
This paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, we provide background on partitions, Young diagrams, impartial games, and Sprague-Grundy theory. In Section 3.1, we describe the impartial chess games Downright, Pawn, Knight, Bishop, King, Queen, and Rook. In Section 3.2, we use directed acyclic graphs associated to games to define partition-equivalence and game-equivalence of impartial chess games. In Section 3.3, we discuss partition- and game-equivalence of Pawn, Knight, and Downright. In Section 3.4, we establish Sprague-Grundy values for King on certain classes of partitions. We characterize losing positions for Rook in Section 3.5 and explain why such characterization for Queen is likely to be difficult in Section 3.6. In Section 4 we take two different approaches to the study of the misère forms of impartial chess games. The first involves an operation on directed acyclic graphs known as truncation. The second concerns a classification scheme, which we refer to as CGH classification, based on the interaction between normal and misère forms of a given game. In Section 5 we offer some directions for further investigation.
2 Preliminaries
In this section, we provide background information on partitions, Young diagrams, impartial games, Sprague-Grundy theory, and complexity theory. To simplify expressions throughout the paper we denote the set of integers, nonnegative integers, and positive integers by  $\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}$ , and  $\mathbb{Z}^{>0}$ , respectively. For integers  $a$  and  $b$  with  $a\leq b$ , we denote the set  $\{a,a+1,\ldots,b\}$  by  $[a,b]$  and denote  $[1,b]$  by  $[b]$ . We define  $[a,b]=\emptyset$  if  $a>b$ .
A directed acyclic graph (DAG) is a directed graph  $G=(V,E)$  which does not contain a directed cycle as a subgraph (for standard graph-theoretic definitions see e.g. [6]).
The length of the longest directed path in  $G$  is denoted by  $\mathrm{lp}(G)$ .
2.1 Young diagrams and integer partitions
Let  $n$  be a nonnegative integer. If  $n=\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{r}$ , where  $r$  is a nonnegative integer, each  $\lambda_{j}$  is an integer,
and  $\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{r}>0$ , then we say that  $\lambda=\llbracket\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r}\rrbracket$  is a partition of  $n$ .
The unique partition of 0 is  $\llbracket\rrbracket$ .
The  $\lambda_{j}$ ’s are called the parts of  $\lambda$ .
When some of the parts of a partition are equal, we may abbreviate  $\llbracket\lambda_{1}^{m_{1}},\dots,\lambda_{k}^{m_{k}}\rrbracket$ , where  $\lambda_{j}$  appears  $m_{j}\geq 0$  times.
Let  $\mathbb{Y}$  denote the set of all partitions of nonnegative integers and let  $\mathbb{Y}^{+}=\mathbb{Y}\setminus\{\llbracket\rrbracket\}$ . Let  $\lambda=\llbracket\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{r}\rrbracket$  and  $\beta=\llbracket\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m}\rrbracket$  be partitions.
We define a partial order  $\leq$  on  $\mathbb{Y}$  by
 $\lambda\leq\beta\,\iff r\leq m\text{ and }\lambda_{i}\leq\beta_{i}$ 
for all  $i\in[r]$ 
The relation  $\leq$  defines the Young lattice; see [7, 8].
The Young diagram of  $\lambda$  is a left-justified array of square cells in which the  $i$ th row from the top contains  $\lambda_{i}$  cells. No distinction is made between a partition and its Young diagram.
The conjugate partition  $\lambda^{\prime}$  of  $\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}^{+}$  is defined to be  $\lambda^{\prime}=\llbracket\lambda^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{\prime}_{\lambda_{1}}\rrbracket$ 
where  $\lambda^{\prime}_{j}$  is the largest integer such that  $\lambda_{\lambda^{\prime}_{j}}\geq j$ ; see Figure 1.
Thus, the  $j$ th column of  $\lambda$  contains  $\lambda^{\prime}_{j}$  cells.
The Young diagram of  $\llbracket\rrbracket$  has no cells and  $\llbracket\rrbracket^{\prime}=\llbracket\rrbracket$ .

Let  $\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}^{+}$ , and  $i,j\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}$ .
The subpartition  $\lambda[i,j]$  of  $\lambda$  is defined as  $\lambda[i,j]=\llbracket\lambda_{i+1}-j,\lambda_{i+2}-j,\dots\rrbracket$ , where trailing nonpositive entries are removed.
Thus  $\lambda[0,0]=\lambda$  for any  $\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}^{+}$ . If  $\lambda[i,j]\neq\llbracket\rrbracket$ , we say that such subpartition is well-defined. We define a corner of a nonempty partition  $\lambda$  to be any pair of integers  $(i,j)$  such that  $\lambda[i,j]=\llbracket 1\rrbracket$ .
Now let  $\lambda=\llbracket\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r}\rrbracket>\llbracket 1\rrbracket$ . Define a partition  $\lambda^{-}$  to be the partition obtained from  $\lambda$  by removing all of its corners.

 $\lambda_{i}^{-}=\begin{cases}\lambda_{i}-1&\text{if }i=r\text{ or }\lambda_{i}>\lambda_{i+1},\\

2.2 Impartial games and Sprague-Grundy values
In this paper, our focus will be exclusively on finite impartial combinatorial games, which we will refer to simply as games. A game consists of a set of rules and a (starting) position. If the rules are clear from context we shall not differentiate between the terms position and game. The positions in the games we study are partitions or Young diagrams, which we describe in the following subsection. A subposition of a game is a position that can be reached after a nonnegative number of
moves. A combinatorial game is short if no position can be repeated, and if there exist finitely many subpositions.
A terminal position of a game is a position from which no further moves are possible.
Under normal play, the player who moves to a terminal position is the winner. Under misère play, the player who moves to a terminal position is the loser. In this paper, we assume normal play unless otherwise indicated.
Let  $G$  be a game under normal (resp. misère) play.
We define the set of its winning positions  $\mathcal{N}_{G}$ ,
and the set of its losing positions  $\mathcal{P}_{G}$ , as follows:
1. 
Terminal positions are in  $\mathcal{P}_{G}$  (resp.  $\mathcal{N}_{G}$ ).
2. 
A position is in  $\mathcal{P}_{G}$  if none of its moves leads to a position in  $\mathcal{P}_{G}$ .
3. 
A position is in  $\mathcal{N}_{G}$  if there is a move to a position in  $\mathcal{P}_{G}$ .
We denote  $\mathcal{P}_{G}$  by  $\mathcal{P}$  and  $\mathcal{N}_{G}$  by  $\mathcal{N}$  when the game is clear from context.
A short two player combinatorial game is impartial if both players have the same possible moves in each position.
Let  $p$  be a position of such a game  $G$ .
If there is a move from  $p$  to position  $p^{\prime}$ , we write  $p\to p^{\prime}$ .
For a finite set  $S$  of nonnegative integers the minimum excluded value of  $S$ 
is defined as the smallest nonnegative integer that is not in  $S$  and
is denoted by  $\Mex(S)$ .
The Sprague-Grundy value ( $\mathbb{SG}$ -value) of a position  $A$  within the game  $G$  is defined recursively by  $\mathbb{SG}_{G}(p)=\Mex(\{\mathbb{SG}_{G}(p^{\prime})\mid p\to p^{\prime}\}).$ 
When  $G$  is clear from context we denote  $\mathbb{SG}_{G}(p)$  by  $\mathbb{SG}(p)$ .
If  $\mathbb{SG}(p)=t$  then  $p$  is called a  $t$ -position.
If  $p$  is terminal then  $\mathbb{SG}(p)=\Mex(\emptyset)=0$ .
As introduced in Conway [4], the Misère Grundy value ( $\mathcal{G}^{-}$ -value) of a position  $A$  within the game  $G$  is defined recursively the same way as the  $\mathbb{SG}$ -value of  $A$ , with distinction of terminal positions having  $\mathcal{G}^{-}$ -value  $1$ .
A position with  $\mathbb{SG}$ -value  $t$  and  $\mathcal{G}^{-}$ -value  $\ell$  is called a  $(t,\ell)$ -position. The pairs  $(t,\ell)$  are used to classify combinatorial games as described in [4] and [5].
 $\mathbb{SG}$ -values provide a useful mechanism for determining winning and losing positions for short impartial games under normal play.
In particular, the  $\mathcal{P}$ -positions are precisely those that have  $\mathbb{SG}$ -value  $0$  while the  $\mathcal{N}$ -positions are those with nonzero  $\mathbb{SG}$ -values. The Sprague-Grundy theorem [10] shows that the  $\mathbb{SG}$ -value of the (disjunctive) sum of impartial games under normal play can be obtained by replacing every game-summand with a Nim-pile, with the size corresponding to the  $\mathbb{SG}$ -value of the game. For detailed coverage of combinatorial game theory we refer the reader to [10].
3 Impartial chess
We now describe the family of games played on Young diagrams and inspired by the moves of chess pieces.
We begin by defining movesets that describe the allowable moves for each chess piece.
3.1 Impartial chess games
In [3], we studied the misère version of LCTR and showed that it is equivalent to a normal-form game played on partitions which we refer to as Downright. Downright is closely related to a family of games played on rectangles introduced by Berlekamp [11]. All of these games can be played on partitions and expressed in the language of movesets, which we now explain.
An impartial chess game is a pair  $(M,\lambda)$ , where  $M\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}\setminus\{(0,0)\}$  and  $\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}^{+}$ . We refer to  $M$  as a moveset and an element of  $M$  as a move.
On their turn, the players choose a move  $(i,j)\in M$ , such that  $\lambda[i,j]\in Y^{+}$  and move to  $\lambda[i,j]$ , passing the turn.

Observation 3.2.
Let  $M$  be a moveset and  $\lambda,\beta\in\mathbb{Y}$ .
Then  $\lambda\leq\beta$  implies  $\textnormal{DAG}_{M}(\lambda)$  is an induced subgraph of  $\textnormal{DAG}_{M}(\beta)$ .
The set of partitions that are partition-equivalent to  $\lambda$  in  $M$  has a minimum element (in the sense of Young’s lattice). For example,  $\llbracket 3,3,2\rrbracket$  is the minimum element for its equivalence class in  $\symknight$ , which is
 $\{\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}^{+}:\lambda[1,2]\neq\llbracket\rrbracket\mbox{ and }\lambda[2,1]\neq\llbracket\rrbracket\mbox{ and }\lambda[2,4]=\lambda[3,3]=\lambda[4,2]=\llbracket\rrbracket\}.$ 
For a moveset  $M$  and  $\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}^{+}$  it may happen that there is  $m=(y,x)\in M$  such that  $\lambda[y,x]$  is not well-defined and therefore  $m$  can never be used as a move in  $(M,\lambda)$ . It follows that  $m$  does not appear as an edge label in  $\textnormal{DAG}_{M}(\lambda)$ . Let  $M|_{\lambda}$  be the set of edge labels in  $\textnormal{DAG}_{M}(\lambda)$ .
We now define an equivalence relation on the set of impartial chess games. We say that  $(M_{1},\lambda)$  and  $(M_{2},\mu)$  are game-equivalent if there exists a bijection  $f:M_{1}|_{\lambda}\to M_{2}|_{\mu}$  such that the following recursive procedure transforms  $\textnormal{DAG}_{M_{1}}(\lambda)$  into  $\textnormal{DAG}_{M_{2}}(\mu)$ .
1.

Queen is played with moveset  $\symqueen=(1,0)^{+}\cup(0,1)^{+}\cup(1,1)^{+}.$ 
When played on rectangular partitions, Queen is closely related to Wythoff.
Recall that Wythoff [13], denoted by  $W(a,b)$ , is played with two piles of tokens of size  $a$  and  $b$ ,
where a move consists of removing an arbitrary positive number of tokens from a pile, or removing the same number of tokens from both piles.

4 Misère aspects of impartial chess
In this section we discuss two aspects of misère play for impartial chess games. In Section 4.1, we show how misère play is equivalent to normal play on a “truncated” partition using the same moveset. In Section 4.2, we use a classification scheme initiated by Conway [4] and further developed by Gurvich and Ho [5, 15] to study the interaction of normal play and misère play on impartial chess games.
4.1 The truncation operation
In this section we describe the behavior of misère variants of games studied in Section 3.1.
To this end, we make use of truncation, introduced in [3]. This notion, reformulated in the setting of impartial chess games, has an important property – the resulting game remains an impartial chess game played on modified partition, where the same moveset remains the same.
Definition 4.1 (truncation).
Let  $(M,\lambda)$  be a non-terminal impartial chess game. Its truncation  $\textnormal{trunc}_{M}(\lambda)$  is an impartial chess game  $(M,\llbracket\bar{\lambda}_{1},\ldots\rrbracket)$  where  $\bar{\lambda}_{i}$  is the largest integer such that  $(M,\lambda[i-1,\bar{\lambda}_{i}-1])$  is defined but not terminal.
The truncation of a game need not be the smallest partition with the same game tree, as illustrated below.
Example 4.2.
 $\textnormal{trunc}_{\symknight}(\llbracket 5,5,5,3,3,2,1\rrbracket)=(\symknight,\llbracket 4,3,2,1\rrbracket)$  which is partition-equivalent to  $(\symknight,\llbracket 3,3,2\rrbracket)$ .
The following proposition is easily verified.
Proposition 4.3.
Let  $(M,\lambda)$  be a non-terminal impartial chess game. Then  $\textnormal{trunc}_{M}(\lambda[i,j])=\textnormal{trunc}_{M}(\lambda)[i,j]$ .
Using our terminology,
[3, Theorem 9] can be reformulated as follows.
Theorem 4.4.
The  $\mathcal{P}_{M}$ -positions of any misère impartial chess game  $(M,\lambda)$  exactly correspond to those of  $\textnormal{trunc}_{M}(\lambda)$ .
Theorem 4.4 can also be obtained by using “misère Grundy numbers”  $\mathscr{G}^{-}$  as described in Conway [4]. We denote the misère Grundy number of an impartial chess game  $(M,\lambda)$  by  $\mathscr{G}^{-}_{M}(\lambda)$ .

4.2 Conway-Gurvich-Ho classification
Conway [4] introduced the notion of a tame game. Gurvich and Ho [5] extended this idea to a more general classification of games. Their definitions, included below for convenience, involve pairs  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{M}(\lambda)=(\mathbb{SG}_{M}(\lambda),\mathscr{G}_{M}^{-}(\lambda))$ , which we refer to as Conway pairs. We sometimes omit the subscript when the game is clear from context. If  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{M}(\lambda)\in\{(0,1),(1,0)\}$  then  $(M,\lambda)$  is called swap. If  $k\geq 0$  and  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{M}(\lambda)=(k,k)$ , then  $(M,\lambda)$  is called symmetric.
Definition 4.6.
A impartial game is called
1. 
returnable if for any move from a  $(0,1)$ -position (resp., a  $(1,0)$ -position) to a non-terminal position  $y$ , there is a move from  $y$  to a  $(0,1)$ -position (resp., to a  $(1,0)$ -position).
2. 
forced if each move from a  $(0,1)$ -position results in a  $(1,0)$ -position and vice versa;
3. 
domestic if it has neither  $(0,k)$ -positions nor  $(k,0)$ -positions with  $k\geq 2$ ;
4. 
tame if it has only  $(0,1)$ -positions,  $(1,0)$ -positions, and  $(k,k)$ -positions with  $k\geq 0$ ;
5. 
miserable if for every position  $x$ , one of the following holds: (i)  $x$  is a  $(0,1)$ -position or  $(1,0)$ -position, or (ii) there is no move from  $x$  to a  $(0,1)$ -position or  $(1,0)$ -position, or (iii) there are moves from  $x$  to both a  $(0,1)$ -position and a  $(1,0)$ -position.
6. 
pet if it has only  $(0,1)$ -positions,  $(1,0)$ -positions, and  $(k,k)$ -positions with  $k\geq 2$ .
The relationships between these properties were explored in in [5] and are illustrated in Figure 7. The Conway-Gurvich-Ho (CGH) classification of an impartial game is determined by which of these properties the game has.
The CGH classification of Nim, Subtraction, Wythoff, Mark, LCTR, and  $\mathcal{D}$  are known. Nim was shown to be forced and miserable but not pet in [4]. Subtraction was shown to be pet and returnable but not forced, and Wythoff was shown to be miserable and returnable, but neither pet nor forced, in [5]. LCTR and  $\mathcal{D}$  were shown to be domestic but not tame and returnable but not forced in [3]. Mark was shown not to be domestic in [5] and is easily shown to be not returnable. Other games are classified in [5].
Some of the families of games we study occupy the same region as one of the above games. One family occupies a region not previously known to contain any game; another occupies a region that was only known to contain a single small game.
In determining the CGH classification for a family of impartial chess games, in most cases we state that the family has one or more properties, and also that it does not have one or more properties. In making the former statement, we assert that the property holds for all members of the family. In the making the latter statement, we assert that there exist family members for which the given property does not hold.
Let  $P\subseteq\mathbb{Y}^{+}$  and  $M$  be an impartial chess moveset. When we state that  $(M,P)$  is returnable, we mean that each  $(M,\lambda)$  is returnable, where  $\lambda\in P$ . When we say  $(M,P)$  is not returnable, we mean that there exists  $\lambda\in P$  such that  $(M,\lambda)$  is not returnable.
The same applies to pet, miserable, tame, domestic, and forced.
Let  $G_{1}$  be a set of impartial games and let  $G_{2}\subseteq G_{1}$ . If every game in  $G_{1}$  is pet, then so is every game in  $G_{2}$ . The same applies to miserable, tame, domestic, forced, and returnable. The following observation is a special case.
Observation 4.7.
Let  $P_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{Y}^{+}$  and let  $P_{2}\subseteq P_{1}$  and let  $M$  be an impartial chess moveset. If  $\left(M,P_{1}\right)$  is pet, then so is  $(M,P_{2})$ . The same applies to miserable, tame, domestic, forced, and returnable.

The only nonterminal swap positions are in the subposition  $(\symqueen,\llbracket 5,4^{k}\rrbracket[k-4,1])=(\symqueen,\llbracket 3^{3}\rrbracket)$ . This subposition is returnable but not forced.
∎
4.2.4 Domestic but not tame games
Recall that in a domestic game, a value  $0$  can appear in a Conway pair only if the position is either swap or symmetric.
Theorem 4.14.
  [diagram-symbol]   and  $\symknight$ 
are domestic but not tame and returnable but not forced.
Proof.
To see that  $\symknight$  and   [diagram-symbol]   are not tame, note that   [diagram-symbol]  . Theorem 4.9 shows that  $\symknight$  and   [diagram-symbol]   are not forced.
To see that  $\symknight$  and   [diagram-symbol]   are domestic, note that there are at most two moves from any position, so neither coordinate of the Conway pair can be greater than two. Thus it suffices to show that  $(2,0)$  and  $(0,2)$  cannot occur.
The proof is by contradiction. Let  $\lambda$  be a minimal partition (with respect to the Young lattice) so that  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda)=(0,2)$  or  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda)=(2,0)$ . We treat the case  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda)=(0,2)$ ; the case  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda)=(2,0)$  is similar and is left to the reader.
If  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda)=(0,2)$  then, without loss of generality, it must be true that  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[2,1])=(x,1)$  and  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[1,2])=(y,0)$  for some  $x,y\in\{1,2\}$ . Due to minimality,  $y$  cannot be 2, so  $y=1$ .
If  $x=2$ , then  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[3,3])=(0,2)$ , contradicting minimality, so  $x=1$ .
If  $\lambda[3,3]$  is not well-defined, then  $\lambda[4,2]$  must be well-defined since  $\lambda[2,1]$  cannot be terminal, so  $\lambda[4,2]$  must be terminal and  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[4,2])=(0,1)$ , contradicting  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[2,1])=(1,1)$ .
Thus  $\lambda[3,3]$  is well-defined and
 $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[3,3])=(z,2)$  for some  $z\in\{0,2\}$ . Due to minimality  $z$  cannot be  $0$ , so  $z=2$ . It follows that  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[4,2])=(0,0)$ , which implies
 $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[5,4])=(1,1)$ , from which it follows that  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[4,5])=(0,0)$ . This implies
 $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[2,4])=(1,1)$ , a contradiction with  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[1,2])=(1,0)$ . This concludes the proof for domesticity.
To see that  $\symknight$  is returnable (which implies that   [diagram-symbol]   is returnable), consider a non-terminal partition  $\lambda$  with  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda)=(0,1)$ . Since there are no  $(k,0)$ -positions with  $k\geq 2$ , it must be possible to move to a  $(1,0)$ -position. Similarly a  $(1,0)$  position can always move to a  $(0,1)$ -position. Without loss of generality, suppose that  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[1,2])=(1,0)$ . Then  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[2,1])\in\{(1,2),(1,0),(2,2)\}$ . If  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[2,1])=(1,0)$  then  $\symknight(\lambda)$  is returnable. Also, if  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[2,1])=(2,2)$  then  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[3,3])=(0,1)$ , so  $\symknight(\lambda)$  is again returnable.
Finally, suppose  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[2,1])=(1,2)$ . If  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[3,3])=(0,1)$ , then  $\symknight(\lambda)$  is returnable. The only alternative is that  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[3,3])=(2,1)$ . In this case, we must have  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[4,2])=(0,0)$ . It follows that  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[5,4])=(1,2)$ . From this, we obtain  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[4,5)=(0,0)$ .
Since non-terminal swap positions must move to at least another swap it follows that  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[2,4])=(0,1)$  which is a contradiction with  $\mathscr{G}^{\pm}_{\symknight}(\lambda[4,5])=(0,0)$ .
∎

5 Open questions in impartial chess
Hooks (see Definition 2.1)
are a parametrized class of partitions studied in [16, 17, 18].
The restriction of impartial chess games to hooks is not very interesting from the point of view of CGH classification since they are forced and pet for every chess piece.
A more interesting family to consider are thick hooks [16, 17] of the form  $\llbracket c^{a},b^{r-a}\rrbracket$ , which are simultaneously subpartitions of generalized staircases and generalizations of both rectangles and hooks. Computing the Sprague-Grundy values of generalized staircases might be out of reach for King and Rook, but we believe it could be possible for thick hooks.
For rectangles, the coefficient 2 in Lemma 3.22 can be replaced with the golden ratio [13]. It would be interesting to know if the coefficient 2 can be reduced for arbitrary partitions. Showing that it cannot be reduced would require finding an infinite family of partitions such that the inequality in Lemma 3.22 holds. Two such partitions are  $\llbracket 3^{2}\rrbracket$  and  $\llbracket 9^{2},7^{3}\rrbracket$ .
