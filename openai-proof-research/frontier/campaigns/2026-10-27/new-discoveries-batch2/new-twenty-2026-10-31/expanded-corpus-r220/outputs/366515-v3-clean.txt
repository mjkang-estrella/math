On graphs with large third eigenvalue
Giacomo Leonida
††thanks: gl510@cam.ac.uk, sl2190@cam.ac.uk. Department of Pure Mathematics and Mathematical Statistics
(DPMMS), University of Cambridge, United Kingdom.
Sida Li11footnotemark:

Abstract
Given a graph  $G$ , let  $\lambda_{3}$  denote the third largest eigenvalue of its adjacency matrix. In this paper, we prove various results towards the conjecture that  $\lambda_{3}(G)\leq\frac{|V(G)|}{3}$ , motivated by a question of Nikiforov. We generalise the known constructions that yield  $\lambda_{3}(G)=\frac{|V(G)|}{3}-1$  and prove the inequality holds for  $G$  strongly regular, a regular line graph or a Cayley graph on an abelian group. We also consider the extended problem of minimising  $\lambda_{n-1}$  on weighted graphs and reduce the existence of a minimiser with simple final eigenvalue to a vertex multiplication of a graph on 11 vertices. We prove that the minimal  $\lambda_{n-1}$  over weighted graphs is at most  $O(\sqrt{n})$  from the minimal  $\lambda_{n-1}$  over unweighted graphs. 
MSC Classification: 15A42; 05C50.
Keywords: third largest eigenvalue of a graph; Hong’s problem; Cayley graphs; weighted graphs.
1 Introduction
For a graph  $G$ , let  $A(G)$  denote its adjacency matrix and  $\lambda_{k}$  the  $k$ -th largest eigenvalue of  $A(G)$ . Following Nikiforov’s notation in [10], define  $c_{k}=\sup\{\lambda_{k}(G)/n:|V(G)|=n\geq k\}$ . Nikiforov proved the following upper bound on  $c_{k}$ .
Theorem 1.1 (Nikiforov [10]).
For  $k\geq 2$ ,
 $c_{k}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{k-1}}.$ 
This gives that  $c_{2}=\frac{1}{2}$ , as the construction  $kK_{\frac{n}{k}}$  has  $\lambda_{k}(kK_{\frac{n}{k}})=\frac{n}{k}-1$ , so  $c_{k}\geq\frac{1}{k}$ . Yet for  $k\geq 5$ , Nikiforov provided a construction yielding  $c_{k}>\frac{1}{k-\frac{1}{2}}$ . Additionally, for sufficiently large  $k$ , Nikiforov used the family of strongly-regular graphs introduced by Taylor in [11, 12], widely known as Taylor graphs, for a closely matching lower bound to Theorem 1.1.
Theorem 1.2 (Nikiforov [10]).
There exists  $k_{0}$  such that if  $k>k_{0}$ , then
 $c_{k}>\frac{1}{2\sqrt{k-1}+\sqrt[3]{k}}.$ 
Out of the two remaining cases  $k\in\{3,4\}$ , recently Linz [7] tightened the lower bound on  $k=4$  with the closed blow-up of the icosahedral graph.
Theorem 1.3 (Linz [7]).
 $c_{4}\geq\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{12}\approx 0.26967>\frac{1}{4}.$ 
Thus, we have that  $c_{k}>\frac{1}{k}$  for  $k\geq 4$  and  $c_{k}=\frac{1}{k}$  for  $k\leq 2$ . 
In this paper, we provide evidence for  $c_{3}=\frac{1}{3}$ , providing a partial affirmative response to Nikiforov’s original question.
Question 1.4 (Nikiforov[10]).
Is it true that  $c_{3}=\frac{1}{3}$ ?
First, we demonstrate a family of graphs, denoted  $H_{a,b}$ , satisfying  $\lambda_{3}(G)=\frac{|V(G)|}{3}-1$ , which generalises the only previously-known constructions of  $3K_{n}$  and the closed blow-up of  $C_{6}$ . In particular, these include the first non-regular examples of such graphs and we propose the following conjecture.
Conjecture 1.5.
If  $\lambda_{3}(G)=\frac{|V(G)|}{3}-1$ , then  $G$  is isomorphic to  $H_{a,b}$  for some non-negative integers  $a,b$ .
We then prove that multiple families of graphs, which provide the best known lower bounds for larger values of  $k$ , have  $\lambda_{3}(G)\leq\frac{|V(G)|}{3}$ , further supporting Question 1.4. In particular, we prove and generalise the following theorem.
Theorem 1.6.
If  $G$  is strongly regular, a regular line graph, or a Cayley graph on an abelian group, then  $\lambda_{3}(G)\leq\frac{|V(G)|}{3}$ .
Note that within the table of best-known lower bounds compiled by Linz [7], Taylor graphs are strongly regular, Paley graphs are Cayley over  $\mathbb{Z}_{n}$  and both the Petersen graph and  $C_{6}$  are regular line graphs. In addition, we generalise the methods of resolving Cayley graphs on an abelian group to semi-Cayley graphs on an abelian group, as well as vertex-transitive graphs with abelian automorphism group.
We also consider the extended problem of bounding  $\lambda_{k}$  for weighted graphs with weights in  $[0,1]$ . This was motivated by noting that weighted graphs can be interpreted as graphons in the sense of Lovász and Szegedy [9] and thus we can approximate these with a convergent sequence of pseudo-random graphs.
In particular, we consider the problem of minimising  $\lambda_{n-1}$  over such weighted graphs, which by Weyl’s inequality (see [5] p.19), is analogous to the problem of maximising  $\lambda_{3}$ . By considering the effects of suitable edge operations, we are able to derive various structural results on the minimisers of  $\lambda_{n-1}$ . Namely, we prove that the extension of Question 1.4 to weighted graphs does not affect the desired constant in the following sense.
Theorem 1.7.
For  $n\geq 2$ , if  $\mathcal{W}_{n}$  denotes the set of weighted graphs on  $n$  vertices with weights in  $[0,1]$  and  $\mathcal{G}_{n}$  denotes the set of unweighted graphs on  $n$  vertices, then:
 $\min_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}\lambda_{n-1}(G)-2\sqrt{n}\leq\inf_{G\in\mathcal{W}_{n}}\lambda_{n-1}(G).$ 
Furthermore, we explore the existence of a minimiser of  $\lambda_{n-1}$  with simple final eigenvalue. Let  $\mathcal{S}_{n}$  denote the set of  $n\times n$  symmetric matrices with entries in  $[0,1]$ . We prove the following as part of Theorem 1.7.
Theorem 1.8.
If there exists a minimiser of  $\lambda_{n-1}$  over  $\mathcal{S}_{n}$  with simple final eigenvalue, then there exists an  $11\times 11$  matrix  $T$  with entries in  $\{0,1\}$  and a vertex multiplication  $\tilde{T}$  of  $T$  with:
 $\lambda_{n-1}(\tilde{T})-2\sqrt{n}\leq\inf_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\lambda_{n-1}(S).$ 
We conclude by providing some small structural results regarding minimisers of  $\lambda_{n-1}$  with repeated final eigenvalue.
2 Graphs with  $\lambda_{3}(G)=\frac{|V(G)|}{3}-1$ 
We construct a family of graphs which generalises the  $3K_{m}$  and  $C_{6}^{[m]}$  (the closed blowup of the 6-cycle, as described by Linz [7]) constructions, with third eigenvalue  $\frac{|V(G)|}{3}-1$ . The closed blow-ups of these graphs have third eigenvalue proportion converging to  $\frac{1}{3}$ .
A closed vertex multiplication of a graph  $G$  is constructed by replacing each vertex of  $G$  with a clique and each edge of  $G$  with a complete bipartite graph between the endpoint cliques. If the cliques are the same size, this reduces to the closed blow-up.
Definition 2.1.
Let  $H_{a,b}$  denote the closed vertex multiplication of  $C_{6}$  by  $[a,b,a,b,a,b]$ .
Figure 1:  $H_{4,0}$ ,  $H_{3,1}$  and  $H_{2,2}$  respectively.
In particular, we remark that  $H_{n,0}$  is isomorphic to  $3K_{n}$  and  $H_{n,n}$  is isomorphic to  $C_{6}^{[n]}$ .
Theorem 2.2.
We have  $\lambda_{3}(H_{a,b})=a+b-1=\frac{|V(H_{a,b})|}{3}-1$ . Hence,
 $\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\lambda_{3}(H_{a,b}^{[t]})}{3(a+b)t}=\frac{1}{3}$ .
Proof.
Add a self-loop to each vertex of  $H_{a,b}$ . Then, there exists an equitable partition of this graph given by the 6 cliques. The divisor matrix of this equitable partition is:
 $\begin{pmatrix}a&b&0&0&0&b\\
a&b&a&0&0&0\\
0&b&a&b&0&0\\
0&0&a&b&a&0\\
0&0&0&b&a&b\\
a&0&0&0&a&b\end{pmatrix}.$ 
Hence,  $A(H_{a,b})+I$  has spectrum:
 $\frac{a+b}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{a^{2}+14ab+b^{2}}}{2},a+b,a+b,0,\dots,0,\frac{a+b}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{a^{2}+14ab+b^{2}}}{2},$ 
and  $\lambda_{3}(H_{a,b})=a+b-1=\frac{|V(H_{a,b})|}{3}-1$ . Thus,  $\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\lambda_{3}(H_{a,b}^{[t]})}{3(a+b)t}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{(a+b)t-1}{3(a+b)t}=\frac{1}{3}$ .
∎
3 Bounds on the third eigenvalue of graphs of given type
In this section, we explore bounds on the third eigenvalue for various classes of graphs, proving Theorem 1.6. We start with strongly regular graphs.
3.1 Strongly regular graphs
Several known examples of graphs with large  $k$ -th eigenvalue proportion are strongly regular (see Linz [7]). Hence, we are motivated to demonstrate the following:
Theorem 3.1.
If  $G$  is a strongly regular graph with parameters  $(n,r,e,f)$  and  $n\geq 3$  then  $\lambda_{3}(G)<\frac{n}{3}$ .
We make use of the Theorem 3.6.7 from Cvetković, Rowlinson and Simić [5], stated as a lemma here:
Lemma 3.2 (Cvetković et al. [5]).
Let  $G$  be a strongly regular graph with parameters  $(n,r,e,f)$  with  $n\geq 3$ , such that both  $G$  and  $\bar{G}$  are connected. Then  $G$  has spectrum  $r,\lambda^{k},\mu^{l}$  for some  $r>\lambda>\mu$  and  $k,l\in\mathbb{N}$  with  $n=1+k+l$ . Furthermore:
 $n\leq\min\left\{\frac{k(k+3)}{2},\frac{l(l+3)}{2}\right\}.$ 
Theorem 3.1 now follows by simple casework.
Proof of Theorem 3.1.
By way of contradiction, suppose  $G$  is strongly regular with parameters  $(n,r,e,f)$  with  $n\geq 3$  and  $\lambda_{3}(G)\geq\frac{n}{3}$ .
If  $G$  is disconnected, then  $f=0$ . As  $r(r-e-1)=(n-r-1)f$  for strongly regular graphs, either  $r=0$  or  $r=e+1$ . In the former case,  $G\cong E_{n}$  has  $\lambda_{3}(G)=0<\frac{n}{3}$ . In the latter case, we have  $r+1\mid n$  and  $G\cong\frac{n}{r+1}K_{r+1}$ , so  $G$  has spectrum  $r^{n/(r+1)},(-1)^{nr/(r+1)}$ . For  $\lambda_{3}(G)\geq\frac{n}{3}$ , we require  $\frac{n}{r+1}\geq 3$ , but this gives  $\lambda_{3}(G)=r\leq\frac{n}{3}-1<\frac{n}{3}$ .
Similarly, if  $\bar{G}$  is disconnected, then  $G$  must be the  $m$ -fold blow-up of the complete graph  $K_{n/m}$ , where  $m\mid n$ . Thus,  $G$  has spectrum  $m(\frac{n}{m}-1),0^{\left(n-\frac{n}{m}\right)},(-m)^{\left(\frac{n}{m}-1\right)}$ . So,  $\lambda_{3}(G)=0<\frac{n}{3}$ .
Hence, we require  $G$  to be connected. By Lemma 3.2,  $G$  has spectrum  $r,\lambda^{k},\mu^{l}$ . Following the methods presented by Nikiforov [10], if  $A$  denotes the adjacency matrix of  $G$ , we have:
 $r^{2}+k\lambda^{2}\leq r^{2}+k\lambda^{2}+l\mu^{2}=\text{Tr}(A^{2})=nr,$ 
and therefore  $\lambda^{2}\leq\frac{n^{2}}{4k}$ . Hence, for  $\lambda_{3}(G)\geq\frac{n}{3}$  we require  $k\leq 2$ . By the second part of Lemma 3.2, we require  $n\leq 5$ . However, the only strongly regular graph with  $n\leq 5$  vertices for which  $G$  and  $\bar{G}$  are connected is  $C_{5}$ , but  $\lambda_{3}(C_{5})=\frac{-1+\sqrt{5}}{2}<\frac{5}{3}$ .
∎
3.2 Regular line graphs
It is well known that the line graph of a connected graph is regular if and only if  $G$  is regular or semi-regular bipartite (see Beineke and Bagga [2]). We consider these two cases separately and derive the following bounds.
Theorem 3.3.
If  $G$  is a regular graph, then  $\lambda_{3}(L(G))<\frac{|V(L(G))|}{3}$ .
Proof.
Suppose that  $G$  is  $r$ -regular on  $n$  vertices with  $q=\frac{1}{2}nr$  edges. Let  $M,N$  denote the adjacency matrices of  $G$  and  $L(G)$  respectively. Let  $B$  be the  $n\times q$  matrix indexed by  $V(G)\times E(G)$  such that:
 $B_{v,e}=\begin{cases}1&v\in e\\
0&v\not\in e.\end{cases}$ 
Then, note that  $BB^{T}=M+rI$  and  $B^{T}B=N+2I$ . As  $BB^{T}$  and  $B^{T}B$  share non-zero eigenvalues, we have  $\chi_{N}(t)=(t+2)^{m-n}\chi_{M}(t-r+2)$ . Thus,  $\lambda_{3}(N)$  is either  $-2$  or  $\lambda_{3}(M)+r-2$ . In any case
 $\frac{\lambda_{3}(L(G))}{|V(L(G))|}\leq\frac{2\lambda_{3}(M)+2r-4}{nr}<\frac{4}{n}.$ 
Hence, the result is immediate for  $n\geq 12$ . We can check the 797 line graphs of regular graphs on  $n\leq 11$  vertices.
∎
Theorem 3.4.
If  $G$  is a semi-regular bipartite graph, then  $\lambda_{3}(L(G))\leq\frac{|V(L(G))|}{3}$ .
Proof.
If  $G$  is a semi-regular bipartite graph with parameters  $(n_{1},n_{2},r_{1},r_{2})$  where  $n_{1}\geq n_{2}$ , then note that  $L(G)$  is  $(r_{1}+r_{2}-2)$ -regular on  $n_{1}r_{1}=n_{2}r_{2}$  vertices. Hence, we are done if  $\frac{n_{2}r_{2}}{3}\geq r_{1}+r_{2}-2$ . Namely, if  $n_{2}\geq 6$  then we are done as  $r_{1}+r_{2}-2<2r_{2}\leq\frac{n_{2}r_{2}}{3}$ .
If  $n_{2}=5$ , we are done unless  $r_{2}<\frac{3r_{1}-6}{2}$ . As  $r_{1}\leq 5$ , substituting into the previous inequality gives that  $r_{2}\leq 4$ . However, we require  $r_{2}<\frac{3r_{1}-6}{2}\leq\frac{3r_{2}-6}{2}$ , which gives the contradiction that  $r_{2}>6$ .
If  $n_{2}=4$ , we are done unless  $r_{2}<3r_{1}-6$ . As  $r_{1}\leq 4$ , we must have  $r_{2}\leq 5$ . Moreover,  $r_{2}\leq 3r_{2}-6$  implies that  $r_{2}\geq 3$ . There are only 7 possible sets of parameters:  $(5,4,4,5),(4,4,4,4),(8,4,2,4),(16,4,1,4),(4,4,3,3),(6,4,2,3),(12,4,1,3)$  and there are only 58 graphs with these parameters. We may check these manually and yield that each such line graph has third eigenvalue proportion  $\leq 0.202254248$ .
If  $n_{2}=3$ , then either  $G\cong 3K_{1,r_{2}}$ ,  $G\cong K_{3,n_{1}}$  or  $G$  is isomorphic to the closed vertex multiplication of  $C_{6}$  by  $[1,r_{2},1,r_{2},1,r_{2}]$ . In any case, we have  $\frac{\lambda_{3}(L(G))}{|V(L(G))|}\leq\frac{1}{3}$ .
If  $n_{2}=2$ , we immediately have either  $G\cong 2K_{1,r_{2}}$  or  $G\cong K_{2,n_{1}}$ . In the former case, we have  $\lambda_{3}(L(G))=\lambda_{3}(2K_{r_{2}})=-1$  and in the latter,  $\lambda_{3}(L(G))=\lambda_{3}(K_{n_{1}}\square P_{2})=0$ . Finally, if  $n_{2}=1$  we have  $G\cong K_{1,n_{1}}$ .
∎
3.3 Cayley graphs on abelian groups
Let  $\Gamma=(\Gamma,\cdot,e)$  be a group and  $S$  a subset of  $\Gamma$  such that  $e\not\in S$ ,  $S=S^{-1}$ . The Cayley graph  $G=Cay(\Gamma,S)$  is the graph with  $V(G)=\Gamma$  and  $s_{1}\sim s_{2}\iff s_{1}s_{2}^{-1}\in S$ . We prove the following result and later generalise it.
Theorem 3.5.
Suppose  $G=Cay(\Gamma,S)$  where  $\Gamma$  is abelian and is of order  $n$ . Then  $\frac{\lambda_{3}(G)}{n}\leq\frac{1}{3}$ .
In particular, this deals with the case of Paley graphs, which otherwise provide constructions for other extremal eigenvalue problems. We require the following three lemmas.
Lemma 3.6.
Let  $f(n)$  denote the maximum value of  $\operatorname{Re}(\sum_{\xi\in S}\xi)$  over all subsets  $S$  of the  $n$ -th roots of unity. Let  $\omega=\exp(2i\pi/n)$ . Then  $f(n)=\omega^{-\lfloor{n/4\rfloor}}+\omega^{-\lfloor{n/4\rfloor}+1}+\dots+\omega^{0}+\dots+\omega^{\lfloor{n/4\rfloor}}$ .
Proof.
Consider the  $n$ -th roots of unity on the complex plane. Then the ones with non-negative real part are those with argument in  $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$  hence correspond exactly to  $\{\omega^{-\lfloor{n/4\rfloor}},\omega^{-\lfloor{n/4\rfloor}+1},\dots,\omega^{0},\dots,\omega^{\lfloor{n/4\rfloor}}\}$ . Since  $\omega^{k}$  and  $\omega^{-k}$  are complex conjugates,  $\omega^{k}+\omega^{-k}$  is real and the sum of this subset is real, which by construction attains maximal real part.
∎
Lemma 3.7.
Let  $\omega=\exp(2i\pi/n)$ . Then  $i\frac{\omega+1}{\omega-1}=\cot(\pi/n)$ .
Proof.
We demonstrate a geometric proof.
Figure 2: Two parallelograms.
Construct  $\omega-1$  and  $\omega+1$  in the complex plane; we have parallelograms given by  $\{0,-1,\omega-1,\omega\}$  and  $\{0,1,\omega+1,\omega\}$ . Thus,  $\omega+1$  and  $\omega-1$  are the internal and external angle bisectors of  $\angle(\omega,1)$ . In particular,  $\angle(\omega-1,\omega+1)=\pi/2$ . Hence,  $i\frac{\omega+1}{\omega-1}=\frac{|\omega+1|}{|\omega-1|}=\cot(\pi/n)$ .
∎
Lemma 3.8.
For  $n\geq 3$ , we have  $f(n)\leq n/3$  with  $\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{n}=\frac{1}{\pi}$ .
Proof.
As a geometric series, we convert Lemma 3.6 to  $f(n)=-\omega^{\lfloor{n/4\rfloor}}\frac{\omega^{2\lfloor{n/4\rfloor}+1}-1}{\omega-1}$ . By computing  $f(n)$  for  $3\leq n\leq 150$ , we get  $f(n)\leq n/3$  with equality for  $n=3,6$ , hence we can restrict our focus to  $n>150$ .
Consider  $\delta_{n}=f(n)-\cot(\pi/n)=|\frac{\omega^{2\lfloor{n/4\rfloor}+1}-1}{\omega-1}|-|\frac{\omega^{n/2+1}-1}{\omega-1}|$ . By the triangle inequality,  $|\delta_{n}|\leq\frac{|\omega^{2\lfloor{n/4\rfloor}-n/2}-1|}{|\omega-1|}$ . Note that  $2\lfloor{n/4\rfloor}-n/2\in\{0,-1/2,-1,-3/2\}$ . Hence for  $n>150$ , by considering the distances between roots of unity on one quarter-arc of a circle, we get  $|\omega^{2\lfloor{n/4\rfloor}-n/2}-1|\leq|\omega^{2}-1|\leq|\omega^{2}-\omega|+|\omega-1|=2|\omega-1|$ . Thus,  $|\delta_{n}|\leq 2$ , hence we have  $\frac{f(n)}{n}\leq\frac{\cot(\pi/n)+2}{n}$  and
 $\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\cot(\pi/n)}{n}=\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\pi/n}{\tan(\pi/n)}=\frac{1}{\pi}.$ 
The function  $\frac{\cot(\pi/x)+2}{x}$  has derivative  $\frac{\pi\cot(\pi/x)^{2}+\pi-x\cot(\pi/x)-2x}{x^{3}}$ . Note further that  $\tan(x)>x$  for  $x\in(0,\pi/2)$ , hence  $\cot(\pi/x)<x/\pi$  for  $x>2$ . Now  $\pi y^{2}-xy=y(\pi y-x)<0$  for  $0<y<x/\pi$ , hence in particular  $\pi\cot(\pi/x)^{2}+\pi-x\cot(\pi/x)-2x<\pi-2x<0$  for  $x>2$  and our function is decreasing. We compute for  $x=150$  the value  $0.3315...<1/3$ , hence  $f(n)<n/3$  for  $n>150$  as well.
∎
We now turn to proving Theorem 3.5.
Proof of Theorem 3.5.
Since  $\Gamma$  is abelian, it is equipped with  $n$  irreducible characters, say  $\chi_{1},\chi_{2},\dots,\chi_{n}$ . Babai [1] gave the eigenvalues of  $G$  as
 $\lambda_{\chi_{i}}=\sum_{s\in S}\chi_{i}(s),\;i=1,2,\dots,n.$ 
Fix a character  $\chi$  and let  $d$  be the largest multiplicity within the multiset  $\chi(S)$ . Then  $\chi(\Gamma)$  is a finite subgroup of  $\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ , hence is exactly the  $k$ -th roots of unity for some positive integer  $k$ . As  $d$  is the largest multiplicity, there exists  $\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{d}\}\subset S$  with  $\chi(s_{i}s_{1}^{-1})=1$ , hence  $|\ker(\chi)|\geq d$ . By the First Isomorphism Theorem,  $|\ker(\chi)|=\frac{n}{|\text{Im}(\chi)|}$  which implies that  $d\leq\frac{n}{k}$ .
Eigenvalues of graphs are real. By partitioning the multiset  $\chi(S)$  into  $d$  subsets of distinct elements, each subset has real part at most  $f(k)$ , hence
 $\frac{\lambda_{\chi}}{n}\leq\frac{df(k)}{n}\leq\frac{n}{k}\cdot\frac{f(k)}{n}=\frac{f(k)}{k}.$ 
If  $k\geq 3$ , by Lemma 3.8 we have  $\frac{\lambda_{\chi}}{n}\leq\frac{1}{3}$ . If  $k=1$ , we necessarily have the principal character.
Now suppose two distinct characters  $\chi_{1},\chi_{2}$  have  $k=2$ . Their kernels are distinct index 2 subgroups of  $\Gamma$ , say  $H_{1},H_{2}$ . Now there exists  $x\in H_{1}\setminus H_{2}$ , hence  $H_{1}H_{2}=\Gamma$  and by the Second Isomorphism Theorem,  $|H_{1}/H_{1}\cap H_{2}|=|H_{1}H_{2}/H_{2}|=2$ , so  $H_{1}\cap H_{2}$  has index 4.
In particular,  $n/4$  elements of  $\Gamma$  have  $(\chi_{1}(g),\chi_{2}(g))=(+1,+1)$ , and identically for  $(+1,-1)$ ,  $(-1,+1)$ ,  $(-1,-1)$ . Thus,  $\lambda_{\chi_{1}}+\lambda_{\chi_{2}}=\sum_{s\in S}\chi_{1}(s)+\chi_{2}(s)\leq n/2$  and by pigeonhole principle, one of  $\lambda_{\chi_{1}},\lambda_{\chi_{2}}\leq n/4$ . In particular, at most one character with  $k=2$  can have  $\lambda>n/4$ , so  $\lambda_{3}(G)\leq n/3$  always.
∎
3.4 Semi-Cayley graphs on abelian groups
Let  $\Gamma$  be a group and  $R,S,T\subset\Gamma$  with  $R=R^{-1},S=S^{-1}$  and  $e\not\in R\cup S$ . The semi-Cayley graph  $SC(\Gamma;R,S,T)$  is the graph with  $V(G)=\Gamma\times\{0,1\}$  with  $(h,i)\sim(g,j)$  if and only if one of the following holds:
• 
 $i=j=0$  and  $gh^{-1}\in R$ ,
• 
 $i=j=1$  and  $gh^{-1}\in S$ ,
• 
 $i=0,j=1$  and  $gh^{-1}\in T$ .
Using similar techniques to above, we prove the following result.
Theorem 3.9.
Suppose  $G=SC(\Gamma;R,S,T)$  where  $\Gamma$  is abelian and is of order  $n$ . Then  $\frac{\lambda_{3}(G)}{2n}\leq\frac{1}{3}$ .
As before, we prove a lemma on an analogous function closely related to  $f(n)$ .
Lemma 3.10.
Let  $g(n)$  denote the maximum value of  $|\sum_{\xi\in S}\xi|$  over all subsets  $S$  of the  $n$ -th roots of unity. Then  $g(n)=\sqrt{f(n)^{2}+1}$  if  $4\mid n$ , otherwise  $g(n)=f(n)$ .
Proof.
Consider an arbitrary unit vector  $v$ . The maximal sum of projections of the  $n$ -th roots of unity onto  $v$  occurs exactly when you take those that differ by an acute angle. Indeed, given  $\{\xi^{1},\xi^{2},\dots,\xi^{n}\}$  are the roots of unity as vectors in  $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ , we have
 $\sum_{i=1}^{n}v\cdot\xi^{i}\leq\sum_{v\cdot\xi>0}v\cdot\xi.$ 
Note that this induces a subset of consecutive roots of unity. By Cauchy-Schwarz,
 $\left|v\cdot\sum_{v\cdot\xi>0}\xi\right|\leq|v|\cdot\left|\sum_{v\cdot\xi>0}\xi\right|=\left|\sum_{v\cdot\xi>0}\xi\right|$ 
and this sum of projections provides a lower bound on the magnitude of these roots of unity.
For any subset  $S$  of the roots of unity, we then have  $|\sum_{\xi\in S}\xi|$  is the length of its projection onto its own unit vector, which we bounded above by the magnitude of the sum of consecutive roots of unity. Thus, the maximum value of  $|\sum_{\xi\in S}\xi|$  is achieved by consecutive roots, where WLOG the start is 1 as rotation doesn’t affect magnitude.
Going clockwise around the unit circle and summing roots of unity, one constructs a regular  $n$ -gon. Thus, we equivalently want the maximal distance between two vertices of a regular  $n$ -gon with unit side length.
Construct its circumcircle, then the distance is increasing until the antipode then decreasing after. Hence, we require the vertices to be  $\lfloor{n/2\rfloor}$  or  $\lceil{n/2\rceil}$  apart and  $g(n)$  is the magnitude of the sum of either  $\lfloor{n/2\rfloor}$  or  $\lceil{n/2\rceil}$  consecutive roots of unity. Considering cases on  $n\pmod{4}$ , we get the desired form.
∎
As in the proof of Theorem 3.5, we start with an analysis of characters for which  $k\geq 3$  and then consider  $k=2,1$ .
Proof of Theorem 3.9.
Since  $\Gamma$  is abelian, we have  $n$  irreducible characters  $\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{n}$ . Gao and Luo [6] proved that the eigenvalues of  $G$  corresponding to character  $\chi$  are given by
 $\lambda_{\chi}=\frac{\lambda_{\chi}^{R}+\lambda_{\chi}^{S}\pm\sqrt{(\lambda_{\chi}^{R}-\lambda_{\chi}^{S})^{2}+4|\lambda_{\chi}^{T}|^{2}}}{2}$ 
where  $\lambda_{\chi}^{R}=\sum_{r\in R}\chi(r)$  is the eigenvalue of  $Cay(\Gamma,R)$  corresponding to  $\chi$  and identically for  $S$ . Since  $R=R^{-1}$ ,  $S=S^{-1}$ ,  $\lambda_{\chi}^{R}$  and  $\lambda_{\chi}^{S}$  are necessarily real. We also define the analogue  $\lambda_{\chi}^{T}=\sum_{t\in T}\chi(t)$  for  $T$ , where  $\lambda_{\chi}^{T}$  need not be real.
Running the same multiplicity argument as in Theorem 3.5 and generalising Lemma 3.8 to negative sums, we get  $|\lambda_{\chi}^{R}|,|\lambda_{\chi}^{S}|\leq\frac{nf(k)}{k}$ . Analogously, by partitioning  $\chi(T)$  into subsets  $T_{1},\dots,T_{d}$  with distinct elements and noting that  $|\sum_{t\in T}\chi(t)|\leq|\sum_{t\in T_{1}}\chi(t)|+\dots+|\sum_{t\in T_{d}}\chi(t)|$  by the triangle inequality, Lemma 3.10 gives  $|\lambda_{\chi}^{T}|\leq\frac{ng(k)}{k}$ . Taking the larger root in (*), we seek to bound from above  $\lambda_{\chi}$  given these constraints.
Note that  $\lambda_{\chi}$  is increasing in  $|\lambda_{\chi}^{T}|$ . But we also have the derivative with respect to  $\lambda_{\chi}^{R}$  is  $\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{\lambda_{\chi}^{R}-\lambda_{\chi}^{S}}{\sqrt{(\lambda_{\chi}^{R}-\lambda_{\chi}^{S})^{2}+4|\lambda_{\chi}^{T}|^{2}}})>0$  since  $|\lambda_{\chi}^{R}-\lambda_{\chi}^{S}|\leq\sqrt{(\lambda_{\chi}^{R}-\lambda_{\chi}^{S})^{2}+4|\lambda_{\chi}^{T}|^{2}}$ . Thus, by symmetry  $\lambda_{\chi}$  is also increasing in  $\lambda_{\chi}^{R}$  and  $\lambda_{\chi}^{S}$ . In particular,
 $\lambda_{\chi}\leq\frac{2\frac{nf(k)}{k}+2\frac{ng(k)}{k}}{2}$ 
and therefore
 $\frac{\lambda_{\chi}}{2n}\leq\frac{f(k)+g(k)}{2k}.$ 
For  $k\geq 3$  and  $4\nmid k$ , this gives  $\frac{\lambda_{\chi}}{2n}\leq\frac{f(k)}{k}\leq\frac{1}{3}$  as before. For  $k\geq 3$  and  $4\mid k$  note that we get the equality  $2\lfloor{n/4\rfloor}-n/2=0$  hence  $\delta_{n}=0$  in the proof of Lemma 3.8. Thus  $f(k)=\cot(\pi/k)\leq k/\pi$ . In particular,  $\frac{f(k)+g(k)}{2k}\leq\frac{k/\pi+\sqrt{(k/\pi)^{2}+1}}{2k}=\frac{1+\sqrt{1+(\pi/k)^{2}}}{2\pi}$ . This is a decreasing function in  $k$  and is less than  $1/3$  for  $k=8$ . By direct computation, this also holds for  $k=4$ , hence for  $k\geq 3$  in general we again obtain  $\frac{\lambda_{\chi}}{2n}\leq\frac{1}{3}$ .
To control  $k=2$ , we first bound  $\lambda_{\chi}$  in two cases:
1. 
Case 1:  $|\lambda_{\chi}^{T}|\leq n/6$ . Then note that  $(\lambda_{\chi}^{R}-\lambda_{\chi}^{S})^{2}+4|\lambda_{\chi}^{T}|^{2}\leq(|\lambda_{\chi}^{R}-\lambda_{\chi}^{S}|+2|\lambda_{\chi}^{T}|)^{2}$  hence
 $\lambda_{\chi}\leq\max(\lambda_{\chi}^{R},\lambda_{\chi}^{S})+|\lambda_{\chi}^{T}|\leq\frac{n}{2}+\frac{n}{6}=\frac{2n}{3}.$ 
2. 
Case 2:  $|\lambda_{\chi}^{T}|\leq n/3$  and  $|\lambda_{\chi}^{R}-\lambda_{\chi}^{S}|\geq n/2$ . Let  $|\lambda_{\chi}^{R}-\lambda_{\chi}^{S}|=n/2+t$ . Then because exactly  $n/2$  elements of  $\Gamma$  have  $\chi(g)=\pm 1$  each,  $|\lambda_{\chi}^{R}|,|\lambda_{\chi}^{S}|\leq n/2$  hence WLOG  $\lambda_{\chi}^{R}\geq 0$  and  $\lambda_{\chi}^{S}\leq-t$ . Then
 $\lambda_{\chi}\leq\frac{n}{4}-\frac{t}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{n}{2}+t\right)^{2}+\frac{n^{2}}{9}}$ 
which has a maximum at  $t=0$ , giving  $\frac{2n}{3}$ .
Consider two characters  $\chi_{1},\chi_{2}$  with  $k=2$ . As in the proof of Theorem 3.5,  $\lambda_{\chi_{1}}^{R}+\lambda_{\chi_{2}}^{R}\leq n/2$ ,  $\lambda_{\chi_{1}}^{S}+\lambda_{\chi_{2}}^{S}\leq n/2$  and individually they’re bounded by  $n/2$  as well.
We also claim that  $|\lambda_{\chi_{1}}^{T}|+|\lambda_{\chi_{2}}^{T}|\leq n/2$ . Indeed, suppose there are  $p$  elements of  $T$  satisfying  $(\chi_{1}(t),\chi_{2}(t))=(+1,+1)$ . Analogously, define  $q,r,s$  for  $(+1,-1),(-1,+1),(-1,-1)$  respectively. Then we get  $|p+q-r-s|+|p-q+r-s|=2u-2v\leq 2u\leq n/2$  for some  $u,v\subset\{p,q,r,s\}$ , where  $0\leq p,q,r,s\leq n/4$ . Thus, if we let  $|\lambda_{\chi_{1}}^{T}|=y$ , we have  $|\lambda_{\chi_{2}}^{T}|\leq n/2-y$ .
As before, we prove that at least one of  $k=2$  gives  $\lambda_{\chi}\leq\frac{2n}{3}$  via pigeonhole. Note that the smaller eigenvalue for each  $\chi$  is bounded above by  $\frac{n/2+n/2}{2}=n/2$ , hence it suffices to consider only the larger one.
- 
If  $|\lambda_{\chi}^{R}-\lambda_{\chi}^{S}|\leq n/2$  holds for both, we obtain
 $\lambda_{\chi_{1}}+\lambda_{\chi_{2}}\leq\frac{n+\sqrt{(n/2)^{2}+4y^{2}}+\sqrt{(n/2)^{2}+4(n/2-y)^{2}}}{2}.$ 
Regarding as a function of  $y$ , we get a maximum at  $y=0$  (or  $y=n/2$ ) where we get  $\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{4}n<\frac{4n}{3}$ .
- 
If  $|\lambda_{\chi}^{R}-\lambda_{\chi}^{S}|\geq n/2$  holds for both, then we either have both satisfying  $|\lambda_{\chi}^{T}|\leq n/3$ , or one yields  $|\lambda_{\chi}^{T}|>n/3$  and the other  $|\lambda_{\chi}^{T}|<n/6$ , since  $|\lambda_{\chi_{1}}^{T}|+|\lambda_{\chi_{2}}^{T}|\leq n/2$ . In the former case, both are Case 2, and in the latter, we have one of each of Case 1, Case 2. Thus  $\lambda_{\chi_{1}}+\lambda_{\chi_{2}}\leq\frac{4n}{3}$ .
- 
If one of each, suppose WLOG  $|\lambda_{\chi_{1}}^{R}-\lambda_{\chi_{1}}^{S}|=n/2+t>n/2$ . Then as in Case 2 we have say  $\lambda_{\chi_{1}}^{R}\geq 0$  and  $\lambda_{\chi_{1}}^{S}\leq-t$ , hence  $\lambda_{\chi_{1}}^{S}+\lambda_{\chi_{2}}^{S}\leq n/2-t$ . Combined with  $\lambda_{\chi_{1}}^{R}+\lambda_{\chi_{2}}^{R}\leq n/2$ , we get
 $\lambda_{\chi_{1}}+\lambda_{\chi_{2}}\leq\frac{(n-t)+\sqrt{(n/2+t)^{2}+4y^{2}}+\sqrt{(n/2)^{2}+4(n/2-y)^{2}}}{2}$ 
over  $0\leq t\leq n/2$ ,  $0\leq y\leq n/2$ . The derivative with respect to  $t$  is  $\frac{1}{2}(-1+\frac{n/2+t}{\sqrt{(n/2+t)^{2}+4y^{2}}})$  which is always non-positive. Thus, maximum lies at  $t=0$ , reducing to (*) where  $\lambda_{\chi_{1}}+\lambda_{\chi_{2}}\leq\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{4}n<\frac{4n}{3}$ .
The final case to eliminate is if both eigenvalues for  $k=1$ , which are  $\frac{|R|+|S|\pm\sqrt{(|R|-|S|)^{2}+4|T|^{2}}}{2}$  and one for  $k=2$  yield  $\lambda_{\chi}>\frac{2n}{3}$ . Let  $\chi$  correspond to  $k=2$ . Let  $\frac{|R|+|S|}{2}=\frac{2n}{3}+y$ , then smaller eigenvalue greater than  $\frac{2n}{3}$  means  $\sqrt{(|R|-|S|)^{2}+4|T|^{2}}<2y$  and  $|T|<y$ . Note also  $|R|+|S|\leq 2n$  so  $y\leq n/3$ . If we further have  $y\leq n/6$  then this falls in Case 1, else if  $n/6\leq y\leq n/3$  with  $|\lambda_{\chi}^{R}-\lambda_{\chi}^{S}|\geq n/2$  we get Case 2. Otherwise,  $y>n/6$  and  $|\lambda_{\chi}^{R}-\lambda_{\chi}^{S}|<n/2$ .
Since there are  $n/2$  of each of  $\pm 1$  in  $\chi(\Gamma)$ , if  $|R|=n/2+r$  then necessarily  $\lambda_{\chi}^{R}\leq n/2-r$  and similarly for  $S$ . In particular,  $|R|+|S|=\frac{4n}{3}+2y$  and therefore  $\lambda_{\chi}^{R}+\lambda_{\chi}^{S}\leq n-(n/3+2y)=\frac{2n}{3}-2y$ . Hence altogether we have
 $\lambda_{\chi}\leq\frac{n}{3}-y+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(\lambda_{\chi}^{R}-\lambda_{\chi}^{S})^{2}+4|\lambda_{\chi}^{T}|^{2}}\leq\frac{n}{3}-y+\frac{n}{4}+y\leq\frac{2n}{3}.\qed$ 
Gao and Luo [6] note that Cayley graphs of the dihedral,  $D_{2n}$ , and dicyclic,  $Q_{4n}$ , groups are semi-Cayley graphs. Hence, we can apply the above result to these non-abelian groups.
Corollary 3.11.
Suppose  $G=Cay(\Gamma,S)$  with  $\Gamma$  of order  $n$  and either dihedral or dicyclic. Then  $\frac{\lambda_{3}(G)}{n}\leq\frac{1}{3}$ .
3.5 Vertex-transitive graphs
Using the above result on Cayley graphs on abelian groups, we can generalise to vertex-transitive graphs with abelian automorphism group, as demonstrated by Lovász [8].
Corollary 3.12.
Let  $G$  be a vertex-transitive graph of order  $n$  with abelian automorphism group  $\Gamma$ . Then  $\frac{\lambda_{3}}{n}\leq\frac{1}{3}$ .
Proof.
Lovász [8] proved that the lexicographical product of  $G$  and the empty graph on  $|\Gamma|/n$  vertices yields a Cayley graph on  $\Gamma$ , say  $G^{\prime}$ . It has adjacency matrix  $A(G^{\prime})=A(G)\otimes J_{|\Gamma|/n}$ , hence has spectrum  $\frac{|\Gamma|}{n}\lambda_{1},\dots,\frac{|\Gamma|}{n}\lambda_{n},0,\dots,0$ . If  $\lambda_{3}<0$ , we immediately get the conclusion and otherwise we have  $\lambda_{3}(G^{\prime})=\frac{|\Gamma|}{n}\lambda_{3}$ . By Theorem 3.5, we get  $\frac{\lambda_{3}}{n}=\frac{|\Gamma|}{n}\frac{\lambda_{3}}{|\Gamma|}\leq\frac{1}{3}$ .
∎
3.6 Pivalous graphs
In an attempt to generalise the pattern of the  $3K_{m}$  and  $C_{6}^{[m]}$  constructions, we have found a family of graphs with surprising third eigenvalue proportion.
Definition 3.13.
Let  $Pi_{n}$  denote the graph with  $V(G)=\{1,2,\dots,n\}$  and  $i\sim j\iff 0\leq i-j\leq\lfloor{\frac{n}{4}\rfloor}\pmod{n}$  or  $0\leq j-i\leq\lfloor{\frac{n}{4}\rfloor}\pmod{n}$ . We call these the ‘Pivalous’ graphs.
Figure 3: Examples of Pivalous graphs. From left to right:  $Pi_{3}$ ,  $Pi_{6}$ ,  $Pi_{10}$ ,  $Pi_{20}$ ,  $Pi_{30}$ .
This family of graphs produces circulant graphs, hence Cayley graphs on an abelian group. Note that  $Pi_{3}=3K_{1}$  and  $Pi_{5},Pi_{6},Pi_{7}$  are  $C_{5},C_{6},C_{7}$  respectively. In the background of the proof of Theorem 3.5, this represents the equality case.
Theorem 3.14.
The (unordered) spectrum of  $Pi_{n}$  asymptotically grows on the order of:
 $\frac{n}{2},+\cot\frac{\pi}{n},-1,-\cot\frac{3\pi}{n},+1,+\cot\frac{5\pi}{n},-1,\dots$ 
In particular,  $\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\lambda_{3}(Pi_{n})}{n}\to\frac{1}{\pi}$ .
Proof.
Note that  $Pi_{n}=Cay(\mathbb{Z}_{n},\{-\lfloor{\frac{n}{4}\rfloor},\dots,\lfloor{\frac{n}{4}\rfloor}\})$ . The characters of  $\mathbb{Z}_{n}$  are precisely  $\chi_{i}(a)=\exp(2i\pi a/n)$ . Hence, the eigenvalues of  $Pi_{n}$  are  $(\omega^{t})^{-\lfloor{n/4\rfloor}}+(\omega^{t})^{-\lfloor{n/4\rfloor}+1}+\dots+(\omega^{t})^{-1}+(\omega^{t})^{1}+\dots+(\omega^{t})^{\lfloor{n/4\rfloor}}$  for  $t=0,1,\dots,n-1$ . For simplification, we work with  $4\mid n$  and add a self-loop at each vertex, which increases every eigenvalue by 1 and hence doesn’t affect overall growth.
First,  $\lambda_{1}=n/2+1$  represents the regularity. Otherwise, we have  $\lambda=(\omega^{t})^{-n/4}\frac{(\omega^{t})^{n/2+1}-1}{\omega^{t}-1}$ . We now consider cases on  $t\pmod{4}$ .
0: 
 $\lambda=\frac{\omega^{t}-1}{\omega^{t}-1}=+1$ .
1: 
 $\lambda=-i\frac{-\omega^{t}-1}{\omega^{t}-1}=i\frac{\omega^{t}+1}{\omega^{t}-1}=+\cot(\frac{t\pi}{n})$  by Lemma 3.7.
2: 
 $\lambda=-\frac{\omega^{t}-1}{\omega^{t}-1}=-1$ .
3: 
 $\lambda=i\frac{-\omega^{t}-1}{\omega^{t}-1}=-\cot(\frac{t\pi}{n})$ .
Note that we get  $+\cot(\frac{\pi}{n})=-\cot(\frac{(n-1)\pi}{n})$ . These are the second and third eigenvalues by size comparison to the rest, hence  $\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\lambda_{3}(Pi_{n})}{n}\to\frac{1}{\pi}$ .
∎

4 Extremising  $\lambda_{n-1}$  over weighted graphs
In this section, we consider an extension of the main problem as follows.
Question 4.1.
Let  $\mathcal{S}_{n}$  denote the set of  $n\times n$  real symmetric matrices with entries in the interval  $[0,1]$ . Which matrices in  $\mathcal{S}_{n}$  have extremal  $k$ -th eigenvalue; what are these extremal  $k$ -th eigenvalues?
In other words, we consider the extension of the problem to weighted undirected graphs (possibly with loops) with weights in  $[0,1]$ . Remark that the function  $\lambda_{k}:\mathcal{S}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}$  is well defined and if we equip  $\mathcal{S}_{n}$  with the metric induced by the operator norm  $||\cdot||_{\text{opp}}$  on  $n\times n$  matrices, Weyl’s inequality gives:
 $|\lambda_{k}(M)-\lambda_{k}(N)|\leq||M-N||_{\text{opp}}.$ 
Thus,  $\lambda_{k}$  is continuous. As  $\mathcal{S}_{n}$  is compact,  $\lambda_{k}$  is bounded and attains its extrema, so the question is well-posed.
Rather than considering the problem of maximising  $\lambda_{3}$  directly, it is simpler to instead consider the problem of minimising  $\lambda_{n-1}$ . By Weyl’s inequality we have:
 $\lambda_{3}(M)+\lambda_{n-1}(J-I-M)\leq\lambda_{2}(J-I)=-1,$ 
and thus,
 $\sup_{M\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\lambda_{3}(M)\leq-1-\inf_{M\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\lambda_{n-1}(M).$ 
With this in mind, we conjecture the following:
Conjecture 4.2.
The function  $\lambda_{n-1}:\mathcal{S}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}$  satisfies:
 $-\frac{n}{3}\leq\inf_{M\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\lambda_{n-1}(M).$ 
In this section, we make progress to answering Question 4.1 for  $k=n-1$  and motivate Conjecture 4.2. Firstly, we remark the following lemma, similar to that of Theorem 1.2 of Csikvári [4].
Lemma 4.3.
Suppose that there exists some  $\kappa\in\mathbb{R}$  and  $f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}$  with  $\lambda_{3}(G)\leq\kappa|V(G)|+f(|V(G)|)$  for all simple graphs  $G$ . Then, if  $\frac{f(n)}{n}\to 0\text{ as }n\to\infty$ , for all simple graphs  $G$ :
 $\lambda_{3}(G)\leq\kappa|V(G)|-1.$ 
Proof.
Suppose by way of contradiction there exists some  $H$  with  $\lambda_{3}(H)=\kappa|V(H)|-1+\varepsilon$  with  $\varepsilon>0$ . Then, the closed-blowup  $H^{[t]}$  of  $H$  satisfies:
 $\kappa t|V(H)|+\varepsilon t-1=t(\lambda_{3}(H)+1)-1\leq\lambda_{3}(H^{[t]})\leq\kappa t|V(H)|+f(t|V(H)|)$ 
Thus,  $\varepsilon\leq\frac{f(t|V(H)|)+1}{t}$  for all  $t\geq 0$ . But, as  $t\to\infty$ ,  $\frac{f(t|V(H)|)+1}{t}=\frac{f(t|V(H)|)+1}{t|V(H)|}\cdot|V(H)|\to 0$ , contradicting that  $\varepsilon>0$ .
∎
4.1 Implications of the existence of an extremiser of  $\lambda_{n-1}$  with  $\lambda_{n}$  simple
As previously remarked, the infimum of  $\lambda_{n-1}$  is attained at some matrix  $M\in\mathcal{S}_{n}$ . Suppose that there exists such a minimiser with  $\lambda_{n}(M)<\lambda_{n-1}(M)$ , i.e. with  $\lambda_{n}$  simple. We explore its structure should such a matrix exist.
Definition 4.4.
Let a simple minimiser denote a matrix  $M\in\mathcal{S}_{n}$  with  $\lambda_{n}(M)<\lambda_{n-1}(M)$  such that  $\forall M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{n},\lambda_{n-1}(M)\leq\lambda_{n-1}(M^{\prime})$ .
Theorem 4.5.
Suppose  $M\in\mathcal{S}_{n}$  is a simple minimiser. Then, up to reordering rows and columns:
 $M=\begin{pmatrix}O_{P}&J_{P,N}&X\\
J_{N,P}&O_{N}&Y\\
X^{T}&Y^{T}&W\end{pmatrix}$ 
where  $O_{m}$  denotes the  $m\times m$  matrix of zeros,  $J_{m,l}$  denotes the  $m\times l$  matrix of ones and  $X,Y,W$  are  $P\times Z$ ,  $N\times Z$  and  $Z\times Z$  matrices with  $[0,1]$  entries respectively, with  $\frac{1}{\sqrt{P}}X^{T}\mathbb{j}_{P}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}Y^{T}\mathbb{j}_{N}$ . If  $\mathbb{w}$  denotes the eigenvector of  $M$  corresponding to  $\lambda_{n-1}$ , then  $\mathbb{w}^{T}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\mathbb{j}_{P}^{T}}{\sqrt{2P}}&-\frac{\mathbb{j}_{N}^{T}}{\sqrt{2N}}&\mathbb{0}_{Z}^{T}\end{pmatrix}$  and  $\lambda_{n-1}=-\sqrt{PN}$ .
Proof.
Suppose that  $M$  is a simple minimiser with eigenvalues  $\lambda_{n}(M)<\lambda_{n-1}(M)$  and corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors  $\mathbb{v,w}$ . Suppose that  $E$  is an  $n\times n$  matrix such that  $\exists\eta>0$  with  $M+\varepsilon E\in\mathcal{S}_{n}$  we have  $\forall 0<\varepsilon<\eta$ . Then, by the min-max principle:
 $\displaystyle\lambda_{n-1}(M+\varepsilon E)-\lambda_{n-1}(M)$ 
 $\displaystyle\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbb{x}\in\braket{\mathbb{v,w}}\\
||\mathbb{x}||=1\end{subarray}}\left(\mathbb{x}^{T}(M+\varepsilon E)\mathbb{x}\right)-\lambda_{n-1}(M)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in[-1,1]\\
\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}=1\end{subarray}}\left(\alpha^{2}(\lambda_{n}(M)-\lambda_{n-1}(M))+\varepsilon(\alpha\mathbb{v}+\beta\mathbb{w})^{T}E(\alpha\mathbb{v}+\beta\mathbb{w})\right)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\max_{\alpha\in[-1,1]}\left(\alpha^{2}(\lambda_{n}(M)-\lambda_{n-1}(M)+\varepsilon(S-R))+2\varepsilon\alpha\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}Q+\varepsilon R\right),$

 $\displaystyle\lambda_{n-1}(M+\varepsilon E)-\lambda_{n-1}(M)$

Let  $W_{+}=\{i:w_{i}>0\}$ ,  $W_{0}=\{i:w_{i}=0\}$  and  $W_{-}=\{i:w_{i}<0\}$  and let  $P=|W_{+}|$ ,  $N=|W_{-}|$  and  $Z=|W_{0}|$ .  $E_{xy}=\begin{cases}-1&\{x,y\}=\{i,j\}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$

 $\displaystyle J_{P,N}\mathbb{w}_{N}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\lambda_{n-1}\mathbb{w}_{P}$ 
 $\displaystyle J_{N,P}\mathbb{w}_{P}$

4.2 Extremisers of  $\lambda_{n-1}$  with  $\lambda_{n}$  repeated
Throughout this section, we assume that all minimisers  $M$  of  $\lambda_{n-1}$  have  $\lambda_{n}(M)=\lambda_{n-1}(M)$  (otherwise, we apply the results of the previous section). We give some tools to analyse the structure of such weighted graphs.
Lemma 4.8.
Let  $M$  be a minimiser of  $\lambda_{n-1}$  with  $\lambda_{n}(M)=\lambda_{n-1}(M)$  and  $\mathbb{v,w}$  corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors. If there exists some symmetric  $E$  such that  $\mathbb{x}^{T}E\mathbb{x}\leq 0$  for each  $\mathbb{x}\in\braket{\mathbb{v,w}}$ , then  $\lambda_{n-1}(M+E)=\lambda_{n-1}(M)$  and if  $n\geq 5$ :
 $E\mathbb{v}=E\mathbb{w}=\mathbb{0}.$ 
Proof.
Similar to Theorem 4.5, using min-max we have:
 $\displaystyle 0\leq\lambda_{n-1}(M+E)-\lambda_{n-1}(M)$ 
 $\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbb{x}\in\braket{\mathbb{v},\mathbb{w}}\\
||\mathbb{x}||=1\end{subarray}}(\mathbb{x}^{T}(M+E)\mathbb{x})-\lambda_{n-1}(M)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbb{x}\in\braket{\mathbb{v},\mathbb{w}}\\
||\mathbb{x}||=1\end{subarray}}(\mathbb{x}^{T}E\mathbb{x})\leq 0.$ 
We hence require that  $\lambda_{n-1}(M+E)=\lambda_{n-1}(M)$ , so  $M+E$  is also a minimiser of  $\lambda_{n-1}$  and by the assumption at the beginning of this section,  $\lambda_{n}(M+E)=\lambda_{n-1}(M+E)$ .
If  $\lambda_{n-2}(M+E)=\lambda_{n-1}(M+E)$  also, then:
 $3(\lambda_{n-1}(M+E))^{2}\leq\text{Tr}((M+E)^{2})-(\lambda_{1}(M+E))^{2}\leq\text{Tr}((M+E)^{2})-\left(\frac{\text{Tr}(M+E)}{n}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{n^{2}}{4}.$ 
Thus if  $n\geq 15$ , we have  $\lambda_{n-1}(M+E)\geq-\frac{n}{2\sqrt{3}}>-\frac{n-2}{3}$ . But, the complements of  $H_{a,b}$  have  $\lambda_{n-1}(\overline{H_{a,b}})=-\frac{|V(\overline{H_{a,b}})|}{3}$ , contradict the extremality of  $\lambda_{n-1}(M+E)$ . The authors have also verified for  $5\leq n\leq 14$ , there exists weighted graphs with penultimate eigenvalue lower than  $-\frac{n}{2\sqrt{3}}$ .
Thus,  $\lambda_{n-1}(M+E)<\lambda_{n-2}(M+E)$ .
We also require:
 $\lambda_{n-1}(M+E)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbb{x}\in\braket{\mathbb{v},\mathbb{w}}\\
||\mathbb{x}||=1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{x}^{T}(M+E)\mathbb{x}.$ 
(*)
Let  $\tilde{\mathbb{v}}_{n},\tilde{\mathbb{v}}_{n-1},\dots,\tilde{\mathbb{v}}_{1}$  be orthonormal eigenvectors of  $M+E$ , with corresponding eigenvalues  $\tilde{\lambda}_{n}=\tilde{\lambda}_{n-1},\dots\tilde{\lambda}_{1}$ . Then, consider the vector space  $V=\braket{\mathbb{v,w}}\cap\braket{\mathbb{\tilde{v}}_{1},\dots\mathbb{\tilde{v}}_{n-1}}$ . Note that  $V$  has dimension at least 1, hence there exists some  $\mathbb{z}\in V$  with  $||\mathbb{z}||=1$ . Thus, if  $\mathbb{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_{i}\mathbb{\tilde{v}}_{i}$ , then:
 $\mathbb{x}^{T}(M+E)\mathbb{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\tilde{\lambda}_{i}a_{i}^{2}\geq\tilde{\lambda}_{n-1},$ 
with equality iff  $\mathbb{x}=\pm\mathbb{\tilde{v}}_{n-1}$ . However,  $\mathbb{x}^{T}(M+E)\mathbb{x}\leq{\lambda}_{n-1}(M+E)$  by ( $*$ ), so  $\tilde{\mathbb{v}}_{n-1}\in\braket{\mathbb{v,w}}$ . Running an identical argument but replacing  $\mathbb{\tilde{v}}_{n-1}$  with  $\mathbb{\tilde{v}}_{n}$  yields that  $\tilde{\mathbb{v}}_{n}\in\braket{\mathbb{v,w}}$  and therefore  $\braket{\mathbb{v,w}}=\braket{\tilde{\mathbb{v}}_{n},\tilde{\mathbb{v}}_{n-1}}$ . Thus,  $E\mathbb{v}=E\mathbb{w}=\mathbb{0}$  follows immediately.
∎
We also have the following restatement of Sylvester’s criterion.
Lemma 4.9.
Let  $\mathbb{v,w}$  be orthonormal vectors and define  $S=\mathbb{v}^{T}E\mathbb{v},Q=\mathbb{v}^{T}E\mathbb{w},R=\mathbb{w}^{T}E\mathbb{w}$ . Then,  $\mathbb{x}^{T}E\mathbb{x}\leq 0$  for all  $\mathbb{x}\in\braket{\mathbb{v,w}}$  iff  $RS\geq Q^{2}$  and  $R\leq 0$ ,  $S\leq 0$ .
Proof.
Remark that:
 $\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbb{x}\in\braket{\mathbb{v,w}}\\
||\mathbb{x}||=1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{x}^{T}E\mathbb{x}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\\
\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}=1\end{subarray}}(S\alpha^{2}+2Q\alpha\beta+R\beta^{2}).$ 
If  $S\neq 0$ , completing the square gives:
 $\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbb{x}\in\braket{\mathbb{v,w}}\\
||\mathbb{x}||=1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{x}^{T}E\mathbb{x}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\\
\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}=1\end{subarray}}\left(S\left(\alpha+\frac{Q}{S}\beta\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{RS-Q^{2}}{S}\right)\beta^{2}\right),$ 
which is less than or equal to  $0$  if and only if  $S<0$  and  $RS\geq Q^{2}$  (implying that  $R\leq 0$ ). Identical logic applies when  $R\neq 0$ .
Finally, if  $R=S=0$ , then:
 $\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbb{x}\in\braket{\mathbb{v,w}}\\
||\mathbb{x}||=1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{x}^{T}E\mathbb{x}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\\
\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}=1\end{subarray}}(2Q\alpha\beta)=|Q|,$ 
which is less than or equal to zero iff  $0=RS\geq Q^{2}$ .
∎
Applying Lemma 4.8 and Lemma 4.9 for various choices of matrix  $E$  give rise to various structural properties of  $M$ .
Lemma 4.10.
If  $M$  is a minimiser of  $\lambda_{n-1}$ , then the matrix formed by setting the diagonal entries of  $M$  to 0 is also a minimiser of  $\lambda_{n-1}$ .
Proof.
Suppose  $M$  is a minimiser of  $\lambda_{n-1}$  and some diagonal entry of  $M$  is non-zero. Then, consider the matrix:
 $E_{xy}=\begin{cases}-M_{ii}&x=y\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$ 
 $M+E$  is precisely the matrix formed by setting the diagonal entries of  $M$  to 0. Using the notation from Lemma 4.9,  $S=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{ii}v_{i}^{2}\leq 0$ ,  $R=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{ii}w_{i}^{2}\leq 0$  and  $Q=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{ii}v_{i}w_{i}$ . Applying Cauchy-Schwarz yields  $RS\geq Q^{2}$ . The first part of Lemma 4.8 thus gives  $\lambda_{n-1}(M+E)=\lambda_{n-1}(M)$ , so  $M+E$  is also a minimiser of  $\lambda_{n-1}$ .
∎
Lemma 4.11.
There exists a minimiser  $M$  of  $\lambda_{n-1}$  with at most 1 entry of each row and column not  $0$  nor  $1$ .
Proof.
Let  $M$  be a minimiser of  $\lambda_{n-1}$  with diagonal entries all zero and the least number of entries that are not  $0$  nor  $1$ . Suppose by way of contradiction some row (or equivalently column) of  $M$  has at least 2 entries  $M_{ab},M_{ac}\in(0,1)$  for some  $a,b,c$  distinct. Consider symmetric matrices  $E$  with 0 entries everywhere other than at  $E_{ab},E_{ac},E_{bc}$  (and  $E_{ba}$ ,  $E_{ca}$ ,  $E_{cb}$ ). Then, we have that:
 $\displaystyle S$ 
 $\displaystyle=2(E_{ab}v_{a}v_{b}+E_{ac}v_{a}v_{c}+E_{bc}v_{b}v_{c})$ 
 $\displaystyle Q$ 
 $\displaystyle=E_{ab}(v_{a}w_{b}+w_{a}v_{b})+E_{ac}(v_{a}w_{c}+w_{a}v_{c})+E_{bc}(v_{b}w_{c}+w_{b}v_{c})$ 
 $\displaystyle R$ 
 $\displaystyle=2(E_{ab}w_{a}w_{b}+E_{ac}w_{a}w_{c}+E_{bc}w_{b}w_{c}).$ 
Define  $[x,y]$  as  $v_{x}w_{y}-v_{y}w_{x}$ . Then, we have that:
 $\displaystyle Q^{2}-RS$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sum_{cyc}E_{ab}^{2}(v_{a}w_{b}-v_{b}w_{a})^{2}+2\sum_{cyc}E_{ab}E_{bc}(v_{b}w_{a}-v_{a}w_{b})(v_{b}w_{c}-v_{c}w_{b}).$ 
Namely, if we consider such matrices with  $E_{bc}=E_{cb}=0$ , we have that  $RS\geq Q^{2}\iff 0\geq(E_{ab}[a,b]-E_{ca}[c,a])^{2}\iff E_{ab}[a,b]=E_{ca}[c,a]$ . Moreover,  $S\leq 0$  iff  $E_{ab}v_{a}v_{b}+E_{ac}v_{a}v_{c}\leq 0$ . Thus, provided we pick  $E_{ab}\neq 0$  of the correct sign for  $S\leq 0$  and pick  $E_{ac}$  such that  $E_{ab}[a,b]=E_{ca}[c,a]$ , we have that  $\lambda_{n-1}(M+E)=\lambda_{n-1}(M)$ . In any case, we can pick  $E$  such that one of  $(M+E)_{ab}\in\{0,1\}$  or  $(M+E)_{ac}\in\{0,1\}$ . Thus,  $M+E$  is a minimiser of  $\lambda_{n-1}$  with strictly fewer non- $\{0,1\}$  entries than  $M$  (contradicting the hypothesis).
∎
This yields the following corollary:
Corollary 4.12.
Regardless of the existence of a simple minimiser, there exists a matrix  $N$  with  $0,1$  entries such that for all  $M\in\mathcal{S}_{n}$ :
 $\lambda_{n-1}(N)\leq\lambda_{n-1}(M)+2\sqrt{n}.$ 
Thus:
 $\displaystyle\inf_{m\geq 2}\left\{\frac{\lambda_{m-1}(M)}{m}:M\in\mathcal{S}_{m}\right\}=\inf\left\{\frac{\lambda_{|V(G)|-1}(G)}{|V(G)|}:G\text{ unweighted, }|V(G)|\geq 2\right\}.$ 
We conclude this section with the following geometric constraint on the structure of minimisers  $M$  of  $\lambda_{n-1}$  with  $\lambda_{n}(M)=\lambda_{n-1}(M)$ .
Lemma 4.13.
Suppose that  $\lambda_{n}(M)=\lambda_{n-1}(M)=\lambda$  has corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors  $\mathbb{v,w}$ . Define the vectors  $\mathbb{x}_{1},\dots\mathbb{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$  by:
 $\mathbb{x}_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}v_{i}\\
w_{i}\end{pmatrix}.$ 
If there exists some  $i\in\{1,\dots,n\}$ , some  $\lambda^{\prime}\leq\lambda$  and some  $\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in[0,1]$  such that:
 $\lambda^{\prime}\mathbb{x}_{i}=\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}\mathbb{x}_{j},$ 
then replacing the  $i$ -th row and column of  $M$  with  $\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}&\dots&\alpha_{n}\end{pmatrix}$  does not increase  $\lambda_{n-1}$ .
Proof.
Consider the matrix  $E$  defined by:
 $E_{xy}=\begin{cases}\alpha_{y}-M_{iy}&x=i,y\neq i\\
\alpha_{x}-M_{xi}&x\neq i,y=i\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$ 
Then, using the notation in Lemma 4.9, we have:
 $\displaystyle S$ 
 $\displaystyle=2v_{i}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}(\alpha_{j}-M_{ij})v_{j}\right)$ 
 $\displaystyle Q$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sum_{j\neq i}(\alpha_{j}-M_{ij})(v_{i}w_{j}+v_{j}w_{i})$ 
 $\displaystyle R$ 
 $\displaystyle=2w_{i}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}(\alpha_{j}-M_{ij})w_{j}\right).$ 
As  $\lambda^{\prime}v_{i}=\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}v_{j}=\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{j}v_{j}+\alpha_{i}v_{i}$ , we have that  $S=2v_{i}(\lambda^{\prime}v_{i}-\alpha_{i}v_{i})-2\lambda v_{i}^{2}=2(\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda-\alpha_{i})v_{i}^{2}\leq 0$ . Similarly, we have that  $R=2(\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda-\alpha_{i})w_{i}^{2}$  and:
 $Q=2v_{i}w_{i}(\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda-\alpha_{i}).$ 
Thus,  $RS=Q^{2}$ . So, we have that  $\lambda_{n-1}(M+E)\leq\lambda_{n-1}(M)$ .
∎
5 Concluding remarks
With  $\lambda_{3}\leq\frac{n}{3}$  proven for strong candidates for counter-examples, we also conjecture that  $c_{3}=\frac{1}{3}$ . Furthermore, given our work in Section 4 reducing a simple minimiser to a vertex multiplication, we are confident in the intuition that  $\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}$  holds for all extremal graphs, which was originally motivated by the final step in obtaining Nikiforov’s bound [10]. Originally, we had conjectured regular to be an additional necessary property, but our discovery of  $H_{a,b}$  suggests otherwise.
An alternative perspective on  $H_{a,b}$  with  $a\leq b$  is as an intermediate graph in the process of ‘morphing’ a  $3K_{b}$  into a  $C_{6}^{[b]}$  by adding vertices to the smaller cliques in between. We also explored morphing through edges, namely turning a  $3K_{2n}$  into a  $C_{6}^{[n]}$  by splitting each  $K_{2n}$  into two copies of  $K_{n}$ , pairing each copy with a copy from another  $K_{2n}$ , and adding edges between each pair. However, if we do this randomly with probability  $p$ , we obtain a graphon with spectrum
 $\frac{p+2}{6},\frac{1+\sqrt{p^{2}-p+1}}{6},\frac{1+\sqrt{p^{2}-p+1}}{6},\frac{1-\sqrt{p^{2}-p+1}}{6},\frac{1-\sqrt{p^{2}-p+1}}{6},\frac{-p}{6}$ 
and hence third eigenvalue proportion approximately  $\frac{1+\sqrt{p^{2}-p+1}}{6}$ . This only reaches  $\frac{1}{3}$  at  $p\in\{0,1\}$  and is worst at  $p=\frac{1}{2}$  which gives  $\frac{2+\sqrt{3}}{12}\approx 0.311$ .
It is worth noting that our methods for Cayley graphs on abelian groups do not generalise to non-abelian, at least not easily. For example, we have by Babai [1] that for a natural number  $t$  and an irreducible character  $\chi_{i}$  of degree  $n_{i}$ ,
 $\lambda_{i,1}^{t}+\dots+\lambda_{i,n_{i}}^{t}=\sum_{t_{1},\dots,g_{t}\in H}\chi_{i}\left(\prod_{s=1}^{t}g_{s}\right),$ 
which provides a polynomial whose roots are the respective eigenvalues, but these are nonetheless difficult to extract. Regardless, due to the inherent link between sums of roots of unity and pivalous graphs, it seems plausible that the functions  $f$  and  $g$  would be involved again in a proof of the same result.
Regarding Theorem 4.6, although we were unable to establish explicit bounds on the penultimate root of the characteristic polynomial of the vertex multiplications of  $T$ , we suspect that with further simplifications or insights, such bounds could be derived. For example, if we vertex multiply  $T$  by  $[x_{1},\dots,x_{11}]$ , we see that  $x_{11}$  does not affect the non-zero eigenvalues of  $T$ , thus we may assume that  $x_{11}=0$ . Note that the undirected, simple graph corresponding to  $T$  has automorphism group isomorphic to  $V_{4}\cong C_{2}\times C_{2}$  which simplifies matters slightly.
Finally, while the strategy of constructing matrices  $E$  for which  $M+E\in\mathcal{S}_{n}$  and  $E$  is negative semi-definite on  $\braket{\mathbb{v,w}}$  proves effective for matrices with relatively few entries in  $\{0,1\}$ , this approach becomes more challenging for matrices with almost all entries in  $\{0,1\}$ . Nevertheless, the techniques presented provide a foothold on whether a given matrix has locally extremal penultimate eigenvalue, which we hope can be further built upon.
