A proof of a conjecture on permutation pentanomials



Krishna Mallick

Address: Department of Computer Science and Engineering, IIT Kharagpur, Kharagpur 721302, India

Email address: krishna.mallickmath@gmail.com

 and 
Mohit Pal

Address: Department of Informatics, University of Bergen, PB 7803, N-5020, Bergen, Norway

Email address: mathmohit@outlook.com



Abstract.
    

In this paper, we use finite fields and linear algebra methods to resolve a conjecture by T. Zhang, L. Zheng, H. Wang, J. Peng and Y. Li (Finite Fields Appl. 110 (2026) 102743) concerning permutation pentanomials.
  




1. Introduction




Let  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}$  be the finite field with  $q^{n}$  elements, where  $q$  is a prime power and  $n$  is a positive integer. We denote by  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}^{*}$ , the multiplicative cyclic group of non-zero elements of  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}$ . It is well-known that every mapping from  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}$  into itself can be uniquely represented by a polynomial in  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}[X]$  of degree less than  $q^{n}$ . Accordingly, we may use the terms function and polynomial interchangeably. A polynomial  $f\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}[X]$  is called a permutation polynomial if the associated mapping  $c\mapsto f(c)$  permutes the elements of  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}$ . Permutation polynomials have been an active area of research since they have applications in various areas such as coding theory [2, 6], cryptography [7, 9] and combinatorial designs [3]. For more background material on permutation polynomials, we refer to Chap. 7 of [8].




Permutation polynomials with a few terms are of particular interest due to their simple algebraic structures. The simplest class of polynomials is given by monomial permutation polynomials  $X^{e}$ , which permute  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}$  if and only if  $\gcd(e,q^{n}-1)=1$ . Unlike monomials, the classification of permutation polynomials having a few terms, such as binomials, trinomials, quadrinomials, and pentanomials, is non-trivial and has not yet been completely resolved. For a survey of recent developments on permutation binomials and trinomials over finite fields, the reader may refer to [5]. We refer the interested reader to [4, Appendix] for recent advancements on permutation quadrinomials and pentanomials.




Recently, Zhang et al. [10] studied permutation pentanomials of the form




 $f(X)=\epsilon_{0}X^{d_{0}}+L(\epsilon_{1}X^{d_{1}}+\epsilon_{2}X^{d_{2}}),$ 



where  $L(X)=X^{q}+X$  and  $d_{0}\in\{1,2,4\}$ . Based on the numerical results, the authors proposed the following conjecture.



Conjecture 1.1.



[10, Conjecture 1] 
Let  $m,k$  be positive integers with  $q=2^{m}$ . Then




 $f_{1}(X)=X^{2^{k}+1}+X^{2^{k}q^{2}+1}+X^{q+2^{k}}+X^{q^{2}+2^{k}}+X^{2^{k}q+1}$ 



and




 $f_{2}(X)=X^{2^{k}+1}+X^{(2^{k}+1)q}+X^{q+2^{k}}+X^{q^{2}+2^{k}}+X^{2^{k}q^{2}+q}$ 



are permutation polynomials over  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}$  if and only if  $\gcd(2^{k}+1,q-1)=1$ .





It is the intent of our paper to completely prove this conjecture. The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we recall some basic notions and related results that will be used in the proof of the conjecture. In Section 3, we present a proof of Conjecture 1.1. Finally, we summarize the paper with some concluding remarks in Section 4.





2. Preliminaries




Throughout this paper, let  $q=2^{m}$ , where  $m$  is a positive integer. Let  ${\rm Tr}$  be the relative trace map from  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}$  to  $\mathbb{F}_{q}$  defined as  ${\rm Tr}(X):=X+X^{q}+X^{q^{2}}$ . Let  $C_{0}$  be a set defined as




 $C_{0}:=\{X\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}\mid{\rm Tr}(X)=0\}.$ 



It is well-known that  $C_{0}$  is a  $2$  dimensional vector space over  $\mathbb{F}_{q}$ . For any  $u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}$ , define the coset




 $C_{u}:=u+C_{0}=\{u+X\mid X\in C_{0}\}.$ 



We use the convention that  $C_{0}$  is the coset corresponding to  $u=0$ . One may note that for any  $Y\in C_{u}$ , we have  $Y=u+X$  for some  $X\in C_{0}$  and hence




 ${\rm Tr}(Y)={\rm Tr}(u+X)={\rm Tr}(u)+{\rm Tr}(X)=u{\rm Tr}(1)=u.$ 



It is straightforward to see that  $\lvert C_{u}\rvert=q^{2}$  for all  $u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}$ . Also, for any  $u,v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}$  with  $u\neq v$ , we have  $C_{u}\cap C_{v}=\phi$  as any  $Y\in C_{u}\cap C_{v}$  would imply that  $Y=u+X_{1}$  and  $Y=v+X_{2}$  for some  $X_{1},X_{2}\in C_{0}$ . Therefore, we have




 $u+X_{1}=v+X_{2}\implies{\rm Tr}(u+X_{1})={\rm Tr}(v+X_{2})\implies u=v,$ 



which is a contradiction. Thus, we have the following partition of  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}$ .




 $\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}=\bigcup_{u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}C_{u}.$ 



In the next section, we shall use this partition of  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}$  to prove the permutation property of  $f_{1}$  and  $f_{2}$ . We shall also use the following lemma in the proof of Conjecture 1.1.



Lemma 2.1.



[1, Lemma 2.1] 
Let  $\alpha,\beta$  be positive integers. Then




 $\gcd(2^{\alpha}+1,2^{\beta}-1)=\begin{cases}1~&~\mbox{if}~v_{2}(\beta)\leq v_{2}(\alpha),\\
2^{\gcd(\alpha,\beta)}+1~&~\mbox{if}~v_{2}(\beta)>v_{2}(\alpha),\\
\end{cases}$ 



where  $v_{2}(\alpha),v_{2}(\beta)$  are the highest powers of  $2$  that divide  $\alpha,\beta$ , respectively.






3. A proof of the conjecture




In this section, we give a proof of Conjecture1.1. We prove the permutation property of  $f_{1}$  and  $f_{2}$ , separately. The following theorem proves the first part of Conjecture 1.1.



Theorem 3.1.



Let  $m,k$  be positive integers with  $q=2^{m}$ . Then




 $f_{1}(X)=X^{2^{k}+1}+X^{2^{k}q^{2}+1}+X^{q+2^{k}}+X^{q^{2}+2^{k}}+X^{2^{k}q+1}$ 



is a permutation polynomial over  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}$  if and only if  $\gcd(2^{k}+1,q-1)=1$ .




Proof.



It is easy to observe that




 $\begin{split}f_{1}(X)&=X^{2^{k}+1}+X^{2^{k}}(X+X^{q}+X^{q^{2}})+X(X^{2^{k}}+X^{2^{k}q}+X^{2^{k}q^{2}})\\
&=X^{2^{k}+1}+X^{2^{k}}{\rm Tr}(X)+X\left({\rm Tr}(X)\right)^{2^{k}}\\
&=X^{2^{k}+1}+X^{2^{k}}{\rm Tr}(X)+X\left({\rm Tr}(X)\right)^{2^{k}}+\left({\rm Tr}(X)\right)^{2^{k}+1}+\left({\rm Tr}(X)\right)^{2^{k}+1}\\
&=\left(X+{\rm Tr}(X)\right)^{2^{k}+1}+\left({\rm Tr}(X)\right)^{2^{k}+1}.\end{split}$ 



We may note that for any  $Y\in C_{u}$ , we have a unique  $X\in C_{0}$  such that  $Y=u+X$ . Thus, we have




 $\begin{split}f_{1}(Y)&=f_{1}(u+X)\\
&=\left(u+X+{\rm Tr}(u+X)\right)^{2^{k}+1}+\left({\rm Tr}(u+X)\right)^{2^{k}+1}\\
&=X^{2^{k}+1}+u^{2^{k}+1}.\end{split}$ 



We first assume that  $\gcd(2^{k}+1,q-1)=1$ . Our aim is to show that  $f_{1}$  is a permutation of  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}$ . From Lemma 2.1, it is easy to see that




 $\begin{split}\gcd(2^{k}+1,q-1)=1&\iff v_{2}(m)\leq v_{2}(k)\\
&\iff v_{2}(3m)\leq v_{2}(k)\\
&\iff\gcd(2^{k}+1,q^{3}-1)=1.\end{split}$ 



Thus,  $X^{2^{k}+1}$  is injective on  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}$ . It is easy to observe that the restriction of the map  $f_{1}$  on the coset  $C_{u}$  is injective. As for any  $Y_{1},Y_{2}\in C_{u}$ , we have unique  $X_{1},X_{2}\in C_{0}$  such that  $Y_{1}=X_{1}+u$  and  $Y_{2}=X_{2}+u$ , moreover




 $f_{1}(Y_{1})=f_{1}(Y_{2})\implies X_{1}^{2^{k}+1}+u^{2^{k}+1}=X_{2}^{2^{k}+1}+u^{2^{k}+1}\implies X_{1}=X_{2}\implies Y_{1}=Y_{2}.$ 



Since  $u$  is arbitrary, the map  $f_{1}$  is injective on  $C_{u}$  for all  $u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}$ . To prove the permutation property of  $f_{1}$ , it is sufficient to show that the sets  $f_{1}(C_{u}):=\{f_{1}(Y)\mid Y\in C_{u}\}$  are disjoint. On the contrary, let us assume that  $Z\in f_{1}(C_{u})\cap f_{1}(C_{v})$  for some  $u,v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}$  and  $u\neq v$ . Then, we have  $Z=X_{1}^{2^{k}+1}+u^{2^{k}+1}$  and  $Z=X_{2}^{2^{k}+1}+v^{2^{k}+1}$  for some  $X_{1},X_{2}\in C_{0}$ . Therefore, we have



(3.1)

 $X_{1}^{2^{k}+1}+u^{2^{k}+1}=X_{2}^{2^{k}+1}+v^{2^{k}+1}\iff X_{1}^{2^{k}+1}+X_{2}^{2^{k}+1}=u^{2^{k}+1}+v^{2^{k}+1}.$ 



Now, since  $\gcd(2^{k}+1,q-1)=1$ , we have  $u^{2^{k}+1}+v^{2^{k}+1}:=w\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}$ . We shall show that there does not exist any  $(X_{1},X_{2})\in C_{0}\times C_{0}$  such that



(3.2)

 $X_{1}^{2^{k}+1}+X_{2}^{2^{k}+1}=w.$ 



Since  $w\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}$  neither of  $X_{1},X_{2}$  can be  $0$ . Now raising power  $q$  to Equation (3.2) and adding it to Equation (3.2), we have



(3.3)

 $X_{1}^{2^{k}+1}+X_{1}^{q(2^{k}+1)}=X_{2}^{2^{k}+1}+X_{2}^{q(2^{k}+1)}.$ 



Now, we shall show that Equation (3.3) has only a trivial solution  $X_{1}=X_{2}$ , or in other words the map  $g(X)=X^{2^{k}+1}+X^{q(2^{k}+1)}$  is injective on  $C_{0}$ . It is easy to see that  $g(0)=0$ . Next, we show that  $g(X)\neq 0$  for all  $X\in C_{0}\backslash\{0\}$ . Assume that  $g(X)=0$  for some  $X\in C_{0}\backslash\{0\}$ . Then we have




 $\begin{split}&X^{2^{k}+1}(1+X^{(q-1)(2^{k}+1)})=0\\
\implies&X^{(q-1)(2^{k}+1)}=1\\
\implies&X^{q-1}=1~\quad~\mbox{(as}~\gcd(2^{k}+1,q^{3}-1)=1)\\
\implies&X\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*},\\
\end{split}$ 



which is a contradiction, as  $C_{0}\cap\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}=\phi.$  Thus  $g(X)\neq 0$  for all  $X\in C_{0}\backslash\{0\}$ . Now, raising power  $q-1$  to Equation (3.3), we have



(3.4)

 $\begin{split}&(X_{1}^{2^{k}+1}+X_{1}^{q(2^{k}+1)})^{q-1}=(X_{2}^{2^{k}+1}+X_{2}^{q(2^{k}+1)})^{q-1}\\
\implies&\frac{X_{1}^{q(2^{k}+1)}+X_{1}^{q^{2}(2^{k}+1)}}{X_{1}^{2^{k}+1}+X_{1}^{q(2^{k}+1)}}=\frac{X_{2}^{q(2^{k}+1)}+X_{2}^{q^{2}(2^{k}+1)}}{X_{2}^{2^{k}+1}+X_{2}^{q(2^{k}+1)}}\\
\implies&\frac{X_{1}^{2^{k}+1}+X_{1}^{2^{k}+q}+X_{1}^{2^{k}q+1}}{X_{1}^{2^{k}+1}+X_{1}^{q(2^{k}+1)}}=\frac{X_{2}^{2^{k}+1}+X_{2}^{2^{k}+q}+X_{2}^{2^{k}q+1}}{X_{2}^{2^{k}+1}+X_{2}^{q(2^{k}+1)}}~\mbox{(as}~X_{i}^{q^{2}}=X_{i}^{q}+X_{i},i\in\{1,2\})\\
\implies&\frac{X_{1}^{2^{k}+1}(1+X_{1}^{q-1}+X_{1}^{2^{k}(q-1)})}{X_{1}^{2^{k}+1}(1+X_{1}^{(q-1)(2^{k}+1)})}=\frac{X_{2}^{2^{k}+1}(1+X_{2}^{q-1}+X_{2}^{2^{k}(q-1)})}{X_{2}^{2^{k}+1}(1+X_{2}^{(q-1)(2^{k}+1)})}\\
\implies&\frac{1+r+r^{2^{k}}}{1+r^{2^{k}+1}}=\frac{1+s+s^{2^{k}}}{1+s^{2^{k}+1}}\\
\implies&r+r^{2^{k}}+s^{2^{k}+1}+rs^{2^{k}+1}+r^{2^{k}}s^{2^{k}+1}=s+s^{2^{k}}+r^{2^{k}+1}+r^{2^{k}+1}s+r^{2^{k}+1}s^{2^{k}}\\
\implies&r+s+(r+s)^{2^{k}}+r^{2^{k}+1}+s^{2^{k}+1}+rs(r+s)^{2^{k}}+r^{2^{k}}s^{2^{k}}(r+s)=0,\end{split}$ 



where  $r=X_{1}^{q-1}$  and  $s=X_{2}^{q-1}$ . Now, we shall consider two cases, namely,  $r+s=0$  and  $r+s\neq 0$ .




Case 1. Let  $r+s=0$ . In this case, we have  $X_{1}^{q-1}=X_{2}^{q-1}\implies X_{1}=wX_{2}$  for some  $w\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}$ . Putting the value of  $X_{2}$  in Equation (3.3), we have



(3.5)

 $X_{1}^{2^{k}+1}+X_{1}^{q(2^{k}+1)}=w^{2^{k}+1}(X_{1}^{2^{k}+1}+X_{1}^{q(2^{k}+1)})\implies w^{2^{k}+1}=1.$ 



Since  $\gcd(2^{k}+1,q^{3}-1)=1$ , Equation (3.5) implies that  $w=1$  and hence  $X_{1}=X_{2}$ . It is straightforward to see that  $X_{1}=X_{2}$  cannot be a solution of Equation (3.2).




Case 2. Let  $r+s\neq 0$ . In this case, dividing Equation (3.4) by  $(r+s)^{2^{k}+1}$ , we have



(3.6)

 $\begin{split}Z^{2^{k}}+Z+1=0.\end{split}$ 



where  $\displaystyle Z=\frac{1+r+rs}{r+s}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}$ . Raising power  $2^{k}$  to Equation (3.6) and adding it to Equation (3.6), we have




 $Z^{2^{2k}}+Z=0\implies Z\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{\gcd(2k,3m)}}.$ 



Since  $\gcd(2^{k}+1,q^{3}-1)=1$ , we have  $v_{2}(3m)\leq v_{2}(k)$  and hence  $v_{2}(3m)<v_{2}(2k)$  and hence  $\gcd(2k,3m)=\gcd(k,3m).$  But
 $Z\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{\gcd(k,3m)}}\implies Z^{2^{k}}+Z=0$ , which is a contradiction to Equation (3.6). This completes the proof.
∎





The following theorem proves the second part of Conjecture 1.1.



Theorem 3.2.



Let  $m,k$  be positive integers with  $q=2^{m}$ . Then




 $f_{2}(X)=X^{2^{k}+1}+X^{2^{k}q+q}+X^{q+2^{k}}+X^{q^{2}+2^{k}}+X^{2^{k}q^{2}+q}$ 



is a permutation polynomial over  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}$  if and only if  $\gcd(2^{k}+1,q-1)=1$ .




Proof.



It is easy to observe that




 $\begin{split}f_{2}(X)&=X^{2^{k}+q}+X^{2^{k}}(X+X^{q}+X^{q^{2}})+X^{q}(X^{2^{k}}+X^{2^{k}q}+X^{2^{k}q^{2}})\\
&=X^{2^{k}+q}+X^{2^{k}}{\rm Tr}(X)+X^{q}\left({\rm Tr}(X)\right)^{2^{k}}\\
&=X^{2^{k}+q}+X^{2^{k}}{\rm Tr}(X)+X^{q}\left({\rm Tr}(X)\right)^{2^{k}}+\left({\rm Tr}(X)\right)^{2^{k}+q}+\left({\rm Tr}(X)\right)^{2^{k}+q}\\
&=\left(X+{\rm Tr}(X)\right)^{2^{k}+q}+\left({\rm Tr}(X)\right)^{2^{k}+q}.\end{split}$ 



It is easy to see that  $f_{2}(X)$  is a permutation of  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}$  if and only if



(3.7)

 $\begin{split}f_{2}^{\prime}(X)&=(f_{2}(X))^{q^{2}}\\
&=\left(X+{\rm Tr}(X)\right)^{q^{2}2^{k}+1}+\left({\rm Tr}(X)\right)^{q^{2}2^{k}+1}\\
&=\left(X+{\rm Tr}(X)\right)^{2^{k^{\prime}}+1}+\left({\rm Tr}(X)\right)^{2^{k}+1},\end{split}$ 



where  $k^{\prime}=k+2m$ , is a permutation of  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}$ . We may note that for any  $Y\in C_{u}$ , we have a unique  $X\in C_{0}$  such that  $Y=u+X$ . Thus, we have




 $\begin{split}f_{2}^{\prime}(Y)&=f_{2}^{\prime}(u+X)\\
&=\left(u+X+{\rm Tr}(u+X)\right)^{2^{k^{\prime}}+1}+\left({\rm Tr}(u+X)\right)^{2^{k}+1}\\
&=X^{2^{k^{\prime}}+1}+u^{2^{k}+1}.\end{split}$ 



We first assume that  $\gcd(2^{k}+1,q-1)=1$ . Our aim is to show that  $f_{2}^{\prime}$  is a permutation of  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}$ . From Lemma 2.1, it is easy to see that




 $\begin{split}\gcd(2^{k}+1,q-1)=1&\iff v_{2}(m)\leq v_{2}(k)\\
&\iff v_{2}(3m)\leq v_{2}(k)\\
&\iff v_{2}(3m)\leq v_{2}(k+2m)\\
&\iff\gcd(2^{k^{\prime}}+1,q^{3}-1)=1.\end{split}$ 



Thus,  $X^{2^{k^{\prime}}+1}$  is injective on  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}$ . Similarly to the previous theorem, the map  $f_{2}^{\prime}$  is injective on  $C_{u}$  for all  $u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}$ . To prove the permutation property of  $f_{2}^{\prime}$ , it is sufficient to show that the sets  $f_{2}^{\prime}(C_{u}):=\{f_{2}^{\prime}(Y)\mid Y\in C_{u}\}$  are disjoint. On the contrary, let us assume that  $Z\in f_{2}^{\prime}(C_{u})\cap f_{2}^{\prime}(C_{v})$  for some  $u,v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}$  and  $u\neq v$ . Then, we have  $Z=X_{1}^{2^{k^{\prime}}+1}+u^{2^{k}+1}$  and  $Z=X_{2}^{2^{k^{\prime}}+1}+v^{2^{k}+1}$  for some  $X_{1},X_{2}\in C_{0}$ . Therefore, we have



(3.8)

 $X_{1}^{2^{k^{\prime}}+1}+u^{2^{k}+1}=X_{2}^{2^{k^{\prime}}+1}+v^{2^{k}+1}\iff X_{1}^{2^{k^{\prime}}+1}+X_{2}^{2^{k^{\prime}}+1}=u^{2^{k}+1}+v^{2^{k}+1}.$ 



Now, since  $\gcd(2^{k}+1,q-1)=1$ , we have  $u^{2^{k}+1}+v^{2^{k}+1}:=w\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}$ . Similar to Theorem 3.1, it can be shown that there does not exist any  $(X_{1},X_{2})\in C_{0}\times C_{0}$  such that



(3.9)

 $X_{1}^{2^{k^{\prime}}+1}+X_{2}^{2^{k^{\prime}}+1}=w.$ 



This completes the proof.
∎





Following the pattern of Theorem 3.1 and Theorem 3.2, it can be easily shown that the following pentanomial  $f_{3}$  also induces a permutation of  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}$ .



Theorem 3.3.



Let  $m,k$  be positive integers with  $q=2^{m}$ . Then




 $f_{3}(X)=X^{2^{k}+1}+X^{2^{k}+q}+X^{2^{k}+q^{2}}+X^{q2^{k}+q^{2}}+X^{q^{2}2^{k}+q^{2}}$ 



is a permutation polynomial over  $\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}$  if and only if  $\gcd(2^{k}+1,q-1)=1$ .






4. Concluding Remarks




In this paper, we proved a conjecture by Zhang et al. [10] concerning permutation pentanomials. As an immediate consequence, we showed that the polynomials  $f_{1}(X)$  and  $f_{2}(X)$  in the conjecture belongs to a more general class of permutation polynomials of the form




 $f(X)=\left(X+{\rm Tr}(X)\right)^{2^{k}+q^{i}}+\left({\rm Tr}(X)\right)^{2^{k}+q^{i}},$ 



where  $i\in\{0,1,2\}$  and  $\gcd(2^{k}+1,q-1)=1$ .
