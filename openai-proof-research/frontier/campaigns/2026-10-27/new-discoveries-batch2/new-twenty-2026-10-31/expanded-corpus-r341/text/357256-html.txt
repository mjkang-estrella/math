On distance graphs in rational spaces
A. A. Sokolov
Note: Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology, Department of Discrete Mathematics, sokolov.aa@phystech.edu
Abstract
 
For any positive definite rational quadratic form $q$ of $n$ variables let $G(\mathbb{Q}^{n},q)$ denote the graph with vertices $\Q^{n}$ and $x,y\in\Q^{n}$ connected iff $q(x-y)=1$ . This notion generalises standard Euclidean distance graphs.
In this article we study these graphs and show how to find the exact value of clique number of the $G(\mathbb{Q}^{n},q)$ .
 
We also prove rational analogue of the Beckman–Quarles theorem that any unit-preserving mapping of $\mathbb{Q}^{n}$ is an isometry.
 
1 Introduction
Let $\mcQ_{n}$ be a set of all rational positive definite quadratic forms on $n$ variables.
Definition.
For every $n\in\N$ and $q\in\mcQ_{n}$ graph $G(\Q^{n},q)=(V,E)$ is defined as follows:
 $V=\Q^{n},\quad E=\{(\x,\y)\quad|\quad q(\x-\y)=1\}.$ 
This definition generalises standard Euclidean distance graph.
Indeed, if $I_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+x_{n}^{2}$ is standard Euclidean quadratic form, then $G(\Q^{n},I_{n})$ and $G(\Q^{n},\frac{1}{d}I_{n})$ are standard Euclidean distance graphs with distance 1 and $\sqrt{d}$ respectively.
In section 2 we study basic properties of such graphs i.e. the non-emptiness and the connectedness.
In section 3 we study clique numbers of such graphs, i.e. the maximal number of vertices connected to each other.
An algorithm on finding exact value of clique number of $G(\Q^{n},q)$ any $n$ and $q$ is presented.
This algorithm was implemented for the case $q=\frac{1}{d}I_{n}$ using SageMath, respective computer program can be found at [1].
More about values of $\omega(G(\Q^{n},I_{n}))$ and $\omega(G(\Q^{n},\frac{1}{d}I_{n}))$ reader can find in [2], [3], [4], [5], [6].
In section 4 we state the following question:
"Given two rational quadratic forms $q_{1}$ and $q_{2}$ , are graphs $G(\Q^{n},q_{1})$ and $G(\Q^{n},q_{2})$ isomoprhic or not? If so, what do these isomorphisms look like?"
This question is closely related to the rational analogue of Beckman–Quarles Theorem which states that any automorphism of $G(\Q^{n},I_{n})$ is an isometry.
This analogue is proven for all $n$ .
More about Beckman–Quarles theorem reader can find in [7], [8], [9], [10].
Through this article notions and theorems from the theory of rational quadratic forms will be used which can be found in A.
2 Definitions and main properties
Let $\mcQ_{n}$ be the set of all rational positive definite quadratic forms on $n$ variables. Let $\mcQ=\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mcQ_{n}$ .
Definition.
For every $q\in\mcQ_{n}$ a $G(\Q^{n},q)=(V,E)$ is defined as follows:
 $V=\Q^{n},\quad E=\{(\x,\y)\quad|\quad q(\x-\y)=1\}.$ 
Definition.
Forms $q_{1}$ and $q_{2}$ are called rationally equivalent and denoted as $q_{1}\eqQ q_{2}$ iff $\dim q_{1}=\dim q_{2}$ and there exists linear transformation $T\in GL\left(n,\Q\right)$ such that $q_{1}(\x)=q_{2}(T\x)$ and call such forms .
Lemma 1.
If $q_{1}\eqQ q_{2}$ then corresponding graphs $G(\Q^{\dim q_{1}},q_{1})$ and $G(\Q^{\dim q_{1}}q_{2})$ are isomorphic.
Proof.
Let $E_{1}$ , $E_{2}$ be edge sets of graphs $G(\Q^{n},q_{1})$ and $G(\Q^{n},q_{2})$ respectively.
Let $T\in GL\left(n,\Q\right)$ such that $q_{1}(\x)=q_{2}(T\x)$ . We can see that
 $(\x,\y)\in E_{1}\Longleftrightarrow q_{1}(\x-\y)=1\Longleftrightarrow q_{2}(T\x-T\y)=1\Longleftrightarrow(T\x,T\y)\in E_{2},$ 
Since $T$ is invertible we can see that $T$ is indeed an isomorphism of $G(\Q^{n},q_{1})$ and $G(\Q^{n},q_{2})$ .
∎
Definition.
We will write $q_{1}\embed q_{2}$ if $q_{1}$ can be built up to the equivalent of $q_{2}$ , i.e. there exists $r\in\mcQ_{\dim q_{1}-\dim q_{2}}$ such that quadratic form $h(\x,\y):=q_{1}(\x)+r(\y)$ holds $h\eqQ q_{2}$ .
Lemma 2.
If $q_{1}\embed q_{2}$ then graph $G(\Q^{\dim q_{1}},q_{1})$ is a subgraph of $G(\Q^{\dim q_{2}},q_{2})$ .
Proof.
By definition there exists $r\in\mcQ_{\dim q_{1}-\dim q_{2}}$ such that quadratic form $h(\x,\y):=q_{1}(\x)+r(\y)$ holds $h\eqQ q_{2}$ . Hence $G(\Q^{\dim q_{2}},q_{2})\simeq G(\Q^{\dim h},h)$ . If we constrain latter graph on the first $\dim q_{1}$ coordinates we will get $G(\Q^{\dim q_{1}},q_{1})$ .
∎
Let us state a lemma that we are going to use later.
Lemma 3 (Meyer, [11], chapter IV, theorem 8, corollary 2).
A quadratic form of rank $\geqslant 5$ represents 0 in $\Q$ if and only of it represents $0$ in $\R$ (i.e. there exists $\x\in\R^{n}$ , $\x\neq\zero$ such that $q(\x)=0$ ).
Theorem 1.
For any $n\geqslant 4$ and $q\in\mcQ_{n}$ graph $G(\Q^{n},q)$ is nonempty.
Proof.
 $G(\Q^{n},q)$ is nonempty iff there exists rational solution to $q(\x)=1$ . Consider following quadratic form
 $r(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},x_{n+1})=q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})-x_{n+1}^{2}$ 
This is indefinite quadratic form of rank $n+1\geqslant 5$ . Hence by lemma 3 there exists nontrivial solution $(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})$ to the equation $r(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})=0$ . It is easy to see that $x_{n+1}\neq 0$ (otherwise $q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0$ hence $x_{1}=\ldots=x_{n}=0$ ) and $q\left(\frac{x_{1}}{x_{n+1}},\ldots,\frac{x_{n}}{x_{n+1}}\right)=1$ .
∎
The following statement shows that the latter theorem is exact, i.e. for $n<4$ there exists $q\in\mcQ_{n}$ such that $G(\Q^{n},q)$ is empty.
Proposition.
Graphs $G(\Q^{2},2x^{2}+3y^{2})$ and $G(\Q^{3},2x^{2}+3y^{2}+3z^{2})$ are empty.
Proof.
Assume the contrary, i.e. there exists a rational solution to $2x^{2}+3y^{2}+3z^{2}=1$ . Let $p,q,r,s$ be minimal integers such that
 $2p^{2}+3q^{2}+3r^{2}=s^{2}$ 
Then $2p^{2}-s^{2}$ is divisible by 3 which means that $p$ and $s$ are divisible by 3. Then $2p^{2}-s^{2}$ is divisible by 9 and $q^{2}+r^{2}$ is divisible by 3. Then $q$ and $r$ are divisible by 3.
This contradicts to the minimality of $p,q,r,s$ (we can divide them by 3).
Since $G(\Q^{2},2x^{2}+3y^{2})$ is a subgraph of $G(\Q^{3},2x^{2}+3y^{2}+3z^{2})$ , it is also empty.
∎
Next lemma gives the exact criteria of $G(\Q^{n},q)$ nonemptiness for any $n$ and $q$ .
Lemma 4.
Graph $G(\Q^{n},q)$ is nonempty iff $I_{1}\embed q$ or equivalently there exists $r\in\mcQ_{n-1}$ such that
 $q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\eqQ x_{1}^{2}+r(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})$ 
Proof.
If $q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\eqQ x_{1}^{2}+r(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})$ , then by lemma 1 there holds $G(\Q^{n},q)\simeq G(\Q^{n},x_{1}^{2}+r(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}))$ and we can find the edge $(1,0,\ldots,0)$ in the latter.
If graph $G(\Q^{n},q)$ is nonempty, consider solution $v=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n})$ of $q(\x)=1$ . We can take $T\in GL(n,\Q)$ such that $v$ is one of the coordinate axis (for example $x_{1}$ ). Now it is easy to see that $q(T\x)$ has the form $x_{1}^{2}+r(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})$ .
∎
Chilakamarri [2] proved that graphs $G(\Q^{2},I_{2})$ , $G(\Q^{3},I_{3})$ and $G(\Q^{4},I_{4})$ are disconnected. He also showed that for $n\geqslant 5$ graphs $G(\Q^{n},I_{n})$ are also connected.
The following theorem is the generalization of this statement.
Theorem 2.
For any $n\geqslant 5$ and $q\in\mcQ_{n}$ graph $G(\Q^{n},q)$ is connected.
Proof.
Consider $T\in GL(n,\Q)$ such that $q(T\x)$ is diagonal. We will redefine $q(\x)$ as $q(T\x)$ , for by lemma 1 this will not change the considered graph.
Let $q(\x)=ax_{1}^{2}+r(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})$ , $a>0$ , $r\in\mcQ_{n-1}$ .
Consider any rational $0<c<\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}$ and quadratic form $q_{1}(\x)=r(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})-(1-ac^{2})x_{1}^{2}$ . By lemma 3 there exists rational solution to $q_{1}(\x)=0$ . Let us divide this solution by $x_{1}^{2}$ which is clearly nonzero.
Now let $(x_{2}^{0},\ldots,x_{n}^{0})$ be the obtained solution to $r(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})=1-ac^{2}$ .
It is easy to see that points $(0,0,\ldots,0)$ , $(c,x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})$ and $(2c,0,\ldots,0)$ form a path of length 2 in $G(\Q^{n},q)$ . Using that we can find path from $(0,0,\ldots,0)$ to $(2kc,0,\ldots,0)$ for any $k\in\N$ . Since we can take arbitrary small $c$ and arbitrary large $k$ , we can see that there exists path from $(0,0,\ldots,0)$ to $(x,0,\ldots,0)$ for any $x\in\Q$ .
Applying this consequently for other coordinates we can see that graph $G(\Q^{n},q)$ is connected.
∎
3 Clique numbers of $G(\Q^{n},q)$ 
This chapter is devoted to finding the clique number $\omega(G(\Q^{n},q))$ of $G(\Q^{n},q)$ , i.e. the maximum number of pairwise connected vertices.
We will use notions and theorems from theory of rational quadratic forms that can be found in the appendix and in the book [11].
Let us denote
 $S_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant j\leqslant n}x_{i}x_{j}$ 
Theorem 3.
 $\omega\left(\Q^{n},q\right)=n+1$ iff $S_{n}\eqQ q$ .
Proof.
If $q\eqQ S_{n}$ then $G(\Q^{n},q)\simeq G(\Q^{n},S_{n})$ . It is easy to check that in $G(\Q^{n},S_{n})$ points
 $\e_{0}=(0,0,0,\ldots,0)$ 
 $\e_{1}=(1,0,0,\ldots,0)$ 
 $\e_{2}=(0,1,0,\ldots,0)$ 
 $\ldots$ 
 $\e_{n}=(0,0,0,\ldots,1)$ 
form a clique of size $(n+1)$ .
To the contrary, let $\omega\left(\Q^{n},q\right)=n+1$ and $v_{0}=(0,0,\ldots,0),v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}$ is $(n+1)$ -clique in $G(\Q^{n},q)$ . Consider $T\in GL(n,\Q)$ such that $Tv_{i}=e_{i}$ for any $(1\leqslant i\leqslant n)$ . Let $\displaystyle r(\x)=q(T\x)=\sum_{i\leqslant j}r_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}$ .
Since $r(v_{i}-v_{0})=1$ we get $r_{ii}=1$ .
Since $r(v_{i}-v_{j})=1$ we get $r_{ii}+r_{jj}-r_{ij}=1$ hence $r_{ij}=1$ .
So we obtain $r=S_{n}$ .
∎
Theorem 4.
 $\omega(G(\Q^{n},q))=\max_{k\colon S_{k}\embed q}k+1$ 
Proof.
If $S_{k}\embed q$ then $G(\Q^{k},S_{k})\subseteq G(\Q^{n},q)$ which means
 $\omega(G(\Q^{n},q))\geqslant\omega(G(\Q^{k},S_{k}))=k+1.$ 
Assume $\omega(G(\Q^{n},q))\geqslant k+1$ . Then consider affine subspace $V$ of dimension $k$ that contains $(k+1)$ -clique. Then by theorem 3 in this subspace quadratic form $q$ is equivalent $S_{k}$ , hence $S_{k}\embed q$ .
∎
Theorem 5.
For any $q\in\mcQ_{n}$ there holds $\omega(G(\Q^{n},q))\geqslant n-2$ .
Proof.
By lemma 8 from appendix $S_{n-3}\embed q$ . Using previous theorem we get
 $\omega(G(\Q^{n},q))\geqslant\omega(G(\Q^{n-3},S_{n-3}))=n-2.$ 
∎
4 Graph isomorphisms
As showed in lemma 1, if $q_{1}\eqQ q_{2}$ then $G(\Q^{n},q_{1})\simeq G(\Q^{n},q_{2})$ .
Definition.
By $G_{0}(\Q^{n},q)$ we denote a connected component of $G(\Q^{n},q)$ that contains point $\zero$ .
Conjecture 1.
If $G_{0}(\Q^{n},q_{1})\simeq G_{0}(\Q^{n},q_{2})$ then $q_{1}\eqQ q_{2}$ . Moreover any isomorphism between these graphs is a linear transformation $T$ such that $q_{1}(\x)=q_{2}(T\x)$ .
This conjecture indeed generalises rational Beckman–Quarles theorem since if we take $q_{1}=q_{2}=I_{n}$ it will state that any automorphism of $G_{0}(\Q^{n},I_{n})$ is a linear transformation that preserves quadratic form $I_{n}$ . i.e. isometry.
Lemma 5.
Let $v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}$ be linearly independent vectors in $\Q^{n}$ . Then quadratic form $m$ with matrix
 $M=\Big\{(v_{i},v_{j})_{q}\Big\}_{i,j=1}^{n}:=\Big\{\frac{q(v_{i})+q(v_{j})-q(v_{i}-v_{j})}{2}\Big\}_{i,j=1}^{n}$ 
is rationally equivalent to $q$ .
Proof.
Consider Hilbert space $\Q^{n}$ with inner product $\langle\x,\y\rangle=(q(\x)+q(\y)-q(\x-\y))/2$ .
Let $\e_{1},\ldots,\e_{n}$ be a standard basis of $\Q^{n}$ .
It is easy to see that $M$ is a Gram matrix of vectors $v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}$ and the matrix $Q$ of quadratic form $q$ is a Gram matrix of vectors $e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}$ . Since $T$ transforms $e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}$ into $v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}$ , we can see that $M=T^{T}QT$ which is equivalent to $m(\x)=q(T\x)$ . Hence $m\eqQ q$ .
∎
Definition.
Let $p_{G}(a,b,k)$ be a number of paths of length $k$ from $a$ to $b$ .
Lemma 6.
Let $G=G_{0}(\Q^{n},q)$ . Then $p_{G}(a,b,k)=1\Longleftrightarrow q(a-b)=k^{2}$ .
Proof.
Let $a=x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k+1}=b$ be vertices of the path of length $k$ from $a$ to $b$ . For $1\leqslant i\leqslant k$ consider $y_{i}=x_{i+1}-x_{i}$ to be the edges of this path. Then for any $i$ there holds $q(y_{i})=1$ and $y_{1}+\ldots+y_{k}=b-a$ . If not all edges are equal then there is $y_{i}\neq y_{i+1}$ . Then between $a$ and $b$ there will be another path that consists of edges
 $y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{i-1},y_{i+1},y_{i},y_{i+2},\ldots,y_{n}.$ 
If $y_{1}=y_{2}=\ldots=y_{n}$ , then $q(a-b)=q(k\cdot y_{1})=k^{2}$ .
∎
Conjecture 2.
For any $q\in\mcQ_{n}$ if graph $G_{0}(\Q^{n},q)$ 
is nonempty then there exists $(n+1)$ -simplex with rational lengths in $(\Q^{n},q)$ .
Theorem 6.
Conjecture 2 implies conjecture 1.
Proof.
Let $q_{1},q_{2}\in\mcQ_{n}$ , such that $G_{1}=G_{0}(\Q^{n},q_{1})$ and $G_{2}=G_{0}(\Q^{n},q_{2})$ are isomorphic. By conjecture 2 there exists a simplex with rational edges in $(\Q^{n},q_{1})$ .
Because we can find a simplex with rational sides we can find a simplex with natural lengths, i.e. there are points $x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\Q^{n}$ such that for any $1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n$ there holds $q_{1}(x_{i}-x_{j})=\ell_{ij}^{2}$ , where $\ell_{ij}\in\N$ .
This means that there are points $x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\in\Q^{n}$ , such that $p_{G_{1}}(x_{i},x_{j},\ell_{ij})=1$ . Since $G_{1}\simeq G_{2}$ , there are points $y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\in\Q^{n}$ , such that $p_{G_{2}}(y_{i},y_{j},\ell_{ij})=1$ and $q_{2}(y_{i}-y_{j})=\ell_{i,j}^{2}$ .
Using lemma 5 we get that forms $q_{1}$ and $q_{2}$ are both equivalent to a quadratic form with matrix $\Big\{\frac{\ell_{1i}^{2}+\ell_{1j}^{2}-\ell_{ij}^{2}}{2}\Big\}_{i,j=1}^{n}$ , therefore are equivalent one to another.
∎
Theorem 7.
Conjectures 2 and 1 are true provided by $n=2$ .
Proof.
Consider $q\in\mcQ_{2}$ such that $G_{0}(\Q^{2},q)$ is nonempty. Using theorem 4 we can say that $q\eqQ x^{2}+ny^{2}$ and we can check that there is a triangle with rational sides in $(\Q^{2},x^{2}+ny^{2})$ .
For this we need to consider triangle with coordinates $(0,0),(n-1,2),(-n+1,2)$ . Its sides are equal to $n+1,n+1$ and $2n-2$ .
∎
Theorem 8 (Rational Beckman–Quarles theorem).
Conjectures 2 and 1 hold $q_{1}=q_{2}=I_{n}$ $(n>1)$ .
Proof.
Using theorem 6 we need to find rational simplex in $\Q^{n}$ with rational sides.
For even $n$ we can consider the following simplex:
 $\begin{matrix}(1,&1,&0,&0,&\ldots&0,&0),\\
(1,&-1,&0,&0,&\ldots&0,&0),\\
(0,&0,&1,&1,&\ldots&0,&0),\\
(0,&0,&1,&-1,&\ldots&0,&0),\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ddots&\ldots&\ldots\\
(0,&0,&0,&0,&\ldots&1,&1),\\
(0,&0,&0,&0,&\ldots&1,&-1),\\
(1,&1/15,&0,&0,&\ldots&0,&0)\\
\end{matrix}$ 
For odd $n$ we can consider the following simplex:
 $\begin{matrix}(1,&1,&0,&0,&\ldots&0,&0,&0),\\
(1,&-1,&0,&0,&\ldots&0,&0,&0),\\
(0,&0,&1,&1,&\ldots&0,&0,&0),\\
(0,&0,&1,&-1,&\ldots&0,&0,&0),\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ddots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\\
(0,&0,&0,&0,&\ldots&1,&1,&0),\\
(0,&0,&0,&0,&\ldots&1,&-1,&0),\\
(1,&1/15,&0,&0,&\ldots&0,&0,&0)\\
(0,&0,&0,&0,&\ldots&0,&0,&7/4)\\
\end{matrix}$ 
It is easy to see that all sides of these simplices are rational.
∎
Lemma 2 shows that if $q_{1}\embed q_{2}$ then $G(\Q^{\dim q_{1}},q_{1})\subseteq G(\Q^{\dim q_{2}},q_{2})$ . Does the contrary hold?
Proposition.
Consider $q(x,y,z)=\frac{1}{3}(x^{2}+y^{2}+2z^{2})$ . Then
 $G(\Q^{2},I_{2})\subset G(\Q^{3},q_{3})$ , but $I_{2}\notembed q$ .
Proof.
As shown in article [12], graph $G(\Q^{2},I_{2})$ is bipartite, let $G(\Q^{2},I_{2})=G_{1}\sqcup G_{2}$ where $G_{1},G_{2}$ — are parts of this graph.
Consider following mapping $\varphi:\Q^{2}\to\Q^{3}$ 
 $\varphi(x,y)=\begin{cases}(x_{1},x_{2},0),&\text{if }(x_{1},x_{2})\in G_{1}\\
(x_{1},x_{2},1),&\text{if }(x_{1},x_{2})\in G_{2}\end{cases}$ 
It is easy to see that $(\x,\y)$ is the edge of $G(\Q^{2},I_{2})$ then $(\varphi(\x),\varphi(\y))$ is the edge of $G(\Q^{3},q_{3})$ .
Now using the statement 7 we obtain that $I_{2}\notembed q$ .
∎
Appendix A Rational quadratic forms
Let $\Nu=\{p$ : $p$ is prime $\}\cup\{\infty\}$ . We denote by $\Q_{\infty}=\R$ , and by $\Q_{p}$ the $p$ -adic number field for a prime $p$ .
We also denote $\Q_{*}=\Q\backslash\{0\}$ and $\Q_{*}^{2}=\{x^{2}|x\in\Q_{*}\}$ .
Let $A=\diag\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right),$ where $a_{i}\in\Q$ and $a_{i}>0$ . Then
1. 
 $\displaystyle D\left(A\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}$ is a determinant of $A$ 
2. 
For any $\nu\in\Nu$ we denote a Hasse–Minkowski invariant
 $E_{\nu}\left(A\right)=\prod_{i<j}\left(a_{i},a_{j}\right)_{\nu}\in\{\pm 1\}$ 
where $\left(a,b\right)_{\nu}:\left(\Q_{*}/\Q_{*}^{2}\right)^{2}\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}$ is the Hilbert symbol on $\Q_{\nu}$ .
Hilbert symbol $\left(a,b\right)_{\nu}$ equals 1 iff equation $z^{2}=ax^{2}+by^{2}$ has nontrivial solution $\left(x,y,z\right)$ over $\Q_{\nu}^{3}$ .
This value is easily computable and has following properties [11]:
1. 
 $\left(a,b\right)_{\nu}=\left(b,a\right)_{\nu}$ ;
2. 
 $\left(a,c^{2}\right)_{\nu}=1$ ;
3. 
 $\left(a,-a\right)_{\nu}=\left(a,1-a\right)_{\nu}=1$ ;
4. 
 $\left(a,bc\right)_{\nu}=\left(a,b\right)_{\nu}\cdot\left(a,c\right)_{\nu}$ ;
5. 
 $\left(a,ab\right)_{\nu}=\left(a,-b\right)_{\nu}$ ;
6. 
 $\displaystyle\prod_{\nu\in\Nu}\left(a,b\right)_{\nu}=1$ .
In [11] it is shown that $D\mod\Q_{*}^{2}$ and $E_{\nu}$ for $\nu\in\Nu$ are the only invariants of rationally positive definite quadratic forms.
Lemma 7.
 $A\embed B$ iff there exists matrix $X$ such that
1. 
 $\dim X=\dim B-\dim A$ ;
2. 
 $D\left(X\right)\equiv D\left(A\right)D\left(B\right)\mod\Q_{*}^{2}$ ;
3. 
for every $\nu$ there holds $E_{\nu}\left(X\right)=E_{\nu}\left(B\right)\cdot E_{\nu}\left(A\right)\cdot\left(D\left(A\right),-D\left(B\right)\right)_{\nu}$ .
Proof.
Without loss of generality we assume that $A=\diag\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right)$ and $B=\diag\left(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}\right)$ . $A\embed B$ iff there exists $X=\diag\left(x_{n+1},\ldots,x_{m}\right)$ such that $C=\diag\left(a_{1},\ldots a_{n},x_{n+1},\ldots,x_{m}\right)\eqQ B$ .
As stated above, for $C\eqQ B$ we need to satisfy the following two conditions:
1. 
 $D\left(C\right)\equiv D\left(B\right)\mod\Q_{*}^{2}$ 
2. 
 $E_{\nu}(C)=E_{\nu}(B)$ for every $\nu\in\Nu$ .
 $D\left(C\right)=D\left(A\right)D\left(X\right)\equiv D\left(B\right)\mod\Q_{*}^{2}$ so $D\left(X\right)\equiv D\left(A\right)D\left(B\right)\mod\Q_{*}^{2}$ .
Then for every $\nu\in\Nu$ we get
 $E_{\nu}\left(B\right)=E_{\nu}\left(C\right)=\prod_{i<j\leqslant n}\left(a_{i},a_{j}\right)_{\nu}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j=n+1}^{m}\left(a_{i},x_{j}\right)_{\nu}\cdot\prod_{n+1\leqslant i<j}\left(x_{i},x_{j}\right)_{\nu}=E_{\nu}\left(A\right)\cdot\left(D\left(A\right),D\left(X\right)\right)_{\nu}\cdot E_{\nu}\left(X\right)$ 
 $E_{\nu}\left(X\right)=E_{\nu}\left(A\right)\cdot E_{\nu}\left(B\right)\cdot\left(D\left(A\right),D\left(A\right)D\left(B\right)\right)_{\nu}=E_{\nu}\left(A\right)\cdot E_{\nu}\left(B\right)\cdot\left(D\left(A\right),-D\left(B\right)\right)_{\nu}$ 
∎
Now we need to state Proposition 7 in Chapter 4 from [11].
Proposition.
Let numbers $D$ , $E_{\nu}$ and $\left(r,s\right)$ satisfy the following conditions:
1. 
 $E_{v}=1$ for almost all $\nu\in\Nu$ and $\prod_{\nu\in\Nu}E_{\nu}=1$ 
2. 
 $E_{\nu}=1$ if $n=1$ or if $n=2$ and image of $D_{v}$ of $D$ in $\Q_{\nu}/\Q_{\nu}^{2}$ equals $-1$ .
3. 
 $r,s\geqslant 0$ and $r+s=n$ 
4. 
 $D_{\infty}=\left(-1\right)^{s}$ ,   $E_{\infty}=\left(-1\right)^{s\left(s-1\right)/2}$ 
Then there exists quadratic form of rank $n$ over $\Q$ with invariants $D,E_{\nu}$ and $\left(r,s\right)$ , where $\left(r,s\right)$ is the signature of form $X$ .
Combining these statements we obtain the following lemma.
Lemma 8.
Consider $q_{1}\in\mcQ_{n}$ and $q_{2}\in\mcQ_{m}$ . If $m\geqslant n+3$ then $q_{1}\embed q_{2}$ .
For every $k$ we define
 $\lambda_{k}=\begin{cases}k+1,\quad k\text{ is even; }\\
\left(k+1\right)/2,\quad k\text{ is odd. }\end{cases}$ 
We can see that $D\left(S_{k}\right)=\frac{k+1}{2^{k}}\equiv\lambda_{k}\mod\Q_{*}^{2}$ 
Lemma 9.
 $E_{\nu}\left(S_{k}\right)=\left(k+1,\lambda_{k-1}\right)_{\nu}=\begin{cases}\left(k+1,-2\right)_{\nu},\quad k\text{ is even; }\\
\left(k+1,-1\right)_{\nu},\quad k\text{ is odd. }\end{cases}$ 
Proof.
We prove this statement by induction over $k$ .
For $k=1$ we get $E_{\nu}\left(S_{k}\right)=1$ and $\left(2,-2\right)_{\nu}=1$ . Then for every $k$ we get
 $E_{\nu}\left(S_{k+1}\right)=E_{\nu}\left(S_{k}\right)\cdot\left(D\left(S_{k}\right),\frac{k+1}{2\left(k+2\right)}\right)_{\nu}=E_{\nu}\left(S_{k}\right)\cdot\left(\lambda_{k},\frac{\left(k+2\right)\left(k+1\right)}{2}\right)_{\nu}=$ 
 $=\left(k+1,\lambda_{k-1}\right)_{\nu}\cdot\left(\lambda_{k},\frac{\left(k+1\right)\left(k+2\right)}{2}\right)_{\nu}=\left(k+1,\lambda_{k}\right)_{\nu}\cdot\left(k+1,\lambda_{k-1}\lambda_{k}\right)_{\nu}\cdot\left(\lambda_{k},\frac{\left(k+1\right)\left(k+2\right)}{2}\right)_{\nu}=$ 
 $=\left(\lambda_{k},\frac{k+2}{2}\right)_{\nu}\cdot\left(k+1,\frac{k\left(k+1\right)}{2}\right)_{\nu}=\left(\lambda_{k},\frac{k+2}{2}\right)_{\nu}\cdot\left(k+1,2\right)_{\nu}=\left(\lambda_{k},k+2\right)_{\nu}\cdot\left(\lambda_{k},2\right)_{\nu}\cdot\left(k+1,2\right)_{\nu}$ 
Thus we need to check for every $k$ that $\left(\lambda_{k},2\right)_{\nu}=\left(k+1,2\right)_{\nu}$ .
If $k$ is even then this equality holds because $\lambda_{k}=k+1$ .
If $k$ is odd then $\left(\lambda_{k},2\right)_{\nu}=\left(\frac{k+1}{2},2\right)_{\nu}=\left(k+1,2\right)_{\nu}\cdot\left(2,2\right)_{\nu}$ . Now we only have to use the fact that $\left(2,2\right)_{\nu}=1$ holds for every $\nu\in\Nu$ .
∎
