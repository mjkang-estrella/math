Abstract
 
Let $[n]=X_{1}\cup X_{2}\cup X_{3}$ be a partition with $\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\rfloor\leq|X_{i}|\leq\lceil\frac{n}{3}\rceil$ and define $\mathcal{G}=\{G\subset[n]\colon|G\cap X_{i}|\leq 1,1\leq i\leq 3\}$ . It is easy to check that the trace $\mathcal{G}_{\mid Y}:=\{G\cap Y\colon G\in\mathcal{G}\}$ satisfies $|\mathcal{G}_{\mid Y}|\leq 12$ for all 4-sets $Y\subset[n]$ . For $n\geq 25$ it is proven that whenever $\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{[n]}$ satisfies $|\mathcal{F}|>|\mathcal{G}|$ then $|\mathcal{F}_{\mid C}|\geq 13$ for some $C\subset[n]$ , $|C|=4$ . Several further results of a similar flavor are established as well.
 
1 Introduction
Let $[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}$ be the standard $n$ -element set, $2^{[n]}$ its powerset. For a family $\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{[n]}$ and a subset $Y\subset[n]$ let $\mathcal{F}_{\mid Y}=\{F\cap Y\colon F\in\mathcal{F}\}$ denote the trace of $\mathcal{F}$ on $Y$ . Hajnal [3] introduced the arrow relation $(n,m)\rightarrow(a,b)$ to denote that for all $\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{[n]}$ with $|\mathcal{F}|\geq m$ there exists an $a$ -element set $Y\subset[n]$ such that $|\mathcal{F}_{\mid Y}|\geq b$ . For $\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{[n]}$ , let $\mathcal{F}\rightarrow(a,b)$ denote that there exists an $a$ -element set $Y\subset[n]$ such that $|\mathcal{F}_{\mid Y}|\geq b$ .
One of the most important results in extremal set theory, the Sauer-Shelah-Vapnik-Chervonenkis Theorem ([20],[22],[25]) is equivalent to the arrow relation
 $\displaystyle\left(n,1+\sum_{i<k}\binom{n}{i}\right)\rightarrow(k,2^{k})\mbox{ for all }n\geq k\geq 0.$ 
(1.1)
Lovász [17] conjectured and the first author [9] proved
 $\displaystyle\left(n,\left\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor+n+2\right)\rightarrow(3,7).$ 
(1.2)
A family $\mathcal{F}$ is called a down-set (or complex) if $F\in\mathcal{F}$ always implies $2^{F}\subset\mathcal{F}$ . Both the above results are direct consequences of the following
Lemma 1.1.
If $\mathcal{F}\not\rightarrow(a,b)$ for some family $\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{[n]}$ then there is a down-set with the same property.
Example 1.2.
Let $\ell$ be a positive integer and $[n]=X_{0}\cup\ldots\cup X_{\ell-1}$ a partition with $|X_{i}|=\left\lfloor\frac{n+i}{\ell}\right\rfloor$ , $0\leq i<\ell$ . Define
 $\mathcal{F}(n,\ell)=\{F\subset[n]\colon|F\cap X_{i}|\leq 1,0\leq i<\ell\}.$ 
Clearly, $|\mathcal{F}(n,\ell)|=\prod\limits_{0\leq i<\ell}\left(1+\left\lfloor\frac{n+i}{\ell}\right\rfloor\right)$ and for $Y\in\binom{[n]}{\ell+1}$ , $|\mathcal{F}(n,\ell)_{\mid Y}|\leq 3\cdot 2^{\ell-1}$ is easy to verify.
In particular, $|\mathcal{F}(n,2)|=\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{4}\rfloor+n+1$ shows that $(n,\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{4}\rfloor+n+1)\not\rightarrow(3,7)$ , i.e., the corresponding arrow relation does not hold.
For general $\ell$ , the example shows that
 $\displaystyle\left(n,\prod_{0\leq i<\ell}\left\lfloor\frac{n+\ell+i}{\ell}\right\rfloor\right)\not\rightarrow(\ell+1,3\cdot 2^{\ell-1}+1).$ 
(1.3)
As (1.1) and (1.2) show (1.3) is best possible for $\ell=1$ and 2.
It is very limited evidence but let us make a conjecture for the general case.
Conjecture 1.3.
 $\displaystyle\left(n,1+\prod_{0\leq i<\ell}\left\lfloor\frac{n+\ell+i}{\ell}\right\rfloor\right)\rightarrow(\ell+1,3\cdot 2^{\ell-1}+1)\mbox{ for all }n>\ell>0.$ 
(1.4)
As we will see in the next section, (1.4) is closely related to some classical results. Our main result settles the $\ell=3$ case for $n\geq 25$ .
Theorem 1.4.
(1.4) holds for $\ell=3$ and $n\geq 25$ .
In view of Lemma 1.1 to check the veracity of (1.4) we can restrict ourselves to down-sets. Moreover, we may assume that $\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{[n]}$ contains no members of size exceeding $\ell$ . We shall use these facts without further mention.
We need the following notations:
 $\mathcal{F}(i)=\{F\setminus\{i\}\colon i\in F\in\mathcal{F}\},\ \mathcal{F}(\bar{i})=\{F\in\mathcal{F}:i\notin F\}.$ 
Note that $|\mathcal{F}|=|\mathcal{F}(i)|+|\mathcal{F}(\bar{i})|$ . For $i,j\in[n]$ , we also use
 $\mathcal{F}(i,j)=\{F\setminus\{i,j\}\colon\{i,j\}\subset F\in\mathcal{F}\},\ \mathcal{F}(\bar{i},\bar{j})=\{F\in\mathcal{F}\colon F\cap\{i,j\}=\emptyset\}.$ 
For $\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{[n]}$ , let $\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}$ denote the subfamily $\{F\in\mathcal{F}\colon|F|=\ell\}$ .
2 Cancellative families
Let us recall that an $\ell$ -graph $\mathcal{H}\subset\binom{[n]}{\ell}$ is called cancellative if $\mathcal{H}$ contains no three edges $H_{1},H_{2},H_{3}$ such that $|H_{1}\cap H_{2}|=\ell-1$ and $H_{1}\bigtriangleup H_{2}\subset H_{3}$ where $\bigtriangleup$ denotes the symmetric difference.
Claim 2.1.
If $\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{[n]}$ is a down-set and $\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}$ is not cancellative, then $\mathcal{F}\rightarrow(\ell+1,3\cdot 2^{\ell-1}+1)$ .
Proof.
Choose $F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}\in\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}$ such that $|F_{1}\cap F_{2}|=\ell-1$ and $F_{1}\bigtriangleup F_{2}\subset F_{3}$ . Set $Y=F_{1}\cup F_{2}$ . Then $|Y|=\ell+1$ and both $2^{F_{1}}$ and $2^{F_{2}}$ are contained in $\mathcal{F}_{\mid Y}$ . Note that $|2^{F_{1}}\cup 2^{F_{2}}|=2\cdot 2^{\ell}-2^{\ell-1}=3\cdot 2^{\ell-1}$ . Since the 2-element set $F_{1}\bigtriangleup F_{2}$ is in $2^{Y}\setminus(2^{F_{1}}\cup 2^{F_{2}})$ and $F_{1}\bigtriangleup F_{2}\subset F_{3}$ , $F_{1}\bigtriangleup F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mid Y}$ as well. Thus $|\mathcal{F}_{\mid Y}|\geq 3\cdot 2^{\ell-1}+1$ .
The following statement was proved for $\ell=2$ by Mantel [18], for $\ell=3$ by Bollobás [2] and for $\ell=4$ by Sidorenko [23].
Theorem 2.2.
Let $2\leq\ell\leq 4$ and $\mathcal{H}\subset\binom{[n]}{\ell}$ . If $\mathcal{H}$ is cancellative then
 $\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}|\leq\prod_{0\leq i<\ell}\left\lfloor\frac{n+i}{\ell}\right\rfloor.$ 
(2.1)
Let us suppose that $\ell=3$ and $\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{[n]}$ is a down-set with $\mathcal{F}\not\rightarrow(4,13)$ . Then $\mathcal{F}^{(k)}=\emptyset$ for $k\geq 4$ and by (2.1) $|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}|\leq\lfloor\frac{n+2}{3}\rfloor\lfloor\frac{n+1}{3}\rfloor\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\rfloor$ . Consequently,
 $|\mathcal{F}|\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n+2}{3}\right\rfloor\left\lfloor\frac{n+1}{3}\right\rfloor\left\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\right\rfloor+\binom{n}{2}+\binom{n}{1}+\binom{n}{0}.$ 
That is,
 $\displaystyle\left(n,\left\lfloor\frac{n+2}{3}\right\rfloor\left\lfloor\frac{n+1}{3}\right\rfloor\left\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\right\rfloor+\binom{n}{2}+n+2\right)\rightarrow(4,13).$ 
(2.2)
This shows that (1.4) is “asymptotically” true for $\ell=3$ .
Similarly, the $\ell=4$ case of (2.1) and Lemma 1.1 imply
 $\displaystyle\left(n,\prod_{0\leq i<4}\left\lfloor\frac{n+i}{4}\right\rfloor+\binom{n}{3}+\binom{n}{2}+n+2\right)\rightarrow(5,25).$ 
(2.3)
Unfortunately, (2.1) is no longer true for $\ell\geq 5$ . In particular for $\ell=5$ and 6 Frankl and Füredi [12] showed that the maximum possible size $m(n,\ell)$ of a cancellative family $\mathcal{F}\subset\binom{[n]}{\ell}$ satisfies
 $\displaystyle m(n,5)\leq\frac{6}{11^{4}}n^{5}\mbox{ with equality iff }11|n\mbox{ and }$ 
 $\displaystyle m(n,6)\leq\frac{11}{12^{5}}n^{6}\mbox{ with equality iff }12|n,$ 
which is much larger than $(n/\ell)^{\ell}$ .
Let us define $m^{*}(n,\ell)$ as the maximum size of $\mathcal{F}\subset\binom{[n]}{\ell}$ where $\mathcal{F}$ contains no three distinct edges satisfying $F_{1}\bigtriangleup F_{2}\subset F_{3}$ . Unlike with cancellative families, we do not require $|F_{1}\cap F_{2}|=\ell-1$ . Thus $m^{*}(n,\ell)\leq m(n,\ell)$ .
Katona conjectured $m^{*}(n,\ell)=\prod\limits_{0\leq i<\ell}\left\lfloor\frac{n+i}{\ell}\right\rfloor$ . However, Shearer [21] disproved this conjecture for $\ell>10$ .
3 Proof of Theorem 1.4
We need the following inequality.
Lemma 3.1.
Let $a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m}\geq 0$ . Then
 $\displaystyle\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}a_{i}a_{j}\leq\frac{m-1}{2m}\left(\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}a_{i}\right)^{2}.$ 
(3.1)
Proof.
Note that
 $\displaystyle\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}a_{i}a_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}a_{i}\right)^{2}-\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}a_{i}^{2}\right).$ 
(3.2)
Since $x^{2}$ is convex, by Jensen’s inequality
 $\frac{1}{m}\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}a_{i}^{2}\geq\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}a_{i}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\left(\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}a_{i}\right)^{2}.$ 
It follows that
 $\sum\limits_{1\leq i\leq m}a_{i}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{m}\left(\sum\limits_{1\leq i\leq m}a_{i}\right)^{2}.$ 
By (3.2) we conclude that (3.1) holds.
Proof of Theorem 1.4.
Let $\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{[n]}$ be a down-set satisfying $\mathcal{F}\not\rightarrow(4,13)$ and $|\mathcal{F}|$ is maximal. Clearly, $|\mathcal{F}|\geq|\mathcal{F}(n,3)|=\lfloor\frac{n+3}{3}\rfloor\lfloor\frac{n+4}{3}\rfloor\lfloor\frac{n+5}{3}\rfloor$ .
We showed that $\mathcal{F}^{(3)}$ is cancellative however we are not going to use the bound (2.1).
Claim 3.2.
Let $\mathcal{F}^{\prime}$ be a family obtained from $\mathcal{F}$ by removing all edges $F\in\mathcal{F}$ with $y\in F$ and adding the edges $\{y\}\cup G$ for $G\in\mathcal{F}(x,\bar{y})$ . Then $\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\not\rightarrow(4,13)$ .
Proof.
Indeed, otherwise let $C$ be a 4-set satisfying $|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\mid C}|\geq 13$ . Then clearly $y\in C$ . If $x\in C$ , then by $\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(x,y)=\emptyset$ 
 $|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\mid C}|\leq 2^{|C\setminus\{x\}|}+2^{|C\setminus\{y\}|}-2^{|C\setminus\{x,y\}|}=2^{3}+2^{3}-2^{2}=12,$ 
a contradiction. Thus $x\notin C$ . Setting $C^{\prime}=(C\setminus\{y\})\cup\{x\}$ , $|\mathcal{F}_{\mid C^{\prime}}|=|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\mid C}|\geq 13$ , a contradiction again.
There are two simple conditions to guarantee for a 4-set $C$ (with respect to a family $\mathcal{F}$ ) that $|\mathcal{F}_{\mid C}|\leq 12$ .
(i) 
 $\exists\{x,y\}\in\binom{C}{2}$ such that no $F\in\mathcal{F}$ contains $\{x,y\}$ .
(ii) 
 $\exists\{x,y\}\in\binom{C}{2}$ such that $\mathcal{F}(x)=\mathcal{F}(y)$ .
Note that if $\{x,y\}\subset F\in\mathcal{F}$ then $F\setminus\{x\}\in\mathcal{F}(x)$ but $F\setminus\{x\}\notin\mathcal{F}(y)$ . Thus (ii) implies (i).
In view of these conditions if $\{x,y\}\not\subset F$ for all $F\in\mathcal{F}$ then we can symmetrize $\mathcal{F}$ by removing all $F\in\mathcal{F}$ with $y\in F$ and adding all $\{y\}\cup G$ with $G\in\mathcal{F}(x)$ . Thereby $\mathcal{F}(x)=\mathcal{F}(y)$ for the new family. By Claim 3.2 the new family preserves the property $\mathcal{F}\not\rightarrow(4,13)$ . If $|\mathcal{F}(x)|\geq|\mathcal{F}(y)|$ then the new family has at least as many members as the old one. Thus we may assume that for all distinct $x,y\in[n]$ either $\exists F\in\mathcal{F}$ with $\{x,y\}\subset F$ or $\mathcal{F}(x)=\mathcal{F}(y)$ .
It is easy to see that $\mathcal{F}(x)=\mathcal{F}(y)$ is an equivalence relation. Thus we get a partition $[n]=Z_{1}\cup Z_{2}\cup\ldots\cup Z_{r}$ and an auxiliary family $\mathcal{H}\subset 2^{[r]}$ such that each $Z_{i}$ is an equivalence class, $F\in\mathcal{F}$ iff $|F\cap Z_{i}|\leq 1$ for all $i$ and $\{i\colon F\cap Z_{i}\neq\emptyset\}\in\mathcal{H}$ . Let us choose $\mathcal{F}$ such that $r$ is minimal over all families $\mathcal{F}$ with $\mathcal{F}\not\rightarrow(4,13)$ and $|\mathcal{F}|$ maximal.
Note that $\mathcal{F}(x)=\mathcal{F}(y)$ forces that $x$ and $y$ are in the same $Z_{i}$ . Hence if $1\leq i<i^{\prime}\leq r$ , $x\in Z_{i}$ , $y\in Z_{i^{\prime}}$ then $\{x,y\}\subset F$ for some $F\in\mathcal{F}$ . Consequently, $\binom{[r]}{2}\subset\mathcal{H}$ .
Claim 3.3.
If $H,H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}^{(3)}$ then $|H\cap H^{\prime}|\leq 1$ .
Proof.
Suppose the contrary. WLOG $H=(1,2,3)$ , $H^{\prime}=(1,2,4)$ . Since $(3,4)\in\mathcal{H}$ , $|\mathcal{F}{\mid_{C}}|\geq 13$ for the corresponding $C=\{z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4}\}$ (where $z_{i}\in Z_{i}$ ), a contradiction.
Let $b_{i}=|Z_{i}|$ , $i=1,2,\ldots,r$ . If $r=3$ , then the theorem follows from the fact that $(b_{1}+1)(b_{2}+1)(b_{3}+1)$ is maximized when $b_{1}=\lfloor\frac{n+2}{3}\rfloor$ , $b_{2}=\lfloor\frac{n+1}{3}\rfloor$ and $b_{3}=\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\rfloor$ . Thus in the rest of the proof we assume $r\geq 4$ .
Claim 3.4.
For $x\in Z_{i}$ , $y\in Z_{j}$ with $i\neq j$ ,
 $\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}(x,\bar{y})|<|\mathcal{F}(y)|.$ 
(3.3)
Proof.
If $|\mathcal{F}(x,\bar{y})|\geq|\mathcal{F}(y)|$ then for every $y\in Z_{j}$ we remove all edges $F\in\mathcal{F}$ with $y\in F$ and add the edges $\{y\}\cup G$ for $G\in\mathcal{F}(x,\bar{y})$ . By Claim 3.2 the new family $\mathcal{F}^{\prime}$ satisfies $\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\not\rightarrow(4,13)$ and $|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|\geq|\mathcal{F}|$ . However, $\mathcal{F}^{\prime}$ has $r-1$ classes, contradicting the minimality of $r$ .
Claim 3.5.
There exists $z\in[n]$ such that
 $\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z)|>\frac{n^{2}}{9}-\frac{n}{2}+1.$ 
(3.4)
Proof.
Let $z\in[n]$ be a vertex with $|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z)|$ maximal. Note that $\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}^{(3)}\cup\mathcal{F}^{(2)}\cup\mathcal{F}^{(1)}\cup\mathcal{F}^{(0)}$ . Since $|\mathcal{F}^{(0)}|+|\mathcal{F}^{(1)}|=n+1$ 
and $|\mathcal{F}^{(2)}|\leq\binom{n}{2}$ ,
 $|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}|=|\mathcal{F}|-|\mathcal{F}^{(0)}|-|\mathcal{F}^{(1)}|-|\mathcal{F}^{(2)}|\geq\left\lfloor\frac{n+3}{3}\right\rfloor\left\lfloor\frac{n+4}{3}\right\rfloor\left\lfloor\frac{n+5}{3}\right\rfloor-n-1-\binom{n}{2}.$ 
It follows that
 $\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z)|\geq\frac{3|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}|}{n}\geq\frac{3}{n}\left\lfloor\frac{n+3}{3}\right\rfloor\left\lfloor\frac{n+4}{3}\right\rfloor\left\lfloor\frac{n+5}{3}\right\rfloor-\frac{3n}{2}-\frac{3}{2}-\frac{3}{n}.$ 
For $n=3t$ ,
 $|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z)|\geq\frac{3}{n}\frac{(n+3)^{3}}{27}-\frac{3n}{2}-\frac{3}{2}-\frac{3}{n}=\frac{n^{2}}{9}-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{3}{2}.$ 
For $n=3t+1$ and $n\geq 8$ ,
 $\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z)|\geq\frac{3}{n}\frac{(n+2)^{2}(n+5)}{27}-\frac{3n}{2}-\frac{3}{2}-\frac{3}{n}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{n^{2}}{9}-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{7}{6}-\frac{7}{9n}>\frac{n^{2}}{9}-\frac{n}{2}+1.$ 
For $n=3t+2$ and $n\geq 8$ ,
 $\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z)|\geq\frac{3}{n}\frac{(n+1)(n+4)^{2}}{27}-\frac{3n}{2}-\frac{3}{2}-\frac{3}{n}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{n^{2}}{9}-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{7}{6}-\frac{11}{9n}>\frac{n^{2}}{9}-\frac{n}{2}+1.$ 
Let $x\in Z_{i}$ and assume $z\in Z_{j}$ . If $i=j$ then clearly $\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x)=\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z)$ . If $i\neq j$ then $|\mathcal{F}(z,x)|\leq n-b_{i}-b_{j}+1$ . By (3.3),
 $|\mathcal{F}(z)|-|\mathcal{F}(x)|\leq|\mathcal{F}(z,x)|+|\mathcal{F}(z,\bar{x})|-|\mathcal{F}(x)|\leq|\mathcal{F}(x,z)|-1\leq n-b_{i}-b_{j}.$ 
Since $|\mathcal{F}^{(2)}(x)|=n-b_{i}$ and $|\mathcal{F}^{(2)}(z)|=n-b_{j}$ ,
 $\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z)|-|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x)|$ 
 $\displaystyle=(|\mathcal{F}(z)|-|\mathcal{F}^{(2)}(z)|-1)-(|\mathcal{F}(x)|-|\mathcal{F}^{(2)}(x)|-1)$ 
 $\displaystyle\leq n-b_{i}-b_{j}+(b_{j}-b_{i})$ 
 $\displaystyle=n-2b_{i}.$ 
By (3.4) and $n\geq 25$ , it follows that for all $x\in[n]$ 
 $\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x)|\geq|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z)|-(n-2b_{i})>\frac{n^{2}}{9}-\frac{3n}{2}+1+2b_{i}\geq\frac{n^{2}}{9}-\frac{3n}{2}+3\geq\frac{n^{2}}{18}.$ 
(3.5)
If $r=4$ , then by Claim 3.3 we may assume that $\mathcal{H}^{(3)}=\{(1,2,3)\}$ . Then $\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x)=\emptyset$ for all $x\in Z_{4}$ , contradicting (3.5).
Let us fix $x_{i}\in Z_{i}$ , $i=1,2,\ldots,r$ .
Claim 3.6.
 $r\neq 5$ .
Proof.
By Claim 3.3 and symmetry, we may assume that $\mathcal{H}^{(3)}\subset\{(1,2,3),(1,4,5)\}$ , $b_{2}\geq b_{3}$ and $b_{4}\geq b_{5}$ .
Then
 $|\mathcal{F}|=b_{1}(b_{2}b_{3}+b_{4}b_{5})+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 5}b_{i}b_{j}+n+1.$ 
Let $\mathcal{F}^{\prime}$ be the family obtained from $\mathcal{F}$ by merging $Z_{2}$ and $Z_{5}$ , $Z_{3}$ and $Z_{4}$ . Then
 $|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|=b_{1}(b_{2}+b_{5})(b_{3}+b_{4})+b_{1}(b_{2}+b_{5}+b_{3}+b_{4})+(b_{2}+b_{5})(b_{3}+b_{4})+n+1.$ 
Using $b_{1}\geq 1$ , we obtain that
 $|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|-|\mathcal{F}|=b_{1}(b_{2}b_{4}+b_{3}b_{5})-b_{2}b_{5}-b_{3}b_{4}\geq(b_{2}-b_{3})(b_{4}-b_{5})\geq 0.$ 
Clearly $\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\not\rightarrow(4,13)$ and $\mathcal{F}^{\prime}$ is 3-partite. This contradicts the minimality of $r$ .
Claim 3.7.
 $r\neq 6$ .
Proof.
If there are two disjoint edges in $\mathcal{H}^{(3)}$ , then by Claim 3.3 $|\mathcal{H}^{(3)}|=2$ . Without loss of generality, assume that $\mathcal{H}^{(3)}=\{(1,2,3),(4,5,6)\}$ and $b_{4}+b_{5}+b_{6}\leq\frac{n}{2}$ . Then by (3.5)
 $\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{4})|+|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{5})|+|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{6})|=b_{5}b_{6}+b_{4}b_{6}+b_{4}b_{5}>\frac{n^{2}}{6}.$ 
(3.6)
By (3.1), we infer that
 $b_{4}b_{5}+b_{4}b_{6}+b_{5}b_{6}\leq\frac{(b_{4}+b_{5}+b_{6})^{2}}{3}\leq\frac{n^{2}}{12},$ 
contradicting (3.6).
Thus $|H\cap H^{\prime}|=1$ for all $H,H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}^{(3)}$ . Up to isomorphism there is only one triple-system with four triples on six vertices. By symmetry we may assume that
 $\mathcal{H}^{(3)}\subset\{(1,3,5),(1,4,6),(2,3,6),(2,4,5)\}.$ 
Then
 $\displaystyle b_{1}b_{5}+b_{2}b_{6}\geq|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{3})|>\frac{n^{2}}{18},$ 
 $\displaystyle b_{1}b_{6}+b_{2}b_{5}\geq|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{4})|>\frac{n^{2}}{18},$ 
 $\displaystyle b_{1}b_{3}+b_{2}b_{4}\geq|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{5})|>\frac{n^{2}}{18},$ 
 $\displaystyle b_{1}b_{4}+b_{2}b_{3}\geq|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{6})|>\frac{n^{2}}{18}.$ 
Adding these inequalities, we get
 $\displaystyle(b_{1}+b_{2})(b_{3}+b_{4}+b_{5}+b_{6})>\frac{2n^{2}}{9}.$ 
(3.7)
Moreover,
 $\displaystyle b_{3}b_{5}+b_{4}b_{6}\geq|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{1})|>\frac{n^{2}}{18},$ 
 $\displaystyle b_{3}b_{6}+b_{4}b_{5}\geq|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{2})|>\frac{n^{2}}{18}.$ 
It implies that
 $\displaystyle(b_{3}+b_{4})(b_{5}+b_{6})>\frac{n^{2}}{9}.$ 
(3.8)
Note that $b_{1}+b_{2}+b_{3}+b_{4}+b_{5}+b_{6}=n$ . If $b_{1}+b_{2}\geq\frac{n}{3}$ , then $b_{3}+b_{4}+b_{5}+b_{6}\leq\frac{2n}{3}$ . It follows that $(b_{3}+b_{4})(b_{5}+b_{6})\leq\frac{n^{2}}{9}$ , contradicting (3.8). If $b_{1}+b_{2}<\frac{n}{3}$ , then
 $(b_{1}+b_{2})(b_{3}+b_{4}+b_{5}+b_{6})<\frac{2n}{9},$ 
contradicting (3.7).
For $H\in 2^{[r]}$ , let $b_{H}=\prod_{i\in H}b_{i}$ .
Claim 3.8.
 $r=7$ .
Proof.
Suppose that $r\neq 7$ . Then by Claims 3.6 and 3.7, $r\geq 8$ . For each $i=1,2,\ldots,r$ ,
 $|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{i})|=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{H}^{(3)}(i)}b_{P}>\frac{n^{2}}{18}.$ 
By Claim 3.3, $\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{i})\cap\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{j})=\emptyset$ for all $1\leq i<j\leq r$ . By (3.1), we obtain that
 $\displaystyle\frac{rn^{2}}{18}<\sum_{1\leq i\leq r}|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{i})|\leq\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq r}b_{i}b_{j}\leq\frac{r-1}{2r}\left(b_{1}+b_{2}+\ldots+b_{r}\right)^{2}=\frac{r-1}{2r}n^{2}.$ 
(3.9)
It follows that
 $\frac{r}{9}<1-\frac{1}{r},$ 
which leads to a contradiction for $r\geq 8$ .
Now we assume that $r=7$ .
Claim 3.9.
For $n\geq 17$ , $\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq 7}b_{i}\leq n/2$ .
Proof.
Assume that $b_{1}\geq b_{2}\geq\ldots\geq b_{7}$ . By (3.5), for all $x\in[n]$ 
 $|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x)|>\frac{n^{2}}{9}-\frac{3n}{2}+3.$ 
It is easy to check that for $n\geq 17$ the RHS is greater than $\frac{n^{2}}{32}$ . Assume that $b_{1}\geq\frac{n}{2}$ and we distinguish two cases.
Case 1. $(1,2,3)\in\mathcal{H}$ .
Then
 $|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{3})|\geq b_{1}b_{2},\ |\mathcal{F}^{(2)}(x_{3})|=n-b_{3},\ |\mathcal{F}(x_{1},x_{3})|=b_{2}+1,$ 
and
 $|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{1})|\leq b_{2}b_{3}+\left(\frac{n-b_{1}-b_{2}-b_{3}}{2}\right)^{2},\ |\mathcal{F}^{(2)}(x_{1})|=n-b_{1}.$ 
By (3.3), $|\mathcal{F}(x_{3},\overline{x_{1}})|<|\mathcal{F}(x_{1})|$ . It follows that
 $b_{1}b_{2}+n-b_{3}-(b_{2}+1)\leq b_{2}b_{3}+n-b_{1}+\left(\frac{n-b_{1}-b_{2}-b_{3}}{2}\right)^{2}.$ 
Equivalently,
 $\displaystyle b_{2}(b_{1}-b_{3})+b_{1}-b_{2}-b_{3}-1\leq\left(\frac{n-b_{1}-b_{2}-b_{3}}{2}\right)^{2}.$ 
(3.10)
Note that $b_{1}\geq\frac{n}{2}$ implies $b_{1}\geq b_{2}+b_{3}$ . If $b_{1}=b_{2}+b_{3}$ then $b_{1}+b_{2}+b_{3}=n$ and (3.10) cannot hold. Thus $b_{1}>b_{2}+b_{3}$ . Then (3.10) implies
 $\displaystyle b_{2}(b_{1}-b_{3})\leq\frac{n-b_{1}-b_{2}-b_{3}}{4}(n-b_{1}-b_{2}-b_{3}).$ 
(3.11)
If $b_{2}\geq\frac{n-b_{1}-b_{2}-b_{3}}{4}$ , then (3.11) implies
 $b_{1}-b_{3}\leq n-b_{1}-b_{2}-b_{3}.$ 
It follows that $2b_{1}+b_{2}\leq n$ , contradicting $b_{1}\geq\frac{n}{2}$ . Thus $b_{2}<\frac{n-b_{1}-b_{2}-b_{3}}{4}$ . That means $5b_{2}+b_{3}<n-b_{1}$ . Then $6\frac{b_{2}+b_{3}}{2}\leq n-b_{1}\leq\frac{n}{2}$ . It implies $\frac{b_{2}+b_{3}}{2}\leq\frac{n}{12}$ . Therefore
 $|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{1})|\leq\left(\frac{n}{12}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{n}{12}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{n}{12}\right)^{2}<\frac{n^{2}}{32},$ 
a contradiction.
Case 2. $(1,2,3)\notin\mathcal{H}$ .
Then
 $|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{1})|\leq b_{2}b_{4}+b_{3}b_{5}+b_{6}b_{7}.$ 
The maximum should be for $b_{6}=b_{7}=0$ , $b_{3}=b_{4}$ . Set $b_{2}=\alpha n$ , $b_{3}=b_{4}=\beta n$ , $b_{5}=\gamma n$ , then
 $\displaystyle\frac{|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{1})|}{n^{2}}\leq\alpha\beta+\beta\gamma=\frac{1}{2}[2\beta(\alpha+\gamma)]\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2\beta+\alpha+\gamma}{2}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{n-b_{1}}{2n}\right)^{2}.$ 
(3.12)
The RHS is at most $\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{32}$ for $b_{1}\geq\frac{n}{2}$ . Thus we get $|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{1})|\leq\frac{n^{2}}{32}$ , a contradiction.
By (3.9), we have
 $\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i\leq 7}|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{i})|\leq\frac{3n^{2}}{7}.$ 
(3.13)
By (3.4),
 $|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z)|>\frac{n^{2}}{9}-\frac{n}{2}+1.$ 
Assume $z\in Z_{j}$ . Then Claim 3.9 implies $n-b_{j}\geq\frac{n}{2}$ . By (3.5), we obtain that
 $\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i\leq 7}|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{i})|$ 
 $\displaystyle=|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z)|+\sum_{i\neq j}\left(\frac{n^{2}}{9}-\frac{3n}{2}+1+2b_{i}\right)$ 
 $\displaystyle>\frac{n^{2}}{9}-\frac{n}{2}+1+6\left(\frac{n^{2}}{9}-\frac{3n}{2}+1\right)+2(n-b_{j})$ 
 $\displaystyle\geq\frac{7n^{2}}{9}-\frac{19n}{2}+7+n$ 
 $\displaystyle\geq\frac{7n^{2}}{9}-\frac{17n}{2}+7.$ 
It is easy to check that the RHS is greater than $\frac{3n^{2}}{7}$ for $n\geq 24$ , contradicting (3.13).
4 Other results for $(n,m)\rightarrow(4,b)$ 
Let us introduce the general notation
 $m(n,a,b)=\min\left\{m\colon(n,m)\rightarrow(a,b)\right\}.$ 
In this section we consider $m(n,4,b)$ for $b\leq 16$ . With this notation the Sauer-Shelah-Vapnik-Chervonenkis Theorem is equivalent to $m(n,4,16)=1+\sum\limits_{0\leq i\leq 3}\binom{n}{i}$ .
For $5<b<16$ , it is easy to see that all extremal families satisfying $\mathcal{F}\not\rightarrow(4,b)$ span $[n]$ , i.e., $\cup_{F\in\mathcal{F}}F=[n]$ . Hence $\binom{[n]}{\leq 1}\subset\mathcal{F}$ . This motivates us to introduce the following auxiliary definitions. Set $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}=\mathcal{F}^{(2)}\cup\mathcal{F}^{(3)}$ and say that $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}$ is complete if $\partial\mathcal{F}^{(3)}\subset\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ , that is, if $P\subset T\in\mathcal{F}^{(3)}$ and $|P|=2$ then $P\in\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ .
Let us introduce the notation $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\hookrightarrow(4,c)$ if there exists a 4-set $C$ with $|\mathcal{F}^{(2)}\cap\binom{C}{2}|+|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}\cap\binom{C}{3}|\geq c$ . If $\mathcal{F}$ is a down-set with $|F|\leq 3$ for all $F\in\mathcal{F}$ and $\cup\mathcal{F}=[n]$ then $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\hookrightarrow(4,c)$ is equivalent to $\mathcal{F}\rightarrow(4,c+5)$ .
Finally, for $1\leq c<11$ we introduce the notation
 $\tilde{m}(n,4,c)=\min\left\{\tilde{m}\colon|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}|\geq\tilde{m}\mbox{ implies }\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\hookrightarrow(4,c)\mbox{ for a complete family }\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}\right\}.$ 
Clearly, $\tilde{m}(n,4,c)=m(n,4,c+5)-n-1$ .
Let $\mathcal{T}(r,n)$ be a complete $r$ -partite graph on $n$ vertices with each part of size $\lfloor\frac{n}{r}\rfloor$ or $\lceil\frac{n}{r}\rceil$ and let $t(r,n)$ be the number of edges in $\mathcal{T}(r,n)$ . We have the following results.
Table 1: $\tilde{m}(n,4,c)$ for $1\leq c\leq 8$ , $n\geq 5$ 
 $\tilde{m}(n,4,1)=1$ 
 $\tilde{m}(n,4,2)=2$ 
 $\tilde{m}(n,4,3)=\lfloor\frac{2}{3}n\rfloor+1$ 
 $\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{3/2}+o(n^{3/2})\leq\tilde{m}(n,4,4)\leq\frac{1}{2}n^{3/2}+O(n)$ 
 $\tilde{m}(n,4,5)=\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{4}\rfloor+1$ 
 $\tilde{m}(n,4,6)=t(3,n)+1$ 
 $\tilde{m}(n,4,7)=\binom{n}{2}+1$ for $n\neq 6$ , $\tilde{m}(6,4,7)=17$ 
 $\tilde{m}(n,4,8)=\left\lfloor\frac{n+2}{3}\right\rfloor\left\lfloor\frac{n+1}{3}\right\rfloor\left\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\right\rfloor+1$ for $n\geq 25$ 
The cases $c=9,10$ will be discussed later. To prove the above statements for each particular choice of $c$ , we assume that $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}=\mathcal{F}^{(2)}\cup\mathcal{F}^{(3)}$ is a complete family with $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\not\hookrightarrow(4,c)$ .
Since $\mathcal{F}^{(3)}\neq\emptyset$ forces $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\hookrightarrow(4,4)$ , for the case $1\leq c\leq 4$ we may assume $\mathcal{F}^{(3)}=\emptyset$ . Then $\tilde{m}(n,4,c)=c$ is trivial for $c=1$ and $c=2$ .
Let $c=3$ . Consider $\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ , a graph in which no four vertices span more than 2 edges. Thus $\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ has maximum degree at most two and without a path or cycle of length three. Hence each connected component of $\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ is a single edge or a path of length two. Consequently, $|\mathcal{F}^{(2)}|\leq\frac{2}{3}n$ , proving $\tilde{m}(n,4,3)=\lfloor\frac{2}{3}n\rfloor+1$ .
For $c=4$ , $\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ is a graph that contains no subgraph on 4 vertices with 4 or more edges. Let $C_{3}^{+}$ be a triangle plus a pendant edge. It follows that $\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ is $C_{3}^{+}$ -free and $C_{4}$ -free. Consequently if $\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ contains a triangle, then it is a connected component. It follows that each connected component of $\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ with at least 4 vertices is $\{C_{3},C_{4}\}$ -free. For a given family $\mathscr{F}$ of graphs, let $ex(n,\mathscr{F})$ denote the maximum number of edges in an $n$ -vertex graph which does not contain any member in $\mathscr{F}$ as its subgraph. The Zarankiewicz number $z(n,C_{4})$ is the maximum number of edges in an $n$ -vertex bipartite graph without containing a $C_{4}$ . It is well known that $z(n,C_{4})=\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{3/2}+o(n^{3/2})$ (see [6], [15]). Since bipartite graphs are $C_{3}$ -free, we see that $ex(n,\{C_{3},C_{4}\})\geq z(n,C_{4})$ .
Erdős-Rényi-Sós [7] and Brown [4] showed that $ex(q^{2}+q+1,C_{4})\geq\frac{1}{2}q(q+1)^{2}$ for all prime powers $q$ . Füredi [13, 14] proved that $ex(q^{2}+q+1,C_{4})=\frac{1}{2}q(q+1)^{2}$ for all prime powers $q\geq 14$ . As
it is shown in [16] this implies $ex(n,C_{4})=\frac{1}{2}n^{3/2}+O(n)$ all $n$ . Thus, $ex(n,\{C_{3},C_{4}\})\leq ex(n,C_{4})=\frac{1}{2}n^{3/2}+O(n)$ . These results imply that $\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{3/2}+o(n^{3/2})\leq\tilde{m}(n,4,4)\leq\frac{1}{2}n^{3/2}+O(n)$ .
Proposition 4.1.
 $\tilde{m}(n,4,5)=\left\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor+1.$ 
Proof.
Note that $\mathcal{T}(2,n)\not\hookrightarrow(4,5)$ . This shows that $\tilde{m}(n,4,5)\geq\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{4}\rfloor+1$ .
Let $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}=\mathcal{F}^{(2)}\cup\mathcal{F}^{(3)}$ be a complete family satisfying $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\not\hookrightarrow(4,5)$ .
We prove $|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}|\leq\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{4}\rfloor$ by induction on $n$ . Clearly it holds for $n=4$ . Now we assume that it holds for $4,5,\ldots,n-1$ and prove it for $n$ . If there exists $F_{0}\in\tilde{\mathcal{F}}$ with $|F_{0}|=3$ , then for any $y\in[n]\setminus F_{0}$ , $\{x,y\}\notin\tilde{\mathcal{F}}$ for all $x\in F_{0}$ . It follows that $|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}|\leq\lfloor\frac{(n-3)^{2}}{4}\rfloor+4\leq\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{4}\rfloor$ . Thus we may assume that $\mathcal{F}^{(3)}=\emptyset$ .
If there are two triangles with a common edge in $\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ , let $C$ be the set of these 4 vertices. Then $|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\mid C}|\geq 5$ , a contradiction. Thus $\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ contains no two triangles with a common edge. Assume $\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ contains a triangle, say $\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}$ . Then each $y\in[n]\setminus\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}$ has at most one neighbor in $\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}$ . Therefore,
 $|\{P\in\mathcal{F}^{(2)}\colon P\cap\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}\neq\emptyset\}|\leq 3+(n-3)=n.$ 
Using the induction hypothesis, it follows that $|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}|\leq\lfloor\frac{(n-3)^{2}}{4}\rfloor+n\leq\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{4}\rfloor$ . Finally if $\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ is triangle-free, then by Mantel’s theorem [18], $|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}|=|\mathcal{F}^{(2)}|\leq\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{4}\rfloor$ .
Proposition 4.2.
 $\tilde{m}(n,4,6)=t(3,n)+1.$ 
Proof.
Clearly $\mathcal{T}(3,n)\not\hookrightarrow(4,6)$ . We see that $\tilde{m}(n,4,6)\geq t(3,n)+1$ .
Let $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}=\mathcal{F}^{(2)}\cup\mathcal{F}^{(3)}$ be a complete family satisfying $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\not\hookrightarrow(4,6)$ .
We prove $|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}|\leq t(3,n)$ by induction on $n$ . Clearly it holds for $n=4$ . Now assume that it holds for $4,5,\ldots,n-1$ and we prove it for $n$ . If there exists $F_{0}=\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}\in\tilde{\mathcal{F}}$ , then by $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\not\hookrightarrow(4,6)$ for every $y\in[n]\setminus F$ at most one of $\{x_{1},y\},\{x_{2},y\},\{x_{3},y\}$ is in $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}$ . It follows that
 $|\{P\in\mathcal{F}^{(2)}\colon P\cap F_{0}\neq\emptyset\}|\leq 3+n-3=n.$ 
Note that $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\not\hookrightarrow(4,6)$ implies $|F\cap F^{\prime}|\leq 1$ for all distinct $F,F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{(3)}$ . We infer that $\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{i})$ is a matching. Let $\mathcal{G}(x_{i})=\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x_{i})\cap\binom{[n]\setminus F_{0}}{2}$ , $i=1,2,3$ .
We claim that for $1\leq i<j\leq 3$ , $\mathcal{G}(x_{i})$ and $\mathcal{G}(x_{j})$ are disjoint. For otherwise since $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}$ is complete, we shall find $y\in[n]\setminus F_{0}$ such that two of $\{x_{1},y\},\{x_{2},y\},\{x_{3},y\}$ are in $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}$ , a contradiction. Hence $\mathcal{G}(x_{1})\cup\mathcal{G}(x_{2})\cup\mathcal{G}(x_{3})$ is a matching. Therefore,
 $|\{F\in\mathcal{F}^{(3)}\colon F\cap F_{0}\neq\emptyset\}|\leq 1+\left\lfloor\frac{n-3}{2}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor.$ 
By the induction hypothesis,
 $\displaystyle|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}|\leq t(3,n-3)+n+\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor.$ 
(4.1)
Claim 4.3.
 $\displaystyle t(3,n)-t(3,n-3)=2n-3.$ 
(4.2)
Proof.
Note that
 $t(3,n)=\binom{n}{2}-\binom{\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\rfloor}{2}-\binom{\lfloor\frac{n+1}{3}\rfloor}{2}-\binom{\lfloor\frac{n+2}{3}\rfloor}{2}.$ 
Then
 $\displaystyle t(3,n)-t(3,n-3)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\binom{n}{2}-\binom{n-3}{2}-\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\right\rfloor-1+\left\lfloor\frac{n+1}{3}\right\rfloor-1+\left\lfloor\frac{n+2}{3}\right\rfloor-1\right)$ 
 $\displaystyle=(3n-6)-(n-3)=2n-3.$ 
Since $n\geq 5$ implies $n+\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor\leq 2n-3$ , by (4.1) and (4.2) we obtain $|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}|\leq t(3,n)$ .
Thus we may assume that $\mathcal{F}^{(3)}=\emptyset$ .
Since $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\not\hookrightarrow(4,6)$ implies $\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ is $K_{4}$ -free, by Turán’s Theorem [24] $|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}|=|\mathcal{F}^{(2)}|\leq t(3,n)$ .
Proposition 4.4.
 $\tilde{m}(n,4,7)=\binom{n}{2}+1$ for $n\neq 6$ and $\tilde{m}(6,4,7)=17$ .
Proof.
Note that $\binom{[n]}{2}\not\hookrightarrow(4,7)$ . It follows that $\tilde{m}(n,4,7)\geq\binom{n}{2}+1$ .
For $n=6$ , define $\mathcal{F}^{(3)}=\{\{1,3,5\},\{1,4,6\},\{2,3,6),\{2,4,5\}\}$ , $\mathcal{F}^{(2)}=\partial\mathcal{F}^{(3)}$ and $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}=\mathcal{F}^{(2)}\cup\mathcal{F}^{(3)}$ . Then $\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ is a complete 3-partite graph on parts $\{1,2\}$ , $\{3,4\}$ and $\{5,6\}$ . Now every 4-set $C\subset[6]$ contains at least one full part and at most one edge in $\mathcal{F}^{(3)}$ . It follows that $|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\mid C}|\leq 1+(6-1)=6$ . Thus $\tilde{m}(6,4,7)\geq 4+12+1=17$ .
Suppose that $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}=\mathcal{F}^{(2)}\cup\mathcal{F}^{(3)}$ is a complete family of the maximal size satisfying $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\not\hookrightarrow(4,7)$ .
Claim 4.5.
For any $P=\{z_{1},z_{2}\}\notin\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ , $|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z_{1})|\leq\lfloor\frac{n-2}{2}\rfloor$ and $|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z_{2})|\leq\lfloor\frac{n-2}{2}\rfloor$ .
Proof.
Note that $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\not\hookrightarrow(4,7)$ implies $|F\cap F^{\prime}|\leq 1$ for all distinct $F,F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{(3)}$ . It follows that $\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(x)$ is a matching for all $x\in[n]$ .
Let $T_{1},T_{2},\ldots,T_{r}$ be the triples in $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}$ that contain $z_{1}$ . Since $P\notin\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ , none of them contain $z_{2}$ and $T_{1}\setminus\{z_{1}\},\ldots,T_{r}\setminus\{z_{1}\}$ are pairwise disjoint. Hence $|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z_{1})|=r\leq\lfloor\frac{n-2}{2}\rfloor$ . Similarly, $|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z_{2})|\leq\lfloor\frac{n-2}{2}\rfloor$ .
Let us construct a bipartite graph $\mathcal{B}$ between $\mathcal{F}^{(3)}$ and $\binom{[n]}{2}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ by connecting $T\in\mathcal{F}^{(3)}$ and $P\in\binom{[n]}{2}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ iff $T\cap P\neq\emptyset$ . Note that in this case $|T\cap P|=1$ by completeness of $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}$ .
For $x\notin T\in\mathcal{F}^{(3)}$ , $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\not\hookrightarrow(4,7)$ implies that at least one of the edges $\{x,y\}$ , $y\in T$ is missing from $\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ . Thus the degree of $T$ in $\mathcal{B}$ is at least $n-3$ . Should the maximum degree of $P\in\binom{[n]}{2}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ in $\mathcal{B}$ be at most $n-3$ , $|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}|\leq\binom{n}{2}-|\mathcal{F}^{(2)}|$ and thereby $|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}|\leq\binom{n}{2}$ would follow.
Assume next that $P=\{z_{1},z_{2}\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ and it has degree at least $n-2$ . By Claim 4.5 $|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z_{i})|\leq\lfloor\frac{n-2}{2}\rfloor$ , $i=1,2$ . If $n$ is odd we infer $|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z_{1})|+|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z_{2})|\leq n-3$ , a contradiction. The only remaining possibility is that $n$ is even and $\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z_{i})$ is a perfect matching for $i=1,2$ . We need only one of them.
Let $\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z_{1})=\{E_{i}\colon 1\leq i\leq\frac{n-2}{2}\}$ . We claim that at least two of the possible four edges between $E_{i}$ and $E_{j}$ are missing from $\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ . Indeed otherwise we fix $x\in E_{j}$ that is joined (in $\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ ) to both vertices of $E_{i}$ . However this forces that $\{z_{1},x\}\cup E_{i}$ span a $K_{4}$ in $\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ whence $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\hookrightarrow(4,7)$ .
Consequently, together with $P$ there are at least $2\binom{\frac{n-2}{2}}{2}+1$ missing edges from $\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ . As to $T\in\mathcal{F}^{(3)}$ , $T\cap P=\emptyset$ would force that $T\cup\{z_{i}\}$ spans a $K_{4}$ in $\mathcal{F}^{(2)}$ and $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\hookrightarrow(4,7)$ . Thus $|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}|=|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z_{1})|+|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}(z_{2})|\leq n-2$ . For $n\geq 8$ , $2\binom{\frac{n-2}{2}}{2}+1>n-2$ implies $|\mathcal{F}^{(2)}|+|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}|<\binom{n}{2}$ and we are done.
For $n=6$ we infer $|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}|=|\mathcal{F}^{(2)}|+|\mathcal{F}^{(3)}|\leq\binom{6}{2}-3+4=16$ .
What remains are $m(n,4,14)$ and $m(n,4,15)$ . These are closely related to the famous unsolved problems of Turán on $3$ -graphs: $K_{4}^{(3)}$ and $K_{4}^{(3)-}$ , where $K_{4}^{(3)}$ denotes the complete 3-graph on 4 vertices and $K_{4}^{(3)-}$ denotes $K_{4}^{(3)}$ minus an edge. For a $k$ -graph $F$ , let $ex_{k}(n,F)$ denote the maximum number of edges in an $n$ -vertex $k$ -graph which does not contain $F$ as a subgraph. It is well known that $\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\binom{n}{k}^{-1}ex_{k}(n,F)$ exists. It is called the Turán density of $F$ and denoted by $\pi(F)$ . Turán [24] proposed a construction showing that $\pi(K_{4}^{(3)})\geq\frac{5}{9}$ . Chung and Lu [5] proved $\pi(K_{4}^{(3)})\leq\frac{3+\sqrt{17}}{12}\approx 0.593592\cdots$ . By applying the flag algebra method invented by Razborov, Razborov [19] showed $\pi(K_{4}^{(3)})\leq 0.561666$ . For $K_{4}^{(3)-}$ , Frankl and Füredi [11] proved that $\frac{2}{7}\leq\pi(K_{4}^{(3)-})\leq\frac{1}{3}$ . In [8], by using the flag algebra method Falgas-Ravry and Vaughan showed $\pi(K_{4}^{(3)-})\leq 0.286889$ .
Let us derive the formula for $m(n,4,14)$ and $m(n,4,15)$ from a more general statement. In analogy with $3$ -graphs let $K_{r}^{(k)}$ and $K_{r}^{(k)-}$ denote the complete $k$ -graph and complete $k$ -graph minus an edge on $r$ vertices, respectively.
Proposition 4.6.
Let $\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{[n]}$ be a down-set. Then (i) and (ii) hold.
(i) 
 $\mathcal{F}\not\rightarrow(k+1,2^{k+1}-1)$ iff $\mathcal{F}^{(k)}$ is $K_{k+1}^{(k)}$ -free.
(ii) 
 $\mathcal{F}\not\rightarrow(k+1,2^{k+1}-2)$ iff $\mathcal{F}^{(k)}$ is $K_{k+1}^{(k)-}$ -free.
Proof.
Since the proofs are almost identical let us show (ii) only. If $Y\in\binom{[n]}{k+1}$ spans $K_{k+1}^{(k)-}$ in $\mathcal{F}$ , then being a down-set forces $\binom{Y}{\ell}\subset\mathcal{F}$ for all $0\leq\ell<k$ . Hence $|\mathcal{F}_{\mid Y}|\geq 2^{k+1}-2$ .
On the other hand if $\mathcal{F}$ is a $K_{k+1}^{(k)-}$ -free down-set then $|F|\leq k$ for all $F\in\mathcal{F}$ and $|\mathcal{F}\cap\binom{Y}{k}|\leq\binom{k+1}{k}-2$ for all $Y\in\binom{[n]}{k+1}$ . Thus $\mathcal{F}\not\rightarrow(k+1,2^{k+1}-2)$ .
Corollary 4.7.
(i) 
 $m(n,k+1,2^{k+1}-1)=1+\sum\limits_{0\leq\ell<k}\binom{n}{\ell}+ex_{k}(n,K_{k+1}^{(k)})$ .
(ii) 
 $m(n,k+1,2^{k+1}-2)=1+\sum\limits_{0\leq\ell<k}\binom{n}{\ell}+ex_{k}(n,K_{k+1}^{(k)-})$ .
Let us close this paper by stating an old but attractive conjecture. Recall that $\mathcal{F}$ is antichain if $F\subset F^{\prime}$ never holds for distinct members $F,F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}$ .
Conjecture 4.8 ([10]).
Let $k$ be a non-negative integer, $n\geq 2k$ . Suppose that $\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{[n]}$ is an antichain with $\mathcal{F}\not\rightarrow(k+1,2^{k+1})$ . Then $|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n}{k}$ .
Let us note that the statement was proved in [10] for $k\leq 2$ and by Anstee and Sali [1] for $k=3$ .
