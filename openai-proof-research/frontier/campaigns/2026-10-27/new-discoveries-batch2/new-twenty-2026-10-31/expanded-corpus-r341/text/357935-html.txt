Abstract
 
We study the distinguishability of LRS (LRS) codes by defining and analyzing analogs of the square-code and the Overbeck distinguisher for classical RS and Gabidulin codes, respectively. Our main results show that the square-code distinguisher works for GLRS (GLRS) codes defined with the trivial automorphism, whereas the Overbeck-type distinguisher can handle LRS codes in the general setting. We further show how to recover defining code parameters from any generator matrix of such codes in the zero-derivation case. For other choices of automorphisms and derivations simulations indicate that these distinguishers and recovery algorithms do not work. The corresponding LRS and GLRS codes might hence be of interest for code-based cryptography.
 
1 Introduction
Researchers have made tremendous progress in the design and realization of quantum computers in the last decades.
As it was shown that quantum computers are capable of solving both the prime-factorization and the discrete-logarithm
problem in polynomial time, attackers can break most of today’s public-key cryptosystems (as e.g. RSA and ECC) if
they have a powerful quantum computer at hand.
The urgent need for quantum-safe cryptography is obvious, especially since store now, harvest later attacks
allow to save encrypted data now and decrypt it as soon as the resources are available.
This is reflected in the standardization process that NIST started for post-quantum cryptography in 2016.
The first KEM were standardized in July 2022 after three rounds of the competition and some of the submissions
were forwarded to a fourth round for further investigation [1].
Three out of the four remaining KEM candidates in round four are code-based.
Moreover, the fourth one (namely, SIKE) was recently broken [7].
This explains why the community has high hopes and trust in coding-related primitives
even though no code-based candidate has been chosen for standardization so far.
Code-based cryptography mostly relies on McEliece-like schemes that are inspired by the seminal paper [17].
The main idea is to choose a generator matrix of a secret code and disguise its algebraic structure by applying some,
in most cases isometric or near-isometric, transformations such that an adversary cannot derive the known (or any other) efficient decoder from the
mere knowledge of the scrambled matrix.
McEliece-like instances based on a variety of code families and disguising functions in the Hamming and the rank
metric were proposed over time.
For example, the works [25, 4] are based on RS (RS) codes in the Hamming metric and the GPT
system and its variants (see e.g. [8, 9, 23]) use Gabidulin codes in the rank metric.
But in both cases, polynomial-time attacks were proposed and broke several of the systems:
RS codes can be distinguished from random codes by using the square-code approach introduced
in [25, 28] and Overbeck-like strategies [20, 19, 21, 10, 11]
yield a distinguisher for Gabidulin codes.
The works also explain the recovery of an equivalent secret key which enables the attacker to decrypt with respect
to the public code.
The sum-rank metric was first established in 2005 [13, Sec. III] and generalizes both the Hamming and the
rank metric.
It is thus natural to investigate if McEliece-like cryptosystems based on sum-rank-metric codes can ensure secure
communication.
The work [22] considers generic decoding of sum-rank-metric codes and hence gives guidance for the
security-level estimation of sum-rank-based cryptography.
Martínez-Peñas [14] introduced LRS codes which are the sum-rank analogs of RS and
Gabidulin codes and thus could be a first naive choice for secret codes in McEliece-like systems.
We focus on the task of distinguishing LRS codes from random codes and present two distinguishers that are
inspired by the square-code idea and by Overbeck’s approach, respectively.
Our results can be applied to distinguish GLRS codes which we define as LRS codes with nonzero
block multipliers.
As this more general code family is closed under semilinear equivalence, the methods also apply to GLRS codes with isometric disguising.
We finally focus on the zero-derivation case and show how an efficient decoding algorithm can be recovered from a GLRS
generator matrix that was disguised by means of semilinear isometries.
2 Preliminaries
Let us first gather some notions and results that we will use later on.
In particular, let $q$ be a prime power and denote the finite field of order $q$ by $\mathbb{F}_{q}$ .
For $m\geq 1$ , we further consider the extension field $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}\supseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}$ of order $q^{m}$ .
For a matrix $\bm{M}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n}$ , let $\langle M\rangle$ denote the $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ -linear vector space spanned by the rows of $\bm{M}$ .
2.1 The Sum-Rank Metric
An (integer) composition of $n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}$ into $\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}$ parts (or
 $\ell$ -composition for short) is a vector ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}$ with
 $n_{i}>0$ for all $1\leq i\leq\ell$ that satisfies $n=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}n_{i}$ .
If $\textstyle n$ contains $k$ distinct elements $\tilde{n}_{1},\dots,\tilde{n}_{k}$ , let $\lambda_{j}$ denote the number of
occurrences of $\tilde{n}_{j}$ in $\textstyle n$ for each $j=1,\dots,k$ and write $\lambda({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}}):=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}$ .
Throughout the paper, $n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}$ usually refers to the length of the considered codes and we will stick to one
particular $\ell$ -composition ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})$ of $n$ .
We often divide vectors ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ or matrices $\bm{M}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n}$ with $k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}$ into blocks
with respect to $\textstyle n$ .
Namely, we write ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}=({{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}}^{(1)}\mid\dots\mid{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}}^{(\ell)})$ with ${{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n_{i}}$ for all
 $1\leq i\leq\ell$ and $\bm{M}=({\bm{M}}^{(1)}\mid\dots\mid{\bm{M}}^{(\ell)})$ with ${\bm{M}}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n_{i}}$ for all $1\leq i\leq\ell$ , respectively.
The sum-rank weight of a vector ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ (with respect to $\textstyle n$ ) is defined as
 $\wt_{\Sigma R}^{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}})=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\rk_{q}({{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}}^{(i)})$ , where $\rk_{q}({{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}}^{(i)})$ is the maximum
number of $\mathbb{F}_{q}$ -linearly independent entries of ${{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}}^{(i)}$ for $i=1,\dots,\ell$ .
The hereby induced sum-rank metric is given by $d_{\Sigma R}^{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle y$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle y$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle y$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle y$}}})=\wt_{\Sigma R}^{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}-{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle y$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle y$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle y$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle y$}}})$ 
for ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle y$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle y$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle y$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle y$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ .
Since we always consider the same $\ell$ -composition $\textstyle n$ , we write $\wt_{\Sigma R}$ and $d_{\Sigma R}$ 
for simplicity.
An $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ -linear sum-rank-metric code $\mathcal{C}$ is an $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ -subspace of $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ .
Its length is $n$ and its dimension $k:=\dim(\mathcal{C})$ .
We further define its minimum (sum-rank) distance as
 $d(\mathcal{C}):=\{d_{\Sigma R}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}_{1},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}_{2}):{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}_{1},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}_{2}\in\mathcal{C},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}_{1}\neq{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}_{2}\}\\
=\{\wt_{\Sigma R}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}):{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}\in\mathcal{C},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}\neq\mathbf{0}\},$ 
(1)
where the last equality follows from the linearity of the code.
A matrix $\bm{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n}$ is a generator matrix of $\mathcal{C}$ if $\mathcal{C}=\langle\bm{G}\rangle$ .
If $\mathcal{C}$ is the kernel of a matrix $\bm{H}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{(n-k)\times n}$ , $\bm{H}$ is called a parity-check matrix
of $\mathcal{C}$ .
The code generated by any parity-check matrix $\bm{H}$ of $\mathcal{C}$ is the dual code of $\mathcal{C}$ and
denoted by $\mathcal{C}^{\perp}$ .
2.2 Automorphisms, Derivations, and Conjugacy
An automorphism $\theta$ on $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ is a mapping $\theta:\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}\to\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ with the properties
 $\theta(a+b)=\theta(a)+\theta(b)$ and $\theta(a\cdot b)=\theta(a)\cdot\theta(b)$ for all $a,b\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ .
We denote the group of all $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ -automorphisms by $\Aut(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})$ .
Note that every automorphism fixes a subfield of $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ pointwise.
The cyclic subgroup of $\Aut(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})$ , whose elements fix (at least) $\mathbb{F}_{q}$ pointwise, is called the Galois group of the
field extension $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}$ and we denote it by $\Gal(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q})$ .
Every $\sigma\in\Gal(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q})$ is a power of the Frobenius automorphism (with respect to $q$ ) that is defined as
 $\varphi:\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}\to\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}},\quad a\mapsto a^{q}.$ 
(2)
Namely, $\sigma\in\{\varphi^{0},\dots,\varphi^{m-1}\}$ .
The fixed field of $\sigma=\varphi^{l}$ with $l\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}$ is $\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\gcd(l,m)}$ .
For simplicity, assume in the following that $\sigma=\varphi^{l}$ with $\gcd(l,m)=1$ , i.e., let the fixed field of $\sigma$ be $\mathbb{F}_{q}$ .
A $\sigma$ -derivation is a map $\delta:\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}\to\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ that satisfies both $\delta(a+b)=\delta(a)+\delta(b)$ and
 $\delta(a\cdot b)=\delta(a)\cdot b+\sigma(a)\cdot\delta(b)$ for all $a,b\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ .
In our finite-field setting, every $\sigma$ -derivation is an inner derivation, that is
 $\delta=\gamma(\Id-\sigma)$ for a $\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ and the identity $\Id$ on $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ .
When the automorphism $\sigma$ is clear from the context, we often write $\delta_{\gamma}$ to refer to the derivation
corresponding to $\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ .
For a fixed pair $(\sigma,\delta)$ , we can group the elements of $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ with respect to an equivalence relation called $(\sigma,\delta)$ -conjugacy:
Definition 1
Two elements $a,b\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ are called $(\sigma,\delta)$ -conjugate if there is a $c\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ast}$ with
 ${a}^{c}:=\sigma(c)ac^{-1}+\delta(c)c^{-1}=b.$ 
(3)
All conjugates of $a\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ are collected in the respective conjugacy class
 $\mathcal{K}(a):=\{a^{c}:c\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ast}\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}.$ 
(4)
For $\delta=\delta_{\gamma}$ with $\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ , the class $\textsc{$\mathcal{K}$}(\gamma)$ is called trivial conjugacy class.
2.3 Isometries in the Sum-Rank Metric
As most code-based cryptosystems use isometric disguising, we quickly recall the characterization of sum-rank isometries.
Note that we have to differentiate between $\mathbb{F}_{q}$ -linear and $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ -linear isometries.
The former were studied in [18, Prop. 4.26], whereas the latter were considered
in [15, 2].
Precisely, the special case of equal block lengths (i.e., ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}}=\left(\frac{n}{\ell},\dots,\frac{n}{\ell}\right)$ ) was treated in [15, Thm. 2] and the
generalization to arbitrary block lengths and the extension to semilinear isometries is due
to [2, Sec. 3.3].
We focus on $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ -(semi)linear isometries because of our motivation from code-based cryptography.
Namely, we consider the following:
Definition 2
A bijective map $\iota:\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ is a (sum-rank) isometry on $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ if it is
sum-rank preserving, that is if $d_{\Sigma R}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}})=d_{\Sigma R}(\iota({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}))$ holds for all ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ .
We call an isometry linear when it is $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ -linear.
A semilinear isometry $\iota$ is additive and there exists an $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ -automorphism $\theta$ such that
 $\iota$ fulfills $\iota(a{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}})=\theta(a)\iota({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}})$ for all $a\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ and all ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ .
Recall that the general linear group $\GL(n,\mathbb{F}_{q})$ contains all full-rank matrices of size $n\times n$ 
over $\mathbb{F}_{q}$ and that the symmetric group $\Sym_{n}$ consists of all permutations of $n$ elements.
We introduce the notations
 $\displaystyle\GL({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},\mathbb{F}_{q})$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\GL(n_{1},\mathbb{F}_{q})\times\dots\times\GL(n_{\ell},\mathbb{F}_{q})$ 
(5)
 $\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\Sym_{\lambda({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}})}$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\Sym_{\lambda_{1}}\times\dots\times\Sym_{\lambda_{k}},$ 
(6)
where $\lambda({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}})$ counts the occurrences of distinct entries of $\textstyle n$ (see Subsection 2.1).
Note that $\Sym_{\lambda({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}})}$ is a subgroup of $\Sym_{\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}}=\Sym_{\ell}$ .
Theorem 2.1 (Sum-Rank Isometries [15, 2])
The group of $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ -linear isometries on $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ is
 $\mathrm{LI}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}):=\left((\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ast})^{\ell}\times\GL({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},\mathbb{F}_{q})\right)\rtimes\Sym_{\lambda({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}})}.$ 
(7)
Its action $\mathrm{act}_{\mathrm{LI}}:\mathrm{LI}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n})\times\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ is defined as
 $\mathrm{act}_{\mathrm{LI}}(\iota,{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}):=\left(c_{1}{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}}^{(\pi^{-1}(1))}\bm{M}_{1}\mid\dots\mid c_{\ell}{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}}^{(\pi^{-1}(\ell))}\bm{M}_{\ell}\right)$ 
(8)
for $\iota=\left((c_{1},\dots,c_{\ell}),(\bm{M}_{1},\dots,\bm{M}_{\ell}),\pi\right)$ and
 ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ .
Similarly, the group of $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ -semilinear isometries on $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ is
 $\displaystyle\mathrm{SI}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n})$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\mathrm{LI}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n})\rtimes\Aut(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})$ 
(9)
and its action $\mathrm{act}_{\mathrm{SI}}:\mathrm{SI}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n})\times\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ is given by
 $\mathrm{act}_{\mathrm{SI}}((\iota,\theta),{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}):=\theta(\mathrm{act}_{\mathrm{LI}}(\iota,{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}))$ 
(10)
for $(\iota,\theta)\in\mathrm{SI}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n})$ and ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ .
Since MacWilliams’ extension theorem does not hold in this general setting (see [3, Ex. 2.9 (a)]
for a counterexample in the rank-metric case), code equivalence in the sum-rank
metric is defined by means of isometries of the whole space (cp. [2, Def. 3.9]).
Definition 3
Two sum-rank-metric codes $\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ are called
linearly equivalent if there is a linear isometry $\iota\in\mathrm{LI}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n})$ such that
 $\mathrm{act}_{\mathrm{LI}}(\iota,\mathcal{C}):=\{\mathrm{act}_{\mathrm{LI}}(\iota,{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}):{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}\in\mathcal{C}\}=\mathcal{D}.$ 
(11)
They are semilinearly equivalent if there is $(\iota,\theta)\in\mathrm{SI}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n})$ such that
 $\mathrm{act}_{\mathrm{SI}}((\iota,\theta),\mathcal{C}):=\{\mathrm{act}_{\mathrm{SI}}((\iota,\theta),{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}):{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}\in\mathcal{C}\}=\mathcal{D}.$ 
(12)
2.4 Skew Polynomials
The skew-polynomial ring $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}[x;\sigma,\delta]$ is defined as the set of polynomials $f(x)=\sum_{i}f_{i}x^{i}$ 
with finitely many nonzero coefficients $f_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ .
It is equipped with conventional polynomial addition but the multiplication is determined by the rule
 $xa=\sigma(a)x+\delta(a)$ .
Similar to conventional polynomial rings, we define the degree of a nonzero skew polynomial
 $f(x)=\sum_{i}f_{i}x^{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}[x;\sigma,\delta]$ as $\deg(f):=\max\{i:f_{i}\neq 0\}$ and set the degree of the zero
polynomial to $-\infty$ .
Note that despite lots of similarities to $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}[x]$ , the same evaluation strategy (i.e., $f(c)=\sum_{i}f_{i}c^{i}$ for
 $c\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ ) does not work in this setting.
Instead, the literature provides two different ways to adequately evaluate skew polynomials: remainder evaluation
and generalized operator evaluation.
We will focus on the latter in this work.
For $a,b\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ , define the operator
 $\mathcal{D}_{a}(b):=\sigma(b)a+\delta(b)$ 
(13)
and its powers $\mathcal{D}_{a}^{i}(b):=\mathcal{D}_{a}(\mathcal{D}_{a}^{i-1}(b))$ for $i\geq 0$ (with $\mathcal{D}_{a}^{0}(b)=b$ and
 $\mathcal{D}_{a}^{1}(b)=\mathcal{D}_{a}(b)$ ).
For ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell})\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ell}$ , and $\bm{B}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n}$ , we write
 $\mathcal{D}_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}(\bm{B}):=(\mathcal{D}_{a_{1}}({\bm{B}}^{(1)})\mid\dots\mid\mathcal{D}_{a_{\ell}}({\bm{B}}^{(\ell)}))$ , where
 $\mathcal{D}_{a_{i}}({\bm{B}}^{(i)})$ stands for the elementwise application of $\mathcal{D}_{a_{i}}(\cdot)$ to the entries of
 ${\bm{B}}^{(i)}$ for $1\leq i\leq\ell$ .
This notation also applies to vectors ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle b$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ and can be extended to powers of the operator.
In the zero-derivation case, the $i$ -fold application of the above defined operator can be expressed as
 $\mathcal{D}_{a}^{i}(b)=\sigma^{i}(b)\cdot\mathcal{N}_{i}\left(a\right)$ 
(14)
for any $a,b\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ and $i\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}$ .
Here, $\mathcal{N}_{i}\left(a\right):=\prod_{j=0}^{i-1}\sigma^{j}(a)=\sigma^{i-1}(a)\dots\sigma(a)\cdot a$ denotes
the generalized power function.
Lemma 1
The equality $\mathcal{D}_{a}(bc)=\sigma(b)\mathcal{D}_{a}(c)+\delta(b)c$ holds for any $a,b,c\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ .
Proof.
The definition of $\mathcal{D}_{a}(\cdot)$ and the product rule for derivations yield
 $\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{a}(bc)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sigma(bc)a+\delta(bc)=\sigma(bc)a+\delta(b)c+\sigma(b)\delta(c)$ 
(15)
 $\displaystyle=\sigma(b)(\sigma(c)a+\delta(c))+\delta(b)c=\sigma(b)\mathcal{D}_{a}(c)+\delta(b)c.$ 
(16)
∎∎
Let us now define the generalized operator evaluation of skew polynomials:
Definition 4
The generalized operator evaluation of a skew polynomial $f(x)=\sum_{i}f_{i}x^{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}[x;\sigma,\delta]$ at
a point $b\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ with respect to an evaluation parameter $a\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ is given by
 ${f}(b)_{a}:=\sum_{i}f_{i}\mathcal{D}_{a}^{i}(b).$ 
(17)
For a vector ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ , a vector ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell})\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ell}$ , and a parameter
 $d\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}$ , the generalized Moore matrix $\mathfrak{M}_{d}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}})_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}$ is defined as
 $\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{d}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}})_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\left(\bm{V}_{d}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}^{(1)})_{a_{1}},\dots,\bm{V}_{d}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}^{(\ell)})_{a_{\ell}}\right)\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{d\times n},$ 
(18)
 $\displaystyle\text{where }\bm{V}_{d}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}^{(i)})_{a_{i}}$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}x^{(i)}_{1}&\cdots&x^{(i)}_{n_{i}}\\
\mathcal{D}_{a_{i}}(x^{(i)}_{1})&\cdots&\mathcal{D}_{a_{i}}(x^{(i)}_{n_{i}})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathcal{D}_{a_{i}}^{d-1}(x^{(i)}_{1})&\cdots&\mathcal{D}_{a_{i}}^{d-1}(x^{(i)}_{n_{i}})\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{for }1\leq i\leq\ell.$ 
(19)
If $\textstyle a$ contains representatives of pairwise distinct nontrivial conjugacy classes of $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ and $\rk_{q}\left({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}^{(i)}\right)=n_{i}$ for all $1\leq i\leq\ell$ , we have by [14, Thm. 2]
and [12, Thm. 4.5] that $\rk_{q^{m}}\left(\mathfrak{M}_{d}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}})_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}\right)=\min(d,n)$ .
2.5 (Generalized) Linearized Reed–Solomon Codes
Let us recall the definition of LRS codes that generalize both RS and Gabidulin codes.
LRS codes are evaluation codes with respect to skew polynomials, which specialize to conventional and
linearized polynomials in the Hamming- and the rank-metric setting, respectively.
Definition 5 (Linearized Reed–Solomon Codes [14, Def. 31])
Let ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell})\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ell}$ consist of representatives of distinct nontrivial
conjugacy classes of $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ .
Choose a vector $\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ whose blocks $\bm{\beta}^{(i)}=\left({\beta}^{(i)}_{1},\dots,{\beta}^{(i)}_{n_{i}}\right)$ contain $\mathbb{F}_{q}$ -linearly independent elements for all
 $i=1,\dots,\ell$ .
Then, a LRS (LRS) code of length $n$ and dimension $k$ is defined as
 $\mathrm{LRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k]:=\left\{\left({{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}}^{(1)}(f)\mid\dots\mid{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}}^{(\ell)}(f)\right):f\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}[x;\sigma,\delta]_{<k}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ 
(20)
where ${{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}}^{(i)}(f):=\left({f}({\beta}^{(i)}_{1})_{a_{i}},\ldots,{f}({\beta}^{(i)}_{n_{i}})_{a_{i}}\right)$ .
Note that LRS codes reach the Singleton-like bound $d\leq n-k+1$ from [14, Prop. 34] with
equality, where $d$ denotes the minimum sum-rank distance of the code.
They are thus MSRD (MSRD) codes.
The generalized Moore matrix $\mathfrak{M}_{k}(\bm{\beta})_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}$ is a generator matrix of the code $\mathrm{LRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k]$ .
Since a generator matrix of this form is desirable as it e.g. gives rise to known efficient decoding algorithms, we call it a
canonical generator matrix of $\mathrm{LRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k]$ .
Note that the parameters $\bm{\beta}$ and $\textstyle a$ of a canonical generator matrix are in general not uniquely determined,
and not even fixing a particular $\textstyle a$ ensures the uniqueness of $\bm{\beta}$ .
In the zero-derivation case, the dual of an LRS code can be described as
 $\mathrm{LRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k]^{\perp}=\mathrm{LRS}[\bm{\alpha},\sigma^{-1}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}});{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},n-k]_{\sigma^{-1}},$ 
(21)
where the index $\sigma^{-1}$ on the right-hand side stands for the fact that it is an LRS code with respect to
the inverse automorphism $\sigma^{-1}$ (see [5, 6]).
The vector $\bm{\alpha}=(\bm{\alpha}^{(1)}\mid\dots\mid\bm{\alpha}^{(\ell)})\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ satisfies
 $\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\alpha_{j}^{(i)}\mathcal{D}_{a_{i}}^{h-1}(\beta_{j}^{(i)})=0\quad\text{for all }h=1,\dots,n-1$ 
(22)
and has sum-rank weight $\wt_{\Sigma R}(\bm{\alpha})=n$ according to [16, Thm. 4].
In particular, the dual of a zero-derivation LRS code is again an LRS code.
When nonzero derivations are allowed, the duals of LRS codes are linearized Goppa codes which are
(noncanonically) isomorphic to LRS codes [5, 6].
As the proof of Theorem 3.1 shows, codes that are (semi)linearly
equivalent to LRS codes are not necessarily LRS codes themselves.
However, this is true for a more general code family that is obtained by allowing nonzero block multipliers.
We define GLRS codes as follows:
Definition 6 (Generalized Linearized Reed–Solomon Codes)
Let $\mathcal{C}:=\mathrm{LRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k]$ be an LRS code as in Definition 5.
Further, let ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}}=\left(v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell}\right)\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ell}$ be a vector of nonzero $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ -elements.
We define the GLRS code $\mathrm{GLRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k]$ as
 $\mathrm{GLRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k]:=\left\{\left(v_{1}{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}}^{(1)}\mid\dots\mid v_{\ell}{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}}^{(\ell)}\right):{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}\in\mathcal{C}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}.$ 
(23)
Remark that we recover LRS codes from GLRS codes for $\textstyle v$ being the all-one vector.
Since multiplying blocks with different nonzero $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ -elements is a sum-rank isometry according
to Theorem 2.1, we obtain the following corollary:
Corollary 1
The minimum sum-rank distance of the code $\mathrm{GLRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k]$ is $d=n-k+1$ .
Therefore, GLRS codes are MSRD.
The code $\mathrm{GLRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k]$ has a generator matrix of the form
 $\bm{G}=\left(v_{1}\bm{V}_{k}({\bm{\beta}}^{(1)})_{a_{1}}\mid\dots\mid v_{\ell}\bm{V}_{k}({\bm{\beta}}^{(\ell)})_{a_{\ell}}\right).$ 
(24)
Similar to the LRS case, we call any generator matrix of this form a canonical generator matrix of $\mathrm{GLRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k]$ .
Note that a canonical generator matrix of a GLRS code depends not only
on the parameters $\bm{\beta}$ and $\textstyle a$ but also on the block multipliers $\textstyle v$ .
3 Problem Statement
The main problem we want to solve is distinguishing GLRS codes, that were disguised by means of
 $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ -semilinear isometries, from random sum-rank-metric codes of the same length and dimension.
Formally, we state this task as follows:
Problem 1 (Distinguishing GLRS Codes up to Semilinear Equivalence)
Given a full-rank matrix $\bm{M}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n}$ , decide if there are parameters $\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ ,
 ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ell}$ , ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ell}$ , $\sigma\in\Gal(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q})$ , and $\delta$ being a $\sigma$ -derivation,
such that $\langle\bm{M}\rangle$ is semilinearly equivalent to $\mathrm{GLRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k]$ .
We now investigate how $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ -semilinear transformations affect GLRS codes to get a better understanding of the
problem.
Theorem 3.1 shows that every semilinear isometry (cp. Theorem 2.1)
transforms a GLRS code into another GLRS code with possibly different parameters:
Theorem 3.1
Let $\mathcal{C}=\mathrm{GLRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k]$ be a GLRS code with respect to $\sigma$ and $\delta:=\delta_{\gamma}$ .
Let further $\iota\in\mathrm{LI}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n})$ denote an
 $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ -linear isometry with $\iota=\left((c_{1},\dots,c_{\ell}),(\bm{M}_{1},\dots,\bm{M}_{\ell}),\pi\right)$ .
Then, the linearly equivalent code $\hat{\mathcal{C}}:=\mathrm{act}_{\mathrm{LI}}(\iota,\mathcal{C})$ is also a GLRS code with respect to $\sigma$ and $\delta$ .
Namely, $\hat{\mathcal{C}}=\mathrm{GLRS}[\hat{\bm{\beta}},\hat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}},\hat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k]$ with
 $\hat{\bm{\beta}}=({\bm{\beta}}^{(\pi^{-1}(1))}\bm{M}_{1}\mid\dots\mid{\bm{\beta}}^{(\pi^{-1}(\ell))}\bm{M}_{\ell})$ ,
 $\hat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}=(a_{\pi^{-1}(1)},\dots,a_{\pi^{-1}(\ell)})$ , and
 $\hat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}}}=(c_{1}v_{\pi^{-1}(1)},\dots,c_{\ell}v_{\pi^{-1}(\ell)})$ .
For a semilinear isometry $(\iota,\theta)\in\mathrm{SI}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n})$ with $\iota$ as above and $\theta\in\Aut(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})$ ,
the code $\mathrm{act}_{\mathrm{SI}}((\iota,\theta),\mathcal{C})$ is a GLRS code with
respect to the automorphism $\sigma$ and the possibly different derivation $\delta_{\theta(\gamma)}:=\theta(\gamma)(\Id-\sigma)$ .
Its parameters are $\theta(\hat{\bm{\beta}})$ , $\theta(\hat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}})$ , and $\theta(\hat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}}})$ , where $\theta$ is
applied elementwise to the vectors.
Proof.
Let us use the shorthand notations $v_{\pi_{i}}:=v_{\pi^{-1}(i)}$ , $a_{\pi_{i}}:=a_{\pi^{-1}(i)}$ , and
 ${\bm{\beta}}^{(\pi_{i})}:={\bm{\beta}}^{(\pi^{-1}(i))}$ throughout this proof.
 $\mathcal{C}$ has a generator matrix of the form $\bm{G}:=\left(v_{1}\bm{V}_{k}(\bm{\beta}^{(1)})_{a_{1}},\dots,v_{\ell}\bm{V}_{k}(\bm{\beta}^{(\ell)})_{a_{\ell}}\right)$ .
If $\iota$ acts on the $j$ -th row of $\bm{G}$ for $j\in\{1,\dots,k\}$ , we obtain
 $\left(c_{1}v_{\pi_{1}}\mathcal{D}_{a_{\pi_{1}}}^{j-1}({\bm{\beta}}^{(\pi_{1})})\bm{M}_{1}\mid\dots\mid c_{\ell}v_{\pi_{\ell}}\mathcal{D}_{a_{\pi_{\ell}}}^{j-1}({\bm{\beta}}^{(\pi_{\ell})})\bm{M}_{\ell}\right).$ 
(25)
Since generalized operator evaluation is $\mathbb{F}_{q}$ -linear, we get
 $\mathcal{D}_{a_{\pi_{i}}}^{j-1}({\bm{\beta}}^{(\pi_{i})})\bm{M}_{i}=\mathcal{D}_{a_{\pi_{i}}}^{j-1}({\bm{\beta}}^{(\pi_{i})}\bm{M}_{i})$ 
for all $i=1,\dots,\ell$ 
and thus, (25) is exactly the $j$ -th row of
 $\hat{\bm{G}}:=\left(c_{1}v_{\pi_{1}}\bm{V}_{k}(\bm{\beta}^{(\pi_{1})}\bm{M}_{1})_{a_{\pi_{1}}},\dots,c_{\ell}v_{\pi_{\ell}}\bm{V}_{k}(\bm{\beta}^{(\pi_{\ell})}\bm{M}_{\ell})_{a_{\pi_{\ell}}}\right).$ 
(26)
As $\hat{\bm{G}}$ generates $\hat{\mathcal{C}}$ , this proves the first part of the theorem.
The second one follows from the observation
 $\theta(v\mathcal{D}_{a}^{j-1}(\beta))=\theta(v)\left(\mathcal{D}_{\theta(a)}^{\sigma,\delta_{\theta(\gamma)}}\right)^{j-1}(\theta(\beta))$ 
(27)
for any $v,a,\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ast}$ and $j\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}$ with
 $\mathcal{D}_{\cdot}^{\sigma,\delta_{\theta(\gamma)}}(\cdot)$ denoting the generalized operator evaluation with
respect to the automorphism $\sigma$ and the derivation $\delta_{\theta(\gamma)}:=\theta(\gamma)(\Id-\sigma)$ .
(27) can be verified by induction over $j$ .
∎∎
In fact, this shows that GLRS codes with respect to a fixed automorphism and a fixed derivation are closed
under linear equivalence.
If we allow different derivations for a fixed automorphism, GLRS codes are even closed under semilinear
equivalence.
This means, intuitively speaking, that 1 boils down to distinguishing GLRS
codes.
We hence formulate and focus on 2:
Problem 2 (Distinguishing GLRS Codes)
Given a full-rank matrix $\bm{M}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n}$ , decide if there are parameters $\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ ,
 ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ell}$ , ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ell}$ , $\sigma\in\Gal(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q})$ , and $\delta$ being a $\sigma$ -derivation,
such that $\langle\bm{M}\rangle=\mathrm{GLRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k]$ .
Let us describe more precisely how the two above-defined problems are related in case we assume the knowledge of the automorphism $\sigma$ and the derivation
 $\delta:=\delta_{\gamma}$ .
If we restrict ourselves to linear equivalence, 1 is equivalent to 2
since every code that is linearly equivalent to a GLRS code with respect to $\sigma$ and $\delta$ is a GLRS code
with respect to the same automorphism and derivation.
In the more general, semilinear setting, we can solve 1 by solving
multiple instances of 2.
Namely, we have to consider 2 for all derivations $\delta_{\theta(\gamma)}:=\theta(\gamma)(\Id-\sigma)$ 
with $\theta\in\Aut(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})$ according to Theorem 3.1.
As $|\Aut(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})|=sm$ for $s$ being the extension degree of $\mathbb{F}_{q}$ over its prime field, we obtain that 1
is equivalent to $sm$ instances of 2.
We present two polynomial-time distinguishers that partly solve 2 when $\sigma$ and
 $\delta$ are known in Section 4.
However, the pure knowledge whether a matrix generates a GLRS code or not does not yet break a hypothetical McEliece-like cryptosystem based on GLRS codes.
We rather wish to recover an efficient decoding algorithm for the publicly known code by e.g. finding a
canonical generator matrix.
Therefore, the following problem is of great interest:
Problem 3 (Recovering a Canonical GLRS Generator Matrix)
Given an arbitrary generator matrix $\bm{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n}$ of a GLRS code $\mathcal{C}$ ,
find parameters $\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ , ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ell}$ , ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ell}$ , $\sigma\in\Gal(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q})$ , and $\delta$ being a $\sigma$ -derivation,
such that $\left(v_{1}\bm{V}_{k}({\bm{\beta}}^{(1)})_{a_{1}}\mid\dots\mid v_{\ell}\bm{V}_{k}({\bm{\beta}}^{(\ell)})_{a_{\ell}}\right)$ 
is a canonical generator matrix of $\mathcal{C}$ .
We study 3 in Section 5 and show two techniques to partially solve it for GLRS codes in the zero-derivation case
with known automorphism $\sigma$ .
4 Distinguishers for GLRS Codes
This section contains two approaches that solve 2, that is the task of distinguishing
GLRS codes from random codes, for many instances.
In both cases, we assume the knowledge of the automorphism $\sigma$ and the derivation $\delta$ with respect to which
the code should be distinguished.
In Subsection 4.1, we focus on a square-code distinguisher that is inspired by an RS-code
distinguisher.
It works for GLRS codes constructed by means of the identity automorphism and zero derivation.
Afterwards, we present an Overbeck-like distinguisher inspired by the rank-metric case
in Subsection 4.2.
This approach can handle any valid combination of automorphism and derivation but requires the knowledge of the
evaluation-parameter vector $\textstyle a$ .
Moreover, the Overbeck-type distinguisher cannot deal with block multipliers and is thus applicable
to LRS codes only.
However, GLRS codes can still be handled by applying the distinguisher at most $(q^{m}-1)^{\ell}$ times (see Subsection 4.2
for more details).
We experimentally verified all results presented in this section for different parameter sets with an implementation in SageMath [26].
4.1 A Square-Code Distinguisher
The first polynomial-time attack on a McEliece/Niederreiter variant based on GRS (GRS) codes was proposed by SaS in [25].
The attack was later on refined by Wieschebrink to attack the improved Berger–Loidreau cryptosystem [27], which is based on GRS subcodes.
The approach from [27] was further improved in [28] to work with smaller subcodes and thus to break the cryptosystem for most practical parameters.
The attack in [28] is based on the properties of the elementwise product (or Schur-square) of a code.
For any vectors ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle y$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle y$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle y$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle y$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ we define the elementwise product (also referred to as Schur or star product) of $\textstyle x$ and $\textstyle y$ as
 ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}\star{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle y$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle y$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle y$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle y$}}}:=(x_{1}y_{1},x_{2}y_{2}.\dots,x_{n}y_{n}).$ 
The square-code of an $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ -linear code $\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ is defined as
 $\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}:=\left\{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}_{1}\star{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}_{2}:{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}_{1},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle c$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle c$}}}_{2}\in\mathcal{C}\right\}.$ 
The main observation for distinguishing a random linear code in $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ from a GRS code $\mathcal{C}$ is that the squared GRS code has dimension $\dim(\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C})=\min(n,\,2k-1)$ , which is small compared to the expected dimension of a squared random linear code.
Note that a similar technique was used for the power decoding of RS codes beyond the unique-decoding radius (see [24, Lemma 1]).
We will now derive a similar distinguisher for GLRS codes constructed from skew-polynomial rings with identity automorphism $\sigma=\Id$ . Observe that in this case the only possible derivation is the zero derivation.
Lemma 2 provides some basic results required for deriving a square-code distinguisher for GLRS codes:
Lemma 2
For $\sigma=\Id$ , let $\mathcal{C}=\mathrm{GLRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k]$ be a GLRS code constructed by polynomials from $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}[x;\sigma]_{<k}=\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}[x]_{<k}$ .
Then we have that
 $\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\bigl\{$ 
 $\displaystyle\bigl(f(a_{1}),\dots,f(a_{1})\mid\dots\mid f(a_{\ell}),\dots,f(a_{\ell})\bigr)\cdot\diag\bigl((v_{1}{\bm{\beta}}^{(1)}\mid\dots\mid v_{\ell}{\bm{\beta}}^{(\ell)})\bigr)$ 
(28)

4.2 An Overbeck-like Distinguisher
Overbeck proposed a distinguisher for Gabidulin codes in [20, 19, 21].
The main idea is to repeatedly apply the Frobenius automorphism to the public generator matrix and stack the results
vertically.
Since there is a generator matrix of a Gabidulin code whose $i$ -th row is the $(i-1)$ -fold application of the Frobenius
automorphism to a generating vector, the rank of the stacked matrix will only increase by one for each new matrix
block.
But random full-rank matrices behave differently and the stacked matrix has much higher rank in general.
HaMaR [11] used a slightly different approach, which we will call HMR approach
for short, to recover the secret parameters of a Gabidulin code.
We mention their technique because it gives rise to a distinguisher and it is similar to Overbeck’s approach,
as it also makes use of the repeated application of the Frobenius automorphism to the public generator matrix.
But instead of considering the sum of the corresponding codes, the HMR approach focuses on the
intersection of the codes and shows that its dimension only decreases by one for each iteration step.
Again, random codes show a different behavior under this operation.
We now present a generalization of Overbeck’s approach to LRS codes in the sum-rank metric.
In contrast to the square-code distinguisher, the Overbeck-like distinguisher works for the general setting with an
arbitrary automorphism $\sigma$ and any valid $\sigma$ -derivation $\delta$ .
Since it does not support block multipliers, i.e., GLRS codes, let us quickly describe how we can apply
distinguishers for LRS codes to GLRS codes in general.
Recall therefore that a GLRS code $\mathrm{GLRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k]$ has a generator matrix of the form
 $(v_{1}{\bm{G}}^{(1)}\mid\dots\mid v_{\ell}{\bm{G}}^{(\ell)})$ , where $\bm{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n}$ is a generator
matrix of $\mathrm{LRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k]$ .
But this implies that the Overbeck-like distinguisher will (at least) succeed if we apply it to the matrix
 $(v_{1}^{-1}{\bm{M}}^{(1)}\mid\dots\mid v_{\ell}^{-1}{\bm{M}}^{(\ell)})$ , where $\bm{M}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n}$ denotes
the public generator matrix of the GLRS code.
We can thus run the Overbeck-like distinguisher for different choices of ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}}^{-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ell}$ until it either
succeeds or all possible $(q^{m}-1)^{\ell}$ (inverse) block multipliers were checked in the worst case.

But since $\bm{S}$ has full rank, there is a nonzero entry in its $k$ -th column, say $S_{l^{\ast},k}$ .
Now (37) shows that the $l^{\ast}$ -th row of $\mathcal{D}_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}(\bm{G})$ has the form
 $\sigma(S_{l^{\ast},k})\mathcal{D}_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{k}(\bm{\beta})+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\sigma(S_{l,i})\mathcal{D}_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{i}(\bm{\beta})+\delta(S_{l,i})\mathcal{D}_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{i-1}(\bm{\beta}),$ 
(38)
where the right-hand side is clearly contained in $\mathrm{LRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k]$ .
As $\sigma(S_{l^{\ast},k})$ is nonzero if and only if $S_{l^{\ast},k}\neq 0$ , this shows
 $\langle\mathcal{D}_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}(\bm{G})\rangle\supseteq\langle\mathcal{D}_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{k}(\bm{\beta})\rangle$ and hence
 $\langle\Gamma_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}(\bm{G})\rangle\supseteq\mathrm{LRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k+1]$ .
Summing up, we obtain $\langle\Gamma_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}(\bm{G})\rangle=\mathrm{LRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k+1]$ , which directly implies
 $\rk_{q^{m}}(\Gamma_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}(\bm{G}))=k+1$ .
For $j>1$ , the results follow inductively from the fact that
 $\langle\Gamma_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{j}(\bm{G})\rangle=\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{j-1}(\bm{G})\\
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{j}(\bm{G})\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle\overset{(\circ)}{=}\langle\Gamma_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}(\Gamma_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{j-1}(\bm{G}))\rangle,$ 
(39)
since all rows that are added in step $(\circ)$ are already contained in the row space of $\Gamma_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{j-1}(\bm{G})$ .
The statements $\langle\Gamma_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{j}(\bm{G})\rangle=\mathrm{LRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k+j]$ and hence
 $\rk_{q^{m}}(\Gamma_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{j}(\bm{G}))=k+j$ follow with the knowledge of
 $\Gamma_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{j-1}(\bm{G})=\mathrm{LRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k+j-1]$ and the proof for $j=1$ .
∎∎
In contrast, randomly chosen full-rank matrices over $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ tend to behave quite differently when $\Gamma_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}$ is
applied.
This is analogous to [20, Assumption 2].
Conjecture 1
Let $\bm{M}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n}$ be a randomly chosen matrix with full $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ -rank and such that each block
 ${\bm{M}}^{(i)}$ for $i=1,\dots,\ell$ has full column rank over $\mathbb{F}_{q}$ .
Assume that ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ell}$ consists of randomly chosen representatives of distinct nontrivial conjugacy
classes of $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ and fix a parameter $j\in\{1,\dots,n-k\}$ .
Then, $\rk_{q^{m}}(\Gamma_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{j}(\bm{M}))=\min((j+1)k,n)$ holds with high probability.
With these results, we can solve 2 for LRS codes in polynomial time if $\sigma$ , $\delta$ ,
and $\textstyle a$ are known.
We summarize it in Theorem 4.2:
Theorem 4.2 (Overbeck-like Distinguisher)
Let $\bm{M}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n}$ be an arbitrary full-rank matrix.
We can decide with high probability22
 2
 
 
 
 In fact, the distinguisher recognizes a
GLRS code with probability one. But, with a small probability, it might wrongly declare a non-GLRS code to be a GLRS code.
if $\bm{M}$ generates an LRS code with respect to $\sigma$ , $\delta$ , and $\textstyle a$ 
in $\mathcal{O}({n^{5}})$ operations in $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ .
Proof.
First, choose a $0\leq j\leq n-k$ for which $k+j<\min((j+1)k,n)$ holds.
We set up the matrix $\Gamma_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{j}(\bm{M})\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{(j+1)k\times n}$ in $\mathcal{O}({jkn})\subseteq\mathcal{O}({n^{3}})$ 
operations in $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ .
Next, we compute its rank in $\mathcal{O}({n^{5}})$ $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ -operations.
By Corollary 2 and Conjecture 1, we know with high
probability that $\bm{M}$ generates an LRS code with respect to the given parameters if
 $\rk_{q^{m}}(\Gamma_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{j}(\bm{M}))=k+j$ holds.
If however $\rk_{q^{m}}(\Gamma_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{j}(\bm{M}))>k+j$ , we know for sure that $\bm{M}$ is no generator matrix of an
LRS code with respect to the given parameters.
∎∎
Remark 1
We empirically verified by simulations that the distinguisher can in most cases not recognize an LRS code if it is executed with respect to a
different set of evaluation parameters.
This is the case even if the conjugacy classes of the evaluation parameters ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell})$ 
are known and only other representatives $\hat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}:=({a_{1}}^{c_{1}},\dots,{a_{\ell}}^{c_{\ell}})$ 
with $c_{1},\dots,c_{\ell}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ast}$ are used for the distinguisher.
This means that not even side information about the chosen conjugacy classes helps the distinguishing process
but knowledge of the exact evaluation parameters is needed.
If we do not have access to this information, we have to try exponentially many possibilities in the worst case.
We use the remainder of this section to give a short outline of how the distinguisher using the HMR approach
can be generalized to the LRS case.
If a full-rank matrix $\bm{M}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n}$ and parameters $\sigma$ , $\delta$ , and $\textstyle a$ are given, we focus on the
intersection code instead of considering the addition code as we indicated earlier.
Similar to Corollary 2, we can derive Corollary 3
whose proof we omit for brevity.
Corollary 3
Let $\bm{G}$ be an arbitrary generator matrix of the code $\mathrm{LRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k]$ .
Then, the $j$ -fold intersection code $\bigcap_{i=0}^{j}\langle\mathcal{D}_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{i}(\bm{M})\rangle$ equals
 $\mathrm{LRS}[\mathcal{D}_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{j}(\bm{\beta}),{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k-j]$ and has thus $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ -dimension $k-j$ for all $0\leq j\leq k-1$ .
Heuristically speaking, the application of $\mathcal{D}_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}(\cdot)$ to a random full-rank matrix produces another
essentially random code.
For small dimension $k$ , it is hence reasonable to assume that the $j$ -fold intersection code
from Corollary 3 has a much lower dimension.
This illustrates why Corollary 3 can serve as a
distinguisher for LRS codes that can, of course, also be applied to GLRS codes as explained in the
beginning of this section.
5 Recovery of a Canonical Generator Matrix
If only a scrambled and possibly further disguised generator matrix of a GLRS code is known, it is a crucial task
to recover a canonical generator matrix of the same code.
The secret code structure, that is revealed by a canonical generator matrix, is (up to now) directly linked to the
knowledge of efficient decoding algorithms.
We partly tackle 3 in this section and show how the recovery can be done in the case of GLRS codes with zero derivation for which
the automorphism $\sigma$ is given.
As for the distinguishers, the following results were also implemented in SageMath and checked for several parameter sets.
The first approach requires the identity automorphism and finds suitable evaluation parameters $\textstyle a$ and block multipliers $\textstyle v$ , whereas
the second one assumes the knowledge of $\textstyle a$ and allows to recover $\bm{\beta}$ for an arbitrary but known automorphism.
If GLRS codes with respect to the identity automorphism are considered, we can thus combine the two distinguishers to recover first $\textstyle a$ and $\textstyle v$ , and then
 $\bm{\beta}$ .
5.1 Square-Code Approach
For this approach, we focus on the identity automorphism which allows zero derivation only.
The recovery strategy is based on the fact that we can extract a GRS code from an arbitrary generator matrix of
a GLRS code as described in Subsection 4.1.
We then recover the parameters of the GRS code and afterwards the ones of the GLRS code.
Theorem 5.1
Let $\bm{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n}$ denote a generator matrix of a GLRS code $\mathcal{C}$ with respect to the
identity automorphism and zero derivation.
We can recover parameters ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ell}$ for which a canonical generator matrix of $\mathcal{C}$ exists in $\mathcal{O}({k^{2}n})$ operations in $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ .
Proof.
Recall from Lemma 3 that the matrix consisting of one column of each block ${\bm{G}}^{(i)}$ of the generator matrix $\bm{G}$ generates a GRS code of length $\ell$ and dimension $k$ . If $\ell>k$ this is a nontrivial GRS code, whereas for $\ell\leq k$ the code is the whole space $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ell}$ . For the description of the recovery process we will differentiate between these two cases:
1. 
In the case where $\ell\geq k$ holds we can simply choose the evaluation points to be ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}=(1,\alpha,\alpha^{2},\dots,\alpha^{\ell})$ for a primitive element $\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ . Clearly, the Vandermonde matrix with these parameters is full-rank and hence spans the whole space, i.e., it is a generator matrix of the trivial RS code. Furthermore, $1,\alpha,\alpha^{2},\dots,\alpha^{\ell}$ represent distinct conjugacy classes (since we consider zero derivation) and are hence a valid choice for the LRS code. Note that we do not need to consider column multipliers in this setting, i.e., we can assume $\textstyle v$ to be the all-one vector.
2. 
In the other case, i.e., where $\ell<k$ , the resulting GRS code and its dual code are nontrivial, i.e., they both have minimum distance greater than one. We can now use the Sidelnikov–Shestakov algorithm from [25] on the parity-check matrix of our GRS code to find suitable $\textstyle a$ and ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ell}$ .
This requires $\mathcal{O}({k^{2}n})$ operations in $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ .
∎∎
Depending on how the code is disguised in a potential cryptosystem, an attacker can use the fact about the square-code dimension from Lemma 3 to find suitable subcodes of the public code.
Then, the parameter-recovery algorithm from Theorem 5.1 can be applied to the obtained subcodes.
5.2 Overbeck-like Approach
In the literature, there are three different approaches for recovering the secret parameters of Gabidulin codes based
on ideas similar to Overbeck’s distinguisher:
1. 
Overbeck [20] considers the sum of the codes obtained by repeated application of the Frobenius
automorphism until a code of codimension one is obtained.
The secret parameters are then recovered from a generator of the one-dimensional dual code.
2. 
HaMaR [11] compute the intersection of the codes that arise from repeated
application of the Frobenius automorphism until the result is a one-dimensional code.
A generator of the latter yields the secret parameters of the code.
3. 
Another approach by HaMaR [10] maps the task to the problem of finding
rank-one codewords in the code generated by the public matrix and a corrupted codeword.
We present the first two approaches for LRS codes in the zero-derivation regime where the automorphism $\sigma$ 
and the evaluation parameters ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ell}$ are known.
Note that the third technique is also applicable to our setting but omitted for brevity.
Moreover, the recovery methods extend to GLRS codes by executing them after guessing the block multipliers,
similar to the distinguishing strategy explained in Subsection 4.2.
Theorem 5.2
Let $\bm{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n}$ denote a generator matrix of an LRS code $\mathcal{C}:=\mathrm{LRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},k]$ with respect to a known
automorphism $\sigma$ and zero derivation.
If the evaluation parameters ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ell}$ are known, we can recover code locators $\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ 
such that $\mathfrak{M}_{k}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}})_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}$ generates $\mathcal{C}$ in $\mathcal{O}({n^{5}})$ operations in $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ .
Proof.
First note that any $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ast}$ -multiple $\tilde{\bm{\beta}}$ of $\bm{\beta}$ is sufficient because
 $\mathfrak{M}_{k}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}})_{a}=\diag\left((c,\sigma(c),\dots,\sigma^{k-1}(c))\right)\cdot\mathfrak{M}_{k}(\bm{\beta})_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}$ 
(40)
holds for $\tilde{\bm{\beta}}:=c\cdot\bm{\beta}$ with $c\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ast}$ .
Since the diagonal matrix has full rank, the row spaces of $\mathfrak{M}_{k}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}})_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}$ and
 $\mathfrak{M}_{k}(\bm{\beta})_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}$ both equal $\mathcal{C}$ .
We show how to recover such a $\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ with the first two of the three approaches mentioned above:
1. 
From Corollary 2, we obtain the equality
 $\langle\Gamma_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{n-k-1}(\bm{G})\rangle=\mathrm{LRS}[\bm{\beta},{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},n-1]$ and the dual $\mathcal{D}$ of
this code has dimension one.
The solution $\bm{H}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ of the system $\Gamma_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{n-k-1}(\bm{G})\cdot\bm{H}^{\top}=\mathbf{0}$ is a generator
matrix (or rather a generator vector) of $\mathcal{D}$ .
Since we are in the zero-derivation case, we can use the result (21) about duals of
LRS codes and recover a suitable $\tilde{\bm{\beta}}$ from $\bm{H}$ via (22).
2. 
We first compute the intersection space $\bigcap_{i=0}^{k-1}\langle\mathcal{D}_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{i}(\bm{G})\rangle$ which is equal to
 $\mathrm{LRS}[\mathcal{D}_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{k-1}(\bm{\beta}),{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}};{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle n$}}},1]$ according to Corollary 3.
Therefore, every generator ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle g$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle g$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle g$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle g$}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}$ of this space (and in particular the one that we computed) has
the form $c\cdot\mathcal{D}_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{k-1}(\bm{\beta})$ for a $c\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\ast}$ .
Note that, in the zero-derivation case, the inverse of the operator $\mathcal{D}_{a}^{i}(\cdot)$ for fixed $a\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ and $i\geq 0$ is
 $\left(\mathcal{D}_{a}^{i}\right)^{-1}(b):=\sigma^{-i}\left(\frac{b}{\mathcal{N}_{i}\left(a\right)}\right)\quad\text{for all }b\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}.$ 
(41)
We use this fact to derive the following equation from ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle g$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle g$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle g$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle g$}}}=c\cdot\mathcal{D}_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{k-1}(\bm{\beta})$ :

The complexity is in both cases dominated by computing the reduced row-echelon form of $\Gamma_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{n-k-1}(\bm{G})$ 
and $\Gamma_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle a$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle a$}}}}^{k-1}(\bm{G})$ , respectively.
This can be achieved in $\mathcal{O}({n^{5}})$ operations in $\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}$ .
∎∎
6 Conclusion
We introduced GLRS codes as LRS codes with nonzero block multipliers and proposed two distinguishers for this code family that are inspired by similar techniques in the Hamming and the rank
metric.
The square-code distinguisher works for the identity automorphism and zero derivation, whereas the
Overbeck-like distinguisher can handle arbitrary automorphisms and derivations.
Both have polynomial runtime when the automorphism $\sigma$ , the derivation $\delta$ , and in the latter case additionally the evaluation parameters $\textstyle a$ and the
block multipliers $\textstyle v$ are known.
Since many McEliece-like cryptosystems use isometric disguising, we further studied codes that are semilinearly
equivalent to GLRS codes.
We showed that GLRS codes are closed under semilinear
equivalence for a fixed automorphism and some possible choices for the derivation.
Finally, we partially solved the problem of recovering a canonical generator matrix (and thus finding an efficient decoder) from an
arbitrary generator matrix of a GLRS code in the zero-derivation case.
The complexity is again polynomial if either $\sigma=\mathrm{Id}$ or $\sigma$ , $\textstyle v$ and $\textstyle a$ are known.
More precisely, we showed that the square-code code approach allows to recover suitable evaluation parameters $\textstyle a$ and block multipliers $\textstyle v$ of a GLRS code
in the identity-automorphism setting, and that an Overbeck-like strategy can recover suitable code locators $\bm{\beta}$ of
a GLRS code for arbitrary automorphisms and zero derivations if $\textstyle a$ and $\textstyle v$ are known.
This work is a first step towards building quantum-secure cryptosystems in the sum-rank metric.
Naturally, many other research questions arise in this field:
As simulations show, the Overbeck-like distinguisher seems not to work when
the wrong evaluation parameters are used.
This is the case even when the parameters are chosen from the correct conjugacy classes, what makes it interesting to
study.
Another idea is to find a new operation with respect to which the square-code distinguisher works also for
arbitrary automorphisms.
We further want to investigate more distinguishing methods as e.g. augmenting the generator matrix or applying
near-isometries and see also how GLRS codes and their distinguishers carry over to the skew metric.
