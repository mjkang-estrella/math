the total number of the near-perfect matchings where the hole is not at
the center is a multiple of 2k+3 . If the hole is not on any of the symmetry
axes, then there are 8 isomorphic positions for the hole, and thus, this orbit
contributes a multiple of 8 × 2k = 2k+3 , as required. On the other hand, if
the hole is on the central row or column but not at the center, then there
are 4 isomorphic positions for the hole, and by the assumption, this orbit
contributes a multiple of 4 × 2k+1 = 2k+3 . Thus, it suffices to show that
the contribution of the second case is also a multiple of 2k+3 .

Suppose h is a hole on one of the two diagonals but not both. Since there are
again 4 isomorphic positions for this hole, it suffices to show that a(2k+1; h)
is a multiple of 2k+1 . Consider a near-perfect matching M with the specific

M of (2k + 1) × (2k + 1) that yield S is a multiple of 2k .

the mirror images of the cycles in S and a path P ′ connecting h and h′
that is a mirror image of the path P . Thus, the number of near-perfect
matchings that yield S ′ is equal to the number that yield S. Since the

3     A rectangle minus a hole
The question for a rectangular grid with a hole is less explored. Here the
total number of near-perfect matchings can be odd. So an obvious question
is, “When is it odd?” We address this by again looking at the question
for specific holes. For odd r and c let a(r, c) be the number of near-perfect
matchings of the r × c grid, and for any particular hole h let a(r, c; h)
denote the number of near-perfect matchings that leave that specified hole
unmatched. Note that due to the reduced symmetry of the rectangle, most
of the arguments used in the previous section are not applicable in this
case.

Say we have an r × c grid with r and c odd imbued with a checkerboard
coloring. We say a vertex is white if it is of the majority coloring; otherwise,
it is black. Further, a white vertex is odd or even based on the parity of
its row or column (so that white vertices on the boundary are odd).

Theorem 3.1. For a white hole h, the number of near-perfect matchings
a(r, c; h) is odd if and only if the following three conditions all hold:
    (i) at least one of r or c is congruent to 1 (mod 4),
 (ii) gcd(r + 1, c + 1) = 2, and
(iii) the hole h is odd.

Our proof of Theorem 3.1 is covered in the following three lemmas. First,

Theorem 3.2. The number of perfect matchings in any graph is even if and
only if there is a nonempty subset S of the vertices such that every vertex
has an even number of neighbors in S.

Lemma 3.3.

 (b) If both r and c are odd and gcd(r + 1, c + 1) is divisible by some odd

Proof. (a) We assign (x, y)-coordinates to vertices in an r ×c rectangle such

Lemma 3.4. For all odd r and c,
 (a) if the white hole h is even, then a(r, c; h) is even;
 (b) if the white hole h is odd, then a(r, c; h) has the same parity regardless

Proof. We use the ideas behind spanning webs (see, for example, Bouttier
et al. [2]). Specifically, associated with the r × c grid R, we define a graph
Ho whose vertices are the odd white vertices of R, where two vertices are
adjacent if they are two apart in the same row or column of R. As noted
for example in [2], given a near-perfect matching M of R, one can associate
a directed spanning subgraph So of Ho , called a spanning web. Specifically,
for each odd white vertex w1 , consider the edge e in M (if any) that covers
w1 . (See the left picture in Figure 3.3, where the thickened edges are the

let w2 be the other neighbor of b in the same row or column (which exists
since all white vertices on the outside of R are odd), and associate this edge
e in M with the arc w1 w2 directed from w1 to w2 . This produces a directed
subgraph So of Ho where every covered white vertex has an out-degree one
and where every uncovered white vertex has an out-degree zero.

of near-perfect matchings is even.

Assume second that the hole h is odd. Then every vertex of So has out-

off the near-perfect matchings for which there is a directed cycle, based

restrict to the near-perfect matchings where the spanning web has no cycle,

has the same parity as the number of rooted spanning trees, but the number
of rooted spanning trees with a fixed root is just the number of spanning
trees and is thus the same for all choices of h.

For the hole h∗ in the middle of the first row, it holds that a(r, c; h∗ ) is odd.

By Lemma 3.3 and 3.4(a), the number of near perfect matchings is even
if any of the three conditions in the theorem fail. By Lemma 3.4(b), in
the remaining case the parity of the number of near-perfect matching is
independent of the exact placement of the hole, and by Lemma 3.5 this is
odd. This completes the proof of Theorem 3.1.

Since the total number of near-perfect matchings is odd if and only if the
first two conditions of Theorem 3.1 hold and the number (r + 1)(c + 1)/4 of
odd white vertices is odd, as a consequence we get the following theorem:

Theorem 3.6. For odd r, c, the number a(r, c) of near-perfect matchings of
the r × c grid is odd if and only if both r and c are congruent to 1 (mod 4)
and gcd(r + 1, c + 1) = 2.

From graph parameters to class properties. Given a graph parameter ρ, we say that a graph
class G has bounded ρ if there exists an integer c such that for every graph G ∈ G, every induced
subgraph G′ of G satisfies ρ(G′ ) ≤ c. Note that a graph class G has bounded ρ if and only if its
hereditary closure has bounded ρ. Our definition differs slightly from the more common definition
stating that a graph class G has bounded ρ if there exists a constant c such that ρ(G) ≤ c for all graphs
G in G. However, the two definitions coincide for hereditary graph classes, as well as for any graph
parameter ρ that is monotone under induced subgraphs.
    A class property is a family of graph classes. Given a class property P and a graph class G, we say
that G satisfies P if G ∈ P. Any graph parameter ρ naturally leads to a class property Bρ , consisting of
precisely those graph classes G that have bounded ρ. The identification of substructures “responsible”
for a parameter ρ to be large leads to a characterisation of the corresponding class property Bρ . For
example:
   • The Grid-Minor Theorem states that a minor-closed graph class has bounded treewidth if and
     only if it excludes some grid.

   • Ramsey’s theorem states that a hereditary graph class has bounded order if and only if it excludes
     some complete graph and some edgeless graph.

Generalisations. Further class properties can be obtained by considering two graph parameters at
once, by requiring that one of the parameters is bounded by a function of the other one. This includes
the following class properties:
   • A hereditary graph class G is χ-bounded if in the class G large cliques are the only structures
     responsible for large chromatic number, that is, there exists a function f such that every graph G
     in G satisfies χ(G) ≤ f (ω(G)), where χ(G) and ω(G) denote the chromatic number and the clique
     number of G, respectively (see [46]).

   • A hereditary graph class G is (tw, ω)-bounded if in the class G large cliques are the only structures
     responsible for large treewidth (see, e.g., [20, 37]).
The above two examples are special cases of the following framework. Given two graph parameters ρ
and σ, we say that a graph class G is (ρ, σ)-bounded (or has σ-bounded ρ) if there exists a nondecreasing
function f such that, for every graph G in the class G, every induced subgraph G′ of G satisfies
ρ(G′ ) ≤ f (σ(G′ )).
    In general, there is no assumption on G to be hereditary, and this is the reason why the condition is
imposed on all induced subgraphs of graphs in G. However, in our paper we focus on hereditary graph
classes, in which case it of course suffices to require that ρ(G) ≤ f (σ(G)) for all graphs G in G.
    In the above terminology, χ-bounded and (tw, ω)-bounded graph classes are said to have ω-bounded
chromatic number and ω-bounded treewidth, respectively. The latter class property is a subfamily of
the class property of ω-bounded expansion studied by Jiang, Nešetřil, and Ossona de Mendez [59].
Several other class properties captured by the above framework were studied in the literature, including
χ-bounded colouring number (see the book by Jensen and Toft [58]), χ-bounded minimum degree
(see [47]), degeneracy-bounded treewidth (by Gyárfás and Zaker [14]), and ω-bounded pathwidth (by
Hermelin, Mestre, and Rawitz [51]).
Clique-bounded class properties. As exemplified by the properties of χ-boundedness, ω-bounded
treewidth, and ω-bounded pathwidth, an important special case of the above framework for defining
properties of graph classes is the case when the parameter σ is the clique number, ω. In this case, given
a graph parameter ρ, we may also say that a graph class that has ω-bounded ρ has clique-bounded ρ and
denote by Cρ the corresponding class property. Thus, we speak of graph classes with clique-bounded
pathwidth, clique-bounded treewidth, etc.

satisfying the class property Bρ (bounded ρ) also satisfies the property Cρ (clique-bounded ρ), which

 3     Cayley graphs of abelian groups
In this section, we study Cayley graphs of abelian groups with the goal to find a charac-
terization of the Hamiltonian-transitive ones. Recall that it is an open question whether
all Cayley graphs with at least three vertices are Hamiltonian, but this is well known to
hold for Cayley graphs of abelian groups [5]. In this section, we extend this result and show
that many Cayley graphs of abelian groups even contain multiple Hamiltonian cycles up to
symmetry (Theorem 2 and Theorem 3).
