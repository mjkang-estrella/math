                                                                                                 Abstract
                                                       For a graph G, we denote by α(G) the size of a maximum independent set and by ω(G) the
                                                   size of a maximum clique in G. Our paper lies on the edge of two lines of research, related to α
                                                   and ω, respectively. One of them studies α-variants of graph parameters, such as α-treewidth or
                                                   α-degeneracy. The second line deals with graph classes where some parameters are bounded by
                                                   a function of ω(G). A famous example of this type is the family of χ-bounded classes, where the
                                                   chromatic number χ(G) is bounded by a function of ω(G).
                                                       A Ramsey-type argument implies that if the α-variant of a graph parameter ρ is bounded by a
                                                   constant in a hereditary class G, then ρ is bounded by a function of ω in G. If the reverse implication
                                                   also holds, we say that ρ is awesome. Otherwise, we say that ρ is awful. In the present paper, we
                                                   identify a number of awesome and awful graph parameters, derive some structural and algorithmic
                                                   applications of awesomeness, and propose a number of open problems related to these notions.

                                           Keywords: graph class, graph parameter, clique number, independence number, Ramsey’s theorem

                                           1       Introduction
                                           Graph parameters play a central role in structural and algorithmic graph theory, capturing key com-
                                           binatorial properties of graphs. Often, a desirable algorithmic or structural property is established
                                           by identifying a suitable graph parameter and showing that the target behaviour follows from its
                                           boundedness. For example, bounded degeneracy ensures that every subgraph has a low-degree vertex,
                                           enabling compact graph representations [75]; bounded treewidth yields efficient algorithms for problems
                                           expressible in monadic second-order logic that are NP-hard in general [31].
                                               A potential limitation of this approach is that boundedness of a graph parameter can substantially
                                           restrict graph structure: for instance, graphs of bounded treewidth are necessarily sparse, so boundedness
                                           of treewidth does not capture dense graphs. This motivates the study of relaxations or generalisations
                                           of boundedness of graph parameters that retain useful structural properties or algorithmic tractability
                                           while encompassing richer classes.
                                               The present paper is on the edge of two such lines of research. The first concerns clique-bounded
                                           parameters, where the value of a parameter is bounded as a function of the clique number ω. A central

example is the theory of χ-bounded classes—hereditary classes in which the chromatic number χ(G) is
bounded by a function of ω(G)—which emerged as a generalisation of perfect graphs [46], and has been
the subject of extensive study (see, e.g., [73]). A more recent example is the family of (tw, ω)-bounded
classes, where the treewidth is bounded by a function of the clique number; such classes are known to
have useful algorithmic applications (see [20, 37]).
    The second line focuses on independence variants of parameters. The independence variant of a
parameter ρ, denoted α-ρ, is obtained by relaxing the size constraint in the definition of ρ to one on the
independence number. These variants are motivated in part by algorithmic considerations: for example,
bounded α-treewidth—a parameter introduced independently by Yolov [77] and by Dallard, Milanič,
and Štorgel (under the name tree-independence number [38, 39])—still guarantees polynomial-time
solvability of the Maximum Weight Independent Set problem (and many others; see [21, 34, 38,
61, 77]), while capturing significantly larger graph classes than bounded treewidth. Another example of
this type is the following independence variant of degeneracy: the α-degeneracy of a graph G is the
minimum k such that every non-null induced subgraph of G has a vertex whose neighbourhood has
independence number at most k. This parameter was studied under the name inductive independence
number by Ye and Borodin [76], from both structural and algorithmic points of view.
    A simple Ramsey-type argument (see Section 3.4) shows that bounded α-ρ always implies that ρ is
clique-bounded. This observation allows properties implied by clique-boundedness of ρ to be transferred
automatically to classes with bounded α-ρ. It is natural to ask whether the converse holds:
                   Does clique-boundedness of ρ always imply boundedness of α-ρ?
Such an implication is not always straightforward. For example, motivated by the complexity of
the Maximum Weight Independent Set problem, it was conjectured that clique-boundedness of
treewidth implies boundedness of α-treewidth [39] (see also [37]); this remained open for some time before
being recently refuted by Chudnovsky and Trotignon [30]. Nevertheless, the question of whether the
Maximum Weight Independent Set problem admits a polynomial-time algorithm in any hereditary
graph class with clique-bounded treewidth, raised by Dallard, Milanič, and Štorgel in [37, 39], remains
open.
    Motivated by the example of treewidth, in this paper, we formalise the notions of clique-boundedness
and independence variants of graph parameters, and investigate when the two notions coincide. Specifi-
cally, a graph parameter ρ is said to be awesome if clique-boundedness of ρ is equivalent to boundedness
of α-ρ, and is awful otherwise. Formal treatment of these notions is given in Section 3. In Section 4, we
show that treedepth and pathwidth are awful parameters. In Section 5, we identify some parameters
that are awesome, and derive structural and algorithmic consequences of awesomeness. These include,
in particular:
   • an infinite family of graph parameters ρ such that the Maximum Weight Independent Set
     problem admits a polynomial-time algorithm in any hereditary graph class with clique-bounded ρ
     (Theorems 5.26 and 5.31); one of these results provides a partial answer to the aforementioned
     question by Dallard et al. [39],
   • a characterisation of hereditary graph classes with bounded α-vertex cover number (Theorem 5.29),
     and
   • a dense analogue of a result of Bonamy et al. [15] establishing logarithmic treewidth in classes
     of graphs excluding some fixed biclique as a subgraph and without many pairwise independent
     cycles (Theorem 5.32); this provides an affirmative answer to a question of Hilaire, Milanič, and
     Vasić [53].
We conclude with a discussion of further questions and open problems in Section 6. In the next section,
we introduce basic notation and main notions.

2       Preliminaries
We denote by N the set of all positive integers. All graphs considered in this paper are finite, simple,
and undirected. Let G = (V, E) be a graph. We denote by V (G) and E(G) the vertex set and the
edge set of G, respectively. For a set S ⊆ V (G), we denote by G[S] the subgraph of G induced by
S. The neighbourhood of a vertex v in G, denoted        by NG (v), is the set of vertices adjacent to v in
G. For a set S ⊆ V (G), we denote NG (S) = ( v∈S NG (v)) \ S. When the graph G is clear from the
                                                  S
context, we may omit the subscript and simply write N (v) and N (S). A vertex v is universal in G if
NG (v) = V (G) \ {v}. The degree of v is the cardinality of the set NG (v). The maximum degree of a
graph G, denoted by ∆(G), is the largest degree of a vertex in G. We use the standard notation Kp,q ,
Ks , Ps , and Cs , for the complete bipartite graph with p and q vertices in the parts, the complete graph,
the path, and the cycle on s vertices, respectively. A star with q leaves is the complete bipartite graph
K1,q . Given a graph H and a nonnegative integer r, we denote by rH the vertex-disjoint union of r
copies of H. An isomorphism from a graph G to a graph G′ is a bijective function f : V (G) → V (G′ )
such that two distinct vertices u, v ∈ V (G) are adjacent in G if and only if their images f (u) and f (v)
are adjacent in G′ . The graphs G and G′ are said to be isomorphic (to each other) if there exists an
isomorphism from G to G′ .
    A clique is a set of pairwise adjacent vertices; an independent set is a set of pairwise nonadjacent
vertices. The clique number of G, denoted by ω(G), is the maximum cardinality of a clique in G; the
independence number of G, denoted by α(G), is the maximum cardinality of an independent set in G.
For a set S ⊆ V (G), the independence number of S is the independence number α(G[S]) of the induced
subgraph G[S]. A set M ⊆ E(G) is a matching in G if no two edges in M share a common vertex. A set
S ⊆ V (G) is a vertex cover in G if S contains at least one vertex of every edge of G. The vertex cover
number of G, denoted by vc(G), is the minimum cardinality of a vertex cover in G. A set S ⊆ V (G)
is a feedback vertex set in G if S contains at least one vertex of every cycle of G. The feedback vertex
set number of G, denoted by fvs(G), is the minimum cardinality of a feedback vertex set in G. A set
S ⊆ V (G) is an odd cycle transversal in G if S contains at least one vertex of every odd cycle of G.
The odd cycle transversal number of G, denoted by oct(G), is the minimum cardinality of an odd cycle
transversal in G.
    A tree decomposition of a graph G is a pair T = (T, β) where T is a tree and β is a mapping
assigning to each node x of the tree T a subset β(x) of the vertex set of G (also called a bag of T ) such
that the following conditions hold: each vertex of G is contained in some bag, for each edge e of G
there exists a bag containing both endpoints of e, and for each vertex v of G, the set of nodes x of T
such that v ∈ β(x) induces a connected subgraph of T . The width of a tree decomposition (T, β) is
defined as the maximum value of |β(x)| over all nodes x of the tree T .1 The treewidth of a graph G,
denoted tw(G), is defined as the minimum width of a tree decomposition of G. A path decomposition
of a graph G is a tree decomposition (P, β) of G such that P is a path. The pathwidth of a graph G,
denoted pw(G), is defined as the minimum width of a path decomposition of G. A rooted tree is a pair
(T, r) such that T is a tree and r is a vertex of T called the root. A rooted forest is a disjoint union of
rooted trees; in particular, a rooted forest may have more than one root. A leaf of a rooted forest F is
a vertex of out-degree 0 in the digraph obtained from F by orienting the edges away from the roots.
Given a rooted forest F , the depth of F is the maximum number of vertices on a root-to-leaf path. The
transitive closure of a rooted forest F is the graph G obtained from F by adding edges to it so that each
root-to-leaf path in F becomes a clique in G (and adding no other edges). A treedepth decomposition of
a graph G is a rooted forest F such that G is a subgraph of the transitive closure of F . The treedepth
of a graph G, denoted td(G), is defined as the minimum depth of a treedepth decomposition of G.
    1
    We deviate from the standard way of defining the width by subtracting 1 from its value. This way, the width simply
corresponds to the cardinality of the largest bag. This small difference, which does not affect boundedness, simplifies the
generalisation to hyperparameters (see p. 8).

3        The framework
In this section, we formalise the key concepts used in the paper. In Section 3.1, we recall the formal
definitions of graph classes and graph parameters, and discuss how the boundedness of graph parameters
can be used to define families of classes (i.e., class properties). We then consider more general ways of
defining class properties, where one parameter is bounded as a function of another parameter, rather
than by a constant. Prominent examples include χ-bounded and (tw, ω)-bounded classes. To treat
these approaches to defining class properties uniformly, we introduce in Section 3.2 the notion of a
hyperparameter. In Section 3.3, we demonstrate how various classical graph parameters, as well as their
independence variants (e.g., the tree-independence number), can be expressed as hyperparameters. In
Section 3.4, we observe that for any cardinality based hyperparameter ρ, boundedness of its independence
variant in a class of graphs implies that ρ is clique-bounded in that class. Finally, in Section 3.5, we
formally state the central question of our study: for which hyperparameters does the converse implication
hold?

3.1        Graph parameters, graph classes, and class properties

Graph classes and graph parameters. A graph class is a collection of graphs closed under
isomorphism. A graph class is hereditary if it is closed under vertex deletion; in other words, for every
graph in the class, all of its induced subgraphs also belong to the class. Given a graph class G, the
hereditary closure of G is the class of all induced subgraphs of graphs in G. For a set F of graphs, we
say that a graph G is F-free if no graph in F is isomorphic to an induced subgraph of G. If F consists
of a single graph F , we write F -free instead of {F }-free. It is a folklore fact that a graph class G is
hereditary if and only if there exists a set F of graphs such that a graph G belongs to G if and only if G
is F-free.
    A graph parameter (also called graph invariant) is a function mapping graphs to the set of integers2
that does not distinguish between isomorphic graphs. A graph parameter is monotone under induced
subgraphs if, for every graph G and every induced subgraph H of G, the value of the parameter on H
is at most its value on G. Many questions in graph theory can be formulated in terms of hereditary
graph classes and graph parameters. In particular, structural graph theory aims at understanding
which substructures, when present in a graph, are “responsible” for a certain parameter to be large. For
example:

        • Ramsey’s theorem (see [71]) states that a necessary and sufficient condition for the number of
          vertices of a graph to be large is the presence of either a large clique or a large independent set.

        • The Grid-Minor Theorem of Robertson and Seymour (see [72]) states that a necessary and sufficient
          condition for the treewidth of a graph to be large is the presence of a large grid as a minor.

        • The Strong Perfect Graph Theorem due to Chudnovsky, Robertson, Seymour, and Thomas (see [29])
          can be stated as follows. A necessary and sufficient condition for the maximum difference, over all
          induced subgraphs, between the chromatic number and the clique number to be strictly positive is
          the presence of an induced odd cycle of length at least five in the graph or its complement.
