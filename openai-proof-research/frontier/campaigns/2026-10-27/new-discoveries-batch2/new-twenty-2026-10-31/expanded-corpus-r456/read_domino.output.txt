                                           A Note on One-Hole Domino Tilings of Squares and
                                                             Rectangles
                                                                 Seok Hyun Byun and Wayne Goddard
                                                School of Mathematical and Statistical Sciences, Clemson University

                                                                                 Abstract

                                                     We consider the number of domino tilings of an odd-by-odd rectangle
                                                that leave one hole. This problem is equivalent to the number of near-perfect




arXiv:2502.05918v2 [math.CO] 3 Jun 2025
                                                matchings of the odd-by-odd rectangular grid. For any particular position
                                                of the vacancy on the (2k + 1) × (2k + 1) square grid, we show that the
                                                number of near-perfect matchings is a multiple of 2k , and from this follows
                                                a conjecture of Kong that the total number of near-perfect matchings is a
                                                multiple of 2k . We also determine the parity of the number of near-perfect
                                                matchings with a particular vacancy for the rectangle case.



                                          1     Introduction

                                          Consider a rectangular board. A domino is the union of two adjacent unit squares
                                          on the board and a domino tiling of the board is a collection of dominoes that
                                          cover the board without gaps and overlaps. Since every domino consists of two
                                          unit squares, if the board has odd area, it cannot have a domino tiling. However,
                                          in this case, one can instead think of domino tilings of the board with one vacancy.
                                          This paper proves some number-theoretic properties of the total numbers of such
                                          tilings.

                                              These problems can be equivalently phrased in terms of graphs and their
                                          (near) perfect matchings. Given a graph with an even number of vertices, a
                                          perfect matching is a collection of edges such that every vertex is contained in
                                          exactly one edge. Similarly, given a graph with an odd number of vertices, a
                                          near-perfect matching is a collection of edges such that all but one vertex is
                                          contained in exactly one edge and the one vertex is unmatched. Consider an
                                          m × n rectangular grid. If either m or n is even, then a domino tiling of the board
                                          is equivalent to a perfect matching of the grid, and if both m and n are odd, then
                                          a domino tiling with one vacancy is equivalent to a near-perfect matching of the
                                          grid. Throughout this paper, we phrase our results in terms of grids and their

                                                                                     1
(near) perfect matchings, though one can also state them using the language of
domino tilings.

    The case when at least one of the side-lengths is even is well-studied. For
example, the number of perfect matchings of such a grid is given by an ex-
plicit product formula due to Kasteleyn [6] and independently to Temperley and
Fisher [5, 11]. If we restrict ourselves to square grids with even side-length, it is
known that the number of perfect matchings exhibits some interesting number-
theoretic properties. For instance, Pachter [9] showed that the number of perfect
matchings of a square grid of side-length 2k is of the form 2k (2ℓ+1)2 , and Cohn [4]
further studied the 2-adic behavior of this number as k varies.

    On the other hand, much less is known and studied for the grid with odd side-
length. In [7], Kong gave a computational approach to the problem of finding
the number of near-perfect matchings of the odd square grid, and made some
conjectures analogous to the results for the even square grid in [9] and [4]. Note
that Bouttier et al. [2] also considered this problem from a more graph-theoretic
point of view.

    In the first part of the paper, we consider one of the problems posed by Kong
in [7] and give a proof of it. More precisely, for any particular position of the
vacancy on the square (2k + 1) × (2k + 1) grid, we show that the number of
near-perfect matchings is a multiple of 2k . From this result, we conclude that
the total number of near-perfect matchings is a multiple of 2k and prove one of
Kong’s conjectures.

    In the second half of the paper, we consider a rectangular grid with a fixed
vacancy and give some parity and modulo 4 residue results on the number of
near-perfect matchings of it.



2    The Square Minus a Hole

Let a(2k + 1) be the number of near-perfect matchings of the (2k + 1) × (2k + 1)
grid. Kong [7] conjectured that a(2k + 1) is an odd multiple of 2k . We present a
proof of this conjecture in Theorems 1 and 2 below.

    We need a result about near-perfect matchings with a specific (or fixed) hole.

                                         2
For any particular hole h of the (2k + 1) × (2k + 1) grid, let a(2k + 1; h) denote
the number of near-perfect matchings with h unmatched, or equivalently the
number of perfect matchings of the subgraph obtaining by deleting h. (Note that
in what follows we implicitly assume h has the same color as the corners under
the checkerboard coloring, since otherwise there is no near-perfect matching, as
can be easily seen using a coloring argument.) This problem has been studied
for some particular positions of h. For example, when h is on the boundary,
by the bijection of Temperley [10], the value a(2k + 1; h) equals the number of
spanning trees of the (k + 1) × (k + 1) grid, no matter where on the boundary h
is. Importantly here, Tenner [12] showed that if h is the center, then a(2k + 1; h)
is 2k times an odd square (see Corollary 2 in that paper). Also, Bouttier et
al. [2] generalized Temperley’s bijection to show that there is a bijection between
(i) near-perfect matchings with a fixed hole, and (ii) spanning webs whose tree
component contains the vacancy, together with a choice of orientation for each
loop of the spanning web and each loop of the dual web. This bijection will be
discussed further and used when we prove Lemma 7 in Section 3.

   We now present our first result. In its proof, we use the idea behind the proof
of the Matching Factorization Theorem of Ciucu [3].


Theorem 1 For any particular hole h of the (2k + 1) × (2k + 1) grid, a(2k + 1; h)
is a multiple of 2k .


Proof. By the result of Tenner [12] mentioned above, we know the result is true
for the central hole. So assume h is not the center. Fix some diagonal d of the
grid that does not contain h.

   Consider a near-perfect matching M with the specified hole h. (For an exam-
ple, see the left picture in Figure 1.) Let M ′ be the near-perfect matching that
is the mirror image of M around d, and let S be the spanning multi-graph with
edge set M ∪ M ′ . Then, every vertex in S has degree 2 except for the hole h and
its mirror image h′ . Thus S consists of a collection of (alternating) cycles (where
some cycles might have length 2) and an (alternating) path P connecting h′ to h
(see the right picture in Figure 1). Further, any cycle of S that contains a vertex
of d is symmetric, as is the h′ –h path. In particular, this means that a cycle that

contains a vertex of d contains exactly two of them, and the h′ –h path contains
exactly one vertex of d.
   Let X denote the set of edges in M that are not part of components in S
containing vertices of d. We partition the set of near-perfect matchings with
the specified hole h by the pair (S, X). There are k cycles that contain vertices
of d, and each cycle can be produced in two ways (depending on which half is M
and which half is M ′ ). So, for each choice of (S, X), the number of near-perfect
matchings is 2k . Hence, when one sums up over all choices of (S, X), one obtains
that a(2k + 1; h) is a multiple of 2k , as required.   2

   Now, for every choice of the hole except the center, there are at least four
isomorphic positions for it. Thus, each orbit collectively contributes a multiple
of 2k+2 to the sum. So, by the result for the middle hole of Tenner, the overall
count is 2k ck for some odd ck . The conjecture of Kong follows:

Theorem 2 For all k, the number a(2k + 1) of near-perfect matching of a (2k +
1) × (2k + 1) square grid is an odd multiple of 2k .

   Kong [7] made some further conjectures on the properties of ck . For example:

Conjecture 1 For all k, a(2k + 1) = 2k ck where ck is congruent to 1 modulo 8.

    We show that Conjecture 1 can be reduced to a much simpler statement.


Theorem 3 If we assume that a(2k + 1; h) is a multiple of 2k+1 for every hole h
that lies on the central row or column but not at the center, then Conjecture 1
holds.


    Note that the statement “a(2k + 1; h) is a multiple of 2k+1 for every hole h
that lies on the central row or column but not at the center” has been checked
for all integers k ≤ 16.

    Proof. Depending on the position of the hole h, we consider the following
four cases.

    • h is the central hole,

    • h is on one of the two diagonals but not at the center,

    • h is on the central row or column but not at the center, and

    • h is not on any of the symmetry axes.

    If h is the central hole, then by the result of Tenner [12], a(2k + 1; h) is 2k
times an odd square. Since an odd square is congruent to 1 modulo 8, we have
a(2k + 1; h)/2k ≡ 1 mod 8 in this case. Thus, it suffices to show that the total
number of the near-perfect matchings where the holes is not at the center is a
multiple of 2k+3 . If the hole is not on any of the symmetry axes, then there are
8 isomorphic positions for the hole, and thus, this orbit contributes a multiple of
8 × 2k = 2k+3 , as required. On the other hand, if the hole is on the central row or
column but not at the center, then there are 4 isomorphic positions for the hole,
and by the assumption, this orbit contributes a multiple of 4 × 2k+1 = 2k+3 .
Thus, it suffices to show that the contribution of the second case is also a multiple
of 2k+3 .

    Suppose h is a hole on one of the two diagonals but not both. Since there are
again 4 isomorphic positions for this hole, it suffices to show that a(2k + 1; h) is
a multiple of 2k+1 . Consider a near-perfect matching M with the specific such
h (for example, see the left picture in Figure 2). Let d′ be the diagonal that h

lies on, and d be the other diagonal. As in the proof of Theorem 1, we reflect M
across d. Then, we get the spanning multi-graph S that consist of (alternating)
cycles and an (alternating) path P connecting h and h′ (see the middle picture
in Figure 2). As before, P contains exactly one vertex on d and S contains k
cycles that pass through d. Thus for any specified S, the number of near-perfect
matchings M of (2k + 1) × (2k + 1) that yield S is a multiple of 2k .

    Now, observe that the configuration S cannot be symmetric across d′ , since
vertex h has only one incident edge. Let S ′ be the mirror image of S across d′
(see the right picture in Figure 2). Then, by symmetry, S ′ consists of the mirror
images of the cycles in S and a path P ′ connecting h and h′ that is a mirror
image of the path P . Thus, the number of near-perfect matchings that yield S ′ is
equal to the number that yield S. Since the number a(2k + 1, h) can be written
as a sum over S of the number of matchings M such that M ∪ M ′ is equal to S
or S ′ , the number a(2k + 1, h) is a multiple of 2k+1 . This completes the proof.

3    A Rectangle minus a Hole

The question for a rectangular grid with a hole is less explored. Here the total
number of near-perfect matchings can be odd. So an obvious question is: when
is it odd? We address this by again looking at the question for specific holes.


                                        6
For odd r and c, let a(r, c) be the number of near-perfect matchings of the r × c
grid; and for any particular hole h, let a(r, c; h) denote the number of near-perfect
matchings that leave that specified hole unmatched. Note that due to the reduced
symmetry of the rectangle, most of the arguments used in the previous section
are not applicable in this case.

   Say we have a r×c grid with r and c odd imbued with a checkerboard coloring.
We say a vertex is white if it is of the majority coloring; otherwise, it is black.
Further, a white vertex is odd or even based on the parity of its row or column
(so that white vertices on the boundary are odd).


Theorem 4 For a white hole h, the number of near-perfect matchings a(r, c; h)
is odd if and only if the following three conditions all hold:
(i) At least one of r or c is congruent to 1 mod 4;
(ii) gcd(r + 1, c + 1) = 2; and
(iii) the hole h is odd.


   Our proof of Theorem 4 consists of three parts. The first part is to show that
if neither condition (i) nor condition (ii) holds, then a(r, c; h) is always even. We
need an old theorem of Lovász (see [8]):


Theorem 5 The number of perfect matchings in any graph is even if and only
if there is a nonempty subset S of the vertices such that every vertex has an even
number of neighbors in S.


Lemma 6 (a) If both r and c are congruent to 3 mod 4, then a(r, c; h) is even.
(b) If both r and c are odd and gcd(r + 1, c + 1) is divisible by some odd f > 1,
then a(r, c; h) is even.


Proof. (a) We assign (x, y)-coordinates to vertices in a r-by-c rectangle such
that the vertex at the bottom left corner has a coordinate (1, 1). Let S be
all vertices with coordinates (i + 1, j), (i − 1, j), (i, j − 1) and (i, j + 1) where
i, j ≡ 2 mod 4. Figure 3 gives an example of S with r = 7 and c = 11. It can
readily be checked that S has the desired property in Theorem 5; that is, every
vertex has an even number of neighbors in S. Now, note that all vertices in

S are black (their coordinates sum to odd). Thus, the set S also satisfies the
desired property after removing any collection of white vertices from the grid. In
particular, a(r, c; h) is even for any white hole h.
   (b) Note that both r + 1 and c + 1 are multiples of 2f . First partition the
rectangle into (2f − 1) × (2f − 1) subgraphs with one empty row and one empty
column between consecutive subgraphs. Then form S by, in each subgraph, taking
the 4(f − 1) vertices whose Manhattan distance from the center is f . Figure 4
is an example with f = 3: namely r = 5 and c = 17. Again, S satisfies the
condition of Theorem 5 and all vertices in S are black. Hence, again the graph
with any white vertex removed has an even number of perfect matchings.          2

   We continue the proof of Theorem 4.

Lemma 7 For all odd r and c,
(a) If the white hole h is even, then a(r, c; h) is even.

Figure 5: The left picture show a near-perfect matching M of a 7 × 9 grid with

(b) If the white hole h is odd, then a(r, c; h) has the same parity regardless of
where the hole actually is.


Proof. We use the ideas behind spanning webs (see, for example, Bouttier et
al. [2]). Specifically, associated with the r × c grid R, we define a graph Ho whose
vertices are the odd white vertices of R, where two vertices are adjacent if they
are two apart in the same row or column of R. As noted for example in [2], given a
near-perfect matching M of R, one can associate a directed spanning subgraph So
of Ho , called a spanning web. Specifically, for each odd white vertex w1 consider
the edge e in M (if any) that covers w1 . (See the left picture in Figure 5, where
the thickened edges are the ones covering odd white vertices.) Say e also covers
black vertex b. Then let w2 be the other neighbor of b in the same row/column
(exists since all white vertices on the outside of R are odd); and associate this
edge e in M with the arc w1 w2 directed from w1 to w2 . This produces a directed
subgraph So of Ho where every covered white vertex has an out-degree one, and
every uncovered white vertex has an out-degree zero.

   Assume first that the hole h is even. Consider a near-perfect matching M of R.
Then every vertex of So has out-degree exactly one, and thus the corresponding
spanning web necessarily contains a directed cycle. This cycle corresponds to
an alternating cycle in R. If one reverses the cycle in So , then one obtains the
“complementary” near-perfect matching M ′ (see the right picture in Figure 5).
A parity argument (for example noted in [2]) shows that all cycles surround h.
Thus one can pair off the near-perfect matchings, say by their innermost cycle.

Hence the total number of near-perfect matchings is even.

   Assume second that the hole h is odd. Then every vertex of So has out-
degree one, except for h itself, which has out-degree zero. The directed subgraph
So might or might not have a cycle. But again one can pair off the near-perfect
matchings for which there is a directed cycle, based on reversing say the innermost
cycle. So to determine the parity, one can restrict to the near-perfect matchings
where the spanning web has no cycle, and so is a spanning tree of Ho rooted at h.
(See Figure 6.) As noted in [2] (or due to Temperley’s bijection [10]), there is
a unique near-perfect matching for each such So . So the total number of near-
perfect matchings has the same parity as the number of rooted spanning trees;
but the number of rooted spanning trees with a fixed root is just the number of
spanning trees, and is thus the same for all choices of h.         2

   We next determine the parity in one particular case:


Lemma 8 Assume c is congruent to 1 mod 4 and gcd(r + 1, c + 1) = 2. For the
hole h∗ in the middle of the first row, it holds that a(r, c; h∗ ) is odd.


Proof. We count the number of near-perfect matchings. Those that are not
symmetric with respect to the middle column can be paired off. So a(r, c; h∗ ) has
the same parity as the number of near-perfect matchings M that are symmetric
with respect to the middle column.

   This means that the middle column except for h∗ is filled by vertical edges
of M . Hence this number equals the number of perfect matchings of the c′ =

(c−1)/2 by r subrectangle that results. Note that c′ is even and so gcd(c′ +1, r+1)
is odd. At the same time, gcd(c′ + 1, r + 1) divides gcd(2(c′ + 1), r + 1)) =
gcd(c + 1, r + 1) = 2. That is, gcd(c′ + 1, r + 1) = 1. So by Proposition 5.10 of
Barkley and Liu [1], the number of perfect matchings of such a rectangle is odd.

    By Lemma 6 and 7(a), the number of near perfect matchings is even if any of
the three conditions in the theorem fail. By Lemma 7(b), in the remaining case
the parity of the number of near-perfect matching is independent of the exact
placement of the hole, and by Lemma 8 this is odd. This completes the proof of
Theorem 4.

    Since the total number of near-perfect matchings is odd if and only if the first
two conditions of Theorem 4 hold and the number (r + 1)(c + 1)/4 of odd white
vertices is odd, as a consequence we get:


Theorem 9 For odd r, c, the number a(r, c) of near-perfect matchings of the
r × c grid is odd if and only if both r and c are congruent to 1 mod 4, and
gcd(r + 1, c + 1) = 2.


    A natural next question is to consider counts modulo 4. For example, the
proof of Lemma 7(b) can be extended to show that the number of near perfect
matchings for an odd white hole is the same modulo 4 for all odd white holes.
This uses the fact that in this case there is also guaranteed to be a cycle in Se ,
the graph analogous to So except that its vertices are the even white vertices.
We omit the details.

    We conclude with one example conjecture:


Conjecture 2 Except sometimes when r and c are both congruent to 3 modulo 4,
the count a(r, c; h) is a multiple of 4 for any even white hole h.
