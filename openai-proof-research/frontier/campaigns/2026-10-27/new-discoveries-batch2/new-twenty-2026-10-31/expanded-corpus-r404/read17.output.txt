                                                                                                    Abstract
                                                     An Italian dominating function on a (di)graph G with vertex set V (G) is a function f : V (G) →
                                                 {0, 1, 2} such that every vertex v ∈ V (G) such that f (v) = 0 has an (in)neighbour assigned 2 or two
                                                 (in)neighbours assigned 1. We complete the investigation of the Italian domination numbers of Cartesian
                                                 products of directed cycles.


                                          1      Introduction
                                          A dominating set S of a graph G is a subset of the vertices of G such that every vertex not in S has a
                                          neighbour in S. The domination number γ(G) is the minimum cardinality of a dominating set in G. A
                                          2-dominating set S of a graph G, introduced by Fink and Jacobson [5], is a subset of the vertices of G
                                          such that every vertex not in S has two neighbours in S. The 2-domination number γ2 (G) is the minimum
                                          cardinality of a 2-dominating set in G.
                                              A Roman dominating function of a graph G, introduced by Cockayne et al. [3] is a function f : V (G) →
                                          {0, 1, 2} such that for every v ∈ V (G) with f (v) = 0, it is adjacent
                                                                                                         P        to a vertex w with f (w) = 2. The Roman
                                          domination number γR (G) is the minimum weight, i.e.             v∈V (G) f (v), of a Roman dominating function
                                          on G. A weak Roman dominating function of a graph G, introduced by Henning and Hedetniemi [7] is a
                                          function f : V (G) → {0, 1, 2} such that for every v ∈ V (G) with f (v) = 0, it is adjacent to a vertex w with
                                          f (w) 6= 0, and the derived function g : V (G) → {0, 1, 2} with g(v) = 1, g(w) = f (w) − 1, and g(x) = f (x)
                                          for all x 6= v, w is such that g is a dominating function. The weak Roman domination number γr (G) is the
                                          minimum weight of a weak Roman dominating function.
                                              A 2-rainbow dominating function of a graph G, introduced by Brešar et al. [1], is a function f : V (G) →
                                          P({1, 2}) such that for every v ∈ V (G) with f (v) = ∅, it is adjacent to a vertex w with 1 ∈ f (w) and it is
                                          adjacent to a vertex x with 2 ∈ f (x). The 2-rainbow domination number γr2 (G) is the minimum weight, i.e.
                                          P

                                              A {2}-dominating function of a graph G, introduced by Domke et al. [4], is a function f : V (G) → {0, 1, 2}
                                          such that for every v ∈ V (G), its closed neighbourhood has weight at least two. The {2}-domination number
                                          γ{2} (G) is the minimum weight of a {2}-dominating function.
                                              An Italian dominating function, or Roman {2}-dominating function of a graph G, introduced by Chellali
                                          et al. [2] is a function f : V (G) → {0, 1, 2} such that for every v ∈ V (G) with f (v) = 0, it is adjacent to a
                                          vertex w with f (w) = 2 or two vertices x1 , x2 such that f (x1 ) = f (x2 ) = 1. The Italian domination number
                                          γI (G) is the minimum weight of a Italian dominating function.

                                                                                        Abstract
                                             Ramsey Theory deals with avoiding certain patterns. When constructing an instance that avoids
                                         one pattern, it is observed that other patterns emerge. For example, repetition emerges when avoid-
                                         ing arithmetic progression (Van der Waerden numbers), while reflection emerges when avoiding
                                         monochromatic solutions of a + b = c (Schur numbers). We exploit observed patterns when col-
                                         oring a grid while avoiding monochromatic rectangles. Like many problems in Ramsey Theory, this
                                         problem has a rapidly growing search space that makes computer search difficult. Steinbach et al.
                                         obtained a solution of an 18 by 18 grid with 4 colors by enforcing a rotation symmetry. However that
                                         symmetry is not suitable for 5 colors.
                                             In this article, we will encode this problem into propositional logic and enforce so-called internal
                                         symmetries, which preserves satisfiability, to guide SAT-solving. We first observe patterns with 2 and
                                         3 colors, among which the “shift pattern” can be easily generalized and efficiently encoded. Using
                                         this pattern, we obtain a new solution of the 18 by 18 grid that is non-isomorphic to the known
                                         solution. We further analyze the pattern and obtain necessary conditions to further trim down the
                                         search space. We conclude with our attempts on finding a 5-coloring of a 26 by 26 grid, as well as
                                         further open problems on the shift pattern.


                                         1     Introduction
                                         Consider coloring an m by n grid with k colors, such that there is no monochromatic rectangle.
                                         When this is possible, we say that the m by n grid is k-colorable. Many results have been derived
                                         by pure combinatorial approach: for example, a generalization of Van der Waerden’s Theorem can
                                         give an upper bound, and Fenner et al. [5] showed that for each prime power k, a k2 + k by k grid is
                                         k-colorable but adding a row makes it not k-colorable. However, these results are unable to decide
                                         many grid sizes: whether an 18 by 18 grid is 4-colorable is an example. This grid had been the last
                                         missing piece of the question of 4-colorability, and a challenge prize was raised to close the gap in 2009
                                         [7]. This innocuous problem turned out to be an extremely complex computational challenge. Three
                                         years later, Steinbach et al. [15] found a valid 4-coloring of that grid by encoding the problem into
                                         propositional logic and applying SAT-solving techniques. SAT techniques have also been effective
                                         for various other mathematical problems, including Erdős discrepancy problem [13], the Pythagorean
                                         Triples problem [10], and Keller’s conjecture [3]. The solution by Steinbach et al. is shown in Figure 1.
                                         Notice that the color assignments are highly symmetric: assignments of red is obtained by rotating
                                         the assignments of white around the center by 90 degrees, blue by 180 degrees, and so on. By now,
                                         the k-colorability has been decided for k ∈ {2, 3, 4} for all grids.
                                             Therefore, it is natural to ask, what about 5 colors? Applying the aforementioned theorem of
                                         Fenner et al. [5], the 25 by 30 grid is 5-colorable, but for other grids such as 26 by 26 the problem
                                         remains open. Like many combinatorial search problems, the rectangle-free grid coloring problem
                                         is characterized by enormous search space and rich symmetries. Symmetry breaking is a common
                                         technique to trim down the search space while preserving satisfiability. While breaking symmetries
                                         between different solutions is definitely helpful, breaking the so-called “internal symmetries” that
                                         is within a specific solution has also been proved to be effective [11]. Enforcing observed patterns

            However, the rotation internal symmetry that Steinbach et al. [15] applied cannot translate to 5
       colors. In finding a 4-coloring of the 18 by 18 grid, Steinbach et al. [15] generated a “cyclic reusable
       assignment” for one color, and rotate the solution by 90, 180, and 270 degrees to assign to the
       remaining three. While rotation by 90 degrees is a natural symmetry for 4 colors, this does not apply
       to the number of colors k that are not multiples of 4.
            Thus, to find a 5-coloring of 26 by 26, or rather, to find a valid coloring for any number of colors
       k in general, an internal symmetry that is applicable to all k is very desirable. With these missions
       in mind, we found a novel internal symmetry that is unrestricted by the number of colors k: As
       Figure 1 shows, this pattern is compatible with the known Steinbach’s solution of 18 by 18 grid, and
       it leads to new solutions of 18 by 18 grid that cannot be obtained from the existing one. Search time
       can be further reduced for this pattern: further analysis poses number of occurrences constraints to
       colors; a correct color distribution solution to these constraints can reduce the search time for G24,24
       and G25,25 to a matter of minutes. Equipped with this new pattern, we also made attempts to solve
       the 26 by 26 grid; many attempts came down to only 2 or 3 unsatisfied clauses, but none of them
       succeeded.

       our attempts on the 26 by 26 grid problems, as well as conjectures collected.


       2     Preliminaries
       Definitions and terminologies Let Gm,n denote an m by n grid, and label the cells with
       ordered pairs in the set [m] × [n]. For a fixed coloring of Gm,n , a monochromatic rectangle is four
       distinct points (a, b), (a, d), (c, b), (c, d) ∈ [m] × [n] that are assigned the same color. A grid Gm,n is
       k-colorable if, for some coloring χ : [m] × [n] → [k], there is no monochromatic rectangle, and such χ
       is called a k-coloring.
           Throughout this article, we shall use the word “pattern” and “internal symmetry” interchangeably.

Experimental setup Throughout the article, we used the conflict-driven clause learning (CDCL)
SAT solver CaDiCaL and the local search SAT solver YalSAT developed by Biere [2]. CaDiCaL was
used for smaller grid instances of size up to 18 by 18. For larger grid instances with patterns that
are likely to be satisfiable, we used PalSAT, a parallel version of YalSAT. In our experience, local
search outperforms CDCL on the satisfiable instances, similar to other hard-combinatorial satisfiable
problems [10, 9]. To solve integer constraints for color distribution solutions discussed in Section 4.3,
we used Z3 Theorem Prover developed by Microsoft [4].
   For smaller grid instances of size up to 18 by 18, we ran CaDiCaL on the general purpose linux
hosts at Carnegie Mellon University, as these instances tend to be not resource-intensive and have
reasonably short runtimes.
   For 5-colorings of larger grid instances, the SAT solvers were ran on the Lonestar 5 cluster of
Texas at Texas Advance Computing Center, which has Xeon E5-2690 processors with 24 cores per
node. We ran PalSAT on one node with option “-t 12 $RANDOM$RANDOM”, which specifies 12
worker threads to be created and a random seed.
