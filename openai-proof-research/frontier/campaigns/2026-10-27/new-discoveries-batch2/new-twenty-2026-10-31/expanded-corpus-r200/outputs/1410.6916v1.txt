2      Proof of Theorem 1.6 for k = 2
Proof of Theorem 1.6 for k = 2. Assume we have n balls labeled with the numbers 1, . . . , n respectively.
Suppose we have two ball boxes: a red box with room for exactly p1 balls and a blue box with room
for exactly p2 balls. Let Sr be the sum of the labels on the balls in the red box and Sb the sum of the
labels on the balls in the blue box. We want to show that we can distribute the balls among the two
boxes so that Sr = Sb . If we put all p1 high balls in the red box and all the p2 low balls in the blue box,
then clearly Sr ⩾ sn,2 , by (1). We call this arrangement Arrangement 1. If we put all p1 low balls in
the red box and all the p2 high balls in the blue box, then clearly Sb ⩾ sn,2 , since p2 ⩾ p1 . We call this
arrangement Arrangement 2. We shall show that we can switch from Arrangement 1 to Arrangement
2 by a sequence of steps, such that in each step we exchange two balls labeled with adjacent numbers
between the two boxes. Since in each step Sr and Sb change by 1, there must be a stage in which Sr = Sb .
    We start with the blue box containing the balls {1, 2, . . . , p2 } and the red box containing the balls
{p2 + 1, p2 + 2, . . . , n} (Arrangement 1). We use the notation a ↔ b to mean that the ball labeled a moves
from the blue box to the red box and the ball labeled b moves from the red box to the blue box. In the
first sequence of exchanges we do the following: p2 ↔ p2 + 1, p2 − 1 ↔ p2 , . . ., 1 ↔ 2. This sequence
of exchanges results in the blue box containing the balls {2, 3, . . . , p2 + 1} and the red box containing
{1, p2 + 2, p2 + 3, . . . , n}. We now proceed exchanging p2 + 1 ↔ p2 + 2 and on to the next sequence of
exchanges which results in {3, 4, . . . , p2 + 2} and {1, 2, p2 + 3, p2 + 4, . . . , n}, respectively. Eventually we
arrive at Arrangement 2.

Proof of Claim 7. From Claim 4 we know that {z1 , z1 + 1, . . . , x, x + 1} ⊂ A1 ∪ A2 ∪ A3 . We need to

5     Discussion
It might be possible to generalize the continuity approach used for k = 2 to higher k’s by using k boxes
and applying a higher dimensional continuity technique, such as Sperner’s theorem. For this, it seems
that the condition (1) is just what we need in order to color the vertices of a multidimensional simplex.
The problem that remains is to define a correct division into (k − 1)-dimensional simplices.
