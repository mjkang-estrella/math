Definition 1.4. Let n and k be positive integer such that sn,k is an integer. We say that the partition

    This conjecture was in fact proved in [6] for k = 2, 3. The case k = 2 was independently proved in
[4]. The main result in this paper is:

Theorem 1.6. Conjecture 1.5 holds for k = 4.
    The approach used for proving Theorem 1.6 also provides simple proofs for the cases k = 2, 3 (Re-
marks 2.8 and 2.11).
    Thus, the k-partite complete graphs for which a magic distance labeling exits are characterized for
k ⩽ 4. This also solves the above mentioned closed distance magic labeling problem from [1] for k = 4.
    Note that the case where p1 = 1 is left out in Conjecture 1.5, as this case is different and straight-
forward. Suppose p1 = 1. In order for a partition which implements {pi }ki=1 to be equitable, the set of
size 1 must be {n}, and thus, n = sn,k , which is equivalent to k = (n + 1)/2. Since there can be only
one set of size 1 in an equitable partition, all the other sets must be of size 2. Thus, we can partition the
remaining [n − 1] into k − 1 pairs, in the obvious way, to get an equitable partition.

d + 1, . . . , d + k − 1 ∈ A3 and d + k 6∈ A3 . If d + k ∈ A4 and k > 2, let c = d + k − 1 and define
A0 = χc,c+1 (χx,x+1 (A)). We have A0 ≡ A and since c 6= d + 1 (k > 2), A0 has d in its low set and d + 1
in its high set, contradicting Observation 2.6. If k = 2, that is, d + 2 ∈ A4 , let A0 = χx,x+1 (A). The
partition A0 is minimal and has the same setup that was excluded in Claim 1. Hence, we must have that
either d + k ∈ A2 or d + k ∈ A1 . We show that in the second case d + k = x. Suppose d + k 6= x. We
handle separately the two cases: k > 2 and k = 2. If k > 2, let A0 be the partition consisting of the sets
A01 = A1 \{x, d+k}∪{x+1, d+k−1}, A02 = A2 \{x+1, d}∪{x, d+1}, A03 = A3 \{d+1, d+k−1}∪{d, d+k}
and A04 = A4 . We have A0 ≡ A, x + 1 ∈ A01 , and x + 2 ∈ A04 , contradicting Observation 2.6. If k = 2,
that is, d + 2 ∈ A1 , let A0 = χx+1,x+2 (χx,x+1 (A)). Then, A0 has the same structure that was ruled out
in Claim 1. It follows that d + k = x. (Note that the application of χx+1,x+2 may ruin the assumption
of Claim 3, but this assumption is not needed in Claim 1).
Claim 5. x is the minimal element in A1 .
Proof of Claim 5. Suppose, for contradiction, that t is the maximal element in A1 such that t < x. So,
we must have t < z1 , by Claim 4. Now, t + 1 6∈ A4 , by Observation 2.6, and t + 1 6∈ A2 , by Lemma 3.5(ii).
So, t + 1 ∈ A3 . Let k ⩾ 1 be such that t + 1, . . . , t + k ∈ A3 and t + k + 1 6∈ A3 . Suppose t + k + 1 ∈ A4 . If
k > 1, let A0 = χt+k,t+k+1 (A). Since k > 1 we have t + 1 6= t + k and hence t + 1 is in the high set of A0 ,
contrary to Observation 2.6. If k = 1, then we have the setup excluded in Claim 1 (with t instead of d).
Hence, t + k + 1 6∈ A4 . It follows that t + k + 1 ∈ A2 . Let A0 = χx+1,x+2 (A). Now, t + k + 1 is in the high
set of A0 and we have a similar setup as the one just above. (Note that the application of χx+1,x+2 may
ruin the assumption of Claim 3, but this assumption is not needed in Observation 2.6 or Claim 1).

   Let w be the smallest number in A1 − x. By Claims 4 and 5, w − 1 6∈ A2 and, by Lemma 3.5(vi) and
Claim 2, w − 1 6∈ A4 . Thus, w − 1 ∈ A3 .
Claim 6. There is no element t ∈ B4 such that t + 1 ∈ A3 − (w − 1)
Proof of Claim 6. Suppose there is such t. Let A0 = χt,t+1 (χx,x+2 (A)). We have A0 ≡ A, w − 1 in the
low set and w in the high set of A0 , contradicting Observation 2.6. (Note that the application of χx,x+2
may ruin the assumption of Claim 2, but this assumption is not needed in Observation 2.6).

Proof of Claim 7. From Claim 4 we know that {z1 , z1 + 1, . . . , x, x + 1} ⊂ A1 ∪ A2 ∪ A3 . We need to

5     Discussion
In the case k = 2 there is yet another simple  Pp1 proof:
    Suppose p1 + p2 = n, p1 ⩽ p2 and i=1                  n − i + 1 ⩾ sn,2 (Condition 2). Assume we have n balls
labeled 1, . . . , n, respectively and two ball boxes: a red box with room for exactly p1 balls and a blue
box with room for exactly p2 balls. Let Sr be the sum of the labels on the balls located inside the red
box and Sb the sum of the labels on the balls located inside the blue box. We want to show that we can
distribute the balls among the two boxes so that Sr = Sb . If we put all p1 balls with the highest labels
in the red box and all the p2 balls with the lowest labels in the blue box, then clearly Sr ⩾ sn,2 , by (2).
We call this arrangement Arrangement 1. If we put all p1 balls with the lowest labels in the red box and
all the p2 balls with the highest labels in the blue box, then clearly Sb ⩾ sn,2 , since p2 ⩾ p1 . We call this
arrangement Arrangement 2. We shall show that we can switch from Arrangement 1 to Arrangement 2
by a sequence of steps, where in each step we exchange two balls labeled with adjacent numbers between
the two boxes. Since in each step Sr and Sb change by 1, there must be a stage in which Sr = Sb .
    We start with the blue box containing the balls {1, 2, . . . , p2 } and the red box containing the balls
{p2 +1, p2 +2, . . . , n} (Arrangement 1). The notation a ↔ b will describe a move in which the balls labeled
a and b are exchanged. The first sequence of moves is p2 ↔ p2 + 1, p2 − 1 ↔ p2 , . . ., 1 ↔ 2. This results
in the blue box containing the balls {2, 3, . . . , p2 + 1} and the red box containing {1, p2 + 2, p2 + 3, . . . , n}.
We proceed by exchanging p2 + 1 ↔ p2 + 2 and on to the next sequence of exchanges which results in
{3, 4, . . . , p2 + 2} and {1, 2, p2 + 3, p2 + 4, . . . , n}, respectively. Eventually we arrive at Arrangement 2.
    It might be possible to generalize the continuity approach used for k = 2 to higher k’s by using k
boxes and applying a higher dimensional continuity technique, such as Sperner’s theorem. The problem
is to define the right division into (k − 1)-dimensional simplices.
