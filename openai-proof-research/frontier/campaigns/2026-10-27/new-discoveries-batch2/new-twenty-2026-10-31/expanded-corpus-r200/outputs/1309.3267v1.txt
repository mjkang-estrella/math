                                                  Abstract. We prove that the set of quadratic growths attainable by integer-
                                                  valued superharmonic functions on the lattice Z2 has the structure of an Apol-
                                                  lonian circle packing, affirming a conjecture posed by the authors in [7], and
                                                  completely characterizing the PDE which determines the continuum scaling
                                                  limit of the Abelian sandpile on the lattice Z2 .

   Our proof of Theorem 1.2 is a recursive construction which lifts the recursive
structure of Apollonian circle packings. Identifying a circle with center x ∈ R2 and
radius 1c ∈ R with its curvature coordinates (c, cx1 , cx2 ) (some care must be taken
in the case of lines), the Soddy circles C0 and C4 of a pairwise tangent triple of
circles C1 , C2 , C3 satisfy the linear equality
(1.5)                       C0 + C4 = 2(C1 + C2 + C3 ).
This is a consequence of the Extended Descartes Theorem of Lagarias, Mallows,
and Wilks [6], and can be used, for example, to prove that every circle in B has
6            LIONEL LEVINE, WESLEY PEGDEN, AND CHARLES K. SMART


integral curvature coordinates. Pairwise tangent circles C1 , C2 , C3 , C4 constitute a
Descartes quadruple, and, under permutation of indices, (1.5) gives four different
ways of producing new Descartes quadruples sharing three circles in common with
the original quadruple. These four transformations correspond to the four gener-
ators of the Apollonian group of Graham, Lagarias, Mallows, Wilks, and Yan [5],
which has a natural action on the Descartes quadruples of a packing. Our proof
works by explicitly determining the action of the same Apollonian group on the set
of maximal superharmonic representatives, by giving an operation on our family of
integer superharmonic representatives analogous to the operation (1.5) for circles.
In particular, referring to Figure 1.3, the example tile T0 is seen to decompose as
2 copies each of T1 , T2 , T3 , with the two copies of T1 overlapping on a copy of T4 .
(In terms of area, at least, we immediately notice a direct analogy with (1.5).) The
essence of our proof is a precise understanding and careful use of this kind of tile
decomposition, at the level of integer superharmonic representatives.
   Our interest in integer superharmonic matrices arose in our study of the Abelian
sandpile model of Bak, Tang, and Wiesenfeld [1], a simple deterministic diffusion
process on the integer lattice that generates striking fractal configurations. As was
shown by the second two authors [11], the Abelian sandpile model on Z2 has a
continuum scaling limit that is determined by the set of integer superharmonic
matrices; indeed, in [7], the present authors used Theorem 1.1 (there, as a con-
jecture) to prove results about the scaling limit of the classical sandpile process
on the integer lattice. Additionally, the algorithm described above to find inte-
ger superharmonic representatives consists of the sandpile dynamics applied to the
Laplacian ∆h and tracked by the changes in h; and, empirically, the finite-stage
results of many natural Abelian Sandpile processes on the square lattice appear to
include patches of integer superharmonic representatives of matrices AC (C ∈ B).
In particular, periodic patterns such as those in Figure 1.2 make frequent appear-
ances, and the patterns have attracted much attention [2, 9, 10, 12]. Of particular
interest are the thesis of Paoletti [10] and the work of Caracciolo, Paoletti, and
Sportiello [2]; they give an experimental protocol that produces 2-dimensional pe-
riodic “backgrounds” and 1-dimensional periodic “strings.” The essential idea is
that, by performing certain sandpile operations on a background, one generates
defects, or strings, whose components can then be used to build new backgrounds.
While their procedure does not make any explicit reference to Apollonian circle
packings, quadratic forms, or tile decomposition, we believe that the 2-dimensional
backgrounds they obtain are precisely the Laplacians of the odometers gC .

                              3. Circles and Lattices
   When defining odometers for the Ford and Diamond circles above, we extended
a finite amount initial data to all of Z2 via periodicity conditions of the form
                              g(x + v) = g(x) + a · x + k,
where v, a ∈ Z2 and k ∈ Z. In each of Lemma 2.1 and Lemma 2.4, three pairs
of vectors (vi , ai ) appear in such conditions, and these vectors satisfy a number of
suggestive properties. In particular, {v1 , v2 , v3 } form a superbasis: they sum to zero
and generate a primitive lattice.
   In this section, we construct these vectors from the associated circles in B in a
way that allows us to recover the correct periodicity conditions for the general case.
Our calculations largely follow those of Stange [13], who, motivated by data on our
lattices LC for C ∈ B and Conway’s association of lattices to quadratic forms [3],
studied ways to associate lattices to circles in an Apollonian circle packing.
   We say a triple of circles is pairwise tangent if each pair is tangent with a distinct
point of tangency. In particular, although we consider parallel lines to be tangent
at ∞, no three lines form a pairwise tangent triple. A Descartes quadruple of circles
is a list of 4 circles such that any three of the four circles form a pairwise-tangent
triple; as any pairwise tangent triple of circles has exactly two Soddy circles, any
pairwise tangent triple of circles can likewise be completed to exactly two Descartes
quadruples.
   We call a Descartes quadruple (C0 , C1 , C2 , C3 ) ∈ B 4 proper if the curvatures
satisfy c0 > max{c1 , c2 , c3 } and points of tangency between C0 and C1 , C2 , C3 are
are clockwise around C0 , or if
      C0 = (1, 1 + 2z + 2i) and (Ci , Cj , Ck ) = ( (1, 1 + 2z), (0, 1), (0, −1) )
for z ∈ Z[i] and (i, j, k) a rotation of (1, 2, 3). We define P ⊆ B 4 to be the set
of proper Descartes quadruples. Note that if (C0 , C1 , C2 , C3 ) ∈ P is a proper
Descartes quadruple, then so is the parent rotation (C0 , C2 , C3 , C1 ) and the succes-
sor quadruple (C, C0 , C2 , C3 ) for C = (2(C0 + C2 + C3 ) − C1 ). Finally, note that
up to parent rotations, a circle C0 ∈ B which is not a line uniquely determines a
Descartes quadruple (C0 , C1 , C2 , C3 ).
   For the rest of this paper, we will simplify notation and computations greatly
by making use of complex numbers. We identify C with R2 and Z[i] with Z2 .
In particular, we will be choosing vectors in, and constructing sublattices of, Z[i].
Curvature coordinates are made complex by writing C = (c, cz) ∈ R × C for a
circle with radius 1c and center z ∈ C. As noted in the introduction, a pairwise-
tangent triple (C1 , C2 , C3 ) is related linearly to its Soddy circles C0 , C4 in curvature
coordinates by
