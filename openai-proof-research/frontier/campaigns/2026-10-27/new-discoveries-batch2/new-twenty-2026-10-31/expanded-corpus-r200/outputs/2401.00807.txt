                                                                                                   1. Introduction
                                             Let G = (V, E) be a finite, simple, undirected graph of order n. Denoting [n] = {1, 2, .P    . . , n}, a distance
                                          magic labeling of G [8] (or Σ-labeling [4]) is a bijection f : V → [n] such that for all x ∈ V , y∈N (x) f (y) = c
                                          for some constant c. The notion of distance magic labeling has been extensively studied for many families of
                                          graphs (see [6], [1], [3] for surveys). The following problem is equivalent to determining whether a complete
                                          multipartite graph [8], and other types of graphs [2], has a distance magic labeling:
                                          Problem 1.1 P        ([2], Problem 6.3). Let n, k and p1 ≤ p2 ≤ · · · ≤ pk be positive integers such that p1 +· · ·+pk = n
                                                                  n
                                          and k divides i=1 i. When is it possible to find a partition of the set [n] into k subsets of respective sizes
                                          p1 , . . . , pk , such that the element sum in each subset is equal?
                                             Anholcer, Cichacz and Peterin [2] observed that a necessary condition for such a partition to exist is that:

                                             It is known that this condition is sufficient whenever k = 2, 3 ([4] and [8]) and k = 4 [7]. Consequently,
                                          it was conjectured in [8] and [7] that this holds in general. In this work, we used computing to refute this
                                          conjecture and to find infinite families of counter-examples.
                                             The outline of the paper is as follows: In Section 2 we introduce definitions and notation; in Section 3
                                          we describe the algorithms we used to find a partition of [n] as described in problem 1.1, given n, k and
                                          p1 ≤ p2 ≤ · · · ≤ pk ; in Section 4 we introduce two criteria for identifying cases where such a partition does
                                          not exist (and thus, refuting the above-mentioned conjecture); in Section 5 we describe the computational
                                          results of running the algorithms and implementing the criteria on all relevant partitions for n ≤ 200; In
                                          Section 6 we describe some infinite families of counter-examples; In Section 7 we point at possible directions
                                          for future research and introduce a few open questions.

                                    3. Constructing balanced partitions
   In this section, we describe our algorithms for searching a balanced partition of [n] implementing a given
SSC partition in APn,k . This search enabled us to find the partitions in Example 2.9 and many others.
   The problem is to find a practical computational procedure for checking if a given SSC partition P ∈ APn,k
is equitable. Since a brute force search over all possible partitions of [n] implementing P is not practical, we
seek some heuristics for constructing such a balanced partition, if it exists. The algorithms we consider have
two main stages:
    (1) Generating an initial partition A implementing P.
    (2) Reducing the value of d(A) by repeatedly exchanging elements among the parts of A, aiming to
         eventually attain d(A) = 0.

3.1. Initialization methods. For stage (1) we can either use a general-purpose initialization method (meth-
ods (a)-(c) below) or an initialization method specifically designed to obtain a low value of d(A) (method
(d)).
   (a) Random placement: The sets A1 , A2 , . . . , Ak are a uniform random partition of [n] with |Ai | = pi .
   (b) Whole sets placement:
          The numbers n, n − 1, . . . , 2, 1 are placed in the sets A1 , A2 , . . . , Ak , in this order, so that numbers
       are not placed in Aj before A1 , . . . , Aj−1 are filled to their allotted sizes p1 , . . . , pj−1 . For example,
       for the partition P = {2, 3, 4} the initial partition of [9] will be {{9, 8}, {7, 6, 5}, {4, 3, 2, 1}}.
   (c) Round-robin placement:
          The numbers n, n − 1, . . . , 2, 1 are placed in round-robin order, starting from A1 down to Ak and
       then back to A1 and so forth, placing elements in sets that are not yet full. For example, for the
       partition P = {2, 3, 4} the initial partition of [9] will be {{9, 6}, {8, 5, 3}, {7, 4, 2, 1}}.
   (d) Adaptive Round Robin placement algorithm:
          An approach based on the well-known Weighted Fair Queuing (WFQ) scheduling algorithm ([9]
       chapter 5). Starting from n and going down to 1, we assign each number to the set that is most
       ‘in need’, where the measure for ’need’ is the average number needed to complete the sum of the
       elements in the set to sn,k . More precisely, for a set Aj this is the amount needed to reach sn,k
       divided by the number of slots still available in Aj . If at some stage Aj has already been assigned
       nj < pj elements, whose sum is sj , then the ’need’ of Aj is

(3.1)                                     n(Aj ) = (sn,k − sj )/(pj − nj ).

        Thus, the set that gets the next number is the one with the maximum need.
           Note that when referring to a set Aj we are using the term ‘set’ loosely, not as a collection of some
        fixed elements, but rather as a collection of a fixed number of slots in which we can place elements
        (an element in each slot). Initially, these slots are empty and we fill them, one-by-one in the process.
           As done in the WFQ algorithm, the procedure described above can be improved upon. So, instead
        of calculating the current ’need’ we calculate the resulting need if the next number is assigned to the
        set. So, if the next number to be placed is x and Aj , nj and sj are as above, then we revise (3.1) to:


(3.2)                               n(Aj , x) = (sn,k − sj − x)/(pj − nj − 1),

        where in the case that pj = nj + 1 we set the denominator of (3.2) to 1, to avoid division by zero.

Definition 3.3. Let A be a partition of [n] and a, b ∈ [n]. We say that χa,b is an improving exchange if
d(χa,b (A)) < d(A) and a neutral exchange if d(χa,b (A)) = d(A).
   Given the initial partition A, as described in the previous subsection, we aim to reduce the value of
d(A), possibly down to zero, by performing improving exchanges. So, we need to find pairs a, b so that χa,b
is improving, that is, b − a < S(Aj ) − S(Ai ), by Observation 3.2. For convenience, we limit ourselves to
transpositions. This approach is supported by the fact that any exchange is the composition of transpositions,
as it can be shown, that similarly to the case of symmetry groups, for any a < b ∈ [n],
                      χa,b = χa,a+1 χa+1,a+2 · · · χb−2,b−1 χb−1,b χb−2,b−1 · · · χa+1,a+2 χa,a+1 .
Note that this does not mean that any improving exchange is the composition of improving transpositions,
so, we cannot guarantee that any possible improving exchange can be achieved by a series of improving
transpositions, but in practice, this approach seems to work.
   We shall describe a heuristic that applies transpositions in order to find a balanced partition implementing
a given equitable ascending partition. The heuristic contains three types of steps:
    (1) Improving step: perform improving transpositions as long as possible.
    (2) Neutral step: If after step (1) still d(A) > 0 we perform a neutral transposition and go back to step
         (1)
    (3) Perturbation step: If d(A) > 0 and there are no improving or new neutral transpositions to be made,
         or after performing a fixed number of rounds of steps (1) and (2) still d(A) > 0, we perturb the
         system by performing a fixed number of random exchanges and go back to step (1).
   The algorithm stops once we obtain d(A) = 0 or after a fixed number of rounds of step (3). In the latter
case, the equitability status of the partition is unresolved.

                                      4. Criteria for non-equitability
    Verifying whether an ascending partition P is equitable by trying to construct a balanced partition imple-
menting P is very time-consuming and is not guaranteed to produce the correct answer if P is not equitable.
As will be detailed in Section 5, running the software for all SSC ascending partitions for n ≤ 200, left some
ascending partitions undecided, namely, it failed to find a corresponding balanced partition of [n]. Individ-
ually verifying that each of these partitions is non-equitable, as illustrated in Examples 2.9, is not practical,
so instead we generalized that idea into a low-complexity criterion. In this section, we introduce two such
criteria that suffice to detect all non-equitable SSC ascending partitions for n ≤ 200, where each one of them
is not sufficient on its own. There is no guarantee that these two criteria suffice for partitions with n > 200.
    The two criteria for identifying non-equitable partitions are restricted to P = [2e , pf , . . .] ∈ APn,k with
e, f > 0 and p ≥ 3, for which we introduce the following notation:

                                          5. Experimental results
   Our experiments included going exhaustively over all ascending partitions APn,k for n = 1, . . . , 200 and
all legal values of k where p1 ≥ 2. Analysis of the case p1 = 1 is completely understood and is discussed
elsewhere (see, for example, [7]). For each SSC partition P, we ran the algorithms described in Section 3 to
search for a balanced partition implementing the partition P, and in the case that such an assignment was
not found, we checked if it is non-equitable by one of the two criteria described in Section 4. This enabled
us to exhaustively classify all P ∈ APn,k for n ≤ 200 as one of the following:


    (1) Non-SSC (and thus, non-equitable)
    (2) Equitable, witnessed by some implementing assignment
    (3) Non-equitable SSC, where criteria 1 or 2 or both hold


Although smallest in size, the third category is the most intriguing class, as it contains the counterexamples
refuting the conjecture made in [8] and [7], that SSC implies equitability. All algorithms where implemented
in C++ and ran on a Linux workstation1. The total run time of the experiment was 59 hours for processing
all partitions for n ≤ 200. In Figure 1 one can see the number of partitions in each of the above three

Remark 6.5. The values of e and f in Theorem 6.1 were chosen so that e is maximal for h > 0, and f is
minimal so that f > h. This was sufficient for showing that each one of the families APn,k mentioned in
Theorem 6.1 contains non-equitable SSC partitions. As the results of the computation in Section 5 indicate,
if we allow smaller values of e (which results in larger h and thus, larger f ) and f larger than h + 1, while
keeping the other constraints intact, we may obtain more such partitions.

                                                   7. Conclusion
   The computerized search work described in Section 3 was initiated aiming to verify the conjecture that
every SSC ascending partition is equitable and possibly suggest a method for proving it. However, the search
uncovered many counter-examples, suggesting new possible lines of research.
   One line of research is trying to enumerate families of non-equitable SSC partitions. We have seen that the
classes AP3k,k and AP3k−1,k contain infinitely many non-equitable SSC members. This is also true for the
classes AP 38 k,k , AP 83 k−1,k , AP 25 k,k , and AP 25 k−1,k . So we could ask whether there is a rule about the relation
between n and k that allows the class APn,k (when non-empty) to contain non-equitable SSC partitions. We
can restrict to the case of a linear relation and ask:
Question 7.1. For which values of α and β does APαk+β,k contain non-equitable SSC partitions for infinitely
many values of k?
  We have seen that it is easy to create constraints on equitability when the partition contains pairs, but
can we do the same when the smallest part is larger than 2? in other words,
14       AN INFINITE FAMILY OF COUNTEREXAMPLES TO A CONJECTURE ON DISTANCE MAGIC LABELING




Question 7.2. Are there non-equitable SSC ascending partitions with p1 > 2?
   A positive answer to this question requires the uncovering of new families of non-equitable SSC partitions,
possibly by expanding the search to much larger values of n.
   Now, we go back to the original question posed in Problem 1.1. The fact that the necessary condition
in (1.1) is not always sufficient does not mean that there are no other sufficient conditions for equitability.
However, the different families of non-equitable SSC ascending partitions found so far and the two criteria
for non-equitability introduced in Section 4 suggest that there might be many more families (as implied by
Question 7.1) and criteria that may be hard to enumerate. This gives rise to the following questions:
Question 7.3. Is the decision problem of whether a given partition is equitable NP-complete?
     or alternatively,
Question 7.4. Is there an efficient (polynomial) procedure for deciding whether a given partition is equitable?
  The question is basically about the complexity and richness of the space of non-equitable SSC partitions.
The problem has some resemblance to other NP-complete problems involving sums of subsets, such as the
subset sum problem, the partition problem and the 3-partition problem.
  Another possible direction of research is to identify special cases in which the conjecture holds. One
approach may be to fix k. We know that the conjecture is true for k ≤ 4 [8, 4, 7], but does it hold for
any fixed k (and sufficiently large n)? Another approach is to limit the number of distinct values in P. For
example, Cichacz and Gőrlich [5] solved the case where there are two distinct values, each appearing the
same number of times. We suggest a new case, which we believe is equitable as well:
