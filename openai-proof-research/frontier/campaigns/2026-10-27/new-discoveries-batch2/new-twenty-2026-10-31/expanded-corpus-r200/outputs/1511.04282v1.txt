                                                           A matching queue is described via a graph G together with a
                                                       matching policy. Speciﬁcally, to each node in the graph there is a
                                                       corresponding arrival process of items which can either be queued,
                                                       or matched with queued items in neighboring nodes. The match-

                                              1. Introduction. We consider a continuous-time matching queueing
                                           system in which items of different classes arrive one by one, and depart
                                           in pairs. Specifically, we assume that any item is either matched with ex-
                                           actly one other item immediately upon arrival, and both items leave the
                                           system, or is stored in a buffer until it is matched. Since matchings are pair-
                                           wise, such a matching model can be represented via an undirected graph,
                                           in which each node represents a class of arrivals, and an edge between two
                                           nodes represents that items of the two corresponding classes can be matched
                                           together. A matching policy describes the matching rule whenever more than
                                           one matching is possible for an incoming item.
                                              The model just described is closely related to the discrete-time stochastic
                                           matching model introduced in [23], in which items enter the system at discrete
                                           time points, and their class is drawn upon arrival from a given probability
                                           distribution on the set of classes. We elaborate below.
                                           Matching models in the literature. Matching queueing models arise directly
                                           in several applications (e.g., organ transplantation [13] and public-housing
                                           assignments [30]) and can also be employed as relaxations for complex queue-
                                           ing and inventory systems. We refer to [1, 10, 16] for elaborate discussions
                                           on existing applications.

   The term matching queues was introduced in [16]. In that reference, items
can be matched by groups of size two or more, and the goal is to minimize
finite-horizon cumulative holding costs. Moreover, the controller can keep
matchable items in storage for more “profitable” future matches. A myopic,
discrete-review control is shown to be asymptotically optimal, as the arrival
rate grows large. Thus, both the model and the objectives of [16] are different
than ours here, since we consider pairwise matchings, and are concerned with
stability properties.
   The stability of matching models operating under the first-come first-
served (FCFS) policy was studied for several particular graphs in [10], as-
suming that both the arrivals and the departures occur by pairs. (If items
arrive one by one, the model can never be stable, as will become clear below.)
A discrete-time Markov chain representation was employed in combination
with Lyapunov techniques, to study the stability (ergodicity) of certain mod-
els having bipartite graphs. An important (and expected) observation from
this latter reference is that characterizing the stability region, namely, the
law of the arrival process under which the system is stable, is nontrivial even
for models with relatively simple graphs.
   The matching model in [10] was further analyzed in [9]. An alternative
Markov representation was introduced, leading to a more complete picture
of the stability problem. General sufficient and necessary conditions for sta-
bility of the underlying Markov chain were given, together with properties
of several matching policies. In particular, the stability of any model apply-
ing the ’match the longest’ policy (under which an item that has more than
one matching option upon arrival is matched with an item from the longest
queue) was proved, assuming the necessary condition for stability holds.
   The necessary condition for stability in [9] wa employed in [1] to prove the
existence of unique matching rates for models satisfying a certain “complete
resource pooling” condition. The models considered in [1] are again bipar-
tite and operate in the FCFS matching policy. Interestingly, the stationary
distribution of the Markov chain in [1] is shown to have a product form.
   In [2] a continuous-time model is considered for a bipartite matching sys-
tem operating under the FCFS-ALIS (Assign Longest-Idle Server) policy.
In this paper, Markovian service system (i.e., service times are considered)
with skill-based routing, are modeled as matching queues, and the stationary
distribution, when it exists, is shown to have a product form. Fluid limits
are employed in the overloaded case to prove the existence of a local steady
state.
   The graphs in [1, 2, 9, 10] are all bipartite. Therefore, to make the question
of stability nontrivial, items are assumed to arrive in pairs, as was mentioned
                    INSTABILITY OF MATCHING QUEUES                            3

above. This assumption is dropped in [23], which introduces the aforemen-
tioned (discrete-time) stochastic matching model with general graph topol-
ogy. In [23], a thorough study of the structure of the stability region of the
model is proposed, partially relying on the results in [9]. A natural necessary
condition, named Ncond, for the stability of any such stochastic matching
model is introduced, implying, in particular, that no model can be stable if
the matching graph is a tree, or more generally, a bipartite graph. (Hence
the need for the assumption that arrivals occur in pairs in the papers cited
above.) In addition, a study of a particular simple model in [23] shows that
Ncond is not sufficient in general. It is conjectured that, except for a certain
class of graphs, namely, the non-bipartite separable ones - see Definition 1
below - one can find an arrival process satisfying Ncond, together with a
matching policy, under which the system is unstable. The Lyapunov-stability
techniques that were employed in [23] render the proof of this conjecture im-
practical in the discrete settings.
   As is easily seen, and will be shown in Theorem 2 below, the stability re-
gion of a discrete-time stochastic model can be studied by embedding it in an
appropriate continuous-time model. Thus, the continuous-time counterparts
of the results in [23] hold for our matching queues, and vice versa. The ad-
vantage of the continuous time setting is that powerful fluid-limit techniques
can be employed, which greatly facilitate stability analysis.
Stability of Stochastic Networks via Fluid Limits. The necessary condition
Ncond for stability, defined in (5) below, can be thought of as an analogue
to the usual traffic condition of standard queueing networks, requiring that
the long-term rate of arrivals to each service station be less than the long-run
output rate at that station. Therefore, our work relates to the literature on
(in)stability of subcritical stochastic networks, which we briefly review.
   Consider a stochastic queueing network with d ≥ 1 service stations and
K ≥ 1 classes. Let aki denote the (long-run) arrival rate of class-k jobs into
service station i, and let mki denote the mean service time of class-k jobs in
this station, 1 ≤ k ≤ K. Then the system is subcritical if

It is well-known that Condition (1) is not sufficient to ensure stability of
stochastic networks in general. The first examples of this fact are the deter-
ministic Lu-Kumar network [21] (and its stochastic counterpart; §3 in [8]),
and the Rybko-Stolyar network [29], both of which consider static priority
service policies. A subcritical, multiclass, two-station network, having a Pois-
son arrival process and exponential service times in both service stations for

all classes, was shown to be unstable under the FIFO discipline in [6]. See
[8] for an elaborate discussion, including a comprehensive literature review
of the subject.
   The most general method to showing that a given stochastic queueing
network is stable employs fluid limits. Specifically, Dai [11] showed that,
under general regularity conditions, if all the (subsequential) fluid limits of
the queues, for all possible initial conditions, converge to 0 in finite time,
then the system is stable. However, since we are interested in demonstrating
instability of matching queues, a unique characterization of the fluid limit is
required. In particular, we must prove a functional weak-law of large numbers
(FWLLN), and show that the unique fluid limit may not converge to the
origin.
   An interesting feature of the fluid limits we obtain is that their dynamics
are determined by the stationary distribution of a “fast” continuous-time
Markov chain (CTMC). Specifically, if the fluid queue associated with one
of the nodes is positive, then the relevant time scale for this queue is slower
than the time scale for the queues that are empty. In the limit, the effect of
the “fast” (i.e., null) queues on the evolution of the positive fluid queues is
averaged-out instantaneously, a phenomena known as a stochastic averaging
principle (AP) in the literature. See [25, 26] and the references therein, as
well as [22, 35] for recent examples of fast averaging in queueing networks.

   3.1. Strategy of the Proof of Theorem 3. To prove Theorem 3, we will
show that, for any connected and non-bipartite graph G which is not separa-
ble, it is possible to find an admissible matching policy and a λ ∈ Ncondc (G),
such that (G, λ, Φ)c is unstable. The proof of this claim hinges on the fol-
lowing fact, which will be proved in Section 6.1 (statement (i) in Lemma
4) : any connected, non-bipartite and non-separable graph induces a pendant
graph or an odd cycle of size 5 or more.
   Due to this result, we adopt the following strategy. We start by considering
the set whose elements are the pendant graph and all the odd cycles of
size 5 or more, and construct an instable model for any graph in this set
under Ncond, the instability of which is shown using fluid-limit arguments.
The fluid limits are developed in Section 4 below. Finally, we show a “non-
chaoticity” property (Section 6.2), implying that the instability of a matching
queue on a graph Ğ induced in a graph G leads to the instability of a related
matching queue on G.

   8. Implications to Matching Algorithms on Random Graphs.
Our results also have interesting graph-related implications, which we now
briefly describe. In graph theory, a matching on a graph G (not to be con-
fused with the matching of items discussed thus far) is a subgraph of G in
which each node has exactly one neighbor. The matching is said to be perfect
if G and G have the same set of nodes.
   A current active line of literature is devoted to the construction of match-
ings on random graphs (see e.g. [15] and the references therein), i.e. on graphs
in which the existence of edges between the nodes is random. Building on the
present model, we propose an original random graph model, together with a
construction of a matching on that random graph.
   Consider the following procedure. We start with a given (deterministic)
graph G = (V, E) of size p. Consider a random graph GN with N nodes,
and the set of nodes is partitioned into p subsets, which we call types of
nodes. We identify the set of types of nodes of GN with V. The edges of
GN are determined according to the following rule: two nodes u and v of
GN , of respective types i and j, are neighbors in GN if and only if i−j in
G. The types of the nodes of GN are drawn at random, according to a given
probability measure on V.
   A possible construction of GN , together with a matching on GN , can be
achieved by the following procedure. Fix a matching queue (G, λ, Φ)c .
  (i) Initially, all N nodes in GN are undifferentiated (i.e. the types of the
      nodes are not decided yet), unmatched and there are no edges.
 (ii) At each point of a Poisson process, say of Ni , take a node u uniformly at
      random among all undifferentiated ones, assign to u the corresponding
      type (type i in this case). We then say that u is differentiated, and of
      type i. Then, create an edge in GN between u and all the differentiated
      nodes of any type j such that i−j in G.
(iii) If the set of unmatched neighbors of u is nonempty, apply Φ to select
      a unique node v in the latter set. We call v the match of u, and say
      that both u and v are matched nodes.
(iv) As long as there remain undifferentiated nodes in GN , reiterate steps
      (ii) and (iii).
At the end of the procedure, all the nodes are differentiated. The various
nodes may be matched or not, and the matching we have constructed on
GN is the subgraph consisting in the matched nodes in GN and the edges
between matched couples. Moreover, if we now let N increase to infinity
then, by the strong law of large numbers, w.p.1 GN is p-partite (all types of
nodes are represented in GN , and there is no edge between any two nodes
42                             MOYAL AND PERRY

of the same type), and the proportions of nodes of the various types tend to
the measure µλ defined by (6).
   As it relies on an exploration of the nodes and the progressive attachment
of explored ones to the graph, the latter construction is related to the pro-
cedure of uniform random pairing; see [34]. This latter procedure leads to
the so-called configuration model introduced by Bollobas [5] (see also e.g.
[27]), generating a realization of a random graph where the degree, i.e., the
number of neighbors of each node, is fixed beforehand.

   Hence, whenever G is non-bipartite, there exist an intensity λ ∈ Ncondc (G)
and a matching policy Φ leading to an asymptotic perfect matching. Nev-
ertheless, it follows from (ii) that, except for the case of separable graphs

policy, namely, a matching policy leading to an imperfect matching on GN
for a large enough N .

   9. Summary and Future Research. In this paper we proved that
matching queues on non-separable and non-bipartite graphs satisfying Ncond
need not be stable. Our proof employed a fluid-limit whose characterization
builds on estimating the stationary distribution of a related fast-time-scale
CTMC.
There are many directions for future research. We specify three which we are
currently investigating.
   Identifying bottlenecks. The fluid limit may be used to construct a
procedure determining the “bottlenecks” (namely, unstable) nodes of general
unstable matching queues. Moreover, when the stationary distribution of the
fast-time-scale CTMC can be computed explicitly, the fluid limit provides
the exact rate of increase of the queues corresponding to the unstable nodes.
   Non-Graphical Models. In our model, items depart the system by
pairs. However, in many applications (e.g., manufacturing and assemble-to-
order systems) matchings can occur in groups that are larger than 2 (as, for
example, in [16]). Thus, it remains to establish an analogue to Ncond when
the compatibility between items cannot be represented by a graph.
   Applications to Matchings on Graphs. The observation following
Corollary 6 is reminiscent of the difficulty of constructing a perfect matching
for large graphs using a greedy algorithm. This opens two possible directions
for future investigations: (i) connect the condition for existence of a perfect
matching in Corollary 6, to Tutte’s condition [31] for deterministic graphs,
and (ii) investigate a possible generalization of the algorithm in Section 8 to
the case where each node is not connected to all of the nodes of its compatible
types but, for example, its connectivity follows a given degree distribution.
