1. Introduction

1.1. Simplified de Bruijn graph and its subgraphs

  Let A(k) = {0, 1, . . . , k − 1} be a k-letter alphabet. For any integers n ≥ 2 and m ≥ 0, let Anm (k) be the following set of
words over A(k):

         Anm (k) = {x1 x2 . . . xn : xi ∈ A(k) and |xi − xi+1 | ≥ m}.

In particular, An0 (k) is the set of all words over A(k) of length n.
   A de Bruijn graph B(n, k) is a digraph with vertex set An0 (k) having an arc from x1 x2 . . . xn to y1 y2 . . . yn if and only if
xi+1 = yi for all i ∈ {1, 2, . . . , n − 1}. De Bruijn graphs are a useful tool in combinatorics on words [8] and they find
applications in several areas outside of mathematics, for example, in bioinformatics [10].
   The notion of the simplified de Bruijn graph is introduced in [9], and we believe it to be a very natural and potentially
useful tool in graph theory and its various applications. The simplified de Bruijn graph S(n, k) is the simple graph obtained
from B(n, k) by removing orientations and loops and replacing multiple edges between a pair of vertices by a single edge.
Denote by Sm (n, k) the induced subgraphs of S(n, k) with vertex set Anm (k). See Fig. 1 for examples of just introduced
objects.

                                                                                        1. Introduction

                                               Let G be a finite group written multiplicatively. A sequence over G is a finite multiset of
                                           elements of G. Such a sequence is called product-one when its terms can be ordered so that their
                                           product is the identity. The Gao constant E(G) is the least integer ℓ such that every sequence over
                                           G of length ℓ has a product-one subsequence of length |G|. The small Davenport constant d(G) is
                                           the maximal length of a sequence with no nonempty product-one subsequence.
                                               The Erdős–Ginzburg–Ziv theorem [12], proved in 1961, asserts that every sequence of 2n − 1
                                           integers has an n-term subsequence whose sum is divisible by n. The bound is sharp, as shown
                                           by the sequence consisting of n − 1 zeros and n − 1 ones. Consequently, E(Cn ) = 2n − 1, where
                                           Cn denotes the cyclic group of order n. Together with the study of the Davenport constant,
                                           this theorem forms one of the foundations of zero-sum theory. Although much of the early work
                                           concerned abelian groups, product-one problems provide a natural extension to arbitrary finite
                                           groups.
                                               In 1976, Olson [40] proved that E(G) ≤ 2|G| − 1 for every finite group. Subsequent work
                                           established sharper bounds for non-cyclic solvable groups. In 1984, Yuster and Peterson [55]
                                           obtained the bound 2|G| − 2, and Yuster [54] derived further improvements in 1988. Gao [16]
                                           proved the bound 11|G|/6 − 1 in 1996, and Gao and Li [18] subsequently strengthened it to
                                           7|G|/4 − 1 in 2010. The latter authors also proposed the following conjecture [18, Conjecture 3].
                                           Conjecture 1.1. Every finite non-cyclic group G satisfies E(G) ≤ 3|G|/2.

                                               In 2015, Han [27] proved that E(G) ≤ |G| + |G|p
                                                                                               + p − 2 for every finite non-cyclic nilpotent
                                           group, where p is the smallest prime divisor of |G|. This result establishes Conjecture 1.1 for
                                           nilpotent groups. He also proposed the following stronger conjecture [27, Conjecture 3.1].
                                           Conjecture 1.2. Every finite non-cyclic group G satisfies E(G) ≤ |G| + |G|
                                                                                                                   p
                                                                                                                      + p − 2, where p is the
                                           smallest prime divisor of |G|.

    Further cases of Conjecture 1.1 have since been established. In 2021, Gao, Li and Qu [19] estab-
lished it for non-cyclic groups of odd order. Recently, in 2026, Godara, Joshi, and Mazumdar [24]
verified it for non-cyclic groups of the form A ⋊−1 C2 , where A is a finite abelian p-group and
the nonidentity element of C2 acts on A by inversion. In 2026, Zhao and Wang [56] proved it for
non-cyclic groups whose orders are not divisible by four and characterized the equality case.
    The problem of determining d(G) for a finite abelian group G was proposed by Davenport in
1966, motivated by connections between d(G) + 1 and algebraic number theory. This problem has
received considerable attention (see [22, 29] for example). Nevertheless, exact values are known
only for certain classes of finite abelian groups. In 1996, Gao [15] proved that E(G) = d(G) + |G|
for every finite abelian group. For non-abelian groups, the Davenport constant also has close
connections with the Noether number (see [4–6, 28, 48] for further details). The extension of this
identity to non-abelian groups has also attracted considerable interest. In 2005, Zhuang and
Gao [57] established it for dihedral groups of order 2p where p ≥ 4001 is a prime, and proposed
the following conjecture [57, Conjecture 2].
Conjecture 1.3. Every finite group G satisfies E(G) = |G| + d(G).

    Subsequent research has devoted considerable attention to finite non-abelian groups containing
a cyclic subgroup of index two. In 2008, Gao and Lu [20] extended the preceding result to dihedral
groups of order 2n for all n ≥ 23. Extending their method, Bass [7] proved the conjecture for all
dihedral groups and dicyclic groups, as well as for non-abelian groups of order pq, where p and q
are primes. In 2023, Avelar, Brochero Martínez and Ribas [3] verified it for non-abelian semidirect
products C2 ⋉ Cn , apart from a single exceptional case, which was subsequently resolved by Oh,
Ribas, Zhao and Zhong [36]. Yang, Zhang and Feng [53] in 2024 and Ribas [46] in 2025 established
the conjecture for certain non-split metacyclic groups containing a cyclic subgroup of index two.
More recently, Qu, Gao and Li [42] proved it for every finite non-cyclic group containing a cyclic
subgroup whose index is the smallest prime divisor of the group order.
    For the small Davenport constant, Qu, Li and Teeuwsen [44] established the optimal upper
bound d(G) ≤ |G|/p − 1 for every non-cyclic group G containing no cyclic subgroup of index p,
where p is the smallest prime divisor of |G|. In this paper, we further investigate the Gao constant
E(G) for these groups and establish the following main result.

Proof strategy of Theorem 1.4. The proof mainly consists of the following two aspects.
   • Solvable groups. We first establish the bound for nilpotent groups. Theorem 4.2 then shows
     that a solvable counterexample of minimal order must fall into one of five configurations. The
     estimates ruling out these configurations are established in Sections 5–7 and summarized in
     Section 4.3.
   • Non-solvable groups. Proposition 3.2 reduces the problem to non-abelian simple groups. For
     these groups, Theorem 9.8 handles sequences of bounded height, while Lemma 10.1 provides
     a method for treating the remaining sequences. The subsequent arguments are organized
     according to the product constructions they employ, and a final proposition verifies that all
     relevant groups and element orders are covered.
   Section 2 collects the preliminary results, and Section 3 presents the reduction for non-solvable
groups. Sections 4–7 establish the result for solvable groups, while Sections 8–10 develop the
bounded-height argument and the common completion criteria. The treatment of sequences
containing a term of high multiplicity is organized into four parts: subgroup and compatible
extraction in Section 11, short product-one subsequences in Section 12, growth of conjugation
orbits in Section 13, and cyclic seeds in Section 14. Numerical applications of each method are
summarized in tables. Section 15 verifies that all relevant cases are covered and completes the
proof for simple groups. Finally, Section 16 combines these results with the argument for solvable
groups to complete the proof of Theorem 1.4.
