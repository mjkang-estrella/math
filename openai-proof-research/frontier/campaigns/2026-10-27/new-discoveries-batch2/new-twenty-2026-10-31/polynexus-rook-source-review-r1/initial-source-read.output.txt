## Exact semantics

The source M_n is the contiguous-single-diagonal submonoid, not the entire rook monoid R_n. Its nonzero matrices have entries x_ij=1 precisely when k<=i<=m and j=i+d, with 1-min(0,d)<=k<=m<=n-max(0,d). Equivalently a matrix is uniquely an ordered pair (I,J) of nonempty integer intervals in [n] of equal length, with the unique increasing translation I->J. This equivalence follows by I=[k,m], J=[k+d,m+d]; conversely d=min J-min I and k=min I,m=max I satisfy every printed inequality. Distinct pairs yield distinct matrices by their row and column supports.

The source counts ordered operand pairs (x,y) with xy nonzero, not distinct output matrices. Equation (53), the literal sextuple sum, makes this binding explicit. If 'products' instead means distinct outputs, the identity already gives every nonzero element as an output and the claimed polynomial is not that count. That alternative is a wording issue and earns no credit. Here the intended source binding is retained without changing the raw record.

For x=(I,J) and y=(K,L), matrix multiplication is nonzero iff J intersects K. Indeed a nonzero summand x_ij*y_jh requires j in J intersect K; conversely any such j gives i in I,h in L and a positive product entry. This also reproduces source Theorem 3: max(k,k'-d)<=min(m,m'-d). No drawing is needed for these algebraic definitions.

## Complete count

There are T_n=sum_{a=1}^n(n-a+1)^2=n(n+1)(2n+1)/6 nonzero operands. A proof of the square sum is induction: the displayed polynomial vanishes at 0 and its difference at consecutive integers is n^2.

Let D_n count zero pairs for which J lies strictly to the left of K. The opposite ordering contributes exactly D_n, by exchanging the lengths and the supports; every disjoint pair has exactly one ordering. Fix |J|=a, |K|=b with a,b>=1 and put t=n-a-b>=0. The numbers of unused positions before J, between J and K, and after K are three nonnegative integers summing to t. Thus there are binom(t+2,2) placements (bijection with two separators among t+2 positions). For each placement I can be any of n-a+1 intervals, and L can be any of n-b+1 intervals, independently. Consequently

D_n=sum_{a,b>=1,a+b<=n} binom(n-a-b+2,2)(n-a+1)(n-b+1).

This is an exact bijective reduction of the source's six summations, including the sign constraints on d,d'. Neither identity removal nor distinct-output quotienting is performed.

1 Introduction
For n = 2, 3, 4, . . ., the rook monoid Rn , also known as the symmetric inverse
semigroup IS n , consists of all partial injective transformations of {1, 2, . . . , n}.
Any such transformation can be represented as an n × n matrix whose entries
are 0 or 1, and with at most one 1 in every row and every column. If we think
of the matrix as an n × n chessboard with rooks placed at the nonzero matrix
entries, then no rook attacks any other rook; hence the name rook monoid,
coined in 2002 by Solomon [1, 2].
    This paper introduces a subsemigroup Sn of Rn and studies its properties
(note that the symbol Sn does not denote the permutation group). Our Sn
is also a subsemigroup of POI n , i.e., the set of all partial order-preserving
injective transformations [3–5], which is itself a subsemigroup of Rn . Besides
Sn , we discuss two closely related semigroups, namely the monoid Mn = Sn ∪
{1} (where 1 is the monoid identity, represented by the n × n identity matrix),
as well as a semigroup of countably infinite order to be denoted by S∞ .
    We refer to a number of well-known properties of Rn . They are summarized
in the lemma that follows.

12 Summary
In this paper, we defined a noncommutative, inverse monoid Mn ; showed that
the nonzero elements of Mn can be represented as hd, k, mi, where d, k, and m
are properly defined integers; and used this “triplet representation” to study
numerous properties of Mn . Both Mn and the set Sn = Mn \ {1} (where
1 is the monoid identity, corresponding to the n × n identity matrix) are
inverse subsemigroups of the well-known rook monoid Rn , also known as the
symmetric inverse semigroup. The monoid Mn consists of those elements of
                    Springer Nature 2021 LATEX template


                                   A subsemigroup of the rook monoid        25

Rn whose ones lie on a single diagonal and form an uninterrupted block (i.e.,
no zero lies between any two ones). Equivalently, the rook-n diagram of any
monoid element has the form shown in Figure 5.
    In Sections 3 and 4, we regarded Mn as a subsemigroup of a certain
semigroup S∞ of countably infinite order; this helped us find the triplet rep-
resentation of the product of two monoid elements (Theorem 3). Our results
on Mn include:
• A simple formula for powers (Corollary 2).
• A proof that any element is idempotent or nilpotent (Theorem 4). Proposi-
  tion 9 further gives the numbers of idempotents and nilpotents.
• A formula (in Theorem 4) for the index ` of any nonzero nilpotent in terms
  of d, k, and m.
• A proof that the jth root of any given nonzero element either does not exist;
  or does exist, is unique, and has the triplet representation given explicitly
  in Theorem 6.
• Green’s relations (Theorem 12), enabling a determination of all ideals
  (Theorem 13).
• An investigation of the (n-dependent) number of nonzero products in
  Sn \ {0}; this number is 50% of the total number of products when n = 2,
  and appears to be slightly larger than 50% for all other n (Conditional
  Proposition 1 and Figure 4).
• The use of rook-n diagrams to give graphical interpretations of many of our
  results (Section 11).
• An investigation of some subsemigroups of Mn (Section 9). Two of
  these are the familiar monogenic subsemigroups generated by the well-
  known backward and forward shift matrices (Proposition 16). For another
  subsemigroup—denoted by SU Tn and consisting of all strictly upper tri-
  angular matrices of Mn —we found a minimal generating set An , gave a
  (non-unique) explicit formula for any x ∈ SU Tn , and computed the rank of
  SU Tn (Theorem 17).
• The surprising result (Theorem 19) that, irrespective of n, two elements
  suffice to generate Sn .
• Section 9.4, finally, lists more subsemigroups that can be investigated along
  similar lines.

1 Introduction
The symmetric inverse semigroup IS n , also known as the rook monoid, consists
of the partial injective transformations of {1, 2, . . . , n} [1, 2]. Any element of
IS n can be represented as an n × n matrix whose entries are 0 or 1, with at
most one 1 in every row and every column.
    In a previous paper [3], we introduced a submonoid Mn of IS n and studied
its properties. The monoid Mn consists of the zero matrix together with those
matrices of IS n whose 1s lie on a single diagonal and form an uninterrupted
block (i.e., no 0 lies between any two 1s). Let d be the said diagonal (d =
−n + 1, . . . , n − 1, with d = 0 being the main diagonal), let k be the row of the
northwestern 1, and let m be the row of the southeastern 1. The study of [3] was
facilitated by representing the elements of Mn as triplets ⟨d, k, m⟩ ∈ Z3 (d, k,
and m are appropriately restricted), and developing a closed-form expression
representing the product of two elements.
    This short note is an extension that allows ⟨d, k, m⟩ ∈ R3 ; the restrictions
on the parameters d, k, m, as well as the product formula, remain unaltered.
We thus study a new monoid M n , of which the Mn of [3] is a submonoid. For
reasons of symmetry, we switch the order of the first two arguments and use
the notation ≺k, d, m≻ for an x ∈ M n , so that

                    x =≺k, d, m≻= ⟨d, k, m⟩,       k, d, m ∈ Z.                 (1)

of the notation of [3]. For example, we retain the symbol xT for the semigroup

and ideal means two-sided ideal. We use the traditional notations for Green’s
relations, associated equivalence classes, and principal ideals, as well as the
usual notations r(x) and d(x) [4, 5] for xxT and xT x, respectively. A jth root
of x ∈ M n is a y ∈ M n such that x = y j (j ∈ N).

Proof By (4), (8), and (47), B n is an inverse subsemigroup of M n . Therefore B n
inherits its natural partial order ≤ from M n . By (47) and Corollary 1, x ≤ y iff
x = y (x, y ∈ P n ), meaning that in P n = B n \ {0}, the ≤ reduces to an equality.
Equivalently [4], all idempotents of B n \ {0} are primitive.
    Now let I ⊆ B n be an ideal of B n . Assume I ̸= {0}, so that some nonzero x
belongs to I. Choose any y in B n . By Lemma 3, this y belongs to the principal ideal
B n xB n , so that B n ⊆ B n xB n . As B n xB n ⊆ I, we further have B n ⊆ I, so I = B n .
Therefore the only ideals of B n are {0} and B n itself, meaning that B n is 0-simple.
    Inverse, 0-simple semigroups with at least one primitive idempotent are Brandt
semigroups [4], completing our proof.                                                  □
