1 Introduction The symmetric inverse semigroup \(\mathcal{I}\mathcal{S}_n\) , also known as the rook monoid, consists of the partial injective transformations of \(\{1,2,\ldots ,n\}\) [ 4 , 12 ]. Any element of \(\mathcal{I}\mathcal{S}_n\) can be represented as an \(n\times n\) matrix whose entries are 0 or 1, with at most one 1 in every row and every column. In a previous paper [ 3 ], we introduced a submonoid \(M_n\) of \(\mathcal{I}\mathcal{S}_n\) and studied its properties. The monoid \(M_n\) consists of the zero matrix together with those matrices of \(\mathcal{I}\mathcal{S}_n\) whose 1s lie on a single diagonal and form an uninterrupted block (i.e., no 0 lies between any two 1s). Let d be the said diagonal ( \(d=-n+1,\ldots , n-1\) , with \(d=0\) being the main diagonal), let k be the row of the northwestern 1, and let m be the row of the southeastern 1. The study of [ 3 ] was facilitated by representing the elements of \(M_n\) as triplets \(\langle d,k,m\rangle \in {\mathbb {Z}}^3\) ( d , k , and m are appropriately restricted), and developing a closed-form expression representing the product of two elements. This note is an extension that allows \(\langle d,k,m\rangle \in {\mathbb {R}}^3\) ; the restrictions on the parameters d , k , m , as well as the product formula, remain unaltered. We thus study a new monoid \({\overline{M}}_n\) , of which the \(M_n\) of [ 3 ] is a submonoid. For reasons of symmetry, we switch the order of the first two arguments and use the notation \(\prec k,d, m\succ \) for an \(x\in {\overline{M}}_n\) , so that $$\begin{aligned} x=\prec k,d, m \succ =\langle d,k,m \rangle ,\quad k,d,m\in {\mathbb {Z}}. \end{aligned}$$ (1) To facilitate comparisons with “the integer case,” however, we maintain much of the notation of [ 3 ]. For example, we retain the symbol \(x^T\) for the semigroup inverse; the underlying reason is that inverting \(x\in M_n\) amounts to transposing the matrix represented by x . As in [ 3 ], \({\textbf{0}}\) and \({\textbf{1}}\) denote monoid zero and identity, and ideal means two-sided ideal. We use the traditional notations for Green’s relations, associated equivalence classes, and principal ideals, as well as the usual notations \({\textbf{r}}(x)\) and \({\textbf{d}}(x)\) [ 6 , 8 ] for \(xx^T\) and \(x^Tx\) , respectively. A j th root of \(x\in {\overline{M}}_n\) is a \(y\in {\overline{M}}_n\) such that \(x= y^j\) ( \(j \in {\mathbb {N}}\) ). 2 The inverse monoid \({\overline{M}}_n\) Let \(n\in {\mathbb {Z}}\) with \(n\ge 2\) . Our definition of \({\overline{M}}_n\) is $$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} {\overline{M}}_n=\{{\textbf{0}}\} \cup \{\prec k,&d, m \succ :\ k,d,m\in {\mathbb {R}};\\ {}&1-\min (0,d)\le k \le m\le n-\max (0,d)\}. \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$ (2) Note that the restrictions in ( 2 ) further imply $$\begin{aligned} -(n-1)\le d\le n-1\quad \textrm{and}\quad 1\le k\le m\le n. \end{aligned}$$ (3) As in [ 3 ], the formula for the product of two nonzero elements is $$\begin{aligned} \prec k, d, m \succ \prec k', d', m' \succ = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \prec k'', d'', m'' \succ ,\quad k'' \le m'',\\ \ {\textbf{0}},\quad k''> m'', \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$ (4) in which the parameters \(k''\) , \(d''\) , and \(m''\) are $$\begin{aligned} k''=\max (k,k'-d),\quad d''=d+d',\quad m''=\min (m,m'-d). \end{aligned}$$ (5) We can use the definitions ( 2 ), ( 4 ), and ( 5 ) to show that \({\overline{M}}_n\) is a monoid with \({\textbf{1}}=\prec 1, 0, n \succ \) . We can also verify a formula for powers: Lemma 1 For \(x=\prec k, d, m \succ \in {\overline{M}}_n\setminus \{{\textbf{0}}\}\) and \(j\in {\mathbb {N}}\) we have $$\begin{aligned} x^j={\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \prec k^{(j)}, d^{(j)}, m^{(j)}\succ ,\quad \text {if}\quad k^{(j)}\le m^{(j)}, \\ {\textbf{0}},\quad \text {if}\quad k^{(j)}>m^{(j)}, \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$ (6) where $$\begin{aligned} k^{(j)}=k-(j-1)\min (0,d),\quad d^{(j)}=jd,\quad m^{(j)}=m-(j-1)\max (0,d). \end{aligned}$$ (7) In particular, \(x^2=x\) iff \(d=0\) . Let us define $$\begin{aligned} x^T= {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} {\textbf{0}},\quad x={\textbf{0}},\\ \prec k+d, -d, m+d \succ , \quad x=\prec k, d, m \succ \in {\overline{M}}_n \setminus \{{\textbf{0}}\}. \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$ (8) The inequalities in ( 2 ) ensure that \(x^T\in {\overline{M}}_n\) . The multiplication formula ( 4 ) gives $$\begin{aligned} {\textbf{r}}(x)=xx^T= {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} {\textbf{0}},\quad x={\textbf{0}},\\ \prec k, 0, m \succ , \quad x=\prec k, d, m \succ \in M_n\setminus \{{\textbf{0}}\} \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$ (9) and $$\begin{aligned} {\textbf{d}}(x)=x^Tx= {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} {\textbf{0}},\quad x={\textbf{0}},\\ \prec k+d, 0, m+d \succ , \quad x=\prec k, d, m \succ \in M_n\setminus \{{\textbf{0}}\}, \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$ (10) as well as \(xx^Tx=x\) and \(x^Txx^T=x^T\) . Thus \(x^T\) is an inverse of x and \({\overline{M}}_n\) is a regular semigroup. The idempotents are \({\textbf{0}}\) and the elements \(\prec k, 0, m \succ \) ; and by ( 4 ), these idempotents commute. Accordingly [ 6 ], \({\overline{M}}_n\) is an inverse semigroup. In sum, we have arrived at Proposition 1 The \({\overline{M}}_n\) defined in ( 2 )–( 5 ) is a noncommutative inverse monoid with zero, whose identity is \({\textbf{1}}=\prec 1, 0, n \succ \) . The \(x^T\) given in ( 8 ) is the unique inverse of \(x\in {\overline{M}}_n\) . The semilattice of idempotents—to be denoted by \({\textbf{E}}\left( {\overline{M}}_n\right) \) —consists of \({\textbf{0}}\) together with all elements \(\prec k, 0, m \succ \) (in which \(d=0\) ). Proof If \({\textbf{0}}\ne x\le y\) and x is idempotent, then \(y\ne {\textbf{0}}\) , so we can set \(x=\prec k, 0, m \succ \) and \(y=\prec k', d', m' \succ \) . Then \(d'=0\) by ( 11 ), so that y is idempotent. In \(M_n\) , the triplet of integers represents the \(n\times n\) matrix described in [ 3 ] and our Introduction. Analogously, we can interpret the triplets of \({\overline{M}}_n\) as line segments that are contained within a \((n-1)\times (n-1)\) square and are parallel to the diagonal shown in Fig. 1 . Note that segment endpoints are permitted to lie on the square boundary, including its corners. Remark 1 It goes without saying that a segment representing a nonzero product remains within the closed square. In particular, it is true that $$\begin{aligned} \prec k, d, m \succ \prec k', d', m' \succ \ne {\textbf{0}}\implies |d+d'|\le n-1. \end{aligned}$$ (14) ( 14 ), which can readily be verified directly from ( 2 )–( 5 ), will prove useful in Sect. 4 . Stated otherwise, the inequality \(|d+d'|>n-1\) guarantees that the product is zero. Much of Sect. 2 can now be interpreted graphically: \({\textbf{0}}\) corresponds to the square being empty, while \({\textbf{1}}\) corresponds to the diagonal shown in Fig. 1 . Two inverse segments ( x and \(x^T\) ) are symmetric with respect to this diagonal. The segments that lie on the diagonal make up the nonzero idempotents, with multiplication in \({\textbf{E}}\left( {\overline{M}}_n\right) \) corresponding to segment intersection, as found in Corollary 3 . The segment \({\textbf{r}}(x)\) ( \({\textbf{d}}(x)\) ) is obtained by horizontally (vertically) translating x till the diagonal. It follows that the segments x and y are horizontal (vertical) translations of each other iff \({\textbf{r}}(x)={\textbf{r}}(y)\) ( \({\textbf{d}}(x)={\textbf{d}}(y)\) ), a fact to be used in Sect. 4 . Two segments of \({\overline{M}}_n\) are comparable—in the sense of the natural partial order discussed in Corollary 1 —iff one lies upon and is contained within the other, in which case the shorter segment is \(\le \) the longer one. Corollary 4 means that the restricted product \(x\cdot y\) of the underlying groupoid is defined iff y is a horizontal translation of the inverse \(x^T\) (equivalently, iff x is a vertical translation of the inverse \(y^T\) ); and that, when defined, \(x\cdot y\) is a horizontal translation of x . It follows from ( 20 ) that \(h(y)\le h(w)\) . Since \(h(w)<\mu \) , we have \(h(y)<\mu \) , so that \(y\in {K}_\mu \) . Hence \({\overline{M}}_n{K}_\mu \subseteq {K}_\mu \) , so \({K}_\mu \) is a left ideal by definition. Similarly, \({K}_\mu \) is a right ideal. Thus \({K}_\mu \) is a two-sided ideal, completing our proof. \(\square \) Theorem 6 of [ 3 ] discusses j th roots for the integer case: In \(M_n\) , a nonzero element \(x=\prec k, d, m \succ \) has a j th root iff d is an integer multiple of j ; and the j th root, when it exists, is unique. The theorem that follows shows that, in \({\overline{M}}_n\) , a unique root y always exists. In other words (and in complete analogy to the case of \({\mathbb {R}}_{>0}\) and its subset \({\mathbb {N}}\) ) any nonzero element \(x\in {\overline{M}}_n\) ( \(x\in {\mathbb {R}}_{>0}\) ) has a unique root \(y\in {\overline{M}}_n\) ( \(y\in {\mathbb {R}}_{>0}\) ); but in the special case \(x\in M_n\) ( \(x\in {\mathbb {N}}\) ), the said root y is not necessarily in \(M_n\) (in \({\mathbb {N}}\) ). Theorem 7 Let \(j\in {\mathbb {N}}\) . The element \(x=\prec k, d, m \succ \,\,\in {\overline{M}}_n{\setminus }\{{\textbf{0}}\}\) has a unique j th root in \({\overline{M}}_n\) . It is given by \(y=\prec k', d', m' \succ \,\,\in {\overline{M}}_n{\setminus }\{{\textbf{0}}\}\) , where $$\begin{aligned} k'=k+(j-1)\min (0,d'),\quad d'=\frac{d}{j},\quad m'=m+(j-1)\max (0,d'). \end{aligned}$$ (44) Proof Assume \(d\ge 0\) , so that ( 2 ) implies $$\begin{aligned} 1\le k\le m\le n-d. \end{aligned}$$ (45) We seek \(y\in {\overline{M}}_n\) such that \(x=y^j\) . As \(y\ne {\textbf{0}}\) , we set \(y=\prec k', d', m' \succ \) . By Lemma 1 , \(d'=d/j\ge 0\) . Invoking ( 2 ), we thus require $$\begin{aligned} 1\le k'\le m'\le n-d'. \end{aligned}$$ (46) By Lemma 1 , \(x=y^j\) is equivalent to the three equations $$\begin{aligned} k=k',\quad d=jd',\quad m=m'-(j-1)d'. \end{aligned}$$ These are uniquely solvable for \(k'\) , \(d'\) , \(m'\) and the solution is given in ( 44 ). Equations ( 44 ) and ( 45 ) then imply ( 46 ), completing the proof for \(d\ge 0\) . We can extend to \(d<0\) by taking the inverse. \(\square \) Remark 6 One could also consider the submonoid \(A_n\) of \({\overline{M}}_n\) in which \(k,d,m\in {\mathbb {Q}}\) . For \(x\in A_n\setminus \{{\textbf{0}}\}\) , the unique j th root y given in ( 44 ) also belongs to \(A_n\setminus \{{\textbf{0}}\}\) . Thus in \(A_n\setminus \{{\textbf{0}}\}\) , a unique root y always exists. Consequently, despite the aforementioned analogy of \({\overline{M}}_n\) to \({\mathbb {R}}_{>0}\) and \(M_n\) to \({\mathbb {N}}\) , the submonoid \(A_n\) is not analogous to \({\mathbb {Q}}_{>0}\) . We give a proof that builds upon previous results in the present paper, as well as concepts and results on inverse semigroups that can be found in [ 6 ]. Theorem 8 \({\overline{B}}_n\) is a Brandt semigroup. Proof By ( 4 ), ( 8 ), and ( 47 ), \({\overline{B}}_n\) is an inverse subsemigroup of \({\overline{M}}_n\) . Therefore \({\overline{B}}_n\) inherits its natural partial order \(\le \) from \({\overline{M}}_n\) . By ( 47 ) and Corollary 1 , \(x\le y\) iff \(x=y\) ( \(x,y\in {\overline{P}}_n\) ), meaning that in \({\overline{P}}_n={\overline{B}}_n\setminus \{{\textbf{0}}\}\) , the \(\le \) reduces to an equality. Equivalently [ 6 ], all idempotents of \({\overline{B}}_n\setminus \{{\textbf{0}}\}\) are primitive. Now let \(I\subseteq {\overline{B}}_n\) be an ideal of \({\overline{B}}_n\) . Assume \(I\ne \{{\textbf{0}}\}\) , so that some nonzero x belongs to I . Choose any y in \({\overline{B}}_n\) . By Lemma 3 , this y belongs to the principal ideal \({\overline{B}}_nx{\overline{B}}_n\) , so that \({\overline{B}}_n\subseteq {\overline{B}}_nx{\overline{B}}_n\) . As \({\overline{B}}_nx{\overline{B}}_n\subseteq I\) , we further have \({\overline{B}}_n\subseteq I\) , so \(I={\overline{B}}_n\) . Therefore the only ideals of \({\overline{B}}_n\) are \(\{{\textbf{0}}\}\) and \({\overline{B}}_n\) itself, meaning that \({\overline{B}}_n\) is 0-simple. Inverse, 0-simple semigroups with at least one primitive idempotent are Brandt semigroups [ 6 ], completing our proof. \(\square \) Corollary 6 of [ 3 ] determines a minimal generating set for \(M_n\) that, for any n , consists of only three elements. ORIGINAL 4 Monoid \(M_n\) and semigroup \(S_n\)
                     

Although it is possible to discuss \(M_n\) directly, we avoid calculations by introducing \(M_n\) as a subsemigroup of \(S_\infty \). Specifically, for any fixed n with \(n=2,3,\ldots \) we define

$$\begin{aligned} M_n=\{{\mathbf {0}}\}\cup \{x\in S_\infty \setminus \{{\mathbf {0}}\}: m\le n-\max (0,d)\}. \end{aligned}$$

                    (17)
                
Thus \(M_n\) can be regarded as a semigroup of \(n\times n\) matrices, in the sense explained in Sect. 2; that is to say, any element of \(M_n\) is an \(n\times n\) submatrix (on the top left) of the infinite matrix in Fig. 3.
For any \(x\in M_n\setminus \{{\mathbf {0}}\}\), properties (i)–(iii) of Sect. 3 continue to hold. Any nonzero \(x\in M_n\) has matrix entries \(x_{ij}\) that are given by (4), but with \(i,j\in \{1,2,\ldots ,n\}\), and with the meaning of the integers d, k, m remaining the same. Clearly, \(M_n\) is a subsemigroup of the rook monoid \(R_n\).
In terms of triplets, the set of elements of \(M_n\) is

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} M_n&=\{{\mathbf {0}}\}\cup \{\langle d,k,m\rangle :\ d\in {\mathbb {Z}};\ k, m\in {\mathbb {N}}; \ 1-\min (0,d)\\&\qquad \le k \le m\le n-\max (0,d)\}. \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$

                    (18)
                
In addition to \(k,m\in \{1,2,\ldots ,n\}\), the restrictions written in (18) imply

$$\begin{aligned} -(n-1)\le d\le n-1, \end{aligned}$$

                    (19)
                
for any \(\langle d,k,m\rangle \in M_n\setminus \{{\mathbf {0}}\}\); (19) is the range of the diagonal d, as expected.
The multiplication formula (6) remains unaltered, but \(M_n\) is associated with an additional restriction on \(d''\) and \(m''\). Furthermore—and even if the underlying semigroup \(S_\infty \) is not a monoid (Proposition  2)—we now have a \({\mathbf {1}}\) :
                
Theorem 3
                
\(M_n\) is a noncommutative monoid with \({\mathbf {1}}=\langle 0,1,n\rangle \). The product of two nonzero monoid elements \(\langle d,k,m\rangle \) and \(\langle d',k',m'\rangle \)—see (18)—is given by

$$\begin{aligned} \langle d,k,m\rangle \langle d',k',m'\rangle = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \langle d'',k'',m''\rangle ,\quad k'' \le m'',\\ \ {\mathbf {0}},\quad k''> m'', \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$

                    (20)
                
in which the parameters \(d''\), \(k''\), and \(m''\) are

$$\begin{aligned}&d''=d+d', \end{aligned}$$

                    (21)
                

$$\begin{aligned}&k''=\max (k,k'-d), \end{aligned}$$

                    (22)
                

$$\begin{aligned}&m''=\min (m,m'-d). \end{aligned}$$

                    (23)
                
Irrespective of whether \(k''\le m''\) or not, we have

$$\begin{aligned} 1-\min (0,d'')\le k''\quad \mathrm {and}\quad m''\le n-\max (0,d''). \end{aligned}$$

                    (24)
                
              
                
Proof
                
As \(\langle d,k,m\rangle \) and \(\langle d',k',m'\rangle \) belong to \(M_n\setminus \{{\mathbf {0}}\}\), (18) gives

$$\begin{aligned} m\le n-\max (0,d)\quad \mathrm {and}\quad m'\le n-\max (0,d'), \end{aligned}$$
from which it is easy to show (e.g. by distinguishing cases depending on the signs of d, \(d'\), and \(d''\)) that \(d''\) and \(m''\) satisfy the second inequality in (24). Since \(M_n\) is a subsemigroup of \(S_\infty \), (20)–(23) follow immediately from Theorem 1, as does the first inequality in (24). Since \(\langle 0,1,n\rangle \) represents the \(n\times n\) identity matrix, the equality \({\mathbf {1}}=\langle 0,1,n\rangle \) is obvious. (Alternatively, we can use (20)–(23) and the restrictions in (18) to show that \(x\langle 0,1,n\rangle =\langle 0,1,n\rangle x =x\) for every \(x=\langle d,k,m\rangle \in M_n\setminus \{{\mathbf {0}}\}\).) \(\square \)
