Proposition 2 Let n , q be integers \(\ge 2\) . The map \(\varphi : {\overline{M}}_n\rightarrow {\overline{M}}_q\) given by $$\begin{aligned} {\textbf{0}}\mapsto {\textbf{0}}, \quad x_n=\prec k_n, d_n, m_n \succ \,\mapsto \, x_q=\prec k_q, d_q, m_q \succ , \end{aligned}$$ (23) where $$\begin{aligned} k_q-1=\frac{q-1}{n-1} (k_n-1),\quad d_q=\frac{q-1}{n-1} d_n,\quad m_q-1=\frac{q-1}{n-1} (m_n-1), \end{aligned}$$ (24) is a monoid isomorphism. Therefore any \({\overline{M}}_n\) is isomorphic to \({\overline{M}}_2\) , and any \({\overline{S}}_n\) is isomorphic to \({\overline{S}}_2\) . Proof \(\varphi \) is bijective by ( 2 ). \(\varphi (x_ny_n)=\varphi (x_n)\varphi (y_n)\) and \(\varphi (\prec 1, 0, n \succ )=\prec ~1, 0, q \succ \) follow from ( 4 ). \(\square \) Remark 3 By Proposition 2 , a stand-alone study of \({\overline{M}}_n\) would be facilitated if one took \(n=2\) , corresponding to segments lying in a \(1\times 1\) closed square. Remark 4 Equation ( 16 ) and Proposition 2 imply that, for \(x_n \in {\overline{M}}_n\setminus \{{\textbf{0}}\}\) , $$\begin{aligned} h(x_q)=\frac{q-1}{n-1} h(x_n). \end{aligned}$$ (25) Equation ( 25 ) shows why, for \({\overline{M}}_n\) , we use the height h ( x ) instead of extending (to the non-integer case) the quantity \(\textrm{rnk}(x)=m-k+1\) mentioned in Remark 2 : In \({\overline{M}}_n\) , the latter quantity would scale in an unnatural manner. The idempotents of \({\overline{M}}_n\) were identified in Proposition 1 . The next proposition states that all other \(x\in {\overline{M}}_n\) are nilpotents, and gives the nilpotent indexes i ( x ). Proposition 3 An element \(x=\prec k, d, m \succ \,\in {\overline{M}}_n{\setminus } \{{\textbf{0}}\}\) is nilpotent if \(d\ne 0\) . The index i ( x ) of the nilpotent is given by $$\begin{aligned} i(x) =2+\left\lfloor \frac{m-k}{|d|} \right\rfloor =2+\left\lfloor \frac{h(x)}{|d|} \right\rfloor , \end{aligned}$$ (26) where \(\lfloor \beta \rfloor \) denotes the floor of \(\beta \in {\mathbb {R}}\) . In particular, \(i(x)=2\) when \(x\in {\overline{P}}_n\) ; and \(i(x)\rightarrow \infty \) as \(d\rightarrow 0\) (with \(h(x)=m-k\) held fixed and positive). Proof When \(d>0\) , the \(m^{(j)}\) in ( 7 ) decreases linearly with j , while \(k^{(j)}=k\) remains constant. Thus \(k^{(j)}>m^{(j)}\) for large enough j , in which case \(x^j={\textbf{0}}\) by ( 6 ). The index i ( x ) is the smallest such j and is given by ( 26 ). The proof for \(d<0\) is similar. \(\square \) Remark 5 In the special case of integer parameters ( \(x\in M_n\) ) we can show that ( 26 ) reduces to formula (28) of [ 3 ] (which involves the ceiling rather than the floor function). However, (28) of [ 3 ] does not hold for the more general case \(x\in {\overline{M}}_n\) . Corollary 5 \({\overline{M}}_n\) and \({\overline{S}}_n={\overline{M}}_n\setminus \{{\textbf{1}}\}\) are periodic inverse semigroups. \({\overline{M}}_n\) is not categorical at zero, and neither is \({\overline{S}}_n\) . Proof A semigroup is periodic when all its elements are of finite order, i.e., when the monogenic subsemigroup generated by any semigroup element has finite cardinality [ 5 ]. As all \(x\in {\overline{M}}_n\) are idempotent and/or nilpotent, both \({\overline{M}}_n\) and \({\overline{S}}_n\) are periodic. By definition [ 1 , 6 ], a semigroup with zero is categorical at zero if \(xyz={\textbf{0}}\) implies \(xy={\textbf{0}}\) or \(yz={\textbf{0}}\) . This is not true of \({\overline{M}}_n\) or \({\overline{S}}_n\) , because there are nilpotents x of index \(i(x)=3\) . \(\square \) 4 Further results In this section, we determine Green’s relations and show that \({\overline{M}}_n\) is a strongly \(E^*\) -unitary, combinatorial, fundamental, and completely semisimple inverse monoid. Then, we explicitly determine all ideals of \({\overline{M}}_n\) , discuss issues pertaining to j th roots, and point out differences between \({\overline{M}}_n\) and \(M_n\) .
