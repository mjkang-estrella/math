     For a finite graph G, we study the maximum 2-edge colorable subgraph problem
     and a related ratio µ(G)/ν(G), where ν(G) is the matching number of G, and
     µ(G) is the size of the largest matching in any pair (H, H ′ ) of disjoint matchings
     maximizing |H | + |H ′ | (equivalently, forming a maximum 2-edge colorable
     subgraph). Previously, it was shown that 45 ≤ µ(G)/ν(G) ≤ 1, and the class of
     graphs achieving 45 was completely characterized. We show here that any rational
     number between 45 and 1 can be achieved by a connected graph. Furthermore,
     we prove that every graph with ratio less than 1 must admit special subgraphs.

                                       1. Introduction

We investigate the ratio µ(G)/ν(G) of two parameters of a finite graph G, where
ν(G) is the size of a maximum matching in G and µ(G) is the size of the largest
matching in a pair of disjoint matchings whose union is as large as possible. More
formally,
λ(G) := max{|H |+|H ′ | : H and H ′ are disjoint matchings in G},
µ(G) := max{|H | : H and H ′ are disjoint matchings in G with |H |+|H ′ |= λ(G)}.
   One motivation for studying the ratio µ(G)/ν(G) is the problem of covering
as many edges of G as possible by k matchings, which is known as the maximum
k-edge-colorable subgraph problem. Let νk (G) denote the maximum number of
edges of G that can be covered by k matchings. When k = 1, it is clear that one
just takes a maximum matching, so ν1 (G) = ν(G). We consider the problem with
k = 2, as ν2 (G) = λ(G).

1A. Computational considerations. Recall that a proper edge coloring of a graph G
is an assignment of a color to each edge of G such that no two adjacent edges have
the same color. In a proper edge coloring, the set of edges with a given color is by
definition a matching, so a proper edge coloring with k colors corresponds exactly
to covering G with k matchings. We can therefore reframe νk to be the maximum
size in edges of a subgraph of G that is k-edge-colorable.
    Edge coloring is a computationally complex problem; I. Holyer [1981] showed
that the problem of determining whether a graph admits a 3-edge coloring is NP-
complete even in the case of cubic graphs. Note that a cubic graph is 3-edge
colorable if and only if λ is equal to the number of vertices, and so Holyer’s result
is equivalent to the NP-completeness of the problem of determining whether λ is
equal to the number of vertices.
    Furthermore, U. Feige, E. Ofek, and U. Wieder [Feige et al. 2002] showed
that, for all k ≥ 2, the k-edge-colorable subgraph problem (computing νk ) is NP-
hard (note that there are polynomial-time algorithms computing ν1 , the first due
to J. Edmonds [1965]). Therefore, unless P = NP, we cannot compute λ(G) in
polynomial time. However, A. Agrawal et al. [2020] showed that computing λ(G)
is fixed-parameter tractable.
    A greedy algorithm to compute k matchings realizing νk (G) could first take a
maximum matching of the whole graph, and then recursively take a maximum
matching of the remaining edges until k matchings have been computed. However,
this may not yield a cover with maximum number of edges νk (G); see Figure 1
for a minimal example. The parameter µ(G)/ν(G) measures the failure of the
optimality of this greedy algorithm for k = 2.

                            2. Definitions and notation

Here, we define the main notions we use, referring to [West 1996] for basic graph-
theoretic terminology.
   By a graph, we mean a pair G := (V, E), where V is a set of vertices and E a set
of edges, i.e., E ⊆ V 2 . We will only be considering finite, simple, undirected graphs
with no loops, i.e., (v, v) ∈
                            / E and (u, v) = (v, u) for all u, v ∈ V. We identify the
corresponding undirected edge by uv. A matching in G is a set of edges such that
no two are adjacent. A perfect matching is a matching M that covers every vertex
(see Figure 2), and a near perfect matching is a matching M such that, for every
vertex v in G except for one, there exists an e ∈ M such that v is incident to e.
   Below we define the parameters that we will investigate throughout the rest of
the paper, as well as some additional definitions:
• λ(G) := max{|H | + |H ′ | : (H, H ′ ) are disjoint matchings on G}.

• 3(G) is the set of pairs (H, H ′ ) of disjoint matchings satisfying |H |+|H ′ | = λ(G).

• µ(G) := max{|H | : there exists H ′ such that (H, H ′ ) ∈ 3(G)}.

• 3µ (G) is the subset of 3(G) of pairs (H, H ′ ) with |H | = µ(G).

• µ′ (G) := λ(G) − µ(G).

   If A, B ⊆ E, then an A, B alternating path is a path such that alternating edges
are in A \ B and the others in B \ A. We will frequently refer to M, H alternating
paths for the M and H described above.
  A path/cycle is even (odd ) if it has even (odd) number of edges.

  A central vertex of a tree is a vertex with degree larger than 2.
   A leg is a copy of P2 (the path graph of length 2, i.e., with two edges). We say Gb

                                     4. Ratio 1

In this section, we provide several sufficient conditions for a graph to have ratio 1,
most notable of which is Theorem 1.2. The latter involves diamond spanners, which,
by definition, are spanners with any number (possibly zero) of central diamonds as
in Figure 3. We will prove Theorem 1.2 after some observations and lemmas.
   Throughout, we let G := (V, E) be a finite connected graph and n := |V |.
4A. Observations and examples for ratio 1.
Observation 4.1. ν(G) ≤ ⌊n/2⌋.
Lemma 4.2. If G admits two disjoint matchings whose union contains at least
n − 1 edges, then µ(G) = ⌊n/2⌋ = ν(G).
Proof. The hypothesis implies that λ(G) ≥ n − 1, i.e., λ(G) is n − 1 or n. Either
way, µ(G) ≥ ⌈(n − 1)/2⌉ = ⌊n/2⌋, so µ(G) = ν(G) by Observation 4.1.            □
Definition 4.3. A path/cycle in G is called Hamiltonian if each vertex of G appears
in it exactly once.
Corollary 4.4. If G admits a Hamiltonian path, then µ(G) = ν(G).
Proof. A Hamiltonian path has n − 1 edges, which lie in a pair of disjoint matchings,
so Lemma 4.2 applies.                                                             □

Definition 4.8. Choose matchings (M, H, H ′ ) such that |M| = ν(G), |H | = µ(G),

intersection triple.

            Appendix: Characterization of graphs achieving ratio 54

For the reader’s convenience, here we provide a short exposition of Tserunyan’s char-
acterization [2009] of exactly when the lower bound 45 is achieved by a connected
graph.
   Let S denote the spanner as a concrete graph, and note that S is a tree. Call a
vertex of the spanner a j-vertex if it has degree j. For a 1-vertex or 2-vertex v, call
the nearest 3-vertex the base of v. Call an edge between an i-vertex and a j-vertex
          PAIRS OF DISJOINT MATCHINGS AND RELATED CLASSES OF GRAPHS                            263


an i- j edge. Define two sets associated to the spanner S:

               U (S) := {e ∈ E(G) : e is incident to a 1-vertex},
                L(S) := {e ∈ E(G) \ U (S) : e is incident to a 2-vertex}.

For an arbitrary graph G, call a spanning subgraph where every connected com-
ponent is isomorphic to S an S-forest. Let G admit an S-forest F. Denote the
connected components by S1 , S2 , . . . , Sk , and define
                                  k
                                  [                            k
                                                               [
                       U (F) :=          U (S j ),   L(F) :=         L(S j ),
                                   j=1                         j=1

             1(G, F) := {e ∈ E(G) : e connects a 1-vertex to its base},
              B(G, F) := E(G) \ (U (F) ∪ L(F) ∪ 1(G, F)).

Note that U, L are subsets of E(F), whereas 1, B are subsets of E(G).
    Call a nonrepeating sequence of edges (e1 , e2 , . . . , ek ) such that ei is adjacent to
ei+1 for all i a trail. If u i−1 , u i are the vertices incident to ei , call a trail closed if
u 0 = u k . For sets A, B ⊆ E(G), say a trail T = (e1 , e2 , . . . , ek ) is A, B-alternating
if the edges of even index belong to A \ B and those of odd index belong to B \ A
or vice versa.

Theorem A.1. A connected graph G has ratio 45 if and only if G admits a spanning
S-forest F such that:

 (1) 1-vertices of F are not incident to any edge from B(G, F).
 (2) If u is a 1-vertex and is incident to an edge of 1(G, F), then the 2-vertex
     adjacent to u is not incident to any edges of B(G, F).
 (3) For any L(F), B(G, F)-alternating closed trail C containing a 2-2 edge, we
     have C ∩ B(G, F) is not bipartite.
