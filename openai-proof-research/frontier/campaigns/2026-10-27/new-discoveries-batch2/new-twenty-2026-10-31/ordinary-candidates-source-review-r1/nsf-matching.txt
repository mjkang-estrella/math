                                          1. Introduction
   We investigate the ratio of two parameters of a finite graph G, µ(G)
                                                                    ν(G)
                                                                         , where ν(G) is the
size of a maximum matching in G, and µ(G) is the size of the largest matching in a pair of
disjoint matchings whose union is as large as possible. More formally,
    λ(G) ..= max {|H| + |H ′ | : H and H ′ are disjoint matchings in G} ,
    µ(G) ..= max {|H| : H and H ′ are disjoint matchings in G with |H| + |H ′ | = λ(G)} .
  To place the mentioned parameters λ, µ and the ratio µν in the context of what is commonly
considered in graph theory, recall that for k ∈ N, a proper edge-coloring of a graph G with
k colors is exactly a partition of the edge-set into k disjoint matchings. Thus, determining
λ is the k = 2 case of the following well-known problem:
Maximum k-edge colorable subgraph problem (k-ECS). For a given k ∈ N, what
is the maximum number νk of edges of a given finite graph G that can be covered with k
disjoint matchings?
  We note here that ν = ν1 and λ = ν2 , so the k = 1 case of this problem is the maximum
matching problem, while the k = 2 case is what we are concerned with here. Before describing
our result, we overview what is known about k-ECS concerning its computational complexity,
numerical bounds, and structural aspects.

a polynomial-time algorithm [Edm65]. On the other hand, 2-ECS is already N P -hard; in
fact, the problem of determining whether λ(G) is equal to the number of vertices of the
graph is N P -complete even for cubic graphs. This follows immediately from Holyer’s work

                               2. Definitions & notation
   Throughout, we denote by G = (V, E) a finite graph with vertex set V and edge set E.
A matching in G is a set of edges such that no two are adjacent. In particular, we will
consider the following specific types of matchings.
• A maximum matching is a matching M of maximum size, i.e., for all matchings M ′ ,
   |M ′ | ≤ |M |. We let ν(G) denote the size of a maximum matching.
• A perfect matching is a matching M such that for every vertex v in G, there exists an
   e ∈ M such that v is incident to e.
• A near perfect matching is a matching M such that for every vertex v in G except for
   one, there exists an e ∈ M such that v is incident to e.
   In addition to sizes of maximum matchings, we will also pay special attention to the size
of the union of two disjoint matchings whose union is of maximum size. More formally:
• λ(G) := max{|H| + |H ′ | : (H, H ′ ) are disjoint matchings in G};
• Λ(G) is the set of pairs (H, H ′ ) of disjoint matchings satisfying |H| + |H ′ | = λ(G);
• µ(G) := max{|H| : ∃H ′ such that (H, H ′ ) ∈ Λ(G)}.
   ν, λ, and µ are the main concern of this paper. We present an example with the spanner
graph to illustrate our key parameters. The left spanner of Fig. 2 has the unique maximum
matching, M , highlighted. It has size 5, and so ν(G) = 5 for the spanner. The largest
matching disjoint from M has size 2, for combined size of 7. However, if we choose a
separate set of disjoint matchings, we can achieve λ(G) = 8. On the right of the same
figure, our maximal disjoint matchings H and H ′ are highlighted. They each have size 4, so

• Λµ (G) is the subset of Λ(G) of pairs (H, H ′ ) with |H| = µ(G).
• µ′ (G) := λ(G) − µ(G).
• By a chain in G, we mean a sequence (v0 , e1 , v1 , e2 , . . . , eℓ , vℓ ), ℓ ∈ N, where the vi are
  vertices and the ej are edges of G such ei = (vi−1 , vi ) for all i ≤ ℓ; we also assume that our
  chains are simple, i.e. all edges are distinct. We refer to ℓ as the length of the chain and
  to v0 and vℓ as its endpoints.
• By an odd (resp. even) chain we mean that the length (i.e., the number of edges on it)
  is odd (resp. even).
• By a path, we mean a simple path, i.e. a chain with distinct vertices, and by a cycle
  we mean a simple cycle, i.e. a chain whose endpoints are equal but all other vertices are
  distinct.
• An edge ei on an odd path v0 e1 v1 e2 . . . ek+1 v2k+1 is called even (resp. odd) if i is even
  (resp. odd). Note that this would not change if the path was written in the reverse order.
• If A, B ⊆ E, then an A − B alternating chain is a chain such that edges of odd index
  are in A \ B and edges of even index are in B \ A, or vice versa. Note that if A and B are
  both matchings, then the chain must be either a path or a cycle, or else some vertex will
  be incident to two edges of the same matching.
• If A ⊂ E is a subset of the edges of the graph G, then Ac := E \ A is the complement in
  E of A.
The majority of the techniques used in Section 5 make use of the properties of the alternating
paths of various matchings.

                                   3. PEC decompositions
   Now, we present a type of decomposition for graphs that highlights matching structure
and gives an alternative way to compute the parameters ν(G), λ(G), and µ(G). Several
of the following lemmas will be used in the proof of our main theorems. Throughout this
section, let G := (V, E) be a graph and n := |V |.
• A subgraph of G is a graph G′ := (V ′ , E ′ ) with V ′ ⊆ V and E ′ ⊆ E. A subgraph
  G′ := (V ′ , E ′ ) of G is spanning if V ′ = V .
                                                  5
• A pec decomposition of G is a spanning subgraph G′ := (V, E ′ ), each of whose (con-
  nected) components is a path or an even cycle. (Isolated vertices are treated as even
  paths.)
• If A is a subset of E, then we use the notation GA := (V, A) to denote the spanning
  subgraph of G with edge set A. In particular, we use this notation in the case that A is
  the union of certain matchings.
For a pec decomposition G′ of G, let
• p(G′ ) (resp., ep(G′ )) denote the number of components of G′ that are paths (resp., even
  paths);
• p(G) := min{p(G′ ) : G′ is a pec decomposition of G};
• ep(G) := min{ep(G′ ) : G′ is a pec decomposition of G}.
These last three parameters describe structural properties of the graph that can be assessed
with matchings and alternating paths. They are a direct parallel to our original ν, µ, and λ.

                                     4. Basics tools
In this section we will state and prove several structural lemmas about alternating chains
(i.e., paths or cycles) that will be used to prove our main theorems. Let G ..= (V, E) be a
finite connected graph. Among the maximum matchings and maximum disjoint matchings
in G, there are some that have maximal intersection, and this condition allows for sharper
restrictions on the possible edge configurations. More specifically, we define maximally in-
tersecting matchings:
Definition 13. Matchings M , H, and H ′ are called maximally intersecting if they satisfy
the following:
(i) M is a maximum matching.
(ii) (H, H ′ ) ∈ Λµ (G) with |H| = µ(G).
(iii) |M ∩ (H ∪ H ′ )| is maximum among all M, H, H ′ satisfying (i)–(ii).
(iv) |M ∩ H| is maximum among all M, H, H ′ satisfying (i)–(iii).

   As we will demonstrate in the next section, these matchings capture the most important
structural behavior of µ(G)
                       ν(G)
                            < 1 graphs. Throughout this section, let M , H, H ′ be maximally
intersecting matchings. Here are a few basic facts about M -H alternating chains. Define an
end-edge of a path to be an edge in the path incident to one of the path’s terminal vertices.
Lemma 14.
(14.a) The maximal M -H alternating chains are odd paths with end-edges in M .
(14.b) The end-edges of M -H alternating paths are in H ′ .
(14.c) All vertices on maximal M -H alternating paths are incident to H ′ .
Proof. (14.a) Let P be such a chain. Since these chains are alternating, P cannot be an odd
cycle. If P is an even path or cycle, define M ′ ..= (M \ P ) ∪ (P \ M ). M ′ is a matching
since M is a matching and since P is alternating. Furthermore, |M ′ | = |M |, and so M ′ is
a maximum matching. However, |M ′ ∩ (H ∪ H ′ )| ≥ |M ∩ (H ∪ H ′ )| since not all M ∪ P
edges may have been in H ′ , and since |M ′ ∩ H| > |M ∩ H|, either of (iii) or (iv) must be
violated, a contradiction. Now suppose that some end-edge of P is not in M . This edge, e0 ,
is in H \ M , and is adjacent to exactly one M edge e1 in P . But then if e1 is removed from
M and e0 is added to M , |M ∩ H| is again increased, a contradiction.
(14.b) Consider an end-edge e0 in M \ H. Again note that e0 can be incident to H on only
one side. If e0 is not in H ′ , then we can remove e1 , the preceding edge, from H, add e0 to
H, and again increase |M ∩ (H ∪ H ′ )|.
(14.c) Suppose instead some interior vertex is not incident to H ′ , and note that this vertex
is incident to no end-edges. This vertex is incident to exactly one edge in M , which we call
                                              8
eM . eM is incident to H ′ at most once. Via a similar argument to above, if we take eM to
be in H ′ instead of this adjacent edge, we increase |M ∩ (H ∪ H ′ )|.                  □
  We now present similar structural lemmas about maximal H-H ′ alternating paths.
Lemma 15.
(15.a) If a maximal H-H ′ alternating path is odd, then its end-edges are in H.
(15.b) The end-edges of maximal M -H alternating paths (which we know are in H ′ ) are
       end-edges of even H-H ′ alternating paths.
(15.c) All inner (not end) vertices of maximal M -H alternating paths are inner vertices of
       H-H ′ alternating paths. In other words, each end-vertex of an H-H ′ alternating path
       is either an end-vertex of a maximal M -H alternating path or is not on any M -H
       alternating path.
(15.d) On every even maximal H-H ′ alternating chain, the edges in H ∩ M are at least as
       many as those in H ′ ∩ M .

(15.d) Otherwise, swapping H and H ′ contradicts condition (iv).
