                                                                                      1. Introduction
                                              We investigate the ratio of two parameters of a finite graph G, µ(G)
                                                                                                              ν(G)
                                                                                                                   , where ν(G) is the size
                                           of a maximum matching in G, and µ(G) is the size of the largest matching in a pair of disjoint
                                           matchings whose union is as large as possible. More formally, if λ(G) = max{|H| + |H 0 | :
                                           H and H’ are disjoint matchings in G}, then µ(G) = max{|H| : H and H’ are disjoint
                                           matchings in G with |H| + |H 0 | = λ(G)}.
                                              To place the mentioned parameters λ, µ and the ratio µν in the context of what is commonly
                                           considered in graph theory, recall that for k ∈ N, a proper edge-coloring of a graph G with
                                           k colors is exactly a partition of the edge-set into k disjoint matchings. Thus, the problem
                                           of determining whether a proper edge-coloring with k colors exists (call such graphs k-edge-
                                           colorable) is a special case of the following more general quantitative problem:
                                           Problem 1. For a given k ∈ N, what is the maximum number of edges of a given finite
                                           graph G that can be covered with k disjoint matchings?
                                              The parameter λ(G) is the solution to this problem for k = 2. Although determining
                                           whether a graph is 2-edge-colorable is trivial (those are precisely the graphs whose connected
                                           components are paths or even cycles), the problem of finding the largest such subgraph is
                                           not easy. While to solve Problem 1 for k = 1, one just takes a maximum matching, it is not
                                           clear what to do already for k = 2. The greedy algorithm would firstly take a maximum
                                           matching of the whole graph and then take a maximum matching of the remaining edges.
                                           However, this may not cover the maximum number of edges λ(G): see Fig. 1 for an example
                                           where λ(G) = 4+4 = 8, while the greedy algorithm yields 5+2 = 7. The ratio µ(G)  ν(G)
                                                                                                                                 measures
                                           the failure of optimality of the greedy algorithm for k = 2; in particular, this ratio is 54 for
                                           the graph in Fig. 1.

                               2. Definitions & notation
   Throughout, we denote by G = (V, E) a finite graph with vertex set V and edge set E.
A matching in G is a set of edges such that no two are adjacent. In particular, we will
consider the following specific types of matchings.
      • A maximum matching is a matching M of maximum size, i.e., for all matchings
         M 0 , |M 0 | ≤ |M |. We let ν(G) denote the size of a maximum matching.
      • A perfect matching is a matching M such that for every vertex v in G, there exists
         an e ∈ M such that v is incident to e.
      • A near perfect matching is a matching M such that for every vertex v in G except
         for one, there exists an e ∈ M such that v is incident to e.
   In addition to sizes of maximum matchings, we will also pay special attention to the size
of the union of two disjoint matchings whose union is of maximum size. More formally,
we define λ(G) := max{|H| + |H 0 | : (H, H 0 ) are disjoint matchings on G}. Furthermore,
define:
      • Λ(G) is the set of pairs (H, H 0 ) of disjoint matchings satisfying |H| + |H 0 | = λ(G).
      • µ(G) := max{|H| : ∃H 0 such that (H, H 0 ) ∈ Λ(G)}.
   ν, λ, and µ are the main concern of this paper. We present an example with the spanner
graph to illustrate our key parameters. The left spanner of Fig. 2 has the unique maximum
matching, M , highlighted. It has size 5, and so ν(G) = 5 for the spanner. The largest
matching disjoint from M has size 2, for combined size of 7. However, if we choose a
separate set of disjoint matchings, we can achieve λ(G) = 8. On the right of the same
figure, our maximal disjoint matchings H and H 0 are highlighted. They each have size 4, so

                                3. PEC decompositions
   Now, we present a type of decomposition for graphs that highlights matching structure
and gives an alternative way to compute the parameters ν(G), λ(G), and µ(G). Several
of the following lemmas will be used in the proof of our main theorems. Throughout this
section, let G := (V, E) be a graph and n := |V |.
      • A subgraph of G is a graph G0 := (V 0 , E 0 ) with V 0 ⊆ V and E 0 ⊆ E. A subgraph
        G0 := (V 0 , E 0 ) of G is spanning if V 0 = V .
      • A pec decomposition of G is a spanning subgraph G0 := (V, E 0 ), each of whose
        (connected) components is a path or an even cycle. (Isolated vertices are treated as
        even paths.)
For a pec decomposition G0 of G, let
      • p(G0 ) (resp., e(G0 )) denote the number of components of G0 that are paths (resp.,
        even paths).
      • p(G) := min{p(G0 ) : G0 is a pec decomposition of G}.
      • e(G) := min{e(G0 ) : G0 is a pec decomposition of G}.
These last three parameters describe structural properties of the graph that can be assessed
with matchings and alternating paths. They are a direct parallel to our original ν, µ, and λ.

                                       4. Basics tools
In this section we will state and prove several structural lemmas about alternating chains
(i.e., paths or cycles) that will be used to prove our main theorems. Let G ..= (V, E) be a
finite connected graph. Among the maximum matchings and maximum disjoint matchings
in G, there are some that have maximal intersection, and this condition allows for sharper
restrictions on the possible edge configurations. More specifically, we define maximally in-
tersecting matchings:
Definition 14. Matchings M , H, and H 0 are called maximally intersecting if they satisfy
the following:
                                                6
(i)     M is a maximum matching.
(ii)    (H, H 0 ) ∈ Λµ (G) with |H| = µ(G).
(iii)   |M ∩ (H ∪ H 0 )| is maximum among all M, H, H 0 satisfying (i)–(ii).
(iv)    |M ∩ H| is maximum among all M, H, H 0 satisfying (i)–(iii).

   Throughout this section, let M , H, H 0 be maximally intersecting matchings. Here are a
few basic facts about M -H alternating chains. Define an end-edge of a path to be an edge
in the path incident to one of the path’s terminal vertices.
Lemma 15.
(15.a) The maximal M -H alternating chains are odd paths with end-edges in M .
(15.b) The end-edges of M -H alternating paths are in H 0 .
(15.c) All vertices on maximal M -H alternating paths are incident to H 0 .
Proof. (15.a) Let P be such a chain. Since these chains are alternating, P cannot be an odd
cycle. If P is an even cycle, define M 0 ..= (M \P ) ∪ (P \M ). This set of edges is a matching
since M is and since P is alternating. Furthermore, |M 0 | = |M |, and so M 0 is a maximum
matching. However, |M 0 ∩ H| > |M ∩ H|, a contradiction. Now suppose that some end-edge
of P is not in M . This edge, e0 , is in H\M , and is adjacent to exactly one M edge e1 in
P . But then if e1 is removed from M and e0 is added to M , |M ∩ H| is again increased, a
contradiction. This implies that the length of P is odd.

(15.b) Consider an end-edge e0 in M \H. Again note that e0 can be incident to H on only
one side. If e0 is not in H 0 , then we can remove e1 , the preceding edge, from H, add e0 to
H, and again increase |M ∩ (H ∪ H 0 )|.

(15.c) Suppose instead some interior vertex is not incident to H 0 , and note that this vertex
is incident to no end-edges. This vertex is incident to exactly one edge in M , which we call
eM . eM is incident to H 0 at most once. Via a similar argument to above, if we take eM to
be in H 0 instead of this adjacent edge, we increase |M ∩ (H ∪ H 0 )|.                      
   We now present similar structural lemmas about maximal H-H 0 alternating paths.
Lemma 16.
(16.a) If a maximal H-H 0 alternating path is odd, then its end-edges are in H.
(16.b) The end-edges of maximal M -H alternating paths (which we know are in H 0 ) are
       end-edges of even H-H 0 alternating paths.
(16.c) All inner (not end) vertices of maximal M -H alternating paths are inner vertices of
       H-H 0 alternating paths. In other words, each end-vertex of an H-H 0 alternating path
       is either an end-vertex of a maximal M -H alternating path or is not on any M -H
       alternating path.
(16.d) On every even maximal H-H 0 alternating chain, the edges in H ∩ M are at least as
       many as those in H 0 ∩ M .
(16.e) Each end-edge of a maximal H-H 0 alternating path that is in H is also in M .
Proof. (16.a): Otherwise, we would switch H and H 0 on that path, keeping |H ∪ H 0 | the
same, but increasing H.
                                                7
(16.b): Each such edge is adjacent to H exactly on one side, so it is an end-edge of a maximal
H-H 0 alternating path, which hence must be even by (16.a).
(16.c) This is just because each inner vertex of a maximal M -H alternating path is incident
to H by definition and is also incident to H 0 by (15.c).
(16.d) Otherwise, swapping H and H 0 contradicts condition (iv).
(16.e) Every edge in H \ M is an inner edge on a maximal M -H alternating path, so it
cannot be an end-edge of a maximal H-H 0 alternating path by (16.c).               

   We are now prepared to state and prove our main theorems. For d ∈ N, we call a vertex
in a graph a d-vertex if its degree is d, and we say that a graph has degree d if the degree of
each vertex is at most d. An edge incident to a leaf (a 1-vertex) is called a leaf-edge.
   A path in a graph whose end-vertices are leaves is called leaf-to-leaf. Call an edge e on
an odd path p odd (resp. even) if its edge-distance from either end-edge of p is even (resp.
odd). Similarly, call a vertex v on an even path p odd (resp. even) if its edge-distance from
either end-edge of p is odd (resp. even).
   Our main theorems, Theorem 20 and Theorem 21, establish that the core structural prop-
erties of graphs with µ(G)
                       ν(G)
                            < 1 are due to a graph’s skeleton structure.
Definition 17. A connected nonempty graph G is called a skeleton if it is of degree 3
and admits a (not necessarily spanning) subgraph G0 ..= (V 0 , E 0 ) satisfying the following
conditions:
(17.i) G0 consists of vertex-disjoint odd leaf-to-leaf paths of length at least 5.
(17.ii) For each odd edge on a maximal path in G0 , either both of its incident vertices have
          G-degree 3 (call these edges rich) or none of them do.
(17.iii) Each maximal path in G0 contains a 3-vertex.
(17.iv) The vertices in V \ V 0 have G-degree at most 2.
(17.v) The graph G − V 0 obtained from G by removing V 0 consists of vertex-disjoint odd
          paths.
(17.vi) Let G00 the subgraph of G obtained by removing the rich edges. The G00 -connected
          component of each end-vertex of a maximal path in G0 is an even path and these
          are the only connected components that are even paths.
(17.vii) G00 is bipartite.
(17.viii) Let M be the matching containing each odd edge of G0 and each even edge of G−V 0 .
          Then G contains no M, M c alternating cycles.
Take note in particular of the final condition involving M, M c alternating cycles. Some of
the following proofs argue by contradiction to construct such a cycle. Call a skeleton a
k-skeleton if G0 has k-many connected components (odd paths). The spanner is an example
of a 1-skeleton.
Remark 18. It follows from (17.ii) and (17.iv) that removing the rich edges from G results in
a graph G00 of degree 2, so each connected component of G00 is a path or a cycle. Condition
(17.vi) demands that the connected components of the end-vertices of the maximal paths in
G0 are exactly those that are even paths (necessarily of length at least 4). Condition (17.vii)
demands that there are no odd cycles in G00 .

Proposition 19. Every skeleton admits a unique perfect matching.
Proof. Let G be a skeleton graph and let G0 be as in Definition 17. Because the connected
components of G0 and G − V 0 are odd paths and each odd path admits a perfect matching,
the whole graph G admits a perfect matching (the union of the matchings on each odd path).
Call this matching M .
   It remains to show uniqueness. Suppose towards a contradiction that G contains another
perfect matching M 0 . Observe that if M = M 0 on each of the paths in G0 then this forces
