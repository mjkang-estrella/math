A directed graph is a pair \(G=(V(G),E(G))\) of finite sets, where (1) V ( G ) is the set of vertices of G , (2) E ( G ) is the set of edges, each of which is directed. That is, of the two vertices at the two ends of each edge, one is the initial vertex, the other is the terminal vertex. In this paper, we do not assume that graphs are simple. That is, we allow loops (edges initiate and terminate at the same vertex) and multiple edges from one vertex to another. But, to simplify some of our statements, we assume that all graphs in this paper are without vertices of degree 0. Given a directed graph G , a directed path in G from a vertex u to a different vertex v is a sequence \(u=v_0,x_0,v_1,x_1,\ldots ,x_{n-1},v_n=v\) such that (1) \(v_0,v_1,\ldots ,v_n\) are pairwise distinct vertices of G , (2) each \(x_i\) is an edge of G with initial vertex \(v_i\) and terminal vertex \(v_{i+1}\) . Two directed paths from u to v are called edge-disjoint if they have no common edges. The amount of directed paths in G from u to v is often measured by: the maximal number \(\alpha _{u\rightarrow v}(G)\) of pairwise edge-disjoint directed paths in G from u to v . By the Edge Version of Directed Menger’s Theorem, $$\begin{aligned} \alpha _{u\rightarrow v}(G)= & {} \text {minimal number of edges in } G \text { whose removal from } G \\&\text {destroys all directed paths in } G \text { from } u \text { to }v. \end{aligned}$$ In this paper, we will also consider the following naive upper bound for \(\alpha _{u\rightarrow v}(G)\) . the minimal number \(\beta _{u\rightarrow v}(G)\) of edges in G incident at u or v whose removal from G destroys all directed paths in G from u to v . A directed cycle in G is a closed directed path, that is, a sequence \(v_0,x_0,v_1,x_1,\ldots ,x_{n-1},v_n,x_n,v_{n+1}=v_0\) satisfying (1) \(v_0,v_1,\ldots ,v_n\) are pairwise distinct vertices of G , (2) each \(x_i\) is an edge of G with initial vertex \(v_i\) and terminal vertex \(v_{i+1}\) . Note that two such sequences represent the same directed cycle if one is a circular permutation of the other. That is, the directed cycle given by \(v_0,x_0,v_1,x_1,\ldots ,x_{n-1},v_n,x_n,v_0\) is the same as the one given by \(v_1,x_1,\ldots ,x_{n-1},v_n,x_n,v_0,x_0,v_1\) . We call a directed graph acyclic if it does not contain any directed cycles. Two directed cycles in G are called edge-disjoint if they have no common edges. Two directed cycles in G are called disjoint if they have no common vertices. Footnote 1 For a directed graph G , we define \(\alpha (G)\) to be the maximal number of pairwise edge-disjoint directed cycles in G , \(\tilde{\alpha }(G)\) to be the maximal number of pairwise disjoint directed cycles in G , \(\beta (G)\) to be the minimal number of edges in G whose removal from G destroys all directed cycles in G . \(\alpha (G)\) is known as the cycle packing number of G . We call \(\tilde{\alpha }(G)\) the strong cycle packing number of G . \(\beta (G)\) is known as the cyclomatic number of G . Clearly, \(\tilde{\alpha }(G) \le \alpha (G) \le \beta (G)\) . Moreover, by the Lucchesi–Younger Theorem [ 6 ], \(\alpha (G) = \beta (G)\) if G is planar. For a vertex v of G , we define \(\alpha _v(G)\) to be the maximal number of pairwise edge-disjoint directed cycles in G , each of which contains v , \(\beta _v(G)\) to be the minimal number of edges incident at v whose removal from G destroys all directed cycles in G containing v . Again, \(\alpha _v(G) \le \beta _v(G)\) . More generally, for a subset E of E ( G ), define \(\alpha _E(G)\) to be the maximal number of pairwise edge-disjoint directed cycles in G , each of which contains at least one edge in E . \(\beta _E(G)\) to be the minimal number of edges in E whose removal from G destroys all directed cycles in G containing at least one edge in E . Once more, \(\alpha _E(G) \le \beta _E(G)\) . For two distinct vertices u and v of the directed graph G , define a directed graph \(G_{u \rightarrow v}\) by (1) deleting all edges that have u as their terminal vertex, (2) deleting all edges that have v as their initial vertex, (3) after the previous two steps, identifying the vertices u and v . Denote by \(u\# v\) the vertex of \(G_{u \rightarrow v}\) from the vertices u and v of G . One can see: (1) The natural inclusion map \(E(G_{u \rightarrow v}) \hookrightarrow E(G)\) induces a one-to-one correspondence between directed paths in G from u to v and directed cycles in \(G_{u \rightarrow v}\) containing \(u\# v\) . (2) Under this correspondence, a collection of directed paths in G from u to v is pairwise edge-disjoint if and only if the corresponding collection of directed cycles in \(G_{u \rightarrow v}\) containing \(u\# v\) is pairwise edge-disjoint. (3) \(\alpha _{u\rightarrow v}(G) = \alpha _{u\# v}(G_{u \rightarrow v})\) and \(\beta _{u\rightarrow v}(G) = \beta _{u\# v}(G_{u \rightarrow v})\) . A flow network N is a quadruple \(N=(V,c,s,t)\) , where (1) V is a finite set, (2) s and t are distinct elements of V , called source and sink of N , (3) \(c:V \times V \rightarrow \mathbb {R}_{\ge 0}\) is the capacity function of N satisfying \(c(v,v)=c(v,s)=c(t,v)=0\) for all \(v \in V\) . A flow f of N is a function \(f:V \times V \rightarrow \mathbb {R}_{\ge 0}\) such that (1) \(f(u,v) \le c(u,v)\) for all \(u,~v \in V\) , (2) \(\sum _{v \in V} f(u,v) = \sum _{v \in V} f(v,u)\) for all \(u \in V {\setminus } \{s,t\}\) . The flow number of f is \(|f|=\sum _{v\in V} f(s,v) = \sum _{v\in V} f(v,t)\) . The maximal flow number of N is \(|N| := \max \{|f| ~|~ f\) is a flow of \(N\}\) . For the flow network N , we define an associated directed graph \(G_N\) by (1) \(V(G_N)=V\) , (2) for any pair \((u,v) \in V\times V\) , there are exactly \(\left\lceil c(u,v) \right\rceil \) directed edges from u to v in \(G_N\) , where \(\left\lceil c \right\rceil \) is the least integer not less than c . One can check that the maximal flow number | N | of N satisfies \(|N|\le \alpha _{s\rightarrow t}(G_N) \le \beta _{s\rightarrow t}(G_N)\) . 1.2 Incidence ideal and incidence set Before stating the definition of the incidence ideal and the incidence set, we first recall the definition of elementary symmetric polynomials. For each \(l \ge 1\) , denote by \(e_l(x_1,\ldots ,x_m)\) the l th elementary symmetric polynomial in the variables \(x_1,\ldots ,x_m\) . That is, $$\begin{aligned} e_l(x_1,\ldots ,x_m) = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} 1 &{} \text {if } l=0, \\ \sum _{1\le i_1<i_2<\cdots <i_l\le m} x_{i_1}x_{i_2}\cdots x_{i_l} &{} \text {if } 1\le l\le m, \\ 0 &{} \text {if } l>m. \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$ (1.1) Definition 1.1 Let G be a directed graph. The polynomial ring \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) is \(\mathbb {Z}\) -graded so that every \(x \in E(G)\) is homogeneous of degree 1. For a vertex v of G , suppose \(x_1,\ldots ,x_m\) are the edges having v as their initial vertex, and \(y_1,\ldots ,y_n\) are the edges having v as their terminal vertex. Footnote 2 Set \(k_v = \max \{m,n\}\) . For \(1\le l \le k_v\) , define $$\begin{aligned} \delta _{v,l}:= e_l(x_1,\ldots ,x_m) - e_l(y_1,\ldots ,y_n) \in \mathbb {Z}[E(G)] \end{aligned}$$ (1.2) and call it the degree l incidence relation at v . Let \(\Delta _G := \{\delta _{v,l}~|~ v \in V(G), ~1\le l \le k_v\}\) be the set (counting multiplicity) of all incidence relations in G . Denote by I ( G ) the ideal of \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) generated by the set \(\Delta _G\) . We call I ( G ) the incidence ideal for G . Note that I ( G ) is a homogeneous ideal. Therefore, it defines a complex projective algebraic set $$\begin{aligned} P(G) := \{p\in \mathbb {CP}^{|E(G)|-1} ~|~ f(p)=0, \quad \forall ~f\in I(G)\}. \end{aligned}$$ We call P ( G ) the incidence set of G . Before stating our main result, we introduce the cycle spectrum of a directed graph. Definition 1.2 Let G be a directed graph. A collection \(\mathcal {C}\) of pairwise edge-disjoint directed cycles in G is called maximal if it is not a subcollection of any collection of pairwise edge-disjoint directed cycles in G . In other words, \(\mathcal {C}\) is maximal if, after removing all edges belonging to directed cycles in \(\mathcal {C}\) from G , the remaining directed graph is acyclic. Define \(\gamma _n(G)\) to be the number of maximal collections of pairwise edge-disjoint directed cycles in G containing exactly n cycles. We call the sequence \(\Gamma (G):= \{\gamma _n(G)\}_{n=1}^\infty \) the cycle spectrum of G . Note that any collection of \(\alpha (G)\) pairwise edge-disjoint directed cycles in G is maximal. But it is possible to have maximal collections of pairwise edge-disjoint directed cycles containing less than \(\alpha (G)\) cycles. Also, the cycle spectrum \(\Gamma (G)\) contains only finitely many nonzero terms since \(\gamma _n(G)=0\) if \(n > \alpha (G)\) . Our main result is that the incidence set determines the cycle packing number and the cycle spectrum. Theorem 1.3 Let G be any directed graph. Then: (1) P ( G ) is a union of finitely many linear subspaces of \(\mathbb {CP}^{|E(G)|-1}\) ; (2) \(\dim P(G) = \alpha (G)-1\) ; (3) \(\deg P(G)=\gamma _{\alpha (G)}(G)\) ; (4) \(\gamma _n(G)\) is equal to the number of irreducible components of P ( G ) of dimension \(n-1\) . Slightly modifying the definition of the incidence set, we get a method of determining the strong cycle packing number and the strong cycle spectrum defined below. Definition 1.4 Let G be a directed graph. For a vertex v of G , suppose \(x_1,\ldots ,x_m\) are the edges having v as their initial vertex, and \(y_1,\ldots ,y_n\) are the edges having v as their terminal vertex. Recall that \(k_v = \max \{m,n\}\) . The set of strong incidence relations of G at v is $$\begin{aligned} \tilde{\Delta }_v:= & {} \{e_1(x_1,\ldots ,x_m) - e_1(y_1,\ldots ,y_n)\}\cup \{e_l(x_1,\ldots ,x_m) ~| 2 \le l \le m\}\\&\cup \{e_l(y_1,\ldots ,y_n) ~|~ 2 \le l \le n\}. \end{aligned}$$ Denote by \(\tilde{I}(G)\) the ideal of \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) generated by the set \(\tilde{\Delta }_G:= \bigcup _{v\in V(G)}\tilde{\Delta }_v\) . We call \(\tilde{I}(G)\) the strong incidence ideal for G . Note that \(\tilde{I}(G)\) is a homogeneous ideal. Therefore, it defines a complex projective algebraic set $$\begin{aligned} \tilde{P}(G) := \{p\in \mathbb {CP}^{|E(G)|-1} ~|~ f(p)=0, \quad \forall ~f\in \tilde{I}(G)\}. \end{aligned}$$ We call \(\tilde{P}(G)\) the strong incidence set of G . A collection \(\mathcal {C}\) of pairwise disjoint directed cycles in G is called strongly maximal if it is not a subcollection of any collection of pairwise disjoint directed cycles in G . In other words, \(\mathcal {C}\) is strongly maximal if, after removing all edges incident at vertices of cycles in \(\mathcal {C}\) from G , the remaining directed graph is acyclic. Define \(\tilde{\gamma }_n(G)\) to be the number of strongly maximal collections of pairwise disjoint directed cycles in G containing exactly n cycles. We call the sequence \(\tilde{\Gamma }(G):= \{\tilde{\gamma }_n(G)\}_{n=1}^\infty \) the strong cycle spectrum of G . Clearly, we have that \(I(G) \subset \tilde{I}(G)\) , \(P(G) \supset \tilde{P}(G)\) and \(\tilde{\gamma }_n(G) \le \gamma _n(G)\) . Corollary 1.5 Let G be any directed graph. Then: (1) \(\tilde{P}(G)\) is a union of finitely many linear subspaces of \(\mathbb {CP}^{|E(G)|-1}\) ; (2) \(\dim \tilde{P}(G) = \tilde{\alpha }(G)-1\) ; (3) \(\deg \tilde{P}(G)=\tilde{\gamma }_{\tilde{\alpha }(G)}(G)\) ; (4) \(\tilde{\gamma }_n(G)\) is equal to the number of irreducible components of \(\tilde{P}(G)\) of dimension \(n-1\) . There are several general purpose software packages for algebraic geometry, such as CoCoA, Macaulay2 and Singular. One can use these packages to compute dimensions and degrees. So, for small directed graphs, Theorem 1.3 and Corollary 1.5 provide an automated method of computing \(\alpha (G)\) , \(\gamma _{\alpha (G)}(G)\) and \(\tilde{\alpha }(G)\) , \(\tilde{\gamma }_{\tilde{\alpha }(G)}(G)\) . A natural question is that, for what G , is P ( G ) or \(\tilde{P}(G)\) a projective variety, that is, an irreducible projective algebraic set? Knowing Theorem 1.3 , it is relatively easy to find a combinatorial answer to this question. Theorem 1.6 Let G be a directed graph. (1) The following statements are equivalent: (a) P ( G ) is a projective variety; (b) P ( G ) is a linear subspace of \(\mathbb {CP}^{|E(G)|-1}\) ; (c) G contains exactly \(\alpha (G)\) distinct directed cycles. (2) The following statements are equivalent: (a) \(\tilde{P}(G)\) is a projective variety; (b) \(\tilde{P}(G)\) is a linear subspace of \(\mathbb {CP}^{|E(G)|-1}\) ; (c) G contains exactly \(\tilde{\alpha }(G)\) distinct directed cycles. (3) If \(\tilde{P}(G)\) is a projective variety, then \(P(G)=\tilde{P}(G)\) . We will prove Theorem 1.3 , Corollary 1.5 and Theorem 1.6 in Sect. 2 . 1.3 Khovanov–Rozansky homology and its Krull dimension In [ 4 , 5 ], Khovanov and Rozansky introduced an innovative method of constructing link homologies whose graded Euler characteristics are versions of the HOMFLYPT polynomial. Such constructions are known as categorifications in knot theory. The homologies constructed by Khovanov and Rozansky are now called the Khovanov–Rozansky homology. Since their initial work, the Khovanov–Rozansky homology has been generalized and reinterpreted by many researchers. See, for example, [ 7 , 9 , 11 , 12 ] and many more. Khovanov and Rozansky’s construction is a two step process. First, they defined a Koszul matrix factorization for each MOY graph. Footnote 3 Then, using the crossing information in a link diagram, they constructed a chain complex of matrix factorizations for each link diagram. The hard part of their work is to prove that their homology is independent of the choice of the diagram of a link. The Khovanov–Rozansky homology of directed graphs defined below is a straightforward generalization of the first step in the construction of the HOMFLYPT version of the Khovanov–Rozansky homology of MOY graphs given in [ 9 ]. The only change is that we no longer require the graph to be MOY. Definition 1.7 Let G be a directed graph. Recall that \(\Delta _G\) is the set (counting multiplicity) of all incidence relations in G . The Khovanov–Rozansky chain complex \(\mathscr {C}_*(G)\) is the graded Koszul chain complex \(C_*^{\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]} (\Delta _G)\) over \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) defined by \(\Delta _G\) . Footnote 4 The Khovanov–Rozansky homology \(\mathscr {H}_*(G)\) of G is the homology of \(\mathscr {C}_*(G)\) . Note that, as a graded Koszul chain complex, \(\mathscr {C}_*(G)\) has a \(\mathbb {Z}\oplus \mathbb {Z}\) -grading. One is the homological grading, and the other is the \(\mathbb {Z}\) -grading of the underlying \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) -module. Clearly, \(\mathscr {H}_*(G)\) inherits this \(\mathbb {Z}\oplus \mathbb {Z}\) -grading. There is also the notion of the Khovanov–Rozansky homology over \(\mathbb {Q}\) . In this case, the chain complex is \(\mathscr {C}_*^\mathbb {Q}(G) := \mathbb {Q}\otimes _{\mathbb {Z}}\mathscr {C}_*(G)\) and the homology is the homology \(\mathscr {H}_*^\mathbb {Q}(G)\) of \(\mathscr {C}_*^\mathbb {Q}(G)\) . Lemma 1.8 If directed graphs G and \(G'\) are isomorphic, then \(\mathscr {C}_*(G) \cong \mathscr {C}_*(G')\) as chain complexes of graded \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) -modules. Therefore, \(\mathscr {H}_*(G) \cong \mathscr {H}_*(G')\) as \(\mathbb {Z}\oplus \mathbb {Z}\) -graded \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) -modules. Proof A graph isomorphism gives one-to-one correspondences \(V(G) \rightarrow V(G')\) and \(E(G) \rightarrow E(G')\) that, when combined, preserve the incidence relations. \(\square \) Next we recall the definition of the Krull dimension. Properties of the Krull dimension relevant to our proofs will be reviewed in Sect. 4 . Definition 1.9 Let R be a commutative ring with 1. The Krull dimension of R is $$\begin{aligned} \mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim R:= & {} \sup \{n\ge 0 ~| \text { there are } n+1 \text { prime ideals } \mathfrak {p}_0, \mathfrak {p}_1,\ldots ,\mathfrak {p}_n \text { of } R \text { such that } \mathfrak {p}_0 \\&\subsetneq \mathfrak {p}_1\subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq \mathfrak {p}_n \subsetneq R\}. \end{aligned}$$ For an R -module M , define \(\mathrm {ann}_R(M)=\{r\in R~|~ rm=0 \quad \forall m\in M\}\) , which is an ideal of R . Then $$\begin{aligned} \mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim _R M := \mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim (R/\mathrm {ann}_R(M)). \end{aligned}$$ It is possible to have \(\mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim R = +\infty \) . But this does not happen for the rings we will discuss in this paper. Also, for an ideal I of R , \(\mathrm {ann}_R(R/I)=I\) . So \(\mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim _R(R/I) = \mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim (R/I)\) , where the left-hand side is the Krull dimension of an R -module, and the right-hand side is the Krull dimension of a ring. The following fact can be found in many introductory books on projective algebraic geometry. Fact 1.10 For a homogeneous ideal I of \(\mathbb {Q}[x_0,\ldots ,x_n]\) , denote by X ( I ) the projective algebraic set $$\begin{aligned} X(I)= \{(x_0:\cdots :x_n) \in \mathbb {CP}^n ~|~ f(x_0,\ldots ,x_n)=0 ~(\forall ~f\in I)\}. \end{aligned}$$ Then \(\dim X(I) = \mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim \mathbb {Q}[x_0,\ldots ,x_n]/I-1\) . By Lemma 3.7 , for a directed graph G , its 0th Khovanov–Rozansky homology is the graded \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) -module \(\mathscr {H}_0 (G) \cong \mathbb {Z}[E(G)] / I(G)\) , and \(\mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim _{\mathbb {Z}[E]}\mathscr {H}_*(G) =\mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim _{\mathbb {Z}[E]}\mathscr {H}_0 (G)\) for any \(E \subset E(G)\) . So, in the discussions below, we state results in terms of Krull dimensions of \(\mathscr {H}_0 (G)\) instead of the whole Khovanov–Rozansky homology \(\mathscr {H}_*(G)\) . 1.4 Khovanov–Rozansky homology and directed cycles Theorem 1.11 Let G be a directed graph. 1. The following statements are equivalent: (1) G is acyclic; (2) \(\mathscr {H}_0 (G)\) is a finitely generated \(\mathbb {Z}\) -module; (3) \(\mathscr {H}_*(G)\) is a finitely generated \(\mathbb {Z}\) -module; (4) There is an \(n \ge 1\) such that \(x^n \in I(G)\) for all \(x \in E(G)\) , where I ( G ) is the incidence ideal of \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) ; (5) \(\mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim _{\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]} \mathscr {H}_0 (G) =1\) ; (6) \(\mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim _{\mathbb {Q}[E(G)]} \mathscr {H}_0^\mathbb {Q}(G) =0\) . 2. \(\alpha (G) = \mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim _{\mathbb {Q}[E(G)]} \mathscr {H}_0^\mathbb {Q}(G) \le \mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim _{\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]} \mathscr {H}_0 (G) -1 \le \beta (G)\) . In particular, if G is planar, then \(\alpha (G) = \mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim _{\mathbb {Q}[E(G)]} \mathscr {H}_0^\mathbb {Q}(G) = \mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim _{\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]} \mathscr {H}_0 (G) -1 = \beta (G)\) . As a by-product of the proof of Theorem 1.11 , we have the following general proposition. Proposition 1.12 For a directed graph G and \(E \subset E(G)\) , $$\begin{aligned} \alpha _E(G) \le \mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim _{\mathbb {Q}[E]} \mathscr {H}_0^\mathbb {Q}(G) \le \mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim _{\mathbb {Z}[E]} \mathscr {H}_0 (G) -1. \end{aligned}$$ If E is the subset of all edges incident at a vertex v of G , then we have stronger results. Theorem 1.13 Let G be a directed graph, and v a vertex in G . Denote by E ( v ) the set of all edges of G incident at v . 1. The following statements are equivalent: (1) There are no directed cycles in G containing v ; (2) \(\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]/\mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]}(\mathscr {H}_0 (G))\) is a finitely generated \(\mathbb {Z}\) -module; (3) \(\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]/\mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]}(\mathscr {H}_*(G))\) is a finitely generated \(\mathbb {Z}\) -module; (4) There is an \(n \ge 1\) such that \(x^n \in I(G)\) for all \(x \in E(v)\) , where I ( G ) is the incidence ideal of \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) ; (5) \(\mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim _{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]} \mathscr {H}_0 (G) =1\) ; (6) \(\mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim _{\mathbb {Q}[E(v)]} \mathscr {H}_0^\mathbb {Q}(G) =0\) . 2. \(\alpha _v(G) \le \mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim _{\mathbb {Q}[E(v)]} \mathscr {H}_0^\mathbb {Q}(G) \le \mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim _{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]} \mathscr {H}_0 (G) -1 \le \beta _v(G)\) . One can also detect directed cycles containing a particular edge using \(\mathscr {H}_0\) . Corollary 1.14 Let G be a directed graph, and x an edge in G . The following statements are equivalent: (1) There are no directed cycles in G containing x ; (2) \(\mathbb {Z}[x]/\mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[x]}(\mathscr {H}_0 (G))\) is a finitely generated \(\mathbb {Z}\) -module; (3) \(\mathbb {Z}[x]/\mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[x]}(\mathscr {H}_*(G))\) is a finitely generated \(\mathbb {Z}\) -module; (4) There is an \(n \ge 1\) such that \(x^n \in I(G)\) , where I ( G ) is the incidence ideal of \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) ; (5) \(\mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim _{\mathbb {Z}[x]} \mathscr {H}_0 (G) =1\) ; (6) \(\mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim _{\mathbb {Q}[x]} \mathscr {H}_0^\mathbb {Q}(G) =0\) . There is a directed cycle in G containing x if and only if \(\mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim _{\mathbb {Q}[x]} \mathscr {H}_0^\mathbb {Q}(G) = \mathrm {K}\text {-}\dim _{\mathbb {Z}[x]} \mathscr {H}_0 (G) -1 =1\) . Corollary 1.14 follows easily from Theorem 1.13 . We will prove Theorems 1.11 , 1.13 , Proposition 1.12 and Corollary 1.14 in Sect. 4 . Proof Follows immediately from Part 2 of Corollary 1.15 . \(\square \) 1.5 Undirected cycles The Khovanov–Rozansky homology also detects undirected cycles in a directed graph. Here, an undirected cycle in a directed graph G is a sequence $$\begin{aligned} v_0,x_0,v_1,x_1,\ldots ,x_{n-1},v_n,x_n,v_{n+1}=v_0 \end{aligned}$$ satisfying (1) \(v_0,v_1,\ldots ,v_n\) are pairwise distinct vertices of G , (2) each \(x_i\) is an edge of G either with initial vertex \(v_i\) and terminal vertex \(v_{i+1}\) or with initial vertex \(v_{i+1}\) and terminal vertex \(v_{i}\) Again, two such sequences represent the same undirected cycle if one is a circular permutation of the other. That is, the undirected cycle given by \(v_0,x_0,v_1,x_1,\ldots ,x_{n-1},v_n,x_n,v_0\) is the same as the one given by \(v_1,x_1,\ldots ,x_{n-1},v_n,x_n,v_0,x_0,v_1\) . We formulate our results on undirected cycles as Theorems 1.17 and 1.18 . Theorem 1.17 Let G be a directed graph. 1. The underlying undirected graph of G is a disjoint union of trees if and only if \(\mathscr {H}_*(G) \cong \mathscr {H}_0(G) \cong \mathbb {Z}\) as graded \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) -modules, where \(\mathbb {Z}\) is the graded \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) -module \(\mathbb {Z}= \mathbb {Z}[E(G)]/(E(G))\) , and ( E ( G )) is the homogeneous ideal of \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) generated by E ( G ). 2. G contains an undirected cycle if and only if \(\mathscr {H}_{0,1} (G) \ne 0\) , where \(\mathscr {H}_{0,1} (G)\) is the homogeneous component of \(\mathscr {H}_{0} (G)\) of module degree 1. 3. Define \(\alpha _\mathrm{undirected}(G)\) to be the maximal number of pairwise edge-disjoint undirected cycles in G , \(\beta _\mathrm{undirected}(G)\) to be the minimal number of edges in G whose removal from G destroys all undirected cycles in G . Then \(\alpha _\mathrm{undirected}(G) \le \mathrm {rank}\mathscr {H}_{0,1} (G) \le \beta _\mathrm{undirected}(G)\) , where \(\mathrm {rank}\mathscr {H}_{0,1} (G)\) is the rank of \(\mathscr {H}_{0,1} (G)\) as a \(\mathbb {Z}\) -module. Theorem 1.18 Let G be a directed graph and v a vertex of G . Denote by E ( v ) the set of edges of G incident at v . Define \(A(v) = \mathbb {Z}[E(v)]/(\mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]} \mathscr {H}_0 (G))\) , which is a graded \(\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]\) -module. 1. There are no undirected cycles in G containing v if and only if \(A(v) \cong \mathbb {Z}\) as graded \(\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]\) -modules, where \(\mathbb {Z}\) is the graded \(\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]\) -module \(\mathbb {Z}= \mathbb {Z}[E(v)]/(E(v))\) , and ( E ( v )) is the homogeneous ideal of \(\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]\) generated by E ( v ). 2. There is an undirected cycle in G containing v if and only if \(A_1(v) \ne 0\) , where \(A_1(v)\) is the homogeneous component of A ( v ) of module degree 1. 3. Define \(\alpha _\mathrm{undirected}(G,v)\) to be the maximal number of pairwise edge-disjoint undirected cycles in G , each of which contains v , \(\beta _\mathrm{undirected}(G,v)\) to be the minimal number of edges in G incident at v whose removal from G destroys all undirected cycles in G containing v . Then \(\alpha _\mathrm{undirected}(G,v) \le \mathrm {rank}A_1(v) \le \beta _\mathrm{undirected}(G,v)\) . The proofs of Theorems 1.17 and 1.18 have the same flavor as that of Theorems 1.11 and 1.13 , but are more elementary. We include these in Sect. 5 . It turns out that \(\mathscr {H}_{0,1} (G)\) is closely related to the vertex-edge incidence matrix. For a directed graph G , we order its vertices and edges. That is, we write \(V(G)=\{v_1,\ldots ,v_m\}\) and \(E(G)=\{x_1,\ldots ,x_n\}\) . Denote by \(\mathbb {Z}\cdot E(G)\) the free \(\mathbb {Z}\) -module generated by E ( G ). Identify the \(\mathbb {Z}\) -modules \(\mathbb {Z}\cdot E(G)\) and \(\mathbb {Z}^n\) by the isomorphism which identifies each \(x_j\) with the row vector with a single 1 at the j th position and 0’s at all other positions. The vertex-edge incidence matrix \(D=(d_{i,j})_{m\times n}\) of G is defined by $$\begin{aligned} d_{i,j}= {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} 1 &{} \text {if } v_i \text { is the terminal vertex, but not the initial vertex of } x_j,\\ -1 &{} \text {if } v_i \text { is the initial vertex, but not the terminal vertex of } x_j,\\ 0 &{} \text {otherwise.} \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$ Clearly, the above identification maps the degree 1 incidence relation \(\delta _{v,1}\) at a vertex v to the row in D corresponding to v . Note that \(\mathscr {H}_{0,1} (G)\) is isomorphic to \(\mathbb {Z}\cdot E(G)\) modulo all degree 1 incidence relations in G , which is isomorphic to \(\mathbb {Z}^n\) modulo the submodule generated by rows of the vertex-edge incidence matrix. In view of this, Theorem 1.17 implies that the following statements are equivalent: (1) There is an undirected cycle in G . (2) The submodule of \(\mathbb {Z}^n\) generated by rows of D is not \(\mathbb {Z}^n\) . (3) The submodule of \(\mathbb {Z}^n\) generated by rows of D has rank less than n . This and some other related results were known prior to our work. The following corollary is a re-formulation of Theorems 1.17 and 1.18 using linear algebra over \(\mathbb {Z}_2\) without referring to the Khovanov–Rozansky homology. Versions of this corollary were known prior to our work. Corollary 1.19 Let G be an undirected graph with finite vertex set V ( G ) and finite edge set E ( G ). For any \(v \in V(G)\) , denote by E ( v ) the set of edges in G incident at v and by L ( v ) the set of loops in G at v (edges connecting v to v ). Define \(\mathbb {Z}_2 \cdot E(G) := \bigoplus _{x \in E(G)} \mathbb {Z}_2 \cdot x\) , which is a linear space over the field \(\mathbb {Z}_2\) . Define S to be the subspace of \(\mathbb {Z}_2 \cdot E(G)\) spanned by \(\{\sum _{x\in E(v){\setminus } L(v)} x ~|~ v \in V(G)\}\) . 1. \(\alpha _\mathrm{undirected}(G) \le |E(G)|-\dim _{\mathbb {Z}_2} S \le \beta _\mathrm{undirected}(G)\) . Footnote 5 In particular, \(|E(G)|>\dim _{\mathbb {Z}_2} S\) if and only if there is an undirected cycle in G . 2. Define \(\mathbb {Z}_2 \cdot E(v) := \bigoplus _{x \in E(v)} \mathbb {Z}_2 \cdot x\) , which is a subspace of \(\mathbb {Z}_2 \cdot E(G)\) . Then $$\begin{aligned}&\alpha _\mathrm{undirected}(G,v) \le |E(v)|-\dim _{\mathbb {Z}_2} (\mathbb {Z}_2 \cdot E(v))\cap S \\&\quad = \dim _{\mathbb {Z}_2} (\mathbb {Z}_2 \cdot E(v)+ S) - \dim _{\mathbb {Z}_2} S \le \beta _\mathrm{undirected}(G,v). \end{aligned}$$ In particular, \(|E(v)|> \dim _{\mathbb {Z}_2} (\mathbb {Z}_2 \cdot E(v))\cap S\) if and only if there is an undirected cycle in G containing v . 3. For an edge x of G , there exists an undirected cycle in G containing x if and only if \(x \notin S\) . Parts 1 and 2 of Corollary 1.19 follow from Theorems 1.17 and 1.18 via an easy computation of \(\mathbb {Z}_2\otimes _{\mathbb {Z}} \mathscr {H}_{0,1} (G)\) and \(\mathbb {Z}_2\otimes _{\mathbb {Z}} A_1(v)\) . Part 3 follows from a simple direct argument. We include the proof of Corollary 1.19 in Sect. 5 . Part 3 of Corollary 1.19 explains how to use the vertex-edge incidence matrix D over \(\mathbb {Z}_2\) to detect the existence of undirected cycles in G containing a particular vertex or edge. Let \(D'\) be the reduced row echelon matrix over \(\mathbb {Z}_2\) obtained by row operations from D . Then the nonzero rows of \(D'\) form a basis for S . By Part 3 of Corollary 1.19 , an edge \(x_j\) of G is not contained in any undirected cycles in G if and only if \(x_j\) , viewed as a row vector in \(\mathbb {Z}_2^n\) , is equal to a row of \(D'\) . Therefore, a vertex v of G is not contained in any undirected cycles in G if and only if all edges incident at v are equal to rows of \(D'\) . 1.6 A homology for undirected graphs We should mention that one can modify the definition of the Khovanov–Rozansky homology to define a version of it for undirected graphs. Facts about projective algebraic geometry used in this section can be found in [ 1 ]. 2.1 Disassembling a directed graph Before stating our definition of disassemblies of a graph, let us first recall the concepts of directed trails and directed circuits. Given a directed graph G , a directed trail in G from a vertex u to a different vertex v is a sequence \(u=v_0,x_0,v_1,x_1,\ldots ,x_{n-1},v_n=v\) such that (1) \(x_0,x_1,\ldots ,x_{n-1}\) are pairwise distinct edges of G , (2) each \(x_i\) is an edge of G with initial vertex \(v_i\) and terminal vertex \(v_{i+1}\) . A directed circuit in G is a closed trail, that is, a sequence \(v_0,x_0,v_1,x_1,\ldots ,x_{n-1},v_n,x_n,v_{n+1}=v_0\) satisfying (1) \(x_0,x_1,\ldots ,x_n\) are pairwise distinct edges of G , (2) each \(x_i\) is an edge of G with initial vertex \(v_i\) and terminal vertex \(v_{i+1}\) . Two such sequences represent the same directed circuit if one is a circular permutation of the other. That is, the directed circuit given by \(v_0,x_0,v_1,x_1,\ldots ,x_{n-1},v_n,x_n,v_0\) is the same as the one given by \(v_1,x_1,\ldots ,x_{n-1},v_n,x_n,v_0,x_0,v_1\) . In paths and cycles, we disallow repeated vertices and, therefore, disallow repeated edges. But, in trails and circuits, we only disallow repeated edges, but allow repeated vertices. Also, we have the following observation. Lemma 2.1 The set of edges of a directed circuit is the set of edges of a collection of pairwise edge-disjoint directed cycles attached together at shared vertices. Definition 2.2 Let G be a directed graph. For a vertex v of G , let the in-degree of v be n and out-degree of v be m . Recall that \(k_v=\max \{m,n\}\) . Set \(l_v = \min \{m,n\}\) . To disassemble G at v is to split v into \(k_v\) vertices such that (1) \(l_v\) of these new vertices have in-degree 1 and out-degree 1. (2) \(k_v-l_v\) of these new vertices have degree 1 such that if \(m\ge n\) , then each of these degree 1 vertices has in-degree 0 and out-degree 1; if \(m<n\) , then each of these degree 1 vertices has in-degree 1 and out-degree 0. Of course, depending on how inward edges and outward edges are matched, there are many different ways to disassemble G at v . To disassemble G is to disassemble G at all vertices of G . Again, there are many ways to disassemble a directed graph. We call each graph resulted from disassembling G a disassembly of G and denote by \(\mathrm {Dis}(G)\) the set of all disassemblies of G . The following lemma is a series of simple observations about disassemblies. Lemma 2.3 Let G be a directed graph, and D a disassembly of G . (1) D is a directed graph whose vertices are all of degree 1 or 2. And, each vertex of degree 2 of D has in-degree 1 and out-degree 1. (2) D is a disjoint union of directed paths and directed cycles. (3) \(E(D)=E(G)\) and there is a natural graph homomorphism from D to G that maps each edge to itself and each vertex v in D the vertex in G used to create v . (4) Under the above natural homomorphism, each directed path in D is mapped to a directed trail in G , each directed cycle in D is mapped to a directed circuit in G , the collection of all directed cycles in D is mapped to a collection of pairwise edge-disjoint circuits in G . (5) \(\alpha (D) \le \alpha (G)\) and \(\alpha (D) = \alpha (G)\) if and only if the collection of all directed cycles in D is mapped to a collection of \(\alpha (G)\) pairwise edge-disjoint directed cycles in G by the natural homomorphism. Proof Parts (1–4) are obvious, we leave their proofs to the reader. Part (5) follows from Part (4) and Lemma 2.1 . \(\square \) Lemma 2.4 Let G be a directed graph. Given any collection \(\mathcal {C}\) of pairwise edge-disjoint directed circuits in G , there is a disassembly D of G such that every directed circuit in \(\mathcal {C}\) is the image of a directed cycle in D under the natural graph homomorphism from D to G . In particular, there is a disassembly D of G such that \(\alpha (D)=\alpha (G)\) . Proof To get such a D , one just needs to make sure that, when disassembling G , each pair of adjacent edges in each circuit in \(\mathcal {C}\) are matched together. Edges not contained in any circuits in \(\mathcal {C}\) can be matched in any possible way when disassembling G . For the second half of the lemma, just pick \(\mathcal {C}\) to be a collection of \(\alpha (G)\) pairwise edge-disjoint directed cycles in G . Then the conclusion follows from the first half and Part (5) of Lemma 2.3 . \(\square \) Lemma 2.5 Let G be a directed graph. (1) For any disassembly D of G , the incidence set P ( D ) of D is a linear subspace of dimension \(\alpha (D) -1\) of \(\mathbb {CP}^{|E(G)|-1}\) . (2) For any two disassemblies \(D_1\) and \(D_2\) of G , \(P(D_1)=P(D_2)\) as linear subspaces of \(\mathbb {CP}^{|E(G)|-1}\) if and only if, under the natural homomorphisms from \(D_1\) and \(D_2\) to G , the collections of all directed cycles in \(D_1\) and \(D_2\) are mapped to the same collection of pairwise edge-disjoint circuits in G . Proof For Part (1), note that P ( D ) is determined by the linear equations $$\begin{aligned} {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} x=0 &{} \text {if } x \text { is not an edge of any directed cycle in } D,\\ x=y &{} \text {if } x \text { and } y \text { are edges in the same directed cycle in } D. \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$ (2.1) The solution space of ( 2.1 ) in \(\mathbb {C}^{|E(G)|}\) is \(\alpha (D)\) -dimensional since there is a one-to-one correspondence between free variables in the solution and directed cycles in D . P ( D ) is the image of this solution space in \(\mathbb {CP}^{|E(G)|-1}\) . So P ( D ) is a linear subspace of dimension \(\alpha (D) -1\) of \(\mathbb {CP}^{|E(G)|-1}\) . Now consider Part (2). First, if collections of all directed cycles in \(D_1\) and \(D_2\) are mapped to the same collection of pairwise edge-disjoint circuits in G , then \(P(D_1)\) and \(P(D_2)\) are defined by the same linear equations. So \(P(D_1)=P(D_2)\) in this case. Next, assume that \(P(D_1)=P(D_2)\) . For an edge x of G and \(i=1,2\) , x is not contained in any directed cycle in \(D_i\) if and only if \(x=0\) at all points in \(P(D_i)\) . Since \(P(D_1)=P(D_2)\) , this implies that x is not contained in any directed cycle in \(D_1\) if and only if x is not contained in any directed cycle in \(D_2\) . Similarly, edges x and y are contained in the same directed cycle in \(D_i\) if and only if \(x=y\) at all points in \(P(D_i)\) and \(x\ne 0\) at some point in \(P(D_i)\) . Again, since \(P(D_1)=P(D_2)\) , this implies that x and y are contained in the same directed cycle in \(D_1\) if and only if x and y are contained in the same directed cycle in \(D_2\) . Thus, there is a bijection between directed cycles in \(D_1\) and directed cycles in \(D_2\) such that corresponding directed cycles have exactly the same edges. This implies that the collections of all directed cycles in \(D_1\) and \(D_2\) are mapped to the same collection of pairwise edge-disjoint circuits in G by the natural homomorphisms. \(\square \) 2.2 Structure of the incidence set In this subsection, we give an explicit description of the incidence set and prove Theorem 1.3 . First, we recall a simple corollary of Vieta’s Theorem. Lemma 2.6 Let \(x_1,\ldots ,x_n\) and \(y_1,\ldots ,y_n\) be two sequences of complex numbers. Then the following statements are equivalent. (1) \(e_k(x_1,\ldots ,x_n)=e_k(y_1,\ldots ,y_n)\) for \(k=1,\ldots ,n\) , where \(e_k\) is the k th elementary symmetric polynomial. (2) There is a bijection \(\sigma :\{1,\ldots ,n\}\rightarrow \{1,\ldots ,n\}\) such that \(x_i = y_{\sigma (i)}\) for \(i=1,\ldots ,n\) . Lemma 2.7 For every directed graph G , $$\begin{aligned} P(G) = \bigcup _{D\in \mathrm {Dis}(G)} P(D) \end{aligned}$$ as subsets of \(\mathbb {CP}^{|E(G)|-1}\) . Proof For a vertex v of G , suppose \(x_1,\ldots ,x_m\) are the edges having v as their initial vertex, and \(y_1,\ldots ,y_n\) are the edges having v as their terminal vertex. Recall that \(k_v=\max \{m,n\}\) . Define two sequences \(In(v)=\{y_{v,1},\ldots ,y_{v,{k_v}}\}\) and \(Out(v)=\{x_{v,1},\ldots ,x_{v,{k_v}}\}\) by \(y_{v,i}= {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} y_i &{} \text {if } i \le n,\\ 0 &{} \text {if } n<i\le k_v, \end{array}\right. }\) \(x_{v,i}= {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} x_i &{} \text {if } i \le m,\\ 0 &{} \text {if } m<i\le k_v. \end{array}\right. }\) From the definition of disassemblies, one can see that a point p is in \(\bigcup _{D\in \mathrm {Dis}(G)} P(D)\) if and only if that, for every vertex v , there is a bijection \(\sigma _v: \{1,\ldots ,k_v\} \rightarrow \{1,\ldots ,k_v\}\) such that p satisfies \(y_{v,i} = x_{v,\sigma _v(i)}\) for all \(v\in V(G)\) and \(1\le i \le k_v\) . By Lemma 2.6 , this is equivalent to that p satisfies all the incidence relations at all vertices of G . In other words, \(p\in P(G)\) . \(\square \) Lemma 2.8 Let G be a directed graph, and D a disassembly of G . Then P ( D ) is not a proper subset of \(P(D')\) for any \(D'\in \mathrm {Dis}(G)\) if and only if the natural homomorphism maps the directed cycles in D to a maximal collection of pairwise edge-disjoint directed cycles in G (in the sense of Definition 1.2 .) Proof For any disassembly D of G , denote by \(\mathcal {C}(D)\) the collection of pairwise edge-disjoint directed circuits in G given by the images of the directed cycles in D under the natural homomorphism. First assume that P ( D ) is not a proper subset of \(P(D')\) for any \(D'\in \mathrm {Dis}(G)\) . If \(\mathcal {C}(D)\) is not a maximal collection of pairwise edge-disjoint directed cycles in G , then 1. either there is a directed circuit \(C'\) in \(\mathcal {C}(D)\) that is not a directed cycle, 2. or there is a directed cycle \(C''\) in G that is edge-disjoint from all directed cycles in \(\mathcal {C}(D)\) . In Case 1, by Lemma 2.1 , one gets a collection \(\mathcal {C}'\) of pairwise edge-disjoint directed circuits in G by replacing \(C'\) with the pairwise edge-disjoint directed cycles whose edges coincide with those of \(C'\) . By Lemma 2.4 , there is a disassembly \(D'\) of G such that \(\mathcal {C}(D')\) contains \(\mathcal {C}'\) . It is easy to see that P ( D ) is a proper subset of \(P(D')\) . In Case 2, by Lemma 2.4 , there is a disassembly \(D''\) of G such that \(\mathcal {C}(D'')\) contains \(\mathcal {C}(D) \cup \{C''\}\) . It is easy to see that P ( D ) is a proper subset of \(P(D'')\) . This shows that \(\mathcal {C}(D)\) is a maximal collection of pairwise edge-disjoint directed cycles in G . Now assume that \(\mathcal {C}(D)\) is a maximal collection of pairwise edge-disjoint directed cycles in G . Let \(D'\) be a disassembly of G such that \(P(D) \subset P(D')\) . Suppose a directed circuit C in \(\mathcal {C}(D')\) contains an edge x that is not an edge of any directed cycle in \(\mathcal {C}(D)\) . Then we know that every point in \(P(D')\) satisfies \(y=x\) for every edge y of C . Since \(P(D) \subset P(D')\) , this implies that \(y=x=0\) in P ( D ) for every edge y of C . Thus, C is edge-disjoint from every directed cycle in \(\mathcal {C}(D)\) . This contradicts the assumption that \(\mathcal {C}(D)\) is a maximal. So the directed circuits in \(\mathcal {C}(D')\) do not contain any edge that is not contained in any directed cycle in \(\mathcal {C}(D)\) . On the other hand, if x is an edge of some directed cycle in \(\mathcal {C}(D)\) , then \(x\ne 0\) at some point in \(P(D) \subset P(D')\) . This implies that x is the edge of some circuit in \(\mathcal {C}(D')\) . Altogether, we have: Conclusion 1. The set of edges of all directed circuits in \(\mathcal {C}(D')\) is equal to the set of edges of all directed cycles in \(\mathcal {C}(D)\) . Let x and y be edges of the same directed circuit in \(\mathcal {C}(D')\) . Then \(x=y\) for all points in \(P(D') \supset P(D)\) . Thus, x and y are in the same directed cycle in \(\mathcal {C}(D)\) . Next, assume x and y are edges of different directed circuits \(C_x\) and \(C_y\) in \(\mathcal {C}(D')\) but are in the same directed cycle \(C_{x,y}\) in \(\mathcal {C}(D)\) . Proof Denote by \(\iota : R \rightarrow R[x]\) the standard inclusion \(\iota (r)=r\) . Then \(\pi _x \circ \iota = \mathrm {id}_R\) . Define an R -module map \(\varPhi : C_*^{R[x]}(x,f_1,\ldots ,f_k) \rightarrow C_*^R(\pi _x(f_1),\ldots ,\pi _x(f_k))\) by $$\begin{aligned} \varPhi (f \cdot 1_{(\varepsilon _0,\varepsilon _1,\ldots ,\varepsilon _k)}) = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \pi _x(f) \cdot 1_{(\varepsilon _1,\ldots ,\varepsilon _k)} &{} \text {if } \varepsilon _0=0, \\ 0 &{} \text {if } \varepsilon _0=1, \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$ where \(f \in R[x]\) and \((\varepsilon _0,\varepsilon _1,\ldots ,\varepsilon _k) \in \{0,1\}^{\times (k+1)}\) . Also, define an R -module map \(\varPsi :C_*^R(\pi _x(f_1),\ldots ,\pi _x(f_k)) \rightarrow C_*^{R[x]}(x,f_1,\ldots ,f_k)\) by $$\begin{aligned} \varPsi (r \cdot 1_{(\varepsilon _1,\ldots ,\varepsilon _k)}) = \iota (r) \cdot 1_{(0,\varepsilon _1,\ldots ,\varepsilon _k)}, \end{aligned}$$ where \(r \in R\) and \((\varepsilon _1,\ldots ,\varepsilon _k) \in \{0,1\}^{\times k}\) . Then \(\varPhi \) and \(\varPsi \) are chain maps preserving the homological grading and the R -module grading, \(\varPhi \circ \varPsi = \mathrm {id}_{C_*^R(\pi _x(f_1),\ldots ,\pi _x(f_k))}\) , \(C_*^{R[x]}(x,f_1,\ldots ,f_k) = \ker \varPhi \oplus \mathrm {Im}\varPsi \) , with \(\varPsi \circ \varPhi |_{\ker \varPhi } =0\) and \(\varPsi \circ \varPhi |_{\mathrm {Im}\varPsi } = \mathrm {id}_{\mathrm {Im}\varPsi }\) . Next, define an R -module map \(H: C_*^{R[x]}(x,f_1,\ldots ,f_k) \rightarrow C_*^{R[x]}(x,f_1,\ldots ,f_k)\) by $$\begin{aligned} H(f \cdot 1_{(1,\varepsilon _1,\ldots ,\varepsilon _k)})= & {} 0, \\ H((r+xf) \cdot 1_{(0,\varepsilon _1,\ldots ,\varepsilon _k)})= & {} f \cdot 1_{(1,\varepsilon _1,\ldots ,\varepsilon _k)}, \end{aligned}$$ where \(r \in R\) , \(f \in R[x]\) and \((\varepsilon _1,\ldots ,\varepsilon _k) \in \{0,1\}^{\times k}\) . It is straightforward to check that $$\begin{aligned} (d\circ H + H \circ d)|_{\ker \varPhi }= & {} \mathrm {id}_{\ker \varPhi }, \\ (d\circ H + H \circ d)|_{\mathrm {Im}\varPsi }= & {} 0. \end{aligned}$$ This implies that \(\mathrm {id}_{C_*^{R[x]}(x,f_1,\ldots ,f_k)} - \varPsi \circ \varPhi = d\circ H + H \circ d\) . Thus, we have shown that \(\varPhi \) and \(\varPsi \) are homotopy equivalences preserving the R -module grading. Therefore, \(C_*^{R[x]}(x,f_1,\ldots ,f_k)\) and \(C_*^R(\pi _x(f_1),\ldots ,\pi _x(f_k))\) are homotopic as chain complexes of graded R -modules. \(\square \) 3.2 Khovanov–Rozansky homology First, we give a more “localized” description of the Khovanov–Rozansky homology of directed graphs, which is closer in spirit to the original definition of the Khovanov–Rozansky homology in knot theory. We stop when we reach a graph \(C'\) that is (1) either a directed cycle with a single removable vertex and a single loop edge, (2) or an undirected cycle with no removable vertices and a positive even number of non-removable vertices. By Lemma 3.10 , $$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {H}_{0} (C) \cong \mathscr {H}_{0} (C') \cong {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \mathbb {Z}[x] &{} \text {in case (1),} \\ \mathbb {Z}[x]/(x^2) &{} \text {in case (2).} \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$ In both cases, \(\mathscr {H}_{0,1} (C') \ne 0\) . Then the aforementioned surjective homomorphism \(\mathscr {H}_{0} (G) \rightarrow \mathscr {H}_{0} (C) \cong \mathscr {H}_{0,1} (C')\) preserving the module grading implies that \(\mathscr {H}_{0,1} (G) \ne 0\) . This proves (b) and completes the proof of Parts 1 and 2 of Theorem 1.17 . Next, we prove Part 3 of Theorem 1.17 . Let \(\alpha =\alpha _\mathrm{undirected}(G)\) . Fix a collection \(\{C_1,\ldots ,C_\alpha \}\) of pairwise edge-disjoint undirected cycles in G and a collection of edges \(\{x_1,\ldots ,x_\alpha \} \subset E(G)\) such that \(x_i\) is contained in \(C_i\) . Note that \(x_i \ne x_j\) if \(i\ne j\) since the collection \(\{C_1,\ldots ,C_{\alpha }\}\) is pairwise edge-disjoint. Denote by \(G'\) the subgraph of G consisting of the vertices and edges of the undirected cycles in this collection. Then, by Lemma 3.8 , there is a surjective homomorphism \(\mathscr {H}_{0} (G) \rightarrow \mathscr {H}_{0} (G')\) preserving the module grading. For each vertex of degree 2 k of \(G'\) split it into k vertices of degree 2 such that, at each of these new vertices, there are two edges of the same undirected cycle in the above collection. This gives a new directed graph \(G''\) that is the disjoint union of the undirected cycles \(C_1,\ldots ,C_\alpha \) . By Lemma 3.11 , there is a surjective homomorphism \(\mathscr {H}_{0} (G') \rightarrow \mathscr {H}_{0} (G'')\) preserving the module grading. Similar to the discussion in the proof of (b) above, we get $$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {H}_{0} (C_i) \cong {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \mathbb {Z}[x_i] &{} \text {if all vertices of } C_i \text { are removable,} \\ \mathbb {Z}[x_i]/(x_i^2) &{} \text {if not all vertices of } C_i \text { are removable.} \\ \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$ But \(\mathscr {H}_{0} (G'') \cong \mathscr {H}_{0} (C_1)\otimes _{\mathbb {Z}} \cdots \otimes _{\mathbb {Z}} \mathscr {H}_{0} (C_\alpha )\) . So, over \(\mathbb {Z}\) , \(\mathscr {H}_{0,1} (G'')\) has rank \(\alpha \) and is spanned by \(x_1,\ldots ,x_\alpha \) . The surjective homomorphisms \(\mathscr {H}_{0} (G) \rightarrow \mathscr {H}_{0} (G')\rightarrow \mathscr {H}_{0} (G'')\) preserving the module grading then imply that \(\mathrm {rank}\mathscr {H}_{0,1} (G) \ge \mathrm {rank}\mathscr {H}_{0,1} (G') \ge \mathrm {rank}\mathscr {H}_{0,1} (G'') = \alpha \) . It remains to show that \(\mathrm {rank}\mathscr {H}_{0,1} (G) \le \beta _\mathrm{undirected}(G)\) . Let \(\beta = \beta _\mathrm{undirected}(G)\) . Fix a set \(\{y_1,\ldots ,y_\beta \}\) of edges of G whose removal from G destroys all undirected cycles in G . Denote by \(\hat{G}\) the directed graph obtained from G by removing the edges \(y_1,\ldots ,y_\beta \) . Then \(\hat{G}\) contains no undirected cycles. By Lemma 3.8 , \(\mathscr {H}_{0} (\hat{G}) \cong \mathscr {H}_{0} (G)/((y_1,\ldots ,y_\beta )\cdot \mathscr {H}_{0} (G))\) , where \((y_1,\ldots ,y_\beta )\) is the ideal of \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) generated by \(y_1,\ldots ,y_\beta \) . So \(\mathscr {H}_{0,1} (\hat{G}) \cong \mathscr {H}_{0,1} (G)/\left\langle y_1,\ldots ,y_\beta \right\rangle \) , where \(\left\langle y_1,\ldots ,y_\beta \right\rangle \) is the \(\mathbb {Z}\) -submodule of \(\mathscr {H}_{0,1} (G)\) spanned by \(y_1,\ldots ,y_\beta \) . But, by Part 1, \(\mathscr {H}_{0,1} (\hat{G})=0\) . So \(\mathscr {H}_{0,1} (G) = \left\langle y_1,\ldots ,y_\beta \right\rangle \) and, therefore, \(\mathrm {rank}\mathscr {H}_{0,1} (G) \le \beta \) . This completes the proof of Part 3. \(\square \) Proof of Theorem 1.18 Similar to the proof of Theorem 1.17 , to prove Parts 1 and 2, we only need to show that (a) There are no undirected cycles in G containing v \(\Rightarrow \) \(A(v) \cong \mathbb {Z}\) . (b) There is an undirected cycle in G containing v \(\Rightarrow \) \(A_1(v) \ne 0\) . Assume that there are no undirected cycles in G containing v . Denote by \(G_0\) the connected component of G containing v . Consider the graph \(G_0'\) given by \(V(G_0')= V(G_0)\) and \(E(G_0')= E(G_0){\setminus } E(v)\) . Since there are no undirected cycles in G containing v , there is exactly one edge in G connecting v and each connected component of \(G_0'\) that is not v itself. Now define a graph \(\hat{G}_0\) by \(E(\hat{G}_0) = E(G_0)\) and \(V(\hat{G}_0) = V(G_0)/\sim \) , where \(\sim \) is the equivalence relation \(u \sim w\) if and only if u and w are in the same connected component of \(G_0'\) . Then \(\hat{G}_0\) satisfies: If \(u \in V(\hat{G}_0)\) and \(u \ne v\) , then there is a single edge in \(\hat{G}_0\) connecting u and v . If \(u,w \in V(\hat{G}_0)\) and \(u \ne v\) , \(w \ne v\) , \(u \ne w\) , then there are no edges in \(\hat{G}_0\) connecting u and w . There are no loops in \(\hat{G}_0\) at v . Let \(\hat{G} = (G {\setminus } G_0) \sqcup \hat{G}_0\) . By Lemmas 4.13 and 4.14 , \(\mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]} \mathscr {H}_0 (\hat{G})=(E(v))\) , where ( E ( v )) is the homogeneous ideal of \(\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]\) generated by E ( v ). Thus, \(\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]/(\mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]} \mathscr {H}_0 (\hat{G})) \cong \mathbb {Z}\) . But, by Lemma 3.11 , there is a surjective \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) -module homomorphism \(\mathscr {H}_0 (\hat{G}) \rightarrow \mathscr {H}_0 (G)\) . So, by Lemma 4.1 , $$\begin{aligned} (E(v)) = \mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]} \mathscr {H}_0 (\hat{G}) \subset \mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]} \mathscr {H}_0 (G) = \mathbb {Z}[E(v)]\cap I(G) \subset (E(v)), \end{aligned}$$ where I ( G ) is the incidence ideal for G of \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) . Therefore, \(\mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]} \mathscr {H}_0 (G) = (E(v))\) and $$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {Z}[E(v)]/(\mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]} \mathscr {H}_0 (G)) \cong \mathbb {Z}. \end{aligned}$$ This proves (a). Now assume that there is an undirected cycle in G containing v . Then \(\alpha _v:=\alpha _\mathrm{undirected}(G,v) >0\) . Fix a collection of \(\{C_1,\ldots ,C_{\alpha _v}\}\) of pairwise edge-disjoint undirected cycles in G containing v . For each \(C_i\) , pick an \(x_i \in E(v)\) contained in \(C_i\) . Note that \(x_i \ne x_j\) if \(i\ne j\) since the collection \(\{C_1,\ldots ,C_{\alpha _v}\}\) is pairwise edge-disjoint. Let \(G'\) be the graph given by \(V(G')=\{u\in V(G)~|~ u\) is contained in \(C_i\) for some \(i=1,\ldots ,\alpha _v\}\) , \(E(G')=\{x\in E(G)~|~ x\) is contained in \(C_i\) for some \(i=1,\ldots ,\alpha _v\}\) . For each vertex u in \(G'\) of degree 2 k in \(G'\) , split it into k vertices, each of which is the vertex where two edges of the same \(C_i\) are incident. This changes \(G'\) to another graph \(G''\) . Similar to the computation in the proof of Theorem 1.17 , we have that $$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {H}_{0} (C_i) \cong {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \mathbb {Z}[x_i] &{} \text {if all vertices of } C_i \text { are removable,} \\ \mathbb {Z}[x_i]/(x_i^2) &{} \text {if not all vertices of } C_i \text { are removable,} \\ \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$ and \(\mathscr {H}_{0} (G'') \cong \mathscr {H}_{0} (C_1)\otimes _{\mathbb {Z}} \cdots \otimes _{\mathbb {Z}} \mathscr {H}_{0} (C_{\alpha _v})\) . If \(x\in E(v)\) is an edge of a \(C_i\) , then i is uniquely determined by x since \(C_1,\ldots ,C_{\alpha _v}\) are pairwise edge-disjoint. Define \(\nu (x)\) to be the parity of the number of non-removable vertices of \(C_i\) between x and \(x_i\) , which is well-defined since the total number of non-removable vertices on \(C_i\) is even. Using this notation, \(\mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]} (\mathscr {H}_{0} (G''))\) is the ideal of \(\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]\) generated by the set \((E(v){\setminus } E(G'')) \cup S_1 \cup S_2\) , where $$\begin{aligned} S_1= & {} \{x_i-(-1)^{\nu (x)}x~|~x\in E(v) \cap E(G''),~1\le i \le \alpha _v \text { and } x \text { is contained in } C_i\}, \\ S_2= & {} \{x_i^2~|~ 1\le i \le \alpha _v \text { and } C_i \text { contains a non-removable vertex}\}, \end{aligned}$$ This shows that \(\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]/ \mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]} (\mathscr {H}_{0} (G''))\cong \mathbb {Z}[x_1,\ldots ,x_{\alpha _v}]/ I_2\) , where \(I_2\) is the ideal of \(\mathbb {Z}[x_1,\ldots ,x_{\alpha _v}]\) generated by \(S_2\) . But, by Lemmas 3.8 and 3.11 , there are surjective \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) -module maps \(\mathscr {H}_{0} (G) \rightarrow \mathscr {H}_{0} (G') \rightarrow \mathscr {H}_{0} (G'')\) . So, by Lemma 4.1 , \(\mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]}\mathscr {H}_{0} (G) \subset \mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]}\mathscr {H}_{0} (G') \subset \mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]}\mathscr {H}_{0} (G'')\) . Thus, there is a surjective \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) -module map $$\begin{aligned} A(v)= & {} \mathbb {Z}[E(v)]/\mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]}(\mathscr {H}_{0} (G)) \rightarrow \mathbb {Z}[E(v)]/\mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]}(\mathscr {H}_{0} (G''))\\\cong & {} \mathbb {Z}[x_1,\ldots ,x_{\alpha _v}]/ I_2. \end{aligned}$$ In particular, it follows that \(\mathrm {rank}A_1(v) \ge \alpha _v >0\) . This proves (b) and that \(\mathrm {rank}A_1(v) \ge \alpha _\mathrm{undirected}(G,v)\) . So far, we have proved Parts 1, 2 and half of Part 3. It remains to show that \(\mathrm {rank}A_1(v) \le \beta _\mathrm{undirected}(G,v)\) . Write \(\beta _v = \beta _\mathrm{undirected}(G,v)\) and fix a set \(\{y_1,\ldots ,y_{\beta _v}\}\) of \(\beta _v\) edges in G incident at v , whose removal destroys all undirected cycles in G containing v . Let \(\tilde{G}\) be the graph with \(V(\tilde{G}) =V(G)\) and \(E(\tilde{G})=E(G){\setminus } \{y_1,\ldots ,y_{\beta _v}\}\) . It is clear that: There are no undirected cycles in \(\tilde{G}\) containing v . \(\mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]} \mathscr {H}_{0} (\tilde{G}) = \mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]}\mathscr {H}_{0} (G) + (y_1,\ldots ,y_{\beta _v})\) , where \((y_1,\ldots ,y_{\beta _v})\) is the ideal of \(\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]\) generated by \(\{y_1,\ldots ,y_{\beta _v}\}\) . Denote by J the ideal of A ( v ) generated by \(\{y_1,\ldots ,y_{\beta _v}\}\) . Then, by Part 1, $$\begin{aligned} A(v)/J\cong & {} \mathbb {Z}[E(V)]/(\mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]}\mathscr {H}_{0} (G) + (y_1,\ldots ,y_{\beta _v})) \\= & {} \mathbb {Z}[E(V)]/\mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]} \mathscr {H}_{0} (\tilde{G}) \cong \mathbb {Z}, \end{aligned}$$ which does not contain homogeneous elements of positive degrees. In particular, \(A_1(v)/\left\langle y_1,\ldots ,y_{\beta _v}\right\rangle \cong 0\) , where \(\left\langle y_1,\ldots ,y_{\beta _v}\right\rangle \) is the \(\mathbb {Z}\) -submodule of \(A_1(v)\) spanned by \(\{y_1,\ldots ,y_{\beta _v}\}\) . So \(A_1(v)=\left\langle y_1,\ldots ,y_{\beta _v}\right\rangle \) and \(\mathrm {rank}A_1(v) \le \beta _v=\beta _\mathrm{undirected}(G,v)\) . \(\square \) Proof of Corollary 1.19 Give each edge in G a direction. This makes G a directed graph. Recall that \(\mathscr {H}_0(G) \cong \mathbb {Z}[E(G)]/I(G)\) , where I ( G ) is the incidence ideal of \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) . Note that \(\delta _{v,l}\) is a homogeneous polynomial of degree l . So, as \(\mathbb {Z}\) -modules \(\mathscr {H}_{0,1}(G) \cong \mathbb {Z}\cdot E(G)/M\) , where \(\mathbb {Z}\cdot E(G):= \bigoplus _{x\in E(G)} \mathbb {Z}\cdot x\) and M is the \(\mathbb {Z}\) -submodule of \(\mathbb {Z}\cdot E(G)\) generated by \(\{\delta _{v,1}~|~ v \in V(G)\}\) . In \(\mathbb {Z}_2 \cdot E(G) \cong \mathbb {Z}\cdot E(G)/2(\mathbb {Z}\cdot E(G))\) , \(\delta _{v,1} = \sum _{x\in E(v){\setminus } L(v)} x\) . Thus, under the standard quotient map \(\mathbb {Z}\cdot E(G) \rightarrow \mathbb {Z}_2 \cdot E(G)\) , M is mapped onto S . This implies that \(\mathbb {Z}_2 \otimes _\mathbb {Z}\mathscr {H}_{0,1}(G) \cong \mathbb {Z}_2 \cdot E(G) /S\) . So, by Theorem 1.17 , \(|E(G)|-\dim _{\mathbb {Z}_2} S=\dim _{\mathbb {Z}_2} (\mathbb {Z}_2 \cdot E(G) /S) = \dim _{\mathbb {Z}_2} \mathbb {Z}_2 \otimes _\mathbb {Z}\mathscr {H}_{0,1}(G) \ge \mathrm {rank}\mathscr {H}_{0,1}(G) \ge \alpha _\mathrm{undirected}(G)\) . Repeat the last paragraph of the proof of Theorem 1.17 over \(\mathbb {Z}_2\) , one gets that \(|E(G)|-\dim _{\mathbb {Z}_2} S=\dim _{\mathbb {Z}_2} (\mathbb {Z}_2 \cdot E(G) /S) \le \beta _\mathrm{undirected}(G)\) . This proves Part 1 of Corollary 1.19 . Recall that \(A(v) := \mathbb {Z}[E(v)]/(\mathrm {ann}_{\mathbb {Z}[E(v)]} \mathscr {H}_0 (G))\) in Theorem 1.18 . So \(A_1(v) \cong \mathbb {Z}\cdot E(v) / ((\mathbb {Z}\cdot E(v)) \cap M)\) . This implies that \(\mathbb {Z}_2 \otimes _\mathbb {Z}A_1(v) \cong \mathbb {Z}_2 \cdot E(v) / ((\mathbb {Z}_2 \cdot E(v)) \cap S)\) . So, similar to Part 1, Part 2 of Corollary 1.19 follows from Theorem 1.18 and the last paragraph of its proof. It remains to prove Part 3. First, assume x is contained in an undirected cycle C in G . Modulo \(\mathbb {Z}_2\cdot E(G)\) by the subspace spanned by \(\{y \in E(G)~|~ y \notin E(C)\}\) . This gives a quotient map from \(\mathbb {Z}_2\cdot E(G)\) to \(\mathbb {Z}_2\cdot E(C)\) , which maps S to the subspace S ( E ) of \(\mathbb {Z}_2\cdot E(C)\) spanned by \(\{y+x ~|~ y \in E(C)\}\) . Clearly, \((\mathbb {Z}_2\cdot E(C))/S(E) \cong \mathbb {Z}_2 \cdot x\) . So \(x \notin S(E)\) as a vector in \(\mathbb {Z}_2\cdot E(C)\) . Thus, \(x \notin S\) as a vector in \(\mathbb {Z}_2\cdot E(G)\) . Now assume that x is not contained in any undirected cycles in G . Denote by \(G'\) the undirected graph obtained from G by removing the edge x . Since there are no undirected cycles in G containing x , the two vertices of x belong to two different connected components of \(G'\) . Let \(G'_0\) be one of these two connected components. Then \(x = \sum _{v \in V(G'_0)} \sum _{y \in E(v){\setminus } L(v)} y\) , where \(E(v){\setminus } L(v)\) is the set of non-loop edges in G (not just those in \(G'\) or \(G'_0\) ) incident at v . This is because each y in this sum is either x , which appears once in this sum, or a non-loop edge of \(G'_0\) , which appears twice. This shows that, as a vector in \(\mathbb {Z}_2\cdot E(G)\) , \(x \in S\) . \(\square \) 5.2 Properties of \(\mathscr {U}_*\) Next, we state some basic properties of \(\mathscr {U}_*\) and sketch a proof of Proposition 1.21 . Lemma 5.1 Let G be an undirected graph. 1. \(\mathscr {U}_0 (G) \cong \mathbb {Z}[E(G)]/(\varOmega _G)\) , where \((\varOmega _G)\) is the ideal of \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) generated by the set \(\varOmega _G = \{e_l(E(v))~|~v\in V(G), ~1 \le l \le \deg v\}\) . 2. \(\mathscr {U}_*(G)\) is a finitely generated \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]/(\varOmega _G)\) -module. 3. \(\mathscr {U}_*(G)\) is a finitely generated \(\mathbb {Z}\) -module if and only if \(\mathscr {U}_0 (G)\) is a finitely generated \(\mathbb {Z}\) -module. The proof of Lemma 5.1 is similar to that of Lemma 3.7 . Lemma 5.2 Let G be an undirected graph and \(\hat{G}\) the undirected graph obtained from G by removing a single edge \(x\in E(G)\) incident at \(u,v \in V(G)\) . Under the standard identification \(\mathbb {Z}[E(\hat{G})]\cong \mathbb {Z}[E(G)]/(x)\) , $$\begin{aligned} U_*(\hat{G}) \otimes _{\mathbb {Z}[E(\hat{G})]} U \cong U_*(G)/x\cdot U_*(G) \end{aligned}$$ as graded Koszul chain complexes over \(\mathbb {Z}[E(\hat{G})]\) , where U is the graded Koszul chain complex $$\begin{aligned} U := {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} (0 \rightarrow \underbrace{\mathbb {Z}[E(\hat{G})]\{\deg u\}}_{1} \xrightarrow {0} \underbrace{\mathbb {Z}[E(\hat{G})]}_{0} \rightarrow 0) \otimes _{\mathbb {Z}[E(\hat{G})]} (0 \rightarrow \underbrace{\mathbb {Z}[E(\hat{G})]\{\deg v\}}_{1} \xrightarrow {0} \underbrace{\mathbb {Z}[E(\hat{G})]}_{0} \rightarrow 0) &{} \text {if } u \ne v, \\ (0 \rightarrow \underbrace{\mathbb {Z}[E(\hat{G})]\{\deg u\}}_{1} \xrightarrow {0} \underbrace{\mathbb {Z}[E(\hat{G})]}_{0} \rightarrow 0) \otimes _{\mathbb {Z}[E(\hat{G})]} (0 \rightarrow \underbrace{\mathbb {Z}[E(\hat{G})]\{\deg u -1\}}_{1} \xrightarrow {0} \underbrace{\mathbb {Z}[E(\hat{G})]}_{0} \rightarrow 0) &{} \text {if } u = v. \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$ In particular, \(\mathscr {U}_0 (\hat{G}) \cong \mathscr {U}_0 (G) /x \cdot \mathscr {U}_0 (G)\) as graded \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) -modules under the standard identification \(\mathbb {Z}[E(\hat{G})]\cong \mathbb {Z}[E(G)]/(x)\) . The proof of Lemma 5.2 is straightforward and similar to that of Lemma 3.8 . Lemma 5.3 Let G be an undirected graph, v a vertex of G of degree 1 and x the edge incident at v . Denote by \(\hat{G}\) the undirected graph obtained from G by removing v and x . Then, under the standard identification \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]=\mathbb {Z}[E(\hat{G})][x]\) , \(U_*(G)\) and \(U_*(\hat{G})\) are homotopic as chain complexes of graded \(\mathbb {Z}[E(\hat{G})]\) -modules. In particular, \(\mathscr {U}_*(G) \cong \mathscr {U}_*(\hat{G})\) as \(\mathbb {Z}\oplus \mathbb {Z}\) -graded \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) -modules under the standard identification \(\mathbb {Z}[E(\hat{G})]\cong \mathbb {Z}[E(G)]/(x)\) . The proof of Lemma 5.3 is similar to that of Lemma 3.9 . Lemma 5.4 Let G be an undirected graph, and u , v two distinct vertices of G . Denote by \(G_{u \# v}\) the undirected graph obtained from G by identifying u and v into a single vertex. Note that there is a natural one-to-one correspondence between E ( G ) and \(E(G_{u \# v})\) , which gives an identification \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]=\mathbb {Z}[E(G_{u \# v})]\) . Under this identification, there is a surjective \(\mathbb {Z}[E(G)]\) -module homomorphism \(\mathscr {U}_0 (G_{u \# v}) \rightarrow \mathscr {U}_0 (G)\) . The proof of Lemma 5.4 is similar to that of Lemma 3.11 . Lemma 5.5 Let G be an undirected graph. Then \(\mathscr {U}_0(G)\) is a finitely generated \(\mathbb {Z}\) -module. The proof of Lemma 5.5 is similar to that of Corollary 4.11 . With the above lemmas, we can sketch a proof of Proposition 1.21 . Sketch of a proof of Proposition 1.21 Part 1 follows from Lemmas 5.1 and 5.5 . Proof of Parts 2–4 is extremely similar to that of Theorem 1.17 . One just needs to replace Lemmas 3.7 – 3.11 in that proof by Lemmas 5.1 – 5.4 . \(\square \) Notes Clearly, disjoint directed cycles are also edge-disjoint.
