                                                      Degree bounds for the toric ideal of a matroid

                                                    Abstract. Describing minimal generating set of a toric ideal, or the minimum
                                                    degree in which it is generated, is a well-studied and diﬃcult problem. In 1980
                                                    White conjectured that the toric ideal of a matroid is generated by quadratic
                                                    binomials corresponding to symmetric exchanges. In particular, that it is
                                                    generated in degree 2.
                                                         We prove that the toric ideal associated to a matroid of rank r is generated
                                                    in degree at most (r + 3)!. As a corollary we obtain that checking if White’s
                                                    conjecture is true for matroids of a ﬁxed rank is a decidable problem.

                                           The toric ideal of a matroid M , denoted by IM , is the kernel of the map ϕM .
                                               Neil White made three conjectures of growing diﬃculty that describe generators
                                           of the ideal IM .
                                               Conjecture 1 (White 1980, [11]). The toric ideal of a matroid is generated
                                           in degree 2.
                                                The family B of bases of M satisﬁes symmetric exchange property (the reader
                                           is referred to [9] for background of matroid theory, and to [7] for other exchange
                                           properties). That is, for every bases B1 , B2 and e ∈ B1 \B2 there exists f ∈ B2 \B1 ,
                                           such that both sets B1′ = (B1 \ e) ∪ f and B2′ = (B2 \ f ) ∪ e are bases. In this
                                           case we say that the quadratic binomial yB1 yB2 − yB1′ yB2′ corresponds to symmetric
                                           exchange. It is clear that such binomials belong to the ideal IM .

    Conjecture 2 (White 1980, [11]). The toric ideal of a matroid is generated
by quadratic binomials corresponding to symmetric exchanges.
    The third and the strongest among White’s conjectures describing generators
of the ideal IM turned out to be equivalent to Conjecture 2 (see the discussion in
Section 4 of [6]).
    The original conjectures due to White were expressed in the combinatorial
language. Conjecture 1 asserts that if two multisets of bases of a matroid have
equal union (as a multiset), then one can pass between them by a sequence of
steps, in each step exchanging two bases for another two bases of the same union
(as a multiset). In Conjecture 2 additionally each step corresponds to a symmetric
exchange. Therefore White’s conjectures do not depend on the ﬁeld K.
    Conjectures 1 and 2 are known to be true for many special classes of matroids:
graphic matroids [1], strongly base orderable matroids [6] (so also for transversal
matroids), sparse paving matroids [2], and for matroids of rank at most 3 [5] (see
also other related papers [3, 4, 10]).
    The ﬁrst result valid for arbitrary matroids was the conﬁrmation of White’s
conjecture up to saturation. Let JM be the ideal generated by quadratic binomials
corresponding to symmetric exchanges. Clearly, JM ⊂ IM and Conjecture 2 asserts
that the ideals JM and IM are equal. Recall that two homogeneous ideals are equal
up saturation with respect to the ideal generated by all variables if and only if they
are equal starting from some degree.
    Theorem 3 (Lasoń, Michalek, [6]). The toric ideal of a matroid and the ideal
generated by quadratic binomials corresponding to symmetric exchanges are equal
starting from some degree.
     Here we prove another result towards White’s conjecture which is valid for
arbitrary matroids.
     Consider a single matroid M , then by Hilbert’s basis theorem the ideal IM is
ﬁnitely generated. In particular it is generated in some degree, however so far we
did not have any eﬀective bound. If we ﬁx the size of the ground set, then there
are only ﬁnitely many matroids on it. Hence in this case there is a common bound
on degree in which the corresponding toric ideals are generated. But, when we ﬁx
only the rank, then the number of matroids of that rank is inﬁnite. We prove that
in this case there is also a common bound on degree in which the corresponding
toric ideals are generated. Our bound is explicit.
    Theorem 4. The toric ideal associated to a matroid of rank r is generated in
degree at most (r + 3)!.
    In the combinatorial language the meaning of Theorems 3 and 4 is the following.
Let M be a matroid of rank r. Let A, B be two multisets of k bases of M with equal
union (as a multiset). Theorem 3 asserts that if k ≥ f (M ) (for some function f ),
then one can pass between A and B by a sequence of steps, in each step exchanging
two bases for two bases created by a symmetric exchange. While Theorem 3 asserts
that if k ≥ (r + 3)!, then one can pass between A and B by a sequence of steps,
in each step exchanging (r + 3)! bases for another (r + 3)! bases of the same union
(as a multiset). Thus in Theorem 4 the steps between multisets of bases are less
precise (consist of exchanging (r + 3)! instead of 2 bases), but the lower bound on
k (the size of multisets of bases) is better ((r + 3)! instead of f (M )).

    Corollary 5. Checking if Conjecture 2 is true for matroids of a fixed rank is
a decidable problem (it is enough to check connectivity of a finite number of graphs).
     Equality of saturations of the ideals JM and IM from Theorem 3 implies equal-
ity of their radicals. Since the ideal IM is prime, it is also radical. Thus the radical
of JM equals to IM . In other words, for every element a of the toric ideal of a
matroid there exists a power n, such that an is generated by quadratic binomials
corresponding to symmetric exchanges. We can make this statement more explicit.
    Corollary 6. Suppose the characteristic of the field K is 2. Then for every
matroid of rank r, the 2r(r + 3)!-th power of every element of its toric ideal is
generated by quadratic binomials corresponding to symmetric exchanges.

                                     2. Preliminaries
     Blasiak [1] proposed a very nice and simple translation of the problem of gen-
erating the ideal IM to the problem of connectivity of some graphs naturally con-
structed from matroids. We are going to use this approach.
     Suppose M is a matroid whose ground set can be partitioned into k (for k ≥ 3)
disjoint bases (so the ground set is of size r(E)k). We call such a matroid a k-
matroid, and deﬁne for it a k-base graph denoted by Bk (M ). Vertices of Bk (M )
correspond to sets of k pairwise disjoint bases of M (partitions into bases of the
ground set). An edge joins vertices with nonempty intersection. That is, sets of
bases {B1 , . . . , Bk } and {B1′ , . . . , Bk′ } are connected if for some i, j equality Bi = Bj′
holds. A simple corollary of the proof of Proposition 2.1 from [1] gives the following.
    Proposition 7 (Blasiak, [1]). Let C be a class of matroids that is closed under
deletions (that do not lower the rank of a matroid) and adding parallel elements.
Suppose that for every k > n and for every k-matroid M in C the k-base graph
Bk (M ) is connected. Then for every matroid M in C, the toric ideal IM is generated
in degree at most n.
    In particular, in order to prove Conjecture 1 it is enough to show that for every
k > 2 and for every k-matroid M the k-base graph Bk (M ) is connected.

                                       3. Main result
     Proof of Theorem 4. We apply Proposition 7 to the class of matroids of
rank r, and n = (r + 3)!. Clearly, this class is closed under deletions (that do not
lower the rank of a matroid) and adding parallel elements. The upcoming Theorem
8 applied to r and s = 0 asserts that for every k ≥ (r + 3)! the k-base graph Bk (M )
of a k-matroid M of rank r is connected. We get even that in these graphs any two
vertices are connected by a path of length at most 3. This completes the proof of
Theorem 4.                                                                         
    Proof of Corollary 5. Again using Proposition 7, in order to check if
Conjecture 1 is true for matroids of rank r it is enough to check if for every k from
the range (r + 3)! ≥ k > 2 and for every k-matroid M of rank r the k-base graph
Bk (M ) is connected. That is, to check connectivity of a ﬁnite number of graphs.
To check if Conjectures 1 and 2 are equivalent for matroids of rank r it also suﬃces
to check connectivity of a ﬁnite number of graphs (consult [1]). This completes the
proof of Corollary 5.                                                               

    Theorem 8. For every integers r ≥ 1, s ≥ 0, k ≥ s(r + 1)! + (r + 3)! and for
every
      (1) k-matroid M of rank r with k pairwise disjoint bases B1 , . . . , Bk , and
      (2) its restriction N to k − s pairwise disjoint bases Ds+1 , . . . , Dk
there exists
      (3) a vertex in Bk (M ) connected by an edge with {B1 , . . . , Bk }, and
      (4) a vertex in Bk−s (N ) connected by an edge with {Ds+1 , . . . , Dk }
which have nonempty intersection (a common basis).
    Proof. The proof goes by induction on r. When r = 1, then the statement
becomes trivial.
    Suppose r ≥ 2. Since k ≥ r + 1, then without loss of generality we can assume
that B1 and Ds+1 do not intersect. If there is a vertex in Bk (M ) containing both
B1 and Ds+1 , then the assertion is true. Suppose contrary – there is no such vertex.
Hence, due to matroid union theorem (ﬁrstly formulated for graphs in [8]) there
exists a set A ⊂ E \ (B1 ∪ Ds+1 ) such that
                                    (k − 2)r(A) < |A|.
Of course r(A) > 0, since otherwise A would have to be empty (in a k-matroid M
of rank r > 0 there are no loops) and we would have 0 < 0. We know also that
r > r(A), since otherwise we would have (k−2)r < |A| ≤ |E\(B1 ∪Ds+1 )| = (k−2)r.
    Let Ai = Bi ∩ A for i = 2, . . . , k. We have |Ai | ≤ r(A), since each Bi is a basis.
We get that
                (k − 2)r(A) < |A| = |A2 | + · · · + |Ak | ≤ (k − 1)r(A).
Thus for all i = 2, . . . , k, except at most r(A) − 1, the equality |Ai | = r(A) holds.
Without loss of generality it holds for i = r + 1, . . . , k.
      Let A′ = Ar+1 ∪ · · · ∪ Ak and let E ′ = Br+1 ∪ · · · ∪ Bk . We are going to reduce
our problem to the (k − r)-matroid M ′ = M |E ′ (restriction of M to the set E ′ ),
and use the set A′ to split the problem for M ′ into smaller instances – for M ′ |A′
(restriction of M ′ to A′ ) and for M ′ /A′ (contraction of A′ in M ′ ).
      Again without loss of generality we can assume that Ds+1 , . . . , Ds+t are the only
bases among Ds+1 , . . . , Dk that have nonempty intersection with some of the bases
B1 , . . . , Br . That is, bases Ds+t+1 , . . . , Dk are contained in the set E ′ . Moreover,
t ≤ r2 since each Bi can be intersected by at most r bases Dj .

each Dj is a basis. In order to reduce the problem for M ′ |A′ and M ′ /A′ we need
