                                                                                               Abstract
                                                   We study rowmotion dynamics on interval-closed sets. Our first main result proves a simplification of
                                                the global definition of interval-closed set rowmotion from (Elder, Lafrenière, McNicholas, Striker, and
                                                Welch 2024). We then completely describe the orbits of interval-closed set rowmotion on products of
                                                two chains [2] × [n] and use this understanding to prove a homomesy conjecture from (ELMSW 2024)
                                                involving the signed cardinality statistic.


                                          1    Introduction
                                          Rowmotion is a natural action with surprisingly nice dynamical properties in certain cases. An interesting
                                          feature is that this action has both global and local definitions; globally, it may be seen as a convex closure
                                          operation, while locally, it is given as a composition of toggle operators. Rowmotion was originally studied
                                          on order ideals of a finite poset (equivalently, distributive lattices) [23] and has recently been extended to
                                          generalized realms [9, 3], other combinatorial objects [22, 15], and other types of lattices [2, 24, 7]. Rowmotion
                                          has even been categorified [14]. Many foundational results in dynamical algebraic combinatorics relate to
                                          this action; see the survey articles [17, 21, 13].
                                              Recently, Elder, Lafrenière, McNicholas, Striker, and Welch [11] defined rowmotion on the set IC(P )
                                          of interval-closed sets (or convex subsets) of a poset, a natural superset of order ideals, giving both local
                                          and global characterizations. The local characterization is a natural toggle action for interval-closed sets,
                                          analogous to the definition for order ideals: for x ∈ P , the toggle tx acts on an interval-closed set I by
                                          taking the symmetric difference of {x} and I, provided this is an interval-closed set, and as the identity
                                          otherwise. The local definition of rowmotion applies all toggles in the poset from top to bottom, that is, in
                                          the reverse order of any linear extension. The global characterization of ICS rowmotion, though, is much
                                          more complicated than the analogous definition for order ideals. Our first main theorem is a simplification
                                          of this global definition of rowmotion on interval-closed sets; see Section 2 for the notation.
                                          Theorem 1.1. Given an interval-closed set I ∈ IC(P ), rowmotion on I is given by
                                                                                                                        
                                                          Row(I) = Inc(Ceil(I)) − I ∪ ∆Ceil(I) − ∆(Min(I) ∩ ∆Ceil(I)) .

                                          dynamical properties: the order of rowmotion is m + n [1, 23] and several statistics exhibit homomesy [16], in
                                          which the average value of a statistic f on each orbit of rowmotion equals the global average c. (In this case
                                          we also say f is c-mesic.) We prove some analogous theorems on IC([m] × [n]), though due to the increased
                                          complexity, we focus on the case m = 2. The second theorem below settles [11, Conjecture 4.12 (m = 2)].

                                          Theorem 1.2. The orbits of rowmotion on IC([2]×[n]) are of sizes dividing n+3 or n+5, with the exception
                                          of a single orbit of size 2 and the quadratic orbits described in Theorem 3.26 and Table 4. In particular, the
                                          counts of orbits of each size are given in Tables 3 and 4.

     This homomesy is surprising because the orbits of rowmotion on IC([2] × [n]) are much less well-behaved

     We now give precise definitions of the objects and actions in the title of the paper and discuss relevant
results of the prior paper [11]. We also state analogous dynamical results for order ideals, for the sake of
comparison.
     Let P be a partially ordered set (poset). All posets in this paper are finite. A chain of a poset P is a
totally ordered subset of P . An n-element chain poset has elements 1 < 2 < · · · < n and is denoted as [n].
The Cartesian product of two chains is given as [m] × [n] = {(i, j) | 1 ≤ i ≤ m, 1 ≤ j ≤ n} with partial order
(a, b) ≤ (c, d) if and only if a ≤ c and b ≤ d. An order ideal of P is a downward-closed subset, and an order
filter of P is an upward-closed subset. The set of order ideals of P is denoted J(P ). A linear extension of P
is a total order that respects (or extends) the partial order P . See [19, Ch. 3] for details and other standard
poset terminology.
Definition 1.4. Let P be a poset and I a subset of P . We say that I is an interval-closed set (ICS) of P
if for all x, y ∈ I and z ∈ P such that x ≤ z ≤ y, then z ∈ I. Let IC(P ) denote the set of all interval-closed
sets of P .
    The following definition contains notation that will be used in Section 2; see [11, Examples 2.2 and 2.15]
for examples.
Definition 1.5. Given I ∈ IC(P ), let ∆(I) denote the smallest order ideal (downward-closed set) containing
I and ∇(I) denote the smallest order filter (upward-closed set) containing I. Denote the minimal elements
of I as Min(I) and the maximal elements of I as Max(I). Let Inc(I) be the set of elements in P that are
incomparable with I and IncI (J) be the restriction of Inc(J) to the subset I. The ceiling of I, denoted Ceil(I),
is the set of minimal elements of the order filter of I, not including I. That is, Ceil(I) = Min(∇(I) − I).
    We now review the definition of generalized toggles from [22] where we define toggles relative to any set
of subsets L of a fixed ground set. In this paper, we usually take L = IC(P ), but we also compare to prior
results on order ideals, in which case we use L = J(P ).

2     A simpler global definition of rowmotion
In [11], rowmotion on interval-closed sets was defined in two ways. It was first defined as a composition of
toggles through which we add or remove individual elements in an interval-closed set, i.e., the local definition.
Rowmotion was then characterized globally ([11, Theorem 2.20]) and it was shown that the two definitions
are equivalent. The global definition sped up computations significantly, allowing the authors to search more
effectively for dynamical phenomena. We recall this result now; see Definition 1.5 for necessary notations.
Theorem 2.1 ([11, Theorem 2.20]). Given an interval-closed set I ∈ IC(P ), rowmotion on I is given by

     Row(I) = Inc(I) ∪ ∆IncI Ceil(I) − I ∪ ∆Ceil(I) ∪ ∆Ceil(I) − ∆(Min(I) ∩ ∆Ceil(I)) .

3     Rowmotion orbits and signed cardinality homomesy of interval-
      closed sets of [2] × [n]
Our goals in this section are to prove Theorem 1.2, characterizing the rowmotion orbits on interval-closed
sets of [2] × [n], and Theorem 1.3, a homomesy result on these orbits with respect to the signed cardinality
statistic, when n is odd.
    We begin in Subsection 3.1 by giving a classification of the various types of ICS (Lemma 3.1) and showing
the effect of rowmotion on each (Lemma 3.2). In Subsection 3.2, we discuss some basic properties of signed
cardinality for these ICS. We then analyze orbits of size dividing n+3 in Subsection 3.3, proving Theorem 3.7
and Corollary 3.9, enumerating orbits of sizes dividing n + 3, and Theorem 3.10 and Corollary 3.11, showing
the average signed cardinality over each orbit is 0. We study orbits of size dividing n + 5 in Subsection
3.4, proving Theorems 3.18 and 3.19 on orbit enumeration and homomesy, respectively. We study all other
orbits in Subsection 3.5, proving Theorems 3.26 and 3.27 on orbit structure and homomesy. Subsection 3.6
shows that all the orbits were listed in the previous subsections, allowing us to describe precisely the orbit
structure and complete the proofs of Theorems 1.2 and 1.3.

    Theorem 3.7 will be an immediate consequence of the following lemma, which categorizes the orbits of
size n + 3 into four cases. We use the terminology stacked ranks to mean an ICS consisting of consecutive
full ranks. This type of interval-closed set intersects each of the six types of ICS we defined in Section 3.1.
Specifically, if I ∈ IC(P ) is an ICS in P consisting of 2 or more stacked ranks, then I = [b1 , i1 , a1 : b2 , i2 , a2 ]
has one of four forms:

    • If I contains neither the maximal or minimal element of P , then b1 = b2 + 1, 2 ≤ i1 = i2 , a1 = a2 − 1.
      This falls under the category Double Hook.

    • If I contains the maximal element of P but not the minimal element, then b1 = b2 + 1, 2 ≤ i1 =
      i2 − 1, a1 = a2 = 0. This falls under the category Second Hook.
    • If I contains both the maximal and minimal elements of P , then b1 = b2 = 0 = a1 = a2 , i1 = i2 = n.
      This falls under the category Stacked Diagonals and is specifically when I = P .

    • If I contains the minimal element of P but not the maximal element, then b1 = b2 = 0, 2 ≤ i1 =
      i2 + 1, a1 = a2 − 1. This falls under the category First Hook.

    If instead I consists of only one stacked rank, then I = [b1 , 1, a1 : b2 , 1, a2 ] has one of three forms:

    • If I contains neither the maximal or minimal element of P , then b1 = b2 + 1, i1 = 1 = i2 . This falls
      under the category Disjoint.
    • If I contains the maximal element of P but not the minimal element, then b1 = n. This falls under
      the category High.
    • If I contains the minimal element of P but not the maximal element, then a2 = n. This falls under
      the category Low.

Lemma 3.16. Fix any integers x, y, z such that x, y, z ≥ 4 and x + y + z = n + 5. Let I be the ICS

3.5    The quadratic orbits
In this section, we examine the third class of orbits under rowmotion, those that have large size that grows
quadratically with n. These orbits are unlike other orbit classes, which are either of size 2 or grow linearly
with n. Most orbits in this third class have lower-chain singleton representatives, i.e., interval-closed sets of
the form I = [x − 1, 1, n − x : ∅] = {(1, x)}. We give a characterization of these orbits in Theorem 3.26,
while in Theorem 3.27, we prove the average value of the signed cardinality statistic for each of these orbits
is zero when n is odd. To prove Theorems 3.26 and 3.27, we study commonly appearing orbit substructures,
ultimately focusing our attention on substructures between consecutive appearances of lower-chain singleton
ICS. In particular, we find that for 1 < x ≤ n − 6, the next lower-chain singleton appearing in an orbit
after {(1, x)} is {(1, x + 6)} (Lemma 3.23). A more complicated process determines the next lower-chain
singleton appearing in an orbit after {(1, n − y)} for y < 6 (Lemma 3.25). These two results allow us to use
the ordering of lower-chain singleton representatives to stitch together the larger orbits based on values of x
and n mod 6. In particular, we are able to determine the size and lower-chain representatives of these orbits
from subcycles of a weighted permutation.
    The first substructures we observe give rules for mapping between low and high interval-closed sets under
applications of rowmotion, Propositions 3.20 and 3.21. While these results follow directly from Lemma 3.2,
they provide a useful framework for examining more complex substructures, as seen in Lemmas 3.23, 3.24
and 3.25.

Theorem 1.3. Suppose that n is odd. Then the signed cardinality statistic on IC([2] × [n]) is 0-mesic with
respect to rowmotion.
Proof. We verified with SageMath [20] that all the orbits of [2] × [n] have an average signed cardinality of 0
for n ∈ {1, 3, 5, 7, 9}. Now assume that n ≥ 11 and that n is odd. The proof of Theorem 1.2 gives a complete
description of all the orbits. Those are one orbit of size 2, orbits of size dividing n + 3 and n + 5 and orbits
of quadratic sizes. The orbit of size 2 contains two ICS for which the signed cardinality is 0, which are the
full poset and the empty poset. Following Theorem 3.10 and Corollary 3.11, the signed cardinality also has
an average of 0 over orbits of size dividing n + 3. Theorem 3.19 shows that the average value of signed
cardinality over the orbits of size dividing n + 5 is also 0. As for the quadratic orbits, all of them contain
singleton representatives in the lower chain, except when n ≡ 1 (mod 3), in which case there is one orbit
with representative {(1, n − 2), (1, n − 1)}. Theorem 3.27 says that the average value of signed cardinality
over the orbits with lower-chain singleton representatives is 0. Lemma 3.24 means that the signed cardinality
sums to 0 for the one orbit with no singleton when n ≡ 1 (mod 3). Together, the last two statements mean
that all the orbits of quadratic size also have an average signed cardinality of 0, from which we conclude that
all orbits of [2] × [n] under rowmotion have an average signed cardinality of 0. Hence, the signed cardinality
on IC([2] × [n] is 0-mesic with respect to rowmotion when n is odd.
   It is worth noting that homomesy only occurs for the [2] × [n] poset when n is odd, as shown in the
proposition below.

not 0 for the poset [2] × [n] when n is even. Indeed, the average would be 0 only if the sum of the signed

4     Conjectures and future work
In this section, we discuss some conjectures and directions for future work.

4.1     Rowmotion orbits of interval-closed sets of [m] × [n]
Experimental evidence suggests that it will not be possible to fully catalogue rowmotion orbits in [m] × [n]
for m > 2 and arbitrary n. Nevertheless, some steps in this direction may be possible. It follows from
Theorem 1.2 that for large n, the vast majority of ICS of [2] × [n] belong to orbits of size n + 3. Experimental
evidence suggests that this extends to [m] × [n], in the following sense.
Conjecture 4.1. Fix any positive integer m. Let r(n) be the fraction of interval-closed sets of [m] × [n] that
belong to orbits of size m + n + 1. Then r(n) → 1 as n → ∞.
   One may view this conjecture as asserting that, although the overall behavior of rowmotion on ICS is
complicated and of large order, its behavior on typical ICS is very nice. It would be possible in principle
to prove Conjecture 4.1 by identifying a sufficiently large family of orbits of size m + n + 1, and comparing
with the enumeration of all ICS. We outline a strategy in this direction here.

4.2    Max-minus-min homomesy
Our second main result, Theorem 1.3, proved a homomesy conjecture of [11]. We state below another
conjecture of that paper and give some remarks. The m = 2 case was proved in [11, Thm. 4.7].
Conjecture 4.2 ([11, Conj. 4.9]). The number of maximal elements minus the number of minimal elements
is 0-mesic under rowmotion on IC(P ), for P = [m] × [n].

interval-closed set that contributes +1 with an interval-closed set in the same orbit contributing −1. In that

and +2. However, it is not always possible to pair, over a given orbit, interval-closed sets with values i and
