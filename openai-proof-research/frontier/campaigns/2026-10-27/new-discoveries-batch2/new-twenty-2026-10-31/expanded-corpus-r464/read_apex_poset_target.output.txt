                                              The Kn+5 and K32 ,1n families are obstructions to n-apex.

                                                                 Thomas W. Mattman and Mike Pierce

                                                   Abstract. We give evidence in support of a conjecture that the Y∇Y families
                                                   of Kn+5 and K32 ,1n are minor minimal obstructions for the n-apex property




                                                                                Introduction
                                               A graph is n-apex if deleting n or fewer vertices results in a planar graph.
                                          Kuratowski [K] showed the 0-apex or planar graphs are characterized by the ob-
                                          struction set O0 = {K5 , K3,3 }. Using the formulation of Wagner [W], a graph is
                                          planar if and only if it has neither K5 nor K3,3 as a minor. Recall that H is a minor
                                          of the graph G if H can be obtained by contracting edges in a subgraph of G. As
                                          the n-apex property is minor closed, it follows from the Graph Minor Theorem of
                                          Robertson and Seymour [RS] that there is a corresponding finite obstruction set
                                          On . For each n, On is the set of graphs that are not n-apex even though every
                                          proper minor is. We say that the elements of On are minor minimal not n-apex
                                          or MMNnA.
                                               Determining this obstruction set is likely to be quite difficult. It’s known that,
                                          already, |O1 | > 150 [P], and we expect that |On | grows quickly in size with n. In
                                          the current paper we report on computer searches that show |O2 | ≥ 82, |O3 | ≥ 601,
                                          |O4 | ≥ 520, and |O5 | ≥ 608.

As in Figure 1, we perform a ∇Y or triangle-Y move on a graph by deleting the
edges of a triangle abc and then adding a vertex v and edges av, bv, and cv. The
reverse operation is a Y∇ or Y-triangle move. The set of graphs obtained from
a graph G through a sequence of zero or more ∇Y or Y∇ moves is G’s (Y∇Y)
family.
    In this paper we provide evidence in support of the following conjecture.
    Conjecture 1. For each n > 0, the Kn+5 and K32 ,1n families are in On .
     When n = 0, there are 49 graphs in the K5 family and 10 in K3,3 ’s. However,
O0 = {K5 , K3,3 } as proved by Weber [W] and the analogue of the conjecture does
not hold in this case. On the other hand, a proof for n ≤ 2 follows easily from
earlier work.
    Proposition 2. The conjecture holds for n ≤ 2.
    Proof. For n = 1, the K6 and K3,3,1 families coincide. This collection of
seven graphs is known as the Petersen family since it also includes the Petersen
graph. Barsotti and Mattman [BM] observed that this family is in O1 on the way
to proving that the K7 family is in O2 . In fact, they showed that the K7 family is
exactly the set of graphs in O2 of size 21 or less.
    It remains to show that K32 ,12 is in O2 . In [GMN], Goldberg, Mattman, and
Naimi show that the 58 graphs in this family are all minor minimal intrinsically
knotted or MMIK. This implies they are not 2-apex, see [BBFFHL, OT]. Since
K7 and its 13 descendants are also MMIK [KS], this means that none of these 14
graphs can be a minor of a K32 ,12 family graph.
    So far, we have established that each of the size 22 graphs in the K32 ,12 family
is not 2-apex. If one of these were not in O2 , then it would have to have a 2-apex
minor of size at most 21. By [BM], this minor would lie in the K7 family. We’ve
already argued that 14 of the 20 graphs in that family cannot play this role. We
omit the straightforward verification that none of the remaining six graphs in the
K7 family is a minor of a member of the K32 ,12 family.                            
     We verify the conjecture for n = 3 with the aid of a computer, as described
in Section 2 below. For n = 4, we checked that the 163 graphs in the K9 family
and 357 of the graphs in the K32 ,14 family (all those within four ∇Y or Y∇ moves
of K32 ,14 ) are in O4 . For n = 5, the computer assures us that 608 of the 1681
graphs in K10 ’s family are in O5 . This gives the bounds for O4 and O5 mentioned
earlier. Due to time constraints, we did not attempt to check more graphs in the
K10 family nor in the K32 ,14 family of more than 13 thousand graphs.
     It is straightforward to see that Kn+5 and K32 ,1n themselves are in On . We
also verify this for the three immediate descendants of each of these two graphs in
the following theorem, which we prove in Section 1. Recall that when a sequence
of ∇Y moves take us from G1 to G2 , we say G2 is a descendant of G1 .
    Theorem 3. Let n > 0. The graphs Kn+5 and K32 ,1n and the three immediate
descendants of each are elements of On
    It would be tempting to argue that ∇Y and Y∇ moves preserve the MMNnA
property that characterizes On . Unfortunately, this is not true in general. For
example, the disjoint unions G3 = tn+1                 n+1
                                   i=1 K3,3 and G5 = ti=1 K5 are both MMNnA.

However, a Y∇ move on K3,3 results in a planar graph and, similarly, a Y∇ move
on G3 gives a graph that is not in On . On the other hand, a ∇Y move on K5
results in a graph that is nonplanar, but with proper K3,3 minor. So, the child of
G5 cannot join it in On .
    On the other hand, it’s easy to see that Y∇ moves preserve planarity (or,
equivalently, ∇Y preserves nonplanarity). If G is planar, and a Y∇ move on the
degree 3 vertex v results in G0 (We call this a Y∇ move at v.) then, fixing a
planar embedding of G, we can effect the Y∇ move in a tubular neighborhood of v
and its three edges to give a planar embedding of G0 .
    Using this idea we can show that a Y∇ move at v preserves the n-apex property
provided v is not an apex. For 1-apex graphs, this observation is due to Barsotti
in unpublished work. For W ⊂ V (G), let G − W denote the induced subgraph on
V (G) \ W .
    Lemma 4. Suppose G is n-apex with subset U of V (G) such that |U | ≤ n and
G − U is planar. If G0 is obtained from G by a Y∇ move at the vertex v 6∈ U then
G0 is also n-apex.
    Proof. The key observation is that the Y∇ move preserves planarity so that
G0 − U is again planar and G0 is |U |-apex. The details depend on how many of v’s
neighbors, a, b, and c, are in U .
    If none are, the argument is the same as above: we can effect the Y∇ move in
a tubular neighborhood of v and its edges. If exactly one is, say a ∈ U , then the
Y∇ move replaces the edges vb and vc in G − U with the edge bc in G0 − U so that
planarity is preserved. Similarly, if two or all three neighbors are in U , the Y∇
move will leave G0 − U planar.                                                   

     Although Y∇ and ∇Y moves do not preserve MMNnA in general, our conjec-
ture asserts that they do in the Kn+5 and K32 ,1n family.
     We describe results for O2 and O3 in Section 2. This includes classifications of
all the graphs in O2 through order 9 and size 23. We also give a proof “by hand”
that the 32 graphs in the K8 family are in O3 . This, with the computer verification
for the K32 ,13 family, shows that our conjecture holds for n = 3. But first we prove
Theorem 3 in the next section.

                               1. Proof of Theorem 3
    In this section we prove that Kn+5 , K32 ,1n , and their three immediate descen-
dants are in On . In the introduction, we gave the proof for n ≤ 2, so we will assume
n ≥ 3. Then, the three descendants of K32 ,1n are its children, the graphs obtained
by a single ∇Y move. The graph Kn+5 has a single child, C1 , which in turn has two
children. This child and pair of grandchildren are the three immediate descendants
of Kn+5 referred to in the statement of Theorem 3.
    We begin with a lemma that will be useful for one of the children of K32 ,1n and
which gives the flavor of our approach.

     Proof. (of Theorem 3) We begin with Kn+5 , which is not n-apex since deletion
of any n vertices gives the nonplanar graph K5 . On the other hand, given e ∈
E(Kn+5 ), both deletion and contraction of e result in an n-apex graph as can be
seen by deleting any n vertices not on the edge e. Therefore, every proper minor
of Kn+5 is n-apex and Kn+5 ∈ On .
     Let C1 denote the child of Kn+5 . This graph has a unique vertex, v0 , of degree
three with neighbors v1 , v2 , and v3 . Denote the remaining vertices v4 , v5 , v6 , . . . , vn+5 .
Generally, deleting n vertices results in a graph with K3,3 minor. The exception is
when exactly one of v1 , v2 , and v3 is deleted and v0 is not. In this case, the result
has a K5 minor. Thus, C1 is not n-apex.
     Up to symmetry, there are three types of edges in C1 , represented by v0 v1 ,
v1 v4 , and v4 v5 . For each choice of edge, e, we argue that both deletion, C1 − e, and
contraction, C1 /e, gives an n-apex graph.
     For C1 /e, we observe that, no matter which edge is contracted, by appropriate
deletion of n vertices, we can ensure that v0 (or the vertex formed by identifying v0

and v1 in case e = v0 v1 ) is an isolated vertex in the resulting graph on 5 vertices,
which is, therefore, nonplanar. If e = v0 v1 or v1 v4 , C1 − e is planar after deleting
all vertices but v0 through v6 , while C1 − v4 v5 becomes planar after deletion of at
least one of v1 , v2 , v3 and none of v0 , v4 , v5 .
      Therefore C1 ∈ On and we turn to its children. To form a triangle in C1 , we
use at most one vertex from v1 , v2 , and v3 . This means a triangle cannot include
v0 and, up to symmetry, there are two types of triangles: v1 v4 v5 , resulting in the
child C2 , or v4 v5 v6 , which gives a child we’ll call C3 . For these new graphs, we’ll
use the same vertex labels as in C1 with the addition of a new degree three vertex
w0 .
      Let’s see why C2 ∈ On . First, notice that there is a graph automorphism by
interchanging v0 and w0 and the pair {v2 , v3 } with {v4 , v5 }. Deleting n − 2 of the n
vertices in {v6 , v7 , . . . vn+5 } results in a graph in the K7 family, that is not 2-apex
by Proposition 2. The only way C2 could remain planar after deletion of n vertices
is if at least three of those vertices remain. However the remaining vertices from
that set, together with up to one each of {v2 , v3 } and {v4 , v5 }, form a clique. A
clique on five or more vertices contains K5 and is nonplanar. Thus, we surely have
a nonplanar subgraph by deletion of n vertices except, possibly, in two cases (up to
symmetry): 1) {v6 , v7 , v8 , v9 } remain after deleting the n vertices, or 2) {v6 , v7 , v8 }
and vertices from at most one of {v2 , v3 } and {v4 , v5 }, say the first, remain.
      In the first case, we see that {v2 , v3 , v4 , v5 , v10 , v11 , . . . vn+5 } are the n deleted
vertices and what remains is the graph induced on v0 , v1 , v6 , v7 , v8 , v9 , and w0 ,
which is nonplanar.
      In the second case, we can assume v2 remains as otherwise we must have deleted
the n + 1 vertices {v2 , v3 , v4 , v5 , v9 , v10 , . . . , vn+5 }. Since v2 remains, we see that
{v4 , v5 , v9 , v10 , . . . , vn+5 } are n−1 of the deleted vertices. The graph H that remains
after deleting these is the induced graph on the eight vertices v0 , v1 , v2 , v3 , v6 , v7 ,
v8 , and w0 . It is straightforward to verify that H is not 1-apex. So, no matter
which vertex is chosen as the nth for deletion, the result will be nonplanar in this
case. This completes the argument that C2 is not n-apex.
      Next we show that every proper minor is n-apex by establishing this for C2 − e
and C2 /e whatever edge e is chosen. Up to symmetry, there are six choices for
e: v0 v1 , v0 v2 , v1 v6 , v2 v4 , v2 v6 , and v6 v7 . Recall that deleting n − 2 vertices from
{v6 , v7 , . . . , vn+5 } converts C2 to a graph C20 in the K7 family. Each choice of e
corresponds to an edge in C20 and we’ve already established, in Proposition 2, that
C20 −e and C20 /e are 2-apex. This means the corresponding minors of C2 are n-apex.
This completes the argument that C2 ∈ On .
      Let’s see why C3 is not n-apex. Deleting n−2 vertices in V7+ = {v7 , v8 , . . . , vn+5 }
results in a graph in the K7 family that is not 2-apex by Proposition 2. So, to
achieve a planar graph after deleting n vertices would require at least two vertices
in V7+ to remain. On the other hand the vertices in V7+ induce a clique so, to
avoid the nonplanar K5 , at most four can remain. Up to symmetry, this gives three
cases: 1) {v7 , v8 , v9 , v10 }, 2) {v7 , v8 , v9 }, or 3) {v7 , v8 } are the elements that remain
after deleting n–vertices. To complete the argument that C3 is not n-apex, we’ll
show that none of these lead to a planar graph.
      Any vertex from {v1 , v2 , v3 } or {v4 , v5 , v6 } will also induce a clique with vertices
from V7+ . In the first case, if we are to avoid a nonplanar graph, we must delete the
n+1 vertices {v1 , v2 , . . . v6 , v11 , v12 , . . . , vn+5 }, so we cannot use this case to show C3

{v4 , v5 , v6 , v10 , v11 , . . . , vn+5 } as n − 1 of the deleted vertices. It’s straightforward

any C3 − e or C3 /e is n-apex, it follows that any proper minor is n-apex. This
completes the argument that C3 ∈ On .

POSET CONCLUSION 5     Concluding Remarks and Open Problems
The posets P with dim2 (P) = 2 are C3 the chain on three elements, A2 , V1,1 and its
dual, and the Boolean lattice B2 . The only unknown Rk (P) is when P = B2 . Axenovich
and Walzer [2] showed R2 (B2 ) = 4. Indeed, we can show that R3 (B2 ) = 6 using the
facts of the Boolean rainbow Ramsey numbers. However, the argument we used to derive
it is relatively complicated and cannot be generalized further. So we only publish it on
arXiv. Interested readers can see arXiv:1909.11370 for the details. Anyway, it is nature
to investigate Rk (B2 ) after we have settled Rk (V1,1 ). Hopefully, the result of Rk (V1,1 )
could do some help to this problem.
    Recall that we introduced the weak subposets and mentioned a reference [8] of the
Boolean Ramsey number for the weak subposets. Grosz et al. [14] used Rw (P1 , P2 . . . , Pk )
to denote the weak version of Boolean Ramsey number. It is clear that

                            Rw (P1 , P2 . . . , Pk ) ≤ R(P1 , P2 . . . , Pk )                 (1)

from the definitions. If all the Pi ’s are chains, then equality in (1) holds. Observe that the
colorings we used to derive the lower bounds for Rk (V1,1 ) and R(Vm,m , Vn,n ) also avoid
the weak V -shaped posets. So Rk (V1,1 ) and R(Vm,m , Vn,n ) are the examples for which
the equality in (1) holds. On the other hand, we have Rk (Vm,n ) = nk + 1 for m < n which
implies R2 (V1,2 ) = 5. However, for the weak version we have Rw (V1,2 , V1,2 ) = 4. This is
because for every two coloring of B4 , the nonempty subsets of [4] that have the same color
as ∅ must be a chain or an antichain. Thus, we can find a copy of monochromatic B2 in
the subposet of B4 formed by removing all elements of the same color as ∅. Note that B2
contains V1,2 as a weak subposet. So Rw (V1,2, V1,2 ) ≤ 4. Meanwhile, it is easy to color B3
by two colors so that no monochromatic weak V1,2 exists. In general, given Pi = Vmi ,ni
with 1 ≤ mi < ni for 1 ≤ i ≤ k, one can show Rw (P1 , P2, . . . , Pk ) < R(P1 , P2, . . . , Pk ).
We believe that Theorem 1.3 can be generalized to more posets and the following is true.

Conjecture 5.1 For Pi = Vmi ,mi ,

           Rw (P1 , P2 , . . . , Pk ) = R(P1 , P2, . . . , Pk ) = m1 + m2 + · · · + mk + 1.
