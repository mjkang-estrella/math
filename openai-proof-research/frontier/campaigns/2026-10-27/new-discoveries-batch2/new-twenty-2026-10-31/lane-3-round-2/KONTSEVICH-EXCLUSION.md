# 322185 — decisive known-result exclusion, zero credit

Original primary https://arxiv.org/pdf/1109.2469v1, Section 3, Conjecture 1 (pages 7–8): nine free independent noncommuting entries of a 3x3 matrix M; I1 is matrix inverse, I2 transpose, I3 entrywise inverse. The claim is (I1 o I2 o I3)^3(M)=D_L(M) M D_R(M), with diagonal matrices whose entries are noncommutative rational functions of the nine generators. This is an identity on the generic rational domain modulo left/right diagonal action, not a claim at singular specializations. No commutativity hypothesis is allowed.

Successor checked BEFORE any algebraic proof attempt: Natalia Iyudu and Stanislav Shkarin, *The proof of the Kontsevich periodicity conjecture on noncommutative birational transformations*, https://arxiv.org/abs/1305.1965 (first announced 8 May 2013; v3 16 May 2014), published in Duke Mathematical Journal 164 (2015), DOI https://doi.org/10.1215/00127094-3146603. The author institutional publication record confirms the 2015 publication. Actual primary body https://arxiv.org/pdf/1305.1965v3 was read at Introduction and Theorem 1.1/Proposition 1.2, not just an abstract/search snippet. It proves for EVERY associative unital ring R and EVERY M in dom(J^3), J^3(M)=D1^{-1} M D2 for invertible diagonal D1,D2, where J=J2 o J1, J1=M inverse, J2=transpose of entrywise inverse. Proposition 1.2 explicitly handles domains.

Scope bridge: write A=matrix inverse and B=transpose followed by entrywise inverse. Transposition and entrywise inverse commute and B is an involution. The original map T=A o B and successor map J=B o A are inverse birational maps. Both preserve diagonal orbits (with transformed diagonal factors). The statement that J has cube identity on the quotient is equivalent to the same statement for its inverse T on the generic domain. Apply the theorem over the universal free skew field of the nine generators; the diagonal entries lie in that field. Thus this is the exact original conjecture, not an unrelated commutative specialization or cluster conjecture. The general-ring theorem is stronger.
