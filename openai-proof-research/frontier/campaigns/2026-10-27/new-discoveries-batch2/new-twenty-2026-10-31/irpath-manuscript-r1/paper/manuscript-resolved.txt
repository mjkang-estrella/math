# The path P9 is an IR-graph: a nine-vertex counterexample to the Mynhardt–Roux path conjecture

DRAFT manuscript, 5 November 2026. Not peer reviewed, not submitted, not accepted. Prepared with substantial AI assistance: the example was found by an AI-run computer search, and the proof write-up, recomputations and this text were produced by AI models (see the provenance statement at the end). No human author is listed; authorship and responsibility are to be assigned by the human operator. No priority claim is made.

Abstract. Mynhardt and Roux defined the IR-graph G(IR) of a graph G: its vertices are the irredundant sets of G of maximum cardinality, and two of them are adjacent when one arises from the other by exchanging a single vertex for a neighbour. They proved that P3, P4, P5, C5, C6 and C7 are not IR-graphs, and conjectured that no path Pn with n ≥ 3 and no cycle Cn with n ≥ 5 is an IR-graph. We exhibit a graph G on 9 vertices with 20 edges, obtained from K3,3,3 by deleting seven edges, such that IR(G) = 3, G has exactly nine IR(G)-sets, and G(IR) ≅ P9. Hence the path conjecture is false. The proof is short and can be checked by hand from two small tables. It has also been formalised in Lean 4 with Mathlib, where the negation of the conjunction recorded as MathDB problem 350452 is a kernel-checked theorem. An exhaustive computer search over all graphs of order at most 9 shows that 9 is the smallest order of a graph whose IR-graph is a path of order at least 3, and that at order 9 exactly P7, P8 and P9 occur. Whether P6, or any cycle Cn with n ≥ 5, is an IR-graph remains open.

## 1  The setting and the conjecture

Reconfiguration graphs record how feasible solutions of a problem can be transformed into one another by small steps. Fricke, Hedetniemi, Hedetniemi and Hutson [FHHH] introduced the γ-graph of a graph, whose vertices are its minimum dominating sets, with the “slide” adjacency described below. Mynhardt and Roux [MR-IG] began the study of the analogous graph for maximum irredundant sets. We use their definitions, which we quote from arXiv:1812.03382v3 (Sections 1.1 and 1.2). Graphs are finite and simple. N[v] = N(v) ∪ {v} is the closed neighbourhood, and N[D] is the union of the N[v] for v ∈ D.

> “A D-private neighbour of v ∈ D is a vertex v′ that is dominated by v but by no vertex in D − {v}. … PN(v, D) = N[v] − N[D − {v}].”

> “A set D ⊆ V is irredundant if PN(v, D) ≠ ∅ for each v ∈ D … the upper irredundance number IR(G) is the largest cardinality of an irredundant set of G. … an IR-set of G, sometimes also called an IR(G)-set, is an irredundant set of cardinality IR(G).”

> “… we define the IR-graph G(IR) of G to be the graph whose vertex set consists of the IR(G)-sets, i.e., the irredundant sets of G of maximum cardinality, where two IR(G)-sets D and D′ are adjacent if and only if there exist vertices u ∈ D and v ∈ D′ such that uv ∈ E(G) and D′ = (D − {u}) ∪ {v}.”

> “To prove that a given graph H is an IR-graph, one needs to construct a graph G such that G(IR) ≅ H.”

The paths Pn and cycles Cn have n vertices; the source treats P3 as the star K1,2.

Known results. Mynhardt and Roux [MR-IG] observe that Kn(IR) = Kn. They show that Cartesian products of IR-graphs are IR-graphs, so every hypercube Qn is one (the source of Qn is nK2), and in particular C4 = Q2 is. Every disconnected graph is the IR-graph of infinitely many graphs. An IR-graph of diameter 2 has an induced C4, so the stars K1,n with n ≥ 2 are not IR-graphs, and neither is any non-complete graph with a universal vertex. Part (ii) of their main theorem (labelled Thm_Main in the arXiv TeX source) states: “The cycles C5, C6, C7 and the paths P3, P4, P5 are not IR-graphs.” The only connected IR-graphs of order 4 are K4 and C4. Among trees, the double star S(2,2) is the unique smallest IR-tree of diameter 3, and the double spider Sp(1,1;1,2) is the unique smallest IR-tree of diameter 4. In [MR-IT] the same authors show that the IR-trees of diameter 3 are exactly the double stars S(2n,2n). The survey [MN] summarises these results. The open-problems section of [MR-IG] reads:

> “A direct proof that P5 is not an IR-graph is somewhat simpler than the proof of Theorem […], but not simple enough to easily generalize to a proof that longer paths and cycles are not IR-graphs. Nevertheless, we believe this to be true and state it as conjectures.

> Conjecture 1. Pn is not an IR-graph for each n ≥ 3.

> Conjecture 2. Cn is not an IR-graph for each n ≥ 5.”

Just before this, the authors add: “We conjecture that P6 is not an IR-graph”. The companion paper [MR-IT, Open Problems] restates the two conjectures as parts (i) and (ii) of one conjecture, and the survey [MN] states them as a single sentence. MathDB problem 350452, “Non-IR conjecture for paths and cycles”, which cites [MR-IT], records exactly this conjunction: “The path Pn is not an IR-graph for each n ≥ 3, and the cycle Cn is not an IR-graph for each n ≥ 5.”

Our result. We refute Conjecture 1, and hence the conjunction, with an explicit graph of order 9 (Theorem 1). We do not address Conjecture 2. As far as our bounded search found (Section 6), no realisation of a path of order at least 3 or of a cycle of order at least 5 as an IR-graph has been published.

## 2  The graph and the theorem

Let V = {0, 1, …, 8} with parts A = {0,1,2}, B = {3,4,5} and C = {6,7,8}. Let G be the complete tripartite graph K3,3,3 on these parts with the seven edges 05, 08, 14, 23, 26, 37 and 56 deleted. The deleted edges form the path 8–0–5–6–2–3–7 together with the edge 14. Thus G has the 20 edges

03  04  06  07  13  15  16  17  18  24  25  27  28  36  38  46  47  48  57  58.

Vertices 1, 4, 7 and 8 have degree 5 and the others have degree 4. The graph is connected, and a brute-force check of all 9! permutations shows that its automorphism group has order 2. For S ⊆ V write U(S) = V − N[S] for the set of vertices not dominated by S, with U(∅) = V and U(v) = U({v}). Then U(S) is the intersection of the sets U(u), u ∈ S, and U(u) is the neighbourhood of u in the complement of G. Table 1 lists the closed neighbourhoods and the sets U(v).

Theorem 1. IR(G) = 3, the IR(G)-sets of G are exactly W0, …, W8, and two of them are adjacent in G(IR) if and only if their indices differ by one. Hence G(IR) ≅ P9.

Corollary 2. P9 is an IR-graph. Conjecture 1 of [MR-IG] (part (i) of the conjecture in [MR-IT]) is false, and so is the conjunction recorded as MathDB problem 350452.

## 3  Proof of Theorem 1

Lemma 3 (heredity). Every subset of an irredundant set is irredundant. Indeed, if D′ ⊆ D and v ∈ D′, then N[D′ − {v}] ⊆ N[D − {v}], so PN(v, D) ⊆ PN(v, D′).

Lemma 4. For every graph, every D ⊆ V and every v ∈ D, PN(v, D) = U(D − {v}) − U(D). In particular: (a) if D − {v} dominates G, then v has no D-private neighbour; (b) if D dominates G, then PN(v, D) = U(D − {v}). Indeed, N[D] = N[v] ∪ N[D − {v}], so N[v] − N[D − {v}] = N[D] − N[D − {v}] = U(D − {v}) − U(D).

Step 1: pairs. Intersecting rows of Table 1 gives U(P) for all 36 pairs P (Table 2). Exactly nine pairs dominate G, namely 05, 08, 14, 17, 23, 26, 37, 48 and 56: the seven deleted edges, which are non-edges of G, and the two edges 17 and 48 of G.

Step 2: triples. Each of the 84 three-sets T falls into exactly one of three classes.

(b) T contains no dominating pair and does not dominate G. There are 21 such triples, listed in Table 3 together with a vertex v ∈ T for which U(T − {v}) = U(T). By Lemma 4, PN(v, T) = ∅, so T is not irredundant.

Step 3: no irredundant 4-set. Suppose that D is irredundant and |D| = 4. By Lemma 3 its four 3-subsets are irredundant, so by Step 2 they are four of the sets Wi, and any two of them share two vertices. Two of the sets Wi share two vertices only if their indices differ by one (inspect the 36 pairs). In particular, no three of them pairwise share two vertices, which is a contradiction. By Lemma 3 no irredundant set has more than three vertices, so IR(G) = 3 and the IR(G)-sets are exactly W0, …, W8.

Step 4: adjacency. All IR(G)-sets have three vertices. So D and D′ are adjacent in G(IR) if and only if D − D′ = {u} and D′ − D = {v} with uv ∈ E(G). This relation is symmetric and irreflexive. Of the 36 pairs {Wi, Wj}, the 28 with |i − j| ≥ 2 share at most one vertex, so they are not adjacent. For each of the eight pairs {Wi, Wi+1}, the exchanged vertices are 1→7, 2→4, 0→6, 4→8, 7→1, 6→3, 1→5 and 8→4, and 17, 24, 06, 48, 17, 36, 15 and 48 are edges of G. Hence G(IR) is the path W0W1…W8. ∎

Remark 5 (a problem from [MR-IG]). The authors also ask: “Prove or disprove: if G has an independent IR-set and the IR-graph H of G is connected and has order at least three, then H has maximum degree at least three.” In our graph, the independent sets W0, W4 and W8 are the three parts A, C and B, and they are IR-sets. Yet H = G(IR) ≅ P9 is connected and has maximum degree 2. So the same example answers this problem negatively. This is part of the same result and is not a separate claim.

Remark 6. The source's proof that C6 and C7 are not IR-graphs uses that these graphs have diameter 3. A corollary bounding the diameter from below then shows that a non-independent IR-set has exactly three vertices of positive degree. A lemma (Lem_Not_Path in the TeX source) then needs the distance between such a set and a certain “flip-set” to be 3, in order to force an induced C4 or S(2,2). Our IR-graph has diameter 8, so this argument gives no obstruction. The example says nothing about cycles.

## 4  Exhaustive search through order 9

The following are computations, not part of the proof of Theorem 1. All graphs were included, connected or not. For n ≤ 8, the graphs on n vertices were generated up to isomorphism as one-vertex extensions of the graphs on n − 1 vertices, deduplicated by a canonical form. The canonical form uses colour refinement and individualisation, pruned only by transpositions of twin vertices. The class counts 1, 2, 4, 11, 34, 156, 1044 and 12346 agree with the known numbers of graphs [OEIS]. The IR-graph of each of these 13598 graphs was computed from the definitions. For order 9, all 256 one-vertex extensions of each of the 12346 graphs of order 8 were evaluated, 3160576 labelled graphs in all. Every graph on 9 vertices arises this way up to isomorphism (delete any vertex). The results are in Table 5.

So, computationally: no graph of order at most 8 has Pm (m ≥ 3) or Cm (m ≥ 5) as its IR-graph. At order 9 there are exactly three isomorphism classes with a path IR-graph of order at least 3, giving P7, P8 and P9; the last is the class of G. All three are connected and have IR = 3. No cycle Cm with m ≥ 5, and no P3, …, P6, occurs through order 9. Thus 9 is the smallest order of a graph whose IR-graph is a path of order at least 3. Three implementations agree on these counts: the discovery search (connected graphs), the independent review (all graphs), and the fresh script of this manuscript (all graphs). They are not formally verified. While searching, the discovery lane also found path IR-graphs up to P14 among some graphs of orders 10 and 11. These observations are UNVERIFIED by review, were not exhaustive, and are not claimed. P6, all cycles Cn with n ≥ 5, and whether infinitely many paths are IR-graphs remain open.

Adjacency is the literal swap relation. Nothing is symmetrised and no distinctness clause is added: symmetry on IR-sets follows from their equal cardinality (swap_symm), and irreflexivity from looplessness of G (swap_irrefl). IsIRGraph ranges over all finite simple graphs on vertex types in Type. This could only narrow the notion, so it cannot weaken a refutation. Mathlib's pathGraph n and cycleGraph n have n vertices. The finite steps are kernel decide proofs: all 126 four-sets are not irredundant, the 84 three-sets are irredundant exactly when they are one of the nine walk sets, and the swap relation holds on the 81 ordered pairs of walk sets exactly for consecutive indices. Heredity, irSet_iff_max and Equiv.ofBijective then give pathIso : pathGraph 9 ≃g IRGraph G9. A non-vacuity control proves nonvacuity : IsIRGraph (pathGraph 2) (the IR-graph of K2 is K2).

## 6  Related work and prior-art screen

The γ-graph and its slide adjacency go back to Fricke et al. [FHHH], who showed that every tree is a γ-graph and conjectured that every graph is; Connelly, Hedetniemi and Hutson [CHH] proved this. Mynhardt and Teshima [MT] showed that every graph is a slide π-graph for several domination parameters π, including Γ, and mentioned the study of IR-graphs as an open problem (as reported in [MR-IG]). Irredundance was introduced by Cockayne, Hedetniemi and Miller [CHM]; see also the chapter [MR-ch]. Reconfiguration of dominating sets and colourings is surveyed in [MN]. In contrast with γ-graphs, not every graph is an IR-graph [MR-IG], and paths and cycles were among the first graphs tested.

Bounded screen (campaign lanes, 5 November 2026). All arXiv versions of [MR-IG] (v1–v3), [MR-IT] (v1, v2) and [MN] were read; none resolves or withdraws the conjectures. OpenAlex lists seven works citing the two journal articles and [MR-ch], and none concerns IR-graphs of paths or cycles. Three further OpenAlex queries returned HTTP 503. A few web searches, GitHub repository searches and path screens of the Lean Pool, OpenAI/math and Omega registries found no realisation of a path or a cycle as an IR-graph. The journal versions [MR-IG, MR-IT] (ScienceDirect) were not read. We cannot exclude that they, or works this screen missed, contain related material. We make no priority claim.

## 7  Significance and limitations

The contribution is modest: one explicit small graph that refutes a conjecture stated in [MR-IG] and restated in [MR-IT] and [MN], which its proposers supported with proofs for paths of order n ≤ 5. The proof is elementary. The example also answers negatively the problem quoted in Remark 5, which the authors hoped would help prove the conjectures. Only Conjecture 1 (part (i)) is refuted. Conjecture 2 on cycles is untouched, the status of P6 is open, and we prove nothing about infinitely many paths. The minimality of order 9 and the census at order 9 rest on computation.
