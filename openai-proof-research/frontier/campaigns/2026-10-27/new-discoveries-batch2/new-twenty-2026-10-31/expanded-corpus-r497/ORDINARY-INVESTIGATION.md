## 388051: arbitrary matroid cyclic ordering, three routes

Assume a finite ground set E partitioned into k bases of rank r, so |E|=kr. For every A subset E, each A intersect B_i is independent, so |A intersect B_i|<=rank(A). Summing gives |A|<=k rank(A). Thus uniform density is a paid necessary condition. It is not a new sufficient theorem.

Route A: simultaneous serial exchange. Arrange each basis as a block. At phase j, let C_i be its unused suffix, and let R_i=C_i union the previously selected prefix of B_(i+1). Maintain that all completed sliding r-windows are bases. The next choices p_i in C_i must satisfy simultaneously that R_i-p_i+p_(i+1) is a basis, including the last-to-first condition. Basis exchange can propagate a choice along the chain, but it need not close the cycle. A family of nonempty binary relations need not possess a cyclic compatible tuple: equality, equality, and inequality on two symbols already fail. This is not a matroid counterexample; it exposes the unpaid inference from separate exchange choices to simultaneous choices. Repair by changing earlier prefix elements needs preservation of *all* completed windows, not only the currently active bases.

The acquired split-matroid primary proof uses a special hyperedge representation and tight-set restrictions for this repair. I read the theorem, representation, proof setup and Claims8-10 passages; the full later case analysis was not audited. The special intersection restrictions are additional assumptions absent from arbitrary matroids, so they cannot be imported as universal lemmas. A correct general substitute for those restrictions is the exact unpaid lemma.

Route B: circular fractional placement followed by perturbation. Even if one has a continuous circular placement with independent unit arcs, coincident locations must be separated without creating a dependent r-window. Density controls cardinalities of subsets, not the order of tied elements. For |E|=kr, gcd(|E|,r)=r, so the cited coprime-size result cannot settle positive rank r>1. Deleting or adding an element to make sizes coprime changes which subsets are bases and does not give a deletion-stable r-window certificate. No actual theorem for this perturbation step was read or invoked.

Route C: direct-sum decomposition helps but does not solve the connected case. Suppose each component M_j has n_j=kr_j elements and a good cyclic ordering. Fix a length-r pattern of component labels containing each j exactly r_j times, and repeat it k times. Fill each component's slots consecutively from its ordering. Every r consecutive slots contain exactly r_j occurrences of j, consecutive in that component's cyclic ordering; their union is therefore a basis of the direct sum. This proves a genuine closure lemma, including zero-rank empty components by omission. The existence of such component orderings in arbitrary connected matroids is still the target; the closure cannot replace that step. k=1 is immediate and supplies no new family credit. Empty/rank0 conventions do not resolve the positive-rank question.

## 380621: complexity of distance coloring, sustained routes

The actual original defines the graph on all integers, not a finite point set and not a finite circulant. Let nonempty D have maximum m and fix k. Define states as proper color words of length m. Connect a state to its one-symbol left shift with a new final color exactly when the resulting m+1 word violates no constraint at a distance in D. A proper coloring of Z gives an infinite walk. A repeated state in any sufficiently long forward walk gives a directed closed walk and hence a directed cycle. Conversely periodically repeating the transition labels of a directed cycle gives a bi-infinite coloring, because every distance constraint has length at most m and is checked in some transition block. A cycle may have length less than m; the overlap equations still check cross-period constraints. D empty is trivially colorable. Thus finite-state search decides the target coloring predicate.

Route A fails at NP membership: at most k^m states and a cycle of that length are not a polynomial-size certificate under binary encoding of D. Even unary m only gives an exponential generic bound. A polynomial certificate compression theorem remains unpaid. This route parallels the source's own decidability proof, so it earns zero independently of proof length.

Route B: encode a finite graph by integer labels and use its edge differences. This preserves the chosen graph as a subgraph only if all unwanted differences are excluded. More seriously, the distance graph includes all translates of every chosen edge; a coloring on the chosen finite labels need not extend consistently to the entire integer line. No full reduction with both implications and polynomial bit size has been proved. NP-hardness of finite circulant coloring alone supplies no such extension lemma.

Route C: residue coloring and divisibility. If D contains no multiple of q, residue classes modulo q give a q-coloring. Dividing by gcd(D) reduces to isomorphic components. These sufficient conditions do not characterize arbitrary k>=3. Restricting to divisibility chains or two distances changes the input class and cannot prove the fixed-k general decision statement. Neither NP membership nor NP-hardness is completed.

## 388061: literal decidability and intended efficient problem

For equal bipartition sizes n, order the left vertices. Define F(i,S,t) to mean that the first i left vertices can be matched to exactly the right-vertex subset S using exactly t red edges. Set F(0,empty,0) true and all other i=0 states false. For |S|=i, F(i,S,t) is the disjunction over neighbors v in S of F(i-1,S\{v},t-color(i,v)). Induction proves soundness by adding the last edge and completeness by removing it from any witness matching. Out-of-range t is false, and unequal part sizes cannot admit a perfect matching. This is a full finite algorithm on paper, not implemented or executed. It is exponential and a routine direct decomposition of perfect matchings, so the literal wording without a complexity bound does not create significant discovery credit.
