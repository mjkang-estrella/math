                                                    Abstract. In a set equipped with a binary operation, (S, ·), a subset U is defined to be avoidable if
                                                    there exists a partition {A, B} of S such that no element of U is the product of two distinct elements
                                                    of A or of two distinct elements of B. For more than two decades, avoidable sets in the natural
                                                    numbers (under addition) have been studied by renowned mathematicians such as Erdös, and a

                                                    the generalized notion of an avoidable set and determine the avoidable sets in several families of

                                                                                           1. Introduction
                                                In a set equipped with a binary operation, (S, ·), a subset U is defined to be avoidable if there
                                             exists a partition {A, B} of S such that no element of U is the product of two distinct elements of
                                             A or of two distinct elements of B; the partition {A, B} is said to avoid U .
                                                Avoidable sets were first studied by Alladi, Erdös, and Hoggatt ([1]) in 1978. However, through-
                                             out the intervening decades, avoidable sets have been studied only in the case (S, ·) = (N, +). All
                                             of the relevant concepts in this particular case generalize in the context of the definition presented
                                             above: the operation need not be commutative or even associative. In the case of (N, +), only a
                                             few families of sets ([2], [5], [3]) have been shown to be avoidable, and almost no general facts are
                                             known about the nature of avoidable sets. In this paper, we discuss avoidable sets in a different
                                             setting: namely, when S is a group.

                                                                                         2. Abelian Groups
                                                Note: In this section, all groups will be written additively, and the identity element will be
                                             denoted by 0.
                                                First, we present two basic definitions that will be used throughout the paper. We say that U
                                             is saturated if U is maximal among the collection of avoidable subsets of S. Also, for any subset
                                             U , we define the associated graph, GU , to be the graph whose vertices are the elements of S,
                                             where two elements are connected by an edge if and only if their sum is an element of U ; then U
                                             is avoidable if and only if GU is 2-colorable.
                                                Abelian groups present a particularly nice case to study: if |U | = n, then each vertex x in GU
                                             will have degree n or n − 1, depending on whether 2x is in U or not. In this section we discuss
                                             the avoidable sets in abelian groups, commencing with a complete categorization of the avoidable
                                             sets in Z/nZ, and proceeding with a discussion on the nature of avoidable sets in arbitrary finitely
                                             generated abelian groups. Throughout this section, we will call an element b even if there exists x
                                             such that 2x = b, and odd otherwise.

                                             2.1. Saturated Sets in Z/nZ. In this section, all equalities, unless stated otherwise, are in Z/nZ.
                                                We first note that since all avoidable sets in Z/nZ are finite, to categorize all avoidable sets we
                                             need merely categorize the saturated sets. We present first two lemmas about unavoidable sets.

   As all cycles have the same length, these complements comprise the entirety of the second cycle.
We can then pair off the cycles into pairs which are c-complements of each other and color one
cycle of each pair arbitrarily; this induces a consistent coloring on the other cycle. Therefore, sets
of the form {0, b, 2b + (2k + 1)} (or equivalently, sets of the form {0, c, 2c} for c odd and c 6= n2 ) are
avoidable, and saturated by Lemma 1. The only two-element set containing 0 not contained in any
of these sets is {0, 2k + 1}, which is therefore saturated.
   (c) The proof given for part (b) works when b ≡ 2 (mod 4), with the minor modification that
now either value of x for which 2x = b yields an avoidable set {0, b, x}. Meanwhile, if b ≡ 0 (mod 4),
both such values of x are even, and consequently there are no 3-element avoidable sets containing
{0, b}, so {0, b} is saturated.
   This completes the proof of Theorem 1.                                                                

                                                 5. Conclusion
   In general, the case of non-abelian groups is harder than the case of abelian groups, because the
group may not have a simple, easy-to-manipulate, presentation. There are some recurring themes,
however: all of the saturated sets in the cases of both abelian and non-abelian groups are either
small relative to the order of the group (in the families we have discussed, constant with respect to
the order of the group) or the nontrivial coset of a subgroup of order 2. It is in general true that
the nontrivial coset of a subgroup of order 2 is avoidable (and saturated unless all elements of the
subgroup have order 2); an interesting open question is whether or not all other avoidable sets are
small relative to the size of the group.
   Other desirable results would include linking the avoidable sets in a group to the avoidable sets
in a subgroup or quotient group. In the first case, the problem with passing to a subgroup is that
saturated sets entirely contained in the subgroup will of course still be avoidable, but may not be
saturated; for example, in the group D2n+1 , the subset {e} is saturated, but it is certainly not
                                 Z
saturated in the subgroup (2n+1)Z    . In the second case, we would like to lift avoidable sets in the
quotient group to avoidable sets in the larger group simply by lifting the partition; however, two
distinct lifts of a single element in the quotient group may multiply to some element in the lift of
the avoidable set.
   If these results and a characterization of avoidable sets in the symmetric group Sn were obtained,
the problem for finitely generated groups would be solved. However, the latter also proves daunting.
In Sn , the set of all odd permutations is always avoidable (and saturated for n ≥ 3); in S4 , all the
other saturated sets have size 1. It is not true in general that all the other saturated sets have size
1, though; for instance, in S6 , the set {(12)(34), (56)} is avoidable. Nevertheless, saturated subsets
of Sn other than the set of odd permutations tend to be quite small.

    Let G be a finite abelian group and S a subset of G. The Cayley sum graph Cay+ (G, S) [4] is a graph
with vertex set G and edge set {{1, h} | 1, h ∈ G, 1h ∈ S}, where S is a square free subset of G. When

Wheeler [4] and Lev [10] established the conditions for a Cayley sum graph of a finite abelian group to
be connected, respectively. Besides, Cheyne, Gupta and Wheeler [4] also derived some necessary and
sufficient conditions for a Cayley sum graph of a finite abelian group to be bipartite. Grynkiewicz, Lev
and Serra [5] determined the connectivity of a Cayley sum graph of a finite abelian group. Amooshahi
and Taeri [2] classified simple connected cubic integral Cayley sum graphs. Amooshahi and Taeri [1]
deduced some necessary and sufficient conditions for a Cayley sum graph of a finite group to be connected.
Moreover, they also characterized cubic connected integral Cayley sum graphs. Inspired by the above
results, we consider the necessary and sufficient conditions for generalized Cayley graphs to be connected
and bipartite, respectively.

generalized Cayley graphs of finite abelian groups to be bipartite. In Section 3, we derive some necessary
and sufficient conditions for generalized Cayley graphs of finite groups to be connected. In Section 4, we
determine the groups whose all cubic generalized Cayley graphs are connected and integral by the aid of
the results in Section 3.

Theorem 3.5. Let GC(G, S, α) be a generalized Cayley graph of a finite group G. Then GC(G, S, α) is connected if
and only if one of the following conditions holds:
  (i) S is not contained in a right coset of any proper subgroup of G;
                                  Q. Liao et al. / Filomat 39:30 (2025), 10871–10882                                 10876

 (ii) S is contained in a nontrivial right coset of a proper subgroup H of G, where H = ⟨SS−1 ⟩ and it satisfies
      |G : H| = 2 and α(H) = H.

Proof. Suppose GC(G, S, α) is connected and let H be the smallest subgroup of G such that S is contained
in some right coset of H. Assume S ⊆ H1 for some 1 ∈ G. Then S−1 ⊆ 1−1 H, which yields that SS−1 ⊆
H11−1 H = H. Thus, ⟨SS−1 ⟩ ≤ H. Since for any s ∈ S, we have S ⊆ ⟨SS−1 ⟩s, which implies that S is also
contained in a right coset of ⟨SS−1 ⟩. By the choice of H, ⟨SS−1 ⟩ = H follows. From Lemma 3.2, either H = G
or |H| = 21 |G|. For the latter case, H is a normal subgroup of G. It follows that S−1 S ⊆ 1−1 HH1 = H and
then ⟨S−1 S⟩ ≤ H. By Lemma 3.3, we obtain α(H) = α(⟨SS−1 ⟩) = ⟨S−1 S⟩ ≤ H. Since |α(H)| = |H|, we have
α(H) = H. In addition, we assert that 1 < H. Otherwise, assume 1 ∈ H, then S ⊆ H. From Lemma 3.4, we
obtain the connected component C(e) containing e in GC(G, S, α) satisfies C(e) ⊆ ⟨S⟩ ≤ H, which contradicts
that GC(G, S, α) is connected.
    Conversely, we show that both the conditions (i) and (ii) imply GC(G, S, α) is connected. Let s ∈ S. If S
is not contained in a right coset of any proper subgroup of G, then we have G = ⟨SS−1 ⟩ since S ⊆ ⟨SS−1 ⟩s.
Thus, by Lemma 3.4, C(e) = G, which implies that GC(G, S, α) is connected. Let H = ⟨SS−1 ⟩ < G. If S is
contained in H1 for some 1 ∈ G \ H, where α(H) = H and |G : H| = 2, then by Lemmas 3.3 and 3.4, we have
C(e) = α(H) ∪ Hs = H ∪ Hs = H ∪ H1 = G. Thus, GC(G, S, α) is connected.

  By Theorem 3.5, we obtain another equivalent condition for generalized Cayley graphs to be connected,
which is more convenient to use in general.

Theorem 3.6. Let GC(G, S, α) be a generalized Cayley graph of a finite group G. Then GC(G, S, α) is connected if
and only if G = ⟨S⟩, |G : ⟨SS−1 ⟩| ≤ 2 and α(⟨SS−1 ⟩) = ⟨SS−1 ⟩.

Proof. Necessity. Assume GC(G, S, α) is connected. Since the connected component C(e) containing e in
GC(G, S, α) satisfies C(e) = ⟨S−1 S⟩ ∪ ⟨SS−1 ⟩s ⊆ ⟨S⟩ and C(e) = G for any s ∈ S, we have G = ⟨S⟩. Note that
S ⊆ ⟨SS−1 ⟩s, by Theorem 3.5, ⟨SS−1 ⟩ = G or α(⟨SS−1 ⟩) = ⟨SS−1 ⟩ and |G : ⟨SS−1 ⟩| = 2.
   Sufficiency. We split the proof into two cases:
   Case 1: G = ⟨S⟩ = ⟨SS−1 ⟩ = ⟨S−1 S⟩.
    In this case, we claim that there is no proper subgroup of G such that S is contained in a right coset of
this subgroup. If the claim is not true, let H < G and S ⊆ H1 for some 1 ∈ G. Then SS−1 ⊆ H11−1 H = H.
Further, G = ⟨SS−1 ⟩ ≤ H, a contradiction. Thus, by Theorem 3.5 (i), the result follows.
   Case 2: G = ⟨S⟩, |G : ⟨SS−1 ⟩| = 2 and α(⟨SS−1 ⟩) = ⟨SS−1 ⟩.
    If there is a proper subgroup H of G such that S ⊆ H1 for some 1 ∈ G, then we obtain ⟨SS−1 ⟩ ≤ H. Since
|G : ⟨SS−1 ⟩| = 2, it follows that H = ⟨SS−1 ⟩. Clearly, 1 < H. Otherwise, S ⊆ H and then ⟨S⟩ = G ≤ H, a
contradiction. Using Theorem 3.5 (ii), it follows that GC(G, S, α) is connected.

Remark 3.7. If G is a finite abelian group, then α(⟨SS−1 ⟩) = ⟨SS−1 ⟩ always holds. Thus, the generalized Cayley
graph of a finite abelian group G with respect to the pair (S, α) is connected if and only if G = ⟨S⟩ and |G : ⟨SS−1 ⟩| ≤ 2.

   According to Theorem 3.6, we give the equivalent conditions of the connected generalized Cayley
graphs to be bipartite.

the following statements are equivalent:

  (i) GC(G, S, α) is bipartite;

 (ii) |⟨SS−1 ⟩| = 12 |G|;

(iii) S ∩ ⟨SS−1 ⟩ = ∅.

Proof. As GC(G, S, α) is connected, by Theorem 3.6, we have ⟨S⟩ = G, |G : ⟨SS−1 ⟩| ≤ 2 and α(⟨SS−1 ⟩) =
