                                                                                 1. Introduction
                                              A matroid abstracts linear dependence, and one may ask, as Rota and Crapo did
                                           [Kun20], whether it gives rise to a second matroid built from its own structure. For
                                           realizable matroids there are two candidates, one from the circuits and the other
                                           from the maximal proper flats. The former goes back to Longyear [Lon80] for binary
                                           matroids and was defined for realizable matroids by Oxley and Wang [OW19]; the
                                           latter has appeared as the type I adjoint of Bixby and Coullard [BC88], as the
                                           matroid of the derived code of Jurrius and Pellikaan [JP15], and as the cocircuit
                                           matroid described by Kung, following Crapo [Kun20]. The two constructions are
                                           related by matroid duality, and neither is determined by the matroid alone.
                                              The classical approach to building a matroid on the maximal proper flats without
                                           a realization is the adjoint of Cheung [Che74], a geometric lattice of the same rank
                                           into which the inverted flat lattice of a matroid M embeds with atoms mapped
                                           onto atoms. Yet an adjoint need not exist: the Vámos matroid V8 has none [Che74].
                                           Another approach employs the Dilworth completion, which embeds an arbitrary
                                           finite lattice into a geometric lattice. Applied to the inverted flat lattice of M , it
                                           produces a geometric lattice, called the complete adjoint of M [Bry86b, Exercise
                                           7.59]. However, the embedding does not control the ranks of the images of the flats
                                           of M , so the complete adjoint is not an adjoint in general, as V8 already shows.
                                              In this paper, for every matroid M , we construct two matroids determined by
                                           M alone: the syzygy matroid Syz(M ) on the collection of circuits of M and the
                                           combinatorial adjoint Adj(M ) on the collection of maximal proper flats of M . As
                                           in the realizable case, the two are related by the duality Adj(M ) ≃ Syz(M ∗ ).
                                              The construction of Syz(M ) proceeds in two stages. The first stage takes an
                                           arbitrary finite set as the ground set and extends any seed, that is, any collection
                                           of nonempty subsets of that set, to the dependent-set collection of a matroid by
                                           adding the sets required by the elimination axiom for dependent sets, taking upward
                                           closure, and repeating these two steps until the collection stabilizes. This stage

requires no matroid at the outset, in contrast to single-element extensions, which

   The second stage uses an analogue of Gaussian elimination to measure how many
circuits are needed to span a given collection of circuits. The matroid Syz(M ) is
obtained by applying the first stage to the seed consisting of those collections of
circuits of M that, in this sense, are spanned by fewer circuits than they contain.1
The first stage also yields the combinatorial derived matroid of Freij-Hollanti, Jurrius,
and Kuznetsova [FJK23] when applied to a seed included in this one. However,
the first stage is not monotone in its input, so this inclusion implies no comparison
between the two matroids.
   Dualizing the construction, that is, applying it to M ∗ and taking complements
in E(M ), yields the combinatorial adjoint Adj(M ). Under this complementation,
dependent sets of M ∗ become nonspanning sets of M . The matroid Adj(M ) can
be computed either from Syz(M ∗ ) or directly within the flat lattice of M . Like
Adj(M ), the complete adjoint exists for any matroid, but the two do not coincide
in general; see Remark 5.16.
   Both Syz(M ) and Adj(M ) are simple, and both are connected when M is. We
determine Syz(M ) for M = Uk,k+3 and M = V8 . If M has rank 3, then Adj(M )
is an adjoint of M . In rank 4 this can fail: whenever M has V8 as a restriction,
Adj(M ) has rank 3 and is therefore not an adjoint of M .
   The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the two constructions
in the realizable case and their duality, and Section 3 shows how any collection
of nonempty subsets of an arbitrary finite set generates a matroid. The analogue
of Gaussian elimination is developed in Section 4, where Syz(M ) is constructed,
with loops split off as free summands. Section 5 transports the construction to the
maximal proper flats and studies Adj(M ), with coloops split off dually.
1.1. Notation and terminology. We write E(M ) for the ground set of a matroid
M . We let n and k denote |E(M )| and r(M ), respectively, and identify E(M ) with
[n] = {1, . . . , n} when the elements need to be indexed, in which case we may write
a subset by concatenating its elements, as in 134 = {1, 3, 4}. We denote by C(M ),
B(M ), and L(M ) the collections of circuits, bases, and flats of M , respectively. A
subscript M on a function or operator indicates that it is taken with respect to M ,
as in the rank function rM and the closure operator clM . We omit the subscript
when the matroid is clear from the context.
   Throughout, contains refers to membership and includes to inclusion: a set
contains its elements and includes its subsets. Hyperplane is reserved for its classical
meaning, a codimension-one subspace of a vector or projective space. A flat that is
maximal among the proper flats of M is called a maximal proper flat, or mp-flat for
short; such a flat is commonly called a hyperplane in the matroid literature. We
write H(M ) for the collection of mp-flats of M . For a subset X ⊆ E(M ), the corank
of X in M is corankM (X) := r(M ) − rM (X), so the mp-flats of M are precisely its
flats of corank 1. This quantity should not be confused with the rank rM ∗ (X) of X
in M ∗ , which is sometimes called the corank in the literature.

                    2. Constructions from Realizations
   A realization of a matroid yields one matroid from the circuit vectors and another
from the normal vectors of the hyperplanes corresponding to the mp-flats. Both
depend on the realization, whereas the constructions of Sections 4 and 5 do not.
   Let L be a k × n matrix of rank k over a field F, let V be its row space, and let
V ⊥ be the orthogonal complement of V in Fn . For e ∈ [n] and X ⊆ [n], denote by
Le the column of L indexed by e, and by LX the submatrix of L consisting of the
columns indexed by X. We read L in two complementary ways.
  (LS) Linear span viewpoint. Regard the columns of L as vectors in Fk . The
       sets X ⊆ [n] such that the columns of LX are linearly independent form
       the independent sets of a matroid M on [n] of rank k. When we say that a
       collection of vectors forms a matroid, we mean it in this sense. The zero
       columns of L are the loops of M , and each nonzero column defines a point in
       Pk−1 = P(Fk ); thus M records the linear dependencies among these points.
 (HA) Hyperplane arrangement viewpoint. The projective space P(V ) can
      be identified with Pk−1 through the isomorphism induced by Fk → V ,
      x 7→ xT L. Regard the columns of L as linear functionals on V . These
      functionals satisfy the same linear relations as the columns, and hence also
      form the matroid M . Each nonzero functional defines a hyperplane in P(V ),
      and M records the incidence relations among these hyperplanes.

2.2. Adjoint matroids. Let J ⊆ [n] be a subset of size k − 1. Let LJ,i denote the
matrix obtained from LJ by deleting the i-th row. Define a vector cJ ∈ Fk by
                                            i

The vector cJ is nonzero if and only if the rank of LJ is k − 1. In this case, cJ is
orthogonal to the hyperplane spanned by the columns of LJ .
   Two nonzero vectors cJ , cJ ′ ∈ Fk are parallel if and only if they determine the
same hyperplane, equivalently, if and only if clM (J) = clM (J ′ ). In this case, they
represent the same point in Pk−1 . If H is an mp-flat of M , then the vectors cJ
for the independent sets J ⊆ H with |J| = k − 1 all represent the same point in
Pk−1 , which we denote by cH . We use cH to denote both the point in Pk−1 and any
representative vector in Fk ; the intended meaning will be clear from the context.
From the hyperplane arrangement viewpoint (HA), cH is the point of Pk−1 at which
the hyperplanes defined by the nonzero columns of LH meet.

H ∈ H(M ) form a matroid on H(M ), which we denote by Adj(M, L). It depends
on the realization L of M , as the following example shows.

   Following Cheung [Che74], an adjoint of M is a simple matroid M ′ of the same
rank for which there exists an injective order-preserving map from the inverted flat
lattice L(M )′ to L(M ′ ) sending the atoms of L(M )′ onto those of L(M ′ ). From
viewpoint (HA), one sees that Adj(M, L) is an adjoint of M .
   Since the vectors cH realize Adj(M, L), the construction can be iterated. Let
M (0) denote the simplification of M , so that L(M (0) ) is isomorphic to L(M ), and
let L(0) be a submatrix of L consisting of one column from each parallel class of M .
For i ≥ 1, put M (i) := Adj(M (i−1) , L(i−1) ), and let L(i) be a matrix whose columns
are representative vectors cH for the mp-flats H of M (i−1) , so that L(i) realizes
M (i) and the sequence M (1) , M (2) , . . . is determined by L. Kung [Kun20] calls the
sequence M (0) , M (1) , M (2) , . . . the Crapo sequence.
   The embedding of L(M (i) )′ into L(M (i+1) ) sends each pair of flats of M (i) to a
modular pair of flats of M (i+1) , and it is surjective if and only if M (i) is modular.
Moreover, M (2i) is an extension of M (2i−2) and M (2i+1) is an extension of M (2i−1) ,
as observed by Kung [Kun20]; see also [FTW24, Section 5]. Hence both sequences
M (0) , M (2) , . . . and M (1) , M (3) , . . . stabilize, that is, become constant from some
term on, if and only if M (t) is modular for some t. If F is finite with q elements,
then each M (i) is a restriction of the projective geometry PG(k − 1, q), so both
sequences stabilize. In this case, the matroid at which M (0) , M (2) , . . . stabilizes is a
simple modular extension of M (0) of the same rank, and the largest such extension
realizable over F is PG(k − 1, q), cf. [Kun20].

            3. Generating Matroids from a Collection of Sets

seed we generate a matroid on E whose dependent-set collection includes the seed.
We recall the dependent-set axioms of Brylawski [Bry86a]: a collection D ⊆ 2E is
the dependent-set collection of a matroid on E if and only if

        e ∈ D1 ∩ D2 .

               4. Gaussian Operations and Syzygy Matroids
   We construct a matroid on C(M ) by producing a seed from the circuits of M and
applying the construction of Section 3. The seed records which collections of circuits
are spanned by fewer circuits than they contain, where spanning is understood in
the sense of a matroid-theoretic analogue of Gaussian elimination developed below.
   A loop e of M forms the circuit {e}, which meets no other circuit of M , so
loops play no part in the Gaussian operations. Put p = |clM (∅)|. Then C(M ) is
the disjoint union of C(M \clM (∅)) and the collection of the p circuits {e} with
e ∈ clM (∅). We define

4.2. Gaussian circuit operations. We formulate a matroid-theoretic analogue of
Gaussian elimination for circuit systems of M . Gaussian elimination for a matrix is
carried out by the following three row operations.
    (1) Swap two rows.
    (2) Multiply a row by a nonzero scalar.
    (3) Replace a row by the sum of itself and a scalar multiple of another row.
A circuit system is a collection rather than an ordered list, so swapping two rows
has no effect on it. Scaling a row by a nonzero scalar changes the row but not its
support, and a circuit system records supports and nothing else. Hence neither
operation alters the circuit system, and the third operation is the only one carrying
combinatorial content.
   Suppose that two nonproportional rows are both nonzero at some coordinate.
Adding a suitable multiple of one to the other eliminates that coordinate, leaving a
nonzero vector whose support is included in the set obtained from the union of the
two supports by removing that coordinate. On the combinatorial side, the circuit
elimination axiom asserts that removing a common element from the union of two
distinct circuits leaves a set that includes a circuit, and we take the passage to such
a circuit as the third operation on circuit systems.
Definition 4.6. Let (C1 , C2 ) be an ordered pair of distinct circuits of M and let

4.5. Syzygy matroids. We now construct a matroid on C(M ) from the Gaussian
spanning number. A first candidate for its spanning-set collection is the collection
T = {A ⊆ C(M ) : sp∞ (A) = C(M )} of circuit systems that Gaussian span C(M ).
Recall the spanning-set axioms of Brylawski [Bry86a]: a collection S ⊆ 2E is the
spanning-set collection of a matroid on E if and only if
   (S0) E ∈ S.
   (S1) If S ∈ S and S ⊆ S ′ ⊆ E, then S ′ ∈ S.
   (S2) For each E ′ ⊆ E, the minimal members of S including E ′ are equicardinal.
On the ground set C(M ), the collection T satisfies (S0) and (S1) by Proposition
4.17(1) and (2), but not (S2) in general, as Example 4.24(3) shows. Since Section 3
provides a procedure for adjoining dependent sets but none for adjoining spanning
sets, we turn to dependent sets.
   In a matroid, the rank of a subset X is the least size of a subset whose closure
includes X, so X is dependent precisely when it is spanned by fewer elements than it
contains. The Gaussian spanning number is defined in the same way, with Gaussian
spans in place of closures. Accordingly, we take as the seed the collection of circuit
systems that are Gaussian spanned by fewer circuits than they contain, that is,
those A with |A| > sn(A).

  Dualizing any system of matroid axioms yields another system of matroid axioms.
For example, the spanning-set axioms are obtained from the independent-set axioms
by duality: a set S ⊆ E(M ) spans M if and only if E(M )\S is independent in M ∗ .
We now dualize the dependent-set axioms.

Proposition 5.14. Let M be a matroid of rank 3, with or without coloops. Then
Adj(M ) has rank 3 and is an adjoint of M .
Proof. Suppose first that M has no coloops. Then Adj(M ) has rank 3 by Corollaries

Proposition 5.9, this holds for F = E(M ) and for mp-flats F , and it is clear for
F = clM (∅). Now let F be a rank-1 flat. Since r(M ) = 3, there are x ∈       / F and
y∈ / clM (F ∪ {x}), and H1 := clM (F ∪ {x}) and H2 := clM (F ∪ {y}) are distinct

Then r(N ) = 3 − p, and Adj(M ) ≃ Adj(N ) ⊕ Up,p by the beginning of this section.
Moreover, L(M ) ≃ L(N )×L(Up,p ), and L(Up,p ), the lattice of subsets of a p-element
set, is isomorphic to its inverted lattice via complementation. Hence, taking the
product of the map ψ for N with this isomorphism, we reduce to showing that
Adj(N ) has rank r(N ) and is an adjoint of N . This is immediate for r(N ) ≤ 1. If
r(N ) = 2, then the mp-flats of N are its rank-1 flats, of which there are at least
two, so Adj(N ) is simple on at least two elements by Proposition 5.9, hence has
rank at least 2, and rank exactly 2 by Corollary 5.6. Thus L(N )′ and L(Adj(N ))
are lattices of rank 2, and ψ matches their atoms bijectively, so ψ is an isomorphism
between the two lattices.                                                            □

Remark 5.16. Let M be as in Theorem 5.15. Since an adjoint of M has the same
rank as M , the matroid Adj(M ) is not an adjoint of M , and the failure already
appears at the rank-1 flats of M . Let F be a rank-1 flat of M . Take rank-2 flats
F1 and F2 of M with F1 ∩ F2 = F . Both have corank 2 in M , so ψ(F1 ) and
ψ(F2 ) are rank-2 flats of Adj(M ) by Corollary 5.13, and they are distinct since
ψ is injective. Then ψ(F1 ) ∪ ψ(F2 ) has rank at least 3 in Adj(M ), hence rank 3
by Theorem 5.15, and so spans Adj(M ). Since ψ(F1 ) ∪ ψ(F2 ) ⊆ ψ(F ), we have
clAdj(M ) (ψ(F )) = H(M ). Yet ψ(F ) ̸= H(M ) since rM (F ) = 1, so ψ(F ) is not a flat
of Adj(M ). This is where Adj(M ) departs from the complete adjoint, in which the
image of every flat is a flat [Bry86b, Exercise 7.59]. The complete adjoint of M ,
into which the inverted flat lattice L(M )′ embeds, has rank at least 4 and so does
not coincide with Adj(M ).
