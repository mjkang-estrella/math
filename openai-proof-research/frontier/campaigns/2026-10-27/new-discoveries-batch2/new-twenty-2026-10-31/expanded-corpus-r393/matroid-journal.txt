                                               Abstract
              Let M be an arbitrary matroid with circuits C(M ). We propose a definition of
          a derived matroid δM that has as its ground set C(M ). Unlike previous attempts
          of such a definition, our definition applies to arbitrary matroids, and is completely
          combinatorial. We prove that the rank of δM is bounded from above by |M | −r(M )
          and that it is connected if and only if M is connected. We compute examples
          including the derived matroids of uniform matroids, the Vámos matroid and the
          graphical matroid M (K4 ). We formulate conjectures relating our construction to
          previous definitions of derived matroids.
          Mathematics Subject Classifications: 05B35


1        Introduction
Let M be an arbitrary matroid. Gian-Carlo Rota [16] and Henry Crapo [6, 11] asked,
partly independently and with various precise formulations, for a natural definition of a
matroid δM that has as its ground set the collection of (co)circuits of M . Programmat-
ically, this would mean — among other things — a combinatorialization of notions such
as syzygies and resolutions in algebraic geometry. For represented matroids, such a con-
struction is known [15, 10], but the combinatorics of δM is in general not uniquely defined

by that of M , unless M is projectively unique [14], for example binary. The special case
of binary matroids M was carefully studied by Longyear [12]. Another related notion is
the lattice-theoretic notion of an adjoint geometric lattice to the lattice of flats of M [3],
but this does also not exist for all matroids. Moreover, when an adjoint geometric lattice
exists, it is not necessarily unique [1]. We propose a purely combinatorial construction of
δM , defined via the rank function on M , and via an operation that resembles a closure
operation on the collection of dependent sets.
    To our knowledge, the first actual definition in the literature of a derived matroid is by
Longyear [12], in the case of binary matroids. However, these definitions are predated by
predictions of their basic properties, made by Crapo [6], and more specific questions asked
by Rota [16]. These predictions and questions, as well as some later work in the field,
were formulated in two dual versions, with the ground set of the derived matroid being
either the circuits or the cocircuits of the underlying matroid. For consistency, in this
paper we reformulate all such results, whenever feasible, to the circuit-based definitions.
    We start by introducing the background on matroids and earlier work on derived
matroids in Sections 2 and 3, respectively. In Section 4, we introduce our construction
and give several cryptomorphic descriptions of it. We then give several examples of
combinatorial derived matroids in Section 5. In Section 6 we show that the combinatorial
derived matroid is connected if and only if the matroid is connected. In Section 7 we
investigate the independent sets and rank of our construction, and in particular show that
the rank is bounded from above by |M | − r(M ). Section 8 is devoted to comparing our
construction to the earlier definitions discussed in Section 3, and in particular deriving
some conditions under which they are equal. Finally, in Section 9 we point out some
directions for further research.


2    Preliminaries on matroids
In this paper, we use several cryptomorphic definitions of matroids. Our construction of
the derived matroid will start with a matroid defined in terms of circuits, and construct
the derived matroid in terms of the dependent sets of the latter. We refer the reader to
[13] for a reference on matroid theory.

Definition 1 (Matroid). A matroid M is a pair (E, D) where E is a finite set and D a
family of subsets of E satisfying

(D1) ∅ ∈
       / D;

(D2) if D ∈ D and D ⊆ D0 then D0 ∈ D;

(D3) if D1 , D2 ∈ D and D1 ∩ D2 ∈
                                / D, then (D1 ∪ D2 )\{e} ∈ D for all e ∈ D1 ∩ D2 .

We call the members of D the dependent sets of M . The subsets of E that are not in
D are thus independent. An inclusion-minimal dependent set is called a circuit, and the
family C of circuits of a matroid M is characterized by the axioms

       / C;
(C2) if C1 , C2 ∈ C and C1 ⊆ C2 then C1 = C2 ;
(C3) if C1 , C2 ∈ C are distinct and e ∈ C1 ∩ C2 , then there exists C3 ∈ C such that
     C3 ⊆ (C1 ∪ C2 )\{e}.
Notation 2. Unless explicitly mentioned otherwise, E denotes a finite set, and its power
set is denoted by 2E . The set of natural numbers N includes zero. If A and B are finite

indexed by B. We write Fm×B as a shorthand for F{1,2,...,m}×B , and similarly FA×n as a
shorthand for FA×{1,2,...,n} .
Definition 3 (Rank and nullity). Let M = (E, D) be a matroid on the finite ground set
E. The rank function r : 2E → N is defined by
                                 r(S) = max{|T | : T ⊆ S, T 6∈ D},
and the nullity function n : 2E → N is defined by n(S) = |S| − r(S).
   It follows from property (D2) that rank and nullity are both increasing functions
on 2E , and from (D3) that the rank function is submodular and the nullity function is
supermodular, meaning that
                                r(S) + r(T ) ⩾ r(S ∩ T ) + r(S ∪ T )
and
                               n(S) + n(T ) ⩽ n(S ∩ T ) + n(S ∪ T )
for all S, T ⊆ E [13].
    Let R be a matrix with entries in the field F. The matroid associated to R is a
matroid M (R) whose ground set is the set of columns of R and a subset of columns is
said to independent if the corresponding submatrix is full-rank. A matroid M is called
representable over the field F if there exists some R ∈ Fm×E such that M (R) ∼   = M . We
sometimes refer to the row space of R as a code representing M .
    We will also need the notion of fundamental circuits with respect to a fixed basis B of
M.
Definition 4. Let B be a basis for M . For e ∈ E \ B, the fundamental circuit CeB is the
unique circuit contained in B ∪ {e}.
   Uniqueness follows as n(B ∪ {e}) = 1, and since B is independent it follows that
e ∈ CeB .

3     Earlier definitions of derived matroid
Several authors have investigated structures similar to the combinatorial derived matroid
we are about to define. As explained before, the idea goes back to Crapo and Rota. We
will describe four approaches to the question and explain how these are related.

3.1    Longyear
Longyear [12] introduced the notion of the derived matroid when M is a binary matroid.
The primary motivation in [12] was to display the essential structure of a binary matroid
in a computationally more tractable way. To do so, Longyear defined the notion of circuit
bases, and correspondingly dependent sets of circuits, in terms of Kirkhoff sums, also
known as iterated symmetric difference, and equivalent to binary sums. The Kirkhoff
sum of the sets A1 , . . . Ak is the set

Definition 5. A circuit basis in a matroid M is a minimal collection A ⊆ C(M ) such
that every circuit in C(M ) is the Kirkhoff sum of some subset of A.
    It follows from the definition that a set A ⊆ C(M ) is dependent in δM precisely if it
has a non-empty subset A0 ⊆ A such that the Kirkhoff sum of the circuits in A0 is empty.
In other words, A0 ⊆ A should be such that every element in E is be contained in an even
number of the sets in A0 . Longyear showed that under this definition, the derived matroid
of a binary matroid is also a binary matroid. However, for a non-binary matroid, the set
of circuit bases have no reason to constitute the set of basis for a matroid.
Definition 6. Let M be a binary matroid. The matroid whose ground set is C(M ) and
whose bases are the circuit bases in M is called the Longyear derived matroid, and is
denoted δL (M ).

3.2    The Oxley–Wang definition
A more general definition was introduced in [15] for represented matroids, which are ma-
troids along with a fixed representation. The derived matroid δM (R) of a representation
R of the matroid M is determined by the generator matrix whose column vectors are the
minimal support vectors in the dual of the given representation of M . By construction,
such derived matroids are representable.
    Let M = (E, C) be a matroid on the finite ground set E with the set of circuits C. To
motivate our further definitions, we first consider the case of a matroid M represented by
a code Q ⊆ Fn , with dual code Q⊥ ⊆ Fn for some field F. As is commonplace in coding
theory, for S ⊆ [n], we denote by Q|S the projection of Q to the coordinates in S, and by
Q(S) the shortened code

                                  Q(S) = {q ∈ Q : supp(q) ⊆ S}.

For a circuit C ∈ C(M ), there is a codeword qC in Q⊥ with supp(qC ) = C, and such a
codeword is unique up to scalar multiple. We call qC a circuit vector of Q on C.
Definition 7. Let Q ⊆ Fn be a linear code, and let C be the collection of circuits of the
associated matroid. Moreover, let A ∈ Fn×C be the matrix that has a circuit vector qC in

the electronic journal of combinatorics 30(2) (2023), #P2.8                              4
the column indexed by the circuit C ∈ C. We define the derived code δ(Q) to be the row
span of A, and the Oxley–Wang derived matroid δOW (Q) to be the matroid represented
by δ(Q).

    Clearly, if v ∈ Q⊥ is not a circuit vector, then v can be written as the sum of two
vectors in Q⊥ whose support is strictly contained in supp v. Thus, Q⊥ is generated
by its minimal support vectors, i.e. the circuit vectors. It immediately follows that
the column space of the matrix A in Definition 7 is Q⊥ , and so the rank of δOW is
dim δ(Q) = r(A) = dim(Q⊥ ) = n − dim(Q).
    We can also present a family of explicit bases for δOW via fundamental circuits. For a
fixed basis B and distinct elements e, f 6∈ B, we clearly have e 6∈ Cf B . It follows that the
collection
                                δB = {CeB : e ∈ E \ B}
is independent in δOW . Since |δB| = |E \ B| = n − dim Q, we see that δB is a basis for
δOW (Q) for every basis B of M (Q).

3.3    Jurrius–Pellikaan
Independently of the work in [12], Jurrius and Pellikaan [10] defined the derived code of a
linear code. This work was then not noticed by Oxley and Wang [15]. Both constructions
give a derived matroid for a representable matroid, and we will see that the definitions
are dual to each other. The main difference is thus that the following definition from [10]
uses the cocircuits as the ground set for the derived code. The construction is based on
the construction of a derived hyperplane arrangement given in [5, §5.10].

Definition 8 ([10], Definition 4.1). Let G be a k × n matrix of rank k over a field F. Let
J ⊆ {1, . . . , n}. Let gj be the j-th column of G. Let GJ be the k × |J| submatrix of G
consisting of the columns gj with j in J. Let GJ (X) be the k×(|J|+1) matrix obtained by
adding the column X = (X1 , . . . , Xk )T to GJ , where X1 , . . . , Xk are variables. Let Gi,J be
the (k −1)×|J| matrix obtained by deleting the i-th row of GJ . Let ∆J (X) = det(GJ (X))
and ∆i,J= det(Gi,J ) in case |J| = k − 1. Let D(G) be the derived matrix of G of size
       n
k × k−1     with entries ∆i,J with i = 1, . . . , k and J ⊆ {1, . . . , n} of size k − 1 ordered
lexicographically. The matrix D2 (G) is obtained by removing zero columns and columns
that are a scalar multiple of another column. The derived code D2 (C) is the code with
generator matrix D2 (G).

    It was shown in [10] that the construction of D2 (C) is independent of the choice of
generator matrix G. Geometrically, D2 (G) is constructed as follows. For every subset
of k − 1 columns of G, check if they are independent in Fk . If not, add a zero column
to D(G). If they are independent, take the coefficients of a linear form determining this
(k − 1)-space and make them into a column of D(G). Then remove zeros and multiple
subsets that span the same (k − 1)-space.
    From this geometric interpretation, one can see that this construction is the same as
δOW of the dual matroid. Every set of k − 1 columns of G (i.e., elements of M ) that

the electronic journal of combinatorics 30(2) (2023), #P2.8                                      5
has rank k − 1 contributes to a column in D(G). If another set of k − 1 columns spans
the same (k − 1)-space, the corresponding column in D(G) will be removed from D2 (G).
Hence all the columns of D2 (G) correspond to a hyperplane of the matroid represented
by G. Since there is a one-to-one correspondence between hyperplanes of M and circuits
of M ∗ , this gives the desired duality relation. Note that in the construction of δOW one
gets a representation that is not of full rank, whereas the definition of D2 (G) does, by
definition, give a full rank matrix.

3.4     Adjoint matroid
The construction of an adjoint of a matroid is closely related to that of the derived code.
Where the derived code always exists, the adjoint does not. It was noted in [10, Remark
4.12] that the construction of the derived code could not be generalised to matroids.
    Let L be a geometric lattice. That is, the lattice of flats of a simple matroid. Intuitively,
we construct the adjoint of L as follows: take the diagram of L, flip it upside down, and
add some elements to make this lattice geometric again. Flipping the lattice upside
down gives a lattice with rank function n(A) = r(L) − r(A), the nullity function of the
matroid. This function satisfies the first two of the rank axioms for matroids [13], but it
is not submodular. So, we need to add new elements for every pair A, B ∈ L such that
n(A ∪ B) + n(A ∩ B) > n(A) + n(B). This is the case for every pair that is not modular
in L.
    We will now give a formal definition of an adjoint [3].

Definition 9. Let L be a geometric lattice. Let Lopp be the opposite lattice of L, that
is, the lattice on the same elements with the order relation reversed. We say that L∆ is
an adjoint of L if L∆ is a geometric lattice such that r(L∆ ) = r(Lopp ), there exists an
injective embedding e : Lopp ,→ L∆ , and the points of Lopp are in bijection with the points
of L∆ . We write ψ : L → L∆ for the map that fist takes L to Lopp and then applies e.

      This seemingly not-too-demanding definition has some strict implications [3]:

Lemma 10. The map ψ : L → L∆ has the following properties:

   1. If A covers B in L, then ψ(B) covers ψ(A) in L∆ .

   2. For all A ∈ L, rL∆ (ψ(A)) = r(L) − r(A).

   3. For A, B ∈ L we have that ψ(A ∨ B) = ψ(A) ∧ ψ(B).

   The construction of the derived code by Jurrius and Pellikaan gives in fact an adjoint:
the map ψ defined in [10, Definition 4.15] satisfies the properties of the map ψ in Definition
9.

As with many other notions about matroids, taking the combinatorial derived matroid
commutes with taking the direct sum. That is: the combinatorial derived matroid of a
direct sum of two matroids is the direct sum of the combinatorial derived matroid of these
matroids. The proofs of the two results below are inspired by the proofs of Proposition

Proposition 30. Let M = M1 ⊕ M2 . Then δM = δ(M1 ) ⊕ δ(M2 ).

Proof. The circuits of M are given by C(M ) = C(M1 ) ∪ C(M2 ): every circuit of M is
either a circuit of M1 or a circuit of M2 . This means the ground set of δM can be written
as the disjoint union of C(M1 ) and C(M2 ).
    First we show that E0 (M ) = E0 (M1 )∪E0 (M2 ), where E0 is as defined in Proposition 20.
In other words, we want to prove that any set in E0 (M ) consists of circuits of either only
M1 , or only M2 . Let A ∈ E0 . Then we can write A = A1 ∪ A2 with A1 ⊆ C(M1 ),
A2 ⊆ C(M2 ) and A1 ∩ A2 = ∅. Since A ∈ A0 , we have that |A| > n(A) with n the nullity
function of M . This implies

                              |A1 | + |A2 | = |A|
                                            > n(A)
                                            = n(A1 ∪ A2 )
                                            = n(A2 ∪ A2 ) + n(A1 ∩ A2 )
                                            ⩾ n(A1 ) + n(A2 ).

Since nullity is bounded by cardinality, |A1 | + |A2 | > n(A1 ) + n(A2 ) implies that |Ai | >
n(Ai ) for at least one of i = 1, 2. This means that Ai ∈ A0 (M ). By minimality of A, we
get that A = Ai .
    Let ni be the nullity function of Mi , then n(Ai ) = ni (Ai ), so |Ai | > ni (Ai ) and thus
Ai ∈ A0 (Mi ). In fact Ai has to be in E0 (Mi ), because otherwise it would not be minimal
in A0 (M ). This proves that E0 (M ) = E0 (M1 ) ∪ E0 (M2 ).

constructs new sets from pairs of elements of Ej (M ) that have nonempty intersection,
and so for all j ⩾ 0,

This means that inductively we get Ej (M ) = Ej (M1 ) ∪ Ej (M2 ) for all j. We conclude that
E(M ) = E(M1 ) ∪ E(M2 ) and thus δM = δ(M1 ) ⊕ δ(M2 ).

Theorem 32. Let M be a matroid on ground set E such that supp(δM ) = E. Then M
is connected if and only if its combinatorial derived matroid δM is connected.

some fixed basis B of M are elements of the same connected component of δM . We will
now use the base exchange axiom iteratively to show that all fundamental circuits of M
are elements of the same connected component of δM , proving that δM is connected.
     If M has only one basis, we are done. So, let B1 and B2 be bases of M . We will use
induction on the size of B1 ∆B2 , which is always an even number as all bases have the
same cardinality.

8     Comparison of definitions
In this section, we present some partial results relating our construction to previous
notions of derived matroids from the literature. In Section 8.1, we relate δM to the
Oxley–Wang derived matroid δOW (R), where R is a representation of M , and as a special
case of this to the Longyear derived matroid δL (M ) when M is binary. In Section 8.2,
we compare the lattice of flats F(δM ) to adjoint lattices of the lattice of cyclic sets
U(M ) ∼= F(M ∗ ). All results in this section are admittedly rather preliminary, and mainly
serve as inspiration for further research.

8.1    Oxley and Wang
The relation between the combinatorial derived matroid and the Oxley–Wang derived
matroid is not entirely straightforward. In particular, Example 5.5 shows that when
M = M (K4 ) and F has characteristic two, there are representations of M over F, but
none of these representations yields δOW (R) ∼
                                             = δM , even if we replace F by an extension
field. However, we hope that in the precise sense of Conjecture 43, the combinatorial
derived matroid has less dependencies than any Oxley-Wang derived matroid. Our first
results in this direction are the following.
Lemma 40. If δM = (C, A0 ), then all dependent sets in δM are dependent in δOW (Q)
for every representation Q of M .
Proof. Let A ⊆ C. For a representation Q of M and C ∈ A, denote by qC the circuit
vector supported on C as in Section 3.2. If A ∈ A0 , then we have
                |A| > n(supp A) = dim(Q⊥ (supp S)) ⩾ dim span{qC : C ∈ A}.
Thus the vectors {qC : C ∈ A} are linearly dependent, so A is dependent in δOW (Q).
    This shows that for matroids such that δM = (C, A0 ), the combinatorial derived
matroid is generic, in a precise sense. In particular, this applies to uniform matroids
U (k, n) with k ⩾ n − 3, by Corollary 27. We also expect this to apply to many other
naturally occurring matroids of high rank. The simple Lemma 40 also shows that if
δM = (C, A0 ) we can characterize representations for which the Oxley–Wang derived
matroid equals the combinatorial derived matroid from mere enumerative properties of
δOW (Q).
Corollary 41. If δM = (C, A0 ) and Q is a representation of M such that δOW (Q) has
the same number of dependent sets as δM , then δOW (Q) = δM .
Proof. By Lemma 40 we have D(δM ) = A0 ⊆ D(δOW (Q)), so since the two sets are finite,
they must be equal if they have the same cardinality. Since δM and δOW (Q) also have
the same ground set C, they must thus be equal.
    In general, if M is representable, we conjecture that the combinatorial derived matroid
is generic in two different ways; one categorical and one algebraic-geometric. In order to
state Conjectures 43 and 45, we need some auxiliary standard definitions.

Definition 42. Let E be a finite set. The weak order on the class M of matroids with
ground set E is the partial order with M1 ⩾ M2 if every dependent set in M1 is also
dependent in M2 .

Conjecture 43. Let M be a representable matroid with set of circuits C. Then δM ⩾
δOW (R) for every representation R of M , where the order relation is the weak order on
the set of matroids on C.

    Note that Lemma 40 proves Conjecture 43 in the case that δM = (C, A0 ). It is
conceivable that Conjecture 43 may be approached with techniques similar to those in [9].
There, the authors studied conditions under which the collection of matroids with some
circuits prescribed have a maximal element in the weak order.
    The next example shows that it is possible that M is representable over a field F,
without any of the representations of M over F (or indeed over any extension field of F)
yielding δM as an Oxley-Wang derived matroid.

Example 44. Consider the graphical matroid M = M (K4 ) as in Example 28. Recall
that δM is the non-Fano matroid F7− , with dependent sets A = A0 .
    We will now look at Oxley–Wang derived matroids of M (K4 ). By Lemma 40, all sets
in A = A0 are dependent in δOW (R) for any representation over any field. Moreover, a
triple of circuit vectors can only be linearly dependent if every element in E occurs in at
least two of the circuits, which follows from Lemma 34. The only possible dependent set
in δOW (R), except for the sets in A, is therefore the triple of 4-circuits {1346, 1256, 2345}.
It is straightforward to see that these circuits are linearly dependent in the (projectively
unique) representation of M over a field F or characteristic 2, but not in a representation
over fields of characteristic 6= 2. This can intuitively be understood if we interpret a linear
combination of the circuit vectors supported on the circuits {1346, 1256, 2345} as a formal
sum of the cycles {abcd, abdc, acbd} in the graph. In order for such a formal sum to take
the value zero on the edge 6 = cd, the coefficient in front of the (directed) cycles abcd and
abdc must be the same, say α. But then the formal sum of cycles traverses the edge ab
2α times in the same direction, and so it can only be zero if 2α = 0, i.e., if char(F) = 2.
    Thus, we see that δOW (R) is the Fano matroid in characteristic 2, and the non-Fano
matroid in characteristic 6= 2. In particular, we get δ(M ) = δOW (R) for a representation
R of M if and only if the representation is in characteristic 6= 2.

8.2    Adjoint
The construction of the combinatorial derived matroid is iterative. If a set of circuits of
M satisfies the nullity condition (3) in Definition 13, the set is in A0 hence dependent. If
not, the only way to check its (in)dependence is by constructing iteratively all dependent
sets of δM and see if our set is one of them.
    The fact that there are sets for which (in)dependence is hard to check, means it is
also difficult to define a closure function of δM . We are not optimistic about finding a
definition of closure in δM without some form of iteration.
    As a result of the lack of closure in δM , we have not managed to compare the definition
of δM , that uses dependent sets, to that of the adjoint (Section 3.4), that uses closed sets.
We expect the following to be true.

Conjecture 46. If an adjoint of a matroid M exists, one of them is equal to the com-
binatorial derived matroid of the dual. Moreover, every adjoint of M is less δM ∗ in the
weak order on matroids with ground set C.


9     Suggestions for further research
We have developed the notion of the combinatorial derived matroid. Contrary to earlier
constructions for a derived matroid, our construction exists for all matroids and is unique.
   There are many open questions about our construction. Apart from the questions and
conjectures already stated in the text, we list here some more ideas for further research.

Question 47. Is there a characterization of the class of matroids N that are isomorphic
to the combinatorial derived matroid δM of some matroid M ?

the electronic journal of combinatorics 30(2) (2023), #P2.8                                     24
    By Proposition 4.12, a necessary condition is that every element in N that is contained
in a circuit is also contained in a circuit of size 3. Many more necessary conditions like
this should probably first be obtained, before a complete characterization is feasible.

Question 48. For which matroids does it hold that δM = M or δM = M ∗ ? The latter
property may be more natural, since the rank of δM is bounded from above by the rank
of M ∗ . It is also possible to repeat the procedure of taking the combinatorial derived
matroid of the dual multiple times, i.e. study the sequence

                                      M, δ(M ∗ ), δ(δ(M ∗ )∗ ), . . . .

Does this sequence converge? For earlier constructions of the derived matroid, these
questions have been studied in [15, Section 3] and [10, Section 4], see also [11].

Question 49. Is there any relation between δM and δM ∗ ?

Question 50. There are matroids that do not have an adjoint, whereas the combinatorial
derived matroid always exists. This means one of the properties in Definition 9 and/or
Lemma 10 is not in general satisfied by the combinatorial derived matroid. Which one?

Question 51. If X is a representation of M by topological spheres, as in [17, 2], does X
induce a representation δX of δM by topological spheres?
