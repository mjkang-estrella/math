                                                                                Abstract
                                                     A crucial permutation is a permutation that avoids a given set of
                                                 prohibitions, but any of its extensions, in an allowable way, results in a
                                                 prohibition being introduced.
                                                     In this paper, we introduce five natural types of crucial permutations
                                                 with respect to monotone patterns, notably quadrocrucial permutations
                                                 that are linked most closely to Erdős-Szekeres extremal permutations.
                                                 The way we define right-crucial and bicrucial permutations is consistent
                                                 with the definition of respective permutations studied in the literature
                                                 in the contexts of other prohibitions. For each of the five types, we
                                                 provide its characterization in terms of Young tableaux via the RSK
                                                 correspondence. Moreover, we use the characterizations to prove that
                                                 the number of such permutations of length n is growing when n → ∞,
                                                 and to enumerate minimal crucial permutations in all but one case. We
                                                 also provide other enumerative results.

                                                 Keywords: crucial permutation, right-crucial permutation, top-right-
                                                 crucial permutation, bicrucial permutation, tricrucial permutation, quadro-
                                                 crucial permutation, RSK correspondence, Erdős-Szekeres extremal per-
                                                 mutation

decreasing subsequence of length ℓ. It follows from the Robinson-Schensted-
Knuth (RSK) correspondence and the hook-length formula [11] that the num-
ber of permutations of length (k − 1)(ℓ − 1) without increasing subsequences
of length k and decreasing subsequences of length ℓ, k ≥ ℓ, is given by

This formula, in the case of k = ℓ, has been presented in [12] from 1969, and

    So, extending any permutation of length (k − 1)(ℓ − 1) that avoid in-
creasing and decreasing subsequences of lengths k and ℓ, respectively, by an
extra element to the left, or to the right, results in an occurrence of a mono-
tone subsequence in question. However, depending on k and ℓ, there may
be permutations of smaller lengths that have the same properties, namely,
that avoid the monotone subsequences in question but that do not admit
extensions to the right and/or to the left without introducing an unwanted
monotone subsequence. Such permutations, being of significant combinato-
rial interest, have not received any attention in the literature, and the main
goal of this paper is to fill in this gap.
    In fact, our studies in this paper belong to a much wider context. The
idea of a crucial object appearing, for example, in Combinatorics on Words
and Graph Theory, can be described as follows. Given a set of prohibitions
P , we consider the set S of all objects that avoid P , namely, the set of all
objects that do not contain elements of P as subobjects. Then, a crucial
object s ∈ S is an object that cannot be extended, in a specified way, to a
larger object in S. In other words, any attempt to enlarge s in the specified
way will necessarily introduce a prohibited subobject in P .
    In the context of our paper, the objects are permutations and the set
of prohibitions is increasing subsequences of length k and decreasing subse-
quences of length ℓ, while extensions of permutations by an element will be
defined in five ways (see Section 2 for formal definitions): (i) just to the right
(giving crucial permutations, the same as right-crucial permutations), which
is equivalent to extending just to the left, or by inserting the largest element,
or by inserting the smallest element; (ii) to the right and to the left (giving
bicrucial permutations); (iii) to the right and by inserting the largest element
(giving top-right-crucial permutations); (iv) to the right, to the left and by

2     Preliminaries
A pattern is a permutation of {1, . . . , k} in one-line notation. An occurrence
of a pattern p = p1 · · · pk in a permutation σ = σ1 · · · σn is a subsequence
σi1 · · · σik , where 1 ≤ i1 < · · · < ik ≤ n, such that σij < σim if and only if
pj < pm . For example, the permutation 23514 has two occurrences of the

avoids (that is, has no occurrences of) the pattern 321. A pattern p is
increasing (resp., decreasing) if p1 < p2 < · · · < pk (resp., p1 > p2 > · · · >
pk ) and we denote it ik (resp., dk ). Increasing and decreasing patterns are

2.1      Five types of crucial permutations
Each permutation can be viewed as a two-dimensional permutation diagram
where the height of the points corresponds to the value of the respective
elements. For example, the permutation 526413 (avoiding the patterns 123
and 4321) corresponds to the permutation diagram




Following a natural approach to define crucial permutations when looking at
permutation diagrams, we see that, up to diagram rotation, there are five
ways to specify allowed extensions of permutations to larger permutations:

   • extending just to the right (crucial permutations in the literature cor-
     respond to extending to the right);

   • extending to the right and to the left (corresponding to bicrucial per-
     mutations in the literature);

   • extending to the right and from above;


                                               4
   • extending to the right, to the left and from above (corresponding to
     tricrucial permutations introduced in this paper);

   • extending to the right, to the left, from above and from below (corre-
     sponding to quadrocrucial permutations introduced in this paper).

    Next, we give formal definitions. A permutation is (k, ℓ)-right (resp., left,
top, bottom)-crucial if it avoids the patterns ik and dℓ but any of its exten-
sions to the right (resp., to the left, from above, from below) results in a
permutation containing an occurrence of ik or dℓ . Sometimes, we omit the
word “right” in a “(k, ℓ)-right-crucial permutation” because “right-crucial”
objects in the literature [1, 2, 4] are called “crucial”. A permutation is (k, ℓ)-
bicrucial if it is (k, ℓ)-crucial and its reverse is (ℓ, k)-crucial (equivalently, if
it is (k, ℓ)-right-crucial and (k, ℓ)-left-crucial). A permutation is (k, ℓ)-top-
right-crucial if it is (k, ℓ)-crucial and its any extension from above results
in a permutation containing an occurrence of ik or dℓ . A (k, ℓ)-bicrucial
permutation is (k, ℓ)-tricrucial if its any extension from above results in a
permutation containing an occurrence of ik or dℓ . A (k, ℓ)-tricrucial per-
mutation is (k, ℓ)-quadrocrucial if its any extension from below results in a
permutation containing an occurrence of ik or dℓ .

    For any type of permutations introduced above, a permutation of that
type is minimal (resp., maximal) if there are no other permutations of the
same type of smaller (resp., larger) length. Also, for any type of permuta-
tions introduced above, a permutation of that type is irreducible if there is
no way to delete an element in the permutation that would result, in the
reduced form, in a permutation of the same type. Finally, for any type of
permutations introduced above, a permutation of that type is non-extendable
if there is no way to insert a new element in the permutation (keeping the
order of the other elements the same) that would result in a permutation of
the same type.

lengths, and indeed, (k, ℓ)-(bi,top-right-,tri,quadro)crucial permutations of
lengths between minimal and maximal, is an interesting and challenging re-
search direction.

2.2    The RSK correspondence
The Robinson-Schensted-Knuth correspondence, also referred to as the RSK
correspondence or the RSK algorithm, is a bijection between the set of all
permutations of length n and pairs (P, Q) of standard Young tableaux of the
same shape. Even though the RSK correspondence is a well known object in
algebraic combinatorics, we briefly review it here as several of our key results
in this paper rely on it.
    Let λ = (λ1 , λ2 , . . .)Pbe a partition of n ≥ 0, denoted λ ⊢ n. Hence,
λ1 ≥ λ2 ≥ · · · ≥ 0 and        λi = n. The Young diagram of a partition λ is a
left-justified array of squares with λi squares in the i-th row. For example,
the Young diagram of (4,3,1) is given by




    A standard Young tableau (SYT) of shape λ ⊢ n is obtained by placing
the integers 1, . . . , n (each appearing once) into the squares of the diagram
of λ (with one integer in each square) such that every row and column is
increasing. For example, an SYT of shape (4,2,1) is given by

                                     2 6 8
                                     5

    The basic operation of the RSK correspondence is row insertion, that is,
inserting an integer i into a tableau T with distinct entries and with increasing
rows and columns. Hence, T satisfies the conditions of an SYT except that
its entries can be any distinct integers, not necessarily 1, . . . , n. The process
of row inserting i into T produces another tableau, denoted T ← i, with
increasing rows and columns. If S is the set of entries of T , then S ∪ {i} is
the set of entries of T ← i. Now, T ← i is defined recursively as follows.
   • If the first row of T is empty or the largest entry of the first row of T is
     less than i, then insert i at the end of the first row.

   • Otherwise, i replaces (or bumps) the smallest element j > i in the first
     row of T . We then insert j into the second row of T by the same
     procedure.

3    Miscellaneous results
Lemma 2. Let σ = σ1 · · · σn be a (k, ℓ)-crucial permutation. Then, σ has an
occurrence of ik−1 and an occurrence of dℓ−1 both ending at σn (and having
no other elements in common).

Proof. Consider extending σ by the element σn+1 := σn + 1, which cannot
introduce an occurrence of the pattern dℓ as otherwise, such an occurrence
is given by σj1 > · · · > σjℓ−1 > σn+1 for some j1 < · · · < jℓ−1 < n and
σj1 > · · · > σjℓ−1 > σn would have been an occurrence of dℓ in σ (since
σn+1 −σn = 1), which is impossible. Hence, such an extension must introduce
an occurrence of the pattern ik as σ is (k, ℓ)-crucial. Since σn+1 − σn = 1,
such an occurrence must involve σn or else there would be an occurrence of


                                      7
ik in σ ending with σn . That shows that σ has an occurrence of ik−1 ending
with σn .
    On the other hand, consider extending σ by the element σn+1 := σn ,
which cannot introduce an occurrence of the pattern ik as otherwise, such an
occurrence is given by σj1 < · · · < σjk−1 < σn+1 for some j1 < · · · < jk−1 < n
and σj1 < · · · < σjk−1 < σn would have been an occurrence of ik in σ (since
σn+1 is one less than the element next to it in the obtained permutation of
length n + 1), which is impossible. Hence, such an extension must introduce
an occurrence of the pattern dℓ as σ is (k, ℓ)-crucial. Since σn − σn+1 = 1,
such an occurrence must involve σn or else there would be an occurrence of
dℓ in σ ending with σn . That shows that σ has an occurrence of dℓ−1 ending
with σn .
    Clearly, the only common element in the occurrences of the patterns ik−1
and dℓ−1 in question can be the final element σn .
    The proof of the next lemma is analogous to that of Lemma 2 and hence
is omitted.

Lemma 11. Let σ ∈ Sn . The following statements are equivalent:

  • σ is (k, ℓ)-right-crucial;

  • the reverse permutation r(σ) is (ℓ, k)-left-crucial;

    • the inverse permutation σ −1 is (k, ℓ)-top-crucial;

    • the complement of the inverse permutation c(σ −1 ) is (ℓ, k)-bottom-crucial.
Proof. Let D be the two-dimensional diagram corresponding to a (k, ℓ)-right-
crucial permutation σ. By definition, adding a column to D from the right
results in an occurrence of the pattern ik or dℓ .
    The reverse permutation r(σ) has the diagram r(D) obtained from D by
reversing the order of its columns. Any occurrence of the pattern im in σ
corresponds to an occurrence of the pattern dm in r(σ), and vice versa. Hence,
non-extendability of σ to the right with respect to ik and dℓ is equivalent to
non-extendability of r(σ) to the left with respect to iℓ and dk .
    The inverse permutation σ −1 has the diagram D ′ obtained from D by
transposition with respect to the secondary diagonal. Every occurrence of
the pattern im (resp., dm ) in σ corresponds to an occurrence of the pattern
im (resp., dm ) in σ −1 . Hence, non-extendability of σ to the right with respect
to ik and dℓ is equivalent to non-extendability of σ −1 from above with respect
to ik and dℓ .
    The complement permutation c(σ) has the diagram c(D) obtained from
D by reversing the order of its rows. Any occurrence of the pattern im in σ
corresponds to an occurrence of the pattern dm in c(σ), and vice versa. Hence,
non-extendability of σ to the right with respect to ik and dℓ is equivalent to
non-extendability of c(σ −1 ) from below with respect to iℓ and dk .

Recall that a permutation is (k, ℓ)-tricrucial if it is (k, ℓ)-bicrucial and any of
its extensions from above results in a permutation containing an occurrence of
ik or dℓ . The following theorem is an immediate corollary of Theorems 18 and
19 and the fact that the set of (k, ℓ)-tricrucial permutations is the intersection
of the sets of (k, ℓ)-bicrucial and (k, ℓ)-top-crucial permutations.
Theorem 31. Let σ ∈ Sn and (P, Q) be the pair of SYT corresponding to
σ. Then, σ is (k, ℓ)-tricrucial if and only if the following holds:
    • the number of columns in P (and Q) is k − 1;

    • the number of rows in P (and Q) is ℓ − 1;
    • P contains an increasing sequence of elements p1,k−1, p2,j2 , . . . , pℓ−1,jℓ−1

      for some i2 , . . . , ik−1 ;

    • Q contains an increasing sequence of elements q1,k−1 , q2,j2 , . . . , qℓ−1,jℓ−1
      for some j2 , . . . , jℓ−1 .

9      Directions of further research
We end our paper by stating a conjecture and a number of open problems
(in no particular order).


                                        27
Conjecture 1. Every (k, ℓ)-crucial permutation can be obtained by deleting
elements from a maximal (k, ℓ)-crucial permutation.

Problem 1. Let us fix a Young diagram D. How many crucial permutations
of a given type introduced in this paper exist with the shapes of P and Q
being D? The answer to this question is likely to be difficult to find for an
arbitrary D, so the problem can be reformulated for a fixed class of Young
diagrams, e.g. of some regular type, or having other specified properties.

  For the next problem, recall the definition of an irreducible bicrucial per-
mutation in Section 2.1.

Problem 2. Can we argue that for k > ℓ the only irreducible (k, ℓ)-bicrucial
permutations are of length k + 2ℓ − 5 and 2k + ℓ − 5? (See Remark 23 and
the proof of Theorem 20). Or do such permutations exist of other lengths?

Problem 3. Generalize Corollary 33 by enumerating minimal (k, ℓ)-tricrucial
permutations. This problem is equivalent to enumerating SYT Q of the form
in Theorem 32 with q1,k−1 < q2,2 .

Problem 4. Enumerate (k, ℓ)-(right-top,bi,tri,quadro)crucial permutations.

Problem 5. Explain bijectively the connection between (k, 3)-crucial per-
mutations of next minimal length mentioned in Remark 14 and the combi-
natorial objects mentioned in the sequence A077414 in [10].

    For the next problem, recall the definition of a non-extandable permuta-
tion in Section 2.1.

Problem 6. Note that any non-extendable permutation of any of the five
types introduced in this paper is necessarily quadrocrucial. Do non-extendable
(k, ℓ)-quadrocrucial permutations, apart from the maximal (k, ℓ)-quadrocrucial
permutations, exist? If so, characterize such permutations.
