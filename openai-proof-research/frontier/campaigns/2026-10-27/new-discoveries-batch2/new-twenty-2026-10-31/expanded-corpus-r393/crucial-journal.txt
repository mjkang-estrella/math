                                               Abstract
          A crucial permutation is a permutation that avoids a given set of prohibitions,
      but any of its extensions, in an allowable way, results in a prohibition being intro-
      duced.
          In this paper, we introduce five natural types of crucial permutations with re-
      spect to monotone patterns, notably quadracrucial permutations that are linked
      most closely to Erdős-Szekeres extremal permutations. The way we define right-
      crucial and bicrucial permutations is consistent with the definition of respective
      permutations studied in the literature in the contexts of other prohibitions. For
      each of the five types, we provide its characterization in terms of Young tableaux
      via the Robinson-Schensted correspondence. Moreover, we use the characterizations
      to prove that the number of such permutations of length n is growing when n → ∞,
      and to enumerate minimal crucial permutations in all but one case. We also provide
      other enumerative results.

However, depending on k and ℓ, there may be permutations of smaller lengths that have
the same properties, namely, that avoid the monotone subsequences in question but that
do not admit extensions to the right and/or to the left without introducing an unwanted
monotone subsequence. Such permutations, being of significant combinatorial interest,
have not received any attention in the literature, and the main goal of this paper is to fill
in this gap.
    In fact, our studies in this paper belong to a much wider context. The idea of a
crucial object appearing, for example, in Combinatorics on Words and Graph Theory,
can be described as follows. Given a set of prohibitions P , we consider the set S of all
objects that avoid P , namely, the set of all objects that do not contain elements of P
as subobjects. Then, a crucial object s ∈ S is an object that cannot be extended, in a
specified way, to a larger object in S. In other words, any attempt to enlarge s in the
specified way will necessarily introduce a prohibited subobject in P .
    In the context of our paper, the objects are permutations and the set of prohibitions
is increasing subsequences of length k and decreasing subsequences of length ℓ, while
extensions of permutations by an element will be defined in five ways (see Section 2 for
formal definitions): (i) just to the right (giving crucial permutations, the same as right-
crucial permutations), which is equivalent to extending just to the left, or by inserting
the largest element, or by inserting the smallest element; (ii) to the right and to the
left (giving bicrucial permutations); (iii) to the right and by inserting the largest element
(giving top-right-crucial permutations); (iv) to the right, to the left and by inserting the
largest element (giving tricrucial permutations); and (v) to the right, to the left and
by inserting the largest or smallest elements (giving quadracrucial permutations). The
most relevant to our paper are the studies in [1, 2, 4, 5] on (bi)crucial permutations with
respect to squares and aritheoremetic monotone patterns, although we do not use any of
the results from these papers as our prohibitions are of diﬀerent nature.
    Examples of natural questions to ask about crucial objects with respect to a given set
of prohibitions are: Do crucial objects exist? If so, then do crucial objects exist of any size
above certain size or crucial objects of maximal size exist? What is the size of a minimal
crucial object? What is the structure of crucial objects? How many crucial objects of a
given size are there? And so on.

2.1     Five types of crucial permutations
Each permutation can be viewed as a two-dimensional permutation diagram where the
height of the points corresponds to the value of the respective elements. For example, the
permutation 526413 (avoiding the patterns 123 and 4321) corresponds to the permutation
diagram




Following a natural approach to define crucial permutations when looking at permutation
diagrams, we see that, up to diagram rotation, there are five ways to specify allowed
extensions of permutations to larger permutations:
      • extending just to the right (crucial permutations in the literature correspond to
        extending to the right);

      • extending to the right and to the left (corresponding to bicrucial permutations in
        the literature);

      • extending to the right and from above;

      • extending to the right, to the left and from above (corresponding to tricrucial per-
        mutations introduced in this paper);

      • extending to the right, to the left, from above and from below (corresponding to
        quadracrucial permutations introduced in this paper).
     Next, we give formal definitions. A permutation is (k, ℓ)-right (resp., left, top, bottom)-
crucial if it avoids the patterns ik and dℓ but any of its extensions to the right (resp., to the
left, from above, from below) results in a permutation containing an occurrence of ik or
dℓ . Sometimes, we omit the word “right” in a “(k, ℓ)-right-crucial permutation” because
“right-crucial” objects in the literature [1, 2, 4] are called “crucial”. A permutation is
(k, ℓ)-bicrucial if it is (k, ℓ)-crucial and its reverse is (ℓ, k)-crucial (equivalently, if it is
(k, ℓ)-right-crucial and (k, ℓ)-left-crucial). A permutation is (k, ℓ)-top-right-crucial if it
is (k, ℓ)-crucial and any of its extension from above results in a permutation containing
an occurrence of ik or dℓ . A (k, ℓ)-bicrucial permutation is (k, ℓ)-tricrucial if any of
its extension from above results in a permutation containing an occurrence of ik or dℓ .
A (k, ℓ)-tricrucial permutation is (k, ℓ)-quadracrucial if any of its extension from below
results in a permutation containing an occurrence of ik or dℓ .

Hence, T satisfies the conditions of an SYT except that its entries can be any distinct

      then insert i at the end of the first row.

    • Otherwise, i replaces (or bumps) the smallest element j > i in the first row of T .
      We then insert j into the second row of T by the same procedure.

3    Properties of crucial permutations
In this section we present several properties of crucial permutations. In particular, we de-
scribe the minimal (k, ℓ)-(top-right,bi)crucial, permutations and enumerate them. Also,
we find all possible lengths of (k, ℓ)-crucial permutations. Moreover, we establish the
equivalences between (k, ℓ)-right-, (k, ℓ)-left-, (k, ℓ)-top-, and (k, ℓ)-bottom-crucial per-
mutations. We start with the following lemma.

Lemma 2. Let σ = σ1 · · · σn be a (k, ℓ)-crucial permutation. Then, σ has an occurrence
of ik−1 and an occurrence of dℓ−1 both ending at σn (and having no other elements in
common).

Proof. Consider extending σ by the element σn+1 := σn + 1, which cannot introduce an
occurrence of the pattern dℓ as otherwise, such an occurrence is given by σj1 > · · · >
σjℓ−1 > σn+1 for some j1 < · · · < jℓ−1 < n and σj1 > · · · > σjℓ−1 > σn would have been an
occurrence of dℓ in σ (since σn+1 − σn = 1), which is impossible. Hence, such an extension
must introduce an occurrence of the pattern ik as σ is (k, ℓ)-crucial. Since σn+1 − σn = 1,
such an occurrence must involve σn or else there would be an occurrence of ik in σ ending
with σn . That shows that σ has an occurrence of ik−1 ending with σn .
    On the other hand, consider extending σ by the element σn+1 := σn , which cannot
introduce an occurrence of the pattern ik as otherwise, such an occurrence is given by
σj1 < · · · < σjk−1 < σn+1 for some j1 < · · · < jk−1 < n and σj1 < · · · < σjk−1 < σn would
have been an occurrence of ik in σ (since σn+1 is one less than the element next to it in
the obtained permutation of length n + 1), which is impossible. Hence, such an extension
must introduce an occurrence of the pattern dℓ as σ is (k, ℓ)-crucial. Since σn − σn+1 = 1,
such an occurrence must involve σn or else there would be an occurrence of dℓ in σ ending
with σn . That shows that σ has an occurrence of dℓ−1 ending with σn .
    Clearly, the only common element in the occurrences of the patterns ik−1 and dℓ−1 in
question can be the final element σn .
    The proof of the next lemma is analogous to that of Lemma 2 and hence is omitted.

Lemma 3. Let σ = σ1 · · · σn be a (k, ℓ)-left-crucial permutation. Then, σ has an occur-
rence of ik−1 and an occurrence of dℓ−1 both beginning at σ1 (and having no other elements
in common).

has the following form:

    • the final element is σn = k − 1;

    • the first n − 1 = k − ℓ − 4 positions of σ are occupied by the interleaved occurrences

      formed by the elements in {k, . . . , k+ℓ−3}. In particular, any minimal (k, ℓ)-crucial
      permutation begins with either the smallest or the largest element.

9    Directions of further research
We end our paper by stating a conjecture and a number of open problems (in no particular
order).
Conjecture 39. Every (k, ℓ)-crucial permutation can be obtained by deleting elements
from a maximal (k, ℓ)-crucial permutation.
Problem 40. Let us fix a Young diagram D. How many crucial permutations of a given
type introduced in this paper exist with the shapes of P and Q being D? The answer
to this question is likely to be diﬃcult to find for an arbitrary D, so the problem can
be reformulated for a fixed class of Young diagrams, e.g. of some regular type, or having
other specified properties.
   For the next problem, recall the definition of an irreducible bicrucial permutation in
Section 2.1.
Problem 41. Can we argue that for k > ℓ the only irreducible (k, ℓ)-bicrucial permuta-
tions are of length k +2ℓ−5 and 2k +ℓ−5? (See Remark 23 and the proof of Theorem 20).
Or do such permutations exist of other lengths?
Problem 42. Generalize Corollary 33 by enumerating minimal (k, ℓ)-tricrucial permuta-
tions. This problem is equivalent to enumerating SYT Q of the form in Theorem 32 with
q1,k−1 < q2,2 .
Problem 43. Enumerate (k, ℓ)-(top-right,bi,tri,quadra)crucial permutations.
Problem 44. Explain bijectively the connection between (k, 3)-crucial permutations of
next minimal length mentioned in Remark 14 and the combinatorial objects mentioned
in the sequence A077414 in [10].

    For the next problem, recall the definition of a non-extendable permutation in Sec-
tion 2.1.
Problem 45. Note that any non-extendable permutation of any of the five types intro-
duced in this paper is necessarily quadracrucial. Do non-extendable (k, ℓ)-quadracrucial
permutations, apart from the maximal (k, ℓ)-quadracrucial permutations, exist? If so,
