                                                                                                1. Introduction
                                              Let G = (V (G), E(G)) be an undirected simple graph with vertex set V (G) = {v1 , v2 , . . . , vn } and the
                                          edge set E(G) = {e1 , e2 , . . . , er }, where |V (G)| = n and |E(G)| = r are respectively called the order and
                                          the size of G. We denote the degree of vertex vi , i = 1, 2, . . . , n by di = d(vi ). The Laplacian matrix of G
                                          is a matrix defined as follows: L(G) = D(G) − A(G), where D(G) is the vertex degree diagonal matrix,
                                          i.e., D(G) = diag(d1 , d2 , . . . , dn−1 , dn ) and A(G) is the (0, 1) adjacency matrix of G. Thus, the entries of
                                          the Laplacian matrix have the following form

                                              The study of graphs whose adjacency matrix has integer eigenvalues was probably introduced by
                                          F. Harary and A.J. Schwenk [12]. The same kind of problem has been addressed to the eigenvalues of the
                                          Laplacian matrix. A graph G whose Laplacian matrix has integer eigenvalues is called Laplacian integral.
                                          Some classes of Laplacian integral graphs have been identified. For example, R. Merris [18] showed that
                                          degree maximal graphs are Laplacian integral. J.J. Kirkland [16] characterized Laplacian integral graphs
                                          of maximum degree 3. The most well-known examples for such graphs are the complete graph Kn , the star
                                          graph Sn , and the Petersen graph having eigenvalues of its Laplacian matrix {0, nn−1 }, {0, 1n−2 , n} and
                                          {0, 25 , 54 }, respectively (the superscript indicates the multiplicity of the eigenvalue). Every threshold graph
                                          (i.e., a graph that can be constructed from isolated vertices by the operations of unions and complements)
                                          is Laplacian integral, as well. For more detail on Laplacian integral graphs, we refer the reader to the
                                          works [1, 4, 5, 9, 11, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 20, 21].
                                              One of the way to describe and study all the integral Laplacian graphs (if any) is to study graphs having
                                          certain kind of Laplacian spectra. Thus, starting with graphs with simple integer Laplacian eigenvalues,
                                          one then can consider integral Laplacian graphs with all simple Laplacian eigenvalues excluding one which
                                          is double. On the next step, it is allowed for graphs to have two multiple Laplacian eigenvalues etc.
                                              The first step in this scheme was undertaken by S. Fallat et al. in their work [5] on the integral Laplacian
                                          graphs with simple Laplacian eigenvalues.

Definition 1.1. A set S consisting of elements of {0, 1, . . . , n} (maybe repeated) such that |S| = n and
0 ∈ S is called Laplacian realizable if there exists a connected simple graph on n vertices whose Laplacian
spectrum coincides with S. In that situation, the graph G is said to realize S.
    Thus, the authors in [5] considered the sets of the form
(1.1)                                     Si,n = {0, 1, 2, . . . , n − 1, n} \ {i},
and found out when such sets are Laplacian realizable and completely described the graphs realizing Si,n .
The only problem that remained open in this case is whether the set Sn,n is Laplacian realizable or not.
This problem is now known as the Sn,n -conjecture and states that Sn,n is not Laplacian realizable for
every n ⩾ 2. This conjecture was proved for n ⩽ 11, for prime n, and for n ≡ 2, 3 mod 4 in [5]. Later,
Goldberger and Neumann [7] showed that the conjecture is true for n ⩾ 6, 649, 688, 933. The authors of
the present work established [10] that if a graph is the Cartesian product of two other graphs, then it does
not realize Sn,n .

                                               2. Preliminaries
    Let G be an undirected simple graph. The complement G of the graph G is a graph G on the same
vertices such that two distinct vertices of G are adjacent if and only if they are not adjacent in G.
    Given two graphs G1 = (V1 , E1 ) and G2 = (V2 , E2 ), the union of graphs G1 and G2 , denoted as G1 ∪G2 ,
is the graph G = (V, E) for which V = V1 ∪ V2 and E = E1 ∪ E2 . The union of k copies of the same graph
G is denoted as kG. The join G1 ∨ G2 of the graphs G1 and G2 is the graph obtained from G1 ∪ G2 by
joining every vertex of G1 with every vertex of G2 , that is, G1 ∨ G2 = (G1 ∪ G2 ).
    The Cartesian product of the graphs G1 and G2 is the graph G1 × G2 whose vertex set is the Cartesian
product V (G1 ) × V (G2 ), and for v1 , v2 ∈ V (F ) and u1 , u2 ∈ V (H), the vertices (v1 , u1 ) and (v2 , u2 ) are
adjacent in G1 × G2 if and only if either
       • v1 = v2 and {u1 , u2 } ∈ E(G1 );
       • {v1 , v2 } ∈ E(G2 ) and u1 =u2 .
One of the examples of the Cartesian product is the ladder graph which is the Cartesian product of two
path graphs, one of which has only one edge: Ln,1 = Pn × P2 .

    We denote the eigenvalues of the Laplacian matrix L(G) arranged in increasing order as
                                         0 = µ1 ⩽ µ2 ⩽ . . . ⩽ µn−1 ⩽ µn .
It is well known that L(G) has a zero eigenvalue corresponding to the eigenvector with equal entries, while
other eigenvalues are non-negative [22]. M. Fiedler [6, p. 298] showed that µ2 > 0 if and only if the graph G
is connected. This eigenvalue is usually known as the algebraic connectivity of G denoted by aG . The
Laplacian spectrum σL (G) of a graph G is the spectrum of the Laplacian matrix of G. The Laplacian
spectral radius denoted as ρ(G) of G is the absolute value of the largest eigenvalue of the Laplacian
matrix L(G), i.e., ρ(G) = max |µi |. The Laplacian spectrum of a graph is bounded from above by the
                             1⩽i⩽n
order of the graph, see, [19].
Proposition 2.1. Let G be a simple graph on n vertices. Then ρ(G) ⩽ n.

   As one can see from (2.1), the order of the join of two graphs is a Laplacian eigenvalue of the join. It
turns out that this fact is a necessary and sufficient condition for a graph to be a join, see, e.g., [23].
Theorem 2.4. Let G be a connected graph of order n. Then n is a Laplacian eigenvalue of G if and only
if G is the join of two graphs.
   However, if the order of a graph is a Laplacian eigenvalue of multiplicity 2, then we can get more
information about the graph.
Theorem 2.5. Let G be a connected graph of order n, and let n be the Laplacian eigenvalue of G of
multiplicity 2. Then G = F ∨ H where F is a join of two graphs, while H is not a join. Moreover, the
number 1 does not belong to the Laplacian spectrum of G in this case.
   Thus, the algebraic connectivity of any graph of order n with doubled Laplacian eigenvalue n is greater
than 1.
Proof. Indeed, since n is a Laplacian eigenvalue of G, the graph G is a join of two graphs according to
Theorem 2.4, that is, G = F ∨ H. Suppose that |F | = p and |H| = n − p for some integer number p,
1 ⩽ p ⩽ n − 1.
   Let us denote the Laplacian spectra of the graphs F and H as follows
(2.2)             0 = µ1 ⩽ µ2 . . . ⩽ µp−1 ⩽ µp       and     0 = λ1 ⩽ λ2 . . . ⩽ λn−p−1 ⩽ λn−p .

Then by Theorem 2.3, the Laplacian spectrum of G has the form
(2.3)        σL (G) = {0, (n − p) + µ2 , . . . , (n − p) + µp , p + λ2 , . . . , p + λn−p−1 , p + λn−p , n}.
Here the eigenvalues are not in the increasing order.
   Since the graph G has exactly two eigenvalues n by assumption, one of the numbers n − p + µp or
p + λn−p equals n while the other one is less than n. Without loss of generality, we can suppose that
n − p + µp = n and p + λn−p < n that implies µp = p and λn−p < n − p.
   Now from (2.3) we obtain that µp = p is a simple Laplacian eigenvalue of G. Indeed, if µp is not simple,
then n has multiplicity at least 3 that contradicts the assumption of the theorem. Consequently, the graph
F is a join with simple maximal Laplacian eigenvalue, whereas the graph H is not a join by Theorem 2.4,
since λn−p < n − p.
   According to (2.2)–(2.3), either (or both) numbers (n − p) + µ2 and p + λ2 is the minimal positive
Laplacian eigenvalue of the graph G. However, since the graph F of order p is a join, it is clear that p ⩾ 2,
at the same time n − p ⩾ 1. So both numbers (n − p) + µ2 and p + λ2 are greater than 1, as required. 
   The following proposition provides a necessary and sufficient condition on a graph G to have a Laplacian
eigenvalue 1 provided G is a join.
Theorem 2.6. Let a graph G be a join. The number 1 is a Laplacian eigenvalue of G if and only if
G = F ∨ K1 where F is a disconnected graph of order at least 2.
Proof. Let G be a join that has a Laplacian eigenvalue 1 and let G = F ∨ H where the graph F is of
order p while the graph H is of order n − p. According to (2.2)–(2.3), one of the numbers (n − p) + µ2 or
p + λ2 equals 1.
   If both of the numbers (n − p) + µ2 and p + λ2 equal 1, then µ2 = λ2 = 0 and (n − p) = p = 1. So in
this case G is a join of two isolated vertices, i.e., G = K1 ∨ K1 , and the Laplacian spectrum of G is equal
to {0, 2}. But this contradicts to the assumption that G has a Laplacian eigenvalue 1.
   Thus, we have that one of the numbers (n − p) + µ2 and p + λ2 is greater than 1. Without loss of
generality, we can suppose that (n − p) + µ2 = 1 and p + λ2 > 1 (as p is a positive integer number). This
implies that (n − p) = 1 and µ2 = 0. So the graph F has at least two Laplacian eigenvalues equal zero.
Consequently, p ⩾ 2 and F is disconnected [6, p. 298]. As (n − p) = 1, the graph H consists of a single
vertex K1 .
   Conversely, let G = F ∨ K1 where F is a disconnected graph of order at least 2. Since F is disconnected,
the multiplicity of the Laplacian eigenvalue 0 is at least 2, so by Theorem 2.3, the Laplacian spectrum
of G contains 1 is an eigenvalue of G.                                                                    

                                                 6. Discussion
   Inspired by the work [5] by Fallat et al. on the sets Si,n , we study the Laplacian realizability of the
sets S{i,j}m
           n
             . In the present paper, we completely described the number of sets realizing S{i,j}m    n
                                                                                                        for m = n
and m = n − 1 and gave an algorithm for constructing those graphs. Additionally, we also discussed some
aspects of the Conjecture 5.4 about the realizability of the set S{i,n}m
                                                                       n
                                                                         . This conjecture correlate with the
conjecture on the realizability of sets Sn,n introduced in [5].
   However, there are many open problems around the Laplacian realizability of sets S{i,j}m   n
                                                                                                . In the present
work we fixed the number m and sought for admissible numbers i and j absent in Laplacian spectra of
graphs. At the same time, there exist realizable sets with fixed i, j, and n with different m. For example,
from Tables 1–3 in Appendix A it follows that the sets S{1,3}24 and S{1,3}44 , S{2,4}15 and S{2,4}35 , S{2,4}36 and
S{2,4}56 are Laplacian realizable. Moreover, despite of Conjecture 5.5 for m = n and m = n − 1, there
exist numbers m such that some sets are realizable by more than one graph. For instance, the set S{1,3}47
is realized by two non-isomorphic graphs 2K1 ∨ (K1 ∪ C4 ) and K1 ∨ P2 × P3 , see Table 4. Thus, for the
future work we pose the following problem.
Problem 6.1. Given a set S{i,j}mn
                                   for fixed n and i < j, find all admissible m such that S{i,j}m
                                                                                                n
                                                                                                  is Laplacian
realizable. Find all m admitting realizability of S{i,j}m
                                                        n
                                                          by  a few  non-isomorphic graphs.
  As well, we are interested in Conjectures 5.4–5.5 and will devote to them and Problem 6.1 the next
parts of this work.
