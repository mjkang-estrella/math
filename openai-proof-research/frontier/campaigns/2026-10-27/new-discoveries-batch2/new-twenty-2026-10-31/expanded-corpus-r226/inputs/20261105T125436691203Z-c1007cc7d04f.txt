 Abstract 
    
 We study a two-player game played on undirected graphs called  Trail Trap , which is a variant of a game known as  Partizan Edge Geography . One player starts by choosing any edge and moving a token from one endpoint to the other; the other player then chooses a different edge and does the same. Alternating turns, each player moves their token along an unused edge from its current vertex to an adjacent vertex, until one player cannot move and loses. We present an algorithm to determine which player has a winning strategy when the graph is a tree and partially characterize the trees on which a given player wins. Additionally, we show that it is NP-hard to determine if Player 2 has a winning strategy on  Trail Trap  from the starting position, even for connected bipartite planar graphs with maximum degree  4 4 . We determine which player has a winning strategy for certain subclasses of complete bipartite graphs and grid graphs, and we propose several open problems for further study. 
  
 
 
 1  Introduction 

 
 Let  G = ( V ⁡ ( G ) , E ⁡ ( G ) ) G=(V(G),E(G))  be a graph (all graphs we consider are undirected, finite, and simple). A  walk  on  G G  is a sequence  v 1 ​ v 2 ​ … ​ v m v_{1}v_{2}\ldots v_{m}  of vertices in  V ⁡ ( G ) V(G)  such that  v i ​ v i + 1 ∈ E ⁡ ( G ) v_{i}v_{i+1}\in E(G)  for all  1 ≤ i ≤ m − 1 1\leq i\leq m-1 . A  trail  is a walk with no repeated edges and a  path  is a trail with no repeated vertices. In this work, we study a perfect information game,  Trail Trap , first introduced in  [ 2 ] , in which two players compete to build edge-disjoint trails on  G G . 
 
 
 To begin a game of  Trail Trap  on a graph  G G , Player 1 (P 1 1 ) chooses a starting vertex on which to place their token, then moves the token along an incident edge  e e  (should such an edge exist) to an adjacent vertex, completing their turn. Player 2 (P 2 2 ) then places their token on any vertex, and moves it along an incident edge  f ≠ e f\not=e  (should such an edge exist), to its second endpoint. Continuing with P 1 1 , the players alternate turns, each moving their token from its current vertex along an unused edge to a neighboring vertex. A player cannot make a move along an edge previously used by either player, but there are no restrictions on vertices: a player may place their token on the same vertex multiple times throughout the game, and the two tokens may simultaneously occupy a vertex. We apply the Normal Play convention: the first player unable to make a move loses the game. Since  | E ⁡ ( G ) | |E(G)|  is finite,  Trail Trap  cannot end in a draw. 
 
 
 We denote a  move  on a graph  G G , the act of moving a token from one vertex  u u  to an adjacent vertex  v v , by  u → v u\to v . Interchangeably, we say that a player  moves  from  u u  to  v v . An edge  u ​ v uv  is  used  if either player has moved from  u u  to  v v  or from  v v  to  u u  on any previous turn, and  unused  otherwise. Each player’s sequence of moves, at every turn, forms a trail through the graph. Thus, rather than moving tokens, we can think of the players as constructing two edge-disjoint trails on the graph. 
 
 
 We provide an example of gameplay on the “diamond” graph  G G , illustrated in Figure  1 . Suppose P 1 1  first makes the move  b → c b\to c , and suppose P 2 2  begins their trail with  c → d c\to d . From here, P 1 1 ’s second move must be  c → a c\to a , since this is the only unused edge incident to  c c . Similarly, P 2 2 ’s second move is  d → b d\to b , and P 1 1  then makes the move  a → b a\to b . Since P 2 2  has no available moves out of vertex  b b  (indeed, all the edges in the graph are used), the game is over and P 1 1  wins.

 However, P 2 2 ’s first move  c → d c\to d  wasn’t optimal, and with a different first move, P 2 2  can ensure victory. In fact, with optimal play, P 2 2  can win on  G G  no matter P 1 1 ’s first move. We leave this as an exercise to the reader. 
 
 
 When a game of  Trail Trap  ends, either P 1 1 ’s trail is of length one larger than P 2 2 ’s (and P 1 1  is the winner), or the trails have the same length (and P 2 2  is the winner).
In general, we say that a player has a  winning strategy  on a graph  G G  if they can win  Trail Trap  on  G G  no matter the other player’s moves. If P 1 1  has a winning strategy on  G G , then we say that  G G  is  P 1 1 -win  ( P 2 2 -win  is defined analogously). By the Fundamental Theorem of Combinatorial Games (see  [ 1 ] ), exactly one of P 1 1  and P 2 2  has a winning strategy on any given graph. In this paper, we address the problem of characterizing P 1 1 -win graphs for several families, including complete bipartite graphs and trees. We give a polynomial-time algorithm to determine the winner of  Trail Trap  on an arbitrary tree. On the other hand, we prove that it is NP-hard to determine if P 2 2  has a winning strategy even for connected planar bipartite graphs with maximum degree  4 4 . 
 
 
 In the following subsection, we provide a brief survey of Geography games, of which  Trail Trap  can be considered a variant. Then, in Section  2 , we provide a necessary and sufficient condition for a disconnected graph to be P 1 1 -win as well as a sufficient condition, using automorphisms, for a graph to be P 2 2 -win.
In Section  3 , we give exact win conditions for subsets of complete graphs, complete bipartite graphs, grid graphs, and prism graphs. In Section  4 , we characterize the P 1 1 -win trees and give an  O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2})  algorithm to determine the winner for trees of order  n n . In Section  5 , we prove that determining whether P 2 2  wins is NP-hard on a restricted class of graphs. Open questions arise in each section, and we summarize these in Section  6 . 
 
 
 1.1  Related Geography games 

 
 Trail Trap  can be seen as a variant of (Generalized) Geography, part of a larger family of walk-construction games (see  [ 4 ] ). Geography is a two-player game in which players take turns moving a single, shared token from one vertex to an adjacent vertex, beginning at a fixed starting vertex (or root). In  Vertex Geography , no vertex can be repeated, and in  Edge Geography , no edge can be repeated. As in  Trail Trap , the winner is the last player able to make a legal move. Both  Vertex Geography  and  Edge Geography  can be played on directed or undirected graphs. 
 
 
 Vertex Geography  is the simplest game in the Geography family; Fraenkel, Scheinerman, and Ullman  [ 13 ]  provide an exact characterization of when each player wins on an undirected graph  G G  with a given starting vertex  v v . Specifically, they show that  ( G , v ) (G,v)  is P 1 1 -win if and only if every maximum matching in  G G  includes an edge incident to  v v . In  Unrooted Vertex Geography , a generalization where P 1 1 ’s first move is to choose the starting vertex (and then P 2 2  makes the first “full” move to a new vertex), the graph  G G  is P 2 2 -win if and only if it has a perfect matching. It follows that the winners of both of these variants can be computed in polynomial time  [ 11 ] . Monti and Sinaimeri  [ 17 ]  consider a modified version of  Vertex Geography  where the goal is to determine whether P 1 1  can force a win within  k k  moves. They show that this problem is PSPACE-complete, even for bipartite graphs, but can be solved in polynomial time for trees. 
 
 
 Edge Geography  is, in general, more complicated than  Vertex Geography.  The winner for  Edge Geography  on undirected graphs can be computed in polynomial time for a few special families, including bipartite and complete graphs  [ 13 ] . However,  Undirected Edge Geography  in general is PSPACE-complete, even for subcubic planar graphs. The same holds for an unrooted generalization defined analogously to  Unrooted Vertex Geography.  On directed graphs, both  Vertex Geography  and  Edge Geography  are PSPACE-complete (for these general results, see Lichtenstein and Sipser  [ 16 ] , and Schaefer  [ 19 ] ). 
 
 
 In  partizan  variants of Geography, players have distinct options for their moves, either because the players have different (and often complex) rulesets (for instance, as in  [ 7 ] ) or because the players are using separate tokens.  Partizan  games, where the two players’ moves are no longer symmetric, are much more challenging to analyze than the impartial games mentioned above  [ 9 ] .
In  [ 14 ] , Fraenkel and Simonson considered the (rooted) games  Partizan Vertex Geography  and  Partizan Edge Geography , in which the two players are each assigned a starting vertex as well as their own token to move along unused edges in a graph without repeating a vertex (respectively, an edge).
They showed that the winning player of either game can be determined in  O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2})  time for directed trees and  O ⁡ ( n ) O(n)  time for undirected trees, where  n n  is the order of the tree. However, no other classes are known to be solvable in polynomial time. Fox and Geissler  [ 12 ]  show that  Partizan Vertex Geography  is PSPACE-complete for undirected bipartite graphs.  Partizan Vertex Geography  and  Partizan Edge Geography  are both NP-hard for directed acyclic graphs and directed planar graphs  [ 14 ] . Both games are known to be PSPACE-complete on bipartite digraphs with bounded degree: specifically, where the in-degree and out-degree of each vertex is at most two, and their sum is at most three  [ 14 ] . 
 
 
 Trail Trap  is formally a variant of  Unrooted Partizan Edge Geography . In this variation, each player places a token  and  moves along an edge on their first turn. This contrasts with the unrooted (impartial) games mentioned in  [ 13 ] , where P 1 1  places the token on their first turn, and P 2 2  is the first to move the token. The additional degree of freedom for the players in  Trail Trap  can change the outcome of the game. For example, the path on three vertices is P 1 1 -win in the game where both players place tokens before either moves, but P 2 2 -win in  Trail Trap .  Trail Trap  principally differs from these other games in its wide range of starting positions, and indeed much of our analysis (for instance, in  Theorem   13 ) focuses entirely on the players’ first moves. Most other geography variants are PSPACE-complete, and the reductions needed to prove such a complexity result inherently use a fixed starting location. A proof that Trail Trap is also PSPACE-complete, as we conjecture in Section  6 , would require some new techniques which we suspect would be of independent interest. 
 
 
 
 
 2  Preliminaries 

 
 We briefly introduce definitions and notation pertaining to degrees, distance, and moves. We adopt the standard graph-theoretic notation used in  [ 10 ]  and refer the reader there for additional background. 
 
 
 Given a graph  G G , the  degree  of a vertex  v v  in  V ⁡ ( G ) V(G) , denoted by  deg G ⁡ ( v ) \deg_{G}(v) , is the number of edges in  E ⁡ ( G ) E(G)  incident to  v v . The  distance  between vertices  u , v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) u,v\in V(G) , denoted by  d G ​ ( u , v ) d_{G}(u,v) , is the length of the shortest path from  u u  to  v v . By convention,  d G ​ ( u , v ) = ∞ d_{G}(u,v)=\infty  if there is no path from  u u  to  v v  in  G G . We write  deg ⁡ ( v ) \deg(v)  and  d ⁡ ( u , v ) d(u,v)  if the graph is clear from context. 
 
 
 In a game of  Trail Trap  on  G G , a  move   m m  is formally an ordered pair  u → v u\rightarrow v , where  u ​ v ∈ E ⁡ ( G ) uv\in E(G) . Let  u u  be the  tail  of move  m m  and  v v  be the  head . We let  m − 1 m^{-1}  denote the move  v → u v\rightarrow u .
We label turns by alternating players, such that P 1 1 ’s first move is turn  1 1 , P 2 2 ’s first move is turn  2 2 , and so on. (There is no formal difference between a move and a turn, but the colloquial usage of turn as a “time to make a move” is useful for us.) For a natural number  r ≤ | E ⁡ ( G ) | r\leq|E(G)| , an  r r -partial game   S S  of  Trail Trap  is an ordered  r r -tuple of moves, such that each edge of  G G  occurs at most once and, for all  i ∈ { 1 , … , r − 2 } i\in\{1,\ldots,r-2\} , the head of move  i i  is equal to the tail of move  i + 2 i+2 . An  r r -partial game is  incomplete  (has a legal continuation) if there exists an edge of  G G  not in  S S  and incident to the head of move  r − 1 r-1 . 
 
 
 We also introduce several definitions related to graph connectivity and trails. A graph  G G  is  connected  if there exists a trail in  G G  between any pair of its vertices, and  G G  is  disconnected  if it contains at least two  components , or maximal connected subgraphs. Given a trail  t = v 1 ​ v 2 ​ … ​ v m t=v_{1}v_{2}\ldots v_{m}  on  G G , let  ℓ ⁡ ( t ) = m − 1 \ell(t)=m-1  be the length of  t t . Let  ℓ ⁡ ( G ) \ell(G)  be the maximum length of a trail in  G G . We denote the maximum length of a trail in  G G  that starts at a given vertex  v v  by  ℓ ⁡ ( G , v ) \ell(G,v) . 
 
 
 In  Trail Trap , each player’s trail is restricted to a single component of  G G . On a disconnected graph  G G , there are two ways a game of  Trail Trap  can go: either both players make trails on the same component, or each plays on a separate component. If both players draw trails on the same component  H H , then it suffices to determine a winner for  H H . If both players use different components, however, then the game runs in parallel, with the players unable to interfere with one another’s trails. The longest trail in a disconnected graph is important, but does not completely determine who wins. Figure  2  shows an example graph  G G  where the longest trail is in a component which induces a P 1 1 -win graph, but  G G  itself is P 2 2 -win. In a winning strategy for P 1 1  on  G G , by Proposition  2 , P 1 1  must end their first move on the vertex of degree  3 3 . However, P 1 1  can then create a trail of at most four edges, which P 2 2  can match on the other component. 
 
 
 Figure 2 :  A disconnected P 2 2 -win graph in which the component containing the longest trail is a P 1 1 -win graph. 
 
 
 Proposition 1 . 
 
 A graph  G G  is  P 1 1 -win if and only if there exists a component  H H  with a trail  t = v 1 ​ v 2 ​ … ​ v m + 1 t=v_{1}v_{2}\ldots v_{m+1}  such that there exists a winning strategy for  P 1 1  on  H H  with first move  v 1 → v 2 v_{1}\to v_{2} , and for all other components  H ′ H^{\prime}  of  G G ,  ℓ ⁡ ( H ′ ) < m \ell(H^{\prime})<m . 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 The result is trivial where  G G  is connected, so assume  G G  is disconnected. Suppose first that  G G  is P 1 1 -win and that P 1 1  has a winning strategy on  G G  beginning with the move  v 1 → v 2 v_{1}\to v_{2}  on some component  H H . It follows that  H H  is P 1 1 -win and that there is a winning strategy for P 1 1  on  H H  beginning with the move  v 1 → v 2 v_{1}\to v_{2} , otherwise P 2 2  could win on  G G  by also playing on  H H . Suppose that the longest trail in  H H  beginning with  v 1 ​ v 2 v_{1}v_{2}  has length  m m . If there exists another component  H ′ H^{\prime}  where  ℓ ⁡ ( H ′ ) ≥ m \ell(H^{\prime})\geq m , then P 2 2  can play on  H ′ H^{\prime}  and create a trail at least as long as P 1 1 ’s longest possible trail. Hence, P 2 2  would win on  G G . Thus,  ℓ ⁡ ( H ′ ) < m \ell(H^{\prime})<m . 
 
 
 Conversely, suppose that there exist: (i) a component  H H  such that  H H  is P 1 1 -win, (ii) a winning strategy for  P1 {\rm{P}1}{}  on  H H  that starts with the move  v 1 → v 2 v_{1}\to v_{2} , and (iii) a trail of length  m m  on  H H  beginning with edge  v 1 ​ v 2 v_{1}v_{2} , and suppose that no other component  H ′ H^{\prime}  satisfies  ℓ ⁡ ( H ′ ) ≥ m \ell(H^{\prime})\geq m . We can construct a winning strategy for  P1 {\rm{P}1}{}  on  G G  as follows. First, P 1 1  plays the move  v 1 → v 2 v_{1}\to v_{2} . If P 2 2  plays on a separate component, they get a trail of length less than  m m  while P 1 1  can continue along a trail of length  m m . Thus, P 2 2  is forced to play on  H H . However, as there exists some winning strategy for P 1 1  on  H H , beginning with the move  v 1 → v 2 v_{1}\to v_{2} , P 1 1  can now win by following that P 1 1 -win strategy for  Trail Trap  on  H H .
∎ 
 
 
 
 To determine the winner on a disconnected graph, we must be able to compare the maximum trail length of components in general and from any given starting vertex. Upper and lower bounds are known for maximum trail length on a graph with a given number of vertices and edges  [ 6 ] , but despite the invariant’s simplicity, little else is known. For a given connected graph  G G , it is well known that there is a trail containing every edge in  G G  if and only if  G G  contains at most two vertices of odd degree. If  G G  contains no vertices of odd degree, then  G G  is Eulerian, and there exists a trail containing every edge and beginning and ending on the same vertex. Some related material exists on determining the number of edges by which a graph is deficient from being Eulerian  [ 5 ] . Without an independent means of determining maximum trail length, Proposition  1  is insufficient for determining a winner on a disconnected graph. We further show in Section  5  that determining whether a graph is P 2 2 -win is NP-hard for a certain class of connected graphs, and thus the problem is NP-hard for disconnected graphs as well. 
 
 
 There are some special cases for which the winner of  Trail Trap  can be easily determined. Trivially, if  | E ⁡ ( G ) | = ∅ |E(G)|=\emptyset , then  G G  is P 2 2 -win (P 1 1  is the first to be unable to move), and if  | E ⁡ ( G ) | = 1 |E(G)|=1 , then  G G  is P 1 1 -win. We assume henceforth that  | E ⁡ ( G ) | ≥ 2 |E(G)|\geq 2 . Additionally, a P 1 1 -win graph must contain a vertex of degree at least three. 
 
 
 Proposition 2 . 
 
 Let  G G  be a  P 1 1 -win graph with at least two edges. If  u → v u\to v  is  P 1 1 ’s  first move in a winning strategy, then  deg ⁡ ( v ) ≥ 3 \deg(v)\geq 3 .
In particular, the maximum degree of  G G  is at least  3 3 . 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Let  G G  be a graph with at least two edges. We denote P 1 1 ’s first move by  u → v u\to v  and P 2 2 ’s by  x → y x\to y .
Clearly, if  deg ⁡ ( v ) = 1 \deg(v)=1 , then P 1 1  cannot make a second move and loses.
If  deg ⁡ ( v ) = 2 \deg(v)=2 , then  G G  is again a P 2 2 -win graph, as demonstrated by setting  x = v x=v  and  y ∈ N ⁡ ( v ) − u y\in N(v)-u .
∎ 
 
 
 
 As a result, graphs with maximum degree at most  2 2  and at least two edges (including all nontrivial paths and cycles) are P 2 2 -win. 
 
 
 Another broad strategy is what Berlekamp, Conway, and Guy  [ 3 ]  call the “Tweedledee and Tweedledum” strategy, where P 2 2  mirrors each of P 1 1 ’s moves. The result also holds on multigraphs. An  automorphism  of a graph  G G  is a bijection  ϕ : V ⁡ ( G ) → V ⁡ ( G ) \phi:V(G)\rightarrow V(G)  that preserves edge relationships, i.e., for  v i , v j ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) v_{i},v_{j}\in V(G) , we have  v i ​ v j ∈ E ⁡ ( G ) v_{i}v_{j}\in E(G)  if and only if  ϕ ⁡ ( v i ) ​ ϕ ​ ( v j ) ∈ E ⁡ ( G ) \phi(v_{i})\phi(v_{j})\in E(G) . We call  ϕ \phi   involutive  if  ϕ ⁡ ( ϕ ⁡ ( v ) ) = v \phi(\phi(v))=v  for all  v ∈ G v\in G . An edge  v i ​ v j v_{i}v_{j}  of  G G  is a  fixed edge  of  ϕ \phi  if the edge is mapped to itself; that is, either  ϕ ⁡ ( v i ) = v j \phi(v_{i})=v_{j}  and  ϕ ⁡ ( v j ) = v i \phi(v_{j})=v_{i} , or  ϕ ⁡ ( v i ) = v i \phi(v_{i})=v_{i}  and  ϕ ⁡ ( v j ) = v j \phi(v_{j})=v_{j} . 
 
 
 Proposition 3 . 
 
 If a graph  G G  has an involutive automorphism with no fixed edges, then  G G  is  P 2 2 -win. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Let  G G  be a graph with an involutive automorphism  ϕ : V ⁡ ( G ) → V ⁡ ( G ) \phi:V(G)\rightarrow V(G)  with no fixed edges. If P 1 1  moves  v i → v j v_{i}\to v_{j}  on turn  k k , then P 2 2  moves  ϕ ⁡ ( v i ) → ϕ ⁡ ( v j ) \phi(v_{i})\to\phi(v_{j})  on turn  k + 1 k+1 . The move is legal since, if  k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 , P 2 2  ended their previous move on  ϕ ⁡ ( v i ) \phi(v_{i}) , edge  v i ​ v j v_{i}v_{j}  was unused before turn  k k , and  ϕ \phi  fixes no edges. It follows that  ϕ ⁡ ( v i ) ​ ϕ ​ ( v j ) \phi(v_{i})\phi(v_{j})  was unused before turn  k + 1 k+1 . Hence P 2 2  can always play after P 1 1 ; as  G G  is finite, P 2 2  wins.
∎

 We use a depth-first recursive algorithm to evaluate if a given graph  G G  is P 1 1 -win or P 2 2 -win. We say a player wins an incomplete  r r -partial game  I I  if they can win the game on  G G  with optimal play, given that the sequence of both players’ starting moves constituted the partial game  I I . For any incomplete  r r -partial game  I I , we iterate through all possible moves for the player whose turn it is and assert that they can win  I I  with optimal play if and only if they can win at least one of the resulting  ( r + 1 ) (r+1) -partial games extending  I I .
For highly symmetric graphs, such as complete or complete bipartite graphs, we speed up the computations by limiting the choices for the first few moves, without loss of generality. 
 
 
 We continue by considering complete graphs. For small cases, we are able to determine the winner by hand. In the following proposition, the result that  K 4 K_{4}  is P 1 1 -win is due to Arneman, Catanzaro, and Danison  [ 2 ] , but restated here with a shorter proof. 
 
 
 Proposition 4 . 
 
 The complete graph  K 4 K_{4}  is  P 1 1 -win, and  K 5 K_{5}  is  P 2 2 -win. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Denote the vertices of  K n K_{n}  by  v 1 , v 2 , … , v n v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n} . On  K 4 K_{4} , suppose without loss of generality that P 1 1  initially makes the move  v 1 → v 2 v_{1}\to v_{2} . We consider three cases based on P 2 2 ’s first move: 
 
 (i)  
 
 If P 2 2 ’s first move also ends at  v 2 v_{2} , then P 1 1  makes their second move on the only unused edge incident to  v 2 v_{2} , to win. 
 
 (ii)  
 
 If P 2 2 ’s first move ends at  v 3 v_{3}  (respectively,  v 4 v_{4} ), then P 1 1  should move  v 2 → v 4 v_{2}\to v_{4}  (resp.  v 2 → v 3 v_{2}\to v_{3} ). Irrespective of P 2 2 ’s second move, P 1 1  moves  v 4 → v 1 v_{4}\to v_{1}  (resp.  v 3 → v 1 v_{3}\to v_{1} ) to win. 
 
 (iii)  
 
 If P 2 2 ’s first move is  v 3 → v 1 v_{3}\to v_{1}  (resp.,  v 4 → v 1 v_{4}\to v_{1} ) then P 1 1  should move  v 2 → v 4 v_{2}\to v_{4} . P 2 2  must then move  v 1 → v 4 v_{1}\to v_{4}  (resp.  v 1 → v 3 v_{1}\to v_{3} ) and P 1 1  moves  v 4 → v 3 v_{4}\to v_{3}  to win. 
 
 
 
 
 Therefore, P 1 1  has a winning strategy on  K 4 K_{4} . 
 
 
 On  K 5 K_{5} , suppose again without loss of generality that P 1 1  initially moves  v 1 → v 2 v_{1}\to v_{2} .  4  The Game on Trees 

 
 We now discuss  Trail Trap  on an arbitrary tree  T T . Fraenkel and Simonson  [ 14 ]  showed that  Rooted Partizan Edge Geography  ( Trail Trap  with fixed starting vertices for both players) can be solved in  O ⁡ ( n ) O(n)  time on an undirected tree, which trivially provides an  O ⁡ ( n 3 ) O(n^{3})  time solution to  Trail Trap  on  T T  by iterating over all pairs of starting vertices.
We are able to shave off a factor of  n n  using Lemma  11  which shows that if P 1 1  has a winning strategy on  T T , then they must first move their token to a central vertex.
Since a tree has at most two central vertices, this provides an  O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2})  time algorithm to resolve  Trail Trap  on  T T  (see Theorem  14 ).
To better understand the structure of P 1 1 -win trees, we provide a set of necessary conditions for  T T  to be P 1 1 -win in Theorem  13 . We also discuss certain obstacles to a full characterization of P 1 1 -win trees. 
 
 
 4.1  Basic Observations 

 
 A key observation in solving  Trail Trap  on trees is that every trail in a tree is a path, and there is a unique path between each pair of vertices of a tree. The  eccentricity   ecc G ⁡ ( v ) \ecc_{G}(v)  (or simply  ecc ⁡ ( v ) \ecc(v) ) of a vertex  v v  in a graph  G G  is  max ⁡ { d ⁡ ( v , x ) : x ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) } \max\{d(v,x)\colon x\in V(G)\} . The  radius  of  G G  is  rad ⁡ ( G ) = min v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) ⁡ ecc ⁡ ( v ) \rad(G)=\min_{v\in V(G)}\ecc(v) , and the  diameter  of  G G  is  diam ⁡ ( G ) = max v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) ⁡ ecc ⁡ ( v ) \diam(G)=\max_{v\in V(G)}\ecc(v) . 
 
 
 Note that if  G G  is a tree, then  ℓ ⁡ ( G ) = diam ⁡ ( G ) \ell(G)=\diam(G)  and  ℓ ⁡ ( G , v ) = ecc G ⁡ ( v ) \ell(G,v)=\ecc_{G}(v)  for any vertex  v v , a fact which we will use extensively later. 
 
 
 The  center   C ⁡ ( G ) C(G)  of a graph  G G  is the set of vertices of minimum eccentricity, i.e.,   C ⁡ ( G ) = { x ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) : ecc ⁡ ( x ) = rad ⁡ ( x ) } C(G)=\{x\in V(G)\colon\ecc(x)=\rad(x)\} . A vertex  v ∈ C ⁡ ( G ) v\in C(G)  is called a  central vertex  of  G G .

 We now turn our attention to the algorithmic problem of determining the winner of  Trail Trap  on a tree. It suffices, by
Lemma  11 , to consider the case where P 1 1  starts by moving their token to
a central vertex.
Once P 2 2  makes their first move, determining the winner of  Trail Trap  is equivalent to determining the winner of a game of  Rooted Partizan Edge Geography . As part of providing an algorithm for a Geography variant on directed graphs, Fraenkel and Simonson  [ 14 ]  give an  O ⁡ ( n ) O(n)  time algorithm for determining the winner of  Rooted Partizan Edge Geography  on an undirected tree. Hence, we have an efficient algorithm to resolve the trees left unresolved by the partial characterization of P 1 1 -win trees in Theorem  13 .
One can naively determine the winner of  Trail Trap  on a tree in  O ⁡ ( n 3 ) O(n^{3})  time by running Fraenkel and Simonson’s algorithm once for each pair of starting moves.
However, the center of a tree can be determined in linear time  [ 8 ] , allowing us to only vary over the starting moves for P 2 2  and shave off a factor of  n n . 
 
 
 Theorem 14 . 
 
 The winner of  Trail Trap  on a tree  T T  can be determined in  O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2})  time. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 We informally sketch the algorithm. We assume that we have a  O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) -time subroutine  R ​ P ​ E ​ G RPEG  which takes in a tree  T T , P 1 1 ’s first move  u → v u\to v , and P 2 2 ’s first move  x → y x\to y  and returns P 1 1 -win or P 2 2 -win. Note that  u , v , x , y u,v,x,y  may not be distinct. We have that  R P E G ( T , u → v , x → y ) RPEG(T,u\to v,x\to y)  returns P 1 1 -win if and only if P 1 1  wins  Rooted Partizan Edge Geography  on  T T  with the given starting moves  u → v u\to v  and  x → y x\to y . 
 
 
 Our algorithm first computes the central vertices of  T T . This can be done in  O ⁡ ( n ) O(n)  time  [ 8 ] . 
 
 
 If  T T  has one central vertex  c c , our algorithm first checks if  c c  has degree  3 3 , and returns P 2 2 -win if not. If  c c  has degree  3 3 , our algorithm iterates over P 1 1 ’s starting moves  u → c u\to c  for each neighbor  u u  of  c c , then iterates over all starting moves  x → y x\to y  for P 2 2 , and finally runs  R P E G ( T , u → c , x → y ) RPEG(T,u\to c,x\to y) . If there is a starting move  x → y x\to y  for P 2 2  such that  R P E G ( T , u → c , x → y ) RPEG(T,u\to c,x\to y)  returns P 2 2 -win, then our algorithm considers a different starting edge for P 1 1 . If P 2 2  has a winning starting edge for each of the three choices of a starting edge for P 1 1 , then our algorithm returns P 2 2 -win. Otherwise, our algorithm returns P 1 1 -win. 
 
 
 As there are three possible starting edges for P 1 1  and  O ⁡ ( n ) O(n)  starting edges for P 2 2  (as  T T  is a tree), our algorithm runs in  O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2})  time on a tree with one central vertex. 
 
 
 If  T T  has two central vertices  c 1 c_{1}  and  c 2 c_{2}  with  deg ⁡ ( c 1 ) ≥ deg ⁡ ( c 2 ) \deg(c_{1})\geq\deg(c_{2}) , our algorithm checks if  deg ⁡ ( c 1 ) = 3 \deg(c_{1})=3  and  deg ⁡ ( c 2 ) = 2 \deg(c_{2})=2 , and returns P 2 2 -win if not. If these degree conditions are satisfied, the algorithm then iterates over all starting moves  x → y x\to y  for P 2 2  and runs  R P E G ( T , c 2 → c 1 , x → y ) RPEG(T,c_{2}\to c_{1},x\to y) . If there is an edge  x → y x\to y  such that  R P E G ( T , c 2 → c 1 , x → y ) RPEG(T,c_{2}\to c_{1},x\to y)  returns P 2 2 -win, then our algorithm returns P 2 2 -win and returns P 1 1 -win otherwise. 
 
 
 As there is exactly one possible starting move for P 1 1  by Lemma  11  and  O ⁡ ( n ) O(n)  possible starting moves for P 2 2 , our algorithm runs in  O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2})  time on a tree with two central vertices. 
 
 
 The correctness of the algorithm then follows from Lemmas  11  and  12 .
∎

 For which  n ∈ ℕ n\in\mathbb{N}  is  K n K_{n}  a  P 1 1 -win graph? 
 
 
 
 For  n > 9 n>9 , determining the winner of  Trail Trap  on  K n K_{n}  requires excessive computation time with our current implementation. Similarly, we are unable to compute the winner on complete bipartite graphs exceeding  16 16  vertices (besides those covered by Theorem  5 ). However, we pose a conjecture for balanced complete bipartite graphs. Since  K 3 , 3 , K 5 , 5 K_{3,3},K_{5,5} , and  K 7 , 7 K_{7,7}  are each P 2 2 -win, we conjecture the same in general. 
 
 
 Conjecture 21 . 
 
 For  n ≥ 3 n\geq 3  odd,  K n , n K_{n,n}  is  P 2 2 -win. 
 
 
 
 In Section 3, we prove that grid graphs  P m ​ □ ​ P n P_{m}\,\square\,P_{n}  are P 1 1 -win if  m = 2 m=2  and  n n  is an odd integer at least  5 5 , but more generally P 2 2 -win where  m m  and  n n  have the same parity. We conjecture that all larger grid graphs are P 2 2 -win. 
 
 
 Conjecture 22 . 
 
 For any  m , n ≥ 3 m,n\geq 3 , the grid graph  P m □ P n P_{m}\mathbin{\square}P_{n}  is  P 2 2 -win. 
 
 
 
 We show above that  Trail Trap  P 2 2 -win  is NP-hard even on rather restricted classes of graphs. We do not prove NP-completeness because we suspect that the problem is not in NP. Furthermore, we conjecture the following stronger statement is true. 
 
 
 Conjecture 23 . 
 
 Trail Trap  P 2 2 -win  is PSPACE-complete. 
 
 
 
 Since a study of cubic graphs is crucial to our proof that  Trail Trap  P 2 2 -win  is NP-hard, the following is of interest. 
 
 
 Question 24 . 
 
 Is there a nontrivial restricted class of cubic graphs that are  P 1 1 -win? For instance, what can be said about  Trail Trap  on Hamiltonian cubic graphs? 
 
 
 
 Finally, we find that most small graphs are P 2 2 -win. Indeed, much of our work focuses on finding graph classes where P 1 1  wins. As a result, we conjecture the following. 
 
 
 Conjecture 25 . 
 
 The proportion of  P 1 1 -win graphs on  n n  vertices tends to  0 0  as  n → ∞ n\to\infty . 
 
 
 
 The conjecture would be of interest given that we have shown that it is NP-hard to determine whether P 2 2  wins  Trail Trap  even on restricted families of graphs.
