 Abstract. 
    
 In their seminal paper introducing the theory of random graphs, Erdős and Rényi considered the evolution of the structure of a random subgraph of  K n K_{n}  as the density increases from  0 0  to  1 1 , identifying two key points in this evolution — the percolation threshold, where the order of the largest component seemingly jumps from logarithmic to linear in size, and the connectivity threshold, where the subgraph becomes connected. Similar phenomena have been observed in many other random graph models, and in particular, works of Ajtai, Komlós and Szemerédi and of Erdős and Spencer determine corresponding thresholds for random subgraphs of the hypercube. 
    
 The permutahedron, like the hypercube, has many equivalent representations, and arises as a natural object of study in many areas of combinatorics. In particular, as a highly-symmetric simple polytope, like the  n n -simplex and  n n -cube, percolation on the permutahedron arises naturally as an analogue to the Erdős–Rényi random graph and the percolated hypercube. 
    
 We determine the percolation threshold and the connectivity threshold for random subgraphs of the permutahedron. Along the way we develop a novel graph exploration technique which can be used to find exponentially large clusters after percolation in high-dimensional geometric graphs, and we initiate the study of the isoperimetric properties of the permutahedron. 
  
 
 
 1.  Introduction 

 
 Given a graph  G G  and a probability  p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) p\in(0,1)  the  percolated random subgraph   G p G_{p}  is the random subgraph of  G G  obtained by including each edge of  G G  independently with probability  p p .
In a sense,  G p G_{p}  represents the  typical  structure of a subgraph of  G G  with relative density  p p .
This model was first considered in the context of statistical physics, where it was used to model the flow of liquid through a porous medium whose channels could be randomly blocked  [ 18 ] .
There is a natural way to couple the random subgraphs  G p G_{p}  for  p ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] p\in[0,1]  so that they are increasing (as subgraphs) as  p p  increases, and in this way we can think of the structure of  G p G_{p}  as  evolving  as we increase  p p  from  0 0  to  1 1 , with  G p G_{p}  gradually growing from an empty graph to the full  host  graph  G G . 
 
 
 Perhaps the simplest percolation model is when we take the host graph  G G  to be a complete graph  K n K_{n} , in which case we recover the classical  binomial random graph model   G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p) .

 In this paper we study the evolution of the structure of a random subgraph of a different graph, the  n n -dimensional  permutahedron .
The permutahedron is a well-studied combinatorial object which has multiple equivalent representations, perhaps the simplest of which is its representation as a (left-multiplication) Cayley graph of the symmetric group 5 5

We note that the generating set of the Cayley graph above are precisely the generators in the presentation of  S n + 1 S_{n+1}  as a  Coxeter group  (for background on Coxeter groups, see e.g.,  [ 23 ] ). 
 
 
 However,  Perm ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{Perm}(n)  has a number of other equivalent definitions.
It is the  1 1 -skeleton of a polytope  P ⁡ ( n ) P(n) , which can be described as the convex hull of the points in  ℝ n + 1 \mathbb{R}^{n+1}  whose coordinates are given by the word representations of all permutations in  S n + 1 S_{n+1} , but which can also be seen to be a  zonotope , a Minkowski sum of line segments.
Note that, since  P ⁡ ( n ) P(n)  lives in an affine hyperplane of  ℝ n + 1 \mathbb{R}^{n+1} , it has intrinsic dimension  n n .
See  [ 58 ]  for more on zonotopes and convex polytopes in general.
The permutahedron can also be defined as the covering graph of a particular lattice, the  weak order  or  weak Bruhat lattice , on  S n + 1 S_{n+1} . 
 
 
 A weak analogy can be drawn here with the  n n -dimensional hypercube, which also has numerous equivalent representations, as the standard Cayley graph of the group  ℤ 2 n \mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n} , with generators which represent the group as a Coxeter group, as the  1 1 -skeleton of the hypercube polytope, which is also a zonotope, or as the covering graph of the lattice of subsets of  [ n ] [n] .
As with the hypercube, due to its many equivalent representations, the permutahedron arises naturally in a variety of combinatorial contexts.
However, whilst the graph theoretical properties of the hypercube have been well-studied, from both the probabilistic and the extremal viewpoint  [ 37 ] , the permutahedron has mostly been considered in the context of algebraic and enumerative combinatorics, and much less is known about the structure of the permutahedron as a graph. 
 
 
 One reason that it is perhaps natural to study bond percolation on the permutahedron, is in the context of percolation on polytopes.
Indeed, both  K n K_{n}  and  Q n Q^{n}  are  1 1 -skeletons of particularly symmetric polytopes, the  n n -simplex and the  n n -cube, respectively, and in some sense these are perhaps the most natural polytopes on which to study this percolation model since they are  simple 6 6

 2.  Preliminaries 

 
 In this section, we collect some tools and definitions which will be useful later.
The graph theory terminology we use is standard (see, e.g.,  [ 15 ] ).
For polytope theory, we follow the terminology in  [ 58 ] , but provide a summary of the key definitions.

 2.1.  A brief introduction to polytopes 

 
 A  𝒱 ​ − p ​ o ​ l ​ y ​ t ​ o ​ p ​ e \mathcal{V}\emph{-polytope}  is the convex hull of a finite set of points of  ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n}  for some  n ∈ ℕ n\in\mathbb{N} .
An  ℋ ​ − p ​ o ​ l ​ y ​ t ​ o ​ p ​ e \mathcal{H}\emph{-polytope}  in  ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n}  is a bounded set of the form  { 𝒙 ∈ ℝ n : 𝑨 ​ 𝒙 ⩾ 𝒃 } \{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\bm{A}\bm{x}\geqslant\bm{b}\}  for some  𝑨 ∈ ℝ k × n \bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}  and  𝒃 ∈ ℝ k \bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{k} , that is, a bounded intersection of halfspaces.
It can be shown that every  P ⊆ ℝ n P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}  which is either an  ℋ \mathcal{H} -polytope or a  𝒱 \mathcal{V} -polytope is both.
Such sets are called  polytopes .
The  dimension  of a non-empty polytope  P P , denoted by  dim ( P ) \dim(P) , is the dimension of the smallest affine plane in which it is contained, and it is useful to define  dim ( ∅ ) = − 1 \dim(\emptyset)=-1 . 
 
 
 Let  P ⊆ ℝ n P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}  be a polytope.
If  𝒂 ∈ ℝ n \bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}  and  b ∈ ℝ b\in\mathbb{R}  are such that  𝒂 ⊺ ​ 𝒙 ⩾ b \bm{a}^{\intercal}\bm{x}\geqslant b  holds for every  𝒙 ∈ P \bm{x}\in P , then the set  F = P ∩ { 𝒙 ∈ ℝ n : 𝒂 ⊺ ​ 𝒙 = b } F=P\cap\{\,\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\bm{a}^{\intercal}\bm{x}=b\,\}  is called a  face  of  P P , and the inequality  𝒂 ⊺ ​ 𝒙 ⩾ b \bm{a}^{\intercal}\bm{x}\geqslant b   defines   F F .
One may easily check that  ∅ \emptyset  and  P P  are always faces of  P P .
Other faces, if any, are called  proper .
Clearly, a face of a polytope is a polytope itself.
A face of  P P  of dimension  0 0 ,  1 1  or  dim ( P ) − 1 \dim(P)-1  is called a  vertex , an  edge  or a  facet  of  P P , respectively.
The set of vertices of  P P  is denoted by  V ⁡ ( P ) V(P) .
Any proper face of  P P  may be written as an intersection of facets of  P P , and a face of a face of  P P  is also a face of  P P .

Two polytopes are said to be  combinatorially equivalent  if their face lattices are isomorphic.

 2.2.  Zonotopes 

 
 A  zonotope   Z Z  is the image of a cube  [ − 1 , 1 ] p [-1,1]^{p}  under an affine projection.
Writing the projection as  π ⁡ ( 𝒙 ) = 𝑽 ​ 𝒙 + 𝒛 \pi(\bm{x})=\bm{V}\bm{x}+\bm{z}  and denoting the columns of  𝑽 \bm{V}  by  𝒗 𝟏 , … , 𝒗 𝒑 \bm{v_{1}},\ldots,\bm{v_{p}} , we see that a set  Z ⊆ ℝ n Z\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}  is a zonotope if and only if there exist  p ⩾ 0 p\geqslant 0  and vectors  𝒛 , 𝒗 𝟏 , … , 𝒗 𝒑 \bm{z},\bm{v_{1}},\ldots,\bm{v_{p}}  such that  Z = { 𝒛 } + [ − 𝒗 𝟏 , 𝒗 𝟏 ] + ⋯ + [ − 𝒗 𝒑 , 𝒗 𝒑 ] Z=\{\bm{z}\}+[-\bm{v_{1}},\bm{v_{1}}]+\cdots+[-\bm{v_{p}},\bm{v_{p}}] , where  + +  denotes the Minkowski sum.
The vectors  𝒗 𝟏 , … , 𝒗 𝒑 \bm{v_{1}},\ldots,\bm{v_{p}}  are called  generators  of  Z Z .
For any zonotope, one may choose generators such that all  𝒗 𝒊 \bm{v_{i}}  are nonzero and no two  𝒗 𝒊 \bm{v_{i}}  are parallel (as the Minkowski sum is commutative and the sum of two parallel segments is a segment).
Every zonotope is a polytope, and a  simple  zonotope is a zonotope which is a simple polytope.

 2.3.  Coxeter groups 

 
 A group  Γ \Gamma  which has a presentation of the form 
 

 
 
 ⟨ r 1 , r 2 , … , r n | ( r i r j ) m i , j = e ⟩ , \bigl\langle r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{n}\,\bigm|\,(r_{i}r_{j})^{m_{i,j}}=e\bigr\rangle,

Given such a presentation we call  ( Γ , { r 1 , … , r n } ) (\Gamma,\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}\})  a  Coxeter system . 
 
 
 Given a Coxeter system  ( Γ , { r 1 , … , r n } ) (\Gamma,\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}\})  and a subset  I ⊆ [ n ] I\subseteq[n]  let us write  Γ I \Gamma_{I}  for the subgroup of  Γ \Gamma  generated by  { r i : i ∉ I } \{r_{i}:i\not\in I\} .
We will need the following elementary fact about Coxeter groups. 
 
 
 Lemma 2.2   ( [ 23 , Theorem 4.1.6] ) . 
 
 Let  ( Γ , { r 1 , … , r n } ) (\Gamma,\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}\})  be a Coxeter system and let  I , J ⊆ [ n ] I,J\subseteq[n] .
Then 
 

 
 
 Γ I ∩ Γ J = Γ I ∪ J . \Gamma_{I}\cap\Gamma_{J}=\Gamma_{I\cup J}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Recall from elementary group theory that, for any subgroup  H H  of  Γ \Gamma  and any  a ∈ Γ a\in\Gamma , the coset of  H H  containing  a a  can be written as  { b ∈ Γ : a − 1 ​ b ∈ H } \{b\in\Gamma:a^{-1}b\in H\} .
This implies that, for two subgroups  H H ,  K K  of  Γ \Gamma , the intersection of a coset of  H H  and a coset of  K K  is either empty or a coset of the intersection subgroup  H ∩ K H\cap K .
Hence, by Lemma  2.2 , the intersection of a coset of  Γ I \Gamma_{I}  and a coset of  Γ J \Gamma_{J}  is either empty or a coset of  Γ I ∪ J \Gamma_{I\cup J} .
In particular, for any tuple  ( g 1 , g 2 , … , g n ) (g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{n})  there is at most one element  h ∈ ⋂ i = 1 n g i ​ Γ { i } h\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}\Gamma_{\!\{i\}} , and so we may identify  Γ \Gamma  with a particular subset of  Γ n \Gamma^{n}  in this fashion, giving a coordinate representation of sorts, which will be useful later in the paper.

 4.  The projection lemma 

 
 A key property of the permutahedron that will form the basis of our analysis is that it has a certain type of fractal self-symmetry.
Roughly, given a small set  X X  of vertices, we can cover  X X  with a family of disjoint subgraphs each of which is in some way a lower-dimensional graph with similar properties to the permutahedra.
Similar results have been key to the analysis of percolation in the hypercube and other high-dimensional product graphs  [ 20 ,  27 ,  25 ,  29 ] .

 5.  Projection-first search 

 
 A classic technique in the study of percolated subgraphs is to explore the component (cluster)  C v C_{v}  in  G p G_{p}  containing a vertex  v v  using breadth-first search (BFS), where the ‘existence’ or ‘non-existence’ of an edge  e ∈ E ⁡ ( G ) e\in E(G)  in  G p G_{p}  is exposed dynamically as they are processed in the algorithm.
When the host graph  G G  is  n n -regular, then, at least in the early stages, before we have discovered  Θ ⁡ ( n ) \Theta(n)  many vertices, this process behaves like a Galton–Watson branching process with child distribution  Bin ⁡ ( n , p ) \operatorname{Bin}(n,p) .
In this way, we can relate the probability that  v v  lies in a ‘large’ cluster to the survival probability of this branching process. 
 
 
 In  G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p)  this intuition can be used to show that in the supercritical regime whp the correct proportion of vertices lie in components of linear order, and a simple sprinkling argument shows that these linear components all merge into a unique  giant component .
However, when the order of the host graph is much larger than its regularity, as in many high-dimensional geometric graphs such as the hypercube or permutahedron, we cannot necessarily guarantee that the clusters will grow larger than  Θ ⁡ ( n ) \Theta(n)  with positive probability in this manner, which causes difficulties in the merging step. 
 
 
 In this section, we describe a variant of the BFS algorithm, which we call projection-first search (PFS), which grows significantly larger percolation clusters.
The idea here is to avoid ‘backtracking’ by carefully separating the vertices of the BFS tree into disjoint parts using the projection lemma.
In this way, we can guarantee that the number of explored neighbours of a vertex will decrease much more slowly during the process: linearly in the depth of the BFS tree, rather than linearly in the size of the tree.
This will ensure that the process can be approximated by a  Bin ⁡ ( n , p ) \operatorname{Bin}(n,p)  branching process until the tree grows to  depth   Θ ⁡ ( n ) \Theta(n) , rather than size  Θ ⁡ ( n ) \Theta(n) .
Since, in the supercritical regime, we expect the layers of the BFS tree to be growing exponentially quickly, we should then be able to build exponentially large clusters in this manner.

 8.  Discussion 

 
 As we saw in Sections  2 ,  4  and  5 , much of the work in this paper generalises to the case of Cayley graphs of Coxeter groups or zonotopes.
However, a key missing part is some control over the large scale isoperimetric properties of these graphs, as in Proposition  1.8 .
In the case of the permutahedron, spectral methods give a close to optimal bound on the Cheeger constant, up to a small polynomial factor in the dimension/regularity.
This is then a key ingredient to the proofs of Theorems  1.6  and  1.7 , allowing us to merge the large clusters we grow in a sprinkling step.
However, a much weaker bound on the Cheeger constant, for example anything sub-exponential, would be sufficient in this argument. 
 
 
 It is therefore interesting to ask if we can give any general bounds on the Cheeger constant of these classes of graphs.
In the case of zonotopes, we make a rather bold conjecture. 
 
 
 Conjecture 8.1 . 
 
 Let  Z = ∑ i = 1 k [ − 𝐯 𝐢 , 𝐯 𝐢 ] Z=\sum_{i=1}^{k}[-\bm{v_{i}},\bm{v_{i}}]  be a zonotope and let  G G  be its  1 1 -skeleton.
Then  i ⁡ ( G ) i(G)  is at worst inverse polynomial in  k k . 
 
 
 
 Here, it would seem that the ‘worst’ examples should come from cycles of length  2 ​ k 2k , which have Cheeger constant around  2 k \frac{2}{k} .
There is a similar, well-known conjecture of Mihail and Vazirani  [ 52 ]  on the expansion of  0 / 1 0/1  polytopes, where  Q d Q^{d}  is conjectured to have the worst expansion.
The specific case of matroid basis polytopes was solved in a breakthrough result very recently  [ 5 ] . 
 
 
 More generally, it would be interesting to know how the phase transition at the percolation threshold develops in other classes of polytopes — how the critical probability relates to the degree distribution and the largest cluster sizes in the sub- and super-critical regimes.
If we consider the Cartesian powers of some fixed low-dimensional polytope with an irregular  1 1 -skeleton, the negative answer to Question  A.2  shows that we do not always have this dichotomy between logarithmic and linear sized clusters.
However, it would be very interesting to know if the quantitative behaviour in Theorems  1.1 ,  1.2  and  1.6  was perhaps universal to ‘compatible’ sequences of  simple  polytopes. 
 
 
 In  [ 31 ,  25 ] , similar methods to those in Section  6  are used to not only show the existence of a giant component in the supercritical percolated graphs, but to demonstrate that this giant component likely has good expansion properties, from which bounds on various structural parameters of the giant component such as its diameter, circumference and mixing time can be derived. 
 
 
 Question 8.2 . 
 
 What can we say about the structural properties of the giant component  L L  of a supercritical percolated  n n -dimensional permutahedron? 
 
 (1)  
 
 Does  L L  whp contain a path of length  Θ ⁡ ( ( n + 1 ) ! ) \Theta((n+1)!) ? 
 
 (2)  
 
 What is the likely diameter of  L L ? 
 
 (3)  
 
 What is the likely mixing time of a lazy random walk on  L L ? 
 
 
 
 
 
 Using the methods of  [ 31 ,  25 ]  together with the tools developed in this paper, it should be relatively straightforward to obtain  some  lower bound on the expansion of the giant component in  Perm ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{Perm}(n) , in particular one which is inverse polynomial in  n n , which would give answers to Question  8.2  ( 1 )–( 3 ) which are tight up to some power of  n n .
However, it seems unlikely that one could obtain an optimal bound (even up to polylogarithmic factors) as in  [ 25 ]  via these methods without first understanding better the isoperimetric properties of  Perm ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{Perm}(n) , in particular for large sets.
For this reason, we have not made much attempt to optimise the quantitative aspects of the arguments in Section  6 . 
 
 
 Furthermore, for the diameter and mixing time, it is not obvious what natural lower bounds there are for these quantities.
In the case of  G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p)  and  Q p n Q^{n}_{p} , similar arguments as in Lemma  6.1  show the likely existence of a bare path of length  Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \Omega(\log n)  and  Ω ⁡ ( n ) \Omega(n)  respectively, leading to natural lower bounds on the diameter and mixing time in these cases.
However, the methods of Lemma  6.1  only apply to trees with small depth and maximum degree, and so in particular are not effective for counting paths in  Perm ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{Perm}(n) .
For this reason, it would be interesting to know what the length of the longest bare path in  Perm ⁡ ( n ) p \operatorname{Perm}(n)_{p}  is, and to this end to effectively estimate the number of paths of length  Θ ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) \Theta(n\log n)  in  Perm ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{Perm}(n) .
From the other side, the diameter of  Perm ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{Perm}(n)  is clearly  ( n 2 ) \binom{n}{2}  and the mixing time of the lazy random walk on  Perm ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{Perm}(n)  is known to be  Θ ⁡ ( n 2 ​ log ⁡ n ) \Theta(n^{2}\log n)   [ 57 ,  45 ] .
Whilst neither parameter is increasing under taking subgraphs, analogues to the case of  G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p)  and  Q p n Q^{n}_{p}  suggest that in the supercritical regime we should expect the giant component to have a diameter and mixing time which is slightly larger (by a logarithmic factor) than that of the host graph. 
 
 
 As mentioned above, whilst we can determine quite precisely the expansion of small sets in  Perm ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{Perm}(n) , the isoperimetric properties of  Perm ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{Perm}(n)  in general seem to not be very well-understood.
We discussed the edge-isoperimetric problem in Section  3 , but the analogous  vertex-isoperimetric problem  in  Perm ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{Perm}(n)  is also very interesting.
A reasonably natural conjecture here would be that the vertex-boundary is minimised by Hamming balls in Kendall’s  τ \tau -metric (the distance metric on  S n + 1 S_{n+1}  induced by  Perm ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{Perm}(n) ). 
 
 
 Moving on to the connectivity threshold, in both  G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p)  and  Q p n Q^{n}_{p} , it has been shown that the connectivity threshold is asymptotically the same as the threshold for containing a perfect matching and Hamiltonian cycle.
In the case of the hypercube, both the connectivity threshold and the threshold for the existence of a perfect matching can be shown using quite delicate but elementary first moment method arguments, which rely on strong and explicit isoperimetric inequalities for the hypercube, in particular Theorem  3.2 .
On the other hand, the threshold for containing a Hamiltonian cycle in this model was only recently resolved, in groundbreaking work by Condon, Espuny Díaz, Girão, Kühn and Osthus  [ 22 ] . 
 
 
 However, in the case of the permutahedron, even the existence of a Hamiltonian cycle in the host graph is non-trivial (see, e.g.,  [ 44 , Section 7.2.1.2] ). 
 
 
 Question 8.3 . 
 
 What is the threshold for the existence of a perfect matching or Hamiltonian cycle in  Perm ⁡ ( n ) p \operatorname{Perm}(n)_{p} ?
Do they coincide with the connectivity threshold? 
 
 
 
 Finally, viewing the permutahedron as a  𝒱 \mathcal{V} -polytope rather than as a graph, there is perhaps another natural notion of a random substructure of  P ⁡ ( n ) P(n)  given by taking a random subset of the permutations, given by including each permutation independently and with probability  p p , and considering the convex hull of these points, which we denote by  P ⁡ ( n , p ) P(n,p) .
From the other direction, considering the permutahedron as an  ℋ \mathcal{H} -polytope, we could equally consider the constraints determining the permutahedron as an intersection of hyperplanes and choose a random subset of these constraints. 
 
 
 Question 8.4 . 
 
 Let  p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) p\in(0,1) . 
 
 (1)  
 
 What is the likely volume of  P ⁡ ( n , p ) P(n,p) ? 
 
 (2)  
 
 What is the likely number of lattice points inside  P ⁡ ( n , p ) P(n,p) ? 
 
 (3)  
 
 What is the likely number of facets of  P ⁡ ( n , p ) P(n,p) ? 
 
 (4)  
 
 What is the likely number of edges of  P ⁡ ( n , p ) P(n,p) ? 
 
 
 
 
 
 In the case of  P ⁡ ( n ) = P ⁡ ( n , 1 ) P(n)=P(n,1)  these parameters have simple combinatorial interpretations, and it would be interesting to see if there are combinatorial, or stochastically combinatorial, interpretations of the corresponding parameters in  P ⁡ ( n , p ) P(n,p) .
The model is also closely related to the model of random  0 / 1 0/1  polytopes considered in  [ 46 ] , where they showed that a weaker form of Mihail and Vazirani’s conjecture holds for almost all  0 / 1 0/1  polytopes.
This result was later strengthened by Ferber, Krivelevich, Sales and Samotij  [ 34 ] .
It would be interesting to know if the typical expansion in  P ⁡ ( n , p ) P(n,p)  is always smaller than that of  P ⁡ ( n ) P(n) , or even just if it can be bounded as an inverse polynomial in  n n .
