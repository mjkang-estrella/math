 Abstract 
    
 We study a two-player game played on undirected graphs called Trail Trap, which is a variant of a game known as Partizan Edge Geography. One player starts by choosing any edge and moving a token from one endpoint to the other; the other player then chooses a different edge and does the same. Alternating turns, each player moves their token along an unused edge from its current vertex to an adjacent vertex, until one player cannot move and loses. We present an algorithm to determine which player has a winning strategy when the graph is a tree, and partially characterize the trees on which a given player wins. Additionally, we show that Trail Trap is NP-hard, even for connected bipartite planar graphs with maximum degree  4 4  as well as for disconnected graphs. We determine which player has a winning strategy for certain subclasses of complete bipartite graphs and grid graphs, and we propose several open problems for further study. 
  
 
 
 1  Introduction 

 
 Let  G = ( V ⁡ ( G ) , E ⁡ ( G ) ) G=(V(G),E(G))  be a graph (all graphs we consider are undirected, finite, and simple). A  walk  on  G G  is a sequence  v 1 ​ v 2 ​ … ​ v m v_{1}v_{2}\ldots v_{m}  of vertices in  V ⁡ ( G ) V(G)  such that  v i ​ v i + 1 ∈ E ⁡ ( G ) v_{i}v_{i+1}\in E(G)  for all  1 ≤ i ≤ m − 1 1\leq i\leq m-1 . A  trail  is a walk with no repeated edges and a  path  is a trail with no repeated vertices. In this work, we study a perfect information game,  Trail Trap , first introduced in  [ 1 ] , in which two players compete to build edge-disjoint trails on  G G . 
 
 
 To begin a game of Trail Trap on a graph  G G , Player 1 (P 1 1 ) chooses a starting vertex on which to place their token, then moves the token along an incident edge  e e  (should such an edge exist) to an adjacent vertex, completing their turn. Player 2 (P 2 2 ) then places their token on any vertex, and moves it along an incident edge  f ≠ e f\not=e  (should such an edge exist), to its second endpoint. Continuing with P 1 1 , the players alternate turns, each moving their token from its current vertex along an unused edge to a neighboring vertex. A player cannot make a move along an edge previously used by either player, but there are no restrictions on vertices: a player may place their token on the same vertex multiple times throughout the game, and the two tokens may simultaneously occupy a vertex. The first player unable to make a move loses the game. Since  | E ⁡ ( G ) | |E(G)|  is finite, Trail Trap cannot end in a draw. 
 
 
 We denote a  move  on a graph  G G , the act of moving a token from one vertex  u u  to an adjacent vertex  v v , by  u → v u\to v . Interchangeably, we say that a player  moves  from  u u  to  v v . An edge  u ​ v uv  is  used  if either player has moved from  u u  to  v v  or from  v v  to  u u  on any previous turn, and  unused  otherwise. Each player’s sequence of moves, at every turn, forms a trail through the graph. Thus, rather than moving tokens, we can think of the players as constructing edge-disjoint trails on the graph. 
 
 
 We provide an example of gameplay on the “diamond” graph  G G , illustrated in Figure  1 . Suppose P 1 1  first makes the move  b → c b\to c , and suppose P 2 2  begins their trail with  c → d c\to d . From here, P 1 1 ’s second move must be  c → a c\to a , since this is the only unused edge incident to  c c . Similarly, P 2 2 ’s second move is  d → b d\to b , and P 1 1  then makes the move  a → b a\to b . Since P 2 2  has no available moves out of vertex  b b  (indeed, all the edges in the graph are used), the game is over and P 1 1  wins.

 However, P 2 2 ’s first move  c → d c\to d  wasn’t optimal, and with a different first move, P 2 2  can ensure victory. In fact, with optimal play, P 2 2  can win on  G G  no matter P 1 1 ’s first move. We leave this as an exercise to the reader. 
 
 
 To win, P 1 1  must construct a trail strictly longer than that of P 2 2 , but P 2 2  wins if the trails are the same length. In general, we say that a player has a  winning strategy  on a graph  G G  if they can win Trail Trap on  G G  no matter the other player’s moves. If P 1 1  has a winning strategy on  G G , then we say that  G G  is  P 1 1 -win  ( P 2 2 -win  is defined analogously). As Trail Trap is a two-player perfect information game, exactly one of P 1 1  and P 2 2  wins on any given graph. In this paper, we address the problem of characterizing P1-win graphs for several families, including complete bipartite graphs and trees. We also give a polynomial-time algorithm to determine the winner of Trail Trap on trees and prove that determining a winner is NP-hard even on some restricted graph classes. 
 
 
 In the following subsection, we provide a brief survey of Geography games, of which Trail Trap can be considered a variant. Then, in Section  2 , we provide a P 1 1 -win condition and a P 2 2 -win condition for Trail Trap which each apply to many classes of graphs. In Section  3 , we give exact win conditions for subsets of complete graphs, complete bipartite graphs, grid graphs, and prism graphs. In Section  4 , we characterize the P 1 1 -win trees and give an  O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2})  algorithm to determine the winner for trees of order  n n . In Section  5 , we prove that determining the winner is NP-hard on several restricted classes of graphs. Open questions arise in each section, and we summarize these in Section  6 . 
 
 
 1.1  Related Geography games 

 
 Trail Trap can be seen as a variant of Geography, part of a larger family of walk-construction games (see  [ 3 ] ). Geography is a two-player game in which players take turns moving a single, shared token from one vertex to an adjacent vertex, beginning at a fixed starting vertex (or root). In Vertex Geography, no vertex can be repeated, and in Edge Geography, no edge can be repeated. As in Trail Trap, the winner is the last player able to make a legal move. Both Vertex and Edge Geography can be played on directed or undirected graphs. 
 
 
 Vertex Geography is the simplest game in the Geography family, and Fraenkel, Scheinerman, and Ullman  [ 11 ]  provide an exact characterization of when each player wins on an undirected graph  G G  with a given starting vertex  v v . Specifically, they show that  ( G , v ) (G,v)  is P 1 1 -win if and only if every maximum matching in  G G  includes an edge incident to  v v . In Unrooted Vertex Geography, a generalization where P 1 1 ’s first move is to choose the starting vertex (and then P 2 2  makes the first “full” move to a new vertex), the graph  G G  is P 2 2 -win if and only if it has a perfect matching. It follows that the winners of both of these variants can be computed in polynomial time  [ 9 ] . Monti and Sinaimeri  [ 15 ]  consider a modified version of Vertex Geography where the goal is to determine whether P 1 1  can force a win within  k k  moves. They show that this problem is PSPACE-complete, even for bipartite graphs, but can be solved in polynomial time for trees. 
 
 
 Edge Geography is typically more complicated than Vertex Geography. The winner for Edge Geography on undirected graphs can be computed in polynomial time for a few special families, including bipartite and complete graphs  [ 11 ] . However, Undirected Edge Geography in general is PSPACE-complete, even for subcubic planar graphs. The same holds for an unrooted generalization defined analogously to Unrooted Vertex Geography. On directed graphs, both Vertex Geography and Edge Geography are PSPACE-complete (for these general results, see Lichtenstein and Sipser  [ 14 ] , and Schaefer  [ 17 ] ). 
 
 
 Alternatively, there are  partizan  variants of Vertex and Edge Geography, where each player has a separate token and only moves their own token. (Note that in the literature “partizan” sometimes has an alternate meaning where there are distinct rules for each player; for instance, in  [ 6 ] ). Partizan games are typically more computationally complex than shared-token games. Fraenkel and Simonson  [ 12 ]  show that the winning player in both Partizan Vertex Geography and Partizan Edge Geography can be determined in  O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2})  time for directed trees and  O ⁡ ( n ) O(n)  time for undirected trees, where  n n  is the order of the graph. However, no other classes are known to be solvable in polynomial time. Fox and Geissler  [ 10 ]  show that Partizan Vertex Geography is PSPACE-complete for undirected bipartite graphs. Partizan Vertex Geography and Partizan Edge Geography are both NP-hard for directed acyclic graphs and directed planar graphs  [ 12 ] . Both games are known to be PSPACE-complete on bipartite digraphs with bounded degree: specifically, where the in-degree and out-degree of each vertex is at most two, and their sum is at most three  [ 12 ] . 
 
 
 Trail Trap is formally a variant of Unrooted Partizan Edge Geography.
In this variation, each player places a token  and  moves along an edge on their first turn. In contrast, in the other unrooted (unpartizan) games considered in  [ 12 ] , P 1 1  places the token on their first turn, and P 2 2  is the first to move the token. This additional degree of freedom for the players can change the outcome of the game. For example, the path on three vertices is P 1 1 -win in the game where both players place tokens before either moves, but P 2 2 -win in Trail Trap. 
 
 
 
 
 2  Preliminaries 

 
 We briefly introduce definitions and notation pertaining to degrees, distance, and moves. We adopt the standard graph-theoretic notation used in  [ 8 ]  and refer the reader there for additional background. 
 
 
 Given a graph  G G , the  degree  of a vertex  v v  in  V ⁡ ( G ) V(G) , denoted by  deg G ⁡ ( v ) \deg_{G}(v) , is the number of edges in  E ⁡ ( G ) E(G)  incident to  v v . The  distance  between vertices  u , v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) u,v\in V(G) , denoted by  d G ​ ( u , v ) d_{G}(u,v) , is the length of the shortest path from  u u  to  v v . By convention,  d G ​ ( u , v ) = ∞ d_{G}(u,v)=\infty  if there is no path from  u u  to  v v  in  G G . We write  deg ⁡ ( v ) \deg(v)  and  d ⁡ ( u , v ) d(u,v)  if the graph is clear from context. 
 
 
 In a game of Trail Trap on  G G , a  move   m m  is formally an ordered pair  u → v u\rightarrow v , where  u ​ v ∈ E ⁡ ( G ) uv\in E(G) . Let  u u  be the  tail  of move  m m  and  v v  be the  head . We let  m − 1 m^{-1}  denote the move  v → u v\rightarrow u . Let  M M  be the set of possible moves on  G G . We label turns alternating players, such that P 1 1 ’s first move is turn  1 1 , P 2 2 ’s first move is turn  2 2 , and so on. (There is no formal difference between a move and a turn, but the colloquial usage of turn as a “time to make a move” is useful for us.) For a natural number  r ≤ | E ⁡ ( G ) | r\leq|E(G)| , an  r r -partial game   S S  of Trail Trap is an ordered  r r -tuple of moves, such that each edge of  G G  occurs at most once and, for all  i ∈ { 1 , … , r − 2 } i\in\{1,\ldots,r-2\} , the head of move  i i  is equal to the tail of move  i + 2 i+2 . An  r r -partial game is  incomplete  (has a legal continuation) if there exists an edge of  G G  not in  S S  and incident to the head of move  r − 1 r-1 . Let  𝒮 \mathcal{S}  be the set of all  r r -partial games, over all  0 ≤ r ≤ | E ⁡ ( G ) | 0\leq r\leq|E(G)| , and let  ℐ ⊆ 𝒮 \mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{S}  be the subset of incomplete  r r -partial games. 
 
 
 We also introduce several definitions related to graph connectivity and trails. A graph  G G  is  connected  if there exists a trail on  G G  between any pair of its vertices, and  G G  is  disconnected  if it contains at least two  components , or maximal connected subgraphs. Given a trail  t = v 1 ​ v 2 ​ … ​ v m t=v_{1}v_{2}\ldots v_{m}  on  G G , let  ℓ ⁡ ( t ) = m − 1 \ell(t)=m-1  be the length of  t t . Let  ℓ ⁡ ( G ) \ell(G)  be the maximum length of a trail on  G G . We denote the maximum length of a trail on  G G  that starts at a given vertex  v v  by  ℓ ⁡ ( G , v ) \ell(G,v) . 
 
 
 In Trail Trap, each player’s trail is restricted to a single component of  G G . On a disconnected graph  G G , there are two ways a game of Trail Trap can go: either both players make trails on the same component, or each plays on a separate component. If both players draw trails on the same component  H H , then it suffices to determine a winner for  H H . If both players use different components, however, then the game runs in parallel, with the players unable to interfere with one another’s trails. The longest trail on a disconnected graph is important, but does not completely determine who wins. Figure  2  shows an example graph  G G  where the longest trail is in a component which induces a P 1 1 -win graph, but  G G  itself is P 2 2 -win. In a winning strategy for P 1 1  on  G G , P 1 1  must end their first move on the vertex of degree  3 3  (see Corollary  10  later for a proof). However, P 1 1  can then create a trail of at most four edges, which P 2 2  can match on the other component. 
 
 
 Figure 2 :  A disconnected P 2 2 -win graph in which the component containing the longest trail is a P 1 1 -win graph. 
 
 
 Proposition 1 . 
 
 A disconnected graph  G G  is  P 1 1 -win if and only if there exists a component  H H  with a trail  t = v 1 ​ v 2 ​ … ​ v m + 1 t=v_{1}v_{2}\ldots v_{m+1}  such that there exists a winning strategy  f f  for  P 1 1  on  H H  with  f ⁡ ( ∅ ) = v 1 → v 2 f(\emptyset)=v_{1}\to v_{2} , and for all other components  H ′ H^{\prime}  of  G G ,  ℓ ⁡ ( H ′ ) < m \ell(H^{\prime})<m . 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Let  G G  be disconnected. Suppose first that  G G  is P 1 1 -win and that P 1 1  has a winning strategy on  G G  beginning with the move  v 1 → v 2 v_{1}\to v_{2}  on component  H H . It follows that  H H  is P 1 1 -win and that there is a winning strategy for P 1 1  on  H H  beginning with the move  v 1 → v 2 v_{1}\to v_{2} . Suppose that the longest trail on  H H  beginning with  v 1 ​ v 2 v_{1}v_{2}  has length  m m . If there exists another component  H ′ H^{\prime}  where  ℓ ⁡ ( H ′ ) ≥ m \ell(H^{\prime})\geq m , then P 2 2  can play on  H ′ H^{\prime}  and create a trail at least as long as P 1 1 ’s longest possible trail. Hence, P 2 2  would win on  G G . Thus,  ℓ ⁡ ( H ′ ) < m \ell(H^{\prime})<m . 
 
 
 Conversely, suppose that there exists a component  H H  such that  H H  is P 1 1 -win, a winning strategy for  P1 {\rm{P}1}{}  on  H H  that starts with the move  v 1 → v 2 v_{1}\to v_{2} , a trail of length  m m  on  H H  beginning with edge  v 1 ​ v 2 v_{1}v_{2} , and no other component  H ′ H^{\prime}  satisfying  ℓ ⁡ ( H ′ ) ≥ m \ell(H^{\prime})\geq m . We can construct a winning strategy for  P1 {\rm{P}1}{}  on  G G  as follows. First, P 1 1  plays the move  v 1 → v 2 v_{1}\to v_{2} . If P 2 2  plays on a separate component, they get a trail of length less than  m m  while P 1 1  can continue along a trail of length  m m . Thus, P 2 2  is forced to play on  H H . However, as there exists some winning strategy for P 1 1  on  H H , beginning with the move  v 1 → v 2 v_{1}\to v_{2} , P 1 1  can now win by following that P 1 1 -win strategy for Trail Trap on  H H .
∎ 
 
 
 
 To determine the winner on a disconnected graph, we must be able to compare the maximum trail length of components in general and from any given starting vertex. In Section  5  we discuss the computational complexity of determining the maximum trail length of a graph, and show that this problem is NP-hard. Upper and lower bounds are known for maximum trail length on a graph with a given number of vertices and edges  [ 5 ] , but nothing more specific is known, despite the invariant’s simplicity. For a given connected graph  G G , it is well known that there is a trail containing every edge in  G G  if and only if  G G  contains at most two vertices of odd degree. If  G G  contains no vertices of odd degree, then  G G  is Eulerian, and there exists a trail containing every edge and beginning and ending on the same vertex. Some related material exists on partitioning the edges of a graph into Eulerian trails  [ 13 ] , and determining the number of edges by which a graph is deficient from being Eulerian  [ 4 ] . Without an independent means of determining maximum trail length, Proposition  1  is insufficient for determining a winner on a disconnected graph. As a counterpart to Proposition  1 , we show in Section  5  that Trail Trap is NP-hard even on disconnected graphs. 
 
 
 There are some special cases for which the winner of Trail Trap can be easily determined. Trivially, if  | E ⁡ ( G ) | = ∅ |E(G)|=\emptyset , then  G G  is P 2 2 -win (P 1 1  is the first to be unable to move), and if  | E ⁡ ( G ) | = 1 |E(G)|=1 , then  G G  is P 1 1 -win. We assume henceforth that  | E ⁡ ( G ) | ≥ 2 |E(G)|\geq 2 .
Observe also that graphs with maximum degree at most  2 2  (including all paths and cycles) are P 2 2 -win since P 1 1  is limited to making only one move (either by the choice of move or by P 2 2  making a move on the only edge where P 1 1  could have moved next). A generalization of this observation is discussed in Corollary  10 . 
 
 
 Another broad strategy is what Berlekamp, Conway, and Guy  [ 2 ]  call the “Tweedledee and Tweedledum” strategy, where P 2 2  mirrors each of P 1 1 ’s moves. The result also holds on multigraphs. 
 
 
 Proposition 2 . 
 
 If a graph  G G  has an involutive automorphism with no fixed edges, then  G G  is  P 2 2 -win. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Let  G G  be a graph with an involutive automorphism  ϕ : V ⁡ ( G ) → V ⁡ ( G ) \phi:V(G)\rightarrow V(G)  with no fixed edges. If P 1 1  moves  v i → v j v_{i}\to v_{j}  on turn  k k , then P 2 2  moves  ϕ ⁡ ( v i ) → ϕ ⁡ ( v j ) \phi(v_{i})\to\phi(v_{j})  on turn  k + 1 k+1 . The move is legal since, if  k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 , P 2 2  ended their previous move on  ϕ ⁡ ( v i ) \phi(v_{i}) , and since edge  v i ​ v j v_{i}v_{j}  was unused before turn  k k , it follows that  ϕ ⁡ ( v i ) ​ ϕ ​ ( v j ) \phi(v_{i})\phi(v_{j})  was unused before turn  k + 1 k+1 . Hence P 2 2  can always play after P 1 1 ; as  G G  is finite, P 2 2  wins.
∎

 We use a depth-first recursive algorithm to evaluate if a given graph  G G  is P 1 1 -win or P 2 2 -win. We say a player wins an incomplete  r r -partial game  I I  if they can win the game on  G G  with optimal play, given that both players’ starting sequence of moves was the partial game  I I . For any incomplete  r r -partial game  I I , we iterate through all possible moves for the player whose turn it is and assert that they can win  I I  with optimal play if and only if they can win at least one of the resulting  ( r + 1 ) (r+1) -partial games extending  I I . 
 
 
 We justify why the above algorithm works. Given  G G  and an incomplete  r r -partial game  I I  on  G G , let  E I E_{I}  be the set of edges of  G G  unused in  I I  and let  G I = ( V ⁡ ( G ) , E I ) G_{I}=(V(G),E_{I}) . Consider the set  𝒥 \mathcal{J}  of  ( r + 1 ) (r+1) -partial games extending  I I , such that for any given  J ∈ 𝒥 J\in\mathcal{J} , the sequence of the first  r r  moves of  J J  is equal to  I I , and the tail of move  r + 1 r+1  is equal to the head of move  r − 1 r-1 . Suppose  r r  is odd, such that P 1 1  plays first on  G J G_{J} . If for all  J ∈ 𝒥 J\in\mathcal{J}  there exists a winning strategy for P 1 1  on  G J G_{J}  where the tail of the first move is equal to the head of move  r r , then by following the strategy, P 1 1  wins  I I  with optimal play. Failing this, P 2 2  wins, since there exists an  ( r + 1 ) (r+1) -partial game  J J  unwinnable by P 1 1 . If  r r  is even, then an analogous argument shows that P 2 2  wins  I I  with optimal play if and only if for all  J ∈ 𝒥 J\in\mathcal{J}  there exists a winning strategy for P 2 2  on  G J G_{J}  where the tail of the first move is equal to the head of move  r r . 
 
 
 For highly symmetric graphs, such as complete or complete bipartite graphs, we speed up the computations by limiting the choices for the first few moves, without loss of generality. 
 
 
 We continue by considering complete graphs. For small cases, we are able to determine the winner by hand. The following proposition is due to Arneman, Catanzaro, and Danison  [ 1 ] , but restated here with a shorter proof. 
 
 
 Proposition 3 . 
 
 The complete graph  K 4 K_{4}  is  P 1 1 -win, and  K 5 K_{5}  is  P 2 2 -win. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Denote the vertices of  K n K_{n}  by  v 1 , v 2 , … , v n v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n} . On  K 4 K_{4} , suppose without loss of generality that P 1 1  initially makes the move  v 1 → v 2 v_{1}\to v_{2} . We consider three cases based on P 2 2 ’s first move: 
 
 (i)  
 
 If P 2 2 ’s first move also ends at  v 2 v_{2} , then P 1 1  makes their second move on the only unused edge incident to  v 2 v_{2} , to win. 
 
 (ii)  
 
 If P 2 2 ’s first move ends at  v 3 v_{3}  (respectively,  v 4 v_{4} ), then P 1 1  should move  v 2 → v 4 v_{2}\to v_{4}  (resp.  v 2 → v 3 v_{2}\to v_{3} ). Irrespective of P 2 2 ’s second move, P 1 1  moves  v 4 → v 1 v_{4}\to v_{1}  (resp.  v 3 → v 1 v_{3}\to v_{1} ) to win. 
 
 (iii)  
 
 If P 2 2 ’s first move is  v 3 → v 1 v_{3}\to v_{1}  (resp.,  v 4 → v 1 v_{4}\to v_{1} ) then P 1 1  should move  v 2 → v 4 v_{2}\to v_{4} . P 2 2  must then move  v 1 → v 4 v_{1}\to v_{4}  (resp.  v 1 → v 3 v_{1}\to v_{3} ) and P 1 1  moves  v 4 → v 3 v_{4}\to v_{3}  to win. 
 
 
 
 
 Therefore, P 1 1  has a winning strategy on  K 4 K_{4} . 
 
 
 On  K 5 K_{5} , suppose again without loss of generality that P 1 1  initially moves  v 1 → v 2 v_{1}\to v_{2} . Have P 2 2  move  v 2 → v 3 v_{2}\to v_{3}  and then, without loss of generality, P 1 1  responds  v 2 → v 4 v_{2}\to v_{4} . Have P 2 2  move  v 3 → v 1 v_{3}\to v_{1} . We consider three cases based on P 1 1 ’s third move: 
 
 
 
 (i)  
 
 If P 1 1  now moves  v 4 → v 1 v_{4}\to v_{1} , then P 2 2  moves  v 1 → v 5 v_{1}\to v_{5}  to win. 
 
 (ii)  
 
 If P 1 1  now moves  v 4 → v 3 v_{4}\to v_{3} , then P 2 2  moves  v 1 → v 5 v_{1}\to v_{5} . P 1 1  must now move  v 3 → v 5 v_{3}\to v_{5} , and then P 2 2  moves  v 5 → v 4 v_{5}\to v_{4} . P 1 1  must now move  v 5 → v 2 v_{5}\to v_{2} , and then P 2 2  moves  v 4 → v 1 v_{4}\to v_{1}  to win. 
 
 (iii)  
 
 If P 1 1  now moves  v 4 → v 5 v_{4}\to v_{5} , then P 2 2  moves  v 1 → v 4 v_{1}\to v_{4} . Irrespective of P 1 1 ’s next move, P 2 2  moves  v 4 → v 3 v_{4}\to v_{3}  to win.  ∎ 
 
 
 
 
 
 For larger complete graphs and other families, analyzing games by hand is prohibitively difficult. We determine computationally that  K 6 K_{6}  and  K 7 K_{7}  are P 1 1 -win, and  K 8 K_{8}  and  K 9 K_{9}  are P 2 2 -win. 
 
 
 We now turn to complete bipartite graphs, for which we are able to determine a winner on some infinite subfamilies. 
 
 
 Theorem 4 . 
 
 The complete bipartite graph  K p , q K_{p,q}  is  P 2 2 -win if at least one of  p , q p,q  is even. If  p = 3 p=3  and  q ≥ 5 q\geq 5  is odd, then  K p , q K_{p,q}  is  P 1 1 -win. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Given  G = K p , q G=K_{p,q} , let  ( L , R ) (L,R)  be the bipartition of  V ⁡ ( G ) V(G)  with  L = { u 1 , … , u p } L=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{p}\}  and  R = { v 1 , … , v q } R=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{q}\} . Consider the involutive automorphism  ϕ \phi  which sends  u i u_{i}  to  u p + 1 − i u_{p+1-i}  and  v j v_{j}  to  v q + 1 − j v_{q+1-j} . The only way an edge is fixed is if the edge  u i ​ v j u_{i}v_{j}  satisfies  i = p + 1 − i i=p+1-i  and  j = q + 1 − j j=q+1-j . However, as  i i  and  j j  are integers, this only occurs if  p p  and  q q  are both odd. Thus, if either  p p  or  q q  is even, then  K p , q K_{p,q}  is P 2 2 -win by Proposition  2 . 
 
 
 For  p = 3 p=3  and  q ≥ 13 q\geq 13  odd, we show that P 1 1  has a winning strategy. Let P 1 1 ’s first move be  u 1 → v 1 u_{1}\to v_{1} ; subsequently, whenever P 1 1  is moving from  L L  to  R R , they follow the strategy of moving to a vertex  v j v_{j}  in  R R  that has not been previously visited by either player, as long as this is possible. This ensures that even if P 2 2  immediately uses an edge incident to  v j v_{j} , P 1 1  will be able to move back to  L L  via an unused edge. 
 
 
 Suppose first that P 2 2  initially also moves from  L L  to  R R . This means P 2 2  is always moving to the partite set ( L L  or  R R ) that P 1 1  is currently on. Before P 2 2  makes a move from  L L  to  R R , every vertex in  R R , except for P 1 1 ’s current vertex has an odd number of unused edges. If P 2 2  moves to a vertex with one unused edge, then they can make no further moves, and P 1 1  can win by moving back to  L L  along an unused edge incident to their current vertex. If P 2 2  moves to a vertex  v j v_{j}  with two unused edges, this must be P 1 1 ’s current vertex, so then P 1 1 ’s subsequent move uses the last available edge incident to  v j v_{j}  to win. Thus it suffices to consider/assume that whenever P 2 2  moves to  R R , they move to a vertex with all three edges previously unused. 
 
 
 According to P 1 1 ’s strategy of moving to vertices in  R R 
with three incident unused edges whenever possible, the two players are taking turns visiting previously unvisited vertices in  R R . Since  | R | = q |R|=q  is odd, P 2 2  will run out of previously unvisited vertices in  R R  to move to right after P 1 1  moves to their  ( q + 1 ) 2 th \frac{(q+1)}{2}^{\text{th}}  vertex in  R R . Thus, P 2 2  will be forced to move to a vertex in  R R  with one or two unused vertices, ensuring their defeat as described earlier. 
 
 
 Suppose instead that P 2 2  initially moves from  R R  to  L L . As long as there are vertices in  R R  with three unused edges, P 1 1  can move to such a vertex when going from  L L  to  R R  and subsequently return to  L L  along an unused edge. As such, P 1 1  cannot lose until all vertices from  R R  have been visited. We show that P 1 1  can force a game position where both P 1 1  and P 2 2  have their tokens on vertices in  L L , it is P 2 2 ’s turn, all vertices in  R R  have zero, one, or three unused edges, and an even number have three unused edges. Given this, we show next that P 1 1  can win by forcing P 2 2  to move to a vertex of  R R  with one unused edge. 
 
 
 Without loss of generality, P 2 2  can either start from  v 1 v_{1}  or from some other vertex of  R R . If P 2 2  begins at  v 1 v_{1} , then without loss of generality, they move to  u 2 u_{2} . P 1 1  can then move to  u 3 u_{3} . At this point, both players are in  L L , it is P 2 2 ’s turn, and  R R  contains one vertex with zero unused edges and  q − 1 q-1  vertices with three unused edges. 
 
 
 If otherwise P 2 2  begins at  v 2 v_{2} , then up to symmetry we assume P 2 2  moves to  u 1 u_{1}  or  u 2 u_{2} . P 1 1  can then move to  u 3 u_{3} . P 1 1  can then move to  v 2 v_{2} . Suppose that both P 1 1  and P 2 2  now return to any sensible (not immediately losing) vertices in  L L . At this point, it is P 2 2 ’s turn and  R R  contains one vertex with no unused edges, two vertices with one unused edge, and  q − 3 q-3  vertices with three unused edges.

Since  q q  is by assumption odd, there are an even number of vertices left in  R R  with three unused edges. It is P 2 2 ’s turn, so ultimately, P 2 2  will be forced to be first to visit a vertex in  R R  with one unused edge, assuming P 1 1  uses the strategy of moving to a vertex in  R R  with three unused edges whenever possible. Once this happens, P 2 2  cannot make another move and P 1 1  can win if they are able to make one final move from  L L  to  R R . 
 
 
 Next, we show that we can specify P 1 1 ’s midgame strategy to ensure they have a final (winning) move available, i.e., that when P 2 2  enters a vertex in  R R  along its last unused edge, P 1 1  can still make one more move.  4  The Game on Trees 

 
 We now discuss Trail Trap on an arbitrary tree  T T . Fraenkel and Simonson  [ 12 ]  showed that Rooted Partizan Edge Geography (Trail Trap with fixed starting vertices for both players) can be solved in  O ⁡ ( n ) O(n)  time on an undirected tree, which trivially provides an  O ⁡ ( n 3 ) O(n^{3})  time solution to Trail Trap on  T T  by iterating over all pairs of starting vertices.
We are able to shave off a factor of  n n  using Lemma  11  which shows that if P 1 1  has a winning strategy on  T T , then they must first move their token to a central vertex.
Since a tree has at most two central vertices, this provides an  O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2})  time algorithm to resolve Trail Trap on  T T  (see Theorem  14 ).
To better understand the structure of P 1 1 -win trees, we provide a set of necessary conditions for  T T  to be a P 1 1 -win in Theorem  13 . 
 
 
 We also discuss certain obstacles to a full characterization of P 1 1 -win trees. 
 
 
 4.1  Basic Observations 

 
 A key observation in solving Trail Trap on trees is that every trail in a tree is a path, and there is a unique path between each pair of vertices of a tree. The  eccentricity   ecc ⁡ ( v ) \ecc(v)  of a vertex  v v  in a graph  G G  is  max ⁡ { d ⁡ ( v , x ) : x ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) } \max\{d(v,x)\colon x\in V(G)\} . The  radius  of a graph  G G  is  rad ⁡ ( G ) = min v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) ⁡ ecc ⁡ ( v ) \rad(G)=\min_{v\in V(G)}\ecc(v) , and the  diameter  of  G G  is  diam ⁡ ( G ) = max v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) ⁡ ecc ⁡ ( v ) \diam(G)=\max_{v\in V(G)}\ecc(v) . 
 
 
 Note that if  G G  is a tree, then  ℓ ⁡ ( G ) = diam ⁡ ( G ) \ell(G)=\diam(G)  and  ℓ ⁡ ( G , v ) = ecc G ⁡ ( v ) \ell(G,v)=\ecc_{G}(v)  for any vertex  v v , a fact which we will use extensively later. 
 
 
 The  center   C ⁡ ( G ) C(G)  of a graph  G G  is the set of vertices of minimum eccentricity, i.e.,   C ⁡ ( G ) = { x ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) : ecc ⁡ ( x ) = rad ⁡ ( x ) } C(G)=\{x\in V(G)\colon\ecc(x)=\rad(x)\} . A vertex  v ∈ C ⁡ ( G ) v\in C(G)  is called a  central vertex  of  G G . 
 
 
 Lemma 8   (Folklore) .

Furthermore, if  c 1 c_{1}  is not the unique central vertex of its component in  T − c 1 ​ c 2 T-c_{1}c_{2} , then P 2 2  has a winning strategy, as described above.
∎ 
 
 
 
 Theorem  13  provides a set of necessary conditions for a tree  T T  to be P 1 1 -win.
Figure  6  depicts an example of a tree with a single central vertex which does not meet condition  (i)  in Theorem  13 , and thus is P 2 2 -win. 
 
 
 We have established that, for P 1 1  to win on a tree  T T , their first move must end at a central vertex of  T T .
Now, suppose that  T T  satisfies the necessary conditions of Theorem  13  but is still P 2 2 -win.
Further, suppose that P 1 1  starts by moving  x → c x\to c  as in the proof of Theorem  13 , where  c c  is a central vertex and the component of  T − c ​ x T-cx  containing  x x  has diameter at most  ecc ⁡ ( c ) ∈ { r − 1 , r } \ecc(c)\in\{r-1,r\} .
P 2 2 ’s first move in every winning strategy is in the component of  T − c ​ x T-cx  containing  c c .
Further, P 2 2  moves their token towards the center in every winning strategy starting with the same P 1 1  move; otherwise, P 2 2  obtains a trail of length at most  r r , and P 1 1  obtains one of length  r + 1 r+1 .
Other conditions for P 1 1  to win can be proved in this case, but they require more extensive case analysis; a simple characterization of P 1 1 -win trees remains elusive.
For instance, if a tree  T T  with one central vertex meets the necessary conditions of Theorem  13 , and if  c c  is contained in every path of length greater than  r r  in its component of  T − c ​ x T-cx , then  T T  is a P 1 1 -win.
On the other hand, if the latter condition does not hold, and if P 1 1  does not move their token towards P 2 2 ’s on their second move, then P 2 2  has a winning strategy for the resulting partial game.

 Figure  7  depicts two such scenarios.
Let  T T  be the tree in Figure  7(a) . To determine that  T T  is P 1 1 -win, we must consider the case in which the two players move towards one another on the top branch from  c c : if P 2 2  starts by moving from a leaf along the path of length  4 4  in this branch, then P 1 1  must move towards P 2 2  to win.
Having done so, P 1 1  is able to stop P 2 2  from taking this path, and wins.

We leave this as an exercise for the reader. 
 
 
 Now, if there exists a path  P P  of length at least  r r  which does not contain the center  c c , it suffices to consider Trail Trap where P 1 1  has played into  c c  and P 2 2  has played an edge  x → y x\to y  elsewhere in the tree. This variation of Trail Trap with fixed starting locations is precisely the game of Rooted Partizan Edge Geography. As part of providing an algorithm for a Geography variant on directed graphs, Fraenkel and Simonson  [ 12 ]  give an  O ⁡ ( n ) O(n)  time algorithm for determining the winner of Rooted Partizan Edge Geography on an undirected tree. Hence, we have an efficient algorithm to resolve the trees left unsolved by our partial characterization of P 1 1 -win trees in Theorem  13 . 
 
 
 As we have noted, one can naively determine the winner of Trail Trap on a tree in  O ⁡ ( n 3 ) O(n^{3})  time by running Fraenkel and Simonson’s algorithm once for each pair of starting moves. Using Lemmas  11  and  12 , we can shave off a factor of  n n . 
 
 
 Theorem 14 . 
 
 The winner of Trail Trap on a tree  T T  can be determined in  O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2})  time. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 We informally sketch the algorithm. We assume that we have a  O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) -time subroutine  R ​ P ​ E ​ G RPEG  which takes in a tree  T T , P 1 1 ’s first move  u → v u\to v , and P 2 2 ’s first move  x → y x\to y  and returns P 1 1 -win or P 2 2 -win. Note that  u , v , x , y u,v,x,y  may not be distinct. We have that  R P E G ( T , u → v , x → y ) RPEG(T,u\to v,x\to y)  returns P 1 1 -win if and only if P 1 1  wins Rooted Partizan Edge Geography on  T T  with the given starting moves  u → v u\to v  and  x → y x\to y . 
 
 
 Our algorithm first computes the central vertices of  T T . This can be done in  O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2})  time by running a breadth-first search from every vertex. 
 
 
 If  T T  has one central vertex  c c , our algorithm first checks if  c c  has degree  3 3 , and returns P 2 2 -win if not. If  c c  has degree  3 3 , our algorithm iterates over P 1 1 ’s starting moves  u → c u\to c  for each neighbor  u u  of  c c , then iterates over all starting moves  x → y x\to y  for P 2 2 , and finally runs  R P E G ( T , u → c , x → y ) RPEG(T,u\to c,x\to y) . If there is a starting move  x → y x\to y  for P 2 2  such that  R P E G ( T , u → c , x → y ) RPEG(T,u\to c,x\to y)  returns P 2 2 -win, then our algorithm considers a different starting edge for P 1 1 . If P 2 2  has a winning starting edge for each of the three choices of a starting edge for P 1 1 , then our algorithm returns P 2 2 -win. Otherwise, our algorithm returns P 1 1 -win. 
 
 
 As there are three possible starting edges for P 1 1  and  O ⁡ ( n ) O(n)  starting edges for P 2 2  (as  T T  is a tree), our algorithm runs in  O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2})  time. 
 
 
 If  T T  has two central vertices  c 1 c_{1}  and  c 2 c_{2}  with  deg ⁡ ( c 1 ) ≥ deg ⁡ ( c 2 ) \deg(c_{1})\geq\deg(c_{2}) , our algorithm checks if  deg ⁡ ( c 1 ) = 3 \deg(c_{1})=3  and  deg ⁡ ( c 2 ) = 2 \deg(c_{2})=2 , and returns P 2 2 -win if not. If these degree conditions are satisfied, the algorithm then iterates over all starting moves  x → y x\to y  for P 2 2  and runs  R P E G ( T , c 2 → c 1 , x → y ) RPEG(T,c_{2}\to c_{1},x\to y) . If there is an edge  x → y x\to y  such that  R P E G ( T , c 2 → c 1 , x → y ) RPEG(T,c_{2}\to c_{1},x\to y)  returns P 2 2 -win, then our algorithm returns P 2 2 -win and returns P 1 1 -win otherwise. 
 
 
 As there is exactly one possible starting move for P 1 1  by Lemma  11  and  O ⁡ ( n ) O(n)  possible starting moves for P 2 2 , our algorithm runs in  O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2})  time on a tree with  2 2  central vertices. 
 
 
 The correctness of the algorithm then follows from Lemmas  11  and  12 .
∎ 
 
 
 
 
 
 5  NP-hardness 

 
 In this section we prove that Trail Trap is NP-hard for both connected and disconnected graphs. In the case of connected graphs, we prove that Trail Trap is NP-hard even on a subclass of bipartite planar graphs. As a tool in our proofs we will consider the following decision problems. Recall that a Hamiltonian path in a graph is a path that visits every vertex of the graph exactly once. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Hamiltonian path problem 
 
 
 
 Input: A graph  G G . 
 
 Question: Does  G G  have a Hamiltonian path? 
 
 Long trail problem 
 
 Input: A graph  G G . 
 
 Question: Does  G G  have a trail of length at least  | V ⁡ ( G ) | + 1 |V(G)|+1 ? 
 
 Fixed long trail problem 
 
 Input: A graph  G G  and a vertex  u ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) u\in V(G) . 
 
 Question: Does  G G  have a trail of length at least  | V ⁡ ( G ) | + 1 |V(G)|+1  starting at  u u ? 
 
 
 
 
 Before we prove that Trail Trap is NP-hard, we recall a known result and establish the computational complexity of the auxiliary problems mentioned above. Recall that if a problem is NP-complete, then it is also NP-hard. 
 
 
 Theorem 15   ( [ 16 , Theorem 23] ) . 
 
 The  Hamiltonian path problem  is NP-complete on bipartite cubic planar graphs. 
 
 
 
 It turns out that the  Hamiltonian path problem  on cubic graphs is closely related to the problem of finding the length of the longest trail on a cubic graph. Observe also that if  G G  is a cubic graph, then every trail in  G G  is of length at most  | V ⁡ ( G ) | + 1 |V(G)|+1 .
The following approach was inspired by  [ 7 ] . 
 
 
 Lemma 16 . 
 
 The  Long trail problem  is NP-complete on bipartite cubic planar graphs. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Given a trail in a graph  G G , we can check in polynomial time if the length of the trail is at least  | V ⁡ ( G ) | + 1 |V(G)|+1 . Thus the  Long trail problem  is in NP. 
 
 
 Let  G G  be a bipartite cubic planar graph on  n n  vertices. We prove that  G G  has a trail of length  n + 1 n+1  if and only if  G G  has a Hamiltonian path. 
 
 
 Let  v 1 ​ … ​ v n v_{1}\ldots v_{n}  be a Hamiltonian path in  G G . Since  G G  is cubic, there is a vertex  v i v_{i} ,  i ∉ { 2 , n } i\notin\{2,n\} , such that  v 1 ​ v i ∈ E ⁡ ( G ) v_{1}v_{i}\in E(G) , and there is a vertex  v j v_{j} ,  j ∉ { n − 1 , i } j\notin\{n-1,i\} , such that  v n ​ v j ∈ E ⁡ ( G ) v_{n}v_{j}\in E(G) . Then  v i ​ v 1 ​ … ​ v n ​ v j v_{i}v_{1}\ldots v_{n}v_{j}  is a trail of length  n + 1 n+1  in  G G . 
 
 
 Suppose instead that  G G  has a trail  𝒯 \mathcal{T}  of length  n + 1 n+1 , say  v i 1 ​ … ​ v i n + 2 v_{i_{1}}\ldots v_{i_{n+2}} . Since  G G  is cubic, a vertex appears in  𝒯 \mathcal{T}  once, or possibly twice if it is an end-vertex of  𝒯 \mathcal{T} . Since  𝒯 \mathcal{T}  visits  n + 2 n+2  vertices, all vertices of  G G  appear on  𝒯 \mathcal{T}  at least once, with end-points appearing on  𝒯 \mathcal{T}  twice. Thus  v i 2 ​ … ​ v i n + 1 v_{i_{2}}\ldots v_{i_{n+1}}  is a Hamiltonian path in  G G  as it visits every vertex of  G G  exactly once. 
 
 
 Hence it follows from Theorem  15  that the  Long trail problem  is NP-complete on bipartite cubic planar graphs.
∎ 
 
 
 
 Corollary 17 . 
 
 The  Fixed long trail problem  is NP-complete on bipartite cubic planar graphs. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Since the  Long trail problem  is in NP, so too is the  Fixed long trail problem . We will reduce the  Long trail problem  on bipartite cubic planar graphs to the  Fixed long trail problem  on bipartite cubic planar graphs. Suppose that for bipartite cubic planar graphs, we have an algorithm with complexity  f ⁡ ( n ) f(n)  to decide if there is a trail of length  n + 1 n+1  in  G G  starting at  u u . Then by repeating this algorithm  n n  times (at each vertex) we would obtain an algorithm with complexity  n ​ f ​ ( n ) nf(n)  for deciding whether there is a trail of length  n + 1 n+1  in  G G , starting anywhere. If  f ⁡ ( n ) f(n)  is polynomial in  n n , then so is  n ​ f ​ ( n ) nf(n) . Thus, by Lemma  16 , determining whether  G G  is has a trail of length  n + 1 n+1  starting at a fixed vertex is also NP-hard, and hence also NP-complete.
∎ 
 
 
 
 We are now able to prove that Trail Trap is NP-hard on connected bipartite planar graphs with maximum degree 4. 
 
 
 Theorem 18 . 
 
 Trail Trap is NP-hard on connected bipartite planar graphs with maximum degree 4. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Let  G G  be a connected bipartite planar cubic graph on  n n  vertices and fix a vertex  w ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) w\in V(G) . We construct the graph  G ^ \widehat{G}  from  G G  by adding a new vertex  u u  adjacent to  w w , i.e.  V ⁡ ( G ^ ) = V ⁡ ( G ) ∪ { u } V(\widehat{G})=V(G)\cup\{u\}  and  E ⁡ ( G ^ ) = E ⁡ ( G ) ∪ { u ​ w } E(\widehat{G})=E(G)\cup\{uw\} . Since  G G  is cubic, the maximum trail length of  G G  is at most  n + 1 n+1 , so every trail in  G ^ \widehat{G}  that does not start or end at  u u  is of length at most  n + 1 n+1  (since  deg G ^ ⁡ ( u ) = 1 \deg_{\widehat{G}}(u)=1 ). Therefore  G ^ \widehat{G}  has a trail of length  n + 2 n+2  if and only if  G G  has a trail of length  n + 1 n+1  starting at  w w . By Corollary  17 , determining whether  G ^ \widehat{G}  has a trail of length  n + 2 n+2  is NP-hard, since it is equivalent to determining whether  G G  has a trail of length  n + 1 n+1  starting at  w w . 
 
 
 We reduce finding a trail of length  n + 2 n+2  in  G ^ \widehat{G}  to Trail Trap on some connected bipartite planar graph (with maximum degree 4) as follows. Let  Q Q  be the path with vertices  v 1 , … , v 2 ​ n + 3 v_{1},\ldots,v_{2n+3}  and edges  v i ​ v i + 1 v_{i}v_{i+1}  for  1 ≤ i ≤ 2 ​ i + 2 1\leq i\leq 2i+2 . Since  v n + 2 v_{n+2}  is the unique central vertex of  Q Q , we refer to it as  c c . Let  G ′ G^{\prime}  be the graph obtained from the disjoint union of  Q Q  and  G ^ \widehat{G}  by adding an edge  u ​ c uc  (see Figure  8 ). Observe that  G ′ G^{\prime}  is connected, bipartite, planar and has maximum degree 4. We claim that P 2 2  wins in Trail Trap on  G ′ G^{\prime}  if and only if  G ^ \widehat{G}  has a trail of length  n + 2 n+2 .

 If  G ^ \widehat{G}  does not have a trail of length  n + 2 n+2 , then P 1 1 ’s winning strategy is to start the game by playing  u → c u\to c , thus securing a trail of length  n + 2 n+2 . If P 1 1  does so, then P 2 2  can only get a trail of length at most  n + 1 n+1 , regardless of whether they begin play on  Q Q  or  G ^ \widehat{G} . Thus, P 1 1  wins. 
 
 
 If  G ^ \widehat{G}  does have a trail of length  n + 2 n+2 , then there are several options for P 1 1 ’s first move. 
 
 (i)  
 
 P 1 1 ’s first move is  x → y x\to y  for some  x ​ y ∈ E ⁡ ( G ^ ) xy\in E(\widehat{G}) .
 If P 2 2  moves  u → c u\to c , then since  G G  is cubic and  G ^ \widehat{G}  is defined as above,  ℓ ⁡ ( G ′ − x ​ y − u ​ c , y ) ≤ n + 1 \ell(G^{\prime}-xy-uc,y)\leq n+1 , and clearly  ℓ ⁡ ( G ′ − x ​ y − u ​ c , c ) = n + 1 \ell(G^{\prime}-xy-uc,c)=n+1 . Thus by Lemma  9 , P 2 2  wins. 
 
 (ii)  
 
 P 1 1 ’s first move is  c → u c\to u  or  v i ± 1 → v i v_{i\pm 1}\to v_{i} , where  v i ≠ c v_{i}\neq c .
 Since  deg ⁡ ( u ) = 2 \deg(u)=2  and  deg ⁡ ( v i ) ≤ 2 \deg(v_{i})\leq 2 , P 2 2  wins by Corollary  10 . 
 
 (iii)  
 
 P 1 1 ’s first move is  x → c x\to c  for some  x ∈ V ⁡ ( G ′ ) x\in V(G^{\prime}) .
 This means the longest trail P 1 1  can get is of length  n + 2 n+2 . P 2 2 ’s winning strategy is to make their first move by playing  u → w u\to w  (possible no matter which of the three options is P 1 1 ’s first move), and then continue along a trail of length  n + 2 n+2  in  G ^ \widehat{G} . Thus, P 2 2  secures a trail of length  n + 2 n+2  and wins. 
 
 
 
 
 Thus, P 2 2  wins Trail Trap on  G ′ G^{\prime}  if and only if  G ^ \widehat{G}  has a trail of length  n + 2 n+2 , which implies that Trail Trap is NP-hard on connected bipartite planar graphs with maximum degree 4.
∎ 
 
 
 
 We conclude the section by noting that Trail Trap is also NP-hard on disconnected graphs. Our reduction works as follows. Starting with a connected graph  G G  we define a disconnected graph  G ′ G^{\prime}  as a disjoint union of  G G  and  K 1 K_{1} . Observe that players can only make valid moves on the component isomorphic to  G G . Thus P 1 1  wins Trail Trap on  G G  if and only if P 1 1  wins Trail Trap on  G ′ G^{\prime} . Since Trail Trap is NP-hard on connected graphs by Theorem  18 , Trail Trap is also NP-hard on disconnected graphs. 
 
 
 Corollary 19 . 
 
 Trail Trap is NP-hard on disconnected graphs. 
 
 
 
 Observe that the proof of Corollary  19  actually verifies that Trail Trap is NP-hard even on disconnected bipartite planar graphs with maximum degree 4. 
 
 
 
 6  Open Problems 

 
 There are a number of problems remaining, some of which we mention here. 
 
 
 Question 20 . 
 
 For which  n ∈ ℕ n\in\mathbb{N}  is  K n K_{n}  a  P 1 1 -win graph? 
 
 
 
 For  n > 9 n>9 , determining the winner of Trail Trap on  K n K_{n}  requires excessive computation time with our current implementation. Similarly, we are unable to compute the winner on complete bipartite graphs exceeding  16 16  vertices (besides those covered by Theorem  4 ). However, we pose a conjecture for balanced complete bipartite graphs. 
 
 
 Conjecture 21 . 
 
 For  n ≥ 3 n\geq 3  odd,  K n , n K_{n,n}  is  P 2 2 -win. 
 
 
 
 We show above that Trail Trap is NP-hard even on rather restricted classes of graphs. We suspect that the following stronger statement is true. 
 
 
 Conjecture 22 . 
 
 Trail Trap is PSPACE-complete. 
 
 
 
 Since a study of cubic graphs is crucial to our proof that Trail Trap is NP-hard, the following is of interest. 
 
 
 Question 23 . 
 
 Is there a non-trivial restricted class of cubic graphs that are  P 1 1 -win? For instance, what can be said about Trail Trap on Hamiltonian cubic graphs? 
 
 
 
 Finally, we find that nearly all small graphs are P 2 2 -win. Indeed, much of our work focuses on finding graph classes where P 1 1  wins. As a result, we conjecture the following: 
 
 
 Conjecture 24 . 
 
 The proportion of  P 1 1 -win graphs on  n n  vertices tends to  0 0  as  n → ∞ n\to\infty .
