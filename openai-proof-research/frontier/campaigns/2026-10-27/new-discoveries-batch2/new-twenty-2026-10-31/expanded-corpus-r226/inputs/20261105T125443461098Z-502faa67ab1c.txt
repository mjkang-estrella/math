 Abstract 
    
 Let  G G  be a  d d -regular graph and let  F ⊆ { 0 , 1 , 2 , … , d } F\subseteq\{0,1,2,\ldots,d\}  be a list of forbidden out-degrees. Akbari, Dalirrooyfard, Ehsani, Ozeki, and Sherkati conjectured that if  | F | < 1 2 ​ d |F|<\tfrac{1}{2}d , then  G G  should admit an  F F -avoiding orientation, i.e., an orientation where no out-degrees are in the forbidden list  F F . The conjecture is known for  d ≤ 4 d\leq 4  due to work of Ma and Lu, and here we extend this to  d ≤ 6 d\leq 6 . The conjecture has also been studied in a generalized version, where  d , F d,F  are changed from constant values to functions  d ⁡ ( v ) , F ⁡ ( v ) d(v),F(v)  that vary over all  v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) v\in V(G) . We provide support for this generalized version by verifying it for some new cases, including when  G G  is 2-degenerate and when every  F ⁡ ( v ) F(v)  has some specific structure. 
  
 
 
 1  Introduction 

 
 In this paper all graphs are loopless, although parallel edges are permitted. We follow  [ 9 ]  for standard terms not defined here. 
 
 
 An  orientation   D D  of a graph  G G  is an assignment of direction to each edge in  G G , so that an edge points  out  of one of its endpoints and  in  to its other endpoint. For a given orientation, the number of edges pointing out of a vertex is its  out-degree  (and the number pointing in is its  in-degree ). In this paper we are concerned about the existence of orientations where certain out-degree values are forbidden.
Consider the following conjecture. 
 
 
 Conjecture 1   (Akbari, Dalirrooyfard, Ehsani, Ozeki, and Sherkati  [ 1 ] ) . 
 
 Let  G G  be a  d d -reglar graph and let  F ⊆ { 0 , 1 , … , d } F\subseteq\{0,1,\ldots,d\}  be a list of forbidden out-degrees. If  | F | < 1 2 ​ d |F|<\tfrac{1}{2}d , then  G G  admits an  F F -avoiding orientation, that is, an orientation where no out-degrees are in the forbidden list  F F . 
 
 
 
 Conjecture  1  is tight if true – in particular, the authors of the conjecture proved in  [ 1 ]  that  K 2 ​ k + 1 K_{2k+1}  has no  F F -avoiding orientation for  F = { k , … , 2 ​ k − 1 } F=\{k,\ldots,2k-1\} . They also proved that their conjecture holds for all bipartite graphs and all cliques. In terms of specific  d d , Conjecture  1  is trivial for  d = 1 , 2 d=1,2  (where the forbidden list is empty), and true for  d = 3 , 4 d=3,4  (see Ma and Lu  [ 8 ] ). Here we verify the next cases of  d = 5 , 6 d=5,6  with the following theorem. 
 
 
 Theorem 2 . 
 
 Let  G G  be a  d d -regular graph with  d ≥ 5 d\geq 5 , and let  F ⊆ { 0 , 1 , 2 , … , d } F\subseteq\{0,1,2,\ldots,d\}  with  | F | ≤ 2 |F|\leq 2 . Then  G G  admits an  F F -avoiding orientation. 
 
 
 
 Our proof of Theorem  2  appears in Section 4 of this paper, and we shall say more about it shortly. 
 
 
 Conjecture  1  can be generalized in two directions, by changing  d , F d,F  from constant values to functions  d ⁡ ( v ) , F ⁡ ( v ) d(v),F(v)  that vary over every  v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) v\in V(G) . Given an orientation  D D  of a graph  G G , the degree  d ⁡ ( v ) d(v)  of a vertex  v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) v\in V(G)  is partitioned into out-degree  d D + ​ ( v ) d_{D}^{+}(v)  and in-degree  d D − ​ ( v ) d_{D}^{-}(v)  in  D D . Given a function  F : V ⁡ ( G ) → 2 ℕ F:V(G)\to 2^{\mathbb{N}}  of forbidden values for each vertex, we say that the orientation  D D  is  F F -avoiding  if  d D + ​ ( v ) ∉ F ⁡ ( v ) d^{+}_{D}(v)\notin F(v)  for all  v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) v\in V(G) . 
 
 
 Conjecture 3   (Akbari, Dalirrooyfard, Ehsani, Ozeki, and Sherkati  [ 1 ] ) . 
 
 Let  G G  be a graph and let  F : V ⁡ ( G ) → 2 ℕ F:V(G)\to 2^{\mathbb{N}} . If  | F ⁡ ( v ) | < 1 2 ​ d ​ ( v ) |F(v)|<\tfrac{1}{2}d(v)  for all  v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) v\in V(G) , then  G G  admits an  F F -avoiding orientation. 
 
 
 
 The known tight examples for Conjecture  3  are the same as those for Conjecture  1 . On the other hand, while bipartite graphs were shown to also satisfy the more general conjecture in  [ 1 ]  (in fact even with list sizes equal to  1 2 ​ d ​ ( v ) \tfrac{1}{2}d(v) ), cliques were not, and indeed Conjecture  3  is still unproven for cliques of size  6 6  and greater. Here, we add to the families known to satisfy Conjecture  3  with the following two results. 
 
 
 Theorem 4 . 
 
 Let  G G  be a 2-degenerate graph and let  F : V ⁡ ( G ) → 2 ℕ F:V(G)\to 2^{\mathbb{N}}  with  F ⁡ ( v ) < 1 2 ​ d ​ ( v ) F(v)<\tfrac{1}{2}d(v)  for all  v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) v\in V(G) . Then  G G  has an  F F -avoiding orientation. 
 
 
 
 Theorem 5 . 
 
 Let  G G  be a graph and let  F : V ⁡ ( G ) → 2 ℕ F:V(G)\to 2^{\mathbb{N}}  where  | F ⁡ ( v ) | < 1 2 ​ d ​ ( v ) |F(v)|<\frac{1}{2}d(v)  for all  v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) v\in V(G) . Suppose that  G G  can be decomposed into a bipartite graph and a subgraph  H H  with  Δ ⁡ ( H ) ≤ 2 \Delta(H)\leq 2  and such that every vertex  v ∈ V ⁡ ( H ) v\in V(H)  with  d H ​ ( v ) = 2 d_{H}(v)=2  has  d G ​ ( v ) ≡ 0 ( mod 2 ) d_{G}(v)\equiv 0\pmod{2} . Then  G G  admits an  F F -avoiding orientation. 
 
 
 
 Note that by a 2-degenerate graph we mean a graph where every subgraph contains a vertex of degree at most  2 2 . Hence the class of 2-degenerate graphs includes all 1-degenerate graphs (i.e. all forests). It also includes all planar graphs of girth at least 6. (To see this, note that for a planar graph  G G  of girth  g g , Euler’s formula gives  ( mad ⁡ ( G ) − 2 ) ​ ( g − 2 ) < 4 (\mad(G)-2)(g-2)<4 , where  mad ⁡ ( G ) \mad(G)  is the  maximum average degree  of  G G  defined by  mad ⁡ ( G ) = max ⁡ { 2 ​ | E ⁡ ( H ) | | V ⁡ ( H ) | : H ⊆ G } \mad(G)=\max\{\tfrac{2|E(H)|}{|V(H)|}:H\subseteq G\} ). Our proof of Theorem  4 , which is in Section 2, involves arguing about smallest possible counterexamples; as part of Section 2 we also prove a general list of properties that any smallest counterexample to Conjecture  3  must have. The same sort of arguments allow us to bootstrap the afore-mentioned result for bipartite graphs from  [ 1 ]  into Theorem  5 , the proof of which is also contained in Section 2. 
 
 
 There has been more success with approximations to Conjecture  3  than verifications for special families. In their initial paper, Akbari et al. [ 1 ]  showed that replacing  1 2 \tfrac{1}{2}  with  1 4 \tfrac{1}{4}  results in a true statement. Recently Bradshaw, Chen, Ma, Mohar, and Wu  [ 5 ]  improved this upper bound to  ⌊ 1 3 ​ d G ​ ( v ) ⌋ \lfloor\frac{1}{3}d_{G}(v)\rfloor  using the combinatorial nullstellensatz of Alon and Tarsi  [ 4 ] [ 2 ] . 
 
 
 Another approach to Conjecture  3  has been to consider special sorts of lists. To this end, let  G G  be a graph with forbidden lists  F : V ⁡ ( G ) → 2 ℕ F:V(G)\to 2^{\mathbb{N}} . For every vertex  v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) v\in V(G)  we can partition  { 0 , … , d ⁡ ( v ) } \{0,\ldots,d(v)\}  into maximal intervals in  F ⁡ ( v ) F(v) , which we call  holes , and maximal intervals not in  F ⁡ ( v ) F(v) , which we call  homes . Ma and Lu  [ 8 ]  proved that given a graph  G G  and function  F : V ⁡ ( G ) → 2 ℕ F:V(G)\to 2^{\mathbb{N}} , if every hole has size at most one then  G G  admits an  F F -avoiding orientation. Note that lists of this sort may satisfy  | F ⁡ ( v ) | ∼ 1 2 ​ d G ​ ( v ) |F(v)|\sim\tfrac{1}{2}d_{G}(v)  for all  v v . In this paper we take another step in this direction and allows holes of size two, given that the overall ratio is somewhat worse, and given a condition on the  end-intervals . The  end-intervals  for a vertex  v v  are those holes or homes containing  0 0  or  d ⁡ ( v ) d(v) ; note that each vertex has two end-intervals but they need not be distinct. 
 
 
 Theorem 6 . 
 
 Let  G G  be a graph and let  F : V ⁡ ( G ) → 2 ℕ F:V(G)\to 2^{\mathbb{N}}  be such that for each  v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) v\in V(G) : all holes have size at most two; between any pair of distinct holes is a home of size at least 3, and both end-intervals are homes of size at least two. Then  G G  admits an  F F -avoiding orientation. 
 
 
 
 Observe that in  Theorem   6 , the lists may satisfy
 | F ⁡ ( v ) | ∼ 2 5 ​ d G ​ ( v ) |F(v)|\sim\tfrac{2}{5}d_{G}(v) , beating the  1 3 \tfrac{1}{3}  bound. Our proof of  Theorem   6  uses a new tool for modifying orientations called a  lasso ; we will introduce this concept and prove  Theorem   6  in Section 4. In Section 3 we prove the following two results about very special forbidden lists that satisfy Conjecture  3 . 
 
 
 Theorem 7 . 
 
 Let  G G  be a graph and let  F : V ⁡ ( G ) → 2 ℕ F:V(G)\to 2^{\mathbb{N}}  where  | F ⁡ ( v ) | ≤ 1 2 ​ d ​ ( v ) |F(v)|\leq\frac{1}{2}d(v)  for all  v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) v\in V(G) . If every hole is an end-interval, then  G G  admits an  F F -avoiding orientation. 
 
 
 
 Theorem 8 . 
 
 Let  k ∈ ℤ + k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+} . Every  ( 2 ​ k + 1 ) (2k+1) -regular graph admits a  { 1 , 2 , … , k } \{1,2,...,k\} -avoiding orientation. 
 
 
 
 We will see that that proof of Theorem  7  follows from a classic theorem on orientations due to Frank and Gyarfas  [ 6 ]  (also Hakimi  [ 7 ] ), and Theorem  8  follows from a result about maximum directed cuts due to Along, Bollobás, Gyárfás, Lehel and Scott  [ 3 ] . Theorem  8  is interesting in that it is close to the tightness result of Akbari et al.  [ 1 ]  mentioned above. The reason we prove it here however it that it is a needed special case towards our proof of Theorem  2 . We will see that the other needed cases come from Theorem  6  and our new lasso technique.

 4  The lasso and proofs of Theorems  2  and  6 

 
 Given a graph  G G  and forbidden list  F : V ⁡ ( G ) → 2 ℕ F:V(G)\to 2^{\mathbb{N}} , for any orientation  D D  of  G G  we define  D i = { v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) : d D + ​ ( v ) = i } D_{i}=\{v\in V(G):d^{+}_{D}(v)=i\}  and  D F = { v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) : d D + ​ ( v ) ∈ F ⁡ ( v ) } D_{F}=\{v\in V(G):d^{+}_{D}(v)\in F(v)\} .  A common proof strategy when working with orientations is to find a directed path and then reverse the direction of all of its edges; note that in this modification only the endpoints of the path change their out-degrees. In this section we find another directed subgraph, which we’ll call a  lasso , which can also be used to alter out-degrees in a controlled way. 
 
 
 Consider the graph obtained from the path  ( v 1 , … , v k ) (v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})  by adding a single edge between  v k v_{k}  and  v i v_{i}  for some  2 ≤ i ≤ k − 1 2\leq i\leq k-1 .  D F = { v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) : d D + ​ ( v ) ∈ F ⁡ ( v ) } D_{F}=\{v\in V(G):d^{+}_{D}(v)\in F(v)\} ; we now extend this definition so that we may also refer to  D A , D B , D X D_{A},D_{B},D_{X} , that is, those vertices  v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) v\in V(G)  with out-degree in the set  A ⁡ ( v ) , B ⁡ ( v ) A(v),B(v) , or  X ⁡ ( v ) X(v) , respectively. 
 
 
 Let  D D  be an orientation which minimizes  | D F | |D_{F}|  and subject to that, maximizes  | D X | |D_{X}| . If  | D F | = 0 |D_{F}|=0  then  D D  is an  F F -avoiding orientation, so suppose for a contradiction that  | D F | ≠ 0 |D_{F}|\neq 0 ; hence we may choose  v ∈ D F v\in D_{F} . Note that since all holes of  v v  are of size at most  2 2 , and we cannot have a hole of size two as en end-interval, either  d D + ​ ( v ) + 1 ∈ A ⁡ ( v ) d^{+}_{D}(v)+1\in A(v) , or  d D + ​ ( v ) − 1 ∈ B ⁡ ( v ) d^{+}_{D}(v)-1\in B(v) . We shall consider both cases. 
 
 
 Case 1:  d D + ​ ( v ) + 1 ∈ A ⁡ ( v ) d^{+}_{D}(v)+1\in A(v) . We wish to increase the out-degree of  v v  by one, moving  v v  into a home. To this end, note that there exists  w ∈ T v ∖ { v } w\in T_{v}\setminus\{v\} , since  d D + ​ ( v ) + 1 ∈ A ⁡ ( v ) d^{+}_{D}(v)+1\in A(v)  implying that  d D + ​ ( v ) ≠ d ⁡ ( v ) d^{+}_{D}(v)\neq d(v) . Choose some directed path from  w w  to  v v  in  D D , and let  D ′ D^{\prime}  be the orientation obtained from  D D  by reversing the direction of every edge of this path. Note  d D ′ + ​ ( v ) = d D + ​ ( v ) + 1 d^{+}_{D^{\prime}}(v)=d^{+}_{D}(v)+1  so  v ∉ D F ′ v\notin D^{\prime}_{F}  and thus by minimality of  | D F | |D_{F}| ,  w ∈ D F ′ w\in D^{\prime}_{F}  and  w ∉ D F w\notin D_{F} . Since  d D ′ + ​ ( w ) = d D + ​ ( w ) − 1 d^{+}_{D^{\prime}}(w)=d^{+}_{D}(w)-1 , this means that  w ∈ D A w\in D_{A} . Moreover,  T v ∖ { v } ⊆ D A T_{v}\setminus\{v\}\subseteq D_{A} . 
 
 
 Suppose now that there exists some  u ∈ S v ∩ T v ∖ { v } u\in S_{v}\cap T_{v}\setminus\{v\} . Since  u ∈ T v ∖ { v } u\in T_{v}\setminus\{v\} , we also know that  u ∈ D A u\in D_{A} . Since  u ∈ S v ∖ { v } u\in S_{v}\setminus\{v\} , we can let  D ′′ D^{\prime\prime}  be the orientation obtained from  D D  by reversing the direction of each edge in some directed path from  v v  to  u u . Then  d D ′′ + ​ ( v ) = d D + ​ ( v ) − 1 d^{+}_{D^{\prime\prime}}(v)=d^{+}_{D}(v)-1  and  d D ′′ + ​ ( u ) = d D + ​ ( u ) + 1 d^{+}_{D^{\prime\prime}}(u)=d^{+}_{D}(u)+1 . Since  u ∈ D A u\in D_{A}  this means that  u ∈ D X ′′ u\in D^{\prime\prime}_{X}  (since all holes between homes have size at least three). We may have  v ∈ D F ′′ v\in D^{\prime\prime}_{F}  or not, depending on whether the hole is size one or two. But in any case, we have  | D F ′′ | ≤ | D F | |D^{\prime\prime}_{F}|\leq|D_{F}|  and  | D X ′′ | > | D X | |D^{\prime\prime}_{X}|>|D_{X}| , and we obtain a contradiction. Thus, we may assume  S v ∩ T v = { v } S_{v}\cap T_{v}=\{v\} . 
 
 
 Note that  T v ∖ { v } T_{v}\setminus\{v\}  contains no sources, since  T v ∖ { v } ⊆ D A T_{v}\setminus\{v\}\subseteq D_{A} , and since end-intervals cannot be homes of size one. Thus, by  Lemma   12  there exists an in-lasso starting at  v v  of type  ( d D + ​ ( v ) , d D + ​ ( w 1 ) , d D + ​ ( w 2 ) ) (d^{+}_{D}(v),d^{+}_{D}(w_{1}),d^{+}_{D}(w_{2}))  for some  w 1 , w 2 ∈ T v ∖ { v } w_{1},w_{2}\in T_{v}\setminus\{v\}  which implies  w 1 , w 2 ∈ D A w_{1},w_{2}\in D_{A} . Let  D ∗ D^{*}  be the orientation obtained from  D D  by flipping the in-lasso. Then the flipped in-lasso is of type  ( d D + ​ ( v ) + 1 , d D + ​ ( w 1 ) − 2 , d D + ​ ( w 2 ) + 1 ) (d^{+}_{D}(v)+1,d^{+}_{D}(w_{1})-2,d^{+}_{D}(w_{2})+1)  with respect to  D ∗ D^{*} . By the assumption of this case, this means that  v ∈ D A ∗ v\in D^{*}_{A} . Since  w 2 ∈ D A w_{2}\in D_{A} , and between any two distinct holes is a home of size at least  3 3 , we also get that  w 2 ∈ D X ∗ w_{2}\in D^{*}_{X} . We may have  w 1 ∈ D F ∗ w_{1}\in D^{*}_{F}  or not, depending on whether the hole is size one or two. However, in any case,  | D F ∗ | ≤ | D F | |D^{*}_{F}|\leq|D_{F}|  and  | D X ∗ | > | D X | |D^{*}_{X}|>|D_{X}| , and we obtain a contradiction. 
 
 
 Case 2:   d D + ​ ( v ) − 1 ∈ B F ​ ( v ) d^{+}_{D}(v)-1\in B_{F}(v) . We wish to decrease the out-degree of  v v  by one, moving  v v  into a home. To this end, note that there exists  u ∈ S v ∖ { v } u\in S_{v}\setminus\{v\} , since  d D + ​ ( v ) − 1 ∈ B ⁡ ( v ) d^{+}_{D}(v)-1\in B(v)  implying that  d D + ​ ( v ) ≠ 0 d^{+}_{D}(v)\neq 0 . Choose some directed path from  v v  to  u u  in  D D , and let  D ′ D^{\prime}  be the orientation obtained from  D D  by reversing the direction of every edge of this path. Note  d D ′ + ​ ( v ) = d D + ​ ( v ) − 1 d^{+}_{D^{\prime}}(v)=d^{+}_{D}(v)-1  so  v ∉ D F ′ v\notin D^{\prime}_{F}  and thus by minimality of  | D F | |D_{F}| ,  u ∈ D F ′ u\in D^{\prime}_{F}  and  u ∉ D F u\notin D_{F} . Since  d D ′ + ​ ( u ) = d D + ​ ( u ) + 1 d^{+}_{D^{\prime}}(u)=d^{+}_{D}(u)+1 , this means that  u ∈ D B u\in D_{B} . Moreover,  S v ∖ { v } ⊆ D B S_{v}\setminus\{v\}\subseteq D_{B} . 
 
 
 Suppose now that there exists some  w ∈ S v ∩ T v ∖ { v } w\in S_{v}\cap T_{v}\setminus\{v\} . Since  w ∈ S v ∖ { v } w\in S_{v}\setminus\{v\} , we also know that  w ∈ D B w\in D_{B} . Since  w ∈ S v ∖ { v } w\in S_{v}\setminus\{v\} , we can let  D ′′ D^{\prime\prime}  be the orientation obtained from  D D  by reversing the direction of each edge in some directed path from  v v  to  w w . Then  d D ′′ + ​ ( v ) = d D + ​ ( v ) − 1 d^{+}_{D^{\prime\prime}}(v)=d^{+}_{D}(v)-1  and  d D ′′ + ​ ( w ) = d D + ​ ( w ) + 1 d^{+}_{D^{\prime\prime}}(w)=d^{+}_{D}(w)+1 . Since  w ∈ D B w\in D_{B}  this means that  w ∈ D X ′′ w\in D^{\prime\prime}_{X}  (since all holes between homes have size at least three). We may have  v ∈ D F ′′ v\in D^{\prime\prime}_{F}  or not, depending on whether the hole is size one or two. But in any case, we have  | D F ′′ | ≤ | D F | |D^{\prime\prime}_{F}|\leq|D_{F}|  and  | D X ′′ | > | D X | |D^{\prime\prime}_{X}|>|D_{X}| , and we obtain a contradiction. Thus, we may assume  S v ∩ T v = { v } S_{v}\cap T_{v}=\{v\} . 
 
 
 Note that  S v ∖ { v } S_{v}\setminus\{v\}  contains no sinks, since  S v ∖ { v } ⊆ D B S_{v}\setminus\{v\}\subseteq D_{B} , and since end-intervals cannot be homes of size one. Thus, by  Lemma   12  there exists an out-lasso starting at  v v  of type  ( d D + ​ ( v ) , d D + ​ ( u 1 ) , d D + ​ ( u 2 ) ) (d^{+}_{D}(v),d^{+}_{D}(u_{1}),d^{+}_{D}(u_{2}))  for some  u 1 , u 2 ∈ S v ∖ { v } u_{1},u_{2}\in S_{v}\setminus\{v\} . Let  D ∗ D^{*}  be the orientation obtained from  D D  by flipping the out-lasso. Then the flipped out-lasso is of type  ( d D + ​ ( v ) − 1 , d D + ​ ( u 1 ) + 2 , d D + ​ ( u 2 ) − 1 ) (d^{+}_{D}(v)-1,d^{+}_{D}(u_{1})+2,d^{+}_{D}(u_{2})-1)  with respect to  D ∗ D^{*} . By the assumption of this case, this means that  v ∈ D B ∗ v\in D^{*}_{B} . Since  u 2 ∈ D B u_{2}\in D_{B} , and between any two distinct holes is a home of size at least  3 3 , we also get that  u 2 ∈ D X ∗ u_{2}\in D^{*}_{X} . We may have  u 1 ∈ D F ∗ u_{1}\in D^{*}_{F}  or not, depending on whether the hole is size one or two. However, in any case,  | D F ∗ | ≤ | D F | |D^{*}_{F}|\leq|D_{F}|  and  | D X ∗ | > | D X | |D^{*}_{X}|>|D_{X}| , and we obtain a contradiction.
∎ 
 
 
 
 We can now use  Theorem   6  (and Theorem  8 ) to validate  Conjecture   1  for  5 5 - and  6 6 -regular graphs. Our methods work for all cases of the following theorem, although when all holes of are of size one, we will just quote the after-mentioned result of Ma and Lu  [ 8 ]  (namely that Conjecture  3  holds when all holes have size 1), for the sake of efficiency. 
 
 
 Theorem 2 . 
 
 Let  G G  be a  d d -regular graph with  d ≥ 5 d\geq 5 , and let  F ⊆ { 0 , 1 , 2 , … , d } F\subseteq\{0,1,2,\ldots,d\}  with  | F | ≤ 2 |F|\leq 2 . Then  G G  admits an  F F -avoiding orientation. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Let  G ′ G^{\prime}  be the graph obtained from  G G  by adding a dummy vertex and joining it to every vertex of odd degree in  G G . There are an even number of these, so  G ′ G^{\prime}  is Eulerian, and can be decomposed into cycles. By orienting each of these cycles consistently we get an orientation  D ′ D^{\prime}  of  G ′ G^{\prime}  where  d D ′ + ​ ( v ) = d D ′ − ​ ( v ) d^{+}_{D^{\prime}}(v)=d_{D^{\prime}}^{-}(v)  for all  v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ′ ) v\in V(G^{\prime}) . Letting  D D  be the restriction of  D ′ D^{\prime}  to  G G  we get that  d D + ​ ( v ) ∈ { ⌊ d 2 ⌋ , ⌈ d 2 ⌉ } d^{+}_{D}(v)\in\{\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor,\lceil\frac{d}{2}\rceil\}  for all  v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) v\in V(G) . 
 
 
 As discussed above, following Ma and Lu  [ 8 ]  we may assume that  F = { x , x + 1 } F=\{x,x+1\}  for some  x ∈ { 0 , 1 , … , d } x\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\} . So, if  D D  is not an  F F -avoiding orientation then  { x , x + 1 } ∩ { ⌊ d 2 ⌋ , ⌈ d 2 ⌉ } \{x,x+1\}\cap\{\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor,\lceil\frac{d}{2}\rceil\}  is nonempty. 
 
 
 Suppose first that  x = ⌊ d 2 ⌋ x=\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor . Then  F F  consists of one hole of size two and two end-homes of sizes  ⌊ d 2 ⌋ \lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor  and  ⌈ d 2 ⌉ − 1 \lceil\frac{d}{2}\rceil-1 , respectively. Since  d ≥ 5 d\geq 5  these end-homes are both of size at least two, and hence by Theorem  6   G G  admits an  F F -avoiding orientation. 
 
 
 Suppose next that  x = ⌊ d 2 ⌋ − 1 x=\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor-1 . Then  F F  consists of one hole of size two and two end-homes of sizes  ⌊ d 2 ⌋ − 1 \lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor-1  and  ⌈ d 2 ⌉ \lceil\frac{d}{2}\rceil , respectively. If  d ≥ 6 d\geq 6  then these end-home are both of size at least two, and again Theorem  6  implies that  G G  admits an  F F -avoiding orientation. If  d = 5 d=5  then  F = { 1 , 2 } F=\{1,2\}  and we get that  G G  admits an  F F -avoiding orientation by Theorem  8 . 
 
 
 We may now assume that  x = ⌈ d 2 ⌉ = d + 1 2 x=\lceil\frac{d}{2}\rceil=\tfrac{d+1}{2}  (so  d d  is odd). Then  F F  consists of one hole of size two and two end-homes of sizes  d + 1 2 \tfrac{d+1}{2}  and  d − 3 2 \tfrac{d-3}{2} , respectively. If  d ≥ 7 d\geq 7  these end-intervals are both of size at least two, and again Theorem  6  implies that  G G  admits an  F F -avoiding orientation. If  d = 5 d=5  then  F = { 3 , 4 } F=\{3,4\} . Apply Theorem  8  to get an orientation  D ∗ D^{*}  that is  { 1 , 2 } \{1,2\} -avoiding. Then reverse the direction of every edges in  D ∗ D^{*} . This new orientation of  G G  is  { 3 , 4 } \{3,4\} -avoiding, completing our proof.
∎
