52:    Consider a graph G = (V, E) with vertex set V = [n], edge set E, and
53: fixed weights {wij }ij∈E assigned to the edges. Consider partitions, or cuts,
54: of the vertex set V = V1 t V2 . The max-cut problem asks for the maximum
55: possible sum of all weights between vertices on opposite sides of the cut. In
56: other words, we wish to maximize

63:    The max-cut problem is a key problem in theoretical computer science and
64: operations research. In particular, it is one of Karp’s 21 NP-complete prob-
65: lems [10]. Furthermore, it has applications in physics and circuit design [3].
66: In statistical physics and magnetism, the Edwards-Anderson model for the
67: ground states of spin glasses is an optimization problem in ±1-variables and
68: can be reduced to the max-cut problem. In Very-Large-Integrated-Scale
69: (VLSI) circuit design, the problem of reducing the number of vias, or con-
70: nections between layers of a circuit board, can be reduced to the max-cut
71: problem.
72:    Although the max-cut problem is NP-complete [10], a breakthrough paper
73: of Goemans and Williamson [9] proved that the max-cut problem can be
74: approximated in polynomial time up to a factor of 0.87854 using a technique
75: known as semidefinite programming (SDP).
76:    A semidefinite program is an optimization problem where we minimize a
77: linear function on the entries of a matrix subject to two types of constraints:
78: the matrix is positive semidefinite (i.e., its eigenvalues are non-negative) and
79: its entries satisfy some linear equations.
80:    Semidefinite programs have far-reaching applications [16,17], most notably
81: in approximation algorithms, which we discuss in this paper.
82:    The max-cut problem can be relaxed to a semidefinite program. The
83: max-cut SDP requires the following definitions.

111:    In this paper, we study how the optimal solution to the max-cut SDP
112: behaves under simple graph operations. The operations covered in the paper
113: are the vertex sum, where two graphs are joined at a common vertex, and the
114: edge sum, where two graphs are joined at a common edge. These operations
115: are special instances of a clique sum, where two graphs are joined at a
116: common clique Kn for some n.
117:    In this paper we pay special attention to the rank of the optimal solution
118: obtained. In general, low rank solutions to the max-cut SDP are desirable.
119: In particular, if the optimal solution is rank 1 then we may recover an exact
120: optimal solution of the max-cut problem. Our current results characterize
121: when cycle graphs have rank 1 solutions to their max-cut SDPs. We also
122: study the behaviour for random weights. For random normally distributed
123: weights, we experimentally estimate the probability that the optimal solution
124: is rank 1.
125: Related work. The max-cut SDP was first studied in [8], where the authors
126: showed promising experimental results. It gained major attention after the
127: seminal work of Goemans and Williamson [9], where they introduced a
128: rounding technique capable of producing a cut which is optimal up to a
129: factor of 0.87854. The gives the best known polynomial time approximation
130: algorithm to the max-cut problem.
131:    Having a low rank solution has many important computational and theo-
132: retical consequences. Indeed, if the optimal solution has low rank then one
133: can take advantage of faster optimization routines [5], and the approximation
134: factor can be also be improved [2, 7].
135:    The rank of the optimal solution matrix is closely related to the geometric
136: structure of the SDP. The geometry of the max-cut SDP has been investigated
137: in [6,12,13]. The paper [14] leverages geometric information to provide upper
138: bounds on the rank of the solution.
139:    It is possible to construct more sophisticated SDPs for the max-cut problem
140: that rely on a technique known as the SOS method [4]. It is an open problem
141: whether such sophisticated SDPs can improve the approximation factor. This
142: problem has received quite a lot of interest in recent years, and it is closely
143: related to a twenty-year old conjecture from computer science known as the
144: unique games conjecture [11]. We do not consider these advanced SDPs here.

249: 2.3. Clique sums of graphs. In this paper, we study ranks of solutions
250: to the primal max-cut SDP in relation to clique-sums. A clique-sum of two
251: graphs G and H which both contain a clique graph Kn , and we join G and
252: H along this clique graph Kn to form a new graph F , which has two vertex
253: sets V1 and V2 such that the following are true:
254:      • The induced subgraph of F on V1 is isomorphic to G,
255:      • The induced subgraph of F on V2 is isomorphic to H,
256:      • The induced subgraph of F on V1 ∩ V2 is isomorphic to Kn , and
257:      • V1 ∪ V2 is the vertex set of H.
258: We then define a vertex sum as a clique-sum with the clique K1 , and an edge
259: sum as a clique-sum with the clique K2 .

263:               3. Experiments on rank of max-cut SDP
264:    Rank is important to the max-cut problem because lower rank solutions
265: will be able to yield better approximate solutions to the max-cut SDP. We
266: have already noted that a rank 1 solution will exactly find the solution to
267: the max-cut problem, while it has been shown that rank 2 solutions yield
268: better approximation algorithms for the max-cut problem.
269:    Given a weighted graph G, the set of optimal primal (or dual) solutions
270: might not be unique. We let rP (G) be the largest rank among all possible pri-
271: mal optimal solutions. Similarly, we may define rD (G) to be the largest rank
272: among all possible dual optimal solutions. We say that strict complemen-
273: tarity holds if rP (G) + rD (G) = n. It is known that strict complementarity
274: holds generically [1]. Hence, we restrict our attention to the primal rank
275: rP (G).
276:    We study the primal ranks rP (G) for random families of weighted graphs.
277: For a given graph G = ([n], E), we consider two probability distributions on
278: the weights {wij : ij ∈ E}. In the first probability distribution, each weight
279: wij is an independent Gaussian random variable. The second probability
280: distribution is similar, except that we force the weights wij to be nonnegative.
281:    The probabilities shown in Table 1 are the experimental values for the
282: probability that the solution to the primal max-cut SDP of a graph returns
283: a certain rank.

286: sphere (for arbitrary weights), then 1000 different weights corresponding to
287: random points on the |E|-dimensional unit sphere in the first orthant (for
288: positive weights). We then counted the number of times that each of the

801:                                  6. Future work
802:    In our project, we began by considering a vertex sum, or what happened
803: when we joined two subgraphs at a vertex. We were able to fully characterize
804: a primal and dual solution to the SDP given solutions to the SDPs for the
805: subgraphs. Doing the same for an edge sum, or joining two graphs at an
806: edge, proved to be much more difficult. We were able to show a result for
807: joining two K3 s given a constraint on the rank. Future work may look to
808: extend our results to the max-cut SDP for general edge sums or larger clique
809: sums. To account for more general clique sums, one possible direction to
810: look at is to add two graphs. In particular, the weight of an edge in this
811: sum would be the sum of the corresponding edge weights of the summand
812: graphs. This area is promising because it gave a good characterization for
813: the theorem on the butterfly graph and for vertex sums.
814:    A conjecture we have proposed for the solutions to the max-cut SDP of
815: the edge sum of two graphs is as follows.

819: tively.

1003: 5     Further directions
1004: In the introduction, we mentioned Conger’s paper which analyzed how the num-
1005: ber of descents of a permutation affects the expected value of the first letter of
1006: the permutation. And similarly in the following sections, we looked at how the
1007: number of k-cycles affects the expected value of the first letter of the permu-
1008: tation. This section will principally look at the obvious generalization: given
1009: some permutation statistic stat : Sn → Z, does the map
1010:                      f (n, m) = E[π(i) | π ∈ Sn , stat(π) = m]                 (5.1)
1011: have any interesting structure?
1012:    But notice that the first letter of a permutation is itself a statistic, so we can
1013: play a more general game. Given pairs of statistics (stat1 , stat2 ), does the map
1014:                   g(n, m) = E[stat1 (π) | π ∈ Sn , stat2 (π) = m]              (5.2)
1015: have any interesting structure?
1016: 
1017: 5.1    FindStat database
1018: The result by Conger gives the expected value of π(1) given des(π), and this
1019: paper gave the expected value of π(1) given the number of k-cycles of π. Of
1020: course, it would be interesting to do analogous analysis with other permutations.
1021: In particular, the FindStat permutation statistics database [7] contains over
1022: 370 different permutation statistics, and many of these appear to have some
1023: structure with respect to the expected value of the first letter of a permutation.

1079: 5.3    An elusive bijection
1080: Let Fk (n, m) be the number of elements of the generalized symmetric group
1081: S(k, n) = (Z/kZ)≀Sn with m fixed points, and recall that Ck (n, m) is the number
