                                                                                           Abstract
                                                    For an additive submonoid M of R≥0 , the weight of an M-labeled directed graph is the sum
                                                of all of its edge labels, while the content is the product of the labels. Having fixed M and a
                                                directed tree E, we prove a general result on the shape of directed M-labeled graphs Γ of weight
                                                N ∈ M maximizing the sum of the contents of all copies E ⊂ Γ.
                                                    This specializes to recover a result of Hajac and Tobolski on the maximal number of length-k
                                                paths in a directed acyclic graph. It also applies to prove a conjecture by the same authors on
                                                the maximal sum of entries of Ak for a nilpotent R≥0 -valued square matrix A whose entries add
                                                up to N . Finally, we apply the same techniques to obtain the maximal number of stars with a
                                                arms in a directed graph with N edges.

                                           Key words: directed acyclic graph, labeled graph, path, star

                                           MSC 2010: 05C35; 05C20; 05C30

1    Preliminaries
All graphs under discussion are finite and directed, so we often drop these adjectives. As in the
Introduction, we abbreviate the phrase ‘directed acyclic graph’ (i.e. one without oriented cycles of
any length, including single-edge loops) as ‘DAG’.

Definition 1.1 Let M be a set with a distinguished symbol ‘0’. An M-labeled directed graph is a
simple directed graph (i.e. no repeated edges) for which every pair (x, y) of vertices carries a label
ℓ(x, y) ∈ M , with label 0 precisely when (x, y) is not and edge.
     Plain directed graphs, possibly with repeated edges, can be alternatively regarded as Z≥0 -
labeled directed graphs without repeated edges, with (x, y) carrying the label m ∈ Z≥0 if there are
m edges x → y.
     The adjacency matrix of an M-labeled graph on the vertex set I is the M-valued matrix whose
(i, j) entry (for i, j ∈ I) is ℓ(x, y).                                                             

The following notion of oriented tree is fairly common in the literature (see e.g. [2, p.310]).
Definition 2.6 An oriented tree with root v (or rooted at v) is an oriented graph with a distin-
guished vertex v such that for each vertex w there is a unique oriented path w → v.
    The arms of a rooted oriented tree are its maximal oriented paths (so they all connect a leaf to
the root).                                                                                         

   In this section we focus on specific classes of rooted directed trees.
Definition 2.7 A rooted directed tree is ℓ-equidistal if all of its arms have the same length ℓ. It is
a star if any two arms intersect only at their common target (i.e. the root of the tree).
    Finally, a rooted directed tree is a ℓ-star if it is both a star and ℓ-equidistal.              

72: A submatrix of a matrix A is a restriction of A to L1 × · · · × Ld , where Li ⊆ In for each

76: For A ∈ M(d, n) and 1 ⩽ s ⩽ d, define the s-permanent of A, denoted pers (A), to be

80: no s-diagonal exists then the s-permanent is an empty sum and hence equal to zero.

32: 
33:                                           A combinatorial polytope P is said to be projectively unique if it has a single real-
34:                                           ization up to projective transformations. In other words, P is projectively unique
35:                                           if any two (embedded) polytopes with its combinatorial structure can be mapped
36:                                           to each other by a projective transformation. Projectively unique polytopes form
37:                                           a very interesting class, where the combinatorics contain all essential information
38:                                           and the realization space is trivial in a very strong sense. In contrast, realization
39:                                           spaces of polytopes can be arbitrarily complicated in general [R-G06].
40:                                           The study of projectively unique polytopes for their own sake goes back more than
41:                                           fifty years, with important pioneering work of Perles, Shepard and McMullen (see
42:                                           for instance [Grü03, Section 4.8],[PS74] and [Mc76]), but the full characterization
43:                                           of all three-dimensional projectively unique polytopes goes back even further, being

67: In higher dimensions, projective uniqueness is much more elusive. In dimension
68: four, there is a list of 11 combinatorial classes of projectively unique polytopes
69: conjectured to be complete by Shephard and McMullen ([Mc76]). A weaker, more
70: general question, posed by Perles and Shephard in [PS74] asks if the number of such
71: combinatorial classes of polytopes in a fixed dimension d ≥ 4 is even finite. This
72: was answered negatively for d ≥ 96 in [AZ15], but remains open for 4 ≤ d ≤ 68.
73: Our work merges two ideas from the literature on projective uniqueness. Very re-
74: cently, the concept of slack ideals introduced in [GPRT17, GMTW19, GMTW20]
75: presents an algebraic take on the study of realization spaces. In [GMTW20] a
76: subclass of projectively unique polytopes was defined, the graphic polytopes, for
77: which one has an algebraic certificate of projective uniqueness. On the other hand,
78: an important early result in the study of projective uniqueness is due to [Mc76],
79: where certain operations on polytopes are introduced and proven to preserve pro-
80: jective uniqueness. Our main result is that these same operations also preserve
81: graphicality. Note that this result neither implies nor is implied by the original
82: McMullen result. This connection gives us an algebraic version of the McMullen’s
83: result that can be used to create large families of graphic polytopes, as well as to
84: prove graphicality for particular polytopes of interest.
85: Graphic polytopes are not only a subclass of projectively unique polytopes but
86: also a subclass of morally 2-level polytopes. Morally 2-level polytopes are those
87: that have 0/1 generalized slack matrices, and in particular they include all 2-level
88: polytopes. Such polytopes play an important role in the theory of semidefinite
89: representations of polyhedra and have been the focus of recent interest. Moreover,
90: they comprise a very large family that includes many interesting polytopes; see for
91: example [ACF18] for combinatorially relevant examples and [BFFFMP19] for a full
92: enumeration in dimension up to 7.
93: A perfect candidate to apply these methods is therefore the family of order poly-
94: topes. Order polytopes were introduced in [S86]. They are constructed from finite
95: posets, and we can translate properties of the poset into properties of its order

97: describe, and furthermore they are 0/1-polytopes and 2-level. Order polytopes of-
98: fer us a combinatorial window on the phenomena we are studying, since we can

1206:                         5. Graphicality of order polytopes
1207: 
1208: In this section, we put together the work developed in the previous two sections to
1209: derive sufficient conditions for graphicality, and consequently projective uniqueness,
1210: to arise in order polytopes. In particular, we will prove that every finite ranked
1211: poset with no 3-antichain has a graphic order polytope.
1212: 
1213: 24         TRISTRAM BOGART, JOÃO GOUVEIA, AND JUAN CAMILO TORRES
1214: 
1215: 
1216: We begin by stating several graphicality results that follow immediately from the
1217: descriptions of order polytopes in Section 4 along with the general results on graphi-
1218: cality in Section 3. The operations denoted in this statement are the ones introduced
1219: in Definitions 4.1 and 4.7.
1220: Proposition 5.1. Let P and Q be finite posets on disjoint ground sets. Then
1221: if O(P) and O(Q) are projectively unique (respectively graphic), so are O(Prev ),
1222: O(P ∨ Q), O(P ⊕ Q) and O(P ⊕(a,b) Q).
1223: 
1224: Proof. This is immediate from Propositions 4.3 and 4.9 that describe the effect of
1225: the operations in the order polytopes and Theorems 2.6 and 3.1 that show that
1226: they all preserve projective uniqueness and graphicality.                       
1227: 
1228: Another operation that preserves graphicality is the splitting of extremal elements,
1229: but that is a little more delicate to show.
1230: Proposition 5.2. Let P be a poset such that O(P) is graphic, and c one of its
1231: extremal elements. Then O(Pc ) is graphic.
1232: 
1233: Proof. From Proposition 4.6 we know that the operation of splitting c correspond
1234: to facet wedging in the order polytope with respect to the facet F given by the
1235: extremal element c. Moreover, Proposition 3.13 gives us a sufficient condition for
1236: such operation to preserve graphicality: that removing the facet F and all of its
1237: neighbors from the non-incidence graph of O(P) does not create any new connected
1238: components.
1239: We may assume c is a maximal element since reversing the poset preserves graph-
1240: icality. Take any pair of facets F̄ and F̃ . There must be a path Γ between them
1241: in the original non-incidence graph, say F̄ w0 F1 w1 . . . Ft wt F̃ . Recall that each wi
1242: corresponds to a filter of the poset and that adding a maximal element to a filter
1243: preserves the filter property, so let wi′ = wi ∪ {c}. None of the wi′ is a neighbor of
1244: F.
1245: Now form a new sequence Γ′ by starting with Γ and replacing wi by wi′ for each
1246: i. If wi′ 6= wi , then the only facet that neighbors wi but not wi′ is F , so the only
1247: potential problem is that F might belong to Γ, in which case Γ′ lacks one edge to
1248: be a path. In this case, let F ′ be the facet associated to a cover d ≺· c (or the
1249: facet induced by c being a minimal element if c is both maximal and minimal) and
1250: modify Γ′ by replacing F by F ′ . Now if wi neighbors F , then it corresponds to a
1251: filter that does not contain c, hence also does not contain d. Then wi′ corresponds
1252: to a filter that does contain c but does not contain d, so wi′ neighbors F ′ . Thus Γ′
1253: is now a path from F̄ to F̃ that avoides F and all of its neighbors, so the condition
1254: in Proposition 3.13 is satisfied.                                                   
