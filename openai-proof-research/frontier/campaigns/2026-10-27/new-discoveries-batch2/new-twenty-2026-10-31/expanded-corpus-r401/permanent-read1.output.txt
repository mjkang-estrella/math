                                                  Abstract. A d-dimensional matrix is called 1-polystochastic if it is non-negative and
                                                  the sum over each line equals 1. Such a matrix that has a single 1 in each line and zeros
                                                  elsewhere is called a 1-permutation matrix. A diagonal of a d-dimensional matrix of
                                                  order n is a choice of n elements, no two in the same hyperplane. The permanent of
                                                  a d-dimensional matrix is the sum over the diagonals of the product of the elements
                                                  within the diagonal.
                                                     For a given order n and dimension d, the set of 1-polystochastic matrices forms a
                                                  convex polytope that includes the 1-permutation matrices within its set of vertices.
                                                  For even n and odd d, we give a construction for a class of 1-permutation matrices with
                                                  zero permanent. Consequently, we show that the set of 1-polystochastic matrices with
                                                                                       3/2
                                                  zero permanent contains at least nn (1/2−o(1)) 1-permutation matrices and contains
                                                  a polytope of dimension at least cn3/2 for fixed c, d and even n → ∞. We also provide
                                                  counterexamples to a conjecture by Taranenko [11] about the location of local extrema
                                                  of the permanent.
                                                     For odd d, we give a construction of 1-permutation matrices that decompose into a
                                                  convex linear sum of positive diagonals. These combine with a theorem of Taranenko
                                                  [11] to provide counterexamples to a conjecture by Dow and Gibson [4] generalising
                                                  van der Waerden’s conjecture to higher dimensions.

                                                  Keywords: permanent; polystochastic; Birkhoff polytope; transversal; hypercube

In this paper we examine attempts to generalise Birkhoff’s Theorem and van der Waer-
den’s conjecture to higher dimensional matrices. For a positive integer n, let In =
{1, . . . , n}. For d ⩾ 2, a d-dimensional matrix A of order n is an array In ×· · ·×In → R.
For (i1 , . . . , id ) ∈ In × · · · × In , we refer to A(i1 , . . . , id ) as the (i1 , . . . , id ) element of A.
Denote by M(d, n) the set of d-dimensional matrices of order n.
A submatrix of a matrix A is a restriction of A to L1 × · · · × Ld , where Li ⊆ In for each
i. If for all i we have |Li | ∈ {1, n}, then the submatrix is called a k-plane, where k is
the number of subsets Li with |Li | = n. We will call a (d − 1)-plane of A a hyperplane,
and a 1-plane of A a line.
For A ∈ M(d, n) and 1 ⩽ s ⩽ d, define the s-permanent of A, denoted pers (A), to be
the sum of all products of ns elements of A, no two in the same (d − s)-plane. We will
call such a selection of ns elements, no two in the same (d − s)-plane, an s-diagonal of
A, and say an s-diagonal is positive if all the elements of the diagonal are positive. If
no s-diagonal exists then the s-permanent is an empty sum and hence equal to zero.
Throughout this paper we are primarily interested in the 1-permanent and 1-diagonals.
However, we acknowledge that all of the questions that we investigate have analogues
for s-permanents that would be worth pursuing. Unless stated otherwise, by a diagonal
we will mean a 1-diagonal and by permanent we will mean the 1-permanent.
Let Λs (d, n) be the set of (0, 1)-matrices in M(d, n) with precisely one 1 in each s-plane.
A matrix in Λs (d, n) will be called an s-permutation matrix. Note that by placing ones
on a diagonal and zeros elsewhere we get a (d − 1)-permutation matrix. A non-negative
A ∈ M(d, n) is called s-polystochastic if the sum of the elements within every s-plane
is equal to 1. Denote by Ωs (d, n) the set of s-polystochastic d-dimensional matrices of
order n. The set Ωs (d, n) is bounded and defined by finitely many linear inequalities,
hence it forms a convex polytope and is given by the convex hull of its vertex set.
A Latin hypercube of dimension d and order n is a d-dimensional matrix of order n
with the property that every line contains precisely one of each element in In . For
each d-dimensional 1-permutation matrix P of order n there is a corresponding Latin
hypercube H of dimension d − 1 and order n, and vice versa. The correspondence is
that P (i1 , . . . , id ) = 1 if and only if H(i1 , . . . , id−1 ) = id . We will write P = P(H) and
           MULTIDIMENSIONAL PERMANENTS OF POLYSTOCHASTIC MATRICES                          3

H = H(P ) = P −1 (P ). Furthermore, we see that a positive diagonal in P corresponds
to a transversal in H, that is, a selection of n elements, no two in the same hyperplane
or sharing the same symbol. Hence per1 (P ) counts transversals in H. On several
occasions we will also use a concept we call a mixed transversal of a set {H1 , . . . , Hk }
of Latin hypercubes of order n. By this we mean a selection of n elements {α1 , . . . , αn }
with each αi ∈ Hj for some j that may depend on i, and such that any two elements
differ in every coordinate and contain different symbols.
There is a natural action of the wreath product Sn ≀ Sd on M(d, n), where Sd permutes
the coordinates and each copy of Sn permutes the values within one coordinate. The
same action works on Λs (d, n) and Ωs (d, n). Given the above correspondence with
Λ1 (d, n), this then induces an action of Sn ≀ Sd on Latin hypercubes of order n and
dimension d − 1. A species of Latin hypercubes or polystochastic matrices is an orbit
under these actions.

Dow and Gibson [4] were the first to seriously consider permanents of higher dimen-
sional matrices. They showed that there are (d − 1)-polystochastic matrices for every
dimension d ⩾ 3 and order n ⩾ 2 (with n 6= 3) which have vanishing permanent (their
paper does not exclude the n = 3 case, but their construction fails in that case). Re-
alising this meant that there could be no higher dimensional analogue of Birkhoff’s
theorem for (d − 1)-polystochastic matrices, they instead made the following conjecture
about the permanent on the convex hull of the (d − 1)-permutation matrices.

Conjecture 1.3. If A ∈ Hull(Λd−1 (d, n)), then per1 (A) ⩾ (n!/nn )d−1 , with equality if
and only if A = n1−d Jnd .


Here Jnd is the d-dimensional matrix of order n with all elements equal to 1. They
showed that the conjecture holds for order 2.
Another approach to generalising van der Waerden’s conjecture would be to look at the
1-polystochastic matrices in dimensions and orders where the minimum permanent is
not known to be zero, possibly leading to:

Conjecture 1.4. If A ∈ Ω1 (d, n) where d is even or n is odd, then per1 (A) ⩾
(n!)d−1 /nn , with equality if and only if A = n−1 Jnd .

A counterexample to Conjecture 1.3 might not be 1-polystochastic and hence might
not yield a counterexample to Conjecture 1.4. Conversely, a counterexample to Con-
jecture 1.4 might not be decomposable into a sum of positive diagonals and hence might
not yield a counterexample to Conjecture 1.3. Therefore it is not clear whether either
of these conjectures implies the other for any given values of d and n.
Taranenko [10] proved the following result, which shows that Conjecture 1.4 fails for
odd dimensions.
Theorem 1.5. The matrix n−1 Jnd is a local extrema of the permanent amongst Ω1 (d, n).
If d is even, n−1 Jnd is a local minimum, and if d is odd, n−1 Jnd is a local maximum.

In a subsequent paper [11] Taranenko offered this conjecture about where other local
extrema can occur.
Conjecture 1.6. All local extrema of the permanent on the polytope Ω1 (d, n) are located
at the vertices or centres of its faces.

In the same paper she showed that Conjecture 1.4 fails asymptotically:
Theorem 1.7. Suppose that the order n is odd or the dimension d ⩾ 3 is even. Then
there exists a 1-polystochastic d-dimensional matrix A of order n whose permanent is
asymptotically less than that of the uniform matrix:

She also showed that Conjecture 1.4 fails for n = 3 and d ⩾ 3. In §3, we use Theorem 1.5

As mentioned already, Dow and Gibson found (d − 1)-polystochastic matrices with
vanishing permanent. There are also known examples of 1-polystochastic matrices that
have vanishing permanent, which we generalise in Theorem 2.3. As a corollary we
provide counterexamples to Conjecture 1.6. However, for even dimension or odd order,
there are no known examples of 1-polystochastic matrices with vanishing permanent,
and Taranenko [11] has conjectured that the permanent is always positive.
Conjecture 1.8. All 1-polystochastic matrices of even dimension or odd order have
positive permanent.

In support of this conjecture, Taranenko proved the order 3 case. More recently Tara-
nenko [12] showed that Conjecture 1.8 is true in dimension 4 and order 4. Since dimen-
sion 2 is the classic result that doubly stochastic permutation matrices have positive
permanent, this was one of the smallest of the unresolved cases. Taranenko [11] surveys
some conditions on the non-zero elements of a matrix to guarantee the positivity of the
permanent.
While there are known examples of 1-polystochastic matrices of odd order that cannot
be decomposed into a sum of diagonals, there are no such known examples for even

dimensional 1-polystochastic matrices. Taranenko [11] has conjectured that a decom-
position is always possible in even dimensions.
Conjecture 1.9. All 1-polystochastic matrices of even dimension can be represented
as a non-negative linear combination of (d − 1)-permutation matrices.

While 1-polystochastic matrices may not decompose into diagonals, it is still true that
Ω1 (d, n) is the convex hull of its vertex set. The 1-permutation matrices are vertices of
this polytope, but in general there are others. There is a simple characterisation of the
vertices in terms of their supports first given by Jurkat and Ryser [7], though stated in
very different terminology than we use here.

Theorem 1.10. A matrix A ∈ Ω1 (d, n) is a vertex if and only if A has minimal support,
that is, if supp(A) ⊇ supp(B) for B ∈ Ω1 (d, n), then A = B.

Ke, Li and Xiao [8] give a formulation of this condition into a system of linear equations
in the elements of A for which a unique solution exists if and only if A is a vertex.
Using this formulation, they compute the entire set of vertices for Ω1 (3, 3) and Ω1 (3, 4).
Finally, Linial and Luria [9] give a lower bound on the number of vertices of Ω1 (3, n)
as n → ∞.
The structure of this paper is as follows. In §2 we consider the zeros of the permanent on
s-polystochastic matrices. We show that whenever the order is even and the dimension
is odd there is a large set of vertices such that the permanent of any linear combination
of these vertices is zero. As a consequence we deduce that Conjecture 1.6 fails. In §3
we show that Conjecture 1.3 fails for odd dimensions.


                           2. Properties of the zero set

In this section we consider Zeros (d, n) = {A ∈ Ωs (d, n) : per1 (A) = 0}, the zero set of
the permanent amongst s-polystochastic matrices of order n and dimension d. Since
Zeros (d, n) is the zero set of a multivariate polynomial on M(d, n) and is contained in-
side the bounded set Ωs (d, n), it is compact. We can also make the following observation
about its structure.
Lemma 2.1. Let s, d, and n be positive integers, then Zeros (d, n) is the union of finitely
many polytopes.

Proof. Whether or not the permanent is zero on a nonnegative matrix depends only on
the support. For any set V of vertices of Ωs (d, n) there is a polytope PV = Hull(V ).
We claim that either PV ⊆ Zeros (d, n) or the interior of PV is disjoint from Zeros (d, n).
This is because Zeros (d, n) is compact and all points in the interior of PV have the same
support. The boundary of a polytope is itself a union of polytopes. There are only
finitely many options for V , and Zeros (d, n) is the union of PV over all choices of V for
which PV ⊆ Zeros (d, n).                                                                 

It is important to note that the polytopes in Lemma 2.1 are not necessarily disjoint. A
concrete example will be given after Theorem 2.3.
We say that the dimension of Zeros (d, n) is the maximum of the dimensions of the
polytopes PV contained in Zeros (d, n). One of our goals for this section is to find a
lower bound on the dimension of Zero1 (d, n) for odd d and even n (when we know that
the permanent can be zero).
We also note that the permanent is a multi-linear function. It is immediate that
Ωs (d, n) \ Zeros (d, n) is convex, since all positive linear combinations of matrices with
positive permanent have positive permanent. A convex polytope with vertices P1 , . . . , Pm
is necessarily closed, having elements a1 P1 + · · ·+ am Pm given by non-negative solutions
to the linear equation a1 + · · · + am = 1. It follows that, if Zeros (d, n) is non-empty,
then Ωs (d, n) \ Zeros (d, n) is not a convex polytope, given that Zeros (d, n) is closed and
Ωs (d, n) is convex and hence connected. Hence, Ωs (d, n) \ Zeros (d, n) is convex but is
not a polytope when Zeros (d, n) is non-empty.

there are 1-permutation matrices with zero permanent. We now give a large family of
such matrices inspired by the result in [3] on Latin squares with no transversals.
