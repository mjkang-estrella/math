23:                                                        The k-coprime graph of order n is the graph with vertex set {k, k + 1, . . . , k + n − 1} in
24:                                                     which two vertices are adjacent if and only if they are coprime. We characterize Hamiltonian
25:                                                     k-coprime graphs. As a particular case, two conjectures by Tout, Dabboucy, Howalla (1982)
26:                                                     and by Schroeder (2019) on prime labeling of 2-regular graphs follow. A prime labeling of a
27:                                                     graph with n vertices is a labeling of its vertices with distinct integers from {1, 2, . . . , n} in
28:                                                     such a way that the labels of any two adjacent vertices are relatively prime.

36:                                           1        Introduction
37: 
38:                                           Let G be a simple graph with n vertices. A prime labeling of G is a labeling of its vertices with
55: known results on this subject see [16].
56:     The notion of prime labeling originated with Entringer and was introduced in [21]. Entringer
57: around 1980 conjectured that all trees are prime. Haxell, Pikhurko, and Taraz [15] proved that
58: there is an integer n0 such all trees with at least n0 vertices are prime. Besides that, several
59: classes of graphs have been shown to be prime, see [13] for more details.
60:     The prime labeling of r-regular graphs have been studied so far for r ≤ 3. For r = 3, i.e., for
61: cubic graphs, Schroeder [19] confirmed a conjecture of Schluchter and Wilson [18] and classified
62: prime cubic graphs: a cubic graph G is prime if and only if G is bipartite and G 6= K3,3 . This
63: result, in particular provides an additional proof that the ladder graph (the Cartesian product
64: Pn × P2 ) is prime for all n ≥ 1, as conjectured by Varkey (see [13, 22]) and first proved by
65: Ghorbani and Kamali [14]. Classification of prime 2-regular graphs has remaind open to date.
66: A 2-regular graph G must be a disjoint union of cycles: G = Cn1 ∪ · · · ∪ Cnm , where each ni is
67: at least 3. The following conjecture was first given by Tout, Dabboucy, and Howalla:
68: 
69: Conjecture 1 ([21]). Let G = Cn1 ∪ · · · ∪ Cnm be a 2-regular graph. Then G is prime if and
70: only if at most one ni is odd.
71: 
72:    Note that if a graph G with n vertices is prime, the vertices with even labels form an
73: independent set. Thus its independence number satisfies α(G) ≥ ⌊n/2⌋. For cycles, we have
74: α(Cℓ ) = ⌊ℓ/2⌋. Consequently, if in G = Cn1 ∪ · · · ∪ Cnm more than one ni is odd, then
75: α(G) < ⌊n/2⌋. Hence, the necessity of the condition that “at most one ni is odd” is obvious.
76: Some partial cases of Conjecture 1 have been settled in the literature which are reported below:
77: 
78:    • m ≤ 4 ([5]);
79: 
80:    • (i) m ≤ 7 provided that all n1 , . . . , nm are even; (ii) for arbitrary m when n1 = · · · = nm
81:      is a sufficiently large even integer ([4]);
82: 
83:    • (i) if each ni is even; (ii) if nm is odd and gcd(nm − 1, n) = 1; (iii) if nm is odd and nm can
84:      be written as 2r + ps , for some r ≥ 1 and odd prime p which is relatively prime to 2r − 1
85:      ([19]).
86: 
87:      Let n1 , . . . , nm−1 be even and nm be odd. By the above result of [19], Cn1 ∪ · · · ∪ Cnm−1 is
88: prime. So to prove Conjecture 1 it suffices to show that Cnm has a prime labeling with labels
89: k, . . . , k +nm −1 with k = n1 +· · ·+nm−1 +1. Note that here both k and nm are odd. Therefore,
90: as observed in [19], Conjecture 1 follows from the following conjecture:
91: 
92: Conjecture 2 ([19]). If n, k are odd integers and n ≥ 3, then Cn has a prime labeling using the
93: labels k, k + 1, . . . , k + n − 1.

99:     Motivated by Conjecture 2, we define the k-coprime graph of order n, denoted CPG(k, n)
100: as the graph with vertex set {k, k + 1, . . . , k + n − 1} in which two vertices a, b are adjacent if
101: and only if they are coprime, i.e. gcd(a, b) = 1. Here n can be any positive integer and k any
102: integer. If 0 happen to be a vertex of our graph, it has at most two neighbors, namely −1 and
103: 1, because for every nonzero integer a, gcd(a, 0) = |a|.
104:      As the main result of this paper, we characterize Hamiltonian k-coprime graphs as follows.
105: 
106: Theorem 3. Let k and n ≥ 3 be integers. Then CPG(k, n) is Hamiltonian if and only if either
107: 
108:     (i) both n and k are odd, or
109: 
110:  (ii) n is even and each of k and k + n − 1 is not divisible by some odd prime less than n.
111: 
112:      Obviously, from Part (i) of Theorem 3, Conjecture 2 and consequently Conjecture 1 follow.

39:                                             1     Introduction
40: 
41:                                             Let G be a connected, simple and finite graph. Consider any edge-coloring of G. A path in G is said to
42:                                             be rainbow if no two edges of it are colored the same. The graph G is rainbow-connected if there is a
43:                                             rainbow path between each pair of its vertices. If there is a rainbow shortest path between every pair of
44:                                             its vertices, we say that G is strongly rainbow-connected. The minimum number of colors required
45:                                             to rainbow-connect G is known as the rainbow connection number of G, and denoted as rc(G).
46:                                             Similarly, the minimum number of colors needed to strongly rainbow-connect G is the strong rainbow
47:                                             connection number of G, denoted as src(G). These measures of rainbow connectivity were introduced
48:                                             by Chartrand et al. [5] in 2008. The concept has gathered significant attention from both combinatorial
49:                                             and algorithmic perspectives. Indeed, the work of Chartrand et al. [5] has already amassed more than
91: parameters, that possibly arise from “tree-related” and “dominating” graph structures. Intuitively, a
92: graph structure that has both characteristics is a maximum induced forest of a graph. Hence, the
93: question arises whether one can bound rc(G) in terms of its forest number f(G), the number of
94: vertices in the largest induced forest in the graph. We answer this in the affirmative by proving the
95: following theorem.
96: Theorem 1. A connected graph G with forest number f(G) has rc(G) ≤ f(G) + 2.
97: Observe that the bound is tight up to an additive factor of 3 due to trees that have rc(G) = n − 1 =
98: f(G) − 1. Our bound improves the upper bound of n − 1 obtained by coloring the edges of a spanning
99: tree in distinct colors, except when f(G) ≥ n − 2. We leave as an open problem the question of whether
100: the stronger upper bound of f(G) − 1 is true.
101:    One might be tempted to conjecture a strengthening of our bound, namely that rc(G) is at most
102: t(G), the number of vertices in the largest induced tree in the graph. However, this turns out to be not
103: true. To see this, one can consider a graph G obtained by taking a Kk for any k ≥ 3 with a pendant
104: vertex attached to each of its vertices. Then, we have that rc(G) = k whereas t(G) = 4.
105:    Finally, we note that the complement of an induced forest is a feedback vertex set. The feedback vertex
106: set number is the size of the smallest set of vertices in a graph whose removal leaves an induced forest.
107: Hence, Theorem 1 directly implies the following.
108: Corollary 1. A connected graph G with feedback vertex set number fvs(G) has rc(G) ≤ |V (G)|−fvs(G)+
109: 2.

1807: 3    Conclusions
1808: 
1809: We gave an upper bound of f(G) + 2 on rc(G), strengthening the intuition that tree-like and dominating
1810: structures are helpful in rainbow-connecting graphs. Our bound is tight up to an additive factor of 3 as
1811: shown by any tree. The question remains whether the bound can be improved to f (G) − 1 so that the
1812: bound is tight even with respect to additive factors. Also, then the bound would be a strict improvement
1813: over the bound n−1 obtained by coloring the edges of a spanning tree in distinct colors. From our insight
1814: developed during the current work, we conjecture such a slightly stronger bound.
1815: 
1816: Conjecture 1. A connected graph G has rc(G) ≤ f(G) − 1.
1817: 
1818: We expect that proving this conjecture requires further extensive case analysis. Further, we note that
1819: Lauri [12] proposed the following stronger version of the above conjecture, discovered using the auto-
1820: mated conjecture-making software GraPHedron [16].
1821: 
1822: Conjecture 2 ([12]). A connected graph G has src(G) ≤ f(G) − 1.
