                                           1     Introduction

                                           Given a graph G = (V, E), a feedback vertex set is a set S ⊆ V such that the subgraph induced by
                                           V \ S is acyclic. If G is directed, this means that the induced subdigraph contains no directed cycle.
                                           The set V \ S is then called an acyclic set. Computing the minimum size of a feedback vertex set,
                                           denoted by f vs(G), is a hard problem even for planar graphs (see [7, 9]) and has been extensively
                                           studied in the literature.

                                           In this paper, we focus on planar graphs. By the Four Color Theorem, it is known that every planar
                                           graph on n vertices contains an independent set of vertices of size n4 . A widely open conjecture
                                           proposes a strengthening of this result:

                                           Conjecture 1 (Albertson and Berman, 1976 [2]). Every simple undirected planar graph G on n
                                           vertices contains an induced forest of size at least n2 .

                                           Equivalently, Conjecture 1 states that every undirected planar graph G on n vertices has a feedback
                                           vertex set of size at most n2 . The best known general bound for Conjecture 1 is due to Borodin [4],
                                           who proved that every undirected planar graph admits an induced forest of size at least 2n 5 , in other
                                           words f vs(G) ≤ 3n 5 . Better upper bounds  for f vs are known depending on the girth (the length of a
                                           smallest cycle) and we summarize them in the second column of Table 1. The third column of this
                                           table shows the conjectured upper bounds, which are tight if true.

We switch to directed graphs (or digraphs) with no parallel arcs and no loops. The length of a
shortest directed cycle of a digraph D is called the digirth of D. An oriented graph is a digraph
with digirth at least 3 (that is, has no digons). As forests are the cycle-free structures in undirected
graphs, acyclic sets are their natural analogs in digraphs.


1.1    Upper bounds

Albertson posed the following weakening of Conjecture 1 (see [11, 17, 21]): does every planar oriented
graph contain an acyclic set of size at least n2 ? In other words, is it true that f vs(D) ≤ n2 for every
planar oriented graph D on n vertices? The best known general result for Albertson’s question is
the same as for Conjecture 1 and is due to Borodin [4]: f vs(D) ≤ 3n     5 . Moreover, the question is a
weakening of the following famous conjecture.
Conjecture 2 (Neumann-Lara, 1985 [18]). Every planar oriented graph can be vertex-partitioned
into two acyclic sets.

While Conjecture 2 remains open in general, it was established by Li and Mohar [14] for planar
oriented graphs without directed triangles. Therefore, f vs(D) ≤ n2 for every planar oriented graph
D of digirth at least 4. Better upper bounds for f vs of planar oriented graphs with a larger digirth
have been obtained by Esperet, Lemoine and Maffray [6]. We summarize the known upper bounds
for f vs in Theorem 3 below.

Theorem 3 (Borodin, 1979 [4], Esperet, Lemoine and Maffray, 2017 [6], Li and Mohar, 2017 [14]).
Let D be an oriented planar graph of digirth g on n vertices. Then

   • f vs(D) ≤ 3n
                5 [4],

   • f vs(D) ≤ n2 [14] if g ≥ 4,

   • f vs(D) ≤ 2n−5
                 4  [6] if g ≥ 5, and
   • f vs(D) ≤ 2n−6
                 g  [6] if g ≥ 6.

Moreover, the authors of [6] suggest that the factor 2 for g ≥ 6 should be reducible to a constant
closer to 1.

A feedback arc set of a digraph D is a set of arcs A such that D − A is acyclic and we denote by
f as(D) the minimum size of a feedback arc set of D. A celebrated result of Lucchesi and Younger [16]
combined with planar duality relates minimal feedback arc sets to maximum families of arc-disjoint
directed cycles.

Theorem 4 (Lucchesi and Younger, 1978 [16]). Let D be a directed planar graph. Let Cmax be a
maximum set of arc-disjoint directed cycles of D. Then f as(D) = |Cmax |.

Our contributions

Our first main contribution in this paper (in Section 2) is to establish a Lucchesi-Younger type
theorem for planar digraphs. Given an embedded planar digraph D (a plane digraph), an arc-disjoint
set of directed cycles N of D is said to be normal if every vertex v of D is alternatingly oriented
with respect to N , that is, the arcs of N incident to v alternate between incoming and outgoing arcs
in the cyclic order around v (see Figure 1 for an illustration).

respectively. Each vertex is alternatingly oriented, see the zoomed-in view of the green vertices.

Theorem 5. Let D be a directed plane graph. Let Nmax be a maximum normal set of cycles of D.
Then f vs(D) ≤ |Nmax |.

Definitions and notations

Given a digraph G, we denote by V (G) and E(G) the sets of vertices and arcs of G. We will always
work on plane graphs, that is, planar graphs with a fixed embedding. For a plane graph G, we denote
by F (G) the set of faces of G in its planar embedding. We denote by nG , mG and fG (or simply n, m
and f when there is no ambiguity) the numbers of vertices, arcs and faces of G.

For a vertex v ∈ V (G) we denote by d−               +
                                       G (v) (resp. dG (v)) the number of incoming (resp. outgoing) arcs
of v in G and we call it the indegree (resp. outdegree) of v. The degree of v is dG (v) = d−        +
                                                                                           G (v) + dG (v).

We denote by ℓF the length of a face F ∈ F (G), that is, the number of arcs on the boundary of F
(counting multiple occurrences of an arc if needed).

For a cycle C of G the notation |C| denotes the length of C which is the number of arcs of C.

A plane sub(di)graph H of a plane (di)graph G is obtained by removing some arcs and vertices of G
without changing the embedding.

Given a set of arc-disjoint cycles C of an plane digraph G, the graph G[C] is the subgraph of G
induced by the arcs of the cycles of C where all isolated vertices (vertices not incident to any arc
of C) are removed. We denote by nC , mC , fC and cC the number of vertices, of arcs, of faces and of
connected components of G[C] respectively. We denote by V (C), E(C) and F (C) the sets of vertices,
of arcs and of faces of G[C].

In order to capture the local structure of arcs around each vertex v, we will use the term half-arcs of
v to refer to the portion of an arc incident to v, considered from v’s perspective. For instance, if v
has a loop, then v has two half-arcs induced by this arc: one outgoing and one incoming. A vertex
v ∈ V (G) is said to be alternatingly oriented if every pair of two consecutive half-arcs around v in
the cyclic order have different orientations with respect to v.

A set N of arc-disjoint directed cycles of G is called normal if G[N ] has only alternatingly oriented
vertices. We give some examples in Figure 1. We denote Nmax (G) a maximum normal set of G.



2     Upper bounds: normal sets of cycles

2.1    Outline of the section

Denote by Cmax (G) a maximum set of arc-disjoint cycles of a directed plane graph G. Since every
normal set is a set of arc-disjoint cycles we have |Nmax (G)| ≤ |Cmax (G)|. Inspired by the ideas from
the proof of Lucchesi and Younger [16], we establish an analogous inequality between f vs(G) and
|Nmax (G)| in the next theorem.
Theorem 5. Let G be a directed plane graph. Then f vs(G) ≤ |Nmax (G)|.

To bound f vs(G) from above, it therefore suffices to bound the size of Nmax (G), which we do in the
following proposition.
Proposition 10. Let G be an oriented plane graph on n vertices of digirth g. Then |Nmax (G)| ≤ n−2

2.3      Strongly connected digraphs: proof of Theorem 7

Proposition 15. Let G be a strongly connected plane digraph. Let F be a connected set of faces
of G such that the boundary of the union of F is a directed cycle C. Then some face in F has a
directed facial boundary.

Proof. Denote by G∗ the dual of G, that is the dual graph of the underlying undirected graph of G
where each arc is oriented by a quarter-turn clockwise from its corresponding primal arc.

Since G is strongly connected, G∗ is acyclic. Since F is a connected set of faces of G, F ∗ is a set of
vertices of G∗ such that the subgraph G∗ [F ∗ ] is connected. Moreover since the boundary of F is a
directed cycle, all the arcs of G∗ that have an endpoint in F ∗ and an endpoint outside of F ∗ are
either all ingoing or all outgoing. Without loss of generality, suppose that they are all ingoing. Since
G∗ [F ∗ ] is a connected acyclic graph, it has a sink F ∗ ∈ F ∗ and from the previous remark, F ∗ is a
sink in G∗ . By duality, F ∗ corresponds to a face F ∈ F whose boundary is a directed cycle.
Lemma 16. Let G be a strongly connected plane digraph. Let N be a normal set of cycles of G.
Then G has at least fN − (cN − 1) faces whose boundary is a directed cycle.

Proof. Let y be the number of faces of G[N ] whose boundary is a directed cycle. We show by
induction on cN that y ≥ fN − (cN − 1).

If G[N ] is connected then since N is a normal set of cycles, every face is bounded by a directed cycle
and the formula holds.

Suppose that G[N ] has cN ≥ 2 connected components. Let G1 be one of the connected components
of G[N ] and set G2 = G[N ] − G1 . Let N1 and N2 be the partition of N such that G1 = G[N1 ]
and G2 = G[N2 ]. We have cN1 = 1 and cN2 = cN − 1. Let y1 and y2 be respectively the number
of faces of G1 and G2 whose boundary is a directed cycle. By induction, y1 ≥ fN1 − (cN1 − 1) and
y2 ≥ fN2 − (cN2 − 1). Now note that y ≥ y1 + y2 − 2. Indeed, let F1 (resp. F2 ) be the face of G1
(resp. G2 ) that contains the other component; these two faces merge into a single face F in G[N ].
Since cN1 = 1, every face of G1 has a directed boundary (base case), so in particular F1 is counted
in y1 . In the worst case F2 is also counted in y2 but the merged face F is not directed, giving

2.6    Valuations of cycles: proof of Theorem 5

In this section, we prove Theorem 5, which states that for every plane digraph G, f vs(G) ≤ |Nmax (G)|.

normal set of G. That is, in every normal set N of G of maximal cardinality |N | = |Nmax (G)|, there

the following lemma, which we prove later in Section 2.6.2.
Lemma 22. Every directed cycle of G contains at least one essential vertex.

Definition 32 (Coating and Skeleton). Let G be a plane undirected graph.

   • A coating of G is a plane digraph H constructed from G as follows. To each vertex v of G we
     associate a directed cycle Cv oriented clockwise. This cycle is said to be associated to vertex
     v of G. For every edge uv in G, the two cycles Cu and Cv of H are connected by merging a
     vertex su of degree 2 of Cu with a vertex sv of degree 2 of Cv into a vertex suv and by adding
     two extra arcs between Cu and Cv : one from the in-neighbor of su to the out-neighbor of sv
     and one from the in-neighbor of sv to the out-neighbor of su (see Figure 11). The vertex suv is
     called link vertex associated to the edge uv and the two arcs added on both sides of the link
     vertex are called link arcs associated to the edge uv. In case of a loop at u in G, the two vertices
     of degree 2 to be merged are taken in the same cycle Cu and are at distance at least 3 so that
     the link arcs are never loops.

3.3    Coating functions

In this section, we introduce the notion of coating functions, which provide an alternative way
to represent a coating of a skeleton graph and are useful for constructing coatings with specific
properties.
Definition 42 (Corner). Let G be a plane graph. Let v be a vertex of G and let e1 , e2 be two
half-edges incident to v that are consecutive in the clockwise order around v. The triple c = (e1 , v, e2 )
is called a corner of G. If v is an isolated vertex, we define the corner as the triple (∅, v, ∅). If v has
degree 1 with incident half-edge e, we define its single corner as (e, v, e). Also, for consistency, if v is
                                                                                        int             ext
a vertex whose only incident edge e is a loop, we define its two corners as (e, v, e) and (e, v, e) .




                                                    24
The set of all the corners of G is denoted by K. Note that each corner c is incident to a unique face
f ∈ F (G) and to a unique vertex v ∈ V (G). For a vertex v ∈ V (G) (resp. a face f ∈ F (G)) denote
by K(v) (resp. K(f )) the sets of corners incident to v (resp. incident to f ).

Let H be a coating of some skeleton graph G. Note that every vertex of H is either a link vertex or
a vertex that lies in a corner of G. Given a directed cycle D of H, we denote by K(D) the set of
corners where the vertices of D lie.
Definition 43 (Coating Function). Given a skeleton plane graph G, a coating function of G is a
function h : K → N∗ where h(c) denotes the number of vertices of the coating lying strictly within
corner c, excluding link vertices. This establishes a bijection between coatings of G and coating
functions of G.

Definition 49. Let ℓ be a positive integer. A family of coatings (Hk )k≥0 of skeletons (Gk )k≥0 is

Lemma 50. Let (Hk )k≥0 be a recursive family of coatings of skeletons (Gk )k≥0 with H0 and H1
having digirth g. Suppose moreover that for every pair u, v of link vertices of H0 , dH1 (u, v) ≥ dH0 (u, v).
Then Hk has digirth g for all k ≥ 0.

Proof. We first establish that for any pair (u, v) of link vertices on the outer face of Gk , dHk+1 (u, v) ≥
dHk (u, v). For k = 0 this is the hypothesis. For k ≥ 1, building Hk+1 from Hk by attaching a new
copy of the G1 -coating is structurally identical to building H1 from H0 , so the same hypothesis
applies.

We now prove by induction on k that Hk has digirth g. The base cases k = 0 and k = 1 are the
hypothesis. Assume Hk has digirth g, and let D be a directed cycle of Hk+1 . Let K(D) be the set of
corners of Gk+1 visited by D.

    • If K(D) ⊆ K(Gk ), then D is a cycle of Hk , so |D| ≥ g by the induction hypothesis.
    • If K(D) ⊆ K(Gk+1 ) \ K(Gk ), then D lies entirely in the new copy of G1 , so |D| ≥ g since H1
      has digirth g.
    • Otherwise, D visits corners from both parts. Since the Hk -part and the new G1 -copy share
      only the link vertices on the outer face of Gk , the cycle D must pass through at least two such
      link vertices v1 and vp . Let P = v1 . . . vp be the portion of D traversing the Hk -part (with all
      intermediate vertices in interior corners of Gk ). Closing P with a shortest path P ′ from vp to
      v1 in Hk yields a cycle D′ in Hk . By the induction hypothesis |D′ | = p + dHk (vp , v1 ) ≥ g, and
      since dHk+1 (vp , v1 ) ≥ dHk (vp , v1 ) we conclude |D| ≥ p + dHk+1 (vp , v1 ) ≥ |D′ | ≥ g.
