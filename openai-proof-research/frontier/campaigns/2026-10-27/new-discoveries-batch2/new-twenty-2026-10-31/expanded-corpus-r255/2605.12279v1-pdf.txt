                                           1     Introduction

                                           Given a (directed) graph G = (V, E), a feedback vertex set is a set S ⊆ V of vertices of G such that
                                           the sub(di)graph induced by V \ S contains no (directed) cycles. In this case, we also say that V \ S
                                           is an acyclic set. Computing the minimum size of a feedback vertex set, denoted by f vs(G), is a hard
                                           problem even for planar graphs (see [7, 9]) and has been extensively studied in the literature.

                                           In this paper, we focus on planar graphs. By the Four Color Theorem, it is known that every planar
                                           graph on n vertices contains an independent set of vertices of size n4 . A widely open conjecture
                                           proposes a strengthening of this result:
                                           Conjecture 1 (Albertson and Berman, 1976 [2]). Every simple undirected planar graph G on n
                                           vertices has f vs(G) ≤ n2 .

                                           Equivalently, Conjecture 1 states that every undirected planar graph G on n vertices has an induced
                                           forest of at least n2 vertices. The best known general upper bound for Conjecture 1 is due to
                                           Borodin [4], who proved that f vs(G) ≤ 3n 5 . Better upper bounds are known depending on the girth
                                           (the length of a smallest cycle) and we summarize them in the second column of Table 1. The third
                                           column of this table shows the conjectured upper bounds, which are tight if true.

Albertson posed the following weakening of Conjecture 1 (see [11,17,21]): does every planar digraph of
digirth at least 3 contain an acyclic set of size at least n2 ? In other words, is it true that f vs3 (n) ≤ n2
or equivalently that τ3 ≤ 21 ? Note that the best known general result for Albertson’s question is the
same as for Conjecture 1 and is due to Borodin [4]. Moreover, the question is a weakening of the
following famous conjecture.
Conjecture 2 (Neumann-Lara, 1985 [18]). Every planar digraph of digirth at least 3 can be
vertex-partitioned into two acyclic sets.

While Conjecture 2 remains open in general, it was established by Li and Mohar [14] for planar
digraphs of digirth at least 4. Therefore, f vs4 (n) ≤ n2 and thus τ4 ≤ 12 . Similar to the undirected
case, better upper bounds for f vsg (n) are known when the digirth g is large. Esperet, Lemoine and
Maffray [6] showed that f vs5 (n) ≤ 2n−5

A feedback arc set of a directed graph D is a set of arcs A such that D − A is a directed acyclic graph
and we denote by f as(D) the minimum size of a feedback arc set of D. Note that the inequality
f vs(D) ≤ mg , which is the directed analogue of the conjecture of Dross, Montassier and the second
author [5] for planar undirected graphs, holds for planar digraphs of digirth g as a consequence of
the Lucchesi–Younger theorem [16]. Indeed, given a planar digraph D, by Lucchesi and Younger’s
theorem combined with planar duality, f as(D) equals the maximum number of arc-disjoint directed
cycles in D. Therefore, since each directed cycle of D has length at least g, clearly g × f as(D) ≤ m
and thus f as(D) ≤ m  g . Taking one endpoint from each arc in a minimum feedback arc set yields a
feedback vertex set of size at most f as(D), hence f vs(D) ≤ f as(D) ≤ m  g . However, the inequality
m     n−2
 g ≤ g−2 does not hold for every planar digraph D of digirth g, as D might contain faces of length
smaller than g (see for instance the digraphs of Figure 1).

Definitions and notations

Given a directed graph G, we denote by V (G) and E(G) the sets of vertices and arcs of G. We will
always work on plane graphs, that is, planar graphs with a fixed embedding. For a plane graph G,
we denote by F (G) the set of faces of G in its planar embedding. We denote by nG , mG and fG (or
simply n, m and f when there is no ambiguity) the numbers of vertices, arcs and faces of G.

For a vertex v ∈ V (G) we denote by d−               +
                                       G (v) (resp. dG (v)) the number of incoming (resp. outgoing) arcs
of v in G and we call it the indegree (resp. outdegree) of v. The degree of v is dG (v) = d−        +
                                                                                           G (v) + dG (v).

We denote by ℓF the length of a face F ∈ F (G), that is, the number of arcs on the boundary of F
(counting multiple occurrences of an arc if needed).

For a cycle C of G the notation |C| denotes the length of C which is the number of arcs of C.

A plane sub(di)graph H of a plane (di)graph G is obtained by removing some arcs and vertices of G
without changing the embedding.

Given a set of arc-disjoint cycles C of a directed plane graph G, the graph G[C] is the subgraph of G
induced by the arcs of the cycles of C where all isolated vertices (vertices not incident to any arc
of C) are removed. We denote by nC , mC , fC and cC the number of vertices, of arcs, of faces and of
connected components of G[C] respectively. We denote by V (C), E(C) and F (C) the sets of vertices,
of arcs and of faces of G[C].

In order to capture the local structure of arcs around each vertex v, we will use the term half-arcs of
v to refer to the portion of an arc incident to v, considered from v’s perspective. For instance, if v
has a loop, then v has two half-arcs induced by this arc - one outgoing and one incoming. A vertex
v ∈ V (G) is said to be alternatingly oriented if every pair of two consecutive half-arcs around v in
the cyclic order have different orientations with respect to v.

A set N of arc-disjoint directed cycles of G is called normal if G[N ] has only alternatingly oriented

Definition 26 (Coating and Skeleton). Let G be a plane undirected graph.

   • A coating of G is a plane digraph H constructed from G as follows. To each vertex v of G we
     associate a directed cycle Cv oriented clockwise. This cycle is said to be associated to vertex
     v of G. For every edge uv in G, the two cycles Cu and Cv of H are connected by merging a
     vertex su of degree 2 of Cu with a vertex sv of degree 2 of Cv into a vertex suv and by adding
     two extra arcs between Cu and Cv : one from the in-neighbor of su to the out-neighbor of sv
     and one from the in-neighbor of sv to the out-neighbor of su (see Figure 11). The vertex suv is
     called link vertex associated to the edge uv and the two arcs added on both sides of the link
     vertex are called link arcs associated to the edge uv. In case of a loop at u in G, the two vertices
     of degree 2 to be merged are taken in the same cycle Cu and are at distance at least 3 so that
     the link arcs are never loops.

       Figure 11: Building a coating H (in blue and green) of a skeleton graph G (in red).


   • If H is a coating of G, we say that G is the skeleton of H and by the previous item of the
     definition it is uniquely defined as a plane graph.

   • If f is a face of G whose incident vertices are in the same connected component of G, the
     vertices of H that lie inside f (excluding link vertices) form a cycle Cf oriented counterclockwise
     (or clockwise if f = fext ). This cycle is said to be associated to the face f of G. In case f
     is a face incident to k connected components in G, then the vertices of H that lie inside f
     (excluding link vertices) form a union of k vertex disjoint directed cycles Cf1 , . . . , Cfk associated
                                                                                               Pk
     to the face f of G. In this case by abusing the notation we will denote |Cf | = i=1 |Cfi | the
     number of edges lying inside f . See Figure 12(left) for an example of a skeleton with four faces.
   • If every vertex of G is transformed into a directed cycle with a fixed length g ≥ 1, we say that
     H is a g-coating of G.

3.3    Coating functions

In this section, we introduce the notion of coating functions, which provide an alternative way
to represent a coating of a skeleton graph and are useful for constructing coatings with specific
properties.
Definition 36 (Corner). Let G be a plane graph. Let v be a vertex of G and let e1 , e2 be two
half-edges incident to v that are consecutive in the clockwise order around v. The triple c = (e1 , v, e2 )
is called a corner of G. If v is an isolated vertex, we define the corner as the triple (∅, v, ∅). If v has
degree 1 with incident half-edge e, we define its single corner as (e, v, e). Also, for consistency, if v is
a vertex whose only incident edge e is a loop, we define its two corners as (e, v, e)int and (e, v, e)ext .

The set of all the corners of G is denoted by K. Note that each corner c is incident to a unique face
f ∈ F (G) and to a unique vertex v ∈ V (G). For a vertex v ∈ V (G) (resp. a face f ∈ F (G)) denote
by K(v) (resp. K(f )) the sets of corners incident to v (resp. incident to f ).

Let H be a coating of some skeleton graph G. Note that every vertex of H is either a link vertex or
a vertex that lies in a corner of G. Given a directed cycle D of H, we denote by K(D) the set of
corners where the vertices of D lie.

Definition 43. Let ℓ be a positive integer. A family of coatings (Hk )k≥0 of skeletons (Gk )k≥0 is
said to be recursive if the following hold:
 (1) G0 is the undirected cycle of length ℓ. The graph G1 has two distinguished facial ℓ-cycles
     Cin = u1 . . . uℓ and Cout = v1 . . . vℓ . For k ≥ 2, Gk is obtained from Gk−1 by identifying the
     vertices of Cout of Gk−1 with those of Cin of a new copy of G1 . Equivalently, Gk is a chain of k
     copies of G1 , with Cout of each copy identified with Cin of the next.
 (2) There exists a coating function hG1 of G1 such that for every k ≥ 0 and every corner c of Gk ,
     the coating function hk of Hk satisfies hk (c) = hG1 (c′ ), where c′ is the corner of G1 in the copy
     of G1 containing c. In other words, the coating function is the same in every copy of G1 .


                                                    25
Lemma 44. Let (Hk )k≥0 be a recursive family of coatings of skeletons (Gk )k≥0 with H0 and H1
having digirth g. Suppose moreover that for every pair u, v of link vertices of H0 , dH1 (u, v) ≥ dH0 (u, v).
Then Hk has digirth g for all k ≥ 0.

Proof. We first establish that for any pair (u, v) of link vertices on the outer face of Gk , dHk+1 (u, v) ≥
dHk (u, v). For k = 0 this is the hypothesis. For k ≥ 1, building Hk+1 from Hk by attaching a new
copy of the G1 -coating is structurally identical to building H1 from H0 , so the same hypothesis
applies.

We now prove by induction on k that Hk has digirth g. The base cases k = 0 and k = 1 are the
hypothesis. Assume Hk has digirth g, and let D be a directed cycle of Hk+1 . Let K(D) be the set of
corners of Gk+1 visited by D.

    • If K(D) ⊆ K(Gk ), then D is a cycle of Hk , so |D| ≥ g by the induction hypothesis.

    • If K(D) ⊆ K(Gk+1 ) \ K(Gk ), then D lies entirely in the new copy of G1 , so |D| ≥ g since H1
      has digirth g.
    • Otherwise, D visits corners from both parts. Since the Hk -part and the new G1 -copy share
      only the link vertices on the outer face of Gk , the cycle D must pass through at least two such
      link vertices v1 and vp . Let P = v1 . . . vp be the portion of D traversing the Hk -part (with all
      intermediate vertices in interior corners of Gk ). Closing P with a shortest path P ′ from vp to
      v1 in Hk yields a cycle D′ in Hk . By the induction hypothesis |D′ | = p + dHk (vp , v1 ) ≥ g, and
      since dHk+1 (vp , v1 ) ≥ dHk (vp , v1 ) we conclude |D| ≥ p + dHk+1 (vp , v1 ) ≥ |D′ | ≥ g.
