                                                                               1. Introduction
                                               Combinatorial games of “making and avoiding” constitute a well-studied and
                                           richly varied domain within combinatorial game theory. While the majority of clas-
                                           sic positional games in this category—such as Tic-Tac-Toe, Gomoku, and Connect-
                                           4—are partisan, featuring players with distinct sets of pieces, there is profound
                                           mathematical interest in their impartial counterparts, where both players share a
                                           single reservoir of indistinguishable pieces.
                                               A paradigmatic example of such an impartial game is “Treblecross”, analyzed
                                           by Berlekamp, Conway, and Guy in Winning Ways [2]. Played on a 1 × n strip of
                                           cells, both players take turns placing an “X”, and the first player to complete three
                                           consecutive X’s wins. In the framework of combinatorial game theory, Treblecross
                                           is the octal game 0.007 (a move removes a gap of length at least three and leaves at
                                           most one cell on each side). Despite its apparent simplicity and decades of study,
                                           it remains open whether the Sprague–Grundy sequence of Treblecross is ultimately
                                           periodic.
                                               In this paper we study the avoidance version of Treblecross, which we call Inverse
                                           Treblecross (Beck [1] calls this kind of avoidance game a “reverse” game). In Inverse
                                           Treblecross, completing a line of three is prohibited: a move is legal only if it does
                                           not create three consecutive X’s. Under the normal play convention a player loses
                                           when they face a position with no legal move.
                                               It is instructive to compare Inverse Treblecross with its lower-order analogue,
                                           “Inverse Duocross” (avoiding two-in-a-row). In Inverse Duocross a move renders
                                           the played cell and its two neighbours unplayable, so a single move cleanly severs
                                           the board into independent empty segments; the game is structurally equivalent to
                                           the normal play of Dawson’s Chess, which decomposes into disjunctive sums and
                                           has a famously periodic SG sequence.

   The difficulty in analyzing Inverse Treblecross is that it resists such a decom-
position: because two X’s may be adjacent, the legality of a move in an empty
segment depends on the configuration of the pieces bounding that segment, and
an arbitrary move is not simply a nim-sum of independent subcomponents. This
forces the more nuanced state-space analysis developed in Section 4.
   Our objective is to evaluate the game from its initial (empty) state; in the
standard terminology this is a weak solution. Evaluating the exact SG value of
an arbitrary partially filled board (a strong solution) remains open. We resolve
the empty-board SG sequence completely and show that its values are confined to
{0, 1}.
Theorem 1.1. An empty board of n cells has Sprague–Grundy value 0 (the second
player wins) if and only if n ≡ 0, 2, 3, 6, 9 (mod 10), and value 1 if n ≡ 1, 4, 5, 7, 8
(mod 10).

                                 2. Preliminaries
Background. We assume the reader is familiar with the basics of impartial combi-
natorial game theory under normal play: the Sprague–Grundy (SG) function, its
value of a disjunctive sum as the nim-sum (⊕, the bitwise XOR) of the values of
the summands, and the classification of positions into P-positions (SG value 0, the
Next Player loses) and N -positions (SG value ̸= 0, the Next Player wins). A game
position with no legal move is terminal and has value 0. A standard reference is
Winning Ways [2].
Board notation. A board is a 1 × n strip of cells. A position is written as a string
in the symbols below, and square brackets [ ] mark the two ends of the board.
      • Pieces: the symbol X denotes an occupied cell.
      • Gaps: a natural number n denotes a run of n consecutive empty cells.
      • Residue classes: the symbol n̄ denotes a run of empty cells of length k ≡ n

        that congruence condition.

                     3. Reducing strategy and single state
   We record several game equalities that shorten a position. Throughout, boards
are invariant under left–right reversal, so we state only one of each mirrored pair
and use it silently in both orientations.
Lemma 3.1. Given any subpositions w, v, the following equalities hold:
   (1) [X1Xw] = [Xw];
   (2) [wX1Xv] = [wX] ⊕ [Xv];
4                              KAI LIANG AND MUXI LI


   (3) [XX1w] = [w];
   (4) [w1XX1v] = [w] ⊕ [v].
(The mirrors [wX1X] = [wX] and [w1XX] = [w] follow by reversal.)
Proof. In each equality the two sides have the same game: the cell(s) adjacent to
a block XX are unplayable (placing there would complete three in a row), so the
game splits or shrinks exactly as indicated. For example, in [XX1w] the single
cell after XX is unplayable and the block XX is inert, leaving exactly the game
[w]; in [w1XX1v] the two cells adjacent to XX are unplayable, so the position
splits into the independent games [w] and [v]. The equalities [X1Xw] = [Xw] and
[wX1Xv] = [wX] ⊕ [Xv] hold because the middle cell of X1X is unplayable, so the
two sides have identical move options.                                         □
    We call a position single if it cannot be reduced by Lemma 3.1.
Remark 3.2. A position is single if and only if it contains no subsequence XX or
X1X.
   The following equalities also simplify positions, but we do not count them as
reductions because they do not remove a whole block at once; they are used only
in Section 4.5.
Lemma 3.3. Given any subpositions w, v,
   (1) [X2Xw] = [2Xw];
   (2) [wX2X] = [wX2];
   (3) [wX4Xv] = [wX2] ⊕ [2Xv].
Proof. For (1), the two cells of the gap 2 are adjacent to X’s on both sides;
whichever of the two cells is played first, the other becomes unplayable and the
leftmost X becomes inert, so the position is [2Xw] with the right X now playing
the role of the boundary. (2) is the mirror of (1). For (3), the four cells of the gap
are split as 2 + 2; playing in one half cannot affect the other half because a move
adjacent to the middle leaves a gap that neither side can bridge, so the position is
the sum [wX2] ⊕ [2Xv].                                                               □

   Our approach to proving the main theorem is as follows. We introduce a class of
“regular positions” (Definition 4.2) and show that a regular position is always either
a P-position or an N -position according to its sign. The proof is an induction
with two parts, stated precisely in Theorem 4.12 and summarized in Section 4.1.
While some case analysis is inevitable, the reduction strategy keeps it small and
gives a compact hand-checked proof in place of a computer search.
