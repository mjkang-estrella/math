The analogous monophonic position problem is obtained from the general position problem by replacing “shortest path” by “induced path.” In this paper the monophonic position number is studied on Cartesian and lexicographic products of graphs. It is proved that in Cartesian products, a monophonic position set can only be in one of three canonical forms, named layered, varied, and cliquey. The monophonic position number of an arbitrary Cartesian product is bounded from below and above. The two bounds coincide if neither of the factors has simplicial vertices. A formula for the monophonic position number of a lexicographic product is given which only contains the clique number and the structure of monophonic sets of the second factor. Key words: general position set; general position number; monophonic position set; monophonic position number; Cartesian product; lexicographic product AMS Subj. Class: 05C57; 05C69 1 Introduction The general position problem for graphs originates in Dudeney’s ‘Puzzle with Pawns’ in his book ‘Amusements in Mathematics’ [ 8 ] from 1917. This problem asked the reader to find the largest number of pawns that can be placed on a chessboard without any three pawns lying on a common straight line in the plane. This problem was generalised to the setting of graph theory independently in [ 5 , 18 ] as follows: a set of vertices S S in a graph G G is in general position if no shortest path of G G contains more than two vertices of S S . The problem then consists of finding the largest set of vertices in general position for a given graph G G . The structure of general position sets in graphs was described in [ 1 ] . The general position problem has been the subject of intensive research, with some 48 papers appearing on the subject since 2018. For some recent developments see [ 2 , 3 , 6 , 11 , 12 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 27 , 29 , 31 ] . A survey of the problem is given in [ 4 ] . One case of particular interest is the general position number of product graphs. The general position problem for Cartesian products has been treated in many papers, including [ 13 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 26 , 28 ] . General position numbers of strong and lexicographic products were investigated in [ 7 ] . Several variations on the general position problem have also been considered. For example, we can replace ‘shortest path’ in the definition of the problem by some other family of paths. In [ 14 ] the authors restrict attention to shortest paths of bounded length, whilst [ 10 ] considers the problem for the widest possible family, i.e. all paths. Another important family is the induced or monophonic paths. Partially inspired by the extensive literature on monophonic convexity (a recent example is [ 19 ] ), the monophonic position problem was introduced in [ 24 ] . Some extremal problems for monophonic position sets are discussed in [ 30 ] , smallest maximal monophonic position sets are treated in [ 6 ] and graph colourings in which every colour class is in monophonic position are explored in [ 22 ] . In the present article we explore the monophonic position problem for various graph products. The plan of this paper is as follows. The next section contains the definitions we need, and we also call up some of the basic results needed later on. In Section 3 we focus on the monophonic position number of Cartesian products. We prove that a monophonic position set of a Cartesian product must take one of three canonical forms, which we call layered, varied, and cliquey. We bound the monophonic position number of an arbitrary Cartesian product from below and above and prove that if neither of the factors has simplicial vertices, then the bounds coincide. We also give a different upper bound for Cartesian products of triangle-free graphs. In Section 4 we turn our attention to lexicographic products and give a formula for the monophonic position number which involves the clique number and the structure of monophonic sets of the second factor. 2 Preliminaries We now define the terminology that will be used in this paper. By a graph G = ( V ⁡ ( G ) , E ⁡ ( G ) ) G=(V(G),E(G)) we mean a finite, undirected, simple graph. We will write u ∼ v u\sim v if vertices u u and v v are adjacent. Recall that a set S S of vertices in a graph G G is a general position set if no shortest path in G G contains more than two vertices of S S . The number of vertices in a largest general position set of G G is called the general position number of G G and is denoted by gp ⁡ ( G ) \gp(G) . A set M ⊆ V ⁡ ( G ) M\subseteq V(G) is a monophonic position set of G G if no three vertices of M M lie on a common monophonic path in G G . Observe that every monophonic position set of a graph G G is also in general position, and hence mp ⁡ ( G ) ≤ gp ⁡ ( G ) \mono(G)\leq\gp(G) . Any pair of vertices is in monophonic position, so for graphs with order n ⁡ ( G ) ≥ 2 n(G)\geq 2 we have 2 ≤ mp ⁡ ( G ) ≤ n ⁡ ( G ) 2\leq\mono(G)\leq n(G) . Trivially mp ⁡ ( G ) = n ⁡ ( G ) \mono(G)=n(G) holds for a connected graph G G if and only if G G is complete. It was shown in [ 24 , 30 ] that for any 2 ≤ a ≤ b 2\leq a\leq b there exists a graph G G with mp ⁡ ( G ) = a \mono(G)=a and gp ⁡ ( G ) = b \gp(G)=b . Interestingly, the largest possible number of edges in a graph with order n n and monophonic position number a a is quadratic in n n (with constructions close in size to the a a -partite Turán graph with order n n ), whereas for fixed general position number the largest size is linear in n n , see [ 30 ] . Thus the general and monophonic position problems are intrinsically different.
