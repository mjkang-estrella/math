We examine this problem in the setting of directed graphs. We provide bounds for the general position number of digraphs, show that the problem is NP-complete, investigate the problem for some important families of digraphs such as circulant digraphs, Kautz digraphs and permutation digraphs, and study the general position numbers obtained from all orientations of an undirected graph. a Department of Mathematics, Mahatma Gandhi College, University of Kerala, Thiruvananthapuram-695004, Kerala, India b Department of Information Engineering, Computer Science and Mathematics, University of L’Aquila, Italy c School of Mathematics, Open University, UK d Department of Mathematics, Greenshaw High School, Sutton, UK e Department of Informatics and Statistics, Klaipėda University, Lithuania Key words: general position number, digraph, general position set, convex set. Class: 05C12; 05C20; 05C69. 1 Introduction The initial inspiration of the general position problem was a chessboard puzzle by the 19th century puzzle-maker Henry Dudeney [ 15 ] , which asked how many pawns can be placed on a chessboard without any three lying on a straight line. The problem was later generalised to the setting of graph theory in [ 7 , 25 ] by asking for the largest possible subset S S of vertices of a graph G G such that no three vertices of S S are contained in a common shortest path of G G . The number of vertices in such a largest subset is called the general position number of G G and is denoted by gp ⁡ ( G ) {\rm gp}(G) . This problem has a very large literature; for a survey see [ 6 ] . Several variations of the general position problem have been explored, including replacing ‘shortest path’ by ‘induced path’ [ 30 ] , considering only shortest paths of bounded length [ 21 ] , replacing the ordinary graph distance by the Steiner distance [ 20 ] , finding smallest maximal general position sets [ 13 ] , sets of edges in general position [ 26 ] , and the mutual-visibility problem, in which for any u , v ∈ S u,v\in S we require only the existence of at least one shortest u , v u,v -path that does not pass through a third vertex of S S [ 12 ] . The contribution of this paper is to extend the general position problem to a different type of network, namely directed graphs, in which each link of the network is assigned a direction. A directed graph D D , or digraph for short, consists of a set V ⁡ ( D ) V(D) of vertices and a set of ordered pairs A ⁡ ( D ) A(D) called arcs. The order of D D is n = | V ⁡ ( D ) | n=|V(D)| and the size is m = | A ⁡ ( D ) | m=|A(D)| . If there is an arc ( u , v ) (u,v) in A ⁡ ( D ) A(D) we write u → v u\rightarrow v and say that v v is adjacent from u u and u u is adjacent to v v . We exclude loops u → u u\rightarrow u and multiple arcs u → v u\rightarrow v with the same direction, as they add nothing of interest for our problem, i.e. we consider only simple digraphs. However we do allow 2-cycles, i.e. we may have u → v u\rightarrow v and v → u v\rightarrow u . A digraph without 2-cycles is an oriented graph . An undirected graph G G is a set of vertices V ⁡ ( G ) V(G) together with a collection of unordered pairs E ⁡ ( G ) E(G) called edges, and we write u ∼ v u\sim v if u ​ v uv is an edge in E ⁡ ( G ) E(G) . Associated with any digraph D D is an underlying undirected graph on the same vertex set in which there is an edge u ∼ v u\sim v if and only if D D contains at least one of the arcs u → v u\rightarrow v or v → u v\rightarrow u . Conversely, assigning directions to each of the edges of an undirected graph G G yields an orientation G → G^{\rightarrow} of G G . Hence a graph is oriented if and only if it is an orientation of an undirected graph. The digraph D ^ \hat{D} obtained by reversing the orientation of all arcs of D D is the converse of D D . In an undirected graph the neighbourhood N ⁡ ( u ) N(u) of a vertex u u is { v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) : v ∼ u } \{v\in V(G):v\sim u\} and the degree d ⁡ ( u ) d(u) is | N ⁡ ( u ) | |N(u)| . An example of a general position set is shown in Figure 1 . Definition 2.1 . For a digraph D D , a subset S ⊆ V ⁡ ( D ) S\subseteq V(D) is in general position if for any u , v ∈ S u,v\in S , no shortest u , v u,v -path in D D passes through a vertex in S − { u , v } S-\{u,v\} . The number of vertices in a largest general position set of D D is the general position number gp ⁡ ( D ) {\rm gp}(D) of D D , and such a largest set is a gp {\rm gp} -set of D D . Figure 1: A gp {\rm gp} -set in a digraph (vertices in red) The recent paper [ 29 ] of Stojanović investigates the related mutual visibility problem in directed graphs. A subset S ⊆ V ⁡ ( G ) S\subseteq V(G) of a connected undirected graph G G is a mutual visibility set if for any u , v ∈ S u,v\in S there exists a shortest u , v u,v -path that does not contain any vertex of S − { u , v } S-\{u,v\} ; and the mutual visibility number μ ⁡ ( G ) \mu(G) of G G is the number of vertices in a largest mutual visibility set of G G , see [ 12 ] . Any general position set of G G is also a mutual visibility set, and so gp ⁡ ( G ) ≤ μ ⁡ ( G ) {\rm gp}(G)\leq\mu(G) for connected graphs. There are two possible definitions of a mutual visibility set for a disconnected graph: either one could define it to be the maximum mutual visibility number over the components, or else the sum of the mutual visibility number of the components. The latter definition ensures that the inequality gp ⁡ ( G ) ≤ μ ⁡ ( G ) {\rm gp}(G)\leq\mu(G) continues to hold for disconnected graphs. A similar issue arises in directed graphs that are not strongly connected, and we now distinguish the two possible definitions. Definition 2.2 . • A subset S ⊆ V ⁡ ( D ) S\subseteq V(D) of a digraph is a strong mutual visibility set if for each ordered pair u , v ∈ S u,v\in S there exists a shortest u , v u,v -path in D D that does not contain any vertices of S − { u , v } S-\{u,v\} . • A subset S ⊆ V ⁡ ( D ) S\subseteq V(D) is a weak mutual visibility set if for each ordered pair u , v ∈ S u,v\in S either d ⁡ ( u , v ) = ∞ d(u,v)=\infty or there exists a shortest u , v u,v -path that does not contain any vertices of S − { u , v } S-\{u,v\} . The ‘strong’ definition is that of Stojanović. For our purposes we prefer the ‘weak’ definition, since it ensures that the inequality gp ⁡ ( D ) ≤ μ ⁡ ( D ) {\rm gp}(D)\leq\mu(D) holds for all digraphs. Also the ‘strong’ mutual visibility number can be found by taking the maximum of the ‘weak’ mutual visibility number over the strong components of D D . Observe that the two definitions coincide for strong digraphs. We will not treat mutual visibility further here, except for the following realisation result to compare the two parameters. One possible direction for investigation is to restrict the orientations considered in Conjecture 4.3 . It is easy to find families of graphs such that all strong orientations, or all source- and sink-free orientations, have the same general position number. Problem 5.1 . Characterise the graphs with the property that all strong orientations (or all source- and sink-free orientations) have the same general position number. It would be especially interesting to find exact values for trees, permutation digraphs and Kautz digraphs for k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 . Problem 5.2 . Find a formula for the general position number of any tree, as well as permutation digraphs and Kautz digraphs for k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 . Regarding complexity, we have shown in Theorem 2.18 that finding the general position number of an oriented graph is NP-complete. However, except for the fact that gp → ​ ( G ) = n {\rm gp^{\rightarrow}}(G)=n can be tested in polynomial time, we have not considered the complexity of finding the gp {\rm gp} -spectrum. We conjecture that it is hard even to find the lower oriented general position number. Conjecture 5.3 . It is NP-hard to decide if a graph G G satisfies gp → ​ ( G ) = 2 {\rm gp_{\rightarrow}}(G)=2 . There are many variants of the general position problem, including monophonic position and mobile general position [ 6 ] . We suggest that it would be rewarding to investigate directed versions of these problems. Finally, we note that a logical next step would be to define and investigate the general position problem in signed graphs. Problem 5.4 . Investigate the general position problem for signed graphs.
