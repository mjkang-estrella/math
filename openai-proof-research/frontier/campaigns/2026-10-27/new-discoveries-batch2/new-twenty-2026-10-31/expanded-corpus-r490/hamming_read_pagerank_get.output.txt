                                                                                     Abstract
                                                  The well-known Erdős–Ko–Rado Theorem states that if F is a family of k-element
                                               subsets  of {1, 2, . . . , n} (n ≥ 2k) satisfying S, T ∈ F ⇒ |S ∩ T | ≥ 1, then |F| ≤
                                                n−1
                                                k−1  . The  theorem also provides necessary and sufficient conditions for attaining the
                                               maximum. We present elementary methods for deriving generalizations of the Erdős–
                                               Ko–Rado Theorem on several classes of combinatorial objects. We also extend our
                                               results to systems under Hamming intersection.


                                         1     Introduction
                                         As the reader knows, mathematics is many things, but it is never a one-stop trip. The
                                         development of a mathematical theory sees shaping by many hands, beginning with the
                                         innovator who first dreamed up its foundations and ending years down the road, if at all.
                                         Often, the discovery of one fundamental theorem will tip over the domino of another result
                                         in an observer’s mind, prompting new results and raising new questions. These results can
                                         then prove to be a catalyst of innovation in the work of others, leading to the development
                                         of a broad-reaching mathematical monolith.
                                              The Erdős–Ko–Rado Theorem is such a fundamental result.
                                              This discovery was announced to the world in 1961 with the publication of the seminal
                                         paper Intersection theorems for systems of finite sets by Paul Erdős, Chao Ko, and Richard
                                         Rado [4]. Interestingly enough, they had the paper essentially completed as early as 1938;
                                         however, one reason they did not publish it was due to what Erdős [15] describes as “relatively
                                         little interest in combinatorics” at the time. Ironically, the Erdős–Ko–Rado paper has since
                                         become perhaps the most oft-cited of Erdős’s joint work with Rado.
                                              So what is this celebrated theorem? Let [n] := {1, 2, . . . , n}, and consider any family F of
                                         k-sets on [n] (by this, we mean k-element subsets of [n]). If we are told that F is intersecting,
                                         that is, every pair of k-sets S, T ∈ F satisfy |S ∩ T | ≥ 1, what is the maximum possible size
                                         of our family F? Note that if n < 2k, by the Pigeonhole Principle every pair of k-sets has
                                         a nonempty intersection, and hence any family F of these sets will be intersecting—not a
                                         hugely subtle case. However, if we restrict n ≥ 2k, things start getting interesting.

inspection reveals that there is an easy-to-define intersecting family that is quite populous:
in particular, take an arbitrary element of [n] (say, 1) and consider the family of all sets
containing that element. Such a family we will dub trivially intersecting,    or just trivial for

short. It is not hard to count that all trivial families have k−1 elements. But as always
in mathematics, we are forced to ponder, can we do any better than this, given sufficient
cleverness? How often is it, really, that the obvious way turns out to be the best one?
    And this is where the Erdős–Ko–Rado Theorem comes into play: the theorem’s statement
is that indeed the trivial family is actually (strictly) the best! (Incidentally, there is a
mnemonic to describe situations such as this: “TONCAS,” or “The Obvious Necessary
Conditions are Also Sufficient.” We borrow this term from West [18], who attributes
it to Nash-Williams and others.) The original method for proving the Erdős–Ko–Rado
Theorem was two pages worth of induction, casework, and contradiction. However, in 1972,
Katona [10] published a quite short and strikingly beautiful probabilistic proof, which we
have adapted below.

For example, if n = 7, k = 3, and σ = (5, 2, 1, 7, 4, 6, 3), then σ would contain {1, 2, 5} and

to be the first element of S to appear in σ.
    Notice that σ can contain at most k elements of F: let i be the least number such that
σ(i) is the head of one of F’s k-sets that is contained in σ. Then the only other possible
heads of these k-sets are σ(i + 1), σ(i + 2), . . . , σ(i + k − 1), since F ’s sets all intersect the
k-set {σ(i), σ(i + 1), . . . , σ(i + k − 1)}.
    On the other hand, every k-set in F is contained in exactly k! · n · (n − k)! (k! orderings
of the set, n choices of which position in σ to place the head, and (n − k)! orderings of the
other elements in σ) permutations of [n]. Since there are n! total permutations, we combine
all of this to obtain

other advanced techniques, we intentionally stay simple in our tactics. While we correspond-

have a natural appeal, and it is always a surprise and a pleasure to discover the true po-

sets, the directions that we will be most concerned with involve finding similar results for
other combinatorial objects. In particular, we will be examining k-permutations, words, and
multisets, including considering what happens when the standard notion of intersection is
replaced by Hamming intersection.


2     Historical Overview
The original EKR Theorem appeared at the head of a wave of results in what is now known
as extremal set theory. It was not strictly the first result in the field, appearing after works
such as Ramsey’s Theorem [16] and the Erdős-Szekeres paper on combinatorial geometry [5]
as well as taking inspiration from the work of Sperner [17]; however, the EKR paper was
certainly a pioneer. In the literature, there exist many generalizations of the theorem, and
we provide only a sampling of them here. Results immediately relevant to our current study
will be mentioned as needed.
    One very direct generalization is to think about families that are t-intersecting; that is,
for S, T ∈ F, we have |S ∩ T | ≥ t. Again, we can think about the “trivial” family that
contains  all the k-sets sharing a fixed core of t elements; such a t-intersecting family contains
 n−t
     
 k−t
       elements.   Indeed, the original EKR paper proved that for n large enough, this is
always optimal. Later, Frankl [6] applied random walks to show that n is “large enough”
when n ≥ (t + 1)(k − t + 1) given that t ≥ 15; Wilson [19] then extended this result to all t
using linear algebraic techniques.
    Others have since determined what happens when n < (t + 1)(k − t + 1). These partial
results culminated in Ahlswede and Khachatrian’s [2] Complete Intersection Theorem. This
theorem is as powerful as its name seems to imply, and its proof both utilized and honed
high powered techniques that have wide-reaching implications for EKR-type results.
    Another vein of generalizations has been in abstracting to objects other than intersecting
families of k-sets. Some have looked at allowing F to be partitioned into a fixed number of
classes which must be intersecting (instead of F as a whole); Frankl and Füredi [7] is one
example of such a study. Other works include Katona [11], which uses elegant techniques
to arrive at an intersection theorem for systems of sets; there are also results for partitions,
such as Meagher and Moura [14] or Ku and Renshaw [12], and a variety of other classes of
combinatorial objects.
    There is also a contingent of EKR papers that seek not to derive new results but instead
to provide new proofs for old ones. Katona’s proof is one such paper, but there are a number
more. These include Balogh and Mubayi [3] as well as Frankl and Tokushige [8], amongst
others. In general, there is much to be gained from a fresh perspective and departure
from standard techniques. We would like to emphasize that this in no way detracts from
the immensely clever and powerful methods used to obtain these results in the first place;
rather, these reproofs serve to broaden our understanding of the relevant results.

3     Families Under Standard Intersection
In this section, we will derive Erdős–Ko–Rado-like theorems for families of objects under the
standard notion of intersection. Our first modification to the EKR problem will be to add
a component of order to our k-sets. So instead of looking at families of k-sets, we will look
at families of k-permutations of [n] (we define a k-permutation as an ordered k-set). We’ll
define a family P of k-permutations to be intersecting if every P, Q ∈ P satisfy |P ∩ Q| ≥ 1.
A trivial family has all of its k-permutations sharing a common element (and contains all
possible sets possessing said element).

    Finally, we make one more transition to round out this section. We now both add a
component of order and remove the restriction that elements must appear only once in our
k-sets. That is, we now consider k-words on [n], or ordered k-tuples such that each entry
is an element of [n]. We will keep the same concept of intersection (the intersection W ∩ V
between two words is defined as the multiset of elements that appear somewhere in both W
and V , multiplicities included), intersecting families, and trivially intersecting families.
Theorem 3.3. Let W be an intersecting collection of k-words on [n], n ≥ 2k. Then

                                       |W| ≤ nk − nk−1 .

Furthermore, |W| attains this maximal value when and only when W is trivial.
Proof. As before, let’s try to relate this version of EKR back to what we already know.
Define an equivalence relation ⊲⊳ on W as follows: for W = w1 . . . wk , V = v1 . . . vk , then
W ⊲⊳ V when, for all 1 ≤ i, j ≤ k,

                                      wi = wj ⇔ vi = vj .

Informally, we could say that W and V have the same pattern of equality of letters. It is
not hard to check that indeed ⊲⊳ is an equivalence relation. If E is an equivalence class of ⊲⊳,
let E ′ be the set obtained by converting all of E’s elements to k-permutations (in particular,

by retaining only the first occurrence of each element). So if E = {113233, 223433, . . .}, we
would have E ′ = {132, 234, . . .}.
    We note that by construction, E ′ is an intersecting family of i-permutations for some
i ≤ k, and hence by Theorem 3.1 has maximal size if it is a trivial family. Furthermore,
|E| = |E ′ |, so we see that |W| is maximized if all of the |E ′ | are maximized. It is not hard to
check that if W is a trivial family, each E ′ is a trivial family of k-permutations, and hence
|W| is maximized. Conversely, if |W| is maximal, then there must be an equivalence class
containing sets with only one distinct element, implying that W is trivial. We calculate the
size of a trivial collection as nk − nk−1 , completing our proof.
    We have sampled only a quick bite of the diversity of generalizations available, and we
hope we have whetted the reader’s appetite for more. In our next section, we consider a
different definition of what it means to be intersecting.


4     Families Under Hamming Intersection
A notion from coding theory will shape our work in this next section. Recall that the
Hamming distance between two words is defined as the number of positions in which the two
words differ (so the Hamming distance between (1, 3, 5) and (1, 4, 5) is 1). Analogously, we
define the size of the Hamming intersection between two words W and V , |W ∩H V |, to be
the number of positions in which W and V agree. Thus we have that |(1, 3, 5)∩H (1, 4, 5) = 2.
    In this new context, we return to and extend our previous results for k-permutations and
k-words (Hamming intersection is not defined for multisets). First of all, we should start
thinking about k-permutations. The concepts we used before extend readily: a family P is
Hamming intersecting if for each pair P, Q ∈ P, |P ∩H Q| ≥ 1, and a trivial family is one
that has a fixed element appear in a fixed position for each permutation in the family (and
contains all such k-permutations).
    Notice that our proof of Theorem 3.1 is useless in this new context, and hence we must
turn to a new technique. As it turns out, we can use a variant of Katona’s [10] probabilistic
method. Ku and Leader [13] were the first to notice this, and they successfully developed
a proof that involved examining bijections between [n] × [n] and [n2 ]. We present our own
variant that is slightly simpler.

    The remaining natural question is to ask is for the case of k-words with Hamming inter-
section. At this point, the reader can likely predict how we’re going to define intersecting
families, but we include it for completeness. We say that W is a Hamming intersecting
family of words if for each W, V ∈ W, we have that |W ∩H V | ≥ 1, and a trivial family is one
that contains all words having a certain fixed element appearing in a certain fixed position.
    Words under Hamming intersection have come up in an EKR context in a variety of
papers. Ahlswede and Khachatrian [1] give an excellent overview of what has been done in
this area. In their paper, Ahlswede and Khachatrian prove an EKR-like theorem for the
t-intersecting case. Incidentally, Frankl and Tokushige [8] came to the same theorem in a
different context, utilizing another set of tactics. While our methods are not powerful enough
to attack the t-intersecting version, we use the toolkit we have been building in this paper
to concisely prove the 1-intersecting case.

Theorem 4.2. Let W be a Hamming intersecting collection of k-words on n. Then

                                          |W | ≤ nk−1 .

Also, we can obtain |W | = nk−1 via a trivial family.

Proof. Recall our equivalence relation ⊲⊳ from the proof of Theorem 3.3, where given W =
w1 . . . wk , V = v1 . . . vk , W ⊲⊳ V when for all 1 ≤ i, j ≤ k,

                                      wi = wj ⇔ vi = vj .

As before, let E be an equivalence class of ⊲⊳, and define E ′ as the set obtained by converting
all of E’s elements to k-permutations.
    We note that by construction, E ′ is a Hamming intersecting collection of i-permutations
for i ≤ n, and hence by Theorem 4.1 has maximal size if it is a trivial family. Furthermore,
|E| = |E ′ |, so we see that |W| is maximized if all of the |E ′ | are maximized. It is not hard
to check that if W is a trivial collection, each E ′ is a trivial construction of k-permutations,
and hence |W| is maximized. We calculate the size of a trivial collection as nk−1 , completing
our proof.

5    Conclusions and Future Directions
At this point, we have visited a number of different generalizations of the Erdős–Ko–Rado
Theorem. In each of our proofs, we noticed that we required only elementary techniques
to arrive at the desired conclusion. Some of our results have long been discovered, but the
methodology is the real gem to mine from this text.
    Our results are only the tip of the iceberg, however. There are many possible directions
to go from here. Some of our proofs generalize immediately to t-intersecting systems (where
the size of the intersection is required to be at least t instead of at least 1); others may
not generalize at all. We note that our proof of Theorem 4.2 in particular has potential
to be generalized to t-Hamming intersecting collections (which would be essentially the
same as the Ahlswede and Khachatrian [1]—Frankl and Tokushige [8] result). However, our
generalization would rely on the following conjecture:

Conjecture 5.1. Let P be a t-Hamming intersecting collection (every P, Q ∈ P satisfies
|P ∩H Q| ≥ t) of k-permutations on [n], n ≥ n0 (k, t). Then

                                                (n − t)!
                                       |W | ≤            .
                                                (n − k)!

   One should note that the Katona-style probabilistic argument we used in Theorem 4.1
breaks down for t-intersecting families. However, all hope is not lost. Recently, there has
been been some work done on trying to generalize Katona’s proof to the 2- and 3-intersecting
cases; see Howard and Károlyi [9] for details.
   Another conjecture we would like to pose is the following:

Conjecture 5.2. Let M be a t-intersecting collection of k-multisets on [n], n ≥ n0 (k, t).
Then                                               
                                       n+k−t−1
                             |M| ≤                    .
                                           k−t
Furthermore, equality is achieved if and only if M is a trivial collection.

    An interesting note is that the conjecture, if true, would imply that there are as many t-
intersecting k-multisets on [n] as there are t-intersecting k-sets on [n+k−1], given appropriate
n, k, t.
    In any case, we hope that the reader has enjoyed this foray into elementary techniques
for Erdős–Ko–Rado results. Our paper is far from the first, and hopefully far from the last,
to attempt to find simple proofs for these facts. We hope that at the very least, we have
inspired the reader to consider simple methods as tools for constructing elegant solutions to
general problems.
