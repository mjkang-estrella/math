A chain-free pure YY-Delta minimum that is not minimal
DRAFT - AI-assisted research manuscript. No human author or affiliation is asserted.
Abstract. We give an explicit doubled simple cubic graph for which exhaustive pure YY-Delta
reduction attains a chain-free graph that is not minimal under the two degree-preserving moves.
The input has 18 vertices. Its labelled pure reduction DAG has 2328 states and 9846 legal-move
arcs; its smallest order is 11, attained by 25 labelled matrices. A selected minimum admits the
7-move sequence below to order 10. Thus Conjecture 5.1 of Jeffries and Yeats [1] is false under
the illustrated, edge-distinct reading of its chain definition. This qualification is part of the
statement, not a claim that the verbal definition is unambiguous in isolation.
The Lean endpoint is Round2.literal_negation : not Literal.Conjecture51. We reuse the previously
replayed packet byte-identically; this manuscript task does not run Lean. The finite result has
modest significance: it disproves the proposed criterion for doubled cubic inputs, without
providing a replacement criterion or a new general simplification theory. No priority claim is
made. This draft and its computation scripts were prepared with OpenAI Codex assistance and
require root review; accepted credit remains zero.
1. The source problem and its context
Jeffries and Yeats study a degree-preserving variant of delta-wye transformations, motivated in
part by graphs and Feynman periods. We use the journal version of [1], Definition 2.1 and
Conjecture 5.1, with the arXiv version retained for comparison. The journal distinguishes two
notions: a minimal graph has the least vertex count in its equivalence class; a minimum graph
additionally has the least number of connections among minimal graphs. The conjecture first takes
a smallest pure-reduction result, which we call a pure minimum. This does not assert either
equivalence-class minimality or connection minimality.
Conjecture 5.1 (verbatim, journal p. 678; typography normalized):
Given a doubled 3-regular graph G, perform the YY-Delta transformation on all wyes in all possible
ways to find a graph G’ of the smallest size. Then G’ is a minimal graph if and only if it does
not contain a chain of one or more 3-cycles, each connected by a vertex, which is also doubly
adjacent to one more vertex, where the 3-cycles on either end of the chain have one vertex with
exactly 4 neighbours and no connections of size greater than 2.
The surrounding remarks are essential. The source allows the depicted vertices to coincide but
requires distinct edges. It says other Figure 14 gadgets already certify nonminimality of
arbitrary graphs using an expansion first. The conjectured extra assertion concerns pure minima
from doubled cubic inputs. Its examples support a heuristic, not a proved classification. Our
example violates exactly that extra assertion.

2. Moves and the illustrated chain predicate
All graphs here are finite undirected loopless multigraphs. A symmetric nonnegative integer matrix
A with zero diagonal records edge multiplicities; the degree is its row sum. A connection is the
entire nonempty set of edges between a pair of vertices. Doubly adjacent means that the ambient
connection has size exactly two. A doubled graph replaces each edge of a simple graph by two
parallel edges.
Definition 2.1 removes a degree-six centre x with exactly three distinct neighbours u, v, w, each
joined to x by two edges. It deletes x and its incident edges, and adds one edge on each of uv,
uw, vw. This is YY-Delta (reduction). Its inverse, Delta-YY (expansion), selects distinct u, v, w
with a positive entry on each triangle pair, subtracts one on those pairs, and adds a new centre
doubly joined to each. Existing parallel edges on the triangle pairs are allowed. Each move
preserves degree six. Equivalence means a finite sequence of either kind, including the empty
sequence; pure reachability uses reductions only.
For precision, the chain predicate follows the verbal description together with Figure 14(a), (b),
(d). Take distinct base vertices b[0],...,b[t] for a positive number t of triangles. Triangle i
uses b[i], b[i+1], and an apex a[i]. Each end base has two double attachments; each internal base
has one double attachment. End bases have four actual neighbours and no ambient connection larger
than two. Every base has degree six. Apex and attachment vertices may coincide with one another or
other vertices whenever the edges can remain distinct.
For every unordered pair, count all schematic edge occurrences, with a double attachment
contributing two. Require that count to be at most the actual multiplicity A[u,v]. This capacity
condition is precisely the availability of distinct parallel-edge copies in each pair’s finite
edge pool. It forbids loops automatically. The bases must be distinct: otherwise their disjoint
six incident edge ends would exceed a degree-six vertex’s capacity (and adjacent identified bases
demand a loop).
A subtlety matters. A depicted internal double attachment reserves two edges, but under allowed
vertex coincidences it can lie in an ambient triple connection, sharing the pair with a separate
triangle edge. Imposing exact ambient double adjacency on every schematic attachment would exclude
the review’s required positive alias control. Likewise, imposing internal row type [1,1,1,1,2]
would be too restrictive. This paper uses the illustrated capacity reading fixed in the ruling and
independently reviewed with that explicit qualification.
The earlier discovery lane used a restricted incidence diagnostic and incorrectly said no chain
could leave an endpoint. That diagnostic is not our chain proof. The argument below allows first
triangles through a triple connection and blocks them by residual capacities. The formal statement
uses this same capacity predicate directly; no separate formal equivalence theorem to an edge-ID
embedding API is claimed.

4. Chain absence by local edge capacities
In H the only possible end bases are 3, 6. Each has single neighbours 1 and 8; their mutual
connection has size three. The first triangle of a chain starting at either end must therefore use
that end together with 1 and 8. This conclusion uses the actual end row [1,1,2,2] and both
prescribed end attachments: its double connections are already consumed, leaving only its two
single edges for the triangle.
Orient the first triangle so the next base is 1; the case with next base 8 is identical by
inspecting the displayed rows. It cannot be the other end of a one-triangle chain, since neither 1
nor 8 has the required end row. Its internal attachment must use the two remaining edges of the
triple connection between 1 and 8. All other available connections at that base are single.
If the initial end was 6, the remaining candidates for the next triangle are 3 and 4. There is no
edge between 3 and 4, so no second triangle fits. If the initial end was 3, the candidates are 4
and 6, joined by a double connection. Either orientation of this second triangle fails to
continue: choosing 4 as the next base leaves no available double attachment at 4. Choosing 6 also
leaves no available double attachment there, because its double connection to 3 was already
consumed as an attachment at the starting end. Termination at 6 would require that same consumed
connection, so it fails as well. Vertex 4 cannot terminate because it is not an end base.
These cases exhaust all first and second triangles, including allowed apex and attachment
coincidences. No longer chain survives its initial prefix. Thus H has no illustrated edge-distinct
chain. The fresh capacity search independently confirms the result and also checks every labelled
pure minimum. The local proof is the reason, not a replacement of the predicate by a row-type
heuristic.
5. A legal descent in the full equivalence class
Starting at H, perform the following operations, always using current indices. An expansion
appends its new vertex; a reduction closes the deleted index gap.

1. expand [1, 6, 8]; order 11 -> 12
2. reduce 6; order 12 -> 11
3. expand [4, 7, 10]; order 11 -> 12
4. expand [3, 7, 10]; order 12 -> 13
5. reduce 10; order 13 -> 12
6. reduce 7; order 12 -> 11
7. reduce 9; order 11 -> 10

Each expansion uses a genuine triangle; each reduction removes a row consisting of three double
connections. The fresh checker verifies symmetry, zero diagonal, nonnegative multiplicities and
degree six after every operation; all intermediate matrices are in recomputation.json. The final
order is 10, strictly below 11. Hence H is not minimal in its equivalence class. We do not claim
that this final graph is globally minimal.
Combining pure minimality, chain absence, and this strict descent contradicts the chain-free
implication of the conjectured biconditional for this doubled cubic input. Other pure minima are
nonminimal too: reverse their path to G, follow the displayed path to H, and then take the
descent.

6. What Figure 14 does and does not explain
No printed Figure 14 gadget occurs in H under the checked edge-distinct interpretation: the chain
argument excludes the first-column cases, and direct capacity embeddings exclude (c), (e), (f),
and (g). This is not a classification of the source’s entire infinite gadget family.
The shrinking trace nevertheless exposes source-known gadgets. After expanding (1,6,8) in the
shorter witness, Figure 14(c) embeds with its two shared triangle bases at 1 and 8, its other
triangle vertices at 4 and 3, and their shared double-attachment image at the new vertex 11. The
two remaining attachments at base 3 go to 2 and 6. Distinct schematic vertices can share that new
attachment image because the edge pools are separate.
The original discovery trace contains a commuting inverse detour: first expand (0,5,7), then
expand (1,6,8), reduce 6, remove the first newly introduced vertex at current index 10, and
continue with the last five moves of the shorter witness. After its two expansions, both (c) and
(g) embed, with full mappings in recomputation.json. Removing the detour leaves the shorter
descent. Thus (c) is exposed after one expansion in the shorter trace; both (c) and (g) occur
after two expansions in the original trace. We do not claim (g) occurs after the shorter trace’s
first expansion: the fresh template check finds none there.
This timing distinction sharpens the brief’s shorthand rather than changing the counterexample.
The source already knew these gadgets reduce arbitrary graphs and knew the usefulness of
expansion-first operations. The new finite obstruction is their hiding behind pure reduction from
a doubled cubic input whose smallest pure results lack chains. It is not a novel local move or a
new proof that the printed gadgets shrink graphs.
