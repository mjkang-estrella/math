18:                                                                                  1. Introduction
19:                                              In coding theory one frequently establishes in a natural way a connection between codes
20:                                           and groups. For instance, a group acting on a code may provide valuable insights into
21:                                           the structure of the code, as with the Mathieu groups acting on the Golay codes.
22:                                              Let C be a binary linear code of length N over F2 . Up to isomorphism, this simply
23:                                           means that C is a subspace of the standard vector space FN  2 of dimension N over the prime
24:                                           field F2 of characteristic 2. There is a natural action of the symmetric group Sym(N) of
25:                                           degree N on FN  2 by means of coordinate permutations. The automorphism group Aut(C)
26:                                           of C is the subgroup of Sym(N) consisting of all permutations which map the subspace
27:                                           C into itself. (In general, there are several ways of associating an automorphism group
28:                                           to a linear code, but the distinctions between these variations disappear in the context of
29:                                           binary linear codes; cf. [3, Section 1.5].) It can be shown that every finite group arises as
30:                                           the automorphism group of a suitable binary linear code; cf. [9]. The question which finite
31:                                           permutation groups, i.e. finite groups with a fixed faithful permutation representation,
32:                                           arise as automorphism groups of binary linear codes is more subtle; a possible approach
33:                                           to this problem was indicated in [7].
34:                                              Recall that the binary linear code C of length N is said to be cyclic if its automorphism
35:                                           group contains a regular cycle of length N. The class of binary cyclic codes is both of
36:                                           theoretical and of practical interest, containing well-known families of codes such as the
37:                                           quadratic residue codes. It turns out that the class of groups which occur as automorphism
38:                                           groups of cyclic codes is much more restricted. Indeed, one motivating force behind our
39:                                           work is the natural and fundamental
40:                                           Problem. Determine the class of finite groups which arise as the automorphism groups
41:                                           of (binary) cyclic codes.

51: the problem for other ground fields. Moreover, one can ask a corresponding question for

53:   We now give a summary of our results. First we exhibit two explicit families of groups
54: which do not arise as automorphism groups of binary cyclic codes.
55: Proposition A. The automorphism group of a binary cyclic code is not isomorphic (as
56: an abstract group) to a non-trivial cyclic group of odd order.
57: Theorem B. The automorphism group of a binary cyclic code is not isomorphic (as an
58: abstract group) to an alternating group Alt(n) of degree n ∈ {3, 4, 5, 6, 7} or n ≥ 9. The
59: group Alt(8) occurs as the automorphism group of a binary cyclic code of length 15.
60:    The exceptional appearance of Alt(8) can be explained by the isomorphism Alt(8) ∼   =
61: PSL(4, 2); cf. our remarks following Theorem E.
62:    Extensive computer calculations show that the automorphism groups of binary cyclic
63: codes can often be described as iterated wreath products of symmetric groups; see [2] for
64: a systematic account of automorphism groups of binary cyclic codes up to length 70. We
65: provide an explicit construction of codes with a prescribed automorphism group of this
66: type.
67: Theorem C. Let r ∈ N, and let n1 , . . . , nr ∈ N≥3 be odd. Let G := Sym(n1 )≀. . .≀Sym(nr )
68: be the iterated wreath product of symmetric groups of degrees n1 , . . . , nr . Then there exists
69: a binary cyclic code C of length N := n1 · · · nr such that Aut(C) ∼= G.
70:    It remains an open problem to construct binary cyclic codes such that the correspond-
71: ing automorphism groups are iterated wreath products of symmetric groups of arbitrary
72: degrees. The computational evidence suggests that such products occur frequently, but
73: that extra care must be taken if the product is to involve as factors the symmetric group
74: of degree 2. A few explicit examples are given in Section 8.
75:    More rarely, one encounters codes whose automorphism group is a direct product of
76: two symmetric groups. Again we are able to offer an explicit construction of binary linear
77: codes C0 (a, b), parameterised by a, b ∈ N with a ≤ b, whose automorphism groups are
78: of this type; for exceptional values of a, b the automorphism groups are, in fact, slightly
79: larger. A detailed description of the family of codes C0 (a, b) is given in Propositions 3.1,
80: 3.2 and Corollary 3.3. As a consequence we record
81: Theorem D. Let a, b ∈ N with 2 < a < b and gcd(a, b) = 1. Then there exist binary
82: cyclic codes C such that Aut(C) ∼
83:                                 = Sym(a) × Sym(b).
84:   Interestingly, some of the codes C0 (a, b) were recently studied by Key and Seneviratne
85: in the context of regular lattice graphs and permutation decoding. In fact, we provide
86: a new, unified treatment of a related family C1 (a, b) of binary linear codes whose study
87: was initiated in [5]. Our approach leads to a complete description of the automorphism
88: groups of these codes, allowing us, for instance, to decide which of the codes C1 (a, b) are

98: Theorem E. Let G ≤ Sym(N) be the automorphism group of a binary cyclic code C, and
99: suppose that G is a primitive permutation group. Then one of the following holds.
100:    (1) Cp  G  AGL(1, p) where p = N ≥ 5 is a prime.
101:    (2) G = Sym(N); in this case C is one of four elementary codes.
102:    (3) G = PΓL(d, q) where d ≥ 3, q = 2k for k ∈ N and N = (q d − 1)/(q − 1).
103:    (4) G = M23 and N = 23.
104: Moreover, each of the groups listed in (2)–(4) does occur as the automorphism group of a
105: suitable binary cyclic code.

107:   It remains an open problem to find out precisely which subgroups of affine groups occur
108: as automorphism groups of binary cyclic codes. This appears to be essentially a question
109: in combinatorial number theory. Computer calculations show that, for instance, there
110: exists a binary cyclic [17, 8, 6]-code whose automorphism group is C8 ⋉ C17 ≤ AGL(1, 17).
111: More explicit examples are given in Section 8.
112:   Part (3) of Theorem E can be regarded as a generalisation of the well-known fact that
113: the automorphism group of the binary Hamming code of length 2d − 1 is PSL(d, 2).
114: Organisation. The paper is divided into eight sections. Section 2 contains a brief sum-
115: mary of general notions and terminology as well as the constructions of the specific code
116: families C0 (a, b), C1 (a, b) and K(n1 , . . . , nr ). Propositions 3.1, 3.2 and Corollary 3.3 in

118: tions 4.1, 4.2 and 4.3 in Section 4 describe the structure of the codes C1 (a, b) related to

698: under the Frobenius automorphism of R. The latter induces a permutation π of the
699: standard basis, given by X m 7→ X 2m where exponents are to be read as integers modulo
700: N. As π fixes the basis element 1, it can only belong to a regular cyclic group, if it is
701: trivial. Thus N = 1 or N = 2. Since N is odd, we conclude that C = {0}, and Aut(C) is
702: trivial.                                                                                    
703: Proof of Theorem B. Let C be a binary cyclic code of length N such that Aut(C) is iso-
704: morphic to an alternating group Alt(n) of degree n ≥ 3. An exact factorisation of Alt(n)
705: consists of two subgroups G, H ≤ Alt(n) such that Alt(n) = GH and G ∩ H = 1.
706: Since Aut(C) contains a regular cyclic subgroup of order N which is complemented by
707: any point stabiliser, this provides an exact factorisation Alt(n) = GH with one of the
708: groups G, H cyclic of order N. Exact factorisations of alternating groups were studied
709: by Wiegold and Williamson [10]. Adhering to the notation in [10, Theorem A], our set-
710: ting allows for two possibilities. It could be that G is cyclic of odd order n = N and
711: H ∼= Alt(n − 1), but this would contradict Corollary 6.2. The only other possibility is
712: that n = 8, that G ∼  = AGL(3, 2) is an affine group and H is cyclic of order N = 15.

719:                          7. Primitive permutation groups
720:   In this section we prove Theorem E, using results from the well-developed theory of
721: permutation groups and modular permutation representations.
722:   Let N ∈ N. Let G ≤ Sym(N) be the automorphism group of a binary cyclic code C,
723: and suppose that G is a primitive permutation group. Then G contains a regular cyclic
724: subgroup and hence one of the following holds; see [4, Theorem 3].
725:      (1) Cp ≤ G ≤ AGL(1, p) where p = N is prime.
726:      (2) G = Sym(N), or G = Alt(N) where N ≥ 3 is odd.
727:      (3) PGL(d, q) ≤ G ≤ PΓL(d, q) where d ≥ 2, q = pk is a prime power and N =
728:          (q d − 1)/(q − 1).
729:      (4) G = PSL(2, 11), M11 or M23 where N = 11, 11 or 23 respectively.
730: 
731: Proof of Theorem E. We consider the four cases listed above.
732:    (1) Suppose that Cp ≤ G ≤ AGL(1, p) where p = N is prime. Here AGL(1, p) denotes
733: the affine group of degree 1. If p = 2 then G = Sym(2) will be covered by case (2)
734: below. So suppose that p ≥ 3. Proposition A shows that Cp  G. If p = 3, then
735: G = AGL(1, 3) = Sym(3) will be covered by case (2) below. Now suppose that p ≥ 5.
736: For a contradiction assume that G = AGL(1, p). From [8, Table 1 and Lemma 2] we
737: deduce that the underlying code C is elementary, and hence G = Aut(C) = Sym(p), a
738: contradiction.
739:    (2) In Section 6 it was shown that the symmetric group Sym(N) occurs as the automor-
740: phism group of the elementary codes. According to Corollary 6.2 the alternating group
741: Alt(N), N ≥ 3, does not occur as the automorphism group of a binary linear code of
742: length N.
743:    (3) Suppose that PGL(d, q) ≤ G ≤ PΓL(d, q) where d ≥ 2, q = pk is a prime power
744: and N = (q d − 1)/(q − 1). First we assume that d = 2 and arrive at a contradiction.
745: As PGL(2, q) acts 3-transitively on 1-dimensional projective space P1 (Fq ), we deduce
746: from [8, Table 1 and Lemma 2] that the underlying code C is elementary, and hence
747: G = Aut(C) = Sym(N), a contradiction. Hence d ≥ 3, and, similarly, we deduce from [8,
748: Table 1 and Lemma 2] that p = 2 and hence q = 2k .
749:    Let V denote the permutation module over F2 , associated to the natural action of
750: PGL(d, q) on (d − 1)-dimensional projective space Pd−1 (Fq ). Let U1 be a PGL(d, q)-
751: submodule of V. We claim that U1 is automatically PΓL(d, q)-invariant. Indeed, let σ
752: be a generator of the cyclic group PΓL(d, q)/ PGL(d, q) ∼   = Aut(Fq |F2 ). Then U2 := Uσ1 ,
753: regarded as a PGL(d, q)-module, is simply a twist of U1 . Writing F2 for the algebraic
754: closure of F2 , we conclude that the composition factors of the F2 PGL(d, q)-modules U1 :=
755: F2 ⊗ U1 and U2 := F2 ⊗ U2 are the same. The submodules of the F2 PGL(d, q)-module
756: V := F2 ⊗ V are uniquely determined by their composition factors; see [1]. Hence we
757: conclude that U1 = U2 and this implies U1 = U2 . We obtain G = PΓL(d, q), as wanted.
758:    For d ≥ 3 and q = 2k we still need to justify that the permutation group PΓL(d, q) does
759: indeed occur as the automorphism group of a suitable binary cyclic code K. The explicit
760: 
761:                            AUTOMORPHISM GROUPS OF CODES                                17
762: 
763: description of permutation modules of PGL(d, q) in [1] guarantees the existence of a non-
764: elementary binary cyclic code K of length N = (q n − 1)/(q − 1) such that PGL(d, q) ≤
765: Aut(K). From [6] we conclude that either Aut(K) ⊆ PΓL(d, q) or Alt(N) ⊆ Aut(K).
766: Proposition 6.1 rules out the second possibility, and hence our argument above implies
767: that Aut(K) = PΓL(d, q).
768:    (4) A finite computation shows that of the three possible permutation groups precisely
769: one, namely the Mathieu group M23 acting as a permutation group of degree 23, occurs
770: as the automorphism group of a binary cyclic code, namely the binary Golay code.       

843:    Let p be a prime. Then every binary cyclic code of length N = p can be realised as
844: an ideal of the residue class ring R := F2 [X]/(X p − 1). Let f denote the order of 2 in
845: the multiplicative group F∗p and put e := (p − 1)/f . Then X p − 1 factorises over F2 as
846: a product of X − 1 and e distinct irreducible polynomials of degree f . Accordingly, R
847: decomposes as a direct sum of the field F2 and e copies of the field F2f . Hence the number
848: of ideals of R is 2e+1 . The binary cyclic codes corresponding to these ideals fall into a
849: certain number of isomorphism classes. The dimensions of the codes range over the values
850: kf and kf + 1, where k ∈ {0, . . . , e}. In the special case where e = 1, there are only
851: four codes, namely the elementary codes discussed in Section 6. Considering a different
852: example, if p = 2l − 1 is a Mersenne prime, then e = (p − 1)/l and f = l so that there are
