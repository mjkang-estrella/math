                                                                                               Abstract

                                                        This paper paper concerns the study of forts, the sets that obstruct zero forcing. We show
                                                    that every block graph on n vertices has at least n/3 minimal forts, extending a recent bound for
                                                    trees by Cameron and and Li (arXiv:2512.12874). The number of forts, and with it the number
                                                    of compatible collections, grows exponentially for every tree, every connected non-star graph of
                                                    bounded degree, and every connected graph of linear minimum degree, answering a question in
                                                    the negative by Hicks et al. (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2022) for these graph classes.
                                                    We finish by counting the minimal forts of a tree exactly, in linear time and space.

                                                    Keywords: zero forcing; minimal fort; block graph; tree.
                                                    MSC (2020): 05C50; 05C85; 68R10.


                                           1       Introduction

                                           Zero forcing was introduced to bound the maximum nullity of symmetric matrices described by a
                                           graph [3, 9, 8] and was found independently in quantum information theory as graph infection [1]
                                           and in graph searching as fast-mixed search [5, 6]. Let G = (V, E) be a graph and let S ⊆ V be a
                                           set of initially blue vertices, the remaining vertices being white. The color change rule allows a blue
                                           vertex u to force a white vertex v to become blue when v is the unique white neighbor of u. Blue
                                           vertices remain blue. The set S is a zero forcing set if repeated application of the rule colors every
                                           vertex of G blue, and the zero forcing number Z(G) is the minimum cardinality of a zero forcing
                                           set. Computing Z(G) is known to be NP-hard [2].
                                               In this paper we investigate the obstruction structure of zero forcing. A fort is a non-empty set
                                           of vertices no vertex outside which has exactly one neighbor inside; a fort is minimal if none of its
                                           proper subsets is a fort. Forts characterize zero forcing sets exactly: a set S is a zero forcing set
                                           if and only if its complement contains no fort [7]. Minimal forts therefore drive the set covering
                                           and integer programming formulations used in computational zero forcing, and they carry a growing
                                           structural load, for instance in bounding zero forcing numbers of graph products and in the study
                                           of maximum nullity through compatible collections of forts [9, 12].
                                              We contribute with three results in this direction. The first extends the recent lower bound of
                                           Cameron and Li [11] from trees to all block graphs: every block graph on n vertices has at least
                                           n/3 minimal forts (Theorem 2.8). The second takes up Question 1 of Hicks et al. [9], which asks
                                           which graphs admit only polynomially many compatible sets of minimal forts. Furst, Hutchens,

                                                                                                   1
Mitchell, and Zhang [12] showed that the minimum-rank bound motivating that question in fact
requires compatible collections of arbitrary forts, and it is the corrected question that we study. The
number of forts, and hence the number of compatible collections of forts, grows exponentially for
every tree, every connected non-star graph of bounded maximum degree, and every connected graph
of minimum degree linear in n, so the corrected question has a negative answer on each of these
classes (Corollary 3.11). Whether families of unbounded maximum degree and sublinear minimum
degree can behave differently we do not know. This should be contrasted with the count of minimal
forts, which can be polynomial and whose extremal behaviour was studied by Becker, Cameron,
Hanely, Ong, and Previte [10]. The third is an exact counting algorithm; dynamic programming
over a rooted tree computes the number of minimal forts of any tree in linear time and linear
space (Theorem 4.14), complementing the bounds and the enumeration approach of Nguyen and
Kenter [14].

3    Counting Compatible Collections of Forts

Hicks et al. [9] posed as their Question 1 the problem of characterizing the graphs that admit
polynomially many compatible sets of minimal forts. Our work is shaped by a subsequent correction.
Furst, Hutchens, Mitchell, and Zhang [12] observed that the minimum-rank bound motivating that
question relies on the assumption that the minimal supports of null vectors of a matrix A ∈ Sym(G)
coincide with the minimal forts of G, and they exhibited the Petersen graph as a counterexample:
every sub-collection of Fortmin (P ) attaining the transversal number 5 fails to be compatible. To
repair the bound they replace minimal forts by arbitrary forts, defining the fort transversal number
T (G) = max{τ (F) : F ⊆ Fort(G) compatible} and proving N (G) ≤ T (G) ≤ Z(G). Since it is this
corrected quantity that the maximum-nullity and minimum-rank application requires, we study the
analogue of Question 1 for compatible collections of arbitrary forts.
Question 1. For which graphs is the number of compatible sets of forts bounded by a polynomial in
the order of the graph?

   Our results show that the answer is negative on three broad classes: trees, connected non-star
graphs of bounded maximum degree, and connected graphs of minimum degree linear in the order.
The mechanism is that the quantity being bounded already dominates the number of forts itself,
which we show is exponential on each of these classes. Whether a family with unbounded maximum
degree and sublinear minimum degree can behave differently we do not know. The contrast with the
count of minimal forts is worth keeping in view: that quantity can be polynomial, and its extremal
behaviour was studied by Becker, Cameron, Hanely, Ong, and Previte [10].
    We write V≥2 for the vertices of degree at least 2, L for the number of leaves, δ(G) for the
minimum degree, α(G) for the independence number, and dom(G) for the number of dominating
sets of a graph G.
   It is convenient to phrase the fort condition through complements. For S ⊆ V put F = V \ S.
Then F is a fort if and only if S ̸= V and no s ∈ S has exactly one neighbor outside S. We call
such a proper subset S admissible. Equivalently, by a result of Becker, Cameron, Hanely, Ong, and
Previte [10, Thm. 10], admissible sets are the failed zero forcing sets of G, and minimal forts are
complements of maximal failed zero forcing sets.

4    Counting Zero Forcing Minimal Forts on Trees

Bounds on the number of zero forcing minimal forts of a tree, together with an algorithm for
computing them, were obtained by Nguyen and Kenter [14]. We take a different route and give an
algorithm that computes fm (T ), the number of minimal forts of a tree T , exactly, in linear time and
linear space.
    Throughout this subsection T = (V, E) is a tree on n vertices rooted at a distinguished vertex
r ∈ V , and N (v) denotes the open neighborhood of v in T . For v ∈ V we write ch(v) for the set of
children of v, qv = |ch(v)|, and Tv for the sub-tree induced by v together with all of its descendants.
We fix once and for all an arbitrary ordering c1 , . . . , cqv of ch(v), and for 0 ≤ i ≤ qv we let Tvi

and Tvqv = Tv .
    We begin with the effect of deleting a single vertex from a fort. Let F be a fort of T and let
v ∈ F . A vertex u ∈ V is a witness for the removal of v if u ∈ V \(F \{v}) and |N (u)∩(F \{v})| = 1.
Thus, when |F | ≥ 2 the set F \ {v} is nonempty and fails to be a fort precisely when a witness for
the removal of v exists; when F = {v} the deletion is empty and is not a fort by definition, whether
or not a witness exists.

Lemma 4.1. Let F be a fort of T and v ∈ F . Then a witness for the removal of v is either v itself
or a vertex in N (v) \ F . More precisely, u is a witness for the removal of v if and only if one of the
following holds:

  (i) u = v and |N (v) ∩ F | = 1; or

 (ii) u ∈ N (v) \ F and |N (u) ∩ F | = 2.

Proof. Let F ′ = F \ {v}. By definition u is a witness for the removal of v exactly when u ∈ V \ F ′
and |N (u) ∩ F ′ | = 1. Since V \ F ′ = (V \ F ) ∪ {v}, every candidate u falls into one of two cases. If
u = v, as v ∈
            / F ′ , v is a candidate, and since v ∈
                                                  / N (v) we have N (v) ∩ F ′ = N (v) ∩ F . Hence v is a
witness if and only if |N (v) ∩ F | = 1.
    If u ∈ V \F , because F is a fort, |N (u)∩F | ̸= 1. Deleting v gives |N (u)∩F ′ | = |N (u)∩F |−1[v ∈
N (u)]. For u to be a witness we need |N (u) ∩ F ′ | = 1; since the count can drop by at most one, this
forces v ∈ N (u) and |N (u) ∩ F | = 2, i.e. u ∈ N (v) \ F with |N (u) ∩ F | = 2.
    Conversely both conditions clearly produce a witness. Finally, any u ∈ V \ F with v ∈   / N (u) has
|N (u) ∩ F ′ | = |N (u) ∩ F | ̸= 1 and is never a witness, so the two cases are exhaustive.

   Quantifying Lemma 4.1 over all vertices of F yields a necessary condition for minimality, stated
here for an arbitrary graph.

Proposition 4.2. Let F be a fort of a graph G with |F | ≥ 2. Then no set of the form F \ {v}
with v ∈ F is a fort of G if and only if every v ∈ F satisfies at least one of the following: either
|N (v) ∩ F | = 1, or some u ∈ N (v) \ F satisfies |N (u) ∩ F | = 2. In particular, every minimal fort
with at least two vertices satisfies this condition at each of its vertices.

Proof. By Lemma 4.1 the set F \ {v} fails to be a fort exactly when v admits a witness, and the two
alternatives in the statement are the two kinds of witness. A minimal fort with at least two vertices
has no fort among its proper subsets, hence none among its co-singleton subsets.

    The condition of Proposition 4.2 is necessary but not sufficient, and the reason is that forts are
closed under unions: if F1 and F2 are forts and u ∈   / F1 ∪F2 , then |N (u)∩F1 | ̸= 1 and |N (u)∩F2 | ̸= 1
force |N (u) ∩ (F1 ∪ F2 )| ̸= 1, since the latter count vanishes when both counts vanish and is at least
two otherwise. A fort may therefore contain a fort properly while every one of its vertices still admits
a witness.

Remark 4.3. Let T be the double star obtained from an edge c1 c2 by attaching leaves x1 , x2 , x3 to
c1 and y1 , y2 , y3 to c2 , and let F = {x1 , x2 , y1 , y2 }. Since |N (c1 ) ∩ F | = |N (c2 ) ∩ F | = 2, every
vertex of F admits a witness, so no co-singleton subset of F is a fort; nevertheless F contains the
fort {x1 , x2 } properly. The phenomenon is not confined to unions of disjoint forts: in the tree with


                                                     11
edges x1 s, x2 s, st and tz, the set F = {x1 , x2 , s, z} is a fort, admits a witness at each of its vertices,
is not a disjoint union of two forts, and properly contains the fort {x1 , x2 }, which is obtained from
it by deleting s and z simultaneously.

    Minimality must therefore be certified against the deletion of arbitrary subsets, and this is what
the following reformulation records.

Definition 4.4. Let F be a fort of a graph G. A split of F is an ordered partition F = D ⊔ K into
a deleted part D and a kept part K. The split is valid if D ̸= ∅, K ̸= ∅, and K is a fort of G.

Lemma 4.5. A fort F of a graph G is minimal if and only if F admits no valid split.

Proof. If F = D ⊔ K is a valid split, then K is a fort with ∅ ̸= K ⊊ F , so F is not minimal.
Conversely, if F is not minimal, then some fort K satisfies ∅ ̸= K ⊊ F , and D = F \ K yields a
valid split.

    Lemma 4.1 is the case |D| = 1 of Lemma 4.5: a fort admits a valid split whose deleted part is a
singleton if and only if one of its vertices admits no witness. The algorithm developed below tracks
splits with deleted parts of arbitrary size, and the point that makes this possible in linear time is
that a bounded amount of information about the boundary of a sub-tree suffices.

In the pseudocode below, T (s) denotes the reduced type set of the state s as given by Lemma 4.11,
µ denotes the merge operation of Lemma 4.12, and α(S) and M (S) denote the two components read

and are precomputed once as lookup tables indexed by the at most 46 states.

Algorithm 1 Counting the minimal forts of a tree
Input: a tree T rooted at r, with the children of each vertex ordered arbitrarily
Output: the number fm (T ) of minimal forts of T
 1: compute a post-order traversal of T
 2: for each vertex v in post-order do
 3:    B ← 0; B[(1, 0, 0, 0, ∅)] ← 1; B[(0, 0, 1, 0, ∅)] ← 1
 4:    for each child c of v, in the fixed order do
 5:        B′ ← 0
 6:        for each state s = (b, λ, e, α, M ) with B[s] > 0 do
 7:           for each state s′ = (b′ , λ′ , e′ , α′ , M ′ ) with A[c][s′ ] > 0 do
 8:               if b′ = 0 and λ′ + b = 1 then
 9:                    continue
10:               end if
