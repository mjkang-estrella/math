                                                                                         1. Introduction
                                             All graphs are assumed to be finite and simple, meaning that they have no loops or multiple edges.
                                          The vertex set and edge set of a graph G are denoted by V (G) and E(G), respectively.
                                             Graph complexes are simplicial complexes constructed from graphs by encoding their combinatorial
                                          structure into higher-dimensional simplices. In topological combinatorics, graph complexes are one of
                                          the main objects of study. Various topological invariants of these complexes, such as homotopy type,
                                          connectivity, homology, and their refinements, provide powerful tools for understanding combinatorial
                                          properties of graphs. A classical and one of the most celebrated examples of graph complexes is the
                                          neighbourhood complex, introduced by L. Lovász in 1978 [29]. Lovász gave a lower bound for the
                                          chromatic number of a graph in terms of the topological connectivity of its neighbourhood complex,
                                          and using this bound, he proved the Kneser conjecture [27].
                                             Graph complexes are prevalent across the literature, and have applications in various areas of
                                          mathematics, engineering, and data analysis. They are central to graph theory and combinatorics,
                                          where the topology of these complexes is related to the combinatorics of the underlying graphs (see
                                          [1, 13, 31, 32, 33, 37]), and have found many uses in data analysis and machine learning through
                                          simplicial representation of networks and topological data analysis (see [9, 17, 20]). Graph complexes
                                          also appear extensively in commutative algebra, where they are intricately related to monomial ideals
                                          and resolutions (see [14, 15, 24, 35, 36, 40, 42]). In representation theory, they are used in the study of
                                          symmetric group actions on simplicial complexes and associated homological representations (see [2, 5,
                                          8]). Graph complexes have connections to geometric group theory, where they serve as combinatorial
                                          models for classifying spaces and group actions (see [21, 22, 43]), and also have applications in random
                                          topology (see [6, 26, 34, 39]). For comprehensive treatments of graph complexes, we refer the reader
                                          to the books [25], [28] and [30].
                                             Recently, Bayer et al. in [4] and [3] introduced two new families of graph complexes: total cut
                                          complexes and cut complexes. For k ≥ 1, the total k-cut complex of a graph G, denoted as ∆tk (G),
                                          is the simplicial complex whose facets (maximal simplices) are σ ⊆ V (G) such that |σ| = |V (G)| − k
                                          (where | · | denotes cardinality) and the induced subgraph G[V (G) \ σ] does not contain any edge (see

    In this article, we consider the total 2-cut complexes (hence 2-cut complexes) of three families of
graphs: (i) powers of cycle graphs, (ii) Cartesian product of complete graphs and path graphs, and
(iii) Cartesian product of complete graphs and cycle graphs.
    For a graph G and a positive integer p, the p-th power graph of G is a graph Gp with V (Gp ) = V (G)
and {u, v} ∈ E(Gp ) if and only if there exists a path between u and v of length at most p in G (here,
the length of a path is the number of edges in the path). Clearly, G1 = G.
    Let Γ be a group and let S ⊂ Γ be a subset not containing the identity element. The Cayley graph
of Γ with respect to S is the graph G(Γ, S) having vertex set Γ, and {u, v} ∈ E(G(Γ, S)) if and only
if uv −1 ∈ S ∪ S −1 , where S −1 = {x−1 | x ∈ S}. For n ≥ 2 and S ⊂ {1, 2, . . . , n − 1}, the circulant
graph Cn (S) is a Cayley graph of Zn , the cyclic group of order n.
    For n ≥ 3, let Cn denote the cycle graph on n vertices {0, 1, . . . , n − 1}. Observe that for p ≥ 1,
V (Cnp ) = V (Cn ) = {0, 1, . . . , n − 1} and E(Cnp ) = {{i, i + j (mod n)} | 0 ≤ i ≤ n − 1 and 1 ≤ j ≤ p}.
It is easy to check that for n ≥ 2p + 1, Cnp is a circulant graph Cn ({1, 2, . . . , p}).
    In [4], the authors studied ∆tk (Cn ) (Theorem 4.15) and ∆t2 (Cn2 ) (Proposition 4.19). They proved
that these complexes (if nonvoid) are homotopy equivalent to wedges of spheres. In [38], the authors
determined the homotopy type of ∆t3 (Cn2 ) (Theorem 1.5) and, for k ≥ 3, the homotopy type of ∆tk (Cn2 )
when n = 3k + 1, 3k + 2 (Theorem 1.6).
    The k-cut complexes of powers of cycle graphs have been studied by many authors. In [3, Propo-
sition 7.11], the authors proved that for k ≥ 3, ∆k (Cn ) is shellable1, and in [12, Theorem 1.4], it
has been proved that ∆3 (Cn2 ) is shellable for n ≥ k + 6. In [11], the authors proved that ∆3 (Cnp ) is
shellable for n ≥ 6p − 3. Hence all of these complexes are wedges of spheres up to homotopy type.

  This section presents some basic definitions and results used in this article.

2.1. Graph. A graph G is a pair (V (G), E(G)), where V (G) is its vertex set and E(G) ⊆ V (G)
                                                                                                         
                                                                                                      2    is
the edge set. For any u, v ∈ V (G), we say that u and v are adjacent if {u, v} ∈ E(G). We write u ∼ v
for adjacency and u ≁ v for non-adjacency. A subgraph H of G is a graph with V (H) ⊆ V (G) and
E(H) ⊆ E(G). For a subset U ⊆ V (G), the induced subgraph G[U ] is the subgraph with V (G[U ]) = U
and E(G[U ]) = {{a, b} ∈ E(G) | a, b ∈ U }.
   For u, v ∈ V (G), a path from u to v is a sequence of distinct vertices u = v0 , v1 , . . . , vn = v such
that vi ∼ vi+1 for all 0 ≤ i ≤ n − 1. The length of a path is the number of edges in the path. A graph
is connected if there exists a path between each pair of its vertices; otherwise, it is disconnected.
   The complete graph Kn on n vertices is the graph in which any two distinct vertices are adjacent.
For n ≥ 1, the path graph Pn is the graph with V (Pn ) = {0, 1, . . . , n − 1} and E(Pn ) = {{i, i +
1} | 0 ≤ i ≤ n − 2}. For n ≥ 3, the cycle graph Cn is the graph with V (Cn ) = {0, 1, . . . , n − 1} and
E(Cn ) = {{i, i + 1} | 0 ≤ i ≤ n − 2} ∪ {{0, n − 1}}.
   We refer the reader to [7] and [41] for more details about the graph terminologies used in this
article.

2.2. Simplicial complex. A finite abstract simplicial complex ∆ is a collection of finite sets such
that if τ ∈ ∆ and σ ⊂ τ , then σ ∈ ∆. The elements of ∆ are called simplices of ∆. If σ ⊂ τ , we say
that σ is a face of τ . The dimension of a simplex σ is equal to |σ| − 1. The dimension of an abstract
simplicial complex is the maximum of the dimensions of its simplices. If a simplex has dimension
d, it is said to be d-dimensional. The 0-dimensional simplices are called vertices of ∆. An abstract
simplicial complex which is an empty collection of sets is called the void abstract simplicial complex,
and is denoted by ∅. A simplex that is not a face of any other simplex is called a maximal simplex or
facet. A subcomplex ∆′ of ∆ is a simplicial complex such that σ ∈ ∆′ implies σ ∈ ∆.
   In this article, we consider any simplicial complex as a topological space, namely, its geometric
realization (see [28] for details). For terminologies related to algebraic topology, we refer to [23].

2.3. Discrete Morse theory. Discrete Morse theory was introduced by Robin Forman [18] in 1998
as a combinatorial adaptation of classical Morse theory. For more details, we refer the reader to [25,
Chapter 4] and [28, Chapter 11], which serve as the primary sources for the definitions and results in
this section.
   Throughout this section, let X be a set and let Σ be a finite family of finite subsets of X.
Definition 2.1. A matching on Σ is a family M of pairs {σ, τ } with σ, τ ∈ Σ such that no set is
contained in more than one pair in M.
  A set σ ∈ Σ is said to be matched in M if it is contained in some pair of M; for convenience,
we write σ ∈ M. Otherwise, σ is said to be critical or unmatched with respect to M, and we write
σ∈/ M.
Definition 2.2. A matching M is said to be partial if {σ, τ } ∈ M implies either σ ≺ τ (i.e., σ ⊂ τ
and no ρ ∈ Σ satisfies σ ⊂ ρ ⊂ τ ), or σ ≻ τ .
   Note that M is a partial matching on Σ if and only if there exists Σ′ ⊂ Σ and an injective map
µ : Σ′ → Σ \ Σ′ such that µ(σ) ≻ σ for all σ ∈ Σ′ .
Definition 2.3. A partial matching M on Σ is called acyclic if there does not exist a cycle
              µ(σ1 ) ≻ σ1 ≺ µ(σ2 ) ≻ σ2 ≺ µ(σ3 ) ≻ σ3 ≺ · · · ≺ µ(σt ) ≻ σt ≺ µ(σ1 ), t ≥ 2.
Definition 2.4. Let x ∈ X. A matching Mx is called an element matching on Σ using x if every
pair in Mx is of the form {σ \ {x}, σ ∪ {x}} for some σ ∈ Σ.
    It is easy to observe that any element matching is a partial matching.

Theorem 2.7 ([25, Theorem 4.14]). Let ∆ be a simplicial complex and let M be an acyclic matching
