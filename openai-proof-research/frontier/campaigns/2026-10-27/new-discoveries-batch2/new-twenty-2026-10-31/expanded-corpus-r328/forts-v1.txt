                                                                                            Abstract

                                                         In 2008, the zero forcing number of a graph was shown to be an upper bound on the
                                                     graph’s maximum nullity. In 2018, the concept of forts was introduced to provide a set
                                                     covering characterization of zero forcing sets. Since then, forts have been integrated
                                                     into integer programming models for zero forcing and have been used to yield bounds
                                                     on the zero forcing number. In 2025, researchers explored the combinatorial question
                                                     of how many minimal forts a graph can have. They demonstrated that the number
                                                     of minimal forts in a graph with an order of at least six is strictly less than Sperner’s
                                                     bound. Moreover, the authors derived an explicit formula for the number of minimal
                                                     forts in path, cycle, and spider graphs. For example, the number of minimal forts in a
                                                     path graph aligns with the Padovan sequence, and thus exhibits exponential growth.
                                                     In this article, we demonstrate that no tree with an order of n can have fewer than
                                                     n/3 minimal forts. Additionally, we provide a characterization of the trees that possess
                                                     exactly n/3 minimal forts. We conjecture that the n/3 bound is valid for all graphs of
                                                     order n. We present experimental evidence supporting this conjecture and prove that
                                                     it holds for all Eulerian graphs.

                                                     Keywords: Zero Forcing; Minimal Forts; Trees

                                           1         Introduction
                                           Zero forcing is a binary coloring game played on a graph, where a set of filled vertices can
                                           force non-filled vertices to become filled according to a color change rule. A zero forcing set is
                                           a set of filled vertices that can force the entire graph; the zero forcing number is the minimum
                                           cardinality of a zero forcing set. In 2007, researchers Burgarth and Giovannetti independently
                                           developed the concept of zero forcing for the control of quantum systems, referring to it as
                                           graph infection [6]. The following year, it was demonstrated that the zero forcing number
                                           serves as an upper bound on the maximum nullity of a real symmetric matrix associated with
                                           a graph [12]. It was also established in 2008 that the combinatorial optimization problem

for the zero forcing number is NP-hard since the corresponding decision problem was shown
to be NP-complete [1].
     In 2014, the concept of failed zero forcing sets was introduced [14]. These are subsets of
filled vertices that do not have the ability to force the entire graph. The failed zero forcing
number represents the maximum size of such a set. The literature has shown considerable
interest in failed zero forcing, covering topics such as extreme values, the impact of graph
operations, and the NP-hardness of the related combinatorial optimization problem [8, 14,
10, 11, 15, 17, 18]. More recently, in 2025, the concept of well-failed graphs was defined [13].
These are graphs where every maximal failed zero forcing set is also a maximum failed zero
forcing set. A characterization of all well-failed trees was also provided.
     In 2018, the concept of forts in a graph was introduced [9]. A fort is a non-empty subset
of vertices such that no vertex outside this set has exactly one neighbor within it. Forts have
a close relationship with failed zero forcing sets, since a subset is a minimal fort if and only
if its complement is a maximal failed zero forcing set. Consequently, the NP-hardness of
identifying a maximum failed zero forcing set implies the NP-hardness of finding a minimum
fort within a graph. Additionally, a subset of vertices from a graph is a zero forcing set if
and only if it intersects every fort of the graph; thus, the zero forcing sets of a graph form
a set cover for the forts of the graph. This relationship has been applied in computational
methods for determining the minimum zero forcing set of a graph [3, 4].
     In 2025, researchers explored the combinatorial question of how many minimal forts a
graph can have. They demonstrated that the number of minimal forts in a graph with an
order of at least six is strictly less than Sperner’s bound. This bound was established by
Emanuel Sperner in 1928, and applies to the size of a collection of subsets in which no subset
includes another [5]. Moreover, the authors derived an explicit formula for the number of
minimal forts in path, cycle, and spider graphs. For example, the number of minimal forts
in a path graph aligns with the Padovan sequence, and thus exhibits exponential growth.
     In this article, we demonstrate that no tree with an order of n can have fewer than n/3
minimal forts. Additionally, we provide a characterization of the trees that possess exactly
n/3 minimal forts. We conjecture that the n/3 bound is valid for all graphs of order n.
We present experimental evidence supporting this conjecture and prove that it holds for all
Eulerian graphs.


2     Preliminaries
Throughout this article, we let G denote the set of all finite simple unweighted graphs. For
each G ∈ G, we have G = (V, E), where V is the vertex set, E is the edge set, and {u, v} ∈ E
if and only if u ̸= v and there is an edge between vertices u and v. If the context is not clear,
we use V (G) and E(G) to specify the vertex set and edge set of G, respectively. The order
of G is denoted by n = |V | and the size of G is denoted by m = |E|; when m = 0, we refer
to G as the empty graph.
    Let G ∈ G. Given u ∈ V , we define the neighborhood of u as N (u) = {v ∈ V : {u, v} ∈ E}.
We refer to every v ∈ N (u) as a neighbor of u, and we say that u and v are adjacent. If the
context is not clear, we use NG (u) to denote the neighborhood of u in the graph G. The
closed neighborhood of u is defined by N [u] = N (u) ∪ {u}. If u, v ∈ V satisfy N (u) = N (v),

                                               2
then we say that u and v are twins. The degree of u is denoted by d(u) = |N (u)|; when
d(u) = 0, we refer to u as an isolated vertex, when d(u) = 1, we refer to u as a pendant
vertex, and when d(u) ≥ 3, we refer to u as a junction vertex. The minimum degree of G is
denoted by δ(G) = min{d(u) : u ∈ V }.
     The graph G′ = (V ′ , E ′ ) is a subgraph of G if V ′ ⊆ V and E ′ ⊆ E. Given W ⊆ V , the
subgraph induced by W is the subgraph of G made up of the vertices in W and the edges
{u, v} ∈ E such that u, v ∈ W . We reference this subgraph as an induced subgraph and
denote it by G[W ]. If G[W ] is empty, then W forms an independent set. Furthermore, W is
a maximal independent set if W is not contained in another independent set.
     Given vertices u, v ∈ V , we say that u is connected to v if there exists a list of distinct
vertices (w0 , w1 , . . . , wl ) such that u = w0 , v = wl , {wi , wi+1 } ∈ E for all i = 1, . . . , l − 1,
and wi ̸= wj for all i ̸= j. Such a list of vertices is called a path; in particular, we reference
it as a (u, v)-path. The connected to relation is an equivalence relation, which partitions
the vertex set V into disjoint subsets: W1 , W2 , . . . , Wk , where for all i = 1, . . . , k and for all
u, v ∈ Wi , u is connected to v.
     For each i = 1, . . . , k, the induced subgraph G[Wi ] is called a connected component of
G. The graph G is connected if it has exactly one connected component. Given u ∈ V ,
the graph G − u is the induced subgraph G[V \ {u}]. Also, given {u, v} ∈ E, the graph
G − {u, v} is the subgraph of G obtained by deleting the edge {u, v}. If G − u has more
connected components than G, then u is a cut vertex of G. Similarly, if G − {u, v} has more
connected components than G, then {u, v} is a cut edge of G.
     A pendant generalized star or spider graph is a tree with exactly one junction vertex. We
let Sl1 ,...,lk denote a spider graph with junction (or central ) vertex v and pendant vertices
u1 , . . . , uk where k ≥ 3. For i = 1, . . . , k, li is the length of the path from v to ui , and we
often say li is the ith pendant length. We call the path between the central vertex v and
some pendant vertex as a pendant path. For convenience, we will also refer to path graphs
as spider graphs with exactly two pendant paths, though such spider graphs do not have a
junction vertex.
     Zero forcing is a binary coloring game on a graph, where vertices are either filled or
non-filled. In this article, we denote filled vertices by the color gray and non-filled vertices
by the color white. An initial set of gray vertices can force white vertices to become gray
following a color change rule. While there are many color change rules, see [16, Chapter 9],
we will use the standard rule which states that a gray vertex u can force a white vertex v
if v is the only white neighbor of u. Since the vertex set is finite, there comes a point in
which no more forcings are possible. If at this point all vertices are gray, then we say that
the initial set of gray vertices is a zero forcing set of G; otherwise, we refer to the initial
set of gray vertices as a failed zero forcing set of G. The zero forcing number of G, denoted
Z(G), is the minimum cardinality of a zero forcing set of G.
     Given a graph G ∈ G, a non-empty subset F ⊆ V is a fort if no vertex u ∈ V \ F has
exactly one neighbor in F . Note that the zero forcing sets of G form a set cover for the
forts of G; in particular, Theorem 2.1 states that a subset of vertices is a zero forcing set if
and only if that subset intersects every fort. While one direction of this result was originally
proven in [9, Theorem 3], both directions are shown in [3, Theorem 8].

Theorem 2.1 (Theorem 8 in [3]). Let G ∈ G. Then, S ⊆ V is a zero forcing set of G if

and only if S intersects every fort in FG .

    It is worth noting that Theorem 2.1 holds for the minimal forts of G, where a fort F is
minimal if every proper subset of F is not a fort of G. Also, a maximal failed zero forcing set
of G is a failed zero forcing set S such that every proper superset of S is a zero forcing set
of G. The following theorem establishes a connection between minimal forts and maximal
failed zero forcing sets.

Theorem 2.2 (Theorem 10 in [2]). Let G ∈ G. Then, F ⊆ V is a minimal fort of G if and
only if S = V \ F is a maximal failed zero forcing set of G.

    Given a graph G = (V, E), we let FG denote the set of minimal forts of G. If v ∈ V \ F ,
for all F ∈ FG , then v is called a fort irrelevant vertex of G. Similarly, a vertex v ∈ V is zero
forcing irrelevant if it not a member of any minimal zero forcing set of G, where a minimal
zero forcing set is a zero forcing set S such that every proper subset of S is a failed zero
forcing set. Note that, by Theorem 2.2, fort irrelevant vertices of G are those vertices that
are in every maximal failed zero forcing set. Furthermore, the following theorem establishes
that fort irrelevant vertices are exactly those that are zero forcing irrelevant.

Theorem 2.3 (Theorem 2.4 in [13]). Let G = (V, E) be a graph and let v ∈ V . Then, v is
fort irrelevant if and only if v is zero forcing irrelevant.


3     Properties of Minimal Forts of Trees
In this section, we present some results on the structures of minimal forts of trees. The first
two results hold more generally for any pair of connected graphs.

Proposition 3.1. Let G1 , G2 ∈ G, and let v1 ∈ V (G1 ) be fort-irrelevant. For some v2 ∈
V (G2 ), if G is the graph formed with vertex set V (G) = V (G1 ) ∪ V (G2 ) and edge set E(G) =
E(G1 ) ∪ E(G2 ) ∪ {{v1 , v2 }}, then v1 is also fort-irrelevant in G.

Proof. It suffices to show that no minimal fort of G contains v1 . By way of contradiction,
assume there exists a minimal fort F of G that contains v1 . Let R = F ∩ V (G1 ) and
Q = F ∩ V (G2 ) (so F = R ∪ Q). Since v1 ∈ R, R is not a minimal fort of G1 . Therefore,
R is either a non-minimal fort or not a fort of G1 . If R is a non-minimal fort of G1 , R
contains a minimal fort F ′ of G1 . Since v1 ∈/ F ′ , F ′ ⊂ F would be a minimal fort of G,
contradicting the minimality of F . Otherwise, if R is not a fort of G1 , then there exists a
vertex w ∈ V (G1 ) \ R such that |N (w) ∩ R| = 1. Since u ∈ R, we have w ̸= u, and it follows
that F is not a fort.

   Next, we show that every minimal fort of a tree contains at least two leaves.

Proposition 3.3. Let T be a tree of order n ≥ 2, and let S ⊆ L(T ) where L(T ) is the set
of leaves of T . If |S| = |L(T )| − 1 or |S| = |L(T )|, then S is a zero forcing set of T .

Proof. Let T be a tree of order n ≥ 2. It suffices to show that any set S ⊆ L(T ) such that
|S| = |L(T )| − 1 is a zero forcing set of T . We proceed with induction on n. If n = 2, then
T is the path graph P2 with two leaves. Let S be one of the leaves of P2 . Then S is a zero
forcing set of P2 with |S| = 1, and our base case is satisfied. Now, let k ≥ 2 and assume
that for any tree T of order k, any set S ⊆ L(T ) such that |S| = |L(T )| − 1 is a zero forcing
set of T . To construct a tree T ′ of order k + 1, we append a vertex u to a vertex v ∈ V (T ).
If v is a leaf of T , let S = L(T ) \ {v} where |S| = |L(T )| − 1. By our inductive hypothesis,
S is a zero forcing set of T . Hence, v was forced at some step, and it follows that v will also
force u. Therefore, S is also zero forcing set of T ′ . Additionally, since v is a leaf of T and
u was appended to v, |L(T )| = |L(T ′ )|, whence our inductive step holds. Conversely, if v is
not a leaf of T , we construct the set S ′ = S ∪ {u} where S is a zero forcing set of T with
cardinality |S| = |L(T )| − 1. It follows that S ′ is a zero forcing set of T ′ with cardinality
S ′ = |L(T )|. Since |L(T ′ )| = |L(T )| + 1, we have |S ′ | = |L(T ′ )| − 1, and our inductive step
holds.

Proposition 3.4. Let T be a tree of order n ≥ 2. Then every minimal fort of T contains
at least two leaves.

Proof. By way of contradiction, assume there exists a minimal fort F of T that contains no
more than one leaf. Consider the set S = V (G) \ F ; since F contains no more than one leaf,
S contains all leaves of T or all but one leaf of T . It follows from Proposition 3.3 that S is a
zero forcing set of T . However, by Theorem 2.2, S is a maximal failed zero forcing set, and
we arrive at a contradiction.

    Fort-irrelevant vertices are characterized by star centers, and star centers are identified
through star removals. In particular, it was identified in [13] that a vertex of a tree is
fort-irrelevant if and only if it is a star center.
Definition 3.1 ([13]). Let G ∈ G. For a vertex v ∈ V (G), let L(v) denote all neighbors of
v that are leaves and let L[v] = {v} ∪ L(v). If G contains a vertex v such that |L(v)| ≥ 2,
then we can create a new graph G′ = G[V (G) \ L[v]]. We call the process of producing G′
from G a star removal. Note that G′ may not necessarily be connected.
Definition 3.2 (Definition 1.8 in [13]). Let G ∈ G. We define B0 to be the set of all vertices
of G such that for each v ∈ B0 , |L(v)| ≥ 2. We perform star removals for each vertex v ∈ B0
to create the graph G1 . Next, we define B1 to be the set of all vertices of G1 such that for
each v ∈ B1 , |L(v)| ≥ 2. We perform star removals for each vertex v ∈ B1 to create the
graph G2 . Continuing k times, we construct the graph Gk+1 and form sets B0 , . . . , Bk . The
vertices in B0 , . . . , Bk are star centers.
Lemma 3.7 (Corollary 2.5 in [13]). Let T be a tree. Then v ∈ V (T ) is fort-irrelevant if and
only if v is a star center of T .

Lemma 3.8. Let T be a tree and let v ∈ V (T ) be a fort-irrelevant vertex. Then |N (v)| ≥ 2
and there exists a minimal fort of T that contains exactly two neighbors of v.
Proof. Since v is fort-irrelevant, v cannot be a leaf of T , so |N (v)| ≥ 2. Additionally, Lemma
3.7 implies v is a star center. If v has two leaves, our result follows. Otherwise, let Ti be
the graph obtained after i ≥ 1 star removals (where T0 ≡ T ). Then for some j ≥ 1, v has
two leaves as neighbors on the tree Tj . Let w be one of the leaves of v on Tj . It follows
that w cannot also be a star center since the star removal on Tj−1 would have removed
w. Therefore, there exists a minimal fort F that includes w, whence F must contain two
neighbors of v.

5     On the Fort Number and Zero Forcing Number
In this section, we discuss results on the fort number and zero forcing number of graphs. As
defined in [7], the fort number of a graph G is the size of maximum possible collection of
disjoint minimal forts of G. In particular, the fort number provides a lower bound on the
zero forcing number; that is, ft(G) ≤ Z(G). We show that the number of minimal forts of a
graph also bounds the zero forcing number.

Proposition 5.1. Let G ∈ G. Then Z(G) ≤ |FG |.

Proof. By way of contradiction, assume that Z(G) > |FG |. Let the minimum zero forcing
set of G be S. Now, we construct a set S ′ by selecting a vertex (not necessarily unique) from
each minimal fort in FG . By construction, S ′ intersects every minimal fort of G, so S ′ is a
zero forcing set. However, the cardinality of S ′ is given by |S ′ | ≤ |FG | whence |S ′ | < |S|,
contradicting the minimality of S.

    Proposition 5.1 implies that all known lower bounds for the zero forcing number also
provide a lower bound for the number of minimal forts. Additionally, we observed that every
graph of order n with |FG | = n/3 also exhibited the property that ft(G) = Z(G) = n/3.
In other words, when the number of minimal forts is exactly n/3, there exists a packing
of minimal forts such that every minimal fort is disjoint, and the graph can be forced by
selecting a vertex from each disjoint minimal fort. Now, we provide some results for when
either |FG | = ft(G) or |FG | = Z(G).

Proposition 5.2. Let G ∈ G. If |FG | = ft(G), then Z(G) = |FG | = ft(G).

                                               18
Proof. Since ft(G) ≤ Z(G) ≤ |FG |, if |FG | = ft(G), it immediately follows that Z(G) =
|FG | = ft(G).
   To show that |FG | = Z(G) implies ft(G) = |FG | = Z(G), we require the following lemma.

Lemma 5.3. Let G ∈ G, and let H = (FG , S, E) be a finite bipartite graph where FG is
the set of all minimal forts of G, S is a minimum zero forcing set of G, and E = {{v, F } |
v ∈ S and ∃F ∈ FG s.t. v ∈ F }. Then there exists an FG -perfect matching if and only if
|FG | = Z(G).

Proof. We first show the reverse direction. Assume that |FG | = Z(G). It suffices to show
that H satisfies Hall’s marriage condition; that is, a perfect matching exists if and only
if for every subset W ⊆ FG , |W | ≤ |N (W )|. The condition holds for W = FG since
|W | = |FG | ≤ |N (W )| = |S| = Z(G). By way of contradiction, assume for some W ⊂ FG
that |W | > |N (W )|. We define δ = |W | − |N (W )| > 0 and the nonempty set F ′ G = FG \ W .
Now, let R be an initially empty set and iteratively select one vertex (not necessarily unique)
from each minimal fort F ∈ F ′ G and insert it into R. After the final iteration, R has
cardinality |R| ≤ |FG \ W |. Next, we define the set S ′ = R ∪ N (W ) and note that S ′
intersects every minimal fort of G, whence S ′ is a zero forcing set. In particular, S ′ has
cardinality

                                 |S ′ | = |R ∪ N (W )|
                                        ≤ |R| + |N (W )|
                                        ≤ |FG \ W | + |N (W )|
                                        = |FG \ W | + (|W | − δ)
                                        = |FG | − δ
                                        = |S| − δ
                                        < |S|.

Therefore, S ′ is a zero forcing set with cardinality smaller than S, and we arrive at a contra-
diction. Hence, no such set W exists, and it follows that H satisfies Hall’s marriage condition.

For the forward direction, we proceed by contraposition. Assume that |FG | ̸= Z(G). Propo-
sition 5.1 implies |FG | > Z(G). Let S be the minimum zero forcing set of G. Then Hall’s
marriage condition fails for W = FG :

                            |W | = |FG | > |N (W )| = |S| = Z(G).

Therefore, no perfect matching exists for H.

Proposition 5.4. Let G ∈ G. If |FG | = Z(G), then ft(G) = |FG | = Z(G).

Proof. Since |FG | = Z(G), Lemma 5.3 allows us to construct a set S by selecting a distinct
vertex from each minimal fort. By construction, S intersects every minimal fort and has
cardinality |S| = |FG | = Z(G), so S is a minimum zero forcing set. To show that ft(G) =
|FG |, it suffices to show that no two minimal forts intersect. By way of contradiction, assume

                                               19
for some F, F ′ ∈ FG that F ∩ F ′ ̸= ∅. Let S ∩ F = {v}, S ∩ F ′ = {v ′ }, and u ∈ F ∩ F ′ . In
particular, the set constructed using Lemma 5.3 implies that v ̸= v ′ . Then (S \ {v, v ′ }) ∪ {u}
intersects every minimal fort of G, whence S ′ is a zero forcing set with cardinality smaller
than S. Therefore, S ′ contradicts the minimality of S, so no such minimal forts exist.
   In particular, we have the following characterizations for trees where |FT | = n/3.
Theorem 5.5. Let T be a tree of order n = 3k for some k ∈ N. Then ft(T ) = Z(T ) = k if
and only if T = H ◦ E2 for some tree H of order k.
Proof. For the forward direction, suppose that ft(T ) = Z(T ) = k. Then T has a maximum
collection of disjoint forts F = {F1 , F2 , . . . , Fk }. Additionally, T has a minimum path cover
P = {P1 , P2 , . . . , Pk }. Let ℓ(T ) denote the number of leaves of T . By Proposition 3.4,
every fort of T contains at least two leaves. Since the forts of F are disjoint, ℓ(T ) ≥ 2k.
Additionally, each path in P contains at most two leaves of T , whence ℓ(T ) ≤ 2k. It follows
that ℓ(T ) = 2k. Since ℓ(T ) = 2k and P is a path cover of T , each path in P contains exactly
two leaves. Furthermore, no path in P can be disconnected from T , whence every path in
P has order at least 3. Since T has order n = 3k, it follows that every path in P has order
exactly 3. Therefore, for each i ∈ {1, 2, . . . , k}, we write the vertex set of Pi as

                                         V (Pi ) = {xi , yi , zi }

and the edge set of Pi as
                                    E(Pi ) = {{xi , yi }, {yi , zi }},
where the leaves xi , zi are also leaves of T . Since T is connected, each yi is adjacent to a yj
where i ̸= j. We define H to be the tree with vertex set

                                    V (H) = {yi |≤ 1 ≤ i ≤ k}

and edge set
                               E(H) = {{yi , yj } | {yi , yj } ∈ E(T )}.
Then T = H ◦ E2 .

For the reverse direction, suppose that T = H ◦ E2 for some tree H of order k. Let

                                         ft(T ) ≤ Z(T ) ≤ k.

Moreover, for each i ∈ {1, . . . , k}, Fi = {xi , yi } is a minimal fort of T . Since the forts Fi , Fj
are disjoint for any i ̸= j, it follows that

                                         ft(T ) = Z(T ) = k.

Theorem 5.7. Let T be a tree of order n = 3m for some m ∈ N. Then |FT | = m if and
only if T = H ◦ E2 for some tree H of order m.
Proof. Let H be a tree of order m. The reverse direction is straightforward: if T = H ◦ E2 ,
then |FT | = n/3, where each minimal fort is a pair of leaves that share the same junction
vertex. For the forward direction, we proceed by contraposition. Assume that T ̸= H ◦ E2 ;
we must show that |FT | > m = n/3. Since T ̸= H ◦ E2 , for some vertex v ∈ V (T ), v does
not have two leaves. Now, we iteratively remove spiders from T until we are left with only a
spider Sl1 ,...,lk of order s that contains v. Since v does not have two leaves, Sl1 ,...,lk ̸= S1,1 . It
follows from Lemma 5.6 that the number of minimal forts of Sl1 ,...,lk is strictly greater than
s/3. Next, we reconstruct the tree T by adding each spider we have previously removed.
In each iteration, we repeat the constructions used in the proof of Theorem 4.1. However,
since |FSl1 ,...,lk | > s/3, inequality (1) in the proof of Theorem 4.1 becomes a strict inequality.
Since the inequality is strict for each iteration, it follows that |FT | > n/3.
Corollary 5.8. Let T be a tree of order n = 3m for some m ∈ N. Then |FT | = m if and
only if ft(T ) = Z(T ) = m.


6     Concluding Remarks
In this paper, we investigated the structures of minimal forts of trees, the number of minimal
forts of trees, and relationships between the fort number, the zero forcing number, and the
number of minimal forts of graphs. In particular, we have shown that the number of minimal
forts of a tree T of order n satisfies |FT | ≥ n/3. Notably, this bound can be demonstrated
despite having almost zero information about the structure of the tree itself. We believe that
this bound also holds for graphs with cycles, and we have tested this computationally for all
graphs of order up to n = 10. Given the number of graphs of orders above 10, it becomes
impractical to enumerate the minimal forts for each graph. Nevertheless, we will formally
state this conjecture now.

                                                   21
Conjecture 6.1. Let G ∈ G be a graph of order n. Then |FG | ≥ n/3.

     The difficulty of the problem lies in the fact that the order of a graph provides very
little information on its structure. In trees, this difficulty can be alleviated since every tree
contains a spider graph. No such structures exist for graphs in general. However, the bound
can be demonstrated for other more structured families of graphs. For example, the bound
also holds for Eulerian graphs, which we prove here. Other families of graphs we believe are
worth considering include planar graphs and bipartite graphs.

Theorem 6.2. Let G ∈ G be an Eulerian graph of order n. Then the number of minimal
forts of G satisfies
                                          n
                                   |FG | ≥ .
                                          3
Proof. We first note that for any n ≥ 3, the number of minimal forts of the cycle graph Cn
satisfies |FCn | ≥ n/3. For any n, let v ∈ V (Cn ) and let |ḞCn | denote the minimal forts of Cn
that do not contain v. Then |ḞCn | = |FPn−1 |, since if v is not in the fort, its neighbors must
be in the fort.

A graph G is Eulerian if and only if it can be decomposed into edge-disjoint cycles. Let
G be a Eulerian graph with k edge-disjoint cycles. We proceed with induction on k. If
k = 1, we have a cycle graph and our result follows. Now, let G′ be an Eulerian graph
of order n′ that can be decomposed into k edge-disjoint cycles. Let Cn be a cycle graph
on n vertices and let v ∈ V (Cn ) and v ′ ∈ V (G′ ). To construct an Eulerian graph with
k + 1 edge-disjoint cycles, we perform a vertex sum to form the graph G by combining
the vertices v, v ′ into one vertex u. In particular, G has order m = n + n′ − 1, vertex set
V (G) = (V (Cn )\v)∪(V (G′ )\v ′ )∪{u}, and edge set E(G) = (E(Cn )\Sv )∪(E(G′ )\Sv′ )∪Su ,
where Sv denotes all edges of Cn incident on v, Sv′ denotes all edges of G′ incident on v ′ , and
Su = {{u, w} | w ∈ NCn (v) ∪ NG′ (v ′ )}. Let ḞG′ denote the set of all minimal forts of G′ that
do not contain v ′ and let F̊G′ denote the set of all minimal forts of G′ that contains v ′ . For
each minimal fort F ′ ∈ F˙G′ , F = (F ′ \ {v ′ }) ∪ {u} is a minimal fort of G. For each minimal
fort F ′ ∈ F̊G′ , F = (F ′ \ {v ′ }) ∪ (FC \ {v}) ∪ {u} is a minimal fort of G, where FC is any
minimal fort of Cn that includes v. Additionally, every minimal fort F ∈ ḞCn is a minimal
fort of G. It follows that

                           |FG | ≥ |FG′ | + |ḞCn | = |FG′ | + |FPn−1 |.

From our inductive hypothesis, |FG′ | ≥ n′ /3. Additionally, from Proposition 4.3, |FPn−1 | ≥
(n − 1)/3. It follows that

                                       n′ n − 1   n′ + n − 1  m
                            |FG | ≥      +      =            = ,
                                       3    3          3      3
and our inductive step is satisfied.
    In this paper, we have also discussed trees with exactly n/3 minimal forts and provided
a few characterizations for such trees. In fact, graphs with cycles also attain the n/3 lower
bound. Furthermore, the structures of such graphs are much more varied. We provide three

examples of graphs (each of order n = 9) with exactly 3 minimal forts in Figure 5. Each
graph also exhibits the property that ft(G) = Z(G) = n/3. Indeed, for every graph where

state this observation as follows.

Conjecture 6.3. Let G ∈ G. If |FG | = n/3, then Z(G) = n/3.

   We have also shown that the converse of Conjecture 6.3 holds for trees. However, the
converse is false in general: for example, the cycle graph on 6 vertices C6 has ft(C6 ) =
Z(C6 ) = 2, but |FC6 | = 5.
