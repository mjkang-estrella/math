20:                                           Abstract
21:                                           Code loops are Moufang loops constructed from doubly even binary codes.
22:                                           Then, given a code loop L, we ask which doubly even binary code V produces
23:                                           L. In this sense, V is called a representation of L. In this article we define
24:                                           and determine all minimal and reduced representations of nonassociative
25:                                           code loops of rank 3 and 4.
26:                                           Keywords: binary codes, code loops, characteristic vectors, representations.

80:     So far we’ve shown how to build code loops from doubly even binary
81: codes, but we would like to investigate to the other side. In other words,
82: given a certain code loop L, we would like to determine the doubly even
83: codes V such that L ≃ L(V ).
84:     A representation of a given code loop L is a doubly even code V ⊆ K m
85: such that L ≃ L(V ) and the number m is called degree of the representation.
86: We notice that there are many different representations for a same code loop.

13:                                                     For a non-decreasing sequence S = (s1 , s2 , . . . , sk ), an S-packing coloring
14:                                                  of a graph G is a vertex coloring using the colors s1 , s2 , . . . , sk such that any
15:                                                  two vertices assigned the same color si are at distance greater than si . A
16:                                                  subcubic graph is said to be k-saturated, for 0 ≤ k ≤ 3, if every vertex of
17:                                                  degree 3 is adjacent to at most k vertices of degree 3. The local girth of
18:                                                  a vertex is the length of the smallest cycle containing it. Brešar, Kuenzel,
19:                                                  and Rall [Discrete Math. 348(8) (2025), 114477] proved that every claw-
20:                                                  free cubic graph is (1, 1, 2, 2)-packing colorable, confirming the conjecture
21:                                                  for this family. Equivalently, a claw-free cubic graph is one in which each
22:                                                  3-vertex has local girth 3. Motivated by this observation and by recent
23:                                                  progress on S-packing colorings of k-saturated subcubic graphs, we study the
24:                                                  influence of local girth on their S-packing colorability. We establish a series
25:                                                  of results describing how the parameters of saturation and local girth jointly
26:                                                  determine the admissible S-packing sequences. Sharpness is verified through
27:                                                  explicit constructions, and several open problems are posed to delineate the
28:                                                  remaining cases.

50: 1 Introduction
51: All graphs considered in this paper are finite, simple, and undirected. For a graph G, we
52: denote by V (G) and E(G) its vertex and edge sets, respectively. For a vertex x ∈ V (G),
53: we denote by N (x) the set of the neighbors of x and by N2 (x) the set of vertices at
54: distance exactly two from x. For a subset X ⊆ V (G), we write N [X] = X ∪ N (X)
55: for the closed neighborhood of X. The degree of a vertex x is denoted by dG (x) (or
56: simply d(x)), and we write ∆(G) and δ(G) for the maximum and minimum degrees of
57: G. A vertex v with d(v) = k is called a k-vertex. A graph G is said to be subcubic if
58: ∆(G) ≤ 3 and cubic if dG (v) = 3 for every vertex v. A subcubic graph is said to be
59: k-saturated, for 0 ≤ k ≤ 3, if every vertex of degree 3 is adjacent to at most k vertices of
60: degree 3. Equivalently, a 3-vertex in a k-saturated graph has at least 3 − k neighbors of
61: degree 2. In a 3-saturated subcubic graph, we call a 3-vertex heavy if all its neighbors are
62: 3-vertices. A 3-saturated subcubic graph is said to be (3, k)-saturated, for 0 ≤ k ≤ 3, if
63: each heavy vertex is adjacent to at most k heavy vertices. This classification, introduced
64: in [15, 16], provides a natural hierarchy of subcubic graphs according to the density of
65: adjacent 3-vertices and will serve as one of the structural parameters in our analysis. A
66: chord in a cycle is an edge joining two vertices that are not adjacent on the cycle.
67:    Given a non-decreasing sequence of positive integers S = (s1 , s2 , . . . , sk ), an S-packing
68: coloring of a graph G is a partition of V (G) into subsets V1 , V2 , . . . , Vk such that for
69: any two distinct vertices u, v ∈ Vi , we have distG (u, v) > si . The concept, introduced
70: by Goddard et al. [9], has since inspired intensive research, particularly on graphs of
71: maximum degree three (see the survey [2]). The smallest integer k such that G admits
72: a (1, 2, . . . , k)-packing coloring is called the packing chromatic number of G, denoted
73: χρ (G). This parameter was introduced by Goddard et al. [9] under the name broadcast
74: chromatic number, who showed that deciding whether χρ (G) ≤ 4 is NP-hard. The study
75: of bounding χρ (G) and χρ (S(G)), where S(G) denotes the graph obtained from G by
76: subdividing every edge, has been the subject of many works [1, 3, 4, 8, 11, 19]. The ques-
77: tion of whether χρ is bounded for subcubic graphs was answered negatively by Balogh,
78: Kostochka, and Liu [1], with explicit unbounded constructions later provided in [2].
79: However, Balogh, Kostochka, and Liu [1] proved that χρ (S(G)) ≤ 8 for every subcubic
80: graph G, while Gastineau and Togni [8] raised the question of whether χρ (S(G)) ≤ 5.
81: This conjecture was later supported by Brešar et al. [4], who proposed that χρ (S(G)) ≤ 5
82: holds for all subcubic graphs. Moreover, Gastineau and Togni [19] observed that if a
83: subcubic graph G is (1, 1, 2, 2)-packing colorable, then it satisfies χρ (S(G)) ≤ 5.
84:    Several significant results have been obtained on the S-packing coloring of subcubic
85: graphs. It was recently shown in [13] that every subcubic graph G admits a (1, 1, 2, 2, 3)-
86: packing coloring, which implies the general upper bound χρ (S(G)) ≤ 6 for this class.
87: Later, El Zein and Mortada [6] proved that every subcubic graph with minimum degree
88: less than three is (1, 1, 2, 2)-packing colorable, confirming that shorter S-sequences suffice
89: in the subcubic case. Their work also established that every connected cubic graph
90: admits a (1, 1, 2, 2, k)-packing coloring in which at most one vertex receives color k, for
91: any k ≥ 3, thereby improving the result of [13]. This line of research continues and
92: unifies earlier contributions by Mortada and Togni, who introduced and systematically
93:

99: 18], as well as by Mortada and El Zein [14], whose recent work further refined the
100: saturation framework. The present paper builds upon these foundations, combining the
101: saturation-based perspective with the newly introduced local girth parameter to obtain
102: a unified theory of S-packing colorability.
103:    Subsequently, Brešar, Kuenzel, and Rall [5] showed that every claw-free cubic graph
104: is (1, 1, 2, 2)-packing colorable. Their result, obtained through a detailed structural
105: decomposition, revealed the importance of local restrictions in achieving compact S-
106: packings and inspired the present investigation.
107:    The notion of local girth provides a natural parameter for describing the local cyclic
108: structure of a vertex. For a vertex v, its local girth, denoted g(v), is the length of the
109: shortest cycle containing v, and it is supposed to be ∞ if v belongs to no cycle. A
110: claw-free cubic graph is precisely a cubic graph in which every vertex has local girth 3.
111: Consequently, Brešar, Kuenzel, and Rall [5] admits a natural reformulation in terms of
112: local girth: Every cubic graph in which every vertex has local girth 3 is (1, 1, 2, 2)-packing
113: colorable.
114:    Motivated by this characterization, El Zein and Mortada [7] proved that every sub-
115: cubic graph such that each 3-vertex has local girth at most 4 is (1, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2)-packing
116: colorable, extending the claw-free case beyond the cubic setting.
117:    In this paper, we unify and extend these two directions—the study based on the
118: saturation level and that guided by the local girth—by analyzing the impact of lo-
119: cal girth on the S-packing coloring of k-saturated subcubic graphs. We develop a gen-
120: eral framework in which the local girth of 3-vertices governs the admissible packing
121: configurations within each saturation class. For a subcubic graph G, we denote by
122: g3 (G) = max{g(v) : dG (v) = 3} the local girth of 3-vertices, that is, the smallest length
123: of a cycle containing any 3-vertex. Our results cover and generalize several known cases
124: in a unified manner.

139: constructive nature of the most of these arguments naturally leads to explicit procedures
140: that can be formulated as efficient algorithms for producing the required S-packing

37:                                              The matching complexes were ﬁrst introduced by Bouc [4] in connection with Brown
38:                                           complexes and Quillen complexes. His main aim was to understand the homology groups
39:                                           of M1 (Kn ) as Sn representations, where Kn is the complete graph on n vertices and Sn

41:                                           characteristic of M1 (Kn ). Prior to Bouc’s work on M1 (Kn ), Garst [5], dealing with Tits
42:                                           coset complexes, introduced the chessboard complex, now known as 1-matching complex of
43:                                           complete bipartite graphs Km,n . He showed that M1 (Km,n ) is Cohen-Macaulay if and only
44:                                           if 2m − 1 ≤ n. For detailed survey of work done on these complexes, we refer the reader to

49:                                           [9], M1 (G) has the homotopy type of wedge of spheres, and in some cases, like Km,n for

87:    A graph is an ordered pair G = (V, E) where V is called the set of vertices and E ⊆ V × V ,
88: the set of unordered edges of G. The vertices v1 , v2 ∈ V are said to be adjacent, if (v1 , v2 ) ∈ E.
89: The number of vertices adjacent to a vertex v is called degree of v, denoted deg(v). If
90: deg(v) = 1, then v is called a leaf vertex. A vertex v is said to be adjacent to an edge e, if
91: v is an end point of e, i.e., e = (v, w). Two graphs G and H are called isomorphic, denoted
92: G∼ = H, if there exists a bijection, f : V (G) → V (H) such that (v, w) ∈ E(G) if and only if
93: (f (v), f (w)) ∈ E(H).
94:    A graph H with V (H) ⊆ V (G) and E(H) ⊆ E(G) is called a subgraph of the graph G.
95: For a nonempty subset H of E(G), the induced subgraph G[H], is the subgraph of G with
96: edges E(G[H]) = H and V (G[H]) = {a ∈ V (G) : a is adjacent to e for some e ∈ H}. For a
97: nonempty subset U of V (G), the induced subgraph G[U], is the subgraph of G with vertices
98: V (G[U]) = U and E(G[U]) = {(a, b) ∈ E(G) | a, b ∈ U}.
