Distance Reduction in Bouquet Decompositions
 and Toric Ideals of Graphs 
 
 
 Oliver Clarke
 
    
 Dimitra Kosta
 
    
 Alexander Milner
 

 Abstract 
 
 The distance-reduction property for a generating set, i.e., a Markov basis, of a toric ideal is a condition that ensures tight connectivity of its fibres. In this paper, we study the distance-reduction property for toric ideals of graphs and move on to explore the relationship between the distance-reduction property and the bouquet structure of homogeneous toric ideals, which includes the class of toric ideals of graphs. For toric ideals of graphs which are complete intersection, we show that the minimal Markov bases are distance-reducing if and only if they distance-reduce the circuits of the ideal. We then consider how the distance-reduction properties interact with the bouquet structure of the toric ideal. Bouquets are a combinatorial structure that capture the essential combinatorial information of the toric ideal. Under the condition of homogeneity, we show that, for toric ideals with the same bouquet structure and signature, the distance-reduction properties are preserved. For homogeneous toric ideals whose bouquet matrix is a monomial curve in 𝔸 3 \mathbb{A}^{3} , we give necessary and sufficient conditions for when the minimal Markov bases are distance-reducing.

 1 Introduction 

 
 A fundamental problem in Algebraic Statistics is the construction of Markov bases for toric ideals.
Given an integer matrix A A , a Markov basis corresponds to a minimal generating set of the toric ideal I A I_{A} , and its elements can be considered as moves of a Markov chain that connects any two points in the same fiber of A A (see [ 9 ] ).
The application of this Markov chain is in sampling from the space of so-called contingency tables, which may be used in Fisher’s exact test in statistical significance testing.
In practice, we not only want connectivity in the fibers but a quick convergence rate of the Markov chain as well, i.e., a low mixing time. 
 
 
 This is the idea behind the distance-reduction property , introduced by Aoki
and Takemura [ 2 , 1 ] , which requires a Markov basis M M to reduce a certain metric distance between points in a fiber. In this case, the diameter of the fibers can be bounded from above in terms of their fiber values [ 2 , Section 3.1] and these diameters can, in turn, be used to bound the time for Markov chains to reach their stationary distribution (see [ 9 ] or [ 8 ] ). In particular, we will focus on the metric given by the 1 1 -norm on the fibers, which is not only easy to compute, but also has nice properties when working with homogeneous toric ideals. 
 
 
 Understanding which Markov bases are distance-reducing is a subtle question.
By [ 6 ] , for a general integer matrix A A ,
checking whether a Markov basis M M is distance-reducing requires verifying the condition against every element of the Graver basis Gr ⁡ ( A ) {\rm Gr}(A) , the set of primitive lattice vectors in ker ℤ ⁡ ( A ) \ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(A) .
It follows immediately that the Graver basis is always distance-reducing, but it is typically orders of magnitude larger than any given Markov basis.
So, a natural goal is to identify structured families of toric ideals for which distance reduction can be certified by checking an easily accessible set of elements.
For instance, in this paper, we will show that the circuits of A A , which are the elements of I A I_{A} of inclusion-wise minimal support, are sufficient for testing distance reduction in the case of complete intersection toric ideals of graphs. 
 
 
 In this paper, we study the distance-reduction property from two perspectives. Firstly, we consider toric ideals of graphs and leverage their combinatorial characterisation to study the distance-reduction property of their Markov bases. Secondly, we consider how the bouquet structure of a homogeneous toric ideal affects the distance-reduction property of its Markov bases. Homogeneity is a unifying feature of both perspectives, as toric ideals of graphs are homogeneous. 
 
 
 Toric ideals of graphs. Toric ideals of graphs, their generating sets as well as several binomial sets of interest have been studied in a series of papers; the circuits of toric ideals associated to graphs were characterised by Villarreal in [ 22 ] , the Graver basis of the toric ideals of a graph was first investigated by Ohsugi and Hibi in [ 14 ] , while the explicit structure of its elements was later established by Reyes, Tatakis and Thoma in [ 17 ] . Moreover, in [ 13 ] De Loera, Sturmfels and Thomas determined the universal Gröbner basis for toric ideals of graphs with at most eight vertices, while Tatakis and Thoma gave a graph theoretical characterisation of the elements of the universal Gröbner basis of the toric ideal of any graph in [ 20 ] . 
 
 
 The incidence matrix A G A_{G} of a simple graph G G provides a natural class of 0 / 1 0/1 -matrices, each of whose columns contains exactly two ones.
When it comes to distance-reduction, toric ideals of graphs are the natural next class to study after the trivial case with exactly one 1 in each column, and before the full generality of matrices with at most three ones per column, where any bouquet structure can appear, see [ 16 ] .
Note that, since every column has exactly two ones, the toric ideal of A G A_{G} is homogeneous.
We note that, for a homogeneous ideal, by Proposition  2.5 , the distance-reduction condition simplifies to the following: an element z z is distance-reduced by z ^ \hat{z} 
if and only if z ^ + ≤ z + \hat{z}^{+}\leq z^{+} and Supp ⁡ ( z ^ − ) ∩ Supp ⁡ ( z − ) ≠ ∅ {\rm Supp}(\hat{z}^{-})\cap{\rm Supp}(z^{-})\neq\emptyset up to the signs of z ^ \hat{z} and z z . We exploit this simplification throughout. 
 
 
 In the setting of toric ideals of graphs, the Graver basis is in bijection with the primitive even closed walks of G G by [ 14 ] and [ 18 ] , and the circuits correspond precisely to the subset of even cycles and pairs of odd cycles either intersecting in exactly one vertex or being vertex-disjoint and joined by a path, see [ 22 , Proposition 4.2] .
Our first main result is about complete intersection toric ideals of graphs for which we prove the following. 
 
 
 Theorem (Theorem  3.14 ) . 
 
 Let G G be a simple connected graph such that I G I_{G} is a complete intersection,
and let M M be a minimal Markov basis for I G I_{G} . Then M M is distance-reducing if
and only if M M distance-reduces the circuits of A G A_{G} . 
 
 
 
 The statement of Theorem 3.14 is motivated by [ 6 , Corollary 5.2] where it is shown that, for A ∈ ℤ 1 × n A\in\mathbb{Z}^{1\times n} , which gives rise to a monomial curve, such that I A I_{A} is a complete intersection toric ideal, a minimal Markov basis is distance-reducing if and only if it distance-reduces the circuits of A A . The proof of this result involves exploiting the specific form of minimal Markov bases of complete intersection monomial curves. Analogously, we prove Theorem 3.14 using a result by Tatakis and Thoma, restated in Theorem 3.12 , about the structure of minimal Markov bases of complete intersection toric ideals coming from graphs.
However, we suspect that the complete intersection hypothesis can be dropped from this theorem, see Conjecture  3.1 , which we have computationally verified for all graphs on at most 7 7 vertices and all graphs with 8 8 vertices and at most 16 16 edges. 
 
 
 We note that if we try to extend Conjecture  3.1 to all toric ideals arising from 0 / 1 0/1 -matrices, then the statement is immediately false.  Bouquet decompositions. The bouquet structure of a toric ideal, introduced by Petrović, Thoma, and Vladoiu in [ 15 ] , partitions the columns of A A into subsets called bouquets .
Each of these bouquets is said to be free, non-mixed, or mixed according to its signature .
This decomposition captures a great deal of the essential combinatorial structure of I A I_{A} .
For instance, it is known that two matrices that share the same bouquet ideal and signature have isomorphic sets of circuits, Graver bases, indispensable elements and even minimal Markov bases, with an explicit bijection between them (see [ 15 , 12 ] ).
We investigate the natural question of whether the distance-reduction property is also an invariant of the bouquet ideal and signature. 
 
 
 We show that the answer is no in general. See Example  4.1 , which exhibits two matrices with the same bouquet ideal and signature, each of which has two minimal Markov bases. But, for one of these matrices both minimal Markov bases are distance-reducing while for the other matrix only one is distance-reducing. This difference arises from the observation that the bijection does not preserve the 1 1 -norms of elements in fibers.
However, we show that imposing homogeneity on both toric ideals completely removes this obstruction. 
 
 
 Theorem (Theorem  4.9 ) . 
 
 Let I A 1 , I A 2 I_{A_{1}},I_{A_{2}} be homogeneous T ω T_{\omega} -robust ideals for some integer matrix T T and signature ω \omega . Then, I A 1 I_{A_{1}} and I A 2 I_{A_{2}} have the same distance-reducing properties: for z ^ , z ∈ ker ℤ ⁡ ( A 1 ) \hat{z},z\in\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(A_{1}) , the element z z is distance-reduced by z ^ \hat{z} if and only if ϕ ⁡ ( z ) \phi(z) is distance-reduced by ϕ ⁡ ( z ^ ) \phi(\hat{z}) , where ϕ \phi is a certain bijection between the kernels. 
 
 
 
 For this result, we think of the distance-reduction property in two parts:
there is a divisibility condition on positive parts and an overlapping condition on supports of negative parts.
The proof of this theorem passes through two lemmas on the poset of signatures in which we track how
these two conditions change as we move through this poset.
In particular, we show that the divisibility condition is preserved moving up the poset,
while the support-overlap condition is preserved by moving down.
Therefore, both these conditions are preserved when we consider two toric ideals corresponding to the same element of the poset.
It is worth noting that even in the non-homogeneous case, the divisibility condition is still preserved moving up the poset. However, homogeneity allows us to work with this support-overlap condition instead of the more complicated 1-norm bound condition, which is not preserved moving down the poset in the non-homogeneous case. 
 
 
 We also give an intrinsic characterisation of homogeneity in terms of the bouquet data.
We show that an ideal I A I_{A} is homogeneous if and only if the vector 𝐜 ¯ = ( 𝐜 ¯ 1 , … , 𝐜 ¯ s ) \mathbf{\bar{c}}=(\mathbf{\bar{c}}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{\bar{c}}_{s}) of column-sums of the bouquet-index-encoding vectors lies in the row span of the bouquet matrix A B A_{B} . See Theorem  4.12 . This gives an efficient way to construct examples of homogeneous T ω T_{\omega} -robust ideals and demonstrates that homogeneity depends only on the data of the bouquet matrix and the bouquet-index-encoding vectors. 
 
 
 Classification over A 3 A_{3} . Finally, we turn to a complete classification of distance reduction for toric ideals whose bouquet matrix is a monomial curve over 𝔸 3 \mathbb{A}^{3} given by a matrix T = ( n 1 n 2 n 3 ) T=\begin{pmatrix}n_{1}&n_{2}&n_{3}\end{pmatrix} .
In this setting, the ideal I T I_{T} and hence the original ideal, have exactly three circuits, whose structure is completely explicit and we prove the following. 
 
 
 Theorem (Theorem  4.18 ) . 
 
 Let T = ( n 1 n 2 n 3 ) ∈ ℤ > 0 3 T=\begin{pmatrix}n_{1}&n_{2}&n_{3}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{3} , ω ⊆ [ 3 ] \omega\subseteq[3] , and let A A be a homogeneous T ω T_{\omega} -robust matrix with minimal Markov basis M M . Then M M fails to be distance-reducing if and only if all three of the following conditions hold: ω = [ 3 ] \omega=[3] , the monomial curve T T is a complete intersection, and M M contains two elements that are both circuits. In this case, M M distance-reduces every element of Gr ⁡ ( A ) {\rm Gr}(A) except the unique circuit of A A not contained in M M .

 2 Background 

 
 We write ℕ = { 0 , 1 , 2 , … } \mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\dots\} for the set of non-negative integers and [ n ] := { 1 , 2 , … , n } [n]:=\{1,2,\dots,n\} . Given an integer vector z ∈ ℤ n z\in\mathbb{Z}^{n} , we define z + , z − ∈ ℕ n z^{+},z^{-}\in\mathbb{N}^{n} to be the unique non-negative integer vectors such that z = z + − z − z=z^{+}-z^{-} . Throughout, we use the 1 1 -norm on ℤ n \mathbb{Z}^{n} , which is written as ∥ ⋅ ∥ \lVert\cdot\rVert . For any vector z ∈ ℕ n z\in\mathbb{N}^{n} , we denote its support by Supp ⁡ ( z ) = { i ∈ [ n ] : z i ≠ 0 } \operatorname{Supp}(z)=\{i\in[n]:z_{i}\neq 0\} . We use ≤ \leq to denote the component-wise partial order on ℕ n \mathbb{N}^{n} that extends the natural order on ℕ \mathbb{N} explicitly given by 
 

 
 
 if  ​ u , v ∈ ℕ n ​  then  ​ u ≤ v ⇔ u i ≤ v i ​  for all  ​ i ∈ [ n ] . \text{if }u,v\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\text{ then }u\leq v\iff u_{i}\leq v_{i}\text{ for all }i\in[n]. 
 
 
 Equivalently, we have u ≤ v u\leq v if v − u ∈ ℕ n v-u\in\mathbb{N}^{n} .
Given a polynomial ring R = k ⁡ [ x 1 , … , x n ] R=k[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}] and a vector u ∈ ℕ n u\in\mathbb{N}^{n} , we use multi-index notation for monomials x u := x 1 u 1 ​ … ​ x n u n x^{u}:=x_{1}^{u_{1}}\dots x_{n}^{u_{n}} . 
 
 
 2.1 Toric ideals 

 
 Let A ∈ ℤ d × n A\in\mathbb{Z}^{d\times n} be an integer matrix and R = k ⁡ [ x 1 , … , x n ] R=k[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}] a polynomial ring over a field k k . Assume that ker ℤ ⁡ ( A ) ∩ ℕ n = { 0 } \ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)\cap\mathbb{N}^{n}=\{0\} . For each z ∈ ker ℤ ⁡ ( A ) z\in\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(A) , we write β ⁡ ( z ) = x z + − x z − \beta(z)=x^{z^{+}}-x^{z^{-}} for the binomial associated to z z . We define the toric ideal I A = ⟨ β ( z ) : z ∈ ker ℤ ( A ) ⟩ I_{A}=\langle\beta(z):z\in\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)\rangle .
We say that I A I_{A} (or A A ) is homogeneous if the vector 𝟏 n = ( 1 , 1 , … , 1 ) ∈ ℤ n \mathbf{1}_{n}=(1,1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n} appears in the row-span of A A . 
 
 
 The affine semigroup of A A is ℕ ​ A := { A ​ u ∈ ℤ d | u ∈ ℕ n } \mathbb{N}A:=\{Au\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\,|\,u\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\} , which is the semigroup generated by the columns of A A . Given a monomial x u ∈ R x^{u}\in R for some u ∈ ℕ n u\in\mathbb{N}^{n} , its A A -degree is defined to be the vector deg A ⁡ ( x u ) = A ​ u ∈ ℕ ​ A \deg_{A}(x^{u})=Au\in\mathbb{N}A .
For each b ∈ ℕ ​ A b\in\mathbb{N}A , the b b -fiber of A A is given by ℱ b := { u ∈ ℕ n | A ​ u = b } \mathcal{F}_{b}:=\{u\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\,|\,Au=b\} . 
 
 
 Circuits are irreducible binomials of a toric ideal I A I_{A} with minimal support. In vector notation, a vector 𝐮 ∈ ker ℤ ⁡ ( A ) {\bf u}\in\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(A) is called a circuit of the matrix A A if Supp ⁡ ( 𝐮 ) \operatorname{Supp}({\bf u}) is minimal and the components of 𝐮 {\bf u} are relatively prime. We write C ⁡ ( A ) ⊆ ker ℤ ⁡ ( A ) C(A)\subseteq\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(A) for the set of circuits of A A . 
 
 
 A Markov basis M ⊆ ker ℤ ⁡ ( A ) M\subseteq\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(A) for A A (or for I A I_{A} ) is a collection of vectors such that the set { β ⁡ ( z ) : z ∈ M } \{\beta(z):z\in M\} is a generating set of I A I_{A} . It is minimal if this generating set is minimal and we ignore the signs of the vectors. The intersection of all Markov bases for A A is the indispensable set , denoted by S ⁡ ( A ) S(A) , and the union of Markov bases is called the universal Markov basis , and is denoted by U ⁡ ( A ) U(A) . For ease of notation, a set of vectors in ker ℤ ⁡ ( A ) \ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(A) , such as a Markov basis, is denoted by a matrix whose rows are the set of vectors.
We recall the following forms of vector decomposition for exponents in toric ideals.

 •

A binomial x u − x v ∈ I A x^{u}-x^{v}\in I_{A} is called primitive if there is no other binomial x u ′ − x v ′ ∈ I A x^{u^{\prime}}-x^{v^{\prime}}\in I_{A} such that x u ′ | x u x^{u^{\prime}}|x^{u} and x v ′ | x v x^{v^{\prime}}|x^{v} .

primitive binomials is finite and coincides with Gr ⁡ ( I A ) \operatorname{Gr}(I_{A}) . Additionally, the circuits of A A are contained within the Graver basis of A A . We reference [ 18 , Chapter 4] for both of these results. 
 
 
 Semi-conformal decompositions allow us to give an equivalent characterisation of the indispensable set. 
 
 
 Proposition 2.2 ( [ 4 , Proposition 1.1] ) . 
 
 The set S ⁡ ( A ) S(A) of indispensable elements is equal to the subset of ker ℤ ⁡ ( A ) \ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(A) that do not admit a proper semi-conformal decomposition. 
 
 
 
 
 2.2 Distance reduction 

 
 The theory of distance reduction, introduced in [ 2 ] , is about studying 1 1 -norm distance between points in fibers of A A . In this section we recall the basic definitions and prove some small results that will be used throughout the paper. 
 
 
 Definition 2.3 . 
 
 For z ^ , z ∈ ker ℤ ⁡ ( A ) \hat{z},z\in\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(A) , we say that z z is distance-reduced by z ^ \hat{z} if 
 
 1. 
 
 z ^ + ≤ z + \hat{z}^{+}\leq z^{+} 
 
 2. 
 
 ‖ z − z ^ ‖ < ‖ z ‖ \|z-\hat{z}\|<\|z\| 
 
 
 up to the signs of z ^ \hat{z} and z z .
Explicitly, we require at least one of the following to hold: 
 
 •

 •

 • 
 
 z ^ − ≤ z + \hat{z}^{-}\leq z^{+} and ‖ z + z ^ ‖ < ‖ z ‖ \|z+\hat{z}\|<\|z\| , 
 
 • 
 
 z ^ − ≤ z − \hat{z}^{-}\leq z^{-} and ‖ z − z ^ ‖ < ‖ z ‖ \|z-\hat{z}\|<\|z\| . 
 
 
 We say that an element z ∈ ker ℤ ⁡ ( A ) z\in\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(A) is distance-irreducible if the only element of ker ℤ ⁡ ( A ) \ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(A) that distance-reduces z z is itself. We write D ⁡ ( A ) D(A) for the set of distance-irreducible elements.

Let b := A ​ z + = A ​ z − b:=Az^{+}=Az^{-} so z + z^{+} and z − z^{-} lie in the fiber ℱ b \mathcal{F}_{b} . Then, condition ( 1 ) of Definition 2.3 tells us that z ^ \hat{z} is “applicable” to z z i.e., applying the move z ^ \hat{z} to z + z^{+} keeps us in the fiber ℱ b \mathcal{F}_{b} . Furthermore, A ⁡ ( z + − z ^ ) = A ​ z + = b A(z^{+}-\hat{z})=Az^{+}=b since z ^ ∈ ker ℤ ⁡ ( A ) \hat{z}\in\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(A) so z ^ + − z ^ \hat{z}^{+}-\hat{z} lies in the fiber ℱ b \mathcal{F}_{b} . Then, rewriting condition ( 2 ) of Definition 2.3 , ∥ ( z + − z ^ ) − z − ∥ < ∥ z + − z − ∥ \lVert(z^{+}-\hat{z})-z^{-}\rVert<\lVert z^{+}-z^{-}\rVert , this is telling us that after applying the move z ^ \hat{z} to z + z^{+} , our new position in the fiber, which is z + − z ^ z^{+}-\hat{z} , is closer to z − z^{-} than z + z^{+} was.  3 Toric ideals of graphs 

 
 In this section we study distance reduction for toric ideals of graphs. For us, a graph means a finite simple undirected graph, i.e., it has no loops or multi-edges. Given a graph G G , its corresponding incidence matrix A G A_{G} has columns indexed by edges e ∈ E ⁡ ( G ) e\in E(G) , rows indexed by vertices i ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) i\in V(G) , and entries A i , e = 1 A_{i,e}=1 if i ∈ e i\in e and A i , e = 0 A_{i,e}=0 if i ∉ e i\notin e . We note that every column of A G A_{G} contains two ones. Thus, if we add up all the rows and divide by 2, we get the vector 𝟏 n \mathbf{1}_{n} meaning A G A_{G} is homogeneous. Thus, throughout this section, we will use the restatement of the distance-reduction condition, namely Proposition 2.5 . We will write the toric ideal corresponding to a graph as I G := I A G I_{G}:=I_{A_{G}} which now lives in the ring R = k ⁡ [ e | e ∈ E ⁡ ( G ) ] R=k[e\,|\,e\in E(G)] . We note that supports of vectors are now sets of edges of G G . 
 
 
 In Section  3.1 , we introduce some notation and state some key results about toric ideals of graphs.
Then, in Section  3.2 we prove the following conjecture in the case that I G I_{G} is a complete intersection toric ideal. 
 
 
 First we recall that, for a integer matrix A A , the circuits of A A have been shown to characterise the distance reduction property for complete intersection monomial curves [ 6 , Corollary 5.2] . We conjecture that the same holds for toric ideals of graphs. 
 
 
 Conjecture 3.1 . 
 
 Let G G be a simple graph and M M a minimal Markov basis for I G I_{G} . Then M M is distance-reducing if and only if M M reduces the distance of the circuits of A G A_{G} .

 Suppose G G is a bipartite graph. Then Conjecture  3.1 holds for G G . 
 
 
 
 Proof.

Since the set of circuits is equal to the Graver basis then we immediately have that M M is distance-reducing if and only if M M distance-reduces the circuits of A G A_{G} .

Let G G be a graph and w w an even closed walk of G G . Then, the walk w w is primitive if and only if

 The primitivity of an even closed walk w w of G G restricts the amount of times each vertex of G G can be visited by w w . We will need this result for Theorem 3.14 so we provide a proof. 
 
 
 Lemma 3.11 . 
 
 Let G G be a graph and w w a primitive even closed walk of G G . Then, each vertex of G G is visited at most twice by w w and exactly twice if and only if it is a cut vertex of 𝐰 \mathbf{w} . 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Since w w is primitive, we can use conditions ( 1 ), ( 2 ) and ( 3 ) of Theorem 3.10 . 
 
 
 We first note that if a vertex v v in the walk w w is not a cut vertex of 𝐰 \mathbf{w} , then it is only visited once by w w . To see this, we notice that v v only appears in one block B B of w w . Since both vertices in a block which is a cut edge are cut vertices then, by condition ( 1 ), B B is a cycle. In addition, w w only traverses edges in B B once otherwise, by condition ( 2 ), the edge would be a cut edge and be its own block. Thus, since v v only appears in the block B B , v v is only visited once by w w . 
 
 
 We now prove the first part of the statement. Suppose there is a vertex of G G , v v , which is visited at least 3 3 times by w w . By our previous observation then v v is a cut vertex of 𝐰 \mathbf{w} . Thus, by condition ( 3 ), v v belongs to exactly 2 2 blocks of w w , say B B and B ′ B^{\prime} , and is a sink of both. Since v v is a sink, any time w w visits v v , it switches the block it is travelling through. Consider the set of edges of B B adjacent to v v . The number of times each is visited by w w must add up to at least 3 3 . If one of them is a double edge of w w then, by condition ( 2 ), the block B B is that cut edge in which case this sum would be 2 2 . By condition ( 2 ), each edge of w w is traversed at most twice, so each edge of B B adjacent to v v is only traversed at most once by w w . Thus, there must be at least 3 3 edges of B B which are adjacent to v v . This contradicts condition ( 1 ) since then B B is not a cycle or a cut edge. Thus, any vertex of G G is visited at most twice by w w . 
 
 
 We now prove the second part of the statement. The ( ⇒ \Rightarrow ) direction follows by our observation that, if v v is not a cut vertex of 𝐰 \mathbf{w} then v v is only visited once by w w . For the ( ⇐ \Leftarrow ) direction, if v v is a cut vertex, then, as noted before, every time w w visits v v , it switches the block it is travelling through. Since w w is a closed walk, then w w must visit v v at least twice to get back to where the walk started.
∎

 4 Bouquet structure and distance reduction 

 
 Let A A be an integer matrix. In [ 15 ] , the bouquets { B 1 , … , B s } \{B_{1},\dots,B_{s}\} of A A , the bouquet matrix A B A_{B} and its associated bouquet ideal I A B I_{A_{B}} are defined. These objects capture important combinatorial information about the toric ideal associated to A A . Each bouquet is either free, non-mixed or mixed, which we label by 0 , + 0,+ and − - respectively and this is called the signature of the bouquets. In addition, there is a bijection between ker ℤ ⁡ ( A ) \ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(A) and ker ℤ ⁡ ( A B ) \ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(A_{B}) which preserves elements of the Graver basis and the circuits. In [ 15 ] , the authors also present an inverse construction where we begin with a matrix (which acts as our bouquet matrix) and a set of primitive vectors (which control the signature of the bouquets) and this produces a matrix, known as the generalised Lawrence matrix , which has the desired bouquet ideal and signature. It is worth noting that, by [ 15 , Corollary 2.3] , every toric ideal arises as a generalised Lawrence matrix so this inverse construction produces all possible toric ideals which have the given bouquet ideal and signature. 
 
 
 In [ 12 ] , it is shown that two matrices which have the same bouquet ideal and signature have more in common than just their Graver basis and circuits and, in fact, there is a bijection between minimal Markov bases as well. When considering the distance-reduction property, this naturally prompts the question of whether the distance-reduction property is preserved between two such matrices.

 4.1 The poset of signatures 

 
 In the counterexample above, the distance-reduction information is not preserved between the two toric ideals, not because the structure of the fibers is different, but because elements in the fiber have different 1-norms. However, we can amend this, simply by requiring that the toric ideals are homogeneous and we prove this in Theorem 4.9 . 
 
 
 We begin by recalling some more technicalities of the theory of bouquets from [ 15 ] and [ 12 ] , for completeness and also to set some notation. For a toric ideal I A I_{A} , we let B 1 , … , B s B_{1},\dots,B_{s} denote the bouquets of I A I_{A} . Fixing a simple toric ideal I T ⊆ k ⁡ [ x 1 , … , x s ] I_{T}\subseteq k[x_{1},\dots,x_{s}] for some integer matrix T T and a signature ω ⊆ [ s ] \omega\subseteq[s] , we recall the definition of a T ω T_{\omega} -robust ideal.

 4.2 Homogeneity in terms of bouquets 

 
 As outlined at the start of Section 4 , in [ 15 , Theorem 2.1] , an inverse construction is described. Given a matrix and a set of primitive vectors, this produces a generalised Lawrence matrix. In particular, for a simple toric ideal I T I_{T} and a signature ω ⊆ [ s ] \omega\subseteq[s] , this allows us to construct infinitely-many T ω T_{\omega} -robust ideals by letting T T be our matrix and ensuring that our i i th primitive vector has a negative component if and only if i ∉ ω i\not\in\omega . 
 
 
 We will use this inverse construction in Examples 4.11 , 4.13 , 4.14 and 4.21 . However, based on Section 4.1 , we only want to deal with homogeneous toric ideals I A I_{A} .
This motivates Theorem 4.12 in which we describe a necessary and sufficient condition on the bouquet matrix A B A_{B} and bouquet-index-encoding vectors 𝐜 1 , … , 𝐜 s \mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{s} such that the toric ideal I A I_{A} is homogeneous. In particular, homogeneity of I A I_{A} depends only on its bouquet data. In addition, since the starting matrix and primitive vectors of the generalised Lawrence matrix A A act as the bouquet matrix and bouquet-index-encoding vectors, this allows us to easily construct infinitely-many homogeneous T ω T_{\omega} -robust ideals. 
 
 
 As in the previous section, we let B 1 , … , B s B_{1},\dots,B_{s} be the bouquets of A A with bouquet-index-encoding vectors 𝐜 i \mathbf{c}_{i} and bijection D : ker ℤ ⁡ ( A B ) → ker ℤ ⁡ ( A ) D:\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(A_{B})\rightarrow\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(A) .
