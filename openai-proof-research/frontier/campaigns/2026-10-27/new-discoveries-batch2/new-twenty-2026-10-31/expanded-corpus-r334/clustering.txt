 Abstract. 
 
 We generalize the perfectly clustering words of Simpson and Puglisi and relate them to band bricks over certain gentle algebras. This allows us to prove a generalization of a conjecture by the second author on perfectly clustering words.

 1. Introduction 

 
 Christoffel words form a family of well-studied words on two letters associated with certain lattice paths. We refer to [ 4 ] for a comprehensive introduction. Their importance stems from their appearance in various contexts, e.g. when studying palindromes, (positive) primitive elements of the free group on two generators, continued fractions, Markoff numbers, and discretization of lines.
Perfectly clustering words, introduced in [ 24 ] , generalize (lower) Christoffel words to alphabets with any number of letters. They arise in connection with word combinatorics and free groups, number theory, and dynamical systems. 
 
 
 In this article, we unveil a new relationship between perfectly clustering words and representation theory, via a specific family of gentle algebras . This brings new tools into the study of perfectly clustering words, and hence of Christoffel words, that we apply in order to prove a conjecture from [ 17 ] . 
 
 
 We now describe some of our main results. 
 
 
 1.1. Perfectly clustering words 

 
 Let A A be a totally ordered alphabet and let w ∈ A ∗ w\in A^{\ast} be a non-empty word. The Burrows–Wheeler transformation of w w , denoted by B ​ W ​ ( w ) BW(w) , is defined as follows. Consider all words w 1 , … , w r w_{1},\ldots,w_{r} that are conjugate (i.e. cyclically equivalent) to w w , ordered in lexicographic order. Then B W ( w ) = a 1 a 2 ⋯ a r BW(w)=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{r} where a 1 , … , a r a_{1},\ldots,a_{r} are the last letters of w 1 , … , w r w_{1},\ldots,w_{r} , respectively. 
 
 
 If a 1 ≥ a 2 ≥ ⋯ ≥ a r a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq\cdots\geq a_{r} , and if w w is primitive, meaning that we cannot write w w as a power of another word, then w w is called perfectly clustering . 
 
 
 For example,
if w = a ​ c ​ a ​ b w=acab , then w 1 = a ​ b ​ a ​ c w_{1}=aba\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{c} , w 2 = a ​ c ​ a ​ b w_{2}=aca\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{b} , w 3 = b ​ a ​ c ​ a w_{3}=bac\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{a} , w 4 = c ​ a ​ b ​ a w_{4}=cab\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{a} , and w w is perfectly clustering.
On the contrary, if w = a ​ c ​ b ​ a w=acba , then
 w 1 = a ​ a ​ c ​ b w_{1}=aac\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{b} , w 2 = a ​ c ​ b ​ a w_{2}=acb\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{a} , w 3 = b ​ a ​ a ​ c w_{3}=baa\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{c} , w 4 = c ​ b ​ a ​ a w_{4}=cba\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{a} , and w w is not perfectly clustering.

 1.3. The link between perfectly clustering words and representations of Λ n \Lambda_{n} 

 
 With each integer i ∈ { 1 , … , n } i\in\{1,\ldots,n\} , we associate the cycle 
 

 
 
 z i = α 1 α 2 ⋯ α i − 1 β i − 1 − 1 ⋯ β 2 − 1 β 1 − 1 . z_{i}=\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{i-1}\beta_{i-1}^{-1}\cdots\beta_{2}^{-1}\beta_{1}^{-1}. 
 
 
 With each primitive word w = i 1 ⋯ i r ∈ { 1 , … , n } ∗ w=i_{1}\cdots i_{r}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}^{\ast} on n n letters, we associate the cycle φ ( w ) = z i 1 ⋯ z i r \varphi(w)=z_{i_{1}}\cdots z_{i_{r}} .
Our first main result is 
 
 
 Theorem 1.1 (see Theorem   5.12 ) . 
 
 A primitive word w w on n n letters is perfectly clustering if and only if the band Λ n \Lambda_{n} -module B φ ⁡ ( w ) , 1 , λ B_{\varphi(w),1,\lambda} is a brick for some (equivalently any) λ ∈ k × \lambda\in k^{\times} . 
 
 
 
 In the statement above, a representation M M of Λ n \Lambda_{n} is called a brick if i ​ d M id_{M} forms a basis of the k k -vector space of endomorphisms of M M , i.e. if End Λ n ⁡ ( M ) ≅ k \operatorname{End}_{\Lambda_{n}}(M)\cong k .

 1.4. Consequences for words 

 
 The map  Φ \Phi due to Gessel and the fifth author (defined in Section   5.1 ) is a map Φ \Phi from words in A ∗ A^{*} to multiset of conjagacy classes of primitive words. More precisely, for w ∈ A ∗ w\in A^{*} , Φ ⁡ ( w ) \Phi(w) is the multiset of conjugacy classes of words obtained from the inverse of its standard permutation. The map Φ \Phi gives an inverse map for B ​ W BW for perfectly clustering words. 
 
 
 The second author, in her thesis, expressed a conjecture regarding the number of distinct lengths of perfectly clustering words appearing in Φ ⁡ ( n α n ​ … ​ 2 α 2 ) \Phi(n^{\alpha_{n}}\dots 2^{\alpha_{2}}) . (For compatibility with the rest of the article, we work with the alphabet { 2 , … , n } \{2,\dots,n\} .) 
 
 
 Conjecture 1.2 ( [ 17 ] ) . 
 
 Let n ⩾ 2 n\geqslant 2 and ( α 2 , … , α n ) (\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}) be a ( n − 1 ) (n-1) -tuple of nonnegative integers. The number of distinct lengths of conjugacy classes of words appearing in Φ ⁡ ( n α n ​ … ​ 2 α 2 ) \Phi(n^{\alpha_{n}}\dots 2^{\alpha_{2}}) is at most ⌈ ( n − 1 ) / 2 ⌉ \lceil(n-1)/2\rceil . 
 
 
 
 Thanks to a more general result in our setting ( Theorem   6.7 ),
we prove the following strengthening of the previous conjecture. 
 
 
 Corollary 1.3 (see Corollary   6.9 ) . 
 
 Let n ⩾ 1 n\geqslant 1 and ( α 2 , … , α n ) (\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}) be a ( n − 1 ) (n-1) -tuple of nonnegative integers. The number of distinct conjugacy classes of words appearing in Φ ⁡ ( n α n ​ … ​ 2 α 2 ) \Phi(n^{\alpha_{n}}\dots 2^{\alpha_{2}}) is at most ⌈ ( n − 1 ) / 2 ⌉ \lceil(n-1)/2\rceil . 
 
 
 
 That is to say, rather than bounding the number of distinct lengths of conjugacy classes of words, we actually bound the number of distinct conjugacy classes. 
 
 
 
 1.5. Consequences for Λ n \Lambda_{n} modules 

 
 Let M M be a Λ n \Lambda_{n} module. The g g -vector of M M is an integer vector which records the multiplicities of indecomposable summands of the minimal projective presentation of M M . It is defined more precisely in the next section. 
 
 
 Two brick modules B B and B ′ B^{\prime} are mutually compatible if 
 

 
 
 Hom Λ n ⁡ ( B , B ′ ) = 0 = Hom Λ n ⁡ ( B ′ , B ) . \operatorname{Hom}_{\Lambda_{n}}(B,B^{\prime})=0=\operatorname{Hom}_{\Lambda_{n}}(B^{\prime},B). 
 
 
 A module M M is called a semibrick if it decomposes as a direct sum of mutually compatible brick modules. 
 
 
 In this article, we focus on band modules that are also bricks, which we refer to as band bricks . We also consider direct sums of mutually compatible band bricks, which we refer to as band semibricks . 
 
 
 We recall some important results of [ 10 ] , specialized to our setting.
Band semibricks of Λ n \Lambda_{n} are uniquely determined by their g g -vectors (see Corollary   4.11 ) up to choices of the parameters λ \lambda of the brick modules.
Denote by Σ n \Sigma_{n} the set of cones in the space of g g -vectors which are spanned by collections of g g -vectors corresponding to mutually compatible band bricks. Then Σ n \Sigma_{n} is a fan, and the g g -vectors corresponding to the band bricks which are the direct summands of a band semibrick M M are the rays of the minimal cone of Σ n \Sigma_{n} containing the g g -vector of M M . 
 
 
 In our setting, we explicitly describe the support of the fan Σ n \Sigma_{n} (see Corollary   4.11 ).
For n = 4 n=4 , we explicitly describe the rays of the fan ( Theorem   6.10 ).
This recovers and extends results of Coll, Giaquinto, and Magnant [ 7 ] . 
 
 
 
 
 2. Gentle algebras 

 
 2.1. Definition of gentle algebras and their representations 

 
 A quiver is a 4 4 -tuple Q = ( Q 0 , Q 1 , s , t ) Q=(Q_{0},Q_{1},s,t) where Q 0 Q_{0} and Q 1 Q_{1} are (finite) sets called respectively the vertices and arrows , and s , t : Q 1 ⟶ Q 0 s,t:Q_{1}\longrightarrow Q_{0} are the source and target maps. We think of a quiver as a directed graph.
Let Q Q be a quiver. A path in Q Q is either
a sequence of arrows γ r ​ … ​ γ 1 \gamma_{r}\dots\gamma_{1} such that t ⁡ ( γ i ) = s ⁡ ( γ i + 1 ) t(\gamma_{i})=s(\gamma_{i+1}) for 1 ≤ i ≤ r − 1 1\leq i\leq r-1 , or a formal element e i e_{i} at each vertex i i called a lazy path of length zero. For any path γ = γ r ​ … ​ γ 1 \gamma=\gamma_{r}\ldots\gamma_{1} , we define s ⁡ ( γ ) = s ⁡ ( γ 1 ) s(\gamma)=s(\gamma_{1}) to be the source of γ \gamma and t ⁡ ( γ ) = t ⁡ ( γ r ) t(\gamma)=t(\gamma_{r}) the target of γ \gamma . We further define s ⁡ ( e i ) = t ⁡ ( e i ) = i s(e_{i})=t(e_{i})=i . 
 
 
 Let k k be an
algebraically closed field. We write k ​ Q kQ for the path algebra 
of Q Q over k k . It is a vector space over k k with as a basis the paths of Q Q , equipped with a multiplication: we define the product of two paths σ \sigma and τ \tau as their concatenation σ ​ τ \sigma\tau if this is a path;
otherwise the product is zero. The product is then defined on linear combination of paths by bilinearity. An oriented cycle is a path of length at least one whose starting and ending points coincide. 
 
 
 Assume that for each vertex of Q Q there are at most two outgoing arrows and at most two incoming arrows. A quotient of a path algebra k ​ Q / I kQ/I is called gentle if
 I I is an ideal generated by a collection  R R of paths of length two such that, at
every vertex  i i , 
 
 • 
 
 for any arrow α \alpha with target i i there is at most one
arrow β \beta with source i i and β ​ α ∉ R \beta\alpha\not\in R , and at most one
arrow γ \gamma with source i i and γ ​ α ∈ R \gamma\alpha\in R , 
 
 • 
 
 dually, for any arrow δ \delta with source i i , there is at most one
arrow β \beta with target i i such that δ ​ β ∉ R \delta\beta\not\in R ,
and at most one arrow γ \gamma with target i i such that δ ​ γ ∈ R \delta\gamma\in R , 
 
 • 
 
 for any oriented cycle α r ⋯ α 1 \alpha_{r}\cdots\alpha_{1} of Q Q , there is some
pair of consecutive arrows in R R (i.e., some i i with α i + 1 ​ α i ∈ R \alpha_{i+1}\alpha_{i}\in R or α 1 ​ α r ∈ R \alpha_{1}\alpha_{r}\in R ). 
 
 
 
 
 A pair ( Q , R ) (Q,R) is called a gentle quiver if Q Q is a quiver and R R is a collection of paths satisfying the above conditions.
All the above conditions except the one requiring some relations in each cycle

 The category of left k ​ Q / I kQ/I -modules is equivalent to the category of representations of ( Q , R ) (Q,R) over k k .  Given two representations X X and Y Y ,

 2.3. Band representations 

 
 The definition of bands is slightly more complicated than
the definition of strings. A
 band walk is a non-lazy string walk subject to the additional conditions: 
 
 • 
 
 its starting and ending points coincide, 
 
 • 
 
 it is primitive (i.e., not a power of a smaller walk), 
 
 • 
 
 all of its powers are strings. 
 
 
 
 
 Given a band walk z z , a positive integer m m , and λ ∈ k × = k ∖ { 0 } \lambda\in k^{\times}=k\setminus\{0\} , we define a representation B z , m , λ B_{z,m,\lambda} . Let r r be the length of z z . 
 
 
 We introduce a collection of linearly independent vectors ϵ i p \epsilon_{i}^{p} for
 0 ≤ i ≤ r − 1 0\leq i\leq r-1 and 1 ≤ p ≤ m 1\leq p\leq m . We define the vector spaces of the representation by 
 

 
 
 ( B z , m , λ ) i = ⨁ z s = i , 0 ≤ s ≤ r − 1 , 1 ≤ p ≤ m k ​ ϵ s p . (B_{z,m,\lambda})_{i}=\bigoplus_{z_{s}=i,0\leq s\leq r-1,1\leq p\leq m}k\epsilon^{p}_{s}. 
 
 
 This has the effect that each vertex visited by z z except the last one
contributes m m basis elements to the vector space over the corresponding
vertex. We fix the convention that ϵ r p = ϵ 0 p \epsilon^{p}_{r}=\epsilon^{p}_{0} . 
 
 
 Now, let γ \gamma be an arrow of Q Q from vertex i i to vertex j j . We
define ( B z , m , λ ) γ (B_{z,m,\lambda})_{\gamma} almost in the same way as for a string walk.
Specifically, ( B z , m , λ ) γ ​ ( ϵ s p ) (B_{z,m,\lambda})_{\gamma}(\epsilon^{p}_{s}) is defined as follows
unless γ \gamma is the arrow which z z takes between z r − 1 z_{r-1} and
 z r z_{r} : 
 
 • 
 
 ϵ s + 1 p \epsilon^{p}_{s+1} if z z travels from z s z_{s} to z s + 1 z_{s+1} forwards along
 γ \gamma , 
 
 • 
 
 ϵ s − 1 p \epsilon^{p}_{s-1} if z z travels from z s − 1 z_{s-1} to z s z_{s} backwards along
 γ \gamma , 
 
 •

 The remaining case splits into two possibilities. The
first is when z r − 1 = i z_{r-1}=i , z r = j z_{r}=j , and γ \gamma is the arrow which

as a linear map
to ⨁ p = 1 m k ​ ϵ r p \bigoplus_{p=1}^{m}k\epsilon^{p}_{r} given, with respect to these bases,

 2.4. Brick modules 

 
 A module M M is called a brick if the ring End ⁡ ( M ) := Hom ⁡ ( M , M ) \operatorname{End}(M):=\operatorname{Hom}(M,M) is a skew field.

 3. Representations of gentle algebras and curves on surfaces 

 
 We will be using a topological model developed in [ 1 , 3 , 19 , 20 ] to study gentle algebras and their representations. Although we will mainly be interested in the family of gentle algebras Λ n \Lambda_{n} defined in the introduction, we will devote this section to the description of the general case. 
 
 
 3.1. Surfaces and dissections 

 
 By surface we will always mean an oriented compact surface  S S with a finite number (which may be zero) of open discs removed. We denote the boundary of  S S by  ∂ S \partial S . Such a surface is determined by its genus and by the number of connected components of  ∂ S \partial S ; we will refer to these connected components as the boundary components of  S S . 
 
 
 A marked surface is a pair  ( S , M ) (S,M) , where  S S is a surface and  M M is a finite set of marked points of  S S satisfying the following: 
 
 • 
 
 M M is the disjoint union of two subsets  M ∘ M_{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}}} and  M ∙ M_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bullet}} ; 
 
 • 
 
 on each boundary component of  S S , marked points in  M ∘ M_{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}}} and  M ∙ M_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bullet}} alternate, and there is at least one of each; 
 
 • 
 
 marked points are also allowed in the interior of the surface. 
 
 
 
 
 A ∘ {\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}} -arc is a curve on  ( S , M ) (S,M) joining two points in  M ∘ M_{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}}} ; formally, it is a continuous map from the closed interval  [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] to  S S with endpoints in  M ∘ M_{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}}} and with the image of its interior is disjoint from M M . A ∙ {\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bullet} -arc is defined similarly. 
 
 
 A ∘ {\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}} -arc is simple if it does not intersect itself (except perhaps at its endpoints). 
 
 
 A  ∘ {\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}} -dissection of  ( S , M ) (S,M) is a collection of pairwise non-intersecting simple  ∘ {\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}} -arcs which cut the surface into polygons, called the cells of the dissection, each of which contains exactly one marked point in  M ∙ M_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bullet}} . We define a ∙ {\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bullet} -dissection similarly.

 3.7. Band bricks and band semibricks 

 
 A semibrick module is a direct sum of finitely many bricks such that
there are no non-zero morphisms between distinct summands.
We will specifically be interested in semibrick modules which are built as a
direct sum of band bricks. We refer to these as band semibricks . 
 
 
 Given a surface with
 ∘ {\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}} -dissection,
 ( S , M , Δ ∘ ) (S,M,\Delta^{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}}}) , and dual ∙ {\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bullet} -dissection Δ ∙ \Delta^{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bullet}} , there is a corresponding gentle quiver ( Q ⁡ ( Δ ∘ ) , R ⁡ ( Δ ∘ ) ) (Q(\Delta^{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}}}),R(\Delta^{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}}})) .
As we have seen, band brick for ( Q ⁡ ( Δ ∘ ) , R ⁡ ( Δ ∘ ) ) (Q(\Delta^{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}}}),R(\Delta^{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}}})) 
come in one-parameter families,
and these one-parameter families correspond to simple, primitive, closed curves on S S . 
 
 
 We will define a simple closed multicurve on S S 
to be a finite collection of primitive closed curves on S S which have no self-intersections, and which do not intersect each other. A simple closed
multislalom on S S is a simple closed multicurve on S S each of whose
components is a slalom with respect to Δ ∙ \Delta^{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bullet}} .
(Equivalently, each of the components is an accordion with respect to
 Δ ∘ \Delta^{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}}} , but it is the former perspective which will be more relevant for us.) 
 
 
 For 𝒞 \mathcal{C} such a simple closed multislalom,
we say that a module
corresponds to 𝒞 \mathcal{C} if it is a direct sum of one brick for each closed curve,
where the brick is chosen from the one-parameter family corresponding to
the closed curve, and if multiple curves appear corresponding to the same
family of bricks, then the corresponding bricks are chosen so as to be
non-isomorphic. 
 
 
 Proposition 3.21 . 
 
 Let ( S , M , Δ ∘ ) (S,M,\Delta^{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}}}) be a surface with a
 ∘ {\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}} -dissection. Every band semibrick for ( Q ⁡ ( Δ ∘ ) , R ⁡ ( Δ ∘ ) ) (Q(\Delta^{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}}}),R(\Delta^{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}}})) 
corresponds to a simple closed multislalom on S S . Conversely, any
module corresponding to such a simple closed multislalom is a band semibrick. 
 
 
 In this correspondence, band bricks correspond to individual slaloms. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 We already know that each band brick defines a primitive closed slalom without
self-intersections. Given a band semibrick, the corresponding curves necessarily
either coincide up to homotopy or can be drawn so as not to intersect,
by Lemma 3.13 . This shows that any band semibrick
corresponds to a simple closed multislalom as stated. 
 
 
 For the converse, we must also observe that if B B and B ′ B^{\prime} are
two non-isomorphic band bricks corresponding to homotopic curves, then
 Hom ⁡ ( B , B ′ ) = 0 \operatorname{Hom}(B,B^{\prime})=0 .
∎ 
 
 
 
 The previous proposition means that understanding band semibricks for the gentle quiver ( Q ⁡ ( Δ ∘ ) , R ⁡ ( Δ ∘ ) ) (Q(\Delta^{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}}}),R(\Delta^{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.5898,0.0078}{\circ}}})) amounts to understanding the simple closed multislaloms on S S .

 4.2. The Dyck path model 

 
 Given a simple closed multislalom 𝒞 \mathcal{C} on S n S_{n} , there is a particular way

describe.
Recall that a Dyck word is a word w w over the binary alphabet { u , d } \{u,d\} such that w w contains as many u u ’s as d d ’s and each prefix of w w contains at least as many u u ’s as d d ’s.
An elementary step is a segment of the form [ ( x , y ) , ( x + 1 , y + 1 ) ] [(x,y),(x+1,y+1)] or [ ( x , y ) , ( x + 1 , y − 1 ) ] [(x,y),(x+1,y-1)] .
A lattice path is a sequence of consecutive elementary steps in the plane ℤ × ℤ \mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z} , beginning at the origin.
A Dyck path is a lattice path representing a Dyck word such that u u corresponds to an up-step given by [ ( x , y ) , ( x + 1 , y + 1 ) ] [(x,y),(x+1,y+1)] and d d corresponds to a down-step given by [ ( x , y ) , ( x + 1 , y − 1 ) ] [(x,y),(x+1,y-1)] . For example, the Dyck path representing the word u ​ u ​ u ​ u ​ d ​ d ​ d ​ u ​ u ​ d ​ d ​ d uuuuddduuddd is drawn in Figure  11 .
Dyck paths can be characterized as lattice paths which begin and end on the

 6. Band semibricks 

 
 6.1. The band brick compatibility fan 

 
 We have already established in Corollary 4.11 that there is a bijection between band semibricks of Λ n \Lambda_{n} and g g -vectors ( a 1 , … , a n ) (a_{1},\dots,a_{n}) satisfying that the initial partial sums are non-positive and the total sum is zero. 
 
 
 The question arises: which g g -vectors correspond to a single one-parameter
family of bricks, rather than a direct sum of multiple band bricks? We refer to
such g g -vectors as band brick g g -vectors. 
 
 
 Further, given a g g -vector corresponding to a band semibrick, how can we decompose it into the band brick g g -vectors of the band bricks that make it up? We refer to this as the band semibrick decomposition problem . 
 
 
 It turns out to be natural to address the two questions together. 
 
 
 A fan is a collection  ℱ \mathcal{F} of cones such that any face of a cone in  ℱ \mathcal{F} is a cone in  ℱ \mathcal{F} and such that the intersection of any two cones in  ℱ \mathcal{F} is a face of each. 
 
 
 We say that two one-parameter families of band bricks
(or two band brick g g -vectors) are compatible if they can
appear in a band semibrick together, i.e., if the two one-parameter
families of bricks do not have any non-zero morphisms between them. 
 
 
 Definition 6.1 . 
 
 The band brick compatibility fan   Σ n \Sigma_{n} of  Λ n \Lambda_{n} is the collection of cones in the space of g g -vectors which
are spanned by a collection of mutually compatible band brick g g -vectors. 
 
 
 
 It follows from results of C. Geiss, D. Labardini-Fragoso, and J. Schröer on tame algebras that
 Σ n \Sigma_{n} is indeed a fan [ 10 , Theorem 3.2] (see also  [ 22 , Theorem 3.8] ). The following result is a reformulation in our special cases knowing that gentle algebras are tame. 
 
 
 Proposition 6.2 . 
 
 The set Σ n \Sigma_{n} is a simplicial fan, whose support consists of the band semibrick g g -vectors.
The band brick g g -vectors which appear in the band semibrick decomposition of a band semibrick g g -vector 𝐚 \mathbf{a} are the rays of the minimal cone of Σ n \Sigma_{n} containing 𝐚 \mathbf{a} . 
 
 
 
 The ensuing corollary is also needed for some arguments in the next subsection. 
 
 
 Corollary 6.3 . 
 
 Let 𝐚 1 , … , 𝐚 r \mathbf{a}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}^{r} be pairwise distinct g g -vectors of mutually compatible
bricks. Then 𝐚 1 , 𝐚 2 , … , 𝐚 r \mathbf{a}^{1},\mathbf{a}^{2},\dots,\mathbf{a}^{r} are linearly independent.
