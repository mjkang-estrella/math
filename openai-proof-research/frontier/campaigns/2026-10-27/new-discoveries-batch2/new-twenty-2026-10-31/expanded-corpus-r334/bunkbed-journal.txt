1. Introduction

   In 1985, Kasteleyn posed the bunkbed conjecture, which then circulated as an intriguing problem
in percolation theory (see [1, Remark 5]). Interest in the problem has waxed and waned over time,
but, especially in recent years, the conjecture has attracted significant attention. Indeed, the work
presented here has since been built on to produce a counterexample to the bunkbed conjecture;
see Section 1.1 and [2]. A feature of the problem which has made it particularly appealing is its
apparent simplicity and how, as has been noted by several authors, it appeared to be intuitively
clear.

   We now provide some definitions and state our main results before returning to the history of
the problem. While most work on the topic is concerned with simple graphs, we work in a more
general setting, and thus define the bunkbed graph in several cases, as follows.

Definition 1.1 (Bunkbed Graph). For Q a graph, digraph, or hypergraph, we can define the bunkbed
structure Q̃ as follows. Take two copies, Q0 and Q1 , of Q , labelling the copy of vertex v and edge e
in Qi as v (i) and e(i) respectively. We will call such edges e(i) horizontal edges. E(Q̃ ) then consists of
E(Q0 ) ∪ E(Q1 ) along with an edge between v (0) and v (1) for each v ∈ V (Q ), which will be called a
vertical edge. In the case of directed graphs, this vertical edge will be bidirectional. Q0 will be called
the lower bunk and Q1 the upper bunk of Q̃ .

   We will be concerned in this paper with percolation on the bunkbed graph under various uniform
models of percolation. We will consider two types of model: site percolation and bond percolation.
In both cases, we will ultimately take a family E (Q̃ ) of subsets of E(Q̃ ), which we will sample from
uniformly at random to produce some set X ⊆ E(Q̃ ) of edges which will be retained. We now define
these concepts formally.

Definition 1.2 (Percolation Model). If Q is a graph, digraph, or hypergraph, then a percolation model
is a family E (Q̃ ) is a family of subsets of E(Q̃ ). We perform percolation by selecting an element
X ∈ E (Q̃ ) uniformly at random, and then retain precisely those edges in X . For two vertices
x, y ∈ V (Q̃ ), we write
            E (Q̃ )                    E (Q̃ )
       P(x −−→ y)         or     P(x ←−→ y)
for the probability that there is a directed path of retained edges from x to y in the former, or a
bidirectional path in the latter case, in Q̃ under model E (Q̃ ). When Q is a hypergraph, a path in Q
is a sequence of vertices in which consecutive vertices are both members of some hyperedge in Q .

  We can then use this definition to define site percolation as a special case of bond percolation.
We will only consider site percolation on graphs, so for simplicity we only define this case.

Definition 1.3 (Site Percolation Model). If G is a graph, then a percolation model E (G̃) is said to be a
site percolation model if, for every X ∈ E (G̃) there is some set U ⊆ V (G̃), which we shall refer to as
the set of open vertices, such that X consists of precisely those edges with both ends in U.

   We consider site percolation as a special case of our general (bond) percolation model for
convenience; it will be useful for us to still have all vertices present, even if they are in no edges.
   We remark that in much other work on the topic, percolation is phrased in terms of sampling
edges independently at random with some probability p. Our sampling as above is equivalent to
this in the case that E (Q̃ ) is the powerset P (E(Q̃ )), and p = 1/2. This restriction to p = 1/2 turns
out to not be important, as will be discussed later.
   We now provide a very general statement of the bunkbed conjecture in terms of a general model,
before we proceed to consider several special cases.

Definition 1.4 (Bunkbed Conjecture for a General Model E ). Let Q be a graph, hypergraph, or digraph,
E (Q̃ ) be a model of percolation on Q̃ as defined above. The bunkbed conjecture for model E states
that for any Q , and any vertices u, v ∈ V (Q ), we have
                E (Q̃ )            E (Q̃ )
       P(u(0) −−→ v (0) ) ≥ P(u(0) −−→ v (1) ).
Let BBC(E (Q )) denote the above statement.

   There are several different statements which are referred to as ‘the bunkbed conjecture’, and
they are used somewhat interchangeably in the literature; Rudzinski and Smyth [3] detail a few
of these statements. One such form of the conjecture, which fits the notation we have described
above, concerns the following model of percolation.

Model 1.5 (E0 (G, T ), the Standard Bunkbed Model). For an undirected graph G and a fixed set
T ⊆ V (G), a set X ⊆ E(G̃) of horizontal edges is chosen uniformly at random, and retained along
with precisely those vertical edges v (0) v (1) such that v ∈ T . Equivalently, each horizontal edge of G̃
is retained independently at random with probability 0.5.
    In this setting, BBC(E0 (G, T )) is equivalent to many statements referred to as ‘the bunkbed
conjecture’. When stated, the conjecture is often given in a slightly more general form, retaining
each horizontal edge of G̃ with probability p for some fixed p ∈ (0, 1), as in for example the work
of Häggström [4,5]. However, as proved by Rudzinski and Smyth [3, Corollary 1.7], the case for any
specific probability p implies the general case, and so the truth of BBC(E0 (G, T )) would suffice to
prove the more general conjecture.
    The conjecture BBC(E (G)) is false for general percolation models E ; this is unsurprising as our
definition has no requirement of symmetry, and so permits many pathological models. However,
we will show that BBC(E (G)) is also false for several highly symmetric models E , some of which
have been conjectured to be true by various authors. This is particularly surprising as much of the
same intuition which tells us that BBC(E0 (G, T )) should be true also applies to these other – false –
cases.
    The conjecture has attracted significant attention over the last few years, being resolved in
progressively more general special cases. One initial sequence of results by de Buyer and then
van Hintum and Lammers [6–8] resolved the case of the complete graph. This was then further
generalised by Richthammer [9] to a broader class of highly symmetric graphs. Linusson [10,11] took
a different route, and both considered generalisations of the conjecture and related the conjecture
to a problem concerning randomly directed graphs. This work was then built upon by Hutchcroft,
Nizić-Nikolac, and Kent [12], who resolved the conjecture in the case of general G and probability
p ↑ 1. A different angle on the p ↑ 1 limit was later considered by the author [13], proving the
conjecture holds in this case by working with graph cuts.
    We now turn our attention to more general models; it is natural in an attempt to prove
the bunkbed conjecture to try to generalise the statement of the problem, in the hope that this
may be conducive to an inductive proof. Indeed, upon attacking the conjecture BBC(E0 (G, T )), a
generalisation to hypergraphs soon arises as a tempting possibility. Likewise, directed graphs allow
for the possibility of attaching ‘gadgets’ to a graph to easily generalise the conjecture further, and on
generalising to site percolation, one no longer needs to consider paths which return to an already-
visited pair of vertices in the bunkbed graph. However, our main result is the following, which says
that all of these generalisations are in fact false.

Theorem 1.6.         The generalised bunkbed conjecture is false for site percolation, hypergraphs, and
digraphs. More precisely, there are counterexamples to the statement BBC(E (Q )) for each of the models
S1 (G), S2 (G, T ), H4 (H , T ), H5 (H), D6 (D, T , F ), and D7 (D) to be discussed in Sections 3 and 4.

   We now briefly discuss some significant recent developments in the area, before providing an
outline of the rest of this paper.

1.1. Subsequent disproof of the bunkbed conjecture

    Before proceeding with the main content of this paper, we note that a few months after the
initial release of this paper as a preprint on arXiv, the work presented here was used by Gladkov, Pak
and Zimin [2] to produce a counterexample to the bunkbed conjecture. In particular, they started
from the hypergraph construction shown in Fig. 4, and, by replacing each hyperedge with a suitably
constructed graph, were able to produce a graph on which the bunkbed conjecture fails.
    While it is impossible to directly simulate site percolation with bond percolation – or indeed
bond percolation on a hypergraph by bond percolation on a simple graph (as was shown by Gladkov
and Zimin [14]) – they were able to simulate a modified hypergraph percolation model due to
Wierman and Ziff [15]. By then correctly tuning the parameters of this model of percolation, a
counterexample to the conjecture was found.
    The relation of [2] to the work presented here is discussed further in Section 6.

1.2. Paper overview

   Before diving into our results, we first describe in Section 2 the terminology we will use, and
also state and prove a simple lemma concerning a symmetry that we will often make use of.
   In Section 3 we then focus on the case of site percolation, and the main aim of this section is to

   After dealing with site percolation, we then move on to directed graphs and hypergraphs. In

containing one of the vertices of e. For this reason, we will only ever consider flips of sets of vertices
or sets of edges, and never of a mix of vertices and edges.

Definition 2.2 (Symmetric Percolation Models). For a graph, hypergraph, or digraph Q , a set of edges
F ⊆ E(Q̃ ) and any set of vertices W ⊆ V (Q ), the flip of F in W is the set fW [F ].
    Then, a subset A ⊆ E (Q̃ ) is said to be symmetric if for every F ∈ A and every W ⊆ V (Q ), we
have fW [F ] ∈ A. Moreover, we will say that E (Q̃ ) is symmetric if A = E (Q̃ ) is symmetric as a subset
of itself.

   In particular, all bond percolation models considered here will be symmetric. However, the site
percolation models that we work with are in fact not symmetric by the above definition, and so we
need another, site-percolation-specific definition of symmetry.

Definition 2.3 (Symmetric Site Percolation Models). Given a graph G and site percolation model E (G̃),
take some F ∈ E (G̃) and let U ⊆ V (G̃) be the corresponding set of open vertices. Then for any set
W ⊆ V (G), the site-flip of F in W is the set sfW (F ) of edges with both ends in the set fW [U ].

    Throughout the paper, we will refer to models as either ‘conditioned’ or ‘unconditioned’. While
this is not precise terminology, it should be taken to mean the following. In the unconditioned cases,
our model will be equivalent to one in which every edge (or vertex, in the case of site percolation)
is retained independently with probability 1/2. The conditioned cases then have further restrictions
that move us away from this situation. We now put our terminology and result to use in resolving
the case of site percolation.

3. Site percolation

   The goal of this section is to show that the bunkbed conjecture is false for site percolation. We
consider the case of site percolation before moving on to the other structures (hypergraphs and
digraphs) for two purposes: for ease of notation, as we are in this section concerned only with
ordinary graphs, and to introduce our counterexamples in a more structured manner, as the results
of Section 4 are built on top of the results of this section. In the framework introduced in Sections 1
and 2, the site percolation we consider comes in two flavours.

Model 3.1 (S1 (G); Unconditioned Site Percolation). For an undirected graph G, let the set of open
vertices W ⊆ V (G̃) be chosen uniformly at random among all subsets of vertices. We then retain
precisely those edges with both endpoints in W .
   In order to construct a counterexample to the bunkbed conjecture for site percolation, we first
consider a conditioned form of site percolation, in which we fix which vertices are to be posts. We
also condition further, insisting that among those v ∈ V (G) which are not posts, precisely one of
the vertices v (0) and v (1) is open.

Model 3.2 (S2 (G, T ); Conditioned Site Percolation). For an undirected graph G and subset T ⊆ V (G),
a set of open vertices is selected uniformly at random from all sets of the following form. If v ∈ T ,
both v (0) and v (1) are open, and for v ∈/ T precisely one of v (0) and v (1) is open, and the other is
closed.
   We now construct our counterexamples for site percolation, beginning with the case of condi-
tioned site percolation defined as Model 3.2.

4. Models

   We consider a total of four further models in addition to those discussed in Section 3, two
for each of the cases of hypergraphs and directed graphs. Following the structure of the proofs
in Section 3, in each case we will consider first a conditioned model, and then an unconditioned
version. In particular, in all cases, the conditioned model on a structure Q (hypergraph or digraph)
will have precisely one of the (hyper)edges e(0) , e(1) retained for each edge e ∈ E(Q ).
   We will first consider hypergraphs, and now define the models we will consider.

Model 4.1 (H4 (H , T ); Conditioned Hypergraphs). For a hypergraph H and subset T ⊆ V (H), let
H4 (H , T ) be the set of those F ⊆ E(H̃) containing precisely one of e(0) and e(1) for each e ∈ E(H),
and precisely those vertical edges v (0) v (1) for which v ∈ T .

   It was conjectured by Linusson [10] that a version of the bunkbed conjecture should also hold
on hypergraphs. In our notation, the conjecture is as follows.

Conjecture 4.2 ([10] Conjecture 2.7). For any hypergraph H and set T ⊆ V (H) of posts, BBC(H4 (H , T ))
holds. That is, for any vertices u, v ∈ V (H), we have
                    H4 (H ,T )                H4 (H ,T )
          P(u(0) ←−−−→ v (0) ) ≥ P(u(0) ←−−−→ v (1) ).

    Note that Theorem 1.6, once proved, will disprove this conjecture. Similarly to in the site perco-
lation case, we will use our results concerning Model 4.1 to prove a result about an unconditioned
model.

Model 4.3 (H5 (H); Unconditioned Hypergraphs). For a hypergraph H, let F ⊆ E(H̃) be chosen
uniformly at random amongst all such subsets. Keep precisely these edges, and discard the others.

   Finally, we consider digraphs, and, following the same pattern as before, have two models.

Model 4.4 (D6 (D, T , F ); Conditioned Digraphs). For a digraph D, subset T ⊆ V (D) of vertices, and
subset F ⊆ E(D) of (directed) edges, let D6 (D, T , F ) be the family of those subsets of E(D̃) containing
both e(0) and e(1) for all e ∈ F , exactly one of e(0) and e(1) for all e ∈ E(D) \ F , and precisely those
vertical edges v (0) v (1) for which v ∈ T .

   It was conjectured by Leander [16] that the bunkbed conjecture holds for acyclic directed graphs

hold for any directed graph. The latter is disproved by Theorem 1.6, but the example we construct
has many cycles, and so it remains open whether the conjecture holds for acyclic directed graphs.

multigraphs.

Model 4.5 (D7 (D); Unconditioned Digraphs). For a directed multigraph D, let F ⊆ E(D̃) be chosen

false for suitable hypergraphs H, digraphs D, subsets of vertices T and subsets of edges F . In the next
section, we give constructions of such graphs.

6. Conclusion

    We have shown that the bunkbed conjecture does not hold when generalised to site percolation
or hypergraphs. The situation with directed graphs is a little more complex, as we have only dealt
with the case of multigraphs with vertical edges which are bidirectional. To be precise, the directed
edges v (0) v (1) and v (1) v (0) are either both retained or both removed. In the interest of considering
fully directed graphs (wherein e.g. v (0) v (1) can be retained and v (1) v (0) removed simultaneously), we
define the following model:

Model 6.1 (D8 ; Fully Directed Graphs). For a simple digraph D, construct the bunkbed graph D̃ by
adding both directed edges v (0) v (1) and v (1) v (0) for all v ∈ V (D). A subset of edges F ⊆ E(D̃) is
selected uniformly at random, and precisely the edges in F are retained.

  We conjecture that it is not necessary that our example in Section 5.2 has multiple edges and
undirected vertical edges.

Conjecture 6.2. There is a simple digraph D for which BBC(D8 , D) is false.

   We furthermore repeat the following question of Leander [16] in our context.

Question 6.3. Do BBC(D6 (D, T , F )) and BBC(D7 (D)) hold for all acyclic digraphs D?

    It seems unclear to the author whether the answer to Question 6.3 should be positive or negative.
Indeed, this problem is far more amenable to inductive techniques than the case of graphs with
cycles, but actually performing this induction seems far from trivial.
    However, one case that does seem approachable, though was found by the author to be more
difficult than expected, is expressed by the following conjecture.

Conjecture 6.4. BBC(D7 (Tn )) holds for Tn , the transitive tournament on n vertices.

    We remark here that, while it may seem natural to apply the same techniques as used for the
(undirected) complete graph (e.g. in [7,8]), these do not directly apply, in short because Tn does not
have the same symmetries as Kn .
    It would be interesting to consider whether the bunkbed conjecture holds after restricting to
various classes of graphs, with a view towards ascertaining whether or not the bunkbed conjecture
failing for site percolation is in general the exception or the rule. Indeed, one may ask the following
(perhaps optimistic) question.

Question 6.5. What is the probability that the assertion in the bunkbed conjecture for site
percolation BBC(S1 (G)) holds for G ∼ G(n, p) the Erdős–Rényi random graph for n large? To what
extent does this probability depend on the behaviour of p as a function of n?

   There are of course many other classes of graph one could consider; graphs of diameter 2 (or
simply of radius 1), and highly symmetric graphs (e.g. those with transitive automorphism group)
would seem to be natural candidates for investigation.
   Returning now to the original bunkbed conjecture BBC(E (G)), as was discussed in Section 1.1,
the work presented here has since been used by Gladkov, Pak and Zimin [2] to construct a
                                                    14
L. Hollom                                                           European Journal of Combinatorics 128 (2025) 104188


counterexample to the bunkbed conjecture. This has constituted a rapid reversal of the direction
of the previous literature, which had been almost entirely dedicated to attempts to prove that the
bunkbed conjecture true.
    Now that the conjecture is known to be false in general, it is not immediately clear what the
next direction for work in the area should be. One question to ask is for which graphs the bunkbed
conjecture is in fact true; there are several highly symmetric graphs for which the conjecture is
still unknown. Grid graphs are raised as a case of interest in [2], and we add to this the following
conjecture.

Conjecture 6.6.     The bunkbed conjecture BBC(E0 (Qd , T )) holds for the graph Qd and any subset
T ⊆ v (Qd ) and any integer d, where Qd is the hypercube of dimension d.
    Moreover, even the simpler version of Conjecture 6.6, wherein one does not condition on a set
T of posts and takes a subset of the edges of Qd uniformly at random is – to the best of the author’s
knowledge – also open. Indeed, this is equivalent to the appealing statement that the connection
probability in uniform bond percolation on the hypercube is decreasing in the distance.
    A natural generalisation of Conjecture 6.6, which also encompasses many other cases of interest,
is the following question.

Question 6.7. Does the bunkbed conjecture BBC(E0 (G, T )) hold for any Cayley graph G and subset
T ⊆ V (G)?
   While Question 6.7 appears natural, it seems (as appears to be the case with many problems in
this area) that a solution would require some significant new ideas.
