         In this paper we give a combinatorial proof of the entanglement of a graph whose number
         of vertices is a product of two primes, and has exactly one entangled edge, or all of whose
         entangled edges are emanating from one vertex. Thereby, we provide easier and more
         direct proof of the conjecture by Braunstein, Ghosh, and Severini in [2].

         Keywords: laplacian of a graph, entanglement, quantum information
         Communicated by: G Milburn


Introduction
One of the major problems in Quantum Mechanics is to characterize entangled states of
a quantum system. There are several partial criteria for entanglement of mixed states [6],
[8], [10], but yet there is no general criterion. Entanglement is connected to the important
concept of non-locality in Quantum Mechanics. Entangled states are also useful in quantum
cryptography and other quantum information processing tasks [1],[9]. A mixed quantum
state is separable (and entangled, otherwise) if it can be written as a convex combination of
pure separable states. Solving the quantum separability problem simply means determining
whether a given quantum state is entangled or separable.
     Following [2], a class of states that are represented by the density matrices of graphs
are considered. It is shown in [2] that certain classes of graphs always represent entangled
(separable) states. Here we positively answer two open problems raised in [2, Conjecture 6.5].
Similar results are presented in [11].
     A graph G = (V, E) is a pair of a non-empty and ﬁnite set V (or V (G)) whose elements
are called vertices; and a non-empty set of unordered pairs of vertices E (or E(G)), whose
elements are called edges. A loop is an edge of the form {vi , vi }, for some vertex vi . We assume
that E(G) does not contain only loops [2]. Two distinct vertices vi and vj are adjacent if
{vi , vj } ∈ E(G). The adjacency matrix of a graph G on n vertices is an n × n matrix M (G),
having rows and columns labeled by the vertices of G, and ij-th entry deﬁned to be 1 if vi
and vj are adjacent; and 0, otherwise. The degree of a vertex vi is the number dG (vi ) of edges
                                                        n
adjacent to vi . The degree of G is deﬁned by dG = i=1 dG (vi ). Note that dG = 2|E(G)|.
The degree matrix of G is an n × n matrix A(G), having ij-th entry dG (vi ), if i = j; and 0,
otherwise. The Laplacian matrix of a graph G is the matrix L(G) = A(G) − M (G). Note

general result in this direction is obtained by C. Wu [11], but our method of proof is combi-
natorial and gives a better intuition in this special case. All over this section, G is a graph
with |V (G)| = pq, where p and q are two natural numbers.

    We may use our technique combined with results of [11] to give diﬀerent and simpler proofs
of some of the results proved in [2]. The next three results are of this kind.
    In [11], the author considers the set S consisting of symmetric real matrices with non-
negative row sums and nonpositive oﬀ-diagonal elements. These matrices are clearly positive
semideﬁnite [11, Section 4]. If S1 (S10 , respectively) is the subset of S consisting of those
matrices in S with unit trace (and zero row sums), then for each A ∈ S10 of size pq × pq,
the corresponding (p, q)-partial transpose ATB has zero row sums if and only if it is positive
semideﬁnite [11, Theorem 3]. When A is the density matrix of a graph, then ATB has the
same row sums. It is shown in [11, Sect. 4] that our Theorem 2 is also valid for density
matrices in S10 which are not necessarily coming from graphs.
    The next result considers the separability of tensor products of graphs. For graphs G and
H, their tensor product G ⊗ H (also called strong or canonical product) is the graph whose
adjacency matrix is M (G ⊗ H) = M (G) ⊗ M (H). Let V1 be the set of nonnegative symmetric

matrices of unit trace which are diagonally dominant (i.e. Aii ≥ i=j Aij , for each i), then
for a pq × pq density matrix A ∈ V1 , if all the blocks Aij of ATB are line sum symmetric (i.e.
the k th column sum is equal to the k th row sum, for each k), then A is separable in Cp ⊗ Cq
[11, Theorem 9]. We use this fact to show that the density matrix of the tensor product of
two graphs is separable [2, Lemma 4.1].

                                                                                                 Abstract
                                                     Given a family S of k–subsets of [n], its lower shadow ∆(S) is the family of (k − 1)–subsets which are
                                                 contained in at least one set in S. The celebrated Kruskal–Katona theorem gives the minimum cardinality
                                                 of ∆(S) in terms of the cardinality of S. Füredi and Griggs (and Mörs) showed that the extremal families
                                                 for this shadow minimization problem in the Boolean lattice are unique for some cardinalities and asked
                                                 for a general characterization of these extremal families.
                                                     In this paper we prove a new combinatorial inequality from which yet another simple proof of the
                                                 Kruskal–Katona theorem can be derived. The inequality can be used to obtain a characterization of
                                                 the extremal families for this minimization problem, giving an answer to the question of Füredi and
                                                 Griggs. Some known and new additional properties of extremal families can also be easily derived from
                                                 the inequality.

6    Final remarks
In this paper we have focussed in the arithmetic nature of the Kruskal–Katona theorem. The key inequality
in Lemma 2 has interest in its own. Even if binomial decompositions appear mostly in connection to
the Kruskal–Katona theorem, they are also related to the study of simplicial complexes and combinatorial
geometry (the initial motivation in the Kruskal paper), and in isoperimetric problems in the n–cube, where
the characterization of extremal sets is still an open problem (see e.g. Bezrukov [1, section 4]). We think
that the role of the arithmetic inequality (3) in the shadow optimization problem is remarkable and gives
perhaps the more natural way to prove the Kruskal–Katona theorem. In particular, it easily leads to the
characterization of the extremal families given in Theorem 3. We note that the key inequality in Lemma 10
for k ′ , k ′′ ≥ k can be obtained from the Kruskal–Katona theorem, but we have not found a way to derive
the significant case k ′ = k, k ′′ = k − 1 from it.

One possible measure of the distance between extremal sets and initial segments of the colex order is the

provide an explicit algorithm polynomial in n to construct extremal families which are at a given distance
