                                                                                                Abstract
                                                    For a finite forest F let ik (F ) be the number of independent sets of F with k vertices. Zhang
                                                and Li proved that the sequence i0 (F ), i1 (F ), . . . , iα(F ) (F ) is unimodal for every finite forest F ,
                                                which answers Erdős Problem 993. We give a second proof. It starts from their decomposition
                                                relative to a fixed independent set and from the bounds of Zhang and Li and of Fang, Lu, Nevo,
                                                Yao and Zheng that confine a valley of the sequence to an explicit window of ranks. For a forest
                                                with at least 25 vertices, one moment argument excludes a valley at every rank of the window:
                                                at the activity where the hard-core mean equals the rank, the size of a random independent set
                                                is a mixture of binomial laws over an independent set of maximum weight, a valley is a moment
                                                inequality for this mixture, and it is excluded by duality given three bounds that hold for every
                                                forest, on the variance of the number of free vertices and on its Laplace transforms, and on the
                                                variance ratio. The variance bound is proved by hand up to finitely many interval checks and the
                                                other two bounds are verified by computer on finite interval-arithmetic coverings; on the resulting
                                                parameter domain a valley is excluded by exact tests on finitely many rational boxes while the
                                                mean number of free vertices is below an explicit starting mean between 19 and 50, and above
                                                it by one inequality, with explicit constants, for the fibers of a weighted valley kernel, proved by
                                                hand up to a finite list of explicit checks and averaged over the mixture. Forests with at most 24
                                                vertices are treated by exact counting, by hand except for exact rational evaluations of two explicit
                                                formulas at 43 parameter triples. No forest is enumerated. A formal proof of the theorem in Lean 4
                                                accompanies the paper.

                                          1 Introduction
                                          For a graph G, let V (G) and E(G) denote its vertex set and edge set, respectively. Throughout this
                                          paper all graphs considered are finite and simple. The degree of a vertex v in a graph G, denoted
                                          by dG (v), is the number of edges of G incident with v. An acyclic graph is one that contains no
                                          cycles. The graph with no vertices is the null graph. A nonnull connected acyclic graph is called a
                                          tree. Acyclic graphs are called forests. For X ⊆ V (G), the subgraph of G induced by X, denoted by
                                          G[X], has vertex set X and edge set consisting of all edges of G with both end vertices in X. A claw
                                          is the graph with four vertices u, v1 , v2 , v3 and three edges uv1 , uv2 , uv3 . A graph G is claw-free if no
                                          induced subgraph of it is a claw.

                                                                                                     1
    A stable set in a graph is a set of vertices no two of which are adjacent. Stable sets are also
commonly known as independent sets. A stable set is maximum if the graph contains no larger stable
set. The stability number of G, denoted by α(G), is the size of a maximum stable set in G. A stable
set is maximal if the set cannot be extended to a larger stable set. A tree having at most one vertex
of degree ≥ 3 is called a spider. A graph is well-covered if all maximal independent sets are of the
same size.
    Let ik (G) be the number of independent sets of size k in G, where 0 ≤ k ≤ α(G), and let i0 (G) =
1. The sequence (i0 (G), i1 (G), . . . , iα(G) (G)) is the independence sequence, or vertex independence
                                                                 Pα(G)
sequence, of G. The independence polynomial is I(G; x) = k=0 ik (G)xk . A sequence (a0 , . . . , am ) is
weakly increasing if a0 ≤ · · · ≤ am and weakly decreasing if a0 ≥ · · · ≥ am . A sequence (a0 , a1 , . . . , am )
is unimodal if there is an index r such that a0 ≤ · · · ≤ ar ≥ · · · ≥ am . We say that a polynomial
is unimodal if its sequence of coeﬀicients is unimodal. A sequence (a0 , . . . , am ) of positive numbers
is log-concave if a2k ≥ ak−1 ak+1 for 1 ≤ k ≤ m − 1. A polynomial with positive coeﬀicients is called
log-concave if the sequence of its coeﬀicients is log-concave. Any log-concave sequence of positive
numbers is also unimodal. The independence polynomial was first defined by Gutman and Harary
in 1983 [10]. Unimodality problems arise naturally in many branches of mathematics and have been
extensively investigated.
    A matching in a graph G is a set M ⊆ E(G) such that no vertex of G is incident with more
than one edge of M . The matching sequence of a graph G is the sequence whose kth term is the
number of matchings of size k in G. In 1981, Schwenk proved that the matching sequence of a graph
is unimodal [22]. H. Wilf asked whether the vertex independence sequence was likewise unimodal. In
1987, Alavi, Malde, Schwenk and Erdős showed that the independent set sequence of a graph is not in
general unimodal [1]. This led to the question of which classes of graphs have unimodal independent
set sequences. In the same paper, Alavi, Malde, Schwenk and Erdős posed the following question [1].
Conjecture 1.1. For trees (or perhaps forests), is the vertex independence sequence unimodal?
    There have been numerous partial results, mostly exhibiting families of trees with unimodal inde-
pendent set sequences. In 1990, Hamidoune proved that the independence polynomial of a claw-free
graph is log-concave [11]. Chudnovsky and Seymour proved that the independence polynomial of any
claw-free graph has only real zeros and therefore is unimodal [5]. Levit and Mandrescu proved that the
independence polynomials of well-covered spiders and some other well-covered trees are unimodal [16].
Li, Li, Yang and Zhang proved that all spiders with a vertex of degree at least 3 have log-concave
independence polynomials [19].
    There are also results that establish monotonicity for the two ends of the independence sequence.
For every forest F , Levit and Mandrescu proved i⌈(2α(F )−1)/3⌉ (F ) ≥ · · · ≥ iα(F ) (F ) [18]. For a graph
G on n ≥ 1 vertices, Basit and Galvin proved that ik (G) ≥ ik+1 (G) whenever dα(G)(n − 1)/(α(G) +
n)e ≤ k < α(G) [3]. For a tree T chosen uniformly from all trees with vertex set {1, . . . , n}, Basit
and Galvin proved that (i0 (T ), . . . , i⌊0.280n⌋ (T )) is weakly increasing and (i⌈0.347n⌉ (T ), . . . , iα(T ) (T )) is
weakly decreasing, with probability tending to 1 as n tends to infinity [3].
    In 2004, Levit and Mandrescu conjectured that the independence polynomials of all forests are
log-concave [17]. Computations run by Radcliffe showed that all trees on 25 or fewer vertices have
log-concave independent set sequences [2]. Kadrawi, Levit, Yosef and Mizrachi found two trees on
26 vertices whose independence sequences are not log-concave [15]. Kadrawi and Levit subsequently
constructed infinite families of such trees [14]. Galvin showed that log-concavity can fail arbitrarily
far from the end of the independent set sequence [9]. The failure of log-concavity does not imply a
failure of unimodality. In 2026, Fang, Lu, Nevo, Yao and Zheng proved that there is a positive integer
N0 such that the independence sequence of every forest with at least N0 vertices is unimodal [8].
However, this result does not settle Conjecture 1.1 for trees and forests of all sizes. In this paper, we
prove Conjecture 1.1. Our main result is the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1. For every finite forest F the independence sequence i0 (F ), i1 (F ), . . . , iα(F ) (F ) is uni-
modal.

decomposition of independent sets relative to a fixed independent set, the conditional binomial mixture,
and the bounds that confine a possible valley to a window of ranks; the initial increasing segment is
credited to Fang, Lu, Nevo, Yao and Zheng [8]. It also uses several of their counting inequalities
and the forms of their analytic bounds. The new route treats forests with at most 24 vertices by
exact counting and forests with at least 25 vertices by a single moment argument. The finite-order
certificates of the earlier proof are not used in the counting argument, while the analytic part still
depends on explicitly recorded finite computations. The sources of the individual inputs are credited
where they are used.

                                                                               Abstract
                                                    We introduce generalised orbit algebras. The purpose here is to mea-
                                                sure how some combinatorial properties can characterize the action of a
                                                group of permutations of the elements of Ω on the subsets of Ω. The
                                                similarity with orbit algebras is such that it took the author a long time
                                                to find a generalised orbit algebra not arising from a permutation group.
