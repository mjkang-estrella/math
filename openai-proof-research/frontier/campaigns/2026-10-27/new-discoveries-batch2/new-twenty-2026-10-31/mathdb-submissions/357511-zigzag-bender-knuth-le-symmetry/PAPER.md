# Bender–Knuth symmetry of even zigzag posets

## Source statement and conventions

J. H.-H. Chiang, A. T. N. Hoang, M. Kendall, R. Lynch, S. Nguyen, B. Przybocki and J. Xia, *Bender–Knuth involutions on linear extensions of posets*, Discrete Mathematics **347** (2024), 114068; arXiv:2302.12425v2. The source conjecture is Conjecture 4.17 in both v2 and the journal version (Conjecture 4.19 in v1).

The following is Conjecture 4.17 verbatim, with mathematical typesetting restored from the journal PDF:

> The zigzag-poset \(Z_n\) (Fig. 5), defined to be a poset of \(n\) elements with the relations
> \[
> v_1<v_2>v_3<v_4>\ldots>v_{n-3}<v_{n-2}>v_{n-1}<v_n,
> \]
> satisfies \(\mathcal{BK}_{Z_n}\cong S_{\mathcal L(Z_n)}\) when \(n\) is even.

Here the group is a specified subgroup of the permutation group on extensions, so the intended assertion is equality in that action. This is exactly the LE-symmetry statement of MathDB 357511. The source reports computational checks for even n ≤ 10; the result here is the uniform theorem for all even n, rather than a new small-case computation. The empty order and order 2 are included in the formal endpoint; both have a singleton extension set.

Definition 2.4, source text:

> A linear extension of \(P\) is a linear order \((\ell,\le_\ell)\) which extends \(P\) in the sense that \(x\le_P y\) implies \(x\le_\ell y\).

The source then states:

> More precisely, a linear extension \(\ell\) of \(P\) is a bijection \(\ell:P\to\{1,\ldots,n\}=:[n]\) such that \(x<_P y\) implies \(\ell(x)<\ell(y)\).

It also explicitly permits the inverse ordered-list convention. Definition 2.5 reads:

> For a given \(n\)-element poset \(P\) and \(1\le i\le n-1\), the Bender–Knuth move \(t_i\) is an involution of \(\mathcal L(P)\) that sends \(\ell=(p_1,\ldots,p_n)\) to
> \[
> (p_1,\ldots,p_{i+1},p_i,\ldots,p_n)
> \]
> if \(p_i\) and \(p_{i+1}\) are incomparable, and fixes \(\ell\) otherwise.

Definition 2.7 fixes the generated permutation action:

> For a given \(n\)-element poset \(P\), the Berenstein–Kirillov group of \(P\), denoted by \(\mathcal{BK}_P\), is the permutation group of the set \(\mathcal L(P)\) generated by the BK moves \(t_i\) \((1\le i\le n-1)\).

This is precisely the subgroup closure used in BKgroup below.

The formal definitions, alongside those quotations, are:

```lean
def zigLt (n : ℕ) (a b : Fin n) : Prop :=
  a.val % 2 = 0 ∧ (b.val = a.val + 1 ∨ a.val = b.val + 1)
def IsLE {n : ℕ} (R : Fin n → Fin n → Prop) (w : Equiv.Perm (Fin n)) : Prop :=
  ∀ a b, R a b → w.symm a < w.symm b
abbrev LinearExtensions {n : ℕ} (R : Fin n → Fin n → Prop) :=
  {w : Equiv.Perm (Fin n) // IsLE R w}
abbrev ZigzagLE (n : ℕ) := LinearExtensions (zigLt n)
def bk (n : ℕ) (i : ℕ) (hi : i + 1 < n) : Equiv.Perm (ZigzagLE n) :=
  move (zigLt n) i hi
def BKgroup (n : ℕ) : Subgroup (Equiv.Perm (ZigzagLE n)) :=
  Subgroup.closure {g | ∃ i h, g = bk n i h}
```

The complete definition of move is in the source appendix. Its underlying word equals the swapped word if and only if the two adjacent vertices are incomparable; otherwise it equals the input word. This equivalence is proved by rawSwap_isLE_iff and move_apply, and involutivity is proved for every relation admitting an input extension. In particular, no default permutation has been substituted for the BK move.

The height-two relation zigLt is proved irreflexive and transitive. SourceBridge proves both that comparability is exactly path adjacency and that minima are exactly the even Lean vertices. The mandatory kernel-checked counts are
\[
|\mathrm{ZigzagLE}\ 4|=5,\qquad |\mathrm{ZigzagLE}\ 6|=61.
\]
Both use plain decide. These computations refer to the literal bijection type above, so they exclude an empty or singleton mis-oriented model.

## The theorem

For every even n, the Bender–Knuth involutions generate the full symmetric group on the linear extensions of Z_n. We give a complete proof in one-based source coordinates, and an unconditional Lean formalization on zero-based vertices.

## 1. Elementary group tools

For a subset \(X\) of a finite permutation domain, write \(A(X)\) for the alternating group on \(X\), acting identically off \(X\). We use the classical simplicity of \(A_d\) for \(d\ge5\), and generation of alternating groups by 3-cycles. Both have library proofs in Mathlib, including `alternatingGroup.isSimpleGroup` in `GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Alternating/Simple.lean`.

### 1.1 Simple sections and an isolated diagonal action

A section of a finite group is a quotient of one of its subgroups. Fix a nontrivial simple group \(S\). Say a group is \(S\)-free if none of its sections is isomorphic to \(S\).

Groups of order less than \(|S|\) are \(S\)-free. The property is inherited by subgroups and quotients and is closed under finite direct products and extensions. Here is the extension argument, including the point that will matter below. Suppose \(N\triangleleft G\), and a section \(J/K\cong S\) exists in \(G\). The image of \(J\cap N\) in \(J/K\) is normal, so is either trivial or all of \(J/K\). In the latter case \(S\) is a section of \(N\). In the former, \(J\cap N\subseteq K\), so \(S\) is a section of the image of \(J\) in \(G/N\). This proves the assertion. Finite-product closure follows by induction using the coordinate kernels.

Suppose \(H\le S_d\times B\), its projection onto \(S_d\) is surjective, \(d\ge5\), and \(B\) is \(A_d\)-free. Then \(H\) contains \(A_d\times1\). Indeed, let \(K=\ker(H\to B)\), and let \(J\triangleleft S_d\) be its first projection. The intersection \(J\cap A_d\) is normal in \(A_d\). If it is trivial, \(S_d/J\) contains a copy of \(A_d\). But \(S_d/J\) is a quotient of the image of \(H\) in \(B\): an element of that image is sent to the first coordinate modulo \(J\), independently of its lift. This contradicts the section property of \(B\). Thus \(A_d\subseteq J\), proving the claim.

### 1.2 A bound for disconnected posets

Suppose an \(r\)-element poset \(Q\) is a disjoint union of components \(Q_1,\ldots,Q_c\). Put \(e_j=|\mathcal L(Q_j)|\). If
\[
 r!<|S|,\qquad e_j!<|S|\quad(1\le j\le c),                 \tag{1}
\]
then \(\mathcal{BK}_Q\) is \(S\)-free.

To prove this, record an extension by its **shuffle word** (the component of each successive letter), together with an internal linear extension of each component. On shuffle words, \(t_i\) is exactly the interchange of positions \(i,i+1\). Hence the image of the group on shuffle words is a quotient of \(S_r\).

For a fixed starting shuffle and a fixed word in the generators, the resulting shuffle is independent of the internal extensions. Whenever two adjacent positions have the same component, the internal action is a specified BK generator of that component; its index is the number of occurrences of that component up to that position. When the components differ, the internal extensions do not change. Consequently an element of the shuffle kernel, on each shuffle fiber, acts coordinatewise by elements of \(\prod_j\mathcal{BK}_{Q_j}\). The kernel embeds in a product, indexed by the shuffles, of these coordinate groups. Each coordinate group embeds in \(S_{e_j}\). All groups at the bottom of this construction have order less than \(|S|\) by (1). The closure facts in 1.1 prove the claim. This is a section bound, not merely a bound on the size of the extension set.

### 1.3 Two support facts

If permutations \(a,b\), each a product of disjoint 3-cycles, have supports meeting in exactly one point \(x\), then their commutator is a single 3-cycle with support
\[
 \{x,a(x),b(x)\}.
\]
For the convention \([a,b]=aba^{-1}b^{-1}\), direct evaluation gives the cycle \((x,a(x),b(x))\). All other cycles of \(a,b\) commute or cancel.

If \(|X|\ge4\), \(e\notin X\), a group contains a 3-cycle on \(\{x,y,e\}\) with distinct \(x,y\in X\), and its subgroup acts as \(A(X)\) on \(X\) while fixing \(e\), then conjugation supplies every cycle \((e,u,v)\) for distinct \(u,v\in X\). These generate \(A(X\cup\{e\})\). It is enough that the subgroup have additional synchronized actions elsewhere: those actions do not affect this conjugation, since the original cycle fixes all other points.

Finally, if \(|X|,|Y|\ge4\) and \(X\cap Y\ne\varnothing\), the groups \(A(X),A(Y)\) generate \(A(X\cup Y)\). For overlap of at least two points, a cycle using those two points adjoins each new point, using the preceding fact. For overlap exactly one, take 3-cycles in the two sets through the common point; their commutator first supplies a bridging 3-cycle, after which the overlap has size at least two. Thus a connected family of overlapping sets, each of size at least four, generates the alternating group on its union.

## 2. Fence-extension counts and the order separation

Let \(E_j=|\mathcal L(Z_j)|\); reversing an order does not change this count. We need only
\[
 E_{r-1}<E_r,\qquad E_r>r\quad\text{for even }r\ge4.       \tag{2}
\]
The numbers \(E_j\) are nondecreasing: append a new endpoint to a fence, placing it first if it is minimal and last if maximal, to obtain an injection on extensions. For odd \(j\ge3\), the last vertex of \(Z_j\) is minimal and cannot be the last letter of an extension. A new maximal endpoint can therefore be inserted either last or immediately before the last letter. These give disjoint injections, so \(E_{j+1}\ge2E_j\). In particular \(E_{r-1}<E_r\) and
\[
 E_r\ge 5\cdot2^{(r-4)/2}>r\quad(r\ge4\text{ even}),
\]
starting from \(E_4=5\); the final elementary inequality follows by induction on \(r/2\).

Fix now even \(n\ge6\), put \(r=n-2\) and \(d=E_r\). A proper connected component in an \(r\)-vertex disconnected induced subposet of a fence is a fence or its dual on at most \(r-1\) vertices, so has at most \(E_{r-1}<d\) extensions. Therefore
\[
 e_j!<d!/2=|A_d|,\qquad r!<d!/2.                          \tag{3}
\]
The latter follows from \(d>r\ge4\). Thus 1.2 applies to all such disconnected induced subposets with \(S=A_d\).

## 3. Inductively obtaining a diagonal alternating group

Let \(G=\mathcal{BK}_{Z_n}\), and assume the theorem for \(Z_r\). Let
\[
 H=\langle t_2,t_3,\ldots,t_{n-2}\rangle.
\]
Its orbits are precisely the fibers with specified first and last vertices. Within any such fiber its action is the full BK action of the remaining induced poset, because adjacent swaps connect the linear extensions of every finite poset. For completeness, this connectivity follows by moving a chosen maximal vertex to the last position, across incomparable vertices, and inducting.

The first vertex is odd and the last vertex even. Deleting them leaves a connected induced path precisely for the three endpoint pairs
\[
 (1,2),\quad(1,n),\quad(n-1,n).                           \tag{4}
\]
Indeed, deleting two vertices from a path leaves one interval precisely when they are its first two vertices, its last two, or its two endpoints. All other fibers have disconnected residual posets.

Write \(W=\mathcal L(Z_r)\). The three fibers in (4) have the following coordinate maps, with concatenation understood:
\[
 A(w)=(1,w_1+2,\ldots,w_r+2,2),
\]
\[
 B(w)=(1,n-w_1,\ldots,n-w_r,n),
\]
\[
 C(w)=(n-1,w_1,\ldots,w_r,n).                             \tag{5}
\]
These are poset isomorphisms on the residual sets, so every \(t_{i+1}\in H\) acts as the same \(t_i\) on all three copies of \(W\). By the induction hypothesis, the image here is a diagonal copy of \(S_d\).

The combined action on every other fiber is \(A_d\)-free by (3), 1.2, and finite-product closure. The action of \(H\) on all fibers is faithful. Applying 1.1 shows that for **every** \(\alpha\in A(W)\), \(G\) contains the permutation
\[
 D_\alpha:A(w)\mapsto A(\alpha w),\quad
 B(w)\mapsto B(\alpha w),\quad
 C(w)\mapsto C(\alpha w),                                \tag{6}
\]
which is the identity on all other fibers. Crucially, (6) is diagonal, not three separately supported actions.

## 4. Breaking the diagonal with one support intersection

Put \(h=t_1t_{n-1}\), an involution swapping the first two and last two letters whenever they are incomparable. Let \(U=A(W)\cup B(W)\cup C(W)\). Define
\[
 R=\{w\in W:w_1=r-1,\ w_r=r\},\qquad
 L=\{w\in W:w_1=1,\ w_r=2\}.
\]
Checking the first and last letters in (5) gives exactly:

* \(hB(W)\cap U=\varnothing\);
* \(hA(w)\in U\) precisely when \(w\in R\); it then belongs to \(C(L)\);
* \(hC(w)\in U\) precisely when \(w\in L\); it then belongs to \(A(R)\).

In the second case the coordinate correspondence is
\[
 hA(s)=C(t),\qquad t=(1,s_2+2,\ldots,s_{r-1}+2,2).         \tag{7}
\]
All end swaps used here are legal: the first two letters are distinct minima, and the last two are distinct maxima.

Here are four distinct extensions, for every even \(r\ge4\):
\[
\begin{aligned}
 s&=(r-1,1,3,\ldots,r-3,2,4,\ldots,r-2,r),\\
 t&=(1,s_2+2,\ldots,s_{r-1}+2,2),\\
 z&=(1,3,\ldots,r-1,2,4,\ldots,r),\\
 z'&=(3,1,5,7,\ldots,r-1,4,6,\ldots,r,2).
\end{aligned}                                           \tag{8}
\]
The ellipses mean the indicated arithmetic progressions, possibly empty. Their odd vertices precede all even vertices, so they are extensions. We have \(s\in R\), \(t\in L\), and \(z,z'\notin R\cup L\).

Take
\[
 a=D_{(s\ z\ z')},\qquad b=hD_{(t\ z\ z')}h.
\]
By (7) and the three support observations, their supports meet in exactly the point \(A(s)\). Their commutator is consequently a single 3-cycle. Its support is
\[
 \{A(s),A(z),e\},\qquad e=hC(z).                         \tag{9}
\]
The point \(e\notin U\): its first vertex is 1 and its last is \(r=n-2\), whereas the three endpoint pairs of \(U\) are (4).

The permutations (6) fix \(e\) and are 2-transitive on \(A(W)\), since \(d\ge5\). Conjugating (9) by them therefore yields all 3-cycles through \(e\) and two points of \(A(W)\). By 1.3,
\[
                         A(A(W))\le G.                  \tag{10}
\]
This is now a separately supported alternating group.

## 5. Primitivity and the alternating group

Here we use the block formulation followed in Lean, replacing the normal-closure propagation proof in the original manuscript. A block B for G is a set such that each translate is either equal to B or disjoint from B. The action of G on all extensions is transitive: match any target word from left to right by bubbling the desired next letter left. Each crossed letter must be incomparable with it, since otherwise its relative order in one of the two extensions would violate the poset relation. Thus every crossing is a legal BK move.

Suppose a block B contains A(u) and at least one other point. The separately supported group A(A(W)) from (10) implies that B contains all of A(W). If the other point lies outside A(W), every supported alternating permutation fixes it, so preserves B; transitivity on A(W) gives the claim. If it lies inside A(W), the stabilizer of A(u) is transitive on the other A-points because d>=5. These stabilizer elements preserve B, so B contains every A-point other than A(u) as well. Consequently every element of H preserves B: its translate intersects B at an A-point. Since H is transitive within each specified-first-and-last fiber, B contains the whole of any such fiber it meets.

Use g_R=t_(n-1)t_(n-2)t_(n-1). There is an A-word ending in (3,4,2), fixed by g_R: start with 1, put the other minima before their successors, and finish with this triple. More formally, assign an order-compatible priority with 1 uniquely first and 3,4,2 uniquely last in that order, then take a linear extension of the priority-refined poset. As g_R fixes a point of B, it preserves B.

For any maximum c other than 2, choose a third maximum b distinct from 2,c, possible since n>=6. An A-word with all minima before all maxima can end in (c,b,2); g_R sends that triple to (2,b,c) and keeps first vertex 1. Thus B meets every fiber starting with 1 and contains all words starting with 1.

Now g_L=t_1 t_2 t_1 fixes an extension starting (1,3,2), so it also preserves B. This prefix is possible: 1,3 are minima and are precisely the predecessors of 2. To reach any minimum a other than 1, choose a third minimum b distinct from 1,a. A word starting (1,b,a), with the remaining minima before all maxima, can end at any specified maximum c. The braid g_L sends its initial triple to (a,b,1), keeping its last vertex. Thus B meets every endpoint fiber and equals the entire extension set.

The needed witness extensions can all be obtained from order-compatible priorities; this is proved in PrimOrder and instantiated in PrimWitness. The braid evaluations are proved in PrimBraid, and the block propagation in PrimBlock. Transitivity moves any nontrivial block to one meeting A(W). The preceding argument therefore proves primitivity of G.

By Section 4, G contains a literal 3-cycle. Jordan's theorem for a primitive finite permutation group containing a 3-cycle gives
\[
 A(\mathcal L(Z_n))\le G. \tag{11}
\]
Lean uses the pinned Mathlib theorem `alternatingGroup_le_of_isPreprimitive_of_isThreeCycle_mem`, whose hypotheses are exactly these. This replaces the original manuscript's connected-support generation argument; no unproved equivalence of permutation actions is involved.

## 6. An odd generator in every even order

We give a sign recurrence which replaces the Euler-number congruence and
fixed-point count argument in the original ordinary proof. Write sigma=t_2
and tau=t_(n-1), for even n>=6. These moves use disjoint pairs of positions
and commute. Divide all extension words into three invariant sets:

1. words fixed by tau, equivalently with comparable final two letters;
2. words moved by tau whose final two letters are in increasing numerical order;
3. words moved by tau whose final two letters are in decreasing numerical order.

The first set and the incomparability decision at the final pair are
sigma-invariant, since sigma changes only positions 2 and 3. The final pair
itself is unchanged by sigma, so the other two sets are also invariant. Tau
exchanges the latter sets, is its own inverse, and commutes with sigma.
Therefore it conjugates the two restrictions of sigma. Their signs are equal,
and their product is +1. The sign on all words is consequently the sign of
sigma restricted to the tau-fixed set. This uses only multiplicativity of
sign over an invariant partition and its invariance under conjugacy; no
cardinality congruences are assumed.

A comparable final pair must be a minimum a followed by a maximum b. Every
successor of a must occur later than its penultimate position, hence must
be b. In an even fence the only minimum with one successor is the endpoint
vertex 1: every other minimum has two distinct maximum neighbors. Thus the
final pair is exactly 1,2. Conversely 1,2 is comparable, so all extension
words ending in 1,2 are fixed by tau.

Deleting this final pair and subtracting 2 from every remaining vertex gives
exactly an extension of Z_(n-2). Conversely, append 1,2 to any such smaller
extension after adding 2 to its vertices. All relations among remaining
vertices are unchanged; vertex 1 precedes its only successor 2, and vertex
3 also precedes 2. Thus these are mutually inverse bijections on the entire
fixed set. They intertwine t_2 on both extension sets, because its positions
lie in the remaining prefix. Hence

\[
 \operatorname{sgn}(t_2\text{ on }\mathcal L(Z_n))
 =\operatorname{sgn}(t_2\text{ on }\mathcal L(Z_{n-2})).
\]

At n=4, t_2 is the single transposition displayed in Section 7. Induction in
steps of two proves that t_2 is odd for every even n>=4. In Lean these are
`odd_sign_recurrence`, `odd_sign_even`, and `oddStep_proved`. The generic
cancellation theorem `odd_sign_cancel_separated` works for arbitrary finite
relations; the literal fence-tail classification and bijection are proved
in `OddTail` and `OddTailFixed`.

## 7. Bases and conclusion

For \(n=0,2\), the extension set is a singleton. For \(n=4\), list the extensions as
\[
 a=1324,\ b=1342,\ c=3124,\ d=3142,\ e=3412.
\]
Then
\[
 t_1=(a\ c)(b\ d),\quad t_2=(d\ e),\quad
 t_3=(a\ b)(c\ d).
\]
Conjugating \((d\ e)\) by the subgroup generated by \(t_1,t_3\), which fixes \(e\) and is transitive on \(a,b,c,d\), supplies all star transpositions through \(e\). Hence \(G=S_5\).

For even \(n\ge6\), Sections 2–5 use the theorem at \(n-2\) to obtain (11), and Section 6 supplies an odd generator. A subgroup containing the alternating group and an odd permutation is the full symmetric group. Induction proves the source's conjecture in its full range. ∎

## What Lean proves

The final, unconditional theorem is:

```lean
theorem Zigzag.even_zigzag_LE_symmetric (n : ℕ) (hn : Even n) :
    Zigzag.BKgroup n = ⊤
```

It applies `even_zigzag_LE_symmetric_from_steps alternatingStep_proved oddStep_proved n hn`. There are no extra hypotheses, finite-order cutoff, abstract substitute for the extensions, or paper-only bridge. All definitions are printed by FinalAudit; the cardinality sanity theorems give 5 and 61. SourceBridge proves the inverse-label identification and the action of each BK move on labels. ## Significance and limitations

This proves the full even-fence LE-symmetry assertion in its specified permutation action. It is a specialized finite-poset group-action result; no general classification of BK groups or resolution of the source’s other conjectures is claimed. Standard finite-group tools are combined with a fence-specific simple-section separation, a synchronized three-fiber construction and its singleton-overlap commutator, and endpoint braid propagation. The formal route adds a short parity recurrence in place of Euler-number congruences. The independent written-proof review found no gap and judged the assembly substantive; that judgment is not an exhaustive novelty certificate. The size of this formalization is substantially greater than the early estimate. Pinned upstream Mathlib artifacts and the Lean toolchain remain trusted; no independent rebuild of Mathlib is claimed.
