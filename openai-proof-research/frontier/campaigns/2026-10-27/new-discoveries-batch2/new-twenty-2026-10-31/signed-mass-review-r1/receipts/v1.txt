Minimizing Eigenvalues of the Fractional Laplacian



Alvis Zahl

Address: Alvis Zahl. Department of Mathematics
Rutgers University
110 Frelinghuysen Rd., Piscataway, NJ 08854, USA

Email address: a.z@math.rutgers.edu




Abstract.
    

We study the minimizers of
    




 \lambda_{k}^{s}(A)+|A| 


    

where  \lambda^{s}_{k}(A)  is the  k -th Dirichlet eigenvalue of the fractional Laplacian on  A . Unlike in the case of the Laplacian, the free boundary of minimizers exhibit distinct global behavior. Our main results include: the existence of minimizers, optimal Hölder regularity for the corresponding eigenfunctions, and in the case where  \lambda_{k}  is simple, non-degeneracy, density estimates, separation of the free boundary, and free boundary regularity. We propose a combinatorial toy problem related to the global configuration of such minimizers.
  




Key words and phrases: Free boundary problems, optimal regularity, fractional Laplacian.



2020 Mathematics Subject ClassificationPrimary: 35B65, 35R35. Secondary: 35R11.



1. Introduction




In this paper, we study the minimization problem



(1.1)

 \{\lambda_{k}(A)+|A|:A\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\} 



where  A\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n} , and  \lambda_{k}(A)  is the  k-th  eigenvalue of the fractional Laplacian, that is,



(1.2)

 \displaystyle\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{s}u=\lambda_{k}u\ &\hbox{in}~A,\\
u=0&\hbox{in}~{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus A,\end{cases} 



where  0<s<1 , and  (-\Delta)^{s}  is defined as:



(1.3)

 (-\Delta)^{s}u(x):=C_{n,s}\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:|y-x|\geq\delta\}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dy. 






The interest in our free boundary problem (1.1) arises from a natural generalization of the problem of minimizing  k-th  eigenvalue of  -\Delta . However, even in this case, the problem is imperfectly understood. We refer the reader to [20] for a detailed discussion. A classic breakthrough was given in [8], which shows that such problems admit minimizers in the class of quasi-open sets, and is further improved in [6], [7], [25]. The boundary regularity is recently developed in [21], [22], and [14].




We note that (1.1) can be considered as a vector-valued version of the classical Bernoulli type free boundary problem. The scalar version of the one-phase Bernoulli type problem is studied in [5] and a more generalized two-phase version is studied in [10], [11], [12].




The fractional Laplacian has attracted many interests in the last years. Besides being a natural generalization of the Laplacian, such non-local operators often arise in different fields, including probability theory, mathematical physics, and applied sciences. For a comprehensive discussion of this type of non-local operator, we refer the reader to [18], [19]. Therefore, it is natural to study the free boundary problem in the setting of the fractional Laplacian. Such Bernoulli-type free boundary problems involving  (-\Delta)^{s}  are studied in [13], [16], [17], for the one-phase problem, and the two-phase problem is studied in [2]. One main estimate that we use is developed in [23].




We point out that there are three main difficulties in studying problem (1.1). Firstly, the eigenvalues are given by a min-max of the Rayleigh quotient which is a critical point of the functional instead of the minimum in, for example, the Bernoulli problem. We overcome this difficulty by following the idea of [6]. A major problem arises when the multiplicity of the minimizing eigenvalue is not  1 , in which case, eigenfunctions might be degenerate.




Second,  (-\Delta)^{s}  is non-local, which poses more technical difficulties than the local case, where we used estimates and techniques in [23]. The non-locality also gives rise to the difficulty in studying the global configuration of minimizers due to the interaction between different pieces of the domain. To capture the behavior at infinity, our strategy is to study minimizers on a union of different copies of  {\mathbb{R}}^{n}  that don’t interact with each other (Definition 2.3).




Third, it is not a priori clear over what class of sets that we are minimizing over. To deal with this, we rephrase the problem using level sets of  W^{s,2}  functions. We note it is not necessary to do so and one could follow the idea of [6] and consider the minimization problem (1.1) in the class of appropriate quasi-open sets. The main benefit of our approach is that we avoid the potential theory involved completely, and we refer the reader to [1] for discussions on capacity and quasi-open sets.




Main Results.




Theorem 1.4.



There exists an open bounded measurable set  A\subset\cup_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}  such that the minimum of (1.1) is achieved, that is



(1.5)

 \lambda(A)+|A|\leq\inf\{\lambda_{k}(A^{\prime})+|A^{\prime}|\}. 







Here the infimum is taken over a set of appropriate domains in  \cup_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbb{R}}^{n} , which will be made precise in the next section. We point out that in the local case, minimizers are given by unions of disjoint sets [6], which are translation invariant. In the nonlocal case, it could happen that these disjoint pieces are infinitely away from each other, and thus we consider the existence of minimizers in copies of  {\mathbb{R}}^{n} .




Theorem 1.6.



There exists an eigenfunction  u_{k} , corresponding to the minimizing eigenvalue  \lambda_{k}  of (1.1), such that  u_{k}\in C^{0,s}(\overline{A}) .





Similar to the local case discussed in [7], the perturbation of eigenfunctions gives information about a linear combination of the eigenfunctions corresponding to  \lambda_{k}(A) , and therefore, Theorem 1.6 only guarantee Hölder continuity for one eigenfunction.




If  \lambda_{k}(A)  is simple, we moreover have the following results.




Theorem 1.7.



Suppose the minimizing eigenvalue  \lambda_{k}  in (1.1) is simple. Let  u_{k}  be the corresponding eigenfunction to  \lambda_{k} , then

(1) 



(Non-degeneracy.) For all  r_{0}>0 , there exists  r<r_{0} ,  c_{0} , such that for  |B_{r/8}(x_{0})\cap A|>0 , we have




 \sup_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|u_{k}|\geq c_{0}r^{s}. 



(2) 



(Separation of the free boundary.)




 \partial\{u_{k}(x)>0\}\cap\partial\{u_{k}(x)<0\}=\varnothing. 



(3) 



(Density estimate.) Suppose  x  is a free boundary point, then there exists  C,r_{0}  such that for all  r<r_{0}  small, we have



(1.8)


 \displaystyle|\{u_{k}>0\}\cap B_{r}(x)|\geq Cr^{n}, 




 \displaystyle|\{u_{k}=0\}\cap B_{r}(x)|\geq Cr^{n}, 



and  |\partial A|=0 .

(4) 



(Free boundary regularity.) Let  u  be the extension of  u_{k}  (defined in section 5). Assume that  \|u\|_{C^{0,s}(B_{1})}\leq C , and  |u-U|\leq\tau_{0}  in  B_{1}  where  U  (depending only on  x_{n},y ), in polar coordinates  x_{n}=\rho\cos(\theta) ,  y=\rho\sin(\theta) , is




 U(x,y)=\rho^{1/2}\cos(\theta/2)^{2s}. 



Then  F(u)=\partial\{u_{k}\neq 0\}  is  C^{1,\gamma_{0}}  in  B_{1/2} , for some constant  \gamma_{0} .







Outline of paper.




In section 2, we give a formal definition of (1.1). In particular, we extend our domain to a union of  k  copies of  {\mathbb{R}}^{n} . We introduce notation, and prove some useful preliminary estimates.




Sections 3 and 4 deal with the existence of minimizers. We first prove an a priori estimate showing that shape minimizers have to be bounded following the idea of [6]. Using the boundedness of minimizers we show the existence of shape minimizers of (1.1).




In Section 5, we prove optimal regularity for some eigenfunctions. However, it is not clear which eigenfunction has such regularity due to the difficulty mentioned earlier, and we assume that  \lambda_{k}  is simple in the rest of the paper. In Section 6, we prove that the corerrsponding eigenfunction is non-degenerate.




In Section 7, we follow the idea of [2] and conduct blow-up analysis using a Weiss type monotonicity formula and we conclude that the free boundaries are separated in Section 8. This behavior of separation also yields density estimates. In Section 9, we show that the eigenfunction corresponding to the minimizing  \lambda_{k}  is an almost minimizer in the setting of [3], which implies that the free boundary is  C^{1,\gamma_{0}} .




In the Section 10, we study the stability of the shape minimizer under translations and pose a toy problem that helps us in understanding the global configuration. In particular, we conjecture that there is no stable configurations where the disconnected pieces can interact with each other, and the minimizer has to be a union of  k  congruent balls in different copies of  {\mathbb{R}}^{n} . In particular, this implies that  \lambda_{k}  is not simple.




2. Preliminaries




We introduce notations, definitions, and preliminary results in this section.




Definition 2.1.



Let  \Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}  be any measurable set. Define the Sobolev space  \tilde{W}^{s,2}_{0}(\Omega)  as the completion of  C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)  with respect to the Gagliardo norm



(2.2)

 [u]_{W^{s,2}}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy. 







We always understand the problem (1.1) in the following sense.




Definition 2.3.



For any  k>0 , define




 \mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}=\cup_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbb{R}}_{i}^{n}, 



Here  {\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{i}  is identical to  {\mathbb{R}}^{n}  indexed by  i . We say  u\in\tilde{W}^{s,2}(\mathcal{R}_{k})  if  u^{i}=u|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{i}}\in\tilde{W}^{s,2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}) . Consider the following class of functions



(2.4)

 \mathfrak{S}_{k}=\{u\in\tilde{W}^{s,2}(\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}):|{\{u\neq 0\}}|<C\}. 



Define the Rayleigh quotient




 \mathfrak{R}_{k}=\frac{1}{\int_{\mathcal{R}_{k}^{n}}|u|^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}\frac{(u^{i}(x)-u^{j}(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy. 



We understand problem (1.1) in the following sense.



(2.5)

 \lambda_{k}+|\{u\neq 0\}|=\min_{V_{k}\subset\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\max_{u\in V_{k}}\frac{1}{\int_{\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}}|u|^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{(u^{i}(x)-u^{i}(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy+|\{u\neq 0\}|, 



where the minimum is taken over  k  dimensional subspace  V_{k}  of  \mathfrak{S}_{k} .
If  u_{k}  minimizes (2.5), we say




 A=\{u\neq 0\}=\{u^{1}\neq 0\}\cup\cdots\cup\{u^{k}\neq 0\} 



a  \lambda_{k} -minimizer, call  \lambda_{k}(A)  the minimizing eigenvalue and  u_{k}  the corresponding eigenfunction.





Remark 2.6.



The motivation for above definition is due to a concentration compactness phenomenon. Unlike in the local case where one can translate the domain, it could happen that a minimizing sequence of domains has disjoint pieces that move away from each other. We consider the limiting “infinitely away pieces” as lying in different copies of  {\mathbb{R}}^{n}  and don’t interact with each other. When  \mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}={\mathbb{R}}^{n} , the above definition of  \mathfrak{R}_{k}  is the usual Rayleigh quotient. 






Remark 2.7.



It is intentional that our space  \mathfrak{S}_{k}  and the eigenvalue that we are interested in minimizing  \lambda_{k}  depend on the same  k , since for minimizing  \lambda_{k} , we need at most  k  copies of  {\mathbb{R}}^{n} . In particular, for any  k^{\prime}>k , any k dimensional subspace of  \mathfrak{S}_{k^{\prime}}  is contained in  \mathfrak{S}_{k} , and thus (2.5) is equivalent to




 \displaystyle\min_{V_{k}\subset\mathfrak{S}_{k^{\prime}}}\max_{u\in V_{k}}\frac{1}{\int_{\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k^{\prime}}}|u|^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{k^{\prime}}\frac{(u^{i}(x)-u^{i}(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy+\{u\neq 0\}. 



It could happen that we only need  m<k  copies, and in this case we have  u|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{j}}=0  for all  j>m .





Notations 2.8.



If  x,y\in\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k} , and  x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{i},y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{j} , with  i\neq j , we formally define




 |x-y|=+\infty\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{1}{|x-y|}=0. 



In particular, for  u\in\tilde{W}^{s,2}(\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}) , we have




 \displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}\frac{(u^{i}(x)-u^{j}(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy, 



and



(2.9)

 R[u]:=\mathfrak{R}_{k}[u]=\frac{1}{\int_{\mathcal{R}_{k}^{n}}|u|^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy. 



For any measurable  A\subset\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k} ,




 \mathfrak{S}_{k}(A)=\{u\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}:u=0\text{ a.e. on }\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}\setminus A\}. 



Let  K(x,y)  be the kernel:



(2.10)

 K(x,y)=\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}. 



Let  B[\cdot,\cdot]  be the inner product of  \tilde{W}^{s,2}(\mathcal{R}_{k}^{n}) 




 B[u,v]=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy. 



We sometimes omit the domain of integration if the domain the whole space, and we omit  dxdy  when there is no confusion.





Definition 2.11.



For any measurable  \Omega\in\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k} , we say  u  is a weak solution to




 \displaystyle\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{s}u=f\ &\hbox{in}~\Omega,\\
u=0&\hbox{in}~\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}\setminus\Omega,\end{cases} 



if  u\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}(\Omega)  and




 \int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))dxdy=\int_{\Omega}u\varphi dx. 



for all  \varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}) , that is  \varphi|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{i}}\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}) .





We proceed to prove some useful estimates.




Proposition 2.12.



If  u\in\tilde{W}^{s,2}(\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k})  satisfies in the weak sense:




 \begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{s}u=\lambda u\ &\hbox{in}~\{u\neq 0\},\\
u=0&\hbox{in}~\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}\setminus\{u\neq 0\},\end{cases} 



Then  u\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k})  and




 \|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k})}\leq C\lambda^{\frac{n}{2s}}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k})}. 







Proof.



Let  \Omega=\{u\neq 0\} . Define  v(t,x)=e^{-\lambda t}u  on  \Omega\times[0,T)  for some  T>0 , then  v\in L^{2}(\tilde{W}^{s,2}_{0}(\Omega))  is a weak solution to the heat equation:




 \begin{cases}\partial_{t}v+(-\Delta)^{s}v=0&\hbox{in}~\Omega\times[0,\infty),\\
v(0,x)=u(x)&\hbox{in}~\Omega\times\{t=0\}.\end{cases} 



For every  t>0 , we extend  v(t,x)  to  \mathcal{R}_{k}^{n}  by  0  and still denote it  v . Then we have in the weak sense:



(2.13)

 \partial_{t}|v|+(-\Delta)^{s}|v|\leq 0 



We want to compare  v  with another function  w  defined in the following ways. First extend  u  to  \mathcal{R}_{k}^{n}  by  0  and denote the extended function by  \tilde{u} . Let  w  be the solution to the following heat equation:



(2.14)

 \begin{cases}\partial_{t}w+(-\Delta)^{s}w=0&\hbox{in}~\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}\times[0,\infty),\\
w(0,x)=\tilde{u}(x)&\hbox{in}~\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}\times\{t=0\}.\end{cases} 



We claim that:




 |v|\leq w. 



Indeed,  |v|-w  is a subsolution and




 \max_{\partial\Omega\times[0,T)}|v|-w=\max_{\partial\Omega\times[0,T)}-w\leq 0, 



where the last inequality used the maximum principle for  w . Using maximum principle again, we conclude that  |v|\leq w . 
We also know:




 w(t,x)=\int_{\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}}p(x-y,t)u(y)dy, 



where  p(x,t)  is the fractional heat kernel. It is known, for example from [15],




 |p(x,t)|\leq C\min\{t^{-\frac{n}{2s}},\frac{t}{|x|^{n+2s}}\}\leq Ct^{-\frac{n}{2s}}. 



Therefore,




 \displaystyle|v(t,x)|\leq w(t,x)\leq\int_{\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}}|p(x-y,t)||u(y)|dy\leq Ct^{-\frac{n}{2s}}\|u\|_{L^{1}(\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k})}\leq Ct^{-\frac{n}{2s}}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k})}. 



Finally, we observe that:




 |u(x)|=|e^{\lambda t}v(t,x)|\leq Ce^{\lambda t}t^{-\frac{n}{2s}}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k})}\leq C\lambda^{\frac{n}{2s}}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k})}, 



where the last inequality follows from choosing  t=\frac{n}{2s\lambda} .
∎





Lemma 2.15.



(Cacciopoli inequality.) Let  u\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{k}  be the weak solution of



(2.16)

 (-\Delta)^{s}u=f\text{ in }\{u\neq 0\}, 



where  f\in L^{1}(U) , for some  U\subset\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}  open, containing  \{u\neq 0\} . Then for any  B_{r}\subset\{u\neq 0\} ,



(2.17)

 \int_{B_{9r/10}}\int_{B_{9r/10}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\leq\sup_{B_{r}}u\|f\|_{L^{1}(B_{r})}+Cr^{n}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k})}\sup_{B_{r}}|u|. 







Proof.



Let  \eta  be a smooth cutoff such that  \eta=1  in  B_{9r/10}  and  \eta=0  outside  B_{r} , and  |\nabla\eta|\leq Cr^{-1} . Multiply (2.16) by  \eta^{2}u  and integration by part gives




 \int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(\eta(x)u(x)-\eta(y)u(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}=\int_{\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}}f\eta^{2}u\leq\sup_{B_{r}}u\|f\|_{L^{1}(B_{r})}. 



We note that for the left hand side,




 \int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\leq\int_{B_{9r/10}}\int_{B_{9r/10}}+2\int_{B_{9r/10}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus B_{9r/10}} 



with the same integrand. The first term above is the one that we are estimating, and we proceed to estimate the second term. We note that for the integrand, we can rewrite as follows



(2.18)

 \displaystyle(u(x)-u(y))(\eta(x)u(x)-\eta(y)u(y)) 
 \displaystyle=(\eta(x)u(x)-\eta(y)u(y))^{2} 


(2.19)


 \displaystyle+\eta(x)u(x)u(y)(\eta(y)-\eta(x))+\eta(y)u(x)u(y)(\eta(x)-\eta(y)) 




 \displaystyle=(\eta(x)u(x)-\eta(y)u(y))^{2}-u(x)u(y)(\eta(x)-\eta(y))^{2}. 



We note that the first term is positive, and for the second term, we have



(2.20)

 \displaystyle\int_{B_{r}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{u(x)u(y)(\eta(x)-\eta(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}} 
 \displaystyle=\int_{B_{r}}\int_{|x-y|<1}\frac{u(x)u(y)(\eta(x)-\eta(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}} 




 \displaystyle+\int_{B_{r}}\int_{|x-y|>1}\frac{u(x)u(y)(\eta(x)-\eta(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}. 



For the  |x-y|<1  piece, we use that  |\eta(x)-\eta(y)|<|x-y| ,




 \int_{B_{r}}\int_{|x-y|<1}\frac{u(x)u(y)(\eta(x)-\eta(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\leq\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k})}\sup_{B_{r}}|u|\int_{|x-y|<1}\int_{B_{r}}\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\leq Cr^{n}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k})}\sup_{B_{r}}|u|. 



For the  |x-y|>1  piece, we use  |\eta|<1 ,




 \int_{B_{r}}\int_{|x-y|>1}\frac{u(x)u(y)(\eta(x)-\eta(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\leq\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k})}\sup_{B_{r}}|u|\int_{|x-y|>1}\int_{B_{r}}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\leq Cr^{n}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k})}\sup_{B_{r}}|u|. 



Combining all the terms, we have




 \int_{B_{9r/10}}\int_{B_{9r/10}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\leq\sup_{B_{r}}u\|f\|_{L^{1}(B_{r})}+Cr^{n}\sup_{B_{r}}|u|. 



∎





3. Boundedness of Shape Minimizers




In this section, we make use of the observation of Bucur in [6] that our shape minimizer,  A=\{u\neq 0\}  in problem (2.5) is a priori a local shape subsolution. Then we prove that if  A  is a local shape subsolution, then it must be bounded.





Define the torsion energy:



(3.1)

 E(A):=\min_{u\in\tilde{W}^{s,2}(\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k})}\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy-\int_{\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}}u(x)dx. 



We note that if  u_{A}  minimizes  E(A) , then  u_{A}  satisfies



(3.2)

 \displaystyle\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{s}u_{A}-1=0\ &\hbox{in}~A\\
u_{A}=0&\hbox{in}~\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}\setminus A\end{cases} 



in the weak sense. It follows that  u_{A}\geq 0 . Define the corresponding  \gamma  distance between  A,B  as



(3.3)

 d_{\gamma}(A,B):=\int_{\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}}|u_{A}-u_{B}|dx 



where  u_{A} ,  u_{B}  minimize  E(A),E(B)  respectively and are extended by  0 .





We first prove a uniform bound of  E(A) .




Definition 3.4.



(Symmetric decreasing rearrangement [4].) Let  f:\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{+} ,  \alpha(n)  denote the volume of unit ball in  {\mathbb{R}}^{n} . Define the radius  R_{f}(y)  to be such that



(3.5)

 \alpha(n)R_{f}(y)^{n}=\int_{\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}}\chi_{\{f(x)>y\}}dx. 



We define the symmetrization  f^{*}  of  f  to be



(3.6)

 f^{*}(x)=\sup\{y:R_{f}(y)>|x|\}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\chi_{\{B_{0}(R_{f}(y))\}}(x)dy. 







Lemma 3.7.





(3.8)

 E(B)\leq E(A)(\leq 0) 



where  B  is a ball and  |B|=|A| .





Proof.



Let  w_{A}  be a function that attains the minimum of (3.1). Let  w_{A}^{*}  be the symmetric rearrangement of  w_{A}  defined above, then it follows from Theorem 9.2[4] that



(3.9)


 \displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}w_{A}^{*}dx=\int_{\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}}w_{A}dx, 


(3.10)


 \displaystyle B[w_{A}^{*},w_{A}^{*}]\leq B[w_{A},w_{A}]. 



Therefore,



(3.11)

 E(B)\leq\frac{1}{2}B[w_{A}^{*},w_{A}^{*}]-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}w_{A}^{*}dx\leq\frac{1}{2}B[w_{A},w_{A}]-\int_{\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}}w_{A}dx=E(A). 



∎





Corollary 3.12.





(3.13)

 E(A)\leq C|A|^{\frac{n+2s}{n}}. 







Proof.



It is known from [26] that for any  r>0 ,  x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n} , the solution of



(3.14)

 \displaystyle\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{s}u=1\ &\hbox{in}~B_{r}(x_{0})\\
u=0&\hbox{in}~{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus B_{r}(x_{0})\end{cases} 



is given explicitly by



(3.15)

 u(x)=\frac{2^{-2s}\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{n+2s}{2})\Gamma(1+s)}(r^{2}-|x-x_{0}|^{2})^{s}. 



Therefore, for  |B|=Cr^{n} , we have



(3.16)

 |E(A)|\leq|E(B)|=|\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}u|\leq Cr^{n+2s}. 



Since  |A|=|B| , the inequality of the Corollary follows.
∎





Definition 3.17.



We say that a measurable set  A  of finite measure is a local shape subsolution for the torsion energy problem (3.1) if there exists  \delta>0  and  \Lambda>0  such that for every measurable set  \tilde{A}  satisfying  |A\setminus\tilde{A}|=0 ,  d_{\gamma}(A,\tilde{A})\leq\delta , we have:



(3.18)

 E(A)+\Lambda|A|\leq E(\tilde{A})+\Lambda|\tilde{A}|. 







By an observation of Bucur in [6], the  \lambda_{k} -minimizer of our problem (1.1) is a local subsolution in the sense of above definition. The proof of the following theorem is given in the Appendix.




Theorem 3.19.



Let  A  be a  \lambda_{k} -minimizer of (1.1). Then  A  is a local shape subsolution for the torsion energy problem (3.1).





The key ingredient in proving the boundedness of subsolutions is the following non-degeneracy lemma.

4. Existence of Shape Minimizers




Let  \Omega=B_{R}  be a ball with radius  R  in  {\mathbb{R}}^{n} . We first show that  \lambda_{k} -minimizers over sets in  B_{R}  exists, and then show that we can take  R\rightarrow\infty . The strategy is to first construct  A  as the positivity set of the functions  u_{i}  such that the minimum below is obtained, and then show that this  A  is the  \lambda_{k} -minimizer of problem (1.1). Let



(4.1)

 \displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{k}=\inf_{u_{i}\in\tilde{W}^{s,2}(\Omega)}\max_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\setminus\{0\}}\left(R[\sum_{1}^{k}\alpha_{i}u_{i}]\ s.t.\ \sum_{1}^{k}|\{u_{i}\neq 0\}|\leq C,(u_{i},u_{j})_{W^{2,s}}=\delta_{ij}\right). 





(4.2)

 \displaystyle\lambda_{k}(A)=\inf_{u_{i}\in\tilde{W}^{s,2}(\Omega)}\max_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\setminus\{0\}}\left(R[\sum_{1}^{k}\alpha_{i}u_{i}]\ s.t.\ u_{i}=0\ a.e.\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus A,(u_{i},u_{j})_{W^{2,s}}=\delta_{ij}\right). 



Here  R[\cdot]  is the Rayleigh quotient (2.9).




Lemma 4.3.



The minimum in (4.1) is attained for some  u\in\tilde{W}^{s,2}(\Omega)  and  |\{u\neq 0\}|\leq C .





Proof.



Take a minimizing sequence  R(u^{l})\rightarrow\tilde{\lambda}_{k} ,




 u^{l}=\frac{\sum_{1}^{k}\alpha^{l}_{i}u_{i}^{l}}{\|\sum_{1}^{k}\alpha^{l}_{i}u_{i}^{l}\|_{L^{2}}}. 



Since  R(u^{l})  is bounded, and that  \|u^{l}\|_{L^{2}}=1 , we know  {u^{l}}  is bounded in  \tilde{W}^{s,2}(\Omega) . Thus  u_{l}\rightarrow u  for some  u  weakly in  \tilde{W}^{s,2}(\Omega) . By compact embedding,  u^{l}\rightarrow u  strongly in  L^{2}(\Omega) . Thus, there exists a subsequence  u^{l}\rightarrow u  pointwise a.e. Since the volume of level sets are lower-semicontinuous with respect to pointwise convergence, we have




 |\{u>0\}|+|\{u<0\}|\leq\liminf(|\{u^{l}>0\}|+|\{u^{l}<0\}|)\leq C. 



∎





Theorem 4.4.



The  \lambda_{k} -minimizer of problem (1.1) exists in the sense of Definition 2.3.





Proof.



Take  \Omega=B_{R} , we first show that the minimizer exists over sets contained in  B_{R}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n} . Let  u_{R}=\sum_{1}^{k}\alpha_{i}u_{i}  where the  \{u_{i}\} ’s are the minimizers in (4.1), and let  A_{R}=\{u_{R}\neq 0\} . We first observe that  \tilde{\lambda}_{k}\leq\lambda_{k}(A_{R})  since  \{u:u=0  a.e. in  {\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus A_{R}\}\subset\{u:|\{u\neq 0\}|\leq C\} . Moreover, since the the infimum in (4.1) is achieved by construction in (4.2), we have




 \tilde{\lambda}_{k}=\lambda_{k}(A_{R}), 



that is,  u_{R}  is the  k -th eigenvalue of  A_{R}  and  A_{R}  is the minimizing set.




We’d like to show that as  R\rightarrow\infty , the shape minimizer  A_{R}  converges in some sense. Indeed, by Theorem 3.42 and the remark that follows, we can find  R_{0}>0  such that for all  R>R_{0} , we have  u_{R}^{i}\in\tilde{W}^{s,2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}) ,  1\leq i\leq k  such that

(1) 



 u_{R}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{R}^{i} .

(2) 



 \{u_{R_{0}}^{i}\neq 0\}  is bounded.

(3) 



 u_{R}^{i}  is a translation of  u_{R_{0}}^{i} .

(4) 



 \text{dist}(\{u_{R}^{i}\neq 0\},\{u_{R}^{j}\neq 0\})\rightarrow\infty  as  R\rightarrow\infty  for all  i\neq j .






Now we consider  u_{R_{0}}^{i}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{i} , and define



(4.5)

 \displaystyle u 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{R_{0}}^{i}\subset\mathfrak{S}_{k}, 



 \displaystyle A 
 \displaystyle=\{u\neq 0\}\subset\mathcal{R}_{k}^{n}. 



We observe that  \lambda_{k}(A_{R})\rightarrow\lambda_{k}(A) , and  |A|=|A_{R}| .




We claim that  A  is the  \lambda_{k} -minimizer of (2.5). If not, there exists  A^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{R}_{k}^{n}  such that  |A^{\prime}|=|A|  and  \lambda_{k}(A^{\prime})<\lambda_{k}(A) . By Theorem 3.42 and the remark that follows, we can find a minimizing sequence  u^{\prime}_{R}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{u^{\prime}_{R}}^{i}  that satisfies (1)-(4) above. Let  A^{\prime}_{R}=\{u^{\prime}_{R}\neq 0\} , then  \lambda_{k}(A^{\prime}_{R})\rightarrow\lambda_{k}(A^{\prime})<\lambda_{k}(A)  and  |A^{\prime}_{R}|=|A^{\prime}|=|A| , but this contradicts the minimality of  \lambda_{k}(A_{R}) , which finished the proof of claim and the theorem.

Remark 7.29.



Let  u_{r}=\frac{u(rx)}{r^{s}} , then  W(1,u_{r})=W(r,u) .





One important consequence of the above almost monotonicity formula is that it shows that the blow up, defined as the limit of




 u_{r}=\frac{u(rx)}{r^{s}}\rightarrow u_{0} 



is homogeneous of degree  s . We first show that  u_{0}  satisfy a PDE on the compliment of  \Lambda(u) .




Lemma 7.30.



Let  u_{r}\rightarrow u_{0}  be a blow up, we have




 {\rm div}(y^{a}\nabla u_{0})=0\text{ in }\mathcal{R}^{n+1}_{k}\setminus\{y=0\}. 







Proof.



Since  u_{r}\rightarrow u_{0}  in  H^{1}(a,U) ,  u_{0}  satisfy  {\rm div}(y^{a}\nabla u_{0})=0  on  \mathcal{R}^{n+1}_{k}\setminus\{y=0\} . On the thin space  \mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}\times\{0\} , we have




 (-\Delta)^{s}u_{k}=\lambda_{k}u_{k}. 



Since




 (-\Delta)^{s}u_{k}=-\lim_{y\rightarrow 0}y^{a}\partial_{y}u(x,y). 



So  u_{r}  satisfies




 (-\Delta)^{s}u_{r}^{\prime}=r^{2s}\lambda_{k}u_{r}^{\prime}. 



Since  u_{r}^{\prime}\rightarrow u_{0}^{\prime}  uniformly locally, we have  (-\Delta)^{s}u_{0}^{\prime}=0  on  \{u_{0}^{\prime}\neq 0\} , and thus




 -\lim_{y\rightarrow 0}y^{a}\partial_{y}u_{0}(x,y)=0. 



So, the even reflection satisfies  {\rm div}(y^{a}\nabla u_{0})=0  on  \mathcal{R}_{k}^{n}\times\{0\}\cap\{u_{0}\neq 0\}  (by [9] Lemma 4.1).
∎





Corollary 7.31.



Let  u_{r}\rightarrow u_{0}  be a blow up at  (x_{0},0) , then  u_{0}  is homogeneous of degree  s .





Proof.



By (10.29) and optimal regularity,  W(0+,u,x_{0})  is bounded from below.
We claim that




 W(r,u)=W(1,u_{r})\rightarrow W(1,u_{0})=W(0,u_{r})=L. 



and



(7.32)

 W(1,u_{0})=\frac{1}{r^{n}}\left(\int_{B_{1}}|y|^{a}|\nabla u_{0}|^{2}+\int_{B_{1}^{\prime}}\chi_{\{u_{0}\neq 0\}}\right)-\frac{s}{r^{n-1}}\int_{\partial B_{1}}|y|^{a}u_{0}^{2}=L. 



We first note that if the claim is true, then the above equation grantees that  u_{0}  is homogeneous of degree  s . We proceed to the proof of the claim.




Taking  r\rightarrow 0  in (7.2) we wish to show that the three terms converge to the corresponding limit respectively. By Corollary 5.21, the third term converges to the corresponding term in (7.32).
For the first term, let  \eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}  with  \eta>0 , and a sequence  \epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}  such that  \{u_{0}\neq 0\}  is a neighborhood of supp  \eta . We have




 \int|y|^{a}|\nabla u_{0}|^{2}=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int|y|^{a}\nabla u_{0}\nabla(u_{0}-\epsilon)_{+}\eta=-\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int|y|^{a}(u_{0}-\epsilon)_{+}\nabla\eta\nabla u_{0}=-\int|y|^{a}u_{0}\nabla\eta\nabla u_{0}. 



where the first and last equality follows from dominated convergence theorem, and the second equality uses integration by part with respect to the measure  |y|^{a}dx  and that  u_{0}  is harmonic on  \{u_{0}>\epsilon\} . Note that above calculation also works if we replace  u_{0}  by  u_{r} , and thus




 \int|y|^{a}|\nabla u_{0}|^{2}=-\int|y|^{a}u_{0}\nabla\eta\nabla u_{0}=-\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}|y|^{a}u_{r}\nabla\eta\nabla u_{r}=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\int|\nabla u_{r}|^{2}\eta. 



This shows that  u_{r}\rightarrow u_{0}  in  H^{1}_{\text{loc}}(a,U) , and thus




 \int_{B_{1}}|y|^{a}|\nabla u_{r}|^{2}\rightarrow\int_{B_{1}}|y|^{a}|\nabla u_{0}|^{2}. 






It is left to check that




 \int_{B_{r}^{\prime}}\chi_{\{u_{r}\neq 0\}}\rightarrow\int_{B_{1}^{\prime}}\chi_{\{u_{0}\neq 0\}}. 






First we note that  1_{\{u_{r}>0\}}\leq 1_{\{u_{0}>0\}}  by lower semi-continuity and  C^{\beta}  convergence (Prop 5.21). We proceed to check the other inequality.




Let  x  be such that  u(x)=0 , and there exists a sequence  x_{r}\rightarrow x  such that  u_{r}(x_{r})>0 . Fix  \epsilon>0 , for all  r>0 , there exists  y_{r}\in B_{\epsilon}(x)  such that  u^{\prime}_{r}(y_{r})\geq c\epsilon^{s} . Since  u\rightarrow u_{0}  uniformly in compact sets, we have  y_{r}\rightarrow y_{0}\in B_{\epsilon}(x)  such that  u^{\prime}_{0}(y_{0})\geq c\epsilon^{s} . Moreover, by Hölder continuity, there exists  \delta>0 ,  B_{\delta\epsilon}(y_{0})\subset B_{\epsilon}(x)  such that  u^{\prime}_{0}(B_{\delta\epsilon}(y_{0}))\geq c\epsilon^{s} .
This implies that




 \sup_{B_{\epsilon}(x)}u^{\prime}_{0}>C\epsilon^{s},\text{ and }|\{u_{0}>0\}\cup B_{r}|>Cr^{n}, 



that is,  x  is in the measure theoretic closure of  {\{u^{\prime}_{0}>0\}} . By Lebesgue density theorem, the measure theoretic boundary satisfies  |\partial_{*}\{u^{\prime}_{0}>0\}|=0 . The case where  x\in\{u_{0}<0\}  follows from the same argument and the claim follows.
∎





8. Separation of the Free Boundary and Density Estimates




We have established that if the minimizing eigenvalue  \lambda_{k}(A)  of problem (1.1) is simple, then the corresponding eigenfunction  u_{k}  is  C^{0,s}  and non-degenerate. In this section we follow the idea of [2] to show that the free boundary is separated.




Let  u_{k}  be the corresponding eigenfunction to  \lambda_{k} . Let  u(x,y)=u(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n},y)  be the extension of  u_{k} ,  u_{r}\rightarrow u_{0}  be a blow up of  u . Let



(8.1)

 \Lambda(u)=\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}\times\{0\}\cap\{u=0\}.

10. The Global Configuration and a Toy Problem




In light of Theorem 8.3, the  \lambda_{k} -minimizer  A\subset\mathcal{R}^{n}_{k}  is a disjoint union of connected components for  k>2 . We wish to study how translating the disconnected components of  A  affect  \lambda_{k} .




Let  u_{k}  be the eigenfunction corresponding to the minimizing  \lambda_{k} . Let  u=u_{k}|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{i}}  for any  1\leq i\leq k , choose  \|u\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}=1 . Let  A=\{u\neq 0\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n} , then we can write



(10.1)

 A=A_{1}\cup A_{2},\text{ with }A_{1}\cap A_{2}=\varnothing. 



Let  C={\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus\{A_{1}\cup A_{2}\} . We investigate how translating  A_{1}  and  A_{2}  affects  \lambda_{k} . We decompose the region of integration  {\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}  in the following way.



(10.2)

 \displaystyle\lambda_{k}(A)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}=\int_{A_{1}}\int_{A_{1}} 
 \displaystyle+\int_{A_{2}}\int_{A_{2}}+\int_{C}\int_{C} 


(10.3)


 \displaystyle+2\int_{A_{1}}\int_{A_{2}}+2\int_{A_{1}}\int_{C}+2\int_{A_{2}}\int_{C}. 



We first note that the integrals on  A_{1}\times A_{1} ,  A_{2}\times A_{2} , and  C\times C  (which is  0 ), are invariant under translation. To study the cross terms, we have



(10.4)

 \displaystyle\int_{A_{1}}\int_{C}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}} 
 \displaystyle=\int_{A_{1}}\int_{C}\frac{u(y)^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}} 


(10.5)


 \displaystyle=\int_{A_{1}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{u(y)^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}-\int_{A_{1}}\int_{A_{1}}\frac{u(y)^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}-\int_{A_{1}}\int_{A_{2}}\frac{u(y)^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}} 


(10.6)

 \displaystyle\int_{A_{2}}\int_{C}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}} 
 \displaystyle=\int_{A_{2}}\int_{C}\frac{u(y)^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}} 




 \displaystyle=\int_{A_{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{u(y)^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}-\int_{A_{2}}\int_{A_{1}}\frac{u(y)^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}-\int_{A_{2}}\int_{A_{2}}\frac{u(y)^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}} 



where the only non-invariant pieces are the integrals on  A_{1}\times A_{2} . Combining these two integrals with the the integral on  A_{1}\times A_{2}  in (10.2) gives



(10.7)

 \displaystyle\int_{A_{1}}\int_{A_{2}}\frac{(u(x)^{2}-2u(x)u(y)+u(y)^{2})}{|x-y|^{n+2s}} 
 \displaystyle-\int_{A_{1}}\int_{A_{2}}\frac{u(y)^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}} 


(10.8)


 \displaystyle-\int_{A_{2}}\int_{A_{1}}\frac{u(y)^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}=\int_{A_{1}}\int_{A_{2}}\frac{-2u(x)u(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}. 



This is the only non-invariant term under translation of  A_{1} ,  A_{2} . If  u  has the same sign on  A_{1},A_{2} ,  u(x)u(y)>0  then  \lambda_{k}  decreases by moving  A_{1},A_{2}  closer, and if  u  has different signs,  u(x)u(y)<0  then  \lambda_{k}  decreases by moving  A_{1},A_{2}  further.




If  A  is connected, we expect  A  to be a ball by symmetric decreasing rearrangements. If  A\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}  has more than one disjoint connected components, by Theorem 8.3,  u  has a sign on each connected component. We can choose  A_{1}  to be any union of the the connected components with the same sign, and  A_{2}=A\setminus A_{1} . Since  A  is a  \lambda_{k} -minimizer,  \lambda_{k}  cannot be reduced by translating any union of connected components. We believe this is false and one can always reduce the energy in this case. Thus, we expect that a  \lambda_{k} -minimizer is a union of balls in different copies of  {\mathbb{R}}^{n} .




Conjecture 10.9.



For  k\geq 2 , let  A  be a  \lambda_{k} -minimizer, and  u_{k}  the corresponding eigenfunction. If  \lambda_{k}  is simple, then  \{u_{k}|_{{\mathbb{R}}_{i}^{n}}\neq 0\}  is a ball for all  i .





We observe that since  u  is not sign-changing on each of the balls, then  u  must be the first eigenfunction on each ball. Since  \lambda_{k}  is a minimizing eigenvalue,  A  has to consists of  k  disjoint balls of the same size. In particular, it follows that




 \lambda_{1}(A)=\cdots=\lambda_{k}(A), 



which contradicts that  \lambda_{k}  is simple. Therefore, Conjecture 10.9 implies the following.




Corollary of Conjecture 10.10.



For  k\geq 2 , let  A  be a  \lambda_{k} -minimizer, then  \lambda_{k}(A)  is not simple.





Remark 10.11.



(1) 



Corollary of Conjecture 10.10 is the nonlocal analogy of the conjecture made in [20] which states that in the local case, such minimizing  \lambda_{k}  is not simple.

(2) 



Conjecture 10.9 is true if  A  consists of  m=2,3  disjoint connected sets. When there are two disjoint connected sets, if  u  has the same sign on them,  \lambda_{k}(A)  decreases by translating them close together, and if  u  has different signs on each of them,  \lambda_{k}(A)  decreases by translating them further away in the direction opposite of each other. If  m=3 , either they all have the same sign, in which case moving them closer together decreases  \lambda_{k}(A) , or exactly one of them has a different sign, in which case, moving this one away from the other two decreases the energy. We note that we can always do above translations smoothly when  m=2,3 , and these perturbations keep the order of the eigenvalues.

(3) 



If  A  consists of  m>3  disjoint connected sets, let  A^{+}=\{u>0\} ,  A^{-}=\{u<0\} . We note that we can put  A^{+} ,  A^{-}  in different copies of  {\mathbb{R}}^{n} , or equivalently, put  A^{+}  and  A^{-}  infinitely away from each other, which reduces the energy in (10.2). However, it is not clear that this reduced energy corresponds to the  k-th  eigenvalue.

(4) 



As  s\rightarrow 1 , our  (-\Delta)^{s}\varphi=-\Delta\varphi  for any  \varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}) . Thus, this nonlocal problem (1.1) offers a new perspective in the study of the minimizer of the  k-th  eigenvalue of  -\Delta .

∎





10.2. Proof of theorem 5.19




Proof.



Let  x_{0}  be a free boundary point. Let  h  be the  s -harmonic lifting of  u_{\alpha}  in a ball  B_{r}(x_{0})  (similarly defined as in (5.1)). Let  \tilde{h}  be its extension. Let  u  the the extension of  u_{\alpha} , then for  \rho<r<1 ,



(10.26)

 \displaystyle\int_{B_{\rho}(x_{0},0)}|y|^{a}|\nabla{u}|^{2} 
 \displaystyle\leq 2\int_{B_{\rho}}|y|^{a}|\nabla({u}-\tilde{h})|^{2}+2\int_{B_{\rho}}|y|^{a}|\nabla\tilde{h}|^{2} 


(10.27)


 \displaystyle\leq 2\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{((u_{\alpha}-h)(x)-(u_{\alpha}-h)(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}+2\left(\frac{\rho}{r}\right)^{n+1-|a|}\int_{B_{r}}|y|^{a}|\nabla\tilde{h}|^{2} 


(10.28)


 \displaystyle\leq Cr^{n}+C\left(\frac{\rho}{r}\right)^{n+1-|a|}\int_{B_{r}}|y|^{a}|\nabla\tilde{h}|^{2} 




 \displaystyle\leq Cr^{n}+C\left(\frac{\rho}{r}\right)^{n+1-|a|}\int_{B_{r}}|y|^{a}|\nabla u|^{2}. 



By Proposition 5.4 and Lemma 5.17. Now choose  \delta<1/2  with




 r=\delta^{k},\quad\rho=\delta^{k+1},\quad\mu=\delta^{n}, 



we obtain




 \int_{B_{\delta^{k+1}}}|y|^{a}|\nabla{u}|^{2}\leq C\mu^{k}+C\mu\delta^{1-|a|}\int_{B_{\delta^{k}}}|y|^{a}|\nabla u|^{2}. 



Now choose  \delta  such that  C\delta^{1-|a|}<1 . By induction, we have




 \int_{B_{\delta^{k}}}|x_{n}|^{a}|\nabla u|^{2}\leq\frac{C^{2}}{1-C\delta^{1-|a|}}\mu^{k-1}. 



Then for all  r<1/2 , and a different constant,



(10.29)

 \int_{B_{r}(x^{\prime},0)}|x_{n}|^{a}|\nabla u|^{2}\leq Cr^{n}, 



and we can conclude that




 \int_{B_{r}(x^{\prime},0)}|\nabla u|\leq Cr^{n+s}. 



Now we can use Morrey’s theorem to conclude that



(10.30)

 |u(x^{\prime},0)-\overline{u_{B}}|\leq Cr^{s} 



so that  u  is  C^{0,s}  in the thin space  \mathcal{R}_{k}^{n}\times\{0\} . It is left to show that we have the same Hölder growth in the  |x_{n}|  direction. For a fixed point  (y^{\prime},0) , we consider



(10.31)

 u_{r}(x)=\frac{u((y^{\prime},0)+xr)-u(y^{\prime},0))}{r^{s}}. 



By (10.29), we have a universal  L^{2}  gradient bound in  B^{*}=B_{1/2}(0,\cdots,0,1) . Using (10.30) for  |u_{r}| , we have that the average of  |u_{r}|  on  B_{3/2}(0)  is universally bounded. Thus, the average of  |u_{r}|  over  B^{*}  is universally bounded. By Poincare inequality in  B^{*} , we have




 \|u_{r}\|_{{W^{1,2}}(B_{1/2}(0,\cdots,0,1))}\leq C. 



By first variation,  {\rm div}(|x_{n+1}|\nabla|u_{r}|)=0  if  |x_{n+1}|>0 . Away from the thin space, we use regularity theory for uniformly elliptic equations and conclude that each  u_{r}  is continuous in  B^{*}  and we have the weak Harnack inequality




 \|u_{r}\|_{W^{1,2}(B_{1/4}(0,\cdots,0,1))}\leq C. 



This shows Hölder growth off the thin space, that is,




 \frac{|u(y^{\prime},0)-u(x)|}{|(y^{\prime},0)-x|^{s}}\leq C. 



Let  x,y\in B_{1} . If  |y_{n+1}|\leq|x-y| , we may use the above to bound




 \frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|^{s}}. 



If  |y_{n+1}|>|x-y| , we then rescale with




 u_{r}=\frac{u((x^{\prime},0)+xr)-u(x^{\prime},0))}{r^{s}} 



and use interior gradient bounds on uniformly elliptic equations to conclude




 \frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|^{s}}\leq C. 



∎
