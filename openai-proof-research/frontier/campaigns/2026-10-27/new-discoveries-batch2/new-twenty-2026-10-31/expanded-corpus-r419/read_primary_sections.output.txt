                                                  Abstract. A frame is scalable if each of its vectors can be rescaled in such a way
                                                  that the resulting set becomes a Parseval frame. In this paper, we consider four
                                                  different optimization problems for determining if a frame is scalable. We offer
                                                  some algorithms to solve these problems. We then apply and extend our methods
                                                  to the problem of reweighing (finite) graph so as to minimize the condition number
                                                  of the resulting Laplacian.

44:     We confirm a conjecture by Everett, Sinclair, and Dankelmann [Some Centrality
45: results new and old, J. Math. Sociology 28 (2004), 215–227] regarding the problem of
46: maximizing closeness centralization in two-mode data, where the number of data of
47: each type is fixed. Intuitively, our result states that among all networks obtainable
48: via two-mode data, the largest closeness is achieved by simply locally maximizing
49: the closeness of a node. Mathematically, our study concerns bipartite graphs with
50: fixed size bipartitions, and we show that the extremal configuration is a rooted tree
51: of depth 2, where neighbors of the root have an equal or almost equal number of
52: children.

57: 1     Introduction
58: A social network is often conveniently modeled by a graph: nodes represent individual
61: relation R, which pairs of individual are in relation according to R.
62:     Centrality is a crucial concept in studying social networks [8, 12]. It can be seen
63: as a measure of how central is the position of an individual in a social network.
64: Various node-based measures of the centrality have been proposed to determine the
65: relative importance of a node within a graph (the reader is referred to the work
66: of Koschützki et al. [9] for an overview). Some widely used centrality measures are
67: the degree centrality, the betweenness centrality, the closeness centrality and the
68: eigenvector centrality (definitions and extended discussions are found in the book
69: edited by Brandes and Erlebach [5]).
70:     We focus on closeness centrality, which measures how close a node is to all other
71: nodes in the graph: the smaller the total distance from a node v to all other nodes,
72: the more important the node v is. Various closeness-based measures have been
73: developed [1, 2, 4, 13, 11, 14, 16, 13].
74:     Let us see an example: suppose we want to place a service facility, e.g., a school,
75: such that the total distance to all inhabitants in the region is minimal. This would
76: make the chosen location as convenient as possible for most inhabitants. In social
77: network analysis the centrality index based on this concept is called closeness cen-
78: trality.
