A family of spanning trees of a graph is t -intersecting if any pair of spanning trees in the family have t or more edges in common. For sufficiently large n and t\leq n/C\log_{2}n for some absolute constant C>0 , we give a nearly complete characterization of the extremal t -intersecting families of spanning trees in balanced complete bipartite graphs with parts of order n . In particular, for t=1 , we give exact bounds and a full characterization of the extremal families. For t\geq 2 , our bounds are tight up to lower-order terms, and we show that any extremal t -intersecting family is of the form \mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\prime} where \mathcal{F} is a family of all trees containing a fixed t -matching, and \mathcal{S}^{\prime} is a distinguished set of exceptional trees of size |\mathcal{S}^{\prime}|=o(|\mathcal{F}|) . 1. Introduction We say that two spanning trees of a graph t -intersect if they have t or more edges in common. A family of spanning trees of a graph is t -intersecting if every two members of the family t -intersect. (We often omit “ t -” if t=1 .) In recent work [ 3 ] , Frankl, Hurlbert, Ihringer, Kupavskii, Lindzey, Meagher, and Pantangi investigated t -intersecting families of spanning trees in the complete graph K_{n} on n vertices and proved the following Erdős–Ko–Rado-type theorem. Theorem 1.1 (Frankl et al. [ 3 ] ) . For sufficiently large n and 2\leq t\leq\frac{n}{4032\log_{2}n} , the largest t -intersecting families of spanning trees of K_{n} are those obtained by taking all spanning trees that contain a fixed t -matching, i.e., a set of t disjoint edges of K_{n} . For the t=1 case, they show that one must also include all of the stars , i.e., trees on n vertices with a single non-leaf vertex, as any such spanning tree intersects every other spanning tree of K_{n} . Several open questions were posed in [ 3 ] , some of which have already been resolved. Saengrungkongka [ 16 ] proved [ 3 , Conjecture 7.1] , and Iarovikova and Kupavskii [ 6 ] resolved the stronger conjecture [ 3 , Conjecture 7.2] regarding the complete characterization of the extremal t -intersecting families of spanning trees of K_{n} for sufficiently large n . In this work, we investigate an open question posed in [ 3 ] on t -intersecting families of spanning trees in other families of host graphs. Perhaps the most natural next step in this direction is to consider the bipartite analogue, namely, the balanced complete bipartite graph K_{n,n} (cf., [ 4 , 10 ] ) with parts of order n . While superficially similar to K_{n} , several essential properties of spanning trees do not hold in the bipartite setting, which presents new challenges. Our main result is essentially the bipartite analogue of Theorem 1.1 , which we distribute across three theorems, as the K_{n,n} case raises more exceptions than the K_{n} case. Before we state our main theorems, we give some definitions. A vertex of a spanning tree of K_{n,n} is called ( almost ) saturated if it is adjacent to all (but one) other vertices from the opposite part. A spanning tree having precisely one saturated vertex is called a star , while a spanning tree having two saturated vertices (belonging to different parts of K_{n,n} ) is called a bi-star . A spanning tree having two non-adjacent almost saturated vertices (again, belonging to different parts of K_{n,n} ) is called an almost bi-star . Theorem 1.2 . For sufficiently large n , an intersecting family \mathcal{F} of spanning trees of K_{n,n} has size no greater than |\mathcal{F}|\leq 2\cdot n^{2n-3}\left(1-\frac{1}{2n}\right)+2n^{n-2}(n-1)^{2}+(n-1)^{4}. Moreover, equality holds if and only if \mathcal{F} is a family obtained by taking all spanning trees that contain a fixed edge e of K_{n,n} along with all stars, bi-stars, and almost bi-stars that do not contain e . This gives an interesting example of an Erdős–Ko–Rado result where the trivial intersecting families (i.e., families obtained by taking all objects containing a fixed element) are not extremal and the size of an extremal family is appreciably larger than a trivial one. In particular, in the K_{n} case, the extremal intersecting families have just n-2 more trees than a trivial intersecting family \mathcal{F}[e] , whereas the extremal families for K_{n,n} have roughly \sqrt{|\mathcal{F}[e]|} more spanning trees than \mathcal{F}[e] . (Here, \mathcal{F}[e] denotes the family of all spanning trees of an ambient graph containing a fixed edge e .) Theorem 1.3 . For sufficiently large n , a 2 -intersecting family \mathcal{F} of spanning trees of K_{n,n} has size no greater than |\mathcal{F}|\leq 4\cdot n^{2n-4}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)+2n^{n}+n^{4}-2n^{3}. In particular, if \mathcal{F} is a largest 2 -intersecting family, then \mathcal{F} is of the form \mathcal{F}=\mathcal{T}_{n,n}[F]~\sqcup~\mathcal{S}_{n,n}^{\prime} where \mathcal{T}_{n,n}[F] is the set of all spanning trees of K_{n,n} containing a 2 -matching F of K_{n,n} , and \mathcal{S}_{n,n}^{\prime} is a set of size at most 2n^{n}+n^{4}-2n^{3} , consisting of all bi-stars of K_{n,n} , along with all stars and almost bi-stars having precisely one edge of F and no (almost) saturated vertex in F . Theorem 1.4 . For sufficiently large n and 3\leq t\leq\frac{n}{C\log_{2}n} for some absolute constant C>0 , a t -intersecting family \mathcal{F} of spanning trees of K_{n,n} has size no greater than |\mathcal{F}|\leq 2^{t}\cdot n^{2n-t-2}\left(1-\frac{t}{2n}\right)+2n^{n}+n^{4}-2n^{3}. In particular, if \mathcal{F} is a largest t -intersecting family, then \mathcal{F} is of the form \mathcal{F}=\mathcal{T}_{n,n}[F]~\sqcup~\mathcal{S}_{n,n}^{\prime} where \mathcal{T}_{n,n}[F] is the set of all spanning trees of K_{n,n} containing a t -matching F of K_{n,n} , and \mathcal{S}_{n,n}^{\prime} is a set of at most 2n^{n}+n^{4}-2n^{3} stars, all of which contain a (t-1) -matching of F and their saturated vertices are not in F . We note that the upper bounds in Theorem 1.3 and Theorem 1.4 are tight up to lower order terms. An exact characterization of the size and structure of the extremal families in Theorem 1.3 and Theorem 1.4 (i.e., an exact description of \mathcal{S}_{n,n}^{\prime} ) requires a delicate combinatorial analysis and is omitted here. The main technical component of the proof is an approximate count (Lemma 3.4 ) that can be seen as an estimation of certain probabilities (relevant to EKR-type results) in the probability space of all spanning trees of K_{n,n} . The analogous probability space for K_{n} is particularly well-behaved and is a classical subject in enumerative combinatorics (see [ 15 ] , for example). This was leveraged in [ 3 ] to make short work of some enumerative obstacles via the Lopsided Lovász Local Lemma (LLLL). Moving to the bipartite setting presents new challenges. For instance, if A_{e} is the event that an edge e appears in a random spanning tree of K_{n,n} , and two edges e,f are disjoint, then Lemma 2.1 shows that A_{e} and A_{f} are dependent (unlike the K_{n} case). In other words, the natural negative dependency graphs that arise in the LLLL argument of [ 3 ] are no longer negative dependency graphs in the bipartite case. The events A_{e},A_{f} however are asymptotically independent ; i.e., they are independent in the limit n\rightarrow\infty , but we are not aware of any LLLL variant that is applicable in this setting (see [ 1 ] for more details). We overcome this obstacle in Section 3 (Lemma 3.4 ) by using a collection of spectral techniques, e.g., Weyl inequalities, Kirchhoff’s matrix-tree theorem, and Laplacian eigenvalue estimates of trees. It is worth noting that the spectral proof of Lemma 3.4 can be modified to replace a part of the proof in [ 3 ] that uses the LLLL argument. There are other technical challenges that arise in the characterization of the extremal families due to graph-theoretical differences between K_{n} and K_{n,n} . For example, the only spanning trees that intersect every other spanning tree in K_{n} are the stars. Moreover, no spanning tree of K_{n} t -intersects every other spanning tree for t\geq 2 . On the other hand, there are \Omega(\exp(\sqrt{n})) -many isomorphism classes of spanning trees of K_{n,n} that 1-intersect every spanning tree, and there are also spanning trees that 2 -intersect every spanning tree. This presents more graph-theoretical challenges that were only of minor concern in the non-bipartite case. 2. Preliminaries 2.1. Spanning trees of K_{m,n} The following is a generalization of Cayley’s formula for the number of spanning trees and an analogue of Moon’s formula for counting spanning trees in complete graphs containing a fixed spanning forest [ 14 ] . Lemma 2.1 (Dong, Ge [ 2 ] ) . For any spanning forest F of K_{m,n} with connected components T_{1},\dots,T_{k} , the number of spanning trees containing F is (1) |{\mathcal{T}}_{m,n}[F]|=\frac{1}{mn}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}(m_{i}n+n_{i}m)\right)\left(1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{m_{i}n_{i}}{m_{i}n+n_{i}m}\right), where m_{i}=|X\cap V(T_{i})| and n_{i}=|Y\cap V(T_{i})| for all i=1,\ldots,k . To apply this result to any forest F , one extends F to a spanning forest by adding, if necessary, connected components T_{i} that are singletons (i.e., with \{n_{i},m_{i}\}=\{0,1\} ) consisting of the vertices of K_{m,n} not covered by F . We will use Lemma 2.1 with m=n to prove the following result. Lemma 2.2 . Next, let F^{\prime} be any forest of K_{n,n} on \ell edges that is not a matching. As described above, we may assume that the forest F^{\prime} is spanning; let k denote the number of its connected components. We break the calculations bounding the size of \mathcal{T}_{n,n}[F^{\prime}] into two parts. In the notation of Lemma 2.1 , we make the following two claims. • Claim 1: If n\geq\ell , then \prod_{i=1}^{k}n(m_{i}+n_{i})\leq\frac{3}{4}2^{\ell}n^{2n-\ell}. • Claim 2: If n\geq 3\ell , then 1-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{m_{i}n_{i}}{m_{i}+n_{i}}\leq\frac{6}{5}\left(1-\frac{\ell}{2n}\right). Further, say F^{\prime} has p non-singleton components which span \ell edges. As each connected component T_{i} with m_{i}+n_{i} vertices contains m_{i}+n_{i}-1 edges, we have \sum_{i=1}^{p}(m_{i}+n_{i})=\ell+p . This gives us \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}|V(T_{i})|=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}(m_{i}+n_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{p}(m_{i}+n_{i})+\sum_{i=p+1}^{\ell}1=\ell+p+\ell-p=2\ell. Therefore, the first \ell components span 2\ell vertices. Since each of the remaining 2n-2\ell vertices makes up a component of F^{\prime} , we have in total k=\ell+2n-2\ell=2n-\ell connected components. Noting that m_{i}+n_{i}=1 for any singleton component T_{i} , we see that \prod_{i=1}^{k}n(m_{i}+n_{i})=n^{2n-\ell}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}(m_{i}+n_{i}) for F^{\prime} . Let us show that \prod_{i=1}^{\ell}(m_{i}+n_{i})\leq\frac{3}{4}2^{\ell} . Since F^{\prime} is not a matching, its largest connected component has at least 3 vertices, i.e., m_{1}+n_{1}\geq 3 . We will iteratively repartition the largest component of F^{\prime} along with a singleton component to create a component with one fewer vertex and another one that is a disjoint edge. To simplify our notation, given a forest F^{\prime} on \ell edges, we let c(F^{\prime})=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}(m_{i}+n_{i}) be the product of the number of vertices in its first \ell components. So, c(F^{\prime})=(m_{1}+n_{1})\prod_{i=2}^{\ell}(m_{i}+n_{i}) . Suppose that F^{\prime} has exactly j components of order 2, where j\leq\ell-2 . Set F^{\prime}_{1}:=F^{\prime} . Without loss of generality, let the largest component T_{1} of F^{\prime}_{1} have a leaf x in the part X . As n\geq\ell , the part Y has a singleton component, say T_{k} . Now, remove from F_{1}^{\prime} the edge incident to the leaf x in T_{1} and add the edge between x and T_{k} . Thus, we have repartitioned the first and the last components of F^{\prime}_{1} into two components: one of order m_{1}+n_{1}-1 and another one of order 2 . We call the resulting forest F^{\prime}_{2} . Clearly, F^{\prime}_{2} has the same number of edges as F^{\prime}_{1} but one more non-singleton component on two vertices. As (m_{1}+n_{1}-1)\cdot 2>(m_{1}+n_{1})\cdot 1 if m_{1}+n_{1}\geq 3 , it follows that c(F^{\prime}_{2})>c(F^{\prime}_{1}) . If the largest component of F^{\prime}_{2} still has at least 3 vertices, we repeat the above process to obtain a forest F^{\prime}_{3} with c(F^{\prime}_{3})>c(F^{\prime}_{2}) , and continue so until we obtain F^{\prime}_{\ell-j}\cong M_{\ell} . As shown above, for all i=1,\ldots,\ell-j-1 , we have that c(F^{\prime}_{i+1})>c(F^{\prime}_{i}) . Moreover, F^{\prime}_{\ell-j-1} has its first \ell components of orders 3,2,\ldots,2,1 . Thus, c(F^{\prime}_{\ell-j-1})=3\cdot 2^{\ell-2}=\frac{3}{4}2^{\ell}\quad\text{ and }\quad c(F^{\prime})\leq c(F^{\prime}_{\ell-j-1})=\frac{3}{4}2^{\ell} for all forests F^{\prime}\not\cong M_{\ell} on \ell edges, which proves the first claim. To prove the second claim, note that since n\geq 3\ell , we have that \frac{6}{5}\left(1-\frac{\ell}{2n}\right)\geq\frac{6}{5}\left(1-\frac{1}{6}\right)=1. However, 1-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{m_{i}n_{i}}{m_{i}+n_{i}}\leq 1 , with equality if and only if F^{\prime} has no edges. Thus, 1-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{m_{i}n_{i}}{m_{i}+n_{i}}\leq 1\leq\frac{6}{5}\left(1-\frac{\ell}{2n}\right), which proves the claim. Lemma 2.3 . |{\mathcal{S}}_{m,n}|=mn(n^{m-2}+m^{n-2}+mn-m-n) . In particular, we have |{\mathcal{S}}_{n,n}|=2n^{n}+n^{4}-2n^{3} . Proof. Suppose a spanning tree T of K_{m,n} has a saturated vertex in X . As every other vertex of X is adjacent in T to precisely one vertex in Y , there are n^{m-1} different choices for the remaining edges of T . Thus, there are mn^{m-1} spanning trees having a saturated vertex in X . Taking into account bi-stars, we get that the number of spanning trees of K_{m,n} having a saturated vertex is mn^{m-1}+nm^{m-1}-mn . Laplacian eigenvalues Let G be a graph on n vertices. Let A(G) and D(G) denote the adjacency and degree diagonal matrices of G , respectively. Then L(G)=D(G)-A(G) is the Laplacian matrix of G . Observe that if H is an edge-induced subgraph of G with V(G)=V(H) , then (3) L(G)=L(G-H)+L(H). Since L(G) is symmetric and positive semi-definite, it has n non-negative eigenvalues, say \lambda_{1}(G)\geq\lambda_{2}(G)\geq\ldots\lambda_{n-1}(G)\geq\lambda_{n}(G)\geq 0 . In fact, it is known that \lambda_{n}(G)=0 , and the multiplicity of the zero eigenvalue is the number of connected components of G . We write the spectrum of L(G) as [\lambda_{1}^{(m_{1})},\lambda_{2}^{(m_{2})},\ldots,\lambda_{k}^{(m_{k})}] where \lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\ldots>\lambda_{k} are all pairwise distinct Laplacian eigenvalues of G and m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{k} are their respective multiplicities. For example, [n^{(n-1)},0^{(1)}] is the Laplacian spectrum of the complete graph K_{n} . Recall Weyl’s inequalities, which bound the eigenvalues of the sum of two Hermitian matrices in terms of their individual eigenvalues. Lemma 2.5 (Weyl [ 18 ] ) . Let A,B be two n\times n Hermitian matrices with respective eigenvalues \lambda_{1}(A)\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{n}(A) and \lambda_{1}(B)\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{n}(B) . Then the eigenvalues \lambda_{1}(A+B)\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{n}(A+B) are related to the eigenvalues of A and B as follows: (4) \lambda_{i+j-1}(A+B)\leq\lambda_{i}(A)+\lambda_{j}(B) for all 1\leq i,j\leq n such that i+j-1\leq n , and (5) \lambda_{i}(A)+\lambda_{j}(B)\leq\lambda_{i+j-n}(A+B) for all 1\leq i,j\leq n such that 1\leq i+j-n . The eigenvalues of L(G) determine the number of spanning trees of a graph G as shown by the following classical theorem of Kirchhoff. Lemma 2.6 (Kirchhoff matrix tree theorem [ 7 ] ) . The number of spanning trees in a graph G on n vertices is |{\mathcal{T}}_{G}|=\frac{1}{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{i}(G). Below we collect some useful results on Laplacian eigenvalues of graphs. For more details, we refer the reader to a comprehensive survey by Zhang [ 19 ] . Lemma 2.7 (Merris [ 12 , Equation (5)] ) . Let G be a graph of order n and let G^{c} be its complement. Then \lambda_{1}(G)\leq n and \lambda_{n-i}(G^{c})=n-\lambda_{i}(G), for all i=1,\ldots,n-1 . The following is an unpublished result due to Brendan McKay, which was also proved by Mohar in [ 13 ] . Lemma 2.8 (Mohar [ 13 , Theorem 4.2] ) . Let G be a connected graph of order n . Then \lambda_{n-1}(G)\geq\frac{4}{n\cdot{\mathsf{diam}}(G)}. Lemma 2.9 (Guo [ 5 , Theorem 4] ) . Let T be a tree of order n . Then \lambda_{k}(T)\leq\left\lceil\frac{n}{k}\right\rceil\text{ for $1\leq k\leq n-1$} with equality if and only if k\mid n , and T is spanned by k vertex disjoint copies of the star graph K_{1,\frac{n}{k}-1} . Lemma 2.10 . Let F be a forest of order n . Then \lambda_{k}(F)\leq\left\lceil\frac{n}{k}\right\rceil\text{ for $1\leq k\leq n-1$}. Proof. If F is disconnected, add edges between its connected components to obtain a tree T . Let E:=L(T-F) be the Laplacian of the graph induced by the edges added to F to obtain T . Applying Equation ( 5 ) with A:=L(F) , B=E=L(T-F) , and j=n , we obtain that \lambda_{k}(F)+\lambda_{n}(E)=\lambda_{k}(F)\leq\lambda_{k}(T), for all 1\leq k\leq n . Lemma 2.9 then gives \lambda_{k}(F)\leq\lambda_{k}(T)\leq\left\lceil\frac{n}{k}\right\rceil\text{ for all $1\leq k\leq n-1$}, as desired. ∎ 3. Counting trees avoiding a tree Recall that {\mathcal{S}}_{m,n} is the set of all spanning trees of K:=K_{m,n} that intersect every spanning tree of K . Let T_{0} be a spanning tree of K such that T_{0}\not\in{\mathcal{S}}_{m,n} , and F be a forest on t edges of K . By {\mathcal{T}}_{K}[T_{0};F] † † margin: {\mathcal{T}}_{K}[T_{0};F] , we denote the set of spanning trees of K that contain all edges of F but no edges of T_{0}-F ; we say that such a spanning tree from {\mathcal{T}}_{K}[T_{0};F] contains F and avoids T_{0} outside F , formally: {\mathcal{T}}_{K}[T_{0};F]=\{T\in\mathcal{T}_{K}:E(F)\subseteq E(T)\text{ and }E(T)\cap E(T_{0}-F)=\varnothing\}. We consider the following minimization problem over all spanning trees T_{0} and forests F on t edges: (6) D_{t}:=D_{t}(K)=\min_{F\colon|E(F)|=t}\min_{~~T_{0}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{K}\setminus{\mathcal{S}}_{m,n}}|\mathcal{T}_{K}[T_{0};F]|. In this section, we will give a lower bound on D_{t}(K_{n,n}) as follows. First, given a spanning tree T_{0} of K , we estimate how many spanning trees of K avoid T_{0} (regardless of F ). Suppose now we are given a forest F in K . By removing the edges and vertices of F from K and from T_{0} , we obtain a smaller complete bipartite graph K^{\prime}:=K_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}} (for some m^{\prime}\leq m=n , n^{\prime}\leq n ) and its spanning forest T_{0}^{\prime} . This reduces our minimization problem to the first step, i.e., counting the number of spanning trees of K^{\prime} avoiding the spanning tree (forest) T_{0}^{\prime} of K^{\prime} . While the strategy outlined above is indeed crude, the resulting lower bound on D_{t} will suffice for our purposes in Section 5 . We note that similar results on D_{t}(K_{m,n}) hold provided that the partition sizes are not “too unbalanced”, but we do not pursue this here. 3.1. Conclusion A natural next step is to improve the dependency of t with respect to n in our main results to be linear in n . During the preparation of this paper, Saengrungkongka [ 16 ] derived such a result for K_{n} using spread approximations, in particular, via the so-called “density boost lemma” [ 16 , Lemma 3.1] . Such results are likely applicable to our setting, but this would at least require a sharper estimate on D_{t} than the one given here, which we leave for future work. Given the complexity of characterizing the extremal t -intersecting families for t\ll n , a full characterization for all t,n along the lines of [ 6 ] currently seems out of reach. One of the notable distinctions between the non-bipartite [ 3 ] case and the bipartite case is the difference in the structure of the extremal t -intersecting families. Here, the size of |\mathcal{S}_{n,n}\setminus\mathcal{T}_{n,n}[e]| versus \mathcal{T}_{n,n}[e] played a significant role, so it is natural to wonder how large the gap between these two quantities is for other graph families. This question degenerates when the graphs have bridges, so consider an infinite family of 2-edge-connected 3-regular graphs \{G_{n}\} . Let \mathcal{T}_{G_{n}} be the set of spanning trees of G_{n} . By the hand-shaking lemma, we have |E(G_{n})|=3n/2 , which shows for n\geq 6 that G_{n} cannot have a pair of disjoint spanning trees, i.e., every spanning tree of G_{n} intersects every other spanning tree. If we further impose that each G_{n} is edge-transitive (such families exist, see [ 11 ] , for example), then we have |\mathcal{T}_{G_{n}}[e]|=|\mathcal{T}_{G_{n}}[e^{\prime}]| for any two edges e,e^{\prime}\in E(G_{n}) . In particular, by a standard double counting argument, we have |\mathcal{T}_{G_{n}}[e]|\approx 2|\mathcal{T}_{G_{n}}|/3 for all e\in E(G_{n}) , hence the number of spanning trees of G_{n} that avoid any edge e\in E(G_{n}) is \approx|\mathcal{T}_{G_{n}}|/3 . While the intersection problem becomes trivial for this class of graphs, the foregoing shows that the number of spanning trees that avoid some edge and intersect every other spanning tree can be asymptotically proportional to \max_{e\in E(G_{n})}|\mathcal{T}_{G_{n}}[e]| . In general, it would be interesting to establish under what conditions one can determine the higher-order term of the size of a largest intersecting family of spanning trees of graph families. Finally, it is likely that the techniques presented here in conjunction with recent enumerative results on spanning trees [ 17 ] can be used to prove similar results in the multipartite setting. Our techniques can also be extended to include slightly unbalanced bipartite graphs, but we have omitted these results in favor of a cleaner exposition.
