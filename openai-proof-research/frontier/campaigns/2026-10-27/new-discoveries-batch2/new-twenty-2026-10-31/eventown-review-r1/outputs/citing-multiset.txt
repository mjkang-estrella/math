A multiset is called m -bounded if each element has multiplicity at most m . The intersection of two multisets is defined as the multiset in which each element has multiplicity equal to the minimum of its multiplicities in the two original multisets. A family of m -bounded multisets is called an Eventown family if every member and the intersection of every pair of distinct members has even cardinality, with cardinalities counted with multiplicities. We determine the maximum size E_{m}(n) of such a family for every m and n . We show that E_{2q}(n)=(q+1)^{n}, and E_{2q+1}(n)=(q+1)^{n-2k}\bigl((q+1)^{2}+1\bigr)^{k}, where k=\lfloor n/2\rfloor . In particular, the case m=1 recovers the classical Eventown theorem for sets. We also characterize all extremal families for m\geq 2 . For even m , the unique extremal family consists of all multisets in which every element has even multiplicity. For odd m\geq 3 , every extremal family has a product structure arising from two explicit families on pairs of coordinates, together with an additional coordinate restricted to even multiplicities when n is odd. Consequently, there are exactly k+1 extremal types up to permutations of the ground set. Key words: Intersection Theorem; Eventown Family; Multisets. AMS Subject Classification: 05D05. 1 Introduction A family \mathcal{A}\subseteq 2^{[n]} is called an Eventown family if every member has even cardinality and the number of intersections of every two distinct members is even, where [n]=\{1,\ldots,n\} . Equivalently, |A\cap B| is even for all A,B\in\mathcal{A} . To answer the question of what the maximum possible size of such a family can be, posed by Erdős, Berlekamp [ 3 ] and Graver [ 6 ] independently showed that |\mathcal{A}|\leq 2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}. Equality is attained by partitioning 2\lfloor n/2\rfloor elements into pairs and taking all unions of these pairs. This is also known as classical Eventown problem. On the other hand, the maximum size of an Oddtown family, defined as a collection of odd-sized subsets of [n] such that every pair of distinct sets has an even-sized intersection, is n . Thus, changing the parity of the set sizes changes the maximum family size from exponential to linear. The proofs of these results demonstrated the effectiveness of linear algebraic methods in addressing problems in extremal set theory. For an account of these methods and their applications to families of sets with restricted intersection sizes, including modular conditions, see Babai and Frankl [ 1 ] . The study of parity-restricted intersections extends beyond ordinary set systems, with analogues of the Oddtown and Eventown problems arising in more general settings, such as, vector subspaces over finite field [ 4 ] , permutations [ 2 , 8 ] , and lattice paths [ 10 ] . This naturally leads to the investigation of parity-restricted intersection problems in other combinatorial frameworks. Here, we study an Eventown problem for multisets, that is, collections in which elements may occur with higher multiplicity. Multiset analogues of intersection theorems have been studied in several settings. Füredi, Gerbner, and Vizer [ 5 ] and Meagher and Purdy [ 9 ] investigated intersection restrictions for families of fixed-cardinality multisets. More recently, Liao, Lv, Cao, and Lu [ 7 ] established an Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem for multisets with bounded multiplicities. These results concern nonempty intersections or intersections of a prescribed minimum size. In this paper, we consider a parity restriction instead: intersections must have even cardinality, while the total cardinalities of the members are allowed to vary. Once the maximum size of families of a given type has been determined, a natural structural question is to characterize those families that attain the corresponding extremal bound. In the classical Eventown problem, the standard construction using disjoint pairs is not unique, and there exist many non-isomorphic extremal families; see Section 1.1 of [ 11 ] . Sudakov and Vieira [ 11 ] investigated this structural question for r -wise Eventown families of subsets of [n] , in which the intersection of every r distinct members has even cardinality. For r\geq 3 and n sufficiently large depending on r , they characterized the extremal families and established a corresponding stability result. Now, in this paper, we have characterized the extremal Eventown families of multisets in which at least one element occurs with multiplicity greater than one. The Eventown family of multisets in which each element occurs with multiplicity at most one is just a classical Eventown family. 1.1 The Multiset Eventown Problem Fix positive integers m and n . A multiset on [n] is a collection of elements of [n] in which repetitions are allowed. The number of occurrences of an element is called its multiplicity . A multiset is called m -bounded if every element has multiplicity at most m . Every m -bounded multiset x on [n] corresponds uniquely to a multiplicity vector x\equiv(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\{0,1,\ldots,m\}^{n}, where x_{i} is the multiplicity of the element i in x . Throughout this paper, we identify m -bounded multisets with their multiplicity vectors. For two multisets x and y , their intersection x\cap y is the multiset in which each element i has multiplicity \min\{x_{i},y_{i}\} . The cardinality of their intersection, counting multiplicities, is therefore I(x,y)=|x\cap y|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min\{x_{i},y_{i}\}. Thus, the intersection counts the copies shared by the two multisets, rather than merely the elements appearing in both supports. We call a family of multisets \mathcal{F}\subseteq\{0,1,\ldots,m\}^{n} an Eventown family if I(x,y) is even for every x,y\in\mathcal{F} . In particular, each member has an even total multiplicity. Let E_{m}(n) denote the maximum possible cardinality of such a family. This extends the classical Eventown problem by taking m=1 . However, allowing multiplicities introduces an additional difficulty. For ordinary sets, the parity of the intersection cardinality is determined by the scalar product of their characteristic vectors over \mathbb{F}_{2} . For multisets, in contrast, the parities of the individual multiplicities do not, in general, determine the parity of the intersection cardinality, since the latter depends on the minimum of the corresponding multiplicities. Consequently, one cannot simply reduce the multiplicity vectors modulo 2 and apply the orthogonality arguments used in the proof of the classical Eventown theorem. A trivial upper bound for E_{m}(n) is 2^{\lfloor mn/2\rfloor} . To obtain this bound, represent each m -bounded multiset as a subset of [n]\times[m] , where a multiplicity x_{i} is represented by selecting the first x_{i} elements of the block \{i\}\times[m] . Under this representation, the cardinality of the intersection of two multisets equals the cardinality of the intersection of their corresponding subsets. The classical Eventown theorem therefore yields the stated upper bound. However, this bound is not sharp in general. In this paper, we determine E_{m}(n) for every m and n , and characterize the extremal families for m\geq 2 . We prove that the family consisting of all multiplicity vectors with even coordinates is the unique extremal family for every even m . Whereas for odd m\geq 3 and n\geq 2 , there are more than one extremal family. We characterize all the extremal families for every odd m\geq 3 . 1.2 Main Results Our main result determines the exact value of E_{m}(n) for all positive integers m and n . Theorem 1 . Let E_{m}(n) be the maximum size of an Eventown family of m -bounded multisets on [n] . Then E_{m}(n)=\begin{cases}(q+1)^{n},&m=2q,\\[5.69054pt] \bigl((q+1)^{2}+1\bigr)^{n/2},&m=2q+1,\quad n\text{ even},\\[5.69054pt] (q+1)\bigl((q+1)^{2}+1\bigr)^{(n-1)/2},&m=2q+1,\quad n\text{ odd}.\end{cases} In particular, E_{1}(n)=2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor} , recovering the classical Eventown bound. We also characterize all families attaining the above bound. For even m=2q , we prove that the family \{0,2,\ldots,2q\}^{n} is the unique extremal family. For odd m=2q+1\geq 3 , we define \mathcal{A}=\{(0,0)\}\cup\{1,3,\ldots,m\}^{2},\qquad\mathcal{B}=\{(m,m)\}\cup\{0,2,\ldots,2q\}^{2}, and show that up to a permutation of coordinates the extremal families are precisely the families, \mathcal{A}^{t}\times\mathcal{B}^{k-t}\times\{0,2,\ldots,2q\}^{\,n-2k}, for some integer 0\leq t\leq k , where k=\lfloor n/2\rfloor . Here, the final factor is omitted when n is even. The case, m=1 is exceptional. Identifying sets with their characteristic vectors, the extremal families are precisely the self-orthogonal subspaces of \mathbb{F}_{2}^{n} of dimension \lfloor n/2\rfloor . Therefore, such extremal families need not admit the paired form described above. 1.3 Outline of the Proof Given an Eventown family \mathcal{F} of m -bounded multisets, we successively transform the multiplicities of one coordinate at a time, preserving the Eventown property at each step. For even m , the transformation decreases the size of the family by at most a factor of r=q+1 at each step. This gives us the desired upper bound. When m is odd, we transform the family \mathcal{F} into an Eventown family \mathcal{G} without decreasing its size, such that each coordinate takes values from one of two prescribed sets. We then establish the upper bound using parity vectors, linear algebra, and counting arguments. We next characterize the cases of equality. For even m , we prove that every extremal family consists precisely of all m -bounded multisets in which every element has even multiplicity. For odd m\geq 3 , we show that \mathcal{G} has a product structure determined by \mathcal{A} - and \mathcal{B} -pairs, with an additional coordinate restricted to even multiplicities when n is odd. Finally, equality forces all intermediate families to have maximum size and a backward induction then shows that each transformation leaves the family unchanged, and hence \mathcal{F}=\mathcal{G} . 2 Proof of Theorem 1 We begin with an elementary linear-algebraic observation that will be used in the subsequent counting argument. Lemma 2.1 . Let C\leq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n} be a d -dimensional self-orthogonal subspace, so that, c\cdot c^{\prime}=0\ \ \ \ \ \ \ (c,c^{\prime}\in C). Then there exist disjoint coordinate sets I,K\subseteq[n] , each of cardinality d , such that the coordinate restriction maps to either I or K are injective on C . In particular, the coordinates indexed by either I or K uniquely determine every vector in C . Proof. If d=0 , take I=K=\varnothing . Assume d>0 , and let G be a d\times n matrix whose rows form a basis of C . For J\subseteq[n] , let G_{J} denote the submatrix of G consisting of the columns indexed by J . Choose d independent columns, indexed by I . Since G_{I} is invertible, the restriction or projection map \pi_{I}:C\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2}^{I} is a bijection. Here, \mathbb{F}_{2}^{I} denotes the space of binary vectors indexed by I . We claim that restriction to the complementary coordinates is also injective. Indeed, suppose z\in C is supported on I , that is, z_{i}=0 for all i\notin I . By the self-orthogonality of C , we have 0=z\cdot c=\sum_{i\in I}z_{i}c_{i},\qquad\text{for every }c\in C. Since c|_{I} ranges over all of \mathbb{F}_{2}^{I} , this forces z|_{I}=0 , and hence z=0 . Therefore restriction to I^{c}=[n]\setminus I is injective, and thus G_{I^{c}} has rank d . Choosing d independent columns of this matrix gives a set K\subseteq I^{c} , of size d , such that restriction to K is also injective. ∎ We next examine the multiplicities that can occur in a fixed coordinate of an Eventown family. At the i -th iteration, define \displaystyle T_{e}^{i} \displaystyle=\{u:\iota_{i}(s,u)\in\mathcal{F}^{i-1}\text{ for some }s\in V_{e}\}, \displaystyle T_{o}^{i} \displaystyle=\{u:\iota_{i}(s,u)\in\mathcal{F}^{i-1}\text{ for some }s\in V_{o}\}, \displaystyle a_{i} \displaystyle=\max_{u}\left|\{s\in V_{0}:\iota_{i}(s,u)\in\mathcal{F}^{i-1}\}\right|, \displaystyle b_{i} \displaystyle=\max_{u}\left|\{s\in V_{1}:\iota_{i}(s,u)\in\mathcal{F}^{i-1}\}\right|, where u ranges over \{0,1,\ldots,m\}^{n-1} . Thus, a_{i} and b_{i} are the maximum numbers of even and odd extensions, respectively, in coordinate i . 3.1 Case of Even m Let m=2q . The case q=0 is immediate. Suppose |\mathcal{F}|=r^{n} . Now we recall Case I in the proof of Theorem 1 . Writing \mathcal{F}^{i} for the family after i iterations, equality forces |\mathcal{F}^{i}|=r^{n-i} . Thus, at each iteration the size of the family decreases by a factor of r . Since a_{i}+b_{i}\leq r and b_{i}\leq r-1 , the condition |T_{o}^{i}|>|T_{e}^{i}| gives us |\mathcal{F}^{i-1}|\leq a_{i}|T_{e}^{i}|+b_{i}|T_{o}^{i}|<r|T_{o}^{i}| which contradicts the equality, |\mathcal{F}^{i-1}|=r|T_{o}^{i}| . Thus, |T_{o}^{i}| can not be greater than |T_{e}^{i}| . So, every chosen constant is 0 and therefore \mathcal{F}^{n}=\{(0,0,...,0)\} . Now, we recover the original family \mathcal{F} . Suppose |T_{e}^{i}|=|T_{o}^{i}| at the step i . Consider an Eventown family \mathcal{K}=\{\iota_{i}(1,u):u\in T_{o}^{i}\}. Clearly |\mathcal{K}|=r^{n-i}. We keep its first i coordinates fixed and apply the same procedure to the remaining coordinates. At each remaining iteration, the size of the family decreases exactly by a factor r , and therefore chooses 0 . The member of the final singleton family consequently has exactly one coordinate equal to 1 , contradicting the Eventown condition and thus |T_{e}^{i}|\neq|T_{o}^{i}| . Hence |T_{e}^{i}|>|T_{o}^{i}| at every step i . So we have r|T_{e}^{i}|=|\mathcal{F}^{i-1}|\leq a_{i}|T_{e}^{i}|+b_{i}|T_{o}^{i}|\leq r|T_{e}^{i}|-b_{i}(|T_{e}^{i}|-|T_{o}^{i}|), which implies b_{i}=0 . Since there are |T_{e}^{i}| tuples and |\mathcal{F}^{i-1}|=r|T_{e}^{i}| member in, each tuple must have all r even extension. Thus each iteration fixes a coordinate that could take any value in V_{e} . Reversing the iteration process, from \mathcal{F}^{n}=\{0\}^{n} we obtain \mathcal{F}=V_{e}^{n}. Therefore V_{e}^{n} is the unique extremal Eventown family when m is even. 3.2 Case of Odd m Let M=r^{n-2k}(r^{2}+1)^{k}. Let us consider the following sequence of families obtained from the iteration process described in Case II in the proof of the Theorem 1 \mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}^{0},\mathcal{F}^{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}^{n}=\mathcal{G}. Since |\mathcal{F}|=M , we have |\mathcal{F}^{i}|=M\qquad(0\leq i\leq n). Now, we proceed by backward induction to show that every intermediate family coincides with \mathcal{G} . Let us fix i and consider \mathcal{F}^{i}=\mathcal{G} . Recalling the fact established in Case II in the proof of the Theorem 1 and Lemma 2.2 we have M\leq a_{i}|T_{e}^{i}|+b_{i}|T_{o}^{i}|,\qquad a_{i}+b_{i}\leq r+1. Moreover, if a_{i}+b_{i}=r+1 , then every even extension precedes every odd extension in coordinate i . Also recall that, by the characterization of \mathcal{G} , its coordinates can be grouped into \lfloor n/2\rfloor pairs, with both coordinates in each pair using the same allowed set, either Q_{e} or Q_{o} . When n is odd, the remaining unpaired coordinate takes every value in V_{0} , independently of the other coordinates. Subcase I. Coordinate i belongs to a pair in \mathcal{G} . Both even and odd multiplicities occur in coordinate i , and the iteration producing \mathcal{F}^{i} retains both corresponding groups, T_{e}^{i} and T_{o}^{i} . Now, if the coordinate i belongs to a \mathcal{B} -pair, then \mathcal{F}^{i}=\{\iota_{i}(s,u):s\in V_{0},\ u\in T_{e}^{i}\}\cup\{\iota_{i}(m,v):v\in T_{o}^{i}\}. Let L denote the number of multiplicity assignments on the other n-2 coordinates. Since \mathcal{B}=V_{0}^{2}\cup\{(m,m)\}, we have |T_{e}^{i}|=rL,\qquad|T_{o}^{i}|=L. Consequently, M\leq a_{i}|T_{e}^{i}|+b_{i}|T_{o}^{i}|\leq r|T_{e}^{i}|+|T_{o}^{i}|=M. Since a_{i}+b_{i}=r+1 , the equality gives us (b_{i}-1)\bigl(|T_{e}^{i}|-|T_{o}^{i}|\bigr)=0. Again, since r>1 and |T_{e}^{i}|=r|T_{o}^{i}| , we have b_{i}=1 and a_{i}=r . Thus some tuple in T_{e}^{i} has all even extensions, including 2q . The ordering property forces every odd extension in coordinate i to be m . Hence \mathcal{F}^{i-1}\subseteq\{\iota_{i}(s,u):s\in V_{0},\ u\in T_{e}^{i}\}\cup\{\iota_{i}(m,v):v\in T_{o}^{i}\}=\mathcal{F}^{i}. Since both families have size M , they are equal. So F^{i-1}=\mathcal{G} . Now we consider when the coordinate i belongs to an \mathcal{A} -pair. Here, \mathcal{F}^{i}=\{\iota_{i}(0,u):u\in T_{e}^{i}\}\cup\{\iota_{i}(t,v):t\in V_{1},\ v\in T_{o}^{i}\}. and |T_{e}^{i}|=L,\qquad|T_{o}^{i}|=rL. Now, similarly, we have a_{i}=1 and b_{i}=r . Some tuples in T_{o}^{i} have all odd extensions, including 1 . This forces every even extension in coordinate i to be 0 . Consequently, \mathcal{F}^{i-1}\subseteq\{\iota_{i}(0,u):u\in T_{e}^{i}\}\cup\{\iota_{i}(t,v):t\in V_{1},\ v\in T_{o}^{i}\}=\mathcal{F}^{i}. Equal cardinalities again imply equality and hence \mathcal{F}^{i-1}=\mathcal{G} . Subcase II. Coordinate i is unpaired in \mathcal{G} . This possibility occurs only when n is odd. In this case, the i -th coordinate of the members of \mathcal{F}^{i} ranges over all possible values in V_{0} . So the replacement family \mathcal{F}^{i}=\{\iota_{i}(s,u):s\in V_{0},\ u\in T_{e}^{i}\},\qquad\text{ and }M=r|T_{e}^{i}|. The family \{\iota_{i}(t,v):t\in V_{1},\ v\in T_{o}^{i}\}\cup\{\mathbf{0}\} is Eventown. This implies r|T_{o}^{i}|+1\leq M=r|T_{e}^{i}|,\text{ in particular, }|T_{o}^{i}|<|T_{e}^{i}|. Suppose that T_{o}^{i}\neq\varnothing , then b_{i}>0 . Now r|T_{e}^{i}|=M\leq a_{i}|T_{e}^{i}|+b_{i}|T_{o}^{i}|<(a_{i}+b_{i})|T_{e}^{i}|. Since a_{i}+b_{i}\leq r+1 , we have a_{i}+b_{i}=r+1 . The family \{\iota_{i}(s,u):s\in V_{0},\ u\in T_{e}^{i}\}\cup\{\iota_{i}(m,v):v\in T_{o}^{i}\} is also an Eventown family of size r|T_{e}^{i}|+|T_{o}^{i}|=M+|T_{o}^{i}|>M, which contradicts the upper bound. Therefore T_{o}^{i}=\varnothing , and hence \mathcal{F}^{i-1}\subseteq\{\iota_{i}(s,u):s\in V_{0},\ u\in T_{e}^{i}\}=\mathcal{F}^{i}, which implies \mathcal{F}^{i-1}=\mathcal{F}^{i}=\mathcal{G} . So starting with \mathcal{F}^{n}=\mathcal{G} we have \mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}^{0}=\mathcal{F}^{1}=\cdots=\mathcal{F}^{n}=\mathcal{G}. Since \mathcal{G} has already been characterized in Lemma 2.3 and \mathcal{F}=\mathcal{G} , the desired characterization follows. This characterizes the maximum Eventown family of multisets.
