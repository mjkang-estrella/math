2.1. Acyclic matchings. We begin with two examples of matchings and a re-
lated definition.
Definition 2.1. The support of a matching f : A → B, denoted by supp(f ), is
the subset of elements x ∈ A at which the multiplicity function mf : G → Z≥0 is
positive; that is, the subset of elements x ∈ G that may be realized as a + f (a)
for an element a of A.
Example 2.2. Let us examine matchings f : A → B in which the subsets A and
B of the abelian group G are as large as possible. If #A = #B = #G − 1, then
B = G \ {0}, and f should be in the form of
                   f : A = G \ {g1 } → B = G \ {0} : a 7→ g1 − a
for an appropriate g1 ∈ G. The support of f only contains g1 at which mf takes
the value #G − 1. This clearly shows that the matching above is acyclic.
   Next, suppose A and B are of cardinality #G − 2. Write A as G \ {g1 , g2 }
and B as G \ {0, g3 } where g1 6= g2 and g3 6= 0. We claim that any matching
f : A → B is acyclic. For any a ∈ A, f (a) should be either g1 − a or g2 − a
because otherwise a + f (a) ∈ A. Suppose the former occurs l times and the latter
#G − 2 − l times. Therefore, the multiplicity function mf : G → Z≥0 attains the
value l at g1 , the value #G − l − 2 at g2 , and is zero elsewhere. The knowledge
of the subset A′ := {a ∈ A : f (a) = g1 − a} determines the matching. Notice
that (g2 − g1 ) + A′ ⊂ A′ ∪ {g1 , g2 } because otherwise there exists a′ ∈ A′ with
a′′ := (g2 − g1 ) + a′ ∈ A \ A′ which is impossible since then f (a′ ) = g1 − a′
coincides with f (a′′ ) = g2 − a′′ . One can easily check that due to the inclusion

of {g1 + (g1 − g2 ), g1 + 2(g1 − g2 ), . . . , g1 + l(g1 − g2 )}.
Example 2.3. Let us consider a family of matchings f : A → B for which
A ∩ (A + B) = ∅ as in Question 1.4. Imposing an extra condition that B ∪ {0}

Definition 3.4. Let L/F be a field extension and A and B two F -subspaces of

   THE WEAK ACYCLIC MATCHING PROPERTY IN ABELIAN
                      GROUPS

                          MOHSEN ALIABADI1 AND PETER TAYLOR2

         Abstract. A matching from a finite subset A ⊂ Zn to another subset B ⊂ Zn is
         a bijection f : A → B with the property that a + f (a) never lies in A. A matching
         is called acyclic if it is uniquely determined by its multiplicity function. Alon et
         al. established the acyclic matching property for Zn , which was later extended to
         all abelian torsion-free groups. In a prior work, the authors of this paper settled
         the acyclic matching property for all abelian groups. The objective of this note is
         to explore a related concept, known as the weak acyclic matching property, within
         the context of abelian groups.



                                          1. Introduction
  Let (G, +) denote an abelian group. Consider nonempty finite subsets A, B ⊆ G.
A matching from A to B is defined as a bijection f : A → B satisfying the condition
a + f (a) ∈
          / A for every a ∈ A. When such a matching exists from A to B, we say
that A is matched to B. This concept, introduced by Fan and Losonczy in [8], serves
as a tool in exploring Wakeford’s classical problem concerning canonical forms for
symmetric tensors [14]. It is evident that the existence of a matching from A to B
requires |A| = |B| and 0 ∈/ B.
  An abelian group G possesses the matching property if the aforementioned con-
ditions on A and B suffice to ensure the existence of a matching from A to B.
Losonczy addressed the question of which groups possess this property in [13], giv-
ing the following criterion.
Theorem 1.1. An abelian group G possesses the matching property if and only if
G is torsion-free or cyclic of prime order.
  For a matching f : A → B, the associated multiplicity function mf : G → Z≥0 is
defined as follows:
                      for all x ∈ G, mf (x) = |{a ∈ A : a + f (a) = x}|.
  A matching f : A → B is called acyclic if for any matching g : A → B, mf = mg
implies f = g. If an acyclic matching from A to B exists, we say that A is acyclically
matched to B. A group G exhibits the acyclic matching property if for any pair of
subsets A and B in G with |A| = |B| and 0 ∈/ B, there is at least one acyclic matching

A and B where A ∩ (A + B) = ∅. Intuitively, the situation might be complex when
A ∩ (A + B) = ∅ because there could be as many as |A|! matchings, increasing
the probability of at least one acyclic matching existing. On the other hand, the
number of matchings may grow faster than the number of multiplicity functions, so
the average number of matchings per multiplicity function may be large reducing
the probability of at least one acyclic matching existing. The following special cases
of acyclic matching were proved in [1]:
Theorem 1.3. Let A be a subset of the cyclic group Z/pZ where p is a prime
number. Suppose A satisfies A ∩ 2A = ∅ where 2A = {2a | a ∈ A}and A is of size

Theorem 1.4. Let A and B be subsets of an abelian group G. Suppose A and B are
of the same size satisfying A ∩ (A + B) = ∅. Then there exists an acyclic matching
f : A → B if B is a Sidon set. (That is, the equation x + y = z + w has no solution
in B with {x, y} ∩ {z, w} = ∅.)

  Following [2], we define an abelian group G to possess the weak acyclic matching
property if, for every finite nonempty subsets A and B of G with |A| = |B| and
A ∩ (A + B) = ∅, A is acyclically matched to B. In [2], the following conjecture
concerning the weak acyclic matching property was proposed.
Conjecture 1.5. There are infinitely many n for which Z/nZ has the weak acyclic
matching property.
  The following question was also raised in [1].
Question 1.6. Does Z/pZ possess the weak acyclic matching property for every
prime p?
  In this note, our main result, stated as Theorem 2.3, provides an affirmative
answer to Conjecture 1.5 and thoroughly addresses Question 1.6. Furthermore,
Theorem 2.3 classifies all abelian groups with respect to their adherence to the weak
acyclic matching property.
Remark 1.7. It is worth noting that while the matching theory developed in this
paper is related to the classical theory introduced by Philip Hall in 1935, the two
are conceptually distinct. In particular, to address a linear algebra problem posed
by Wakeford [14], Fan [8] constructed a bipartite graph G = (V (G), E(G)) based on
two sets A, B ⊂ Zn , which may overlap. Each element a ∈ A is assigned a symbol
xa , and each b ∈ B a symbol yb , forming vertex sets
                    X = {xa : a ∈ A} and Y = {yb : b ∈ B}.
The graph has vertex set V (G) = X ∪ Y , with an edge between xa ∈ X and yb ∈ Y
if and only if a + b ∈
                     / A.
   In graph-theoretic terms, a matching is a set of edges with no shared endpoints,
and a perfect matching is one that covers all vertices. In the bipartite graph asso-
ciated with A and B, perfect matchings correspond to bijections f : A → B such
that a + f (a) ∈/ A for all a ∈ A. Wakeford’s question thus reduces to determining
the existence of such perfect matchings, known as acyclic matchings from A to B,
discussed earlier.
