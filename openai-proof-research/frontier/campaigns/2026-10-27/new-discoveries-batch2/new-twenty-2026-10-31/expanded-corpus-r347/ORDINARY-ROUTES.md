## 366628: exact lattice specialization of the source six-point argument

Endpoint: for every finite simple connected graph G with a K(2,3) minor, replace each edge by a path of FOUR unit edges (three inserted vertices). Its vertex shortest-path metric fails negative type: there is a finitely supported real weight w with sum zero and sum_{p,q} w(p)w(q)d(p,q)>0. A disconnected graph requires restricting to the relevant connected component; an infinite-distance extended metric is not silently a metric. The literal record does not address that convention.

The initial shortcut fails: the continuous metric-graph theorem is not a vertex-metric theorem. Scaling its displayed bound gives 4/12 while a six-point rounding can lose 15; 4/12-15 is negative. Rounding cannot certify the desired sign. No solver, matrix enumeration or numerical search was used.

The stronger route keeps the source's exact six-point witness and removes rounding. Work in the metric realization of the subdivided graph H, suppressing inserted vertices only for discussing branch locations. All original edge segments have length 4. A K(2,3) minor supplies a theta: equivalently a subdivision of K(2,3), since its maximum degree is three. To see this bridge, choose disjoint connected branch sets for the minor, take within each branch set a minimal tree joining its at most three incident attachment vertices, and suppress degree-two vertices. A three-attachment tree has one possible degree-three branch, so the resulting paths form the required topological subdivision. Thus some theta exists. Choose a topological theta T of minimum total edge length, with branch vertices u,v and internally disjoint paths P1,P2,P3 of lengths a<=b<=c. Branch vertices and every possible exit from T are original vertices; lengths a,b,c and coordinates of original vertices along each Pi are multiples of four.

Here are the two elementary minimal-theta lemmas used by the source, with their justifications. If an external path S connects z on Pi to y on Pj, i!=j, and is internally disjoint from T, replacing a suitable one of the four branch-to-endpoint arms by S makes another theta. Minimal total length gives |S| at least each of those four arm lengths, hence at least each of d_T(u,z), d_T(v,z), d_T(u,y), d_T(v,y). If both endpoints are on the same Pi, replacing the subpath between them instead gives |S| at least that subpath's length.

For a point x on the first original edge of P1 at distance t<=2 from u, its distances to all points of T equal their intrinsic distances in T. Suppose a shorter path first creates an improvement by an excursion S from z to y. The prefix has length d_T(x,z). If z,y are on the same Pi the preceding replacement argument rules out an improvement. Otherwise its length is at least d_T(u,y). Exits occur at original vertices; reaching any exit z from x along T costs at least t, since x lies in a length-four edge and min(t,4-t)=t. Therefore the proposed path has length at least t+d_T(u,y)>=d_T(x,y), a contradiction. This is the source's local-distance lemma rescaled, without auditing its upstream theorem.

Put x_j on P1 at coordinate j from u for j=0,1,2. On P2 put y_j at coordinate (a+b)/2-j from u; on P3 put z_j at coordinate (a+c)/2-j. These are the respective cycle antipodes of x_j. Their coordinates are integers, so they are actual vertices of H. The intervals [y_2,y_0] and [z_2,z_0] have length two with EVEN endpoints; every original branch or attachment has coordinate divisible by four. Thus neither interval contains an attachment in its interior. Their interiors are disjoint because P2 and P3 are internally disjoint. They may share the endpoint v, which will be handled by multiplicities. All consecutive distances inside each triple x,y,z equal one. For y,z this follows also from distinct vertices joined by one unit edge; for x it is immediate.

For i=0 or 1, antipodality and the local-distance lemma give
 d(x_i,y_i)+d(x_{i+1},y_{i+1})
 -d(x_i,y_{i+1})-d(x_{i+1},y_i)=2,
and the same equation with z in place of y. For example the first two distances are (a+b)/2 each, and the two crossed distances are (a+b)/2-1 each. This remains valid when an interval endpoint is v.

Because the y,z intervals have no interior attachments, every path between their middle vertices exits through an endpoint of each. Consequently
 d(y_1,z_1)=2+min_{r,s in {0,2}} d(y_r,z_s).
Choose a minimizing corner. If it is (0,0), the triangle inequalities d(y_0,z_1)<=d(y_0,z_0)+1 and d(y_1,z_0)<=1+d(y_0,z_0) give
 d(y_0,z_0)+d(y_1,z_1)>=d(y_0,z_1)+d(y_1,z_0).
If it is (2,2), use i=1 instead. If it is (0,2), use i=0 and the bounds d(y_0,z_1)<=d(y_0,z_2)+1 and d(y_1,z_0)<=1+d(y_0,z_0). If it is (2,0), interchange y and z to obtain the same conclusion for i=0. In all cases there is i in {0,1} for which the aligned y,z sum dominates the crossed sum.

Let R be the three-entry list (x_i,y_i,z_i) and B the list (x_{i+1},y_{i+1},z_{i+1}). Write S_R,S_B for unordered within-list distance sums, and S_cross for all nine cross-list distances. The preceding equations give a surplus of at least four over the SIX nonaligned cross terms. The other three cross terms are the consecutive x,y,z distances, each one. Hence S_R+S_B-S_cross>=1. Assign weight +1 to each occurrence in R and -1 to each occurrence in B, merging repeated occurrences by addition. The total weight is zero and its ordered negative-type quadratic form is 2(S_R+S_B-S_cross)>=2>0. Shared endpoints cause no difficulty: a distance of an occurrence to itself is zero, and merging weights preserves the bilinear sum. This proves the vertex-metric endpoint on the stated standard finite connected domain.

## 374217: complete geometric obstruction, domain correction only

The source defines prototiles up to congruence, allows non-edge-to-edge tilings, and inserts all polygon vertices into the boundary graph (Definitions 2.1, 2.7). Its introduction describes an FLC goal; literal Conjecture 2.11 omits FLC. Even restricting to translated unit-square prototiles, local finiteness does not imply finitely many edge shapes.

Construct for each integer j the horizontal row of closed unit squares
 Q_{j,m}=[m+a_j,m+1+a_j] x [j,j+1], m in Z,
where a_j=1/(j+2) for j>=0 and a_j=0 for j<0. Interiors of distinct squares are disjoint, and rows cover the plane. All tiles are translates of one square. Every compact set meets finitely many rows and finitely many squares in each row: the tiling is locally finite. At the common boundary y=j+1 for j>=0, the two rows' vertices occur at horizontal coordinates Z+a_j and Z+a_{j+1}. Their offset is delta_j=a_j-a_{j+1}=1/((j+2)(j+3)), which is in (0,1) and strictly decreases with j. Consecutive vertices from the two grids delimit edges of length delta_j (and 1-delta_j). Hence the boundary graph has infinitely many distinct edge lengths.

If it were a union of translates of finitely many finite geometric subgraphs P_1,...,P_N, every graph edge would be the translate of an edge in one of the P_k. A finite graph has finitely many edges and translations preserve length. The union would therefore have finitely many edge lengths, contradiction. This rules out the required partition before any weights are chosen. It is independent of whether partition means edge-disjoint (the natural compatible-boundary convention); a vertex-disjoint interpretation cannot rescue it. If the subgraphs are treated as abstract unembedded graphs, the word 'translations' loses the geometric definition on which this argument relies; that alternative semantic endpoint has not been silently adopted.

First shortcut and repair: Keusch plus compactness gives a global weighting, not a finite geometric patch alphabet. If a graph has FINITELY MANY translation types of edges, a global weighting does imply such an alphabet: choose one representative oriented geometric edge for each type and each of the three weights; assign every edge to its type and its existing weight, with Lambda the corresponding translation vectors. This is an exact edge-partition into finitely many singleton-edge templates, and sums are unchanged. Local finiteness alone does not give the finite-type hypothesis. The explicit row construction explains exactly why the shortcut fails in the literal scope. With an FLC restriction the finite-type hypothesis holds; the resulting existential singleton-edge reduction is classical/source-routine, not a constructive local labeling algorithm. The source's periodic quotient proof (Theorem 3.1) is a different, stronger periodic conclusion. Stawiski Lemma 6 proof was read for the compactness bridge: finite-ball restrictions need not already solve the boundary, but a diagonal/Konig limit stabilizes every full finite incident star.

Beyond that shortcut, consider extending a deck-equivalent pair. Adding s isolated vertices gives an elementary formula for a k-card: a disjoint union of a (k-r)-card of the original graph and r isolated vertices, with multiplicity binomial(s,r). Equality of every original deck up to k therefore transfers to the k-deck. However it destroys connectedness and so supplies no tree counterexample. Attaching leaves at a root keeps a tree but needs ROOTED deck data: a card containing new leaves depends on whether the chosen root is in the original card, and on its rooted isomorphism type. Equality of the unrooted 7-decks gives no such rooted equality. Furthermore, enlarging n while retaining the threshold n>=2ell+1 forces the observed card size k=n-ell to grow, and equality of the 7-decks does not give equality of an 8-deck. Downward reconstruction is valid; upward reconstruction is not.

A proof approach via Kelly-type incidence equations has the same missing step: star-card counts determine some degree statistics, but not the rooted attachments or the full spine order for arbitrary trees. The newer source's caterpillar theorem assumes n>=48 and a path of nonleaf vertices. It cannot reconstruct a general branching core.
