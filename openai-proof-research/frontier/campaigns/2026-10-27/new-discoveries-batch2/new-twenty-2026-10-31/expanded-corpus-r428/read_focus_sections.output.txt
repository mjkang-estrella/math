68:                              1. Introduction and Preview
69:    The famous n-Queens Problem is to place n nonattacking queens—the largest conceivable
70: number—on an n × n chessboard, or more broadly, to count the number of placements. (See,
71: for instance, [3] on the former and [11] on the latter.) The counting problem has no known
72: solution except by individual computation for relatively small values of n.
73:    This article is Part I of a series [5] that presents a natural generalization we call the
74: q-Queens Problem, wherein we arbitrarily fix the number of queens, q, and vary n, the
75: size of the board; also, the “queen” may be any of a large class of traditional and fairy
76: chess pieces called “riders”. We show (Theorem 4.1) that for each separate problem the
77: number of solutions is, aside from a denominator of q!, a quasipolynomial function of n,
78: which means it is given by a cyclically repeating sequence of polynomials. This form of
79: solution could be inferred from empirical formulas for small q found over the decades (see
80: [9]) though it was never proved; our approach makes it obvious. Remarkably, the coefficients
81: of this quasipolynomial in n are themselves (up to a normalization) polynomial functions of
178: of size n = −1 (!).
179:    Section 6 tackles the fundamental problem of bounding the quasipolynomial period, or
180: rather its natural upper bound the denominator. We explain a fairly simple approach from
181: [6] involving subdeterminants of matrices. Examples suggest it provides an inefficient bound;
182: still, some bound is better than none at all.
183:    In the final section we propose new research directions, among which are pieces of different
184: kinds on the same board, pieces on higher-dimensional boards, and even a wild generalization
185: where the attacking moves depend on which piece is attacked as well as which does the
186: attacking. Indeed, throughout the series we list open directions and conjectures. Two
187: problems seem to be of highest importance. One that is fundamental to our approach but
188: very complex is that of determining all the subspaces needed to apply our general formulas
189: in examples; this question (see Section 7.2) lies in the overlap of matroid theory and real
190: incidence geometry. Then in Section II.7.2 we discuss the great dissimilarity between the
191: period of the counting function and the length of a recurrence for its values according to
192: the (largely unproved but highly suggestive) work of Kotěšovec. An understanding of this
193: phenomenon should permit a vast reduction in the computing power required to get provable
194: formulas.
195:    We end each part with a dictionary of notation for the benefit of the authors and readers.

767:    For each basic move mr ∈ M from a fixed location, the line hmr i is naturally directed, so
768: it has a left and right side. Given a nonattacking configuration of q pieces, record, for each
769: piece Pi at location zi and each move line zi + hmr i through Pi , oriented in the direction of
770: mr , the indices of the points Pj that lie on the left side of the line. The set of these lists,
771: for every pair, is the combinatorial type of the labelled configuration, briefly the labelled
772: configuration type. (Our configuration types are always nonattacking; we are not interested
773: in attacking types.)
774:    Another way to describe a labelled configuration type is by building a nonattacking con-
775: figuration, one piece at a time. Place the first piece, labelled P1 , at z1 ∈ tB◦ ∩ Z2 . This
776: creates |M| lines through z1 that may not be occupied by any other piece, and 2|M| regions
777: that may be occupied. The second piece, P2 at z2 , will be in one of these regions. Now
778: we have |M| forbidden lines through z2 ; these lines in combination create permitted regions
779: that will be occupied by the other pieces. Placing the third piece further subdivides these
780: into a larger number of regions, and similarly with each placement up to the last piece. The
781: sequence of choices of region is equivalent to the labelled configuration type.
782:    By forgetting the order of the pieces we have an unlabelled combinatorial type of config-
783: uration, for short an unlabelled configuration type. We want to know how many unlabelled
784: configuration types there are.
785: Lemma 5.1. There are q! labelled nonattacking configuration types for each unlabelled type.
786: Proof. In the left-to-right direction −m⊥   r perpendicular to a move line hmr i, the q labelled
787: pieces appear in a definite order, (P1 , P2 , . . . , Pq ). This is indicated by the left-side list of the
788: ith piece with respect to mr , which is {1, . . . , i − 1}. Renumbering the pieces changes the
789: order, hence the left-side list of at least one piece, and therefore the labelled configuration
790: type.                                                                                                   

823:    Still another way to look at the type of a configuration is through isotopy. Two labelled
824: configurations z and z′ are isotopic if one can be deformed into the other by a continuous
825: movement in the configuration space R2q without at any time crossing a move hyperplane.
826: It is clear from the correspondence between lists and regions in the proof of Lemma 5.2, and
827: the convexity of regions, that this is possible if and only if z and z′ have the same left-side
828: lists, and then the isotopism can be performed along a line segment in the interior of tBq .
829:    On the other hand, one might ask about discrete isotopy, where we move one piece at a
830: time on the board. A discrete isotopism is a sequence of steps, z = z0 → z1 → · · · → zk = z′
831: where zj−1 and zj differ only by making a legitimate move of a single piece that does not
832: change the combinatorial type of the configuration. One should allow any amount of inflation,
833: i.e., one should be allowed to multiply all the coordinates by a very large positive integer
834: before performing the isotopism (which one can think of as replacing the (1/t)-lattice by a
835: finer (1/kt)-lattice). One would naively expect this to be equivalent to continuous isotopy,
836: and indeed it is, once we overcome two difficutlies.
837:    First, if there is only one basic move m, the only configurations that can be reached from a
838: nonattacking configuration z by allowed moves are those in the line through z in the direction
839: of m (in some zi plane). Therefore, there must be more than one move in M.
840:    Second, even if there are two basic moves, there can be configurations that are unreachable
841: from each other in a board tB if t is fixed; for instance, in a configuration of bishops no moves
842: can change the numbers of bishops on squares of each color. That problem is solvable by
843: inflation.

29:                                           Theorem (Theorem 3.1). The Hurwitz number hg,µ is a positive integer except for the cases hg,(1) = 0
30:                                           (g ∈ Z≥1 ) and hg,(2) = hg,(1,1) = 1/2 (g ∈ Z≥0 ).
31:                                              By the term “Hurwitz number”, we mean the weighted counting of the connected coverings of the pro-
32:                                           jective line P1 which have a ramification at one point with the profile data given by µ, and which also have
33:                                           simple ramifications at r points. Here µ is a partition and g is a non-negative integer (the genus of the
