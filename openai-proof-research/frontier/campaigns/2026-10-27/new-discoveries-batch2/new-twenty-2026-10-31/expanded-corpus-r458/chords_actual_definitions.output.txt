485 We now turn to describing two classes of combinatorial objects A which satisfy an identity
486 of the form (10), thereby formulating our generating function solutions to equations (4) and
487 (5). This requires defining the relevant combinatorial objects, connected chord diagrams, as
488 well as various properties and features of these objects, including some which will not be
489 needed until later in the paper.
490 Definition 4.1 (Chord diagram, intersection graph, crossing, nesting). A rooted chord
491 diagram C of size n is a perfect matching of {1, 2, . . . , 2n}; the chord containing 1 is the
492 root of C and we view the empty set ∅ as a diagram of size 0. By convention, the elements
493 of C are ordered pairs (x, y) with x < y; x and y are called the source and sink, respectively,
494 of chord (x, y). A subset D ⊆ C is a subdiagram of C. The directed intersection graph G(C)
495 of C has the chords as vertices and two chords c1 = (x1 , y1 ) and c2 = (x2 , y2 ) with x1 < x2
496 joined by a directed edge c1 c2 if x2 < y1 < y2 . In this case, we say that c2 is a right neighbor
497 of c1 and c1 is a left neighbor of c2 . Forgetting direction, the edge c1 c2 is also referred to as
498 a crossing and c1 and c2 are said to cross. On the other hand, if x1 < x2 < y2 < y1 then c2
499 is nested under c1 and together these two chords form a nesting.
500 
501                                                                           9

503     Note that we treat a subdiagram D of C as a proper chord diagram on {1, 2, . . . , 2|D|}
504 by applying an order-preserving bijection. In the sequel, any graph-theoretic notions used
505 in the context of a chord diagram or its elements should be understood as referring to the
506 appropriate feature of its directed intersection graph (e.g. c1 is in the neighborhood of c2 ).
507 Furthermore, for brevity, we will use the generic term ‘diagram’ to refer to a chord diagram.
508 We will also extend the notion of nesting to subdiagrams in the obvious way: D0 ⊆ C is
509 nested under D ⊆ C if each chord of D0 is nested under every chord of D. Unsurprisingly, a
510 diagram is called nonnesting (resp. noncrossing) if it contains no nestings (resp. crossings).
511     There are two representations of a chord diagram used in the literature. The circular
512 representation involves arranging points labeled by 1, 2, . . . , 2n on a circle and, for each
513 chord, joining the source and sink points with a straight line. We can obtain the linear
514 representation from the circular one by cutting the circle just before the root point labeled 1
515 and straightening the resulting curve, letting the lines corresponding to each chord bend into
516 smooth curves lying above the straightened curve, and then deleting the curve (see Figure 1).
517 While the circulation representation becomes unrooted after dropping the labels, the linear
518 representation is naturally rooted at the leftmost point and the element labels are determined
519 by the linear order of the points. We will use the linear representation throughout this paper.

521 Definition 4.2 (Connectivity, connected diagram). The vertex connectivity κ(C), or simply
522 connectivity, of a diagram C is the vertex connectivity κ(G(C)) of its intersection graph, that
523 is, the minimum number of vertices whose removal disconnects G(C). The edge connectivity
524 λ(C) of C is defined similarly by removing edges instead of vertices. A diagram C is k-
525 connected if κ(C) ⩾ k; in particular, it is connected if it is 1-connected.
526 
527     Note that this definition is really in terms of the undirected intersection graph; the
528 directions on the edges do not play a role. A disconnected diagram can be equivalently defined
529 as one which can be partitioned into two nonempty subdiagrams with no crossings between
530 them. The strictly stronger notion of decomposability of a diagram arises by excluding both
531 crossings and nestings between the two subdiagrams.

533 Definition 4.3 (Diagram concatenation, indecomposable diagram). Let C1 and C2 be dia-
534 grams. The concatenation of C1 and C2 is the diagram C1 C2 of size |C1 | + |C2 | whose perfect
535 matching restricts to C1 on the first 2|C1 | elements and to C2 on the next 2|C2 | elements (as
536 subdiagrams). A diagram C is decomposable if it can be expressed as the concatenation of
537 two smaller diagrams and indecomposable otherwise.

561 Definition 4.5 (Standard order, intersection order). The standard order of C is given by

573 Definition 4.6 (k-terminal chords and diagrams). Let C be a chord diagram. For k ∈ N, a
574 chord c ∈ C is k-terminal if it is incident to at most k − 1 outgoing edges in G(C), that is,
575 it has at most k − 1 right neighbors. We refer to 1-terminal chords as simply terminal; these
576 are the maximal elements in the reachability poset. We extend this language to diagrams: C
577 is k-terminal if there is no j-terminal chord before the jth-to-last chord for all 1 ⩽ j ⩽ k.
578 Furthermore, the terminality of C is k if C is k-terminal but not (k + 1)-terminal.
579     Since the chord with rightmost sink in each connected component is necessarily terminal,
580 it follows immediately from the definition that k-terminal diagrams are connected and the
581 last k + 1 chords form a clique; in particular, there are exactly j j-terminal chords for all
582 1 ⩽ j ⩽ k. Furthermore, clearly k-terminal chords and diagrams are also j-terminal for
583 1 ⩽ j ⩽ k. The indices of the terminal chords will play an important role in both our
584 generating function solutions and the rest of the paper. Accordingly, we will denote the
585 index of the jth terminal chord in the intersection order of a diagram C by tj (C). We now
