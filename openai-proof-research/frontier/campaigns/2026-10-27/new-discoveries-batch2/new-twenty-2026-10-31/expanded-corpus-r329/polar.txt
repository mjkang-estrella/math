We identify universal polar dual pairs of spherical codes C and D such that for a large class of potential functions h the minima of the discrete h -potential of C on the sphere occur at the points of D and vice versa. Moreover, the minimal values of their normalized potentials are equal. These codes arise from the known hierarchies of sharp codes embedded in the even unimodular extremal lattices E 8 and Λ 24 (Leech lattice). This embedding allows us to use the lattices’ properties to find new universal polar dual pairs. In the process we extensively utilize the interplay between the binary Golay codes and the Leech lattice. As a byproduct of our analysis, we identify a new universally optimal (in the sense of energy) code in the projective space ℝ ​ ℙ 21 with 1408 points (lines). Furthermore, we extend the Delsarte-Goethals-Seidel definition of derived codes from their seminal 1977 paper and generalize their Theorem 8.2 to show that if a τ -design is enclosed in k ≤ τ parallel hyperplanes, then each derived code is a ( τ + 1 − k ) -design in the ambient subspace. Keywords : Discrete potentials, sharp spherical configurations, linear programming, Gauss-Jacobi quadrature, universal polarization bounds, polar duality, E 8 and Leech lattices . Introduction A non-empty finite subset C of the unit sphere 𝕊 n − 1 := { x ∈ ℝ n : | x | = 1 } , n ≥ 2 , in Euclidean space is called a spherical code . For a function h : [ − 1 , 1 ] → ( − ∞ , + ∞ ] , continuous in the extended sense on [ − 1 , 1 ] and finite on [ − 1 , 1 ) , we consider the discrete h -potential associated with C given by U h ​ ( x , C ) := ∑ y ∈ C h ⁡ ( x ⋅ y ) , where x ∈ 𝕊 n − 1 is arbitrary and x ⋅ y denotes the inner product in ℝ n . Definition 1.1 . Let C ⊂ 𝕊 n − 1 be a spherical code. We say that D ⊂ 𝕊 n − 1 is the set of universal minima 1 1 1 We emphasize that for most codes C a universal minima set does not exist. associated with C if D = argmin x ∈ 𝕊 n − 1 ​ U h ​ ( x , C ) , for all strictly absolutely monotone potentials h on [ − 1 , 1 ) (that is h ( k ) ( t ) > 0 , k = 0 , 1 , … ). A pair of spherical codes ( C , D ) , such that D is the set of universal minima associated with C and C is the set of universal minima associated with D , is called a universal polar dual pair . Geometrically, a set of universal minima must be contained in the set of deep holes; that is, the points in 𝕊 n − 1 whose distance to the code C is maximal (see [ 18 , Proposition 14.4.1] and [ 24 , Theorem 4.8(i)] ). For example, the universal minima set of the regular icosahedron is its dual dodecahedron and vice versa. Hence, they form a universal polar dual pair (see [ 15 , Theorems 3.3 and 3.4] ) and their universal minima sets coincide with the sets of deep holes. The same situation occurs with the remaining Platonic solids. The octahedron and the cube form a universal polar dual pair, while the tetrahedron forms a universal polar dual pair with its own antipode. The phenomenon of certain regular spherical codes forming pairs where each code is the set of minima of the potential of the other code was first observed for the case of power-law interactions by Stolarsky in 1975 (see [ 60 , 61 ] ) and subsequently by Nikolov and Rafailov [ 52 , 53 ] . These authors demonstrated this phenomenon for the set of vertices of a regular N -gon on 𝕊 1 (which forms such a pair with N midpoints of the arcs joining its neighboring vertices), regular simplex on 𝕊 n − 1 (which forms such a pair with its antipode), and the cube–cross-polytope pair on 𝕊 n − 1 . Hardin, Kendall, and Saff [ 43 ] , established this phenomenon for the regular N -gon and a π / N rotational counterpart for potential interactions given by a decreasing and convex function of the geodesic distance (this includes a certain class of absolutely monotone kernels). Later Borodachov [ 14 , 16 ] showed that the regular N -gon pairs, the n -simplex and its antipode, as well as the cross-polytope and the n -cube form universal polar dual pairs on 𝕊 n − 1 (see also [ 23 ] ). Some of the codes mentioned above are also known solutions to the max-min polarization (maximizing the minimum value of the potential of N points over the sphere) and min-max polarization (minimizing the maximum value of the potential of N points over the sphere). The optimality of the set of vertices of a regular N -gon was proved for max-min polarization in the works by Ambrus [ 1 ] , Nikolov and Rafailov [ 52 ] , Ambrus, Ball, and Erdelýi [ 2 ] , Erdelýi and Saff [ 37 ] for particular potentials, and for the general case in Hardin, Kendall, and Saff [ 43 ] . For min-max polarization this optimality was derived by Stolarsky [ 60 ] , Nikolov and Rafailov [ 52 ] , and Borodachov [ 14 ] . The set of vertices of a regular simplex on 𝕊 n − 1 was shown to have both max-min and min-max polarization properties by Su [ 62 ] and Borodachov [ 12 , 14 ] . While the max-min polarization property of the cross-polytope is still open, it was established by Boyvalenkov et al. in [ 23 , Proposition 6.7] for all centered codes (i.e.,codes for which there is a point on the sphere whose inner products to points of the cross-polytope are in the interval [ − 1 / n , 1 / n ] ; this class includes all antipodal 2 ​ n -point codes). A common property for the regular N -gon on 𝕊 1 , the regular simplex, and the cross-polytope in 𝕊 n − 1 is that they are sharp codes in the sense of Cohn and Kumar [ 28 ] ; that is, they are spherical ( 2 ​ m − 1 ) - or 2 ​ m -designs (see Definition 1.6 ) that have m different inner products between distinct points in the code. All such codes known to the authors are listed in [ 28 , Table 1] . In this article we shall verify the following Claim highlighting prominently the connection between sharp codes and universal polar dual pairs. Claim: All spherical sharp codes known to date (aside from the generalized quadrangles family), as well as the 24 -cell generate universal polar dual pairs. Remark 1.2 . To illustrate the term “generate” above, in Theorem 4.1 (e) we consider the Clebsch code C 16 = ( 5 , 16 , 3 ) , whose set of universal minima is the cross-polytope C 10 in ℝ 5 . However, the set of universal minima of C 10 is the symmetrized Clebsch code, where for a code C we refer to C ∪ ( − C ) as the symmetrization of C (see the caption of Table 1 ). In fact, more generally, for any sharp code in the above Claim, we establish that its symmetrization is a member of a universal polar dual pair. In addition, if a sharp code C is a spherical design of even strength, then the universal polar dual pair is ( C , − C ) (see Remark 2.4 ). Remark 1.3 . As our definition of universal polar dual pairs refers to finite sets C and D , we exclude degenerate simplexes in the above Claim. For example, if C is the set of vertices of an equilateral triangle on the equator in 𝕊 2 , the set of universal minima D consists of the two poles. However, the set of minimal points of the potential of D is the entire equator. We next provide additional examples illustrating the concept of universal polar dual pairs in the context of lattices. We highlight the polar duality with the case of the regular hexagon (see Figure 1 ). Let us embed the regular hexagon A = { A i } i = 1 6 in a hexagonal lattice with 0 at the center of the hexagon. The global minima of the potential U h ​ ( x , A ) , where x belongs to the circumscribing circle, are the midpoints of the arcs between the six vertices of A (see [ 43 ] ), which are also projections of the second layer B = { B i } i = 1 6 onto the circle, and vice versa, the global minima of U h ​ ( y , B ) , where y belongs to the circumscribing circle of B , are the projections of A i onto that circle. Thus, we can think of the universal polar dual pair ( A , B ) geometrically as the first and second layers of the hexagonal lattice. We shall see in Theorems 4.1 (a) and 5.3 that the first two layers of the celebrated E 8 and the Leech lattices form universal polar dual pairs. For the purposes of verification and derivation of universal polar dual pairs we make use of properties of spherical designs [ 4 , 5 , 6 , 31 , 34 , 38 , 49 ] , as well as lower bounds for minima of discrete potentials which we refer to as polarization universal lower bounds (PULB) of spherical codes and designs [ 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 23 , 24 ] . Our approach of embedding the universal polar dual pairs into even unimodular extremal lattices, such as the E 8 and Leech lattices, and utilizing the basic properties of these lattices to find a split of a spherical code into subcodes contained in parallel hyperplanes (thereby satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem 1.9 or 1.10 ) is new and even for known PULB-optimal codes provides simpler and shorter proofs of their optimality and their sets of minima. Numerous new PULB-optimal configurations are found in the process as well. Moreover, the embedding allows us to prove the maximality of the respective PULB-optimal pairs utilizing the self-duality of E 8 and Λ 24 . This approach empowers us to derive and identify the universal polar dual pairs in Table 1 . We make extensive use of the interplay between the binary Golay codes and the Leech lattice. A prominent role in our proofs of the maximality of the PULB-optimal pairs is played by the Smith normal form (see Subsection 2.5 ). Our tools also include an extension of Theorem 8.2 by Delsarte-Goethals-Seidel [ 34 ] ; namely, we prove that if a spherical τ -design is contained in k ≤ τ parallel hyperplanes, then the subcodes in each of the hyperplanes are spherical ( τ + 1 − k ) -designs in the lower dimension, a result of independent interest. Lattices, generators, and the Smith normal form A discrete set Λ ⊂ ℝ n is a lattice in ℝ n if there is a finite set { b 1 , … , b k } ⊂ ℝ n such that Λ = { ∑ i = 1 k x i b i ∣ x 1 , x 2 , … , x k ∈ ℤ } = B ℤ k , where B is the n × k matrix with columns b 1 , … , b k . We also write Λ = ispan ⁡ ( b 1 , … , b k ) where ispan denotes the set of linear combinations of the vectors b 1 , … , b k with integer coefficients. If rank ⁡ ( B ) = n , then Λ is called a full-rank lattice . If Λ is a full-rank lattice in ℝ n , then one can find n linearly independent vectors a 1 , … , a n such that Λ = A ​ ℤ n = ispan ⁡ ( a 1 , … , a n ) , where A is the n × n non-singular matrix with columns a 1 , … , a n , in which case we call A a generator for Λ . The co-volume of a lattice Λ with generator A is | Λ | := | det A | which is the volume of any choice of measurable fundamental domain Ω Λ = ℝ n / Λ and, in particular, the volume of the fundamental parallelotope A [ 0 , 1 ) n . A lattice Φ that is a subset of a lattice Λ is called a sublattice of Λ . The index | Λ : Φ | of Φ in Λ is the cardinality of the quotient Λ / Φ and is easily shown to equal the ratio of the co-volumes | Λ : Φ | = | Φ | / | Λ | . A lattice is called unimodular if its co-volume equals one; i.e., if | det A | = 1 . The dual lattice Λ ∗ of a full-rank lattice Λ is defined to be Λ ∗ := { v ∈ ℝ n ∣ w ⋅ v ∈ ℤ , ∀ w ∈ Λ } and has generator A − t := ( A t ) − 1 if Λ is generated by A . It follows that the co-volume of Λ ∗ is | Λ ∗ | = 1 / | Λ | . A general reference to the subject is [ 31 ] . The lattices of main interest in this paper, namely the E 8 and Leech lattices, can be scaled to be sublattices of ℤ n for n = 8 , 24 , respectively. We will find the Smith normal form [ 58 , 59 ] useful for computing a generator and the co-volume of a sublattice of ℤ n from a set of linearly dependent vectors whose integer linear combinations span that sublattice. A new universally optimal code in the real projective space ℝ ​ ℙ 21 Strictly speaking, the code A 2 = ( 22 , 2816 , 5 ) from Section 5.3 is not new and even might be well known to the specialists in lattices. However, its universal optimality as a code in the real projective space was not mentioned neither by Levenshtein in his tables from [ 48 ] (1992) and [ 49 ] (1998) nor by Cohn, Kumar, and Minton [ 30 ] (2016, when the concept of universal optimality by Cohn and Kumar [ 28 ] was already in use). The optimality of A 2 among all antipodal spherical codes on 𝕊 21 of cardinality 2816 follows from the Levenshtein bound [ 46 ] on codes on 𝕊 n − 1 with bounded modulus of the inner product from 1982. In slightly different setting of the linear programming approach, it was first explicitly noticed by the second author in 1993 (cf. Theorem 6 in [ 20 ] ). Theorem 6.1 .
