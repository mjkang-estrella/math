   Our main motivation for finding invariant polydiagonal subspaces is taken from our ef-
forts [9, 10, 13] to find and understand bifurcations of solutions to differential equations.
The Bifurcation Lemma for Invariant Subspaces (BLIS) [13] assumes the existence of nested
invariant subspaces to prove the existence of bifurcating solutions in many cases. The code
available at [12] contains an implementation of the corresponding BLIS algorithm which
requires the invariant polydiagonal subspaces as input, both the anomalous invariant sub-
spaces [9] and the ubiquitous, well-understood fixed point subspaces. The current paper
shows how to compute these invariant subspaces.

                                     2. Preliminaries
   We recall some terminology from [15]. The definition in [3] is also relevant. This provides
the precise mathematical definition of synchrony and anti-synchrony subspaces. A partial
involution on a set X is a partial function x 7→ x∗ : X̃ → X̃ that is its own inverse. Note
that X̃ ⊆ X and x∗∗ = x. A tagged partition of a finite set C is a partition P of C together
with a partial involution P 7→ P ∗ : P̃ → P̃ on P that has at most one fixed point. The
equivalence class of i ∈ C is denoted by [i], so that i ∈ [i] ∈ P. The polydiagonal subspace
of a tagged partition P of C = {1, . . . , n} is the subspace
               ∆P := {x ∈ Rn | xi = xj if [i] = [j] and xi = −xj if [i]∗ = [j]}
of Rn . Note that if [i]∗ = [i], then xi = 0 for all x ∈ ∆P . A subspace ∆ of Rn is called
polydiagonal if ∆ = ∆P for some tagged partition P. The polydiagonal subspace ∆P is
called a synchrony subspace if the partial involution is the empty function. Otherwise, it is
called an anti-synchrony subspace. In the next section, we describe polydiagonal subspaces
in terms of coloring vectors.
   For a graph with vertex set {1, . . . , n}, the adjacency matrix is the n × n matrix A for
which Ai,j = 1 if there is an edge between i and j and Ai,j = 0 otherwise. The Laplacian
matrix of the graph is L = D − A, where D is the diagonal matrix consisting of the degrees
of the vertices.

                                  3. Coloring vectors
   Coloring vectors were introduced in [11] to encode partitions. We generalize this notion
for tagged partitions.
Definition 3.1. An element c of Zn is called a coloring vector if
   (1) c1 ∈ {0, 1};
   (2) −mi ≤ ci ≤ 1 + mi for all i ∈ {2, . . . , n},
where mi := max{c1 , . . . , ci−1 }.
  In other words, the first non-zero component of a coloring vector is 1, and for any k ≥ 1
a component equal to −k or k + 1 must be preceded by a component equal to k.
Remark 3.2. A simple inductive argument shows that if c is a coloring vector, then
   (1’) −i + 1 ≤ ci ≤ i for all i.
On the other hand, this condition guarantees that c1 ∈ {0, 1}, so it is stronger than Defini-
tion 3.1(1). Hence we can use (1’) instead of (1) in Definition 3.1. The advantage is that

Example 3.3. The tuples (1, 0, −1, 2, 1, 2) and (0, 0, 1, 2, 3) are coloring vectors, while the

  The following is easy to verify.
Proposition 3.4. The conditions of Definition 3.1 are equivalent to the following conditions:
  (1) −i + 1 ≤ ci ≤ i for all i;
  (2) for all i ∈ {2, . . . , n} there is a j ∈ {1, . . . , i − 1} such that ci ≤ 1 + cj ;
  (3) for all i ∈ {2, . . . , n}, either ci ≥ 0 or ci ∈ {−c1 , . . . , −ci−1 }.
Definition 3.5. Given a coloring vector c ∈ Zn , let [i] := {j | ci = cj }, Pc := {[i] | i ∈
{1, . . . , n}}, and [i]∗ := {j | cj = −ci }.
Proposition 3.6. If c is a coloring vector in Zn , then Pc endowed with the partial involution
[i] 7→ [i]∗ : P̃ → P̃ for P̃ = {[i] | [i]∗ ̸= ∅} is a tagged partition of C = {1, . . . , n}.
Proof. It is easy to see that we have a partial involution on a partition of C. If ci ̸= 0 then [i]
is not a fixed point of this partial involution. So the only possible fixed point is {i | ci = 0}
when this set is non-empty.                                                                     □
Proposition 3.7. The map c 7→ Pc is a bijection between the set of coloring vectors of Zn
and the set of tagged partitions of C = {1, . . . , n}.
Proof. The map is clearly injective. To show that the map is also surjective, consider a
tagged partition P of C. Let A0 be the fixed point of the partial involution if it exists. Let
Q := {A ∈ P̃ | min(A∗ ) ≤ min(A)}. Note that A0 ∈ Q if A0 exists. Let (A1 , . . . , Ad ) be the
ordering of the classes in P \ Q according to the natural order of their minimum elements.
That is, min(Ar ) < min(As ) for all 0 < r < s. Let A−k := A∗k if A∗k ∈ Q. Now every element
in P has a unique label. Define c ∈ Zn by ci = k if [i] = Ak . The construction guarantees
that c is a coloring vector with Pc = P.                                                     □

                           4. Invariant polydiagonal subspaces
   A subspace V of Rn is invariant under the matrix M ∈ Rn×n if M V ⊆ V . The following
is an easy consequence of Proposition 3.11.
Example 4.1. The partitions corresponding to synchrony subspaces that are invariant under
the adjacency matrix of a graph are exactly the equitable partitions.
Proposition 4.2. The polydiagonal subspace ∆Pc of Rn is invariant under M ∈ Rn×n if
and only if for all k ∈ Kc the conditions
                         (k)    (k)
   (1) ci = cj implies wi = wj ;
                                (k)         (k)
   (2) ci = −cj implies wi             = −wj ;
                          (k)
   (3) ci = 0 implies wi = 0
hold for all distinct i and j, where w(k) := M b(k) .
Remark 4.3. In Proposition 4.2, the k ∈ Kc condition can be replaced with k ∈ {1, . . . , n−1}.
This formulation is more suitable for the constraint satisfaction problem in Table 2. Since
b(k) = 0 for k ∈ N \ Kc , we can allow k ∈ {1, . . . , n} since {b(1) , . . . , b(n) } is a spanning set.
Note that b(n) is nonzero only if c = (1, . . . , n). In this case ∆Pc = Rn , so checking the
conditions for k = n is unnecessary as they are automatically satisfied.
  Note that the coloring vector of a synchrony subspace only has positive components. So
Conditions (2) and (3) of Proposition 4.2 are automatically satisfied for synchrony subspaces.
Remark 4.4. The set of polydiagonal subspaces invariant under a matrix is a lattice ordered
by reverse inclusion [1, 2, 3]. This ordering induces an ordering of the corresponding coloring
vectors. In this order c(1) ≤ c(2) if and only if
         (1)   (1)           (2)    (2)
   (1) ci = cj implies ci = cj ;
         (1)      (1)                 (2)    (2)
   (2) ci = −cj implies ci = −cj ;
         (1)               (2)
    (3) ci = 0 implies ci = 0
hold for all distinct i and j. Conditions (1)–(3) can be applied even if c(1) , c(2) ∈ Rn . Using
this extended order, the conditions of Proposition 4.2 can be simply stated as c ≤ w(k) for
all k.
  We often consider M to be the adjacency or the Laplacian matrix of a graph. However,
any square matrix is the adjacency matrix of a digraph with weighted edges, as seen in the
next example [15].
Example 4.5. The Hasse diagram of the lattice of coloring vectors corresponding to polydi-
agonal subspaces invariant under M are shown in Figure 1. The matrix M is the in-adjacency
matrix of the weighted digraph shown. Note that b(1) = (1, 0, 1) and b(2) = (0, 1, 0) for
c = (1, 2, 1). Hence w(1) = M b(1) = (2, 0, 2) ≥ c and w(2) = M b(2) = (−1, −1, −1) ≥ c,
verifying that ∆Pc = {(a, b, a) | a, b ∈ R} is invariant under M .

   We developed code that solves this constraint satisfaction problem using four Python
interfaces to three different solvers. Two of them are available in the appendices. All four
programs are available at our companion website [8].
   In these programs, the dependent variables can be actual variables or expressions, de-
pending on the constraint solver. After a solver finds all possible coloring vectors, the
corresponding invariant polydiagonal spaces can be built using Proposition 3.7.
   The best performing version for smaller problems uses DOcplex, the Python interface to
IBM’s CP Optimizer [6]. The versions using the CP-SAT solver [16] handle larger or more
complex problems better. We use the CP-SAT solver through two interfaces. One of them
uses the native ORtools interface, the other uses the CPMpy [5] interface. The ORtools
version performs better if we use the conditions of Proposition 3.4. The version using the
Z3 Theorem Prover [4] is significantly slower. This is probably because Z3 is designed to
solve more generic problems. We include the Z3 version because it is easily accessible, user
friendly, and has a non-commercial license.

                        6. Comparison to existing approaches
  No algorithm, other than brute force, is currently available to compute all invariant polydi-
agonal subspaces. Our constraint programming approach is the first practical way to handle
medium-size matrices.
  The first useful algorithm to compute only the invariant synchrony subspaces was devel-
oped in [7] and further improved in [11] as the split and cir algorithm. These algorithms rely
on the computation of the coarsest invariant refinement (cir) of a partition. The synchrony
subspace corresponding to the cir is the invariant synchrony subspace with the smallest
dimension that contains the original synchrony subspace.
  The split and cir algorithm starts with the singleton partition {{1, . . . , n}}, corresponding
to the coloring vector c = (1, . . . , 1). The one-dimensional subspace ∆Pc is not necessarily
invariant. The queue of candidates is initialized with this partition. For each candidate
in the queue, the cir is computed and added to the list of partitions producing invariant
synchrony subspaces. For each new invariant partition, each class is split into two classes

in all possible ways, which are added to the candidate queue. The queue will eventually be
empty, terminating the algorithm.
   Our constraint programming code greatly outperforms the split and cir algorithm. A
theoretical algorithm for computing invariant polydiagonal subspaces using eigenvectors of
the matrix M is described in [1, 3]. Since no implementation is available, it is hard to
compare the performance of this approach to ours. One advantage of our approach is that
it uses exact arithmetic.

  Polydiagonal subspaces that are invariant under a collection of matrices are also often
useful as was shown in [11]. The constraint programming approach for finding invariant
polydiagonal subspaces easily generalizes to this setting. It only requires the constraints
corresponding to the conditions of Proposition 4.2 for each matrix in the collection.
                                           7. Examples
   Let Aut(G) be the automorphism group and M be the adjacency matrix of the simple
graph G. Define Γ := Aut(G) × Z∗ , where and Z∗ = {−1, 1} is the unit group of Z. For
(ϕ, σ) ∈ Γ and x ∈ Rn , define (ϕ, σ)x := (σxϕ(1) , . . . , σxϕ(n) ). Then Γ acts on the set of
M -invariant polydiagonal subspaces by (ϕ, σ)V := {(ϕ, σ)x | x ∈ V }. The point stabilizer
of such a subspace V is
                      pStab(V ) := {(ϕ, σ) ∈ Γ | (∀x ∈ V ) (ϕ, γ)x = x}.
The fixed point subspace Fix(Θ) of a subgroup Θ of Γ is the maximal M -invariant subspace
whose point stabilizer is Θ. We call Fix(pStab(V )) the fixed point subspace corresponding
to V . If Fix(pStab(V )) ̸= V , then V is an Anomalous Invariant Subspace (AIS). In other
words, an AIS is an M -invariant polydiagonal subspace that is not a fixed point subspace of
the Γ action. Although fixed point subspaces can be found by analyzing representations of
the Γ action [9], finding each AIS is very difficult without constraint programming.

5.7. Finite difference method for PDE. The current study of M -invariant partitions was
motivated by work where finite difference approximations to PDEs gave invariant subspaces
beyond those forced by symmetry [34, 35, 36, 37]. Invariant subspaces are crucial to analyzing
bifurcations and also reduce the dimension of the computations. The region of the PDE is
approximated by a grid of points, and a graph is obtained by joining nearest neighbors with
an edge. The matrix that approximates the Laplacian operator with boundary conditions

on the region is used in the finite difference method. The graph Laplacian matrix gives
the approximation for the Laplacian operator with zero Neumann boundary conditions [34],
so the synchrony subspaces of exo-balanced partitions are invariant. For other boundary
conditions, the subspaces that are invariant under the matrix approximating the Laplacian
include the synchrony subspaces of balanced partitions.

5.8. Network controllability. Network controllability concerns the possibility to transfer
the state of a dynamical system with external controls from any given initial state to any
final desired state in finite time [5, 28, 29, 48]. Such a network system is of the form
                                      ẋ = −M x + Bu,
where the matrix M describes the coupling between the components of the network, often
the adjacency or Laplacian matrix, the matrix B encodes the application of the external
controls u, and x encodes the state of the network.
   Invariant subspaces of the state space are an obstruction to controllability, since a target
state in an invariant subspace cannot be reached in finite time from an initial state not
in that invariant subspace. For example, the invariant subspaces forced by symmetry are
an obstacle toward the controllability of networks if the matrix B is chosen in a way that
preserves the invariant subspace. Similarly, an initial state in an invariant subspace cannot
reach a target state that is not in the subspace.
   It has been shown that symmetries are not necessary for uncontrollability [41]. Building on
this work, [5, Theorem 3] showed that if M is the Laplacian of the network and B is chosen
to respect a nontrivial almost equitable partition, then the network is uncontrollable. They
observed that almost equitable partitions appear frequently in many real-world networks and
account for many of the uncontrollable leader selections that do not come from symmetry.
A result of [49] provides an upper bound for the controllable subspace of networks in terms
of maximal almost equitable partitions. They give an algorithm for finding such partitions
given a set of singleton leaders. Our cir algorithm is a generalization of their algorithm with
the addition that we allow invariance with respect to multiple, arbitrary matrices.
   Further background and references for the subject of network controllability, particularly
as pertains to synchrony and almost equitable partitions, can be found in [4, 18, 30, 43].
