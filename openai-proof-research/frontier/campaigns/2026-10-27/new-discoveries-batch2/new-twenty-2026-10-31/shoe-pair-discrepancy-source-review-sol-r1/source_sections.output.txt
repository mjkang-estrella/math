10:                                               Abstract. Suppose one desires to randomly sample a pair of ob-
11:                                               jects such as socks, hoping to get a matching pair. Even in the sim-
12:                                               plest situation for sampling, which is sampling with replacement,
13:                                               the innocent phrase “the distribution of the color of a matching
14:                                               pair” is ambiguous. One interpretation is that we condition on the
15:                                               event of getting a match between two random socks; this corre-
16:                                               sponds to sampling two at a time, over and over without memory,
17:                                               until a matching pair is found. A second interpretation is to sam-
18:                                               ple sequentially, one at a time, with memory, until the same color
19:                                               has been seen twice.
20:                                                  We study the difference between these two methods. The input
21:                                               is a discrete probability distribution on colors, describing what
22:                                               happens when one sock is sampled. There are two derived distri-
23:                                               butions — the pair-color distributions under the two methods of
24:                                               getting a match. The output, a number we call the discrepancy of
25:                                               the input distribution, is the total variation distance between the
26:                                               two derived distributions.
27:                                                  It is easy to determine when the two pair-color distributions
28:                                               come out equal, that is, to determine which distributions have
29:                                               discrepancy zero, but hard to determine the largest possible dis-
30:                                               crepancy. We find the exact extreme for the case of two colors, by
31:                                               analyzing the roots of a fifth degree polynomial in one variable. We
32:                                               find the exact extreme for the case of three colors, by analyzing the
33:                                               49 roots of a variety spanned by two seventh-degree polynomials
34:                                               in two variables. We give a plausible conjecture for the general sit-
35:                                               uation of a finite number of colors, and give an exact computation
36:                                               of a constant which is a plausible candidate for the supremum of
37:                                               the discrepancy over all discrete probability distributions.
38:                                                  We briefly consider the more difficult case where the objects
39:                                               to be matched into pairs are of two different kinds, such as male-
40:                                               female or left-right.

13:                                                                           Abstract
14:                           For a collection of objects such as socks, which can be matched according to a
15:                           characteristic such as color, we study the innocent phrase ‘the distribution of the color
16:                           of a matching pair‘ by looking at two methods for selecting socks. One method is
17:                           memoryless and effectively samples socks with replacement, while the other samples
18:                           socks sequentially, with memory, until the same color has been seen twice. We prove
19:                           that these two methods yield the same distribution on colors if and only if the initial
20:                           distribution of colors is a uniform distribution. We conjecture a nontrivial maximum
21:                           value for the total variation distance of these distributions in all other cases.
22:                           Keywords: Total variation distance; random sampling; computational algebraic geometry;
23:                           Poisson process; pair-derived distribution

203:                                                             4. Total variation distance
204:                     We wish to quantify the following. Given a probability distribution p, with the matching pair
205:                 chosen by method (M1) or method (M2), how far apart are the two distributions with respect
206:                 to the color of the matching pair?
207:                    A metric on the space of all probability measures is the total variation distance.
208: 
209:                 Deﬁnition 1. For two real-valued random variables X and Y , the total variation distance
210:                 between the laws of X and Y is deﬁned as
