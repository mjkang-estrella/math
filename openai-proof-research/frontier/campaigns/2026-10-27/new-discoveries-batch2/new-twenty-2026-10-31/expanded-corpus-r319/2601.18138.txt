Abstract.
 

In 2013, Sun conjectured that the partition function p(n) is never a perfect power for n\geq 2 . Building on this, Merca, Ono, and Tsai recently observed that for any fixed integers d\geq 0 and k\geq 2 , there appear to be only finitely many integers n such that p(n) differs from a perfect k th power by at most d . Denoting by M_{k}(d) the largest such n , they conjectured that M_{k}(d)=o(d^{\epsilon}) for every \epsilon>0 .
 

In this paper, we investigate the asymptotic growth of analogs of M_{k}(d) for a wide class of partition functions. We establish sharp lower bounds and provide heuristics which suggest that M_{k}(d) in fact grows polylogarithmically in d , i.e. of order \log^{2}(d) . More generally, we prove that if f(n) is a suitably random chosen function with asymptotic growth rate similar to that of p(n) , then the set of integers n for which f(n) is a perfect power is finite with probability 1.
 



Key words and phrases: Partition function, perfect powers, probabilistic models

1. Introduction




For each positive integer n , let p(n) be the number of partitions of n , that is the number of non-increasing sequences of positive integers (\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{k}) such that \lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\dots+\lambda_{k}=n . We further define p(0)=1 . In 1751, Euler obtained the following infinite product generating function for p(n) ,



(1)

 \sum_{n\geq 0}p(n)q^{n}=\prod_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{1-q^{n}}, 



and in 1918, Hardy and Ramanujan [HR18] pioneered the circle method to prove the following asymptotic formula



(2)

 p(n)\sim\frac{1}{4n\sqrt{3}}\exp{\Bigg(\pi\sqrt{\frac{2n}{3}}\Bigg)}\;\text{ as }n\to\infty. 



This was later improved by Rademacher [Rad37], who obtained an exact infinite series that converges to p(n) .




The partition function has long been an object of broad and sustained interest, with connections to many areas of mathematics including mathematical physics (see [And76, AE04, Apo76] for example). And it continues to inspire intriguing observations. In 2013, Zhi-Wei Sun [Sun13] made the following conjecture (see also [Sun18], [Sun21, Conjecture 8.9 (iii)] and [Amd17, Fre]).



Conjecture A (Sun).



The partition function p(n) is never a perfect power of the form a^{b} for integers a,b>1 .





This conjecture has been verified for all n\leq 10^{8} by Alekseyev [Sun18], but still remains an open problem.




It’s natural to consider whether analogs of Sun’s conjecture hold for various different partition functions f(n) . One category of restricted partition functions can be described by p_{A}(n) , the number of partitions of n with parts taken from the set A\subset\mathbb{N} . The generating function for p(A) is




 \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{A}(n)x^{n}=\prod_{a\in A}\frac{1}{1-x^{a}}. 



Similar conjectures to Sun’s conjecture regarding whether p_{A}(N) can be a perfect square were formulated by Tengely and Ulas [TU21, Section 5]. In the case where A is a finite set consisting of the first k positive integers \{1,2,\dots,k\} , the analog of Sun’s conjecture was recently resolved by Ono [Ono25].




In recent work expanding upon Sun’s conjecture for p(n) , Merca, Ono and Tsai [MOT25] consider the proximity of p(n) to perfect powers more generally. For fixed n\geq 1 and k>1 , let




 \Delta_{k}(n):=\min\left\{|p(n)-m^{k}|:m\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}, 



the distance from p(n) to the closest k th power. Merca-Ono-Tsai make the following conjecture about \Delta_{k}(n) .



Conjecture B.



([MOT25], Conj. 1) For fixed integers k>1 and d\geq 0 , there are at most finitely many n for which \Delta_{k}(n)\leq d .





Assuming Conjecture B, there is a well-defined maximal integer n for which p(n) is within d of a k th power. Let



(3)

 M_{k}(d):=\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\Delta_{k}(n)\leq d\}. 



Then a proof of Sun’s conjecture would be equivalent to showing that M_{k}(0)=1 for all k>1 . Merca-Ono-Tsai give a table of conjectured values of M_{k}(d) , but proving even a single value would be an achievement, since none are currently known. In this paper we investigate the growth of M_{k}(d) for fixed k as well as for fixed d , motivated by the following conjectures of Merca-Ono-Tsai.




If we fix k>1 , Merca-Ono-Tsai predict that for p(n) , \{M_{k}(d)\}_{d=1}^{\infty} grows more slowly than any power of d .



Conjecture C.



([MOT25], Conj. 2) For each integer k>1 and every real \varepsilon>0 , we have that M_{k}(d)=o(d^{\varepsilon}) .





Here, we take a more general approach to these questions by considering any arithmetic function f(n) with f(n)\to\infty as n\to\infty and defining analogous functions






(4)

 \displaystyle\Delta_{f,k}(n) 
 \displaystyle:=\min\left\{|f(n)-m^{k}|:m\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}, 


(5)

 \displaystyle M_{f,k}(d) 
 \displaystyle:=\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\Delta_{f,k}(n)\leq d\}. 






In Proposition 3.3 we prove a lower bound for M_{f,k}(d) for fixed k and sufficiently large d . In Proposition 3.4, we are able to make this bound explicit when the asymptotic growth of f(n) is comparable to that of p(n) . In particular, for p(n) , we obtain the following lower bound.



Theorem 1.1.



Let k\geq 2 . We have for sufficiently large d ,




 M_{k}(d)\geq\frac{3}{2\pi^{2}}\Big(\frac{k}{k-1}\Big)^{2}(\log{d})^{2}+O(\log d\log\log d). 







Motivated by computational data discussed in Section 8, we make the following conjecture.



Conjecture D.



Let k\geq 2 . Then M_{k}(d)\sim\frac{3}{2\pi^{2}}\Big(\frac{k}{k-1}\Big)^{2}(\log{d})^{2} as d\to\infty .





When we instead fix d , Merca-Ono-Tsai assert that M_{k}(d) stabilizes in a predictable way. To state their conjecture, we first make the following definition. For each fixed integer d\geq 0 , define L(d) by



(6)

 L(d):=\max\{n:p(n)\leq d+1\}. 





Conjecture E.



([MOT25], Conj. 3 and 4)
There is a positive integer N_{d} such that for all k\geq N_{d} ,




 M_{k}(d)=L(d). 







Additionally, they observe that the bound N_{d} appears to grow like O(\log d) .




Again we take a more general approach, and in Proposition 2.3 we prove a generalization of Conjecture E in the cases when M_{f,k}(d) is bounded as k\to\infty . Using this assumption we also give, in Theorem 4.1, an explicit lower bound for the smallest value of k for which \{M_{f,k}(d)\}_{k=1}^{\infty} stabilizes. In particular, for the partition function p(n) , we prove the following.



Theorem 1.2.



If \{M_{k}(d)\}_{k=1}^{\infty} is bounded then there exists a smallest positive integer N_{d} such that M_{k}(d)=L(d) for all k\geq N_{d} . Furthermore, we have




 N_{d}\geq\lfloor\log_{2}(d)\rfloor+A\log\log d 



for any 0<A<\frac{1}{\log 2} and for all sufficiently large d .





1.1. A heuristic approach




Proving an explicit upper bound for \{M_{k}(d)\}_{d=1}^{\infty} remains elusive. More generally, proving precise values of M_{f,k}(d) for generic k and d , even for many simple functions such as f(n)=\lfloor e^{n}\rfloor , is a very difficult problem, but heuristic methods can provide a more tractable approach. We can use a random variable to model the behavior of functions f(n) similar to the partition function, then apply this heuristic to investigate which statements hold with probability 1.




Such methods have been frequently used to model the prime numbers. Cramér [Cra36] originally considered a heuristic model of the prime numbers to give a conjectured growth rate for the maximal gap between consecutive prime numbers. Such models were later refined by Granville [Gra95] and more recently by Banks–Ford–Tao [BFT23].




Heuristic models have also been used to study the statistics of zeros of L -functions (e.g. see the Katz-Sarnak philosophy [KS99a, KS99b]), the asymptotic behaviour of the Möbius function \mu(n) (e.g. see [Sar]) as well as give predictions for class groups of number fields (e.g. see [CL84] or [Coh93, p. 295]).11
 1
 
 
 
 For a further explanation for such probabilistic models, we refer the reader to the excellent blog post of Tao [Tao15].




We take care to emphasize that the results of this heuristic approach by no means constitute rigourous proofs regarding how close f(n) is to perfect powers, but they can at least tell us morally speaking which statements and conjectures we expect to be true, assuming that f(n) behaves in a “sufficiently random” way, which we shall make precise below.




In Section 6, we develop a general heuristic model for any sufficiently fast-growing function f(n) . Here we briefly describe that model for p(n) . We set A(n)=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{3}n}\exp(\pi\sqrt{\frac{2n}{3}}) , the main term of the Hardy-Ramanujan asymptotic for p(n) . Let \{\varepsilon_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} be a sequence of positive real numbers such that \varepsilon_{n}\to 0 as n\to\infty . To control the rate at which \varepsilon_{n} converges to 0, we also assume that



(7)

 \varepsilon_{n}\gg\frac{\log A(n)}{\sqrt{A(n)}}\asymp n\exp{\Big(-\frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2n}{3}}\Big)} 



as n\to\infty , where \asymp denotes asymptotically equivalent up to a constant. Next we let \{\mathbf{p}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} be a sequence of discrete random variables defined so that for each n , \mathbf{p}_{n} takes a uniform distribution among the set



(8)

 \mathcal{S}_{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{N}\mid A(n)(1-\varepsilon_{n})\leq x\leq A(n)(1+\varepsilon_{n})\}. 






For any integer m\in\mathbb{Z} , we define the probability mass function




 \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{p}_{n}=m)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\#{\mathcal{S}_{n}}}&\text{if }m\in\mathcal{S}_{n},\\
0&\text{otherwise. }\end{cases} 






We can now define the random variables




 \mathbf{\Delta}_{k}(n):=\min\big\{|\mathbf{p}_{n}-m^{k}|:m\in\mathbb{Z}\big\} 



and




 \mathbf{M}_{k}(d):=\max\{n:\mathbf{\Delta}_{k}(n)\leq d\} 



analogously to \Delta_{k}(n) and M_{k}(n) .




We are then able to prove the following theorem for p(n) .



Theorem 1.3.



The following statements are each true with probability 1.

(a) 



For any d\geq 0 and k\geq 2 , the random variable \mathbf{M}_{k}(d) is finite.

(b) 



The sequence of random variables \{\mathbf{p}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} will take only finitely many perfect powers.

(c) 



Let k\geq 2 and assume moreover than \varepsilon_{n}\gg A(n)^{-1/k} as n\to\infty . For any \varepsilon>0 and sufficiently large d , the random variable \mathbf{M}_{k}(d) satisfies the upper bound




 \mathbf{M}_{k}(d)<(4+\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{3}{2\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{k}{k-1}\right)^{2}(\log d)^{2}. 



In particular, \mathbf{M}_{k}(d)\asymp(\log d)^{2} as d\to\infty .

(d) 



For any fixed d\geq 0 , the sequence of random variables \{\mathbf{M}_{k}(d)\}_{k=2}^{\infty} is bounded.







More generally, we have analogous results to Theorem 1.3 for any function f(n) that meets the conditions of our model.



Remark 1.4.



Note that part (a) of Theorem 1.3 above gives a heuristic proof of Conjecture B, part (b) is in the direction of Sun’s Conjecture, part (c) gives a heuristic proof of Conjecture D, and part (d) gives a heuristic proof of Conjecture E.





This paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, we introduce the notation \Delta_{f,k}(n) and M_{f,k}(d) for an arbitrary function f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N} , and study their asymptotic behavior. In Section 3, we obtain bounds for \Delta_{f,k}(n) and M_{f,k}(d) and prove Theorem 1.1. In Section 4 we obtain bounds for N_{f,d} and prove Theorem 1.2. In Section 5 we provide strong computational evidence that the sequence \{\{\sqrt[k]{p(n)}\}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} of fractional parts of \sqrt[k]{p(n)} is equidistributed modulo 1, which motivates our probabilistic model and heuristic results.




In Section 6, we introduce our probabilistic model and define a sequence of random variables \{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} , used to model any sufficiently fast-growing function f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N} . We define the random variables \mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n) and \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{f},k}(d) analogous to \Delta_{f,k}(n) and M_{f,k}(d) , and prove various bounds on \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d) . In Section 7, we prove a heuristic upper bound for \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{f},k}(d) , and give a proof of Theorem 1.3. In Section 8, we extend the computations of Merca–Ono–Tsai and provide further computational evidence towards Conjecture B both for p(n) and various other partition functions. Finally, in Section 9, we provide further conjectures for other partition functions and study related functions \widetilde{\Delta}_{f,a}(n) and \widetilde{M}_{f,a}(d) .

2. Asymptotic behavior of \Delta_{f,k}(n) and M_{f,k}(d) 




Throughout this paper we let f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N} denote a function such that f(n)\rightarrow\infty as n\rightarrow\infty . For any integers k\geq 2 and n\geq 1 , let \Delta_{f,k}(n) be the distance between f(n) and its nearest k th power, namely



(9)

 \Delta_{f,k}(n):=\min\left\{|f(n)-m^{k}|:m\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}. 



We observe that \Delta_{f,k}(n) can be computed as



(10)

 \Delta_{f,k}(n)=\min\left(f(n)-\left\lfloor\sqrt[k]{f(n)}\right\rfloor^{k}\>,\>\left\lceil\sqrt[k]{f(n)}\right\rceil^{k}-f(n)\right), 



i.e., to compute \Delta_{f,k}(n) , it suffices to take the minimum of (i) the distance between f(n) and the largest k th power less than or equal to f(n) , and (ii) the distance between f(n) and the smallest k th power greater than or equal to f(n) .



Remark 2.1.



If k=2 , then also \Delta_{f,2}(n)=\left|f(n)-\left\lfloor\sqrt{f(n)}\,\right\rceil^{2}\right| , where \lfloor m\rceil represents the closest integer function applied to m . However this simplification does not generalize to k>2 . For example, if f(n)=4 for some fixed n , then \Delta_{f,3}(n)=3 , but \left|f(n)-\left\lfloor\sqrt[3]{f(n)}\right\rceil^{3}\right|=4 .





Since 1 is a k th power for all k>1 , we have \Delta_{f,k}(n)\leq|f(n)-1^{k}|=f(n)-1 for all k,n . We show in the following proposition that \{\Delta_{f,k}(n)\}_{k=1}^{\infty} in fact always stabilizes at that value.



Proposition 2.2.



The sequence \{\Delta_{f,k}(n)\}_{k=1}^{\infty} converges to f(n)-1 .




Proof.



Fix n\in\mathbb{N} . First note that \Delta_{f,k}(n) is defined for all k\geq 1 by well-ordering. Find K\in\mathbb{N} such that f(n)\leq 2^{K-1} . Then for all k\geq K , 2f(n)<2^{K}+1\leq 2^{k}+1 , so f(n)-1<2^{k}-f(n) , and hence 1 is the closest k th power to f(n) . Thus \Delta_{k}(n)=f(n)-1 for all k\geq K .
∎





Now fixing k>1 , we define



(11)

 M_{f,k}(d):=\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\Delta_{f,k}(n)\leq d\} 



if such an n exists, and \infty otherwise. We note that Conjecture B is equivalent to the statement that M_{f,k}(d) is finite for all k>1 and d\geq 0 .




In order for \{\Delta_{f,k}(n)\}_{n=1}^{\infty} to tend to \infty , it is necessary that M_{f,k}(d) is finite for all d\geq 0 . Conversely, if M_{f,k}(d) is finite for all d\geq 0 , then \{M_{f,k}(d)\}_{d=0}^{\infty}\rightarrow\infty and furthermore is monotonically increasing, since if d\leq e , then any n satisfying \Delta_{k}(n)\leq d will also satisfy \Delta_{k}(n)\leq e .




If we instead fix d and let k grow, we determine that if the sequence \{M_{f,k}(d)\}_{k=2}^{\infty} is bounded, then the limiting behavior of M_{f,k}(d) matches that in Conjecture E. Generalizing (6), define



(12)

 L_{f}(d)=\max\{n:f(n)\leq d+1\} 



if such an n exists, and \infty otherwise. From the definition we observe that L_{f}(d) is monotonically increasing in d .



Proposition 2.3.



If the sequence \{M_{f,k}(d)\}_{k=2}^{\infty} is bounded, then




 \lim_{k\to\infty}M_{f,k}(d)=L_{f}(d). 






Proof.



Since f(n) grows with n , L_{f}(d) must be finite. We first show that M_{f,k}(d)\geq L_{f}(d) for all k . Note that we have f(L_{f}(d))-1\leq d . Now for any k\geq 2 , as 1 is clearly a k th power, this implies \Delta_{f,k}(L_{f}(d))\leq f(L_{f}(d))-1\leq d . and therefore by definition M_{f,k}(d)\geq L_{f}(d) for all k\geq 2 .




Now let A be the smallest integer such that M_{f,k}(d)\leq A for all sufficiently large k . Note that A exists as \{M_{f,k}(d)\}_{k=1}^{\infty} bounded (by assumption), and A\geq L_{f}(d) (as M_{f,k}(d)\geq L_{f}(d) for all k ). We shall prove that A=L_{f}(d) .




Let us assume for contradiction that A>L_{f}(d) . By definition of A , there are infinitely many integers k such that M_{f,k}(d)=A . In particular, we can choose some K sufficiently large such that M_{f,K}(d)=A and 2^{K}>f(A)+d . We now claim that \Delta_{f,K}(A)>d . As A>L_{f}(d) , this implies f(A)>1+d , and as 2^{K}>f(A)+d , this implies that |f(A)-m^{K}|>d for all m\in\mathbb{Z} . Therefore




 \Delta_{f,K}(A):=\min\{|f(A)-m^{K}|:m\in\mathbb{Z}\}>d, 



but, M_{f,K}(d)=A also implies that \Delta_{f,K}(A)\leq d , which is a contradiction!




Thus A=L_{f}(d) , which implies M_{f,k}(d)=L_{f}(d) for all sufficiently large k . Thus \lim_{k\to\infty}M_{f,k}(d) exists and equals L_{f}(d)=\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}:f(n)\leq d+1\} .
∎





Progress toward bounding \{M_{f,k}(d)\}_{k\geq 2} in general is given by the following result.



Proposition 2.4.



Let f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N} and fix d\geq 0 and k\in\mathbb{N} . Then M_{f,k}(d)\geq M_{f,jk}(d) for all j\in\mathbb{N} .




Proof.



Let j\in\mathbb{N} and suppose that M_{f,jk}(d)=n . Then for some m\in\mathbb{Z} we have |f(n)-m^{jk}|\leq d . So, also, |f(n)-(m^{j})^{k}|\leq d , hence M_{f,k}(d)\geq n=M_{f,jk}(d) .
∎





By Proposition 2.4, it is sufficient to bound \{M_{f,p_{j}}(d)\}_{j\geq 1} for all primes p_{j}\in\mathbb{N} in order to show that \{M_{f,k}(d)\}_{k\geq 2} is bounded.



Remark 2.5.



For the partition function p(n) , our computations suggest that M_{2}(d)\geq M_{p}(d) for all primes p . If this could be proved, then Conjecture A would follow by proving that M_{2}(0)=1 . One possible approach to proving that M_{2}(d) is an upper bound would be to obtain sufficiently precise asymptotics for the growth of M_{k}(d) as d\rightarrow\infty for each fixed k .




Remark 2.6.



We note there are sequences where, for some fixed d\geq 0 , M_{f,k}(d) is finite for all k\geq 2 , but the sequence \{M_{f,k}(d)\}_{k=2}^{\infty} is unbounded. For example, consider the sequence f(n)=2^{p_{n}} where p_{n} is the n -th prime. Then, for any n\geq 1 , we have M_{f,p_{n}}(0)=n . Furthermore, by Mihăilescu’s proof of the Catalan conjecture [Ma04], we know that the only two consecutive perfect powers are 8 and 9, and thus we have that M_{f,k}(1) is finite for all k\geq 2 .




More generally, Pillai’s conjecture would imply that, given any d\geq 0 , there are only finitely many pairs of perfect powers whose difference is at most d (e.g. see [Guy04, Problem D9]). This would therefore imply that M_{f,k}(d) is finite for all d and for all k .






3. Bounds for \Delta_{f,k}(n) and M_{f,k}(d) 




In this section we prove upper bounds for \Delta_{f,k}(n) and lower bounds for M_{f,k}(d) for fixed k as d\to\infty .



Lemma 3.1.



Fix k\geq 2 . For n\geq 1 , we have the upper bound




 \Delta_{f,k}(n)\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\binom{k}{i}f(n)^{(k-i)/k}. 



Then for d\geq 1 , we have the lower bound




 M_{f,k}(d)\geq\max\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}\;\middle|\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\binom{k}{i}f(n)^{(k-i)/k}\leq d\right\}. 






Proof.



Note that by (10), \Delta_{f,k}(n) can be expressed as the distance from f(n) to either \left\lfloor\sqrt[k]{f(n)}\right\rfloor^{k} or \left\lceil\sqrt[k]{f(n)}\right\rceil^{k} , whichever is closer to f(n) . Therefore, by taking half of the distance between \left\lfloor\sqrt[k]{f(n)}\right\rfloor^{k} and \left\lceil\sqrt[k]{f(n)}\right\rceil^{k} , this gives us the following upper bound for \Delta_{f,k}(n) :




 \displaystyle\Delta_{f,k}(n)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\lceil\sqrt[k]{f(n)}\right\rceil^{k}-\left\lfloor\sqrt[k]{f(n)}\right\rfloor^{k}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\left\lfloor\sqrt[k]{f(n)}\right\rfloor+1\right)^{k}-\left\lfloor\sqrt[k]{f(n)}\right\rfloor^{k}\right). 



Now as (x+1)^{k}-x^{k} is an increasing sequence in x , by an application of the binomial theorem, we have




 \displaystyle\Delta_{f,k}(n)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\sqrt[k]{f(n)}+1\right)^{k}-\sqrt[k]{f(n)}^{k}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\binom{k}{i}f(n)^{(k-i)/k}. 



Then by the definition of M_{f,k}(d) , the given lower bound follows.
∎





For sufficiently large n , we can simplify the bound for \Delta_{f,k}(n) in Lemma 3.1 as follows.



Proposition 3.2.



Let k\geq 2 and let \varepsilon>0 . Then for all sufficiently large n we have




 \Delta_{f,k}(n)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{k}{2}f(n)^{(k-1)/k}. 






Proof.



This follows by expanding the binomial sum \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\binom{k}{i}f(n)^{(k-i)/k} in Lemma 3.1 to obtain




 \frac{k}{2}f(n)^{(k-1)/k}+\frac{k(k-1)}{4}f(n)^{(k-2)/k}+\dots+\frac{k}{2}f(n)^{1/k}+\frac{1}{2}, 



which has leading term \frac{k}{2}f(n)^{(k-1)/k} . Then since f(n)\to\infty we have that



(13)

 \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\binom{k}{i}f(n)^{(k-i)/k}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{k}{2}f(n)^{(k-1)/k} 



for all sufficiently large n .
∎





For sufficiently large d we have a corresponding lower bound for M_{f,k}(d) .



Proposition 3.3.



Let k\geq 2 and let 0<\varepsilon<1 . Then, for all sufficiently large d , we have




 M_{f,k}(d)\geq\max\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}\;\middle|\;f(n)\leq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\left(\frac{2d}{k}\right)^{k/(k-1)}\right\}. 






Proof.



Let \varepsilon^{\prime}:=(1/(1-\varepsilon))^{k/(k-1)}-1 , and choose d large enough that the largest n satisfying \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\binom{k}{i}f(n)^{(k-i)/k}\leq d also satisfies the inequality (13) with \varepsilon^{\prime} . Then by Lemma 3.1, for all such d we have




 \displaystyle M_{f,k}(d) 
 \displaystyle\geq\max\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}\;\middle|\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\binom{k}{i}f(n)^{(k-i)/k}\leq d\right\} 




 \displaystyle\geq\max\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}\;\middle|\;(1+\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\frac{k}{2}f(n)^{(k-1)/k}\leq d\right\} 




 \displaystyle=\max\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}\;\middle|\;f(n)\leq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\left(\frac{2d}{k}\right)^{k/(k-1)}\right\}. 



∎





In cases where f(n) grows asymptotically in a similar way to p(n) , we can make the lower bound in Proposition 3.3 explicit.



Theorem 3.4.



Let f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N} be a function such that f(n)\sim an^{-b}\exp(\alpha n^{c}) for some constants a>0,b\in\mathbb{R},c>0 and \alpha>0 . Then for any fixed k\geq 2 , we have




 M_{f,k}(d)\geq\left(\frac{k}{\alpha(k-1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{c}}(\log d)^{\frac{1}{c}}\left(1+\frac{O(\log\log d)}{\log d}\right). 






Proof.



Fix k\geq 2 and \varepsilon\in(0,1) . By Proposition 3.3, a lower bound for M_{f,k}(d) is given by the the largest n such that



(14)

 f(n)\leq(1-\varepsilon)\left(\frac{2d}{k}\right)^{\frac{k}{(k-1)}}. 



Since f(n)\sim an^{-b}\exp(\alpha n^{c}) , then for any fixed \eta\in(0,1) there exists N_{\eta} such that for all n\geq N_{\eta} ,




 f(n)\leq(1+\eta)an^{-b}\exp({\alpha n^{c}}). 



Thus we guarantee (14) by requiring




 (1+\eta)an^{-b}\exp({\alpha n^{c}})\leq(1-\varepsilon)\left(\frac{2d}{k}\right)^{k/(k-1)}, 



or equivalently




 n^{-b}\exp({\alpha n^{c}})\leq C_{0}d^{k/(k-1)} 



for some constant C_{0}>0 . Taking logarithms yields



(15)

 \alpha n^{c}\leq\frac{k}{k-1}\log d+b\log n+O(1). 



Now we solve this inequality asymptotically as d\to\infty by proposing that n has the form




 n=\left(\frac{k}{\alpha(k-1)}\right)^{1/c}(\log d)^{1/c}(1+\beta_{d}) 



where \beta_{d}=o(1) is a function to be chosen later. We have




 \alpha n^{c}=\frac{k}{k-1}(\log d)(1+c\beta_{d}+O(\beta_{d}^{2}))=\frac{k}{k-1}\log d+\frac{k}{k-1}c\beta_{d}\log d+O(\beta_{d}^{2}\log d), 



and




 b\log n=\frac{b}{c}\log\log d+O(1)+O(\beta_{d}). 



Substituting these values in (15), we have




 \frac{k}{k-1}c\beta_{d}\log d\leq\frac{b}{c}\log\log d+O(1)+O(\beta_{d})+O(\beta_{d}^{2}\log d). 



Now we see that we can choose \beta_{d}=C_{1}\frac{\log\log d}{\log d} for some constant C_{1}>0 so that \beta_{d} and \beta_{d}^{2}\log d are each o(\log\log d) and the inequality is satisfied for sufficiently large d .
∎





In particular, applying Theorem 3.4 to f(n)=p(n) and using the Hardy-Ramanujan asymptotic proves Theorem 1.1.





4. Lower bounds for N_{f,d} 




In this section we consider f such that for any d ,



(16)

 \{M_{f,k}(d)\}_{k=2}^{\infty}\rightarrow L_{f}(d), 



where L_{f}(d) is as defined in (12). In particular, L_{f}(d) is monotonically increasing in d . Note that if f(n)\rightarrow\infty as n\rightarrow\infty , then it follows that L_{f}(d)\rightarrow\infty as d\rightarrow\infty .




Under hypothesis (16), we define the constant N_{f,d} by



(17)

 N_{f,d}=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}:M_{f,k}(d)=L_{f}(d)\text{ for all }k\geq n\}, 



i.e., N_{f,d} is the smallest value of k past which \{M_{f,k}(d)\}_{k=1}^{\infty} is stable at L_{f}(d) .




Merca–Ono–Tsai [MOT25, p. 104] observed that, for the partition function p(n) , the value of N_{d} appears to grow like O(\log{d}) . In the following, we prove a logarithmic lower bound for functions f(n) with sufficiently controlled growth.



Theorem 4.1.



If f(n) satisfies (16) and f(n+1)<\frac{3}{2}f(n) for all sufficiently large n , then




 N_{f,d}\geq\lfloor\log_{2}(d)\rfloor+1 



for all sufficiently large d .




Proof.



Since f(n)\rightarrow\infty we have L_{f}(d)\rightarrow\infty as d\rightarrow\infty . Thus for sufficiently large d , we have



(18)

 f(L_{f}(d)+1)<\frac{3}{2}f(L_{f}(d))\leq\frac{3}{2}(d+1) 



by our hypothesis. Since \{M_{f,k}(d)\}_{k=2}^{\infty}\rightarrow L_{f}(d) , to prove the claim it suffices to show that M_{f,k}(d)>L_{f}(d) for k=\left\lfloor\log_{2}(d)\right\rfloor .




By definition of L_{f}(d) , it follows that f(L_{f}(d)+1)>d+1 . Fixing k=\left\lfloor\log_{2}(d)\right\rfloor , we have d\geq 2^{k} , and therefore f(L_{f}(d)+1)-2^{k}>0 . We also have that \frac{d}{2}<2^{k} , and by considering the parity of d it follows that \frac{d+1}{2}\leq 2^{k} . Therefore from (18) we have




 0<f(L_{f}(d)+1)-2^{k}<\frac{3}{2}(d+1)-\frac{d+1}{2}=d+1. 



It follows that |f(L_{f}(d)+1)-2^{k}|<d+1 , and so |f(L_{f}(d)+1)-2^{k}|\leq d . Hence by (9), \Delta_{f,k}(L_{f}(d)+1)\leq d , which implies M_{f,k}(d)>L_{f}(d) . This proves the claim.




∎





If f(n) has the property that f(n)\sim an^{-b}\exp(\alpha n^{c}) for \alpha>0 and c\in(0,1) , then f satisfies the assumptions of Theorem 4.1. In this case we prove a more refined bound for N_{f,d} .



Theorem 4.2.



Suppose f(n) satisfies (16) and also that f(n)\sim an^{-b}\exp(\alpha n^{c}) 
as n\to\infty , for some \alpha>0 and c\in(0,1) .
Then for any constant 0<A<(1-c)/(c\log 2) , we have




 N_{f,d}>\log_{2}(d)+A\log\log d 



for all sufficiently large d .




Proof.



From our hypotheses we have that for every \epsilon>0 , there exists N_{\epsilon} such that for all n\geq N_{\epsilon} ,




 (\alpha-\epsilon)n^{c}\leq\log f(n)\leq(\alpha+\epsilon)n^{c}. 



Thus for n\geq N_{\epsilon} ,



(19)

 \exp((\alpha-\epsilon)n^{c})\leq f(n)\leq\exp((\alpha+\epsilon)n^{c}). 



Fix \epsilon\in(0,\alpha) . Since f(n)\rightarrow\infty we have L_{f}(d)\rightarrow\infty , so for sufficiently large d we ensure that L_{d}\geq N_{\epsilon} . Using (19) and the fact that f(L_{f}(d))\leq d+1 by definition, we have




 \exp((\alpha-\epsilon)L_{f}(d)^{c})\leq f(L_{f}(d))\leq d+1. 



Thus,




 L_{f}(d)^{c}\leq\frac{\log(d+1)}{\alpha-\epsilon}, 



and setting C_{2}=\left(\frac{1}{\alpha-\epsilon}\right)^{1/c} , we have for sufficiently large d ,



(20)

 L_{f}(d)\leq C_{2}(\log d)^{1/c}. 



Further set C_{1}=\left(\frac{1}{\alpha+\epsilon}\right)^{1/c} . Considering n_{0}=C_{1}(\log(d+1))^{1/c} , (19) implies




 \log f(n_{0})\leq(\alpha+\epsilon)n_{0}^{c}\leq\log(d+1), 



and thus f(n_{0})\leq d+1 and n_{0}\leq L_{f}(d) . Hence, L_{f}(d)\geq C_{1}(\log(d+1))^{1/c} , and with (20) we obtain that for sufficiently large d ,



(21)

 C_{1}(\log d)^{1/c}<L_{f}(d)\leq C_{2}(\log d)^{1/c}. 






We now derive an asymptotic for the difference g(n):=f(n+1)-f(n) . By the Maclaurin series expansion for e^{-x} , we note that we have the asymptotic 1-e^{-x}\sim x as x\to 0 . Following a similar strategy as done in [AGH+23, Theorem 1], the asymptotic for the ratio g(n)/f(n) is given by




 \frac{g(n)}{f(n)}=\frac{f(n+1)}{f(n)}-1\sim\exp{\left(b\log\Big(\frac{n}{n+1}\Big)+\alpha\left((n+1)^{c}-n^{c}\right)\right)}-1. 



Since 1-e^{x}\sim-x\text{ as }x\to 0 , we obtain \tfrac{g(n)}{f(n)}\sim\alpha cn^{c-1} , using that facts that \log\!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{n+1}\bigr)=-\tfrac{1}{n}+O(n^{-2}) , and (n+1)^{c}-n^{c}=cn^{c-1}+O(n^{c-2}) .
Therefore, we have the asymptotic



(22)

 g(n)\sim f(n)\alpha cn^{c-1} 



as n\to\infty .




Using (22), there exists N_{1}>0 such that for all n\geq N_{1} , we have



(23)

 B_{1}f(n)n^{c-1}\leq f(n+1)-f(n)\leq B_{2}f(n)n^{c-1}, 



for some two positive constants B_{1},B_{2}>0 .




For d large enough to ensure L_{f}(d)\geq\max\{N_{\epsilon},N_{1}\} , define



(24)

 n_{d}:=\max\{n\geq L_{f}(d):f(j+1)-f(j)\leq 2d\text{ for all }L_{f}(d)\leq j\leq n\}. 



Using (21) and (23), it follows that the above set is non-empty at j=L_{f}(d) for sufficiently large d . Moreover, n_{d} is finite since (19) and (23) together imply that f(n+1)-f(n)\rightarrow\infty as n\rightarrow\infty . By the maximality of n_{d} , we have that f(n_{d}+2)-f(n_{d}+1)>2d , and for any L_{f,d}\leq n\leq n_{d} ,



(25)

 f(n+1)-f(n)\leq 2d. 



Note that (25) implies that any real y\in[f(L_{f}(d)+1),f(n_{d}+1)] can be at most d away from the nearest f(j) value. In particular, for any such y there exists an integer s_{y}\in[L_{f}(d)+1,n_{d}+1] such that



(26)

 |f(s_{y})-y|\leq d. 






Moreover, since f(n_{d}+2)-f(n_{d}+1)>2d , using (23) gives




 2d<f(n_{d}+2)-f(n_{d}+1)\leq B_{2}f(n_{d}+1)(n_{d}+1)^{c-1}. 



This implies that



(27)

 f(n_{d}+1)>\frac{2d}{B_{2}}(n_{d}+1)^{1-c}, 



and thus



(28)

 \log f(n_{d}+1)>\log d+(1-c)\log(n_{d}+1)+O(1). 



Since n_{d}\geq L_{f}(d) , (21) implies that for d sufficiently large, n_{d}>C_{1}(\log d)^{1/c} . Therefore,



(29)

 \log(n_{d}+1)>\log C_{1}+c^{-1}\log\log d. 



Substituting the value of (29) in (28), we obtain that




 \log f(n_{d}+1)>\log d+\frac{1-c}{c}\log\log d+O(1). 



Therefore, we have



(30)

 \log_{2}f(n_{d}+1)=\frac{\log f(n_{d}+1)}{\log 2}>\log_{2}d+\frac{1-c}{c\log 2}\log\log d+O(1). 



We define



(31)

 k_{d}:=\lfloor\log_{2}f(n_{d}+1)\rfloor, 



which immediately gives



(32)

 2^{k_{d}}\leq f(n_{d}+1)<2^{k_{d}+1}. 



From (30) we observe that for any fixed 0<A<\frac{1-c}{c\log 2} , we have for all sufficiently large d ,



(33)

 \log_{2}f(n_{d}+1)>\log_{2}d+A\log\log d. 



From (31) and (33), we deduce that k_{d}\geq\lfloor\log_{2}d+A\log\log d\rfloor for all sufficiently large d . Further, since \frac{f(n_{d}+1)}{f(L_{f}(d)+1)}\rightarrow\infty as d\rightarrow\infty ,
we have f(n_{d}+1)\geq 2f(L_{f}(d)+1) for sufficiently large d . With (32) this gives




 f(L_{f}(d)+1)\leq\frac{1}{2}f(n_{d}+1)<2^{k_{d}}\leq f(n_{d}+1). 






Thus by (26), there exists an integer s with L_{f}(d)+1\leq s\leq n_{d}+1 such that |f(s)-2^{k_{d}}|\leq d . Thus with (9) and (11) we have




 M_{f,k_{d}}\geq s>L_{d}. 



From (17), we conclude that N_{f,d}>k_{d} . Thus using (31) and (33), we obtain




 N_{f,d}\geq k_{d}+1>\log_{2}f(n_{d}+1)\geq\log_{2}d+A\log\log d 



for sufficiently large d .
∎





Since (2) guarantees that p(n) satisfies the growth condition in the hypotheses of Theorem 4.2, then Proposition 2.3 and Theorem 4.2 together prove Theorem 1.2.





5. Equidistribution




Here we present strong supporting evidence for the following conjecture. Let \{r\} denote the fractional part of a real number r .



Conjecture 5.1.



For each fixed integer k\geq 2 , the sequence of the fractional parts of p(n)^{1/k} ,




 \{\{\sqrt[k]{p(n)}\}\}_{n\geq 1}, 



is equidistributed in [0,1) .





We have computed the sequence for 1\leq n\leq N with N=5000 and N=500,000 and tested equidistribution using several complementary diagnostics: histograms, empirical distribution functions, and discrepancy estimates.







(a) k=2 , n\leq 5000 



(b) k=2 , n\leq 500\,000 




(c) k=3 , n\leq 5000 



(d) k=3 , n\leq 500\,000 




(e) k=4 , n\leq 5000 



(f) k=4 , n\leq 500\,000

Figure 1 shows histograms of fractional parts of p(n)^{1/k} for k=2,3,4 and n\leq N , with N=5000 and N=500,000 .
We notice, as N increases, the histograms become progressively flatter, suggesting that the distribution of fractional parts stabilizes toward equidistribution.
While histogram-based diagnostics depend on the choice of subintervals, we observed this stabilization for increasing N across a wide range of sizes of subintervals, providing consistent numerical evidence for equidistribution.




To remove this dependence and to obtain a more quantitative picture, we next plot the empirical distribution function (EDF) of the fractional parts of p(n)^{1/k} , given by




 F_{N}(x)=\frac{\#\{n\leq N\ |\ \{p(n)^{1/k}\}\leq x\}}{N}, 



against the diagonal corresponding to the uniform distribution on [0,1) .







(a) n\leq 5000 



(b) n\leq 500\,000

Figure 2 compares the empirical distribution functions of the fractional parts of p(n)^{1/k} for k=2,3,4 and n\leq N with N=5000 and N=500,000 with the uniform distribution.
We observe that the empirical distribution functions closely track the diagonal y=x , and the deviations appear to decrease significantly as N increases.




We next compute the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic




 D_{N}=\max_{x\in[0,1)}|F_{N}(x)-x|. 



This discrepancy measures the maximal deviation of the EDF from the uniform distribution and provides a quantitative complement to the visual EDF plots.

Taken together, the histogram plots, empirical distribution functions, and discrepancy statistics provide strong numerical evidence in support of Conjecture 5.1. We emphasize that these plots are purely experimental and do not constitute proof.





6. A probabilistic model




Throughout Sections 6 and 7, all random variables are written in boldface (e.g. \mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C} ). We use \mathbb{P}E to denote the probability that some event E occurs, and \mathbb{E}\mathbf{X} to denote the expected value of some random variable \mathbf{X} . We shall also use Vinogradov’s notation f\ll g to mean f=O(g) .




6.1. Setup of the heuristic model




Here we give a precise description of our probabilistic model, motivated by the evidence for Conjecture 5.1 given in Section 5. Let A:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{+} be a positive real-valued function on the natural numbers \mathbb{N} .
We impose the growth condition that A(n)\to\infty as n\to\infty , and moreover that \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}A(n)^{-\frac{1}{2}} converges. To model f(n) we will set A(n) to be the main term of the asymptotic for f(n) .




Let \{\varepsilon_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} be a sequence of positive real numbers such that \varepsilon_{n}\to 0 as n\to\infty . To control the rate at which \varepsilon_{n} converges to 0, we also assume that as n\to\infty ,






(34)

 \varepsilon_{n}\gg\frac{\log A(n)}{\sqrt{A(n)}}. 






Let \{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} be a sequence of discrete random variables defined so that for each n , \mathbf{f}_{n} takes a uniform distribution among the set



(35)

 \mathcal{S}_{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{N}\mid A(n)(1-\varepsilon_{n})\leq x\leq A(n)(1+\varepsilon_{n})\}. 



We note that



(36)

 2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)-1\leq\#{\mathcal{S}_{n}}\leq 2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)+1. 






Now we explicitly give the probability mass function (PMF) for each \mathbf{f}_{n} . For any integer m\in\mathbb{Z} , we have




 \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{f}_{n}=m)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\#{\mathcal{S}_{n}}}&\text{if }m\in\mathcal{S}_{n},\\
0&\text{otherwise. }\end{cases} 






We note that any possible sequence of outcomes from \{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} will yield a function f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N} such that f(n)\sim A(n) as n\to\infty . Conversely, any such function satisfying f(n)\sim A(n) can arise as a possible sequence of outcomes from \{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} , for some suitable choice of the errors \{\varepsilon_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} .




We can now define the random variable




 \mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n):=\min\big\{|\mathbf{f}_{n}-m^{k}|:m\in\mathbb{Z}\big\} 



analogously to \Delta_{f,k}(n) .




For a fixed k\geq 2 , we compute the probability that \mathbf{f}_{n} is within d of a k th power.



Lemma 6.1.



Let \{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} be a sequence of random variables as defined in (35). Fix some integers k\geq 2 and d\geq 0 . Then there exists some constant B>0 (independent of k and d ), such that for all n>B we have the upper bound




 \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d)\leq\frac{2d+1}{kA(n)^{1-\frac{1}{k}}}+\frac{6d+3}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}+\frac{(8d+4)\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}{A(n)^{1-1/k}}. 



Furthermore, there exists a constant B_{d}>0 (possibly depending on d ), such that for all n>B_{d} , we have the lower bound




 \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d)\geq\frac{2d+1}{kA(n)^{1-\frac{1}{k}}}-\frac{10d+3}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}. 






Proof.



Fix some integers k\geq 2 and d\geq 0 . We note that




 \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d)=\frac{\#{\{x\in\mathcal{S}_{n}:x\text{ is within $d$ of a $k$th power}\}}}{\#{\mathcal{S}_{n}}}. 



Recall from (36) that for each n ,




 2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)-1\leq\#{\mathcal{S}_{n}}\leq 2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)+1. 



We now wish to count the number of k th powers in the set \mathcal{S}_{n} . For k\geq 2 set




 N_{k}(n):=\#{\{m\in\mathcal{S}_{n}:m=t^{k}\ \text{for some}\ t\in\mathbb{N}\}}. 



The total number of k th powers up to x is \lfloor x^{1/k}\rfloor . Therefore, this gives the following lower and upper bounds for N_{k}(n) ,




 (A(n)(1+\varepsilon_{n}))^{1/k}-(A(n)(1-\varepsilon_{n}))^{1/k}-1\leq N_{k}(n)\leq\\
(A(n)(1+\varepsilon_{n}))^{1/k}-(A(n)(1-\varepsilon_{n}))^{1/k}+1. 






Recall that, for any x\in\mathbb{R} such that |x|<1 , we have the generalised binomial expansion




 \displaystyle(1+x)^{1/k}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\binom{1/k}{i}x^{i} 



where \binom{1/k}{i}=\frac{1/k(1/k-1)\cdots(1/k-i+1)}{i!} is the generalised binomial coefficient. Thus, if |\varepsilon_{n}|\leq 1/2 , we have that




 \displaystyle(1+\varepsilon_{n})^{1/k}-(1-\varepsilon_{n})^{1/k}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\binom{1/k}{i}\varepsilon_{n}^{i}-\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\binom{1/k}{i}(-\varepsilon_{n})^{i}=\frac{2\varepsilon_{n}}{k}+2\varepsilon_{n}^{3}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\binom{1/k}{2i+3}\varepsilon_{n}^{2i}. 



Since the binomial coefficients satisfy |\binom{1/k}{i}|\leq 1 and given that |\varepsilon_{n}|\leq 1/2 , this gives us that




 \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\binom{1/k}{2i+3}\varepsilon_{n}^{2i}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{2i}}<2, 



which proves that




 \frac{2\varepsilon_{n}}{k}<(1+\varepsilon_{n})^{1/k}-(1-\varepsilon_{n})^{1/k}<\frac{2\varepsilon_{n}}{k}+4\varepsilon_{n}^{3}. 






Thus, for all n such that |\varepsilon_{n}|\leq 1/2 , we have the following lower and upper bounds for N_{k}(n) ,




 \displaystyle\frac{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)^{1/k}}{k}-1<N_{k}(n)<\frac{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)^{1/k}}{k}+4\varepsilon_{n}^{3}A(n)^{1/k}+1. 



We now note that the number of integers x\in\mathcal{S}_{n} which are at most d away from a k th power is at most (2d+1)N_{k}(n) . Thus we have




 \#{\{x\in\mathcal{S}_{n}:x\text{ is within $d$ of a $k$th power }\}}\leq(2d+1)N_{k}(n). 



This therefore gives the upper bound




 \displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d) 
 \displaystyle=\frac{\#{\{x\in\mathcal{S}_{n}:x\text{ is within $d$ of a $k$th power}\}}}{\#{\mathcal{S}_{n}}}\leq\frac{(2d+1)N_{k}(n)}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)-1} 




 \displaystyle\leq\frac{(2d+1)(2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)^{1/k}/k+4\varepsilon_{n}^{3}A(n)^{1/k}+1)}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}\cdot\frac{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)-1} 




 \displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{(2d+1)}{kA(n)^{1-1/k}}+\frac{(2d+1)}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}+\frac{(4d+2)\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}{A(n)^{1-1/k}}\right)\cdot\left(1+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)-1}\right). 



As \varepsilon_{n}A(n)\gg\sqrt{A(n)} , then for n sufficiently large, we have that 2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)-1\geq\varepsilon_{n}A(n) . This gives us the upper bound




 \displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d) 
 \displaystyle\leq\frac{(2d+1)}{kA(n)^{1-1/k}}+\frac{(2d+1)}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}+\frac{(4d+2)\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}{A(n)^{1-1/k}} 




 \displaystyle\quad+\frac{(2d+1)}{k\varepsilon_{n}A(n)^{2-1/k}}+\frac{(2d+1)}{2\varepsilon_{n}^{2}A(n)^{2}}+\frac{(4d+2)\varepsilon_{n}}{A(n)^{2-1/k}}. 



If we now choose n sufficiently large such that |A(n)|>1 and |\varepsilon_{n}A(n)|>1 , then we can combine the fourth and fifth terms above into the second term, and the sixth term above into the third term. This gives us the upper bound




 \displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d)\leq\frac{2d+1}{kA(n)^{1-1/k}}+\frac{6d+3}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}+\frac{(8d+4)\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}{A(n)^{1-1/k}} 



for all n sufficiently larger than some absolute constant B , not depending on d or k .




To obtain a lower bound for \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d) we assume that n is sufficiently large such that \sqrt{A(n)}>10(2d+1) and \varepsilon_{n}\leq 1/2 . In particular, this implies that we have



(37)

 \left\lceil(A(n)(1-\varepsilon_{n}))^{1/k}\right\rceil^{k}-\left\lceil(A(n)(1-\varepsilon_{n}))^{1/k}-1\right\rceil^{k}\geq 2d+1. 



This implies that every k th power in \mathcal{S}_{n} will be at least 2d away from another k th power. This therefore gives the lower bound




 \displaystyle(2d+1)N_{k}(n)-2d\leq 
 \displaystyle\#{\{x\in\mathcal{S}_{n}:x\text{ is within $d$ of a $k$th power }\}}. 



Similarly, we thus obtain a lower bound for \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d) ,




 \displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d) 
 \displaystyle=\frac{\#{\{x\in\mathcal{S}_{n}:x\text{ is within $d$ of a $k$th power}\}}}{\#{\mathcal{S}_{n}}}\geq\frac{(2d+1)N_{k}(n)-2d}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)+1} 




 \displaystyle\geq\frac{(2d+1)(2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)^{1/k}/k-1)-2d}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}\cdot\frac{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)+1} 




 \displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{(2d+1)}{kA(n)^{1-1/k}}-\frac{(4d+1)}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}\right)\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)+1}\right). 



Again, as 2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)+1\geq\varepsilon_{n}A(n) , we have that




 \displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d) 
 \displaystyle\geq\frac{(2d+1)}{kA(n)^{1-1/k}}-\frac{(4d+1)}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}-\frac{(2d+1)}{k\varepsilon_{n}A(n)^{2-1/k}}-\frac{(4d+1)}{2\varepsilon_{n}^{2}A(n)^{2}}. 



By similarly assuming that |\varepsilon_{n}A(n)|>1 and absorbing the second and fourth terms into the second, we obtain the lower bound




 \displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d) 
 \displaystyle\geq\frac{2d+1}{kA(n)^{1-1/k}}-\frac{10d+3}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)} 



for all n larger than B and such that condition (37) holds. This proves the lemma.
∎





By thus considering the asymptotic limit as n\to\infty in Lemma 6.1, we obtain the following corollary.



Corollary 6.2.



Let \{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} be a sequence of random variables as defined in (35). Fix some integers k\geq 2 and d\geq 0 . Then




 \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d)=\frac{2d+1}{kA(n)^{1-\frac{1}{k}}}+O_{d}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}+\frac{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}{A(n)^{1-\frac{1}{k}}}\right) 



as n\to\infty . In particular, in the case d=0 , we get




 \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{f}_{n}\text{is a $k$th power})=\frac{1}{kA(n)^{1-\frac{1}{k}}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}+\frac{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}{A(n)^{1-\frac{1}{k}}}\right) 



as n\to\infty .





We can also compute the probability that \mathbf{f}_{n} is within d of any perfect power.



Lemma 6.3.



Let \{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} be a sequence of random variables as defined in (35). Fix some integer d\geq 0 . Then there exists some constant A_{d}>0 (possibly depending on d ) such that, for all n>A_{d} , we have the upper bound



(38)

 \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d\text{ for some }k\geq 2)\\
\leq\frac{2d+1}{2\sqrt{A(n)}}+\frac{2d+1}{A(n)^{2/3}}+\frac{(8d+5)\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}{\sqrt{A(n)}}+\frac{(6d+3)\log_{2}A(n)}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}. 



Furthermore, there exists a constant B_{d}>0 (possibly depending on d ), such that for all n>B_{d} , we have the lower bound




 \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d\text{ for some }k\geq 2)\geq\frac{2d+1}{2\sqrt{A(n)}}-\frac{10d+3}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}. 






Proof.



To show an upper bound, we can apply the following union bound,




 \displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d\text{ for some }k\geq 2)\leq\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d). 






Now as \mathbf{f}_{n} attains a value between A(n)(1-\varepsilon_{n}) and A(n)(1+\varepsilon_{n}) , then if A(n)(1-\varepsilon_{n})>1+d and A(n)(1+\varepsilon_{n})<2^{k}-d , then \mathbf{f}_{n} cannot be within d to a k th power and thus \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d)=0 . Therefore, assuming n sufficiently large such that |\varepsilon_{n}|\leq 1/2 and A(n)/2>1+d , then we have




 \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d\text{ for some }k\geq 2)\leq\sum_{k=2}^{\left\lfloor\log_{2}(2A(n)+d)\right\rfloor}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d)\\
\leq\sum_{k=2}^{\left\lfloor\log_{2}(A(n)+d)\right\rfloor+1}\left(\frac{2d+1}{kA(n)^{1-\frac{1}{k}}}+\frac{6d+3}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}+\frac{(8d+4)\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}{A(n)^{1-1/k}}\right). 



By separating out the small terms for k=2 and k=3 , we obtain the lower bound




 \displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d 
 \displaystyle\text{ for some }k\geq 2)\leq\frac{2d+1}{2\sqrt{A(n)}}+\frac{2d+1}{3A(n)^{2/3}}+\frac{(2d+1)\log_{2}(A(n)+d)}{4A(n)^{3/4}} 




 \displaystyle\quad+\frac{(8d+4)\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}{\sqrt{A(n)}}+\frac{(8d+4)\log_{2}(A(n)+d)\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}{A(n)^{2/3}}+\frac{(6d+3)\log_{2}(A(n)+d)}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}. 



By taking n large enough so that A(n)^{1/12}>\log_{2}(A(n)+d) , we can give an upper bound of




 \displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d\text{ for some }k\geq 2) 
 \displaystyle\leq\frac{2d+1}{2\sqrt{A(n)}}+\frac{2d+1}{A(n)^{2/3}}+\frac{(8d+5)\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}{\sqrt{A(n)}}+\frac{(6d+3)\log_{2}A(n)}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}. 



To show the lower bound, we can simply consider the case for k=2 and apply the lower bound from Lemma 6.1,




 \displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d\text{ for some }k\geq 2)\geq\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},2}(n)\leq d) 
 \displaystyle\geq\frac{2d+1}{2\sqrt{A(n)}}-\frac{10d+3}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}. 



∎





Remark. One can also similarly prove bounds for \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d\text{ for some }k\geq 2) by noting that




 \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d\text{ for some }k\geq 2)=\frac{\#{\{x\in\mathcal{S}_{n}:x\text{ is within $d$ of a perfect power}\}}}{\#{\mathcal{S}_{n}}} 



and then obtaining estimate on the size of the numerator by using results of Nyblom [Nyb06, Nyb08] and Jakimczuk [Jak11] to count the number of perfect powers in the set \mathcal{S}_{n} .




By again considering the asymptotic limit as n\to\infty in Lemma 6.3, we also obtain the following corollary.



Corollary 6.4.



Let \{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} be a sequence of random variables as defined in (35). Fix some integer d\geq 0 . Then




 \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d\text{ for some }k\geq 2)=\frac{2d+1}{2\sqrt{A(n)}}+O_{d}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}{\sqrt{A(n)}}+\frac{1}{A(n)^{2/3}}+\frac{\log A(n)}{\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}\right) 



as n\to\infty . In particular, in the case d=0 , we get




 \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{f}_{n}\text{ is a perfect power})=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{A(n)}}+O\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}{\sqrt{A(n)}}+\frac{1}{A(n)^{2/3}}+\frac{\log A(n)}{\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}\right). 









7. Heuristics




In this section we use the probabilistic model given in Section 6 to prove general heuristic results which give the parts of Theorem 1.3 as special cases. As discussed in Remark 1.4, these provide heuristic proofs for generalizations of Conjectures B–E, as well as heuristic support for Sun’s conjecture (Conj. A), and possible generalizations.




When \{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} is a sequence of random variables as defined in (35), define the random variable




 \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{f},k}(d):=\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d\}, 



allowing \infty to be a possible outcome. The following result proves part (a) of Theorem 1.3 by setting \mathbf{f}_{n}=\mathbf{p}_{n} .



Lemma 7.1.



Fix d\geq 0 and k\geq 2 . For \{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} defined as in (35), we have that \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{f},k}(d) is finite with probability 1.




Proof.



Fix d\geq 0 and k\geq 2 . From (34) we observe that \epsilon_{n}A(n)\gg\sqrt{A(n)}\log A(n) . Then using Corollary 6.2, we have as n\to\infty ,




 \displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d) 
 \displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{A(n)}}. 



Therefore, from the assumption in §6.1 that \sum A(n)^{-1/2} converges, we have that \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d) converges. By the Borel-Cantelli Lemma, \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{f},k}(d) is thus finite with probability 1.
∎





Letting d=0 in Lemma 7.1, we obtain that \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{f},k}(0) is finite with probability 1. This yields the following corollary which proves part (b) of Theorem 1.3 by setting \mathbf{f}_{n}=\mathbf{p}_{n} .



Corollary 7.2.



If k\geq 2 , then \{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} as defined in (35) will take only finitely many values that are perfect k th powers with probability 1.





In order to consider all perfect powers instead of only k th powers, for \{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} as defined in (35), we define the random variable



(39)

 \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{f}}(d):=\max\{n:\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d\text{ for some }k\geq 2\}, 



allowing \infty to be a possible outcome.



Lemma 7.3.



For \{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} as defined in (35),
we have that \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{f}}(d) is finite for all d\geq 0 with probability 1.




Proof.



Fix some integer d\geq 0 . By Lemma 6.3, we note that by using the lower bound on \varepsilon_{n} , as n\to\infty ,




 \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d\text{ for some }k\geq 2)\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{A(n)}}+\frac{\log A(n)}{\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{A(n)}}. 



As in the proof of Lemma 7.1, using that A(n)\rightarrow\infty , we have that




 \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d\text{ for some }k\geq 2) 



converges. By the Borel-Cantelli Lemma, \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{f}}(d) is finite with probability 1.
∎





Setting d=0 in Lemma 7.3, we obtain the following corollary.



Corollary 7.4.



Any \{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} as defined in (35)
will take only finitely many values that are perfect powers with probability 1.





We note that this motivates the expectation that many partition functions, while perhaps not directly satisfying an analogue of Sun’s conjecture, as stated in Conjecture A, should at at least have only finitely many exceptions. In §7.2 we compute the expected number of perfect power values taken by several partition counting functions.

To obtain bounds on various probabilities involving such functions, we first establish the following lemma.



Lemma 7.5.



Let b,c,\beta be fixed real numbers with c,\beta>0 . Then for all sufficiently large x , we have




 \int_{x}^{\infty}t^{b}\exp(-ct^{\beta})\,dt\leq\frac{2}{\beta c}x^{b+1-\beta}\exp(-cx^{\beta}). 






Proof.



We make the substitution u=ct^{\beta} . Thus we get t=(u/c)^{1/\beta} , du=c\beta t^{\beta-1}\,dt , and dt=\beta^{-1}u^{-1}(u/c)^{1/\beta}du . Thus




 \int_{x}^{\infty}t^{b}\exp(-ct^{\beta})dt=\int_{cx^{\beta}}^{\infty}\beta^{-1}u^{-1}(u/c)^{(b+1)/\beta}\exp{(-u)}du\\
=\beta^{-1}c^{-(b+1)/\beta}\Gamma\left(\frac{b+1}{\beta},cx^{\beta}\right), 



where \Gamma(s,z) denotes the upper incomplete gamma function \int_{z}^{\infty}t^{s-1}e^{-t}dt . Now, given the well-known asymptotic \Gamma(s,z)/(z^{s-1}e^{-z})\to 1 as z\to\infty (e.g. see [Tem75]), we have that \Gamma(s,z)\leq 2z^{s-1}e^{-z} for all sufficiently large z . This gives the result.
∎





We now prove our main theorem for this section which proves part (c) of Theorem 1.3 by setting \mathbf{f}_{n}=\mathbf{p}_{n} .



Theorem 7.6.



Let A(n)=an^{-b}\exp(cn^{\beta}) with a,b,c,\beta>0 , set \{\varepsilon_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} a sequence of positive real numbers such that \varepsilon_{n}\gg A(n)^{-1/k} as n\to\infty , let \{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} be as defined in (35), and fix k\geq 2 . Then as d\to\infty ,




 \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{f},k}(d)<(2^{1/\beta}+\varepsilon)\left(\frac{k}{c(k-1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta}}(\log d)^{\frac{1}{\beta}}, 



with probability 1.




Proof.



Fix some \varepsilon>0 . For each d\geq 0 , let E_{d} denote the event that




 \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{f},k}(d)>(2^{1/\beta}+\varepsilon)\left(\frac{k}{c(k-1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta}}(\log d)^{\frac{1}{\beta}}. 



We shall show that \sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}E_{d} is finite, and thus by the Borel-Cantelli lemma, only finitely many of the events E_{d} occur, with probability 1.




For brevity, we shall write X_{d}:=(2^{1/\beta}+\varepsilon)\Big(\frac{k}{c(k-1)}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\beta}}(\log d)^{\frac{1}{\beta}} . Note that




 \displaystyle\mathbb{P}E_{d}=\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{f},k}(d)>X_{d}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{n=X_{d}}^{\infty}\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d\right)\leq\sum_{n=X_{d}}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d\right), 



where the last inequality follows from the union bound. By Lemma 6.1, there is an absolute constant C (depending only on the function A(n) and \varepsilon_{n} , and not on d or k ) such that for all n>C ,




 \mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d\right)\leq\frac{2d+1}{kA(n)^{1-\frac{1}{k}}}+\frac{6d+3}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}+\frac{(8d+4)\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}{A(n)^{1-1/k}}. 



We now consider some sufficiently large d satisfying X_{d}>C . Then using our hypothesis that \varepsilon_{n}\gg A(n)^{-1/k} , we obtain that




 \displaystyle\mathbb{P}E_{d}\leq\sum_{n=X_{d}}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d\right) 
 \displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=X_{d}}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2d+1}{kA(n)^{1-1/k}}+\frac{6d+3}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)}+\frac{(8d+4)\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}{A(n)^{1-1/k}}\right) 




 \displaystyle\leq 10d\sum_{n=X_{d}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{A(n)^{1-1/k}}\qquad(\text{as }\varepsilon_{n}\to 0) 




 \displaystyle\leq\frac{10d}{A(X_{d})^{1-1/k}}+\int_{X_{d}}^{\infty}\frac{10d}{A(t)^{1-1/k}}dt, 



where we’ve used the integral test to obtain an upper bound for \sum_{n=X_{d}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{A(n)^{1-1/k}} , noting that A(t) is eventually non-decreasing as t\to\infty .




To estimate the first term, we note that there exists some 0<\varepsilon^{\prime}<(2^{\frac{1}{\beta}}+\varepsilon)^{\beta}-2 such that




 A(X_{d})^{1-1/k}\geq d^{2+\varepsilon^{\prime}} 



for all sufficiently large d .
To estimate the second term, we use the integral bounds from Lemma 7.5. In particular, we note that




 \displaystyle\int_{X_{d}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{A(t)^{1-1/k}}dt 
 \displaystyle=a^{1/k-1}\int_{X_{d}}^{\infty}t^{b(k-1)/k}\exp{\Big(\frac{-c(k-1)}{k}\cdot t^{\beta}\Big)}dt 




 \displaystyle\leq a^{1/k-1}\cdot\frac{2k}{\beta c(k-1)}\cdot X_{d}^{\frac{b(k-1)}{k}+1-\beta}\cdot\exp{\Big(\frac{-c(k-1)}{k}\cdot X_{d}^{\beta}\Big)}. 



By the definition of X_{d} , we note that




 \exp{\Big(\frac{-c(k-1)}{k}\cdot X_{d}^{\beta}\Big)}=\frac{1}{d^{(2^{1/\beta}+\varepsilon)^{\beta}}}. 



Since (2^{1/\beta}+\varepsilon)^{\beta}>2 , there exists some \delta=\delta_{\beta,\varepsilon} such that




 \exp{\Big(\frac{-c(k-1)}{k}\cdot X_{d}^{\beta}\Big)}=\frac{1}{d^{2+\delta}}, 



and therefore for all sufficiently large d , we have the bound




 \displaystyle 10d\int_{X_{d}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{A(t)^{1-1/k}}dt\ll_{k}(\log{d})^{\frac{b(k-1)+k}{k\beta}-1}\cdot\frac{1}{d^{1+\delta}}\leq\frac{1}{d^{1+\varepsilon^{{}^{\prime\prime}}}}, 



for some \varepsilon^{\prime\prime}>0 . This gives the bound




 \mathbb{P}E_{d}\ll_{k}\frac{1}{d^{1+\eta}}\;\text{where}\;\eta=\min(\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}), 



for all sufficiently large d . Thus, the sum \sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}E_{d} converges, and by the Borel-Cantelli lemma, only finitely many of the events E_{d} occur, with probability 1.
∎





Combining the (unconditional) lower bound for M_{f,k}(d) shown in Proposition 3.4 with Theorem 7.6 gives the following corollary.



Corollary 7.7.



Let A(n)=an^{-b}\exp(cn^{\beta}) and let \{\varepsilon_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} be a sequence of positive real numbers such that \varepsilon_{n}\gg A(n)^{-1/k} as n\to\infty . Let \{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} be a sequence of random variables as defined in (35). Fix some positive integer k\geq 2 . Then




 \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{f},k}(d)\asymp(\log d)^{1/\beta} 



as d\to\infty , with probability 1.





We next consider for fixed d\geq 0 , the boundedness of the sequence \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{f},k}(d) as k\to\infty . The following result proves part (d) of Theorem 1.3 by setting \mathbf{f}_{n}=\mathbf{p}_{n} .



Theorem 7.8.



Let \{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} be as defined in (35), and fix d\geq 0 . Then \{\mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{f},k}(d)\}_{k=2}^{\infty} is bounded with probability 1.




Proof.



By Lemma 6.3, we have that there exists some constant A_{d} such that equation (38) holds.
For each k\geq 2 , let E_{k} denote the event that




 \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{f},k}(d)>A_{d}. 



As in the proof of Theorem 7.6, we shall show that \sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}E_{k} is finite, and thus by the Borel-Cantelli lemma, only finitely many of the events E_{k} occur, with probability 1. This will imply that \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{f},k}(d) is eventually bounded for all sufficiently large k with probability 1.




Using the union bound, we have that




 \displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}E_{k}=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{f},k}(d)>L_{d}) 
 \displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\sum_{n=A_{d}+1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d) 




 \displaystyle=\sum_{n=A_{d}+1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d), 



where the last equality conditionally follows by Fubini’s theorem, once we’ve established absolute convergence of the double sum.




By an argument analogous to that in the proof of Lemma 6.3, we have




 \sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{f},k}(n)\leq d)\leq\frac{2d+1}{2\sqrt{A(n)}}+\frac{2d+1}{A(n)^{2/3}}+\frac{(8d+5)\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}{\sqrt{A(n)}}+\frac{(6d+3)\log_{2}A(n)}{2\varepsilon_{n}A(n)} 



for all sufficiently large n . In particular, this implies that




 \sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}E_{k}\ll_{d}\sum_{n=A_{d}+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{A(n)}}. 



As the infinite sum \sum\frac{1}{\sqrt{A(n)}} converges by assumption, this proves the theorem.




∎






7.2. Computing expectation




We illustrate Corollary 7.4 for various partition functions by explicitly computing the expected number of perfect powers. Let \mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{f}} be a random variable denoting the number of perfect powers the sequence \{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} takes. While we know that \mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{f}} is finite, we can calculate \mathbb{E}\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{f}} for a choice of functions A(n) and \varepsilon_{n} , using



(41)

 \mathbb{E}\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{f}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{f}_{n}\text{ is a perfect power})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\#{\{x\in\mathcal{S}_{n}:x\text{ is a perfect power}\}}}{\#{\mathcal{S}_{n}}}. 






For each partition function f(n) , we set A(n)=an^{-b}\exp{(cn^{\beta})} for fixed a,b,c,\beta . As the value of \mathbb{E}\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{f}} depends also on a choice of errors \varepsilon_{n} , we chose to set \varepsilon_{n} as the second order term in the asymptotic expansion of f(n)/A(n) , generalizing the Rademacher expansion for p(n) . In all cases considered, this was of the form \varepsilon_{n}=B/n^{\beta} for some suitable constant B . We observe from (41) that the expectation for the number of perfect powers obtained by the functions considered is very small.




For the usual partition function p(n) , we set a=1/(4\sqrt{3}) , b=1 , c=\pi\sqrt{2/3} , and \beta=1/2 . We then set \varepsilon_{n} to be the second order term in the Rademacher expansion of p(n)/A(n) , in particular \varepsilon_{n}=(\sqrt{3/2}\,\pi+\pi/(24\sqrt{6}))/\sqrt{n} . The computation yields




 \mathbb{E}\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{p}}\approx 2.526. 






For the strict partition function q(n) , we set a=1/(4\sqrt[4]{3}n^{3/4}) , b=3/4 , c=\pi/\sqrt{3} , and \beta=1/2 . We then set \varepsilon_{n}=(\pi/(48\sqrt{3})-3\sqrt{3}/(8\pi))/\sqrt{n} . The computation yields




 \mathbb{E}\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{q}}\approx 5.805. 






For the self-conjugate partition function, we set a=1/(2\cdot 24^{1/4}) , b=3/4 , c=\pi/\sqrt{6} , and \beta=1/2 . We then set \varepsilon_{n}=(3\sqrt{6}/(8\pi)+\pi/(48\sqrt{6}))/\sqrt{n} . The computation yields




 \mathbb{E}\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{sc}}\approx 13.426. 






For the non-unitary partition function, we set a=\pi/(12\sqrt{2}) , b=3/2 , c=\pi\sqrt{2/3} , and \beta=1/2 . We then set \varepsilon_{n}=(3\sqrt{3/2}/\pi+13\pi/(24\sqrt{6}))/\sqrt{n} . The computation yields




 \mathbb{E}\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{r}}\approx 4.093. 






For the plane partition function, we set a=A_{\text{pl}} , b=25/36 , c=3\zeta(3)^{1/3}/2^{2/3} , and \beta=2/3 . We then set \varepsilon_{n}=(277/(864(2\zeta(3))^{1/3})-\zeta(3)^{2/3}/(1440\cdot 2^{1/3}))/n^{2/3} . The computation yields




 \mathbb{E}\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{pl}}\approx 0.758.

9. Conjectures




In addition to Conjecture D, in this section we make a number of further conjectures motivated by the computational data discussed in Section 8, and in a similar spirit to the conjectures of Merca–Ono–Tsai [MOT25].




Our first conjecture is a direct analogue of Sun’s conjecture, as stated in Conjecture A for plane partitions.



Conjecture 9.1.



The plane partition function pl(n) is never a perfect power of the form a^{b} for integers a,b>1 .





We also give an analogue of Conjecture D for the overpartition function \overline{p}(n) .



Conjecture 9.2.



Let k\geq 2 . Then M_{\overline{p},k}(d)\sim\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\Big(\frac{k}{k-1}\Big)^{2}(\log{d})^{2} as d\to\infty .





We next consider analogues of Conjecture E for overpartitions and r -colored partitions, i.e., that the hypothesis in Proposition 2.3 that \{M_{f,k}(d)\}_{k=2}^{\infty} is satisfied for these counting functions.



Conjecture 9.3.



For each non-negative integer d , there is a positive integer d such that for all k\geq N_{d} , we have




 M_{\overline{p},k}(d)=L_{\overline{p}}(d). 







Further, for any given non-negative integers n and d , we have that L_{\overline{p}}(d)=n is repeated \overline{p}(n+1)-\overline{p}(n) times.



Conjecture 9.4.



For each non-negative integer d , there is a positive integer d such that for all k\geq N_{d} , we have




 M_{p_{r},k}(d)=L_{p_{r}}(d). 







Further, for any given non-negative integers n and d , we have that L_{p_{r}}(d)=n is repeated p_{r}(n+1)-p_{r}(n) times.




We further take a different approach by considering the following function analogous to \Delta_{f,k}(n) . Let




 \widetilde{\Delta}_{f,a}(n):=\min\{|f(n)-a^{k}|:k\in\mathbb{N}\}. 



Here instead of fixing the power k and letting the base vary as we find the distance between p(n) and the closest k th power, we instead fix the base a and let the power vary as we find the distance between p(n) and the closest power of a .
