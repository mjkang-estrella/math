Abstract.
 

A pair (A,B) of hypergraphs is called orthogonal if |a\cap b|=1 for every pair of edges a\in A,~b\in B . An orthogonal pair of hypergraphs is called a loom if each of its two members is the set of minimum covers of the other. Looms appear naturally in the context of a conjecture of Gyárfás and Lehel on the covering number of cross-intersecting hypergraphs. We study their properties and ways of construction, and prove special cases of a conjecture that if true would imply the Gyárfás–Lehel conjecture.
 




1. A conjecture on cross-intersecting hypergraphs, and the definition of “looms”




A hypergraph H is a collection of subsets of a set V=V(H) . The elements of V are vertices and the sets in H are edges. We assume that V(H)=\cup_{e\in H}e .
If all edges of H are of size r we say that H is r -uniform. If H is r -uniform and V(H) is the disjoint union of r sets V_{1},\ldots,V_{r} such that |e\cap V_{i}|=1 for every 1\leqslant i\leqslant r and every edge e of H , then H is called r -partite. The sets V_{i} are then called sides of H .




A matching is a set of disjoint edges. The collection of all matchings in a hypergraph  H is denoted by \mathcal{M}(H) . A matching M\in\mathcal{M}(H) is perfect \bigcup M=\cup_{e\in M}e=V(H) .
The matching number \nu(H) of a hypergraph H is \max_{M\in\mathcal{M}(H)}|M| . A cover of H is a set of vertices meeting all edges of  H . The covering number \tau(H) of H is the minimal size of a cover of  H .




The union of all edges in a maximal matching is a cover, and hence in an r -uniform hypergraph  H , \tau(H)\leqslant r\nu(H) .
A famous conjecture of Ryser is a sharpening when H is r -partite:



Conjecture 1.1.



If
 H is r -partite then \tau(H)\leqslant(r-1)\nu(H) .





This conjecture, usually attributed to Ryser, first appeared in a thesis of one of his students, Henderson [10].




The case r=2 is the Frobenius-König-Hall theorem. The case r=3 was proved in [1]. The conjecture is open for r\geqslant 4 . When  \nu(H)=1 , Gyárfás proved the case r=4 , and Tuza [14] proved the case r=5 .




A pair of hypergraphs  (A,B) is cross-intersecting (a notion coined by Bollobás) if e\cap f\neq\emptyset for any e\in A and f\in B .




A pair (a,b) of sets is said to be orthogonal if |a\cap b|=1 . We write then a\perp b . A pair (A,B) of hypergraphs is orthogonal if a\perp b whenever a\in A,b\in B . We write then A\perp B .
Let H^{\perp}=\{e\subseteq V(H)\mid\{e\}\perp H\} .
An r -uniform hypergraph H is r -partite if and only if \nu(H^{\perp})=r .




If (A,B) are cross-intersecting, then
for any pair of edges e\in A and f\in B , e\cup f is a cover of A\cup B , and hence if A is a -uniform and B is b -uniform, then \tau(A\cup B)\leqslant a+b-1 .
Professedly motivated by Ryser’s conjecture, Gyárfás and Lehel conjectured that this bound can be improved when A and B are both r -partite, sharing the same r -partition.



Conjecture 1.2.



[8, 11, 5]
  If A,B are non-empty cross-intersecting r -partite hypergraphs, sharing the same r -partition, then \tau(A\cup B)\leqslant 2r-2 .





If true, then the conjecture is tight.
An example showing this (possibly essentially the only one) is obtained by taking the vertex set to be the r\times(2r-2) grid, letting A be the set of first r-1 columns, and B 
the set of r -tuples meeting all edges of A 
and all rows of the grid.




In fact, Conjecture 1.2 belongs to a different realm from that of Ryser’s conjecture: it is about rainbow matchings.
For a system \mathcal{H}=(H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}) of (not necessarily distinct) hypergraphs, let \nu_{R}(\mathcal{H}) denote the maximal size of a rainbow matching, namely a matching consisting of a choice of edges from distinct H_{i} s (not necessarily all). A pair \mathcal{H}=(H_{1},H_{2}) of non-empty hypergraphs is cross-intersecting if and only if \nu_{R}(\mathcal{H})<2 .




Haxell [9] proved:



Theorem 1.3.



If H_{1},\dots,H_{m} are r -uniform and
 \tau(\bigcup_{i\in I}H_{i})>(2r-1)(|I|-1) 
for every I\subseteq[m] then \nu_{R}(\mathcal{H})=m (namely there exists a full rainbow matching).





This explains the interest in \tau(A\cup B) in Conjecture 1.2.




Conjecture 1.2 can be generalized along the same lines:



Conjecture 1.4.



If \mathcal{H}=(H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}) is a family of r -partite hypergraphs sharing the same r -partition and
 \tau(\bigcup_{i\in I}H_{i})>(2r-2)(|I|-1) 
for every I\subseteq[m] , then \nu_{R}(\mathcal{H})=m .





Conjecture 1.2 is the case m=2 .
Later we shall meet indications that the condition of r -partiteness is unnecessarily strong
— it may suffice to assume the existence of one set orthogonal to all H_{i} (in an r -partite hypergraph every side satisfies this).




For a hypergraph  H let C(H) be the set of covers of H , and let C_{min}(H) be the set of minimal covers of H . For an integer k , let




 C_{k}(H):=\{K\in C(H):|K|=k\}. 



For our later applications, we may assume that V(C_{k}(H))=V(H) for every  k .
Clearly, if H is r -uniform, then for every s 



(1)

 C_{r}(C_{s}(H))\supseteq H. 



For two hypergraphs K and J on the same vertex set, if K\subseteq J , then C_{r}(J)\subseteq C_{r}(K) , and hence C_{s}(C_{r}(C_{s}(H)))\subseteq C_{s}(H) . Applying (1) with C_{r}(H) replacing H , we get C_{r}(H)\subseteq C_{r}(C_{s}(C_{r}(H))) , and interchanging r and s (it is possible since the above argument works for any number  r and  s ), we get



(2)

 C_{s}(C_{r}(C_{s}(H)))=C_{s}(H). 






Towards a proof of Conjecture 1.2, we may assume that A and B are orthogonal,
because if |e\cap f|\geqslant 2 for some e\in A and f\in B , then e\cup f forms a cover of A\cup B of size at most 2r-2 .
Note that \tau(A),\tau(B)\leqslant r as any edge of A is a cover of B and vice versa.
We may also assume that \tau(A)=\tau(B)=r , otherwise a cover of (say) A of size at most r-1 , together with an edge of  A , forms a cover of A\cup B of size at most 2r-2 .
The negation assumption on the conjecture, namely that \tau(A\cup B)>2r-2 , is preserved under replacing A and B by hypergraphs containing them, and hence we may assume that A=C_{r}(B) and B=C_{r}(A) . Indeed, let A^{\prime}=C_{r}(B) , we have A\subseteq A^{\prime} . Let B^{\prime}=C_{r}(A^{\prime}) . By (1) we have B\subseteq C_{r}(C_{r}(B))=B^{\prime} . Furthermore by (2), we have A^{\prime}=C_{r}(B)=C_{r}(C_{r}(C_{r}(B)))=C_{r}(C_{r}(A^{\prime}))=C_{r}(B^{\prime}) .




We tag a pair of r -uniform hypergraphs satisfying all these conditions an (r,r) -loom. This can be generalized to pairs of hypergraphs of not necessarily equal uniformities.



Definition 1.5.



Let r,s\geqslant 1 . An (r,s) -loom is a pair \mathbb{L}=(A,B) of orthogonal hypergraphs satisfying:

(1) 



 A is r -uniform,

(2) 



 B is s -uniform,

(3) 



 \tau(A)=s,\tau(B)=r , and

(4) 



 A=C_{r}(B),B=C_{s}(A) .






Examples 1.6.



(1) 



The simplest loom
is the (1,1) -loom \mathbb{U} in which A=B=\{\{v\}\} .

(2) 



For any r let \mathbb{V}_{r}=(A,B) be the (unique) (r,1) -loom, in which
 A=\{\{v_{1},\ldots.v_{r}\}\} and B=\{\{v_{1}\},\ldots\{v_{r}\}\} .

(3) 



More generally, let A be an r -uniform matching with s edges, and B=C_{s}(A) the set of all transversals of A . Then (A,B) is an (r,s) -loom.

(4) 



On the r\times r grid let A consist of the r rows and r columns, and let B be the set of r! permutation subgrids.
Then (A,B) is an (r,r) -loom, a fact provable by induction on r . For future reference, we name it \mathbb{L}_{r,r} .
Another representation of this loom is setting V=E(K_{r,r}) , A the set of stars of size r in K_{r,r} , and B the set of perfect matchings.







A few observations:



Lemma 1.7.



If \mathbb{L}=(A,B) is an (r,s) -loom then V(A)=V(B) .




Proof.



If, say, v\in V(A)\setminus V(B) , then v\in a for some a\in A and a\setminus\{v\} is a cover of B , contradicting the assumption \tau(B)=r .
∎




Lemma 1.8.



If \mathbb{L}=(A,B) is an (r,r) -loom and r>1 
then \nu(A)>1 . Moreover, every edge of A has an A -edge disjoint from it.




Proof.



If e\in A does not have an A -edge disjoint from it then it belongs to C_{r}(A) , and hence to B . This contradicts the orthogonality condition.
∎





For a hypergraph H and a vertex v of H , let
 star_{H}(v)=\{e\in H:v\in e\} .



Lemma 1.9.



Let \mathbb{L}=(A,B) be an (r,r) -loom. If star_{A}(x)\subseteq star_{A}(y) , then star_{A}(x)=star_{A}(y) .




Proof.



Negation means that there is an edge e\in A containing both x and y and an edge e^{\prime}\in A containing y but not x . Let f be an edge of B containing x . Since f meets e^{\prime} and is orthogonal to e , it contains a vertex in e^{\prime}\setminus e . Then (f\setminus\{x\})\cup\{y\} is also a cover of A as all the edges containing x must contain y . Thus f^{\prime}=(f\setminus\{x\})\cup\{y\}\in B . Then |f^{\prime}\cap e^{\prime}|>1 , contrary to the assumption that A\perp B .
∎




Lemma 1.10.



Let \mathbb{L}=(A,B) be an (r,s) -loom. A matching M\in\mathcal{M}(A) is perfect if and only if |M|=s .




Proof.



If M is perfect then its edges meet all vertices of any f\in B . The orthogonality condition implies then that |M|=|f|=s .
For the other direction, assume |M|=s . If there exists a vertex v\in V(\mathbb{L})\setminus V(M) then any f\in B containing v is not large enough to meet all edges in M .
∎




Notation 1.11.



For a function f:S\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0} let |f|=\sum_{s\in S}f(s) .





Given a hypergraph H , a fractional matching of H is a function f:E(H)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0} satisfying \sum_{e\in H:v\in e}f(e)\leqslant 1 for every v\in V(H) . It is called perfect if equality holds for all v\in V .
The fractional matching number \nu^{*}(H) is the maximum of |f| over all fractional matchings f of H . The characteristic function of a matching is a fractional matching, hence
 \nu(H)\leqslant\nu^{*}(H) .
A fractional cover of H is a function g:V(H)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0} satisfying \sum_{v:v\in e}g(v)\geqslant 1 for every e\in H . The fractional covering number \tau^{*}(H) is the minimum of |g| over all fractional covers   g of H .
The characteristic function of a cover is a fractional covering, hence
 \tau^{*}(H)\leqslant\tau(H) .
By LP duality, \tau^{*}(H)=\nu^{*}(H) ,
so




 \nu(H)\leqslant\nu^{*}(H)=\tau^{*}(H)\leqslant\tau(H). 





Lemma 1.12.



In an (r,s) -loom \mathbb{L}=(A,B) , A has a perfect fractional matching if and only if \nu^{*}(A)=s .




Proof.



Given b\in B , for any fractional matching f of A ,




 |f|=\sum_{u\in b}\sum_{a\in A:u\in a}f(a). 



The right-hand side is s if and only if f is saturated at each u\in b . The lemma follows from this and the fact that \bigcup B=V(\mathbb{L}) .
∎





Of course, symmetric claims of these lemmas hold for B .




Looms are relevant to Conjecture 1.2, but they are also interesting on their own merit. Presently we know precious little about them.
A central question we do not know the answer to is whether each component of a loom must have a perfect fractional matching. As we shall see in Section 4, a positive answer would imply
Conjecture 1.2.





2. Bounding the fractional matching number




Let us indeed start with the fractional case of Conjecture 1.2. It turns out to be true, with a margin: it does not require r -partiteness, and the 2 -factor is redundant.



Theorem 2.1.



If A and B are cross-intersecting r -uniform hypergraphs then \tau^{*}(A\cup B)\leqslant r .





This was proved in [4], and generalized in [2]
to:



Theorem 2.2.



Let \mathcal{H}=(H_{1},H_{2},\ldots,H_{m}) be a system of r -uniform hypergraphs. If
 \tau^{*}(\bigcup_{i\in I}H_{i})>r(|I|-1) 
for every I\subseteq[m] then \nu_{R}(\mathcal{H})=m (namely there exists a full rainbow matching).





A standard deficiency argument yields a generalization:



Theorem 2.3.



If \nu_{R}(\mathcal{H})<m then there exists a subset I of [m] for which
 \tau^{*}(\cup_{i\in I}H_{i})\leqslant r(|I|-(m-\nu_{R}(\mathcal{H}))) .





If \nu_{R}(\mathcal{H})=1 
and H_{i}\neq\emptyset for all i , then necessarily the set I in the theorem is [m] . This yields:



Theorem 2.4.



[4, 2]
If \nu_{R}(\mathcal{H})=1 
and H_{i}\neq\emptyset for all i ,
then \tau^{*}(\bigcup_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m}H_{i})\leqslant r .





In [4] it was shown that the fractional cover witnessing this result can be chosen as a convex combination of \{\chi_{e}\}_{e\in H_{i}} for some i . ( \chi_{e} is the characteristic function of e , namely \chi_{e}(v)=1 if v\in e and \chi_{e}(v)=0 if v\notin e .)



Corollary 2.5.



If A,B are cross-intersecting, A is r -uniform
and B is s -uniform
then \tau^{*}(A\cup B)\leqslant\max(r,s) .




Proof.



Assume r\geqslant s . Replace every edge b\in B by b\cup dummy(b) , where
 dummy(b) is a set of size r-s , disjoint from V(A)\cup V(B) and dummy(b)\cap dummy(c)=\emptyset whenever b\neq c . Apply now Theorem 2.1.
∎





It can be shown that equality holds in Theorem 2.4 if and only if \nu^{*}(H_{i})=r for some 1\leqslant i\leqslant m . Let us state and prove this just for m=2 .



Theorem 2.6.



If H_{1},H_{2} are cross-intersecting r -uniform hypergraphs and \tau^{*}(H_{1}\cup H_{2})=r then \max_{i=1,2}\nu^{*}(H_{i})=r .




Proof.



Assume, for contradiction, that






(3)

 \max_{i=1,2}\nu^{*}(H_{i})<r. 






Let V=V(H_{1}\cup H_{2}) . Let C_{i}=conv(\{\chi_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|}\mid e\in H_{i}\}) be the convex hull of the characteristic functions for i=1,2 . By the cross-intersection assumption \chi_{e_{1}}\cdot\chi_{e_{2}}\geqslant 1 for any e_{1}\in H_{1} and e_{2}\in H_{2} . Hence w_{1}\cdot w_{2}\geqslant 1 for any w_{1}\in C_{1} and w_{2}\in C_{2} .




Let u_{i} be the shortest vector in C_{i}  ( i=1,2 ).
Since H_{i} is r -uniform, we have u_{i}\cdot\bar{1}=r , where \bar{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|} is the all 1 s vector. Since
 u_{1}\cdot u_{2}\geqslant 1 , at least one of u_{1},~u_{2} has length at least 1 .




Assume first that one of u_{1},u_{2} is strictly longer than 1 , say ||u_{1}||>1 .
Since u_{1} is the shortest vector in C_{1} ,
we have u_{1}\cdot\chi_{e}\geqslant u_{1}\cdot u_{1}>1 for every e in H_{1} .
Therefore, for some 0<\alpha<1 we have \alpha u_{1}\cdot\chi_{e}\geqslant 1 for every e\in H_{1} . By (3), there exists a fractional cover g of H_{2} satisfying |g|<r . Then w=\alpha u_{1}+(1-\alpha)g is a fractional cover of H_{1}\cup H_{2} . Since |w|=w\cdot\bar{1}<\alpha r+(1-\alpha)r=r , this contradicts the assumption of the theorem.




We may thus assume that ||u_{1}||=||u_{2}||=1 .
This implies u_{1}=u_{2}=:u , since otherwise u_{1}\cdot u_{2}<1 . The vector u is then a fractional cover of H_{1}\cup H_{2} of weight u_{1}\cdot\bar{1}=r .



Claim 2.6.1.



Let supp(u):=\{v\in V\mid u(v)>0\} . Then
 |supp(u)|=r^{2} , and u(v)=\frac{1}{r} for every v\in supp(u) .




Proof of the claim.



We first show that |supp(u)|\leqslant r^{2} .
Let f be a fractional matching of H_{1}\cup H_{2} with |f|=r . By complementary slackness, \sum_{e:v\in e}f(e)=1 for every v\in supp(u) . Therefore




 \displaystyle|supp(u)|=\sum_{v\in supp(u)}1 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{v\in supp(u)}\sum_{v\in e}f(e) 




 \displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{e:v\in e}f(e)=\sum_{e}\sum_{v:v\in e}f(e)=r\sum_{e}f(e)=r^{2}. 






For the inverse inequality, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have:




 r=u\cdot\chi_{supp(u)}\leqslant||u||\times||\chi_{supp(u)}||=\sqrt{|supp(u)|} 



so |supp(u)|\geqslant r^{2} . The claim that u is constant follows from the characterization of equality in the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
∎





To deduce Theorem 2.6 from the claim, write u=\sum_{e\in H_{1}}\alpha_{e}\chi_{e} with \sum_{e\in H_{1}}\alpha_{e}=1 , and let f:E(H_{1})\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0} be defined by f(e)=r\alpha_{e} . Since \sum_{e\in H_{1}:v\in e}f(e)=ru(v)=1 for every v\in V(H_{1}) ,    f is a fractional matching of H_{1} . Its weight f is \sum_{e\in H_{1}}f(e)=r\sum_{e\in H_{1}}\alpha_{e}=r , which proves the theorem.
∎




Remark 2.7.



The condition \tau^{*}(H_{1}\cup H_{2})=r is necessary: it is not always true that if A,B are cross-intersecting then \nu^{*}(A\cup B)=\max(\nu^{*}(A),\nu^{*}(B)) . For example, if H_{1}=\{ab,bc\} and H_{2}=\{ac\} then \nu^{*}(H_{i})=1~~(i=1,2) while \nu^{*}(H_{1}\cup H_{2})=\frac{3}{2} .






3. Many mutually cross-intersecting hypergraphs




Famously, there can be no more than r+1 mutually orthogonal r -uniform matchings of size r each. This is usually expressed in terms of mutually orthogonal Latin squares — there can be no more than r-1 of those of order r (see, e.g., [13, Theorem 6.29]).
The bound is attained when there exists an (r+1) -uniform projective plane, in particular when r is a power of a prime.
Since a matching of size r has \tau^{*}=r , the following is a fractional generalization of this theorem.



Theorem 3.1.



Let (H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}) be a family of pairwise cross-intersecting r -uniform hypergraphs, where r\geqslant 2 . If m>r+1 then \tau^{*}(H_{i})<r for some 1\leqslant i\leqslant m .




Proof.



Since e\cap V(H_{i}) is a cover of H_{i} for every e\in H_{j} , there holds |e\cap V(H_{i})|=r , so e\subseteq V(H_{i}) . This proves that V(H_{i})=V(H_{j}) for all i,j\leqslant m .




Every H_{i} is covered by any edge from any H_{j},~j\neq i , hence \tau^{*}(H_{i})\leqslant\tau(H_{i})\leqslant r . Thus, the negation assumption on the theorem is that
 \tau^{*}(H_{i})=r for all i\leqslant m .
Let m_{i}:H_{i}\to\mathbb{R} be a fractional matchings of H_{i} satisfying |m_{i}|=r .
Removing edges of zero weight we may assume that H_{i}=supp(m_{i}) .
Let w_{i}(v)=\sum_{e\in H_{i}:v\in e}\frac{m_{i}(e)}{r} .



Claim 3.1.1.



For any j\leqslant m , w_{i} is a minimum fractional cover of H_{j} .




Proof of the claim.



For any f\in H_{j} we have



(4)

 \sum_{v\in f}w_{i}(v)=\sum_{v\in f}\sum_{e\in H_{i}:v\in e}\frac{m_{i}(e)}{r}=\sum_{e\in H_{i}}\frac{m_{i}(e)}{r}|f\cap e|\geqslant\sum_{e\in H_{i}}\frac{m_{i}(e)}{r}=1. 



On the other hand




 |w_{i}|=\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{e\in H_{i}}\frac{m_{i}(e)}{r}=\sum_{e\in H_{i}}\sum_{v\in e}\frac{m_{i}(e)}{r}=\sum_{e\in H_{i}}\frac{m_{i}(e)}{r}\cdot r=r, 






proving the claim. ∎





By complementary slackness, for each h\in supp(m_{j})=H_{j} , we have \sum_{v\in h}w_{i}(v)=1 . Thus in (4) we have equality, and hence



(5)

 |h\cap e|=1 



for any e\in H_{i} and h\in H_{j} .




Let v\in V(H_{1}) and e_{1}\in H_{1} be such that v\not\in e_{1} (we may assume that there exist such, since otherwise |H_{1}|=1 and \tau^{*}(H_{1})=1<2 .) For each 2\leqslant j\leqslant r+1 there exists e_{j}\in H_{j} such that v\in e_{j} . But H_{1} and H_{j} are cross-intersecting, therefore e_{j}\cap e_{1}\neq\emptyset . By pigeonhole, there exist x\in e_{1} and 2\leqslant i<j\leqslant m such that x\in e_{i}\cap e_{1} and x\in e_{j}\cap e_{1} . Therefore \{v,x\}\subseteq e_{i}\cap e_{j} , contradicting  (5).
∎




Question 3.2.



We know that the result is sharp for r a prime power. Is it sharp also for other values of r ? Namely, are there examples with m=r+1 and \nu^{*}(H_{i})=r ( 1\leqslant i\leqslant m ) for general r ?




Definition 3.3.



For integers r,m 
let g(r,m) be the maximum, over all m -tuples (H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}) of pairwise cross-intersecting r -uniform hypergraphs, of \min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m}\tau^{*}(H_{i}) .





For r having a projective plane \mathbb{P}_{r} of uniformity r , we have g(r,m)\geqslant r-1+\frac{1}{r} , by taking H_{i}=\mathbb{P}_{r} for all i .



Conjecture 3.4.



For m\geqslant r+2 we have g(r,m)\leqslant r-1+\frac{1}{r} , with equality if and only if there is a projective plane of uniformity r .





The conjecture is true for r=2 , since in this case \tau^{*}(H_{i})<2 means \nu(H_{i})=1 , implying \tau^{*}(H_{i})\leqslant\frac{3}{2} .



Theorem 3.5.



For every r there exists m=m(r) such g(r,m)\leqslant r-1+\frac{1}{r} .




Proof.



Let C=C(r) be such that in any r -uniform hypergraph of size C there exists a \Delta -system with at least r+1 leaves. (A \Delta -system with \ell leaves is a hypergraph  D consisting of \ell edges for which there exists a “core” set K , such that e\cap f=K 
for all pairs e\neq f\in D . The existence of C as above was proved in [6]).
If there exists  H_{i} of size larger than  C , then H_{i} contains a \Delta -system with r+1 leaves, and then its core is a cover for every other H_{j} , and hence \tau(H_{j})\leqslant r-1 for all j\neq i .




Thus we may assume that |E(H_{i})|\leqslant C for all 1\leqslant i\leqslant m . For large enough m there exist then j_{1}\neq j_{2} for which H_{j_{1}}=H_{j_{2}} . Then \nu(H_{j_{1}})=\nu(H_{j_{2}})=1 , and hence by a theorem of Füredi [7, Theorem], we have \tau^{*}(H_{j_{1}})\leqslant r-1+\frac{1}{r} .
∎






4. The fractional matching number of looms




Apart from the present section, the rest of the paper is devoted to the construction of looms. But before embarking on this project, we present a conjecture that implies Conjecture 1.2. This way we can accompany each construction with a verification of the conjecture in the constructed loom.




For a loom \mathbb{L}=(A,B) let




 \tau(\mathbb{L}):=\tau(A\cup B),\quad\nu(\mathbb{L}):=\nu(A\cup B)\quad\text{and}\quad\tau^{*}(\mathbb{L}):=\tau^{*}(A\cup B). 



Of course, \nu(\mathbb{L})=\max(\nu(A),\nu(B)) .




By Corollary 2.5 if \mathbb{L}=(A,B) is an (r,s) -loom then
 \tau^{*}(\mathbb{L})\leqslant\max(r,s) .
It may well be that equality holds.



Conjecture 4.1.



If \mathbb{L}=(A,B) is an (r,s) -loom then \tau^{*}(\mathbb{L})=\max(r,s) .





By Theorem 2.6, if r=s then this would imply that
 \max(\tau^{*}(A),\tau^{*}(B))=r . More generally:



Conjecture 4.2.



If \mathbb{L}=(A,B) is an (r,s) -loom then \tau^{*}(A)=s,\tau^{*}(B)=r .




Proposition 4.3.



If Conjecture 4.2 is true for an (r,s) -loom (A,B) , then |V(A)|=|V(B)|=rs .




Proof.



Let w:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0} be a fractional matching of size s . By Lemma 1.12, \sum_{e\in A:v\in e}w(e)=1 for every v\in V . Thus we have




 |V(A)|=\sum_{v\in V(A)}1=\sum_{v\in V(A)}\sum_{e\in A:v\in e}w(e)=\sum_{e\in A}\sum_{v\in V:v\in e}w(e)=\sum_{e\in A}rw(e)=rs. 






∎





In particular, Conjecture 4.2
implies Conjecture 1.2, since if the hypergraphs are r -partite and |V(\mathbb{L})|=r^{2} , then there exists a side of size at most r , and since every side is a cover this implies \tau(\mathbb{L})\leqslant r . Note that this result is not stronger than the original conjecture (bounding the covering number by 2r-2 ), because we used the negation assumption on the latter.




4.1. Pinnability



Definition 4.4.



A hypergraph H is said to be pinnable if H^{\perp}\neq\emptyset .





We suspect that in  Conjecture 1.2 the milder assumption of pinnability suffices:



Conjecture 4.5.



If A,B are cross-intersecting r -uniform hypergraphs and A\cup B is pinnable, then \tau(A\cup B)\leqslant 2r-2 .





This conjecture can be formulated also for hypergraphs of different uniformities.



Conjecture 4.6.



If A is r -uniform, B is s -uniform, A and B are cross-intersecting, V(A)=V(B) and A\cup B is pinnable, then \tau(A\cup B)\leqslant r+s-2 .




Theorem 4.7.



Conjecture 4.6 is true for s=2 .




Proof.



Let p\in(A\cup B)^{\perp} be a pinning set for A\cup B .
Then B is a bipartite graph with sides p,V\setminus p . Let v\in p . By the assumption that V(A)=V(B) , v is contained in a triple e\in A . By the cross-intersection property e\setminus\{v\}\supseteq N_{B}(p\setminus\{v\}) , and hence
 |N_{B}(p\setminus\{v\})|\leqslant r-1 , so v+N_{B}(p\setminus\{v\}) is a covering set of A\cup B of size at most  r .
∎





The condition V(A)=V(B) is essential — the theorem is not true, for example, if one of the hypergraphs is empty.




A strengthening of Conjecture 1.4 is:



Conjecture 4.8.



Let \mathcal{H}=(H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}) be a family of r -uniform hypergraphs. If H_{1}\cup\dots\cup H_{m} is pinnable and \tau(\cup_{i\in I}H_{i})>(2r-2)(|I|-1) for all I\subseteq[m] then \mathcal{H} has a full rainbow matching, i.e, \nu_{R}(\mathcal{H})=m .




Theorem 4.9.



For an (r,s) -loom (A,B) , if \tau^{*}(A)=s then B=A^{\perp} .




Proof.



Let p\in A^{\perp} , we wish to show that p\in B . It suffices to show that |p|\leqslant s .
Let f be a maximum fractional matching, namely of size s . By complementary slackness |f|=|p| , hence |p|\leqslant s , as desired.
∎




Theorem 4.10.



If \mathbb{L}=(A,B) is an (r,r) -loom and \tau^{*}(A)=r , then |p|=r for every set p\in(A\cup B)^{\perp} .




Proof.



Let f be a fractional matching of size r of A , then by Lemma 1.12 it is a perfect fractional matching. Let p\in(A\cup B)^{\perp} . We have




 |p|=\sum_{v\in p}\sum_{e\in A:v\in e}f(e)=|f|=r. 



∎







5. Constructions




Looms are varied, and yet scarce enough to make their construction challenging.
The simplest loom \mathbb{U} , in which A=B=\{\{v\}\} , has already been mentioned. From it we shall be able to construct many others, using simple operations, the first of which is composition.




5.1. Composition of looms




Given two hypergraphs A and C on disjoint vertex sets, their join A*C is \{a\cup c\mid a\in A,c\in C\} .
Let \mathbb{L}_{1}=(A,B_{1}) be an (a,b) -loom and \mathbb{L}_{2}=(C,B_{2}) be a (c,b) -loom, where V_{1}=V(A)=V(B_{1}) is disjoint from V_{2}=V(C)=V(B_{2}) .
Let S=A*C and T=B_{1}\cup B_{2} . The pair \mathbb{L}_{1}\boxtimes_{1}\mathbb{L}_{2}=(S,T) is called the 1 -composition of \mathbb{L}_{1} and \mathbb{L}_{2} . The 1 indicates that the * operation is applied to the first coordinate. A similar definition and notation apply to 2 -compositions.



Lemma 5.1.



For an (a,b) -loom \mathbb{L}_{1} and a (c,b) -loom \mathbb{L}_{2} on disjoint vertex sets, \mathbb{L}_{1}\boxtimes_{1}\mathbb{L}_{2} is an (a+c,b) -loom.





The proof is easy, and is contained in the proof of  Theorem 5.6 below.




A loom that is the composition of two looms is said to be decomposable.
A hypergraph is defined to be connected if its 1 -skeleton (the collection of subsets of size 2 of the edges) is a connected graph.




For sets S and T , we define




 S\upharpoonright T=\{s\cap T\mid s\in S\}. 





Lemma 5.2.



A loom is decomposable if and only if one of its components is not connected.




Proof.



If \mathbb{L}=(A,B) is decomposable, then by definition, at least one of A or B is not connected.




For the other direction, let \mathbb{L}=(A,B) be an (r,s) -loom in which one of the components, say B , is not connected. That is, B=B_{1}\cup B_{2} , where V(B_{1})\cap V(B_{2})=\emptyset .




Let A_{1}=A\upharpoonright V(B_{1})=\{a\cap V(B_{1})\mid a\in A\} and A_{2}=A\upharpoonright V(B_{2})=\{a\cap V(B_{2})\mid a\in A\} . We shall prove that (A_{1},B_{1}),(A_{2},B_{2}) are looms and (A,B)=(A_{1},B_{1})\boxtimes_{1}(A_{2},B_{2}) .



Claim 5.2.1.



For i=1,2 , A_{i} is r_{i} -uniform for some r_{i} satisfying r_{1}+r_{2}=r .




Proof of the claim.



Let e,f\in A , and assume for negation that (say) |f\cap V(B_{1})|<|e\cap V(B_{1})| . Then (f\cap V(B_{1}))\cup(e\cap V(B_{2})) is a cover of B of size smaller than  r=|e| , contrary to the assumption that \tau(B)=r .
∎





Let r_{i} be the uniformity of A_{i} for i=1,2 , which by Claim 5.2.1 satisfies r_{1}+r_{2}=r .



Claim 5.2.2.



 (A_{i},B_{i}) is an (r_{i},s) -loom for i=1,2 .




Proof of the claim.



First we prove that (A_{i},B_{i}) are orthogonal. Let a_{i}\in A_{i},b\in B_{i}\subseteq B . Then a_{i}=a\cap V(B_{i}) for some a\in A . Since (A,B) are orthogonal, |a\cap b|=1 and a\cap b\subseteq V(B_{i}) , meaning that a_{i}\cap b=a\cap b and |a_{i}\cap b|=1 .




Next we show that \tau(B_{i})=r_{i} and C_{r_{i}}(B_{i})=A_{i} for i=1,2 .
For any a_{i}\in A_{i} , we have a_{i}=a\cap V(B_{i}) for some a\in A . Since a is a cover of B_{i} , then a_{i}=a\cap V(B_{i}) is also a cover of B_{i} . Therefore A_{i}\subseteq C(B_{i}) and \tau(B_{i})\leqslant r_{i} . Suppose e is a cover of B_{i} of size at most r_{i} . We take a\in A and a_{j}=a\cap V(B_{j}) is a cover of B_{j} for j\neq i . Hence e\cup a_{j} is a cover of B=B_{i}\cup B_{j} . Therefore \tau(B_{i})=r_{i} (otherwise we have a cover of B of size less than r_{i}+r_{j}=r ) and e\cup a_{j}=a^{\prime} for some a^{\prime}\in A (otherwise C_{r}(B)\neq A ) so that e=a^{\prime}\cap V(B_{i})\in A_{i} , which proves C_{r_{i}}(B_{i})=A_{i} .




Finally we show that \tau(A_{i})=s and C_{s}(A_{i})=B_{i} . Since a cover of A_{i} is a cover of A , we have \tau(A_{i})\geqslant\tau(A)\geqslant s and C_{s}(A_{i})\subseteq C_{s}(A)\upharpoonright V(B_{i})=B_{i} .
On the other hand, any element of B_{i} is a cover of A_{i} , therefore \tau(A_{i})=s and B_{i}\subseteq C_{s}(A_{i}) . Hence C_{s}(A_{i})=B_{i} .
∎





The above proof shows that A_{1}*A_{2}\subseteq C_{r}(B)=A , and by definition we have A\subseteq A_{1}*A_{2} . Therefore A=A_{1}*A_{2} and then (A,B)=(A_{1},B_{1})\boxtimes_{1}(A_{2},B_{2}) .
∎





On the way we proved:



Lemma 5.3.



Let (A,B) be a loom. If C is a connected component of B , then (A\upharpoonright V(C),C) is a loom.





The composition \mathbb{U}^{\boxtimes_{1}r} of \mathbb{U} with itself r times
is \mathbb{V}_{r} , from Example 1.6 (2).
The s -fold \boxtimes_{2} -composition of \mathbb{V}_{r} with itself
results in the loom of Example 1.6 (3).





5.2. Blow-ups: a generalization of composition




Composition produces new looms from given ones. There is a more general operation of this sort, which we call “blow-up”. It takes a cross-intersecting pair \mathbb{P} of hypergraphs and replaces each vertex with a loom. Under certain conditions, the resulting pair of hypergraphs is a loom. A sufficient condition is that \mathbb{P} itself is a loom and we replace its vertices by looms of the same size, but there are more general settings in which this is attained.




Let \mathbb{P}=(A,B) be a pair of orthogonal hypergraphs (not necessarily uniform), satisfying \bigcup A=\bigcup B=V=[n] . Then A\subseteq C_{min}(B) (and B\subseteq C_{min}(A) ): a-v is not a cover for every v\in a , since there is an edge of B meeting a just in v .




Let \mathbb{P}_{i}=(A_{i},B_{i}) with 1\leqslant i\leqslant n on disjoint vertex sets, and let




 C=\bigcup\{A_{i_{1}}*\cdots*A_{i_{p}}\mid\{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}\}\in A\}, 






 D=\bigcup\{B_{j_{1}}*\cdots*B_{j_{q}}\mid\{j_{1},\dots,j_{q}\}\in B\}. 



We then call (C,D) a blow-up of \mathbb{P} and denote it by \mathbb{P}[\mathbb{P}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{P}_{n}] .



Remark 5.4.



To see that compositions are a special case, note the following. If \mathbb{L}=(A,B) is the (2,1)-loom \mathbb{V}_{2} , and the uniformities of the second components of \mathbb{L}_{1} and \mathbb{L}_{2} are the same, then \mathbb{L}[\mathbb{L}_{1},\mathbb{L}_{2}]=\mathbb{L}_{1}\boxtimes_{1}\mathbb{L}_{2} .





Theorem 5.6 below provides sufficient conditions for blow-ups to be looms. Before delving into them, here is an example
against which they can be checked.




For x,y,z<10 we write below xy for \{x,y\} and xyz for
 \{x,y,z\} .



Example 5.5.



Number the vertices of the 3\times 3 grid 1,2,\ldots,9 so that the columns are 147,258,369 and the rows 123,456,789 .




Let




 C=\{147,258,369,159,158,247,259,368\} 



and




 D=\{123,456,789,357,126,345,489,567\}. 



Denote the pair (C,D) by
 \mathbb{V}_{3,3} .




To see that \mathbb{V}_{3,3} is a blow-up, let \mathbb{P}=(A,B) be a pair of hypergraphs on \{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5}\} , where




 A=\{x_{1}x_{4},x_{2}x_{5},x_{1}x_{3}x_{5}\}\text{ and }B=\{x_{1}x_{2},x_{4}x_{5},x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}\}. 



Let \mathbb{L}_{1}=(\{\{1\},\{2\}\},\{12\}) and \mathbb{L}_{5}=(\{\{8\},\{9\}\},\{89\}) be (1,2) -looms, \mathbb{L}_{2}=(\{36\},\{\{3\},\{6\}\}) and \mathbb{L}_{4}=(\{47\},\{\{4\},\{7\}\}) be (2,1) -looms, and \mathbb{L}_{3}=(\{\{5\}\},\{\{5\}\}) be a (1,1) -loom.
Then \mathbb{P}[\mathbb{L}_{1},\mathbb{L}_{2},\mathbb{L}_{3},\mathbb{L}_{4},\mathbb{L}_{5}]=(C,D) .




We claim that \mathbb{V}_{3,3} is a (3,3) -loom. This can be checked directly, but it also follows from the next theorem.




Theorem 5.6.



Let \mathbb{P}=(A,B) be an orthogonal pair of hypergraphs satisfying \bigcup A=\bigcup B .
Let \mathbb{L}_{i}=(A_{i},B_{i}) be (r_{i},s_{i}) -looms for 1\leqslant i\leqslant n . If \mathbb{P}[\mathbb{L}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{L}_{n}]=(C,D) satisfies

(1) 



 C and D are uniform - say C is c -uniform and D is d -uniform, and

(2) 



For any minimal cover f of A that is not in B , we have \sum_{j\in f}s_{j}>d , and

(3) 



For any minimal cover e of B that is not in A , we have \sum_{i\in e}r_{i}>c .



Then (C,D) is a (c,d) -loom.





Note that in Example 5.5, \{x_{1},x_{5}\} is a minimal cover of A and is not in B , but the uniformity of the join of the second components of \mathbb{L}_{1} and \mathbb{L}_{5} is 4, which is greater than 3. Therefore the blow-up satisfies the assumption of Theorem 5.6, which implies that \mathbb{V}_{3,3} is a loom.



Proof of Theorem 5.6.


Claim 5.6.1.



 C\perp D .




Proof of the claim.



Let e\in C and f\in D . By the construction e=e_{i_{1}}\cup\cdots\cup e_{i_{p}} where \{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}\}\in A and e_{i_{\ell}}\in A_{i_{\ell}} for every \ell , and f=f_{j_{1}}\cup\cdots\cup f_{j_{q}} where \{j_{1},\dots,j_{q}\}\in B and f_{j_{\ell}}\in B_{j_{\ell}} for every \ell .
Then by A\perp B , we have \{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}\}\cap\{j_{1},\dots,j_{q}\}=\{k\} , and by the disjointness of V(\mathbb{L}_{i}) we have e\cap f=e_{k}\cap f_{k} . Since (A_{k},B_{k}) is a loom, |e\cap f|=|e_{k}\cap f_{k}|=1 , as claimed.
∎





It remains to show that \tau(C)=d , and C_{d}(C)\subseteq D (so that Claim 5.6.1 implies C_{d}(C)=D ). \tau(D)=c and C_{c}(D)\subseteq C will follow symmetrically.



Claim 5.6.2.



If f is a minimum cover of C , then for each j\in[n] , if f\cap V(B_{j}) is non-empty then it belongs to B_{j} .




Proof of the claim.



Suppose f\cap V(B_{j})\neq\emptyset . We claim that it is a minimum cover of A_{j} .




First if it is not a cover of A_{j} ,
let f^{\prime}=f\setminus V(B_{j}) . By the minimality of |f| , there is an edge \cup_{i\in a}e_{i}\in C , where a\in A and e_{i}\in A_{i} , not covered by f^{\prime} .
In particular, we have j\in a . Then e=\cup_{i\in a\setminus\{j\}}e_{i}\cup e_{j}^{\prime}\in C is not covered by f , where e_{j}^{\prime}\in A_{j} is an edge not covered by f\cap V(B_{j}) , a contradiction to the fact that f is a cover of C .




Now if f\cap V(B_{j}) 
is not a minimum cover of A_{j} , we can take a minimum cover e_{j} of A_{j} and set f^{\prime}=\Big(f\setminus\big(f\cap V(B_{j})\big)\Big)\cup e_{j} . Note that the size of f^{\prime} is strictly smaller than f but f^{\prime} covers the same edges of C as f , contradicting the minimum assumption on f .
∎




Claim 5.6.3.



If f is a minimum cover of C , then J=\{j:f\cap V(B_{j})\neq\emptyset\} is a minimal cover of A and is in B .




Proof of the claim.



If J is not a cover of  A , then there exists a\in A such that a\cap J=\emptyset . Choose an edge e_{i}\in A_{i} for each i\in a . Then
 f\cap(\cup_{i\in a}e_{i})=\emptyset , a contradiction to the assumption that f is a cover of  C .
So J is a cover of A . If it is not minimal, we can remove a subset R so that J\setminus R is a minimal cover of A , then by Claim 5.6.2, f\setminus(\cup_{j\in R}f\cap V(B_{j})) is still a cover of C but is of smaller size than f , a contradiction to the minimum assumption of  f .
To see that J is in B , negating this statement means that f is of size strictly larger than f^{\prime} for any f^{\prime}\in D . But f^{\prime} is a cover of C , a contradiction to the fact that f is a minimum cover.
∎





Combining Claim 5.6.2 and Claim 5.6.3 with the definition of the blow-up (C,D)=\mathbb{P}[\mathbb{L}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{L}_{n}] , we have \tau(C)=d,C_{d}(C)\subseteq D so that C_{d}(C)=D by Claim 5.6.1, and symmetrically we have \tau(D)=c,C_{c}(D)=C — the remaining conditions required on (C,D) for being a loom. This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎




Corollary 5.7.



Let \mathbb{L}=(A,B) be a (p,q) -loom on the vertex set V=[n] , and for each 1\leqslant i\leqslant n let \mathbb{L}_{i}=(A_{i},B_{i}) be an (r_{i},s_{i}) -loom, where V(\mathbb{L}_{i}) are disjoint. If r_{i_{1}}+\cdots+r_{i_{p}}=c for every \{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}\}\in A and s_{j_{1}}+\cdots+s_{j_{q}}=d for every \{j_{1},\dots,j_{q}\}\in B , and r_{i_{1}}+\cdots+r_{i_{p+1}}>c for any distinct vertices i_{1},\dots,i_{p+1}\in V and s_{j_{1}}+\dots+s_{j_{q+1}}>d for any distinct vertices j_{1},\dots,j_{q+1}\in V , then (C,D)=\mathbb{L}[\mathbb{L}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{L}_{n}] is a (c,d) -loom.




Proof.



(1) is valid by the assumption of the corollary. By the symmetry between (2) and (3),
we only need to verify (3) of Theorem 5.6:
if e is a minimal cover of B but is not in A , then e is not a minimum cover of B , which means |e|>p . Therefore by the assumption of the corollary, \sum_{i\in e}r_{i}>c . Hence \mathbb{L}[\mathbb{L}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{L}_{n}] satisfies (3) of Theorem 5.6.
∎





Next we show that having perfect matchings and fractional perfect matchings are preserved (in a sense to be stated below) by blow-ups.



Theorem 5.8.



Let \mathbb{L}=(A,B) be a (p,q) -loom, and for every i\in V(\mathbb{L})=[n] , let \mathbb{L}_{i}=(A_{i},B_{i}) be an (r_{i},s_{i}) -loom. Let (C,D)=\mathbb{L}[\mathbb{L}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{L}_{n}] , and suppose it is a (c,d) -loom. If s_{i_{1}}=\cdots=s_{i_{p}} for \{i_{1},\cdots,i_{p}\}\in A and \nu(A)=q , then \nu(C)=d if and only if \nu(A_{i})=s_{i} for each i .




Proof.



For the “if” part, let a_{1},\dots,a_{q} be a matching in A and e_{i,1},\dots,e_{i,s_{i}} be a matching in A_{i} . Then \cup_{i\in a_{\ell}}e_{i,j} for j=1,\dots,s_{i} , \ell=1,\dots,q is a matching of size d in C .




For the “only if” part, by Lemma 1.10, assume that a_{1},\dots,a_{d} is a perfect matching in C . Then a_{1}\cap V(A_{i}),\dots,a_{d}\cap V(A_{i}) form a perfect matching of A_{i} (ignoring sets a_{j}\cap V(A_{i}) that are empty),
so by Lemma 1.10 their matching number is s_{i} .
∎




Corollary 5.9.



Let \mathbb{L}=\mathbb{L}_{1}\boxtimes_{1}\mathbb{L}_{2}=(C,D) for \mathbb{L}_{1}=(A_{1},B_{1}) and \mathbb{L}_{2}=(A_{2},B_{2}) . Then C has a perfect matching if and only if A_{1} and A_{2} both have perfect matchings. Also, D has a perfect matching if and only if both B_{1} and B_{2} have a perfect matching.





Next we claim that having a perfect fractional matching is preserved by the blow-up operation.



Theorem 5.10.



Given a (c,d) -loom (C,D)=\mathbb{L}[\mathbb{L}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{L}_{n}] for \mathbb{L}=(A,B),\mathbb{L}_{i}=(A_{i},B_{i}) , where (A,B) is (p,q) -loom and \mathbb{L}_{i} are (r_{i},s_{i}) -looms. If s_{i_{1}}=\cdots=s_{i_{p}} for \{i_{1},\cdots,i_{p}\}\in A , and
 \nu^{*}(A)=q,\nu^{*}(A_{i})=s_{i} for each i then \nu^{*}(C)=d .




Proof.



By Lemma 1.12, there exists
a perfect fractional matching f:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0} of  A , and w:A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0} a perfect fractional matching of each A_{i} . Note that
 w_{i}:=\sum_{e\in A_{i}}w(e)=s_{i} .
For each e=e_{i_{1}}\cup\cdots\cup e_{i_{p}}\in C , where a=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}\}\in A and e_{i_{\ell}}\in A_{i_{\ell}} , let g(e):=f(a)\prod_{\ell=1}^{p}w_{i_{\ell}}/s(a)^{p-1} , where s(a):=s_{i_{1}}=\cdots=s_{i_{p}} . We claim that g is a perfect fractional matching of C , which by Lemma 1.12 implies \nu^{*}(C)=d .




It is enough to show that for every v\in V(C) , \sum_{e\in C:v\in e}g(e)=1 . Indeed, assume v\in V(A_{i}) . Then





 \displaystyle\sum_{e\in C:v\in e}g(e) 



 \displaystyle= 
 \displaystyle\sum_{a=\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{p}\}\in A:i\in a}\sum_{e_{1}\in A_{i}:v\in e_{1}}\sum_{e_{2}\in A_{i_{2}}}\cdots\sum_{e_{p}\in A_{i_{p}}}g(e_{1}\cup\cdots\cup e_{p}) 



 \displaystyle= 
 \displaystyle\sum_{a=\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{p}\}\in A:i\in a}\sum_{e_{1}\in A_{i}:v\in e_{1}}\sum_{e_{2}\in A_{i_{2}}}\cdots\sum_{e_{p}\in A_{i_{p}}}f(\{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}\})w_{1}\cdots w_{p}/s(a)^{p-1} 



 \displaystyle= 
 \displaystyle\sum_{a=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}\}\in A:i\in a}f(\{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}\})\sum_{e_{1}\in A_{i}:v\in e_{1}}w_{1}\sum_{e_{2}\in A_{i_{2}}}w_{2}\cdots\sum_{e_{p}\in A_{i_{p}}}w_{p}/s(a)^{p-1} 



 \displaystyle= 
 \displaystyle 1\cdot 1\cdot s(a)^{p-1}/s(a)^{p-1}=1, 



which completes the proof.
∎




Corollary 5.11.



If \mathbb{L},\mathbb{L}_{1},\cdots,\mathbb{L}_{n} satisfy the assumptions of Theorem 5.10 and Conjecture 4.2, then \mathbb{L}[\mathbb{L}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{L}_{n}] satisfies Conjecture 4.2. In particular, if the (r_{1},s) -loom \mathbb{L}_{1} and the (r_{2},s) -loom L_{2} satisfy Conjecture 4.2, so does \mathbb{L}_{1}\boxtimes_{1}\mathbb{L}_{2} .







6. Stars versus perfect matchings




For a graph G let PM(G) be the set of perfect matchings in the graph G and ST(G)=\{star_{G}(v)\mid v\in V(G)\} - both hypergraphs having E(G) as their ground set. For an s -regular graph  G on an even number n of vertices, let r=\frac{n}{2} . Then A=PM(G) and B=ST(G) are r -uniform and s -uniform, respectively. They are orthogonal, and A=C_{r}(B) . Let \mathbb{L}(G)=(A,B) . Note that despite the notation, this is not necessarily a loom, because possibly B\neq C_{s}(A) .



Examples 6.1.



(1) 



For G=K_{n} with even n\geqslant 6 , the pair (A,B) as above is a loom. (See Theorem 6.2 below.)

(2) 



If G=K_{n,n} , then \mathbb{L}(G) is isomorphic to \mathbb{L}_{n,n} from  Example 1.6 (4).






Theorem 6.2.



For n\geqslant 6 even, \mathbb{L}(K_{n}) is an (\frac{n}{2},n-1) -loom.




Proof.



Let A=PM(K_{n}) and B=ST(K_{n}) .
It is routine to check that A and B are orthogonal and C_{\frac{n}{2}}(B)=A . It remains to show that \tau(A)=n-1 and C_{n-1}(A)=B .
By Tutte’s theorem it suffices to show that after removing a non-star set \Gamma of n-1 edges, there is no S\subseteq V(G) such that the number t of odd components of G-\Gamma in V\setminus S satisfies t>|S| .




Suppose there exist such \Gamma and S violating this claim. Let s=|S| . The even-ness of n and the inequality t>s imply that




 t\geqslant s+2. 



Assume first that S=\emptyset . Then t\geqslant 2 , and if one of the components of G-\Gamma is of size  p , then |\Gamma|\geqslant p(n-p) . The assumption that \Gamma is not a star implies that p(n-p)>n-1=|\Gamma| (here we used the condition n\geqslant 6 ), a contradiction.




Assume next that S\neq\emptyset .
Let 1\leqslant c_{1}\leqslant\dots\leqslant c_{t} be the sizes of the odd components of G-\Gamma in V\setminus S . Since s+\sum_{i=1}^{t}c_{i}\leqslant n , we have s+2\leqslant t\leqslant n-s and then




 s\leqslant\frac{n-2}{2}. 



The number of removed edges is at least \binom{n-s}{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{t}\binom{c_{i}}{2} . Note that
 \sum_{i=1}^{t}c_{i}\leqslant n-s and c_{i}\geqslant 1 , we have \sum_{i=1}^{t}\binom{c_{i}}{2}\leqslant\binom{n-s-(t-1)}{2} (which can easily be prove by induction on t ) so that




 \displaystyle n-1 
 \displaystyle\geqslant\binom{n-s}{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{t}\binom{c_{i}}{2}\geqslant\binom{n-s}{2}-\binom{n-s-(t-1)}{2} 




 \displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(t-1)(2n-2s-t). 






Note that \frac{1}{2}(t-1)(2n-2s-t) attains minimum -\frac{3}{2}s^{2}+(n-\frac{5}{2})s+n-1 at t=s+2 for s+2\leqslant t\leqslant n-s . Therefore we should have




 n-1\geqslant-\frac{3}{2}s^{2}+(n-\frac{5}{2})s+n-1. 



Then the minimum of the right-hand side is




 \min\Big(2n-5,\quad\frac{(n-2)(n-4)}{8}+n-1\Big) 



at s=1 or \frac{n-2}{2} . But it is impossible to have




 n-1\geqslant\min\Big(2n-5,\quad\frac{(n-2)(n-4)}{8}+n-1\Big), 



assuming n\geqslant 6 , which is a contradiction.
∎





If G is an s -regular graph on n vertices and ST(G)\subsetneqq C_{s}(PM(G)) , namely \mathbb{L}(G) is not a loom, we may hope that the pair
 (PM(G),C_{s}(PM(G))) is a loom. For this to be true, we need that C_{s}(PM(G))\perp PM(G) . This sometimes happens and sometimes not, as shown by the following two examples.



Examples 6.3.



(1) 



Let P be the Petersen graph, A=PM(P),B=C_{3}(PM(P)) . The hypergraph B includes, besides the stars, five triples that are themselves perfect matchings.
In the drawing of Petersen’s graph, such a triple consists of two parallel edges, one on the inner pentagon and one on the outer, and an edge perpendicular to both, connecting the two pentagons. Then (A,B) is a (5,3) -loom in which B has a perfect matching, and A does not (since the chromatic index of the Petersen graph is 4).

(2) 



Let G be the 3 -regular graph obtained from Petersen’s graph, by blowing up (in the graph theory sense. Not to confuse with the blow-ups defined in Section 5.2) every vertex by a triangle. The set of 3 edges incident with every triangle (=blown-up vertex) is obviously a cover for A , and on the other hand, it is contained in a perfect matching of the blow-up graph.







The following theorem states that any loom of the form (PM(G),C_{s}(PM(G))) for some s -regular graph G satisfies Conjecture 4.2.



Theorem 6.4.



For any s -regular graph, if \Big(PM(G),C_{s}(PM(G))\Big) is an (r,s) -loom, then both its components, PM(G) and C_{s}(PM(G)) , have perfect fractional matchings.





 ST(G) has a perfect fractional matching, obtained by putting weight \frac{1}{2} on every star. Since C_{s}(PM(G))\supseteq ST(G) , this is also a perfect fractional matching of  C_{s}(PM(G)) .




The existence of a perfect fractional matching of PM(G) is given by the following theorem.



Theorem 6.5.



Let G be an s -regular graph on n vertices and let A=PM(G) . If \tau(A)\geqslant s-1 then \nu^{*}(A)=s .





The fractional edge-chromatic number \chi_{e}^{*}(G) (sometimes denoted by \chi_{f}^{\prime}(G) ) of a graph G is the minimum total weight of a fractional edge-coloring of G , i.e., \min\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}(G)}f(M) over all non-negative real functions f on \mathcal{M}(G) satisfying




 \sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}(G):e\in M}f(M)\geqslant 1 



for every e\in E(G) .




Given U\subseteq V(G) , let t(U)=\frac{2|E(G[U])|}{|U|-1} . Let




 t(G)=\max_{U\subseteq V(G):\text{$|U|$ is odd}}t(U). 






We use the following result [12, Theorem 4.2.1].



Lemma 6.6.






 \chi_{e}^{*}(G)=max(\Delta(G),t(G)). 






Proof of Theorem 6.5.



To prove \nu^{*}(A)=s , we claim that it is enough to show that \chi_{e}^{*}(G)=s .
Indeed, let f:\mathcal{M}(G)\to[0,1] be a fractional edge coloring of G with \sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}(G)}f(M)=s . We may assume that \sum_{M\in\mathcal{M(G)}:e\in M}f(M)=1 for any e\in E(G) as \mathcal{M}(G) is a simplicial complex.
Then




 \displaystyle|V(G)|s 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V(G)}\sum_{e\in E(G):v\in e}\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}:e\in M}f(M) 




 \displaystyle=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{e:e\in M}\sum_{v:v\in e}f(M)=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}}2|M|f(M) 



Note that |M|\leqslant|V(G)|/2 , and \sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}}f(M)=s . This implies that M is a perfect matching of G whenever f(M)>0 . Therefore this f forms a fractional matching of A of total weight  s .




To prove \chi_{e}^{*}(G)=s , note that for any U\subseteq V(G) of odd size, \emptyset\subsetneqq U\subsetneqq V(G) as |V(G)| is even, therefore the cut E(U,V(G)\setminus U) is a cover of A . Since |E(U,V(G)\setminus U)|=s|U|-2|E(G[U])| has different parity from s-1 and since \tau(A)\geqslant s-1 , we have |E(U,V(G)\setminus U)|\geqslant s . Then |E(G[U])|\leqslant\frac{s|U|-s}{2} , implying




 t(U)=\frac{2|E(G[U])|}{|U|-1}\leqslant\frac{2\frac{s(|U|-1)}{2}}{|U|-1}=s. 



Since  \Delta(G)=s ,
then Lemma 6.6 implies that \chi_{e}^{*}(G)=\max(\Delta(G),t(G))=s .
∎





This concludes the proof of Theorem 6.5, and hence also of Theorem 6.4.




So far, in all our examples at least one component of the looms has a perfect (integral) matching.
In the following example, neither component of the loom has a perfect matching.



Example 6.7.



Let \mathbb{L}_{1}=(A_{1},B_{1})=(ST(K_{10}),PM(K_{10})) , which is a (9,5) -loom by Theorem 6.2 and \nu(A_{1})=1<5 . Let \mathbb{L}_{2}=(A_{2},B_{2})=(PM(K_{6}),ST(K_{6})) , which is a (3,5) -loom by Theorem 6.2 and \nu(B_{2})=1<3 . Then \mathbb{L}=(A,B)=\mathbb{L}_{1}\boxtimes_{1}\mathbb{L}_{2} is a (12,5) -loom with no perfect matching in either component by Theorem 5.8.






7. (r,2) -looms 




An (r,1) -loom is just \mathbb{V}_{r} , from Example 1.6 (2), meaning that if it is non-trivial then it is decomposable. As we shall see, this is not true for (r,s) -looms when r,s\geqslant 3 , but it is true for s=2 .



Theorem 7.1.



Any (r,2) -loom \mathbb{L}=(A,B) is decomposable.





By Lemma 5.2 it suffices to show that at least one of A,B is disconnected. This will follow from:



Lemma 7.2.



In an (r,2) -loom \mathbb{L}=(A,B) , every connected component D of B is a complete bipartite graph with two sides of the same size.





This already proves the theorem: in an (r,2) -loom \mathbb{L}=(A,B) , if B is connected, then A , which consists of the two sides of the complete bipartite graph, is disconnected.



Proof of Lemma 7.2.



As to bipartiteness, suppose, for contradiction, that B contains an odd cycle O . Then any edge of A , being a cover of B , must contain both endpoints of some edge of O , contradicting the fact that A\perp B .
To prove that D is complete, it suffices to show that if N_{B}(x)\cap N_{B}(y)\neq\emptyset then N_{B}(x)=N_{B}(y) . So, assume for negation that u\in N_{B}(x)\cap N_{B}(y) and v\in N_{B}(x)\setminus N_{B}(y) . Then any a\in A not containing y must contain u , hence it avoids x , and to cover xv it must contain v . Thus yv is a cover of A , and being minimum it must belong to B , as desired.
Therefore each connected component of B is a complete bipartite graph.




Let D_{i},~1\leqslant i\leqslant m be the connected components of B , and let X_{i,j},~~j=1,2 be the sides of D_{i} , where |X_{i,1}|\leqslant|X_{i,2}| . Then \tau(B)=\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m}|X_{i,1}| . Remember that \tau(B)=r .




We claim that




 |X_{i,2}|=|X_{i,1}| 



for all 1\leqslant i\leqslant m .
To prove the claim, assume, for contradiction, that
 |X_{i,2}|>|X_{i,1}| for some i . Let a be an edge of A meeting
 X_{i,2} . By the orthogonality condition a\cap X_{i,1}=\emptyset , and since it is a cover of B , it contains X_{i,2} . For the same reason a contains a side from each D_{i} , so |a|>r , contradicting the fact that A is r -uniform.
This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎





The above discussion provides a characterization of all (r,2) -looms:



Theorem 7.3.



For an (r,2) -loom (A,B) , there exist pairs of sets (X_{i,1},X_{i,2})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant t} satisfying:

• 



All X_{i,j} are pairwise disjoint, |X_{i,1}|=|X_{i,2}|=:q_{i} ,

• 



 \sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant t}q_{i}=r ,

• 



 A=\{\bigcup X_{i,\sigma(i)}\mid\sigma\in[2]^{[t]}\} and B=\bigcup_{1\leqslant i\leqslant t}\{uv\mid u\in X_{i,1},v\in X_{i,2}\} .







Here [2]^{[t]} is the set of functions from [t] to [2] .




Let




 \displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{i} 
 \displaystyle=\Big(\{X_{i,1},X_{i,2}\},\{uv\mid u\in X_{i,1},v\in X_{i,2}\}\Big) 




 \displaystyle=\Big(\{X_{i,1}\},\big\{\{x\}\big\}_{x\in X_{i,1}}\Big)\boxtimes_{2}\Big(\{X_{i,2}\},\big\{\{y\}\big\}_{y\in X_{i,2}}\Big) 




 \displaystyle=\mathbb{V}_{q_{i}}\boxtimes_{2}\mathbb{V}_{q_{i}}. 






Then \mathbb{L}=\mathbb{L}_{1}\boxtimes_{1}\cdots\boxtimes_{1}\mathbb{L}_{t}=(\mathbb{V}_{q_{1}}\boxtimes_{2}\mathbb{V}_{q_{1}})\boxtimes_{1}\cdots\boxtimes_{1}(\mathbb{V}_{q_{t}}\boxtimes_{2}\mathbb{V}_{q_{t}}) .




Theorem 7.3 implies that \nu(A)=2 and \nu(B)=r , thus \nu^{*}(A)=2 and \nu^{*}(B)=r . Hence Conjecture 4.2 is true when s=2 .





8. (3,3) -looms



Theorem 8.1.



Let \mathbb{L}=(A,B) be a (3,3) -loom on the vertex set V .
Then

(1) 



 |V|=9 ,

(2) 



 \nu(A)=\nu(B)=3 ,

(3) 



For any pair e,f of disjoint edges in A , V\setminus(e\cup f)\in A .






Proof.


Claim 8.1.1.



The three conditions are equivalent.




Proof of the claim.



The implication (3) \Rightarrow (2) follows from Lemma 1.8.




(1) \Rightarrow  (3): suppose |V|=9 . For any two disjoint edges e,f\in A and any g\in B , we have |g\cap e|=|g\cap f|=|g\cap(V\setminus(e\cup f))|=1 , which implies V\setminus(e\cup f)\in C_{3}(B)=A so that \nu(A)=3 .




(2) \Rightarrow (1): let e,f,g be three disjoint edges in A . If there exists a vertex v\not\in e\cup f\cup g , then an edge h\in B containing v cannot cover e,f,g , contradicting the assumption that B=C_{3}(A) .
∎





Back to the proof of the theorem, let k=\max_{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\in A}|\cup_{i=1}^{3}e_{i}| . (2) follows from the next result.



Claim 8.1.2.



 k=9 .




Proof of the claim.



By Lemma 1.8 there are two disjoint edges, e_{1},e_{2} belonging to A . By the orthogonality condition, no edge of B is contained in e_{1}\cup e_{2} , so
 V\setminus(e_{1}\cup e_{2}) is a cover for B , and hence it is of size at least \tau(B)=3 . This shows that |V|\geqslant 9 .
Adding to e_{1},e_{2} any edge meeting V\setminus(e_{1}\cup e_{2}) proves k>6 .




Next we exclude the possibility k=8 .
 k=8 means that there exist edges e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\in A such that e_{1}\cap e_{2}=e_{2}\cap e_{3}=\emptyset and |e_{1}\cap e_{3}|=1 , say e_{1}\cap e_{3}=\{x\} . Take y\in V\setminus(e_{1}\cup e_{2}\cup e_{3}) . Then star_{B}(y)\subseteq star_{B}(x) , since for an edge b\in B containing y to meet all edges e_{1},e_{2},e_{3} it must contain x . By Lemma 1.9 star_{B}(x)=star_{B}(y) . Since e_{3} is a cover of B , we have that (e_{3}\setminus\{x\})\cup\{y\} is also a cover of B , so it is in A . Together with e_{1} and e_{2} it contains 9 vertices, contrary to the assumption that k=8 .




It remains to exclude the case k=7 . Assuming for negation that this is the case, since \nu(A),\nu(B)\geqslant 2 , we can number the vertices so that 123,456\in A , 714,825\in B . Let e be an edge of A containing 9 . Since k=7 , the edge e contains vertices x\in\{2,5\} and y\in\{1,4\} . Since star_{A}(x)\neq star_{A}(9) and star_{A}(y)\neq star_{A}(9) , which implies star_{A}(9)\nsubseteq star_{A}(x) and star_{A}(9)\nsubseteq star_{A}(y) by Lemma 1.9 so that \{9\}\cup\{2,5\}\setminus\{x\} and \{9\}\cup\{1,4\}\setminus\{y\} are each contained in some edge of  A . On the other hand, any f\in B containing 9 must intersect the edges 123 and 456 of  A , but such  f cannot include any element of \{1,2,4,5\} by the orthogonality assumption. Therefore 936\in B and we have \nu(B)=3 and |V|=k=9 , a contradiction.




This completes the proof of Claim 8.1.2.
∎





By Claim 8.1.1 this also proves the theorem.
∎





In particular, this entails that in a (3,3) -loom (A,B) there holds \nu^{*}(A)=\nu(A)=\nu^{*}(B)=\nu(B)=3 .



Corollary 8.2.



Conjecture 4.2 is true for r=s=3 , and hence Conjecture 1.2 is true for r=3 .





The next theorem characterizes (3,3) -looms. If a (3,3) -loom \mathbb{L}=(A,B) is decomposable, then
 (A,B)=(A_{1},B_{1})\boxtimes_{1}(A_{2},B_{2}) for a (2,3) -loom (A_{1},B_{1}) and a (1,3) -loom (A_{2},B_{2}) , whose structure we know by Theorem 7.3. So we only need to know how indecomposable (3,3) -looms look. It turns out that there are just two such looms.



Theorem 8.3.



The only indecomposable (3,3) -looms are  \mathbb{L}_{3,3} in Example 1.6 (4) and  \mathbb{V}_{3,3} in Example 5.5.




Proof.



Let \mathbb{L}=(A,B) be an indecomposable (3,3) -loom. Let M,N be perfect matchings in A and in B , respectively.
Number the vertices so that “columns” M=\{147,258,369\} and N=\{123,456,789\} 
and view them as the sets of rows (resp. columns) of a 3\times 3 grid. Since A is connected, without loss of generality, we may assume that at least one of the edges of 159 or 158 is in A .




(1) 



 159\in A . By Lemma 1.8, there exists an edge of A disjoint from 159 .

(a) 



Suppose there exists in A another “permutation” edge (whose vertices are in distinct columns and rows), say 267 . Then by Lemma 8.1, [9]\setminus(159\cup 267)=348 is in A .
By renaming the vertices (swap 1 with 7, 5 with 8, and 3 with 6), the current known six edges of A become “rows” and “columns” and the three edges of B become “even permutation” edges. Since B is also connected and the edges of B are orthogonal to those in A , then the remaining edges of B must be “odd permutation” edges. By Lemma 1.8 three odd permutation edges are in B . Therefore, in this case, the loom is isomorphic to \mathbb{L}_{3,3} in Example 1.6 (4).

(b) 



Suppose there is no permutation edge of A other than 159 . By Lemma 1.8, there exists an edge of A disjoint from 159 . Without loss of generality, 247\in A .
Then 368=[9]\setminus(159\cup 247)\in A . Also 158=[9]\setminus(247\cup 369)\in A and 259=[9]\setminus(147\cup 368)\in A . We now have eight edges in A , and we turn to B . Since B is connected, by the orthogonality of A and B and by the covering property, the only possible edges to connect 123 with 456 or 789 are 126 , or 345 , or 357 .

(i) 



If 357\in B , then by Lemma 1.8 and Theorem 8.1, there is an edge e of B containing 6 and being disjoint from 357 . By the orthogonality, this edge cannot contain 8 or 9 as 369,368\in A . Since e is disjoint from 357 it does not contain 5 , so to cover 259\in A , it must contain 2 . By the orthogonality, e cannot contain 4 or 7 , as 247\in A . Since e is disjoint from 357 , to cover 147\in A , it must be 126 . Then 489=[9]\setminus(357\cup 126)\in B . This implies 345=[9]\setminus(126\cup 789)\in B and 756=[9]\setminus(489\cup 123)\in B . We now have eight edges of B , and it is easy to check that (A,B) is isomorphic to  \mathbb{V}_{3,3} in Example 5.5.

(ii) 



Note that 126\cup 345\cup 789=[9] . If one of 126 and 345 is in B , then the other is also in B . The edge 789 is connected to 123 or 456 in B , symmetrically, one of 756 or 489 or 357 is in B . If 357 is in  B , then we have done by the above case. We may assume that both 756 and 489 are in B (as 756\cup 489\cup 123=[9] ). Then 357=[9]\setminus(126\cup 489)\in B and we are done.





(2) 



Suppose 158 is in A and no permutation edge (that intersects each of 147,258,369 at one vertex) is in A .
Then 247=[9]\setminus(158\cup 369) is in A . Since A is connected, we may assume there is an edge of A contained in 258\cup 369 .

(a) 



 269 or 358 is in A . Note that 147\cup 269\cup 358=[9] . If one of them is in A , then so is the other. Then 169=[9]\setminus(247\cup 358) is in A and then 347=[9]\setminus(169\cup 258) is in A .
Let us now go back to B . By assumption it is connected, and hence 123 should be connected to 456 or to 789 . But for any x\in 123 and y\in 456\cup 789 , xy is contained in some edge of A , contradicting the orthogonality of A and B .

(b) 



 259 and 368=[9]\setminus(259\cup 147) are in A . Then 159=[9]\setminus(247\cup 368) is in A , a contradiction to the assumption that no permutation edge is in A .





We complete the proof of the theorem.
∎
