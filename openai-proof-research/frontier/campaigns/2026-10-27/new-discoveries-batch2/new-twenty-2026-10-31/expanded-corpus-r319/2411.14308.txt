Abstract.
 

In this paper we establish some new results similar to Lagrange’s four-square theorem.
For example, we prove that any integer n>1 can be written as
 w(5w+1)/2+x(5x+1)/2+y(5y+1)/2+z(5z+1)/2 with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z} .
 

Let a and b be integers with a>0 , b>-a and \gcd(a,b)=1 . When 2\nmid ab , we show that
any sufficiently large integer can be written as
 




 \frac{w(aw+b)}{2}+\frac{x(ax+b)}{2}+\frac{y(ay+b)}{2}+\frac{z(az+b)}{2} 


 

with w,x,y,z nonnegative integers. When 2\mid a and 2\nmid b , we prove that any sufficiently large integer can be written as
 




 w(aw+b)+x(ax+b)+y(ay+b)+z(az+b) 


 

with w,x,y,z nonnegative integers.
 



Key words and phrases: Additive base, quadratic polynomial, polygonal number.

1. Introduction




For any integer m\geqslant 3 , the polygonal numbers of order m (or m -gonal numbers) are the nonnegative integers




 p_{m}(n)=(m-2)\binom{n}{2}+n=\frac{n((m-2)n-(m-4))}{2}\ \ (n=0,1,2,\ldots). 



Those




 \bar{p}_{m}(n)=p_{m}(-n)=\frac{n((m-2)n+(m-4))}{2}\ \ (n=0,1,2,\ldots) 



are called second m -gonal numbers, and those p_{m}(x) with x\in\mathbb{Z} 
are called generalized m -gonal numbers. Note that




 \displaystyle p_{3}(x)=\frac{x(x+1)}{2},\ p_{4}(x)=x^{2},\ p_{5}(x)=\frac{x(3x-1)}{2}, 



 \displaystyle p_{6}(x)=x(2x-1)=p_{3}(2x-1),\ p_{7}(x)=\frac{x(5x-3)}{2},\ p_{8}(x)=x(3x-2). 






In 1638 Fermat claimed that each n\in\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\} is a sum of m m -gonal numbers;
this was confirmed by
Lagrange, Gauss and Cauchy for the cases m=4 , m=3 , and m\geqslant 5 , respectively (cf. [5, 6]).
Lagrange’s four-square theorem states that any n\in\mathbb{N} can be written as w^{2}+x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2} 
with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} .
In 1830 Legendre refined Cauchy’s polygonal number theorem by showing that for any integer m\geqslant 5 
we can write each integer n\geqslant 28(m-2)^{3} as




 p_{m}(w)+p_{m}(x)+p_{m}(y)+p_{m}(z)+\delta_{m}(n), 



where w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} , \delta_{m}(n)=0 if 2\nmid m , and \delta_{m}(n)\in\{0,1\} if 2\mid m .
M. B. Nathanson ([5] and [6, p. 33]) simplified the proofs of Cauchy’s and Legendre’s theorems.
As a supplement to Legendre’s theorem, X.-Z. Meng and the author [4] prove that
for any integer m\geqslant 6 with m\equiv 2\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 4) each integer
 n\geqslant 28(m-2)^{3} can be expressed as
 p_{m}(w)+p_{m}(x)+p_{m}(y)+p_{m}(z) with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} , and that for any integer m\geqslant 8 with 4\mid m 
there are infinitely many positive integers not of the form
 p_{m}(w)+p_{m}(x)+p_{m}(y)+p_{m}(z) with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} .




In additive number theory, a subset A of \mathbb{N} is called a base of order n if




 nA=\{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}:\ a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in A\} 



coincides with \mathbb{N} . When \mathbb{N}\setminus nA is finite, we call A an asymptotic base of order n .
In this terminology, for each integer m\geqslant 3 the set \{p_{m}(n):\ n\in\mathbb{N}\} 
is a base of order m , and \{p_{m}(n):\ n\in\mathbb{N}\} is an asymptotic base of order four if and only if
 m=4 or 4\nmid m .




In 1994 R. K. Guy [1] observed that each n\in\mathbb{N} is a sum of three generalized pentagonal numbers
(see also the comments around [7, (1.4)]), that is, the set \{p_{5}(x):\ x\in\mathbb{Z}\} is a base of order three.
In 2016 the author [8] proved that \{p_{8}(x):\ x\in\mathbb{Z}\} 
is a base of order four; moreover, each n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\} 
can be written a sum of four generalized octagonal numbers one of which is odd.
This is quite similar to Lagrange’s four-square theorem.
For any integer m\geqslant 5 , J. Li and H. Wang [3] used Jacobi forms to obtain a formula for




 R_{m}(n)=|\{(w,x,y,z)\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}:\ p_{m}(w)+p_{m}(x)+p_{m}(y)+p_{m}(z)=n\}| 



in terms of Hurwitz class numbers. Consequently,
for any integer n\geqslant(m-2)^{3}/8+m/2+1 ,
we have R_{m}(n)>0 if m or n is odd.




For any a\in\mathbb{Z}^{+} and b\in\mathbb{Z} with b>-a and a\equiv b\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 2) , we clearly have




 \left\{\frac{x(ax+b)}{2}:\ x\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{N}. 



In [9, 10] the author investigated those tuples (a,b,c,d,e,f)\in\mathbb{Z}^{6} 
with a,c,e\in\mathbb{Z}^{+} and a+b,c+d,e+f\in 2\mathbb{Z}^{+} 
such that each n\in\mathbb{N} can be written as




 \frac{x(ax+b)}{2}+\frac{y(cy+d)}{2}+\frac{z(ez+f)}{2} 



with x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} . For example, the author [10] conjectured that
each n\in\mathbb{N} can be written as x(x+1)/2+y(3y+1)/2+z(5z+1)/2 with x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} , and proved that




 \left\{\frac{x(x+1)}{2}+\frac{y(3y+1)}{2}+\frac{z(5z+1)}{2}:\ x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}=\mathbb{N}. 






Motivated by the above work, we establish some new results
similar to Lagrange’s four-square theorem.



Theorem 1.1.



Let a\in\mathbb{Z}^{+} and b\in\mathbb{Z} with b>-a , 2\nmid ab and \gcd(a,b)=1 .
Then the set \{x(ax+b)/2:\ x\in\mathbb{N}\} is an asymptotic base of order four. Moreover, for any
 n\in\mathbb{N} with




 n>2a(7a(a-1)+1)+(7a-2)b+(2a(2a-1)+b)\sqrt{6(2a(a-1)+b)}, 

(1.1)


there are w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} such that




 n=\frac{w(aw+b)}{2}+\frac{x(ax+b)}{2}+\frac{y(ay+b)}{2}+\frac{z(az+b)}{2}. 







Via Theorem 1.1 and some easy numerical computations, we obtain the following corollary
involving second pentagonal numbers and second heptagonal numbers.



Corollary 1.1.



We have





 \displaystyle\left\{\bar{p}_{5}(w)+\bar{p}_{5}(x)+\bar{p}_{5}(y)+\bar{p}_{5}(z):\ w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N}\right\} 



 \displaystyle= 
 \displaystyle\ \mathbb{N}\setminus\{1,3,5,10,12,20,25,27,38,53,65,153,165\}. 



Also,
any integer n\geqslant 786 can be written as
 \bar{p}_{7}(w)+\bar{p}_{7}(x)+\bar{p}_{7}(y)+\bar{p}_{7}(z) 
with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} .




Remark 1.1.



By Legendre’s refinement of Cauchy’s polygonal number theorem,
any integer n\geqslant 28\times(5-2)^{3}=756 is a sum of four pentagonal numbers,
and integer n\geqslant 28\times(7-2)^{3}=3500 is a sum of four heptagonal numbers.
Combining this with direct checks for small numbers, we find that




 \displaystyle\left\{p_{5}(w)+p_{5}(x)+p_{5}(y)+p_{5}(z):\ w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N}\right\}=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{9,21,31,43,55,89\} 



and




 \{p_{7}(w)+p_{7}(x)+p_{7}(y)+p_{7}(z):\ w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N}\}\supseteq\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\ n\geqslant 1004\}. 







Theorem 1.1 with (a,b)=(5,\pm 1) has the following consequence.



Corollary 1.2.



Each integer n>1 can be written as




 \frac{w(5w+1)}{2}+\frac{x(5x+1)}{2}+\frac{y(5y+1)}{2}+\frac{z(5z+1)}{2} 



with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z} . Moreover, any integer n\geqslant 776 can be written as




 \frac{w(5w+1)}{2}+\frac{x(5x+1)}{2}+\frac{y(5y+1)}{2}+\frac{z(5z+1)}{2} 



with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} , and
any integer n\geqslant 884 can be written as




 \frac{w(5w-1)}{2}+\frac{x(5x-1)}{2}+\frac{y(5y-1)}{2}+\frac{z(5z-1)}{2} 



with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} .




Remark 1.2.



Via a computer we can easily verify that the set




 \left\{\frac{w(5w+1)}{2}+\frac{x(5x+1)}{2}+\frac{y(5y+1)}{2}+\frac{z(5z+1)}{2}:\ w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}\right\} 



contains 2,3,\ldots,775. 
So the first assertion in Corollary 1.2 follows from the second assertion in the corollary.





For convenience, for a\in\mathbb{Z}^{+} and b\in\mathbb{Z} with a\equiv b\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 2) , we set




 S_{a,b}=\left\{\frac{w(aw+b)}{2}+\frac{x(ax+b)}{2}+\frac{y(ay+b)}{2}+\frac{z(az+b)}{2}:\ w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}. 






Via Theorem 1.1 and some easy computations via a computer, we have the following corollary.



Corollary 1.3.



We have




 S_{7,1}=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1,2,5\},\ S_{7,3}=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1,3,25\},\ S_{7,5}=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{5,23\}. 



Also,




 \displaystyle S_{9,1} 
 \displaystyle=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1,2,3,6,7,11,35,37\}, 



 \displaystyle S_{9,5} 
 \displaystyle=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1,3,5,10,12,31,67\}, 



 \displaystyle S_{9,7} 
 \displaystyle=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{5,6,7,15,29,65\}. 







Now we state our second theorem.



Theorem 1.2.



Let a\in\mathbb{Z}^{+} and b\in\mathbb{Z} with b>-a , 2\mid a and \gcd(a,b)=1 .
Then the set \{x(ax+b):\ x\in\mathbb{N}\} is an asymptotic base of order four. Moreover, if n 
is an odd integer greater than




 4a(7a(a-1)+1)+2(7a-2)b+2(2a(2a-1)+b)\sqrt{6(2a(a-1)+b)}, 



or n is an even integer with n/4 greater than




 28a^{3}-14a^{2}+a+(7a-1)b+\left(2a(4a-1)+b\right)\sqrt{3(2a(2a-1)+b)}, 



then we can write n as




 w(aw+b)+x(ax+b)+y(ay+b)+z(az+b) 



with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} .





For a\in\mathbb{Z}^{+} and b\in\mathbb{Z} , we define




 T_{a,b}=S_{2a,2b}=\{w(aw+b)+x(ax+b)+y(ay+b)+z(az+b):\ w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}\}. 





Corollary 1.4.



We have




 T_{4,1}=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1,2,4,7,30\},\ \max(\mathbb{N}\setminus T_{6,1})=168,\ \max(\mathbb{N}\setminus T_{6,5})=182. 



Moreover, any integer n\geqslant 2283 can be written as
 w(4w+1)+x(4x+1)+y(4y+1)+z(4z+1) with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} , and each integer n>4500 can be written as
 w(4w-1)+x(4x-1)+y(4y-1)+z(4z-1) with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} .




Remark 1.3.



Meng and Sun [4, Corollary 1.1]
showed that T_{4,3}=\{p_{10}(w)+p_{10}(x)+p_{10}(y)+p_{10}(z):\ w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}\} 
coincides with \mathbb{N}\setminus\{5,6,26\} .





Now we present our third theorem.



Theorem 1.3.



Let a\in\mathbb{Z}^{+} and b\in\mathbb{Z} with b>-a , 2\mid b and \gcd(a,b)=1 . Then, for any integer n\not\equiv 0\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 4) greater than




 4a(7a(a-1)+1)+2(7a-2)b+2(2a(2a-1)+b)\sqrt{6(2a(a-1)+b)}, 



there are w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} such that




 n=w(aw+b)+x(ax+b)+y(ay+b)+z(az+b). 






Remark 1.4.



Let a\in\mathbb{Z}^{+} and b\in\mathbb{Z} with b>-a , 2\mid b and \gcd(a,b)=1 .
As a+b is odd, for each n\in\mathbb{N} either n or n-(a+b) is not divisible by 4 .
So Theorem 1.3 implies that the set \{x(ax+b):\ x\in\mathbb{N}\} is an asymptotic base of order five.




Corollary 1.5.



We have




 \{n\in\mathbb{N}:\ 4\nmid n\ \text{and}\ n\not\in T_{5,2}\}=\{1,2,5,11,18\} 



and




 \{n\in\mathbb{N}:\ 4\nmid n\ \text{and}\ n\not\in T_{5,4}\}=\{5,6,7,17\}. 






Remark 1.5.



Sun [8] proved that T_{3,2}=\mathbb{N} . It seems that
both \mathbb{N}\setminus T_{5,2} and \mathbb{N}\setminus T_{5,4} contain infinitely many multiples of 4 .





In 2020 D. Krachun and the author [2] proved that




 \{2p_{5}(w)+p_{5}(x)+p_{5}(y)+p_{5}(z):\ w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N}\}=\mathbb{N}, 



which was previously conjectured by the author [8].
Motivated by this, we establish the following theorem.



Theorem 1.4.



Let a\in\mathbb{Z}^{+} and b\in\mathbb{Z} with b>-a and \gcd(a,5b)=1 .
Then, for any integer




 n\geqslant 550a(60a^{2}+b)+25(120a^{2}+b)\sqrt{2(60a^{2}+b)}. 

(1.2)


with 2\mid n if 2\nmid ab , there are w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} such that




 2w(aw+b)+x(ax+b)+y(ay+b)+z(az+b)=n. 

(1.3)





Remark 1.6.



We guess that \gcd(a,5b) in Theorem 1.4 can be replaced by \gcd(a,b) ,
but we are unable to prove this.





Theorem 1.4 in the case 2\nmid ab yields the following result.



Corollary 1.6.



Let a\in\mathbb{Z}^{+} and b\in\mathbb{Z} with b>-a , 2\nmid ab and \gcd(a,5b)=1 .
Then any integer




 \geqslant 275a(60a^{2}+b)+\frac{25}{2}(120a^{2}+b)\sqrt{2(60a^{2}+b)}. 



can be written as




 w(aw+b)+\frac{x(ax+b)}{2}+\frac{y(ay+b)}{2}+\frac{z(az+b)}{2} 

(1.4)


with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} .





In the case (a,b)=(3,1) , Corollary 1.6 yields the following concrete result.



Corollary 1.7.



We have





 \displaystyle\left\{2\bar{p}_{5}(w)+\bar{p}_{5}(x)+\bar{p}_{5}(y)+\bar{p}_{5}(z):\,w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N}\right\} 




 \displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1,3,5,12,27,50\}, 







Theorem 1.4 with (a,b)=(2,\pm 1) yields the following corollary.



Corollary 1.8.



We have




 \{2w(2w-1)+x(2x-1)+y(2y-1)+z(2z-1):\ w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N}\}=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{11,26,50\}. 



Also, any integer n\geqslant 90 can be written as 2w(2w+1)+x(2x+1)+y(2y+1)+z(2z+1) 
with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} .





Meng and Sun [4, Theorem 1.3] proved that
for each integer m\geqslant 5 , any integer n\geqslant 924(m-2)^{3} can be written as
 3p_{m}(w)+p_{m}(x)+p_{m}(y)+p_{m}(z) with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} . We extend this as follows.



Theorem 1.5.



Let a\in\mathbb{Z}^{+} and b\in\mathbb{Z} with b>-a and \gcd(a,b)=1 .
Let n\in\mathbb{N} with ab or n even. Suppose that




 \frac{n}{6}>66a^{2}(9a-1)+a+(33a-1)b+(2a(18a-1)+b)\sqrt{15(2a(9a-1)+b)}. 

(1.5)


Then there are w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} such that




 3w(aw+b)+x(ax+b)+y(ay+b)+z(az+b)=n. 







Theorem 1.5 in the case 2\nmid ab yields the following result.



Corollary 1.9.



Let a and b be odd integers with a>0 , b>-a and \gcd(a,b)=1 .
Then, any integer n with




 \frac{n}{3}>66a^{2}(9a-1)+(33a-1)b+(2a(18a-1)+b)\sqrt{15(2a(9a-1)+b)}. 

(1.6)


can be written as




 3\frac{w(aw+b)}{2}+\frac{x(ax+b)}{2}+\frac{y(ay+b)}{2}+\frac{z(az+b)}{2} 



with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} .





Applying Corollary 1.9 with some concrete odd integers a and b , we obtain the following corollary.



Corollary 1.10.



(i) We have




 \left\{3\bar{p}_{5}(w)+\bar{p}_{5}(x)+\bar{p}_{5}(y)+\bar{p}_{5}(z):\ w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N}\right\}=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1,3,5,18,31\}. 






(ii) Any integer n>1 can be written as




 3\frac{w(5w+1)}{2}+\frac{x(5x+1)}{2}+\frac{y(5y+1)}{2}+\frac{z(5z+1)}{2} 



with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z} . Moreover, each integer n>151 can be written as




 3\frac{w(5w-1)}{2}+\frac{x(5x-1)}{2}+\frac{y(5y-1)}{2}+\frac{z(5z-1)}{2} 



with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} , and any integer n>682 can be written as




 3\frac{w(5w+1)}{2}+\frac{x(5x+1)}{2}+\frac{y(5y+1)}{2}+\frac{z(5z+1)}{2} 



with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} .




(iii) 1,2,5,14 are the only natural numbers not of the form




 3\frac{w(7w+1)}{2}+\frac{x(7x+1)}{2}+\frac{y(7y+1)}{2}+\frac{z(7z+1)}{2}\ \ (w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}), 



and 1,3,14 are the only natural numbers not of the form




 3\frac{w(7w+3)}{2}+\frac{x(7x+3)}{2}+\frac{y(7y+3)}{2}+\frac{z(7z+3)}{2}\ \ (w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}). 






Remark 1.7.



Meng and Sun [4, Corollary 1.3] proved that





 \displaystyle\ \{3p_{7}(w)+p_{7}(x)+p_{7}(y)+p_{7}(z):w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N}\} 



 \displaystyle= 
 \displaystyle\ \mathbb{N}\setminus\{13,16,27,31,33,49,50,67,87,178,181,259\} 



and




 \{3p_{9}(w)+p_{9}(x)+p_{9}(y)+p_{9}(z):w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}\}=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{17\}. 







Theorem 1.5 with (a,b)=(4,\pm 1) yields the following corollary.



Corollary 1.11.



We have





 \displaystyle\{3w(4w+1)+x(4x+1)+y(4y+1)+z(4z+1):\ w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}\} 




 \displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1,2,4,7,16\}. 



Moreover, any integer n>1204 can be written as
 3w(4w-1)+x(4x-1)+y(4y-1)+z(4z-1) with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} , and

In contrast with Corollary 1.11, Meng and Sun [4, Corollary 1.3] proved that

2. Proofs of Theorems 1.1 and 1.3




The following lemma is essentially [4, Lemma 2.4].



Lemma 2.1.



Let c,d\in\mathbb{N} with c\equiv d\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 2) and 4\nmid c . If




 4c>d^{2}\ \text{and}\ 3c<d^{2}+2d+4, 

(2.1)


then there are w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} such that




 w^{2}+x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=c\ \text{and}\ w+x+y+z=d. 

(2.2)





Remark 2.1.



Lemma 2.1 in the case 2\nmid cd is Cauchy’s Lemma
(cf. [6, p. 31]) used to prove Cauchy’s polygonal number theorem.




Lemma 2.2.



Suppose that ac+bd=n\geqslant 2a with a\in\mathbb{Z}^{+} , b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z} and b>-a .
Then




 d>2\ \ \text{and}\ \ \frac{d^{2}}{4}<c<\frac{d^{2}+2d+4}{3} 

(2.3)


if and only if




 \frac{\sqrt{3a(n-a+b)+\frac{9}{4}b^{2}}-a-3b/2}{a}<d<\frac{2}{a}(\sqrt{an+b}-b). 

(2.4)






Proof. Clearly,




 3a(n-a+b)+\frac{9}{4}b^{2}\geqslant 3a^{2}+3ab+\frac{9}{4}b^{2}=2a^{2}+\left(a+\frac{3b}{2}\right)^{2}\geqslant 2. 



Thus d>2 if (2.4) holds.




Now assume d>2 . Then ad+2b>a(d-2)>0 . Note that




 n>a\frac{d^{2}}{4}+bd\iff 4an>(ad+2b)^{2}-4b^{2}\iff d<\frac{2}{a}\left(\sqrt{an+b^{2}}-b\right). 



As




 ad+a+\frac{3b}{2}>\frac{3}{2}(a+b)>0, 



we have





 \displaystyle\ n<\frac{a}{3}(d^{2}+2d+4)+bd 



 \displaystyle\iff 
 \displaystyle\ 3an<a^{2}d^{2}+ad(2a+3b)+4a^{2}=\left(ad+a+\frac{3b}{2}\right)^{2}+3a(a-b)-\frac{9}{4}b^{2} 



 \displaystyle\iff 
 \displaystyle\sqrt{3a(n-a+b)+\frac{9}{4}b^{2}}<ad+a+\frac{3b}{2} 



 \displaystyle\iff 
 \displaystyle d>\frac{\sqrt{3a(n-a+b)+9b^{2}/4}-a-3b/2}{a}. 



As n=ac+bd , by the above, d^{2}/4<c<(d^{2}+2d+4)/3 if and only if (2.4) holds.




In view of the above, we have completed the proof. ∎




Clearly, Theorems 1.1 and 1.3 follow from the following theorem.



Theorem 2.1.



Let a\in\mathbb{Z}^{+} and b\in\mathbb{Z} with b>-a and \gcd(a,b)=1 . Let n\in\mathbb{N} with




 n>4a(7a(a-1)+1)+2(7a-2)b+2(2a(2a-1)+b)\sqrt{6(2a(a-1)+b)}. 

(2.5)


Suppose that 2\nmid ab and 2\mid n , or 2\mid a and 2\nmid n , or 2\mid b and 4\nmid n .
Then we can write n as




 w(aw+b)+x(ax+b)+y(ay+b)+z(az+b) 



with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} .





Proof. Note that




 2a(2a-1)+b>2a(a-1)+b\geqslant 2a(a-1)-a+1=a(2a-3)+1\geqslant 0. 



If a>1 , then by (2.5) we have




 \frac{n}{2}>2(7a^{2}-7a+1)+(7a-2)b>2(7a^{2}-7a+1)-a(7a-2)=a(7a-12)+2>a. 



When a=1 , we have b\geqslant 1 and hence

We need the following lemma which is [2, Lemma 2.1].



Lemma 3.1.



Any even n\in\mathbb{N} not in the set

Our proof of Theorem 1.4 extends the method in [2].




Proof of Theorem 1.4. Note that \gcd(a+b,a)=\gcd(b,a)=1 
and \gcd(5(a+b),2a)=\gcd(a+b,2) divides n . So the congruence 5(a+b)x\equiv n\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 2a) 
is solvable (over \mathbb{Z} ). When n=5q with q\in\mathbb{Z} , we choose \delta\in\{1,-1\} such that
 4aq+b^{2}+\delta\not\equiv\pm 2\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 5) , and hence the congruence (2ax+b)^{2}\equiv 4aq+b^{2}+\delta\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 5) 
is solvable. Thus, by the Chinese Remainder Theorem, among any 10a consecutive integers, there is an integer B such that
 5(a+b)B\equiv n\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 2a) , and

 \displaystyle 0\leqslant\frac{n-5bB}{a}-5B^{2}\leqslant B^{2}

5. Some remarks and conjectures




It is easy to see that




 \left\{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}:\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}=\{x(2x+1):\ x\in\mathbb{Z}\}. 



For integers a>b\geqslant 0 and a\equiv b\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 2) , clearly




 \left\{\frac{x(ax+b)}{2}:\ x\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{N}. 



The author [9, 10] investigated those tuples (a,b,c,d,e,f)\in\mathbb{Z}^{6} 
with a>b\geqslant 0 , c>d\geqslant 0 , e>f\geqslant 0 and a-b,c-d,e-f\in 2\mathbb{Z}^{+} 
such that each n\in\mathbb{N} can be written as




 \frac{x(ax+b)}{2}+\frac{y(cy+d)}{2}+\frac{z(ez+f)}{2} 



with x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z} . Some further results along this direction were proved in [12].




The following lemma can be easily obtained based on the proof of Lemma 2.1
given in [4].



Lemma 5.1.



Let c,d\in\mathbb{N} with 4\nmid c , c\equiv d\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 2) and 4c>d^{2} . Then there are
 s,t,u,v\in\mathbb{Z} such that




 s^{2}+t^{2}+u^{2}+v^{2}=c\ \text{and}\ s+t+u+v=d. 

(5.1)






Using this lemma and some arguments similar to but easier than those in Section 2, we can deduce the following two theorems.



Theorem 5.1.



Let a and b be positive odd integers with a>b and \gcd(a,b)=1 .
Then any integer n>a^{3}/2+ab can be written as




 \frac{w(aw+b)}{2}+\frac{x(ax+b)}{2}+\frac{y(ay+b)}{2}+\frac{z(az+b)}{2} 



with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z} .




Theorem 5.2.



Let a,b\in\mathbb{Z}^{+} with a>b , a\not\equiv b\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 2) and \gcd(a,b)=1 .




(i) If 2\mid a , n\in\mathbb{N} and n\geqslant 2^{1+(-1)^{n}}a^{3}+(3+(-1)^{n})ab , then there are w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z} such that




 n=w(aw+b)+x(ax+b)+y(ay+b)+z(az+b).

 w(aw+b)+x(ax+b)+y(ay+b)+z(az+b)

 \displaystyle\{\bar{p}_{5}(x)+\bar{p}_{5}(y)+\bar{p}_{5}(z):\ x,y,z\in\mathbb{N}\} 
 \displaystyle\supseteq\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\ n>24036\}, 



 \displaystyle\{p_{6}(x)+p_{6}(y)+p_{6}(z):\ x,y,z\in\mathbb{N}\} 
 \displaystyle\supseteq\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\ n>146858\}, 



 \displaystyle\{\bar{p}_{6}(x)+\bar{p}_{6}(y)+\bar{p}_{6}(z):\ x,y,z\in\mathbb{N}\} 
 \displaystyle\supseteq\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\ n>138158\}. 







Motivated by Corollary 1.2, we pose the following four conjectures.



Conjecture 5.3.



(i) Any integer n>1 can be written as
 x(5x+1)+y(5y+1)/2+z(5z+1)/2 with x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z} .




(ii) If n\in\mathbb{N} can be written uniquely as x(5x+1)+y(5y+1)/2+z(5z+1)/2 with x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z} 
and y\leqslant z , then it belongs to the set




 \{0,\,2,\,3,\,5,\,7,\,14,\,16,\,19,\,37,\,43,\,58,\,61,\,79\}. 






Remark 5.1.



For the number of ways to write n\in\mathbb{N} as x(5x+1)+y(5y+1)/2+z(5z+1)/2 with x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z} 
and y\leqslant z , the reader may consult [11].




Conjecture 5.4.



We have




 \displaystyle\left\{\frac{x(5x+1)}{2}+\frac{y(5y+1)}{2}+\frac{z(5z+1)}{2}:\ x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}\right\} 
 \displaystyle=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1,10,19,94\}, 



 \displaystyle\left\{x(5x+1)+\frac{y(5y+1)}{2}+\frac{3z(5z+1)}{2}:\ x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}\right\} 
 \displaystyle=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1,5,32\}, 



 \displaystyle\left\{x(5x+1)+2y(5y+1)+\frac{z(5z+1)}{2}:\ x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}\right\} 
 \displaystyle=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1,5,70\}, 



 \displaystyle\left\{x(5x+1)+\frac{y(5y+1)}{2}+\frac{5z(5z+1)}{2}:\ x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}\right\} 
 \displaystyle=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1,5\}. 






Conjecture 5.5.



Any integer n>51 can be written as




 \frac{w(5w-1)}{2}+\frac{x(5x-1)}{2}+\frac{y(5y+1)}{2}+\frac{z(5z+1)}{2} 



with w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} .




Conjecture 5.6.



Let




 N(1)=114862,\ N(-1)=166897,\ N(3)=196987,\ N(-3)=273118. 



Then, for each r\in\{\pm 1,\pm 3\} , we can write any integer n>N(r) as
 x(5x+r)/2+y(5y+r)/2+z(5z+r)/2 with x,y,z\in\mathbb{N} .




Conjecture 5.7.



We have




 \{p_{7}(x)+2p_{7}(y)+3p_{7}(z):\ x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}\}=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{31,\,77\} 



and




 \{p_{7}(x)+2p_{7}(y)+5p_{7}(z):\ x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}\}=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{10,\,16\}. 






Remark 5.2.



In 2016, the author [8, Conjecture 5.4(i)] conjectured that




 \{p_{7}(x)+p_{7}(y)+2p_{7}(z):\ x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}\}=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{23\}.
