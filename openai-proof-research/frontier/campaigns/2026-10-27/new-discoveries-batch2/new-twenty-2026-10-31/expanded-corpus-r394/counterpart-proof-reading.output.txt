10:                                                    Abstract. We give counterexamples to two well-known conjectures about matroids: White’s conjecture
11:                                                    on the generation of the toric ideal by symmetric exchange binomials, and a conjecture of Mason on the
12:                                                    log-concavity of the counts of flats of a given rank.
13: 
14:                                              In this paper, we describe counterexamples to two conjectures about matroids, White’s conjecture that
15:                                           the symmetric exchange binomials generate the toric ideal of a matroid (Conjecture 1.1(2)) and a conjecture
16:                                           of Mason that counts of flats of a given rank in a matroid form a log-concave sequence (Conjecture 2.1). Our
17:                                           proofs do not require the use of a computer. We also recount the history of these conjectures and related
18:                                           results.
19:                                              One feature of these conjectures is that, for matroids of reasonable size, they can be checked easily using a
20:                                           computer. There are many known methods for producing interesting matroids. Until recently, it was tedious
21:                                           to test if these methods give counterexamples, but frontier large language models are capable of implementing
22:                                           these methods and checking if they give counterexamples. I have included detailed descriptions of how the
23:                                           counterexamples in this paper were found.

32:                                                                                          1. White’s conjecture
33:                                              Given bases B1 and B2 of a matroid M, a symmetric exchange of the ordered pair (B1 , B2 ) is an ordered
34:                                           pair of bases (B̃1 , B̃2 ) with the property that there are x ∈ B1 and y ∈ B2 such that B̃1 = (B1 \ x) ∪ y and
35:                                           B̃2 = (B2 \ y) ∪ x. It is known that for any pair (B1 , B2 ) and x ∈ B1 \ B2 , there is some y ∈ B2 \ B1 such
36:                                           that (B1 \ x) ∪ y and (B2 \ y) ∪ x are both bases. In [Whi80], White made three conjectures about basis
37:                                           exchange properties of matroids. These conjectures are in decreasing order of strength.
38:                                           Conjecture 1.1. [Whi80, Conjecture 12] Let M be a matroid, and let (B1 , . . . , Bs ) and (B1′ , . . . , Bs′ ) be
39:                                           sequences of bases of M such that the multiset union of B1 , . . . , Bs is equal to the multiset union of B1′ , . . . , Bs′ .
40:                                           Then
41:                                               (1) It is possible to obtain (B1′ , . . . , Bs′ ) from (B1 , . . . , Bs ) by repeatedly choosing two bases Bi and Bj
42:                                                   with i < j, choosing a symmetric exchange (B̃i , B̃j ) of (Bi , Bj ), and replacing (B1 , . . . , Bs ) by
43:                                                   (B1 , . . . , Bi−1 , B̃i , . . . , Bj−1 , B̃j , . . . , Bs ).
44:                                               (2) It is possible to obtain the multiset {B1′ , . . . , Bs′ } from the multiset {B1 , . . . , Bs } by repeatedly choos-
45:                                                   ing two bases Bi and Bj , choosing a symmetric exchange (B̃i , B̃j ), and replacing {B1 , . . . , Bs } by
46:                                                   {B1 , . . . , Bi−1 , B̃i , . . . , Bj−1 , B̃j , . . . , Bs }.
47:                                               (3) It is possible to obtain the multiset {B1′ , . . . , Bs′ } from the multiset {B1 , . . . , Bs } by repeatedly choosing
48:                                                   two bases Bi and Bj , choosing any pair of bases (B i , B j ) with the property that the multiset union of

60: which is sometimes called Farber’s conjecture after [FRS85, Far89].
61: Conjecture 1.2. Let M be a matroid, and let {B1 , B2 } and {B1′ , B2′ } be unordered pairs of bases such that
62: the multiset union of B1 and B2 is equal to the multiset union of B1′ and B2′ . Then it is possible to obtain
63: {B1′ , B2′ } from {B1 , B2 } by repeatedly replacing {B1 , B2 } by a symmetric exchange {B̃1 , B̃2 }.
64:    We will give a counterexample to Conjecture 1.2 in Example 1.4.
65:    Clearly Conjecture 1.1(1) holds for a matroid M if and only if Conjecture 1.1(2) holds for M and the
66: following strengthening of Conjecture 1.2 holds for M: for any ordered pair of bases (B1 , B2 ), it is possible
67: to obtain (B2 , B1 ) by a sequence of symmetric exchanges. As observed in [LM14, Section 4], this holds if
68: Conjecture 1.2 holds for M⊕M. If Conjecture 1.2 holds for M⊕M, then the three forms of Conjecture 1.1 for M
69: are equivalent. A different way of reducing Conjecture 1.1(1) to Conjecture 1.1(2) is given in [Bon13, Theorem
70: 2.5].
71:    Conjecture 1.1 can be interpreted in terms of the toric ideal of the matroid, the ideal of the homogeneous
72: coordinate ring of the toric variety corresponding to the matroid polytope P (M). If the ground set of M is
73: {1, . . . Q
74:           , n}, this ideal can be described as the kernel of the map k[xB : B basis] → k[x1 , . . . , xn ] which sends
75: xB to i∈B xi . Conjecture 1.1(3) states that this ideal is generated by quadrics and Conjecture 1.1(2) states
76: that this ideal is generated by quadrics corresponding to symmetric exchange binomials. Conjecture 1.1(1)
77: can be interpreted as giving generators for a version of the toric ideal in a non-commutative polynomial ring.
78:    In [HH02], Herzog and Hibi conjectured an extension of Conjecture 1.1(2) to polymatroids, and they asked
79: whether the homogeneous coordinate ring of the toric variety of P (M) is Koszul, and whether the toric ideal
80: satisfies the stronger property of having a quadratic Gröbner basis. By work of Sturmfels [Stu96, Theorem
81: 8.3, Corollary 8.9], it is known that the toric ideal has a quadratic Gröbner basis if and only if the polytope
82: P (M) has a regular unimodular triangulation whose underlying simplicial complex is flag, i.e., all minimal
83: non-faces have size 2. A regular unimodular triangulation of P (M) was constructed in [BL25], but it was
84: recently shown that the matroid polytope of the Fano matroid does not have a flag regular unimodular
85: triangulation [DLFMR26, BCL+ 26].
86:    Note that, in Conjecture 1.2, we can immediately reduce to the case when B1 ∩ B2 = ∅ and B1 ∪ B2 is the
87: ground set of M by deleting elements not contained in B1 ∪ B2 and contracting elements in B1 ∩ B2 . This
88: means that for matroids of rank at most r, Conjecture 1.2 reduces to the case of matroids on a ground set
89: of size at most 2r. This is a finite computation, and one can check that Conjecture 1.2 holds for matroids of
90: rank at most 4.

92: of matroids. We mention some of the most relevant results. Conjecture 1.1(2) was proven for graphic
93: matroids by Blasiak [Bla08] (after Conjecture 1.2 was proven in [FRS85] for graphic matroids); this result
94: was extended by McGuinness [McG20]. Conjecture 1.1(2) was proven for transversal matroids in [LM14]

146: Theorem 1.5. Conjecture 1.2 and Conjecture 1.1(2) fail in Example 1.4.
147: Proof. The ground set {1, . . . , 18} divides into three blocks: {1, . . . , 6}, {7, . . . , 12}, and {13, . . . , 18}. It is
148: not hard to check that the element of S18 which cyclically permutes these blocks (sending 1 to 7, 7 to 13, 13
149: to 1, 2 to 8, and so on) is an automorphism of this matroid. The restriction of M to each block is a matroid
150: of rank 4 which has three coparallel classes of size 2. This divides the ground set into 9 pairs of the form
151: {2i − 1, 2i} for i = 1, . . . , 9. There is an action of S4 on each block, where the action of S4 on a block is the
152: same as its action on the edges of the complete graph K4 , and the pairs correspond to vertex-disjoint edges.
153: These elements generate a subgroup of the automorphism group of M isomorphic to (S4 )3 ⋊ Z/3Z.

159: are split by B, i.e., |B ∩ {2i − 1, 2i}| = 1. Because the rank of M is odd, s(B) must be odd. Note that
160: s(B) = s(B c ), and that if B̃ differs from B by a single symmetric exchange, then s(B) − s(B̃) ∈ {−2, 0, 2}.
161: We claim that s(B) lies in {3, 7, 9}. All three values occur: we have
162:     s({1, 2, 3, 7, 8, 9, 13, 14, 15}) = 3, s({1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15}) = 7, and s({1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15, 18}) = 9,
163: and each of the sets above is a basis whose complement is also a basis. The pairs {B, B c } with s(B) = 3 cannot
164: be transformed into a pair {B ′ , (B ′ )c } with s(B ′ ) ∈ {7, 9}, showing that Conjecture 1.2 and Conjecture 1.1(2)
165: fail for M.
166:     Because B and B c are both bases and the rank of each block is 4, the intersection of B with any block has
167: either 2, 3, or 4 elements. The intersection of B with the three blocks either gives sets of size 2, 3, and 4 or
168: sets of size 3, 3, and 3. The union of any two pairs in a block is a circuit, so an independent set of size 4 in a
169: block must split 2 pairs. This implies that s(B) ̸= 1. Because both B and B c are bases, an independent set
170: of size 2 in a block must split 2 pairs. If the intersection of B with a block is a set of size 4, then, up to the
171: action of the automorphisms, the set must be {1, 2, 3, 5}. If the intersection has size 2, then, up to the action
172: of automorphisms, the set must be {1, 3}. If the intersection has size 3, then there are three possibilities: we
173: could get {1, 3, 5}, {1, 3, 6}, or {1, 2, 3}; there are three orbits of subgraphs of K4 with 3 edges. These split
174: 3, 3, and 1 pairs, respectively.
175:     We consider sets of size 9 which split exactly 5 pairs and satisfy the conditions in the previous paragraph,
176: up to the action of automorphisms. There are only four: representatives are
177: {1, 2, 3, 7, 8, 9, 13, 15, 18}, {1, 2, 3, 7, 8, 9, 13, 15, 17}, {1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 9, 13, 14, 15}, and {1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 9, 13, 15}.
178: The complement of a set of the first type is a set of the second type, and the complement of a set of the
179: third type is a set of the fourth type. The first and third sets are bases, but the second and fourth sets are
180: not, so none of these gives rise to pairs of bases {B, B c } with s(B) = 5.                                 □

223:                                  2. Log-concavity of counts of flats
224:   For a matroid M, let Wi denote the number of flats of M of rank i. These are known as the Whitney
225: numbers of the second kind.
226: Conjecture 2.1. Let M be a matroid of rank r. Then for any 1 ≤ i ≤ r − 1, we have Wi2 ≥ Wi+1 Wi−1 .
227:    This was conjectured by Mason in [Mas72, Conjecture 1B], where he also conjectured two stronger versions
228: predicting the ultra log-concavity of the Wi . The weaker statement that the Wi are unimodal was conjectured
229: by Rota in [Rot71]. Conjecture 2.1 is Problem 25(c) in Stanley’s list of positivity problems and conjectures
230: in algebraic combinatorics [Sta00]. There, he also mentions the log-concavity conjectures for the coefficients
231: of the characteristic polynomial of a matroid and for the number of independent sets of a given size in
232: a matroid. He speculates that all three of these conjectures are false. However, the log-concavity of the
233: coefficients of the characteristic polynomial and the number of independent sets were proven in [AHK18].
234:    One case that has attracted particular attention is log-concavity at W2 : that W22 ≥ W1 W3 . This is known
235: as the points-lines-planes conjecture, although this is sometimes used to refer to the stronger conjectures
236:                                         1 −1
237: that W22 ≥ 32 W1 W3 or that W22 ≥ 32 W W1 −2 W1 W3 . This strongest form was proven by Seymour for matroids
238: where each flat of rank 2 has size at most four [Sey95]. In particular, this applies to all binary matroids.
239: This case was previously proven for graphic matroids in [Sto75], and it was subsequently studied by Dukes
240: [Duk08].
241:    In contrast to Conjecture 1.1, there are very few families of matroids for which Conjecture 2.1 is known.
242: It holds trivially for paving matroids, and it is easy to check for matroids associated to the complete graph
243: or projective geometries. See [Aig87] for a survey and a few additional cases.
244:    There are two general results which can be interpreted as giving evidence for Conjecture 2.1. The first is
245: the log-concavity of the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of a matroid, proved in [Huh12, HK12]
246: for realizable matroids and in [AHK18] for all matroids. The coefficients of the characteristic polynomial can
247: be interpreted as counting the number of flats of a given rank, except that we weight a flat F by the Möbius
248: value |µ(∅, F )|. The second is the Dowling–Wilson top-heavy conjecture [DW74, DW75], which states that
249: for a matroid of rank r, we have W0 ≤ W1 ≤ · · · ≤ W⌊r/2⌋ , and Wi ≤ Wr−i for i ≤ r/2. This was proven in
250: [HW17] for realizable matroids and in [BHM+ 20] for all matroids. The proof was simplified in [AHL25].

20:                                                                                               Abstract
21:                                                        A vertex-ordered graph is a graph equipped with a linear ordering of its vertices. A pair of
22:                                                    independent edges in an ordered graph can exhibit one of the following three patterns: separated,
23:                                                    nested or crossing. We say a pair of independent edges is non-separated if it is either crossing
24:                                                    or nested. Non-nested and non-crossing pairs are deﬁned analogously.
25:                                                        We are interested in the following Turán-type problems: for each of the aforementioned six
26:                                                    patterns, determine the maximum number of edges of an n-vertex ordered graph that does not
27:                                                    contain a k-matching such that every pair of edges exhibit the ﬁxed pattern. Exact answers
28:                                                    have already been obtained for four of the six cases. The main objective of this paper is to
29:                                                    investigate the two remaining open cases, namely non-separated and non-nested matchings.
30:                                                        We determine the exact maximum number of edges of an n-vertex ordered graph that does
31:                                                    not contain a non-separated k-matching, which has the form 23 (k − 1)n + Θ(k 2 ). For the non-
32:                                                    nested case, we show the maximum number of edges lies between (k − 1)n and (k − 1)n + k−1
33:                                                                                                                                                   
34:                                                                                                                                                 2 .
35:                                                    We also determine the exact maximum number of edges of an n-vertex ordered graph that
36:                                                    does not contain an alternating path of given length. We discuss some related problems and
37:                                                    raise several conjectures. Furthermore, our results and conjectures yield consequences to certain
38:                                                    Ramsey-type problems for non-nested matchings and alternating paths.

58:     In recent years, there has been accelerating interest on Turán-type questions for ordered graphs,
59: see e.g. [17]. For a family of unordered graphs H, the Turán number ex(n, H) denotes the maximum
60: number of edges of an n-vertex graph, which does not contain a copy of any H ∈ H. The celebrated
61: Erdős–Stone–Simonovits theorem determines ex(n, H) asymptotically for H ﬁxed, provided H con-
62: tains no bipartite graph. Analogous notions exist for ordered graphs. We say an ordered graph G
63: contains a copy of an ordered graph H if there exists an injective graph isomorphism from H to G,
64: which preserves the order of the vertices.
65: 
66: Deﬁnition 1.1. Given a family of ordered graphs H, we write ex< (n, H) to denote the maximum
67: number of edges in an ordered graph with vertex set [n] not containing any copy of H ∈ H.

70: setting. The crucial parameter here is the interval chromatic number χ< (H), deﬁned as the size of
71: a smallest proper interval colouring of H.

111: Deﬁnition 1.4 (k-matchings with special patterns). A matching is a collection of independent
112: edges. A matching consisting of k ∈ N edges is called a k-matching. For any matching whose
113: vertices are positive integers, we say such matching is:
114:    • separated if every pair of its edges are separated,
115:    • nested if every pair of its edges are nested,
116:    • crossing if every pair of its edges are crossing,
117:    • non-separated if every pair of its edges are not separated,
118:    • non-nested if every pair of its edges are not nested,
119:    • non-crossing if every pair of its edges are not crossing.
120: As a convention, we denote a separated (resp. nested and crossing) k-matching as Sep-Mk (resp.
121: Nest-Mk and Cross-Mk ). Similarly, we denote a non-separated (resp. non-nested and non-crossing)
122: k-matching as ¬Sep-Mk (resp. ¬Nest-Mk and ¬Cross-Mk ).

175:     The functions ex< (n, Sep-Mk ) and ex< (n, Nest-Mk ) have also been considered, under diﬀerent
176: terminology. A stack (resp. queue and arch) is a set of edges that are non-crossing (resp.non-nested
177: and non-separated). A k-stack layout of an ordered graph G, is a partition of the edge set E(G) into
178: k stacks; the notions of k-queue layout and k-arch layout are deﬁned analogously. In particular, G
179: admits a k-queue layout (resp. a k-arch layout) if and only if it does not contain a nested (resp.
180: separated) (k + 1)-matching, see e.g. [7, Lemmata 1 and 2]. Dujmović and Wood [7] determined
181: the maximum number of edges in an ordered graph with a k-queue layout or a k-arch layout [7,
182: Lemmata 8 and 10], thus obtaining the following.
