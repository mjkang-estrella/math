There is no mni clutter whose core is the projective plane
of order 3
Paulo Tavares1,* , João Luís Soares2
1. Mathematics Department, University of Aveiro, Aveiro, Portugal
2. Mathematics Department, University of Coimbra, Coimbra, Portugal

 Abstract: In 1998, Lütolf and Margot [18] claimed, in a sidenote, that the projective plane of order 3,
 also called the ternary plane, or 𝑃𝐺(2, 3), cannot be the core of any minimally non-ideal clutter. In this
 paper, we show that, if 𝒞 is a minimally non-ideal clutter whose core is the 𝑃𝐺(2, 3) then 𝒞 must be the
 blocker of the 𝑃𝐺(2, 3). Since no projective plane of order 𝑛 ≥ 3 is minimally non-ideal, the nonexistence
 of 𝒞 is a consequence of the fact that a clutter is minimally non-ideal if and only its blocker is minimally
 non-ideal.
 Keywords: Clutters, Blockers, Ideal and minimally nonideal clutters, Projective planes.

1. Introduction
The Set Covering problem, which is to find to a vector x of zeros and ones, that maximizes a linear function
cx subject to linear constraints 𝐴x ≥ 1, where 𝐴 stands for a matrix of zeros and ones and 1 stands for a
column-vector of zeros and ones, is a pro-eminent problem in the operations research community. Clutters
and their blockers provide a way to understand and characterize the structure of set families, which can
lead to more efficient solution procedures for the set covering problem. Long standing questions are: under
which conditions do integrality properties hold? Can we devise “efficient” cutting plane algorithms for the
set covering problem that would not plague the solution process with additional extreme points?
      A clutter is a pair 𝒞 ≡ (𝑉 , 𝐸), where 𝐸 is a family of subsets of a finite and nonempty set 𝑉 such
that no element in 𝐸 contains another. A clutter 𝒞 is ideal if the polyhedron 𝑄(𝐴) ≡ {x : 𝐴x ≥ 1, x ≥ 0}
is integral, where 𝐴 ≡ 𝑀(𝒞 ) is a matrix whose rows are the characteristic vectors of the elements of 𝐸,
i.e., all the extreme points of 𝑄(𝐴) have integer components. The matrix 𝐴 is usually referred to as the
(element-member) incidence matrix of the clutter 𝒞 . A minor of 𝒞 is a clutter that stems from the clutter 𝒞
after one or more operations of deletion or contraction of an element of 𝑉. A clutter is minimally non-ideal,
or mni for short, if it is not ideal but all its proper minors are. Lehman [17] proved that an mni clutter 𝒞
(that is not a specific Delta clutter) has a precise structure, it is the extension of a core clutter, identified
as core(𝒞 ). This core clutter is defined by a Lehman matrix. A Lehman matrix is an 𝑟-regular matrix 𝑌 of
zeros and ones such that 𝑌𝑍𝑇 = 𝑑𝐼 + 11𝑇 holds for some positive integer 𝑑 and some matrix 𝑍 of zeros and
ones. Point-line incidence matrices of projective planes are natural candidates.
      In 1998, Lütolf and Margot [18] provided a computational study of mni clutters and constructed a
partial catalog with clutters up to 20 vertices. In a sidenote, they claimed, maybe as an observation from
the computations, that the projective plane of order 3 - also called the ternary plane, or 𝑃𝐺(2, 3) -, though
not mni, cannot be the core of any mni clutter. There, the incidence matrix of the 𝑃𝐺(2, 3) is identified as
𝐿413 (1). In 2019, Mayhew at al [19] provided a noncomputational proof, but we believe there is a flaw in
their proof of Lemma 26 and also some fundamental details are missing in the the proof of Theorem 22.
      In this paper, we will show that if 𝒞 is an mni clutter such that the core of 𝒞 is the 𝑃𝐺(2, 3) (the only
nonisomorphic projective plane of order 3) then 𝒞 must be the blocker of the 𝑃𝐺(2, 3). But, this would
imply that the 𝑃𝐺(2, 3), being the blocker of the blocker of the 𝑃𝐺(2, 3), is also mni, because a clutter is mni

    Our main result, Theorem 5.4, stems basically from putting three results together. The first two hold
more generally when core(𝒞 ) is a projective plane of any order. So, the first is [19, Lemma 23]: every edge
of 𝒞 that is not an edge of core(𝒞 ) must be an edge of the blocker of core(𝒞 ). The second is [19, Lemma 25]:

blocker of the 𝑃𝐺(2, 3).
    The paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, we review the definitions of clutter, blocker and minor.
In Section 3, we recall the definition of mni clutters and Lehman’s characterization of mni clutters, which
involves the concept of Lehman matrix and core clutter. Then, in Section 4, we review the definition and
some basic properties of projective planes of any order. We also review the connection with mni clutters and
recall some results from [19], whose proofs we choose to include using our own words for completeness and
for coherency with the notation used (for clarity purposes). In some cases our proofs are shorter. Finally, in
Section 5, we look specifically into the 𝑃𝐺(2, 3). Building upon the results of [9], we characterize the blocker

and 0-corners [19] are the same sets. We conclude this section with the main result. In Section 6, we explain
why we believe there is a flaw in the proof of [19, Lemma 26] and address some research questions.

2. Clutters
A clutter is a pair 𝒞 ≡ (𝑉 , 𝐸), where 𝐸 is a family of subsets of a finite and nonempty set 𝑉 such that no
element in 𝐸 contains another [11, 7]. The set 𝑉 is often referred to as the ground set and the set 𝐸 as the
edge (or member) set (of the clutter 𝒞 ). When 𝐸 is the empty set or contains the empty set we say that the
clutter is trivial. In combinatorics, a clutter is a Sperner family [10]; in graph theory, it is an hypergraph
where no edge properly contains another [27, 1]; in other areas of mathematics it is an independence system
or an irredundant system.
     The (vertex-edge) incidence matrix, or clutter matrix, of 𝒞 is the matrix, denoted 𝑀(𝒞 ), whose rows
(resp., columns) are indexed by 𝐸(𝒞 ) (resp., 𝑉(𝒞 )) and contain the characteristic vectors of the edges in
terms of its elements. From the definition of clutter, the relation “≥ (componentwise)” does not hold for any
pair of rows of 𝑀(𝒞 ). Obviously, the clutter matrix of 𝒞 is not unique because depending on the ordering
imposed on vertices and edges we may have a different matrix. If 𝐴 ≡ 𝑀(𝒞 1 ) and 𝐵 ≡ 𝑀(𝒞 2 ) we will say
that clutters 𝒞 1 , 𝒞 2 are the same if 𝐴, 𝐵 are isomorphic, in the sense that there are permutation matrices
𝑃, 𝑄 such that 𝐵 = 𝑃𝐴𝑄 𝑇 .
     A few examples are as follows. If 𝐺 is a simple graph (i.e., a graph without loops and multiple edges)
then 𝒞 ≡ (𝑉(𝐺), 𝐸(𝐺)) is a clutter; if 𝐶(𝐺) is the collection of all minimal vertex covers of 𝐺 (also called
minimal transversals) then 𝒞 ≡ (𝑉(𝐺), 𝐶(𝐺)) is also a clutter; if 𝑃𝑠,𝑡 (𝐺) is the collection of all edge-sets of
(𝑠, 𝑡)-paths, where 𝑠, 𝑡 ∈ 𝑉(𝐺) are distinct, then 𝒞 ≡ (𝐸(𝐺), 𝑃𝑠,𝑡 (𝐺)) is a clutter; if 𝐶 𝑠,𝑡 (𝐺) is the collection
of all minimal (𝑠, 𝑡)-cuts of 𝐺, where 𝑠, 𝑡 ∈ 𝑉(𝐺) are distinct, then 𝒞 ≡ (𝐸(𝐺), 𝐶 𝑠,𝑡 (𝐺)) is a clutter. if,
additionally, 𝐺 is connected graph then, if 𝑇(𝐺) is the collection of the edge sets of spanning trees of 𝐺 then
𝒞 ≡ (𝐸(𝐺), 𝑇(𝐺)) is a clutter; if 𝐶(𝐺) is the collection of the edge sets of minimal edge cutsets in 𝐺 then
𝒞 ≡ (𝐸(𝐺), 𝐶(𝐺)) is a clutter. A particular example, that plays a specific role, is the Delta clutter. For a
given integer 𝑡 ≥ 2, the clutter Δ𝑡 ≡ (𝑉 , 𝐸) is such that 𝑉 ≡ {0, 1, , . . . , 𝑡} and the edge set composed of the
𝑡-element set {1, 2, . . . , 𝑡} and the two-element sets {0, 𝑖}, for every 𝑖 = 1, 2, . . . , 𝑡.
     The blocker of a clutter 𝒞 ≡ (𝑉 , 𝐸), denoted bl(𝒞 ) ≡ (𝑉 , 𝐹), is a clutter with edge set consisting of all
minimal sets 𝐵 ⊆ 𝑉 such that |𝐵 ∩ 𝐴| ≥ 1, for every 𝐴 ∈ 𝐸. Of course, the blocker of a trivial clutter is also
trivial. It can be shown that bl bl(𝒞 ) = 𝒞 [14, 11]. The elements of 𝐹 are often called minimal covers or
minimal transversals (of the clutter 𝒞 ). It may be shown that, when 𝐺 is a simple graph and 𝑠, 𝑡 are two
distinct vertices then the blocker of 𝒞 ≡ 𝐺 is the clutter of all minimal vertex covers (of 𝐺); the blocker of
the clutter of all (𝑠, 𝑡)-paths of 𝐺 is the clutter of all minimal (𝑠, 𝑡)-cuts of 𝐺. If 𝐺 is a connected graph then
the blocker of 𝒞 ≡ (𝐸(𝐺), 𝑇(𝐺)), the clutter of all spanning trees of 𝐺, is 𝒞 ≡ (𝐸(𝐺), 𝐶(𝐺)), the clutter of all
minimal edge cutsets in 𝐺. Also bl(Δ𝑡 ) = Δ𝑡 .

4. Projective Planes
Every (finite) projective plane defines a clutter [3, 17]. A projective plane (finite and nondegenerate) 𝒞 is an
ordered pair (𝑉 , 𝐸), where 𝑉 denotes a finite nonempty set - whose elements can be associated with points
on the plane - and 𝐸 denotes a family of subsets of 𝑉 - whose elements can be associated with lines on
the plane that pass through some of the points in 𝑉 - satisfying the following three axioms - see, e.g., [12,
Section 8.2]:

                   (AX1) Any two distinct points are in one and only one common line;
                   (AX2) Any two distinct lines contain one and only one common point;
                   (AX3) There exist four points, no three of which are in a common line.

The third axiom excludes a few cases that we would call degenerate - see [2, Theorem 1,page 7] - that include
the Delta clutter that we have seen before. The most well-known projective plane is the Fano plane with 7
points and 7 lines. Projective planes are the basis of popular game Dobble.
    If 𝒞 ≡ (𝑉 , 𝐸) is a projective plane then there is an integer 𝑛 ≥ 2 such that |𝑉| = |𝐸| = 𝑛 2 + 𝑛 + 1, and every
line contains exactly 𝑛 + 1 points, and every point belongs to exactly 𝑛 + 1 lines. We will, then, say that 𝒞 is
a projective plane of order 𝑛, in which case its (point-line) incidence matrix is (𝑛 2 + 𝑛 + 1) × (𝑛 2 + 𝑛 + 1) and

     A blocking set of a projective plane 𝒞 is a set of points that intersect every line [6]. By definition, every
line intersects every other line in exactly one point. Therefore, we will say that a blocking set is nontrivial if
it does not contain a line. Hence, a nontrivial blocking set is a set 𝑆 of points that, for every line 𝐿, intersects
both 𝐿 and 𝑉 \ 𝐿. In other words, 𝑆 is a nontrivial blocking set if and only if 𝑉 \ 𝑆 is a nontrivial blocking set.
In clutter language, the minimal blocking sets are the edges of the blocker. The projective plane 𝑃𝐺(2, 2)
has no nontrivial blocking sets [6].

      Di Paola [9] refers a nontrivial blocking set as blocking coalition, and a nontrivial blocking set of minimum
cardinality as minimum blocking coalition. We remark that ’of minimum cardinality’ and ’minimal’ is not
necessarily the same. Di Paola [9], and Richardson [23], proved that the size of a minimum blocking
coalition is, respectively, 6, 7, 9, 12, 13, 13 for projective planes of order 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9 - a fact that can be
verified computationally, we believe. Di Paola also showed several properties of projective triangles. For
example, in a projective plane of order 𝑛, if 𝑇 is a projective triangle of side-length (𝑛 + 3)/2 then 𝑇 is a
blocking coalition [9, Theorem 13]. If 𝑛 ∈ {3, 5, 7} then there is, at least, one projective triangle of side-length
(𝑛 + 3)/2. Such a projective triangle is also a minimum blocking coalition [9, Theorem 14]. If 𝑛 = 3 (resp.
𝑛 = 5) the reciprocal is also true, i.e., a minimum blocking coalition is also a projective triangle of side-length
3 (resp. 4) [9, Theorems 15,16].

Theorem 5.4. There is no mni clutter 𝒞 such that core(𝒞 ) is a projective plane of order 3.

Proof. Let 𝒞 be an mni clutter such that core(𝒞 ) = 𝑃𝐺(2, 3). From Theorem 4.3, every edge of 𝒞 that is not
an edge of the 𝑃𝐺(2, 3) must be an edge of the blocker of the 𝑃𝐺(2, 3). Hence, from Corollary 5.2, it must
be a projective triangle of side-length 3 of the 𝑃𝐺(2, 3). Or, from Proposition 5.3, it must be a 0-corner of
the 𝑃𝐺(2, 3). On the other hand, from Lemma 4.5, every 0-corner of the 𝑃𝐺(2, 3) is an edge of 𝒞 . Hence, 𝒞
must be the blocker of the 𝑃𝐺(2, 3), a contradiction to the fact that, from Theorem 4.2, the 𝑃𝐺(2, 3) is not
mni.                                                                                                       □
