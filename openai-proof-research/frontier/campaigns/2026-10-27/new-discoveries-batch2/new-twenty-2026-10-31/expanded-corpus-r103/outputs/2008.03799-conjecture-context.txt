29:                                                Ordering polytopes have been instrumental to the study of problems from a variety of fields including compara-
30:                                            tive probability Anscombe, Aumann et al. (1963); Chevyrev, Searles and Slinko (2013), computational social choice
31:                                            Barthelemy and Monjardet (1981); Marley and Regenwetter (2016) and group decision-making Marti and Reinelt
32:                                            (2011a); Yoo and Escobedo (2021); Yoo, Escobedo and Skolfield (2020). The derivation of facet defining inequalities
33:                                            (FDIs) for these polytopes is a focal point owing primarily to their usefulness in tackling challenging combinatorial op-
34:                                            timization problems. For example, their implementation via branch-and-cut approaches has been cited as an effective
35:                                            solution methodology (e.g., see Coll, Marenco, Díaz and Zabala (2002); Grötschel and Wakabayashi (1990); Marti
36:                                            and Reinelt (2011a)). Such polyhedral studies have historically centered on the linear ordering polytope (e.g., see
37:                                            Bolotashvili, Kovalev and Girlich (1999); Fishburn (1992); Grötschel, Jünger and Reinelt (1985); Marti and Reinelt
38:                                            (2011b)). In recent years, different classes of FDIs have been introduced for various other ordering polytopes (e.g.,
39:                                            see Doignon and Fiorini (2001); Doignon and Rexhep (2016); Müller (1996); Oswald and Reinelt (2003)), driven by
40:                                            a fundamental need to consider a broader range of ordinal relationships. For example, the incorporation of non-strict
41:                                            orderings (i.e., possibly containing ties) is regarded as a fundamental form of preference expression in various group

82: 4. Each VI class is obtained by considering a relatively small number of ordinal relationship combinations, specifically
83: the orderings of only up to two alternatives with another and with an unspecified number of other alternatives, which
84: are treated as a group. We prove that five of the six new large classes of VIs are FDIs. This is done by characterizing
85: the weak orders that satisfy a VI at equality into a small number of ranking structures and then devising construction

91: other large classes of VIs, many of which we expect to be FDIs.
