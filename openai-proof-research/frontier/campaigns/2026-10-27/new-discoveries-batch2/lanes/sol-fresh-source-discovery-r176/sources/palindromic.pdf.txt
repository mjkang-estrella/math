                                                  Abstract. Let P be a ﬁnite collection of cells. Two non-attacking rook conﬁgurations on P
                                                  are switching-equivalent if one can be obtained from the other by a sequence of switches inside
                                                  rectangles contained in P. The switching rook polynomial of P enumerates the resulting equivalence
                                                  classes according to the number of rooks. We characterize the collections of cells whose switching
                                                  rook polynomial is palindromic. More precisely, we introduce a new combinatorial property, called
                                                  domino-stability, and we prove that the switching rook polynomial of P is palindromic if and only
                                                  if P is domino-stable.




                                                                                               Introduction
                                              The classical rook problem asks for the number of ways to place non-attacking rooks on a board,
                                           where no two rooks are allowed to lie in the same row or column. The corresponding generating
                                           function is called the rook polynomial. Rook placements on skew diagrams are closely related to
                                           the enumeration of permutations with forbidden positions, a connection ﬁrst investigated in [11]
                                           and subsequently developed in [19]; see also [21, Chapter 2] for a comprehensive account.
                                              Several variants of the rook polynomial have been introduced over the years; see, for instance,
                                           [6]. In this paper, we study the switching rook polynomial of a collection of cells. Let P be a
                                           collection of cells, regarded as a pruned chessboard. A rook conﬁguration on P is non-attacking if
                                           no two of its rooks are connected by a horizontal or vertical path of edge-adjacent cells of P. Two
                                           conﬁgurations of k non-attacking rooks are said to be switching-equivalent if one can be obtained
                                           from the other by a ﬁnite sequence of switches. Each switch replaces two rooks occupying one
                                           pair of opposite corner cells of a rectangle contained in P by two rooks occupying the other pair
                                           of opposite corner cells.

  The main problem addressed in this paper is to determine when reP (t) is palindromic. Recall
that a polynomial p(t) = p0 + p1 t + · · · + pd td is palindromic if pk = pd−k for every k ∈ {0, . . . , d}.
To answer this question, we introduce a new combinatorial property of collections of cells, called
domino-stability, and we prove the following result.
Theorem 0.1 (Theorem 4.4). Let P be a collection of cells. Then the switching rook polynomial
reP (t) is palindromic if and only if P is domino-stable.
   For skew diagrams, the switching rook polynomial is also referred to as the nested rook polynomial
(see [1]); in this setting, our characterization recovers [1, Corollary 5.16]. Moreover, for simple thin
polyominoes and parallelogram polyominoes, domino-stability is equivalent to the S-property.
Thus, our theorem recovers the corresponding characterizations in [18, Theorem 4.2] and [17,
Theorem 4.10], respectively.
   The proof of the suﬃcient direction of Theorem 4.4 is constructive. Let P be domino-stable
and set d = r(P). We ﬁrst construct a d-rook conﬁguration T = {T1 , . . . , Td } which is unique
up to switches and has exactly one rook in every row and every column of P. This conﬁguration
provides a reference system for all the other rook conﬁgurations. More precisely, if a rook of a
rook conﬁguration R lies in the row containing Ta and in the column containing Tb , we associate
with it the arrow a −→ b. Since the rooks of R are non-attacking, the resulting directed graph
on {1, . . . , d} has indegree and outdegree at most one at each vertex. Its connected components
are therefore directed paths, directed cycles, and isolated vertices. Using this decomposition, for
every k ∈ {0, . . . , d} we construct an explicit bijection between the switching classes of k-rook
conﬁgurations and those of (d − k)-rook conﬁgurations.
For the converse, suppose that reP (t) is palindromic. Since re0 (P) = 1, palindromicity gives red (P) =
1. Starting from a representative T of the unique switching class of d-rook conﬁgurations, we
construct an injective map τ attaching to a rook a switching class of a (d − 1)-rook conﬁguration
of P. We then prove that, if P is not domino-stable, then τ is not surjective, that is, the number
of switching classes of (d − 1)-rook conﬁgurations is strictly greater than the number of cells of P,
contradicting palindromicity.
   The switching rook polynomial arises naturally in combinatorial commutative algebra. Let
SP = K[xv : v is a vertex of P] and let IP ⊆ SP be the binomial ideal generated by the inner
2-minors of P. The quotient K[P] = SP /IP is called the coordinate ring of P. This construction
was introduced in [16] and later included in [9, Chapter 8], as a generalization of the theory of
determinantal ideals of order two; see also the recent survey [14].
The connection between rook theory and inner 2-minor ideals was initially observed in [5]. So
far, it is known that for several classes of collections of cells the switching rook polynomial of
P coincides with the h-polynomial of K[P], denoted by hK[P](t) (see [3, 4, 10, 12, 15, 17, 18,
23]). Therefore, whenever hK[P] (t) = reP (t), our main theorem can be combined with Stanley’s
results on palindromic h-polynomials [20]. In particular, Gorensteinness of K[P] implies that P is
domino-stable, and the converse holds when K[P] is a Cohen–Macaulay domain. Computational
experiments reported in [13] suggest that domino-stability may be suﬃcient for the Gorenstein
property even without the domain hypothesis.
   The main theorem also yields a numerical criterion for palindromicity. More precisely, let d =
r(P) and assume that red (P) = 1. Then
                           reP (t) is palindromic      ⇐⇒          re1 (P) = red−1 (P);

see Corollary 4.5. The numerical criterion stated above is formally parallel to a recent result [8,
Theorem 1.1]. More precisely, if
                                   h(K[G]; t) = h0 + h1 t + · · · + hs ts
is the h-polynomial of the edge ring of a bipartite graph G, it is proved that the conditions hs = 1
and h1 = hs−1 imply that K[G] is Gorenstein and hence that h(K[G]; t) is palindromic. Thus,
both [8, Theorem 1.1] and Corollary 4.5 show that, once the leading coeﬃcient is equal to one, the
equality of the ﬁrst and next-to-leading coeﬃcients forces the symmetry of all the coeﬃcients.
The two results overlap in the case of weakly connected simple collections of cells. Indeed, the
associated bipartite graph G(P) is weakly chordal and IP coincides with the toric ideal deﬁning its
edge ring (see [2, Proposition 3.2 and Theorem 3.3]), so [8, Theorem 1.1] and our criterion concern
the same polynomial and give compatible conclusions whenever hK[P] (t) = reP (t). On the other
hand, Corollary 4.5 is formulated entirely in terms of switching rook conﬁgurations and applies to
arbitrary collections of cells, without any connectedness or hole-freeness assumption. In this sense,
our result extends to arbitrary collections of cells the same numerical principle for palindromicity.
   The paper is organized as follows. In Section 1, we recall the basic deﬁnitions concerning
collections of cells, rook conﬁgurations, and switching equivalence. Section 2 introduces the gluing
of rectangles and the notion of domino-stability, and establishes some properties of domino-stable
collections needed in the sequel.
   In Section 3, we prove that domino-stability implies palindromicity. After constructing the
unique switching class of maximum rook conﬁgurations, we use the decomposition into directed
paths and directed cycles described above to obtain the bijections between the switching classes
of k-rook and (r(P) − k)-rook conﬁgurations.
   Section 4 proves the converse implication. We construct an injective map from one-rook classes
to (r(P)−1)-rook classes and show that this map cannot be surjective when P is not domino-stable.
This completes the proof of Theorem 4.4 and yields the numerical criterion of Corollary 4.5.
   Finally, Section 5 discusses the algebraic consequences of the main theorem. For collections
of cells P satisfying hK[P] (t) = reP (t), Corollary 5.1 relates domino-stability to the Gorenstein
property of K[P]. We conclude with Conjecture 5.2, motivated by the computational experiments
in [13].

                1. Collections of cells and switching rook polynomials
  In this section, we present the combinatorial preliminaries on collections of cells and provide the
deﬁnition and basic properties of the switching rook polynomial.
1.1. Collections of cells. Let (i, j), (k, l) ∈ Z2 . We deﬁne a partial order on Z2 by setting
(i, j) ≤ (k, l) if and only if i ≤ k and j ≤ l. Given a = (i, j) and b = (k, l) in Z2 with a ≤ b, the
set [a, b] = {(m, n) ∈ Z2 : i ≤ m ≤ k, j ≤ n ≤ l} is called an interval of Z2 . If i < k and j < l, the
interval [a, b] is said to be proper ; in this case, we refer to a and b as the diagonal corners of [a, b],
and to c = (i, l) and d = (k, j) as the anti-diagonal corners. More precisely, a, b, c, and d are
respectively called the lower left, upper right, upper left, and lower right corners of [a, b]. If j = l
(respectively, i = k), then a and b are in a horizontal (respectively, vertical ) position. We denote by
]a, b[ the set {(m, n) ∈ Z2 : i < m < k, j < n < l}. A proper interval C = [a, b] with b = a+(1, 1)
is called a cell of Z2 . If C = [a, b] is a cell with c, d anti-diagonal corners, then we denote the
set of vertices and edges of C by V (C) = {a, b, c, d} and E(C) = {{a, c}, {c, b}, {b, d}, {a, d}},
respectively. LetS P be a non-empty collection

P as V (P) = C∈P V (C) and E(P) = C∈P E(C), respectively. The rank of P, denoted by |P|,
is the number of cells in P.
We say that P is a polyomino if for any two distinct cells C and D in P there exists a sequence of

1.2. Switching rook polynomial. We now introduce the terminology of non-attacking rooks on
a collection of cells and the switching rook polynomial.
  Let P be a collection of cells. We say that two rooks R1 and R2 coincide or overlap if they are
located in the same cell of P. Two rooks R1 and R2 are in attacking position or attacking rooks

R1 and R2 are placed. In contrast, two rooks are in non-attacking position or non-attacking rooks
in P if they do not overlap and they are not in attacking position. A j-rook conﬁguration in P

and the indices t1 and t2 are distinct, because the two maximal paths are disjoint. Therefore, Cs1 t1
and Cs2 t2 are opposite corner cells of a rectangle contained in E, whose other two corner cells are

other rooks of R∨ and U ∨ coincide. Consequently, R∨ ∼ U ∨ .

                     5. Algebraic consequences and an open problem
   Let P be a collection of cells, and let SP = K[xv | v ∈ V (P)] be the polynomial ring associated
with P, where K is a ﬁeld. We recall that a proper interval [a, b] of Z2 is called an inner interval
of P if all cells in R([a, b]) belong to P. If [a, b] is an inner interval of P, with diagonal corners a
and b and anti-diagonal corners c and d, then the binomial xa xb − xc xd is called an inner 2-minor
of P. The ideal IP of SP generated by all inner 2-minors of P is called the ideal of 2-minors of P.
The quotient ring K[P] = SP /IP is called the coordinate ring of P.
   Let hK[P](t) denote its h-polynomial, that is, the numerator of the reduced Hilbert-Poincaré
series of K[P]. For several classes of collections of cells, it is known that hK[P](t) = reP (t); see, for
instance, [3, 4, 10, 12, 15, 17, 18, 23]. The following consequence of Theorem 4.4 applies whenever
this equality holds.
Corollary 5.1. Let P be a collection of cells such that hK[P] (t) = reP (t). Then the following
statements hold:
    (1) If K[P] is Gorenstein, then P is domino-stable.
    (2) If K[P] is a Cohen–Macaulay domain, then K[P] is Gorenstein if and only if P is domino-
        stable.
Proof. Suppose ﬁrst that K[P] is Gorenstein. By [20, Theorem 4.1], the h-polynomial of K[P] is
palindromic. Since hK[P](t) = reP (t), the switching rook polynomial of P is palindromic. Therefore,
Theorem 4.4 implies that P is domino-stable.
    PALINDROMIC SWITCHING ROOK POLYNOMIALS AND DOMINO-STABLE COLLECTIONS OF CELLS 19

   Suppose now that K[P] is a Cohen–Macaulay domain. The forward implication follows from
part (1). Conversely, assume that P is domino-stable. By Theorem 4.4, reP (t) is palindromic. It
follows from the assumed equality hK[P] (t) = reP (t) that the h-polynomial of K[P] is palindromic.
Hence [20, Theorem 4.4] yields that K[P] is Gorenstein.                         
   For simple thin polyominoes and parallelogram polyominoes, the preceding result recovers [18,
Theorem 4.2] and [17, Theorem 4.10], respectively. More generally, Corollary 5.1 applies to every
class of collections of cells for which the equality hK[P](t) = reP (t) and the Cohen–Macaulay domain
property are known.
   The Cohen–Macaulay domain assumption in Corollary 5.1 is essential for the application of
Stanley’s criterion. Nevertheless, computational experiments reported in [13] suggest that domino-
stability may be suﬃcient for the Gorenstein property even without this assumption. This moti-
vates the following conjecture.
Conjecture 5.2. Let P be a collection of cells. If P is domino-stable, then K[P] is Gorenstein.
