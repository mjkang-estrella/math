                                                   SWITCHING ROOK POLYNOMIAL OF COLLECTIONS OF CELLS

                                                     FRANCESCO NAVARRA, AYESHA ASLOOB QURESHI, AND GIANCARLO RINALDO

                                                   Abstract. We explore the novel connection between rook placements on collections of cells, also
                                                   known as pruned chessboards, and the algebraic properties of ideals generated by 2-minors. We
                                                   design an algorithm to compute the switching rook polynomial of a collection of cells and show that
                                                   it coincides with the h-polynomial of the associated coordinate ring for all collections up to rank 10
                                                   and polyominoes up to rank 12. Motivated by this evidence, we conjecture that the correspondence

                                                                                            Introduction
                                              Polyominoes are planar ﬁgures formed by joining unit squares edge to edge. Originating in recre-
                                           ational mathematics and classical combinatorics, they have been extensively studied in connection
                                           with tiling and enumeration problems; see [5]. The study of polyomino ideals, initiated in [11], has
                                           attracted considerable attention due to the rich interplay between the combinatorial structure of
                                           a polyomino and the algebraic properties of its associated binomial ideal. Given a ﬁeld K and a
                                           polyomino P, the polyomino ideal IP encodes the adjacency relations between the cells of P, and
                                           its quotient ring K[P] = SP /IP captures important algebraic invariants deeply inﬂuenced by the
                                           geometry of P. Since their ﬁrst appearance in 2012, polyomino ideals have been the subject of a
                                           broad and growing body of literature; see, for example, [9, Chapter 8], [3, 7, 12, 13, 14, 17], and the
                                           references therein. Let X be an m × n matrix of indeterminates. Polyomino ideals are generated
                                           by certain sets of 2-minors of X, generalizing the notion of determinantal ideals It (X) for t = 2,
                                           and include several well-known classes of binomial ideals, such as those generated by 2-minors of
                                           ladder shapes and the join-meet ideals of planar distributive lattices (toric ideal of Hibi rings). We
                                           refer reader to [12] for more details.
                                              The initial motivation for connecting polyominoes with binomial ideals was to facilitate the
                                           study of ideals generated by 2-minors and to translate their algebraic properties into combinatorial
                                           aspects of polyominoes. In recent years, however, a surprising and elegant connection has emerged
                                           between polyomino ideals and a classical object in enumerative combinatorics: the rook polynomial,
                                           originally introduced to count non-attacking rook placements on chessboards. For instance, the
                                           number of ways to place k indistinguishable non-attacking rooks on an m×n rectangular chessboard
                                           Bm,n is captured by the well-known rook polynomial

Interestingly, algebraic invariants of the polyomino ideal, such as the Hilbert series and the Casteln-
uovo–Mumford regularity of K[P], encode combinatorially meaningful data: namely, the switching
rook polynomial (also referred to as the nested rook polynomial in [1]) and the rook number of P,
respectively. This connection was ﬁrst observed in [3], where the authors showed that the Castel-
nuovo–Mumford regularity of K[P] coincides with the rook number of P when P is an L-convex
polyomino. The connection was further explored in [14], where it was observed that for simple thin
polyominoes, the rook polynomial coincides with the h-polynomial of K[P], and the rook number
again matches the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity. Subsequently, Qureshi, Rinaldo and Romeo
proposed the following conjecture:
Conjecture 0.1. [12, Conjecture 3.2] If P is a simple polyomino (a polyomino without holes),
then its switching rook polynomial coincides with the h-polynomial of the associated coordinate
ring K[P].
   Since then, several researchers have investigated various classes of polyominoes to further explore
the relationship between rook polynomials and their algebraic counterparts. In this article, we
signiﬁcantly extend the previous conjecture to the more general setting of arbitrary collections
of cells. We computationally verify that the switching rook polynomial of a collection of cells
coincides with the h-polynomial of the associated coordinate ring for all collections up to rank 10
and for all polyominoes up to rank 12. Furthermore, we show that the conjecture holds for convex
polyominoes P satisfying the conditions described in Theorem 3.1. Our results oﬀer an algebraic
framework for computing and understanding these combinatorial quantities for broad classes of
convex polyominoes, including directed convex polyominoes, see Figure 2. In fact, we prove our
main result, Theorem 4.1, in a more general setting by considering convex collections of cells rather
than polyominoes alone.
   The paper is organized as follows. In Section 1, we recall the necessary background on collections
of cells and their associated ideals of 2-minors, along with relevant deﬁnitions from commutative
algebra.
   Section 2 provides a brief overview of the switching rook polynomial and related terminology,
and then presents Theorem 2.3, our main computational result. Complete details of the algorithm
are given in [10], where the reader can ﬁnd: SageMath scripts to generate polyominoes and collec-
tions of cells, Macaulay2 code to compute the switching rook polynomial, the rook number, and
the h-polynomial of the coordinate ring, and pre-generated datasets of collections of cells up to
rank 10 and polyominoes up to rank 12 suitable for use with the code. Finally, based on these
computations, we state Conjecture 2.4, which signiﬁcantly extends [12, Conjecture 3.2] to arbi-
trary collections of cells, and also includes the correspondence between the rook number and the
Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity of the coordinate ring.
   In Section 3, we review the conditions introduced in [11] under which a collection of cells P
admits a quadratic Gröbner basis. These are presented in Theorem 3.1 as Condition (#) and
Condition (#′ ).
   In Section 4, we prove our main result Theorem 4.1: if a convex collection of cells P (in particular,
any convex polyomino) satisﬁes either of Condition (#) or Condition (#′ ), then the switching
rook polynomial of P coincides with the h-polynomial of K[P], the coordinate ring associated
to P. Moreover, the rook number of P equals the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity of K[P].
This framework allows rook polynomials and rook numbers to be computed using tools from
computational algebra. For example, we demonstrate in Example 4.12 how Macaulay2 [6] can be
used to eﬃciently compute these invariants, providing a practical enumeration in more general

1.1. Ideals of 2-minors Associated to Collections of Cells. Let P be a collection of cells,
and let SP = K[xv : v ∈ V (P)] be the polynomial ring associated to P, where K is a ﬁeld. A
proper interval [a, b] is called an inner interval of P if all cells in P[a,b] belong to P. If [a, b] is
an inner interval of P, with diagonal corners a and b and anti-diagonal corners c and d, then the
binomial xa xb − xc xd is called an inner 2-minor of P. The ideal IP ⊂ SP generated by all inner
2-minors of P is called the ideal of 2-minors of P. If P is a polyomino, then IP is referred to as
the polyomino ideal of P. The quotient ring K[P] = SP /IP is called the coordinate ring of P.
Remark 1.1. Let P be a collection of cells, and let Q be the collection obtained from P by a
symmetry of the plane—i.e., by a translation, rotation, reﬂection, or glide reﬂection. Then IP and
IQ deﬁne the same ideal up to a relabeling of variables; in particular, K[P] ∼= K[Q].

placement of j rooks arranged in non-attacking positions in P, where j > 0; for convention, the
0-rook conﬁguration is ∅. Figure 3 illustrates two examples of 5-rook conﬁgurations.

collections of cells and n = 12 for polyominoes were veriﬁed using the HPC cluster Tosun provided
by Sabanci University, due to their large computational complexity, which made it impossible to
verify them on a standard machine.                                            
  The previous result leads us to formulate the following.
Conjecture 2.4. Let P be a collection of cells. Then the switching rook polynomial of P coincides
with the h-polynomial of K[P], and the rook number of P equals the regularity of K[P].
   This extends previous conjectures, which were restricted to simple polyominoes [12, Conjecture
3.2] or non-simple polyominoes ([7, Conjecture 4.9]): here the conjecture is veriﬁed computation-
ally for collections of cells up rank 10, including also polyominoes. Although our experiments
provide strong evidence for the validity of the conjecture, they do not amount to a general proof.
Nevertheless, as we will show in the next section, the validity of Conjecture 2.4 could oﬀer an
eﬃcient algebraic method to determine the switching rook polynomial and the rook number for
arbitrary collections of cells (see Example 4.12).

             |V (P ′′ )| = |V (P)| − |V (Pv )| + π(DPv ) + |Γ(RPv )|,          and |P ′′ | = |P| − |Pv |.

   Observe that there is a one-to-one correspondence between the cells of Pv and their lower-right
corners, which correspond exactly to V (Pv )\(H ∪V ), where H and V are the horizontal and vertical

                                    = |V (P)| − |P| − d + d = |V (P)| − |P|.

  This conﬁrms the claim. Hence, by Equation (2), we obtain that hK[P] (t) = r̃P ′ (t) + tr̃P ′′ (t).

To establish this, observe the following:
   (1) r̃i (P ′ ) is the cardinality of R̃i (P ′ ), which can be viewed as the number of i-rook conﬁgura-
       tions in P, up to switches, such that no rook is placed in the top-left corner cell X.
   (2) Assume that a rook T1 is placed on X, and let C be an (i − 1)-rook conﬁguration in P. If
       a rook of C, say T2 , lies on a cell of Pv , then T1 and T2 occupy anti-diagonal cells of some
       inner interval PI of Pv . This implies that T1 and T2 are in switching position. Hence, the
       conﬁguration
                                      C ′ := (C \ {T1 , T2 }) ∪ {T1′ , T2′ },
       where T1′ and T2′ are the rooks placed in the diagonal cells of PI , belongs to Ri (P ′ ). However,
       such a conﬁguration has already been counted in point (1). Therefore, we only need to
       count the (i − 1)-rook conﬁgurations in P, up to switches, where all rooks lie in P \ Pv .

       on X, coincides with the number of (i − 1)-rook conﬁgurations in P ′′ , up to switches, that
       is, r̃i−1 (P ′′ ).
From the previous two points, we easily deduce that the i-th coeﬃcient of r̃(t) is the number of
i-rook conﬁgurations of P, up to switches. Hence hK[P] (t) is the switching rook polynomial of P.

collection of cells, then K[P] is a Cohen-Macaulay domain by [2, Proposition 1.3], so reg K[P]
is the degree of the h-polynomial of K[P] by [18, Corollary B.4.1], which is the rook number of
