A note on the failure of Payne’s inequality on manifolds with boundary



Davide Buoso

Address: (D. Buoso) Dipartimento per lo Sviluppo Sostenibile e la Transizione Ecologica, Università degli Studi del Piemonte Orientale “A. Avogadro”, Piazza Sant’Eusebio 5, 13100 Vercelli, Italy

Email address: davide.buoso@uniupo.it

 and 
Vladimir Lotoreichik

Address: (V. Lotoreichik) Department of Mathematics, Faculty of Nuclear Sciences and Physical Engineering, Czech Technical University in Prague, Trojanova 13, 120 00, Prague, Czech Republic

Email address: vladimir.lotoreichik@fjfi.cvut.cz



Abstract.
 

We show that Payne’s conjecture on the comparison between Dirichlet Laplacian eigenvalues and buckling eigenvalues does not hold on flat and
hyperbolic cylindrical surfaces and on (round) hemispheres.
 



Key words and phrases: Buckling eigenvalues, Dirichlet spectrum, Payne’s conjecture, counterexamples


2020 Mathematics Subject Classification35P15, 58J50, 53C21




1. Introduction




Let $\Omega$ be a bounded domain with smooth non-empty boundary $\partial\Omega$ 
in a complete smooth $d$ -dimensional Riemannian manifold with $d\geq 2$ . The domain $\Omega$ is equipped
with the metric $g$ inherited from the ambient manifold. Denote by
 $\lambda_{k}(\Omega)$ the Dirichlet eigenvalues of the positive
Laplace–Beltrami operator on $\Omega$ and by $\Lambda_{k}(\Omega)$ the clamped
buckling eigenvalues, both ordered increasingly and counted
with multiplicity. We will address the validity of the inequality



(GP)

 $\Lambda_{k}(\Omega)\geq\lambda_{k+1}(\Omega),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N},$ 



which is known as (generalized) Payne’s conjecture.
Payne [18] proved the inequality for $k=1$ in the case of bounded planar Euclidean domains; a small gap in his
argument was later closed by Friedlander [7],
who also showed that the stronger comparison $\Lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{3}$ 
fails in general even in the case of planar Euclidean domains. Liu’s proposed all-index proof [16] was
withdrawn after an error was identified by Friedlander
[8]. Levitin and Polterovich [12]
recently proved (GP) for every odd index $k$ on bounded planar domains and on certain classes of flat manifolds;
their result also covers an even index $k$ when
 $\Lambda_{k}=\Lambda_{k+1}$ , however the general even-index case remains open.
On the other hand, Buoso, Luzzini, Provenzano, and Stubbe [3] showed that (GP)
fails at every even index in dimension one. Note that Payne’s conjecture (GP) sparkled a
wide range of interest for comparison between eigenvalues of related yet different operators;
see [1, 2, 6, 9, 10, 14, 19]
and the references therein.




In this paper we provide some counterexamples to (GP) on curved and flat
manifolds. In particular, we show that a two-dimensional flat cylindrical surface with a sufficiently short periodic direction
retains the one-dimensional failure at $k=2$ . Additionally, by means of perturbation and scaling we are able to show that the two-dimensional hyperbolic cylindrical surface of every prescribed negative constant Gaussian curvature also provides a counterexample to (GP).
Moreover, by means of constructing a $(d+1)$ -dimensional clamped trial space on the hemisphere $\mathbb{S}^{d}_{+}$ , we can show that



(1.1)

 $\Lambda_{d+1}(\mathbb{S}^{d}_{+})\leq\frac{19d^{2}+56d+48}{7d+8}<3(d+2)=\lambda_{d+2}(\mathbb{S}^{d}_{+}),\qquad d\geq 2.$ 



We remark that domains on the sphere also serve as counterexamples [17] to the validity of Friedlander-Filonov inequality (cf.  [11, §3.2.4]) on manifolds with boundary.




We also note that we prompted AI to conduct numerical simulations of (GP) for bounded domains in $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ and $\mathbb{H}^{d}$ for $d=2,3,4,5$ ; the prompt given was specifically designed to search for possible counterexamples to (GP). However, after refining the numerical scheme, no counterexample was reported in those spaces.




The paper is organized as follows: in Section 2 we provide the variational setup, and Section 3 is devoted to the counterexamples to inequality (GP).





2. Variational framework




Throughout the present paper, all function spaces consist of real-valued functions.
We will denote by $-\Delta_{g}$ the positive Laplace-Beltrami operator on $\Omega$ equipped with the metric $g$ inherited from the ambient Riemannian manifold. We recall the Dirichlet eigenvalue problem for $-\Delta_{g}$ on a domain $\Omega$ 




 $\begin{cases}-\Delta_{g}u=\lambda u,&\text{in\ }\Omega,\\
u=0,&\text{on\ }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}$ 



which has a discrete set of positive eigenvalues $\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}$ , which in turn can be variationally characterized as



(2.1)

 $\lambda_{k}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}E\subset H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\\
\dim E=k\end{subarray}}\max_{0\neq u\in E}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle_{g}{\,\mathrm{d}}V_{g}}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}V_{g}},$ 



where $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{g}$ stands for the inner product in the tangent space with respect to the metric $g$ .
Similarly, we recall the buckling eigenvalue problem on a domain $\Omega$ 



(2.2)

 $\begin{cases}\Delta_{g}^{2}U=-\Lambda\Delta_{g}U,&\text{in\ }\Omega,\\
U=\partial_{\nu}U=0,&\text{on\ }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}$ 



where $\partial_{\nu}U$ stands for the normal derivative of $U$ .
This spectral problem has a discrete set of positive eigenvalues $\{\Lambda_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}$ , which in turn can be variationally characterized as



(2.3)

 $\Lambda_{k}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}E\subset H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\\
\dim E=k\end{subarray}}\max_{0\neq u\in E}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\Delta_{g}u)^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}V_{g}}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle_{g}{\,\mathrm{d}}V_{g}}.$ 



Here and occasionally in the following, we use the shorthand notation $\lambda_{k}$ and $\Lambda_{k}$ instead of $\lambda_{k}(\Omega)$ and $\Lambda_{k}(\Omega)$ , respectively.




It is nowadays classical to show that $\Lambda_{k}\geq\lambda_{k}$ for any domain of finite measure (see e.g., [2, Theorem 2.2]). If one supposes, in addition, that $\Omega$ is oriented and is bounded with a boundary of class $C^{2}$ , then it is possible to show that in fact $\Lambda_{k}>\lambda_{k}$ (see e.g., [15, Theorem 1.1]).




We also observe that provided variational formulation for (2.2) is essentially the only one in Euclidean spaces, but as soon as the curvature is non-zero then substantially different formulations may arise, since the so-called Hessian energy may differ from the Laplacian energy due to the Reilly identity [20, Theorem 1] (see also [5, Eq. 3.24]) for $u\in H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)$ 



(2.4)

 $\int_{\Omega}|D^{2}u|^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}V_{g}=\int_{\Omega}(\Delta_{g}u)^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}V_{g}-\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{Ric}(\nabla u,\nabla u){\,\mathrm{d}}V_{g},$ 



where $D^{2}u$ is the Hessian of $u$ , $|D^{2}u|$ is its Hilbert-Schmidt norm, and $\operatorname{Ric}(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the Ricci tensor on $\Omega$ .
For constant sectional curvature $\kappa$ , $\operatorname{Ric}=(d-1)\kappa g$ ,
so min–max principle gives the exact shift



(2.5)

 $\Lambda_{k}^{\mathrm{Hess}}=\Lambda_{k}-(d-1)\kappa,$ 



where




 $\Lambda_{k}^{\mathrm{Hess}}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}E\subset H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\\
\dim E=k\end{subarray}}\max_{0\neq u\in E}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|D^{2}u|^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}V_{g}}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle_{g}{\,\mathrm{d}}V_{g}},$ 



Moreover, we remark that both $\lambda_{k}$ and $\Lambda_{k}$ have the same scaling law: given a constant metric rescaling $\widehat{g}=cg$ with $c>0$ on a fixed domain $\Omega$ , we have



(2.6)

 $\lambda_{k}(\Omega,\widehat{g})=c^{-1}\lambda_{k}(\Omega,g),\qquad\Lambda_{k}(\Omega,\widehat{g})=c^{-1}\Lambda_{k}(\Omega,g),\qquad K_{\widehat{g}}=c^{-1}K_{g},$ 



where we explicitly indicated the dependence of the eigenvalues on the metric and where by $K_{g}$ and $K_{\widehat{g}}$ we denote the sectional curvature on $\Omega$ corresponding to the metrics $g$ and $\widehat{g}$ , respectively.




Finally, we also recall the following continuity result, which will be used later on.



Lemma 2.1.



Suppose that the smooth metrics $g_{\varepsilon}$ , on the closure of a fixed
bounded smooth domain $\Omega$ , $C^{2}$ -converge to the smooth metric $g_{0}$ as $\varepsilon\rightarrow 0$ . Then, for every $k\in\mathbb{N}$ ,




 $\lambda_{k}(\Omega,g_{\varepsilon})\longrightarrow\lambda_{k}(\Omega,g_{0}),\qquad\Lambda_{k}(\Omega,g_{\varepsilon})\longrightarrow\Lambda_{k}(\Omega,g_{0}),\qquad\text{as}~\varepsilon\rightarrow 0.$ 






Proof.



The Sobolev spaces for metrics $g_{\varepsilon}$ and $g_{0}$ are topologically equivalent. We use the same notation for these spaces $L^{2}(\Omega)$ , $H^{1}(\Omega)$ and $H^{2}(\Omega)$ and the respective norms $\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}:=\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}V_{g_{0}}$ , $\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}:=\||\nabla u|\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}$ , and $\|u\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}:=\||D^{2}u|\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}$ will be for convenience evaluated in the limiting metric $g_{0}$ .
We introduce the symmetric bilinear forms




 $\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}_{g}[u,v]$ 
 $\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle_{g}{\,\mathrm{d}}V_{g},$ 
 $\displaystyle\qquad\mathrm{dom}\,\mathfrak{d}_{g}=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),$ 



 $\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}_{g}[u,v]$ 
 $\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(\Delta_{g}u)(\Delta_{g}v){\,\mathrm{d}}V_{g},$ 
 $\displaystyle\qquad\mathrm{dom}\,\mathfrak{a}_{g}=H^{2}_{0}(\Omega),$ 



 $\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}_{g}[u,v]$ 
 $\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}uv{\,\mathrm{d}}V_{g},$ 
 $\displaystyle\qquad\mathrm{dom}\,\mathfrak{b}_{g}=L^{2}(\Omega).$ 



The Laplace-Beltrami operator in local coordinates acts as




 $\Delta_{g}u=|g|^{-1/2}\partial_{i}\bigl(|g|^{1/2}g^{ij}\partial_{j}u\bigr).$ 



Uniform convergence of the coefficients in the expression for the Laplace-Beltrami operator
implies that there exists $\delta_{\varepsilon}>0$ satisfying $\delta_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow 0$ as $\varepsilon\rightarrow 0$ such that




 $\displaystyle|\mathfrak{a}_{g_{\varepsilon}}[u]-\mathfrak{a}_{g_{0}}[u]|$ 
 $\displaystyle\leq\delta_{\varepsilon}\|u\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},\qquad$ 
 $\displaystyle\text{for all}\,u\in H^{2}_{0}(\Omega),$ 



 $\displaystyle|\mathfrak{d}_{g_{\varepsilon}}[u]-\mathfrak{d}_{g_{0}}[u]|$ 
 $\displaystyle\leq\delta_{\varepsilon}\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2},\qquad$ 
 $\displaystyle\text{for all}\,u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),$ 



 $\displaystyle|\mathfrak{b}_{g_{\varepsilon}}[u]-\mathfrak{b}_{g_{0}}[u]|$ 
 $\displaystyle\leq\delta_{\varepsilon}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},$ 
 $\displaystyle\qquad\text{for all}\,u\in L^{2}(\Omega).$ 



The Dirichlet elliptic estimate and Poincaré inequality yield
that there exists a constant $C\geq 1$ such that
 $\|u\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C\mathfrak{a}_{g_{0}}[u]$ for all $u\in H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)$ ; $\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C\mathfrak{d}_{g_{0}}[u]$ for all $u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)$ . Thus, setting
 $\hat{\delta}_{\varepsilon}=C\delta_{\varepsilon}$ , we get




 $\begin{split}(1-\hat{\delta}_{\varepsilon})\mathfrak{a}_{g_{0}}&\leq\mathfrak{a}_{g_{\varepsilon}}\leq(1+\hat{\delta}_{\varepsilon})\mathfrak{a}_{g_{0}},\\
(1-\hat{\delta}_{\varepsilon})\mathfrak{d}_{g_{0}}&\leq\mathfrak{d}_{g_{\varepsilon}}\leq(1+\hat{\delta}_{\varepsilon})\mathfrak{d}_{g_{0}},\\
(1-\hat{\delta}_{\varepsilon})\mathfrak{b}_{g_{0}}&\leq\mathfrak{b}_{g_{\varepsilon}}\leq(1+\hat{\delta}_{\varepsilon})\mathfrak{b}_{g_{0}},\end{split}$ 



as quadratic forms. The two
min-max principles (2.1) and (2.3) combined with the above estimates bound each perturbed eigenvalue corresponding to the metric $g_{\varepsilon}$ between factors
tending to one times its unperturbed value corresponding to the metric $g_{0}$ , from which the desired continuity of the eigenvalues follows.
∎






3. Counterexamples




In this section, we will present several counterexamples to the validity of Payne’s inequality on manifolds with boundary. In particular, we will demonstrate in Subsection 3.1 that the inequality can fail on a flat cylindrical surface, in Subsection 3.2 we will show that the inequality can also fail on a cylindrical surface with arbitrary constant negative Gaussian curvature and, finally, in Subsection 3.3 we will show that it also fails on a round hemisphere of any dimension.




3.1. Flat cylindrical surface




Let $\rho>0$ and let $\mathbb{S}^{1}_{\rho}$ be the circle with metric $\rho^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}\theta^{2}$ ,
where $0\leq\theta<2\pi$ . The points on this circle can be identified with the points in the interval $[0,2\pi)$ .
Define the cylindrical surface of height $1$ with the base $\mathbb{S}^{1}_{\rho}$ as




 $C_{\rho}=(0,1)\times\mathbb{S}^{1}_{\rho}$ 



equipped with the metric




 $g={\,\mathrm{d}}x^{2}+\rho^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}\theta^{2}.$ 



Note that the boundary components of $C_{\rho}$ are smooth circles.



Theorem 3.1.



If $\rho\in(0,\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}\pi})$ , then




 $\Lambda_{2}(C_{\rho})=4z_{1}^{2}<9\pi^{2}=\lambda_{3}(C_{\rho}),$ 



where $z_{1}\in(\pi,3\pi/2)$ is the first positive nonzero root of the transcendental equation
 $\tan z=z$ .




Proof.



By separation of variables, the Dirichlet eigenvalues of the Laplacian on $C_{\rho}$ can be listed as



(3.1)

 $\lambda_{n,m}=\pi^{2}n^{2}+\frac{m^{2}}{\rho^{2}},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}.$ 



Clearly, the total sequence of Dirichlet eigenvalues of the Laplacian on $C_{\rho}$ is the total enumeration of all the above values, where repetitions account for multiplicities.
The assumption on $\rho$ places the first non-constant angular level ( $|m|=1$ )
strictly above $9\pi^{2}$ . Thus the first three eigenvalues of the Dirichlet Laplacian
on $C_{\rho}$ are simple and given by $\pi^{2}$ , $4\pi^{2}$ , and $9\pi^{2}$ (all of them correspond to $m=0$ ). In particular, $\lambda_{3}(C_{\rho})=9\pi^{2}$ .




Again applying the separation of variables, we observe that the buckling eigenvalues on $C_{\rho}$ can be listed as



(3.2)

 $\Lambda_{n,m}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}E\subset H^{2}_{0}(0,1)\\
\dim E=n\end{subarray}}\max_{0\neq f\in E}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left(-f^{\prime\prime}+\tfrac{m^{2}}{\rho^{2}}f\right)^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}x}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left((f^{\prime})^{2}+\tfrac{m^{2}}{\rho^{2}}f^{2}\right){\,\mathrm{d}}x},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}.$ 



The total family of buckling eigenvalues on $C_{\rho}$ is the enumeration of all the above values, where repetitions account for multiplicities.
The values $\Lambda_{n,0}$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$ 
can be found as values $q^{2}$ for $q>0$ for which the following system




 $\begin{cases}f^{(4)}+q^{2}f^{\prime\prime}=0,\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{in}\,\,(0,1),\\
f(0)=f(1)=f^{\prime}(0)=f^{\prime}(1)=0,\end{cases}$ 



has a non-trivial solution in $C^{\infty}([0,1])$ . The existence of several linearly independent solutions for a given value $q^{2}$ accounts for the multiplicity of the respective eigenvalue.




The general solution of $f^{(4)}+q^{2}f^{\prime\prime}=0$ is




 $f(x)=a+bx+c\cos(qx)+d\sin(qx),$ 



with constants $a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R}$ .
The conditions at $x=0$ give $a=-c$ and $b=-qd$ .
The remaining equations are




 $\begin{pmatrix}\cos q-1&\sin q-q\\
-\sin q&\cos q-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c\\
d\end{pmatrix}=0.$ 



The corresponding determinant equals



(3.3)

 $2(1-\cos q)-q\sin q=4\sin(q/2)\bigl[\sin(q/2)-(q/2)\cos(q/2)\bigr].$ 



From the construction we notice that all the eigenvalues are simple.
The apparent root $q=0$ gives no nonzero clamped eigenfunction and
is excluded. The positive roots are $q=2k\pi$ , $k\in\mathbb{N}$ and $q=2z_{k}$ ,
where $z_{k}\in(\pi k,\pi k+\pi/2)$ , $k\in\mathbb{N}$ solves the transcendental equation $\tan z=z$ .
On the intervals $(\pi k,\pi k+\pi/2)$ the function $\tan z-z$ increases from a negative number to
 $+\infty$ , with derivative $\tan^{2}z$ ; there are no other positive
roots. Therefore we get that
 $\Lambda_{1,0}=4\pi^{2}$ and $\Lambda_{2,0}=4z_{1}^{2}$ . Moreover, we notice that $4z_{1}^{2}<9\pi^{2}$ .




Let $m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}$ . Let us expand $f\in H^{2}_{0}(0,1)\setminus\{0\}$ into the series
 $f(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{k}\sqrt{2}\sin(\pi kx)$ with some real coefficients $\{c_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}$ .
This is a series in terms of normalized eigenfunctions of the Dirichlet Laplacian on the interval $(0,1)$ . Since $f$ is a non-zero function, we can exclude the case when all coefficients $c_{k}$ are equal to zero. Then we obtain that




 $\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left(-f^{\prime\prime}+\tfrac{m^{2}}{\rho^{2}}f\right)^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}x}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left((f^{\prime})^{2}+\tfrac{m^{2}}{\rho^{2}}f^{2}\right){\,\mathrm{d}}x}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\pi^{2}k^{2}+\tfrac{m^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)^{2}|c_{k}|^{2}}{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\pi^{2}k^{2}+\tfrac{m^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)|c_{k}|^{2}}\geq\pi^{2}+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}>9\pi^{2}.$ 



Hence, we conclude from (3.2) that $\Lambda_{1,m}>9\pi^{2}$ for all $m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}$ . Thus, $\Lambda_{2}(C_{\rho})=4z_{1}^{2}$ and the claim follows
from the observation that $4z_{1}^{2}<9\pi^{2}=\lambda_{3}(C_{\rho})$ .
∎





We observe that Theorem 3.1 is a counterexample to (GP) for $k=2$ and therefore does not violate the results in [12], where inequality (GP) is shown for all flat two-dimensional cylindrical surfaces for all $k$ odd. In fact, we would like to highlight that it is possible to construct cylindrical surfaces such that inequality (GP) is violated for any given $k$ even [13].





3.2. Hyperbolic cylindrical surface




The aim of this subsection is to show that by deformation of the previous counterexample, one can construct domains in two-dimensional manifolds with arbitrary negative Gaussian curvature, for which Payne’s inequality fails.



Theorem 3.2.



For every $\kappa<0$ , there is a bounded smooth domain $\Omega$ in a two-dimensional manifold of constant Gaussian curvature $\kappa$ for which
 $\Lambda_{2}(\Omega)<\lambda_{3}(\Omega)$ .




Proof.



On the manifold $\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}$ we define the metric




 $g_{\varepsilon}={\,\mathrm{d}}x^{2}+\rho^{2}\cosh^{2}(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,x){\,\mathrm{d}}\theta^{2},\qquad\varepsilon>0,$ 



with $\rho=\frac{1}{10}<\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}\pi}$ .
Consider now the domain $\Omega=(-1/2,1/2)\times\mathbb{S}^{1}$ .




For a metric ${\,\mathrm{d}}x^{2}+f(x)^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}\theta^{2}$ , the Gaussian curvature
is $K(x,\theta)=-f^{\prime\prime}(x)/f(x)$ (cf.  [4, §4-6]). Here $f^{\prime\prime}=\varepsilon f$ , so $K=-\varepsilon$ .
These metrics converge to the flat cylindrical surface metric in $C^{\infty}$ 
on $\overline{\Omega}$ as $\varepsilon\rightarrow 0$ . The strict gap in
Theorem 3.1 and Lemma 2.1
imply $\Lambda_{2}(\Omega,g_{\varepsilon})<\lambda_{3}(\Omega,g_{\varepsilon})$ 
for every sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$ .




Fix one such $\varepsilon$ , write $\kappa=-a$ with $a>0$ , and rescale the metric by




 $\widehat{g}=\frac{\varepsilon}{a}g_{\varepsilon}.$ 



Equation (2.6) gives $K_{\widehat{g}}=-a=\kappa$ ,
while multiplying both eigenvalue sequences by $a/\varepsilon$ 
preserves their strict ordering.
∎





We remark that the domains in the above theorem are not isometric to
bounded domains in ${\mathbb{H}}^{2}$ . Thus this construction
does not contradict the fact that we could not find numerically
a domain in ${\mathbb{H}}^{2}$ for which Payne’s inequality fails.





3.3. Round hemispheres




In this subsection, we show that Payne’s inequality fails on a round hemisphere of any dimension.
We embed $\mathbb{S}^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}$ and define
 $\mathbb{S}^{d}_{+}=\{x=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d+1})\in{\mathbb{S}}^{d}\colon x_{d+1}>0\}$ . The equator is the smooth boundary of ${\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+}$ .
The metric on $\mathbb{S}^{d}_{+}$ is induced by its natural embedding in ${\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}$ .



Theorem 3.3.



For every $d\geq 2$ ,



(3.4)

 $\Lambda_{d+1}(\mathbb{S}^{d}_{+})\leq Q_{d}:=\frac{19d^{2}+56d+48}{7d+8}<3(d+2)=\lambda_{d+2}(\mathbb{S}^{d}_{+}).$ 



The strictly positive variational margin is



(3.5)

 $3(d+2)-Q_{d}=\frac{2d(d+5)}{7d+8}.$ 






Proof.



We split the argument into two steps. In the first step, we compute $\lambda_{d+2}({\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+})$ and in the second step we estimate $\Lambda_{d+1}({\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+})$ from above.




Step 1. Dirichlet Laplacian eigenvalues on ${\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+}$ .
Odd reflection across the totally geodesic equator extends
an eigenfunction of the Dirichlet Laplacian on ${\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+}$ to an eigenfunction of the Laplacian on ${\mathbb{S}}^{d}$ .
Conversely, the restrictions to ${\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+}$ of the eigenfunctions of the Laplacian on ${\mathbb{S}}^{d}$ which are odd with respect to reflection $x_{d+1}\mapsto-x_{d+1}$ constitute the complete family of the eigenfunctions of the Dirichlet Laplacian on ${\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+}$ .
Below we rely on the analysis of spherical harmonics in [11, §1.2.3].
Degree $\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}$ spherical harmonics correspond to the eigenvalue
 $\ell(\ell+d-1)$ of the Laplacian on ${\mathbb{S}}^{d}$ . Using the explicit characterization of spherical harmonics
we can single out those which are odd with respect to reflection $x_{d+1}\mapsto-x_{d+1}$ .
We observe that for $\ell=0$ there are no such harmonics, for $\ell=1$ 
the basis of such spherical harmonics is $\{x_{d+1}\}$ , while for $\ell=2$ the basis of such spherical harmonics is $\{x_{d+1}x_{1},\ldots,x_{d+1}x_{d}\}$ .
There are non-zero odd spherical harmonics corresponding to $\ell=3$ ; for instance
 $x_{d+1}x_{1}x_{2}$ .
Hence



(3.6)

 $\lambda_{1}({\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+})=d,\qquad\lambda_{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+})=\cdots=\lambda_{d+1}({\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+})=2(d+1),\qquad\lambda_{d+2}({\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+})=3(d+2).$ 






Step 2. Trial space for the clamped buckling problem.
Take the trial space




 $E=\Span\big\{x^{2}_{d+1},x^{2}_{d+1}x_{1},\ldots,x^{2}_{d+1}x_{d}\big\}.$ 



These $d+1$ functions are clearly linearly independent.
Their values and gradients vanish on $\partial{\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+}$ , so
 $E\subset H^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{S}^{d}_{+})$ . Let us denote in the following by $\Delta$ and $\nabla$ , respectively, the Laplacian and the gradient on ${\mathbb{S}}^{d}$ and by $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ the inner product in the tangent space to ${\mathbb{S}}^{d}$ with respect to the round metric.
We have standard identities for $i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,d+1\}$ 




 $\Delta x_{j}=-dx_{j},\qquad\langle\nabla x_{i},\nabla x_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}-x_{i}x_{j}.$ 



The product rule gives



(3.7)

 $\displaystyle\Delta(x_{d+1}^{2})$ 
 $\displaystyle=2-2(d+1)x^{2}_{d+1},$ 



 $\displaystyle\Delta(x_{d+1}^{2}x_{i})$ 
 $\displaystyle=x_{i}[2-2(d+1)x_{d+1}^{2}]-dx_{d+1}^{2}x_{i}+4x_{d+1}\langle\nabla x_{d+1},\nabla x_{i}\rangle$ 




 $\displaystyle=2x_{i}-3(d+2)x_{d+1}^{2}x_{i},\qquad 1\leq i\leq d.$ 



Reflections $x_{i}\mapsto-x_{i}$ show that both bilinear forms
 $(\Delta u,\Delta v)_{L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+})}$ and $(-\Delta u,v)_{L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+})}$ are diagonal in the basis
 $\{x_{d+1}^{2},x_{d+1}^{2}x_{1},\dots,x_{d+1}^{2}x_{d}\}$ , that is for all $i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}$ with $i\neq j$ 



(3.8)

 $\displaystyle\big(\Delta(x_{d+1}^{2}),\Delta(x_{d+1}^{2}x_{i})\big)_{L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+})}$ 
 $\displaystyle=0,$ 



 $\displaystyle\big(\Delta(x_{d+1}^{2}x_{i}),\Delta(x_{d+1}^{2}x_{j})\big)_{L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+})}$ 
 $\displaystyle=0,$ 



 $\displaystyle\big(\Delta(x_{d+1}^{2}x_{i}),x_{d+1}^{2}x_{j}\big)_{L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+})}$ 
 $\displaystyle=0,$ 



 $\displaystyle\big(\Delta(x_{d+1}^{2}),x_{d+1}^{2}x_{j}\big)_{L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+})}$ 
 $\displaystyle=0.$ 



Let us introduce the notation for the clamped buckling Rayleigh quotient




 ${\mathsf{R}}[u]:=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+}}(\Delta u)^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}V}{\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+}}(-\Delta u)u{\,\mathrm{d}}V},\qquad u\in H^{2}_{0}({\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{+})\setminus\{0\}.$ 



This Rayleigh quotient is deduced from (2.3) upon integration by parts in the denominator.
Permutations among the first $d$ coordinates imply that the Rayleigh quotients evaluated on the last
 $d$ functions $\{x_{d+1}^{2}x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d}$ are
equal; i.e. 




 ${\mathsf{R}}[x_{d+1}^{2}x_{i}]={\mathsf{R}}[x_{d+1}^{2}x_{j}],\qquad i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}.$ 



We use the mapping ${\mathsf{E}}\colon L^{1}({\mathbb{S}}^{d})\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}$ defined by




 ${\mathsf{E}}[u]:=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{d}}u{\,\mathrm{d}}V}{|{\mathbb{S}}^{d}|}.$ 



We stress that every integrand below is even in $x_{d+1}$ , and its
hemisphere mean equals its full-sphere mean.
For nonnegative integers $\{a_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d+1}$ , we find



(3.9)

 $\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{d+1}x_{j}^{2a_{j}}\right]$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}e^{-|x|^{2}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d+1}x_{j}^{2a_{j}}\right){\,\mathrm{d}}x}{\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}e^{-|x|^{2}}|x|^{\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}2a_{j}}{\,\mathrm{d}}x}$ 




 $\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma((d+1)/2)}{\Gamma((d+1)/2+\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}a_{j})}\prod_{j=1}^{d+1}\frac{\Gamma(a_{j}+1/2)}{\Gamma(1/2)}.$ 



In particular, for any $i\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}$ 



(3.10)

 $\begin{gathered}{\mathsf{E}}[x_{d+1}^{2}]=\frac{1}{d+1},\qquad{\mathsf{E}}[x_{d+1}^{4}]=\frac{3}{(d+1)(d+3)},\qquad{\mathsf{E}}[x_{i}^{2}]=\frac{1}{d+1},\\
{\mathsf{E}}[x_{d+1}^{2}x_{i}^{2}]=\frac{1}{(d+1)(d+3)},\qquad{\mathsf{E}}[x_{d+1}^{4}x_{i}^{2}]=\frac{3}{(d+1)(d+3)(d+5)}.\end{gathered}$ 



By (3.7) combined with (3.10) we obtain




 $\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}[x_{d+1}^{2}(-\Delta(x_{d+1}^{2}))]$ 
 $\displaystyle=2(d+1){\mathsf{E}}[x_{d+1}^{4}]-2{\mathsf{E}}[x_{d+1}^{2}]=\frac{4d}{(d+1)(d+3)},$ 



 $\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}[(\Delta(x_{d+1}^{2}))^{2}]$ 
 $\displaystyle=4(d+1)^{2}{\mathsf{E}}[x_{d+1}^{4}]-8(d+1){\mathsf{E}}[x_{d+1}^{2}]+4=\frac{8d}{d+3}.$ 



Thus, we get



(3.11)

 ${\mathsf{R}}[x_{d+1}^{2}]=\frac{{\mathsf{E}}[(\Delta(x_{d+1}^{2}))^{2}]}{{\mathsf{E}}[x_{d+1}^{2}(-\Delta(x_{d+1}^{2}))]}=2(d+1).$ 



Analogously, using (3.7) combined with (3.10) we get for $i\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}$ 




 $\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}[x_{d+1}^{2}x_{i}(-\Delta(x_{d+1}^{2}x_{i}))]$ 
 $\displaystyle=3(d+2){\mathsf{E}}[x_{d+1}^{4}x_{i}^{2}]-2{\mathsf{E}}[x_{d+1}^{2}x_{i}^{2}]$ 




 $\displaystyle=\frac{7d+8}{(d+1)(d+3)(d+5)},$ 



 $\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}[(\Delta(x_{d+1}^{2}x_{i}))^{2}]$ 
 $\displaystyle=9(d+2)^{2}{\mathsf{E}}[x_{d+1}^{4}x_{i}^{2}]-12(d+2){\mathsf{E}}[x_{d+1}^{2}x_{i}^{2}]+4{\mathsf{E}}[x_{i}^{2}]$ 




 $\displaystyle=\frac{19d^{2}+56d+48}{(d+1)(d+3)(d+5)}.$ 



Thus, we obtain that for all $i\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}$ 



(3.12)

 ${\mathsf{R}}[x_{d+1}^{2}x_{i}]=\frac{{\mathsf{E}}[(\Delta(x_{d+1}^{2}x_{i}))^{2}]}{{\mathsf{E}}[x_{d+1}^{2}x_{i}(-\Delta(x_{d+1}^{2}x_{i}))]}=\frac{19d^{2}+56d+48}{7d+8}=Q_{d}.$ 



Moreover, we observe that




 $Q_{d}-2(d+1)=\frac{5d^{2}+26d+32}{7d+8}>0.$ 



We conclude from (3.11), (3.12) combined with (3.8) that




 $\max_{u\in E\setminus\{0\}}{\mathsf{R}}[u]=Q_{d}.$ 



The min-max principle gives the first inequality in (3.4);
subtracting $Q_{d}$ from $3(d+2)$ gives (3.5).
∎
