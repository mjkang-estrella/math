Abstract
Given K_{n} whose edges are coloured red and blue, and a forest F of order n, we seek embeddings of F with small imbalance, that is, difference between the numbers of red and blue edges.
We show that if the 2-colouring of the edges of K_{n} is balanced, meaning that the numbers of red and blue edges are equal, and F has maximum degree \Delta, then one can find an embedding of F into K_{n} whose imbalance is at most \Delta/2+18, which is essentially best possible and resolves a conjecture of Mohr, Pardey, and Rautenbach.
Furthermore, we give a tighter bound for the imbalance for small values of \Delta.
In particular, we prove that the imbalance can be taken to be constant in the case where \Delta<n(1/4-\eta) for any constant \eta>0.
1 Introduction
Problems concerning substructures appearing when the edges of the complete graph are 2-coloured have become an area of interest in the past decades.
At one extreme lies Ramsey theory which asks for a monochromatic copy of a given graph H.
The problem of determining r(H) – the minimal n such that any 2-colouring of the edges of K_{n} contains a monochromatic copy of H – has grown to be a central area of research with remarkable recent breakthroughs by Campos, Griffiths, Morris and Sahasrabudhe [3] and by Mattheus and Verstraete [13].
Discrepancy is a notion which is closely related to those studied by Ramsey Theory.
Relaxing upon finding a monochromatic copy, one is looking for a copy of a given graph where some colour is much more represented than the other colours.
A significant amount of work has been produced on the subject; see for example [1, 2, 7, 9, 10].
At the other extreme of Ramsey-type and discrepancy problems, one could ask for a copy of H as far from monochrome as possible, that is to say, a colour-balanced copy of H.
We think of the edges of K_{n} as coloured with colours -1 and +1, and seek for a copy of H in which the sum of the colours of its edges is close to 0.
It is this question we focus on here, where H is an n-vertex forest.
Thus, consider the complete graph K_{n} with an edge colouring c\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}E(K_{n})\rightarrow\{-1,+1\}.
For convenience we think of the colours blue and red as -1 and +1, respectively.
For a subgraph H of K_{n} we define its sum to be c(H)\coloneqq\sum_{e\in E(H)}c(e).
We say that H is balanced if c(H)=0.
Given a graph H, we wish to find a balanced copy of it in K_{n} equipped with an edge colouring c\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}E(K_{n})\rightarrow\{-1,+1\}.
As this needs not be always possible, we seek for such a copy H^{\prime} of H in K_{n} such that |c(H^{\prime})| is as small as possible. For doing so, it makes sense to firstly consider an edge colouring of K_{n} which is balanced itself, meaning that precisely half of the edges are coloured -1 and precisely half of them are coloured +1, or in other words, so that c(K_{n})=0.
In this paper, given a balanced colouring of the edges of K_{n} by colours -1 and +1, we consider the problem of embedding an n-vertex forest F into K_{n} such that the colouring induced on F is as balanced as possible.
Some results are known for specific n-vertex forests, such as a perfect matching [6, 11] (and also [15] for balanced colouring with r\geq 3 colours), factors of P_{3}’s and P_{4}’s [14] and spanning paths [5].
For the general case where F is any fixed n-vertex forest, Mohr, Pardey and Rautenbach posed the following conjecture.
Conjecture 1.1 (Conjecture 3 in [14]).
Let c\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}E(K_{n})\rightarrow\{-1,+1\} be a balanced colouring and let F be an n-vertex forest with maximum degree \Delta.
Then there exists a copy F^{\prime} of F in K_{n} with
\left|c(F^{\prime})\right|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta-1).
Mohr, Pardey and Rautenbach remarked that one can always find a copy F^{\prime} of F satisfying \left|c(F^{\prime})\right|\leq\Delta+1.
Indeed, swapping the image of any vertex of F^{\prime} with the image of a leaf induces a change of c(F^{\prime}) by at most 2(\Delta+1).
Therefore, one can ‘interpolate’ between two copies F_{-} and F_{+} of F, which satisfy c(F_{-})\leq 0 and c(F_{+})\geq 0, by swapping the images of vertices by using a leaf as intermediate.
This way one can obtain a copy F^{\prime} satisfying |c(F^{\prime})|\leq\Delta+1.
See Lemma 2.1 for the details of a slight generalisation of this argument.
Pardey and Rautenbach [16] made progress towards proving Conjecture 1.1, showing that for any \varepsilon>0 there exists a constant C_{\varepsilon} such that there is a copy F^{\prime} of F in any balanced colouring of E(K_{n}) with colours -1 and +1 such that \left|c(F^{\prime})\right|\leq\left(\frac{3}{4}+\varepsilon\right)\Delta+C_{\varepsilon}.
In this paper we resolve Conjecture 1.1 up to an additive constant term.
Theorem 1.2.
Let F be an n-vertex forest with maximum degree \Delta, and let c\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}E(K_{n})\rightarrow\{-1,+1\} be a balanced colouring of the edges of K_{n}. Then there exists a copy F^{\prime} of F in K_{n} such that
\displaystyle\left|c(F^{\prime})\right|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta+18.
(1.1)
In fact, we prove the following stronger result for certain values of \Delta, from which we derive Theorem 1.2.
Theorem 1.3.
Let F be an n-vertex forest with maximum degree \Delta, and let c\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}E(K_{n})\rightarrow\{-1,+1\} be a balanced colouring of the edges of K_{n}.
Then there exists a copy F^{\prime} of F in K_{n} such that
\displaystyle\left|c(F^{\prime})\right|\leq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\Delta+9&\text{ if }\Delta\geq\frac{1}{2}n\text{ or }\Delta\leq 15,\\
\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta-\frac{1}{4}n+3\right)+\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}\left(\Delta-\frac{1}{4}n+1\right)^{2}+4n}&\text{ if }15<\Delta<\frac{1}{2}n.\end{cases}
(1.2)
For values of \Delta almost all the way up to \frac{n}{4}, Theorem 1.3 gives a constant upper bound.
Corollary 1.4.
Let F be an n-vertex forest with maximum degree 15\leq\Delta\leq\left(\frac{1}{4}-\eta\right)n, for some constant \eta\in\left(0,\frac{1}{4}\right).
Let c\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}E(K_{n})\rightarrow\{-1,+1\} be a balanced colouring of the edges of K_{n}.
Then there exists a copy F^{\prime} of F in K_{n} such that
\displaystyle\left|c(F^{\prime})\right|\leq(1+o(1))\frac{4}{\eta}.
While the additive constant of -\frac{1}{2} in Conjecture 1.1 cannot hold in general, as the example of embedding a Hamiltonian path into K_{n} where n is even shows (the absolute imbalance would have to be at least 1 due to parity considerations), we have made little effort to optimise the additive constant 18 in our bound.
We conclude this introduction by mentioning related work in the field.
Previous research has established the existence of spanning forests with small imbalance, although without specifying the isomorphic class of the forest.
For instance, Caro and Yuster [4] studied edge-colourings of K_{n} with colours -1 and +1 which have small imbalance, and show tight bounds on the imbalance that allows for the existence of an almost zero-sum spanning tree T, but without fixing the isomorphic class of T.
Caro, Hansberg, Lauri and Zarb [5] extended this study to sparser graphs G in place of the complete graph K_{n}.
Finally, Füredi and Kleitman [8] showed that when the edges of K_{n} are coloured using the set \{0,\dots,n-1\}, the graph contains a spanning tree whose sum is 0 mod n-1, a result for which Schrijver and Seymour later provided a simpler proof [17].
1.1 Notation and terminology
Throughout our proofs, we will often need to refer to the number of edges of a particular colour or within a particular subgraph, and to this end we use the following notation.
We associate the colour blue with -1 and the colour red with +1, and we will, without comment, switch between conventions as convenient.
For a graph H and a vertex v\in V(H) we denote by N_{H}(v) the neighbourhood of v in H and by \deg_{H}(v)=\left|N_{H}(v)\right| the degree of v in H.
Similarly, for a subgraph H^{\prime}\subset H we respectively denote by N_{H^{\prime}}(v) and \deg_{H^{\prime}}(v)=\left|N_{H^{\prime}}(v)\right| its neighbourhood and degree in H^{\prime}.
Given a colouring c\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}E(K_{n})\rightarrow\{-1,+1\}, we denote by B and R the subgraphs of K_{n} spanned by the edges coloured -1 (blue) and +1 (red), respectively.
In particular, N_{B}(v) and N_{R}(v) stand for the blue and red neighbourhoods and \deg_{B}(v) and \deg_{R}(v) stand for the blue and red degrees of v in K_{n} under c, respectively.
As mentioned earlier, we say that a colouring c\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}E(K_{n})\rightarrow\{-1,+1\} is balanced if we have c(K_{n})=0, where we recall that for a graph H its sum c(H) is defined to be c(H)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{e\in E(H)}c(e).
We will slightly abuse notation and say that a graph H is (almost) colour-balanced or simply (almost) balanced if the induced colouring on H contains (almost) as many blue edges than red edges, where the term “almost” is not defined quantitatively and serves mostly as a heuristic.
Given a graph H we denote by \Delta(H) and \delta(H) its maximum and minimum degrees.
For a real number x\in\mathbb{R} we write (x)_{+}=\max(x,0).
2 Preliminaries
We start with stating and proving Lemma 2.1.
This lemma allows us to find an almost balanced embedding, provided that we already know of the existence of two embeddings: one with a non-negative sum, and one with a non-positive sum.
This is a slight strengthening of a lemma appearing in previous papers (see, e.g., [14]).
Lemma 2.1.
Let n be an integer and suppose that c~\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}~E(K_{n})\rightarrow\{-1,+1\} is a balanced colouring.
Let H be a graph on n vertices with minimum degree \delta_{H}\coloneqq\delta(H).
Let h_{B},h_{R}~\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}~V(H)\rightarrow V(K_{n}) be two embeddings of H into K_{n}, with images H_{B},H_{R}, such that c(H_{B})\leq 0\leq c(H_{R}).
Let I\coloneqq\left\{v\in V(H)~\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}~h_{B}(v)\neq h_{R}(v)\right\} and set \Delta_{I}=\max\left\{\deg_{H}(v)~\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}~v\in I\right\}.
Then there exists a subgraph H^{\prime} of K_{n} which is isomorphic to H and such that |c(H^{\prime})|\leq\Delta_{I}+\delta_{H}.
Proof.
Since c(H_{B})\leq 0\leq c(H_{R}), we may assume that c(H_{R})>\Delta_{I}+\delta_{H} and c(H_{B})<-(\Delta_{I}+\delta_{H}), as otherwise we are done by choosing H^{\prime} to be either H_{R} or H_{B}.
We define a sequence of embeddings h_{R}=h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{\ell}=h_{B} of H into K_{n}, for some integer \ell\geq 1, such that for each i\in[\ell], h_{i} is obtained from h_{i-1} by switching the images of precisely two vertices in I.
Note that this is possible, as there is a permutation \sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}V(H)\rightarrow V(H) of the vertices of H for which h_{R}(\sigma(v))=h_{B}(v) for all v\in V(H).
More precisely, we define \sigma to be a product of swaps, all of which fix all elements in V(H), including I, except for two vertices in I which are swapped.
Let \sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{\ell} be this sequence of swaps, and for each i\in[\ell] let u_{i},v_{i}\in I be such that \sigma_{i}(u_{i})=v_{i}, \sigma_{i}(v_{i})=u_{i} and everything else is fixed.
Let i\in[\ell].
Given h_{i-1}, we define h_{i} as follows.
Assume first that, without loss of generality, we have \deg_{H}(v_{i})=\delta_{H} (the case where \deg_{H}(u_{i})=\delta_{H} is similar).
Then we set
\displaystyle h_{i}(x)\mathrel{\coloneqq}\begin{cases}h_{i-1}(v_{i})&\text{ if }x=u_{i},\\
h_{i-1}(u_{i})&\text{ if }x=v_{i},\\
h_{i-1}(x)&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}
i.e., we swap the images of u_{i} and v_{i}.
Let H_{i} be the image of the graph H under h_{i}, for all i\in[\ell].
Note that in this case we have
\displaystyle\left|c(H_{i})-c(H_{i-1})\right|\leq 2(\deg_{H}(u_{i})+\deg_{H}(v_{i}))\leq 2(\Delta_{I}+\delta_{H}).
Assume now that \deg_{H}(v_{i})>\delta_{H} and \deg_{H}(u_{i})>\delta_{H}.
In this case we swap the images of u_{i} and v_{i} in three steps using two auxiliary functions.
Let w\in V(H)\setminus\{u_{i},v_{i}\} be a vertex with \deg_{H}(w)=\delta_{H}.
We define
\displaystyle h^{\prime}_{i}(x)\mathrel{\coloneqq}\begin{cases}h_{i-1}(u_{i})&\text{ if }x=w,\\
h_{i-1}(w)&\text{ if }x=u_{i},\\
h_{i-1}(x)&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}
\displaystyle h^{\prime\prime}_{i}(x)\mathrel{\coloneqq}\begin{cases}h^{\prime}_{i}(v_{i})&\text{ if }x=w,\\
h^{\prime}_{i}(w)&\text{ if }x=v_{i},\\
h^{\prime}_{i}(x)&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}
and
\displaystyle h_{i}(x)\mathrel{\coloneqq}\begin{cases}h^{\prime\prime}_{i}(u_{i})&\text{ if }x=w,\\
h^{\prime\prime}_{i}(w)&\text{ if }x=u_{i},\\
h^{\prime\prime}_{i}(x)&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}
That is, h^{\prime}_{i} is h_{i-1} with the images of u_{i} and w swapped, h^{\prime\prime}_{i} is h^{\prime}_{i} with the images of v_{i} and w swapped and, finally, h_{i} is h^{\prime\prime}_{i} with the images of u_{i} and w swapped.
Note that h_{i} agrees with h_{i-1} everywhere except for swapping u_{i} and v_{i}, and in particular h_{i}(w)=h_{i-1}(w).
Moreover, if we denote by H_{i},H^{\prime}_{i} and H^{\prime\prime}_{i} the images of H under h_{i},h^{\prime}_{i} and h^{\prime\prime}_{i}, respectively, we have
\displaystyle\left|c(H^{\prime}_{i})-c(H_{i-1})\right|\leq 2(\deg_{H}(u_{i})+\deg_{H}(w))\leq 2(\Delta_{I}+\delta_{H}),
\displaystyle\left|c(H^{\prime\prime}_{i})-c(H^{\prime}_{i})\right|\leq 2(\deg_{H}(w)+\deg_{H}(v_{i}))\leq 2(\Delta_{I}+\delta_{H}),
and
\displaystyle\left|c(H_{i})-c(H^{\prime\prime}_{i})\right|\leq 2(\deg_{H}(u_{i})+\deg_{H}(w))\leq 2(\Delta_{I}+\delta_{H}).
In any case for every i\in[\ell] we have h_{i}(\sigma_{i}(v))=h_{i-1}(v) for all v\in V(H), and therefore h_{0}=h_{R} and h_{\ell}=h_{B}.
As c(H_{R})>\Delta_{I}+\delta_{H} and c(H_{B})<-(\Delta_{I}+\delta_{H}), by the above we get that there exist i\in[\ell] for which |c(\hat{H})|\leq\Delta_{I}+\delta_{H}, where \hat{H}\in\left\{H_{i},H^{\prime}_{i},H^{\prime\prime}_{i}\right\}.
∎
Consider an embedding f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}V(F)\rightarrow V(K_{n}).
We introduce the following definition.
Definition 2.2.
The sign of f is defined to be the sign of c(f)=\sum_{e\in E(F)}c(f(e)).
Furthermore, given a subset U\subset V(K_{n}) we say that a subset L\subset V(F) is U-sign-fixing for F if any two embeddings f,f^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}V(F)\rightarrow V(K_{n}) such that f(L),f^{\prime}(L)\subset U have the same sign whenever f|_{L}=f^{\prime}|_{L}.
Note that, given a subset U\subset V(K_{n}), if a subset L\subset V(F) is U-sign-fixing for F, then so is any subset L^{\prime}\subset V(F) with |L^{\prime}|\leq|U| which contains L.
In our proof of Theorem 1.3 we consider a certain subset U\subset V(K_{n}) and we will show that if a set L of vertices of large degrees is not U-sign-fixing, then we can find an embedding of F with a small sum (in absolute value) using Lemma 2.1.
We then show that if L is U-sign-fixing, then, by considering a minimal U-sign-fixing set M\subset L, we can derive the desired bound.
Once a U-sign-fixing set L of vertices is embedded in U, the sign of any embedding of F which extends L is already determined, no matter how the remaining vertices are embedded.
In such a situation, we will also refer to the sign of an embedding g\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}L\rightarrow U, and we will say that g is red if any embedding g^{\prime} of F extending g satisfies c(g^{\prime}(F))\geq 0, and we say that g is blue if c(g^{\prime}(F))\leq 0 for any such embedding g^{\prime}.
Definition 2.3.
Given a 2-colouring of the edges of K_{n} and an integer r, we say that a vertex in K_{n} is r-balanced if it is adjacent to at least r edges in each colour.
In the following lemma we show that if r is not too big, one can find many r-balanced vertices in a balanced colouring of K_{n}.
Lemma 2.4.
Let n\geq 5 be an integer and \frac{1}{n}\leq\varepsilon<\frac{1}{4}.
Then in any balanced 2-colouring of the edges of K_{n} by red and blue there are at least \varepsilon n+1 many vertices which are \left(\frac{1}{4}-\varepsilon\right)n-balanced.
We remark that a version of Lemma 2.4 for \varepsilon=0 is Theorem 2 of [14].
The proof of Lemma 2.4 is somewhat technical and not relevant to our other arguments, and so we include it in Appendix A.
3 Embedding an n-vertex forest
We prove Theorem 1.3 by dealing separately with the cases \Delta\leq 15, 15<\Delta\leq\frac{1}{2}n and \Delta>\frac{1}{2}n.
In the proof of the second case, the following lemma will be the crux of our argument.
Recall that for a real number x\in\mathbb{R} we denote (x)_{+}=\max(x,0).
Lemma 3.1.
Let c\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}E(K_{n})\rightarrow\{-1,+1\} be a balanced colouring of the edges of K_{n}, for some integer n\geq 32, and let F be an n-vertex forest with maximum degree \Delta\leq\frac{1}{2}n.
Let \frac{1}{n}\leq\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{8}.
Then there is a copy F^{\prime} of F in K_{n} such that
\displaystyle|c(F^{\prime})|\leq\max\left(1+\tfrac{2}{\varepsilon},2+\left(\Delta-\left(\tfrac{1}{4}-2\varepsilon\right)n\right)_{+}\right),
(3.1)
Proof.
By Lemma 2.4 we know that there exists a subset U\subset V(K_{n}) of at least \varepsilon n+1 many \left(\frac{1}{4}-\varepsilon\right)n-balanced vertices, under the colouring c.
Define the set L_{\varepsilon}\subset V(F) of vertices of ‘large degree’ to be
L_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\left\{v\in V(F)~\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}~\deg_{F}(v)\geq\tfrac{2}{\varepsilon}\right\}.
Note that |L_{\varepsilon}|\leq\frac{2n}{2/\varepsilon}=\varepsilon n.
We separate the proof into two cases.
If L_{\varepsilon} is not U-sign-fixing then we find a copy F^{\prime} of F in K_{n} with |c(F^{\prime})|\leq 1+\frac{2}{\varepsilon} by using Lemma 2.1.
On the other hand, if L_{\varepsilon} is U-sign-fixing then we consider a minimal U-sign-fixing set M\subset L_{\varepsilon} and we find a copy F^{\prime} of F with |c(F^{\prime})|\leq 2+(\Delta-(\tfrac{1}{4}-2\varepsilon)n)_{+} using the definition of U.
Suppose first that L_{\varepsilon} is not U-sign-fixing.
Then there are embeddings h_{B},h_{R} of F with h_{B}|_{L_{\varepsilon}}=h_{R}|_{L_{\varepsilon}} and h_{B}(L_{\varepsilon}),h_{R}(L_{\varepsilon})\subset U such that c(h_{B}(F))\leq 0\leq c(h_{R}(F)).
Note further that, by definition, we have
I\coloneqq\left\{v\in V(F)~\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}~h_{R}(v)\neq h_{B}(v)\right\}\subset V(F)\setminus L_{\varepsilon},
implying that \Delta_{I}\coloneqq\max\{\deg_{F}(v)~\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}~v\in I\}\leq\frac{2}{\varepsilon}.
Since \delta_{F}=1, by Lemma 2.1, we find that there is an embedding h of F with F^{\prime}\coloneqq h(F) for which
\displaystyle\lvert c(F^{\prime})\rvert\leq\tfrac{2}{\varepsilon}+1,
(3.2)
so in particular (3.1) holds in this case.
Suppose now that L_{\varepsilon} is U-sign-fixing for F, and let M\subset L_{\varepsilon} be a minimal U-sign-fixing set.
Consider the subset
\displaystyle N\coloneqq\left\{v\in M~\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}~\deg_{F[M]}(v)\geq 2\right\}.
(3.3)
As c is a balanced colouring we have that M is non-empty, and moreover, as F is a forest we have N\subsetneq M.
Moreover, since M is a minimal U-sign-fixing set for F, and since F is a forest, we know that N is not U-sign-fixing.
Hence, there exist f,g\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}M\rightarrow U with f|_{N}=g|_{N} and such that f is red and g is blue.
That is, for every f^{\prime},g^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}V(F)\rightarrow V(K_{n}) such that f^{\prime}|_{M}=f and g^{\prime}|_{M}=g we have c(f^{\prime})\geq 0 and c(g^{\prime})\leq 0.
Note that we have |M|\leq|L_{\varepsilon}|\leq\varepsilon n and recall that |U|\geq\varepsilon n+1.
In particular, there exists a \left(\frac{1}{4}-\varepsilon\right)n-balanced vertex a\in U\setminus g(M).
Label the vertices of M\setminus N as \{v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\}, where r\geq 1 is some integer.
As a\notin g(M) we also have a\notin g(N)=f(N).
However, it is possible to have a\in f(M\setminus N).
If this is the case, then we may assume, without loss of generality, that f(v_{r})=a.
We now define a sequence of embeddings h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{3r} of M into U.
Intuitively, we define these embeddings to ‘interpolate’ between g and f, with h_{k-1} and h_{k} differing only in where they send a single vertex v.
The technicalities arise due to the possibility that the images of f and g may overlap, and so we must ensure that each h_{k} is indeed an injection.
Define the functions h_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}M\rightarrow V(K_{n}) inductively, as follows.
Firstly set h_{0}(v_{j})\coloneqq g(v_{j}) for all j\in[r], and for all k\in[3r] set h_{k}|_{N}=f|_{N}=g|_{N}.
Then for each i\in[r] in turn define
\displaystyle h_{3i-2}(v_{j})
\displaystyle\mathrel{\coloneqq}\begin{cases}a&\text{ if }j>i,h_{3i-3}(v_{j})=f(v_{i})\neq h_{3i-3}(v_{i}),\\
h_{3i-3}(v_{j})&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}
\displaystyle h_{3i-1}(v_{j})
\displaystyle\mathrel{\coloneqq}\begin{cases}f(v_{i})&\text{ if }j=i,\\
h_{3i-2}(v_{j})&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}
\displaystyle h_{3i}(v_{j})
\displaystyle\mathrel{\coloneqq}\begin{cases}h_{3i-3}(v_{i})&\text{ if }j>i,h_{3i-1}(v_{j})=a,\\
h_{3i-1}(v_{j})&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}
In words, in the ith step, these three functions ensure that v_{i} is mapped to f(v_{i}).
As we wish to ‘interpolate’ between g and f using injections only, we must make sure that no other vertex except for v_{i} is sent to f(v_{i}).
If this is not the case, and some vertex v_{j} is already mapped to f(v_{i}) at this point, then h_{3i-2} moves it to a, where it is temporarily sent.
Then h_{3i-1} sends v_{i} to f(v_{i}), and h_{3i} sends v_{j} to where v_{i} used to be sent, freeing up the vertex a.
Note that if f(v_{i})\notin h_{3i-3}(M), then h_{3i-2}=h_{3i-3} and h_{3i}=h_{3i-1}.
We now prove that these embeddings h_{i} have the properties we desire.
Claim 3.2.
For all k\in[3r] we have h_{k}(M)\subset U, where h_{k} is injective, and the functions h_{k-1} and h_{k} differ in where they embed at most one vertex.
Furthermore, for all 1\leq i<r, a\notin h_{3i}(M), and for all 1\leq j\leq i\leq r, h_{3i}(v_{j})=f(v_{j}).
Proof.
It is immediate from the definition that h_{k} is injective, that h_{k-1} and h_{k} differ in where they embed at most one vertex.
Moreover, as g(M),f(M)\subset U and since for all k\in[3r] and all v\in M we have h_{k}(v)\in\{g(v),f(v),a\}, we get that h_{k}(M)\subset U as well.
We prove the rest of the claim inductively, noting that h_{0} does indeed satisfy all of the required properties.
Let i\in[r] and assume that a\notin h_{3i-3}(M) and h_{3i-3}(v_{j})=f(v_{j}) for all j\leq i-1.
We prove that the same holds for h_{3i} as well.
As, by their definition, h_{3i}(v_{j})=h_{3i-3}(v_{j})=f(v_{j}) for all j\leq i-1, it is enough to show that h_{3i}(v_{i})=f(v_{i}) and that if i<r then a\notin h_{3i}(M).
If we already have h_{3i-3}(v_{i})=f(v_{i}) then we get h_{3i-3}=h_{3i-2}=h_{3i-1}=h_{3i} and we are done, so we may assume that this is not the case.
If f(v_{i})\notin h_{3i-3}(M) then we get h_{3i-3}=h_{3i-2}, and then h_{3i-1}(v_{i})=f(v_{i}).
As we further have in this case that h_{3i}=h_{3i-1} and a\notin h_{3i-1}(M), we are done.
Hence, we may assume that f(v_{i})\in h_{3i-3}(M) and that h_{3i-3}(v_{j})=f(v_{i}) for some j>i.
In this case we have h_{3i-1}(v_{j})=h_{3i-2}(v_{j}) for all j\neq i.
By definition we have h_{3i-1}(v_{i})=f(v_{i}), and moreover, we have h_{3i}(v_{j})=h_{3i-1}(v_{j}) for all j\leq i, so in particular h_{3i}(v_{i})=f(v_{i}).
Further, note that if i=r then we may have f(v_{r})=a in which case a\in h_{3r}(M).
If i<r, then although a\not\in h_{3i-3}(M), we may still have a\in h_{3i-2}(M) or a\in h_{3i-1}(M).
This happens precisely when there exists j>i for which h_{3i-3}(v_{j})=f(v_{i}).
However, we then get that h_{3i-1}(v_{j})=h_{3i-2}(v_{j})=a, but then h_{3i}(v_{j})=h_{3i-3}(v_{j})\neq a, as a\notin h_{3i-3}(M), so in particular a\notin h_{3i}(M).
This completes the induction, and hence the proof of Claim 3.2.
∎
Recall that M is sign fixing, so each h_{i} is either blue or red, and we know that h_{0}=g is blue and h_{3r}=f is red.
Hence, there exists an index t\in[3r] for which h_{t-1} is blue and h_{t} is red.
By Claim 3.2, h_{t-1} and h_{t} differ only in the image of one vertex v\in M\setminus N, as h_{t-1}|_{N}=h_{t}|_{N}, so in particular, recalling (3.3), we have \deg_{M}(v)\leq 1.
Moreover, by Claim 3.2 we know that h_{t-1}(v)\in U, meaning it is \left(\frac{1}{4}-\varepsilon\right)n-balanced.
We now define embeddings h^{\prime}_{t-1} and h^{\prime}_{t} of F into K_{n}, extending h_{t-1} and h_{t}.
Our goal is to do so in such a way that the difference c(h^{\prime}_{t}(F))-c(h^{\prime}_{t-1}(F)) is bounded from above.
As h_{t-1} is blue and h_{t} is red, we are guaranteed to have c(h^{\prime}_{t-1}(F))\leq 0\leq c(h^{\prime}_{t}(F)) no matter how we choose these two extensions.
We consider the vertex h^{\prime}_{t-1}(v), which we know to be a \left(\frac{1}{4}-\varepsilon\right)n-balanced vertex.
We then define an extension h_{t-1} so that, locally, it contains as many red edges as possible in the red neighbourhood of h^{\prime}_{t-1}(v).
As h^{\prime}_{t-1}(v) is \left(\frac{1}{4}-\varepsilon\right)n-balanced, the number of red neighbours of h^{\prime}_{t-1}(v) in K_{n} outside of h_{t-1}(M)\cup\{h_{t}(v)\}\setminus\{h_{t-1}(v)\} is at least
\displaystyle\left(\tfrac{1}{4}-\varepsilon\right)n-|M|\geq\left(\tfrac{1}{4}-2\varepsilon\right)n.
(3.4)
Let h^{\prime}_{t-1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}V(F)\rightarrow V(K_{n}) be an embedding of F such that h^{\prime}_{t-1}|_{M}=h_{t-1} and with the following two properties.
The first is that h^{\prime}_{t-1} has as many red neighbours of h_{t-1}(v) outside h_{t-1}(M) as possible; more formally, with either h^{\prime}_{t-1}(N_{F}(v)\setminus M)\subset N_{R}(h_{t-1}(v)) or N_{R}(h_{t-1}(v))\subset h^{\prime}_{t-1}(N_{F}(v)\setminus M).
Thus, by (3.4), we get that the number of blue edges in h^{\prime}_{t-1}(F)\subset K_{n} from h_{t-1}(v) to a vertex not in h_{t-1}(M)\cup\{h_{t}(v)\}\setminus\{h_{t-1}(v)\} is equal to
\displaystyle e_{B}\left(h_{t-1}(v),h^{\prime}_{t-1}(N_{F}(v))\setminus\left(h_{t-1}(M)\cup\{h_{t}(v)\}\right)\right)\leq\left(\Delta-\left(\tfrac{1}{4}-2\varepsilon\right)n\right)_{+}.
(3.5)
The second property that h^{\prime}_{t-1} has to satisfy is that it maps a leaf of F to h_{t}(v).
Note that, as h_{t-1} and h_{t} differ only at where they map v to, we have h_{t}(v)\notin h_{t-1}(M).
Hence, we may indeed map a leaf of F to h_{t}(v) under h^{\prime}_{t-1}.
We denote w\coloneqq(h^{\prime}_{t-1})^{-1}(h_{t}(v)).
Having defined h^{\prime}_{t-1}, we define h^{\prime}_{t} to be an extension of h_{t}, as follows.
\displaystyle h^{\prime}_{t}(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}f(v)&\text{ if }x=v,\\
h^{\prime}_{t-1}(v)&\text{ if }x=w,\\
h^{\prime}_{t-1}(x)&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}
i.e. h^{\prime}_{t} is equal to h^{\prime}_{t-1} but with v and w swapped.
Since h_{t-1} is blue and h^{\prime}_{t-1}|_{M}=h_{t-1} we know that c(h^{\prime}_{t-1}(F))\leq 0.
Similarly, as h_{t} is red and h^{\prime}_{t}|_{M}=h_{t} we know that c(h^{\prime}_{t}(F))\geq 0.
However, by (3.5) we know that all but at most \left(\Delta-\left(\frac{1}{4}-2\varepsilon\right)n\right)_{+} many edges from v to outside of M were embedded by h^{\prime}_{t-1} to red edges.
Thus, we get that
\displaystyle c(h^{\prime}_{t}(F))-c(h^{\prime}_{t-1}(F))\leq 2\deg_{M}(v)+2\deg_{F}(w)+2\left(\Delta-\left(\tfrac{1}{4}-2\varepsilon\right)n\right)_{+}.
(3.6)
Recall that w is a leaf of F and that v\in M\setminus N so we have \deg_{M}(v)\leq 1.
Hence, by (3.6), there exists an embedding h of F with F^{\prime}\coloneqq h(F) (which is either h^{\prime}_{t-1} or h^{\prime}_{t}), for which we have
\displaystyle|c(F^{\prime})|\leq 2+\left(\Delta-\left(\tfrac{1}{4}-2\varepsilon\right)n\right)_{+},
(3.7)
finishing the proof.
∎
For the proof of Theorem 1.3 we need one more technical lemma to deal with the case 15<\Delta<\frac{1}{2}n.
More precisely, Lemma 3.1 implies Theorem 1.3 when 15<\Delta<\frac{1}{2}n by optimising (3.1) over \varepsilon, as given in the following lemma.
Lemma 3.3.
Let \xi\in\left[-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{16}{n},\frac{1}{4}\right] and n\geq 32 be an integer.
Then
\displaystyle\phi_{n}(\xi)
\displaystyle\coloneqq\inf_{\frac{1}{n}\leq\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{8}}\max\left(\tfrac{2}{\varepsilon}+1,2+(2\varepsilon n+\xi n)_{+}\right)\leq\frac{3+\xi n}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{(1+\xi n)^{2}+16n}}{2}.
(3.8)
The proof of Lemma 3.3 is purely technical and consists only of elementary techniques.
As it does not serve to enlighten the rest of the paper, it is given in Appendix B.
We are now ready to prove our main result.
As mentioned earlier, the case \Delta\leq\frac{1}{2}n follows immediately from Lemmas 3.1 and 3.3.
The case \Delta\geq\frac{1}{2}n is obtained by explicitly embedding up to three vertices of F of largest degrees.
Proof of Theorem 1.3.
Firstly, note that if \Delta\leq 15 then Lemma 2.1 immediately implies (1.2).
Hence, we assume that \Delta>15 and we continue the proof by further splitting into two cases according to the value of \Delta.
Case 1: 15<\Delta<\frac{1}{2}n.
Note that n\geq 32.
Let \xi\in\left[-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{16}{n},\frac{1}{4}\right] be such that \Delta=\left(\frac{1}{4}+\xi\right)n.
Given \frac{1}{n}\leq\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{8}, we know by Lemma 3.1 that there exists a copy F^{\prime} of F in K_{n} for which
\left|c(F^{\prime})\right|\leq\max\left(1+\tfrac{2}{\varepsilon},2+(2\varepsilon n+\xi n)_{+}\right).
As \xi n=\Delta-\frac{1}{4}n, by optimising over all possible values of \varepsilon, Lemma 3.3 implies (1.2) for this case.
Case 2: \Delta\geq\frac{1}{2}n.
We explicitly embed a few vertices of largest degrees in F.
Let v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n} be an ordering of the vertices of F such that \Delta=\deg_{F}(v_{1})\geq\deg_{F}(v_{2})\geq\cdots\geq\deg_{F}(v_{n}), and set \Delta^{\prime}=\deg_{F}(v_{2}).
Let \varepsilon=\frac{1}{n}.
By Lemma 2.4 we know that there exists a vertex x\in V(K_{n}) which is \left(\frac{1}{4}-\varepsilon\right)n-balanced.
We proceed with simple analyses of two subcases.
Case 2.1: \Delta^{\prime}<\frac{1}{2}\Delta.
We embed v_{1} at x and consider an extension of this embedding f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}V(F)\rightarrow V(K_{n}) chosen uniformly at random.
As x is \left(\frac{1}{4}-\varepsilon\right)n-balanced, we have -\left(\frac{1}{2}+2\varepsilon\right)n\leq\deg_{R}(x)-\deg_{B}(x)\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+2\varepsilon\right)n, and since c(K_{n})=0 we get that
\displaystyle\left|c(K_{n}\setminus\{x\})\right|=\left|c(K_{n})-\deg_{R}(x)+\deg_{B}(x)\right|\leq\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+2\varepsilon\right)n.
(3.9)
Then, by the linearity of expectation, we have
\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[c(f(F))\right]\right|
\displaystyle\leq\left|\mathbb{E}\left[c(f(\{v_{1}\}\cup N(v_{1})))\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[c(f(F\setminus\{v_{1}\}))\right]\right|
\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Delta}{n-1}\cdot\left|\deg_{R}(x)-\deg_{B}(x)\right|+\frac{n-\Delta}{\binom{n-1}{2}}\cdot\left|c(K_{n}\setminus\{x\})\right|
\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Delta}{n-1}\cdot\left|\deg_{R}(x)-\deg_{B}(x)\right|+\frac{n(n-\Delta)}{\binom{n-1}{2}}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+2\varepsilon\right)
\displaystyle\leq\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+2\varepsilon\right)\Delta+\tfrac{\Delta}{n-1}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+2\varepsilon\right)+1+4\varepsilon
\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta+4,
(3.10)
where the last inequality holds as n\geq 17.
Assume, for a contradiction, that there exists no embedding f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}V(F)\rightarrow V(K_{n}) such that \left|c(f(F))\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\Delta+4.
Then, by inequality (3.10), we get that there exist two embeddings f_{B},f_{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}V(F)\rightarrow V(K_{n}), both mapping v_{1} to x, such that
\displaystyle c(f_{B}(F))<-\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta-4
and
\displaystyle c(f_{R}(F))>\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta+4.
Applying Lemma 2.1 with f_{B},f_{R} and \Delta_{I}\leq\Delta^{\prime}, we get that there exists a copy F^{\prime} of F in K_{n} such that \left|c(F^{\prime})\right|<\frac{1}{2}\Delta+1, a contradiction.
Case 2.2: \Delta^{\prime}\geq\frac{1}{2}\Delta.
Assume without loss of generality that x has red degree \deg_{R}(x)\geq\frac{n-1}{2}.
As c is a balanced colouring, there exists a vertex y\in V(K_{n}) which has red degree \deg_{R}(y)\leq\frac{n-1}{2}.
We will define an embedding f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}V(F)\rightarrow K_{n} which sends v_{1} to x and v_{2} to y.
If \deg_{R}(y)\geq\frac{1}{4}n then we extend f uniformly at random.
Denote \Delta^{\prime\prime}=\deg_{F}(v_{3}), so we have
\Delta^{\prime\prime}\leq n-1-\Delta-\Delta^{\prime}+2\leq n+1-\tfrac{1}{2}n-\tfrac{1}{4}n\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta+1.
Note that, similarly to (3.9), we also have |c(K_{n}\setminus\{x,y\})|\leq 2n.
Denote Z=1_{\{v_{1}v_{2}\in E(F)\}}.
Then similarly to the analysis in Case 2.1, we get that
\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[c(f(F))\right]\right|\leq\,
\displaystyle\Bigl|\mathbb{E}\left[c(f(\{v_{1}\}\mathbin{\cup}N(v_{1})))\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[c(f(\{v_{2}\}\mathbin{\cup}N(v_{2})))\right]
\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[c(f(F\setminus\{v_{1},v_{2}\}))\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[c(f(F[\{v_{1},v_{2}\}]))\right]\Bigr|
\displaystyle\leq\,
\displaystyle\Biggl|\frac{\Delta-Z}{n-2}\Bigl(\deg_{R}(x)-\deg_{B}(x)-c(xy)Z\Bigr)\Biggr|+\Biggl|\frac{\Delta^{\prime}-Z}{n-2}\Bigl(\deg_{R}(y)-\deg_{B}(y)-c(xy)Z\Bigr)\Biggr|
\displaystyle+\Biggl|\frac{n-\Delta-\Delta^{\prime}+Z}{\binom{n-2}{2}}\cdot 2n\Biggr|+\bigl|c(xy)\bigr|
\displaystyle\leq\,
\displaystyle\frac{\Delta}{n-2}\cdot\max\left(|\deg_{R}(x)-\deg_{B}(x)|,|\deg_{R}(y)-\deg_{B}(y)|\right)+\frac{\Delta+\Delta^{\prime}}{n-2}
\displaystyle+\frac{n-\Delta-\Delta^{\prime}+1}{\binom{n-2}{2}}\cdot 2n+1
\displaystyle\leq\,
\displaystyle\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+2\varepsilon\right)\Delta+\frac{2\Delta}{n-2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+2\varepsilon\right)+\frac{\Delta+\Delta^{\prime}}{n-2}+\frac{\frac{1}{4}n+1}{\binom{n-2}{2}}\cdot 2n+1
\displaystyle\leq\,
\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta+9,
where the last inequality follows since for n\geq 17 we have
2\varepsilon\Delta+\frac{2\Delta}{n-2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+2\varepsilon\right)+\frac{\Delta+\Delta^{\prime}}{n-2}+\frac{\frac{1}{4}n+1}{\binom{n-2}{2}}\cdot 2n+1\leq 9.
Continuing with an argument similar to the one in Case 2.1, with \Delta_{I}\leq\Delta^{\prime\prime}\leq\frac{1}{2}\Delta when applying Lemma 2.1, we get that there exists a copy F^{\prime} of F in K_{n} with |c(F^{\prime})|\leq\frac{1}{2}\Delta+9.
We are left with the case \deg_{R}(y)<\frac{1}{4}n.
In this case we construct an embedding in the following way.
Let X_{R},X_{B}\subset N_{F}(v_{1})\setminus\{v_{2}\} be two disjoint subsets with |X_{R}|=\left\lfloor\frac{3}{8}n\right\rfloor and |X_{B}|=\left\lfloor\frac{1}{8}n\right\rfloor-1.
As F is a forest we have \left|N_{F}(v_{2})\cap\left(N_{F}(v_{1})\cup\{v_{1}\}\right)\right|\leq 1.
Moreover, we have \deg_{F}(v_{2})\geq\frac{1}{4}n, so we may choose a subset Y_{B}\subset N_{F}(v_{2})\setminus\left(N_{F}(v_{1})\cup\{v_{1}\}\right) with |Y_{B}|=\left\lfloor\frac{1}{4}n\right\rfloor-1.
Since \deg_{B}(y)>\frac{3}{4}n, for any f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}V(F)\rightarrow K_{n} we have that \left|N_{B}(y)\setminus(f(X_{R})\cup f(X_{B}))\right|\geq\frac{1}{4}n.
Recall that we require f to map v_{1} to x and v_{2} to y.
Hence, since x is \left(\frac{1}{4}-\varepsilon\right)n-balanced we may let f be an embedding of F such that
•
f(X_{R})\subset N_{R}(x),
•
f(X_{B})\subset N_{B}(x), and
•
f(Y_{B})\subset N_{B}(y)\setminus\left(f(X_{R})\cup f(X_{B})\right).
We then get an embedding f of F with at least \left\lfloor\frac{3}{8}n\right\rfloor red edges and at least \left\lfloor\frac{3}{8}n\right\rfloor-2 blue edges, so that |c(f(F))|\leq\frac{1}{4}n+4\leq\frac{1}{2}\Delta+4.
∎
Theorem 1.2 follows from Theorem 1.3 as a simple corollary.
Proof of Theorem 1.2.
It suffices to consider 15<\Delta<\frac{1}{2}n.
It is easy to show by simple algebraic manipulations that for any |x|\leq\frac{1}{4}n, we have
\sqrt{\left(\tfrac{x}{2}\right)^{2}+4n}\leq\tfrac{1}{8}n+16.
Applying this inequality to x=\Delta-\tfrac{1}{4}n+1, we obtain
\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\Delta-\tfrac{1}{4}n+3\right)+\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{4}\left(\Delta-\tfrac{1}{4}n+1\right)^{2}+4n}
\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\Delta-\tfrac{1}{4}n+3\right)+\tfrac{1}{8}n+16
\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta+18,
which, together with Theorem 1.3, implies (1.1).
∎
We end this section with a proof of Corollary 1.4 as a direct consequence of Theorem 1.3.
Proof of Corollary 1.4.
Let \xi\in\left[-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{16}{n},-\eta\right] be such that \xi n=\Delta-\frac{1}{4}n.
By Theorem 1.3 and Bernoulli’s inequality, we get that there exists a copy F^{\prime} of F in K_{n}, for n sufficiently large, such that
\displaystyle\left|c(F^{\prime})\right|
\displaystyle\leq\frac{2+\xi n}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{(2+\xi n)^{2}+16n}}{2}
\displaystyle=\frac{2+\xi n}{2}-\frac{2+\xi n}{2}\sqrt{1+\frac{16n}{(2+\xi n)^{2}}}
\displaystyle\leq\frac{2+\xi n}{2}-\frac{2+\xi n}{2}\left(1+\frac{8n}{(2+\xi n)^{2}}\right)
\displaystyle=\frac{4n}{|2+\xi n|}
\displaystyle\leq\frac{4n}{\eta n-2}
\displaystyle\leq(1+o(1))\frac{4}{\eta},
as wanted.
∎
4 Concluding remarks and further work
The first natural further line of research would be to obtain tight bounds concerning Theorem 1.3 for \Delta\geq\frac{1}{4}n.
If n is a multiple of 4, the following construction was noted in [14].
We let V(K_{n})=\{u_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}i\in I\}\cup\{v_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}i\in I\} for I=[n/2] and we let c_{0}\colon E(K_{n})\to\{-1,+1\} be defined as
\displaystyle c_{0}(xy)=\begin{cases}-1&\text{if }xy=u_{i}u_{j}\text{ for some }i,j\in I,\text{ or }xy=u_{i}v_{j}\text{ for some }i,j\in I\text{ s.t.\ }i+j\text{ is odd, }\\
+1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
(4.1)
If F is an n-vertex star, then every embedding has sum precisely equal to \frac{\Delta-1}{2} in absolute value.
Furthermore, if F is close to a star – that is F has one vertex of degree \Delta\geq\frac{3}{4}n and some other arbitrary vertices and edges – then the absolute value of the sum of any embedding in colouring c_{0} is at least 2\left(\Delta-\frac{3}{4}n\right)+1.
It is worth noting that these lower bounds are far from our upper bounds.
In particular, we have no nontrivial lower bound when \frac{1}{4}n\leq\Delta<\frac{3}{4}n.
One different line of further research would be to determine whether a form of Theorem 1.3 still holds when the complete graph is replaced by a dense enough host graph.
Indeed, Komlós, Sárközy, and Szemerédi [12] have proved that if \delta>0, and G is a graph on n vertices and minimum degree \delta(G)\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\delta\right)n, then G contains every spanning tree T with maximum degree \Delta(T)=O\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right), provided that n is sufficiently large.
Whether a low-sum copy of T can be found in a graph G with high minimum degree equipped with a balanced 2-colouring is an open question.
It would also be interesting to see whether an analogue of the main result can still be obtained when the complete graph is coloured with a small imbalance.
We remark that in this case the bound would depend on the perturbation of the 2-colouring, as our final result shows.
Theorem 4.1.
For every sufficiently small \varepsilon>0 and sufficiently large n there exists a red-blue colouring of the edges of K_{n} such that the density of the red edges is \frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\leq d_{R}\leq\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon and every copy T of a star on n vertices in K_{n} satisfies |c(T)|\geq(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon^{2})n\geq(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon^{2})\Delta.
We prove Theorem 4.1 by using a modification of the colouring (4.1) in Appendix C.
One final line of research would be to investigate whether a similar bound as Theorem 1.2 could be obtained if we do not restrict the graph F to be a forest.
Indeed, the argument by Mohr, Pardey, and Rautenbach [14] mentioned in the introduction and slightly generalised in Lemma 2.1 show that, for every graph H on n vertices, maximum degree \Delta_{H} and minimum degree \delta_{H}, and every balanced colouring of K_{n}, then there exists a copy H^{\prime} of H in K_{n} such that |c(H^{\prime})|\leq\Delta_{H}+\delta_{H}\leq 2\Delta_{H}.
It would be interesting to see whether this simple argument is in some sense optimal, or whether tighter bounds could be obtained.
More precisely, we ask the following.
Question 4.2.
Let H be a graph on n vertices, with maximum degree \Delta=\Delta(H), and let c\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}E(K_{n})\rightarrow\{-1,+1\} be a balanced colouring of the edges of K_{n}.
Does there exists a copy H^{\prime} of H in K_{n} such that
\displaystyle\left|c(F^{\prime})\right|\leq\alpha\Delta,
(4.2)
for some universal constant \alpha<2?

Proof of Lemma 2.4.

\displaystyle D^{-}

\displaystyle D^{+}
\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{v\in V~\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}~\deg_{R}(v)>\left(\tfrac{3}{4}+\varepsilon\right)n\right\},
\displaystyle D
\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{v\in V~\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}~\left(\tfrac{1}{4}-\varepsilon\right)n\leq\deg_{R}(v)\leq\left(\tfrac{3}{4}+\varepsilon\right)n\right\}.
Note that D^{-}\cup D^{+}\cup D is a partition of the vertices of K_{n}.
By symmetry, we may assume that |D^{+}|\leq|D^{-}|, and consequently that |D^{-}|\geq\frac{1}{2}(n-|D|).
Then the number of red edges in the graph satisfies
\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\tbinom{n}{2}=e_{R}
\displaystyle=e_{R}(D^{-},V)+e_{R}(V\setminus D^{-},V\setminus D^{-})
\displaystyle\leq\left(\tfrac{1}{4}-\varepsilon\right)n|D^{-}|+\tbinom{n-|D^{-}|}{2}.
Note that the function g(u)=\left(\frac{1}{4}-\varepsilon\right)nu+\binom{n-u}{2} is a quadratic polynomial in u with a positive leading coefficient, and therefore attains its maximum on an interval [a,b] at either a or b.
As \frac{1}{2}(n-|D|)\leq|D^{-}|\leq n, it follows that g(|D^{-}|)\leq\max\left(g\left(\frac{1}{2}(n-|D|)\right),g(n)\right).
It can be easily verified that for any n\geq 5, since |D|\geq 0, we have
\displaystyle g\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(n-|D|)\right)=\left(\frac{1}{4}-\varepsilon\right)\frac{n(n-|D|)}{2}+\binom{\tfrac{1}{2}(n+|D|)}{2}\geq\left(\frac{1}{4}-\varepsilon\right)n^{2}=g(n),
and hence
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\binom{n}{2}=e_{R}\leq g(|D^{-}|)\leq g\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(n-|D|)\right)=\left(\frac{1}{4}-\varepsilon\right)\frac{n(n-|D|)}{2}+\binom{\tfrac{1}{2}(n+|D|)}{2}.
Rearranging, we get
\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}|D|^{2}+\left(\left(\frac{1}{4}+\varepsilon\right)n-\frac{1}{2}\right)|D|-\varepsilon n^{2}\geq 0,
which implies
\displaystyle 2\lvert D\rvert\geq\sqrt{(1+24\varepsilon+16\varepsilon^{2})n^{2}-4(1+4\varepsilon)n+4}-(1+4\varepsilon)n+2.
Thus to show that \lvert D\rvert\geq\varepsilon n+1, it suffices to show that
(1+24\varepsilon+16\varepsilon^{2})n^{2}-4(1+4\varepsilon)n+4\geq(1+6\varepsilon)^{2}n^{2}.
Rearranging, it suffices to show that
\displaystyle(3\varepsilon-5\varepsilon^{2})n^{2}-(1+4\varepsilon)n+1\geq 0.
As 0<\varepsilon<\frac{1}{4}, we have (3\varepsilon-5\varepsilon^{2})>0, and therefore it suffices to show that
\displaystyle n\geq\frac{1+4\varepsilon+\sqrt{1-4\varepsilon+36\varepsilon^{2}}}{2\varepsilon(3-5\varepsilon)}.
(A.1)
It is easy to show that \frac{1+4\varepsilon+\sqrt{1-4\varepsilon+36\varepsilon^{2}}}{2(3-5\varepsilon)}\leq 1 for every 0<\varepsilon<\frac{1}{4}.
Therefore our assumption n\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon} implies that (A.1) is indeed satisfied, finishing the proof.
∎
Appendix B Appendix: Proof of Lemma 3.3
Proof of Lemma 3.3.
Let f(\varepsilon)=\frac{2}{\varepsilon}+1 and g_{\xi,n}(\varepsilon)=2+(\xi n+2\varepsilon n)_{+} so that
\phi_{n}(\xi)=\inf_{\frac{1}{n}\leq\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{8}}\max\left(f(\varepsilon),g_{\xi,n}(\varepsilon)\right).
It is clear that f is decreasing in \varepsilon, and g_{\xi,n} is non-decreasing.
Moreover, since \xi\geq-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{16}{n}, we have
\displaystyle(f-g_{\xi,n})\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\right)>0
and
\displaystyle(f-g_{\xi,n})\left(\tfrac{1}{8}\right)<0.
Hence, as both f and g_{\xi,n} are continuous for \varepsilon>0, there exists \varepsilon_{*} such that \frac{1}{n}\leq\varepsilon_{*}\leq\frac{1}{8} and f(\varepsilon_{*})=g_{\xi,n}(\varepsilon_{*})=\phi_{n}(\xi).
In fact, since \frac{2}{\varepsilon_{*}}+1>2, we get that \varepsilon_{*} satisfies
2+\xi n+2\varepsilon_{*}n=\frac{2}{\varepsilon_{*}}+1.
Rearranging this and solving the second degree equation in \varepsilon_{*}, we obtain
\displaystyle\varepsilon_{*}=\frac{-1-\xi n+\sqrt{(1+\xi n)^{2}+16n}}{4n},
implying
\displaystyle\phi_{n}(\xi)=2+\xi n+2\varepsilon_{*}n=\frac{3+\xi n}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{(1+\xi n)^{2}+16n}}{2},
(B.1)
as required.
∎
Appendix C Appendix: Proof of Theorem 4.1
Proof of Theorem 4.1.
It suffices to prove that for n sufficiently large there exists a red-blue
colouring of the edges of K_{n} such that the density of the red edges is \frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\leq d_{R}\leq\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon and every vertex v satisfies \deg_{R}(v)\geq\left(\frac{3}{4}+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\right)n or \deg_{R}(v)\leq\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\right)n.
For this purpose, we generalise the construction (4.1) given in [14].
We let A and B be a partition of [n] such that |A|=\left(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)n and |B|=\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)n.
We colour blue those edges with both endpoints in A and red those edges with both endpoints in B.
Let d=\frac{x}{y} be a rational number such that 0<d<1 to be chosen later.
Writing A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{|A|}\} and B=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{|B|}\} we define
\displaystyle c(a_{i}b_{j})=\begin{cases}\text{red}&\text{ if }\text{mod}_{y}(i+j)\leq x,\\
\text{blue}&\text{ if }\text{mod}_{y}(i+j)>x,\end{cases}
where by \text{mod}_{y}(i+j), we mean the number in the set [y] which is congruent to i+j modulo y.
Note that the desired conditions on red/blue degrees can be rewritten as
\displaystyle\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)+d\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)\geq\tfrac{3}{4}+\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}+O_{d}\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\right)
(C.1)
for the red degree, and
\displaystyle\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)+(1-d)\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)\geq\tfrac{3}{4}+\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}+O_{d}\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\right)
(C.2)
for the blue degree.
Moreover, note that the density of red edges in this graph is
d_{R}=2d\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)+\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)^{2}+O_{d}\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\right),
so the conditions on the density of red edges can be rewritten as
\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{4}-\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon\leq d\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)^{2}+O_{d}\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\right)\leq\tfrac{1}{4}+\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon.
(C.3)
Thus, to prove our theorem, it suffices to show that there is a rational number 0<d<1 which simultaneously satisfies (C.1), (C.2) and (C.3).
By (C.1), (C.2) we get that such d should satisfy
\displaystyle\frac{1/4+\varepsilon^{2}/2-\varepsilon}{1/2-\varepsilon}<d<\frac{1/4-\varepsilon^{2}/2}{1/2+\varepsilon},
(C.4)
and by (C.3) such d should satisfy
\displaystyle\frac{1/8-\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{2}/2}{1/4-\varepsilon^{2}}<d<\frac{1}{2}.
(C.5)
To show the existence of such d, it suffices to check that the two open intervals of values of d given by (C.4) and (C.5) are non empty and moreover, have a non empty intersection.
In fact, it is easy to check that for any 0<\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2} we have
\displaystyle\frac{1/8-\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{2}/2}{1/4-\varepsilon^{2}}<\frac{1/4+\varepsilon^{2}/2-\varepsilon}{1/2-\varepsilon}<\frac{1/4-\varepsilon^{2}/2}{1/2+\varepsilon}<\frac{1}{2},
meaning that the open interval given by (C.4) is non empty and is completely contained in the interval given by (C.5).
This finishes the proof.
∎
