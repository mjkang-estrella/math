1064: Many engineering applications call for efficient methods to generate smooth inter-
1065: polating curves on non-Euclidean spaces. This is the case for instance in path plan-
1066: ning for mechanical systems whose configuration spaces have components which
1067: are Lie groups or symmetric spaces. Interpolation over a spherical surface, also
1068: has immediate applications in manufacturing industry. Several methods to gener-
1069: ate interpolating curves on Riemannian manifolds are available in the literature.
1070: They correspond to appropriate generalizations of classical methods which have
1071: been around for many years. We mention the variational approach to splines on
1072: manifolds [1, 3, 7], which may also be reformulated via a Hamiltonian formalism;
1073: the geometric approach that corresponds to the De Casteljau algorithm [2, 8]; and
1074: the analytic approach undertaken in [4]. These methods posed interesting new
1075: mathematical problems and challenges regarding implementation. Even for the
1076: simplest cases, say SO3 or S 2 , explicit solutions are hard to obtain. Following [5],
1077: we present a method to generate interpolating curves on smooth manifolds, which
1078: is based on a rolling and unwrapping technique. Examples considered in this talk
1079: are S n and SOn . The solution of the interpolating problem is given explicitly in
1080: terms of the coordinates of the embedding space. Moreover, because our solution
1081: curves are given in closed form they are easily implemented, see [10, 11].

1348: We study the convex hull of a mixed-integer set S by expressing each continuous
1349: variable as the average of k integral variables. This allows us to model S as a pure
1350: integer set. We use the additional integral variables to strengthen the inequalities
1351: that describe S.
1352:    We concentrate on a mixed-integer set defined as follows: Given a bipartite
1353: graph G = (U ∪ V, E), a set I ⊆ U ∪ V and rational numbers bij , ij ∈ E, let
1354:            S (G,I) = {x ∈ RU∪V | xi + xj ≥ bij     ij ∈ E; xi ∈ Z i ∈ I}.
1355:                             (G,I)
1356:     We show that the set S        is equivalent to the “network dual” set introduced
1357: and studied recently by Conforti, Di Summa, Eisenbrand and Wolsey [1]. Conforti
1358: et al. give an extended formulation for the polyhedron conv(S (G,I) ) and discuss
1359: cases in which the formulation is compact.
1360:     An extended formulation of a polyhedron P in the x-space is an inequality
1361: description Ax + Bµ ≥ d of a polyhedron Q in the (x, µ)-space such that P is
1362: the projection of Q in the x-space. We are interested in polyhedra P that are
1363: convex hulls of mixed integer sets such as conv(S (G,I) ). We say that an extended
1364: formulation is compact if the size of the system that describes Q is polynomially
1365: bounded in the size of the system of inequalities that describes the mixed integer
1366: set.
1367:     The smallest integer k such that kbij ∈ Z for all ij ∈ E is important in the
1368: study of the polyhedron conv(S (G,I) ). Indeed the size of the extended formulation
1369: of conv(S (G,I) ) grows with k. For the case k = 2, Conforti, Gerards and Zambelli
1370: [3] have given an explicit description of conv(S (G,I) ) in the space of the x variables
1371: by computing the projection of the extended formulation. However the essential
1372: inequalities that describe conv(S (G,I) ) become more and more complicated as k
1373: grows.
1374:     Our goal is to describe the polyhedron conv(S (G,I) ) in the space of the x vari-
1375: ables. To this end we derive an extended formulation for conv(S (G,I) ) that differs
1376: slightly from that proposed in [1] but whose projection cone is easier to analyze.
1377: Our principal result is a characterization of the structure of facet-defining inequal-
1378: ities when the graph G is a tree. In several cases in which such a characterization
1379: is known, including the mixing set studied by Günlük and Pochet [4] and the
1380: continuous mixing set studied by Van Vyve [5], this gives further insight into the
1381: inequalities, and it leads to the first explicit description for the continuous mixing
1382: set with flows treated by Conforti, Di Summa and Wolsey [2]. It also suggests the
1383: following conjecture, that we describe next.

2466:   The Virtual Private Network Design Problem with Concave Costs
2467:                            Dirk Oliver Theis
2468:     (joint work with Samuel Fiorini, Gianpaolo Oriolo, and Laura Sanità)
2469: 
2470: The symmetric Virtual Private Network Design (VPND) problem is concerned
2471: with buying capacity on links (edges) in a communication network such that cer-
2472: tain traffic demands can be met. The precise definition is below. It was shown
2473: by Fingerhut, Suri and Turner [3] and later, independently, by Gupta, Kleinberg,
2474: Kumar, Rastogi and Yener [8] that VPND can be solved in polynomial time if it
2475: has the so-called tree routing property, that is, each instance has an optimal solu-
2476: tion whose support is a tree. It was conjectured that VPND has the tree routing
2477: property, see, e.g., Erlebach and Rüegg [2], Italiano, Leonardi and Oriolo [12] and
2478: Hurkens, Keijsper and Stougie [11]. The conjecture was recently solved affirma-
2479: tively by Goyal, Olver and Shepherd [5] by settling an equivalent conjecture, due
2480: to Grandoni, Kaibel, Oriolo and Skutella [7], claiming that another problem called
2481: the Pyramidal Routing (PR) problem has the tree routing property. This fact had
2482: previously been established only for cycles [7, 11] and outerplanar graphs [4].
2483: 
2484: Combinatorial Optimization                                                      2927
2485: 
2486:    In recent work we have investigated a natural generalization of VPND where
2487: the cost per unit of capacity may decrease if a larger amount of capacity is reserved
2488: (economies of scale principle). The growth of the cost of capacity is modelled by
2489: a non-decreasing concave function f . We call the problem the concave symmetric
2490: Virtual Private Network Design (cVPND) problem. This problem is APX-hard
2491: for general f due to the fact that it contains the minimum Steiner tree problem
2492: as a special case.
2493:    Our main contributions are as follows. First, as a corollary to Goyal et al. [5],
2494: we show that a generalization of the PR problem which we call Concave Routing
2495: (CR) problem, and hence also cVPND, has the tree routing property. Second, we
2496: study approximation algorithms for cVPND. For general f , using known results
2497: on the so-called Single Source Buy at Bulk problem by Grandoni and Italiano [6],
2498: we give a randomized 24.92-approximation algorithm.

102: 3-chromatic case of this theorem.
103:    Obviously, Hamiltonian cycles and their powers have constant bandwidth. In
104: addition, Chung [9] proved that trees with constant maximum degree have band-
105: width at most O(n/ log n). Recently this result was extended to planar graphs, and

122: having a tower-type dependence on 1/γ.

124: grow with n. In [25], Komlós, Sárközy, and Szemerédi showed that every graph

127: while to try to extend Theorem 1 in a similar direction. The part in our proof
128: which partitions H in such a way that it can be embedded into G does in fact
129: not need an upper bound on ∆(H), see Lemma 8 below. However for the blow-up
130: lemma, which we then apply to find the required embedding, ∆(H) needs to be
131: bounded by a constant.
132:      Finally we would like to address the rôle of the chromatic number in Theorem 1
133: again. In the same way that the Hamiltonian cycle on an odd number of vertices
134: is forced as a spanning subgraph in any graph of minimum degree 21 n (although it
135: is 3- and not 2-chromatic), other (r + 1)-chromatic graphs are forced already when
136: δ(G) ≥ ( r−1  r + γ)n. It seems that one important question here is whether all r + 1
137: colours are needed by many vertices. For example, the critical chromatic number
138: χcr (H) of a graph H is defined as (χ(H) − 1)|V (H)|/(|V (H)| − σ), where σ denotes
139: the minimum size of the smallest colour class in a colouring of H with χ(H) colours.
140: Obviously, χ(H)−1 < χcr (H) ≤ χ(H), with (approximate) equality for σ tending to

142: proved that a minimum degree condition of δ(G) ≥ (χcr (H) − 1)n/χcr (H) suffices
143: to find a family of disjoint copies of H covering all but εn vertices of G. Kühn and
144: Osthus [27] further investigated this question and managed to determine for every
145: H the corresponding minimum degree condition (up to an additive constant) for
146: the containment of a spanning H-factor.

151: t ≤ t′ ≤ t + x such that σ(u) 6= 0 for all u ∈ [t′ , t′ + y]. We also say that the interval
152: [t′ , t′ + y] is zero free.
