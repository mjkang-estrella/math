On the asymptotic behaviour of the quantiles in the gamma distribution


Henrik Laurberg Pedersen


Abstract
    

The asymptotic behaviour of the quantiles in the gamma distribution are investigated as the shape parameter tends to zero. Some remarks about the behaviour at infinity are given.
  



Keywords: Gamma distribution, quantile, incomplete gamma function, asymptotic expansion.



2020 MSC: Primary 41A60; Secondary 33B20.




1 Introduction



The p-quantile m_{p}(x) in the gamma distribution of shape parameter x and scale parameter 1 is, for p\in(0,1), given implicitly as




\int_{0}^{m_{p}(x)}e^{-t}t^{x-1}\,dt=p\Gamma(x).

(1)


This equation can be written as P(x,m_{p}(x))=p, where P is a normalized incomplete gamma function. See [5, 8.2.4].
The asymptotic behaviour at infinity of the quantiles in the gamma distribution was investigated in the papers [7] and [4] (and for the beta distribution in [8]). We complement this by an investigation at the origin and revisit the expansion at infinity via an elementary method, inspired by [2]. Notice that the main focus in [2] (and [3]) was on monotonicity properties of the median m=m_{1/2}, and the so-called Chen-Rubin conjecture.



The behaviour of m and m^{\prime} at the origin was in [2] shown to be




m(x)\sim e^{-\gamma}e^{-\frac{\log 2}{x}},\quad m^{\prime}(x)\sim\frac{\log 2}{x^{2}}e^{-\gamma}e^{-\frac{\log 2}{x}}\quad\text{as}\ x\to 0.



Introducing the function
u(x)=e^{\nicefrac{{\log 2}}{{x}}}m(x)
it thus follows that u(x)\to e^{-\gamma} for x\to 0. A plot of the function u leaves the impression that also its derivative has a finite and positive limit at zero. See Figure 1. Let us mention that the asymptotic behaviour of m and m^{\prime} at the origin as displayed above is not sufficient for determining the behaviour of u^{\prime}. In fact, these relations only yield that




x^{2}u^{\prime}(x)=x^{2}e^{\frac{\log 2}{x}}m^{\prime}(x)-(\log 2)e^{\frac{\log 2}{x}}m(x)



tends to zero as x tends to zero.



We define, for p\in(0,1) and x>0,




u_{p}(x)=e^{-\frac{\log p}{x}}m_{p}(x).

(2)


Experimenting with plots of these functions (for different p) indicate that their behaviour at the origin do not depend on p. This motivates the results of the paper in which the asymptotic behaviour of u_{p} at the origin is studied.



In Proposition 2.4 it is shown that u_{p}\in C^{\infty}([0,\infty)) and that any derivative of u_{p} at the origin can be computed explicitly, and does not depend on p. (It follows from the implicit function theorem that m_{p} and hence also u_{p} are C^{\infty}-functions on (0,\infty).) In Proposition 3.2 and Corallary 3.3 the asymptotic behaviour of u_{p} is used to obtain the asymptotic behaviour of m_{p} and its derivatives at the origin. In particular it is proved that for any n\geq 0,




m_{p}^{(n)}(x)\sim\frac{(-\log p)^{n}}{x^{2n}}e^{-\gamma}e^{\frac{\log p}{x}}\quad\text{as}\ x\to 0,



generalizing the two relations mentioned above. See [1] for the asymptotic behaviour at zero of the quantiles in the beta distribution as a function of one of the parameters.



In Appendix A some remarks on the asymptotic behaviour of m_{p} at infinity are given. Another procedure (compared to [7] and [4]) is described and Maple code is given. We stress that our result, Theorem A.6, is also given in [4].


Figure 1: The graph of u



Let us end the introduction with a basic result about the quantiles.


Lemma 1.1.


The function m_{p} is strictly increasing on (0,\infty). Furthermore, \lim_{x\to 0}m_{p}(x)=0 and \lim_{x\to\infty}m_{p}(x)=\infty.



Proof.


The family \{e^{-t}t^{x-1}dt/\Gamma(x)\}_{x>0} is a convolution semigroup of probabilities on [0,\infty) and the assertions in the lemma hold for any convolution semigroup of probabilities on [0,\infty). This is proved in the same way as [2, Proposition 2.1], replacing \nicefrac{{1}}{{2}} by p.
∎






2 The behaviour of u_{p} at the origin



The limit of u_{p} (defined in (2)) at zero is given in Proposition 2.2 and depends on the next lemma.


Lemma 2.1.


We have \lim_{x\to 0}m_{p}(x)^{x}=p.



Proof.


Integration by parts yields




\displaystyle m_{p}(x)^{x}e^{-m_{p}(x)}
\displaystyle=x\int_{0}^{m_{p}(x)}t^{x-1}e^{-t}\,dt-\int_{0}^{m_{p}(x)}t^{x}e^{-t}\,dt




\displaystyle=p\Gamma(x+1)-\int_{0}^{m_{p}(x)}t^{x}e^{-t}\,dt,

(3)


and since m_{p}(x)\to 0 for x\to 0 the proof is complete.
∎



Proposition 2.2.


We have \lim_{x\to 0}u_{p}(x)=e^{-\gamma}.



Proof.


Differentiation of (3) yields after some manipulation




m_{p}(x)^{x}\ell_{p}^{\prime}(x)e^{-m_{p}(x)}=p\Gamma^{\prime}(x+1)-\int_{0}^{m_{p}(x)}t^{x}e^{-t}\log t\,dt,



where \ell_{p}(x)=x\log m_{p}(x). From Lemma 1.1 and Lemma 2.1 it follows that \lim_{x\to 0}\ell_{p}^{\prime}(x)=\Gamma^{\prime}(1)=-\gamma. Applying l’Hospital’s rule we obtain




\lim_{x\to 0}\log u_{p}(x)=\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\ell_{p}(x)-\log p}{x}=\lim_{x\to 0}\ell_{p}^{\prime}(x)=-\gamma



and this completes the proof.∎




The function u_{p} appears when making the change of variable s=e^{-\frac{\log p}{x}}t in the relation (1) which then becomes




\int_{0}^{u_{p}(x)}e^{-se^{\frac{\log p}{x}}+(x-1)\log s}\,ds=\Gamma(x).



Differentiation gives us




\displaystyle u_{p}^{\prime}(x)e^{-u_{p}(x)e^{\frac{\log p}{x}}}u_{p}(x)^{x-1}
\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{u_{p}(x)}e^{-se^{\frac{\log p}{x}}}s^{x}\,ds\,e^{\frac{\log p}{x}}\tfrac{\log p}{x^{2}}




\displaystyle\phantom{=}-\int_{0}^{u_{p}(x)}e^{-se^{\frac{\log p}{x}}}s^{x-1}\log s\,ds+\Gamma^{\prime}(x).



The limit of the sum of the two last terms on the right-hand side evaluates as \infty-\infty. In order to overcome this difficulty we rewrite as follows.




\displaystyle u_{p}^{\prime}(x)e^{-u_{p}(x)e^{\frac{\log p}{x}}}u_{p}(x)^{x-1}
\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{u_{p}(x)}e^{-se^{\frac{\log p}{x}}}s^{x}\,ds\,e^{\frac{\log p}{x}}\tfrac{\log p}{x^{2}}




\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\int_{0}^{u_{p}(x)}\left(1-e^{-se^{\frac{\log p}{x}}}\right)s^{x-1}\log s\,ds




\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\int_{0}^{u_{p}(x)}\frac{e^{-s}-1}{s}s^{x}\log s\,ds+\int_{u_{p}(x)}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-s}}{s}s^{x}\log s\,ds.



Putting




\displaystyle I_{1}(x)
\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{u_{p}(x)}e^{-se^{\frac{\log p}{x}}}s^{x}\,ds,



\displaystyle I_{2}(x)
\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{u_{p}(x)}\left(1-e^{-se^{\frac{\log p}{x}}}\right)s^{x-1}\log s\,ds,



\displaystyle I_{3}(x)
\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{u_{p}(x)}\frac{e^{-s}-1}{s}s^{x}\log s\,ds,



\displaystyle I_{4}(x)
\displaystyle=\int_{u_{p}(x)}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-s}}{s}s^{x}\log s\,ds,



the relation above can be written as




u_{p}^{\prime}(x)e^{-m_{p}(x)}u_{p}(x)^{x-1}=-I_{1}(x)e^{\frac{\log p}{x}}\tfrac{\log p}{x^{2}}+I_{2}(x)+I_{3}(x)+I_{4}(x).

(4)



Proposition 2.3.


The limit \lim_{x\to 0}u_{p}^{\prime}(x) exists and equals




e^{-\gamma}\left(\int_{0}^{e^{-\gamma}}\frac{e^{-s}-1}{s}\log s\,ds+\int_{e^{-\gamma}}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-s}}{s}\log s\,ds\right).





Proof.


It is easy to see that (writing u_{p}(0) instead of e^{-\gamma})




\lim_{x\to 0}I_{1}(x)=u_{p}(0),






\lim_{x\to 0}I_{3}(x)=\int_{0}^{u_{p}(0)}\frac{e^{-s}-1}{s}\log s\,ds,






\lim_{x\to 0}I_{4}(x)=\int_{u_{p}(0)}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-s}}{s}\log s\,ds,



and using the estimate |1-e^{-y}|\leq y for y\geq 0 we obtain




\lim_{x\to 0}I_{2}(x)=0.



These relations yield




\lim_{x\to 0}u_{p}^{\prime}(x)e^{-m_{p}(x)}u_{p}(x)^{x-1}=\int_{0}^{u_{p}(0)}\frac{e^{-s}-1}{s}\log s\,ds+\int_{u_{p}(0)}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-s}}{s}\log s\,ds,



i.e.




\lim_{x\to 0}u_{p}^{\prime}(x)=u_{p}(0)\left(\int_{0}^{u_{p}(0)}\frac{e^{-s}-1}{s}\log s\,ds+\int_{u_{p}(0)}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-s}}{s}\log s\,ds\right).



This completes the proof.
∎




By differentiation of the relation (4) one obtains an involved expression containing only one term with u_{p}^{\prime\prime}(x). It turns out that it is possible to compute the limit u_{p}^{\prime\prime}(0) and express it in terms of u_{p}^{\prime}(0) and u_{p}(0). The next step is to show that any derivative u_{p}^{(n)}(x) has a limit as x tends to 0, and hence that u_{p}\in C^{\infty}([0,\infty)).


Proposition 2.4.


The limit




\lim_{x\to 0}u_{p}^{(n)}(x)



exists for any n\geq 0 and is independent of p.




The proof of this proposition is carried out by an inductive argument. In order to do so some computational lemmas are needed. We shall also use the notation \partial_{x}^{j} to denote the jth derivative when it is convenient.
As one would expect, the function




\alpha_{p}(x)=e^{\frac{\log p}{x}}

(5)


plays a key role in the development. Its behaviour is determined by the following lemma.


Lemma 2.5.


For any n\geq 1,




\partial_{x}^{n}\left(e^{-1/x}\right)=p_{n}(1/x)e^{-1/x},



where p_{n}(t)=\sum_{k=n+1}^{2n}a_{k,n}t^{k} with a_{k,n}\in\mathbb{Z}. Furthermore a_{2n,n}=1, a_{2n-1,n}=-n(n-1) and a_{2n-2,n}=n(n-1)^{2}(n-2)/2.



Proof.


This is a standard induction argument, and the sequence \{p_{n}\} is easily shown to satisfy p_{n+1}(t)=t^{2}(p_{n}(t)-p_{n}^{\prime}(t)).
∎



Lemma 2.6.


Suppose that the limit \lim_{x\to 0}u_{p}^{(j)}(x) exists in \mathbb{R} for j=0,\ldots,q. Then \lim_{x\to 0}m_{p}^{(j)}(x)=0 for j=0,\ldots,q.



Proof.


This follows from the relation (with \alpha_{p} as in (5))




m_{p}^{(j)}(x)=\sum_{l=0}^{j}\binom{j}{l}\alpha_{p}^{(l)}(x)u_{p}^{(j-l)}(x),



combined with the fact that \alpha_{p}^{(l)}(x)\to 0 for x\to 0 for all l\geq 0.
∎



Lemma 2.7.





I_{1}(x)=u_{p}(x)^{x+1}\left(\frac{1}{x+1}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\frac{m_{p}(x)^{k}}{x+k+1}\right).





Proof.


Using the Taylor series expansion of the exponential function we get




\displaystyle I_{1}(x)
\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}e^{\frac{\log p}{x}k}\int_{0}^{u_{p}(x)}s^{x+k}\,ds




\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}e^{\frac{\log p}{x}k}\frac{u_{p}(x)^{x+k+1}}{x+k+1},



which gives the desired formula.
∎



Lemma 2.8.





\displaystyle I_{2}(x)
\displaystyle=u_{p}(x)^{x}\log u_{p}(x)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(k+1)!}\frac{m_{p}(x)^{k+1}}{x+k+1}




\displaystyle\phantom{M}-u_{p}(x)^{x}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(k+1)!}\frac{m_{p}(x)^{k+1}}{(x+k+1)^{2}}.





Proof.


This follows the same lines as the proof of Lemma 2.7, noticing that




\int_{0}^{u_{p}(x)}s^{x+k}\log s\,ds=\frac{u_{p}(x)^{x+k+1}}{x+k+1}\log u_{p}(x)-\frac{u_{p}(x)^{x+k+1}}{(x+k+1)^{2}}.



∎



Lemma 2.9.


Suppose that \lim_{x\to 0}u_{p}^{(j)}(x) exists in \mathbb{R} for j=0,\ldots,q. Then




\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\partial_{x}^{j}I_{1}(x)
\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to 0}\partial_{x}^{j}\left(\frac{u_{p}(x)^{x+1}}{x+1}\right),\ \text{and}



\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\partial_{x}^{j}I_{2}(x)
\displaystyle=0



for j=0,\ldots,q.



Proof.


This follows from Lemma 2.7 and Lemma 2.8, noticing that termwise differentiation is permitted and that the limit of any term involving m_{p}(x) in a positive power equals zero.
∎




The integrals I_{3} and I_{4} are analyzed using Lemma 2.10. Here, u_{p} could be replaced by any positive and smooth function, and the lemma is proved by induction.


Lemma 2.10.


The relation




\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{j}\int_{0}^{u_{p}(x)}g(s)\log s\,s^{x}\,ds
\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{j}\partial_{x}^{j-l}\left(u_{p}^{\prime}(x)u_{p}(x)^{x}g(u_{p}(x))(\log u_{p}(x))^{l}\right)




\displaystyle\phantom{M}+\int_{0}^{u_{p}(x)}g(s)(\log s)^{j+1}\,s^{x}\,ds



holds when g\in C^{\infty}([0,\infty)) and the relation




\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{j}\int_{u_{p}(x)}^{\infty}h(s)\log s\,s^{x}\,ds
\displaystyle=-\sum_{l=1}^{j}\partial_{x}^{j-l}\left(u_{p}^{\prime}(x)u_{p}(x)^{x}h(u_{p}(x))(\log u_{p}(x))^{l}\right)




\displaystyle\phantom{M}+\int_{u_{p}(x)}^{\infty}h(s)(\log s)^{j+1}\,s^{x}\,ds



holds when h\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty)) and decays exponentially at infinity.




Proof of Proposition 2.4.
Suppose that for j=0,\ldots,q, \lim_{x\to 0}\partial_{x}^{j}u_{p}(x) exists in \mathbb{R} and is independent of p. We set out to verify that \lim_{x\to 0}\partial_{x}^{q+1}u_{p}(x) exists in \mathbb{R} and is independent of p.
Differentiation of (4) q times yields




\partial_{x}^{q}\left(u_{p}^{\prime}(x)e^{-m_{p}(x)}u_{p}(x)^{x-1}\right)=\partial_{x}^{q}\left(I_{1}(x)\alpha_{p}^{\prime}(x)+I_{2}(x)+I_{3}(x)+I_{4}(x)\right).

(6)


The left hand side becomes




\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{q}\left(u_{p}^{\prime}(x)e^{-m_{p}(x)}u_{p}(x)^{x-1}\right)=




\displaystyle u_{p}^{(q+1)}(x)e^{-m_{p}(x)}u_{p}(x)^{x-1}+\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\binom{q}{j}u_{p}^{(j+1)}(x)\partial_{x}^{q-j}\left(e^{-m_{p}(x)}u_{p}(x)^{x-1}\right).



Notice that according to Lemma 2.6, m_{p}^{(k)}(x)=o(1) as x\to 0 for k\leq q, and therefore we get




\partial_{x}^{q-j}\left(e^{-m_{p}(x)}u_{p}(x)^{x-1}\right)=\partial_{x}^{q-j}u_{p}(x)^{x-1}+o(1).



This gives




\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{q}\left(u_{p}^{\prime}(x)e^{-m_{p}(x)}u_{p}(x)^{x-1}\right)
\displaystyle=u_{p}^{(q+1)}(x)e^{-m_{p}(x)}u_{p}(x)^{x-1}+




\displaystyle\phantom{=}\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\binom{q}{j}u_{p}^{(j+1)}(x)\partial_{x}^{q-j}u_{p}(x)^{x-1}+o(1).



The right hand side of (6) is rewritten as




\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{q}
\displaystyle\left(I_{1}(x)\alpha_{p}^{\prime}(x)+I_{2}(x)+I_{3}(x)+I_{4}(x)\right)




\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{q}\binom{q}{j}\partial_{x}^{j}I_{1}(x)\alpha_{p}^{(1+q-j)}(x)+\partial_{x}^{q}I_{2}(x)+\partial_{x}^{q}I_{3}(x)+\partial_{x}^{q}I_{4}(x).



From Lemma 2.5 it follows that \lim_{x\to 0}\alpha_{p}^{(k)}(x)=0 for any k\geq 0 and by Lemma 2.9 and Lemma 2.10 we see that, with g(s)=(e^{-s}-1)/s and h(s)=e^{-s}/s,




\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{q}
\displaystyle\left(I_{1}(x)\alpha_{p}^{\prime}(x)+I_{2}(x)+I_{3}(x)+I_{4}(x)\right)




\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\partial_{x}^{q-j}\left(u_{p}^{\prime}(x)u_{p}(x)^{x}(g(u_{p}(x))-h(u_{p}(x)))(\log u_{p}(x))^{j}\right)




\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\int_{0}^{u_{p}(x)}g(s)(\log s)^{q+1}\,s^{x}\,ds+\int_{u_{p}(x)}^{\infty}h(s)(\log s)^{q+1}\,s^{x}\,ds+o(1)




\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{q}\partial_{x}^{q-j}\left(u_{p}^{\prime}(x)u_{p}(x)^{x-1}(\log u_{p}(x))^{j}\right)




\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\int_{0}^{u_{p}(0)}g(s)(\log s)^{q+1}\,ds+\int_{u_{p}(0)}^{\infty}h(s)(\log s)^{q+1}\,ds+o(1).



Combination of these relations yield




\displaystyle u_{p}^{(q+1)}(x)e^{-m_{p}(x)}u_{p}(x)^{x-1}




\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{q}\partial_{x}^{q-j}\left(u_{p}^{\prime}(x)u_{p}(x)^{x-1}(\log u_{p}(x))^{j}\right)-\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\binom{q}{j}u_{p}^{(j+1)}(x)\partial_{x}^{q-j}u_{p}(x)^{x-1}




\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\int_{0}^{u_{p}(0)}g(s)(\log s)^{q+1}\,ds+\int_{u_{p}(0)}^{\infty}h(s)(\log s)^{q+1}\,ds+o(1).



The limit, as x\to 0, of the right hand side of this relation is an expression involving u_{p}^{(j)}(0), j\leq q, which by our induction hypothesis are independent of p.
 \square



Letting




s_{q}=\int_{0}^{e^{-\gamma}}g(s)(\log s)^{q+1}\,ds+\int_{e^{-\gamma}}^{\infty}h(s)(\log s)^{q+1}\,ds,



where g(s)=(e^{-s}-1)/s and h(s)=e^{-s}/s it is straight forward to verify that




\displaystyle u_{p}^{\prime}(0)
\displaystyle=e^{-\gamma}s_{1},

(7)


\displaystyle u_{p}^{\prime\prime}(0)
\displaystyle=e^{-\gamma}\left(s_{2}+s_{1}^{2}+2\gamma s_{1}\right).

(8)






3 Asymptotic behaviour of the quantiles at zero



The first result is simply the Taylor expansion of u_{p}.


Proposition 3.1.


As x tends to 0 we have, for any n,




m_{p}(x)=e^{\frac{\log p}{x}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{u_{p}^{(k)}(0)}{k!}x^{k}+O\left(x^{n+1}\right)\right).





Proposition 3.2.


If \{p_{k}\} are the polynomials from Lemma 2.5 then the relation




m_{p}^{(n)}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(-\log p)^{-k}p_{k}\left(-\frac{\log p}{x}\right)e^{\frac{\log p}{x}}u_{p}^{(n-k)}(x)



holds.



Proof.


From Lemma 2.5 we get that




\partial_{x}^{k}\left(e^{\frac{\log p}{x}}\right)=(-\log p)^{-k}p_{k}\left(-\frac{\log p}{x}\right)e^{\frac{\log p}{x}}



and the result follows from Leibniz’ rule.
∎




Proposition 3.2 gives us information on the asymptotic expansion of the derivatives of m_{p}:


Corollary 3.3.


For any n\geq 0 we have, recalling that a_{l,k} are the coefficients of p_{k} in Lemma 2.5,




\displaystyle e^{-\frac{\log p}{x}}m_{p}^{(n)}(x)
\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{2k}\binom{n}{k}a_{l+k,k}\left(-\log p\right)^{l}\frac{u_{p}^{(n-k+j)}(0)}{j!\,x^{k+l-j}}+O(x)




\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{2n}\frac{z_{k,n}}{x^{k}}+O(x)



for x\to 0,
where




\displaystyle z_{2n,n}
\displaystyle=e^{-\gamma}(-\log p)^{n},\ \text{and}



\displaystyle z_{2n-1,n}
\displaystyle=e^{-\gamma}(-\log p)^{n-1}\left(-s_{1}\log p-n(n-1)\right).





Proof.


The first identity follows from the expansion




u_{p}^{(n-k)}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{2k}\frac{u_{p}^{(n-k+j)}(0)}{j!}x^{j}+O(x^{2k+1})



and Proposition 3.2.
To find z_{2n,n} we identify the set of triples (k,l,j) of indices for which k+l-j=2n. This is \{(n,n,0)\}. To find z_{2n-1,n} the corresponding set is \{(n,n,1),(n,n-1,0)\}.
The formulae follow by computation, using (7).
∎



Remark 3.4.


We have




\displaystyle z_{2n-2,n}
\displaystyle=e^{-\gamma}(-\log p)^{n-2}\left(\frac{(\log p)^{2}}{2}(s_{2}+s_{1}^{2}+2\gamma s_{1})+n(n-1)s_{1}\log p\right.




\displaystyle\phantom{=}\left.+\frac{n(n-1)^{2}(n-2)}{2}-\frac{n^{2}(n-1)^{2}(n-2)}{2}s_{1}\log p\right).



To see this notice that the set of indices is \{(n,n,2),(n,n-1,1),(n,n-2,0),(n-1,n-1,0)\}, use (8) and Lemma 2.5.






Appendix A Asymptotic behaviour at infinity; Maple code



The purpose of this appendix is to outline a method where the asymptotic behaviour of the function




\varphi_{p}(x)=\log\left(\frac{x}{m_{p}(x)}\right)



is used to find the asymptotic behaviour of m_{p}. Our method is quite elementary, basically only Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem is used. The notation




f(x)\sim\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\beta_{k}}{x^{k}},\quad x\to\infty



means that for any N, f(x)-\sum_{k=0}^{N}\beta_{k}/x^{k}=O(x^{-N-1}) as x\to\infty.
Inserting Euler’s integral representation of \Gamma(x) in (1), changing variables to u=\log(x/t) and performing some manipulations we obtain




\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\varphi_{p}(x)}e^{-x(e^{-u}+u)}\,du
\displaystyle=(1-p)\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x(e^{-u}+u)}\,du-p\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x(e^{u}-u)}\,du.

(9)


By multiplication by e^{x} and the change of variable s=\sqrt{x}u we get




\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x)}e^{-s^{2}h(-\nicefrac{{s}}{{\sqrt{x}}})}\,ds
\displaystyle=(1-p)J_{1}(x)-pJ_{2}(x),

(10)


where h is the positive and increasing function on \mathbb{R} defined as




h(u)=\frac{e^{u}-1-u}{u^{2}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(k+2)!}u^{k},

(11)


and




J_{1}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s^{2}h(-\nicefrac{{s}}{{\sqrt{x}}})}\,ds,\quad J_{2}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s^{2}h(\nicefrac{{s}}{{\sqrt{x}}})}\,ds.



The asymptotic behaviour of \varphi_{p} is found using the behaviour of the right hand side of (10) (see Proposition A.3) and by relating the behaviour of \varphi_{p} to the integral in the left hand side of (10).
A fundamental sequence of polynomials is introduced in the next lemma (which is proved by induction).


Lemma A.1.


Let n\geq 0. There exists a polynomial Q_{n} of n+1 variables such that




\partial_{v}^{n}e^{-s^{2}h(v)}=Q_{n}(s,h^{\prime}(v),\ldots,h^{(n+1)}(v))e^{-s^{2}h(v)}.



For fixed v\in\mathbb{R}, Q_{n} is an even polynomial of s of degree 2n.




The first few of these polynomials are Q_{1}(s,y_{1})=-s^{2}y_{1},
Q_{2}(s,y_{1},y_{2})=s^{4}y_{1}^{2}-s^{2}y_{2} and
Q_{3}(s,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})=-s^{6}y_{1}^{3}+3s^{4}y_{1}y_{2}-s^{2}y_{3}.


Lemma A.2.


The function




J(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s^{2}h(xs)}\,ds



belongs to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}) and




J^{(n)}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{n}Q_{n}(s,h^{\prime}(xs),\ldots,h^{(n+1)}(xs))e^{-s^{2}h(xs)}\,ds,



Q_{n} being the polynomial introduced in Lemma A.1.



Proof.


By the chain rule,




\partial_{x}^{n}e^{-s^{2}h(xs)}=s^{n}Q_{n}(s,h^{\prime}(xs),\ldots,h^{(n+1)}(xs))e^{-s^{2}h(xs)}.



The proof of the lemma consists now of obtaining an integrable majorant of this expression for -R\leq x\leq R where R>0 is arbitrary. Assume first that x\geq 0. For u\geq 0 we have an exponential upper bound on the derivatives of h:




h^{(j)}(u)=\partial_{u}^{j}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{k}}{(k+2)!}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(k+j+2)(k+j+1)}\frac{u^{k}}{k!}\leq e^{u}.



Writing




s^{n}Q_{n}(s,y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+1})=\sum_{(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n+1})}\alpha_{(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n+1})}s^{m_{1}}y_{1}^{m_{2}}\cdots y_{n+1}^{m_{n+1}}



we get




\left|\partial_{x}^{n}e^{-s^{2}h(xs)}\right|\leq\sum_{(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n+1})}|\alpha_{(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n+1})}|s^{m_{1}}e^{m_{2}Rs}\cdots e^{m_{n+1}Rs}e^{-s^{2}/2},



which is integrable on [0,\infty).
Assuming that x\leq 0 we remark that any derivative of h is bounded on (-\infty,0] so that, for some polynomial r(s),




|\partial_{x}^{n}e^{-s^{2}h(xs)}|\leq r(s)e^{-s^{2}h(xs)}\leq r(s)e^{-s^{2}h(-Rs)},



which is integrable on [0,\infty).
∎




To ease notation, let




Q_{k}(s)=Q_{k}(s,h^{\prime}(0),\ldots,h^{(k+1)}(0)).

(12)



Proposition A.3.


As x\to\infty the functions J_{1} and J_{2} admit the expansions




\displaystyle J_{1}(x)
\displaystyle\sim\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\frac{c_{k}}{x^{k/2}}\quad\text{and}\quad J_{2}(x)\sim\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\frac{c_{k}}{x^{k/2}},



where




c_{k}=\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{k}Q_{k}(s)e^{-s^{2}/2}\,ds.





Proof.


Immediate using J_{1}(x)=J(-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{x}}}) and J_{2}(x)=J(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{x}}}).
∎




The values of c_{k} can be computed via the explicit formulas Q_{k}, the derivatives of h at the origin and the elementary formula




\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{j}e^{-s^{2}/2}\,ds=2^{(j-1)/2}\Gamma\left((j+1)/2\right).



We remark that
J_{2}(x)=x^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}-x}e^{x}\Gamma(x,x), where \Gamma(x,x) is the complementary incomplete gamma function (see [5, 8.2.2]) and this can also be used to obtain the expansion of J_{2}. For sharp error bounds and exponential asymptotics, see [6].



Notice that when L\in\mathbb{R}, the relation




\int_{0}^{L}e^{-s^{2}h(-\nicefrac{{s}}{{\sqrt{x}}})}\,ds=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\frac{d_{k}}{x^{k/2}},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

(13)


where




d_{k}=\int_{0}^{L}s^{k}Q_{k}(s)e^{-s^{2}/2}\,ds.



is obtained directly from Lemma A.1 and Taylor expansion.



We let L_{p} denote the (1-p)-quantile in the standard Gaussian distribution:




\int_{0}^{L_{p}}e^{-s^{2}/2}\,ds=(1-2p)\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s^{2}/2}\,ds=(1-2p)\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}.

(14)



Lemma A.4.


The function \varphi_{p} admits an asymptotic expansion at infinity:




\varphi_{p}(x)\sim\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{b_{k}}{x^{k/2}}=\frac{L_{p}}{x^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}+\frac{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}+\nicefrac{{L_{p}^{2}}}{{6}}}{x}+\frac{\nicefrac{{L_{p}^{3}}}{{36}}+\nicefrac{{5L_{p}}}{{36}}}{x^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}}+\ldots\ .





Proof.


From (10) and Proposition A.3 it follows that




\lim_{x\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x)}e^{-s^{2}h(-\nicefrac{{s}}{{\sqrt{x}}})}\,ds=(1-2p)\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}.



For s\geq 0 and x\geq 1, h(-\nicefrac{{s}}{{\sqrt{x}}})\geq h(-s) so that
e^{-s^{2}h(-\nicefrac{{s}}{{\sqrt{x}}})}\leq e^{-s^{2}h(-s)}=e^{-(e^{-s}+s-1)},
which is integrable. For s\leq 0, h(-\nicefrac{{s}}{{\sqrt{x}}})\geq h(0)=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}} so that
e^{-s^{2}h(-\nicefrac{{s}}{{\sqrt{x}}})}\leq e^{-\nicefrac{{s^{2}}}{{2}}},
which is also integrable. Let \alpha=\limsup_{x\to\infty}\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x) and choose a sequence \{x_{n}\} tending to infinity such that \lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{x_{n}}\varphi_{p}(x_{n})=\alpha. By dominated convergence it follows that




\int_{0}^{\alpha}e^{-\nicefrac{{s^{2}}}{{2}}}\,ds=(1-2p)\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}.



Repeating the argument using \beta=\liminf_{x\to\infty}\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x) we see that \alpha=\beta=L_{p}, where L_{p} is given in (14) and hence that \lim_{x\to\infty}\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x)=L_{p}.



The next step is to rewrite relation (10) in the form




\displaystyle\int_{L_{p}}^{\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x)}e^{-s^{2}h(-\nicefrac{{s}}{{\sqrt{x}}})}\,ds
\displaystyle=(1-p)J_{1}(x)-pJ_{2}(x)-\int_{0}^{L_{p}}e^{-s^{2}h(-\nicefrac{{s}}{{\sqrt{x}}})}\,ds.

(15)


By (13) (with L=L_{p}) and Proposition A.3 the right hand side of (15) admits the asymptotic expansion




\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{A_{k}}{x^{k/2}},



where A_{k}=\left((1-p)(-1)^{k}c_{k}-pc_{k}-(-1)^{k}d_{k}\right)/k!. (Notice that A_{0}=0.)
Looking at the left-hand side of (15), we have




\int_{L_{p}}^{\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x)}e^{-s^{2}h(-\nicefrac{{s}}{{\sqrt{x}}})}\,ds=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\int_{L_{p}}^{\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x)}s^{k}Q_{k}(s)e^{-s^{2}/2}\,ds\,\frac{1}{x^{k/2}}.

(16)


The Taylor series of q_{k}(s)=s^{k}Q_{k}(s)e^{-s^{2}/2} centered at L_{p}
is inserted in (16) and we thus get




\sum_{k,j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}q_{k}^{(j)}(L_{p})}{k!(j+1)!}\left(\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x)-L_{p}\right)^{j+1}\frac{1}{x^{k/2}}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{A_{k}}{x^{k/2}}+o\left(\frac{1}{x^{N/2}}\right).

(17)


We prove, using (17), that there is a sequence \{a_{k}\} such that, for any N\geq 0,




\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x)-L_{p}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{a_{k}}{x^{k/2}}+o\left(\frac{1}{x^{N/2}}\right).

(18)


(This proves the lemma.) For N=0 it is proved above. For N=1, (17) yields




\sqrt{x}\left(\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x)-L_{p}\right)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{q_{0}^{(j)}(L_{p})}{(j+1)!}\left(\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x)-L_{p}\right)^{j}=A_{1}+o\left(1\right).



Since the infinite sum on the left-hand side equals q_{0}(L_{p})+o(1) it follows that




a_{1}=\nicefrac{{A_{1}}}{{q_{0}(L_{p})}}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}\left(1+\nicefrac{{L_{p}^{2}}}{{2}}\right).



Assume now that (18) holds for N\geq 1.
Then first of all, for j\geq 0, we have, for some numbers a_{j,l},




\left(\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x)-L_{p}\right)^{j+1}=\sum_{l=1+j}^{N+j}\frac{a_{j,l}}{x^{l/2}}+o\left(\frac{1}{x^{(N+j)/2}}\right).

(19)


It follows from (17) that




\displaystyle q_{0}(L_{p})\left(\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x)-L_{p}\right)
\displaystyle=-\sum_{1\leq k+j\leq N}\frac{(-1)^{k}q_{k}^{(j)}(L_{p})}{k!(j+1)!}\left(\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x)-L_{p}\right)^{j+1}\frac{1}{x^{k/2}}




\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\frac{A_{k}}{x^{k/2}}+o\left(\frac{1}{x^{(N+1)/2}}\right),



since \left(\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x)-L_{p}\right)^{j+1}/x^{k/2}=o(1/x^{(N+1)/2}) for j+k\geq N+1 and this shows that \sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x)-L_{p} has an asymptotic expansion up to order (N+1)/2. It also shows how to compute the coefficients a_{k}(=a_{0,k}) recursively. Let T_{N} denote the triples of non-negative integers (k,l,j) such that




k+j\geq 1,\quad j+1\leq l\leq N+j,\quad k+l=N+1.



Then




\displaystyle a_{N+1}=\frac{1}{q_{0}(L_{p})}\left(\sum_{(k,l,j)\in T_{N}}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}q_{k}^{(j)}(L_{p})}{k!(j+1)!}a_{j,l}+A_{N+1}\right).

(20)


Since T_{1}=\{(1,1,0),(0,2,1)\} and a_{1,2}=a_{1}^{2} we find that
a_{2}=\nicefrac{{L_{p}^{3}}}{{36}}+\nicefrac{{5L_{p}}}{{36}}.
∎



Remark A.5.


It is evident that also J_{1}^{\prime} and J_{2}^{\prime} have asymptotic expansions at infinity. From the relation




\displaystyle(\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x))^{\prime}e^{-(\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x))^{2}}h(
\displaystyle-\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x)/\sqrt{x})=(1-p)J_{1}^{\prime}(x)-pJ_{2}^{\prime}(x)




\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\int_{0}^{\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x)}e^{-2^{2}h(-s/\sqrt{x})}h^{\prime}(-s/\sqrt{x})s^{3}\,ds\frac{1}{2x^{3/2}}



it follows that also (\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x))^{\prime} has an asymptotic expansion at infinity.



Theorem A.6.


The p-quantile m_{p} has an asymptotic expansion at infinity:




m_{p}(x)\sim\sum_{n=-2}^{\infty}\frac{\tau_{n}}{x^{n/2}}=x-L_{p}\,x^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}+\nicefrac{{(L_{p}^{2}-1)}}{{3}}+(\nicefrac{{L_{p}(7-L_{p}^{2})}}{{36}})\,x^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}+\ldots.





Proof.


This follows from Lemma A.4 and
the relation m_{p}(x)=xe^{-\varphi_{p}(x)}.
∎




This result was obtained in [4, Theorem 1.3], where it was also obtained that the coefficient \tau_{n} is a polynomial of degree n+2 with rational coefficients independent of p, evaluated at L_{p}. It is an even (resp. odd) polynomial when n is even (resp. odd). These facts, except the degree being n+2, can also be obtained via the method outlined above but we skip the details.



Maple code for computing the polynomials in the expansions of \varphi_{p} and m_{p} is given below. We get (in accordance with [4, Table 2] since L_{1-p}=-L_{p})

\displaystyle=-\nicefrac{{t^{4}}}{{270}}-\nicefrac{{t^{2}}}{{810}}+\nicefrac{{8}}{{405}}



\displaystyle\tau_{3}(t)
\displaystyle=-\nicefrac{{t^{5}}}{{4320}}-\nicefrac{{8t^{3}}}{{1215}}+\nicefrac{{433t}}{{38880}}



\displaystyle\tau_{4}(t)
\displaystyle=\nicefrac{{t^{6}}}{{17010}}-\nicefrac{{t^{4}}}{{840}}-\nicefrac{{923t^{2}}}{{204120}}+\nicefrac{{184}}{{25515}}



\displaystyle\tau_{5}(t)
\displaystyle=\nicefrac{{139t^{7}}}{{5443200}}+\nicefrac{{1451t^{5}}}{{48988800}}-\nicefrac{{289517t^{3}}}{{146966400}}-\nicefrac{{289717t}}{{146966400}}.



The procedure Qpol computes the polynomial Q_{k}(s) defined in (12):
Qpol := proc(k, s)
    local T, l, h;
    h := v -> (exp(v) - v - 1)/v^2;
    T := 1;
    for l to k do
       T := diff(T, v) - s^2*T*diff(h(v), v);
    end do;
    limit(T, v = 0);
end proc;


The procedure qkj computes q_{k}^{(j)}(L) from the proof of Lemma A.4 at L:
qkj := proc(j, k)
    local T;
    if j = 0 then
       T := s^k*Qpol(k, s)*exp(-1/2*s^2);
    else
       T := diff(s^k*Qpol(k, s)*exp(-1/2*s^2), s $ j);
    end if;
    eval(T, s = L);
end proc;


The procedure compute(j,n) computes the coefficient a_{j,n} of x^{-n/2} in the asymptotic expansion of \left(\sqrt{x}\varphi_{p}(x)-L_{p}\right)^{j+1}.
compute := proc(j, n)
    local T, q;
    global a;
    T := 0;
    if j = 0 then
       T := a[0, n - j];
    else for q from 0 to n - j - 1 do
            T := T + a[j - 1, q + j]*a[0, n - j - q];
         end do;
    end if;
    T;
end proc;


The numbers A_{k} can be computed using the functions
c := k -> int(s^k*Qpol(k, s)*exp(-1/2*s^2), s = 0 .. infinity);
d := k -> int(s^k*Qpol(k, s)*exp(-1/2*s^2), s = 0 .. L);
A := k -> ((1 - p)*(-1)^k*c(k) - p*c(k) - (-1)^k*d(k))/k!;


For a given M, the coefficients a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{M+1} are computed using (20):
M := 7;
for j from 0 to M do
    for l from 0 to M do a[j, l] := 0; end do;
end do;
a[0, 1] := (1 + L^2/2)/3;
for N from 1 to M do
    S := 0;
    for k from 0 to N do
        l := N + 1 - k;
        for j from max(0, 1 - k) to l - 1 do
            a[j,l] := compute(j,l);
            S := S + (-1)^(k+1)*qkj(j, k)*a[j,l]/(k!*(j+1)!);
        end do;
    end do;
    S := S + A(N + 1);
    p := 1/2 - erf(L/sqrt(2))/2;
    a[0, N + 1] := simplify(S*exp(L^2/2));
end do

for l from 0 to M + 1 do
    print(eval(a[0, l]));
end do


Finally the expansion of m_{p} is found using m_{p}(x)=xe^{-\phi_{p}(x)}. We found it convenient to change variable to y=1/\sqrt{x} and use taylor:
phip_of_y := L*y + sum(a[0, l]*y^(l + 1), l = 0 .. M);
temp := convert(taylor(exp(-phip_of_y), y = 0, M + 1), polynom);
collect(expand(x*eval(temp, y = 1/x^(1/2))), x);
