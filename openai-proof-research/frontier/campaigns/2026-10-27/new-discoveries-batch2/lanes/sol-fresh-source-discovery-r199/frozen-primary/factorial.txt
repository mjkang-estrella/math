 Abstract. 
 Assume that the Riemann zeta function has no zeros in
 \Re(s)>1-\delta , s\neq 1 , for some fixed 0 
 Key words and phrases:  arithmetic complexity, factorial multiples, smooth numbers, short intervals, genus-two Jacobians
 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification Primary 11Y55; Secondary 11N25, 11G20, 68Q15
 1. Introduction 
 The purpose of this paper is to bound the arithmetic complexity of a
suitable positive multiple of each factorial. The main result,
Theorem  A , obtains the scale
 \exp(O_{\delta}(\sqrt{\log k\log\log k})) from a fixed zero-free
half-plane for the Riemann zeta function, without assuming Cheng’s
smooth-number conjecture for elliptic Hasse intervals.
Here \tau(n) measures the number of additions, subtractions and
multiplications needed to construct n from 1 and 2 , and \mu(k) 
denotes the least such cost among positive multiples of k! .
The precise circuit model and both quantities are specified in
Definition  4.1 . 
 The arithmetic cost of integers was studied by de Melo and Svaiter
 [ dMS96 ] , who obtained elementary universal bounds and
lower bounds valid for almost all integers. Moreira [ Mor97 ] 
sharpened the latter to the scale \log n/\log\log n and established a
matching asymptotic upper scale for all sufficiently large integers.
These general estimates leave substantial room for special sequences:
repeated squaring produces some very large integers with very short
programs, and an almost-all lower bound does not determine the complexity
of factorials or of their easiest multiples. Shub and Smale
 [ SS95 ] showed that failure of a polynomial bound in \log k 
for ultimate factorial complexity would imply
 P_{\mathbb{C}}\neq NP_{\mathbb{C}} . Upper bounds for \mu(k) thus form
part of a broader study of explicit integers and algebraic complexity. 
 Further connections arise from the arithmetic complexity of explicit
integer sequences. Koiran [ Koi04 ] relates the construction of
integers such as \lfloor 2^{n}\log 2\rfloor to Valiant’s theory of
arithmetic circuits. In particular, a polynomial-size constant-free
circuit family for the permanent would give a polynomial bound in
 \log n for the cost of this sequence. With the additional assumption
 P=PSPACE , his results give such a bound for \tau(n!) as well.
Thus lower bounds for particular integer sequences can have consequences
for polynomial circuit complexity, beyond the almost-all estimates for
integers of a given size. 
 There is also a computational line of work on concrete upper bounds.
Markström [ Mar15 ] uses exhaustive searches for short
straight-line programs to determine exact values and bounds for small
factorials, primorials, and their ultimate complexities.
These calculations complement asymptotic constructions by exhibiting
short programs for individual integers. On the asymptotic side,
successive multiplication gives \mu(k)\leq\tau(k!)=O(k) , while
Strassen’s factorial algorithm gives the bound
 \tau(k!)=O(\sqrt{k}(\log k)^{2}) ; see
 [ Mar15 , Section 2] . The possibility of choosing a multiple
of k! permits the much smaller bounds discussed next. 
 Cheng [ Che04 , Conjecture 1 and Theorem 1] obtained 
 \mu(k)\leq\exp\!\left(O(\sqrt{\log k\log\log k})\right) 
 under a conjecture about smooth numbers in the elliptic Hasse intervals
 [p+1-2\sqrt{p},p+1+2\sqrt{p}] .
Writing L_{p}[a]=\exp(a\sqrt{\log p\log\log p}) , that conjecture asks,
for every fixed a>0 , for \sqrt{p}\,L_{p}[-1/(2a)+o(1)] many
 L_{p}[a] -smooth integers in such an interval.
His construction uses elliptic-curve division polynomials and also gives
a randomized subexponential-time procedure for producing the programs. 
 The interval length in this conjecture is essential. Younis
 [ You24 , Theorem 1.3] , assuming RH, proves smooth-number
asymptotics for x^{\theta}\leq h\leq x and
 (\log x)^{K(\theta)}\leq y\leq 2x , for each fixed \theta>1/2 .
Cheng’s smoothness scale exceeds this lower bound on y , but his
intervals have length 4\sqrt{p} at centers comparable to p .
For every fixed \theta>1/2 , 4\sqrt{p}/p^{\theta}\to 0 .
Consequently, Younis’s RH theorem does not establish Cheng’s conjecture:
the endpoint interval scale remains outside its stated range. 
 A different algebraic construction changes the required interval scale.
Zhang [ Zha26 , Theorem 1.2] obtained the unconditional bound 
 \mu(k)\leq\exp\!\left(O((\log k)^{2/3}(\log\log k)^{1/3})\right) 
 using genus-two Jacobians, a family with a non-torsion marked divisor
class, and integer circuits encoding nonzero arithmetic certificates.
The order-counting theorem of Lenstra, Pila and Pomerance
 [ LPP02 , Theorem 1.1] supplies the relevant genus-two orders.
The intervals used in this construction are 
 I_{p}=\left[p^{2}-\frac{1}{2}p^{3/2},\,p^{2}+\frac{1}{2}p^{3/2}\right]. 
 Their centers are of size p^{2} and their lengths are p^{3/2} ,
corresponding to the exponent 3/4 rather than 1/2 . 
 The main contribution here is to recover Cheng’s square-root logarithmic
upper-bound scale by combining this genus-two construction with a
short-interval smooth-number estimate under a quasi-Riemann hypothesis.
More precisely, any fixed \delta>0 with
 \zeta(s)\neq 0 for \Re(s)>1-\delta , s\neq 1 , suffices.
Theorem  B proves the required density estimate at every
sufficiently large center on the x^{3/4} interval scale, with smoothness
cutoff \exp(\sqrt{\log x\log\log x}) .
The transfer in Section  4 then yields the bound for
 \mu(k) with all integer gates charged. This removes the need for
Cheng’s smooth-number conjecture in obtaining that upper-bound scale;
it does not prove the conjecture itself. The genus-two family and
certificate construction are those of [ Zha26 ] , while the
analytic argument adapts Younis’s contour method to the fixed-strip
hypothesis. 
 Our main result is the following. 
 Theorem A (Positive factorial multiples) . 
 Assume that a fixed real number 0 
 \zeta(s)\neq 0\qquad\bigl(\Re(s)>1-\delta,\ s\neq 1\bigr). 
 There are constants C>0 and k_{0}\geq 3 , depending only on \delta , such that, for every integer k\geq k_{0} , there is a positive integer m_{k} satisfying 
 \tau(m_{k}k!)\leq\exp\!\left(C\sqrt{\log k\log\log k}\right). 
 All logarithms are natural. The constants may depend on \delta but are independent of k . 
 The multiplier is a positive integer existence witness. The circuit computes the multiple m_{k}k! from 1 and 2 . 
 The new ingredient concerns smooth integers at every sufficiently large real center. We use the counting function \Psi(x,y) and saddle point \alpha(x,y) defined in Definition  2.1 . 
 Theorem B (Smooth integers under a fixed-strip hypothesis) . 
 Assume that a fixed real number 0 
 \zeta(s)\neq 0\qquad\bigl(\Re(s)>1-\delta,\ s\neq 1\bigr). 
 For x>e , put 
 L=\log x,\qquad S=\sqrt{L\log L},\qquad y=e^{S},\qquad u=\frac{L}{S}, 
 and let \alpha=\alpha(x,y)>0 be determined by 
 \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq y\\
q\ {\rm prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\log q}{q^{\alpha}-1}=L. 
 There are constants C_{\delta} and X_{\delta} , depending only on \delta , such that, for every real x\geq X_{\delta} and every real h with
 x^{3/4}\leq h\leq x , 
 \left|\frac{\Psi(x+h,y)-\Psi(x,y)}{\alpha(h/x)\Psi(x,y)}-1\right|\leq\frac{C_{\delta}}{u}. 
 After increasing X_{\delta} , the same range satisfies 
 \Psi(x+h,y)-\Psi(x,y)\geq\frac{h}{4}\exp\!\left(-3\sqrt{\log x\log\log x}\right). 
 The analytic method underlying Theorem  B follows
 [ You24 , Section 3] . In particular, the construction of the contour
as the right envelope of a central path, paths associated with zeta zeros,
and a remaining vertical line is adapted from that work.
Sections  2 and  3 supply the details
needed to apply this method under the fixed hypothesis
 \zeta(s)\neq 0 for \Re(s)>1-\delta , s\neq 1 .
Section  2 collects the unconditional estimates used
in the argument, and Section  3 verifies the local shift,
the envelope construction, and the contributions of all contour pieces for
 y=\exp(\sqrt{\log x\log\log x}) and x^{3/4}\leq h\leq x .
These verifications yield thresholds depending on \delta and estimates
uniform in h ; the underlying contour construction is due to Younis. 
 The factor \alpha is retained throughout, including at h=x .
Recent AI-assisted work addresses the fixed-half-plane hypothesis directly
in the classical setting. OpenAI’s preprints [ OA26a , OA26b ] 
report proofs of nonvanishing for \Re(s)>7/8 and \Re(s)>11/12 ,
respectively; the latter is a shorter argument written with human assistance.
These conclusions supply the hypothesis of Theorems  A 
and  B with \delta=1/8 and \delta=1/12 , respectively.
Cureton and Thorner [ CT26 , Theorem 1.1] report the
corresponding result for Dedekind zeta functions over arbitrary number fields,
with \delta=1/6000 . For our application, the classical Riemann zeta
function alone is sufficient. We state both theorems with their common
zero-free hypothesis explicit and prove the resulting implications below;
either of the reported classical nonvanishing results then yields an
unconditional specialization.
In that specialization, the deduction of the square-root logarithmic
bound is complete using the cited arithmetic inputs and
Theorem  B ; no separate conjecture about smooth numbers in
elliptic Hasse intervals is required. 
 The local shift uses [ You24 , Lemma 2.3] , with the fixed positive strip width making its errors small at the chosen value of y . Zero density bounds the sum of the retained zero paths. The other analytic inputs are the unconditional estimates in [ You24 , (1.3), (1.5), (1.6), (1.9), (1.13), and Lemma 3.6] . 
 The arithmetic construction is inherited from [ Zha26 ] . Its marked family is given by [ Zha26 , (7)–(9) and Lemma 4.1] . We use its charged positive certificate [ Zha26 , Proposition 5.1] and its transfer to the marked slice [ Zha26 , Lemma 6.1] . Recording rational failures and amplifying prime support are the constructions of [ Zha26 , Lemmas 3.2 and 3.1] . These are established ingredients of the present proof. 
 Section  2 fixes the analytic notation and records the exact unconditional inputs. Section  3 proves Theorem  B , including the local shift, central curves and horizontal connectors. Section  4 gives the density-to-complexity transfer with all gate costs and proves Theorem  A . 
 2. Preliminaries 
 We use the smooth-number notation and saddle-point estimates of [ You24 ] , with natural logarithms and real interval endpoints. 
 The von Mangoldt function is 
 \Lambda(n)=\begin{cases}\log q,&n=q^{j},\quad q\text{ prime},\quad j\geq 1,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases} 
 Definition 2.1 (Smooth integers and the saddle point) . 
 A positive integer is y -smooth if all its prime divisors are at most y ; the integer 1 is included. For real x\geq y\geq 2 , define 
 \Psi(x,y)=\#\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid n\leq x,\ n\text{ is }y\text{-smooth}\},\qquad Z_{y}(s)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq y\\
q\ {\rm prime}\end{subarray}}(1-q^{-s})^{-1}\quad(\Re(s)>0). 
 Let \alpha=\alpha(x,y)>0 be the unique solution of 
 F_{y}(\alpha)=\log x,\qquad F_{y}(a)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq y\\
q\ {\rm prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\log q}{q^{a}-1}. 
 Equivalently, \alpha uniquely minimizes x^{a}Z_{y}(a) over a>0 . 
 The function F_{y} is continuous and strictly decreasing on (0,\infty) . Its limits at the two endpoints are \infty and 0 , respectively. This proves existence and uniqueness in Definition  2.1 . The equivalence follows from 
 \frac{d}{da}\log(x^{a}Z_{y}(a))=\log x-F_{y}(a). 
 For \Re(s)>0 , each Euler factor is holomorphic and nonzero, since |q^{-s}| 
 (2.1) 
 -\frac{Z_{y}^{\prime}(s)}{Z_{y}(s)}=\sum_{q\leq y}\frac{\log q}{q^{s}-1}=\sum_{q\leq y}\sum_{j\geq 1}(\log q)q^{-js}. 
 On the other hand, 
 \sum_{n\leq y}\Lambda(n)n^{-s}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\ {\rm prime},\ j\geq 1\\
q^{j}\leq y\end{subarray}}(\log q)q^{-js}. 
 Consequently, 
 (2.2) 
 -\frac{Z_{y}^{\prime}(s)}{Z_{y}(s)}=\sum_{n\leq y}\Lambda(n)n^{-s}+R_{y}(s),\qquad R_{y}(s):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq y,\ j\geq 2\\
q^{j}>y\end{subarray}}(\log q)q^{-js}, 
 where q ranges over primes. Only exponents j\geq 2 occur in the remainder, because q\leq y . 
 Notation 2.2 . 
 We write 
 u=\frac{\log x}{\log y},\qquad W=x^{\alpha}Z_{y}(\alpha). 
 2.1. Unconditional saddle-point inputs 
 The following statements are used in their first-version forms. Each is independent of an RH assumption. 
 Proposition 2.3 (Saddle-point mass, [ You24 , (1.3)] ) . 
 For real 2\leq y\leq x , 
 W\ll\Psi(x,y)\sqrt{\log x\log y}. 
 Proposition 2.4 (Location of the saddle point, [ You24 , (1.5)] ) . 
 For \log x 
 \alpha=1-\frac{\log(u\log(u+1))+O(1)}{\log y}, 
 with a uniform bounded error. 
 Proposition 2.5 (Decay away from the real axis, [ You24 , (1.6)] ) . 
 Fix r>0 . For \log x 
 \frac{1}{\log y}\leq|t|\leq\exp\!\left((\log y)^{3/2-r}\right), 
 there is a constant c_{r}>0 such that 
 \frac{|Z_{y}(\alpha+it)|}{Z_{y}(\alpha)}\ll_{r}\exp\!\left(-\frac{c_{r}ut^{2}}{t^{2}+(1-\alpha)^{2}}\right). 
 Proposition 2.6 (Truncated counting integral, [ You24 , (1.9)] ) . 
 For 2\leq y,h,H\leq x , 
 \Psi(x+h,y)-\Psi(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\alpha-iH}^{\alpha+iH}Z_{y}(s)\frac{(x+h)^{s}-x^{s}}{s}\,ds+O\!\left(\frac{x\log x}{H}\right). 
 Proposition 2.7 (The central integral, [ You24 , Lemma 3.6] ) . 
 Uniformly for 2\leq y\leq x , 
 \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\alpha-i/\log y}^{\alpha+i/\log y}x^{s}Z_{y}(s)\,ds=\alpha\Psi(x,y)\left(1+O\!\left(\frac{1}{u}+\frac{\log y}{y}\right)\right). 
 On the same segment, 
 \int_{\alpha-i/\log y}^{\alpha+i/\log y}|x^{s}Z_{y}(s)|\,|ds|\ll\Psi(x,y) 
 and 
 \int_{\alpha-i/\log y}^{\alpha+i/\log y}|s-1|\,|x^{s}Z_{y}(s)|\,|ds|\ll\Psi(x,y)\frac{\log(u+1)}{\log y}. 
 These are the saddle-point and central-integral estimates recorded in the cited source. In particular, its smoothness restriction (1.2) is not imposed on the inputs above in the ranges just stated. 
 2.2. A local zero-free estimate 
 Proposition 2.8 (Local von Mangoldt sum, [ You24 , Lemma 2.3] ) . 
 Let z\geq 2 , 0 
 \Re(s)>1-\eta,\qquad|\Im(s)-t| 
 For s=a+it\neq 1 with
 1-(1-e)\eta\leq a 
 \displaystyle\left|\sum_{n\leq z}\Lambda(n)n^{-s}\right| 
 \displaystyle\leq\frac{z^{1-a}+1}{|1-s|}+O_{e}\!\left((\log(|t|+2))^{b}\right)+O\!\left(\frac{(\log z)^{2}}{z^{e\eta}}\right) 
 (2.3) 
 \displaystyle\quad+O_{e}\!\left(\frac{((\log(|t|+z^{\eta}+2))^{b}+1/\eta)\log(z^{\eta}/\eta)}{z^{e\eta/2}}\right), 
 where b=b(e) 
 This local estimate assumes neither RH nor the smoothness restriction (1.2) of the cited source. 
 2.3. Zero density 
 Proposition 2.9 (Zero density, [ You24 , (1.13)] ) . 
 Let N(\sigma,T) count nontrivial zeros \rho=\beta+i\gamma of \zeta with \beta\geq\sigma and |\gamma|\leq T , with multiplicity. For each fixed r>0 , 
 N(\sigma,T)\ll_{r}T^{(30+r)(1-\sigma)/13}\qquad\left(\frac{1}{2}\leq\sigma\leq 1,\ T\geq 2\right). 
 Consequently, there is an absolute constant C\geq 1 such that 
 N(1-v,T)\leq CT^{(12/5)v}\qquad\left(0\leq v\leq\frac{1}{2},\ T\geq 2\right). 
 For the last statement, take r=1/100 and use
 (30+1/100)/13 [ You24 , (1.13)] . 
 3. Fixed-strip short intervals 
 In this section, we prove Theorem  B by establishing the local shift and then estimating every part of the counting contour. 
 3.1. A long-interval estimate 
 Proposition 3.1 (Long-interval density and a saddle-point lower bound) . 
 For all real x\geq y\geq 16 , 
 (3.1) 
 \Psi(x,y)\geq x\exp\!\left(-\log y-\frac{\log x}{\log y}\log(8\log x)\right). 
 There is an absolute x_{0}>e such that, for every real x\geq x_{0} , writing 
 L=\log x,\qquad S=\sqrt{L\log L},\qquad y=e^{S}, 
 we have 
 (3.2) 
 \Psi(x,y)\geq xe^{-3S} 
 and 
 (3.3) 
 \alpha(x,y)\geq 1-\frac{2\log L}{S}>\frac{1}{2}. 
 Proof. 
 We first obtain the prime-counting estimate needed for the argument. Let \pi(t) count primes at most t , and take t\geq 16 . Put n=\lfloor t/2\rfloor . Then n\geq 8 and n\geq t/4 . Since the central binomial coefficient is the largest coefficient of order 2n , 
 \binom{2n}{n}\geq\frac{4^{n}}{2n+1}\geq 2^{n}. 
 Here 2n+1\leq 2^{n} for n\geq 3 , as follows by induction. For each prime q , let v_{q}(N) denote the exponent of q in the prime factorization of N . Then 
 v_{q}\!\binom{2n}{n}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{2n}{q^{j}}\right\rfloor-2\left\lfloor\frac{n}{q^{j}}\right\rfloor\right). 
 Each summand is either zero or one, and it vanishes when q^{j}>2n . Thus
 q^{v_{q}(\binom{2n}{n})}\leq 2n for every prime q\leq 2n , and 
 2^{n}\leq\binom{2n}{n}\leq(2n)^{\pi(2n)}\leq t^{\pi(t)}. 
 Taking logarithms gives 
 (3.4) 
 \pi(t)\geq\frac{n\log 2}{\log t}\geq\frac{t\log 2}{4\log t}\geq\frac{t}{8\log t}. 
 Now let x\geq y\geq 16 , and put 
 L=\log x,\qquad v=\log y,\qquad r=\left\lfloor\frac{L}{v}\right\rfloor,\qquad N=\pi(y). 
 Unique factorization distinguishes the products associated with multisets of r primes at most y . Every such product is at most y^{r}\leq x . Therefore 
 \Psi(x,y)\geq\binom{N+r-1}{r}=\frac{N(N+1)\cdots(N+r-1)}{r!}\geq\left(\frac{N}{r}\right)^{r}\geq\left(\frac{y}{8vr}\right)^{r}. 
 The inequalities L-v\leq rv\leq L and r\leq L/v imply 
 \log\Psi(x,y)\geq rv-r\log(8vr)\geq L-v-\frac{L}{v}\log(8L). 
 This proves ( 3.1 ). 
 For S=\sqrt{L\log L} and y=e^{S} , eventually 16\leq y\leq x and L\geq 8 . Hence 
 \log\frac{\Psi(x,y)}{x}\geq-S-\frac{L}{S}\log(8L)\geq-S-\frac{2L\log L}{S}=-3S. 
 It remains to bound \alpha . Set 
 a=1-\frac{2\log L}{S}, 
 which exceeds 1/2 for large L . By ( 3.4 ) and
 \pi(\sqrt{y})\leq\sqrt{y} , 
 \pi(y)-\pi(\sqrt{y})\geq\frac{y}{8S}-\sqrt{y}\geq\frac{y}{16S} 
 for sufficiently large L . Indeed, e^{S/2}/(16S)\to\infty . For
 \sqrt{y} S/2 and q^{a}-1\leq y^{a} . Thus 
 F_{y}(a)\geq\frac{y}{16S}\frac{S}{2y^{a}}=\frac{y^{1-a}}{32}=\frac{L^{2}}{32}>L 
 when L>32 . Since F_{y} is strictly decreasing and F_{y}(\alpha)=L , this gives \alpha>a . One absolute threshold handles all the preceding conditions.
∎ 
 3.2. A local horizontal shift 
 Lemma 3.2 (Fixed-width local shift) . 
 Fix real constants 0 
 x\geq X_{0}(d,e),\qquad\exp((\log\log x)^{2})\leq y\leq x,\qquad d\leq\eta\leq\frac{1}{2}. 
 Let \sigma_{1} 
 1-(1-e)\eta\leq\sigma_{1} 
 and let 0 
 \Re(s)>1-\eta,\qquad\Im(s)\in(t-10y^{\eta},t+10y^{\eta}). 
 If 
 |t|\geq\frac{4}{e\log x}\bigl(y^{1-\sigma_{1}}+1\bigr), 
 then 
 |x^{\sigma_{1}+it}Z_{y}(\sigma_{1}+it)|\leq x^{-(1-e)(\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{1})}|x^{\sigma_{2}+it}Z_{y}(\sigma_{2}+it)|. 
 Proof. 
 Put L=\log x and let a\in[\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}] . Apply Proposition  2.8 with z=y . Since t\neq 0 , its first term is at most 
 \frac{y^{1-\sigma_{1}}+1}{|t|}\leq\frac{eL}{4}. 
 The logarithms involving t in the error terms are O(L) , because
 |t|,y\leq x and \eta\leq 1/2 . Also, 
 (\log L)^{2}\leq\log y\leq L,\qquad\eta\geq d. 
 The last two errors in ( 2.3 ) are consequently bounded by 
 O_{d,e}\!\left(L^{2}e^{-ed(\log L)^{2}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad O_{d,e}\!\left((L^{b}+1)Le^{-(ed/2)(\log L)^{2}}\right), 
 respectively. Each is o(L) uniformly: after division by L , the negative quadratic term in \log L dominates the logarithms of the remaining factors. The other error is O_{e}(L^{b})=o(L) . Enlarging X_{0}(d,e) makes their sum at most eL/4 . Thus 
 \left|\sum_{n\leq y}\Lambda(n)n^{-a-it}\right|\leq\frac{eL}{2}\qquad(\sigma_{1}\leq a\leq\sigma_{2}). 
 By ( 2.2 ), it remains to control R_{y}(a+it) . Since a\geq 1-(1-e)\eta\geq(1+e)/2 , the geometric-series identity gives 
 \displaystyle|R_{y}(a+it)| 
 \displaystyle\leq\sum_{q\leq y}(\log q)\sum_{j\geq 2}q^{-ja}=\sum_{q\leq y}\frac{(\log q)q^{-2a}}{1-q^{-a}} 
 \displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-2^{-(1+e)/2}}\sum_{q\leq y}\frac{\log q}{q^{1+e}} 
 \displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-2^{-(1+e)/2}}\sum_{n\geq 2}\frac{\log n}{n^{1+e}}=O_{e}(1). 
 This convergent series depends only on e . Increasing X_{0}(d,e) once more gives 
 \left|\frac{Z_{y}^{\prime}(a+it)}{Z_{y}(a+it)}\right|\leq eL\qquad(\sigma_{1}\leq a\leq\sigma_{2}). 
 Finally, 
 \frac{d}{da}\log|Z_{y}(a+it)|=\Re\frac{Z_{y}^{\prime}(a+it)}{Z_{y}(a+it)}. 
 Integration from \sigma_{2} to \sigma_{1} yields 
 \log\frac{|Z_{y}(\sigma_{1}+it)|}{|Z_{y}(\sigma_{2}+it)|}\leq eL(\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{1}). 
 Adding (\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})L and exponentiating proves the assertion.
∎ 
 3.3. The contour and its contributions 
 Throughout this subsection, use the parameters of Theorem  B : 
 L=\log x,\qquad S=\sqrt{L\log L},\qquad y=e^{S},\qquad\log y=S,\qquad u=L/S. 
 Fix \epsilon=1/1000 . All sufficiently large thresholds below depend only on \delta and are uniform for x^{3/4}\leq h\leq x . 
 Parameter preparation. 
Put a_{0}=1-\alpha . Proposition  2.4 gives 
 \displaystyle a_{0} 
 \displaystyle=\frac{\log(u\log(u+1))+O(1)}{S}=O\!\left(\frac{\log(u+1)}{S}\right)=O(1/u), 
 (3.5) 
 \displaystyle y^{a_{0}} 
 \displaystyle=\exp\!\left(\log(u\log(u+1))+O(1)\right)\asymp u\log(u+1). 
 Here a_{0}>0 for large x , and the first line uses (\log L)/S=1/u and \log(u+1)=O(\log L) . Thus, for sufficiently large x , 
 y\leq x^{\epsilon},\qquad a_{0}\leq\epsilon\delta,\qquad\alpha>1-\epsilon,\qquad S\geq(\log L)^{2}. 
 Define 
 (3.6) 
 D=\frac{8}{\epsilon L},\qquad t_{0}=Dy^{a_{0}},\qquad H=\frac{xe^{L^{9/10}}}{h},\qquad T=H+10\sqrt{y}. 
 The key size estimates are 
 \displaystyle t_{0} 
 \displaystyle\asymp\frac{u\log(u+1)}{L}=\frac{\log(u+1)}{S}, 
 \displaystyle e^{L^{9/10}} 
 \displaystyle\leq H\leq x^{1/4}e^{L^{9/10}}=x^{1/4+L^{-1/10}}, 
 \displaystyle 2\leq T 
 \displaystyle\leq x^{1/4+L^{-1/10}}+10x^{S/(2L)}\leq x^{3/10}. 
 In particular, H\leq x . These estimates are uniform throughout the stated range of h . 
 Proof of Theorem  B . 
 Step 1. The right envelope. 
We build a finite contour on which the local shift applies. 
 Put b=(1+\epsilon)/2 , and let \mathcal{Z}_{T} be the multiset of nontrivial zeros 
 \rho=\beta+i\gamma,\qquad\beta\geq\frac{1}{2},\qquad|\gamma|\leq T, 
 counted with multiplicity. By Proposition  2.9 ,
 \#\mathcal{Z}_{T}=N(1/2,T)\ll T^{6/5} 
 \displaystyle\Gamma_{\rho}^{\mathrm{v}} 
 \displaystyle=\{1-(1-\epsilon)\nu+it:|t-\gamma|\leq 10y^{\nu}\}, 
 \displaystyle\Gamma_{\rho}^{\pm} 
 \displaystyle=\{1-(1-\epsilon)v+i(\gamma\pm 10y^{v}):\nu\leq v\leq 1/2\}, 
 \displaystyle\Gamma_{\rho} 
 \displaystyle=\Gamma_{\rho}^{\mathrm{v}}\cup\Gamma_{\rho}^{+}\cup\Gamma_{\rho}^{-}. 
 The curves \Gamma_{\rho}^{\pm} join the endpoints of \Gamma_{\rho}^{\mathrm{v}} to the line \Re(s)=b . No zero with |\gamma|>T can contribute at a height |t|\leq H , since every point of its attached path satisfies |t-\gamma|\leq 10\sqrt{y} . 
 Define the central path by 
 \displaystyle\Gamma_{0}^{\mathrm{v}} 
 \displaystyle=\{\alpha+it:|t|\leq t_{0}\}, 
 \displaystyle\Gamma_{0}^{\pm} 
 \displaystyle=\{1-v\pm iDy^{v}:a_{0}\leq v\leq(1-\epsilon)/2\}, 
 \displaystyle\Gamma_{0} 
 \displaystyle=\Gamma_{0}^{\mathrm{v}}\cup\Gamma_{0}^{+}\cup\Gamma_{0}^{-}.

 4. Arithmetic transfer and the final bound 
 In this section, we transfer the density conclusion of Theorem  B to actual integer circuits, using the established certificate and marked-slice transfer of [ Zha26 ] . 
 We follow the straight-line program convention of de Melo and Svaiter
 [ dMS96 ] and the notation of
 [ Zha26 , Definition 1.1] . 
 Definition 4.1 (Arithmetic complexity) . 
 An integer straight-line program begins with 1 and 2 ; each subsequent
gate adds, subtracts or multiplies two available values. Values computed
earlier may be reused. The length is the number of gates, independent of
the bit lengths of their operands. For n\in\mathbb{Z} , let \tau(n) 
denote the minimum length of such a program producing n . For an integer
 k\geq 2 , define 
 \mu(k)=\min_{m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}\tau(mk!). 
 Only these three binary operations are counted as gates. Other integer
constants must be constructed, and division, modular reduction, gcd,
determinant and group operations must be implemented by the allowed
operations whenever they are used in a circuit.
Starting from 1 alone changes \tau by at most one operation.
Allowing negative nonzero multipliers in the definition of \mu changes
the minimum by at most a constant, since the sign can be reversed using
a bounded number of arithmetic operations.

 For one coordinate of the integer sampler, every residue modulo p has mass at least 
 \frac{\lfloor H/p\rfloor}{H}\geq\frac{1-p/H}{p}\geq\frac{9}{10p}. 
 Coordinate independence gives every residue vector at least (9/10)^{5} times its uniform mass. Since 
 \left(\frac{9}{10}\right)^{5}=\frac{59049}{100000}>\frac{1}{2}, 
 the actual integer sampler has the favorable event with probability at least \delta_{k} . The grid and all its parameters are the same for every tested prime. 
 Step 3. One common scalar. 
We make every favorable smooth order divide a single positive integer. 
 Define 
 (4.5) 
 N_{k}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\leq B\\
\ell\ {\rm prime}\end{subarray}}\ell^{\lfloor A_{k}/\log\ell\rfloor}. 
 The trivial bound on the number of primes gives 
 (4.6) 
 \log N_{k}\leq A_{k}\#\{\ell\leq B\mid\ell\text{ prime}\}\leq A_{k}B. 
 Every B -smooth positive integer n\leq 16k^{2} divides N_{k} . Indeed, if
 \ell^{j}\mid n , then
 j\log\ell\leq\log n\leq A_{k} , so
 j\leq\lfloor A_{k}/\log\ell\rfloor . 
 On the favorable event of Step 2, the Jacobian order n is in I_{p} , whose upper endpoint satisfies 
 p^{2}+\frac{1}{2}p^{3/2} 
 Hence n\mid N_{k} . The order of an element of a finite group divides the group cardinality, so 
 [N_{k}](D_{\mathbf{z}}\bmod p)=0. 
 Proposition  4.3 consequently supplies 
 p\mid Z(N_{k},\mathbf{z}). 
 For each of the finitely many literal vectors in the common grid, fix one certificate circuit from its rational data N_{k},\mathbf{z} . Make this choice once, independently of all tested primes. Its length is at most 
 C_{0}\left(1+\lceil\log_{2}N_{k}\rceil+\lceil\log_{2}(H+1)\rceil\right). 
 The scalar’s binary digits choose a schedule of at most
 2\lfloor\log_{2}N_{k}\rfloor group additions, whose scalar expansion is included in this charge by [ Zha26 , Proposition 5.1] . The five literals and every retained branch-test inverse are included as well. Definition ( 4.5 ) supplies no free prime list, logarithm, exponentiation or register containing N_{k} . Its scheduling cost is bounded through ( 4.6 ). 
 It follows that, for each tested prime, 
 (4.7) 
 \Pr_{\mathbf{z}}\bigl(p\mid Z(N_{k},\mathbf{z})\bigr)\geq\delta_{k}. 
 Step 4. Simultaneous prime coverage. 
A finite union bound chooses one successful deterministic list of samples. 
 Draw J_{k} independent samples from the same grid. For a fixed tested prime, ( 4.7 ) bounds the probability that all certificate outputs miss it by 
 (1-\delta_{k})^{J_{k}}\leq e^{-\delta_{k}J_{k}}\leq\frac{e^{-1}}{k}. 
 There are fewer than k tested primes. Summing these failure probabilities shows that the probability of missing at least one tested prime is less than e^{-1} 
 \mathbf{z}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{z}^{(J_{k})} 
 covers every prime in [p_{0},k] . This uses independence among samples only. 
 Construct p_{0}! directly, using an incrementing counter and a product accumulator, in at most 2p_{0} gates. Concatenate the selected certificate circuits and multiply each positive output into the accumulator. The resulting positive integer 
 Q_{k}=p_{0}!\prod_{j=1}^{J_{k}}Z(N_{k},\mathbf{z}^{(j)}) 
 is divisible by every prime at most k . Its circuit length is at most 
 (4.8) 
 2p_{0}+J_{k}\left[C_{0}\left(1+\lceil\log_{2}N_{k}\rceil+\lceil\log_{2}(10k+1)\rceil\right)+1\right]. 
 The added 1 inside the brackets charges each multiplication into the accumulator. 
 Step 5. Factorial valuations. 
We apply the prime-support amplification of
 [ Zha26 , Lemma 3.1] to Q_{k} . 
 Put r_{k}=\lceil\log_{2}k\rceil and append exactly r_{k} squaring gates. For each prime p\leq k , 
 v_{p}(k!)=\sum_{j\geq 1}\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p^{j}}\right\rfloor 
 Since p\mid Q_{k} ,
 v_{p}(Q_{k}^{2^{r_{k}}})\geq 2^{r_{k}} . For p>k , the factorial valuation is zero. Unique factorization gives 
 Q_{k}^{2^{r_{k}}}=m_{k}k! 
 for a positive integer m_{k} . This quotient exists in the proof; it is not computed by a division gate. 
 Step 6. A common explicit constant. 
We bound the full length, including the finite patch and appended squarings. 
 For k\geq 2 , 
 A_{k}\geq\log 64>4,\qquad S_{k}\geq 1,\qquad\log A_{k}\leq S_{k},\qquad\log(10k+1)\leq A_{k}. 
 Using ( 4.6 ), 
 \displaystyle 1+\lceil\log_{2}N_{k}\rceil+\lceil\log_{2}(10k+1)\rceil 
 \displaystyle\leq 3+\frac{A_{k}B+A_{k}}{\log 2} 
 \displaystyle\leq 6A_{k}B. 
 For the last inequality, use B\geq 1 , A_{k}>4 , and
 3+2A_{k}B/\log 2\leq 6A_{k}B . Also, 
 J_{k}\leq 1+192000A_{k}^{5}e^{c_{d}S_{k}}\leq 192001A_{k}^{5}e^{c_{d}S_{k}}, 
 because \log k+1\leq A_{k} and A_{k}^{5}e^{c_{d}S_{k}}\geq 1 . Finally, 
 B\leq 2e^{c_{b}S_{k}},\qquad r_{k}\leq 1+\frac{A_{k}}{\log 2}\leq 3A_{k}. 
 Adding the squarings to ( 4.8 ), the full length is at most 
 \displaystyle 2p_{0}+384002(6C_{0}+1)A_{k}^{6}e^{(c_{b}+c_{d})S_{k}}+3A_{k} 
 \displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\leq D_{0}A_{k}^{6}e^{(c_{b}+c_{d})S_{k}}. 
 Taking logarithms and using \log A_{k}\leq S_{k} and S_{k}\geq 1 gives 
 D_{0}A_{k}^{6}e^{(c_{b}+c_{d})S_{k}}\leq\exp\!\left((c_{b}+c_{d}+6+\log D_{0})S_{k}\right).

 4.3. Proof of the arithmetic bound 
 Proof of Theorem  A . 
 Fix \delta as in the hypothesis of Theorem  A .
Theorem  B applies with this same \delta and gives a threshold
 X_{\delta} , depending only on \delta , such that, for every real x\geq X_{\delta} and every
 x^{3/4}\leq h\leq x , 
 (4.9) 
 \Psi(x+h,e^{S_{x}})-\Psi(x,e^{S_{x}})\geq\frac{h}{4}e^{-3S_{x}},\qquad S_{x}=\sqrt{\log x\log\log x}. 
 This statement holds at every such center. 
 For a prime p\geq 5 , take 
 x=x_{p}=p^{2}-\frac{1}{2}p^{3/2},\qquad h=h_{p}=p^{3/2}. 
 Since x_{p}\leq p^{2} ,
 x_{p}^{3/4}\leq h_{p} . Also,
 h_{p}\leq x_{p} is equivalent to \sqrt{p}\geq 3/2 , which holds here. Finally,
 x_{p}\geq p^{2}/2\to\infty . 
 Choose one integer p_{\star}\geq 5 , depending only on \delta , such that, for every prime
 p\geq p_{\star} , 
 x_{p}\geq X_{\delta},\qquad\log x_{p}>1,\qquad S_{p}\geq\log 4. 
 Applying ( 4.9 ), and using
 (x_{p},x_{p}+h_{p}]\subseteq I_{p} , gives 
 \displaystyle\#\{n\in I_{p}\cap\mathbb{N}\mid n\text{ is }e^{S_{p}}\text{-smooth}\} 
 \displaystyle\geq\frac{h_{p}}{4}e^{-3S_{p}} 
 \displaystyle\geq h_{p}e^{-4S_{p}}. 
 The last inequality is exactly e^{S_{p}}\geq 4 . This is the hypothesis of
Theorem  4.5 with 
 c_{b}=1,\qquad c_{d}=4. 
 The parameters c_{b}=1 and c_{d}=4 are absolute, while p_{\star} may depend
on \delta ; all are fixed before k varies. 
 Choose the integer p_{0}>8100 used in that theorem, depending only on
 \delta , and let C_{0}\geq 1 be the absolute certificate constant. The transfer theorem gives an intermediate bound with 
 C_{1}=11+\log\!\left(2p_{0}+384002(6C_{0}+1)+3\right). 
 Thus, for every k\geq 2 , there is a positive integer m_{k} with 
 \tau(m_{k}k!)\leq\exp\!\left(C_{1}\sqrt{A_{k}\log A_{k}}\right),\qquad A_{k}=\log(16k^{2}). 
 The existence of p_{0} follows from S_{p}=o(\log p) , so it does not vary with k .
Consequently, C_{1} depends only on \delta . 
 This composition uses one grid H=10k and one scalar N_{k} , with 
 B=\left\lceil\exp\!\left(\sqrt{A_{k}\log A_{k}}\right)\right\rceil,\qquad\log N_{k}\leq A_{k}B. 
 The proof of Theorem  4.5 charges every certificate, literal construction, product multiplication and scalar-schedule instruction. It also charges the small-prime patch p_{0}! and the
 \lceil\log_{2}k\rceil squarings supplying factorial valuations. The quotient defining m_{k} is positive and requires no circuit division. 
 To obtain the stated bound, put L=\log k and take k\geq 64 . Then 
 A_{k}=2L+\log 16\leq 3L. 
 Also L\geq\log 64>3 , so \log L\geq\log 3 and 
 \log A_{k}\leq\log(3L)\leq 2\log L. 
 Consequently, 
 \sqrt{A_{k}\log A_{k}}\leq\sqrt{6L\log L}\leq 3\sqrt{L\log L}. 
 Taking C=3C_{1} and k_{0}=64 proves Theorem  A under the
same fixed-half-plane hypothesis as Theorem  B .
