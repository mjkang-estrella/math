 Abstract 
 Ranjan and Singh proved that for every composite n which is not twice an odd prime, \varphi(n) is at least one plus the sum of \varphi over the prime divisors of n , with equality exactly for n=4,12,30 . We study the weighted equation \varphi(n)=c+\alpha\sum_{p\mid n}\varphi(p) . We determine the parameters (c,\alpha) admitting infinite families of solutions, prove that all remaining solutions are finite in number and arise from an explicit linear mechanism, and compute them exactly in a range of parameters; the finiteness of \{4,12,30\} becomes a special case. We also settle the analogous equations for the divisor functions \tau and \sigma and for the Jordan totients, and, from known results on the sum of the prime divisors, derive the full asymptotic expansion of the average deficiency. In the range |c|\leq 10 , 1\leq\alpha\leq 6 the composite solution set is determined completely, and the number and the size of the sporadic solutions are shown to be bounded by polynomials in |c|+\alpha . Finally, replacing the prime values \varphi(p) by their iterates \varphi^{(k)}(p) , we determine all solutions of \varphi(n)=1+\sum_{p\mid n}\varphi^{(k)}(p) : the solution sets are \{1,4,12,30\} , \{1,3,4,10\} and, for every k\geq 3 , \{1,3,4\} ; for the weighted iterated equation we exhibit an infinite family governed by the safe primes.

 Keywords: Euler’s totient function, divisor functions, Jordan totient, prime–composite characterizations, average orders, iterated totient. 
 1 Introduction 
 Euler’s totient function \varphi(n) counts the positive integers up to n that are coprime to n . Like most multiplicative functions its behaviour is governed by the prime factorization of the argument, and a recurring theme in elementary number theory is the classification of the integers at which an arithmetic identity, generically true for primes or generically failing for composites, admits exceptional solutions. The prototype is Lehmer’s still-open problem [ 8 ] of whether \varphi(n)\mid n-1 can hold for composite n ; a solved relative is Subbarao’s theorem [ 10 ] that his primality congruence admits exactly the composite solutions n\in\{4,6,22\} . 
 Ranjan and Singh [ 9 ] recently added a new instance to this family. Observing that 
 \varphi(4)=1+\varphi(2),\qquad\varphi(12)=1+\varphi(2)+\varphi(3),\qquad\varphi(30)=1+\varphi(2)+\varphi(3)+\varphi(5), 
 they asked for which composite numbers such an identity can hold and proved: 
 Theorem 1.1 (Ranjan–Singh [ 9 ] ) . 
 Let n be a composite number which is not twice an odd prime, and let p_{1},\dots,p_{r} be its distinct prime divisors. Then 
 \varphi(n)\;\geq\;1+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\varphi(p_{i}), 
 (1) 
 with equality if and only if n\in\{4,12,30\} . Moreover \varphi(2p) 
 Their proof rests on two elementary product–sum inequalities borrowed from Ecker [ 5 ] : ab\geq a+b for a,b\geq 2 (equality if and only if a=b=2 ), and a_{1}\cdots a_{m}>a_{1}+\cdots+a_{m} whenever 2\leq a_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{m} and m\geq 3 . 
 The purpose of the present paper is fourfold. First (§ 3 ) we treat the analogous equation 
 f(n)\;=\;1+\sum_{p\mid n}f(p) 
 (2) 
 for the divisor-count function \tau , the sum-of-divisors function \sigma , and the Jordan totients J_{k} , obtaining in each case a complete answer. The outcomes are strikingly different from one function to the next: for \tau the composite solution set is the infinite family \{p^{2}:p\text{ prime}\} ; for \sigma and for J_{k} ( k\geq 2 ) it is empty; for \varphi it is the finite set \{4,12,30\} . Second (§ 4 –§ 5 ) we study weighted and simultaneous versions of ( 2 ), where parity constraints, an exact classification of the infinite solution families, and a finiteness theorem give a complete structure theory — from which the finiteness of the Ranjan–Singh exceptional set follows as a corollary; in the range |c|\leq 10 , 1\leq\alpha\leq 6 the solution sets are then computed completely, and uniform polynomial bounds in |c|+\alpha are proved. Third (§ 6 ) we measure how far a typical integer is from satisfying ( 1 ): a simple identity reduces the average deficiency to classical results of Alladi–Erdős and De Koninck–Ivić, which yields its full asymptotic expansion. Fourth (§ 7 ) we replace the prime values \varphi(p) in ( 1 ) by their iterates \varphi^{(k)}(p) and determine all solutions for every k\geq 1 : the monotonicity of the iterates reduces each case to Theorem 1.1 , and the solution sets stabilise at \{1,3,4\} for all k\geq 3 . 
 2 Notation and preliminaries 
 Throughout, n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{r}^{a_{r}} denotes the canonical factorization of an integer n\geq 2 , with p_{1} of n . We write \tau(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}1 , \sigma(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}d , and for k\geq 1 
 J_{k}(n)\;=\;n^{k}\prod_{p\mid n}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p^{k}}\Bigr), 
 the Jordan totient, so that J_{1}=\varphi and J_{k}(p^{a})=p^{k(a-1)}(p^{k}-1) [ 11 ] . All three of \varphi,\tau,\sigma and every J_{k} are multiplicative. On primes, 
 \varphi(p)=p-1,\qquad\tau(p)=2,\qquad\sigma(p)=p+1,\qquad J_{k}(p)=p^{k}-1. 
 We shall use the elementary fact that \varphi(n) is even for every n\geq 3 , the fact that \varphi(n)=n-1 if and only if n is prime, and the bound \varphi(n)\leq n/2 for even n . For k\geq 1 , \varphi^{(k)} denotes the k -fold iterate of \varphi , with \varphi^{(1)}=\varphi . 
 3 The equation f(n)=1+\sum_{p\mid n}f(p) for \tau , \sigma and J_{k} 
 3.1 The divisor-count function 
 Since \tau(p)=2 , equation ( 2 ) for f=\tau reads \tau(n)=1+2\,\omega(n) . 
 Theorem 3.1 . 
 A composite number n satisfies \tau(n)=1+\sum_{p\mid n}\tau(p) if and only if n=p^{2} for some prime p . 
 Proof. 
 If n=p^{a} is a prime power, the equation reads a+1=3 , i.e. a=2 ; every n=p^{2} is a solution and no other prime power is. 
 Suppose now \omega(n)=r\geq 2 . Since each exponent satisfies a_{i}+1\geq 2 , 
 \tau(n)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(a_{i}+1)\;\geq\;2^{r}. 
 For r\geq 3 we get \tau(n)\geq 2^{r}>1+2r , because 2^{r}-2r-1 is positive at r=3 and increasing; hence no solutions. For r=2 the equation demands \tau(n)=5 , a prime value of \tau ; but \tau(n)=(a_{1}+1)(a_{2}+1) with both factors \geq 2 is composite. No solutions exist with r=2 , which completes the proof.
∎ 
 Thus, in contrast with the finite exceptional set \{4,12,30\} of Theorem 1.1 , the \tau -analogue possesses the infinite solution family \{4,9,25,49,121,\dots\} (the squares of primes, OEIS A001248). 
 3.2 The sum-of-divisors function

 For the higher Jordan totients the left-hand side of ( 2 ) grows polynomially in n while the right-hand side grows only with the prime support, and again no composite solutions survive.

 Let k\geq 2 . For every composite n , 
 J_{k}(n)\;>\;1+\sum_{p\mid n}J_{k}(p),

 Remark 3.4 . 
 The three theorems above, together with Theorem 1.1 , exhibit the full spectrum of behaviours of equation ( 2 ) within one natural family of multiplicative functions: an infinite solution set ( \tau ), a finite nonempty one ( \varphi ), and empty ones ( \sigma , all J_{k} with k\geq 2 ). Heuristically, the size of the solution set is governed by the growth of f(p^{a}) against f(p) : the slower the growth, the richer the solution set. 
 4 The weighted equation: a complete structure theory 
 Consider, for integer parameters c and \alpha\geq 1 , the weighted equation 
 \varphi(n)\;=\;c+\alpha\sum_{p\mid n}\varphi(p), 
 (4) 
 of which Theorem 1.1 is the case (c,\alpha)=(1,1) . Write S_{m}:=\sum_{q\mid m}\varphi(q) for the prime-support sum, so that ( 4 ) reads \varphi(n)=c+\alpha S_{n} . In this section we determine the structure of the composite solution set of ( 4 ) completely: parity constraints (Theorem 4.1 ), an exact classification of the infinite families (Theorem 4.2 ), and a finiteness theorem for what remains (Theorem 4.4 ). The structure theorem is then made completely explicit in the range |c|\leq 10 , 1\leq\alpha\leq 6 (Theorem 4.12 ), and uniform polynomial bounds in |c|+\alpha are derived from it (Theorem 4.13 ). 
 4.1 Parity constraints 
 Theorem 4.1 (Parity constraints) . 
 Let n\geq 4 be a composite solution of ( 4 ). Then:
 (i) if c is odd and \alpha is even, no such n exists;
 (ii) if c and \alpha are both odd, n is even;
 (iii) if c is even and \alpha is odd, n is odd;
 (iv) if c and \alpha are both even, no parity restriction holds. 
 Proof. 
 For n\geq 3 the value \varphi(n) is even. On the other side, \varphi(2)=1 is the only odd value of \varphi at a prime, whence S_{n}\equiv\mathbf{1}_{2\mid n}\pmod{2} and the right-hand side of ( 4 ) is congruent to c+\alpha\,\mathbf{1}_{2\mid n} modulo 2 . Requiring it to be even yields exactly the four cases. Case (iv) is realized: (c,\alpha)=(2,2) has both the even solution 8 and the odd solution 9 .
∎ 
 4.2 Classification of the infinite families 
 The exclusion of n=2p in Theorem 1.1 is not an accident of the proof: the integers 2p form an exact infinite family for the neighbouring pair (c,\alpha)=(-1,1) , since \varphi(2p)=p-1=S_{2p}-1 . This phenomenon is completely general. 
 Theorem 4.2 (Infinite families) . 
 Let m\geq 2 be fixed. Every integer n=mp , with p prime and p\nmid m , satisfies ( 4 ) if and only if 
 \alpha=\varphi(m)\qquad\text{and}\qquad c=-\varphi(m)\,S_{m}. 
 We call such a pair (c,\alpha) matched , with modulus m . For each \alpha there are only finitely many matched moduli, since \varphi(m)=\alpha has finitely many solutions m . 
 Proof. 
 For p\nmid m , multiplicativity gives \varphi(mp)=\varphi(m)(p-1) and S_{mp}=S_{m}+p-1 . Equation ( 4 ) becomes the identity in p 
 \varphi(m)(p-1)\;=\;c+\alpha S_{m}+\alpha(p-1), 
 which holds for infinitely many (equivalently, for all admissible) primes p if and only if the coefficients of p-1 and the constant terms match: \varphi(m)=\alpha and c+\alpha S_{m}=0 .
∎ 
 For example, the matched pairs in the range |c|\leq 10 , 1\leq\alpha\leq 6 are 
 \begin{array}[]{c|c|c}m&(c,\alpha)&\text{family}\\
\hline\cr 2&(-1,1)&\{2p\}\\
4&(-2,2)&\{4p\}\\
3&(-4,2)&\{3p\}\\
6&(-6,2)&\{6p\}\\
8&(-4,4)&\{8p\}\end{array} 
 and the pair (1,1) of Theorem 1.1 is unmatched : \varphi(m)=1 forces m=2 , which would require c=-1\neq 1 . 
 4.3 Finiteness outside the families, and the sporadic mechanism 
 Lemma 4.3 . 
 Let n be a composite solution of ( 4 ), let P be the largest prime factor of n and write n=m^{\prime}P^{a} with P\nmid m^{\prime} , a\geq 1 . If a=1 then 
 \bigl(\varphi(m^{\prime})-\alpha\bigr)(P-1)\;=\;c+\alpha S_{m^{\prime}}, 
 (5) 
 while if a\geq 2 then (P-1)\bigl(\varphi(m^{\prime})P^{a-1}-\alpha\bigr)=c+\alpha S_{m^{\prime}} . 
 Proof. 
 Immediate from \varphi(n)=\varphi(m^{\prime})P^{a-1}(P-1) and S_{n}=S_{m^{\prime}}+P-1 .
∎ 
 Equation ( 5 ) is the engine of the whole subject. When m^{\prime} is a matched modulus both sides vanish and P is unconstrained: this reproduces the infinite families. Otherwise P is determined by m^{\prime} , and the point of the next theorem is that only finitely many m^{\prime} can produce a legitimate P . 
 Theorem 4.4 (Structure theorem) . 
 For every pair (c,\alpha) , the set of composite solutions of ( 4 ) is the union of the families \{mp:p\ \text{prime},\,p\nmid m\} over the finitely many matched moduli m , together with a finite sporadic set F(c,\alpha) . In particular, if (c,\alpha) is unmatched —as is (1,1) — the composite solution set is finite. 
 Proof. 
 Let n=m^{\prime}P^{a} be a solution as in Lemma 4.3 which does not belong to a matched family. We use the elementary bounds \varphi(m)\geq\sqrt{m/2} and \omega(m)\leq\log_{2}m , and we note that P exceeds every prime factor of m^{\prime} (being the largest prime factor of n , and coprime to m^{\prime} ); thus, with Q the largest prime factor of m^{\prime} (set Q=1 if m^{\prime}=1 ), we have P\geq Q+1 and S_{m^{\prime}}\leq\omega(m^{\prime})(Q-1) . 
 Case a=1 . If \varphi(m^{\prime})=\alpha then, m^{\prime} being unmatched, c+\alpha S_{m^{\prime}}\neq 0 and ( 5 ) is contradictory; besides, \varphi(m^{\prime})=\alpha already confines m^{\prime} to a finite set. So assume \varphi(m^{\prime})\neq\alpha ; then ( 5 ) determines P-1=(c+\alpha S_{m^{\prime}})/(\varphi(m^{\prime})-\alpha) , at most one prime for each m^{\prime} . If moreover \varphi(m^{\prime})>\alpha , the requirement P-1\geq Q forces 
 Q\;\leq\;\frac{|c|+\alpha\,\omega(m^{\prime})(Q-1)}{\varphi(m^{\prime})-\alpha},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad\varphi(m^{\prime})\; 
 Since \varphi(m^{\prime})\geq\sqrt{m^{\prime}/2} grows faster than \log_{2}m^{\prime} , only finitely many m^{\prime} qualify; the finitely many m^{\prime} with \varphi(m^{\prime}) 
 Case a\geq 2 . Here (P-1)\bigl(\varphi(m^{\prime})P^{a-1}-\alpha\bigr)=c+\alpha S_{m^{\prime}} . If the second factor is nonpositive, then \varphi(m^{\prime})P^{a-1}\leq\alpha , which bounds m^{\prime} , P and a . Otherwise it is at least 1 , and since S_{m^{\prime}}\leq\omega(m^{\prime})(Q-1)\leq\omega(m^{\prime})(P-1) , dividing by P-1 gives 
 \varphi(m^{\prime})P^{a-1}\;\leq\;\alpha+|c|+\alpha\,\omega(m^{\prime})\;\leq\;\alpha+|c|+\alpha\log_{2}m^{\prime}. 
 As \varphi(m^{\prime})\geq\sqrt{m^{\prime}/2} , this bounds m^{\prime} , then P^{a-1} , hence P and a . In all cases only finitely many solutions arise.
∎ 
 Corollary 4.5 . 
 The finiteness of the exceptional set in Theorem 1.1 is a special case of Theorem 4.4 : the pair (1,1) is unmatched, and its sporadic set is F(1,1)=\{4,12,30\} . The elements 12 and 30 arise from ( 5 ): for n=12=4\cdot 3 , (\varphi(4)-1)(3-1)=2=1+S_{4} , and for n=30=6\cdot 5 , (\varphi(6)-1)(5-1)=4=1+S_{6} ; the element 4=2^{2} arises from the case a\geq 2 of Lemma 4.3 , with m^{\prime}=1 : (2-1)(1\cdot 2-1)=1=1+S_{1} . 
 Corollary 4.6 . 
 If c is odd and \alpha is even —for instance (c,\alpha)=(1,2) — equation ( 4 ) has no composite solution at all (Theorem 4.1 (i)). 
 4.4 Exact determination of the sporadic sets 
 Theorem 4.4 is effective, and for the five matched pairs above the bounds are small enough to compute the sporadic sets exactly. 
 Lemma 4.7 (Explicit bounds) . 
 Let (c,\alpha) be one of the five matched pairs (so |c|\leq 6 , \alpha\leq 4 ), and let n=m^{\prime}P^{a} be a sporadic composite solution, decomposed as in Lemma 4.3 . Then \omega(m^{\prime})\leq 3 and \varphi(m^{\prime})\leq 18 , hence m^{\prime}\leq 648 ; moreover P\leq 83 and a\leq 5 . 
 Proof. 
 Suppose first a=1 ; then m^{\prime}\geq 2 and Q\geq 2 , where Q is the largest prime factor of m^{\prime} . One checks that each of the five pairs has a unique matched modulus; hence if \varphi(m^{\prime})=\alpha , either n lies in the matched family or c+\alpha S_{m^{\prime}}\neq 0 and ( 5 ) fails. If \varphi(m^{\prime}) \alpha , then from ( 5 ) and P-1\geq Q , 
 Q\;\leq\;P-1\;=\;\frac{c+\alpha S_{m^{\prime}}}{\varphi(m^{\prime})-\alpha}\;\leq\;\frac{|c|+\alpha\,\omega(m^{\prime})(Q-1)}{\varphi(m^{\prime})-\alpha}, 
 whence \varphi(m^{\prime})\leq\alpha+|c|/Q+\alpha\,\omega(m^{\prime})\leq 7+4\,\omega(m^{\prime}) . Since \varphi(m^{\prime})\geq\prod_{q\mid m^{\prime}}(q-1)\geq 1\cdot 2\cdot 4\cdot 6=48 as soon as \omega(m^{\prime})\geq 4 , we conclude \omega(m^{\prime})\leq 3 and \varphi(m^{\prime})\leq 19 , hence \varphi(m^{\prime})\leq 18 since \varphi(m^{\prime}) is even; the bound m^{\prime}\leq 2\cdot 18^{2}=648 follows from \varphi(m)\geq\sqrt{m/2} . Every prime q\mid m^{\prime} then satisfies q-1\leq 18 , and a direct check gives S_{m^{\prime}}\leq 19 (the maximum being S_{38}=19 ); therefore P\leq 1+|c|+\alpha S_{m^{\prime}}\leq 1+6+4\cdot 19=83 . 
 Now let a\geq 2 . If \varphi(m^{\prime})P^{a-1}\leq\alpha\leq 4 we are in a finite explicit range. Otherwise, as in the proof of Theorem 4.4 , \varphi(m^{\prime})P^{a-1}\leq\alpha+|c|+\alpha\,\omega(m^{\prime})\leq 10+4\,\omega(m^{\prime}) ; the same comparison with \prod_{q\mid m^{\prime}}(q-1) gives \omega(m^{\prime})\leq 3 , hence \varphi(m^{\prime})P^{a-1}\leq 22 . Thus P^{a-1}\leq 22 , so that P\leq 19 and a\leq 5 .
∎ 
 Theorem 4.8 (Exact sporadic sets) . 
 For the five matched pairs in the range |c|\leq 10 , 1\leq\alpha\leq 6 , the sporadic sets are: 
 \displaystyle F(-1,1)=F(-2,2)=F(-4,2)=\varnothing, 
 \displaystyle F(-6,2)=\{10\},\qquad F(-4,4)=\{77,\,90,\,182,\,210\}.

 Proof. 
 By Lemma 4.7 every sporadic solution arises, through the canonical decomposition n=m^{\prime}P^{a} , from a triple (m^{\prime},P,a) in the explicit finite range m^{\prime}\leq 648 , P\leq 83 , a\leq 5 satisfying the mechanism of Lemma 4.3 . Enumerating all such triples for each of the five pairs, discarding those whose product lies in the matched family, yields precisely the sets displayed. (Each surviving element is then verified directly; e.g. n=90=18\cdot 5 for (-4,4) : (\varphi(18)-4)(5-1)=8=-4+4S_{18} , and \varphi(90)=24=-4+4(1+2+4) .)
∎

 4.6 Uniform bounds 
 Lemma 4.9 also makes the finiteness of Theorem 4.4 quantitative, uniformly in the parameters. For x\geq 1 let N_{\varphi}(x)=\#\{m\geq 1:\varphi(m)\leq x\} ; it is classical that N_{\varphi}(x)\ll x , indeed N_{\varphi}(x)\sim\frac{\zeta(2)\zeta(3)}{\zeta(6)}\,x [ 2 ] . 
 Theorem 4.13 (Uniform bounds) . 
 Let c\in\mathbb{Z} , \alpha\geq 1 , L:=|c|+\alpha\geq 2 , and let \omega_{0} be the largest integer k with k!\leq L(k+1) , so that \omega_{0}\ll\log L/\log\log L ; put B:=L(1+\omega_{0}) . Let n=m^{\prime}P^{a} be a sporadic composite solution of ( 4 ), decomposed as in Lemma 4.3 . 
 (i) 
 If a=1 , then \varphi(m^{\prime})\leq B and P\leq L(B+2) . 
 (ii) 
 If a\geq 2 , then \varphi(m^{\prime})P^{a-1}\leq B and P\leq\alpha+1+\sqrt{L} . 
 Consequently, with absolute implied constants, 
 |F(c,\alpha)|\;\ll\;L^{2}(\log L)^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\max F(c,\alpha)\;\ll\;L^{4}(\log L)^{3}. 
 Proof. 
 Throughout, Q is the largest prime factor of m^{\prime} ( Q=1 if m^{\prime}=1 ), so that P\geq Q+1 and S_{m^{\prime}}\leq\omega(m^{\prime})(Q-1)\leq\omega(m^{\prime})(P-1) ; and \varphi(m^{\prime})\geq\omega(m^{\prime})! , since the i -th prime is at least i+1 . 
 (i) Let a=1 , so m^{\prime}\geq 2 and P\geq 3 . As in Proposition 4.10 , \varphi(m^{\prime})=\alpha is excluded. If \varphi(m^{\prime}) \alpha , dividing ( 5 ) by P-1\geq 2 gives 
 \varphi(m^{\prime})-\alpha\;\leq\;\frac{|c|}{P-1}+\alpha\,\omega(m^{\prime})\;\leq\;\frac{|c|}{2}+\alpha\,\omega(m^{\prime}), 
 hence \omega(m^{\prime})!\leq\varphi(m^{\prime})\leq L\bigl(1+\omega(m^{\prime})\bigr) , so \omega(m^{\prime})\leq\omega_{0} and \varphi(m^{\prime})\leq B . In both cases ( 5 ) and Lemma 4.9 give P-1\leq|c+\alpha S_{m^{\prime}}|\leq|c|+\alpha(\varphi(m^{\prime})+1)\leq|c|+\alpha(B+1) , whence P\leq L(B+2) . 
 (ii) Let a\geq 2 and write ( 4 ) as (P-1)\bigl(\varphi(m^{\prime})P^{a-1}-\alpha\bigr)=c+\alpha S_{m^{\prime}} . If \varphi(m^{\prime})P^{a-1}\leq\alpha , both assertions are trivial. Otherwise the right-hand side is positive; dividing by P-1 and using S_{m^{\prime}}\leq\omega(m^{\prime})(P-1) gives \varphi(m^{\prime})P^{a-1}\leq\alpha+|c|+\alpha\,\omega(m^{\prime})\leq L(1+\omega(m^{\prime})) , and \omega(m^{\prime})!\leq\varphi(m^{\prime}) yields \omega(m^{\prime})\leq\omega_{0} and \varphi(m^{\prime})P^{a-1}\leq B . For the bound on P , use instead Lemma 4.9 : c+\alpha S_{m^{\prime}}\leq|c|+\alpha\varphi(m^{\prime})+\alpha , so that 
 \varphi(m^{\prime})\bigl(P^{a-1}(P-1)-\alpha\bigr)\;\leq\;|c|+\alpha P. 
 If P(P-1)\leq\alpha then P\leq\alpha . If P(P-1)>\alpha then, as P^{a-1}\geq P and \varphi(m^{\prime})\geq 1 , we get P^{2}-(\alpha+1)P-(\alpha+|c|)\leq 0 , hence P\leq\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\alpha+1+\sqrt{(\alpha+1)^{2}+4L}\bigr)\leq\alpha+1+\sqrt{L} . 
 Counting. For a=1 each m^{\prime} determines P , so there are at most N_{\varphi}(B)\ll B sporadic solutions. For a\geq 2 there are \ll B choices of m^{\prime} , \ll L choices of P and \ll\log B choices of a , since P^{a-1}\leq B . Hence |F(c,\alpha)|\ll BL\log B\ll L^{2}(\log L)^{2} , as B\ll L\log L . 
 Size. Using m^{\prime}\leq 2\varphi(m^{\prime})^{2} (Hardy–Wright [ 7 ] ), a solution with a=1 satisfies n=m^{\prime}P\leq 2B^{2}\cdot L(B+2)\ll LB^{3}\ll L^{4}(\log L)^{3} , and a solution with a\geq 2 satisfies n=m^{\prime}P^{a-1}\cdot P\leq 2\varphi(m^{\prime})^{2}P^{a-1}\cdot P\leq 2B^{2}(\alpha+1+\sqrt{L})\ll L^{3}(\log L)^{2} .
∎ 
 Thus |F(c,\alpha)| and \max F(c,\alpha) are bounded by polynomials in |c|+\alpha . The census of Theorem 4.12 ( |F|\leq 9 for L\leq 16 ), extended by a direct scan of n\leq 2\cdot 10^{6} to |c|\leq 40 , \alpha\leq 12 (where \max|F|=19 , at (36,12) ), suggests that the truth is far smaller; see Question 9.1 . 
 Corollary 4.14 . 
 (i) 
 If \alpha\geq 3 is odd, there is no matched modulus, and for every c the composite solution set of ( 4 ) is finite, of cardinality \ll(|c|+\alpha)^{2}\log^{2}(|c|+\alpha) . 
 (ii) 
 For \alpha=1 and c\leq 0 one has F(c,1)=\varnothing : the equation \varphi(n)=c+S_{n} has composite solutions only for c=-1 (the family 2p ) and for c\geq 1 . 
 Proof. 
 (i) A matched modulus m satisfies \varphi(m)=\alpha , which is impossible for odd \alpha\geq 3 since \varphi(m) is even for m\geq 3 and \varphi(1)=\varphi(2)=1 ; Theorem 4.13 then bounds the whole solution set. (ii) If n is composite and not twice an odd prime, Theorem 1.1 gives \varphi(n)\geq 1+S_{n}>c+S_{n} for c\leq 0 ; if n=2p then \varphi(n)=S_{n}-1 , so c=-1 and n belongs to the matched family.
∎

 5 Simultaneous equations 
 Write (E_{\varphi}) , (E_{\tau}) , (E_{\sigma}) for equation ( 2 ) with f=\varphi,\tau,\sigma respectively. Combining Theorems 1.1 , 3.1 and 3.2 yields complete answers for all conjunctions. 
 Theorem 5.1 . 
 (i) The unique composite integer satisfying (E_{\varphi}) and (E_{\tau}) simultaneously is n=4 .
 (ii) No integer n\geq 2 satisfies (E_{\varphi}) , (E_{\tau}) and (E_{\sigma}) simultaneously; indeed already (E_{\sigma}) alone has no composite solution, and no prime satisfies (E_{\tau}) . 
 Proof. 
 (i) By Theorems 1.1 and 3.1 the composite solutions of (E_{\varphi}) are \{4,12,30\} and those of (E_{\tau}) are the prime squares; the intersection is \{4\} , since neither 12 nor 30 is the square of a prime. One checks directly: \varphi(4)=2=1+\varphi(2) and \tau(4)=3=1+\tau(2) . 
 (ii) For a prime p , (E_{\tau}) reads 2=3 , false; combined with Theorem 3.2 for composites this settles the claim.
∎ 
 Thus, imposing several of these equations at once collapses the solution set, first to \{4\} and then to \varnothing . 
 6 The average size of the deficiency 
 Theorem 1.1 says that the deficiency 
 \Delta(n)\;:=\;\varphi(n)-1-\sum_{p\mid n}\varphi(p) 
 is nonnegative for composite n\neq 2p and vanishes only thrice. How large is it on average? The answer is: as large as \varphi itself, the subtracted term being negligible at the scale x^{2} . 
 Theorem 6.1 . 
 Let A_{j}=(-1)^{j-1}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{j-1}}{\mathrm{d}s^{j-1}}\Bigl(\dfrac{\zeta(s)}{s}\Bigr)\Big|_{s=2} , so that A_{1}=\dfrac{\pi^{2}}{12} and A_{2}=\dfrac{\zeta(2)}{4}-\dfrac{\zeta^{\prime}(2)}{2} . For every fixed M\geq 1 , 
 \sum_{n\leq x}\Delta(n)\;=\;\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}\,x^{2}\;-\;\sum_{j=1}^{M}A_{j}\,\frac{x^{2}}{\log^{j}x}\;+\;O\!\left(\frac{x^{2}}{\log^{M+1}x}\right). 
 In particular the mean value \frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leq x}\Delta(n)\sim\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}x . 
 Proof. 
 Since \varphi(p)=p-1 , we have \sum_{p\mid n}\varphi(p)=\beta(n)-\omega(n) , where \beta(n)=\sum_{p\mid n}p is the sum of the distinct prime divisors of n . Hence 
 \sum_{n\leq x}\Delta(n)\;=\;\sum_{n\leq x}\varphi(n)\;-\;\lfloor x\rfloor\;-\;\sum_{n\leq x}\beta(n)\;+\;\sum_{n\leq x}\omega(n). 
 The first sum equals \frac{3}{\pi^{2}}x^{2}+O(x\log x) [ 7 , Th. 330] , and the last one is O(x\log\log x) [ 7 , Th. 430] . The leading term of \sum_{n\leq x}\beta(n) , namely \frac{\pi^{2}}{12}x^{2}/\log x , is due to Alladi and Erdős [ 3 ] , and the full expansion \sum_{n\leq x}\beta(n)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}A_{j}x^{2}/\log^{j}x+O(x^{2}/\log^{M+1}x) to De Koninck and Ivić [ 4 ] . Combining these estimates proves the theorem.
∎ 
 Remark 6.2 . 
 The leading term also follows from the exact identity \sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{p\mid n}\varphi(p)=\sum_{k\geq 1}P(x/k) , where P(y)=\sum_{p\leq y}(p-1) , together with the prime number theorem. Numerically, \bigl(\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{p\mid n}\varphi(p)\bigr)\log x/x^{2} equals 0.9153 , 0.8978 , 0.8858 at x=10^{5},10^{6},10^{7} (exact sieve computations), whereas the expansion \sum_{j\leq M}A_{j}/\log^{j-1}x truncated at M=2 gives 0.8989 , 0.8862 , 0.8771 and at M=3 reduces the discrepancy at x=10^{7} to 0.2\% . 
 7 Iterated totients on the prime divisors 
 In this section we replace the prime values \varphi(p) in ( 1 ) by their iterates and determine, for every k\geq 1 , all positive integers n with 
 \varphi(n)\;=\;1+\sum_{p\mid n}\varphi^{(k)}(p). 
 (7) 
 The answer rests on Theorem 1.1 and on the following monotonicity. 
 Lemma 7.1 . 
 For every n\geq 1 the sequence \bigl(\varphi^{(j)}(n)\bigr)_{j\geq 1} is non-increasing. Moreover, for every prime p\geq 3 and every k\geq 2 , \varphi^{(k)}(p)\leq\varphi(p-1)\leq\frac{p-1}{2} . 
 Proof. 
 Since \varphi(m)\leq m for all m\geq 1 , we have \varphi^{(j+1)}(n)=\varphi\bigl(\varphi^{(j)}(n)\bigr)\leq\varphi^{(j)}(n) . For p\geq 3 prime, \varphi^{(2)}(p)=\varphi(p-1) and p-1 is even, so \varphi(p-1)\leq(p-1)/2 ; the first part gives \varphi^{(k)}(p)\leq\varphi^{(2)}(p) for k\geq 2 .
∎ 
 Write X:=\{1,2,4,12,30\}\cup\{p,\,2p:\ p\ \text{odd prime}\} . For n\notin X , n is composite and not twice an odd prime, and n\notin\{4,12,30\} ; hence Theorem 1.1 gives \varphi(n)>1+\sum_{p\mid n}\varphi(p)\geq 1+\sum_{p\mid n}\varphi^{(k)}(p) by Lemma 7.1 . Thus, for every k\geq 1 , every solution of ( 7 ) lies in X , and it remains to examine the elements of X . 
 Theorem 7.2 . 
 The solutions of ( 7 ) are exactly: 
 (i) 
 n\in\{1,4,12,30\} if k=1 ; 
 (ii) 
 n\in\{1,3,4,10\} if k=2 ; 
 (iii) 
 n\in\{1,3,4\} if k\geq 3 . 
 Proof. 
 The values involved are collected in the following table, where the right-hand side of ( 7 ) is denoted R_{k}(n) : 
 \begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c}n&\varphi(n)&R_{1}(n)&R_{2}(n)&R_{k}(n),\ k\geq 3\\
\hline\cr 1&1&1&1&1\\
2&1&2&2&2\\
4&2&2&2&2\\
12&4&4&3&3\\
30&8&8&5&4\\
3&2&3&2&2\\
6&2&4&3&3\\
10&4&6&4&3\end{array} 
 (For n=1 the sum is empty. For k\geq 3 , \varphi^{(k)}(2)=\varphi^{(k)}(3)=1 and \varphi^{(k)}(5)=1 .) This settles n\in\{1,2,4,12,30\} for all k , and n\in\{6,10\} once these are shown to be the only candidates among the 2p . 
 Case k=1 . For an odd prime p , \varphi(p)=p-1 
 Odd primes, k\geq 2 . Let n=p be an odd prime. For k=2 , ( 7 ) reads p-1=1+\varphi(p-1) , i.e. \varphi(p-1)=p-2 , which forces p-1 to be prime; p-1 and p being consecutive primes, p=3 . For k\geq 3 , Lemma 7.1 gives p-1=1+\varphi^{(k)}(p)\leq 1+\frac{p-1}{2} , hence p\leq 3 , and p=3 is a solution by the table. 
 Twice an odd prime, k\geq 2 . Let n=2p with p an odd prime, so \varphi(n)=p-1 and R_{k}(2p)=1+\varphi^{(k)}(2)+\varphi^{(k)}(p)=2+\varphi^{(k)}(p) . For k=2 this gives \varphi(p-1)=p-3 ; since \varphi(p-1)\leq\frac{p-1}{2} we get p\leq 5 . For k\geq 3 , Lemma 7.1 gives p-1\leq 2+\frac{p-1}{2} , again p\leq 5 . Thus n\in\{6,10\} , and the table shows that 10 is a solution for k=2 only, while 6 never is.
∎ 
 Remark 7.3 . 
 The solution sets shrink from k=1 to k=2 and stabilise for all k\geq 3 . The only solution lost between k=2 and k=3 is n=10 , because \varphi^{(3)}(5)=1 7 ) can only reach \varphi(n) for the smallest values of n . All three solution sets were confirmed by exhaustive computation up to 10^{5} for k\leq 8 . 
 The weighted form of ( 7 ), \varphi(n)=c+\alpha\sum_{p\mid n}\varphi^{(k)}(p) , behaves differently from the case k=1 treated in § 4 : since \varphi^{(k)}(p) is no longer linear in p , the matched families of Theorem 4.2 disappear, but infinite families of a new, conditional kind appear. The simplest instance is the following. 
 Lemma 7.4 . 
 Let p\geq 7 be a prime. Then \varphi(p-1)=\frac{p-3}{2} if and only if \frac{p-1}{2} is prime, i.e. p is a safe prime. 
 Proof. 
 Write p-1=2^{e}u with u odd, e\geq 1 . Then \varphi(p-1)=2^{e-1}\varphi(u) . If e\geq 2 , then \varphi(p-1)\leq 2^{e-1}u=\frac{p-1}{2}\cdot\frac{\varphi(u)}{u}\leq\frac{p-1}{2} , with \varphi(p-1)=\frac{p-3}{2} only if 2^{e-1}(u-\varphi(u))=1 , impossible for e\geq 2 . So e=1 , p-1=2u , \varphi(p-1)=\varphi(u) , and \varphi(u)=u-1 holds if and only if u=\frac{p-1}{2} is prime; since p\geq 7 , u\geq 3 is odd.
∎ 
 Proposition 7.5 . 
 The composite solutions of \varphi(n)=4\sum_{p\mid n}\varphi^{(2)}(p) are exactly 
 \{8,\,24,\,60\}\;\cup\;\{3p,\ 4p:\ p\ \text{safe prime},\ p\geq 7\}\;\cup\;\{6p:\ p=4q+1\ \text{prime},\ q\ \text{prime}\}. 
 In particular, the solution set is infinite if and only if there are infinitely many primes p with \frac{p-1}{2} or \frac{p-1}{4} prime; under Dickson’s conjecture all three families are infinite. 
 Proof. 
 Let P be the largest prime factor of n and write n=mP^{a} , P\nmid m . If a\geq 2 , the argument of Theorem 4.4 (case a\geq 2 ) applies verbatim, since \varphi^{(2)}(p)\leq\varphi(p) , and the resulting finite search gives only n=8 . Let a=1 and m\geq 2 . Writing S^{(2)}_{m}=\sum_{q\mid m}\varphi(q-1) , the equation reads 
 \varphi(m)(P-1)\;=\;4S^{(2)}_{m}+4\varphi(P-1). 
 Since \varphi(P-1)\leq\frac{P-1}{2} , this gives \varphi(m)\leq 2+\frac{4S^{(2)}_{m}}{P-1} . If \varphi(m)\geq 4 then P-1\leq 2S^{(2)}_{m}\leq 2\sum_{q\mid m}(q-2) , which bounds P in terms of m while P exceeds every prime factor of m ; the finitely many cases are n=24=8\cdot 3 and n=60=12\cdot 5 , where equality \varphi(m)=2+4S^{(2)}_{m}/(P-1)=4 holds. Otherwise \varphi(m)=2 , i.e. m\in\{3,4,6\} . For m\in\{3,4\} one has S^{(2)}_{m}=1 and the equation becomes \varphi(P-1)=\frac{P-3}{2} , i.e. P is a safe prime by Lemma 7.4 (and P\geq 7 since P>3 and P=5 fails). For m=6 , S^{(2)}_{6}=2 and the equation becomes \varphi(P-1)=\frac{P-5}{2} ; writing P-1=2^{e}u as in Lemma 7.4 , this forces e=2 and \varphi(u)=u-1 , i.e. P=4q+1 with q prime.
∎ 
 The sets were confirmed by exhaustive computation up to 2\cdot 10^{5} : of the 1029 composite solutions, 872 are of the form 3p or 4p with p safe, 154 of the form 6p with p=4q+1 , and 3 are sporadic. Thus, for k\geq 2 , finiteness of the solution sets of the weighted iterated equation is not a theorem but is tied to the distribution of primes p for which \varphi(p-1) is prescribed, a question of Sophie Germain type. 
 8 Numerical verification 
 All finite claims of this paper were verified by an exhaustive computation over 2\leq n\leq 2\cdot 10^{5} using SymPy. The composite solution sets found were: \{4,12,30\} for (E_{\varphi}) , in agreement with [ 9 ] ; \{p^{2}\leq 2\cdot 10^{5}\} and nothing else for (E_{\tau}) ; the empty set for (E_{\sigma}) , for J_{2} , for the weighted equation with (c,\alpha)=(1,2) , and for the triple system; and \{4\} for the pair (E_{\varphi})\wedge(E_{\tau}) . The expansion of Theorem 6.1 was compared with exact sieve computations up to x=10^{7} , and the sporadic sets of Theorem 4.8 were obtained by the bounded mechanism enumeration of Lemma 4.7 , independently cross-checked against the direct scan. The solution sets of Theorem 7.2 were checked for n\leq 10^{5} and 1\leq k\leq 8 . The census of Theorem 4.12 was cross-checked against a direct scan of all composites n\leq 10^{6} , and the values |F(c,1)| for 1\leq c\leq 2000 quoted in Question 9.2 were obtained by the bounded enumeration of Theorem 4.13 (i)–(ii). The verification scripts are available from the authors. 
 9 Concluding remarks and open problems 
 The results assembled here suggest that the natural home of the Ranjan–Singh theorem is the family of prime–composite characterization problems, alongside Subbarao’s \{4,6,22\} [ 10 ] and, in the unsolved direction, Lehmer’s problem [ 8 ] . We close with four problems. 
 Question 9.1 . 
 Theorem 4.13 gives |F(c,\alpha)|\ll L^{2}(\log L)^{2} with L=|c|+\alpha , whereas Theorem 4.12 gives |F(c,\alpha)|\leq 9 for L\leq 16 , a direct scan of n\leq 2\cdot 10^{6} gives |F(c,\alpha)|\leq 19 for |c|\leq 40 , \alpha\leq 12 , and |F(c,1)|\leq 9 for all 1\leq c\leq 2000 . Is \sup_{c}|F(c,\alpha)| finite for each fixed \alpha ? The maximizers observed, (6,6) , (12,4) , (40,8) , (27,9) , (36,12) , all satisfy \alpha\mid c , as one expects from ( 5 ): when \alpha divides c and \varphi(m^{\prime}) , the divisibility obstruction in P-1=(c+\alpha S_{m^{\prime}})/(\varphi(m^{\prime})-\alpha) disappears. Is |F(c,\alpha)| governed by \gcd(c,\alpha) and the divisor structure of \alpha ? 
 Question 9.2 . 
 For \alpha=1 , equation ( 4 ) reads \Delta(n)=c-1 , with \Delta the deficiency of § 6 , so that |F(c,1)| is the multiplicity of the value c-1 of \Delta on composite integers outside the family \{2p\} , the analogue for \Delta of the classical multiplicity problems for \varphi [ 6 ] . For 1\leq c\leq 2000 , F(c,1) is empty for 341 values of c , the first being 6,12,22,23,24,30,42,46,60,72 ; the mean multiplicity is stable, close to 2 ; and every multiplicity 0,1,\dots,9 occurs, first at c=6,2,4,1,3,27,15,403,1047,943 . Thus the analogue of Carmichael’s conjecture fails for \Delta ( F(c,1) is a singleton for c=2,8,10 ). Does every multiplicity occur, as Ford proved for \varphi ? Does the set of c with F(c,1)=\varnothing have positive density? Its parity bias is striking: it contains about 30\% of the even values of c against 5\% of the odd ones, an even c requiring an odd n by Theorem 4.1 (iii). 
 Question 9.3 . 
 Theorem 7.2 settles the iterated equation with c=\alpha=1 , and Proposition 7.5 shows that for (c,\alpha)=(0,4) and k=2 the solution set is infinite if and only if there are infinitely many safe primes. Describe, for general (c,\alpha) and k\geq 2 , the conditional families of the weighted iterated equation \varphi(n)=c+\alpha\sum_{p\mid n}\varphi^{(k)}(p) : they are of the form \{mp\} with \varphi(m)\leq\alpha and p subject to a condition \varphi^{(k-1)}(p-1)=\ell p+r , and one expects each of them to be infinite under Dickson’s conjecture. 
 Question 9.4 . 
 Theorem 6.1 describes the average of \Delta(n) . Determine the distribution of \Delta(n)/\varphi(n) , which is expected to tend to 1 in mean. 
 Finally, apart from the squares of primes (OEIS A001248), none of the solution sets occurring here, in particular \{4,12,30\} , appears to be catalogued in the OEIS at the time of writing; submitting them would usefully anchor the problem in the literature.
