 Abstract 
 In this paper, zeon matrix groups are discussed for the first time. Extending the concept of unitary complex matrices (i.e., matrices A such that A^{\dagger}A=\mathbb{I} ) to matrices having entries in the complex zeon algebra \mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z} , a “zeon dagger” operator {\hat{{\dagger}}} is defined and used to declare a zeon matrix \Xi to be unitary if \Xi^{\hat{{\dagger}}}\Xi=\mathbb{I} . The unitary zeon matrices of order n form a subgroup \mathfrak{U}_{n} of all invertible order- n zeon matrices. A more interesting notion is that of “weak unitarity” of a zeon matrix. In particular, a zeon matrix \Xi will be said to be weakly unitary if \Xi^{\hat{{\dagger}}}\Xi=\mathbb{I}+\Gamma , where \Gamma is a diagonal nilpotent zeon matrix. The weakly unitary matrices of order n form a group \mathfrak{W}_{n} , of which \mathfrak{U}_{n} is a subgroup. It is further shown that the exponential of a graph’s nilpotent adjacency matrix has a natural orthogonal decomposition into the sum of a weakly unitary zeon matrix and a nilpotent zeon matrix. Eigenvalues of the weakly unitary zeon matrix reveal information about the graph’s cycles and paths.
 Keywords: zeons, matrix groups, graph processes, linear operators 
 1 Introduction 
 Zeon algebras can be thought of as commutative fermion algebras; i.e., their generators commute and square to zero. They arise as subalgebras of Clifford algebras (of appropriate signature) and have natural applications in combinatorics and graph theory [ 1 , 3 , 4 ] . 
 In this paper, the concept of unitary complex matrices (i.e., matrices A such that A^{\dagger}A=\mathbb{I} ) is extended to matrices over the complex zeon algebra \mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z} ; a “zeon dagger” operator {\hat{{\dagger}}} is defined and used to declare a zeon matrix \Xi to be unitary if \Xi^{\hat{{\dagger}}}\Xi=\mathbb{I} . The unitary zeon matrices of order n form a subgroup \mathfrak{U}_{n} of all invertible order- n zeon matrices A much more interesting notion is that of “weak unitarity” of a zeon matrix. In particular, a zeon matrix \Xi is said to be weakly unitary if \Xi^{\hat{{\dagger}}}\Xi=\mathbb{I}+\Gamma , where \Gamma is a diagonal nilpotent zeon matrix. The weakly unitary matrices \mathfrak{W}_{n} of order n form a subgroup of \mathfrak{U}_{n} . 
 The paper is laid out as follows. Essential terminology and background on zeon algebras is presented in Section 1.1 . Graph-theoretic results related to zeon algebras are reviewed in Section 1.2 , while Section 2.0.2 illustrates how zeon matrix exponentials reveal paths in sequences of graphs. 
 Motivated by the discussion of zeon path matrices, the notions of unitary and weakly unitary zeon matrices are presented in Section 3 , where it is shown that when \Phi is the nilpotent adjacency matrix of a graph, the matrix exponential \mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi) , can be decomposed into an orthogonal sum of a weakly unitary matrix and a nilpotent zeon matrix. Further, the weakly unitary matrix and its eigenvalues reveal information about paths and cycles contained in the graph. Writing the nilpotent adjacency matrix \Phi of a graph as the Hadamard product of “fermion adjacency matrices,” an alternative formulation of \mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi) is presented, potentially opening a new avenue of potential discovery. 
 The paper closes with Section 4 , in which some avenues for further investigation are suggested. 
 1.1 Preliminaries 
 For n\in\mathbb{N} , let \mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n} denote the complex commutative algebra generated by the collection \{\zeta_{\{i\}}:1\leq i\leq n\} along with the scalar 1=\zeta_{\varnothing} subject to the following multiplication rules: 
 \displaystyle\zeta_{\{i\}}\,\zeta_{\{j\}}=\zeta_{\{i,j\}}=\zeta_{\{j\}}\,\zeta_{\{i\}}\,\,\text{ \rm for }i\neq j,\text{ \rm and} 
 \displaystyle{\zeta_{\{i\}}}^{2}=0\,\,\text{ \rm for }1\leq i\leq n. 
 It is evident that a general element u\in\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n} can be expanded as u=\displaystyle\sum_{I\in 2^{[n]}}u_{I}\,\zeta_{I} , or more simply as \sum_{I}u_{I}\zeta_{I} , where I\in 2^{[n]} is a subset of the n -set , [n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\} , used as a multi-index, u_{I}\in\mathbb{R} , and \displaystyle\zeta_{I}=\prod_{\iota\in I}\zeta_{\iota} . The algebra \mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n} is called the ( n -particle) complex zeon algebra . 
 As a vector space, this 2^{n} -dimensional algebra has a canonical basis of basis blades of the form \{\zeta_{I}:I\subseteq[n]\} . The null-square property of the generators \{\zeta_{i}:1\leq i\leq n\} guarantees that the product of two basis blades satisfies the following: 
 \zeta_{I}\zeta_{J}=\begin{cases}\zeta_{I\cup J}&I\cap J=\varnothing,\\
0&\text{\rm otherwise.}\end{cases} 
 (1.1) 
 It should be clear that \mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n} is graded. For non-negative integer k , the grade- k part of element u=\sum_{I}u_{I}\zeta_{I} is defined as 
 \langle u\rangle_{k}=\sum_{\{I:|I|=k\}}u_{I}\zeta_{I}. 
 An inner product is defined on \mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n} by linear extension of 
 \displaystyle\left =u_{J}. 
 The basis blades \{\zeta_{I}:I\in 2^{[n]}\} form an orthonormal collection with respect to this inner product; thus, any element u\in\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n} can be expanded as \displaystyle u=\sum_{I}\left \zeta_{I}. Moreover, an inner product norm is naturally induced on \mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n} by \|u\|=\langle u,u\rangle^{1/2} . 
 It is often convenient to separate the scalar term of a zeon element from the rest of it. To this end, for z\in\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n} we write \mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}z=\langle z\rangle_{0} for the scalar part of z , and \mathfrak{D}z=z-\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}z for the dual part of z . The dual part of any zeon element is always nilpotent; i.e., (\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}z)^{k}=0 for some positive integer k . The least such positive integer is called the index of nilpotency of \mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}z and is denoted by \kappa(\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}z) . 
 The finite-dimensional algebra is extended to zeon algebras of arbitrary dimension by defining 
 \mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}={\rm span}\{\zeta_{I}:I\subset\mathbb{N},I\,\text{\rm finite}\}, 
 which we call the infinite-dimensional (complex) zeon algebra . 
 An element u\in CZ is uniquely invertible if and only if \mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u\neq 0 , and the inverse is given by 
 u^{-1}=\frac{1}{\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u}\sum_{j=0}^{\kappa-1}(-1)^{j}(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u)^{-j}(\mathfrak{D}u)^{j}. 
 The maximal ideal consisting of nilpotent zeon elements will be denoted by {\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}}^{\circ}=\{u\in\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}:\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u=0\} . The multiplicative Abelian group of invertible zeon elements is denoted by 
 {\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}}^{\times}=\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}\setminus{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}}^{\circ}=\{u\in\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}:\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u\neq 0\}. 
 1.2 Zeons and Graph Theory 
 For purposes of the discussion at hand, a graph on n vertices is an ordered pair G=(V,E) of sets. Elements of V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\} are the vertices of G , and elements of E are unordered pairs of distinct vertices, called the edges of G . Two vertices v_{i},v_{j}\in V are said to be adjacent if there exists an edge \{v_{i},v_{j}\}\in E . 
 A walk (v_{0},\ldots,v_{k}) in a graph G is a finite sequence of vertices in G with initial vertex v_{0} and terminal vertex v_{k} such that there exists an edge \{v_{j},v_{j+1}\}\in E for each 0\leq j\leq k-1 . A walk of length k , or k -walk contains k edges. A path is a walk in which no vertex appears more than once. A closed walk is a walk whose initial vertex is also its terminal vertex. A cycle is a closed path whose terminal vertex is its initial vertex (i.e., v_{k}=v_{0} ). A Hamiltonian cycle is an n -cycle in a graph on n vertices; i.e., each vertex in the graph appears exactly once (with the exception of the terminal/initial vertex). 
 Definition 1.1 . 
 Let G=(V,E) be a graph on n labeled vertices V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\} and edges E consisting of unordered pairs of vertices. The nilpotent adjacency matrix of G is defined as the n\times n matrix \Phi=(\varphi_{ij}) having entries in {\mathfrak{Z}_{n}} determined as follows: 
 \varphi_{ij}=\begin{cases}0&\{v_{i},v_{j}\}\notin E,\\
\zeta_{\{j\}}&\{v_{i},v_{j}\}\in E.\end{cases} 
 Modifying Dirac notation, let us define \langle\zeta_{\{i\}}| to denote the row vector whose entries are all zero, except the i th column, which is \zeta_{\{i\}} . Similarly, |j\rangle will denote the column vector with 1 in the j th row and zeros elsewhere. 
 Because the generators of \mathfrak{Z}_{n} square to zero, a straightforward inductive argument shows that the nonzero terms of \left are multivectors corresponding to two types of k -walks from v_{i} to v_{j} :
self-avoiding walks (i.e., walks with no repeated vertices) and walks in which v_{i} is repeated exactly once at some step but are otherwise self-avoiding. For i\neq j , multiplication by \zeta_{\{i\}} cancels walks of the second type, so that nonzero terms represent paths from v_{i} to v_{j} . 
 When i=j , walks of the second type are zeroed in the k^{\rm th} step when the walk is closed. Hence, terms of \left represent the collection of k -cycles based at v_{i} . We thereby arrive at the following result, as first presented in [ 5 ] . 
 Nil-Structure Theorem 
 Let \Phi be the nilpotent adjacency matrix of an
 n -vertex graph G , and let k\in\mathbb{N} . For 1\leq i\neq j\leq n , 
 \left =\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{(w_1,\ldots, w_k)\in V^k}{(w_k=v_j)\wedge(m\ne\ell\Rightarrow w_m\ne w_\ell)}}\zeta_{(w_{1},\ldots,w_{k})}=\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{I\subseteq V}{|I|=k+1}}\omega_{I}\zeta_{I}, 
 where \omega_{I} denotes the number of k -paths from v_{i} to v_{j} . Further, when 1\leq i\leq n , 
 \left =\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{I\subseteq V}{|I|=k}}\omega_{I}\zeta_{I}, 
 where \omega_{I} denotes the number of k -cycles on vertex set I based at v_{i}\in I . 
 2 The Nilpotent Adjacency Matrix Exponential 
 It is worth noting that any square matrix of order m having entries in \mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}^{\circ} must be nilpotent by the pigeonhole principle. For any X\in\mathbb{M}_{m}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}^{\circ}) , the index of nilpotency of X is denoted by \kappa(X) . The exponential of any nilpotent square matrix X\in\mathbb{M}_{m}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}^{\circ}) can therefore be expressed as the finite sum 
 \mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{\kappa(\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}(X))-1}\frac{X^{k}}{k!}. 
 Hence, the following zeon elementary functions of X can be computed as finite sums: 
 \displaystyle\mathfrak{cos}\mspace{1mu}(X)=\frac{1}{2}(\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}X)+\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(-\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}X)), 
 \displaystyle\qquad\mathfrak{sin}\mspace{1mu}(X)=\frac{1}{2}(\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}X)-\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(-\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}X)), 
 \displaystyle\mathfrak{cosh}\mspace{1mu}(X)=\frac{1}{2}(\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(X)+\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(-X)), 
 \displaystyle\qquad\mathfrak{sinh}\mspace{1mu}(X)=\frac{1}{2}(\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(X)-\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(-X)). 
 Letting \Phi\in\mathbb{M}_{m}({\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{m}}^{\circ}) be the nilpotent adjacency matrix of a graph G=(V,E) on m vertices, it follows that 
 \mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\Phi)=\sum_{k=0}^{\kappa(\Phi)-1}\frac{1}{k!}\Phi^{k}=\mathbb{I}+\Psi, 
 where \Psi\in\mathbb{M}_{m}({\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{m}}^{\circ}) . 
 The off-diagonal entries of \Psi satisfy 
 \langle\zeta_{\{i\}}|\Psi|j\rangle=\sum_{I\subseteq([n]\setminus\varnothing)}\frac{\omega_{I}}{(|I|-1)!}\zeta_{I},\hskip 12.0pt(i\neq j), 
 (2.1) 
 where \omega_{I} is the number of paths in G from v_{i} to v_{j} on vertices indexed by I .
The diagonal entries of \Psi satisfy 
 \langle i|\Psi|i\rangle=\sum_{I\subseteq([n]\setminus\varnothing)}\frac{\xi_{I}}{|I|!}\zeta_{I}, 
 (2.2) 
 where \xi_{I} is the number of cycles 1 1 
 1 
 Edges are regarded as 2 -cycles. in G on vertices indexed by I , and containing v_{i} . 
 For these reasons, the matrix \Psi=\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\Phi) will be referred to as the zeon path matrix of G . 
 2.0.1 Elementary Functions and Graph Properties 
 Lemma 2.1 . 
 Let G=(V,E) be a graph on n vertices with nilpotent adjacency matrix \Phi . Then 
 1. 
 G is bipartite if and only if \tr\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\Phi)=\tr\mathfrak{cosh}\mspace{1mu}(\Phi) . 
 2. 
 G is Hamiltonian if and only if \tr(\Phi^{n})\neq\mathbf{0} , which is true if and only if \langle\tr\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\Phi)\rangle_{n}\neq 0 . 
 Proof. 
 A well known result of graph theory is that G is bipartite if and only if all cycles are of even length, proving 1 . Item 2 follows immediately from the Nil-Structure Theorem and the definition of the matrix exponential.
∎ 
 2.0.2 Matrix Exponentials and Graph Processes 
 Let \mathcal{G}=\{G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{m}\} be a collection of graphs on a common fixed set of n vertices, and let \Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{m} be their respective nilpotent adjacency matrices. Then, \exists\Psi\in{\rm Mat}(n;{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{\circ}) such that 
 \mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\Phi_{1}+\cdots+\Phi_{m})=\mathbb{I}+\Psi. 
 Let us refer to any sequence of (not necessarily distinct) graphs from \mathcal{G} as a graph process on \mathcal{G} . In this case, \Psi represents all paths and cycles in all graph processes on \mathcal{G} : 
 \Psi=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})\in[m]^{k}}\frac{1}{k!}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\Phi_{u_{j}}. 
 Moreover, the (ordered) products of nilpotent adjacency matrix exponentials enumerate paths in sequences of graphs. For example, 
 \mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\Phi_{1})\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\Phi_{2})=(\mathbb{I}+\Psi_{1})(\mathbb{I}+\Psi_{2})=\mathbb{I}+\Psi_{1}+\Psi_{2}+\Psi_{1}\Psi_{2}, 
 where \Psi_{1} and \Psi_{2} enumerate paths and cycles in G_{1} and G_{2} , respectively, and \Psi_{1}\Psi_{2} enumerates paths v_{i}\to v_{j}\to v_{k} where v_{i}\to v_{j} are paths in G_{1} and paths v_{j}\to v_{k} exist in G_{2} . More formally, 
 \displaystyle\langle\zeta_{\{i\}}|\Psi_{1}\Psi_{2}|j\rangle 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\langle\zeta_{\{i\}}|\Psi_{1}|\ell\rangle\langle\ell|\Psi_{2}|j\rangle 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{I}\omega_{I}\zeta_{I}\right)\left(\sum_{J}\nu_{J}\zeta_{J}\right) 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{K}\varpi_{K}\zeta_{K}, 
 where for each \ell , \omega_{I} is the number of paths in G_{1} from v_{i} to v_{\ell} on vertices indexed by I and \nu_{J} is the number of paths in G_{2} from v_{\ell} to v_{j} on vertices indexed by J . Then \varpi_{K} is the number of paths having initial vertex v_{i} and terminal vertex v_{j} on vertices indexed by K in which some number of steps occur initially in G_{1} and subsequently in G_{2} . Extending the concept by induction, we arrive at the following proposition. 
 Proposition 2.2 . 
 Let \vec{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{1},\ldots,G_{m}) be a sequence of graphs on fixed vertex set V and let (\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{m}) be the sequence of nilpotent adjacency matrices associated with \vec{\mathcal{G}} . Defining the matrix \Omega by 
 \Omega=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\Phi_{j})=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(\mathbb{I}+\Psi_{j}), 
 it follows that 
 \langle\zeta_{\{i\}}|\Omega|j\rangle=\sum_{K}\varpi_{K}\zeta_{K}, 
 where \varpi_{K} is the number of paths from v_{i} to v_{j} on vertices indexed by K in the graph process \vec{\mathcal{G}} . Moreover, 
 \langle j|\Omega|j\rangle=\sum_{K}\xi_{K}\zeta_{K}, 
 where \xi_{K} is the number of cycles based at vertex v_{j} in the graph process \vec{\mathcal{G}} . 
 Obviously, the collection of matrices \mathfrak{I}_{n}=\{\mathbb{I}+\Psi:\Psi\in\mathbb{M}_{n}({\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}}^{\circ})\}\subset\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}) forms a multiplicative subgroup of the order- n invertible zeon matrices, \mathfrak{G}_{n} . The subgroup \mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}_{n} motivates our discussion of unitary and weakly unitary zeon matrices. 
 3 Unitary and Weakly Unitary Zeon Matrices 
 In order to generalize the concept of the Hermitian conjugate of a complex matrix for use with zeon matrices, it is necessary to find a suitable “zeon dagger operator.” To begin, we define the zeon grade involution of an element u=\sum_{I}u_{I}\zeta_{I}\in\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z} by \hat{u}=\sum_{I}(-1)^{|I|}u_{I}\zeta_{I} . Note that \hat{\hat{u}}=u and \widehat{uv}=\hat{u}\hat{v} for all u,v\in\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z} . Grade involution extends entrywise to zeon matrices. 
 As will become evident, the composition of the zeon grade involution with the complex conjugate transpose now provides a suitable operator for defining unitary and weakly unitary zeon matrices. 
 Definition 3.1 . 
 The zeon dagger operator {\hat{{\dagger}}} is defined on \mathbb{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}) by \Xi^{\hat{{\dagger}}}={\widehat{\Xi}}^{\dagger} , where \widehat{\Xi} is the zeon grade involution of \Xi\in\mathbb{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}) . 
 For any \Xi\in\mathbb{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}) , it is obvious that the operator {\hat{{\dagger}}} satisfies 
 {\Xi^{\hat{{\dagger}}}}^{\hat{{\dagger}}}=({\widehat{\Xi}}^{\dagger})^{\hat{{\dagger}}}=(\widehat{({\widehat{\Xi}}^{\dagger})})^{\dagger}\\
=(\Xi^{\dagger})^{\dagger}=\Xi. 
 Moreover, for matrices \Psi,\Xi\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}) , we have (\Psi\Xi)^{\hat{{\dagger}}}=\Xi^{\hat{{\dagger}}}\Psi^{\hat{{\dagger}}} . 
 Remark 3.2 . 
 The zeon dagger operator restricts to the ordinary conjugate transpose for scalar matrices; i.e., (\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\Xi)^{\hat{{\dagger}}}=(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\Xi)^{\dagger} for any \Xi\in\mathbb{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}) . 
 Unitary zeon matrices are now defined in the expected way. 
 Definition 3.3 . 
 An invertible zeon matrix \Xi\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}) is said to be (strongly) unitary if 
 \Xi^{\hat{{\dagger}}}\Xi=\Xi\Xi^{\hat{{\dagger}}}=\mathbb{I}. 
 We note that the collection \mathfrak{U}_{n} of unitary matrices in \mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}) form a multiplicative group, since \mathbb{I}\in\mathfrak{U}_{n} and for any \Psi,\Xi\in\mathfrak{U}_{n} , we have 
 (\Psi\Xi)^{\hat{{\dagger}}}(\Psi\Xi)=\Xi^{\hat{{\dagger}}}(\Psi^{\hat{{\dagger}}}\Psi)\Xi=\mathbb{I}. 
 Necessary and sufficient conditions for a zeon matrix to be unitary are established in the next lemma. 
 Lemma 3.4 . 
 Let \Xi\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}) . Then, \Xi=\in\mathfrak{U}_{n} if and only if the matrices C=\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\Xi and D=\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}\Xi satisfy the following: 
 1. 
 C^{\dagger}C=\mathbb{I} , i.e., C is a unitary complex matrix; 
 2. 
 D^{\hat{{\dagger}}}D=\mathbf{0} ; 
 3. 
 C^{\dagger}D=-D^{\hat{{\dagger}}}C . 
 Proof. 
 Letting C=\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\Xi and D=\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}\Xi , we see that 
 \displaystyle\Xi^{\hat{{\dagger}}}\Xi=(C+D)^{\dagger}(C+D) 
 \displaystyle=C^{\hat{{\dagger}}}C+C^{\hat{{\dagger}}}D+D^{\hat{{\dagger}}}C+D^{\hat{{\dagger}}}D 
 \displaystyle=C^{\dagger}C+C^{\dagger}D+D^{\hat{{\dagger}}}C+D^{\hat{{\dagger}}}D. 
 Clearly, C^{\dagger}C+C^{\dagger}D+D^{\hat{{\dagger}}}C+D^{\hat{{\dagger}}}D=\mathbb{I} if and only if the conditions in the statement of the lemma are satisfied.
∎ 
 The conditions of Lemma 3.4 are quite restrictive. Relaxing these conditions leads to a subgroup of matrices more useful for matters at hand. 
 Definition 3.5 . 
 An invertible zeon matrix \Xi\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}) is said to be weakly unitary if there exists a diagonal nilpotent zeon matrix \Gamma\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}^{\circ}) such that 
 \Xi^{\hat{{\dagger}}}\Xi=\Xi\Xi^{\hat{{\dagger}}}=\mathbb{I}+\Gamma. 
 The weakly unitary zeon matrices of order n clearly form a subgroup \mathfrak{W}_{n} of \mathfrak{G}_{n} , the invertible zeon matrices of order n . Further, the unitary matrices \mathfrak{U}_{n} are a subgroup of \mathfrak{W}_{n} . We shall refer to \mathfrak{W}_{n} as the weak unitary group of n\times n zeon matrices. 
 A zeon-valued inner-product is defined on \mathbb{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}) by 
 \langle\psi,\omega\rangle_{\hat{{\dagger}}}=\Tr(\omega^{\hat{{\dagger}}}\psi) 
 (3.1) 
 where \Tr(\Xi)=\frac{1}{n}\tr(\Xi) denotes normalized trace of \Xi\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}) . It follows immediately that every weakly unitary zeon matrix \psi\in\mathfrak{W}_{n} satisfies \langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{\hat{{\dagger}}}=1+\gamma for some \gamma\in\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}^{\circ} . If \gamma=0 , then \psi\in\mathfrak{U}_{n} . 
 Let \Phi be the nilpotent adjacency matrix of a graph G on n vertices. The exponential of \mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi reduces to the finite sum 
 \mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi)=\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\kappa(\Phi)-1}\frac{\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}^{\ell}}{\ell!}\Phi^{\ell}=\mathbb{I}+\Psi, 
 where \Psi\in{\rm Mat}(n;{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{\circ}) . 
 Recalling ( 2.1 ), the off-diagonal entries of \Psi satisfy 
 \langle\zeta_{\{i\}}|\Psi|j\rangle=\sum_{I\subseteq([n]\setminus\varnothing)}\frac{\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}^{|I|-1}\omega_{I}}{(|I|-1)!}\zeta_{I},\hskip 12.0pt(i\neq j), 
 where \omega_{I} is the number of paths in G from v_{i} to v_{j} on vertices indexed by I .
Finally, the diagonal entries of \Psi satisfy 
 \langle i|\Psi|i\rangle=\sum_{I\subseteq([n]\setminus\varnothing)}\frac{\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}^{|I|}\xi_{I}}{|I|!}\zeta_{I}, 
 where \xi_{I} is the number of cycles in G on vertices indexed by I , and containing v_{i} . 
 For convenience, we define the rank-one projector \varpi_{j} on {\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}}^{n} by \varpi_{j}=|j\rangle\langle j| for 1\leq j\leq n . We further define \Delta_{n}={\rm diag}(\zeta_{\{1\}},\ldots,\zeta_{\{n\}})\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}) to be the diagonal matrix whose nonzero entries are the generators of \mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n} . With these notational preliminaries in mind, we can state our next result. 
 Theorem 3.6 . 
 Let \Phi be the nilpotent adjacency matrix of a graph G=(V,E) on n vertices. Then, there exist unique matrices \Psi_{1}\in\mathfrak{W}_{n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}) and \Psi_{0}\in\mathbb{M}_{n}({\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{\circ}) such that \mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi)=\Psi_{1}+\Psi_{0} . In particular, \Psi_{1} is given by 
 \Psi_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\varpi_{j}\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi){\varpi_{j}}^{\dagger}+\Delta_{n}\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}(\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi)). 
 (3.2) 
 Moreover, the decomposition is orthogonal: 
 \langle\Psi_{0},\Psi_{1}\rangle_{\hat{{\dagger}}}=\langle\Psi_{1},\Psi_{0}\rangle_{\hat{{\dagger}}}=0. 
 Proof. 
 Writing \mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi)=(\phi_{\ell j}) , we see that 
 \sum_{j=1}^{n}\varpi_{j}\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi){\varpi_{j}}^{\dagger}={\rm diag}(\phi_{11},\phi_{22},\ldots,\phi_{nn}), 
 the diagonal matrix whose main diagonal is that of \mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi) . Further, the matrix \Delta_{n}\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}(\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi)) is obtained by multiplying the off-diagonal elements of the path matrix by zeon generators corresponding to initial vertices of paths. Since the nilpotent main diagonal elements correspond to cycles, such initial vertices are already accounted for and hence zeroed out by \Delta_{n} . Hence, all nonzero entries of \Delta_{n}\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}(\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi)) correspond to paths in the graph G . In accordance with previous results, when i\neq j , 
 \langle i|\Psi_{1}|j\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{\{I:|I|=k\}}\frac{\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}^{|I|-1}\omega_{I}}{(k-1)!}\zeta_{I} 
 where \omega_{I} is the number of paths in G from v_{i} to v_{j} on vertices indexed by I .
Moreover, 
 \langle j|\Psi_{1}|j\rangle=1+\sum_{k=2}^{n}\sum_{\{I:|I|=k\}}\frac{\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}^{|I|}\xi_{I}}{k!}\zeta_{I}, 
 where \xi_{I} is the number of cycles in G based at vertex v_{j} on vertices indexed by I .
To see that \Psi_{1} is weakly unitary, we consider the matrix product {\Psi_{1}}^{\hat{{\dagger}}}\Psi_{1} . Writing \Psi_{1}=(y_{ij}) , we see that when i\neq j , the i th row and j th column of {\Psi_{1}}^{\hat{{\dagger}}}\Psi_{1} is 
 \langle i|{\Psi_{1}}^{\hat{{\dagger}}}\Psi_{1}|j\rangle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\overline{y_{\ell i}}y_{\ell j}=0, 
 since \zeta_{\ell} annihilates each term of y_{\ell j} and y_{i\ell} . Further, diagonal entries of {\Psi_{1}}^{\hat{{\dagger}}}\Psi_{1} are given by 
 \langle j|{\Psi_{1}}^{\hat{{\dagger}}}\Psi_{1}|j\rangle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\overline{y_{\ell j}}y_{\ell j}=1+2\sum_{I}\frac{\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}^{|I|}\xi_{I}}{|I!}\zeta_{I}, 
 where \xi_{I} is the number of cycles in G based at vertex v_{j} on vertices indexed by I . Thus, {\Psi_{1}}^{\hat{{\dagger}}}\Psi_{1}=\mathbb{I}+\Gamma where \Gamma is a diagonal matrix over \mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}^{\circ} . 
 Let \Psi_{0}=\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi)-\Psi_{1}=(z_{ij}) . We first observe that z_{jj}=0 for j=1,\ldots,n . Further, for i\neq j , 
 \langle i|\Psi_{0}|j\rangle=z_{ij}=(1-\zeta_{\{i\}})\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\{I:|I|=k\}}\frac{\omega_{I}}{(k-1)!}\zeta_{I}, 
 where \omega_{I} is the number of paths in G from v_{i} to v_{j} on vertices indexed by I . In particular, z_{ij}\zeta_{\{j\}}=0 for j=1,\ldots,n . Recalling that \zeta_{\{j\}}y_{\ell j}=0 for \ell=1,\ldots,n . It follows immediately that 
 \displaystyle\langle\Psi_{1},\Psi_{0}\rangle_{\hat{{\dagger}}} 
 \displaystyle=\Tr({\Psi_{0}}^{\hat{{\dagger}}}\Psi_{1}) 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\overline{z_{\ell j}}y_{\ell j}=0. 
 Similar reasoning shows \langle\Psi_{0},\Psi_{1}\rangle_{\hat{{\dagger}}}=0 .
∎ 
 Remark 3.7 . 
 Vertices of the graph G are in one-to-one correspondence with states \varpi_{j} such that \langle\varpi_{j}|\Psi_{1}|\varpi_{j}\rangle_{\hat{{\dagger}}} is the expectation of \Psi_{1} in the state \varpi_{j} . Moreover, this expectation is expressed in terms of the numbers of cycles and their supporting vertices in the graph G . 
 As seen in [ 7 ] , \lambda\in\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z} is an eigenvalue of zeon matrix \Xi\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}) if and only if \mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\lambda is a simple zero of the characteristic polynomial of the scalar matrix \mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}(\Xi) . If, on the other hand, \lambda\in\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z} is a zero of \chi_{\Xi}(u) and \mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\lambda is a multiple zero of \chi_{\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}(\Xi)}(u) , then \chi_{\Xi}(u) has infinitely many zeon zeros. In particular, we have the following result. 
 Proposition 3.8 . 
 Let \Phi be the nilpotent adjacency matrix of a graph G=(V,E) on n vertices. Writing
 \mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi)=\Psi_{1}+\Psi_{0} where \Psi_{1}\in\mathfrak{W}_{n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}) , it follows that for each j=1,\ldots,n , the diagonal entry \langle j|\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi)|j\rangle=\langle j|\Psi_{1}|j\rangle is a zero of the characteristic polynomial \chi_{\Psi_{1}}(u) . Moreover, \chi_{\Psi_{1}}(u) has infinitely many zeon zeros. 
 Proof. 
 Fixing j\in\{1,\ldots,n\} and setting \lambda=\langle j|\Psi_{1}|j\rangle , it is sufficient to show that |\lambda\mathbb{I}-\Psi_{1}|=0 . Write \lambda\mathbb{I}-\Psi_{1}=(a_{\ell k}) and consider cofactor expansion in the j th column: 
 |\lambda\mathbb{I}-\Psi_{1}|=\begin{vmatrix}a_{jj}-a_{11}&-a_{12}&\cdots&-a_{1j}&\cdots&-a_{1n}\\
-a_{21}&a_{jj}-a_{22}&\cdots&-a_{2j}&\cdots&-a_{2n}\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0&\cdots&-a_{jn}\\
-a_{n1}&-a_{n2}&\cdots&-a_{nj}&\cdots&a_{jj}-a_{nn}\end{vmatrix}. 
 Note that a_{\ell j} annihilates a_{i\ell} for all \ell\neq j since each is a sum over paths including the common vertex \ell , and that a_{jj}=0 . Hence, all terms of the cofactor expansion of the determinant are zero.
∎ 
 In [ 6 ] , it was shown that eigenvalues of a graph’s zeon combinatorial Laplacian reveal information about the graph’s cycles. Not surprisingly, eigenvalues of weakly unitary components of nilpotent matrix exponentials can reveal the same information. 
 Theorem 3.9 . 
 Let \Phi be the nilpotent adjacency matrix of a graph G=(V,E) on n vertices. Let u=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}) be a column vector in \mathbb{C}^{n} such that u^{\dagger}u=1 and z_{i}\neq z_{j} for i\neq j . Let U denote the rank-one projector uu^{\dagger} and let \mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi)=\Psi_{1}+\Psi_{0} be the orthogonal decomposition seen in Theorem 3.6 . Then, U\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi)=U\Psi_{1}+U\Psi_{0} , where U\Psi_{1} is a weakly unitary zeon matrix having exactly n distinct zeon eigenvalues \lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n} , such that \mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\lambda_{j}=z_{j} for j=1,\ldots,n . Moreover, the eigenvalues of U\Psi_{1} are precisely the diagonal entries \lambda_{j}=\langle j|U\Psi_{1}|j\rangle : 
 \lambda_{j}=z_{j}\langle j|\Psi_{1}|j\rangle=z_{j}\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\frac{\xi_{I}}{|I|!}\zeta_{I}, 
 where \xi_{I} is the number of |I| -cycles in G based at vertex v_{j} , on vertices indexed by I . 
 Proof. 
 Observing that \mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}(U\Psi_{1})=U , it follows that U\Psi_{1} has n distinct zeon eigenvalues \lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n} whose scalar parts are \mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\lambda_{j}=z_{j} for j=1,\ldots,n . 
 Write \mathfrak{A}=U\Psi_{1}=(a_{ij}) , so that a_{ij}=\langle i|U\Psi_{1}|j\rangle . Fixing j\in\{1,\ldots,n\} , consider the determinant 
 \displaystyle\chi_{\mathfrak{A}}(a_{jj}) 
 \displaystyle=|a_{jj}\mathbb{I}-\mathfrak{A}| 
 \displaystyle=\begin{vmatrix}a_{jj}-a_{11}&-a_{12}&-a_{13}&\cdots&\cdots&-a_{1n}\\
-a_{21}&a_{jj}-a_{22}&-a_{23}&\cdots&\cdots&-a_{2n}\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\\
-a_{j1}&-a_{j2}&\cdots&0&\cdots&-a_{jn}\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\\
-a_{n1}&-a_{n2}&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&a_{jj}-a_{nn}\end{vmatrix}. 
 Computing the determinant by cofactor expansion in the j th row, we note first that the cofactor of 0 is determined by the only invertible minor in the determinant. All other summands in the cofactor expansion are nilpotent. Secondly, we note that \zeta_{\{\ell\}}a_{j\ell}=0 for each \ell because terms of a_{j\ell} represent paths from v_{j} to v_{\ell} in G . Computing the cofactor (-1)^{j+\ell}|\widehat{\mathfrak{A}_{j\ell}}| by cofactor expansion of the minor in row \ell , we further see that \zeta_{\{\ell\}}|\widehat{\mathfrak{A}_{j\ell}}|=0 . Hence, (-1)^{j+\ell}a_{j\ell}|\widehat{\mathfrak{A}_{j\ell}}|=0 for each \ell=1,\ldots,n . 
 We conclude that \chi_{\mathfrak{A}}(a_{jj})=|a_{jj}\mathbb{I}-\mathfrak{A}|=0 for j=1,\ldots,n , so that the eigenvalues of \mathfrak{A}=U\Psi_{1} are precisely \lambda_{j}=a_{jj} .
∎ 
 Remark 3.10 . 
 As was the case in Theorem 3.6 , U\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi)=U\Psi_{1}+U\Psi_{0} is an orthogonal decomposition; i.e., \langle U\Psi_{1},U\Psi_{0}\rangle_{\hat{{\dagger}}}=0 . 
 In the next theorem, an approach is offered to obtain an eigenvalue of \Psi_{1} while only concerning ourselves with real zeon matrices, i.e., matrices in \mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathfrak{Z}_{n}) . 
 Theorem 3.11 . 
 Let \Phi be the nilpotent adjacency matrix of a graph G=(V,E) on n vertices. Let U={\rm diag}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\{\pm 1\}^{n} be a diagonal matrix satisfying z_{j}=1 for some j\in[n] and z_{\ell}=-1 for \ell\neq j , and let \mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi)=\Psi_{1}+\Psi_{0} be the orthogonal decomposition seen in Theorem 3.6 . Then, U\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi)=U\Psi_{1}+U\Psi_{0} , where U\Psi_{1} is a weakly unitary zeon matrix having unique eigenvalue \lambda_{j}=\langle j|U\Psi_{1}|j\rangle=\langle j|\Psi_{1}|j\rangle\in\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n} . This eigenvalue is also a zero of the characteristic polynomial of \Psi_{1} and is given by 
 \lambda_{j}=\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\frac{\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}^{|I|}\xi_{I}}{|I|!}\zeta_{I}, 
 where \xi_{I} is the number of |I| -cycles in G based at vertex v_{j} , on vertices indexed by I . Moreover, a unit right eigenvector |\mu\rangle=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\underset{j\text{\rm th pos}}{\underbrace{1}},\ldots\mu_{n})^{\intercal} is given by 
 \mu_{\ell}=-\frac{1}{2}\langle\ell|U\Psi_{1}|j\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\frac{\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}^{|I|-1}\omega_{I}}{(|I|-1)!}\zeta_{I}, 
 where \omega_{I} denotes the number of paths from v_{\ell} to v_{j} in G on vertices indexed by I . 
 Proof. 
 The fact that \lambda_{j} is an eigenvalue follows from the proof of Theorem 3.9 . Proceeding to the characterization of the associated eigenvector, let |\mu\rangle=(\mu_{1},\ldots,1,\ldots,\mu_{n})^{\intercal} as stated above, and let us consider U\Psi_{1}|\mu\rangle . Note that paths k\to\ell and paths \ell\to j are annihilated by \zeta_{\ell} , so it follows that \langle k|U\Psi_{1}|\ell\rangle\langle\ell|U\Psi_{1}|j\rangle=0 when \ell\neq j . Thus, the k th component of U\Psi_{1}\mathbf{v} is given by 
 \displaystyle\langle k|U\Psi_{1}|\mu\rangle 
 \displaystyle=\lambda_{j}\langle k|U\Psi_{1}|j\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell\neq j}\langle k|U\Psi_{1}|\ell\rangle\langle\ell|U\Psi_{1}|j\rangle 
 \displaystyle=\lambda_{j}\mu_{k}, 
 which shows that U\Psi_{1}|\mu\rangle=\lambda_{j}|\mu\rangle .
∎

 Hadamard Decomposition 
 Observing that each zeon generator can be written as a product of disjoint fermion annihilators (or creators), we have an obvious decomposition of the nilpotent adjacency matrix into an Hadamard product of fermion adjacency matrices. 
 Letting \mathfrak{F} denote the algebra of fermion annihilators generated by \{f_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\} , zeon generators can be defined by \zeta_{\{j\}}=f_{2j-1}f_{2j} for j\in\mathbb{N} . The nilpotent adjacency matrix \Phi of a graph G=(V,E) on n vertices can then be written as the Hadamard product of two fermion adjacency matrices associated with G . In particular, \Phi=\Phi_{1}\odot\Phi_{2} , where \Phi_{1} and \Phi_{2} are determined by 
 \displaystyle\langle i|\Phi_{1}|j\rangle=\begin{cases}f_{2j-1}&\{v_{i},v_{j}\}\in E,\\
0&\text{\rm otherwise,}\end{cases}\hskip 12.0pt 
 \displaystyle\langle i|\Phi_{2}|j\rangle=\begin{cases}f_{2j}&\{v_{i},v_{j}\}\in E,\\
0&\text{\rm otherwise.}\end{cases} 
 It is well known 2 2 
 2 
 See, for example, [ 2 ] . that the Hadamard product is related to the Kronecker product by 
 \Phi_{1}\odot\Phi_{2}=J(\Phi_{1}\otimes\Phi_{2})J^{\dagger}, 
 where J is an appropriately constructed selection matrix. When \Phi_{1},\Phi_{2} are square matrices of order n , J is the n\times n^{2} matrix 
 J=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{e}_{1}&\mathbf{e}_{n+2}&\mathbf{e}_{2n+3}&\cdots&\mathbf{e}_{n^{2}}\end{pmatrix}^{\intercal}, 
 whose n rows \mathbf{e}_{j} are unit coordinate vectors of \mathbb{C}^{n^{2}} . 
 The nilpotent (zeon) adjacency matrix is then written in terms of fermion adjacency matrices via \Phi=J(\Phi_{1}\otimes\Phi_{2})J^{\dagger} . It is worth noting that J^{\dagger}J is a rank- n orthogonal projection. 
 Letting \Theta=J^{\dagger}J(\Phi_{1}\otimes\Phi_{2}) , we find that for each positive integer m\geq 2 , 
 \displaystyle\Phi^{m} 
 \displaystyle=(J(\Phi_{1}\otimes\Phi_{2})J^{\dagger})^{m} 
 \displaystyle=J\Theta^{m}J^{\dagger}. 
 Hence, 
 \displaystyle\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\kappa(\Phi)}\frac{\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}^{\ell}}{\ell!}\Phi^{\ell} 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\kappa(\Phi)}\frac{\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}^{\ell}}{\ell!}J\Theta^{\ell}J^{\dagger} 
 \displaystyle=J\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\kappa(\Phi)}\frac{\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}^{\ell}}{\ell!}\Theta^{\ell}\right)J^{\dagger} 
 \displaystyle=J\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Theta)J^{\dagger}. 
 4 Concluding Remarks 
 The motivation for considering unitary and weakly unitary zeon matrices is twofold. First, they arise naturally as exponentials of nilpotent adjacency matrices. Second, they provide zeon-algebraic analogues of unitary operators, which are of particular importance in quantum probability and quantum computation. Some avenues of further exploration follow. 
 Zeon Matrix Groups 
 The preliminary investigation of unitary zeon matrices leads to a much larger problem of classifying the various zeon matrix groups and subgroups. As we have seen, the groups \mathfrak{U}_{n} , \mathfrak{W}_{n} , and \mathfrak{G}_{n} satisfy \mathfrak{U}_{n}\leq\mathfrak{W}_{n}\leq\mathfrak{G}_{n} . Further, the group \mathfrak{I}_{n}=\{\mathbb{I}+\Psi:\Psi\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}^{\circ})\}\leq\mathfrak{G}_{n} . 
 The relationships among zeon matrix groups, including chains and lattices, are yet to be investigated. The relationships among weakly unitary matrices obtained from orthogonal decompositions of nilpotent adjacency matrix exponentials could be interesting–particularly from a graph-theoretic perspective. 
 Zeon Lie Theory 
 The algebra \mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}^{\circ}) of nilpotent zeon matrices constitutes a Lie algebra with the bracket [X,Y]=XY-YX . The exponential map \mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}:\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}^{\circ})\to\mathfrak{G}_{n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}) then satisfies \mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(X)=\mathbb{I}+\Psi where \Psi\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}^{\circ}) . In fact, the scalar part of the determinant satisfies \mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}(|\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(X)|)=1 for every X\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}^{\circ}) . 
 For any nilpotent adjacency matrix \Phi , the exponential map satisfies |\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Phi)|=1 . Moreover, given any linear combination \Psi=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\Phi_{j} of nilpotent adjacency matrices, it can be shown that |\mathfrak{exp}\mspace{1mu}(\mathbbm{i}\mspace{1mu}\Psi)|=1 .
One obvious question is: What can be said about Lie groups in this context?
