                                                  Abstract. Let L be a planar semimodular lattice. We call L slim, if it has

                                                      In 2016, I proved a property of congruences of SPS lattices (Two-cover

                          2. Finite distributive lattices
   Small distributive lattices are easy to compute in. Larger ones, not so much.
Birkhoff duality (see G. Birkhoff [2]) provides a solution.
   For a finite ordered set P , call A ⊆ P a down set iff x ∈ A and y ≤ x in P ,
imply that y ∈ A. For H ⊆ P , there is a smallest down set containing H, namely,
{ x | x ≤ h, for some h ∈ H }; we use the notation ↓ H for this set. If H = {a},
we write ↓ a for ↓ {a}. Let Dn P denote the set of all down sets ordered by set
inclusion. Then Dn P is a finite distributive lattice.
   Conversely, for a finite distributive lattice D, let P denote the ordered set of
join-irreducible elements of D.
         Birkhoff duality.
     (i) The assignment P → Dn P is a bijection between finite ordered sets and finite
         distributive lattices. It assigns a ∈ P to ↓ a ∈ Dn P .
    (ii) The inverse map is D → J(D). It assigns a ∈ D to id(a) ∩ P .
   Figure 1 provides an example. The finite distributive lattice D on the left has
20 elements and a fairly complicated structure. The corresponding finite ordered
set P on the right has only 6 elements.




           Figure 1. A finite distributive lattice and the corresponding finite
           ordered set
   For a finite lattice L, the congruences form a finite distributive lattice, Con L.
The corresponding finite ordered set is the ordered set of join-irreducible congru-
ences, J(Con L). For a ≺ b in L, we can form con(a, b), the smallest congruence
of L collapsing a and b. Then con(a, b) ∈ J(Con L) and conversely.

                                  3. How it started
   The first result in this field is in a 1942 paper, N. Funayama and T. Nakayama
[34]: the congruence lattice of a lattice is distributive. So the congruence lattice of
a finite lattice is a finite distributive lattice.
            AN OPEN PROBLEM ON CONGRUENCES OF FINITE LATTICES                         3


  The converse was discovered by R. P. Dilworth, unpublished, circa 1944 (see the
book, K. P. Bogart, R. Freese, and J. P. S. Kung, eds, [3] for a discussion).
Theorem 1. Every finite distributive lattice D can be represented as the congruence
lattice of a finite lattice L.
  The first proof of the this result was published in 1962 (see G. Grätzer and E. T.
Schmidt [59]); it represented D with a sectionally complemented lattice L.

                 4. Representing with semimodular latices
   Can we represent every finite distributive lattice D as Con L, the congruence
lattice of a finite semimodular lattice L?
   Or, equivalently, can we represent every finite ordered set P as J(Con)L, the
ordered set of join-irreducible congruences of a finite semimodular lattice L?
   Indeed, we can, as verified in G. Grätzer, H. Lakser, and E. T. Schmidt [58].
Theorem 2. Every finite distributive lattice D can be represented as the congruence
lattice of a finite planar semimodular lattice L.
   It was a great surprise to us that the proof yielded a planar lattice.

properly in the right corner. In the rest of the lattice we do the same: we look

same color generate the same congruence.
   Finally, we remember the element e ∈ P . We add a “tail” to the lattice and
color it e.
   The resulting lattice L is planar and semimodular and J(Con L) is isomorphic
to P with the isomorphism x 7→ con(x) for x ∈ {a, b, c, d, e}.

                                       5. The Problem
   According to Theorem 2, every finite distributive lattice D can be represented as
the congruence lattice of a finite planar semimodular lattice L. In the construction,
we strongly utilize M3 sublattices. Could we do the construction without them?
   Let us call a finite planar semimodular lattice L slim, if it has no M3 sublattice.
Let us define an SPS lattice as a slim, finite, planar semimodular lattice L without
an M3 sublattice. (SPS stands for Slim, Planar, Semimodular.)
   In the paper [43], I proposed the following.

                         6. The Two-cover Property
   A finite ordered set P satisfies the Two-Cover Condition (see my paper [43]),
if any element of P has at most two covers. Figure 7 shows the diagram of an
ordered set F3 failing the Two-Cover Condition. A finite ordered set P satisfies the
Two-Cover Condition iff F3 has no cover-preserving embedding into P .
   The following is the main result of my paper [43].
Theorem 3 (Two-Cover Theorem). The ordered set of join-irreducible congruences
of an SPS lattice L has the Two-Cover Condition.
   The Two-Cover Condition is a necessary condition. G. Czédli [7] proves that it
is not sufficient. My paper [41] provides a short proof.
   Let us say that a finite ordered set P satisfies the Two-max Condition, if P has
at least two maximal elements.

Theorem 4 (Two-max Theorem). The ordered set of join-irreducible congruences

7.2. Natural diagrams and C1 -diagrams. As in my joint paper with E. Knapp

chain has exactly one doubly-irreducible element, rc(D), and these elements are
complementary, that is,
                                 lc(D) ∨ rc(D) = 1,

7.3. Lamps. Let L be a SR lattice. On the edges of L with meet-irreducible
                                 in
bottoms, the internal swing, p  x   q, is a reflexive, symmetric, and transitive binary
relation. So these edges are partitioned into equivalence classes; G. Czédli [13] calls
them lamps.
   The concept of a lamp is a crucial tool for the discovery of most deep properties
of congruence lattices of SPS lattices. It is a very deep and technical concept, and
it will await Czédli to write an introduction to the field in the style of this paper.
   G. Czédli [18] also utilizes lamps to find an infinite family of independent prop-
erties of congruence lattices of SPS lattices.

    There are a number of ways to view how congruences spread in SP lattices. The
first approach uses trajectories, see G. Czédli [6] and my paper [44]. The second is
the Swing Lemma. The third is lamps. They are very similar in some ways, but
quite different technically.

   In this section, I sketch the proofs of the Partition and the Two-pendant Four-
crown Properties of congruences of SR lattices, based on my paper [48].
The Partition Property. We start with a lemma.
Lemma 14. Let X and Y be distinct edges on the upper-left boundary of K. Then
there is no edge Z of K such that
(3)                               col Z ≺ col X, col Y.
Proof. By way of contradiction, let Z be an edge such that (3) holds. Since X
and Y are on the upper-left boundary, there exist normal-up edges SX , SY and
steep edges TX , TY such that

                 X ∼ SX   x    TX ,   Y ∼ SY   x    TY ,   Z ∈ TX ∩ TY .
The third formula implies that TX = TY . Since SX and SY are normal-up, it
follows that X = Y , contradicting the assumption that X and Y are distinct. 

   The set of maximal elements of P is the same as the set of colors of edges in
the upper boundaries. The latter we can partition into the set of edges L in the
upper-left boundary and the set of edges R in the upper-right boundary. Let X
and Y be distinct edges in L. By Lemma 14, there is no edge Z of K such that
col Z ≺ col X, col Y . By symmetry, this verifies the Partition Property.

The Two-pendant Four-crown Property. By way of contradiction, assume
that the ordered set R of Figure 16 is a cover-preserving ordered subset of P,

upper boundary of K, so that col A = a, col B = b, col C = c, col D = d. By

of K. Since p ≺ a, b in P, it follows from Lemma 14 that the edge B is on the
upper-right boundary of K, and so is D. Similarly, C is on the upper-left boundary
of K.

                              11. One more problem
   Let D be a finite distributive lattice of the form Dh uB2 , the glued sum of a finite
distributive lattice Dh with B2 . One can think of Dh as D with the hat removed.

    Recall that a rectangular lattice L is a patch lattice, if the corners are dual atoms.
It is easy to verify that a rectangular lattice L is a patch lattice iff Con L is a glued
sum of a finite distributive lattice Conh L with B2 .
Patch Problem. Characterize the congruence lattice of a patch lattice L in terms
of the properties of the finite distributive lattice Conh L.
