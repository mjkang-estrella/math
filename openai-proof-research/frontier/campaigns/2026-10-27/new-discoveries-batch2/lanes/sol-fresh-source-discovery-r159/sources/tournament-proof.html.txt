 Random Tournaments Are Asymptotically Almost Surely Link-irregular 
 
 
 Lily Zhang
 † † thanks: Department of Mathematics, University of California, Berkeley. Email address: pink@berkeley.edu . 
 October 17, 2026 

 Abstract 
 
 
 Bastien and Khormali (2025) conjectured that a nontrivial link-irregular tournament with n vertices exists if and only if n\geq 6 . Their work establishes the conjecture for n\leq 100 . We present a probabilistic argument for the existence of link-irregular tournaments with n vertices when n\geq 9 to resolve the conjecture. In fact, we show that a random tournament is asymptotically almost surely link-irregular. 
 
 
 
 
 Keywords: Link-irregular digraphs, directed graphs, random tournaments, probabilistic method. 
 
 
 
 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 05C20, 05D40, 05C80. 
 
 
 
 1 Introduction 

 
 
 The study of links of graphs was first introduced by Ali, Chartrand, and Zhang [ 1 ] . This study has since been extended to links of directed graphs by Bastien and Khormali [ 2 ] . However, for the practical purposes of this paper, we assert the definitions involving link for directed graphs only. 
 
 
 
 Given a vertex v\in V(G) of a directed graph, we denote its neighborhood (set of in-neighbors and out-neighbors) by N(v) . The link of a vertex v\in G is defined by 
 

 
 
 L(v)=G[N(v)]. 
 
 
 
 In other words, L(v) is the subgraph formed by the neighborhood of v\in G . G is link-irregular if for all distinct u,v\in G , 
 

 
 
 L(u)\not\cong L(v). 
 
 
 
 
 
 It’s easy to see that link-irregular tournaments are directed graphs T which satisfy for all distinct u,v\in V(T) : 
 

 
 
 T-u\not\cong T-v. 
 
 
 
 Bastien and Khormali have conjectured that nontrivial link-irregular tournaments with n vertices exist if and only if n\geq 6 . They prove that the conjecture holds up to n=100 [ 2 ] . 
 
 
 
 In what follows, we resolve the conjecture by proving that link-irregular tournaments with n vertices exist when n\geq 9 . Our work invokes a probabilistic argument, which does not provide explicit examples of such directed graphs. However, the robustness of probabilistic reasoning provides us with a proof that uniformly random tournaments with n vertices are asymptotically almost surely link-irregular. 
 
 
 
 Both of the main results have been formalized in the Lean 4 proof assistant and are available on GitHub [ 5 ] .

 It goes without saying that we may assume without loss of generality that V(T)=\{1,\dotsc,n\} .

 \binom{S}{k}=\{A\subseteq S:\left\lvert A\right\rvert=k\} 
 
 
 
 to denote the set of subsets of S with k elements. 
 
 
 
 For a permutation \sigma\in\operatorname{Sym}(S) of a finite set, the action \underline{\cdot}:\langle\sigma\rangle\times\binom{S}{2}\to\binom{S}{2} is defined by 
 

 
 
 \sigma^{m}\cdot\{x,y\}=\{\sigma^{m}(x),\sigma^{m}(y)\}. 
 
 
 
 We denote o_{2}(\sigma)=\left\lvert\binom{S}{2}/\langle\sigma\rangle\right\rvert to be the amount of orbits of the action \langle\sigma\rangle on \binom{S}{2} .

 3 Proof of Existence 

 
 
 Our goal is to prove that if n\geq 9 , then there exists a link-irregular tournament with n vertices, whence the conjecture follows from the facts we mentioned in the introduction. We do this via Theorem 6 , which additionally shows that random tournaments are asymptotically almost surely link-irregular. 
 
 
 
 There is notation that we wish to introduce that’s only applicable for Lemma 2 and Theorem 3 . 
 
 
 
 Given distinct u,v\in\{1,\dotsc,n\} and a bijection 
 

 
 
 \varphi\colon\{1,\dotsc,n\}\setminus\{u\}\to\{1,\dotsc,n\}\setminus\{v\}, 
 
 
 
 define the event 
 

 
 
 B_{\varphi}=\{\varphi\text{ is an isomorphism from }T_{n}-u\text{ to }T_{n}-v\}. 
 
 
 
 For every x\not=u , draw the directed edge x\longrightarrow\varphi(x) . Using the convention that loops x\longrightarrow x are directed cycles of length 1 , let C_{\varphi} be the set of vertices on directed cycles, and define \sigma_{\varphi}\colon C_{\varphi}\to C_{\varphi} via \sigma_{\varphi}(x)=\varphi(x) .

 Lemma 2 . 
 
 
 Let n\geq 2 be an integer, let u,v\in\{1,\dotsc,n\} be distinct, and let
 \varphi\colon\{1,\dotsc,n\}\setminus\{u\}\to\{1,\dotsc,n\}\setminus\{v\} be a bijection. Then 
 

 
 
 \mathbb{P}(B_{\varphi})\leq 2^{-\binom{n-1}{2}+o_{2}(\sigma_{\varphi})}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 Let D_{\varphi} be the directed graph whose vertices are V(D_{\varphi})=\binom{\{1,\dotsc,n\}}{2} , and the \binom{n-1}{2} directed edges are 
 

 
 
 \{x,y\}\longrightarrow\{\varphi(x),\varphi(y)\} 
 
 
 
 where x,y\not=u . 
 
 \{v,c_{1}\} \{a,c_{2}\} \{b,c_{3}\} \{u,c_{1}\} \{c_{1},c_{2}\} \{c_{2},c_{3}\} \{c_{3},c_{1}\} 
 Figure 2: A path component and a cycle component of D_{\varphi} . 
 
 
 
 A vertex \{x,y\}\in V(D_{\varphi}) lies on a directed cycle if and only if x,y\in C_{\varphi} , indicating that the directed cycles of D_{\varphi} are precisely the orbits of f:\binom{C_{\varphi}}{2}\to\binom{C_{\varphi}}{2} by 
 

 
 
 f(\{x,y\})=\{\sigma_{\varphi}(x),\sigma_{\varphi}(y)\}. 
 
 
 
 So there are o_{2}(\sigma_{\varphi}) connected components that are directed cycles. 
 
 
 
 Since \varphi is a bijection, each vertex \{x,y\}\in V(D_{\varphi}) satisfies
 \deg^{-}(\{x,y\}),\deg^{+}(\{x,y\})\leq 1 . So every connected component of D_{\varphi} is either a directed path or a directed cycle. Therefore the amount of connected components of D_{\varphi} is 
 

 
 
 r=\left\lvert V(D_{\varphi})\right\rvert-\left\lvert E(D_{\varphi})\right\rvert+o_{2}(\sigma_{\varphi})=\binom{n}{2}-\binom{n-1}{2}+o_{2}(\sigma_{\varphi}). 
 
 
 
 
 
 Assuming B_{\varphi} occurs, the orientation of one unordered pair in T_{n} determines the orientations of all unordered pairs within this connected component. We conclude at most 2^{r} tournaments for which B_{\varphi} occurs, thus 
 

 
 
 \mathbb{P}(B_{\varphi})\leq\frac{2^{r}}{2^{\binom{n}{2}}}=\frac{2^{\binom{n}{2}-\binom{n-1}{2}+o_{2}(\sigma_{\varphi})}}{2^{\binom{n}{2}}}=2^{-\binom{n-1}{2}+o_{2}(\sigma_{\varphi})}, 
 
 
 
 exactly as desired.
∎ 
 
 
 
 Theorem 3 . 
 
 
 Let n\geq 2 be an integer. Let u,v\in\{1,\dotsc,n\} be distinct. Then 
 

 
 
 \mathbb{P}(T_{n}-u\cong T_{n}-v)\leq(n-2)!\cdot 2^{-\binom{n-1}{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}g_{k}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 Taking the union over all bijections
 \varphi:\{1,\dotsc,n\}\setminus\{u\}\to\{1,\dotsc,n\}\setminus\{v\} gives the equality of events 
 

 
 
 \{T_{n}-u\cong T_{n}-v\}=\bigcup_{\varphi}B_{\varphi}. 
 
 
 
 Applying the union bound and Lemma 2 , we obtain 
 

 
 
 \mathbb{P}(T_{n}-u\cong T_{n}-v)\leq\sum_{\varphi}\mathbb{P}(B_{\varphi})\leq 2^{-\binom{n-1}{2}}\sum_{\varphi}2^{o_{2}(\sigma_{\varphi})}. 
 
 
 
 Our goal is to prove the sufficient inequality 
 

 
 
 \sum_{\varphi}2^{o_{2}(\sigma_{\varphi})}\leq(n-2)!\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}g_{k}. 
 
 
 
 Fix k\in\{0,\dotsc,n-2\} . We wish to evaluate the left sum restricted to \left\lvert C_{\varphi}\right\rvert=k 
 

 
 
 \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\\
\left\lvert C_{\varphi}\right\rvert=k\end{subarray}}2^{o_{2}(\sigma_{\varphi})}. 
 
 
 
 • 
 
 
 There are \binom{n-2}{k} ways to choose the k vertices out of the n-2 vertices that aren’t u,v to determine C_{\varphi} . 
 
 • 
 
 
 For a fixed C\in\binom{\{1,\dotsc,n\}\setminus\{u,v\}}{k} and permutation \sigma:C\to C , there are (n-k-2)! ways to order the remaining n-k-2 vertices to form the directed path from v to u . So there are (n-k-2)! bijections \varphi with C_{\varphi}=C , \sigma_{\varphi}=\sigma . 
 
 • 
 
 
 For a fixed C_{\varphi} , summing 2^{o_{2}(\sigma_{\varphi})} over all permutations of C_{\varphi} yields 
 

 
 
 \sum_{\sigma\in\operatorname{Sym}(C_{\varphi})}2^{o_{2}(\sigma)}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}2^{o_{2}(\sigma)}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Taking the sum from k=0 to n-2 , we obtain 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle\sum_{\varphi}2^{o_{2}(\sigma_{\varphi})} 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\\
\left\lvert C_{\varphi}\right\rvert=k\end{subarray}}2^{o_{2}(\sigma_{\varphi})} 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\binom{n-2}{k}\cdot(n-k-2)!\cdot\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}2^{o_{2}(\sigma)} 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\binom{n-2}{k}\cdot(n-k-2)!\cdot k!\cdot g_{k} 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=(n-2)!\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}g_{k}, 
 
 
 
 where the third equality follows from Burnside’s Lemma. In fact, the sufficient inequality we wanted to prove is an equality!
∎ 
 
 
 
 Lemma 4 . 
 
 
 Let n\geq 8 be an integer. Then 
 

 
 
 g_{n}<2\cdot\frac{2^{\binom{n}{2}}}{n!}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 Buhrman, Li, Tromp, and Vitányi [ 3 , Corollary 2] already supply the asymptotically stronger 
 

 
 
 g_{n}\leq\frac{2^{\binom{n}{2}}}{n!}\left(1+\frac{4n^{4}}{2^{n}}\right). 
 
 
 
 We merely show that 2^{n}>4n^{4} for n\geq 19 via induction, then we perform a finite check on n\in[8,18] . 
 
 
 
 Base case ( n=19 ): 2^{19}=524288>521284=4\cdot 19^{4} . 
 
 
 
 Induction step: Assuming it for n-1 , we just show after dividing by 2^{n-1}>4(n-1)^{4} that 
 

 
 
 2>\frac{n^{4}}{(n-1)^{4}}. 
 
 
 
 This is true since the fourth root of the right side is 
 

 
 
 \frac{n}{n-1}\leq\frac{20}{19}<1.18<\sqrt[4]{2}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Now we establish the inequality for n\in[8,18] . This is shown through exhaustive casework using the values of g_{n} explicitly listed in Lemma 1 . Let h_{n}=2\cdot\frac{2^{\binom{n}{2}}}{n!} , so we need to show g_{n}<h_{n} .  Lemma 5 . 
 
 
 Let n\geq 7 be an integer. Then 
 

 
 
 \sum_{k=0}^{n}g_{k}<4\cdot\frac{2^{\binom{n}{2}}}{n!} 
 
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 We show this via induction on n . 
 
 
 
 Base case ( n=7 ): Just compare 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle g_{0}+\dotsb+g_{7}=1+1+2 
 \displaystyle+4+11+34+156+1044=1253. 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle 4\cdot\frac{2^{\binom{7}{2}}}{7!} 
 \displaystyle=\frac{2^{23}}{5040}=1664+\frac{128}{315}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Induction step: Assuming it true for n-1 , we derive using Lemma 4 that 
 

 
 
 \sum_{k=0}^{n}g_{k}<4\cdot\frac{2^{\binom{n-1}{2}}}{(n-1)!}+2\cdot\frac{2^{\binom{n}{2}}}{n!}. 
 
 
 
 It’s enough to show 
 

 
 
 2\cdot\frac{2^{\binom{n-1}{2}}}{(n-1)!}\leq\frac{2^{\binom{n}{2}}}{n!}, 
 
 
 
 where division reduces this to 
 

 
 
 \frac{2^{\frac{n(n-1)-(n-1)(n-2)}{2}}}{n}=\frac{2^{n-1}}{n}\geq 2. 
 
 
 
 Obviously this is true for n\geq 8 .
∎ 
 
 
 
 Theorem 6 . 
 
 
 If n\geq 9 is an integer, then there exists a link-irregular tournament with n vertices. In fact, T_{n} is asymptotically almost surely link-irregular. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 Let u,v\in\{1,\dotsc,n\} be distinct. 
 
 
 
 If n-2\geq 8 , combining Theorem 3 and Lemma 5 results in the chain of inequalities 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{n}-u\cong T_{n}-v) 
 \displaystyle\leq(n-2)!\cdot 2^{-\binom{n-1}{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}g_{k} 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle<(n-2)!\cdot 2^{-\binom{n-1}{2}}\cdot 4\cdot\frac{2^{\binom{n-2}{2}}}{(n-2)!} 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=4\cdot 2^{\frac{(n-2)(n-3)-(n-1)(n-2)}{2}} 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=2^{-n+4}. 
 
 
 
 Taking the union bound over all \binom{n}{2} unordered pairs u\not=v of \{1,\dotsc,n\} , we derive 
 

 
 
 \mathbb{P}(T_{n}\text{ is not link-irregular})<\underbrace{\binom{n}{2}\cdot 2^{-n+4}}_{a_{n}}\;\overset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\;0. 
 
 
 
 This gives us the second part of the theorem. 
 
 
 
 It’s not hard to show that a_{n} is decreasing in n , and also 
 

 
 
 a_{10}=\binom{10}{2}\cdot 2^{-10+4}=\frac{45}{64}<1. 
 
 
 
 Hence the first part of the theorem for n\geq 10 . 
 
 
 
 We handle the n=9 case separately, which follows via directly applying a similar union bound and Theorem 3 : 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{9}\text{ is not link-irregular}) 
 \displaystyle\leq\binom{9}{2}\cdot 7!\cdot 2^{-\binom{8}{2}}(g_{0}+\dotsb+g_{7}) 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=\frac{36\cdot 7!\cdot 1253}{2^{28}} 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=\frac{3552255}{4194304} 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle<1. 
 
 
 
 ∎
