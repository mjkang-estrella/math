                                                   symmetric function in which the variable t records the number of distinct colors used in a proper coloring.
                                                   Although ZG (x; t) is equivalent to the chromatic symmetric function as a graph invariant, its principal
                                                   specializations retain substantially more information than those of the chromatic symmetric function. This

                                              The chromatic symmetric function of a graph was introduced by Stanley in the mid-1990s as a symmetric-
                                           function analogue of the chromatic polynomial [22]. Let x = (x1 , x2 , . . .) be a sequence of commuting
                                           variables. For a simple graph G = (V, E), the chromatic symmetric function of G is

                                           where PC G denotes the set of proper colorings of the vertices of G with colors from N, and xκ = v∈V xκ(v) .

                                           Although the chromatic polynomial, χG (k), records only the number of proper colorings using a set of k
                                           colors, the chromatic symmetric function retains information about how the colors are distributed among
                                           the vertices. In particular, two graphs with the same chromatic polynomial can have different chromatic
                                           symmetric functions.
                                              Since its introduction, several generalizations and specializations of the chromatic symmetric function
                                           have been developed, in part motivated by the question of how much information about a graph is encoded
                                           by its proper colorings. Gebhard and Sagan introduced a chromatic symmetric function in noncommuting
                                           variables, YG (y), that retains information about which vertices receive which colors and satisfies a deletion-
                                           contraction relation, providing a refinement of Stanley’s invariant [9]. Aliste-Prieto, de Mier, and Zamora [3]
                                           studied the U -polynomial of Noble and Welsh [19], which is equivalent to the chromatic symmetric function
                                           as an invariant of trees. Loehr and Warrington introduced a rooted version of the chromatic symmetric
                                           function [17]. Together, these developments highlight the broader question of how much information about
                                           a graph can be recovered from its proper colorings and how refinements of the chromatic symmetric function
                                           can improve its ability to distinguish non-isomorphic graphs.
                                              One of the central questions surrounding the chromatic symmetric function is whether it distinguishes
                                           trees. Despite the fact that all trees on the same number of vertices have the same chromatic polynomial,
                                           Stanley was unable to find two non-isomorphic trees with the same chromatic symmetric function [24,
                                           Problem 7.47(b)]. This led to what is now known as the Tree Isomorphism Conjecture: if T and T ′ are
                                           non-isomorphic trees, then
                                                                                         XT (x) ̸= XT ′ (x).

The conjecture has been verified for all trees with at most 29 vertices by Heil and Ji [12], and several
important families of trees are known to be distinguished by their chromatic symmetric functions [1, 4, 6, 8,
10, 13, 16, 18, 26, 28]. Nevertheless, the general conjecture remains open.
   A natural approach to Stanley’s question is to simplify the chromatic symmetric function while retaining
enough information to distinguish trees. One such simplification is obtained through principal specialization.
For a symmetric function f (x1 , x2 , . . .), its nth principal specialization is
                                       psn (f ) = f (1, q, q 2 , . . . , q n−1 , 0, 0, . . .).
For the chromatic symmetric function, the coefficient of q i in psn (XG ) counts proper n-colorings of G using
colors {0, 1, 2, . . . , n − 1} having a total chromatic sum of i. Thus, principal specialization turns a symmetric
function in infinitely many variables into a univariate polynomial encoding a weighted enumeration of proper
colorings.
   Principal specializations have been studied in connection with the Tree Isomorphism Conjecture. Loehr
and Warrington conjectured that psn (XT ) distinguishes trees with at most n vertices [17], and verified the
conjecture for trees with at most 17 vertices. More recently, Bajo, Beck, and Vindas-Meléndez introduced
the q-chromatic polynomial and showed that its leading coefficient is equivalent to the stable principal spe-
cialization of the chromatic symmetric function [5]. They conjectured that this stable principal specialization
distinguishes trees. These developments suggest that principal specialization may provide a useful way to
extract structural information from the chromatic symmetric function and a way to approach the Tree
Isomorphism Conjecture.
   In [21], Shareshian and Wachs introduced a t-analogue of the chromatic symmetric function. This resulted
in a quasisymmetric function that was useful to address other conjectures related to the chromatic symmetric
function. In this paper, we introduce a new t-analogue of the chromatic symmetric function
                                                             X
                                                 ZG (x; t) =      tℓ(κ) xκ ,
                                                                κ∈PC G

where ℓ(κ) is the number of distinct colors used by κ. Our t-analogue is symmetric and we believe it is better
suited to help with the Tree Isomorphism Conjecture.
   We focus on principal specializations of this function,
                                   psn (ZG (x; t)) := ZG (1, q, . . . , q n−1 , 0, . . . ; t),
which is a bivariate polynomial. The coefficient of q i tj in this polynomial counts proper n-colorings of G
using colors {0, 1, 2, . . . , n − 1} having a chromatic sum of i that use exactly j distinct colors. Thus, unlike
the principal specialization of XG , the specialization of ZG retains information about the number of colors
used by a coloring. In particular, although ZG and XG are equivalent as graph invariants, their principal
specializations need not be equivalent. Indeed, there are graphs G and H for which psn (XG ) = psn (XH )
but psn (ZG ) ̸= psn (ZH ) (see Examples 3.10 and 3.12).
    The additional information contained in psn (ZT (x; t)) is particularly useful for recovering structural prop-
erties of trees. In Figure 1, we show how our new function and its specializations relate to objects in the
literature.
    Our primary focus is on caterpillars. A caterpillar is a tree obtained by attaching leaves to the vertices
of a path, called the spine of the caterpillar. A caterpillar can naturally be represented by a composition
α = (α1 , . . . , αℓ ), where ℓ is the number of vertices in the spine, α1 , αℓ ≥ 2, and αi − 1 is the number of leaves
attached to the ith spine vertex. Two such caterpillars are isomorphic precisely when their corresponding
compositions are equal up to reversal. A bistar is a caterpillar with ℓ = 2 and an elongated bistar is a
caterpillar with corresponding composition (a, 1r , b) such that a ≥ b ≥ 2 and ℓ ≥ 0.
    Our first results describe the information about caterpillars that is already visible in the second prin-
cipal specialization. In Theorem 3.16, we show that for a connected bipartite graph, the coefficient of t2
in psn (ZG (x; t)) records the unordered pair of bipartition sizes. Consequently, ps2 (ZG (x; t)) allows us to
recover the sizes of the two bipartition classes of a caterpillar. We use this observation to characterize the
ps2 -classes of caterpillars in Theorem 3.19. In particular, we describe all caterpillars having a prescribed
second principal specialization and determine the elongated bistars (Theorem 3.22) and three-spine-vertex
caterpillars (Theorem 3.23) that occur in these classes.
    The second principal specialization does not, however, distinguish caterpillars in general. This leads us
to consider the third principal specialization. Our main results show that ps3 (ZG (x; t)) contains enough

   The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review the necessary background on
compositions, graphs, colorings, symmetric functions, and the chromatic symmetric function. In Section 3,
we introduce the t-chromatic symmetric function ZG (x; t) and its principal specializations, establish their
basic properties, and characterize the second principal specialization classes of caterpillars. In Section 4,
we give an explicit reconstruction algorithm for elongated bistars from their third principal specializations.
In Section 5, we prove the corresponding reconstruction theorem for caterpillars with three spine vertices.
Finally, in Section 6, we discuss limitations of the third principal specialization and formulate directions for
future work.

Remark 3.18. The minimum q-degree of psn (ZG (x; t)) is the same as that of psn (XG (x)). For a tree, this
common value need not be equal to the size of the smallest part of the unique bipartition. In other words,
a coloring of the tree of minimal weight (which contributes to the minimal q-degree) is not always achieved
by a 2-coloring. The problem of finding a coloring of minimal weight of a graph, called the chromatic sum
problem, was first investigated by Kubicka [14] who showed it to be an NP-complete problem for general
graphs. For trees, she constructed for each positive integer k a family of trees whose chromatic sum requires
at least k colors to be achieved. As a consequence, the bipartition of a tree may be much harder to recover
from psn (XG (x)) than it is to recover from psn (ZG (x; t)).

However, we have been unable to find two nonisomorphic caterpillars with the same ps4 (ZT (x; t)).
Conjecture 6.1. ps4 (ZT (x; t)) distinguishes caterpillars.
  What is more, it seems this is likely to hold at t = 1.
Conjecture 6.2. ps4 (XT (x)) distinguishes caterpillars.
   We have verified the above conjectures on caterpillars with at most 25 vertices, as well as caterpillars
with 5 internal vertices with 30, 35, 40, 45 and 50 total vertices. In fact, we have been unable to find two
nonisomorphic trees with fewer than 26 vertices that have the same ps4 (XT (x)). We also record t-versions
of the Loehr–Warrington and Bajo–Beck–Vindas–Melendes conjecture. These are strictly weaker than their
counterparts.
Conjecture 6.3. For each n ≥ 2, psn (ZT (x; t)) distinguishes trees with at most n vertices.
Conjecture 6.4. ps(ZT (x; t)) distinguishes trees.

                          Appendix A. Counting elongated bistar colorings
     Here we prove some auxiliary lemmas that are used in Section 4. Recall that for the elongated bistar
(a, 1r , b), we have the leaf sets A, B, and spine vertices vA and vB . We will compute certain coefficients
[q s t3 ]ps3 (Z(a,1r ,b) ) by counting the contributions from each set of colorings where vA and vB are assigned
fixed colors from among {0, 1, 2}. These endpoint colorings will be denoted, for example, ⟨2, 0⟩ to indicate
that vA is colored 2 and vB is colored 0.
Lemma A.1. Let (a, 1r , b) be an elongated bistar with a ≥ b ≥ 2. Then for all r ≥ 0 even, we have
                          [q ⌊r/2⌋+3 t3 ]ps3 (Z(a,1r ,b) ) = (⌊r/2⌋ + 2) (δb=2 + δa=2 ) + r + 2,
and if r is odd we have
                                       [q ⌊r/2⌋+3 t3 ]ps3 (Z(a,1r ,b) ) = ⌊r/2⌋ + 2.
Proof. First we handle the case with r even. We proceed by examining the contribution from each possible
endpoint coloring, of which there are 9.
     • ⟨0, 0⟩. Note that this endpoint coloring is only possible if r > 0. We need to color all of the vertices
        from A and B with at least a 1, and a valid r-spine coloring has minimum weight ⌊r/2⌋ + 2 for
        r > 0. But then since |A| ≥ |B| ≥ 1, any coloring will have total weight at least r/2 + 4, so this set
        of colorings contributes nothing to the coefficient.
     • ⟨1, 0⟩. An r-spine coloring has minimum weight ⌊r/2⌋ by using only 0’s and 1’s, and all the leaves
        in B must be colored at least 1. However if |B| > 1, then any proper coloring which includes a 2
        would have weight at least r/2 + 4, so we only have a contribution if b = 2. Supposing b = 2, if the
        r-spine is assigned the minimum weight coloring then the leaf in B must be colored 2 since if it were
        colored 1 it would be impossible to assign 2 to a leaf in A without exceeding the total weight. If
        r > 0, we can also do an r-spine coloring of weight ⌊r/2⌋ + 1 in one of ⌊r/2⌋ ways (substituting a 2
        for a 1 on the r-spine), and then color the leaf in B as 1. Hence, these endpoint colorings contribute
        (⌊r/2⌋ + 1)δb=2 .
     • ⟨2, 0⟩. A minimal r-spine coloring has weight ⌊r/2⌋, so we can only produce a coloring of desired
        weight if |B| = 1 in which case that leaf is assigned 1. Thus, the contribution is δb=2 .
     • ⟨0, 1⟩. Symmetrizing the argument given for the case ⟨1, 0⟩, we get a contribution of (⌊r/2⌋ + 1)δa=2 .
     • ⟨0, 2⟩. Symmetrizing the argument given for the case ⟨2, 0⟩, we get a contribution of δa=2 .
     • ⟨1, 1⟩. Any r-spine coloring must use a 2. Choose where on the r-spine to put a 2. The rest of the
        coloring is uniquely determined as the pattern of alternating 0’s and 1’s, starting with 0. Thus, this
        contributes r.
     • ⟨2, 2⟩. Any r-spine coloring has weight at least ⌊r/2⌋, so the total weight is already too large and
        this contributes 0.
     • ⟨1, 2⟩. The unique coloring which alternates 0’s and 1’s from left to right along the r-spine (that is,
        with the spine vertex adjacent to vA colored 0) and colors all leaves 0 is the only 3-coloring with the
