                                                           Regular Graphs Without Isomorphic Bisections

                                                                                          Elie Feinsilber
                                                                                      efeinsilber@wesleyan.edu




                                                                                              Abstract
                                                     A graph is bisectable if its vertex set can be split into two sets inducing isomorphic subgraphs.




arXiv:2610.35274v1 [math.CO] 26 Oct 2026
                                                 Ando conjectured that every cubic graph is bisectable, and Das, Pokrovskiy, and Sudakov proved
                                                 it for large enough connected cubic graphs. We give a constructive proof that, for every d ≥ 5,
                                                 there exists a non-bisectable d-regular graph of even order.

                                           Keywords: Graphs; regular graphs; isomorphic bisections; Ando’s conjecture; graph partitions.


                                           1    Introduction
                                           Partitions of a graph into isomorphic pieces have a long history, but mostly for edges. Isomorphic
                                           factorisations, i.e. partitions of E(G) into pairwise isomorphic spanning subgraphs, were studied
                                           systematically by Harary, Robinson and Wormald in a series of papers beginning with [5]. Jacobson
                                           and Schönheim separately asked for the largest s such that every graph with m edges contains two
                                           edge-disjoint isomorphic subgraphs with s edges; after bounds of Erdős, Pach and Pyber [4], Lee,
                                           Loh and Sudakov [6] showed that s = Θ (m log m)2/3 .
                                               In this paper, we consider the isomorphic bisection of a graph G: a triple (A, B, φ) with
                                           V (G) = A ⊔ B and φ an isomorphism from the subgraph induced by the vertex set A, G[A],
                                           onto G[B]. We say G is bisectable if it has an isomorphic bisection. We take all graphs to be finite,
                                           simple, and undirected, and use “bisection” as vocabulary shortcut for “isomorphic bisection”. For
                                           cubic graphs, bisectability is Ando’s conjecture, made in the 1990s and first stated in print in [1].
                                           It holds for 3-edge-colourable cubic graphs: two colour classes form a 2-factor of even cycles, and
                                           colouring each cycle alternately gives two halves that induce matchings of the same size [2, 1].
                                           Abreu, Goedgebeur, Labbate and Mazzuoccolo [1] verified the conjecture by computer for all cubic
                                           graphs with at most 32 vertices, and Das, Pokrovskiy and Sudakov [3] proved it for all sufficiently
                                           large connected cubic graphs.
                                               We show that the conjecture does not extend to d-regular graphs with d ≥ 5. It is clear that
                                           a bisectable graph must have even order, so for d even, Kd+1 is directly not bisectable for parity
                                           reasons alone. We go further and prove the theorem below using a construction which attaches a
                                           dense block to a circulant graph, or a graph of girth at least 5 depending on d.

                                           Theorem 4. For all d ≥ 5, there exists a connected d-regular graph of even order that is not
                                           bisectable.

                                               More precisely, for all d ≥ 5 and every even n ≥ 2d2 + 200 we show that there is a connected d-
                                           regular graph of order n that is not bisectable. It suffices that n ≥ 4d+8 if d is even, n ≥ 2d2 −d+3
                                           if d ≥ 7 is odd, and n ≥ 208 if d = 5.
                                               The construction begins with a dense block of odd order, which any partition of the vertices
                                           must split unevenly. Lemma 1 shows that this prevents an isomorphic bisection, provided every

2    Results
For even d let Wd = Kd+1 − uv, with u, v of degree d − 1. For odd d let Wd = Kd+2 − Fd , where
Fd is the disjoint union of a path qpz and a set Md of (d − 1)/2 further disjoint edges, with p of
degree d − 1. All other vertices have degree d in Wd , and |V (Wd )| is odd.

Lemma 1. Let d ≥ 5 and let G be a d-regular graph of even order with a set W of vertices such
that G[W ] ∼= Wd . Suppose that every triangle of G lies in W , and, if d = 5, that every induced
4-cycle of G lies in W . Then G is not bisectable.

Proof. Let F be Fd if d is odd, and the single edge uv if d is even. In either case G[W ] = KW − F ,
where F has maximum degree at most 2 and contains no triangle. For S ⊆ V (G) let τ (S) be the
set of vertices lying in a triangle of G[S]; by assumption τ (S) = τ (S ∩ W ).
    Remark that if S ⊆ W and |S| ≥ 6, or d is even and |S| ≥ 4, then τ (S) = S. Indeed, if |S| ≥ 6,
each vertex in S has at least |S| − 3 ≥ 3 neighbours in S since the “worst” case for the F removed
is the disjoint union of a two edge path and (d − 1)/2 disjoint edges. Two of any three of these
neighbours are adjacent because F is triangle-free and we are in a complete graph on at least 6
vertices. If d is even, G[S] is KS or KS minus an edge. Thus every vertex of S lies in a triangle.
    Now towards a contradiction, let (A, B, φ) be a bisection. So φ maps the triangles of G[A]
to those of G[B] and |τ (A ∩ W )| = |τ (B ∩ W )|. Let S = A ∩ W and k = |S|. Since |W | is
odd by construction, we may assume k = |A ∩ W | > |B ∩ W |. If τ (A ∩ W ) = A ∩ W then
|τ (B ∩ W )| ≤ |B ∩ W | < k, a contradiction. This settles even d ≥ 6: |V (Wd )| = d + 1 so
k = |S| ≥ (d + 2)/2 ≥ 4. As well as odd d ≥ 9 (where k ≥ (d + 3)/2 ≥ 6); and leaves k = 5 for
d = 7, and k ∈ {4, 5} for d = 5.
    First, for d = 7, k = 5. Let S = A ∩ W . If |τ (S)| = 5 we are done since |B ∩ W | = 4. So let
v ∈ S \τ (S). It has at least 5−1−2 = 2 neighbours in S, so it must have exactly two (else a triangle
would form), they are joined in F , and v has two F -neighbours in S. Hence (v = p), q, z ∈ S, and
the other two vertices of S form an edge of M7 . So either |τ (S)| = 5 > |B ∩ W |, contradicting
|τ (S)| = |τ (B ∩ W )|, or S = {p, q, z, r, r′ } with rr′ ∈ M7 . In the latter case, both {q, z, r} and
{q, z, r′ } are triangles, while p lies in none, so τ (S) = {q, z, r, r′ }. Meanwhile, B ∩ W consists of
the endpoints of the other two edges of M7 . Its induced subgraph is therefore K4 with two disjoint
edges deleted, i.e. a 4-cycle. Thus τ (B ∩ W ) = ∅, again contradicting |τ (S)| = |τ (B ∩ W )| (see
Figure 1).
    Now for d = 5 and k = 5, k = |A ∩ W | = 5 implies |B ∩ W | = 2, so we would need τ (A ∩ W ) = ∅
for the bisection. The four edges of F5 cover all seven vertices of W , so at most three of them lie
in the five vertices of A ∩ W , and G[A ∩ W ] is a graph on five vertices with at least 7 edges, thus
not triangle-free by Mantel’s theorem.
    Finally, let d = 5, k = 4. Any four vertices of W contain at most two edges of F5 , so G[A ∩ W ]
is K4 , K4 − e, C4 , or a triangle with a pendant edge (when three of the four vertices of A ∩ W
are {p, q, z}). In these cases, |τ (A ∩ W )| is 4, 4, 0, 3 respectively. |τ (A ∩ W )| = 4 would be a
direct contradiction. If |τ (A ∩ W )| = 3, then it is the triangle with pendant edge, which makes
|τ (B ∩ W )| = 3 impossible: p, q, z ∈ S so whichever three of the remaining four vertices are in

   Now we give a lemma that proves key to the construction of what will sit outside of Wd . For
m ≥ 3 and S ⊆ Zm \ {0} with S = −S, the circulant Cm (S) has vertex set Zm and x ∼ y iff
y − x ∈ S; it is |S|-regular. Let Im = {x ∈ Z : m/3 < x < 2m/3}, read modulo m.
Lemma 2. If S ⊆ Im and S = −S, then Cm (S) is triangle-free. If moreover S contains two
consecutive integers, Cm (S) is connected.
Proof. Im is sum-free modulo m: for x, y ∈ Im we have 2m/3 < x + y < 4m/3, so the residue of
x + y lies in (2m/3, m) ∪ [0, m/3). A triangle x, y, z would give s1 + s2 + s3 = 0 with s1 = y − x,
s2 = z − y, s3 = x − z ∈ S, so s1 + s2 = −s3 ∈ S, which is impossible. If s, s + 1 ∈ S, the subgroup
generated by S contains 1.

   Before proving our main result, we give below the construction that will be necessary for d = 5.
Lemma 3. For m ≥ 100 let Bm be the graph on Zm × {0, 1} with edges

           (x, 0)(x + 1, 0),     (x, 1)(x + 20, 1),           (x, 0)(x + t, 1) (t ∈ T = {0, 5, 12}).

Then Bm is connected, 5-regular, of order 2m and girth at least 5.
Proof. Each vertex has two neighbours in its own layer (as m > 40) and three in the other. For
example, for some (x, 0) in layer 0, it has neighbours (x + 1, 0) and (x − 1, 0) in that layer, and
(x + t, 1) for t ∈ {0, 5, 12} in the other. The first layer (layer 0) is a Hamiltonian cycle, and every
vertex of the second layer is joined to it (per our third rule with t = 0). Thus Bm is connected and
5-regular.
    Now, towards a contradiction, assume there exists a cycle ℓ of length at most 4. There are four
kind of ways to walk through this cycle, and we can record the coordinates changes at each step:
±1 inside layer 0, ±20 inside layer 1, +t from layer 0 to layer 1 and −t back, with t ∈ T . Since
we must return to the vertex we began with, the sum of coordinate changes must be 0 mod m.
And since the cycle has at most four steps, each with coordinate change at most 20, 4 · 20 < m so
the sum of coordinate changes must be exactly 0. Remark that consecutive vertices of a cycle are
distinct and no vertex repeats, so two consecutive steps inside the same layer have the same sign,
and a step +t followed by −t′ (or −t followed by +t′ ) has t ̸= t′ . Note also that we must change
layer an even amount of times: 0, 2, or 4 times.

    Per construction of Bm , combinations of t ∈ T have results in T − T \ {0} = {±5, ±7, ±12}. We
can therefore see whether a cycle can occur based on the number of changes of layer. If there are
none, the sum of the steps of size 1 or 20 without repeats does not amount to 0. If we change layer
twice, for a 3-cycle, with say intra-layer step s and traversing steps ±t, we would need s + t − t′ = 0.
Since s ∈ {±1, ±20} and we know T − T \ {0} = {±5, ±7, ±12}, this is impossible. For a 4-cycle,
depending on whether the intra-layer steps happen in the same layer or a different one, we would
need t′ − t ∈ {±2, ±40} or {±19, ±21}, again impossible. Finally, with four changes of layer,
we would need t1 − t2 + t3 − t4 = 0 (consecutively distinct). The six possible sums t + t′ are
0, 5, 10, 12, 17, 24, all distinct, so {t1 , t3 } = {t2 , t4 } as multisets, which implies t1 = t2 or t1 = t4 .
    In each case we get a contradiction, so Bm has no cycle ℓ of length inferior or equal to 4.

    We can now prove the main result.

Theorem 4. For all d ≥ 5, there exists a connected d-regular graph of even order that is not
bisectable.

Proof. For d ≥ 6 even and even n ≥ 4d + 8, let m = n − d − 1, which is odd and at least 3d + 7. The
open interval (m/3, m/2) contains at least m/6 − 1 ≥ d/2 consecutive integers; let J be a set of
d/2 consecutive of them and S = J ∪ (−J) ⊆ Im . By Lemma 2, Cm (S) is a connected triangle-free
d-regular graph. Delete an edge xy of Cm (S), add Wd , and add the edges ux and vy (where uv
was a non-edge per our construction of Wd in the case where d is even). The result G is d-regular
of order n, and connected, since x and y are joined through Wd . Every triangle of G lies in Wd :
Cm (S) is triangle-free, and a triangle meeting both parts would use ux or vy, but u and x have no
common neighbour, and neither have v and y. Thus, by Lemma 1, G is not bisectable.
     Now for d ≥ 7 odd and even n ≥ 2d2 − d + 3, let m = n − d − 3, which is even and at
least 2d(d − 1) ≥ 3d + 3. Let J be a set of (d − 1)/2 consecutive integers in (m/3, m/2) and
S = {m/2} ∪ J ∪ (−J) ⊆ Im ; then Cm (S) is a connected triangle-free d-regular graph. It has
an induced matching x1 y1 , . . . , xs ys with s = (d − 1)/2. Indeed, choosing edges greedily, each
choice xy excludes all edges meeting N [x] ∪ N [y], which is at most 2d2 . And Cm (S) has dm/2 ≥
d · 2d(d − 1)/2 ≥ 2d2 · (d − 1)/2 = 2d2 s edges, so the matching exists. Now delete these s edges,
add a vertex w adjacent to x1 , y1 , . . . , xs , ys , add Wd and the edge pw. The result G is d-regular of
order (d + 2) + (m + 1) = n and connected (each deleted edge xi yi is replaced by the path xi wyi ).
The neighbours of w are pairwise non-adjacent, since the matching is induced and its edges were
deleted, and p, w have no common neighbour; so every triangle of G lies in Wd , and Lemma 1
applies.
     Finally, for d = 5 and even n ≥ 208, let m = (n − 8)/2 ≥ 100 and h = ⌊m/2⌋. In Bm of
Lemma 3, the edges e1 = (0, 0)(1, 0) and e2 = (h, 0)(h + 1, 0) are at distance at least 3: each step
changes the first coordinate by at most 20, and their first coordinates are at circular distance at
least m/2 − 2 ≥ 48. Delete e1 , e2 and add a vertex w adjacent to their four ends; call the resulting
graph H. A cycle through w leaves and re-enters w at two of these ends: if they are the ends of
the same ei , their distance in Bm − ei is at least 4 because Bm has girth at least 5; otherwise it
is at least 3. So H has girth at least 5; it is connected of order 2m + 1, and all its degrees are 5
except w which has deg(w) = 4. Add W5 and the edge pw. The resulting graph G is connected,
5-regular, of order 7 + 2m + 1 = n. Since pw is a bridge, every cycle of length at most 4 lies in W5
or in H, hence in W5 , and Lemma 1 applies again.
     Finally 4d + 8, 2d2 − d + 3 and 208 are at most 2d2 + 200, hence the bound mentioned in the
introduction.
