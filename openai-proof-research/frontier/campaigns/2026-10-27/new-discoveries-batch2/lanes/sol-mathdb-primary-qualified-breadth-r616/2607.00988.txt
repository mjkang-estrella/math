From Gradient Descent to Harmonic Interpolation:
A Geometric Theory of Binary Classification

Abstract
We propose a precise dictionary between binary classification in machine
learning and differential geometry. Classifiers are horizontal sections of vector
bundles over the data space; training labels are Dirichlet boundary conditions on
the section; the kernel of an RKHS interpolant is the Green’s function of the
Laplace–Beltrami operator on the data manifold; backpropagation is a degenerate
flat-geometry limit of the exact geometric problem (§13.6).
The framework does not add new Yang-Mills theory: the equations we solve are
classical potential theory and harmonic analysis.
The central contribution is the geometric dictionary: the identification
that the harmonic interpolation boundary value problem — find the
minimum-Dirichlet-energy classifier satisfying Δg​f=0 away from the
data with Dirichlet conditions f⁡(xi)=yi⋅r — is precisely the
problem that RKHS interpolation and kernel ridge regression already solve.
The representer theorem of Kimeldorf and Wahba [13] and the
SPDE–Green’s function correspondence of Lindgren, Rue, and Lindström
[8] are the analytic backbone; the contribution is the
potential-theoretic interpretation: the kernel is the Green’s function
(Theorem 13.3), the coefficients are capacitances, and the
decision boundary is the zero equipotential of a classical electrostatic field.
The hard-margin SVM is a related margin-constrained variant
(Corollary 13.4). For finite data on any smooth manifold, flat O⁡(2)
solutions always exist (Theorem 8.2 — genuinely new). The density of
O⁡(2) harmonic interpolants in the space of continuous classifiers
(Theorem 10.1) is the universal kernel theory of Steinwart
[21] and Micchelli–Xu–Zhang [2] recast in
O⁡(2) geometric language.

1. Introduction
The dominant paradigm in machine learning treats classification as an optimisation
problem: minimise a loss functional over a space of parameters. The geometry
underlying this process is typically Riemannian — gradient descent follows the
metric on parameter space — but the classifier itself is rarely given a geometric
interpretation.
In this paper we propose a different geometric frame. We observe that in both the
support vector machine (SVM) — a maximum-margin classifier whose decision function
is defined via a kernel K and support vector weights — and the one-hidden-layer
neural network without activation or bias — where the classifier is the linear map
f⁡(x)=W2​W1​x — the classifier arises from a section of a vector
bundle over the data space M. This is a new framing, not a new object:
SVMs and neural networks are well-understood, but their identification as sections
of vector bundles with the training problem as a Dirichlet BVP — and the resulting
geometric dictionary with Riemannian geometry and potential theory — is the
contribution. Learning is reformulated as finding a connection on that
bundle for which the section is horizontal.
Before stating the principles, we briefly recall the geometric objects involved,
for the reader’s convenience.
A vector bundle E→M is a family of vector spaces (the
fibers) parameterised smoothly by points of the base space M. In our
setting M⊆ℝn is the data space and each fiber Ex≅ℝm is a
copy of ℝm sitting above the data point x∈M. A section
s:M→E is a smooth choice of one vector s⁡(x) in each fiber — it assigns
a hidden representation to every data point. The structure group
G⊆GL⁡(m) is the group of linear automorphisms of the fiber regarded
as symmetries of the bundle. Two sections s and s′=g⋅s related by a
smooth map g:M→G (a gauge transformation, or G-transformation)
represent the same geometric object: the physically meaningful quantity is not s
itself but its gauge equivalence class (or G-equivalence class)
[s]. The structure group therefore controls which transformations of the hidden
representation are invisible to the classifier. A connection ∇ on
E is a rule for differentiating sections: it specifies, at each point of E,
a decomposition of the tangent space into a vertical part (along the fiber)
and a horizontal part (transverse to it). A section is called
horizontal (or parallel) if its derivative lies entirely in the
horizontal part, meaning it does not move in the fiber direction as x varies
across M. The curvature F of the connection measures the failure of
the horizontal distribution to be integrable: zero curvature means parallel
transport around any loop returns the fiber to itself, while non-zero curvature
means transport around a loop can rotate the fiber non-trivially. Finally, the
readout map φ:E→ℝ is a map that is linear on each fiber
separately: for every x∈M, the restriction φ|Ex:Ex≅ℝm→ℝ is a linear functional. Equivalently, φ is a global section
of the dual bundle E∗. Note that φ is not required to be linear
as a map on the total space E itself, which is not a vector space. Concretely,
φ is the output weight vector W2∈ℝ1×m, acting fiberwise
as φ⁡(s⁡(x))=W2​s​(x). The classifier is σ=φ∘s:M→ℝ, and the decision boundary is Γ=σ−1​(0).
Notation convention. Throughout this paper, φ denotes the
readout map E→ℝ, while ϕ (a different glyph) denotes the O⁡(2)
fibre angle function ϕ:M→ℝ that parametrises the section as
s=r​(cos⁡ϕ,sin⁡ϕ)⊤. The two symbols are visually close but play
entirely different roles; this convention is maintained consistently.
Three principles organise the framework:
(1)
Two independent geometric choices replace the two arbitrary choices of
classical ML: the structure group G replaces the activation
function; the Riemannian metric g replaces the kernel.
Both are developed precisely in §5.
(2)
A section of the bundle together with a fiberwise linear readout
φ and Dirichlet conditions at the data points constitutes a
classification problem.
(3)
The minimum-geometric-energy solution satisfying those Dirichlet
conditions is the unique geometrically preferred solution — found by
solving a classical PDE, not by gradient descent.
The paper is organised as follows. §2 surveys related work. §3 recalls
background on connections. §4 develops the SVM case. §5 develops the neural
network case and the refined definitions. §6 works out XOR with G=O⁡(2).
§7 develops the flower-shaped problem. §8 computes curvature and proves every
finite classification problem admits a flat solution (Theorem 8.2).
§9 develops text classification on spheres. §10 discusses the structure group
hierarchy and proves the geometric universality theorem
(Theorem 10.1). §11 connects the structure group to data
symmetry. §12 proposes persistent homology as a guide to the structure group.
§13 develops classification as a geometric BVP with exact solutions. §14
presents a numerical experiment. §15 crystallises the framework as a precise
dictionary. §16 states the remaining open conjecture and open problems.
The paper has three arcs. Arc 1 (§3–§9): examples and the bundle
framework — SVM, XOR, flower, curvature, spheres. Arc 2
(§10–§12): which structure group to use — hierarchy, universality,
symmetry efficiency, and TDA. Arc 3 (§13–§14): the exact geometric
solution — harmonic interpolation, the BVP, and the numerical experiment.
Readers who prefer a deductive presentation may read §5 (definitions) then
§13 (the general BVP) before the examples.
2. Related Work
The literature relating geometry and machine learning divides cleanly into two
programmes, both distinct from the present work.
Geometry used for machine learning.
The geometric deep learning programme [17] places neural network
architectures on a group-theoretic foundation: convolutions, graph networks, and
transformers are unified as instances of blueprint invariant or equivariant maps
under a symmetry group. Gauge-equivariant convolutional networks
[4] extend this to principal bundles, associated bundles, and
gauge transformations, giving architectures whose filters are literally sections of
associated bundles over the input manifold. This work shares vocabulary with the
present paper — bundles, connections, gauge transformations — but uses them
differently: it constrains the architecture to be equivariant, while we identify
the classification problem as a BVP on a bundle. Miolane et al.’s Geomstats
library [5] and the broader programme of geometric
statistics [6] develop Riemannian and Lie-group geometry as
computational tools for ML, with applications to shape analysis, medical imaging,
and structured data. Information geometry [7] equips the space of
probability distributions with a Riemannian structure (the Fisher metric) and
studies gradient descent in those terms. Topological data analysis
[18] extracts homological invariants from data, applied here in
§12 to guide structure group selection. All of these use geometric
tools to improve or understand ML. None identifies ML problems as classical
geometric BVPs.
Neural networks described by kernel theory.
The neural tangent kernel (NTK) [3] shows that an infinitely wide
neural network trained by gradient descent is equivalent to kernel gradient descent
with a specific kernel derived from the network architecture. In the infinite-width
limit, training dynamics become linear and the solution is a kernel regression
estimator. This line of work establishes that wide networks behave like kernel
machines — a convergence result. The present paper works in the opposite
direction: we identify the kernel machine itself as the exact solution to a
geometric BVP, and show that the network is an iterative approximation to that
exact problem.
The Matérn–Green’s function correspondence.
The identification of Matérn kernels as Green’s functions of elliptic operators is
known in spatial statistics. Lindgren, Rue, and Lindström [8]
establish an explicit link between Gaussian fields with Matérn covariance and
solutions of SPDEs of the form (Δ+κ2)ν/2​x=𝒲
(white noise), used computationally to obtain sparse precision matrices via finite
elements. Whittle [9] established the spectral-domain version of
this correspondence earlier. In the ML kernel literature, this connection is noted
in the Gaussian process regression context [15]. Our Theorem 13.3
identifies the same correspondence as the geometric content of RKHS interpolation:
the kernel is the Green’s function, the interpolation coefficients are capacitances,
and the solution is a harmonic potential. The contribution is the geometric
interpretation — the dictionary between the kernel machine and the
classical BVP — rather than the analytic correspondence itself.
What is new.
The present paper does not use geometry as a tool for ML, nor does it show that one
ML object approximates another. It establishes an isomorphism: the binary
classification problem, as formulated by the SVM and RKHS interpolation, is
a classical Dirichlet BVP on a vector bundle; the kernel is the Green’s
function; the training is potential theory. The arbitrariness of current
ML — the choice of activation, kernel, learning rate — resolves into two
independent geometric choices (structure group G, Riemannian metric g) with
precise mathematical meaning. To our knowledge, no prior work frames binary
classification as an element of the solution space of a geometric BVP in this
way.
3. Background: Connections on Vector Bundles
3.1. Line bundles and the abelian case
Let M be a smooth manifold [19]. A line bundle over M is a rank-1 vector
bundle π:E→M. A connection is specified by a 1-form A∈Ω1​(M),
giving covariant derivative ∇Af=d​f+A⋅f. A section
f:M→ℝ is horizontal if ∇Af=0, integrating to
f(x)=Cexp(−∫γA) on simply connected domains. The curvature is
F=d​A∈Ω2​(M).
3.2. Vector bundles and the non-abelian case
A rank-m vector bundle E→M with structure group G⊆GL⁡(m)
carries a connection
𝒜=𝒜μ​d​xμ∈Ω1​(M,𝔤),
where 𝔤=Lie⁡(G). The covariant derivative of a section
s:M→ℝm is ∇μs=∂μs+𝒜μ​(x)⋅s, and
the curvature is
F=d​𝒜+𝒜∧𝒜∈Ω2​(M,𝔤).
The term 𝒜∧𝒜 involves the Lie bracket of 𝔤 and vanishes
when 𝔤 is abelian, but is generically non-zero otherwise.
4. Support Vector Machines as a Line Bundle Problem
4.1. The bundle and the connection
A support vector machine (SVM) [15] is a binary classifier that finds a
decision boundary of maximum margin between two classes. Given labeled training
data {(xi,yi)} with xi∈ℝn and yi∈{+1,−1}, the SVM
with kernel K:ℝn×ℝn→ℝ produces a decision function:
f⁡(x)=∑iαi​yi​K​(xi,x)+b,
where K is a positive definite kernel function, αi≥0 are
weights supported on the support vectors, and b∈ℝ is a bias term.
The kernel implicitly maps the data into a (possibly infinite-dimensional)
reproducing kernel Hilbert space [15].
The line bundle framework applies to any real-valued classifier f:M→ℝ.
Let M=ℝn and E=M×ℝ with structure group ℝ∗. The
classification problem has tuple
(M=ℝn,g=gEucl,E=M×ℝ,G=ℝ∗,φ=id,𝒟),
where gEucl=∑i=1n(d​xi)2 is the canonical flat metric on
ℝn. The Laplace–Beltrami operator reduces to the standard Laplacian
Δg=∑i∂i2. Its Green’s function — the Newton/Riesz kernel
𝒢⁡(x,y)∝|x−y|2−n (for n≥3) or −12​π​log⁡|x−y|
(for n=2) — is singular on the diagonal and not positive definite, hence not
directly usable as an RKHS kernel. The Matérn regularisation of
§13.3 resolves this by replacing Δg with
(Δg+κ2)ν, giving a positive-definite kernel. The decision
function f defines a section σ⁡(x)=(x,f⁡(x)). Horizontality
∇Af=0 forces:
A=−d​ff=−d​log⁡|f|,
(1)
defined on M∖Γ where Γ=f−1​(0) is the decision boundary.
Two distinct estimators arise within this framework, and they must not be
conflated:
RKHS interpolation.
Minimise ‖f‖ℋK2 subject to
equality constraints f⁡(xi)=yi⋅r at every training point.
The solution is the harmonic interpolant f⁡(x)=∑iαi​yi​K​(xi,x)
with 𝜶=𝐊−1​𝐯 — a single linear solve,
dense 𝜶 (Theorem 13.3).
Hard-margin SVM.
Minimise ‖f‖ℋK2 subject to
inequality constraints yi​f​(xi)≥1 (the margin).
The solution has the same form but with sparse 𝜶: only
support vectors (points where yi​f​(xi)=1) contribute.
The SVM is the RKHS interpolant on the endogenously determined support vector
set; the quadratic programme is needed precisely because that set is unknown
a priori.
The geometric framework developed in §13 characterises the
RKHS interpolant as the minimum-Dirichlet-energy harmonic function satisfying the
prescribed Dirichlet data — classical potential theory. The SVM adds the margin
constraint on top of this geometric picture.
4.2. Flatness and the singular locus
The connection (1) is flat on M∖Γ: since
A=−d​log⁡|f| is exact, F=d​A=0. On simply connected M, all the geometry
is concentrated in the logarithmic singularity of A along Γ.
5. Neural Networks: Refined Definitions
5.1. The gauge symmetry
We consider a one-hidden-layer neural network for binary classification on
ℝn [12], in the simplified setting of no activation function and no bias
term. The omission of activations is deliberate: we will show that the structure
group of the bundle plays precisely the geometric role that activations play in the
standard setting, making activations redundant once the bundle geometry is correctly
specified. The omission of biases is a simplification that does not affect the
geometric structure.
The network computes:
f⁡(x)=W2​W1​x,W1∈Mat⁡(m,n),W2∈Mat⁡(1,m),
where x∈ℝn is the input, W1 maps to a hidden layer of dimension m,
and W2 collapses to a scalar output. In the standard network, activations would
be applied to W1​x before multiplying by W2; here we omit them. The
resulting map is linear in x, and we will see that this forces the decision
boundary to be a hyperplane — precisely the geometric content of Remark 5.4.
The map x↦W2​W1​x is invariant under G⋅(W1,W2)=(G​W1,W2​G−1)
for any G∈GL⁡(m). This defines a principal GL⁡(m)-bundle over the space of
effective classifiers, with associated rank-m vector bundle E=M×ℝm
over data space M=ℝn. The classification problem has tuple
(M=ℝn,g=gEucl,E=M×ℝm,G=O⁡(m),φ=(1,0,…,0),𝒟),
where gEucl is the canonical flat metric on ℝn.
5.2. Sections, readouts, and the refined definitions
Definition 5.1.
A classification problem is a tuple (M,g,E,G,φ,𝒟)
where:
•
(M,g) is the data manifold with a Riemannian metric g, which
determines the Laplace–Beltrami operator Δg and hence the kernel
K=𝒢Δg (the geometry of the data space);
•
E→M is a vector bundle with structure group G, which determines
what connections exist and what decision boundaries are achievable
(the geometry of the fiber);
•
φ:E→ℝ is a fiberwise linear readout (a section of E∗);
concretely W2∈ℝ1×m;
•
𝒟={(xi,yi)} is a labeled dataset, yi∈{+1,−1}.
The metric g and the structure group G are independent geometric
choices playing distinct roles: g determines the geometry of the base M and corresponds to
the choice of kernel; G acts on the fiber Ex and corresponds to the
expressive capacity of the classifier.
Definition 5.2.
A solution to the classification problem (for separable data) is the
gauge equivalence class [𝒜,f] of the minimum-geometric-energy
connection-potential pair satisfying the Dirichlet conditions
φ⁡(s𝒜​(xi))=yi⋅r at each data point, where the
geometric energy ℰ⁡[𝒜,f] is:
•
ℰ=∫M‖F𝒜‖2​dvolg
(Yang-Mills energy of the connection) in the non-abelian case F≢0;
•
ℰ=∫M‖∇f‖2​dvolg
(Dirichlet energy of the potential) in the abelian/flat case,
where F≡0 and the classifier f is the relevant quantity.
The decision boundary is Γ={x∈M:φ⁡(s𝒜​(x))=0}.
Remark 5.3 (Horizontal vs covariantly harmonic).
Throughout this paper, solutions are described as horizontal (parallel)
sections: ∇𝒜s=0. This is the flat-connection limit. In the
Yang–Mills–Higgs framework of the companion paper [1],
the section instead satisfies DA∗​DA​ϕ=0 away from the data
— it is covariantly harmonic, not horizontal. On flat connections
(F=0) and on contractible M, the two conditions coincide: DA=∇
and covariant harmonicity reduces to ordinary harmonicity, which on contractible
domains is equivalent to the flat horizontal condition. Horizontality
is thus the degenerate limit of covariant harmonicity, appropriate for this
paper’s regime.
5.3. Two independent geometric choices
A classification problem (M,g,E,G,φ,𝒟) involves two
independent geometric choices that play entirely different roles and should not
be conflated.
The structure group G acts on the fiber Ex≅ℝm. It
determines: (i) the gauge symmetries; (ii) the Lie algebra 𝔤 in which
the curvature lives; (iii) the orbit geometry — which G-invariant
subsets of the fiber the section is constrained to inhabit.
For G=O⁡(2): since O⁡(2) acts by isometries on ℝ2, the orbit of any
non-zero vector is the sphere S1(r)={∥s∥=r}. The section is constrained
to this circle, and the angle ϕ parametrises its position on S1​(r). Sign
changes in σ=s1=r​cos⁡ϕ occur when ϕ passes through π/2 —
at that point s=(0,r)≠0. The section rotates through the
perpendicular direction rather than vanishing.
For G=ℝ∗ (line bundle): the section is a scalar function; sign changes require
it to pass through zero. There is no G-invariant sphere to rotate on.
This is the precise content of the dictionary entry activation ↔G:
an activation function (tanh, sigmoid) constrains the hidden representation to an
interval or curve (−1,1) in the output — a “sphere” in 1D. The structure group
O⁡(m) constrains the section to Sm−1​(r) — a sphere in m dimensions. Both
are sphere-type constraints on the hidden representation; the structure group
determines the dimension and geometry of that sphere. The specific path traced on
the sphere as x varies is the connection (the angle function ϕ⁡(x)); the
sphere itself is the orbit of G. Crucially, G does not correspond to a kernel
or to a metric — it has no counterpart in the data space M.
The Riemannian metric g determines the geometry of the base M.
It determines
the Laplace–Beltrami operator Δg, whose Green’s function 𝒢g​(x,y)
is the kernel of the classifier: K⁡(x,y)=𝒢g​(x,y). Different choices of g
give different kernels. In the traditional SVM, the kernel is chosen empirically;
here, choosing g is choosing the geometric structure on the data space. The
metric has no counterpart in the fiber.
The dictionary entry is therefore:
activation function⟷G(fiber geometry),kernel ​K⟷g(base geometry).
Both are geometric choices, but they are independent and govern different parts
of the bundle.
Remark 5.4 (Linearity without activation).
When G=GL⁡(m) and no activation is present, the composed map x↦W2​W1​x is a linear functional on ℝn, so the decision boundary is always
a hyperplane. The section s⁡(x)=W1​x is linear in x, and the connection
𝒜μ=−W1​eμ​(W1​x)† has F=0 wherever s≠0. The
non-abelian term 𝒜∧𝒜 contributes nothing beyond the abelian case:
enlarging the structure group to GL⁡(m) without introducing non-trivial fiber
transformations leaves the classifier linear.
6. The XOR Problem with G=O⁡(2)
6.1. Setup and failure of the abelian case
The XOR dataset consists of four points in M=ℝ2:
(0,0)↦−1,(1,1)↦−1,(0,1)↦+1,(1,0)↦+1.
This section studies Problem A (finite labeled data): only the four
point values are prescribed; the global angle function ϕ is not unique.
The particular ansatz (2) below is one solution; any other smooth
ϕ satisfying the same four Dirichlet conditions is equally valid, and may
have different geometric properties (different decision boundary, different
oscillation count). The classification problem has tuple
(M=ℝ2,g=gEucl,E=M×ℝ2,G=O⁡(2),φ=(1,0),𝒟XOR).
No linear classifier separates them. With G=ℝ∗, any continuous f with the
correct signs requires Γ=f−1​(0) to separate the plane into two regions
containing diagonally opposite pairs. This is topologically impossible with a
single connected curve in ℝ2 without enclosing a point of the wrong class:
a topological obstruction, not a failure of optimisation.

Remark 6.1 (Local validity of the ansatz).
The angle function (2) is unbounded: ϕ→−∞ as
|x1−x2|→∞, so cos⁡ϕ oscillates indefinitely and the global
classifier produces infinitely many parallel sign-change stripes. This is a local
construction, valid near the four data points. To obtain a well-behaved global
classifier with exactly two regions, restrict to the strip
|x1−x2|≤1/2+ε for small ε>0, or
replace the polynomial ansatz with a bounded angle function (such as the
tanh-based construction of §7) that saturates to 0 and π
far from the data.
7. The Flower Problem with G=O⁡(2)
7.1. Setup
We consider the planar flower classification problem, a standard benchmark in
neural network pedagogy. This section studies Problem B (continuous boundary
representation): the decision boundary Γ is given analytically, and we
construct a connection-section pair that represents it exactly. This is distinct
from Problem A (finite labeled data), where only point evaluations are prescribed
and the boundary is determined by the interpolating solution. The data space is
M=ℝ2, equipped with polar coordinates (r,α). The classification
problem has tuple
(M=ℝ2,g=gEucl,E=M×ℝ2,G=O⁡(2),φ=(1,0),𝒟).

Remark 7.1 (k as a property of the solution, not the problem).

Remark 7.2 (Why O⁡(2) appears in textbook examples).
The appearance of G=O⁡(2) in both the XOR and flower examples is not a
coincidence of the framework — it reflects the way such examples are
constructed. Textbook classification benchmarks are typically generated
algorithmically by the authors: XOR is built from reflections across the diagonals
of the unit square; the flower is built by rotating a radial template k times.
Both constructions use rotations and reflections, which are exactly the elements
of O⁡(2). The symmetry group of the data generation process is a subgroup of
O⁡(2), and so the structure group O⁡(2) fits the problem perfectly.
In real classification problems — sentiment analysis, fraud detection, medical
diagnosis — the data is not generated by rotations. There is no reason for the
relevant structure group to be O⁡(2) or any other rotation group. The symmetry
group of the data distribution may be discrete, non-abelian, high-dimensional, or
difficult to identify. Choosing the right G for a real problem requires
understanding the symmetries of the data — a point we return to in §11.
Remark 7.3 (Bridge to the sphere).
The transition from ℝ2 to a non-contractible base space M changes the
status of k fundamentally. On S2, oriented rank-2 real bundles with structure
group S​O​(2) are classified by H2​(S2,ℤ)=ℤ,
with the integer being the Euler class e⁡(E)=k. Any section of the degree-k
bundle must satisfy ∫S2F=2​π​k, making k a quantised topological
charge rather than a local geometric feature. The flower example over ℝ2 can
therefore be seen as the flat, degenerate limit of a richer family of classifiers
over S2, where the petal count becomes a topological invariant of the bundle.

We now investigate precisely when the connection induced by a section is flat.
This leads to an explicit curvature formula, a counterexample to the naive
flatness conjecture, and a positive theorem showing that every classification
problem admits a flat solution.

8.3. Counterexample: a section mapping to S2
Take M=ℝ2 and s:M→S2⊂ℝ3 defined by

8.4. Every classification problem admits a flat solution
The counterexample shows that not every section induces a flat connection. However,
for any classification problem there always exists a section inducing a
flat connection.
Theorem 8.2 (Existence of flat solutions).
Let M be a smooth manifold and 𝒟={(xi,yi)} a finite binary
classification dataset. Then there exists a section s:M→ℝ2↪ℝm of constant norm and a flat O⁡(2)-connection ∇ on
E=M×ℝm such that [∇,s] solves the classification problem
with readout φ=(1,0,…,0).
Proof.
By a standard partition of unity argument, there exists a smooth function
f:M→[−1,1] with f⁡(xi)=yi for all i. Define
θ⁡(x)=π2​(1−f⁡(x))∈[0,π],
so that f⁡(xi)=+1⇒θ⁡(xi)=0 and f⁡(xi)=−1⇒θ⁡(xi)=π. Set

Corollary 8.3.
Every finite binary classification problem on any smooth manifold M can be
solved with a flat O⁡(2)-connection. In particular, for finite data:
(i)
Curvature is never necessary;
(ii)
The problem always reduces to the O⁡(2) case, regardless of the ambient
fiber dimension m;
(iii)
The non-trivial geometry (when m≥3) encodes topological information
about the solution as a map s:M→Sm−1, not additional expressive
power over O⁡(2).
Remark 8.4.
No condition on the topology of M is required. The partition of unity
argument produces a smooth interpolating function on any smooth manifold;
d2​θ=0 and d​θ∧d​θ=0 hold by the elementary
properties of the exterior derivative, independent of the topology of M;
and the bundle E=M×ℝm is trivial by construction. The topological
obstruction described in §9 — where flat solutions can fail to
exist — arises only when one fixes a non-trivial bundle over a
non-contractible base, a situation outside the scope of this theorem.
Remark 8.5 (Role of curvature).
If curvature is never necessary for finite data, what does it encode?
The curvature of the induced connection is the pullback of the canonical
curvature of the tautological bundle over Sm−1: it measures how the
section s:M→Sm−1 wraps around the sphere. Among all solutions
to a given classification problem, the flat O⁡(2) solution minimises the
curvature functional ∫M‖F‖2​dvol. Solutions with non-zero
curvature are geometrically richer and may carry better regularity or
generalisation properties — a question connected to the Yang-Mills variational
problem [10] (Q3 in §16).
9. Text Classification on Spheres
9.1. Embeddings and cosine similarity
In modern natural language processing, documents are represented as vectors in
ℝn called embeddings. Comparing documents by cosine similarity
— the inner product of their normalised versions — is equivalent to comparing
their projections onto the unit sphere Sn−1⊂ℝn. Binary
classification (positive/negative sentiment, topic A vs topic B) is then a
problem over M=Sn−1, not over ℝn.
This changes the geometry fundamentally. Unlike ℝn, which is contractible,
Sn−1 is non-contractible and supports non-trivial vector bundles.
§8 established that over any smooth manifold every finite
classification problem admits a flat O⁡(2) solution (Theorem 8.2).
Over spheres this can fail: certain bundles have non-zero characteristic classes
that force non-zero curvature and impose topological constraints on the decision
boundary independent of any training procedure.
The dimension n determines which obstructions arise. We develop three cases.
9.2. M=S1: circular semantics and the Möbius classifier
Setting.
S1 arises for 2-dimensional normalised embeddings, and more generally models
circular semantic spaces: a political spectrum that wraps around, a periodic

9.3. M=S2: the Dirac monopole and forced curvature
Setting and structure group.
S2 arises for 3-dimensional normalised embeddings. A real binary classifier
f:S2→ℝ lives in a real line bundle with G=ℝ∗; real line bundles
over S2 are classified by w1∈H1​(S2,ℤ/2)=0 — they are all
trivial and carry no topological obstruction.
The topologically interesting case uses the rank-2 real bundle setup of the rest
of the paper (G=O⁡(2), fiber ℝ2, readout φ=(1,0)). Restricting
to orientation-preserving gauge transformations gives G=S​O​(2)
acting on ℝ2. Such oriented rank-2 real bundles over S2 are classified by
the Euler class e⁡(E)∈H2​(S2,ℤ)=ℤ. The
classification problem has tuple
(M=S2,g=ground,Ek→S2,G=SO(2),φ=(1,0),𝒟),
where Ek is the oriented rank-2 real bundle with Euler class k.
Curvature is topologically forced.
For e=k≠0, any connection ∇ on Ek satisfies:
∫S2F∇= 2​π​k≠ 0.
If F=0 everywhere, the integral would vanish — contradicting k≠0.
Therefore no flat connection exists on Ek for k≠0.
This is the first case in the framework where Theorem 8.2 does not
apply: the flat O⁡(2) reduction is topologically obstructed.

The relationship is: at a zero x0 of s (where s⁡(x0)=𝟎),

but the boundary itself remains a curve.
Remark 9.3 (The hairy ball theorem as a learnability obstruction).
The tangent bundle T​S2 has Euler class χ⁡(S2)=2: every smooth vector
field on S2 vanishes at least twice (the hairy ball theorem). In our framework:
if the natural bundle for a classification problem on S2 has k=2, every
section s must vanish at two forced points, so the decision boundary must pass
through those two points regardless of training. These are topological constraints
on the classifier’s geometry, not limitations of the optimiser.
Bridge from the flower.
In §6, the petal count k of the flower classifier over ℝ2 was a local
metric invariant of the connection 1-form — the oscillation frequency of
θα along Γ. Over S2, the same integer k=e⁡(Ek)∈ℤ
is a global topological invariant of the bundle, quantised and independent of
any local choice. The promotion from metric to topological is the key effect of
passing from a contractible to a non-contractible base space.
9.4. Implications for learnability
The sphere analysis extends the framework in three directions.
Curvature can be necessary. Over ℝn and over S1,
every finite classification problem has a flat solution. Over S2, for Problem A
(finite data), Theorem 8.2 still applies: the trivial bundle always
admits a flat O⁡(2) solution. The topological obstruction bites in two situations:
(i) Problem B, where a continuous boundary Γ with non-zero winding requires a
non-trivial bundle; or (ii) when a specific non-trivial bundle Ek is
chosen as the geometric model — then e≠0 forces curvature and the
flat reduction is obstructed.
Forced points on the decision boundary. For a chosen degree-k bundle
over S2 with e=k≠0, any section must vanish completely at |k|
points, which forces the decision boundary curve to pass through those points.
This is a topological constraint on the geometry of the classifier given the
choice of bundle, not a prohibition on binary classification itself.
Parity constraints on S1. The number of decision boundary points on
a circular semantic space must have a definite parity determined by the bundle.
Attempting to train a classifier with the wrong parity will always fail.

geometric restatement of universal kernel theory, not a new density result.
Theorem 10.1 (Geometric universality).
Let (M,g) be a compact smooth Riemannian n-manifold without boundary, and let

Proof.
Step 1 (L2 density of {K⁡(⋅,x)}).
Let TK:L2​(M,g)→L2​(M,g) be the integral operator with kernel K.
Since L is a positive-definite elliptic operator with K=L−1 (as an integral

Suppose f∈L2​(M,g) satisfies ⟨f,K⁡(⋅,x)⟩L2=0 for all

span​{K⁡(⋅,x):x∈M}¯L2​(M,g)=L2(M,g).
Step 2 (RKHS identification and C1​(M) density).
By the Kimeldorf–Wahba correspondence [13], the reproducing
kernel Hilbert space of K is ℋK=Hν​(M,g) (Sobolev space of
order ν), with squared norm ‖f‖ℋK2=⟨f,L​f⟩L2.

identically; hence span​{K⁡(⋅,x):x∈M} is dense in

Chaining these two densities:
span​{K⁡(⋅,x):x∈M}¯C1​(M)=C1(M).

|∇ϕN​(x)−∇ϕ0​(x)|<δ.
Since |∇ϕ0|≥c0>0 on Γ (regularity of the level set), for
δ<c0/2 we have |∇ϕN|>c0/2>0 near Γ. The implicit
function theorem therefore applies to ϕN near Γ, and the standard
quantitative version gives a constant c>0 (depending only on c0=infΓ|∇ϕ0| and the C1 geometry of ϕ0) such that
dH​(ϕN−1​(π/2),ϕ0−1​(π/2))≤c​δ.
Choose δ=ε/c to obtain dH​(ϕN−1​(π/2),Γ)<ε.
Step 5 (Flat O⁡(2) section).
The section sN​(x)=r​(cos⁡ϕN​(x),sin⁡ϕN​(x))⊤ is horizontal for the
connection θ=−d​ϕN∈Ω1​(M,𝔰​𝔬​(2)). Curvature:
F=−d2ϕN⋅J=0; the connection is flat. Embedded as
sN↪(sN,0,…,0)⊤∈ℝm for any m≥2, it is
a horizontal section of E=M×ℝm with structure group O⁡(2)⊂O⁡(m).
With readout φ=(1,0,…,0), the classifier is rcosϕN.
Step 6 (Sign conditions).
Choose δ<mini⁡|ϕ0​(xi)−π/2|, which is positive since the
training data lies strictly off Γ. Then |ϕN​(xi)−ϕ0​(xi)|<δ
implies ϕN​(xi) and ϕ0​(xi) lie on the same side of π/2, so
sign⁡(cos⁡ϕN​(xi))=sign⁡(cos⁡ϕ0​(xi))=yi.

Remark 10.2 (Compactness and the worked examples).
Theorem 10.1 requires (M,g) compact without boundary. This covers
the text-classification setting of §9 (M=Sn−1, compact) but
not the worked examples of §4–§7 and the
experiment of §14, which all live on M=ℝ2 (non-compact).
On non-compact M, the Matérn kernel is still well-defined and the capacitance
linear solve still produces an interpolant, but the C1​(M) density argument of
Steps 1–2 requires modification: one needs either a compact support assumption,
decay conditions at infinity, or a weighted Sobolev space framework. The finite
interpolation problem (Theorem 8.2) is unaffected by compactness —
the partition-of-unity construction works on any smooth manifold. The universality
theorem should be understood as covering the compact setting; its extension to
ℝn is an open problem.
11. Data Symmetry, Structure Groups, and Efficiency
11.1. The symmetry group of the data
Remark 7.2 observed that O⁡(2) appears in textbook examples
because those examples are built using rotations. This raises a precise question:
for a given classification problem, what is the right structure group?
We propose the following principle: the natural structure group for a classification
problem is the symmetry group of the data distribution. More precisely,
let 𝒟 be the data distribution on M, and let Gdata be
the group of transformations of M that leave 𝒟 invariant and commute
with the labelling. Then the bundle with structure group Gdata is
the geometrically natural choice: the connection can exploit the data symmetry
directly, and the horizontal section inherits the same symmetry.
For real classification problems the data symmetry is rarely O⁡(2). It may be:
•
A discrete group ℤk or Sn (permutation symmetry
in tabular data);
•
A product group G1×G2×⋯ when independent
features have independent symmetries;
•
A continuous group such as S​O​(3) (3D point clouds, molecular
data) or S​E​(3) (rigid body transformations);
•
No symmetry at all (generic tabular or text data), in which case
the universal O⁡(2) solution is the only geometric option.
11.2. Symmetry efficiency: the main observation
The connection between the symmetry of the data and the structure group has a
concrete consequence for efficiency. Before stating the conjecture, we must be
precise about its scope and — crucially — its definition.
Scope.
Theorem 8.2 shows that for any finite dataset, fiber dimension
m=2 suffices for any G⊇O⁡(2). So for finite data,
m∗​(Gdata)=m∗​(G)=2 and the inequality is trivially 2≤2
with no content. The conjecture is therefore a statement about the
continuous boundary approximation problem: given a continuous decision
boundary Γ⊂M (invariant under Gdata) and a target
precision ε>0, what is the minimum fiber dimension needed to
approximate Γ to within ε?
The equivariance constraint.
A key subtlety: without an additional constraint, a larger structure group
G⊇Gdata trivially helps, since any
Gdata-bundle is a G-bundle (by inclusion) and any
Gdata-equivariant section is a G-section. This would give
m∗​(G,ε)≤m∗​(Gdata,ε) for free —
the opposite direction, with no content.
The conjecture becomes non-trivial only when we require sections to be
G-equivariant: the section must transform according to a specific
representation ρ:G→O⁡(m) of the full structure group G, not
merely exist as some section of a G-bundle. Formally:
Definition 11.1.
Let Gdata act on M, and let G⊇Gdata
with a unitary representation ρ:G→O⁡(m) on the fiber ℝm. A section
s:M→E is G-equivariant if s⁡(g⋅x)=ρ⁡(g)​s​(x)
for all g∈G and x∈M. For a Gdata-invariant continuous
boundary Γ, define
m∗(G,ε)=min{m∈ℕ:∃ a G-equivariant section s of a rank-m G-bundle with dH({φ(s)=0},Γ)<ε}.
The equivariance constraint makes m∗​(G,ε) genuinely sensitive to the
choice of G: a G-equivariant section must respect all symmetries of G,
not just those of Gdata. When G⊋Gdata, the
space of G-equivariant candidates is a proper sub-space of the
Gdata-equivariant candidates, potentially requiring more fiber
dimensions to achieve the same approximation quality.
Conjecture 11.2 (Symmetry efficiency).
Let Gdata⊆G act on M, and let Γ be a continuous
boundary with symmetry group containing Gdata. With m∗​(G,ε)
as in Definition 11.1:
m∗​(Gdata,ε)≤m∗​(G,ε)for all ​ε>0.
The conjecture says: the matched group Gdata is the most efficient
choice because its equivariant sections span the largest function space consistent
with the data symmetry. A mismatched G⊋Gdata imposes
additional equivariance requirements not present in Γ, reducing the space of
candidates and potentially forcing a larger fiber dimension to maintain the same
approximation quality. The representation-theoretic content is: the irreducible
representations of Gdata that appear in the decomposition of a
Gdata-equivariant approximating function need not appear as
irreps of G — the larger group may require more channels to represent the
same geometric content.
11.3. Connection to equivariant networks
Conjecture 11.2 gives the geometric content of equivariant network
theory: matching the architecture symmetry group G to the data symmetry
Gdata reduces the required fiber dimension, equivalently the
number of neurons in a one-hidden-layer network. The geometric deep learning
programme [17] is therefore a special case of this conjecture, with the connection
framework giving the empirical observation a precise geometric formulation.
12. Persistent Homology as a Guide to the Structure Group
The question of how to select the structure group G from data admits a partial
answer via topological data analysis [18]. Given a labelled dataset 𝒟, apply
a Vietoris–Rips or Čech filtration to the point cloud {xi} and read off
the Betti numbers β0,β1,β2,… at the dominant persistence
scale. The topological structure group is:
GTDA=O​(2)β1×S​O​(2)β2,
where each β1 loop contributes an O⁡(2) factor (encoding holonomy around a
non-contractible cycle) and each β2 void contributes an S​O​(2) factor
(encoding a Chern class over a 2-cycle). This is deterministic: given the
data, GTDA is computed, not chosen.
The pipeline is principled but not complete. Persistent homology captures global
topological features but misses local geometric symmetry (e.g. S​O​(3)
equivariance in 3D point clouds), non-abelian π1 (Betti numbers give only the
abelianisation π1ab=H1), and ambient domain symmetries such
as translation or permutation invariance. Known domain symmetries should be
added separately, giving G=GTDA×Gdomain.
A category remark is needed: the Betti numbers βk are computed from the
point cloud {xi}, not from the base manifold M. If M=ℝn
(contractible), all bundles over M are trivial regardless of β1.
The intended object is the topology of the support of the data
distribution — the sub-manifold or subset of ℝn near which the data
concentrates — which may have non-trivial loops and voids even when M itself
does not. The proposal below is a heuristic for reading that support topology
from the point cloud.
Heuristic Proposal 12.1 (Topological structure group).
The group GTDA computed from the Betti numbers of the point cloud
is a proposed lower bound on the structure group required to capture the
topology of the data distribution’s support. Concretely: each independent loop
(β1 contribution) in the point cloud suggests an O⁡(2) factor; each
independent void (β2 contribution) suggests an S​O​(2) factor. Any G
intended to represent the full topology of the support should contain
GTDA up to the abelianisation of π1.
This is a heuristic proposal, not a theorem. The predicate “consistent with
the global topology” is not defined with sufficient precision to constitute a
mathematical statement, and the connection between point-cloud Betti numbers and
bundle structure over M requires further development.
The true optimal group satisfies GTDA⊆G∗⊆GL⁡(m).
Even when GTDA captures only the topological component of G∗, it
provides a principled, computable starting point for structure group selection —
strictly better than arbitrary choice of activation function. Making this
heuristic precise is an open problem (Q2 in §16).
Arc 2 ends here. With the structure group identified,
§13 (Arc 3) formulates the exact geometric BVP that was
illustrated by the examples of Arc 1, and derives the harmonic interpolant as
its closed-form solution.
13. Classification as a Boundary Value Problem
13.1. Classification as a geometric boundary value problem
The fundamental shift proposed in this paper is the replacement of optimisation
over parameters with a geometric existence problem: find a connection whose
horizontal section satisfies sign conditions at the data points. This section
addresses Problem A (finite labeled data): the data enters as Dirichlet
conditions at finitely many points, and the solution interpolates between them.
We now sharpen this: the data should enter not as terms in a functional to be
minimised, but as Dirichlet boundary conditions on the connection.
We formulate the problem using the Yang-Mills equations D∗​F=0 as a unifying
language, though the honest scope of this language must be stated at the outset.
In the solved abelian case (abelian G, so [F,⋅]=0), D∗​F=0 reduces to the scalar
Laplace equation Δg​f=0 — classical potential theory. In the flat
O⁡(2) case, F=0 identically and the Yang-Mills condition is trivially
satisfied; the relevant equation is the Dirichlet problem for the angle function
ϕ. Genuine non-abelian Yang-Mills theory, where D∗​F=0 is a non-trivial
PDE on a connection with F≠0, arises only for G=O⁡(m) with m≥3
— which is the open problem of Q1 in §16. The Yang-Mills language
is adopted because it correctly identifies the structural type of each BVP and
gives the right Euler-Lagrange equation in the non-abelian case; it is not a
claim that new Yang-Mills theory is being done.
Given a bundle E→M with structure group G, Riemannian metric g on
M (which determines the Laplace–Beltrami operator Δg and hence the
Green’s function 𝒢g and kernel K=𝒢g), bundle metric on E, and
labeled dataset 𝒟, the
classification problem for separable data is the following geometric
boundary value problem: find the connection 𝒜 that
(i)
satisfies the Yang-Mills equations on the complement of the data:
D𝒜∗​F𝒜= 0on ​M∖{x1,…,xN},
(7)
(ii)
satisfies Dirichlet conditions at each data point:
φ(s𝒜(xi))=yi⋅r,i=1,…,N,
(8)
(iii)
minimises the geometric energy ℰ⁡[𝒜,f]
among all connections satisfying (i) and (ii), where:
•
in the non-abelian case (F𝒜≢0):
ℰ=∫M‖F𝒜‖2​dvolg
(Yang-Mills energy of the connection);
•
in the abelian/flat case (F𝒜≡0):
ℰ=∫M‖∇f‖2​dvolg
(Dirichlet energy of the potential f).
There is no loss function, no gradient descent. The data
enters purely as prescribed values of the section at finitely many points. The
solution is the minimum-geometric-energy solution interpolating the data.
Remark 13.1 (Geometric parameters vs optimisation parameters).
The framework eliminates two things: the loss function (data enters as hard
Dirichlet constraints, not a penalty) and iterative gradient descent (the solution
is a single linear solve). It does not eliminate parameters. The geometric
parameters — length scale ℓ (determining the Riemannian metric g),
Matérn order ν (determining the RKHS regularity), Tikhonov regularisation
ε (required for well-posedness, see below), and section scale r
(fiber metric) — play a role analogous to λ in the penalised formulation.
What changes is their interpretation: each is a property of the geometry of the
data manifold or bundle, not an arbitrary knob tuned against a training loss. The
length scale ℓ is the choice of Riemannian metric; ν is the order of the
elliptic operator; ε is a conductor radius in the electrostatic
analogy. The reframing is genuine — the parameters have precise geometric meaning
— but parameter-free it is not.
Remark 13.2 (Why Dirichlet, not penalisation).
The penalised functional ∫‖F‖2+λ​∑iℓ⁡(yi,φ⁡(s⁡(xi)))
is a soft relaxation of the Dirichlet problem: as λ→∞ it converges
to the hard constraint (8). Writing the functional with finite
λ carries the implicit assumption that approximate satisfaction of the data
conditions is acceptable — which is the machine learning mindset. The geometric
mindset: for separable data, the constraints can and should be satisfied exactly.
The λ functional appears naturally only for non-separable data
(§13.5) or as a degenerate limit recovering backpropagation
(§13.6).
13.2. Exact solutions: the abelian case and harmonic interpolation
For G=ℝ+ (abelian, real line bundle), the Yang-Mills equations (7) reduce,
via the connection 1-form A=−d​log⁡|f| of §4, to a
scalar elliptic equation for the classifier f:M→ℝ away from
the data. We work throughout with the regularised operator
L=(Δg+κ2)ν for ν>n/2+1 and κ>0, whose
Green’s function 𝒢L is positive definite and finite on the diagonal
(Remark 13.7). The formal bare-Laplacian case
Δg​f=0 on M∖{x1,…,xN} is a useful heuristic
on compact M but requires the regularisation κ>0 for a
well-posed capacitance matrix (§13.4).
The solution of minimum Dirichlet energy
∫M|∇f|2​dvolg (equivalently, minimum RKHS norm

13.3. The kernel is the Green’s function
The harmonic interpolant (9) has the same structure as an RKHS
interpolant f⁡(x)=∑iαi​yi​K​(xi,x). The identification is exact:
Theorem 13.3 (RKHS interpolation is the abelian harmonic interpolant).
Let LK be a self-adjoint positive-definite elliptic operator on (M,g)
whose Green’s function K⁡(x,y)=𝒢LK​(x,y) is finite on the
diagonal (e.g. LK=(Δg+κ2)ν with ν>n/2,
κ>0). Then:
(i)
The kernel K is the reproducing kernel of the Hilbert space
ℋK=range(LK−1/2) with norm
‖f‖ℋK2=⟨f,LK​f⟩.
(ii)
The RKHS interpolant f⁡(x)=∑iαi​yi​K​(xi,x) with
𝜶=𝐊−1​𝐯 is the unique
minimum-ℋK-norm function satisfying the Dirichlet conditions
f⁡(xi)=yi⋅r. It solves the abelian harmonic interpolation
problem (9)–(10) with operator LK.
(iii)
For the Matérn kernel Kν​(x,y)=𝒢(Δg+κ2)ν​(x,y)
of order ν: ℋKν=Hν​(M,g) (Sobolev space), and
the solution minimises the Hν seminorm
∫‖(Δg+κ2)ν/2​f‖2.
(iv)
Different kernels correspond to different operators LK, equivalently
different Riemannian metrics and regularisation orders on M. The choice
of kernel is the choice of geometric structure on the data manifold.
Corollary 13.4.
The RKHS interpolant solves the electrostatic capacitance problem: each data
point (xi,yi) acts as a conductor at prescribed potential yi⋅r,
and f is the resulting Green’s function field. The weights
𝛂=𝐊−1​𝐯 are the capacitance
coefficients. No optimisation — classical potential theory.
The hard-margin SVM is a related but distinct object: it minimises
‖f‖ℋK2 subject to inequality constraints
yi​f​(xi)≥1 (the margin), so most αi are zero. The SVM is
the RKHS interpolant restricted to its own support vectors — the active
constraints yi​f​(xi)=1 — whose identity is determined endogenously by
the quadratic programme. The two estimators coincide if and only if all training
points happen to be support vectors.
Remark 13.5 (Relationship to known results).
The representer theorem for interpolation [13] establishes
that the minimum-ℋK-norm function satisfying f⁡(xi)=yi​r has
the form f=∑iαi​K​(xi,⋅) with 𝜶=𝐊−1​𝐯 — this is the mathematical content of
Theorem 13.3(i)–(ii). The identification of Matérn kernels as
Green’s functions of (Δg+κ2)ν is the SPDE correspondence of
Lindgren, Rue, and Lindström [8]. The SVM dual solution
being supported on active constraints is standard KKT theory [15].
What Theorem 13.3 and Corollary 13.4 add is the
geometric interpretation: the linear system is the capacitance equation of
classical potential theory; the solution is the harmonic potential of point
sources at {xi}; and the decision boundary is the zero equipotential.
The dictionary between the RKHS interpolant and the Dirichlet BVP is the
contribution, not the analytic correspondence itself.
Remark 13.6.
The Gaussian (RBF) kernel corresponds to the limit ν→∞ of the Matérn
family — an infinitely smooth Riemannian structure — and has no finite-order
Green’s function representation. Every other common kernel (Matérn, polynomial,
Laplacian) has an exact Green’s function interpretation. Kernels whose RKHS is
dense in C⁡(M) — equivalently, whose Green’s function span is total — are
called universal kernels [21]; these are precisely the
kernels for which the geometric universality theorem (Theorem 10.1)
applies in its strongest form.

Remark 13.7 (Regularisation requirement).

13.5. The non-separable case: geometric relaxation
When no connection satisfying the geometric BVP admits a horizontal section
fulfilling all Dirichlet conditions exactly
(non-separable data), we relax the equality constraints to inequality
constraints: require only that the section values lie in the correct
open half-fiber:
φ⁡(s𝒜​(xi))⋅yi> 0∀i.
The minimum-geometric-energy connection satisfying all inequalities is the constrained
geometric BVP. The geometric “slack” at data point xi is the signed
distance φ⁡(s𝒜​(xi))⋅yi/r — how far inside the correct
half-fiber the section value lies. The SVM margin is the minimum of these
geometric slacks over the data.
Remark 13.9 (The λ functional as soft relaxation).
The penalised functional ℱ⁡[𝒜]=∫‖F‖2+λ​∑iℓ⁡(yi,φ⁡(s⁡(xi))) is the soft-margin (Lagrangian) relaxation of the
inequality-constrained problem, with λ the inverse penalty for constraint
violation. It is a useful computational approximation when the constraints are
difficult to enforce directly, but it is not the primary formulation: it
introduces a free parameter λ with no geometric meaning.
13.6. Gradient descent as degenerate Yang-Mills
When M is contractible and F𝒜=0 (flat connection), the Yang-Mills
equations (7) are satisfied trivially. The problem reduces to
finding a flat connection satisfying the sign conditions — Theorem 8.2
provides one. If instead of the exact geometric solution we seek to approximate it
by gradient flow on the space of flat connections:
∂𝒜∂t=−∂∂𝒜∑iℓ(yi,φ(s𝒜(xi))),
this is gradient descent on the data loss: in the neural network parametrisation
𝒜↔(W1,W2), it is exactly backpropagation.
Remark 13.10 (Backpropagation as the flat-connection limit).
Backpropagation is an iterative numerical method for the flat-connection
(F=0) regime. It is degenerate in the precise technical sense: it
restricts to flat connections, discarding curvature information, and replaces
the direct geometric solution with a gradient flow converging asymptotically.
The geometric BVP gives the solution in a single step, at the cost of
choosing a kernel (length scale ℓ, order ν). Neither approach is
universally superior: the iterative method is flexible and parameter-free
in a different sense; the direct solve is exact and fast for separable data.

solution is an exact interpolant obtained from a single ×60
linear system solve in 2.6​ms. The one-hidden-layer network
requires 5,000 gradient steps (1.30​s, a ≈500×
slowdown) with the gradient-norm tolerance not reached at tol=10−9
— though accuracy itself is fully converged. The experiment illustrates the
flat-connection limit of §13.6: gradient descent iteratively
approximates the same geometric problem that the linear solve resolves directly.
The decision boundaries differ qualitatively. The geometric boundary (panel
a) is the zero equipotential of the harmonic Green’s function field: the
electrostatic picture of Corollary 13.4, where each training
point acts as a conductor at prescribed potential ±r and ∇f⟂Γ at every boundary point. The equipotential lines make the
full field structure visible. The network boundary (panel b) is the emergent
zero of an iteratively optimised composite function, with no such natural
geometric structure.
The background colour field (Figure 4, left) displays the
harmonic interpolant f⁡(x)=∑iαi​K​(x,xi) over ℝ2.
14.3. Implementation note
The geometric solution requires only numpy and scipy: assemble
the N×N kernel matrix 𝐊 (Matérn-3/2), add Tikhonov
regularisation, and call numpy.linalg.solve. The complete implementation,
including the code used to generate Figure 4, is available at
https://github.com/catalinvasii/geometry-classification.
15. The Geometric Dictionary
The central contribution of this paper is not a new theorem in Yang-Mills theory
— the equations we solve are classical potential theory, complex analysis, and
harmonic map theory, all well-understood. The contribution is a precise
dictionary between machine learning concepts and geometric objects,
which reveals that the two theories are describing the same mathematical content
in different languages.
There are two independent geometric choices in the framework (see §5
for the full treatment): the structure group G acting on the fiber
Ex (replacing the activation function) and the Riemannian metric g
determining the base geometry (replacing the kernel). These govern different parts
of the bundle and must not be conflated.

16. Open Conjecture and Open Problems
We distinguish between the one remaining main conjecture — the genuine
open mathematical core of the framework — and secondary open problems that arise
naturally but whose resolution is not essential to the framework.
16.1. Note on geometric universality
Geometric universality is proved as Theorem 10.1 in
§10. Steps 1–2 of the proof are the universal kernel
theory of Steinwart [21] and Micchelli–Xu–Zhang
[2]: density of the Green’s function span in C1​(M) for
ν>n/2+1 via Sobolev embedding. The O⁡(2) section construction
(Steps 5–6) is the geometric packaging specific to this paper. The theorem
should be read as a geometric restatement of universal kernel theory, not
a new density result.
16.2. The remaining main conjecture
Conjecture (Symmetry efficiency, Conjecture 11.2).
Let Gdata⊆G act on M. For the continuous boundary
approximation problem with G-equivariant sections (Definition 11.1),
the minimal fiber dimension satisfies m∗​(Gdata,ε)≤m∗​(G,ε) for all ε>0. The equivariance constraint is
essential: without it, larger G trivially gives smaller m∗ by inclusion,
reversing the inequality.
The conjecture is non-trivial for continuous boundaries; for finite
data it is vacuous by Theorem 8.2 (m∗=2 in both cases).
This is the precise geometric formulation of why equivariant networks are
parameter-efficient: G-equivariance is a stronger constraint than
Gdata-equivariance when G⊋Gdata,
reducing the candidate space and potentially forcing larger fiber dimension.
16.3. Secondary open problems
Q1.
Non-abelian exact solutions.
The abelian (G=ℝ+) and O⁡(2) cases are solved exactly. For G=O⁡(m)
with m≥3, the equations are non-abelian and require the ADHM construction
[20] or Nahm transform. Do the resulting classifiers correspond to known architectures?
Q2.
TDA pipeline: completeness.
Proposition 12.1 establishes GTDA as a lower bound.
When does GTDA=G∗? How should non-abelian π1 and torsion
in H1 be incorporated?
Q3.
Curvature and generalisation.
The harmonic interpolant and the flat O⁡(2) solution (Theorem 8.2)
give different decision boundaries. Does the minimum-geometric-energy
solution generalise better? The Green’s function regularisation corresponds to
Sobolev-norm regularisation; its relationship to weight decay and Tikhonov
regularisation should be made precise. This question connects to the neural
tangent kernel literature [3]: NTK theory shows that wide
networks trained by gradient descent converge to kernel ridge regression with
a specific kernel. The geometric framework suggests that this kernel has a
Green’s function interpretation and that the choice of optimiser (gradient
descent vs direct solve) determines which geometric object is computed.
Q4.
Topological invariants and learning.
For m≥3, different solutions have different topological charge (degree of
s:M→Sm−1). Can classifiers with the same decision boundary but
different topological charge have different generalisation behaviour?
Q5.
Unified energy functional.
The geometric energy ℰ⁡[𝒜,f] takes two forms: the Yang-Mills
energy ∫‖F𝒜‖2 (energy of the connection) in the non-abelian
case, and the Dirichlet energy ∫|∇f|2 (energy of the
section as a map f:M→ℝ) in the abelian/flat case — and these
live on genuinely different objects. The standard Yang–Mills–Higgs functional
∫(‖F𝒜‖2+‖∇𝒜s‖2) is gauge-invariant but
vanishes identically for horizontal sections (∇𝒜s=0 by
definition), so it does not distinguish harmonic interpolants from other flat
solutions. The Weyl decomposition R=W+E+S [11], where
different tensor pieces capture curvature at different geometric levels, is an
analogy for this hoped-for structure. This question is resolved in the companion
paper [1]: the Yang–Mills–Higgs functional
∫‖FA‖2+∫‖DA​ϕ‖2, where DA​ϕ is the covariant
derivative of the section (not the connection-covariant derivative of a horizontal
section), recovers the Dirichlet energy ∫|∇f|2 in the flat abelian
limit (where DA=d on sections) and the Yang–Mills energy in the non-abelian
regime. The two limits do not coincide because in this paper sections are
horizontal (DA​ϕ=0), while in the companion paper sections are
covariantly harmonic (DA∗​DA​ϕ=0) — the more general variational
condition.
Q6.
Topological obstructions.
Is there a necessary condition for the existence of a solution, stated in terms
of characteristic classes of E? This would be a geometric analogue of the VC
dimension: a topological obstruction to learnability rather than a sample
complexity bound.
Q7.
Holonomy and generalisation.
Is there a relationship between Hol⁡(∇)⊆G and generalisation?
Smaller holonomy corresponds to simpler connection geometry and might correspond
to better generalisation in the spirit of minimum description length.
17. Conclusion
We have proposed a geometric reformulation of binary classification. The central
claim is a precise dictionary between machine learning and differential
geometry (§15): classifiers are horizontal sections of vector
bundles, training labels are Dirichlet conditions, the SVM kernel is a Green’s
function, and backpropagation is a degenerate flat-geometry approximation to an
exact geometric problem. The framework does not add new Yang-Mills theory — it
identifies that the equations already used in machine learning are classical
potential theory and harmonic analysis, expressed in an unfamiliar language.
The contribution has three layers. The first is the dictionary itself:
the identification that RKHS interpolation is the abelian harmonic interpolant
(Theorem 13.3 — a geometric restatement of Kimeldorf–Wahba
[13] and the SPDE correspondence
[8]); the hard-margin SVM is the same interpolant
restricted to its endogenously determined support vectors
(Corollary 13.4); and the decision boundary is the zero
equipotential of a classical Green’s function field. The dictionary gives these
known analytic facts a unified geometric interpretation they did not previously
have. The second is the existence and density theory: every finite
classification problem on any smooth manifold has an exact flat O⁡(2) solution
(Theorem 8.2 — new); the density of O⁡(2) harmonic interpolants
in C1​(M) (Theorem 10.1) is universal kernel theory
[21, 2] recast in geometric language. Curvature is
topologically forced on non-contractible spaces (§9).
The third is the data-driven geometry: persistent homology as a
principled guide to the structure group (§12), replacing an arbitrary choice of
activation function with a computable topological invariant.
A numerical experiment (§14) confirms the theoretical picture:
the geometric solution is an exact interpolant obtained from a single linear
system solve, while the neural network requires 5,000 gradient steps without
reaching the convergence criterion. Both achieve high test accuracy on a
two-moons dataset; the comparison is about method, not outcome.
The one open mathematical problem that would complete the framework is the symmetry
efficiency conjecture (Conjecture 11.2): a representation-theoretic
statement that matching the structure group to the data symmetry group minimises
the required fiber dimension. Proving this conjecture would make the dictionary
complete and actionable.
