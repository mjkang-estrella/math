Sharp Upper-Half and Five-Dimensional Laws

Abstract
    

How much risk does a small reweighted training support retain? For finite
weighted least squares with the minimum-norm learner, we prove the exact law
Γd​(n)=3−n/d\Gamma_{d}(n)=3-n/d throughout ⌈3​d/2⌉≤n≤2​d−1\lceil 3d/2\rceil\leq n\leq 2d-1.
The guarantee covers every observed feature rank and uses selections that
preserve the full feature span. Balanced simplex anchors reduce dimension;
positive-weight lifting and independent-line compression close the risk
bound. Shifted coordinate pairs attain the matching lower bound. The
complete dataset-level upper bound and sharpness construction are verified
in Lean 4. At the smaller budget (d,n)=(5,6)(d,n)=(5,6), we also prove
Γ5​(6)=11/5\Gamma_{5}(6)=11/5, matching the simplex-block prediction from 5=3+25=3+2
over arbitrary interacting configurations. Circuit covers, comparison
second moments, and circuit-plane probabilities give the sharp excess
6/56/5, while polar-face geometry resolves shared rank-three circuits.
The general simplex-block frontier connects these laws within the
intermediate-budget selection problem.
  












1 Introduction



Data selection changes the training data while keeping the learner fixed.
For least squares, a selector can retain a few original examples and assign
them new positive weights. The question is how well the resulting predictor
performs on the full dataset. This separates the information carried by a
small training support from the computational method used to find it.
In the very small-support regime, even the rule for choosing among multiple
least-squares solutions matters: balancing the selected gradients need
not recover the full solution if the selected feature span loses rank.



We study exact worst-case risk inflation for the minimum-norm learner.
The evaluation weights pp specify the target finite distribution; they
may encode multiplicities or importance weights. The training weights
qq are the selector’s decision variables. This distinction lets a small
support represent a nonuniform target without changing the evaluation
criterion. The guarantee concerns this finite-distribution risk, rather
than population generalization or the running time of a selection algorithm.



We establish two sharp laws for arbitrary weighted configurations.
Throughout the upper half of the intermediate-budget interval,
Γd​(n)=3−n/d\Gamma_{d}(n)=3-n/d for ⌈3​d/2⌉≤n≤2​d−1\lceil 3d/2\rceil\leq n\leq 2d-1.
Balanced anchors and full-span lifting give a dimension induction whose
complete dataset-level statement is verified in Lean 4. At the smaller
budget (d,n)=(5,6)(d,n)=(5,6), the unrestricted bound Γ5​(6)≤11/5\Gamma_{5}(6)\leq 11/5
matches a seven-point example. This second result handles intersecting
positive circuits through more detailed five-dimensional geometry.

A training-realization viewpoint.



The proof asks which low-risk geometric points can actually be returned
by the stipulated learner. A positive circuit of rank rr has r+1r+1
rows whose positive weighted sum is zero. Combining it with 5−r5-r
independent interpolating rows gives a six-row full-rank training
problem. This interface allows polar geometry, comparison second moments,
and circuit-plane graphs to control the risk of actual fits.
The main text derives both sources of the sharp excess 6/56/5 and states
the exhaustive classification connecting them. The appendix retains the
full shared-circuit analysis rather than assuming that all instances split
into independent blocks.



The same concern with realizability motivates our complementary
certificate theory. Full-span certificate complexity identifies when
exact recovery is available and when maximal complexity forces independent
one-dimensional blocks. Exact one-point penalties on those blocks yield
the weighted boundary law and its extremal conditions. Thus the boundary
and interior results share an interface between positive dependence and
the output of a fixed learner, while requiring different geometry.





Relation to approximation and sampling.



Regression coresets approximate a full objective or its solution
(Feldman & Langberg, 2011; Drineas et al., 2006; Dasgupta et al., 2009; Boutsidis et al., 2013). Sketching and leverage-based methods provide
computational and statistical guarantees
(Clarkson & Woodruff, 2009; Clarkson & Woodruff, 2017; Pilanci & Wainwright, 2015; Ma et al., 2015; Raskutti & Mahoney, 2016); volume sampling gives randomized
subset guarantees (Dereziński & Warmuth, 2018). Here we instead determine
a deterministic infimum over reweighted original supports at a fixed
small budget, including deficient-rank designs. Positive-basis theory
(Davis, 1954; Nævdal, 2019) supplies geometric inputs;
the additional requirement is to implement the resulting certificate
by positive-weight training with the correct minimum-norm output.

Proposition 5 (Exhaustive circuit-rank split).


For every finite root system satisfying (6), exactly
one of the following circuit configurations occurs.


(i) 


A positive circuit has rank four or five. Then δ6≤1\delta_{6}\leq 1.


(ii) 


All circuits have rank at most three, and two rank-three circuits
share a root and jointly span ℝ5\mathbb{R}^{5}. Then δ6≤1\delta_{6}\leq 1.


(iii) 


A rank-three circuit exists, every circuit has rank at most three,
and the shared-root configuration in (ii) is absent. Then either the
two-line quotient argument gives δ6≤1\delta_{6}\leq 1, or two circuit spans
of dimensions three and two cover EE, giving δ6≤6/5\delta_{6}\leq 6/5.


(iv) 


All circuits have rank at most two. Either a four-plus-one
decomposition (or an independent-line system) gives δ6≤1\delta_{6}\leq 1,
or the circuit-plane graph gives δ6≤6/5\delta_{6}\leq 6/5.



In (ii)–(iii), the good-boundary-root alternative is first discharged
with bound one; the exceptional-root assertions concern the other alternative.





Why the split is sufficient.



The four configurations are disjoint and exhaustive by the maximum
circuit rank and the presence of a shared-root rank-three pair. A rank-five
circuit trains exactly at zero. For a rank-four circuit, one maximum
absolute normal projection supplies the interpolating row and squared
norm at most one. The shared-root case is handled by signed polar faces,
as explained below and proved in Appendix B.3.
For (iii), quotienting by a rank-three circuit either yields only two
independent lines, or yields a seven-root positive generating subset.
The two extreme rays of its relation cone have supports of sizes
(4,3)(4,3), disjoint, or (4,4)(4,4), sharing one root. The latter is excluded
by (iii). In the former, any crossing exceptional root either forces
the forbidden shared-root pair or reduces all exceptional projections
to two quotient lines; with no crossing exceptional roots the (3,2)(3,2)
cover remains. The support and crossing arguments are given in
Appendix B.5. Finally, circuit-plane intersections
give the alternatives in (iv), proved in Appendix B.4.
The next two subsections derive the constant in the branches that need
the full excess 6/56/5.

3.2 Second mechanism: circuit-plane graphs and actual risk



Suppose all circuits have rank at most two. Form a graph GG whose
vertices are distinct planes of rank-two circuits, joining two planes
when their intersection is zero. If GG has a triangle or four-cycle,
the circuit geometry forces every root into a four-space and a
complementary line. Their evaluation second moments are orthogonal
projections, so the four-dimensional five-row input and one interpolated
line use six rows with total excess at most one.



Otherwise GG has no triangles or four-cycles. Neighbors of a vertex
are pairwise intersecting planes. They either share a line or lie in
a three-space. Circuit elimination bounds these alternatives by two
and three neighbors, respectively (Lemma 19), hence
Δ⁡(G)≤3\Delta(G)\leq 3. If there are no circuit planes, all roots lie on five
independent lines and the maximum-absolute-coefficient interpolant gives
bound one. We therefore consider a nonempty GG.

3.3 The shared-root branch: the invariant that closes the cases

A.4 Convex-geometric preliminaries



We record the two external convex-geometric inputs and the positivity facts
used below. If a finite set GG spans a linear space VV and zero is a
strictly positive convex combination of all points in GG, then




0∈relintV⁡conv⁡(G).0\in\operatorname{relint}_{V}\operatorname{conv}(G).



Indeed, a supporting functional at zero would be nonnegative on GG.
Applying it to the strictly positive combination forces it to vanish on every
point of GG, and hence on VV, a contradiction. Conversely, if
0∈relint⁡conv⁡(G)0\in\operatorname{relint}\operatorname{conv}(G), then zero has a convex representation with a
strictly positive coefficient on every point of GG. This is the standard
identity that a linear map sends the relative interior of a simplex onto the
relative interior of its image.



Carathéodory’s theorem gives a small convex representation, but such a
representation need not retain the full-dimensional relative-interior
property required here Scherk (1966).



We use Steinitz’s theorem in the following relative-interior form: if zero is
in the relative interior of the convex hull of a finite subset of an
rr-dimensional linear space, then a subset of at most 2​r2r points still
has an rr-dimensional convex hull containing zero in its relative interior
Steinitz (1916); Ivanov & Naszódi (2024).



We also use the extremal structure of a positive basis. An
inclusion-minimal positive spanning set in an mm-dimensional space has at
most 2​m2m vectors; if it has exactly 2​m2m, then, after rescaling and
reindexing, it is




{ajvj,−bjvj:j∈[m]},aj,bj>0,\{a_{j}v_{j},-b_{j}v_{j}:j\in[m]\},\qquad a_{j},b_{j}>0,



for a basis v1,…,vmv_{1},\ldots,v_{m}. This is the maximal-cardinality case of the
positive-basis structure theorem; see
Davis (1954); Regis (2016) and the discussion preceding
Theorem 2 of Nævdal (2019).

B.4 Systems whose positive circuits have rank at most two



Assume now that every positive circuit has rank at most two. A
circuit plane is the span of an actual positive triangle, that is,

a coefficient perturbation until one coefficient vanishes. If two such
representations give opposite directions in subspaces intersecting in a
line, their union has exactly one dependence and is a positive circuit.

B.6 Transfer to the original weighted regression problem



We use the four-dimensional five-point upper bound two from
(Zhang, 2026, Theorem 47), including its construction by

Appendix E Exact verification and proof index




E.1 Reproduction and scope



The anonymous source package includes
verification/verify_exact.py. With Python 3.12 and SymPy 1.13.2,
run the following from the package root:
python -m pip install -r verification/requirements.txt
python verification/verify_exact.py


The script uses exact rational arithmetic and symbolic identities. It
writes verification/exact_check_results.json, including the
hashes of the script and the five-dimensional proof source. It requires
no dataset, network connection after installation, or access to a prior
draft. The reported checks cover algebraic identities, selected symbolic
ranks, a finite rational grid for one boundary-rank diagnostic, all
nonempty supports of size at most six in the seven-point example, and
the explicit path/cycle/star probability constructions. The script is
not a formal proof assistant and does not test all root configurations.



The lower-bound support check exhausts all 126 nonempty supports: 19
have full rank and 107 have deficient rank. On the full-rank supports it
checks the block counts used in the analytic argument; on every deficient
support it computes the minimum evaluation-norm squared distance from the
translation vector to the selected span. The smallest such distance is
five. It also solves the stated attaining full-rank selection and obtains
the ratio 11/511/5 exactly. The analytic interpolation argument in
Appendix B.7, rather than a finite sample of training
weights, covers arbitrary positive weights on each full-rank support.
