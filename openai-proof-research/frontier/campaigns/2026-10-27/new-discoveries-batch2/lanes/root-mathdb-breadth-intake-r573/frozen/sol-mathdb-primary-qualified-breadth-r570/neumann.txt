Abstract.
    
In this article, we study sharp bounds for the Neumann eigenvalues of the Laplace operator on graphs. We first obtain monotonicity results for the Neumann eigenvalues on trees. In particular, we show that increasing any number of boundary vertices while keeping interior vertices unchanged in a tree does not affect the Neumann eigenvalues. However, increasing an interior vertex to a tree reduces the value of corresponding Neumann eigenvalues. As a consequence of this result, we provide an upper bound for the second Neumann eigenvalue and a lower bound for the largest Neumann eigenvalue on trees. Then, we obtain a sharp upper bound for the second Neumann eigenvalue on paths in terms of its diameter, and as an application, we show that the second Neumann eigenvalue cannot be bounded below by a positive real number on the family of paths. We also prove that under a diameter constraint on trees, the largest Neumann eigenvalue cannot be bounded from above. Finally, we obtain a lower bound for the second Neumann eigenvalue on graphs.
  


Key words and phrases: Neumann eigenvalues, Trees, Graphs with boundary, Diameter, Laplacian, Variational Characterization

1. Introduction


The Neumann boundary value problem was introduced in the 19th century by Carl Gottfried Neumann, a German mathematician, while studying potential theory. On a bounded smooth domain  $M\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}$  with boundary  $\partial M$ , the Neumann eigenvalue problem is to find  $(\lambda,f)$  satisfying



(1.1)

 $\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta f(x)&=\lambda f(x),\quad x\in M,\\
\frac{\partial f}{\partial n}(x)&=0,\quad x\in\partial M.\end{cases}$ 


Here, real number  $\lambda$  is called a Neumann eigenvalue on  $M$  and  $f$  is called an eigenfunction corresponding to eigenvalue  $\lambda$ . The Neumann eigenvalues on  $M$  are discrete and can be arranged as  $0=\lambda_{1}(M)<\lambda_{2}(M)\leq\lambda_{3}(M)\cdots\nearrow\infty$ . The Neumann eigenvalues can also be interpreted as the frequency of a freely vibrating membrane.
These eigenvalues are also interesting from Spectral Geometry point of view, as they contain information about the geometry of the domain under consideration. The problem of finding bounds for Neumann eigenvalues in terms of geometric quatities has been studied extensively in past few years [8, 11, 14]. In general, it is not possible to calculate Neumann eigenvalues explicitly except for some particular type of domains. However, some conclusions can be drawn about these eigenvalues in terms of the geometry of the domain. To see this, we consider the following examples:


Example 1.

Consider a rectangular domain  $M=[0,a]\times[0,b]\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}$ . It is well known that the Neumann eigenvalues on domain  $M$  are given as




 $\displaystyle\lambda_{j,s}=\pi^{2}\left(\frac{j^{2}}{a^{2}}+\frac{s^{2}}{b^{2}}\right)\quad j,s=0,1,2,\dots.$ 


If we arrange  $\lambda_{j,s}$ ’s in an increasing order, then  $\lambda_{1}(M)=\lambda_{0,0}=0$  and value of each  $\lambda_{i}(M),2\leq i<\infty$  depends on the values of  $a$  and  $b$ .



Example 2.

Let  $M_{\epsilon}$  denote a dumbbell domain in  $\mathbb{R}^{2}$  (Fig. 1), consisting of two identical domains which are connected by a strip of width  $\epsilon$ . Then the second Neumann eigenvalue  $\lambda_{2}(M_{\epsilon})$  on  $M_{\epsilon}$  approaches to  $0$  as  $\epsilon\to 0$ . For more details, see [9].

From the above examples, we conclude the following well known behaviour of Neumann eigenvalues on domains contained in the Euclidean space.

(1) 

Monotonicity Property: In Fig. 2, we consider domains  $M_{1}$ ,  $M_{2},M_{3}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}$ , where  $M_{1}$  is a large dumbbell-shaped domain,  $M_{2}$  is a rectangular domain, and  $M_{3}$  is a small dumbbell-shaped domain, with  $M_{3}\subseteq M_{2}\subseteq M_{1}$ . For  $M_{1}$  and  $M_{3}$ , by choosing width of the strip very small positive real number,  $\lambda_{2}(M_{1})$  and  $\lambda_{2}(M_{3})$  can be made arbitrarily small. Therefore, by keeping  $M_{2}$  fixed and taking strip width of  $M_{1}$  and  $M_{3}$  arbitrary small, using example 1 and 2, we get




 $\displaystyle\lambda_{2}(M_{1})\leq\lambda_{2}(M_{2})\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{2}(M_{3})\leq\lambda_{2}(M_{2}),$ 


where  $\lambda_{2}$  represents the second Neumann eigenvalue. Thus monotonicity property does not hold for Neumann eigenvalues.

(2) 

Lower bound under diameter constraint: From Example 2, we see that given any positive real numbers  $D,c$ , it is always possible to construct a dumbbell of diameter  $D$  having strip width small enough such that its second Neumann eigenvalue is smaller than real number  $c$ . Therefore, it is not possible to establish a positive lower bound for the second Neumann eigenvalue on domains under a diameter constraint.


Our plan is to explore the above properties of Neumann eigenvalues on trees (defined in Section 2) as trees are considered as a discrete analogue to Hadamard manifolds. A Hadamard manifold is defined as a simply connected Riemannian manifold with non positive sectional curvature.


Studying eigenvalue problems on graphs helps us understand spectrum of corresponding differential operators on manifolds. It is worth to mention some classical articles where eigenvalues on graphs have been explored and that information has been utilized to study the Laplacian spectrum on manifolds. Following the work of Buser [3], it has been recognized that discretizing a compact Riemannian manifold can be a highly effective approach for studying the spectrum of the Laplacian on that manifold. The idea of discretizing a manifold has also been used to study the Steklov eigenvalues [7]. For more results related to this technique, see [1, 2, 4, 5, 17].


Recently, various studies have been carried out for Steklov eigenvalues on different graphs. In [18], the author provided lower bounds for the first Steklov eigenvalue on graphs in terms of the extrinsic diameter of the boundary. He-Hua provided sharp upper bounds for Steklov eigenvalues on trees depending on the size of the boundary and the diameter respectively in [13]. Some comparison results between Steklov eigenvalues and Laplace eigenvalues on graphs has been studied in [21]. A monotonicity result for the second Steklov eigenvalue on trees has been proved in [12] and it was further extended to more general graphs than trees for higher Steklov eigenvalues in [23].
For more results related to Steklov eigenvalues on Graphs, we refer to [16, 19, 20] and references therein. In [22], the authors investigated Dirichlet, Neumann and Laplacian eigenvalues on weighted graphs. As applications, they derived Lichnerowicz-type, Fiedler-type and Friedman-type estimates for Dirichlet eigenvalues and Neumann eigenvalues. Monotonicity property of Neumann eigenvalues and Dirichlet eigenvalues have been obtained for some particular type of graphs in [10]. Although, there are several results available in the literature about Steklov eigenvalues on graphs, the Neumann eigenvalues on graphs are not explored much.


In this article, we study the monotonicity property of Neumann eigenvalues on trees. We prove that the Neumann eigenvalues decrease as we add new edges to the tree such that the graph obtained by adding these edges is again a tree. We also provide a result which states that any two consecutive Neumann eigenvalues on a tree  $G_{2}$  are separated by exactly one Neumann eigenvalue on  $G_{1}$  and vice versa, where  $G_{2}$  is obtained from  $G_{1}$  by adding a new vertex adjacent to a boundary vertex of  $G_{1}$  (Theorem 4.1). As a consequence of this monotonicity property, we obtain that on any tree the second Neumann eigenvalue is bounded above by  $2$  and the largest Neumann eigenvalue is bounded below by  $2$ . Next, we give an upper bound for the second Neumann eigenvalue on paths in terms of its diameter (Theorem 5.1). As a corollary of this result, we conclude that it is not possible to find a positive lower bound of the second Neumann eigenvalue on the collection of paths. Next, we prove that on family  $\mathcal{G}$  of all trees with diameter  $D$ , The set  $\{\lambda_{max}(G)|G\in\mathcal{G}\}$  is not bounded above, where  $\lambda_{max}(G)$  is the largest Neumann eigenvalue on  $G$  (Theorem 5.2). Finally, we obtain a lower bound for the second Neumann eigenvalue on graphs (Theorem 6.1).


This article is organised as follows. In Section 2, we give some basic graph terminology to state the Neumann eigenvalue problem and present variational characterizations for Neumann eigenvalues. In Section 3, some auxilliary results are proved which are crucial for obtaining the main results of this article. An interesting observation about Neumann eigenfunctions on trees is also mentioned in Remark 3.1, which is used very frequently in this article. In Section 4, we have presented the monotonicity results for Neumann eigenvalues on trees. Certain behaviour of Neumann eigenvalues on trees under diameter constraint have been discussed in Section 5. A lower bound for the second Neumann eigenvalue on graphs has been obtained in Section 6.



2. Preliminaries


2.1. Graph Terminology


Let  $G=(V,E)$  be a simple undirected graph with vertex set  $V$  and edge set  $E$ . Two vertices  $x,y\in V$  are called adjacent, denoted by  $x\sim y$  or  $(x,y)\in E$ , if there is an edge connecting  $x$  and  $y$ . A path of length  $\ell$  in  $G$  is a sequence of distinct  $(\ell+1)$  vertices  $v_{0},v_{1},\cdots,v_{\ell}$  such that consecutive vertices are adjacent. This path in  $G$  is called a closed path if  $v_{0}$  coincide with  $v_{\ell}$ . A closed path is called a cycle. A graph without cycle is called non-cylic. The distance  $d(x,y)$  between two vertices  $x,y\in V$  is the length of the shortest path connecting them. The diameter  $D$  of  $G$  is the maximum distance among all pairs of vertices, namely,  $D=\displaystyle\max_{x,y\in V}d(x,y)$ .
A graph  $G$  is connected if for every pair of vertices  $x,y\in V$ , there exists a path connecting them. A tree is a non-cyclic connected graph. In other words, a tree is a connected graph with  $|V|-1$  edges. The degree of a vertex  $v\in V$ , denoted  $\deg(v)$ , is the number of edges incident to it, given by,  $\deg(v)=|\{u\in V:u\sim v\}|$ . For any  $S\subset V$ , we define two fundamental boundaries. The edge boundary of  $S$ , denoted  $\partial S$ , is set of edges connecting  $S$  to its complement  $S^{c}$ , defined as  $\partial S$  =  $\{\{i,j\}\in E\mid i\in S,j\in S^{c}\}$ . The vertex boundary of  $S$ , denoted  $\delta S$ , is set of vertices in  $S^{c}$  that are adjacent to  $S$ , given by,  $\delta S=\{i\in S^{c}\mid i\sim j\text{ for some }j\in S\}$ . A graph with boundary is a pair  $(G,B)$  where  $B\subset V(G)$  which satisfies  $\delta(V\setminus B)=B$  and there is no edge connecting two vertices of  $B$  i.e.,  $E(B,B)=\emptyset$ . The set  $B$  is called the boundary and  $\Omega_{G}=V\setminus B$  is called the interior of the graph G. Note that  $B=\delta\Omega_{G}$ .


In this article, we will mostly study Neumann eigenvalue problem on graph  $G$  with total  $n$  vertices and  $k$  interior vertices that is,  $|V|=n$  and  $|\Omega_{G}|=k$ . In case if  $G$  is a tree, we consider  $n\geq 3$  to exclude the case  $\Omega_{G}=\emptyset$  or  $k=0$ . All vertices of degree one in tree  $G$  are considered as boundary vertices of  $G$ .


Now we state the Neumann eigenvalue problem on a graph.



2.2. Neumann Eigenvalue Problem


Let  $G$  be a graph with interior  $\Omega_{G}$  and boundary  $\delta\Omega_{G}$ . The vector space of real-valued functions on set of vertices  $V$  over  $\mathbb{R}$ , denoted by  $\mathbb{R}^{V}$ , is a finite-dimensional Euclidean space. For any  $f,g\in\mathbb{R}^{V}$ , we consider the inner product  $\langle f,g\rangle_{\Omega_{G}}=\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\Omega_{G}}f(x)g(x)$ . For  $f\in\mathbb{R}^{V}$ , the Laplace operator on graph is defined as




 $\displaystyle(\Delta f)(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in V\\
y\sim x\end{subarray}}(f(x)-f(y)),\quad x\in\Omega_{G}.$ 




Within this framework, for any  $f\in\mathbb{R}^{V}$  and  $x\in\delta\Omega_{G}$ , the outward normal derivative operator  $\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}:\mathbb{R}^{V}\to\mathbb{R}^{\delta\Omega_{G}}$ ,
which maps  $f$  to  $\frac{\partial f}{\partial\nu}$ , is expressed as




 $\frac{\partial f}{\partial\nu}(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in{\Omega_{G}}\\
y\sim x\end{subarray}}(f(x)-f(y))=(\Delta f)(x).$ 




We now introduce the Neumann Eigenvalue problem for the Laplace operator on graphs with boundary  $(G,\delta\Omega_{G})$ . This problem is formulated as finding a nonzero function  $f\in\mathbb{R}^{V}$  and a real number  $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$ , which satisfy the following



(2.1)

 $\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta f(x)&=\lambda f(x),\quad x\in\Omega_{G},\\
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\nu}(x)&=0,\quad x\in\delta\Omega_{G}.\end{cases}$ 


The operator  $\Lambda$  corresponding to Neumann eigenvalue problem is given as follows. Define map  $\Lambda:\mathbb{R}^{\Omega_{G}}\to\mathbb{R}^{V}$  as




 $\displaystyle\Lambda(f)(x)=\begin{cases}f(x),&x\in\Omega_{G},\\
\frac{1}{\operatorname{deg}(x)}\displaystyle\sum_{y\sim x}f(y),&x\in\delta\Omega_{G},\end{cases}$ 


for any  $f\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega_{G}}$ . It is straightforward to verify that for each  $x\in\delta\Omega_{G}$ ,




 $\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\Lambda(f))(x)}{\partial\nu}=0.$ 


The Neumann eigenvalues on the pair  $(G,\delta\Omega_{G})$  are same as the eigenvalues of the operator referred to as the Neumann Laplacian operator  $\Delta^{N}:\mathbb{R}^{\Omega_{G}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\Omega_{G}}$ , where




 $\displaystyle\Delta^{N}f=\Delta(\Lambda(f))|_{\Omega_{{}_{G}}}.$ 


Now, we show that  $\Delta^{N}$  is a non-negative self-adjoint operator. For any  $f\in\mathbb{R}^{V}$ , first we define a map  $d:\mathbb{R}^{V}\to A^{1}(G)$  as




 $\displaystyle df(x,y):=\begin{cases}f(y)-f(x),&\{x,y\}\in E,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$ 


Here  $A^{1}(G)$  is the space of skew-symmetric functions  $\alpha$  on  $V\times V$  such that  $\alpha(x,y)=0$  when  $x\nsim y$ , equipped with inner product  $\langle.,.\rangle$ 



(2.2)

 $\displaystyle\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in V}\alpha(x,y)\beta(x,y)=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}\alpha(x,y)\beta(x,y).$ 


Then for  $f,g\in\mathbb{R}^{V}$ , it follows easily by direct computation that




 $\displaystyle\langle\Delta^{N}f,g\rangle_{\Omega_{G}}=\langle d(f),d(g)\rangle.$ 


Thus operator  $\Delta^{N}$  is self-adjoint and non-negative and hence, all its eigenvalues are real and non negative. Therefore, for a graph  $G$ , there are  $|\Omega_{G}|$  Neumann eigenvalues. These eigenvalues are positive and can be arranged in an increasing order.




 $\displaystyle 0=\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{|\Omega_{G}|}.$ 


Here  $\lambda_{1}=0$  because constant functions are eigenfunctions for the Neumann Laplacian operator,  $\lambda_{2}>0$  due to the assumption that  $G$  is connected.
In the next proposition, we present some well-known variational characterizations for  $\lambda_{i},1\leq i\leq|\Omega_{G}|$  and their proofs are similar to the proof of variational characterization of Laplace eigenvalues [6].


Proposition 2.1.

Let  $G$  be a graph with  $n$  vertices and  $k$  interior vertices. For  $i\leq k$ , let  $V_{i}=\operatorname{span}\{f^{1},f^{2},\ldots,f^{i}\}$ , where  $f^{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$  is the eigenfunction corresponding to Neumann eigenvalue  $\lambda_{j},1\leq j\leq k$  on  $G$ . Then the  $i$ -th Neumann eigenvalue satisfies the following characterization



(2.3)

 $\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(G)=R(f^{i},G)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in V_{i}\\
f\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(f,G)$ 


(2.4)


 $\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in V_{i-1}^{\perp}\\
f\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(f,G)$ 


(2.5)


 $\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
\dim E=i\end{subarray}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in E\\
f\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(f,G)$ 


(2.6)


 $\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
\dim E=i-1\end{subarray}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in E^{\perp}\\
f\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(f,G).$ 


Here E is a subspace of  $\mathbb{R}^{n}$  and  $R(f,G)=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\sim v_{j}}(f(v_{s})-f(v_{j}))^{2}}{\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}}f(v_{s})^{2}}$  is the Rayleigh quotient. The sum  $\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\sim v_{j}}$  is taken over all unordered pairs  ${v_{s},v_{j}}$  for which  $v_{s}$  and  $v_{j}$  are adjacent. The space  $E^{\perp}$  is defined as




 $\displaystyle E^{\perp}=\{f\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ \langle f,g\rangle_{\Omega_{G}}=0\text{ for all }g\in E\}.$ 







3. Some Auxiliary Results


In this section, we provide some results that are used to prove the main theorems of this article. Using the definition of linearly independent vectors, the following lemma can be concluded easily.


Lemma 3.1.

Let  $s\leq n$ , Consider a set of  $s$  vectors  $\{\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2},...,\mathbf{q}_{s}\}$  in  $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$  defined as




 $\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{1}$ 
 $\displaystyle=(a_{1,1},a_{1,2},\ldots,a_{1,n},a_{1,n+1}),$ 



 $\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{2}$ 
 $\displaystyle=(a_{2,1},a_{2,2},\ldots,a_{2,n},a_{2,n+1}),$ 




 $\displaystyle\quad\vdots$ 



 $\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{s}$ 
 $\displaystyle=(a_{s,1},a_{s,2},\ldots,a_{s,n},a_{s,n+1}),$ 


such that  $a_{t,n+1}=a_{t,j_{0}}$  for all  $1\leq t\leq s$  and for some fix  $1\leq j_{0}\leq n$ . Then vectors  $\{\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2},...,\mathbf{q}_{s}\}$  are linearly independent if and only if the following set of vectors in  $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ 




 $\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{1}$ 
 $\displaystyle=(a_{1,1},a_{1,2},\ldots,a_{1,n}),$ 



 $\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{2}$ 
 $\displaystyle=(a_{2,1},a_{2,2},\ldots,a_{2,n}),$ 




 $\displaystyle\quad\vdots$ 



 $\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{s}$ 
 $\displaystyle=(a_{s,1},a_{s,2},\ldots,a_{s,n}),$ 


obtained by omitting the  $(n+1)$ -th component of each  $\mathbf{q}_{t}$ , are linearly independent.



In the following lemma, we discuss about the Rayleigh quotient of a function which is a linear combination of Neumann eigenfunctions.


Lemma 3.2.

Let  $G$  be a graph with total  $n$  vertices and  $k$  interior vertices. Denote the Neumann eigenvalues of  $G$  by  $\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{k}$ . Consider a function  $h$  defined as  $h=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\beta_{i}f^{m_{i}}$ , where  $\beta_{1},\beta_{2},...,\beta_{p}$  are scalar coefficients, and  $f^{m_{1}},f^{m_{2}},\ldots,f^{m_{p}}$  are orthogonal eigenfunctions corresponding to eigenvalues  $\lambda_{m_{1}}\leq\lambda_{m_{2}}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{m_{p}}$  with  $1\leq m_{1},m_{2},\cdots,m_{p}\leq k$ . Then the Rayleigh quotient of function  $h$  satisfies




 $\displaystyle\lambda_{m_{1}}(G)\leq R(h,G)\leq\lambda_{m_{p}}(G).$ 


Further,  $\lambda_{m_{1}}(G)=\displaystyle\min_{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{p}}R(h,G)$  and  $\lambda_{m_{p}}(G)=\displaystyle\max_{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{p}}R(h,G)$ .



Proof.

If  $p=1$ , let  $h=\beta_{1}f^{m_{1}}$ , where  $f^{m_{1}}$  is an eigenfunction corresponding to the eigenvalue  $\lambda_{m_{1}}(G)$ . Then Rayleigh quotient of function  $h$  is given as




 $\displaystyle R(h,G)=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\sim v_{j}}(\beta_{1}f^{m_{1}}(v_{s})-\beta_{1}f^{m_{1}}(v_{j}))^{2}}{\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}}(\beta_{1}f^{m_{1}}(v_{s}))^{2}}=\frac{\beta_{1}^{2}\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\sim v_{j}}(f^{m_{1}}(v_{s})-f^{m_{1}}(v_{j}))^{2}}{\beta_{1}^{2}\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}}f^{m_{1}}(v_{s})^{2}}=R(f^{m_{1}},G).$ 


By equation (2.3) of Proposition 2.1,  $R(h,G)=R(f^{m_{1}},G)=\lambda_{m_{1}}$ .


If  $p=2$ , then function  $h$  will be of the form  $h=\beta_{1}f^{m_{1}}+\beta_{2}f^{m_{2}}$ , where  $f^{m_{i}}$  is an eigenfunction corresponding to eigenvalue  $\lambda_{m_{i}}(G)$  for  $i=1,2$ . Now we simplify Rayleigh quotient of  $h$  which is given as




 $\displaystyle R(h,G)=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\sim v_{j}}(h(v_{s})-h(v_{j}))^{2}}{\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}}h(v_{s})^{2}}.$ 


We begin by calculating the numerator



(3.1)

 $\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\sim v_{j}}(h(v_{s})-h(v_{j}))^{2}=\sum_{v_{s}\in V}h(v_{s})\left(h(v_{s})d_{v_{s}}-\sum_{v_{s}\sim v_{j}}h(v_{j})\right).$ 


Now, at any boundary vertex  $v_{s}\in\delta\Omega_{G},$ 




 $\displaystyle f^{m_{t}}(v_{s})d_{v_{s}}-\sum_{v_{j}\sim v_{s}}f^{m_{t}}(v_{j})=0\text{ for }t=1,2.$ 


Similarly, at any interior vertex  $v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}$ ,




 $\displaystyle f^{m_{t}}(v_{s})d_{v_{s}}-\sum_{v_{j}\sim v_{s}}f^{m_{t}}(v_{j})=\lambda_{m_{t}}f^{m_{t}}(v_{s})\text{ for }t=1,2.$ 


Using above conditions equation (3.1) can be written as




 $\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\sim v_{j}}(h(v_{s})-h(v_{j}))^{2}=\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}}\left(\beta_{1}f^{m_{1}}(v_{s})+\beta_{2}f^{m_{2}}(v_{s})\right)\left(\beta_{1}\lambda_{m_{1}}f^{m_{1}}(v_{s})+\beta_{2}\lambda_{m_{2}}f^{m_{2}}(v_{s})\right).$ 


The orthogonality condition  $\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}}f^{m_{1}}(v_{s})f^{m_{2}}(v_{s})=0$  gives




 $\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\sim v_{j}}(h(v_{s})-h(v_{j}))^{2}=\beta_{1}^{2}\lambda_{m_{1}}\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}}f^{m_{1}}(v_{s})^{2}+\beta_{2}^{2}\lambda_{m_{2}}\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}}f^{m_{2}}(v_{s})^{2}.$ 


The denominator of  $R(h,G)$  can be expressed as




 $\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}}h(v_{s})^{2}=\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}}\left(\beta_{1}f^{m_{1}}(v_{s})+\beta_{2}f^{m_{2}}(v_{s})\right)^{2}=\beta_{1}^{2}\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}}f^{m_{1}}(v_{s})^{2}+\beta_{2}^{2}\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}}f^{m_{2}}(v_{s})^{2}.$ 


Therefore,




 $\displaystyle R(h,G)=\frac{\displaystyle\beta_{1}^{2}\lambda_{m_{1}}\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}}f^{m_{1}}(v_{s})^{2}+\beta_{2}^{2}\lambda_{m_{2}}\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}}f^{m_{2}}(v_{s})^{2}}{\beta_{1}^{2}\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}}f^{m_{1}}(v_{s})^{2}+\displaystyle\beta_{2}^{2}\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}}f^{m_{2}}(v_{s})^{2}}.$ 


Since  $\lambda_{m_{1}}\leq\lambda_{m_{2}}$ , we conclude that  $\lambda_{m_{1}}(G)\leq R(h,G)\leq\lambda_{m_{2}}(G)$ . Further, for  $\beta_{1}=1$  and  $\beta_{2}=0,R(h,G)=\lambda_{m_{1}}(G)$ , and for  $\beta_{1}=0$  and  $\beta_{2}=1,R(h,G)=\lambda_{m_{2}}(G)$ . Thus  $\lambda_{m_{1}}(G)=\displaystyle\min_{\beta_{1},\beta_{2}}R(h,G)$  and  $\lambda_{m_{2}}(G)=\displaystyle\max_{\beta_{1},\beta_{2}}R(h,G)$ .
Using the same idea, the desired result can be proved easily for any function  $h=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\beta_{i}f^{m_{i}}$ , where  $\beta_{1},\beta_{2},...,\beta_{p}$  are scalar coefficients for any  $p\in\mathbb{N}$ .

Remark 3.1.

Recall that in a tree graph  $G$ , all vertices of degree one are considered as the boundary of the graph. Thus any boundary vertex is adjacent to exactly one interior vertex. Let  $f$  be an eigenfunction corresponding to Neumann eigenvalue  $\lambda(G)$  and  $v_{t}$  be any boundary vertex adjacent to the interior vertex  $v_{s}$ . Then using the Neumann boundary condition on boundary vertex  $v_{t}$ , we get  $f(v_{t})-f(v_{s})=0$  i.e.,  $f(v_{t})=f(v_{s}).$ 



Using Remark 3.1, Lemma 3.1 and 3.2, a simplified expression of variational characterization of Neumann eigenvalues on trees is established in the following lemma.


Proposition 3.1.

Let  $G$  be a tree on  $n$  vertices, out of which there are  $k$  interior vertices. Then for  $1\leq i\leq k$ ,  $\lambda_{i}$  can also be characterized as



(3.2)

 $\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(G)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{E}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}\\
\dim\tilde{E}=i\end{subarray}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{f}\in\tilde{E}\\
\tilde{f}\neq 0\end{subarray}}R({f},G).$ 


Here for any function  $\tilde{f}$  defined on  $\Omega_{G}$ , its extension  $f$  on  $G$  is defined as follows



(3.3)

 $\displaystyle f(v_{j})=\begin{cases}\tilde{f}(v_{j}),\quad v_{j}\in\Omega_{G},\\
\tilde{f}(v_{l}),\quad v_{j}\in{\delta\Omega_{G}},\end{cases}$ 


where  $v_{l}\in\Omega_{G}$  is such that  $v_{j}\sim v_{l}.$ 



Proof.

We know that




 $\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(G)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}E\subset\mathbb{R}^{|V|}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
\dim E=i\end{subarray}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in E\\
g\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(g,G).$ 


Let




 $\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(G)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{E}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}\\
\dim\tilde{E}=i\end{subarray}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{f}\in\tilde{E}\\
\tilde{f}\neq 0\end{subarray}}R({f},G).$ 


Then our aim is to prove  $\lambda_{i}(G)=\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(G)$ , for which we first show that  $\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(G)\leq\lambda_{i}(G)$  for all  $1\leq i\leq k$ .
We know that  $\lambda_{i}(G)$  is achieved by  $E=\operatorname{span}\{g^{1},g^{2},\ldots,g^{i}\}$ , where  $g^{j}$  is an eigenfunction corresponding to  $\lambda_{j}(G)$ ,  $1\leq j\leq k$ . Also, by Remark 3.1,  $g^{j}(v_{s})=g^{j}(v_{l})$ ,  $1\leq j\leq k$  for any  $v_{l}\in\Omega_{G},v_{s}\in\delta\Omega_{G}$  and  $v_{s}\sim v_{l}$ . Let  $v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}$  be interior vertices of  $G$ . Define  $\tilde{f}^{j}(v_{i})=g^{j}(v_{i})$  for  $1\leq i\leq k$ , the restriction of function  $g^{j}$  on the interior vertices. Note that the extension  $f^{j}$  of function  $\tilde{f}^{j}$ , defined as in (3.3), is equal to function  $g^{j}$  on G.


Consider  $\tilde{E}=\operatorname{span}\{\tilde{f^{1}},\tilde{f^{2}},...,\tilde{f^{i}}\}$  for  $1\leq i\leq k$ .
Then by Lemma 3.1,  $\tilde{E}$  is an  $i$ -dimensional subspace of  $\mathbb{R}^{k}$ ,



(3.4)

 $\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(G)\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{f}\in\tilde{E}\\
\tilde{f}\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(f,G)=\lambda_{i}(G).$ 


The first inequality holds by the definition of  $\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(G)$ . Since function  $f^{j}=g^{j}$  on G, the second equality holds as a consequence of Expression 2.3 of Proposition 2.1.


Similarly, to show that  $\lambda_{i}(G)\leq\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(G)$ , let  $\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(G)$  is achieved by  $\tilde{E}=\operatorname{span}\{\tilde{h}^{1},\tilde{h}^{2},...,\tilde{h}^{i}\}$ 
which is an  $i$ -dimensional subspace of  $\mathbb{R}^{k}$ . Let  ${h^{j}}$  be extension of  $\tilde{h}^{j}$  defined as in equation (3.3). Set  $E=\operatorname{span}\{h^{1},h^{2},...,h^{i}\}$ , then by Lemma 3.1,  $E$  is an  $i$ -dimensional subspace of  $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ . Using expression (2.5) of variational characterization given in Proposition 2.1 and Lemma 3.2,



(3.5)

 $\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(G)\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in E\\
h\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(h,G)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{h}\in\tilde{E}\\
\tilde{h}\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(h,G)=\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(G).$ 


Combining both inequalities (3.4) and (3.5) gives




 $\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(G)=\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(G).$ 


This completes the proof.
∎



Remark 3.2.

Using Proposition 3.1, we get the following simplified characterization of  $\lambda_{k}(G),$ 




 $\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(G)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\\
\tilde{f}\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(f,G).$ 


Here  $k$  is the number of interior vertices in  $G$  and  $f$  is the extension of  $\tilde{f}$  on  $G$  defined in (3.3).



The following lemma is introduced to ensure a one-to-one correspondence between the orthogonal complement of certain subspaces of  $\mathbb{R}^{n}$  and  $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ , respectively. This will help us to use variational characterization (2.6) in proof of Proposition 4.2.


Lemma 3.3.

Let { $f^{1},f^{2},\ldots,f^{i-1}$ } be an orthogonal set of vectors in  $\mathbb{R}^{n}$  with  $f^{\alpha}=(f^{\alpha}_{1},f^{\alpha}_{2},\ldots,f^{\alpha}_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ for }1\leq\alpha\leq i-1,$  then  $E=\operatorname{span}\{f^{1},f^{2},\ldots,f^{i-1}\}$  is an  $(i-1)$ -dimensional subspace of  $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ . Define subspace  $\widetilde{E}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$  as




 $\displaystyle\widetilde{E}=\operatorname{span}\{\widetilde{f}^{1},\widetilde{f}^{2},\ldots,\widetilde{f}^{i-1},u_{l}\},$ 


where  $l$  is fixed natural number between  $1$  to  $n$  and  $\widetilde{f}^{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$  is defined by




 $\displaystyle\widetilde{f}^{\alpha}_{j}=\begin{cases}f^{\alpha}_{j},&\text{for }j=1,2,\ldots,n\text{ with }j\neq l,\\
0,&\text{for }j=l,\\
f^{\alpha}_{l},&\text{for }j=n+1,\end{cases}$ 


and  $u_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$  is defined as




 $u_{l}=(0,0,\ldots,0,\underbrace{1}_{l\text{-th}},0,\ldots,0).$ 


Then the linear map  $\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$  defined by




 $\displaystyle\phi(\mathbf{v})=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{l-1},0,v_{l+1},\ldots,v_{n},v_{l})\text{ for }\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ 


is an isomorphism between  $E^{\perp}$  and  $\widetilde{E}^{\perp}$ .



Proof.

To establish a one-to-one correspondence between  $E^{\perp}$  and  $\widetilde{E}^{\perp}$ , we construct a bijective linear map between them. Note that  $\dim(\widetilde{E})=i$ , then  $\dim(\widetilde{E}^{\perp})=(n+1)-i=(n-i+1)$ . Since  $\dim(E^{\perp})=(n-i+1)$ , the spaces  $E^{\perp}$  and  $\widetilde{E}^{\perp}$  have the same dimension and therefore they are isomorphic.
Consider the linear map  $\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$  defined by




 $\displaystyle\mathbf{\tilde{v}}=\phi(\mathbf{v})=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{l-1},0,v_{l+1},\ldots,v_{n},v_{l}),$ 


where  $\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$  and  $\mathbf{\tilde{v}}=(\tilde{v}_{1},\tilde{v}_{2},\ldots,\tilde{v}_{n+1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ . Note that  $\widetilde{f}^{\alpha}=\phi({f}^{\alpha})$ . Also, for any  $\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ ,  $\mathbf{\tilde{v}}$  is orthogonal to  $u_{l}$ . The one-to-one correspondence between  $E^{\perp}$  and  $\widetilde{E}^{\perp}$  will be shown by restricting  $\phi$  to  $E^{\perp}$ . For any  $\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ ,



(3.6)

 $\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{\tilde{v}},\mathbf{\tilde{w}}\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\tilde{v}_{j}\tilde{w}_{j}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\tilde{v}_{j}\tilde{w}_{j}+\tilde{v}_{n+1}\tilde{w}_{n+1}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq l\end{subarray}}^{n}v_{j}w_{j}+v_{l}w_{l}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}v_{j}w_{j}=\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\rangle.$ 


Thus,  $\langle\mathbf{\tilde{v}},\widetilde{f}^{\alpha}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{v},f^{\alpha}\rangle$  and  $\langle\mathbf{\tilde{v}},u_{l}\rangle=0$  for all  $\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ . Hence, linear map  $\phi$  is an isomorphism between  $E^{\perp}$  and  $\widetilde{E}^{\perp}$ . This completes the proof.
∎




4. Monotonicity property for Neumann eigenvalues


We are now prepared to present our first main result of this article which is the monotonicity property of the Neumann eigenvalues on trees.


Proposition 4.1.

Let  $G_{1}$  be a finite tree with total  $n$  vertices and  $k$  interior vertices, and tree  $G_{2}$  be obtained from  $G_{1}$  by adding a new vertex adjacent to some interior vertex of  $G_{1}$ . Then for  $1\leq i\leq k$ ,



(4.1)

 $\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(G_{1})=\lambda_{i}(G_{2}).$ 





Proof.

Let  $G_{1}$  be a finite tree having  $n$  vertices and  $k$  interior vertices with vertex set  $V_{1}=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\}$ . Let  $G_{2}$  be obtained from  $G_{1}$  by adding a new vertex, say  $v_{n+1}$  adjacent to some interior vertex  $v_{l}$  of  $G_{1}$ . Observe that the interior vertices in tree  $G_{2}$  are the same as the interior vertices in  $G_{1}$ . However,  $G_{2}$  has exactly one additional boundary vertex as compared to  $G_{1}$  (See Fig.3(a), 3(b)).
To establish the equality  $\lambda_{i}(G_{1})=\lambda_{i}(G_{2})$ , we first show that  $\lambda_{i}(G_{2})\leq\lambda_{i}(G_{1})$  for all  $1\leq i\leq k$ .
For any function  $f=(f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{n})$ , where  $f_{j}={f}(v_{j})$  for  $1\leq j\leq n$ , defined on  $G_{1}$ , we define its extension  $\widetilde{f}$  on  $G_{2}$  as




 $\displaystyle\widetilde{f}_{j}=\widetilde{f}(v_{j})=\begin{cases}f_{j},&\text{for }1\leq j\leq n,\\
f_{l},&\text{for }j=n+1,\end{cases}$ 


where  $v_{l}$  is the interior vertex of  $G_{1}$  to which vertex  $v_{n+1}$  is adjacent. Since there are  $k$  interior vertices, let  $f^{1},f^{2},\ldots,f^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$  be orthogonal eigenfunctions corresponding to  $\lambda_{1}(G_{1}),\lambda_{2}(G_{1}),\ldots,\lambda_{k}(G_{1})$ , respectively. For a fix  $i$ ,  $1\leq i\leq k$ , let  $E=\operatorname{span}\{f^{1},f^{2},\ldots,f^{i}\}$  be an  $i$ -dimensional subspace of  $\mathbb{R}^{n}$  and  $\widetilde{E}$  be the subspace of  $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$  generated by  $\widetilde{f}^{1},\widetilde{f}^{2},\ldots,\widetilde{f}^{i}$ .




 $\displaystyle\widetilde{E}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &(\alpha_{1}\widetilde{f}_{1}^{1}+\alpha_{2}\widetilde{f}_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{i}\widetilde{f}_{1}^{i},\\
&\alpha_{1}\widetilde{f}_{2}^{1}+\alpha_{2}\widetilde{f}_{2}^{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{i}\widetilde{f}_{2}^{i},\\
&\quad\vdots\\
&\alpha_{1}\widetilde{f}_{j}^{1}+\alpha_{2}\widetilde{f}_{j}^{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{j}\widetilde{f}_{j}^{i},\\
&\quad\vdots\\
&\alpha_{1}\widetilde{f}_{n+1}^{1}+\alpha_{2}\widetilde{f}_{n+1}^{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{i}\widetilde{f}_{n+1}^{i})\end{aligned}\ \Bigg|\ \alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}$ 




Since  $\dim({E})=i$ , by Lemma 3.1, we have  $\dim(\widetilde{E})=i$ . Recall the variational characterization given in equation (2.5),




 $\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(G_{2})=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\\
\dim(S)=i\end{subarray}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\widetilde{g}\in S\\
\widetilde{g}\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(\widetilde{g},G_{2}),$ 


where S is any  $i$ -dimensional subspace of  $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ . Since  $\widetilde{E}$  is a fixed  $i$ -dimensional subspace of  $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ , we obtain the inequality



(4.2)

 $\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(G_{2})\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\widetilde{g}\in\widetilde{E}\\
\widetilde{g}\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(\widetilde{g},G_{2})=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in E\\
g\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(g,G_{1})=\lambda_{i}(G_{1}).$ 




The first equality holds since the denominator will remain unchanged (as the interior vertices are same), and presence of one additional boundary vertex in  $G_{2}$  will not contribute anything to the numerator, since function value at it is same as the function value at the vertex adjacent to it i.e.,  $\widetilde{f}(v_{l})=\widetilde{f}(v_{n+1})$  . The second equality holds as a consequence of expression (2.3) of Proposition 2.1.


Conversely, we will show  $\lambda_{i}(G_{1})\leq\lambda_{i}(G_{2})$ .
Now, let  $\bar{f}=\operatorname{span}\{\bar{f}_{1},\bar{f}_{2},...,\bar{f}_{n+1}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$  be an eigenfunction corresponding to eigenvalue  $\lambda_{i}(G_{2})$ . Define a vector  $\dot{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$  (on the vertex set of  $G_{1}$  as the restriction of  $\bar{f}$  to  $G_{1}$ ),




 $\displaystyle\dot{f}_{j}=\bar{f}_{j}\quad\text{for all }j=1,\ldots,n.$ 




Let  $\bar{E}=(\bar{f^{1}},\bar{f^{2}},...,\bar{f^{i}})$  in  $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$  and define  $\dot{E}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}$ 
as  $\dot{E}=\operatorname{span}\{\dot{f^{1}},\dot{f^{2}},...,\dot{f^{i}}\}$  . By Lemma 3.1, both  $\dot{E}$  and  $\bar{E}$  are  $i$ -dimensional vector spaces.
Therefore, using the same argument as given for inequality (4.2), we get



(4.3)

 $\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(G_{1})\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\dot{f}\in\dot{E}\\
\dot{f}\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(\dot{f},G_{1})=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bar{f}\in\bar{E}\\
\bar{f}\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(\bar{f},G_{2})=\lambda_{i}(G_{2}).$ 


Combining both the inequalities (4.2) and (4.3) gives




 $\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(G_{1})=\lambda_{i}(G_{2}).$ 


As specified, the proof is complete.
∎

Proposition 4.2.

Let  $G_{1}$  be a finite tree with  $n$  vertices and  $k$  interior vertices, and tree  $G_{2}$  be obtained from  $G_{1}$  by adding a new vertex adjacent to some boundary vertex of  $G_{1}$ . Then for  $1\leq i\leq k$ ,

(1) 

 $\lambda_{i}(G_{2})\leq\lambda_{i}(G_{1})$ .

(2) 

 $\lambda_{i}(G_{1})\leq\lambda_{i+1}(G_{2})$ .





Proof.

Let  $G_{1}$  be a finite tree having  $n$  vertices and  $k$  interior vertices with vertex set  $V_{1}=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\}$ . Let  $G_{2}$  be obtained from  $G_{1}$  by adding a vertex, say  $v_{n+1}$ , adjacent to some boundary vertex  $v_{l}$  of  $G_{1}$ . The key observation is that number of boundary vertices in tree  $G_{2}$  are same as the boundary vertices in  $G_{1}$ . However,  $G_{2}$  has exactly one additional interior vertex than  $G_{1}$  (See Figs. 3(a) and 3(c)).


 $(1)$  We first show that  $\lambda_{i}(G_{2})\leq\lambda_{i}(G_{1})$  for all  $1\leq i\leq k$ . Let  $f^{1},f^{2},\ldots,f^{i}$  be orthogonal eigenfunctions corresponding to  $\lambda_{1}(G_{1}),\lambda_{2}(G_{1}),\ldots,\lambda_{i}(G_{1})$  respectively. Define  $\widetilde{f}^{\alpha}$  on  $G_{2}$  for  $\alpha=1,2,\ldots,i$  by




 $\displaystyle\widetilde{f}^{\alpha}_{j}=\widetilde{f}^{\alpha}(v_{j})=\begin{cases}f^{\alpha}(v_{j}),&\text{for }j=1,2,\ldots,l\ldots,n,\\
f^{\alpha}(v_{l}),&\text{for }j=n+1.\end{cases}$ 




Let  $E=\operatorname{span}\{f^{1},f^{2},\ldots,f^{i}\}$  and  $\widetilde{E}=\operatorname{span}\{\widetilde{f}^{1},\widetilde{f}^{2},\ldots,\widetilde{f}^{i}\}$ . By Lemma 3.1, both  $E$  and  $\widetilde{E}$  are  $i$ -dimensional vector spaces. Then




 $\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(G_{2})\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\widetilde{f}\in\widetilde{E}\\
\widetilde{f}\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(\widetilde{f},G_{2})\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in E\\
f\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(f,G_{1})=\lambda_{i}(G_{1}).$ 


The inequality  $\lambda_{i}(G_{2})\leq\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\widetilde{f}\in\widetilde{E}\\
\widetilde{f}\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(\widetilde{f},G_{2})$  holds by the variational characterization given in equation (2.5) and the fact that  $\widetilde{E}$  is an  $i$ -dimensional subspace of  $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ . The inequality
 $\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\widetilde{f}\in\widetilde{E}\\
\widetilde{f}\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(\widetilde{f},G_{2})\leq\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in E\\
f\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(f,G_{1})$  holds, since the numerator remains unchanged in both the cases; while the denominator of  $R(\widetilde{f},G_{2})$  is larger than the denominator of  $R(f,G_{1})$ , since  $G_{2}$  has one extra interior vertex than  $G_{1}$ . Finally  $\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in E\\
f\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(f,G_{1})=\lambda_{i}(G_{1})$  is true by Proposition 2.1. Therefore, we conclude that




 $\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(G_{2})\leq\lambda_{i}(G_{1}).$ 


This completes proof of the first part of the Proposition.



 $(2)$  Next, we prove the second part i.e.,  $\lambda_{i}(G_{1})\leq\lambda_{i+1}(G_{2})$ .
Let  $f^{1},f^{2},\ldots,f^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$  be an orthogonal set of eigenfunctions of  $G_{1}$  corresponding to the eigenvalues  $\lambda_{1}(G_{1}),\lambda_{2}(G_{1}),\cdots,\lambda_{k}(G_{1})$ , respectively. Here,




 $\displaystyle f^{\alpha}=(f^{\alpha}_{1},f^{\alpha}_{2},\ldots,f^{\alpha}_{n})=(f^{\alpha}(v_{1}),f^{\alpha}(v_{2}),\ldots,f^{\alpha}(v_{n}))\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ for all }1\leq\alpha\leq k.$ 


Define an ( $i-1$ )-dimensional subspace  $E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}$  as




 $\displaystyle E=\operatorname{span}\{f^{1},f^{2},\ldots,f^{i-1}\}.$ 


We now construct test functions in  $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$  corresponding to eigenvalue  $\lambda_{i+1}(G_{2})$ . Let  $v_{l}$  be the vertex where the new vertex  $v_{n+1}$  is attached. For each vector  $f^{\alpha}=(f^{\alpha}_{1},f^{\alpha}_{2},\ldots,f^{\alpha}_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$  for  $\alpha=1,2,\ldots,i-1$ , define its extension  $\widetilde{f}^{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$  as follows




 $\displaystyle\widetilde{f}^{\alpha}_{j}=\begin{cases}f^{\alpha}_{j}&\text{for }j=1,2,\ldots,n\text{ and }j\neq l,\\
0&\text{for }j=l,\\
f^{\alpha}_{l}&\text{for }j=n+1.\end{cases}$ 


Define the vector  $u_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$  as the standard basis vector with a  $1$  at the  $l$ -th position and  $0$  elsewhere i.e.




 $\displaystyle u_{l}=(0,\ldots,0,\underbrace{1}_{l\text{-th}},0,\ldots,0).$ 


Now, construct the  $i$ -dimensional subspace  $\widetilde{E}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$  for  $G_{2}$ 




 $\displaystyle\widetilde{E}=\operatorname{span}\{\widetilde{f}^{1},\widetilde{f}^{2},\ldots,\widetilde{f}^{i-1},u_{l}\}.$ 


It follows from Lemma 3.3 that for any vector  $g\in E^{\perp}$ , its corresponding extension  $\widetilde{g}\in\widetilde{E}^{\perp}$ . By variational characterization of  $\lambda_{i}(G_{1})$  given in Proposition 2.1, Equation (2.6)



(4.4)

 $\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(G_{1})=R(f^{i},G_{1})=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in E^{\perp}\\
f\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(f,G_{1}).$ 


Note that for any  $f\in E^{\perp}$ ,




 $\displaystyle R(f,G_{1})=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\sim v_{j}\in G_{1}}(f_{s}-f_{j})^{2}}{\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G_{1}}}f_{s}^{2}}\ \leq\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\sim v_{j}\in G_{1}}(f_{s}-f_{j})^{2}+2f_{l}^{2}}{\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G_{1}}}f_{s}^{2}}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\sim v_{j}\in G_{2}}(\widetilde{f}_{s}-\widetilde{f}_{j})^{2}}{\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G_{2}}}\widetilde{f}_{s}^{2}}=R(\widetilde{f},G_{2}).$ 


Taking minimum over all nonzero  $f\in E^{\perp}$ , this inequality gives



(4.5)

 $\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(G_{1})=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in E^{\perp}\\
f\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(f,G_{1})\leq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\widetilde{f}\in\widetilde{E}^{\perp}\\
\widetilde{f}\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(\widetilde{f},G_{2})\leq\lambda_{i+1}(G_{2}).$ 


where the last inequality follows from the Expression (2.6) of variational characterization given in Proposition 2.1 of  $\lambda_{i+1}(G_{2})$  . Hence we have the desired inequality.
∎



Using Proposition 4.1 and 4.2, we conclude the following monotonicity property of the Neumann eigenvalues on trees.


Theorem 4.1.

Let  $G_{1}$  and  $G_{2}$  be two trees such that  $G_{1}\subset G_{2}$  that is,  $G_{2}$  is obtained from  $G_{1}$  by adding finitely many new edges. Let  $k_{1}$  and  $k_{2}$  be the number of interior vertices in  $G_{1}$  and  $G_{2}$ , respectively. Then for any  $1\leq i\leq k_{1}$ , the  $i^{\text{th}}$  Neumann eigenvalue satisfies  $\lambda_{i}(G_{2})\leq\lambda_{i}(G_{1})\leq\lambda_{i+k_{2}-k_{1}}(G_{2})$  .



Proof.

First inequality  $\lambda_{i}(G_{2})\leq\lambda_{i}(G_{1})$  follows directly from Proposition 4.1 and part (1) of Proposition 4.2. For the inequality  $\lambda_{i}(G_{1})\leq\lambda_{i+k_{2}-k_{1}}(G_{2})$ , we use Proposition 4.1 and part (2) of Proposition 4.2. Let  $G_{2}$  be obtained from  $G_{1}$  in the  $j$  steps, where in each step we add exactly one edge i.e.,  $G_{1}=G_{1,1}\subset G_{1,2}\subset G_{1,3}\subset\cdots\subset G_{1,j+1}=G_{2}$ , where  $G_{1,s+1}$  has exactly one extra edge and vertex (interior or boundary) from  $G_{1,s}$  for each  $1\leq s\leq j$ . Fix some  $i$  between  $1$  and  $k_{1}$ , then for each  $1\leq s\leq j,$ 




 $\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(G_{1,s})\leq\begin{cases}\lambda_{i}(G_{1,s+1}),&\quad\text{ If }G_{1,s+1}\text{ have one extra boundary vertex,}\\
\lambda_{i+1}(G_{1,s+1}),&\quad\text{ If }G_{1,s+1}\text{ have one extra interior vertex.}\end{cases}$ 


Note that in the process of obtaining  $G_{2}$  from  $G_{1}$  by adding one vertex at each step, there will be exactly  $(k_{2}-k_{1})$  steps in which an interior vertex will increase. Combining all the above facts, we can conclude  $\lambda_{i}(G_{1})\leq\lambda_{i+k_{2}-k_{1}}(G_{2})$ .
∎



As a consequence of the above theorem, the following result is obtained, providing an upper bound for  $\lambda_{2}$  and a lower bound for  $\lambda_{k}$  for trees having  $k$  interior vertices.


Corollary 4.1.

For any tree  $T$  with  $k$  interior vertices and diameter at least  $3$ , the second Neumann eigenvalue  $\lambda_{2}$  and the largest Neumann eigenvalue  $\lambda_{k}$  satisfies




 $\lambda_{2}(T)\leq 2\leq\lambda_{k}(T).$ 





Proof.

Note that any tree  $T$  with diameter at least  $3$  contains a path  $P_{3}$  of diameter three. It is easy to compute that the path graph of diameter 3 has two Neumann eigenvalues  $\lambda_{1}(P_{3})=0,\lambda_{2}(P_{3})=2$ . Then  $\lambda_{2}(T)\leq\lambda_{2}(P_{3})\leq\lambda_{2+k-2}(T)=\lambda_{k}(T)$ . Since  $\lambda_{2}(P_{3})=2,$  we get the desired result.
∎



Remark 4.1.

Theorem 4.1 does not hold in general for arbitrary graphs. Consider graphs  $G_{1},G_{2}$  and  $G_{3}$  as shown in Figures 4(a), 4(b) and 4(c). Then Neumann eigenvalues on  $G_{1},G_{2}$  and  $G_{3}$  are given as  $\lambda_{2}(G_{1})=1.5,\lambda_{3}(G_{1})=2.5$ ;  $\lambda_{2}(G_{2})=1.5,\lambda_{3}(G_{2})=3.5$  and  $\lambda_{2}(G_{3})=0.85380,\lambda_{3}(G_{3})=1.6986$ . 5. Sharp Bound under Diameter constraint on trees


In this section, we investigate Neumann eigenvalues on trees under diameter constraint.


In the following theorem, we give an upper bound for the second Neumann eigenvalue on any path graph in terms of its diameter (length)  $D$ .


Theorem 5.1.

Let  $P$  be a path graph of diameter  $D\geq 3$ , then




 $\displaystyle\lambda_{2}(P)\leq\begin{cases}\frac{2}{D-2},&\text{for }D\text{ even},\\
\frac{4}{D-1},&\text{for }D\text{ odd}.\end{cases}$ 


Further, equality holds for path graphs of diameter  $3$  and  $4$ , respectively.



Proof.

Let vertices along path  $P$  of length  $D$  be labeled as  $v_{1},v_{2},...,v_{i},...,v_{D},v_{D+1}$ . We consider two cases based on the diameter  $D$ .


Case 1: Suppose  $D$  is even, we define the test function by




 $\displaystyle g^{1}(v_{i})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }i\leq D/2,\\
0,&\text{if }i=D/2+1,\\
-1,&\text{if }i>D/2+1.\end{cases}$ 


For the above test function, The Rayleigh quotient  $R(g^{1},P)$  is




 $\displaystyle R(g^{1},P)=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\sim v_{j}}(g^{1}(v_{s})-g^{1}(v_{j}))^{2}}{\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}}(g^{1}(v_{s}))^{2}}=\frac{2}{(D-2)}.$ 




Case 2: Suppose that  $D$  is odd. In this case, we consider the following test function




 $\displaystyle g^{2}(v_{i})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }i\leq(D+1)/2,\\
-1,&\text{if }i>(D+1)/2.\end{cases}$ 


The Rayleigh quotient is




 $\displaystyle R(g^{2},P)=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\sim v_{j}}(g^{2}(v_{s})-g^{2}(v_{j}))^{2}}{\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}}(g^{2}(v_{s}))^{2}}=\frac{4}{(D-1)}.$ 


By equation (2.4) of Proposition 2.1, eigenvalue  $\lambda_{2}(P)$  is the infimum over all functions  $f$  orthogonal to constant function (that is  $\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}}f(v_{s})=0$ ), Thus  $\lambda_{2}(P)\leq R(g^{i},P),i=1,2$  depending on the diameter of  $P$ . Therefore




 $\displaystyle\lambda_{2}(P)\leq\begin{cases}\frac{2}{D-2},&\text{for }D\text{ even},\\
\frac{4}{D-1},&\text{for }D\text{ odd}.\end{cases}$ 


∎



Remark 5.1.

This result provides a better upper bound for the second Neumann eigenvalue on path than that obtained in corollary 4.1 as  $\frac{2}{D-2}$  and  $\frac{4}{D-1}$  are strictly smaller than 2 for all  $D\geq 3.$ 



As an outcome of the above theorem it can also be stated that infimum of the second Neumann eigenvalue over family of all paths tends to zero as presented in the next corollary.


Corollary 5.1.

Let  $\mathcal{P}$  be the collection of all paths, then the second Neumann eigenvalue satisfies




 $\displaystyle\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\lambda_{2}(P)=0.$ 





Proof.

From Theorem 5.1, we know that for any path  $P\in\mathcal{P}$  with diameter  $D$ ,




 $\lambda_{2}(P)\leq\begin{cases}\frac{2}{D-2},&\text{for }D\text{ even},\\
\frac{4}{D-1},&\text{for }D\text{ odd}.\end{cases}$ 




Since  $\frac{2}{D-2}$  and  $\frac{4}{D-1}$  can be made very close to zero for  $D$  large enough. Combining this with the fact that for any path  $P$  the second Neumann eigenvalue  $\lambda_{2}(P)\geq 0$ , it can be concluded that  $\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\lambda_{2}(P)=0$ .
∎



Next, we provide a theorem demonstrating that under diameter constraint, the set of largest Neumann eigenvalues on the family of trees is unbounded.


Theorem 5.2.

Let  $\mathcal{G}$  be the collection of all trees with diameter  $D\geq 3$ , then the set  $\{\lambda_{max}(G)|G\in\mathcal{G}\}$  is not bounded above. Here,  $\lambda_{max}(G)$  represents the largest Neumann eigenvalue of  $G$ .



Proof.

 $o$  $c_{1}$  $c_{2}$  $b_{1}$  $a_{1}$  $b_{l}$  $a_{l}$  $b_{2}$  $a_{2}$  $b_{j}$  $a_{j}$  $b_{j+1}$  $a_{j+1}$  $c_{3}$  $c_{4}$  $c_{D-3}$  $c_{D-2}$ 

Figure 5. Tree  $G_{j+1}$  with diameter  $D$ , where  $o,b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{j+1},c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{D-3}$  are interior vertices, and  $a_{1},a_{2}\dots,a_{j+1},c_{D-2}$  are leaves.



To prove this theorem, we construct a sequence of trees  $G_{j+1},j\in\mathbb{N}$  (see fig.5) as follows:
Start with a path  $P$  on  $D-1$  vertices, labeled as
 $o,c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{D-2}$ . To the vertex  $o$ , attach  $j+1$  paths of length  $2$  each. That is, for each  $t=1,2,\ldots,j+1$ , introduce two vertices  $a_{t}$  and  $b_{t}$  such that  $o\sim b_{t}$  and  $b_{t}\sim a_{t}.$ 
The resulting tree  $G_{j+1}$  has diameter  $D$  with  $j+D-1$  interior vertices and  $j+2$  boundary vertices. Now we will show that the maximum Neumann eigenvalue of  $G_{j+1}$  converges to infinity as  $j$  approaches to infinity. Using Remark 3.2, we write




 $\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(G)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega_{G}}\\
\tilde{f}\neq 0\end{subarray}}R(f,G)=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\sim v_{j}}(f(v_{s})-f(v_{j}))^{2}}{\displaystyle\sum_{v_{s}\in\Omega_{G}}f(v_{s})^{2}}.$ 


Define test function  $f$  on  $G_{j+1}$  as




 $\displaystyle f(v)=\begin{cases}-(j+D-2),&\text{if }v=o,\\
1,&\text{otherwise }.\end{cases}$ 




Then




 $\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(G_{j+1})\geq\frac{(1+(j+D-2))^{2}(j+2)}{1^{2}\cdot({j+1})+1^{2}\cdot{(D-3)}+(j+D-2)^{2}\cdot 1}.$ 


Simplifying this expression, we get




 $\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(G_{j+1})$ 
 $\displaystyle\geq\frac{(j+D-1)^{2}(j+2)}{(j+D-2)+(-(j+D-2))^{2}}$ 




 $\displaystyle=\frac{(j+2)(j+D-1)^{2}}{(j+D-2)(1+j+D-2)}$ 




 $\displaystyle=\frac{(j+2)(j+D-1)}{j+D-2}$ 




 $\displaystyle=\frac{j^{2}(1+\frac{2}{j})(1+\frac{D-1}{j})}{j(1+\frac{D-2}{j})}$ 




 $\displaystyle\to\infty\quad\text{as }j\to\infty.$ 


Hence, the proof is complete.
∎




6. Lower bound for the second Neumann eigenvalue


We begin this section by discussing some properties of Neumann eigenfunctions on Graph. These properties will be used in calculating sharp lower bound of second Neumann eigenvalue of Graph.


Lemma 6.1.

Let  $G$  be a connected graph with boundary, having  $k$  interior and  $n$  total number of vertices. For  $2\leq i\leq k$ , let  $f^{i}=(f_{1}^{i},f_{2}^{i},\ldots,f_{k}^{i},\ldots,f_{n}^{i})$  be an eigenfunction corresponding to Neumann eigenvalue  $\lambda_{i}(G)$ . Then function  $f^{i}$  satisfies the following property.

(1) 

The maximum value and minimum value of  $f^{i}_{j}$ ,  $1\leq j\leq n$  will be attained at some interior vertices.

(2) 

The maximum value of  $f^{i}_{j}$ ,  $1\leq j\leq n$  will be strictly positive and the minimum value of  $f^{i}_{j}$ ,  $1\leq j\leq n$  will be strictly negative.





Proof.

 $(1)$  If  $f^{i}$  is an eigenfunction corresponding to Neumann eigenvalue  $\lambda_{i}(G)$ . Then for any  $x_{j}\in\delta\Omega_{G}$ , the definition of Neumann problem 2.1 gives,




 $\frac{\partial f^{i}}{\partial n}(x_{j})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in{\Omega_{G}}\\
y\sim x_{j}\end{subarray}}(f^{i}(x_{j})-f^{i}(y))=0.$ 


Thus,  $f^{i}(x_{j})$  is equal to the average of function values at all the (interior) vertices adjacent to  $x_{j}$ . It ensures that the maximum and minimum values of  $f^{i}_{j}$  will be achieved at some interior vertices.



 $(2)$  It can be easily proved by using (1) and orthogonality condition  $\displaystyle\sum_{v_{j}\in\Omega_{G}}f^{i}_{j}=0$ .
∎



We now introduce a family of graphs that will be used to demonstrate the sharpness of the lower bound of second Neumann eigenvalue on graph.


Definition 6.1.

Let  $s\geq 1$  and  $\alpha\geq 1$  be integers. Then graph  $G(s,\alpha)$  is constructed as follows. Start with a path  $P_{2s+1}:v_{0}\sim v_{1}\sim\cdots\sim v_{2s+1}.$  For each vertex  $v_{j}$  with  $1\leq j\leq 2s$ , attach a complete graph  $K_{\alpha-1}^{(j)}$  by connecting  $v_{j}$  to every vertex of  $K_{\alpha-1}^{(j)}$ .


 $\cdots$  $v_{0}$  $v_{1}$  $v_{2}$  $v_{2s-1}$  $v_{2s}$  $v_{2s+1}$  $K^{(1)}_{\alpha-1}$  $K^{(2)}_{\alpha-1}$  $K^{(2s-1)}_{\alpha-1}$  $K^{(2s)}_{\alpha-1}$ 
Figure 6. The graph  $G(s,\alpha)$ . Boundary vertices are shown as large dots, while interior vertices are shown as small dots.


Now we will give a lemma, the proof of which follows using the similar strategy as that of Lemma  $2.4$  in [15]. In our case, we have to choose different test function in the variational characterizetion to get the desired bound.


Lemma 6.2.

Let  $G(s,\alpha)$  be a graph defined as above. Let  $k$  be the number of interior vertices.
If  $d_{\Omega}$  denotes the interior diameter of  $G$ , then



(6.1)

 $\displaystyle\lambda_{2}(G)\leq\frac{12k}{(k-1)^{2}\,d_{\Omega}}.$ 


Here,  $k=2\alpha s$  and  $d_{\Omega}=2s+1$  is the maximum distance among all pairs of interior vertices of  $G$ .



Proof.

Let  $v_{0}\sim v_{1}\sim\cdots\sim v_{2s+1}$  be the path of graph  $G=G(s,\alpha)$ .
For  $1\leq t\leq 2s$ , set
 $F_{t}:=\{v_{t}\}\cup V\!\left(K^{(t)}_{\alpha-1}\right).$ 
Then  $k=2s\alpha$  and  $d_{\Omega}=2s+1$ .
Define a test function  $f$  by



(6.2)

 $\displaystyle f(v)=\begin{cases}s-(t-1),\quad&\text{if }v\in F_{t},\qquad 1\leq t\leq s,\\
-(t-s),\quad&\text{if }v\in F_{t},\qquad$s+1$\leq t\leq 2s,\\
s\quad&\text{if }v=v_{0},\\
-s\quad&\text{if }v=v_{2s+1}.\end{cases}$ 


Then




 $\displaystyle\lambda_{2}(G)\leq R(f,G)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{(s-1)+4+(s-1)}{{2\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{s}}j^{2}}$ 




 $\displaystyle=\frac{2s+2}{2\alpha(s(s+1)(2s+1)/6)}$ 




 $\displaystyle=\frac{6(s+1)}{\alpha s(s+1)(2s+1)}$ 




 $\displaystyle=\frac{6}{\alpha s\,d_{\Omega}}=\frac{12}{2\alpha\,s\,d_{\Omega}}$ 




 $\displaystyle=\frac{12}{k\,d_{\Omega}}=\frac{12k}{k^{2}\,d_{\Omega}}\leq\frac{12k}{(k-1)^{2}\,d_{\Omega}}.$ 


By (2.4) of Proposition 2.1, the eigenvalue
 $\lambda_{2}(G)$  is characterized as the infimum of the Rayleigh quotient over all
functions  $f$  orthogonal to the constant function. Note that the function  $f$  defined in (6.2) satisfies  $\sum_{v_{j}\in\Omega_{G}}f(v_{j})=0.$  Hence,  $\lambda_{2}(G)\leq R(f,G)$ . Therefore,  $\lambda_{2}(G)\leq\frac{12k}{(k-1)^{2}\,d_{\Omega}}$ .
∎



Next, we provide a lower bound for the second Neumann eigenvalue on graphs. The idea of the proof is the same as used in Theorem  $1$  of [18], where a similar bound has been derived for the second Steklov eigenvalue on graphs.


Theorem 6.1.

Let  $G$  be a connected graph with boundary, having interior diameter  $d_{\Omega}$  and  $k$  interior vertices. Then
we have




 $\displaystyle\lambda_{2}(G)\geq\frac{k}{(k-1)^{2}\,d_{\Omega}}.$ 


The bound is sharp up to a constant factor.



Proof.

Let  $V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\}$  denote the vertices of  $G$  and  $f=(f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{k},\ldots,f_{n})$  be a normalized eigenfunction corresponding to eigenvalue  $\lambda_{2}(G)$  where  $f_{j}=f(v_{j})$  for  $1\leq j\leq n$ . Using Lemma (6.1), we get interior vertices  $v_{k_{1}}$  and  $v_{k_{2}}$  such that  $\displaystyle\max_{v_{j}\in\Omega_{G}}f_{j}=f_{k_{1}}$  and  $\displaystyle\min_{v_{j}\in\Omega_{G}}f_{j}=f_{k_{2}}$ . Without loss of generality, we assume  $f_{k_{1}}\geq|f_{k_{2}}|$ .
From the normalization condition  $\displaystyle\sum_{v_{j}\in\Omega_{G}}f_{j}^{2}=1$  we have




 $\displaystyle 1=\sum_{v_{j}\in\Omega_{G}}f_{j}^{2}\leq k\,f_{k_{1}}^{2}\quad\implies\quad f_{k_{1}}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{k}\quad\implies\quad f_{k_{1}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}.$ 


Using condition  $\displaystyle\sum_{v_{j}\in\Omega_{G}}f_{j}=0$  we have




 $\displaystyle f_{k_{1}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{j}\in\Omega_{G}\\
j\neq{k_{1}}\end{subarray}}f_{j}=0\quad\implies\quad-f_{k_{1}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{j}\in\Omega_{G}\\
j\neq{k_{1}}\end{subarray}}f_{j}.$ 


Since  $f_{k_{2}}$  is the smallest value of  $f_{j},1\leq j\leq n$  on  $\Omega_{G}$ , we may write




 $\displaystyle-f_{k_{1}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{j}\in\Omega_{G}\\
j\neq{k_{1}}\end{subarray}}f_{j}\geq(k-1)f_{k_{2}}\quad\implies\quad f_{k_{2}}\leq-\frac{f_{k_{1}}}{k-1}.$ 


Substituting the earlier bound  $f_{k_{1}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}$ ,




 $\displaystyle f_{k_{2}}\leq-\frac{f_{k_{1}}}{k-1}\leq-\frac{1}{(k-1)\sqrt{k}}.$ 


Therefore, we have




 $\displaystyle f_{k_{1}}-f_{k_{2}}\geq\frac{k}{(k-1)\sqrt{k}}.$ 


Given the graph is connected and the interior diameter of graph is  $d_{\Omega}$ , there exists a path of length  $\gamma\leq d_{\Omega}$  joining  $v_{k_{1}}$  and  $v_{k_{2}}$ . We denote the  $\gamma+1$  vertices of the path by  $v_{i_{1}},\dots,v_{i_{(\gamma+1)}}$ , where  $i_{1}={k_{1}}$  and  $i_{(\gamma+1)}={k_{2}}$ . Using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain




 $\displaystyle\lambda_{2}(G)=\sum_{v_{s}\sim v_{j}}(f_{s}-f_{j})^{2}\geq\sum_{t=1}^{\gamma}(f_{i_{t}}-f_{i_{t+1}})^{2}\geq\frac{(f_{k_{1}}-f_{k_{2}})^{2}}{\gamma}\geq\frac{1}{d_{\Omega}}\left(\frac{k}{(k-1)\sqrt{k}}\right)^{2}=\frac{k}{(k-1)^{2}\,d_{\Omega}}.$ 


The bound is sharp up to a constant factor, as follows from Lemma 6.2.
∎



Remark 6.1.

Using similar technique, we can also establish a lower bound for the second Neumann eigenvalue on weighted graphs, which we plan to present in our future work.



We conclude this article with the following conjecture.


Conjecture 6.1.

On a path graph  $P$  with  $n$  vertices, the largest Neumann eigenvalue  $\lambda_{n-2}(P)$  satisfies




 $\displaystyle\lambda_{n-2}(P)\leq 4.$
