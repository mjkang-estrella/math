Abstract.
Let (V,0)(V,0) be an isolated complex hypersurface singularity defined by
f∈ℂ⁡{x1,…,xn}f\in\mathbb{C}\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}.
Let A⁡(V)A(V) be the moduli(Tjurina) algebra,
let A∗​(V)A^{*}(V) be the generalized moduli algebra, and let
L⁡(V)=Derℂ⁡(A⁡(V),A⁡(V)),L∗​(V)=Derℂ⁡(A∗​(V),A∗​(V))L(V)=\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(A(V),A(V)),\qquad L^{*}(V)=\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(A^{*}(V),A^{*}(V))
be the Yau algebra and the new Yau algebra of VV, respectively.
Write λ⁡(V)=dimℂL⁡(V)\lambda(V)=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}L(V) and
λ∗​(V)=dimℂL∗​(V)\lambda^{*}(V)=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}L^{*}(V).
We prove that the difference between these two dimensions is determined by
the Hessian corank. In the Morse case, the generalized moduli algebra is zero, and hence
both derivation Lie algebras are zero. There is
λ∗​(V)=λ⁡(V)−{1,corank⁡Hess​(f)​(0)=1,0,corank⁡Hess​(f)​(0)≠1.\lambda^{*}(V)=\lambda(V)-\begin{cases}1,&\operatorname{corank}\operatorname{Hess}(f)(0)=1,\\
0,&\operatorname{corank}\operatorname{Hess}(f)(0)\neq 1.\end{cases}
In particular, λ∗​(V)=λ​(V)\lambda^{*}(V)=\lambda(V) when n≥2n\geq 2 and
mult⁡(f)≥3\operatorname{mult}(f)\geq 3. This proves Conjecture 1.1 proposed in [3]. We construct an exact sequence whose two end terms are
copies of the socle of the Milnor algebra. This homological approach reveals the detailed algebraic structure underlying the dimension count. We also give a alternative proof that pushes the original arguments in the proof of [3, Theorem C] further.
The general case is obtained by combining Saito’s criterion for the
non-quasi-homogeneous case.
Key words and phrases: isolated hypersurface singularity, moduli algebra, generalized moduli algebra, Yau algebra, derivation Lie algebra, Hessian determinant, socle, splitting lemma

1. Introduction
Let
𝒪n=ℂ⁡{x1,…,xn},𝔪=(x1,…,xn),\mathcal{O}_{n}=\mathbb{C}\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\},\qquad\mathfrak{m}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),
and let f∈𝔪2f\in\mathfrak{m}^{2} have an isolated critical point at the origin. Set
(V,0)=V⁡(f)(V,0)=V(f) and write
Jf=(∂f∂x1,…,∂f∂xn),hf=detHess⁡(f),J_{f}=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}},\ldots,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{n}}\right),\qquad h_{f}=\det\operatorname{Hess}(f),
where JfJ_{f} is the Jacobian ideal and hfh_{f} is the Hessian determinant.
The quotient
A⁡(V)=𝒪n/(f,Jf)A(V)=\mathcal{O}_{n}/(f,J_{f})
is the moduli algebra, also known as the Tjurina algebra. The
Mather–Yau theorem states that its isomorphism class determines and is determined by the
analytic isomorphism type of the isolated hypersurface germ
[10].
A key object of study is the derivation Lie algebra of A⁡(V)A(V),
L⁡(V)=Derℂ⁡(A⁡(V),A⁡(V)),L(V)=\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(A(V),A(V)),
which is a finite-dimensional Lie algebra.
Yau proved that for any isolated hypersurface singularity, L⁡(V)L(V) is a solvable Lie algebra; see [14],[21]. This result led to L⁡(V)L(V) being named the Yau algebra [14, 22, 23], and
λ⁡(V)=dimℂL⁡(V)\lambda(V)=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}L(V)
is its Yau number [7]. This establishes a profound connection between singularity theory and solvable Lie algebras.
In [3],the authors introduced the Artinian local algebra, i.e., the finite-dimensional quotient
algebra
A∗​(V)=𝒪n/(f,Jf,hf)A^{*}(V)=\mathcal{O}_{n}/(f,J_{f},h_{f})
and called it the generalized moduli algebra
[3]. The construction is motivated by Dimca’s
theorem on zero-dimensional isolated complete intersections
[5]: in the quasi-homogeneous case, the singular subspace of
the Jacobian complete intersection has coordinate algebra A∗​(V)A^{*}(V).
Together with the Mather–Yau theorem, this shows that A∗​(V)A^{*}(V) is a
complete invariant within the quasi-homogeneous class; see
[3, Remark 2.1]. The corresponding derivation
Lie algebra and its dimension are
L∗​(V)=Derℂ⁡(A∗​(V),A∗​(V)),λ∗​(V)=dimℂL∗​(V).L^{*}(V)=\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(A^{*}(V),A^{*}(V)),\qquad\lambda^{*}(V)=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}L^{*}(V).
We refer to L∗​(V)L^{*}(V) as the new Yau algebra. When A∗​(V)A^{*}(V) is
the zero algebra, we use the convention Derℂ⁡(A∗​(V),A∗​(V))=0\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(A^{*}(V),A^{*}(V))=0.
The construction of Yau algebras belongs to a broader interaction
between singularity theory and finite-dimensional Lie theory.
Brieskorn’s work relating rational double points to simple algebraic
groups provides a classical instance of this interaction
[16].
By the Levi decomposition, every finite-dimensional complex Lie algebra
admits a decomposition
𝔤=𝔰⋉rad⁡(𝔤),\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{s}\ltimes\operatorname{rad}(\mathfrak{g}),
where 𝔰\mathfrak{s} is a semisimple Levi subalgebra and
rad⁡(𝔤)\operatorname{rad}(\mathfrak{g}) is the solvable radical of
𝔤\mathfrak{g}. The semisimple part admits a uniform classification in terms of root
systems and Dynkin diagrams [17, 18], whereas solvable, and in particular nilpotent, Lie algebras do not admit
a comparably uniform classification [19].
Yau’s
construction provides a complementary geometric direction: it
associates with every isolated hypersurface singularity a
finite-dimensional solvable Lie algebra. It therefore gives a
geometrically organized family of solvable Lie algebras that can be
studied through local invariants of singularities; see also
[14, 15, 20].
The new Yau algebra belongs to the same solvable Lie-theoretic framework
throughout the range mult⁡(f)≥4\operatorname{mult}(f)\geq 4. Indeed, if ff is not
quasi-homogeneous, then A∗​(V)=A​(V)A^{*}(V)=A(V) and consequently
L∗​(V)=L​(V)L^{*}(V)=L(V). If ff is quasi-homogeneous and mult⁡(f)≥4\operatorname{mult}(f)\geq 4, it has been proved that L∗​(V)L^{*}(V) is solvable
[3, Corollary 2.1]. Hence, for every isolated
hypersurface singularity of multiplicity at least four, L⁡(V)L(V) and
L∗​(V)L^{*}(V) are two finite-dimensional solvable Lie algebras naturally
associated with the same singularity.
The comparison of their dimensions also has a direct geometric
interpretation in terms of local algebras. For a finite-dimensional
commutative ℂ\mathbb{C}-algebra BB, the derivation algebra
Derℂ⁡(B,B)\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(B,B) is the tangent Lie algebra at the identity of the
algebraic automorphism group of BB; equivalently, it describes the
infinitesimal algebra automorphisms of BB. Thus λ⁡(V)\lambda(V) and
λ∗​(V)\lambda^{*}(V) measure the dimensions of the infinitesimal symmetry
algebras encoded by the two intrinsic local algebras A⁡(V)A(V) and
A∗​(V)A^{*}(V), respectively. Since
A∗​(V)=A⁡(V)/(hf¯),A^{*}(V)=A(V)/(\overline{h_{f}}),
determining the difference λ​(V)−λ∗​(V)\lambda(V)-\lambda^{*}(V) asks how imposing
the additional Hessian relation changes the dimension of the
infinitesimal symmetry algebra.
From both the Lie-theoretic and geometric
viewpoints, it is therefore a natural and meaningful numerical problem.
The following conjecture was proposed in
[3].
Conjecture 1.1 ([3, Conjecture 1.1]).
Let n≥2n\geq 2, and let f∈𝒪nf\in\mathcal{O}_{n} define an isolated hypersurface
singularity of multiplicity at least three. Then
(1.1)
λ∗​(V)=λ​(V).\lambda^{*}(V)=\lambda(V).
The non-quasi-homogeneous case was already settled in the same paper.
Indeed, Saito’s criterion gives hf∈(f,Jf)h_{f}\in(f,J_{f}), and hence
A∗​(V)=A​(V)A^{*}(V)=A(V) [12, Corollary 3.8].
For weighted
homogeneous singularities, the authors proved
λ∗​(V)≤λ⁡(V)\lambda^{*}(V)\leq\lambda(V) in arbitrary dimension and equality when
2≤n≤42\leq n\leq 4. They also obtained equality for canonical weights
d≥2​w1≥2​w2≥⋯≥2​wn>0d\geq 2w_{1}\geq 2w_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 2w_{n}>0
under the additional condition wn≥w1/2w_{n}\geq w_{1}/2, and for binomial
singularities; see
[3, Theorems C and D, Remark 6.1, and Theorem 7.1].
Our first result proves 1.1 for weighted homogeneous
singularities in every dimension, without an additional restriction on
the weights. Combining this with the non-quasi-homogeneous case proves
the conjecture in full. We then remove the multiplicity assumption and distinguish the two dimensions using Hessian corank.
Theorem A (Main theorem).
Let f∈𝔪2⊂𝒪nf\in\mathfrak{m}^{2}\subset\mathcal{O}_{n} define an isolated hypersurface singularity,
and set
c⁡(f):=corank⁡Hess⁡(f)​(0)=dimℂker⁡Hess⁡(f)​(0),c(f):=\operatorname{corank}\operatorname{Hess}(f)(0)=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker\operatorname{Hess}(f)(0),
where
Hess⁡(f)​(0)=(∂2f∂xi​∂xj​(0))1≤i,j≤n.\operatorname{Hess}(f)(0)=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(0)\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq n}.
Then the following statements hold.
(1)
If c⁡(f)=0c(f)=0, then ff is Morse,
A⁡(V)≅ℂ,A∗​(V)=0,A(V)\cong\mathbb{C},\qquad A^{*}(V)=0,
and
λ⁡(V)=λ∗​(V)=0.\lambda(V)=\lambda^{*}(V)=0.
(2)
If c⁡(f)=1c(f)=1, then the Milnor number μ=μ⁡(V)\mu=\mu(V) satisfies
μ≥2\mu\geq 2, and
A⁡(V)≅ℂ⁡[t]/(tμ),A∗​(V)≅ℂ⁡[t]/(tμ−1).A(V)\cong\mathbb{C}[t]/(t^{\mu}),\qquad A^{*}(V)\cong\mathbb{C}[t]/(t^{\mu-1}).
Hence
λ⁡(V)=μ−1,λ∗​(V)=μ−2.\lambda(V)=\mu-1,\qquad\lambda^{*}(V)=\mu-2.
(3)
If c⁡(f)≥2c(f)\geq 2, then
λ∗​(V)=λ​(V).\lambda^{*}(V)=\lambda(V).
We indicate the main steps of the proof. Suppose first that ff is a
weighted homogeneous polynomial, n≥2n\geq 2, and mult⁡(f)≥3\operatorname{mult}(f)\geq 3. The
moduli algebra is then the Milnor algebra
Mf=ℂ⁡[x1,…,xn]/Jf,M_{f}=\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/J_{f},
and the class of hfh_{f} generates its one-dimensional socle. The
adjugate-Hessian derivations and the relevant degree estimate appear
already in the proof of [3, Theorem C]. Using
relative derivations, we organize this calculation into the exact
sequence of complex vector spaces
(1.2)
0⟶Soc⁡(Mf)⊕n\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow\operatorname{Soc}(M_{f})^{\oplus n}
⟶Derℂ⁡(Mf,Mf)\displaystyle\longrightarrow\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M_{f},M_{f})
⟶Derℂ⁡(A∗​(V),A∗​(V))⟶Soc⁡(Mf)⊕n⟶0.\displaystyle\longrightarrow\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(A^{*}(V),A^{*}(V))\longrightarrow\operatorname{Soc}(M_{f})^{\oplus n}\longrightarrow 0.
The two end terms have the same dimension, giving the required dimension
equality. We also record a shorter proof that combines the calculation
in [3, Theorem C] with preservation of the
socle under derivations.
Finally, the holomorphic splitting lemma
[8, Theorem 2.47] reduces an arbitrary
isolated hypersurface germ to a residual germ in c⁡(f)c(f) variables. The
Morse case has no residual variables, one residual variable gives the
corank-one exception, and a residual germ in at least two variables has
multiplicity at least three. This proves the full corank formula.
The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 develops the local
analytic background, the finite-dimensional algebras attached to a
hypersurface germ, their derivation Lie algebras, and the required
invariance facts. In Section 3 we
review socles and treat the non-quasi-homogeneous case. In Section 4 we
give the two proofs described above. In Section 5 we use the splitting
lemma to prove
A. Section 6 gives consequences and examples.
2. Preliminaries and invariance
2.1. Hypersurface germs and their local algebras
Let
Pn=ℂ⁡[x1,…,xn]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P_{n}=\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]}
be the polynomial ring in nn variables. The local analytic counterpart
of PnP_{n} is the ring 𝒪n\mathcal{O}_{n} of germs of holomorphic functions at the
origin of ℂn\mathbb{C}^{n}. Recall that a germ records a holomorphic function only
in an unspecified neighborhood of the origin: two functions represent the
same germ when they agree on some smaller neighborhood. Taking Taylor
series identifies
𝒪n=ℂ⁡{x1,…,xn},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{O}_{n}=\mathbb{C}\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\},}
the ring of convergent power series. It is a local ℂ\mathbb{C}-algebra with
maximal ideal
𝔪=(x1,…,xn)={g∈𝒪n:g⁡(0)=0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathfrak{m}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\{g\in\mathcal{O}_{n}:g(0)=0\}}
and residue field 𝒪n/𝔪≅ℂ\mathcal{O}_{n}/\mathfrak{m}\cong\mathbb{C}. A germ u∈𝒪nu\in\mathcal{O}_{n} is a unit
precisely when u⁡(0)≠0u(0)\neq 0.
For f∈𝔪f\in\mathfrak{m}, the zero set of a representative of ff in a sufficiently
small neighborhood of the origin defines a hypersurface germ, denoted by
(V,0)=V⁡(f)⊂(ℂn,0).{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(V,0)=V(f)\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n},0).}
Only the behavior near the origin is retained. In particular, replacing
ff by u​fuf, where uu is a unit, does not change the hypersurface germ.
The local function algebra of VV is
F⁡(V)=𝒪n/(f),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}F(V)=\mathcal{O}_{n}/(f),}
where (f)(f) is the principal ideal generated by ff. This algebra is
usually infinite-dimensional over ℂ\mathbb{C}, but it is the natural local
coordinate ring of the germ.
Throughout the paper, f∈𝔪2f\in\mathfrak{m}^{2}, so the origin is a critical point.
The multiplicity of ff is
mult⁡(f)=max⁡{r≥1:f∈𝔪r};{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\operatorname{mult}(f)=\max\{r\geq 1:f\in\mathfrak{m}^{r}\};}
equivalently, it is the least total degree of a nonzero term in the
power-series expansion of ff. Write
Jf=(∂f∂x1,…,∂f∂xn){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}J_{f}=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}},\ldots,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{n}}\right)}
for the Jacobian ideal. We say that ff has an isolated critical point
at the origin if, in a sufficiently small neighborhood, the gradient
∇f\nabla f vanishes only at the origin. Algebraically, this is equivalent
to
Jf=𝔪,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sqrt{J_{f}}=\mathfrak{m},}
or, equivalently, to JfJ_{f} being 𝔪\mathfrak{m}-primary . Thus the quotient
Mf:=𝒪n/Jf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{f}:=\mathcal{O}_{n}/J_{f}}
is finite-dimensional. It is called the Milnor algebra of the
defining germ ff. Whenever the defining germ is fixed, we also use the
notation M⁡(V)=MfM(V)=M_{f}. Its dimension
μ⁡(V)=dimℂMf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mu(V)=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}M_{f}}
is the Milnor number.
The Hessian matrix and its determinant are denoted by
Hess⁡(f)=(∂2f∂xi​∂xj)1≤i,j≤n,hf=detHess⁡(f).{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\operatorname{Hess}(f)=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq n},\qquad h_{f}=\det\operatorname{Hess}(f).}
If f¯\overline{f} and h¯f\overline{h}_{f} are their classes in M⁡(V)M(V), then the moduli
algebra can be written as
A⁡(V)=𝒪n/(f,Jf)=M⁡(V)/(f¯).{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}A(V)=\mathcal{O}_{n}/(f,J_{f})=M(V)/(\overline{f}).}
Thus A⁡(V)A(V) is its local
coordinate algebra( also called the Tjurina algebra). The generalized moduli algebra is
A∗​(V)=𝒪n/(f,Jf,hf)=M⁡(V)/(f¯,h¯f),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}A^{*}(V)=\mathcal{O}_{n}/(f,J_{f},h_{f})=M(V)/(\overline{f},\overline{h}_{f}),}
and is obtained by supposing the additional Hessian relation. Both are
finite-dimensional quotients of the Milnor
algebra. The number
τ⁡(V)=dimℂA⁡(V)\tau(V)=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}A(V)
is the Tjurina number.The Mather–Yau theorem shows that the finite-dimensional algebra A⁡(V)A(V)
determines and is determined by the analytic isomorphism type of the isolated
hypersurface germ [10] . The role of A∗​(V)A^{*}(V) and the
reason for adjoining the Hessian determinant are recalled below.
2.2. Weighted homogeneous singularities and the Hessian socle
Assign positive rational weights w1,…,wnw_{1},\ldots,w_{n} to the variables. The
weighted degree of a monomial is
degw(x1a1⋯xnan)=∑i=1naiwi.{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\deg_{w}(x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}w_{i}.}
A polynomial ff is weighted homogeneous, also called
quasi-homogeneous, of weighted degree dd if every monomial
occurring in ff has weighted degree dd. The tuple
(w1,…,wn,d){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n};d)}
is its weight type. Multiplying all weights and dd by a common positive
integer allows them to be taken integral. A hypersurface germ is called
quasi-homogeneous if , after an analytic change of coordinates , it has a
weighted homogeneous representative.
The weighted Euler vector field
E=∑i=1nwi​xi​∂∂xi{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}}
satisfies the Euler identity
E⁡(f)=∑i=1nwi​xi​∂f∂xi=d​f.{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E(f)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}x_{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}=df.}
Consequently, f∈Jff\in J_{f} for a weighted homogeneous representative, and
therefore
A⁡(V)=M⁡(V),τ⁡(V)=μ⁡(V).{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}A(V)=M(V),\qquad\tau(V)=\mu(V).}
The grading also gives
degw⁡(∂f∂xi)=d−wi,degw⁡(∂2f∂xi​∂xj)=d−wi−wj.{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\deg_{w}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}\right)=d-w_{i},\qquad\deg_{w}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\right)=d-w_{i}-w_{j}.}
Every term in the Hessian determinant consequently has weighted degree
degw⁡(hf)=n​d−2​∑i=1nwi.{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\deg_{w}(h_{f})=nd-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}.}
As explained below, this is the socle degree denoted by σ\sigma in
Section 4.
For an isolated weighted homogeneous singularity, the Milnor number may
also be read from the weights:
(2.1)
μ⁡(V)=∏i=1nd−wiwi;{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mu(V)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{d-w_{i}}{w_{i}};}
indeed, the partial derivatives form a weighted homogeneous regular
sequence of degrees d−w1,…,d−wnd-w_{1},\ldots,d-w_{n}, and the formula follows from
the corresponding complete-intersection Hilbert series; see
[3, Proposition 2.1] . When the weights are
ordered and satisfy
d≥2​w1≥2​w2≥⋯≥2​wn>0,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d\geq 2w_{1}\geq 2w_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 2w_{n}>0,}
the weight type is called canonical.
We next recall the socle statement that links the Hessian determinant to
the generalized moduli algebra. If (R,𝔫)(R,\mathfrak{n}) is a finite-dimensional
local ℂ\mathbb{C}-algebra, its socle is
Soc(R)=(0:R𝔫)={r∈R:𝔫r=0}.{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\operatorname{Soc}(R)=(0:_{R}\mathfrak{n})=\{r\in R:\mathfrak{n}r=0\}.}
Thus the socle consists of the elements annihilated by the maximal ideal;
if 𝔫s+1=0\mathfrak{n}^{s+1}=0 and 𝔫s≠0\mathfrak{n}^{s}\neq 0, then
𝔫s⊆Soc⁡(R)\mathfrak{n}^{s}\subseteq\operatorname{Soc}(R). Since JfJ_{f} is 𝔪\mathfrak{m}-primary and is generated by
nn elements in the regular local ring 𝒪n\mathcal{O}_{n}, its generators form a
regular sequence. Hence M⁡(V)M(V) is an
Artinian complete intersection and, in particular, an Artinian
Gorenstein algebra; see [2, Chapter 2] . A theorem of
Scheja and Storch identifies its socle generator
[13] :
(2.2)
Soc⁡(M⁡(V))=ℂ​hf¯.{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\operatorname{Soc}(M(V))=\mathbb{C}\overline{h_{f}}.}
In the quasi-homogeneous case , f∈Jff\in J_{f}, and it follows that
A∗​(V)=M⁡(V)/(h¯f)=M⁡(V)/Soc⁡(M⁡(V)).{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}A^{*}(V)=M(V)/(\overline{h}_{f})=M(V)/\operatorname{Soc}(M(V)).}
Consequently,
dimℂA∗​(V)=μ⁡(V)−1.{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}A^{*}(V)=\mu(V)-1.}
When μ⁡(V)=1\mu(V)=1, the Milnor algebra is ℂ\mathbb{C}, its socle is the whole
algebra, and A∗​(V)A^{*}(V) is the zero algebra.
2.3. Derivations and the Yau algebras
Let RR be a commutative ℂ\mathbb{C}-algebra and let NN be an RR-module. A
ℂ\mathbb{C}-linear map D:R→ND:R\to N is a derivation if it satisfies the Leibniz
rule
D⁡(a​b)=a​D​(b)+b​D​(a)D(ab)=aD(b)+bD(a)
for all a,b∈Ra,b\in R. In particular, D⁡(1)=0D(1)=0. We write
Derℂ⁡(R,N)\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(R,N) for the vector space of such maps . Equivalently,
Derℂ⁡(R,N)≅HomR⁡(ΩR/ℂ,N),\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(R,N)\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(\Omega_{R/\mathbb{C}},N),
where ΩR/ℂ\Omega_{R/\mathbb{C}} is the module of Kähler differentials; see
[6, Section 16.1].
For a concrete presentation, let P=ℂ⁡[x1,…,xn]P=\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}] and
R=P/IR=P/I. A derivation D:R→ND:R\to N is determined by the elements
uj=D⁡(xj¯)∈N,1≤j≤n.{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u_{j}=D(\overline{x_{j}})\in N,\qquad 1\leq j\leq n.}
For every relation g∈Ig\in I, these elements must satisfy
0=D⁡(g¯)=∑j=1n∂g∂xj¯​uj.{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}0=D(\overline{g})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\overline{\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{j}}}\,u_{j}.}
Conversely, any tuple (u1,…,un)(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}) satisfying these relations
defines a derivation. In particular, when I=JfI=J_{f}, differentiating the
relations ∂f/∂xi\partial f/\partial x_{i} produces the Hessian matrix. This is
the presentation of relative derivations used in Section 4.
When N=RN=R, the commutator
[D1,D2]=D1​D2−D2​D1[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D_{1},D_{2}}]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=D_{1}D_{2}-D_{2}D_{1}}

3. Socles and the non-quasi-homogeneous case
Let (R,𝔫)(R,\mathfrak{n}) be a finite-dimensional local ℂ\mathbb{C}-algebra. The next two
facts about its socle are standard. We include their short proofs because
they are used at the central step of the argument. General background on
Artinian Gorenstein rings and socles can be found in
[2, Chapter 3].
Lemma 3.1.
Every nonzero ideal of RR meets Soc⁡(R)\operatorname{Soc}(R) nontrivially. If RR is
Artinian Gorenstein, then every nonzero ideal contains Soc⁡(R)\operatorname{Soc}(R).
Proof.
Let I⊆RI\subseteq R be a nonzero ideal. Since RR is an Artinian local ring, its maximal ideal 𝔪\mathfrak{m} is nilpotent. Thus, there exists a well-defined maximum integer r≥0r\geq 0 such that 𝔪r​I≠0\mathfrak{m}^{r}I\neq 0. By the maximality of rr, we have 𝔪r+1​I=0\mathfrak{m}^{r+1}I=0, which means 𝔪⁡(𝔪r​I)=0\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{m}^{r}I)=0. Consequently, the nonzero submodule 𝔪r​I\mathfrak{m}^{r}I is annihilated by 𝔪\mathfrak{m}, implying that 0≠𝔪r​I⊆Soc⁡(R)0\neq\mathfrak{m}^{r}I\subseteq\operatorname{Soc}(R). This proves the first assertion.
If RR is additionally Gorenstein, Soc⁡(R)\operatorname{Soc}(R) is a one-dimensional vector space over the residue field R/𝔪R/\mathfrak{m}. Since 𝔪r​I\mathfrak{m}^{r}I is a nonzero subspace of Soc⁡(R)\operatorname{Soc}(R), it must coincide with the entire socle. Hence, Soc⁡(R)=𝔪r​I⊆I\operatorname{Soc}(R)=\mathfrak{m}^{r}I\subseteq I, completing the proof.
∎
Lemma 3.2 (Socle preservation).
Let (R,𝔫)(R,\mathfrak{n}) be a finite-dimensional local ℂ\mathbb{C}-algebra. Every

Let us first show that D⁡(𝔫)⊆𝔫D(\mathfrak{n})\subseteq\mathfrak{n}. Since RR is a finite-dimensional local ℂ\mathbb{C}-algebra, it is an Artinian local ring. Therefore, its unique maximal ideal 𝔫\mathfrak{n} coincides with its nilradical, which implies that every element a∈𝔫a\in\mathfrak{n} is nilpotent.
Let a∈𝔫a\in\mathfrak{n}, and suppose that D⁡(a)D(a)
is a unit. Choose the least r≥1r\geq 1 with ar=0a^{r}=0. Since the
characteristic is zero,
0=D⁡(ar)=r​ar−1​D​(a).0=D(a^{r})=ra^{r-1}D(a).
Cancelling the unit D⁡(a)D(a) gives ar−1=0a^{r-1}=0, a contradiction. Thus

The last term is zero because D⁡(a)∈𝔫D(a)\in\mathfrak{n}. Hence D⁡(s)D(s) is annihilated by

4.5. An alternative proof

HilbM⁡(t)=∏i=1n1−td−wi1−twi,σ=n​d−2​W.\operatorname{Hilb}_{M}(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1-t^{d-w_{i}}}{1-t^{w_{i}}},\qquad\sigma=nd-2W.
In particular,
Mσ=ℂh¯,Bq=0(q≥σ),dimℂB=μ(V)−1.M_{\sigma}=\mathbb{C}\overline{h},\qquad B_{q}=0\quad(q\geq\sigma),\qquad\dim_{\mathbb{C}}B=\mu(V)-1.
These are standard facts and were used as inputs to the exploration.
The
relations defining derivations give
Derℂ(M,B)≅ker(H:Bn⟶Bn)\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,B)\cong\ker\bigl(H:B^{n}\longrightarrow B^{n}\bigr)
and
Derℂ(B,B)≅ker([H∇h¯t]:Bn⟶Bn+1).\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(B,B)\cong\ker\left(\begin{bmatrix}H\\
\overline{\nabla h}^{\,t}\end{bmatrix}:B^{n}\longrightarrow B^{n+1}\right).
Under these identifications, a derivation D:M→BD:M\to B induces a
derivation of B=M/(h¯)B=M/(\overline{h}) precisely when it vanishes on
(h¯)=ker⁡(M⟶B),(\overline{h})=\ker(M\longrightarrow B),
or equivalently when D⁡(h¯)=0D(\overline{h})=0. This condition is measured by
ob:ker(H:Bn⟶Bn)⟶B,u⟼∇h¯tu.\operatorname{ob}:\ker\bigl(H:B^{n}\longrightarrow B^{n}\bigr)\longrightarrow B,\qquad u\longmapsto\overline{\nabla h}^{\,t}u.
Equivalently, if uu represents D∈Derℂ⁡(M,B)D\in\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,B), then
ob⁡(D)=D⁡(h¯).\operatorname{ob}(D)=D(\overline{h}).
It follows that
dimℂDerℂ⁡(M,B)−dimℂDerℂ⁡(B,B)=rankℂ⁡(ob).\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,B)-\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(B,B)=\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{C}}(\operatorname{ob}).
This converts the dimension comparison into a question about one
specified map.
A.3. Exact sequence and Cramer derivations
The authors proposed to compare
Derℂ⁡(M,M)\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,M) and Derℂ⁡(B,B)\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(B,B) through the intermediate
space Derℂ⁡(M,B)\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,B). A pre-manuscript note generated with AIM
developed this proposal into a preliminary two-step argument for the
weighted homogeneous case.
The adjugate-Hessian derivations and the degree estimate used in this
argument already appear in
[3, Theorem C]. The AIM-generated note did not
originate these ingredients. It used them in the comparison of the
three derivation spaces described above.
The first step compares Derℂ⁡(M,M)\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,M) with
Derℂ⁡(M,B)\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,B). Since
B=M/(h¯),Soc⁡(M)=ℂ​h¯,B=M/(\overline{h}),\qquad\operatorname{Soc}(M)=\mathbb{C}\overline{h},
the quotient map Mn→BnM^{n}\to B^{n} gives a short exact sequence
0⟶Soc⁡(M)⊕n⟶Mn⟶Bn⟶0.0\longrightarrow\operatorname{Soc}(M)^{\oplus n}\longrightarrow M^{n}\longrightarrow B^{n}\longrightarrow 0.
The Hessian matrix acts on both MnM^{n} and BnB^{n}. Since
mult⁡(f)≥3\operatorname{mult}(f)\geq 3, its entries lie in the maximal ideal of MM, and
hence
H​Soc⁡(M)⊕n=0.H\operatorname{Soc}(M)^{\oplus n}=0.
Therefore there is a commutative diagram with exact rows:
0{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}Soc⁡(M)⊕n{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Soc}(M)^{\oplus n}}Mn{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M^{n}}Bn{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B^{n}}0{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}0{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}Soc⁡(M)⊕n{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Soc}(M)^{\oplus n}}Mn{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M^{n}}Bn{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B^{n}}0.{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0.}0\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}H\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H}H\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H}
Applying the snake lemma to this diagram, and then using
H​adj⁡(H)=h​InH\operatorname{adj}(H)=hI_{n}
gives the exact sequence
0⟶Soc⁡(M)⊕n⟶Derℂ⁡(M,M)⟶Derℂ⁡(M,B)→∂Soc⁡(M)⊕n⟶0.0\longrightarrow\operatorname{Soc}(M)^{\oplus n}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,M)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,B)\xrightarrow{\partial}\operatorname{Soc}(M)^{\oplus n}\longrightarrow 0.
Consequently,
dimℂDerℂ⁡(M,B)=dimℂDerℂ⁡(M,M).\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,B)=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,M).
For p=1,…,np=1,\ldots,n, the pp-th column of
adj⁡(H)\operatorname{adj}(H) defines a derivation Dp:M→BD_{p}:M\to B by
Dp​(xj¯)=π⁡(adj⁡(H)j​p¯),1≤j≤n,D_{p}(\overline{x_{j}})=\pi\bigl(\overline{\operatorname{adj}(H)_{jp}}\bigr),\qquad 1\leq j\leq n,
where π:M→B\pi:M\to B is the quotient map. Cramer’s identity gives
∂(Dp)=h¯​ep.\partial(D_{p})=\overline{h}\,e_{p}.
The classes of D1,…,DnD_{1},\ldots,D_{n} therefore span the quotient
Q:=Derℂ⁡(M,B)/im⁡(Derℂ⁡(M,M)⟶Derℂ⁡(M,B)).Q:=\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,B)\big/\operatorname{im}\bigl(\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,M)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,B)\bigr).
The second step determines which derivations D:M→BD:M\to B define
derivations of the quotient algebra B=M/(h¯)B=M/(\overline{h}). Such a derivation
defines a derivation of BB precisely when
D⁡(h¯)=0.D(\overline{h})=0.
The AIM-generated note organized this condition through the map
ob:Derℂ⁡(M,B)⟶B,ob⁡(D)=D⁡(h¯).\operatorname{ob}:\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,B)\longrightarrow B,\qquad\operatorname{ob}(D)=D(\overline{h}).
Preservation of the one-dimensional socle of MM shows that
ob\operatorname{ob} vanishes on the image of
Derℂ⁡(M,M)\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,M). For the derivations DpD_{p}, one has
deg⁡(Dp)=ϵp=σ−d+wp.\deg(D_{p})=\epsilon_{p}=\sigma-d+w_{p}.
The degree estimate gives ϵp≥0\epsilon_{p}\geq 0. Since h¯\overline{h} has
degree σ\sigma and
Bq=0(q≥σ),B_{q}=0\qquad(q\geq\sigma),
it follows that
ob⁡(Dp)=Dp​(h¯)∈Bσ+ϵp=0.\operatorname{ob}(D_{p})=D_{p}(\overline{h})\in B_{\sigma+\epsilon_{p}}=0.
The classes of the DpD_{p} span QQ. Hence
ob=0\operatorname{ob}=0 on all of Derℂ⁡(M,B)\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,B). Every
derivation M→BM\to B therefore defines a derivation of BB, and
dimℂDerℂ⁡(B,B)=dimℂDerℂ⁡(M,B).\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(B,B)=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,B).
Together with the first step, this gives
dimℂDerℂ⁡(B,B)=dimℂDerℂ⁡(M,M).\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(B,B)=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,M).
The AIM-generated note contained this preliminary two-step argument.
The authors checked every map, degree calculation, and exactness
statement, corrected the intermediate argument where necessary, and
wrote the final proof given in the manuscript.
A.4. AIM-assisted examples
AIM was used to explore and check examples that were later included in
the final section of the paper. For the AkA_{k} singularities, the
calculation shows that the natural map
L​(V)⟶L∗​(V)L(V)\longrightarrow L^{*}(V)
has a one-dimensional kernel. AIM-assisted calculations also gave
λ⁡(V)=λ∗​(V)=4\lambda(V)=\lambda^{*}(V)=4
for f=x3+y3+∑zi2f=x^{3}+y^{3}+\sum z_{i}^{2}, and a difference of one for
f=xa​y+y2f=x^{a}y+y^{2}. For the family
fs=x3+y3+s​x​y+z2,f_{s}=x^{3}+y^{3}+sxy+z^{2},
both dimensions are zero for s≠0s\neq 0, whereas for s=0s=0, both
dimensions are four. Thus their common value may jump even though the
equality between them is preserved.
The authors selected the examples, verified all calculations, and
included them to illustrate the main result.
A.5. Scope of assistance
The main purpose of the paper is to prove
λ∗​(V)=λ​(V)\lambda^{*}(V)=\lambda(V)
for isolated hypersurface singularities with n≥2n\geq 2 and
mult⁡(f)≥3\operatorname{mult}(f)\geq 3, thereby proving Conjecture 1.1. The authors
formulated the problem and proposed comparing
Derℂ⁡(M,M)\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,M) and Derℂ⁡(B,B)\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(B,B) through
Derℂ⁡(M,B)\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb{C}}(M,B). A pre-manuscript note generated with AIM contained
a preliminary two-step argument for the weighted homogeneous case. AIM was
also used for preliminary calculations of the examples.
The adjugate-Hessian derivations and the required degree estimate were
already known from earlier work and are not attributed to AIM. The
authors checked and corrected the preliminary argument, supplied the
remaining arguments needed to prove the conjecture in full, and wrote
the final proof. They take responsibility for every mathematical
statement and calculation in the paper.
