                                              Let A be an n × n random matrix with i.i.d., mean 0, variance 1 subgaussian entries.

                                          A is exponentially small. They also obtained a quantitative version of the least singular
                                          value estimate. Let σmin (A) denote the least singular value of A, then by [32], we can find

    Now that we have a comprehensive understanding of the singularity of A and the esti-
mates for σmin (A), there are still some open problems concerning the invertibility of A that
are not implied by all these results. In this paper we study two such problems, namely
    (1) Is it true that with probability 1 − o(1), all singular values of A are distinct?
    (2) Can we prove a joint least singular value estimate for σmin (A − λ1 In ) and σmin (A −
        λ2 In ) for relatively distant λ1 , λ2 ∈ R (i.e., with a lower bound on |λ1 − λ2 | ) so that
                 P(σmin (A − λ1 In ) ≤ ǫ, σmin (A − λ2 In ) ≤ ǫ) ≤ Cǫ2 + e−cn ?
        We can use this to show with high probability there are no two real eigenvalues
        λ1 , λ2 of A satisfying any fixed linear relation, say, aλ1 + bλ2 = c for fixed a, b, c.
    The problem (1) was stated as an open problem in Vu [42], Conjecture 8.5. In fact, he
stated the problem for both a real symmetric random matrix and an i.i.d. random matrix,
and claimed problem (1) is open for both models. We justify this Conjecture 8.5 for an i.i.d.
matrix A with an exponential bound on the probability of simple singular value spectrum.
    The problem (2) is related to an intrinsic difficulty in least singular value estimates,
and to our best knowledge such a joint estimate has never been proven before for any
random matrix with discrete entry distribution. We will prove a version of the estimate
in (2) that works for any λ1 6= λ2 , with a factor before ǫ2 depending on the distance
|λ1 − λ2 |quantitatively. Then we further generalize the estimate to complex-valued random
matrices with genuinely complex shift z1 , z2 , and obtain a bound of order ǫ4 .
1.1. Simplicity of singular value spectrum. Now we rigorously state the main results
of this paper. The first result concerns the singular value spectrum.
Theorem 1.1. Let ξ be a mean 0, variance 1 subgaussian real random variable and let A
be an n × n random matrix with independent identically distributed entries of distribution
ξ. Denote by σ1 (A) ≥ · · · ≥ σn (A) the singular values of A arranged in decreasing order.
Then for any ǫ > 0 we have
                       max         P(|σk+1 (A) − σk (A)| ≤ ǫn−1/2 ) ≤ Cǫ + e−cn ,
                    k=1,··· ,n−1

for some constants C, c > 0 depending only on ξ.
    In particular, we have
                   P(All the singular values of A are distinct) ≥ 1 − e−cn .
    This positively proves a version of [42], Conjecture 8.5 for i.i.d. matrices, and provides a
stronger statement that the probability of two equal singular values is exponentially small.
This bound is best possible up to the constant c > 0: when the distribution ξ is discrete
valued, then with an exponentially small probability A may have two zero singular values.
Remark 1.2. In [42], Conjecture 8.5 another conjecture was made concerning whether the
singular values of a random symmetric matrix are distinct with high probability, which
is equivalent to whether two eigenvalues of the matrix sum up to 0. Our method, after
significant generalization, can possibly be used to show that the linear span of two eigen-
vectors of the matrix has no rigid arithmetic structure (i.e., the essential LCD (2.2), (3.8)
of the vector pair is large). While this result is nontrivial and of independent interest, this

does not completely solve the original problem. In [21] the author attempted to solve this
conjecture but the result only works when the entry distribution has a bounded density.
    After drafting the manuscript, we realized that the proof of Theorem 1.1 can be gener-
alized to rectangular random matrices with i.i.d. entries. We have the following theorem:
Theorem 1.3. Let ξ be a random variable as in Theorem 1.1. Fix a > 1 and let N, n be
positive integers satisfying an ≥ N ≥ n. Let Z be an N × n rectangular random matrix with
i.i.d. coordinates of distribution ξ. Denote by σ1 (Z) ≥ · · · ≥ σn (Z) the singular values of
Z. Then all the claims in Theorem 1.1 remain true with σk (Z) in place of σk (A), and with
constants C, c > 0 depending only on ξ and a. In particular, Z has distinct singular values
with probability at least 1 − exp(−Ω(n)).
    The assumption N ≥ n is clearly necessary, as otherwise Z has n − N zero singular
values. Theorem 1.3 shows that this is essentially the only barrier to a simple singular value
spectrum, since all non-zero singular values should be separated. As we focus on square
matrices in this paper, we will not alter the proof of Theorem 1.1 but present in Section 2.8
an outline of how the proof of Theorem 1.1 can be modified to yield Theorem 1.3.
    After the paper was submitted to the arXiv, the author learned about a concurrent
work [8] which also considered simplicity of singular values. They considered slightly more
general models and relaxed moment assumptions, but did not achieve an exponential bound
in probability and the singular gap estimate is slightly weaker.

1.4. Main difficulties, essential steps and related works. As one may expect, the
main technical difficulty in proving Theorem 1.1 and 1.4 lies in the discrete entry distribu-
tion. Indeed, if the distribution of entries of A has a bounded density, then we can prove
both theorems in a considerably simplified way. For discrete entry distribution, one usu-
ally needs to eliminate arithmetic obstructions via applying inverse Littlewood-Offord type
theorems [32]. This helps us to prove that the normal vector to a certain subspace has no
simple arithmetic structure with very high probability. Implementing this step in our model
is however very difficult for both Theorem 1.1 and 1.4. For example, in Theorem 1.1 let v
be a singular vector of A, then we have A∗ Av = λv, but the entries of A∗ A are all correlated
so that no Littlewood-Offord type theorem can be used. In Theorem 1.4 the problem is
as follows. For any square matrix A let A[1] denote the matrix A with its first row set to
zero. Then to apply the framework of Rudelson and Vershynin [32] we need to prove that
the linear span of the orthogonal complement of both (A − λ1 In )[1] and (A − λ2 In )[1] has
no vectors with simple arithmetic structure. Unfortunately this space is not annihilated by
any linear equation of A but only by a quadratic equation of A, and again the correlation
of entries of A2 prevents the use of classical Littlewood-Offord theorems.

     We now briefly review the history of invertibility of a symmetric random matrix, that
is, the probability that a random ±1 matrix is singular. The problem was first considered
by Costello, Tao and Vu [12] who proved almost sure invertibility. Then the invertibility
probability was strengthened by Nguyen [28] to n−ω(1) , Vershynin [40] to exp(−nc ), Ferber
and Jain [16] to c = 1/4, and Campos, Mattos, Morris, and Morrison [5] to c = 1/2.
Finally, the exponential upper bound c = 1 was proven by Campos, Jensen, Michelen and
Sahasrabudhe [7]. An optimal singular value estimate was then derived in [6]. Our proof of
Theorem 1.1 takes many fruitful ideas from these works. As our block matrix (the left one
in (1.5)) has less randomness than the symmetric matrix in these cited works, our estimates
are more sensitive to the loss of randomness if we cannot fully decouple dependence of two
identical blocks.
     For Theorem 1.1, we will use the conditioned inverse Littlewood-Offord theorem and a
double counting technique which is similar to the one in [7]. This idea was first introduced
in the breakthrough work of [39] and was coined “inversion of randomness”, where we use
randomness both in the matrix and from the random choice of a vector from the net. The
idea was then made into a different usage in [7] to prove that the singularity probability of
a random symmetric matrix is exp(−Ω(n)). In this paper we will take the latter viewpoint,
but use the random net construction in a different way for the first matrix in (1.5).
     In proving Theorem 1.4 and 1.5, we face difficulties of another kind when dealing with
the second matrix in (1.5). Here we no longer need the conditioned version of Littlewood-
Offord theorems from [7], but for a vector (v, w) ∈ Rn+n in the kernel of the matrix, we need
to stratify all possibilities of overlap between v and w. Construction of nets depending on
this overlap is also highly involved for lack of rotational symmetry. We will use a two-stage
process to define the nets, the first stage uses a randomized procedure and the second stage
constructs a net for vectors where either v or w has sub-exponentially large LCD. To set
up the two-point estimate of least singular values, we will use the no-gaps delocalization
property from [30] for an initial reduction, and we feel this is a novel step not appearing in
any previous works. A more careful description of the proof for both Theorem 1.1 and 1.5
are given in later chapters.

                      3. Two-point invertibility: the real case
     This section contains the proof of Theorem 1.5, where we show a quantitative control
of least singular values jointly over two locations λ1 , λ2 .
3.1. Proof outline of Theorem 1.5. We will begin with an adaptation of the geometric
approach to quantitative invertibility by Rudelson and Vershynin [32]. However, on a high
level, three difficulties will arise in our proof:
    (1) As we consider joint estimates at two locations simultaneously, when we use a lemma
         in [32] that reduces invertibility over incompressible vectors to the inner product of a
         column with another normal vector, we face a difficulty as the singular vector at the
         two different locations may not have an overlapping support of good property. More
         precisely, through a standard procedure we can prove that both singular vectors are
         incompressible, but that only means they have cρ,δ n indices of absolute value within
         [κ0 n−1/2 , κ1 n−1/2 ]. When cρ,δ < 12 we cannot conclude that both singular vectors
         at two locations have a common support on which the absolute value of vector
         coordinates lie in this range. Fortunately, we have an additional input of no-gaps
         delocalization from [30], which confirms in a strong form that this is true. Therefore
         we are now able to generalize [32] and reduce two-location singular value estimates
         to the joint small ball probability of a column with two different normal vectors.
    (2) After the reduction in (1), we wish to apply Littlewood-Offord type inequalities to
         eliminate normal vectors having a rigid arithmetic structure. But this time we have
         two normal vectors, and we need to show the vector pair has large LCD with high
         possibility. The difficulty here is that linear span of two normal vectors is not lying
         in the kernel of one linear equation, say it is not annihilated by either A − λ1 In or
         A − λ2 In (with one column or row removed), or by any linear combinations of them.
         (There is an exception when |λ1 − λ2 | → 0 so that we could still use the standard
         strategy, as is explored in [19] and [26], but this is not working in our general case.)
         This linear span can be annihilated by a quadratic equation of A, but Littlewood-
         Offord type theorems are difficult to apply for quadratic equations. This is the
         major technical difficulty of our proof, and we will solve it by taking a linearization
         reduction and consider a 2n-dimensional system instead. To eliminate all vectors
         in the linear span, we need to consider a continuously parametrized family of the
         linearization matrix all at once, and show with high possibility the kernel of any of
         them has no vector with small LCD.
    (3) A further technical difficulty arises when |λ1 − λ2 | is very small but still larger than
         e−cn . In this case it is hard to control the inner product of the two normal vectors
         to A − λ1 In , A − λ2 In (with a column removed) and their inner product could be
         arbitrarily close to 1. Here we will take a stratification of unit vectors in Rd × Rd
         SIMPLICITY OF SINGULAR VALUE SPECTRUM AND TWO-POINT INVERTIBILITY                    27

        by constructing nets of vectors with small LCD depending on the inner product,
        such that when the inner product is close to 1, the anticoncentration bound from
        Littlewood-Offord theorem is weaker but we also have a smaller net, and everything
        gets balanced out. This is philosophically similar to the real-complex correlation
        considered in [30], and then in [19] and [26]. But in our case, we feel there is a
        technical difficulty to construct vector pairs with intermediate LCD. In [26], the
        author can multiply the eigenvector by a phase so the LCD of the vector pair of
        the real and complex parts for the singular vector is achieved by its real part. We
        cannot do this here, so we prefer to use a random method to generate the net with
        intermediate LCD, in the same way as in Section 2. Although the proof is longer,
        it becomes more transparent and unified, and also works efficiently when |λ1 − λ2 |
        is very very small. Note that in contrast to Section 2, it is not necessary to use the
        idea of inversion of randomness, or the conditioned Littlewood-Offord theorem 2.18
        here. But unlike in Section 2, we face new challenge around the LCD of vector pairs
        and arbitrarily large overlap here.

3.1.4. A brief outline of the rest of the section. The remaining parts of this section are highly
technical, so we outline here the main technical steps, and how our method is different from
other methods. First in Section 3.2 and 3.3 we rule out compressible vectors in the linear
span of X ∗ [1]j and X ∗ [2]j . Even this step is nontrivial: the method we take is to note
that any vector in the linear span is annihilated by a quadratic polynomial of A (but up
to a certain error). This error term will require us to study how large is the j-th entry of
the vector. Thus we first show that this j-th entry cannot be too large, and then show all
vectors in the linear span should be incompressible with high probability.
    Then we rule out vectors in the linear span with small LCD. This would typically follow
from an ǫ-net argument via LCD, but here we have a new difficulty when constructing the
net as we have to know for which θ ∈ [0, 2π] we have cos(θ)X ∗ [1]j + sin(θ)X ∗ [2]j has the
least LCD (in other cases such as [26], we can multiply the vector by a complex phase and
assume that the smallest LCD is achieved, say by X ∗ [1]j , but not here). The setting further
complicates when |λ1 − λ2 | is small and X ∗ [1]j , X ∗ [2]j could have large overlap. We take
the random generation method from Section 2 to avoid these complications, and to keep a
coherent style throughout the whole paper (despite a bit lengthy argument).
    Specifically, Section 3.4 is a technical preparation for the random generation method
and Section 3.5 defines a family of nets Nǫ,ǫ1 (c) in terms of small ball probability of a certain
truncated matrix MA . Here ǫ is the scale of small ball probability and ǫ1 roughly measures
32                                              YI HAN

the overlap between X ∗ [1]j and X ∗ [2]j . Section 3.5 also defines a threshold function τL,ǫ1 to
measure the arithmetic structure of the vector with respect to MA , in a way parameterized
by ǫ1 > 0. We construct discrete approximations for the vector pair but with different size
of the mesh grid in its two components, taking into account ǫ1 > 0. Then in Section 3.6 we
upper bound the cardinality of Nǫ,ǫ1 (c) via Littlewood-Offord theorems and sow Nǫ,ǫ1 (c) is
a net via a random rounding technique. Here we use the randomness in the base vector as
we exhaust all of them in an integral lattice: we can show that it has large two-dimensional
essential LCD with super-exponential probability. Finally, in Section 3.7 we move back
from the net Nǫ,ǫ1 (c) to the net constructed from essential LCD, and obtain Theorem 3.8.
    Overall, we can successfully deal with all possible cases of overlaps of X ∗ [1]j , X ∗ [2]j in a
unified way and avoid certain difficulties in constructing nets of vector pairs based on LCD
when we cannot rotate the direction of smallest LCD to one axis. We will generalize this
construction in Section 4 to complex i.i.d. matrices.
