                             Local Removal Lemmas
                                     Fan Wei
                  (joint work with Jacob Fox, Hung Le, Ruilin Shi)

The graph removal lemma is among the central results in extremal combinatorics
and has broad applications, including to extremal problems for graphs and hy-
pergraphs, additive combinatorics, discrete geometry, and theoretical computer
science. It qualitatively says that if a graph has relatively few copies of a ﬁxed
graph H, then it is close to a H-free graph.
   It is natural to study removal lemmas under a stronger metric. The usual edit
distance is equivalent to the normalized L1 length of the degree sequence of the
removed spanning subgraph. It is also natural to consider the Lp distance for any
ﬁnite p > 0, but any of these metrics are equivalent, meaning if one is small (o(1))
then the others are small, and so are not that interesting for further study. The
L∞ distance is the normalized maximum degree in the subgraph of the removed
edge. Two graphs G1 , G2 on the same vertex set V have L∞ distance at most ε if
the graph on V with edge set E(G1 )∆E(G2 ) has maximum degree at most ε|V |.
Thus the L∞ distance is at least the corresponding L1 distance. Hence, the L∞
distance is a stronger metric than the edit distance.
   It is natural to ask the following question.
         Does a variant of the graph removal lemma hold under the L∞
         distance?
   To see why this is a natural notion, recall in the usual graph removal lemma,
we bound the total number of edges removed, which is equivalent to bounding
the average degree of the spanning subgraph of the removed edges. In contrast,
here we are asking to bound the maximum degree of the spanning subgraph of the
removed edges.
   The following theorem resolves the question for cliques.
Combinatorics                                                                      59

is δ = δ(ε, k) > 0 such that every graph on n vertices has a vertex in δnk−1 copies
of Kk or can be made Kk -free by deleting the edges of a subgraph of maximum
degree at most εn.
  We further characterize graphs which are o(1)-close to being Kk -free under L∞ .

               Ramsey Cayley graphs over non-abelian groups
                           Carl Schildkraut

Ramsey’s theorem is one of the foundational results in extremal graph theory, and
there is a great amount of literature on its variations, extensions, and quantitative
aspects. The following deﬁnition is inspired by its quantitative aspects.
Deﬁnition 1. Let C > 0. A graph on n vertices is C-Ramsey if its clique number
and independence number are both at most C log2 n.
   The proof of Ramsey’s theorem by Erdős and Szekeres [7] yields that no graph is
C-Ramsey for C < 1/2, while Erdős [6] showed 12 years later, in one of the ﬁrst ap-
plications of the probabilistic method, that a random n-vertex graph is 2-Ramsey
with high probability. However, we do not yet know any explicit constructions of
C-Ramsey graphs for any constant C.
   To this end, it is natural to ask where C-Ramsey graphs can be found, and
what properties they can have. The following conjecture along these lines is due
to Noga Alon:
Conjecture 2 (Alon [1, 2]). There exists some absolute constant C > 0 for which
every ﬁnite group G admits a C-Ramsey Cayley1 graph.
  The ﬁrst positive result in the line of this conjecture was given by Green [8], who
showed that Conjecture 2 holds for cyclic groups. Recently, a signiﬁcant advance
was made by Conlon, Fox, Pham, and Yepremyan:
Theorem 3 (Conlon–Fox–Pham–Yepremyan [5]). Conjecture 2 holds for the fam-
ily of abelian groups whose orders are relatively prime to 6. Moreover, Conjecture 2
holds for all abelian groups of almost all2 orders.
   Our main result is an extension of Theorem 3 to not-necessarily-abelian groups.

Non-abelian–to–abelian tools for dense sets. Many tools are used in additive
combinatorics in the abelian setting, and only some transfer over to non-abelian
settings. (In the case of the proof of Lemma 5, the failure in the non-abelian setting
is quite basic: the proof directly uses the commutativity of the group operation
to set up an application of the pigeonhole principle.) One trick, due originally to
Bergelson, McCutcheon, and Zhang [4], to get around such issues in some settings
is to ﬁnd an abelian subgroup of the ambient non-abelian group and to work only
within its cosets. This is best accomplished by the following (asymptotically tight)
theorem of Pyber.
Theorem 7 (Pyber [10]). For some constant c > 0, every ﬁnite group G has an
abelian subgroup of order at least exp(c log1/2 |G|).

   Theorem 7 is quantitatively quite strong, and has been used to great eﬀect to
show that dense subsets of arbitrary ﬁnite groups contain various patterns (see
[3, 4, 12, 13] for a few examples). For example, it can be used to provide upper
bounds on the size of a subset of a non-abelian group avoiding three-term arith-
metic progressions of nearly the same quantitative strength as we have for Roth’s
theorem (the same statement in cyclic groups). The main diﬀerence between such
applications and Lemma 6 is the size of the subset of the group in question. If
A ⊂ G is quite dense in G, and H ≤ G is a subgroup, then there exists some coset
gH of H for which A ∩ gH is quite dense in gH, while if A is sparse then there
may not even be a single coset which contains more than one element of A.

                 The generalised Erdős-Rothschild problem
                             Katherine Staden
        (joint work with Pranshu Gupta, Yanitsa Pehova, Emil Powierski)


The Erdős-Rothschild problem from 1974 [5] asks for the maximum number of
s-edge colourings in an n-vertex graph which avoid a monochromatic copy of Kk ,
given positive integers n, s, k. We systematically study the generalisation of this
problem to a given forbidden family of colourings of Kk .
    Given a graph G, and a partition P of the edges of Kk which we call a pattern,
let F (G; P, s) be the number of s-edge-colourings of G such that there is no copy of
Kk in which the edge-partition given by the colour classes is isomorphic to P . The
original ‘monochromatic’ problem is the case when the pattern P is the partition
into one part. We are interested in the generalised Erdős-Rothschild problem, that
is, determining
                              F (n; P, s) := max F (G; P, s)
                                              v(G)=n

in the case when n is large.
   This problem typically exhibits a dichotomy whereby for some values of s,
the extremal graph is the ‘trivial’ one, namely the Turán graph on k − 1 parts,
with no copies of Kk ; while for others, we know the extremal graph has many
copies of Kk but determining it becomes much harder. There are very few results,
asymptotic or exact, which are known. For the monochromatic problem, the only
pairs (k, s) where there is a solution are (k, 2), (k, 3), which are known to be the
only values with trivial solution; and (3, 4), (3, 5) (asymptotic only), (3, 6), (3, 7)
and (4, 4) [1, 4, 9, 11, 12]. The ‘rainbow triangle’ problem where k = 3 and P has
three parts each containing one edge, has been solved completely: for s = 3 the
extremal graph is a complete graph while for s ≥ 4 it is the trivial one [2]. There
are a handful of results for other patterns.
