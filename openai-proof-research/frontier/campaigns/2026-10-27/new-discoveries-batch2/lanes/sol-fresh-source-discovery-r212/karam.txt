                                                   Abstract. Let Γ be a connected metric graph of first Betti number b ≥ 2, and let Γb be
                                                   a trivalent graph of the same Betti number with all edges of unit length. We show that if

                                                   length(Γ) ≤ λ length(Γb ), then the universal cover of Γ contains a vertex around which, at
                                                   every radius R ≥ 0, the ball is at least as long as the ball of radius R in the trivalent tree
                                                   whose edges all have length 3λ. In particular, for λ ≤ 31 , this ball is at least as long as the ball
                                                   of the same radius in the universal cover of Γb , with no multiplicative loss. This improves an
                                                   earlier theorem of the author, which gives the weaker lower bound (1 − 3λ) times the ball in
                                                   the universal cover of Γb , and only for λ ≤ 16 . The proof rests on an elementary comparison
                                                   principle. Among trees whose vertices have degree at least three and whose edges have length
                                                   at most c, the trivalent tree with all edges of length c has the smallest balls at every radius.
                                                   As an application, we double the exponential growth rate in the author’s earlier uniform lower
                                                   bound for the area of balls in the universal cover of small-area surfaces of hyperbolic type,
                                                   from ln 2 to 2 ln 2, beyond the hyperbolic rate. We also obtain explicit constants in the positive
                                                   answer to Guth’s question for surfaces. Finally, we conjecture the sharp, all-scale version of
                                                   the minimal volume entropy inequality of Kapovich–Nagnibeda and Lim, and show that it
                                                   would be optimal.

1.2. Metric graphs and notation. A graph is a one-dimensional CW-complex, possibly
with loops and multiple edges; unless stated otherwise it is finite. The degree deg v of a vertex
v is the number of edges incident to it, loops being counted twice. A graph is trivalent if all
its vertices have degree 3. A subgraph is a union of vertices and edges. The first Betti number
of a finite connected graph Γ with e edges and v vertices is b(Γ) = e − v + 1; it is the rank of
H1 (Γ; Z).
   A metric graph (Γ, h) is a graph in which each edge is identified with a compact interval
of positive length, endowed with the induced length metric d. The length of a subset A ⊂ Γ,
denoted length(A), is its one-dimensional Hausdorff measure; in particular length(Γ, h) is the
sum of the edge lengths. A metric tree is a connected, simply connected, locally finite metric
graph, possibly infinite. The universal cover of a connected metric graph (Γ, h), with the lifted
metric, is a metric tree denoted (Γ,   h); its vertices are the lifts of the vertices of Γ. For a metric

                                       2. Preliminaries
2.1. Arcs and smoothing.
Definition 2.1. Let Γ be a finite connected graph with b(Γ) ≥ 2 and no vertex of degree one.
Then Γ has vertices of degree at least 3, which we call branch vertices. Indeed, if all degrees
were 2, then Γ would be a cycle and b(Γ) = 1. An arc of Γ is a path whose endpoints are
branch vertices (possibly equal) and whose interior vertices all have degree 2. Every edge lies
in exactly one arc. Smoothing Γ means forgetting the vertices of degree 2, so that each arc
becomes a single edge of the same length. This yields a graph structure in which every vertex
has degree at least 3, on the same metric space.

                                       4. Short cores
  The following lemma isolates the combinatorial content of the induction in [5, Section 4].
Lemma 4.1. Let (Γ, h) be a finite connected metric graph with b(Γ) ≥ 2, and let c > 0 be such
that length(Γ, h) ≤ c (b(Γ) − 1). Then Γ contains a connected subgraph Γ0 such that
    (1) b(Γ0 ) ≥ 2 and length(Γ0 ) ≤ c (b(Γ0 ) − 1);
    (2) Γ0 has no vertex of degree one;
    (3) every arc of Γ0 (Definition 2.1) has length at most c.
6                                           STEVE KARAM

Proof. We argue by induction on b = b(Γ). First, repeatedly delete vertices of degree one
together with their incident edges. This changes neither b nor connectedness and does not
increase the length, so we may assume that Γ has no vertex of degree one. If every arc of Γ
has length at most c, take Γ0 = Γ.
   If b = 2, this is always the case. Indeed, Γ then has at least two arcs, each of positive length,
so every arc is strictly shorter than length(Γ) ≤ c. This settles the base case.
   Let b ≥ 3 and suppose some arc a has length(a) > c. Remove the edges and the interior
vertices of a. The remaining graph has length length(Γ) − length(a) < c(b − 1) − c = c(b − 2).
   If a does not disconnect Γ, the remainder Γ′ is connected with b(Γ′ ) = b − 1 ≥ 2 and
length(Γ′ ) < c (b(Γ′ ) − 1). By induction, Γ′ , and hence Γ, contains the required subgraph.
   If a disconnects Γ, the remainder is a disjoint union Γ′ ⊔ Γ′′ of two connected graphs with
b(Γ′ ) + b(Γ′′ ) = b. In each of Γ′ , Γ′′ , every vertex has degree at least 2: the endpoint of a it
contains had degree at least 3, and all other degrees are unchanged. Hence both contain a
cycle, so b(Γ′ ), b(Γ′′ ) ≥ 1, and both have positive length. Since
                     length(Γ′ ) + length(Γ′′ ) < c (b(Γ′ ) − 1) + c (b(Γ′′ ) − 1),
one of them, say Γ′ , satisfies length(Γ′ ) < c (b(Γ′ ) − 1). As its length is positive, b(Γ′ ) ≥ 2, and
the induction hypothesis applied to Γ′ concludes the proof.                                            □

                             7. Sharpness and the conjecture
    For λ > 0, let Γλb denote the graph Γb with all edges of length λ.
Proposition 7.1. Conjecture D, if true, is optimal in two senses.
     (1) For every λ > 0, the graph Γλb satisfies the hypothesis of Conjecture D, and every vertex
         ũ of its universal cover satisfies length B(ũ, R) = Vλ (R) for every R ≥ 0. Hence Vλ
         cannot be replaced by Vλ′ with λ′ < λ.
     (2) The threshold λ ≤ 1, under which the conjecture gives length B(ũ, R) ≥ V1 (R) for all
         R, is optimal. For λ ∈ (1, 2), every vertex ũ of the universal cover of Γλb satisfies
         length B(ũ, λ) = 3λ < 6λ − 3 = V1 (λ).

Proof. The universal cover of Γλb is Tλ , which gives the equality in (1). If λ′ < λ and R ∈
(λ′ , λ], then Vλ′ (R) > 3R = Vλ (R) by Definition 1.1. Part (2) follows from the same formula:
Vλ (λ) = 3λ and V1 (λ) = 3 + 6(λ − 1) for λ ∈ [1, 2].                                        □

Where the factor 3 comes from. Both ingredients of Theorem A are sharp: Theorem B
for T = Tc , and Lemma 4.1 by Remark 4.2. Their extremal configurations are incompatible,
however. Let Γ0 be a subgraph as in Lemma 4.1, and put b0 = b(Γ0 ). If all branch vertices of
Γ0 were trivalent and all its arcs had length close to c, then Γ0 would have 3b0 − 3 arcs and
length close to 3c(b0 − 1). This is three times the bound c(b0 − 1) guaranteed by Lemma 4.1(1).
Conversely, the rose of Remark 4.2 has a branch vertex of very large degree and therefore very
large balls. A proof of Conjecture D along these lines would need a comparison principle for
trees whose edges are short only on average, weighted by degrees as in Lim’s formula for the
entropy-minimising metric [7].
Example 7.2 (Equality for non-equilateral theta graphs). Let Θ be the theta graph whose
three edges have lengths x ≤ y ≤ z, where x + y + z = 3 = length(Γ2 ) and z ≤ 32 . Then z ≥ 1
and x + y ≥ z. The universal cover of Θ is a trivalent tree in which each vertex meets one
edge of each length, and all its vertices are equivalent under isometries. Fix a vertex ũ and,
for r > 0, let N (r) be the number of points at distance exactly r from ũ. Geodesics issuing
from ũ follow edge paths that never traverse an edge immediately followed by the same edge
in the opposite direction. Such paths reach a second vertex after length x, y or z, and a third
vertex after length at least x + y ≥ z. So N = 3, 4, 5 on (0, x), (x, y), (y, z) respectively, and
                             Z z

Moreover length B(ũ, R) ≥ V1 (R) on [0, z]. On [0, 1] this holds because N ≥ 3 and V1 (R) = 3R
there. On [1, z] it holds because V1 has slope 6 there while R 7→ length B(ũ, R) has slope at
most 5, and the two agree at z. Thus Conjecture D for λ = 1 is attained with equality
