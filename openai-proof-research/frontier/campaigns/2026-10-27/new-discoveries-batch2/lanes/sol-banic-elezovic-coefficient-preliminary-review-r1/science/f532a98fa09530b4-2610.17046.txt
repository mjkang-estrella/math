1    Introduction
                                                    Pn−1
The variance of the discrete total variation d = i=1 |Xi+1 − Xi | of an ordered
uniform multinomial sample is governed by three kinds of covariances between
absolute edge differences. The least expected concerns two edges that share no bin:
D1 = X2 − X1 and D3 = X4 − X3 are exactly uncorrelated and asymptotically
independent, yet Cov(|D1 |, |D3 |) = −1/(πn) + O(1/N ). This is a correlation of
relative order 1/N , invisible in the Gaussian limit, whose constant is proportional
to the exact joint fourth cumulant κ2,2 (D1 , D3 ) = −4N/n2 . Computing Var(d) to
two asymptotic orders, and identifying this constant together with its adjacent-pair
analogue a∗ , is the object of the present paper.
    Introduced as a histogram statistic in [2], d is the ℓ1 norm of the discrete
derivative of a histogram. Because it compares neighboring bins it depends on the
ordering of the categories, to which Pearson’s chi-square is invariant; in the empiri-
cal comparisons of [3] this gives power gains concentrated on period-2 (alternating)
departures pi = n1 + (−1)i ε (n even), chi-square being preferable against smooth
and longer-period departures. The variance of d was left open in [2], which gave
Var(Xi+1 − Xi ) = 2N/n under the uniform law and an exact double-sum formula
for the mean√ (closed at n = 2), but handled the dispersion of d only empirically,
through a N law fitted by simulation; the present paper supplies it. Part 1 [4]
computes the expectation of d under the uniform null; the present paper computes
its variance. The exact null law of d is computable by dynamic programming for N
up to a few hundred, and beyond that the comb test [3] uses a gamma law matched
to simulated moments; the closed-form mean (Part 1) and variance (this paper)
supply those two moments analytically and are the inputs of any moment-based
(gamma, normal or Edgeworth) approximation to the null law.
    The statistic sits in a classical  lineage. It is the ℓ1 analogue of the mean-
square successive difference i (Xi+1 − Xi )2 [15] — von Neumann’s ratio [14], and
                              P
the Durbin–Watson statistic in the regression setting [12] — with the absolute
value in place of the square; its direct ancestor is the mean absolute successive
difference of a normal sample, whose moments were obtained by Hoel [19] and
Kamat [21] and which reappears here as the Gaussian limit of d (Theorem 3.4). It
also belongs among the order-sensitive goodness-of-fit tools for ordered categories
(Neyman’s smooth test and its components [13], the cumulative chi-square [18],
runs tests [30]), whereas Pearson’s χ2 , being invariant under permutations of the
categories, ignores the order.
    The variance is harder than the mean for a structural reason. Under the uni-
form law the edge variables Dj = Xj+1 − Xj are pairwise correlated only between
neighbors, with the n-independent correlation ρ = − 21 , and uncorrelated at any
larger separation; yet Var(d) is built not from the Dj but from the absolute val-
ues |Dj |, and the absolute value does not respect this clean structure. Three
structurally distinct pieces enter: the per-edge variance Var(|D1 |), the adjacent
covariance Cov(|D1 |, |D2 |), and the disjoint covariance Cov(|D1 |, |D3 |). The last
is the non-Gaussian point. Distant edges are asymptotically independent, so their
absolute values decorrelate at the Gaussian order; at constant order, however,
Cov(|D1 |, |D3 |) tends to the non-zero value −1/(πn) — a relative-order-1/N effect
of the fixed sample size, proportional to the joint fourth cumulant κ2,2 , which the

leading term N α(n) and the closed-form constant correction β(n) are extracted,
the latter assembled from three structurally different pieces: a per-edge term, an
adjacent-pair term whose constant a∗ is the technical heart of the paper, and a
disjoint-pair term whose constant-order residual −1/(πn) the Gaussian limit cannot
see.
    We build this layer by layer: the exact variance structure, the constants A,
B and C1 , the per-edge and disjoint contributions, the adjacent contribution, and
finally their synthesis into β(n) and the second-order coefficient γ(n). One running
                                                                               √
theme is worth keeping in view: the constant 1/π recurs in every piece and 3/π
in the adjacent ones.
    For orientation, this section contains only the parts of the calculation that are
either exact or Gaussian. Nothing delicate about lattice corrections is needed yet.
The exact identity (3.2) tells us which three building blocks must be computed. The
central limit theorem tells us their leading order, hence the coefficient α(n). The
only constant-order information included here is the per-edge correction, because
it follows immediately from the known expansion of the mean. The two harder
constant-order corrections, disjoint and adjacent, are deferred to §4–§6.

Methodological map and proof dependencies
The paper uses several methods that enter at different levels of precision. It is
helpful to state their roles before the calculations begin.
    1. The exact variance decomposition (3.2) separates the problem into a per-
       edge term, an adjacent-pair term, and a disjoint-pair term. This is an exact
       combinatorial reduction; no asymptotics enter. The decomposition is proved
       in §3.1, using only the exchangeability and pair counts of the edge variables.
    2. The multivariate central limit theorem together with the Nabeya formula
       determines the coefficient of order N in all three parts. It does not deter-
       mine the constant-order corrections. The normal absolute-moment identity is
       Lemma 3.1, its Price-theorem derivation is recorded in §3.3, and the leading
       coefficient α(n) is assembled in Theorem 3.4.
    3. Conditioning on the number of observations in the relevant bins reduces ex-
       act finite-N moments to symmetric random walks. For disjoint pairs this
       produces the skeleton h(m) and its Bessel generating function in §4.1, The-
       orem 4.1 and Proposition 4.2. For adjacent pairs the analogous conditioning
       on L = X1 + X2 + X3 is set up in §5.1.
    4. The disjoint constant −1/(πn) is proved from the exact skeleton h(m) (§4.4,
       every n ≥ 4). An exact characteristic-function representation (Lemma 4.4


                                          9
      and Steps 1–4 of §4.4) explains it as a fourth-cumulant effect — κ2,2 times a
      lattice origin probability — and gives a second proof for n ≥ 5: the periodic
      kernel carries the non-smooth weight, an algebraic factor cancels its singular-
      ity, and a two-dimensional lattice expansion supplies the origin probability.
   5. The adjacent constant a∗ requires both a smooth bivariate Edgeworth cor-
      rection and a genuinely lattice correction. The latter is caused by the index-3
      support lattice and by the two kink lines of |d1 ||d2 |. The conditional reduc-
      tion and the statement of a∗ are in §5.1; the detailed proof is organized in
      §5–§6, with the final assembly in §6.5.
   6. Only after the three constant-order pieces have been proved separately are
      they assembled into the closed form for β(n) in §7, Theorem 7.1. One order
      further, the same decomposition gives γ(n) for n ≥ 5 in Theorem 7.6, with
      the remaining explicit evaluation isolated in the triangular-lattice constant
      a△ .
Thus the paper deliberately moves from exact algebra, through Gaussian leading
terms, to local lattice analysis. Whenever a later step invokes an earlier method,
the relevant dependency is stated explicitly.

3.2      The constants A, B, C1 and the leading variance
Here we name the three constants  √ used repeatedly in what follows. They look

4    The disjoint-pair contribution
The disjoint term is the first place where the variance sees something that the
covariance matrix of the raw differences does not show. The raw variables D1 =
X2 −X1 and D3 = X4 −X3 are uncorrelated under the uniform law, but the absolute

Gaussian order and appears only as a constant-order residue.

full multinomial model, average over M ∼ Bin(N, 4/n), and extract the asymptotic
covariance. The second step is the more delicate one: it explains why the residual
is −1/(πn), rather than zero.

4.3    Fourier dictionary for absolute values

is the usual central Edgeworth correction, computed as if the normalized law had
a smooth density near the origin. The second is a lattice correction: even after
normalization, the exact mass lies on one coset of an index-3 lattice, and the test
function |w1 ||w2 | has kinks on the coordinate axes. The smooth calculation gives
asm ; the lattice calculation gives alatt ; their sum is the desired constant.

6.3    The lattice corner correction: real-space guide
The smooth calculation of the preceding subsections deliberately ignored one piece
of information: the exact pair (D1 , D2 ) is not distributed over all integer points in
the plane. It lives on a thinner lattice. Indeed, from

          D1 − D2 = (X2 − X1 ) − (X3 − X2 ) = 3X2 − ℓ ≡ −ℓ                (mod 3),

              Λ = {d ∈ Z2 : d1 ≡ d2        (mod 3)},           covol(Λ) = v = 3.

The factor v is the density correction: one point of the coset represents an area
of size 3, so a smooth integral over the plane is approximated by v times the sum
over the occupied coset.

or Poisson-summation error is lower order. The function used here, however, is not

6.4    Uniform closure in the dual domain
It remains to justify that the formal expansion may be paired with the singular
kernel and that no hidden constant arises from the residue classes or from the com-
plement of the origin saddle; the outcome is that the dual-domain proof accounts
only for the pure singular origin piece cL ℓ+asm and the two mixed singular-smooth

Conclusion
This paper isolates the variance of the linear discrete total variation statistic into
three local mechanisms: a single-edge variance, a disjoint-pair covariance, and an
adjacent-pair covariance. The first is elementary given the mean expansion of
Part 1; the second reduces to the closed skeleton h(m); the third is the only gen-
uinely new local analysis, because adjacent edges live on a triangular sublattice and
the absolute-value test function has two kink lines. The disjoint mechanism is the
probabilistic point of the result: two edges sharing no bin are asymptotically inde-
pendent, yet their absolute values keep a covariance Cov(|D1 |, |D3 |) → −1/(πn) of
relative order 1/N — the conditioning-on-the-total effect of the fixed sample size,
proportional to the exact joint fourth cumulant κ2,2 = −4N/n2 (Proposition 4.5)
and proved here on the lattice together with its 1/N correction.
    The main closed result is the two-term fixed-n expansion
