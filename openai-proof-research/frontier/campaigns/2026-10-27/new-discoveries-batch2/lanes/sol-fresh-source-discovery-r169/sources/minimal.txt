                                                                       GROUPS

                                                                                       FABIO MASTROGIACOMO




arXiv:2411.03469v1 [math.GR] 5 Nov 2024
                                                     Abstract. Let G be a finite permutation group acting on Ω. A base for G is a subset B ⊆ Ω
                                                     such that the pointwise stabilizer G(B) is the identity. The base size of G, denoted by b(G), is
                                                     the cardinality of the smallest possible base. The minimal degree of G, denoted by µ(G), is the
                                                     smallest cardinality of the support of a non trivial element of G. In this paper, we establish a new
                                                     upper bound for b(G) when G is primitive, and subsequently prove that if G is a primitive group
                                                     different from the Mathieu group of degree 24, then µ(G)b(G) ≤ n log n, where n is the degree of
                                                     G. This bound is best possible, up to a multiplicative constant.


                                                                                           1. introduction
                                             Let G be a finite permutation group acting on a set Ω. The minimal degree of G, denoted by
                                          µ(G), is the smallest number of elements of Ω that are moved by any non-identity element of G. A
                                          base for G is a subset B ⊆ Ω such that G(B) = 1. The base size of G, denoted by b(G), is the
                                          smallest cardinality of a base. These two invariants are among the most important ones studied
                                          in permutation group theory. In particular, the base size plays a crucial role in the investigation
                                          of many properties of the group G. Many authors have consequently studied this invariant and
                                          provided upper bounds for it. On one side, the famous Pyber’s conjecture, now a theorem (see [8]
                                          and [16]), gives an upper bound in term of the size of the group and the degree. On the other side,
                                          some upper bound has been given for the base size depending only on the degree of the group. Some
                                          results have also been obtained regarding the minimal degree, as seen in [4] and [20]. However, it
                                          appears that these two ingredients have not been studied together. Considering, for example, their
                                          product, the only known result about it is the following elementary lower bound, which can be found
                                          in [15, Excercise 3.3.7]. If G is a transitive group of degree n, then
                                                                                              n ≤ b(G)µ(G).
                                          The main topic explored in this paper is the existence of a function f : N → R satisfying µ(G)b(G) ≤
                                          f (n).
                                          As an initial approach to this problem, one might consider leveraging existing results on the base
                                          size of primitive groups. For example, using the classification of finite simple groups, Liebeck [21,
                                          Theorem 1] has proved that if G is a primitive group of degree n, which is not large-base (see Sec-
                                          tion 3.3 for the definition of large-base group), then b(G) ≤ 9 log n (in this paper, all the logarithms
                                          are taken in base 2). Subsequently, this result was improved by Moscatiello and Roney-Dougal in
                                          [23, Theorem 5]; their result proves that, if G is not large-base, then b(G) ≤ 2 + log n. Using these
                                          results, one may establish inequalities of the form
                                                                                          µ(G)b(G) ≤ 9n log n,
                                          or
                                                                                       µ(G)b(G) ≤ n(2 + log n).

However, bounds of this form are far from sharp and do not apply to large-base groups. The main
result of this paper presents an improvement on these earlier inequalities and provides a uniform
bound.
Theorem 1.1. Let G be a primitive permutation group of degree n. If G is not the Mathieu group
M24 in its action of degree 24, then
                                              µ(G)b(G) ≤ n log n.
   Observe that if G = M24 is the Mathieu group of degree 24, then b(G) = 7 and µ(G) = 16, so
b(G)µ(G) = 112, while n log n ≈ 110.
Our proof of Theorem 1.1 heavily relies on bounds on the base size of primitive groups, since more
information is known about it. In particular, we found it necessary to derive new bounds for the
base size of certain primitive groups. The bound we have established to prove Theorem 1.1, which
is of independent interest, improves upon the result of Moscatiello and Roney-Dougal and is stated
in the following theorem.
Theorem 1.2. Let G be a finite primitive group of degree n, and suppose that G is not large-base.
Suppose moreover that G is neither an almost simple group with socle G0 acting on the set Ω, with
(G0 , Ω) as in Table 1, nor a subgroup of AGLd (q), with q = 2, 3. Then,

   The bound in Theorem 1.2 is asymptotically best possible. For instance, consider the affine group
G = AGLd (4). The base size of this group is d+1a, while n = 4d , and d+1 ∼ (log n)/2+6. Although
the constant 6 could potentially be reduced in certain cases, it is sufficient for our purposes to keep
it as 6.
Regarding the sharpness of the bound in Theorem 1.1, this is asymptotically optimal up to a mul-
tiplicative constant. Indeed, there are infinitely many primitive groups G of degree n such that
µ(G)b(G) ≥ (n log n)/2. For example, take G = AGLd (2) in its natural action of degree 2n .
Here, we have µ(G) = n/2 (see for example [15, Excercise 3.3.3]) and b(G) = 1 + log n, so that
µ(G)b(G) > (n log n)/2.

    The proof of Theorem 1.2 is carried out through a case by case analysis using the O’Nan-Scott
Theorem.
For what regards Theorem 1.1, if G is a primitive group of degree n that is not among the exceptions
listed in Theorem 1.2, the inequality µ(G)b(G) ≤ n log n will easily follow from Theorem 1.2. In
cases where G is one of the exceptions listed in Theorem 1.2, the result is established by examining
the exceptions listed in Table 1.

   The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we prove Theorem 1.2. In particular, in Subsec-
tion 2.1, we prove Theorem 1.2 for almost simple groups. In Subsection 2.2 we prove Theorem 1.2
for diagonal groups. In Subsection 2.3 we prove Theorem 1.2 for affine groups. In Subsection 2.4 we
prove Theorem 1.2 for product type groups.
Section 3 is devoted to the proof of Theorem 1.1. In particular, in Subsection 3.1 we prove The-
orem 1.1 for affine groups with underlying field with 2 or 3 elements. In Subsection 3.2 we prove
Theorem 1.1 for almost simple groups having socle G0 acting on Ω, with (G0 , Ω) as in Table 1. In
Subsection 3.3 we prove Theorem 1.1 for large-base groups.

                                   2. Proof of Theorem 1.2
  In this section, we prove Theorem 1.2. We divide the proof according to the O’Nan-Scott theorem.

2.1. Almost simple groups. Let G be an almost simple group with socle G0 in a primitive action
of degree n. From now on, we will use the following notations for actions of classical groups.

Notation. Let G be a classical group, with natural module V . We write Pk to refer to the set of all
k-dimensional subspaces of V . Moreover, we write Sk for a G-orbit of totally singular subspaces of V
of dimension k, and Nk for a G-orbit of non-degenerate or non-singular subspaces of V of dimension
k. For the orthogonal groups of even dimension, let W be a space in the orbit if dk is even, and the
orthogonal complement of such a space if dk is odd. Then we write Nkǫ , with ǫ ∈ {+, −} to indicate
that the restriction of the form to W is of type ǫ.
We let e1 , . . . , ed be the canonical basis of V = Fdq .

  Before embarking on the proof of Theorem 1.2, we first recall the definition of subspace subgroup.
Definition 2.1. Let G be an almost simple classical group over Fq , where q = pf and p is prime,
with socle G0 and associated natural module V . A subgroup H of G not containing G0 is a subspace
subgroup if, for each maximal subgroup M of G0 containing H ∩ G0 , one of the following holds:
   (2.1) M is the stabilizer in G0 of a proper nonzero subspace U of V , where U is totally singular,
         non-degenerate, or, if G0 is orthogonal and p = 2, a nonsingular 1-space (U can be any
         subspace if G0 = PSL(V )).
   (2.2) G0 = Sp′d (q), p = 2, and M = GO±  d (q).

  A subspace action of the classical group G is the action of G on the coset space [G : H], where
H is a subspace subgroup of G.
Definition 2.2. A transitive action of G on a set Ω is said to be standard if, up to equivalence of
actions, one of the following holds:
   (2.1) G0 = Alt(m) and Ω is an orbit of subsets or uniform partitions of {1, . . . , m}.
   (2.2) G is a classical group in a subspace action.
   For an almost simple primitive permutation group in a non-standard action, the base size is
bounded by an absolute constant. This was conjectured by Cameron and Kantor (see [11, 13]) and
then settled in the affirmative by Liebeck and Shalev in [24, Theorem 1.3]. The constant was then
made explicit in subsequent work [5, 6, 7, 9, 10]. The following theorem summarizes these results.
