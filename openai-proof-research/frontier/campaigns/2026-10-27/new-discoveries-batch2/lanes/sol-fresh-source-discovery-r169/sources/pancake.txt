                                                     Abstract: The cubic pancake graphs are Cayley graphs over the symmetric
                                                     group Sn generated by three prefix reversals. There is the folllowing open
                                                     problem: characterize all the sets of three prefix reversals that generate Sn .
                                                     Blanco and Golubyatnikov give a complete solution of the problem when any
                                                     of the two smallest or the two largest lengths but n are included in a triple
                                                     of prefix reversals. According to their conjectures, we give some conditions
                                                     implying a triple of prefix reversals does not generate Sn . Moreover, Using the
                                                     symmetric groups that can already be generated by three prefix reversals, we
                                                     can derive some recurrence conditions.

                                           1         Introduction
                                             A classical and celebrated result [1] states that two random permutations of a set
                                           of n elements almost surly generate either the symmetric or the alternating group of
                                           degree n, and similar results hold when more generators are considered. In particular,
                                           the symmetric group can always be generated by the transposition (1 2) together with
                                           the n-cycle (1 2 · · · n). Two well-known generating sets of n − 1 transpositions are the
                                           Coxeter generators {(i (i + 1) | 1 ≤ i < n} and the star generators {(1 i) | 1 < i ≤ n} .
                                             Prefix reversals are a classical family of involutions arising in permutation sorting and
                                           the study of Cayley graphs. Let Sn denote the symmetric group on {1, . . . , n}. For each
                                           2 ≤ i ≤ n, define the prefix reversal ri ∈ Sn by

Thus, ri reverses the first i positions and fixes all remaining positions. The problem of
sorting a permutation using as few prefix reversals as possible has attracted considerable
attention. Gates and Papadimitriou [2] established bounds on the maximum number
of reversals required, and Chitturi et al. [3] subsequently improved the upper bound.
Bulteau, Fertin, and Rusu [4] proved that finding an optimal sorting sequence is NP-
hard. These results concern the length of a sorting sequence when all prefix reversal
lengths are available.
  Restricting the allowed reversal lengths leads to a related generation problem: which
subsets of prefix reversals suffice to generate every permutation? This question has a
natural interpretation in terms of Cayley graphs. The pancake graph is the Cayley graph

                                 Pn = Cay(Sn , {r2 , . . . , rn }),

and retaining only the edges associated with a subset of reversals gives a spanning sub-
graph that is connected precisely when those reversals generate Sn . Bass and Sudborough
[5] studied restricted prefix reversals and the corresponding fixed-degree Cayley networks,
providing an important connection between permutation generation and network struc-
ture.
   The case of three distinct prefix reversals is of particular interest, since a generating
triple determines a connected cubic spanning subgraph of the pancake graph. Related
questions also arise in permutation enumeration. Sawada and Williams [6] studied suc-
cessor rules for generating permutations by prefix reversals and considered the restriction
to the three lengths n − 2, n − 1, and n. Konstantinova and Son [7] investigated the
girths of several families of cubic pancake graphs. These studies motivate a systematic
understanding of which triples of prefix reversals generate the full symmetric group.
   Since every prefix reversal of length less than n fixes the position n, any generating
triple must contain rn . The problem therefore reduces to determining when

                       Gn := ⟨rn , rm , rk ⟩ = Sn ,         2 ≤ k < m < n.

Blanco and Golubyatnikov [8] obtained complete classifications for triples containing at
least one of r2 , r3 , rn−1 , and rn−2 , together with further necessary conditions for genera-
tion.
   Their computational investigations also suggested relations between generating triples
in different degrees. In particular, they conjectured that generation is preserved when
all three reversal lengths are increased by four, and that a corresponding shift by two
preserves generation when n ≡ 0, 1 (mod 4). They further proposed a non-generation

condition describing a triangular region in the parameter space. These conjectures con-
cern structural features extending beyond the individual families covered by the existing
classification results.
  The present paper establishes the two shift properties and the parity-dependent non-
generation condition. Thus, our results explain how generating triples give rise to further
generating triples in larger degrees and provide restrictions on the parameters for which
generation is possible. The precise statements are given in the following theorems.


2      Preliminaries
  Let Ω = {1, 2, . . . , n}, and denote by Altn the alternating groups on Ω. We use cycle
notation for permutations. In cycle notation, (a1 a2 · · · aℓ ) maps ai to ai+1 for 1 ≤ i < ℓ,
maps aℓ to a1 , and fixes all remaining elements of Ω. Products of permutations are
evaluated from right to left; thus

                        (στ )(i) = σ(τ (i))        (σ, τ ∈ Symn , i ∈ Ω).

Throughout the proofs, we use suitable products of prefix reversals to construct permu-
tations with prescribed cycle structures.
  We next recall some terminology concerning permutation groups. Let G be a finite
group acting on Ω. The orbit of x ∈ Ω under G is

                                    Gx = {g(x) : g ∈ G}.

A subset of Ω is G-invariant if and only if it is a union of G-orbits. The action is transitive
if, for every x, y ∈ Ω, there exists g ∈ G such that g(x) = y.
    A nonempty subset B ⊆ Ω is a block for the action of G if, for every g ∈ G, either

                            g(B) = B          or       g(B) ∩ B = ∅,

where g(B) = {g(b) : b ∈ B}. If the action is transitive, the distinct translates of B form
a G-invariant partition of Ω, called a block system. The singleton subsets and Ω itself are
the trivial blocks. A transitive action is primitive if it admits no nontrivial blocks, and
imprimitive otherwise. We write H < G to indicate that H is a proper subgroup of G.
    For ease of reference in the subsequent proofs, we introduce the following notation:

         a = n − m,    b = n − k,    N ∈ {n + 2, n + 4} ,       s = a + c,   t = N − s.

Lemma 2.1 ([5, 2.2]) Necessary conditions for a set of flips to generate Sn .
  In order for a set of flips to be able to generate all permutations, all of the following
conditions must be satisfied: (these are not sufficient conditions)

                                                   3
 (1) One of the flips must be of size n.

 (2) One of the flips must be of size i, where n2 < i < n.Otherwise, there is no way to
                                               

     move an element from the first half to the second half.

 (3) One of the flips must be of size l, where l is even. Otherwise, no element can be
     moved from an odd-numbered position to an even-numbered position.

 (4) One of the flips must be an odd permutation. Otherwise, one can only get the set of
     all even permutations.

 (5) The set of flips must include three flips with relatively prime sizes. Otherwise, if
     l > 1 is the greatest common divisor of the flips sizes, then some of the blocks of l
     contiguous elements can never be separated from each other.

    Q. Since U (N ) = N , we have

                                        U (B ′ ) ∩ B ′ ̸= ∅,

    and the block property yields U (B ′ ) = B ′ . The transitivity of U on each of P and
    Q now implies
                                   P ∪ Q ∪ {N } ⊆ B ′ .
    Hence B ′ = Ω, and therefore B = Ω.
    Next, suppose that P ⊆ B. Since a ≥ 3,

                            s − 1 = a + b − 1 > b + 1 = N − a,

    so
                           Ta (s − 1) = N + 1 − (s − 1) = 3 ∈ P.
    Consequently, Ta (B) ∩ B ̸= ∅, and hence Ta (B) = B. In particular,

                                    Ta (1) = a + 1 ∈ Q ∩ B.

    Furthermore, U (P ) = P ⊆ B, so U (B) ∩ B ̸= ∅, giving U (B) = B. Since B meets
    Q and U acts transitively on Q, we obtain Q ⊆ B. The preceding argument then
    gives B = Ω, proving the claim.
    We now establish primitivity. Let B be an GN -invariant partition of Ω with at least
    two parts, and let B ∈ B contain N . Since U fixes N , the block B is U -invariant.
