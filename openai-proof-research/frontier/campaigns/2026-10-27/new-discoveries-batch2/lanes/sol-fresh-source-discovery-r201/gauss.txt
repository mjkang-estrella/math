                                                                              Abstract
                                                   Nakamura [J. Comput. Appl. Math. 131 (2001)] proved that
                                               the arithmetic–harmonic matrix iteration converges quadratically to
                                               the geometric matrix mean, the Riemannian midpoint of the affine-
                                               invariant metric on positive-definite matrices. We consider the more
                                               general problem of when a Riemannian midpoint is a Gauss compound
                                               mean. A Riemannian metric and an affine connection determine three
                                               midpoint maps associated with the connection, its metric dual, and
                                               the Levi–Civita connection. We show that the Gauss composition of
                                               the first two equals the Levi–Civita midpoint if and only if the latter is
                                               invariant under one step of the iteration. We give a sufficient condition
                                               for this invariance in terms of an isometry which acts as the point re-
                                               flection about the Levi–Civita midpoint and exchanges the connection
                                               with its dual. Under this invariance, the iterations converge quadrati-
                                               cally for sufficiently close initial pairs.
                                                   Nakamura’s iteration is an example of this criterion. The criterion
                                               also applies to dual pairs of matrix power means whose Gauss com-
                                               position is the geometric matrix mean. We give a dually flat Hessian
                                               example which shows that duality alone does not suffice. We also give
                                               non-flat examples with a Euclidean metric and a parallel cubic form
                                               in every dimension larger than one. In dimension one, we characterize
                                               the invariance completely by using the Matkowski–Sutô equation. For
                                               Euclidean dual pairs, we conjecture that the invariance implies that
                                               the cubic form is constant. We prove this conjecture in dimension one
                                               and for scalar multiples of a constant cubic form.

1.1   Gauss’s compound means
Gauss’s arithmetic–geometric mean (AGM) is the classical example of a com-
pound mean. Denote the arithmetic and geometric means by A(p, q) =
                       √
(p + q)/2 and G(p, q) = pq for p, q > 0, respectively. The AGM of p and q
is defined as
                      AGM(p, q) = lim an = lim gn ,
                                     n→∞       n→∞

where an = A(an−1 , gn−1 ) and gn = G(an−1 , gn−1 ) with a0 = p and g0 = q.
Given two means A and M , set a0 = p and m0 = q and define

         an = A(an−1 , mn−1 ),      mn = M (an−1 , mn−1 ) (n ≥ 1).

If both sequences converge to a common limit, that limit is called their Gauss
composition [4, 19] and is denoted by (A ⊗ M )(p, q).
    The Gauss composition need not belong to the same class as its com-
ponent means. For example, the arithmetic and geometric means are ho-
mogeneous quasi-arithmetic means, but the AGM is not a quasi-arithmetic
mean. Indeed, only power means are homogeneous quasi-arithmetic means
[5, Theorem 84], and the AGM is not a power mean.

    We give a sufficient condition for the invariance identity in terms of point
symmetry (Theorem 4.1). For each fixed admissible pair, suppose that there
is an isometry on a suitable invariant domain which acts as the point reflec-
tion at Mg (P, Q) and exchanges ∇ and ∇∗ . If this isometry has no other
fixed point in the domain, then the invariance identity (1.1) holds. This
criterion does not require flatness, affine coordinates, or explicit formulas for
the midpoint maps. It gives a proof by symmetry of the one-step midpoint
invariance in Nakamura’s iteration. It also gives the same invariance for the
dual pairs in the power-mean family ∇(α) of α-connections on SPD(m). The
corresponding mean-type iterations converge quadratically to the geometric
matrix mean from every initial pair (Proposition 5.1).
    However, duality alone does not suffice. We give an explicit dually flat
counterexample using a Hessian metric on SPD(2), for which A ⊗ M ̸=
Mg (Section 6). We also consider a Euclidean metric with a parallel cubic
form. This construction gives non-flat examples in every dimension d ≥
2 for which the invariance identity holds locally for sufficiently close pairs
(Section 7). Thus dual flatness is neither sufficient nor necessary for local
midpoint invariance.
    In dimension one, the invariance condition is completely characterized
(Section 8). After an affine change of the primal coordinate and a con-
stant rescaling of the metric, the structure is either trivial or of scalar
arithmetic–harmonic–geometric type. Restriction to suitable common au-
toparallel curves then yields necessary conditions in higher dimensions.
    Finally, we consider Euclidean structures determined by a smooth to-
tally symmetric cubic form C. We conjecture that local midpoint invariance
implies that C is constant. Thus, according to the conjecture, the parallel-
cubic examples give all Euclidean dual solutions of the invariance identity
(Section 9). By a fourth-order expansion of the geodesic midpoint maps at
the diagonal, we obtain a pointwise polynomial necessary condition. Using
this condition, we prove the conjecture in dimension one and for cubic forms
that are scalar multiples of a constant one. We also give an example which
shows that the duality between the two connections cannot be dropped.

2    The dualistic geometric setting
Let (P, g) be a Riemannian manifold and let ∇ be an affine connection on
P. The g-dual, or conjugate [22], connection ∇∗ of ∇ with respect to g is
defined by
                  X[g(Y, Z)] = g(∇X Y, Z) + g(Y, ∇∗X Z),            (2.1)
for any vector fields X, Y , and Z. Since the metric g is non-degenerate, this
identity uniquely determines the dual connection ∇∗ . It also gives (∇∗ )∗ =
∇. We write ∇g for the Levi–Civita connection of g.
    Assume that every relevant pair of points is joined by a unique geodesic
for each connection under consideration. This assumption is a restriction
even for the Levi–Civita connection. The injectivity radius guarantees
uniqueness of the minimizing geodesic, but does not exclude longer geodesic
segments with the same endpoints. For the Stiefel manifold with the canon-
ical and Euclidean metrics, the shortest geodesic loops and the injectivity
radius are determined in [27]. On Hadamard manifolds (Example 2.3(1)
below) uniqueness holds globally. In local constructions, uniqueness is un-
derstood among geodesic segments contained in the specified convex normal
neighborhood, as in Sections 3 and 7. For the Levi–Civita connection on a
strongly convex neighborhood, we use the unique minimizing segment.

Definition 2.1 (Midpoint mean). Let D be an affine connection on P,
and let P and Q be joined by a unique affinely parametrized D-geodesic
γ : [0, 1] → P satisfying γ(0) = P and γ(1) = Q, with uniqueness understood
in the ambient manifold or in the specified convex normal neighborhood, as
above. The midpoint mean of P and Q with respect to D is MD (P, Q) :=
γ(1/2). We denote the midpoint means of the three connections by

 A(P, Q) := M∇ (P, Q), M (P, Q) := M∇∗ (P, Q), Mg (P, Q) := M∇g (P, Q).

    The following two properties of MD follow from Definition 2.1 and the
uniqueness assumption. First, the reversed curve t 7→ γ(1 − t) is an affinely
parametrized D-geodesic which joins Q to P and has midpoint γ(1/2). By
uniqueness, it is the geodesic defining MD (Q, P ). Thus MD is symmet-
ric, that is, MD (P, Q) = MD (Q, P ). Second, the constant curve at P is
a D-geodesic joining P to itself. By uniqueness it is the geodesic defining


                                      5
MD (P, P ), so MD is idempotent, that is, MD (P, P ) = P . Both properties
enter the proof of Theorem 3.2.

Remark 2.2 (Affine parameter versus arc length). In Definition 2.1, the
midpoint is taken with respect to the affine parameter of D, not with respect
to g-arc length. For D = ∇g the affine parameter is proportional to arc
length, so Mg (P, Q) is the equidistant point on the Riemannian geodesic
joining P and Q. For ∇ and ∇∗ , however, the g-speed of a geodesic need
not be constant, and the affine-parameter midpoint generally differs from
the arc-length midpoint. This distinction is needed even in dimension one.
The geodesics of ∇, ∇∗ , and ∇g joining two points have the same image,
but their parametrizations may differ. These parametrizations account for
the differences between A, M , and Mg on the scalar rays of Sections 5 and 6
and in the one-dimensional classification of Section 8.
Example 2.3. (1) On a Hadamard manifold, Mg (P, Q) is globally defined
and coincides with the unique equal-weight Karcher mean of P and Q; see
[14].
(2) If ∇ = ∇E is the Euclidean flat connection on a convex matrix domain,
then A(P, Q) = (P + Q)/2.
    The mean A is determined by ∇. The dual connection ∇∗ and its mean
M are determined by the pair (g, ∇), and the Riemannian midpoint Mg is
determined by g. Thus, once g and ∇ are fixed, the mean M cannot be
chosen independently as the midpoint mean of the g-dual connection. Affine
changes of dual coordinates and the usual affine gauge of a Hessian potential
do not change the induced midpoint map.

3    The Gauss composition
Starting from P0 = P and Q0 = Q, consider the mean-type iteration [19, 18]

                  Pn+1 = A(Pn , Qn ),       Qn+1 = M (Pn , Qn ).

If both sequences converge to the same point, we define their Gauss compo-
sition by
                   (A ⊗ M )(P, Q) := lim Pn = lim Qn .
                                        n→∞          n→∞

Theorem 3.1 (Invariance criterion for the Gauss composition to match the
Levi–Civita midpoint). Let D ⊂ P × P be a domain on which the mid-
point means A, M , and Mg are all defined, that is, each pair (P, Q) ∈
D is joined by a unique geodesic of each of the three connections (Defini-
tion 2.1). Set T (P, Q) := (A(P, Q), M (P, Q)), and assume that T (D) ⊆ D.
For n = 0, 1, . . ., let T n denote the n-fold iterate of T , with T 0 = idD , so
that T n (P, Q) = (Pn , Qn ). Suppose that, for every (P, Q) ∈ D, there exists
a point L(P, Q) ∈ P such that

                T n (P, Q) −→ (L(P, Q), L(P, Q)) ,       n → ∞,

so that the Gauss composition (A ⊗ M )(P, Q) = L(P, Q) is defined for ev-
ery (P, Q) ∈ D, and suppose that Mg is also defined at each limit pair
(L(P, Q), L(P, Q)) and is continuous there. Then A ⊗ M = Mg on D if
and only if the invariance identity (1.1) holds on D, that is,

             Mg (A(P, Q), M (P, Q)) = Mg (P, Q),        (P, Q) ∈ D.

Proof. Fix (P, Q) ∈ D and write L = L(P, Q). Since T (P, Q) ∈ D and
T n (T (P, Q)) = T n+1 (P, Q) → (L, L), we have L(T (P, Q)) = L(P, Q). In-
deed, removing the first iterate does not change the limit. Thus A ⊗ M is
invariant under one application of the mean-type mapping T . If A⊗M = Mg
on D, this is the identity (1.1) at (P, Q).


                                        7
   Conversely, if (1.1) holds on D, then Mg (Pn , Qn ) = Mg (P, Q) for all n,
and the continuity of Mg at (L, L) together with the idempotence Mg (L, L) =
L gives

           Mg (P, Q) = Mg (Pn , Qn ) −→ Mg (L, L) = L,      n → ∞.

Hence (A ⊗ M )(P, Q) = L = Mg (P, Q).
    Duality of connections alone does not imply the invariance identity (1.1).
Consequently, even though A, M , and Mg arise from the same dualistic
structure, it is possible that A ⊗ M ̸= Mg .
    Theorem 3.1 assumes convergence of the mean-type iteration. This as-
sumption holds near the diagonal. For any two sufficiently regular symmetric
idempotent maps, the iteration converges quadratically for sufficiently close
pairs. In particular, this applies to the local midpoint maps of any two
affine connections. The existence result does not require a metric, duality,
or the invariance identity (1.1). These are used only to identify the limit in
Theorem 3.3 below.

is called symmetric if N (X, Y ) = N (Y, X) and idempotent if N (X, X) = X

symmetric and idempotent. Given such maps A and M , we write T (X, Y ) :=
(A(X, Y ), M (X, Y )) and (Pn+1 , Qn+1 ) := T (Pn , Qn ), as in Theorem 3.1.

A     Remarks on dualistic structures
In this appendix, we give further explanations of the dualistic structure in
Section 2 and compare it with related results in the literature. These remarks
are not needed for the main arguments.

Remark A.1 (Duality versus metric compatibility). Duality with respect
to g and metric compatibility of either member of the dual pair are different
notions. Indeed, the defining identity (2.1) gives

    (∇X g)(Y, Z) = g (Y, (∇∗X − ∇X )Z) ,   (∇∗X g)(Y, Z) = −(∇X g)(Y, Z).
                                                                       (A.1)
These identities are standard in the theory of conjugate connections; see
[1, 22]. Since g is nondegenerate, (A.1) implies the equivalence

                 ∇g = 0      ⇐⇒      ∇ = ∇∗      ⇐⇒      ∇∗ g = 0.             (A.2)

                                         42
    Thus, the statement that the dual of a metric connection is again metric
is correct only with respect to the same metric g used in the duality relation
(2.1) (see balanced metrics [30]). In that case, the two connections in fact
coincide. Metricity with respect to some other metric does not imply (A.2).
Remark A.2 (The arithmetic mean of a dual pair). Even when neither
connection is g-metric, their arithmetic mean ∇ := (∇ + ∇∗ )/2 is always
g-metric. If both ∇ and ∇∗ are torsion-free, then ∇ is also torsion-free.
Therefore, the fundamental theorem of Riemannian geometry yields ∇ = ∇g
and hence ∇∗ = 2∇g − ∇, which is (2.3). In particular, if either member of
a torsion-free dual pair is g-metric, then

                       ∇ = ∇∗ = ∇g ,        A = M = Mg .

    If torsion is allowed but either member of the dual pair is still g-metric,
(A.2) gives ∇ = ∇∗ and hence A = M , but this common metric connection
need not be the Levi–Civita connection. Consequently, A = Mg does not
follow in general.
Remark A.3 (Torsion, Codazzi coupling, and statistical manifolds). The
duality relation (2.1) also relates the antisymmetric part of ∇g to the differ-
ence of the torsion tensors. If

denotes the torsion of an affine connection D, then

                        (∇X g)(Y, Z) = (∇Y g)(X, Z)                       (A.4)

Remark A.4 (Curvature-freeness and affine coordinates). We explain the

coordinates. If ∇ is torsion-free and R∇ is nonzero at a point, then A
is still a geodesic midpoint mean, but it admits no such quasi-arithmetic
representation on a neighborhood of that point. The torsion-free hypothesis
is essential here: the connection
