The Isotropic Curvature Model (ICM) provides a curvature-based explanation for this update by asking when gradient orthogonalization minimizes a one-step local-loss surrogate. We investigate the stability of this explanation when the fixed input radius of the ICM is replaced by a general radial law. We introduce the Random-Radius Isotropic Curvature Model (RR-ICM) and the Polar-Factor Admissibility Set, which encodes exact scaled-polar optimality as a geometric condition on the positive polar factor of the gradient. We derive an exact characterization of this set for arbitrary radial laws and show that all nonscalar admissible responses arise from radial atoms aligned with curvature kinks. Consequently, every atomless radial law exhibits zero-capacity rigidity: exact scaled-polar optimality is possible only when the gradient is already a scaled column isometry. To connect the curvature-based framework with the established spectral geometry of Muon, we introduce the Minimax RR-ICM by replacing directional averaging with worst-case optimization over probability laws on the sphere. We prove that every nonzero global minimizer lies on the polar ray of the gradient, while its scale is determined by a one-dimensional convex problem. The corresponding admissibility geometry is a positive-semidefinite trace slab, in sharp contrast to the spherical-zonoid geometry of the RR-ICM. Together, these results expose the radial fragility of average-curvature explanations and provide a new minimax variational characterization of Muon's polar direction. △ Less

Its accuracy is limited by "staircasing": the dielectric contrast of a faceted particle is integrated across boundaries that cut the quadrature grid, so standard quadrature converges at low algebraic order. We show that this non-smoothness has a single geometric origin, the tangencies of the integration sphere to the faces and edges of the particle, which produce jumps, kinks, and half-integer branches according to the tangency type, in all three integration directions. A boundary-conformal scheme removes them using closed-form azimuthal coefficients, panel splitting at the analytically known tangency loci, and a square-root substitution $x \mapsto x_c + t^2$ that absorbs the half-integer branches. For a hexagonal prism the azimuthal integration becomes exact and the zenithal and radial directions recover spectral and fourth-order convergence; because the construction depends only on the contact geometry, it extends to any convex polyhedron, demonstrated on the solid hexagonal bullet (a faceted ice habit with tilted faces). The zenithal crossing is a square-root branch rather than a kink, so the established interval-splitting alone gives only $\mathcal{O}(N^{-3})$, while the radial step removes the half-integer edge branch that caps the Riccati recurrence on faceted particles. The convergence orders are fixed by the local contact geometry and verified size-independent up to $k\,r_{\max} = 20$; what grows with size is the resolution needed to reach each asymptotic regime, not the order. △ Less

In this paper, we prove relations among three convex relaxations: a semidefinite relaxation that computes a full matrix, a chordal relaxation based on a chordal extension of the network graph, and a second-order cone relaxation that computes the smallest partial matrix. We prove a bijection between the feasible sets of the OPF in the bus injection model and the branch flow model, establishing the equivalence of these two models and their second-order cone relaxations. Our results imply that, for radial networks, all these relaxations are equivalent and one should always solve the second-order cone relaxation. For mesh networks, the semidefinite relaxation is tighter than the second-order cone relaxation but requires a heavier computational effort, and the chordal relaxation strikes a good balance.
