The Discrete Monotonicity Property in Rank Two



Fei Xue

Address: School of Mathematical Sciences, Nanjing Normal University, Nanjing 210046, China

Email address: 05429@njnu.edu.cn


Date: October 28, 2026


Abstract.
    

The Betke–Henk–Wills conjecture predicts a bound for lattice-point counts in terms of successive minima. Malikiosis introduced the discrete monotonicity property for normalized residue counts as a sufficient condition for this bound and its extension to nonsymmetric convex bodies. We prove that every compact convex set satisfies this property for lattice subgroups of rank at most two, in arbitrary ambient dimension.
  



Key words and phrases: discrete monotonicity, lattice-convex sets, residue classes, finite tori, successive minima


2020 Mathematics Subject ClassificationPrimary 11H06, 52C05; Secondary 52C07, 05A20




1. Introduction




For an  o -symmetric convex body  C=-C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}  and a full-rank lattice  \Lambda , the  j th successive minimum  \lambda_{j}(C,\Lambda)  is the least  \lambda>0  for which  \lambda C  contains  j  linearly independent vectors of  \Lambda . For an arbitrary convex body  K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d} , Malikiosis’s extension of the Betke–Henk–Wills conjecture asserts that




(1.1)

 \#(K\cap\Lambda)\leq\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{2}{\lambda_{j}\bigl((K-K)/2,\Lambda\bigr)}\right\rfloor+1\right). 



For  K=-K , this is the original conjecture of Betke, Henk, and Wills [5, Conjecture 2.1], who proved it in dimension two [5, Theorem 2.2]. Malikiosis proved (1.1) in dimensions at most three [6, Corollary 4.1.1]. Tointon [7] obtained improved bounds in general dimension.




Malikiosis introduced a discrete monotonicity property that compares normalized residue counts at different moduli. His criterion for (1.1) uses only subgroups of rank below the ambient dimension [6, proof of Theorem 4.2.1], so our rank-two result recovers the known three-dimensional bound. For a compact convex set  K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d} , a lattice  \Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d} , and a subgroup  \Gamma\leq\Lambda , write




(1.2)

 D_{\Lambda}(K,r\Gamma):=\#\{x+r\Gamma:x\in K\cap\Lambda\}\qquad(r\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}). 



Thus  D_{\Lambda}(K,r\Gamma)  counts the cosets of  r\Gamma  containing a lattice point of  K . The set  K  has the discrete monotonicity property with respect to  \Lambda  if




(1.3)

 r\longmapsto\frac{D_{\Lambda}(K,r\Gamma)}{r^{\rank\Gamma}} 



is nonincreasing on the positive integers for every subgroup  \Gamma\leq\Lambda  [6, Definition 4.2.1]. This is a discrete counterpart of the quotient-volume monotonicity in classical proofs of Minkowski’s second theorem [2].



Theorem 1.1 (Rank-two discrete monotonicity).



Let  K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}  be compact and convex, let  \Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}  be a lattice, and let  \Gamma\leq\Lambda  have rank  i\leq 2 . Then




(1.4)

 \frac{D_{\Lambda}(K,r\Gamma)}{r^{i}}\geq\frac{D_{\Lambda}(K,s\Gamma)}{s^{i}} 



for all positive integers  r\leq s .





By the reductions and initial cases in Section 2, it suffices to compare moduli  r  and  r+1  for planar lattice-convex sets. We first choose a lattice-convex subset containing exactly one point from each represented class at the larger modulus (Lemma 3.1). A suitable lattice basis then places the points of each class at the smaller modulus among four corners of a lattice parallelogram (Corollary 3.3). Unless all classes at the smaller modulus are represented, we select lattice segments whose number equals the difference between the residue counts at moduli  r+1  and  r . Their residue images are distinct lines in at most three directions (Section 4). By Proposition 5.1, these lines are contained in a cover of the finite torus with  2r+1=(r+1)^{2}-r^{2}  lines. Lemma 5.2 bounds the number of points covered by the selected lines, proving the comparison from  r  to  r+1 .





2. Preliminaries




Throughout, a lattice has full rank in its real span. For a finite subset  A  of a lattice  L , put




(2.1)

 N_{r}(A;L):=\#\{a+rL:a\in A\}\qquad(r\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}). 



This is the number of distinct cosets of  rL  meeting  A . We omit  L  when the ambient lattice is fixed. We call  A  lattice-convex if  A=\conv(A)\cap L . If  A=K\cap L  is nonempty and  P=\conv(A) , then  P\subseteq K  and  P\cap L=A . Thus residue counts for compact convex sets reduce to those for finite lattice-convex sets. If  A=\varnothing , all residue counts are zero.




A nonzero vector of  L  is primitive if it is not an integer multiple  kv  with  v\in L  and  k\geq 2 .



Lemma 2.1 (Affine-coset reduction).



Let  K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}  be compact and convex, let  \Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}  be a lattice, and let  \Gamma\leq\Lambda . For each coset  \xi\in\Lambda/\Gamma , choose  z_{\xi}\in\xi  and put





 A_{\xi}:=(K\cap\xi)-z_{\xi}\subseteq\Gamma. 



Every nonempty  A_{\xi}  is lattice-convex in  \Gamma , only finitely many are nonempty, and




(2.2)

 D_{\Lambda}(K,r\Gamma)=\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda/\Gamma}N_{r}(A_{\xi};\Gamma)\qquad(r\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}). 






Proof.



Congruence modulo  r\Gamma  never identifies points in different  \Gamma -cosets, which gives (2.2). Moreover,  \conv(A_{\xi})+z_{\xi}\subseteq K , so  \Gamma\cap\conv(A_{\xi})=A_{\xi} . Changing  z_{\xi}  translates  A_{\xi}  by an element of  \Gamma  and preserves its residue counts. Finiteness follows from the compactness of  K  and the discreteness of  \Lambda .
∎





It suffices to compare consecutive moduli. For a nonempty set in rank  i ,  N_{1}(A)=1  and  N_{2}(A)\leq 2^{i} , so  1\to 2  is immediate.



Lemma 2.2 (Line formula).



Let  L  have rank  i\geq 1 , and let  A=\conv(A)\cap L  be a nonempty finite set of affine dimension at most one. If  n=|A| , then





 N_{q}(A;L)=\min(n,q)\qquad(q\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}). 



Consequently,  N_{q}(A;L)/q^{i}  is nonincreasing in  q .




Proof.



The formula is immediate for a singleton. Otherwise, translate one point of  A  to the origin and put  H=\lin_{\mathbb{R}}(A-A)  and  L_{H}=L\cap H . The set  A  consists of  n  consecutive points of the rank-one lattice  L_{H} . Since





 qL\cap H=q(L\cap H)=qL_{H}, 



congruence on  A  modulo  qL  is the same as congruence modulo  qL_{H} . This proves the formula. Finally,





 \frac{\min(n,q)}{q^{i}}=q^{1-i}\min\{n/q,1\} 



is a product of two nonnegative nonincreasing functions.
∎





Together with Lemma 2.1, this proves the rank-one case of Theorem 1.1 and handles planar sets of affine dimension at most one. For rank zero, the residue count is independent of the modulus.




We next prove the comparison from modulus  2  to modulus  3 . The argument turns a missing parity class into three missing classes modulo  3L .



Proposition 2.3 (Full-lattice base step).



Let  A=\conv(A)\cap L  be a finite lattice-convex set in a rank-two lattice. Then





 \frac{N_{2}(A)}{4}\geq\frac{N_{3}(A)}{9}. 






Proof.



The empty case is immediate, and Lemma 2.2 handles sets of affine dimension at most one. Suppose that  P=\conv(A)  is two-dimensional. A triangle of least positive area with vertices in  A  has no other lattice points. By Pick’s theorem, its two edge vectors at a vertex form a basis of  L , so  N_{2}(A)\geq 3 . The case  N_{2}(A)=4  is immediate. It remains to show that




(2.3)

 N_{2}(A)=3\quad\Longrightarrow\quad N_{3}(A)\leq 6. 






Choose a missing class  u+2L  and put  Q=(P-u)/2 . Since  Q  is compact and disjoint from  L , slightly enlarge it without meeting  L  and extend the enlarged set to a maximal lattice-free convex set  M . Thus  Q\subset\operatorname{int}M  and  \operatorname{int}M\cap L=\varnothing . By [4, Theorem 2.2 and Corollary 2.2],  M  is a strip with lattice boundary lines or a bounded polygon with a lattice point in the relative interior of every edge.




Choose  z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}\in\partial M\cap L  distinct modulo  3L  as follows. For a strip, take three consecutive lattice points on one boundary line. For a polygon, take lattice points in the relative interiors of three different edges. In the polygon case, a congruence  z_{i}\equiv z_{j}\pmod{3L}  with  i\neq j  would give





 \frac{2z_{i}+z_{j}}{3}\in\operatorname{int}M\cap L, 



a contradiction.




If  p\in P\cap(u+2z_{i}+3L) , then





 \frac{p-u+z_{i}}{3}=\frac{2}{3}\frac{p-u}{2}+\frac{1}{3}z_{i}\in\operatorname{int}M\cap L, 



a contradiction. Thus  P  misses the three distinct classes  u+2z_{i}+3L , and  N_{3}(A)\leq 6 .
∎






3. Residue representatives and lattice boxes




We work in a rank-two lattice  L  and write  N_{q}(A)  for  N_{q}(A;L) . For  x\in L  and primitive  v\in L , the segment  [x,x+mv] ,  m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} , has lattice length  m .




Our aim is to compare  r  and  s=r+1 . We first replace  A  by a lattice-convex subset containing exactly one point in each class represented modulo  sL .



Lemma 3.1 (Residue-preserving reduction).



Let  L  be a rank-two lattice, let  A=\conv(A)\cap L  be finite and nonempty, and let  s  be a positive integer. There is a lattice-convex set  B\subseteq A  such that




(3.1)

 \lvert B\rvert=N_{s}(B)=N_{s}(A). 






Proof.



Suppose that two points of  A  differ by  su\neq 0 , and let  v  be the primitive direction of  u . The polygon  P=\conv(A)  has a chord of lattice length at least  s  in direction  v . If  P  is a segment, choose an endpoint  z  from which  P  contains  s  primitive steps in direction  v . Otherwise, the longest-chord lemma of Bárány and Füredi [3, Lemma 1] supplies a longest chord in this primitive direction having a lattice vertex  z  of  P  as one endpoint. Orient  v  into the chord. Then  z,z+sv\in A .




Delete  z . Since  z  is a vertex,





 A\setminus\{z\}=\conv(A\setminus\{z\})\cap L, 



so lattice convexity is preserved. The class of  z  modulo  sL  remains represented by  z+sv . Replace  P  by the convex hull of the current set and iterate. Each deletion removes one point, so the process terminates with reduction modulo  sL  injective, giving (3.1).
∎





Since  B\subseteq A , we also have  N_{r}(B)\leq N_{r}(A)  for every  r . Thus a comparison for  B  transfers to  A :





 \frac{N_{r}(A)}{r^{2}}\geq\frac{N_{r}(B)}{r^{2}}\geq\frac{N_{s}(B)}{s^{2}}=\frac{N_{s}(A)}{s^{2}}. 






For the adjacent comparison, let  s=r+1  and suppose that  A  is injective modulo  sL . Put




(3.2)

 t:=\lvert A\rvert-N_{r}(A)=N_{s}(A)-N_{r}(A). 



Since  s^{2}-r^{2}=2r+1 , the adjacent comparison is equivalent to




(3.3)

 (2r+1)N_{r}(A)\geq r^{2}t. 



We next place  A  in a lattice box. For  s=r+1 , this gives the four-corner description of the fibers modulo  rL  in Corollary 3.3.



Lemma 3.2 (Lattice-box containment).



Let  s  be a positive integer, let  L  be a rank-two lattice, and let  P  be a two-dimensional lattice polygon. Suppose that  P\cap L  is injective modulo  sL . There are  z\in L , a basis  e_{1},e_{2}  of  L , and nonnegative integers  m_{1},m_{2}  such that




(3.4)

 P\subseteq z+[0,m_{1}]e_{1}+[0,m_{2}]e_{2},\qquad m_{i}\leq\left\lfloor\frac{3s-1}{2}\right\rfloor. 






Proof.



For a linear form  f:\lin_{\mathbb{R}}L\to\mathbb{R}  with  f(L)\subseteq\mathbb{Z} , put





 w(f)=\max_{x\in P}f(x)-\min_{x\in P}f(x). 



The longest-chord argument in Lemma 3.1 gives




(3.5)

 \lambda_{1}(P-P,L)>\frac{1}{s}. 



The planar transference inequality of Ambro and Ito [1, Theorem 6.4] therefore gives two linearly independent forms  f_{1},f_{2}  satisfying  w(f_{i})<3s/2 .




Fix a basis of  L  and compute determinants using the coefficient vectors of the forms in this basis. Among the independent pairs satisfying the width bounds, choose  f_{1},f_{2}  to minimize the positive integer  \Delta=|\det(f_{1},f_{2})| . If  \Delta>1 , the centered parallelogram spanned by  f_{1},f_{2}  contains a nonzero form  g  taking integer values on  L , with





 g=\alpha f_{1}+\beta f_{2},\qquad|\alpha|,|\beta|\leq\frac{1}{2}. 



Then





 w(g)\leq|\alpha|w(f_{1})+|\beta|w(f_{2})<3s/2. 



Moreover,





 |\det(f_{1},g)|=|\beta|\Delta,\qquad|\det(g,f_{2})|=|\alpha|\Delta. 



Since  g\neq 0 , at least one of the pairs  (f_{1},g)  and  (g,f_{2})  is independent. This pair satisfies the width bounds and has absolute determinant at most  \Delta/2<\Delta , contradicting the choice of  f_{1},f_{2} . Thus  \Delta=1 , and the vectors dual to  f_{1},f_{2}  form a basis  e_{1},e_{2}  of  L .




Put  a_{i}=\min_{x\in P}f_{i}(x)  and  m_{i}=w(f_{i}) . These are integers, since the extrema occur at lattice vertices. For  z=a_{1}e_{1}+a_{2}e_{2} , we obtain  P\subseteq z+[0,m_{1}]e_{1}+[0,m_{2}]e_{2}  and  m_{i}<3s/2 , which gives (3.4).
∎





For  r\geq 3  and  s=r+1 , the bound in (3.4) is at most  2r-1 . We use the basis supplied by Lemma 3.2. Inside this box, the points of a fixed residue class modulo  rL  have at most two values in either coordinate, separated by  r . They therefore lie among the four corners of a translate of  [0,r]e_{1}+[0,r]e_{2} .



Corollary 3.3 (Four-corner fibers).



Let  r\geq 3 , let  s=r+1 , and let  P  be a two-dimensional lattice polygon in a rank-two lattice  L . Suppose that  A=P\cap L  is injective modulo  sL . In the basis of Lemma 3.2, every fiber  F=A\cap(a+rL)  has at most four points, and for each such  F  there is a  b_{F}\in L  such that




(3.6)

 F-b_{F}\subseteq r\{0,e_{1},e_{2},e_{1}+e_{2}\}. 



If  |F|=4 , then





 A=\bigl(b_{F}+[0,r]e_{1}+[0,r]e_{2}\bigr)\cap L, 



and hence





 \frac{N_{r}(A)}{r^{2}}=\frac{N_{s}(A)}{s^{2}}=1. 






Proof.



Only the four-point assertion needs proof. The four corners force the parallelogram  b_{F}+[0,r]e_{1}+[0,r]e_{2}  into  P . It contains  (r+1)^{2}=s^{2}  lattice points. Injectivity modulo  sL  gives  |A|\leq s^{2} , so these are all the points of  A , and they represent all  r^{2}  classes modulo  rL .
∎






4. Lattice segments and their intersections




Fix  r\geq 3  and  s=r+1 . By Lemmas 3.1 and 2.2, we may work with a two-dimensional lattice polygon  P  in a rank-two lattice  L  such that  A=P\cap L  is injective modulo  sL . We use the basis  e_{1},e_{2}  of Corollary 3.3 and assume that no four-point fiber modulo  rL  occurs, since that corollary already settles the comparison in this case. By (3.2),




(4.1)

 t=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in L/rL\\
A\cap\xi\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}(|A\cap\xi|-1). 



From each two-point fiber, select the segment joining its points. From each three-point fiber, select the joining segment parallel to  e_{1}  and the one parallel to  e_{2} . A singleton contributes no segment. Thus a fiber  F  contributes  |F|-1  segments, at most two, giving exactly  t  segments in total. Each selected segment has the form




(4.2)

 Q=[x,x+rv]\subseteq P,\qquad Q\cap L=\{x,x+v,\ldots,x+rv\}\subseteq A. 



Here  v  is primitive and, up to sign, is one of




(4.3)

 e_{1},\qquad e_{2},\qquad e_{1}+e_{2},\qquad e_{1}-e_{2}. 



Selected diagonal segments arise only from two-point fibers.




The image of  Q\cap L  modulo  rL  is the line




(4.4)

 \ell_{r}(Q):=\{x+jv+rL:j=0,\ldots,r-1\}\subseteq L/rL. 



Since a primitive vector extends to a basis of  L , it has order  r  in  L/rL . Thus  Q  contains  r+1  lattice points, whereas  \ell_{r}(Q)  has  r  points.




The next two configurations give  N_{r}(A)=r^{2} . The desired comparison then follows from  N_{s}(A)=|A|\leq s^{2} .



Lemma 4.1 (Repeated images of parallel segments).



Suppose that two distinct parallel selected segments  Q,Q^{\prime}  have the same image modulo  rL . Then  N_{r}(A)=r^{2} .




Proof.



Choose a basis in which the segment direction is horizontal. The segments cannot lie on the same line, since their convex hull would have lattice length at least  r+1=s . Thus their horizontal levels are distinct. Equality of their images modulo  rL  means that these levels differ by  mr  for some integer  m\geq 1 .




By convexity,  \conv(Q\cup Q^{\prime})\subseteq P . This convex hull has a horizontal section of length  r , measured in the chosen lattice coordinates, at every intermediate integer level. Since  mr\geq r , we can choose  r  consecutive integer levels on or between the two lines. They give all vertical residues modulo  r , and every horizontal section of length  r  contains at least  r  consecutive lattice points. Thus every horizontal residue also occurs. All lattice points just counted belong to  A=P\cap L ; hence all  r^{2}  classes are represented.
∎




Lemma 4.2 (Determinant-two directions).



Let  r\geq 3  be an integer, let  L  be a rank-two lattice, and let  P  be a lattice polygon such that  A=P\cap L  is injective modulo  (r+1)L . If  P  contains two segments of lattice length  r  in primitive directions  u,v  whose determinant in a basis of  L  has absolute value two, then  N_{r}(A)=r^{2} .




Proof.



Injectivity modulo  (r+1)L  forbids a segment of lattice length  r+1  in  P . The classes of  u,v  modulo  2L  are nonzero and linearly dependent, so  u\equiv v\pmod{2L} . Consequently,  (u+v)/2  and  (u-v)/2  belong to  L  and have determinant of absolute value one, hence form a basis of  L . In these coordinates, after translation, the two segments are





 [(0,0),(r,r)],\qquad[(a,r+c),(a+r,c)],\qquad a,c\in\mathbb{Z}. 



Interchanging the coordinates swaps  a,c . Interchanging the segments and applying  (x,y)\mapsto(x-a,r+c-y)  replaces  (a,c)  by  (-a,c) . These operations allow us to assume  a\geq c\geq 0 .




By convexity,  P  contains the triangle with vertices  (0,0),(a,r+c),(a+r,c) . Its intersection with  y=c  contains





 \left[\frac{ac}{r+c},\,a+r\right]\times\{c\}. 



The absence of a segment of lattice length  r+1  therefore gives





 a+r-\left\lceil\frac{ac}{r+c}\right\rceil\leq r,\qquad\text{hence}\qquad ar<r+c\leq r+a. 



Thus  (r-1)a<r , so  a\leq 1 . If  a=1 , the strict inequality forces  c=1 . Consequently  (a,c)  is either  (0,0)  or  (1,1) .




In the first case, the convex hull of the two segments is  [0,r]^{2} . In the second,  (1,1),(r,r)  lie on the first segment, and joining  (1,1)  to the endpoints of the second gives  (r,1),(1,r)\in P . Hence  [1,r]^{2}\subseteq P  in both cases. Its  r^{2}  lattice points represent every class modulo  rL , proving  N_{r}(A)=r^{2} .
∎





Two primitive directions are unimodular if they form a basis of  L . Outside the two cases above, the direction classes are pairwise unimodular: among (4.3), only the two diagonals have determinant of absolute value two. Thus at most three direction classes remain.



Lemma 4.3 (Intersections and concurrence).



Two selected segments whose directions form a basis of  L  meet in a lattice point. Moreover, if three selected segments have pairwise-unimodular directions and their images modulo  rL  have a common point, then the three segments meet at one lattice point.




Proof.



Modulo  sL , a primitive vector has order  s , so the reduction of  Q\cap L  is a complete affine line of  s  points. The two affine lines in these basis directions meet in exactly one class modulo  sL . Each of the two segments contains a representative of that class, and injectivity modulo  sL  forces the two representatives to coincide.




For the second assertion, let  p_{ij}  be the intersection of the  i th and  j th segments. Pairwise unimodularity implies that all  p_{ij}  lie in the common class modulo  rL . If they are not all equal, they are pairwise distinct: equality of two puts one point on all three segments. On each segment, write the difference of its two intersection points as  ku , where  u  is its primitive direction and  0<|k|\leq r . Their congruence modulo  rL  gives  ku\in rL . Since  u  extends to a basis of  L , this forces  r\mid k , so  |k|=r  and the intersection points are the two endpoints. All three segments would therefore have their endpoints in one  rL -fiber, whereas at most two segments were selected from any fiber.
∎






5. Covering and counting on the finite torus




For the geometric argument below, let  r\geq 3  be an integer. Sections 3 and 4 handle four-point fibers, parallel segments with the same image modulo  rL , and determinant-two pairs: each forces all classes modulo  rL  to be represented. In the remaining case, the selected segments have distinct images modulo  rL  and pairwise-unimodular direction classes. The two diagonal directions in (4.3) cannot both occur. Replacing  e_{2}  by  -e_{2}  if necessary, we may assume that the occurring directions are among





 e_{1},\qquad e_{2},\qquad e_{1}+e_{2}, 



up to sign. Identify  L  with  \mathbb{Z}^{2}  and  \lin_{\mathbb{R}}L  with  \mathbb{R}^{2}  using the basis  e_{1},e_{2} . We use these coordinates both for the segments and for their residue classes. Thus  L/rL  is identified with





 G_{r}:=(\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z})^{2}. 



Write  \mathcal{H},\mathcal{V},\mathcal{D}  for the three families of lines in  G_{r}  given respectively by





 y=a,\qquad x=b,\qquad x-y=c\qquad(a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z}). 



Each family contains  r  disjoint lines of  r  points, and every point lies on one line from each class. If  \mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{D} , put





 U(\mathcal{S}):=\bigcup_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}}\ell. 



The transformations





 (x,y)\longmapsto(y,x),\qquad(x,y)\longmapsto(x,x-y) 



generate every permutation of the three unoriented direction classes, up to a sign change of a line parameter. These integer linear maps preserve lattice lengths, congruences, and intersections.




The main geometric step is to complete the images of the selected segments to a cover with  2r+1  lines. The construction uses the intersection and concurrence properties established in Lemma 4.3.



Proposition 5.1 (Completion to a cover).



Let  r\geq 3  be an integer, and let  \mathcal{Q}  be a family of segments selected as in Section 4. Suppose that it uses at most three direction classes and that every pair of distinct classes is unimodular. Put





 \mathcal{S}:=\{\ell_{r}(Q):Q\in\mathcal{Q}\}. 



Assume that distinct parallel segments have distinct images modulo  rL . Then there is a family  \mathcal{C}  of exactly  2r+1  distinct lines from a pairwise-unimodular triple extending the direction classes occurring in  \mathcal{Q}  such that





 \mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{C},\qquad U(\mathcal{C})=G_{r}. 






Proof.



If fewer than three direction classes occur, extend them to a pairwise-unimodular triple; this fixes three ambient classes but adds no selected line. Use the common lattice coordinates described above. Let  u,v,w  be the numbers of selected horizontal, vertical, and diagonal lines, respectively; some of these numbers may be zero. If, say,  u+v\leq r+1 , include all  r  lines of the third class and add  r+1-u-v  unused lines from the first two classes. There are  2r-u-v  such unused lines, so this gives a cover with exactly  2r+1  lines.




We may therefore suppose that every pairwise sum among  u,v,w  is at least  r+2 . In particular, all three selected families are nonempty. Let  Y,X,W\subseteq\mathbb{Z}  be the integer parameters of the lines containing the selected segments:  y\in Y ,  x\in X , and  x-y\in W , respectively. These are their parameters in  \mathbb{R}^{2} , before reduction modulo  r . Put





 a=\max Y-\min Y,\qquad b=\max X-\min X,\qquad c=\max W-\min W. 



Each span is at most  r . For example, any selected vertical segment meets the selected horizontal segments at the smallest and largest parameters by Lemma 4.3, and both intersection points lie on that segment of lattice length  r . Equality would give two parallel segments with the same image modulo  rL , so




(5.1)

 a,b,c\leq r-1. 



Since an integer parameter set of size  u  has span at least  u-1 , the pairwise-sum assumption gives




(5.2)

 a+b\geq r,\qquad a+c\geq r,\qquad b+c\geq r. 






Use the three-class symmetry to make  a  the smallest span. Apply the translation  (x,y)\mapsto(x-\min Y-\min W,y-\min Y) . Denote the translated parameter sets again by  Y,X,W  and put  d=\min X . Then




(5.3)

 Y\subseteq[0,a],\qquad X\subseteq[d,d+b],\qquad W\subseteq[0,c]. 



Here and below, intervals denote sets of integers; their residues index torus lines. An interval with upper endpoint below its lower endpoint is empty.




If  d>c , the selected horizontal segment at level  0  contains its intersections with the lines  x-y=0  and  x=d+b , and therefore  d+b\leq r . Together with  a+b\geq r , this gives  d\leq a\leq c , a contradiction. If  d<a-b , the selected horizontal segment at level  a  contains its intersections with  x=d  and  x-y=c , so  a+c-d\leq r ; the inequality  b+c\geq r  then yields  d\geq a-b , again a contradiction. Hence




(5.4)

 a-b\leq d\leq c. 






We next arrange  0\leq d\leq a . Since  a\leq r-1 , the intervals in (5.8) will then lie in  [0,r-1] .




If  d\geq 0 , apply  (x,y)\mapsto(x,x-y) . The new parameter sets are





 Y^{\prime}=W,\qquad X^{\prime}=X,\qquad W^{\prime}=Y. 



Their spans are  c,b,a , respectively, and  \min X^{\prime}=d . By (5.4),  0\leq d\leq c .




If  d<0 , apply  (x,y)\mapsto(-y,-x)  and translate both coordinates by  d+b . Then





 Y^{\prime}\subseteq[0,b],\quad X^{\prime}\subseteq[d+b-a,d+b],\quad W^{\prime}\subseteq[0,c]. 



The new horizontal, vertical, and diagonal spans are  b,a,c , respectively, and  \min X^{\prime}=d+b-a . Since  d<0  and (5.4) gives  d\geq a-b , we have  0\leq d+b-a\leq b . After relabeling in either case we have




(5.5)

 Y\subseteq[0,a],\quad X\subseteq[d,d+b],\quad W\subseteq[0,c],\quad 0\leq d\leq a. 






The extremal horizontal segments at levels  0  and  a  contain their intersections with the extremal vertical and diagonal segments. Hence




(5.6)

 d+b\leq r,\qquad a+c-d\leq r. 



Both inequalities are strict. If  d+b=r , the selected segments on  y=0 ,  x=r , and  x-y=0  have pairwise intersections  (0,0),(r,0),(r,r) . Their images modulo  rL  concur, but the segments do not, contradicting Lemma 4.3. If  a+c-d=r , use the selected segments on  y=a ,  x=d , and  x-y=c : their pairwise intersections are  (d,a),(a+c,a),(d,d-c) , and the same contradiction applies. Therefore




(5.7)

 d+b\leq r-1,\qquad a+c-d\leq r-1. 






Set




(5.8)

 Y^{*}=[0,a],\qquad X^{*}=[d,r-1],\qquad W^{*}=[0,r-1-a+d]. 



These intervals lie in  [0,r-1] , contain  Y,X,W  by (5.7), and have total cardinality  (a+1)+(r-d)+(r-a+d)=2r+1 . The corresponding lines cover  G_{r} : a point on neither a  Y^{*} -line nor an  X^{*} -line has





 y\in[a+1,r-1],\qquad x\in[0,d-1], 



and thus





 x-y\pmod{r}\in[1,r+d-a-2]\subseteq W^{*}. 



When one of these displayed intervals is empty, the implication is vacuous. The corresponding torus lines form the required  \mathcal{C} . Pulling them back through the affine-unimodular normalizations completes the proof.
∎





It remains to estimate how many points the selected lines cover. The next lemma applies to every subfamily of a cover of the size just constructed.



Lemma 5.2 (Counting in a cover).



Let  r\geq 3 , and let  \mathcal{C}  be a cover of  G_{r}  by exactly  2r+1  distinct lines from  \mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{D} . For every  \mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{C} ,




(5.9)

 |U(\mathcal{S})|\geq\frac{r^{2}|\mathcal{S}|}{2r+1}. 






Proof.



Put  m=|\mathcal{S}| , and let  u,v,w  be its three direction counts. If  m\leq 3r/2 , inclusion–exclusion gives





 |U(\mathcal{S})|\geq rm-(uv+uw+vw)\geq rm-\frac{m^{2}}{3}\geq\frac{rm}{2}\geq\frac{r^{2}m}{2r+1}. 






Suppose that  m>3r/2 , and put  M=\lfloor(r-1)/2\rfloor . We restore the lines of  \mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{S}  one at a time. For a current family  \mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{T}\subsetneq\mathcal{C} , let  a,b,c  count the lines outside  \mathcal{T}  in the three full direction classes. Thus





 a+b+c=3r-|\mathcal{T}|<\frac{3r}{2}. 



Adding a horizontal line of  \mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{T}  covers at most  \min\{b,c\}  new points: both the vertical and diagonal line of a new point must lie outside  \mathcal{T} , and each meets the horizontal line once. The other directions have analogous bounds.




If all remaining lines have one direction, the missing lines in the other two classes lie outside  \mathcal{C} . Their total number is at most  3r-(2r+1)=r-1 , so a remaining line covers at most  M  new points. Otherwise, if every remaining line covered at least  M+1  new points, the bounds from two different directions would imply  a,b,c\geq M+1 . This contradicts





 a+b+c<\frac{3r}{2}\leq 3(M+1). 



Hence a line adding at most  M  new points can always be restored. The condition  |\mathcal{T}|>3r/2  is preserved throughout. Since the final family covers  G_{r} ,





 r^{2}-|U(\mathcal{S})|\leq(2r+1-m)M\leq\frac{r^{2}(2r+1-m)}{2r+1}, 



where  M\leq(r-1)/2<r^{2}/(2r+1) . Rearranging proves the claim.
∎




Proof of Theorem 1.1.



By Section 2, it suffices to compare moduli  r  and  s=r+1  for  r\geq 3  and a nonempty finite lattice-convex set  A  in a rank-two lattice  L . By Lemma 3.1, we may assume that reduction modulo  sL  is injective on  A . If  A  has affine dimension at most one, use Lemma 2.2. Otherwise put  P=\conv(A)  and  t=|A|-N_{r}(A) . Since  N_{s}(A)=|A| , the desired comparison is equivalent to




(5.10)

 N_{r}(A)\geq\frac{r^{2}t}{2r+1}. 



If  N_{r}(A)=r^{2} , the comparison follows directly from  N_{s}(A)=|A|\leq s^{2} . We may therefore assume  N_{r}(A)<r^{2} .




Use the coordinates of Corollary 3.3. No fiber has four points, since that would give  N_{r}(A)=r^{2} . Select  t  segments as in Section 4. Lemmas 4.1 and 4.2 exclude equal images of parallel segments and determinant-two pairs. Thus the selected direction classes are pairwise unimodular. Images in different classes are also distinct, since they meet in exactly one point and each has  r>1  points. Their family  \mathcal{S}  therefore has  t  lines.




Proposition 5.1 gives a cover with  2r+1  lines containing  \mathcal{S} . Lemma 5.2 gives





 N_{r}(A)\geq|U(\mathcal{S})|\geq\frac{r^{2}t}{2r+1}, 



where the first inequality holds because every lattice point on a selected segment belongs to  A . This proves (5.10).




Lemma 3.1 transfers the comparison to the original set. Chaining consecutive comparisons and then summing over the affine cosets in (2.2) proves the theorem.
∎




Remark 5.3.



In Lemma 5.2, the last comparison in each case is strict when  \varnothing\neq\mathcal{S}\subsetneq\mathcal{C} . Hence equality in (5.9) holds precisely for  \mathcal{S}=\varnothing  or  \mathcal{S}=\mathcal{C} .




Under the assumptions of Theorem 1.1, put  i=\rank\Gamma  and  c=D_{\Lambda}(K,\Gamma) . For integers  1\leq r<s ,





 \frac{D_{\Lambda}(K,r\Gamma)}{r^{i}}=\frac{D_{\Lambda}(K,s\Gamma)}{s^{i}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D_{\Lambda}(K,s\Gamma)=s^{i}c. 



Thus equal values occur only at the maximum  c ; once the normalized counts fall below  c , they decrease strictly.




If  c=0  or  i=0 , the assertion is immediate. Otherwise, Lemma 2.1 expresses the difference of the normalized counts as a sum of nonnegative differences over the nonempty slices  A_{\xi} . Equality forces equality on every slice, and monotonicity then gives





 s^{i}N_{s-1}(A_{\xi};\Gamma)=(s-1)^{i}N_{s}(A_{\xi};\Gamma). 



Since  \gcd(s^{i},(s-1)^{i})=1  and  1\leq N_{s}(A_{\xi};\Gamma)\leq s^{i} , we obtain  N_{s}(A_{\xi};\Gamma)=s^{i} . Summing over the  c  nonempty slices proves the forward implication. Conversely, monotonicity and the value  c  at modulus one give  c\geq D_{\Lambda}(K,r\Gamma)/r^{i}\geq D_{\Lambda}(K,s\Gamma)/s^{i}=c .
