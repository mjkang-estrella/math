 Abstract 
    
 We study permutations covered by occurrences of a
consecutive permutation pattern, with successive occurrences
overlapping in a fixed number of positions. Several special cases of
this construction have been studied in the theory of consecutive
patterns and via the cluster method. Here we study the sequences
obtained by repeating a fixed overlap. We elicit many structural
properties of such pattern covers, mainly in terms of posets whose
linear extensions are in bijection with the permutations in question.
The enumeration of these permutations for different patterns gives
rise to a great variety of sequences, many classical, but most of them
not found in the  OEIS . We conjecture that, with minor modifications,
all of these sequences are moments of probability measures on the real
line. 
    
 We develop a general enumerative and order-theoretic framework for
these objects. For overlap one, we give a generating function whose
coefficients enumerate permutations covered by an arbitrary fixed
pattern with any prescribed number of occurrences. For every overlap
at most half the length of the pattern, we derive a recursive formula
which, for fixed pattern and overlap, yields a polynomial-time
enumeration algorithm. We then associate to each pattern, overlap,
and number of occurrences a naturally defined poset whose linear
extensions are in bijection with the corresponding pattern-covered
permutations. 
    
 The regularity of this poset construction makes possible a structural
analysis that in many cases leads to enumerative formulas for large
classes of patterns. We prove uniform restrictions on the local
structure of the Hasse diagrams, describe their branches (pendant
chains) in terms of the corresponding patterns, and characterize when
the poset is a tree (seen as an undirected graph) or chain. 
    
 We also construct patterns whose Hasse diagrams have a simple
grid-like structure. Some of these results yield explicit families of
formulas, explain unexpected equinumerations, and exhibit classes of
new enumerative phenomena in consecutive permutation patterns. 
    
 In a forthcoming companion paper  [ 2 ] , we
investigate the sequences arising here from a probabilistic
perspective, viewing them both as point processes and as moment
sequences. We also obtain integral and matrix representations for
important overlap  1 1  and  2 2  classes of pattern-covering sequences.

 1  Introduction 

 
 Let  𝒮 ℓ \mathcal{S}_{\ell}  be the set of permutations of the set
 { 1 , 2 , … , ℓ } \{1,2,\ldots,\ell\} . Given permutations  π ∈ 𝒮 K \pi\in\mathcal{S}_{K}  and
 σ ∈ 𝒮 n \sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n} , an  occurrence of the pattern   π \pi  in
 σ \sigma  is obtained by selecting entries of  σ \sigma  whose relative
order is the same as that of  π \pi . If the selected entries are
required to occupy consecutive positions, then  π \pi  is called a
 consecutive pattern . The systematic study of permutation
patterns has its origins in work of Knuth and of Simion and Schmidt
 [ 12 ,  15 ] . For some background on consecutive
patterns, see  [ 16 ] . 
 
 
 Two occurrences of a consecutive pattern containing some of the same entries of
a permutation impose order conditions on these entries, beyond what each
occurrence must satisfy. Thus the enumeration is not determined merely by the
length and structure of the pattern or by the number of occurrences: it depends
on how two copies of that particular pattern can fit together, at the beginning
of one and the end of the other. Cluster methods and related generating-function
techniques have provided the principal general framework for studying these
interactions  [ 3 ,  8 ,  10 ,  11 ,  13 ] . The role of
overlap also underlies refined notions of consecutive  Wilf equivalence 
(two patterns having the same enumeration) and the study of patterns two of
whose occurrences can overlap in at most one entry of a permutation
 [ 4 ,  5 ] . 
 
 
 In this paper, we require successive occurrences of a consecutive pattern in a
permutation  σ \sigma  to overlap in exactly  m m  entries for some given  m m , and
study the structure of such permutations and their enumeration. Concretely, fix
a pattern  π ∈ 𝒮 K \pi\in\mathcal{S}_{K} , an overlap  m < K m<K , and  j ≥ 1 j\geq 1 , the prescribed
number of occurrences. We count permutations  σ ∈ 𝒮 n \sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}  containing
occurrences of  π \pi  beginning at 
 

 
 
 1 , 1 + ( K − m ) , 1 + 2 ​ ( K − m ) , … , 1 + ( j − 1 ) ​ ( K − m ) , 1,1+(K-m),1+2(K-m),\ldots,1+(j-1)(K-m), 
 
 
 so that every two successive prescribed occurrences overlap in exactly
 m m  positions, and every entry of  σ \sigma  belongs to some occurrence.
These conditions of course constrain the size of the permutation
 σ \sigma  to be  n = K + ( j − 1 ) ​ ( K − m ) n=K+(j-1)(K-m) . We call such corresponding
permutations  pattern packings  and denote their count by
 N π m ​ ( j ) N_{\pi}^{m}(j) . (Note that we disregard potential occurrences outside
the prescribed ones.) 
 
 
 The individual numbers  N π m ​ ( j ) N_{\pi}^{m}(j)  are instances of the refined
cluster numbers studied by Dwyer and Elizalde
 [ 5 ] , where occurrences may be prescribed at an
arbitrary set of starting positions. Here we instead systematically
study the constant-overlap specialization in which the starting
positions form an arithmetic progression and the number of prescribed
occurrences varies. 
 
 
 Because the same overlap is repeated at every step, a packing with
 j + 1 j+1  occurrences is obtained from one with  j j  occurrences by
adjoining another copy of the same local configuration. Although
adding a new copy in this way typically allows for rearrangement of
the previous entries, this highlights a recursive structure that can
in many cases be exploited for understanding of the global structure
and for enumeration of the permutations in question, even though it
does not make the enumeration uniformly simple. Even for short
patterns, the sequences 
 

 
 
 ( N π m ​ ( j ) ) j ≥ 1 \bigl(N_{\pi}^{m}(j)\bigr)_{j\geq 1} 
 
 
 display strikingly different behavior. Some are given by Catalan or
Fuss–Catalan numbers, some by products of factorials or binomial
coefficients, and most appear not to belong to previously studied
families. Small changes in the pattern may alter the sequence
substantially, while quite different patterns may unexpectedly produce
the same sequence. The problem is therefore to understand how much of
this variation can be explained by general mechanisms, rather than by
a separate argument for each pattern. 
 
 
 The covering of permutations by occurrences of a consecutive pattern
partly generalizes the notion of the  descent set , which is the
set of starting positions of descents in a permutation, that is,
positions  i i  such that  σ ⁡ ( i ) > σ ⁡ ( i + 1 ) \sigma(i)>\sigma(i+1) , which is simply an
occurrence of the consecutive pattern 21. In a seminal
paper  [ 9 ]  Gessel and Viennot gave a
formula, in terms of determinants, for the number of permutations in
 𝒮 n \mathcal{S}_{n}  with any given descent set (a special case of the celebrated
Lindström-Gessel-Viennot Lemma). A general formula for the number of
permutations with any set of starting points for a given consecutive
pattern seems out of reach, given how hard the general problem is for
a single pattern occurring at regular intervals. The only case we
present here that goes beyond regular intervals and overlaps involves
patterns whose occurrences are allowed to overlap in either one or two
places, see Proposition  6.1 . 
 
 
 The first part of the paper develops such mechanisms on the
enumerative side. We establish symmetries that preserve the packing
numbers and identify further transformations specific to a fixed
overlap. For overlap  m = 1 m=1 , where the enumeration depends only on the
first and last entry of a pattern, we express  N π 1 ​ ( j ) N_{\pi}^{1}(j)  as a
coefficient of a single multivariate formal power series. 
 
 
 For general overlap satisfying  m ≤ K / 2 m\leq K/2 , we derive a recurrence by analyzing
the effect of adding a new occurrence and how that allows for rearrangement of
the previous entries. For each such pair  ( π , m ) (\pi,m)  the recurrence gives an
algorithm for computing  N π m ​ ( j ) N_{\pi}^{m}(j)  as  j j  varies. This algorithm is very
effective for  m = 1 m=1 , but less so for greater  m m . 
 
 
 The second part of the paper develops an order-theoretic approach to
the same problem. Each occurrence of  π \pi  imposes a chain of
inequalities among the entries occupying its positions. Superimposing
the chains from the prescribed occurrences produces a finite poset,
which we call the  pattern-packing poset  and denote by
 𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j) . Its linear extensions are naturally in bijection with the
corresponding pattern packings, so that 
 

 
 
 N π m ​ ( j ) = | ℒ ⁡ ( 𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) ) | , N_{\pi}^{m}(j)=\left|\mathcal{L}\bigl({\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j)\bigr)\right|, 
 
 
 where  ℒ ⁡ ( 𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) ) \mathcal{L}({\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j))  is the set of linear extensions of  𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j) . 
 
 
 Special cases of these posets, encoding the inequalities imposed by
prescribed overlapping occurrences, arise in cluster constructions and
in the framework of
 [ 3 ,  5 ,  8 ] . Here
we treat the constant-overlap posets as a sequence indexed by  j j  and
exploit the repeated structure arising from the regularly spaced
starting positions of occurrences. 
 
 
 To make that structure visible, we introduce a  stack 
representation in which each prescribed occurrence contributes a
translated copy of the same chain, and overlapping positions are
identified. Although the defining chains are individually simple,
their transitive closure can produce complicated structures. In
particular, repeated local constraints may create undirected cycles,
branching, interleaving, or grid-like regions in the Hasse
diagram. This is another manifestation of the central difficulty: the
local rule is specified by a single permutation, but its repeated
application can generate a much less transparent global order. 
 
 
 We prove several restrictions valid for every pattern-packing poset.
Each element is covered by at most two elements and covers at most two
elements, and the poset decomposes into a  core  —which is a
lattice bounded by elements that belong to every chain of maximal
length — and (possibly) chain components attached to the core by
single edges. We then characterize several distinguished families
depending on the structure of their Hasse diagrams and the
corresponding undirected graph, which in many cases leads to
enumerative formulas. We give necessary and sufficient conditions for
the Hasse diagram to be a tree, and, when  m ≤ K / 2 m\leq K/2 , express these
conditions directly in terms of the prefix and suffix of the pattern.
We similarly characterize the case where the poset is a chain, and
construct a class of patterns whose packing posets form  shifted
grids , posets obtained from rectangular subsets of the integer
lattice  ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}^{2}  by shifting  h h  steps to the right the unit rectangles
at height  h h . These classifications identify finite features of the
pattern that control the structure of the entire family 
 

 
 
 𝒫 π m ​ ( 1 ) , 𝒫 π m ​ ( 2 ) , 𝒫 π m ​ ( 3 ) , … . {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(1),{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(2),{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(3),\ldots. 
 
 
 
 
 The poset viewpoint also clarifies many equinumerations observed among
the enumerating sequences. Some arise from elementary symmetries of
the patterns, some from isomorphisms of the associated posets, and
some from two posets differing in ways that leave the number of linear
extensions invariant for trivial reasons. However, we also find pairs
of patterns with identical packing sequences despite having
nonisomorphic posets with visibly different structures, including
examples in which one Hasse diagram is a tree and the other contains
cycles, and even such pairs of patterns of different lengths and
overlaps. Such examples indicate that the constant-overlap setting has
a substantial equivalence theory that is not yet fully understood. 
 
 
 Wilf equivalences have been much studied for pattern avoidance. In
our setting it seems to be much richer, since such equinumerations are
not restricted to patterns of the same length and the same overlap.
We present several such cases that are easily proved, but those only
seem to scratch the surface. 
 
 
 Some of the results presented here were presented in our extended
abstract  [ 1 ]  for the conference  fpsac  in
2023. 
 
 
 In a forthcoming companion paper  [ 2 ] , we study the sequences
arising here as point processes and moment sequences. Using probabilistic
methods, we derive integral and matrix representations for important overlap- 1 1 
and overlap- 2 2  families. We further conjecture, and for many families are able
to prove, that the pattern-packing  uniformly  gives rise to moments of
probability measures on the real line. This not only identifies natural new
connections between permutation patterns and probability theory, but also opens
up more general approaches in a field of combinatorics where structure remains
pattern-dependent. 
 
 
 The paper is organized as follows. Section  2  formally
defines the relevant objects and establishes the notation. In
Section  3  we establish symmetries of the packing
numbers and discuss their relation to consecutive Wilf
equivalence. Section  4  contains the coefficient-extraction
formula for overlap one and the recurrence for overlaps at most half
the pattern length. Section  5  introduces the
pattern-packing posets and develops their structural theory, including
the core-and-branch decomposition, the tree and chain criteria, and
the shifted-grid construction. In Section  6  we
mention one case of permutations covered by irregularly spaced
occurrences of a pattern, with overlaps either 1 or 2. Finally,
Section  7  presents open problems concerning further Wilf
equivalences, extremal packing numbers, and other structural
properties of pattern-packing posets than those we treat explicitly. 
 
 
 
 2  First Definitions and Notation 

 
 As is habitual in combinatorics, we let  [ n ] := { 1 , … , n } [n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}  and
denote by  𝒮 n \mathcal{S}_{n}  the set of all bijections  [ n ] → [ n ] [n]\to[n] . The set
 𝒮 n \mathcal{S}_{n}  forms a group ( the symmetric group ) under functional
composition  ∘ \circ , with the identity permutation as the identity
element. 
 
 
 The study of permutation patterns is predominantly combinatorial, with
the group structure rarely playing a role. Results in this area are
typically formulated in the language of words, where  𝒮 n \mathcal{S}_{n}  is the
set of permutations of  { 1 , 2 , … , n } \{1,2,\ldots,n\} , that is, the elements of
the symmetric group: 
 
 
 Definition 2.1   (Permutations as words (the one-line notation)) . 
 
 We
interpret elements of  𝒮 n \mathcal{S}_{n}  as words of length  n n  in the alphabet  [ n ] [n] 
formed of distinct letters. We write  σ = σ 1 ​ … ​ σ n \sigma=\sigma_{1}\ldots\sigma_{n}  and refer
to this as the  one-line notation . For  σ \sigma  a word of length  n n  and
 σ ′ \sigma^{\prime}  a word of length  n ′ n^{\prime} , we denote by  σ ​ σ ′ \sigma\sigma^{\prime}  their
 concatenation , that is, the word of length  n + n ′ n+n^{\prime}  whose first  n n 
letters are a copy of  σ \sigma  and the remaining  n ′ n^{\prime}  letters are a copy of
 σ ′ \sigma^{\prime} . 
 
 
 
 When talking about patterns and permutations in one-line notation we will
interchangeably talk about their  letters  and  elements . 
 
 
 When necessary, we mix the two interpretations of permutations. For
example,  σ i − 1 \sigma^{-1}_{i}  denotes the  i i th letter, in the one-line
notation, of the functional inverse of  σ \sigma . 
 
 
 Definition 2.2 . 
 
 Given words  w w  and  w ′ w^{\prime}  of
length  ℓ \ell  on the alphabet  [ n ] [n] , we say that  w w  and  w ′ w^{\prime}  are  order
isomorphic , denoted  w ∼ w ′ w\sim w^{\prime} , if for every  i , j ∈ [ ℓ ] i,j\in[\ell] ,  w i < w j w_{i}<w_{j}  if
and only if  w i ′ < w j ′ w_{i}^{\prime}<w_{j}^{\prime} . 
 
 
 
 For example, 4936 is order isomorphic to 2413. 
 
 
 We now consider ways in which two permutations can be compared to one
another by inclusion. 
 
 
 Definition 2.3   (Permutation Patterns) . 
 
 Fix
 K K  and  π ∈ 𝒮 K \pi\in\mathcal{S}_{K} . For any  σ ∈ 𝒮 n \sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n} , where  n ≥ K n\geq K ,
we say that a tuple of indices  ( i 1 , … , i K ) (i_{1},\ldots,i_{K}) , with  1 ≤ i 1 < i 2 < ⋯ < i K ≤ n 1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{K}\leq n , forms an  occurrence  of the
 classical permutation pattern  π \pi  if the word formed by
restricting  σ \sigma  to indices  i 1 , … , i K i_{1},\ldots,i_{K}  is isomorphic to
 π \pi , that is,  σ i 1 ​ … ​ σ i K ∼ π \sigma_{i_{1}}\ldots\sigma_{i_{K}}\sim\pi . 
 
 
 In contrast, for  ( i 1 , … , i K ) (i_{1},\ldots,i_{K})  to form an occurrence in  σ \sigma 
of the  consecutive permutation pattern   π \pi , the indices must
be consecutive, that is,  i K = i K − 1 + 1 = ⋯ = i 1 + K − 1 i_{K}=i_{K-1}+1=\cdots=i_{1}+K-1 , so that the
occurrence is a contiguous subword of  σ \sigma . 
 
 
 The indices  i 1 , … , i K i_{1},\ldots,i_{K}  are referred to as the  positions 
or  places  of the occurrence, with  i 1 i_{1}  being the
 starting position .   □ \square 
 
 
 
 Remark 2.4 . 
 
 There is a more general inclusion
rule that interpolates between the classical and consecutive
patterns. These are the  vincular patterns   [ 16 ] 
(previously called  generalized patterns ), which we don’t
consider here. 
 
 
 
 When a permutation  σ \sigma  contains no occurrences of a pattern
 π \pi , we say that  σ \sigma   avoids  π \pi  (or  is an
avoider of  π \pi ). A central question in the study of permutation
patterns is this: Given a pattern  π \pi , how many permutations
 σ ∈ 𝒮 n \sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}  avoid  π \pi  (for any given  n n )? Here, instead, we
count permutations with occurrences of a given consecutive pattern
appearing at regular, overlapping intervals. 
 
 
 2.1  Main objects 

 
 Henceforth, ’pattern’ will refer to a consecutive permutation
pattern. Furthermore, as the size of the pattern will recur in the
indexation and enumeration of various quantities, we assign it a
capital letter and make it an explicit definition: 
 
 
 Definition 2.5 . 
 
 A  K K -pattern  is a
consecutive permutation pattern  π ∈ 𝒮 K \pi\in\mathcal{S}_{K} . 
 
 
 
 The following is the main definition of this paper. 
 
 
 Definition 2.6   (Pattern packing) . 
 
 Fix  K ≥ 2 K\geq 2 ,
 j ≥ 1 j\geq 1 ,  π \pi  a  K K -pattern, and  1 ≤ m < K 1\leq m<K . We say that a
permutation  σ ∈ 𝒮 n \sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}   m m -packs  j j  occurrences of  π \pi 
or that  σ \sigma   ( m , j ) (m,j) -packs  π \pi  if  n = K + ( j − 1 ) ​ ( K − m ) n=K+(j-1)(K-m)  and
 σ \sigma  contains occurrences of  π \pi  starting in positions
 { 1 , 1 + d , 1 + 2 ​ d , … , 1 + ( j − 1 ) ​ d } \{1,1+d,1+2d,\ldots,1+(j-1)d\} , where  d = K − m d=K-m . In other words, 
 

 
 
 σ 1 + ( i − 1 ) ​ d ​ … ​ σ K + ( i − 1 ) ​ d ∼ π , \sigma_{1+(i-1)d}\ldots\sigma_{K+(i-1)d}\;\sim\;\pi, 
 
 (2.1) 
 
 where  ∼ \sim  is order isomorphism and  i i  runs from 1
to  j j . We also say that  π \pi   covers  σ \sigma  with overlaps
 m m , or that  σ \sigma  is  m m -covered by  π \pi , when the number
of occurrences need not be specified. 
 
 
 
 Remark 2.7 . 
 
 Definition  2.6  does not preclude additional occurrences, outside
of the regularly occurring ones. For example, the permutation 192837465
1-packs 2 occurrences of 15243 since it contains occurrences of 15243 starting
in positions 1 and 5. But it also contains an occurrence of 15243 starting in
position 3, which we do not prohibit. Whether it is possible to have
additional occurrences of a pattern depends on the length of the pattern
 π \pi , the length of the overlaps and the  autocorrelation  of  π \pi ,
that is, the maximum possible overlap of two distinct occurrences of  π \pi  in
a permutation. In order for a  K K -pattern  π \pi  to possibly occur in more
places in a permutation than those prescribed by overlaps  m ≥ 1 m\geq 1 ,  π \pi 
must have autocorrelation at least  ( K + m ) / 2 (K+m)/2 . In the remainder of the paper,
an ’occurrence’ will always refer to one of the  regular  occurrences,
that is, those starting in positions  { 1 , 1 + d , 1 + 2 ​ d , … , 1 + ( j − 1 ) ​ d } \{1,1+d,1+2d,\ldots,1+(j-1)d\} , where
 d = K − m d=K-m . 
 
 
 
 Remark 2.8 . 
 
 The Main Definition focuses on
the case where the regular occurrences overlap (that is,  1 ≤ m < K 1\leq m<K ).
However, allowing regular gaps between occurrences (which we can
interpret as letting  m < 0 m<0 ), or having occurrences adjacent without
overlap ( m = 0 m=0 ), simply adds trivially computed multiplicative factors
to any enumerative formula. 
 
 
 
 
 2.2  Symmetries and Wilf equivalences 

 
 In pattern  avoidance , two patterns  π \pi  and  π ′ \pi^{\prime}  are said to
be  Wilf equivalent  if, for all  n n , the subset of  𝒮 n \mathcal{S}_{n}  that
avoids  π \pi  has the same size as that avoiding  π ′ \pi^{\prime} . Some such
equivalences are proved simply by applying the same symmetry operation
on both a pattern and the permutations avoiding it, such as reversing
both, or taking complements, which involves replacing the letter  i i 
in a permutation/pattern of length  n n  by  n + 1 − i n+1-i . 
 
 
 Both of the above mentioned operations also preserve enumeration in
the case of the pattern packing studied here, but there are a few
others that apply to all patterns given a fixed overlap. For example,
if the overlap  m m  is at most  K / 2 K/2  for a  K K -pattern then
pattern-packing enumeration is invariant under the action of switching
the  m m -suffix and  m m -prefix (Lemma  3.10 ), as
well as permuting the  m m -prefix and  m m -suffix of a  K K -pattern in
the same way (Lemma  3.8 ). In addition to
these, and some further general symmetries listed in
Section  3 , there seem to be myriad cases of
pattern/overlap combinations with the same enumeration without falling
under any of these general symmetries. Such equinumerations occur
both when we fix pattern length and overlap length and for pairs of
patterns of different lengths and with different overlaps, whereas for
avoidance of single patterns, Wilf equivalence is only possible for
patterns of the same length. 
 
 
 In Section  7  we mention several conjectured Wilf
equivalences that do not fall under any of the general symmetries we
exhibit in Section  3 . 
 
 
 
 2.3  Pattern-packing posets and enumerating their linear
extensions 

 
 In Section  5 , we show that the inequalities satisfied
by the elements of a permutation  m m -packing a pattern  π \pi  give rise
to a poset, which we term a  pattern-packing poset  (not to be
confused with the posets in  [ 6 ]  and earlier papers
cited there). These posets have previously been described for certain
classes of patterns by Dotsenko and
Khoroshkin  [ 3 ]  and by Elizalde and
Noy  [ 8 ] . While the structure of such a poset
depends on the underlying pattern, the pattern-packing posets enjoy
some general properties. For example, an element in such a poset can
cover (and be covered by) at most two elements. Furthermore, due to
the regularity of the underlying construction
(Definition  2.6 ), these posets themselves exhibit
regularity. As a result, pattern-packing posets can be classified
according to the structure of their Hasse diagrams. In particular, we
characterize patterns that give rise to trees
(Theorem  5.20 ), and construct patterns realizing
multilevel grids (Theorem  5.33 ). 
 
 
 For a given pattern  π \pi , the number of permutations  m m -packing  j j 
occurrences of  π \pi  equals the number of linear extensions of a poset
defined in terms of  π \pi ,  m m  and  j j . The regularity of the
pattern-packing posets enables us in some cases to explicitly count
their linear extensions, developing methods that we present in
Section  5 . The enumerative results take the form of: 
 
 ∙ \bullet  
 
 A polynomial-time algorithm, for any overlap  m ≤ K / 2 m\leq K/2 . (Theorem  4.7 .) 
 
 ∙ \bullet  
 
 A generating function, whenever
 m = 1 m=1 . (Theorem  4.1 .) 
 
 ∙ \bullet  
 
 Explicit enumeration formulas for specific families. 
 
 ∙ \bullet  
 
 Various integral formulas and matrix product formulas for
general classes of patterns (to appear in our forthcoming companion
paper  [ 2 ]  to this one).

 Remark 3.12 . 
 
 By
Lemma  3.8 , when  m ≤ K / 2 m\leq K/2  and  𝐩 ∼ 𝐬 \mathbf{p}\sim\mathbf{s} ,
we may sort the prefix, the suffix, and the letters between them,
respectively, to increasing order without affecting the enumeration of
permutations covered by  π \pi  for a given overlap. Without affecting
the enumeration, we may also swap the prefix and suffix, by
Lemma  3.10 , and also apply any of the symmetries
in Lemma  3.2 . In what follows most of the examples
and results we give for particular patterns or families of patterns
consist of the  canonical representative  of each equivalence
class with respect to these symmetries, which is the lexicographically
least pattern in the class. As an example, the canonical
representative of the class containing 37461285, for overlap 3, is
14738256, obtained by taking the reverse complement 41783526 of
37461285 and then sorting each of 417, 83 and 526 to increasing order.
This is equivalent to first switching prefix and suffix, then taking
complement and finally sorting each of the three parts. 
 
 
 
 Definition 3.13 .

 Lemma 3.14 . 
 
 Suppose  π \pi  and  π ′ \pi^{\prime}  are two  K K -patterns, each having
 m m -prefix isomorphic to its  m m -suffix where  K / 2 < m ≤ 2 ​ K / 3 K/2<m\leq 2K/3 . If the

their  m m -suffixes, then  π \pi  and  π ′ \pi^{\prime}  have the same enumeration. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Since  m > K / 2 m>K/2 , the  m m -prefix  𝐩 \mathbf{p}  and  m m -suffix  𝐬 \mathbf{s} 
of  π \pi  intersect in  ℓ = 2 ​ m − K \ell=2m-K  letters, and likewise for  π ′ \pi^{\prime} .
We denote the word consisting of this intersection by  π x \pi_{x} ,
respectively  π x ′ \pi^{\prime}_{x} . The content of  π x \pi_{x}  and  π x ′ \pi^{\prime}_{x}  must be
the same since the prefixes of  π \pi  and  π ′ \pi^{\prime}  have the same content
and their suffixes have the same content, respectively. 
 
 
 Let  π = π i ​ π a ​ π x ​ π b ​ π f \pi=\pi_{i}\pi_{a}\pi_{x}\pi_{b}\pi_{f} , where  π i , π x \pi_{i},\pi_{x}  and  π f \pi_{f} 
each have length  ℓ = 2 ​ m − K \ell=2m-K , so  π a \pi_{a}  and  π b \pi_{b}  each have length

 𝐩 = π i ​ π a ​ π x \mathbf{p}=\pi_{i}\pi_{a}\pi_{x}  and  𝐬 = π x ​ π b ​ π f \mathbf{s}=\pi_{x}\pi_{b}\pi_{f} , and therefore, since
 𝐩 \mathbf{p}  and  𝐬 \mathbf{s}  are order isomorphic,  π i , π x \pi_{i},\pi_{x}  and  π f \pi_{f}  must
all be order isomorphic, and  π a \pi_{a}  and  π b \pi_{b}  must be order
isomorphic, and likewise for  π ′ \pi^{\prime} . Note that if  m = 2 ​ K / 3 m=2K/3  (so

 5  Pattern-packing posets 

 
 In Table  1  we presented enumerative results for a
few patterns whose enumeration by combinatorial arguments is fairly
simple in the cases where we have explicit formulas, all of which are
presented in greater generality later in this section. For most
patterns, however, computing the enumeration is far from
straightforward, as they translate to very different combinatorial
problems, requiring distinct arguments and often diverse
techniques. Different patterns also pose vastly different levels of
challenge. While the explicit results in Table  1 
are low-hanging fruit, the other sequences, apart from 14325 with
overlap 2, were not known at the time of initiating this study (at
least not found in the  OEIS   [ 14 ] ), and that is the case for
the vast majority of sequences we have encountered. 
 
 
 In this section we show that the permutations  ( m , j ) (m,j) -packing a
pattern  π \pi  are in one-to-one correspondence with the  linear
extensions  of a poset determined by  π \pi ,  m m  and  j j , where the
poset for  j j  is an induced subposet of the one for  j + 1 j+1 . A linear
extension of a poset  𝒫 \mathcal{P}  is a total ordering  𝒪 \mathcal{O}  of its elements
so that if  x < y x<y  in  𝒫 \mathcal{P}  then  x x  precedes  y y  in  𝒪 \mathcal{O} . 
 
 
 This correspondence was previously described for certain classes of
patterns by Dotsenko and Khoroshkin  [ 3 ] 
and by Elizalde and Noy  [ 8 ]  and used to derive
generating functions counting packings of certain patterns. Here we
elicit several general structural properties of these posets and
characterize completely those patterns the Hasse diagrams of whose
posets are trees or, in particular, chains (total orders). These
structural properties in many cases suggest effective techniques for
enumerating linear extensions, which often explains why permutations
covered by different patterns have the same enumeration.

 5.1  The general structure of pattern
posets 

 
 The  dual  of a poset
 𝒫 \mathcal{P}  is the poset with the same elements as  𝒫 \mathcal{P}  where  y ≤ x y\leq x  if
and only if  x ≤ y x\leq y  in  𝒫 \mathcal{P} . The Hasse diagram of the dual of
 𝒫 \mathcal{P}  is obtained by turning the Hasse diagram of  𝒫 \mathcal{P}  upside down. 
 
 
 Remark 5.9 . 
 
 It is straightforward to
verify that apart from taking the complement of a pattern (dealt with
in the following Lemma  5.10 ), the maps proving the lemmas
about enumeration equivalences in Section  3  have
corresponding maps on the posets for the patterns in question that
give isomorphic posets, but with the labels of the elements permuted.
As an example, to obtain the poset of the reverse of a pattern  π \pi 
we complement its elements, that is, replace the largest label of an
element in  𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j)  with 1, the second largest with 2, and so on. 
 
 
 
 Lemma 5.10 . 
 
 The dual of the pattern poset  𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j) 
is the pattern poset of the complement  C ⁡ ( π ) C(\pi)  of  π \pi . 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 The reverse of the inverse of a permutation  π \pi  is the
inverse of the complement of  π \pi . Thus, the inverses of  π \pi  and
 C ⁡ ( π ) C(\pi)  are the reverses of each other. Therefore, each line of the
stack for  C ⁡ ( π ) C(\pi)  is the reverse of the corresponding line of the
stack for  π \pi , which implies that the order relations of the posets
(for a given number  j j  of occurrences) for  π \pi  and  C ⁡ ( π ) C(\pi)  are
each other’s opposites. Therefore, these posets are each other’s
duals.
∎ 
 
 
 
 Theorem 5.11 . 
 
 An element in  𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j)  can cover
at most two elements and can be covered by at most two elements. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 If an element  x x  in  𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j)  is covered by  y y , then
 y y  must immediately follow  x x  in  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 i {\mathsf{shift}}_{i}  for some  i i . We then
say that  y y  is a successor of  x x  (in  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 i {\mathsf{shift}}_{i} ). In order for an
element  x x  of  𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j)  to be covered by three different elements in
 𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j) ,  x x  must have three different successors, in
 𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 a , 𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 b {\mathsf{shift}}_{a},{\mathsf{shift}}_{b}  and  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 c {\mathsf{shift}}_{c} , respectively, for some  a < b < c a<b<c .
But since  x x  belongs to each of these shifts, every letter in
 𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 b {\mathsf{shift}}_{b}  that is smaller (in size) than  x x  must also belong to
 𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 a {\mathsf{shift}}_{a} , and every letter in  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 b {\mathsf{shift}}_{b}  that is greater than  x x 
must also belong to  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 c {\mathsf{shift}}_{c} . In particular, the successor  z z  of
 x x  in  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 b {\mathsf{shift}}_{b}  must also belong to either  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 a {\mathsf{shift}}_{a}  or  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 c {\mathsf{shift}}_{c} .
If  z z  also belongs to  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 a {\mathsf{shift}}_{a}  then  z z  and the successor of  x x  in
 𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 a {\mathsf{shift}}_{a}  are comparable and so they can’t both cover  x x  in
 𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j) . The case where  z z  belongs to  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 c {\mathsf{shift}}_{c}  is analogous, and so
no element of  𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j)  can be covered by three different elements.
Since the dual of a pattern poset is a pattern poset
(Lemma  5.10 ), no letter can cover more than two letters.
∎

 An  upper bound  of two elements  a , b a,b  in a poset  𝒫 \mathcal{P}  is an
element  x x  such that  a , b ≤ x a,b\leq x . A  least upper bound  of  a a

 y y  of  a a  and  b b . A  greatest lower bound  is defined
analogously, with ’ ≤ \leq ’ replaced by ’ ≥ \geq ’. An order theoretic
 lattice  is a poset where each pair of elements has a least
upper bound and a greatest lower bound. 
 
 
 Theorem 5.18 . 
 
 The core of a pattern poset
 𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j)  is a lattice. In particular, if the  m m -suffix and  m m -prefix
of  π \pi  between them contain both 1 and  K K  then  𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j)  is a
lattice. Also, the poset obtained from  𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j)  by adding an element
greater than all elements of  𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j)  and an element smaller than all
elements of  𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j)  is a lattice. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Since the core has a bottom and a top, every pair of its
elements has an upper and a lower bound. We show, more generally, that
whenever two elements of  𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j)  have common upper bounds, they have a
least upper bound. 
 
 
 Suppose  a , b a,b  have common upper bounds but no least upper bound.
They must therefore have two distinct common upper bounds  x x  and  y y 
that are minimal with respect to being common upper bounds, and they
are necessarily incomparable. Choose four saturated chains, from  a a 
to  x x ,  a a  to  y y ,  b b  to  x x  and  b b  to  y y . 
 
 
 Every element other than  x , y x,y  on either chain from  a a  is
incomparable to every element other than  x , y x,y  on either chain from
 b b . Namely, if  u u  is on a chain from  a a  and  v v  on a chain from
 b b , with  u ≤ v u\leq v , then  v v  is a common upper bound for  a , b a,b 
strictly below  x x  or  y y , contradicting minimality. The case  v ≤ u v\leq u 
is analogous. 
 
 
 Consecutive elements of each chain appear together on some line of the
stack, since their comparison is a cover relation, as the chains are
saturated. By Lemma  5.7 , the lines containing
elements of either chain from  a a , with  x , y x,y  omitted, form an
interval of lines, for each chain meets consecutive lines, and the two
chains meet at  a a . The same holds for the chains from  b b . These two
intervals are disjoint, by the incomparability established
above. Assume, without loss of generality, that the interval for the
chains from  a a  precedes that for the chains from  b b . 
 
 
 Let  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 r {\mathsf{shift}}_{r}  be the last line of the former interval. Each of  x , y x,y 
occurs on a line in each interval, since it occurs together with its
predecessor on each of the two chains ending there. By
Lemma  5.7 , both  x x  and  y y  therefore occur on
 𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 r {\mathsf{shift}}_{r} . This makes them comparable, a contradiction. Hence  a , b a,b 
have a least upper bound. Since the dual of a pattern poset is a
pattern poset this also applies to greatest lower bounds. 
 
 
 If the prefix and suffix between them contain both  1 1  and  K K  then
the core is the whole poset, so  𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j)  is a lattice. 
 
 
 After adjoining  1 ^ \widehat{1}  and  0 ^ \widehat{0} , existing least upper and
greatest lower bounds remain unchanged. A pair with no common upper
bound now has least upper bound  1 ^ \widehat{1} , and a pair with no common
lower bound has greatest lower bound  0 ^ \widehat{0} , since these are
common bounds, and the only possible such. The resulting poset is
therefore a lattice.
∎

least upper (and greatest lower) bounds.

 The following theorem characterizes the trees among pattern posets.
The idea of the proof is that having a cycle in the Hasse diagram,
seen as a graph, implies that some four elements,  a < x , y < b a<x,y<b , of that
cycle must satisfy the conditions in the theorem, and conversely. We
first need a lemma. 
 
 
 A  simple cycle  in a graph is a sequence of vertices, each
connected by edges to its predecessor and successor, and such that the
only repeated vertex in the sequence is the first and last one. If a
graph has a cycle then it necessarily has a simple cycle. A chain  C C 
is  saturated  if no element of the poset lies strictly between
two consecutive elements of  C C . A chain  x 1 < x 2 < ⋯ < x n x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{n}  being
saturated is equivalent to each of its inequalities being a cover
relation. Note that if  a < b a<b  is a cover relation in a pattern poset
then  a a  must immediately precede  b b  on some line of the stack. The
converse is not true in general, because we might have another element
 x x  between  a a  and  b b  on some other line of the stack. 
 
 
 In the remainder of the paper, the  interior  of a vector or
sequence of any sort is that thing itself take away its first and last
element. 
 
 
 Lemma 5.19 . 
 
 If a pattern poset  𝒫 \mathcal{P}  is
not a tree then it has a simple cycle that consists of the two
elements  a a  and  b b  and two saturated chains  C C  and  D D  that are
disjoint apart from both having  a a  as minimum and  b b  as maximum, and
such that each element in the interior of  C C  is incomparable to every
element in the interior of  D D . 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 If a pattern poset is not a tree then it has a simple
cycle, seen as a graph, and such a cycle must belong entirely to the
core of  𝒫 \mathcal{P} . Namely, a cycle that wanders onto a branch can only
be closed by leaving that branch again, via its only edge connected to
the core, and so would not be simple. 
 
 
 Let  a a  be the smallest element of the core of  𝒫 \mathcal{P}  that is covered
by two different elements  x 1 x_{1}  and  y 1 y_{1} . (If  a a  is not the bottom
element of  𝒫 \mathcal{P}  then it is the maximum element in the chain of
elements at the bottom of  𝒫 \mathcal{P}  all of whose other elements are
covered by only one element.) Since the core of  𝒫 \mathcal{P}  is a lattice,
by Theorem  5.18 ,  x 1 x_{1}  and  y 1 y_{1}  have a least upper
bound  b b . Then  𝒫 \mathcal{P}  has two saturated chains

 Theorem 5.20 . 
 
 A pattern poset  𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j)  has a
cycle if and only if there are two lines  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 s , 𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 t {\mathsf{shift}}_{s},{\mathsf{shift}}_{t} , with
 s < t s<t , and elements  a , b , x , y a,b,x,y  satisfying the following conditions: 
 
 ∙ \bullet  
 
 No line contains both  x x  and  y y . 
 
 ∙ \bullet  
 
 On  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 s {\mathsf{shift}}_{s} ,  x x  lies between  a a  and  b b . 
 
 ∙ \bullet  
 
 On  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 t {\mathsf{shift}}_{t} ,  y y  lies between  a a  and  b b . 
 
 ∙ \bullet  
 
 No element lies between  a a  and  b b  on two distinct lines among
 𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 s , 𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 s + 1 , … , 𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 t {\mathsf{shift}}_{s},{\mathsf{shift}}_{s+1},\ldots,{\mathsf{shift}}_{t} . 
 
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Suppose first that there are elements and lines as
described. We show that  x x  and  y y  are incomparable. 
 
 
 Any comparison between  x x  and  y y  can be established using only the
lines from  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 s {\mathsf{shift}}_{s}  to  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 t {\mathsf{shift}}_{t} . Indeed, any part of a comparison
passing through earlier lines has its entry and exit letters on
 𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 s {\mathsf{shift}}_{s} , and can therefore be replaced by their comparison on that
line. The same argument applies to lines after  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 t {\mathsf{shift}}_{t} . 
 
 
 If  x x  and  y y  were comparable, a sequence of comparisons on these
lines would consequently establish  x < y x<y , or  y < x y<x . Since no line
contains both, this sequence must pass from one line to another at
some intermediate element  z z . But  z z  then lies strictly between  a a 
and  b b  on both lines, contrary to the last condition. Thus  x x  and
 y y  are incomparable. Since both lie between  a a  and  b b , there are
two distinct saturated chains from  a a  to  b b , whose union contains a
cycle. Hence  𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j)  is not a tree. 
 
 
 Conversely, suppose  𝒫 π m ​ ( j ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(j)  is not a tree. By
Lemma  5.19 , it has an interval that is not a
chain. Choose such an interval  [ a , b ] [a,b]  with as few elements as
possible. Every proper subinterval is then a chain. 
 
 
 Distinct maximal chains in  [ a , b ] [a,b]  can meet only at  a a  and  b b , and
their interior elements are pairwise incomparable. Otherwise, a
meeting or comparison between them would give two distinct maximal
chains in a proper subinterval. Since  a a  is covered by at most two
elements,  [ a , b ] [a,b]  therefore consists of exactly two saturated chains
 C C  and  D D , disjoint apart from their endpoints. 
 
 
 The lines containing interior elements of  C C  are consecutive: each
element occurs on consecutive lines, and consecutive elements of  C C 
occur together on some line because their comparison is a cover
relation. The same holds for  D D . These two sets of lines are
disjoint, since no line can contain incomparable elements. We may
assume that the lines containing interior elements of  C C  precede
those containing interior elements of  D D . 
 
 
 Let  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 s {\mathsf{shift}}_{s}  be the last line containing an interior element  x x  of
 C C , and let  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 t {\mathsf{shift}}_{t}  be the first line containing an interior
element  y y  of  D D . Each of  a a  and  b b  occurs on a line with an
interior element of each chain, by the cover relations at the ends of
the chains. Consequently, both  a a  and  b b  occur on every line from
 𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 s {\mathsf{shift}}_{s}  to  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 t {\mathsf{shift}}_{t} . Thus  x x  and  y y  lie between  a a  and  b b  on
their respective lines, and no line contains both. 
 
 
 Finally, every element strictly between  a a  and  b b  belongs to  C C  or
 D D . Among the lines from  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 s {\mathsf{shift}}_{s}  to  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 t {\mathsf{shift}}_{t} , an interior
element of  C C  can occur only on  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 s {\mathsf{shift}}_{s} , and an interior element
of  D D  only on  𝗌𝗁𝗂𝖿𝗍 t {\mathsf{shift}}_{t} . No such element can therefore occur on two
of these lines. This proves the last condition and completes the
proof.
∎

 5.3  Shifted grid posets 

 
 There is a standard partial order on the integer lattice  ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}^{2}  where
 ( x , y ) (x,y)  covers both  ( x − 1 , y ) (x-1,y)  and  ( x , y − 1 ) (x,y-1)  for all  x x  and  y y . Its

 𝒫 \mathcal{P}  can be embedded in  ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}^{2}  if  ℋ \mathcal{H}  can be obtained from a
connected subset of the Hasse diagram of  ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}^{2}  by adding vertices to
some of its edges. An example is given in Figure  5.7 .

 Lemma 5.30 . 
 
 Let  π \pi  be a  K K -pattern,  m ≤ K / 2 m\leq K/2  and let  𝐝𝐩 \mathbf{dp}  and  𝐝𝐬 \mathbf{ds}  be the interiors of the gap vectors of
its  m m -prefix  𝐩 \mathbf{p}  and  m m -suffix  𝐬 \mathbf{s} . The number of simple
cycles in  𝒫 π m ​ ( 2 ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(2)  equals the number of indices  i i  such that
 𝐝𝐩 i \mathbf{dp}_{i}  and  𝐝𝐬 i \mathbf{ds}_{i}  are both positive. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 A cycle in  𝒫 π m ​ ( 2 ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(2)  must have elements from both the
first and second lines of its stack, since each line forms a chain in
 𝒫 π m ​ ( 2 ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(2) . The minimal and maximal elements of such a cycle must
belong to both lines, and thus can only be the letters
 K − m + 1 , K − m + 2 , … , K K-m+1,K-m+2,\ldots,K . 
 
 
 By symmetry we may assume that  𝐩 \mathbf{p}  and  𝐬 \mathbf{s}  are increasing, so
these common letters appear in increasing order on both lines, call
them  c 1 < c 2 < ⋯ < c m c_{1}<c_{2}<\cdots<c_{m} . Each common letter separates the preceding
gaps from the following ones, so a simple cycle cannot extend across
an intermediate common letter. 
 
 
 In order for there to be a simple cycle with two of these elements,  c i c_{i}  and
 c i + 1 c_{i+1} , as minimum and maximum, there must be, on each of the two lines, a
nonempty set of elements strictly between  c i c_{i}  and  c i + 1 c_{i+1} . On the first
line, the places of  c i c_{i}  and  c i + 1 c_{i+1}  are determined by  𝐬 i \mathbf{s}_{i}  and
 𝐬 i + 1 \mathbf{s}_{i+1} , which must differ by at least 2 in order for there to be some
elements strictly between  c i c_{i}  and  c i + 1 c_{i+1}  on the first line, which is
equivalent to  𝐝𝐬 i > 0 \mathbf{ds}_{i}>0 . Conversely, if  𝐝𝐬 i = 0 \mathbf{ds}_{i}=0  then  c i c_{i}  and  c i + 1 c_{i+1} 
are adjacent on the first line, so there are no letters between them that can
form one of the two chains of a cycle in  𝒫 π m ​ ( 2 ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(2) . 
 
 
 On the second line, the places of  c i c_{i}  are determined by  𝐩 \mathbf{p} , and an
analogous argument shows that  𝐝𝐩 i \mathbf{dp}_{i}  must be positive in order for there to be
elements strictly between  c i c_{i}  and  c i + 1 c_{i+1}  that contribute to a cycle in
 𝒫 π m ​ ( 2 ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(2) .
∎

 𝒫 π m ​ ( 2 ) {\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{m}(2)  seen as a simplicial complex, that is, equals the rank of

 6  Pattern cover with varying overlap size 

 
 So far, the special cases considered involve pattern packing with a
fixed overlap  m m . For some classes of patterns, we observe a further
decoupling that allows us to more directly ‘upgrade’ the results
obtained for regular overlaps to arbitrary ones. In particular, let
 π \pi  be a  K K -pattern with auto-correlation at least 2. Tracking only
the occurrences of  π \pi  arising from overlaps of sizes  1 1  and  2 2 ,
we can express the vector of successive overlaps in terms of runs of
1s and 2s as 
 

 
 
 1 ¯ r 1 , 2 ¯ q 1 , … , 1 ¯ r a , 2 ¯ q a \underline{1}_{r_{1}},\underline{2}_{q_{1}},\ldots,\underline{1}_{r_{a}},\underline{2}_{q_{a}} 
 
 
 where  a ≥ 1 a\geq 1 ,  r 1 , q a ≥ 0 r_{1},q_{a}\geq 0 , and  q i , r i + 1 ≥ 1 q_{i},r_{i+1}\geq 1  for  1 ≤ i < a 1\leq i<a  and
 x ¯ n \underline{x}_{n}  is the word  x , … , x x,\ldots,x  of length  n n  (or the empty
word when  n = 0 n=0 ). 
 
 
 Proposition 6.1 . 
 
 Let  π \pi  be a  K K -pattern
of the form  1 ​ σ ​ K 1\sigma K  with  K ≥ 3 K\geq 3 . Let
 1 ¯ r 1 , 2 ¯ q 1 , … , 1 ¯ r a , 2 ¯ q a \underline{1}_{r_{1}},\underline{2}_{q_{1}},\ldots,\underline{1}_{r_{a}},\underline{2}_{q_{a}} 
specify all successive prescribed overlaps, disregarding potential additional
occurrences. Then the number of such permutations is given by the product 
 

 
 
 N q 1 ⋅ N q 2 ⋅ ⋯ ⋅ N q a , N_{q_{1}}\cdot N_{q_{2}}\cdot\cdots\cdot N_{q_{a}}, 
 
 
 where  N q = | 𝒮 π 2 ​ ( q + 1 ) | N_{q}=|\mathcal{S}_{\pi}^{2}(q+1)|  for any  q ≥ 0 q\geq 0 . 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Given a permutation  σ \sigma  of this form we claim that
the letter  ℓ \ell  of the leftmost overlap of size 1 in  σ \sigma  is
greater than all letters preceding  ℓ \ell . Now,  ℓ \ell  is clearly
greater than all the letters in the occurrence of  π \pi  of which
 ℓ \ell  is the last letter. Therefore, if this occurrence overlaps in
two letters with a preceding occurrence,  ℓ \ell  is larger than both
letters in that overlap. In particular, it is larger than the last
letter of that overlap, which in turn is greater than all other
letters in that preceding occurrence. An inductive argument now shows
that  ℓ \ell  must be greater than all the letters preceding it in
 σ \sigma . An analogous argument shows that  ℓ \ell  is smaller than all
letters following it in  σ \sigma . 
 
 
 This determines which subset of letters of  σ \sigma  belongs to each of
the segments between successive 1-overlaps in  σ \sigma . A segment
between two successive 1-overlaps is thus a permutation covered by
 π \pi  with overlaps 2, whose number is  N i N_{i}  where  i i  is the number
of 2-overlaps in that segment. Since the ordering of letters in such
a segment is independent of the ordering in any other such the proof
follows.
∎ 
 
 
 
 Therefore, for  π = 1324 \pi=1324 , for example, which can have overlaps of
sizes 1 and 2 only, we obtain the full analogue of the enumeration by
descent set where we count permutations with occurrences of  π \pi  in
arbitrary prescribed positions, using
Proposition  5.37 : 
 
 
 Corollary 6.2 . 
 
 The number of permutations
 σ \sigma  with occurrences of the consecutive pattern 1324 prescribed
by the overlaps
 1 ¯ r 1 , 2 ¯ q 1 , … , 1 ¯ r a , 2 ¯ q a \underline{1}_{r_{1}},\underline{2}_{q_{1}},\ldots,\underline{1}_{r_{a}},\underline{2}_{q_{a}} 
is 
 

 
 
 C q 1 + 1 ⋅ C q 2 + 1 ⋅ ⋯ ⋅ C q a + 1 . C_{q_{1}+1}\cdot C_{q_{2}+1}\cdot\cdots\cdot C_{q_{a}+1}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 For non-overlapping occurrences, the picture is even simpler; see
Remark  4.8 . 
 
 
 
 7  Open problems, questions and remarks 

 
 1.   Lemma  5.10  and Theorems  5.11 ,
 5.16  and  5.18  provide
restrictions that apply to all pattern posets. Is it possible to give
a complete characterization? 
 
 
 2.   For which pattern-overlap combinations can we generalize
Proposition  6.1  about varying overlaps? 
 
 
 3.   Obviously we can, in a trivial sense, compute the enumeration
for a pattern from its gap vectors, since they determine prefix and
suffix (see the paragraph preceding Theorem  5.23 ).
For the patterns in Proposition  5.25  the
product formula is easily computed directly from the gap vectors and
the same is true of the patterns in
Proposition  5.28 . Are there other classes of
patterns whose enumeration can be derived in reasonably simple ways
from their gap vectors?

 For overlap  m ≤ K / 2 m\leq K/2 , the
maximum is obtained when prefix and suffix are interleaved and
minimal, that is, for patterns with prefix  135 ​ … ​ ( 2 ​ m − 1 ) 135\ldots(2m-1)  and
