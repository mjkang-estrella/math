                                                                                           Abstract
                                                                             ∗
                                                    The saturation number µ (G) of a graph G is the minimum cardinality of a maximal match-
                                                ing, and H(G) is its harmonic index. TxGraffiti conjectured in 2023 that µ∗ (G) ≤ H(G)
                                                for every nontrivial connected graph G, and Bıyıkoğlu refuted this by showing that the ratio
                                                µ∗ (G)/H(G) can be made arbitrarily large. Restricting to trees bounds the ratio sharply. Every
                                                nontrivial tree T satisfies µ∗ (T ) < 32 H(T ), with the constant 3/2 best possible. A complemen-
                                                tary bound H(G) < 4µ∗ (G) holds for every graph with an edge, so on a nontrivial tree the
                                                saturation number is pinned to 41 H(T ) < µ∗ (T ) < 32 H(T ), both constants best possible. The
                                                friendship graph F4 is a smallest counterexample to the conjecture, on nine vertices, and the
                                                smallest tree counterexample is the subdivided star on eleven vertices. For each positive inte-
                                                ger m a family of graphs with m hubs has ratio approaching m + 1, while the conjecture holds
                                                whenever all vertices have equal degree. Both invariants arise in applications, the harmonic
                                                index as a molecular descriptor and the saturation number as a measure of adsorption ineffi-
                                                ciency, and the bounds estimate the latter, which is NP-hard to compute, by the former, which
                                                is computable in linear time.


                                           1    Introduction
                                           Fajtlowicz’s Graffiti [Faj88] makes conjectures by searching a database of graphs for a relation
                                           between invariants, usually an inequality, that no stored graph violates, then offering the surviving
                                           relation as a conjecture. The system TxGraffiti [Dav26], inspired by Graffiti, has produced con-
                                           jectures that became published theorems relating domination numbers, zero forcing numbers, and
                                           matching parameters of graphs. Davila, Brimkov, and Pepper [DBP25] selected four of its most
                                           durable open conjectures. The present paper concerns the fourth and most recent of these, posed
                                           in 2023. All graphs considered here are finite and simple. For a graph G, E(G) is its edge set, n
                                           the number of vertices, and d(v) the degree of a vertex v.
                                           Conjecture ([DBP25, Conjecture 4]). Let G be a nontrivial connected graph. The saturation
                                           number µ∗ (G), P
                                                          the minimum cardinality of a maximal matching in G, satisfies µ∗ (G) ≤ H(G),
                                           where H(G) = {u,v}∈E(G) 2/(d(u) + d(v)) is the harmonic index.
                                           The conjecture was checked against a dataset of 335 connected graphs [DBP25] without a counterex-
                                           ample. It was subsequently disproved by Bıyıkoğlu [Bıy26], who showed that the join of a disjoint
                                           union of edges with an independent set satisfies µ∗ > H for a range of sizes, with unbounded
                                           violation ratio µ∗ /H. Each such join contains a triangle, since every vertex of the independent set
                                           is adjacent to both endpoints of every edge of the union.

        The saturation number has been studied as a graph invariant [AS18],1 and satisfies the identity
µ∗ (G) = i(L(G)), where i(L(G)) is the independent domination number of the line graph [DBP25,
TD23]. The harmonic index was introduced by Fajtlowicz [Faj87] and has since been studied in
chemical and extremal graph theory [Zho12, Ili12]. Prior work relates the harmonic index to the
matching number ν(G), the maximum cardinality of a matching, by determining the trees and
unicyclic graphs of least harmonic index among those with a prescribed matching number [LL14,
LLS14]; the general sum-connectivity index χa for a ≥ 0 is likewise maximised over bipartite graphs
with a given vertex cover number [Vet23], the harmonic index corresponding to a = −1. These
extremal questions, with a parameter held fixed, differ from bounding one invariant by the other.
The saturation number is moreover a different and smaller parameter, with µ∗ (G) ≤ ν(G) for every
graph. For the path P4 the three invariants are µ∗ = 1, ν = 2, and H = 11/6, so µ∗ < ν while
ν itself exceeds H. Beyond the conjecture itself, no bound between the harmonic index and the
saturation number appears to have been established; in particular, none appears in the survey of
harmonic-index bounds [AZG19]. Theorem 5 bounds it below for every graph, and Theorem 2
above for trees.
    Every nontrivial tree T satisfies µ∗ (T ) < 32 H(T ) (Theorem 2), and the constant 3/2 cannot be
lowered, since the subdivided stars approach it (Theorem 1). The harmonic index also bounds the
saturation number from below, with H(G) < 4µ∗ (G) for every graph with an edge (Theorem 5),
sharp for the balanced double stars, so a nontrivial tree satisfies 14 H(T ) < µ∗ (T ) < 32 H(T ). The
subdivided star S5 , on eleven vertices, is the smallest tree on which the conjecture fails. The friend-
ship graph F4 is a counterexample on nine vertices, the smallest order admitting one (Theorem 3),
and no counterexample of maximum degree at most four arises on up to eleven vertices. For every
positive integer m a family of graphs with m hubs has µ∗ /H tending to m + 1 (Theorem 4). In
the opposite direction, the conjecture holds for every regular graph (Theorem 6). Section 6 relates
both invariants to their origins outside graph theory.

Proof. For the saturation number, the k outer edges {mi , ℓi }, joining each middle vertex mi to its
leaf ℓi , saturate every middle and leaf vertex. The only unsaturated vertex is the hub, and each of
its neighbours mi is saturated, so these k edges form a maximal matching and µ∗ (Sk ) ≤ k. For the
lower bound, let M be any maximal matching. The hub is incident only to the edges {hub, mi },
so M saturates the hub through at most one leg. For every leg i not carrying that hub edge,
neither mi nor ℓi is saturated by a hub edge, and ℓi has no other neighbour, so maximality forces
{mi , ℓi } ∈ M , since otherwise this edge could be added. Hence M contains the outer edge of all but
at most one leg, and the remaining leg contributes its hub edge, so |M | ≥ k. Therefore µ∗ (Sk ) = k.
    For the harmonic index, each of the k edges {hub, mi } joins the degree-k hub to a degree-2
middle vertex, contributing 2/(k + 2), and each of the k outer edges joins a degree-2 to a degree-1
vertex, contributing 2/3. Summing gives H(Sk ) = 2k/(k + 2) + 2k/3.
    The inequality µ∗ (Sk ) ≤ H(Sk ) reads k ≤ 2k/(k + 2) + 2k/3, that is, 1/3 ≤ 2/(k + 2), which
holds if and only if k ≤ 4, with equality at k = 4. Dividing, µ∗ (Sk )/H(Sk ) = 1/[ 2/(k + 2) + 2/3 ],
which is strictly below 3/2 for every finite k and tends to 3/2 as k → ∞. As a tree, Sk is bipartite
and triangle-free.

3    General graphs
The friendship graph Fk , also called the windmill graph, consists of k triangles sharing a single hub
vertex. It is the case of Bıyıkoğlu’s construction in which the independent set is a single vertex,
and has n = 2k + 1 vertices and 3k edges, one hub of degree 2k and 2k rim vertices of degree 2.

Theorem 3 (Saturation number and harmonic index of Fk ). For the friendship graph Fk (k ≥ 1),

Consequently, µ∗ (Fk ) ≤ H(Fk ) if and only if k ≤ 3, with equality at k = 3 (F3 , seven vertices).

Proof. The hub is the only vertex shared by two triangles, and it is not a rim vertex, so the k
rim pairs impose independent demands on any maximal matching (Figure 3). The k rim edges
form a maximal matching of size k, because every rim vertex is saturated and so no spoke can
extend the matching. For the lower bound, each rim edge ai bi is the unique edge both of whose
endpoints lie in triangle i’s rim, so a maximal matching must cover at least one endpoint of ai bi
for each i, since otherwise ai bi could be added to the matching. Each rim edge lies in one triangle,
and a matching edge incident to the hub saturates rim vertices in at most one triangle, so each

matching edge covers at most one of the k disjoint rim pairs. Hence at least k matching edges are
needed and µ∗ (Fk ) = k. For the harmonic index, each spoke joins the hub (degree 2k) to a rim
vertex (degree 2), contributing 2/(2k + 2) = 1/(k + 1), and each rim edge joins two rim vertices,
contributing 2/(2 + 2) = 1/2. The 2k spokes and k rim edges give H(Fk ) = 2k/(k + 1) + k/2.
Rearranging, µ∗ (Fk ) ≤ H(Fk ) reduces to k(k − 3) ≤ 0, which holds if and only if k ≤ 3.

    In particular, F4 is a counterexample on nine vertices. An exhaustive enumeration of all non-
isomorphic connected graphs on n ≤ 8 vertices, produced by the program geng from the nauty
suite [MP14], confirms µ∗ (G) ≤ H(G) for every such graph. The connected-graph counts agree
with OEIS A0013493 at each order, a total of 12,112 graphs for n = 2, . . . , 8. Nine vertices is
therefore the smallest order admitting a counterexample. At n = 9 (261,080 graphs), exactly
eight counterexamples appear, all with µ∗ = 4 and each having a unique vertex of maximum
degree. Among them F4 is the unique friendship graph, and it attains the smallest harmonic index
H = 18/5, giving the largest violation ratio µ∗ /H = 10/9 ≈ 1.111. Each of these eight graphs has
maximum degree at least five. Harmonic indices are computed in exact rational arithmetic, so every
equality and every violation is decided without rounding. The saturation number is computed both
through the identity µ∗ (G) = i(L(G)) and by direct enumeration of minimum maximal matchings,
and the two methods agree on every graph.
    For all k ≥ 1, µ∗ (Fk )/H(Fk ) = 2(k + 1)/(k + 5) < 2. Enlarging the single hub into an
independent set of m hubs removes this ceiling.

Corollary 1. For every constant c > 0 there exists a connected graph G with µ∗ (G) > c · H(G).

Proof. Given c > 0, fix an integer m > c. By Theorem 4, µ∗ (Gm,k )/H(Gm,k ) → m + 1 as k → ∞,
and m + 1 > c, so µ∗ (Gm,k ) > c · H(Gm,k ) for all sufficiently large k.

Remark 1. The generalized windmill coincides with the construction of Bıyıkoğlu [Bıy26]. The join
of k disjoint edges with an independent set of m vertices is exactly Gm,k , with the m independent
vertices serving as the hubs and the k disjoint edges as the blade pairs. Theorem 4 reproves the
unbounded separation and adds the exact limiting ratio m + 1.
    In both families the k edges of a minimum maximal matching determine the limiting ratio,
which equals the reciprocal of their common harmonic weight. For the subdivided star these are
the outer edges, each ending in a leaf with weight 2/3, so the limit is 3/2. For the generalized
windmill they are the blade edges, joining two degree-(m + 1) vertices with weight 1/(m + 1), so
the limit m + 1 grows without bound. The saturation number can thus exceed the harmonic index
by any factor, yet it cannot fall far below it.

4    Regular graphs
Every counterexample above has vertices of unequal degrees. The friendship graph Fk has hub
degree 2k and rim degree 2, the windmill Gm,k has hub degree 2k and blade degree m + 1, and the
subdivided star Sk has degrees k, 2, and 1. When all degrees are equal, the conjecture holds.

Theorem 6 (Regular graphs). If G is a connected graph in which every vertex has degree r (r ≥ 1),
then H(G) = n/2, and consequently µ∗ (G) ≤ ⌊n/2⌋ ≤ H(G).

Proof. Every edge {u, v} has d(u) + d(v) = 2r, so each edge contributes 2/(2r) = 1/r to the
harmonic index. The graph has nr/2 edges, so H(G) = (nr/2)/r = n/2. Since every matching has
at most ⌊n/2⌋ edges, µ∗ (G) ≤ ⌊n/2⌋ ≤ n/2 = H(G).

   Equality µ∗ (G) = H(G) = n/2 forces n even and every maximal matching to be perfect. Such
graphs are called randomly matchable. Sumner [Sum79] proved that the only connected randomly
matchable graphs are the complete graph K2t and the balanced complete bipartite graph Kt,t . An
exhaustive verification on all 54 connected regular graphs with at most nine vertices confirms that
H = n/2 holds exactly, that the conjecture holds for each, and that equality µ∗ = n/2 occurs
precisely for K2 , K4 , C4 ∼
                           = K2,2 , K6 , K3,3 , K8 , K4,4 . These graphs are all of the form K2t or Kt,t ,
consistent with Sumner’s theorem.


5    Graphs of maximum degree four
An exhaustive search finds no counterexample to µ∗ ≤ H of maximum degree at most four on up to
eleven vertices, in contrast to the smallest counterexamples without that restriction, which appear
already on nine vertices (Section 3). This suggests the inequality holds throughout the class of
subquartic graphs, those of maximum degree at most four.

Conjecture 1 (Subquartic graphs). Every connected graph of maximum degree at most four sat-
isfies µ∗ (G) ≤ H(G).

    Among the graphs of maximum degree at most four on at most nine vertices, equality µ∗ = H
holds for exactly six: the complete graphs K2 and K4 , the four-cycle C4 , the complete bipartite
graphs K3,3 and K4,4 , and the subdivided star S4 . The first five are regular and of the form K2t
or Kt,t (Theorem 6), while the subdivided star S4 is the only non-regular extremal graph. A proof
of Conjecture 1 must therefore meet equality both at the regular graphs of Theorem 6 and at the
subdivided star S4 .

6    Discussion
Both invariants, the saturation number and the harmonic index, carry meaning outside graph the-
ory. The harmonic index arose as a molecular descriptor and is used in chemical graph theory to
relate the structure of a compound to its properties [Faj87, Zho12]. The saturation number has
a separate, physical reading. A maximal matching models a jammed state of diatomic molecules
adsorbed onto a substrate, each molecule occupying a pair of adjacent sites, and the saturation
number is the size of the smallest such state, a measure of the worst-case inefficiency of adsorp-
tion [AK17]. The inequality µ∗ ≤ H and the bounds proved above thus connect two quantities of
independent applied origin.
    The two quantities differ sharply in computational cost. The harmonic index is computed in
linear time, by summing one term over each edge. The saturation number, equal to the independent
domination number of the line graph (Section 1), is NP-hard to compute, and remains so on planar
graphs of maximum degree three [AK17]. The bounds established above therefore estimate an
NP-hard quantity by a linear-time one. The universal inequality H(G) < 4µ∗ (G) yields the lower
bound µ∗ (G) > 41 H(G) for every graph. On trees, where the saturation number is itself computable
in polynomial time, the two-sided bound 41 H(T ) < µ∗ (T ) < 32 H(T ) confines it to within a constant
factor of the harmonic index, both constants best possible. The generalized windmills show that
the upper bound does not extend to all graphs, since a single high-degree hub drives µ∗ /H above
any constant.
    These bounds are structural, not algorithmic, and do not by themselves improve adsorption
models or domination algorithms. Their interest lies in that a linear-time degree quantity brackets
an NP-hard matching invariant, sharply on trees and from below on every graph, and that the
bracket fails outside the tree class in a controlled way, through vertices of high degree.


7    Conclusion
TxGraffiti’s conjecture is false, but its failure is structured. A connected graph violates µ∗ ≤ H
through the edges that a maximal matching is forced to use, and the size of the violation tracks
the harmonic weight of those edges. When the forcing edges have high-degree endpoints, as in
the generalized windmill, their weight vanishes and µ∗ /H grows without bound, so no degree-
independent correction µ∗ ≤ c H can hold (Corollary 1). The reverse inequality needs no such
correction: each saturated vertex absorbs harmonic weight below 2, so H(G) < 4µ∗ (G) for every
graph with an edge (Theorem 5), and the harmonic index bounds the saturation number from
below universally. When every degree is equal the bound holds outright (Theorem 6), and on trees,
where the forcing edges end in leaves, it holds up to the constant 3/2 (Theorem 2), which is best
possible. An upper bound valid for every connected graph must therefore temper the harmonic
index by a measure of degree disparity. Identifying the right measure remains open.
