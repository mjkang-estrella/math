                                        1. Introduction
1.1. Avant-propos. The center problem is a basic problem in graph theory, computational ge-
ometry, and optimization. It naturally arises in facility location, communication and transporta-
tion networks, network analysis, robot-motion planning, and other areas. The center problem
can be defined for any metric space (X, d). In the unweighted version, given a finite set P ⊆ X
of points, it consists in computing a point c ∈ X minimizing the largest distance from c to a
point of P . In the weighted version, each point of P has a positive multiplicative weight and one
seeks to compute a point c ∈ X minimizing the largest weighted distance from c to a point of
P . The goal of the center problem is to design efficient algorithms for computing c. The center
problem has a long history, both in discrete and continuous settings. Sylvester [96] formulated
in 1857 the center problem in the Euclidean plane (the smallest enclosing circle). Around the
same time, Jordan [74] defined the center of a tree and established that it always consists of
one or two adjacent vertices located in the middle of a diametral path. The center problem was
extensively studied in Euclidean spaces, in polygons (simple or with holes), and more relevant to
this work, in graphs. Under plausible computational assumptions, it was shown in [2] that the
unweighted center problem in graphs cannot be solved in subquadratic time. However, faster
algorithms can be designed if the radius function has additional properties. Unimodality of a
function (i.e., the property that its local minima are global minima) is at the heart of descent
methods of optimization. These methods have numerous variations and specifications and, more
importantly, numerous practical applications in optimization, combinatorial optimization, oper-
ations research, machine learning, statistics, and discrete and metric geometry. Convex functions
constitute the most common setting of unimodality. The distance function of a normed space
is an example of a convex function; the objective functions in facility location problems (center,
median, and p-moments) in normed spaces are thus convex and can be minimized by descent
algorithms. The radius functions in trees and in simple polygons with geodesic metric are also
convex (see [97] and [90]); [90] also establishes that simple polygons satisfy other properties of
CAT(0) metric spaces (e.g., convexity of balls); convexity of balls also holds in simple polygons
with link metric [77]. In fact, metric trees and simple polygons are CAT(0) and the convexity
of the distance function is among the most important and useful properties of CAT(0) geome-
tries [22,89], an important type of metric geometries extensively investigated in geometric group
theory and metric graph theory.
   In this paper, we introduce the Gp -unimodality of the (weighted and unweighted) radius
functions rπ in graphs G and use it to design subquadratic-time local search algorithms for the
center problem in several classes of graphs, among which are (weakly) bridged graphs, graphs
with convex balls, bipartite Helly graphs, and cube-free median graphs. Our results can be
viewed as a contribution in the case of radius functions to the general question “How does the
geometry of the graph influence the complexity of local search?”, recently raised in [21].
1.2. Related work. There is a vast literature on the center problem. We mostly review the
results related to unimodality and to the classes of graphs occurring in this paper. By [74], the
unweighted center of a tree T is located in the middle of a diametral path of T . It is folklore that
a diametral pair of vertices can be found in O(n) time by picking an arbitrary vertex u of T ,
computing a furthest from u vertex v ∈ T , and then computing a furthest from v vertex w ∈ T ;
then the pair v, w is diametral. The weighted center problem in trees is much more difficult:
Kariv and Hakimi [75] presented an O(n log n)-time algorithm combining divide-and-conquer,
convexity, and centroid computation. At each step, a centroid v of a subtree T ′ of T and a
neighbor v ′ of v with rπ (v ′ ) < rπ (v) are computed. The radius function rπ of T is convex, thus
either v ′ does not exist and v is central or the subtree of T ′ rooted at v ′ contains the center and
the recursion is performed on it. We refer to this method as cut-on-best-neighbor. Meggido [79]
refined this approach into an optimal O(n)-time algorithm by significantly refining the approach
of [75] and using the same ideas as his linear-time algorithm for linear programming in R2 and
R3 . In [79], he also presented the first O(n)-time algorithm for the smallest enclosing circle
(disproving the conjecture that O(n log n) is its best complexity). Both algorithms are based on
the same ideas as the linear-time algorithm for linear programming in R2 and R3 [79]. Linear time

    One computation of a furthest vertex is called a FP scan [78]. Thus the unweighted center
of a tree is computed via two FP scans. This FP scans paradigm was successful in several
cases, e.g., [32] uses it to compute an unweighted center of a chordal graph G in O(m) time.
In this case, a pair v, w returned after two FP scans is almost diametral and there is a center
of G located in the neighborhood of the middle of (v, w)-geodesics. The algorithm of [77] is
based on the fact that the link-radius of a simple polygon (which can be viewed as an infinite
connected graph) is approximatively equal to half of the link-diameter. Knowing the diameter
(the link-diameter is O(n)-time computable), one can locate the link-center around the middle
of a diametral pair. The graphs investigated in [32] and [77] have small hyperbolicity. Chepoi
et al. [34] proved that similar results hold for the center problem in δ-hyperbolic graphs G: two
FP scans return a pair v, w such that d(v, w) ≥ diam(G) − 2δ and the center of G is contained
in the ball of radius 5δ + 1 centered at a middle vertex of any (v, w)-geodesic. This provides a
general framework for the unweighted center problem (parameterized by hyperbolicity).

    Buseman [24] introduced peakless functions by replacing the quantitative inequality f (z) ≤
λ · f (x) + (1 − λ) · f (y) by the qualitative one f (z) ≤ max{f (x), f (y)}, where equality holds only
if f (x) = f (z) = f (y). Buseman and Phadke [25] investigated peakless functions in Busemann
spaces (which generalize CAT(0) spaces and where each pair of points can be connected by a
unique geodesic [89]). In the case of discrete metric spaces, where there exist several geodesics
between the same points, Arkhipova and Sergienko [5] introduced the notion of r-convex func-
tions (see also [76]). A function f is r-convex if for each pair of points there exists a geodesic
on which f is convex. Bandelt and Chepoi [8] combined these notions and defined a function f
to be weakly peakless if for each pair of points there is a geodesic on which f is peakless. More
recently, Bénéteau et al. [17] introduced the notions of p-weakly peakless functions by requiring
that each pair of vertices is connected by a step-p geodesic on which the function is peakless.
While peakless, r-convex, and weakly peakless functions are unimodal on G, p-weakly peakless

functions are unimodal on the pth power Gp of G. All these classes of functions were suitable
for investigating the unimodality of the median and radius functions on graphs. Namely, it
was shown in [8] that the graphs in which all median functions are unimodal are exactly the
graphs in which all median functions are weakly peakless. This bijection was extended in [17]
to Gp -unimodality of median functions and p-weakly peaklessness of all median functions. The
results of [8] and [17] show that numerous important classes of graphs have G- or G2 -unimodal
median functions. As we mentioned before, Dragan [43,44] established that the graphs in which
all radius functions are G-unimodal are exactly the Helly graphs. Properties close to relaxed
unimodality (called locality) were established and used in the case of δ-hyperbolic graphs [49]
and graphs with αi -metrics [45].
1.3. Our results. One can ask to what extent the two results about the center problem in Helly
graphs are generalizable. In this paper, we investigate the unimodality of the radius function
rπ of a graph G not in G but in some its power Gp . We show that several important classes of
graphs have Gp -unimodal radius functions, i.e., every vertex that minimizes the radius function
in its ball of radius p is a central vertex:
Theorem A. (Propositions 5.5,6.2,11.5,8.1,8.9,11.5) For bridged and weakly bridged graphs,
graphs with convex balls, bipartite Helly graphs, and cube-free median graphs, all radius functions
rπ are G2 -unimodal. For δ-hyperbolic graphs, all radius functions rπ are G4δ+1 -unimodal and
for δ-coarse Helly graphs all 0-1 radius functions rπ are GO(δ) -unimodal.
   To prove Theorem A, a stronger property, p-weakly peaklessness of the radius function rπ is
established; we recall, as mentioned earlier, that it is a relaxation of convexity, which originates
from Busemann/CAT(0) geometry [24, 25], and was used before in [8, 17].
   The recognition problem for graphs with Gp -unimodal radius functions is polynomial:
Theorem B. (Theorem 9.1) Given a graph G with n vertices and a positive integer p, one can
decide in O(n4 ) time whether all radius functions rπ of G are Gp -unimodal.
  To implement efficiently local search on graphs with Gp -unimodal radius functions, we need
an efficient algorithm that for any vertex v outputs an improving p-neighbor of v if it exists. In
general, this cannot be done in subquadratic time even for p = 2:
Theorem C. (Theorem 9.2) Assuming the Hitting Set Conjecture, for any ε > 0, deciding if v
is a local minimum in G2 of the radius function rπ for a graph G cannot be solved in O(n2−ε )
time, even if G has only O(n)

1.4. Bridged, median, and (bipartite) Helly graphs. Together with hyperbolic graphs,
median graphs, bridged graphs and Helly graphs constitute the most important classes of graphs
in metric graph theory [9, 27]. All these classes have been also extensively studied in geometric
group theory.
   Median graphs are exactly the 1-skeleta of CAT(0) cube complexes [31]. Gromov [64] char-
acterized CAT(0) cube complexes as the simply connected cube complexes in which links are
flag complexes. Median graphs can also be characterized as the domains of event structures [15]
from concurrency theory and as the solution sets of 2-SAT formulas [92]. For characterizations
and applications of median graphs to discrete mathematics and geometric group theory, see the
book [23] and the surveys [9] and [91].
   Bridged graphs are the graphs in which balls around convex sets are convex (which is one of
the basic properties of Euclidean convexity and CAT(0) spaces) and they have been characterized
in [59, 95] as the graphs in which all isometric cycles have length 3. Bridged graphs generalize
chordal graphs. Chepoi [31] presented a local-to-global characterization of bridged graphs as the
1-skeleta of simply connected flag simplicial complexes with 6-large links (i.e., they do not have
induced 4- and 5-cycles). Those simplicial complexes have been rediscovered by Januszkiewicz
and Świaţkowski [73] and dubbed systolic complexes. Systolic complexes are contractible [31,73]
and they are considered as combinatorial analogs of simplicial nonpositive curvature. The groups
acting on systolic complexes/bridged graphs have been studied in numerous papers; [71, 73,
87, 94] is a small sample. Graphs with convex balls generalize bridged graphs. They have
been introduced and characterized in [59, 95] and thoroughly studied in [29]. Weakly bridged
graphs constitute a subclass of graphs with convex balls; they have been introduced in [86] and
characterized in [38].
   Helly graphs are the graphs in which the balls satisfy the Helly property, i.e., any collection
of pairwise intersecting balls has a nonempty intersection. The bipartite Helly graphs (alias
bipartite absolute retracts) are the bipartite analogs of Helly graphs. Namely, bipartite Helly
graphs are the bipartite graphs in which the family of half-balls (the intersections of balls with
the halves of the bipartition) satisfies the Helly property. Helly graphs and bipartite Helly graphs
are the discrete analogues of hyperconvex spaces [6] (complete geodesic metric spaces in which
the set of closed balls satisfies the Helly property). In perfect analogy with hyperconvexity,
there is a close relationship between Helly graphs and absolute retracts. A graph is an absolute
retract exactly when it is a retract of any larger graph into which it embeds isometrically.
Then absolute retracts and Helly graphs are the same. Analogously, the bipartite Helly graphs
are the absolute retracts in the category of bipartite graphs [10], which is why they are also
called bipartite absolute retracts. Helly graphs have been characterized in the papers [12, 13]
metrically and in [27] topologically, in a local-to-global way. The study of groups acting on Helly
graphs was initiated in the paper [28] and continued in many other papers, e.g. [66, 69, 72, 88].
Numerous examples of such groups are also known: hyperbolic groups and groups acting on
CAT(0) cube complexes are Helly [28]. The bipartite Helly graphs have been introduced and
metrically characterized in [10]. Bandelt, Hell, and Farber [11] established a correspondence
between Helly graphs and bipartite Helly graphs, using natural transformations between reflexive
graphs and bipartite graphs. An important subclass of bipartite Helly graphs is the class of
hereditary modular graphs [7]. Since the hereditary modular graphs are exactly the graphs in
which all isometric cycles have length 4 [7], they can be viewed as bipartite analogs of bridged
graphs. Hoda [68] associated cell complexes to hereditary modular graphs and investigated their
topological properties as well as the groups acting on them.
  From the point of view of graph theory, median, bridged, and (bipartite) Helly graphs are
quite general and universal. First, any connected graph can be isometrically embedded into a
Helly graph and any connected bipartite graph can be isometrically embedded into a bipartite
Helly graph. Furthermore, in analogy to injective hulls/tight spans of general metric spaces, for
any connected graph (respectively, connected bipartite graph) H there exists a unique minimal
6                 J. CHALOPIN, V. CHEPOI, F. DRAGAN, G. DUCOFFE, AND Y. VAXÈS

Helly graph (respectively, bipartite Helly graph) G containing H as an isometric subgraph; this
graph G is called the Hellyfication (respectively, the bipartite Hellyfication) of H. Second, any
connected graph H is a minor of a cube-free median or K4 -free bridged graph. Furthermore,
any graph H not containing any induced C4 and C5 is an induced subgraph of a bridged graph.
The local structure of median and bridged graphs is also universal because arbitrary graphs
may occur in their stars. For any graph H (respectively, any triangle-free graph H) its simplex
graph (the covering graph of the poset of cliques of H ordered by inclusion) is a median graph
(respectively, a cube-free median graph) [14]. The stars (the union of all cubes containing a given
vertex) of any median graph G are simplex graphs. In the same vein, Weetman [98] described a
nice construction W (H) of (infinite) graphs in which the links (neighborhoods) of all vertices are
isomorphic to a prescribed finite graph H of girth ≥ 6. For example, if H = C6 , then W (H) is
the triangular grid. If H = Cn for n ≥ 7, then W (H) is the graph of a tiling of the (hyperbolic)
plane into triangles in which each vertex has n neighbors. For general H, W (H) are always
bridged and K4 -free.
   Nonetheless, median, bridged, hyperbolic, and (bipartite) Helly graphs have a rich and versa-
tile metric structure. Median graphs can be viewed as discrete ℓ1 -geometries, bridged graphs and
graphs with convex balls as discrete ℓ2 -geometries, and Helly graphs as discrete ℓ∞ -geometries.
This structure has been thoroughly studied in metric graph theory, see [9] for a survey. On
the other hand, the algorithmic results about median, bridged, and (bipartite) Helly graphs are
still rare. For median graphs, we can mention the polynomial-time algorithm for computing the
geodesic between any two points in the associated CAT(0) cube complex [67], the linear time
algorithm for computing the median [16], and the subquadratic-time algorithms for computing
the 0-1-centers [18–20]. For bridged graphs, our algorithm for computing a central vertex is, to
the best of our knowledge, the first such algorithmic result. Previously, it was only known how
to compute the 0-1-centers in linear time for chordal graphs [32] and planar bridged graphs [36].
1.5. The structure of the paper. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2
we present the main notions and definitions used in all other sections. More specific definitions
are given in respective sections. In Section 3 we present the main ideas of the algorithm sample-
select-descent for bridged graphs and bipartite Helly graphs, and of the algorithm cut-on-best-
neighbor for cube-free median graphs. Section 4 shows how to derandomize the sample-select-
descent algorithm for 0-1-profiles. Section 5 presents our results about the center problem in
bridged and weakly bridged graphs, namely: the G2 -unimodality of their radius functions, the
sample-select-descent algorithm, and its correctness and complexity. Similar results for bipartite
Helly graphs are presented in Section 6. Section 7 presents the cut-on-best-neighbor algorithm
for cube-free median graphs and establishes its correctness and complexity. Section 8 establishes
the GO(δ) unimodality of all radius functions in δ-hyperbolic graphs and (for 0-1-profiles) in δ-
coarse Helly graphs. In Section 9 we prove that the graphs with Gp -unimodal radius functions
can be recognized in polynomial time; we also establish super-linear lower bounds for deciding
if a vertex is a local minimum of the radius function in a graph, which holds even if all radius
functions are G2 -unimodal. Section 10 presents some perspectives of our work and open questions
(other open questions are locally presented in previous sections). The final Section 11 generalizes
the results of Section 5 to all graphs with convex balls. Since the resulting complexity is worse
than for (weakly) bridged graphs and the technical details are much more involved we decided
to present Section 11 as an appendix of the paper.

graph G is an idempotent nonexpansive mapping of G into itself, that is, φ2 = φ : V (G) → V (G)
with d(φ(x), φ(y)) ≤ d(x, y) for all x, y ∈ V (G). The subgraph H of G induced by the image
of G under φ is referred to as a retract of G. A graph H is called an absolute retract if H
is a retract of any graph G into which H is isometrically embedded. Analogously, a bipartite
graph H is a bipartite absolute retract if H is a retract of any bipartite graph G into which H is
isometrically embedded. A family of sets F on a universe V satisfies the Helly property if any
subfamily F ′ of F has a nonempty intersection whenever each pair of sets of F ′ intersects.
    Vertices v1 , v2 , v3 form a metric triangle v1 v2 v3 if I(v1 , v2 ), I(v2 , v3 ), and I(v3 , v1 ) pairwise
intersect only in common end-vertices, i.e., I(vi , vj ) ∩ I(vi , vk ) = {vi } for any 1 ≤ i, j, k ≤ 3.
If d(v1 , v2 ) = d(v2 , v3 ) = d(v3 , v1 ) = k, then this metric triangle is called equilateral of size k.
An equilateral metric triangle v1 v2 v3 of size k is called strongly equilateral if d(vi , x) = k for
any vertex x ∈ I(vj , vk ), where {i, j, k} = {1, 2, 3}. Obviously, all metric triangles of size 0
or of size 1 are strongly equilateral. A metric triangle v1 v2 v3 of G is a quasi-median of the
triplet x, y, z if the following metric equalities are satisfied: d(x, y) = d(x, v1 ) + d(v1 , v2 ) +
d(v2 , y), d(y, z) = d(y, v2 ) + d(v2 , v3 ) + d(v3 , z), and d(z, x) = d(z, v3 ) + d(v3 , v1 ) + d(v1 , x) (such
a quasi-median always exists). A quasi-median v1 v2 v3 of x, y, z that is a metric triangle of size
0 (i.e., v1 = v2 = v3 ) is called a median.

2.2. Weakly modular graphs. A graph G is weakly modular [27,30] if G satisfies the triangle
(TC) and quadrangle (QC) conditions:
 (TC) Triangle Condition: for v, x, y ∈ V with d(v, x) = d(v, y) = k and x ∼ y, ∃z ∈ Bk−1 (v),
      z ∼ x, y.
 (QC) Quadrangle Condition: for v, x, y, u ∈ V with d(v, x) = d(v, y) = k = d(v, u) − 1 and
      u ∼ x, y, x ≁ y, ∃z ∈ Bk−1 (v), z ∼ x, y.
   By [30], the weakly modular graphs are exactly the graphs in which all metric triangles are
strongly equilateral. A graph G is called modular if all metric triangles have size 0, i.e., the
quasi-medians of all triplets are medians. Median graphs are the modular graphs in which each
triplet has a unique median. Cube-free median graphs are the median graphs not containing the
3-cube as a subgraph.
   Helly graphs are the graphs in which the family of balls satisfies the Helly property. Helly
graphs are weakly modular, namely all quasi-medians in Helly graphs are metric triangles of size
0 or 1. Bipartite Helly graphs are the bipartite graphs in which the family of half-balls satisfies
the Helly property. Bipartite Helly graphs are modular. We stress here the fact that the class of
bipartite Helly graphs is not a subclass of Helly graphs, because in Helly graphs and in bipartite

Helly graphs the Helly property holds for different families of sets: balls in the first case and
half-balls in the second case.
   A graph G is bridged if any isometric cycle of G has size 3. By [59,95], the bridged graphs are
exactly the graphs in which the balls Br (A) around convex sets A are convex. Bridged graphs
are weakly modular, in fact they are exactly the weakly modular graphs not containing induced
C4 and C5 [30, 31]. A graph with convex balls (a CB-graph) is a graph in which all balls are
convex. Obviously, bridged graphs are CB-graphs. CB-graphs are not weakly modular but they
satisfy (QC) and the Triangle-Pentagon Condition (TPC) asserting that for any v, x, y ∈ V such
that d(v, x) = d(v, y) = k and x ∼ y either (TC) or the following (PC) holds [29]:
  (PC) Pentagon Condition: there exist z, w, w′ ∈ V such that z ∈ Bk−2 (v) and xwzw′ y is a
         pentagon of G.
   The weakly bridged graphs are the weakly modular graphs with convex balls. By [38] they are
exactly the weakly modular graphs not containing induced C4 .
2.3. Radius function and Gp -unimodality. A profile (or a weight function) is a map π :
V → R+ ∪ {0} with finite support supp(π) = {v ∈ V : π(v) > 0}. The radius function is
given by rπ (v) = maxu∈supp(π) π(u)d(u, v). A vertex v minimizing rπ (v) is called a center of
G with respect to π and the set of all centers is the center Cπ (G). The value rπ (v) for any
v ∈ Cπ (G) is the radius of G with respect to π and is denoted by radπ (G). For v ∈ V , let
Fπ (v) = {z ∈ supp(π) : π(z)d(v, z) = rπ (v)} be the set of furthest vertices of v. If π(v) ∈ {0, 1}
for any v, then we call π a 0-1-profile and rπ a 0-1-radius function.
   Let f : V → R be a function defined on the vertex-set V of G. The global minima of f is the
set arg min(f ) of vertices of G minimizing f on G. By a local minimum one means a vertex v
such that f (v) ≤ f (w) for any w in B1 (v). A function f is called unimodal on G if every local
minimum of f is global, that is, if the inequality f (v) ≤ f (y) holds for all neighbors of y of a
vertex v of G then it holds for all vertices y of G. Weakly peakless functions introduced in [8]
is an important subclass of unimodal functions: f : V → R is weakly peakless if for any two
non-adjacent vertices u, v it satisfies the following condition WP(u, v):
WP(u, v): there exists w ∈ I ◦ (u, v) such that f (w) ≤ max {f (u), f (v)}, and equality holds only
            if f (u) = f (w) = f (v).
In [17] the notions of unimodal and weakly peakless functions have been generalized to Gp -
unimodal and p-weakly peakless functions, with p a positive integer. A function f is Gp -unimodal
if every local minimum of f in the pth power Gp of G is a global minimum of f . A function f
is called p-weakly peakless if for any two vertices u, v such that d(u, v) ≥ p + 1 it satisfies the
condition WP(u, v). It is shown in [17] that p-weakly peakless functions are Gp -unimodal. A
function f is locally-p-weakly peakless on G if it satisfies WP(u, v) for any u, v with p + 1 ≤
d(u, v) ≤ 2p. By [17], f is p-weakly peakless iff f is locally-p-weakly peakless. Note that if f is
p-weakly peakless, then any pair of vertices u, v of G can be connected by a p-geodesic (a subset
of a geodesic in which consecutive vertices have distance ≤ p) along which f is peakless. Using
p-weakly peaklessness is more handy when proving Gp -unimodality because we can better use
the metric structure of graphs. Note that any radius function rπ is defined with respect to the
distance function of G, but its unimodality is considered in Gp .

   In this section, we present the technical contributions of this paper at a high level. We begin

3.2. The case of bridged graphs. We describe how to implement ImproveEccentricity(G, π, v)
(i.e., to compute a vertex u ∈ B2 (v) with rπ (u) < rπ (v)) in the case of bridged graphs. For
graphs with convex balls this is done analogously but some technical extra work is needed for
the last step (i.e., to assert that v is a local minimum of rπ in G2 ), details are given in the
appendix (Section 11).
   In bridged graphs G, the balls around convex sets are convex. This property is crucial for
ImproveEccentricity(G, π, v) and the proof of its correctness. In particular, using it, we show
that any 1-ball B of G is outergated (see Section 2.1) and we can compute in O(m) time the
outergates on B. Using Lemma 2.1, we compute u ∈ B1 (v) minimizing rπ in O(m) time. If
rπ (u) < rπ (v), then we are done. Otherwise, v is a local minimum of rπ in G and we have
to decide if v is a local minimum of rπ in G2 . The key step of the algorithm is to identify a
neighbor wmax of v such that wmax ∼ u if there exists a vertex u ∈ B2 (v) with rπ (u) < rπ (v)
(see Figure 1 for an illustration). For this, consider the set Fπ (v) of all furthest from v vertices
of the profile. For every w ∈ N (v), the shadow Ψπ (w) of w is the set of all z ∈ Fπ (v) such that
w ∈ I(v, z), i.e., w lies on a shortest (v, z)-path. Next, we partition the neighborhood N (v) of v
into equivalence classes W0 , W1 , . . . , Wk , where two vertices w, w′ ∈ N (v) are equivalent if they
have the same shadows Ψπ (w) = Ψπ (w′ ). Using outergates and partition refinement techniques,
this partition of N (v) can be computed in O(m) time. Doing so, we also compute |Ψπ (Wi )| for
every equivalence class Wi (where Ψπ (Wi ) = Ψπ (w) for any w ∈ Wi ). Assume that W0 is the
equivalence class maximizing the size |Ψπ (W0 )| of its shadow. Since v is a local minimum of rπ
in G, Fπ (v) ̸= Ψπ (W0 ). We pick any vertex z ∈ Fπ (v) \ Ψπ (W0 ) and compute in O(m) time the
intersection N (v) ∩ I(v, z) = N (v) ∩ N (z ′ ), where z ′ is any outergate of z on the 1-ball B1 (v).
Let wmax ∈ N (v) ∩ I(v, z) be a vertex maximizing |N (wmax ) ∩ W0 |. Using the convexity of balls,
we prove that if v is not a local minimum of rπ in G2 , then wmax has a neighbor v + such that
rπ (v + ) < rπ (v) (see Proposition 5.11). Using Lemma 2.1, the algorithm selects in O(m) time
a vertex v + minimizing rπ in B1 (wmax ). If rπ (v + ) = rπ (v), then v ∈ Cπ (G) and otherwise we
return v + as an improving vertex u ∈ B2 (v).

3.4. Cut-on-best-neighbor for cube-free median graphs. Cube-free median graphs are
bipartite Helly graphs and the previous algorithm performs a single step of local search for rπ in
12                 J. CHALOPIN, V. CHEPOI, F. DRAGAN, G. DUCOFFE, AND Y. VAXÈS

O(m) time. The complexity of the sample-select-descent also depends on the number of descent
steps and the size of the initial set U . Therefore sample-select-descent cannot be used to get an
algorithm faster than O( e √nm).
    For cube-free median graphs, we design an e     O(m)-time algorithm. To do so, we use the G2 -
unimodality of rπ differently: we generalize in a non-trivial way the cut-on-best-neighbor method
by Kariv and Hakimi [75] for weighted centers in trees. Their algorithm computes a centroid v
of T and a neighbor v ′ of v with rπ (v ′ ) < rπ (v). Since the radius function rπ of T is convex, if v ′
does not exist then v is central. Otherwise, the center belongs to the subtree T ′ of T containing
v ′ and the recursion is performed on it. For median graphs G, the role of subtree T ′ is played by
halfspaces, i.e., convex sets with convex complements. The halfspaces of G are exactly the sets
of the form H(x, y) = {z ∈ V : d(z, x) < d(z, y)} where xy is an edge of G and can be elegantly
constructed in linear time by LexBFS [16].
    Let G = (V, E) be a cube-free median graph; then G contains at most 2n edges and rπ is
   2
G -unimodal. We start with G as the search region R. At each iteration, the search region R
is convex (and thus gated) and contains the center Cπ (G). Furthermore, R is the intersection
of the search region R′ of the previous iteration with a certain halfspace H of G and has size
at most one half of the size of R′ : R = R′ ∩ H and |R| ≤ |R′ |/2. Consequently, the algorithm
has log2 n iterations. Now, we show how, given the search region R, to compute in O(n log n)
time a halfspace H such that Cπ (G) ⊆ H and |R ∩ H| ≤ |R|/2. First, a median/centroid v
of R is computed in O(|R|) time using [16]. Then we compute the star St(v) ⊆ B2 (v) of v in
R (the union of all edges and squares containing v) and rπ (z) for all z ∈ St(v) (this can be
done in linear time because St(v) is gated). If v minimizes rπ (v) in the star St(v), using the
G2 -unimodality of rπ , we show that v is a central vertex of G and we return it. Otherwise, we
compute a halfspace H of R separating v from Cπ (G), i.e., Cπ (G) ⊆ H and v ∈ R \ H. Since v
is a median of R and Cπ (G) ⊆ H, |R ∩ H| ≤ |R|/2 holds.
    The computation of the halfspace H is the main technical issue of the algorithm. This is done
in O(n log n) time by using the function ReduceConvexRegion, which is described in Section 7 and
which we now outline (see Figure 3 for an illustration). A vertex z ∈ St(v) with rπ (z) < rπ (v)
is called an improving neighbor of v if z ∼ v and an improving second neighbor if z ≁ v. First
we show that v has at most two improving neighbors. If v does not have improving neighbors
but has an improving second neighbor w ∈ R, then as H we consider a halfspace H(z, v) where
z ∼ v, w. Otherwise, we pick an improving neighbor z and we construct the fiber ΦSt(v) (z) of z
(i.e., the set of vertices having z as their gates in St(v)). The boundary ΥSt(v) (z) is the set of
vertices of ΦSt(v) (z) that have a neighbor outside of ΦSt(v) (z). For cube-free median graphs, this
boundary is a tree with gated branches. Using the cut-on-best-neighbor approach for trees, we
find in O(n log n) time a local-minimum u of the radius function rπ on ΥSt(v) (z) (i.e., a vertex
u ∈ ΥSt(v) (z) such that rπ (u) ≤ rπ (u′ ) for all neighbors u′ of u in ΥSt(v) (z)). Then we compute
in linear time the star St(u) of u and rπ (t) for all vertices t ∈ St(u). If u is a local minimum
in St(u), we return u as a central vertex. If u has one improving neighbor t, then we select
the halfspace H(t, u) as H. Analogously, if u does not have an improving neighbor but has an
improving second neighbor w, then we take as H a halfspace H(t, u), where t ∼ u, w and t does
not belong to the (z, u)-path of ΥSt(v) (z). Finally, if u has two improving neighbors, we prove
that they belong to ΦSt(v) (z) and that the halfspace H(z, v) defined by the edge vz separates v
from Cπ (G) (and thus can be taken as H). Since ReduceConvexRegion is called O(log n) times,
we obtain an algorithm with complexity O(n log2 n).

                     6. The center problem in bipartite Helly graphs
   In this section, we present a subquadratic-time algorithm for the center problem in bipartite
Helly graphs, based on G2 -unimodality of the radius function. The main technical contribution
is an O(m)-time implementation of the function ImproveEccentricity.
6.1. Bipartite Helly graphs. Recall that a graph G is a Helly graph if the set of balls of
G satisfies the Helly property: any collection of pairwise intersecting balls has a nonempty
intersection. Analogously, a graph G is a bipartite Helly graph if G is bipartite and the family
of half-balls of G satisfies the Helly property. As we noticed above (see Section 2.2), bipartite
Helly graphs are modular. A topological local-to-global characterization of Helly graphs was
given in [27], however such a characterization for bipartite Helly graphs is missing.
   Bipartite Helly graphs have been introduced and characterized in several nice ways by Bandelt,
Dählmann, and Schütte [10], some of which we recall next. The graph Bn is the bipartite
complete graph Kn,n minus a perfect matching, i.e., the bipartition of Bn is defined by a1 , . . . , an
and b1 , . . . , bn and ai is adjacent to bj iff i ̸= j. The graph B
                                                                   bn is obtained from Bn by adding two
adjacent new vertices a and b such that a is adjacent to b1 , . . . , bn and b is adjacent to a1 , . . . , an .
Theorem 6.1 ( [10]). For a bipartite graph G = (V0 ∪ V1 , E), the following conditions are
equivalent:
(1) G is a bipartite Helly graph;
(2) G is a modular graph such that every induced Bn (n ≥ 4) extends to B     bn in G;
(3) G satisfies the following interval-outergate property: for any vertices u and v with d(u, v) ≥
    3, the neighbors of v in I(u, v) have a second common neighbor x in I(u, v).

                  7. The center problem in cube-free median graphs
   In this section, we present an O(n log2 n)-time divide-and-conquer algorithm for computing
a weighted center of a cube-free median graph G. Our method generalizes the algorithm of [75]
for computing the center of a tree and uses similar ingredients: divide-and-conquer, median
computation, and unimodality of the radius function.
7.1. Median graphs. Recall that a median of u, v, w is a vertex from the intersection I(u, v) ∩
I(v, w) ∩ I(w, u). A graph G is median if any triplet u, v, w has a unique median. A graph G is
cube-free median if G does not contain the cube Q3 as a subgraph. Median graphs satisfy (QC),
are modular and thus bipartite. In fact, the median graphs are exactly the K2,3 -free modular
graphs. A topological local-to-global characterization of median graphs as 1-skeleta of CAT(0)
cube complexes was given in [31].
   It is not immediately clear from the definition, but median graphs have a rich metric and
cubical structure. For that, let us recall some important notations and terminology. The star
St(v) of v is the union of all cubes containing v. For an edge uv, let H(u, v) = {x ∈ V (G) :
d(x, u) < d(x, v)}. In a bipartite graph G, and thus in a median graph, the sets H(u, v) and
H(v, u) define a bipartition of V (G). For a gated set M and z ∈ M , let ΦM (z) be the set of all
x ∈ V whose gate in M is z and call ΦM (z) the fiber of z. The boundary ΥM (z) of ΦM (z) is the
subgraph induced by all vertices of ΦM (z) having neighbors in other fibers ΦM (z ′ ), z ′ ∈ M . A
branch of a tree T rooted at a vertex z is a path of T with one end in z and another end in a vertex
of T . The following properties of median graphs are well-known, see for example [9, 16, 39, 85]:
Theorem 7.1. For a median graph G = (V, E), we have:
(1) The convex sets, the locally-convex sets, and the gated sets of G are the same;
(2) The stars St(v) and the sets H(u, v), H(v, u) are convex, and thus gated;
(3) If G has n vertices and the largest cube has dimension d, then G contains at most dn edges.
    In particular, any cube-free median graph contains at most 2n edges;
(4) If M is gated, each fiber ΦM (u) is gated and u′ ∈ ΦM (u), v ′ ∈ ΦM (v) and u′ ∼ v ′ , u ̸= v
    imply u ∼ v [85];
(5) For a cube-free median graph G, for each fiber ΦSt(v) (z) of a star St(v), its boundary ΥSt(v) (z)
    is a tree with gated branches [39];
(6) For any u and v, u and all neighbors of u in I(u, v) belong to a hypercube of I(u, v). If G
    is cube-free, then u has at most two neighbors in I(u, v) [85].
  Call the last property the downward cube property (DCP ). For an edge uv of a median
graph G, the sets H(u, v) and H(v, u) are called halfspaces. The edges running between two
complementary halfspaces H(u, v) and H(v, u) of G are called Θ-classes. Notice that any two
edges from the same Θ-class of G define the same pair of halfspaces.

Lemma 7.4. If P = (v, z, w) is a gated path of G, then 2rπ (z) ≤ rπ (v) + rπ (w). In particular,
z is an i-neighbor of v if rπ (v) > rπ (w). Furthermore, one cannot have rπ (v) = rπ (z) = rπ (w).
Proof. Let x be a furthest from z vertex, i.e., a vertex of π maximizing π(x)d(x, z). If x belongs
to the fiber ΦP (z) of z, then d(v, x) = d(w, x) = d(z, x) + 1, whence rπ (v) ≥ π(x)d(v, x) =
π(x)d(z, x) + π(x) = rπ (z) + π(x). Analogously, rπ (w) ≥ π(x)d(w, x) = rπ (z) + π(x) and in
this case we obtain 2rπ (z) < rπ (v) + rπ (w). Now, suppose that x belongs to one of the fibers
ΦP (v) or ΦP (w), say x ∈ ΦP (w). Then d(z, x) = d(w, x) + 1 and d(v, x) = d(z, x) + 1, whence
rπ (w) ≥ π(x)d(w, x) = rπ (z) − π(x) and rπ (v) ≥ π(x)d(v, x) = rπ (z) + π(x). This implies that
2rπ (z) ≤ rπ (v) + rπ (w) also in this case.
   Now, suppose by way of contradiction that rπ (v) = rπ (z) = rπ (w). Since 2rπ (z) < rπ (v) +
rπ (w) if x ∈ ΦP (z), this case is impossible. On the other hand, in the case when x ∈ ΦP (w)
we deduce that rπ (v) ≥ π(x)d(v, x) = rπ (z) + π(x). Therefore, we conclude that also the case
when x belongs to one of the fibers ΦP (v) or ΦP (w) is impossible. This establishes the second
assertion.                                                                                      □

v, z1 , z2 are included in a square of G.
Proof. Suppose v has three i-neighbors z1 , z2 , z3 . Let x ∈ Fπ (v). Since rπ (zi ) < rπ (v), z1 , z2 , z3 ∈
I(v, x), contradicting the fact that G is cube-free. Now, let v have two i-neighbors z1 , z2 and
x ∈ Fπ (v). Then z1 , z2 ∈ I(v, x). By (QC) there exists a common neighbor w of z1 , z2 one step
closer to x. Consequently, v, z1 , z2 , w induce a square S.                                                □
Lemma 7.6. If v ∈   / Cπ (v) has no i-neighbor in direction c ∈ Cπ (G), then v has no i-neighbor
at all but has a unique is-neighbor w in all directions c′ ∈ Cπ (G).
Proof. Pick c ∈ Cπ (G) and suppose v has no i-neighbor in direction c. Since v ∈              / Cπ (G),
d(v, c) ≥ 2. We assert that v has an is-neighbor. This is so when d(v, c) = 2 since c is an is-
neighbor of v. If d(v, c) ≥ 3, since rπ is 2-weakly peakless, there exists w ∈ I(v, c) ∩ B2 (v) with
rπ (w) < rπ (v). Since v has no i-neighbor in direction c, d(v, w) = 2, i.e., w is an is-neighbor of v.

c′ ∈ Cπ (G). First, note that w ∈ St(v). Indeed, if v and w have a unique common neighbor z, by

z1 , z2 . Pick the square S = vz1 wz2 , which is gated. For u ∈ S, let xu ∈ supp(π) such that

7.3. The algorithm. Let G be a cube-free median graph; then G contains at most 2n edges.
We start with G as the search region R. At all iterations, the search region R is convex and
contains the center Cπ (G). At each iteration, we compute a median/centroid v of the current
region R in O(|R|) time [16]. We compute the star St(v) of v in R and rπ (z) for all vertices z
of St(v) (see Section 7.6). If R = {v} or rπ (v) ≤ rπ (z) for all z ∈ St(v), we return v as a central
vertex of G. Otherwise, we show that v has an i-neighbor or an is-neighbor in R. Using this
improving (2-)neighbor of v, we either compute a center or a halfspace H of R separating Cπ (G)
from v, i.e., Cπ (G) ⊆ H and v ∈ R\H. Then the search is continued in H. Algorithm 4 presents
the main routine of the divide-and-conquer algorithm. Algorithm 5 presents the division step,
which computes a separating halfspace H of R.

  Output: A vertex of Cπ (G)

7.5. Computing a local minimum of rπ in the tree ΥSt(v) (z). We use divide-and-conquer
and we start with ΥSt(v) (z) as the search region T ; T will always contain a local minimum of
rπ . At each iteration, we compute a centroid u of T , and rπ (u′ ) for each u′ ∈ N [u] in T . For
that, since N [u] ⊂ St(u), we simply compute in O(n) time rπ (u′ ) for all u′ ∈ St(u) (see the
next Section 7.6 for details). If |T | = 1 or rπ (u) ≤ rπ (u′ ) for any u′ ∈ N [u], then u is a local
minimum of rπ in ΥSt(v) (z) and we return u. Otherwise, if rπ (u′ ) < rπ (u) for some neighbor
u′ of u in T , then clearly the subtree T ′ of T containing u′ and not containing u will contain
a local minimum of rπ in ΥSt(v) (z). Then we continue with T ′ as the search region. Since u
is a centroid of T , |T ′ | ≤ |T |/2, the algorithm has log n′ iterations, where n′ is the number of
vertices of ΥSt(v) (z). Since each iteration is performed in O(n) time and n′ ≤ n, the algorithm
has complexity O(n log n).

v of R using [16]. Then we compute in O(n) time rπ (z) for all z ∈ St(v). We either return v
as a center (Case 0) or v has an i-neighbor or/and is-neighbor. If v has no i-neighbor, then in

halfspace H of R (Cases 3-5). Namely, H = R ∩ H(a, b), where ab is an edge of R such that
ab = zv in Cases 1,5 and ab = tu in Cases 3,4. To compute R ∩ H(a, b), first we compute in
O(n) time the Θ-classes of G using [16], then we traverse the edges of the Θ-class Θi of ab. For
each such edge we select the end belonging to H(a, b). Then we perform a BFS in R starting
from the set of all such vertices and which does not involve the edges of Θi . The vertices of
R which are reached by this BFS are exactly the vertices of R ∩ H(a, b), and it requires O(n)
time to compute them. Since we have log n iterations and the complexity of each iteration is in
O(n log n), we obtain an algorithm with complexity O(n log2 n). Consequently, we have proven
the following main result of this section (which is Theorem E):
Theorem 7.8. If G is a cube-free median graph with arbitrary weights, then a central vertex of
G can be computed in deterministic time O(n log2 n).

  In this section, we prove that the radius functions of δ-hyperbolic and α-coarse Helly graphs
are Gp -unimodal for some constant p depending respectively only on δ and α. We further refine
our algorithmic results for δ-hyperbolic (weakly) bridged or bipartite Helly graphs.
8.1. δ-Hyperbolic graphs. Gromov’s hyperbolicity is an important notion in metric geometry,
which revolutionized the geometric group theory. A graph G = (V, E) is δ-hyperbolic [64] for
δ ≥ 0 if for any u, v, x, y ∈ V , the two largest of the sums d(u, v) + d(x, y), d(u, x) + d(v, y),
d(u, y) + d(v, x) differ by at most 2δ ≥ 0. Gromov hyperbolic graphs constitute a very important
class of graphs in metric graph theory. The unweighted center problem for this class of graphs
and the relationship between the radius, the diameter, and the δ-hyperbolicity were studied
in [34]. Almost unimodality properties of the classical radius function in δ-hyperbolic graphs
(i.e., when π(v) = 1 for all v ∈ V ) were investigated earlier in [4, 48, 49, 51]. Here we consider
arbitrary profiles.
   The following result establishes the statement of Theorem A related to δ-hyperbolic graphs:
Proposition 8.1. All radius functions rπ of a δ-hyperbolic graph G are (4δ + 1)-weakly peakless.
Proof. Let u, v be any pair of vertices of G with d(u, v) ≥ 4δ + 2. Pick any geodesic γ(u, v)
between u and v and let w be a vertex of γ(u, v) such that d(w, u) ≥ 2δ + 1 and d(w, v) ≥ 2δ + 1.

This implies that d(u, v) > max(d(u, w), d(v, w))+2δ. We assert that rπ (w) < max{rπ (u), rπ (v)}.
Indeed, let x be a furthest from w vertex of supp(π), i.e., x is a vertex from the profile such
that rπ (w) = π(x)d(x, w). As d(u, v) + d(w, x) ≤ max{d(u, x) + d(v, w), d(v, x) + d(u, w)} + 2δ
because G is δ-hyperbolic, and d(u, v) > max{d(u, w), d(w, v)} + 2δ, we deduce that d(w, x) <
max{d(u, x), d(v, x)}. Consequently, rπ (w) < max{rπ (u), rπ (v)}, establishing that the radius
function rπ is strictly (4δ + 1)-weakly peakless.                                               □

   Let the δ-hyperbolicity of a graph G be the smallest δ for which G is δ-hyperbolic. Theorem

the largest size κ of an isometric square grid of G: δ = Θ(max{µ, κ}). Similarly to [27, Theorem

   The δ-hyperbolicity of median graphs, (weakly) bridged, and (bipartite) Helly graphs can
be characterized by forbidding more structured patterns. By Corollary 4 of [34] and Lemma
9.7 of [27], the hyperbolicity of a median graph G is characterized by the size of the largest
isometric square grid of G. A similar result can be proved for bipartite Helly graphs. Similarly,
the hyperbolicity of a Helly graph G is characterized by the size of the largest isometric king
grid of G (the king grid is obtained from the square grid by adding the two diagonals of each
square); see [28, Proposition 4.7.] and [47]. Finally, by Corollary 4 of [34], the hyperbolicity δ of
a bridged graph G is characterized by the size ν of a largest isometric deltoid of G: δ = Θ(ν). A
deltoid of size ν is a subgraph of G that is isometric to the equilateral triangle with side ν of the
triangular grid (the tessellation of the plane into equilateral triangles with side 1). As a graph,
the triangular grid can be obtained from the square grid by adding one diagonal to each square
such that all such added diagonals lie on parallel lines. The deltoid of size ν contains a triangular
grid with side ν2 × ν2 (this grid has the form of a lozenge and is obtained by gluing two deltoids
with side ν2 along a common side). Therefore, the hyperbolicity of bridged graphs can be again
characterized by the largest size of a triangular grid (largest lozenge). This characterization can
be extended to all weakly bridged graphs.

that for every vertex s of I ◦ (z, z ′ ) the inequality rπ (s) ≥ max{rπ (z), rπ (z ′ )} holds. By the
construction of G, either at least one of the vertices z, z ′ belongs to Y or one of the vertices z, z ′
coincides with one of the vertices w, c. Consequently, in both cases, at least one of the vertices
z, z ′ , say z, is simplicial. Pick any s ∈ I ◦ (z, z ′ ) adjacent to z. Since z is simplicial, we have
B1 (z) ⊆ B1 (s). We cannot have rπ (s) = π(z), because otherwise, rπ (z ′ ) ≥ 3π(z) > π(z) = rπ (s),
contrary to our assumption about the pair z, z ′ . Therefore, by the above claim, rπ (s) ≤ rπ (z).
Since rπ (s) ≥ max{rπ (z), rπ (z ′ )}, we obtain that rπ (s) = rπ (z). Since z is not a furthest
from s vertex, the equality rπ (s) = rπ (z) implies that z cannot be of degree one, and so,
that it is different from vertices w and c. Consequently, z ∈ Y and s ∈ U . If furthermore
rπ (z ′ ) ≥ rπ (z), then we are done because rπ (s) = rπ (z) ≤ max{rπ (z), rπ (z ′ )} and equality holds
when rπ (z) = rπ (z ′ ). Thus, from now on we can suppose that rπ (z ′ ) < rπ (z) = rπ (s).
   Assume further that z ′ is not simplicial (otherwise, we conclude the proof by considering z ′
instead of z and s′ ∈ N (z ′ )∩I(z, z ′ ) instead of s). Since d(z, z ′ ) ≥ 3, necessarily z ′ ∈ X ∪{v}. Let
t be a furthest from s vertex. Since rπ (z) = rπ (s), we must have d(z, t) ≤ d(s, t). This implies
that t must be different from the vertices a, b, c, v, w, z ′ . On the other hand, since rπ (z ′ ) < rπ (s),
we must have d(z ′ , t) < d(s, t). This implies that t cannot belong to U ∪ Y , as we prove it now.
Indeed, for any vertex u ∈ U we have d(s, u) ≤ 1 (since s ∈ U ) and d(z ′ , u) ≥ 1 (since z ′ ∈        / U ).
Analogously, for any y ∈ Y we have d(s, y) ≤ 2 (since s ∈ U and U is a clique) and d(z ′ , y) ≥ 2
(since z ′ ∈ X ∪ {v}). If z ′ ∈ X, then furthermore t cannot belong to X \ z ′ , because d(z ′ , x) = 2
and d(s, x) ≤ 2 for any x ∈ X \ z ′ . This leads us to a contradiction. Therefore, z ′ = v.
Consequently, in this case, a ∈ I ◦ (z, z ′ ). Suppose rπ (a) ≥ rπ (s) = rπ (z) > rπ (z ′ ) (otherwise, we
34                 J. CHALOPIN, V. CHEPOI, F. DRAGAN, G. DUCOFFE, AND Y. VAXÈS

are done), and let r be a furthest from a vertex. Since rπ (v) = rπ (z ′ ) < rπ (a), we must have
d(v, r) < d(a, r). This implies that r must coincide with one of the vertices v, w. But then,
a ∈ I ◦ (z, r), which implies rπ (z) > rπ (a), a contradiction. Consequently, any radius function rπ
is 2-weakly peakless.                                                                             □

     This concludes the proof of Theorem 9.2.                                                     □


                                       10. Perspectives
   In this paper, using G2 -unimodality of the radius functions, we presented local search sub-
quadratic algorithms (randomized or deterministic) for the weighted and unweighted center
problems on several important classes of graphs: bridged and weakly bridged graphs, graphs
with convex balls, and bipartite Helly graphs. We also presented a divide-and-conquer algo-
rithm with deterministic complexity O(n log2 n) for the weighted center problem on cube-free
median graphs (a subclass of bipartite Helly graphs). Prior to our work, it was only known
how to compute in linear time the 0-1-centers of chordal graphs (a well-known class of graphs
at the intersection of bridged and hyperbolic graphs) and a subquadratic-time algorithm for the
0-1-centers of median graphs.
   We do not have analogous results for the weighted centers of δ-hyperbolic graphs (and, more
generally, of δ-coarse Helly graphs) and we believe that they do not exist. This is because at
small scale (in balls of radius δ), δ-hyperbolic graphs may be arbitrary. Therefore, the algorithm
of [34] for approximating the radius and returning an approximate center of a δ-hyperbolic graph
seems to be the best one can get for the center problem. The results of [34] are for 0-1-profiles,
thus generalizing them to arbitrary profiles is open:

Question 10.1. Is it possible to compute for any profile π on a δ-hyperbolic graph G = (V, E)
in O(|E|) time (or even in subquadratic time) a vertex v that has distance at most O(δ) from
the center Cπ (G)?

  There are also several more general perspectives on the center problem (and our paper provides
partial answers to each of them). The first is about the graphs for which the center problem can
be solved efficiently:

Question 10.2. Characterize the graphs for which the center problem can be solved in sub-
quadratic time for all profiles.

   In the same vein, it will be important to investigate the metric properties of graphs, which
can be useful in the efficient computation of radius, center, and diameter. The 0-1-center
problem in chordal graphs can be solved in linear time, however assuming the Orthogonal Vectors
Conjecture, the diameter problem cannot be solved in subquadratic time. It will be interesting
to investigate the center problem for classes of graphs for which the diameter problem cannot be
solved efficiently. This is related to the question how to compute the radius of a graph without
computing the eccentricities of all its vertices. It is also important to understand the power and
the limits of local search and Gp -unimodality for the center problem in graphs.
   More generally, it will be important to investigate general Gp -unimodal functions (and their
weakly peakless variants) in graphs from the perspectives of discrete convex analysis [70, 82–84]
and the relationships between the complexity of local search and the geometry of graphs [21].
Last but not least, it will be important and challenging to create an algorithmic theory for the
classes of graphs studied in Metric Graph Theory. This question is intimately related to the
notion of metric (fat) minor of a graph, independently introduced in papers [35] and [60] and the
coarse and metric graph theory (an interesting research program on coarse graph theory based
on the notion of metric/fat minor was suggested in recent paper [63]).

                      11. Appendix: The center problem in CB-graphs
   In this section, we first recall the definition of CB-graphs together with characterizations and
properties of these graphs. They are useful to prove that their radius functions are G2 -unimodal.
Then, we consider the algorithmic implications of this result and of the descent algorithms from
Section 4. This part is similar to that for weakly bridged graphs (see Section 5), but it is
technically more involved, and the correctness proof requires additional distance conditions to
hold for the center. Hence, we need to complete the algorithm with a different, and costlier,
search procedure, which ensures that it is always correct even if such distance conditions do not
hold.
11.1. CB-graphs. A graph G is called a graph with convex balls (abbreviately, a CB-graph) if
all balls Br (v) of G are convex [59, 95]. A topological characterization of CB-graphs via their
triangle-pentagon complexes was given in [29].
   As (weakly) bridged graphs, CB-graphs satisfy the Interval Neighborhood Condition (INC),
i.e., for any u, v ∈ V, u ̸= v, the neighbors of v in I(u, v) form a clique. CB-graphs do not satisfy
the triangle condition, but they satisfy the Triangle-Pentagon Condition (TPC), i.e., for any
v, x, y ∈ V such that d(v, x) = d(v, y) = k and x ∼ y, either there exists z ∈ Bk−1 (v) such that
z ∼ x, y, or there exists z ∈ Bk−2 (v), w ∼ x, z, and w′ ∼ y, z such that xwzw′ y is a pentagon.
   Recall now the metric characterizations of CB-graphs.
Theorem 11.1. For a graph G, the following are equivalent:
(1) G is a CB-graph
(2) G satisfies (INC) and isometric cycles have length 3 or 5 [59, 95].
(3) G satisfies the conditions (INC) and (TPC) [29].
     As in (weakly) bridged graphs, the 1-balls in CB-graphs are outergated.
Lemma 11.2 ([29]). Any CB-graph G satisfies the interval-outergate property.
     We will also need the following result about the metric triangles:
Lemma 11.3 ([29]). In a CB-graph G, any metric triangle uvw is strongly equilateral or of type
(2, 2, 1), i.e., d(u, v) = d(u, w) = 2 and d(v, w) = 1 (assuming that d(u, v) ≥ d(u, w) ≥ d(v, w)).
     In CB-graphs, the property of Lemma 5.4 holds but only for vertices at distance at least 3.
Lemma 11.4. If G is a CB-graph and u, v, x ∈ V such that d(x, u) < d(x, v) and d(u, v) ≥ 3,
then there exists wx ∈ N (v) ∩ I(v, u) such that wx ∈ I(v, x).
Proof. Let u′ v ′ x′ be a quasi-median (metric triangle) of the triplet u, v, x and assume that
d(u, v) = d(u, u′ ) + d(u′ , v ′ ) + d(v ′ , v), d(u, x) = d(u, u′ ) + d(u′ , x′ ) + d(x′ , x), and d(v, x) =
d(v, v ′ ) + d(v ′ , x′ ) + d(x′ , x). If v ′ ̸= v, then any neighbor of v in I(v, v ′ ) ⊂ I(v, u) ∩ I(v, x) can
be selected as the desired wx . Now suppose that v ′ = v. Since d(x, u) < d(x, v), the vertices
u′ , v ′ , x′ are pairwise distinct and the metric triangle u′ v ′ x′ cannot be equilateral. Therefore, by
Lemma 11.3, the metric triangle u′ v ′ x′ must have type (2, 2, 1), and we conclude that d(x′ , u′ ) =
1, d(x′ , v) = d(u′ , v) = 2. Since d(u, x) = d(u, u′ ) + 1 + d(x′ , x) < d(v, x) = 2 + d(x′ , x), we must
have u = u′ . However, this contradicts the assumption that d(u, v) ≥ 3.                                       □
11.2. G2 -unimodality of the radius function. We show that as in (weakly) bridged graphs,
in a CB-graph, any radius function is 2-weakly peakless.
Proposition 11.5. All radius functions rπ of a CB-graph G are 2-weakly peakless.

to our assumption that v ∗ is a terminal vertex. Notice also that if Cπ (G) ⊆ B2 (v ∗ ) and π

d(v ∗ , x) = 2 for any x ∈ Cπ (G), if rπ (x) ≤ rπ (v ∗ ) − 2, then for any common neighbor w of x and
v ∗ we will have rπ (w) = rπ (x) + 1 < rπ (v∗), contrary with the fact that v ∗ is a terminal vertex.
