The Characterization Problem asks whether a finite abelian group with Davenport constant at least four is determined, up to isomorphism, by its system of length sets. This is a central inverse problem in non-unique factorization theory: it asks whether the possible numbers of irreducible factors retain enough information to recover the underlying class group. For rings of integers of algebraic number fields, an affirmative answer means that the ideal class group can be recovered from these factorization lengths alone. We resolve this problem for all homocyclic groups C_{n}^{r}: if G is a finite abelian group with \mathsf{D}(G)\geq 4 and \mathcal{L}(G)=\mathcal{L}(C_{n}^{r}), then G\cong C_{n}^{r}. The cross number of a sequence is the sum of the reciprocals of the orders of its terms. This weighted measure of sequence length links zero-sum theory with half-factoriality, and determining its maximal value for minimal zero-sum sequences is a longstanding extremal problem. We also obtain an exact formula for the maximal cross number of minimal zero-sum sequences over subsets whose minimum distance is divisible by the rank of the ambient group minus one. For G\cong C_{n_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n_{r}} with 1 \mathsf{K}(G,r-1)=1+(r-1)\sum_{p\mid n_{1}}\left(1-p^{-\mathsf{v}_{p}(n_{1})}\right), which is a key ingredient in resolving the Characterization Problem for homocyclic groups. Introduction and main results Factorization theory has its origins in algebraic number theory, where the ideal class group measures the failure of unique factorization in rings of integers. A foundational result is Carlitz’s theorem of 1960: the ring of integers of an algebraic number field is half-factorial if and only if its class number is at most two. Thus even the requirement that all factorizations of each element have the same length detects a restriction on the class group. From the late 1980s onwards, the study of non-unique factorizations developed systematically in commutative monoids and integral domains. For an account of this development and the relation between arithmetical invariants and algebraic structure, see the survey [ 14 ] . A central invariant in this programme is the system of length sets. Let H be an atomic monoid. For a nonunit a\in H, the set \mathsf{L}_{H}(a) consists of the lengths of all factorizations of a into atoms. The family \mathcal{L}(H)=\{\mathsf{L}_{H}(a)\colon a\in H\} is the system of length sets of H; for units, we set \mathsf{L}_{H}(a)=\{0\}. Transfer homomorphisms connect the arithmetic of many rings and monoids with additive combinatorics. If H is a transfer Krull monoid over a finite abelian group G, then \mathcal{L}(H)=\mathcal{L}(G), where \mathcal{L}(G):=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B}(G)) is the system of length sets of the monoid \mathcal{B}(G) of zero-sum sequences over G. Rings of integers of algebraic number fields are classical examples, with G their ideal class group [ 4 , 14 ] . The converse direction leads to the Characterization Problem : for finite abelian groups G and G^{\prime} with \mathsf{D}(G)\geq 4, does \mathcal{L}(G)=\mathcal{L}(G^{\prime})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad G\cong G^{\prime} hold? Here \mathsf{D}(G) is the Davenport constant of G. This question belongs to the programme initiated by Narkiewicz in the 1970s of recovering class groups from arithmetical properties. Its significance is that length sets record only the possible numbers of irreducible factors, yet their full system is conjectured to determine the group up to isomorphism. The small-group restriction is necessary: for example, \mathcal{L}(C_{3})=\mathcal{L}(C_{2}^{2}). For background on this problem, see [ 4 , Section 6] and [ 14 , Problem C] , where it is formulated as a long-term problem in factorization theory. Earlier progress is surveyed in [ 25 ] . Schmid established the characterization of C_{n}^{2} in [ 24 ] , and Geroldinger and Schmid treated groups of rank two in [ 10 ] . Products of two atoms provide another source of characterizing information [ 1 ] . For homocyclic groups C_{n}^{r}, that is, direct sums of r cyclic groups of the same order n, the work of Geroldinger and Zhong [ 13 ] and Zhong [ 28 , 27 ] developed methods based on minimal distances and refined elasticities. In particular, Zhong proved the characterization for r\leq n-3, and for r\geq n-1 when n is a prime power [ 27 , Theorem 1.2] . The cases left open by these results are r=n-2 and r\geq n-1 when n is not a prime power. A difficulty is that individual numerical invariants need not distinguish groups. For example, the largest minimal distance is \max\{\exp(G)-2,\mathsf{r}(G)-1\}, so its value alone does not separate the contributions of the exponent and the rank. Zhong’s invariants \rho^{*}(G,d) and \mathsf{K}(G,d) combine restrictions on minimal distances with elasticity and cross numbers [ 27 ] . These invariants provide finer information about the integers n_{1},\ldots,n_{r} in the decomposition G\cong C_{n_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n_{r}} with 1 [ 11 , 15 , 16 ] ; general background is provided by [ 8 , 9 , 7 ] . Cross numbers have a history closely tied to these arithmetical questions. Introduced by Krause in 1984 in the study of algebraic number fields [ 20 ] , and developed by Krause and Zahlten for Krull monoids [ 21 ] , the cross number of a sequence S=g_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot g_{\ell} is \mathsf{k}(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{\ord(g_{i})}. The Davenport constant is the maximum length of a minimal zero-sum sequence. The maximal cross number is the corresponding weighted maximum, with each term g assigned weight 1/\ord(g). The cross-number criterion for half-factoriality gives a direct connection with factorization theory: a subset G_{0} of a finite abelian group is half-factorial if and only if every atom over G_{0} has cross number one [ 8 ] . More generally, cross numbers allow one to compare factorization lengths by using an additive weight on zero-sum sequences. Determining the structure of minimal zero-sum sequences attaining the maximal cross number is an associated inverse problem in additive combinatorics [ 6 ] . Determining the maximal cross number \mathsf{K}(G) is a difficult extremal problem. If G\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}C_{q_{i}}, where each q_{i} is a prime power, the classical cross-number conjecture predicts \mathsf{K}(G)=\frac{1}{\exp(G)}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\left(1-\frac{1}{q_{i}}\right). The right-hand side is attained by an explicit construction; the difficulty is to prove the matching upper bound for arbitrary minimal zero-sum sequences [ 21 , 19 ] . The formula holds for p-groups [ 5 ] , while subsequent work has treated further families and developed bounds and structural methods [ 6 , 18 , 19 ] . For groups with several prime divisors, projecting a minimal zero-sum sequence to its primary components need not preserve minimality, which obstructs a direct reduction to the p-group case. The invariant considered here restricts the cross-number problem to subsets satisfying a condition on their minimum distance. Its exact value can then be used, through refined elasticities, to recover information about the cyclic factors of the group. We study the invariant \mathsf{K}(G,d) introduced in [ 27 , Definition 3.3] . It is the largest cross number of an atom over a subset whose set of distances is nonempty and has minimum divisible by d. Precise definitions are given in Section 2 . Our first result determines this invariant at d=\mathsf{r}(G)-1 when the rank is at least \exp(G)-2. The formula shows that, in this range, the invariant depends only on the rank r and the order n_{1} of the smallest cyclic factor in the displayed decomposition. Theorem 1.1 . Let G\cong C_{n_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n_{r}} be a finite abelian group with 1 \mathsf{K}(G,r-1)=1+(r-1)\sum_{p\mid n_{1}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{\mathsf{v}_{p}(n_{1})}}\right), where the sum runs over the distinct prime divisors of n_{1}. For G=C_{n}^{r} and r\geq n-1, Theorem 1.1 proves Conjecture 5.1, C2, of [ 27 ] . It also proves C1: letting s=n\sum_{p\mid n}(1-p^{-\mathsf{v}_{p}(n)}), one has \gcd(s,n)=1. Indeed, for each prime p\mid n, the integer s is congruent to -n/p^{\mathsf{v}_{p}(n)} modulo p. Our second result resolves the Characterization Problem for homocyclic finite abelian groups. Theorem 1.2 . Let n\geq 2 and r\geq 1 be integers, and let G be a finite abelian group with \mathsf{D}(G)\geq 4. If \mathcal{L}(G)=\mathcal{L}(C_{n}^{r}), then G\cong C_{n}^{r}. Zhong proved the assertion when r\leq n-3, and also when r\geq n-1 and n is a prime power [ 27 , Theorem 1.2] . Theorem 1.1 , together with [ 27 , Proposition 5.2] , gives the result for r\geq n-1. The remaining case is r=n-2. For n\geq 6, we compare \rho^{*}(G,n-3) with the corresponding invariant of C_{n}^{n-2}; the cases n\in\{4,5\} are treated separately. The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the notation and collects the preliminary results. Section 3 establishes the cross-number formula in Theorem 1.1 . Sections 4 and 5 develop the structural results and elasticity comparisons used in Section 6 to prove Theorem 1.2 . Finally, Section 7 presents further questions. 2. Background on zero-sum sequences and length sets We fix our notation and recall the definitions needed in the sequel. For general background on zero-sum sequences and factorization theory, we refer to [ 8 , 9 , 7 ] . We denote by \mathbb{N} the set of positive integers and set \mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}. For integers a\leq b, we write [a,b]=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:a\leq k\leq b\}. For a prime p and m\in\mathbb{N}, let \mathsf{v}_{p}(m) denote the p-adic valuation of m. Throughout, groups are written additively. Let G be a nontrivial finite abelian group, and write G\cong C_{n_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n_{r}},\qquad\text{ with }\quad 1 where C_{m} denotes a cyclic group of order m. We denote by \mathsf{r}(G)=r and \exp(G)=n_{r} the rank and exponent of G, respectively. For g\in G, let \ord(g) denote its order; in particular, \ord(0)=1. For a prime p, we denote by G_{p} the p-primary component of G and by \pi_{p}:G\to G_{p} the canonical projection. For an integer m and a subset A\subset G, we set m\cdot A=\{ma:a\in A\}. Let G_{0}\subset G. We denote by \mathcal{F}(G_{0}) the multiplicatively written free abelian monoid with basis G_{0}. Its elements are finite unordered sequences over G_{0}, with repetitions allowed. For a sequence S=g_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot g_{\ell}=\prod_{g\in G_{0}}g^{\mathsf{v}_{g}(S)}\in\mathcal{F}(G_{0}), we denote by \mathsf{v}_{g}(S) the multiplicity of g in S and set |S|=\ell,\qquad\supp(S)=\{g\in G_{0}:\mathsf{v}_{g}(S)>0\},\qquad\sigma(S)=g_{1}+\ldots+g_{\ell}=\sum_{g\in G_{0}}\mathsf{v}_{g}(S)g. The identity element 1=1_{\mathcal{F}(G_{0})} is the trivial sequence with |1|=0 and \sigma(1)=0. We call a sequence squarefree if each of its terms has multiplicity one. We write T\mid S if T is a subsequence of S. In this case, we write ST^{-1} for the sequence obtained by deleting T from S. We call S a zero-sum sequence if \sigma(S)=0, and zero-sum free if it has no nontrivial zero-sum subsequence. The monoid of zero-sum sequences over G_{0} is \mathcal{B}(G_{0})=\{S\in\mathcal{F}(G_{0}):\sigma(S)=0\}. Its atoms (or irreducibles) are the minimal zero-sum sequences, that is, the nontrivial zero-sum sequences having no proper nontrivial zero-sum subsequence. We set \mathcal{A}(G_{0}):=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{B}(G_{0})) and denote by \mathsf{D}(G)=\max\{|U|:U\in\mathcal{A}(G)\} the Davenport constant of G. For B\in\mathcal{B}(G_{0}), we denote by \mathsf{L}(B) its length set. Thus k\in\mathsf{L}(B) if and only if B has a factorization into k atoms, and \mathsf{L}(1)=\{0\} by convention. We set \mathcal{L}(G_{0}):=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B}(G_{0}))=\{\mathsf{L}(B):B\in\mathcal{B}(G_{0})\}. We call G_{0} half-factorial if every length set in \mathcal{L}(G_{0}) is a singleton. For a finite nonempty set L\subset\mathbb{N}, we set the elasticity \rho(L)=\max L/\min L, and set \rho(\{0\})=1. For B\in\mathcal{B}(G_{0}), its elasticity is \rho(\mathsf{L}(B)). For G_{0}\neq\emptyset, we set \rho(G_{0})=\sup\left\{\rho(\mathsf{L}(B))\colon B\in\mathcal{B}(G_{0})\right\}, and set \rho(\emptyset)=1. For the empty subset, we have \mathcal{F}(\emptyset)=\mathcal{B}(\emptyset)=\{1\}, \mathcal{L}(\emptyset)=\{\{0\}\}, and \Delta(\emptyset)=\emptyset. For S\in\mathcal{F}(G_{0}), its cross number is \mathsf{k}(S)=\sum_{g\in G_{0}}\frac{\mathsf{v}_{g}(S)}{\ord(g)}. For G_{0}\neq\emptyset, we set \mathsf{K}(G_{0})=\max\{\mathsf{k}(U):U\in\mathcal{A}(G_{0})\}. We call G_{0} a large cross number set (an LCN-set ) if every atom over G_{0} has cross number at least one. For cross-number invariants and half-factorial subsets, see [ 21 , 18 , 19 , 3 , 22 , 23 ] . For a finite set L\subset\mathbb{Z}, let \Delta(L) denote the set of positive differences between consecutive elements of L. We set \Delta(G_{0}):=\Delta(\mathcal{B}(G_{0}))=\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}(G_{0})}\Delta(\mathsf{L}(B)) and \Delta^{*}(G)=\{\min\Delta(G_{0}):G_{0}\subset G,\ \Delta(G_{0})\neq\emptyset\}. We call \Delta(G_{0}) the set of distances of G_{0} and \Delta^{*}(G) the set of minimal distances of G. If \Delta(G_{0})\neq\emptyset, then \min\Delta(G_{0}) is the minimum distance of G_{0}. In particular, G_{0} is half-factorial if and only if \Delta(G_{0})=\emptyset. We denote by \mathsf{m}(G) the largest minimum distance of a non-half-factorial LCN-subset of G, and set \mathsf{m}(G)=0 if no such subset exists. For results on distances and minimal distances, see [ 26 , 12 , 17 , 28 ] . We denote by \Delta_{1}(G) the set of all d\in\mathbb{N} for which \mathcal{L}(G) contains arbitrarily long almost arithmetic progressions of difference d with a common bound, in the sense of [ 27 , Section 2] . For d\in\Delta_{1}(G), define \mathsf{K}(G,d)=\max\{\mathsf{K}(G_{0}):G_{0}\subset G,\ \Delta(G_{0})\neq\emptyset,\ d\mid\min\Delta(G_{0})\} and \rho^{*}(G,d)=\max\{\rho(G_{0}):G_{0}\subset G,\ \Delta(G_{0})\neq\emptyset,\ d\mid\min\Delta(G_{0})\}. These maxima are taken over nonempty families [ 27 , Definition 3.3] . Thus \rho^{*}(G,d) is the largest elasticity of a non-half-factorial subset whose minimum distance is divisible by d; \mathsf{K}(G,d) is the corresponding maximum of the cross numbers of atoms over such subsets. For every d\in\Delta_{1}(G), we have \rho^{*}(G,d)\geq\mathsf{K}(G,d). The structural arguments in Sections 4 and 5 concern atoms of cross number less than one at minimum distances \exp(G)-3 and \exp(G)-2, respectively. Their supports have three and two elements. More generally, Lemma 2.4 .1 determines their lengths when the minimum distance is at least \lfloor\exp(G)/2\rfloor. Descriptions of the subgroups generated by larger supports, and of their intersections with the subgroups generated by the remaining elements, may yield further elasticity comparisons and applications to the Characterization Problem.
