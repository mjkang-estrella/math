Abstract
    
Subtraction games have a rich literature as normal-play combinatorial games (e.g., Berlekamp, Conway, and Guy, 1982). Recently, the theory has been extended to zero-sum scoring play (Cohensius et al. 2019). Here, we take the approach of cumulative self-interest games, as introduced in a recent framework preprint by Larsson, Meir, and Zick. By adapting standard Pure Subgame Perfect Equilibria (PSPE) from classical game theory, players must declare and commit to acting either “friendly” or “antagonistic” in case of indifference.
Whenever the subtraction set has size two, we establish a tie-breaking rule monotonicity: a friendly player can never benefit by a deterministic deviation to antagonistic play. This type of terminology is new to both “economic” and “combinatorial” games, but it becomes essential in the self-interest cumulative setting. The main result is an immediate consequence of the tie-breaking rule’s monotonicity; in the case of two-action subtraction sets, two antagonistic players are never better off than two friendly players, i.e., their PSPE utilities are never greater. For larger subtraction sets, we conjecture that the main result continues to hold, while tie-breaking monotonicity may fail, and we provide empirical evidence in support of both statements.

  



1 Introduction



We study two-player alternating play perfect information games, and we adapt the following definition of a self-interest symmetric cumulative subtraction game  $S\subset\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}$ . There is a single heap of pebbles, of size  $h\in\mathbb{N}_{0}$ , and the players alternately remove  $s\in S$  pebbles from the heap, until the current player is not able to move from some position  $h$  because, for all  $s\in S,h-s<0$ . The players are called Alice and Bob, and whatever they remove, from the common heap, they put in their own individual pocket. Each player aims to maximize the size of their pocket (i.e., their cumulative utility) when the game ends.




We adapt standard tools from game theory, in particular the classical backward induction procedure, applied to player profiles in Pure Subgame Perfect Equilibrium (PSPE). To avoid ambiguity in the recursive evaluation and to ensure the game tree is well-defined, we assume deterministic tie-breaking in cases of indifference. We refer to a pair of tie-breaking conventions, one for each player, as deterministic if they satisfy six properties. They cannot be random or hidden in any way, and they must be universal, meaning that they are applied to every position whenever there is indifference between options, with respect to PSPE-computation. They must be uniform, meaning that the same conventions are used across all positions. They must be Markovian (or history-independent), that is, they cannot depend on previous actions in any way. Finally, they are unilateral, meaning that each player’s convention is independent of the other player’s choice. One can prove by induction that PSPE play is indeed well defined by using these tie-breaking axioms.




By using a pair of deteministic tie-breaking conventions, players still maximize their own utility. However, when multiple options yield the same personal outcome, the choice among them may influence the opponent’s utility. We classify this influence as friendly when it benefits the opponent and antagonistic when it harms them. This effect is understood to be local, meaning that it depends only on outcome discrepancies within any given pair of deterministic tie-breaking conventions. As shown in the Appendix, page Appendix: Unilateral deviation for larger subtraction sets, such local choices can have globally counterintuitive consequences, when viewed across ranges of possible conventions.




Deterministic tie-breaking conventions are commitments that are applied consistently throughout the game tree, rather than as decisions made at individual nodes. For instance, given a pair of tie-breaking conventions, for every heap size, even one as large as  $10^{10}$ , a player is required to know whether they are indifferent. If so, for example a player who adopts a friendly convention, must then select a move that maximizes the opponent’s result, based on the already computed PSPE utilities of the resulting subpositions.




Following standard assumptions in combinatorial game theory, we assume the existence of such perfectly rational and computationally capable players. When both players adopt the friendly convention, we refer to the resulting setting as FvF or the friendly convention. If both adopt antagonistic conventions, we denote it AvA or the antagonistic convention. These will serve as our primary self-interest frameworks throughout the paper.11

          In our model, the tie-breaking rule is fixed in advance and is not determined as part of the PSPE. Analyzing strategic choice over tie-breaking behavior would require a different model, such as a simultaneous-play normal form with mixed strategies. Here, we focus instead on the recursive structure and properties of combinatorial games.




Subtraction games have been studied as win/loss games, mostly in the last-move convention “a player who cannot move loses” [7, 30], but also more recently as zero-sum scoring-play games [14]. The current self-interest setting was introduced in the framework preprint [31]. Let us first recall that setting, and after that (in Definition 1) we will introduce a slight generalization that will be convenient for the purpose of this paper and will also raise new interesting research problems.




In an antagonistic self-interest cumulative subtraction game, we define the (self-interest) outcome function of a game  $S\subset\mathbb{N}$  for the two players as the ordered pair  $o_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvA}}(h)=(o^{1}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvA}}(h),o^{2}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvA}}(h))$ , where  $h\in\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}$  represents the current heap size.
The cumulative utilities (a.k.a individual outcomes) can be recursively calculated using the outcome of heaps of smaller size. Let the first player choose  $s\in S$  as their first move in PSPE play. Then  $o^{1}(h)=s+o^{2}(h-s)$  and  $o^{2}(h)=o^{1}(h-s)$ . Thus, after the first move the heap size is reduced to  $(h-s)$  and the order of the players is switched (since the next move is made by the originally second player). Here, whenever there is indifference (i.e., more than one action leading to the same PSPE cumulation for the current player), the action chosen minimizes the PSPE cumulation for the other player, representing antagonistic behavior. In the case of friendly cumulative play, the individual outcomes  $o^{1}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}$  and  $o^{2}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}$  are analogous, but the action chosen maximizes the other player’s cumulation in case of indifference, representing friendly behavior. Note that these two settings remain symmetric, in the sense that the outcome pairs do not depend on “who starts”, Alice or Bob. Thus, the setting represents a distant relative to the classical ‘impartial’ combinatorial games.




The first question is whether there will be any outcome-discrepancies between both players adapting friendly tie-breaking rules versus both players adapting antagonistic tie-breaking rules. Let player  $i$ ’s discrepancy be  $\delta^{i}(h)=o^{i}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvA}}(h)-o^{i}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}(h)$ , for  $i\in\{1,2\}$ .

The corresponding PSPE move sequence is 3-3-5-3, where Alice makes the first and third removal. We experimentally discovered a number of such examples of discrepancies in the two and three action settings, summarized in Section 4, with related conjectures. The main result of this paper, Theorem 2, is that, if the subtraction set has size 2, then, for any heap size, each player’s utility is no worse in the FvF setting than the AvA setting. We conjecture that this result continues to hold if  $|S|>2$ ; see Section 2.




Readers familiar with zero-sum games may note that the move sequence in the previous paragraph cannot appear in an optimal-play zero-sum setting, where player removals add and subtract to a common score, respectively. Alice would instead start by removing 5, and hence Bob must take 5, and Alice gets the last move by taking 3. This, of course leads to a loss in “social welfare” as the whole heap would not be consumed.
It related topic explores the similarities between the  ${\rm AvA}$  antagonistic and the zero-sum settings. We explore discrepancies between the games in a follow up paper [28], where we prove that if  $|S|=2$ , stated here as a conjecture (see Section 5), then optimal play coincides for zero-sum games and the antagonistic setting. However, experimental studies reveal that discrepancies appear already for  $|S|=3$  (related conjectures appear in Section 5).




Let us return to the main topic for this paper, concerning discrepancies between friendly and antagonistic tie-breaking rules. If the players play many rounds of the same game, and they have so far both been committed to the friendly setting, could either player benefit by deviating and becoming antagonistic?




Question 1.

Could any personalized player benefit by a unilateral deviation from a deterministic friendly tie-breaking?

2 The model and main result



Question 1 induces two more settings. Let us list them all.


FvF: 

both players act friendly, in case of indifference;




FvA: 

Alice, as a starting player, acts friendly and Bob is antagonistic, in case of indifference;




AvF: 

Bob, as the second player, acts friendly whereas Alice is antagonistic, in case of indifference;




AvA: 

both players are antagonistic, in case of indifference.








Observe that this requires a generalization of the outcome function.
Let  $\tau=\{{\rm FvF},{\rm AvF},{\rm FvA},{\rm AvA}\}$ , and let  $X\in\tau$ . The dual of  $X$ , denoted  $\mathsf{d}(X)$ , is defined by:





 $\mathsf{d}(X)=\begin{cases}{\rm FvF}&\text{if }X={\rm FvF},\\
{\rm FvA}&\text{if }X={\rm AvF},\\
{\rm AvF}&\text{if }X={\rm FvA},\\
{\rm AvA}&\text{if }X={\rm AvA}.\end{cases}$ 


Thus, for all  $X$ ,  $\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{d}(X))=X$  and  $\mathsf{d}(X)=X$  if and only if  $X\in\{{\rm FvF},{\rm AvA}\}$ .




Definition 1 (Outcomes).

Consider a heap size  $h\geq 0$  and  $S(h):=S\cap\{1,\ldots,h\}$ . Let, for all  $X\in\tau$ ,  $o^{1}_{X}(h)=o^{2}_{X}(h)=0$ , if  $h<\min S$ . Otherwise, let





 $\displaystyle o^{1}_{X}(h)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\max\{o^{2}_{\mathsf{d}(X)}(h-s)+s\ |\ s\in S(h)\},$ 



 $\displaystyle o^{2}_{X}(h)$ 
 $\displaystyle=o^{1}_{\mathsf{d}(X)}(h-s^{*}),$ 


where





 $s^{*}=\textrm{argmin}_{s\in S^{*}(h)}\ o^{1}_{\mathsf{d}(X)}(h-s),$ 


if  $X\in\{{\rm AvF},{\rm AvA}\}$ , and otherwise





 $s^{*}=\textrm{argmax}_{s\in S^{*}(h)}\ o^{1}_{\mathsf{d}(X)}(h-s).$ 


Here  $S^{*}(h)\subseteq S$  denotes the set of indifferent actions; this set contains the set of actions the current player can take from  $h$  that give them the same cumulation.33
              3
              
              
              
            There is some abuse of notation, since this action may not be unique; but if it is not unique, then there is no meaningful difference between them, as they will lead to the same PSPE cumulations for both players, when the game ends.
For all  $X\in\tau$ , for all  $h\geq 0$ , let  $o_{X}(h)=(o^{1}_{X}(h),o^{2}_{X}(h))$  be the  $X$ -outcome of  $h$ .






Given a tiebreaking convention, we usually refer to a pair of (EGT) utilities in PSPE simply as a (CGT) outcome. Note that even if the ruleset is symmetric, since the players may have different tie-breaking rules, a game played is not necessarily symmetric in the strict sense. If one player is antagonistic at some stage of play then they are committed to being antagonistic at all stages of play, and similarly for “friendly”. It is a situation that cannot appear in standard CGT impartial games. The move options, in this sense, depend on their committed tie-breaking rule. To our best knowledge such situations have not yet been studied in the literature.




Thus there are four deviation cases to consider, listed as FvF  $\rightarrow$  AvF, FvF  $\rightarrow$  FvA, AvA  $\rightarrow$  FvA and AvA  $\rightarrow$  AvF. For example in FvF  $\rightarrow$  AvF, in the FvF setting both players resolve ties in a friendly manner, while in AvF, Alice (playing first) deviates with an antagonistic tie-breaking rule.




In the case  $|S|=2$ , we here prove that a unilateral deviation from friendly tie-breaking to antagonistic ditto never benefits either player; as we will see, whenever starting from FvF, the deviating player will be unaffected, while the other player may suffer in some cases. As a consequence we will get:




Theorem 2 (Main Theorem).

Consider any subtraction set  $S$ , with  $|S|=2$ , and suppose both players act friendly in case of indifference. Then each player’s PSPE utility is never worse than if both players have antagonistic tie-breaking rules.






Our methods to prove this result do not apply in general if  $|S|>2$ , and we provide a counter example, when unilateral deviation is non-monotone, namely if  $S=\{4,5,9\}$ , in Table 3 in the Appendix.
We will study the two unilateral deviations from FvF, to FvA and AvF, while the converse/symmetric deviations from AvA, to AvF and FvA are analogous. A unilateral deviation from friendly to antagonistic may, in rare cases, be beneficial for the deviating player; still, we have numerical evidence that Theorem 2 continues to hold for any subtraction set of size greater than 2 (see Section 6). However, we currently have no formal method to jump directly from both friendly to both antagonistic.




Conjecture 3.

Consider any subtraction set  $S$ , and suppose both players act friendly in case of indifference. Then each player’s PSPE utility is never worse than if both players have antagonistic tie-breaking rules.






Consider ordered pairs of integers,  $(a,b)$  and  $(c,d)$ . Then  $(a,b)\leq(c,d)$  if  $a\leq c$  and  $b\leq d$ , and similar with “ $\geq$ ”.






3 Unilateral tie-breaking deviation and the proof of main result



Suppose both players follow deterministic tie-breaking conventions. We are interested in comparing the outcome under one such setting, where both players are universally friendly, with the setting in which exactly one of the players deviates, by committing to an antagonistic tie-breaking rule.




Whenever the subtraction set,  $S=\{s_{2},s_{1}\}$ , has exactly two actions, then we claim that the deviating player’s PSPE-utilities will be exactly the same as if they would have remained friendly, and the other player’s PSPE-utilities cannot improve. This claim holds independently of who starts, and we prove it in Lemma 7 in this section.




In the coming sections, we will fix the convention  $s_{2}<s_{1}$ .




Through Lemma 7, we see how the claims in the first paragraph combine in an induction proof. For any two-action subtraction set  $S$ , let  $\Delta=s_{1}-s_{2}>0$ . A greedy move from  $h\geq s_{1}$  is to play to  $h-s_{1}$ , and otherwise a player sacrifices. The endgame refers to play to a heap size  $h<s_{1}$ . The slack is a leftover that cannot be attained by either player, and so it refers to a heap of size  $h<s_{2}$ .




There is a general proof of the next result in the zero-sum setting [14]. Here it only holds in the two-action setting. See Table 3 in the Appendix on page Appendix: Unilateral deviation for larger subtraction sets, where  $S=\{4,5,9\}$  and where heaps of sizes  $61,75,\ldots$  have strictly better PSPE utility for the second player.




Lemma 4 (First Player Advantage).

If  $|S|=2$ , then the starting player’s utility in PSPE is never worse than that of the second player.






Proof.

Recall  $s_{1}>s_{2}$ . Suppose Alice plays greedily, until she cannot play  $s_{1}$ . Then she gets utility  $ks_{1}+\ell s_{2}$ , some  $k>0$ , some  $\ell\geq 0$ . If Bob gets to play  $k$  copies of  $s_{1}$  then Alice has first player advantage at the endgame too, and hence, Bob cannot get more than  $ks_{1}+\ell s_{2}$ . If Bob can only collect  $k-1$  copies of  $s_{1}$ , then he can at most get one more copy of  $s_{2}$  than Alice, but their utility difference is at least  $\Delta>0$  in Alice’s favor.
∎






We distinguish two parameter regimes for  $S=\{s_{2},s_{1}\}$ :


• 

dominance:  $2s_{2}\leq s_{1}$ ;




• 

non-dominance:  $2s_{2}>s_{1}$ .





In case of dominance, the boundary case  $2s_{2}=s_{1}$  is the balanced regime.




The usefulness of a sacrifice may appear limited while playing on a single heap of tokens. However, simple arithmetic shows that if  $S=\{2,3\}$  then Alice benefits strictly when playing from a heap of size  $7$ , and if  $S=\{3,4\}$ , then she can benefit strictly by sacrificing twice, playing from  $h=17$ . There are many more such examples, and those are analogous to the zero-sum setting studied in [14], but in two-action zero-sum games, the usefulness of a sacrifice is limited to basic aritmetic considerations for small heap sizes.
However, self-interest play appears more multifaceted. As shown in Table 1, there are situations where, under a deterministic tie-breaking rule, Alice sacrifices solely to benefit Bob. Note that the example occurs in the non-dominant regime. By contrast, the dominant regime generally exhibits less complex behavior.




Lemma 5 (Dominance and Greedy).

Consider PSPE play under any deterministic tie-breaking rule, and let  $S=\{s_{2},s_{1}\}$  with  $s_{2}<s_{1}$ . In the dominant regime, i.e. if




(1)

 $2s_{2}\leq s_{1},$ 


then a player cannot benefit strictly by sacrificing.






Proof.

The proof will use a strategy-stealing argument to establish a contradiction. Suppose Alice sacrifices in her first move and profits strictly in PSPE play. This means that she must get at least  $k$  more  $s_{2}$  actions to compensate for her initial sacrifice, if  $s_{1}=ks_{2}+r$ ,  $0\leq r<k$ . However, since  $k\geq 2$ , this requires that Bob also plays a sacrifice at some point. For otherwise Bob gets at least  $3\Delta-s_{1}\geq\Delta>0$  larger utility than Alice, while Lemma 4 assures that Alice as a starting player never gets worse than Bob in PSPE play. But Bob can replace this sacrifice with  $s_{1}$ , by stealing  $ks_{2}+r\leq(k+1)s_{2}$  of Alice’s required removal segment. Hence Alice’s first sacrifice cannot lead to a strict gain.
∎






A consequence of this result is that, in case of dominance,  $s_{1}$  is never a bad choice for the current player (if feasible). And indeed, experimental results do point towards consistently zero discrepancies in the dominant regime.




In a zero-sum combinatorial game setting, parity consideration can be central, determining whether you or the opponent gets the last move. Here, it remains a decisive factor in the following sense. The concept of a ‘last move’ is individualized; for all sufficiently large heaps (depending on  $S$ ) both players have a last move. Sometimes you can sacrifice and secure some parity advantage, you win a last move that you otherwise would not have reached.




We are often interested in understanding play from the options of a heap of size  $h$ . This motivates us to denote the branches  $H_{1}=h-s_{1}$  and  $H_{2}=h-s_{2}$ , and think of the  $H_{i}$ s as games with full game trees, rather than ‘heap sizes’.




Lemma 6 (Last Move and Sacrifice).

If a player sacrifices playing in PSPE from a heap of size  $h$ , independently of tie-breaking rules, they know that one of the two scenarios apply:


(i) 

they will be able to replace their last move  $s_{2}$  in  $H_{1}$  for  $s_{1}$  in the  $H_{2}$  branch.




(ii) 

they will be able to play a last move on the  $H_{2}$  branch that they would not have been able to play on the greedy branch  $H_{1}$  (they get one additional move on the  $H_{2}$  branch), and the regime is not strictly dominant;










Proof.

Suppose that they sacrifice without either of the items being satisfied. Then play greedily on  $H_{1}$ , and get a larger utility, by at least  $\Delta$  on this branch.




More explicitly, consider (i). Here, their last move  $s_{2}$  in  $H_{1}$  is replcaed by  $s_{1}$  in  $H_{2}$ , giving them a gain of  $\Delta$  in  $H_{2}$ . Meanwhile, the first move in  $H_{1}$  and  $H_{2}$  is  $s_{1}$  and  $s_{2}$  respectively. Therefore, in the first move they have a loss of  $\Delta$  in  $H_{2}$ . Thus, with the gain and loss balanced out in  $H_{1}$  and  $H_{2}$ , both sacrifice and greedy play is in PSPE.




In case of (ii), the sacrificing player loses  $\Delta=s_{1}-s_{2}$  in their first move. Since the slack, at the end of  $H_{1}$ , is strcitly less than  $s_{2}$ , the gain in the added last move in  $H_{2}$  is strictly less than  $s_{2}+\Delta=s_{1}$ . Thus, the last move can only be  $s_{2}$ . Therefore, in the strictly dominant regime ( $\Delta>s_{2}$ ), the  $H_{2}$  branch gives a strcitly lesser utility and thus sacrifice cannot be in PSPE. In balanced regime ( $\Delta=s_{2}$ ) a player performs equally in  $H_{1}$  and  $H_{2}$ , and in non-dominant regime ( $\Delta<s_{2}$ ),  $H_{2}$  branch gives a strictly better utility. Thus, in both these cases sacrifice play is in PSPE.




Now, suppose a player sacrifices, but neither (i) nor (ii) applies. Then on the branch  $H_{2}$  they cannot obtain an extra move and cannot upgrade a final  $s_{2}$  to  $s_{1}$ . Thus, their subsequent utility from  $H_{2}$  is the same as from  $H_{1}$ . But they are behind by  $\Delta=s_{1}-s_{2}$  already after the first move. Hence the greedy branch  $H_{1}$  gives strictly larger utility. This contradicts the assumption that the sacrifice is played in PSPE. Therefore in PSPE any sacrifice must be justified by either (i) or (ii).
∎






Lemma 7 (Tie-breaking Monotonicity, FvF).

Consider  $S=\{s_{2},s_{1}\}$ , with  $s_{2}<s_{1}$ . Then, for any heap size  $h$ ,


1. 

 $o^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h)\geq o^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvA}}}(h)$ ;




2. 

 $o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h)=o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvA}}}(h)$ ;




3. 

 $o^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h)=o^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvF}}}(h)$ ;




4. 

 $o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h)\geq o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvF}}}(h)$ .










Proof.

The statement holds for  $h<s_{2}$ . Suppose the result holds for all heap sizes smaller than  $h\geq s_{2}$ .
Alice has at least one PSPE move in FvF; denote this by  $s^{\prime}$ , and similarly, for a given  $X\in\{{\rm FvA},{\rm AvF}\}$ , she has at least one PSPE move; denote this by  $s^{\prime\prime}$ . (Here  $s^{\prime}$  and  $s^{\prime\prime}$  may differ or be the same.) Thus, we have:


• 

 $o^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h)=s^{\prime}+o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h-s^{\prime})$ ;




• 

 $o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h)=o^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h-s^{\prime})$ ;




• 

 $o^{1}_{X}(h)=s^{\prime\prime}+o^{2}_{\mathsf{d}(X)}(h-s^{\prime\prime})$ ;




• 

 $o^{2}_{X}(h)=o^{1}_{\mathsf{d}(X)}(h-s^{\prime\prime})$ .








Hence, concerning the two first items in the statement (so  $X={\rm FvA}$ ), it suffices to prove that


(i) 

 $s^{\prime}+o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h-s^{\prime})\geq s^{\prime\prime}+o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvF}}}(h-s^{\prime\prime})$ ;




(ii) 

 $o^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h-s^{\prime})=o^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvF}}}(h-s^{\prime\prime})$ .





By induction, using item (4) in the statement, we have that  $o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h-s^{\prime\prime})\geq o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvF}}}(h-s^{\prime\prime})$ , and thus, by the PSPE assumption on  $s^{\prime}$  in FvF, the inequality in item (i) holds.





To prove the equality in item (ii), observe that, if  $s^{\prime}=s^{\prime\prime}$ , then the equality holds by induction, since, by item (3) in the statement, we may replace  $o^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h-s^{\prime})$  with  $o^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvF}}}(h-s^{\prime})$ . We study two cases when the actions differ.





Case 1,  $s^{\prime}>s^{\prime\prime}$ : Suppose that  $s^{\prime}=s_{1}$  and  $s^{\prime\prime}=s_{2}$ , corresponding to Alice’s choices, playing from  $h$  in FvF and FvA, respectively, and let  $H_{1}=h-s_{1}$  and  $H_{2}=h-s_{2}$ . By induction we may identify  $o^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(H_{1})=o^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvF}}}(H_{1})$ , and study both options in the same convention, say AvF. Thus, it suffices to prove that




(2)

 $\displaystyle o^{1}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvF}}(H_{1})=o^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvF}}}(H_{2}),$ 


given that Alice sacrificed in her first PSPE move from  $h$  in FvA. Recall, Bob plays first from the options of  $h$ .




First we prove that Bob cannot do strictly better on the smaller heap  $H_{1}=H_{2}-\Delta$  in PSPE. This follows because he can mimic (couple) all moves from the smaller heap to the larger heap. If Alice at some stage plays  $s_{1}$  on  $H_{2}$  and  $s_{2}$  on  $H_{1}$ , then this coupled move pair compensates exactly the initial  $\Delta$  difference, so the heaps are equalized. And, given PSPE play by Alice on both heaps, since they follow the same tie-breaking convention (2) Bob’s mimic strategy obviously remains a valid strategy. (If Alice plays a smaller move on  $H_{2}$ , but the larger one on  $H_{1}$ , Bob’s strategy remains feasible.)




Now we show that Bob cannot do strictly better on the larger heap  $H_{2}$ . Alice sacrificed in her first PSPE move on  $H_{2}$ , so we know that she does not have a parity disadvantage on that heap. Let us argue first in case of dominance. By Lemma 5, Lemma 6 (ii) applies, and so the initial  $\Delta$  loss will be compensated by  $\Delta$  in the endgame. In case of non-dominance, Lemma 6 (i) might apply. But in this case  $\Delta<s_{2}$ , so Bob’s moves on the larger heap can be coupled on the smaller heap, because Alice’s share of the larger heap increased by  $s_{2}$  due to parity advantage.





Case 2,  $s^{\prime}<s^{\prime\prime}$ : Suppose next that  $s^{\prime}=s_{2}$  and  $s^{\prime\prime}=s_{1}$ . By the induction argument in Case 1, again it suffices to justify (2), which is already established.





Concerning the two last items in the statement (so  $X={\rm AvF}$ ), it suffices to prove that


(iii) 

 $s^{\prime}+o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h-s^{\prime})=s^{\prime\prime}+o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvA}}}(h-s^{\prime\prime})$ ;




(iv) 

 $o^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h-s^{\prime})\geq o^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvA}}}(h-s^{\prime\prime})$ .





For item (iii), by induction, we have that




(3)

 $\displaystyle o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h-s^{\prime\prime})=o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvA}}}(h-s^{\prime\prime})$ 


and




(4)

 $\displaystyle o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h-s^{\prime})=o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvA}}}(h-s^{\prime}).$ 





Hence item (iii) holds, whenever Alice is indifferent between her move options from  $h$  in FvF or AvF , or if  $s^{\prime}=s^{\prime\prime}$ . Thus, the only case we must rule out is whenever the two settings have differing single-move PSPE options. But then  $s^{\prime}+o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h-s^{\prime})>s^{\prime\prime}+o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h-s^{\prime\prime})$  and  $s^{\prime}+o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvA}}}(h-s^{\prime})<s^{\prime\prime}+o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvA}}}(h-s^{\prime\prime})$ , where  $s^{\prime}$  and  $s^{\prime\prime}$  are PSPE in FvF and FvA, respectively. Thus, by induction, using (3) and (4),  $s^{\prime}+o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h-s^{\prime})>s^{\prime\prime}+o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h-s^{\prime\prime})$  and  $s^{\prime}+o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h-s^{\prime})<s^{\prime\prime}+o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h-s^{\prime\prime})$ , a contradiction.




For item (iv), the proof is analogous to the second part of (ii), but using inequality instead in (2) via the induction step.




∎






We have an analogous lemma concerning unilateral friendly deviation from the AvA setting.




Lemma 8 (Tie-breaking Monotonicity, AvA).

Consider  $S=\{s_{2},s_{1}\}$ , with  $s_{2}<s_{1}$ . Then, for any heap size  $h$ ,


1. 

 $o^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvA}}}(h)=o^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvA}}}(h)$ ;




2. 

 $o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvA}}}(h)\leq o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvA}}}(h)$ ;




3. 

 $o^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvA}}}(h)\leq o^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvF}}}(h)$ ;




4. 

 $o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvA}}}(h)=o^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvF}}}(h)$ .










Proof.

This is analogous to the proof of Lemma 7.
∎






Recall that the main result Theorem 2 concerns subtraction sets of size two, and we have to prove that, if both players act friendly in case of indifference, then the outcome is never worse than if both players have antagonistic tie-breaking rules.




Proof of Theorem 2.

Combine Lemmas 7 and 8.
∎






In case of dominance, we arrive at a stronger version of the main theorem.




Proposition 9.

Consider  $S=\{s_{1},s_{2}\}$ , in the dominant regime when  $2s_{2}\leq s_{1}$ . Then there is no heap  $h$  for which the  ${\rm FvF}$  and  ${\rm AvA}$  settings have a discrepancy. In more generality, for any heap size  $h$ , and any  $X,Y\in\tau$ ,  $o_{X}(h)=o_{Y}(h)$ , i.e.,


• 

 $o^{1}_{X}(h)=o^{1}_{Y}(h)$ ;




• 

 $o^{2}_{X}(h)=o^{2}_{Y}(h)$ .










Proof.

From Lemma 5, we know that in the dominant regime, a player cannot strictly benefit by sacrificing. We claim that greedy play is in PSPE independently of tie-breaking rules.




Consider the  $H_{2}$  branch, where the previous player (Alice) has made a sacrifice. Let there be strict dominance. Thus we may assume that we are in case Lemma 6 (i), which implies that Alice lost  $\Delta$  in her first move, and regained  $\Delta$  in her last move. Thus, Bob’s PSPE utility remains the same as in the  $H_{1}$  branch, since he does not gain anything from Alice’s sacrifice, irrespective of tie-breaking rules.




In case of the balanced regime, both Lemma 6 (i) and (ii) are possible, but in either case the argument is the same as in the previous paragraph, since there is no change in Alice’s utility in both cases.
∎

7 Related work



An important design choice in our framework concerns deterministic tie-breaking: how players resolve indifference between equally optimal moves. In classical combinatorial games, such ambiguity does not arise; perfect information induces unique optimal values. By contrast, our self-interest model naturally produces local indifferences, making tie-breaking an essential component of pure subgame-perfect equilibrium (PSPE) computation.




We resolve this by assigning each player a deterministic tie-breaking identity—friendly or antagonistic—applied uniformly throughout the game tree. One may likewise impose a deterministic max/min (greedy/sacrifice) choice. This allows backward induction to proceed unambiguously, but raises subtle philosophical and computational issues. While the legal move set is simple and local (e.g., subtract  $3$  or  $5$ ), the choice between friendly versus antagonistic (or greedy vs. sacrificial) tie-breaking can depend on the entire continuation, and hence requires full recursive evaluation. In this sense, a tie-breaking rule is both a behavioral commitment and a computational burden. In line with combinatorial game theory tradition, we assume perfect players capable of such reasoning. (In a purely recreational setting, a self-interest subtraction game should of course be played without any such commitment.)




This design contrasts with many settings in economics, AI, and multi-agent systems, where tie-breaking rules are typically exogenous, randomized, or fixed to promote fairness or efficiency. In our setting, the tie-breaking rule is part of the player’s identity; it is not merely a local resolver of ambiguity, but prompts questions about efficiency in a global sense, as different deterministic conventions can be compared across positions and instances. A natural question is whether there exists a pair of deterministic tie-breaking rules that jointly outperform any other pair for both players.




Across several domains, tie-breaking is often treated as a technicality, yet small local choices can propagate to global effects. In voting, different schemes (e.g., parallel-universes tiebreaking, PUT) alter strategies and collective outcomes [20]. In fair division, when multiple near-fair allocations exist, deterministic selection among them can shift welfare and perceived fairness [29]. In tournaments, stable refinements (e.g., minimal stable sets) show how resolving cycles and indifference determines which winners emerge [10]. Recent studies of simple stochastic games emphasize multiple objectives ordered lexicographically; this ordering resolves indifference and shapes equilibrium paths [12]. That said, the impact of tie-breaking is model-dependent: in well-behaved private-value auctions, equilibria can be invariant to the rule [22]. Overall, our results fit a broader pattern in which deterministic conventions for resolving indifference create system-level consequences, while also highlighting boundaries where such rules have no effect.




In computational social choice, a voting rule must specify what happens when two (or more) candidates are exactly tied. Deterministic conventions such as lexicographic priority versus random-candidate tie-breaking are often treated as “mere details”. However, recent work shows that the choice of tie-breaker has global consequences: it can change equilibria, manipulation incentives, and welfare. In particular, [5] analyze standard rules and prove that neither deterministic convention dominates across instances: each can make elections more or less manipulable and can change price-of-anarchy bounds. Thus, resolving local indifference (who wins a tie) re-routes the strategic landscape.




In sequential social-learning models (wherein agents act one-by-one, each observing predecessors’ actions and a private signal), an informational cascade arises when agents rationally ignore their signals and follow the crowd; learning may then fail even as more signals arrive [8]. A knife-edge occurs when public information and a private signal exactly balance and the agent is indifferent; [27] show that the existence and properties of cascades can hinge on this local tie-breaking convention. Relaxing the usual “if indifferent, pick action  $A$ ” assumption can admit equilibria without cascades. Again, a seemingly harmless local choice has global repercussions: whether society asymptotically learns the true state.




Our self-interest subtraction games exhibit a similar structural phenomenon. Deterministic tie-breaking is necessary to compute PSPE (well-posed recursion), but it is not always neutral. Empirically (see Appendix), we find subtraction sets for which some heaps make the supposedly indifferent player strictly better or worse under one tie-breaking rule than under another; no single rule dominates the others. As with elections and cascades, repeated local indifference can induce global divergences whenever  $|S|>2$ .




We now survey additional areas where tie-breaking rules have sometimes surprising consequences.




7.1 Voting



Tie-breaking in voting systems is a widely studied concern in computational social choice. Freeman, Brill, and Conitzer develop general frameworks for tie-breaking and show how the chosen method can alter both axiomatic properties and computational behavior [20]. Studies on manipulation complexity reveal that allowing flexible tie-breaking rules can increase the difficulty of manipulation [19], and that randomized tie-breaking shifts complexity trade-offs [36]. The chair’s power to break ties has been examined from a control perspective [34], demonstrating that the ability to choose among co-winners can itself serve as a computational lever. More recently, Bailey and Tovey analyze how lexicographic versus random tie-breaking affects equilibrium outcomes under strategic voting via price-of-anarchy metrics [6]. Collectively, these works support our thesis that tie-breaking conventions, though local and seemingly innocuous, shape global strategic structure.






7.2 Fair division



Tie-breaking also plays a critical role in fair division, where it can impact both perceived fairness and strategic behaviour. In the context of maximin share allocations, Procaccia and Wang show that even deterministic approximations can result in envy or inefficiency when tie-breaking is required between nearly equal outcomes [29]. Thus, local fairness does not guarantee global satisfaction. Aziz, Caragiannis, Igarashi, and Walsh study the allocation of indivisible goods and chores and show that deterministic tie-breaking can affect feasibility and tractability [4]. More generally, fair division algorithms often assume canonical or fixed tie-breaking to ensure determinism and reproducibility. Still, this determinism itself introduces systemic effects, especially in recursive or multi-agent settings. These findings align with our analysis of self-interest subtraction games: tie-breaks that appear harmless locally can induce global asymmetries.






7.3 Tournaments



In tournament theory, tie-breaking is essential to resolve outcome ambiguity in the presence of cycles and indifference. Tournament solutions refine the set of winners based on structural properties of pairwise dominance. Brandt analyzes stable sets in tournaments and introduces minimal stable sets as a refinement guaranteeing internal and external stability while respecting recursive consistency [10]. These refinements serve a role analogous to deterministic tie-breaking in dynamic games: they ensure that selections made under local symmetry yield globally coherent outcomes. Brandt, Brill, and Harrenstein offer a broader view of tournament solutions, examining axiomatic foundations and algorithmic complexity [11]. Small adjustments in refinement criteria (analogous to tie-breaking choices) can lead to drastically different solution sets.






7.4 Lexicographic games



Lexicographic objectives provide a principled way to resolve indifference in game-theoretic settings. Chatterjee et al. analyze stochastic games with lexicographic objectives and show that optimal strategies exist which respect strict preference orderings among multiple goals [12]. In a related line of work, Aghajohari, Avni, and Henzinger show that in discrete-bidding infinite-duration games, the choice of tie-breaking can influence determinacy; some rules preserve determinacy, while others do not [1]. These findings reinforce our perspective that deterministic tie-breaking conventions in cumulative games function analogously to lexicographic priority rules: locally applied, but with global strategic consequences.






7.5 Auction design



Tie-breaking plays a strategic role in auction design, especially when players are indifferent among bids or when allocation mechanisms yield multiple optimal responses. Jackson and Swinkels analyze private-value auctions and show that while equilibria can exist under various tie-breaking rules, their structure and efficiency may depend sensitively on how ties are resolved [22]. In computational settings, Chen and Peng prove that computing an approximate Bayesian Nash equilibrium in a first-price auction becomes PPAD-complete when the tie-breaking rule is part of the input [13]. Experimental evidence from Llorente-Saguer, Sheremeta, and Szech shows that favorable tie-breaking toward weaker bidders in all-pay auctions substantially alters strategic behavior and bidding intensity [33]. These studies emphasize that even seemingly minor, mechanical rules can affect equilibrium existence, efficiency, and fairness—aligning with our treatment of deterministic tie-breaking in recursive game settings.






7.6 Mechanism design



Tie-breaking is also central in mechanism design and matching markets with indifferences. Ashlagi and Nikzad [3] compare single versus multiple tie-breaking in school choice and show that relative performance depends critically on demand–supply dynamics. Ehlers [18] characterizes priority structures that admit strategy-proof tie-breaking under weak priorities, delineating when tie-breaking can be implemented without sacrificing incentives. More abstractly, Imamura and Tomoeda [21] propose the axiom ETI (equal treatment in indifference classes) for choice functions and study its compatibility with substitutability, consistency, and size monotonicity, clarifying design trade-offs. These results reinforce our view that deterministic, structured tie-breaking is a design variable rather than an afterthought.






7.7 Combinatorial auctions



Tie-breaking is a critical concern in combinatorial auctions, where multiple allocations may be equally optimal under reported valuations. In winner determination, tie-breaking can influence both revenue and allocative fairness. Conitzer and Sandholm show that even with truthful valuations, the tie-breaking rule in VCG can alter which bidder receives which bundle, raising questions about incentives and fairness [15]. In computational settings, Day and Cramton analyze core-selecting combinatorial auctions and show how deterministic tie-breaking (via quadratic payments) affects convergence and the stability of price trajectories [17]. More recently, Narosky, Spiritus, and Szymczak studied priority tie-breaking in combinatorial exchanges and showed that even slight differences in rule design can shift equilibrium outcomes and agent utilities [35]. These studies affirm that tie-breaking is a central design choice with both theoretical and practical ramifications.






7.8 Network routing



Tie-breaking also plays a critical role in graph search and network routing, particularly when multiple equally optimal paths exist. In routing protocols such as OSPF and IEEE 802.1aq, equal-cost paths are resolved using deterministic conventions (e.g., lexicographic ordering of node identifiers) to ensure consistency across distributed nodes. This mirrors heuristic search algorithms such as A⁢ where multiple nodes may share the same evaluation. Asai [2] and Corrêa et al. [16] show that tie-breaking strategies can significantly affect the performance and expansion behavior of A⁢, and propose principled methods for prioritizing among equally valued states. More generally, Jacobs [23] develops a theoretical framework for analyzing tie-breaking in graph algorithms, highlighting the structural impact of seemingly arbitrary decisions. In dynamic settings, Bodwin and Parter [9] examine tie-breaking in shortest-path computation under edge failures, showing that consistent rules enable fast recovery and robust path selection. These results align with our approach: deterministic tie-breaking is not merely a technical detail but a structural component of recursive decision-making.






7.9 ML



Tie-breaking decisions also arise in machine learning and AI, particularly in structured prediction and search-based planning. In classification, models like decision trees and ensembles may encounter examples that fit multiple branches or classes equally well; deterministic resolution affects not only prediction stability but also interpretability. In reinforcement learning [37], when various actions have equal Q-values, consistent selection policies can influence convergence and long-run reward. Across these domains, tie-breaking converts local symmetry into globally meaningful behavior.






7.10 Richman bidding



Recent work on Richman bidding combinatorial games illustrates another facet of tie-breaking. In these games, players bid to gain the right to move, paying their bid to the opponent, and ties are resolved via a marker that grants one player priority in case of equal bids. In the classical continuum setting, the theory is not sensitive to any particular tie-breaking convention [32], since equal bids occur with probability zero. In discrete settings, however, the marker becomes a strategic asset, influencing equilibrium behavior; see the survey [24]. Moreover, tie-breaking is central in [25, 26], where games generalize alternating normal play (last-move-wins): players may include the marker in their bid to counter an impending zugzwang. Switching to random or one-sided tie-breaking would lead to a different (and less rich) theory.






7.11 Extensive-form perfect-information games



Tranæs [38] studies tie-breaking under indifference as a way to influence the opponent’s choices in finite extensive-form games of perfect information. A credible threat (antagonistic tie-break) selects, among tied continuations, a move that leaves the mover’s payoff unchanged while reducing the opponent’s continuation payoff, thereby inducing earlier choices favorable to the mover. Symmetrically, a credible promise (friendly tie-break) selects, under indifference, a move that increases the opponent’s continuation payoff, again steering earlier choices. These conventions are incorporated into the equilibrium concept of path-perfect subgame-perfect equilibrium: an equilibrium for which, along the path and in every subgame, there is no scope for indifference-based threats or promises to influence earlier choices profitably. Tranæs proves that every finite extensive-form game of perfect information admits a path-perfect pure subgame-perfect equilibrium.

Appendix: Unilateral deviation for larger subtraction sets



Our fish-pile-wise Penguins have learned that acting friendly in cases of indifference, through a sacrifice, can benefit the other player. This was evident in Table 1, where the available slack was used precisely for this purpose. Due to the recursive definition of outcomes, other curious effects await our friends, the Penguins.





Suppose Alice and Bob are faced with a heap of size 61, from which they may grab 4, 5, or 9 fishes. Bob has promised his Penguin clan to return with a total of 28 fishes. Alice, as the starting player, has pledged to remain friendly throughout. The question arises: should Bob maintain his friendly disposition, or should he deviate and act antagonistically?
In this particular instance, it turns out that deviating benefits him. By becoming antagonistic, Bob secures one fish for immediate consumption, warming his beak, while still delivering the promised 28 fishes to his clan. If he remains friendly, however, he receives nothing for himself. Bob is puzzled, for his computational skills do not stretch beyond one heap of fish at a time: his tie-breaking rule is designed to leave him indifferent in such cases, yet here he is clearly rewarded for turning against Alice, who correspondingly loses a fish. The next day, they return to the fish pile, but the rules have changed. On their turns, they must now take 3, 8, 11, or 13 fishes, starting from a heap of size 36. Remembering the previous day’s outcome, and despite being fish-pile-wise, Bob is tempted by hunger. He once again turns antagonistic.
However, this time the result is unfavorable. Bob’s clan only requires 14 fishes, knowing that Alice will start, but his tie-breaking choice leads to a total of only 13 fishes, which is one short of the requirement. His reputation within the clan is tarnished, and his beak remains unsatisfied.





In order to understand what happened, let us study the initial outcomes for all tie-breaking combinations of the following rulesets:


• 

 $S_{1}=\{3,8,11,13\}$  (see Table 2), and




• 

 $S_{2}=\{4,5,9\}$  (see Table 3).





We will reveal discrepancies that do not occur in the two-action case and explain how they arise through backward induction. For the reader’s convenience, in the tables, we list the PSPE moves in the same columns as the outcomes.




The ruleset  $S_{1}=\{3,8,11,13\}$ 



Recall that in the two-action setting in FvF, a player who deviates unilaterally cannot harm their own utility (unless the opponent also deviates and becomes antagonistic). Consider the ruleset  $S_{1}$ , with outcome discrepancies and optimal moves for all heap sizes smaller than  $50$  listed in Table 2. For all heap sizes below  $28$ , PSPE utilities are independent of tie-breaking conventions. At heap size  $28$ , however, if Alice deviates from FvF to AvF, then Bob loses one unit: in AvF, Alice chooses  $3$  instead of her FvF choice  $11$ .




This leads to a curious effect at heap size  $36$ , namely Bob loses a unit by becoming antagonistic, that is, by playing in the FvA setting instead of remaining friendly in FvF. Let us justify this phenomenon, since it shows how deteministic tie-breaking becomes much more intricate in multi-action games than in the simpler two-action case. In the FvF setting, with  $h=36$ , Alice plays  $8$ , producing the outcome  $(22,14)$ . But in the FvA setting, while remaining friendly, she does not choose  $8$ , since that yields her utility  $8+13=21$ , whereas playing  $11$  yields  $11+11=22$ . Conversely, in the FvF setting Alice does not choose  $11$ , which would lead to Bob’s utlity  $13$  (the first player PSPE utility at a heap of size  $25$ ), because by instead reducing the heap by  $8$ , she ensures that Bob receives  $14$  units.






The ruleset  $S_{2}=\{4,5,9\}$ 



The ruleset  $S_{2}$  contains a diametral property of  $S_{1}$ , namely here a player can benefit strictly by unilateral deviation to antagonistic. As Lemma 7 shows, this is impossible whenever  $|S|=2$ . For  $S_{2}$ , however, such a case occurs at heap size  $h=61$ .
By Alice removing  $4$  in the FvA setting, instead of  $5$  or  $9$  (the PSPE moves in the FvF setting), Bob both adds one unit to his own PSPE utility, and removes one unit from Alice’s.
The PSPE utilities of all options at  $h=61$  are:





 $o_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h-4)=(29,28),o_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h-5)=(28,28),o_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm FvF}}}(h-9)=(28,24)$ 


and





 $o_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvF}}}(h-4)=(29,28),o_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvF}}}(h-5)=(28,27),o_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm AvF}}}(h-9)=(28,23).$ 





In the FvA setting, Alice observes that all three moves give her the same utility of  $32$ . Acting friendly, she therefore chooses the option that maximizes Bob’s outcome, namely removing  $4$ , which gives him  $29$  rather than  $28$ . By contrast, in FvF the situation is entirely different: here she can obtain  $33$  by playing either  $5$  or  $9$ , while playing  $4$  yields only  $32$ . In FvF there is no indifference that affects Bob’s PSPE utility, and there is no reason for her to sacrifice by playing  $4$ . This explains the interesting discrepancies that arise in the FvA setting at  $h=61$  (and many more at larger heaps). It is also interesting that whenever the deviating player gets a higher utility, the utility of the other player reduces by the same amount. This is unlike the case when the deviating player gets a lower utility, while the utility of the other player remains the same.




Purely self-interested players, as we define in this paper, aim solely to maximize their own utility, while disregarding that of their opponent. This example illustrates that a self-interested player may be strictly motivated to deviate and become antagonistic; this is a non-trivial observation while the defined outcomes do not reveal any such incentive. With only two actions, no such intrinsic motivation exists, as established earlier in this paper.
