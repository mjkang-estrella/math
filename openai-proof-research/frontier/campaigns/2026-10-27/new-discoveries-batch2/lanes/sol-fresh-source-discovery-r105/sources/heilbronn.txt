 From Global Optimization to Exact Coordinates:
 Heilbronn’s Triangle Problem on the Unit Square
 Using Mixed-Integer Nonlinear Optimization 
 
 
 Nathan Sudermann-Merx
 † † thanks:  Institute for Computer Science, DHBW Mannheim. Email:  nathan.sudermann-merx@dhbw.de 
 October 2026 

 Abstract 
    
 We develop a mixed-integer nonlinear programming (MINLP) approach for the Heilbronn triangle problem on the unit square.
The formulation exploits the boundary structure of optimal configurations, which we establish rigorously, to fix coordinates and orientation variables a priori.
With this formulation, a general-purpose global solver solves the case  n = 9 n=9  to global optimality within its numerical tolerances in about eight minutes on a laptop and without any precomputation, compared with about 23 hours of total computation for the best previous approach.
The same model also solves  n = 10 n=10  for the first time, in under seven hours, and confirms the configuration of Comellas and Yebra (2002).
The numerical solutions then guide an exact symbolic reconstruction, and the minimum triangle area of every reconstructed configuration is verified in exact arithmetic.
This yields exact coordinates for all  n ≤ 10 n\leq 10 , including the configurations of Comellas and Yebra (2002) for  n = 9 n=9  and  n = 10 n=10 , together with rigorous lower bounds.
Finally, we derive structural properties of optimal configurations, formulate a conjecture on the number of critical triangles, and pose an open question.
All code and data are publicly available. 
  
 
 
 1  Introduction 

 
 1.1  The problem 

 
 The Heilbronn triangle problem arises in discrete geometry and asks: 
 
 
 
 How should  n n  points be placed in the unit square so that the smallest triangle determined by any three of them is as large as possible? 
 
 
 
 The problem is named after the mathematician Hans Heilbronn (and not after the city in southern Germany). Born into a German-Jewish family, he was dismissed from his position at the University of Göttingen in 1933 and emigrated to England  [ 10 ] . The problem first appeared in print in Roth’s 1951 paper  [ 35 ] , where the unit square was replaced by an arbitrary closed convex region in the plane of positive measure, and the emphasis was placed on the asymptotic behavior of the minimal triangle area rather than on explicit constructions for small values of  n n . 
 
 
 
 1.2  Upper bounds 

 
 We denote the minimal triangle area in an optimal configuration of  n n  points in the unit square by  Δ n \Delta_{n} .
Heilbronn conjectured that  Δ n \Delta_{n}  should satisfy an asymptotic upper bound of the form 
 

 
 
 Δ n = O ⁡ ( 1 n 2 ) . \Delta_{n}=O\!\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right). 
 
 
 As we will see in Section  1.3 , this turned out to be incorrect.
Roth observed in  [ 35 ]  that the problem admits a simple upper bound, 
 

 
 
 Δ n ≤ 1 n − 2 . \Delta_{n}\leq\frac{1}{n-2}. 
 
 
 To see this, note that the convex hull of any configuration of  n n  points can be partitioned into  n − 2 n-2  triangles with pairwise disjoint interiors and total area at most  1 1 , implying that at least one of these triangles must have area no greater than  1 / ( n − 2 ) 1/(n-2) .
Over the following decades, the upper bounds were steadily refined. In 1972, Schmidt  [ 38 ]  obtained a significant improvement, which was sharpened by Roth  [ 36 ,  37 ]  later that year and further strengthened by Komlós, Pintz, and Szemerédi  [ 25 ]  in 1981, establishing what remained the best-known estimate for over forty years.
A major breakthrough came in 2023, when Cohen, Pohoata, and Zakharov  [ 14 ]  proved that for all sufficiently large  n n , 
 

 
 
 Δ n ≤ n − 8 / 7 − 1 / 2000 , \Delta_{n}\leq n^{-8/7-1/2000}, 
 
 
 which is currently the strongest known asymptotic upper bound. Figure  1  illustrates that this bound provides a reasonably tight envelope for all best-known configurations with  n ≥ 6 n\geq 6 .

 1.3  Lower bounds 

 
 A simple lower bound of order  c / n 3 c/n^{3}  for  Δ n \Delta_{n}  can be obtained as follows.
Without regard to the unit-square constraint, consider the  n n  points  ( i , i 2 ) (i,i^{2})  for  i = 0 , … , n − 1 i=0,\ldots,n-1 .
No three of these points are collinear, and hence every triangle determined by them has strictly positive area.
Moreover, each triangle area can be expressed as half the absolute value of a  3 × 3 3\times 3  determinant formed from the vertices’ coordinates (see Section  2.1 ).
Since all coordinates are integers and the area is positive, the determinant has absolute value at least  1 1 , implying that every triangle has area at least  1 / 2 1/2 .
To place the configuration inside the unit square, scale the  x x -coordinates by  1 / n 1/n  and the  y y -coordinates by  1 / n 2 1/n^{2} .
Areas then scale by  1 / n 3 1/n^{3} , giving 
 

 
 
 Δ n ≥ 1 2 ​ n 3 . \Delta_{n}\geq\frac{1}{2n^{3}}. 
 
 
 As noted in the appendix of Roth’s paper  [ 35 ] , Paul Erdős refined this idea by choosing a prime  p p  with  n ≤ p ≤ 2 ​ n n\leq p\leq 2n  and considering the points  ( i , i 2 mod p ) (i,i^{2}\bmod p)  for  i = 0 , … , p − 1 i=0,\ldots,p-1 .
This construction preserves the key properties used above, namely integral coordinates and the absence of collinear triples, while having the advantage that the  y y -coordinates are bounded by  p p .
Consequently, scaling to the unit square reduces areas only by a factor of  1 / p 2 1/p^{2} , leading to 
 

 
 
 Δ n ≥ 1 2 ​ p 2 ≥ 1 8 ​ n 2 \Delta_{n}\geq\frac{1}{2p^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{8n^{2}} 
 
 
 for all  n n . 
 
 
 Combined with Heilbronn’s conjecture, this would have yielded the correct asymptotic order of magnitude for the problem.
However, in 1982, following a suggestion by Roth that the conjecture might be false, Komlós, Pintz, and Szemerédi proved in  [ 26 ]  that there exists a constant  c > 0 c>0  such that 
 

 
 
 Δ n ≥ c ​ log ⁡ n n 2 \Delta_{n}\geq\frac{c\log n}{n^{2}} 
 
 
 for infinitely many  n n , using a nonconstructive probabilistic argument.
Since  log ⁡ n \log n  is unbounded, this lower bound eventually exceeds  C / n 2 C/n^{2}  for every constant  C C , so the conjectured bound  Δ n = O ⁡ ( 1 / n 2 ) \Delta_{n}=O(1/n^{2})  cannot hold. 
 
 
 This gap between existence and construction was closed in 2000 by Bertram-Kretzberg, Hofmeister, and Lefmann  [ 7 ] , who presented a polynomial-time algorithm producing configurations with asymptotic lower bound  Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n / n 2 ) \Omega(\log n/n^{2}) . 
 
 
 
 1.4  Results for small- n n -configurations 

 
 For many years, best-known configurations for the Heilbronn problem were documented on  Erich’s Packing Center , a website maintained by Erich Friedman  [ 21 ] .
Since 2026, the records are maintained on the website of Tej Stead  [ 40 ] , which continues the tables of the Packing Center in a considerably more elaborate and automated form, reflecting the strong renewed interest in the problem.
For each record, the site lists exact coordinates, closed forms where known, critical triangles, history, and proofs, and all submitted configurations are verified automatically in exact arithmetic. We use it as the reference for best-known values.
The first case requiring a nontrivial proof,  n = 5 n=5 , was resolved in 1991 by Yang, Zhang, and Zeng  [ 41 ] , who proved
 Δ 5 = 3 / 9 \Delta_{5}=\sqrt{3}/9 .
In the same work, they disproved a conjecture of Goldberg, who had suggested that optimal configurations for  n ≤ 7 n\leq 7  arise from affine images of regular  n n -gons, a statement that turns out to hold only for  n = 6 n=6 .
In 1995, Dress, Yang, and Zeng  [ 19 ]  established the optimal value  Δ 6 = 1 / 8 \Delta_{6}=1/8 . 
 
 
 In 2002, Comellas and Yebra  [ 15 ]  used simulated annealing followed by an analytical refinement procedure to discover best-known configurations and exact coordinates for many cases with  n ≤ 12 n\leq 12 .
Their configurations for  n = 7 n=7  and  n = 8 n=8  have since been proved optimal, their configurations for  n = 9 n=9  and  n = 10 n=10  are optimal according to all available numerical evidence, including the computations in this paper, and their configuration for  n = 12 n=12  remains the best known to date.
The optimality of their configuration for  n = 7 n=7 , with  Δ 7 ≈ 0.083859 \Delta_{7}\approx 0.083859 , was proved in 2011 by Zeng and Chen  [ 42 ] , who decomposed the problem into 226 nonlinear optimization subproblems.
For  n = 9 n=9 , Chen, Xu, and Zeng  [ 12 ]  used an extensive search on CPU and GPU clusters, requiring a total of 125 days of wall-clock time, to enclose  Δ 9 \Delta_{9}  in a narrow numerical interval.
In 2022, Dehbi and Zeng  [ 18 ]  combined numerical search with symbolic computation to prove that the configuration for  n = 8 n=8  found by Comellas and Yebra, satisfying
 Δ 8 = ( 13 − 1 ) / 36 \Delta_{8}=(\sqrt{13}-1)/36 ,
is indeed optimal. 
 
 
 For  13 ≤ n ≤ 16 13\leq n\leq 16 , the best-known configurations are due to Peter Karpov ( n = 13 n=13 ), Mark Beyleveld ( n = 14 , 16 n=14,16 ), and the author ( n = 15 n=15 , found in 2026 with a heuristic search). None of them has been formally published. Karpov’s configuration is available on his website  [ 24 ] , Beyleveld’s only through Erich Friedman. We reproduce all of them in Appendix  B . 
 
 
 More recently, Monji, Modir, and Kocuk  [ 33 ]  applied general-purpose MINLP solvers to the problem and solved  n = 9 n=9  with Gurobi to global optimality within solver tolerances.
Their final solve took 633 seconds and relies on structural properties of optimal configurations that they establish in precomputations of about 23 hours in total (Section  4.6 ). 
 
 
 Deterministic global optimization has a long record on extremal problems in discrete geometry.
Global QCQP methods determined the largest small octagon  [ 3 ]  and a series of related extremal polygons  [ 2 ] , and interval branch-and-bound has produced computer-assisted proofs for covering problems  [ 9 ] .
What has changed since then is efficiency, driven by hardware, solver implementations, relaxations, preprocessing and, not least, the mathematical formulation.
Recent systematic studies by Berthold et al.  [ 6 ,  5 ]  show that off-the-shelf MINLP solvers can match or improve best-known solutions for a range of packing problems.
The present paper illustrates the role of formulation for the Heilbronn problem: Gurobi, which in  [ 33 ]  needed about 23 hours of precomputation for  n = 9 n=9 , solves our formulation in about eight minutes, and  n = 10 n=10  in under seven hours, because the structural information enters through an analytical result rather than a computation. 
 
 
 
 1.5  Related work on symmetry 

 
 Problems of placing points in a region typically combine the Euclidean symmetries of the region with the permutation symmetry of the points, and exploiting both is standard in deterministic global optimization.
General techniques for symmetry in integer programming are surveyed by Margot  [ 31 ] , and the automatic detection and exploitation of symmetry in mathematical programs is studied by Liberti  [ 29 ] .
For packing equal circles in a square, Locatelli and Raber  [ 30 ]  derive geometric properties of optimal configurations to fix coordinates and eliminate symmetric solutions, and Costa, Hansen, and Liberti  [ 16 ]  study the effect of symmetry-breaking constraints on spatial branch-and-bound.
For Lennard-Jones clusters, coordinates are fixed relative to reference particles  [ 23 ,  27 ] , and for the Thomson problem, branch-and-bound has been combined with analytical arguments and symmetry elimination  [ 39 ,  28 ] .
Our symmetry breaking follows the same principle but derives the fixings from the boundary structure of optimal Heilbronn configurations (Proposition  2 ).
A feature specific to determinant-based formulations is that the fixed boundary order also determines the orientation of many triangles, which allows sign variables to be fixed a priori (Section  3.2.3 ). 
 
 
 
 1.6  Contributions 

 
 We make the following contributions. 
 
 
 A compact MINLP formulation with analytically justified structure. 

 
 Our model fixes several coordinates a priori using the boundary structure of optimal configurations, which also fixes the signs of many triangle areas. It needs no precomputation and solves  n = 9 n=9  to global optimality within solver tolerances in about eight minutes on a laptop. It also solves  n = 10 n=10 , for the first time, in under seven hours. An ablation study quantifies the contribution of each ingredient (Section  4 ). 
 
 
 
 A complete justification of the boundary structure. 

 
 The argument previously used for the boundary structure relies on a lemma of Zeng and Chen  [ 42 ]  that does not address two adjacent corners of the square. We give a self-contained proof without this case and reduce the remaining configurations, by an area-preserving shear, to two further types, which we solve with separate models. 
 
 
 
 Exact coordinates via an optimize-then-refine framework. 

 
 The numerical solution identifies the critical triangles and the boundary points, which yields a polynomial system that we solve exactly. Every resulting configuration is verified in exact arithmetic over all  ( n 3 ) \binom{n}{3}  triangles. For each  n = 5 , … , 10 n=5,\dots,10  we obtain exact coordinates of one configuration (for  n = 6 n=6 , of a one-parameter family), which is proven optimal for  n ≤ 8 n\leq 8  and optimal within solver tolerances for  n = 9 n=9  and  n = 10 n=10 , matching those of Comellas and Yebra  [ 15 ] . 
 
 
 
 Structural observations. 

 
 We determine for which optimal configurations the linear independence constraint qualification holds ( n = 5 , 7 , 9 n=5,7,9 ) and fails ( n = 6 , 8 n=6,8 ), conjecture a lower bound of  2 ​ n − 7 2n-7  on the number of critical triangles, and document a clustering of the noncritical triangle areas that symmetry alone does not explain. We also report an observation of Tej Stead (personal communication, 2026) on the cube structure of the configuration for  n = 16 n=16 . 
 
 
 
 Reproducibility and data. 

 
 We collect the best-known configurations for  n ≤ 16 n\leq 16 , several of which were never formally published. The source code, data, solution files, and the scripts that generate all tables are available at  https://github.com/spiralulam/heilbronn . 
 
 
 
 Mathematical status of the results. 

 
 Since the solver works in floating-point arithmetic, we distinguish throughout between rigorous lower bounds (exactly verified configurations), numerical upper bounds (solver dual bounds), and proofs of optimality, which are available in the literature for  n ≤ 8 n\leq 8  but not for  n = 9 n=9  and  n = 10 n=10  (Section  5.1 ).

 2  Original formulation and theoretical results 

 
 This section establishes a basic formulation of the Heilbronn problem in Section  2.1 , which already suffices to prove its solvability.
The results of Section  2.2  on the boundary structure of optimal configurations are not only of theoretical interest but provide structure that is exploited in the optimization model of Section  3 . 
 
 
 2.1  The original formulation and its solvability 

 
 Let  n ≥ 3 n\geq 3  denote the number of points  p 1 = ( x 1 , y 1 ) , … , p n = ( x n , y n ) p_{1}=(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,p_{n}=(x_{n},y_{n})  we want to place in  ℝ 2 \R^{2} ,  I = { 1 , … , n } I=\{1,\ldots,n\} , and 
 

 
 
 T = { ( i , j , k ) |  1 ≤ i < j < k ≤ n } ⊂ ℕ 3 T\ =\ \{(i,j,k)|\ 1\leq i<j<k\leq n\}\subset\N^{3} 
 
 
 the set of all triangles, where each triangle  t = ( i , j , k ) ∈ T t=(i,j,k)\in T  is identified with the indices of the defining points  p i p_{i} ,  p j p_{j}  and  p k p_{k} .
By construction,  | T | = ( n 3 ) |T|=\binom{n}{3} . 
 
 
 Remark 1 . 
 
 The enumeration of the points heavily influences the symmetry breaking and therefore
becomes a crucial modeling choice, as discussed in Section  3.2.2 . 
 
 
 
 For a triangle  t = ( i , j , k ) ∈ T t=(i,j,k)\in T , the  signed area 
 

 
 
 A t ± = A i ​ j ​ k ± := 1 2 ​ det ( x i y i 1 x j y j 1 x k y k 1 ) = 1 2 ​ ( x i ​ y j + x j ​ y k + x k ​ y i − x i ​ y k − x j ​ y i − x k ​ y j ) A^{\pm}_{t}=A^{\pm}_{ijk}:=\frac{1}{2}\det\!\begin{pmatrix}x_{i}&y_{i}&1\\
x_{j}&y_{j}&1\\
x_{k}&y_{k}&1\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\left(x_{i}y_{j}+x_{j}y_{k}+x_{k}y_{i}-x_{i}y_{k}-x_{j}y_{i}-x_{k}y_{j}\right) 
 
 
 is positive if  p i , p j , p k p_{i},p_{j},p_{k}  are ordered counterclockwise, negative if they are ordered clockwise, and zero if they are collinear, and  A t := | A t ± | A_{t}:=|A^{\pm}_{t}|  is the area of the triangle.
The sign information plays a key role in the optimization model of Section  3 .
With  S = [ 0 , 1 ] 2 S=[0,1]^{2} , the Heilbronn problem is the optimization problem 
 

 
 
 P Δ : max x , y min t ∈ T A t s.t.  ( x i , y i ) ∈ S , i ∈ I , P_{\Delta}:\qquad\max_{x,y}\ \min_{t\in T}A_{t}\quad\text{s.t. }(x_{i},y_{i})\in S,\ i\in I, 
 
 
 and  Δ n \Delta_{n}  is its optimal value.
All further specifications of this problem amount to modeling decisions, of which there are many and which must be made carefully.
As stated,  P Δ P_{\Delta}  is a nonsmooth, nonconvex optimization problem over a box, and it has the following properties. 
 
 
 Proposition 1 . 
 
 The optimization problem  P Δ P_{\Delta}  admits a solution for every  n n , and its optimal value  Δ n \Delta_{n}  is strictly positive. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Existence of a solution follows from the Weierstraß theorem.
The objective function is continuous, and the feasible set is nonempty, closed, and bounded, hence compact.
Therefore, the maximum is attained. Strict positivity of  Δ n \Delta_{n}  follows, for example, from Erdős’ construction discussed in Section  1.3 , which provides an explicit positive lower bound.
∎ 
 
 
 
 Remark 2 . 
 
 In  [ 35 ] , Roth argues that  P Δ P_{\Delta}  is solvable because its feasible set  “is a closed convex region” .
However, closedness and convexity alone do not guarantee existence of a maximizer. Compactness (or an equivalent coercivity argument) is required. 
 
 
 
 The following corollaries are immediate consequences of Proposition  1 , since otherwise the configuration would contain a triangle of zero area. 
 
 
 Corollary 1 . 
 
 An optimal configuration for the Heilbronn problem does not contain two coincident points. 
 
 
 
 Corollary 2 . 
 
 An optimal configuration for the Heilbronn problem does not contain three collinear points.

This subsection shows that this is justified.

A  configuration  is a set  P = { p 1 , … , p n } ⊆ S P=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}\subseteq S  of  n n  points.

 Remark 3 .

That lemma covers the cases of no common vertex, one common vertex, and two opposite common vertices, but not two adjacent common vertices. For the fixed unit square this case must be handled explicitly, which is done in Lemma  3  and Theorem  1  below.

 3  Mixed-integer nonlinear optimization models 

 
 In Section  3.1  we present a baseline MINLP formulation that is an exact reformulation of the nonsmooth problem  P Δ P_{\Delta}  without any additional structure. It solves small instances to global optimality within solver tolerances but does not solve  n = 7 n=7  within one hour.
Section  3.2  then develops several enhancements, in particular a symmetry-breaking strategy based on the boundary structure of Section  2.2  that, as a byproduct, also fixes the signs of certain triangle areas.
The resulting final model is collected in Section  3.3 . Section  3.4  describes the variants for the configuration types II and III of Theorem  1 , and Section  3.5  compares our formulation with that of Monji, Modir, and Kocuk  [ 33 ] .

 3.2  Enhancements 

 
 We enhance the baseline model with three groups of improvements. 
 
 
 3.2.1  Bounds and variable substitution 

 
 Removing a point from an optimal configuration with  n n  points does not decrease the minimum triangle area, so  Δ n ≤ Δ n − 1 \Delta_{n}\leq\Delta_{n-1} , and we add the constraint  z ≤ Δ n − 1 z\leq\Delta_{n-1} .
For  n ≤ 9 n\leq 9  we use the proven values of  Δ n − 1 \Delta_{n-1} . For  n = 10 n=10 , where no proof for  Δ 9 \Delta_{9}  is available, we use the dual bound  0.0548818 0.0548818  computed for  n = 9 n=9  (Section  4.2 , rounded up). The cap only removes configurations whose minimum area exceeds this bound, but our result for  n = 10 n=10  therefore also rests on the numerical result for  n = 9 n=9 . 
 
 
 Additionally, we introduce auxiliary variables  w i ​ j ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] w_{ij}\in[0,1]  with  w i ​ j = x i ​ y j w_{ij}=x_{i}y_{j}  for all  i , j ∈ I i,j\in I . The signed areas then become linear in  w w ,

 and all nonconvexity of the area definitions is concentrated in the  n 2 n^{2}  bilinear equations  w i ​ j = x i ​ y j w_{ij}=x_{i}y_{j} .
Global solvers handle such equations by spatial branch-and-bound  [ 4 ,  8 ] . For two bounded variables, the McCormick inequalities  [ 32 ]  describe the convex hull of the graph of the bilinear term over the box. They are a relaxation of  w i ​ j = x i ​ y j w_{ij}=x_{i}y_{j} , not an exact linearization, and spatial branching tightens them.
Since each product occurs in the  n − 2 n-2  triangles containing both  p i p_{i}  and  p j p_{j} , tightening the relaxation of one  w i ​ j w_{ij}  strengthens all of them at once.
Monji, Modir, and Kocuk  [ 33 ]  use the same substitution, so it does not explain the difference between the two formulations. In the ablation of Section  4.4  its effect turns out to be negligible, presumably because Gurobi introduces such auxiliary variables itself. 
 
 
 
 3.2.2  Symmetry breaking 

 
 The primary obstacle is the enormous symmetry: the dihedral group  D 4 D_{4}  acts on coordinates (8 symmetries: 4 rotations, 4 reflections) and the symmetric group  S n S_{n}  permutes point labels, yielding a combined symmetry group of order  8 ⋅ n ! 8\cdot n! . For  n = 9 n=9 , this is about 2.9 million equivalent copies of every configuration.
By Theorem  1 , some optimal configuration has type I, II, or III. The model of this section covers type I and requires the boundary pattern of Definition  1 . This amounts to three steps. 
 
 
 Step 1: Choice of the left edge. 

 
 An edge carrying two boundary points is designated as the left edge, and the constraint  x 2 ≤ x 4 x_{2}\leq x_{4}  selects one of the two configurations related by the reflection  ( x , y ) ↦ ( x , 1 − y ) (x,y)\mapsto(x,1-y) . 
 
 
 
 Step 2: Canonical boundary labeling. 

 
 The five boundary points  p 1 , … , p 5 p_{1},\ldots,p_{5}  are labeled counterclockwise starting from the left edge:  p 1 p_{1}  (left, lower),  p 2 p_{2}  (bottom),  p 3 p_{3}  (right),  p 4 p_{4}  (top),  p 5 p_{5}  (left, upper). The constraint  y 5 ≥ y 1 y_{5}\geq y_{1}  fixes the order of the two points on the left edge. 
 
 
 
 Step 3: Interior point ordering. 

 
 The remaining points  p 6 , … , p n p_{6},\ldots,p_{n}  are ordered by nondecreasing  x x -coordinate. 
 
 
 These steps fix five coordinate variables outright and remove most, but not all, of the symmetry.
If several edges carry two boundary points, or if the configuration contains a corner of  S S , several rotated or reflected copies remain feasible. Points with equal  x x -coordinates can still be interchanged in Step 3.

 3.2.3  Sign fixing 

 
 The boundary pattern allows us to fix the signs for two groups of triangles: 
 
 1.  
 
 All triangles in the set  T + = { ( i , j , k ) ∈ T ∣ k ≤ 5 } T^{+}=\{(i,j,k)\in T\mid k\leq 5\}  have their three vertices on the boundary, and  p 1 , … , p 5 p_{1},\dots,p_{5}  occur in counterclockwise order along  ∂ S \partial S . Since no three points of an optimal configuration are collinear,  A t ± > 0 A_{t}^{\pm}>0  for all  t ∈ T + t\in T^{+} . 
 
 2.  
 
 Since  p 1 p_{1}  and  p 5 p_{5}  are located on the left edge with  y 5 ≥ y 1 y_{5}\geq y_{1} , we have  A 15 ​ k ± = − 1 2 ​ ( y 5 − y 1 ) ​ x k ≤ 0 A^{\pm}_{15k}=-\tfrac{1}{2}(y_{5}-y_{1})x_{k}\leq 0  for all  t ∈ T − := { ( i , j , k ) ∈ T ∣ i = 1 , j = 5 } t\in T^{-}:=\{(i,j,k)\in T\mid i=1,\ j=5\} . 
 
 
 
 
 This allows us to add the constraints

 3.5  Comparison with Monji, Modir, and Kocuk 

 
 Concurrent work by Monji, Modir, and Kocuk  [ 33 ]  was the first to apply general-purpose MINLP solvers to the Heilbronn problem and solved  n = 9 n=9  to global optimality within solver tolerances. Our formulation differs in several respects: 
 
 
 
 1.  
 
 Absolute value handling:  Monji et al. linearize  z ≤ | A t ± | z\leq|A_{t}^{\pm}|  with binary variables and big- M M  type coefficients. We use the bilinear constraint  z ≤ ( 2 ​ b t − 1 ) ​ A t ± z\leq(2b_{t}-1)A_{t}^{\pm} , which avoids such coefficients and is handled by the solver’s spatial branching. 
 
 2.  
 
 Symmetry breaking:  We fix five boundary points on specific edges in counterclockwise order, eliminating five coordinates outright, whereas Monji et al. order the points by their  y y -coordinates. The fixed boundary order also enables sign fixing for  ( 5 3 ) + ( n − 5 ) \binom{5}{3}+(n-5)  binary variables. 
 
 3.  
 
 Minimal auxiliary variables:  We introduce no edge-proximity, strip-assignment, or grid-cell auxiliary binary variables. 
 
 4.  
 
 No precomputations:  The structural information that Monji et al. obtain in about 23 hours of precomputation follows here from Theorem  1 , which is proved analytically (Section  4.6 ). 
 
 
 
 
 Section  4.6  compares the total computational effort of both approaches. 
 
 
 
 
 4  Computational study 

 
 4.1  Setup 

 
 All computations were carried out on an Apple MacBook Pro with an Apple M5 Pro chip (15-core CPU) and 48 GB of RAM.
The models are implemented in Python 3.12 using Gurobi 13.0.3 through its Python interface  gurobipy . The symbolic computations use SymPy 1.14.0.
The only non-default Gurobi parameter is  MIPFocus = 2 , which shifts the emphasis towards the dual bound.
All other parameters keep their default values. In particular, the relative optimality gap is  10 − 4 10^{-4} , the absolute gap  10 − 10 10^{-10} , the feasibility and optimality tolerances  10 − 6 10^{-6} , and the integrality tolerance  10 − 5 10^{-5} . Gurobi used all 15 cores of the machine.
No initial solution is provided, except in the warm-start experiments of Section  4.5 .
Since running times of MINLP solvers vary with the random seed, every instance of the main experiment is solved with five seeds, and we report the median and the range.
The ablation and comparison runs use a single seed and a time limit of four hours. The baseline for  n = 7 n=7  has a time limit of one hour, and the runs for  n = 10 n=10  a time limit of 24 hours.
All tables in this section are generated by a script from the solver output. The code, the logs, and the solution files are part of the accompanying repository. 
 
 
 The tolerances determine what the numbers mean.
A configuration returned by Gurobi satisfies the constraints up to the feasibility tolerance, so its objective value can deviate from the minimum triangle area of its coordinates by about  10 − 6 10^{-6} , and the reported objective value can even exceed the true optimum. For  n = 8 n=8 , for example, Gurobi reports  0.07237703 0.07237703 , while the exact optimal value is  ( 13 − 1 ) / 36 ≈ 0.07237642 (\sqrt{13}-1)/36\approx 0.07237642 .
Coordinates are therefore reliable to about six decimal places, and the dual bound determines the optimal value only up to the relative gap of  10 − 4 10^{-4}  and up to floating-point effects. This is why we use the solver output as numerical evidence and as input to the exact computations of Section  5 , and not as a proof.

 Strip capacities. 

 
 Monji et al.  [ 33 , Table 1]  bound the sorted  y y -coordinates by the number of points that fit into a horizontal strip of height  1 / κ 1/\kappa  without creating a triangle of area below a lower bound  H ≤ Δ n H\leq\Delta_{n} , and they determine these capacities by solving restricted Heilbronn problems.
The capacities also follow without computation.
An affine scaling maps a strip of height  t t  onto the unit square and multiplies all areas by  1 / t 1/t . If  m + 1 m+1  points with minimum triangle area at least  H H  fit into the strip, their images have minimum area at least  H / t H/t , which is impossible if  H / t > Δ m + 1 H/t>\Delta_{m+1} .
Hence every strip of height  t < H / Δ m + 1 t<H/\Delta_{m+1}  along any edge of the square contains at most  m m  points of a configuration with value at least  H H .
With the proven values  Δ 3 , … , Δ 8 \Delta_{3},\dots,\Delta_{8}  this reproduces all bounds of  [ 33 , Table 1]  for  n = 7 , 8 , 9 n=7,8,9 , and choosing  t t  just below  H / Δ m + 1 H/\Delta_{m+1}  instead of  1 / κ 1/\kappa  gives slightly stronger ones. For  n = 9 n=9 , for example, at most two points lie below  y = 0.1097 y=0.1097 , compared with  y ≥ 1 / 10 y\geq 1/10  for the third point in  [ 33 ] .
The resulting constraints are valid for every configuration with value at least  H H , independently of labeling and symmetry breaking, so they can be added to  P Δ ⋆ P^{\star}_{\Delta} . We model them with one binary variable per point, strip, and edge.
The strip capacities do not speed up the model (Table  4 ). For  n = 9 n=9 , the running time of 578 seconds lies within the range of the seeds of the full model. The boundary pattern and the ordering of the interior points apparently already contain most of the information that the capacities provide.

 5  The optimize-then-refine framework 

 
 5.1  The framework 

 
 Our approach proceeds in two stages.
In the first step, we solve the MINLP formulation  P Δ ⋆ P_{\Delta}^{\star}  from Section  3.3  with Gurobi to global optimality within the solver tolerances.
The solver returns a numerical configuration together with a dual bound that agrees with its objective value up to the relative gap of  10 − 4 10^{-4} . Both are computed in floating-point arithmetic (Section  4.1 ).
From the numerical coordinates we identify the  critical triangles , that is, the triangles whose area equals the minimum up to the solver tolerance, and record which points lie on which edges of the unit square.
In the second step, we treat these structural observations as an ansatz for deriving exact symbolic coordinates:
setting the areas of the critical triangles equal yields a system of polynomial equations that can be solved in closed form with a computer algebra system.
Finally, we verify the resulting configuration in exact arithmetic: all  ( n 3 ) \binom{n}{3}  triangle areas are computed exactly, and we check that none of them is smaller than the claimed minimum and that exactly the expected triangles attain it. 
 
 
 The three results of the pipeline have a different mathematical status, and we keep them apart.
The exact verification makes the reconstructed configuration a  rigorous lower bound  on  Δ n \Delta_{n} .
It does not prove optimality. The structure used in Step 2 is inferred from the numerical solution, so the solution of the polynomial system is exact only for this structure, and a configuration with a different set of critical triangles is not excluded by Step 2.
The global upper bound comes from Step 1 and is numerical, so it is not a mathematical proof  [ 34 ] .
For  n ≤ 8 n\leq 8 , optimality is proven in the literature (Section  1.4 ), and our computations confirm these results. For  n = 9 n=9  and  n = 10 n=10 , the exact configuration matches the solver’s numerical upper bound, but no rigorous proof of optimality is known. 
 
 
 Remark 6 . 
 
 A key practical concern is the identification of critical triangles and boundary points in the first step, since these decisions rest on numerical data subject to solver tolerances.
In all instances considered here, the gaps are unambiguous:
the area of the smallest non-critical triangle exceeds that of the critical triangles by a margin that is several orders of magnitude larger than the solver tolerance, and every point classified as lying on an edge has a coordinate that deviates from the boundary value by less than  10 − 6 10^{-6} .
This clear separation makes the structural input to Step 2 unambiguous in all cases considered.

 Given the formulation of Section  3 , Step 1 is a routine solver call. Step 2 is more involved.
The equal-area conditions on the critical triangles form a system of polynomial equations whose unknowns are the free coordinate parameters remaining after boundary constraints and coordinate coincidences have been exploited.
The number  k k  of these parameters ranges from  3 3  (for  n = 10 n=10 ) to  8 8  (for  n = 9 n=9 ), and we employ three solving strategies, tried in succession, and for  n = 10 n=10  an additional stationarity condition. 
 
 
 
 1.  
 
 Direct symbolic solve. 
For small systems ( k ≤ 6 k\leq 6 , covering  n = 5 , 6 , 8 n=5,6,8 ), SymPy’s symbolic solver produces exact solutions directly, including the detection of the one-parameter family for  n = 6 n=6 . 
 
 2.  
 
 Gröbner basis. 
A Gröbner basis of a polynomial ideal with respect to a monomial order is a generating set with particularly good division properties  [ 17 ] . For a lexicographic order it is triangular: the last polynomials involve only the last variables, so the system can be solved by back-substitution, much like Gaussian elimination for linear systems.
For  n = 7 n=7 , the solution lies in a cubic extension field, and a lexicographic Gröbner basis computation
yields a triangular system from which all coordinates follow by back-substitution through the minimal polynomial  19 ​ f 3 − 27 ​ f 2 + 11 ​ f − 1 = 0 19f^{3}-27f^{2}+11f-1=0  (see Section  5.2 ). 
 
 3.  
 
 Numeric-to-symbolic refinement. 
For  n = 9 n=9 , where  k = 8 k=8 , neither a direct solve nor a Gröbner basis computation terminates in reasonable time.
Instead, we apply a numeric-to-symbolic approach: from the numerical coordinates we first identify the underlying number field  ℚ ⁡ ( 65 ) \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{65})  by recognizing  65 \sqrt{65}  in the simplest coordinate, then express all coordinates as elements of this field using SymPy’s  nsimplify  function with the known algebraic extension. 
 
 
 
 
 The numeric-to-symbolic step involves a risk:
numerical proximity does not guarantee algebraic correctness, since the coordinates returned by Gurobi are reliable only to about six decimal places (Section  4.1 ).
For instance, for  n = 9 n=9  the spurious expression  19 166 + 3 ​ 65 166 ≈ 0.26017 \tfrac{19}{166}+\tfrac{3\sqrt{65}}{166}\approx 0.26017 
is numerically indistinguishable from the true value  9 16 − 3 ​ 65 80 ≈ 0.26017 \tfrac{9}{16}-\tfrac{3\sqrt{65}}{80}\approx 0.26017 
at solver precision, yet only the latter satisfies the equal-area equations.
We therefore verify every candidate solution symbolically by substituting it into the polynomial system of the critical triangles.
Any coordinate that fails this check is corrected by fixing the validated coordinates and solving the resulting reduced (and much smaller) subsystem exactly. 
 
 
 For  n = 10 n=10 , the equal-area conditions alone do not determine the configuration. With the coordinate coincidences read off from the numerical solution,  k = 3 k=3  parameters remain, and the 16 critical areas take only three distinct forms  A 1 A_{1} ,  A 2 A_{2} ,  A 3 A_{3} . The equations  A 1 = A 2 = A 3 A_{1}=A_{2}=A_{3}  therefore leave a curve of solutions. Since the configuration maximizes the common area along this curve, we add the Lagrange condition  det ( ∇ A 1 , ∇ A 2 , ∇ A 3 ) = 0 \det(\nabla A_{1},\nabla A_{2},\nabla A_{3})=0 . A lexicographic Gröbner basis of the resulting system, after the removal of degenerate solutions in which a parameter vanishes, contains the polynomial  ( 2 ​ g − 1 ) ​ ( 12 ​ g 3 − 27 ​ g 2 + 20 ​ g − 4 ) (2g-1)(12g^{3}-27g^{2}+20g-4)  in the coordinate  g g  of the point  p 7 = ( g , g ) p_{7}=(g,g) . The root  g = 1 / 2 g=1/2  is spurious, and the other two parameters follow as polynomials in the real root of the cubic factor. This reproduces the configuration of Comellas and Yebra  [ 15 ] , who also obtained it by an analytical refinement. 
 
 
 The final exact verification is reported in Section  6 .
Step 2 takes less than one second for every  n ≤ 10 n\leq 10 , and the exact verification less than seven seconds per configuration.

 7  Further observations and research questions 

 
 This section collects structural observations on the configurations for  n ≤ 10 n\leq 10  and on the best-known configurations for  11 ≤ n ≤ 16 11\leq n\leq 16  (Appendix  B ).
For  n ≤ 10 n\leq 10  they are based on the exact coordinates of Section  6 . For  n ≥ 11 n\geq 11  they are based on coordinates with 30 significant digits, and two areas are considered equal if they differ by less than  10 − 12 10^{-12} . 
 
 
 7.1  How many triangles are critical? 

 
 At an optimal configuration, no small motion of the points can enlarge the smallest triangle: every such motion either shrinks a critical triangle or pushes a point out of the square. If the optimum is isolated and nondegenerate, this blocking requires at least as many active constraints as there are degrees of freedom, just as a vertex of a polyhedron in  ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d}  lies on at least  d d  facets.
To make this precise, note that locally, at a configuration without collinear points, the Heilbronn problem is the smooth problem of maximizing  z z  in the  2 ​ n + 1 2n+1  variables  ( x , y , z ) (x,y,z)  subject to  z ≤ ± A t ± z\leq\pm A^{\pm}_{t}  for all triangles  t t , with the sign of the current orientation, and to the box constraints.
At an optimal configuration, the active constraints are the critical triangles and the box constraints of the coordinates on  ∂ S \partial S , where a corner contributes two. The box constraints cannot do much of the blocking: 
 
 
 Lemma 4 . 
 
 At most eight box constraints are active at a configuration without three collinear points. Consequently, if at least  2 ​ n + 1 2n+1  constraints are active at an optimal configuration, then it has at least  2 ​ n − 7 2n-7  critical triangles. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Every active box constraint corresponds to a pair of a point and an edge of  S S  that contains it, where a corner belongs to two edges and accounts for two active constraints. No edge contains three points, since they would be collinear, so there are at most  4 ⋅ 2 = 8 4\cdot 2=8  such pairs. The second statement follows, since the critical triangles are the only other active constraints.
∎ 
 
 
 
 If optimal configurations are rigid in this sense, we therefore expect many critical triangles. 
 
 
 
 Conjecture 1 . 
 
 An optimal configuration of  n ≥ 5 n\geq 5  points has at least  2 ​ n − 7 2n-7  critical triangles.

The outlier  n = 16 n=16 , with  64 64  critical triangles, is explained by the special structure of that configuration (Section  7.2 ).
The heuristic is not a proof, since a nonlinear optimum can be isolated with fewer active constraints than variables. The configuration for  n = 5 n=5  is an example, with ten active constraints and eleven variables. It still satisfies the bound, as does the one-parameter family for  n = 6 n=6 , because fewer than eight box constraints are active in both cases. For  n = 6 n=6 , the missing active constraint matches the one degree of freedom of the family. 
 
 
 The conjecture is a lower bound on the number of active constraints. A remark of Oliver Stein points to an upper bound: if the linear independence constraint qualification (LICQ) holds, the gradients of the active constraints are linearly independent in  ℝ 2 ​ n + 1 \mathbb{R}^{2n+1} , so at most  2 ​ n + 1 2n+1  constraints are active. At a rigid optimum at which LICQ holds, both bounds meet, and exactly  2 ​ n + 1 2n+1  constraints are active. LICQ need not hold, however, and we checked it for all configurations of Table  10 . 
 
 
 Proposition 3 . 
 
 At the configurations of Section  6 , LICQ holds for  n = 5 n=5 ,  7 7 , and  9 9 , and it fails for  n = 6 n=6 ,  8 8 , and  10 10 . 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 For  n = 8 n=8 , twelve triangle constraints are active by the exact verification (Section  6 ), and the corners  ( 0 , 0 ) (0,0)  and  ( 1 , 1 ) (1,1)  and four further points on  ∂ S \partial S  give eight active box constraints. These  20 20  gradients in  ℝ 17 \mathbb{R}^{17}  cannot be linearly independent. For  n = 10 n=10 , sixteen triangle constraints and eight box constraints, one for each of the eight points on  ∂ S \partial S , give  24 24  gradients in  ℝ 21 \mathbb{R}^{21} .
For the other values of  n n , we computed the rank of the matrix of active gradients exactly by Gaussian elimination over the respective number field. For  n = 5 n=5 ,  7 7 , and  9 9 , the rank equals the number of active constraints ( 10 10 ,  15 15 , and  19 19 ). For the member  c = 1 / 8 c=1/8  of the family for  n = 6 n=6 , the  12 12  active gradients have rank  11 11 .
∎ 
 
 
 
 The configurations for  n = 7 n=7  and  n = 9 n=9  are therefore nondegenerate in the strongest sense: exactly  2 ​ n + 1 2n+1  constraints are active, and their gradients are linearly independent.
For  n = 6 n=6 , LICQ fails although fewer constraints than variables are active, which reflects the one-parameter family of optima.
For the best-known configurations, LICQ fails whenever more than  2 ​ n + 1 2n+1  constraints are active, that is, for  n = 11 n=11 ,  12 12 ,  14 14 , and  16 16 . For  n = 13 n=13  and  n = 15 n=15 , a numerical rank computation with 50 digits indicates that it holds, with smallest singular values  0.045 0.045  and  0.022 0.022  of the matrix of active gradients.
The script for these computations is part of the accompanying repository.

 Not all configurations cluster, however. Almost all triangle areas of the asymmetric best-known configurations for  n = 13 n=13  and  n = 15 n=15  are distinct (Figure  9 b). Either these configurations are not optimal, or the clustering is limited to small  n n  and to configurations with special structure. This leads to the following question. 
 
 
 
 Open question 1 . 
 
 Do the noncritical triangle areas of optimal Heilbronn configurations generally cluster at only a few values, and if so, why? 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 8  Outlook and final remarks 

 
 We have presented an  optimize-then-refine  framework for the Heilbronn triangle problem on the unit square. An MINLP model whose structure rests on Theorem  1  solves all  n ≤ 10 n\leq 10  to global optimality within solver tolerances without precomputation, and exact symbolic computation turns the numerical solutions into exactly verified configurations. 
 
 
 Several directions remain open.
First, the upper bounds of this paper are numerical. A rigorous proof of optimality for  n = 9 n=9  would require the dual bounds to be validated, for example by interval-based global optimization  [ 34 ,  9 ] , by verified certificates for the branch-and-bound tree  [ 13 ] , or by an independent verification of the kind used for the traveling salesman problem  [ 1 ] .
Second, the next cases are within reach. The step from  n = 9 n=9  to  n = 10 n=10  multiplied the running time by about 36, and a similar factor would put  n = 11 n=11  at about a week on a laptop. Since the growth comes from the dual bound, stronger relaxations or valid inequalities are the natural next step.
Third, Conjecture  1  on critical triangles and Open question  1  on the clustering of triangle areas remain open. The latter is directly relevant for the search for better configurations for  n = 13 n=13  and  n = 15 n=15 . 
 
 
 More broadly, the two-step methodology applies to other packing and covering problems in discrete geometry whose conjectured optimal configurations involve algebraic numbers of moderate degree.
