Abstract.
    

Proving a 2009 conjecture of Itai Benjamini, we show:
    

Theorem
For any C there is an ε>0\varepsilon>0 such that for any simple graph G on VV of size nn,
and X0,…X_{0},\ldots an ordinary random walk on G,
    




ℙ⁡({X0,…,XC​n}=V)<e−ε​n.\mathbb{P}(\{X_{0},\ldots,X_{Cn}\}=V)<e^{-\varepsilon n}.


    

A first ingredient in the proof of this is a similar statement for Markov
chains in which all transition probabilities are sufficiently small relative to CC.
  




1. Introduction




We are motivated by a surprisingly basic
question that we first heard from Ori Gurel–Gurevich
in 2010: is it true that for any fixed CC and nn-vertex
simple graph GG, the probability that a random walk on GG covers V⁡(G)V(G)
in C​nCn steps is exponentially small in nn?
(Some usage notes are included at the end of this section.)




A positive answer was conjectured by Itai Benjamini in 2009 ([3]; see also [4]),
and given some support by
a quite amazing argument of Benjamini, Gurel-Gurevich,
and Morris [4], showing that the answer is yes if we assume
any fixed bound Δ\Delta on the maximum
degree of GG (with the constant in the exponent then depending on Δ\Delta
as well as CC). That the answer is yes for trees was shown
by Yehudayoff [12], who also observed that when GG is an expander,
a positive answer follows easily from the (less easy) large deviation bound of Gillman [8].




Here we answer the question:



Theorem 1.1.



For any C there is an ε>0\varepsilon>0 such that for any simple graph G on VV of size nn,
and X0,…X_{0},\ldots an ordinary random walk on G (with any rule for X0X_{0}),




ℙ⁡({X0,…,XC​n}=V)<e−ε​n.\mathbb{P}(\{X_{0},\ldots,X_{Cn}\}=V)<e^{-\varepsilon n}.







The machine underlying the proof of Theorem 1.1 is the following statement for
general Markov chains, which seems of independent interest.
Here and in the corollary that follows, (Xt)(X_{t}) is a Markov chain on VV
(of size nn)
with transition probabilities φ⁡(⋅,⋅)\varphi(\cdot,\cdot), and
XIX_{I} is the set {Xt:t∈I}\{X_{t}:t\in I\} (for a set of indices II).



Theorem 1.2.



For each CC and β>0\beta>0 there is a δ=δ⁡(C,β)>0\delta=\delta(C,\beta)>0 such that
if W⊆VW\subseteq V, |W|>β​n|W|>\beta n, M≤C​|W|M\leq C|W|, and



(1)

max{φ(v,w):v∈V,v≠w∈W}<δ,\max\{\varphi(v,w):v\in V,v\neq w\in W\}<\delta,



then




ℙ⁡(X[M]⊇W)=exp⁡[−ΩC,β​|W|].\mathbb{P}(X_{[M]}\supseteq W)=\exp[-\Omega_{C,\beta}|W|].







In particular (roughly) the conclusion of
Theorem 1.1 holds for any Markov chain in which the transition
probabilities are small enough relative to CC. This includes
Theorem 1.1 itself when the minimum degree of GG
is sufficiently large:



Corollary 1.3.



For each C there are ε>0\varepsilon>0 and d such that for any RW (Xt)(X_{t}) on a graph of
minimum degree at least dd,




ℙ⁡(X[C​n]=V)<e−ε​n.\mathbb{P}(X_{[Cn]}=V)<e^{-\varepsilon n}.







This again seems interesting in its own right; e.g., we don’t know another way to prove
Theorem 1.1 even for the Hamming Cube ({0,1}m\{0,1\}^{m} with the natural adjacencies),
the scene of some of our early skirmishes with the present problem.




One might hope that Theorem 1.1 could now be handled by some combination of
Corollary 1.3 and the ideas of [4], but this seems to be a dead end.
(We did at least manage to “borrow” [4]’s title.
The only antecedent we know of
for what follows
is [1, Lemma 2], whose sibling,
the present Lemma 3.1, was our starting point.
In particular, beautiful work of [7, 6, 13], showing (see [13, Theorem 1.1])
that cover time is “exponentially concentrated” in a different sense,
seems
unconnected to what we do here.)




The actual proof of Theorem 1.1 is
based especially on the following easy consequence of Theorem 1.2,
which again applies to general Markov chains
(and in which φW\varphi_{W} refers to the “induced” chain on WW; see “Usage” below).



Corollary 1.4.



With |V|=n|V|=n, suppose the partition

and Ui⊆ViU_{i}\subseteq V_{i} (i∈[k]i\in[k]), satisfy

Since |∪Vi|>γ​n|\cup V_{i}|>\gamma n, if X[C​n]⊇∪ViX_{[Cn]}\supseteq\cup V_{i} then there is an ii

∎




In what follows γ\gamma will be a “true” constant, meaning one not depending on CC,
and ϑ\vartheta will be a function of CC; thus the implied constant in (3)

the desired partition.
Of course not every Markov chain admits such a partition
(or we would have the nonsensical claim that Theorem 1.1 holds for general chains),
but it seems possible that RW (again, on a simple GG) does.




We will find it convenient to set (for the rest of the paper)

but stress that any slightly small
“true” constant would do as well.



Question 1.5.



Is it true that for each CC there is a ϑ\vartheta for which, for any GG, RW on GG admits
a partition as in Corollary 1.4?





(Of course for Theorem 1.1 it would be enough to have a positive answer with
γ\gamma also a function of CC.)




In the event, we are only able to produce (more accurately, show existence of) such
a partition under a pair of restrictions on GG, but can also show that if either of these
is violated then Theorem 1.1 holds for other reasons.
Failure of the first restriction, which forbids too many large degrees, is handled by
the next lemma, which may be thought of (not quite accurately because of the difference
in the degree bounds) as a substantial extension of Corollary 1.3.



Lemma 1.6.



For each D there is a Δ\Delta such that if

then ℙ⁡(X[D​n]=V)=exp⁡[−Ω⁡(n)]\mathbb{P}(X_{[Dn]}=V)=\exp[-\Omega(n)].





We postpone specifying the second restriction, which will be easier to do in the context of
Section 5
(see Lemma 5.4 and (51)).




Remark.
Lemma 1.6 is the only place where we use simplicity of GG, the
rest of what we do being valid for general reversible chains.
At that level, Theorem 1.1 does not hold without some restriction, but e.g.,
the argument of Section 5 goes through essentially
unchanged to show (with π\pi denoting stationary distribution):



Theorem 1.7.



Let (Xi)i≥0(X_{i})_{i\geq 0} be a reversible Markov chain on VV, and suppose there exists W⊆VW\subseteq V with |W|≥α​n|W|\geq\alpha n and πv≤K​πw​∀v,w∈W\pi_{v}\leq K\pi_{w}~\forall v,w\in W. Then




ℙ⁡(X[D​n]=V)=exp⁡[−Ω⁡(n)],\mathbb{P}(X_{[Dn]}=V)=\exp[-\Omega(n)],



where the implied constant depends on α\alpha, KK, and DD.





Before closing this discussion we mention an obvious challenge:



Question 1.8.



Can one say anything reasonable about the value of ε\varepsilon
in Theorem 1.1?





Whatever value can be extracted from our argument will be quite bad
(We suspect it’s not as bad as what could be gotten from [4], but are not volunteering
to make this comparison.) As far as we know, it could be that, for slightly large CC,
complete graphs—for which ℙ⁡(cover)\mathbb{P}(\mbox{cover}) is roughly exp⁡[−e−C​n]\exp[-e^{-C}n]—are
more or less the worst case;
but note that for C=1C=1 (e.g.), the probability is larger for a path.
At any rate, given how far we are from a decent value, there’s clearly no point
in trying to optimize anything, and we instead do what we can to keep things reasonably simple.

Usage.
We consider Markov chains (Xt)t≥0(X_{t})_{t\geq 0} on default state space VV of size nn,
as usual using π\pi for stationary distribution.
We use ℙv\mathbb{P}_{v} and 𝔼v\mathbb{E}_{v} for probability and expectation given X0=vX_{0}=v.
For B⊆VB\subseteq V, the hitting time of BB is
TB=min⁡{t:Xt∈B}T_{B}=\min\{t:X_{t}\in B\}, and
the positive hitting time is TB+:=min⁡{t>0:Xt∈B}T_{B}^{+}:=\min\{t>0:X_{t}\in B\}
(=TB=T_{B} if X0∉BX_{0}\not\in B).




We use φ\varphi for transition probabilities
and φW\varphi_{{}_{W}} for
transition probabilities in the induced chain on W⊆VW\subseteq V; that is,
φW​(u,v)=ℙu​(XTW+=v)\varphi_{W}(u,v)=\mathbb{P}_{u}(X_{T_{W}^{+}}=v). (This usage is not universal;
e.g. [2] uses “chain watched on WW” here and
“induced chain” differently.)




Throughout G=(V,E)G=(V,E) is a (finite, connected) simple graph, with (again) |V|=n|V|=n.
Usage here is pretty standard: NvN_{v} for the neighborhood of (i.e. vertices
adjacent to) vv; dvd_{v}—or, if necessary, dG​(v)d_{G}(v)—for |Nv||N_{v}| (the degree of vv);
and, for X⊆VX\subseteq V, N(X)=∪x∈XNxN(X)=\cup_{x\in X}N_{x}.

We use [n][n] for {1,2,…,n}\{1,2,\ldots,n\} and always assume nn is large enough
to support our arguments.
To avoid needless clutter, we allow a few irrelevant abuses such as (usually)

We collect here only a few items that will be needed below (and that most
readers might profitably skip).
For general background on both Markov chains and martingales, see e.g. [9].

πu​φ​(u,v)=πv​φ​(v,u);\pi_{u}\varphi(u,v)=\pi_{v}\varphi(v,u);
