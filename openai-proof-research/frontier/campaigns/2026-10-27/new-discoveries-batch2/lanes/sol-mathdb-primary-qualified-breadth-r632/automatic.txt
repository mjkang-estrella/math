On the complexity of algebraic numbers I.
Expansions in integer bases




Boris ADAMCZEWSKI  (Lyon)  &  Yann BUGEAUD ††2000 Mathematics Subject Classification :  11J81, 11A63, 11B85,
68R15. (Strasbourg)



Abstract



Let b≥2b\geq 2 be an integer. We prove that the bb-adic
expansion of every irrational algebraic number cannot have low complexity.
Furthermore, we establish that irrational morphic numbers are
transcendental, for a wide class of morphisms. In particular,
irrational automatic numbers are transcendental.
Our main tool is a new, combinatorial transcendence criterion.





1. Introduction




Let b≥2b\geq 2 be an integer. The bb-adic expansion of every
rational number is eventually periodic, but what can be said on the
bb-adic expansion of an irrational algebraic number? This
question was addressed for the first time by Émile Borel [11],
who made the conjecture that such an expansion should satisfies the same
laws as do almost all real numbers. In particular, it is expected that every
irrational algebraic number is normal in base bb. Recall that a real
number θ\theta is called normal in base bb if,
for any positive integer nn,
each one of the bnb^{n} blocks of length nn
on the alphabet {0,1,…,b−1}\{0,1,\ldots,b-1\}
occurs in the bb-adic expansion of θ\theta with the same frequency 1/bn1/b^{n}.
This conjecture is reputed to be out of reach:
we even do not know whether the digit 77 occurs infinitely often in
the decimal expansion of 2\sqrt{2}.
However, some (very) partial
results have been established.




As usual, we measure the complexity
of an infinite word 𝐮=u1​u2​…{\bf u}=u_{1}u_{2}\ldots
defined on a finite alphabet by counting the
number p⁡(n)p(n) of distinct blocks of length nn occurring in the
word 𝐮{\bf u}. In particular, the bb-adic expansion of every
real number normal in base bb satisfies p⁡(n)=bnp(n)=b^{n} for any
positive integer nn.
Using a clever reformulation of a theorem of Ridout [31],
Ferenczi and Mauduit [18]
established the transcendence of the
real numbers whose bb-adic expansion is a
non eventually periodic sequence of minimal complexity,
that is, which satisfies p⁡(n)=n+1p(n)=n+1 for every n≥1n\geq 1
(such a sequence is called a Sturmian sequence,
see the seminal papers by Morse and Hedlund
[26, 27]).
The combinatorial criterion given in [18] has
been used subsequently to exhibit further examples of
transcendental numbers with low complexity
[3, 6, 4, 32]. It also implies
that the complexity of the bb-adic expansion of every
irrational algebraic number
satisfies lim infn→∞(p⁡(n)−n)=+∞\liminf_{n\to\infty}(p(n)-n)=+\infty.
Although this is very far away from what is expected,
no better result is known.




In 1965, Hartmanis and Stearns [19]
proposed an alternative approach
for the notion of complexity of real numbers, by emphasizing the
quantitative aspect of the notion of calculability introduced by
Turing [39]. According to them, a real number is
said to be computable in time T⁡(n)T(n) if there exists a multitape
Turing machine
which gives the first nn-th terms of its binary
expansion in (at most) T⁡(n)T(n) operations. The ‘simpler’ real numbers in that
sense, that is, the numbers for which one can choose T⁡(n)=O⁡(n)T(n)=O(n),
are said to be computable in real time. Rational numbers share
clearly this property. The problem of Hartmanis and Stearns, to
which a negative answer is expected, is the following: do there
exist irrational algebraic numbers which are computable in real time?
In 1968, Cobham [13] suggested to restrict this problem to a
particular class of Turing machines, namely to the case of finite
automata (see Section 3 for a definition). After several attempts by Cobham
[13] in 1968 and
by Loxton and van der Poorten [21] in 1982,
Loxton and van der Poorten
[22] finally claimed to have
completely solved the restricted problem in 1988.
More precisely, they asserted that the bb-adic expansion
of every irrational algebraic number cannot be generated by a finite
automaton. The proof proposed in [22], which
rests on a method introduced by Mahler
[23, 24, 25],
contains unfortunately a rather serious gap,
as explained by Becker [8]
(see also [40]).
Furthermore, the
combinatorial criterion established in [18] is too weak
to imply this statement, often referred to as the conjecture of
Loxton and van der Poorten.




In the present paper, we prove new results concerning both notions
of complexity. Our Theorem 1 provides a sharper lower estimate for the
complexity of the bb-adic expansion of every irrational algebraic number.
We are still far away from proving that such an expansion is normal,
but we considerably improve upon the earlier known results.
We further establish (Theorem 2) the conjecture of Loxton
and van der Poorten, namely that
irrational automatic numbers are transcendental.
Our proof yields more general statements and allows us to
confirm that irrational morphic numbers are transcendental, for a wide
class of morphisms (Theorems 3 and 4).




We derive Theorems 1 to 4 from a refinement
(Theorem 5) of the combinatorial
criterion from [18], that we obtain as a
consequence of the Schmidt Subspace Theorem.




Throughout the present paper, we adopt the following convention.
We use small letters (aa, uu, etc.) to denote letters from
some finite alphabet 𝒜{\cal A}.
We use capital letters (UU, VV, WW, etc.) to denote
finite words. We use bold small letters (𝐚{\bf a}, 𝐮{\bf u}, etc.)
to denote infinite sequence of letters. We often identify
the sequence 𝐚=(ak)k≥1{\bf a}=(a_{k})_{k\geq 1} with the infinite word
a1​a2​…a_{1}a_{2}\ldots, also called 𝐚{\bf a}. This should not cause any confusion.




Our paper is organized as follows. The main results are stated in
Section 2 and proved in Section 5. Some definitions from
automata theory and combinatorics on words are recalled in Section 3.
Section 4 is devoted to
the new transcendence criterion and its proof. Finally, we show
in Section 6 that the Hensel expansion of every irrational
algebraic pp-adic number cannot have a low complexity, and we conclude
in Section 7 by miscellaneous remarks.




Some of the results of the present paper were announced
in [2].

Florian Luca for their useful comments. The first author
is also most grateful to
Jean-Paul Allouche and Valérie Berthé for their constant support.




2. Main results




As mentioned in the first part of the Introduction,
we measure the complexity of a real number
written in some integral base b≥2b\geq 2 by counting,
for any positive integer nn, the
number p⁡(n)p(n) of distinct blocks of nn digits (on the alphabet
{0,1,…,b−1}\{0,1,\ldots,b-1\}) occurring in its bb-adic expansion.
The fonction pp is commonly called the complexity function.
It follows from results of Ferenczi and Mauduit [18]
(see also [4], Théorème 3)
that the complexity function pp
of every irrational algebraic number satisfies




lim infn→∞(p⁡(n)−n)=+∞.\liminf_{n\to\infty}\,(p(n)-n)=+\infty.

(1)


As far as we are aware, no better result is known, although
it has been proved [3, 6, 32]
that some special real numbers with linear complexity are transcendental.




Our first result is a considerable improvement of (1).



Theorem 1



Let b≥2b\geq 2 be an integer.
The complexity function of the bb-adic expansion of
every irrational algebraic number satisfies




lim infn→∞p⁡(n)n=+∞.\liminf_{n\to\infty}\,{p(n)\over n}=+\infty.







It immediately follows from Theorem 1 that every irrational real number with
sub-linear complexity (i.e., such that
p⁡(n)=O⁡(n)p(n)=O(n)) is transcendental. However, Theorem 1 is
slightly sharper, as is illustrated by an example due to
Ferenczi [17]: he established the existence of a sequence
on a finite alphabet whose complexity function pp satisfies




lim infn→∞p⁡(n)n=2andlim supn→∞p⁡(n)nt=+∞for any t>1.\liminf_{n\to\infty}\,{p(n)\over n}=2\quad{\rm and}\quad\limsup_{n\to\infty}\,{p(n)\over n^{t}}=+\infty\quad\hbox{for any $t>1$}.






Most of the previous attempts towards a proof of
the conjecture of Loxton and van der Poorten have been
made via the Mahler method [21, 22, 8, 29].
We stress that Becker [8]
established that, for any given non-eventually periodic
automatic sequence 𝐮=u1​u2​…{\bf u}=u_{1}u_{2}\ldots, the real number
∑k≥1uk​b−k\sum_{k\geq 1}\,u_{k}b^{-k} is transcendental, provided that
the integer bb is sufficiently large (in terms of 𝐮{\bf u}).
Since the complexity function pp of any automatic sequence
satisfies p⁡(n)=O⁡(n)p(n)=O(n) (see Cobham [14]), Theorem 1 confirms
straightforwardly this conjecture.



Theorem 2



Let b≥2b\geq 2 be an integer.
The bb-adic expansion of any irrational algebraic
number cannot be generated by a finite automaton.
In other words, irrational automatic numbers are transcendental.





Although Theorem 2 is a direct consequence of Theorem 1, we give in Section 5
a short proof of it, that rests on another result of Cobham [14].




Theorem 2 establishes a particular case of
the following widely believed conjecture (see e.g. [5]).
The definitions of morphism, recurrent morphism, and morphic number
are recalled in Section 3.



Conjecture



Irrational morphic numbers are transcendental.





Our method allows us to confirm this conjecture for a wide class
of morphisms.



Theorem 3



Binary algebraic irrational numbers cannot be generated by a
morphism.





As observed by Allouche and Zamboni [6], it follows from
[18] combined with a result of
Berstel and Séébold [9] that binary irrational numbers
which are fixed point of a primitive morphism or of a
morphism of constant length ≥2\geq 2 are transcendental.
Our Theorem 3 is much more general.




Recently, by a totally different method,
Bailey, Borwein, Crandall, and Pomerance [7]
established new, interesting results on the density of the digits in
the binary expansion of algebraic numbers.




For bb-adic expansions with b≥3b\geq 3, we obtain
a similar result as in Theorem 3, but an additional assumption is needed.



Theorem 4



Let b≥3b\geq 3 be an integer. The bb-adic expansion of an algebraic
irrational number cannot be generated by a recurrent morphism.





Unfortunately, we are unable to prove that ternary
algebraic numbers cannot be generated by a morphism.
Consider for instance the fixed point




𝐮=01212212221222212222212222221222​…{\bf u}=01212212221222212222212222221222\ldots



of the morphism defined by 0→0120\to 012, 1→121\to 12,
2→22\to 2, and set α=∑k≥1uk​3−k\alpha=\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\,u_{k}3^{-k}.
Our method does not apply to show the transcendence
of α\alpha. Let us mention that this α\alpha is known to be transcendental:
this is a consequence of deep transcendence results
proved in [10] and in [15], concerning the values of theta series
at algebraic points.




The proofs of Theorems 1 to 4 are given in Section 5.
The key point for them is a
new transcendence criterion, derived from the
Schmidt Subspace Theorem, and stated in Section 4.
Actually, we are able to deal also, under some conditions,
with non-integer bases (see Theorems 5 and 5A).
Given a real number β>1\beta>1, we can expand in base β\beta every real
number ξ\xi in (0,1)(0,1) thanks to the greedy algorithm:
we then get the β\beta-expansion of ξ\xi,
introduced by Renyi [30].
Using Theorem 5, we easily see that
the conclusions of Theorems 1 to 4 remain true with the expansion in base bb
replaced by the β\beta-expansion when β\beta is a Pisot or a Salem number.
Recall that a Pisot (resp. Salem) number is
a real algebraic integer >1>1, whose complex conjugates
lie inside the open unit disc (resp. inside the closed unit disc,
with at least one of them on the unit circle). In particular,
any integer b≥2b\geq 2 is a Pisot number.
For instance, we get the
following result.



Theorem 1A



Let β>1\beta>1 be a Pisot or a Salem number.
The complexity function of the β\beta-expansion of
every algebraic number in (0,1)∖𝐐⁡(β)(0,1)\setminus{\bf{Q}}(\beta) satisfies




lim infn→∞p⁡(n)n=+∞.\liminf_{n\to\infty}\,{p(n)\over n}=+\infty.







Likewise, we can also state Theorems 2A, 3A, and 4A accordingly:
Theorems 1 to 4 deal with algebraic irrational numbers, while
Theorems 1A to 4A deal with algebraic numbers in (0,1)(0,1) which do not lie
in the number field generated by β\beta.




Moreover,
our method also allows us to prove that pp-adic
irrational numbers whose Hensel expansions have low complexity are
transcendental, see Section 6.




3. Finite automata and morphic sequences




In this Section, we gather classical definitions from
automata theory and combinatorics on words.




Finite automata and automatic sequences.
Let kk be an integer with k≥2k\geq 2. We denote by Σk\Sigma_{k} the set
{0,1,…,k−1}\left\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\right\}. A kk-automaton is defined as a 66-tuple




A=(Q,Σk,δ,q0,Δ,τ),A=\left(Q,\Sigma_{k},\delta,q_{0},\Delta,\tau\right),

the initial state, Δ\Delta is the output alphabet and τ:Q→Δ\tau:Q\rightarrow\Delta is the output function.




For a state qq in QQ and for a finite word W=w1​w2​…​wnW=w_{1}w_{2}\ldots w_{n}
on the alphabet Σk\Sigma_{k},
we define recursively δ⁡(q,W)\delta(q,W) by
δ⁡(q,W)=δ⁡(δ⁡(q,w1​w2​…​wn−1),wn)\delta(q,W)=\delta(\delta(q,w_{1}w_{2}\ldots w_{n-1}),w_{n}).
Let n≥0n\geq 0 be an integer and let
wr​wr−1​…​w1​w0w_{r}w_{r-1}\ldots w_{1}w_{0} in (Σk)r\left(\Sigma_{k}\right)^{r}
be the kk-ary expansion
of nn; thus, n=∑i=0rwi​kin=\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{r}w_{i}k^{i}. We denote by WnW_{n} the
word w0​w1​…​wrw_{0}w_{1}\ldots w_{r}. Then, a sequence 𝐚=(an)n≥0{\bf a}=(a_{n})_{n\geq 0} is
said to be kk-automatic if there exists a kk-automaton AA such that
an=τ⁡(δ⁡(q0,Wn))a_{n}=\tau(\delta(q_{0},W_{n})) for all n≥0n\geq 0.




A classical example of a 22-automatic sequence is given by the binary
Thue-Morse sequence 𝐚=(an)n≥0=0110100110010​…{\bf a}=(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}=0110100110010\ldots
This sequence is defined
as follows: ana_{n} is equal to 00 (resp. to 11)
if the sum of the digits in the
binary expansion of nn is even (resp. is odd). It is easy to
check that this sequence can be generated by the 22-automaton




A=({q0,q1},{0,1},δ,q0,{0,1},τ),A=\bigl(\{q_{0},q_{1}\},\{0,1\},\delta,q_{0},\{0,1\},\tau\bigr),



where




δ⁡(q0,0)=δ⁡(q1,1)=q0,δ⁡(q0,1)=δ⁡(q1,0)=q1,\delta(q_{0},0)=\delta(q_{1},1)=q_{0},\qquad\delta(q_{0},1)=\delta(q_{1},0)=q_{1},



and τ⁡(q0)=0\tau(q_{0})=0, τ⁡(q1)=1\tau(q_{1})=1.




Morphisms.
For a finite set 𝒜{\cal A}, we denote by 𝒜∗{\cal A}^{*} the free monoid
generated by 𝒜{\cal A}. The empty word ε\varepsilon is the neutral element
of 𝒜∗{\cal A}^{*}. Let 𝒜{\cal A} and ℬ{\cal B} be two finite sets. An
application from 𝒜{\cal A} to ℬ∗{\cal B}^{*} can be uniquely extended to an
homomorphism between the free monoids 𝒜∗{\cal A}^{*} and ℬ∗{\cal B}^{*}. We
call morphism from 𝒜{\cal A} to ℬ{\cal B} such an homomorphism.




Sequences generated by a morphism.
A morphism
ϕ\phi from 𝒜{\cal A} into itself is said to be prolongable if there exists a
letter aa such that ϕ⁡(a)=a​W\phi(a)=aW, where WW is a non-empty word such
that ϕk​(W)≠ε\phi^{k}(W)\not=\varepsilon for every k≥0k\geq 0. In that case, the
sequence of finite words (ϕk​(a))k≥1(\phi^{k}(a))_{k\geq 1} converges in
𝒜𝐍{\cal A}^{{\bf N}} (endowed with the product topology of the discrete
topology on each copy of 𝒜{\cal A})
to an infinite word 𝐚{\bf a}. This infinite word is clearly
a fixed point for ϕ\phi and we say that 𝐚{\bf a} is generated by the morphism
ϕ\phi. If, moreover, every letter occurring in 𝐚{\bf a}
occurs at least twice, then we say that
𝐚{\bf a} is generated by a recurrent morphism.
If the alphabet 𝒜{\cal A} has two letters, then we say that
𝐚{\bf a} is generated by a binary morphism.
More generally, an infinite sequence 𝐚{\bf a} in 𝒜𝐍{\cal A}^{{\bf N}}
is said to be morphic
if there exist a sequence 𝐮{\bf u} generated by a morphism defined over
an alphabet ℬ{\cal B} and a morphism from ℬ{\cal B} to 𝒜{\cal A} such that
𝐚=ϕ⁡(𝐮){\bf a}=\phi({\bf u}).




For instance, the Fibonacci morphism σ\sigma defined from the alphabet
{0,1}\{0,1\} into
itself by σ⁡(0)=01\sigma(0)=01 and σ⁡(1)=0\sigma(1)=0 is
a binary, recurrent morphism which generates the Fibonacci infinite
word




𝐚=limn→∞σn​(0)=010010100100101001​…{\bf a}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sigma^{n}(0)=010010100100101001\ldots



This infinite word is an example of a Sturmian sequence and its complexity
function satisfies
thus p⁡(n)=n+1p(n)=n+1 for every positive integer nn.




Automatic and morphic real numbers.
Following the previous definitions, we say that a real number α\alpha
is automatic
(respectively, generated by a morphism, generated by a recurrent
morphism, or morphic) if there exists an integer b≥2b\geq 2 such that the
bb-adic expansion of α\alpha is automatic (respectively, generated by a
morphism, generated by a recurrent
morphism, or morphic).




A classical example of binary automatic number is given by




∑n≥1122n\sum_{n\geq 1}\,{1\over 2^{2^{n}}}



which is transcendental, as proved by Kempner [20].




4. A transcendence criterion for stammering sequences




First, we need to introduce some notation.
Let 𝒜{\cal A} be a finite set. The length of a word
WW on the alphabet 𝒜{\cal A}, that is, the number of letters
composing WW, is denoted by |W||W|.
For any positive integer ℓ\ell, we write
WℓW^{\ell} for the word W​…​WW\ldots W (ℓ\ell times repeated concatenation
of the word WW). More generally, for any positive real number
xx, we denote by WxW^{x} the word
W⌊x⌋​W′W^{\lfloor x\rfloor}W^{\prime}, where W′W^{\prime} is the prefix of
WW of length ⌈(x−⌊x⌋)​|W|⌉\left\lceil(x-\lfloor x\rfloor)|W|\right\rceil.
Here, and in all what follows, ⌊y⌋\lfloor y\rfloor and
⌈y⌉\lceil y\rceil denote, respectively, the integer part and the upper
integer part of the real number yy.
Let 𝐚=(ak)k≥1{\bf a}=(a_{k})_{k\geq 1} be a sequence of elements from 𝒜{\cal A},
that we identify with the infinite word a1​a2​…a_{1}a_{2}\ldots
Let w>1w>1 be a real number.
We say that 𝐚{\bf a}
satisfies Condition (∗)w(*)_{w} if 𝐚{\bf a} is not
eventually periodic and if there exist
two sequences of finite words (Un)n≥1(U_{n})_{n\geq 1},
(Vn)n≥1(V_{n})_{n\geq 1} such that:




(i) For any n≥1n\geq 1, the word Un​VnwU_{n}V_{n}^{w} is a prefix
of the word 𝐚{\bf a};




(ii) The sequence
(|Un|/|Vn|)n≥1({|U_{n}|}/{|V_{n}|})_{n\geq 1} is bounded from above;




(iii) The sequence (|Vn|)n≥1(|V_{n}|)_{n\geq 1} is
increasing.




As suggested to us by Guy Barat, a sequence satisfying Condition (∗)w(*)_{w}
for some w>1w>1 may be called a stammering sequence.



Theorem 5



Let β>1\beta>1 be a Pisot or a Salem number.
Let 𝐚=(ak)k≥1{\bf a}=(a_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be a bounded sequence of rational integers.
If there exists a real number w>1w>1
such that 𝐚{\bf a} satisfies Condition (∗)w(*)_{w}, then the real
number




α:=∑k=1+∞akβk\alpha:=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\,{a_{k}\over\beta^{k}}



either belongs to 𝐐⁡(β){\bf{Q}}(\beta), or is transcendental.





The proof of Theorem 5 rests on the Schmidt Subspace Theorem
[37] (see also [38]), and more precisely
on a pp-adic generalization due to Schlickewei [34, 35]
and Evertse [16]. Note that the particular case when β\beta is an
integer ≥2\geq 2 was proved in [2].
Note also that Adamczewski [1] proved that, under a stronger
assumption on the sequence (ak)k≥1(a_{k})_{k\geq 1}, the number α\alpha
defined in the statement of Theorem 5 is transcendental.




Remarks.




∙{\bullet} Theorem 5 is considerably stronger than the
criterion of Ferenczi and Mauduit [18]:
our assumption w>1w>1 replaces their
assumption w>2w>2. This type of condition is rather flexible,
compared with the Mahler method, for which a functional equation
is needed. For instance, the conclusion of Theorem 5 also
holds if the sequence 𝐚{\bf a} is an unbounded
sequence of integers that does not increase
too rapidly. Nevertheless, one should acknowledge that,
when it can be applied, the Mahler method gives the transcendence
of the infinite series ∑k=1+∞ak​β−k\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\,a_{k}\beta^{-k}
for every algebraic number β\beta such that this series converges.




∙{\bullet} We emphasize that if a sequence 𝐮{\bf u}
satisfies Condition (∗)w(*)_{w} and if ϕ\phi is a non-erasing morphism
(that is, if the image by ϕ\phi of any letter has length at least 11),
then ϕ⁡(𝐮)\phi({\bf u}) satisfies Condition (∗)w(*)_{w}, as well.
This observation is used in the proof of Theorem 2.




∙{\bullet} If β\beta is an algebraic number which is neither a
Pisot, nor a Salem number, it is still possible to get
a transcendence criterion using the approach followed for
proving Theorem 5. However, the
assumption w>1w>1 should then
be replaced by a weaker one, involving the Mahler measure of β\beta
and lim supn→∞|Un|/|Vn|\limsup_{n\to\infty}\,{|U_{n}|}/{|V_{n}|}.
Furthermore, the same approach shows
that the full strength of Theorem 5 holds when β\beta is a Gaussian
integer. More details will be given in a subsequent work.




Before beginning the proof of Theorem 5, we quote a version of the
Schmidt Subspace Theorem, as formulated by Evertse [16].

ϕ\phi defined on an alphabet 𝒜{\cal A}.
As we have already noticed in the beginning of
the proof of Theorem 3, there exist a
letter aa and a strictly increasing sequence
of positive integers (nk)k≥1(n_{k})_{k\geq 1} such that for every k≥1k\geq 1 we have




|ϕnk​(a)|=max⁡{|ϕnk​(j)|:j∈𝒜}.|\phi^{n_{k}}(a)|=\max\{|\phi^{n_{k}}(j)|:j\in{\cal A}\}.



Since by assumption
the sequence 𝐚{\bf a} is recurrent there exist at least two
occurrences of the letter aa. We then apply the same trick as in the
proof of Theorem 3, and we again conclude by applying Theorem 5.
 
 
     





6. Transcendence of pp-adic numbers




Let pp be a prime number. As usual, we denote by 𝐐p{\bf{Q}}_{p} the field of
pp-adic numbers. We call algebraic (resp. transcendental) any element of
𝐐p{\bf{Q}}_{p} which is algebraic (resp. transcendental) over 𝐐{\bf{Q}}.
A suitable version of the Schmidt Subspace Theorem,
due to Schlickewei [33],
can be applied to derive a lower bound for the complexity
of the Hensel expansion of every irrational algebraic number in 𝐐p{\bf{Q}}_{p}.



Theorem 1B



Let α\alpha be an irrational algebraic
number in 𝐐p{\bf{Q}}_{p}
and denote by




α=∑k=−m+∞ak​pk\alpha=\sum_{k=-m}^{+\infty}\,a_{k}p^{k}



its Hensel
expansion. Then, the complexity function pp of the sequence
(ak)k≥−m(a_{k})_{k\geq-m} satisfies




lim infn→∞p⁡(n)n=+∞.\liminf_{n\to\infty}\,{p(n)\over n}=+\infty.







Likewise (see Section 2), we can also state Theorems 2B, 3B and 4B.
Theorem 1B follows from Theorem 6 below, along
with the arguments used in the proof of Theorem 1.




The method of proof of Theorem 5 applies to provide us with a new
transcendence criterion for pp-adic numbers.



Theorem 6



Let pp be a prime number and let
(ak)k≥−m(a_{k})_{k\geq-m} be a sequence taking its values in
{0,1,…,p−1}\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}. Let w>1w>1 be a real number.
If the sequence (ak)k≥1(a_{k})_{k\geq 1}
satisfies Condition (∗)w(*)_{w}, then the pp-adic number




α:=∑k=−m+∞ak​pk\alpha:=\sum_{k=-m}^{+\infty}\,a_{k}p^{k}



is transcendental.





We briefly outline the proof of Theorem 6.
Let pp and (ak)k≥−m(a_{k})_{k\geq-m} be as in the statement of
this theorem.
There exist a parameter w>1w>1 and two sequences (Un)n≥1(U_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and (Vn)n≥1(V_{n})_{n\geq 1} of finite words
as in the definition of Condition (∗)w(*)_{w}. For any n≥1n\geq 1,
set rn=|Un|r_{n}=|U_{n}| and sn=|Vn|s_{n}=|V_{n}|.
To establish Theorem 6, it is enough to prove that the pp-adic number




α′:=∑k=1+∞ak​pk\alpha^{\prime}:=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\,a_{k}p^{k}



is transcendental. As in the proof of Theorem 5,
the key fact is the observation
that α′\alpha^{\prime} admits infinitely many good rational approximants
obtained by truncating its Hensel expansion and completing by periodicity.
Precisely, for any positive integer nn, we define the sequence
(bk(n))k≥1(b_{k}^{(n)})_{k\geq 1} exactly as in Section 4, and we set




αn=∑k=1+∞bk(n)​pk.\alpha_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\,b_{k}^{(n)}p^{k}.



An easy calculation shows that we have




|α′−αn|p≤p−rn−w​sn,αn=pnpsn−1,|\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha_{n}|_{p}\leq p^{-r_{n}-ws_{n}},\quad\alpha_{n}={p_{n}\over p^{s_{n}}-1},



where




pn=(∑k=1rnak​pk)​(psn−1)−∑k=1snarn+k​prn+k.p_{n}=\biggl(\sum_{k=1}^{r_{n}}a_{k}\,p^{k}\biggr)(p^{s_{n}}-1)-\sum_{k=1}^{s_{n}}a_{r_{n}+k}\,p^{r_{n}+k}.






Assuming that α′\alpha^{\prime} is an algebraic number in 𝐐p{\bf{Q}}_{p},
we apply Theorem 4.1 of Schlickewei [33] with the linear forms
L1,p​(x,y,z)=xL_{1,p}(x,y,z)=x, L2,p​(x,y,z)=yL_{2,p}(x,y,z)=y,
L3,p​(x,y,z)=α′​x+α′​y+zL_{3,p}(x,y,z)=\alpha^{\prime}x+\alpha^{\prime}y+z,
L1,∞​(x,y,z)=xL_{1,\infty}(x,y,z)=x, L2,∞​(x,y,z)=yL_{2,\infty}(x,y,z)=y, and
L3,∞​(x,y,z)=zL_{3,\infty}(x,y,z)=z. Setting 𝐱n:=(psn,−1,−pn){\bf x}_{n}:=(p^{s_{n}},-1,-p_{n}),
we get |L1,p​(𝐱n)|p=p−sn|L_{1,p}({\bf x}_{n})|_{p}=p^{-s_{n}} and
|L3,p​(𝐱n)|p≤p−rn−w​sn|L_{3,p}({\bf x}_{n})|_{p}\leq p^{-r_{n}-ws_{n}}.
We then follow the same lines as in the proof of Theorem 5,
and we end up in a contradiction. This proves that α′\alpha^{\prime}
is transcendental.  
 
     





7. Concluding remarks




It is of interest to compare our result with a celebrated theorem
of Christol, Kamae, Mendès France, and Rauzy [12]
concerning algebraic elements of the field 𝐅p​((X)){\bf F}_{p}((X)).
Their result asserts that, for any given prime number pp,
the sequence of integers 𝐮=(uk)k≥1{\bf u}=(u_{k})_{k\geq 1} is
pp-automatic if and only if the formal power series ∑k≥1uk​Xk\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}u_{k}X^{k}
is algebraic over the field of rational functions 𝐅p​(X){\bf F}_{p}(X).
Thanks to Theorem 2, we thus easily derive the following statement.



Theorem 7



Let b≥2b\geq 2 be an integer and pp be a prime number.
The formal power series ∑k≥1uk​Xk\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}u_{k}X^{k} and
the real number ∑k≥1ukbk\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}{u_{k}\over b^{k}} are both algebraic
(over 𝐅p​(X){\bf F}_{p}(X) and over 𝐐{\bf{Q}}, respectively)
if and only if they are rational.





Note that Theorem 2B (see Section 6) naturally gives
rise to a similar result where the real number
∑k≥1ukbk\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}{u_{k}\over b^{k}} is
replaced by the qq-adic number
∑k≥1uk​qk\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}u_{k}q^{k}, for an arbitrary prime number qq.
In particular, this holds true for q=pq=p.




In 1991, Morton and Mourant [28] proved the following result:
If k≥2k\geq 2 is an integer, PP is
a non-zero pattern of digits in base kk, and if
ek,P,b​(n)∈{0,1,…,b−1}e_{k,P,b}(n)\in\{0,1,\ldots,b-1\} counts the number of occurrences
modulo bb of PP in the kk-ary expansion of nn, then the real number
α⁡(k,P,b)=∑n=0+∞ek,P,b​(n)bn\alpha(k,P,b)=\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}{e_{k,P,b}(n)\over b^{n}} is
transcendental except when k=3k=3, P=1P=1 and b=2b=2. Moreover,
we have α⁡(3,1,2)=2/3\alpha(3,1,2)=2/3 in this particular case.




The proof given by Morton and Mourant is based on Theorem 2 and their paper
refers to the work of Loxton and van der Poorten [22].
The present work validates their result.




It is interesting to remark that the simplest case k=2k=2, P=1P=1 and b=2b=2
corresponds to the well-known Thue–Morse number, whose transcendence has
been proved by Mahler [23].
The theorem of Morton and Mourant can thus be seen as a
full generalisation of the Mahler result.
