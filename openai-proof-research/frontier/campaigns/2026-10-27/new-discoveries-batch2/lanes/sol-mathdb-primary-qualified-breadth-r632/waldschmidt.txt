Abstract




Diophantine Analysis is a very active domain of mathematical research
where one finds more conjectures than results.




We collect here a number of open questions concerning Diophantine
equations (including Pillai’s Conjectures), Diophantine
approximation (featuring the a​b​cabc conjecture) and
transcendental number theory (with, for instance, Schanuel’s
Conjecture). Some questions related to Mahler’s measure
and Weil absolute logarithmic height are then considered
(e.g. Lehmer’s Problem). We also discuss Mazur’s question on
the density of rational points on a variety, especially in
the special case of algebraic groups, in connection with
transcendence problems in several variables. We say only a
few words on metric problems, equidistribution questions,
Diophantine approximation on manifolds and Diophantine
analysis on function fields.




This survey grew out of lectures given in several places including India
(October 2000 and September 2002), Ivory Coast (February 2001), Italy (April
2001), Canada (May 2001), Lebanon (November 2002) and France. The author is grateful to all colleagues
who gave him the opportunity to speak on this topic, and also to all those who
contributed by relevant remarks and suggestions.




1.  Diophantine Equations




1.1. Points on Curves




Among the 23 problems of
Hilbert [Hi 1900], [Gu 2000] the tenth one is the shortest statement:




 Given a Diophantine equation with any number of unknown
quantities and with integral numerical coefficients: To devise a process
according to which it can be determined by a finite number of operations
whether the equation is solvable in rational integers.




An equation of the
form
f⁡(x¯)=𝟢f(\underline{x})=0, where f∈ℚ⁡[X1,…,Xn]f\in{{{\tenmsb Q}}}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}] is a given polynomial, while
the unknowns x¯=(x1,…,xn)\underline{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}) are
rational integers, is a Diophantine equation. To solve this
equation amounts to determine the integer points on the corresponding
hypersurface of the affine space. Hilbert’s tenth problem is to give an
algorithm which tells us whether such a given Diophantine equation has a solution
or not.




There are other types of Diophantine equations. First of all one may consider
rational solutions instead of integer ones: in this case one considers
rational points on a hypersurface. Next one may consider integer or rational
points over a number field. There is a situation which is intermediate between
integer and rational points, where the unknowns are SS-integral points. This means that SS is a fixed finite set of prime
numbers (rational primes, or prime ideals in the number field), and
the denominators of the solutions are restricted to belong to
SS. Examples are the Thue-Mahler equation




F(x,y)=p1z1⋯pkzkF(x,y)=p_{1}^{z_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{z_{k}}



where FF is a homogeneous polynomial with integer coefficients and p1,…,pkp_{1},\ldots,p_{k} fixed primes (the
unknowns are
x,y,z1,…,zkx,y,z_{1},\ldots,z_{k} rational integers with zi≥𝟢z_{i}\geq 0) and the generalized Ramanujan-Nagell equation
x2+D=pnx^{2}+D=p^{n} where DD is a fixed integer, pp a fixed prime, and the
unknowns are
xx and
nn rational integers with n≥𝟢n\geq 0 (see for instance [ShT 1986], [Ti 1998]
[Sh 1999] and [BuSh 2001] for these and similar other
questions).




Also, it is interesting to deal with simultaneous Diophantine
equations, i.e. to investigate rational or integer points on algebraic
varieties.




The final answer to Hilbert original 10th problem has been given in 1970
by Yu. Matiyasevich, after the works of M. Davis, H. Putnam
and J. Robinson. This was the culminating stage of a rich and
beautiful theory (see [DaMR 1976],
[Mat 1999] and Ma 19??]).
The solution is negative: there is no hope nowadays to achieve
a complete theory of the subject. But one may still hope that
there is a positive answer if one restricts Hilbert’s initial
question to equations in few variables, say n=𝟤n=2, which
amounts to considering integer points on a plane curve. In
this case deep results have been achieved during the 20th
century and many results are known, but much more remains unveiled.




The most basic results are Siegel’s (1929) and Faltings’s
(1983) ones. Siegel’s Theorem deals with integer points and
produces an algorithm to decide whether the set of solutions
forms a finite or an infinite set. Faltings’s result, solving
Mordell’s Conjecture, does the same for rational solutions,
i.e. rational points on curves. To these two outstanding
achievements of the 20th century, one may add Wiles’s
contribution which not only settles Fermat’s last Theorem, but
also provides a quantity of similar results for other curves
[Kr 1999].




Some natural questions occur:




a) To answer Hilbert’s tenth problem for this special case of plane
curves, which means to give an algorithm to decide whether a given Diophantine
equation
f⁡(x,y)=𝟢f(x,y)=0 has a solution (in ℤ{{{\tenmsb Z}}}, and the same problem for ℚ{{{\tenmsb Q}}}).




b) To give an upper bound for the number of either rational or integral points
on a curve.




c) To give an algorithm for solving explicitly a given Diophantine equation in
two unknowns.




Further questions may be asked. For instance in question b) one might ask for
the exact number of solutions; it may be more relevant to consider more
generally the number of points on any number field, or the number of points of
bounded degree and to investigate related generating series… the number of
open problems is endless!




Our goal here is not to describe in detail the state of the art on these
questions (see for instance [La 1991]). It will suffice to say




- that a complete answer to question a) is not yet available: there is no
algorithm so far (even conjectural ones) to decide whether a curve has a
rational point or not,




- that a number of results are known on question b), the
latest work on this topic being due to G. Rémond [Re 2000] who produces
an effective upper bound for the number of rational points on a curve of
genus ≥𝟤\geq 2,




- and that
question c) is unanswered even for integer points, and even for the special case
of curves of genus 𝟤2.




We do not request a practical algorithm, but only (to
start with) a theoretical one. So our first open problem will be an
effective refinement to Siegel’s Theorem.



Problem 1.1



Let f∈ℤ⁡[X,Y]f\in{{{\tenmsb Z}}}[X,Y] be a polynomial such that the equation f⁡(x,y)=0f(x,y)=0 has only
finitely many solutions (x,y)∈ℤ×ℤ(x,y)\in{{{\tenmsb Z}}}\times{{{\tenmsb Z}}}. Give an upper bound for
max⁡{|x|,|y|}\max\{|x|,|y|\} when (x,y)(x,y) is such a solution, in terms of the degree of ff
and of the maximal absolute value of the coefficients of ff.





That such a bound exists is part of the hypothesis, but the
question is to give it explicitly (and, if possible, in a closed form).




Further similar questions might also be asked for more variables (rational
points on varieties), for instance Schmidt’s norm form equations. We refer
the reader to [La 1974] and
[La 1991] for such questions, including the
Lang-Vojta Conjectures.




Even the simplest case of
quadratic forms gives rise to open problems: the determination of all
positive integers which are represented by a given binary form is far
from being solved. Also it is expected that infinitely many real
quadratic fields have class number one, but it is not even known that
there are infinitely many number fields (without restriction on the degree) with class
number one. Recall that the first complete solution of Gauss’s class
number
𝟣1 and 𝟤2 problems (for imaginary quadratic fields) have been reached
by transcendence methods (Baker and Stark) so it may be considered as a Diophantine
problem. Nowadays more efficient methods (Goldfeld, Gross-Zagier,…–
see [La 1991] Chap. V § 5) are available.




A related open problem is the determination of Euler numeri idonei
[Ri 2000]. Fix a positive integer nn. If pp is an odd
prime for which there exist integers x≥𝟢x\geq 0 and y≥𝟢y\geq 0 with p=x2+n​y2p=x^{2}+ny^{2},
then




(i) gcd⁡(x,n​y)=𝟣\gcd(x,ny)=1




(ii) the equation p=X2+n​Y2p=X^{2}+nY^{2} in integers X≥𝟢X\geq 0
and
Y≥𝟢Y\geq 0 has the only solution X=xX=x and Y=yY=y.




Now let pp be an odd integer such that there exist integers x≥𝟢x\geq 0 and y≥𝟢y\geq 0 with p=x2+n​y2p=x^{2}+ny^{2} and such that the conditions (i) and (ii) above are
satisfied. If these properties imply that pp is prime, then the number nn is
called numeri idonei. Euler found 65 such integers nn:

𝟧𝟪58,
𝟨𝟢60,
𝟩𝟢70, 𝟩𝟤72, 𝟩𝟪78, 𝟪𝟧85, 𝟪𝟪88, 𝟫𝟥93, 𝟣𝟢𝟤102, 𝟣𝟢𝟧105, 𝟣𝟣𝟤112, 𝟣𝟤𝟢120, 𝟣𝟥𝟢130, 𝟣𝟥𝟥133, 𝟣𝟨𝟧165,

𝟥𝟧𝟩357, 𝟥𝟪𝟧385, 𝟦𝟢𝟪408, 𝟦𝟨𝟤462, 𝟧𝟤𝟢520, 𝟩𝟨𝟢760, 𝟪𝟦𝟢840, 𝟣𝟥𝟤𝟢1320, 𝟣𝟥𝟨𝟧1365, 𝟣𝟪𝟦𝟪1848.




It is known that there is at most one more number in the list, but one expects there
is no other one.




Here is just one example ([Sie 1964] problem 58 p. 112; [Guy 1994]
D18) of an open problem dealing with simultaneous Diophantine quadratic
equations: Is there a perfect integer cuboid? The existence of a box
with integer edges x1,x2,x3x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}, integer face diagonals
y1,y2,y3y_{1},y_{2},y_{3} and integer body diagonal zz, amounts to solving the system of four
simultaneous Diophantine equations in seven unknowns




x12+x22=y32x22+x32=y12x32+x12=y22x12+x22+x32=z2\eqalign{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}=&y_{3}^{2}\cr x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}=&y_{1}^{2}\cr x_{3}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}=&y_{2}^{2}\cr x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}&=z^{2}\cr}



in ℤ{{{\tenmsb Z}}}. We don’t know whether there is a solution, but it is known that
there is no perfect integer cuboid with the smallest edge ≤𝟤31\leq 2^{31}.




1.2. Exponential Diophantine Equations




In a Diophantine equation, the unknowns occur as the variables of
polynomials, while in an exponential Diophantine
equation (see [ShT 1986]), some exponents also
are variables. One may consider the above-mentioned Ramanujan-Nagell equation
x2+D=pnx^{2}+D=p^{n} as an exponential Diophantine equation.




A famous problem which was open until 2002 is Catalan’s one which dates back
to 1844 [Cat 1844], the same year where Liouville constructed the first
examples of transcendental numbers (see also [Sie 1964] problem 77
p. 116; [Sie 1970] n∘ 60 p. 42;
[ShT 1986] Chap. 12;
[N 1986] Chap. 11;
[Ri 1994]; [Guy 1994] D9;
[Ri 2000] Chap. 7). The ⟨⟨\scriptscriptstyle\langle\!\langleNote
extraite d’une lettre adressée
à l’Éditeur par Monsieur E. Catalan, Répétiteur à l’école
polytechnique de Paris⟩⟩\scriptscriptstyle\,\rangle\!\rangle, published in Crelle Journal [Cat 1844], reads:




⟨⟨\scriptscriptstyle\langle\!\langleJe vous prie, Monsieur, de bien vouloir énoncer, dans votre recueil,
le théorème suivant, que je crois vrai, bien que je n’aie pas encore
réussi
à le démontrer complètement: d’autres seront peut-être plus
heureux:




Deux nombres entiers consécutifs, autres que 88 et 99, ne peuvent
être des puissances exactes; autrement dit: l’équation xm−yn=1x^{m}-y^{n}=1,
dans laquelle les inconnues sont entières et positives, n’admet qu’une
seule solution.⟩⟩\scriptscriptstyle\,\rangle\!\rangle




This means that the only example of consecutive numbers which are perfect powers
xpx^{p} with p≥𝟤p\geq 2 should be (𝟪,𝟫)(8,9). Further information on the history of this question
is available in Ribenboim’s book [Ri 1994]. Tijdeman’s
result [Ti 1976b] in 1976 shows that there are only
finitely many solutions. More precisely, for any
solution x,y,p,qx,y,p,q, the number max⁡{p,q}\max\{p,q\} can be bounded by an effectively
computable absolute constant. Once max⁡{p,q}\max\{p,q\} is bounded, only finitely
many exponential Diophantine equations remain to be considered, and there are
algorithms to complete the solution (based on Baker’s method).
Such a bound has been computed, but
it is somewhat large: according to M. Mignotte,
any solution x,y,p,qx,y,p,q to Catalan’s equation should satisfy




max⁡{p,q}<𝟣𝟢18.\max\{p,q\}<10^{18}.






Catalan’s claim was finally substantiated by Preda Mihailescu in 2002
[Mi 2002] (see also [Bi 2003]).



Theorem 1.2  (Catalan’s Conjecture)



The equation




xp−yq=𝟣,x^{p}-y^{q}=1,



where the unknowns xx, yy, pp and qq are integers all ≥2\geq 2, has only one
solution (x,y,p,q)=(3,2,2,3)(x,y,p,q)=(3,2,2,3).





The final solution by Mihailescu involves not only sharp measures of linear independence of logarithms of algebraic numbers
(here a specific estimate for two logarithms due to Laurent, Mignotte and Nesterenko is required),
but also
tools from the theory of cyclotomic fields.




Catalan asked for integral solutions, like in Siegel’s Theorem,
while Falting’s Theorem deals with rational points. Dipendra Prasad suggested that the
set of tuple
(x,y,p,q)(x,y,p,q) in ℚ2×ℕ2{{{\tenmsb Q}}}^{2}\times{{{\tenmsb N}}}^{2} satisfying the conditions




 xp−yq=𝟣x^{p}-y^{q}=1, and the
curve
Xp−Yq=1X^{p}-Y^{q}=1 has genus ≥1\geq 1




should be finite – evidence for this is provided by the a​b​cabc Conjecture
(see § 2.1).




The fact that the right hand side in Catalan’s equation is 𝟣1 is
crucial: nothing is known if one replaces it by another positive
integer. The next conjecture was proposed by S.S. Pillai [Pi 1945] at a
conference of the Indian Mathematical Society held in Aligarh (see also
[Sie 1964] problem 78 p. 117;
[ShT 1986]; [Ti 1998]; [Sh 1999]).



Conjecture 1.3  (Pillai)



Let kk be a positive integer.
The equation




xp−yq=k,x^{p}-y^{q}=k,



where the unknowns xx, yy, pp and qq are integers all ≥2\geq 2, has only
finitely many solutions (x,y,p,q)(x,y,p,q).





This means that in the increasing sequence of perfect powers xpx^{p}, with x≥𝟤x\geq 2
and p≥𝟤p\geq 2:




𝟦, 8, 9, 16, 25, 27, 32, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, 121, 125, 128, 144, 169,…,4,\,8,\,9,\,16,\,25,\,27,\,32,\,36,\,49,\,64,\,81,\,100,\,121,\,125,\,128,\,144,\,169,\,\dots,



the difference between two consecutive terms tends to infinity. It is not even
known that for, say, k=𝟤k=2, Pillai’s equation has only
finitely many solutions. A related open question is whether the number 𝟨6 occurs as
a difference between two perfect powers: Is there a solution to the
Diophantine equation
xp−yq=6x^{p}-y^{q}=6?  (see
[Sie 1970] problem 238a p. 116).




A conjecture which implies Pillai’s one has been suggested by T.N. Shorey in
[Sh 2000]: this is the very problem which motivated C.L. Siegel
in [Si 1929]. Let
f∈ℤ⁡[X]f\in{{{\tenmsb Z}}}[X] be a polynomial of degree nn with at least two distinct roots and
f⁡(𝟢)≠𝟢f(0)\not=0. Let LL be the number of nonzero coefficients of ff: write




f⁡(X)=b1​Xn1+⋯+bL−1​XnL−1+bLf(X)=b_{1}X^{n_{1}}+\cdots+b_{L-1}X^{n_{L-1}}+b_{L}



with n=n1>n2>⋯>nL−1>𝟢n=n_{1}>n_{2}>\cdots>n_{L-1}>0 and bi≠𝟢b_{i}\not=0 (𝟣≤i≤L1\leq i\leq L).
Set H=H⁡(f)=max1≤i≤L⁡|bi|H={\rm H}(f)=\max_{1\leq i\leq L}|b_{i}|.



Conjecture 1.4  (Shorey)



There exists a positive number CC which depends only on LL and HH with the
following property. Let mm, xx and yy be rational integers with m≥2m\geq 2 and
|y|>1|y|>1 satisfying




ym=f⁡(x).y^{m}=f(x).



Then either m≤Cm\leq C, or else there is a proper subsum in




ym−b1​xn1−⋯−bL−1​xnL−1−bLy^{m}-b_{1}x^{n_{1}}-\cdots-b_{L-1}x^{n_{L-1}}-b_{L}



which vanishes.





Consider now the positive integers which are perfect powers yqy^{q}, with q≥𝟤q\geq 2, and such that all digits in some basis x≥𝟤x\geq 2 are 𝟣1’s. Examples are
𝟣𝟤𝟣121 in basis 𝟥3, 𝟦𝟢𝟢400 in basis 𝟩7 and 𝟥𝟦𝟥343 in basis 𝟣𝟪18. To find all
solutions amounts to solve the exponential Diophantine equation




xn−𝟣x−1=yq,{x^{n}-1\over x-1}=y^{q},



where the unknown x,y,n,qx,y,n,q are positive rational integers with x≥𝟤x\geq 2,

(x,y,n,q)=(𝟥,𝟣𝟣,𝟧,𝟤),(𝟩,𝟤𝟢,𝟦,𝟤),(𝟣𝟪,𝟩,𝟥,𝟥),(x,y,n,q)=\;(3,11,5,2),\;(7,20,4,2),\;(18,7,3,3),



corresponding to the above-mentioned three examples.
One does not know whether these are the only solutions (see
[ShT 1986]; [Guy 1994] D10;
[Ti 1998]; [Sh 1999],
[BuM 1999] and [Sh 2000]) but it is expected
that there is no other one.




The next question is to determine all perfect powers with identical digits in some
basis, which amounts to solve the equation




z​xn−𝟣x−1=yq,z{x^{n}-1\over x-1}=y^{q},



where the unknown x,y,n,q,zx,y,n,q,z are positive rational integers with x≥𝟤x\geq 2,
y≥𝟣y\geq 1,
n≥𝟥n\geq 3, 𝟣≤z<x1\leq z<x and q≥𝟤q\geq 2.




Another type of exponential Diophantine equation has been studied in a joint
paper by H.P. Schlickewei and W.M. Schmidt [ScV 2000] where
they state the following conjecture.



Conjecture 1.5



Let k≥2k\geq 2
be an integer and α1,…,αn\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n} be non-zero elements in a field
KK of zero characteristic, such that no quotient
αi/αj\alpha_{i}/\alpha_{j} with j≠ij\not=i is a root of unity. Consider the
function




F⁡(X1,…,Xk)=det(α1X1⋯αkX1⋱α1Xk⋯αkXk).F(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})=\det\pmatrix{\alpha_{1}^{X_{1}}&\cdots&\alpha_{k}^{X_{1}}\cr\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\cr\alpha_{1}^{X_{k}}&\cdots&\alpha_{k}^{X_{k}}\cr}.



Then the equation




F⁡(𝟢,x2,…,xk)=𝟢F(0,x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})=0



has only finitely many solutions (x2,…,xk)∈ℤk−1(x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})\in{{{\tenmsb Z}}}^{k-1} such that
in the corresponding determinant, all
(k−1)×k(k-1)\times k and all k×(k−1)k\times(k-1) submatrices have rank k−1k-1.





Further exponential Diophantine equations are worth of study. See for instance
[N 1986] Chap. III, [ShT 1986],
[Ti 1998] and [Sh 1999].




Among the numerous applications of Baker’s transcendence method are several
questions related to the greatest prime factors of certain numbers. In
this connection we mention Grimm’s Conjecture ([Gri 1969],
[N 1986] Chap. III
§ 3, [Guy 1994] B32):



Conjecture 1.6  (Grimm)



Given kk
consecutive composite integers n+1,…,n+kn+1,\ldots,n+k, there exist
kk distinct primes
p1,…,pkp_{1},\ldots,p_{k} such that n+jn+j is divisible by pjp_{j}, 1≤j≤k1\leq j\leq k.





M. Langevin [Lan 1977] rephrased this conjecture as follows: given an
increasing sequence of positive integers n1<⋯<nkn_{1}<\cdots<n_{k} for which the
product
n1⋯nkn_{1}\cdots n_{k} has fewer than kk distinct prime factors, there is a
prime pp in the range n1≤p≤nkn_{1}\leq p\leq n_{k}. The equivalence of this with the original fromulation follows from the marriage “theorem”.




According to P. Erdős and R.G. Selfridge, a consequence of
Conjecture 1.6 is that each interval
[N2,(N+𝟣)2][N^{2},(N+1)^{2}],
N≥𝟣N\geq 1, should contain a prime number.




A weaker form of Conjecture 1.6, also open, is:



Conjecture 1.7



If there is no prime in
the interval
[n+1,n+k][n+1,n+k], then the product (n+1)⋯(n+k)(n+1)\cdots(n+k) has at least kk distinct prime
divisors.





M. Langevin [Lan 1977] extended Grimm’s Conjecture
to arithmetical progressions, and also suggested a
stronger statement than Conjecture 1.6:



Conjecture 1.8
 (Langevin)



Given an increasing sequence n1<n2<⋯<nkn_{1}<n_{2}<\cdots<n_{k} of
positive integers such that n1,n2,…,nkn_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{k}
are multiplicatively dependent, there exists
a prime number in the interval [n1,nk][n_{1},n_{k}].





Even if they may not be classified as Diophantine questions, the following
open problems (see [La 1996]) are related to this topic: the twin
prime conjecture, Goldbach problem (is every even integer
≥4\geq 4 the sum of two primes?), Bouniakovsky’s conjecture, Schinzel’s
hypothesis (H) (see also [Sie 1964] § 29) and the Bateman-Horn

2.3. Irrationality and Linear Independence Measures




Given a real number θ\theta, the first Diophantine question is to decide
whether θ\theta is rational or not. This is a qualitative question, and it
is remarkable that an answer is provided by a quantitative property of
θ\theta: it depends ultimately on the quality of rational Diophantine
approximations to θ\theta. Indeed, on one hand, if θ\theta is rational,
then there exists a positive constant c=c⁡(θ)c=c(\theta) such that




|θ−pq|>cq\left|\theta-{p\over q}\right|>{c\over q}



for any p/q∈ℚp/q\in{{{\tenmsb Q}}}.
An admissible value for cc is 𝟣/b1/b when θ=a/b\theta=a/b. On the other hand, if
θ\theta is irrational, then there are infinitely many rational numbers
p/qp/q such that




𝟢<|θ−pq|<𝟣q2​⋅0<\left|\theta-{p\over q}\right|<{1\over q^{2}}\mathchar 25089\relax



Hence, in order to prove that θ\theta is irrational, it suffices to prove
that
for any ϵ>𝟢\epsilon>0
there is a rational number
p/qp/q such that




𝟢<|θ−pq|<ϵq​⋅0<\left|\theta-{p\over q}\right|<{\epsilon\over q}\mathchar 25089\relax



This is a rather weak requirement: there are rational approximations in 𝟣/q21/q^{2},
and we need only to produce rational approximations better than the trivial
ones in c/qc/q. Accordingly one should expect that it rather easy to
prove the irrationality of a given real number. In spite of that, the class of
“interesting” real numbers which are known to be irrational is not as large as
one would expect

Here is another irrationality question raised by P. Erdős and

Assume now that the first step has been completed and that we know
our number θ\theta is irrational. Then there are (at least) two directions
for further investigation:




1) Considering several real numbers θ1,…,θn\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}, a
fundamental question is to decide whether or not they are linearly independent
over
ℚ{{{\tenmsb Q}}}. One main example is to start with the successive powers of one number:
𝟣,θ,θ2,…,θn−11,\theta,\theta^{2},\ldots,\theta^{n-1}; the goal is to decide
whether θ\theta is algebraic of degree <n<n. If nn is not fixed, the
question is whether

complex numbers, will be considered in the next section. Notice also that the
problem of algebraic independence is included here: it amounts to the linear
independence of monomials.

quantitative refinement of the irrationality statement, namely an irrationality measure: we wish to bound from below the nonzero number

namely if one can prove only that one at least of

Earlier we distinguished two directions for research once we know the

investigates the quality of rational approximation. One can combine both: a
quantitative version of a result of ℚ{{{\tenmsb Q}}}-linear independence of nn real numbers

For some specific classes of transcendental numbers,

A general and important problem is to improve the known

The next step, which is to get sharp measures of algebraic

Another topic which belongs to Diophantine approximation is the theory of equidistributed sequences. For a positive integer r≥𝟤r\geq 2, a normal

5. Further Topics




5.1. Metric Problems




Among the motivations to study metric problems in Diophantine analysis
(not to mention secular perturbations in astronomy and the statistical
mechanics of a gas – see [Ha 1998]), one expects to guess the behavior
of certain classes of numbers (like algebraic numbers, logarithms of
algebraic numbers, and numbers given as values of classical functions,
suitably normalized [La 1971] p. 658 and 664).




A first example is related to Wirsing-Schmidt’s
Conjecture: in 1965, V.G. Sprindzuk
showed that the conjecture 4.11 is true for almost
all
θ\theta (for Lebesgue measure).




A second example is the
question of refining Roth’s Theorem: Conjecture 2.12 is
motivated by Khinchine’s Theorem ([Sp 1979] Chap. I, § 1, Th. 1 p. 1)
which answers the question of rational Diophantine approximation for almost
all real numbers. In 1926 A. Khinchine himself extended his result to
simultaneous Diophantine rational approximation




max1≤i≤n⁡|q​αi−pi|\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|q\alpha_{i}-p_{i}|



([Sp 1979] Chap. I, § 4, Th. 8 p. 28), and in 1938 A.V. Groshev
proved the first very general theorem of Khinchine type for systems of linear
forms




max1≤i≤n⁡|q1​αi​1+⋯+qm​αi​m−pi|\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|q_{1}\alpha_{i1}+\cdots+q_{m}\alpha_{im}-p_{i}|



([Sp 1979] Chap. I, § 5, Th. 12 p. 33). Using the same heuristic, one
may extend Conjecture 2.12 in the context of simultaneous
linear combinations of algebraic numbers.




In Conjecture 2.12 (as well as in
Khinchine’s result for almost
all real numbers) the function q​ψ​(q)q\psi(q) is assumed to be nonincreasing.
A conjecture of Duffin and Schaeffer (see [Sp 1979] Chap. 1, § 2,
p. 17 and [Ha 1998]) would enable one to work without such a
restriction. Denote by φ⁡(n)\varphi(n) Euler’s function

The Khinchine-Goshev Theorem has been extended to certain manifolds
(see [Sp 1979], [BeD 1999], as well as more recent papers
by V. Bernick, M. Dodson, D Kleinbock and G. Margulis). Further, connections
between the metrical theory of Diophantine approximation on one hand,

including D. Sullivan, S.J. Dani,

5.2. Function Fields




Let KK be a field and 𝒞=K⁡((T−1)){\cal C}=K((T^{-1})) the field of Laurent
series on KK. The field 𝒞{\cal C} has similar properties to the real
number field, where
ℤ{{{\tenmsb Z}}} is replaced by K⁡[T]K[T] and ℚ{{{\tenmsb Q}}} by
K⁡(T)K(T). An absolute value on
𝒞{\cal C} is defined by selecting |T|>𝟣|T|>1: we set
|α|=|T|k|\alpha|=|T|^{k} if α=∑n∈ℤan​T−n\alpha=\sum_{n\in{{{\sevenmsb Z}}}}a_{n}T^{-n} is a nonzero element of
𝒞{\cal C}, where k=deg⁡(α)k=\deg(\alpha) denotes the least rational integer such that
ak≠𝟢a_{k}\not=0. Hence
𝒞{\cal C} is the completion of K⁡(T)K(T) for this absolute value.




A theory of Diophantine approximation has been developed on 𝒞{\cal C}
in analogy with the classical one. If KK has zero characteristic, the result
are very similar to the classical ones. But if KK has finite characteristic,
the situation is completely different (see
[dML 1999] and [Sch 2000]). It is not yet even clear
how to describe the situation from a conjectural point of view: a conjectural
description of the set of algebraic numbers for which a Roth type inequality
is valid is still missing. Some algebraic elements satisfy a Roth type inequality, while for some others, Liouville’s estimate is optimal. However, from a certain point of view, much more is known in the function field case, since the exact approximation
exponent is known for several classes of algebraic numbers.




There is also a transcendence theory over function fields. The starting point is
a work of Carlitz in the 40’s. He defines functions on 𝒞{\cal C} which behave like
an analog of the exponential function (Carlitz module). A generalization
is due to Drinfeld (Drinfeld modules), and a number of results on the
transcendence of numbers related to these objects are known, going much further
than their classical (complex) counterpart. However the theory is far from
being complete. An analog of Schanuel’s Conjecture for Drinfeld modules is
proposed by W.D. Brownawell in [Bro 1998], together with many further
related problems, including large transcendence degree, Diophantine geometry,
values of Carlitz-Bessel functions and values of gamma functions.




For the study of Diophantine approximation, an important tool (which is not
available in the classical number theoretic case) is the derivation d/d​Td/dT.
In the transcendence
theory this gives rise to new questions which started to be investigated by
L. Denis. Also in the function field case interesting new questions arise
by considering several characteristics. So Diophantine analysis for
function fields involve different aspects, some which are reminiscent of the
classical theory, and some which have no counterpart.




For the related transcendence theory involving
automata theory, we refer to the paper by D. Thakur [Th 1998]
(especially on p.389–390) and to [AS-2003] for the state of the art concerning the following
open problem:



Conjecture 5.4  (J.H. Loxton and A.J. van der Poorten)



Let (ni)i≥0(n_{i})_{i\geq 0} be an
increasing sequence of positive integers. Assume there is a prime
number pp such that the power series




∑i≥0zni∈𝔽p​[[z]]\sum_{i\geq 0}z^{n_{i}}\in{{{\tenmsb F}}}_{p}[[z]]



is algebraic over 𝔽p​(z){{{\tenmsb F}}}_{p}(z) and irrational (not in 𝔽p​(z){{{\tenmsb F}}}_{p}(z)). Then the real
number
