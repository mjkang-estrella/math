1       Background
For a number of years we have been investigating the geometric and algebraic properties of a family
of discrete sets of points in Euclidean space generated by a simple binary operation: pairwise affine
combination by a fixed parameter, which we call fixed-parameter extrapolation. By varying the
parameter and the set of initial points, a large variety of point sets emerge. To our surprise, many of
these sets display aperiodic order and share properties of so-called “quasicrystals” or “quasilattices.”
Such sets display some ordered crystal-like properties (e.g., generation by a regular set of local rules,
such as a finite set of tiles, and possessing a kind of repetitivity), but are “aperiodic” in the sense
that they have no translational symmetry. The most famous of such systems are Penrose’s aperiodic
tilings of the plane [16]. Mathematically, a widely accepted way of capturing the idea of aperiodic
order is via the notion of Meyer sets, which we define later. Our goal is to classify the sets generated
by fixed-parameter extrapolation in terms of a number of these properties, including but not limited
to aperiodicity, uniform discreteness, and relative density. We also seek to determine exactly which
parameter values lead to which types of sets.
     In this column, we set down the basic definitions, and highlight some of the main results obtained
so far. Results are stated without proof, and we present just enough to convey an overall sense of
the work, towards the goal of formulating some of our many open problems. For all details and
proofs, we refer the reader to our paper at https://arxiv.org/abs/1212.2889. This work falls
mainly in the area of discrete geometry, but we present several computational questions as well.
     We begin with our main definition, which generalizes the notion of convexity. We usually work
with the complex numbers C, although we occasionally consider other fields F such as R. In the
standard definition, a set A ⊆ C is said to be convex iff, for any a, b ∈ A, for any real number
λ ∈ [0, 1], we have (1 − λ)a + λb ∈ A. It is not hard to see that the definition still holds if we fix the
parameter λ to be some real number strictly between 0 and 1, provided we take the (topological)
closure of the resulting set. E.g., in the case of λ = 1/2, we say that if a topologically closed

Definition 1.2. For any λ ∈ C and any set S ⊆ C, we define the λ-closure of S, denoted Qλ (S), to
be the ⊆-minimum λ-convex superset of S. We let Qλ be shorthand for Qλ ({0, 1}), the λ-closure

Furthermore, properties of sets Qλ (S) for |S| > 2 can often be inferred from Qλ . The points 0
and 1 are often chosen for convenience as well, but since λ-convexity is invariant under C-affine
transformations, any two initial points would yield a set with the same essential properties.
    After obtaining the results we discuss here, we discovered that fixed-parameter extrapolation
(under various names, including, for example, s-convexity, quasicrystal addition, quasiaddition,
and τ -inflation) had previously been investigated in the literature, and has quite a long history,
especially in the context of the study of quasicrystals. The earliest to investigate the operation were
Calvert [3] and, a few years later, Pinch [17], who proved a number of properties of λ-convex subsets
of R for real λ. Berman & Moody [2] were the first to apply the idea to the study of quasicrystals,
specifically in the case λ = 1 + ϕ (where ϕ is the golden ratio, encountered again below). This
value of λ is significant, because it gives the simplest example where Qλ is discrete and aperiodic.
Subsequently, other authors have looked at other values of λ that result in quasicrystal structures
(most relevant to our work are [9], [10], and [11]). However, as far as we know, all work up to this
point has dealt exclusively with λ ∈ R. Ours is the first in-depth study that considers non-real λ,
introduces the idea of strong PV numbers (which generalize the subset of real irrationals considered
in [17]), and furthermore, is applied to domains other than R and C. Most of the generalizations
to complex λ are far from trivial. We indicate, in the course of our discussion of specific results,
how our work relates to or generalizes prior work.

We begin this survey of our results with a few basic observations. First, owing to the continuity of
the ?λ operation, one can easily show that if A is any λ-convex set, then its (topological) closure,
denoted A, is also λ-convex. Second, if Qλ is convex (by the standard definition of convexity), then
there are only three possibilities: It is the unit interval, the real line, or the entire complex plane.
Theorem 1.3. Suppose Qλ is convex.
  1. If λ ∈ [0, 1], then Qλ = [0, 1].
  2. If λ ∈ R \ [0, 1], then Qλ = R (essentially proved in [17, Proposition 7]).
  3. If λ ∈ C \ R, then Qλ = C.
   In light of this theorem, most of our work concentrates on determining, for various λ ∈ C,
whether or not Qλ is convex, and if not, characterizing Qλ . To this end, we can begin by broadly
categorizing Qλ as either having convex closure (λ ∈ C) or being discrete (λ ∈ D).
Definition 1.4. Let C := {λ ∈ C : Qλ is convex}. Let D := C \ C.
Highlights of this characterization include the following:

1.2      sPV Numbers, Delone sets, and Meyer sets
A Pisot-Vijayaraghavan number (or PV number for short)1 is an algebraic integer α > 1 whose
Galois conjugates α0 (other than α) all lie inside the unit disk in C, i.e., satisfy |α0 | < 1. PV
numbers arise naturally in the study of aperiodic order. The notion can be relaxed to allow for
non-real α by excluding both α and its complex conjugate α∗ from the norm requirement. For our
work, we need a stronger definition.
Definition 1.6. We call an algebraic integer α ∈ C a strong PV number iff its conjugates, other
than α and α∗ , all lie in the open unit interval (0, 1). In this case, we also say that α is sPV.
We say that a strong PV number α is trivial if it has no conjugates other than α and α∗ (i.e., no
conjugates in (0, 1)). Otherwise, α is nontrivial.
                                                              √
    Nontrivial strong PV numbers include 1 + ϕ and 2 + 2, and there are infinitely many real,
irrational—hence nontrivial—strong PV numbers. Every  √     strong PV number greater than 1 is a PV
number, but not conversely; for example, ϕ and 1 + 2 are PV numbers but not sPV. Theorem 1.9
below implies that Qλ is discrete for all strong PV numbers λ. This result does not extend to all
PV numbers; for example, Qϕ = Q1+√2 = R.
    The geometric context for Theorem 1.9 derives from discrete geometry, particularly concepts
relating to ordered but aperiodic point sets in Euclidean space. The theory of such sets gained much
impetus following the experimental discovery of so-called “quasicrystals”—materials whose atomic
arrangements share many properties of crystals (e.g., sharp spikes in X-ray diffraction patterns),
but—unlike true crystals—lack translational symmetry. Much of the mathematical theory of the
corresponding point sets came well before this experimental discovery, and is due to Yves Meyer [13,
14]. The theory also relates closely to aperiodic tilings of the plane [4, 5, 16]. This section draws
somewhat from the recent exposition of Baake & Grimm [1] as well as papers by Moody [2, 15],
and related papers by Lagarias [7, 8]. We need not present the concepts in their full generality.
Definition 1.7. Let X be any metric space (think Rn ), and let A be any subset of X with at least
two elements.
      • A is uniformly discrete iff there exists an r > 0 such that |x − y| > r for all distinct x, y ∈ A.
      • A is relatively dense (in X) iff there exists an R > 0 such that, for all x ∈ X there exists
        y ∈ A such that |x − y| < R.
      • A is a Delone set (in X) iff it is both uniformly discrete and relatively dense (in X).
    As we mentioned before, one can show that if Qλ is discrete, then points in Qλ are at least unit
distance apart, thus Qλ is uniformly discrete. This was shown by Pinch for real λ [17, Proposi-
tion 10], but the generalization to complex λ is not straightforward.

    One way to define point sets in Euclidean space with “aperiodic order” is via so-called “Meyer
sets” (Definition 1.8, below), in particular those which lack any translational symmetry. It turns
out that many Meyer sets are of the form Qλ (S) for certain λ and S. There are many equivalent
characterizations of Meyer sets; see, for example, Moody [15, Theorem 9.1]. All Meyer sets are
Delone sets, but Meyer sets have additional properties, such as finite local complexity, not shared
by all Delone sets.2
    One way of producing aperiodic Meyer sets is through a construction called a cut-and-project
scheme (and this was Meyer’s original approach). We do not define this concept here, except to
say that it gives rise to the notion of a “model set’—also called a “cut-and-project set,” a certain
kind of Delone set—and Meyer sets are relatively dense subsets of these sets. (We give an example
of a cut-and-project set below.)
    Luckily for us, citing work of Meyer [13] and Lagarias [7] as well as his own work, Moody [15]
characterizes Meyer sets in various ways, some of which do not require knowledge of cut-and-project
schemes. We will take one of these characterizations as our “definition” of a Meyer set:

Definition 1.8 (Lagarias, Moody [7, 15]). Let A be a relatively dense subset of Rn . Then A is
a Meyer set if and only if A − A is uniformly discrete, where A − A = {x − y | x, y ∈ A} is the
Minkowski difference of A with itself.

       We can now state our main mechanism for obtaining discrete Qλ (S).

Theorem 1.9. If λ is sPV, then Qλ (S) is uniformly discrete for any finite set S ⊆ Q(λ). In fact,
Qλ (S) − Qλ (S) is uniformly discrete for all such S.

Remark. Note that we do not show that Qλ (S) is relatively dense. If true, then it would be a
Meyer set. This is one of our primary open problems. See Open Question 2.5, below.         
    Pinch proved the special case of Theorem 1.9 where λ ∈ R and S = {0, 1} [17, Proposition 12].
Our proof of this theorem essentially shows that Qλ (S) is a subset of a model or cut-and-project
set. Thus, for example, the right-hand side of Eq. (1) below is a cut-and-project set. Although it
would then follow directly from previous results of Meyer [13] (also see [15]) that Qλ (S) is uniformly
discrete, our paper includes a self-contained proof for the sake of completeness. (The case where λ
is not real and S = {0, 1} was proved by Rohit Gurjar [6].) Probably our most important conjecture
is that the converse of this theorem holds (Conjecture 2.1).
    To illustrate this with one specific case—where S = {0, 1}—the proof of Theorem 1.9 gives the
following containment for all algebraic integers λ (letting d be the degree of λ):

 Qλ ⊆ {p(λ) | p ∈ Z[x] & deg(p) < d & 0 ≤ p(µ) ≤ 1 for all µ ∈ (0, 1) and conjugate to λ} . (1)

When λ is sPV, then the right-hand side of (1) is uniformly discrete. As an example, equality holds
in (1) for λ = 1 + ϕ, and we get that Q1+ϕ = {1} ∪ {dn/ϕe + nϕ | n ∈ Z}. This set contains no
infinite arithmetic progressions and hence has no translational symmetry. Except for 0 and 1, any
two adjacent points in Q1+ϕ differ either by ϕ or by 1 + ϕ.
    The degree requirement on p can be dropped in (1), since any polynomial with degree ≥ d can
be reduced modulo the minimal polynomial of λ without affecting its value on λ or any of the µ.

We have many more questions than we can investigate in any reasonable length of time. We only
give a sampling in this section. Some may be easy, but we have just not looked at them in depth.
    Recall the set C (Definition 1.4) and its complement D := C \ C. We know that D is closed,
discrete, and contains only algebraic integers. We also know that D contains all strong PV numbers

Conjecture 2.1. Qλ is discrete if and only if λ is a strong PV number.

But beyond the fact that D consists only of algebraic integers, we know little about C and D.

2.2    L-convex, auto-convex, and λ-semiconvex sets
Definition 2.8. Let L and S be any subsets of C.

   • S is L-convex iff S is λ-convex for all λ ∈ L.

   • Let QL (S) be the least L-convex superset of S.

   • S is auto-convex iff S is S-convex.

    All the Qλ sets are auto-convex by the following result:4

Proposition 2.9. For any λ, µ ∈ C, if µ ∈ Qλ , then Qλ is µ-convex, and consequently, Qµ ⊆ Qλ .

    The same goes for all Qλ . We conjecture the converse.

Conjecture 2.10. If S ⊆ C contains 0 and 1, is topologically closed, and is auto-convex, then
S = Qλ for some λ ∈ C.

2.3   Computational Questions
Questions such as Research Plan 2.4 lead us to the following line of inquiry. If Qλ is discrete, then
each element of Qλ can be expressed as a polynomial in λ, of fixed degree, with integer coefficients.
Qλ is countably infinite. Furthermore, it is computably enumerable: If x ∈ Qλ , by enumerating all
extrapolations beginning with {0, 1}, we will eventually obtain x. It is possible by similar reasoning
that Qλ is in NP; however it is not at all obvious that a succinct proof that x ∈ Qλ (to say nothing
of x 6∈ Qλ ) can always be found, and its computational complexity is wide open. For those λ that
satisfy Open Question 2.3, we have an effective procedure to determine if any x is in Qλ . For other
λ, however, especially if λ is not a strong PV number but Qλ is nevertheless discrete, we do not
know if Qλ is decidable (although we do know that Q[x] , defined in the next section, is decidable).
Open Question 2.13. If Qλ is discrete, is it decidable? Uniformly in λ? If it is decidable, what
is its computational complexity? When is it true that Qλ ∈ NP, and how does this depend on λ?
    Note that if Conjecture 2.1 is false, it’s possible that the answer depends on whether or not λ is
a strong PV number. We conjecture that Qλ is always decidable, but have no intuition regarding
its containment in NP, to say nothing of P.
