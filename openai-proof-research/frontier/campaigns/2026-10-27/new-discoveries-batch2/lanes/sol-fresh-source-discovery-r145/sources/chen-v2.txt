On the mixed metric dimension of 2-connected graphs




Shi Chen and Xuanlong Ma

Note: Corresponding author

Affiliation: School of Science, Xi’an Petroleum University, Xi’an 710065, P. R. China



Abstract
    

We show that for a 2-connected graph G which is not a cycle, the mixed metric dimension of G is at most 2c(G), where c(G) is the cyclomatic number of G.
As an immediate application, we prove a conjecture
proposed by Sedlar and Škrekovski (2021).
    

Keywords: Mixed metric dimension; Cyclomatic number; 2-connected graph
    

2020 MSC: 05C12; 05C38
  
††
      
      
    E-mail addresses: nuoyoushi@gmail.com (Chen), xuanlma@xsyu.edu.cn (Ma)



1 Introduction




Every graph considered in our paper is finite, simple, and connected.
Let G be a graph. Denote by V(G) and E(G) the vertex set and edge set of G, respectively.
Let u and v be two vertices of G. The distance between u and v is denoted by {\rm d}_{G}(u,v), or simply by {\rm d}(u,v) when no confusion can arise.
For distinct x,y\in V(G), we say a vertex v\in V(G)
distinguishes x and y whenever
{\rm d}(v,x)\neq{\rm d}(v,y).
A subset S\subseteq V(G) is called a metric generator of G if for every
pair of distinct vertices x,y\in V(G), there exists a vertex in S such that this vertex distinguishes x and y.
A metric generator of G with minimum cardinality is called
a metric basis
of G, and the cardinality of a metric basis is referred to as the metric dimension of G.
The concept of metric dimension was independently introduced in the 1970s by Harary and Melter [4] and Slater [13]. The aim is to distinguish vertices of a graph using distances.
The metric dimension of a graph has a wide range of applications, including robot navigation in networks [8], chemistry [3], pattern recognition and image processing [10], and the game of Mastermind [1].




As a further variation of the classical notion of a metric generator, the concept of an edge metric generator was introduced in [7] to distinguish the edges of a graph by means of distances from a prescribed set of vertices. Although a metric basis of a connected graph G uniquely determines every vertex of G through its distance representation, it does not necessarily distinguish all edges of G. This observation motivates the following definitions.
For a vertex v\in V(G) and an edge e=\{u,w\}\in E(G), the distance between v and e is defined by
{\rm d}(v,e)=\min\{{\rm d}(v,u),{\rm d}(v,w)\}.
A vertex x\in V(G) is said to distinguish two distinct edges e_{1},e_{2}\in E(G) on condition that
{\rm d}(x,e_{1})\neq{\rm d}(x,e_{2}).
A set S\subseteq V(G) is called an edge metric generator for G provided that every pair of distinct edges of G is distinguished by some vertex of S. An edge metric generator of minimum cardinality is called an edge metric basis of G, and its cardinality is called the edge metric dimension of G.




Given a graph G, we write




O(G)=V(G)\cup E(G).



A vertex v\in V(G) is said to distinguish two distinct elements x,y\in O(G) (vertices or edges) if
{\rm d}(v,x)\neq{\rm d}(v,y).
A set S\subseteq V(G) is called a mixed metric generator for G if for every pair of distinct elements x,y\in O(G), there exists a vertex of S such that
it can distinguish x,y.
The cardinality of a mixed metric generator with minimum size, denoted by {\rm dim_{m}}(G), is called the mixed metric dimension of G.
The mixed metric dimension of a graph was first introduced in [6].
For a comprehensive overview of the variants of metric dimension and their applications to network detection, graph isomorphism, coin-weighing problems, and machine learning, we refer the reader to the two surveys [9, 14].




In [6],
the authors determined the mixed metric dimension of several families of graphs, including cycles, complete bipartite graphs, trees, and grid graphs.
They also showed that the problem of determining the
mixed metric dimension of a graph is NP-hard in the general case.
A leaf of G is a vertex with degree 1.
Equivalently, a leaf of G is a vertex of G that is incident to exactly one edge. Denote by \ell(G)
the number of all leaves of G.
The cyclomatic number of G, denoted by c(G), is
the number of independent cycles of G. Namely




c(G)=|E(G)|-|V(G)|+1.



In [12],
Sedlar and Škrekovski obtained the exact value of the mixed metric dimension of a unicyclic graph and they proposed the following conjecture:



Conjecture 1.1



([12, Conjecture 13])
Let G be a connected graph which is not a cycle.
Then {\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq\ell(G)+2c(G).





Conjecture 1.1 holds for trees and 3-connected graphs, see [12].
In [11], the authors showed that Conjecture 1.1 holds for all Theta graphs. Recently, Chakraborty, Foucaud, and Hakanen [2] proved that Conjecture 1.1 holds for a connected graph which is not a tree such that \ell(G)\geq 1, and a connected graph G with \ell(G)=0 and has a cut-vertex.
In [5], the authors gave some graphs satisfying Conjecture 1.1.
In this paper, we will prove the following theorem:



Theorem 1.2



Let G be a 2-connected graph which is not a cycle.
Then




{\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq 2c(G).







As an immediate application of Theorem 1.2, we confirm Conjecture 1.1.





2 Preliminaries




Let G be a graph.
A thread in a graph G is a path whose internal vertices all have degree 2 in G.
Let P=(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{l}) be a thread of G.
We use G-P^{0} to denote the subgraph of G that is obtained by removing the internal vertices of this thread P and keeping the endpoints p_{0} and p_{l}.
For convenience, throughout this paper, we denote the vertex p_{i} of P by [i,i], and the edge \{p_{j},p_{j+1}\} of P by [j,j+1], where 0\leq i\leq l and 0\leq j\leq l-1.
Namely, every element of P can be expressed as




[a,b],~~b-a\in\{0,1\},~~0\leq a\leq b\leq l.



If G-P^{0} is connected and l<{\rm d}_{G-P^{0}}(p_{0},p_{l}), then
P is called a short thread of G. If \deg(p_{0})\geq 3 and \deg(p_{l})\geq 3, then this thread P is called
maximal. By [15], a 2-connected graph which is not a cycle can be decomposed into distinct maximal threads.
Write




\mathcal{B}=\{v\in V(G):\deg(v)\geq 3\}.





Lemma 2.1



Let G be a graph with a u,v-thread P of length l. If there exists z\notin V(P) such that
{\rm d}(u,z)+{\rm d}(v,z)\leq l, then every shortest path from
u to z does not pass through any internal vertex of
P.





Proof. Suppose, for a contradiction, that there exists a shortest path from u to z pass through some internal vertex of
P. Then clearly, {\rm d}(u,z)=l+{\rm d}(v,z). It follows that
l+2{\rm d}(v,z)={\rm d}(u,z)+{\rm d}(v,z)\leq l, which is impossible as {\rm d}(v,z)\neq 0.
\hfill\Box\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt



Lemma 2.2



Let G be a graph with a u,v-thread P of length l. If there exists an edge e=\{a,b\}\in E(G)\setminus E(P) such that
{\rm d}(u,e)+{\rm d}(v,e)\leq l-1, then there exists a u,v-path W in G-P^{0} such that |W|\leq l.





Proof. Assume that {\rm d}(u,e)={\rm d}(u,u^{\prime}) and {\rm d}(v,e)={\rm d}(v,v^{\prime}), where u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in\{a,b\}.




Suppose, for a contradiction, that there exists a shortest path from u to u^{\prime} pass through some internal vertex of
P. Then we have that {\rm d}(u,u^{\prime})=l+{\rm d}(v,u^{\prime}).
Note that {\rm d}(v,u^{\prime})\geq{\rm d}(v,e).
It follows that




{\rm d}(u,e)+{\rm d}(v,e)={\rm d}(u,u^{\prime})+{\rm d}(v,v^{\prime})=l+{\rm d}(v,u^{\prime})+{\rm d}(v,v^{\prime})\leq l-1,



which is a contradiction. We conclude that every shortest path from u to u^{\prime} do not pass through any internal vertex of
P. Similarly, every shortest path from v to v^{\prime} do not pass through any internal vertex of P.




Take now a shortest path from u to u^{\prime} and a shortest path from v to u^{\prime}. If u^{\prime}=v^{\prime},
then we concatenate the two paths at
u^{\prime} to obtain a u,v-path, say W, which implies that
W in G-P^{0} and |W|={\rm d}(u,e)+{\rm d}(v,e)\leq l-1<l, as desired.
If u^{\prime}\neq v^{\prime}, then we obtain the u,v-path of G-P^{0} has length {\rm d}(u,e)+{\rm d}(v,e)+1\leq l, as wanted.
\hfill\Box\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt



Lemma 2.3



Let G be a 2-connected graph without leaves.
Then |\mathcal{B}|\leq 2c(G)-2.





Proof. It is well known that \sum_{v\in V(G)}\deg(v)=2|E(G)|.
Note that G has no leaves.
We deduce that




\sum_{v\in V(G)}(\deg(v)-2)=\sum_{v\in V(G)}\deg(v)-2|V(G)|=2(|E(G)|-|V(G)|)=2c(G)-2.



It follows that |\mathcal{B}|\leq\sum_{v\in V(G)}(\deg(v)-2)=2c(G)-2, as desired.
\hfill\Box\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt



Lemma 2.4



Let G be a graph with a thread P=(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{l}), where p_{0}=u and p_{l}=v. Suppose that H=G-P^{0} is connected and l\geq{\rm d}_{H}(u,v). Then




{\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq{\rm dim_{m}}(H)+2.



In particular, if \mathcal{R} is a mixed metric generator of H, then \mathcal{R}\cup\{u,w\} is a mixed metric generator of G, where w=p_{\lceil l/2\rceil}.





Proof. Write d={\rm d}_{H}(u,v).
We first claim that for any pair of vertices a,b\in V(H),
{\rm d}_{H}(a,b)={\rm d}_{G}(a,b). Indeed, {\rm d}_{G}(a,b)\leq{\rm d}_{H}(a,b).
In G, if there exists a shortest path from a to b
that avoids the internal vertices of P, then
{\rm d}_{G}(a,b)={\rm d}_{H}(a,b), as desired. Otherwise, every shortest path from a to b must contain an internal vertex of P. Since P is a thread, it follows that every shortest path from a to b must pass through all internal vertices of P, which implies any shortest path from a to b contains the entire P. Now, notice that H is connected and l\geq d. It follows that {\rm d}_{G}(a,b)\geq{\rm d}_{H}(a,b), and so we have {\rm d}_{G}(a,b)={\rm d}_{H}(a,b). We conclude that the above claim is valid.




Now, let \mathcal{R} be a mixed metric generator of H with the smallest cardinality, that is |\mathcal{R}|={\rm dim_{m}}(H).
Without loss of generality, we may assume that there exists
r\in\mathcal{R} such that {\rm d}_{H}(r,u)\geq{\rm d}_{H}(r,v).
Write




A={\rm d}_{H}(r,u),~~B={\rm d}_{H}(r,v),~~\delta_{r}=A-B,



then \delta_{r}\geq 0.
Furthermore, any element of P can be represented as




z=[a,b],~~b-a\in\{0,1\},~~0\leq a\leq b\leq l.



Note that d={\rm d}_{H}(u,v). It follows that




{\rm d}_{G}(u,z)=\min\{a,d+l-b\}

(1)


and




{\rm d}_{G}(r,z)=\min\{A+a,B+l-b\}=B+\min\{\delta_{r}+a,l-b\}.

(2)


Now let m=\lceil\frac{l}{2}\rceil and let w=p_{m}. Then




{\rm d}_{G}(w,z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}m-b,&\hbox{if
$b\leq m$;}\\
a-m,&\hbox{if $a\geq m$.}\end{array}\right.

(3)





Claim 1. Any two distinct elements of P are distinguished by some vertex of \{w,u,r\}.




We say that an element [a,b] of P lies on the left (resp. right) side of w if b\leq m (resp. a\geq m).
Naturally, the element [m,m] of P lies on both the left and the right sides of w.
In the following, we consider two cases.




Case 1. The two distinct elements of P lie on the same side of w.




If the two distinct elements of P lie on the left side of w, then by (1) and (3), we see that the two distinct elements are distinguished by some vertex of \{u,w\}, as desired. Suppose now that the two distinct elements of P lie on the right side of w, say [a,b] and [x,y]. If a\neq x, then w can distinguish them by (3). In the following, assume a=x. Then by (2), we have that




{\rm d}_{G}(r,[a,b])=B+\min\{\delta_{r}+a,l-b\},~~{\rm d}_{G}(r,[x,y])=B+\min\{\delta_{r}+a,l-y\}.

(4)


Since y\geq a, b\geq a, and 2a\geq l, we have that a\geq l-a\geq l-b and a\geq l-y. Note that b\neq y. It follows from (4) that [a,b] and [x,y] are distinguished by r, as desired.




Case 2. The two distinct elements [a,b] and [x,y] of P lie on opposite sides side of w.




Without loss of generality, assume that z_{1}=[a,b] lies on the left side of w and z_{2}=[x,y] lies on the right side of w.
Namely, b\leq m\leq x. From (1), it follows that
{\rm d}_{G}(u,z_{1})=\min\{a,d+l-b\}=a and {\rm d}_{G}(u,z_{2})=\min\{x,d+l-y\}. If a\neq\min\{x,d+l-y\}, then z_{1} and z_{2} are distinguished by u, as desired. Thus, in the following, we assume that




a=\min\{x,d+l-y\}.

(5)





Suppose that a=x. Then z_{1}=[m,m]=w and z_{2}=[m,m+1].
We deduce that z_{1} and z_{2} lie on the right side of w, and so Case 1 ends the proof.




We next suppose that a<x. If b=y, then y=b\leq m\leq x\leq y, which implies that z_{1} and z_{2} lie on the left side of w, and so Case 1 completes our proof. Thus, in the following, assume that b\neq y, that is, y>b.
Now, in view of (2), we have that




{\rm d}_{G}(r,z_{1})=B+\min\{\delta_{r}+a,l-b\},~~{\rm d}_{G}(r,z_{2})=B+\min\{\delta_{r}+x,l-y\}.



It follows from (5) that a=d+l-y. Since d\geq 1 and \delta_{r}\geq 0, we conclude that




l-y=a-d<a<x\leq x+\delta_{r}.



As a result, we have {\rm d}_{G}(r,z_{2})=B+l-y.
If {\rm d}_{G}(r,z_{1})=B+l-b, then clearly, {\rm d}_{G}(r,z_{1})\neq{\rm d}_{G}(r,z_{2}), and so z_{1} and z_{2} are distinguished by r, as wanted. Otherwise, {\rm d}_{G}(r,z_{1})=B+\delta_{r}+a. Consequently, we have




B+\delta_{r}+a-(B+l-y)=\delta_{r}+a-(a-d)=\delta_{r}+d>0.



This forces that {\rm d}_{G}(r,z_{1})\neq{\rm d}_{G}(r,z_{2}), which implies that z_{1} and z_{2} are distinguished by r, as required. Thus, Claim 1 is valid.




Claim 2. For any two elements z\in O(P)\setminus O(H) and z^{\prime}\in O(H), there exists an element in \{u,w\}\cup\mathcal{R} such that it can distinguish z and z^{\prime}.




Note that P is a thread. It follows that




{\rm d}_{G}(w,z^{\prime})=\min\{m+{\rm d}_{H}(u,z^{\prime}),l-m+{\rm d}_{H}(v,z^{\prime})\}\geq l-m.

(6)


If l=2k for some positive integer k, then
{\rm d}_{G}(w,z)\leq k-1 and {\rm d}_{G}(w,z^{\prime})\geq k, which implies that
w distinguishes z and z^{\prime}, as required.




In the following, we assume that l=2k+1\geq 3 for some positive integer k, that is, m=k+1. Then by (3) and (6), it is easy to see that if z\notin\{[1,1],[0,1]\}, then z and z^{\prime} are distinguished by w.
In the following, we consider two cases.




Case 1. z=[1,1].




Indeed, we may assume that w does not distinguish z and z^{\prime}. It follows that




{\rm d}_{G}(w,z^{\prime})={\rm d}_{G}(w,z)=k.



In view of (6), it can be inferred that l-m+{\rm d}_{H}(v,z^{\prime})=k+{\rm d}_{H}(v,z^{\prime})=k, and hence {\rm d}_{H}(v,z^{\prime})=0.
Now, by (2), we have that




{\rm d}_{G}(r,z)=B+\min\{\delta_{r}+1,l-1\}\geq B+1.



However, because of {\rm d}_{H}(v,z^{\prime})=0, we have
{\rm d}_{G}(r,z^{\prime})\leq B. It follows that r distinguishes z and z^{\prime}, as desired.




Case 2. z=[0,1].




Clearly, we may assume that both w and u do not distinguish z and z^{\prime}. Therefore, we have
{\rm d}_{G}(u,z^{\prime})={\rm d}_{G}(u,z)=0, which means
{\rm d}_{G}(u,z^{\prime})={\rm d}_{H}(u,z^{\prime})=0. Moreover, we also have
{\rm d}_{G}(w,z^{\prime})={\rm d}_{G}(w,z)=k by (3).
Now




{\rm d}_{G}(w,z^{\prime})=\min\{k+1+{\rm d}_{H}(u,z^{\prime}),k+{\rm d}_{H}(v,z^{\prime})\}=k=k+{\rm d}_{H}(v,z^{\prime})



implies {\rm d}_{H}(v,z^{\prime})=0. As a consequence,
{\rm d}_{H}(v,z^{\prime})={\rm d}_{H}(u,z^{\prime})=0. We conclude that z^{\prime}=\{u,v\}\in E(G), that is, {\rm d}_{G}(u,v)={\rm d}_{H}(u,v)=1. It follows that there exists a vertex q\in\mathcal{R} such that q can distinguish u and z^{\prime}. Consequently,
{\rm d}_{H}(q,u)\neq{\rm d}_{H}(q,z^{\prime}), which forces {\rm d}_{H}(q,z^{\prime})={\rm d}_{H}(q,v). Hence, we must have
{\rm d}_{H}(q,v)<{\rm d}_{H}(q,u). Now




{\rm d}_{G}(q,z)=\min\{{\rm d}_{H}(q,u),{\rm d}_{H}(q,v)+l-1\}>{\rm d}_{H}(q,v)={\rm d}_{H}(q,z^{\prime})={\rm d}_{G}(q,z^{\prime})



implies that q distinguishes z and z^{\prime}, as desired.
Thus, Claim 2 holds.




Now note that \mathcal{R} is a mixed metric generator of H with the smallest cardinality. Combining Claims 1 and 2, we have that \mathcal{R}\cup\{u,w\} is a mixed metric generator of G. It follows that {\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq{\rm dim_{m}}(H)+2.
\hfill\Box\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt



Lemma 2.5



Let G be a 2-connected graph which is not a cycle.
If every thread of G is short, then \mathcal{B} is a mixed metric generator of G.





Proof. Take distinct z,w\in O(G). It suffices to show that there exists an element of \mathcal{B} such that it can distinguish z and w. We consider three cases as follows.




Case 1. z and w lie on the same thread, and z,w\notin\mathcal{B}.




Take a maximal thread P=(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{l}) of G, let p_{0}=u and p_{l}=v. Then u,v\in\mathcal{B} and
l<{\rm d}_{H}(u,v), where H=G-P^{0} is connected.
Now assume that z,w\in O(P)\setminus\{u,v\}. Note that




{\rm d}_{G}(u,[i,i])=i,~~{\rm d}_{G}(v,[i,i])=l-i,~~0\leq i\leq l,



and




{\rm d}_{G}(u,[j-1,j])=j-1,~~{\rm d}_{G}(v,[j-1,j])=l-j,~~1\leq j\leq l.



It follows that z,w must be distinguished by some element of \{u,v\}, as desired.




Case 2. One z and w belongs to \mathcal{B}.




Without loss of generality, let w\in\mathcal{B}.
If z is a vertex or an edge which is not incident with w,
then {\rm d}_{G}(w,z)>0, which implies that z and w are distinguished by w, as required.
Thus, in the following, we may assume that z is an edge incident with w. Note that \deg(w)\geq 3.
Starting from w, we can obtain a maximal thread Q containing z. Let w^{\prime} be the other endpoint of this maximal thread and let the length of Q be s.
Then w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B} and s\leq{\rm d}_{G-Q^{0}}(w,w^{\prime}). We conclude that




{\rm d}_{G}(w^{\prime},w)=s,~~{\rm d}_{G}(w^{\prime},z)=s-1.



As a result, z and w are distinguished by w^{\prime}, as required.




Case 3. z and w lie on distinct threads, and z,w\notin\mathcal{B}.




Let z lies on the maximal thread P=(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{l}) of G, and let p_{0}=u,p_{l}=v.
In the following, we may assume that both u and v do not distinguish z and w. Namely,




{\rm d}_{G}(u,z)={\rm d}_{G}(u,w),~~{\rm d}_{G}(v,z)={\rm d}_{G}(v,w).

(7)


As a result, we have that




{\rm d}_{G}(u,z)+{\rm d}_{G}(v,z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}l,&\hbox{if
$z$ is a vertex;}\\
l-1,&\hbox{if $z$ is an edge.}\end{array}\right.

(8)





Suppose, for contradiction, that w is a vertex. Then by (7) and (8), we deduce that {\rm d}_{G}(u,w)+{\rm d}_{G}(v,w)=l.
Note that w\notin O(P).
Now Lemma 2.1 implies that
{\rm d}_{G-P^{0}}(u,v)\leq l,
this contradicts that P is short.




We conclude that w is an edge.
Similarly, we can also obtain that z is an edge.
Applying (7) and (8) again,
we know that {\rm d}_{G}(u,w)+{\rm d}_{G}(v,w)=l-1.
It follows from Lemma 2.2 that there exists a path in G-P^{0} such that its length is at most l.
This forces {\rm d}_{G-P^{0}}(u,v)\leq l,
which contradicts that P is short.
Thus, one of u and v must distinguish z and w, as wanted.




Now, combining the above three cases, we conclude that the required result is valid.
\hfill\Box\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt





3 Proof of the Main Result




In this section, we will show Theorem 1.2 and confirm Conjecture 1.1. We begin by citing three known results.



Theorem 3.1



([2, Corollary 16])
Let G be a connected graph which is not a tree such that \ell(G)\geq 1. Then {\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq\ell(G)+2c(G).




Theorem 3.2



([6, Theorem 4.3])
Let G be a tree. Then {\rm dim_{m}}(G)=\ell(G).




Theorem 3.3



([2, Corollary 12(i)])
Let G be a connected graph with \ell(G)=0. If
G has a cut-vertex, then {\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq 2c(G).





We are now ready to prove Theorem 1.2.




Proof of Theorem 1.2.
Note that G is not a cycle and \ell(G)=0. Then
c(G)\geq 2.
If every thread of G is short, then by
Lemmas 2.3 and 2.5, we have that




{\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq|\mathcal{B}|\leq 2c(G)-2\leq 2c(G),



as desired.




In the following, we may assume that
G has a maximal thread P=(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{l}) such that
{\rm d}_{G-P^{0}}(u,v)\leq l. Now let H=G-P^{0}. Clearly, H is connected and has no leaves. It follows that




c(H)=|E(H)|-|V(H)|+1=(|E(G)|-l)-(|V(G)|-(l-1))+1,



that is,




c(G)=c(H)+1.

(9)





Suppose that H is a cycle. Then c(G)=2. Namely, G is a graph in which p_{0} and p_{l} are the only vertices of degree 3, while every other vertex has degree 2.
It follows from [11, Corollaries 8 and 11] that
{\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq 2c(G), as required.




In the following, suppose that H is not a cycle.
Assume that H has a cut-vertex. Theorem 3.3
implies that {\rm dim_{m}}(H)\leq 2c(H).
Moreover, by Lemma 2.4 and (9), we deduce that
{\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq{\rm dim_{m}}(H)+2\leq 2c(H)+2=2c(G),
as wanted.
Thus, next, we may assume that H has no cut vertices. It follows that H is 2-connected.




Note that H is a 2-connected graph which is not a cycle. To complete the proof of this theorem, we now proceed by induction on c(G).
If c(G)=2, then by (9), H is a cycle, and so
the result follows from the above proof.




Now suppose that c(G)\geq 3, and assume that the assertion
holds for every graph G^{\prime} satisfying the hypotheses of the
theorem with c(G^{\prime})<c(G).
Note that H satisfies the hypotheses of our
theorem. In view of (9), by the induction hypothesis, we have {\rm dim_{m}}(H)\leq 2c(H).
Moreover, by Lemma 2.4, we conclude that




{\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq{\rm dim_{m}}(H)+2\leq 2c(H)+2=2c(G).



This completes the induction and the proof.
\hfill\Box\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt




Note that a 2-connected graph has no leaves.
Now, combining Theorems 3.1, 3.2, 3.3, and 1.2,
we conclude that Conjecture 1.1 holds for any connected graph which is not a cycle. Namely, Conjecture 1.1 is valid.
