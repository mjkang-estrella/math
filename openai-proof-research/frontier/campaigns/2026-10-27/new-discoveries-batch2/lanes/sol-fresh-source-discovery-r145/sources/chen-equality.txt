arXiv:2608.17944v1 [math.CO] 18 Aug 2026




Graphs attaining an upper bound on the mixed metric dimension



Shi Chen and Xuanlong Ma

Note: Corresponding author

Affiliation: School of Science, Xi’an Petroleum University, Xi’an 710065, P. R. China



Abstract
    

Given a graph G, we show that the mixed metric dimension of G is exactly \ell(G)+2c(G)
if and only if G is either a cactus graph in which every cycle has precisely one vertex of degree at least 3, or a
balanced \Theta-graph, where \ell(G) and c(G) denote the number of leaves and
the cyclomatic number of G, respectively.
This provides an affirmative answer to a conjecture proposed
by Sedlar and Škrekovski (2021).
    

Key words: Mixed metric dimension; Cyclomatic number; Cactus graph; \Theta-graph
    

2010 MSC: 05C25; 05C12
  
††
      
      
    E-mail addresses: nuoyoushi@gmail.com (Chen), xuanlma@xsyu.edu.cn (Ma)



1 Introduction




All graphs considered in this paper are finite, simple, and connected.
We always use G to denote a graph.
Denote by V(G) and E(G) the vertex set and edge set of G, respectively.
The minimum degree of a graph G, denoted by \delta(G), is the smallest integer k such that there exists a vertex with degree k in this graph.
For two vertices u and v of a graph G, the distance between
u and v is denoted by {\rm d}_{G}(u,v), or simply by {\rm d}(u,v) when no confusion can arise.
For distinct x,y\in V(G), we say a vertex v\in V(G)
distinguishes x and y whenever
{\rm d}(v,x)\neq{\rm d}(v,y).
A subset S\subseteq V(G) is called a metric generator of G if for every
pair of distinct vertices x,y\in V(G), there exists a vertex in S such that this vertex distinguishes x and y.
A metric generator of G with minimum cardinality is called
a metric basis
of G, and the cardinality of a metric basis is referred to as the metric dimension of G.




In the 1970s,
the concept of metric dimension was introduced independently by Harary and Melter [3], and Slater [10].
Their aim is to distinguish vertices of a graph using distances.
The metric dimension of a graph has a wide range of applications, including robot navigation in networks, chemistry, pattern recognition, and image processing, and so on. See the survey [11] for more results and applications of the metric dimension.
In 2018,
as a further variation of the classical notion of a metric generator, Kelenc et al. [7] introduced
the concept of an edge metric generator, which is
to distinguish the edges of a graph by means of distances from a prescribed set of vertices.
For a vertex v\in V(G) and an edge e=\{u,w\}\in E(G), the distance between v and e is defined by




{\rm d}(v,e)=\min\{{\rm d}(v,u),{\rm d}(v,w)\}.



A vertex x\in V(G) is said to distinguish two distinct edges e_{1},e_{2}\in E(G) if
{\rm d}(x,e_{1})\neq{\rm d}(x,e_{2}).
A set S\subseteq V(G) is called an edge metric generator for G provided that every pair of distinct edges of G is distinguished by some vertex in S.
An edge metric generator of minimum cardinality is called an edge metric basis of G, and its cardinality is called the edge metric dimension of G.




For a graph G, let




O(G)=V(G)\cup E(G).



We say a vertex v of (G) distinguishes two distinct elements x,y\in O(G) (vertices or edges) if
{\rm d}(v,x)\neq{\rm d}(v,y).
For a subset S of V(G), we say S is a
mixed metric generator of G provided that, for every pair of distinct elements x,y\in O(G), there exists a vertex in S such that this vertex distinguishes x and y.
The cardinality of a mixed metric generator with minimum size, denoted by {\rm dim_{m}}(G), is called the mixed metric dimension of G.
Kelenc et al. [6] first introduced the concept of a mixed metric dimension.




In [6],
the authors determined the mixed metric dimension of several families of graphs and established an upper bound in terms of the girth. They also proved that computing the mixed metric dimension of a general graph is NP-hard.
A leaf of G is a vertex of degree 1.
Denote by \ell(G)
the number of all leaves of G.
The cyclomatic number of G, denoted by c(G), is
the number of independent cycles of G. It is well known that c(G)=|E(G)|-|V(G)|+1.
In [9],
Sedlar and Škrekovski obtained the exact value of the mixed metric dimension of a unicyclic graph and they proposed the following conjecture:



Conjecture 1.1



([9, Conjecture 13])
Let G be a connected graph which is not a cycle.
Then {\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq\ell(G)+2c(G).





In [9], the authors showed that
Conjecture 1.1 holds for trees and 3-connected
graphs.
A Theta graph
(or \Theta-graph) is a graph with exactly two vertices of degree 3 and all others of degree 2.
Let H be a \Theta-graph with distinct vertices u,v of degree 3.
Then H is called balanced provided that the lengths of all three paths connecting u and v differ by at most 1; Otherwise, H is called unbalanced.
In [8], the authors proved that Conjecture 1.1 holds for all \Theta-graph. In 2025,
Chakraborty et al. [1] proved that Conjecture 1.1 is valid for a connected graph which is not a tree such that \ell(G)\geq 1, and a connected graph G with \ell(G)=0 and has a cut-vertex.
In [4], the authors gave some graphs satisfying Conjecture 1.1. Recently, Chen and Ma [2] showed that for a 2-connected graph G which is not a cycle, the mixed metric dimension of G is at most 2c(G).
As an immediate application, Conjecture 1.1 was proved.




If G is a cycle, then by
[6, Theorem 3.8 and Proposition 4.1] we have {\rm dim_{m}}(G)=3. As a result, {\rm dim_{m}}(G)=\ell(G)+2c(G) is not valid for a cycle. Moreover,
by [8, Corollary 8],
{\rm dim_{m}}(G)=\ell(G)+2c(G) is valid for a
balanced \Theta-graph G.
In the concluding section of [8], the authors proposed the following conjecture:



Conjecture 1.2



([8, Conjecture 12])
Let G be a graph. Then




{\rm dim_{m}}(G)=\ell(G)+2c(G)

(1)


if and only if G is either a cactus graph in which every cycle has
precisely one vertex of degree at least 3 or a
balanced \Theta-graph.





This paper will provide an affirmative answer to Conjecture 1.2.





2 Preliminaries




In order to prove Conjecture 1.2, we will establish several lemmas in this section.



Lemma 2.1



Let G be a 2-connected graph with c(G)=2. Then G is a \Theta-graph.





Proof. Note that G is not a cycle and \delta(G)\geq 2. Then we have




\sum_{v\in V(G)}(\deg(v)-2)=2|E(G)|-2|V(G)|=2c(G)-2=2.



We conclude that the degree of a vertex of G is at most 4.




Suppose, for a contradiction, that G has a vertex u with degree 4. Then all other vertices of G have degree 2. Let x,y,z,w be the four vertices adjacent to u. If we delete u in G, then any of x,y,z,w has degree 1.
It follows that the subgraph of G obtained by delete u is the union of two disjoint paths. Thus, u is a cut-vertex of G, this contradicts that G is 2-connected.




Thus, G has precisely two vertices with degree 3, and all other vertices have degree 2. This means that
G is a \Theta-graph, as desired.
\hfill\Box\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt



Lemma 2.2



Let G be a balanced \Theta-graph and let a,b be two distinct vertices of G.
Then there exists a mixed metric generator \mathcal{R} of G
such that




|\mathcal{R}|=4,~~a\in\mathcal{R},~~{\rm d}(r,a)\geq{\rm d}(r,b)\text{ for some $r\in\mathcal{R}$}.







Proof. Let x,y be the two vertices of degree 3, and let these three paths connecting x and y be




P_{i}=(v_{i,0},v_{i,1},v_{i,2},\ldots,v_{i,l_{i}}),~~x=v_{i,0},~~y=v_{i,l_{i}},



where 1\leq i\leq 3. Let q=\min\{l_{1},l_{2},l_{3}\}. Then
l_{i}\in\{q,q+1\} for any 1\leq i\leq 3.
Note that among the three paths P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}, there are at most two paths of length q+1.




Claim A.
Take u\in V(P_{k})\setminus\{x,y\} and v\in V(P_{l})\setminus\{x,y\} from two distinct paths P_{k},P_{l}, and if the path P_{t} has length q+1, then one of u,v must lie on P_{t}. Then \{x,y,u,v\} is a mixed metric generator of G.




We refer to the above u,v as marked vertices.
In the following, denote by [t,t]_{i} and [t,t+1]_{i} the vertex v_{i,t} and edge \{v_{i,t},v_{i,t+1}\} in P_{i}, respectively. Let \varepsilon\in\{0,1\}. Then for any [t,t+\varepsilon]_{i}\in O(P_{i})\setminus\{x,y\}, we have that




{\rm d}(x,[t,t+\varepsilon]_{i})=t,~~{\rm d}(y,[t,t+\varepsilon]_{i})=l_{i}-t-\varepsilon.

(2)


By (2), if P_{i} has length q, then
any pair of distinct elements of O(P_{i}) can be distinguished by one of x,y.
Suppose that P_{i} has length q+1. Let u be the marked vertex of P_{i}. Then u can distinguish not only x and [0,1]_{i}, but also y and [l_{i}-1,l_{i}]_{i}. By (2) again,
every two distinct elements of O(P_{i}) can be distinguished by one of x,y,u.




In the following, we consider two elements z,z^{\prime} lying on distinct paths. We may assume that every of x,y can not distinguish z,z^{\prime}. In view of (2), let




z=[t,t+\varepsilon]_{i},~~z^{\prime}=[t,t+\varepsilon^{\prime}]_{j},~~i\neq j,



where \varepsilon^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}. Since {\rm d}(y,z)={\rm d}(y,z^{\prime}), we have that




l_{i}-\varepsilon=l_{j}-\varepsilon^{\prime}.

(3)


By the choice of a marked vertex, at least one marked vertex comes from P_{i} or P_{j}. Without loss of generality, let u=v_{i,p}\in O(P_{i}) be a marked vertex. We deduce that




{\rm d}(u,z)=h=\min\{|p-t|,|p-t-\varepsilon|\}



and




{\rm d}(u,z^{\prime})=\min\{p+t,l_{i}-p+l_{j}-t-\varepsilon^{\prime}\}.



If p\leq t, then h=t-p, which implies that




(p+t)-h=2p>0,~~(l_{i}-p+l_{j}-t-\varepsilon^{\prime})-h=2l_{i}-2t-\varepsilon>0~(\text{by \eqref{eq-2}}).



If p\geq t+1, then h=p-t-\varepsilon, this forces that




(p+t)-h=2t+\varepsilon>0,~~(l_{i}-p+l_{j}-t-\varepsilon^{\prime})-h=2(l_{i}-p)>0~(\text{by \eqref{eq-2}}).



Combining the above two cases, we conclude that
{\rm d}(u,z^{\prime})>{\rm d}(u,z). It follows that u distinguishes z,z^{\prime}. As a result, the set of x,y and all marked vertices is a mixed metric generator of G, which proves Claim A.




Now, suppose that a=x.
Take \mathcal{R}=\{x,y,u,v\} as in Claim A.
Then for any b\in V(G)\setminus\{a\},
we infer that {\rm d}(y,a)\geq{\rm d}(y,b), which concludes the proof of this lemma. Similarly, we also conclude our proof if a=y. Thus, in the following, let a\notin\{x,y\}.




Suppose that a can serve as a marked vertex. Let u be the other marked vertex. Then by Claim A,
\{x,y,a,u\} is a mixed metric generator of G.
Now for any v\in V(G), it follows that




{\rm d}(x,v)+{\rm d}(y,v)\in\{q,q+1\}.

(4)


If both {\rm d}(x,a)<{\rm d}(x,b) and {\rm d}(y,a)<{\rm d}(y,b) hold, then




{\rm d}(x,b)+{\rm d}(y,b)\geq{\rm d}(x,a)+{\rm d}(y,a)+2,



which is impossible as (4).
We conclude that either {\rm d}(x,a)\geq{\rm d}(x,b) or {\rm d}(y,a)\geq{\rm d}(y,b), which proves our lemma.




Suppose that a cannot serve as a marked vertex. Then it must be that among the three paths P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}, there are precisely two paths of length q+1, say P_{2} and P_{3}, and
a is an internal vertex of P_{1}. For convenience, now let




P_{1}=(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{q}),~~P_{2}=(b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{q+1}),~~P_{3}=(c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{q+1}),



where x=a_{0}=b_{0}=c_{0} and y=a_{q}=b_{q+1}=c_{q+1}. Write a=a_{t} for some 1\leq t\leq q-1.




Claim B. \{x,a_{t},b_{1},c_{q}\} is a mixed metric generator of G.




For any two elements z,z^{\prime} of O(G),
if {\rm d}(x,z)\neq{\rm d}(x,z^{\prime}), then x distinguishes them.
As a result, we next consider the elements at distance

If b=x, then taking r=x, we deduce that
{\rm d}(a,r)=t\geq 0={\rm d}(b,r), as desired.
If b=y, then taking r=c_{q}, we conclude that
{\rm d}(a,r)=q-t+1\geq 1={\rm d}(b,r), as required.
In the following, we may assume that b\notin\{x,y\}.




Suppose, for a contradiction,
that for any r\in\{x,b_{1},c_{q}\}, we all have
{\rm d}(a,r)<{\rm d}(b,r). If b=a_{i} for 1\leq i\leq q-1, then for r=x and c_{q}, we have that t<i and q-t+1<q-i+1 respectively, a contradiction.
If b=b_{j} for 1\leq j\leq q, then for r=x and c_{q}, we deduce that t<j and j<t+1 respectively, which is impossible.
Finally, if b=c_{k} for 1\leq k\leq q, then for r=x and c_{q}, we infer that t<k and k<t-1 respectively, which is impossible.
\hfill\Box\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt




Denote by L(G) the set of all leaves of G.
A block of a graph is a maximal connected subgraph that has no cut-vertex.
Given a connected graph G with at least a cut-vertex, we next define the graph B(G) with vertex set \mathcal{B}\cup C, where
\mathcal{B} is the set of all blocks of G, and C is the set of all cut-vertices of G.
In fact, B(G) is bipartite with bipartition (\mathcal{B},C), and a block B\in\mathcal{B} and a cut-vertex v\in C is adjacent if and only
if v\in V(B). Clearly, B(G) is a tree, and so we call B(G) the block tree of G
(cf. [5]).




For a connected graph G with at least a cut-vertex,
let \mathcal{F} be the set of all blocks containing a cycle.
For D\in\mathcal{F}, let X_{D} be a mixed metric generator of D, and let C_{D} be the set of the cut-vertices in D.



Lemma 2.3



Let G be a connected graph with at least a cut-vertex. Then




L(G)\cup(\bigcup_{D\in\mathcal{F}}X_{D}\setminus C_{D})



is a mixed metric generator of G.





Proof. If every B\in\mathcal{B} has no cycle, then G is a tree, and so L(G) is a mixed metric generator of G, as desired.
In the following, assume that \mathcal{F}\neq\emptyset and let D\in\mathcal{F}. Now let
c be a cut-vertex belonging to D, and let E\in\mathcal{B}\setminus\{D\} with c\in E.




Claim A. Suppose that E belongs to \mathcal{F}. Then (X_{D}\cup X_{E})\setminus\{c\} is a mixed metric generator of D\cup E.




If two distinct elements of O(D) (resp. O(E)) are distinguished by c, then they must be distinguished by an element of X_{E}\setminus\{c\} (resp. X_{D}\setminus\{c\}).
Now take
z_{1}\in O(D)\setminus\{c\},z_{2}\in O(E)\setminus\{c\}.
Without loss of generality, let
{\rm d}_{D\cup E}(z_{1},c)\leq{\rm d}_{D\cup E}(z_{2},c).




Suppose, for a contradiction, that for any x\in X_{D}\setminus\{c\}, we have that




{\rm d}_{D}(x,z_{1})={\rm d}_{D}(x,c)+{\rm d}_{D}(c,z_{1}).

(6)


If {\rm d}(c,z_{1})=0, then z_{1} is an edge with an endpoint c, which implies that c and z_{1} cannot
be distinguished by any of X_{D}\cup\{c\} by (6), a contradiction.
Now let {\rm d}_{D}(c,z_{1})=r>0.
Choose a shortest path form c to v_{r} in D as follows:




(c,v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{r-1},v_{r}),



where if z_{1} is a vertex, then v_{r}=z_{1}; otherwise, v_{r} is an endpoint of z_{1}. Then




{\rm d}_{D}(x,v_{r-1})={\rm d}_{D}(x,c)+r-1={\rm d}_{D}(x,c)+{\rm d}_{D}(c,\{v_{r-1},v_{r}\})={\rm d}_{D}(x,\{v_{r-1},v_{r}\})

(7)


and




{\rm d}_{D}(c,v_{r-1})={\rm d}_{D}(c,\{v_{r-1},v_{r}\}).

(8)


Naturally, if v_{r-1}=c, then both (7) and (8) hold.
It follows that, in this case, \{v_{r-1},v_{r}\} and v_{r-1} cannot
be distinguished by any of X_{D}\cup\{c\} by (6), this contradicts that X_{D} is a mixed metric generator of D.




We conclude that there exists y\in X_{D}\setminus\{c\}, such that
{\rm d}_{D}(y,z_{1})\leq{\rm d}_{D}(x,c)+{\rm d}_{D}(c,z_{1}).
It follows that




{\rm d}_{D\cup E}(y,z_{1})<{\rm d}_{D\cup E}(y,c)+{\rm d}_{D\cup E}(c,z_{1})\leq{\rm d}_{D\cup E}(y,c)+{\rm d}_{D\cup E}(c,z_{2})={\rm d}_{D\cup E}(y,z_{2}),



which means that z_{1} and z_{2} are distinguished by y. As a result, (X_{D}\cup X_{E})\setminus\{c\} is a mixed metric generator of D\cup E, so Claim A holds.




Claim B. Suppose that E is an
edge \{c,c^{\prime}\} and c^{\prime} is a leaf. Then (X_{D}\cup\{c^{\prime}\})\setminus\{c\} is a mixed metric generator of D\cup E.




Claim B easily follows from the proof of Claim A.




Now, suppose that E is a bridge, say \{c,c_{1}\}, where c_{1} is not a leaf. Then there exists E_{1}\in\mathcal{B} such that c_{1}\in V(E_{1}). Certainly, E_{1} can also be a bridge, say \{c_{1},c_{2}\}, where c_{2} is not a leaf. Now let
every edge of this path P=(c,c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n}) is a bridge, and let E_{n}\in\mathcal{B} be either an
edge \{c_{n},c^{\prime}\} and c^{\prime} is a leaf, or a block containing c_{n} and belonging to \mathcal{F}.
If E_{n}\in\mathcal{F}, then it is similar to
Claim A, (X_{D}\cup X_{E_{n}})\setminus\{c,c_{n}\} is a mixed metric generator of D\cup E\cup P.
If not, it is similar to
Claim B, (X_{D}\cup\{c^{\prime}\})\setminus\{c\} is a mixed metric generator of D\cup E\cup P.




Finally, by the block tree of G, we can “glue” all the blocks of G together at their cut vertices. As a result, L(G)\cup(\cup_{D\in\mathcal{F}}X_{D}\setminus C_{D})
is a mixed metric generator of G, as desired.
\hfill\Box\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt




Given a graph G, let




\mathcal{S}=\{v\in V(G):\deg(v)\geq 3\}.



A thread in a graph G is a path whose internal vertices all have degree 2 in G.
Let P=(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{l}) be a thread of G.
We use G-P^{0} to denote the subgraph of G that is obtained by removing the internal vertices of this thread P and keeping the endpoints p_{0} and p_{l}. If G-P^{0} is connected and l<{\rm d}_{G-P^{0}}(p_{0},p_{l}), then
P is called a short thread of G. If \deg(p_{0})\geq 3 and \deg(p_{l})\geq 3, then this thread P is called
maximal.



Lemma 2.4



Let G be 2-connected with c(G)\geq 3. Then {\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq 2c(G)-1.





Proof. Let c(G)=n.
If every thread of G is short, it follows from [2, Lemmas 2.3 and 2.5] that
{\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq|\mathcal{S}|\leq 2n-2<2n-1, as desired.
In the following, we always assume that G has a maximal thread P=(u=p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{l}=v) such that l\geq{\rm d}_{H}(u,v), where H=G-P^{0}. Then clearly, H is connected and has no leaves. Thus, we have that




c(H)=|E(H)|-|V(H)|+1=(|E(G)|-l)-(|V(G)|-(l-1))+1,



namely,




n=c(G)=c(H)+1.

(9)


To end our proof, we now proceed by induction on n.




Suppose first that n=3. Then c(H)=2
by (9).
Assume that H is a 2-connected graph. Then Lemma 2.1 implies that H is a \Theta-graph.
If H is an unbalanced \Theta-graph, then
in view of [8, Theorem 10], we obtain that
{\rm dim_{m}}(H)=3, which implies that
{\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq{\rm dim_{m}}(H)+2\leq 2n-1 by [2, Lemma 2.4], as required.
Next, assume that H is a balanced \Theta-graph.
Then, it follows from Lemma 2.2 that
there exists a mixed metric generator \mathcal{R} of H
such that




|\mathcal{R}|=4,~~u\in\mathcal{R},~~{\rm d}_{H}(r,u)\geq{\rm d}_{H}(r,v)\text{ for some $r\in\mathcal{R}$}.



Note that {\rm dim_{m}}(H)=4 by [8, Theorem 7].
Thus, \mathcal{R} is a mixed metric generator of H with the smallest cardinality. Now let
w=p_{\lceil l/2\rceil}. In view of [2, Lemma 2.4], it is obvious that
\mathcal{R}\cup\{u,w\}=\mathcal{R}\cup\{w\} is a mixed metric generator of G. It follows that
{\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq 2n-1, as desired.




Assume that H is not 2-connected.
Note that H has no leaves and contains at least a cut-vertex. It follows from c(H)=2 that H is a
cactus graph with exactly two cycles. Now let C_{u} and C_{v} be two distinct cycles with u\in C_{u} and v\in C_{v}, and let a_{u} and a_{v} be cut-vertices with a_{u}\in C_{u} and a_{v}\in C_{v}. Clearly, u\neq a_{u} and v\neq a_{v}, but a_{u} and a_{v}
are not necessarily distinct. For C_{u},
taking a thread from u to a_{u} satisfying the hypotheses
of [2, Lemma 2.4], we deduce that there exists x\in V(C_{u})\setminus\{u,a_{u}\} such that \{u,a_{u},x\} is a mixed metric generator of C_{u}. Similarly, we may assume that \{v,a_{v},y\} is a mixed metric generator of C_{v}.
It follows from Lemma 2.3 that
\{u,v,x,y\} is a mixed metric generator of H.
Write, now, w=p_{\lceil l/2\rceil}.
By [2, Lemma 2.4] again, we conclude that
\{u,v,x,y\}\cup\{w\} is a mixed metric generator of G.
As a consequence, {\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq 2n-1, as desired.




Now, assume that the statement holds for
every 2-connected graph whose cyclomatic number is at least 4 and less than n.
We then prove this result holds for n.




Case 1. H is 2-connected.




If c(H)=2, then c(G)=3 by (9), which implies that the required result holds by the above proof.
Suppose nest that c(H)\geq 3.
Note that c(H)<n.
Then by the induction hypothesis, we deduce that
{\rm dim_{m}}(H)\leq 2c(H)-1. It follows from [2, Lemma 2.4] that
{\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq{\rm dim_{m}}(H)+2, which implies that




{\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq{\rm dim_{m}}(H)+2\leq 2c(H)-1+2=2c(H)+1=2n-1



by (9), as desired.




Case 2. H is not 2-connected.




Then H has at least a cut-vertex.
As a result, H has at least two blocks containing a cycle.
Let D be a block of H that contains a cycle.
If D is a cycle, then [9, Lemma 4] implies that we can find a mixed metric generator X_{D} containing a cut-vertex.
Suppose that c(D)=2. Then D is a \Theta-graph by Lemma 2.1. If D is unbalanced, then [8, Theorem 10] manes that {\rm dim_{m}}(D)=2c(D)-1;
Otherwise, by Lemma 2.2 we may choose
a mixed metric generator X_{D} containing a cut-vertex.
If c(D)\geq 3, since D is 2-connected and c(D)<n,
we have {\rm dim_{m}}(D)\leq 2c(D)-1 by the induction hypothesis, which implies that we can take a mixed metric generator X_{D} of D that has size 2c(D)-1.




Case 2.1. H has a block D^{\prime} that contains a cycle but is not itself a cycle.




In view of Lemma 2.3, we have that




\displaystyle{\rm dim_{m}}(H)
\displaystyle\leq\Big|\bigcup_{D\in\mathcal{F}}(X_{D}\setminus C_{D})\Big|

(10)



\displaystyle\leq\Big|\bigcup_{D\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\{D^{\prime}\}}(X_{D}\setminus C_{D})\Big|+\big|X_{D^{\prime}}\setminus C_{D^{\prime}}\big|




\displaystyle\leq\sum_{D\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\{D^{\prime}\}}2c(D)+2c(D^{\prime})-1




\displaystyle=2c(H)-1.



Now, from [2, Lemma 2.4], it follows that




{\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq{\rm dim_{m}}(H)+2\leq 2c(H)+1=2c(G)-1,



as desired.




Case 2.2. Every D\in\mathcal{F} is a cycle.




In this case we have that H is a cactus graph.
By [9, Corollary 10], we conclude that {\rm dim_{m}}(H)\leq 2c(H) and the equality is attained
if and only if every cycle in H has exactly one root vertex.
If {\rm dim_{m}}(H)\leq 2c(H)-1, then the desired result follows
from [2, Lemma 2.4]. Suppose now that {\rm dim_{m}}(H)=2c(H). Since G is 2-connected, we deduce that H is a
cactus graph with exactly two cycles. As a result, we have that c(H)=2, which implies that c(G)=3, and the required result follows.
\hfill\Box\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt





3 An affirmative answer to Conjecture 1.2




We are now ready to prove Conjecture 1.2.




Proof of Conjecture 1.2.
By [8, Corollary 8],
(1) holds for a
balanced \Theta-graph G.
Moreover, it follows from [9, Theorem 9] that
(1) holds for a cactus graph in which every cycle has precisely one vertex of degree at least 3.
Thus, the sufficiency follows.




We next prove the necessity.
Suppose that (1) holds for graph G.
If c(G)=0, then G is a tree, which implies that G is a cactus graph in which every cycle has precisely one vertex of degree at least 3 (note that a tree has no cycles), as desired. In the following, we always assume that c(G)\geq 1.




Case 1. G has no cut-vertices.




If c(G)=1, then G is a cycle, which implies that
(1) is not valid by
[6, Theorem 3.8 and Proposition 4.1].
Suppose that c(G)=2. Then by Lemma 2.1, G is a \Theta-graph. Furthermore, if G is an unbalanced \Theta-graph, then by [8, Theorem 10] we have {\rm dim_{m}}(G)=3, which implies (1) does not hold.
Otherwise, G is a balanced \Theta-graph, as desired.




Now, suppose that c(G)>2. Then Lemma 2.4 means that




{\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq 2c(G)-1<2c(G)\leq\ell(G)+2c(G),



which is impossible.




Case 2. G has at least a cut-vertex.




If G has a block that contains a cycle but is not itself a cycle, then by Lemma 2.3, it is similar to (10), we deduce that {\rm dim_{m}}(G)\leq 2c(G)-1, which is impossible. It follows that every block of G that contains a cycle is itself a cycle. As a result, G is a cactus graph.
Moreover, by [9, Theorem 9], we see that G is a cactus graph in which every cycle has precisely one vertex of degree at least 3, as desired.
\hfill\Box\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt
