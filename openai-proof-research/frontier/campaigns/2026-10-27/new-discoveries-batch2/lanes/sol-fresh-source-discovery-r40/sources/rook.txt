                                                                                         Abstract
                                                      We show by an explicit example that the Garsia–Remmel q-rook numbers of Ferrers
                                                   boards do not all have unimodal sequences of coefficients. This resolves in the negative
                                                   a question from 1986 by the aforementioned authors.

                                                      keywords: q-rook numbers, rook placements, Ferrers boards, unimodality,
                                                                       counterexamples, q-Stirling numbers


                                                The central objects in rook theory are the placements of (nonattacking) rooks on a
                                           board. Formally, a board B is a finite subset of Z>0 × Z>0 , and a rook placement on B is a
                                           subset w of B such that no two elements of w agree on either coordinate. The terminology
                                           comes from the natural visual representation of these objects, as in Figure 1. The rook
                                           number Rr (B) is defined as the number of placements of r rooks on the board B.
                                                A case of particular interest (beginning in the first systematic study [KR46] of the field)
                                           is when B = Bλ is a Ferrers board associated to an integer partition λ: that is, λ =
                                           ⟨λ1 , . . . , λℓ ⟩ is a finite nonincreasing sequence of positive integers and Bλ := {(i, j) : 1 ≤
                                           i ≤ ℓ, 1 ≤ j ≤ λi }. For example, in the case of the staircase partition δn := ⟨n − 1, n −
                                           2, . . . , 2, 1⟩, one has that
                                                                                     Rk (Bδn ) = S(n, n − k)
                                           is a Stirling number of the second kind [KR46, §5]. In general, two partitions λ, µ are said to
                                           be rook equivalent if Rk (Bλ ) = Rk (Bµ ) for all k [GJW75]. This condition can be phrased

   In [GR86], Garsia and Remmel introduced a q-analogue of the rook number. This ana-
logue is in terms of the inversion number, defined as follows: given a rook placement w
on a Ferrers board Bλ , for each rook (a, b) ∈ w, delete from Bλ all elements (i, b) with i ≤ a
and (a, j) with j ≤ b; the inversion number inv(w) is the number of undeleted cells. Then
the (Garsia–Remmel) q-rook number of rank k is

                               Rk (λ; q) :=              q inv(w) .
                                                w a placement of

By definition, Rk (λ; q) is a polynomial in the formal variable q with nonnegative integer
coefficients such that Rk (λ; 1) = Rk (Bλ ). For example, the rank-3 q-rook number of the
partition ⟨6, 5, 3, 3⟩ is q 11 + 4q 10 + 10q 9 + 19q 8 + 27q 7 + 30q 6 + 25q 5 + 15q 4 + 6q 3 + q 2 , and
the rook placement in Figure 1 is one of the 25 that contribute to the coefficient of q 5 .
    Analogously to the case q = 1, one says two partitions are q-rook equivalent if they
have the same q-rook numbers. Surprisingly, two partitions are rook equivalent if and only
if they are q-rook equivalent [GR86, p. 257]. In the particular case λ = δn , the polynomial
Rk (λ; q) is a q-Stirling number of the second kind [GR86, Eq. I.9]; for more on these numbers,
see [Mil82; WW91] and Remarks 8 and 10 below.
    The same polynomials Rk (λ; q) also arise in enumerative linear algebra, when counting
matrices of rank k over a finite field whose nonzero entries all belong to Bλ [Hag98, Thm. 1];
this connection has led to applications in coding theory, since up to a power of q, the Rk (λ; q)
give the weights of certain rank-metric codes (e.g., see [Rav18; GR20; GR24]). The q-rook
numbers and the related q-hit numbers have also recently appeared in the expansions of
chromatic symmetric functions of graphs corresponding to certain Dyck paths called abelian
[AN21; CMP23; NT21; HOY24].
    Based on an assortment of empirical and circumstantial evidence, Garsia and Remmel
conjectured [GR86, p. 250] that the polynomials Rk (λ; q) always have unimodal sequence
of coefficients, i.e., that if

                          Rk (λ; q) = aN q N + aN −1 q N −1 + . . . + a1 q + a0

then for some m it holds that aN ≤ aN −1 ≤ · · · ≤ am+1 ≤ am ≥ am−1 ≥ · · · ≥ a1 ≥ a0 . As
we now show, it is not difficult to prove the Garsia–Remmel unimodality conjecture for the
cases of the maximum and minimum nontrivial numbers of rooks.
Proposition 1. For every partition λ = ⟨λ1 , . . . , λℓ ⟩, Rℓ (λ; q) is unimodal.
Proof. In the case of ℓ rooks, there must be one rook in each row. Consider iteratively
placing the rooks beginning with the last row. Regardless of how one has placed rooks in
rows k + 1, . . . , ℓ, there will be λk − (ℓ − k) legal positions to place a rook in row k, and the
different choices will leave 0, 1, . . . , λk − (ℓ − k) − 1 inversions in row k. Consequently

                         Rℓ (λ; q) = [λℓ ]q · [λℓ−1 − 1]q · · · [λ1 − (ℓ − 1)]q                (2)

where [n]q := 1 + q + q 2 + . . . + q n−1 . Thus Rℓ (λ; q) is a product of log-concave polynomials
(i.e., polynomials for which the coefficient sequence satisfies a2i ≥ ai−1 ai+1 ) with nonnegative
coefficients and no internal zeros. The family of such polynomials is closed under products
[Sta89, Prop. 2], and furthermore every such polynomial is unimodal [Sta89, p. 500].
Proposition 2. For every partition λ, R1 (λ; q) is unimodal.
Proof. It is easy to see [CGR23, proof of Cor. 3.2] that

because, taking m to be the maximum index for which dm = m and fixing a choice of a box
(a, m + 2 − a) that does not belong to the (m + 1)st diagonal of Bλ , we have that the map

    Progress on the general conjecture has been limited. One reason for this is that, although
q-rook numbers of maximum rank are always log-concave (per the proof of Proposition 1),
this is not the case for lower-rank q-rook numbers, as shown in the next example. Thus, the
assorted machinery that has been built up (especially in the past decade [AHK18; BH20;
AOV21; CP24]) to prove log-concavity results is not directly useful here.
Example 3. One has (either from (3) or by direct examination) that

                                R1 (⟨4, 1⟩; q) = q 4 + 2q 3 + q 2 + q,

which is not log-concave (since a22 = 1 < 2 = a1 · a3 ).
   A second reason that progress on the general conjecture has been limited is that the
conjecture is false.

Proposition 4. The q-rook number

                                             R2 (⟨10, 9, 3, 2, 1⟩; q)

is not unimodal.
Proof. By considering whether or not the cell (ℓ, λℓ ) has a rook, it is easy to see that the
q-rook numbers satisfy the recurrence relation

  Rk (⟨λ1 , . . . , λℓ−1 , λℓ ⟩; q) = q · Rk (⟨λ1 , . . . , λℓ−1 , λℓ − 1⟩; q) +
                                                                           Rk−1 (⟨λ1 − 1, . . . , λℓ−1 − 1⟩; q). (4)

(This is a special case of Dworkin’s “deletion-contraction” recurrence for q-rook numbers
[Dwo98, Thm. 6.10].) Repeated applications of (4) yield

              R2 (⟨10, 9, 3, 2, 1⟩; q) = q · R2 (⟨10, 9, 3, 2⟩; q) + R1 (⟨9, 8, 2, 1⟩; q),
                R2 (⟨10, 9, 3, 2⟩; q) = q 2 · R2 (⟨10, 9, 3⟩; q) + (1 + q) · R1 (⟨9, 8, 2⟩; q),

and

                    R2 (⟨10, 9, 3⟩; q) = q 3 · R2 (⟨10, 9⟩; q) + (1 + q + q 2 ) · R1 (⟨9, 8⟩; q).

By (2), we have R2 (⟨10, 9⟩; q) = ([9]q )2 , while the various instances of R1 can be computed
from (3). Plugging these values in and simplifying produces

  R2 (⟨10, 9, 3, 2, 1⟩; q) = q 22 + 3q 21 + 7q 20 + 13q 19 + 18q 18 + 21q 17 +
                                         22q 16 + 20q 15 + 21q 14 +
                                              20q 13 + 17q 12 + 12q 11 + 5q 10 + 4q 9 + 3q 8 + 2q 7 + q 6 ,

which is manifestly non-unimodal.
Remark 5. The example in Proposition 4 is the smallest partition that has a non-unimodal
q-rook number: we checked by exhaustive computation (carried out in Sage [The24], imple-
mented with the simple recursion (4), with base case R0 (λ; q) = q |λ| ) that if |λ| ≤ 24, then
the q-rook number Rk (λ; q) is unimodal for all k. The only other partitions of size 25 with a
non-unimodal q-rook number are rook equivalent to ⟨10, 9, 3, 2, 1⟩. For larger n, the number
of partitions of n with non-unimodal q-rook number in rank 2 grows steadily; for example,
there are 67 rook equivalence classes of partitions of size 40 with R2 non-unimodal (out of
1113 total classes).

    It seems reasonable to conjecture that for each integer k > 1, there is some partition λ
for which Rk (λ; q) is not unimodal. Typically, it seems that when Rk (λ; q) is not unimodal,
there is a gap between k and the number of parts ℓ(λ) of λ. For example, every partition
λ with less than 100 boxes for which R2 (λ; q) is non-unimodal has ℓ(λ) ≥ 5. The smallest
example with a gap of size 2 is for the non-unimodal polynomial R3 (⟨24, 15, 14, 3, 2⟩; q). Is
Rℓ−1 (λ; q) always unimodal?

Remark 8. The q-Stirling numbers Sq (n, k) of the second kind, defined by Sq (n, k) :=
Rn−k (δn ; q), or alternatively by the recurrence

 Sq (n, k) = q k−1 Sq (n − 1, k − 1) + [k]q Sq (n − 1, k) for 0 < k ≤ n,                       Sq (0, k) = δ0,k , (5)

were further conjectured to be log-concave by Wachs–White [WW91, p. 45]. This conjecture
was discussed more recently in work of Sagan and Swanson [SS24, Conj. 7.4], who verified it
for 1 ≤ k ≤ n ≤ 50. We have checked it for 1 ≤ k ≤ n ≤ 250. See [SS24, §1.2] for a history
of these polynomials.
    It follows easily from the recurrence (5) that these q-Stirling numbers can be obtained
from the following evaluation of a complete symmetric polynomial [Gou61]:
                                                   k
                                  Sq (n, k) = q (2) hn−k ([1]q , [2]q , . . . , [k]q ).

Such complete symmetric functions are instances of Schur functions: hn−k (x1 , . . . , xk ) =
sn−k (x1 , . . . , xk ). Could the evaluation above be useful to show log-concavity or unimodality
of Sq (n, k), as in the principal specialization [Sta99, §7.8] of Schur functions [Sta89, Thm. 13],
or by exploiting the denormalized Lorentzian property of Schur polynomials [Huh+22]?

Remark 9. For all partitions λ of size |λ| ≤ 75 and all k, the rank-k q-rook number Rk (Bλ ; q)
is log-concave whenever R1 (Bλ ; q) is log-concave (i.e., if the sequence of diagonal lengths di (λ)
are log-concave). Could this be true for all λ? Note that R1 (δn ; q) is log-concave, so this is
a strengthening of the Wachs–White conjecture mentioned in the previous remark.

Remark 10. In [Sag92, Cor. 2.6], Sagan showed that the q-Stirling numbers of the second
kind satisfy the following alternative notion of log-concavity:1

                                Sq (n, k)2 − Sq (n, k − 1)Sq (n, k + 1) ∈ N[q].

Based on calculations for partitions λ such that |λ| ≤ 50, we conjecture that the q-rook
numbers Rk (λ; q) satisfy this notion of log-concavity, i.e., that

                                  Rk (λ; q)2 − Rk−1 (λ; q)Rk+1 (λ; q) ∈ N[q]

for all k and λ. At q = 1, this conjecture is a consequence of [Nij76, Thm. 1]; see [HOW99]
for more about log-concavity for the q = 1 case.
