One of them turns out to be secretly linear , and we can compute its periods exactly. One is a permutation hiding a small, perfectly orderly piece inside a large, random-looking one. The third looks thoroughly random, but it still has a few provable features. Along the way we meet open problems that need nothing beyond a first course in abstract algebra to state. In particular N C ​ ( k ) ≤ 2 k − 1 , with equality exactly when x k + x + 1 is primitive. Proof. Step 1: reduce to 𝔽 2 . Put y n = [ x n = 1 ] . By the rule, x n = 1 exactly when exactly one of y n − 1 , y n − k equals 1 . Hence, for n ≥ k + 1 , y n = y n − 1 + y n − k in ​ 𝔽 2 , a linear recurrence, with y 1 = ⋯ = y k − 1 = 0 and y k = 1 . Step 2: the period of y . Let v n = ( y n − k + 1 , … , y n ) ∈ 𝔽 2 k . Then v n + 1 = A ​ v n , where A is the companion matrix of p ⁡ ( t ) = t k + t k − 1 + 1 . Since p ⁡ ( 0 ) = 1 , A is invertible, so y is purely periodic and can be implemented backwards. The vectors v k , v k + 1 , … , v 2 ​ k − 1 form a basis: they are “triangular”, because the first 1 of y moves one place left each time. If A m ​ v k = v k , then A m fixes every A i ​ v k , so A m = I . The period of y is therefore the order of the matrix A . That is the least m with p | t m − 1 , because p is the minimal polynomial of its companion matrix. Finally, p and its reversal x k + x + 1 divide exactly the same polynomials x m − 1 , so they have the same order. (This is the standard link between linear recurring sequences and orders of polynomials; see [ 6 , Ch. 8] .) The orbit of the nonzero vector v k never reaches 0 , so the period is at most 2 k − 1 . Since T is a bijection, states fixed by T 4 ​ k correspond to two-sided sequences ( x n ) n ∈ ℤ of period dividing 4 ​ k satisfying x n = x n − k + b n − 1 , where b n = [ x n ≠ 0 ] . Arrange one period in a 4 × k array, with x j + t ​ k in row t ∈ ℤ / 4 and column j ∈ { 0 , … , k − 1 } . Going down a column, the four increments x j + t ​ k − x j + ( t − 1 ) ​ k = b j + t ​ k − 1 are the b -values of the previous column (for j = 0 , the last column shifted by one row). They add up to 0 mod 3 . Each column has 1 or 4 zeros. The number of nonzero entries in a column is 0 or 3 , because it is a multiple of 3 and at most 4 . If some column is all zeros, the next column has all increments 0 , so it is constant. It must then also be all zeros, since a constant nonzero column has no zeros at all. Repeating this around the array gives x ≡ 0 , the fixed point. Where the single zeros sit. Suppose the zero of one column is in row u . The next column then reads a , a , a + 1 , a + 2 in rows u − 1 , u , u + 1 , u + 2 . The value a = 0 would give two zeros, a = 1 puts the zero in row u + 2 , and a = 2 puts it in row u + 1 . In terms of positions mod 4 ​ k , consecutive zeros (one per column) differ by k + 1 or 2 ​ k + 1 , and the wrap-around from the last column to the first behaves the same way. Throughout this section let b n = [ x n ≠ 0 ] and d n = x n − x n − 1 = b n − k for n ≥ k + 1 . Proposition 11 . For k ≥ 2 we have N A ​ ( k ) ≥ 4 . Proof. First, x is not eventually 0 . Otherwise, let M be least with x n = 0 for all n ≥ M . Then M > k , and for n ≥ M + 1 we get b n − k = d n = 0 . That means x n = 0 for all n ≥ M + 1 − k < M , contradicting the choice of M . Now take a window of one full period n = m + 1 , … , m + N , with m large. The increments d n add up to x m + N − x m = 0 mod 3 . But their sum is the number Z of nonzero terms in another full period, the one shifted by k . So 3 | Z , and Z > 0 , which gives N ≥ Z ≥ 3 . If N = 3 , then every term is nonzero, so every d n = 1 , and the window contains all three residues, including 0 . That is a contradiction. ∎ Proposition 12 . (1) So once we are past index k , the sequence can only stay put or go up by 1 (mod 3 ) at each step. Everything below follows from this observation. In the main paper we saw that when k ≡ 2 or 3 ( mod 4 ) , the zeros of x can line up 4 apart forever, which gives a 4 -cycle. Here we show the opposite for the other two residue classes: for those k , zeros spaced 4 apart can only form short runs. What we mean by a run Definition 15 . Let r ≥ k + 1 and g ≥ 1 be integers. We say that x has a run of zeros spaced exactly 4 apart, with g gaps, starting at r , if 1. x r = x r + 4 = x r + 8 = ⋯ = x r + 4 ​ g = 0 , and 2. x n ≠ 0 for every other n with r < n < r + 4 ​ g . In words: the g + 1 positions r , r + 4 , … , r + 4 ​ g are zeros, there are no other zeros between r and r + 4 ​ g , and so the gap between each pair of consecutive zeros in this stretch is exactly 4 . The number g counts the gaps , not the zeros. For example, if the zeros of x near some index r ≥ k + 1 sit at r , r + 4 , r + 8 , r + 11 , then positions r , r + 4 , r + 8 form a run with g = 2 gaps. Position r + 11 is not part of it, because the gap from r + 8 to r + 11 is 3 . We ask for r ≥ k + 1 so that ( 1 ) holds for every step inside the run. (Every step n we use satisfies n > r ≥ k + 1 .) Nothing is assumed about what happens before r or after r + 4 ​ g , so the run need not be maximal. The result Theorem 16 . Suppose k ≡ 0 or k ≡ 1 ( mod 4 ) . Then every run of zeros spaced exactly 4 apart (in the sense of Definition 15 ) has at most ⌊ ( k − 1 ) / 4 ⌋ gaps, that is, g ≤ ⌊ k − 1 4 ⌋ . Concretely, g ≤ k 4 − 1 if k ≡ 0 ( mod 4 ) , and g ≤ k − 1 4 if k ≡ 1 ( mod 4 ) . The proof uses one simple lemma about what a single block of a run must look like. Lemma 17 . Inside a run as in Definition 15 , every block of five consecutive terms from one zero to the next reads x m , x m + 1 , x m + 2 , x m + 3 , x m + 4 = 0 , 1 , ∗ , 2 , 0 ( m = r , r + 4 , … , r + 4 ​ g − 4 ) , where ∗ is 1 or 2 . Consequently, for r < n ≤ r + 4 ​ g : • if n ≡ r ( mod 4 ) , then d n = 0 − 2 = 1 (the step that lands on a zero); • if n ≡ r + 1 ( mod 4 ) , then d n = 1 − 0 = 1 (the step that leaves a zero). Proof. Write the block as 0 , a , c , e , 0 with a , c , e ≠ 0 . Its four steps are a , c − a , e − c , − e , and by ( 1 ) each of them is 0 or 1 . • The first step is a ∈ { 0 , 1 } , and a ≠ 0 , so a = 1 . • The last step is − e ∈ { 0 , 1 } , so e ∈ { 0 , 2 } . Since e ≠ 0 , we get e = 2 . The middle entry c is 1 or 2 , and either value gives steps in { 0 , 1 } . The two bullet points then read off the first and last steps. ∎ Proof of Theorem 16 .
