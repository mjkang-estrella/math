Using valuation rings theory, basis extension in infinite-dimensional vector spaces and extension of field embeddings to algebraic extensions, we obtain that for every real t > 1 transcendental over ℚ , the compatible lattice order can be chosen such that 0 ≺ 1 , 0 ∨ i = t , and 1 ∧ t = 0 . A partial order ⪯ on a field L is compatible with its ring operations if translation preserves order and the product of two nonnegative elements is nonnegative. Thus x ⪯ y implies x + a ⪯ y + a , and 0 ⪯ x , 0 ⪯ y imply 0 ⪯ x ​ y . Such an order is a lattice order if every pair of elements has a least upper bound and a greatest lower bound, denoted by x ∨ y and x ∧ y . Throughout this paper, a lattice-ordered field means a field equipped with these ring-order and lattice properties. The order need not be total, and positivity of inverses is not included as an additional axiom. The familiar obstruction to ordering ℂ concerns total field orders. In a totally ordered field every nonzero square is positive, which is incompatible with i 2 = − 1 . For a partial order, however, an element may be incomparable with zero, so this argument does not exclude a compatible lattice order. Conversely, the coordinate order has joins and meets but is incompatible with multiplication: it makes i nonnegative while i 2 = − 1 is negative. These two observations identify the difficulty. One must construct enough order structure to obtain least and greatest bounds while retaining multiplicative closure of the positive cone. In their 1956 paper on lattice-ordered rings, G. Birkhoff and R. S. Pierce posed the question whether the complex field admits a lattice order compatible with its ring operations [ 2 ] . This question, usually called the Birkhoff–Pierce problem for the complex field, belongs to a broader programme concerning partial orders on familiar fields and division rings. In 1963, Fuchs formulated the related Problem 31, asking for a description of the directed orders on the complex and quaternion fields [ 5 , p. 212] . Directedness requires common upper and lower bounds, whereas a lattice order requires least upper and greatest lower bounds. Every lattice order is directed, but the converse fails; maintaining this distinction is essential when comparing the subsequent existence results. An important early development concerned the real field. Wilson constructed non-total lattice orders on subfields of ℝ and proved that ℝ admits 2 𝔠 distinct lattice orders, where 𝔠 = 2 ℵ 0 is the cardinality of the continuum [ 11 ] . Thus the usual total order does not exhaust the lattice orders compatible with real-field arithmetic. This result established considerable flexibility on the real side, but it did not supply an order on ℂ , where the presence of i prevents the use of total-order arguments. Schwartz subsequently investigated the structure of lattice-ordered fields, particularly their relation to totally ordered subfields and to algebraic extensions [ 8 ] . As recalled by Schwartz and Yang [ 7 ] , this work also gives an obstruction in the algebraic-number setting: a number field admitting a compatible lattice order must be real. The directed version of the existence problem developed further through constructions allowing negative squares. Yang proved the existence of directed rings and algebras with this feature [ 12 ] . Schwartz and Yang then established the existence of compatible directed partial orders on 𝒞 [ 9 ] . Their later characterization states that a field admits a compatible directed partial order precisely when it has characteristic zero and is either formally real or has positive transcendence degree over ℚ [ 7 , Theorem 4] . In particular, transcendence permits directed orders on fields that cannot be totally ordered. This criterion settles the directed existence question, but it does not provide joins and meets. Other work has studied partial orders on quadratic extensions and quaternion algebras while keeping a specified ordered coefficient domain fixed; see, for example, Ma [ 6 ] . More recently, Wang, Yuan, Zhang, and Zhu constructed explicit directed positive cones on ℂ using integral closures of local rings [ 10 ] . Their nonzero positive elements are closed under inversion. They proved that none of the orders in that family is a lattice order, since 0 and i have no least upper bound. Their September 2026 preprint accordingly still records the general lattice-order problem as open and conjectures a negative answer. The failure of joins for those cones is a statement about that construction, rather than a general obstruction to all compatible partial orders on ℂ . In the present paper, we give an affirmative answer to the Birkhoff–Pierce problem. We construct lattice orders on the usual complex field and prescribe specific lattice identities involving the original complex numbers 1 , i , and a chosen transcendental real parameter. More precisely, our principal result is the following. Theorem 1.1 . For every real number t > 1 transcendental over ℚ , there exists a partial order ⪯ on the usual field ℂ that is compatible with addition and multiplication and makes ℂ a lattice. Its restriction to ℚ is the usual order, and it satisfies 0 ≺ 1 , 0 ∨ i = t , and 1 ∧ t = 0 . Moreover, the construction preserves the order and the binary lattice operations on a specified countable algebraically closed subfield containing ℚ ⁡ ( t , i ) . Here x ≺ y means x ⪯ y and x ≠ y . Since the value of 0 ∨ i is determined by the order relation, distinct parameters t produce distinct compatible lattice orders. There are 𝔠 real transcendental numbers greater than 1 , so ℂ admits at least 𝔠 distinct such orders. This is a lower bound for distinct order relations on the fixed complex field; no classification up to field automorphism is asserted. Our approach separates the construction into a countable algebraic stage and a passage to the full complex field. Fix the parameter t and put F = ℚ ⁡ ( t ) . Let K be the set of elements of ℂ algebraic over F . Then K is countable and algebraically closed. Countability makes it possible to enumerate all its elements and to satisfy their spanning requirements one at a time. At this stage the objective is stronger than the construction of a directed cone: we seek a ℚ -basis B of K for which every product of basis elements is a finite nonnegative rational linear combination of basis elements. Such a basis immediately gives a lattice order. Its nonnegative coordinate cone P B defines x ≤ K y by y − x ∈ P B , and the join and meet are obtained by taking coordinatewise maxima and minima. Multiplicative compatibility is equivalent to the required nonnegativity of the multiplication coefficients on basis products. An arbitrary basis offers no reason for those coefficients to be nonnegative. The main constructive issue is therefore to enlarge a basis while imposing finitely many positivity requirements and preserving all coordinate calculations already made. We resolve this issue by a finite basis-extension lemma. Suppose an existing basis spans a subspace V , and a finite set of new targets lies in an affine hyperplane on which a rational linear functional has value 1 , while the functional vanishes on V . A suitable change of the new basis vectors, together with a common correction in the old nonnegative cone, places all targets in the enlarged nonnegative cone. The enlarged basis contains the original basis, and the new cone intersects V in exactly the old cone. This finite-dimensional calculation is the mechanism that preserves earlier stages. Its affine normalization also explains the role of valuation theory in the argument. On F , the degree of a nonzero rational function is deg ⁡ p − deg ⁡ q for p ⁡ ( t ) q ⁡ ( t ) with nonzero p , q ∈ ℚ ⁡ [ x ] . The negative of this function is the valuation at infinity. Extending it to the algebraic field K gives a rational-valued degree function δ , with δ ⁡ ( t ) = 1 , δ ⁡ ( q ) = 0 for q ∈ ℚ × , δ ⁡ ( x ​ y ) = δ ⁡ ( x ) + δ ⁡ ( y ) , and δ ⁡ ( x + y ) ≤ max ⁡ { δ ⁡ ( x ) , δ ⁡ ( y ) } . Standard valuation-extension results and the torsion property of value-group extensions in algebraic field extensions explain why the values can be normalized in 𝒬 ; see Engler and Prestel [ 3 ] . We also give the requisite construction through a dominating valuation ring. Write 𝒪 = { x ∈ K : δ ⁡ ( x ) ≤ 0 } and m = { x ∈ K : δ ⁡ ( x ) < 0 } , with δ ⁡ ( 0 ) = − ∞ . The residue field k = 𝒪 / m contains a canonical copy of ℚ . For each nonnegative integer n , the subspace K n = { x ∈ K : δ ⁡ ( x ) ≤ n } carries a linear map ℓ n ​ ( x ) = res ⁡ ( x / t n ) into k . This map vanishes exactly on the elements of degree less than n and satisfies ℓ n ​ ( t n ) = 1 . Furthermore, the maps respect multiplication across the filtration: ℓ r + s ​ ( x ​ y ) = ℓ r ​ ( x ) ​ ℓ s ​ ( y ) when x ∈ K r and y ∈ K s . These properties allow products and spanning targets to be normalized by the same functional. The inductive construction produces finite layers B n , starting with B 0 = { 1 } and B 1 = { t , t − i } , such that δ ⁡ ( b ) = n and ℓ n ​ ( b ) = 1 for b ∈ B n . At stage n , we impose positivity on t n and on the products b ​ c with b ∈ B r , c ∈ B s , r , s ≥ 1 , and r + s = n . Both factors then belong to earlier stages, so these product requirements introduce no circular dependence. To ensure that the final basis spans K , choose an enumeration K = { x 1 , x 2 , … } and strictly increasing positive integers N j > δ ⁡ ( x j ) . At stage N j , include the additional target t N j − x j . Once both this target and t N j belong to the constructed span, so does x j . Every target at stage n has ℓ n -value 1 , while every element of the old span has ℓ n -value zero. Although k need not equal ℚ , the restriction of ℓ n to the space generated by the old span and these targets takes values in the canonical copy of ℚ . The finite basis-extension lemma therefore applies over precisely the required scalar field. At the limit, every element of K has entered the span and every product of basis elements has entered the nonnegative cone. The union B = ⋃ n ≥ 0 B n is consequently a positive basis with the desired multiplication property. The initial choice of B 1 yields i = t − ( t − i ) , and the coordinate lattice formulas give 0 ∨ K i = t and 1 ∧ K t = 0 . It remains to obtain an order on all of ℂ . Rather than carry out the same finite scheduling directly through an uncountable construction, we pass to the ultrapower E = K ℕ / 𝒰 for a nonprincipal ultrafilter 𝒰 on ℕ . This step preserves the field operations, the order relation, and the binary lattice operations simultaneously. The usual preservation principle for ultraproducts provides the general model-theoretic background [ 13 ] ; here the required properties are verified directly on representatives. In particular, E is algebraically closed of characteristic zero, and the diagonal embedding j : K ⟶ E preserves and reflects order and preserves joins and meets. The cardinality calculation is equally important. Countability of K gives | E | ≤ 𝔠 , and a coding of binary sequences by their finite initial segments gives the reverse inequality. Thus | E | = 𝔠 . Since K is countable, both E and ℂ have transcendence degree 𝔠 over their respective copies of K . Matching transcendence bases gives an isomorphism of the corresponding rational function fields. An embedding-extension argument for algebraic extensions then produces a field embedding Φ : E ⟶ ℂ satisfying Φ ⁡ ( j ⁡ ( a ) ) = a for every a ∈ K . Its image is algebraically closed, and ℂ is algebraic over that image, so Φ is surjective. Finally, transport the lattice order of E through Φ . Because Φ is a field isomorphism, the transported order is compatible with the original addition and multiplication on 𝒞 . Because Φ fixes the diagonal copy of K , the prescribed identities for the actual numbers t and i survive the transport. Fixing K is therefore essential: an unspecified abstract isomorphism would not by itself ensure those identities for the chosen complex numbers. The proof combines valuation rings, finite extensions of bases in infinite-dimensional rational vector spaces, nonprincipal ultrafilters, transcendence bases, and extension of field embeddings. The scope of the resulting order is reflected in its prescribed identities. Since t > 1 in the ordinary real order but 1 ∧ t = 0 in our lattice, the order does not extend the usual order on all of ℝ . It does extend the usual rational order. Also, t is positive whereas neither t − 1 nor − t − 1 is nonnegative. On the countable field this follows from the fact that every nonzero element of P B has nonnegative integral degree, while δ ⁡ ( t − 1 ) = − 1 , and it remains true after transport. Thus closure under inversion is absent, distinguishing the construction from the inversion-closed directed cones discussed above. The argument is algebraic and uses choice through maximality arguments and the ultrafilter construction. The paper develops these ingredients in the order required by the proof. We first establish the finite positive basis-extension principle and the valuation and residue calculations. We then construct the countable positive basis and describe its coordinate lattice order, including the initial layers and the degree restrictions on positive elements. The ultrapower construction and the field isomorphism fixing K provide the passage to ℂ . The final results give the main theorem and explicit formulas for comparisons, joins, and meets in terms of representatives and rational basis coordinates. 2 Preliminaries We collect the terminology and algebraic tools used in the construction. All rings are commutative with identity, all subrings contain the ambient identity, and all field embeddings preserve it. We write ℕ 0 = { 0 , 1 , 2 , … } and 𝔠 = 2 ℵ 0 . 2.1 Positive cones and compatible lattice orders A positive cone in a field L is a subset P such that 0 ∈ P , P + P ⊆ P , P ​ P ⊆ P , and P ∩ ( − P ) = { 0 } . It defines a partial order by x ≤ y if and only if y − x ∈ P . This order is translation invariant, and products of nonnegative elements are nonnegative. We call it a compatible lattice order if every pair has a least upper bound x ∨ y and a greatest lower bound x ∧ y . A directed order requires only common upper and lower bounds. Lattice orders are directed; they need not be total, and positivity of inverses is not assumed. These conventions concern lattice-ordered rings whose underlying rings are fields [ 5 ] . 2.2 Rational bases and finite positive extensions A ℚ -basis B of a vector space V is a Hamel basis: every x ∈ V has a unique expansion x = ∑ b ∈ B x b ​ b , with x b ∈ ℚ and finite support. Its coordinate cone is P B = { ∑ b ∈ B q b b : q b ∈ ℚ ≥ 0 , finite support } . Then x ≤ B y is equivalent to x b ≤ y b for every b ∈ B . The lattice operations are x ∨ B y = ∑ b max { x b , y b } b and x ∧ B y = ∑ b min { x b , y b } b . Both sums have support contained in the union of the supports of x and y . If 1 ∈ B , the order on ℚ is the usual one. When V is a field, the condition b ​ c ∈ P B for all b , c ∈ B guarantees P B ​ P B ⊆ P B by distributivity. The finite extension lemma proved in our construction asserts the following. If B is a basis of V , A is a nonempty finite subset of a larger rational vector space, W = V + span ℚ ⁡ A , and a rational linear map λ : W → ℚ satisfies λ ⁡ ( V ) = 0 and λ ⁡ ( a ) = 1 for a ∈ A , then a finite set B ′ can be chosen so that B ∪ B ′ is a basis of W , λ ⁡ ( b ′ ) = 1 for b ′ ∈ B ′ , A ⊆ P B ∪ B ′ , and P B ∪ B ′ ∩ V = P B . Thus finitely many positivity requirements can be imposed without altering the old coordinates. 2.3 Local rings, valuation rings, and residue maps A local ring ( R , 𝔪 R ) has a unique maximal ideal; equivalently, its nonunits form the ideal 𝔪 R . A module M is finitely generated if M = R ​ u 1 + ⋯ + R ​ u r for finitely many elements u j . Nakayama’s lemma states that 𝔪 R ​ M = M then implies M = 0 . A local subring ( S , 𝔫 ) of a field dominates ( R , 𝔪 R ) if R ⊆ S and 𝔫 ∩ R = 𝔪 R . Every local subring of a field is dominated by a valuation ring in that field. A valuation ring 𝒪 of L is characterized by the property that x ∈ 𝒪 or x − 1 ∈ 𝒪 for every x ∈ L × . A rational-valued valuation ν : L × → ℚ is additive on products and satisfies ν ⁡ ( x + y ) ≥ min ⁡ { ν ⁡ ( x ) , ν ⁡ ( y ) } when x + y ≠ 0 ; set ν ⁡ ( 0 ) = + ∞ . For the present application, fix a real transcendental t > 1 , put F = ℚ ⁡ ( t ) , and let K ⊆ ℂ consist of the elements algebraic over F . The field K is countable and algebraically closed. The valuation at infinity on F , given by ν 0 ​ ( p ⁡ ( t ) / q ⁡ ( t ) ) = deg ⁡ q − deg ⁡ p for nonzero p , q ∈ ℚ ⁡ [ X ] , extends to a ℚ -valued valuation ν on K [ 3 ] . Set δ = − ν and δ ⁡ ( 0 ) = − ∞ . Then δ ⁡ ( x ​ y ) = δ ⁡ ( x ) + δ ⁡ ( y ) for nonzero x , y , and δ ⁡ ( x + y ) ≤ max ⁡ { δ ⁡ ( x ) , δ ⁡ ( y ) } . Moreover, δ ⁡ ( t ) = 1 and δ ⁡ ( q ) = 0 for q ∈ ℚ × . The associated valuation ring and maximal ideal are 𝒪 = { x : δ ⁡ ( x ) ≤ 0 } and 𝔪 = { x : δ ⁡ ( x ) < 0 } . Write k = 𝒪 / 𝔪 and res : 𝒪 → k for the residue map. Its restriction to ℚ is injective. For n ∈ ℕ 0 , put D n = { x ∈ K : δ ⁡ ( x ) ≤ n } and ℓ n ​ ( x ) = res ⁡ ( x / t n ) . This is a well-defined ℚ -linear map on D n , with ℓ n ​ ( x ) = 0 exactly when δ ⁡ ( x ) < n , and ℓ n ​ ( t n ) = 1 . It also satisfies ℓ r + s ​ ( x ​ y ) = ℓ r ​ ( x ) ​ ℓ s ​ ( y ) for x ∈ D r , y ∈ D s . These identities supply the normalized functionals for the finite basis extensions. 2.4 Nonprincipal ultrafilters and ultrapowers An ultrafilter 𝒰 on ℕ is a proper filter such that, for every A ⊆ ℕ , exactly one of A and ℕ ∖ A belongs to 𝒰 . Here a proper filter contains ℕ , excludes ∅ , and is closed under finite intersections and passage to supersets. It is nonprincipal if it contains no finite set. Such ultrafilters exist by extending the cofinite filter using Zorn’s lemma. Define ( x n ) ∼ ( y n ) when { n : x n = y n } ∈ 𝒰 , and write E = K ℕ / 𝒰 for the resulting ultrapower. Addition and multiplication descend coordinatewise, making E a field. If K is algebraically closed, so is E : roots of coordinate polynomials give a root in the quotient. A compatible lattice order on K induces one on E by [ ( x n ) ] ≤ E [ ( y n ) ] if { n : x n ≤ K y n } ∈ 𝒰 . Joins and meets are computed coordinatewise. The diagonal map j ⁡ ( a ) = [ ( a , a , … ) ] is a field embedding preserving and reflecting order and preserving the lattice operations. These are instances of the ultraproduct preservation principle [ 13 ] . For countably infinite K and nonprincipal 𝒰 on ℕ , one has | E | = 𝔠 . 2.5 Transcendence bases and extension of field embeddings A set T ⊆ L is algebraically independent over a subfield k 0 if no nonzero polynomial over k 0 in finitely many distinct elements of T vanishes. A maximal such set is a transcendence basis ; then L / k 0 ​ ( T ) is algebraic, and | T | = trdeg k 0 ⁡ L . If k 0 is countable and L is uncountable, then | L | = trdeg k 0 ⁡ L . Indeed, rational functions involve finitely many variables, and algebraic extensions add at most finitely many roots for each polynomial. We also use the embedding-extension theorem: if L / D is algebraic, Ω is algebraically closed, and σ : D ↪ Ω is a field embedding, then σ extends to an embedding L ↪ Ω . It follows that algebraically closed extensions of a fixed field with equally sized transcendence bases are isomorphic over that field. Here, matching transcendence bases over K and extending the induced rational-function-field isomorphism gives Φ : E → ℂ with Φ ⁡ ( j ⁡ ( a ) ) = a for a ∈ K . Surjectivity follows because the image is algebraically closed and ℂ is algebraic over it. Transporting the order through Φ preserves both field compatibility and the prescribed lattice identities on K . 3 Conventions and finite positive basis extensions All rings are commutative and have an identity.
