<H1><A NAME="SECTION00050000000000000000">&#160;</A>
<A NAME="sec:wqo">&#160;</A>
<BR>
WQO Theory
</H1>

<P>
We now turn from Ramsey theory to another important branch of
combinatorics: WQO theory.  Like Ramsey theory, WQO theory is of
special interest from the viewpoint of reverse mathematics, because
many of the proofs seem to need unusually strong set existence axioms.

<P>
A <EM>quasiordering</EM> is a set <I>Q</I> together with a reflexive,
transitive relation <IMG
 WIDTH="18" HEIGHT="31" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img66.gif"
 ALT="$\le$">
on <I>Q</I>.  A <EM>well quasiordering</EM>
(abbreviated WQO) is a quasiordering such that for every function

<!-- MATH: $f:\mathbb{N}\to Q$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="80" HEIGHT="33" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img67.gif"
 ALT="$f:\mathbb{N}\to Q$">
there exist 
<!-- MATH: $m,n\in\mathbb{N}$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="70" HEIGHT="32" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img68.gif"
 ALT="$m,n\in\mathbb{N} $">
such that <I>m</I>&lt;<I>n</I> and 
<!-- MATH: $f(m)\le
f(n)$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="98" HEIGHT="34" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img69.gif"
 ALT="$f(m)\le
f(n)$">.
Let 
<!-- MATH: $[\mathbb{N} ]^{\infty}$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="41" HEIGHT="34" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img70.gif"
 ALT="$[\mathbb{N} ]^{\infty}$">
be the space of infinite subsets of

<!-- MATH: $\mathbb{N}$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="17" HEIGHT="18" ALIGN="BOTTOM" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img12.gif"
 ALT="$\mathbb{N} $">.
A <EM>better quasiordering</EM> (abbreviated BQO) is a
quasiordering such that for every Borel function 
<!-- MATH: $f:[\mathbb{N} ]^{\infty}\to
Q$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="103" HEIGHT="34" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img71.gif"
 ALT="$f:[\mathbb{N} ]^{\infty}\to
Q$">
there exists 
<!-- MATH: $X\in[\mathbb{N} ]^{\infty}$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="76" HEIGHT="34" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img72.gif"
 ALT="$X\in[\mathbb{N} ]^{\infty}$">
such that 
<!-- MATH: $f(X)\le
f(X\setminus\{\min(X)\})$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="191" HEIGHT="34" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img73.gif"
 ALT="$f(X)\le
f(X\setminus\{\min(X)\})$">.
It can be shown that every BQO is a WQO
but not conversely.

<P>
Generally speaking, WQO theory is an appropriate tool when considering
quasiorderings of finite structures, but BQO theory is better adapted
to infinite structures.  For example, a famous theorem of WQO theory
is <EM>Kruskal's theorem</EM>:
<BLOCKQUOTE>
Finite trees are WQO under embeddability.
</BLOCKQUOTE>(Here a <EM>tree</EM> is a connected acyclic graph, and
<EM>embeddings</EM> are required to take vertices to vertices, and edges
to paths.)  Kruskal's theorem has been generalized to infinite trees,
but the proof is much more difficult and involves BQO theory.
Detailed references are in [<A
 HREF="node10.html#sosoa">32</A>, &#167;X.3].

<P>
There are some important results about the strength of various
theorems of WQO theory.  For instance, Friedman (see Simpson
[<A
 HREF="node10.html#trees">29</A>]) showed that Kruskal's theorem is not provable in

<!-- MATH: $\mathsf{ATR}_0$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="45" HEIGHT="33" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img74.gif"
 ALT="$\mathsf{ATR}_0$">,
and he characterized exactly the strength of Kruskal's
theorem, in proof-theoretic terms.  This had remarkable consequences
for Friedman's foundational program of finding mathematically natural,
finite combinatorial statements which are proof-theoretically strong.

<P>
Consider now the following generalization of Kruskal's theorem, due to
Kriz [<A
 HREF="node10.html#kriz-gap">17</A>].  Let <I>T</I><SUB>1</SUB> and <I>T</I><SUB>2</SUB> be finite trees where each
edge is labeled with a positive integer.  Write 
<!-- MATH: $T_1\le T_2$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="62" HEIGHT="32" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img75.gif"
 ALT="$T_1\le T_2$">
to mean
that there exists an embedding of <I>T</I><SUB>1</SUB> into <I>T</I><SUB>2</SUB> such that the label
of each edge of <I>T</I><SUB>1</SUB> is less than or equal to the minimum of the
labels of the corresponding edges of <I>T</I><SUB>2</SUB>.  <EM>Kriz's theorem</EM>
says that this quasiordering is a WQO.

<P>
What is the strength of Kriz's theorem?  By results of Friedman (see
Simpson [<A
 HREF="node10.html#trees">29</A>]), Kriz's theorem is at least as strong as
<IMG
 WIDTH="25" HEIGHT="36" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img43.gif"
 ALT="$\Pi^1_1$">-
<!-- MATH: $\mathsf{CA}_0$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="35" HEIGHT="33" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img61.gif"
 ALT="$\mathsf{CA}_0$">.
It may be much stronger, but little is known.  This
is an open problem which may have a big payoff.

<P>
We now consider a famous theorem of BQO theory.  If <I>Q</I> is a countable
quasiordering, let 
<!-- MATH: $\widetilde{Q}$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="19" HEIGHT="42" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img76.gif"
 ALT="$\widetilde{Q}$">
be the set of countable transfinite
sequences of elements of <I>Q</I>.  Quasiorder 
<!-- MATH: $\widetilde{Q}$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="19" HEIGHT="42" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img76.gif"
 ALT="$\widetilde{Q}$">
by putting
<IMG
 WIDTH="41" HEIGHT="31" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img77.gif"
 ALT="$s\le t$">
if and only if there exists a one-to-one order-preserving map

<!-- MATH: $f:\mathrm{lh}(s)\to\mathrm{lh}(t)$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="122" HEIGHT="34" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img78.gif"
 ALT="$f:\mathrm{lh}(s)\to\mathrm{lh}(t)$">
such that 
<!-- MATH: $s(i)\le t(f(i))$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="101" HEIGHT="34" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img79.gif"
 ALT="$s(i)\le t(f(i))$">
for all 
<!-- MATH: $i<\mathrm{lh}(s)$ -->
<I>i</I>&lt;lh(<I>s</I>).
The <EM>Nash-Williams transfinite sequence theorem</EM>
[<A
 HREF="node10.html#bqo-fraisse">28</A>,<A
 HREF="node10.html#vems">35</A>] says that if <I>Q</I> is BQO then 
<!-- MATH: $\widetilde{Q}$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="19" HEIGHT="42" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img76.gif"
 ALT="$\widetilde{Q}$">is BQO.

<P>
Marcone [<A
 HREF="node10.html#am:nwt">23</A>] has shown that the Nash-Williams theorem is
provable in <IMG
 WIDTH="25" HEIGHT="36" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img43.gif"
 ALT="$\Pi^1_1$">-
<!-- MATH: $\mathsf{CA}_0$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="35" HEIGHT="33" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img61.gif"
 ALT="$\mathsf{CA}_0$">
but not equivalent to <IMG
 WIDTH="25" HEIGHT="36" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img43.gif"
 ALT="$\Pi^1_1$">-
<!-- MATH: $\mathsf{CA}_0$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="35" HEIGHT="33" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img61.gif"
 ALT="$\mathsf{CA}_0$">.
Shore [<A
 HREF="node10.html#shore-fraisse">26</A>] has shown that the Nash-Williams theorem
implies 
<!-- MATH: $\mathsf{ATR}_0$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="45" HEIGHT="33" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img74.gif"
 ALT="$\mathsf{ATR}_0$">
over 
<!-- MATH: $\mathsf{RCA}_0$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="45" HEIGHT="33" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img2.gif"
 ALT="$\mathsf{RCA}_0$">.
There remains the problem of closing
the gap.  We conjecture that the Nash-Williams theorem is provable in

<!-- MATH: $\mathsf{ATR}_0$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="45" HEIGHT="33" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img74.gif"
 ALT="$\mathsf{ATR}_0$">,
hence equivalent to 
<!-- MATH: $\mathsf{ATR}_0$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="45" HEIGHT="33" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img74.gif"
 ALT="$\mathsf{ATR}_0$">
over 
<!-- MATH: $\mathsf{RCA}_0$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="45" HEIGHT="33" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img2.gif"
 ALT="$\mathsf{RCA}_0$">.

<P>
Another famous theorem of BQO theory is <EM>Laver's theorem</EM>
[<A
 HREF="node10.html#bqo-fraisse">28</A>,<A
 HREF="node10.html#vems">35</A>]:
<BLOCKQUOTE>
The set of all countable linear orderings is WQO (in fact BQO) under
  embeddability.
</BLOCKQUOTE>The strength of Laver's theorem is an open problem.  Shore
[<A
 HREF="node10.html#shore-fraisse">26</A>] has shown that Laver's theorem implies 
<!-- MATH: $\mathsf{ATR}_0$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="45" HEIGHT="33" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img74.gif"
 ALT="$\mathsf{ATR}_0$">over 
<!-- MATH: $\mathsf{RCA}_0$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="45" HEIGHT="33" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img2.gif"
 ALT="$\mathsf{RCA}_0$">,
and we conjecture that Laver's theorem is provable in

<!-- MATH: $\mathsf{ATR}_0$ -->
<IMG
 WIDTH="45" HEIGHT="33" ALIGN="MIDDLE" BORDER="0"
 SRC="img74.gif"
 ALT="$\mathsf{ATR}_0$">.
