Unfortunately, controlling N (Bdp , D, ε) seems to still be a remarkably challenging task. In the case p = 2
Latala provides a covering number bound for this space which is then combined with an intricate chaining-

Lastly, let me point out some interesting related work about norms of nonhomogeneous random tensors
(tensors with very irregular randomness patterns) outside of the setting r = p = 2. Quite recently Aden-
Ali removed a logarithmic factor and a constant dependency on p in [AA25] from our bound in [BGJ+ 24]
by hammering the problem with the so-called PAC-Bayesian Lemma, which also provides a slick proof of
Latala’s chaos moment estimate. Boedihardjo proved in [Boe24] a remarkably sharp inequality for tensors
with independent entries when p = 2, which is often tight up to a constant. In the independent entry case for
matrices people have gone beyond p = 2 to prove stunningly precise norm estimates [APSS24, LS25]. The
independent entry world seems to allow a fight against logarithmic factors to get dimension-free estimates,
while in the general case it seems that we are not even close to proving a crude bound of the form (2). For
the case p = 2 it is also possible to adapt Rudelson’s argument in [Rud96] to get the analogous bound for
rank-1 tensors (though just as in the independent entry case, it is much more straightforward to prove a
bound with suboptimal logs). In [MT05] the authors use Hardy space theory to bound the injective ℓ2 norm
of random hankel tensors and they get in fact a two-sided estimate with the right logarithmic factor. In their

paper the tensor has unit-variance rademacher entries, but it can be slightly generalized to allow for other
coefficients. In the case p = ∞ people have used Kikuchi matrix techniques [JM24, BHKL24] to get bounds
for matching tensors, which tend to have nastier covariance structures, as unlike in the independent entry
case, the tensors Ti are not sparse. All of the mentioned examples are consistent with Problem 16 (and in
fact many of them completely outclass it), unfortunately it still seems to be far out of (my) reach.


Entry 9          The Lovász number of random circulant graphs (DD)
For a graph G = (V, E), the clique number ω(G) and the chromatic number χ(G) are fundamental properties
studied in combinatorics and graph theory. It is known that computing these quantities is, in general, NP-
hard. However, in certain cases, it is possible to efficiently provide upper and lower bounds. As an example,
for a d-regular graph G, it holds that χ(G) ≤ d + 1. In [Lov79], Lovász proposed a function ϑ(G), which was
later named the Lovász number or the Lovász theta function, such that it can be efficiently computed and

                                              ω(G) ≤ ϑ(G) ≤ χ(G),                                             (7)

where G denotes the complement of the graph G.
    The initial motivation of Lovász in [Lov79] was to bound the Shannon capacity of a graph. Consider
the following setting: label the vertices of a graph G with distinct letters, and say that two letters can be
confused if their respective vertices are adjacent. Similarly, two sequences of length k ≥ 1 can be confused,
if for each 1 ≤ i ≤ k, the vertices of the i-th letters are the same or adjacent. Let mk be the maximum
number of sequences of length k, such that no two sequences can be confused. Clearly, m1 = α(G), where
α(G) is the independence number of G. The Shannon capacity of the graph G is denoted by Θ(G) and is
defined by
                                             Θ(G) := lim (mk )1/k .                                       (8)
                                                      k→∞

Surprisingly, even for simple graphs, such as the cycle on 7 vertices C7 , the precise value of Θ(C7 ) is unknown.
The independence number and the Lovász number provide lower and upper bounds on the Shannon capacity,
respectively:

                                                          ϑ(G)ϑ(G) ≥ n.                                                            (13)

Note the connection to the inequality w(G)χ(G) ≥ n via (7). For vertex-transitive graphs G, which include
circulant graphs, (13) holds with equality.
    Circulant graphs (see Figure 1) are examples of highly structured graphs and are defined as Cayley
graphs over the cyclic group Zn . In other words, their adjacency matrix is circulant: if (i, j) ∈ E(G), then
(i + 1 mod n, j + 1 mod n) ∈ E(G). A celebrated example of a circulant graph from number theory is the
Paley graph. For a prime p ≡ 1 mod 4, in order to define the Paley graph of order p, consider the vertex
set [p] and connect two vertices i and j if and only if i − j is a quadratic residue modulo p. Paley graphs
are of great importance in combinatorics and theoretical computer science, since they are believed to behave
pseudorandomly: they are deterministic graphs which share certain properties with a typical realization of
G(p, 1/2). Current rigorous evidence of this phenomenon relies strongly on results from number theory and
additive combinatorics.
    Random (dense) circulant graphs can be viewed as a middle step between a ’fully random’ G(p, 1/2)
and a ’fully deterministic’ Paley graph. To sample a random circulant graph, we only need to sample the
neighbors of a single vertex, say vertex 0. Since (0, k) ∈ E(G) implies that (0, n − k) ∈ E(G), we sample a
vector x ∈ {0, 1}⌈ 2 ⌉ uniformly at random, and xk = 1 stands for (0, k) ∈ E(G).

Entry 10                Injectivity and Stability of Phase Retrieval (ASB)
Throughout this post K will stand for either R or C.
    Given A ∈ KN ×M , the Phase Retrieval Problem aims to recover a vector x ∈ KM from |Ax|, where | · |
is the entrywise modulus. In other words, one has access to the modulus of N linear measurements, and the
goal is to recover x ∈ KM . Let TK denote the unit torus in K (corresponding to the unit circle in C or {±1}
in R). One can only hope to recover x modulo TK (meaning up to a global phase shift in C or a global sign
flip in R).
    We say A is injective for phase retrieval (for K) if the map

                                            A : x mod TK 7→ |Ax|,

is injective.


                                                      6
     In 2013 a few collaborators and I [BCMN14] conjectured that N ≥ 4M − 4 linear measurements were
necessary for injectivity over C, and that generic 4M −4 measurements were injective. While the second part
of this conjecture was proven in [CEHV15], the first was shown to be false when Cynthia Vinzant found an
injective set of 11 linear measurements in C4 [Vin15].4 Cynthia stated a refined version of the conjecture that
still remains open (see https://dustingmixon.wordpress.com/2015/07/08/conjectures-from-sampta/)
Conjecture 19. Let N = 4M − 5 and draw A ∈ CN ×M at random (here the important thing is for im(A)
to be drawn uniformly from the Grassmannian of M -dimensional subspaces of C4M −5 , it can e.g. be with iid
gaussian entries). Let pM be the probability that the mapping x mod T 7→ |Ax|2 is injective.

 (a) pM < 1 for all M .
 (b)   lim pM = 0.
       M →∞

    Over R the question of injectivity is significantly easier and it is known that N ≥ 2M − 1 is necessary
that that generic 2M − 1 measurements are injective. A crucial step in the proof of this statement is the
fact that any partition of the rows of A(2M −1)×M into two sets yields one set that spans RM . This is known
as the complement property (see, e.g., [BCMN14]).
    A natural question is whether such phaseless measurements allow for stable reconstruction of x. In this
direction, Balan and Wang [BW13] showed that stability of the map A is related to the condition number
of subsets of rows of A that span RM .

    The complement property says that A is injective if and only if ω(A) > 0. [BW13] shows that stability
is tightly connected to the inverse of this quantity. They also make the following intriguing conjecture.

Open Problem 21. What is the value of ω(A) for A with iid standard gaussian entries? Does it satisfy
the bound in the conjecture? If so, what is β?

Entry 11          Mutually Unbiased Bases, ETFs, and Zauner’s Conjecture
                  (ASB)
Finite frame theory is the source of many beautiful questions. A remarkable example concerns the existence
of Mutually Unbiased Bases, objects of interest in Quantum Physics (see [MW24] for a review).
  4 A prize was awarded for this finding: https://dustingmixon.wordpress.com/2015/07/08/conjectures-from-sampta/.




                                                          7
Definition 11.1 (Mutually Unbiased Bases).   √    Two orthonormal bases {v1 , . . . , vd }, {w1 , . . . , wd } ⊂ Cd are
called mutually unbiased if |⟨vi , wj ⟩| = 1/ d for all i, j. Let MUB(d) denote the largest possible number of
(simultaneously) mutually unbiased bases (MUBs) in Cd .
    In [BBRV02] it is shown that MUB(d) ≤ d + 1 and that equality is achieved when d is a prime power.
For d not a prime power, the problem of determining MUB(d) is still open. The smallest open instance is
particularly well known for being a tantalizing open problem that has remained open (see Open Problem
6.2 in [Ban16]).
Conjecture 22. Let MUB(d) denote the largest possible number of (simultaneously) mutually unbiased bases
(MUBs) in Cd . We have MUB(6) < 7.

The isoperimetric problem dates back to the ancient Greeks who conjectured that in R2 , the shape with fixed
boundary length that maximizes area is given by a circle. While this has been proven a long time ago, the

probability measure µ on Rn , and Euclidean distance d, the isoperimetric problem consists in determining
the set S that minimizes the boundary measure µ+ (S) given a fixed volume µ(S).

Entry 14          Sharp Bounds for Graph Matrices (PNN)
Given a computation problem on a random input, one natural question is whether there is some polynomial
time algorithm solving it with high probability. To argue that such a problem is hard, we usually compare
it against a set of algorithms that are believed to be strong. Whenever the problem can be phrased as
a polynomial optimization problem, a good choice of algorithms is the Sum-of-Squares (SoS) hierarchy.
This hierarchy offers an increasing sequence of convex relaxations to the polynomial optimization problem,
indexed by the degree of polynomials each relaxation uses. If this degree is d, it is known that a solution
can be found in nO(d) time using semidefinite programming (SDP). The past decade has seen vast interest
in studying this hierarchy as a benchmark, due to the following two reasons.


                                                      12
  1. For many problems, efficient algorithms were constructed based on it [GW95, ARV04, HSS15], some
     of which are still state-of-the-art, and for some problems, they have even been shown to be optimal
     among all SDP relaxations [LRS15].
  2. There has been a well developed machinery to show lower bounds against SoS hierarchies, meaning
     that a certain degree d relaxation fails to certify with high probability.

   In this post, we focus on the technical aspects of the latter point, for which we highly recommend the
survey by Potechin and Rajendran [PR23].

called pseudo-expectation, satisfying
  1. (normalization) Ẽ[1] = 1,
  2. (feasibility) Ẽ[f gi ] = 0 for every i = 1, . . . , m and polynomial f with deg(f gi ) ≤ d,

  3. (positivity) Ẽ[f 2 ] ≥ 0 for all polynomials f with deg(f ) ≤ d/2.
Interestingly, by duality, if there is no such Ẽ, the SoS algorithm will succeed. However, finding such Ẽ can be
challenging, particularly because the feasibility constraint depends on the random input of the certification
problem. A commonly used heuristic is the so-called pseudo-calibration [BHK+ 19], and once it finds some Ẽ,
the hardest step is checking positivity. We rephrase this condition in terms of the positive semi-definiteness
