                                                that keep me puzzled and amused

                                              This is a collection of open questions in geometry that I think of as
                                          puzzles: they stick to my brain — I see grips, but no spare hands. Puzzle-
                                          charm is the only criterion for inclusion here; importance is ignored.
                                              These puzzles grew out of my collection of solved puzzles [1]. I kept
                                          it aside since things around open questions change rapidly.
                                              Please send me an email if you solve one of the puzzles, or see a good
                                          approach.


                                          Homemade
                                          1   Two-convexity
                                          Note that an open connected set Ω ⊂ Rn is convex (in the usual sense)
                                          if the following holds: for any triangle △, if two sides of △ lie in Ω, then
                                          △ ⊂ Ω. This observation motivates the definition of two-convexity used
                                          in the following problem.
                                           Let Ω be a simply connected open set in Rn . Assume that Ω is two-
                                          convex; that is, if 3 faces of a 3-simplex △ lie in Ω, then △ ⊂ Ω.
                                              Is it true that any component of the intersection of Ω with any 2-plane
                                          is simply connected?
                                              If the boundary of Ω is a smooth hypersurface, then the answer is
                                          “yes”. Also, the answer is “yes” if n = 3.
                                              To prove the first statement, note that two-convexity implies that at
                                          most one of the principal curvatures of the boundary is negative. Then
                                          follow the Morse-type argument in Lefschetz theorem [see Section 21 in
                                          2]. In the case n = 3, one can mimic the Morse-type argument; it also
                                          follows from [7.17 in 3].
                                              The first argument also proves that if Ω is a simply connected open
                                          two-convex set in Rn with a smooth boundary, then any component of the

is simply connected. The example below shows that a general 2-convex
open set does not have this property.
    One may hope to prove the statement by constructing an approxi-

Example. We will construct a two-convex simply connected open set Ω

   Set
                     Π = { (x, y, z, t) ∈ R4 | y < x2 }.

    Since Π is open and two-convex, so is the intersection Ω = Π ∩ Π′ .

Remark. Two-convexity shows up in comparison geometry — the maximal
open flat sets in the manifolds of nonnegative or nonpositive curvature are
two-convex [4].

2   Braid space
Let us define braid space Bn as the universal covering of Cn infinitely
branching along the complex hyperplanes zi = zj for i ̸= j. We sup-
pose that Cn is equipped with the canonical Euclidean metric, and Bn is
equipped with the lifted length metric.
    In other words, if Wn denotes the complement of complex hyperplanes
zi = zj in Cn , then the braid space Bn is the completion of the universal
metric covering of Wn .
    The following problem is a special case of a conjecture formulated by
Daniel Allcock [5, Conjecture 1.4], which is related to an earlier conjecture


                                     2
of Ruth Charney and Michael Davis [6, Conjecture 3], which in turn is
motivated by the K(π, 1)-conjecture [7, 8]. Dmitri Panov and the author
are also responsible for a more general version of Allcock’s conjecture [4,
Conjecture 1.1].
 Is it true that Bn is CAT(0) for any n?
    The answer is “yes” for n ⩽ 3. The case n = 2 is trivial, and the
case n = 3 follows from the theorem of Ruth Charney and Michael Davis
about branched coverings of the 3-sphere [9]; another proof is given by
Dmitri Panov and the author [10]. The case of the real braid space with
4 strings was settled by Ruth Charney thru a detailed analysis of short
galleries [11, Theorem 5.4]. The real braid space is defined as the inverse
image of the real subspace Rn ⊂ Cn under the projection Bn → Cn . This
is a convex subset of Bn ; therefore, the question about real braid space is
a special case of our problem.
    The space Bn has plenty of convex functions, which might help to
understand its geometry. The following construction is inspired by the
argument given by Ian Agol [12]: (1) choose n distinct points in C (that
is, an element of Wn ), (2) choose a closed curve z in its complement, (3)
consider the function Bn → R that returns the greatest lower bound of
lengths of closed curves that are homotopic to z.
    Existence of convex functions forbids geodesic loops in Bn ; this would
also follow if Bn is CAT(0). To show that Bn is CAT(0) one has to forbid
geodesic digons [3, 6.6].

3   Quotients of Hilbert space

 Suppose R is a compact simply connected Riemannian manifold that
is isometric to a quotient of the Hilbert space by a group of isometries
(or, more generally, R is the target of a Riemannian submersion from the
Hilbert space). Is it true that R is isometric to a biquotient? That is, is
it true that R is a quotient of a compact Lie group G with a bi-invariant
metric by a group of isometries?
    Any biquotient can appear as a quotient of the Hilbert space by a
group of isometries. Let us sketch the argument suggested by Alexander
Lytchak; see [13]. Another proof follows from the construction of Chuu-
Lian Terng and Gudlaugur Thorbergsson given in [14, Section 4].
    Denote by Gn the direct product of n copies of G. Consider the map
φn : Gn → G//H defined by

                     φn : (α1 , . . . , αn ) 7→ [α1 · · · αn ]H ,

where [x]H denotes the H-orbit of x in G.

    Note that φn is a quotient map for the action of H × Gn−1 on Gn
defined by

 (β0 , . . . , βn )·(α1 , . . . , αn ) = (β0 ·α1 ·β1−1 , β1 ·α2 ·β2−1 , . . . , βn−1 ·αn ·βn−1 ),

where βi ∈ G and (β0 , βn ) ∈ H < G × G.                                  √
    Denote by ρn the product metric on Gn rescaled by the factor n.
Note that the quotient (Gn , ρn )/(H × Gn−1 ) is isometric to G//H =
= (G, ρ1 )//H. Let φn : (Gn , ρn ) → G//H be the corresponding quotient
map; clearly, φn is a submetry.
    As n → ∞, the curvature of (Gn , ρn ) converges to zero and its injectiv-
ity radius goes to infinity. Therefore, the ultralimit of (Gn , ρn ), with the
identity element as the basepoint, is a Hilbert space H, and the submetries
φn ultraconverge to a submetry φ : H → G//H.

4    Nested convex surfaces

 Describe the Riemannian metrics on Sn that are isometric to smooth
nested convex surfaces; that is, a complete smooth convex hypersurface in
a complete smooth convex hypersurface in ... in a Euclidean space.
    If n = 2, then by Alexandrov’s embedding theorem these are all Rie-
mannian metrics with nonnegative curvature.
    Direct calculations show that the metric has nonnegative cosectional
curvature; the latter means that, at each point, the curvature tensor can
be expressed as a linear combination with nonnegative coefficients of the
curvature tensors of S2 × Rn−2 . (In dimension three, nonnegative co-
sectional curvature is equivalent to nonnegative sectional curvature; in
dimension 4, it is equivalent to nonnegative curvature operator.)
    It might happen that any metric with nonnegative cosectional curva-
ture on Sn is isometric to a nested convex surface. (It’s embarrassing,
but I don’t understand the situation with the Berger spheres.) If one
drops completeness from the definition of nested convex surfaces and as-
sumes that the cosectional curvature is strictly positive, then the answer
is “yes” [15].

5    Maximal finite subgroups

 Suppose that a group Γ acts effectively, isometrically, and properly
discontinuously on Euclidean space En . Show that Γ contains at most 2n
maximal finite subgroups up to conjugation.
   Let F be a maximal finite subgroup in Γ. Its fixed-point set Fix(F ) is
an affine subset of En . Furthermore, Fix(F ) is mapped to a singular set


                                                4
SF in the quotient space X = En /Γ. Moreover, (1) a conjugation of F
does not change the set SF ; and, since F is maximal, (2) SF is a simple
singular set; that is, SF does not have a proper singular subset. So instead
of counting subgroups up to conjugation, we may count simple singular
sets in X. Crude bounds on this number were obtained by Tadashi Fujioka
[16].
    It is expected that if the number of simple singular sets is maximal,
then each of them has dimension 0; that is, each set SF is an isolated
point in X. If this is indeed true, then the problem essentially boils down
to the following well-known problem in discrete geometry: show that one
can choose at most 2n points in En such that any triangle with vertices
among the chosen points is acute or right. This problem was posed by
Paul Erdős and solved by Ludwig Danzer and Branko Grünbaum [17].
More on the subject can be found in the paper by Nina Lebedeva [18].


6    Shortcut in a connected set

 Let p and q be a pair of points in a compact connected subset K ⊂ Rn .
Is it possible to connect p to q by a curve γ such that the length of γ \ K
is arbitrarily small?
    Be aware that there are compact connected sets which contain no
nontrivial paths; for example, the pseudoarc.
    The problem remains open for n ⩾ 3. In the case n = 2, an affirmative
answer was obtained by Taras Banakh [19]. His argument is based on the
following claim:
    Suppose K is a connected compact set in the plane and p, q ∈ K. Then,
given ε > 0 there is a sequence of compact connected subsets K0 , . . . , Kn
with pairs of points pi , qi ∈ Ki for each i such that (1) diam Ki < ε for
each i, (2) p0 = p, qn = q, and (3) if ℓi denotes the line segment [qi−1 pi ],
then
                      length ℓ1 + · · · + length ℓn < ε.

    Assume that the claim is proved. Then we can prove the statement
by applying it recursively to each Ki , choosing a much smaller value of ε
at each step. Indeed, consider the closure L of the set of all points pi and
qi for all iterations; it is a closed subset in K. By adding to L the line
segments ℓi for all the iterations we obtain a curve connecting p to q that
spends an arbitrarily small length outside of L. Hence the 2-dimensional
case follows.
    Now let us prove the claim. Consider a coordinate grid Γ on the plane
with step 2ε . Since the intersection Γ ∩ K is compact, it can be covered
by a finite collection of closed line segments {I1 , . . . , In } in the grid such

7    Involution of the 3-sphere

 Suppose that a closed geodesic γ is the fixed-point set of an isometric
involution of (S3 , g). Assume that the sectional curvature of g is at least 1.
Is it true that length γ ⩽ 2·π?
    A nonsharp universal upper bound on length γ follows from a result
of Tadashi Fujioka [16]. The same question can be asked about spherical
polyhedral metrics with curvature at least 1 in the sense of Alexandrov.
    If, instead of an isometric involution, we have an S1 -action, then the
answer is “yes”. It follows since the quotient space (S3 , g)/S1 is a convex
disc with sectional curvature at least 1, and γ projects isometrically to its
boundary.

8    Faceting Fubini–Study
A polyhedral metric on a compact manifold is a length metric such that,
for some triangulation, each simplex is isometric to a simplex in Euclidean
space. Such a metric is said to be nonnegatively curved if the total an-
gle around each codimension-two simplex does not exceed 2·π; this is
equivalent to nonnegative curvature in the sense of Alexandrov.
 Let d be the Fubini–Study metric on the complex projective plane CP2 .
Is it possible to find a sequence of nonnegatively curved polyhedral metrics
dn on CP2 that converges to d?
    Nonnegatively curved polyhedral metrics on CP2 have a number of in-
teresting properties. For example, any such metric is Kähler; that is, one
can choose a complex structure on each simplex of its triangulation that is
respected by the gluings. Furthermore, these complex structures coincide


                                       6
with the standard complex structure on CP2 for a right parametrization,
and in this parametrization, the singular set forms an algebraic submani-
fold. Such metrics on CP2 were constructed and studied by Dmitri Panov
[20]; see also his paper with Martin de Borbon [21].

9    Octahedron comparison
Suppose Γ is a graph with vertices v1 , . . . , vn . We say that a metric
space X meets the Γ-comparison if, for any set of points in X labeled by
the vertices of Γ, there is a model configuration ṽ1 , . . . , ṽn in the Hilbert
space H such that if vi is adjacent to vj , then |ṽi − ṽj |H ⩽ |vi − vj |X ,
and otherwise, |ṽi − ṽj |H ⩾ |vi − vj |X ; here |p − q|M denotes the distance
between points p and q in the metric space M .

 Is it true that O3 -comparison holds in any geodesic CAT(0) space?
    This problem is motivated by the question of Michael Gromov [22,
1.19+ (e)].
    Note that the 4-cycle C4 is an induced subgraph of O3 . Therefore,
O3 -comparison implies C4 -comparison; the latter describes the CAT(0)-
comparison [23]. In general, C4 -comparison does not imply O3 -comparison,
but for intrinsic metrics it is unknown; a weaker inequality was proved by
Tetsu Toyoda [24].
    An affirmative answer might lead to a classification of six-point spaces
that admit an isometric embedding in a geodesic CAT(0) space. The
five-point case was solved by Tetsu Toyoda [25] (see also [26]).
    On the other hand, Nina Lebedeva and the author proved that O3 -
comparison holds in products of trees [27]. Therefore, a counterexample
would mean that O3 -comparison is something new and interesting; so
both answers are good.
Postscript. The problem has been solved by Jihun Kim [28].

10    Group generated by central symmetries
 Consider the Lobachevsky space of sufficiently large dimension. Does
it admit a discrete cocompact isometric action generated by


                                        7
 (a) elements of finite order?
 (b) central symmetries?
 (c) rotations around subspaces of codimension k?
   Ernest Vinberg [29, 30] proved that there are no such actions generated
by reflections. This solves part (c) for k = 1; the remaining questions are
completely open.

11    Milnor’s cartography problem

 Let Ω be a round disc of radius α < π2 on the unit sphere S2 , and let
Ω′ be a convex domain in S2 with the same area as Ω. Show that there is
a (1, sinα α )-bi-Lipschitz map from Ω′ to the plane.
    The question was asked by John Milnor [31]. Polar coordinates with
the origin at the center of Ω provide an example of a (1, sinα α )-bi-Lipschitz
map and this constant sinα α is optimal.
    If, in the formulation of the problem, one replaces “area” with “perime-
ter”, then the answer is “yes”. It can be proved using the idea of Gary
Lawlor [32].
Postscript. The problem has since been solved by the author [33].

12    Convex hull in CAT(0)
Let X be a geodesic metric space. Recall that a set C ⊂ X is called convex
if, for any two points x, y ∈ C, every geodesic from x to y lies in C. The
closed convex hull of a subset K ⊂ X is defined as the intersection of all
closed convex sets containing K.
 Let K be a compact subset of a complete length CAT(0) space X. Is
it true that the closed convex hull of K is compact?
    Note that the space is not assumed to be locally compact; otherwise,
the statement is evident. I believe in a counterexample, perhaps even
with negatively pinched curvature (in the sense of Alexandrov).
    The closed convex hull of a compact set (in particular, of a three-point
set) might look ugly even in a smooth 3-dimensional manifold. If in doubt,
check the note of Alexander Lytchak and the author [34].
    This problem was mentioned by Mikhael Gromov [35, 6.B1 (f)] and
restated by Eva Kopecká and Simeon Reich [36], but it seems to have
been known in mathematical folklore for a long time.
    The problem remains open even when K consists of three points. A
counterexample is known if the CAT(0) assumption is weakened to the
existence of a conical geodesic bicombing; it was constructed by Giuliano
Basso, Yannick Krifka, and Elefterios Soultanis [37].

13    Homeomorphism of the torus
Let T denote the 2-dimensional torus.
   A homeomorphism h : T → T is called simple if it is supported on an
embedded open disc; that is, there is an embedded open disc ∆ ⊂ T such
that h is the identity outside of ∆.
   The disc norm of a homeomorphism h : T → T is defined as the least
number of simple homeomorphisms h1 , . . . , hn such that
                             h = h1 ◦ · · · ◦ hn .

 Is there a homeomorphism of the 2-dimensional torus with arbitrarily
large disc norm?
    Analogous questions for the cylinder and the sphere are solved, but
for the Möbius band the problem is open. This problem is discussed by
Dmitri Burago, Sergei Ivanov, and Leonid Polterovich in [38].
Postscript. Jonathan Bowden, Sebastian Hensel, and Richard Webb [39]
constructed unbounded quasimorphisms on the group of homeomorphisms
of the torus that are isotopic to the identity, which implies an affirmative
answer to the problem.

14    Open projection
Let us denote by Dn a closed n-dimensional ball.
   Recall that a map is called open if the image of any open set is open.
 Is it possible to construct an embedding D4 ,→ S2 × R2 such that the
projection D4 → S2 is an open map?
     It is easy to construct an embedding D3 ,→ S3 such that its compo-
sition with the Hopf fibration is open. Therefore, there is an open map
f3 : D3 → S2 .
     Composing f3 with any open map D4 → D3 , one gets an open map
f4 : D4 → S2 . However, this map is not a projection of an embedding
D4 ,→ S2 × R2 .
     The question was asked by ε-δ (an anonymous mathematician) [40].

15    Zero-curvature along geodesics
 Let M be a surface of genus 2 with a Riemannian metric of nonpositive
curvature. Show that, with probability 1, a random geodesic in M runs
into the set of negative curvature.
   I learned this problem from Dmitri Burago; it originated from a dis-
cussion with Keith Burns, Michael Brin, and Yakov Pesin [see also 41].

                                      9
Weisheng Wu [42] proved the statement under the additional assumption
that the set of points with negative curvature has finitely many connected
components. It seems that there are no known examples of a surface M
with a nonperiodic geodesic that runs in the set of zero curvature.

16    Ambrose problem
 Let (M, g) be a simply connected complete Riemannian manifold, and
let p ∈ M . Denote by h the pullback of the metric tensor g to the tangent
space Tp M along the exponential map expp : Tp M → M ; in general, h
might degenerate at some points. Does h define the manifold (M, g) and
the point p up to isometry?
    This simple-looking question was asked a long time ago by Warren
Ambrose [43]. Affirmative answers were obtained in three cases: (1) in
dimension 2, (2) for analytic metrics, and (3) for generic metrics; see [44–
47] and the references therein.

17    Optical fibers
Suppose that V is a body in R3 bounded by two plane figures F1 and
F2 (the ends) and a smooth surface Σ (a tube). Suppose that a light
ray entering V thru one end and bouncing with perfect reflection from
the interior walls of Σ will emerge from the other end with probability
1; moreover, a light ray that starts in the interior of V , after bouncing,
emerges from one of the ends with probability 1. In this case, we say that
V is an optical fiber.
    A small tubular neighborhood of a smooth curve γ : [a, b] → R3 with
disk ends provides an example of an optical fiber. Let us describe a
more general example, which can be constructed in the same way using
any plane figure bounded by a smooth curve instead of a disk. Suppose
a plane curve is given by a periodic parametrization θ 7→ (x(θ), y(θ)).
Choose a parallel normal frame e1 , e2 along γ (that is, e′i (t) ∥ γ̇(t) for
all t), and consider the tube [a, b] × S1 ,→ R3 defined by

                   (t, θ) 7→ γ(t) + x(θ)·e1 (t) + y(θ)·e2 (t)

(The condition that the frame is parallel implies that any normal plane
to γ cuts the tube at a right angle.) This way we get an optical fiber with
congruent ends.
    Are there any constructions of optical fibers different from the one
described above? In other words:
 Is it always possible to slice an optical fiber into plane figures that meet
the walls at right angles?

                                      10
       In particular,
 Is there an optical fiber with noncongruent ends?
    By Liouville’s theorem, it is clear that the ends must have the same
area.
    If the walls are only assumed to be piecewise smooth, then one can
construct an optical fiber with a pair of equidecomposable figures at the
ends. (The construction is the same, but one splits the tube into a few
tubes along the way and then reassembles them.)
    The problem is based on two questions posted on MathOverflow [48,
49].

18       Guth’s sponge

 Show that there is εn > 0 such that any bounded open set in Rn with
volume at most εn admits a length-increasing embedding into a unit ball.
   The question was asked by Larry Guth in his thesis [50]. It is open
in all dimensions n ⩾ 2. In dimension 2, Parker Glynn-Adey proved a
weaker version using a strip between parallel lines instead of the ball [51].
