Summary: In the context of the two-tier stochastic frontier model, we present a new closed-form
stochastic specification, the half-normal one. Expressions to calculate average and conditional
measures of inefficiency are provided, for regression specifications in levels and in logs. Some results
related to model validation and maximum likelihood estimation are also presented.

Keywords: Two-tier stochastic frontier analysis, half -normal distribution, maximum likelihood,
informational efficiency.

JEL CODES: C16, C21, D82, D83.


        1. Introduction and literature review

        What distinguishes stochastic frontier (SF) analysis from an econometric point of view

is the composite error specification where, alongside the usual zero-mean symmetric

disturbance, a one-sided error term appears (positive or negative depending on the context),

which is exactly the component that is used to measure the subject matter of SF analysis, the

deviations from full efficiency assumed by economic theory.

        Polachek and Yoon (1987) have introduced a three-component error structure with two

one-sided terms, one positive and one negative, in order to capture imperfections on both

sides of a transaction (the "Two-tier Stochastic Frontier Model", hereafter 2TSF model). They

originally applied their model to the labor market in order to estimate the earnings equation,

where the two one-sided error terms were interpreted as representing the quantitative impact

of informational deficiencies of employers and of workers separately, leading the former to

pay more than workers' reservation wages, and the latter to work for less than maximum

wages acceptable by employers. Groot and Oosterbeek (1994), using an earnings-related data

set from Netherlands, extended the model to be able to link informational inefficiencies to

individual characteristics, by assuming that the moments of each one-side disturbance is a
                                             2 of 14


function of the regressors or other variables (technically, this amounts to assume

heteroskedastic one-sided errors). In the same spirit but using another route, Polachek and

Yoon (1996) applied their original model to panel data in order to disentangle unobserved

individual heterogeneity from the informational imperfections. They found that the panel data

approach improved the quality of the results, and that the significance of informational

inefficiencies, although it was reduced, persisted. Sharif and Dar (2007) applied the original

2TSF model in cross-sectional earnings data from Canada focusing on immigrants, while

Murphy and Strobl (2008) applied the model as extended by Groot and Oosterbeek to data

from Trinidad & Tobago. The results of these studies are mixed, regarding the sources of

deviations from full-information wage. In some, "employers' ignorance" emerges as the main

factor leading the wage above its perfect information level, in others, it is "employees'

ignorance" that dominates and leads the wage below it. But in all cases, the quantitative

significance of these informational inefficiencies is clear.

      The 2TSF model has also seen applications in the Health Services market, where

informational asymmetries and deficiencies are believed to abound. Gaynor and Polachek

(1994) applied it to the physician services' market in an attempt to explain wide price

variations, while Chawla (2002) used it on data from Egypt, and found that doctors were

extracting a large surplus in their transactions with the patients. Tomini et al. (2012) used the

model to measure the effects of incomplete information on the informal (under-the-table)

payments made to physicians in Albania. Interestingly, they found that in this case the

deficiencies were in favor of patients, against conventional wisdom. But this result accords

with intuition, and strengthens the case in favor of the 2TSF model as a valid method to

unearth existing phenomena: in the absence of a public market and price system, buyers-

patients tend to exchange more information regarding informal costs, while on the other hand

sellers possess less information for the same reasons, and also tend to avoid exchanging

information because informal revenues are considered unethical and usually are illegal.
                                            3 of 14


      Recently, the application of the 2TSF model seems to be picking pace, and it starts to

be used in innovative ways and sometimes in highly unexpected contexts, thus showing its

flexibility and potential. Kumbhakar and Parmeter (2009) used the model to measure the

effects of bargaining over wage in a framework of productivity uncertainty, while Kinukawa

and Motohashi (2010) followed their lead and bargaining model but with a paper on alliances

in the biotechnology market. Kumbhakar and Parmeter (2010) presented a 2TSF generalized

hedonic price model with an application to the house market. Ferona and Tsionas (2012)

applied the model to an auction setting, using data from timber auctions, in order to assess the

extent of underbidding and overbidding behavior. But also Groot and van den Brink (2007)

used it to measure "optimism" and "pessimism" in assessing quality of life, while Poggi

(2010) re-applied the model to the labor market, but this time in order to measure job

satisfaction. Researchers in China seem also to have taken an interest in the model: Lian &

Chung (2008) and Yu and Liang (2012) applied it to investigate the effects of financing

constraints and agency costs on investment behavior and on dividend policies respectively, of

Chinese firms. Also, Zheng and Zhang (2012), and Huang (2013) used the model to separate

the "premium" effect from the "underpricing" effect in IPOs of Chinese firms. In all cases the

2TSF model has delivered significant results, providing quantitative insights into the

magnitude and effects of imbalances and conflicting forces of various kinds on both sides of

economic transactions (or of psychological matters).

      We aim at contributing to this momentum with the present paper, the remaining of

which is structured as follows: in Sect. 2 we present a new stochastic specification for the

composite error term in closed form, namely the half-normal specification. In Sect. 3 we

present measures of the effects of inefficiencies, at various levels of aggregation, and for

different regression specifications. In Sect. 4 we provide some discussion on model selection

and also formulas to be used in the context of maximum likelihood estimation, completing the

basic toolkit needed to implement the new specification. Section 5 concludes, with
                                                  4 of 14


suggestions for further research. For reasons of brevity, proofs of the various results are

omitted1.



       2. The half-normal specification for the 2TSF model

       Without any exception, all studies mentioned in the previous section have assumed the

same distributional specification: a normal symmetric disturbance, and two exponential one-

sided errors.     Polachek and Yoon introduced this stochastic specification emphasizing

tractability. Moreover, for the basic, one-tier stochastic frontier specification, experience had

shown that, at least qualitatively, it mattered little whether the one-sided term was assumed to

follow an exponential or a half-normal distribution. But this similarity does not fully extend to

the case of the 2TSF model. Tsionas (2012) compared results from a 2TSF model under the

normal-exponential error specification, against results under a normal-gamma specification

(estimated by a Fourier Transform algorithm that approximates distributional specifications

unavailable in closed form). He found that the exponential specification, which is nested by

the gamma one, was rejected for at least one of two one-sided random variables, and also that

there were significant differences in the estimates of the terms related to the error components

and their implications for the economic phenomenon under study. Although this is just one

instance of inadequacy of the available exponential specification, it is somehow impressive

that it happened the first time it was subjected to such a trial. Therefore, providing an

alternative specification appears worthwhile. We chose the (normal)-half normal specification

since it is the prevailing one in the one-tier model.

        4. Model selection and maximum likelihood estimation

        To validate statistically the use of the one-tier stochastic frontier model, it is natural to

perform an ordinary least-squares estimation and then test the residuals for departures from

normality, or perhaps more appropriately as argued by Schmidt and Lin (1984), to test

separately for the existence of skewness. Godfrey and Orme (1991) and Bera and Premaratne

(2001) have developed separate skewness tests that remain robust under non-normality. For

the 2TSF model, due to its two-sided nature, absence of skewness should be followed by a

test for excess kurtosis given zero skewness, since as we mentioned in Section 2, if

 12   22   2 the two-tier specification will possess a density symmetric around zero, but

will exhibit excess kurtosis. Finally, since the 2TSF nests both the one-tier and the symmetric

normal specification, a Likelihood Ratio test could also be used (which is an advantage over

the exponential specification).

      5. Conclusions and further research

      The Two-tier Stochastic Frontier model has recently seen an increase in the number of

studies in which it is used, and more importantly, in the areas of economic (and even non-

economic) activity in which it is applied, bearing out the predictions of the scholars that have

implemented it during its first phase of existence. In this paper, we have presented a new

stochastic specification for the model, the half-normal, where the two one-sided error terms

are assumed to follow half-normal distributions. In an empirical study, not presented here, we

have compared results from the existing exponential specification with the half normal one.

For the specific data set the composite error distribution appeared symmetric with excess

kurtosis. The two specifications were almost identical as regards the estimated regressor

coefficients and the maximized value of the likelihood. But they produced different estimates

for the scale parameters of the one-sided error-terms, and hence different estimates for the

various measures of inefficiency presented previously. We conjecture that the presence of

skewness will intensify the distance between the two specifications.

      Therefore, a fruitful area of further research would be to develop procedures for the

evaluation of, and selection between, the two specifications, possibly suited to the specific

stochastic frontier framework, apart from the available general methods for non-nested model

selection. But the flexibility and applicability of the Two-tier stochastic frontier model

through additional stochastic specifications can be further enriched, and for good reason: one

direction is to obtain hybrid setups, like an exponential/half normal or a half-

normal/exponential specification. As mentioned earlier, in Tsionas (2012) the exponential

specification was rejected for one of the two one-sided error terms but not for the other and

this is an indication that inefficiencies may follow different distributions in each side of an

economic transaction.

      Another related direction of research is to obtain closed-form specifications for the one-

sided error terms that nest the existing ones. For the half normal case, this would be the

truncated normal specification. Such a specification would permit to test whether the modes
                                           13 of 14


of the distributions of the one-sided error terms are non-zero. Both the exponential and the

half-normal densities have their mode at zero, and, as it has been noted early on by Stevenson

(1980) for the one-tier stochastic frontier model, this imposes a restrictive a priori assumption

regarding the likelihood of inefficiencies characterizing market participants.
