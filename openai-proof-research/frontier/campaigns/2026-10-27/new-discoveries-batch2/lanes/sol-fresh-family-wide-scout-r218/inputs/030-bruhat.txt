                                                                                    1. Introduction
                                              In 1979 Kazhdan and Lusztig introduced the remarkable polynomials Px,y (q) for compa-
                                           rable elements of a Coxeter group [22]; for Weyl groups, their coefficients encode the dimen-
                                           sions of local intersection cohomology groups of Schubert varieties [23]. The purely combi-
                                           natorial definition of these polynomials inspired Stanley to introduce toric g-polynomials
                                           for convex polytopes [31], which have a similar interpretation in the intersection cohomol-
                                           ogy of toric varieties when the polytopes are rational [12, 18]. The marvelous positivity
                                           theorems of Elias–Williamson [16] and Karu [21] extend the positivity of the coefficients
                                           of the Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials and toric g-polynomials in the geometric settings to
                                           arbitrary Coxeter groups and arbitrary convex polytopes, respectively.
                                              Nevertheless, the two families of polynomials have quite different numerical behavior.
                                           A famous theorem of Polo [29] states that any polynomial in 1 + qZ≥0 [q] appears as a
                                           Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomial in some symmetric group. In contrast, toric g-polynomials
                                           of polytopes satisfy strong restrictions: they cannot have internal zeros and are conjectured
                                           to have coefficients forming an M -sequence (see [7, Theorem 1.4 and Section 1.2]).
                                              The Kazhdan–Lusztig–Stanley theory provides a unified framework for these construc-
                                           tions [32]. Its input is a ranked poset P together with an auxiliary element (known as a
                                           P -kernel) in its incidence algebra. Classical Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials arise from the
                                           Kazhdan–Lusztig R-polynomial kernel of the Bruhat intervals in Coxeter groups, while
                                           toric g-polynomials arise from the Eulerian kernel (q − 1)rk(a,b) on face posets of polytopes
                                           (see [32, 30]). Note that the face poset of any convex polytope is a lattice. It is known

the Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials in this case coincide with toric g-polynomials [10]. Our
main result shows that this agreement comes from an actual convex polytope, building a

Theorem 1.1. Let (W, S) be any Coxeter system and let x < y in the Bruhat order. If
the Bruhat interval [x, y] is a lattice, then there is a convex polytope Lx,y of dimension
ℓ(x, y) − 1 whose complete face lattice is isomorphic to [x, y].
   Here the complete face lattice includes both the empty face and the whole polytope.
They correspond to x and y, respectively, under the isomorphism. This uniform construc-
tion will be provided in Section 3.
   We explain some related geometry. Suppose that W is a finite Weyl group of a complex
semisimple algebraic group G and that the Bruhat interval [x, y] ⊂ W is a lattice. In this
case, Theorem 1.1 was recently proved by Bossinger–Gorsky–Simental [6, Theorem 1.8
and Corollary 6.8] for finite Weyl groups based on earlier work of Gorsky–Kim–Sherman-
Bennett in type A [19] using toric Richardson varieties.
   More precisely, let Rx,y be the closed Richardson variety in the complete flag variety,
and let Rx,y be its open Richardson stratum. For finite Weyl groups, the lattice condition
is equivalent to Rx,y being an algebraic torus and to Rx,y being toric (see Proposition 4.1).
The acting torus may be unexpected, since it need not be a subtorus of the maximal
torus T of the algebraic group G and its rank can be significantly larger than that of the
maximal torus of G (see [19, Section 5.3]). The faces of the moment polytope Qx,y of
Rx,y are indexed by subintervals of [x, y], and a vertex figure of Qx,y at the vertex indexed
by [x, x] then has complete face lattice isomorphic to [x, y] [6, Corollary 6.8]. In other
words, in the geometric setting, a rational polytopal realization of [x, y] is obtained as the
moment polytope of a projective toric variety Xx,y , which is a torus-invariant divisor in
a toric modification of the closed Richardson variety Rx,y . When Rx,y is toric and the
maximal torus T has a dense orbit on Rx,y , the moment polytope Qx,y can be realized as
a Bruhat interval polytope defined by Kodama–Williams [25] and studied by Tsukerman–
Williams [33]. In type A, Lee–Masuda–Park [26, Theorem 5.1] showed that a Bruhat
interval polytope is toric (that is, its dimension is equal to the length of the Bruhat
interval) precisely when every subinterval is realized as a face. In particular, in this case
its face lattice is the interval poset of the underlying Bruhat interval.
   This picture allows us to express the local and global intersection cohomology of toric
Rx,y in terms of the toric Kazhdan–Lusztig–Stanley polynomials of the interval poset
Int([x, y]) (see Theorem 4.3). We also give several equivalent conditions for the rational
smoothness of the closed Richardson variety Rx,y (see Theorem 4.4). In particular, we
show that for a lattice Bruhat interval in a finite Weyl group,
        Rx,y is rationally smooth     ⇐⇒     Px,y (q) = 1   ⇐⇒     [x, y] is Boolean.
In this case, the moment polytope Qx,y of the closed Richardson variety Rx,y is combina-
torially a cube and the ordinary cohomology Poincaré polynomial of Rx,y is (1 + q)ℓ(x,y) .
See Section 4 for precise statements and proofs.

2.2. Lattice Bruhat intervals. For x ≤ y in the Bruhat order, the directed Bruhat graph
Γ(x, y) contains [x, y] as vertices, and w → wt as edges, where x ≤ w < wt ≤ y, t ∈ T .
The left coroot label of this edge w → wt is wαt∨ which is positive. Write Γ̂(x, y) for its
underlying undirected graph and Ex,y for its edge set. A subset of edges F ⊆ Ex,y is called
diamond-closed if whenever it contains two adjacent edges in a 4-cycle of Γ̂(x, y), it also
contains the other two. The diamond closure of F is the smallest diamond-closed subset
containing F . And F ⊆ Ex,y is diamond-generating if its diamond closure equals Ex,y .
Write gx,y for the minimum cardinality of a diamond-generating set. See [28] and [1] for
more details on diamond-generating sets.
   Denote the atoms of [x, y] by At(x, y) := {z : x ⋖ z ≤ y} and the coatoms by Co(x, y) :=
{z : x ≤ z ⋖ y}. It is easy to see that any maximal chain is a diamond-generating set, and
that {(x, a) : a ∈ At(x, y)} and {(c, y) : c ∈ Co(x, y)} also diamond generate.
   An edge w → wt is called long if ℓ(wt) − ℓ(w) > 1. An interval [x, y] is S3 -free if it
contains no subinterval isomorphic to the Bruhat order on S3 , also called a 2-crown. Here
is a summary of some of the known characterizations of a lattice Bruhat interval.
Theorem 2.1. The following are equivalent for a Bruhat interval [x, y].
  (1) The Bruhat interval [x, y] is a lattice.
  (2) The Bruhat interval [x, y] is S3 -free.
  (3) The Bruhat graph Γ(x, y) does not contain a long edge.
  (4) The R-polynomial satisfies Rx,y (q) = (q − 1)ℓ(x,y) .
  (5) Every maximal chain of [x, y] is a minimum-size diamond generating set.
   The implication (1)⇒(2) is immediate with the converse being an unpublished result of
Dyer (see also [19, Section 1]). The equivalence of (2) and (3) follows from [15, Propo-
sition 3.3], while the equivalence of (2) and (4) is Brenti’s criterion [10, Theorem 6.3].
Condition (5) is saying that gx,y = ℓ(x, y) while [1] gives gx,y = −[q ℓ(x,y)−1 ]Rx,y (q) =
(−1)ℓ(x,y)−1 [q]Rx,y (q). The equivalence of (4) and (5) is then seen from [10, Theorem 6.3].
   We say that [x, y] is a lattice Bruhat interval if the Bruhat interval [x, y] is a lattice.
2.3. Face structures of polytopes and cones. For a polytope P ⊂ V where V is a real
vector space, its faces are given by Ff = {v ∈ P : f (v) = maxp∈P f (p)}, where f ∈ V ∗ is
called a supporting vector and Ff is the corresponding supporting face. The complete face
lattice F(P ) of a polytope P is the poset of all faces of P ordered by inclusion, together
with the (−1)-dimensional face ∅ which is the minimum of this poset and P itself which is
the maximum. It is easy to see that this poset is indeed a lattice, with the meet operation
given by intersection.
   In this paper, a cone always means a convex polyhedral cone. We say that a cone C ⊂ V
is pointed if there exists f ∈ V ∗ such that f (v) > 0 for all v ∈ C \ {0}. For a pointed cone
C, its faces are given by Ff = {v ∈ C : f (v) = 0} where f ∈ V ∗ satisfies that f (v) ≥ 0
for all v ∈ C. Analogously, f is called a supporting vector and Ff is the corresponding
supporting face. The complete face lattice F(C) of a pointed cone C is the poset of all
faces of C ordered by inclusion, together with maximum C and minimum {0}.
   Given a polytope P ⊂ V , we can construct a pointed cone C(P ) = {(tv, t) : v ∈ P, t ∈
R≥0 } ⊂ V ⊕ R. They have the same face structure. Conversely, given a pointed cone C,
                                              5
we can take its vertex figure VF(C) = {v ∈ C : f (v) = 1} for some f ∈ V ∗ such that
f (v) > 0 for all v ∈ C \ {0}. This is a polytope with the same face structure as C. Only
the combinatorial type of VF(C) matters for our purposes, as it is independent of the
chosen strictly positive functional.
   Similarly, for a polytope P and a vertex p, we can also take the vertex figure of P at p,
defined as VFp (P ) = {v ∈ P : f (v) = f (p) − ϵ} where f is a supporting vector of p, and
ϵ > 0 is small enough. The complete face lattice of VFp (P ) is then the principal order
filter in F(P ) generated by p.
   After translating a polytope P and working in its affine span, we may assume, for
combinatorial purposes, that P ⊂ V is full-dimensional and contains the origin in its
interior. Its polar dual is
                          P ∗ := {f ∈ V ∗ : f (v) ≤ 1 for all v ∈ P }.
The complete face lattice of P ∗ is dual to that of P .

tion and terminology of [30], which we recommend as a wonderful survey in this theory.
   Let L be a finite ranked poset. For a ≤ b, let r(a, b) denote their rank difference. The
incidence algebra I(L) of L consists of families

replaces a decreasing two-edge segment of a maximal chain by the other side of its diamond.

many maximal chains, every sequence of descent flips terminates. Successive edges have
distinct left coroot labels and hence distinct numerical labels, so a terminal chain is strictly
increasing. We claim that the terminal chain is independent of all choices. Suppose

overlap in an edge, the flips commute. Otherwise, both segments lie in a rank-three
                                                  9
subinterval. After either first flip, further descent flips within that subinterval terminate

resulting chains have a common descendant. Since descent flips terminate and are locally
confluent, Newman’s lemma [27, Theorem 3] (also called Rome’s lemma) implies that
every maximal chain has a unique terminal chain. Finally, maximal chains are connected
by diamond flips, and each diamond flip is a descent flip in one direction. Therefore all
maximal chains have the same terminal chain.

atom of the common terminal chain that is strictly increasing. Starting from a maximal

height. This gives a path from a to a0 in the atom graph A(x, y) along which λa (φ)/λa (θ)
strictly decreases. Thus, each of the nonempty sets in the lemma statement contains a0 ,
and each of its vertices is connected to a0 within the set.

full sets cause no difficulty. Otherwise, every selected height is strictly smaller than every
unselected height. Choose a threshold strictly between these two finite sets of heights, and
perturb φ to a sufficiently close generic section.                                          □

  We are now ready to establish the consequences of our main theorem, which provide
additional information for the Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials for lattice Bruhat intervals.
Proof of Theorem 1.2. Let P = L∗x,y , so that dim P = n − 1 and
                                       Px,y (q) = g(P ; q).
Then Kalai’s vanishing theorem proves (1).
  For x < z < y, the face of P corresponding to z has complete face lattice anti-isomorphic
to [z, y], while its face figure in P has complete face lattice anti-isomorphic to [x, z]. Their
                                                17
toric g-polynomials are Pz,y and Px,z by Proposition 5.2. Thus Kalai’s monotonicity gives
(2).
   Note that the vertices of P correspond to the coatoms of [x, y]. Substitution of f0 (P ) =
m and d = n − 1 into the generalized upper bound theorem proves (3).
   Note that by Proposition 5.2, the linear coefficient of the toric g-polynomial (see [7, (5),
p. 1153]) gives the linear coefficient
                                  [q]Px,y (q) = f0 (P ) − n = m − n.
Consequently Px,y (q) = 1 forces P to have exactly n vertices, and hence to be a simplex.
Its face lattice, and therefore [x, y], is Boolean. The converse follows from the fact that
the toric g-polynomial of a simplex is 1 (see [7, p. 1153]). This proves (4).            □
