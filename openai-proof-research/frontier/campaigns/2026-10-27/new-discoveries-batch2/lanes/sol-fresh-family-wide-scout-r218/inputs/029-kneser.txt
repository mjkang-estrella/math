                                                   Abstract. Extending the notions of Kneser graphs and Schrijver graphs,
                                                   we define the G-stable Kneser graph KG(G, k) (where k ≥ 2 and G is a




arXiv:2610.17884v1 [math.CO] 13 Oct 2026
                                                   graph on n ≥ 2k vertices) as the graph whose vertices are the k-element
                                                   independent sets in G and whose edges join pairs of disjoint indepen-
                                                   dent sets. The well-known result of Lovász on the chromatic number of
                                                   Kneser graphs implies that χ(KG(G, k)) ≤ n − 2k + 2 for any G. We
                                                   call G k-tight if equality holds, and we study the following question:
                                                   Which graphs are k-tight? A result of Daneshpajouh et al. implies a
                                                   characterization of 2-tight graphs. We prove a necessary condition for a
                                                   graph to be k-tight for k ≥ 3 which extends this characterization, and
                                                   we conjecture it to be sufficient. Another result gives a lower bound
                                                   to the chromatic number of the G-stable Kneser graphs when G is a
                                                   disjoint union of cycles. We use this result to give a short proof of the
                                                   fact that 4-regular graphs on 3k vertices consisting of two vertex-disjoint
                                                   cycles and k cycles of length 3 admit an independent set of size k.




                                                                            1. Introduction
                                              Recall that the Kneser graph KG(n, k), where n ≥ 2k ≥ 2, is the graph
                                           whose vertices are the k-subsets of [n] = {1, . . . , n} and whose edges join
                                           disjoint k-subsets. In 1955 Kneser [14] observed that KG(n, k) can be col-
                                           ored with n − 2k + 2 colors, and conjectured that this is optimal. This
                                           became known as Kneser’s conjecture, and was proved by Lovász [15] in
                                           1978 using topological methods. In the same year, Schrijver [19] showed
                                           that the subgraph SG(n, k) induced by the k-sets that are independent in
                                           the n-cycle Cn (with vertices numbered by elements of [n] in cyclic order)
                                           still has chromatic number n − 2k + 2. Schrijver called these graphs stable
                                           Kneser graphs; they are also widely known as Schrijver graphs.
                                              To express the above results within a more general framework, we adopt
                                           the following notion used, e.g., in [16, Section 3.3] or [6]. The Kneser graph
                                           KG(F ) of a hypergraph F has the hyperedges of F as vertices, and has
                                           edges joining disjoint hyperedges. For a graph G and integer k ≥ 2, let
                                           Ik (G) be the hypergraph on the vertex of set of G whose hyperedges are the
                                           independent k-sets of G. We define the G-stable Kneser graph KG(G, k) as
                                                                        KG(G, k) = KG(Ik (G)).
                                              Then SG(n, k) is precisely KG(Cn , k), and KG(n, k) can likewise be ob-
                                           tained as KG(Kn , k), where Kn is the edgeless graph on n vertices. As

another example, choosing G as the (s − 1)-st power of Cn (where s ≥ 3)
yields the s-stable Kneser graphs studied in [17] and in [5, 12].
   It is easy to see that if G′ is a spanning subgraph of G, then KG(G, k) is
an induced subgraph of KG(G′ , k). By taking G′ to be Kn , it follows that for
any n-vertex graph G and for k ≤ n2 , the chromatic number of KG(G, k) is
at most n − 2k + 2. The theorem of Schrijver says that G = Cn achieves this
maximum possible chromatic number. By the above, all spanning subgraphs
of Cn achieve it as well. Are there other n-vertex graphs with this property
for some k?
   This is the main problem we study in this paper. For brevity, we call an
n-vertex graph G k-tight if χ(KG(G, k)) = n − 2k + 2. Thus, Cn and all its
spanning subgraphs are k-tight for any k ≤ n2 . Furthermore, k-tight graphs
on 2k vertices are easy to characterize: they are the graphs whose vertex
set can be partitioned into two independent k-sets, i.e., all subgraphs of the
complete bipartite graph Kk,k . In what follows, we can therefore focus on
k-tight graphs on at least 2k + 1 vertices.
   Our research is inspired by [6] whose subject matter is the chromatic
number of KG(F ) as defined above, in the case where the hypergraph F is
actually a graph G (i.e., its hyperedges have size 2). In our notation, KG(F )
is then just KG(G, 2), where the bar stands for complement. By the above
theorem of Lovász, the chromatic number of this graph is n − 2, and the
main result of [6] is a characterization of the graphs G for which this value
is achieved. It translates to the following characterization of 2-tight graphs:
Theorem 1.1. A graph on at least 5 vertices is 2-tight if an only if it is a
disjoint union of paths and of cycles of length at least 5.
  Our main result proves a corresponding necessary condition for every
k ≥ 2:
Theorem 1.2. For k ≥ 2, if a graph G on at least 2k + 1 vertices contains
a vertex of degree at least 3, or properly contains a cycle of length at most
4k − 4, then G is not k-tight.
  In particular, a k-tight graph is a disjoint union of paths and cycles, and
every cycle it properly contains has length at least 4k−3. We conjecture that
the converse holds as well, which would extend Theorem 1.1 to all k ≥ 2.
Conjecture 1.3. For any k ≥ 2, a graph G on at least 2k + 1 vertices is
k-tight if and only if either G is a cycle, or every component of G is a path
or a cycle of length at least 4k − 3.
  For k = 2, this is Theorem 1.1. For k ≥ 3, the conjecture is consistent
with our computational tests, but even the simplest case not covered by
Schrijver’s theorem, a cycle of length at least 4k−3 together with an isolated
vertex, is open; we were only able to prove it asymptotically.
Theorem 1.4. For all k ≥ 2 there exists n(k) such that for all n ≥ n(k),
the graph Cn−1 ⊔ K1 is k-tight.
  Interestingly, together with Theorem 1.2 this shows that Cn is not the
only maximal k-tight graph: for n ≥ n(k), so is Cn−1 ⊔ K1 , since adding any
edge to it creates a vertex of degree 3.

   Theorem 1.2 shows that a disjoint union of at least two cycles, one of
which has length at most 4k − 4, is not k-tight, but says nothing about how
far χ(KG(G, k)) falls below n − 2k + 2. In Section 4 we bound it from below,
with no restriction on the cycle lengths.
Theorem 1.5. Let c, k, n be integers with c ≥ 1, k ≥ 2 and n ≥ 2k + c − 1.
If G is a disjoint union of c cycles of total length n, then χ(KG(G, k)) ≥
n − 2k + 3 − c.
   The bound is sharp: for each c ≤ k there is a disjoint union of c cycles
attaining it. In Section 5 we use Theorem 1.5 in place of Schrijver’s theorem
in an argument of Aharoni et al. [1] to obtain a short proof of the extension
of a conjecture of Du, Hsu and Hwang [7] to disjoint unions of two cycles.

                           2. Explicit colorings
   This section is devoted to proving Theorem 1.2.
   First, we give two simple statements that allow us to reduce our problem
to subgraphs. For a vertex v of G, the star centered at v is the set of all
vertices of KG(G, k) that contain v. Any two of them meet in v, so a star
is an independent set of KG(G, k); consequently, if the stars at the vertices
of some S ⊆ V (G) cover KG(G, k), then χ(KG(G, k)) ≤ |S|.
Lemma 2.1. If H ⊆ G, then χ(KG(G, k)) ≤ χ(KG(H, k)) + |V (G) \ V (H)|.
Proof. The vertices of KG(G, k), viewed as independent k-sets of G, can be
partitioned into two sets: those containing at least one vertex of V (G) \
V (H), and those contained entirely in H. The first set is covered by the
stars at the vertices of V (G) \ V (H), so it can be colored with |V (G) \ V (H)|
colors. Those in the second set are vertices of KG(H, k), so can be colored
using χ(KG(H, k)) colors.                                                      □
Corollary 2.2. The family of k-tight graphs is closed with respect to sub-
graphs; that is, k-tightness is a monotone property.
   Next, we treat the case in which the maximum degree is larger than 2.
Recall that a claw is a copy of K1,3 ; we call its vertex of degree three the
root, and its three vertices of degree one the leaves.
Lemma 2.3. Let G be the disjoint union of a claw and 2k − 3 isolated
vertices, for k ∈ N. Then χ(KG(G, k)) ≤ 2.
Proof. We partition the vertices of KG(G, k) into the following color classes:
    (1) k-sets containing two of the leaves of the claw,
    (2) all other k-sets.
   Let U, W be vertices of KG(G, k) containing two of the leaves of the claw.
By the pigeonhole principle, U ∩ W ̸= ∅.
   Let U, W be vertices of KG(G, k) containing at most one of the leaves
of the claw. They can only contain one vertex of the claw, as containing
the root is incompatible with containing a leaf. Therefore they must have
k − 1 vertices in the 2k − 3 vertices of G not in the claw. Therefore by the
pigeonhole principle, U ∩ W ̸= ∅.                                           □

Corollary 2.4. If G has n ≥ 2k +1 vertices, and contains a vertex of degree
at least 3, then χ(KG(G, k)) ≤ n − 2k + 1.
Proof. Let H be a subgraph of G containing a vertex of degree at least 3,
three of its neighbors and 2k−3 other vertices. By Lemma 2.3, χ(KG(H, k)) ≤
2. Therefore, by Lemma 2.1,
    χ(KG(G, k)) ≤ 2 + |V (G) \ V (H)| = 2 + n − (2k + 1) = n − 2k + 1,

  In the remainder of this section we focus on the case where G contains
a short cycle. In the first two lemmas the cycle has length at most 2k.
We use the notation [a, b], where a and b are integers, to denote the set
{a, a + 1, . . . , b}.
Lemma 2.5. Let G be the disjoint union of a (2k − 2j − 1)-cycle and 2j + 1
isolated vertices, for some j ∈ [0, k − 2]. Then χ(KG(G, k)) ≤ 1.
Proof. Any independent k-set of G can have at most k − j − 1 vertices in
the cycle and therefore must contain at least j + 1 of the isolated vertices.
By the pigeonhole principle, any two such sets intersect, so KG(G, k) is
edge-free.                                                                 □
Lemma 2.6. Let G be the disjoint union of a (2k − 2j)-cycle and 2j + 1
isolated vertices, for some j ∈ [0, k − 2]. Then χ(KG(G, k)) ≤ 2.
Proof. The two color classes are as follows:
    (1) k-sets containing an odd-labeled vertex of the cycle.
    (2) all other k-sets.
   Any independent set of G has at most k − j vertices in the cycle. A
set with fewer vertices on the cycle contains at least j + 1 of the isolated
vertices, so by the pigeonhole principle, any two such sets must intersect.
Any independent k-set that contains k − j vertices in the cycle and an
odd-labeled vertex must contain all odd vertices of the cycle, therefore it
intersects with all vertices in the color class.
   For the second class, suppose for a contradiction that U and W are disjoint
independent k-sets containing no odd-labeled vertex of the cycle. The union
U ∪W contains at most k −j vertices of the cycle, and at most 2j +1 isolated
vertices, so 2k = |U | + |W | ≤ (k − j) + (2j + 1) = k + j + 1, contradicting
j ≤ k + 2 and completing the proof.                                         □

Corollary 2.14. Let k ≥ 2 and let G be a graph on n ≥ 2k + 1 vertices. If
G contains a cycle of length at most 4k − 4 and G is not itself a cycle, then
χ(KG(G, k)) ≤ n − 2k + 1.

then by Lemma 2.6 we find χ(KG(H, k)) = 2 = |V (H)| − 2k + 1. If t is odd
then by Lemma 2.5 we get χ(KG(H, k)) = 1 = |V (H)| − 2k + 1. Hence in
either case H is not k-tight.

tells us that H is not k-tight.
   Thus in all cases, Corollary 2.2 confirms that G is not k-tight, as required.

    Theorem 1.2 now follows from Corollaries 2.4 and 2.14.

                           5. Cycles plus triangles
   A cycle-plus-triangles graph is a 4-regular multigraph on 3k vertices whose
edge set can be decomposed into a Hamiltonian cycle and k vertex-disjoint
triangles. Du, Hsu and Hwang [7] conjectured that every cycle-plus-triangles
graph has an independent set of size k. Erdős made the stronger conjecture
that such graph are 3-colorable: its vertices can be partitioned into three
independent k-sets.
   Erdős’s conjecture was proved by Fleischner and Stiebitz [8] via one of
the first applications of the Alon–Tarsi polynomial method [4]. Soon there-
after an elementary—though by no means simple—inductive proof of Erdős’s
conjecture was found by Sachs [18]. The original conjecture of Du, Hsu and
Hwang is an almost immediate corollary of [2] (see e.g. the survey [10],
Thm 4.14), in which the single cycle can be replaced by any disjoint union
of cycles whose lengths are all divisible by three. The following stronger
result for unions of cycles was shown by Aharoni, Holzman, Howard and
Sprüssel [3].
Theorem 5.1 (Aharoni, Holzman, Howard and Sprüssel [3]). Let G be the
disjoint union of cycles of total length 3k, at most two of which have length
1 (mod 3), and k vertex-disjoint triangles on the same vertex set. Then G
has an independent set of size k.
   Haxell and Wdowinski [11] showed that this is best possible: they con-
structed, for infinitely many k, a union of three cycles of length 1 (mod 3)
and total length 3k together with k vertex-disjoint triangles on the same
vertex set, having no independent set of size k.
   A second proof of the Du–Hsu–Hwang conjecture was found by Aharoni
et al. [1], using a clever application of Schrijver’s theorem. In fact their
argument showed the existence of two disjoint independent k-sets (instead
of the three disjoint independent k-sets given by [8, 18]).
   As an application of Theorem 1.5, we give a short proof of the following
weakening of Theorem 5.1. The argument is exactly that of Aharoni et
al. [1], with Theorem 1.5 in place of Schrijver’s theorem.
Corollary 5.2. Let G be the disjoint union of at most two cycles of total
length 3k and k vertex-disjoint triangles on the same vertex set. Then G
has an independent set of size k.
            THE CHROMATIC NUMBER OF G-STABLE KNESER GRAPHS                            13

Proof. Let H be the spanning subgraph of G consisting of c ≤ 2 cycles,
and let T1 , T2 , . . . , Tk be the vertex sets of the triangles of G. For each
vertex A of KG(H, k), color A with color i if |A ∩ Ti | ≥ 2; otherwise leave A
uncolored. This defines a k-coloring of an induced subgraph of KG(H, k).
By Theorem 1.5, χ(KG(H, k)) ≥ 3k−2k+3−c ≥ k+1, so at least one vertex
A∗ of KG(H, k) is uncolored. This means |A∗ ∩ Ti | ≤ 1 for every i ∈ [k], and
therefore |A∗ ∩ Ti | = 1 for every i ∈ [k]. Hence, A∗ is an independent k-set
in G.                                                                        □
   Note that if H consists of more than one cycle, it cannot be k-tight: one
of the cycles has length at most 3k/2 ≤ 4k − 4 (since k ≥ 2). So the lower
bound of k + 1 in the above proof is optimal for H consisting of two cycles.
   If H consists of a single cycle (as in the original argument of Aharoni et
al. [1]), Schrijver’s theorem gives the stronger bound χ(KG(H, k)) ≥ k + 2,
and the uncolored subgraph of KG(H, k) contains at least one edge. This
leads to the stronger conclusion mentioned above, namely, that there are
two disjoint independent k-sets.
   If H consists of three or more cycles, the bound coming from Theorem 1.5
is too weak to guarantee the existence of any uncolored vertices (and there-
fore, of any independent k-set). This is consistent with Haxell and Wdowin-
ski’s construction [11].
