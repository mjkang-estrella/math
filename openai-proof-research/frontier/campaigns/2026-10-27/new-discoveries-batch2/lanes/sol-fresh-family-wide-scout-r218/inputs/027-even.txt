                                                                                 1. Introduction
                                            The cardinality of the image of a finite point set under projection reflects the co-
                                         incidences created by the projection direction. In the plane, this quantity is also the
                                         number of occupied lines in a parallel family. Questions about small projection images
                                         and about the range of possible image sizes have been studied from several perspectives.
                                         Skiena [Ski90] investigated the number of directions admitting a projection with at most
                                         a prescribed number of distinct images, while Charlton et al. [CDD+ 12] studied consec-
                                         utive ranges of projection cardinalities attainable by a single planar set. In this paper
                                         we consider a simultaneous parity condition: can every one-dimensional projection of a
                                         nonempty finite point set have even cardinality?
                                            The question motivating this work, due to Juan Benigno Seoane-Sepúlveda and pre-
                                         sented by Francisco Santos at an open problems session at the conference Combinatorics
                                         and Geometry in Mytilene 2026, is whether every nonempty finite point set in R3 has a
                                         one-dimensional projection of odd cardinality. We investigate this question through the
                                         geometric restrictions imposed by requiring every such projection to have even cardinality.
                                            By a direction in Rd we mean an element ℓ of the real projective space P(Rd ), the set
                                         of all one-dimensional linear subspaces of Rd . Meaning, for every u ∈ Rd \ {0}, we have

span(u1 ) = span(u2 ) = ℓ, and u2 = −u1 .
   Let S ⊂ Rd be a non-empty finite set of points. Given a direction ℓ ∈ P(Rd ), choose
an arbitrary u ∈ Rd \ {0} such that ℓ = span(u), and define

This is a well-defined positive integer, since replacing u by a non-zero scalar multiple of it

ℓ that meet S. We call S all-even if mS (ℓ) is even for all ℓ ∈ P(Rd ).
   For the case d = 3, a direction ℓ ∈ P(R3 ) such that mS (ℓ) is odd is called an odd
plane direction for S. We formulate Seoane-Sepúlveda’s question as follows:

Question 1.1 (Seoane-Sepúlveda). Is it true that every finite nonempty set S ⊂ R3 has
an odd plane direction?

  We conjecture that the answer is positive. In other words:

Conjecture 1.2 (weak conjecture). There exists no all-even set in R3 : For every finite
nonempty set S ⊂ R3 there exists a direction ℓ ∈ P(R3 ) with mS (ℓ) odd.

   Clearly, if |S| is odd, then a generic direction is an odd plane direction for S. Also, if S
is coplanar, then a normal direction to the plane containing S is an odd plane direction
for S. Furthermore, for S to be all-even, it cannot be centrally symmetric: Suppose S
is centrally symmetric around the origin, and let π be a plane containing the origin and
at least one point of S. Then S \ π consists of two symmetric parts, so an even number
of additional planes parallel to π are required to cover S. Hence, mS (ℓ) is odd for the
direction ℓ orthogonal to π.
   Thus, any all-even set in R3 , if it exists, must have even size, it must affinely span R3 ,
and it cannot be centrally symmetric.
   The same question can be asked in any dimension. Moreover, if the answer is yes in
R , then the answer is yes in Rk for every k ≥ d. Indeed, let S ⊂ Rk be finite, and

               2. line-all-even sets and a stronger conjecture
  Let S ⊂ Rd be a nonempty finite point set. For a direction ℓ ∈ P(Rd ), let

                                               πℓ : Rd → Rd /ℓ

be the quotient map, and define

                                               nS (ℓ) = |πℓ (S)|.

Equivalently, nS (ℓ) is the number of affine lines parallel to ℓ that meet S. We say that ℓ
is a secant direction of S if it is determined by a pair of points of S. A direction ℓ such
                                                        3
that nS (ℓ) is odd is called an odd line direction for S. We call S line-all-even if it
has no odd line direction. Clearly, if |S| is odd, then every non-secant direction of S is an
odd line direction. Hence, every line-all-even set in Rd must have even size. Furthermore,
every all-even set in R2 , when considered as a subset of Rd , is clearly a line-all-even set.
  We conjecture the following in dimension 3:
Conjecture 2.1 (strong conjecture). Let S ⊂ R3 be a finite set that affinely spans R3 .
Then there exists a direction ℓ ∈ P(R3 ) such that nS (ℓ) is odd.
  The strong conjecture implies the weak conjecture by the following observation, which
Santos also presented at the same open problems session:
Proposition 2.2. Let S ⊂ R3 be finite. If S has an odd line direction, then it has an
odd plane direction. In other words, if S is all-even then it is line-all-even.
Proof. Let ℓ ∈ P(R3 ) be a direction such that nS (ℓ) is odd. Choose a non-zero vector
u ∈ ℓ⊥ generically, so that distinct affine lines parallel to ℓ and meeting S lie in distinct
affine planes perpendicular to u. Then these planes are exactly the occupied u-levels of
S, hence mS (u) = nS (ℓ), and u determines an odd plane direction for S.                   □
   Since any all-even set in R3 must affinely span R3 , the strong conjecture indeed implies
the weak conjecture. Hence, a proof of the strong conjecture would give a positive answer
to Seoane-Sepúlveda’s original question in R3 .
   As in the case of the weak conjecture, the validity of the strong conjecture can be
asked in any dimension, and, if the answer is yes in Rd , then the answer is yes in Rk
for every k ≥ d ≥ 2. Indeed, let S ⊂ Rk be finite, of even size, that affinely spans Rk .
Choose a generic surjection π : Rk → Rd , that is injective on S, and which creates no new
parallelisms among the secants of S. Such a map exists by choosing ker(π) to avoid the
finitely many secant lines and the finitely many 2-planes spanned by pairs of nonparallel
secants. By the assumed result in Rd , applied to π(S), there exists a direction ℓ such
that nπ(S) (ℓ) is odd. Since |S| is even, ℓ must be a secant direction determined by some
π(p), π(q) ∈ π(S). By the fact that no new parallelisms were created by π we deduce
that
                                       nS (L) = nπ(S) (ℓ)
for L = span(q − p) ⊂ Rk .

                 3. Height-rigidity and the strong conjecture
  The purpose of this section is to relate the strong conjecture to a rigidity property
of planar all-even sets. Let us first note how the terminology from Sections 2 and 1
specializes to the plane. Let A ⊂ R2 be finite. Then nA (ℓ) is the number of affine lines
parallel to ℓ that meet A. In dimension two it is also equivalent to the hyperplane counting
function. Indeed, if L ∈ P(R2 ) is a direction and ℓ = L⊥ , then the affine hyperplanes
perpendicular to L are exactly the affine lines parallel to ℓ. Hence
                                      mA (L) = nA (ℓ).
                                              4
Thus, for finite subsets of R2 , the all-even and line-all-even conditions coincide. We shall
therefore simply say that A is all-even if nA (ℓ) is even for every direction ℓ ∈ P(R2 ).
Notice that every all-even set has even cardinality, since for a generic direction ℓ no two
points of A lie on the same affine line parallel to ℓ, and therefore nA (ℓ) = |A|.

3.2. The rigidity reduction. We first record the generic projection fact that will be
used in the reduction.

Lemma 3.2. Let S ⊂ R3 be finite. There exists a surjective linear map π : R3 → R2 such
that π is injective on S and creates no new parallelisms among secants of S. Namely,
whenever p, q, r, s ∈ S, p ̸= q, r ̸= s, and
                                π(q) − π(p) ∥ π(s) − π(r),
we already have
                                       q − p ∥ s − r.

Proof. Choose the kernel of π to be a line K ⊂ R3 . We need to avoid finitely many bad
choices of K.
  First, K should contain no non-zero secant vector q − p with p, q ∈ S. This guarantees
that π is injective on S.
  Second, if q − p and s − r are non-parallel secant vectors of S, then K should not lie in
the plane span(q − p, s − r). There are only finitely many such planes, so a generic line
K avoids all of them.
  Now suppose that
                                π(q) − π(p) ∥ π(s) − π(r).
Then some non-trivial linear combination of q − p and s − r lies in K. If q − p and s − r
were not parallel, then K would lie in span(q − p, s − r), contrary to the choice of K.
Hence q − p ∥ s − r.                                                                   □

Theorem 3.3 (Rigidity reduction). The strong conjecture in R3 (Conjecture 2.1) is
equivalent to the statement: every all-even set A ⊂ R2 is height-rigid.

Proof. Suppose first that there exists an all-even set A ⊂ R2 that is not height-rigid.
Then A has an admissible height function z such that A     ez affinely spans R3 . But Lemma
3.1 implies that A ez is line-all-even, contradicting Conjecture 2.1.
  For the other direction, suppose that every all-even set in R2 is height-rigid. Suppose,
contrary to Conjecture 2.1, that there exists a line-all-even set Â ⊂ R3 that affinely spans
R3 . Let π be the map guaranteed by Lemma 3.2. By applying a shear parallel to the
xy-plane, we can assume without loss of generality that that π is in fact an orthogonal
projection (since affinities preserve parallelisms as well as the line-all-even property). In
other words, π maps every point (ax , ay , az ) ∈ Â into (ax , ay ) ∈ A ⊂ R2 . But then,
the height function z : A → R that provides back the z-coordinate az of every point
(ax , ay ) ∈ A, is an admissible height function, contradicting the assumption that A is
height-rigid.                                                                              □

               4. The rigidity matrix: an algebraic formulation
  The definition of height-rigidity can be expressed in algebraic terms. Let
                                  A = {a1 , . . . , an } ⊂ R2
                                               6
be finite and non-collinear, and let e1 , . . . , en denote the standard basis of Rn . The
height-rigidity matrix R(A) has a column for each point of A, and has a row for each
parallel pair of secants. That is, whenever we have two parallel secants {i, j} and {k, l}
satisfying
                                   aj − ai = λ(al − ak ),
we include the row
                                     ej − ei − λel + λek .
The secants may share a point. Their orientations and order affect the row only by a
non-zero scalar. Repeated rows are retained.
  Considering a height function on A as a vector z = (z1 , . . . , zn ) ∈ Rn , the equation
                                         R(A) · z = 0
is precisely the condition that z be admissible. In other words, the kernel of R(A) is the
space of admissible height functions. Writing xA , yA for the coordinate vectors of A, the
kernel always contains the 3-dimensional space of affine heights
                                span (1, xA , yA ) ⊆ ker R(A).
Thus, we have
                        A is height-rigid    ⇐⇒      dim ker R(A) = 3                       (2)
and, more generally, the dimension of the space of admissible heights modulo affine heights
is n − 3 − rank R(A).
   Height-rigidity is a special case of affine rigidity in the sense of Gortler et al. [GGLT13].
Associate to A the hypergraph whose hyperedges are its collinear triples and its non-
collinear four-point subsets formed by two parallel secants. A height function is admissible
precisely when its restriction to each hyperedge is the restriction of an affine function,
which may depend on the hyperedge. The affine dependencies on these hyperedges span
the row space of R(A); relations supported on four collinear points are already generated
by the triple relations. Thus R(A) has the same row space as a strong affinity matrix
for this realised hypergraph, and the criterion dim ker R(A) = 3 is the corresponding
affine-rigidity criterion [GGLT13, Theorem 1].

  The rigidity matrix records enough information to determine whether A is all-even:

Proposition 4.3 (Combinatorial reconstruction). The row space of R(A) determines,
up to relabeling the points, the secant-direction blocks and the occupied-line partition for
each block. In particular, it determines the counts nA (ℓ) and whether A is all-even.

Proof. Put W = row R(A). For distinct secants {i, j} and {k, l} and any λ ̸= 0, we have

                 aj − ai = λ(al − ak )    ⇐⇒       ej − ei − λ(el − ek ) ∈ W.

The forward implication follows from the definition of R(A). The reverse follows because
every vector in W annihilates xA and yA .
   This criterion recovers the blocks Eℓ (A). For each block, the connected components of
the graph ([n], Eℓ (A)), including isolated vertices, are precisely the occupied lines parallel
to ℓ. Their numbers and sizes give the claimed data; non-secant directions have n occupied
lines.                                                                                       □

Remark 4.4. Different direction blocks may contain proportional rows. For example, a
parallelogram relation

gives the same row, up to scaling, for both ap ar ∥ aq as and ap as ∥ aq ar . Thus the blocks
classify the generating secants, not disjoint sets of numerical row values.

Proposition 4.5 (Affine reconstruction). If A is height-rigid, the row space of R(A)
determines A up to affine equivalence and relabelling.
                                               8
Proof. Choose a basis {1, x, y} of ker R(A). Since {1, xA , yA } is another basis, the points

   Both reconstruction results survive row reduction. The height-rigidity assumption is
essential for the second: when dim ker R(A) > 3, the original subspace of affine heights
is not, in general, determined by the row space alone.

Remark 4.6. By the rigidity reduction, the strong conjecture is equivalent to rank R(A) =
n − 3 for every non-collinear planar all-even set A. Corollary 4.2 translates all-evenness
into parity conditions on the directional ranks. These ranks cannot simply be added,
since the directional row spaces may intersect. The remaining problem is to establish the
required global rank.


                               5. Contagion strategies
   We describe several propagation rules that provide sufficient conditions for height-
rigidity. Throughout this section, A ⊂ R2 is finite and non-collinear, and n = |A|. The
strategies apply whether or not A is all-even.

5.1. Contagious triangles. Let T = {a, b, c} ⊆ A be a non-collinear triple. Start with
the partition of A in which T is one class and the remaining points are singleton sets.
Repeatedly merge the classes containing x, y whenever xy ∥ pq, for some distinct points
p, q belonging to the same class. The secants xy, pq may share an endpoint. The process
terminates at a partition independent of the order of the merges. We call T a contagious
triangle if the terminal partition consists of one class.

Lemma 5.1. If A has a contagious triangle, then A is height-rigid.

Proof. Let z : A → R be admissible, let f be the affine function agreeing with z on
T , and put h = z − f . Then h is admissible and vanishes on T . If h(p) = h(q) and
y − x = λ(q − p), admissibility gives

                            h(y) − h(x) = λ (h(q) − h(p)) = 0.

Thus h is constant on every class throughout the process. If T is contagious, h vanishes
on all of A, so z = f .                                                               □

  A triangle T = {a1 , a2 , a3 } is called monotonically contagious if the remaining
points can be ordered as a4 , . . . , an so that, for every r > 3,

                     ar − ak ∥ aj − ai     for some i, j, k < r,   i ̸= j.

Equivalently, starting with U = T , one can repeatedly add a point y ∈ A \ U for which
xy ∥ pq, with x, p, q ∈ U and p ̸= q. This only uses merges that attach a singleton to the
class containing T . Consequently, every monotonically contagious triangle is contagious.

                      6. Constructions of all-even sets in R2
  Let Pn = {(cos(2πi/n), sin(2πi/n)) | 0 ≤ i < n} denote the set of vertices of a regular
n-gon. As Seoane-Sepúlveda showed, the set P15 \ P3 is all-even.
  In this section we present several other all-even sets, including some infinite families.
The constructions presented here are of two types:
     • Subsets of a regular n-gon Pn . We call such sets cyclotomic.
     • Subsets of the integer grid Z2 .
  Regular polygons also arise in the study of planar point sets determining few directions.
Ungar [Ung82] proved that a non-collinear set of n planar points determines at least
2⌊n/2⌋ directions. Among n-point sets with no three points collinear, Jamison [Jam86]
characterised those determining exactly n directions as the affine images of the vertices
of a regular n-gon. Pilatte [Pil20] proved that, for n ≥ 7, a set of n planar points
with no three collinear that determines exactly n + 1 directions is affinely equivalent to
the vertex set of a regular (n + 1)-gon with one vertex removed. These results concern
the total number of secant directions, whereas all-evenness constrains the parity of the
occupied-line count in every direction.

                                   8. A six-line theorem
  In this section we prove the following:

Theorem 8.1. Let A ⊂ R2 be all-even, and suppose there exists a direction ℓ ∈ P(R2 )
for which nA (ℓ) ≤ 6. Then A is height-rigid.

   Hence, if there exists any all-even set A ⊂ R2 that is not height-rigid, it must satisfy
nA (ℓ) ≥ 8 for all directions ℓ. Therefore, we must have |A| ≥ 10: If |A| ≤ 8 then a secant
direction ℓ would yield nA (ℓ) < 8, and thus nA (ℓ) ≤ 6.
   The rest of this section proves Theorem 8.1.

                    9. Concluding remarks and open problems
  By Theorem 3.3, the strong conjecture (Conjecture 2.1) is equivalent to the assertion
that every planar all-even set is height-rigid. The results above suggest several ways to
approach this assertion: contagion from small rigid subsets, analysis of the rigidity matrix,
and restrictions arising from configurations supported on few parallel lines. We conclude
with questions prompted by these approaches and by the constructions of Section 6.

Contagion and height-rigidity. The finite examples and the explicitly analysed infi-
nite families of Section 6.3 admit monotonically contagious triangles, and hence satisfy
the strongest condition in the hierarchy of Section 5. This suggests the following strength-
ening of the strong conjecture.

Conjecture 9.1. Every planar all-even set A has a monotonically contagious triangle.

   An affirmative answer would show that every planar all-even set admits a particu-
larly simple certificate of height-rigidity: starting from three non-collinear points, the
                                                25
remaining points can be added one at a time using parallelisms with previously deter-
mined secants. By Theorem 3.3 and Proposition 2.2, this would also settle the strong
conjecture and answer Seoane-Sepúlveda’s original question.
  The hierarchy provides weaker possible sufficient conditions for the strong conjecture.
One may ask whether every planar all-even set satisfies C, or, more generally, whether
there is an absolute integer k ≥ 3 such that every such set satisfies Pk . The latter
asks whether height-rigidity can always be certified by propagation through rigid subsets
of uniformly bounded size. The separation examples in Section 5.4 show that these
distinctions matter for general planar point sets. It remains open whether any of these
separations can occur among all-even sets.

The algebraic approach. Section 4 expresses both all-evenness and height-rigidity in
terms of the rigidity matrix. For an all-even set A of size n, every secant-direction block
Rℓ (A) has odd rank, while height-rigidity is equivalent to
                                   rank R(A) = n − 3.
The difficulty is to pass from the parity of the individual directional ranks to this global
rank statement. The directional row spaces may intersect, so their dimensions cannot
simply be added. A successful argument must therefore use the compatibility imposed
by their common geometric origin.
   This suggests studying how the parallelism relations of a planar configuration con-
strain the dependencies between its directional row spaces. The reconstruction results
of Section 4 show that the full row space retains the secant-direction blocks and the
occupied-line partitions. Thus the algebraic formulation preserves the data defining all-
evenness; the remaining task is to understand why those data should force the space of
admissible heights to consist only of affine functions. A direct argument of this kind could
establish the strong conjecture without resolving the stronger contagion questions.

Constructions and contagion. The cyclotomic and integral classes considered in Sec-
tion 6 provide natural settings in which to study the rigidity conjectures. Establishing
height-rigidity for every cyclotomic all-even set, or for every integral all-even set, would
already give substantial special cases of the strong conjecture. In each class, one may
also ask whether the stronger property M always holds.
   The repetition construction raises a more specific question. Lemma 6.2 shows that
repeating the binary word of a cyclotomic all-even set 4k + 1 times preserves all-evenness.
Does this operation also preserve M whenever the original set satisfies M ? Lemma 6.5
establishes this conclusion under the additional hypothesis that the original set has an
auxiliary-contagious pair; in fact, that hypothesis ensures M after any repetition with at
least two copies. The remaining question is whether the auxiliary-pair hypothesis can be
replaced by M for the all-even repetitions considered here.

Beyond six parallel lines. Theorem 8.1 establishes height-rigidity when an all-even set
is supported on at most six parallel lines, with no bound on its cardinality. Consequently,
                                             26
every planar all-even set A that is not height-rigid must satisfy
                               nA (ℓ) ≥ 8      for every ℓ ∈ P(R2 ).

Problem 9.2. Is every planar all-even set A satisfying nA (ℓ) ≤ 8 for some direction ℓ
height-rigid? More generally, extend Theorem 8.1 to larger even bounds on the number
of occupied parallel lines.

   The proof of Theorem 8.1 combines information about the projected configuration
with restrictions on the point sets carried by the parallel lines of an admissible lift. A
singly-determined direction in the projected configuration is immediately excluded. For
six non-collinear projected points, Appendix A reduces the remaining possibilities to two
affine types, which are then treated using extremal points and polynomial identities.
   At eight projected points, a new feature appears: the configurations with no singly-
determined direction already include planar all-even sets, as the eight-point examples of
Section 6 demonstrate. The aim is to identify conditions on the point sets carried by
the parallel lines that force the lifted set to be coplanar, preferably without a separate
analysis of every projected configuration.
   The encoding by real-exponent polynomials over F2 in Section 8.2 offers one possible
ingredient. It translates parity constraints into identities in an integral domain, allowing
cancellation. The pair-count identities used there rely on the absence of collinear triples in
the projected configuration. Extending the method to configurations with collinear triples
therefore requires an encoding that accounts for the contribution of lines containing more
than two points. Such an extension may help treat larger projected configurations. An
extension of Theorem 8.1 to every even bound would settle the strong conjecture, since
for each all-even set A one may take the bound to be |A|.

without a vertex.


Proof. It is easily verified that G is a realization of sequence 4, with (a, b, c, d, e, f ) =
(p5 , p4 , p6 , p3 , p2 , p1 ). We now show that sequence 4 has a unique realization.
   Without loss of generality let a = (0, 0), b = (0, 1), c = (1, 0). Then relations
[ab][cd]ef, e[df]b[ac], [ef]d[bc]a yield d = (1, y), e = (z, y − z + x), f = (x, y)
for some x, y, z. Then relations b[ad][ce]f, [bd][ae][cf], d[be][af]c, [de][bf]ac
yield the equations

                                        y(z − 1) = y − z + x,
                                        (y − 1)z = y − z + x,
                               (y − 1)(x − 1) = y,
                            (y − z + x − 1)x = yz,
                                       (x − z)x = (y − 1)(z − 1).
