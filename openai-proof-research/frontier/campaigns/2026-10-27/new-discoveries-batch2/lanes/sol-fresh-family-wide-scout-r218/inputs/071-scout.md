## Ranked next-investment shortlist

1. **Richardson Conjecture 5.1 (1999), reciprocal binomial Hankel integrality.** [Primary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/9905079), printed p5. For every positive n,r, let b_k=binomial(k+r−1,r), R_n=(1/b_(i+j−1))_(1≤i,j≤n). The claim is R_n^−1 integral iff, for every prime power q dividing r, n≡0 or1 mod q. Equivalently it suffices to impose the maximal prime powers in r. Closest actual theorem: the same source's Theorem 5.2 supplies an inverse for indeterminate r; its proof uses double-sum telescoping. Route: simplify that inverse to products whose p-adic denominators can be classified uniformly; necessity should first be attacked at a corner entry. **Decisive next calculation:** derive a closed formula for the last diagonal/corner entries, factor their denominators symbolically for arbitrary n,r, and see whether these alone force the stated local alternatives. **Objection:** 2001 Fibonacci Quarterly version and 25 years of citing literature remain unexamined; a standard beta-moment/Hankel inverse theorem might subsume the result. No routine exclusion is asserted without that bridge. Search for reciprocal Hankel/Fibonomial returned the primary but is incomplete.

2. **Tyszka Conjecture 3, arbitrary additive subgroup (2009 latest body).** [Primary v86](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0702558), printed p21. For EVERY additive subgroup G⊆C and EVERY consistent finite system in n variables of equations xi=1 or xi+xj=xk, a solution exists in (G∩Q)^n with |xi|≤2^(n−1). Closest proof: Theorem 11 and its Cramer/Hadamard argument give a rational solution bounded by sqrt(5)^(n−1) when the group contains Q. The complete determinant bridge below improves that to 2^(n−1) in the Q-containing case, which is therefore **not** a fresh candidate. The surviving full source question is the arbitrary-G quantifier. **Route:** analyze the subgroup of rational denominators forced by a consistent original solution using Smith normal form, then choose a bounded solution preserving that denominator subgroup. **Decisive next calculation:** formulate and prove, or find a concrete failure of, a bounded integral representative theorem after localizing at precisely those denominators in G∩Q. **Objection:** the rational projection must land in G; Cramer division alone does not. Related 0807.3010 has overlapping conjectures and was obtained/read; full later literature unresolved. Do not count the Q-containing subcase or determinant lemma.

3. **Akman division-graph completeness (2006).** [Primary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0612618), abstract and Section4 pp17–20. Definition2.3: D(G) is the disjoint union of unramified splitting-type graphs, one per conjugacy class of cyclic decomposition subgroups, on the entire intermediate-subgroup diagram. Closest actual proof: Lagarias Theorem2.2 identifies components with divisions; Theorem4.1 recovers the subgroup lattice, conjugacy of cyclic subgroups, normalizers, center, abelian structure and more. **Route:** seek a full finite counterexample among nonisomorphic groups already sharing subgroup lattices and marks, then compare the entire splitting refinement, including incidences between subgroup levels. **Decisive next calculation:** derive a canonical double-coset/orbit-incidence encoding from Proposition2.4 and test one known same-mark group pair, rather than enumerate arbitrary groups. **Objection:** equal tables of marks or equal element orders do not establish equal D(G); color conventions require exact care, and external prior work on this invariant is not cleared. No counterexample is claimed.

4. **Tyszka Conjecture 5, simultaneous additive error compression.** [Primary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0702558), printed p25. For each x∈R^n (respectively C^n), find y in the SAME field with |yi|≤2^(n−1), |yi−1|≤|xi−1|, and |yi+yj−yk|≤|xi+xj−xk| for ALL i,j,k. ## Complete routine determinant bridge for #6

Put d=n−1 and delete any column. Before deletion, every allowed row of B is e_i, 2e_i−e_j, or e_i+e_j−e_k with indicated distinct nonzero positions. Split these respectively into one incidence row, e_i+(e_i−e_j), and e_i+(e_j−e_k). After deleting the same column each summand still has at most one+1 and one−1. Expand the determinant multilinearly by rows: at most2^d terms occur. Each term has determinant0,+1 or−1. Here is a complete proof of that last fact: transpose the matrix; each column has at most two nonzeros, and if two, their signs oppose. If every column has two nonzeros, the sum of the rows is zero, so the determinant vanishes. Otherwise expand along a zero/singleton column, and induct on d. Therefore the original determinant's modulus is at most2^d, for all n and every deleted column, exactly Conjecture4. This is a full classical incidence/unimodularity bridge, not exclusion from shortness. Applying it in the actual Cramer argument of Theorem11 resolves the2^(n−1) bound for G⊇Q, but does not force the Cramer solution into an arbitrary G. Neither receives novelty credit.
