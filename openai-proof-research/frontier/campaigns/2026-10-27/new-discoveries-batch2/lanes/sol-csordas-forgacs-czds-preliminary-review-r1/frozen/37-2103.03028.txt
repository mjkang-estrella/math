                                          1     Introduction
                                          Our topic involves the q-Onsager algebra Oq [2, 25]. This infinite-dimensional associative
                                          algebra is presented by two generators W0 , W1 and two relations called the q-Dolan/Grady
                                          relations; see Definition 9.1 below.
                                          The algebra Oq first appeared in algebraic combinatorics, in the study of Q-polynomial
                                          distance-regular graphs; to our knowledge the q-Dolan/Grady relations first appeared in [24,
                                          Lemma 5.4]. The algebra Oq belongs to a family of algebras called tridiagonal algebras
                                          [25, Definition 3.9]. One can think of Oq as the “most general” tridiagonal algebra [19,
                                          Section 1.2]. There is an object in linear algebra called a tridiagonal pair [16, Definition 1.1].
                                          A finite-dimensional irreducible Oq -module is essentially the same thing as a tridiagonal pair
                                          of q-Racah type [25, Theorem 3.10]. These tridiagonal pairs are classified up to isomorphism
                                          in [18, Theorem 3.3]. See [15] for a survey about Oq and tridiagonal pairs.
                                          Over the past two decades the algebra Oq has appeared in a wide variety of contexts. For
                                          instance, Oq is used to study boundary integrable systems [1–8, 10]. The algebra Oq can

The results listed above show that Oq is related to Aq in the same way that Uq+ is related
to the alternating central extension of Uq+ [31], [32]. For this reason, we propose to call Aq

We believe that Aq is a profound discovery and worthy of much further study. In the future

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 contains some preliminaries and the definition
of Aq . In Section 3 the algebra Aq is described using generating functions. The results of
Section 3 are used in Sections 4, 5 to obtain some equations called reduction rules. In Section
6 we use the reduction rules and the Bergman diamond lemma to show that the generators
(1) give a PBW basis for Aq . In Section 7 we obtain a filtration of Aq that will help us
understand how Aq is related to Oq . In Section 8 we obtain some central elements {Zn }∞     n=1
in Aq . The results of Sections 6, 7, 8 are used in Sections 9, 10 to describe how Aq is related
to Oq . In Appendix A we list many identities that hold in Aq . Appendix B contains some
data and examples that are meant to clarify Section 8. Appendix C contains some details
from our application of the Bergman diamond lemma.


2     The algebra Aq
We now begin our formal argument. Throughout the paper, the following notational con-
ventions are in effect. Recall the natural numbers N = {0, 1, 2, . . .} and integers Z =
{0, ±1, ±2, . . .}. Let F denote a field. Every vector space and tensor product discussed
in this paper is over F. Every algebra discussed in this paper is associative, over F, and
has a multiplicative identity. Let A denote an algebra. By an automorphism of A we mean
an algebra isomorphism A → A. The algebra Aopp consists of the vector space A and the
multiplication map A × A → A, (a, b) → ba. By an antiautomorphism of A we mean an
algebra isomorphism A → Aopp .

Definition 2.1. (See [14, p. 299].) Let A denote an algebra. A Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt (or
PBW) basis for A consists of a subset Ω ⊆ A and a linear order < on Ω such that the
following is a basis for the vector space A:

           a1 a2 · · · an     n ∈ N,        a1 , a2 , . . . , an ∈ Ω,      a1 ≤ a2 ≤ · · · ≤ an .

We interpret the empty product as the multiplicative identity in A.
Definition 2.2. A grading of an P algebra A is a sequence {An }n∈N of subspaces of A such
that (i) 1 ∈ A0 ; (ii) the sum A = n∈N An is direct; (iii) Ar As ⊆ Ar+s for r, s ∈ N.
Definition 2.3. (See [13, p. 202].) A filtration of an algebra A is a sequence {An }n∈N of
subspaces of A such that (i) 1 ∈ A0 ; (ii) An−1 ⊆ An for n ≥ 1; (iii) A = ∪n∈N An ; (iv)
Ar As ⊆ Ar+s for r, s ∈ N.

We will prove Theorem 6.1 after a brief discussion.
We have some comments about the linear order < in the theorem statement. By Proposition

               Gi+1 < Gj+1 ,      W−i < W−j ,            Wi+1 < Wj+1 ,       G̃i+1 < G̃j+1

We discuss the notion of an Aq -word. By an Aq -letter we mean an alternating generator
for Aq . For n ∈ N, by an Aq -word of length n we mean a sequence of Aq -letters a1 a2 · · · an .
We interpret the Aq -word of length zero to be the multiplicative identity 1 ∈ Aq . An Aq -
word a1 a2 · · · an is called reducible whenever there exists an integer i (2 ≤ i ≤ n) such
that ai−1 > ai . An Aq -word is called irreducible whenever it is not reducible. An Aq -word
a1 a2 · · · an is irreducible if and only if a1 ≤ a2 ≤ · · · ≤ an . Theorem 6.1 asserts that the
irreducible Aq -words give a basis for the vector space Aq .

Next we define a partial order  on the set of Aq -words. Let w, w ′ denote Aq -words, and write
w = a1 a2 · · · an . We say that w dominates w ′ whenever there exists an integer i (2 ≤ i ≤ n)
such that ai−1 > ai , and w ′ is obtained from w by replacing ai−1 ai by one of its descendents.
In this case, either (i) w ′ has length n − 1; or (ii) w ′ has length n and wt(w ′ ) < wt(w). By
these comments the transitive closure of the domination relation is a partial order on the set
of Aq -words; we denote this partial order by .
Proof of Theorem 6.1. We will use the Bergman diamond lemma [11, Theorem 1.2]. We
refer to the above partial order  on the set of Aq -words. By construction  is a semigroup
partial order [11, p. 181] and satisfies the descending chain condition [11, p. 179]. We show
that the reduction rules are compatible with  in the sense of Bergman [11, p. 181]. Let
w = a1 a2 · · · an denote a reducible Aq -word. There exists an integer i (2 ≤ i ≤ n) such that
ai−1 > ai . By construction ai−1 ai is a reducible Aq -word of length 2. Using the corresponding
reduction rule we eliminate ai−1 ai from w, and thereby express w as a linear combination of
Aq -words, each ≺ w. Therefore, the reduction rules are compatible with  in the sense of
Bergman [11, p. 181].
In order to employ the diamond lemma, we must show that the ambiguities are resolvable in
the sense of Bergman [11, p. 181]. There are potentially two kinds of ambiguities; inclusion
ambiguities and overlap ambiguities [11, p. 181]. For the present example there are no
inclusion ambiguities. There are four types of overlap ambiguities, and these are displayed
below:

                     W−i Gj+1 Gk+1 ,         Wi+1 Gj+1 Gk+1 ,         G̃i+1 Gj+1 Gk+1 ,

The proof of Theorem 6.1 illustrates how to write a reducible Aq -word as a linear combination
of irreducible Aq -words. Let w = a1 a2 · · · an denote a reducible Aq -word. There exists an
integer i (2 ≤ i ≤ n) such that ai−1 > ai . By construction ai−1 ai is a reducible Aq -word of

express w as a linear combination of Aq -words, each ≺ w. We iterate this procedure and
after a finite number of steps, w is expressed as a linear combination of irreducible Aq -words,
each ≺ w.
