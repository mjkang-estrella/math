                                                   Abstract. Let P be a polynomial with integer coefficients and degree at least two. We
                                                   prove an upper bound on the number of integer solutions n ≤ N to n! = P (x) which yields
                                                   a power saving over the trivial bound. In particular, this applies to a century-old problem
                                                   of Brocard and Ramanujan. The previous best result was that the number of solutions is
                                                   o(N ). The proof uses techniques of Diophantine and Padé approximation.

                                                                                     1. Introduction
                                              Henri Brocard posed, first in 18761 [9] and again in 18852 [10], the problem of finding all
                                           integer solutions to the equation
                                                                                         n! + 1 = x2 .                                           (1)
                                           In 19133 Ramanujan [22] (see also [5]) independently proposed the same problem. Computer
                                           calculations show that any solution n other than the known solutions n = 4, 5, 7 must be
                                           large [6], and it is conjectured that no other solutions exist. While not offering a complete
                                           resolution of the problem, a weakened version of ABC conjecture (the “weak form of Szpiro’s
                                           conjecture”) implies that (1) has only finitely many solutions [20].
                                              More generally, one may inquire about solutions to the polynomial-factorial equation
                                                                                          n! = P (x),                                            (2)
                                           where P is an integer polynomial of degree at least two. The ABC conjecture again implies
                                           there are only finitely many solutions (see [15, 13]), and the question is what can be said
                                           about (2) unconditionally. For some polynomials one can prove that (2) in fact has only
                                           finitely many solutions. For instance, n! is never a perfect power for n > 1, so the equation
                                           n! = xd has only one solution for any d ≥ 2. Less trivially, it is known that n! = xd − 1 has
                                           no solutions if d ≥ 3 [14, 21]. Berend and Harmse [3] made a general study of polynomial-
                                           factorial equations and proved several sufficient conditions for (2) to have finitely many
                                           solutions. Takeda [27] recently showed finiteness results for polynomials P related to norm
                                           forms of algebraic number fields, and for n restricted to certain infinite subsets of the integers.
                                           It is also possible to generalize (2) even further and prove finiteness results for solutions to
                                           f (P (x)) = n!, where f is an arithmetic function (see e.g. [24]).
                                              For a polynomial P for which it is unknown at present whether (2) has finitely many
                                           solutions, such as in the case of the Brocard-Ramanujan problem, one can at least ask

Theorem 1.1 (Power saving for number of solutions). Let P ∈ Z[x] be a polynomial of
degree r ≥ 2, and let s ∈ Z\{0} be fixed. Then there exists a positive constant C = C(P, s),
depending only on P and s, such that for all positive integers N we have
                 #{N ≤ n < 2N : s · n! = P (x) for some x ∈ Z} ≤ CN 33/34 .
  In particular, Theorem 1.1 implies that the Brocard-Ramanujan equation (1) has ≪ N 33/34
solutions with n ≤ N.
Remark 1.2. We assume in the course of proving Theorem 1.1 that N is sufficiently large
depending on P and s, since for bounded N the statement of Theorem 1.1 follows trivially
from adjusting the constant C.
Remark 1.3. There is no harm in supposing in Theorem 1.1 that s, x, and the leading
coefficient of P are positive. By changing x → −x we may assume that x is nonnegative, at
the cost of adjusting the constant C, and possibly changing the sign of the leading coefficient
of P (if P has odd degree). If s and the leading coefficient of P have opposite signs then
there are at most finitely many solutions to sn! = P (x). Therefore, we may assume s and
the leading coefficient of P have the same sign, and hence assume both are positive upon
multiplying through by −1 as necessary.

to the actual proof, so the sketch here should be viewed as illustrative only. We consider all
of the solutions n1 < n2 < · · · < nR to ni ! = P (xi ) with N ≤ ni < 2N and put the solutions

number of tuples with ni+2 − ni larger than M are easily bounded, so we must bound the
number of tuples with ni+2 − ni less than M.

   We choose not to frame our results in the language of G-functions and instead take a
different, though related, approach. Namely, we proceed via the method of Padé approxi-
mations (also sometimes called the hypergeometric method or the Thue-Siegel method, see
e.g. [1, 2, 11, 12]). Following a well-known method of Rickert [23], we need to construct
rational numbers with certain special properties. These rational numbers will arise as the
values of Padé polynomials evaluated at a certain point. The Padé polynomials P0 , P1 , P2 of
degree ≤ D are chosen to have small height (i.e. coefficients of small size), and so that the
approximating form
                           R(x) = P0 (x) + P1 (x)ω1 (x) + P2 (x)ω2 (x)
vanishes to high order at x = 0. Here “high order” means ≥ (3−ǫ0 )D for some parameter ǫ0 ∈
(0, 1) (the reader may pretend ǫ0 ≈ 12 without losing much). We construct the polynomials
via Siegel’s lemma. The height of the polynomials increases as ǫ0 decreases, and we choose
ǫ0 optimally to effect the right balance between high order of vanishing and low-height
polynomials.

                            8. Possible extensions and investigations
     We close the paper by offering additional questions one might study.
                                                                33
      (1) The most obvious question is whether the exponent 34     of Theorem 1.1 can be reduced.
          One possibility is to determine whether the polynomials Pi of Lemma 4.4 can be made
          explicit, as can be done in some situations when the functions ωi (x) are replaced by
          binomial functions (1 + x)α [23, 1]. Another possibility is to make an in-depth study
          of the coefficients of the ωi functions and attempt to gain from more advanced forms
          of Siegel’s lemma [8].
      (2) The upper bound of Theorem 1.1 depends on the polynomial P . It would be desirable
          to prove an upper bound of the form CN 1−δ where the constant C depends only on
          the degree of the polynomial.
      (3) It is an easy exercise to show that, for any degree r ≥ 2, there are infinitely many
          integer polynomials of degree r representing r + 1 factorial values. It would be
          interesting to know whether there are polynomials of degree r representing ≥ r + 2
          factorial values. This is a generalization of a question of Ulas [28, Question 2.6].
     POWER SAVINGS FOR COUNTING SOLUTIONS TO POLYNOMIAL-FACTORIAL EQUATIONS                           25

          One conjectures that for any r ≥ 2 there exists a positive constant Cr such that,
       for any degree r polynomial P , the equation n! = P (x) has ≤ Cr solutions. Such
       a result, if true, must be very deep, since it does not seem to follow from the ABC
       conjecture. However, one might ask whether the result follows from some combination
       of standard conjectures in arithmetic geometry (ABC, Bombieri-Lang, Vojta, etc).
   (4) It would be interesting to obtain bounds, along the lines of Theorem 1.1, for the
       number of solutions to the equation Hn = P (x), where Hn is a “highly divisible”
       sequence as in [3]. For example, one might consider Hn = lcm(1, 2, . . . , n), or Hn =
       p1 p2 · · · pn , the product of the first n primes. In both of these cases it seems difficult
       to obtain a power saving bound as in Theorem 1.1. However, one should be able to
       obtain some effective saving over the trivial bound by adapting our techniques.
   (5) Let k ≥ 2 be a positive integer, and let δ > 0 be a small positive constant. The
       ABC conjecture implies there are only finitely many solutions (depending on k and
       δ, with n and x coprime) to n! = xk + O(xk−1−δ ). On the other hand, an easy greedy
       argument gives infinitely many solutions to |n! − xk | ≪k xk−1 .
          It is possible to adapt our method to obtain a bound of the form ≪k,δ N 1−ε(δ) for
       the number of solutions to n! = xk + O(xk−1−δ ), where ε(δ) → 0 as δ → 0. One
       works with n ≥ n0 (δ) solutions rather than three solutions as in Lemma 3.3, the key
       point being that if M is a very small power of N then the analogous quantity to χ
       in the proof of Proposition 3.2 satisfies χ ≈ n1 . We leave the details to the interested
       reader.
