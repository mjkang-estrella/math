1.1   Introduction and basic notions
Let k be a field. A k-algebra A is called an evolution algebra if it admits a basis B =
{ e i } i∈Λ , called a natural basis, such that e i e j = 0 whenever i ̸= j . Evolution algebras
are commutative, and in general they are neither associative nor power-associative. If

is non-degenerate if e2i ̸= 0 for every element of a natural basis; this does not depend
on the natural basis, and it is equivalent to Ann( A ) = 0 [22]. It is perfect (also called
idempotent or regular) if A 2 = A ; for finite-dimensional algebras this means that MB is
invertible. The basic rigidity phenomenon is that a perfect finite-dimensional evolution
algebra has a unique natural basis up to permutation and non-zero scalars [48]; conse-
quently its graph is an invariant of the algebra and its automorphisms are monomial.
In general, a non-perfect algebra may have many essentially different natural bases (for
instance, the zero algebra has all bases as natural bases), and the corresponding graphs
need not be isomorphic.

Five basic results
The following statements are used throughout the paper; they are the backbone of the
finite-dimensional theory.

Theorem 4 (Elduque–Labra [48]). If A is a perfect finite-dimensional evolution algebra
and { e i }, { f i } are natural bases, then there are a permutation σ and scalars t i ∈ k× with
f i = t i e σ(i) . In particular Γ( A, B) does not depend on B up to isomorphism, every automor-
phism of A is monomial, and Aut( A ) is finite.

Theorem 5 ( [30, 36, 48]). For a finite-dimensional evolution algebra A the following are
equivalent: A is nil (with respect to the principal powers x k+1 = x k x; see Proposition 9 for
plenary powers); A is right nilpotent; A is nilpotent; for some (equivalently, every) natural
basis B the graph Γ( A, B) has no oriented cycles; in some natural basis the structure
matrix is strictly upper triangular. The nilpotency index of an n-dimensional nilpotent
evolution algebra is at most 2n−1 + 1, and this bound is attained.

Theorem 6 (Cabrera Casado–Siles Molina–Velasco [22]). A non-degenerate evolution al-
gebra is the direct sum of irreducible evolution ideals, uniquely up to order; the summands
correspond to the connected components of the underlying undirected graph of Γ( A, B). A
finite-dimensional evolution algebra is simple if and only if it is perfect and Γ( A, B) is
strongly connected.

Theorem 7 (Elduque–Labra [50]). For a perfect finite-dimensional evolution algebra the
automorphism group scheme is an extension of a constant group of permutations of the
vertices of the graph by a finite diagonalisable group scheme D, both determined by the
graph; see (2) below. In particular Der( A ) = 0 if char k ∈ {0, 2}.

Theorem 8 (Costoya–Ligouras–Tocino–Viruel [43]). For every field k and every finite
group G there are infinitely many pairwise non-isomorphic perfect evolution k-algebras
A with Aut( A ) ∼
                = G.

A short chronology
The history of the subject can be summarised as follows. The notion was introduced in
2006 [113] and systematised in Tian’s 2008 monograph [111]. Between 2010 and 2014
Rozikov, Omirov, Ladra and their coauthors connected evolution algebras with popu-
lation dynamics, Gibbs measures, chains and dibaric algebras [34, 74, 79, 80, 106], and
the first structural results (nilpotency, derivations, classification in dimension two) ap-
peared [30, 31, 35, 36]. In 2015–2017 three foundational contributions changed the land-
scape: the graph-theoretic viewpoint and the uniqueness of natural bases for perfect
algebras [48, 49], the structure theory in arbitrary dimension [22] and the classifica-
tion in dimension three [23]; at the same time nilpotent algebras were classified in
low dimension [64] and gonosomal and isotopic viewpoints were introduced [51, 115].
From 2019 onwards automorphism group schemes [50], universality [43, 109], random
walks on graphs [25, 26], Hilbert evolution algebras [118] and the systematic study of
ideals through graphs [9, 19] became central, and a historical survey appeared [38]. The
most recent work concerns non-degenerate algebras [29], counting over finite fields [119],
lattices of subalgebras [75–78], deformations [83], idempotents and solvability [58, 66],
Grothendieck rings [1] and embeddings of commutative algebras [42].

1.2     Origins: genetics, Markov chains and Tian’s programme
Evolution algebras were introduced by Tian and Vojtěchovský [113] and developed sys-
tematically in Tian’s monograph [111]. The motivation comes from non-Mendelian ge-
netics: in asexual (uniparental) inheritance, for instance of organelle genes, a gamete
of type e i reproduces by “self-crossing”, and the offspring distribution is encoded by
e2i = j ω i j e j , while distinct gametes do not interact, which gives e i e j = 0. When the
     P

rows of MB are probability vectors, the evolution operator is the transition operator of a
discrete-time Markov chain, and Tian interpreted the basic notions of Markov chains in
algebraic terms: persistent and transient generators, communication classes, periodicity
and absorption. He proved a hierarchical (“semi-direct”) decomposition of an evolution
algebra into simple components and transient spaces, related algebraic periods to the
periods of Markov chains, and introduced the evolution algebra of a graph, defined by
the adjacency matrix. He also gave applications to the population genetics of organelle
heredity and to the analysis of Markov processes. These foundational ideas, collected
in [111] and in the programmatic paper [112], set the agenda for much of the later work.
    Soon afterwards evolution algebras were connected with other parts of mathematical
biology and statistical mechanics. Rozikov and Tian [106] associated evolution algebras
with Gibbs measures on graphs, and Ladra and Rozikov introduced the evolution alge-
bra of a bisexual population [79], a non-baric but dibaric algebra; see also [74] for the
connections with dibaric algebras and population genetics, the “chicken population” al-
gebras [46, 80, 105] and the constrained algebras of [47]. Varro generalised the bisexual
construction to gonosomal algebras, which model sex determination systems [115]. Fur-
ther models include mosquito populations [107] and pulse processes in networks [2].


1.3     Classification in low dimension
1.3.1   Dimension two and three
Two-dimensional evolution algebras over C were classified by Casas, Ladra, Omirov and
Rozikov [36], who also described an algorithm deciding isomorphism of evolution alge-
bras; real two-dimensional algebras were treated by Murodov [92] and by Bekbaev [4].
A geometric invariant, the “square” of a two-dimensional evolution algebra, was intro-
duced in [33], where identities of degree at most four, derivations, automorphism group
schemes and associative representations of all two-dimensional algebras are determined.
    The three-dimensional case is substantially harder. Cabrera Casado, Siles Molina
and Velasco classified three-dimensional evolution algebras over fields of characteristic
different from 2 containing roots of orders 2, 3 and 7 [23]; the classification uses the
graph, the dimension of A 2 and the annihilator, and produces a long list including sev-
eral parametric families. An alternative approach, based on chains of two-dimensional
algebras and on the annihilator and socle stabilising indices, was developed in [7], and a

                                             5
classification adapted to dimensions two and three over arbitrary fields appears in [40].
Simple evolution algebras of dimensions two and three were classified over arbitrary
fields in [18], using the graph, moduli sets, inductive limit groups and the Zariski topol-
ogy.

1.3.2   Higher dimensions and special classes
Four-dimensional perfect non-simple evolution algebras were classified in [15], with the
isomorphism problem completed in [3]. Evolution algebras with one-dimensional square
were classified by means of inner product spaces [84]: if dim A 2 = 1 and a generates A 2 ,
then x y = 〈 x, y〉a for a symmetric bilinear form, and three broad classes appear accord-
ing to the position of a. Calderón, Fernández Ouaridi and Kaygorodov [29] attached to
every non-degenerate evolution algebra an invariant consisting of an ordered sequence
of lower-dimensional evolution algebras from a specific family; it yields a classification
method for non-degenerate evolution algebras, carried out up to dimension three, and
descriptions of derivations and of the variety of evolution algebras with square of dimen-
sion at most one. Power-associative evolution algebras are Jordan algebras and admit a
Wedderburn decomposition; they are classified up to dimension six [96].

1.3.3   Isotopy
Isotopisms of evolution algebras correspond to mutations of genotypes. Falcón, Falcón
and Núñez showed, using computational algebraic geometry, that there are exactly four
(strong) isotopism classes of two-dimensional evolution algebras over any field [51], and
determined the isotopism classes of three-dimensional ones [52]. Ternary derivations
and the group scheme of ternary automorphisms of perfect evolution algebras were com-
puted in [85].

1.3.4   Counting
Isomorphism classes of perfect n-dimensional evolution algebras correspond to orbits of
the semidirect product S n ⋉ (k× )n acting on invertible structure matrices. Wei and Zou
[119] turned this into a counting problem over finite fields, derived a general counting
formula and obtained explicit formulas for n ≤ 4.

1.3.5   Recognising evolution algebras
Deciding whether a given algebra is an evolution algebra amounts to deciding whether n
symmetric matrices describing the product are simultaneously diagonalisable by congru-
ence (the SDC problem) [6]; necessary and sufficient conditions for simultaneous orthog-
onalisation of families of inner products over arbitrary fields were given in [13]. In [6]
it is also shown that classical genetic algebras of Mendelian and auto-tetraploid inher-
itance are not evolution algebras but are limits of evolution algebras. Imomkulov and
Rozikov [67] approximated an arbitrary algebra by the family of evolution algebras given
by the Jacobians of its evolution operator.

1.4     Structure theory
1.4.1   Subalgebras, ideals and decompositions
Evolution subalgebras and evolution ideals (those having a natural basis that extends to
a natural basis of the algebra) behave much better than arbitrary subalgebras and ideals.
Cabrera Casado, Siles Molina and Velasco developed the theory in arbitrary dimension
[22]: they described ideals generated by one element, characterised simple evolution
algebras, proved that a non-degenerate evolution algebra is irreducible if and only if
its graph is connected, and established existence and uniqueness of a decomposition
of a non-degenerate algebra into irreducible components (the fragmentation process);
uniqueness fails in the degenerate case. Basic ideals were introduced and studied in [14].
Boudi, Cabrera Casado and Siles Molina [5] introduced the evolution rank, proved that in
a non-degenerate algebra every family of natural orthogonal vectors extends to a natural
basis, showed that ideals of perfect evolution algebras are basic, and located nilpotent
elements of order three through principal minors of the structure matrix.
    The relation between ideals and the graph was made precise in [19]: there is a mono-
tone Galois connection between absorption ideals and hereditary saturated subsets of
vertices, which describes maximal ideals and absorption ideals combinatorially and char-
acterises finitely generated evolution algebras under suitable graph conditions. The in-
tersection M( A ) of all maximal ideals and a subdirect-product structure theorem were
studied in [21]. The absorption radical can be read off from the acyclic vertices of the
graph [24], and the reductive radical was introduced in [20]. Cabrera Casado et al. [9]
related non-degeneracy, zero annihilator, the absorption property, von Neumann regular-
ity and primeness to properties of the family of all graphs of A , showed that primeness is
equivalent (under semiprimeness) to downward directedness of the graphs, proved that
semiprimeness and perfection cannot be characterised combinatorially, and described
the centroid as the functions constant along connected components. Peirce decomposi-
tions relative to idempotents were studied by Paniello [98].
    Arbitrary subalgebras of evolution algebras need not be evolution algebras [32]. Ca-
macho, Khudoyberdiyev and Omirov classified the nilpotent evolution algebras all of
whose subalgebras are evolution subalgebras with extendable natural bases, and for-
mulated conjectures for the non-nilpotent case [32]; two of them were proved by García-
Martínez and Pérez-Rodríguez [58]. Ladra and Pérez-Rodríguez proved that subalgebras
of perfect (regular) evolution algebras are evolution algebras, and characterised the ex-
istence of subalgebras of codimension one [77]. The subalgebra lattice was investigated
in [75] (distributivity in the nilpotent case, modularity of solvable algebras with maximal
index of solvability) and in [78], where modular evolution algebras are fully characterised
in the nilpotent case and among supersolvable regular algebras. A Frattini theory, based
on the supersolvable nilradical, was developed in [76]. Order-theoretic structures on sub-
structures and the socle of an evolution algebra were studied by González Nevado [62].

1.4.2   Radicals and spectra
Velasco described the maximal modular ideals and the Jacobson radical of an evolution
algebra and introduced two notions of spectrum of an element, computed via the eigen-
values of suitable matrices built from the structure constants [116]. Analytic aspects
(Banach algebra norms, continuity of the evolution operator) were studied by Mellon
and Velasco [86].


                                            7
1.4.3   Idempotents and solvability
The idempotents of an evolution algebra are the solutions of (1). For algebras com-
ing from biological models they are the equilibrium states of the population and were
computed in many concrete cases, see e.g. [47, 79, 94]. García-Martínez and Pérez-
Rodríguez [58] analysed the solution set of the generic system by elementary algebraic
geometry: every perfect complex evolution algebra has a non-zero idempotent. They con-
jectured that a complex evolution algebra is solvable if and only if it has no non-zero
idempotents. One implication is clear, since an idempotent lies in every term of the de-
rived series. Hu and Wen [66] found a three-dimensional counterexample to the other
implication (an isomorphic algebra appears in [42, Remark 3.9], as an evolution envelope
of a two-dimensional algebra); it is the exceptional member of a one-parameter family,
and they proved the equivalence whenever A (∞) is itself an evolution algebra. Resolubil-
ity of evolution algebras was also studied in [54].

1.4.4   The evolution operator
The evolution operator depends on the natural basis. Its behaviour, the set of all evolu-
tion operators of an algebra, and the algebras whose evolution operator is an endomor-
phism were studied by Fernández-Ternero, Gómez-Sousa and Núñez-Valdés [53, 55].


1.5     Nilpotency
Nilpotency was the first structural property to be understood. Casas, Ladra, Omirov and
Rozikov proved that nil, right nilpotent and nilpotent finite-dimensional evolution alge-
bras are exactly those whose structure matrix is strictly upper triangular in a suitable
natural basis [36]. Camacho, Gómez, Omirov and Turdibaev characterised nilpotency
by matrix properties and proved that the maximal nilpotency index of an n-dimensional
evolution algebra is 2n−1 + 1 [30]; algebras with maximal index were characterised and
classified in [35], where wide classes with index 2n−s + 1 are also constructed. Elduque
and Labra gave the conceptual statement: an evolution algebra is nilpotent if and only
if Γ( A, B) has no oriented cycles [48].
    Classification of some classes of nilpotent evolution algebras proceeds by annihilator
extensions [64]. Hegazi and Abdelwahab classified nilpotent evolution algebras up to
dimension four over arbitrary fields [64] and in dimension five over algebraically closed
fields [65]. Elduque and Labra [49] pointed out a subtle gap in the extension method (a
natural basis of a quotient need not lift to a natural basis), introduced the type and fur-
ther invariants attached to the upper annihilating series, constructed families of nilpo-
tent evolution algebras from non-degenerate symmetric bilinear forms and commuting
symmetric endomorphisms, and completed the classification up to dimension five over
algebraically closed fields of characteristic different from two. Derivations, automor-
phisms, local and 2-local derivations of nilpotent evolution algebras of maximal nilindex
were determined in [89], the group exp(Der(E )) was shown to be a normal subgroup of
Aut(E ) of computable index in [90], and tensor products of nilpotent evolution algebras
were studied in [100]. Rota–Baxter operators of weights 0 and 1 on nilpotent algebras
of maximal nilindex were classified in [101], and local Rota–Baxter operators of weight
zero in [102]. Formal deformations of evolution algebras and the degenerations among
nilpotent evolution algebras of dimension at most four were determined by Makhlouf



                                            8
and Pérez-Rodríguez [83]; see [70] for a survey of the geometric classification of non-
associative algebras.
    In infinite dimension the situation changes: Tian and Zou constructed finitely gener-
ated evolution algebras (over rings with nilpotent elements) which are nil but not nilpo-
tent [114], and nil and nilpotent are not equivalent for Hilbert evolution algebras [28].
    In finite dimension, the word “nil” in Theorem 5 has to be understood with respect to
the principal powers x k+1 = x k x: for the plenary powers x[1] = x, x[m+1] = ( x[m] )2 , nil and
nilpotent are not equivalent even in finite dimension, as the following example, based on
the evolution envelopes of [42], shows.

respect to principal powers.

1.6.2   Derivations
Camacho, Gómez, Omirov and Turdibaev proved that Der( A ) = 0 for complex evolution
algebras with non-singular structure matrix and described derivations when the rank
is n − 1 [31]. The exact sequence (2) shows that for perfect algebras Der( A ) = 0 in char-
acteristic 0 or 2, while in other characteristics Der( A ) is abelian and determined by the
graph [50]. Derivations of evolution algebras of graphs were described in terms of the
twin partition of the graph; for twin-free graphs the derivation algebra vanishes [12].
Non-degenerate algebras and Volterra evolution algebras (whose structure matrices are
skew-symmetric, and which are connected to ergodicity of Volterra quadratic stochas-
tic operators) were treated in [11], where loops of evolution algebras are introduced.
Biderivations of some evolution algebras were considered in [97].

                                                   11
1.7   Graphs, random walks and combinatorics
The graph Γ( A, B) was introduced by Elduque and Labra [48]; earlier, Camacho et al. [30]
formulated planarity of graphs in terms of evolution algebras, and Tian [111] defined the
evolution algebra of a graph. Systematic dictionaries between evolution algebras and
(pseudo)digraphs were developed by Ceballos, Núñez and Tenorio [37, 39], by González-
López and Núñez [61], and in the study of graphicable and S-graphicable algebras [95].
     There are two natural evolution algebras attached to a finite graph G : A (G ), whose
structure matrix is the adjacency matrix, and A RW (G ), whose structure matrix is the
transition matrix of the symmetric random walk. Cadavid, Rodiño Montoya and Ro-
dríguez [25, 26] proved that they are always strongly isotopic, and that for non-singular
graphs they are isomorphic if and only if G is regular or biregular; they conjectured the
same for singular graphs, and supporting families were given in [27]. The connection
with Markov chains and statistics was explored in [82].
     Tensor products of evolution algebras are evolution algebras, and the graph of the
product relative to the product basis is the categorical product of the graphs. Inher-
itance of non-degeneracy, irreducibility, perfection and simplicity was studied in [17],
where four-dimensional perfect algebras that are tensor products of two-dimensional
ones are classified. Alshatnawi, Costoya and Viruel [1] studied the Grothendieck ring
of finite-dimensional non-degenerate evolution algebras: they proved that the balance of
the graph (the greatest common divisor of the balances of the cycles of the underlying
undirected graph) is independent of the natural basis, that indecomposable factors of
balance one cancel after tensoring while factors of larger balance produce zero-divisors,
and they analysed the subring generated by cyclic algebras. Algebras attached to permu-
tations were studied in [72, 94]. Giacomelli [59] introduced expander evolution algebras,
whose graphs are expanders, and related the Cheeger constant with simplicity, subalge-
bras, growth and spectral gaps.


1.8   Infinite dimension, probability and dynamics
Many results above hold in arbitrary dimension [19, 22]. Hilbert evolution algebras, in-
troduced by Vidal, Cadavid and Rodríguez [118], are Hilbert spaces with an orthonormal
natural basis and a continuous product; they are naturally associated with discrete-time
Markov chains with countable state space and with infinite graphs [117], and their nilpo-
tency was analysed through weighted digraphs [28]. Coletti, de Lima and Luiz [41] con-
structed infinite-dimensional genetic and evolution algebras from Gibbs measures, with
uncountable natural bases; infertility between macroscopically different configurations
produces a decomposition into fertile ideals.
    Casas, Ladra and Rozikov introduced chains of evolution algebras, families A [s,t]
whose structure matrices satisfy the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation [34]. Many ex-
amples in dimensions two and three, and the time behaviour of the baric property, of
absolutely nilpotent elements and of idempotents, were studied in [68, 73, 93, 104, 105];
flows of arbitrary finite-dimensional algebras were introduced in [81]. Continuous evolu-
tion algebras arising from ordinary differential equations and flows on matrix Lie groups
were introduced by Montaner and Paniello [88]. The quadratic evolution operator x 7→ x2
and its normalisations define discrete dynamical systems whose fixed points are idem-
potents; these were analysed for two-dimensional algebras [104] and for many biological
models [47, 79, 107].



                                           12
1.9     Embeddings, rings and further generalisations
Costoya, Fernández Ouaridi and Viruel [42] proved that every finite-dimensional com-
mutative algebra is an ideal of a finite-dimensional evolution algebra, determined the
smallest possible dimension of such an evolution algebra and, in characteristic different
from two, related it to simultaneous Waring decompositions of the squaring map; for
unital algebras the minimal dimension is the Waring rank of x 7→ x2 , which they com-
puted for truncated polynomial algebras. It follows that the class of evolution algebras
satisfies no polynomial identities other than the consequences of commutativity (see Re-
mark 13 below). Evolution algebras over rings appear in [114] and, for free modules of
rank two over domains, in [16], where quasiperfect algebras and coloured graphs are in-
troduced. Isomorphism conditions for algebra functors, including the evolution algebra
functor, were studied in [60]. Further generalisations include S -evolution algebras [91]
and evolution coalgebras.

2.1.1   Polynomial identities
Remark 13. The class of evolution algebras satisfies no polynomial identity other than
the consequences of commutativity. Indeed, let p be a non-zero element of degree at most d
of the free commutative (non-associative) algebra F on x1 , . . . , xm . The quotient F /F>d by
the span of the monomials of degree greater than d is a finite-dimensional commutative
algebra in which p( x1 , . . . , xm ) = p ̸= 0. Since every finite-dimensional commutative alge-
bra is an ideal (in particular, a subalgebra) of a finite-dimensional evolution algebra [42],
p is not an identity of the class of evolution algebras. Hence questions about identities are
meaningful only for subclasses.

2.1.10    Further remarks
Remark 22. Strong connectivity of Γ( A, B), and hence the condensation of the graph,
is not independent of the natural basis for non-degenerate algebras. If char k ̸= 2 and
e21 = e22 = e 1 + e 2 , then Γ( A, { e 1 , e 2 }) is the complete digraph with loops, while f = e 1 − e 2 ,
g = e 1 + e 2 is a natural basis with f 2 = g2 = 2 g, whose graph is not strongly connected.

Remark 23. Let F q be a finite field. The average number of non-zero idempotents of a
perfect evolution algebra with a uniformly random invertible structure matrix is exactly
1. Indeed, for fixed x ̸= 0 we have x(2) ̸= 0, and the number of N ∈ GLn (F q ) with N x(2) = x
is | GLn (F q )|/( q n − 1); summing over the q n − 1 non-zero vectors x gives | GLn (F q )| pairs.

2.2     Open questions
The following list merges and revises the two lists of problems in an earlier version of this
text (nine problems proposed by the authors and colleagues, and thirty further problems
motivated by the literature), and adds new questions. Questions which were (partly)
answered by the observations of Section 2.1 have been reformulated accordingly. After
each question we indicate its source and what is known.

2.2.1   Foundations
Question 1 (Non-cancellation). Let A be a non-degenerate finite-dimensional evolution
k-algebra. Is it true that there exist non-degenerate finite-dimensional evolution algebras
B ̸∼
   = C with A ⊗ B ∼ = A ⊗ C if and only if each indecomposable direct summand of A has
balance greater than 1? Equivalently, is [ A ] a zero-divisor of the Grothendieck ring of
non-degenerate evolution algebras exactly in this case?
Source and status: [1, Conjecture 1.1]; one implication and the case of direct sums of cyclic algebras
are proved in [1]. Proposed by I. Alshatnawi, C. Costoya and A. Viruel.

                                                 20
Question 2 (The category of evolution algebras). The full subcategory Evok of k-algebras
spanned by evolution algebras is not complete, and subalgebras of evolution algebras
need not be evolution algebras. Is there a categorical framework for evolution algebras
(for instance, objects equipped with a natural basis or a family of natural bases, with
suitable morphisms) with good limits, colimits and functorial constructions?
Source and status: Proposed by J. M. Moreno Fernández and A. Viruel.

Question 3 (Homological algebra). Develop a (co)homology theory of evolution algebras
governing extensions of evolution algebras by evolution algebras and formal deformations
inside the class of evolution algebras.
Source and status: Proposed by A. Viruel; deformations are considered in [83].

Question 4 (Evolution algebras over rings). Find a definition of evolution algebras over
a commutative ring R that does not require the underlying module to be free. Do the
reductions E /mE at maximal ideals determine E ? Is there a notion of perfection over
R with a uniqueness theorem for natural bases generalising the one over fields, and a
well-behaved category of such algebras?
Source and status: Proposed by D. Martín Barquero and C. Martín González; rank two over do-
mains is treated in [16].

Question 5 (Tensor products and factorisation). Characterise in terms of the factors
when A ⊗ B is simple, perfect, non-degenerate or indecomposable, and describe the role of
the periods of the graphs. Does every non-degenerate evolution algebra factor into tensor-
indecomposable ones, and to what extent is the factorisation unique?
Source and status: [1, 17]. The question is partially answered in [1, Section 5] in terms of the
balance.

Question 6 (The Grothendieck ring). Find generators and relations, the Krull dimension
and the nilradical of the Grothendieck ring of non-degenerate evolution k-algebras, decide
whether it is Noetherian, and describe all its zero-divisors and its dependence on k.
Source and status: [1].


2.2.2   Structure theory
Question 7 (Intrinsic characterisation of solvability). Find a graph-theoretic or matrix-
theoretic characterisation of solvable finite-dimensional evolution algebras, analogous to
the characterisation of nilpotency by the absence of oriented cycles.
Source and status: Motivated by the García-Martínez–Pérez-Rodríguez conjecture [58] and its
refutation [66]; a counterexample to the conjecture also appears in [42, Remark 3.9].

Question 8 (Stable term of the derived series). For which finite-dimensional evolution
algebras is A (∞) again an evolution algebra?
Source and status: [66, Question 2.11]; when this holds, absence of idempotents is equivalent to
solvability over C [66].

Question 9 (Idempotent-free non-solvable algebras). Classify the idempotent-free non-
solvable evolution algebras of dimension 3 and 4 over C. In dimension 3, is A (∞) always
isomorphic to the algebra W0 of Section 2.1, and do these algebras form a two-parameter
family?
Source and status: [66]; see Section 2.1 for computer evidence that the example of [66] is not unique.

                                                 21
Question 10 (Idempotents of non-perfect algebras). Give a criterion, in terms of the
structure matrix or the graphs, for a (not necessarily perfect) finite-dimensional complex
evolution algebra to have a non-zero idempotent.
Source and status: Described as open in [58].

Question 11 (Idempotents over arbitrary fields). For n ≥ 3, characterise the fields k over
which every perfect n-dimensional evolution k-algebra has a non-zero idempotent. Does
the class of such fields depend on n?
Source and status: Theorem 18 settles n = 2 and gives a sufficient condition for all n; see also
Conjecture 1.

Question 12 (Hereditary evolution algebras). Characterise the evolution algebras all of
whose subalgebras are evolution algebras. Is this property detected by the graphs? Is it
preserved by tensor products and direct sums? What about ideals?
Source and status: [32, 58, 77].

Question 13 (Subalgebra lattices). Characterise modular, distributive and dually atom-
istic evolution algebras in general. If two evolution algebras have isomorphic subalgebra
lattices, how are they related? Characterise the evolution algebras whose Frattini subal-
gebra is an ideal, and the Frattini-free ones.
Source and status: [75, 76, 78].

Question 14 (Natural bases of non-perfect algebras). Describe the set of natural bases
(up to permutation and scaling) of a non-degenerate evolution algebra. When is it finite?
Can it be infinite for an algebra whose automorphism group is finite?
Source and status: New. Proposition 14 shows that the set can be infinite (it contains all orthog-
onal bases of V completed by u) even for a non-degenerate algebra; Remark 22 shows that the
graphs of different natural bases can differ in strong connectivity. [5, Corollary 2.13] describes all
the natural bases of an evolution algebra (degenerate or not) in terms of its decomposition into
natural ideals; it does not give a formula for the number of natural bases, but comes close to it.

Question 15 (Nil versus nilpotent). Give a combinatorial characterisation of nil evolu-

algebra which is nil of bounded index (with respect to principal powers) necessarily nilpo-

Source and status: [28, 114]. With respect to plenary powers the answer is negative, already in
finite dimension (Proposition 9); with respect to principal powers, finite-dimensional nil evolution
algebras are nilpotent (Theorem 5).

Question 16 (Identities of subclasses). By Remark 13, evolution algebras satisfy only the
consequences of commutativity. Describe the identities of natural subclasses: the algebras

and are the varieties generated by a single finite-dimensional algebra?
Source and status: [29, 42].

Question 17 (Radicals and combinatorial detectability). Which algebraic properties of

graphs, and which from neither?
Source and status: [9, 20, 24]; semiprimeness and perfection are not combinatorial [9].

Question 40 (Minimal dimension). For a finite group G and a field k let µk (G ) be the

Source and status: [43, 109]. By Remark 19, for k = C and G = Z3 no perfect algebra of dimension

2.3     Conjectures
Each conjecture below is accompanied by the evidence we have. For each of them a direct
attempt to prove or disprove it (of the order of half an hour of focused work, including the
natural first ideas indicated in the comments) did not succeed; in particular none of them
follows in an obvious way from the results surveyed in Section 1 or from the observations
of Section 2.1. Computer evidence was obtained with short scripts (exact arithmetic over
finite fields, or numerical computation); it is experimental and is not a proof.

Conjecture 1 (Fields with odd-degree extensions). If k has a finite separable extension
of odd degree greater than 1, then for some n there is a perfect n-dimensional evolution
k-algebra without non-zero idempotents. Together with Theorem 18(1) this would charac-
terise the fields over which every perfect evolution algebra has a non-zero idempotent.
Source and status: Proved when k has a cubic extension (Theorem 18(2)). The natural idea of
realising a given odd-degree extension L/k as the residue field of the scheme Z ′ of non-zero idem-
potents requires controlling all 2n − 1 points simultaneously, and we could not do this already for
extensions of degree 5 without cubic subextensions.

Conjecture 2 (Generic Galois group of idempotents). For the generic n-dimensional evo-
lution algebra over Q (structure constants independent indeterminates), the Galois group
of the 2n − 1 non-zero idempotents is the full symmetric group S 2n −1 . Consequently, by
Chebotarev’s theorem, for fixed n and q → ∞ the number of non-zero idempotents of a
random perfect evolution F q -algebra is distributed like the number of fixed points of a
random permutation of 2n − 1 letters; in particular the probability of having no non-zero
                     P n

Source and status: Proved for n = 2 (the example after Theorem 18 specialises the generic cubic

Conjecture 7 (Failure of the Hasse principle). There exist perfect evolution Q-algebras

Conjecture 9 (Spectrum of nilpotency indices). Let k be an infinite field. An n-
