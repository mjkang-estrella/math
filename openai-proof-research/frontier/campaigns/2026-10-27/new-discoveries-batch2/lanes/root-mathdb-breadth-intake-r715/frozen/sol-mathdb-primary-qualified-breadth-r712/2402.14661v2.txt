                                                                                    1. Introduction
                                              Quandles are sets with self-distributive binary operations that axiomatize the three Rei-
                                           demeister moves in classical knot theory [11, 15, 16]. They have been used extensively to
                                           construct invariants of knots in the 3-space and knotted surfaces in 4-space, see for example
                                           [4, 6]. A cohomology theory for quandles was introduced in [6] as a variation of rack homol-
                                           ogy defined by Fenn, Rourke and Sanderson in [14]. Two-dimensional cocycles were used to
                                           construct extensions of quandles [5] and also allowed to define the quandle cocycle invariant
                                           of knots [6]. This homology was extended to a homology theory of set-theoretic Yang-Baxter
                                           equations in [3].
                                              Quandle rings were introduced in [2] as a “linearization” of quandles. Their construction
                                           is similar to group rings [17]. Quandle rings have been investigated in [9] where various prop-
                                           erties of quandle rings were established such as decomposition of quandle rings into simple
                                           left or right ideals, the study of isomorphisms of quandle rings and a proof that quandle rings
                                           are not power-associative rings. In [1] the authors investigated the existence of nontrivial
                                           zero divisors in quandle rings in parallel to Kaplansky’s study of zero divisors in group rings.
                                           It was proved in [12] that integral quandle rings of quandles of finite type that are non-trivial
                                           coverings over nice base quandles admit infinitely many non-trivial idempotents, and gave
                                           their complete description. In [13] it was shown that quandle rings and their idempotents lead
                                           to proper enhancements of the well-known quandle coloring invariant of links. The authors
                                           also determined, for quandles of small cardinality, those for which the set of all idempotents
                                           is itself a quandle. In [7], a set of 26 finite quandles was used whose coloring invariant
                                           distinguishes all knots with 12 or fewer crossings (modulo mirror images and reversal). Fur-
                                           thermore, The authors of [7] were able to find a set of 23 quandles whose coloring invariant
                                           distinguishes 1058 of these knots from their mirror images. The results of both articles [12]
                                           and [7] motivated our investigation. Precisely, in this article, we combine the strength of
                                           idempotents in quandle rings and the state sum invariants of knots to distinguish all 12965
                                           prime oriented knots up to 13 crossings, using only 21 connected quandles and three quandles
                                           made of idempotents in quandle rings. We also distinguish all knots of up to 13 crossings

   2. Review of Quandles, Colorings and State Sum Invariants of Knots
   In this section, we collect some basics about quandles, colorings and state sum invariants
of knots needed for the rest of the article. We begin with the following definition.
Definition 2.1. A quandle is a non empty set X equipped with binary operation ∗ that
satisfies the following axioms:
   (1) For every x ∈ X, x ∗ x = x.
   (2) For every x, y ∈ X, ∃!z ∈ X such that z ∗ x = y.
   (3) For every x, y, z ∈ X, (x ∗ y) ∗ z = (x ∗ z) ∗ (y ∗ z).
   A quandle homomorphism is a map between two quandles f : (X, ∗) → (Y, ⋆) such that
f (x ∗ y) = f (x) ⋆ f (y). A quandle isomorphsim is a bijective quandle homomorphism. A
quandle automorphsim is a quandle isomorphism of X with itself. Each right multiplication
         STATE SUM INVARIANTS OF KNOTS FROM IDEMPOTENTS IN QUANDLE RINGS                       3

Sy : X → X given by Sy (x) = x ∗ y is an automorphism of X fixing the element y. Sy−1 (x)
will be denoted by x∗y = z ⇐⇒ z∗y = x.
  A quandle is completely determined by the set of its right multiplications.
  Some typical examples of quandles are
     • Any set X with operation ∗ given by x ∗ y = x for any x, y ∈ X is a quandle known
       as trivial quandle.
     • A conjugacy class X of a group G with the quandle operation x ∗ y = y −1 xy. We call
       this a conjugation quandle.
     • Any Z[T, T −1 ]-module M is a quandle with x ∗ y = T x + (1 − T )y, x, y ∈ M, called
       an Alexander quandle.
   The set Aut(X) of all automorphisms of a quandle X is a group. The subgroup Inn(X)
generated by the set {Sx , x ∈ X} is a normal subgroup of Aut(X) and is called the inner
group of X. When the action of Inn(X) on X is transitive then the quandle X is said to be
a connected quandle. Through this article, we will consider connected quandles and some of
their idempotents. For more on quandles, the reader can consult [11, 15, 16].

                           3. Review of idempotents in quandle rings
  Given a non-trivial quandle (X, ∗) and an associative ring k with unity, we consider a
nonassociative ring k[X] called the quandle P ring. Precisely, Let k[X] be the set of elements
that are uniquely expressible in the form x∈X ax ex , where x ∈ X and ax = 0 for almost all
x. Then the set k[X] becomes a ring with the natural addition and the multiplication given
by the following rule, where x, y ∈ X and ax , by ∈ k,
                           X              X           X
                         (    ax ex ) · (   by ey ) =   ax by ex∗y .                      (3.1)
                               x∈X                y∈X           x,y∈X

For more on quandle rings, see for example [2, 9].

                       6. A Brief Description of the Algorithm
  In this section, we give a brief description of the algorithm. The algorithm was inspired
by http://shell.cas.usf.edu/~ saito/Maple/. The algorithm has three steps
                                                                                     
     • Step 1. Given a quandle X, we check when the set of idempotents I Z2 [X] is a
       quandle.                                                  
     • Step 2. Using a quandle X and its idempotent I Z2 [X] over Z2 , we calculate the
       colorings of a given knot K using its braid representation.
     • Step 3. After obtaining the coloring, we calculate the State Sum Invariant of the
       knot K using both X and I Z2 [X] .
Below we give a quick description of how to find the coloring of a knot K and the State Sum
Invariant of K.
     • Finding the colorings of a knot K: Since every knot is the closure of a braid, to
       obtain a coloring of the knot, we first color the braid as follows.
        (1) Let m be the braid index of the knot K.
        (2) Let ~x = (x1 , x2 , x3 , ..., xi , xi+1 , ..., xm ) ∈ X m be the top color of the braid.
        (3) At any ith crossing, if it is positive, then ~x becomes (x1 , ..., xi+1 , xi ∗ xi+1 , ..., xm ).
            if the ith crossing is negative, then ~x becomes (x1 , ..., xi+1 ∗¯xi , xi , ..., xm ). Let
            (y1 , y2 , ..., ym ) be the bottom vector of the braid.
        (4) A solution of the system of equations y1 = x1 , ..., ym = xm is a coloring of knot
            K by the quandle X. We abuse the notation and use ~x to denote this coloring.
     • Calculating the State Sum Invariant of K
        (1) Let ~x = (x1 , x2 , x3 , ..., xi , xi+1 , ..., xm ) ∈ X m be the top color of the braid, thus
            giving a color of K
        (2) At a positive crossing with input colors xi and xi+1 , we assign the Boltzmann
            weight φ(xi , xi+1 ) (refer to the left picture of Figure 2). Similarly at a nega-
            tive crossing with output colour xi and xi+1 we assign the Boltzmann weight
            −φ(xi , xi+1 ) (refer to the right picture of Figure 2).
        (3) For each fixed coloring, we compute the product τ φ(x, y)ǫ over all crossings.

            The result is an element of group A. To obtain the State Sum Invariant         P Q of K,ǫ
            we sum over all possible coloring obtaining the element Φ(K) = C τ φ(x, y)
            of the integral group ring A.
