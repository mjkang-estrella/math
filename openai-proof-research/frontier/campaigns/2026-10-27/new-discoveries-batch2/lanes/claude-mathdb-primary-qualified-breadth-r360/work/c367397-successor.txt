                                                 The greedy 3-sumfree sequence S1,g,g+1




arXiv:2606.17447v1 [math.CO] 13 Jun 2026
                                                                          Orion Shtrezi


                                                                             Abstract
                                                    For every integer g ≥ 2, we determine exactly which integers occur
                                                in the greedy 3-sumfree sequence that starts with 1, g, and g + 1.
                                                This gives a direct proof of a conjecture of Bosma, Bruin, Fokkink,
                                                Grube, Reuijl, and Tromp. We also give an explicit description of the
                                                eventual periodicity, including both the preperiod and the repeating
                                                block.


                                           1    Introduction
                                           Let x < y < z be positive integers. The greedy 3-sumfree sequence Sx,y,z is
                                           the increasing sequence that begins with x, y, z and then continues by the
                                           rule that each new term is the least positive integer exceeding the preceding
                                           term that is not a sum of three distinct earlier terms.
                                               Bosma et al. [1, Theorem 15 and Conjecture 16] proved explicit descrip-
                                           tions of S1,2,3 , S1,3,4 , and S1,4,5 with the automatic theorem prover Walnut,
                                           and they conjectured a uniform formula for S1,g,g+1 for every g ≥ 2, which
                                           they verified mechanically for 2 ≤ g ≤ 10. The cases S1,2,3 and S1,3,4 ap-
                                           pear in the OEIS [2] as A026471 and A026475, respectively; a note in entry
                                           A026471 records an earlier proof of the case g = 2 by Matthew Akeran. In
                                           this note we prove the full conjecture by a direct interval-sumset argument.
                                               For integers u and v, we write

                                                                   [u, v] = {n ∈ Z : u ≤ n ≤ v},

                                           with the convention that [u, v] = ∅ when u > v.

Theorem 1. Let g ≥ 2 be an integer. Then the greedy 3-sumfree sequence
S1,g,g+1 is characterized by

      z ∈ S1,g,g+1 ⇐⇒ z ∈ {1, 2g + 1, 6g + 1}
                                                                                (1)
                     or z mod (10g + 3) ∈ [g, 2g] ∪ [6g + 2, 7g + 1].

In particular, after the first g + 4 terms, the sequence modulo 10g + 3 is
periodic with period length 2g + 1.

   Section 2 fixes notation and reduces Theorem 1 to two propositions, which
are proved in Sections 3 and 4; the induction that completes the proof is
carried out at the end of Section 4.

Proof of Proposition 3. By Lemma 4 and (3), the only elements of Ag that
could be forced are 1, e, and f .
   The smallest sum of three distinct elements of Ag is

                          1 + g + (g + 1) = 2g + 2,

so neither 1 nor e = 2g + 1 can be forced.
   It remains to exclude f = 6g + 1. The elements of Ag below f are exactly

                           1, g, g + 1, . . . , 2g, e.

The largest sum of three distinct such elements is

          e + 2g + (2g − 1) = (2g + 1) + 2g + (2g − 1) = 6g < f.

Therefore f is not forced either.

Proof of Theorem 1. We prove by induction on n that
                          n ∈ S1,g,g+1 ⇐⇒ n ∈ Ag .
The claim is clear for 1 ≤ n ≤ g + 1, since both sets contain exactly the
integers 1, g, and g + 1 in this range.
    Now let n > g + 1, and assume that the claim holds for every positive
integer smaller than n. If n ∈/ Ag , then Proposition 2 gives a representation
                                 n=a+b+c
with distinct a, b, c ∈ Ag . Since a, b, c < n, the induction hypothesis gives
a, b, c ∈ S1,g,g+1 . Hence the greedy rule excludes n.
    If n ∈ Ag , then Proposition 3 shows that n is not a sum of three distinct
elements of Ag . By the induction hypothesis, the earlier terms of S1,g,g+1
are exactly the elements of Ag below n. Therefore n is not a sum of three
distinct earlier terms of S1,g,g+1 , so the greedy rule admits n.
    This completes the induction, and therefore S1,g,g+1 = Ag . The descrip-
tion (1) follows immediately.
    After the first g + 4 terms
                    1, g, g + 1, . . . , 2g, 2g + 1, 6g + 1,
the sequence of residues repeats as
               6g + 2, 6g + 3, . . . , 7g + 1, g, g + 1, . . . , 2g
modulo m = 10g + 3. This repeating block has length 2g + 1.
