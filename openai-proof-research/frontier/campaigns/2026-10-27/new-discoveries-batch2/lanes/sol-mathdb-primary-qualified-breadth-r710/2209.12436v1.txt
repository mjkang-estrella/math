                                                                                         Abstract
                                                      The Goulden–Jackson cluster method, adapted to permutations by Elizalde and
                                                  Noy, reduces the problem of counting permutations by occurrences of a prescribed con-
                                                  secutive pattern to that of counting clusters, which are special permutations with a
                                                  lot of structure. Recently, Zhuang found a generalization of the cluster method which
                                                  specializes to refinements by additional permutation statistics, namely the inverse de-
                                                  scent number ides, the inverse peak number ipk, and the inverse left peak number
                                                  ilpk. Continuing this line of work, we study the enumeration of 2134 · · · m-clusters by
                                                  ides, ipk, and ilpk, which allows us to derive formulas for counting permutations by
                                                  occurrences of the consecutive pattern 2134 · · · m jointly with each of these statistics.
                                                  Analogous results for the pattern 12 · · · (m − 2)m(m − 1) are obtained via symmetry
                                                  arguments. Along the way, we discover that 2134 · · · (r + 1)-clusters are equinumerous
                                                  with r-Stirling permutations introduced by Gessel and Stanley, and we establish some
                                                  joint equidistributions between these two families of permutations.

                                           Keywords: consecutive pattern, cluster, descent, peak, r-Stirling permutation, plateau

    Let occσ (π) denote the number of occurrences of σ in π. If occσ (π) = 0, then we say that
π avoids σ (as a consecutive pattern). We let Sn (σ) denote the subset of permutations in
Sn avoiding σ. As observed above, we have 672489153 ∈ S9 (213). For the remainder of this
paper, the notions of occurrence and avoidance of patterns in permutations always refer to
consecutive patterns unless otherwise stated.
    The study of consecutive patterns in permutations was initiated by Elizalde and Noy
[10] in 2003, as a variation of classical patterns in permutations, whose systematic study
originated in the seminal paper of Simion and Schmidt [27]. Among the standard tools
used in the study of consecutive patterns is the cluster method of Goulden and Jackson. In
its original form, the Goulden–Jackson cluster method [15] provides a very general formula
expressing the generating function for words by occurrences of prescribed subwords in terms
of a “cluster generating function”, which is easier to compute. In 2012, Elizalde and Noy
[11] adapted the Goulden–Jackson cluster method to the setting of permutations, which in
a similar vein reduces the problem of counting permutations by occurrences of a prescribed
consecutive pattern σ to that of counting simpler permutations called σ-clusters.
    Work in recent years have led to refinements and variations of the cluster method which
keep track of additional permutation statistics. The first result in this direction is a q-
analogue which provides a refinement by the inversion number inv. This q-cluster method
first appeared in a survey on consecutive patterns by Elizalde [8], and was used by Crane,
DeSalvo, and Elizalde [4] in their study of the Mallows distribution. In short, the q-cluster
method allows us to count permutations by the number of occurrences of σ jointly with the
statistic inv if we can count σ-clusters by inv.
    Subsequently, Zhuang proved a lifting of the cluster method to the Malvenuto–Reutenauer
algebra [31], which we will sometimes refer to as the generalized cluster method, since it spe-
cializes to both Elizalde and Noy’s cluster method for permutations and its q-analogue upon
applying standard homomorphisms. By constructing and applying other homomorphisms,
Zhuang obtained further specializations which keep track of other permutation statistics: the
inverse descent number ides, the inverse peak number ipk, and the inverse left peak number
ilpk. Similar to the q-cluster method, these specializations allow us to count permutations
by the number of occurrences of σ jointly with ides, ipk, or ilpk if we can count σ-clusters
by the corresponding statistic. This was done in [31] for the monotone patterns σ = 12 · · · m
and σ = m · · · 21, as well as the patterns σ = 12 · · · (a − 1)(a + 1)a(a + 2)(a + 3) · · · m where
m ≥ 5 and 2 ≤ a ≤ m − 2. The latter family of patterns are elementary transpositions, i.e.,
transpositions of the form (a, a + 1); for this reason, we call such patterns transpositional
patterns. In fact, this result covers all transpositional patterns except for 1324 and those of
the form 2134 · · · m and 12 · · · (m − 2)m(m − 1) for m ≥ 3.
    The primary goal of the present paper is to study the enumeration of 2134 · · · m-clusters
by the statistics ides, ipk, and ilpk; via the cluster method, this will allow us to count
permutations by occurrences of 2134 · · · m jointly with these statistics. By way of reverse-

1.1. Inverse 2134 · · · m-clusters, r-Stirling permutations, and statistics
Before describing our work further, let us give some additional definitions and establish
notation for our main objects of study. We begin with several permutation statistics: the
descent number des, the peak number pk, the left peak number lpk, and their corresponding
inverse statistics ides, ipk, and ilpk.
      • We call i ∈ [n − 1] a descent of π ∈ Sn if πi > πi+1 . Let des(π) be the number of
        descents of π, and ides(π) := des(π −1 ) the number of descents of the inverse of π.
      • We call i ∈ {2, 3, . . . , n − 1} a peak of π ∈ Sn if πi−1 < πi > πi+1 . Let pk(π) be the
        number of peaks of π, and ipk(π) := pk(π −1 ).
      • We call i ∈ [n − 1] a left peak of π ∈ Sn if i is a peak of π, or if i = 1 and i is a descent
        of π. Let lpk(π) be the number of left peaks of π (equivalently, the number of peaks
        of the sequence 0π obtained by prepending 0 to π), and ilpk(π) := ilpk(π −1 ).
As an example, if we have π = 4351627, then des(π) = 3, pk(π) = 2, and lpk(π) = 3.
Furthermore, the inverse of π is π −1 = 4621357, so ides(π) = 2 and ipk(π) = ilpk(π) = 1.
     Let m ≥ 3 and k ≥ 1. We say that a permutation π ∈ S(m−1)k+1 is a 2134 · · · m-cluster
if std(πi πi+1 πi+m−1 ) = 2134 · · · m for all i ∈ { (m − 1)j + 1 : 0 ≤ j ≤ k − 1 }. (We will define
clusters in full generality in Section 4.1.) Informally, we can think of a 2134 · · · m-cluster as
a permutation consisting of occurrences of 2134 · · · m, each overlapping with the next one.
Clearly, the distributions of ides, ipk, and ilpk over 2134 · · · m-clusters are the same as the
distributions of des, pk, and lpk over inverses of 2134 · · · m-clusters, and it will be easier for
us to work with the latter. So, let us define

                       Pr,k := { π ∈ Srk+1 : π −1 is a 2134 · · · (r + 1)-cluster };

the reason for the change from m to r + 1 will be apparent soon. Continuing the example
from the previous paragraph, we have that π = 4351627 is a 213-cluster, so π −1 = 4621357
is a permutation in P2,3 .
    Even though the study of 2134 · · · m-clusters was the original goal of this project, our
work led us to discover connections between these objects and r-Stirling permutations.
An r-Stirling permutation 1 of order k is a permutation ρ = ρ1 ρ2 · · · ρrk of the multiset
{1r , 2r , . . . , k r } (where the superscript denotes multiplicity) such that if ρi = ρj and i < j,
then ρk ≥ ρi for every i < k < j. For example, ρ = 112223444331 is a 3-Stirling permutation
of order 4, but 123244433211 is not because there is a 2 between two instances of the larger
letter 3. Note that 1-Stirling permutations are ordinary permutations; 2-Stirling permuta-
tions are simply called Stirling permutations and were first studied by Gessel and Stanley in
[13], who also proposed the notion of r-Stirling permutations for larger values of r. There is
now a sizable literature on r-Stirling permutations and their statistics; see, e.g., the works
listed in [14].

1.2. Outline
The organization of this paper is as follows. In Section 2, we prove a collection of enumerative
results for statistics on Pr,k and Qr,k . We begin by giving a simple, alternative characteriza-
tion of permutations in Pr,k . Then we show that the numbers pr,k (i, j) and qr,k (i, j) satisfy the
same recurrence relation and initial conditions, and similarly with p̃r,k (i, j) and q̃r,k (i, j), thus

nomials Cr,k (t, y) and C̃r,k (t, y), and derive differential equations satisfied by the exponential
generating functions for these polynomials. Notably, the proof of one of these differential
equations requires a decomposition of r-Stirling permutations which does not translate easily
to inverse 2134 · · · m-clusters, so our equidistributions play a crucial role there.

2. Refined enumeration of inverse 2134 · · · m-clusters and r-Stirling
   permutations
This section focuses on counting permutations in Pr,k and Qr,k by the statistics defined in
the introduction. After describing a convenient characterization of the elements of Pr,k , our
first goal is to prove the equidistributions in Theorem 1.1. Then we will give recurrence
formulas for the polynomials Cr,k (t, y) and C̃r,k (t, y), along with differential equations for
their exponential generating functions.

2.1. A characterization of inverse 2134 · · · m-clusters
In the following proposition, we will see that an inverse 2134 · · · (r+1)-cluster is characterized
by having an increasing sequence of the values that are not congruent to 2 modulo r, along
with a relatively free placement of the values that are congruent to 2 modulo r. We will
later use this characterization to derive our recursive formulas for the numbers pr,k (i, j) and
p̃r,k (i, j). For this proposition only, we will write π(j) in place of πj to denote the jth entry
of π, so as to streamline the notation when we also use π −1 (a) to denote the ath entry of
π −1 .
Proposition 2.1. Let π ∈ Srk+1 for some r ≥ 2 and k ≥ 1. Then π ∈ Pr,k if and only if
both of the following two conditions are met:
  (i) The letters that are not congruent to 2 modulo r form an increasing subsequence in π;
      that is, if j < j ′ , π(j) 6≡ 2 (mod r), and π(j ′ ) 6≡ 2 (mod r), then π(j) < π(j ′ ).

 (ii) Each letter ri + 2 that is congruent to 2 modulo r comes before the letter ri + 1 in π;
      that is, π −1 (ri + 2) < π −1 (ri + 1).
Proof. First, observe that conditions (i) and (ii) are equivalent to the following conditions
on π −1 :
 (A) If a ∈ [rk] is congruent modulo r to one of 3, 4, . . . , r, then π −1 (a) < π −1 (a + 1).
 (B) If a ∈ [rk] is congruent to 1 modulo r, then π −1 (a) < π −1 (a+2) and π −1 (a) > π −1 (a+1).
So, it suffices to show that π ∈ Pr,k if and only if conditions (A) and (B) are satisfied.
    Suppose that π ∈ Pr,k , so that π −1 is a 2134 · · · (r + 1)-cluster. Assume that a ∈ [n − 1] is
congruent modulo r to one of 3, 4, . . . , r. Then the letters in positions a and a + 1 of π −1 are
both in the 34 · · · (r + 1) segment of the same occurrence of 2134 · · · (r + 1), which implies
π −1 (a) < π −1 (a + 1). This proves condition (A).
    Now, assume a ∈ [n − 1] is congruent to 1 modulo r. Then the letters in positions a,
a + 1, and a + 2 in π −1 correspond precisely to the 213 segment of the same occurrence of
2134 · · · (r + 1), so π −1 (a) < π −1 (a + 2) and π −1 (a) > π −1 (a + 1). This proves condition (B),
and completes the proof of one direction of this proposition.
    Conversely, let π ∈ Srk+1 be a permutation for which conditions (A) and (B) are satisfied.
The order relations in (A) and (B) imply that, for each i ∈ [k], π −1 has an occurrence of
2134 · · · (r + 1) in positions r(i − 1) + 1, r(i − 1) + 2, . . . , ri + 1. It follows that π −1 is a
2134 · · · (r + 1)-cluster, so we conclude that π ∈ Pr,k .

2.2. Equidistributions
Recall the classical recurrence relation on the Eulerian numbers A(n, i):

                         A(n + 1, i) = i A(n, i) + (n − i + 2) A(n, i − 1)

(see, e.g., [29, Section 1.4]). Since A(n, i) is the number of permutations in Sn with i − 1
descents, this recurrence relation can be proved combinatorially by examining where the
letter n + 1 can be inserted into a permutation in Sn and whether this insertion creates a
new descent.
    We will now establish a similar recurrence relations for the numbers pr,k (i, j) and qr,k (i, j),
using the same method of proof as for the Eulerian numbers but keeping track of an additional
parameter. In fact, we will see that the recurrence relation for pr,k (i, j) is exactly the same
as for qr,k (i, j), from which it will follow by induction that pr,k (i, j) = qr,k (i, j), thus proving
that the joint distribution of (des, pk) over Pr,k is the same as that of (lplat, ascplat) over
Qr,k . Note first that the result clearly holds for k = 1, since

     des(2134 · · · (r + 1)) = 1 = lplat(1r ) and     pk(2134 · · · (r + 1)) = 0 = ascplat(1r ).

Proposition 2.2. Let r ≥ 2 and k ≥ 1. Then

pr,k+1(i, j) = jpr,k (i, j)+(i−j+1)pr,k (i, j−1)+(j+1)pr,k (i−1, j)+(rk−i−j+2)pr,k (i−1, j−1).

Proof. Every π ∈ Pr,k+1 can be obtained uniquely by taking a permutation σ ∈ Pr,k and
inserting the letter rk + 2 in one of rk + 1 places (any position except the very end), and
appending the letters rk + 3, rk + 4, . . . , r(k + 1) + 1 in this order. Appending rk + 3, rk +
4, . . . , r(k + 1) + 1 does not change the number of descents or peaks, so the location where
the letter rk + 2 is inserted determines how the statistic values des(π) and pk(π) compare
to des(σ) and pk(σ).
     Suppose that rk + 2 is inserted between σℓ and σℓ+1 (where we set ℓ = 0 if rk + 2 is
inserted at the beginning of σ), and consider the following four options:
    • if ℓ is a peak (and thus also a descent) of σ, then des(π) = des(σ) and pk(π) = pk(σ);
    • if ℓ is a descent but not a peak of σ, then des(π) = des(σ) and pk(π) = pk(σ) + 1;
    • if ℓ + 1 is a peak of σ or ℓ = 0, then des(π) = des(σ) + 1 and pk(π) = pk(σ);
    • in all other cases—i.e., ℓ is an ascent of σ not followed by a descent—we have des(π) =
      des(σ) + 1 and pk(π) = pk(σ) + 1.
Note that the number of positions in the last bullet is rk − des(σ) − pk(σ). Thus, a permu-
tation π ∈ Pr,k+1 with des(π) = i and pk(π) = j can be obtained by inserting rk + 2 in one
of the four following ways, prior to appending rk + 3, rk + 4, . . . , r(k + 1) + 1:
    • by inserting rk + 2 at one of the j peaks of a permutation σ ∈ Pr,k with des(σ) = i
      and pk(σ) = j;
    • by inserting rk + 2 at one of the i−(j −1) descents that are not peaks of a permutation
      σ ∈ Pr,k with des(σ) = i and pk(σ) = j − 1;

                                                  7
   • by inserting rk + 2 at the beginning or immediately to the left of one of the j peaks of
     a permutation σ ∈ Pr,k with des(σ) = i − 1 and pk(σ) = j;
   • by inserting rk + 2 in any of the rk − (i − 1) − (j − 1) ascents not followed by a descent
     of a permutation σ ∈ Pr,k with des(σ) = i − 1 and pk(σ) = j − 1.
This gives the stated recurrence.
Proposition 2.3. Let r ≥ 2 and k ≥ 1. Then

qr,k+1 (i, j) = jqr,k (i, j)+(i−j+1)qr,k (i, j−1)+(j+1)qr,k (i−1, j)+(rk−i−j+2)qr,k (i−1, j−1).

Proof. Every r-Stirling permutation ρ ∈ Qr,k+1 can be obtained uniquely by taking a permu-
tation τ ∈ Qr,k and inserting r consecutive copies of the letter k + 1 in one of rk + 1 places.
The location where the k + 1 block is inserted determines how the statistic values lplat(ρ)
and ascplat(ρ) compare to lplat(τ ) and ascplat(τ ).
    Suppose that the k + 1 block is inserted between ρℓ and ρℓ+1 (where we set ℓ = 0 if the
block is inserted at the beginning of ρ, and ℓ = rk if it is inserted at the end), and consider
the following four options:

   • if ℓ is an ascent-plateau (and thus also a leading plateau) of τ , then lplat(ρ) = lplat(τ )
     and ascplat(ρ) = ascplat(τ );
   • if ℓ is a leading plateau but not an ascent-plateau of τ , then lplat(ρ) = lplat(τ ) and
     ascplat(ρ) = ascplat(τ ) + 1;
   • if ℓ + 1 is an ascent-plateau of τ or ℓ = 0, then lplat(ρ) = lplat(τ ) + 1 and ascplat(ρ) =
     ascplat(τ );
   • in all other cases—i.e., ℓ is a descent, ℓ is a plateau that is not a leading plateau, ℓ is
     an ascent not followed by a plateau, or ℓ = rk—we have lplat(ρ) = lplat(τ ) + 1 and
     ascplat(ρ) = ascplat(τ ) + 1.

Note that the number of positions in the last bullet is rk − lplat(τ ) − ascplat(τ ). Thus, a
permutation ρ ∈ Qr,k+1 with lplat(ρ) = i and ascplat(ρ) = j can be obtained in one of the
four following ways:
   • by inserting r consecutive copies of k+1 at one of the j ascent-plateaus of a permutation
     τ ∈ Qr,k with lplat(τ ) = i and ascplat(τ ) = j;
   • by inserting r consecutive copies of k+1 at one of the i−(j−1) leading plateaus that are
     not ascent-plateaus of a permutation τ ∈ Qr,k with lplat(τ ) = i and ascplat(τ ) = j − 1;
   • by inserting r consecutive copies of k + 1 at the beginning or immediately to the left
     of one of the j ascent-plateaus of a permutation τ ∈ Qr,k with lplat(τ ) = i − 1 and
     ascplat(τ ) = j;
   • by inserting r consecutive copies of k + 1 in any of the rk − (i − 1) − (j − 1) positions
     that are neither leading plateaus, immediately to the left of an ascent-plateau, or at the
     very beginning of a permutation τ ∈ Qr,k with lplat(τ ) = i − 1 and ascplat(τ ) = j − 1.

4. Applying the cluster method
In this section, we apply our results from Section 3 along with the generalized cluster method
from [31] to derive formulas for counting permutations in Sn by occurrences of 2134 · · · m or
12 · · · (m−2)m(m−1), jointly with each of the inverse statistics ides, ipk, and ilpk. We begin
with an expository overview of clusters, the cluster method, and the reverse-complementation
symmetry on permutations.

4.1. Clusters
Given π ∈ Sn , σ ∈ Sm , and S ⊆ [n − m + 1], we say that (π, S) is a marked permutation
on π (with respect to σ) if std(πi πi+2 · · · πi+m ) = σ for all i ∈ S. In other words, for each
i ∈ S, the permutation π has an occurrence of σ starting at position i. Note that π may have
other occurrences of σ not recorded by S. For example, if σ = 312, then (845132967, {1, 3})
is a marked word on π = 845132967 with respect to π. Let us call the occurrences whose
positions are recorded in S marked occurrences.
    Now, let π ∈ Sm and π ′ ∈ Sn . We say that τ ∈ Sm+n is a concatenation of π and π ′
if std(τ1 τ2 · · · τm ) = π and std(τm+1 τm+2 · · · τm+n ) = π ′ . Similarly, if we have two marked
permutations (π, S) and (π ′ , S ′ ) with respect to the same pattern σ, then we say that (τ, T )
is a concatenation of (π, S) and (π ′ , S ′ ) if τ is a concatenation of π and π ′ , and if T =

                                                21
S ∪ { i + m : i ∈ S ′ }. For example, (618923574, {2, 5, 6}) is a concatenation of (2134, {2})
and (12453, {1, 2}), where σ = 123.
    A marked permutation is called a cluster if it is not the concatenation of two nonempty
marked permutations. A cluster with respect to σ is called a σ-cluster. For example,
(618923574, {2, 5, 6}) is not a 123-cluster, but (4351627, {1, 3, 5}) is a 213-cluster.
    It is easy to see that, in order for a marked permutation (π, S) to be a σ-cluster, every
letter of π must belong to at least one marked occurrence in π, and each marked occurrence
(other than the last one) must overlap with the next one. For this reason, it is useful to
consider the overlap set of a pattern σ ∈ Sm , defined by
              Oσ := { i ∈ [m − 1] : std(σi+1 σi+2 · · · σm ) = std(σ1 σ2 · · · σm−i ) },
which encodes the positions where two copies of σ may overlap. If Oσ = {m − 1}—that is, if
two copies of σ cannot overlap in more than one position—then σ is called a non-overlapping
pattern.
    The advantage of working with non-overlapping patterns is that they greatly constrain
how clusters can be formed. More precisely, if σ ∈ Sm is non-overlapping and (π, S) is
a σ-cluster, then the length of π must be equal to (m − 1)k + 1 where k is the number
of marked occurrences of σ in π, and S = { (m − 1)j + 1 : 0 ≤ j ≤ k − 1 }. As such,
for any non-overlapping pattern σ, the length of a σ-cluster determines the positions of its
marked occurrences, so we can simply represent the cluster by its underlying permutation.
Because the pattern 2134 · · · m is non-overlapping, it is now apparent that the definition of
2134 · · · m-cluster given in the introduction is compatible with the more general definition
of cluster given here. The pattern 12 · · · (m − 2)m(m − 1) is non-overlapping as well.

Prior to applying the cluster method, let us briefly discuss how the reverse-complementation
symmetry on permutations affects some of the statistics that we consider. Given π ∈ Sn ,

Proposition 4.5. Let π ∈ Sn with n ≥ 1. Then
 (a) ides(π rc ) = ides(π),

 (c) ilpk(π rc ) = ilpk(π), and

 (d) if n ≥ 2 and πn−1 < πn , then pk(π rc ) = lpk(π),
Proof. Recall that the plot of π ∈ Sn is the set of points in the plane with coordinates (i, πi )
for 1 ≤ i ≤ n. Throughout this proof, we are guided implicitly by the idea that the plot of
π rc is a half-turn rotation of the plot of π, and that the plot of π −1 is a diagonal reflection
of the plot of π. Part (a) is found in [31, Proposition 2.6 (d)], so we focus our attention on
parts (b)–(d).
     Call i ∈ {2, 3, . . . , n} a right valley of π ∈ Sn if πi−1 > πi < πi+1 , or if i = n and n − 1
is a descent of π. Let rval(π) be the number of right valleys of π. We have that i is a left

peak of π rc if and only if n + 1 − i is a right valley of π, so lpk(π rc ) = rval(π). We will now

valley between them, and every two consecutive right valleys of π have exactly one left peak
between them. Thus, the sequence of positions {i : i is a left peak or a right valley of π}

5. Future directions of research
5.1. The consecutive pattern 1324
In [31], the enumeration of permutations by occurrences of a consecutive pattern σ jointly
with each of the statistics ides, ipk, and ilpk was done for the monotone patterns σ = 12 · · · m
and σ = m · · · 21, as well as transpositional patterns of the form 12 · · · (a − 1)(a + 1)a(a +
2)(a + 3) · · · m where m ≥ 5 and 2 ≤ a ≤ m − 2. In this paper, we have continued this work
by considering the transpositional patterns σ = 2134 · · · m and σ = 12 · · · (m − 2)m(m − 1).
The only transpositional pattern that has yet to be studied within this line of research is
1324, so we state the following as an open problem.
Problem 5.1. Count 1324-clusters by ides, ipk, and ilpk.
    As explained in Section 4.1, the patterns 2134 · · · m and 12 · · · (m − 2)m(m − 1) have
the special property of being non-overlapping, which constrains how clusters can be formed.
The patterns 12 · · · (a − 1)(a + 1)a(a + 2)(a + 3) · · · m (for m ≥ 5 and 2 ≤ a ≤ m − 2) do
not have this property, but they are examples of chain patterns (see [11, Section 2.2] for the
definition), which is another restrictive condition that makes their clusters more amenable
to study. Because 1324 is neither a non-overlapping pattern nor a chain pattern, we suspect
that counting 1324-clusters by ides, ipk, and ilpk will be more difficult than for the other
transpositional patterns.
