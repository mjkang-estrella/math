Abstract
In this work, we consider the Frank copula, belonging to the Archimedean family and enjoying several interesting properties, and suggest its use as a bivariate and multivariate latent variable for modeling correlated ordinal data, exploring the relationship between its parameter, which models the dependence among the standard uniform components, and the implied (linear) correlation between any pair of ordinal random variables, also as a function of the assigned marginal ordinal distributions. Based on extensive numerical evidence, we conjecture that discretization of a Frank copula latent variable cannot inflate linear correlation, suggesting a behaviour that appears numerically consistent with the Gaussian extremal property. We discuss inference for the proposed model, which appears to be straightforward, although it requires numerical methods, and can be easily implemented in the
R
programming environment. For assessing the goodness-of-fit of the model, we suggest using a parametric bootstrap procedure, which is particularly useful and preferable to the use of the asymptotic distribution of standard statistics when the observed contingency table presents several small counts. The methodology is illustrated through the analysis of two real datasets with ordinal responses, and compared with the customary latent Gaussian model. Despite its more limited modeling flexibility, due to the use of a single parameter controlling the correlation among variables, the advantage of this latent construction over the customary normal latent variable lies in the availability of a closed-form expression for the joint distribution function, which facilitates and accelerates the evaluation of the resulting joint probability mass function and correlation matrix, and obviously parameter estimation.

Copula Theory and Multivariate Dependence Modeling

Introduction
The bivariate Frank copula (Frank
1978
; Joe
2014
) is an Archimedean copula whose expression is
$$\begin{aligned} C(u_1,u_2) =-\frac{1}{\theta }\ln \left[ 1 {-} \frac{(1-e^{-\theta u_1})(1-e^{-\theta u_2})}{1-e^{-\theta }}\right] , (u_1,u_2)\in [0,1]^2, \end{aligned}$$
(1)
where the parameter
\(\theta \)
can take any real value. When
\(\theta \)
is zero, the expression above must be intended as the limit for
\(\theta \rightarrow 0\)
, which exists and equals
\(\Pi (u_1,u_2)=u_1\cdot u_2\)
. The expression for the copula density function corresponding to (
1
) is
$$\begin{aligned} c(u_1,u_2)= \frac{\theta (1-e^{-\theta })e^{-\theta (u_1+u_2)}}{[(e^{-\theta u_1}-1)(e^{-\theta u_2}-1)+e^{-\theta }-1]^2}. \end{aligned}$$
(2)
Just looking at the expression of the cdf in (
1
), we can state that the Frank copula is exchangeable  (see Nelsen
2006
, p.38). Furthermore, it is also radially symmetric  (see Nelsen
2006
, Definition 2.7.1), i.e., the expression of its cdf coincides with that of its survival copula, that in dimension
\(d=2\)
can be in general defined as
\(\hat{C}(u_1,u_2)=u_1+u_2+C(1-u_1,1-u_2)-1\)
. The Frank copula is the only bivariate Archimedean copula enjoying this property (Frank
1978
).
The Frank copula, like the Gaussian, is also comprehensive (see Nelsen
2006
, p.15). In fact, letting
\(\theta \)
go to
\(-\infty \)
, it tends to the countermonotonicity copula or lower Fréchet bound. Letting
\(\theta \)
go to
\(+\infty \)
, the Frank copula tends to the comonotonicity copula or upper Fréchet bound. When
\(\theta \)
tends to zero, as already seen, the limit of the expression of the cdf in (
1
) is the independence copula. Therefore, we can state that the Frank copula, by varying its parameter
\(\theta \)
, interpolates the entire range of dependence, from countermonotonicity to comonotonicity passing through independence, with the parameter
\(\theta \)
measuring the direction and strength of the dependence; in fact, the Frank copula is concordance-ordered in
\(\theta \)
(Nelsen
2006
).
Notably, like the Gaussian copula with parameter
\(\rho \in (-1,+1)\)
, the Frank copula with parameter
\(\theta \)
is tail-independent, i.e., its coefficients of lower-tail and upper-tail dependence
\(\lambda _L\)
and
\(\lambda _U\)
are both zero for all values of
\(\theta \)
.
For a bivariate copula, Spearman’s rank correlation,
\(\rho _S\)
, can be defined as Pearson’s correlation between the two random components:
\(\rho _S=\textrm{Corr}(U_1,U_2)\)
or, equivalently,
$$ \rho _S = \int _0^1\int _0^1 C(u_1,u_2)\text {d}u_1\text {d}u_2 - 3. $$
An expression for
\(\rho _S\)
for the Frank copula with parameter
\(\theta \)
is available:
$$\begin{aligned} \rho _S(\theta ) ={\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \displaystyle 1 - \frac{12}{\theta }\left[ D_1(\theta )-D_2(\theta )\right] & \theta \ne 0\\ 0 & \theta =0 \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$
(3)
which involves the so-called Debye’s function
\(D_k(x)\)
, which is defined for any positive integer
k
by (see Genest
1987
)
$$ D_k(x) = \frac{k}{x^k}\int _0^x \frac{t^k}{e^t-1}\text {d}t. $$
An approximate relationship between
\(\rho _S\)
and
\(\theta \)
for the full range of
\(\theta \)
was provided by Genest (
1987
):
$$ \rho _S = (1-\theta e^{-\theta /2} - e^{-\theta })(e^{-\theta /2}-1)^{-2}. $$

\(\rho _S\)
, as expressed by (
3
), as a function of
\(\theta \)
.

Kendall’s
\(\tau \)
can be defined as a measure of concordance between two random variables (rvs). Formally, if
\(X_1\)
and
\(X_2\)
are the rvs of interest, and
\((X_1',X_2')\)
is an independent copy of
\((X_1,X_2)\)
, Kendall’s
\(\tau \)
can be defined as
$$\begin{aligned} \tau (X_1,X_2)&= P((X_1-X_1')(X_2-X_2')>0) - P((X_1-X_1')(X_2-X_2')<0)\\ &=\mathbb {E}\left[ \textrm{sign}(X_1-X_2)(X_1'-X_2')\right] , \end{aligned}$$
that is, as the probability of concordance minus the probability of discordance. If
\(X_1\)
and
\(X_2\)
are continuous rvs, then Kendall’s
\(\tau \)
is a functional of the underlying copula
\(C(u_1,u_2)\)
only and can be accordingly expressed as
$$ \tau = 4\int _0^1\int _0^1 C(u_1, u_2) \text {d}C(u_1, u_2) - 1. $$
The expression for Kendall’s
\(\tau \)
for the Frank copula is given by
$$ \tau = 1 -\frac{4}{\theta }\left( 1 - D_1(\theta )\right) . $$
Frank copula is proved to be the minimum information copula under fixed Kendall’s
\(\tau \)
(Sukeda and Sei
2025
).

By contrast, the Frank copula exhibits contour lines that are more evenly distributed over the unit square, with no pronounced clustering near the corners, reflecting a more homogeneous dependence structure.

Frank copula can be extended to any dimension
\(d>2\)
, the cdf becoming
$$ C(u_1,\dots ,u_d) = -\frac{1}{\theta } \ln \left( 1+\frac{\prod _{i=1}^d(e^{-\theta u_i}-1)}{(e^{-\theta }-1)^{d-1}}\right) ,\quad (u_1,\dots ,u_d)\in [0,1]^d. $$
However, the copula parameter
\(\theta \)
now can take values in an interval
\((\theta _{\min },+\infty )\)
, with
\(\theta _{\min }\)
negative, but quickly approaching 0 as
d
increases (Meester and Mackay
1994
; Joe
1997
), and then the
d
-variate Frank copula can practically only model positive dependence, with independence as a limit case for
\(\theta \rightarrow 0^+\)
(see, e.g., Example 4.24 in Nelsen (
2006
)). For instance, when
\(d=3\)
, the lower bound is
\(-\ln 2\approx -0.6931\)
, to which corresponds a value of
\(\rho _S\)
equal to about
\(-0.1143\)
. Indeed, under the
R
programming environment, the package
copula
implements the multivariate Frank copula with positive values only for the parameter
\(\theta \)
. Other properties of the bivariate Frank copula that we mentioned earlier (such as concordance-ordering, radial symmetry and exchangeability) are instead maintained by the multivariate copula.
It is also worth recalling that any bivariate margin of the
d
-variate Frank copula is itself a bivariate Frank copula with the same parameter
\(\theta \)
. This property is also satisfied by other copula families, among which the Gaussian. In fact, any bivariate margin of the
d
-variate Gaussian copula with correlation matrix
P
is a Gaussian copula whose parameter is the corresponding
\(2\times 2\)
submatrix of
P
. This closure property of the Frank copula is particularly useful in the study of multivariate dependence. First, this property ensures structural coherence between the multivariate model and its bivariate projections. The functional form of dependence remains unchanged when moving from
d
variables to any pair, which gives a clear and consistent interpretation of the parameter
\(\theta \)
. In particular,
\(\theta \)
retains the same meaning across dimensions, acting as a single global parameter that governs the overall strength of dependence. Second, the closure property simplifies empirical analysis. Since all bivariate margins belong to the same copula family, dependence between pairs of variables can be studied using well-known tools specific to the Frank copula. For example, Kendall’s
\(\tau \)
depends uniquely on
\(\theta \)
, allowing for a straightforward and coherent interpretation of pairwise dependence within the multivariate framework. Finally, from an inferential and computational perspective, this property facilitates model estimation and validation. The consistency between the multivariate structure and its bivariate margins enables diagnostic checks at the pairwise level and reduces the risk of discrepancies between the global model and the observed pairwise dependence. However, it should be noted that focusing solely on bivariate margins does not capture potential higher-order dependence features.
The aim of this work is to propose the Frank copula as a latent variable model for correlated ordinal data and to investigate whether the discretization of a (bivariate) Frank copula can only attenuate, or may sometimes amplify, linear dependence between ordinal variables. In particular, we compare this behavior with the well-known extremal property of the Gaussian copula in terms of linear correlation. We also seek to emphasize the potential advantages of the model compared with the discretized Gaussian model, in terms of modeling and estimation. Indeed, the use of the Frank copula has already been suggested in Meester and Mackay (
1994
) as a parametric model for cluster-correlated categorical data, both in dimension 2 and in higher dimensions. For bivariate discrete data, a model based on the Frank copula is quite popular (Nikoloulopoulos
2012
and references therein).
Despite its restrictive dependence structure, the latent Frank copula may still be attractive in practice because of its parsimony, interpretability, and computational convenience. In particular, the model provides a natural benchmark for ordinal dependence modeling, since the entire association structure is governed by a single parameter whose interpretation remains unchanged across dimensions. Moreover, because the model accommodates arbitrary ordinal margins, any number of ordered categories, and can be extended to multivariate settings, it is potentially applicable across a broad range of social, behavioral, medical, and survey-based studies involving correlated ordinal responses. Beyond the use of the Frank copula for ordinal data, the present work provides a unified treatment of discretization effect on correlation, likelihood-based inference, and multivariate extensions within a common latent-variable framework.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Sect.
2
, we introduce and discuss the modeling framework. Section
3
addresses parameter estimation, first in the bivariate case and then in the general multivariate setting. Section
4
presents two applications based on real datasets. The final section concludes with a discussion of the main findings and possible directions for future research.
2
Constructing a multivariate ordinal random vector based on Frank copula
2.1
Discretization of a bivariate Frank copula
In this work, we consider ordinal variables as categorical variables whose levels possess a natural ordering. Typical examples include Likert-type survey items measuring attitudes or satisfaction (e.g., “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”, or “completely dissatisfied” to “completely satisfied”) and rating scales used to evaluate services or institutions (e.g., from “very poor” to “excellent”), where the ordering of the categories is meaningful but the distances between adjacent levels are not necessarily equal. Although ordinal variables are not inherently quantitative, we encode their categories using the numerical labels
\(1,2,\ldots ,m\)
, where each value represents an ordered level rather than a metric quantity. In practice, whenever correlations involving ordinal variables need to be computed, we represent their categories by the numeric scores
\(\{1,2,\ldots ,m\}\)
. Although this coding is not always theoretically justified, it is widely adopted in practice because it simplifies the analysis and allows the use of standard statistical tools (Norman
2010
).
The modeling of correlated ordinal data is often conducted within a latent variable framework. Specifically, the observed ordinal responses are assumed to be generated by an underlying multivariate continuous random vector, where each ordinal variable results from discretizing a latent continuous variable through a set of threshold (or cut-point) parameters. This formulation provides a flexible way to model dependence through the joint distribution of the latent variables, typically assumed to follow a multivariate normal distribution (Olsson
1979
; Muthén
1984
; Agresti
2010
). Latent variable models, possibly based on mixtures of normal distributions, are also employed to model mixed-type data (McParland and Gormley
2016
).
Consider a rv
\((U_1,U_2)\)
whose joint distribution is the Frank copula with parameter
\(\theta \)
. We can obtain a bivariate ordinal rv
\((X_1,X_2)\)
, where
\(X_1\)
and
\(X_2\)
are supported on the first
\(m_1\)
and
\(m_2\)
positive integers, respectively, with assigned marginal distributions,
\(p_{h}^{(1)}=P(X_1=h)\)
,
\(h=1,\dots ,m_1\)
, and
\(p_{k}^{(2)}=P(X_2=k)\)
,
\(k=1,\dots ,m_2\)
, by appropriately discretizing the continuous standard uniform rvs
\(U_1\)
and
\(U_2\)
.
\(X_1\)
can be in fact expressed as
$$X_1:= \sum _{h=1}^{m_1} h\cdot\mathbb {1}(\gamma _{1,{h-1}}\le U_1 < \gamma _{1,h}) $$
with
\(0=\gamma _{1,0}<\dots <\gamma _{1,m_1}=1\)
and the thresholds chosen in order to match the marginal distribution of
\(X_1\)
,
\(\gamma _{1,h}=P(X_1\le h)\)
, for
\(h=1,\dots ,m_1-1\)
. Similarly for
\(X_2\)
:
\(X_2:= \sum _{k=1}^{m_2} k\cdot\mathbb {1}(\gamma _{2,{k-1}}\le U_2 < \gamma _{2,k})\)
, with
\(0=\gamma _{2,0}<\dots <\gamma _{2,m_2}=1\)
and
\(\gamma _{2,k}=P(X_2\le k)\)
, for
\(k=1,\dots ,m_2-1\)
.
The joint probability mass function (pmf) of
\((X_1,X_2)\)
can be easily written in terms of the joint pdf of the underlying
\((U_1,U_2)\)
of Eq. (
2
) as:
$$p_{hk}=P(X_1=h,X_2=k) = \int _{\gamma _{2,k-1}}^{\gamma _{2,k}}\left[ \int _{\gamma _{1,h-1}}^{\gamma _{1,h}} c(u_1,u_2;\theta )\text {d}u_1\right] \text {d}u_2. $$
Equivalently, the joint pmf can be written in terms of the cdf (
1
) by exploiting the rectangle-probability rule as
$$\begin{aligned} p_{hk} = C(\gamma _{1,h},\gamma _{2,k};\theta ) - C(\gamma _{1,h-1},\gamma _{2,k};\theta ) - C(\gamma _{1,h},\gamma _{2,k-1};\theta ) + C(\gamma _{1,h-1},\gamma _{2,k-1};\theta ). \end{aligned}$$
(4)
2.2
Impact of discretization on correlation in the Frank copula
The most interesting point is to investigate how the correlation between
\(X_1\)
and
\(X_2,\)
which we can call the
implied correlation
(as it refers to the bivariate ordinal rv “implied” by the bivariate standard uniform rv
\((U_1,U_2)\)
), varies with the copula parameter
\(\theta \)
, or with its functional Spearman’s
\(\rho _S\)
(which we can call the
latent correlation
, since it is related to a latent copula distribution), and the marginal distributions of
\(X_1\)
and
\(X_2\)
, which can be assigned independently from
\(\theta \)
. In particular, we want to check if the implied correlation between the two ordinal variables is in absolute value smaller than the copula correlation
\(\rho _S\)
, as occurs with the Gaussian distribution, which enjoys the extremal property (Hirschfeld
1935
; Gebelein
1941
; Lancaster
1957
), which we recall here below.
Proposition 1
Let
\((X_1,X_2)\)
be a bivariate normal vector with
$$ \mathbb {E}[X_1]=\mathbb {E}[X_2]=0,\qquad \textrm{Var}(X_1)=\textrm{Var}(X_2)=1,\qquad \textrm{Corr}(X_1,X_2)=\rho ,\quad |\rho |<1. $$
Then for any measurable functions
f
,
g
such that
$$ 0<\textrm{Var}(f(X_1))<\infty ,\qquad 0<\textrm{Var}(g(X_2))<\infty , $$
one has
$$ \bigl |\textrm{Corr}(f(X_1),\,g(X_2))\bigr |\le |\rho |. $$
Moreover,
$$ \sup _{f,g}\ \textrm{Corr}(f(X_1),g(X_2))=\rho , \qquad \inf _{f,g}\ \textrm{Corr}(f(X_1),g(X_2))=-\rho , $$
where the supremum and infimum are taken over all such
f
and
g
. Equality holds if and only if, almost surely,
$$ f(X_1)=aX_1+b,\qquad g(X_2)=cX_2+d $$
for some constants
a
,
b
,
c
,
d
with
\(ac\ne 0\)
, with the sign chosen so that
\(\textrm{Corr}(f(X_1),g(X_2))=\pm \rho \)
.
When we discretize (according to possibly different sets of thresholds) a bivariate (standard) normal rv
\((Y_1,Y_2)\)
, this equals to apply two monotone non-decreasing functions to
\(X_1\)
and
\(X_2\)
, and then, thanks to Proposition (1), the resulting correlation between
\(X_1\)
and
\(X_2\)
reduces in absolute value. This fact was recognized several decades ago by Bollen and Barb (
1981
), who studied the behavior of Pearson’s sample correlation on discretized hypothetically bivariate normal samples, and was later numerically verified by Ferrari and Barbiero (
2012
) with respect to the bivariate normal rv using the
R
package
GenOrd
(Barbiero and Ferrari
2025

The mixed moment of the discretized rv
\((X_1,X_2)\)
can be computed as
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {E}(X_1X_2)&=\sum _{h=1}^{m_i}\sum _{k=1}^{m_j}hkp_{hk}, \end{aligned}$$
with
\(p_{hk}\)
from Eq.(
4
), and the linear correlation between
\(X_1\)
and
\(X_2\)
can be evaluated in a straightforward way as
$$ \textrm{Corr}(X_1,X_2) = \frac{\mathbb {E}(X_1X_2)-\mathbb {E}(X_1)\mathbb {E}(X_2)}{\sqrt{\textrm{Var}(X_1)\textrm{Var}(X_2)}}, $$
where
\(\mathbb {E}(X_1)=\sum _{h=1}^{m_1}hp_{h}^{(1)}\)
,
\(\mathbb {E}(X_2)=\sum _{k=1}^{m_2}kp_{k}^{(2)}\)
,
\(\textrm{Var}(X_1)=\sum _{h=1}^{m_1}h^2p_{h}^{(1)}-(\mathbb {E}(X_1))^2\)
, and
\(\textrm{Var}(X_2)=\sum _{k=1}^{m_2}k^2p_{k}^{(2)}-(\mathbb {E}(X_2))^2\)

It is very interesting to note that, even if we relax the assumption of identical ordinal distributions and only require that the two margins share the same support
\({1,2,\dots ,m}\)

The numerical evidence presented above strongly suggests that discretization of the Frank copula cannot inflate linear correlation, a property analogous to the extremal property of the Gaussian distribution. At present, however, this remains a conjecture, since a general theoretical proof has not been obtained. The only rigorous result currently available is that discretization preserves the sign of the correlation, that is, positively (respectively, negatively) dependent latent variables give rise to positively (respectively, negatively) correlated ordinal variables. We illustrate this result in the following theorem.
Theorem 2
Let
\((U_1,U_2)\)
be a bivariate random vector with joint distribution given by a Frank copula
\(C_\theta \)
, with parameter
\(\theta >0\)
. Let
\(X_1\)
and
\(X_2\)
be obtained by discretizing
\(U_1\)
and
\(U_2\)
through known non-degenerate marginal distributions with finite supports
\(\{1,2,\dots ,m_1\}\)
and
\(\{1,2,\dots ,m_2\}\)
, respectively. Then
\(\textrm{Corr}(X_1,X_2)> 0.\)
Proof
Let
\(X_i=q_i(U_i)\)
, where
\(q_i=F_i^{-1}\)
is the generalized quantile function of
\(X_i.\)
Since
\(F_i^{-1}\)
is non-decreasing,
\(q_i\)
is a non-decreasing step function on [0, 1]. By Hoeffding’s covariance identity for functions of bounded variation,
$$ \textrm{Cov}(X_1,X_2) = \int _0^1\int _0^1 \{C_\theta (u,v)-uv\}\textrm{d}q_1(u)\textrm{d}q_2(v). $$
For
\(\theta >0\)
, the Frank copula is positively quadrant dependent (see, e.g. Joe
2014
) hence
\(C_\theta (u,v)-uv > 0\)
for all
\((u,v)\in (0,1)^2\)
. Moreover, since
\(q_1\)
and
\(q_2\)
are non-decreasing,
\(\textrm{d}q_1\)
and
\(\textrm{d}q_2\)
are non-negative measures. Because the marginal distributions are non-degenerate, each
\(q_i\)
has at least one strictly positive jump at a point in (0, 1). Hence, the product measure
\(\textrm{d}q_1\otimes \textrm{d}q_2\)
assigns positive mass to at least one point in
\((0,1)^2\)
. It follows that the above double integral is strictly positive, implying
\(\textrm{Cov}(X_1,X_2)> 0\)
. Since the marginal variances are positive, it follows that
\(\textrm{Corr}(X_1,X_2)> 0\)
. Consequently, since
\(\rho _S(U_1,U_2)>0\)
for
\(\theta >0\)
, the Pearson correlation of
\((X_1,X_2)\)
has the same sign as the Spearman correlation of
\((U_1,U_2)\)
.
\(\square \)
Remark 1
The restriction
\(\theta >0\)
in Theorem
2
is imposed only to ensure positive association. In the bivariate case, when
\(\theta <0\)
, the Frank copula is negatively quadrant dependent and the associated Spearman correlation satisfies
\(\rho _S<0\)
.
By the same argument used in the proof of Theorem
2
, discretization through non–decreasing step functions preserves the sign of dependence (and is expected to attenuate its magnitude). In particular, if
\(X_1\)
and
\(X_2\)
are obtained by discretizing
\((U_1,U_2)\sim C_\theta \)
with
\(\theta <0\)
, then
\(\textrm{Corr}(X_1,X_2) < 0\)
.
The limiting case
\(\theta =0\)
corresponds to the independence copula, for which
\(C_0(u,v)=uv\)
. Hence
\(\textrm{Cov}(X_1,X_2)=0\)
by Hoeffding’s identity, and therefore
\(\textrm{Corr}(X_1,X_2)=0\)
.
2.3
Comparing (discretized) Frank and Gaussian distributions
In the introduction, we have already mentioned the similarities and differences between the Frank and the Gaussian copulas. In the previous section, we presented extensive numerical evidence suggesting that, like the Gaussian distribution, the Frank copula may also satisfy the extremal property whereby linear correlation is attenuated after discretization of the two random components. A deeper understanding of the difference between the two joint distributions can be obtained by examining the discrepancies between their corresponding discretized distributions, when the same level of Spearman’s correlation and also the same ordinal margins are considered. In order to compare the two distributions we can adopt one of the statistical distances between probability tables available in the literature. One of them is the total variation distance ( Devroye and Lugosi
2001
, chap.5); this distance, evaluated between two probability tables
\(\mathcal {P}=[p_{ij}]\)
and
\(\mathcal {Q}=[q_{ij}]\)
, sharing the same dimension (say,
\(m_1\times m_2\)
), can be written as
$$\begin{aligned} TV(\mathcal {P}, \mathcal {Q}) = \frac{1}{2}\sum _{i=1}^{m_1}\sum _{j=1}^{m_2} |p_{ij} - q_{ij}|, \end{aligned}$$
(5)
and is a measure ranging from 0 (when the two probability tables are identical) to 1 (when the two probability tables are “disjoint”, i.e.,
\(p_{ij}=0\)
if and only if
\(q_{ij}>0\)
).
In order to compare the bivariate ordinal distributions arising from the discretization of the Frank copula and the standard bivariate normal distribution, we set up the following numerical study, which is limited, for the sake of brevity, to
\(m=5\)
categories for both ordinal margins, which we assume to be identically distributed. We considered for
\(\rho _S\)
the values from 0.1 to 0.9 with constant step of 0.1. For the same value of
\(\rho _S\)
we determined the value of
\(\theta \)
of the Frank copula and the value of
\(\rho \)
of the bivariate normal distribution inducing it, and then we determined the bivariate probability mass functions obtained by discretizing the two bivariate continuous distributions,
\(\mathcal {P}\)
(Frank) and
\(\mathcal {Q}\)
(normal). We examined three different patterns for the probabilities of the ordinal margins: i) uniform: each category has probability
\(1/m=1/5\)
), ii) reverse U-shaped:
\(p_1=p_5=0.1\)
,
\(p_2=p_4=0.2\)
,
\(p_3=0.4\)
, iii) increasing:
\(p=i/15\)
,
\(i=1,2,3,4,5\)
. Then, we computed the total variation distance (
5
) between the two bivariate pmf and also the sign of the difference between homologous cells in the bivariate probability tables of discretized Frank and normal distributions (
\(\mathcal {P}-\mathcal {Q}\)
). The values of the total variation distance are reported in Table

2.4
Extension to the multivariate case
As noted in the introduction, Frank’s copula can be extended to any dimension
\(d>2;\)
however, the parameter
\(\theta \)
is now essentially restricted to positive values, and therefore the copula is only able to model the same level of positive correlation among the
d
variables. The closure-under-marginalization property of the Frank copula allows us to study the dependence among the
d
random variables by focusing on pairs of variables at a time.
Determining the induced
d
-variate pmf of the ordinal rv
\(\pmb {X}:=(X_1,X_2,\dots ,X_d)^\top \)
, obtained from discretization of the components of
\(\pmb {U}:=(U_1,U_2,\dots ,U_d)^\top \)
, once the
d
margins and the parameter
\(\theta \)
are assigned, is still straightforward, although increasing the dimensionality makes the computational cost increase more than linearly. Now each rv
\(U_i\)
,
\(i=1,\dots ,d\)
, of the
d
-variate rv
\(\pmb {U}\sim C(\pmb {u};\theta )\)
is discretized, according to the thresholds
\(\gamma _{i,j_i}\)
, with
\(j_i=1,\dots ,m_i-1\)
, into an ordinal rv
\(X_i\)
supported on
\(\left\{ 1,\dots ,m_i \right\} \)
. To determine the joint pmf one can again resort to the rectangle probability formula, which requires the evaluation of the
d
-variate Frank copula
\(2^d\)
times (Nikoloulopoulos and Karlis
2009
): and whose joint pmf can be expressed as
$$\begin{aligned} p(j_1,\dots ,j_d) = P(X_{1}=j_1,\dots ,X_{d}=j_d) = \sum \text {sgn}(\pmb {c})C(c_1,\dots ,c_d;\theta ), \end{aligned}$$
(6)
where
\(\pmb {c} = (c_1,\dots ,c_d)'\)
are vertices where each
\(c_i\)
is equal to either
\(\gamma _{i,j_i}\)
or
\(\gamma _{i,j_i-1}\)
,
\(i=1,\dots ,d\)
, and the sum is taken over all vertices
\(\pmb {c}\)
, and
\(\text {sgn}(\pmb {c})\)
is given by  Nikoloulopoulos and Karlis
2009
, Proposition1.1
$$\begin{aligned} \text {sgn}(\textbf{c}) = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} 1, & \text {if } c_i = \gamma _{i,j_i - 1} \text { for an even number of { i}'s}\\ -1, & \text {if } c_i = \gamma _{i,j_i - 1} \text { for an odd number of { i}'s} . \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$
In order to compute the correlation matrix for
\(\pmb {X}\)
, one can base it on the joint pmf (
6
), after computing the relevant margins for the pair
\((X_i,X_j)\)
, or directly use the algorithm for the bivariate case, relying on the closure under marginalization property of the
d
-variate Frank copula.
A possible way to relax the assumption of homogeneous dependence is to consider nested (hierarchical) Archimedean copulas (McNeil
2008
; Joe
2014
), in particular nested Frank copulas. In this framework, the variables are organized in a tree structure, where each internal node is associated with a Frank copula and its own dependence parameter. The number of parameters therefore equals the number of internal nodes in the hierarchy, allowing for cluster-specific levels of association. However, this additional flexibility comes at a conceptual cost. The resulting model is no longer a
d
-variate Frank copula governed by a single parameter, and exchangeability is lost. Consequently, the interpretation of the latent dependence structure becomes hierarchical rather than global, and the simplicity of the original single-parameter latent Frank construction is sacrificed.
3
Inference
3.1
Maximum likelihood estimation
If we want to estimate the discretized Frank copula model, based on a bivariate sample of ordinal variables, we need to recover the estimate for the copula parameter
\(\theta, \)
the estimates of the
\(m_1\)
probabilities
\(p_{h}^{(1)}\)
for the first margin, and the estimates of the
\(m_2\)
probabilities
\(p_{k}^{(2)}\)
for the second margin (we assume that we know that the first margin is supported on the first
\(m_1\)
positive integers and the second margin on the first
\(m_2\)
positive integers). We can obtain such estimates by maximizing the log-likelihood function:
\(\ell (\pmb {\psi })=\sum _{h=1}^{m_1}\sum _{k=1}^{m_2} n_{hk}\log (p_{hk}(\pmb {\psi }))\)
, where
\(p_{hk}\)
is the bivariate pmf in (
4
) and
\(\pmb {\psi }\)
is the parameter vector gathering the aforementioned scalar parameters and
\(n_{hk}\)
represents the observed frequency of the pair (
h
,
k
). Such an optimization is quite straightforward to implement, provided that certain precautions are adopted. Instead of maximizing over the
\(p_{h}^{(1)}\)
and the
\(p_{k}^{(2)}\)
directly, which can be somewhat challenging, since these parameters need to satisfy some obvious constraints, it is preferable to introduce two sets of unconstrained variables, say
\(z_1,z_2,\dots ,z_{m_1}\)
and
\(w_1,w_2,\dots ,w_{m_2}\)
and rewrite the probabilities according to the softmax transformation, i.e. as
\(p_{h}^{(1)}=e^{z_h}/\sum _{h=1}^{m_1}e^{z_h}\)
and
\(p_{k}^{(2)}=e^{w_k}/\sum _{k=1}^{m_2}e^{w_k}\)
. Then maximize the resulting log-likelihood function using standard unconstrained optimization methods. Their optimal values, say
\(\tilde{z}_1,\tilde{z}_2,\dots ,\tilde{z}_{m_1}\)
and
\(\tilde{w}_1,\tilde{w}_2,\dots ,\tilde{w}_{m_2}\)
are then used to find the optimal
\(\tilde{p}_{h}^{(1)}\)
and
\(\tilde{p}_{k}^{(2)}\)
. If one wants to avoid redundancy, one of the
\(z_h\)
(say
\(z_{m_1}\)
) and one of the
\(w_k\)
(say
\(w_{m_2}\)
) can be set equal to zero. If initial values for the
\(z_h\)
and
\(w_k\)
are needed in the optimization routine, one can consider setting the unknown marginal probabilities equal to the corresponding sample relative frequencies, say
\(\hat{p}_{h}^{(1)}\)
and
\(\hat{p}_{k}^{(2)}\)
, and use as starting value
\(z_h^{(0)}=\ln \hat{p}_{h}^{(1)} - \ln \hat{p}_{m_1}^{(1)}\)
and
\(w_k^{(0)}=\ln \hat{p}_{k}^{(2)} - \ln \hat{p}_{m_2}^{(2)}\)
.
Instead of performing a full-likelihood optimization, one can adopt a simpler (but unavoidably less efficient) two-step estimation procedure. First, we can estimate the marginal probabilities non-parametrically and independently, simply using the empirical cdfs. Then, setting these probabilities equal to their estimates, we can maximize the resulting log-likelihood function with respect to the unique parameter
\(\theta \)
. In this way, the optimization no longer involves
\(m_1+m_2-1\)
parameters, but only the copula parameter. This procedure is analogous to that suggested in Joe and Xu (
1996
) for copula-based models.
In dimension
\(d>2\)
, compared to the bivariate case, the only difference is that there are now
d
probability distributions on the simplex to be estimated, while there is still only one copula parameter, now constrained on
\(\mathbb {R}^+\)
. Then, we can associate to each marginal distribution a set of real variables. To variable
\(X_i\)
, with probabilities
\(p_1^{(i)},p_2^{(i)},\dots ,p_{m_i}^{(i)}\)
, we can associate the variables
\(z_1^{(i)}, \dots z_{m_i-1}^{(i)}\)
, and
\(z_{m_i}^{(i)}=0\)
, for
\(i=1,\dots ,d\)
. The probabilities are related to the unconstrained variables in this way:
$$ p_j^{(i)} = \frac{e^{z_j^{(i)}}}{1+\sum _{j=1}^{m_i-1} e^{z_j^{(i)}}}, j=1,\dots ,m_j-1, $$
whereas the last probability of
\(X_i\)
,
\(p_{m_i}^{(i)}\)
, is obtained by difference as
\(p_{m_i}^{(i)} = 1 - \sum _{j=1}^{m_i-1} p_j^{(i)}\)
. The inverse relationship is
$$ z_j^{(i)} = \log p_j^{(i)} - \log p_{m_i}^{(i)}, j=1,\dots ,m_j-1. $$
The log-likelihood function for the discretized
d
-variate Frank copula can thus be expressed in terms of
\(\sum _{i=1}^d m_i - d\)
unconstrained variables (the
z
’s), plus one additional variable (
\(\theta \)
), which is constrained to be non-negative if
\(d > 2\)
. By numerically maximizing the log-likelihood function, we obtain the MLEs of the
z
’s (and
\(\theta \)
), which can then be transformed back into the original probabilities.
The computation of the standard errors for the MLEs is quite straightforward. Standard errors are obtained from the observed information matrix. Let
\(\varvec{\eta } = (\textbf{z}, \theta )\)
denote the full vector of unconstrained parameters, and let
\(\widehat{\varvec{\eta }}\)
be the MLE. The observed information matrix is given by
$$ \widehat{\mathcal {I}}(\widehat{\varvec{\eta }}) = - \left. \frac{\partial ^2 \ell (\varvec{\eta })}{\partial \varvec{\eta } \, \partial \varvec{\eta }^\top } \right| _{\varvec{\eta }=\widehat{\varvec{\eta }}}, $$
and its inverse provides an estimate of the asymptotic covariance matrix,
$$ \widehat{\textrm{Var}}(\widehat{\varvec{\eta }}) = \widehat{\mathcal {I}}(\widehat{\varvec{\eta }})^{-1}. $$
Since the marginal probabilities are smooth functions of the
z
-parameters through the multinomial logit transformation, their covariance matrix is obtained via the delta method. For each marginal
i
, let
\(\textbf{z}^{(i)} = (z_1^{(i)}, \dots , z_{m_i-1}^{(i)})^\top \)
and
\(\textbf{p}^{(i)} = (p_1^{(i)}, \dots , p_{m_i}^{(i)})^\top \)
. The Jacobian matrix
\(J^{(i)} = \partial \textbf{p}^{(i)} / \partial \textbf{z}^{(i)\top }\)
has entries
$$ \frac{\partial p_j^{(i)}}{\partial z_k^{(i)}} = p_j^{(i)} \left( \delta _{jk} - p_k^{(i)} \right) , \qquad j,k = 1,\dots ,m_i-1, $$
where
\(\delta _{jk}\)
denotes the Kronecker delta, and
$$ \frac{\partial p_{m_i}^{(i)}}{\partial z_k^{(i)}} = - p_{m_i}^{(i)} p_k^{(i)}, \qquad k = 1,\dots ,m_i-1. $$
Thus, each block
\(J^{(i)}\)
has the familiar multinomial logit structure
\(\textrm{diag}(\textbf{p}^{(i)}_{-m_i}) - \textbf{p}^{(i)}_{-m_i} \textbf{p}^{(i)\top }_{-m_i}\)
in its upper part, with an additional final row corresponding to
\(p_{m_i}^{(i)}\)
. The full Jacobian matrix
J
is block-diagonal with blocks
\(J^{(i)}\)
, since each marginal depends only on its own
z
-parameters. The asymptotic covariance matrix of the estimated probabilities is then
$$ \widehat{\textrm{Var}}(\widehat{\textbf{p}}) = J \, \widehat{\textrm{Var}}(\widehat{\textbf{z}}) \, J^\top , $$
where
\(\widehat{\textrm{Var}}(\widehat{\textbf{z}})\)
is the relevant submatrix of
\(\widehat{\textrm{Var}}(\widehat{\varvec{\eta }})\)
. Standard errors are obtained as the square roots of the diagonal elements of this matrix.
As for computation time in the
R
programming environment, increasing the dimension
d
makes the optimization of the log-likelihood function substantially more challenging. In particular, the computational burden grows rapidly with
d
, leading to longer convergence times and a higher risk of numerical instability. To reduce the computational cost, it is often necessary to trade off some estimation accuracy by appropriately tuning the optimization settings, such as the maximum number of iterations, the convergence tolerance, and other parameters controlling the speed and precision of the algorithm. When convergence is slow or fails altogether, it may be convenient to resort to the two-step procedure introduced earlier, in which optimization is carried out only with respect to
\(\theta \)
, while the
d
sets of threshold parameters are estimated separately using the marginal empirical distribution functions. In this case, the estimated asymptotic variance
\(\widehat{\textrm{Var}}(\hat{\theta })\)
can be obtained by inverting the observed information, namely minus the Hessian of the log-likelihood function with respect to
\(\theta \)
, evaluated at its two-step estimate, with the unknown marginal probabilities replaced by the corresponding sample frequencies.

3.3
Remark
In the proposed latent copula model, likelihood-based inference is not affected by the particular numerical labels assigned to the ordinal categories, provided that the ordering of the categories is preserved. Indeed, the likelihood is based on the cell probabilities induced by the thresholds and on the corresponding observed frequencies, and therefore depends on category membership rather than on the numerical scores used to label the categories. However, numerical summaries that explicitly use the assigned scores, such as means, variances, and Pearson correlations, would generally change if the labels were replaced, for example, by unequally spaced values. In fact, Pearson correlation is invariant only under positive linear transformations of the scores. Consequently, the results concerning the implied Pearson correlation and its attenuation after discretization should be interpreted with respect to the conventional coding
\((1,2,\ldots ,m)\)
adopted in the paper, which is by far the most common approach in practice, however.
4
Application to real data
We now apply what we discussed in the previous section to two datasets available in the literature. The objective is to assess whether the latent Frank copula–based model can serve as a valid alternative to the customary latent Gaussian model. We examine the bivariate case in depth, as it is particularly useful for illustrative purposes and for understanding the key features of the proposed approach before moving to the multivariate setting.
4.1
ISSP2000
The dataset
issp2000
used in
prefmod
(Hatzinger and Maier
2023
) comes from the International Social Survey Programme (International Social Survey Programme (ISSP)
2000
) and focuses on individuals’ perceptions of environmental problems, their level of concern about issues such as air and water pollution, climate change, and resource depletion, and their willingness to support environmental protection through behavioral changes or economic costs (e.g., higher taxes or prices). It also includes questions on perceived trade-offs between environmental protection and economic growth, trust in institutions to address environmental problems, and self-reported pro-environmental behaviors. The original dataset covers a wide range of countries, enabling comparative analyses of environmental attitudes across different cultural, economic, and political contexts, as well as the study of how socio-demographic characteristics relate to environmental perceptions and policy preferences. Within
prefmod

issp2000
is commonly used to illustrate how observed rankings or ratings can be interpreted as discretized manifestations of continuous latent variables. The dataset contains 1, 595 observations from respondents in Great Britain and Austria. The primary focus of the analysis is on six environmental risk perception items measured on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“extremely dangerous for the environment”) to 5 (“not dangerous at all for the environment”). The items assess perceived environmental danger related to: air pollution caused by cars (

in parentheses. In order to assess the fit of the model to these data, we compute the usual Pearson chi-square and
G
-square statistics, as
$$\begin{aligned} X^2&=\sum \sum \frac{(n_{ij}-\hat{n}_{ij})^2}{\hat{n}_{ij}} \\ G^2&= 2\sum \sum n_{ij}\log \frac{n_{ij}}{\hat{n}_{ij}} \end{aligned}$$
where
\(n_{ij}\)
denote the observed cell counts and
\(\hat{n}_{ij}\)
the fitted counts. The values of the two statistics are 24.66 and 21.13, respectively. These statistics, under the null hypothesis that the generating model is the discretized Frank copula, are both asymptotically distributed as a Chi-squared rv with
\(m_1m_2-m_1-m_2=15\)
degrees of freedom (there are 9 parameters to be estimated: the
\(2\times 4=8\)
marginal probabilities plus the Frank copula parameter). The approximate
p
-values are then 0.055 and 0.133. However, in contingency tables with sparse cells or small expected counts (like the one we are examining), the usual asymptotic
\(\chi ^2\)
approximation to the distribution of the Pearson statistic may be inaccurate. In particular, when several expected frequencies are small, the convergence to the limiting chi-square distribution can be slow.
To overcome this difficulty, one may approximate the null distribution of the test statistic by means of a parametric bootstrap. Let
\(n_{ij}\)
denote the observed cell counts of a contingency table with total sample size
\(n=\sum _{i}\sum _{j}n_{ij}\)
, and let the discretized model under the null hypothesis
\(H_0\)
specify cell probabilities
\(p_{ij}(\xi )\)
, where
\(\xi \in \Xi \)
is an unknown parameter vector. We estimate
\(\xi \)
under
\(H_0\)
(by maximum likelihood) to obtain
\(\hat{\xi }\)
, and compute the expected counts
\(\hat{n}_{ij}=n\,p_{ij}(\hat{\xi })\)
. Let the value of the Pearson chi-square discrepancy be
\(X^2_{\textrm{obs}}\)
. To approximate its sampling distribution under
\(H_0\)
without relying on asymptotic
\(\chi ^2\)
theory, we can proceed as follows. For
\(b=1,\dots ,B\)
(with
B
sufficiently large, say at least 500), we generate a bootstrap table
\(n_{ij}^{(b)}\)
according to the model with
\(p_{ij}(\hat{\xi })\)
, re-estimate the parameter under
\(H_0\)
to obtain
\(\hat{\xi }^{(b)}\)
, compute
\(\hat{n}_{ij}^{(b)}=n\,p_{ij}(\hat{\xi }^{(b)})\)
, and finally evaluate the bootstrap statistic
$$ X^{2\,(b)}=\sum _{i=1}^{m_1}\sum _{j=1}^{m_2} \frac{\big (n_{ij}^{(b)}-\hat{n}_{ij}^{(b)}\big )^2}{\hat{n}_{ij}^{(b)}}. $$
The bootstrap
p
-value is given by the empirical upper-tail probability
$$ p=\frac{1}{B}\sum _{b=1}^B \mathbb {1}\left( X^{2\,(b)}\ge X^2_{\textrm{obs}}\right) , $$
or, to avoid zero
p
-values,
$$ p=\frac{1}{B+1}\left[ 1+\sum _{b=1}^B \mathbb {1}\left( X^{2\,(b)}\ge X^2_{\textrm{obs}}\right) \right] . $$
The same procedure applies to the
G
-squared statistic or any other statistic that can be computed over a contingency table. This approach provides a data-driven approximation of the null distribution and is particularly useful for sparse or high-dimensional tables. Obviously, since the estimation step has to be repeated
B
times, on
B
different contingency tables, the computation time can increase considerably, particularly in high-dimensional settings where each likelihood maximization is numerically demanding.
For the dataset at hand, the resulting
p
-value (with
\(B = 500\)
bootstrap replicates) is about 0.114, which is larger than the
p
-value obtained from the asymptotic distribution of
\(X^2\)
and indicates a reasonably satisfactory fit of the model. If we instead employ the
\(G^2\)
statistic (whose asymptotic distribution is the same as that of
\(X^2\)
), the bootstrapped
p
-value is 0.152, again larger than the approximate value computed previously.

In the dataset, the most frequent sextuple is (3, 3, 3, 3, 3, 3), which corresponds to a repetition of the central category and occurs 42 times (relative frequency 0.05166). The probability estimated by the fitted model for this sextuple is 0.02512, which is substantially lower, although it is the largest fitted probability among all sextuples. This discrepancy suggests the presence of excess joint concentration in the central (neutral) category that the model fails to capture. The second most frequent sextuple is (1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1), corresponding to a repetition of the lowest category; it occurs 40 times (relative frequency 0.04920). Its estimated probability is 0.02215, which again underestimates the observed sample frequency.
The pronounced over-concentration observed for the sextuples (3, 3, 3, 3, 3, 3) and (1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1) can be plausibly attributed to response-style effects. In particular, central-tendency bias and satisficing behavior may lead some respondents to repeatedly select the neutral category across items, generating excess joint mass in the central cell. Similarly, a subset of respondents may exhibit a stable low-category response style, systematically choosing the lowest option regardless of item content. These respondent-level behaviors induce sharp joint concentrations that go beyond the smooth dependence structure assumed by latent variable copula models, and therefore result in systematic underestimation of the corresponding joint probabilities. The same dataset was fitted using a discretized normal distribution with an unstructured correlation matrix via maximum likelihood estimation, yielding
\(6 \times 4 = 24\)
marginal probability parameters and
\(6 \times 5 / 2 = 15\)
correlation parameters, for a total of 39 parameters. The resulting maximized log-likelihood value is
\(-5388.126\)
. The resulting values of AIC and BIC are 10854.3 and 10889.7, respectively.
Comparison of the relative fit using the AIC and BIC criteria clearly indicates that the discretized normal model outperforms the discretized Frank copula. This result arises because the latter is unable to accommodate the substantial heterogeneity in correlations among the variables. Nevertheless, despite its inferior fit, the discretized Frank copula may still be useful as a more parsimonious and interpretable model when a simpler dependence structure is desired, particularly as a baseline or benchmark latent model, analogous to equicorrelated Gaussian structures.

The stronger smoothing induced by the Gaussian latent normal model dampens localized deviations in dependence, whereas the Frank copula allows for a more nuanced representation of joint behavior. This interpretation is consistent with the higher log-likelihood obtained for the Frank model, indicating a better global fit.

Conclusion
In this paper, we propose the Frank copula as a latent variable model for correlated ordinal data and investigate the effects of discretization on the induced linear dependence structure. Focusing on the bivariate case, we numerically analyze the relationship between the latent dependence parameter—expressed through Spearman’s rho—and the implied Pearson correlation between the resulting ordinal variables, for different choices of their margins. We conjecture, on the basis of extensive numerical evidence, that discretization of a Frank copula latent variable cannot inflate linear correlation, suggesting a behavior that appears numerically consistent with the Gaussian extremal property: in absolute value, the Pearson correlation implied by the discretized variables appears to be bounded in absolute value by the latent Spearman correlation, with equality attained only in limiting cases. The Frank copula provides a clear interpretation of dependence through a single scalar parameter. Moreover, unlike the Gaussian latent model, it offers a closed-form expression for the joint distribution function, which simplifies the evaluation of rectangle probabilities, likelihood construction, and correlation analysis. From an inferential standpoint, maximum likelihood estimation of the discretized Frank model is feasible and can be implemented efficiently using standard optimization techniques, including reparameterizations based on multinomial logit transformations. The use of parametric bootstrap procedures proves particularly valuable for goodness-of-fit assessment in sparse contingency tables, where classical asymptotic approximations may be unreliable. The extension to higher dimensions preserves structural coherence thanks to the closure property of the Frank copula under marginalization, although at the cost of reduced flexibility due to the single copula parameter, which imposes the same level of dependence on every pair of distinct variables. Despite this structural limitation, empirical applications and simulation results indicate that the discretized Frank copula can represent a viable, parsimonious, and interpretable alternative to the customary latent Gaussian approach, especially when a homogeneous and symmetric dependence structure is appropriate.
Its combination of methodological simplicity, computational tractability, and applicability to both bivariate and multivariate ordinal data makes it a useful benchmark model for a wide spectrum of practical applications involving correlated ordinal outcomes.
Future research may explore more flexible Archimedean or multi-parameter copula constructions and potential extensions to regression frameworks or mixed discrete–continuous settings.
