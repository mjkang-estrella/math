                                                 STRUCTURAL AND RIGIDITY PROPERTIES OF LIE SKEW BRACES


                                                                                  MARCO DAMELE AND ANDREA LOI


                                                    Abstract. We investigate structural and rigidity properties of Lie skew braces (LSBs), objects
                                                    essentially known in the literature as post–Lie groups, obtained by endowing a manifold with two
                                                    compatible group laws that share the same identity element. LSBs extend skew left braces, which
                                                    are central to the study of non-involutive set-theoretic solutions of the Yang–Baxter equation, to the




arXiv:2507.06214v3 [math.GR] 25 Feb 2026
                                                    smooth category. Our first main result (Theorem 1.1) shows that, for every connected LSB (G, ·, ◦),
                                                    linearity (in the simply-connected case) and solvability carry over from (G, ·) to (G, ◦), whereas the
                                                    converse direction is rigid: if (G, ◦) is nilpotent (respectively, semisimple) then (G, ·) is forced to
                                                    be solvable (respectively, isomorphic to (G, ◦)). Theorem 1.3 provides two “flexibility” statements:
                                                    every non-linear simply connected Lie group (G, ·) admits an LSB (G, ·, ◦) such that (G, ◦) is linear,
                                                    and every simply connected solvable Lie group (G, ◦) supports an LSB (G, ·, ◦) such that (G, ·) is
                                                    nilpotent. A third result (Theorem 1.4) provides a complete existence table for non-trivial LSBs across
                                                    the six standard Lie-group classes, abelian, nilpotent (non-abelian), solvable (non-nilpotent), simple,
                                                    semisimple (non-simple) and mixed type, identifying precisely when an LSB can be built and when
                                                    only the trivial or no structure occurs. Both the explicit constructions and the properties established
                                                    in our theorems rely on a factorisation technique for Lie groups, on the correspondence between LSBs
                                                    and regular subgroups of the affine group Aff(G, ·), which renders LSB theory equivalent to simply
                                                    transitive affine actions, and on the theory of post–Lie algebras together with their integrability
                                                    properties.

introduced in [2, Sections 3.3 and 4 ]. More precisely, the categories of LSBs and Post-Lie groups
are equivalent (see Subsection 2.2 below). an LSB can be seen as a generalization of the skew left

  In this paper, we investigate the structural and rigidity properties of LSBs, with particular emphasis
on linearity, solvability, and semisimplicity.
  Recall that a Lie group G is said to be linear if there exists a faithful linear representation
                                            ρ : G → GL(V ),
where V is a finite-dimensional real vector space.
   The notions of solvability and semisimplicity are of fundamental importance in the study of Lie
groups, particularly because of the classical Levi–Malcev decomposition, which asserts that any con-
nected and simply-connected Lie group (G, ·) can be expressed as a semidirect product G = R ⋊ S,
where R is a maximal solvable Lie group, called the radical of G, and S is a semisimple Lie subgroup,
referred to as a Levi subgroup. This structural decomposition highlights solvability and semisimplicity
as the key features that one naturally seeks to understand when analyzing the global structure of Lie
groups.
   Our goal is to understand how these structural properties behave under the transition from the Lie
group (G, ·) to the Lie group (G, ◦), and viceversa.
   These phenomena are expressed in the following theorem representing our first main result:
Theorem 1.1. Let (G, ·, ◦) be a connected LSB. Then the following hold:
 (S1) If (G, ·) is linear and simply connected, then (G, ◦) is linear.
 (S2) If (G, ·) is solvable, then (G, ◦) is solvable.
 (R1) If (G, ◦) is nilpotent, then (G, ·) is solvable.
 (R2) If (G, ◦) is semisimple, then (G, ·) and (G, ◦) are locally isomorphic.
    Results (S1) and (S2) show that linearity (in the simply connected case) and solvability are pre-
served when passing from the Lie group (G, ·) to (G, ◦), whereas (R1) and (R2) highlight rigidity
phenomena that constrain the possible structure of (G, ·), given structural assumptions on (G, ◦).
    It is important to observe that the assumption of simple connectedness is essential for (S1) in
Theorem 1.1 to hold (see Remark 6 below). Similarly, without the assumption of connectedness, (S2)
fails. Indeed, there exist LSB structures (G, ·, ◦) with G disconnected such that (G, ·) is solvable, but
(G, ◦) is not (see, for example, [30, Ex. 3.2]). In these examples, the group G has countably many
connected components. In the case where the LSB (G, ·, ◦) has G finite, i.e., dim G = 0 and G consists
of finitely many connected components, and hence we are in the realm of left skew braces, the question
of whether the solvability of (G, ·) implies the solvability of (G, ◦) remains open. This conjecture was
first posed by Leandro Vendramin and Agata Smoktunowicz in [31]. Partial progress on this problem
has been made; see, for instance, [30], [14], and [33].
    In [31] the authors show that if (G, ·, ◦) is a finite left skew brace whose additive group (G, ·)
is nilpotent, then the multiplicative group (G, ◦) is necessarily solvable. Combining this fact with
property (S2) of Theorem 1.1 yields the following compact-group analogue (see Section 3 for a proof
and Remark 7).
Corollary 1.2. Let (G, ·, ◦) be a compact (not necessarily connected) LSB. If (G, ·) is nilpotent, then
(G, ◦) is solvable.
   In contrast to Theorem 1.1, which highlights structural and rigidity phenomena, our second main
result demonstrates a form of flexibility inherent to LSBs:

Theorem 1.3.
 (F1) For any non-linear connected and simply-connected Lie group (G, ·), there exists an LSB
      (G, ·, ◦) such that (G, ◦) is linear.
 (F2) For any connected and simply-connected solvable Lie group (G, ◦), there exists an LSB (G, ·, ◦)

   It is worth pointing out that to the best of the author’s knowledge, the question of whether every
finite solvable group (G, ◦) admits a left skew brace structure (G, ·, ◦), such that (G, ·) is nilpotent,
remains open.

   The proofs of (R1) and (R2) of Theorem 1.1 are based on associating to an LSB (G, ·, ◦) a post Lie
algebra structure, namely a triple (g, [·, ·]· , [·, ·]◦ ), where (g, [·, ·]· ) (resp. (g, [·, ·]◦ )) is the Lie algebra
associated to (G, ·) (resp. (G, ◦)) related by a post-Lie product (cf. Def. 2.8 below).

which PLAs can be integrated, i.e., obtained by differentiating an LSB, is a subtle and active area of
research (see [2, 9, 11, 12, 18] and references therein). It is therefore of particular interest to construct
non-trivial examples of LSBs.

    To conclude, we stress how our results complement and extend, in the solvable and semisimple
cases, the classification provided in [13, Th. 5.1], which concerns PLAs. This comparison allows for
the immediate identification of several PLAs that cannot be integrated into an LSB. For example,
there exist PLAs (g, [·, ·]· , [·, ·]◦ ) where (g, [·, ·]· ) is a non-nilpotent solvable Lie algebra, while (g, [·, ·]◦ )
is reductive but non-abelian (see Example 5 below). According to condition (S2) in Theorem 1.1, such
PLAs are not integrable into an LSB.
    Another interesting implication of our results concerns the following open question posed by the
authors in [13], which relates to the existence of PLAs (g, [·, ·]· , [·, ·]◦ ) such that (g, [·, ·]· ) is nilpotent
and (g, [·, ·]◦ ) is perfect, i.e. g = [g, g]◦ . While we are currently not able to answer this question
definitively, we can nevertheless state that any such PLA, if it exists, would not be integrable into an
LSB due to condition (S2) in Theorem 1.1.

   The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 recalls the necessary background on Lie skew braces,
post–Lie groups and algebras, and their realisation as regular subgroups of the affine group Aff(G, ·);
in particular, it describes in detail the correspondence with simply transitive affine actions, introduces

Lie algebra. Section 3 contains the proofs of Theorem 1.1-1.3 and Corollary 1.2: parts (S1)-(S2) are

rigidity statements (R1)-(R2) follow from corresponding rigidity theorems for post-Lie algebras. The
flexibility results and the existence table are derived through an explicit factorisation technique for Lie
groups, the equivalence between LSBs and simply transitive affine actions (cf. Theorem 2.13 below),
and integrability criteria for post-Lie algebras.

        2. Lie skew braces, post-Lie groups, post-Lie algebras and affine actions
2.1. Lie skew braces. Let (G, ·, ◦) be a Lie skew brace (LSB), meaning that (G, ·) and (G, ◦) are real
Lie groups sharing the same smooth manifold structure, and such that the identity
                                             a ◦ (b · c) = (a ◦ b) · a−1 · (a ◦ c)                                    (1)
                                        −1
holds for all a, b, c ∈ G, where a denotes the inverse of a in the group (G, ·). From this it follows
that the two groups also share the same identity element denoted here by e. One can then define
the category LSB, whose objects are LSBs, and whose morphisms between two LSBs (G1 , ·1 , ◦1 ) and
(G2 , ·2 , ◦2 ) are LSB homomorphisms, that is, smooth maps φ : G1 → G2 such that
                            φ(a ·1 b) = φ(a) ·2 φ(b)         and     φ(a ◦1 b) = φ(a) ◦2 φ(b)
for all a, b ∈ G1 .
   The category of LSB forms a subcategory of left skew braces, as defined in [20] (in the purely
algebraic sense, without requiring a Lie group structure), namely the category consisting of those
objects and morphisms where the group operations are considered on underlying sets rather than
smooth manifolds. Indeed, all the results described in this subsection are natural extensions to the
smooth case of those established for left skew braces in the algebraic setting, as presented in [20].
Example 1. (cf. [20, Ex. 1.3 ]) Let (G, ·) be a real Lie group. Define a ◦ b = a · b for all a, b ∈ G. Then
(G, ·, ◦) is an LSB. Similarly, defining a ◦ b = b · a also yields an LSB structure on G. These LSBs are
isomorphic if and only if (G, ·) is abelian. For our aim these LSBs are not interesting since in both
cases (G, ·) =∼ (G, ◦).
Example 2. Let (G1 , ·1 , ◦1 ) and (G2 , ·2 , ◦2 ) be two LSBs. Then the triple
                                                       (G1 × G2 , ·, ◦)
is also an LSB, where the operations are defined componentwise by
            (a1 , b1 ) · (a2 , b2 ) := (a1 ·1 a2 , b1 ·2 b2 ),   (a1 , b1 ) ◦ (a2 , b2 ) := (a1 ◦1 a2 , b1 ◦2 b2 ),
for all a1 , a2 ∈ G1 and b1 , b2 ∈ G2 . This LSB is referred to as the direct product of the LSBs (G1 , ·1 , ◦1 )
and (G2 , ·2 , ◦2 ).
Example 3. (cf. [20, Ex. 1.6]) Let G be a real Lie group admitting a factorization G = G1 G2 , where
G1 and G2 are Lie subgroups such that G1 ∩ G2 = {1}. In particular, every element a ∈ G can be
uniquely written as a = a1 · a2 for some a1 ∈ G1 , a2 ∈ G2 . This factorization allows us to define a new
group operation ◦ on the underlying manifold of G, as follows: for any a, b ∈ G, let a = a1 · a2 , with
a1 ∈ G1 and a2 ∈ G2 . Then define
                                            a ◦ b := a1 · b · a2 .
With this operation, the triple (G, ·, ◦) becomes an LSB. Indeed the map
                                     Φ : G1 × G2 −→ G,             (a1 , a2 ) 7→ a1 · a−1
                                                                                       2
is a Lie group isomorphism between (G, ◦) and the direct product G1 × G2 .

   Let (G, ·) be a connected real Lie group. The group of Lie group automorphisms of G, denoted
Aut(G, ·), consists of all Lie group isomorphisms of G, i.e. smooth group homomorphisms from G to
itself with smooth inverses. When endowed with the subspace topology induced by the inclusion
                                                 Aut(G, ·) ⊂ C 0 (G, G),
where C 0 (G, G) is the space of continuous maps from G to itself equipped with the compact-open
topology, the group Aut(G, ·) becomes a real Lie group of finite dimension. For further details, see [22]
and [16].
Example 4. Let G1 and G2 be connected real Lie groups, and let
                                                    α : G2 → Aut(G1 )
be a Lie group homomorphism. Then the set G1 × G2 , endowed with the usual componentwise group
operation
                              (a1 , b1 ) · (a2 , b2 ) := (a1 · a2 , b1 · b2 ),
and with the twisted operation
                                   (a1 , b1 ) ◦α (a2 , b2 ) := (a1 · α(b1 )(a2 ), b1 · b2 ) ,
forms an LSB (G1 × G2 , ·, ◦α ). Here, (G1 × G2 , ◦α ) is precisely the semidirect product Lie group
G1 ⋊α G2 .

Definition 2.1. The affine group1 associated to a connected Lie group (G, ·) is the Lie group given by
the semidirect product
                                   Aff(G, ·) = (G, ·) ⋊ Aut(G, ·),
where Aut(G, ·) acts on G by evaluation.
   The group operation on Aff(G, ·) is then given by
                                               (a, f )(b, g) = (a · f (b), f g),
for all a, b ∈ G, f, g ∈ Aut(G, ·). Any Lie subgroup H of Aff(G, ·) acts smoothly on G:
                                        (x, f )a = x · f (a), a ∈ G, (x, f ) ∈ H.
  In particular Aff(G, ·) acts transitively on G and the stabilizer of any a ∈ G is a Lie subgroup of
Aff(G, ·) isomorphic (as Lie group) to Aut(G, ·).
Definition 2.2. Let (G, ·) be a connected Lie group. A subgroup H ⊆ Aff(G, ·) is said to be regular
if for each a ∈ G there exists a unique (x, f ) ∈ H such that x · f (a) = e, where e denote the identity
element of (G, ·).
  If H is a regular subgroup of Aff(G, ·) then it is not hard to see that the projection π1 : Aff(G) → G
onto the first factor, when restricted to H, is a bijection (see [20, Lemma 4.1]).
Definition 2.3. Let (G, ·) be a connected Lie group. A subgroup H ⊆ Aff(G, ·) is said to be Lie
regular if the following conditions are satisfied:
    (1) H is a regular subgroup of Aff(G, ·);
    (2) H is closed as a subspace of Aff(G, ·).

Lemma 2.4. Let (G, ·, ◦) be an LSB. Then the map λ is smooth.

is continuous when Aut(G, ·) is viewed as a subspace of C 0 (G, G) with the compact-open topology.

   The connection between LSBs and regular subgroups of the affine group is expressed by the following
proposition, which generalizes the classical correspondence for skew left braces (see [20, Th. 4.2 and
Prop. 4.3]). We sketch the proof in the smooth setting.
Proposition 2.5. Let (G, ·) be a connected real Lie group. Then there is a one-to-one correspondence
between:
      • LSBs structures (G, ·, ◦), and
      • regular Lie subgroups H ≤ Aff(G, ·),
such that (G, ◦) ≃ H as Lie groups. Moreover, two LSB structures on G are isomorphic if and only if
the corresponding regular subgroups are conjugate in Aff(G, ·) via an element of Aut(G, ·).

where λa is given by (3). The map

2.2. Post-Lie groups. We recall the definition of a post-Lie group given in [2].
Definition 2.6. A post Lie group is a triple (G, ·, ▷) where (G, ·) is a real Lie group and ▷ : G×G → G
is a smooth map such that:
    (1) For every a ∈ G the map
                                       L▷a : G → G, b 7→ L▷a (b) := a ▷ b                               (4)
        is an automorphism of (G, ·), i.e. L▷a (b · c) = (a ▷ b) · (a ▷ c), for all a, b, c ∈ G;
    (2) For all a, b, c ∈ G,
                                     (a · (a ▷ b)) ▷ c = a ▷ (b ▷ c).
  Given two post Lie groups (G1 , ·1 , ▷1 ), (G2 , ·2 , ▷2 ) a smooth map Φ : G1 → G2 is said to be an
homomorphism between the post Lie groups (G1 , ·1 , ▷1 ) and (G2 , ·2 , ▷2 ), if for every a, b ∈ G1 we have
Φ(a ·1 b) = Φ(a) ·2 Φ(b) and Φ(a ▷1 b) = Φ(a) ▷2 Φ(b). Therefore we can speak about the category of
post-Lie group PLG, whose objects are post-Lie groups and whose morphisms are homomorphisms
between the post-Lie groups.
Proposition 2.7. The categories LSB and PLG are isomorphic.
Proof. As shown in [2, Prop. 3.22], there is a functor
                                  F : PLG → LSB,          (G, ·, ▷) 7→ (G, ·, ◦)
where for all a, b ∈ G the operation ◦ is defined algebraically by
                                              a ◦ b := a · (a ▷ b).
  Conversely, by [2, Thm 3.24], one defines a functor
                                  G : LSB → PLG,          (G, ·, ◦) 7→ (G, ·, ▷)
where, for all a, b ∈ G, the post-Lie product is given by
                                                a ▷ b := λa (b),
with λa (b) defined by (3). Since, by Lemma 2.4, λ : G → Aut(G, ·) is smooth, this construction is
well-defined in the category of smooth manifolds. Finally, by [2, Thm 3.25], the two functors F and
G are mutually inverse. Therefore, the categories LSB and PLG are isomorphic.                     □

2.3. Post-Lie algebras. Although the correspondence between Lie groups and Lie algebras is classical
and well known, we briefly recall it here to fix notation. Given any Lie group (G, ·), there is an
associated Lie algebra Lie(G, ·) = (g, [·, ·]· ), defined as the tangent space Te G at the identity element
e, endowed with the Lie bracket induced by the left-invariant vector fields on G. Conversely, for every
finite-dimensional real Lie algebra (g, [·, ·]), there exists a real Lie group (G, ·) whose Lie algebra is
(g, [·, ·]· ). In fact, such a group G can be obtained as a quotient of the unique simply-connected Lie
group integrating (g, [·, ·]).
Definition 2.8. A post-Lie algebra (PLA) is a quadruple (g, [·, ·]· , [·, ·]◦ , ▷) such that both (g, [·, ·]· )
and (g, [·, ·]◦ ) are real Lie algebras (on the same finite dimensional real vector space g), and
                                             ▷ : g × g → g,       (ζ, η) 7→ ζ ▷ η,
is a bilinear map, called the post-Lie product, satisfying the following identities for all ζ, η, γ ∈ g:
    (1) [ζ, η]◦ − [ζ, η]· = ζ ▷ η − η ▷ ζ,
    (2) ζ ▷ [η, γ]· = [ζ ▷ η, γ]· +[η, ζ ▷ γ]· ,
    (3) [ζ, η]◦ ▷ γ = ζ ▷ (η ▷ γ) − η ▷ (ζ ▷ γ).
Remark 2. Notice that by (1) in Definition 2.8 the Lie algebra structure [·, ·]◦ is uniquely determined
by the pair ([·, ·]· , ▷). Hence, one can also define (see, e.g. [2] and [6]) a PLA as a triple (g, [·, ·]· , ▷),
where (g, [·, ·]· ) is a real Lie algebra and
                                             ▷ : g × g → g,       (ζ, η) 7→ ζ ▷ η
is a bilinear map satisfying:
     (1) ζ ▷ [η, γ]· = [ζ ▷ η, γ]· +[η, ζ ▷ γ]· ,
     (2) ([ζ, η]· + ζ ▷ η − η ▷ ζ) ▷ γ = ζ ▷ (η ▷ γ) − η ▷ (ζ ▷ γ).
Then one defines the so-called sub-adjacent Lie algebra (g, [·, ·]◦ ) via
                                             [ζ, η]◦ := [ζ, η]· + ζ ▷ η − η ▷ ζ.
Indeed, it is not hard to verify that the quadruple (g, [·, ·]· , [·, ·]◦ , ▷) is a PLA.
    An important class of examples of post-Lie algebras is given by pre-Lie algebras (also known as
left-symmetric algebras), which arise when the Lie bracket [·, ·]· is trivial. In this setting, the post-Lie
product defines a compatible Lie algebra structure via antisymmetrization.
Definition 2.9. A pre-Lie algebra is a triple (g, [·, ·]◦ , ▷) consisting of a finite dimensional Lie algebra
(g, [·, ·]◦ ) together with a bilinear map
                                             ▷ : g × g → g,       (ζ, ξ) 7→ ζ ▷ ξ
satisfying the identity
                     ζ ▷ (ξ ▷ γ) − ξ ▷ (ζ ▷ γ) = (ζ ▷ ξ) ▷ γ − (ξ ▷ ζ) ▷ γ           for all ζ, ξ, γ ∈ g.                   (5)
   The category of post-Lie algebras, denoted by PLA, is the category whose objects are PLAs

                           ϕ([ξ, η]·1 ) = [ϕ(ξ), ϕ(η)]·2        and   ϕ(ξ ▷1 η) = ϕ(ξ) ▷2 ϕ(η).

2.4. Affine actions. We begin by recalling the notion of a simply transitive affine action.
Definition 2.11. Let (G, ◦) and (K, ·) be two connected real Lie groups. An affine action of G on K
is a Lie group homomorphism
                                 ρ : (G, ◦) −→ Aff(K, ·) := K ⋊ Aut(K, ·).
We say that the affine action is simply transitive if for all k1 , k2 ∈ K, there exists a unique g ∈ G
such that ρ(g)(k1 ) = k2 .
   In the 1970s, Milnor [28] posed a fundamental question:
        Does every solvable, connected and simply-connected Lie group (G, ◦) of dimension n
        admit a simply transitive affine action on (Rn , +)?
   This question stimulated extensive research and initially led to several attempts to give a positive
answer. However, it was eventually shown that the answer is negative. Specifically, Benoist proved in [3]
the existence of an 11-dimensional solvable (even nilpotent), connected and simply connected Lie group
(G, ◦) that does not admit a simply transitive affine action on (R11 , +). Later, Burde and Grunewald
[4] generalize this to a family of examples in dimension 11 in and also exhibited a new example in
dimension 10. The core of their argument is to exhibit Lie groups (G, ◦) whose Lie algebra (g, [·, ·]◦ )
admits no post-Lie product ▷ capable of endowing g with a pre-Lie structure (g, [·, ·]◦ , [·, ·]· = 0).
   On the other hand, if one does not insist on the affine action being on an abelian Lie group, a
positive result can still be obtained:
Theorem 2.12 (Dekimpe [17]). Given a connected and simply-connected solvable Lie group G, there
exists a nilpotent Lie group K such that G acts simply transitively by affine transformations on K.
   The following proposition illustrates a natural connection between LSB structures on a Lie group
and simply transitive affine actions. While this connection is not always stated explicitly, it is implicit
in several works (see, e.g. [5]-[13]), especially at the level of post-Lie algebras and in the case where
(K, ·K ) is simply-connected and nilpotent (in this case one speaks of simply transitive Nill-affine
actions). This proposition makes the relationship precise for our purposes in the general setting,
namely for general connected real Lie groups.
Theorem 2.13. Let (G, ◦G ) be a connected real Lie group. The following are equivalent:
  (1) There exists a Lie group structure ·G on G such that (G, ·G , ◦G ) is an LSB;
  (2) There exists a connected Lie group (K, ·K ) and a simply transitive affine action
                                          ρ : (G, ◦G ) → Aff(K, ·K ).
Moreover, whenever one (hence both) of the above conditions holds, there is a canonical LSB structure
(K, ·K , ◦K ) on K and an isomorphism of LSBs

   Theorem 2.13 shows that studying LSB structures on Lie groups is equivalent to studying sim-
ply transitive affine actions. Consequently, the structural properties of LSBs can be translated into
geometric properties of affine actions, and viceversa.
   The following corollary summarizes several consequences of our results in the setting of affine actions.
It provides a reformulation of Theorem 1.1 and statement (F1) in Theorem 1.3 from the perspective
of affine geometry.
Corollary 2.14. Let ρ : (G, ◦G ) −→ Aff(K, ·K ) be a simply transitive affine action of a connected

 (S1’) If (K, ·K ) is linear and simply-connected, then (G, ◦G ) is linear.
 (S2’) If (K, ·K ) is solvable, then (G, ◦G ) is solvable.
(R1’) If (G, ◦G ) is nilpotent, then (K, ·K ) is solvable.
(R2’) If (G, ◦G ) is semisimple, then ρ induces a group isomorphism: (G, ◦G ) ∼   = (K, ·K ).

 (F1’) For every connected and simply-connected non-linear Lie group (K, ·K ), there exists a real con-
        nected linear Lie group (G, ◦) and a simply transitive affine action ρ : (G, ◦G ) −→ Aff(K, ·K ).

Notice that the proof of (F2) in Theorem 1.3 follows by combining Theorem 2.13 with Dekimpe’s

                              (aG0 ) · (bG0 ) = (a · b)G0 ,         (aG0 ) ◦ (bG0 ) = (a ◦ b)G0 .
   Because G0 is connected, condition (S2) of Theorem 1.1 tells us that the group (G0 , ◦) is solvable.
Next, (G, ·) is compact and nilpotent, and G0 is an open subgroup, so G0 has finite index in (G, ·).
Therefore |G| < ∞, and (G, ·, ◦) is a finite left skew brace. Nilpotency is preserved by quotients, hence
(G, ·) is finite and nilpotent. By [31, Cor. 2.23] the multiplicative group (G, ◦) is solvable. Since the
class of solvable groups is closed under extensions, the group (G, ◦) is also solvable. This completes
the proof 3.                                                                                           □
Remark 7. It is worth pointing out that, if Smoktunowicz–Vandramin’s conjecture stated in the In-
troduction is true, the nilpotency assumption in Corollary 1.2 can be relaxed to mere solvability. In
any case, repeating the argument above and exploiting the fact that, for every finite left skew brace
(B, ·, ◦), the solvability of (B, ·) together with |B| not divisible by 3 forces (B, ◦) to be solvable [19,
Cor. 2.3], we obtain the following consequence: let (G, ·, ◦) be a LSB such that (G, ·) is solvable and
the quotient G/G0 is finite of order not divisible by 3. Then the group (G, ◦) is solvable.
