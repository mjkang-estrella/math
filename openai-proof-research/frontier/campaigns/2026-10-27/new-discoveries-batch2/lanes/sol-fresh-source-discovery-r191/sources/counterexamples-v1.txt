                                                        AN INFINITE FAMILY OF COUNTEREXAMPLES TO THE
                                                                  POLYCIRCULANT CONJECTURE


                                                                           SAUL D. FREEDMAN AND MELISSA LEE


                                                      A BSTRACT. We disprove the Polycirculant Conjecture, which states that every transitive
                                                      2-closed permutation group is non-elusive, i.e. contains a derangement of prime order.
                                                      In fact, we prove a stronger result, answering a long-standing question of Marušič and
                                                      Jordan: there exists a vertex-transitive graph admitting no semiregular automorphism. To
                                                      do so, we employ recently developed methods of Chen et al. for constructing elusive groups
                                                      via non-split extensions, allowing us to construct an elusive group 76 ¨PSU3 p3q of degree




arXiv:2607.23423v1 [math.GR] 26 Jul 2026
                                                      16,464. We show that this group is the full automorphism group of seven of its orbital
                                                      graphs and hence is 2-closed. Our example extends to infinitely many counterexamples
                                                      of the Polycirculant Conjecture, and infinitely many vertex-transitive graphs admitting no
                                                      semiregular automorphism.




                                                                                      1. I NTRODUCTION

                                               A derangement is a finite permutation that has no fixed points. A classical theorem of
                                           Jordan asserts that every finite transitive group contains a derangement. Using the clas-
                                           sification of finite simple groups, Fein, Kantor and Schacher [8] showed that Jordan’s
                                           theorem may be strengthened to require the existence of a derangement of prime power or-
                                           der. However, perhaps surprisingly, it is false that every finite transitive permutation group
                                           must contain a derangement of prime order. For instance, the Mathieu group M11 acting
                                           on 12 points has derangements only of orders 4 and 8. Due to the seeming rarity of finite
                                           transitive permutation groups containing no derangements of prime order, such a group is
                                           termed elusive.
                                               Elusive groups have attracted significant interest (see for example [4, 5, 11]), in part due
                                           to their connection to the well-known Polycirculant Conjecture, posed by Klin [6, Problem
                                           BCC15.12] in 1997. Recall that the 2-closure of a permutation group G ď SympΩq is the
                                           largest subgroup of SympΩq with the same orbits on Ω ˆ Ω as G, and that G is 2-closed if
                                           it is its own 2-closure.
                                           Conjecture 1.1 (The Polycirculant Conjecture). No elusive group is 2-closed.

                                              This conjecture extends the earlier Semiregularity Problem, formulated by Marušič [17]
                                           and Jordan [16] in the 1980s. A non-trivial permutation is called semiregular if all of its
                                           cycles are the same length (so in particular, it has no trivial cycles).

Problem 1.2 (The Semiregularity Problem). Does there exist a vertex-transitive directed
graph admitting no semiregular automorphism?


   This problem includes the case where the arc set of the directed graph is symmetric,
so that the graph can be considered undirected. Observe that each derangement of prime
order is semiregular, and each semiregular permutation has a power equal to a derange-
ment of prime order. Hence the Semiregularity Problem asks if there is a vertex-transitive
directed graph whose full automorphism group is elusive. Note that, by definition, the
automorphism group of any finite directed graph is 2-closed. Hence a positive answer to
the Semiregularity Problem would imply that the Polycirculant Conjecture is false. On the
other hand, there exist 2-closed groups that are not the full automorphism groups of any
directed graph, such as the subgroup C2 ˆ C2 of S4 . Therefore, a counterexample to the
Polycirculant Conjecture might not provide an answer to the Semiregularity Problem.
   The Polycirculant Conjecture and Semiregularity Problem have received considerable
attention; see [1] for a survey of results. Perhaps most notably, Giudici [10] determined all
elusive permutation groups with at least one transitive minimal normal subgroup (hence all
elusive quasiprimitive groups) and showed that each satisfies the Polycirculant Conjecture.
A few years later, Giudici and Xu [12] proved that the conjecture is satisfied for every
group that is biquasiprimitive, i.e. transitive with each minimal normal subgroup having
at most two orbits. Hence in any counterexample to the conjecture, each minimal normal
subgroup must be intransitive, and at least one must have at least three orbits.
   Recently, Chen et al. [7] described a novel method of constructing elusive groups via
non-split extensions, and used the method to construct the first known examples of elusive
groups with odd and twice-odd degree. Their examples again satisfy the Polycirculant
Conjecture.
   In this paper, we further the methods of Chen et al. to construct the first known groups
that are counterexamples to the Polycirculant Conjecture, and the first known graphs that
are positive answers to the Semiregularity Problem. In fact, we construct infinitely many
such groups and graphs.
   To state our main results, we require the following notation. We will write N ¨G to
denote a non-split extension of a group N by a group G, with N : G denoting a split
extension of N by G (not to be confused with the index |G : H| in G of a subgroup H,
nor the set rG : Hs of right cosets of H in G). Additionally, for a prime p and a positive
integer a, we write pa to denote the elementary abelian group of order pa . Recall also that
an orbital O of a permutation group G on a set Ω is an orbit of G on Ω ˆ Ω, and that
the corresponding orbital graph ΓO is the directed graph with vertex set Ω and arc set O.
Clearly, each element g P G is an automorphism of ΓO , with pα, βqg “ pαg , β g q for each
pα, βq P O, and if G is transitive, then ΓO is vertex-transitive. If O is self-paired, i.e. if
pβ, αq P O for all α, β P Ω with pα, βq P O, then we can consider the self-paired orbital
graph ΓO as an undirected graph.

    We also use a graph product construction to extend the graphs from Theorem 1.3 to an

Corollary 1.6. There exists an infinite family of graphs of orders 16,464k for each k ě 1
that are vertex-transitive and do not admit semiregular automorphisms.

    We also need some theory concerning graph products to provide the machinery for
our proof of Corollary 1.6. Let Γ1 “ pV1 , E1 q and Γ2 “ pV2 , E2 q be two simple graphs,
i.e. without loops or multiple edges. We recall two well-known graph products. The Carte-
sian product Γ1 ˝ Γ2 and strong product Γ1 b Γ2 each have vertex set
             V pΓ1 ˝ Γ2 q “ V pΓ1 b Γ2 q “ tpv, wq | v P V pΓ1 q, w P V pΓ2 qu.
Two distinct vertices pv, wq and pv 1 , w1 q are adjacent in the Cartesian product if and only if
either v “ v 1 and w is adjacent to w1 in Γ2 , or w “ w1 and v is adjacent to v 1 in Γ1 . They
are adjacent in the strong product if and only if v and v 1 are either equal or adjacent in Γ1
and w and w1 are either equal or adjacent in Γ2 . Hence Γ1 ˝ Γ2 is a subgraph of Γ1 b Γ2 .
   A graph is called prime with respect to one of these graph products if it is non-trivial
and cannot be written as the product of two non-trivial graphs. If a graph is non-trivial and
not prime, we say that it is decomposable with respect to that product. We further say that
two graphs are relatively prime with respect to a graph product if there is no non-trivial
graph that (up to isomorphism) is a factor of both of them. We now prove some properties
of graphs constructed from Cartesian and strong products.
Proposition 2.5. Let Γ1 “ pV1 , E1 q and Γ2 “ pV2 , E2 q be simple graphs on at least two
vertices. Then the following statements hold.
    (1) The products Γ1 ˝ Γ2 and Γ1 b Γ2 are connected if and only if Γ1 and Γ2 are, and
        vertex-transitive if and only if Γ1 and Γ2 are.
    (2) If, for each i P t1, 2u, no two vertices in Γi have the same closed neighbourhoods,
        then no two vertices in Γ1 ˝ Γ2 have the same closed neighbourhoods. Separately,
        if Γ1 and Γ2 are connected and relatively prime with respect to the Cartesian

        and Γ2 do.

4.2. Proof of Corollary 1.6. Let Γ1 and Γ2 be any two distinct graphs from Computa-
tional Proposition 4.2. We define a family G :“ tGi | i P Nu of graphs by G1 :“ Γ1 and,
for each i P N,
                       G2i :“ G2i´1 ˝ Γ2 ,            and        G2i`1 :“ G2i b Γ1 .

   We claim that each member of G is a connected, vertex-transitive graph admitting no
semiregular automorphism. Our computations reveal that no two vertices in Γ1 or Γ2 have
the same closed neigbourhoods. Moreover, [13, Theorem 6.13] shows that if Γ1 or Γ2 is
not prime with respect to the Cartesian product, then its automorphism group is a direct
product of wreath products. By [13, Lemma 7.2, Theorem 7.18] and the above closed
neighbourhood property, the same is true for the strong product. However, Computational
Proposition 4.2 shows that X is the full automorphism group of each of Γ1 and Γ2 . The
unique proper non-trivial normal subgroup of X is N – 76 , which has no complement
in X. Hence X cannot be written as a direct product, nor as a single wreath product.
Therefore, Γ1 and Γ2 must be prime with respect to the Cartesian and strong products.
    We now proceed via induction. Suppose that, for some i P N, the graph Gi is connected
and vertex-transitive, that no two of its vertices have equal closed neighbourhoods, and that
it does not admit any semiregular automorphism. We immediately deduce from Proposi-
tion 2.5 that Gi`1 is connected and vertex-transitive, and that no two of its vertices have
equal closed neighbourhoods. By applying the previous paragraph when i “ 1, or Propo-
sition 2.6 and the definition of Gi when i ą 1, we see that Gi is prime with respect to the
product used to construct Gi`1 , and also relatively prime to Γ2 and (if i ą 1) Γ1 . Since Γ1
and Γ2 admit no semiregular automorphisms, it follows from Proposition 2.5(2)–(3) and
our inductive hypothesis that Gi`1 also admits no semiregular automorphism. Observing
that |V pGi q| “ 16,464i for each i ě 1 completes the proof of Corollary 1.6.
Remark 4.5. One can replace Γ1 and Γ2 at each step of the construction of G with any of
the seven orbital graphs from Computational Proposition 4.2, provided that the two factors
of G2 are chosen to be distinct. It is easy to see that each graph in the resulting infinite
family of graphs will be vertex-transitive and will not admit semiregular automorphisms.
As a result, this construction produces countably many families of positive answers to the
Semiregularity Problem. There are also likely to be other constructions, perhaps also based
on graph products, that extend Computational Proposition 4.2 to further infinite families of
vertex-transitive graphs that do not admit semiregular automorphisms.
