 Abstract. 
 
 
 In this paper, we investigate the expansion of the integral form Macdonald polynomials J_{\mu}(x;q,t) in the elementary symmetric function basis. Motivated by the connection between chromatic symmetric functions and LLT polynomials, we conjecture the e -positivity of a normalization of J_{\mu}(x;q,t) . We verify the conjecture when \mu is either a hook or a two-column partition. In the hook case, we express the e -coefficients as weighted sums over compositions. In the two-column case, we derive explicit formulas for the e -coefficients. 
 
 
 
 Key words and phrases: integral form Macdonald polynomials, e -positivity, chromatic symmetric functions
 
 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification Primary: 05E05; Secondary: 05A30, 33D15
 
 
 
 1. Introduction 

 
 
 Macdonald polynomials P_{\mu}(x;q,t) are a family of symmetric
functions introduced by Macdonald in 1988 [ 14 , 15 ] . Macdonald polynomials generalize many well-known bases
of symmetric functions, including Schur functions and Hall–Littlewood
polynomials. Moreover, due to their deep connections with various
areas such as special function theory, representation theory,
algebraic geometry, and quantum mechanics, they have been the subject
of extensive study since their introduction. In the same work,
Macdonald also defined the integral form Macdonald polynomials
 J_{\mu}(x;q,t) with coefficients in \mathbb{Z}[q,t] : 
 

 
 (1.1) 
 
 J_{\mu}(x;q,t)=P_{\mu}(x;q,t)\prod_{c\in\mu}(1-q^{\operatorname{arm}(c)}t^{\operatorname{leg}(c)+1}). 
 
 
 
 See Section 2 for the precise definitions. 
 
 
 
 In 2005 , Haglund, Haiman, and Loehr [ 8 ] made a major breakthrough in
Macdonald theory by discovering a combinatorial formula for the modified Macdonald
polynomials \tilde{H}_{\mu}(x;q,t) , defined by 
 

 
 (1.2) 
 
 \tilde{H}_{\mu}(x;q,t)=t^{n(\mu)}J_{\mu}\left[\frac{X}{1-1/t};q,1/t\right], 
 
 
 
 where X=x_{1}+x_{2}+\cdots , the square brackets indicate
 plethystic substitution , and
 n(\mu)=\sum_{i\geq 1}(i-1)\mu_{i} . Haiman [ 11 ] proved
that \tilde{H}_{\mu}(x;q,t) is Schur positive, i.e., its Schur
coefficients belong to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[q,t] . 
 
 
 
 In [ 8 ] , Haglund, Haiman, and Loehr expressed
 \tilde{H}_{\mu}(x;q,t) as a positive linear combination of
 \operatorname{LLT} polynomials. Introduced by Lascoux, Leclerc, and Thibon
 [ 13 ] , the \operatorname{LLT} polynomials \operatorname{LLT}_{\nu}(x;q) are
symmetric functions that are known to be Schur positive [ 6 ] .
In general, LLT polynomials are indexed by tuples of skew shapes. The
special case in which each skew shape consists of a single cell gives
rise to the so-called unicellular LLT polynomials , which can
also be indexed by Dyck paths. 
 
 
 
 The unicellular LLT polynomials are closely related to the
 chromatic symmetric functions \chi_{\gamma}(x;q) 
introduced in [ 17 ] when \gamma is a Dyck path of
length 2n corresponding to the incomparability graph of a natural unit interval
order. More precisely, Haglund and Wilson [ 10 , Eq. (6)] 
observed that the result of Carlsson and Mellit
 [ 3 , Proposition 3.5] is equivalent to 
 

 
 (1.3) 
 
 \chi_{\gamma}(x;q)=\frac{\operatorname{LLT}_{\gamma}[(q-1)X;q]}{(q-1)^{n}}. 
 
 
 
 The chromatic symmetric functions \chi_{\gamma}(x;q) have been shown to
expand positively in the elementary symmetric function basis (a
property we refer to as e -positivity henceforth) when
 q=1 [ 12 , 5 ] , and were conjectured by
Shareshian and Wachs [ 17 ] to have e -coefficients in
 \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[q] . 1 1 
 1 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 OpenAI [ 16 ] has claimed a proof
of this conjecture. 
 
 
 
 We note that ( 1.2 ) is equivalent to 
 

 
 (1.4) 
 
 J_{\mu}\big(x;q,t^{-1}\big)=t^{-\sum_{i\geq 1}i\mu_{i}}\,\tilde{H}_{\mu}\big[(t-1)X;q,t\big]. 
 
 
 
 The present work is motivated by the analogy between ( 1.3 )
and ( 1.4 ). On the right-hand sides, the unicellular LLT
polynomials \operatorname{LLT}_{\gamma}(x;q) and the modified Macdonald
polynomials \tilde{H}_{\mu}(x;q,t) are both Schur positive. In
view of this, the conjectured e -positivity of
 \chi_{\gamma}(x;q) motivates the investigation of
 e -positivity for suitably normalized specializations of integral
form Macdonald polynomials J_{\mu}\big(x;q,t^{-1}\big) . 
 
 
 
 With regard to Schur positivity, Haglund conjectured
(see [ 1 , Eq. (1)] ) that, for every partition \mu 
and every positive integer k , the symmetric function 
 

 
 
 \frac{J_{\mu}(x;q,q^{k})}{(1-q)^{|\mu|}} 
 
 
 
 has Schur coefficients in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[q] . Several special cases
have been proved in [ 2 , 21 , 22 ] . Furthermore,
Wilson [ 20 ] conjectured that, for every negative integer
 k , the expression obtained by replacing (1-q) with
 (q-1) and multiplying by a suitable power of q is also
Schur positive. 
 
 
 
 Building on this conjecture and the preceding discussion of the
 e -positivity of J_{\mu}\big(x;q,t^{-1}\big) under an
appropriate normalization, we propose the following conjecture. 
 
 
 Conjecture 1.1 . 
 
 
 For any partition \mu and any positive integer
 k , the following is e -positive: 
 

 
 (1.5) 
 
 q^{k\sum_{i\geq 1}i\mu_{i}}\frac{J_{\mu}(x;q,q^{-k})}{(q-1)^{|\mu|}}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 In this paper, we prove this conjecture when \mu is either a
hook or a two-column partition. To do this, we first consider the
 e -expansion of the integral form Macdonald polynomial: 
 

 
 
 J_{\mu}(x;q,t)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash|\mu|}c_{\mu,\lambda}(q,t)e_{\lambda}. 
 
 
 
 When \mu is a hook, we express the coefficients
 c_{\mu,\lambda}(q,t)=c_{\mu,\lambda} as weighted sums over compositions
( Theorem 4.2 ). As a consequence, we obtain a positive
 e -expansion of ( 1.5 )
( Corollary 4.6 ). When \mu has at most two columns,
we compute the coefficients explicitly as products
( Theorem 5.1 ), which implies the e -positivity of
( 1.5 ). In addition, for an arbitrary partition
 \mu of n , we give a product formula for the coefficient
 c_{\mu,(n)} of e_{n} ( Theorem 6.1 ). 
 
 
 
 The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In
 Section 2 we recall basic definitions and known
results. In Section 3 we give an e -expansion
formula for J_{\mu}(x;q,t) when \mu is a one-row
partition. In Section 4 we extend this result to hooks and prove
 Conjecture 1.1 in this case. Section 5 treats the
two-column case, and Section 6 the e_{n} -coefficient.
Finally, in Section 7 , we explain a connection to
 \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions and propose a conjecture on
the q\to 1 limit of ( 1.5 ).

 2. Preliminaries 

 
 
 In this section, we introduce the basic definitions and known results
that will be used throughout the paper. 
 
 
 
 2.1. Basic definitions 

 
 
 A composition of n is a sequence
 \alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{l}) of positive integers
satisfying \sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=n , denoted by \alpha\vDash n .
Each \alpha_{i} is called a part of \alpha , and the
number of parts is called the length of \alpha , denoted
by \ell(\alpha) . The sum of the parts of \alpha , denoted
by |\alpha| , is called the size of \alpha . A
 partition is a composition such that the parts are weakly
decreasing. If \sum_{i}\lambda_{i}=n , we say that \lambda 
is a partition of n and write \lambda\vdash n . For
notational convenience, we also write (0) for the empty partition
 \emptyset . 
 
 
 
 If a composition \alpha is equal to a rearrangement of a
partition \lambda , we write \alpha\sim\lambda . For
partitions \lambda,\mu\vdash n , we define the dominance
order by \mu\leq\lambda if 
 

 
 
 \mu_{1}+\cdots+\mu_{i}\leq\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{i}\quad\text{ for all }i\geq 1, 
 
 
 
 where both
partitions are extended by zeros. We also write (0) for the empty composition
 \emptyset 
 
 
 
 Given a partition \lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}) , its
 diagram is an array of unit squares, called cells , with
 \lambda_{i} cells in the i th row from the bottom,
left-justified. We often identify \lambda with its diagram. A
cell in row i and column j is denoted by (i,j) . The
 conjugate \lambda^{\prime} of \lambda is the partition
whose diagram is obtained from \lambda by reflecting across the
diagonal y=x . The reading order of \lambda is the
order of its cells obtained by reading the rows from top to bottom
and the cells in each row from left to right. 
 
 
 
 A partition of the form (r,1^{s}) with r\geq 1 and s\geq 0 
is called a hook . Here, a^{b} means the sequence of b 
 a ’s. Note that hooks include one-row partitions (n) and
one-column partitions (1^{n})=(1,1^{n-1}) . 
 
 
 
 Given a cell c\in\lambda , the arm (resp. coarm ) length of c=(i,j) is the number of cells
in row i to the right (resp. left) of c in \lambda , denoted by \operatorname{arm}(c) (resp. \operatorname{arm}^{\prime}(c) ).
The leg (resp. coleg ) length of c=(i,j) is the number of cells
in column j strictly above (resp. below) c in \lambda , denoted by \operatorname{leg}(c) (resp. \operatorname{leg}^{\prime}(c) ). See Figure 1 for an example. 
 
 c \mathbf{a}^{\prime} \mathbf{a}^{\prime} \mathbf{a}^{\prime} \mathbf{a} \mathbf{a} \mathbf{a} \mathbf{a} \mathbf{l} \mathbf{l} \mathbf{l}^{\prime} 
 Figure 1. For the cell c=(2,4) in \lambda=(9,8,6,6,3) ,
the arm, coarm, leg, and coleg lengths are
 \operatorname{arm}(c)=4 , \operatorname{arm}^{\prime}(c)=3 ,
 \operatorname{leg}(c)=2 and \operatorname{leg}^{\prime}(c)=1 , respectively. 
 
 
 
 For a partition \lambda , a semistandard Young tableau of
shape \lambda is a filling of \lambda with positive
integers such that the entries are weakly increasing from left to
right in each row and strictly increasing from bottom to top in each
column. We use \operatorname{SSYT}(\lambda) to denote the set of semistandard
Young tableaux of shape \lambda . 
 
 
 
 For a nonnegative integer r and a partition \mu , we use
the q -Pochhammer symbols 
 

 
 
 (a;q)_{r}=\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(1-aq^{i}),\qquad(a;q)_{\mu}=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\mu)}(a;q)_{\mu_{i}}, 
 
 
 
 where (a;q)_{0}=1 . For integers n,k with
 0\leq k\leq n , the q -binomial coefficient is defined by 
 

 
 
 \genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{q}=\frac{(q;q)_{n}}{(q;q)_{k}(q;q)_{n-k}}. 
 
 
 
 We also define 
 

 
 
 [n]_{q}=1+q+\cdots+q^{n-1},\qquad[n]_{q}!=\prod_{i=1}^{n}[i]_{q}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 2.2. Symmetric functions 

 
 
 Let \Lambda_{F} denote the F -algebra of symmetric functions
in infinitely many variables x=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots) with coefficients in
 F=\mathbb{Q}(q,t) . For a partition
 \lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{l}) , the monomial
symmetric function m_{\lambda} is defined by
 m_{\lambda}=\sum x^{\alpha}, where the sum is over all
sequences \alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots) obtained by
rearranging (\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{l},0,0,\dots) , and
 x^{\alpha}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}x_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots . The
 elementary symmetric function e_{\lambda} is defined by
 e_{\lambda}=\prod_{i}e_{\lambda_{i}} , where
 e_{n}=\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{n}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{n}} . The
 complete homogeneous symmetric function h_{\lambda} is
defined by h_{\lambda}=\prod_{i}h_{\lambda_{i}} , where
 h_{n}=\sum_{i_{1}\leq\cdots\leq i_{n}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{n}} . The
 power sum symmetric function p_{\lambda} is defined by
 p_{\lambda}=\prod_{i}p_{\lambda_{i}} , where
 p_{n}=\sum_{i}x_{i}^{n} . We also define e_{0}=h_{0}=p_{0}=1 . The
 Schur function s_{\lambda} is defined combinatorially by 
 

 
 
 s_{\lambda}=\sum_{T\in\operatorname{SSYT}(\lambda)}x^{T}, 
 
 
 
 where x^{T}=\prod_{i}x_{i}^{a_{i}} with a_{i} the number of
occurrences of i in T . 
 
 
 
 We note that each of \{m_{\lambda}\} , \{e_{\lambda}\} , \{h_{\lambda}\} , \{p_{\lambda}\} , and \{s_{\lambda}\} forms
a basis of the space of symmetric functions.
With a slight abuse of notation, for any of the bases \{g_{\lambda}\} 
listed above, we write [g_{\lambda}]f for the coefficient of g_{\lambda} 
in the expansion of a symmetric function f\in\Lambda_{F} in that basis. 
 
 
 
 Let E=E(t_{1},t_{2},\dots) be a formal Laurent series in
 t_{1},t_{2},\dots with rational coefficients. For
 f=\sum_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}p_{\lambda}\in\Lambda_{F} , the
 plethystic substitution f[E] is defined by 
 

 
 
 f[E]:=\sum_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}p_{\lambda}[E], 
 
 
 
 where p_{\lambda}[E]=\prod_{i}p_{\lambda_{i}}[E] and
 p_{k}[E]=E\left(t_{1}^{k},t_{2}^{k},\dots\right) . We refer the reader
to [ 15 , Ch. I, §8] for more details on plethysm. 
 
 
 
 
 2.3. Macdonald polynomials 

 
 
 The Macdonald polynomials P_{\mu}=P_{\mu}(x;q,t) are the
unique symmetric functions satisfying 
 

 
 
 P_{\mu}=m_{\mu}+\sum_{\lambda<\mu}u_{\mu\lambda}(q,t)m_{\lambda},\qquad\langle P_{\lambda},P_{\mu}\rangle_{q,t}=0\quad\text{if }\lambda\neq\mu, 
 
 
 
 for suitable coefficients u_{\mu\lambda}(q,t)\in F ; see
 [ 14 , 15 ] . Here,
 \langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{q,t} is the inner product on
 \Lambda_{F} determined by 
 

 
 (2.1) 
 
 \langle p_{\lambda},p_{\mu}\rangle_{q,t}=\delta_{\lambda\mu}z_{\lambda}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}\frac{1-q^{\lambda_{i}}}{1-t^{\lambda_{i}}},\qquad z_{\lambda}=\prod_{r\geq 1}r^{m_{r}(\lambda)}m_{r}(\lambda)!, 
 
 
 
 where m_{r}(\lambda) is the multiplicity of r in
 \lambda . The integral form Macdonald polynomials 
 J_{\mu}(x;q,t) and the modified Macdonald polynomials 
 \tilde{H}_{\mu}(x;q,t) are defined by 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle J_{\mu}(x;q,t) 
 \displaystyle=P_{\mu}(x;q,t)\prod_{c\in\mu}(1-q^{\operatorname{arm}(c)}t^{\operatorname{leg}(c)+1}), 
 
 
 (2.2) 
 
 \displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\mu}[X;q,t] 
 \displaystyle=t^{n(\mu)}J_{\mu}\left[\frac{X}{1-t^{-1}};q,t^{-1}\right], 
 
 
 
 where n(\mu)=\sum_{i}(i-1)\mu_{i} . In [ 8 ] , Haglund,
Haiman, and Loehr discovered a remarkable combinatorial formula for
 \tilde{H}_{\mu}(x;q,t) . To state their result, we need some
definitions. 
 
 
 
 For a partition \mu\vdash n , a filling \sigma:\mu\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{+} is an
assignment of positive integers to the cells in the diagram of \mu .
A filling is standard if the entries are precisely 1,2,\dots,|\mu| .
The monomial x^{\sigma} associated with the filling \sigma is defined by 
 

 
 
 x^{\sigma}=\prod_{c\in\mu}x_{\sigma(c)}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 For a cell c=(i,j)\in\mu , let \operatorname{South}(i,j)=(i-1,j) . Given a filling
 \sigma:\mu\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{+} , the descent set 
 \operatorname{Des}(\sigma) 
and the major index \operatorname{maj}(\sigma) 
are defined by 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle\operatorname{Des}(\sigma) 
 \displaystyle=\{c\in\mu:\operatorname{South}(c)\in\mu,\,\sigma(c)>\sigma(\operatorname{South}(c))\}, 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle\operatorname{maj}(\sigma) 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\operatorname{Des}(\sigma)}(\operatorname{leg}(c)+1). 
 
 
 
 
 
 A triple of \mu is a set \{u,v,w\} of three cells
with u,v\in\mu such that v is in the same row as u 
and strictly to its right, and w=\operatorname{South}(u) , which may be below
the first row. Let \sigma be a filling of \mu . We orient a
triple by starting at the cell whose entry is smallest, proceeding to
the cell with the next-largest entry, and ending at the cell with the
largest entry. If two cells have the same entry, the entry of the cell
that appears earlier in the reading order of \mu is considered
smaller. The triple is an inversion triple if this circular
motion is counterclockwise, and a coinversion triple if it is
clockwise. We denote by \operatorname{inv}(\sigma) 
(resp. \operatorname{coinv}(\sigma) ) the number of inversion triples
(resp. coinversion triples) of \sigma . Here, we define
 \sigma(w)=\infty if w is below the first row. Note that 
 

 
 
 \operatorname{coinv}(\sigma)=n(\mu^{\prime})-\operatorname{inv}(\sigma). 
 
 
 
 
 Theorem 2.1 ( [ 8 , Theorem 2.2] ) . 
 
 
 For a partition \mu , the modified Macdonald polynomial \tilde{H}_{\mu}(x;q,t) 
is given by 
 

 
 (2.3) 
 
 \tilde{H}_{\mu}(x;q,t)=\sum_{\sigma:\mu\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{+}}x^{\sigma}q^{\operatorname{inv}(\sigma)}t^{\operatorname{maj}(\sigma)}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 By ( 2.2 ), we have 
 

 
 (2.4) 
 
 J_{\mu}(x;q,t)=t^{n(\mu)}\tilde{H}_{\mu}[X(1-t);q,t^{-1}]=t^{n(\mu)+n}\tilde{H}_{\mu^{\prime}}[X(t^{-1}-1);t^{-1},q]. 
 
 
 
 To state a combinatorial interpretation for J_{\mu}(x;q,t) , we
introduce the necessary definitions. 
 
 
 
 We say that a cell u\in\mu attacks the cells in the same
row and strictly to its right, and the cells in the row below and
strictly to its left. A filling \sigma:\mu\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{+} is
 nonattacking if there are no cells u,v\in\mu such that
 u attacks v and \sigma(u)=\sigma(v) . 
 
 
 
 Let \mathcal{A}_{+}=\{1,2,\dots\} ,
 \mathcal{A}_{-}=\{\overline{1},\overline{2},\dots\} , and
 \mathcal{A}_{\pm}=\mathcal{A}_{+}\cup\mathcal{A}_{-} . We write
 |i|=i and |\overline{i}|=i . A superfilling is an
assignment \sigma:\mu\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{\pm} of elements in
 \mathcal{A}_{\pm} to the cells in \mu . We denote by
 |\sigma| the filling obtained from \sigma by replacing
each entry \overline{i} with i . We say that a
superfilling \sigma is nonattacking if |\sigma| is
nonattacking. We denote by \operatorname{neg}(\sigma) the number of entries
of \sigma in \mathcal{A}_{-} . Let \operatorname{NASF}(\mu) denote
the set of nonattacking superfillings of \mu . 
 
 
 
 Given a total order \prec on \mathcal{A}_{\pm} , the
 standardization of \sigma with respect to \prec is
the bijective filling
 \sigma^{\mathrm{st}}_{\prec}:\mu\to\{1,\dots,n\} obtained by
relabeling the cells in increasing order of their entries, where
ties between cells holding the same letter are broken by reading
order: a letter in \mathcal{A}_{+} read earlier is regarded as
smaller, and a letter in \mathcal{A}_{-} read earlier is regarded
as larger. 
 
 
 
 Haglund, Haiman, and Loehr [ 8 , Eq. (63)] found a
combinatorial interpretation for J_{\mu}(x;q,t) . We will use
the following reformulation from [ 7 , (A.30)] . 
 
 
 Theorem 2.2 . 
 
 
 We have 
 

 
 (2.5) 
 
 J_{\mu}(x;q,t)=\sum_{\sigma\in\operatorname{NASF}(\mu^{\prime})}x^{|\sigma|}q^{\operatorname{maj}(\sigma)}t^{\operatorname{coinv}(\sigma)}(-t)^{\operatorname{neg}(\sigma)}, 
 
 
 
 where \operatorname{maj}(\sigma)=\operatorname{maj}(\sigma^{\mathrm{st}}_{\prec}) and
 \operatorname{coinv}(\sigma)=\operatorname{coinv}(\sigma^{\mathrm{st}}_{\prec}) , with
standardization taken with respect to the ordering 
 

 
 (2.6) 
 
 1\prec\overline{1}\prec 2\prec\overline{2}\prec\cdots\prec n\prec\overline{n}\prec\cdots. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 3. One-row case 

 
 
 In this section, we give an explicit combinatorial formula for the
 e -coefficients of J_{(n)}(x;q,t) . We give two proofs of
this formula. We first define a weight of a composition with respect
to a one-row partition. 
 
 
 Definition 3.1 . 
 
 
 For a composition \alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}) of n ,
let 
 

 
 (3.1) 
 
 \operatorname{wt}(i,\alpha)=\begin{cases}1-q^{i-1}t^{\alpha_{j}}&\mbox{if $i=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{j-1}+1$ for some $1\leq j\leq k$},\\
q^{i-1}-1&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases} 
 
 
 
 For the one-row partition (n) , define 
 

 
 (3.2) 
 
 \operatorname{wt}_{(n)}(\alpha)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{wt}(i,\alpha). 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 It is helpful to visualize the weight \operatorname{wt}_{(n)}(\alpha) as in the
next example. 
 
 
 Example 3.2 . 
 
 
 Let n=10 and \alpha=(2,1,3,4) . Draw a row divided into
blocks of sizes \alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\alpha_{4} , that is,
 2,1,3,4 , from left to right, as illustrated in
 Figure 2 . The first cells of the blocks have
indices i=1,3,4,7 . We have 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle\operatorname{wt}_{(10)}(2,1,3,4)={} 
 \displaystyle(1-t^{2})(q-1)(1-q^{2}t)(1-q^{3}t^{3}) 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle}{\displaystyle\times(q^{4}-1)(q^{5}-1)(1-q^{6}t^{4})(q^{7}-1)(q^{8}-1)(q^{9}-1).

 Theorem 3.3 . 
 
 
 For n\geq 0 , we have 
 

 
 
 J_{(n)}(x;q,t)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\left(\sum_{\alpha\sim\lambda}\operatorname{wt}_{(n)}(\alpha)\right)e_{\lambda}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 To prove this theorem, it is convenient to use the following notation.
For a composition \alpha , write
 e_{\alpha}=\prod_{j}e_{\alpha_{j}} and let 
 

 
 (3.3) 
 
 Y_{(n)}=Y_{(n)}(x;q,t)=\sum_{\alpha\vDash n}\operatorname{wt}_{(n)}(\alpha)e_{\alpha}. 
 
 
 
 Then the identity in Theorem 3.3 is equivalent to 
 

 
 (3.4) 
 
 J_{(n)}(x;q,t)=Y_{(n)}(x;q,t). 
 
 
 
 We provide two proofs of this identity in the next two subsections.
The first proof is combinatorial and the second proof is recursive. 
 
 
 
 3.1. Combinatorial proof 

 
 
 In this subsection, we prove ( 3.4 ) by comparing monomial
coefficients on both sides using the Haglund–Haiman–Loehr formula
( 2.5 ). 
 
 
 
 For the one-row partition \mu=(n) , we can identify each
superfilling of \mu^{\prime}=(1^{n}) with its reading word. Using this
identification, we first restate ( 2.5 ) in terms of words on
 \mathcal{A}_{\pm} . We denote by W_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{\pm}) the
set of words of length n on \mathcal{A}_{\pm} . We define
 W_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{+}) and W_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{-}) similarly.
For \pi=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{n}\in W_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{\pm}) , we define
 |\pi|=|\pi_{1}|\cdots|\pi_{n}|\in W_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{+}) . For
 \pi=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{n}\in W_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{+}) , we write
 x^{\pi}=x_{\pi_{1}}\cdots x_{\pi_{n}} . 
 
 
 
 For \pi\in W_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{\pm}) , let \operatorname{neg}(\pi) denote the
number of letters of \pi in \mathcal{A}_{-} . We define 
 

 
 
 \operatorname{Des}(\pi)=\{1\leq i\leq n-1:\pi_{i}\succ\pi_{i+1}\text{ or }\pi_{i}=\pi_{i+1}\in\mathcal{A}_{-}\},\qquad\operatorname{maj}(\pi)=\sum_{i\in\operatorname{Des}(\pi)}i, 
 
 
 
 where \prec is the ordering of \mathcal{A}_{\pm} 
in ( 2.6 ).
For later use, we also define 
 

 
 
 \operatorname{Des}^{*}(\pi)=\{1\leq i\leq n-1:\pi_{i}\succ^{*}\pi_{i+1}\text{ or }\pi_{i}=\pi_{i+1}\in\mathcal{A}_{-}\},\qquad\operatorname{maj}^{*}(\pi)=\sum_{i\in\operatorname{Des}^{*}(\pi)}i, 
 
 
 
 where \prec^{*} is the ordering of \mathcal{A}_{\pm} 
given by 
 

 
 (3.5) 
 
 \overline{1}\prec^{*}\overline{2}\prec^{*}\cdots\prec^{*}1\prec^{*}2\prec^{*}\cdots. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Now consider \sigma\in\operatorname{NASF}((1^{n})) and let \pi be the
reading word of \sigma . Then \operatorname{maj}(\sigma)=\operatorname{maj}(\pi) and
 \operatorname{neg}(\sigma)=\operatorname{neg}(\pi) . Since the one-column partition (1^{n}) 
contains no attacking pairs, the map \sigma\mapsto\pi is a
bijection from \operatorname{NASF}((1^{n})) to W_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{\pm}) . Moreover,
since (1^{n}) has no triples, we always have
 \operatorname{coinv}(\sigma)=0 . Therefore, ( 2.5 ) gives 
 

 
 (3.6) 
 
 J_{(n)}(x;q,t)=\sum_{\pi\in W_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{\pm})}x^{|\pi|}q^{\operatorname{maj}(\pi)}(-t)^{\operatorname{neg}(\pi)}. 
 
 
 
 
 Lemma 3.4 . 
 
 
 We have 
 

 
 
 Y_{(n)}(x;q,t)=\sum_{\pi\in W_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{\pm})}x^{|\pi|}q^{\operatorname{maj}^{*}(\pi)}(-t)^{\operatorname{neg}(\pi)}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 For a composition \alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})\vDash n 
and 1\leq i\leq k , we define the i th block of
 \alpha to be 
 

 
 
 B_{i}(\alpha)=\{\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{i-1}+1,\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{i-1}+2,\dots,\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{i}\}. 
 
 
 
 By definition, we have 
 

 
 (3.7) 
 
 Y_{(n)}(x;q,t)=\sum_{\alpha\vDash n}\operatorname{wt}_{(n)}(\alpha)e_{\alpha}=\sum_{(\alpha,\pi)\in C_{n}}\operatorname{wt}_{(n)}(\alpha)x^{\pi}, 
 
 
 
 where C_{n} is the set of pairs (\alpha,\pi) such that
 \alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})\vDash n ,
 \pi=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{n}\in W_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{+}) , and
the subword of \pi at positions in B_{i}(\alpha) is
strictly decreasing for each 1\leq i\leq k . 
 
 
 
 Now we reformulate ( 3.7 ) using words on
 \mathcal{A}_{\pm} . Writing
 B_{i}(\alpha)=\{r_{i},r_{i}+1,\dots,s_{i}\} , we have 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle\operatorname{wt}_{(n)}(\alpha) 
 \displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left((1-q^{r_{i}-1}t^{\alpha_{i}})\prod_{j=r_{i}+1}^{s_{i}}(q^{j-1}-1)\right) 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\prod_{j=r_{i}+1}^{s_{i}}(q^{j-1}-1)+q^{r_{i}-1}(-t)^{\alpha_{i}}\prod_{j=r_{i}+1}^{s_{i}}(1-q^{j-1})\right). 
 
 
 
 Choosing the first or second term in the i th factor can be
interpreted as leaving the subword \pi_{r_{i}}\cdots\pi_{s_{i}} 
unchanged or replacing it with
 \overline{\pi_{s_{i}}}\cdots\overline{\pi_{r_{i}}} ,
respectively. Using this idea, we define D_{n} to be the set of
pairs (\alpha,\pi) such that
 \alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})\vDash n ,
 \pi=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{n}\in W_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{\pm}) , and for each
 1\leq i\leq k , the subword of \pi at positions in
 B_{i}(\alpha) is either a strictly decreasing word on
 \mathcal{A}_{+} or a strictly increasing word on
 \mathcal{A}_{-} with respect to the order \prec in
( 2.6 ). For (\alpha,\pi)\in D_{n} , we define 
 

 
 
 \operatorname{wt}(\alpha,\pi)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{wt}(i,\alpha,\pi), 
 
 
 
 where 
 

 
 
 \operatorname{wt}(i,\alpha,\pi)=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $i=\min(B_{j}(\alpha))$ for some $1\leq j\leq\ell(\alpha)$
and $\pi_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{+}$},\\
q^{i-1}(-t)^{\alpha_{j}}&\mbox{if $i=\min(B_{j}(\alpha))$ for some $1\leq j\leq\ell(\alpha)$
and $\pi_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{-}$},\\
q^{i-1}-1&\mbox{if $i\neq\min(B_{j}(\alpha))$ for all $1\leq j\leq\ell(\alpha)$
and $\pi_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{+}$},\\
1-q^{i-1}&\mbox{if $i\neq\min(B_{j}(\alpha))$ for all $1\leq j\leq\ell(\alpha)$
and $\pi_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{-}$}.\end{cases} 
 
 
 
 Then ( 3.7 ) can be rewritten as 
 

 
 
 Y_{(n)}(x;q,t)=\sum_{(\alpha,\pi)\in D_{n}}\operatorname{wt}(\alpha,\pi)x^{|\pi|}=\sum_{\pi\in W_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{\pm})}x^{|\pi|}\sum_{\alpha:(\alpha,\pi)\in D_{n}}\operatorname{wt}(\alpha,\pi), 
 
 
 
 where x^{|\pi|}=x_{|\pi_{1}|}\cdots x_{|\pi_{n}|} .
Therefore, it suffices to show that for each
 \pi\in W_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{\pm}) , 
 

 
 (3.8) 
 
 \sum_{\alpha\vDash n}\operatorname{wt}(\alpha,\pi)=q^{\operatorname{maj}^{*}(\pi)}(-t)^{\operatorname{neg}(\pi)}, 
 
 
 
 where we extend the definition of \operatorname{wt}(\alpha,\pi) to all
compositions \alpha\vDash n by setting
 \operatorname{wt}(\alpha,\pi)=0 unless (\alpha,\pi)\in D_{n} . 
 
 
 
 Identifying a composition \alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}) 
with its blocks B_{1}(\alpha),\dots,B_{k}(\alpha) , we can construct
a composition of n by choosing whether i and i+1 
belong to the same block for each 1\leq i\leq n-1 . We say that
there is a cut between i and i+1 if they belong to
different blocks. Note that the cuts can be chosen independently, so
there are 2^{n-1} compositions of n in total. 
 
 
 
 Now we fix \pi\in W_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{\pm}) . We define the weights
 c_{i}(\pi) and d_{i}(\pi) for cutting and not cutting,
respectively, between i and i+1 according to the
signs of \pi_{i} and \pi_{i+1} , as follows: 
 

 
 
 \begin{array}[]{c|c|c}\text{signs of }(\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1})&c_{i}(\pi)&d_{i}(\pi)\\
\hline\cr(+,+)&1&(q^{i}-1)\chi(|\pi_{i}|>|\pi_{i+1}|)\\
(-,-)&q^{i}&(1-q^{i})\chi(|\pi_{i}|<|\pi_{i+1}|)\\
(+,-)&q^{i}&0\\
(-,+)&1&0\end{array} 
 
 
 
 Here \chi(P)=1 if P holds and \chi(P)=0 otherwise.
Then, by the construction and the definition of
 \operatorname{wt}(\alpha,\pi) , we have 
 

 
 (3.9) 
 
 \sum_{\alpha\vDash n}\operatorname{wt}(\alpha,\pi)=(-t)^{\operatorname{neg}(\pi)}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(c_{i}(\pi)+d_{i}(\pi)). 
 
 
 
 On the other hand, for each 1\leq i\leq n-1 , we have 
 

 
 
 c_{i}(\pi)+d_{i}(\pi)=\begin{cases}q^{i}&\mbox{if $\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1}\in\mathcal{A}_{+}$ and $\pi_{i}>\pi_{i+1}$},\\
1&\mbox{if $\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1}\in\mathcal{A}_{+}$ and $\pi_{i}\leq\pi_{i+1}$},\\
1&\mbox{if $\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1}\in\mathcal{A}_{-}$ and $|\pi_{i}|<|\pi_{i+1}|$},\\
q^{i}&\mbox{if $\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1}\in\mathcal{A}_{-}$ and $|\pi_{i}|\geq|\pi_{i+1}|$},\\
q^{i}&\mbox{if $\pi_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{+}$ and $\pi_{i+1}\in\mathcal{A}_{-}$,}\\
1&\mbox{if $\pi_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{-}$ and $\pi_{i+1}\in\mathcal{A}_{+}$.}\end{cases} 
 
 
 
 This implies that c_{i}(\pi)+d_{i}(\pi) is equal to q^{i} if
 i\in\operatorname{Des}^{*}(\pi) and 1 otherwise.
Therefore, ( 3.8 ) follows from ( 3.9 ).
∎ 
 
 
 
 
 For a composition \beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})\vDash n , let
 B(\beta) be the set of words
 \pi\in W_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{\pm}) in which i and
 \overline{i} occur \beta_{i} times in total for each
 1\leq i\leq d . Equivalently, \pi\in B(\beta) if and only if
 x^{|\pi|}=x^{\beta} . 
 
 
 Lemma 3.5 . 
 
 
 For \beta\vDash n , we have 
 

 
 
 \sum_{\pi\in B(\beta)}q^{\operatorname{maj}(\pi)}(-t)^{\operatorname{neg}(\pi)}=\sum_{\pi\in B(\beta)}q^{\operatorname{maj}^{*}(\pi)}(-t)^{\operatorname{neg}(\pi)}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 Let \beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})\vDash n . For a word
 \pi^{+} on \mathcal{A}_{+} and a word \pi^{-} on
 \mathcal{A}_{-} , a shuffle of \pi^{+} and \pi^{-} 
is a word \pi on \mathcal{A}_{\pm} such that the subword
of \pi consisting of letters in \mathcal{A}_{+} 
(resp. \mathcal{A}_{-} ) is equal to \pi^{+} 
(resp. \pi^{-} ). Let \operatorname{sh}(\pi^{+},\pi^{-}) denote the set of
such shuffles. Let V(\beta) be the set of pairs
 (\pi^{+},\pi^{-}) of words on \mathcal{A}_{+} and
 \mathcal{A}_{-} , respectively, such that, for each
 1\leq i\leq d , the total number of occurrences of i in
 \pi^{+} and \overline{i} in \pi^{-} is \beta_{i} . Then we
have 
 

 
 (3.10) 
 
 \sum_{\pi\in B(\beta)}q^{\operatorname{maj}(\pi)}(-t)^{\operatorname{neg}(\pi)}=\sum_{(\pi^{+},\pi^{-})\in V(\beta)}(-t)^{k}\sum_{\pi\in\operatorname{sh}(\pi^{+},\pi^{-})}q^{\operatorname{maj}(\pi)}, 
 
 
 
 where k is the total number of letters in \pi^{-} .
Since the identity ( 3.10 ) with each \operatorname{maj} replaced by
 \operatorname{maj}^{*} also holds, it suffices to show that for
 (\pi^{+},\pi^{-})\in V(\beta) , we have 
 

 
 (3.11) 
 
 \sum_{\pi\in\operatorname{sh}(\pi^{+},\pi^{-})}q^{\operatorname{maj}(\pi)}=\sum_{\pi\in\operatorname{sh}(\pi^{+},\pi^{-})}q^{\operatorname{maj}^{*}(\pi)}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Let \sigma^{+} and \sigma^{-} (resp. \tau^{+} and
 \tau^{-} ) be the words such that the concatenation
 \sigma^{+}\sigma^{-} (resp. \tau^{+}\tau^{-} ) is the
standardization of \pi^{+}\pi^{-} with respect to the order
 \prec in ( 2.6 ) (resp. \prec^{*} in ( 3.5 )).
Then by the definitions of \operatorname{maj} and \operatorname{maj}^{*} and by the
well-known fact [ 19 , Ch. 3, Exercise 162(b)] , we have 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\operatorname{sh}(\pi^{+},\pi^{-})}q^{\operatorname{maj}(\pi)} 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\operatorname{sh}(\sigma^{+},\sigma^{-})}q^{\operatorname{maj}(\sigma)}=q^{\operatorname{maj}(\sigma^{+})+\operatorname{maj}(\sigma^{-})}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{q}, 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\operatorname{sh}(\pi^{+},\pi^{-})}q^{\operatorname{maj}^{*}(\pi)} 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in\operatorname{sh}(\tau^{+},\tau^{-})}q^{\operatorname{maj}(\tau)}=q^{\operatorname{maj}(\tau^{+})+\operatorname{maj}(\tau^{-})}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{q}. 
 
 
 
 Since the orderings ( 2.6 ) and ( 3.5 ) agree on
 \mathcal{A}_{+} and on \mathcal{A}_{-} , we
have \operatorname{maj}(\sigma^{+})=\operatorname{maj}(\tau^{+}) and
 \operatorname{maj}(\sigma^{-})=\operatorname{maj}(\tau^{-}) . Therefore, we obtain
( 3.11 ), which completes the proof.
∎ 
 
 
 
 
 Now we prove ( 3.4 ). 
 
 
 First proof of ( 3.4 ). 
 
 
 For every \beta\vDash n , ( 3.6 )
and Lemmas 3.5 and 3.4 give 
 

 
 
 [x^{\beta}]J_{(n)}=\sum_{\pi\in B(\beta)}q^{\operatorname{maj}(\pi)}(-t)^{\operatorname{neg}(\pi)}=\sum_{\pi\in B(\beta)}q^{\operatorname{maj}^{*}(\pi)}(-t)^{\operatorname{neg}(\pi)}=[x^{\beta}]Y_{(n)}. 
 
 
 
 Since both sides of ( 3.4 ) are symmetric functions, the
identity follows.
∎ 
 
 
 
 
 
 3.2. Recursive proof 

 
 
 In this subsection, we give a recursive proof of ( 3.4 ). We
first recall the generating function of J_{(n)}(x;q,t) . 
 
 
 Lemma 3.6 . 
 
 
 We have 
 

 
 
 \sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{J_{(n)}(x;q,t)}{(q;q)_{n}}u^{n}=\prod_{i\geq 1}A(x_{i}u),\qquad A(v):=\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{(t;q)_{m}}{(q;q)_{m}}v^{m}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 Let g_{n}(x;q,t) be the symmetric functions given by
 [ 15 , Ch. VI, (2.8), p. 311] 
 

 
 
 \sum_{n\geq 0}g_{n}(x;q,t)u^{n}=\prod_{i\geq 1}\frac{(tx_{i}u;q)_{\infty}}{(x_{i}u;q)_{\infty}}. 
 
 
 
 By [ 15 , Ch. VI, (4.9), p. 323] and [ 15 , Ch. VI, (8.1)
and (8.3), p. 352] , we have 
 

 
 
 J_{(n)}(x;q,t)=(t;q)_{n}P_{(n)}(x;q,t)=(q;q)_{n}g_{n}(x;q,t). 
 
 
 
 The q -binomial identity [ 15 , Ch. VI, §2, Example 1,
p. 314] gives 
 

 
 
 A(v)=\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{(t;q)_{m}}{(q;q)_{m}}v^{m}=\frac{(tv;q)_{\infty}}{(v;q)_{\infty}}. 
 
 
 
 Combining these identities, we obtain 
 

 
 
 \sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{J_{(n)}(x;q,t)}{(q;q)_{n}}u^{n}=\sum_{n\geq 0}g_{n}(x;q,t)u^{n}=\prod_{i\geq 1}A(x_{i}u), 
 
 
 
 as desired.
∎ 
 
 
 
 Second proof of ( 3.4 ). 
 
 
 We first derive a recurrence for Y_{(n)} by considering the
last part of each composition of n\geq 1 . For 1\leq k\leq n ,
write \alpha=(\beta,k) with \beta\vDash n-k . By
( 3.1 ) and ( 3.2 ), we have 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle\operatorname{wt}_{(n)}(\alpha) 
 \displaystyle=\operatorname{wt}_{(n-k)}(\beta)(1-q^{n-k}t^{k})\prod_{j=n-k+1}^{n-1}(q^{j}-1) 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=(-1)^{k-1}(1-q^{n-k}t^{k})\frac{(q;q)_{n-1}}{(q;q)_{n-k}}\operatorname{wt}_{(n-k)}(\beta). 
 
 
 
 Summing over \beta and k , using
 e_{\alpha}=e_{\beta}e_{k} , and dividing by (q;q)_{n-1} ,
we obtain 
 

 
 (3.12) 
 
 \frac{Y_{(n)}}{(q;q)_{n-1}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^{k-1}(1-q^{n-k}t^{k})e_{k}\frac{Y_{(n-k)}}{(q;q)_{n-k}}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 On the other hand, for the function A(v) in
 Lemma 3.6 , we have 
 

 
 
 (1-v)A(v)=(1-tv)A(qv). 
 
 
 
 Multiplying these identities over i\geq 1 with v=x_{i}u 
and applying Lemma 3.6 gives 
 

 
 
 \left(\prod_{i\geq 1}(1-x_{i}u)\right)\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{J_{(n)}}{(q;q)_{n}}u^{n}=\left(\prod_{i\geq 1}(1-tx_{i}u)\right)\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{J_{(n)}}{(q;q)_{n}}(qu)^{n}. 
 
 
 
 Using \prod_{i\geq 1}(1-x_{i}u)=\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{k}e_{k}u^{k} 
and comparing coefficients of u^{n} , we obtain, for n\geq 1 , 
 

 
 
 \frac{J_{(n)}}{(q;q)_{n-1}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^{k-1}(1-q^{n-k}t^{k})e_{k}\frac{J_{(n-k)}}{(q;q)_{n-k}}. 
 
 
 
 This is the same recurrence as ( 3.12 ). Since
 J_{(0)}=Y_{(0)}=1 , we obtain J_{(n)}=Y_{(n)} for all
 n\geq 0 by induction.
∎ 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 4. Hook case 

 
 
 In this section, we give an e -expansion of J_{\mu}(x;q,t) 
when \mu is a hook using the one-row case
in Section 3 . As a consequence, we prove
 Conjecture 1.1 in this case. 
 
 
 
 4.1. Main formula 

 
 
 We first extend the definition
of the weight \operatorname{wt}_{(n)}(\alpha) given in the previous section. Recall
that \chi(P)=1 if P holds and \chi(P)=0 otherwise. 
 
 
 Definition 4.1 . 
 
 
 For a hook \mu=(r,1^{s}) with r\geq 1 , s\geq 0 , and
 r+s=n , and a composition \alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}) 
of n , define 
 

 
 (4.1) 
 
 \operatorname{wt}_{(r,1^{s})}(\alpha)=\chi(\alpha_{k}>s)(t;t)_{s}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\operatorname{wt}(i,\alpha). 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 The e -coefficients of J_{(r,1^{s})}(x;q,t) have a combinatorial description
in terms of these weights, as follows. 
 
 
 Theorem 4.2 . 
 
 
 Let n,r\geq 1 and s\geq 0 be integers with r+s=n . Then we have 
 

 
 (4.2) 
 
 J_{(r,1^{s})}(x;q,t)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\left(\sum_{\alpha\sim\lambda}\operatorname{wt}_{(r,1^{s})}(\alpha)\right)e_{\lambda}, 
 
 
 
 where the second sum is over all compositions \alpha 
with \alpha\sim\lambda . 
 
 
 
 
 This theorem will be proved in the next subsection. In what follows,
we provide an equivalent description of ( 4.2 ) using ribbon tilings. 
 
 
 
 A ribbon is a connected skew shape containing no
 2\times 2 block. The content of a cell (i,j) 
is j-i , and the head of a ribbon is its cell of
smallest content. 
 
 
 
 A ribbon tiling of a partition \nu is a tiling of
its diagram by ribbons. Its type is the partition formed
by the ribbon sizes. Write \operatorname{RT}(\nu) for the set of ribbon
tilings of \nu , and \operatorname{RT}(\nu,\lambda) for those of
type \lambda . A ribbon tiling is special if each
ribbon contains a cell in the first column of \nu .
Denote the corresponding sets by \operatorname{SRT}(\nu) and
 \operatorname{SRT}(\nu,\lambda) . 
 
 
 
 For T\in\operatorname{RT}(\nu) , define 
 

 
 
 \operatorname{wt}(T)=\prod_{c\in\nu}\operatorname{wt}(c,T,\nu),\qquad\operatorname{wt}(c,T,\nu)=\begin{cases}1-q^{\operatorname{leg}(c)}t^{|\theta|},&\text{if $c$ is the head of a ribbon $\theta\in T$},\\
q^{\operatorname{leg}(c)}-t^{\operatorname{arm}^{\prime}(c)},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases} 
 
 
 
 Here the leg and coarm lengths are measured in \nu . 
 
 
 Theorem 4.3 . 
 
 
 Let \mu=(r,1^{s}) , where r\geq 1 , s\geq 0 , and
 r+s=n . Then 
 

 
 (4.3) 
 
 J_{\mu}(x;q,t)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\left(\sum_{T\in\operatorname{SRT}(\mu^{\prime},\lambda)}\operatorname{wt}(T)\right)e_{\lambda}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 This follows directly from Theorem 4.2 by interpreting each
composition with nonzero weight as a special ribbon tiling of \mu^{\prime} 
with the same weight. 
 
 
 
 
 4.2. Proof of Theorem 4.2 

 
 
 Let 
 

 
 (4.4) 
 
 Y_{(r,1^{s})}=Y_{(r,1^{s})}(x;q,t)=\sum_{\alpha\vDash n}\operatorname{wt}_{(r,1^{s})}(\alpha)e_{\alpha}. 
 
 
 
 Then Theorem 4.2 is equivalent to 
 

 
 (4.5) 
 
 J_{(r,1^{s})}(x;q,t)=Y_{(r,1^{s})}(x;q,t). 
 
 
 
 It is known [ 15 , p. 363] that for r,s\geq 1 , 
 

 
 (4.6) 
 
 (1-q^{r}t^{s})J_{(r)}J_{(1^{s})}=(1-t^{s})J_{\left(r+1,1^{s-1}\right)}+(1-q^{r})J_{\left(r,1^{s}\right)}. 
 
 
 
 We will show that Y_{(r,1^{s})}(x;q,t) satisfies the same
recurrence relation and initial conditions. Note that if
 (r,1^{s})=(1,1^{n-1})=(1^{n}) , then by definition we have
 \operatorname{wt}_{(1,1^{n-1})}(\alpha)=0 unless \alpha=(n) . Thus, 
 

 
 (4.7) 
 
 Y_{(1^{n})}=(t;t)_{n}e_{n}. 
 
 
 
 
 Lemma 4.4 . 
 
 
 For integers r,s\geq 1 , we have 
 

 
 (4.8) 
 
 (1-q^{r}t^{s})Y_{(r)}Y_{(1^{s})}=(1-t^{s})Y_{\left(r+1,1^{s-1}\right)}+(1-q^{r})Y_{\left(r,1^{s}\right)}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 Let n=r+s . By ( 4.4 ), the right-hand side of
( 4.8 ) is equal to 
 

 
 (4.9) 
 
 \sum_{\alpha\vDash n}\left((1-t^{s})\operatorname{wt}_{(r+1,1^{s-1})}(\alpha)+(1-q^{r})\operatorname{wt}_{(r,1^{s})}(\alpha)\right)e_{\alpha}. 
 
 
 
 Fix \alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})\vDash n . If
 \alpha_{k}\leq s-1 , then both weights in ( 4.9 ) vanish. If
 \alpha_{k}\geq s+1 , then r>\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{k-1} , so
 \operatorname{wt}(r+1,\alpha)=q^{r}-1 . Hence, the summand in ( 4.9 ) also
vanishes: 
 

 
 
 (1-t^{s})\operatorname{wt}_{(r+1,1^{s-1})}(\alpha)+(1-q^{r})\operatorname{wt}_{(r,1^{s})}(\alpha)=(t;t)_{s}\bigl((q^{r}-1)+(1-q^{r})\bigr)\prod_{i=1}^{r}\operatorname{wt}(i,\alpha)=0. 
 
 
 
 Therefore, we can restrict the sum in ( 4.9 ) to the
compositions with \alpha_{k}=s . 
 
 
 
 Suppose \alpha=(\beta,s) with \beta\vDash r . Then
 \operatorname{wt}_{(r,1^{s})}(\alpha)=0 and 
 

 
 
 (1-t^{s})\operatorname{wt}_{(r+1,1^{s-1})}(\alpha)=(1-q^{r}t^{s})(t;t)_{s}\operatorname{wt}_{(r)}(\beta). 
 
 
 
 This, together with e_{\alpha}=e_{\beta}e_{s} and ( 4.7 ),
implies that ( 4.9 ) is equal to 
 

 
 
 (1-q^{r}t^{s})(t;t)_{s}e_{s}\sum_{\beta\vDash r}\operatorname{wt}_{(r)}(\beta)e_{\beta}=(1-q^{r}t^{s})Y_{(1^{s})}Y_{(r)}. 
 
 
 
 This is the left-hand side of ( 4.8 ), completing the
proof.
∎ 
 
 
 
 
 Now we are ready to prove Theorem 4.2 . 
 
 
 Proof of Theorem 4.2 . 
 
 
 By Theorem 3.3 , we have 
 

 
 
 J_{(k)}(x;q,t)=Y_{(k)}(x;q,t)\qquad\text{for all }k\geq 1. 
 
 
 
 By [ 15 , p. 339] and ( 4.7 ), we have 
 

 
 
 J_{(1^{k})}(x;q,t)=(t;t)_{k}e_{k}=Y_{(1^{k})}(x;q,t)\qquad\text{for all }k\geq 1. 
 
 
 
 Thus, using ( 4.6 ), ( 4.8 ), and the above
results, we obtain ( 4.5 ) by induction on s .
∎ 
 
 
 
 
 
 4.3. Proof of Conjecture 1.1 for hooks 

 
 
 In this subsection we prove the e -positivity in Conjecture 1.1 
when \mu is a hook. 
 
 
 
 Let \mu=(r,1^{s}) with n=r+s , and fix an integer
 k\geq 1 . For a composition
 \alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell})\vDash n and
 1\leq i\leq r , define 
 

 
 
 \operatorname{wt}_{k}(i,\alpha)=\begin{cases}[k\alpha_{j}]_{q}-[i-1]_{q}&\text{if }i=1+\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{j-1}\text{ for some }1\leq j\leq\ell,\\
[i-1]_{q}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases} 
 
 
 
 Then Theorem 4.2 with t=q^{-k} gives 
 

 
 (4.10) 
 
 q^{k\left(n+\binom{s+1}{2}\right)}\frac{J_{(r,1^{s})}(x;q,q^{-k})}{(q-1)^{n}}=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{s}[kj]_{q}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\vDash n\\
\alpha_{\ell(\alpha)}>s\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\operatorname{wt}_{k}(i,\alpha)\right)e_{\alpha}. 
 
 
 
 If k\geq r-1 , then 
 

 
 
 [k\alpha_{j}]_{q}-[i-1]_{q}=q^{i-1}[k\alpha_{j}-i+1]_{q}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[q]. 
 
 
 
 Thus, Conjecture 1.1 holds in this case. For an arbitrary
 k\geq 1 , we first establish the following one-row formula,
which is different from Theorem 3.3 for t=q^{-k} .
Throughout this section, the notation
 \sum_{c_{0}+\cdots+c_{k-1}=n} means the sum over all nonnegative
integers c_{0},\dots,c_{k-1} satisfying
 c_{0}+\cdots+c_{k-1}=n . 
 
 
 Proposition 4.5 . 
 
 
 Let n,k\geq 1 be integers. Then 
 

 
 (4.11) 
 
 q^{kn}\frac{J_{(n)}(x;q,q^{-k})}{(q-1)^{n}}=[n]_{q}!\sum_{c_{0}+\cdots+c_{k-1}=n}q^{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}ic_{i}}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}e_{c_{i}}. 
 
 
 
 In particular, the left-hand side has e -coefficients in
 \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[q] . 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 Fix k\geq 1 , and let 
 

 
 (4.12) 
 
 H_{n}=\frac{q^{kn}J_{(n)}(x;q,q^{-k})}{(q-1)^{n}[n]_{q}!},\qquad F_{n}=\sum_{c_{0}+\cdots+c_{k-1}=n}q^{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}ic_{i}}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}e_{c_{i}}. 
 
 
 
 Then we must show that H_{n}=F_{n} . 
 
 
 
 We first derive a recurrence for H_{n} . By
( 4.10 ) with r=n and s=0 ,
we have 
 

 
 (4.13) 
 
 [n]_{q}!H_{n}=\sum_{\alpha\vDash n}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{wt}_{k}(i,\alpha)\right)e_{\alpha}. 
 
 
 
 If \alpha=(\beta,a)\vDash n , then the last block of \alpha 
contributes the weight 
 

 
 
 e_{a}\prod_{i=n-a+1}^{n}\operatorname{wt}_{k}(i,\alpha)=e_{a}([ka]_{q}-[n-a]_{q})\frac{[n-1]_{q}!}{[n-a]_{q}!}. 
 
 
 
 Hence, dividing ( 4.13 ) by [n-1]_{q}! gives 
 

 
 (4.14) 
 
 [n]_{q}H_{n}=\sum_{a=1}^{n}([ka]_{q}-[n-a]_{q})e_{a}H_{n-a}\qquad(n\geq 1). 
 
 
 
 
 
 To derive the recurrence for F_{n} , we claim that 
 

 
 (4.15) 
 
 \sum_{a=0}^{n}q^{ka}e_{a}F_{n-a}=\sum_{d_{0}+\cdots+d_{k}=n}q^{\sum_{i=0}^{k}id_{i}}\prod_{i=0}^{k}e_{d_{i}}=\sum_{a=0}^{n}q^{n-a}e_{a}F_{n-a}. 
 
 
 
 The first equality is obtained by setting d_{k}=a and
 d_{i}=c_{i} for 0\leq i\leq k-1 . The second is obtained by
setting d_{0}=a and d_{i}=c_{i-1} for 1\leq i\leq k ,
since 
 

 
 
 \sum_{i=1}^{k}id_{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(i+1)c_{i}=n-a+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}ic_{i}. 
 
 
 
 Solving for F_{n} in ( 4.15 ) yields 
 

 
 
 (q^{n}-1)F_{n}=\sum_{a=1}^{n}(q^{ka}-q^{n-a})e_{a}F_{n-a}. 
 
 
 
 Dividing by q-1 shows that F_{n} satisfies the same
recurrence ( 4.14 ). Since F_{0}=H_{0}=1 ,
induction gives F_{n}=H_{n} for all n\geq 0 .
∎ 
 
 
 
 
 We now use the one-row formula to obtain a positive formula for
every hook. 
 
 
 Corollary 4.6 . 
 
 
 Let r,k\geq 1 and s\geq 0 be integers with n=r+s .
Then 
 

 
 (4.16) 
 
 \qquad\qquad q^{k\left(n+\binom{s+1}{2}\right)}\frac{J_{(r,1^{s})}(x;q,q^{-k})}{(q-1)^{n}}\\
=[r-1]_{q}!\prod_{j=1}^{s}[kj]_{q}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{0}+\cdots+d_{k}=n\\
d_{k}\leq s<d_{0}\end{subarray}}q^{\sum_{i=0}^{k}id_{i}}[k(d_{0}-d_{k})]_{q}\prod_{i=0}^{k}e_{d_{i}}.\qquad\qquad\quad 
 
 
 
 In particular, the expression in ( 1.5 ) has
 e -coefficients in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[q] for every hook
partition. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 Fix k\geq 1 . Let 
 

 
 
 B_{r,s}=q^{k\left(n+\binom{s+1}{2}\right)}\frac{J_{(r,1^{s})}(x;q,q^{-k})}{(q-1)^{n}},\qquad C_{s}=\prod_{j=1}^{s}[kj]_{q}. 
 
 
 
 By the same argument as in the proof of Proposition 4.5 ,
separating the last part a>s of the compositions
 \alpha=(\beta,a)\vDash n in ( 4.10 )
gives 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle B_{r,s} 
 \displaystyle=C_{s}\sum_{a=s+1}^{n}e_{a}([ka]_{q}-[n-a]_{q})\frac{[r-1]_{q}!}{[n-a]_{q}!}\sum_{\beta\vDash n-a}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n-a}\operatorname{wt}_{k}(i,\beta)\right)e_{\beta} 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=C_{s}[r-1]_{q}!\sum_{a=s+1}^{n}([ka]_{q}-[n-a]_{q})e_{a}\frac{B_{n-a,0}}{[n-a]_{q}!}. 
 
 
 
 Applying Proposition 4.5 to B_{n-a,0} and
using [ka]_{q}-[n-a]_{q}=(q^{ka}-q^{n-a})/(q-1) , we obtain 
 

 
 (4.17) 
 
 \frac{B_{r,s}}{C_{s}[r-1]_{q}!}=\sum_{a=s+1}^{n}\frac{q^{ka}-q^{n-a}}{q-1}e_{a}\sum_{c_{0}+\cdots+c_{k-1}=n-a}q^{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}ic_{i}}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}e_{c_{i}}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Consider a tuple (c_{0},\dots,c_{k-1}) of nonnegative integers
with c_{0}+\cdots+c_{k-1}=n-a .
If we let (d_{0},\dots,d_{k})=(c_{0},\dots,c_{k-1},a) ,
then d_{0}+\cdots+d_{k}=n , d_{k}=a>s , and 
 

 
 
 \sum_{i=0}^{k}id_{i}=ka+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}ic_{i}. 
 
 
 
 If we let (d_{0},\dots,d_{k})=(a,c_{0},\dots,c_{k-1}) ,
then d_{0}+\cdots+d_{k}=n , d_{0}=a>s , and 
 

 
 
 \sum_{i=0}^{k}id_{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(i+1)c_{i}=(n-a)+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}ic_{i}. 
 
 
 
 Thus, we can rewrite ( 4.17 ) as 
 

 
 (4.18) 
 
 \frac{B_{r,s}}{C_{s}[r-1]_{q}!}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{0}+\cdots+d_{k}=n\\
d_{k}>s\end{subarray}}\frac{q^{\sum_{i=0}^{k}id_{i}}}{q-1}\prod_{i=0}^{k}e_{d_{i}}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{0}+\cdots+d_{k}=n\\
d_{0}>s\end{subarray}}\frac{q^{\sum_{i=0}^{k}id_{i}}}{q-1}\prod_{i=0}^{k}e_{d_{i}}. 
 
 
 
 Interchanging d_{0} and d_{k} gives 
 

 
 
 \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{0}+\cdots+d_{k}=n\\
d_{k}>s\end{subarray}}q^{\sum_{i=0}^{k}id_{i}}\prod_{i=0}^{k}e_{d_{i}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{0}+\cdots+d_{k}=n\\
d_{0}>s\end{subarray}}q^{kd_{0}+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}id_{i}}\prod_{i=0}^{k}e_{d_{i}}. 
 
 
 
 Thus ( 4.18 ) is equivalent to 
 

 
 
 \frac{B_{r,s}}{C_{s}[r-1]_{q}!}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{0}+\cdots+d_{k}=n\\
d_{0}>s\end{subarray}}\frac{q^{kd_{0}}-q^{kd_{k}}}{q-1}q^{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}id_{i}}\prod_{i=0}^{k}e_{d_{i}}. 
 
 
 
 Since the summand is antisymmetric in d_{0} and d_{k} , the
sum over nonnegative integers d_{i} satisfying both d_{0}>s 
and d_{k}>s vanishes. Thus, we obtain 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle\frac{B_{r,s}}{C_{s}[r-1]_{q}!} 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{0}+\cdots+d_{k}=n\\
d_{k}\leq s<d_{0}\end{subarray}}\frac{q^{kd_{0}}-q^{kd_{k}}}{q-1}q^{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}id_{i}}\prod_{i=0}^{k}e_{d_{i}} 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{0}+\cdots+d_{k}=n\\
d_{k}\leq s<d_{0}\end{subarray}}q^{kd_{k}}[k(d_{0}-d_{k})]_{q}q^{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}id_{i}}\prod_{i=0}^{k}e_{d_{i}}, 
 
 
 
 which is the desired formula.
∎ 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 5. Two-column case 

 
 
 In this section, we provide a formula for the e -coefficients of
 J_{\mu}(x;q,t) and prove Conjecture 1.1 when \mu 
consists of two columns. In this case, each nonzero
 e -coefficient of J_{\mu}(x;q,t) admits a factorization
into |\mu| factors. 
 
 
 Theorem 5.1 . 
 
 
 Let 0\leq\ell\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor and
 \mu=(2^{\ell},1^{n-2\ell}) . Then we have 
 

 
 (5.1) 
 
 J_{\mu}(x;q,t)=\sum_{p=0}^{\ell}\left(q^{\ell-p}(1-t^{n-2p})(t;t)_{n-\ell-p-1}(q^{-1};t)_{\ell-p}\frac{(t;t)_{\ell}}{(t;t)_{\ell-p}}\frac{(qt;t)_{n-\ell}}{(qt;t)_{n-\ell-p}}\right)e_{(n-p,p)}, 
 
 
 
 where we use the convention that (1-t^{n-2p})(t;t)_{n-\ell-p-1}=(t;t)_{n-2p} when p=\ell . 
 
 
 
 Equivalently, we have 
 

 
 
 J_{\mu}(x;q,t)=\sum_{p=0}^{\ell}\left(\prod_{c\in\mu}\operatorname{wt}_{\mu,p}(c)\right)e_{(n-p,p)}, 
 
 
 
 where the weight \operatorname{wt}_{\mu,p}(c) 
of a cell c=(i,j)\in\mu is defined by 
 

 
 
 \operatorname{wt}_{\mu,p}(c)=\begin{cases}1-q^{\operatorname{arm}(c)}t^{\operatorname{leg}(c)+1},&\mbox{if $i\leq p$},\\
1-t^{n-2p},&\mbox{if $c=(p+1,1)$},\\
q^{j-1}-t^{i-p-1},&\mbox{if $i>p$ and $c\neq(p+1,1)$},\end{cases} 
 
 
 
 with arm and leg lengths measured in \mu .
See Figure 3 .

 To prove Theorem 5.1 , we use the following lemma. 
 
 
 Lemma 5.2 . 
 
 
 For a\geq b with n=a+b , we have 
 

 
 (5.2) 
 
 e_{a}\cdot e_{b}=\sum_{j=0}^{b}\frac{(q;t)_{a-j}(q;t)_{b-j}(1-qt^{n-2j})}{(t;t)_{a-j}(t;t)_{b-j}(t;t)_{j}(q;t)_{n-j+1}}J_{(2^{j},1^{n-2j})}(x;q,t). 
 
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 Note that P_{(1^{n})}(x;q,t)=e_{n}(x) . Then the lemma follows
directly from the Pieri rule for Macdonald polynomials in
 [ 15 , Ch. VI, (6.24) (iv)] by taking \mu to be a
one-column partition.
∎ 
 
 
 
 Proof of Theorem 5.1 . 
 
 
 We use Lemma 5.2 to verify the e -coefficients of
 J_{(2^{\ell},1^{n-2\ell})}(x;q,t) . Note that the identities
( 5.2 ) with (a,b)=(n-\ell,\ell) for
 0\leq\ell\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor determine the polynomials
 J_{(2^{\ell},1^{n-2\ell})}(x;q,t) uniquely. Indeed, the
coefficient of J_{(2^{\ell},1^{n-2\ell})} in ( 5.2 )
when j=\ell is 
 

 
 
 \frac{(q;t)_{n-2\ell}\,(1-qt^{n-2\ell})}{(t;t)_{n-2\ell}\,(t;t)_{\ell}\,(q;t)_{n-\ell+1}}\neq 0, 
 
 
 
 so ( 5.2 ) with (a,b)=(n-\ell,\ell) expresses
 J_{(2^{\ell},1^{n-2\ell})} in terms of e_{n-\ell}\,e_{\ell} 
and the J_{(2^{j},1^{n-2j})} with j<\ell , and induction on
 \ell proves the claim. Therefore, it suffices to show that the
right-hand sides of ( 5.1 ) satisfy ( 5.2 ) with
 (a,b)=(n-\ell,\ell) for every
 0\leq\ell\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor , that is, 
 

 
 (5.3) 
 
 e_{(n-\ell,\ell)}(x)=\sum_{p=0}^{\ell}S_{n,\ell,p}e_{(n-p,p)}(x), 
 
 
 
 where 
 

 
 
 S_{n,\ell,p}=\sum_{j=p}^{\ell}q^{j-p}\frac{(1-t^{n-2p})(1-qt^{n-2j})}{(1-q)}\frac{(q^{-1};t)_{j-p}(t;t)_{n-j-p-1}(q;t)_{n-\ell-j}(q;t)_{\ell-j}}{(t;t)_{j-p}(qt;t)_{n-j-p}(t;t)_{n-\ell-j}(t;t)_{\ell-j}}. 
 
 
 
 Since \{e_{\lambda}\} forms a basis, ( 5.3 ) is equivalent to
 S_{n,\ell,p}=\chi(p=\ell) . The case p=\ell is
immediate, so it remains to show that S_{n,\ell,p}=0 for
 0\leq p<\ell\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor . 
 
 
 
 Reversing the summation by replacing j with \ell-j 
and using the identities 
 

 
 
 (a;t)_{r+j}=(a;t)_{r}(at^{r};t)_{j},\qquad(a;t)_{r-j}=\frac{(a;t)_{r}}{(-a)^{j}t^{(r-1)j-\binom{j}{2}}(a^{-1}t^{1-r};t)_{j}}, 
 
 
 
 where the second identity holds for 0\leq j\leq r , we obtain 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle S_{n,\ell,p} 
 \displaystyle=\frac{q^{\ell-p}(1-t^{n-2p})(q^{-1};t)_{\ell-p}(t;t)_{n-\ell-p-1}(q;t)_{n-2\ell}}{(1-q)(t;t)_{\ell-p}(qt;t)_{n-\ell-p}(t;t)_{n-2\ell}} 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-p}t^{j}\frac{(q,t^{p-\ell},t^{n-\ell-p},qt^{n-2\ell};t)_{j}(1-qt^{n-2\ell+2j})}{(t,qt^{p-\ell+1},qt^{n-\ell-p+1},t^{n-2\ell+1};t)_{j}}. 
 
 
 
 We apply the terminating very-well-poised {}_{6}\phi_{5} 
summation formula [ 4 , (II.21)] with base t and
parameters 
 

 
 
 a=qt^{n-2\ell},\qquad b=t^{n-\ell-p},\qquad c=q, 
 
 
 
 and terminating parameter t^{p-\ell} .
Since at^{\ell-p+1}/(bc)=t , the sum above equals 
 

 
 
 (1-a)\frac{(at,at/(bc);t)_{\ell-p}}{(at/b,at/c;t)_{\ell-p}}=(1-qt^{n-2\ell})\frac{(qt^{n-2\ell+1},t^{p-\ell+1};t)_{\ell-p}}{(qt^{p-\ell+1},t^{n-2\ell+1};t)_{\ell-p}}=0. 
 
 
 
 Indeed, since \ell-p\geq 1 , the factor
 (t^{p-\ell+1};t)_{\ell-p} contains 1-t^{0}=0 .
Thus S_{n,\ell,p}=0 , completing the proof.
∎ 
 
 
 
 
 Using Theorem 5.1 , we can prove Conjecture 1.1 when
 \mu has at most two columns. 
 
 
 Corollary 5.3 . 
 
 
 For every partition \mu with at most two columns and every
positive integer k , the expression in
( 1.5 ) has e -coefficients in
 \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[q] . 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 For \mu=(2^{\ell},1^{n-2\ell}) , consider the coefficients 
 

 
 
 A_{j}=[e_{(n-j,j)}]\left(q^{k\sum_{i}i\mu_{i}}\frac{J_{\mu}(x;q,q^{-k})}{(q-1)^{n}}\right),\qquad 0\leq j\leq\ell. 
 
 
 
 Substituting t=q^{-k} into Theorem 5.1 and using
 1-q^{-a}=q^{-a}(q-1)[a]_{q} , we obtain, for
 0\leq j<\ell , 
 

 
 
 A_{j}=q^{j}[k(n-2j)]_{q}\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-j-1}[ki+1]_{q}\prod_{i=1}^{n-\ell-j-1}[ki]_{q}\prod_{i=\ell-j+1}^{\ell}[ki]_{q}\prod_{i=n-\ell-j+1}^{n-\ell}[ki-1]_{q}. 
 
 
 
 For j=\ell , the convention in Theorem 5.1 gives 
 

 
 
 A_{\ell}=q^{\ell}\prod_{i=1}^{n-2\ell}[ki]_{q}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}[ki]_{q}\prod_{i=n-2\ell+1}^{n-\ell}[ki-1]_{q}. 
 
 
 
 Thus A_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[q] for every j , proving the
corollary.
∎ 
 
 
 
 
 
 6. The e_{n} -coefficient 

 
 
 Combining the relation between P_{\mu}(x;q,t) and J_{\mu}(x;q,t) with the triangularity
of P_{\mu}(x;q,t) in the monomial symmetric function basis, we obtain 
 

 
 
 J_{\mu}(x;q,t)=\sum_{\lambda\geq\mu^{\prime}}c_{\mu,\lambda}e_{\lambda}, 
 
 
 
 where 
 

 
 (6.1) 
 
 c_{\mu,\mu^{\prime}}=\prod_{c\in\mu}(1-q^{\operatorname{arm}(c)}t^{\operatorname{leg}(c)+1})=\prod_{c\in\mu^{\prime}}(1-q^{\operatorname{leg}(c)}t^{\operatorname{arm}(c)+1}). 
 
 
 
 In particular, 
 

 
 
 J_{(1^{n})}(x;q,t)=(t;t)_{n}e_{n}. 
 
 
 
 More generally, we compute the e_{n} -coefficient of
 J_{\mu}(x;q,t) for any \mu\vdash n . 
 
 
 Theorem 6.1 . 
 
 
 For n\geq 1 and \mu\vdash n , we have 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle[e_{n}]J_{\mu}(x;q,t) 
 \displaystyle=(1-t^{n})(q-1)\cdots(q^{\mu_{1}-1}-1)\prod_{c\notin\operatorname{Row}_{1}(\mu)}(q^{\operatorname{arm}^{\prime}(c)}-t^{\operatorname{leg}^{\prime}(c)}) 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=(1-t^{n})(q-1)\cdots(q^{\mu_{1}-1}-1)\prod_{c\notin\operatorname{Col}_{1}(\mu^{\prime})}(q^{\operatorname{leg}^{\prime}(c)}-t^{\operatorname{arm}^{\prime}(c)}), 
 
 
 
 where \operatorname{Row}_{1}(\mu) (resp. \operatorname{Col}_{1}(\mu) ) is the set of cells
in the first row (resp. column) of \mu .
Equivalently, 
 

 
 (6.2) 
 
 [e_{n}]J_{\mu}(x;q,t)=(1-t^{n})\prod_{c\in\mu^{\prime}\setminus\{(1,1)\}}(q^{\operatorname{leg}^{\prime}(c)}-t^{\operatorname{arm}^{\prime}(c)}). 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 To prove Theorem 6.1 , we need the following lemma. 
 
 
 Lemma 6.2 . 
 
 
 Let n\geq 1 . If 
 

 
 
 f=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}a_{\lambda}e_{\lambda}, 
 
 
 
 then 
 

 
 (6.3) 
 
 [e_{n}]f=(-1)^{n-1}(1-t^{n})\lim_{u\to 1}f\left[\frac{1-u}{1-t}\right]/(1-u). 
 
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 By [ 15 , Ch. I, (2.14 ′ )] , we have 
 

 
 (6.4) 
 
 e_{m}\left[\frac{1-u}{1-t}\right]=\sum_{\lambda\vdash m}(-1)^{m-\ell(\lambda)}z_{\lambda}^{-1}p_{\lambda}\left[\frac{1-u}{1-t}\right], 
 
 
 
 where z_{\lambda} is given in ( 2.1 ).
Since 
 

 
 
 p_{k}\left[\frac{1-u}{1-t}\right]=\frac{1-u^{k}}{1-t^{k}}=\frac{k(1-u)}{1-t^{k}}+O((1-u)^{2}), 
 
 
 
 we see that only the term \lambda=(m) of the right-hand side of
( 6.4 ) contributes to first order in 1-u . Therefore, 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle f\left[\frac{1-u}{1-t}\right] 
 \displaystyle=a_{(n)}e_{n}\left[\frac{1-u}{1-t}\right]+\sum_{\lambda<(n)}a_{\lambda}e_{\lambda}\left[\frac{1-u}{1-t}\right] 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=a_{(n)}(-1)^{n-1}\frac{1-u}{1-t^{n}}+O((1-u)^{2}). 
 
 
 
 Then the result follows by taking the limit as u approaches 1 and solving
for the coefficient a_{(n)} .
∎ 
 
 
 
 Proof of Theorem 6.1 . 
 
 
 By [ 15 , Ch. VI, (8.8)] , we have 
 

 
 
 J_{\mu}\left[\frac{1-u}{1-t};q,t\right]=\prod_{c\in\mu}\left(t^{\operatorname{leg}^{\prime}(c)}-q^{\operatorname{arm}^{\prime}(c)}u\right). 
 
 
 
 Since the cell (1,1) corresponds to the factor (1-u) , we have 
 

 
 
 \lim_{u\to 1}J_{\mu}\left[\frac{1-u}{1-t}\right]/(1-u)=\prod_{c\in\mu\setminus\{(1,1)\}}\left(t^{\operatorname{leg}^{\prime}(c)}-q^{\operatorname{arm}^{\prime}(c)}\right). 
 
 
 
 By applying Lemma 6.2 , we obtain 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle[e_{n}]J_{\mu}(x;q,t) 
 \displaystyle=(-1)^{n-1}(1-t^{n})\prod_{c\in\mu\setminus\{(1,1)\}}\left(t^{\operatorname{leg}^{\prime}(c)}-q^{\operatorname{arm}^{\prime}(c)}\right) 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=(1-t^{n})\prod_{c\in\mu\setminus\{(1,1)\}}\left(q^{\operatorname{arm}^{\prime}(c)}-t^{\operatorname{leg}^{\prime}(c)}\right). 
 
 
 
 Since all cells in the first row have coleg length 0 , we obtain
the first identity. The remaining expressions follow directly from
analyzing the diagram of \mu .
∎ 
 
 
 
 
 
 7. Final remarks 

 
 
 In this section, we explain the analogy between the normalization of
 J_{\mu}(x;q,t) in Conjecture 1.1 and the
 \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions introduced by Haglund, Oh,
and Yoo [ 9 ] . We then propose a conjecture on an expansion
of the normalized J_{\mu}(x;q,t) in terms of chromatic symmetric
functions when q=1 . 
 
 
 
 For a graph G with vertex set V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\} , the
 chromatic symmetric function \chi_{G}(x) is defined by 
 

 
 
 \chi_{G}(x)=\sum_{c:V\to\mathbb{Z}^{+}}x_{c(v_{1})}\cdots x_{c(v_{n})}, 
 
 
 
 where the sum is over all proper colorings c of G . If
 G is the incomparability graph of a natural unit interval order,
then it can be identified with a Dyck path \gamma of length
 2n . From now on, we consider only such graphs and write
 \chi_{\gamma}(x) in place of \chi_{G}(x) . See [ 18 , 9 ] 
for more details. 
 
 
 
 By ( 1.4 ), the normalization of J_{\mu}(x;q,t) in
 Conjecture 1.1 satisfies 
 

 
 
 q^{k\sum_{i\geq 1}i\mu_{i}}\frac{J_{\mu}(x;q,q^{-k})}{(q-1)^{n}}=\frac{\tilde{H}_{\mu}[(q^{k}-1)X;q,q^{k}]}{(q-1)^{n}}. 
 
 
 
 Applying the same plethystic substitution to the unicellular LLT polynomial
 \operatorname{LLT}_{\gamma}(x;q) and dividing by (q-1)^{n} 
gives the \alpha -chromatic symmetric function at \alpha=k : 
 

 
 
 \chi_{\gamma}^{(k)}(x;q)=\frac{\operatorname{LLT}_{\gamma}[(q^{k}-1)X;q]}{(q-1)^{n}}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 When q=1 , the \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions are
polynomials in \alpha whose coefficients are symmetric
functions. Hence, they can be expanded in terms of the basis
 \{\binom{\alpha}{r}\}_{r\geq 0} of the polynomials in
 \alpha . Haglund, Oh, and Yoo [ 9 , Theorem 4.4] showed
that each coefficient of \binom{\alpha}{r} has a positive expansion
in terms of chromatic symmetric functions: 
 

 
 (7.1) 
 
 \chi_{\gamma}^{(\alpha)}(x;1)=\sum_{r=1}^{n}\binom{\alpha}{r}r!\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}(n,r)}\chi_{\beta(\gamma,S)}(x). 
 
 
 
 Here \mathcal{S}(n,r) denotes the set of all set partitions of
 \{1,2,\dots,n\} into r blocks, and \beta(\gamma,S) 
is a certain Dyck path determined by \gamma and S . By
Hikita’s theorem [ 12 ] , this implies that
 \chi_{\gamma}^{(\alpha)}(x;1) is e -positive for every
positive integer \alpha . 
 
 
 
 Now we define 
 

 
 
 \hat{J}^{(\alpha)}_{\mu}(x)=\lim_{q\rightarrow 1}\frac{J_{\mu}(x;q,q^{-\alpha})}{(q-1)^{|\mu|}}. 
 
 
 
 Using the Haglund–Haiman–Loehr formula
 [ 8 , Proposition 8.1] , one can see that this is also a
polynomial in \alpha . Inspired by ( 7.1 ), we propose the
following question. 
 
 
 Question 7.1 . 
 
 
 Let n,r be positive integers, \mu\vdash n and 0\leq r\leq n .
Denote by \mathcal{OP}(n,r) the set of ordered set partitions of
 \{1,2,\dots,n\} into r blocks. For each
 S\in\mathcal{OP}(n,r) , can we find a Dyck path
 \pi(\mu,S) such that 
 

 
 (7.2) 
 
 \hat{J}^{(\alpha)}_{\mu}(x)=\sum_{r=1}^{n}\binom{\alpha}{r}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{OP}(n,r)}\chi_{\pi(\mu,S)}(x)\ ? 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 Again by the result of Hikita [ 12 ] , an affirmative answer
to this question would imply the e -positivity of \hat{J}^{(\alpha)}_{\mu}(x) for
every positive integer \alpha . 
 
 
 
 We note that the chromatic symmetric functions \{\chi_{\gamma}(x)\}_{\gamma\in\mathcal{D}} ,
where \mathcal{D} is the set of Dyck paths, do not form a basis of the space of
symmetric functions. Thus, an expansion of the form ( 7.2 ) need not be
unique. Note also that if \pi(\mu,S) depends only on \mu 
and the underlying set partition of S , then one can rewrite
( 7.2 ) as 
 

 
 
 \hat{J}^{(\alpha)}_{\mu}(x)=\sum_{r=1}^{n}\binom{\alpha}{r}r!\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}(n,r)}\chi_{\pi(\mu,S)}(x), 
 
 
 
 which has the same form as ( 7.1 ). However, such a rewriting
is not possible in general. For example, if \mu=(2,2,1) , then
the coefficient of \binom{\alpha}{4} has no expansion in terms of
chromatic symmetric functions in which every coefficient is a
nonnegative integer multiple of 4! .
