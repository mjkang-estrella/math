 Abstract. 
 
 
 In this paper, we introduce the \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions \chi^{(\alpha)}_{\pi}[X;q] , extending Shareshian and Wachs’ chromatic symmetric functions with an additional real parameter \alpha . We present positive combinatorial formulas with explicit interpretations. Notably, we show an explicit monomial expansion in terms of the \alpha -binomial basis and an expansion into certain chromatic symmetric functions in terms of the \alpha -falling factorial basis. Among various connections with other subjects, we highlight a significant link to q -rook theory, including a new solution to the q -hit problem posed by Garsia and Remmel in their 1986 paper introducing q -rook polynomials. 
 
 
 
 Key words and phrases: \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions, Schur positivity, \alpha -binomial coefficients, rook polynomials, hit polynomials
 
 2010 Mathematics Subject Classification Primary:05E05 ; Secondary: 05A19, 05A30
 
 
 
 1. Introduction 

 
 
 1.1. Overview 

 
 
 In his seminal paper [ Sta95 ] , Stanley introduced the chromatic symmetric function \chi_{G}[X] as a symmetric function generalization of the chromatic polynomial for graphs. Subsequently, Shareshian and Wachs [ SW16 ] refined this, giving rise to the chromatic (quasi)symmetric function \chi_{G}[X;q] with an additional parameter q . Notably, Shareshian and Wachs showed that \chi_{G(\pi)}[X;q] is symmetric when G(\pi) is the graph associated with a Dyck path \pi . For brevity, we adopt the notation \chi_{\pi}[X;q]=\chi_{G(\pi)}[X;q] . These symmetric functions have gathered substantial interest across diverse mathematical domains. For instance, various combinatorial formulas have been investigated [ Gas96 , Ath15 , SW16 ] , and there is a wealth of research unveiling intriguing connections of chromatic symmetric functions with LLT polynomials [ CM18 ] , Hessenberg varieties [ BC18 , GP16 ] , Hecke algebras [ Hai93 , CHSS16 ] , and rook theory [ AN21 , CMP23 , NT24 ] . 
 
 
 
 In this paper, we study a generalization of Shareshian and Wachs’ chromatic symmetric functions.
We introduce a new parameter \alpha to define the \alpha -chromatic symmetric function \chi^{(\alpha)}_{\pi}[X;q] for a Dyck path \pi , for \alpha\in\mathbb{N} , as follows: 
 

 
 
 \chi^{(\alpha)}_{\pi}[X;q]:=\chi_{\pi}\left[Q_{\alpha}X;q\right], 
 
 
 
 where Q_{\alpha}:=\dfrac{q^{\alpha}-1}{q-1} ,
and extend \alpha to a real parameter using plethysm . Notably, for a Dyck path of size n (referred to here as an n -Dyck path), the coefficients of the \alpha -chromatic symmetric function \chi^{(\alpha)}_{\pi}[X;q] lie within the \mathbb{C}(q) -span of \left\{[\alpha]_{q},[\alpha]_{q}^{2},\dots,[\alpha]_{q}^{n}\right\} , where [\alpha]_{q}:=\dfrac{q^{\alpha}-1}{q-1} . We can express each coefficient using two alternative bases: 
 

 
 (1.1) 
 
 \left\{\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}\right\}_{k=0,1,\dots,n-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\{[\alpha]_{q}^{\underline{k}}\right\}_{k=1,2,\dots,n}. 
 
 
 
 Here, [\alpha]_{q}^{\underline{k}}:=[\alpha]_{q}[\alpha-1]_{q}\cdots[\alpha-k+1]_{q} 
is the q -falling factorial and \footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}=\dfrac{[\alpha+k]_{q}^{\underline{n}}}{[n]_{q}!} denotes the q -binomial coefficient.
This paper presents various positive combinatorial formulas for the \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions in these bases or their specialization at q=1 . Remarkably, Theorem 3.1 provides an explicit combinatorial formula for the monomial expansion, and Theorem 4.4 offers an interpretation in terms of the falling factorial basis as a sum of certain chromatic symmetric functions. Corollary 4.3 addresses the Schur expansion when \alpha\in\mathbb{N} , and Corollary 5.2 discusses Schur positivity in terms of two binomial bases. 
 
 
 
 
 1.2. Motivations 

 
 
 Our results and conjectures intersect with several significant topics, including the LLT polynomials . Introduced by Lascoux, Leclerc, and Thibon [ LLT97 ] , the LLT polynomials represent a class of symmetric functions that can be viewed as a q -analogue of a product of skew Schur functions. These polynomials are indexed by tuples of skew partitions, and when the tuple consists of single cells, they are called the unicellular LLT polynomials. Although Grojnowski and Haiman [ GH07 ] established the Schur positivity of the LLT polynomials, finding a combinatorial formula for the Schur coefficients remains challenging. Interestingly, the Schur coefficients of the \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions are related to those of the unicellular LLT polynomials (Proposition 7.1 ). Thus, studying the Schur expansion of the \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions in terms of the two bases in ( 1.1 ) could shed light on the remarkable problem of finding a combinatorial interpretation for the Schur coefficients of the LLT polynomials. 
 
 
 
 The second motivation for studying the Schur expansion of the \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions arises from analogous phenomena observed with the (integral form) Jack polynomials . Jack polynomials, which include an additional parameter \alpha , form an important family of symmetric functions with applications in mathematical physics, representation theory, and combinatorics. The process of obtaining the \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions from the unicellular LLT polynomials bears a resemblance to the derivation of the Jack polynomials from the modified Macdonald polynomials. While a combinatorial formula for the monomial expansion of the Jack polynomials is known [ KS97 , HHL05a ] , the Schur expansion has been less explored since the Jack polynomials are not Schur positive in general. Nevertheless, Alexandersson, Haglund, and Wang [ AHW21 ] conjectured that after certain rescaling, the Jack polynomials expand positively in terms of 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle\left\{\footnotesize{\binom{\alpha+k}{n}}s_{\lambda}\right\}_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq k\leq n-1\\
\lambda\vdash n\end{subarray}},\qquad\text{or}\qquad\left\{(\alpha)^{\underline{k}}s_{\lambda}\right\}_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq n\\
\lambda\vdash n\end{subarray}}. 
 
 
 
 Given the similarity between the Jack polynomials and the \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions, we hope our findings on the \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions will provide new insights on this problem concerning the Schur expansion of the Jack polynomials. 
 
 
 
 Finally, we explore the connection between the theory of chromatic symmetric functions and rook theory. Previous research has linked rook theory with chromatic symmetric functions, particularly in the ‘abelian’ case, as noted in [ SS93 , AN21 , CMP23 ] . In this paper, we use rook theory to explore the combinatorial properties of the \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions. Briefly, given a sequence of nonnegative integers c_{1},\ldots,c_{n} satisfying 0\leq c_{1}\leq c_{2}\leq\cdots\leq c_{n}\leq n , we let B denote the Ferrers board whose i th column is of height c_{i} , 1\leq i\leq n . (See [ Sta97 , Ch. 3] for background on rook theory). We define \text{PR}(B)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}[\alpha+c_{i}-i+1]_{q} , and call any product of n many q -integers that can be written as \text{PR}(B) for some Ferrers board B a rook product . Garsia and Remmel showed that for any Ferrers board B , 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle\text{PR}(B)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}h_{k}(B;q)}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}[\alpha]_{q}^{\underline{k}}r_{n-k}(B;q) 
 
 
 
 where r_{k}(B;q)\in\mathbb{N}[q] is the q -rook polynomial, h_{k}(B;q)\in\mathbb{N}[q] , and h_{k}(B;1) equals the k th hit number of B . They posed the problem of finding a statistic qstat defined on rook placements C of n nonattacking rooks (no two in the same row or column) in the n\times n square grid containing B , with exactly k rooks on B (i.e., permutations in \mathfrak{S}_{n} that ‘hit’ k many forbidden positions encoded by the cells of B ), such that h_{k}(B;q)=\sum_{C}q^{\text{qstat}(C,B)} . This problem was solved independently by Dworkin and the first author [ Hag98 , Dwo98 ] . In this paper, we provide a new solution to this problem, where the q -statistic we obtain has a natural interpretation in terms of colorings of incomparability graphs. 
 
 
 
 
 1.3. Outline 

 
 
 The structure of this paper is as follows. Section 2 contains background material on symmetric functions and rook theory that we use throughout the paper. For a thorough treatment of common notation and basic properties of the ring of symmetric functions, we refer the reader to [ Mac15 , Ch. 1] or [ Sta24 , Ch. 7] . We also
compute \chi_{\pi}\left[Q_{\alpha};q\right] 
using plethysm and the technique of superization developed in
 [ HHL+05b , HHL05a ] (see also [ Hag08 , Ch. 6] ). 
 
 
 
 Section 3 provides the first main theorem (Theorem 3.1 ), giving a combinatorial formula for coefficients of \chi_{\pi}^{(\alpha)}[X;q] in terms of the 
 

 
 
 \left\{\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}m_{\lambda}\right\}_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq n\\
\lambda\vdash n\end{subarray}} 
 
 
 
 basis. We use the superization technique and a bijective argument to prove it. 
 
 
 
 In Section 4 , we first introduce a novel XY -technique . This XY -technique leads to various positivity results. In particular, we prove the second main result (Theorem 4.4 ), expressing the falling factorial coefficient of \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions as a sum of certain chromatic symmetric functions. 
 
 
 
 Section 5 concerns Schur positivity of the \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions in terms of two types of \alpha -binomial bases. We also discuss certain symmetry between Schur coefficients of the \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions. 
 
 
 
 In Section 6 , we show that for any Dyck path \pi , \chi_{\pi}[Q_{\alpha};q] is a rook product and use this fact to obtain various expansions of \chi_{\pi}[Q_{\alpha};q] into the \footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}} and \footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}} bases. We show how these results, when combined with the known expansion of \chi_{\pi}[X;q] into Gessel’s fundamental quasisymmetric functions, give a new formula for the hit polynomial h_{k}(B;q) , namely, a new solution to the Garsia–Remmel q -hit polynomial problem. 
 
 
 
 Finally, we end this paper with Section 7 which contains remarks about motivating questions and other related problems such as geometric interpretations. 
 
 
 
 
 
 2. Background 

 
 
 2.1. Symmetric functions 

 
 
 A partition \lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{\ell}) of n is a nonincreasing sequence of positive integers satisfying \sum_{i}\lambda_{i}=n . We use \lambda\vdash n to denote that \lambda is a partition of n .
Each \lambda_{i} is called a part of \lambda and the number of parts in \lambda is called
the length of \lambda , denoted by \ell(\lambda) . We identify a partition \lambda with its (Young) diagram which is
the set of cells \{(i,j)\,:\,1\leq i\leq\ell(\lambda),\,1\leq j\leq\lambda_{i}\} . We use the English notation 
to draw the diagram, putting left justified \lambda_{i} cells in each row from top to bottom. 
 
 
 
 Let \Lambda_{F} denote the F -algebra of symmetric functions with coefficients in F=\mathbb{Q}(q,t) .
The elements in \Lambda_{F} may be viewed as formal power series over \mathbb{Q}(q,t) in infinitely many
variables X=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots) with finite degrees that are invariant under permutations of variables.
In this paper, we often replace t by q^{\alpha} , for some \alpha . 
 
 
 
 For \lambda\vdash n , the monomial symmetric functions m_{\lambda} 
are defined by
 m_{\lambda}[X]=\sum_{\nu}X^{\nu}, 
where the sum is over weak compositions \nu such that the nonincreasing rearrangements of the parts of \nu equals
 \lambda , and X^{\nu}=x_{1}^{\nu_{1}}x_{2}^{\nu_{2}}\cdots x_{n}^{\nu_{n}} , for \nu=(\nu_{1},\dotsm\nu_{n}) .
The elementary symmetric functions e_{\lambda} are defined by e_{\lambda}=\prod_{i}e_{\lambda_{i}} ,
where e_{0}=1 and e_{n}=\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{n}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{n}} , for n\geq 1 .
The complete homogeneous symmetric functions h_{\lambda} are defined by h_{\lambda}=\prod_{i}h_{\lambda_{i}} ,
where h_{0}=1 and h_{n}=\sum_{i_{1}\leq\cdots\leq i_{n}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{n}} , for n\geq 1 .
The power sum symmetric functions p_{\lambda} are defined by p_{\lambda}=\prod_{i}p_{\lambda_{i}} ,
where p_{0}=1 and p_{n}=\sum_{i}x_{i}^{n} for n\geq 1 . 
 
 
 
 A semistandard Young tableau of shape \lambda is a filling of \lambda 
with letters in \mathbb{Z}_{>0} such that the entries are weakly increasing across each row and strictly increasing up
each column. For a semistandard Young tableau T , we define the weight \operatorname{wt}(T) of T to be the sequence
 (\operatorname{wt}_{1}(T),\operatorname{wt}_{2}(T),\dots) , where \operatorname{wt}_{i}(T) is the multiplicity of i in T . Especially when the weight
is (1,1,1,\dots,1) , T is called standard . Let \operatorname{SSYT}(\lambda) ( \operatorname{SYT}(\lambda) , resp.) denote the set of semistandard Young tableaux (standard Young tableaux, resp.)
of shape \lambda . Then the Schur functions are defined by 
 

 
 
 s_{\lambda}[X]=\sum_{T\in\operatorname{SSYT}(\lambda)}X^{\operatorname{wt}(T)}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 We let \omega be the involution on symmetric functions such that \omega(p_{\lambda}[X])=(-1)^{|\lambda|-\ell(\lambda)}p_{\lambda}[X] . It sends elementary symmetric functions to homogeneous symmetric functions, \omega(e_{n}[X])=h_{n}[X] , and Schur functions to Schur functions, \omega(s_{\lambda}[X])=s_{\lambda^{\prime}}[X] . Here \lambda^{\prime} is the conjugate partition of \lambda . 
 
 
 
 Next, we briefly review plethystic notation.
Let E=E(t_{1},t_{2},\dots) be a formal Laurent series with rational coefficients in t_{1},t_{2},\dots . The
 plethystic substitution p_{k}[E] is defined by replacing each t_{i} in E by t_{i}^{k} , i.e.,
 p_{k}[E]=E\left(t_{1}^{k},t_{2}^{k},\dots\right) and 
 

 
 
 p_{\lambda}[E]=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}p_{\lambda_{i}}[E]. 
 
 
 
 Here, we summarize some of the basic properties of plethystic substitution. 
 
 (1) 
 
 
 If X=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots) , then
 \displaystyle p_{k}[X]=\sum_{i\geq 1}x_{i}^{k}=p_{k}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots) . 
 
 (2) 
 
 
 For a real parameter z , p_{k}[zX]=z^{k}p_{k}[X] . 
 
 (3) 
 
 
 p_{k}[(1-t)X]=\sum_{i}x_{i}^{k}(1-t^{k})=(1-t^{k})p_{k}[X] . 
 
 (4) 
 
 
 \displaystyle p_{k}\left[\frac{X}{1-q}\right]=\sum_{i}\frac{x_{i}^{k}}{1-q^{k}}=\frac{1}{1-q^{k}}p_{k}[X] . 
 
 
 
 Then for an arbitrary symmetric function f , we define f[E] by \sum_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}p_{\lambda}[E] 
if f=\sum_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}p_{\lambda} . Due to the above property (1), it easily follows that for any f\in\Lambda_{F} , f[X]=f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots) . For this reason, it is a common convention
in plethystic expression that X stands for the summation of variables x_{1}+x_{2}+\cdots . In a similar vein, for X=x_{1}+x_{2}+\cdots,Y=y_{1}+y_{2}+\cdots , we have f[X+Y]=f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,y_{1},y_{2},\dots) and f[XY]=f(x_{1}y_{1},x_{1}y_{2},\dots,x_{2}y_{1},x_{2}y_{2},\dots,) . 
 
 
 
 If we let Z=(-x_{1},-x_{2},\dots) , then p_{k}(Z)=\sum_{i}(-1)^{k}x_{i}^{k} which is different from
 p_{k}[-X]=\sum_{i}(-x_{i}^{k}) . Thus we introduce a symbol \epsilon to denote the negation of variables inside plethystic brackets, i.e., p_{k}[\epsilon X]=\sum_{i}(-1)^{k}x_{i}^{k} .
Then note that for any symmetric function f , we have \omega(f(X))=f[-\epsilon X] .
For a more comprehensive account, see [ Hag08 , Mac15 ] . 
 
 
 
 
 2.2. Combinatorics 

 
 
 For a fixed n , an n - Dyck path is a lattice path from (0,0) to (n,n) consisting of the up-steps (0,1) and right-steps (1,0) which stays above the main diagonal. There is a natural way to associate a Hessenberg function h(\pi) , a natural unit interval order P(\pi) , and a graph G(\pi) for a Dyck path \pi . We outline those correspondences. 
 
 
 
 Throughout this paper, we set [n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\} . A Hessenberg function is a nondecreasing function h:[n]\rightarrow[n] such that h(i)\geq i for all 1\leq i\leq n . For a n -Dyck path \pi , if we let h(\pi) be a function whose value at i is the i -th column height of \pi , then h(\pi) is a Hessenberg function. Therefore, a Dyck path determines a Hessenberg function and vice versa. 
 
 
 
 A natural unit interval order is a poset arising from an arrangement of unit intervals.
It is known by Wine and Freund [ WF57 ] 
that unit interval orders are counted by Catalan numbers. Furthermore, two different bijections to associate a unit interval order to a Dyck path are given by Skandera and Reed [ SR03 ] , and Guay-Paquet, Morales and Rowland [ GPMR14 ] . So, we can represent natural unit interval orders by Dyck paths. 
 
 
 
 Let us describe the correspondence. For a Dyck path \pi , P=P(\pi) is a poset on [n] whose order relation is defined by i<_{P(\pi)}j if the cell (i,j) lies above \pi .
In this case, we also denote the order relation by i<_{\pi}j for i<_{P({\bf m})}j . In addition, we say (i,j) is an attacking pair of \pi , if 1\leq i<j\leq n and i\nless_{\pi}j . 
 
 
 
 Note that a poset P is called (r+s) -free if P does not contain an induced subposet isomorphic to the direct sum of an r -element chain and an s -element chain.
It is well-known that a poset P is a natural unit interval order
if and only if it is (2+2) -free and (3+1) -free. 
 
 
 
 Given a poset P , the incomparability graph \operatorname{inc}(P) is a graph whose vertex set is the elements of P 
with edges connecting pairs of incomparable elements in P . Notice that for a natural unit interval order P(\pi) associated with a Dyck path \pi , the corresponding incomparability graph is encoded in the cells between the Dyck path and the main diagonal. We denote this graph by G(\pi) . 
 
 
 
 When referring to a Dyck path \pi , we will implicitly encompass the associated objects discussed above. In other words, depending on context \pi may represent not only the Dyck path itself but also its corresponding Hessenberg function h(\pi) , the natural unit interval order P(\pi) , and the associated incomparability graph G(\pi) . 
 
 
 Example 2.1 . 
 
 
 Given a Dyck path \pi=\text{NNENNEENEE} , where N denotes the up step and E denotes the east step, starting from the south left corner, the corresponding Hessenberg function h(\pi) has
the function value (2,4,4,5,5) , where i th entry h(i) is the height of the i th column of \pi . 
 
 
 
 The corresponding natural unit interval order P(\pi) is the poset with the relations 
 

 
 
 1<_{\pi}3,\,1<_{\pi}4,\,1<_{\pi}5,\,2<_{\pi}5,\,3<_{\pi}5. 
 
 
 
 For the poset P(\pi) , the incomparability graph G(\pi) is the graph with the vertex set \{1,2,3,4,5\} and the edge set \{\{2,1\},\{3,2\},\{4,2\},\{4,3\},\{5,4\}\} .
See Figure 1 .

 2.3. Chromatic symmetric functions and unicellular LLT polynomials 

 
 
 For an n -Dyck path \pi and a word w\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{n} of length n , we define the inversion statistic as
 \operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(w)=|\{(i,j)~:~i<j,\,i\nless_{\pi}j,\,\text{ and }w_{i}>w_{j}\}| . 
 
 
 
 The chromatic symmetric function indexed by \pi is defined by 
 

 
 (2.1) 
 
 \chi_{\pi}[X;q]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{n}\\
\text{proper}\end{subarray}}q^{\operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(w)}x^{w}, 
 
 
 
 where the sum is over ‘proper’ colorings of G(\pi) , that is, over words of length n such that w_{i}\neq w_{j} for i\nless_{\pi}j . We can think of the terms in this sum as placements of the words w_{1}w_{2}\cdots w_{n} in the main diagonal cells with w_{i} in the cell (i,i) in \pi in Figure 1 , where inversions correspond to cells (i,j) , with the number w_{i} in the i th row from the top being larger than the number w_{j} in the j th column from the right. We note that since \chi_{\pi}[X;q] is symmetric, we can reverse the variable set without changing the function. Equivalently, if we define
 \operatorname{asc}_{\pi}(w)=|\{(i,j)~:~i<j,\,i\nless_{\pi}j,\,\text{ and }w_{i}<w_{j}\}| for w\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{n} of length n , then we have 
 

 
 (2.2) 
 
 \chi_{\pi}[X;q]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{n}\\
\text{proper}\end{subarray}}q^{\operatorname{asc}_{\pi}(w)}x^{w}. 
 
 
 
 Note that the function \chi_{\pi}[X;q] can be defined as a form of ( 2.2 ) for any labeled graphs and the function is not in general symmetric but quasisymmetric. However, it is proved by Shareshian and Wachs [ SW16 , Theorem 4.5] that the chromatic quasisymmetric function is symmetric for incomparability graphs of natural unit interval orders. We will only consider the symmetric case in this paper, and take ( 2.1 ) as the definition of \chi_{\pi} . 
 
 
 
 In definition ( 2.1 ) for the chromatic symmetric function \chi_{\pi} , if we remove the condition for w to be proper ( w_{i}\neq w_{j} for i\nless_{\pi}j ), then it becomes the unicellular LLT polynomial indexed by \pi , namely 
 

 
 (2.3) 
 
 \operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[X;q]=\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{n}}q^{\operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(w)}x^{w}. 
 
 
 
 The original definition of the LLT polynomials given by Lascoux, Leclerc, and Thibon [ LLT97 ] 
uses cospin statistic defined on ribbon tableaux. Later, Bylund and Haiman found
an equivalent definition using dinv statistic defined over k -tuple of semistandard
Young tableaux of skew shapes.
In the case when each skew shape consists of only one cell, the LLT polynomials are called unicellular.
In this case, the diagram of a tuple of single cells corresponds to a Dyck path [ Hag08 , CM18 ] and the definition can be rewritten as ( 2.3 ). 
 
 
 
 Carlsson and Mellit [ CM18 ] utilized an explicit relationship between the unicellular LLT polynomials and the chromatic symmetric functions via a plethystic substitution. 
 
 
 Proposition 2.2 . 
 
 
 For an n -Dyck path \pi , we have 
 

 
 (2.4) 
 
 \chi_{\pi}[X;q]=\dfrac{\operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[(q-1)X;q]}{(q-1)^{n}}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 By replacing (q-1) in the numerator of ( 2.4 ) within the plethysm with (t-1) , and subsequently substituting t=q^{\alpha} , we define the \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions as follows. 
 
 
 Definition 2.3 . 
 
 
 Given an n -Dyck path \pi and \alpha\in\mathbb{R} , we define the \alpha -chromatic symmetric function by 
 

 
 (2.5) 
 
 \chi^{(\alpha)}_{\pi}[X;q]:=\chi_{\pi}\left[Q_{\alpha}X;q\right]=\frac{\operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[(q^{\alpha}-1)X;q]}{(q-1)^{n}}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 2.4. Superization 

 
 
 To deal with the plethystic substitution used in ( 2.5 ),
we review the standard technique of superization following [ HHL+05b , HHL05a ] and [ Hag08 , pp. 99-101] . Let
 \mathcal{A}_{+}=\{1,2,\ldots,n\} and \mathcal{A}_{-}=\{\overline{1},\overline{2},\ldots,\overline{n}\} denote positive and negative alphabets, respectively. Then for any fixed ordering of the
alphabets the following holds. 
 
 
 Proposition 2.4 . 
 
 
 Let f be a symmetric function of homogeneous degree n , written in terms of quasisymmetric functions as 
 

 
 
 f[X]=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}c_{\sigma}F_{n,\text{Des}(\sigma^{-1})}[X], 
 
 
 
 where the c_{\sigma} are constants independent of X .
Then \tilde{f}[X,Y]=\omega_{Y}f[X+Y] (called the superization of f ) has the expansion 
 

 
 
 \tilde{f}[X,Y]=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}c_{\sigma}\tilde{F}_{n,\text{Des}(\sigma^{-1})}[X,Y]. 
 
 
 
 Here for D\subseteq[n-1] , F_{n,D}[X] is Gessel’s fundamental quasisymmetric function corresponding to D and {\tilde{F}}_{n,D}[X,Y] is its superization, defined as 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle F_{n,D}[X] 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq\cdots\leq a_{n}\atop{a_{i}=a_{i+1}\implies i\notin D}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{a_{i}},\qquad\text{and} 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle{\tilde{F}}_{n,D}[X,Y] 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq\cdots\leq a_{n}\atop{a_{i}=a_{i+1}\in\mathcal{A}_{+}\implies i\notin D\atop{a_{i}=a_{i+1}\in\mathcal{A}_{-}\implies i\in D}}}\prod_{i:a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{+}}x_{a_{i}}\prod_{i:a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{-}}y_{|a_{i}|}, 
 
 
 
 with |\overline{i}|=i for 1\leq i\leq n . 
 
 
 
 Definition 2.5 . 
 
 
 . Given a word w\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{n} , we let \text{stan}(w) denote the standardization of w , which is the unique permutation \beta\in\mathfrak{S}_{n} 
satisfying that if w_{i}<w_{j} then \beta_{i}<\beta_{j} , and if w_{i}=w_{j} for i<j , then \beta_{i}<\beta_{j} . For example, \text{stan}(323662)=314562 . More generally, let \mathcal{A}_{\pm}=\mathcal{A}_{+}\cup\mathcal{A}_{-} . Given a superword \tilde{w}\in\mathcal{A}_{\pm}^{n} , we define \text{stan}(\tilde{w}) to be the permutation \beta , as defined above, with the additional convention that if \tilde{w}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{j}\in\mathcal{A}_{-} and i<j , then \beta_{i}>\beta_{j} .
So, equal negative letters standardize in the opposite way of equal positive letters. For example, assuming the
ordering 1<\overline{1}<2<\overline{2}<\cdots<n<\overline{n} , \text{stan}(2\,\overline{2}\,\overline{2}\,\overline{1}\,\overline{1}\,1\,1\,2\,\overline{1})=698541273 . 
 
 
 
 
 Let X_{n}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\} and Y_{n}=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\} be two finite sets of variables. It is a fairly straightforward exercise to show that for any \sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n} , 
 

 
 (2.6) 
 
 \displaystyle F_{n,\text{Des}(\sigma^{-1})}[X_{n}] 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{A}_{+}^{n}\atop\text{stan}(w)=\sigma}x^{w},\qquad\text{and} 
 
 
 (2.7) 
 
 \displaystyle\tilde{F}_{n,\text{Des}(\sigma^{-1})}[X_{n},Y_{n}] 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{\tilde{w}\in\mathcal{A}_{\pm}^{n}\atop\text{stan}(\tilde{w})=\sigma}\prod_{i:w_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{+}}x_{\tilde{w}_{i}}\prod_{i:\tilde{w}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{-}}y_{|\tilde{w}_{i}|}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Let G[X;q] be a symmetric function of homogeneous degree n , depending on a parameter q , satisfying 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle G[X;q]=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{A}_{+}^{n}}q^{\operatorname{stat}(w)}x^{w}. 
 
 
 
 Proposition 2.4 , ( 2.6 ), and ( 2.7 ) imply that if
 \operatorname{stat} is invariant under standardization, then 
 

 
 (2.8) 
 
 \displaystyle\omega^{Y}G[X+Y;q]=\sum_{\tilde{w}\in\mathcal{A}_{\pm}^{n}}q^{\operatorname{stat}(\tilde{w})}\prod_{i:\tilde{w}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{+}}x_{a_{i}}\prod_{i:\tilde{w}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{-}}y_{|a_{i}|}. 
 
 
 
 Here \omega^{Y} sends s_{\lambda}(Y) to s_{\lambda^{\prime}}(Y) , and fixes the X variables.
Note that since \omega s_{\lambda}(Y)=s_{\lambda}[-\epsilon Y] , we can denote the left hand side of
( 2.8 ) as 
 

 
 (2.9) 
 
 \omega^{Y}G[X+Y;q]=G[X-\epsilon Y;q]. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Given an n -Dyck path \pi , for 1\leq i\leq n , let b_{i}(\pi) and a_{i}(\pi) denote the number of cells below \pi and strictly above the diagonal y=x ,
in the i th column (row, respectively), from the right (top, respectively). For example, for the path on the left in Figure 3 of Section 4.1 , we have
 (b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})=(0,1,1,0,1,2,1,1,2) and (a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=(1,1,0,2,1,1,2,1,0) . Also, let \operatorname{area}(\pi)=\sum_{i}a_{i}=\sum_{i}b_{i} . 
 
 
 Proposition 2.6 . 
 
 
 For an n -Dyck path \pi and a real parameter \alpha , 
 

 
 (2.10) 
 
 \displaystyle\chi_{\pi}[Q_{\alpha};q]=q^{\operatorname{area}(\pi)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}[\alpha-a_{i}(\pi)]_{q}=q^{\operatorname{area}(\pi)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}[\alpha-b_{i}(\pi)]_{q}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 By ( 2.4 ), we have (q-1)^{n}\chi_{\pi}[Q_{\alpha};q]=\operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[q^{\alpha}-1;q] . To deal with the plethystic substitution of q^{\alpha}-1 into \operatorname{LLT}_{\pi} ,
we use the ( 2.9 ). Fix the ordering 1<\overline{1}<2<\overline{2}<\cdots<n<\overline{n} in
 \mathcal{A}_{\pm} , and for a superword
 \tilde{w}\in\mathcal{A}_{\pm}^{n} , let \operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(\tilde{w})=\operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(\text{stan}(\tilde{w})) . 
 
 
 
 By using the definition of \operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[X;q] in ( 2.3 ), since \operatorname{inv} is invariant under standardization,
we try to express \operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[q^{\alpha}-1;q] as \operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[X-\epsilon Y;q] for some choice of X,Y so that we can write \operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[q^{\alpha}-1;q] 
as a weighted sum over superwords. That is, we want to
choose X,Y so that X-\epsilon Y=q^{\alpha}-1 . Since \epsilon^{2}=1 , one way to do this is to let X=q^{\alpha} (the set of variables with one element with
weight q^{\alpha} ), and Y=\epsilon (the set of variables with just one element of weight -1 ). With this choice, by ( 2.8 ) we have 
 

 
 (2.11) 
 
 \displaystyle(q-1)^{n}\chi_{\pi}^{(\alpha)}[1;q]=\sum_{\tilde{w}\in\{1,\bar{1}\}^{n}}q^{\operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(\tilde{w})}q^{\alpha\text{pos}(\tilde{w})}(-1)^{\text{neg}(\tilde{w})}, 
 
 
 
 where the sum is over all superwords \tilde{w} of length n 
consisting of 1 ’s and \overline{1} ’s, with \text{pos}(\tilde{w}) denoting the number of 1 ’s and \text{neg}(\tilde{w}) the number of \overline{1} ’s. 
 
 
 
 Note that two adjacent vertices in G_{\pi} which are both colored by \overline{1} ’s create an inversion pair since the \overline{1} in the higher row standardizes to a larger integer than the one in the lower row. Whereas, two 1 ’s which color adjacent vertices in G_{\pi} never create an inversion pair. Furthermore, a pair of adjacent vertices b,c , with b colored 1 and c colored \overline{1} , create an inversion pair if and only if b>c . A moments thought shows that any row containing a \overline{1} contributes inversion pairs for each edge of G_{\pi} in its row, while rows with a 1 do not create any inversion pairs.
So, given a coloring of vertices 2,3,\ldots,n by 1 ’s and \overline{1} ’s,
the cases of coloring vertex 1 with 1 and \overline{1} would together contribute
the weight (q^{\alpha}-q^{a_{i}(\pi)}) to the right hand side of ( 2.11 ).
Hence by induction we have 
 

 
 (2.12) 
 
 (q-1)^{n}\chi_{\pi}^{(\alpha)}[1;q]=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(q^{\alpha}-q^{a_{i}(\pi)}), 
 
 
 
 and note that 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}(q^{\alpha}-q^{a_{i}(\pi)})=q^{\operatorname{area}(\pi)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(q^{\alpha-a_{i}}-1). 
 
 
 
 Using this in ( 2.12 ) and dividing both sides by (q-1)^{n} completes the proof of the first equality in ( 2.10 ). 
 
 
 
 To prove the second inequality, use the well-known fact that \chi_{\pi}[X;q] is invariant under reversing \pi , which replaces row lengths a_{i}(\pi) by column lengths b_{i}(\pi) . We omit the details.

 Remark 2.7 . 
 
 
 Recently, Hikita [ Hik24 ] announced a proof of the Stanley-Stembridge conjecture. His approach uses a probabilistic argument motivated from the observation that 
 

 
 (2.13) 
 
 \sum_{\lambda\vdash n}q^{|\mathsf{e}|-|\mathsf{e}_{\lambda}|}\frac{c_{\lambda,\pi}(q)}{\prod_{i}[\lambda_{i}]_{q}!}=1, 
 
 
 
 where \chi_{\pi}[X;q]=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}c_{\lambda,\pi}(q)e_{\lambda}[X] is the e -expansion of the chromatic quasisymmetric function indexed by a Dyck path \pi ,
 |\mathsf{e}|={n\choose 2}-\text{area}(\pi) is the number of cells above \pi , and |\mathsf{e}_{\lambda}|=\sum_{i<j}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j} .
We can easily derive ( 2.13 ) from ( 2.10 ), by applying the principal specialization of the elementary symmetric functions and by letting \alpha\rightarrow\infty . 
 
 
 
 
 
 2.5. Rook theory 

 
 
 For a nonnegative integer sequence c_{1},\dots,c_{n} , we associate a board B(c_{1},\dots,c_{n}) which is the collection of all cells (i,j) such that 1\leq i\leq c_{i} and 1\leq j\leq n . If the sequence satisfies 0\leq c_{1}\leq c_{2}\leq\cdots\leq c_{n}\leq n , then B=B(c_{1},\dots,c_{n}) is called a Ferrers board .
We say that we place k nonattacking rooks on B for choosing a k -subset of cells in B such that
no two elements have a common coordinate, that is, no two rooks lie in the same row or in the same column.
We identify a permutation \sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n} with the rook placement
 \{(i,\sigma_{i})\,|\,i=1,2,\dots,n\} on [n]\times[n] . 
 
 
 
 For a Ferrers board B=B(c_{1},\dots,c_{n}) , let H_{k}(B) denote the set of n -rook placements on [n]\times[n] with k rooks in B , i.e., 
 

 
 
 H_{k}(B)=\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}:|\sigma\cap B|=k\} 
 
 
 
 and h_{k}(B) be the the k -th hit number defined by h_{k}(B)=|H_{k}(B)| . Let R_{k}(B) denote the set of
all k -rook placements in B 
and r_{k}(B) be the k -th rook number defined by r_{k}(B)=|R_{k}(B)| . The famous Goldman–Joichi–White formula is a factorization formula for the polynomials associated to the rook numbers. 
 
 
 Theorem 2.8 . 
 
 
 [ GJW75 ] Let B=B(c_{1},\dots,c_{n}) be a Ferrers board in [n]\times[n] . Then 
 

 
 (2.14) 
 
 \prod_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha+c_{i}-(i-1))=\sum_{k=0}^{n}r_{n-k}(B)(\alpha)^{\underline{k}} 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 The right-hand-side of ( 2.14 ) can also be expressed as \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{\alpha+k}{n}h_{k}(B) .
We refer the product on the left-hand side of ( 2.14 ) as a rook product . Generally, a product of the form
 \prod_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha+b_{i}) 
is called a rook product when the nonincreasing rearrangement \beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n} of b_{1},\dots,b_{n} satisfies the condition \beta_{i}-1\leq\beta_{i+1} for 1\leq i\leq n-1 ,
and 0\leq\beta_{1}\leq n . 
 
 
 
 Garsia–Remmel introduced a q -analogue of the Goldman–Joichi–White formula ( 2.14 ). To elaborate, let us define q -statistics first. Given a Ferrers board B and
a k -rook placement C , a placement of k nonattacking rooks on B (see Figure 2 for an example), let \operatorname{inv}_{B}(C) denote the number of cells of B which are left after cancelling
all cells which are either weakly to the right in the row, or below in the column, of any rooks in C . Then we define 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle r_{k}(B;q)=\sum_{C\in R_{k}(B)}q^{\operatorname{inv}_{B}(C)}. 
 
 
 
 
 Theorem 2.9 . 
 
 
 [ GR86 ] Let B=B(c_{1},\dots,c_{n}) be a Ferrers board in [n]\times[n] . Then 
 

 
 
 \prod_{i=1}^{n}[\alpha+c_{i}-(i-1)]_{q}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}r_{n-k}(B;q)[\alpha]^{\underline{k}}_{q} 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 Garsia and Remmel also defined q -hit numbers h_{k}(B;q) algebraically in terms of the r_{k}(B;q) 
 

 
 
 \sum_{k=0}^{n}[k]_{q}!r_{n-k}(B;q)\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}(x-q^{i})=\sum_{j=0}^{n}h_{j}(B;q)x^{j}. 
 
 
 
 They also posed the problem of finding a combinatorial statistic \operatorname{stat} on rook placements C satisfying 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle h_{k}(B;q)=\sum_{C\in H_{k}(B)}q^{\operatorname{stat}_{B}(C)}. 
 
 
 
 Dworkin and the first author independently solved this problem [ Dwo98 ] , [ Hag98 ] . Their solutions are closely related to each other. Here is one way to describe the solution of the first author. Given a placement of n rooks on [n]\times[n] , first cancel each cell in the row to the right of any rook. Then for each rook on
 B , count the number of uncancelled cells above it in the column, but still on the board B . Next, for each rook not on B count the number of uncancelled cells as you travel upwards from the rook to the top of [n]\times[n] , then wrap around to the bottom of the column (as on a torus), and continue travelling upwards to the top of the column in B . The statistic \operatorname{stat}(C) is the total number of uncancelled cells counted in this procedure.
For example, for the placement of n rooks on [n]\times[n] on the right in Figure 2 , \operatorname{stat}_{B}(C)=10 (represented by circles). As noted by Dworkin and the first author,
for any Ferrers board B(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}) we have

n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}.

 3.1. Monomial expansion 

 
 
 Given an n -Dyck path \pi , we define an ordering \prec_{\pi} for bi-letters (i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\times[n] as follows: 
 

 
 
 (i,j)\prec_{\pi}(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\iff\begin{cases}&i<i^{\prime}~\text{ or }\\
&i=i^{\prime}~\text{ and }~j<_{\pi}j^{\prime}.\end{cases} 
 
 
 
 For \lambda\vdash n , let M(\lambda) be the set of all words
of weight \lambda , which means all words where i occurs \lambda_{i} times for i\geq 1 . For a bi-word \displaystyle(w,\sigma)\in M(\lambda)\times\mathfrak{S}_{n} , we say that i\in[n-1] 
is an ascent of (w,\sigma) with respect to \pi if 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle(w_{i},\sigma_{i})\prec_{\pi}(w_{i+1},\sigma_{i+1}). 
 
 
 
 We define \operatorname{asc}_{\pi}(w,\sigma) as the number of ascents of (w,\sigma) with respect to \pi .
Let \operatorname{comaj}_{\pi}(w,\sigma) be the sum of positions where the ascents of (w,\sigma) occur. Finally, for a bi-word (w,\sigma)\in M(\lambda)\times\mathfrak{S}_{n} , we define a statistic \operatorname{stat}_{\pi} by 
 

 
 
 \operatorname{stat}_{\pi}(w,\sigma)={\operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(\tilde{w})+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}\operatorname{inv}_{\pi[w^{-1}(\{i\})]}(\sigma[w^{-1}(\{i\})])+\binom{n}{2}-nk+\operatorname{comaj}_{\pi}(w,\sigma)}, 
 
 
 
 where \tilde{w}(i)=w_{(\sigma^{-1})_{i}} , w^{-1}(\{i\}):=\{j\in[n]:w_{j}=i\} , \pi[A] is the induced subgraph \pi on A , and \sigma[A] is the restriction of permutation \sigma to A . Using these notions, we state a monomial expansion of \chi^{(\alpha)}_{\pi}[X;q] into the basis \left\{\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}\right\}_{0\leq k\leq n-1} . 
 
 
 Theorem 3.1 . 
 
 
 For an n -Dyck path \pi , we have 
 

 
 
 \chi_{\pi}^{(\alpha)}[X;q]=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\sum_{(w,\sigma)\in M(\lambda)\times\mathfrak{S}_{n}}q^{\operatorname{stat}_{\pi}(w,\sigma)}\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+\operatorname{asc}_{\pi}(w,\sigma)\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}m_{\lambda}[X].

 3.2. Proof of Theorem 3.1 

 
 
 Recall the definition of the unicellular LLT polynomials 
 

 
 
 \operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[X;q]=\sum_{c\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{n}}q^{\operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(c)}x^{c}. 
 
 
 
 We can derive an expression for \operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[(t-1)X;q] by an extension of our earlier derivation of ( 2.10 ). Consider the ordering 
 

 
 
 1<\overline{1}<2<\overline{2}<\cdots<n<\overline{n} 
 
 
 
 on \mathcal{A}_{\pm} .
By letting f=\operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[X] , X=tX and Y=\epsilon X in ( 2.9 ) and by using ( 2.8 ),
we have 
 

 
 (3.1) 
 
 \operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[(t-1)X;q]=\sum_{\tilde{c}\in\mathcal{A}_{\pm}^{n}}q^{\operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(\tilde{c})}t^{\text{pos}(\tilde{c})}(-1)^{\text{neg}(\tilde{c})}x^{|\tilde{c}|}, 
 
 
 
 where |\tilde{c}| is the positive word obtained by replacing each \overline{k} in \tilde{c} by its absolute value k .
To compute the right hand side of ( 3.1 ), consider the
superfillings \tilde{c} with fixed absolute value c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{n}) .
Suppose that we fixed the values of \tilde{c}_{k} , for 2\leq k\leq n , and
let us consider the cases when \tilde{c}_{1} is c_{1} or \overline{c}_{1} .
If we take \tilde{c}_{1} to be c_{1} , then it does not contribute any inversions since it does not form inversion pairs with any occurrences of c_{1} or \overline{c}_{1} in rows below. However, if we take c_{1} to be \overline{c}_{1} ,
then any attacking pairs with label c_{1} or \overline{c}_{1} in below rows would contribute inversions. Inversions involving other numbers which are neither c_{1} nor \overline{c}_{1} are not affected by this sign changes. As a result, we get
the factor (t-q^{N_{\pi}(1)}) where the term t corresponds to the choice of c_{1} and -q^{N_{\pi}(1)} to the choice of \overline{c}_{1} , where N_{\pi}(i) is the number of j ’s such that i<j , i\nless_{\pi}j and c_{i}=c_{j} , for 1\leq i<n . By
induction we get 
 

 
 
 \operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[(t-1)X;q]=\sum_{c\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{n}}q^{\operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(c)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(t-q^{N_{\pi}(i)})x^{c}. 
 
 
 
 By replacing t by q^{\alpha} and dividing
by (q-1)^{n} , what we get in the left hand side is the \alpha -chromatic symmetric function by Proposition 2.2 . Therefore, we have 
 

 
 (3.2) 
 
 \chi_{\pi}^{(\alpha)}[X;q]=\frac{\operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[(q^{\alpha}-1)X;q]}{(q-1)^{n}}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\sum_{c\in M(\lambda)}q^{{\operatorname{inv}_{\pi}}(c)}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}\left(q^{{\operatorname{area}}(\pi[c^{-1}(i)])}\prod_{j=1}^{\lambda_{i}}[\alpha-a_{j}(\pi[c^{-1}(i)])]_{q}\right)m_{\lambda}[X]. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Now we simplify the above identity ( 3.2 ). For a given positive integer \alpha , a word (coloring) c\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{n} and another word (decoration) d\in{\{0,1,\dots,\alpha-1\}}^{n} , we say that the bi-word (c,d) is an \alpha -decorated proper coloring of \pi of weight \lambda if the weight of c is \lambda , and for any cell (i,j) with i<j and i\nless_{\pi}j , we have (c_{i},d_{i})\neq(c_{j},d_{j}) . Let \mathcal{C}^{(\alpha)}_{\pi,\lambda} denote the set of \alpha -decorated proper colorings of weight \lambda . By Proposition 2.6 and
the definition of the chromatic symmetric function in ( 2.1 ), we have 
 

 
 
 \prod_{j=1}^{n}[\alpha-a_{j}(\pi)]_{q}=q^{-\operatorname{area}(\pi)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\{0,1,\dots,\alpha-1\}^{n}\\
\text{ proper coloring of $\pi$}\end{subarray}}q^{\operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(\sigma)+|\sigma|}. 
 
 
 
 Utilizing ( 3.2 ) and the identity above, we get the monomial expansion of the \alpha -chromatic symmetric function as follows: 
 

 
 (3.3) 
 
 \chi^{(\alpha)}_{\pi}[X;q]=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\sum_{(c,d)\in\mathcal{C}^{(\alpha)}_{\pi,\lambda}}q^{\operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(c)+|d|}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}q^{\operatorname{inv}_{\pi[c^{-1}(i)]}(d(c^{-1}(i)))}m_{\lambda}[X]. 
 
 
 
 
 
 To prove Theorem 3.1 , it suffices to verify the identity for n distinct values of \alpha . To that end, we fix an integer \alpha and prove it by using the bijection below. 
 
 
 Definition 3.3 . 
 
 
 Consider an integer 0\leq\alpha\leq n-1 . Let \binom{[\alpha+k]}{n} denote the multiset of size n whose elements are in \{0,1,\dots,\alpha+k-n\} , namely 
 

 
 
 \binom{[\alpha+k]}{n}=\{\tau=(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\dots,\tau_{n})~|~0\leq\tau_{i}\leq\alpha+k-n,\,\tau_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\tau_{n}\}. 
 
 
 
 Note that the size of this set is equal to \binom{\alpha+k}{n} .
The map 
 

 
 
 \Phi:\bigcup_{k=0}^{n-1}\left\{(w,\sigma)\in M(\lambda)\times\mathfrak{S}_{n}:\operatorname{asc}(w,\sigma)=k\right\}\times\binom{[\alpha+k]}{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{(\alpha)}_{\pi,\lambda} 
 
 
 
 is defined by 
 

 
 
 \Phi(w,\sigma,\tau)_{\sigma_{i}}=(w_{i},\tau_{i}+(i-1)-\operatorname{asc}_{\pi}^{<i}(w,\sigma)). 
 
 
 
 Here, \operatorname{asc}_{\pi}^{<i}(w,\sigma) counts the ascents occurring in the first i-1 positions. 
 
 
 
 Proposition 3.4 . 
 
 
 The map \Phi defined in Definition 3.3 is a bijection. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 Given an \alpha -decorated proper coloring (c,d)\in\mathcal{C}^{(\alpha)}_{\pi,\lambda} , let \sigma_{i} be the index j such that (c_{i},d_{i}) is the j -th element in the colexicographic order among \{(c_{j},d_{j})\}_{j\in[n]} . This defines a unique permutation \sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n} . Let w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n}) be the word where w_{i}=c_{\sigma_{i}} . Finally, let \tilde{d} be the weakly increasing rearrangement of d , that is, \tilde{d}_{i}=d_{\sigma_{i}} . Then \tau=(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{n}) is given by 
 

 
 
 \tau_{i}=\tilde{d}_{i}-(i-1)+\operatorname{asc}_{\pi}^{<i}(w,\sigma). 
 
 
 
 It is straightforward to verify that \Phi(w,\sigma,\tau)=(c,d) , thus the map \Phi is onto. Through this process, the permutation \sigma , the word w , and \tau are uniquely defined, demonstrating that the map \Phi is one-to-one. 
 
 
 
 ∎ 
 
 
 
 Proof of Theorem 3.1 . 
 
 
 Via the bijection \Phi , we can identify \mathcal{C}^{(\alpha)}_{\pi,\lambda} with the corresponding set of bi-words, and so the monomial coefficient in ( 3.3 )
can be rewritten as 
 

 
 
 [m_{\lambda}]\left(\chi^{(\alpha)}_{\pi}[X;q]\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(w,\sigma,\tau)\in(M(\lambda)\times\mathfrak{S}_{n})\times\binom{[\alpha+k]}{n}\\
\operatorname{asc}_{\pi}(w,\sigma)=k\end{subarray}}q^{\operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(\tilde{w})+|\tilde{\tau}|}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}q^{\operatorname{inv}_{\pi[w^{-1}(\{i\})]}(\tilde{\tau}[w^{-1}(\{i\})])}, 
 
 
 
 where \tilde{w}_{i}=w_{(\sigma^{-1})_{i}} , \tilde{\tau}=(\tilde{\tau}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\tau}_{n}) and \tilde{\tau}_{i}=\tau_{i}+i-1-\operatorname{asc}_{\pi}^{<i}(w,\sigma) . 
 
 
 
 First, we claim that given (w,\sigma)\in M(\lambda)\times\mathfrak{S}_{n} with \operatorname{asc}_{\pi}(w,\sigma)=k , the factor 
 

 
 
 q^{\operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(\tilde{w})}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}q^{\operatorname{inv}_{\pi[w^{-1}(\{i\})]}(\tilde{\tau}[w^{-1}(\{i\})])} 
 
 
 
 is invariant under varying \tau\in\binom{[\alpha+k]}{n} . The term q^{\operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(\tilde{w})} is clearly independent of \tau , so we only need to care about the product \prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}q^{\operatorname{inv}_{\pi[w^{-1}(\{i\})]}(\tilde{\tau}[w^{-1}(\{i\})])} . First, observe that 
 

 
 
 \operatorname{inv}_{\pi[w^{-1}(\{i\})]}(\tilde{\tau}[w^{-1}(\{i\})])=\operatorname{inv}_{\pi[w^{-1}(\{i\})]}(\sigma[w^{-1}(\{i\})]), 
 
 
 
 for each i , 1\leq i\leq\ell(\lambda) . This is due to the way we define \sigma according to the colexicographic order on (c,d)\in\mathcal{C}_{\pi,\lambda}^{(\alpha)} , the relative order of the values of \sigma and \tilde{\tau} are the same within the set of w^{-1}(\{i\}) ’s.
In \sigma[w^{-1}(\{i\})] , the inversion of \pi[w^{-1}(\{i\})] occurs when \tilde{\tau}_{l}<\tilde{\tau}_{m} and \sigma_{l}>\sigma_{m} 
for l<m and l\nless_{\pi}m . Since we are considering w^{-1}(\{i\}) , if l\nless_{\pi}m ,
the inequality \tilde{\tau}_{l}<\tilde{\tau}_{m} holds for sure, so we only need to count the number of pairs such that
 \sigma_{l}>\sigma_{m} for l<m and l\nless_{\pi}m . Hence, we can factor out the term \displaystyle q^{\operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(\tilde{w})}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}q^{\operatorname{inv}_{\pi[w^{-1}(\{i\})]}(\sigma[w^{-1}(\{i\})])} from the summation over \tau\in\binom{[\alpha+k]}{n} . 
 
 
 
 Now we take care of the rest of the factor 
 

 
 
 \sum_{\tau\in\binom{[\alpha+k]}{n}}q^{|\tilde{\tau|}}=\sum_{\tau\in\binom{[\alpha+k]}{n}}q^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\tau_{i}+i-1-\operatorname{asc}^{<i}_{\pi}(w,\sigma)\right)}=q^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(i-1-\operatorname{asc}_{\pi}^{<i}(w,\sigma)\right)}\sum_{\tau\in\binom{[\alpha+k]}{n}}q^{|\tau|}, 
 
 
 
 given (w,\sigma)\in M(\lambda)\times\mathfrak{S}_{n} with \operatorname{asc}_{\pi}(w,\sigma)=k .
For a given sequence \tau=(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{n}) , with 0\leq\tau_{i}\leq\alpha+k-n , \tau_{1}\leq\tau_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\tau_{n} , we can identify it with a lattice path contained in a rectangular shape of size n\times(\alpha+k-n) 
with the i -th column height \tau_{i} . Then it is not very hard to see that 
 

 
 
 \sum_{\tau\in\binom{[\alpha+k]}{n}}q^{|\tau|}=\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}. 
 
 
 
 Lastly, via a simple computation, we get
 \displaystyle q^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(i-1-\operatorname{asc}^{<i}_{\pi}(w,\sigma)\right)}=q^{\binom{n}{2}-nk+\operatorname{comaj}_{\pi}(w,\sigma)}, for a given bi-word (w,\sigma)\in W(\lambda)\times\mathfrak{S}_{n} with \operatorname{asc}_{\pi}(w,\sigma)=k .
Thus we obtain Theorem 3.1 .
∎ 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 4. Expansion into (\alpha)^{\underline{k}} basis 

 
 
 4.1. The XY technique 

 
 
 Given two sets of variables X and Y , let XY=\{x_{i}y_{j}:i,j\geq 1\} and \pi be an n -Dyck path. 
 
 
 Theorem 4.1 . 
 
 

 
 (4.1) 
 
 \displaystyle\chi_{\pi}[XY;q]=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}m_{\lambda}[X]\sum_{w\in M(\lambda)}q^{\operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(w)}\chi_{\beta(\pi,w)}[Y;q], 
 
 
 
 where the n -Dyck path \beta(\pi,w) corresponds to the graph G_{\beta} obtained by starting with G_{\pi} colored by w , and then
removing all edges which connect two vertices a,b with different colors
(i.e. vertices satisfying w_{a}\neq w_{b} ). 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 We use the lexicographic order on ordered pairs of positive integers (a,b) , that is: 
 

 
 (4.2) 
 
 \displaystyle\text{if $a<c$ then $(a,b)<(c,d)$ for all $b,d$ and also
$(b,a)<(b,c)$ for all $b$}. 
 
 
 
 By definition, 
 

 
 (4.3) 
 
 \displaystyle\chi_{\pi}[XY;q]=\sum_{C\in(\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2})^{n}\atop C\text{ proper }}q^{\operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(C)}\prod_{i\geq 1}x_{w_{i}}y_{z_{i}}, 
 
 
 
 where C_{i}=(w_{i},z_{i}) with both w and z in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{n} , and we use ( 4.2 ) to determine inequalities among the C_{i} . Now for any i<j where (i,j)\in G_{\pi} , if w_{i}\neq w_{j} , then
whether or not C_{i} and C_{j} contribute to \operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(C) is completely determined by the values of w_{i} and w_{j} . On the other hand, if
 w_{i}=w_{j} , then
whether or not C_{i} and C_{j} contribute to \operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(C) is completely determined by the values of z_{i} and z_{j} , and in this case we also must have
 z_{i}\neq z_{j} , otherwise the coloring C of G_{\pi} would not be proper. It follows that 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle\operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(C)=\operatorname{inv}_{\pi}(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n})+\operatorname{inv}_{\beta(\pi,w)}(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n}), 
 
 
 
 where the first term on the right counts the inversions amongst C_{i} and C_{j} which have unequal first coordinates, and
 \operatorname{inv}_{\beta(\pi,w)} counts the inversions amongst C_{i} and C_{j} which have equal first coordinates. Thus the contribution to \chi_{\pi}[XY;q] from all
colorings C whose first coordinate w has weight \lambda on the right-hand-side of ( 4.3 ) factors into a term of weight \lambda in the X variables, times a sum of terms in the Y variables, which are over proper colorings of G_{\beta(\pi,w)} . We leave it to the reader to show that G_{\beta(\pi,w)} is a unit interval order when
 G_{\pi} is. See Example 4.2 for an illustration of this construction for a given \pi and coloring C .
∎

 Given an n -Dyck path \pi , let

 Then if \alpha\in\mathbb{N} , C_{\pi,\lambda}(\alpha)\in\mathbb{N}[q] and furthermore has a combinatorial interpretation.

 4.2. Set partitions and the (\alpha)^{\underline{k}} expansion 

 
 
 A set partition of [n] is a collection of nonempty subsets of [n] such that each element in [n] is included in exactly one subset. Each subset in a set partition is called a part . The set of set partitions of [n] into k parts is denoted by \operatorname{S}(n,k) . 
 
 
 
 For an n -Dyck path \pi and a set partition S of [n] , we assign a Dyck path \beta(\pi,S) as follows. First, order the parts of S as S=\{S^{(1)},\dots,S^{(k)}\} and let w_{S} be the word obtained by replacing elements in S^{(i)} with i . Now define 
 

 
 
 \beta(\pi,S):=\beta(\pi,w_{S}). 
 
 
 
 Using this, we present the expansion of \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions into (\alpha)^{\underline{k}} basis. 
 
 
 Theorem 4.4 . 
 
 
 For an n -Dyck path \pi , the \alpha -chromatic symmetric function \chi^{(\alpha)}_{\pi}[X;1] at q=1 is expanded into falling factorial basis \left\{(\alpha)^{\underline{k}}\right\}_{1\leq k\leq n} as 
 

 
 (4.4) 
 
 \chi_{\pi}^{(\alpha)}[X;1]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\alpha)^{\underline{k}}\sum_{S\in\operatorname{S}(n,k)}\chi_{\beta(\pi,S)}[X;1]. 
 
 
 
 In particular, the \alpha -chromatic symmetric function \chi_{\pi}^{(\alpha)}[X;1] is positively expanded in terms of the basis \left\{(\alpha)^{\underline{k}}s_{\lambda}\right\}_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq n,\\
\lambda\vdash n\end{subarray}} . 
 
 
 
 Remark 4.5 . 
 
 
 Due to Hikita’s proof of Stanley-Stembridge conjecture [ Hik24 ] , Theorem 4.4 gives
 e -positivity of the \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions when q=1 .

 Next specialize q=1 to obtain

 which finishes the proof.

 Remark 4.7 . 
 
 
 Theorem 4.4 can be shown by using the rook theory. We provide a brief overview of the key ideas. Setting q=1 in equation ( 3.2 ), we obtain the expression 
 

 
 
 \chi_{\pi}^{(\alpha)}[X;1]=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in M(\lambda)\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}\prod_{j=1}^{\lambda_{i}}(\alpha-a_{j}(\pi[w^{-1}(\{i\})]))m_{\lambda}[X]. 
 
 
 
 
 
 By considering lexicographic order on the indices (i,j) in the product \prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}\prod_{j=1}^{\lambda_{i}}(\alpha-a_{j}(\pi[w^{-1}(\{i\})])) , this product can be interpreted as a rook product. Let B(\pi,\sigma) represent the corresponding board. There is a natural correspondence between rook placements and set partitions as follows. Given a k -rook placement on a board B , let f:[n]\rightarrow[n]\setminus\{1\}\cup\{*\} be a function defined by 
 

 
 
 f(i)=\begin{cases}j+1\text{ if there is no rook at $(i,j)$,}\\
*\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases} 
 
 
 
 Then the parts of the corresponding set partition are given by orbits of f minus * . Through this correspondence between rook placements and set partitions, it becomes evident that the union of sets of (n-k) -rooks on the board B(\pi,\sigma) , for varying words \sigma , is in bijection with the union of proper colorings of \beta(\pi,S) over all set partitions S\in S(n,k) . With this in mind, by applying the identity in Thereom 2.8 we have: 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle\chi_{\pi}^{(\alpha)}[X;1] 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in M(\lambda)\end{subarray}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\alpha)^{\underline{k}}r_{n-k}(B(\pi,\sigma))m_{\lambda}[X] 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{S\in S(n,k)}(\alpha)^{\underline{k}}\chi_{\beta(\pi,S)}[X;1]. 
 
 
 
 Alternatively, Theorem 4.4 can be proven by using bijective arguments on p -coefficients of \alpha -chromatic symmetric function, or by employing the modular law for chromatic symmetric functions. In Appendix A , we outline a proof via the modular law. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 5. Further results on Schur expansion 

 
 
 5.1. Schur positivity 

 
 
 In this section we prove the Schur positivity of the \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions in terms of the basis \left\{\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}\right\}_{0\leq k\leq n-1} . Notably, we establish a general result for Schur positivity of s_{\lambda}[Q_{\alpha}X] . 
 
 
 Proposition 5.1 . 
 
 
 For a partition \lambda , s_{\lambda}\left[Q_{\alpha}X\right] is Schur positive in terms of the basis \left\{\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}\right\}_{0\leq k\leq n-1} . 
 
 
 
 
 As a Corollary, the Schur positivity of the \alpha -chromatic symmetric function \chi^{(\alpha)}_{\pi}[X;q] in terms of \left\{\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}\right\}_{0\leq k\leq n-1} follows from the Schur positivity of the usual chromatic symmetric function [ Gas96 , SW16 ] . 
 
 
 Corollary 5.2 . 
 
 
 If a symmetric function f is Schur positive, then f\left[Q_{\alpha}X\right] also exhibits Schur positivity in the basis \left\{\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}\right\}_{0\leq k\leq n-1} . In particular, the \alpha -chromatic symmetric function \chi^{(\alpha)}_{\pi}[X;q] of an n -Dyck path \pi is Schur positive in terms of aforementioned basis. 
 
 
 
 Proof of Lemma 5.1 . 
 
 
 Let us recall the following identity [ Mac15 , Eq (7.9)] : for a partition \lambda\vdash n , 
 

 
 (5.1) 
 
 s_{\lambda}[XY]=\sum_{\mu,\nu\vdash n}g^{\lambda}_{\mu,\nu}s_{\mu}[X]s_{\nu}[Y], 
 
 
 
 where g^{\lambda}_{\mu,\nu} is the Kronecker coefficient . Setting X=Q_{\alpha},Y=X in ( 5.1 ), we obtain 
 

 
 
 s_{\lambda}\left[Q_{\alpha}X\right]=\sum_{\mu,\nu\vdash n}g^{\lambda}_{\mu,\nu}s_{\mu}\left[Q_{\alpha}\right]s_{\nu}[X]. 
 
 
 
 Since the Kronecker coefficients g^{\lambda}_{\mu,\nu} are nonnegative, it is enough to show the positivity of s_{\mu}\left[Q_{\alpha}\right] in terms of the basis \left\{\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}\right\}_{0\leq k\leq n-1} . 
 
 
 
 The principal specialization of Schur functions has the following combinatorial formula [ Sta97 , Proposition 7.19.12] : for a partition \lambda\vdash n , 
 

 
 (5.2) 
 
 s_{\lambda}\left[Q_{\alpha}\right]=\sum_{T\in\operatorname{SYT}(\lambda)}q^{\operatorname{maj}(T)}{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+n-1-d(T)\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}, 
 
 
 
 where d(T) is the number of i such that i+1 appears below i in T , i.e. the descents of the standard tableau T . This establishes positivity of s_{\lambda}\left[Q_{\alpha}\right] in terms of the basis \left\{\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}\right\}_{0\leq k\leq n-1} . 
 
 
 
 ∎ 
 
 
 
 
 It is noteworthy that since \begin{bmatrix}\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q} can be positively expanded in the basis \left\{\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}}\right\}_{1\leq k\leq n} for 0\leq k\leq n-1 , Corollary 5.2 also implies the Schur positivity in terms of the basis
 \left\{\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}}\right\}_{1\leq k\leq n} . However,
 s_{\lambda}\left[Q_{\alpha}X\right] might not be integrally Schur positive in the q -falling factorial basis [\alpha]^{\underline{k}}_{q} . For example, s_{(2)}\left[Q_{\alpha}X\right] at q=1 is 
 

 
 
 \left(\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha\right)s_{(2)}+\left(\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\alpha\right)s_{(1,1)}=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha)^{\underline{2}}s_{(2)}+\left(\frac{1}{2}(\alpha)^{\underline{2}}+(\alpha)^{\underline{1}}\right)s_{(1,1)}, 
 
 
 
 in which the Schur coefficients are not integral in terms of the falling factorial basis (even for q=1 ).
Nevertheless, we conjecture that this stronger Schur positivity in terms of the q -falling factorial basis holds for the \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions. 
 
 
 Conjecture 5.3 . 
 
 
 For an n -Dyck path \pi , the \alpha -chromatic symmetric function \chi_{\pi}^{(\alpha)}[X;q] has a positive integral expansion in terms of
 \left\{[\alpha]^{\underline{k}}_{q}s_{\lambda}\right\}_{1\leq k\leq n,\lambda\vdash n}. 
Moreover, the Schur expansion of \chi^{(\alpha)}_{\pi}[X;q] is of the form 
 

 
 (5.3) 
 
 \chi^{(\alpha)}_{\pi}[X;q]=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\left(\sum_{T\in\operatorname{SYT}(\lambda^{\prime})}q^{\text{stat}(T)}\text{PR}(B(T))\right)s_{\lambda}[X] 
 
 
 
 for some q -power \text{stat}(T) depending on T\in\operatorname{SYT}(\lambda^{\prime}) , where
 \text{PR}(B(T)) is the rook product
 \text{PR}(B(T))=\prod_{i=1}^{n}[\alpha+c_{i}-i+1] with the i th column height given by c_{i} for some board B(T) . 
 
 
 
 Remark 5.4 . 
 
 
 Recall the q -analogue of the Goldman-Joichi-White identity 
 

 
 
 \prod_{i=1}^{n}[\alpha+c_{i}-i+1]_{q}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}h_{k}(B;q)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}[\alpha]_{q}^{\underline{k}}r_{n-k}(B;q) 
 
 
 
 where B is the board associated to the sequence c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n} . By this formula,
the Schur expansion in Conjecture 5.3 would also impliy
the integral positivity in terms of both bases \left\{\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}\right\}_{0\leq k\leq n-1} and \left\{[\alpha]^{\underline{k}}_{q}\right\}_{1\leq k\leq n} .
Furthermore, if we let \alpha approaches infinity in the definition 
 

 
 
 \chi_{\pi}^{(\alpha)}[X;q]=\frac{\operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[(q^{\alpha}-1)X;q]}{(q-1)^{n}}, 
 
 
 
 then we get 
 

 
 
 \operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[X;q]=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\sum_{T\in\operatorname{SYT}(\lambda)}q^{\text{stat}(T)}s_{\lambda}[X]. 
 
 
 
 Thus, figuring out the q -statistic in ( 5.3 ) will automatically give
the Schur expansion of the unicellular LLT polynomials. 
 
 
 
 
 As an evidence of Conjecture 5.3 , we have the following Schur expansion of \chi_{\pi}[X;q] 
when \pi=(n,n,\dots,n) corresponds to the complete graph. 
 
 
 Proposition 5.5 . 
 
 

 
 (5.4) 
 
 \chi_{(n,n,\dots,n)}^{\alpha}[X;q]=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\left(\sum_{T\in\operatorname{SYT}(\lambda)}q^{\text{ch}(T)}\prod_{u\in\lambda}[\alpha-c(u)]_{q}\right)s_{\lambda}[X], 
 
 
 
 where \text{ch}(T) is the charge statistic of T . 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 Note that \operatorname{LLT}_{(n^{n})}[X;q]=H_{(n)}(X;q,t)=J_{(n)}\left[\frac{X}{1-t};q,t\right] , where H_{\lambda}(X;q,t) is the modified Macdonald polynomials and J_{\lambda}(X;q,t) is the integral form Macdonald polynomials (see [ Hag08 ] for the details). 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle\operatorname{LLT}_{(n^{n})}[(t-1)X;q] 
 \displaystyle=H_{(n)}[X(t-1);q,t] 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=J_{(n)}\left[\frac{X(t-1)}{1-t};q,t\right]=J_{(n)}[-X;q,t] 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\prod_{u\in\lambda}(1-q^{a^{\prime}(u)-l^{\prime}(u)}t)\left(\sum_{T\in\operatorname{SYT}(\lambda^{\prime})}q^{\text{ch}(T)}\right)s_{\lambda}[-X] 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\prod_{u\in\lambda}(q^{l^{\prime}(u)-a^{\prime}(u)}t-1)\left(\sum_{T\in\operatorname{SYT}(\lambda)}q^{\text{ch}(T)}\right)s_{\lambda}[X]. 
 
 
 
 So we have, 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle\chi_{(n^{n})}^{\alpha}(X;q) 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\prod_{u\in\lambda}\frac{(q^{\alpha-(a^{\prime}(u)-l^{\prime}(u))}t-1)}{(q-1)^{n}}\left(\sum_{T\in\operatorname{SYT}(\lambda)}q^{\text{ch}(T)}\right)s_{\lambda}[X] 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\prod_{u\in\lambda}[\alpha-(a^{\prime}(u)-l^{\prime}(u))]_{q}\left(\sum_{T\in\operatorname{SYT}(\lambda)}q^{\text{ch}(T)}\right)s_{\lambda}[X]. 
 
 
 
 ∎ 
 
 
 
 
 
 5.2. Symmetry for \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions 

 
 
 We present a symmetry relation for the Schur expansion of the \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions. 
 
 
 Proposition 5.6 . 
 
 
 For an n -Dyck path \pi , if we let 
 

 
 
 \chi^{(\alpha)}_{\pi}[X;q]=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}c_{\pi,\lambda,k}(q)\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}s_{\lambda}[X], 
 
 
 
 then 
 

 
 
 c_{\pi,\lambda,k}(q)=q^{\operatorname{area}(\pi)+\binom{n}{2}}c_{\pi,\lambda^{\prime},n-1-k}(q^{-1}). 
 
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 [ BHM+23 , Proposition 4.1.4] states that for an n -Dyck path \pi , 
 

 
 
 \omega(\operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[X;q])=q^{\operatorname{area}(\pi)}\operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[X;q^{-1}]. 
 
 
 
 Taking the plethystic substitution X\mapsto(q^{\alpha}-1)X , we get 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle\omega\operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[(q^{\alpha}-1)X;q]=q^{\operatorname{area}(\pi)}\operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[((q^{-1})^{(-\alpha)}-1)X;q^{-1}]. 
 
 
 
 By applying Proposition 2.2 and dividing both sides by (q-1)^{n} , we have 
 

 
 
 \displaystyle\omega\chi_{\pi}^{(\alpha)}[X;q]=\frac{q^{\operatorname{area}(\pi)}\operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[((q^{-1})^{(-\alpha)}-1)X;q^{-1}]}{(-q)^{n}(q^{-1}-1)^{n}} 
 \displaystyle=(-1)^{n}q^{\operatorname{area}(\pi)-n}\chi_{\pi}^{(-\alpha)}[X;q^{-1}]. 
 
 
 
 In the right-hand side of the last equation, applying 
 

 
 
 \footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}-\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}=(-1)^{n}q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+n-1-k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}, 
 
 
 
 yields the conclusion. 
 
 
 
 ∎

 7. Final remarks 

 
 
 7.1. Schur expansion of unicellular LLT polynomials 

 
 Proposition 7.1 . 
 
 
 For an n -Dyck path \pi , let c^{k}_{\pi,\lambda}(q) and d^{k}_{\pi,\lambda}(q) be the Schur coefficients of the \alpha -chromatic symmetric function into the bases \left\{\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}\right\}_{0\leq k\leq n-1} and \left\{[\alpha]_{q}^{\underline{k}}\right\}_{1\leq k\leq n} , respectively, i.e. 
 

 
 (7.1) 
 
 \chi_{\pi}^{(\alpha)}[X;q]=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c^{k}_{\pi,\lambda}(q)\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}s_{\lambda}[X]=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}d^{k}_{\pi,\lambda}(q)[\alpha]_{q}^{\underline{k}}s_{\lambda}[X]. 
 
 
 
 Then we have 
 

 
 (7.2) 
 
 \operatorname{LLT}_{\pi}[X;q]=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\dfrac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c^{k}_{\pi,\lambda}(q)}{[n]_{q}!}s_{\lambda}[X]=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}q^{-\binom{n}{2}}d^{n}_{\pi,\lambda}(q)s_{\lambda}[X]. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 By dividing both sides of ( 7.1 ) by (q-1)^{n} , and by taking \alpha\rightarrow\infty , we get ( 7.2 ).
∎ 
 
 
 
 
 As one can see, the Schur coefficients of the \alpha -chromatic symmetric function expanded in the basis \left\{\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+k\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{q}}\right\}_{0\leq k\leq n-1} yield the Schur coefficients for the LLT polynomials, multiplied by [n]_{q}! . Theorem 3.1 can be viewed as a step in this direction to find a combinatorial interpretation of Schur coefficients of the LLT polynomials. 
 
 
 
 On the other hand, when \alpha=1 , we have [\alpha]^{\underline{k}}_{q}=0 for all k except when k=1 , and the \alpha -chromatic symmetric function reduces to the usual chromatic symmetric function. Consequently, in the expansion of the \alpha -chromatic symmetric function \chi^{(\alpha)}_{\pi}[X;q] in the \left\{[\alpha]_{q}^{\underline{k}}\right\} basis, the coefficient of [\alpha]_{q}^{\underline{1}} corresponds to the chromatic symmetric function \chi_{\pi}[X;q] . Building on this observation, Proposition 7.1 suggests that the coefficients of the \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions, when expressed in the q -falling factorial basis, interpolate between the chromatic symmetric functions and the unicellular LLT polynomials. 
 
 
 
 
 7.2. Geometric interpretations 

 
 
 For an n -Dyck path \pi , let \operatorname{Hess}(\pi) be the (regular semisimple) Hessenberg variety associated to \pi (or the Hessenberg function h(\pi) ). Tymoczko’s dot action [ Tym08 ] on torus equivariant cohomology induces an \mathfrak{S}_{n} action on the cohomology H^{*}(\operatorname{Hess}(\pi)) . Brosnan–Chow [ BC18 ] , and Guay-Paquet [ GP13 ] independently proved the conjecture of Shareshian–Wachs [ SW16 ] that this representation coincides with the chromatic symmetric function: 
 

 
 
 \omega\chi_{\pi}[X;q]=\operatorname{Frob}(H^{*}(\operatorname{Hess}(\pi));q), 
 
 
 
 where \operatorname{Frob} is the (graded) Frobenius characteristic map. 
 
 
 
 Since the \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions enjoy various Schur positivity phenomena and symmetry relations, it is tempting to seek a geometric model behind it. For fixed \alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0} , it is quite direct to obtain the following geometric interpretation for the \alpha -chromatic symmetric function. 
 
 
 Proposition 7.2 . 
 
 
 Let \pi be an n -Dyck path and \alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0} be a fixed positive integer. Let \mathbb{P}^{\alpha-1} be the complex projective space of dimension \alpha-1 . Then we have 
 

 
 
 \omega\chi^{(\alpha)}_{\pi}[X;q]=\operatorname{Frob}(H^{*}(\operatorname{Hess}(\pi));q)\otimes\operatorname{Frob}\left((H^{*}(\mathbb{P}^{\alpha-1})^{n});q\right), 
 
 
 
 where \otimes is the Kronecker product for symmetric functions. Here, we consider an \mathfrak{S}_{n} action on H^{*}(\operatorname{Hess}(\pi)) given by Tymockzo’s dot action and on H^{*}((\mathbb{P}^{\alpha-1})^{n}) induced from the \mathfrak{S}_{n} action on (\mathbb{P}^{\alpha-1})^{n} given by permuting coordinates. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 
 Each factor \mathbb{P}^{\alpha-1} has cohomology ring 
 

 
 
 H^{*}(\mathbb{P}^{\alpha-1})\cong\mathbb{Z}[x]/\langle x^{\alpha}\rangle. 
 
 
 
 Since the cohomology of a product is given by the tensor product of the cohomologies of the factors, we have 
 

 
 
 H^{*}\left((\mathbb{P}^{\alpha-1})^{n}\right)\cong\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/\langle x_{1}^{\alpha},\dots,x_{n}^{\alpha}\rangle, 
 
 
 
 where x_{i} denotes the pullback of the first Chern class of the hyperplane bundle from the i -th factor. The symmetric group \mathfrak{S}_{n} acts on (\mathbb{P}^{\alpha-1})^{n} by permuting the factors, which induces an action on the cohomology ring by permuting the variables x_{1},\dots,x_{n} . In particular, each graded piece
 H^{2k}\left((\mathbb{P}^{\alpha-1})^{n}\right) 
inherits an \mathfrak{S}_{n} -representation structure, with basis 
 

 
 
 \{x_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{k_{n}}:k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}=k,\ 0\leq k_{i}<\alpha\}. 
 
 
 
 It follows that the Frobenius characteristic of the cohomology ring of the product of projective spaces is given by 
 

 
 
 \operatorname{Frob}\left(H^{*}((\mathbb{P}^{\alpha-1})^{n});q\right)=\sum_{\lambda}\sum_{\beta\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\cdot\lambda}q^{\operatorname{wt}(\beta)}h_{\lambda}[X], 
 
 
 
 where the sum is over partitions \lambda with all parts less than \alpha , and \operatorname{wt}(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(i-1)\beta_{i} . 
 
 
 
 By the Cauchy identity, we have 
 

 
 
 h_{n}[Q_{\alpha}X]=\sum_{\lambda}m_{\lambda}[Q_{\alpha}]h_{\lambda}[X]=\sum_{\lambda}\sum_{\beta\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\cdot\lambda}q^{\operatorname{wt}(\beta)}h_{\lambda}[X], 
 
 
 
 and hence 
 

 
 
 \operatorname{Frob}\left(H^{*}((\mathbb{P}^{\alpha-1})^{n});q\right)=h_{n}[Q_{\alpha}X]. 
 
 
 
 Furthermore, for any symmetric function f , the Kronecker product (corresponding to the inner tensor product) of f with h_{n}[Q_{\alpha}X] simplifies as 
 

 
 
 f[X]\otimes h_{n}[Q_{\alpha}X]=f[Q_{\alpha}X]. 
 
 
 
 Therefore, we obtain 
 

 
 
 \operatorname{Frob}\left(H^{*}(\operatorname{Hess}(\pi));q\right)\otimes\operatorname{Frob}\left(H^{*}((\mathbb{P}^{\alpha-1})^{n});q\right)=h_{n}[Q_{\alpha}X]\otimes\omega\chi_{\pi}[X;q]=\omega\chi_{\pi}[Q_{\alpha}X;q]=\omega\chi_{\pi}^{(\alpha)}[X;q], 
 
 
 
 which completes the proof.
∎ 
 
 
 
 
 If we let \alpha be general (as a parameter), it is unclear how to interpret the \alpha -chromatic symmetric functions in terms of geometry. This approach might lead us to the desired Schur positivity in Conjecture 5.3 .

 Appendix A Another proof of Theorem 4.4 using the modular law 

 
 
 A.1. Proof using modular law 

 
 
 The modular law is a linear relation among functions on Dyck paths. 
 
 
 Definition A.1 . 
 
 
 Let \mathcal{A} be a \mathbb{Q} -algebra.
We say a function f:\operatorname{Dyck}_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{A} satisfies the modular law if 
 

 
 
 2f(\pi_{1})=f(\pi_{0})+f(\pi_{2}) 
 
 
 
 when either one of the following conditions hold. 
 
 • 
 
 
 There exists i\in[n-1] such that \pi_{1}(i-1)<\pi_{1}(i)<\pi_{1}(i+1) and \pi_{1}(\pi_{1}(i))=\pi_{1}(\pi_{1}(i)+1) or \pi_{1}(i)=n . Moreover, \pi_{0} and \pi_{2} satisfy \pi_{k}(j):=\pi_{1}(j) for every j\neq i and k\in\{0,2\} , while \pi_{k}(i)=\pi_{i}(i)-1+k . 
 
 • 
 
 
 There exists i\in[n-1] such that \pi_{1}(i+1)=\pi_{1}(i)+1 and \pi_{1}^{-1}(i)=\emptyset . Moreover, \pi_{0} and \pi_{2} satisfy \pi_{k}(j)=\pi_{1}(j) for all j\neq i,i+1 and for k=0,2 , while \pi_{0}(i)=\pi_{0}(i+1)=\pi_{1}(i) and \pi_{2}(i)=\pi_{2}(i+1)=\pi_{1}(i+1) . 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 Originally, this relation was discovered by Guay-Paquet [ GP13 ] for chromatic symmetric functions for (3+1) -free graphs, but we restrict our attention to unit interval graphs. An analogous relation for the unicellular LLT polynomials was found by Lee [ Lee21 ] . Using the modular law, Abreu and Nigro [ AN21 ] characterize the chromatic symmetric function as follows. 
 
 
 Theorem A.2 . 
 
 
 Let \mathcal{A} be a \mathbb{Q} -algebra and let f:\operatorname{Dyck}_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{A} be a function that satisfies the modular law. Then f is determined by its values f(K_{n_{1}}\cup K_{n_{2}}\cup\cdots\cup K_{n_{m}}) at the disjoint ordered union of complete graphs and these values are independent of the order in which the union is taken. 
 
 
 
 
 We now give an inductive proof of Theorem 4.4 using this characterization. 
 
 
 Proof of Theorem 4.4 . 
 
 
 Recall the expansion ( 4.4 ) 
 

 
 
 \chi_{\pi}^{(\alpha)}[X;1]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\alpha)^{\underline{k}}\sum_{S\in\operatorname{S}(n,k)}\chi_{\beta(\pi,S)}[X;1]. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Since both sides are symmetric under taking transposes of Dyck paths, it suffices to verify that both satisfy the modular law when the triple (\pi_{0},\pi_{1},\pi_{2}) is of the first type in Definition A.1 . 
 
 
 
 The left-hand side of ( 4.4 ) clearly satisfies the modular law. Now, we proceed to demonstrate that the right-hand side of ( 4.4 ) also follows the modular law. Consider three Dyck paths \pi_{0} , \pi_{1} , and \pi_{2} as described in Definition A.1 , where the only difference is at the i -th column, and \pi_{0}(i)+1=\pi_{1}(i)=\pi_{2}(i)-1=j .
We can categorize possible cases as follows: 
 
 
 Case 1 : 
 
 
 When both j and j+1 are in the same part with i in the set partition S . 
 
 
 
 Case 2 : 
 
 
 When both j and j+1 are not in the same part with i in the set partition S . 
 
 
 
 Case 3 : 
 
 
 When one of j or j+1 is in the same part with i in the set partition S , and the other is not. 
 
 
 
 
 In the first case, it is evident that the new Dyck paths \beta(\pi_{0},S) , \beta(\pi_{1},S) , and \beta(\pi_{2},S) satisfy the conditions in Definition A.1 . Therefore, by applying the modular law, we obtain 
 

 
 
 \chi_{\beta(\pi_{2},S)}-\chi_{\beta(\pi_{1},S)}=\chi_{\beta(\pi_{1},S)}-\chi_{\beta(\pi_{0},S)}. 
 
 
 
 In the second case, all three Dyck paths \beta(\pi_{0},S) , \beta(\pi_{1},S) , and \beta(\pi_{2},S) are identical. This implies \chi_{\beta(\pi_{0},S)}=\chi_{\beta(\pi_{1},S)}=\chi_{\beta(\pi_{2},S)} , resulting in 
 

 
 
 \chi_{\beta(\pi_{2},S)}-\chi_{\beta(\pi_{1},S)}=\chi_{\beta(\pi_{1},S)}-\chi_{\beta(\pi_{0},S)}=0. 
 
 
 
 For the last case, without loss of generality, let us assume that i and j are in the same part of set partition S , and j+1 is not. Now, let S^{\prime} be the set partition obtained by exchanging j and j+1 in S . Then, we have \beta(\pi_{1},S)=\beta(\pi_{2},S) and \beta(\pi_{0},S^{\prime})=\beta(\pi_{1},S^{\prime}) . Moreover, since \pi_{k}(j)=\pi_{k}(j+1) for k=0,1,2 , it follows that 
 

 
 
 \beta(\pi_{0},S)=\beta(\pi_{1},S^{\prime})\quad\text{ and }\quad\beta(\pi_{1},S)=\beta(\pi_{2},S^{\prime}). 
 
 
 
 This leads to the equation 
 

 
 
 \chi_{\beta(\pi_{2},S)}-\chi_{\beta(\pi_{1},S)}+\chi_{\beta(\pi_{2},S^{\prime})}-\chi_{\beta(\pi_{1},S^{\prime})}=\chi_{\beta(\pi_{1},S)}-\chi_{\beta(\pi_{0},S)}+\chi_{\beta(\pi_{1},S^{\prime})}-\chi_{\beta(\pi_{0},S^{\prime})}. 
 
 
 
 Considering all the possible cases, we can conclude that the right-hand side of ( 4.4 ) satisfies the modular law. 
 
 
 
 Let us establish the initial case: disjoint ordered union of complete graphs. For a composition \lambda , let P(\lambda) be the path [\lambda_{1}^{\lambda_{1}},\dots,\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)}^{\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)}}] , i.e. bounce path of \lambda . The path P(\lambda) corresponds to the disjoint union of complete graphs. We first confirm the validity of ( 4.4 ) for the ‘full’ Dyck path \pi=P(n)=[n,n,\dots,n] . Note that \chi_{\pi}[X;q]=[n]_{q}!e_{n}[X] . This leads us to the following expression 
 

 
 
 \chi^{(\alpha)}_{\pi}[X;q]=\chi_{\pi}\left[\frac{1-q^{\alpha}}{1-q}X;q\right]=[n]_{q}!e_{n}\left[\frac{1-q^{\alpha}}{1-q}X;q\right]=[n]_{q}!\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}m_{\lambda}\left[\frac{1-q^{\alpha}}{1-q}\right]e_{\lambda}[X], 
 
 
 
 For the last equality, we used 
 

 
 
 e_{n}[XY]=\sum_{\lambda}b_{\lambda}[X]\omega(b^{\prime}_{\lambda}[Y]), 
 
 
 
 for some basis b_{\lambda} and its dual basis b^{\prime}_{\lambda} for \Lambda_{F} . See [ Mac15 , Sec I.4] for example. By setting q=1 , we derive the following equation 
 

 
 (A.1) 
 
 \chi^{(\alpha)}_{\pi}[X;1]=n!\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
\ell(\lambda)\end{pmatrix}\binom{\ell(\lambda)}{m_{1}(\lambda),m_{2}(\lambda),\dots}e_{\lambda}[X]. 
 
 
 
 Moreover, the chromatic symmetric function of the bounce path P(\lambda) is \chi_{P(\lambda)}[X;1]=\lambda!e_{\lambda} . Here, we used the notation \lambda!=\lambda_{1}!\lambda_{2}!\cdots . For each set partition S of n , the associated path \beta(\pi,S) is the bounce path 
 

 
 
 \beta(\pi,S)=P(\lambda(S)), 
 
 
 
 where \lambda(S) is the composition (|S^{(1)}|,\dots,|S^{\ell(S)}|) . Therefore, the right-hand side of ( 4.4 ) can be expressed as 
 

 
 
 \sum_{k=1}^{n}(\alpha)^{\underline{k}}\sum_{S\in\operatorname{S}(n,k)}X_{\beta(\pi,S)}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\alpha)^{\underline{k}}\sum_{S\in\operatorname{S}(n,k)}\lambda(S)!e_{\lambda(S)}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}(\alpha)^{\underline{\ell(\lambda)}}\begin{pmatrix}n\\
\lambda\end{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\prod_{i\geq 1}m_{i}(\lambda)!}\lambda!e_{\lambda}, 
 
 
 
 This expression aligns with ( A.1 ). 
 
 
 
 Our next task is to prove that ( 4.4 ) holds for \pi=\pi(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell}) for a composition (n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell}) . Given that \chi^{(\alpha)}_{\pi}[X;1]=\chi^{(\alpha)}_{K_{n_{1}}}[X;1]\cdots\chi^{(\alpha)}_{K_{n_{\ell}}}[X;1] , we arrive at the following expression 
 

 
 (A.2) 
 
 \displaystyle\chi^{(\alpha)}_{\pi}[X;1] 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{1\leq k_{1}\leq n_{1},\cdots,1\leq k_{\ell}\leq n_{\ell}}\sum_{S_{i}\in\operatorname{S}(n_{i},k_{i})}(\alpha)^{\underline{k_{1}}}\cdots(\alpha)^{\underline{k_{\ell}}}\chi_{P(\lambda(S_{1},\dots,S_{\ell}))}[X;1] 
 
 
 (A.3) 
 
 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\sum_{S_{1},\dots,S_{\ell}}(\alpha)^{\underline{\ell(S_{1})}}\cdots(\alpha)^{\underline{\ell(S_{\ell})}}\chi_{P(\lambda)}. 
 
 
 
 In the right-hand side of ( A.3 ), the second sum is over set partitions S_{1},\dots,S_{\ell} of n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell} that result in \lambda(S_{1},\dots,S_{\ell})=\lambda . To further explain, the product of falling factorials is expanded in terms of a falling factorial basis as follows [ Sta97 , Ch 2.4] 
 

 
 (A.4) 
 
 (\alpha)^{\underline{k_{1}}}\cdots(\alpha)^{\underline{k_{\ell}}}=\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k,k_{1},\dots,k_{\ell}}(\alpha)^{\underline{k}}, 
 
 
 
 where c_{k,k_{1},\dots,k_{\ell}} denotes the number of set partitions of k_{1}+\cdots+k_{\ell} into k parts, such that the numbers 1,2,\dots,k_{1} are in distinct partitions, k_{1}+1,k_{1}+2,\dots,k_{1}+k_{2} are in distinct partitions, and so forth. Employing ( A.4 ) in ( A.3 ) completes the proof.
∎
