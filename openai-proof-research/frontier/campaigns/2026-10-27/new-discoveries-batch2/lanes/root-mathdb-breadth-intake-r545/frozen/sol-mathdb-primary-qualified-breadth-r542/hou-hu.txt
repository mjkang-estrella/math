Abstract
    

We study non-trivial intersection problems for multi-part hypergraphs, excluding the usual extremal examples determined by fixed vertices or fixed coordinates. Our first result determines the exact value of the non-trivial tt-intersection problem in the symmetric product [n]r[n]^{r} for 1≤t≤r−21\leq t\leq r-2 and all n≥2n\geq 2. Frankl and Nie proved a two-candidate formula for sufficiently large nn and conjectured it for all n≥2n\geq 2; our formula shows that the conjectured expression must be enlarged, in small ranges of nn, by additional Ahlswede–Khachatrian ball-type terms.
    

Our second result concerns intersecting families in general products X1×⋯×XrX_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{r}, where |Xi|=ni|X_{i}|=n_{i}, with no common vertex. Let m0​(1,n1,…,nr)m_{0}(1,n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}) denote the largest size of such a family. We show that this number is equal to the maximum of ∑X∈𝒟∏i∈X(ni−1)\sum_{X\in\mathcal{D}}\prod_{i\in X}(n_{i}-1) over all downsets 𝒟⊆2[r]\mathcal{D}\subseteq 2^{[r]} such that ⋃X∈𝒟X=[r]\bigcup_{X\in\mathcal{D}}X=[r] and no two members of 𝒟\mathcal{D} have union [r][r]. This finite reduction separates the intersection obstruction from the part sizes and yields explicit fully asymmetric formulas for r=4,5,6r=4,5,6.
  




1 Introduction




Throughout [r]={1,…,r}[r]=\{1,\ldots,r\}. Let r≥2r\geq 2 and let X1,…,XrX_{1},\ldots,X_{r} be finite sets. An rr-partite rr-graph with parts X1,…,XrX_{1},\ldots,X_{r} will be identified with a family




ℱ⊆X1×⋯×Xr={(x1,…,xr):xi∈Xi, 1≤i≤r}.\mathcal{F}\subseteq X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{r}=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}):x_{i}\in X_{i},\ 1\leq i\leq r\}.



The fixed-coordinate set of ℱ\mathcal{F} is defined as




⋂ℱ:={i∈[r]:all sequences in ​ℱ​ have the same entry in the ​i​-th coordinate}.\bigcap\mathcal{F}:=\{i\in[r]:\text{all sequences in }\mathcal{F}\text{ have the same entry in the }i\text{-th coordinate}\}.



For two vectors 𝐱=(x1,…,xr)\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}) and 𝐲=(y1,…,yr)\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}) in the same product, let




agr⁡(𝐱,𝐲):={i∈[r]:xi=yi}.\operatorname{agr}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\{i\in[r]:x_{i}=y_{i}\}.



Thus 𝐱\mathbf{x} and 𝐲\mathbf{y} are disjoint as hypergraph edges if and only if agr⁡(𝐱,𝐲)=∅\operatorname{agr}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\emptyset. A family ℱ⊆[n]r\mathcal{F}\subseteq[n]^{r} is tt-intersecting if |agr⁡(𝐱,𝐲)|≥t|\operatorname{agr}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})|\geq t for all 𝐱,𝐲∈ℱ\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{F}; when t=1t=1 we simply say intersecting. A tt-intersecting family ℱ\mathcal{F} is non-trivial if |⋂ℱ|<t|\bigcap\mathcal{F}|<t.




1.1 Non-trivial intersection in rr-partite rr-graphs




The classical Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem [4] states that if ℱ⊆([n]r)\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{r} is intersecting and n≥2​rn\geq 2r, then |ℱ|≤(n−1r−1)|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n-1}{r-1}. The extremal families are the trivial stars, consisting of all rr-sets containing a fixed vertex. This naturally leads to the corresponding non-trivial problem, in which such stars are excluded. Hilton and Milner [10] resolved this question by showing that any non-trivial intersecting family ℱ⊆([n]r)\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{r} with n>2​rn>2r satisfies




|ℱ|≤(n−1r−1)−(n−r−1r−1)+1.|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n-1}{r-1}-\binom{n-r-1}{r-1}+1.



The bound is sharp: The extremal family is called the Hilton–Milner
family




ℋx,B(n)={B}∪{F∈([n]r):x∈F,F∩B≠∅}, where x∈[n]andB∈([n]∖{x}r).\mathcal{H}_{x,B}(n)=\{B\}\cup\left\{F\in\binom{[n]}{r}:x\in F,\ F\cap B\neq\emptyset\right\},~\text{ where }~x\in[n]~\text{and}~B\in\binom{[n]\setminus\{x\}}{r}.






The Hilton–Milner theorem, whose short proof was later given by Frankl and Füredi [8], initiated a line of work in which the common core of an intersecting family is controlled or excluded. For t>1t>1, the corresponding non-trivial problem asks for the largest tt-intersecting family.
Frankl [6] determined this maximum for sufficiently large nn. The extremal family is




𝒜1​(n,r,t)={F∈([n]r):|F∩[t+2]|≥t+1}\mathcal{A}_{1}(n,r,t)=\left\{F\in\binom{[n]}{r}:|F\cap[t+2]|\geq t+1\right\}



when t+1≤r≤2​t+1t+1\leq r\leq 2t+1, while for r>2​t+1r>2t+1 it is the
Hilton–Milner type family




ℋr,t(n)={[r+1]∖{i}:i∈[r]}∪{F∈([n]r):[t]⊆F,F∩[t+1,r+1]≠∅},\mathcal{H}_{r,t}(n)=\left\{[r+1]\setminus\{i\}:i\in[r]\right\}\cup\left\{F\in\binom{[n]}{r}:[t]\subseteq F,\,F\cap[t+1,r+1]\neq\emptyset\right\},



where [t+1,r+1]={t+1,t+2,…,r+1}[t+1,r+1]=\{t+1,t+2,\dots,r+1\}. Ahlswede and Khachatrian [1] then proved the complete non-trivial intersection theorem, determining the maximum for all relevant n,r,tn,r,t.
Their theorem shows that the extremal value is governed by the complete intersection theorem and hence by the Ahlswede–Khachatrian families




𝒜i​(n,r,t)={F∈([n]r):|F∩[t+2​i]|≥t+i},1≤i≤⌊r−t2⌋.\mathcal{A}_{i}(n,r,t)=\left\{F\in\binom{[n]}{r}:|F\cap[t+2i]|\geq t+i\right\},~1\leq i\leq\left\lfloor\frac{r-t}{2}\right\rfloor.



Thus the extremal picture is not merely the Hilton–Milner construction with tt fixed coordinates; depending on the parameter range, ball-type Ahlswede–Khachatrian families also appear.




Turning to multi-part versions, Kwan, Sudakov, and Vieira [12] studied non-trivially intersecting multi-part families, where the ground set is partitioned into parts and each member has a prescribed intersection size with each part. The extremal examples are multi-part Hilton–Milner-type families, constructed by choosing a distinguished part together with possible auxiliary parts.




Our first problem concerns tt-intersecting families in rr-partite rr-graphs.
For positive integers n,t,rn,t,r with n≥r>tn\geq r>t, define




ι0​(t,n,r):=max⁡{|ℱ|:ℱ⊆[n]r​ is non-trivial and ​t​-intersecting}.\iota_{0}(t,n;r):=\max\{|\mathcal{F}|:\mathcal{F}\subseteq[n]^{r}\text{ is non-trivial and }t\text{-intersecting}\}.



Frankl and Nie [9] determined this maximum for sufficiently large nn, proving that




ι0​(t,n,r)=max⁡{nr−t−(n−1)r−t+t⁡(n−1),(t+2)​nr−t−1−(t+1)​nr−t−2},\iota_{0}(t,n;r)=\max\left\{n^{r-t}-(n-1)^{r-t}+t(n-1),(t+2)n^{r-t-1}-(t+1)n^{r-t-2}\right\},



and conjectured that the same two-candidate formula holds for every n≥2n\geq 2. We show that this conjecture is asymptotically correct but fails for certain values of nn: in small parameter ranges, additional Ahlswede–Khachatrian ball-type examples may achieve larger size.



Theorem 1.1.



For integers r≥3r\geq 3, 1≤t≤r−21\leq t\leq r-2, n≥2n\geq 2 and q:=r−tq:=r-t, we have




ι0(t,n;r)=max{Wr,t(n),Ai(t,n;r):1≤i≤⌊q2⌋},\iota_{0}(t,n;r)=\max\left\{W_{r,t}(n),\,A_{i}(t,n;r):1\leq i\leq\left\lfloor\frac{q}{2}\right\rfloor\right\},



where




Wr,t​(n):=nq−(n−1)q+t⁡(n−1),W_{r,t}(n):=n^{q}-(n-1)^{q}+t(n-1),



and




Ai​(t,n,r):=nq−2​i​∑j=0i(t+2​ij)​(n−1)j.A_{i}(t,n;r):=n^{q-2i}\sum_{j=0}^{i}\binom{t+2i}{j}(n-1)^{j}.







In Theorem 1.1, the term Wr,t​(n)W_{r,t}(n) corresponds to a multi-part Hilton–Milner construction, while Ai​(t,n,r)A_{i}(t,n;r) is the product analogue of an Ahlswede–Khachatrian ball. For fixed rr and tt, two terms Wr,t​(n)W_{r,t}(n) and A1​(t,n,r)A_{1}(t,n;r) have the largest possible degree, namely r−t−1r-t-1, whereas Ai​(t,n,r)A_{i}(t,n;r) has degree at most r−t−2r-t-2 for every i≥2i\geq 2. Hence the formula reduces to the Frankl–Nie two-candidate maximum when nn is sufficiently large, but for small nn the lower-degree ball terms may dominate.





1.2 Hilton–Milner case in general multi-part products




We now turn to a different non-trivial condition, formulated in terms of matching and covering numbers. For a family ℱ⊆X1×⋯×Xr\mathcal{F}\subseteq X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{r}, let ν⁡(ℱ)\nu(\mathcal{F}) be the maximum number of pairwise disjoint edges in ℱ\mathcal{F}, and let τ⁡(ℱ)\tau(\mathcal{F}) be the minimum size of a vertex set meeting every edge. For r=2r=2, König’s theorem [11] gives ν⁡(ℱ)=τ⁡(ℱ)\nu(\mathcal{F})=\tau(\mathcal{F}); for r≥3r\geq 3 the two parameters differ in an essential way.




Our second result deals with the Hilton–Milner case in general multi-part products. Given ℱ⊆X1×⋯×Xr\mathcal{F}\subseteq X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{r}, ℱ\mathcal{F} is intersecting means ν⁡(ℱ)≤1\nu(\mathcal{F})\leq 1. Meyer [14] proved that every intersecting subhypergraph of [n]r[n]^{r} has at most nr−1n^{r-1} edges.
Deza and Frankl [3] proved the asymmetric version: if n1≥n2≥⋯≥nrn_{1}\geq n_{2}\geq\cdots\geq n_{r}, then every intersecting subhypergraph of [n1]×⋯×[nr][n_{1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{r}] has at most ∏ℓ=1r−1nℓ\prod_{\ell=1}^{r-1}n_{\ell} edges.
More generally, Frankl [7] showed that ν⁡(ℱ)≤s\nu(\mathcal{F})\leq s implies |ℱ|≤s​∏ℓ=1r−1nℓ|\mathcal{F}|\leq s\prod_{\ell=1}^{r-1}n_{\ell} in the same ordered setting. Since these extremal examples have small covering number, the natural non-trivial condition is τ⁡(ℱ)>s\tau(\mathcal{F})>s; here we treat the case s=1s=1.




Following Frankl and Nie [9], for ni=|Xi|n_{i}=|X_{i}| we define m0​(1,n1,…,nr)m_{0}(1,n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}) to be the maximum size of a family ℱ⊆X1×⋯×Xr\mathcal{F}\subseteq X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{r} satisfying




ν⁡(ℱ)≤1<τ⁡(ℱ).\nu(\mathcal{F})\leq 1<\tau(\mathcal{F}).



Equivalently, ℱ\mathcal{F} is intersecting but has no common vertex. This agrees with ι0​(1,n,r)\iota_{0}(1,n;r) in the symmetric case n1=⋯=nr=nn_{1}=\cdots=n_{r}=n, although for t>1t>1 the condition |⋂ℱ|<t|\bigcap\mathcal{F}|<t differs from the covering condition τ⁡(ℱ)>t\tau(\mathcal{F})>t.
Frankl and Nie [9] studied this problem and proved, among other results, the symmetric formula




m0​(1,n,…,n)=nr−1−(n−1)r−1+n−1m_{0}(1,n,\ldots,n)=n^{r-1}-(n-1)^{r-1}+n-1



for all r≥3r\geq 3 and n≥2n\geq 2, and the asymmetric three-part formula




m0​(1,n1,n2,n3)=n1+n2+n3−2.m_{0}(1,n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})=n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}-2.



The symmetric formula above is the case s=1s=1 of a conjecture of Lu and Ma [13], which states that, for sufficiently large nn, r≥4r\geq 4 and s<ns<n,




m0​(s,n,…,n)=s​nr−1−(n−1)r−1+n−s,m_{0}(s,n,\ldots,n)=sn^{r-1}-(n-1)^{r-1}+n-s,



where m0​(s,n,…,n)m_{0}(s,n,\ldots,n) denotes the analogous maximum under ν⁡(F)≤s<τ⁡(F)\nu(F)\leq s<\tau(F).
Frankl and Nie [9] later confirmed this conjecture for sufficiently large nn, and also pointed out that, already for four parts, no single asymmetric expression governs all parameter ranges. We give a finite exact reduction which accounts for this dependence on the part sizes.




Let ai:=ni−1a_{i}:=n_{i}-1 for i∈[r]i\in[r]. For a subset X⊆[r]X\subseteq[r], set




aX:=∏i∈Xai, and a∅:=1.a_{X}:=\prod_{i\in X}a_{i},\text{ and }\qquad a_{\emptyset}:=1.






A family 𝒟⊆2[r]\mathcal{D}\subseteq 2^{[r]} is called available if it satisfies the following


(D1) 



𝒟\mathcal{D} is a downset: if X∈𝒟X\in\mathcal{D} and Y⊆XY\subseteq X, then Y∈𝒟Y\in\mathcal{D};


(D2) 



⋃X∈𝒟X=[r]\bigcup_{X\in\mathcal{D}}X=[r];


(D3) 



X∪Y≠[r]X\cup Y\neq[r] for all X,Y∈𝒟X,Y\in\mathcal{D}.



Let 𝔇r\mathfrak{D}_{r} be the collection of all available families 𝒟⊆2[r]\mathcal{D}\subseteq 2^{[r]}.



Theorem 1.2.



For every r≥3r\geq 3 and n1,…,nr≥2n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}\geq 2,




m0​(1,n1,…,nr)=max⁡∑X∈𝒟𝒟∈𝔇r⁡aX.m_{0}(1,n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})=\max_{\mathcal{D}\in\mathfrak{D}_{r}}\sum_{X\in\mathcal{D}}a_{X}.







Theorem 1.2 reduces the multi-part Hilton–Milner problem to a weighted problem on the Boolean lattice. Applying this reduction, we determine the fully asymmetric values for r=4,5,6r=4,5,6. For r=4r=4, the optimization reduces to intersecting graphs on four vertices and is governed by the star–triangle dichotomy. For r=5r=5, weighted intersecting graphs still suffice. For r=6r=6, one must also choose among complementary pairs of triples. These cases illustrate the increasing complexity of the asymmetric problem.





1.3 Proof ideas and organization




The proofs of the two main results use a common shifting philosophy, but in different ways. In both problems we first replace an extremal family by a shifted one without changing its size or the relevant intersection property. This makes it possible to record an edge by the set of coordinates in which it differs from a fixed reference edge, and hence to pass from the multi-part product to a weighted problem on 2[r]2^{[r]}.




For the symmetric tt-intersection problem, this reduction leads to a weighted non-trivial tt-intersection problem on the Boolean lattice. The weights are determined by the product structure: a coordinate in the support contributes a factor n−1n-1, while the remaining coordinates contribute freely. After this translation, the Ahlswede–Khachatrian complete non-trivial intersection theorem, together with the weighted form of Bey–Engel, identifies the only possible extremal shapes. Evaluating their weights gives the term Wr,t​(n)W_{r,t}(n) and the ball terms Ai​(t,n,r)A_{i}(t,n;r) in Theorem 1.1. This is also where the additional small-nn candidates enter.




For the multi-part Hilton–Milner problem, the shifted family is encoded more directly by a downset 𝒟⊆2[r]\mathcal{D}\subseteq 2^{[r]}. The condition that the family has no common vertex becomes ⋃X∈𝒟X=[r]\bigcup_{X\in\mathcal{D}}X=[r], while the intersecting condition becomes the finite obstruction X∪Y≠[r]X\cup Y\neq[r] for all X,Y∈𝒟X,Y\in\mathcal{D}. Thus the original product problem is converted into the weighted downset optimization in Theorem 1.2. The explicit formulas for r=4,5,6r=4,5,6 are then obtained by solving this finite optimization: graphs suffice for r=4r=4 and r=5r=5, while for r=6r=6 complementary pairs of triples also have to be considered.




The paper is organized as follows. Section 22 contains the shifting and weighted intersection tools. Section 33 proves Theorem 1.1. Section 44 proves Theorem 1.2 and derives the explicit formulas for r=4,5,6r=4,5,6.






2 Preliminaries




In this section, we collect the notation and preliminary results needed for our proofs. We begin by introducing a multi-part adaptation of the classical shifting operation, due to Kwan, Sudakov, and Vieira [12].



Definition 2.1.



(Shifting)
Let ℱ⊆X1×⋯×Xr\mathcal{F}\subseteq X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{r} be an rr-partite rr-graph, and let ℓ,j\ell,j be integers with 1≤ℓ≤r1\leq\ell\leq r and 1<j≤|Xℓ|1<j\leq|X_{\ell}|.
Define the (1←j)(1\leftarrow j)-shift under [nℓ][n_{\ell}] by Sj(ℓ)​(ℱ)={Sj(ℓ)​(F):F=(a1,…,aℓ,…,ar)∈ℱ}S_{j}^{(\ell)}(\mathcal{F})=\{S_{j}^{(\ell)}(F):F=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell},\ldots,a_{r})\in\mathcal{F}\}, where




Sj(ℓ)​((,,,,,,,,,))={F′=(a1,…,1,…,ar)if ​aℓ=j​ and ​F′∉ℱ,(a1,…,ar)otherwise.S_{j}^{(\ell)}\bigl((a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell},\ldots,a_{r})\bigr)=\begin{cases}F^{\prime}=(a_{1},\ldots,1,\ldots,a_{r})&\text{if }a_{\ell}=j\text{ and }F^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{F},\\
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}







We say that ℱ\mathcal{F} is ℓ\ell-shifted if Sj(ℓ)​(ℱ)=ℱS_{j}^{(\ell)}(\mathcal{F})=\mathcal{F} for all jj.
Further, call ℱ\mathcal{F} coordinate-wise shifted if it is ℓ\ell-shifted for every 1≤ℓ≤r1\leq\ell\leq r. The following result, given by Kwan, Sudakov and Vieira [12], shows that shifting operation maintains the intersecting property.



Theorem 2.2 (Kwan–Sudakov–Vieira [12]).



Let r≥2r\geq 2 and n1,…,nr≥2n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}\geq 2. If there exists a non-trivial
intersecting family ℱ⊆[n1]×⋯×[nr]\mathcal{F}\subseteq[n_{1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{r}], then there exists a maximum-size non-trivial intersecting family ℱ′⊆[n1]×⋯×[nr]\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subseteq[n_{1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{r}] which is coordinate-wise shifted.





We remark that the original definition and theorem of Kwan–Sudakov–Vieira are more general than the form stated above. It applies to families of separated sets




𝒢⊆∏s=1p(Xsks)\mathcal{G}\subseteq\prod_{s=1}^{p}\binom{X_{s}}{k_{s}}



for arbitrary positive integers p,k1,…,kpp,k_{1},\ldots,k_{p}, and uses the usual shifts inside each part. For our purposes, the simplest case stated above (i.e., p=rp=r and k1=⋯=kr=1k_{1}=\cdots=k_{r}=1) suffices.




For a vector
𝐱=(x1,…,xr)\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}), define its projection as




P⁡(𝐱):={i∈[r]:xi=1}.P(\mathbf{x}):=\{i\in[r]:x_{i}=1\}.



For a shifted family of tuples, projections reduce the problem to set systems.

Let 𝒟∈𝔇5\mathcal{D}\in\mathfrak{D}_{5} be optimal. As before, since 𝒟\mathcal{D} is a downset and
⋃X∈𝒟X=[5]\bigcup_{X\in\mathcal{D}}X=[5], all singletons belong to 𝒟\mathcal{D}. Hence 𝒟\mathcal{D} contains no
44-set and no 55-set. Thus only levels 0,1,2,30,1,2,3 can occur.




For e∈([5]2)e\in\binom{[5]}{2}, we define a graph GG on [5][5] by




E⁡(G):={e∈([5]2):e¯∈𝒟}.E(G):=\left\{e\in\binom{[5]}{2}:\bar{e}\in\mathcal{D}\right\}.



If e,f∈E⁡(G)e,f\in E(G) were disjoint, then




e¯∪f¯=[5],\bar{e}\cup\bar{f}=[5],



contradicting the defining condition of 𝔇5\mathfrak{D}_{5}. Hence G∈ℐ5G\in\mathcal{I}_{5}.




For every e∈([5]2)e\in\binom{[5]}{2}, the condition X∪Y≠[5]X\cup Y\neq[5] forbids both ee and
e¯\bar{e} from belonging to 𝒟\mathcal{D}. Conversely, since the original weights aXa_{X} are
positive, optimality forbids both ee and e¯\bar{e} from being absent. Indeed, if
neither ee nor e¯\bar{e} belongs to 𝒟\mathcal{D}, then adding the 22-set ee preserves the
downset property, since all singletons already belong to 𝒟\mathcal{D}. It also preserves the
condition X∪Y≠[5]X\cup Y\neq[5], because the only set of size at most 33 whose union with
ee is [5][5] is e¯\bar{e}, which is absent. Thus exactly one of ee and e¯\bar{e}
belongs to 𝒟\mathcal{D}. Therefore




𝒟={∅}∪([5]1)∪(([5]2)∖E⁡(G))∪{e¯:e∈E⁡(G)}.\mathcal{D}=\{\emptyset\}\cup\binom{[5]}{1}\cup\left(\binom{[5]}{2}\setminus E(G)\right)\cup\{\bar{e}:e\in E(G)\}.



Consequently,




∑X∈𝒟aX=1+∑i=15ai+∑e∈([5]2)ae+∑e∈E⁡(G)(ae¯−ae).\sum_{X\in\mathcal{D}}a_{X}=1+\sum_{i=1}^{5}a_{i}+\sum_{e\in\binom{[5]}{2}}a_{e}+\sum_{e\in E(G)}(a_{\bar{e}}-a_{e}).



This gives the upper bound




m0​(1,n1,…,n5)≤1+∑i=15ai+∑1≤i<j≤5ai​aj+max⁡∑e∈E⁡(G)G∈ℐ5⁡Δe.m_{0}(1,n_{1},\ldots,n_{5})\leq 1+\sum_{i=1}^{5}a_{i}+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 5}a_{i}a_{j}+\max_{G\in\mathcal{I}_{5}}\sum_{e\in E(G)}\Delta_{e}.






Conversely, let G∈ℐ5G\in\mathcal{I}_{5} and define




𝒟G={∅}∪([5]1)∪(([5]2)∖E⁡(G))∪{e¯:e∈E⁡(G)}.\mathcal{D}_{G}=\{\emptyset\}\cup\binom{[5]}{1}\cup\left(\binom{[5]}{2}\setminus E(G)\right)\cup\{\bar{e}:e\in E(G)\}.



We claim that 𝒟G∈𝔇5\mathcal{D}_{G}\in\mathfrak{D}_{5}. First, 𝒟G\mathcal{D}_{G} is a downset. Since 𝒟G\mathcal{D}_{G}
contains ∅\emptyset and all singletons, the only nontrivial point is to check
the 22-subsets of the 33-sets e¯\bar{e} with e∈E⁡(G)e\in E(G). Let
f∈([5]2)f\in\binom{[5]}{2} and suppose f⊆e¯f\subseteq\bar{e} for some e∈E⁡(G)e\in E(G). Then
f∩e=∅f\cap e=\emptyset. Since GG is pairwise intersecting, we have
f∉E⁡(G)f\notin E(G), and hence




f∈([5]2)∖E⁡(G)⊆𝒟G.f\in\binom{[5]}{2}\setminus E(G)\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{G}.



Thus 𝒟G\mathcal{D}_{G} is a downset.




It remains to verify the admissibility condition. Since 𝒟G\mathcal{D}_{G} contains all
singletons, we have




⋃X∈𝒟GX=[5].\bigcup_{X\in\mathcal{D}_{G}}X=[5].



Moreover, no two members of 𝒟G\mathcal{D}_{G} have union [5][5]. Indeed, among sets of sizes at
most 33, a full union can occur only from a complementary 22–33 pair or from
two 33-sets. A complementary 22–33 pair cannot occur, because whenever
e¯∈𝒟G\bar{e}\in\mathcal{D}_{G}, the corresponding 22-set ee has been deleted. If two 33-sets
e¯,f¯∈𝒟G\bar{e},\bar{f}\in\mathcal{D}_{G} had union [5][5], then e∩f=∅e\cap f=\emptyset, contradicting
the fact that GG is pairwise intersecting. Hence 𝒟G∈𝔇5\mathcal{D}_{G}\in\mathfrak{D}_{5}.




Therefore the upper bound is sharp, and




m0​(1,n1,…,n5)=1+∑i=15ai+∑1≤i<j≤5ai​aj+max⁡∑e∈E⁡(G)G∈ℐ5⁡Δe.m_{0}(1,n_{1},\ldots,n_{5})=1+\sum_{i=1}^{5}a_{i}+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 5}a_{i}a_{j}+\max_{G\in\mathcal{I}_{5}}\sum_{e\in E(G)}\Delta_{e}.






Finally, any intersecting graph on five vertices is contained in a star or in a triangle.
The maximum max⁡∑e∈E⁡(G)G∈ℐ5⁡Δe\max_{G\in\mathcal{I}_{5}}\sum_{e\in E(G)}\Delta_{e}
may be checked over subgraphs of stars and triangles.
Since the weights Δe\Delta_{e} may be negative, an optimal graph need not be a full star or a full triangle.
∎






III The case r=6r=6




Let ℐ6\mathcal{I}_{6} be the set of all intersecting graphs on [6][6], including the empty graph.
For e∈([6]2)e\in\binom{[6]}{2}, write e¯=[6]∖e\overline{e}=[6]\setminus e and




Δe:=ae¯−ae.\Delta_{e}:=a_{\overline{e}}-a_{e}.



Set




B2(6):=1+∑i=16ai+∑1≤i<j≤6ai​aj.B_{2}^{(6)}:=1+\sum_{i=1}^{6}a_{i}+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 6}a_{i}a_{j}.



For G∈ℐ6G\in\mathcal{I}_{6}, define




𝒜⁡(G):={C∈([6]3):there is no ​e∈E⁡(G)​ with ​e⊆C}.\mathcal{A}(G):=\left\{C\in\binom{[6]}{3}:\text{there is no }e\in E(G)\text{ with }e\subseteq C\right\}.



The 33-subsets of [6][6] are paired by complementation. Define




Φ⁡(G):=∑{C,[6]∖C}max⁡({0}∪{aC:C∈𝒜⁡(G)}∪{a[6]∖C:[6]∖C∈𝒜⁡(G)}),\Phi(G):=\sum_{\{C,[6]\setminus C\}}\max\left(\{0\}\cup\{a_{C}:C\in\mathcal{A}(G)\}\cup\{a_{[6]\setminus C}:[6]\setminus C\in\mathcal{A}(G)\}\right),



where each complementary pair is counted once.



Theorem 4.3.



For ni≥2n_{i}\geq 2 and ai=ni−1a_{i}=n_{i}-1 for i∈[6]i\in[6], we have




m0​(1,n1,…,n6)=B2(6)+maxG∈ℐ6⁡(∑e∈E⁡(G)Δe+Φ⁡(G)).m_{0}(1,n_{1},\ldots,n_{6})=B_{2}^{(6)}+\max_{G\in\mathcal{I}_{6}}\left(\sum_{e\in E(G)}\Delta_{e}+\Phi(G)\right).






Proof.



Let 𝒟∈𝔇6\mathcal{D}\in\mathfrak{D}_{6} be optimal. Since 𝒟\mathcal{D} contains all singletons, it contains no 55-set and no
66-set. Thus every member of 𝒟\mathcal{D} has size at most 44.




Define




E⁡(G):={e∈([6]2):e¯∈𝒟}.E(G):=\{e\in\binom{[6]}{2}:\overline{e}\in\mathcal{D}\}.



If e,f∈E⁡(G)e,f\in E(G) are disjoint, then e¯∪f¯=[6]\overline{e}\cup\overline{f}=[6], impossible. Thus
G∈ℐ6G\in\mathcal{I}_{6}. As in the r=5r=5 case, optimality forces




𝒟∩([6]2)=([6]2)∖E⁡(G),\mathcal{D}\cap\binom{[6]}{2}=\binom{[6]}{2}\setminus E(G),



for the only obstruction to adding a missing 22-set ee is the presence of its complementary 44-set e¯\overline{e}.
Thus




𝒟∩([6]4)={e¯:e∈E⁡(G)}.\mathcal{D}\cap\binom{[6]}{4}=\{\overline{e}:e\in E(G)\}.



The total contribution from levels 0,1,2,40,1,2,4 is




1+∑i=16ai+∑e∈([6]2)∖E⁡(G)ae+∑e∈E⁡(G)ae¯=B2(6)+∑e∈E⁡(G)(ae¯−ae).1+\sum_{i=1}^{6}a_{i}+\sum_{e\in\binom{[6]}{2}\setminus E(G)}a_{e}+\sum_{e\in E(G)}a_{\overline{e}}=B_{2}^{(6)}+\sum_{e\in E(G)}(a_{\overline{e}}-a_{e}).



It remains to bound the possible contribution from the third level. Put




H:=𝒟∩([6]3).H:=\mathcal{D}\cap\binom{[6]}{3}.



We first show that H⊆𝒜⁡(G)H\subseteq\mathcal{A}(G), if C∈HC\in H and some e∈E⁡(G)e\in E(G) satisfies e⊆Ce\subseteq C, then C∪e¯=[6]C\cup\overline{e}=[6],
contradicting the conditions of 𝔇6\mathfrak{D}_{6}.
Hence H⊆𝒜⁡(G)H\subseteq\mathcal{A}(G). Also HH cannot contain both CC and [6]∖C[6]\setminus C. Hence the total weight of HH is at most Φ⁡(G)\Phi(G).




Since Δe=ae¯−ae\Delta_{e}=a_{\overline{e}}-a_{e}, we obtain




∑X∈𝒟aX≤B2(6)+∑e∈E⁡(G)Δe+Φ⁡(G).\sum_{X\in\mathcal{D}}a_{X}\leq B_{2}^{(6)}+\sum_{e\in E(G)}\Delta_{e}+\Phi(G).



Taking the maximum over G∈ℐ6G\in\mathcal{I}_{6} gives the upper bound




m0​(1,n1,…,n6)≤B2(6)+maxG∈ℐ6⁡(∑e∈E⁡(G)Δe+Φ⁡(G)).m_{0}(1,n_{1},\ldots,n_{6})\leq B_{2}^{(6)}+\max_{G\in\mathcal{I}_{6}}\left(\sum_{e\in E(G)}\Delta_{e}+\Phi(G)\right).






We now prove the reverse inequality. Fix G∈ℐ6G\in\mathcal{I}_{6}. For each
complementary pair {C,[6]∖C}\{C,[6]\setminus C\} of 33-sets, choose a member of maximum
weight among the members of this pair that lie in 𝒜⁡(G)\mathcal{A}(G); if neither member lies
in 𝒜⁡(G)\mathcal{A}(G), choose nothing. Let HGH_{G} be the family of chosen triples. Then




HG⊆𝒜⁡(G),∑C∈HGaC=Φ⁡(G).H_{G}\subseteq\mathcal{A}(G),\qquad\sum_{C\in H_{G}}a_{C}=\Phi(G).



Define




𝒟G:={∅}∪([6]1)∪(([6]2)∖E⁡(G))∪HG∪{e¯:e∈E⁡(G)}.\mathcal{D}_{G}:=\{\emptyset\}\cup\binom{[6]}{1}\cup\left(\binom{[6]}{2}\setminus E(G)\right)\cup H_{G}\cup\{\overline{e}:e\in E(G)\}.



We claim that 𝒟G∈𝔇6\mathcal{D}_{G}\in\mathfrak{D}_{6}.
First, 𝒟G\mathcal{D}_{G} is a downset. Since it contains ∅\emptyset and all singletons, the only nontrivial points to check are the chosen 33-sets and the present 44-sets.
Let C∈HGC\in H_{G}. Since C∈𝒜⁡(G)C\in\mathcal{A}(G), no edge of GG is contained in CC.
Therefore every 22-subset of CC lies in ([6]2)∖E⁡(G)⊆𝒟G\binom{[6]}{2}\setminus E(G)\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{G}.




Now let e¯\overline{e} be a present 44-set, with e∈E⁡(G)e\in E(G). Clearly, all 22-subsets of e¯\overline{e} belong to ([6]2)∖E⁡(G)\binom{[6]}{2}\setminus E(G).
Let C⊆e¯C\subseteq\overline{e} be any 33-subset.

Thus, by the intersection of GG, we must have C∈𝒜⁡(G)C\in\mathcal{A}(G).
On the other hand, the complementary 33-set [6]∖C[6]\setminus C contains ee, and therefore [6]∖C∉𝒜⁡(G)[6]\setminus C\notin\mathcal{A}(G), the construction of HGH_{G} forces C∈HGC\in H_{G}.
This proves that 𝒟G\mathcal{D}_{G} is a downset.




Second, since 𝒟G\mathcal{D}_{G} has no member of size larger than 44, the only possible ways for two members to have union [6][6] are of types 2−42-4, 3−33-3, 3−43-4, and 4−44-4.

Thus X∪Y≠[6]X\cup Y\neq[6] for all X,Y∈𝒟GX,Y\in\mathcal{D}_{G}.

⋃X∈𝒟GX=[6].\bigcup_{X\in\mathcal{D}_{G}}X=[6].



Consequently, 𝒟G∈𝔇6\mathcal{D}_{G}\in\mathfrak{D}_{6}.

∑X∈𝒟GaX\displaystyle\sum_{X\in\mathcal{D}_{G}}a_{X}

=B2(6)+∑e∈E⁡(G)Δe+Φ⁡(G).\displaystyle=B_{2}^{(6)}+\sum_{e\in E(G)}\Delta_{e}+\Phi(G).

Together with the upper bound, this proves

The theorem follows.
