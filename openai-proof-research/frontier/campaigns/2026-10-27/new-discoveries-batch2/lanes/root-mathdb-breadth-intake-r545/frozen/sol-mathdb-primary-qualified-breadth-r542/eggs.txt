Abstract.
    

We explore higher-dimensional generalizations of the classical egg drop problem, in which the goal is to locate a critical breaking point using the fewest number of trials. Beginning with the one-dimensional case, we prove that with kk eggs and NN floors, the minimal number of drops in the worst case satisfies P1​(k)≤⌈k​N1/k⌉P_{1}(k)\leq\lceil kN^{1/k}\rceil. We then extend the recursive algorithm to two and three dimensions, proving similar formulas: P2​(k)≤⌈(k−1)​(M+N)1/(k−1)⌉P_{2}(k)\leq\lceil(k-1)(M+N)^{1/(k-1)}\rceil in 2D and P3​(k)≤⌈(k−2)​(L+M+N)1/(k−2)⌉P_{3}(k)\leq\lceil(k-2)(L+M+N)^{1/(k-2)}\rceil in 3D, and conjecture a general formula for the dd-dimensional case. Beyond the critical point problems, we then study the critical line problems, where the breaking condition occurs along x+y=Vx+y=V (with slope −1-1) or, more generally, α​x+β​y=V\alpha x+\beta y=V (with the slope of the line unknown).
  




1. Introduction




The egg drop problem is a classic puzzle that has fascinated mathematicians and computer scientists. It is often featured in technical interviews at major technology companies such as Microsoft and Google, as it elegantly combines combinatorial reasoning with dynamic programming techniques.




The Egg Drop problem was introduced in [KVW].





Suppose we wish to know which windows in a 36-story building are safe to drop an egg from, and which will cause the egg to break on landing. Suppose two eggs are available. What is the least number of egg droppings that is guaranteed to work in all cases?






We make a few assumptions.


• 



An egg that survives a fall can be used again, with no damage.


• 



A broken egg must be discarded.


• 



The effect of a fall is the same for all eggs.


• 



If an egg breaks when dropped, then it would break from a higher floor.


• 



If an egg survives a fall, then it would survive a shorter fall.


• 



It is not ruled out that the first-floor break eggs, nor is it ruled out that a drop from the 36th floor does not cause an egg to break.






The book displays the optimal solution to the problem – to start at the eighth floor and if the egg breaks return to test every point from the first floor until the second egg breaks; otherwise use the first egg to test the fifteenth floor, and so on (as shown in Table 1). No matter where the first egg breaks, it will always require no more than 88 drops.

Later, Boardman [Bo] generalized this into the problem: Given kk eggs, how many floors can we handle if we have at most nn drops?




Using both generating functions and a direct counting approach, he showed that the total number of floors that can be tested is




∑j=1k(nj).\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{n}{j}.






Building off of Boardman’s work, Parks and Wills [PW] extended the problem using a binary decision tree to calculate the most floors that can be explored with kk eggs and dd drops for two adapted problems.





• 



Replacement Eggs: the supply of eggs is restored to the original number kk whenever the egg that is dropped does not break.


• 



Bonus Eggs: A new egg is received whenever the egg that is dropped does not break.






All of these results employ a dynamic search where each drop decision depends on the previous outcomes. There is another family of methods known as statistical or fixed-step strategies that we use in this paper.




There are also many other adaptations that can be made.
This paper builds on earlier lectures presented by the third author in his Math 331 course (Lecture 9, [Mi2]) at Williams College, as well as at many math camps.
We generalize the traditional egg drop problem into a problem with NN floors and kk eggs and bound the optimal step size and least number of drops required under such a strategy.
Beyond the one-dimensional problem, we also extend the traditional one-dimensional egg drop problem by introducing higher dimensional generalizations.
Instead of searching a point along a vertical line of floors, we explore versions set in a two-dimensional plane and three-dimensional space, where the critical condition depends on multiple coordinates.
Moreover, we continue this line of generalization to search for a critical line instead of a point in a 2D plane.




Our main theorems are the following.




Theorem 2.1: One Dimensional Critical Point Problem:
For k≥1k\geq 1 eggs and NN floors, the minimum number of drops required in the worst-case scenario satisfies




P1​(k)≤⌈k⋅N1/k⌉.P_{1}(k)\ \leq\ \lceil k\cdot N^{1/k}\rceil.






Theorem 3.1: Two Dimensional Critical Point Problem:
For k≥2k\geq 2 eggs and a rectangular region of dimensions M×NM\times N, the minimum number of drops required in the worst-case scenario satisfies




P2​(k)≤⌈(k−1)⋅(M+N)1/(k−1)⌉.P_{2}(k)\ \leq\ \lceil(k-1)\cdot(M+N)^{1/(k-1)}\rceil.






Theorem 6.1: Three Dimensional Critical Point Problem:
For k≥3k\geq 3 eggs and a cubic space of dimensions L×M×NL\times M\times N, the minimum number of drops required in the worst-case scenario satisfies




P3​(k)≤⌈(k−2)⋅(L+M+N)1/(k−2)⌉.P_{3}(k)\ \leq\ \lceil(k-2)\cdot(L+M+N)^{1/(k-2)}\rceil.






Theorem 4.1: Two Dimensional Critical Line x+y=Vx+y=V, Method One:
We perform a recursive jump search along the main diagonal of the rectangular region of dimensions M×NM\times N. For k≥1k\geq 1 eggs, the minimum number of drops required in the worst-case scenario under Method One satisfies




L2(1)​(k)≤⌈k⋅(M+N)1/k⌉.L_{2}^{(1)}(k)\ \leq\ \lceil k\cdot(M+N)^{1/k}\rceil.






Theorem 4.2: Two Dimensional Critical Line x+y=Vx+y=V, Method Two:
We combine a diagonal search in the rectangular region of dimensions M×NM\times N with a final verification of the last uncertain point using the reserved egg. For k≥2k\geq 2 eggs, the minimum number of drops required in the worst-case scenario under Method Two satisfies




L2(2)​(k)≤⌈(k−1)⋅M1/(k−1)⌉.L_{2}^{(2)}(k)\ \leq\ \lceil(k-1)\cdot M^{1/(k-1)}\rceil.






In future work, we plan to extend our study of the two-dimensional critical line x+y=Vx+y=V to the more general case α​x+β​y=V\alpha x+\beta y=V, which introduces new challenges and strategies.




We focused on minimizing the worst-case number of drops, but we could also consider minimizing the average-case performance. Since the worst case occurs only rarely, it might be reasonable to allow some risk by optimizing the average instead. We do not address minimizing the average case in this paper, but leave it as a direction for future research.





2. The One-Dimensional Critical Point Problem




This section reviews a recursive strategy for the one-dimensional Egg Drop problem with kk eggs and NN floors. Distinct from the approach presented in the introduction, our method derives the optimal step size for the search and establishes an upper bound on the worst-case number of drops.11
            1
            
            
            
          The worst-case number of drops ensures that, regardless of where the eggs break, the critical point can always be determined within this number of trials. All results in this section are proved by induction, and our one-dimensional search also serves as the foundation for the higher-dimensional generalizations.




Among a linear array of numbers from 11 to NN, there exists a unique critical point n∈(0,N]n\in(0,N] (both nn and NN are integers) such that





• 



if a magical egg is dropped from any point a<na<n, the egg remains intact with no damage, and


• 



if it is dropped from any point a≥na\geq n, the egg breaks.






We first describe a general strategy for solving this problem. If only one egg is available, test every lattice point from 11 upwards until the egg breaks at the critical point,22
            2
            
            
            
          This method is known as a linear search or sequential search. which is the only reliable method to determine the critical point.




If more than one egg is available, we can introduce a more efficient searching approach. Suppose we have kk eggs and NN floors. We begin by dropping the first egg at positions of the form i⋅S1​P;ki\cdot S_{1P;k}, where i=1,2,3,…i=1,2,3,\ldots. Here S1​P;kS_{1P;k} denotes the kk-egg step size to be determined when performing the critical point (P) search in the one-dimensional setting.33
            3
            
            
            
          This method in known as a jump search. Later we similarly use S2​P;kS_{2P;k} and S3​P;kS_{3P;k} for critical point searches in higher dimensions, and S2​L;kS_{2L;k} for the two-dimensional critical line (L) search. Continue this process until the egg breaks at position T⋅S1​P;kT\cdot S_{1P;k}. Now we know that the critical point must lie within the interval ((T−1)​S1​P;k,T​S1​P;k]((T-1)S_{1P;k},TS_{1P;k}] (the red segment in Figure 1), consisting of exactly S1​P;kS_{1P;k} candidate points, among which S1​P;k−1S_{1P;k}-1 need to be tested.44
            4
            
            
            
          Point T​S1​P;kTS_{1P;k} does not need to be tested again.





Figure 1.  The 1D critical point strategy.




We now face a reduced sub-problem: a (k−1)(k-1)-egg search over a smaller interval of S1​P;k−1S_{1P;k}-1 points. In the following recursive process, one egg is used to narrow down the interval, then the same strategy is applied with the remaining k−1k-1 eggs, until only one egg is left. At this point, we revert to a linear search within the final sub-segment.



Theorem 2.1.



If there are kk eggs, then in the worst-case scenario, the optimal step size under the given strategy is




S1​P;k≤N(k−1)/k,S_{1P;k}\ \leq\ N^{(k-1)/k},



which leads to a minimum number of egg drops




P1​(k)≤⌈k⋅N1/k⌉,for ​k≥1.P_{1}(k)\ \leq\ \lceil k\cdot N^{1/k}\rceil,\quad\text{for }k\geq 1.







2.1. Base Case: k=1k=1




In the worst-case scenario with only one egg, the egg does not break during the sequential search until it is dropped at position NN. Thus, the step size must be 11, and the number of drops required in the worst case is exactly NN.




The base case holds, since the optimal step size is S1​P;1≤N(1−1)/1=1S_{1P;1}\leq N^{(1-1)/1}=1, and the worst case number of drops is P1​(1)≤⌈1⋅N1/1⌉=NP_{1}(1)\leq\lceil 1\cdot N^{1/1}\rceil=N.





2.2. Inductive Case




Assuming that Theorem 2.1 is true for kk, we must show that S1​P;k+1≤Nk/(k+1)S_{1P;k+1}\leq N^{k/(k+1)} and P1​(k+1)≤⌈(k+1)⋅N1/(k+1)⌉P_{1}(k+1)\leq\lceil(k+1)\cdot N^{1/(k+1)}\rceil are also true.




To do so, we apply the recursive strategy described previously. Suppose we drop the first egg at intervals of increment size S1​P;k+1S_{1P;k+1} (i.e., jump search from positions S1​P;k+1,2​S1​P;k+1,…S_{1P;k+1},2S_{1P;k+1},\ldots), stopping when the egg breaks. In the worst case, this requires N/S1​P;k+1N/S_{1P;k+1} drops with the first egg. Then, we are left with a sub-interval (the red sub-segment in Figure 2) of length at most S1​P;k+1−1S_{1P;k+1}-1 candidate points to search using the remaining kk eggs.





Figure 2.  The 1D critical point strategy in the worst case.




By the inductive hypothesis, the worst-case number of drops needed to search a segment of size S1​P;k+1−1S_{1P;k+1}-1 with kk eggs is at most k⋅(S1​P;k+1−1)1/kk\cdot(S_{1P;k+1}-1)^{1/k}, which is approximately equal to, and less than, k⋅S1​P;k+11/kk\cdot S_{1P;k+1}^{1/k} drops.




Thus, the total number of drops in the worst case is approximately and at most




f1​P;k+1​(S1​P;k+1)≤NS1​P;k+1+k⋅S1​P;k+11/k.f_{1P;k+1}(S_{1P;k+1})\ \leq\ \frac{N}{S_{1P;k+1}}+k\cdot S_{1P;k+1}^{1/k}.

To minimize f1​P;k+1​(S1​P;k+1)f_{1P;k+1}(S_{1P;k+1}), we calculate the first derivative




f1​P;k+1′​(S1​P;k+1)=−NS1​P;k+12+S1​P;k+1(1−k)/k.f_{1P;k+1}^{\prime}(S_{1P;k+1})\ =\ -\frac{N}{S_{1P;k+1}^{2}}+S_{1P;k+1}^{(1-k)/k}.

f1​P;k+1′′​(S1​P;k+1)=2​NS1​P;k+13+1−kk⋅S1​P;k+1(1−2​k)/k.f_{1P;k+1}^{\prime\prime}(S_{1P;k+1})\ =\ \frac{2N}{S_{1P;k+1}^{3}}+\frac{1-k}{k}\cdot S_{1P;k+1}^{(1-2k)/k}.

• 



As S1​P;k+1→0+S_{1P;k+1}\to 0^{+}:

f1​P;k+1​(N)=NN+k⋅N1/k= 1+k⋅N1/k.f_{1P;k+1}(N)\ =\ \frac{N}{N}+k\cdot N^{1/k}\ =\ 1+k\cdot N^{1/k}.

f1​P;k+1​(Nk/(k+1))=NNk/(k+1)+k⋅(Nk/(k+1))1/k=(k+1)⋅N1/(k+1).f_{1P;k+1}\left(N^{k/(k+1)}\right)\ =\ \frac{N}{N^{k/(k+1)}}+k\cdot\left(N^{k/(k+1)}\right)^{1/k}\ =\ (k+1)\cdot N^{1/(k+1)}.



According to the AM-GM inequality,55

                  The AM-GM inequality states that for a set of non-negative real numbers, the arithmetic mean is greater than or equal to the geometric mean. The equality holds if and only if all the numbers are equal. we have f1​P;k+1​(N)≥f1​P;k+1​(Nk/(k+1))f_{1P;k+1}(N)\geq f_{1P;k+1}\left(N^{k/(k+1)}\right).

(a1⋅a2⋯ak⋅ak+1)1/(k+1)=(N1/k⋅N1/k⋯N1/k⋅1)1/(k+1)=N1/(k+1).(a_{1}\cdot a_{2}\cdots a_{k}\cdot a_{k+1})^{1/(k+1)}\ =\ (N^{1/k}\cdot N^{1/k}\cdots N^{1/k}\cdot 1)^{1/(k+1)}\ =\ N^{1/(k+1)}.



According to the AM-GM inequality, we get




k⋅N1/k+1k+1≥N1/(k+1).\frac{k\cdot N^{1/k}+1}{k+1}\ \geq\ N^{1/(k+1)}.



Multiply both sides by k+1k+1, yielding




k⋅N1/k+1≥(k+1)⋅N1/(k+1)⟹f1​P;k+1​(N)≥f1​P;k+1​(Nk/(k+1)).k\cdot N^{1/k}+1\ \geq\ (k+1)\cdot N^{1/(k+1)}\ \Longrightarrow\ f_{1P;k+1}(N)\ \geq\ f_{1P;k+1}\left(N^{k/(k+1)}\right).



The equality holds if and only if N=1N=1. In more general cases (i.e., N>1N>1), f1​P;k+1​(N)>f1​P;k+1​(Nk/(k+1))f_{1P;k+1}(N)>f_{1P;k+1}\left(N^{k/(k+1)}\right) implies that the function cannot achieve its global minimum at the endpoint NN.






Thus, the global minimum is achieved at the critical point S1​P;k+1=Nk/(k+1)S_{1P;k+1}=N^{k/(k+1)}, which is the optimal step size.




To minimize f1​P;k+1​(S1​P;k+1)f_{1P;k+1}(S_{1P;k+1}), we treated S1​P;k+1S_{1P;k+1} as a continuous variable to allow differentiation. However, in a real-world setting, such as a building, the tested points correspond to discrete floors and must therefore be integers, so we round up to the nearest integer floor. In an abstract setting, such as a number line, non-integer points can be tested.




In this case, the approximation is reasonable and safe because the derivative of f1​P;k+1​(S1​P;k+1)f_{1P;k+1}(S_{1P;k+1}) decreases and then increases, so we know that the best integer step size lies either immediately above or below the continuous optimal. And there is no problem with rounding UP the step size to the closest integer, since there is only a very small change in the total number of required drops between rounding up and down.66
              6
              
              
              
            For example, with N=100N=100 and k=4k=4, the optimal continuous step size is approximately S1​P;4≈31.62278S_{1P;4}\approx 31.62278, giving f1​P;4​(31.62278)≈12.34778f_{1P;4}(31.62278)\approx 12.34778 drops. Rounding UP to 3232 yields f1​P;4​(32)≈12.63866f_{1P;4}(32)\approx 12.63866, while rounding DOWN to 3131 gives f1​P;4​(31)≈12.66425f_{1P;4}(31)\approx 12.66425. Since we are only aiming for an upper bound, choosing the “wrong” integer step size merely increases the bound by a negligible amount. However, as illustrated by the Knapsack Problem (see, for example, [Mi1]), the integer minimum or maximum can significantly differ from its real-valued counterpart. 5. Future Work




Consider a rectangular region in the plane with coordinates ranging from (0,0)(0,0) to (M,N)(M,N). Without loss of generality, assume M≥NM\geq N. Within this region, there exists a unique critical line of the form α​x+β​y=V\alpha x+\beta y=V that passes through the rectangle, where xx and yy are variables, α\alpha, β\beta and VV are unknown constants with following constraints.





• 



The critical line has a positive yy-intercept.


• 



The slope of the critical line can be negative, 00, or undefined, so that the line can be descending, horizontal or vertical.


• 



The critical line has to go through at least two lattice points within or on the boundary of the rectangle.






The critical line determines the outcome of dropping a magical egg at a point (a,b)(a,b):


• 



if the point (a,b)(a,b) lies strictly below the line (i.e., α​x+β​y<V\alpha x+\beta y<V ), the egg remains intact and undamaged;


• 



otherwise (i.e., if α​x+β​y≥V\alpha x+\beta y\geq V ), the egg breaks.






The objective is to split the (M+1)​(N+1)(M+1)(N+1) lattice points on and within the rectangle into two groups: safe points and breaking points.




5.1. Failure of One-Egg Strategy




It is impossible to complete the classification with only one egg, because we need at least two breaking points to narrow down the range and constrain the slope of the critical line.




For example, in Figure 10, the egg breaks at point (3, 0). There are six possible locations for the critical line, two of which are shown as l1l_{1} and l2l_{2}:


• 



l1l_{1} is a line that enters through point (0,1)(0,1) and exits through point (3,0)(3,0), or


• 



l2l_{2} is a line that enters through point (2,2)(2,2) and exits through point (3,0)(3,0).







Figure 10.  The 2D critical line α​x+β​y=V\alpha x+\beta y=V: 1-egg failure example.




If l1l_{1} is the critical line, the set of safe points includes (0,0)(0,0), (1,0)(1,0) and (2,0)(2,0), and the rest are breaking points. If l2l_{2} is the critical line, there will be four more safe points. Thus, with only one egg, we cannot determine the complete set of breaking and non-breaking points.





5.2. Two-Egg Strategy




With only two eggs, we must use the most conservative approach to ensure that no lattice points on the critical line are skipped.


• 



First, perform a sequential search at every lattice point along the left and top sides of the rectangle using the first egg, and suppose the egg breaks at point (0,a)(0,a) on the left side or point (b,N)(b,N) on top.


• 



If the egg breaks at (0,a)(0,a), we then consider all points (x,y)(x,y) within or on the boundary of the rectangle from (1,0)(1,0) to (M,a)(M,a) and compute their slopes relative to (0,a)(0,a), namely




m1​(x,y)=y−ax.m_{1}(x,y)\ =\ \frac{y-a}{x}.



If the egg breaks at (b,N)(b,N), we can calculate the slopes from (b,N)(b,N) to every point (x,y)(x,y) in the rectangle from (b+1,0)(b+1,0) to (M,N)(M,N), giving




m2​(x,y)=y−Nx−b.m_{2}(x,y)\ =\ \frac{y-N}{x-b}.




• 



The set of slopes is ordered in increasing value, and the corresponding points are tested sequentially from the smallest slope upward, using the second egg, until it breaks at a certain point (c,d)(c,d).


• 



Because the critical line must pass through at least two lattice points, points that share the same slope respect to the first breaking point (either (0,a)(0,a) or (b,N)(b,N)) can be treated as a single candidate point.99

                  Without the constraint that the critical line passes through at least two lattice points, even points with same slope relative to the first breaking point must be strictly tested from the lowest upward.






At this point, we can conclude that all points strictly below the line passing through the first breaking point (0,a)(0,a) (or (b,N)(b,N)) and the second breaking point (c,d)(c,d) are safe, while all points on or above this line are points where the egg is bound to break.




From here we leave this critical line (unknown slope) problem for future work. In particular, we have not yet generalized the strategy to handle more than two eggs—such as cases of three, four, or kk eggs—and have not derived a general formula to compute or bound the number of drops required. It also remains an open question whether alternative strategies could be employed, for instance, using smaller increments with more frequent drops when additional eggs are available.




Another direction for future research is to consider average-case strategies. In this paper, we focus on minimizing the number of drops in the worst-case scenario, but we could also minimize the expected number of drops. Since the worst case occurs only rarely, it may be reasonable to allow some risk to improve average performance. If we assume a probability distribution for the breaking floor—for example, uniform, exponential, or binomial—then the goal would be to optimize the search strategy with respect to that distribution.
