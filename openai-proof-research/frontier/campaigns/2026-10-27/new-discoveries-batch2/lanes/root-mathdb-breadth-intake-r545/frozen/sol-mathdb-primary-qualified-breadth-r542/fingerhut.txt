Abstract.
    

For an even set of points in the plane, choose a max-sum matching, that is, a perfect matching maximizing the sum of Euclidean distances of its edges.
For each edge of the max-sum matching, consider the ellipse with foci at the edge’s endpoints and eccentricity 2/32/\sqrt{3}.
Using an optimization approach, we prove that the convex sets bounded by these ellipses intersect, answering a Tverberg-type question of Andy Fingerhut from 1995.
  



Key words and phrases: Infinite descent, convex optimization, Tverberg theorem, max-sum matching


2010 Mathematics Subject Classification51K99, 05C50, 51F99, 52C99, 05A99




1. Introduction




The celebrated Tverberg theorem [11] is one of the pearls in combinatorial geometry. It asserts that for any set of (d+1)​(r−1)+1(d+1)(r-1)+1 points in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d}, there is a partition with rr parts whose convex hulls intersect. One of the ideas for proving it relies on an optimization approach combined with the method of infinite descent. For example, the proofs of the Tverberg theorem by Tverberg and Vrećica [12] and by Roudneff [9] give a good illustration of this idea.




The following variation of the Tverberg theorem was proposed in [7, 10] and has been actively studied in recent years. For two points a,b∈ℝda,b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}, we denote by B⁡(a​b)B(ab) the closed Euclidean ball for
which the segment a​bab is its diameter. Let GG be a graph whose vertex set is a finite set of points in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d}. We say that GG is a Tverberg graph if




⋂x​y∈E⁡(G)B⁡(x​y)≠∅.\bigcap_{xy\in E(G)}B(xy)\neq\emptyset.






One of the problems arising in this context is to find a particular Tverberg graph (perfect matching, Hamiltonian cycle, etc.) for a finite set S⊂ℝdS\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}. For that, one can choose a proper function over a particular family of graphs with vertex set SS such that a graph maximizing it is a Tverberg graph. For example, it was shown in the papers [7] and [8]*Theorem 4 that a red-blue perfect matching (for any nn red and nn blue points in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d}) maximizing the sum of squared Euclidean distances between the matched points is a Tverberg graph. Also, very recently, the authors of [1] proved that a spanning tree (for a finite point set in the plane) maximizing the sum of edge distances is a Tverberg graph.




For an even set SS of points in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d}, let ℳ\mathcal{M} be a perfect matching and put cost⁡(ℳ)≔∑a​b∈ℳ‖a−b‖\cost(\mathcal{M})\coloneqq\sum_{ab\in\mathcal{M}}\|a-b\|. A max-sum matching of SS is a perfect matching ℳ\mathcal{M} with the maximum value of cost⁡(ℳ)\cost(\mathcal{M}). In 2021, Bereg et al. [2] showed that for any even set of points in the plane, a max-sum matching is a Tverberg graph. Their motivation was to prove a conjecture of Andy Fingerhut [4] from 1995.



Conjecture 1.



Let a matching {aibi:i=1,…,n}\{a_{i}b_{i}:i=1,\dots,n\} be max-sum for a set of 2​n2n points in the plane. Then there exists a point oo in the plane such that




‖ai−o‖+‖bi−o‖≤23​‖ai−bi‖​ for all i=1,…,n.\|a_{i}-o\|+\|b_{i}-o\|\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\|a_{i}-b_{i}\|\text{\hskip 10.22217ptfor all $i=1,\dots,n$.}







Andy Fingerhut [4] observed that, by the Helly theorem, it is enough to show this conjecture for 6 points, that is, for n=3n=3. Also, he mentioned that the factor 2/32/\sqrt{3} is the minimal possible. Indeed, consider a six-point set consisting of vertices of two coinciding equilateral triangles. A max-sum matching of this set corresponds to the sides of these triangles. So the center of these equilateral triangles is the only point oo satisfying Conjecture 1. Hence, one cannot decrease the factor 2/32/\sqrt{3} by a smaller one.




The motivation of Andy Fingerhut was to confirm a conjecture of Subhash Suri [5] in designing communication
networks on minimum Steiner stars. For a finite set SS of points in the plane, denote by yy a point minimizing the sum ∑x∈S‖y−x‖\sum_{x\in S}\|y-x\|. We write t⁡(S)t(S) for the corresponding minimum sum. A geometric graph with all edges connecting yy with the points of SS is called a minimum Steiner star.



Conjecture 2.



For an even set SS of points in the plane with a max-sum matching ℳ\mathcal{M}, we have
t⁡(S)≤23​cost⁡(ℳ).t(S)\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\;\cost(\mathcal{M}).





In 2000, Fekete and Meijer [5] confirmed this conjecture. They derive it from the following observation. (They did not state it as a lemma, however, the next lemma easily follows from their argument.)



Lemma 3.



For any even set of points in the plane, there exists a perfect matching ℳ\mathcal{M} and a point oo in the plane such that either o∈{x,y}o\in\{x,y\} or ∠​x​o​y≥2​π/3\angle xoy\geq 2\pi/3 for all x​y∈ℳxy\in\mathcal{M}.





To prove this observation, the authors of [5] apply a Borsuk–Ulam-type argument. Essentially, the same fact with the same proof was also found by Dumitrescu, Pach, and Tóth in the paper [3]*Lemma 2 from 2012. It is worth mentioning that there are even sets such that no max-matching satisfies Lemma 3; see the example in [2]*Subsection 1.1. So Conjecture 1, a strengthening of Conjecture 2, is still open.




Andy Fingerhut shared Conjecture 1 with David Eppstein, who popularized it on his homepage [4] and proved it with the factor 5/25/2 instead of 2/32/\sqrt{3}. Bereg et al. [2] conclude that their result on max-sum matchings and Tverberg graphs yields the Fingerhut conjecture with the factor 2\sqrt{2}. Our goal is to confirm this conjecture completely.



Theorem 4.



Conjecture 1 is true.





Our proof of Theorem 4 combines an optimization approach and the infinite descent method. A similar idea was used in [12] and [9] to prove the Tverberg theorem. Also, an analogous approach was applied in [8] to find
a perfect red-blue Tverberg matching of any nn red and nn blue points in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d}.




The paper is organized as follows. In Subsection 2.1, we restate the Fingerhut conjecture in terms of the intersection of convex regions bounded by ellipses. In Subsection 2.2, we recall classical facts from convex geometry. Finally, we prove the Fingerhut conjecture in Section 3. (One of the technical observations, Lemma 13, is shown in Subsection 3.3.)




Acknowledgments.




We are grateful to Fedor Gerasimov, Olimjoni Pirahmad, and the members of the Laboratory of Combinatorial and Geometric Structures at MIPT for the stimulating and fruitful discussions.






2. Auxiliary notation and facts




2.1. Ellipses and lenses




For the sake of brevity, we introduce the following notation for the region bounded by an ellipse




ℰλ​(a​b)≔{x∈ℝ:‖a−x‖+‖b−x‖≤λ​‖a−b‖},\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}(ab)\coloneqq\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\|a-x\|+\|b-x\|\leq\lambda\|a-b\|\big\},



where aa and bb are some points in the plane. Slightly abusing notation, we say that ℰλ​(a​b)\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}(ab) is an ellipse. Since the factor λ=2/3\lambda=2/\sqrt{3} plays a main role in Conjecture 1, we put




ℰ⁡(a​b)≔ℰ2/3​(a​b).\mathcal{E}(ab)\coloneqq\mathcal{E}_{2/\sqrt{3}}(ab).



So we can easily restate Conjecture 1.



Conjecture 5.



If ℳ\mathcal{M} is a max-sum matching of an even set of points in the plane, then




⋂x​y∈ℳℰ⁡(x​y)≠∅.\bigcap_{xy\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(xy)\neq\emptyset.







Also, we need the notation of lens. For two distinct points x,yx,y in the plane and α∈(0,π)\alpha\in(0,\pi), denote by α⁡(x​y)\alpha(xy) the so-called α\alpha-lens for the line segment x​yxy, that is,




α⁡(x​y)={z∈ℝ2∖{x,y}:∠​x​z​y≥α}∪{x,y}.\alpha(xy)=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{x,y\}:\angle xzy\geq\alpha\}\cup\{x,y\}.



Next, we state a trivial observation, which we leave without proof.



Observation 6.



For any two distinct points xx and yy in the plane, we have




α⁡(x​y)⊂ℰ⁡(x​y),\alpha(xy)\subset\mathcal{E}(xy),



where α=2​π/3\alpha=2\pi/3.





We refer to [8]*Subsection 9.2 to read about a high-dimensional problem on the intersection of lenses (generalizing Lemma 3 and similar to Conjecture 5).





2.2. Auxiliary facts from convex geometry




We recall a standard notation and theorems from convex geometry; see [6]*Chapter 6.




A function f:ℝn→ℝf:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R} is called convex if it satisfies the inequality




f⁡(λ​x+(1−λ)​y)≤λ​f​(x)+(1−λ)​f​(y)f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\leq\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y)



for any two points x,y∈ℝdx,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d} and 0≤λ≤10\leq\lambda\leq 1.
The subdifferential ∂f⁡(x)\partial f(x) of a convex function ff at a point x∈ℝnx\in\mathbb{R}^{n} is given by




∂f(x):={s∈ℝn:f(y)−f(x)≥⟨s,y−x⟩ for all y∈ℝn}.\partial f(x):=\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f(y)-f(x)\geq\langle s,y-x\rangle\quad\text{ for all }y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}.



According to this definition, the set ∂f⁡(x)\partial f(x) is convex. A vector s∈∂f⁡(x)s\in\partial f(x) is called a subgradient of ff at xx.




Next, we state two simple properties of convex functions.



Theorem 7.



[6]*Corollary 2.1.4
If a convex function f:ℝn→ℝf:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R} is differentiable at a point x∈ℝnx\in\mathbb{R}^{n}, then its only subgradient at xx is its gradient, that is,




∂f⁡(x)={∇f​(x)}.\partial f(x)=\{\nabla f(x)\}.






Theorem 8.



[6]*Theorem 2.2.1
Let f:ℝn→ℝf:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R} be a convex function. Then, ff is minimized at xx over ℝn\mathbb{R}^{n} if and only if the origin oo lies in ∂f⁡(x)\partial f(x).





We need the following notation to state the next theorem and its corollary.
For convex functions f1,…,fm:ℝn→ℝf_{1},\dots,f_{m}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}, consider the function f:ℝn→ℝf:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R} defined by f⁡(x)≔max⁡{f1​(x),…,fm​(x)}.f(x)\coloneqq\max\{f_{1}(x),\dots,f_{m}(x)\}.
For any point x∈ℝx\in\mathbb{R}, we write I⁡(x)≔{1≤i≤m:fi​(x)=f⁡(x)}.I(x)\coloneqq\{1\leq i\leq m:f_{i}(x)=f(x)\}.



Theorem 9.



[6]*Corollary 4.3.2
For any x∈ℝnx\in\mathbb{R}^{n} we have




∂f⁡(x)=conv⁡(∪∂i∈I⁡(x)⁡fi​(x)).\partial f(x)=\conv(\cup_{i\in I(x)}\partial f_{i}(x)).







The key optimization tool we need is the following corollary of Theorems 7, 8, and 9.



Proposition 10.



If the function ff attains its global minimum at the point xx and, for all i∈I⁡(x)i\in I(x), the function fif_{i} is differentiable at xx, then we have




o∈conv⁡{∇fi​(x):i∈I⁡(x)}.o\in\conv\{\nabla f_{i}(x):i\in I(x)\}.






Proof.



By Theorem 9 we have




∂f⁡(x)=conv⁡(∪∂i∈I⁡(x)⁡fi​(x)).\partial f(x)=\conv(\cup_{i\in I(x)}\partial f_{i}(x)).



Since ff attains global minimum at xx, by Theorem 8 we get o∈∂f⁡(x)o\in\partial f(x). Applying Theorem 7 to the function fif_{i}, for all i∈I⁡(x)i\in I(x), we obtain
∂fi​(x)={∇fi​(x)}.\partial f_{i}(x)=\{\nabla f_{i}(x)\}.
Hence,




o∈∂f⁡(x)=conv⁡(∪∂i∈I⁡(x)⁡fi​(x))=conv⁡{∇fi​(x):i∈I⁡(x)},o\in\partial f(x)=\conv(\cup_{i\in I(x)}\partial f_{i}(x))=\conv\{\nabla f_{i}(x):i\in I(x)\},



which finishes the proof.
∎





In our problem, we apply Proposition 10 to a particular collection of functions and obtain its corollary in the form needed to prove the Fingerhut conjecture.




First, we introduce an auxiliary notation. For two vectors xx and yy, put




ℓx​y≔∥y∥‖x‖+‖y‖​x+∥x∥∥x∥+∥y∥​y.\ell_{xy}\coloneqq\frac{\lVert y\rVert}{\|x\|+\|y\|}x+\frac{\lVert x\rVert}{\lVert x\rVert+\lVert y\rVert}y.



Clearly, the vector ℓx​y\ell_{xy} bisects the angle between xx and yy, and moreover, the point ℓx​y\ell_{xy} belongs to the line segment x​yxy.




For any distinct points aa and bb, the function ha​b:ℝ2→ℝh_{ab}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R} is defined by




ha​b​(x):=∥a−x∥+∥b−x∥∥a−b∥.h_{ab}(x):=\dfrac{\lVert a-x\rVert+\lVert b-x\rVert}{\lVert a-b\rVert}.



Clearly, it is well-defined and differentiable everywhere except at aa and bb. For a set ℳ\mathcal{M} of pairs of distinct points in the plane, the function hℳ:ℝ2→ℝh_{\mathcal{M}}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R} is given by hℳ≔maxa​b∈ℳ⁡ha​bh_{\mathcal{M}}\coloneqq\max_{ab\in\mathcal{M}}h_{ab}.



Corollary 11.



Let ℳ\mathcal{M} be a set of pairs of distinct points. If the function hℳh_{\mathcal{M}} attains its global minimum at the origin oo and hℳ​(o)>1h_{\mathcal{M}}(o)>1, then




o∈conv⁡{ℓa​b:a​b∈ℳ1},o\in\conv\{\ell_{ab}:ab\in\mathcal{M}_{1}\},



where




ℳ1≔{a​b∈ℳ:∥a∥+∥b∥∥a−b∥=hℳ​(o)}.\mathcal{M}_{1}\coloneqq\left\{ab\in\mathcal{M}:\dfrac{\lVert a\rVert+\lVert b\rVert}{\lVert a-b\rVert}=h_{\mathcal{M}}(o)\right\}.






Proof.



For all a​b∈ℳ1ab\in\mathcal{M}_{1}, we have




ha​b​(o)=hℳ​(o)>1=ha​b​(a)=ha​b​(b),h_{ab}(o)=h_{\mathcal{M}}(o)>1=h_{ab}(a)=h_{ab}(b),



and thus, the points aa and bb are distinct from oo. So, for all a​b∈ℳ1ab\in\mathcal{M}_{1}, the function ha​bh_{ab} is differentiable at oo.




Applying Proposition 10 to the function h⁡(x)=maxa​b∈ℳ⁡ha​b​(x)h(x)=\max_{ab\in\mathcal{M}}h_{ab}(x), we obtain




o∈conv⁡{∇ha​b​(o):a​b∈ℳ1}.o\in\conv\{\nabla h_{ab}(o):ab\in\mathcal{M}_{1}\}.






To finish the proof, we show that the vectors ∇ha​b​(o)\nabla h_{ab}(o) and ℓa​b\ell_{ab} are collinear in the opposite directions. By a routine computation, one easily get that




∇ha​b​(o)=−1∥a−b∥​(a∥a∥+b∥b∥)=−∥a∥+∥b∥∥a−b∥​∥a∥​∥b∥​ℓa​b,\nabla h_{ab}(o)=-\dfrac{1}{\lVert a-b\rVert}\left(\dfrac{a}{\lVert a\rVert}+\dfrac{b}{\lVert b\rVert}\right)=-\dfrac{\lVert a\rVert+\lVert b\rVert}{\lVert a-b\rVert\lVert a\rVert\lVert b\rVert}\ell_{ab},



which finishes the proof.
∎







3. Intersecting ellipses




3.1. Angular notation




Throughout this section we use the following angular notation. We define the following functions




∡⁡(⋅,⋅):ℝ2∖{o}→[0,2​π)​ and ​∠​(⋅,⋅):ℝ2∖{o}→[0,π].\measuredangle(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{o\}\to[0,2\pi)\text{\ \ and\ \ }\angle(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{o\}\to[0,\pi].



Essentially, ∡⁡(x,y)\measuredangle(x,y) is the directed angle between vectors xx and yy measured in counterclockwise direction and ∠⁡(x,y)\angle(x,y) is the smallest (non-negative) angle between xx and yy. Clearly, these functions are well-defined. Slightly abusing notation, we will also consider the angles ∡⁡(m,n)\measuredangle(m,n) and ∠⁡(m,n)\angle(m,n) for rays mm and nn emanating from the origin oo, that is, ∡⁡(m,n)=∡⁡(x,y)\measuredangle(m,n)=\measuredangle(x,y) and ∠⁡(m,n)=∠⁡(x,y)\angle(m,n)=\angle(x,y), where x∈mx\in m and y∈ny\in n are any two points distinct from oo.





3.2. Proof of Theorem 4




Suppose to the contrary that for an even set of points S⊂ℝ2S\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} and a max-sum matching ℳ\mathcal{M} of SS, we have



(1)

⋂a​b∈ℳℰ⁡(a​b)=∅.\bigcap\limits_{ab\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(ab)=\varnothing.






First, we show that there is no edge with coinciding endpoints, that is, a≠ba\neq b for any edge a​b∈ℳab\in\mathcal{M}.
Indeed, suppose that a​b∈ℳab\in\mathcal{M} and a=ba=b. Since the intersection of the ellipses is empty, there is an edge c​d∈ℳcd\in\mathcal{M} such that the point aa lies outside of the line segment c​dcd. Hence by the triangle inequality, we have




∥a−b∥+∥c−d∥=∥c−d∥<∥a−c∥+∥a−d∥=∥a−c∥+∥b−d∥.\lVert a-b\rVert+\lVert c-d\rVert=\lVert c-d\rVert<\lVert a-c\rVert+\lVert a-d\rVert=\lVert a-c\rVert+\lVert b-d\rVert.



Thus the cost of the matching ℳ∖{a​b,c​d}∪{a​c,b​d}\mathcal{M}\setminus\{ab,cd\}\cup\{ac,bd\} is larger than cost⁡(ℳ)\cost(\mathcal{M}), a contradiction with the maximality of ℳ\mathcal{M}. Therefore, no edge in ℳ\mathcal{M} has length 0.




Recall that the function hℳ:ℝ2→ℝh_{\mathcal{M}}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{R} is given by




hℳ​(x):=maxa​b∈ℳ⁡∥a−x∥+∥b−x∥∥a−b∥,h_{\mathcal{M}}(x):=\max\limits_{ab\in\mathcal{M}}\dfrac{\lVert a-x\rVert+\lVert b-x\rVert}{\lVert a-b\rVert},



and so, it is well defined as a≠ba\neq b for any a​b∈ℳab\in\mathcal{M}. Since hℳh_{\mathcal{M}} is bounded from below, it attains its minimum at some point xℳx_{\mathcal{M}}. Without loss of generality we may assume that xℳx_{\mathcal{M}} coincides with the origin oo. For the sake of brevity, put




λ≔hℳ​(o).\lambda\coloneqq h_{\mathcal{M}}(o).



By (1) we have λ>2/3>1\lambda>2/\sqrt{3}>1.




Consider the submatching ℳ1⊆ℳ\mathcal{M}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{M} consisting of pairs a​bab such that




∥a∥+∥b∥=λ⁡∥a−b∥.\lVert a\rVert+\lVert b\rVert=\lambda\lVert a-b\rVert.



Applying Corollary 11 to the matching ℳ\mathcal{M}, we obtain




o∈conv⁡{ℓa​b:a​b∈ℳ1}.o\in\conv\left\{\ell_{ab}:ab\in\mathcal{M}_{1}\right\}.



Recall that the vector ℓa​b\ell_{ab} bisects the angle between vectors aa and bb, and the endpoint of ℓa​b\ell_{ab} lies on the line segment a​bab.
Let ℳ2\mathcal{M}_{2} be a minimal subset of ℳ1\mathcal{M}_{1} such that



(2)

o∈conv⁡{ℓa​b:a​b∈ℳ2}.o\in\conv\big\{\ell_{ab}:ab\in\mathcal{M}_{2}\big\}.



By the Carathéodory theorem, the size of ℳ2\mathcal{M}_{2} is 2 or 3. Indeed, ℳ2\mathcal{M}_{2} cannot consist of only one edge a​bab. Otherwise, the origin oo lies on the line segment a​bab.
