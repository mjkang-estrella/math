Abstract
We prove the following type of discrete entropy monotonicity for sums of isotropic, log-concave, independent and identically distributed random vectors 
\(X_1,\dots ,X_{n+1}\)
 on 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
: 
$$ H(X_1+\cdots +X_{n+1}) \ge H(X_1+\cdots +X_{n}) + \frac{d}{2}\log {\Bigl (\frac{n+1}{n}\Bigr )} +o(1), $$
where 
o
(1) vanishes as 
\(H(X_1) \rightarrow \infty \)
. Moreover, for the 
o
(1)-term, we obtain a rate of convergence 
\( O\Bigl ({H(X_1)}{e^{-\frac{1}{d}H(X_1)}}\Bigr )\)
, where the implied constants depend on 
d
 and 
n
. This generalizes to 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
 the one-dimensional result of the second named author (2023). As in dimension one, our strategy is to establish that the discrete entropy 
\(H(X_1+\cdots +X_{n})\)
 is close to the differential (continuous) entropy 
\(h(X_1+U_1+\cdots +X_{n}+U_{n})\)
, where 
\(U_1,\dots , U_n\)
 are independent and identically distributed uniform random vectors on 
\([0,1]^d\)
 and to apply the theorem of Artstein, Ball, Barthe and Naor (2004) on the monotonicity of differential entropy. In fact, we show this result under more general assumptions than log-concavity, which are preserved up to constants under convolution. Namely, we consider families of random variables for which, as the determinant of the covariance matrix increases, the probability mass function 
i)
 is bounded above in terms of the determinant of the covariance matrix, 
ii)
 has subexponential tails, 
iii)
 has (discrete) bounded variation. In order to show that log-concave distributions satisfy our assumptions in dimension 
\(d\ge 2\)
, more involved tools from convex geometry are needed because a suitable position is required. We show that, for a log-concave function on 
\(\mathbb {R}^d\)
 in isotropic position, its integral, barycenter and covariance matrix are close to their discrete counterparts. Moreover, in the log-concave case, we weaken the isotropicity assumption to what we call 
almost isotropicity
. One of our technical tools is a discrete analogue to the upper bound on the isotropic constant of a log-concave function, which extends to dimensions 
\(d\ge 1\)
 a result of Bobkov, Marsiglietti and Melbourne (2022) in dimension one and may be of independent interest.

1 
Introduction
1.1 
Monotonicity of Entropy
The 
differential entropy
 of an 
\(\mathbb {R}^{d}\)
-valued random vector 
X
 with density 
f
 is defined as
$$ h(X)=-\int _{\mathbb {R}^d} f(x) \log f(x) dx , $$
if the integral exists. When 
X
 is supported on a strictly lower-dimensional set than 
\(\mathbb {R}^d\)
 (and hence does not have a density with respect to the 
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure), one sets 
\(h(X) =-\infty \)
. The 
entropy power
 of 
X
 is defined by 
\(N(X)=e^{2h(X)/d}\)
.
Let 
\(X_1,\ldots ,X_n\)
 be i.i.d. continuous random variables. A generalization of the classical Entropy Power Inequality (EPI) of Shannon [
25
] and Stam [
27
], due to Artstein, Ball, Barthe and Naor, [
1
] states that
$$\begin{aligned} h\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}\sum _{i=1}^{n+1}X_{i}\right) \ge h\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum _{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right) , \end{aligned}$$
which by the scaling property of differential entropy is equivalent to
$$\begin{aligned} h\left( \sum _{i=1}^{n+1}{X_i}\right) \ge h\left( \sum _{i=1}^{n}{X_i}\right) + \frac{d}{2}\log {\left( \frac{n+1}{n}\right) }. \end{aligned}$$

                    (1)
                
It can also be interpreted as the monotonic increase of entropy along the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) [
3
]. Later, other proofs and generalizations were given by Shlyakhtenko [
26
], Tulino and Verdú [
29
] and Madiman and Barron [
20
].
Let 
A
 be a discrete (finite or countable) set and let 
X
 be a random variable supported on 
A
 with probability mass function (p.m.f.) 
p
 on 
A
. The discrete (Shannon) entropy of 
X
 is
$$\begin{aligned} H(X) = -\sum _{x \in A}{p(x)\log {p(x)}}. \end{aligned}$$
An exact analogue of (
1
) cannot be true for discrete random variables as can be seen by taking 
\(n=1, d=1\)
 and considering deterministic or even close to deterministic random variables. Nevertheless, Tao [
28
], using ideas from additive combinatorics, proved that for any independent and identically distributed random variables 
\(X_1,X_2\)
 taking values in a finite subset of a torsion-free (abelian) group
$$\begin{aligned} H(X_1+X_2) \ge H(X_1) + \frac{1}{2}\log {2} - o(1), \end{aligned}$$

                    (2)
                
where 
\(o(1) \rightarrow 0\)
 as 
\(H(X_1) \rightarrow \infty \)
. This can be seen as a discrete analogue of (
1
) for 
\(d=n=1\)
. Besides (
2
), other discrete versions of the EPI have been studied by several authors. In [
14
, 
15
], a discrete EPI similar to (
2
) was proved, with a worse constant than 
\(\frac{1}{2}{\log {2}}\)
, but with sharp quantitative bounds for the 
o
(1)-term (see also [
11
] for a detailed discussion on connections with that work). An EPI for the binomial family was proved in [
16
, Theorem 1.1]; in [
23
, Theorem 4.6] it was shown that 
\(e^{2H(X)} -1\)
 is superadditive with respect to convolution on the class of uniform distributions on finite subsets of the integers (see also [
21
] for extensions to Rényi entropy and [
22
] for dimensional extensions). Finally the constant 
\(\frac{1}{2}\log {2}\)
 was shown to be improvable for the class of uniform distributions in [
30
].
In addition to (
2
), Tao conjectured that for any independent and identically distributed random variables 
\(X_1,\ldots ,X_{n+1}\)
 taking values in a finite subset of a torsion-free group
$$\begin{aligned} H(X_1 + \cdots +X_{n+1}) \ge H(X_1 + \cdots +X_{n}) + \frac{1}{2}\log {\Bigl (\frac{n+1}{n}\Bigr )} - o(1), \end{aligned}$$

                    (3)
                
as 
\(H(X_1) \rightarrow \infty \)
 depending on 
n
. A special case of this conjecture was recently proven in [
10
], where it is shown that (
3
) is satisfied by log-concave random variables on the integers with explicit rate for the 
o
(1) that is exponential in 
\(H(X_1)\)
:

                  
Theorem 1

                  
( [
10
]) Let 
\(X_1,\ldots ,X_n\)
 be i.i.d. log-concave random variables on 
\(\mathbb {Z}\)
. Then
$$ H\Biggl (\sum _{i=1}^{n+1}{X_i}\Biggr ) \ge H\Biggl (\sum _{i=1}^{n}{X_i}\Biggr ) + \frac{1}{2}\log {\Bigl (\frac{n+1}{n}\Bigr )} - O\Bigl (H(X_1)e^{-H(X_1)}\Bigr ), $$
as 
\(H(X_1) \rightarrow \infty \)
.

                
This statement can be interpreted as a type of “approximate" monotonicity in the 
discrete
 entropic CLT [
12
]. We recall here that an integer valued random variable 
X
 with p.m.f. 
p
 is said to be 
log-concave
 if
$$\begin{aligned} p(k)^2 \ge p(k-1)p(k+1), \end{aligned}$$

                    (4)
                
for every 
\(k \in \mathbb {Z}.\)
1.2 
Main Results and Paper Outline
Our goal is to extend Theorem 
1
 to higher dimensions and to the following larger class of probability distributions:

                  
Definition 2

                  
Let 
\(\mathcal {C}(c_1,c_2,c_3)\)
 be the class of p.m.f.s 
p
 on 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
 for which, denoting 
\(\sigma := \det {(\textrm{Cov}{(p)}})^{\frac{1}{2d}}\)
, 

                    

                      
1.

                      
                        
$$\begin{aligned} \max _{k \in \mathbb {Z}^d}{p(k)} \le \frac{c_1}{\sigma ^d}, \end{aligned}$$

                      
                    

                    

                      
2.

                      
                        
for all 
\(k \in \mathbb {Z}^d\)
 with 
\(\Vert k\Vert _{\infty } \ge c_2 \sigma \)
, 
$$ p(k) \le \frac{c_2}{\sigma ^d}e^{-\frac{\Vert k\Vert _1}{c_2\sigma }} \quad \text { and} $$

                      
                    

                    

                      
3.

                      
                        
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{i=1}^d{\sum _{k \in \mathbb {Z}^d}}{|p(k)-p(k-e_i)|}\le \frac{c_3}{\sigma }. \end{aligned}$$

                      
                    

                  

                
Our first main result is the following one:

                  
Theorem 3

                  
For any i.i.d. random vectors 
\(X_1,\ldots ,X_{n+1}\)
 on 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
 such that their common p.m.f. belongs to the class 
\(\mathcal {C}(c_1,c_2,c_3)\)
, for some absolute constants 
\(c_1, c_2, c_3\)
 that depend on the dimension only,
$$\begin{aligned} H(X_1+\cdots +X_{n+1}) \ge H(X_1+\cdots +X_{n}) + \frac{d}{2}\log {\Bigl (\frac{n+1}{n}\Bigr )} - o(1) \end{aligned}$$

                    (5)
                
as 
\(H(X_1) \rightarrow \infty \)
. Moreover, we have the rate of convergence 
\(o(1) =\)
\( O_{d,n}\Bigl ({H(X_1)}{e^{-\frac{1}{d}H(X_1)}}\Bigr )\)
. In particular, (
5
) holds for any i.i.d. log-concave random vectors 
\(X_1,\ldots ,X_{n+1}\)
 on 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
 with almost isotropic extension.

                
It may be observed that (
5
) is stronger than the original conjecture of Tao by a factor of 
d
 in front of the logarithmic term. This is because of the almost isotropicity assumption which imposes that the random variables are really 
d
-dimensional. In fact, without this assumption we still have the original statement under log-concavity as shown by the corollary below. Clearly, we may not get the constant 
d
 without such a dimensionality assumption, since we may always embed a one-dimensional vector in 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
 for any 
\(d>1.\)

                  
Corollary 4

                  
For any i.i.d. log-concave random vectors 
\(X_1,\ldots ,X_{n+1}\)
 on 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
$$\begin{aligned} H(X_1+\cdots +X_{n+1}) \ge H(X_1+\cdots +X_{n}) + \frac{1}{2}\log {\Bigl (\frac{n+1}{n}\Bigr )} - o(1). \end{aligned}$$

                    (6)
                
Moreover, we have the rate of convergence 
\(o(1) = O_{d,n}\Bigl ({H(X_1)}{e^{-\frac{1}{d}H(X_1)}}\Bigr )\)
.

                
                  
Proof

                  
Note that
$$\begin{aligned}  &   H(X_1+\cdots +X_{n+1}) - H(X_1+\cdots +X_{n}) \nonumber \\  &   \quad = I(X_1+\cdots +X_{n+1};X_{n+1}) \nonumber \\  &   \quad \ge I(g(X_1+\cdots +X_{n+1});g(X_{n+1})), \end{aligned}$$

                    (7)
                
where 
\(I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X|Y)\)
 denotes the mutual information and the inequality holds for any function 
g
 by the data processing inequality.

                  
Take 
\(g:\mathbb {Z}^d\rightarrow \mathbb {Z}\)
 to be the first-coordinate map. It can be verified that if 
X
 belongs to the class 
\(\mathcal {C}\)
 for some constants, then each coordinate also belongs to the corresponding class in 
\(\mathbb {Z}\)
 with appropriate constants depending on the initial ones. We may then apply the result for 
\(d=1\)
 (where almost isotropicity is satisfied trivially) we obtain the result by (
7
). 
\(\square \)

                
In Subsection 
1.3
, we will explicitly detail the definitions we use for 
discrete log-concavity in 
\(\mathbb {Z}^{d}\)
 as well as the definition of 
almost isotropic extension
. Theorem 
3
 readily follows from the next theorem together with the generalized EPI in 
\(\mathbb {R}^d\)
 and can be interpreted as an approximation result of discrete and continuous entropies in higher dimensions:

                  
Theorem 5

                  
For any i.i.d. random vectors 
\(X_1,\ldots ,X_{n+1}\)
 on 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
 such that their common p.m.f. belongs to the class 
\(\mathcal {C}(c_1,c_2,c_3)\)
, for some absolute constants 
\(c_1, c_2, c_3\)
 that depend on the dimension only,
$$\begin{aligned} h(X_1+\cdots +X_n + U_1+\cdots +U_n) = H(X_1+\cdots +X_n) + o(1), \end{aligned}$$

                    (8)
                
where 
\(o(1) \rightarrow 0\)
 as 
\(H(X_1) \rightarrow \infty \)
, 
h
 stands for the differential entropy, 
H
 denotes the discrete Shannon entropy and 
\(U_1,\ldots ,U_n\)
 are independent continuous uniforms on 
\([0,1]^d\)
. Moreover, we have the rate of convergence 
\(o(1) = O_{d,n}\Bigl ({H(X_1)}{e^{-\frac{1}{d}H(X_1)}}\Bigr )\)
. In particular, (
8
) holds for any i.i.d. log-concave random vectors 
\(X_1,\ldots ,X_{n+1}\)
 on 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
 with almost isotropic extension.

                
                  
Remark 6

                  
Let 
X
 be a log-concave random vector on 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
 with almost isotropic extension. Denote the covariance matrix of 
X
 by 
\(K_X\)
, 
\(\sigma ^2:= \det {(K_X)}^{\frac{1}{d}}\)
 and let 
U
 be a uniform on the open box 
\([0,1]^d\)
. Then
$$\begin{aligned} H(X) = h(X+U) \le \frac{d}{2}\log {\biggl (\det {\left( K_X+\frac{1}{12}I_{\textrm{d}}\right) ^{\frac{1}{d}}}2 \pi e\biggr )} \simeq \frac{d}{2}\log {(\sigma ^2 2 \pi e)} , \end{aligned}$$

                    (9)
                
by the fact that the Gaussian maximizes the entropy under fixed covariance matrix, which implies that for any random variable 
X
 on 
\(\mathbb {R}^d\)
 one has
$$\begin{aligned} h(X)\le \frac{d}{2}\log (2\pi e(\det \textrm{Cov}(X))^{1/d}). \end{aligned}$$

                    (10)
                
Thus, 
\(H(X) \rightarrow \infty \)
 implies 
\(\sigma \rightarrow \infty \)
 and therefore it suffices to prove (
8
) with 
\(o(1) \rightarrow 0\)
 as 
\(\sigma \rightarrow \infty \)
.

                
We prove Theorem 
5
 in Section 
2
. First we use the second property of Definition 
2
, which is an exponential tail bound, in order to control the 
entropy tails
. We use the third property, which is a type of smoothness, to control the error term arising from the approximation of the density of 
\(\sum _i{X_i + U_i}\)
 by the p.m.f. of 
\(\sum _i{X_i}\)
. Combined with the Taylor-type estimate of Lemma 
19
, which is analogous to an estimate used in [
28
], this approximation is the main technical step in the proof of Theorem 
5
. The first property of Definition 
2
 ensures that Lemma 
19
 is applicable.
In Section 
3
 we will show that the assumption that the p.m.f. of 
\(X_1\)
 satisfies these three properties is weaker than almost isotropic log-concavity in the sense that any almost isotropic log-concave p.m.f. on 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
 satisfies these properties with appropriate constants. We remark here that, since our definition of log-concavity reduces to the usual one for the one-dimensional case, Theorems 
3
 and 
5
 are also relaxed versions of the corresponding results obtained in [
10
]. We prove the following theorem, which allows us to establish that if 
p
 is log-concave with almost isotropic extension then 
p
 belongs to the class 
\(\mathcal {C}\left( c_1(d),c_2(d),c_3(d)\right) \)
 for some absolute constants 
\(c_1(d),c_2(d),c_3(d)\)
 that depend on the dimension only (in particular, this theorem is essential for proving item 1 in Theorem 
20
):

                  
Theorem 7

                  
Suppose 
p
 is a log-concave p.m.f. on 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
 with almost isotropic extension and covariance matrix 
\(\textrm{Cov}{(p)}\)
. Then there exists an absolute constant 
\(C^{\prime }\)
 (independent of the dimension), such that
$$ \max _{k \in \mathbb {Z}^d}{p(k)} \le \frac{C^{\prime }}{\det (\textrm{Cov}_{\mathbb {Z}^d}(p))^{\frac{1}{2}}} $$
provided that 
\(\det \left( \textrm{Cov}_{\mathbb {Z}^d}(p)\right) \)
 is large enough depending on 
d
.

                
Theorem 
7
 can be seen as 
d
-dimensional analogue of the following result, due to Bobkov, Marsiglietti and Melbourne [
5
], who also studied discrete versions of the EPI (up to multiplicative constants) for Rényi entropies of log-concave distributions and which is an important tool in the one-dimensional case:

                  
Theorem 8

                  
( [
5
, Theorem 1.1]) If a random variable 
X
 follows a discrete log-concave p.m.f. 
f
 on 
\(\mathbb {Z}\)
, then
$$\begin{aligned} \max _{k \in \mathbb {Z}}f(k) \le \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+4\sigma ^2}}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (11)
                
where 
\(\sigma ^2=\textrm{Var}(X)\)
.

                
Theorem 
7
 may also be seen as a discrete analogue of a dimensional upper bound on the isotropic constant 
\(L_f\)
. The corresponding (continuous) lower bound on 
\(L_f\)
 can be deduced from (
10
) and the fact that 
\(e^{-h(X)}\le \max (f)\)
:
$$ L_f=\left( \max _{\mathbb {R}^d}{f}\right) ^{\frac{1}{d}}\textrm{det}(\textrm{Cov}(f))^{\frac{1}{2d}}\ge e^{-\frac{h(X)}{d}}\textrm{det}(\textrm{Cov}(f))^{\frac{1}{2d}}\ge \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi e}}. $$
It is well known that a dimensional upper bound also exists and we denote by 
\(L_d\)
 the maximum of the isotropic constants 
\(L_f\)
 among log-concave functions 
f
 in 
\(\mathbb {R}^d\)
. The latter is related to the famous hyperplane (or slicing) problem in convex geometry, a longstanding conjecture recently solved in a major breakthrough by Klartag and Lehec [
19
], following decisive progress by Guan [
13
]. A second, independent proof was subsequently given by Bizeul [
4
]. The hyperplane and isotropic constant theorems, formerly known as conjectures, are in fact equivalent, as shown in [
6
, Theorem 3.1.2].

                  
Theorem 9

                  
( [
19
, Theorem 1.1]) There exists a universal constant 
\(c > 0\)
 such that, for any dimension 
d
, any convex body 
\(K \subset \mathbb {R}^d\)
 in isotropic position and of volume 1, and any direction 
\(\theta \in \mathbb {S}^{d-1}\)
, one has
$$\begin{aligned} |K \cap \theta ^{\perp }|_{d-1} \ge c. \end{aligned}$$

                    (12)
                
Equivalently, there exists a universal constant 
\(C > 0\)
 such that
$$\begin{aligned} L_d \le C. \end{aligned}$$

                    (13)
                

                
Henceforth, we will be referring to (
12
) of Theorem 
9
 as the 
Hyperplane Theorem
 and to (
13
) as the 
Isotropic Constant Theorem
.
Our method for proving Theorem 
7
 is to use the corresponding continuous result. To this end, we obtain the approximations
$$\begin{aligned} \Bigl |\int _{\mathbb {R}^d}{f} - \sum _{\mathbb {Z}^d}{f}\Bigr |&= o_{d}(1), \quad \text {as } \sigma \rightarrow \infty , \\ \Bigl |\int _{\mathbb {R}^{d}}xf - \sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^{d}}kf(k)\Bigr |&= O_d(1), \quad \text {as } \sigma \rightarrow \infty , \\ \Bigl |\det (\textrm{Cov}_{\mathbb {Z}^d}{(f)})- \det (\textrm{Cov}_{\mathbb {R}^d}{(f)})\Bigr |&= O_d(\sigma ^{2d-1}) , \quad \text {as } \sigma \rightarrow \infty , \end{aligned}$$
for any isotropic log-concave density 
f
. This is done in Section 
3.4
. Before that, after recalling some know facts about Ball’s bodies in Section 
3.1
, we prove the exponential tails bound in Section 
3.2
. Although our results hold under the more general almost isotropicity assumption, for better illustration of the ideas, we assume first in Sections 
3.1
—
3.4
 that the continuous extension 
f
 is isotropic. In Section 
3.5
 we show how to relax this assumption (see Remark 
38
).
Finally, in Section 
4
 we conclude with a brief discussion of our assumptions as well as open questions regarding fundamental issues of log-concavity in 
\(\mathbb {Z}^{d}\)
.
1.3 
Notations and Definitions
In this subsection, we will detail the notations as well as key definitions.
Big- and small-
O
 
 notation.
 Let 
f
 be a real-valued function and 
g
 another strictly positive function. We write 
\(f = O(g)\)
 if there exist positive absolute constants 
N
, 
C
 such that 
\(|f(x)| \le Cg(x)\)
 for every 
\(x \ge N\)
. If 
N
, 
C
 are absolute up to a parameter 
d
, we write 
\(f = O_d(g)\)
. Analogously, we write 
\(f = \Omega (g)\)
, if 
\(|f(x)| \ge Cg(x)\)
 for every 
\(x \ge N\)
. If 
\(f = \Omega (g)\)
 and 
\(f = O(g)\)

\(f = \Theta (g)\)

\(\Omega _d\)

\(\Theta _d\)

Convex bodies.
 A 
convex body
 is a convex set that is compact and has a non-empty interior. For a convex body 
\(K\in \mathbb {R}^{d}\)
, one denotes by |
K
| its volume. The 
convex hull
 of a set 
A
, denoted by 
\(\mathrm {conv(A)}\)
, is the smallest convex set containing the set 
A
.
Log-concavity and convexity in 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
. For 
\(d>1\)
, there are more than one definitions of log-concavity that have been used in different contexts. We will use a quite general definition that implies several other notions of log-concavity, the one of 
extensible log-concave functions
. The reader is referred to Murota [
24
] for an extensive discussion of discrete convexity in higher dimensions. A function 
\(f:\mathbb {Z}^{d}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}\cup \{+\infty \}\)
 is said to be 
convex-extensible
 if there exists a convex function 
\(\bar{f}:\mathbb {R}^{d}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}\cup \{+\infty \}\)
 such that
$$\begin{aligned} \bar{f}(z)=f(z) \hspace{0.3cm} (\forall z\in \mathbb {Z}^{d}) \hspace{0.1cm} . \end{aligned}$$
We refer to 
\(\bar{f}\)
 as the 
extension
 of 
f
. Based on the above, we define

                  
Definition 10

                  
(Log-concave extensible functions) 
 A function 
\(f:\mathbb {Z}^{d}\rightarrow \mathbb {R_+}\)
 is said to be log-concave extensible if there exists 
\(V:\mathbb {Z}^{d}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}\cup \{+\infty \}\)
 convex-extensible such that
$$\begin{aligned} f(z)=e^{-V(z)}. \end{aligned}$$
Throughout when we say that 
f
 is log-concave, we mean that 
f
 is log-concave extensible. We say that a random vector 
X
, taking values in 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
 and p.m.f. 
\(p:\mathbb {Z}^d \rightarrow [0,1]\)
 is log-concave, if 
p
 is log-concave.

                
Similarly, we define a 
\(\mathbb {Z}^{d}\)
-convex set (commonly referred in the literature as 
hole-free
 set [
24
]):

                  
Definition 11

                  
(
\(\mathbb {Z}^{d}\)
-convexity) A set 
\(A\subset \mathbb {Z}^{d}\)
 is said to be 
\(\mathbb {Z}^{d}\)
-convex if and only if its indicator function 
\(\chi _{A}\)
, defined by 
\(\chi _{A}(x)=1\)
 if 
\(x\in A\)
 and 0 otherwise, is log-concave-extensible or equivalently if
$$\begin{aligned} A=\textrm{conv}(A)\cap \mathbb {Z}^{d} \hspace{0.1cm}. \end{aligned}$$
Notice that the support of a log-concave function 
f
 on 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
 is 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
-convex.

                
                  
Remark 12

                  
For 
\(d = 1\)
, our definition of log-concavity is equivalent to the usual definition 
\(p(k)^2 \ge p(k-1)p(k+1), k \in \mathbb {Z}\)
, which is preserved under convolution (see, e.g., Hoggar). However, for 
\(d > 1\)
, log-concavity may not be preserved in general, as pointed out by Murota (Example 3.15) by considering two log-concave distributions supported on 
\(S_1 = \{(0,0),(1,1)\}\)
 and 
\(S_2 = \{(0,1),(1,0)\}\)
 respectively. This is because the Minkowski sum 
\(S_1 + S_2\)
 is not 
\(\mathbb {Z}^2\)
-convex. On the other hand, if 
\(S_1 = S_2 = S \subset \mathbb {Z}^2\)
 is 
\(\mathbb {Z} ^2\)
-convex, then 
\(S + S\)
 is also 
\(\mathbb {Z}^2\)
-convex (see Corollary 2.54 in [7]). In dimension 
\(d\ge 3\)
 this is not true: consider the Reeve tetrahedron 
\(S = \{(0,0,0),(1,0,0),(0,1,0),(1,1,2)\}\)
. Then S is 
\(\mathbb {Z}^3\)
-convex, but 
\(S + S\)
 does not contain the point (1,1,1) and is thus not 
\(\mathbb {Z}^3\)
-convex.

                
Notion of isotropicity
. The isotropic constant of a function 
\(f:\mathbb {R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb {R}_+\)
 is defined by
$$\begin{aligned} L_f := \left( \frac{\max _{\mathbb {R}^d}{f}}{\int _{\mathbb {R}^d}{f}}\right) ^{\frac{1}{d}}\textrm{det}(\textrm{Cov}(f))^{\frac{1}{2d}}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (14)
                
where 
\(\textrm{Cov}{(f)}\)
 is the 
inertia
 or 
covariance
 matrix defined, for 
\(1 \le i,j \le d\)
, by
$$\begin{aligned} [\textrm{Cov}{(f)}]_{ij}:=\frac{\int _{\mathbb {R}^{d}}x_{i}x_{j}f(x)dx}{\int _{\mathbb {R}^ {d}}f(x)dx}-\frac{\int _{\mathbb {R}^{d}}x_{i}f(x)dx\int _{\mathbb {R}^{d}}x_{j}f(x)dx}{\Bigl (\int _{\mathbb {R}^{d}}f(x)dx\Bigr )^2} \hspace{0.1cm} . \end{aligned}$$
We say that 
f
 is 
isotropic
 if 
\(\textrm{Cov}{(f)} = \sigma ^2\textrm{I}_d\)
, for some 
\(\sigma >0,\)
 where 
\(\textrm{I}_d\)
 is the 
\(d \times d\)
 identity matrix. Let 
K
 be a convex body in 
\(\mathbb {R}^{d}\)
. Then its covariance matrix is 
\(\textrm{Cov}(K):=\textrm{Cov}(\mathbbm {1}_K)\)
 and its isotropic constant is 
\(L_K:=L_{\mathbbm {1}_K}\)
. The convex body 
K
 is called 
isotropic
 if 
\(\mathbbm {1}_K\)
 is isotropic.
Similarly, in the discrete case 
\(p:\mathbb {Z}^d \rightarrow \mathbb {R}_+\)
, we define the covariance matrix 
\(\textrm{Cov}{(p)}\)
 by
$$\begin{aligned} [\textrm{Cov}{(p)}]_{ij}:=\frac{\sum _{k \in \mathbb {Z}^d}k_{i}k_{j}p(k)}{\sum _k{p(k)}}-\frac{\sum _{k \in \mathbb {Z}^{d}}k_{i}p(k)\sum _{k \in \mathbb {Z}^{d}}k_{j}p(k)}{\Bigl (\sum _{k \in \mathbb {Z}^{d}}p(k)\Bigr )^2}. \end{aligned}$$

                  
Definition 13

                  
A family 
\(\{f_{\sigma }\}_{\sigma \in \mathbb {R}_+}\)
 of non-negative functions on 
\(\mathbb {R}^d\)
 is 
almost isotropic
 if, as 
\(\sigma \rightarrow \infty \)
,
$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{Cov}{(f_{\sigma })}_{i,j}&= \sigma ^2 + O(\sigma ) \quad \text {for } i=j, \\&= O(\sigma ) \quad \text {for } i \ne j. \end{aligned}$$

                
                  
Remark 14

                  
It is straightforward to check that the family 
\(\{f_{\sigma }\}_{\sigma \in \mathbb {R}_+}\)
 is 
almost isotropic
 if and only if
$$\begin{aligned} ||\textrm{Cov}{(f_{\sigma })}-\sigma ^2\textrm{I}_d||_{\textrm{op}} =O(\sigma ), \end{aligned}$$
where 
\(||A||_{\textrm{op}}\)
 is the operator norm.

                
We are interested in log-concave densities 
f
, for which 
\(\det {\textrm{Cov}{(f)}} \rightarrow \infty \)
. Thus, when we write that 
f
 is almost isotropic, it is meant that 
f
 represents a family of densities with the underlying parameter 
\(\sigma := \det {\textrm{Cov}{(f)}}^{\frac{1}{2d}}\)
 growing large. When its not clear from the context, we write 
\(\textrm{Cov}_{\mathbb {R}}(f)\)
 and 
\(\textrm{Cov}_{\mathbb {Z}}(f)\)
 to distinguish between the continuous and discrete covariance matrix of 
f
.

,
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{j\in \{0,\ldots , n-1\}^{d}}h_{n}^{(d)}(j+z) \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} =\sum _{j\in \{0,\ldots , n-1\}^{d}}\left( \prod _{i=1}^{d}h_{n}(z_{i}+j_{i})\right) =\prod _{i=1}^{d}\left( \sum _{j_i=0}^{n-1}h_{n}(z_{i}+j_{i})\right) =1, \end{aligned}$$
where, for the last equality, we have used the above one-dimensional argument.

                
Let us prove the second item. Using item 1, one has
$$\begin{aligned} f_{n}^{d}(x)-p(k)=f_{n}^{(d)}(k+z)-p(k)=\sum _{i\in \mathbb {Z}^d} p(k-i)h_{n}^{(d)}(i+z)-p(k)=\sum _{i\in \mathbb {Z}^{d}}u(i)v(i), \end{aligned}$$

                    (27)
                
where 
\(u(i)=p(k-i)-p(k)\)
, 
\(v(i)=h_{n}^{(d)}(z+i)\)
. Note that the sum actually ranges over 
\(i \in \{0,\ldots ,n-1\}^d\)
, since 
\(h_n^{(d)}(i+z) = 0\)
 outside this discrete cube. Also, for 
\(i=(i_1,\dots ,i_d)\in \mathbb {Z}^{d}\)
, denote
$$\begin{aligned} W(i):=\left( T(v)\right) _{i}=\sum _{j\ge i} v_{j}, \end{aligned}$$
where 
\(j\ge i\)
 means that 
\(j_{l}\ge i_{l}\)
 for all 
\(1\le l \le d\)
. By a straightforward induction,
$$\begin{aligned} v(i)=(T^{-1}(W))_{i}=\sum _{\epsilon \in \{-1,1\}^{d}}\left( \prod _{j=1}^{d}\left( -\epsilon _{j}\right) \right) W\left( i+\sum _{k=1}^{d}\frac{\epsilon _{k}+1}{2}e_{k}\right) . \end{aligned}$$
Let us introduce the standard dot product in 
\(l^{2}(\mathbb {Z})\)
 as 
\(\langle z,h \rangle _{l^{2}(\mathbb {Z})}:=\sum _{i\in \mathbb {Z}^{d}}z(i)h(i) \)
 for any 
\(z,h\in \mathbb {R}^{\mathbb {Z}^{d}}\)
. Then the sum 
\(\sum _{i\in \mathbb {Z}^{d}}u(i)v(i)\)
 can be rewritten as 
\(\langle u,T^{-1}W \rangle _{l^{2}(\mathbb {Z})}\)
 with
$$T^{-1}:=\sum _{\epsilon \in \{-1,1\}^{d}}\left( \prod _{j=1}^{d}(-\epsilon _{j})\right) S_{\epsilon } , $$
where 
\(S_{\epsilon }\)
 is the left-shift operator, that is, 
\(\left( S_{\epsilon }W\right) _{i}=W\left( i+\sum _{k=1}^{d}\frac{\epsilon _{k}+1}{2}e_{k}\right) \)
. It is well known that the adjoint 
\(S_{\epsilon }^{\star }\)
 of 
\(S_{\epsilon }\)
, is the right-shift operator or in another words 
\((S_{\epsilon }^{\star }(W))_{i}=W(i-\sum _{k=1}^{d}\frac{\epsilon _{k}+1}{2}e_{k})\)
. Thus
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{i\in \mathbb {Z}^{d}}u(i)v(i)&=\sum _{i\in \mathbb {Z}^{d}}\sum _{\epsilon \in \{-1,1\}^{d}}u_{i}\left( \prod _{j=1}^{d}\left( -\epsilon _{j}\right) \right) W\left( i+\sum _{k=1}^{d}\frac{\epsilon _{k}+1}{2}e_{k}\right) \\&=\left\langle u,T^{-1}(W)\right\rangle _{l^{2}(\mathbb {Z})}= \left\langle (T^{-1})^{\star }u, W \right\rangle _{l^{2}(\mathbb {Z})}\\&=\sum _{i\in \mathbb {Z}^{d}}W(i)\sum _{\epsilon \in \{-1,1\}^{d}} \left( \prod _{j=1}^{d} (-\epsilon _{j})\right) u\left( i-\sum _{k=1}^{d}\frac{\epsilon _{k}+1}{2}e_{k}\right) \end{aligned}$$
Thus, by the triangular inequality, summing up to the last coordinate which can be either 1 or 
\(-1\)
, we obtain
$$\begin{aligned} \left| \sum _{i\in \mathbb {Z}^{d}} u(i)v(i)\right|&\le \sum _{i\in \mathbb {Z}^{d}} \sum _{\epsilon \in \{-1,1\}^{d-1}} \left| u\left( i-\sum _{k=1}^{d}\frac{\epsilon _{k}+1}{2}e_{k}\right) -u\left( i-\sum _{k=1}^{d-1}\frac{\epsilon _{k}+1}{2}e_{k}-e_{d}\right) \right| \\&\le 2^{d-1}\sum _{i\in \mathbb {Z}^{d}}\left| u(i)-u(i-e_{d})\right| . \end{aligned}$$
Using the same argument for 
\(j\in \{1,\ldots ,d\}\)
, instead of 
\(j=d\)
 and summing over 
j
 one gets
$$\left| f_{n}(x)-p(k) \right| =\left| \sum _{i\in \mathbb {Z}^{d}}u(i)v(i) \right| \le \frac{2^{d-1}}{d}\sum _{i\in \mathbb {Z}^{d}}\sum _{j=1}^{d}\left| p(i)-p(i+e_{j})\right| \le \frac{2^{d-1}}{d}\frac{c_{3}}{\sigma }, $$
where, in the last inequality, we have used that 
X
 satisfies condition 3 of Definition 
2
.

                
Finally, for item 3, we have by (
27
), since 
\(|h_n| \le 1\)
,
$$\begin{aligned} |f_n^{(d)}(x) - p(k)|&= \biggl |\sum _{i \in \{0,\ldots ,n-1\}^d}{(p(k-i) - p(k))h_n(z+i)}\biggr | \\&\le \sum _{i \in \{0,\ldots ,n-1\}^d}{p(k-i)} + n^dp(k). \end{aligned}$$
But, using hypothesis 2, the assumption 
\(\Vert k\Vert _{\infty } \ge c_2\sigma + n\)
 and the observations that 
\(\Vert k-i\Vert _{\infty } \ge \Vert k\Vert _{\infty } - n \ge c_2\sigma \)
 and 
\(\Vert k-i\Vert _1 \ge \Vert k\Vert _1 - nd,\)
 we have
$$\begin{aligned} |f_n^{(d)}(x) - p(k)|&\le n^d\frac{c_2}{\sigma ^d}e^{-\frac{(\Vert k\Vert _1 - nd)}{c_2\sigma }} + n^d\frac{c_2}{\sigma ^d}e^{-\frac{\Vert k\Vert _1}{c_2\sigma }} \end{aligned}$$
and the claimed bound follows since 
\(f_n^{(d)}(x) \le |f_n^{(d)}(x) - p(k)| + p(k)\)
. 
\(\square \)

Suppose

1.:

3.1 
Ball’s Bodies
In what follows, we set 
\(\omega _{d}\)
 to be the volume of the unit Euclidean ball denoted by 
\(B_{2}^{d}\)

\(K\in \mathbb {R}^{d}\)
 with 0 in its interior, its 
inradius
 
r
(
K

\(r>0\)
 for which 
\(rB_{2}^{d}\subset K\)
 and its 
circumradius
 
R
(
K
) is defined as the smallest 
\(R>0\)
 such that 
\(K\subset RB_{2}^{d}\)
. The support function of 
K
 is defined as 
\(h_{K}(x)=\max _{y\in K}\langle x,y \rangle \)
 and the radial function of 
K
 is 
\(\rho _K(x)=\inf \{t>0;\ tx\in K\}=1/\Vert x\Vert _K\)

\(\Vert x\Vert _K\)
 denotes the gauge associated to

For most results in this section we will assume that 
\(f:\mathbb {R}^{d}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}\)

\( \int _{\mathbb {R}^{d}} f=1\)
, 
\( \int _{\mathbb {R}^{d}} xf=0\)
 and 
\(\int _{\mathbb {R}^{d}}x^Txf=\sigma ^{2}\textrm{I}_d. \)
 To this function

Definition 21

                  
Let 
\(f:\mathbb {R}^{d}\rightarrow [0,\infty )\)
 be an integrable, centered log-concave function. For any 
\(p>0\)
, the set 
\(K_{p}(f)\)
 is defined as follows
$$\begin{aligned} K_{p}(f):=\left\{ x\in \mathbb {R}^{d}: \int _{0}^{\infty } pr^{p-1}f(rx)dr\ge f(0)\right\} . \end{aligned}$$
Ball [
2
] established that the set 
\(K_p(f)\)
 is a convex body. Moreover, its radial function is
$$\begin{aligned} \rho _{K_{p}(f)}(x)=\Bigl (\frac{1}{f(0)}\int _{0}^{\infty }pr^{p-1}f(rx)dr\Bigr )^{\frac{1}{p}} \hspace{0.2cm} \text {for} \hspace{0.2cm} x\ne 0. \end{aligned}$$
From integration in polar coordinates (see [
6
, Proposition 2.5.3]), it follows that 
\(K_{d+1}(f)\)
 is centered and, for any 
\(p\ge 0\)
 and 
\(u\in \mathbb {S}^{d-1}\)
,
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{K_{d+p}(f)}|\langle x,u\rangle |^pdx=\frac{1}{f(0)}\int _{\mathbb {R}^d}|\langle x,u\rangle |^pf(x)dx. \end{aligned}$$

                    (42)
                
Finally, the following inclusion relations between 
\(K_{p}(f)\)
 and 
\(K_{q}(f)\)
 hold [
6
, Proposition 2.5.7], for 
\(p<q\)
,
$$ \frac{\Gamma (p+1)^{\frac{1}{p}}}{\Gamma (q+1)^{\frac{1}{q}}}K_{q}(f)\subset K_{p}(f)\subset e^{\frac{d}{p}-\frac{d}{q}} K_{q}(f). $$
Applying these inclusions first for 
\(p=d\)
 and 
\(q=d+1\)
 then for 
\(p=d+1\)
 and 
\(q=d+2\)
 and using classical inequalities on Gamma functions, it follows that there exist universal constants 
\(0<c_1<c_2\)
 such that for any dimension 
\(d\ge 1\)
, one has
$$\begin{aligned} c_1 K_{d+1}(f)\subset K_{d}(f)\subset c_2 K_{d+1}(f) \quad \hbox {and}\quad c_1 K_{d+2}(f)\subset K_{d+1}(f)\subset c_2 K_{d+2}(f). \end{aligned}$$

                    (43)
                

                
We shall apply to 
\(K_{d+1}(f)\)

, Theorem 4.1] to which we give a new and simple proof.

                  
Theorem 22

                  
Let 
K
 be a centered convex body in 
\(\mathbb {R}^d\)
 and 
\(u\in \mathbb {S}^{d-1}\)
. Then
$$\begin{aligned} \frac{h_K(u)^2}{d(d+2)}\le \frac{1}{|K|}\int _K\langle x,u\rangle ^2dx=\langle \textrm{Cov}(K)u,u\rangle \le \frac{d}{d+2}h_K(u)^2. \end{aligned}$$

                    (44)
                

                
                  
Proof

                  
Let 
\(f(t)=|\{x\in K; \langle x,u\rangle =t\}\)
. Denote by 
\([-b,a]\)
 its support. Then one has 
\(a=h_K(u)\)
,
$$ \int _\mathbb {R}f(t)dt=|K|, \quad \int _\mathbb {R}tf(t)dt=\int _K\langle x,u\rangle dx\quad \hbox {and}\quad \int _\mathbb {R}t^2f(t)dt=\int _K\langle x,u\rangle ^2 dx. $$
We define 
\(g:\mathbb {R}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}_+\)
 by
$$ g(t)=\alpha \left( 1+\frac{t}{da}\right) ^{d-1}\mathbbm {1}_{[-da,a]}(t),\quad \hbox {where}\ \alpha =\frac{1}{a\left( 1+\frac{1}{d}\right) ^d}\int _\mathbb {R}f(t)dt. $$
And let 
\(h:\mathbb {R}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}_+\)
 be defined by 
\(h(t)=dh(-dt)\)
. Then, it is not difficult to see that
$$\begin{aligned} \int _\mathbb {R}g(t)dt= \int _\mathbb {R}h(t)dt=\int _\mathbb {R}f(t)dt \quad \hbox {and}\quad \int _\mathbb {R}tg(t)dt= \int _\mathbb {R}th(t)dt=\int _\mathbb {R}tf(t)dt=0. \end{aligned}$$

                    (45)
                
We shall prove that
$$ \int _\mathbb {R}t^2g(t)dt\le \int _\mathbb {R}t^2f(t)dt \le \int _\mathbb {R}t^2h(t)dt, $$
from which the inequality (
44
) follows immediately by a simple calculation. If 
\(f\ne g\)
, it follows from (
45
) that there exist at least two points 
\(t_1(g)<t_2(g)\)
 such that 
\(g-f\)
 changes sign at these points and, in the same way, if 
\(f\ne h\)
 there exists at least two points 
\(t_1(h)<t_2(h)\)
 such that 
\(f-h\)
 changes sign at these points. Since the function 
\(f^{1/(d-1)}\)
 is concave on its support 
\([-b,a]\)
 and the functions 
\(g^{1/(d-1)}\)
 and 
\(h^{1/(d-1)}\)
 are affine on their respective supports 
\([-da,a]\)
 and 
\([-\frac{a}{d},a]\)
, it follows that the functions 
\(g-f\)
 and 
\(f-h\)
 have exactly two sign changes and that they are non positive on 
\([t_1,t_2]\)
 and non negative outside 
\([t_1,t_2]\)
. Let us define 
v
 to be either 
\(g-f\)
 or 
\(f-h\)
. Then, for any 
\(t\in \mathbb {R}\)
, one has 
\((t-t_1)(t-t_2)v(t)\ge 0\)
. Integrating this inequality, we deduce that 
\(\int t^2v(t)dt\ge 0\)
, which is the result. 
\(\square \)

                
We shall deduce from the preceding theorem the following important technical lemma. We note that Lemma 
23
 (and its consequences) is the only part, where the isotropicity assumption is used.

                  
Lemma 23

                  
Let 
\(d\ge 1\)
 be an integer. There exist two constants 
\(0<C_d'<C_d\)
 such that for any 
\(f:\mathbb {R}^{d}\rightarrow \mathbb {R_+}\)
 centered, isotropic, log-concave density and for every 
\(\theta \in \mathbb {S}^{d-1}\)
,
$$\begin{aligned} C_{d}^{\prime }\le \left( \int _{0}^{\infty } dr^{d-1}f(r\theta )dr\right) ^\frac{1}{d}\le C_{d} , \end{aligned}$$

                    (46)
                
where 
\(C_{d}\)
 and 
\(C_{d}^{\prime }\)
 are constants depending only on the dimension 
d
. In fact, we may take
$$\begin{aligned} C_{d}^{\prime }=\frac{c_1^{d+2}}{\sqrt{2\pi } e^\frac{3}{2}} \quad \hbox {and}\quad C_{d}=(d+1)c_2^{d+2}L_d, \end{aligned}$$

                    (47)
                
where 
\(c_1\)
 and 
\(c_2\)
 are the absolute constants appearing in (
43
).

                
                  
Proof

                  
The function 
f
 being isotropic, we have 
\(\textrm{Cov}(f)=\sigma ^2I_d\)
, for some 
\(\sigma >0\)
 and 
\(\int f=1\)
, thus 
\(L_f=\max (f)^\frac{1}{d}\sigma \)
. Note that proving (
46
) is equivalent to proving that
$$ C_d'B_2^d\subset f(0)^\frac{1}{d}K_{d}(f)\subset C_dB_{2}^{d}. $$
Applying Theorem 
22
 to the centered body 
\(K_{d+1}(f)\)
, we get that for any 
\(u\in \mathbb {S}^{d-1}\)
,
$$\begin{aligned} \frac{h_{K_{d+1}(f)}(u)^2}{d(d+2)}\le \frac{1}{|K_{d+1}(f)|}\int _{K_{d+1}(f)}\langle x,u\rangle ^2dx\le \frac{d}{d+2}h_{K_{d+1}(f)}(u)^2. \end{aligned}$$

                    (48)
                
Then, we use (
42
) for 
\(p=2\)
 to get that
$$ \int _{K_{d+2}(f)}\langle x,u\rangle ^2dx=\frac{1}{f(0)}\int _{\mathbb {R}^d}\langle x,u\rangle ^2f(x)dx=\frac{\sigma ^2}{f(0)}. $$
This gives
$$ c_1^{d+2}\frac{\sigma ^2}{f(0)}\le \int _{K_{d+1}(f)}\langle x,u\rangle ^2dx\le c_2^{d+2}\frac{\sigma ^2}{f(0)}. $$
Using again the inclusion relations (
43
), we get upper and lower bound of 
\(|K_{d+1}(f)|\)
 by 
\(|K_{d}(f)|=1/f(0)\)
, by (
42
) for 
\(p=0\)
. Thus, altogether we have
$$ c_1^{2d+2}\sigma ^2\le \frac{1}{|K_{d+1}(f)|}\int _{K_{d+1}(f)}\langle x,u\rangle ^2dx\le c_2^{2d+2}\sigma ^2. $$
Using these inequalities, (
48
) and the inclusion relations (
43
), we get
$$ \sqrt{\frac{d+2}{d}}c_1^{d+2} \sigma B_2^d\subset K_d(f)\subset \sqrt{d(d+2)}c_2^{d+2} \sigma B_2^d. $$
From [
8
, Theorem 4], we have 
\(f(0)\ge e^{-d}\max (f)\)
 hence
$$ \frac{L_f}{e}\le f(0)^\frac{1}{d}\sigma =\left( \frac{f(0)}{\max (f)}\right) ^\frac{1}{d}L_f\le L_d. $$
Using that 
\(L_f\ge 1/\sqrt{2\pi e}\)
, we conclude.

                  
\(\square \)

                
3.2 
A Concentration Lemma
The following concentration lemma will be required to establish the concentration property 2.

                  
Lemma 24

                  
(Concentration Lemma) Let 
\(c_{d}:=3^{\frac{1}{d}}C_d\)
, where 
\(C_d\)
 is the constant from (
47
). Then, for every log-concave, isotropic, centered density function 
f
 and for every 
\(x \in \mathbb {R}^d\)
 such that 
\(\Vert x\Vert _2\ge c_{d}/f(0)^{\frac{1}{d}}\)
,
$$\begin{aligned} f(x) \le f(0) 2^{-\Vert x\Vert _2\frac{f(0)^{\frac{1}{d}}}{c_d}}. \end{aligned}$$

                    (49)
                

                
                  
Proof

                  
Let 
\(x_{\max }\in \mathbb {R}^{d}\)
 be a value where the maximum of 
f
 is attained and for every 
\(\theta \in \mathbb {S}^{d-1}\)
, let 
\(r_{\max }(\theta )\in \mathbb {R}^{d}\)
 be a value where the maximum of 
\(r\mapsto f(r\theta )\)
 is attained. Since 
f
 is log-concave, for every 
\(\theta \in \mathbb {S}^{d-1}\)
, the function 
\(r\mapsto f(r \theta )\)
 is non-increasing for 
\(r \ge r_{\max }(\theta )\)
. Therefore, for every 
\(r_0(\theta )\ge r_{\max }(\theta )\)
,
$$\begin{aligned} \int _0^{\infty }dr^{d-1}f(r\theta )dr \ge \int _{r_{\max }(\theta )}^{r_{0}(\theta )}dr^{d-1}f(r\theta )dr \ge f(r_0(\theta ))(r_0(\theta )^d-r_{\max }(\theta )^{d}). \end{aligned}$$

                    (50)
                
We choose 
\(r_{0} = r_{0}(\theta )\)
 as
$$\begin{aligned} r_0(\theta )= \left( 2d\int _0^{\infty }{\frac{r^{d-1}f(r\theta )}{f(0)}dr}+r_{\max }^{d}\right) ^{\frac{1}{d}} \hspace{0.1cm}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (51)
                
so that, by (
50
), one has 
\(f(r_0(\theta )) \le \frac{f(0)}{2}\)
. Since 
\(r\mapsto f(r \theta )\)
 is non-decreasing on 
\([0,r_{\max }(\theta )]\)
, one has
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{0}^{+\infty } dr^{d-1}f(r\theta )dr\ge \int _{0}^{r_{\max }(\theta )}dr^{d-1}f(0)dr =f(0)r_{\max }(\theta )^{d}, \end{aligned}$$
and therefore, by Lemma 
23
,
$$\begin{aligned} r_{\max }(\theta )\le \frac{\big (d\int _{0}^{+\infty }r^{d-1}f(r\theta )dr\big )^{\frac{1}{d}}}{f(0)^{\frac{1}{d}}} \le \frac{C_{d}}{f(0)^{\frac{1}{d}}}, \end{aligned}$$
where 
\(C_{d}\)
 is given by (
47
). Thus, by (
51
) and the definition of 
\(c_d\)
,
$$\begin{aligned} r_{0}(\theta )\le \left( \frac{3}{f(0)}\right) ^{\frac{1}{d}}C_d=\frac{c_d}{f(0)^{\frac{1}{d}}}. \end{aligned}$$
Since for every 
\(r \ge r_0(\theta )\)
, one has 
\(r_{0}(\theta )=\frac{r_{0}(\theta )}{r}\cdot r+(1-\frac{r_{0}(\theta )}{r})\cdot 0\)
, by log-concavity, we deduce
$$\begin{aligned} f(r) \le f(0)\left( \frac{f(r_0(\theta ))}{f(0)}\right) ^{\frac{r}{r_0(\theta )}} \le f(0)2^{-{\frac{r}{r_0(\theta )}}}\le f(0)2^{-{\frac{rf(0)^{\frac{1}{d}}}{c_d}}} . \end{aligned}$$
Hence, if 
\(\Vert x\Vert _2\ge c_{d}/f(0)^{\frac{1}{d}}\)
, then
$$\begin{aligned} f(x) \le f(0) 2^{-\Vert x\Vert _2\frac{f(0)^{\frac{1}{d}}}{c_d}}. \end{aligned}$$
\(\square \)

                
3.3 
Sum of Maxima of Isotropic Log-Concave Functions
The following lemma bounds the sum of the maxima of a log-concave density using our previous concentration result.

                  
Lemma 25

                  
Let 
f
 be a centered, isotropic, log-concave density on 
\(\mathbb {R}^d\)
 with covariance 
\(\sigma ^2 \textrm{I}_{\textrm{d}}\)
. Let 
\(0\le i\le 2\)
 and 
\(0\le j\le d-1\)
. Then, as 
\(\sigma \rightarrow \infty \)
,
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{l\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-j}}|l_1|^i\max _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^j}f(k_1,\dots ,k_j,l_1,\dots ,l_{d-j})={O_d}\Bigl (\frac{1}{\sigma ^{j-i}}\Bigr ). \end{aligned}$$

                    (52)
                

                
                  
Proof

                  
Set 
\(\lambda =c_df(0)^{-1/d}\)
, with 
\(c_d\)
 is given in Lemma 
24
. Then 
\(f(x)\le f(0)2^{-\Vert x\Vert _2/\lambda }\)
, for 
\(\Vert x\Vert _2\ge \lambda \)
. Moreover, from the definition of 
\(L_f\)
, its bounds and the inequality 
\(f(0)\ge e^{-d}\max (f)\)
, we have
$$ \frac{c_d\sigma }{L_d}\le \lambda =c_df(0)^{-1/d}\le \frac{c_de\sigma }{L_f}\le c_d\sqrt{2\pi e^3}\sigma . $$
Note that
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{l\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-j}}|l_1|^i\max _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^j}f(k,l)=\sum _{l\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-j},\Vert l\Vert _{\infty } \le \lambda }|l_1|^i\max _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^j}f(k,l)+\sum _{l\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-j},\Vert l\Vert _{\infty }>\lambda }|l_1|^i \max _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^j}f(k,l) , \end{aligned}$$
The first sum is easily upper bounded by 
\({O_d}(\frac{1}{\sigma ^{j-i}})\)
. Indeed,
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{l\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-j},\Vert l\Vert _{\infty } \le \lambda }|l_1|^i\max _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^j}f(k,l)\le &   (\max f) (2\lambda )^{d+i-j}=\frac{L_f^d}{\sigma ^d}(2\lambda )^{d+i-j} \\\le &   \frac{L_d^d(2c_d\sqrt{2\pi e^3})^{d+i-j}}{\sigma ^{j-i}} ={O_d}\left( \frac{1}{\sigma ^{j-i}}\right) . \end{aligned}$$
Using the tails estimates of Lemma 
24
 and the fact that for 
\(l\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-j}\)
, one has 
\(\Vert l\Vert _2\ge \Vert l\Vert _\infty \)
 and 
\(\Vert l\Vert _2\ge \frac{\Vert l\Vert _1}{\sqrt{d-j}}\ge \frac{\Vert l\Vert _1}{\sqrt{d}} \)
 the second sum can be expressed as follows
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{l\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-j},\Vert l\Vert _{\infty }>\lambda }|l_1|^i\max _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^j}f(k,l)&\le f(0) \sum _{l\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-j},\Vert l\Vert _{\infty }>\lambda }|l_1|^i2^{-\frac{\Vert l\Vert _1}{\lambda \sqrt{d}}}\\&= f(0)\left( \sum _{|l_1|>\lambda }|l_1|^{i}2^{-\frac{|l_1|}{\lambda \sqrt{d}}}\right) \left( \sum _{|n|>\lambda }2^{-\frac{|n|}{\lambda \sqrt{d}}}\right) ^{d-j-1}. \end{aligned}$$
Putting 
\(x=2^{-1/\lambda \sqrt{d}}\)
 and assuming that 
\(\lambda \sqrt{d}\ge 1\)
, we have 
\(x\in [\frac{1}{2},1)\)
 and 
\(\frac{1}{2}\le \lambda \sqrt{d}(1-x)\le \ln (2)\)
. Thus
$$ \sum _{|n|>\lambda }2^{-\frac{|n|}{\lambda \sqrt{d}}}=2\sum _{n>\lambda }x^{n}\le \frac{2}{1-x}\le 4\lambda \sqrt{d}. $$
Hence, for 
\(i=0\)
, replacing 
\(\lambda \)
 by its value, for 
\(\sigma \ge \frac{L_d}{\sqrt{d}c_d}\)
, we have 
\(\lambda \ge 1/\sqrt{d}\)
 and thus
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{l\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-j},\Vert l\Vert _{\infty }>\lambda }\max _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^j}f(k,l)\le &   f(0)\left( 4\lambda \sqrt{d}\right) ^{d-j} =\left( 4\sqrt{d}c_d\right) ^{d-j}f(0)^\frac{j}{d} \\\le &   \frac{(4\sqrt{d}c_d)^{d-j}L_d^j}{\sigma ^j}={O_d}\left( \frac{1}{\sigma ^j}\right) . \end{aligned}$$
And, for 
\(i=1\)
, it is not difficult to see that, for 
\(\lambda \ge 1/\sqrt{d}\)
, we have
$$ \sum _{|l_1|>\lambda }|l_1|2^{-\frac{|l_1|}{\lambda \sqrt{d}}}=2\sum _{n>\lambda }nx^n=2x\left( \sum _{n>\lambda }x^n\right) '\le \frac{6}{(1-x)^2}\le 24d\lambda ^2. $$
Thus, again for 
\(\sigma \ge \frac{L_d}{\sqrt{d}c_d}\)
, which ensures that 
\(\lambda \ge 1/\sqrt{d}\)
, we deduce
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{l\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-j},\Vert l\Vert _{\infty }>\lambda }|l_1|\max _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^j}f(k,l)\le &   f(0)\times 24d\lambda ^2\left( 4\lambda \sqrt{d}\right) ^{d-j-1}\le 2f(0)\left( 4\lambda \sqrt{d}\right) ^{d-j+1}\\= &   {O_d}\left( \frac{1}{\sigma ^{j-1}}\right) . \end{aligned}$$
In the same way, for 
\(i=2\)
, we easily get that, for 
\(\lambda \ge 1/\sqrt{d}\)
,
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{|l_1|>\lambda }l_1^22^{-\frac{|l_1|}{\lambda \sqrt{d}}}= &   2\sum _{n>\lambda }n^2x^n=2x^2\left( \sum _{n>\lambda }x^n\right) ''+2\sum _{n>\lambda }nx^n\le \frac{14}{(1-x)^3}+\frac{12}{(1-x)^2}\\\le &   26(2\lambda \sqrt{d})^3. \end{aligned}$$
Thus, again for 
\(\sigma \ge \frac{L_d}{\sqrt{d}c_d}\)
, which ensures that 
\(\lambda \ge 1/\sqrt{d}\)
, we deduce
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{l\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-j},\Vert l\Vert _{\infty }>\lambda }l_1^2\max _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^j}f(k,l)\le &   f(0)\times 26(2\lambda \sqrt{d})^3\left( 4\lambda \sqrt{d}\right) ^{d-j-1}\\\le &   4f(0)\left( 4\lambda \sqrt{d}\right) ^{d-j+2}={O_d}\left( \frac{1}{\sigma ^{j-2}}\right) . \end{aligned}$$
\(\square \)

                
3.4 
Approximating the Integral, Mean and Covariance Discretely
In this section, we will approximate the isotropic constant of an isotropic, log-concave density 
\(f\in \mathbb {R}^{d}\)
 in a discrete way. To do so, we will first approximate the integral as a sum and subsequently approximate the continuous covariance of 
f
 by its discrete covariance.

                  
Proposition 26

                  
Let 
\(f: \mathbb {R}^{d} \rightarrow \mathbb {R}\)
 be a log-concave isotropic density function with covariance matrix of the form 
\(\sigma ^2 \textrm{I}_{\textrm{d}}\)
. Then
$$\begin{aligned} \left| \int _{\mathbb {R}^{d}} fdx-\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^{d}}f(k)\right| ={O_d}\Bigl (\frac{1}{\sigma }\Bigr ) \hspace{0.1cm} . \end{aligned}$$

                
                  
Proof

                  
Let us proceed by induction. Let us first consider the one dimensional base case. Let 
\(f:\mathbb {R}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}\)
 be a log-concave function and let suppose that the maximum of 
f
 is attained at 
\(x_{0}\in \mathbb {R}\)
. Let 
\(k_{0}\in \mathbb {Z}\)
 such that 
\(k_{0}\le x_{0}< k_{0}+1\)
. Then, one has
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}} fdx&=\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}} \int _{k}^{k+1}f(x)dx \\&\ge \sum _{k<k_{0}}f(k)+\sum _{k\ge k_{0}+1}f(k+1)+\min \{f(k_{0}),f(k_{0}+1)\}\\&=\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}} f(k)-f(k_{0})-f(k_{0}+1)+\min \{f(k_{0}),f(k_{0}+1)\} \\&=\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}} f(k)-\max \{f(k_{0}),f(k_{0}+1)\} \\&= \sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}} f(k)-\max _{\mathbb {Z}} f. \end{aligned}$$
The reverse inequality is analogous and thus one gets
$$\begin{aligned} \left| \int _{\mathbb {R}}fdx-\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}}f(k)\right| \le \max _{\mathbb {Z}} f\le \max _{\mathbb {R}} f. \end{aligned}$$

                    (53)
                
For 
\(d=1\)
, we get 
\(\max _{\mathbb {R}}(f)=\frac{L_f}{\sigma }\le \frac{1}{\sigma }\)
, by [
9
], which proves the base case. Notice that the inequality (
53
) holds as soon as 
f
 is quasi-concave on 
\(\mathbb {R}\)
, i.e. if 
f
 is first non-decreasing then non-increasing and not necessarily a density.

                  
Let 
\(x\in \mathbb {R}\)
, 
\(y\in \mathbb {R}^{d-1}\)
 and let 
\(F(y):=\int _{\mathbb {R}}f(x,y)dx\)
. Note that 
F
 is log-concave as the marginal of a log-concave function. Moreover 
F
 is an isotropic density and 
\(\textrm{Cov}_{\mathbb {R}^{d-1}}(F)=\sigma ^2 I_{d-1}\)
. One has
$$\begin{aligned} \left| \int _{\mathbb {R}^{d}} f(x,y)dxdy-\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^{d}}f(k)\right|\le &   \left| \int _{\mathbb {R}^{d-1}} F(y)dy-\sum _{y\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}}F(y)\right| \\  &   \quad +\sum _{y\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}}\left| \int _\mathbb {R}f(x,y)dx-\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}}f(k,y)\right| . \end{aligned}$$
By the inductive hypothesis the first term is upper bounded by 
\(C_{d-1}/\sigma \)
 for some constant 
\(C_{d-1}>0\)
. Since for every 
\(y\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}\)
, the function 
\(x\mapsto f(x,y)\)
 is quasi-concave, one may use (
53
) to get that the second term is upper bounded by
$$ \sum _{y\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}}\max _{x\in \mathbb {Z}} f(x,y). $$
We conclude by Lemma 
25
. 
\(\square \)

                
A remark from convex geometry: the case of isotropic convex bodies.
 Applying the previous proposition to 
\(f=\mathbbm {1}_{K}/|K|_d\)
, where 
K
 is an isotropic convex body in 
\(\mathbb {R}^d\)
 and using that, in this case, 
\(\sigma =L_K|K|^{1/d}\)
, we deduce the following proposition.

                  
Proposition 27

                  
For any isotropic convex body 
K
 in 
\(\mathbb {R}^{d}\)
,
$$\begin{aligned} \left| \frac{ \#(K\cap \mathbb {Z}^{d}) }{|K|_{d}}-1\right| \le \frac{M_d}{|K|^\frac{1}{d}} \hspace{0.1cm}, \end{aligned}$$
for some constant 
\(M_d>0\)
, depending only on the dimension, where 
\(\#K\)
 denotes the cardinality of a discrete set 
K
.

                
                  
Remark 28

                  
The hypothesis of isotropicity is necessary, since in 
\(\mathbb {Z}^{d}\)
, for 
\(d\ge 2\)
, it is easy to construct convex bodies that do not contain integer points, but whose volumes are increasingly large.

                
                  
Proposition 29

                  
Let 
\(f:\mathbb {R}^{d}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}\)
 be a centered, isotropic, log-concave density. Then, for 
\(1\le i\le d\)
,
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^{d}}k_{i}f(k)= \int _{\mathbb {R}^{d}}x_{i}f(x)dx +O_d(1)=O_d(1). \hspace{0.1cm} \end{aligned}$$
In particular, for 
\(d=1\)
, the following finite bound holds for any log-concave centered integrable function
$$\begin{aligned} \left| \int _{\mathbb {R}}xf(x)dx-\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}}kf(k)\right| \le (e+1)\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}} f(k) \hspace{0.1cm} . \end{aligned}$$

                
                  
Proof

                  
The proof is by induction again. Let us make a one dimensional remark that will be used on the base case of the induction. Let 
\(f:\mathbb {R}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}\)
 be a centered log-concave function and let suppose that the maximum of 
f
 is attained at 
\(x_{0}\in \mathbb {R}\)
 and let us suppose without loss of generality that 
\(x_{0}>0\)
. Then since 
f
 is log-concave and by the inequality [
8
, Theorem 4] one has
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}} f(x)dx\ge \int _{0}^{x_{0}}f(x)dx\ge f(0)x_{0}\ge \frac{\max f}{e} x_{0} \hspace{0.1cm} . \end{aligned}$$

                    (54)
                
Hence, we get 
\(x_{0}\max f \le e \int _{\mathbb {R}}f(x)dx\)
. The case of the summation is analogous. Let 
\(k_{0}\in \mathbb {Z}\)
 be such that 
\(k_{0}\le x_{0}< k_{0}+1\)
 . Then, we deduce
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}} f(k)\ge \sum _{k=0}^{k_{0}} f(k)\ge (k_{0}+1)f(0)\ge \frac{\max f}{e}(k_{0}+1) \hspace{0.1cm} . \end{aligned}$$

                    (55)
                
Let us start the induction. Let 
f
 be a centered, isotropic, log-concave density and let suppose that the maximum of 
f
 is attained at 
\(x_{0}\)
. We decompose the integral 
\(\int _{\mathbb {R}} xf(x)dx\)
 as follows
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}} xf(x)dx&= \sum _{k<0} \int _{k}^{k+1}xf(x)dx + \sum _{k=0}^{k_{0}-1} \int _{k}^{k+1}xf(x)dx+\int _{k_{0}}^{k_{0}+1}xf(x)dx\\&\quad +\sum _{k\ge k_{0}+1}\int _{k}^{k+1}xf(x)dx \\&\ge \sum _{k<0}kf(k+1)+\sum _{k=0}^{k_{0}-1}kf(k)+k_{0}\min \{f(k_{0}),f(k_{0}+1)\}+\sum _{k\ge k_{0}+1}kf(k+1)\\&\ge \sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}}kf(k)-k_{0}f(k_{0})-k_{0}f(k_{0}+1)+k_{0}\min \{f(k_{0}),f(k_{0}+1)\}-\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}}f(k). \end{aligned}$$
Therefore,
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}} xf(x)dx&\ge \sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}}kf(k)-\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}}f(k)-k_{0}\max f \\&\ge \sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}}kf(k)-\sum _{k \in \mathbb {Z}}f(k)-e\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}}f(k)=\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}}kf(k)-(e+1)\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}}f(k) , \end{aligned}$$
where in the last line we have used the inequality (
55
). Bounding from the above 
\(\int _{\mathbb {R}} xf(x)dx\)
 we obtain a similar inequality. Thus, we get
$$\begin{aligned} \left| \int _{\mathbb {R}}xf(x)dx-\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}}kf(k)\right| \le (e+1)\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}} f(k) \hspace{0.1cm} , \end{aligned}$$
and the one-dimensional case follows by Proposition 
26
.

                  
For the inductive step, let 
\(F:\mathbb {R}^{d-1} \rightarrow \mathbb {R}\)
 be defined, for 
\(x \in \mathbb {R}, y \in \mathbb {R}^{d-1}\)
, by
$$\begin{aligned} F(y):=\int _{\mathbb {R}} f(x,y)dx. \end{aligned}$$
Then 
F
 is log-concave, centered and isotropic as the marginal of a log-concave, centered and isotropic density and, for 
\(i=1,\ldots ,d-1\)
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}^{d}} y_{i}f(x,y)dxdy=\int _{\mathbb {R}^{d-1}}y_{i}F(y)dy . \end{aligned}$$
We have
$$\begin{aligned}&\left| \int _{\mathbb {R}^{d}} y_{i}f(x,y)dxdy - \sum _{k_1\in \mathbb {Z}, k\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}} k_{i}f(k_1,k)\right| \\&\le \left| \int _{\mathbb {R}^{d}} y_{i}f(x,y)dxdy-\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}}k_{i}F(k)\right| + \left| \sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}}k_{i}F(k)- \sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^{d}}k_{i}f(k)\right| . \end{aligned}$$
But the first term is 
\(O_d(1)\)
 by inductive hypothesis and for the second we note that 
\(f(\cdot ,k)\)
 is log-concave for every 
\(k\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}\)
, so we may apply (
53
) to get, for any 
\(k\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}\)
,
$$ \left| F(k) - \sum _{k_1\in \mathbb {Z}}f(k_1,k)\right| =\left| \int _{\mathbb {R}}{f(x,k)dx}-\sum _{k_1\in \mathbb {Z}}{f(k_1,k)}\right| \le \max _{x\in \mathbb {R}}f(x,k), $$
which gives
$$\begin{aligned}&\left| \int _{\mathbb {R}^{d}} y_{i}f(x,y)dxdy - \sum _{k_1\in \mathbb {Z}, k\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}} k_{i}f(k_1,k)\right| \\  &\quad \le O_d(1)+ \sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}}k_{i} \max _{x\in \mathbb {R}} f(x,k) =O_d\bigl ({1}\bigr ), \end{aligned}$$
where the last equality follows by Lemma 
25
. 
\(\square \)

                
We wish to show that a continuous isotropic, log-concave density 
f
 in 
\(\mathbb {R}^d\)
 is almost isotropic in the discrete sense, meaning that its discrete covariance matrix 
\(\textrm{Cov}_{\mathbb {Z}}{(f)}\)
 has 
\(O(\sigma )\)
 off-diagonal elements and 
\(\sigma ^2 + O(\sigma )\)
 diagonal elements.

                  
Proposition 30

                  
Let 
\(f:\mathbb {R}^{d}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}\)
 be a centered, isotropic, log-concave density with 
\(\textrm{Cov}{(f)} = \sigma ^2\textrm{I}_{\textrm{d}}\)
. Then
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^d} f(k)k_i^{2} = \sigma ^2 +O_d\big (\sigma \big ), \hspace{0.1cm} \text {for every }\ 1\le i \le d. \end{aligned}$$

                
                  
Proof

                  
We proceed by induction on the dimension. For the one-dimensional case let

                  
\(x_0 = \inf \{x \in \mathbb {R}: f(x) = \max {f}\}\)
. Then
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}} f(x)x^{2}=\int _{x\le x_0}f(x)x^{2}dx+\int _{x>x_0} f(x)x^{2}dx . \end{aligned}$$
Noting that 
f
 is non-decreasing on 
\((-\infty ,x_0]\)
 and non-increasing on 
\([x_0,+\infty )\)
 by log-concavity, we get
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}}f(x)x^{2}dx&=\sum _{k< \lfloor x_0 \rfloor } \int _{[k,k+1)}f(x)x^{2}dx + \sum _{k> \lfloor x_0 \rfloor } \int _{[k,k+1)}f(x)x^{2}dx + \int _{\lfloor x_0 \rfloor }^{\lfloor x_0 \rfloor +1}f(x)x^2dx \nonumber \\&\le \sum _{k< \lfloor x_0 \rfloor } f(k+1)(k^{2}+k+\frac{1}{3}) + \sum _{k> \lfloor x_0 \rfloor } f(k)(k^{2}+k+\frac{1}{3}) + \int _{\lfloor x_0 \rfloor }^{\lfloor x_0 \rfloor +1}f(x)x^2dx \nonumber \\&\le \sum _{k \in \mathbb {Z}} f(k)k^{2} - \sum _{k < \lfloor x_0 \rfloor } f(k+1)k - \sum _{k > \lfloor x_0 \rfloor } f(k)k + f(x_0)\Bigl (\lfloor x_0 \rfloor ^2+\lfloor x_0 \rfloor +\frac{1}{3}\Bigr ) \nonumber \\&\le \sum _{k \in \mathbb {Z}} f(k)k^{2} - \sum _{k \in \mathbb {Z}} f(k)k + f(x_0)\Bigl (\lfloor x_0 \rfloor ^2+\lfloor x_0 \rfloor +\frac{1}{3}\Bigr ) \end{aligned}$$

                    (56)
                
We observe that 
\(f(x_0) = \max {f} \le \frac{1}{\sigma }\)
 and, by (
54
), 
\(x_0 = O({\sigma })\)
. Hence, the last term in (
56
) is 
\(O(\sigma )\)
. Furthermore, by Proposition 
26
, the second term in (
56
) is 
O
(1). Thus,
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}}f(x)x^{2}dx \le \sum _{k \in \mathbb {Z}} f(k)k^{2} + O(\sigma ). \end{aligned}$$
Similarly, using the piecewise monotonicity of 
f
 in the reverse way, we obtain
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}}f(x)x^{2}dx \ge \sum _{k \in \mathbb {Z}} f(k)k^{2} + O(\sigma ). \end{aligned}$$
This proves the base case.

                  
Let 
\(x\in \mathbb {R}\)
, 
\(y\in \mathbb {R}^{d-1}\)
 and let 
\(F(y):=\int _{\mathbb {R}} f(x,y)dx\)
. Then, by the inductive hypothesis applied to 
F
, for 
\(i=1,\ldots ,d-1,\)
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}^{d}}f(x,y)y_{i}^2dxdy=\int _{\mathbb {R}^{d-1}}F(y)y_{i}^2dy=\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}}F(k)k_{i}^2+O_d(\sigma ) \hspace{0.1cm} . \end{aligned}$$

                    (57)
                
Since, for every 
\(k\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}\)
, the function 
\(f(\cdot ,k)\)
 is log-concave in 
\(\mathbb {R}\)
, we have, by (
53
),
$$\begin{aligned} \left| F(k)-\sum _{k^{\prime }\in \mathbb {Z}} f(k^{\prime },k)\right| = \left| \int _{\mathbb {R}} f(x,k)dx-\sum _{k^{\prime }\in \mathbb {Z}} f(k^{\prime },k)\right| \le \max _{x} f(x,k) \hspace{0.1cm} . \end{aligned}$$

                    (58)
                
So by (
57
) and (
58
) we get, for 
\(i =1,\ldots ,d-1\)
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}^{d}}f(x,y)y_{i}^2dxdy&=\sum _{ k^{\prime } \in \mathbb {Z},k \in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1} }f(k^{\prime },k)k_{i}^2+\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}}O(\max _{x}f(x,k))k_{i}^2+O_d(\sigma ) \\&= \sum _{ k^{\prime } \in \mathbb {Z},k \in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1} }f(k^{\prime },k)k_{i}^2+O_d(\sigma ), \end{aligned}$$
by Lemma 
25
, and the result follows. 
\(\square \)

                
We will need the following lemma for the base case of the induction of Proposition 
32
.

                  
Lemma 31

                  
Let 
f
 be a centered, isotropic, log-concave density in 
\(\mathbb {R}^2\)
, with 
\(\textrm{Cov}{(f)} = \sigma ^2\textrm{I}_{\textrm{2}}\)
. For 
\(x \in \mathbb {R}\)
, let
$$\begin{aligned} N_{\max }(x) := \inf \{y \in \mathbb {R}: f(x,y) = \max _y{f(x,y)}\}. \end{aligned}$$
Then
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}}xN_{\max }(x)\max _yf(x,y)dx = O(\sigma ) \end{aligned}$$

                    (59)
                
and
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}}{kN_{\max }(k)\max _yf(k,y)} = O(\sigma ). \end{aligned}$$

                    (60)
                

                
                  
Proof

                  
Let 
c
 be an absolute constant to be determined later and denote the set 
\(A_{\sigma }:=\{x\in \mathbb {R}: |x|, |N_{\max }(x)| \le c \sigma \}.\)
 We have
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}}xN_{\max }(x)\max _yf(x,y)dx = \int _{A_{\sigma }}xN_{\max }(x)\max _yf(x,y)dx + \int _{A_{\sigma }^{\textrm{C}}}xN_{\max }(x)\max _yf(x,y)dx \end{aligned}$$
The first term can be trivially bounded as
$$\begin{aligned} \Bigl | \int _{A_{\sigma }}xN_{\max }(x)\max _yf(x,y)dx \Bigr | \le O(\sigma ^3)\max {f} = O(\sigma ). \end{aligned}$$
For the second term we have, for some absolute constant 
\(C^{\prime }_2\)
 and for 
c
 large enough,
$$\begin{aligned} \Bigl |\int _{A_{\sigma }^{\textrm{C}}}|x|N_{\max }(x)\max _yf(x,y)dx\Bigr |&\le \max (f)\int _{A_{\sigma }^{\textrm{C}}}{xN_{\max }(x)e^{-\frac{|x|+N_{\max }(x)}{\sigma }C^{\prime }_2}dx} \nonumber \\&\le O\Bigl (\frac{1}{\sigma ^2}\Bigr )\sigma \int _{x>c\sigma }{x e^{-\frac{x}{\sigma }C^{\prime }_2}dx}\end{aligned}$$

                    (61)
                
$$\begin{aligned}&= O(\sigma ). \end{aligned}$$

                    (62)
                
Here (
61
) follows by using the fact that 
\(ye^{-y}\le 1\)
. This gives (
59
), (
60
) follows in a similar way. 
\(\square \)

                
                  
Proposition 32

                  
Let 
f
 be a centered, continuous, isotropic, log-concave density in 
\(\mathbb {R}^d\)
, with 
\(\textrm{Cov}{(f)} = \sigma ^2\textrm{I}_{\textrm{d}}\)
. Then, for all 
\(i \ne j\)
$$ \sum _{k \in \mathbb {Z}^d}f(k)k_ik_j= O_d(\sigma ). $$

                
                  
Proof

                  
We proceed by induction on 
d
. Consider first the base case 
\(d = 2\)
. Then, letting 
\(N_{\max }(x):= \inf \{y \in \mathbb {R}: f(x,y) = \max _y{f(x,y)}\}\)
 and 
\(M_{\max }(y):= \inf \{x \in \mathbb {R}: f(x,y) = \max _x{f(x,y)}\}\)
 and assuming without loss of generality that the infima are achieved,
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}_+^2}{f(x,y)xydx} = \int _{\mathbb {R}_+}{x\int _{0\le y \le N_{\max }(x)}{f(x,y)ydy} + \int _{y>N_{\max }(x)}{f(x,y)ydy}dx}. \end{aligned}$$
Observe that
$$\begin{aligned}&{\int _{0\le y \le N_{\max }(x)}{f(x,y)ydy}} \le \sum _{0\le k \le N_{\max }(x)}{\int _{[k,k+1)}{f(x,y)ydy}} \nonumber \\&\quad \le \sum _{0\le k \le N_{\max }(x)-1}{(k+1)f(x,k+1)} + \max _{y}f(x,y)(N_{\max }(x)+1) \end{aligned}$$

                    (63)
                
and
$$\begin{aligned} {\int _{ y > N_{\max }(x)}{f(x,y)ydy}}&\le \sum _{k\ge N_{\max }(x)}{(k+1)f(x,k)}. \end{aligned}$$

                    (64)
                
By (
63
) and (
64
)
$$\begin{aligned}  &   \int _{\mathbb {R}_+^2}{f(x,y)xydx}\nonumber \\  &   \quad \le \int _{\mathbb {R}}{x\Bigl (\sum _{k \ge 0}{kf(x,k)} + \sum _{k \ge N_{\max }(x)}{f(x,k)} + \max _{y}f(x,y)(N_{\max }(x)+1)\Bigr ) dx} \end{aligned}$$

                    (65)
                
For the first term in (
65
) we have
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}}{x\sum _{k \ge 0}{kf(x,k)}dx}&\le \sum _{k \ge 0}{\sum _{0\le m\le M_{\max }(k)-1}{k(m+1)f(m+1,k)}} \nonumber \\&\quad + \sum _{k \ge 0}{\sum _{m > M_{\max }(k)}{k(m+1)f(m,k)}} \nonumber \\&\quad + \sum _{k \ge 0}{k(M_{\max }(k)+1)\max _xf(x,k)} \nonumber \\&\le \sum _{k\ge 0}\sum _{m \ge 0}{kmf(m,k)} + \sum _{k \ge 0}{\sum _{m \ge 0}{kf(m,k)}} \nonumber \\&\quad + \sum _{k \ge 0}{k(M_{\max }(k)+1)\max _xf(x,k)} \end{aligned}$$

                    (66)
                
$$\begin{aligned}&\le \sum _{k\ge 0}\sum _{m \ge 0}{kmf(m,k)} + \sum _{k \ge 0}{\sum _{m \ge 0}{kf(m,k)}} + O(\sigma ), \end{aligned}$$

                    (67)
                
where the last inequality follows by Lemma 
31
. On the other hand, for the second term in (
65
) we have
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}}{\sum _{k\ge N_{\max }}xf(x,k)dx} \le \Bigl (\int _{\mathbb {R}}{\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}}x^2f(x,k)dx}\Bigr )^{\frac{1}{2}} = O(\sigma ) \end{aligned}$$

                    (68)
                
by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Proposition 
30
. Finally, the third term in (
65
) is
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}}{\max _{y}f(x,y)(N_{\max }(x)+1)dx} = O(\sigma ) \end{aligned}$$

                    (69)
                
by Lemma 
31
. Plugging (
66
), (
68
) and (
69
) into (
65
) we obtain
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}_+^2}{f(x,y)xydx} \le \sum _{k\ge 0}\sum _{m \ge 0}{kmf(m,k)} + \sum _{k \ge 0}{\sum _{m \ge 0}{kf(m,k)}} + O(\sigma ). \end{aligned}$$

                    (70)
                
Using the reverse bounds to those in (
63
) and (
64
) obtained by the monotonicity of 
f
, we get the lower bound
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}_+^2}{f(x,y)xydx} \ge \sum _{k\ge 0}\sum _{m \ge 0}{kmf(m,k)} + \sum _{k \ge 0}{\sum _{m \ge 0}{kf(m,k)}} + O(\sigma ). \end{aligned}$$
In a completely analogous way, we may bound the integral on the rest of the quadrants and obtain the approximations
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}_-^2}{f(x,y)xydx}&= \sum _{k\le 0}\sum _{m \le 0}{kmf(m,k)} + \sum _{k \le 0}{\sum _{m \le 0}{kf(m,k)}} + O(\sigma ), \end{aligned}$$

                    (71)
                
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}_-\times \mathbb {R}_+}{f(x,y)xydx}&= \sum _{k\ge 0}\sum _{m \le 0}{kmf(m,k)} + \sum _{k \ge 0}{\sum _{m \le 0}{kf(m,k)}} + O(\sigma ) \quad \text {and}\end{aligned}$$

                    (72)
                
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}_+\times \mathbb {R}_-}{f(x,y)xydx}&= \sum _{k\le 0}\sum _{m \ge 0}{kmf(m,k)} + \sum _{k \le 0}{\sum _{m \ge 0}{kf(m,k)}} + O(\sigma ). \end{aligned}$$

                    (73)
                
Since, by Proposition 
29
, 
\(\sum _{k }{\sum _{m }{kf(m,k)}} = O(1)\)
, by adding (
70
)–(
73
) we obtain
$$\begin{aligned} 0= \int _{\mathbb {R}^2}{f(x,y)xydx} = \sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}}\sum _{m \in \mathbb {Z}}{kmf(m,k)} + O(\sigma ). \end{aligned}$$
This completes the base case 
\(d = 2\)
.

                  
The inductive step is similar to that in the proof of Proposition 
30
. Let 
\(x\in \mathbb {R}\)
, 
\(y = (y_1,\ldots ,y_{d-1})\in \mathbb {R}^{d-1}\)
 and let 
\(F(y):=\int _{\mathbb {R}} f(x,y)dx\)
. Then, by the inductive hypothesis, for 
\(1\le i,j \le d-1,\)
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}^{d}}f(x,y)y_{i}y_jdy=\int _{\mathbb {R}^{d-1}}F(y)y_{i}y_jdy=\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}}F(k)k_{i}k_j+O_d(\sigma ) \hspace{0.1cm} . \end{aligned}$$

                    (74)
                
Since for every 
\(k = (k_1,\ldots , k_{d-1})\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}\)
, the function 
\(f(\cdot ,k)\)
 is log-concave 
\(\mathbb {R}\)
, we have by Proposition 
26
$$\begin{aligned} F(k)-\sum _{k^{\prime }\in \mathbb {Z}} f(k^{\prime },k) = \int _{\mathbb {R}} f(x,k)dx-\sum _{k^{\prime }\in \mathbb {Z}} f(k^{\prime },k) = O_d\bigl ( \max _{x} f(x,k)\bigr ) \hspace{0.1cm} . \end{aligned}$$

                    (75)
                
So by (
74
) and (
75
) we get, for 
\(1\le i,j \le d-1\)
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {R}^{d}}f(x,y)y_{i}y_jdxdy=\sum _{ k^{\prime } \in \mathbb {Z},k \in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1} }f(k^{\prime },k)k_{i}k_j+\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}}O\Bigl (\max _{x}f(x,k)\Bigr )k_{i}k_j+O_d(\sigma ). \end{aligned}$$

                    (76)
                
But by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Lemma 
25
, 
\(\sum _{k\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}}O(\max _{x}f(x,k))k_{i}k_j\)
\(=O_d(\sigma )\)
 and the result follows. 
\(\square \)

                
                  
Corollary 33

                  
Let 
f
 be a centered, isotropic, log-concave density in 
\(\mathbb {R}^d\)
, with 
\(\textrm{Cov}{(f)} = \sigma ^2\textrm{I}_d.\)
 Then
$$ \det (\textrm{Cov}_{\mathbb {Z}^d}{(f)}) =\sigma ^{2d}+ O_d(\sigma ^{2d-1}). $$

                
                  
Proof

                  
By Proposition 
32
$$ [\textrm{Cov}_{\mathbb {Z}^d}(f)]_{ij} = O_d(\sigma ), \quad \text {for } i \ne j $$
and
$$ [\textrm{Cov}_{\mathbb {Z}^d}(f)]_{ij} = \sigma ^2 + O_d(\sigma ), \quad \text {for } i = j. $$
Thus,
$$\begin{aligned} \det (\textrm{Cov}_{\mathbb {Z}^d}{(f)})&= \sum _{\tau }\textrm{sgn}(\tau )\prod _{i=1}^d\textrm{Cov}_{\mathbb {Z}^d}(f)_{i\tau (i)}\\&= \sigma ^{2d} + O(\sigma ^{2d-1}), \end{aligned}$$
since for 
\(\tau \)
 being the identity permutation 
\(\textrm{sgn}(\tau )\prod _{i=1}^d{\textrm{Cov}_{\mathbb {Z}^d}{(f)}_{i\tau (i)}} = \bigl (\sigma ^{2}+O(\sigma )\bigr )^d = \sigma ^{2d}+O(\sigma ^{2d-1})\)
 and for any other permutation 
\(\tau ^{\prime }\)
$$ \prod _{i=1}^d{\textrm{Cov}_{\mathbb {Z}^d}{(f)}_{i\tau ^{\prime }(i)}} = O((\sigma ^2)^{d-2}\sigma ^2) = O_d(\sigma ^{2d-2}). $$
\(\square \)

                
Finally, Corollary 
34
 below is a version of Theorem 
7
 with the assumption that the continuous function 
f
 is isotropic. It can be seen that due to use of Corollary 
33
, 
\(\sigma \)
 needs to be taken at least 
\(\Omega (d!)\)
. We do not know whether this is the best (lowest) rate.

                  
Corollary 34

                  
(
Theorem 
7
for isotropic 
f
) Let 
p
 be log-concave p.m.f. on 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
, whose continuous log-concave extension, say 
f
, is isotropic. Then there exists an absolute constant 
\(C^{\prime }\)
 such that
$$ \max _{k \in \mathbb {Z}^d}{p(k)} \le \frac{C^{\prime }}{\det \Bigl (\textrm{Cov}(p)\Bigr )^{\frac{1}{2}}} $$
provided that 
\(\sigma := \det {(\textrm{Cov}_{\mathbb {R}}{(f)})}\)
 is large enough depending on 
d
.

                
                  
Proof

                  
Since 
p
 is extensible log-concave, there exists a continuous log-concave function 
f
 (not necessarily a density) such that 
\(f(k) = p(k)\)
 for all 
\(k\in \mathbb {Z}^d\)
 and by assumption 
f
 is isotropic. Thus
$$\begin{aligned} \max _{k \in \mathbb {Z}^d}p(k) \le \max _{x\in \mathbb {R}^d}f(x)=\frac{L_f^d\int _{\mathbb {R}^d} f}{\sigma ^d} \le \frac{C\int _{\mathbb {R}^d}{f}}{\sigma ^d} \hspace{0.1cm}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (77)
                
where 
C
 is an upper bound of 
\(L_{f}^{d}\)
, which can be taken to be an absolute constant as guaranteed by the Isotropic Constant Theorem 
9
. Noting that by definition 
\(\textrm{Cov}{(Zp)} = \textrm{Cov}{(p)}\)
 for any normalizing constant 
\(Z \in \mathbb {R}_+\)
 and applying Proposition 
26
 and Corollary 
33
 to 
\(\frac{f(\cdot )}{\int {f}}\)
, we obtain
$$\begin{aligned} \max _{k \in \mathbb {Z}^d}p(k) \le \frac{C(1+O_d(\frac{1}{\sigma }))}{(\det (\textrm{Cov}{(p)}) + O_d(\sigma ^{2d-1}))^{1/2}} \le \frac{2C}{\det (\textrm{Cov}{(p)})^{\frac{1}{2}}} \end{aligned}$$
provided that 
\(\sigma \)
 is large enough depending on 
d
, using that 
\(O_d(\sigma ^{2d-1}) \ge -\frac{1}{4}\det (\textrm{Cov}{(p)}) \simeq -\frac{1}{4}\sigma ^{2d}\)
 as 
\(\sigma \rightarrow \infty \)
. Note that implicitly we also used 
\(\int {f} = O_d(\sum _k{f(k)})\)
 (for 
f
 not necessarily a density) which can be seen directly from the proof of Proposition 
26
. The result follows with 
\(C^{\prime } = 2C\)
. 
\(\square \)

                
                  
Proposition 35

                  
Fix 
\(d \ge 1\)
 and let 
p
 be a log-concave p.m.f. on 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
 with almost isotropic extension. Then, for every 
\(1\le i\le d\)
,
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{k \in \mathbb {Z}^d}|p(k) - p(k-e_i)|=O_d\left( \frac{1}{\det {\left( \textrm{Cov}{(p)}\right) ^{\frac{1}{2d}}}}\right) , \end{aligned}$$
where 
\(e_i \in \mathbb {Z}^d\)
 is defined as the vector with the 
i
-th coordinate 1 and all the other coordinates 0.

                
                  
Proof

                  
For any one-dimensional quasi-concave non-negative real sequence 
\((a_n)_{n\in \mathbb {Z}}\)
, 
\(\sum _{n\in \mathbb {Z}}|a_n-a_{n-1}|\le 2\max _{n\in \mathbb {Z}}a_n\)
. Thus, by log-concavity, denoting 
\(\overline{k_{i}}=\sum _{j\ne i}k_je_j\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}\)
, one has
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{k \in \mathbb {Z}^d}|p(k) - p(k-e_i)| \le 2 \sum _{\overline{k_{i}}\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}}\max _{k_{i}\in \mathbb {Z}}p(k_1,\ldots ,k_{i-1},k_{i},k_{i+1},\ldots ,k_d), \end{aligned}$$
Hence, as in (
52
), we have
$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{\overline{k_{i}}\in \mathbb {Z}^{d-1}}\max _{k_{i}\in \mathbb {Z}}p(k_1,\ldots ,k_{i-1},k_{i},k_{i+1},\ldots ,k_d)=O_d\left( \frac{1}{\det {\left( \textrm{Cov}{(p)}\right) ^{\frac{1}{2d}}}}\right) \end{aligned}$$
and the result follows. 
\(\square \)

                
We are ready to give the proof of Theorem 
20
:

                  
Proof of Theorem 20

                  
Part 1 follows directly from Corollary 
34
 (in fact with 
\(c_1(d)\)
 being independent of the dimension 
d
). Part 2 follows from Lemma 
24
, the well-known fact that for a centered log-concave function 
f
 on 
\(\mathbb {R}^d\)
$$\begin{aligned} f(0) \simeq _d \frac{1}{\det {(\textrm{Cov}(f))^{\frac{1}{2}}}} \end{aligned}$$
and Corollary 
33
. Finally, Part 3 follows directly from Proposition 
35
. 
\(\square \)

                
3.5 
Relaxing the Isotropicity Assumption on 
f

                        
In this section, we relax the assumptions of Lemma 
23
, so that we obtain conclusions of the same kind under some weaker assumptions on the eigenvalues of the continuous covariance matrix 
\(\textrm{Cov}_{\mathbb {R}}{(f)}\)
. Specifically, we will prove:

                  
Proposition 36

                  
For every centered and log-concave density 
\(f:\mathbb {R}^{d}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}\)
 and for every 
\(\theta \in \mathbb {S}^{d-1}\)
 one has
$$\begin{aligned} c_1^{d+2}f(0)^\frac{1}{d}\sqrt{\lambda _{\min }(\textrm{Cov}(f))} \le \left( \int _{0}^{\infty }r^{d-1}f(r\theta )dr\right) ^\frac{1}{d}\le (d+1) c_2^{d+2}f(0)^\frac{1}{d}\sqrt{\lambda _{\max }(\textrm{Cov}(f))}. \end{aligned}$$
where 
\(c_{1}\)
 and 
\(c_{2}\)
 are the constants appearing in (
43
).

                
Before giving the proof, we mention the consequences of Proposition 
36
.

                  
Corollary 37

                  
Let 
f
 be a a centered, almost isotropic log-concave density. Then for every 
\(\theta \in \mathbb {S}^{d-1}\)
$$\begin{aligned} \int _{0}^{\infty }r^{d-1}f(r\theta )dr = \Theta _d(1), \end{aligned}$$
as 
\(\sigma := \det {(\textrm{Cov}{(f)})} \rightarrow \infty \)
.

                
                  
Proof

                  
Since 
f
 is almost isotropic, we have
$$ \lambda _{\textrm{min}}\big (\textrm{Cov}(f)\big ) \simeq _d \lambda _{\textrm{max}}\big (\textrm{Cov}(f)\big ) \simeq _d \sigma ^2 $$
as 
\(\sigma \rightarrow \infty \)
. The result follows from Proposition 
36
 since 
\(f(0) \simeq _d \max {f} \simeq _d \frac{1}{\sigma ^d}\)
. 
\(\square \)

                
                  
Remark 38

                  
The isotropicity assumption was only used in Lemma 
23
, which in turn allowed us to prove the concentration Lemma 
24
 which was repeatedly evoked in Section 
3
 to bound the sums of maxima of log-concave functions and thus also the error terms. By Corollary 
37
, a version of Lemma 
24
 holds true for almost isotropic densities, although with possibly different constants and for large enough 
\(\sigma \)
. Therefore, all the previous results of Section 
3
 hold true and thus also the statements of Theorem 
3
 and 
5
 under the almost isotropic log-concave assumption.

                
                  
Remark 39

                  
Note that if the function 
f
 is in addition isotropic i.e. 
\(\textrm{Cov}{(f)} = \sigma ^2\textrm{I}_d\)
, one has that 
\(f(0)\simeq _{d} \frac{1}{\sigma ^{d}}\)
 and so we recover the result of Lemma 
23
.

                
For the above-mentioned proposition, the following lemma which gives bounds for the inradius and circumradius of convex bodies in not necessarily isotropic position will be essential .

                  
Proof of Proposition 36

                  
Let 
\(f:\mathbb {R}^{d}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}\)
 be a centered log-concave density. For simplicity, we denote 
\(\lambda _{\min }=\lambda _{\min }(\textrm{Cov}(f))\)
 and analogously for 
\(\lambda _{\max }\)
. Following the same steps as in the proof of Lemma 
23
, we get that, for any 
\(u\in \mathbb {S}^{d-1}\)
,
$$ \frac{\lambda _{\min }}{f(0)}\le \int _{K_{d+2}(f)}\langle x,u\rangle ^2dx=\frac{1}{f(0)}\int _{\mathbb {R}^d}\langle x,u\rangle ^2f(x)dx\le \frac{\lambda _{\max }}{f(0)}. $$
Using the inclusion relations (
43
), this gives
$$ c_1^{2d+2}\lambda _{\min }\le \frac{1}{|K_{d+1}(f)|}\int _{K_{d+1}(f)}\langle x,u\rangle ^2dx\le c_2^{2d+2}\lambda _{\max }. $$
Using these inequalities, (
48
) and again the inclusion relations (
43
), we get
$$ \sqrt{\frac{d+2}{d}}c_1^{d+2} \sqrt{\lambda _{\min }} B_2^d\subset K_d(f)\subset \sqrt{d(d+2)}c_2^{d+2} \sqrt{\lambda _{\max }} B_2^d, $$
which gives inequality (
36
). 
\(\square \)

                
4 
Concluding Remarks and Open Questions

                

                  
1.

                  
                    
In view of Remark 
12
 it is natural to ask the following question:

                  
                

              

                
Question 40

                
Let 
\(X_1,\dots , X_n\)
 be i.i.d. log-concave random vectors. Is 
\(\sum _{i=1}^n{X_i}\)
 log-concave for every 
n
?

              
We note here that our assumptions of Definition 
2
 are preserved up to constants under convolution as shown in Proposition 
15
 and therefore, assuming that the p.m.f. of 
\(X_1\)
 is log-concave ensures that the p.m.f. of the sum also satisfies these assumptions.
Answering Question 
40
 even for quantized multivariate Gaussians seems to be non-trivial. Nevertheless, the following simple example shows that the answer is positive for quantized isotropic multivariate Gaussians.

                
Example 41

                
Let 
\(X_1\)
 be a random variable with p.m.f. on 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
 proportional to 
\(e^{-\frac{\Vert k\Vert _2^2}{2\sigma ^2}}\)
; that is 
\(X_1\)
 is a multivariate centered isotropic Gaussian quantized on 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
. Then 
\(X_1\)
 has the distribution of 
\((Z_1,\ldots ,Z_d)\)
, where 
\(Z_i\)
 are i.i.d. one-dimensional quantized centered Gaussians with variance 
\(\sigma ^2\)
.

                
Note that if 
p
 is a p.m.f. on 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
 which is a product of log-concave p.m.f.s 
\(\{p_i\}_{i=1}^d\)
 on 
\(\mathbb {Z}\)
$$ p(k) = \prod _{i=1}^dp^{(i)}(k_i), \quad k = (k_1,\ldots ,k_n) \in \mathbb {Z}^d, $$
then 
p
 is clearly log-concave on 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
.

                
Therefore, since log-concavity is preserved under convolution in dimension one [
5
] and the coordinates of 
\(\sum _{i=1}^n{X_i}\)
 are independent, it follows that 
\(\sum _{i=1}^n{X_i}\)
 is log-concave for every 
n
.

              

                

                  
2.

                  
                    
For Theorem 
20
 and therefore for Theorems 
3
 and 
5
 as well, we have assumed that there exists a continuous extension 
f
 which is almost isotropic. We have then shown that 
f
 is also almost isotropic in the discrete sense. It would be more natural to start with the assumption that the discrete p.m.f. is isotropic. However, in this case we would not be able to use the continuous toolkit to prove our concentration lemma in Section 
3.2
. Nevertheless, we suspect that if the discrete p.m.f. is isotropic, then there exists a continuous extension that is almost isotropic and therefore our results for discrete log-concave distributions would still hold under this assumption.

                  
                

              

                
Question 42

                
Let 
X
 be a log-concave random vector with p.m.f. 
p
 on 
\(\mathbb {Z}^d\)
. Assume that 
p
 is almost isotropic. Is there a continuous log-concave extension of 
p
 on 
\(\mathbb {R}^d\)
 which is almost isotropic?
