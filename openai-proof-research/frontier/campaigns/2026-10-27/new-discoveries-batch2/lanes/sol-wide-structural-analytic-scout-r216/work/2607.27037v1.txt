                                                   Abstract. Symmetric nonnegative matrix trifactorizations (SN-Trifactorizations) were introduced
                                                   by Bukovšek–Šmigoc [Linear Algebra Appl. 2023] as a symmetric analogue of nonnegative matrix
                                                   factorizations. A SN-Trifactorization of a symmetric nonnegative matrix A is of the form A =
                                                   BCB T , where B and C are nonnegative matrices, with C symmetric. The associated SNT-rank of
                                                   A is defined as the smallest integer k for which A admits such a factorization with C ∈ Rk×k
                                                                                                                                            + .
                                                      In this paper, we derive sharper upper bounds for the SNT-rank of the Euclidean distance
                                                   matrices considered by Shitov [Linear Algebra Appl. 2025] and Bukovšek–Šmigoc [Linear Algebra
                                                   Appl. 2023]. We also establish several new relationships between the rank and the SNT-rank of

                                                                             1. Introduction and main results
                                              Throughout this paper, Rm×n
                                                                        +    denotes the set of all m × n nonnegative real matrices and Sn+ :=
                                           {A ∈ Rn×n
                                                   +   : A = AT }, denotes the set of all n × n symmetric nonnegative matrices.
                                              Matrix factorizations involving nonnegative factors play an important role in matrix theory,
                                           optimization, combinatorics, statistics, machine learning, and data analysis. Among the most
                                           extensively studied examples are nonnegative matrix factorizations and completely positive factor-
                                           izations, both of which possess rich structural properties and numerous applications.
                                              Given a nonnegative matrix A ∈ Rm×n+   , the nonnegative matrix factorization (NM-Factorization)
                                           problem asks to find nonnegative matrices U ∈ Rm×k+    and V ∈ Rn×k
                                                                                                             +   such that A ≈ U V T , where the
                                           approximation is measured with respect to the Frobenius norm. The exact version of the problem,
                                           one asks for the smallest integer k such that A = U V T . This minimum value of k is called the
                                           nonnegative rank of A and is denoted by rk+ (A), while rk(A) denotes the usual rank of A. It is
                                           well known that
                                                                              rk(A) ≤ rk+ (A) ≤ min{m, n}.
                                           Nonnegative matrix factorization has rich applications in several areas, including text mining, image
                                           processing, hyperspectral imaging, computational biology, and clustering. We refer the reader to
                                           [10, 12] and the references therein for more information.
                                              For symmetric nonnegative matrices, it is natural to consider factorizations that preserve symme-
                                           try as well as nonnegativity. A fundamental example is the completely positive (CP) factorization.
                                           A matrix A ∈ Sn+ is called completely positive if A = U U T for some nonnegative matrix U ∈ Rn×k  + .
                                           The smallest such integer k is called the cp-rank of A, denoted by cp(A); if no such factorization
                                           exists, we set cp(A) = ∞. Completely positive matrices and their cp-ranks have been studied
                                           extensively in matrix theory and optimization [4].

   In this paper, we study the version of symmetric nonnegative matrix trifactorization, which was
recently introduced by Bukovšek–Šmigoc in [5]. More precisely, a symmetric nonnegative matrix
trifactorization (SN-Trifactorization) of a matrix A ∈ Sn+ is a factorization of the form A = BCB T ,
where B ∈ Rn×k +    and C ∈ Sk+ . The smallest integer k for which such a factorization exists is
called the SNT-rank of A and is denoted by st+ (A). For completeness, we set st+ (0) = 0. SN-
Trifactorization provides a framework for studying low-rank structure in symmetric nonnegative
matrices while preserving both nonnegativity and symmetry [1, 8, 11].
   In [5], Bukovšek–Šmigoc established several fundamental properties of the SNT-rank, including
its relationships with the rank, nonnegative rank, and cp-rank. They later developed a graph-
theoretic framework based on set-join covers [6], yielding exact formulas for the SNT-rank of several
graph classes. Despite these advances, determining the SNT-rank of a symmetric nonnegative
matrix remains difficult problem in general. Shitov [14] recently investigated the computational
complexity of the SNT-rank for symmetric nonnegative matrices with integer entries.
   Euclidean distance matrices have attracted considerable attention in the study of the SNT-rank

    We next investigate the SNT-rank of symmetric nonnegative matrices of rank at most three.
Proposition 2.7. Let A ∈ Sn+ . Then the following statements hold:
(a) For each k ∈ {1, 2}, rk(A) = k if and only if st+ (A) = k.
(b) If st+ (A) = 3, then rk(A) = 3.
Proof. (a) We first assume that that rk(A) = 1. Then there exists a nonzero vector v ∈ Rn+ such
that A = vv T . Hence, A admits an SN-Trifactorization with one column, and therefore st+ (A) ≤ 1.
Since rk(A) ≤ st+ (A), st+ (A) = 1.
  Conversely, suppose that st+ (A) = 1. Then A ̸= 0 and by Proposition 2.1, rk(A) = 1.
  If rk(A) = 2, by Proposition 2.3, st+ (A) = 2. To prove the converse, assume that st+ (A) = 2. By
Proposition 2.1(a), rk(A) ≤ 2. Since rk(A) = 1 if and only if st+ (A) = 1, it follows that rk(A) = 2.
  (b) Suppose that st+ (A) = 3. Using Proposition 2.1(a), we have rk(A) ≤ 3. If rk(A) ≤ 2, then
part (a) would imply that st+ (A) ≤ 2, which contradicts the assumption. Therefore, rk(A) = 3. □
  As an immediate consequence, the rank and the SNT-rank coincide for symmetric nonnegative

      3. SNT-rank and nonnegative rank with respect to the Kronecker product
   In this section, we investigate the behavior of the SNT-rank of symmetric nonnegative matrices
under the Kronecker product and prove our main results – Theorems 1.2, 1.3, and 1.4. In particular,
we establish its submultiplicativity and derive several corresponding bounds. We also obtain results
related to conjectures concerning the nonnegative rank under the Kronecker product.

                         4. SNT-rank and Euclidean distance matrices
   In this section, we investigate the SNT-rank of Euclidean distance matrices and prove Theo-
rem 1.1. For n ≥ 3, every Euclidean distance matrix has rank three [13]. Since Proposition 2.7
shows that the SNT-rank of a symmetric nonnegative matrix coincides with its rank whenever the
rank is at most two, rank-three matrices constitute the first nontrivial case. Indeed, the SNT-
rank of a rank-three symmetric nonnegative matrix may strictly exceed its rank (see Example 2.9).
More generally, Shitov [14, Section 3] proved that every rank-three symmetric nonnegative matrix
A of order n satisfies st+ (A) ≤ 294n2/3 , and, in the special  case of the Euclidean distance matrix
A(1, . . . , n), obtained the sharper bound st+ A(1, . . . , n) ≤ 4 log2 n + 4. Our goal in this section is
to obtain improved bounds for the SNT-rank of several classes of Euclidean distance matrices. We
begin with the following definition.
