                                                                                                 Abstract
                                                     We prove that the independence sequence of every sufficiently large forest is strictly barrier
                                                 log-concave: whenever ik+1 > ik , one has i2k > ik−1 ik+1 . Consequently, every common downward
                                                 coefficient tilt is unimodal, with one order threshold valid simultaneously for all 0 < t ≤ 1. As
                                                 an application, we prove that every sufficiently large well-covered tree has a strictly log-concave
                                                 independence polynomial. More generally, the latter conclusion holds for well-covered forests
                                                 with sufficiently large matching number. Every sufficiently large forest with a palindromic
                                                 independence polynomial is also strictly log-concave, with its modes at the central ranks.
                                                 The proof establishes local coefficient-curvature estimates uniformly over forests of unbounded
                                                 maximum degree and over activities tending to zero. Combined with the fixed-density theorem
                                                 of Fang, Lu, Nevo, Yao, and Zheng, these estimates complete the strict log-concavity interval
                                                 down to rank one through ⌊17α/25⌋; the terminal fraction is inherited. The classical decreasing
                                                 tail then yields the barrier property. A cumulative-coefficient corona theorem transfers barrier
                                                 log-concavity of the base forest to full strict log-concavity of its leaf corona.

Theorem 1.1 (Barrier log-concavity of large forests). There exists an absolute integer N such that
every forest F of order n ≥ N satisfies

              ik+1 (F ) > ik (F )   =⇒   ik (F )2 > ik−1 (F )ik+1 (F )   (1 ≤ k < α(F )).

Moreover, for every 0 < t ≤ 1, the coefficient sequence of I(F ; tx) is strictly barrier log-concave and
unimodal. The same N works for all such t.

    Eventual forest unimodality was established by Fang, Lu, Nevo, Yao, and Zheng [23]. Theorem 1.1
strengthens that conclusion: it controls the determinant at every ascent and establishes unimodality
simultaneously for every common downward tilt. It does not assert full log-concavity or settle BL
at all forest orders.
    More recently, Zhang and Li [83, Theorem 1.1 and Propositions 1.2–1.3] state unimodality
for every finite forest in a computer-assisted proof combining coverage through order 60 with an
argument from order 59. Li, Vallier, and Zhang [65, Theorem 1.1 and Section 1] present a second
proof, treating orders at most 24 by exact counting and orders at least 25 by a moment argument
with finite computational checks. They explicitly build on Zhang and Li’s decomposition and other
inputs, and report a Lean 4 formalization. The latter manuscript is explicitly marked provisional.
    Both all-order statements concern the unweighted sequence of I(F ; x). Unimodality alone does
not imply the barrier condition or unimodality of I(F ; tx) for every 0 < t ≤ 1; the Gaussian example
in Section 6.1 illustrates this distinction for positive sequences. Neither all-order unimodality
theorem is a premise of the main results below. The separately stated quantitative input from [65]
is used only in the optional supplement.
    A graph is well-covered if all its maximal independent sets have cardinality α(G). Write µ(F )
for its maximum matching size and z(F ) for its number of isolated vertices. Our main structural
application reaches full log-concavity.

Theorem 1.2 (Log-concavity of large well-covered forests). Let N be the threshold in Theorem 1.1.
Every well-covered forest F with

                         µ(F ) ≥ N,      equivalently      |V (F )| − z(F ) ≥ 2N

has a strictly log-concave independence polynomial at every internal index. The conclusion is
preserved under every common positive coefficient tilt. In particular, every well-covered forest
without isolated vertices of order at least 2N , and hence every well-covered tree of that order, has a
strictly log-concave independence polynomial.

    The hypothesis is on the nonisolated part. A well-covered forest has the form (H ◦ K1 ) ⊔ z(F )K1 ,
where H is a forest and |V (H)| = µ(F ); the classical structural background includes Levit–Mandrescu
[42, 46]. The complete proof in Section 5 applies BL to the whole base forest H and then a cumulative-
coefficient corona transfer. No componentwise lower order bound and no generic BL product-closure
assertion are used. A second application giving log-concavity at every internal rank concerns
palindromic independence polynomials; Corollary 4.3 gives strict log-concavity and exact central
modes under the same hypothesis that the order is sufficiently large.

1.2   Relation to earlier methods
The root recursion, bipartition damping, and Fourier second-difference framework are adapted from
[23, Sections 4–6]; the occupation-odds recursion also underlies Weitz’s tree method [80]. Related
response recursions and innovation-variance identities appear in Zhang–Li [83, Section 7.1, (83)–(86)]
and Li–Vallier–Zhang [65, Section 4.3, Lemmas 4.24–4.25]. Those identities are not claimed as
new here. The additional weighted operator-norm and predictable-clock estimates control third
moments, clock fluctuations, and hence curvature uniformly in activity and maximum degree. A
point-probability error o(σ −1 ) alone does not decide a second difference of order σ −3 .
    Vanishing activity is already allowed in the bounded-degree local limit theorem of Jain, Perkins,
Sah, and Sawhney [37, Theorem 1.5]: for fixed ∆ ≥ 3 and 0 < δ < 1, it allows λn → 0 with
nλn → ∞ and λn ≤ (1 − δ)λc (∆), where λc (∆) = (∆ − 1)∆−1 /(∆ − 2)∆ . Jain, Michelen, Pham,
and Vuong [38, Proposition 10] give high-order Edgeworth expansions at bounded degree with a
fixed positive lower activity bound. Our contribution is simultaneous degree/activity uniformity at
the coefficient-curvature scale, not vanishing activity or high-order approximation alone. Classical



                                                  3
antecedents include Bender [14] and Dobrushin–Tirozzi [21]; zero-based criteria [41, 72] impose
different hypotheses. Pre-Lorentzian certificates [15, 66] concern specified graph constructions.
    The supplement records additional variance bounds in relation to Davies–Sandhu–Tan [20] and
Zhang–Xu [84], as well as a normal-approximation consequence using Raič’s explicit Berry–Esseen
bound [75]. Those auxiliary refinements are not hidden inputs to the proof below. The sharp-prefix
companion [61] proves the first twelve LC inequalities at every forest order; its certificates are not
premises here.

1.3   Earlier partial unimodality and special graph classes
The distinction between a theorem about a whole sequence and one about prescribed parts of it has a
substantial history. Alavi, Malde, Schwenk, and Erdős [1] posed the tree/forest unimodality question
in the setting of essentially unrestricted coefficient orderings for general graphs. The surveys of Levit
and Mandrescu [47, 50] organize the earlier results, including partial unimodality: a nondecreasing
initial segment and a nonincreasing terminal segment, with a possibly unresolved middle. That
terminology does not assert log-concavity of the initial segment or the BL implication used here.
We record the principal deterministic bounds and the relevant positive families separately.

Initial and terminal inequalities. Write a = α(G) and n = |V (G)| in this historical discussion.
Michael and Traves [71] proved i0 ≤ · · · ≤ i⌈a/2⌉ for well-covered graphs. The failure of full
unimodality for general well-covered graphs, and the eventual resolution of the permitted coefficient-
ordering problem by Cutler and Pebody [19], make this a proper partial result, not an all-rank
theorem. Levit and Mandrescu [48] also construct connected well-covered counterexamples for every
independence number at least eight. Levit and Mandrescu’s very well-covered, quasi-regularizable,
and perfect-graph results [52], together with their König–Egerváry formulation [49], give the terminal
inequality
                                         i⌈(2a−1)/3⌉ ≥ · · · ≥ ia
for forests; for very well-covered graphs it accompanies the initial segment through ⌈a/2⌉. That
work also proves unimodality for very well-covered graphs with a ≤ 9 and LC for those with a ≤ 5.
The perfect-graph statement has the clique-number cutoff ⌈(ωa − 1)/(ω + 1)⌉. The 2007 chapter
develops the earlier 2004 preprint; these are not counted as two independent discoveries.
    Levit and Mandrescu [57, Theorem 2.1] also prove that if κ|A| ≤ |N (A)| for every independent
set A, where κ > 0, then                                      
                                   (k + 1)ik+1 ≤ n − (κ + 1)k ik .
This gives a nonincreasing tail beginning at ⌈(n − 1)/(κ + 2)⌉ when that index lies in the support.
We use κ, not the source’s λ, to avoid confusing this expansion parameter with physical activity.
For connected 1-well-covered graphs on more than two vertices, their results give an initial segment
through ⌈2a/3⌉ and a terminal segment from ⌈(n − 1)/3⌉. Here 1-well-covered means that the graph
and every single-vertex deletion are well-covered. Galvin [26] studies other partial-unimodality
regions in regular bipartite graphs; those hypotheses should not be replaced by bipartiteness alone.
    Basit and Galvin [7, Theorems 3, 5, and 6] prove a nonincreasing tail from ⌈a(n − 1)/(n + a)⌉
for every graph. If every maximal independent set has size at least β, they prove an initial segment
through ⌈β/2⌉; for an n-vertex tree this yields ⌈(n − a + 1)/4⌉. These are prior monotonicity bounds,
distinct from the newer forest initial segment through ⌈n/4⌉ and the fixed-density LC interval in
[23]. None of these monotone-segment conclusions by itself asserts a nonnegative Turán determinant
at all of its indices.

Levit and Mandrescu [53] treats G = H ◦ 2K1 , obtained by attaching two private leaves at each
vertex of H. With a = α(G), not α(H), its stated partial ranges are
                           i0 ≤ · · · ≤ i⌊(2a+2)/5⌋ ,       i⌈(3a−2)/5⌉ ≥ · · · ≥ ia .
Their symmetry work [51, 55] and Mandrescu’s later palindromicity argument [69] belong to this line
of research; the latter proves full unimodality for H ◦ 2K1 . Symmetric tree constructions and their
relation to γ-positivity are studied in [32]; further comparisons appear in the quantitative supplement.
These operations differ from the single-leaf corona H ◦ K1 used in the present comparison.
    For the single-leaf corona, Levit and Mandrescu [44] proved unimodality when the base has
independence number at most four; Chen and Wang [17] extended that condition to eight. The
early well-covered-tree papers [43, 45, 46] supply positive families including well-covered spiders and
centipedes. Further coefficient identities and root correspondences for the single-leaf corona appear
in [54, 56]. Brown and Cameron [16] prove strict LC after sufficiently many iterated leafy extensions
of a fixed graph, while Galvin and Hilyard [27, Proposition 1.10] obtain LC by attaching sufficiently
many leaves to every vertex of a fixed graph. The number of iterations, the number of leaves, the
independence number of the base, and its total order are different parameters; the corresponding
hypotheses are not interchangeable. Hoang, Levit, Mandrescu, and Pham [34] establish coefficient
inequalities for Wp graphs and unimodality of clique coronas of stars. These results do not assert
full LC for every single-leaf corona.

Tree families and stronger algebraic certificates. Hamidoune’s claw-free LC theorem [30]
and the real-rootedness theorem of Chudnovsky and Seymour [18] are important tools behind several
constructions. A claw-free tree is a path, so neither theorem covers arbitrary trees. Z.-F. Zhu
[85] treats centipedes and related families. Wang and B.-X. Zhu [79] use factorizations for graph
concatenations, including specified regular caterpillars. Galvin and Hilyard [27] establish LC for
alternating pendant-decoration families. Bahls, Ethridge, and Szabo [5] prove unimodality for
nonregular caterpillars satisfying explicit ordering and growth conditions on their leaf counts, not
for all caterpillars.
    The path-like constructions of Bahls and Salazar [2], Bahls [3], and Bahls, Bailey, and Olsen [4]
give further symmetric-unimodal, LC, or real-rooted families, according to the particular construction.
Bencs [13] uses stable-path-tree methods to prove real-rootedness for families including centipedes
and Fibonacci trees. Partial-order and direct LC approaches for specified tree families appear in
[8, 9, 88]. Mandrescu and Spivak [70] prove LC for the odd-order trees maximizing the number of
maximal independent sets and for certain even-order cases; “maximal trees” there is an extremal
class, not all trees.
    Graph-building and rooted-product methods supply additional families [68, 86, 89, 90, 91, 67].
Their hypotheses concern the chosen building blocks and rooted states; none licenses arbitrary tree
gluing. Bendjeddou and Hardiman [15] give Lorentzian certificates for specified graph constructions.
More recently, Li, Li, Yang, and Zhang [63] prove LC for all spiders, that is, trees with at most
one vertex of degree greater than two, by a chromatic-symmetric-function method. Grace Li [64,
Theorems 1.4–1.5] proves unimodality for both T3,m,n and T3,m,n ∗     with m, n ≥ 1, the two source-
defined families containing the original tree LC counterexamples. That positive result is important
context: an LC failure within such a family does not constitute a unimodality failure. It is not cited
here as a general BL or all-forest theorem.

Random trees and finite evidence. Heilman [31] obtains high-probability partial unimodality
for random trees and separate sparse-random-graph models. For a uniform random labeled tree, the

dependence polynomials. Here ultra log-concavity uses normalization by k at the ambient order
n; the zero coefficients at ranks zero and one are retained. Complementarity does not transfer
these conclusions to I(G; x). Indeed, let G = K6 ∨ 6K1 , where ∨ adds every edge between the two

stronger prefix that proves it. The fixed-density endpoint and the classical tail remain credited
above. The forest-corona consequence in Section 5 uses the complete transfer proved there, rather
than any claim that all well-covered graphs or all single-leaf coronas are already LC. The historical
results in this subsection are context, not additional premises of the central-curvature proof.

form H ◦ K1 for a tree H, with |V (T )| = 2|V (H)|. Conversely, every such corona is well-covered.

Proof. The characterization is classical [24, 46]; we give its tree proof including the small cases. In a
nontrivial tree, both bipartition classes are maximal independent sets. Well-coveredness makes both
have size α(T ). If they are L, R, then S ∪ (R \ N (S)) is independent for S ⊆ L, giving |N (S)| ≥ |S|.
Hall’s theorem supplies a perfect matching. Every matching edge must be pendant: if uv is not,
choose neighbors u′ ̸= v of u and v ′ ̸= u of v. Acyclicity makes u′ , v ′ distinct and nonadjacent. A
maximal independent set extending {u′ , v ′ } misses both u, v and has at most one vertex in every
other matching edge. Its size is at most α(T ) − 1, a contradiction. For T = K2 , use H = K1 .
Otherwise, every nonleaf has exactly one leaf neighbor, and the induced subgraph of nonleaves is
connected and acyclic. It is H. Conversely, every maximal independent set in H ◦ K1 contains
exactly one vertex from each old-vertex/private-leaf pair.

Proof of Theorem 1.2. A maximal independent set in a disjoint union restricts to a maximal in-
dependent set in each component, and any such componentwise choices combine to a maximal
independent set. Thus a forest is well-covered if and only if each component is well-covered. Apply
Lemma 5.3 to every nontrivial component to write

Isolated vertices and scope. The matching-number hypothesis concerns the nonisolated part,
not total order enlarged by isolated vertices. Adding isolated vertices leaves µ(F ) unchanged. For
example, K2 ⊔ sK1 has matching number one at arbitrarily large orders; it is not a counterexample
to log-concavity, but it does not force the base above N . This argument does not exclude a different
large-total-order theorem for well-covered forests. For any family of base trees known to be BL, the
single-leaf corona is strictly LC at every order. This applies, for example, to the binary spherical
family under the hypotheses and proof inputs of [59]. No generic BL product closure is used.

Relation to prior results. Levit–Mandrescu [46, Theorems 4, 5, and 7] proved LC for several
corona classes, including the coronas of stars and paths. Brown–Cameron [16, Theorem 2.4] prove
strict LC after sufficiently many iterated leafy extensions of each fixed graph; the required number
depends on the roots of that graph’s polynomial. Here one extension suffices uniformly over all
sufficiently large forest bases. The published clique-corona theorem in [33] instead guarantees LC
when the attached clique size is sufficiently large relative to the base. It is not used as an all-base,
fixed-K1 theorem. The cumulative-coefficient transfer and the eventual forest theorem are the two
ingredients in the present application. The conclusion does not assert log-concavity for well-covered
forests of every order.


6    Conclusion and open problems
Every sufficiently large forest is strictly barrier log-concave, and every sufficiently large well-covered
tree is strictly log-concave. The stronger well-covered-forest statement uses matching number rather
than total order when isolated vertices are allowed. These results follow from a uniform curvature
theorem, completion of the known forest prefix, and the corona transfer. Coefficient reflection also
gives full strict log-concavity and exact central modes for sufficiently large palindromic forests. The
principal question here is whether the order restriction can be removed from the barrier conclusion.
The all-order unweighted unimodality statements in [83, 65] do not by themselves answer that
stronger question.

Conjecture 6.1 (Barrier log-concavity for trees). For every finite tree T and every 1 ≤ k < α(T ),

                         ik+1 (T ) > ik (T )   =⇒    ik (T )2 ≥ ik−1 (T )ik+1 (T ).

    Equivalently, the independence polynomial of every tree is unimodal under every common
downward activity: I(T ; tx) is unimodal for each 0 < t ≤ 1. For a positive sequence, this equivalence
follows directly from its adjacent ratios. A barrier failure has ratios rk+1 > max{1, rk }; choosing
1/rk+1 < t < min{1, 1/rk } creates a strict valley. Conversely, a downward tilt preserves the
barrier implication and hence unimodality. Ordinary log-concavity counterexamples do not refute
Conjecture 6.1: the conjecture allows curvature failures on the decreasing portion, but excludes
them at an ascending center. On the subclass of trees with palindromic independence polynomials,
Lemma 4.2 makes the BL assertion equivalent to ordinary log-concavity. Corollary 4.3 proves the
strict eventual version without a new supplementary premise.

Conjecture 6.2 (Barrier log-concavity for forests). For every finite forest F , the polynomial I(F ; tx)
is unimodal for every 0 < t ≤ 1; equivalently, I(F ; x) is barrier log-concave.

   The forest assertion includes the tree assertion. However, products of barrier log-concave
sequences cannot be assumed to retain that property, so a tree theorem would require an additional
argument to cover disconnected forests. The present theorem proves the forest assertion once the

                                                    19
order is large enough. The sharp-prefix companion [61] supplies an all-order guarantee at the first
twelve coefficient indices.

Problem 6.3 (Prescribed-prefix curvature). With q(F ) = ⌈(2α(F ) − 1)/3⌉, determine whether
every forest satisfies ∆k (F ) ≥ 0 at each internal center k < q(F ).

    This asks for more than barrier log-concavity: the prescribed interval may contain descending
centers after the actual mode. The present paper establishes the condition for sufficiently large
forests.

Problem 6.4 (Well-covered forests). Does every well-covered forest have a log-concave independence
polynomial?

    Conjecture 6.2 and Section 5 would give the stronger strict conclusion at all internal indices,
including after adjoining isolated vertices. The matching-number threshold in Theorem 1.2 is the
sufficient condition proved here; it is not asserted to be necessary.

Problem 6.5 (Effective bounds and modes). Obtain a substantially smaller explicit order threshold
for the eventual barrier theorem, and determine how accurately the first mode can be located from
structural data or a bounded number of activity moments.

   A useful mode algorithm must account for the cost of computing the coefficients or moments it
queries. An exact ratio-crossing characterization alone is not a running-time bound. A structural
proof of Conjecture 6.1 or 6.2, independent of asymptotic local-limit estimates, would address both
the shape question and the search for more intrinsic mode information.

6.1   Barrier log-concavity beyond forests
Barrier log-concavity may also strengthen unimodality theorems outside forest enumeration. The
general background includes the survey of Stanley [77]. The substantive setting is a natural
nonsymmetric rank sequence for which ordinary log-concavity actually fails. The following elementary
examples use the original cardinality ranks, including the empty object, and do not depend on the
asymptotic forest theorem.

Example 6.6 (Order ideals above an antichain). Let Pr,ℓ consist of r ≥ 2 mutually incomparable
elements below an ℓ-element chain, where ℓ ≥ 1. An order ideal is a subset containing every
predecessor of each of its elements. Ordered by inclusion, the ideals form the distributive lattice
J(Pr,ℓ ); see [78, Chapter 3]. Counting ideals by cardinality gives

Indeed, an ideal either is a subset of the antichain or contains the whole antichain followed by a
nonempty initial segment of the chain. The coefficient sequence is strictly BL: before rank r the
internal triples are binomial, and from rank r onward the coefficients are all one, so no new ascent
occurs. Nevertheless, its determinant at rank r is 1 − r < 0. For example, R4,1 has coefficients

                                      (1, 4, 6, 4, 1, 1),     ∆4 = −3.

Thus even a five-element poset gives a nonsymmetric, strictly BL ideal-rank sequence that is not
log-concave. The ratio at the failure is 1/r, which can be arbitrarily small in this family.

    The example gives a structural preservation rule. If the ideal-rank sequence of a finite nonempty
poset P is BL, adjoining a chain above every element of P preserves BL: the rank polynomial simply
acquires a terminal string of ones. Moreover, if P has b ≥ 2 maximal elements, the previous terminal
rank becomes an internal center with determinant 1 − b < 0. This follows because the coefficient of
rank |P | − 1 is exactly the number of maximal elements. The statement also holds with strict BL
in place of BL. It is a particular operation on posets, not a closure assertion for arbitrary sums or
products of BL polynomials.
