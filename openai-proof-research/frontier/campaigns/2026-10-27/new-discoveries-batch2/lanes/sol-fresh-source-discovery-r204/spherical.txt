                                                                                    Abstract
                                                We study closed sets for which, at every boundary point, there exists a containing
                                                supporting ball whose prescribed radius may vary from point to point. This
                                                extends the notion of spherical support from constant to variable radii. The
                                                variable-radius setting presents a new difficulty: unlike the constant-radius case,
                                                the intersection of the supporting balls need not be strongly convex with respect
                                                to the prescribed radii. We overcome this difficulty by associating with every
                                                such set a canonical set and an induced radius function determined by the active
                                                supporting balls. For continuous prescribed radii, our main result establishes
                                                a geometric dichotomy: the original set either coincides with the canonical set
                                                and is strongly convex with respect to the prescribed radii, or it is contained
                                                in the boundary of the canonical set, which is strongly convex with respect to
                                                the induced radii. An example shows that the induced radii cannot, in general,
                                                be replaced by the prescribed ones in the second alternative. We also recover
                                                the constant-radius theory and characterize variable-radius strong convexity in
                                                terms of the spherical support of the boundary.

                                                Keywords: Spherically supported sets, Strong convexity, Proximal analysis, Convex
                                                analysis




                                           1 Introduction
                                           1.1 Geometric motivation and overview
                                           Supporting hyperplanes are a fundamental tool for describing the geometry of convex
                                           sets. Replacing hyperplanes by spheres leads to stronger geometric requirements: a
                                           supporting ball must contain the entire set and touch it at the boundary point under
                                           consideration. The radius of the ball measures the strength of this requirement, with


                                                                                        1
smaller radii imposing a tighter geometric constraint. Such descriptions are central to
the study of strong convexity, a notion with connections to optimization, variational
analysis, and approximation theory; see [1, 3, 6, 14].
    Two different requirements arise naturally when the supporting radius is fixed.
One may ask for a containing supporting ball of that radius at every boundary point,
or require such a ball in every outward normal direction at each boundary point.
The first requirement defines spherical support, whereas the second describes strong
convexity. Although the second is more demanding, the two are closely related. For a
fixed supporting radius, spherical support implies strong convexity whenever the set
has nonempty interior. More generally, a spherically supported set either is strongly
convex or lies in the boundary of a strongly convex set; see [7, 8].
    Allowing the supporting radius to vary along the boundary provides a more flex-
ible description of the geometry. Variable-radius strong convexity was studied in [6].
The question addressed here is whether the geometric relationship between spherical
support and strong convexity persists when the radius is prescribed separately at each
boundary point. A difficulty arises because intersecting supporting balls with different
radii need not preserve strong convexity with respect to the prescribed radii.
    We answer this question by constructing a canonical containing set as the intersec-
tion of the prescribed supporting balls. When the prescribed radius varies continuously,
the original set either coincides with this set and is strongly convex with respect to the
prescribed radii, or lies in its boundary. In the latter case, the canonical set is strongly
convex with respect to induced radii, whose necessity is illustrated by an example.
We also characterize variable-radius strong convexity through spherical support of
the boundary, extending [7]. The next subsection provides the precise definitions, the
relevant background, and a detailed presentation of our contributions.

1.2 Mathematical background and contributions
A nonempty closed set S ⊂ Rn is said to be r-strongly convex, for some r > 0, if it
can be represented as an intersection of closed balls of radius r. Equivalently, at every
boundary point s of S and for every unit proximal normal direction ζ to S at s, one
has S ⊂ B (s − rζ ; r), where B (x; ρ) denotes the closed ball of radius ρ centered at x.
This notion admits several other equivalent geometric and analytic characterizations;
see [1, 3, 14] and the references therein. A detailed historical account can be found in
the introduction of [6].
    The notion of an r-spherically supported set was introduced in [8]. A nonempty
closed set S ⊂ Rn is r-spherically supported if, at every boundary point s, there
exists a unit proximal normal direction ζ to S at s such that S ⊂ B (s − rζ ; r).
Thus, strong convexity requires this inclusion in every unit proximal normal direction,
whereas spherical support requires it in at least one such direction at each boundary
point. Every r-strongly convex set is therefore r-spherically supported. The converse
fails in general, as demonstrated in [8] by taking S to be the unit circle in the plane.
Nevertheless, Nour and Takche proved in [8, Theorem 1.1] that a nonempty closed
set S not reduced to a singleton is r-strongly convex if and only if it is r-spherically
supported and has nonempty interior. This equivalence was subsequently strengthened
in [7, Theorem 4]. Indeed, after it was proved in [7, Theorem 3] that an r-spherically


                                             2
supported set is either r-strongly convex or is contained in the boundary of a nonempty
closed r-strongly convex set, it was established in [7, Theorem 4] that a nonempty
closed set S not reduced to a singleton is r-strongly convex if and only if it is bounded,
has nonempty interior, and its boundary is r-spherically supported.
     In [6], Nacry and Thibault extended the notion of strong convexity to variable radii
by introducing the class of r(·)-strongly convex sets. For such sets, the supporting
radius at each boundary point s is prescribed by the value r(s) of a positive function
r(·) defined on the boundary. As in the constant-radius setting, they established sev-
eral geometric and analytic characterizations of this class. This naturally raises the
following question: can the results of [7] be extended to the variable-radius setting by
introducing a corresponding notion of r(·)-spherically supported sets?
     The purpose of the present paper is to provide an affirmative answer to this ques-
tion, albeit with an essential modification involving an induced radius function. Given
a positive function r : bdry S −→]0, +∞[, we say that S is r(·)-spherically supported
if, for every s ∈ bdry S , there exists a unit proximal normal direction ζ to S at s such
that                                                    

    Our first main result establishes the fundamental properties of AS . Under the
continuity of r(·), we prove that S , AS , and GS are compact and that AS is R-strongly
convex. When S is not a singleton, we obtain a representation of the normal cone to
AS in terms of the active supporting balls. We further prove that AS is ρ(·)-strongly
convex and that ρ(·) is upper semicontinuous. These properties lead to the principal
geometric characterization of the paper: under the continuity of r(·), every nonempty
closed r(·)-spherically supported set S satisfies one of the following two conclusions:
• S = AS and S is r(·)-strongly convex;
• S is contained in the boundary of the nonempty, compact, and ρ(·)-strongly convex
  set AS .

The first assertion holds whenever S has nonempty interior, whereas the second holds
whenever S has empty interior. Thus, the dichotomy known in the constant-radius
setting persists for variable radii. In the second alternative, however, the naturally
induced function ρ(·) replaces the prescribed function r(·). This distinction is essential.
We provide an explicit two-point example showing that, in general, the induced radius
function cannot be replaced by the prescribed radius function at every point of S . On
the other hand, when r(·) is constant, we show that ρ(·) coincides with r and therefore
recover [7, Theorem 3]. We conclude with a characterization of r(·)-strongly convex
sets that extends [7, Theorem 4] to the variable-radius setting.
    The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the notation and the
basic definitions from nonsmooth and convex analysis used throughout the paper.
In Section 3, we construct the canonical set AS and the induced radius function
ρ(·), establish their main properties, and prove the geometric characterization of r(·)-
spherically supported sets. The section also contains examples illustrating the roles
of the prescribed and induced radii. Finally, Appendix A contains the proof of the
approximation result for extreme normal directions used in the main characterization.
