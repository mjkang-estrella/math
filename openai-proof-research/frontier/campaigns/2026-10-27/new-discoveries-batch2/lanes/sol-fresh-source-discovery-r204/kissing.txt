                                                  NEW UPPER BOUNDS FOR AVERAGE KISSING NUMBERS

                                                                      MARK BOCKHAUS AND ALEXEY GLAZYRIN




                                                  Abstract. A packing of balls in Euclidean space is a set of balls with non-overlapping
                                                  interiors. For a finite packing of balls, its average contact number is the average number




arXiv:2610.36675v1 [math.MG] 27 Oct 2026
                                                  of balls tangent to a ball in this packing, that is, twice the total number of tangencies
                                                  divided by the number of balls. The average kissing number of the space is defined as
                                                  the supremum of average contact numbers over all finite ball packings. In this paper, we
                                                  present a new semidefinite programming approach to average kissing numbers and improve
                                                  upon known upper bounds in all dimensions 3 through 30. In particular, we provide the
                                                  first improvements to the upper bounds in dimensions 10 through 30 obtained from kissing
                                                  numbers. We also fully solve the average kissing number problem in dimensions 8 and 24 by
                                                  showing that the average kissing numbers coincide with the kissing numbers, 240 and 196560,
                                                  respectively. These values are limits of average contact numbers of finite subpackings from
                                                  the E8 and Leech lattices.



                                                                                    1. Introduction
                                              Over three hundred years ago, the kissing number problem was born during a discussion
                                           between Isaac Newton and David Gregory [38], [33], [3]. The problem in question was: Given
                                           a unit three-dimensional sphere, how many non-overlapping unit spheres can simultaneously
                                           touch this initial sphere? While the problem was initially conceived in three dimensions, the
                                           idea can easily be extended to any dimension. Thus, the kissing number problem can be
                                           generalized as: Given an n-dimensional unit ball, how many non-overlapping n-dimensional
                                           unit balls can simultaneously touch this initial ball? This problem has been the subject of
                                           significant research, with the kissing number, τn , being known exactly only in dimensions
                                           1, 2, 3, 4, 8 and 24 [35], [28], [26], [17], [27], [1], [23], [30].
                                              While the kissing number problem requires all balls to be congruent, it is also of interest
                                           to consider what may happen when we allow balls of different radii. Of course, for any
                                           particular ball we can choose an arbitrarily large number of small balls tangent to it, but
                                           the average number of tangencies cannot grow indefinitely. Thus it would be interesting to
                                           determine how large this average number can be for a packing of balls. We clarify that a
                                           ball packing is a set of n-dimensional balls (of any radii) with pairwise disjoint interiors. We
                                           now formalize these notions. Let P be a finite packing of n-dimensional balls in Rn . Let E
                                           be the set of unordered pairs of balls that are tangent to each other or, in other words, the
                                           set of edges in the contact graph of the packing. Then the average contact number of the
                                           packing is given by:

                                           the kissing number problem, we are interested in maximizing this value. Thus, for each

dimension, n, we define the average kissing number as:

                     κn = sup{k(P ) : P is a finite packing of balls in Rn }

   At this point, we also note that κn is the same for all n-dimensional spaces of constant
curvature, that is, for Euclidean spaces, spheres, and hyperbolic spaces, and does not depend
on the curvature. Moreover, Möbius transformations also preserve the contact number of a
ball packing, if the relative position of the balls is unchanged. All this makes the problem
of finding the average kissing number, on the one hand, more fundamental than the kissing
number problem and, on the other hand, more difficult.
   Finding κ2 is straightforward. The contact graph of any packing of disks is planar. The
average degree of a planar graph is not larger than 6 by Euler’s formula. This bound is
attained as a limit for finite configurations from the hexagonal packing of equal disks.
   The same idea for finding lower bounds works in all dimensions. In particular, the lattice
kissing number, τn∗ , which is the largest kissing number for all lattices in Rn , provides a lower
bound for the average kissing number. In some dimensions, non-lattice periodic packings
of unit balls provide a larger average contact number; however, in many cases, the lattice
kissing number remains the best known lower bound.
   In terms of upper bounds, a simple argument shows that in any dimension κn ≤ 2τn .
Indeed, we can order the balls of a packing in increasing order by size. If some balls are of
the same size, we choose an arbitrary order between them. Then for any ball in a packing, it
has no more than τn neighbors occurring later in the chosen order. Counting oriented edges
(from small to large) in a contact graph we immediately get the required bound.
   The problem of determining average kissing numbers was first considered by Kuperberg
and Schramm [20]. They found the first nontrivial bounds for κ3 . Their lower bound

   We note that all upper bounds so far (and our paper is not an exception) use only the local
information about the packing; that is, the packing condition can be relaxed to "all balls
tangent to the same ball do not overlap". It would be interesting to find arguments that
genuinely use the non-overlapping condition akin to how it is done for the sphere packing
problem [18], [5], [37], [6]. [12, Theorem 4.1] extends the framework of [5] and [8] to the
problem of average kissing numbers but it works only when radii are taken from a closed
4                         MARK BOCKHAUS AND ALEXEY GLAZYRIN

interval or a finite set and, in practice, its bounds do not seem to improve bounds produced
by local constraints.
   The paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, we describe the shell approach of Ku-
perberg and Schramm and all the recent upper bounds stemming from it. In Section 3 we
describe the linear programming bound for regular kissing numbers. Our semidefinite pro-
gram essentially contains the linear program of Delsarte as its diagonal slice. In Section 4 we
develop a new approach based on auxiliary functions and explain why it generalizes previous
attempts to solve the problem. In Section 5 we show how the new approach can be imple-
mented as a semidefinite program. In Section 6 we explain how we found exact polynomial
certificates solving the average kissing number problem in dimensions 8 and 24. Finally, in
Section 7 we show how semidefinite programs can be extended to incorporate optimization
over auxiliary functions as well and use it to find upper bounds in other dimensions.

                         2. Upper bounds via shell packings
   In this section we briefly describe how average kissing numbers can be bounded via densi-
ties of auxiliary packings of spherical caps on spherical shells. We consider, without loss of
generality, a unit sphere centered at the origin, S n−1 . We then consider a spherical shell of
radius ρ, 1 < ρ < 3, centered at the origin. Thus, we denote this spherical shell by ρS n−1 .
Let Cr be a ball of radius r > 0 that is tangent to the central ball. We consider the spherical
cap formed by the intersection of this spherical shell and the tangent ball, ρS n−1 ∩ Cr . We
denote the normalized volume of this spherical cap, formed by Sρ (r) = ρS n−1 ∩ Cr , by
                             An,ρ (r) := Vol(Sρ (r))/ Vol(ρS n−1 ).
   The intersections of the tangent balls and the spherical shell form a spherical cap packing.
We are interested in the proportion of the spherical shell, ρS n−1 , that can be taken up by
any given cap packing. For a dimension n and the radius of the spherical shell, ρ, we denote
by densn (ρ) the supremum over all possible cap packings of the proportion of the spherical
shell that is covered by the spherical caps Sρ (r). We clarify that the supremum is taken
over packings induced by non-overlapping balls tangent to the unit ball. The key fact here
is that An,ρ (r) + An,ρ (1/r) reaches its minimum at r = 1. This was shown by Kuperberg and
Schramm [20] in dimension 3 and then by Glazyrin [16] in all dimensions n ≥ 3. A simple
double counting of these sums for all pairs of tangent balls leads to the following bound.

that is, the configuration of balls tangent to the same central ball. This configuration may
be viewed as an extension of the regular kissing configuration. Therefore, it makes sense that
we may obtain bounds on the average kissing number by extending the approaches used to
bound the kissing number.

Remark 4.7. We note that Theorems 4.4 and 4.6 are actually equivalent in the sense that

s = 0 of B (or s = 1), ∂s h ≤ 0 (or ∂s h ≥ 0) on B, ∂u h(p) = ∂v h(p) = 0, and the 2 × 2 Hessian
of h in (u, v) is negative semidefinite at every point of the face of B, then h is non-positive
on the box. Note that several of these hypotheses hold because of the equalities we imposed
in the rounding step. Thus, with Bernstein subdivision and using these results for boxes
with a zero, we may rigorously prove non-positivity on the region described in condition (3).
                    NEW UPPER BOUNDS FOR AVERAGE KISSING NUMBERS                                 17

   Conditions (4) and (5) are also verified with Bernstein subdivision (although, we have
ensured condition (5) holds via construction). Condition (6) was satisfied by construction of
a, and condition (7) can easily be verified.
   After verifying that our solution satisfies each of the constraints from Theorem 4.6, we
then conclude that we have a valid upper bound on the average kissing number. Namely,
the approach described provides upper bounds of 240 and 196560 in 8 and 24 dimensions,
respectively. The E8 lattice and Leech lattice show that these bounds are achieved as the
limits of average contact numbers of their finite subpackings, and thus, we conclude that
κ8 = 240 and κ24 = 196560.
   As we noted earlier in this section, the solutions we found were not unique. We chose
several weight functions, a, that led to exact solutions. Even varying x0 within these weight
functions, the solution often remained exact.

Remark 7.1. Note that there remains an omission from the optimization problem. Namely,
we do not optimize over x0 . Unfortunately, we could not find a way to include x0 as a
variable in the semidefinite program solver. We must choose x0 ∈ (0, 1) explicitly in each
dimension. However, there are still some observations we may make regarding a good choice
for x0 . As we mentioned above, x0 cannot be too close to zero, as if it were, then very small
balls (that may fit between larger balls) would have a positive contribution to a summation
of the weights. Also, x0 cannot be too close to 1, as if it were, then we may create an almost
kissing configuration with balls of radius 1/x0 , in which case the bound would simply be 2τn
or very close to it. With that being said, in general, we found that an "optimal" x0 gets
larger (approaching one) as we increase the dimension. In practice, an appropriate x0 was
found through a numerical search over simple rational choices.
   We now write Problem (6) as a semidefinite program. The ideas are very similar to those
needed to turn Problem (3) into a semidefinite program, with further added conditions. We
now choose x0 to be a simple rational number, with the general observation that it gets closer
to 1 as the dimension increases. We also note that the degree of Gegenbauer polynomials
tends to grow as the dimension goes higher. In general, we used the same (or one higher)
degree, d, as the one that provides the best linear programming upper bound for the kissing
number. Thus, as we reach higher dimensions, obtaining the best possible bounds becomes
limited by computing power. While we use a kernel degree, N , of five for lower dimensions,
due to increased computing cost compared to relatively small improvements on the obtained
bound, we lower this kernel degree to four for higher dimensions. With these choices of
parameters, we now can solve the program.
   After solving the optimization problem, we follow a similar process to that described in the
discussion about solutions in 8 and 24 dimensions. However, in the rational rounding process,
we must now round matrices A0 , . . . , Ad in addition to the bordered matrix corresponding
to condition (2). In this way, the weight function G is rounded as well. We again enforce
positive semidefinite conditions by construction. In other dimensions, there are no exact
lattice conditions to enforce and no exact bound that must be achieved. This makes non-
positivity conditions slightly easier. Particularly, for condition (3), we solve the semidefinite
program by requiring a function, f , that is less than some small −ε. Therefore, we assure
that non-positivity holds after rounding. We do a similar process for conditions (4) and (5),

where we implement this room for error with a small −ε on our given region. However, we
still formally verify conditions (3)-(5) via Bernstein subdivision. With the room for error
introduced, Bernstein subdivision was sufficient (there were no exact zeros, meaning that the
process could successfully complete). Condition (6) can be manually verified. After verifying
that the six conditions from Theorem 4.4 are met by the rounded solution, we obtain new
upper bounds to the average kissing number in dimensions 3 through 30.
