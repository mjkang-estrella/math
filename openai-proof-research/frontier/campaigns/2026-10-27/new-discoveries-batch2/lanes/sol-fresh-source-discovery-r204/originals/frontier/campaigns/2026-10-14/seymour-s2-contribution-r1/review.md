## Why the added operation is substantial

The candidate's decisive addition is a mechanism for closing the source's extremal argument when the inherited subset inequality is only weak. After removing the unique good vertex z with sole successor w, it keeps the exceptional boundary information: every nonempty subset has deficit at most zero, and a subset whose first two images avoid w has deficit at most minus one. This is stronger information than merely applying ordinary SNC to the deleted graph, but weaker than the source's all-subset strict inequality.

Compatibility with the diagonal then selects m−1 fibers anchored inside the w-block. A base predecessor of w has a two-step path to every such anchor through the preceding internal label; a two-step base predecessor also has a lifted path. The resulting forbidden support gives those fibers a deficit gain m. All other fibers can retain the positive residual n. Summation yields n²m−m(m−1)<0 for m=n²+2, separately on both sides. Thus the old pruning objective can close even though individual subsets need not have negative fiber defect.

This is a meaningful new interface: strictness is obtained only after summing a compatibility-constrained family, using a surviving boundary witness. It is not the source blowup theorem with a larger integer parameter, an ordinary weighted specialization, or a new name for the same all-subset hypothesis. The substantiality credit belongs primarily to this boundary-plus-aggregate mechanism (Sections 3 and 5 together). Elementary minimum-counterexample facts, standard blowups, and the final pruning closure receive no independent credit.

Conjecture 2 is precisely S2; Conjecture 3 is the unique-good/sink formulation. Corollary 5 gives two vertices for that restricted class. Proposition 8 uses minimality and arc deletion to relate conjectures. These ideas deserve attribution. The candidate's subset boundary and aggregate compatible-fiber operation remove the structural restriction; they are not present in the compared proof.

[Seacrest, *Seymour's Second Neighborhood Conjecture for Subsets of Vertices*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.06293), Lemma 4 and its full proof, uses edge-minimality, deletion from a subset, and a maximal enlarging set to force a subset inequality. This is the clear deletion antecedent already credited by the pinned source. It assumes a graph with every vertex violating the required ratio. It does not retain a protected unique-good exception or obtain compatible-family aggregate strictness.

[Brantner–Brockman–Kay–Snively, *Contributions to Seymour's second neighborhood conjecture*](https://web.mit.edu/gdb/www/pdfs/contributions-to-seymour.pdf), Theorems 4.1–4.3 and proofs, provides strong-connectivity/edge-minimality deductions and lexicographic amplification of all-bad counterexamples. The product computes individual neighborhood deficits by a scaled base term plus an internal term. This establishes the product antecedent, not the special anchor exclusion and total-family count.

[Guo–Kang–Zwaneveld, *Seymour-tight orientations*, v1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.29626v1), Section 3 and Section 4 through Corollary 4.4, proves preservation of pointwise tightness under products, amplifies hypothetical counterexamples, and uses uniform modules for internal replacement. Its exact neighborhood formulas are pertinent product precedents. They do not turn the deleted exceptional successor into aggregate compatible-family strictness.

[Tahara, *Measuring the moat*, v2.1](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/42tahara/seymour-excess/main/note/measuring-the-moat-v2.1.pdf), Section 2, Section 3.2 and Theorem 4.1, gives controlled-margin constructions and a restricted small-quotient lower bound. Its out-set inclusion operation changes individual margins; its capped cycle blowups construct low-excess examples. It does not prove S2. Excess and the number of Seymour vertices must not be conflated: a single vertex with positive margin can contribute several units of excess.
