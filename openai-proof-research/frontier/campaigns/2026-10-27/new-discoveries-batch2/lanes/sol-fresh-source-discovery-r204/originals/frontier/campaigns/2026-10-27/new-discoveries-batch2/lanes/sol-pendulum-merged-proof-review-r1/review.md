## Independent mathematical audit

Set a=1-cos(2phi), so 0<a<2. On the fixed original interval the squared denominator is at least z; on the moving interval it is at least a. Interior integrals are finite. Under t=1-cos(2x), dt/dx=2sin(2x)=2sqrt(t(2-t)), positive on the open spatial interval. Thus orientation is positive and

    T = (1/2)(integral_0^a + integral_0^z)
                  1/sqrt(t(2-t)(a+z-t)) dt.

The vanishing substitution derivative at zero cannot be cancelled at the endpoint as a pointwise identity. The proof legitimately uses open-interval identities and integrability equivalence; the ordinary manuscript describes truncation and monotone limits. The remaining singularity is t^(-1/2), since a,z>0 and both upper limits are below 2. No signed improper or divergent integral is concealed by Lean's totalized integral.

For M_n=(integral_0^a+integral_0^z)t^n/sqrt(t(a+z-t))dt, put s=a+z. The change t=s sin²(theta) gives 2s^n sin^(2n)(theta)dtheta, with complementary upper angles. Consequently M_0=pi and M_1=pi(a+z)/2-2sqrt(az). Both pieces must be retained. The latter is affine minus a positive multiple of sqrt(z), hence convex. No differentiability premise is needed.

For n>=2, p=n-1/2>1. The fixed moment is an integral of nonnegative multiples of the convex inverse square root of an affine positive argument. The moving moment, after y=z-t, is the integral of (z-y)_+^p/sqrt(a+y). This reversed substitution produces positive orientation after swapping limits; the formal proof uses the interval reflection identity. For a Jensen triple choose one finite L dominating its three z-values. The kernels vanish above L and are continuous on [0,L], so their integral inequality is legitimate. It does not require integrability of the bare half-line weight. The exponent-1/2 moving kernel is not covered by this convexity rule, and the negative-exponent n=0 kernel is still worse; the exact low-order treatment is essential.

The coefficient is c_n=choose(2n,n)/8^n, not /4^n. Its recurrence is (n+1)c_(n+1)=(2n+1)c_n/4. The binomial theorem gives sum c_n t^n=(1-t/2)^(-1/2). Hence

    T = 1/(2sqrt(2)) sum_n c_n M_n.

Every summand is nonnegative. Each integration interval is finite, individual moments integrable, and the full transformed kernel integrable. MCT gives the finite equality; the formal series proof instead uses the dominated series-integral theorem with the same integrable sum. Its assumptions include absolute summability and measurable summands, supplied by nonnegativity and the pointwise HasSum. It never differentiates a series. Termwise convex inequalities pass through convergent real sums at the three fixed arguments, giving interior convexity.

At z=0, the first limiting denominator cos(2x)-cos(2phi) has a simple zero at phi because sin(2phi)>0. Its inverse square root is integrable. The formal EndpointZero proof establishes this through a closed weighted substitution with upper angle pi/2; derivative nonnegativity is needed only in the open interval. The z>=0 fixed kernels are bounded almost everywhere by that endpoint kernel; excluding x=phi is explicit in EndpointContinuity. DCT yields right continuity. The moving integral tends to zero, either by b(z)/sqrt(a) or the proved continuous clamped-kernel primitive.

At z=2, both literal intervals are finite and the denominator is at least a. For z>=2-a/2 it is uniformly at least a/2 even on the entire spatial line. EndpointTwo constructs a globally continuous clamped kernel agreeing locally with the real kernel; parametric primitive continuity then proves the literal endpoint limit with b(2)=pi/2. The independent ordinary indicator/DCT argument in the manuscript is also valid. Interior continuity is proved separately in ClosedContinuity. Finally z_i(e)=(1-e)z_i+e lies in (0,2) for 0<e<1 and preserves affine Jensen combinations. Taking e down to zero proves ConvexOn [0,2]. This closes the boundary transfer rather than assigning arbitrary convex extension values.
