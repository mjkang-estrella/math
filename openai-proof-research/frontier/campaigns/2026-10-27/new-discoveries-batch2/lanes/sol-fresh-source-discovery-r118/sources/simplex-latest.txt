Stochastic Domination of Gaussian Maxima: A Resolution of the Weak Simplex Conjecture



Abhijeet Mulgund
††thanks: This work was supported by NSF under awards 2240532 and 2217023.
Affiliation: University of Illinois Chicago

Email: mulgund2@uic.edu



Abstract
    

Let RR be an m×mm\times m correlation matrix satisfying
R−𝟏𝟏𝖳/m⪰0R-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}/m\succeq 0, let X∼𝒩⁡(0,R)X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R), and let
Z1,…,ZmZ_{1},\ldots,Z_{m} be independent standard Gaussian random variables. We prove
    





maxiXi≤stmaxiZi,\max_{i}X_{i}\leq_{\mathrm{st}}\max_{i}Z_{i},


    

with equality in distribution if and only if R=ImR=I_{m}. We use this comparison
to resolve the Weak Simplex Conjecture: among d+1d+1 equiprobable equal-energy
signals in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} transmitted over an additive white Gaussian noise channel,
the regular simplex is the unique maximizer of the average probability of
correct maximum-likelihood decoding at every positive signal-to-noise ratio. The same
comparison proves the Simplex Mean Width Conjecture and gives the exact
finite-energy performance of deterministic no-feedback AWGN codes with
equiprobable messages, no restriction on the number of channel uses, and a
maximal per-codeword energy constraint.
    

The proof uses a Gaussian product inequality for log-concave functions whose
first moments with respect to standard Gaussian measure vanish. A variational
argument chooses one exponential tilt and one truncation endpoint in each
coordinate so that this product inequality applies and a Gaussian change of
measure returns all coordinates to the prescribed common threshold. A strict
form of the product inequality also shows that, unless R=ImR=I_{m},
ℙ{X≤c𝟏}>Φ(c)m\mathbb{P}\{X\leq c\mathbf{1}\}>\Phi(c)^{m} for every finite cc, and hence gives the
distributional equality statement.
A Lean formalization is available at
https://github.com/abhmul/weak-simplex-conjecture-lean.

Keywords.
Gaussian maxima, stochastic domination, Gaussian inequalities, Weak Simplex
Conjecture, simplex mean width, AWGN codes.



1 Introduction




The Weak Simplex Conjecture traces back to an observation of Shannon reported
by Rice in 1950 [30]; the name was later coined by Massey in 1988
[21]. It asks how to place m=d+1m=d+1 equiprobable signals of equal
energy in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} when the channel adds isotropic Gaussian noise and the receiver
uses maximum-likelihood decoding. The conjecture asserts that the vertices of
a regular simplex maximize the average probability of correct decoding at
every positive signal-to-noise ratio. If the unit signal vectors are
x1,…,xmx_{1},\ldots,x_{m}, if G=(⟨xi,xj⟩)i,jG=(\langle x_{i},x_{j}\rangle)_{i,j} is their Gram matrix, and if
λ>0\lambda>0 is the signal-to-noise ratio, then the correct decoding probability satisfies





Pc​(x1,…,xm,λ)=e−λ2/2m​𝔼​exp⁡{λ​max1≤i≤m​ξi},ξ∼𝒩⁡(0,G).P_{\mathrm{c}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m};\lambda)=\frac{e^{-\lambda^{2}/2}}{m}\mathbb{E}\exp\!\left\{\lambda\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\xi_{i}\right\},\qquad\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,G).

(1)


We refer to ξ\xi as the score vector for the code with Gram matrix GG,
since its coordinates are the random parts of the maximum-likelihood scores.
Thus the conjecture is equivalent to an extremal problem for the
moment-generating function of a Gaussian maximum. The difficulty lies in the
failure of the usual entrywise covariance comparisons, such as Slepian’s
inequality [34], to settle the problem: an arbitrary competing
Gram matrix need not be entrywise comparable with the regular-simplex Gram
matrix.




Our main theorem is a stochastic comparison. Write J=𝟏𝟏𝖳J=\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}. If
RR is a correlation matrix satisfying





R−1m​J⪰0,R-\frac{1}{m}J\succeq 0,

(2)


then, for X∼𝒩⁡(0,R)X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R) and independent standard Gaussian random variables
Z1,…,ZmZ_{1},\ldots,Z_{m},





ℙ{maxiXi≤c}≥Φ(c)m=ℙ{maxiZi≤c}(c∈ℝ).\mathbb{P}\!\left\{\max_{i}X_{i}\leq c\right\}\geq\Phi(c)^{m}=\mathbb{P}\!\left\{\max_{i}Z_{i}\leq c\right\}\qquad(c\in\mathbb{R}).

(3)


Equivalently, maxiXi≤stmaxiZi\max_{i}X_{i}\leq_{\mathrm{st}}\max_{i}Z_{i}. Furthermore, the two maxima have
the same distribution if and only if R=ImR=I_{m}. The common threshold in
(3) is essential: the analogous comparison for arbitrary
threshold vectors is false; see Remark 2.2. The direct
proof in Appendix B gives the stronger conclusion that the
inequality is strict at every finite cc when R≠ImR\neq I_{m}.




The coding problem is connected to (3) by adding the same
independent Gaussian random variable to every score and then rescaling. On
covariance matrices, this operation is





R=m−1m​G+1m​J.R=\frac{m-1}{m}G+\frac{1}{m}J.

(4)


It sends the regular-simplex Gram matrix
mm−1​(Im−J/m)\frac{m}{m-1}(I_{m}-J/m) to ImI_{m}. If
λ=(m−1)/m​μ\lambda=\sqrt{(m-1)/m}\,\mu, then the corresponding Gaussian maxima satisfy





e−μ2/2𝔼eμ​maxi​Xi=e−λ2/2𝔼eλ​maxi​ξi.e^{-\mu^{2}/2}\mathbb{E}e^{\mu\max_{i}X_{i}}=e^{-\lambda^{2}/2}\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda\max_{i}\xi_{i}}.

(5)


Combining (3)–(5) with
(1) proves the Weak Simplex Conjecture. The equality statement
also shows that the regular simplex is the unique maximizer, up to orthogonal
transformation and relabeling, at every λ>0\lambda>0. This also yields
the exact optimal decoding probability in the deterministic no-feedback
finite-energy additive white-Gaussian noise (AWGN) model of Corollary 2.8.




Another consequence is the following bound on expected maxima of Gaussian random variables. Since the added Gaussian random variable has mean zero,





𝔼​maxi​ξi=mm−1​𝔼​maxi​Xi.\mathbb{E}\max_{i}\xi_{i}=\sqrt{\frac{m}{m-1}}\,\mathbb{E}\max_{i}X_{i}.

(6)


Applying (3) to the identity function proves
[19, Conjecture 1.4], which is equivalent to the Simplex Mean
Width Conjecture [19, Conjecture 1.1]. Thus the regular simplex
uniquely maximizes mean width among convex hulls of mm points in the unit ball
of ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} whenever d≥m−1d\geq m-1.



1.1 Proof idea




The first ingredient is a Gaussian product inequality. Let
X∼𝒩⁡(0,R)X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R), where RR is any correlation matrix. If bounded
log-concave functions fi:ℝ→[0,∞)f_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty) satisfy





∫ℝz​fi​(z)​d​γ1​(z)=0,\int_{\mathbb{R}}zf_{i}(z)\,d\gamma_{1}(z)=0,

(7)


then





𝔼​∏ifi​(Xi)≥∏i∫fi​d​γ1.\mathbb{E}\prod_{i}f_{i}(X_{i})\geq\prod_{i}\int f_{i}\,d\gamma_{1}.



After division by its Gaussian integral, each factor in
(7) defines a probability measure of mean zero. The
product inequality is a specialization of the centered Gaussian
forward–reverse Brascamp–Lieb theorem of Milman, Nakamura, and Tsuji
[22]; we record the specialization and give a direct
proof in Appendix B.




The indicator of (−∞,c](-\infty,c] has the correct product but not the required
first moment. We repair this by multiplying by an exponential and allowing
the endpoint to move. Set





r⁡(s)=φ⁡(s)Φ⁡(s),H⁡(s)=s+r⁡(s).r(s)=\frac{\varphi(s)}{\Phi(s)},\qquad H(s)=s+r(s).

(8)


Then





fs(z)=er⁡(s)​z𝟏(−∞,H(s)](z)f_{s}(z)=e^{r(s)z}\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,H(s)]}(z)

(9)


satisfies ∫z​fs​(z)​d​γ1​(z)=0\int zf_{s}(z)\,d\gamma_{1}(z)=0. Applying this exponential tilt
shifts the mean of XX. If one parameter sis_{i} is chosen
for each coordinate and ai=r⁡(si)a_{i}=r(s_{i}), a Gaussian change of measure returns to
the event in (3) precisely when





s+a−R​a=c​𝟏.s+a-Ra=c\mathbf{1}.

(10)


The logarithm of the Gaussian mass of (9), viewed as a
function of its endpoint H⁡(s)H(s), has derivative r⁡(s)r(s). This identity turns
the coupled equations in (10) into the stationarity
equations of a strictly concave finite-dimensional functional. Its maximizer
also gives





∑i=1mlog⁡Φ⁡(si)+12​a𝖳​(Im−R)​a≥m​log⁡Φ⁡(c),\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log\Phi(s_{i})+\frac{1}{2}a^{\mathsf{T}}(I_{m}-R)a\geq m\log\Phi(c),

(11)


which is exactly the remaining estimate needed after the product inequality
and the Gaussian change of measure are combined.




The same product argument also gives the equality statement. For the
truncated exponential factors above, the product inequality is strict whenever
R≠ImR\neq I_{m}. It follows that the common-threshold comparison is strict at
every finite cc, so the maximum laws can agree only when R=ImR=I_{m}. For
completeness, Appendix C gives a different proof that applies
to arbitrary centered Gaussian vectors, without the unit-variance or
semidefinite hypotheses of the main theorem.




1.2 Relation to prior work




The Weak Simplex Conjecture originates in Shannon’s work on communication over Gaussian channels
[30, 32]. Balakrishnan proved several partial results
[2, 3, 4]. Landau and Slepian
gave an argument valid in dimension three [18]; Tanner
explained why it does not extend to all dimensions
[39, 40]. Later formulations appear in Farber’s thesis,
Cover’s problem list, and Massey’s Shannon Lecture
[11, 9, 21]. The equal-codeword-energy Weak
Simplex Conjecture is distinct from the average-energy Strong Simplex
Conjecture, which Steiner disproved [35].




The formulations in terms of Gaussian maxima and mean width were developed in
[5, 16, 19].
Sun, Hu, and Lan proved the four-variable Gaussian maximum case and the
three-dimensional mean-width case [37]. Guédon and Souli
extend a Chevet comparison inequality to a broader class of smooth functions
and prove their comparison for a block-structured family of Gaussian vectors
[14]. Their Conjecture 1.6 records the Gaussian
expected-maximum formulation of the Simplex Mean Width Conjecture resolved
here.




The product inequality used in the proof is related to the
Šidák–Khatri inequality and the Gaussian correlation theorem
[17, 33, 15, 31, 1].
Its present form follows from the forward–reverse Brascamp–Lieb framework of
Liu, Courtade, Cuff, and Verdú [20], together
with the centered Gaussian results of Milman, Nakamura, and Tsuji
[23, 24, 25, 22].
Nakamura and Tsuji’s equality theorem for two centered convex sets also implies
the equality case for finite symmetric rectangles. The direct proof in
Appendix B is shorter for the product form needed here.

After version 1 of this paper was posted, the present author and Su et al. independently
obtained the pointwise strictness and equality cases
[36]. A complete formal proof in the companion Lean
repository was committed on 19 July 202611

Prior announced proofs.




Two earlier preprints announce proofs: Goldsmith for the Simplex Mean Width
Conjecture and Pastore for the Weak Simplex Conjecture
[12, 26]. Our proof is independent of both. In
Appendix D we give a version-specific account of unresolved steps
in the respective submissions arXiv:2112.03393v2 and arXiv:2306.13478v2 that prevent us from using their
announced conclusions. That discussion concerns only those posted versions
and does not rule out repairs or later arguments.





1.3 Organization




Sections 2 and 3 state the main consequences and reduce
the coding problem to the covariance comparison. The proof then proceeds in
three stages: Section 4 supplies the Gaussian product
inequality, Section 5 constructs compatible centered factors, and
Section 6 combines them. Section 7 then returns
to the coding and geometric applications. The appendices contain the
one-dimensional calculations, a direct proof and strict form of the product
inequality, a proof that a centered Gaussian vector whose maximum has the same
law as the maximum of independent standard Gaussians must have covariance
ImI_{m}, a version-specific discussion of two prior proof claims, and the details
needed to apply the Weak Simplex Conjecture to the AWGN sequence model of
[27].





2 Main results




The central comparison is most naturally stated for the transformed
covariance RR. We state it first and then record the consequences obtained
after returning to the original score covariance GG.




Throughout, m≥2m\geq 2, 𝟏=(1,…,1)𝖳∈ℝm\mathbf{1}=(1,\ldots,1)^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}, and
J=𝟏𝟏𝖳J=\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}. We write γk\gamma_{k} for standard Gaussian measure
on ℝk\mathbb{R}^{k}, and φ\varphi and Φ\Phi for the standard Gaussian density and
distribution function, respectively. All logarithms are natural, and vector inequalities
such as x≤yx\leq y are coordinatewise. A correlation matrix is a
positive-semidefinite matrix with unit diagonal and may be singular. For real
random variables UU and VV, we write U≤stVU\leq_{\mathrm{st}}V when
ℙ{U>t}≤ℙ{V>t}\mathbb{P}\{U>t\}\leq\mathbb{P}\{V>t\} for every t∈ℝt\in\mathbb{R}.




Let





𝖬X=max1≤i≤m⁡Xi,𝖬Z=max1≤i≤m⁡Zi,\mathsf{M}_{X}=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}X_{i},\qquad\mathsf{M}_{Z}=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}Z_{i},



where Z1,…,ZmZ_{1},\ldots,Z_{m} are independent standard Gaussian random variables.



Theorem 2.1.



Let RR be an m×mm\times m correlation matrix satisfying





R−1m​J⪰0,R-\frac{1}{m}J\succeq 0,



and let X∼𝒩⁡(0,R)X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R). Then





𝖬X≤st𝖬Z.\mathsf{M}_{X}\leq_{\mathrm{st}}\mathsf{M}_{Z}.

(12)


Equivalently,





ℙ{X≤c𝟏}≥Φ(c)mfor every c∈ℝ.\mathbb{P}\{X\leq c\mathbf{1}\}\geq\Phi(c)^{m}\qquad\text{for every }c\in\mathbb{R}.

(13)


Moreover,





𝖬X=d𝖬Z⟺R=Im.\mathsf{M}_{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathsf{M}_{Z}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad R=I_{m}.

(14)






The event in (13) is the lower orthant
(−∞,c]m(-\infty,c]^{m} with the same threshold cc in every coordinate. This
restriction cannot be removed.

ℙ⁡{X1≤0,X2≤0}=14−12​π​arcsin⁡(13)<14.\mathbb{P}\{X_{1}\leq 0,X_{2}\leq 0\}=\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\arcsin\!\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)<\frac{1}{4}.

2.1 Consequences for Gaussian maxima and simplex codes




The advantage of the distributional comparison is that it controls every
nondecreasing function of the maximum, rather than one moment-generating
function at a time. The equality statement also makes these comparisons
strict for strictly increasing integrable functions.



Corollary 2.4.



Under the hypotheses of Theorem 2.1, every nonnegative, nondecreasing
Borel function ψ\psi satisfies





𝔼​ψ​(𝖬X)≤𝔼​ψ​(𝖬Z),\mathbb{E}\psi(\mathsf{M}_{X})\leq\mathbb{E}\psi(\mathsf{M}_{Z}),



including when one or both expectations are infinite. If R≠ImR\neq I_{m},
ψ:ℝ→ℝ\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R} is strictly increasing, and ψ⁡(𝖬X)\psi(\mathsf{M}_{X}) and
ψ⁡(𝖬Z)\psi(\mathsf{M}_{Z}) are integrable, then





𝔼​ψ​(𝖬X)<𝔼​ψ​(𝖬Z).\mathbb{E}\psi(\mathsf{M}_{X})<\mathbb{E}\psi(\mathsf{M}_{Z}).



In particular, for every λ>0\lambda>0,





𝔼eλ​𝖬X<𝔼eλ​𝖬Z,𝔼𝖬X<𝔼𝖬Z(R≠Im).\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda\mathsf{M}_{X}}<\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda\mathsf{M}_{Z}},\qquad\mathbb{E}\mathsf{M}_{X}<\mathbb{E}\mathsf{M}_{Z}\qquad(R\neq I_{m}).







We now return from the transformed covariance RR to the score covariance GG.
Set





G△=mm−1​(Im−1m​J).G_{\triangle}=\frac{m}{m-1}\left(I_{m}-\frac{1}{m}J\right).

(15)


This is the Gram matrix of the vertices of a regular (m−1)(m-1)-simplex
inscribed in the unit sphere. Conversely, any unit-vector configuration with
this Gram matrix is a regular simplex, up to an orthogonal transformation and
relabeling. Under the covariance transformation in (4),
G△G_{\triangle} is sent to ImI_{m}. Thus Theorem 2.1 compares every score
covariance with the regular-simplex covariance.



Corollary 2.5.



Let GG be any m×mm\times m correlation matrix, let
ξ∼𝒩⁡(0,G)\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,G), and let
ξ△∼𝒩⁡(0,G△)\xi^{\triangle}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,G_{\triangle}). Then





𝔼​exp​{λ​maxi​ξi}≤𝔼​exp​{λ​maxi​ξi△}(λ>0).\mathbb{E}\exp\!\left\{\lambda\max_{i}\xi_{i}\right\}\leq\mathbb{E}\exp\!\left\{\lambda\max_{i}\xi_{i}^{\triangle}\right\}\qquad(\lambda>0).

(16)


If G≠G△G\neq G_{\triangle}, then (16) is strict for every
λ>0\lambda>0. Equivalently, equality for any one λ>0\lambda>0 holds if and
only if G=G△G=G_{\triangle}.





Combining the preceding MGF comparison with the maximum-likelihood identity
in Section 3 gives the coding theorem in its classical form.

7 Applications




Theorem 2.1 compares the maximum associated with the transformed

The variance-completion argument above is sufficient for expected maxima, but
it does not by itself solve the finite-energy decoding problem. When the
codeword norms are unequal, the Gaussian likelihood scores contain the
deterministic offsets −∥ci∥2/N0-\|c_{i}\|^{2}/N_{0} in addition to their random linear
parts. The maximal per-codeword energy constraint nevertheless permits an
exact reduction to the equal-energy setting. Because the sequence model also
requires a measure-theoretic finite-dimensional reduction, we give the full
details in Appendix E and retain here the structure of the argument.



Proof of Corollary 2.8.

characterization stated after Corollary 2.8

The conclusion is specific to deterministic, equiprobable, no-feedback codes

Open problem 8.2 (The rank-constrained Gaussian maximum).



Fix integers m≥3m\geq 3 and 1≤d<m−11\leq d<m-1, and fix λ>0\lambda>0. Determine

The feasible set is compact, so a maximizer exists. Corollary 2.5 gives the
regular (m−1)(m-1)-simplex value as a universal upper bound, but that covariance
has rank m−1m-1 and is not feasible. Thus this problem asks for the optimal
overloaded equal-energy Gaussian code when the number of signals exceeds the
ambient dimension plus one. The unconstrained theorem does not indicate which
boundary point of the rank-constrained elliptope should be optimal, so even
the candidate geometry remains to be identified.



Open problem 8.3 (Universal lower-orthant thresholds).



Determine all vectors t=(t1,…,tm)∈ℝmt=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m} for which





ℙ{Xi≤ti for every i}≥∏i=1mΦ(ti)\mathbb{P}\{X_{i}\leq t_{i}\text{ for every }i\}\geq\prod_{i=1}^{m}\Phi(t_{i})

Theorem 2.1 shows that every constant vector t=c​𝟏t=c\mathbf{1} has this
property, whereas Remark 2.2 shows that arbitrary threshold
vectors do not. A solution would identify the precise reach of the
common-threshold phenomenon. It would also clarify whether the adaptive
centering construction can be modified to treat any genuinely asymmetric
family of one-sided events.



Open problem 8.4 (Average-energy Gaussian codes).



For fixed MM, ambient dimension, noise level, and average-energy budget EE,
maximize the average probability of correct decoding over equiprobable
Gaussian codebooks satisfying





1M​∑i=1M∥ci∥2≤E.\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\lVert c_{i}\rVert^{2}\leq E.



Characterize the optimizers as a function of the signal-to-noise ratio. Can an
optimizer always be chosen from a family consisting of a lower-dimensional
regular simplex together with coincident zero signals, or are additional
geometries unavoidable?





The regular simplex is not always optimal under this weaker constraint: this
is the failure of the Strong Simplex Conjecture proved by Steiner
[35]. The known counterexample literature already exhibits a
substantial family of lower-dimensional degeneracies. In particular, Sun
studied codebooks formed by a regular simplex on KK nonzero signals together
with M−KM-K zero signals, as well as related circle-plus-zero configurations
[38]. These results show that the failure is not confined to a
single exceptional construction, but they do not give a general description
of the global optimizer. The maximal per-codeword constraint used in
Corollary 2.8 rules out precisely this redistribution of energy
among messages.




Other extensions—including unequal priors, feedback, and non-Gaussian
noise—change either the one-dimensional centering equations or the Gaussian
change of measure and therefore require new ideas beyond the argument given
here.

Appendix C Rigidity of the independent Gaussian maximum law

Appendix D Relation to two prior announced proofs




Goldsmith [12] and Pastore [26] announce proofs
of the Simplex Mean Width and Weak Simplex conjectures, respectively. We
record why we do not use the current posted versions as prior proofs. The
discussion concerns only arXiv:2112.03393v2, revised 27 June 2023, and
arXiv:2306.13478v2, revised 13 November 2023. All page, section, theorem, and
equation references below refer to those versions. It is not a claim that
either conjecture is false, that either strategy cannot be repaired, or that
the authors have no later argument.



D.1 Goldsmith’s centroid-monotonicity step




The dependency in [12] is





Theorem 3.1⟹Corollary 3.2⟹Theorem 4.1.\text{Theorem 3.1}\Longrightarrow\text{Corollary 3.2}\Longrightarrow\text{Theorem 4.1}.



Theorem 3.1 is a strict monotonicity statement for spherical centroids under a
longitudinal shift. On pp. 7–9, its proof uses local approximations and
reduces the pointwise Prékopa–Leindler hypothesis to a sufficient
inequality. At the end of p. 9, the manuscript observes that this inequality
fails as the dimension tends to infinity and derives only a restricted
angular condition. The proof then ends without another estimate verifying
the required pointwise hypothesis. Corollary 3.2 invokes Theorem 3.1, and
Theorem 4.1 invokes Corollary 3.2. Thus the posted argument does not establish
the claimed all-dimensional theorem.
