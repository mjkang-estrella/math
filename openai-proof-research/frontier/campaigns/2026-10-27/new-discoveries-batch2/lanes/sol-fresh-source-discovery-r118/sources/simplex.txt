Abhijeet Mulgund
††thanks: This work was supported by NSF under awards 2240532 and 2217023.
Affiliation: University of Illinois Chicago

Email: mulgund2@uic.edu



Abstract
    

We prove a stochastic comparison for Gaussian maxima. Let RR be an
m×mm\times m correlation matrix satisfying
R−𝟏𝟏𝖳/m⪰0R-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}/m\succeq 0, let X∼𝒩⁡(0,R)X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R), and let
Z1,…,ZmZ_{1},\ldots,Z_{m} be independent standard Gaussian random variables. Then
    





max1≤i≤mXi≤stmax1≤i≤mZi,\max_{1\leq i\leq m}X_{i}\leq_{\mathrm{st}}\max_{1\leq i\leq m}Z_{i},


    

or equivalently,
ℙ{Xi≤c for every i}≥Φ(c)m\mathbb{P}\{X_{i}\leq c\text{ for every }i\}\geq\Phi(c)^{m} for every c∈ℝc\in\mathbb{R}.
This comparison resolves the Weak Simplex Conjecture: among d+1d+1
equiprobable equal-energy signals in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} transmitted over an additive
white Gaussian noise channel, the regular simplex maximizes the probability
of correct maximum-likelihood decoding at every signal-to-noise ratio. It
also proves the mean-width inequality asserted by the Simplex Mean Width
Conjecture. In the deterministic no-feedback AWGN model with equiprobable
messages, an unrestricted number of channel uses, and a maximal per-codeword
total-energy constraint, it gives the exact optimal finite-energy performance. The proof
combines a Gaussian product inequality for log-concave functions with an
adaptive tilting argument that makes the inequality applicable to the
one-sided threshold events defining the maximum. A lean formalization of this argument is available at https://github.com/abhmul/weak-simplex-conjecture-lean.

1 Introduction



1.1 Main theorem and consequences




The Weak Simplex Conjecture traces back to an observation of Shannon reported by Rice in 1950 [31]; the name was later coined by Massey in 1988 [20]. It asks how to place m=d+1m=d+1 equiprobable signals of equal energy in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} when the channel adds isotropic Gaussian noise and the receiver uses maximum-likelihood decoding. The conjecture asserts that a regular simplex is optimal at every signal-to-noise ratio. If the unit signal vectors are x1,…,xmx_{1},\ldots,x_{m} and
G=(⟨xi,xj⟩)i,jG=(\langle x_{i},x_{j}\rangle)_{i,j} is their Gram matrix, then the average probability
of correct decoding satisfies





Pc​(x1,…,xm,λ)=e−λ2/2m​𝔼​exp⁡{λ​max1≤i≤m​ξi},ξ∼𝒩⁡(0,G).P_{\mathrm{c}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m};\lambda)=\frac{e^{-\lambda^{2}/2}}{m}\mathbb{E}\exp\!\left\{\lambda\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\xi_{i}\right\},\qquad\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,G).

(1)


Thus the coding problem is an extremal problem for the moment-generating
function (MGF) of a Gaussian maximum.




Our main theorem is a distributional comparison obtained after adding the
same independent Gaussian random variable to all coordinates and rescaling.
Let J=𝟏𝟏𝖳J=\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}. If RR is a correlation matrix satisfying





R−1m​J⪰0,R-\frac{1}{m}J\succeq 0,

(2)


then, for X∼𝒩⁡(0,R)X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R) and independent standard Gaussian random variables
Z1,…,ZmZ_{1},\ldots,Z_{m},





ℙ{maxiXi≤c}≥Φ(c)m=ℙ{maxiZi≤c}(c∈ℝ).\mathbb{P}\!\left\{\max_{i}X_{i}\leq c\right\}\geq\Phi(c)^{m}=\mathbb{P}\!\left\{\max_{i}Z_{i}\leq c\right\}\qquad(c\in\mathbb{R}).

(3)


Equivalently, maxi⁡Xi\max_{i}X_{i} is first-order stochastically dominated by the
independent maximum. The event on the left is the equal-threshold lower
orthant (−∞,c]m(-\infty,c]^{m}; no comparison for arbitrary threshold vectors is
claimed.




The semidefinite condition in (2) is exactly the
condition produced by the coding problem. For every correlation matrix GG,
define





R=m−1m​G+1m​J.R=\frac{m-1}{m}G+\frac{1}{m}J.

(4)


Then R−J/m⪰0R-J/m\succeq 0, and the regular-simplex covariance
mm−1​(I−J/m)\frac{m}{m-1}(I-J/m) is mapped to ImI_{m}. Moreover, if μ>0\mu>0 and
λ=(m−1)/m​μ\lambda=\sqrt{(m-1)/m}\,\mu, then





e−μ2/2𝔼eμ​maxi​Xi=e−λ2/2𝔼eλ​maxi​ξi.e^{-\mu^{2}/2}\mathbb{E}e^{\mu\max_{i}X_{i}}=e^{-\lambda^{2}/2}\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda\max_{i}\xi_{i}}.

(5)


Consequently, (3) gives the MGF comparison required by
(1). It resolves the Weak Simplex Conjecture, implies the
expected-maximum inequality and hence the mean-width inequality asserted by
the Simplex Mean Width Conjecture, and yields an exact finite-energy AWGN
formula when no restriction is imposed on the number of channel uses.




To the best of our knowledge, the present paper gives the first complete proof
of the Weak Simplex Conjecture in all dimensions and at every signal-to-noise
ratio. The same argument proves the optimal-value inequality in the Simplex
Mean Width Conjecture; it does not determine whether the regular simplex is
the unique maximizer.




1.2 The adaptive-tilting argument




The analytic input is a Gaussian product inequality for one-dimensional
log-concave functions fif_{i} satisfying

zero. The inequality is a specialization of the centered Gaussian
forward–reverse Brascamp–Lieb theorem of Milman, Nakamura, and Tsuji
[22]; we identify the precise specialization in
Section 4 and give a direct proof in
Appendix A.




The product inequality cannot be applied directly to the event in
(3), because





∫ℝz𝟏(−∞,c](z)dγ1(z)=−ϕ(c).\int_{\mathbb{R}}z\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,c]}(z)\,d\gamma_{1}(z)=-\phi(c).



The new step is to choose a separate exponential tilt in each coordinate.
Define

(7)

(8)


has zero Gaussian first moment. The cutoff in (8) is H⁡(s)=s+r⁡(s)H(s)=s+r(s). Choosing different
values sis_{i} therefore gives coordinate-dependent cutoffs before the Gaussian
change of measure. A strictly concave variational problem chooses

(9)


Here aa is the exponential-tilt parameter and R​aRa is the mean displacement

1.3 Relation to prior work




Rice’s 1950 account of Shannon’s observation and Shannon’s later geometric
analysis of Gaussian communication [31, 33] led to work of Balakrishnan
[2, 3, 4] and to the
low-dimensional argument of Landau and Slepian [17];
Tanner explained the obstruction to extending that argument
[38, 39]. Farber’s thesis, Cover’s problem list, and
Massey’s Shannon Lecture contain later formulations
[10, 9, 20]. The equal-individual-energy Weak
Simplex Conjecture is distinct from the average-energy Strong Simplex
Conjecture, which Steiner disproved [36].




Later work connected the coding problem to Gaussian maxima and mean width.
Litvak summarized the main formulations [18]; Balitskiy,
Karasev, and Tsigler developed the Gaussian-noise formulation used here
[5]; and Kabluchko, Litvak, and Zaporozhets
proved the equivalence between the Gaussian-maximum and mean-width
formulations and an asymptotic result
[15]. Sun, Hu, and Lan proved the
four-variable case [37]. A July 2026 preprint of Guédon and
Souli still states the all-dimensional expected-maximum inequality as a
conjecture and describes the preceding results as the best known progress
[13].




Classical Gaussian comparison and correlation results do not directly give
(3). Slepian’s comparison is based on an entrywise
covariance order [35]. Pitt’s theorem characterizes association
of Gaussian vectors by nonnegative pairwise correlations
[27], whereas (2) permits negative
off-diagonal entries. The product input used here belongs to the line from
the symmetric rectangle and Gaussian correlation inequalities
[16, 34, 14, 32, 1]
to the forward–reverse framework of Liu, Courtade, Cuff, and Verdú
[19], inverse Brascamp–Lieb methods, and centered
log-concave extensions
[23, 24, 25, 22].




Two earlier preprints announce proofs: Goldsmith for the Simplex Mean Width
Conjecture and Pastore for the Weak Simplex Conjecture
[11, 26]. Our proof is independent of both. In
Appendix B we give a version-specific account of unresolved
steps in arXiv:2112.03393v2 and arXiv:2306.13478v2 that prevent us from using
their announced conclusions. That discussion concerns only those posted
versions and does not rule out repairs or later arguments.




1.4 Scope and organization




The stochastic order is proved for the normalized covariance RR in
(4), not for the original covariance GG. For GG
we obtain an MGF comparison and, by differentiation at the origin, an
expected-maximum comparison. We prove optimal values but do not characterize
all optimizers. The finite-energy result concerns deterministic,
equiprobable, no-feedback AWGN codes under a maximal per-codeword total-energy
constraint, with no bound on the number of channel uses or degrees of
freedom.




The proof has four steps. Section 3 derives the
maximum-likelihood identity and the covariance normalization.
Section 4 isolates the known Gaussian product inequality.
Section 5 proves the variational alignment, which is the main new
step. Section 6 combines these ingredients by a Gaussian
change of measure. The appendices give a direct proof of the product
specialization, document the relation to the two prior announced proofs, and
supply the rigorous operational reduction for the finite-energy AWGN result.

3 From Gaussian classification to a covariance comparison




We first derive the maximum-likelihood identity and then introduce the
normalization that maps the regular-simplex covariance to the identity matrix.
Both reductions are standard in the modern literature on the Weak Simplex
Conjecture [18, 5].

7.4 The finite-energy AWGN formula



Proof of Corollary 2.6.



The detailed operational reduction is given in Appendix C. We summarize
the argument. The likelihood ratios of the MM Gaussian shift measures are
measurable with respect to at most M−1M-1 Gaussian linear statistics, obtained
from an orthonormal basis of the codeword-difference span. These statistics
are sufficient even though the infinite white-noise output is not an
ℓ2\ell_{2}-valued random vector.




A code with ∥ci∥2≤E\lVert c_{i}\rVert^{2}\leq E may then be embedded isometrically with one
additional signal coordinate so that every codeword has energy exactly EE;
the decoder may ignore that coordinate, so optimal performance cannot
decrease. In the finite sufficient-statistic experiment, the equal-energy
means have a common radius r≤Er\leq\sqrt{E} in a space of dimension at most
M−1M-1. The maximum-likelihood identity and Corollary 2.3 bound their
correct-decoding probability by that of a regular (M−1)(M-1)-simplex of radius
rr. The smaller-radius simplex experiment is an explicit
message-independent Gaussian degradation of the larger-radius experiment, so
its optimal correct-decoding probability is no larger
[6]. Hence the radius-E\sqrt{E} regular simplex is optimal.




Represent the feasible centered simplex as
{ui−u¯:1≤i≤M}\{u_{i}-\bar{u}:1\leq i\leq M\}, where the uiu_{i} are pairwise orthogonal and
have norm E​M/(M−1)\sqrt{EM/(M-1)}. The vectors uiu_{i} themselves may have energy
larger than EE; they are used only as an equivalent translated
representation for evaluating the decoding probability. Translating every
signal and the observation by the common vector u¯\bar{u} leaves the
statistical experiment unchanged. Conditioning on the noise in the
transmitted coordinate then yields





Pc∗​(E,M)=𝔼​Φ​(W+E​M/(M−1)N0/2)M−1,W∼𝒩⁡(0,1),P_{\mathrm{c}}^{*}(E,M)=\mathbb{E}\Phi\!\left(W+\frac{\sqrt{EM/(M-1)}}{\sqrt{N_{0}/2}}\right)^{M-1},\qquad W\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),



which is (16). Equation (17) follows from the
definition of M∗M^{*}. Finally, pMp_{M} is continuous and strictly increasing,
with pM​(0)=1/Mp_{M}(0)=1/M and pM​(a)↑1p_{M}(a)\uparrow 1 as a→∞a\to\infty. Solving
(16) for EE proves (18) and its endpoint cases.
∎





This is the deterministic, equiprobable, no-feedback model with an arbitrary
decoder, a maximal per-codeword total-energy constraint, and no restriction on
the number of channel uses, as in [28, Definition 1]. It
establishes the fixed-(M,ϵ)(M,\epsilon) simplex-code assertion discussed in
[29, Remark 21.2].





8 Discussion and open problems




The proof combines a known Gaussian product inequality subject to a vanishing
Gaussian first-moment condition with a new adaptive tilting step. The
product inequality alone does not compare the interval
indicators needed for an equal-threshold lower orthant. The variational
problem couples the coordinate conditions ai=r⁡(si)a_{i}=r(s_{i}) to the covariance
through s+a−R​a=c​𝟏s+a-Ra=c\mathbf{1}, which makes every coordinatewise threshold equal to
cc after the Gaussian change of measure.



Open problem 8.1.



Determine the equality cases in (11),
(13), and the coding and mean-width consequences. In
particular, determine when equality at one nontrivial threshold or one
positive MGF parameter forces R=ImR=I_{m}, or equivalently forces the original
covariance to be G△G_{\triangle} after (21).





The present argument does not answer this question. Strict concavity of the
variational functional gives a unique aligning tilt for fixed RR and cc,
but it neither supplies the equality cases of the product inequality nor
shows that every optimal code is regular.




A second open direction is the rank-constrained problem. For mm unit
vectors in dimension d<m−1d<m-1, Corollary 2.3 gives the regular (m−1)(m-1)-simplex value as a universal upper bound, but the
regular simplex is not feasible in the prescribed dimension. Identifying the best covariance of
rank at most dd is a separate problem.




Another question is whether the adaptive tilting method extends to unequal
thresholds, unequal marginal variances, or other one-sided log-concave
factors. None of these extensions follows from the auxiliary function used
here. In particular, the argument does not prove the stronger
Gaussian-measure conjectures of Balitskiy–Karasev–Tsigler
[5], Guédon–Souli Conjecture 1.7
[13], or conjectures concerning Gaussian minima and other
intrinsic volumes.




For communication, (17) is restricted to the model stated in
Corollary 2.6. Feedback, stop-feedback, average-over-codebook
energy, randomized encoding under weaker constraints, unequal priors, and
non-Gaussian noise require different arguments. The false Strong Simplex
Conjecture shows that the change from individual to average energy is
substantive [36].




The main conclusion can be stated without coding terminology: every
correlation matrix satisfying R⪰J/mR\succeq J/m produces a Gaussian maximum that
is stochastically dominated by the maximum of independent standard Gaussian
variables. The adaptive tilts supply the threshold compatibility that turns
the product inequality into this comparison.

B.1 Goldsmith’s centroid-monotonicity step




The dependency in [11] is





Theorem 3.1⟹Corollary 3.2⟹Theorem 4.1.\text{Theorem 3.1}\Longrightarrow\text{Corollary 3.2}\Longrightarrow\text{Theorem 4.1}.



Theorem 3.1 is a strict monotonicity statement for spherical centroids under a
longitudinal shift. On pp. 7–9, its proof uses local approximations and
reduces the pointwise Prékopa–Leindler hypothesis to a sufficient
inequality. At the end of p. 9, the manuscript observes that this inequality
fails as the dimension tends to infinity and derives only a restricted
angular condition. The proof then ends without another estimate verifying
the required pointwise hypothesis. Corollary 3.2 invokes Theorem 3.1, and
Theorem 4.1 invokes Corollary 3.2. Thus the posted argument does not establish
the claimed all-dimensional theorem.
