                                                          HAMILTONICITY OF GRAPHS OF ACYCLIC ORIENTATIONS
                                                                     AND ACYCLIC POLYNOMIALS

                                                                         LEONIE MÜHLHERR AND GERMAIN POULLOT


                                                  Abstract. We study the graph AO(G) of acyclic orientations of a graph G. Two acyclic orienta-
                                                  tions are adjacent in this graph if they disagree on the orientation of a single arc. In particular, we
                                                  focus on the Hamiltonicity of the graphs AO(G).




arXiv:2609.02249v2 [math.CO] 17 Sep 2026
                                                     Using two methods of pattern lacing which generalize the zig-zag method of Brenner, Cardinal,
                                                  McConville, Merino and Mütze, we characterize which multipaths are AO-Hamiltonian. Moreover,
                                                  we give a criterion for the gluing of a multipath on a given graph to preserve AO-Hamiltonicity.
                                                  Building towards an inductive certification of AO-Hamiltonicity via the (open) ear decomposition
                                                  of 2-connected graphs, we propose three ways of gluing several multipaths to a given graph.
                                                     In addition, we define the acyclic polynomials to encapsulate both the number of acyclic orien-
                                                  tations of a graph and the “parity problem” proposed by Savage, Squire and West: if −1 is not a
                                                  root of the acyclic polynomial of G, then G is not AO-Hamiltonian. We explore numerous proper-
                                                  ties of the acyclic polynomials, proving that they are not instances of the famous Tutte–Whitney
                                                  polynomials, but that they too exhibit a partial deletion-contraction phenomenon.

                                         1. Introduction
   The motivation for this article spans across various mathematical disciplines. At its core, the
question we want to answer is for which graphs the acyclic orientations can be enumerated in a
nice way. One can make this statement more precise by formulating it via Gray codes, which
are enumerations where consecutive objects differ only by a small change. The original and most
common example is the enumeration of binary strings, see [Knu11] for more background. Acyclic
orientations of a graph can be encoded as binary strings, each bit coding for an edge, and 0, 1
representing its two possible orientations. Yet, in this representation, it is hard to check whether a
binary string encodes an acyclic orientation or not. We are interested in the following problem.
Formulation 1. Classify the graphs G for which the list of acyclic orientations admits a Gray code.
   The reader unfamiliar with this version of the problem may take a look at Tables 2 and 3 for
some typical examples. In this article, the main perspective on this question is the graph-theoretical
one: We study the graph of acyclic orientations AO(G) whose nodes are the acyclic orientations
of a graph G, and where two orientations are neighboring if and only if they differ in flipping the
orientation of exactly one arc. We can restate our problem:
Formulation 2. Classify the graphs G whose graph of acyclic orientations AO(G) is Hamiltonian.
   This is the research angle of Savage, Squire and West in their seminal work [SSW93]. They
achieved a classification for some notable example classes, such as cycles, wheel-graphs, ladder-
graphs, and chordal graphs (see [SSW93] or here below Example 2.7). This quest for efficient
ways to produce all the acyclic orientations of a graph was then pursued by numerous authors, see
e.g. Squire [Squ98] and Barbosa–Szwarcfiter [BS99] for generating problems. Most prominently,
Pruesse and Ruskey [PR95] showed that the prism over the graph of acyclic orientations of any
graph is Hamiltonian. Furthermore, Pilaud [Pil24] studied the properties of the poset obtained
from the graph of acyclic orientations of G by orienting it away from a base (acyclic) orientation
D. Remarkably, he determined under which conditions on G and D it is a lattice. In particular,
the rank function of this poset gives the coefficients of the acyclic polynomials that we study in
Section 3. We also refer to Savage’s survey [Sav97] for a presentation of the famous Steinhaus–
Johnson–Trotter algorithm [Joh63] which produces a Hamiltonian cycle in the graph of acyclic
orientations for the complete graph. The interested reader may consult [CHMMM23, Figure 5]
for a comprehensive survey on algorithms and Hamiltonicity of graphs of acyclic orientations of
combinatorial families beyond graphs.
   The results of Savage, Squire and West strongly suggest that parity considerations (such as “Is
Cn a cycle with n even or odd?”) play a key role in determining whether AO(G) is Hamiltonian
                                                  2
or not. In particular, they establish a simple yet powerful criterion for deciding that AO(G) is not
Hamiltonian: fixing any orientation D of G, if AO(G) is Hamiltonian, then the number of acyclic
orientations of G disagreeing with D on the orientation of an even number of arcs is equal to the
number of acyclic orientations of G disagreeing with D on an odd number of arcs. When a graph
does not satisfy this condition, Savage, Squire and West [SSW93] speak of a parity problem (see
also Lemma 2.9).

   This result motivates us to define the rank with respect to D of an acyclic orientation A, denoted
rkD A, as the number of arcs on the orientation of which A and D disagree.    P We encapsulate the
numbers of acyclic orientations of each rank in a polynomial Ψ(D; t) := A∈AO(G) trkD A called
the acyclic polynomial (Section 3). In particular, constructing the acyclic signature as σ(D) :=
Ψ(D; −1) (and σ(G) := |σ(D)| for any orientation D of G), we can rephrase the parity problem
as deciding whether σ(D) = 0 or not. See Table 1 for an overview, and Section 3.3 for various
properties of the acyclic signature.
   Our acyclic polynomial should not be confused with the acyclic orientation polynomial recently
defined in [HJLOY21]: there, the authors assign a variable to each node of a graph, and associate
each acyclic orientation to the product of the variables assigned to the sinks of the orientation.
   Stanley [Sta73] famously proved that the number of acyclic orientations of a graph G can be
recovered from its chromatic polynomial χ(G; x) by evaluating it at −1, i.e. Ψ(D; 1) = χ(G; −1)
for any orientation D of G (see Remark 3.19 for a discussion of this relation). Since then, the
study of the chromatic polynomial itself has grown into a branch of Combinatorics, and the focus
on the evaluation of this polynomial at −1 together with the combinatorial reciprocity theorems it
suggests [GZ83; GS00; Ges01; Las01; BN20] is of particular relevance for our work.
   As the chromatic polynomial can be computed exploiting a deletion-contraction phenomenon,
it is natural to ask whether the acyclic polynomial also admits one. The main objective of Sec-
tion 3.2 is to show that the acyclic polynomials partially admit a deletion-contraction phenomenon
(Propositions 3.54 and 3.58) which extends to the acyclic signatures (Theorem 3.64), allowing for
an algorithm that computes the acyclic signatures of some graphs (Remark 3.66). Furthermore, we
prove in Remark 3.18 that the acyclic polynomial is not an evaluation of the famous Tutte–Whitney
polynomial (see [Whi35; Tut54; Cra69] for the original articles, or [Oxl11] for a comprehensive pre-
sentation in the broader context of matroids). Yet, it seems that there is more to this story, since
recent results show that properties of acyclic orientations, besides counting, are encoded in the
Tutte–Whitney polynomial, see e.g. the work of Backman [Bac18].

   The graph of acyclic orientations AO(G) is known in this context as the tope graph of the
graphic arrangement, describing the neighboring structure of the chambers. Equivalently AO(G) is
also the vertex-edge graph of the zonotope associated to the arrangement, known under the names

consult [Sta07, Section 2] or [BLSWZ99, Section 1.1] for a detailed presentation. The interplay of
properties of the tope graph, the arrangement and the zonotope is an active field of research [Koi26;
BBR26]. (Note that our question need not be restricted to the graphic case in this setting.)
Formulation 3. Classify all (graphic) arrangements whose tope graph admits a Hamiltonian cycle.
Equivalently, classify all graphs such that the vertex-edge graph of the associated graphical zonotope
(a.k.a. acyclotope) is Hamiltonian.
   For example, it is known that the tope graph of so-called reflection arrangements, of which AKℓ
is an example, admits a Hamiltonian cycle [CSW89]. Recently, there has been another development
for this more general setting as two groups of researchers independently found that the tope graph
of so-called supersolvable arrangements are Hamiltonian [BCMMM25; KSSW25]. Applying this
result to graphic arrangements recovers the result for chordal graphs from Savage, Squire and West
[SSW93, Section 4.1]. Furthermore, some of the observations we make in this work about the
evaluations of the acyclic polynomial for graphs are already known in this more general hyperplane
setting, employing different names and arguments (see Remark 3.24).
   In the second main part of our work, Section 4, we prove that some operations can be per-
formed on a graph whose graph of acyclic orientations is Hamiltonian, preserving this property.
We introduce two methods of pattern lacing (Corollary 4.4 and Theorem 4.5) which can be seen
as generalizing the zig-zag method described in [BCMMM25]. This way, we prove that, under
a certain parity condition, adding a path of even length (or two paths of odd lengths) between
nodes u and v preserves having a Hamiltonian graph of acyclic orientations. A graph consisting of
several paths between two nodes u and v is called a multipath. Using these path gluings together
with our results on the acyclic signature, we establish (“G is AO-Hamiltonian” means “AO(G) is
Hamiltonian”):
                                                  4
Theorem B (Corollary 4.13 and Example 4.14). We have the following positive results:
    (i) A multipath G is AO-Hamiltonian if and only if the number of edges of G is odd.
   (ii) Any 2-sum of two cycle graphs Cn and Cm is AO-Hamiltonian if and only if n + m is even.
  (iii) For a tree T = ([s], E) and numbers n1 , . . . , ns ≥ 3, a (T, n1 , . . . , ns )-tree of cycles is any
        graph isomorphic to a 2-sum H ⊕2 Cns where H is a (T ∖ s, n1 , . . . , ns−1 )-tree of cycles. If
        np is odd for all p ∈ [s], then any (T, n1 , . . . , ns )-tree of cycles is AO-Hamiltonian, for any
        tree T .
  (iv) If uv ∈ E is an edge of a AO-Hamiltonian graph G = (N, E), and Cn is a cycle graph, then
        the 2-sum G ⊕2 Cn is AO-Hamiltonian if and only if n is odd.

   This theorem subsumes the one of Savage, Squire and West: “For a cycle Cn on n nodes, AO(Cn )
is Hamiltonian if and only if n is odd” [SSW93, below Lemma 3.1 & Theorem 4.2]. The above
Theorem B is a specialization of the more general Corollary 4.11 made easier for the introduction.
   The motivation behind considering this type of operation arises from the fact that if AO(H) is
Hamiltonian for all the 2-connected components of G, then AO(G) is also Hamiltonian. Moreover,
it is well-known (see [Die25, Section 3.1] for details and figures) that 2-connected graphs admit an
(open) ear decomposition, i.e. we can construct any 2-connected graph by starting with a cycle
and subsequently adding paths between pairs of nodes. Therefore, exploring all the ways of adding
paths to a graph that are compatible with the AO-Hamiltonicity of said graph means iteratively
constructing a large example class with hopes of reaching a complete classification. We show (recall
that a node is simplicial if all its neighbors pairwise share an edge, i.e. form a clique):

Theorem C (Simplified version Corollary 4.18). Let G be a graph such that AO(G) is Hamiltonian.
If G′ is obtained from G by either
    (i) the addition of a simplicial node x◦ on a clique of odd cardinality in G and the (optional)
        gluing of a multipath of even cardinality at some nodes u, v with u ̸= x◦ and v ̸= x◦ ; or
   (ii) the (optional) addition of a simplicial node and the gluing of a multipath of even cardinality
        at some nodes u, v that share an edge in G;
  Then AO(G′ ) is Hamiltonian.

  A more flexible yet notation-heavy version of Theorem C is proven in Corollary 4.18. We also
detail a third way of gluing several multipaths on different pairs of nodes in Section 4.4.

   This article is organized as follows: In Section 2, we summarize all the graph-theoretic prelim-
inaries that are needed for the present work, especially on orientations, acyclic orientations, but
also the usual examples of the Gray code for binary sequences and the Steinhaus–Johnson–Trotter
algorithm for permutations. In Section 3 we introduce the acyclic polynomial and explain its fun-
damental properties. Notably, we decompose it into partial acyclic polynomials (Proposition 3.43),
and prove a deletion-contraction formula in certain cases (Propositions 3.54 and 3.58). This allows
us to better understand the acyclic signature of graphs (Section 3.3) and to compute the acyclic
signature of multipaths (Theorem 3.69), exemplifying how to certify that AO(G) is not Hamilton-
ian for some graph G. On the constructive side of Hamiltonicity considerations, in Section 4, we
give explicit constructions of Hamiltonian cycles for graphs obtained via graph operations such as
path gluings or addition of a simplicial node.
   Generalizations of strategies of pattern lacing can be found in Appendix A, while Appendix B is
devoted to complete bipartite graphs, and Appendix C proposes an alternative proof that multipaths
of even total length are not AO-Hamiltonian using a counting argument. In Appendix D, we give
22 explicit examples of well-known graphs and the properties of their graph of acyclic orientations,
for the reader to enjoy.

2.2. Cycles, Hamiltonian graphs. A (directed) path in a (di)graph G is a sequence of different
nodes i1 , . . . , ir ∈ N (G) such that ip ip+1 is a (directed) edge in G for all p ∈ [r − 1]. A (directed)
cycle is a (directed) path i1 , . . . , ir ∈ N such that ir i1 is a (directed) edge in G. A graph is
Hamiltonian (resp. path-Hamiltonian) if it admits a cycle (resp. a path) containing all its nodes.
Clearly, a Hamiltonian graph is path-Hamiltonian.
   We state here a well-known proposition concerning Hamiltonicity of bipartite graphs and give a
short proof for the sake of self-containment.
Proposition 2.3. Let G = (N, E) be a graph admitting a bipartition N = N1 ⊔ N2 .
    (i) If G is Hamiltonian, then |N1 | = |N2 |;
   (ii) If G is path-Hamiltonian with path i1 , . . . , ir where i1 ∈ N1 , then either |N1 | = |N2 | and ir ∈
        N2 , or |N1 | = |N2 | + 1 and ir ∈ N1 .
Proof. (i) If G is Hamiltonian, let i1 , . . . , ir be a cycle with N = {i1 , . . . , ir }. As ip ip+1 ∈ E for
all p ∈ [r − 1], we get that ip ∈ N1 if and only if ip+1 ∈ N2 and conversely, by definition of the
bipartition. So ip 7→ ip+1 for p ∈ [r − 1] and ir 7→ i1 defines an involution of N mapping N1 onto N2
Thus, |N1 | = |N2 |.
   (ii) If ir ∈ N1 , then ip 7→ ip+1 for p ∈ [r − 1] defines an involution of N ∖ {ir } mapping N1 ∖ {ir }
onto N2 , hence |N1 | = |N2 | + 1. Else, ip 7→ ip+1 for p ∈ [r − 1] and ir 7→ i1 defines an involution
of N mapping N1 onto N2 , hence |N1 | = |N2 | + 1.                                                          □

2.3. Graph of acyclic orientations. A directed graph is acyclic if it contains no directed cycles,
otherwise it is cyclic. For a (di)graph G, its graph of acyclic orientations AO(G) is the subgraph of
its graph of orientations O(G) induced on the set of acyclic orientations of G, see Figure 1. Corre-

G. As sub(di)graphs of bipartite (di)graphs, AO(G) and AO(D) are bipartite. They are graded
by the rank functions rkD . We denote AOk (D) := {A ∈ AO(G) ; rkD A = k}.
   We denote CO(G), CO(D) and COk (D) the counterparts for cyclic orientations.
Example 2.6. For n ≥ 1, the acyclic orientations of the complete graph Kn are in bijection with
the permutations of [n]. Indeed, any orientation D of Kn yields a reflexive and transitive relation:
i ≤ j if and only if there is a directed path from i to j in D. If this orientation is acyclic, then
this relation is anti-symmetric, i.e. it is a permutation of [n]. Two acyclic orientations of Kn are
adjacent if they differ on the orientation of a single arc, that is if the associated permutations differ
on the order of two consecutive elements. Hence the graph of acyclic orientations of the complete
graph AO(Kn ) is isomorphic to the permutahedral graph Πn . Furthermore, choosing any base
acyclic orientation D of Kn , the digraph of acyclic orientations AO(D) is isomorphic to the weak
Bruhat order Weakn .
 Our main motivation in the following sections is analyzing Formulation 2, thus we say that G is
AO-Hamiltonian if AO(G) is Hamiltonian.

3.2.3. Simplicial nodes, chordal graphs, complete graphs. A graph G is chordal if all its induced
cycles have length 3. On the other hand, a perfect elimination ordering of a graph G = (N, E) with
|N | = n is a bijection θ : [n] → N such that for all i ∈ [n] the neighbors of θ(i) in {θ(1), . . . , θ(i−1)}
form a clique in G (i.e. are pairwise connected by an edge in G). We say that θ(i) is simplicial in
G[{θ(1), . . . , θ(i − 1)}]. It is well-known, see [Ros70] that G is chordal if and only if G admits a
perfect elimination ordering.

3.2.5. Automorphisms of (di)graphs and palindromicity of Ψu→v (D ⊔ D′ ; t). It is straightforward
to show that if two orientations D and D′ of G are isomorphic (as digraphs, i.e. if there exists

same. We can go one step further by considering automorphisms of digraphs.
Definition 3.48. For a (directed) graph G = (N, E), an automorphism is a bijection φ : N → N
such that G has the (directed) edge uv ∈ E if and only if G has the (directed) edge φ(u)φ(v).
   The set of automorphisms of a (directed) graph together with the composition of functions forms
a group, which is denoted Aut(G).

but with the reverse orientation, so it would not be an arc of D.

3.3. Acyclic signature. We use the knowledge on the acyclic polynomial to compute the acyclic
signature σ(D) := Ψ(D; −1) of (di)graphs. The actual value of the signature is not very important
for the problem of Hamiltonicity of AO(G): we only care about whether σ(D) is 0 or not. Indeed,
by the parity argument (see [SSW93] or Lemma 2.9), if σ(D) ̸= 0 for some orientation D or G,
then G is not AO-Hamiltonian. The reciprocal is false, yet it seems non-trivial to produce an
interesting 2-connected graph with an even number of edges that would help to understand how
far from characterizing AO-Hamiltonicity the acyclic signature is.

   We want to analyze which operations on graphs we can perform, such that if the base graph is
AO-Hamiltonian, the resulting graph is also AO-Hamiltonian. This means that we generalize this
approach of graphic suspension to a case where the two graphs G[N × {0}] and G[N × {ℓ}] need
not be copies of the same graph, but rather instances of graphs of the subdivisions of neighboring
chambers of the original arrangement through the new hyperplanes. In this case, we find that it
can be necessary to use the edges between two graphs G[{v} × i] and G[{w} × i], where {v, w} is
an edge in the original graph H.

by adding a simplicial node on a clique of size b, then AO(G) is b-suspended over AO(H), so the
zigzag method guarantees that if H is AO-Hamiltonian, then G is also AO-Hamiltonian. We will
discuss this idea in Section 4.3 since we need more conditions on the nodes and certain sequences
associated to them.

more complicated than adding a simplicial node, especially when AO(G) is not suspended over

AO(H). We propose two generalizations of the zigzag method, namely Corollary 4.4 and Theo-
rem 4.5. Both are based on the following core idea: if G = H ⊔ H ′ is a bipartition of the edges of
G, where H ′ shall be thought of as the edges we add to H, then AO(G) is an induced subgraph
of AO(H) × AO(H ′ ) (see Lemma 3.40). For each acyclic orientation A of H, we consider the
graph of acyclic orientations of G of the form A ⊔ A′ for all admissible A′ ∈ AO(H ′ ): if we find
a Hamiltonian path PA through these acyclic orientations and if we manage to guarantee that we
can “string together” the (PA ; A ∈ AO(H)), then we would have a Hamiltonian path (or cycle) of
AO(G). This is the idea of Theorem 4.5.
   Yet, such a straightforward construction is usually not possible. Therefore, instead of just one
single orientation, we consider entire sequences (A1 , . . . , As ) of adjacent acyclic orientations of H
(i.e. directed paths in AO(H)), and find a Hamiltonian path in the subgraph of AO(G) induced
by all the acyclic orientations of the form Ai ⊔ A′ for i ∈ [s] and A′ ∈ AO(H ′ ). We call such
a Hamiltonian path a replacing pattern for (A1 , . . . , As ). To be able to “string together” these
replacing patterns in order to construct a Hamiltonian path (or cycle) for AO(G), we develop a
theory of lacing patterns, leading to Proposition 4.3 and Corollary 4.4.

single edge from As ⊔A′ to As ⊔B ′ for some A′ , B ′ ∈ AO(H ′ |As ). Note that these two nodes As ⊔A′
to As ⊔ B ′ are in the path P ∧ Q since they belong to Qj , only the edge is not there. Hence, all
the nodes appearing in P (resp. Q) also appear in P ∧ Q. As P (resp. Q) is a Hamiltonian path
in AO(H ′ |A1 , . . . , As ) (resp. in AO(H ′ |As , . . . , At )), we indeed get that P ∧ Q is a Hamiltonian
path in AO(H ′ |A1 , . . . , As , . . . , At ).                                                            □
   Finally, a replacing pattern P for a directed cycle (A1 , . . . , As ) in AO(H) with As = A1 can
self-lace if conss (P ) = {1, . . . , |AO(H ′ |As )|} and for the orientation A′◦ ∈ AO(H ′ |As ) such that
ωsP (A′◦ ) = 1 we also have ω1P (A′◦ ) = 1. The self-lacing of P , denoted ∧P , is defined as P without
its edges from As ⊔ A′ to As ⊔ B ′ for some A′ , B ′ ∈ AO(H ′ |As ).
Corollary 4.4. Let G = H ⊔ H ′ be a bipartition of the edges of G. If A is a Hamiltonian cycle for
AO(H) and P a replacing pattern for A that can self-lace, then ∧P is a Hamiltonian cycle for
AO(G).
Proof. Since ωsP and ω1P have the same first element A′◦ , the first node of P is equal to the last
node of P without its edges from As ⊔ A′ to As ⊔ B ′ for some A′ , B ′ ∈ AO(H ′ |As ). Hence ∧P
is a (directed) cycle. Moreover, all the nodes of AO(H ′ |A1 , . . . As−1 ) appear in ∧P , so because
A1 = As , the cycle ∧P is a Hamiltonian cycle for AO(G).                                           □

4.4. Disconnected graphs, 1-sums, strongly even and internally even sequences. It is well-known
that if H and H ′ are Hamiltonian graphs, then the Cartesian product H × H ′ is also Hamiltonian:
for instance, it is the first sentence of [TE78]. Consequently, if G has several connected compo-
nents, each inducing a AO-Hamiltonian graph, then G itself is also AO-Hamiltonian because it is
straightforward to show that AO(G) is the Cartesian product of the graph of acyclic orientations of
its connected components. Similarly, if G = H ⊕1 H ′ is a 1-sum, then AO(G) = AO(H) × AO(H ′ ),
so if H and H ′ are AO-Hamiltonian, then G is also AO-Hamiltonian.
   We can go further and keep track of the 0-evenness. For disconnected graphs, the question is
trivial by definition: if u and v are not in the same connected component, then A(u, v) = (0, 0, . . . , 0)
for any sequence of orientations A, since no orientation can induce a directed path between u
and v. (Note however that Corollary 4.11 does not apply if u and v are not in the same connected
component.) The case of 1-sums is, strangely, richer. We need two notions to further control
evenness.
Definition 4.19. A sequence of integers a = (a1 , . . . , ar ) is strongly even if for all x ∈ {a1 , . . . , ar }
with x ̸= 0:
     • if 1 ≤ i < j ≤ r are such that ai = aj ̸= 0 and ak ̸= ai for all i < k < j, then i and j have
        the same parity; and
     • min{i ; ai = x} is odd and max{i ; ai = x} is even.
Definition 4.20. A sequence of integers a = (a1 , . . . , ar ) is internally even if:
                                                        47
        • for any x ∈ {a1 , . . . , ar } with x ̸= 0, if 1 ≤ i < j ≤ r are such that ai = aj ̸= 0 and ak ̸= ai
          for all i < k < j, then i and j have the same parity; and
        • the parity of min{i ; ai = x} does not depend on x ̸= 0; and the parity of max{i ; ai = x}
          does not depend on x ̸= 0.
Remark 4.21. It could be that a is strongly even but not 0-even, e.g. a = (1, 0, 2, 1, 0, 2). On the
other hand, strongly even implies internally even.

of consecutive 0s between the last non-zero value of the ith row of B(u, v) and the first non-zero
value of its j th row is even. Consequently, B(u, v) is 0-even.
   A similar reasoning as in the proof of Proposition 4.22 for strong evenness shows that B(u, v) is
also internally even.                                                                               □

each red arc (resp. blue arc) of ρ by the corresponding directed path in P (resp. in Q), keeping

any loose lace P ∧ρ Q is a replacing pattern of AO(H ′ |A1 , . . . , At ).
   In the case of usual lacing, the graph at stake is a path with two edges between consecutive
nodes, one of them being green.
   Besides, for a replacing pattern P of (A1 , . . . , As ) with A1 = As , consider the graph whose node

         such that ω1P (B ′ ) = ω1P (A′ ) + 1 and ω1P (A′ ) ∈ cons1 (P ).
We say that P can loosely self-lace if this graph is loop-free and admits a (directed) cycle using all
its red, blue and purple edges (and some, perhaps none, of its green edges). The loose self-lacing
according to ρ, denoted ∧ρ P is defined accordingly.
   A proof similar to the proof of Corollary 4.4 ensures that: If A is a Hamiltonian cycle for
AO(H) and P a replacing pattern for A that can loosely self-lace, then any loose self-lace ∧ρ P is
a Hamiltonian cycle for AO(G).

green edges from some A′ to some B ′ together with another path of red and green edges from the

                         Appendix B. A note on complete bipartite graphs
    The complete bipartite graph Km,n is the graph on nodes (a1 , . . . , am ) and (b1 , . . . , bn ) with edges
ai bj for all i ∈ [m] and j ∈ [n]. The longstanding question of which Km,n are AO-Hamiltonian
already appears in [SSW93, Sections 3 & 5]. In particular, the authors proved that if Km,n is AO-
Hamiltonian, then both m and n are odd, see [SSW93, Theorem 3.3] where they essentially prove
that σ(Km,n ) = 0 if and only if both m and n are odd. We conjecture (as other authors conducting
research on the subject) that the reciprocal is true, i.e. that the complete bipartite graph Km,n is
AO-Hamiltonian if and only if both m and n are odd.

Lemma B.3. The graph AO(m, n) is isomorphic to the graph of flips of bicolored (m, n)-partitions.
Proof. Let A ∈ AO(m, n), and ρ = (Q, Q′ , δ) the associated triple. We first need to show that
flipping an edge (p, q) where p, q are neighboring in ρ will not result in an oriented cycle. Let Si be
the block containing p and Si+1 the neighboring block of the partition ρ (Because of the orientation
of (p, q), p comes before q). If there were an oriented cycle after flipping this edge, then there would
be a directed path from p to q in A. Since the graph is bipartite, the path has length at least 3
and is thus of the form (p, r1 , r2 , q). By construction of A, this means that the node r1 is in a block
between Si and Si+1 , which is a contradiction.
   Conversely, consider an edge (p, q) between two non-neighboring blocks. It follows that there are
at least two blocks between Si containing p and Sj containing q. Take one node of each block, then
these p, q and these two nodes form an oriented path in A and would therefore form an oriented
4-cycle with the edge (p, q). This finishes the proof.                                                 □

   Appendix D. Acyclic polynomials and AO-Hamiltonicity of some small graphs
   To motivate further research, for 22 graphs (from the named-graph database of SageMath [Sag24]),
we computed the number of acyclic orientations ψ(G), the acyclic signature σ(G), and the acyclic
polynomial Ψ(D; t). The chosen orientation D of the graph G is obtained by orienting each edge
ij of G from i to j if i < j. For each graph, we address AO-Hamiltonicity. If σ(G) ̸= 0, then G
is not AO-Hamiltonian; otherwise we need to construct the graph of acyclic orientations AO(G)
and search for a Hamiltonian cycle in it. The latter is a NP-hard problem. Our computations took
minutes for the Moser spindle, and did not finish within 24 hours for the Wagner graph.
