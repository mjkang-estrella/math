                                                                                 Abstract
                                                     How do algebraic relations among unit directions influence the chro-
                                                 matic number of the plane? We introduce monomial Cayley graphs as a
                                                 framework for investigating this question, beginning with graphs whose
                                                 allowed unit steps are signed powers of a single complex parameter. Tran-
                                                 scendental parameters give bipartite graphs, while for roots of unity we
                                                 determine both the chromatic and circular chromatic numbers. As a geo-
                                                 metric consequence, every graph admitting a unit-distance embedding in
                                                 the plane in which the angle between any two edge directions is a ratio-
                                                 nal multiple of π is three-colorable. For algebraic noninteger parameters,
                                                 four colors suffice, and three suffice when the primitive integer minimal
                                                 polynomial has leading coefficient at least three. We conjecture that three
                                                 colors suffice for every algebraic noninteger direction parameter and that
                                                 four suffice for every direction parameter.


                                          1      Introduction
                                          The Hadwiger–Nelson problem asks how many colors are needed to color every
                                          point of the plane so that points at distance one receive different colors. This
                                          number is the chromatic number of the plane, denoted χ(R2 ). A unit-distance
                                          graph is a graph whose vertices can be placed at distinct points of the plane
                                          so that the endpoints of every edge are one unit apart. Every coloring of the
                                          plane restricts to a coloring of such a graph, so a finite unit-distance graph can
                                          provide a lower bound for χ(R2 ). In 2018, de Grey constructed a five-chromatic
                                          unit-distance graph [5], raising the lower bound for the chromatic number of the
                                          plane to five. In a preprint publicly released on October 6, 2026, OpenAI reports
                                          a proof that the plane admits no proper five-coloring, without any regularity
                                          assumption on the color classes [16]. Together with the seven-coloring obtained
                                          from a regular hexagonal tiling, illustrated in Figure 1, this gives

                                                                              6 ≤ χ(R2 ) ≤ 7.

pants sought smaller five-chromatic witnesses, more transparent explanations of
their coloring obstructions, and constructions requiring still more colors [14,19].
One strand of the discussion studied the algebraic structure of the coordinates.
Identifying the plane with C, participants considered rings generated by a few
complex numbers of modulus one. They asked which other elements of these
rings had modulus one and how the relations among these directions could be
used to construct colorings [14]. The Moser spindle [15] provides a small exam-
ple in which both the geometry and the algebra can be seen explicitly.

Every edge vector is therefore a signed monomial in α and β, while every vertex
is an integer linear combination of these monomials. This suggests an infinite
graph containing the spindle: take the ring Z[α, β] as the vertex set and join
two vertices when their difference belongs to

                         Mon(α, β) = {±αa β b : a, b ∈ Z≥0 }.

This is a Cayley graph of the additive group of the ring. More generally, for
an additive group G and a set S = −S of nonzero elements, Cay(G, S) has
vertex set G and an edge between u and v whenever v − u ∈ S. We apply this
construction to any collection of unit complex parameters.

Definition 1.1. Let A be a set of complex numbers of modulus one. Write
Mon(A) for the set of signed finite products of elements of A, including ±1 from
the empty product. The monomial Cayley graph generated by A is

                      MonCay(A) = Cay Z[A], Mon(A) .

For a finite parameter set A = {α1 , . . . , αr }, we also write MonCay(α1 , . . . , αr ).
    The parameters determine both the vertex ring and the allowed steps. Every
vertex is reached from the origin by a finite sequence of these steps, since every
element of Z[A] is an integer linear combination of monomials. Every edge has
length one. There can, however, be pairs of vertices at distance one whose
difference is not a signed monomial. Writing R = Z[A], we have an inclusion of
graphs on the same vertex set:

    This choice makes the allowed directions explicit without requiring a classi-
fication of every unit-modulus element of the ring. Polynomial relations among
the parameters describe the additive relations among the permitted steps, giv-
ing algebraic conditions that can be used to construct colorings. The restriction
to monomial steps still allows the program to recover the chromatic number of
the plane. To see this, take all algebraic unit directions as parameters:

                Ualg = {α ∈ C : α is algebraic over Q, |α| = 1}.

Let Γalg = MonCay(Ualg ). The set Ualg is closed under multiplication and
negation and contains 1, so Mon(Ualg ) = Ualg . Every algebraic unit direction is
therefore an allowed step in this countable graph.
    Every finite unit-distance graph has an embedding with algebraic coordi-
nates. The squared-distance formula turns the unit-distance constraints into a
finite system of quadratic polynomial equations with rational coefficients in the
vertex coordinates. Strict polynomial inequalities ensure that distinct vertices
remain distinct. Both R and the field of real algebraic numbers are real closed,
so the Tarski–Seidenberg transfer principle [1, Theorem 2.80] ensures that a real
solution gives a solution with real algebraic coordinates. For a connected graph,
translate one vertex of this algebraic embedding to the origin. A path from that
vertex to any other expresses the latter as a sum of algebraic unit directions.
The translated graph is consequently a subgraph of Γalg . Since Γalg is itself a
unit-distance graph, the de Bruijn–Erdős compactness theorem [4] gives

                               χ(Γalg ) = χ(R2 ).

    Each finite connected witness uses only finitely many algebraic edge direc-
tions, which may themselves be chosen as its parameters. Thus monomial Cay-
ley graphs with finite parameter sets suffice to recover the chromatic number
of the plane. Combining this observation with the lower bound of OpenAI [16]
gives a finite set A ⊂ Ualg for which

                              χ(MonCay(A)) ≥ 6.

This consequence is existential: the argument does not identify such a param-
eter set or bound its size. The appendix gives explicit embeddings of the five-
chromatic graphs of de Grey and Parts using small collections of algebraic pa-
rameters [5, 18]. The wider aim is to understand how relations among a few
direction parameters govern colorability, to construct explicit witnesses requir-
ing at least six colors, and to investigate whether seven colors can be forced.
    The spindle supplies a four-chromatic finite subgraph using two direction
parameters. We begin the systematic study of this program with one param-
eter: how many colors can be forced by the powers of a single unit complex
number? Let α = eiθ now be an arbitrary unit complex number, and write

Γα = MonCay(α). Its allowed steps are ±αn for nonnegative integers n. Walks
from the origin along these steps reach exactly the vertices of Z[α], each of which
has the form
                         p(α) = am αm + · · · + a1 α + a0
with integer coefficients aj . If θ/π is irrational, the allowed directions are dense
in the unit circle. Yet two colors suffice when α is transcendental: every vertex
has a unique polynomial expression, and each allowed step changes the parity
of the sum of its coefficients. For algebraic α, polynomial relations identify
different expressions for the same vertex. Understanding how these relations
constrain a coloring is the one-parameter problem studied in this paper. Powers
of algebraic integers on the unit circle also occur in Radchenko’s construction of
finitely generated additive groups whose full unit-distance graphs have infinite
vertex degrees [20].
    Our main results describe this algebraic case. When θ is a rational multiple
of π, the parameter α is a root of unity, and we determine χ(Γα ) exactly. It is
two when the order of α is a power of two, and three otherwise. We also de-
termine the circular chromatic number, a refinement of the ordinary chromatic
number based on separation of colors around a circle [25, 28]. As a geomet-
ric consequence, every unit-distance graph whose edge directions form pairwise
angles that are rational multiples of π is three-colorable. For algebraic parame-
ters that are not algebraic integers, the theorem of Garcı́a-Marco, Knauer, and
Menara [6, Theorem 4.3] gives a four-color bound; we give further arithmetic
criteria guaranteeing two or three colors. We conjecture that three colors suffice
for every parameter in this latter class.
    For algebraic integers, four colors can be necessary even with a single di-
rection parameter. We construct such an example, answering the question in
Cervantes and Krebs [2, Example 2.14]. This leads to our central conjecture:
for every unit complex parameter α,

                                    χ(Γα ) ≤ 4.

    Our coloring methods bring together the matrix viewpoint of Cervantes and
Krebs [2, Examples 2.13–2.14] and the multiplier method described in Liu’s sur-
vey of distance graphs [13, Section 2]. In a cyclic quotient Z/pZ, the multiplier
method sends a residue x to λx modulo p, choosing λ so that the allowed step
differences move away from zero around the cycle. For example, multiplication
by 3 modulo 7 sends the steps ±1 to ±3. Placing the residues at equally spaced
points of a circle of circumference one then separates adjacent vertices of the
seven-cycle by circular distance 3/7. Dividing the circle into three half-open
arcs of equal length gives a proper three-coloring.
    In general, the normalized multiplier takes the form x 7→ λx/p + Z. This
is an additive map into R/Z, also called an additive character. We use this
formulation of the multiplier method on Z[α]: a character whose values on all
allowed steps stay sufficiently far from zero gives a coloring by partitioning the
circle into short arcs, as well as a bound on the circular chromatic number. For


                                         5
algebraic α, the minimal polynomial imposes a linear recurrence on the charac-
ter values at successive powers of α. Polynomial evaluations and congruences
provide ways to construct solutions with the required separation. For algebraic
noninteger parameters, we present the character-extension construction under-
lying the semiminimal Cayley graph bound cited above.
    These upper bounds also guide the search for finite subgraphs requiring more
colors, following the screening strategy proposed by Gibbs in Polymath16 [7].
A successful coloring test gives an upper bound for the entire infinite graph;
parameter sets left unresolved can then be examined through finite subgraphs.
A satisfiability solver tests whether a prescribed number of colors suffices, and
an obstruction can be reduced by deleting vertices while preserving its noncol-
orability, as in Heule’s work on five-chromatic unit-distance graphs [10]. The
resulting witness can then be checked independently.
    The four-chromatic example announced above brings these two parts of the
method together. Let α be a unit-modulus root of the sextic polynomial

                          f (x) = x6 − x4 − x3 − x2 + 1.

An explicit periodic character gives a four-coloring of the entire graph Γα . Start-
ing from a graph on the 830 distinct sums of selected powers 1, α, . . . , α9 , our
computational search produces a subgraph with 24 vertices and 51 edges that
admits no proper three-coloring. Its edges are verified by exact polynomial re-
duction, and its non-three-colorability is checked by exhaustive backtracking.
These upper and lower bounds establish χ(Γα ) = 4. Figure 3 displays the finite

2     Colorings from Polynomial Relations
A polynomial relation among the powers of α describes a closed walk in Γα .
To construct a coloring from images of the allowed steps, those images must
respect every such relation. We begin with a worked example in which one rela-
tion yields both a coloring and a matching obstruction. The Shift Criterion and
the multiplier method then provide a common framework for the constructions
that follow. We use Eisenstein quotients to obtain the Cyclotomic Congru-
ence Criterion, and then turn to trace polynomials and norms when the integer
congruence tests give no bound.

repeatedly, specifying the relation that vanishes and the images of the steps in
each target.

5    Conclusion and Future Directions
The one-parameter results give a first illustration of how the monomial Cayley
graph program can turn algebraic relations into information about colorings.
The multiplier method uses these relations to construct colorings, while finite
subgraphs such as the sextic witness show when additional colors are neces-
sary. The next steps are to complete the one-parameter classification and to
understand how several parameters can interact to require more colors.
    Two conjectures remain. Conjecture 4.7, the uniform four-color bound, is
open only for noncyclotomic algebraic integers of degree at least ten. For Con-
jecture 4.3, only algebraic nonintegers with leading coefficient two and degree at
least ten remain. Together with the bipartite criterion, these conjectures would
reduce the classification to distinguishing the algebraic integer parameters that
require three colors from those that require four.
    Preliminary exploratory work with additional cyclotomic tests suggests that
the four-color problem through degree twenty-six can be reduced to a finite
collection of unresolved minimal polynomials. We do not develop these exten-
sions here. One remaining example is the degree-twenty polynomial f∗ (x) =
x10 F∗ (x + x−1 ), where

                       F∗ (y) = y(y 2 − 1)(y 2 − 4)Q∗ (y) − 1

and Q∗ (y) = y 5 + y 4 − 4y 3 − 3y 2 + 3y + 1. Thus all five integer evaluations from
Subsection 4.4 equal −1, so they give no coloring target. Four-colorability for
its unit-modulus roots remains unresolved.


                                         40
    Gross and McMullen’s Salem-polynomial construction [8, Theorem 7.3] gives
a general limitation of fixed-order tests, stated in Theorem A.1. For every pre-
scribed finite set of cyclotomic orders, there are irreducible Salem polynomials
of arbitrarily large degree whose evaluations at those orders are units. Each of
these evaluations gives the trivial quotient, so no fixed finite collection of orders
supplies a coloring target for every parameter. A uniform proof by cyclotomic
targets must therefore select an order from the minimal polynomial itself. It
must also account for the order-dependent threshold Bm in Theorem 2.9: growth
of the norms alone does not ensure that they exceed their respective thresholds.
    For a noncyclotomic algebraic integer parameter with minimal polynomial of
degree d, the recurrence for successive character values defines an automorphism
of Td . A forward orbit contained in [1/4, 3/4]d is sufficient for a four-coloring:
the corresponding character stays separated from zero on every allowed step.
Lindenstrauss and Schmidt’s work on symbolic representations of nonexpansive
automorphisms, including those associated with Salem polynomials, provides a
related setting for studying these orbits [12].
    To pass to several parameters, return to the Moser spindle in Figure 2. Its
parameters α and β have squared cosines 1/4 and 11/12, respectively. Each
individual parameter gives a three-colorable graph, while the spindle forces at
least four colors in MonCay(α, β). The connecting edge makes the interaction
explicit: the mixed relation (1 + α)(β 2 − 1) = α2 β links the two diamonds and
prevents their three-colorings from fitting together. A character must respect
these relations while separating every mixed monomial step from zero.
    This example suggests beginning with parameters α = eiθ for which q =
    2
cos θ is rational. The family builds on the algebraic rotation constructions
explored in Polymath16 [14]; Conway, Radin, and Sadun studied rational linear
relations among angles with rational squared trigonometric functions [3]. Each
parameter satisfies α4 + (2 − 4q)α2 + 1 = 0, so it has degree at most four and
gives a three-colorable graph by Corollary 4.10. The appendix’s embeddings of
the five-chromatic graphs of de Grey and Parts use four parameters from this
family [5, 18].
    The construction also extends to higher dimensions by applying products of
chosen orthogonal transformations to a fixed unit vector. Their orbit generates
the additive vertex group, with the orbit vectors and their negatives as steps.
Unlike planar rotations, these transformations need not commute, so their order
matters and their relations must be expressed as words in the transformations.
The vertex group remains abelian, leaving quotient maps and separated additive
characters available as coloring tools.
    Returning to the plane, the multiparameter problem has an extremal for-
mulation. For each positive integer r, let
                    
       M (r) = max χ(MonCay(A)) : A ⊆ {z ∈ C : |z| = 1}, 1 ≤ |A| ≤ r .

The maximum exists because the possible values are integers bounded above by
χ(R2 ) ≤ 7, and M (r) is nondecreasing. The sextic gives M (1) ≥ 4, with equality
predicted by Conjecture 4.7; the four-parameter embeddings give M (4) ≥ 5.


                                         41
Can two parameters already force five colors? The same question can be asked
with all parameters restricted to rational squared cosines. In that restricted
family, the one-parameter maximum is three, attained by a sixth root of unity.
    The unrestricted function reaches the chromatic number of the plane for
some finite r. Indeed, compactness gives a finite connected unit-distance graph
with chromatic number χ(R2 ) [4]. The algebraic-coordinate argument in the
introduction embeds this graph using finitely many algebraic edge directions.
Choosing those directions as parameters places the graph in a monomial Cayley
graph, giving M (r) = χ(R2 ) for a sufficiently large finite r.
    The lower bound of OpenAI [16] therefore implies that M (r) ≥ 6 for some
finite r. Determining the least such r, and exhibiting parameters that attain it,
are concrete problems for the multiparameter program. The preprint obtains
the existence of a finite graph that is not five-colorable through compactness,
following an analytic and geometric argument. Can a finite obstruction be ex-
tracted from that argument and embedded using a small collection of monomial
generators? An explicit construction would allow its coloring obstruction to be
studied through the algebraic relations among those generators. The broader
question remains: how few parameters suffice to attain the chromatic number
of the plane, and which relations make them do so?

edges in the monomial framework.
