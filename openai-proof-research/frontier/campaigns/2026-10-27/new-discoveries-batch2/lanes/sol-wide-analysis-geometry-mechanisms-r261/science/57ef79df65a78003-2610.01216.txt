                                                                                           Abstract
                                                     Trisecting the dihedral angles of an n-simplex defines its Morley simplex. We study

                                                 the original simplices for which this simplex is regular. A criterion in terms of the Gram
                                                 matrix of the facet normals reduces the problem to a matrix equation. The derivative of
                                                 the Morley map at the regular simplex has two explicit eigenvalues, both nonzero for n ≥ 3;
                                                 thus the regular simplex is an isolated solution. We conjecture that in dimensions four and
                                                 five a regular Morley simplex forces two hyperplane reflections interchanging disjoint pairs of

1    Introduction
Morley’s trisector theorem says that the adjacent angle trisectors of a triangle meet in the
vertices of an equilateral triangle [8]; see [2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 14] for proofs and [1, 4] for extensions.
Svrtan and Veljan considered a three dimensional analogue [12]. In [13] the author defined the
Morley tetrahedron by trisecting the six dihedral angles of a tetrahedron and proposed two
converse conjectures.
    We call a tetrahedron isosceles when its three pairs of opposite edges have equal lengths;
regular tetrahedra are included. Definition 1 of [13] excluded the regular case, but the proof of
the direct theorem establishes preservation of the opposite edge equalities. We use this statement
and the inclusive convention of the companion paper [15].
    They do not. In the companion paper [15] we found a nonisosceles tetrahedron T1 and an
isosceles but nonregular tetrahedron T2 whose Morley tetrahedra are regular. Both have a pair
of opposite edges such that the four remaining edges are equal; in other words, each has two
reflection symmetries that interchange disjoint pairs of vertices. We conjectured in [15] that
a regular Morley tetrahedron always forces this, and proved it for every tetrahedron with a
nontrivial symmetry.
    The Morley construction makes sense in every dimension. For each facet of an n-simplex,
take the n hyperplanes that trisect the dihedral angles along the ridges of that facet, on the side
of the facet. They meet in one point, and the n + 1 points obtained in this way are the vertices
of the Morley simplex. For n ≥ 3 let (Gn ) be the statement:

      every n-simplex whose Morley simplex is regular has two reflection symmetries that
      interchange disjoint pairs of vertices.

The main conjecture of [15] is (G3 ). This paper is about (Gn ) for n ≥ 4, and more generally
about the simplices with regular Morley simplex.

Results
The first part of the paper sets up the tools. The Gram matrix of the unit normals of the Morley
simplex is an explicit function of the Gram matrix H of the unit normals of the original simplex
(Proposition 2.2). Regularity of the Morley simplex becomes the matrix equation (4), which
relates H to the matrix K = (2 cos(θij /3)) built from the trisected dihedral angles (Theorem 2.3).

    In dimensions six and seven the examples have an isometry group of order two. For
8 ≤ n ≤ 200 they belong to a family of simplices with dihedral symmetry of order 2(n + 1)
(Section 9); this family appears to exist for every n ≥ 8 (Conjecture 9.5), which together with the

simplex and two classes U1 , U2 . A cyclic five vertex family contains only the regular simplex
(Theorem 6.1).

Theorem C (Theorems 6.2, 6.3 and 6.4). The classes U1 and U2 are given exactly by a root
of an irreducible polynomial of degree 18, respectively 8, together with square roots. The Galois
groups of the two polynomials are S18 and S8 , and neither simplex can be expressed by radicals.

On the computations
Numerical searches suggested the examples and the conjectures. The qualitative observations
reported below do not enter any proof or establish a classification. Each computer assisted result
is instead reduced to exact algebraic identities and rational interval bounds. Floating point
arithmetic supplies starting points and approximate inverses; the checking programs then treat
these as rational data and verify the required inequalities exactly. The programs use only the
Python standard library and are supplied as ancillary files, with instructions in Appendix B.

3    Reflections and a symmetry theorem
Proposition 3.1. A hyperplane reflection that maps ∆ to itself interchanges exactly one pair of
vertices and fixes the others. The transposition of Ai and Aj is induced by a reflection of ∆ if
and only if |Ai Ak | = |Aj Ak | for every k ∈
                                            / {i, j}, and this happens if and only if K is invariant
under the transposition. More generally, a permutation of the vertices is induced by an isometry
of ∆ if and only if it preserves K.

Proof. A reflection ρ of ∆ permutes the vertices and is not the identity, so it moves some vertex.
If it interchanged two disjoint pairs Ai , Aj and Ak , Al , the vectors Ai − Aj and Ak − Al would
both lie in the (−1)-eigenspace of the linear part of ρ, which has dimension one; they are linearly
independent because the vertices are affinely independent. Hence ρ induces a transposition.
     The perpendicular bisector of Ai Aj contains every other vertex exactly under the stated
condition on edge lengths, and then the reflection in it induces the transposition. A permutation
induced by an isometry preserves dihedral angles and hence K. Conversely, a permutation
preserving K preserves H = φ[K], so it is induced by a similarity of ∆ onto itself (Theorem 2.3);
a similarity that permutes a finite set spanning Rn affinely fixes its centroid and preserves the
sum of the squared distances to it, so it is an isometry.

Part II
Conjectures in higher dimensions
5    The conjectures
In dimension three all known simplices with regular Morley simplex have two reflections on
disjoint pairs of vertices, and this is the content of the main conjecture of [15]. Our experiments,
described in Part III, suggest that this persists in dimensions four and five and breaks down in
dimension six.

Conjecture 5.1 (Two reflections in dimensions four and five). Let n ∈ {4, 5}. If the Morley
simplex of an n-simplex ∆ is regular, then ∆ has two hyperplane reflection symmetries that
interchange disjoint pairs of vertices.

     By Proposition 3.1 each of the two reflections fixes all vertices outside its pair, and the two
mirrors are perpendicular. The conjecture is the statement (G4 ) and (G5 ); it does not claim a
list of all similarity classes.

Conjecture 5.2 (No reflections from dimension six on). For every n ≥ 6 there is an n-simplex
with no hyperplane reflection symmetry whose Morley simplex is regular.

element of type (9, 5, 3, 1) is a 5-cycle. By Jordan’s theorem a primitive group of degree m that
contains a cycle of prime length at most m − 3 contains the alternating group; since an 18-cycle
is odd, G = S18 .

11     Towards the remaining dimensions
                                                                                              k
                                                                                                  
By Corollary 9.4 and Theorem 10.2, Conjecture 5.2 is open only for n ≥ 201 with n ̸=          2       − 1.
We see three ways to proceed.

11.1    The dihedral family for all N
A proof of Conjecture 9.5 would give examples in every remaining dimension. Extending the
computation in Theorem 9.3 reaches further finite ranges, but cannot prove the assertion for
all N . One possible approach is to solve the integral equation in Section 9, prove invertibility
of its linearization, and compare its solution quantitatively with the discrete equations (15).
The estimates would need to be uniform in N and strong enough to meet the certified range.
The Johnson family offers a simpler model: its reduced equation has a nondegenerate limit as
k → ∞. For the dihedral family, the behaviour of the proposed profile near 0 also needs to be
understood.

                                                 19
11.2    Families with a simultaneous interchange of pairs
The examples in dimensions six and seven are invariant under an involution that interchanges
several pairs of vertices at once, with one fixed vertex when the number of vertices is odd. Such
an involution reduces the number of equations without forcing any single transposition, and the
reflection test of Proposition 3.1 can exclude transpositions by strict inequalities. One would
like to find a branch of such solutions whose size grows with the number of pairs and to control
it uniformly. We have not found such a branch.

11.3    Other families
Other permutation actions may reduce the regularity equations to a small number of spectral
equations, as in the circulant and Johnson constructions. A useful family would have solutions
for an unbounded set of dimensions, together with a positive kernel and a way to exclude every
transposition. Finding such a family in dimensions not covered by the Johnson construction
would give another route to Conjecture 5.2.


12     Open problems
  1. Prove Conjecture 5.1, that is, (G4 ) and (G5 ). In dimension four, are the regular simplex,
     U1 and U2 the only simplices with regular Morley simplex, up to similarity? In dimension
     three the corresponding question is equivalent to (G3 ) [15].

  2. Prove Conjecture 9.5, or at least Conjecture 5.2. The smallest open dimension is 201.

  3. Does the dihedral system admit other nonregular branches? Can one construct a seven-
     simplex with regular Morley simplex whose only nonidentity isometry fixes two vertices
     and interchanges three pairs?

  4. Is there a simplex with regular Morley simplex and trivial isometry group, or one with all
     edge lengths distinct? All nonregular examples certified in this paper have a nontrivial
     symmetry.

  5. For fixed n ≥ 3, are there only finitely many similarity classes of n-simplices with regular
     Morley simplex?

  6. How large is the neighbourhood in Theorem 4.1, and how does it depend on n?

  7. Which of these simplices can be expressed by radicals? In dimension three T2 can and T1
     cannot; in dimension four neither U1 nor U2 can. The dihedral and Johnson simplices are
     defined by small polynomial systems for each N or k, and their Galois groups could be
     computed in small cases.

  8. Extend the sector construction of [13, Theorem 6] to higher dimensions and study the
     same questions for it.

Appendix B                 Verification of the computations
The computer assisted steps are checked by the following Python scripts, available as ancillary
files with the arXiv version. They use only the standard library. All inequalities used in
proofs are decided in exact rational arithmetic, or with intervals whose endpoints are rational
numbers rounded outwards; floating point numbers serve only to find approximate solutions and
approximate inverses. The scripts should not be run with Python’s -O option, which disables
assertions.
     From the anc directory, the command python verify all.py runs all six checks and stops if
any fails. The shared toolkit rejects runs with assertions disabled. The accompanying README.md
gives the individual commands and explains how to extend the finite circulant range. The
