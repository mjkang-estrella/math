                                                  the points on the unit circle where the polynomial takes real values. In analogy to the

   The mth Gram point, gm is the value ≥ 10 such that θ(gm ) = mπ. Due to the
smooth nature of θ(t), these points are very regularly spaced. Whilst it was Gram [5]
who numerically observed that the first few zeros of zeta interlace with the Gram points
(which is another way of saying that S(T ) in the counting function stays small for T
within computable ranges), it was Hutchinson [9] who coined the term “Gram’s Law”
for this phenomenon. Hutchinson went on to say that “Gram expressed the belief that
this law is not a general one”, and indeed we now know it regularly fails. However, it is
highly likely that a positive proportion of the time between two consecutive zeros of zeta
there lies exactly one Gram point.
   If a Gram points lies roughly in the middle of two consecutive zeros of zeta, then
Richard Hall (in a conversation with the first author) wondered if zeta evaluated at the
Gram points should be bigger than a typical value of zeta.

  The purpose of this paper is to use random matrix theory to argue that the answer
to Hall’s question, whether zeta is larger at the Gram points than typical, is affirmative:
we conjecture that the 2kth moment of the Riemann zeta function at the Gram points is
always bigger than the 2kth continuous moment, but only in the sub-leading terms. We
anticipate the leading order term will be the same size as the continuous moments. We
arrive at our conjecture by analysing an analogous problem in random matrix theory.

                          2. Random Matrix Gram Points
  We introduce a notion of Gram points in the random matrix setting, motivated by
the classical Gram points associated with the zeta function. Since the Riemann zeta
function takes real values at its Gram points after a suitable normalization, we study
the corresponding question for characteristic polynomials of random matrices. Using the
functional equation satisfied by these characteristic polynomials, we determine the points
at which they become real, thereby identifying the random matrix analogue of Gram
points.

                  Appendix A. Moments without absolute values
   In that case of Gram points, namely when ϕ = 0, the values of the characteristic
polynomial are always real. There is some interest in understanding the discrete moments
without absolute values. Obviously in the case of even integers, this will be exactly the
same as what we calculated before, but for odd integers the size of the moments gives us
an indication of how frequently zeta at the Gram points changes sign.
   We remark that if Gram’s Law held (and we know that it doesn’t), then between every
two consecutive Gram points there will be exactly one zero of zeta, which means the zeta
function will never change sign at the Gram points. This, however, certainly does not
happen. The same proof technique as above allows one to calculate the odd moments
of the characteristic polynomial at the random matrix Gram points (without taking the
absolute value) and they are smaller by a factor of N 1/4 , thus showing the characteristic
polynomial must change sign. We will return to these consequences later.
