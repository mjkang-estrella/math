                                          1    Introduction
                                          A great many practical questions reduce to a single one: is this histogram flat?
                                          One observes counts X1 , . . . , Xn across n ordered categories — analog-to-digital


                                                                                        1
converter codes, digit values, time bins — and must decide whether they are con-
sistent with the uniform law. The classical instrument is Pearson’s chi-square
test [19]: universal, well understood, and rightly the default. But it has one blind
spot, invisible precisely because it is so fundamental: chi-square does not see order.
It treats the bins as an unordered set, so any permutation of the categories leaves
its value unchanged. This is harmless for many alternatives, but not for deviations
that alternate from one bin to the next, pi = n1 + (−1)i ε (a probability vector for
even n; for odd n one endpoint is adjusted). Such “comb” patterns occur, for ex-
ample, in converter codes, digit preferences, and rounded or last-digit data. They
are visible through neighboring differences, but not through an unordered sum of
squared residuals alone. Chi-square is of course consistent against any fixed alter-
native, comb included; the issue is efficiency. The comb’s noncentrality is spread
over n−1 degrees of freedom, whereas a statistic that compares neighbors √   responds
to it directly — the difference that matters for local alternatives ε ∼ c/ N and
moderate N .

Discrete total variation is standard in signal and image processing (total-variation
denoising [22]); its statistical use, as an outlier measure for histograms, was intro-

to compute, and can achieve substantially higher power than chi-square against
comb-like alternatives — a claim documented empirically in [2]; the present paper
proves nothing about power and supplies the first moment of the null calibration.
    The statistic sits in a classical   lineage. It is the ℓ1 analogue of the mean-
square successive difference i (Xi+1 − Xi )2 of Abbe and von Neumann [23, 24],

     A test statistic is only as useful as our knowledge of its null distribution, and here
the price of order-sensitivity comes due. For the first moment the difficulty is not
the dependence of the counts: linearity of expectation and exchangeability reduce
E(d) to a single two-bin marginal. The genuine obstacles at first-moment level are
the non-smoothness
          P               of the absolute value and the asymptotics; the dependence
through i Xi = N and the overlap of neighboring summands |Xi+1 − Xi | begin
to bite at the variance, treated in Part 2, and the exact distribution is harder
still. The exact null law of d is already computable by dynamic programming [2],
with a fitted gamma law calibrating beyond that; closed-form moments are what
an analytic large-N calibration needs. The present paper opens that program with
the first moment under the uniform null, the case where the theory is cleanest.
The expectation has a closed form in √      central trinomial
                                                          √ coefficients, valid for every
n ≥ 2, and an n-independent limit E(d◦ / N n) → 2/ π, together with its complete
1/N expansion — governed by an exact three-term recurrence that delivers the
coefficients in closed recursive form. Two facts drive every computation and are
recalled in the Preliminaries: the edge variable Dj = Xj+1 − Xj depends only on
a two-bin marginal, and that marginal splits into independent binomial steps; the
same mixture-conditioning method also carries the analysis beyond the uniform
case. We note at the outset that DTV is not a universal replacement for chi-square
— it gains over chi-square chiefly against period-2 (alternating) departures, the
gain fading as the period grows [2], and is otherwise complementary to it.
     This paper is the first part of a larger study of exact and asymptotic moments
of discrete total variation under multinomial sampling. Part 2 treats the variance
under the uniform law; Parts 3 and 4 the expectation and variance under a general
reference law r, where, as Part 3 will show, mixture-conditioning still applies;
Part 5 studies the cyclic statistic as its subject proper — its variance, the exact
linear–cyclic transfer identity, and the large-bin variance ridge — whereas here
E(d◦ ) follows from exchangeability at no extra cost. The distributional theory —
the asymptotic null laws of d that complement the exact computation of [2] — is
developed in a companion paper, for which the moments computed in this series
are the inputs. Two regimes must be kept        √ apart there: for fixed n as N → ∞
the number of summands is fixed and d/ N converges to a functional of finitely
many dependent folded Gaussians, which is not normal in general; approximate
normality of d is a large-n phenomenon, the folded summands being 1-dependent
in the Gaussian limit (exactly so after Poissonization), so an m-dependent central
limit theorem [13] applies to their number n (cf. Morris [15] for the multinomial
background). For clarity of scope: the present paper computes first moments under
the uniform null only; it does not treat the variance (Part 2), non-uniform laws
(Parts 3–4), the sparse regime of fixed N and n → ∞ (a separate paper), or the
null distribution (the companion paper). Only the facts needed for the present
expectation calculation are recalled below.

3     Expectation of DTV under the uniform distri-
      bution
Throughout the paper the sample size satisfies N ≥ 1; the empty sample N = 0
carries no information and is excluded, so the statements below omit this standing
hypothesis.
    The uniform null is where the theory is cleanest: the symmetry of the uniform
multinomial makes every edge interchangeable, so the mean of d reduces to that of
a single edge. That mean is the yardstick against which an observed total variation
is judged significant, and it is worth knowing exactly, not merely up to a constant.
    We obtain it in two stages. First, §4 gives an exact closed form in central
trinomial coefficients, valid for every n ≥ 2 and specializing to one-term formulas
at n = 2, 3, 4. Then §5 develops the large-N asymptotics by mixture-conditioning
— less explicit but portable, and the route that survives the passage to non-uniform
reference laws in the later parts.                     √
    After normalization, the cyclic mean tends to 2/ π regardless of the number
of bins: a quantity that looks as though it ought to depend on the whole shape
of the problem collapses, in the limit, to an n-independent constant — the first
of several such collapses in the moment ptheory of DTV. This value is no accident:
Var D1 = 2N/n exactly, so E |D1 | ∼ 2√ N/(nπ) by the central limit theorem, and
                                                        √
summing the n edges and dividing by N n returns 2/ π; the content below is the
exact value and its complete expansion.

5     Asymptotics via mixture-conditioning
Having the exact closed form, we turn to the large-N behavior, which it does not
display directly: the bracket TN − (n − 4)TN −1 is a difference of exponentially
large terms. Mixture-conditioning supplies the asymptotics — it conditions on the
sum K = X1 + X2 , turns the difference into a simple random walk, and delivers
the full expansion of E |D1 |. The method is also portable: it is generalized to
the nonuniform case in Part 3, on the expectation under a general reference law,
whereas the closed form of §4 is tied to the uniform symmetry.
    Under the uniform multinomial, K = X1 + X2 ∼ Bin(N, 2/n) (Proposition 2.4)
with µK = 2N/n, Var(K) = (2N/n)(1 − 2/n). Conditionally X2 | K = k ∼
Bin(k, 1/2) (since q = 1/2 for the uniform). So

6    Concluding remarks
We have given a complete account of the first moment of the discrete total variation
under the uniform multinomial null: an exact single sum, a closed form in central
trinomial coefficients (valid for all n ≥ 2, with one-term evaluations at n = 2, 4 and
a complete D-finite characterization for n = 3), an exact three-term recurrence for
the per-edge√expectation WN , and the full 1/N expansion with its n-independent
constant 2/ π. The complementary sparse regime (fixed N , n → ∞) is treated in
a separate paper.
    This is the first part of a series on the exact and asymptotic moments of discrete
total variation under multinomial sampling. The mixture-conditioning method
used here is the common thread: whereas the closed form of §4 is tied to the
uniform symmetry, conditioning on a two-bin total carries over to an arbitrary
reference law. Part 2 treats the variance of d under the uniform null; Part 3 the


                                          21
expectation under a general reference law r, where this method generalizes; Part 4
the variance under a general reference law; and Part 5 the cyclic statistic d◦ as
its subject proper — its variance, the exact linear–cyclic transfer identity, and
the large-bin variance ridge. Together, these moments form the moment backbone
of the null calibration of d; the asymptotic null laws themselves — the fixed-n
folded-Gaussian limit law, its expansions, and the large-n normal approximation,
which complement the exact dynamic-programming computation of [2] — are the
subject of a companion paper, for which the moments computed in this series are
the inputs.
