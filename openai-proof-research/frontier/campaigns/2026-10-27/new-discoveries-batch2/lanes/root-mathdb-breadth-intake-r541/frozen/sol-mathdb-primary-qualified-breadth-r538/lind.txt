Lehmer’s Problem for
Compact Abelian Groups
Douglas Lind
Address: Department of Mathematics,
Box 354350, University of Washington, Seattle, WA 98195–4350
Email address: lind@math.washington.edu
Date: August 24, 2026
Abstract.
We formulate Lehmer’s Problem about the Mahler measure of
polynomials for general compact abelian groups, introducing a
Lehmer constant for each such group. We show that all
nontrivial connected compact groups have the same Lehmer
constant, and conjecture the value of the Lehmer constant for
finite cyclic groups. We also show that if a group has
infinitely many connected components then its Lehmer constant
vanishes.
Key words and phrases: Lehmer Problem, Mahler measure, compact abelian group
2000 Mathematics Subject ClassificationPrimary: 43A40, 22D40; Secondary: 37B40, 11G50
1. Introduction
Let f\in\mathbb{Z}[x^{\pm 1}] be a Laurent polynomial with integer
coefficients. Define its logarithmic Mahler measure to
be
\mathsf{m}(f)=\int_{0}^{1}\log|f(e^{2\pi is})|\,ds.
Lehmer’s Problem asks whether \mathsf{m}(f) can be arbitrarily small
but positive. Equivalently, does
\inf\{\,\mathsf{m}(f):f\in\mathbb{Z}[x^{\pm 1}],\ \mathsf{m}(f)>0\,\}
equal zero? The smallest positive value of \mathsf{m}(f) known, found
by Lehmer himself [6], is attained by
(1.1)
f_{L}(x)=x^{10}+x^{9}-x^{7}-x^{6}-x^{5}-x^{4}-x^{3}+x+1,
for which \mathsf{m}(f_{L})\approx 0.16235. For accounts of this problem,
see [2], [3], and for connections with dynamics
see [4].
Lehmer’s Problem can be formulated for arbitrary compact abelian
groups. To do so, let G be a compact abelian group with
normalized Haar measure  \mu. Let \widehat{G} denote its
(multiplicative) dual group of characters, and \mathbb{Z}[\widehat{G}] be
the ring of integral combinations of characters. For
f\in\mathbb{Z}[\widehat{G}] define its logarithmic Mahler measure
over G to be
\mathsf{m}(f)=\mathsf{m}_{G}(f)=\int_{G}\log|f|\,d\mu.
Since \log 0=-\infty, if f vanishes on a set of positive
\mu-measure then \mathsf{m}(f)=-\infty, but otherwise \mathsf{m}(f)\geq 0 by
Lemma 2.1 below.
Definition 1.1.
The Lehmer constant of a compact abelian group G is
\lambda(G)=\inf\{\,\mathsf{m}_{G}(f):f\in\mathbb{Z}[\widehat{G}],\ \mathsf{m}_{G}(f)>0\,\}.
Lehmer’s Problem therefore asks whether \lambda(\mathbb{T})=0, where
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}.
We show in Theorem 3.2 that all nontrivial
connected groups have the same Lehmer constant \lambda(\mathbb{T}). On
the other hand, Theorem 5.1 shows that if G
has infinitely many connected components, then \lambda(G)=0. We
compute the Lehmer constant of some finite groups, and conjecture
a simple formula for cyclic groups and also for products of
two-element groups. Finally, we show that if \lambda(\mathbb{T})=0, then
the only groups which have positive Lehmer constant are finite.
2. Preliminaries
Let G be a compact abelian group, and let f\in\mathbb{Z}[\widehat{G}]. If
f vanishes on a set of positive \mu-measure then clearly
\mathsf{m}(f)=-\infty.
Lemma 2.1.
Let f\in\mathbb{Z}[\widehat{G}]. If \mu(\{x:f(x)=0\})=0, then
\mathsf{m}(f)\geq 0.
Proof.
The characters appearing in f generate a subgroup \Delta
of \widehat{G}. Let H=\Delta^{\perp} and \pi\colon G\to G/H be
the quotient map. Then f is constant on cosets of H, and
so defines \widetilde{f}\in\mathbb{Z}[(G/H)^{^}\,]=\mathbb{Z}[\Delta] such
that \widetilde{f}\circ\pi=f. Clearly
\mathsf{m}_{G/H}(\widetilde{f})=\mathsf{m}_{G}(f), and \widetilde{f}
vanishes on a set of positive measure if and only if f
does. Hence we may assume that \widehat{G} is finitely generated.
Let \mathbb{Z}_{\!/n} denote \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}.
Applying the structure theorem for finitely generated abelian
groups to \widehat{G}, we see that G is isomorphic
\mathbb{Z}_{\!/n_{1}}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{\!/n_{r}}\oplus\mathbb{T}^{k}
for suitable integers n_{1},\dots,n_{r} and k. Consider
f\in\mathbb{Z}[\widehat{G}] as a function of r+1 variables
j_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\!/n_{1}}, \dots, j_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\!/n_{r}}, and
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{T}^{k}. Since the set where f vanishes is
null, each function
f_{j_{1}\dots j_{r}}(\mathbf{s})=f(j_{1},\dots,j_{r},\mathbf{s})
is a nonvanishing complex combination of characters on \mathbb{T}^{k},
and hence by [4, Lemma 3.7] we see that
\mathsf{m}_{\mathbb{T}^{k}}(f_{j_{1}\dots j_{r}})>-\infty.
Thus
\displaystyle\mathsf{m}(f)
\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}\dots n_{r}}\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{n_{1}-1}\dots\sum_{j_{r}=0}^{n_{r}-1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{k}}\log|f_{j_{1}\dots j_{r}}(\mathbf{s})|\,d\mathbf{s}
\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}\dots n_{r}}\mathsf{m}_{\mathbb{T}^{k}}(g)>-\infty,
where
g(\mathbf{s})=\prod_{j_{1}=0}^{n_{1}-1}\dots\prod_{j_{r}=0}^{n_{r}-1}f_{j_{1}\dots j_{r}}(\mathbf{s}).
Since for each i the product over j_{i} covers all the
n_{i}th roots of unity, the coefficients of g are algebraic
integers fixed by all elements in the Galois group of the
field they generate, and so g\in\mathbb{Z}[(\mathbb{T}^{k})^{^}\,]. Hence
\mathsf{m}_{\mathbb{T}^{k}}(g)\geq 0 by [4, Lemma 3.7], showing that
\mathsf{m}(f)\geq 0.
∎
We repeatedly use the following observation.
Lemma 2.2.
Let H be a closed subgroup of G. Then
\lambda(G)\leq\lambda(G/H).
Proof.
Let \pi\colon G\to G/H be the quotient map. If
f\in\mathbb{Z}[(G/H)^{^}\,], then f\circ\pi\in\mathbb{Z}[\widehat{G}] and
\mathsf{m}_{G}(f\circ\pi)=\mathsf{m}_{G/H}(f). The conclusion then follows from
the definition of \lambda.
∎
3. Connected groups
In this section we prove that all nontrivial connected groups
have the same Lehmer constant.
Lemma 3.1.
\lambda(\mathbb{T}^{k})=\lambda(\mathbb{T}) for all k\geq 1.
Proof.
Since \mathbb{T}\cong\mathbb{T}^{k}/\mathbb{T}^{k-1}, Lemma 2.2
shows that \lambda(\mathbb{T}^{k})\leq\lambda(\mathbb{T}).
To prove the reverse inequality, fix \epsilon>0. Choose
f\in\mathbb{Z}[(\mathbb{T}^{k})^{^}\,] with
0<\mathsf{m}_{\mathbb{T}^{k}}(f)<\lambda(\mathbb{T}^{k})+\epsilon.
For \mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}^{k} define
f_{\mathbf{r}}(s)=f(r_{1}s,\dots,r_{k}s), so that
f_{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbb{Z}[\widehat{\mathbb{T}}]. By [5] we can
find \mathbf{r} so that
|\mathsf{m}_{\mathbb{T}}(f_{\mathbf{r}})-\mathsf{m}_{\mathbb{T}^{k}}(f)|<\min\{\mathsf{m}_{\mathbb{T}^{k}}(f),\epsilon\}.
It follows that \lambda(\mathbb{T})\leq\lambda(\mathbb{T}^{k})+2\epsilon. Since
\epsilon>0 was arbitrary, we obtain that
\lambda(\mathbb{T})\leq\lambda(\mathbb{T}^{k}).
∎
Theorem 3.2.
If G is a nontrivial connected compact abelian group, then
\lambda(G)=\lambda(\mathbb{T}).
Proof.
Since G is nontrivial and connected, it has a quotient
isomorphic to  \mathbb{T}, and hence \lambda(G)\leq\lambda(\mathbb{T}) by Lemma
2.2.
To prove the reverse inequality, observe that \widehat{G} is
torsion-free. Let f\in\mathbb{Z}[\widehat{G}] with \mathsf{m}_{G}(f)>0. The
subgroup \Delta of \widehat{G} generated by the characters in
f is therefore isomorphic to \mathbb{Z}^{k} for some k\geq 1, and
so \widehat{\Delta}=G/\Delta^{\perp}\cong\mathbb{T}^{k}. As in the
proof of Lemma 2.1, f induces
\widetilde{f}\in\mathbb{Z}[\Delta] with
\mathsf{m}_{\widehat{\Delta}}(\widetilde{f})=\mathsf{m}_{G}(f). By Lemma
3.1, we have that
\mathsf{m}_{G}(f)=\mathsf{m}_{\widehat{\Delta}}(\widetilde{f})\geq\lambda(\mathbb{T}^{k})=\lambda(\mathbb{T}),
proving that \lambda(G)\geq\lambda(\mathbb{T}).
∎
4. Finite groups
In this section we consider the problem of computing the Lehmer
constant of some finite abelian groups.
Trivially \lambda(\mathbb{Z}_{\!/1})=\log 2.
Define characters \chi_{k} on \mathbb{Z}_{\!/n} by \chi_{k}(j)=e^{2\pi ijk/n}. Thus the character group is
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}_{\!/n}}=\{\chi_{0},\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{n-1}\}.
Example 4.1.
Let G=\mathbb{Z}_{\!/2}. For f=a\chi_{0}+b\chi_{1} we have that
\mathsf{m}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\log|a+b|+\log|a-b|\bigr)=\frac{1}{2}\log|a^{2}-b^{2}|.
It is easy to check that the smallest value of |a^{2}-b^{2}|
greater than 1 is 3, so that
\displaystyle\lambda(\mathbb{Z}_{\!/2})=\frac{1}{2}\log 3.
Example 4.2.
Let G=\mathbb{Z}_{\!/3}. A simple calculation shows that
\mathsf{m}(a\chi_{0}+b\chi_{1}+c\chi_{2})=\frac{1}{3}\log|a^{3}+b^{3}+c^{3}-3abc|.
Choosing a=b=1 and c=0 gives a value of 2 for the
expression inside the absolute value, which is clearly the
smallest possible value greater than  1. Hence
\displaystyle\lambda(\mathbb{Z}_{\!/3})=\frac{1}{3}\log 2.
Example 4.3.
Let G=\mathbb{Z}_{\!/4}. Then
\mathsf{m}(a\chi_{0}+b\chi_{1}+c\chi_{2}+d\chi_{3})=\frac{1}{4}\log\bigl|(a+b+c+d)(a-b+c-d)[(a-c)^{2}+(b-d)^{2}]\bigr|.
Putting a=b=c=1 and d=0 gives \displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\log 3.
Suppose the product inside the absolute value were \pm 2. The
the factorization would be either 2\cdot 1\cdot 1,
1\cdot 2\cdot 1, or 1\cdot 1\cdot 2. The first two
factorization cannot occur since the difference of the first
factors must be even. Therefore |a+c|\leq 2 and
(a-c)^{2}+(b-d)^{2}=2. A straightforward search rules out all
possibilities to attain \pm 2. Hence
\displaystyle\lambda(\mathbb{Z}_{\!/4})=\frac{1}{4}\log 3.
There is a simple upper bound for the Lehmer constant of a finite
group.
Lemma 4.4.
Let F be a finite abelian group with cardinality
|F|\geq 3. Then
(4.1)
\lambda(F)\leq\frac{1}{|F|}\log\bigl(|F|-1\bigr).
Proof.
Let \chi_{0}\in\widehat{F} be the trivial character, and
\delta_{0} be the unit mass at 0\in F. Then
g=\sum_{\chi\in\widehat{F}}\chi=|F|\delta_{0}.
Put f=g-\chi_{0}, so that
f(x)=\begin{cases}|F|-1&\text{if $x=0$},\\
-1&\text{if $x\neq 0$}.\end{cases}
Then \displaystyle\mathsf{m}_{F}(f)=\frac{1}{|F|}\log\bigl(|F|-1\bigr)>0 since |F|\geq 3,
proving (4.1).
∎
To estimate the Lehmer constant of a cyclic group, it is
convenient to introduce the following arithmetical function.
Definition 4.5.
For an integer n\geq 2 let \rho(n) denote the smallest prime
number that does not divide n.
Note that \rho(n) grows quite slowly with n.
Theorem 4.6.
For all integers n\geq 2 we have that
\lambda(\mathbb{Z}_{\!/n})\leq\frac{1}{n}\log\rho(n).
Proof.
Let p=\rho(n), and \Phi_{p} be the pth cyclotomic
polynomial. Put f_{p}(j)=\Phi_{p}(e^{2\pi ij/n}), so that
f_{p}\in\mathbb{Z}[\widehat{\mathbb{Z}_{\!/n}}]. Then
\mathsf{m}(f_{p})=\frac{1}{n}\log|R\bigl(\Phi_{p}(x),x^{n}-1\bigr)|,
where R(\cdot,\cdot) denotes the resultant. Since p does
not divide n, if follows from [1] that
R\bigl(\Phi_{p}(x),x^{n}-1\bigr)=p=\rho(n).
∎
Corollary 4.7.
If n is odd then \displaystyle\lambda(\mathbb{Z}_{\!/n})=\frac{1}{n}\log 2.
Proof.
Since \rho(n)=2 we see that
\displaystyle\lambda(\mathbb{Z}_{\!/n})\leq\frac{1}{n}\log 2 by Theorem
4.6. An argument similar to that in the
proof of Lemma 2.1 shows the reverse inequality.
∎
Note that the upper bound in Theorem 4.6 is
actually the correct value of \lambda(\mathbb{Z}_{\!/n}) for n=2,3, and 4
computed in Examples 4.1, 4.2, and
4.3 above. Further computational evidence suggests the
following.
Conjecture 4.8.
\displaystyle\lambda(\mathbb{Z}_{\!/n})=\frac{1}{n}\log\rho(n) for all n\geq 2.
Next, consider groups of the form
\mathbb{Z}_{\!/2}\oplus\dots\mathbb{Z}_{\!/2}=\mathbb{Z}_{\!/2}^{n}.
Example 4.9.
Let G=\mathbb{Z}_{\!/2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{\!/2}. The characters
\chi_{ij}(x,y)=\chi_{i}(x)\chi_{j}(y) form the dual group
\widehat{G}=\{\chi_{00},\chi_{01},\chi_{10},\chi_{11}\}. Then
\mathsf{m}\Bigl(\sum_{i,j=0}^{1}a_{ij}\chi_{ij}\Bigr)=\frac{1}{4}\log\Bigl|\prod_{r,s=0}^{1}\Bigl(\sum_{i,j=0}^{1}(-1)^{ir+js}a_{ij}\Bigr)\Bigr|.
The expression inside the absolute value is 3 when
a_{00}=a_{01}=a_{10}=1 and a_{11}=0. It cannot attain the
value \pm 2, since if so 2 would factor into a product of
four integers whose pairwise differences are all even. Hence
\displaystyle\lambda(\mathbb{Z}_{\!/2}^{2})=\frac{1}{4}\log 3.
Further numerical work suggests the following.
Conjecture 4.10.
\displaystyle\lambda(\mathbb{Z}_{\!/2}^{n})=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\log(2^{n}-1) for all n\geq 2.
Note that this quantity is the upper bound for \lambda(F) in Lemma
4.4 when F=\mathbb{Z}_{\!/2}^{n}.
5. Mixed groups
Here we consider groups G that have a nontrivial connected
component G^{0} of the identity.
Let \Gamma=\widehat{G}, and denote the torsion subgroup of \Gamma
by \mathsf{t}(\Gamma). The totally disconnected group G/G^{0} then
has dual group \mathsf{t}(\Gamma).
Theorem 5.1.
If G/G^{0} is infinite, then \lambda(G)=0.
Proof.
Let \Delta be a finite subgroup of \mathsf{t}(\Gamma), and
suppose that |\Delta|\geq 3. Then by
Lemma 2.2,
(5.1)
\lambda(G)\leq\lambda(G/\Delta^{\perp})=\lambda(\widehat{\Delta})\leq\frac{1}{|\Delta|}\log\bigl(|\Delta|-1\bigr).
If \mathsf{t}(\Gamma) is infinite, we can then find arbitrarily
large finite subgroups \Delta, so that the bound in
(5.1) can be made arbitrarily small.
∎
Remark 5.2.
If G^{0} is nontrivial, then any character of infinite order
that is nontrivial on G^{0} gives a quotient map of G to
\mathbb{T}, and so \lambda(G)\leq\lambda(\mathbb{T}) by Lemma
2.2. If \lambda(\mathbb{T}) were 0, this would show
that only finite groups have positive Lehmer constant.
Example 5.3.
What is the value of
\lambda(\mathbb{T}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{\!/2})? If \lambda(\mathbb{T}) were 0, the previous
remark shows that \lambda(\mathbb{T}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{\!/2}) would also be 0.
On the other hand, if \lambda(\mathbb{T})>0, it seems likely that
\lambda(\mathbb{T}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{\!/2})<\lambda(\mathbb{T}). Some evidence for this comes
from the following example, kindly communicated to us by Peter
Borwein.
Let
f(x)=x^{12}-x^{11}+x^{10}-x^{9}-x^{6}-x^{3}+x^{2}-x+1
and
g(x)=x^{8}-x^{7}+x^{6}-x^{5}+x^{4}.
Then f+g is cyclotomic, so that \mathsf{m}(f+g)=0, while
\mathsf{m}(f-g)\approx 0.30082.
Define h on \mathbb{T}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{\!/2} by
h(s,j)=f(e^{2\pi is})+(-1)^{j}g(e^{2\pi is}).
Then
\displaystyle\mathsf{m}_{\mathbb{T}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{\!/2}}(h)
\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigl[\mathsf{m}(f+g)+\mathsf{m}(f-g)\bigr]
\displaystyle\approx 0.15041<0.16235=\mathsf{m}_{\mathbb{T}}(f_{L}),
where f_{L} is the Lehmer polynomial in
(1.1). Thus \lambda(\mathbb{T}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{\!/2}) is
strictly less than the best current value for \lambda(\mathbb{T}).
