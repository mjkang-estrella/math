         Abstract. Enumerative Combinatorics focuses on the exact and asymptotic
         counting of combinatorial objects. It is strongly connected to several disci-
         plines, including probability theory, geometry, algebra, statistical physics,
         computer science, and biology. Within combinatorics, it has strong links
         with structural graph theory and with algebraic combinatorics, in particular
         through combinatorial representation theory, polytopes and matroids.
             Like other mathematical ﬁelds, Enumerative Combinatorics strongly ben-
         eﬁts from interactions with other ﬁelds. This workshop brought together
         experts in Enumerative Combinatorics and related ﬁelds in order to discuss
         current fundamental problems in the area and to promote cooperation and
         interaction among the participants. This was the fourth of a series of MFO
         workshops in Enumerative Combinatorics (after 2014, 2018, and 2022).

                        Introduction by the Organizers

In March 2014, and again in May 2018, Mireille Bousquet-Mélou (Bordeaux),
Michael Drmota (Vienna), Christian Krattenthaler (Vienna), and Marc Noy
(Barcelona) organized two workshops on Enumerative Combinatorics at the Math-
ematische Forschungsinstitut at Oberwolfach, starting a successful series of work-
shops on this topic. The series continued with another workshop in December
2022. The present workshop took place in January 2026, and is the fourth one in
this series. It was organized by a renewed team consisting of Guillaume Chapuy
(Paris), Sergi Elizalde (Dartmouth) and Ilse Fischer (Vienna).

   Four years after the previous meeting, it was time to meet again and assess
the developments which had taken place since then; in particular, to examine the
impact of the previous workshops, and to witness and discuss the recent trends
and most exciting developments in Enumerative Combinatorics.
   Even though, as expected, there was some intersection between the participants
of this workshop and those of previous editions, there was a signiﬁcant number
(40%) of new participants, many of whom were younger researchers.
   The impact of the last workshops could be felt in several ways. The recogni-
tion by the MFO institute of enumerative combinatorics as a ﬁeld contributed to
create a feeling of community which is very valuable for researchers in the ﬁeld
all around the world. As expected, the presentations and discussions from the
previous workshops resulted in several new collaborations, and in several papers,
as could be witnessed on the arχiv.
This workshop took place in January 12–16, 2026. There were 45 on-site partici-
pants coming from various European countries, as well as from the US, Canada,
and Japan. The program consisted of 2 one-hour keynote lectures, accompanied by
22 shorter 45-minute presentations, and a special session consisting of 15-minute
talks by the four Oberwolfach Leibniz graduate fellows. There was also a par-
ticularly extensive and inspiring problem session (as is reported in detail below)
which gave rise to exciting discussions, and we are conﬁdent it will lead to new
collaborations and to new results in the forthcoming years. The two keynote lec-
tures were delivered by Mireille Bousquet-Mélou and Matjaž Konvalinka.
They provided overviews of recent exciting developments on cyclic sieving and a
generalized poset point of view on alternating sign matrices.
   As a whole, the lecturers in this workshop presented the state of the art in
various areas related to Enumerative Combinatorics, together with relevant new
results. The lectures and short talks ranged over a wide variety of topics including
classical enumerative problems, algebraic combinatorics, asymptotic and proba-
bilistic methods, and statistical physics. Special attention was paid to providing a
platform for younger researchers to introduce themselves and their results to the
community. This report contains extended abstracts of the talks, as well as the
statements of the problems that were posed during the problem session.
   The goal of the workshop was to bring together researchers from diﬀerent ﬁelds
with a common interest in enumeration, whether from an algebraic, analytic, prob-
abilistic, geometric or computational angle, in order to enhance collaboration and
new research projects. The organizers believe this goal was amply achieved, as
demonstrated by the strong interaction among the participants and the lively dis-
cussions in and outside the lecture room during the whole week.
   The working environment was again perfect, and on behalf of all participants,
the organizers would like to thank the staﬀ and the director of the Mathematis-
ches Forschungsinstitut Oberwolfach for providing such a stimulating and inspiring
atmosphere.

   A combinatorial formula for interpolation Macdonald polynomials
                           Houcine Ben Dali
                    (joint work with Lauren Williams)

Knop [3] and Sahi [4] introduced a remarkable family of inhomogeneous symmet-
ric polynomials, deﬁned via vanishing conditions, whose top homogeneous parts
are exactly the Macdonald polynomials. Like the Macdonald polynomials, these
interpolation Macdonald polynomials are closely connected to the Hecke algebra,
and admit non-symmetric versions, which generalize the non-symmetric Macdon-
ald polynomials. I present in this talk a combinatorial formula for interpolation
Macdonald polynomials in terms of signed multiline queues. This formula gener-
alizes the combinatorial formula for Macdonald polynomials in terms of multiline
queues given by Corteel–Mandelshtam–Williams. This talk is based on [1].

        deﬁned as the product of the weights of all the signed balls.
      • We assign to each non-trivial pairing in Q a weight in Q(q, t). This weight

        or in a classical layer. The pairing weight wtpair (Q) of Q is then deﬁned
        as the product of the weights of all pairings.
   Since interpolation ASEP polynomials do not satisfy all the qKZ equations used
to study the homogeneous polynomials (Theorem 3), we adopt a diﬀerent proof
strategy to establish Theorem 4. We ﬁrst prove a factorization formula for the
polynomials fµ∗ indexed by packed compositions, and then, in a second step, use
the action of the Hecke algebra on these polynomials to pass from the packed case
to the general one.
   Theorem 4 gives a combinatorial proof of the integrality result of Knop and
Sahi for the integral symmetric interpolation polynomials Jµ∗ (see [1, Section 6.2]).

         Cyclic sieving phenomena for trees and tree-rooted maps
                          Mireille Bousquet-Mélou
                    (joint work with Christian Krattenthaler)



                    1. Introduction, and first example
The cyclic sieving phenomenon was introduced by Reiner, Stanton and White
about 20 years ago [5]. It deals with the enumerative properties of a set of com-
binatorial objects on which a cyclic group acts.
Deﬁnition 1. Given a set S of combinatorial objects, an action on S of a cyclic
group G, and a polynomial P (q) in q with non-negative integer coeﬃcients, we say
that the triple (S, G, P ) exhibits the cyclic sieving phenomenon (CSP) if for any
g ∈ G the number of elements of S ﬁxed by g equals P (ω) for any primitive d-th
root of unity ω, where d is the order of g in G.
   Reiner, Stanton and White already presented numerous instances of the cyclic
sieving phenomenon in their original article [5]. Since then, the discovery of cyclic
sieving phenomena has almost become an industry, with numerous more having
been discovered and proved since the appearance of [5]. A survey of cyclic sieving
phenomena published until 2011 can be found in [6]. It goes without saying that
even more have been identiﬁed since then.
   In this talk, based on [2], we show that rooted plane trees (also called ordered
trees) are dream objects for cyclic sieving. Our basic example deals with the set
T(n) of plane trees with n edges, rooted at a corner, as shown in Figure 1. It is
well-known that their number is the n-th Catalan number:

   This result is not new, but appears to be (mostly) known in terms of other

both on the set of trees under consideration, and on the rotation itself. Moreover,
one of the variants implies that a CSP phenomenon holds for tree-rooted maps, a
family of decorated plane graphs introduced by Mullin in 1967 [4]. Our proofs use
direct enumeration, and evaluations of various q-analogues at roots of unity.

              The slice decomposition of planar hypermaps
                            Jérémie Bouttier
   (joint work with Marie Albenque, Bertrand Eynard and Thomas Lejeune)

Planar maps are connected graphs drawn on the sphere, considered up to defor-
mation. They consist of vertices, edges, and faces. Planar hypermaps are planar
maps whose faces are properly bicolored. We consider the problem of counting
planar hypermaps with controlled face degrees and colors. This problem is inti-
mately connected with the so-called two-matrix model in theoretical physics, and
the Ising model on random maps. In this talk we develop a bijective approach
to this enumerative problem by extending the method of slice decomposition (al-
ready presented in a previous Oberwolfach workshop in the case of ordinary maps).
Considering hypermaps adds a new twist to the story, since we deal with orien-
tations (induced canonically by the colors), adding new interesting ingredients to
our combinatorial arguments.

     Between weak and Bruhat, the middle order on permutations
                           Mathilde Bouvel
           (joint work with Luca Ferrari and Bridget E. Tenner)

In this contribution, we are interested in partial orders that can be deﬁned on the

             Toric Promotion with Reﬂections and Refractions
                                Colin Defant
              (joint work with Ashleigh Adams and Jessica Striker)

Inspired by recent work on refraction billiards in dynamics, we introduce a notion
of refraction for combinatorial billiards. This allows us to deﬁne a generalization of
toric promotion that we call toric promotion with reﬂections and refractions, which
is a dynamical system deﬁned via a graph G whose edges are partitioned into a

refract through each toric hyperplane in a toric arrangement. We give a simple

when G is a forest. We also completely describe the orbit sizes when G is a cycle

promotion (i.e., when there are no refraction edges). When G is a cycle of even size

Canon permutations are a class of multiset permutations that exhibit rich com-
binatorial structure. For the multiset M = {1m , 2m , . . . , nm } with m = 2, this
class consists of exactly the multiset permutations that avoid the patterns 1221
and 2112. For general m, a canon permutation is a shuﬄe of m copies of a per-

[n] := {1, 2, . . . , n} is identical for all j ∈ [m]. For instance, 13123424 is a canon

                    On the coeﬃcients of Chow polynomials
                               Elena Hoster
The Chow polynomial of a matroid is the Hilbert series of its Chow ring. This
notion extends to posets, where, for a large class of examples, the resulting poly-
nomial exhibits classical combinatorial properties like positivity, palindromicity,
unimodality, and γ-positivity [5]. Further behaviors of the coeﬃcients, such as
log-concavity and real-rootedness, are conjectured and have been veriﬁed for cer-

recursive deﬁnition introduced in [5]. The second expresses the Chow polynomial
in its γ-expansion [8]. Using this latter formula, we derive explicit combinatorial
interpretations for the Chow polynomials of uniform matroids [6]. In particular,
their coeﬃcients are maximal among all matroids of ﬁxed rank and size [4]. This
Enumerative Combinatorics                                                                    115

“maximality” therefore motivates the search for analogous combinatorial interpre-
tations for other classes of posets and matroids.

                             Greta Panova
         (joint work with A. H. Morales, L. Petrov, D. Yeliussizov)

In his 2018 paper “Some Schubert shenanigans” Richard Stanley asked for the

      Log-concavity and -convexity of permutation class sizes via
                          distributive lattices
                            Bruce E. Sagan
                     (joint work with Jinting Liang)

Let L be a distributive lattice. It follows easily from the FKG inequality that if
I and J are lower order ideals in L then |I| · |J| ≤ |L| · |I ∩ J|, where vertical
bars denote cardinality. The inequality is reversed if I is a lower order ideal and
J is an upper order ideal. These inequalities can be used to combinatorially prove
log-concavity and log-convexity results for various sequences including binomial
coeﬃcients, Catalan numbers, Fibonacci numbers, Stirling numbers of the second
kind, order polynomials, and more.

                            Dynamics on webs
                              Jessica Striker
 (joint work with Ashleigh Adams, Chris Fraser, Christian Gaetz, Jesse Kim,
 Rebecca Patrias, Oliver Pechenik, Stephan Pfannerer, Joshua P. Swanson and
                                 Haihan Wu)

In recent work with Gaetz, Pechenik, Pfannerer and Swanson [5], we found a
rotation-invariant sl4 web basis comprised of certain hourglass plabic graphs, re-
solving a 30-year-old problem of Kuperberg [8]. The combinatorial heart of this
work is an equivariant bijection between 4-row rectangular tableaux under promo-
tion and move equivalence classes of sl4 webs under rotation, which yields a new
proof of the cyclic sieving phenomenon for these tableaux.
Theorem 1 ([5]). The collection of web invariants of contracted, fully-reduced,
top 4-hourglass plabic graphs with 4d boundary vertices is a rotation-invariant sl4
web basis. Furthermore, promotion on rectangular 4 row ﬂuctuating tableaux cor-
responds to rotation of web equivalence classes, and the tuple of trip permutations
of the web equals the tuple of promotion permutations of the tableau.




   Keys to the proof include the deﬁnition and detailed study of promotion permu-
tations for tableaux [3], multiple trip permutations for hourglass plabic graphs [5],
and benzene and square moves on webs, which naturally relate to plane partitions
and alternating sign matrices.
   In this talk, we discuss this theorem and subsequent work involving these dy-
namical tools. With Gaetz, Swanson, and Wu [6], we deﬁne and study simplicial
complexes called pockets which give geometric realizations of move equivalence
classes and relate to their lattice structures.
Deﬁnition 2 ([6]). The pocket of a move-class C of fully reduced 4-hourglass
plabic graphs is the simplicial complex P (C) obtained by gluing together the dual
diskoids D(G) for G ∈ C by attaching octahedra between the portions involved
in a square move, hexagonal bipyramids between portions involved in a benzene
move, and tetrahedra between portions involved in an IH move.
Theorem 3 ([6]). The pocket P (C) of a move-class C
    • is a ﬂag simplicial complex that is contractible, but not always homeomor-
      phic to a ball, and

           Reachable endpoint sets and algebraic enumeration of
                           nondeterministic walks
                             Michael Wallner
         (joint work with Mohamed Lamine Lamali and Élie de Panaﬁeu)

Motivated by questions from networks with protocol encapsulation/decapsulation,
we introduce nondeterministic walks (N-walks): one-dimensional lattice walks in
which each step is a ﬁnite set of increments, and all compatible classical trajec-
tories are explored in parallel. The full version of this work is available at [3], a
preliminary conference version at [2].
   The natural analogue of an endpoint is the set of reachable endpoints after
n steps. Tracking this reachable set (which behaves analogously to a state in a
deterministic automaton) leads to a tractable enumerative framework and reveals
strong algebraic structure. Given a set S of integers, called the steps, a walk is a
sequence v = (v1 , . . . , vn ) of steps vi ∈ S. Its length
                                                      Pn is the number n of its steps,
and its endpoint is equal to the sum of the steps i=1 vi . As illustrated in Figure 1,
a walk can be visualized by its geometric realization. Starting from the origin 0,
the steps are added one by one to the previous endpoint. This gives a sequence
                                                                        Pj
(yj )0≤j≤n of ordinates of the walk at discrete time steps yj := y0 + i=1 vi . We
distinguish the following types of walks:
130                                                     Oberwolfach Report 2/2026

      • A bridge is a walk with endpoint yn = 0.
      • A meander is a walk where all points have non-negative ordinate, i.e.,
         yj ≥ 0 for all j = 0, . . . , n.
      • An excursion is a meander with endpoint yn = 0.
    We introduce a new variant of walks, called nondeterministic walks, or N-walks.
In our context, this word does not mean “random”. Instead it is understood in the
same sense as for automata and Turing machines. A process is nondeterministic
if several branches are explored in parallel, and the process is said to end in an
accepting state if one of those branches ends in an accepting state. Let us now
give a precise deﬁnition of these walks.
Deﬁnition 1 (Nondeterministic walks). An N-step is a nonempty set of integers.
Given an N-step set S, an N-walk w is a sequence of N-steps. Its length |w| is
equal to the number of its N-steps.
  As for classical walks we distinguish diﬀerent types.
Deﬁnition 2 (Types of N-walks). An N-walk w = (w1 , . . . , wn ) and a classical
walk v = (v1 , . . . , vn ) are compatible if they have the same length, and for each
i, the i-th step is included in the i-th N-step, i.e., vi ∈ wi . An N-bridge (resp.,
N-meander, N-excursion) is an N-walk compatible with at least one bridge (resp.,
meander, excursion). Thus, N-excursions are particular cases of N-meanders.
  The endpoints of classical walks are central to the analysis. We deﬁne their
nondeterministic analogues: reachable points.
Deﬁnition 3 (Reachable points). The reachable points of a general N-walk are
the endpoints of all walks compatible with it. For N-meanders, the reachable
points are deﬁned as the set of endpoints of compatible meanders. In particular,
all reachable endpoints of an N-meander are non-negative.

          On the Schubert Support of Grothendieck Polynomials
                              Anna Weigandt
                  (joint work with Judy (Hsin-Hui) Chiang)

Lascoux and Schützenberger [1, 2] introduced Grothendieck polynomials as poly-
nomial representatives of the classes of structure sheaves of Schubert varieties in
the K-theory of the complete ﬂag variety. These polynomials extend the notion of
Schubert polynomials from cohomology to K-theory, encoding richer combinato-
rial and geometric information. There has been growing interest in understanding
the Newton polytope and monomial support of Grothendieck polynomials. Specif-
ically, Monical, Tokcan, and Yong [4] conjectured that Grothendieck polynomials
have the saturated Newton polytope (SNP) property, while Mészáros, Setiabrata,
and St. Dizier [3] formulated several related conjectures concerning the divisibility
poset of their monomial supports. Their ﬁrst conjecture states that the maximal
elements of the divisibility poset are the monomials with the maximum possible

                                      1. Definitions
Partitions of sets are classical combinatorial objects. Formulated in terms of the
Boolean lattice Bn of subsets of [n] := {1, . . . , n}, a partition is a collection of
elements B1 , . . . , Bk of the Boolean lattice which satisﬁes two properties:
       • Bi 6= ∅, i = 1, . . . , k,
       • B1 ∪ · · · ∪ Bk = [n],
       • |B1 | + · · · + |Bk | = n.

In terms of the lattice Bn this can be translated using the dictionary:
       • ∅ = 0̂Bn is the unique minimal element of Bn ,
       • B1 ∪ · · · ∪ Br = B1 ∨ · · · ∨ Br , where ∨ is the join operation in Bn ,
       • [n] = 1̂Bn is the unique maximal element of Bn ,
       • |Bi | = rk(Bi ) and n = rk(Bn ), where rk is the rank function of Bn .
   In recent work [2, 4, 5] the authors have studied generalizations to arbitrary
lattices (and beyond) of the procedure by which set partitions arise from the
Boolean lattice. All new results described here are contained in these papers.
   There the authors exhibited various approaches, all using the dictionary above.
In its simplest form this generalization starts with a graded lattice L with unique
minimal element 0̂L , unique maximal element 1̂L , join operation ∨ and rank func-
tion rk. It deﬁnes a decomposition of L as a set {x1 , . . . , xr } ⊆ L such that
       • xi 6= 0̂L , i = 1, . . . , k,
       • 1̂L = x1 ∨ · · · ∨ xr and
       • rk(x1 ) + · · · + rk(xr ) = rk(L).
We set D(L) as the set of all decompositions of L ordered by reﬁnement with an
additional least element 0̂ element added. Note that for set partitions there is
already a unique minimal set partition 1| · · · |n but this is not the case in general
and therefore also in the case of L = Bn we add an additional minimal element 0̂.
   Starting from D(L) we deﬁne ordered decompositions, partial decompositions
and ordered partial decompositions. A partial decomposition of L is a non-empty
subset of a decomposition. We write PD(L) for the set of all partial decompositions
again ordered by reﬁnement and an additional unique minimal element 0̂ added.
An ordered partial decomposition is a tuple (x1 , x2 , . . . , xr ) without repetition
such that {x1 , . . . , xr } ∈ PD(L). We write OD(L) (resp. OPD(L)) for the poset
of all ordered decompositions (resp. ordered partial decompositions) ordered by
order-preserving reﬁnement and with an additional minimal element 0̂.

Weak order and the noncrossing partition lattice. Let’s start again. As-

Objection: If we ﬂip the noncrossing partition lattice onto its side, then
cover relations would become horizontal. Good point. We actually cut the
noncrossing partition lattice into pieces, and then move those pieces around. More
precisely, we can think of each maximal chain in the noncrossing partition lattice
as a factorization of c, and hence a tuple of reﬂections. For each element of w, we
associate the set of maximal chains in the noncrossing partition lattice such that
the product of the corresponding reﬂections increases in Bruhat order from w. We
call these chains concordant with w. This maintains the integrity of the covers—
the “horizontal” cover relations of NC become edges in the permutahedron that
traverse the interior of the polytope. Furthermore, there is a very nice geometric
condition to ﬁgure out which noncrossing chains Bruhat-increase from w.


Objection: How do you cover all n! vertices of weak order with the
Cat(n) vertices of the noncrossing partition lattice? What would you have
said if we had started with the Tamari lattice (or, more generally, a Cambrian
lattice)? These lattices are also “tall and slender,” but they now have Cat(n)
vertices. But now remember that Cambrian lattices are lattice quotients of weak
order. Up to taking inverses, we prove that elements w ∈ W belonging to the same
Cambrian congruence class have the same concordant chains, and so we reuse the
exact same piece of NC(W ). (If you restrict to just sortable elements, then you
get a (non-convex) triangulation of the associahedron.)


Does this have any geometric meaning? Let’s make everything geometric:
the weak order becomes the permutahedron and the noncrossing partition lattice
becomes its order complex. Then the simplices corresponding to the concordant
chains triangulate the permutahedron.

from quiver representation theory. We show that our triangulations are regular by
deﬁning a height function by the “time” t at which a well-chosen “laser” intersects
the noncrossing shards in the hyperplane arrangement.

homotopy between the Salvetti complex and the Bessis–Brady–Watt complex; this
problem remains open. We got stuck because of the duplications of the spheres
coming from non-singleton Cambrian classes, likely due to the fact that our tri-
angulation uses Bruhat order and is a “cheap approximation” of the interaction
between the Artin and dual positive monoids.

          2. Inequalities for skew indecomposable permutations
                          (problem of Miklós Bóna)
Deﬁnitions: Let q = q1 q2 · · · qk be a permutation satisfying q1 < qk , and let
k > 2. Let Avn (q) denote the number of permutations of length n that avoid
q and let Avn,1 (q) denote the number of skew indecomposable permutations of
length n that avoid q. Recall that a permutation is skew indecomposable if it is
not possible to cut it into two parts so that each entry before the cut is larger than
each entry after the cut.
The problem: Prove that for all n ≥ 1, we have the inequality
(1)                                 Avn (q) ≤ Avn+1,1 (q).
The easy cases: Note that (1) is trivially true if qk 6= k as we can postpend any
permutation counted by Avn (q) by the entry n + 1, yielding an injective proof of
the inequality. Similarly, if q1 6= 1, then we can take the reverse complement of
q and proceed as above. Finally, if q1 = 1 and qk = k, but there exists another
permutation q ′ that satisﬁes Avn (q) = Avn (q ′ ) for all n, in other words, when q is
Wilf-equivalent to another pattern q ′ that does not start with 1 or does not end
with k, but whose ﬁrst entry is smaller than the last, then (1) is true for q ′ , and
therefore, it is true for q. (It is easy to see this using generating functions.)
The diﬃcult cases: We do not have a proof of (1) in the cases not covered above.
That is, we do not have a proof when q1 = 1, qk = k, and q is not Wilf-equivalent
144                                                                         Oberwolfach Report 2/2026

to a pattern q ′ that does not have both of those properties. (Note the ﬁrst entry
of q ′ has to be smaller than the last.) So the shortest pattern for which we do not
have a proof of (1) is 1324.
Motivation: If (1) holds, then by the Supercritical Sequence Schema, the ordinary
generating function of the sequence Avn (q) is not rational.
Fun fact: For the pattern q = 12, inequality (1) does not hold, and the generating
function of the sequence Avn (q) is rational. In fact, it is 1/(1 − z). That is why
we need the condition k > 2.

   5. Words in random permutations (problem of Valentin Féray)
Setting. Consider two independent uniform random permutations σ, τ , and set
π = στ σ 2 τ 2 . As π is conjugacy invariant, we are interested in its cycle structure.
  The ﬁrst conjecture deals with the length L1 of the cycle containing 1 in π.
Conjecture 1. The random variable L1 /n converges in distribution to a uniform
random variable in [0, 1].
  We can also ask about the length Mk of the k-th largest cycle in π.
Conjecture 2. The random variable (M1 /n, M2 /n, . . . ) converges in distribution
to a the Poisson Dirichlet process of parameter 1.
   Informally, the two conjectures above mean that we expect π to behave asymp-
totically as a uniform random permutation. Indeed, Conjectures 1 and 2 are
well-known to hold if π is replaced by a uniform random permutation (in fact, in
this case, the distribution of L1 is uniform on {1, . . . , n}). A stronger conjecture
would be that the total variation distance dTV (π, Unif(Sn )) converges to 0. The
counterpart for short cycles follow from a result of Kammoun and Maida [7].
Theorem 3. If Ck is the number of k-cycles in π, then the Ck ’s converge jointly
in distribution to independent Poisson random variables of parameter 1/k.
Generalization to primitive words. Of course, one can ask the question for any
word w in the alphabet {σ, τ }, not only for στ σ 2 τ 2 as above (we could also admit
inverses, though we will not discuss this here). When σ and τ are independent
uniform random permutations, w can be interpreted as a random permutation.
We believe that Conjectures 1 and 2 hold for w as soon as w is primitive, i.e.,
cannot be written as a concatenation power of a shorter word. The theorem above
of Kammoun and Maida holds in this setting.

Words with few occurrences of σ.
      • If one of the letter, say σ, appears only once, then, by conjugacy invariance,
        w has the same distribution as στ k , which is uniformly distributed. Thus
        Conjectures 1 and 2 hold.
      • If one of the letter, say σ, is repeated only twice, then by conjugacy in-
        variance, w has the same distribution as στ k στ ℓ with k ≤ ℓ. Then we set
        ρ = στ k (trick suggested by Lucas Teyssier), and w has the same distribu-

        closed in total variation distance to the uniform distribution on Sn if ℓ − k
