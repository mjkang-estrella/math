                                                                                         Abstract
                                                    We determine weak k-metric dimensions for several families of Hamming graphs. For
                                                n ≥ 3, m > n, and 3 ≤ k ≤ 2n, a cyclic construction proves Conjecture 6.1 of Fernández,
                                                Klavžar, Kuziak, Muñoz-Márquez and Yero (2026) on Kn □Km . For these rectangular
                                                products, we also prove that wdim2 (Kn □Km ) = m exactly when m ≥ 2n − 2. For
                                                hypercubes Qd with d ≥ 2, we show that consecutive parameters 2s − 1 and 2s have
                                                identical weak resolving sets. Near the maximum parameter, a reduction to restricted-
                                                distance binary codes determines wdim2d −t (Qd ) for every feasible deficit 0 ≤ t ≤ 15. For
                                                L ≥ 1 and t ∈ {2L, 2L + 1}, we prove the stabilization formula wdim2d −t (Qd ) = 2d − L
                                                for d ≥ 2L + 1, and show that this threshold is sharp. For each fixed q ≥ 3 and deficit t,
                                                we also determine wdim2qd−1 −t (Kq□d ) in all sufficiently large dimensions, with an explicit
                                                sufficient condition.

                                           Keywords. weak k-metric dimension; Hamming graph; hypercube; restricted-distance code;
                                           Hadamard matrix.

for d ≥ 2 [8]. They conjectured an exact formula for rectangular products and asked for results
on higher-dimensional Hamming graphs. We address both questions through coordinate
counts. Farhan, Kuziak and Yero [7] have also studied weak k-metric dimension for the direct
product Kn × Kn , whose adjacency relation differs from the Cartesian products considered
here.
    For rectangular products, an incidence matrix records the selected landmarks. Bounds on
its column sums give the conjectured lower bound; a cyclic matrix attains it. More precisely,
for n ≥ 3, m > n, and 3 ≤ k ≤ 2n,
                                             (
                                               m⌈k/2⌉,        k even,
                         wdimk (Kn □Km ) =
                                               m⌈k/2⌉ − 1, k odd.

For k = 2, we determine the exact equality threshold m ≥ 2n − 2, refining the proposed
sufficient range m ≥ 2n.
    In higher dimensions we instead record the omitted vertices. For the hypercube Qd = K2□d ,
write N = 2d and k = N − t. The columns of the omitted-vertex matrix form a binary code
with two-sided distance restrictions. Once pairs at distance at least three have sufficient
slack, these restrictions determine the weak dimension exactly. This gives a partial-Hadamard
characterisation, the sharp stabilization theorem

                      wdim2d −t (Qd ) = 2d − ⌊t/2⌋   for   d ≥ 2⌊t/2⌋ + 1,

and explicit values through deficit 15. A parity argument shows that consecutive parameters
2s − 1 and 2s have identical weak resolving sets throughout the feasible range. The nonbinary
case has a different local obstruction: adjacent pairs bound the symbol frequencies in every
coordinate. A cyclic group action attains those bounds, proving eventually that

                  wdim2qd−1 −t (Kq□d ) = q d − q⌊t/2⌋ − (t mod 2),     q ≥ 3.

    Cáceres et al. [3] studied ordinary metric dimension in Cartesian products. In hypercubes,
Kelenč, Masa Toshi, Škrekovski and Yero [12] compared metric, edge metric and mixed metric
dimensions. At k = 1, resolving hypercubes is also closely related to separating systems
and coin-weighing [5, 15]. Our near-maximum parameter regime leads to binary codes with
prescribed sets of distances. Such restrictions occur in the narrow-distance bounds of Roth
and Seroussi [20] and the few-distance problems studied by Barg and Musin [2] and by Barg,
Glazyrin, Kao, Lai, Tseng and Yu [1]. Here the permitted distances are specified by the
deficit, and the code columns must also distinguish the omitted vertices.
    We use standard rank and coding bounds [16], the Singleton bound [21], Delsarte’s linear
programming method [4], and Hadamard constructions [23, 17, 11]. The additional capacity
arguments needed for the exact tables are given in full. Two capacities use finite exhaustive
proofs, specified in the appendix. We do not determine the remaining rectangular k = 2
values below the equality threshold, or the full feasible range of k in higher dimensions.

Same-row pairs. If x = (i, j) and y = (i, j ′ ) share a row, the row indicators in ∆w (x, y) cancel,

Non-aligned pairs. Let x = (i, j), y = (i′ , j ′ ) with i ̸= i′ , j ̸= j ′ . In the four layer sums
hj + hj ′ + gi + gi′ , each selected endpoint is counted twice, which matches its contribution

counted once, which also matches its contribution. The two cross vertices (i, j ′ ) and (i′ , j) are
counted twice but are equidistant from x and y. Subtracting those two excess contributions
gives

2.2.2    Cyclic construction
Lemma 2.3. Under the hypotheses of Theorem 2.1, there is a weak k-resolving set of size
m⌈k/2⌉ when k is even and of size m⌈k/2⌉ − 1 when k is odd.

Proof. Put r = ⌈k/2⌉; the assumption k ≤ 2n gives r ≤ n. Define

          S0 = {(i, j) ∈ Zn × {0, . . . , m − 1} : i − j   (mod n) ∈ {0, 1, . . . , r − 1}}.   (6)

Each column of S0 contains exactly r vertices. The rows j, j + 1, . . . , j + r − 1 are distinct
modulo n because r ≤ n. Among the first n columns, each row occurs exactly r times: row i
occurs in the columns j ≡ i − t (mod n) for t = 0, . . . , r − 1. These are r distinct residues
modulo n, each represented exactly once among columns 0, . . . , n − 1. Since m ≥ n + 1, every
row of S0 contains at least r vertices in total.
Even case, k = 2r. Take S = S0 . Every pair of column sums equals 2r = k and every pair
of row sums is at least 2r = k. Even k in the stated range has k ≥ 4, so (4) handles all
non-aligned pairs. Thus S is weak k-resolving with |S| = mr.

                                                 4
Odd case, k = 2r − 1 ≥ 3. Here r ≥ 2. Column n exists because m ≥ n + 1, and it repeats
the cyclic pattern of column 0, since {n + t mod n : 0 ≤ t < r} = {0, . . . , r − 1}; in particular
(0, n) ∈ S0 . Delete it:
                                       S = S0 \ {(0, n)}.                                       (7)
Column n now has size r − 1 and every other column has size r, so every pair of columns has
combined size at least 2r − 1 = k. Every row other than row 0 still has at least r vertices,
and row 0 retains its r vertices among the first n columns. The deleted vertex lies in column
n, outside those first n columns. Thus every pair of row sums is at least 2r = k + 1. For a
non-aligned pair, (3) yields

                      ∆S (x, y) ≥ (2r − 1) + 2r − 4 = 4r − 5 ≥ 2r − 1 = k,

since r ≥ 2. This includes k = 3. Thus S is weak k-resolving with |S| = mr − 1.

Proof of Theorem 2.1. Lemma 2.2 gives the stated lower bound in each parity case, and

3.2   Universal lower bound and distance two
Adjacent pairs give a universal lower bound. Pairs at distance two then translate the problem
into constraints on the columns of a binary matrix.
Proposition 3.3. For adjacent x, y one has ∆S (x, y) = |S|. Consequently wdimN −t (Qd ) ≥
N − t, and every candidate complement satisfies q ≤ t.
Proof. For adjacent vertices x, y, every vertex of the hypercube is one unit closer to one
endpoint than to the other. Each landmark therefore contributes one to ∆S (x, y), so
∆S (x, y) = |S|. If S is weak (N − t)-resolving, then |S| ≥ N − t, or equivalently q ≤ t.

Lemma 3.4. Let T = V (Qd ) \ S, list its q vertices as the rows of a binary matrix, and denote
its columns by c1 , . . . , cd . If S is weak (N − t)-resolving and L = ⌊t/2⌋, then
                              q − L ≤ dH (ca , cb ) ≤ L          (a ̸= b).                   (8)
Hence q ≤ 2L, and if q > L the columns are distinct.

receives contribution two from the complementary parity class. The two parity classes have
sizes dH (ca , cb ) and q − dH (ca , cb ). Each size must be at most L, which gives (8). Adding

Definition 3.5. For a set D of positive integers, let A(ℓ, D) be the largest size of a code in

its endpoints. Let Cd (t) be the largest q for which a binary q × d matrix exists with distinct
rows and with every pair of columns satisfying (8). For q > L, put γ(q, L) = A(q−1, [q−L, L]).
To normalize an omitted-vertex matrix, translate one row to zero and delete that row. Flipping
a column replaces its distance from another column by q − dH , so the symmetric interval
in (8) is preserved. Set γ(q, L) = ∞ for q ≤ L.

   The definition and Lemma 3.4 give

            Cd (t) = max{q ≤ 2L : γ(q, L) ≥ d and q distinct rows are possible}

and the lower bound wdimN −t (Qd ) ≥ N − Cd (t).
   The matching construction uses the full-cube sum for pairs at distance at least three.

Lemma 3.6. Suppose Fd (r) − rt ≥ N − t for every 3 ≤ r ≤ d. If a complement T is counted

Proof. Pairs at distance one are safe by Proposition 3.3 because |T | ≤ t. Lemma 3.4 gives the

contribution is at least Fd (r) − rt ≥ N − t.

Proof. Lemma 3.4 gives the lower bound N − Cd (t). Lemma 3.6 gives a weak resolving set of

Proof. Theorem 3.2 gives Fd (r) ≥ 3N/2 for r ≥ 3. Thus the hypothesis of Theorem 3.7

fails, pairs at larger distances can force a smaller complement, so we return to the definition.

3.3   Partial Hadamard matrices
The extremal case of the lower bound has a standard interpretation in terms of orthogonal
sign vectors.

Theorem 3.9. Let t > 0 be even and suppose Theorem 3.7 applies. Then wdimN −t (Qd ) =
N − t if and only if a t × d sign matrix with pairwise orthogonal columns and distinct rows
exists. In particular:


                                               8
1. d ≤ t, by linear independence;

2. if t ≡ 2 (mod 4) and d ≥ 3, equality is impossible: three pairwise distances would equal
   the odd number t/2, but the sum of the three pairwise Hamming distances among any three
   binary vectors is even;

Proof. Assume first that equality holds. The omitted set has size q = t = 2L, so Lemma 3.4
forces dH (ca , cb ) = L for every two columns. After mapping 0 to +1 and 1 to −1, the columns
are pairwise orthogonal sign vectors. The rows remain distinct. Conversely, such a sign
matrix gives a complement counted by Cd (t), and Theorem 3.7 gives equality.
    Pairwise orthogonal columns are linearly independent, so d ≤ t. If t ≡ 2 (mod 4) and
d ≥ 3, three binary columns would have pairwise distance t/2, an odd number. This is
impossible because the sum of the three pairwise Hamming distances among three binary
vectors is even.

   For an odd deficit t, separately, the distance-two bound gives q ≤ 2⌊t/2⌋ = t − 1, so
wdimN −t (Qd ) = N − t is impossible.
   The relaxation satisfies Cd (2L) = Cd (2L + 1), since both deficits share the same load limit
L. The corresponding equality of weak dimensions holds without any tail hypothesis, as we
now prove.


4    Paired deficits and sharp stabilization
The paired values follow from a parity property of the resolving sets themselves.

Theorem 4.1 (Parity pairing). Let d ≥ 2 and 1 ≤ s ≤ 2d−1 . A subset of V (Qd ) is weak
(2s − 1)-resolving if and only if it is weak 2s-resolving. Consequently,

                                 wdim2s−1 (Qd ) = wdim2s (Qd ).

Proof. Suppose S is weak (2s − 1)-resolving. Choose two coordinates and consider the two
opposite pairs in a face on those coordinates. Each landmark contributes two to one of these
pairs and zero to the other. Both sums are even and at least 2s − 1, hence at least 2s. Their
sum is 2|S|, so |S| ≥ 2s.
    For a pair at odd distance, each landmark’s distance difference is an odd integer in absolute
value, and is therefore at least one. This is the bipartite parity observation of Peterin, Sedlar,
Škrekovski and Yero [19, Lemma 12]. Thus ∆S (x, y) ≥ |S| ≥ 2s. For a pair at even distance,
every summand is even, so ∆S (x, y) ≥ 2s − 1 implies ∆S (x, y) ≥ 2s. The reverse implication
is immediate.

    The matrix formulation then reduces the evaluation of these paired values to restricted-
distance codes.

Theorem 6.4. Fix q ≥ 3 and t ≥ 0. For every sufficiently large d, with κ = 2q d−1 > t,

                                    wdimκ−t (Kq□d ) = q d − Uq (t).

More explicitly, the formula holds whenever d ≥ 2, κ > t, and

                                     2(q − 2)q d−2 ≥ d Uq (t) − t.                                  (10)

Proof. Lemma 6.3 gives the lower bound. Identify the alphabet with Z/qZ. Simultaneously
adding one to all coordinates partitions the vertex set into q d−1 orbits of size q. Each orbit
contains every symbol once in every coordinate. Put h = ⌊t/2⌋ and take h complete orbits;
when t is odd, add one vertex from another orbit. Since t < 2q d−1 , such an unused orbit
exists. The resulting Uq (t) vertices are distinct and each column has an attaining frequency
profile. Thus every adjacent pair loses at most t.
    For a pair at distance r, divide Proposition 6.1 by q d . The result is E|Z1 + · · · + Zr |,
where independently Zi = 1, −1, 0 with probabilities 1/q, 1/q, (q − 2)/q. Conditional on a
partial sum s, adding one step changes the expected absolute value by 2/q if s = 0 and by
zero otherwise. Hence the full sums are nondecreasing in r, and for every r ≥ 2,

                               Fd,q (r) ≥ Fd,q (2) = κ + 2(q − 2)q d−2 .

Each omitted vertex removes at most r ≤ d. Under (10), every nonadjacent pair therefore
retains at least κ − t. The right side of (10) grows only linearly in d, while the left side grows
exponentially, proving the eventual assertion.


7    Further questions
The rectangular k = 2 values below the threshold m = 2n − 2 remain to be determined. For
hypercubes, Theorem 4.1 halves the number of distinct parameters, but does not determine the
values beyond the fixed-deficit range treated here. Their evaluation requires further restricted-
distance capacities and separating constructions. The binary stabilization threshold in
Theorem 4.3 is sharp; determining a sharp threshold for the nonbinary formula in Theorem 6.4
is another natural question.
