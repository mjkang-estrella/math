                                                      Abstract. Let x be a (non-empty) sequence of positive real numbers. Its achievement set x is the set of all
                                                      the possible sums of the elements of x. The cardinal function of x is the function f : A(x) → N ∪ {ω, c} that
                                                      for every x ∈ A(x) the value f (x) is equal to the number of ways x is represented as a sum of elements of x.
                                                          In this paper we consider possible ranges of cardinal functions of sequences x. We present some general




arXiv:2508.13016v1 [math.CA] 18 Aug 2025
                                                      constructions and several criteria that a set has to satisfy in order to be a range of a cardinal function.
                                                          We put special attention to the case of sets with maximal element equal to 6. In this case, in particular,
                                                      we obtained a full characterisation of sets that are ranges of cardinal functions of interval-filling sequences.

  Let us recall some known facts about cardinal functions. Most of the results come from the paper [8]. For
example, it was proved that F ⊆ C, namely for any finite achievement set its counterpart Cantor set with the
same range of cardinal function was constructed. Moreover if M ∈ F, then M ∪ {c} ∈ I.
  Let us denote
                                                R := I ∪ Cv ∪ C ∪ F,
that is, the set of all the possible ranges of cardinal functions of summable sequences. It is easy to see that
R ⊊ P({1, 2, . . . , n, . . . , ω, c}) \ {∅}, where P(X) is the power set of X. Indeed, for any summable sequences
(xn ) the maximal and minimal elements of A(xn ) are obtained uniquely. Thus for each M ∈ R we have 1 ∈ M .
The more complicated question is whether the equality
                                              
                                         R = {1} ∪ M M ∈ P({2, . . . , n, . . . , ω, c})
holds, since we do not know any nontrivial method of showing that a given set is not in R.
    It is also known that {1} ∈ C \ (I ∪ Cv ), that is, the case when each point in achievement set is obtained
by a unique representation is reserved for Cantor sets (and for F in P       the more trivial sense of finite subsums).

Indeed, note that the mapping {0, 1}N → A((xn )) defined as (εn ) 7→ n=1 εn xn is continuous on the compact
domain. If rng(f ) denotes the range of f , then the equality rng (f ) = {1} means that f is a bijection and
hence a homeomorphism. Thus the image A((xn )) is homeomorphic to the Cantor space {0, 1}N .
    There are still some unsolved problems in the area. For instance, the question: does ω ∈ M imply that
c ∈ M ? remains unanswered. The method of proving that some set belongs to R are not well developed. From
the few of them the most important is that if M ∈ R and L ∈ F, then M · L ∈ R. In particular,          Sp this implies

for each p ∈ N. The problem whether the conditions M ∈ R and L ∈ R imply that M · L ∈ R remains open.
    Probably the simplest set about which we still do not know if it belongs to R is {1, 4}. Note that the sets
{1, 2}, {1, 3}, {1, 2, 3}, {1, 2, 3, 4}, {1, 2, 4}, {1, 3, 4} are all known to be the elements of R. Another known
fact is that if M ∈ R, then M ∪ {2} ∈ R.
    The paper is organised as follows. In Section 2 we present some basic properties. Section 3 is dedicated
to answering the questions does {1, 4} ∈ I1 ? and more general does {1, m} ∈ I1 for m ∈ N? by using the
concept of lockers [5]. In Section 4 we discuss how the upper bound of cardinal function is connected with
Kakeya conditions [11]. Then in Section 5 we introduce a useful tool called Cardinality Invariant Sets which
help us to recognize unbounded ranges. Section 6 is dedicated to distinguish I from I1 by showing that I \ I1
is nonempty. In Section 7 we obtain more results about I1 , which allow us to complete the characterization of
ranges bounded by 6. Sections 8 and 9 are dedicated to studying F, C and Cv respectively. In Section 10 we
briefly summarize the results by presenting a table showing which sets M with max M ≤ 6 belong to sets I,
Cv , C, F and R. Filling this table is the reason, why we write down many corollaries specifically for sets M
with max M ≤ 6.

                  4. Kakeya conditions and ranges for interval-filling sequences
  The conditions in which we use the inequalities between the terms xn and the tails rn are sometimes called
Kakeya conditions, see [11]. They originate from the following celebrated result due to Kakeya.

Theorem 4.1 (Kakeya’s theorem, [10]). Let x = (xn ). The inequality xn ≤ rn holds for almost every n if and
only if A(x) is a finite union of compact intervals.
  If the inequality xn > rn holds for almost every n then A(x) is a Cantor set.

   Basically, no other information about achievement set type can be deduced from only Kakeya conditions
[11].
   We have seen in Lemma 3.7 that the condition rk > xk for some k implies max rng(f ) ≥ 3. In this section
we study the connection between the sizes and structures of the sets {k | rk > xk } and rng(f ).
   In the paper [8, Theorem 3.8] it is proved for interval-filling sequences that if there are finitely many strict
inequalities rn > xn , then the cardinal function f is bounded, and if rn > xn for infinitely many n, then f
attains ω or c. It is worth to mention that in general it is still not known if unboundedness of the range M
can occur with {ω, c} ∩ M = ∅ (see [8, Problems 2.12 and 2.13]). If the answer was positive, then there would
exist ni → ∞ such that ni ∈ M for every i ∈ N.
