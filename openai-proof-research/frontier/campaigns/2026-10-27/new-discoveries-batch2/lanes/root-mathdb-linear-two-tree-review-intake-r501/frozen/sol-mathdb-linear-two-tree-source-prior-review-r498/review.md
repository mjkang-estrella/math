## MATH: independent structural bridge

All graphs below are finite simple 2-trees. For n >= 4, degree-two vertices form an independent set: start with the diamond after the first insertion; on every further insertion old degree-two vertices that survive remain independent, while the new degree-two vertex has only neighbors whose degrees are now at least three. Let c be their count. Adding a vertex on edge xy changes c to c+1-t, where t is the number of degree-two endpoints of xy. After the first insertion t is at most one, so c never decreases from two. If the final graph has exactly two degree-two vertices, every subsequent insertion must therefore have t=1. Conversely such insertions preserve c=2.

This proves equivalence with the source's inductive condition for n >= 4 without assuming any clique-incidence theorem. Now maintain the adjacency graph of triangles, adjacent when sharing an edge. For the diamond it is a path, with its two terminal triangles containing the two degree-two vertices. An edge containing a degree-two vertex belongs to exactly one triangle, namely that vertex and its two neighbors. Attaching on it appends a triangle to that terminal triangle; it cannot attach to an interior triangle. The new triangle has no other shared edge with an old triangle, since its third vertex is new. The old terminal triangle loses its degree-two vertex, and the new triangle acquires the new degree-two vertex. Induction gives a triangle path, with each vertex appearing in an interval of that path (the running-intersection property).

Orient this path from either terminal triangle. Each next triangle meets the union of the earlier triangles in precisely its shared edge: an old third vertex would violate that interval property. If consecutive shared edges were equal, three triangles would use that edge, contrary to the path invariant. Hence, numbering triangles t1,...,tm, each new triangle adds a new w_j on e_(j-1), and its next shared edge has the form e_j={w_j,kappa_j}, kappa_j in e_(j-1). This is exactly the brief's normal form. Its two ears are a=t1\e1 and w_m. Conversely this form has precisely those two degree-two vertices for m >= 2: the initial shared-edge endpoints both gain a neighbor at t2, and every nonfinal new vertex participates in the next shared edge and gains another neighbor.

The **full triangle/edge incidence graph** is not literally a path (boundary-edge nodes give leaves). The path assertion must refer to triangle adjacency, or to incidence restricted to shared edges. For unrestricted 2-trees, even triangle adjacency need not be a tree: a book of three triangles on one common edge gives a clique of three triangle nodes and three degree-two tips. A central triangle with one triangle on each of its three sides also has three ears and a branching dual. Both are excluded by the count proof, not by assumed terminology. Two triangles sharing an edge are allowed; three on the same edge are excluded in this class. These examples address the incidence and branching objections directly.

## MATH: full cut and diameter audit

Use the brief's indices, m=n-2. m=1 was treated above; m=2 is the diamond with diameter 2, so a zero lower bound suffices. For m >= 3 define hubs kappa_2,...,kappa_(m-1). Triangles t1 and t2 contain kappa_2; each t_j, j >= 3, contains kappa_(j-1). Every vertex is therefore adjacent or equal to some hub. Consecutive hubs either agree or are joined by e_(j-1)={w_(j-1),kappa_(j-1)}; in the unequal case kappa_j=w_(j-1). If s counts those unequal transitions for 3 <= j <= m-1, their sequence is a walk with s actual edge steps. Any two vertices can reach this walk in at most one step apiece, giving diam(G) <= s+2. No maximum-degree bound is assumed; long fan runs have s=0 and diameter at most 2.

At a switch j put h=kappa_(j-1), and let x be the other endpoint of e_(j-2). Partition R={w_(j-1),w_j,...,w_m}, L=V\R. All triangles through t_(j-2) lie in L, and all from t_(j+1) onward lie in R, because e_j={w_j,w_(j-1)} and dropped vertices never return. The two straddling triangles give **exactly**

`delta(R) = {x w_(j-1), h w_(j-1), h w_j}`.

These are three distinct edges. Every graph edge was introduced in some triangle, so there are no omitted crossing edges. The first ear a is in L and last ear w_m is in R; deleting this boundary separates the same fixed terminals for every switch. Minimality of the cut is unnecessary.

If j' >= j+2, every edge of its cut has an endpoint w_(j'-1) or w_j' born strictly after step j. Every edge of the j cut has both endpoints already present at j. Thus the cuts are disjoint. Adjacent switches can share an edge, so one cannot take all switches. Selecting switches of ranks 1,3,5,... yields ceil(s/2) disjoint cuts, since their integer indices differ by at least two.

For clarity the electrical step can be checked without trusting a named black box. Orient each selected boundary from L to R. The unit electrical current from a to w_m has net current one across each boundary by summing Kirchhoff's law over L. Cauchy-Schwarz on its three unit resistors gives sum of squared currents on that boundary at least 1/3. Disjointness lets these energy contributions add. Total electrical energy equals effective resistance for unit current. Equivalently this is exactly finite Nash-Williams (2.13), with conductance c(e)=1. Consequently

`max_(u,v) r(u,v) >= r(a,w_m) >= ceil(s/2)/3 >= s/6 >= (diam(G)-2)/6`.

For s=0 the empty cut family gives zero, which suffices; the terminals remain distinct for m >= 2. Choosing D=6 max(M,0)+2 proves the uniform divergence assertion. This is a proof on the full intended class, not an inference from 8190+300 sampled chain constructions.
