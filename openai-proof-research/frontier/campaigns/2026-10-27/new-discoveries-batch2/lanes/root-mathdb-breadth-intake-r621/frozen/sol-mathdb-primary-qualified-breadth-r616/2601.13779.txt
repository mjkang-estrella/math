Abstract.
Extending some properties from the Euclidean plane to any normed plane, we show the validity of the Monma-Paterson-Suri-Yao algorithm for finding the maximum-weighted spanning tree of a set of n points, where the weight of an edge is the distance between the end points measured by the norm and there are not repeated distances. For strictly convex normed planes, we expose an strategy for moving slightly the points of the set in order to obtain distinct distances.
Key words and phrases: maximum spanning tree, normed plane

1. Introduction and notation
Let 𝔼2 be the Euclidean plane, and 𝕄2 be
a Minkowski plane, namely, ℝ2 endowed with a norm ∥⋅∥. For a set S of n points in 𝕄2,
let us consider the undirected graph G, where the vertices are the n points and the edges connect every pair of points and are weighted by the distance between them measured with the norm. A path in a graph is a sequence of edges such that all the vertices (and therefore also all the edges) are distinct.
A tree is a graph in which any two vertices are connected by exactly one path. A maximum spanning tree of G is a tree having the maximum total edge weight. An algorithm for computing an Euclidean maximum spanning tree is presented in [13]. The algorithm runs in O⁡(n​log⁡h) time and requires O⁡(n) space, where h denotes the number of vertices of the convex hull of S. Maximum spanning trees for L1 and L∞ metrics can be computed in O⁡(n) time and O⁡(n) space ([4]).
The notion of Voronoi diagram is widely used in many areas of science. Klein [7] introduces abstract Voronoi diagram as a unifying approach of Voronoi diagram centred on bisecting curves rather on distances. The algorithm in [13] is based on the construction of the furthest Voronoi diagram of S, that is a subdivision of the plane into regions such that the region corresponding to a point p∈S is the locus of points further away from p than from other point of S. The furthest Voronoi diagram can be computed using different algorithms in O⁡(n​log⁡n) time for the Euclidean and Lp (1≤p≤∞) norms ([1], [9], [15]). Mehlhorn et al. ([12]) provide a randomized algorithm to construct the furthest abstract Voronoi diagram in expected O⁡(n​log⁡n) time.
Following the skeleton of the paper of Monma et al. ([13]), we present in Section 3 the structure of the unique maximum spanning tree in 𝕄2 for n points that do not present equalities of distances, and prove in Section 4 that the approach in ([13]) for computing the maximum spanning tree of a set of n points works also in 𝕄2. The computation takes O⁡(n​log⁡n) time whether a subroutine for the construction of any furthest point Voronoi diagram taking O⁡(n​log⁡n) is available. This is the case of a wide range of norms, for instance Lp (1<p<∞), as we comment in the previous paragraph. In Section 5 we expose some applications to other geometrical computation problems.
We prove in last section that given n points in a strictly convex plane 𝕄2, and ϵ>0, each point can be moved a distance less than ϵ in such a way that all the distances between the n points are different. A similar strategy does not work whether the plane is not strictly convex.
2. Notation and preliminaries
Let S={p1,p2,…,pn} be a set of n points in a normed plane 𝕄2. From Section 2 to Section 5 we consider that all the distances between any two points are distinct. This condition guarantees that there exists a unique maximum spanning tree of S, denoted by 𝙼𝚇𝚂𝚃⁡(S) (see Figure 1). Given pi∈S, we call pk∈S the furthest neighbour of pi if ∥pi−pk∥=max{∥pi−pj∥:j=1,…,n}. We denote 𝙵𝙽𝙶⁡(S) the unique directed graph whose vertices are the points of S and whose directed edges connect each point of S to its furthest neighbour.
We call B⁡(x,r) the ball with center x∈𝕄2 and radius r>0. The convex hull of a set A is denoted by conv⁡(A), and its boundary by ∂conv⁡(A). Given x,y∈𝕄2, x​y¯ denotes the line segment connecting the two points, L⁡(x,y) denotes the line through x and y, and bis⁡(x,y) denotes the bisector for x and y, namely, the set of points equidistant from x and y. We say that x is Birkhoff orthogonal to y (denoted by x⟂y) if ‖x‖≤‖x+λ​y‖ for every λ∈ℝ. Birkhoff orthogonality extends the notion of Euclidean orthogonality to 𝕄2.
Lemma 2.1.
Let S be a set of n points in 𝕄2. Then,
(1)
Given p∈𝕄2, all the points where the furthest distance from S to p is reached lie on ∂conv⁡(S), and one of these points is a vertex of conv⁡(S).
(2)
If a,b,c,d are the cyclically ordered vertices of a convex quadrilateral, then ‖a−c‖+‖b−d‖≥max⁡{‖a−b‖+‖c−d‖,‖a−d‖+‖b−c‖}.
(3)
Let a,b,c be the vertices of a triangle. If x∈a​b¯ then ‖c−x‖≤max⁡{‖c−a‖,‖c−b‖}.
Proof.
(1) Let q∈S be a point such that ‖p−q‖=max⁡{‖p−x‖:x∈S}. Therefore, S⊆B⁡(p,‖p−q‖). Let y∈𝕄2 be such that q−p⟂y. The line q+λ​y (λ∈ℝ) supports B⁡(p,‖p−q‖) at q, conv⁡(S) and B⁡(p,‖p−q‖) are completely contained in one of the half-planes determined by the line, and any point x contained in the other half-plane verifies ‖p−x‖≥‖p−q‖. This implies that q belongs to ∂conv⁡(S). If q is not a vertex of conv⁡(S) the segment of ∂conv⁡(S) containing q belongs to the line q+λ​y, and the distance from p to the vertices of such a segment is not smaller that ‖p−q‖.
(2)-(3) See Proposition 7 and Lemma 5 in [11].
∎
The following statement is proved in [13] (Lemma 3).
Lemma 2.2.
Let G={V,E,w:E→ℝ+} be a weighted graph, where V is the set of vertices and E is the set of edges, and let V′⊆V be a subset of V. Among all edges of G having one endpoint in V′ and the other endpoint in V∖V′, let e be an edge of maximum weight. Then e occurs in some maximum spanning tree of G.
3. The geometric structure of maximum spanning trees
Figure 1. 𝙼𝚇𝚂𝚃⁡(S) of a set S of n points in 𝔼2.
Since the distances among any pair of points of S are distinct, every vertex of 𝙵𝙽𝙶⁡(S) has out-degree one (every vertex is the starting point of a unique directed edge) and every (directed) path is a sequence of edges of strictly increasing length until a cycle of two edges is reached. If x,x′ are the vertices of such a cycle, x​x′¯ is a spine, and this spine determines a component Cx​x′ of 𝙵𝙽𝙶⁡(S). A point only can be a vertex of one spine. We say that p∈S belongs to the cluster Cx (respectively, cluster Cx′), if there exists a directed path from p to x (respectively, from p to x′) with a even number of edges. Every point of S belongs to a unique cluster, and every component of 𝙵𝙽𝙶⁡(S) consists of exactly two clusters (each cluster is related to one vertex of the spine). Note that x∈Cx and x′∈Cx′.
Lemma 3.1.
For a set S={p1,p2,…,pn}, the following holds:
(1)
Only the leaves of 𝙵𝙽𝙶⁡(S) can be internal to conv⁡(S).
(2)
Both endpoints of a spine of 𝙵𝙽𝙶⁡(S) lies on ∂conv⁡(S) and each pair of spines of 𝙵𝙽𝙶⁡(S) properly intersects.
(3)
The endpoints of the spines of 𝙵𝙽𝙶⁡(S) can be labeled in cyclic order around ∂conv⁡(S) as p1,p2,…,pk,q1,q2,…,qk, where the spines are pi​qi¯, for i=1,2,…,k.
(4)
All the points of a cluster that lie on ∂conv⁡(S) are contiguous (i.e., if a,a¯ belong to a cluster and b,b¯ belong to a different cluster, then the clockwise order around ∂conv⁡(S) cannot be a​b​a¯​b¯).
Proof.
Lemma 2.1(1) proves (1). Every spine joints two furthest neighbours, that by Lemma 2.1(1) must be on the ∂conv⁡(S). Therefore (1) and Lemma 2.1(2) implies (2). (3) is a consequence of (2).
Let us assume that (4) fails. Then there exist two components Cx​x′ and Cy​y′, and four points a,b,a¯,b¯∈S clockwise situated on ∂conv⁡(S), where a,a¯∈Cx and b,b¯∈Cy.
Let us consider the sequence (a0:=a,a1,…) starting from a and where an+1 is the furthest neighbour of an. Such a sequence reaches x in an even number of steps, and after that, x and x′ alternate. Similarly we denote (a¯0:=a¯,a¯1,…), (b0:=b,b1,…), and (b¯0:=b¯,b¯1,…) the sequences of furthest points starting from a¯,b, and b¯, respectively, which reach x, y, and y, respectively, in an even number of steps each.
Since a0​a1¯, b0​b1¯, a¯0​a¯1¯ and b¯0​b¯1¯ are (directed) edges of 𝙵𝙽𝙶⁡(S), a0​a1​b1​b0 (in this cyclic order) is not a convex quadrilateral (Lemma 2.1(2); see Lemma 4 in [13] for details), and neither a0​a1​a¯1​a¯0, b0​b1​a¯1​a¯0, a¯0​a¯1​b¯1​b¯0, nor b0​b1​b¯1​b¯0. Therefore the clockwise cyclic order a0​b0​a¯0 implies the clockwise cyclic order a1​b1​a¯1 on ∂conv⁡(S), and the clockwise cyclic order a0​b0​a¯0​b¯0 implies the clockwise cyclic order a1​b1​a¯1​b¯1 on ∂conv⁡(S). Repeating the argument, finally we conclude the clockwise cyclic order an​bn​a¯n​b¯n on ∂conv⁡(S) for any n.
Since a,a¯∈Cx, for some step n the sequences an and a¯n coincide in x, and bn and b¯n coincide in y. Nevertheless these coincidences and the clockwise cyclic order an​bn​a¯n​b¯n are not compatible, and we get a contradiction.
∎
Lemma 3.2.
Let 𝕄2 be a normed plane and let p,q∈𝕄2 be two different points. There exist a simple curve B​i​(p,q)⊆bis⁡(p,q) through the midpoint of p and q, which is symmetric with respect to this midpoint, homeomorphic to ℝ, and such that for every r∈B​i​(p,q)
the curve B​i​(p,q) belongs to the double cone defined by the lines L⁡(r,p) and L⁡(r,q) that contains 12​(p+q). This curve separates the plane into two connected parts, P⁡(p,q) and P⁡(q,p), such that ‖p−y‖≤‖q−y‖ for y∈P⁡(p,q), and ‖q−y‖≤‖p−y‖ for y∈P⁡(q,p). Moreover, for any r∈bis⁡(p,q) the curve B​i​(p,q) can be constructed to pass through r. If 𝕄2 is strictly convex, every point of bis⁡(p,q) different from r is an interior point of the cone.
Proof.
The statements are proved in [11] for any strictly convex normed plane and in [6] for any normed plane, except what refers to the last sentence that we prove as follows.
We shall use the fact that for any x,y∈𝕄2 the function μ∈ℝ↦‖x+μ​y‖ is convex, and if 𝕄2 is strictly convex this function cannot be constant in any interval. Assume that there exists t∈bis⁡(p,q) that is not an interior point of the cone, i.e., there exists λ∈ℝ, 0≠λ≠1, such that
t=λ​p+(1−λ)​r and ‖t−p‖=‖t−q‖. The function μ∈ℝ↦f⁡(μ)=‖(1−λ)​(r−p)+μ⁡(p−q)‖ satisfies f⁡(0)=|1−λ|​‖r−p‖, f⁡(1)=‖t−q‖=|t−p|=|1−λ|​‖r−p‖ and f⁡(1−λ)=|1−λ|​‖r−q‖. Then, f⁡(0)=f⁡(1)=f⁡(1−λ), and therefore f⁡(μ) is constant in the interval that contains the point 0, 1, and 1−λ, which is absurd.
∎
Lemma 3.3.
Let Cx​x′, Cy​y′ and Cz​z′ be three components of 𝙵𝙽𝙶⁡(S), such that Cx,Cy,Cz,Cx′,Cy′,Cz′ is the clockwise order. Then the maximum distance from a point of Cx​x′ to Cy​y′∪Cz​z′ is realized between a point of Cx​x′ and a point of a non contiguous cluster (either between Cx and Cz∪Cy′ or between Cx′ and Cy∪Cz′).
Proof.
The structure of our proof is identical to that in [13] for the Euclidean case. For the clarity of the exposition, we present all the steps but emphasizing the arguments that are different in 𝔼2. Let us suppose that the lemma fails. Without loss of generality, let us assume that there exist a′∈Cx′ and c∈Cz such that ‖a′−c‖ is the maximum distance between points of Cx​x′ and Cy​y′∪Cz​z′. Let b​b′¯ the spine of Cy​y′, where b∈Cy and b′∈Cy′. Let c′∈Cz′ be the furthest neighbour of c in S and let a∈Cx be the furthest neighbour of a′. We hold the following:
(1)
‖a′−a‖>‖a′−c‖>‖a′−b‖.
(2)
‖a′−c‖>‖a−c‖,‖a′−c‖>‖b′−a‖.
(3)
‖b′−b‖>‖b′−a‖,‖b′−b‖>‖b′−c‖.
(4)
The points a,b,b′,c′ lie on ∂conv⁡(S) in this cyclic order. This follows by (1) in Lemma 3.1 and the cyclic order of Cx,Cy,Cz,Cx′,Cy′,Cz′.
(5)
c lies on ∂conv⁡(b,c,a′,b′,c′). This follows by observing that c is further from a′ than any other point in {b,c,a′,b′,c′} and Lemma 2.1(1).
(6)
b,c,b′,c′ are the cyclically ordered vertices of a convex quadrilateral. This follows by Lemma 2.1 because b,b′,c′ is the clockwise order on conv⁡(S) and c is further from c than any other point in {b,b′,c′}.
(7)
a′,a,b,c are cyclically ordered vertices of a convex quadrilateral. This follows because (4) and (6) guarantee that a,b,c,b′,c′ are the cyclically ordered vertices of a convex pentagon, and a,c,a′,c must be the cyclically ordered vertices of a convex quadrilateral by Lemma 2.1.
Let bis⁡(a,b) and bis⁡(b,c) be the bisectors defined by the points a,b and b,c, respectively. Let us choose a point O∈bis⁡(a,b)∩bis⁡(b,c). By Lemma 3.2, we can choose three simple curves B​i​(a,c), B​i​(b,c), and B​i​(a,b), each of them homeomorphic to ℝ, that satisfy the following conditions:
I
B​i​(a,c)⊆bis⁡(a,c) goes through the midpoint of a and c, is symmetric with respect to this midpoint, and belongs to the double cone with apex O and through a and c. This curve separates the plane into two connected parts P⁡(c,a) and P⁡(a,c) such that whenever y∈P⁡(c,a), then ‖c−y‖≤‖a−y‖, and whenever y∈P⁡(a,c), then ‖a−y‖≤‖c−y‖.
II
B​i​(b,c)⊆bis⁡(b,c) goes through the midpoint of b and c, is symmetric with respect to this midpoint, and belongs to the double cone with apex O and through b and c. This curve separates the plane into two connected parts P⁡(c,b) and P⁡(b,c) such that whenever y∈P⁡(c,b), then ‖c−y‖≤‖b−y‖, and whenever y∈P⁡(b,c), then ‖b−y‖≤‖c−y‖.
III
B​i​(a,b)⊆bis⁡(a,b) goes through the midpoint of a and b, is symmetric with respect to this midpoint, and belongs to the double cone with apex O and through a and b. This curve separates the plane into two connected parts P⁡(b,a) and P⁡(a,b) such that whenever y∈P⁡(b,a), then ‖b−y‖≤‖a−y‖, and whenever y∈P⁡(a,b), then ‖a−y‖≤‖b−y‖.
The following holds:
(A)
a′ lies in the triangle with vertices {a,O,c}. This follows from the fact a′∈P⁡(c,a)∩P⁡(b,c)∩P⁡(b,a) and the inclusion of B​i​(a,c) inside the double cone with apex O and through a and c, the inclusion of B​i​(b,c) inside the double cone with apex O and through b and c, and the inclusion of B​i​(a,b) inside the double cone with apex O and through a and b.
(B)
b′ lies in the ”wedge” that is the intersection of right half-plane of the directed half-line from a to O and the left-plane of the directed half-line from c to O. This follows from the fact b′∈P⁡(a,b)∩P⁡(c,b) and the inclusion of B​i​(a,b) inside the double cone with apex O and through a and b, and the inclusion of B​i​(b,c) inside the double cone with apex O and through b and c.
(A) and (B) imply that a′∈conv⁡({a,c,b′}), therefore ‖a−a′‖≤max⁡{‖a−c‖,‖a−b′‖} by Lemma 2.1(3), and we get a contradiction with (1) and (2).
∎
We prove now that all the edges of 𝙼𝚇𝚂𝚃⁡(S) that are not in 𝙵𝙽𝙶⁡(S) join adjacent components of 𝙵𝙽𝙶⁡(S), in the cyclic ordering of components.
Lemma 3.4.
Let Cxi−1​xi−1′, Cxi​xi′ and Cxi+1​xi+1′ be three components such that the clusters Cxi−1 and Cxi+1 are adjacent to Cxi and the cyclic ordering is Cxi−1,Cxi,Cxi+1. If there is an edge e∈𝙼𝚇𝚂𝚃⁡(S)∖𝙵𝙽𝙶⁡(S) with one endpoint in Cxi, then the other endpoint of e is either at Cxi−1′ or at Cxi+1′.
Proof.
Note that if e∈𝙼𝚇𝚂𝚃⁡(S)∖𝙵𝙽𝙶⁡(S) then e joins points from two different components. Suppose that e does not satisfy the condition of the lemma. Without of loss of generality we assume that e has an endpoint in Cy such that the cyclic ordering is Cxi−1,Cxi,Cxi+1,Cy. By Lemma 3.3, the length of e is not the maximum distance between points of Cxi​xi′ and Cxi−1​xi−1′∪Cy​y′. We obtain a spanning tree larger than 𝙼𝚇𝚂𝚃⁡(S) replacing e by the edge joining the pair of points where this maximum distance is reached, which is a contradiction.
∎
Lemma 3.5.
If e is an edge of 𝙼𝚇𝚂𝚃⁡(S), then at least one endpoint of e lies on ∂conv⁡(S).
Proof.
Let us assume that e=x​y¯ is an edge of 𝙼𝚇𝚂𝚃⁡(S) such that x,y are not on the boundary of conv⁡(S). Let A and B be the two components of 𝙼𝚇𝚂𝚃⁡(S) obtained when e is deleted, where x∈A and y∈B. We prove in the following that there exist u∈A and v∈B such that ‖u−v‖>‖x−y‖ and at least one of these points is on ∂conv⁡(S). As a consequence, a larger spanning tree could be obtained replacing e by u​v¯, which is a contradiction.

4. The Algorithm
Given a set of points S in 𝔼2 with distinct distances among any pair of points, the algorithm by Monma et al. for computing the 𝙼𝚇𝚃⁡(S) runs as follows (see Figure 3).
Algorithm 4.1
(1)
For each point p∈S compute its furthest neighbour in S.
(2)
Construct the directed graph 𝙵𝙽𝙶⁡(S), whose nodes are the points of S and
whose (directed) edges connect points of S to their respective furthest
neighbours.
(3)
Let Cx1​x1′,Cx2​x2′,…,Cxk​xk′, be the components of 𝙵𝙽𝙶⁡(S), where the clusters Cx1,Cx2,…,Cxk,Cx1′,Cx2′,…,Cxk′ are cyclically ordered around conv⁡(S).
(4)
For i=1,2,…,k, compute the edge (and its extreme points) with the following length (if i=1, then i−1:=k):
ei=max⁡{max{∥x−y∥/x∈Cxi,y∈Cxi−1′∩∂conv(S)},max{∥x−y∥/x∈Cxi−1,y∈Cxi′∩∂conv(S)},max{∥x−y∥/x∈Cxi′,y∈Cxi−1∩∂conv(S)},max{∥x−y∥/x∈Cxi−1′,y∈Cxi∩∂conv(S)},
Discard the edge with the smallest length. Let E be the set of remaining k−1 edges.
(5)
Output 𝙼𝚇𝚃⁡(S) as the union of the edges of 𝙵𝙽𝙶⁡(S) and E.
Theorem 4.1.
Let 𝕄2 be a normed plane. For a given set S of n points with distinct distances among pair of points, the 𝙼𝚇𝚂𝚃⁡(S) can be computed using Algorithm 4.
Proof.
Algorithm 4 locates all edges of 𝙵𝙽𝙶⁡(S) in Step 2. These edges are in 𝙼𝚇𝚂𝚃⁡(S) by Lemma 2.2. Algorithm 4 locates the remaining edges of 𝙼𝚇𝚂𝚃⁡(S) in Step 4, which must connect points of two different components of 𝙵𝙽𝙶⁡(S), based on Lemma 3.4 and on Lemma 3.5 .
∎
Figure 3. The construction of 𝙼𝚇𝚂𝚃⁡(S).
The Voronoi diagram and the furthest Voronoi diagram are some of most frequently investigated structures in Computational Geometry, and the second one is useful in order to locate the furthest neighbour of a point of S. Klein [7] introduces the abstract Voronoi diagram as a unifying approach to Voronoi diagrams based on the notion of bisecting curves instead of the concept of distance. In the abstract Voronoi diagram framework the bisecting curves should be homeomorphic to a line, and each pair of them may intersect in only a finite number of points. For each pair p and q of sites (points, lines, etc.) the existence of a bisector B​i​(p,q) dividing the plane into a p-region P⁡(p,q) and a q-region P⁡(q,p) is postulated. Given a set S of n sites, the nearest abstract Voronoi region of p∈S related to S is the set ∩q∈S,q≠pP(p,q), namely, the locus of points closer to p than to other point of S.
The abstract Voronoi diagram can be represented as a planar graph in a natural way ([7]) and can be computed in expected time O⁡(n​log⁡n) and expected space O⁡(n) ([8]).
Mehlhorn et al. [12] study the so-called furthest abstract Voronoi diagrams. The furthest abstract Voronoi region of p∈S related to S is the locus of points further away from p than from other point of S. They show
that furthest site abstract Voronoi diagrams are trees, have linear size, and that, given a set of n sites, the diagram can be computed by a randomized algorithm in expected time O⁡(n​log⁡n).
Furthest abstract Voronoi diagrams encompass a large number of specific diagrams, e.g., diagrams for point sites under
any Lp-norm (1<p<∞).
Corollary 4.2.
Let 𝕄2 be a normed plane and S be a set of n points with distinct distances among pair of points. The 𝙼𝚇𝚂𝚃⁡(S) can be computed:
(a)
By a randomized algorithm in expected time O⁡(n​log⁡n) if any furthest Voronoi diagram for a set of n points is a furthest abstract Voronoi diagram.
(b)
By a deterministic algorithm in expected time O⁡(n​log⁡n) if any furthest Voronoi diagram for a set of n points is a n-vertex planar subdivision that can be computed in O⁡(n​log⁡n) time by a subroutine.
Proof.
Let S be a set of n points in 𝕄2. Algorithm 4 is suitable and the time complexity depends on the construction of the furthest neighbour of a point in a set of points. Let us consider S1:=S∩∂conv⁡(S). If h is the number of points of S1, we can compute S1 in time O⁡(n​log⁡h).
For (a), the furthest site abstract Voronoi diagram of S1 can be computed by a randomized algorithm in expected time O⁡(h​log⁡h) ([12]);
the location of any point in the diagram can be effected in log⁡h time after O⁡(h​log⁡h) preprocessing time ([14]); the construction of the 𝙵𝙽𝙶⁡(S) therefore takes O⁡(n​log⁡h). Each of the steps (2) and (3) requires O⁡(n) time. Step (4) takes O⁡(ni​log⁡hi) time for every i∈{1,2,…,k}, where ni is the total number of points in Cxi∪Cxi−1∪Cxi′∪Cxi−1′ and hi is the number of these points
lying on ∂conv⁡(S). Thus Step 4 takes O⁡(n​log⁡h) time. The total cost is O⁡(n​log⁡n) time.
For (b), compute the furthest point Voronoi diagrams using the available subroutine instead of the randomized algorithm of Mehlhorn et al. for the furthest abstract Voronoi diagram.
∎
5. Some applications: the 2-clustering problem of minimizing the maximum diameter.
Given a set of points S in a metric space, the k-clustering problem of minimizing the maximum diameter asks how to split S into k sets minimizing the maximum diameter. The following algorithm computes the problem in any normed plane ([10]) for k=2.
Algorithm 5.2
Given a set S of n points in the plane:
(1)
Construct a maximum spanning tree for S. Sort the distances di between the points of the maximum spanning tree of S in increasing order.
(2)
Locate the minimum di that admits a stabbing line11
1
A stabbing line for a set of segments is a line that intersects every segment of the set.
for the maximum spanning tree by a binary search. Use the graph (S,Edi), where Edi is the set of edges at a distance greater than di, and use the algorithm by Edelsbrunner et al. ([3]) in order to find a stabbing line for Edi as a subroutine.
Theorem 5.1.
Let 𝕄2 be a normed plane and S be a set of n points with distinct distances among pair of points. If any furthest Voronoi diagram for a set of n points is a n-vertex planar subdivision that can be computed in O⁡(n​log⁡n) time by a subroutine, then the k-clustering problem of minimizing the maximum diameter of S can be solved
(1)
in O⁡(n​log⁡n) time for k=2,
(2)
in O⁡(n2​log2​n) time for k=3.
Proof.
Algorithm 5 works correctly for k=2 by Theorem 2 in [2] and Theorem 2.9 in [10]. The time cost of step (1) is O⁡(n​log⁡n) by Theorem 4.2; finding the stabbing line takes O⁡(m​log⁡m) times, where m is the number of edges ([3]); the partition into two subsets in step (3) can be obtained in O⁡(n) time easily once the stabbing line is known. Therefore, we hold (1).
Algorithm 3.3 in [10] (see also [5]) computes the solution for k=3 and the complexity depends on the complexity of case k=2. The proof of (2) is exactly the proof of Theorem 3.10 in [10] but considering that the 2-clustering problem can be computed in O⁡(n​log⁡n) by (1).
∎
6. Slight movements to get different distances
We present in Proposition 6.2 a descriptive step-by-step procedure for moving slightly the points in order to break the coincidences of distances and be able to apply Algorithm 4, although maybe the existence of such movement could be proved by a much simpler argument. At the end of this section we justify that a similar result for non strictly convex normed planes is not true.
A thorough view of the proof of Lemma 3.2 justifies the following.
Lemma 6.1.
Let 𝕄2 be a strictly convex normed plane. If y∈B​i​(p,q) for any pair of distinct points p and q, then ‖y+ϵ⁡(y−p)−q‖<‖y+ϵ⁡(y−p)−p‖ for any ϵ>0.
Proposition 6.2.
Let 𝕄2 be a strictly convex normed plane and S be a set of n points. For any ϵ>0 there is a point set S′ in a bijective correspondence with S such that:
(1)
Each point in S′ is within distance ϵ of its corresponding point in S.
(2)
The distances among points of S′ are all distinct.
Proof.
Let ϵ>0. Let us consider the different values {d1,d2,…,dt} of the distances among the points and
m=min{|dr−ds|>0:s,r∈{1,2,…,t}}.
Let {p1,p2,…,pk,pk+1,…,pn} be the points of S such that {p1,p2,…,pk} are the vertices of conv⁡(S) ordered in counterclockwise direction. All the points in S∩∂conv⁡(S) are considered vertices as well for our purpose. A side of conv⁡(S) is a segment jointing two adjacent vertices.
Step 1. Objective: To discard that the sides of conv⁡(S) are involved in any equality of distances.
If pk is involved in any equality of distances, take ϵk,1<min⁡{m,ϵ}
and replace pk by
pk′:=pk+ϵk,12​n​(pk−pk−1‖pk−pk−1‖).
Since ‖pk−1−pk′‖=‖pk−1−pk‖+ϵk,12​n, the distance ‖pk−1−pk′‖ is not involved in any equality of distances by Lemma 6.1 and the election of ϵk,1. Besides, if ‖pi−pk‖=‖pj−pk‖ for some pi,pj∈S (i≠j), the movement implies that ‖pi−pk′‖≠‖pj−pk′‖ by Lemma 3.2 and Lemma 6.1. For simplicity, let us denote pk:=pk′. Therefore, after this movement:
•
The distance ‖pk−1−pk‖ is not involved in any equality of distances.
•
‖pi−pk‖≠‖pj−pk‖ ∀pi,pj∈S,i≠j.
Reevaluate m and repeat the above argument: if pk−1 is involved in any equality of distances, take
ϵk−1,1<min⁡{m,ϵ}
and replace pk−1 by
pk−1′:=pk−1+ϵk−1,12​n​(pk−1−pk−2‖pk−1−pk−2‖).
Denoting pk−1:=pk−1′ and using again Lemma 3.2 and Lemma 6.1, we obtain the following:
•
The distance ‖pk−2−pk−1‖ is not involved in any equality of distances.
•
‖pi−pk−1‖≠‖pj−pk−1‖ ∀pi,pj∈S,i≠j.
After applying similar movements to the rest of vertices of conv⁡(S) as many times as necessary, we get that:
•
The equalities of distances do not affect to any side of conv⁡(S).
•
‖pi−ph‖≠‖pj−ph‖ ∀i,j∈{1,2,…,n},i≠j, and ∀h∈{1,…,k}.
Note that some distances like ‖pk−2−pk‖ may not have changed after this Step 1 (even when pk or pk−2 are moved) and ‖pk−2−pk‖ could be involved in any equality of distances.
Step 2. Objective: to discard that ‖ph−2−ph‖ is involved in any equality of distances for all h∈{1,…,k} (denote p0:=pk and p−1:=pk−1).
Reevaluate m. If pk−2−pk is involved in any equality of distances, take
ϵk,2<min⁡{m,ϵ} and replace pk by
pk′:=pk+ϵk,22​n​(pk−pk−2‖pk−pk−2‖).
This movement implies that ‖pk′−pk−2‖ is not involved in any equality of distance by Lemma 3.2 and Lemma 6.1. Let us denote pk:=pk′.
Applying similar movements to each vertex of conv⁡(S) as many times as necessary we achieve the objective of Step 2.
Step 3. Objective: to discard that ‖ph−3−ph‖ is involved in any equality of distances for all h∈{1,…,k} (denote p0:=pk, p−1:=pk−1, and p−2:=pk−2).
Reevaluate m. If pk−pk−3 is involved in any equality of distances, take
ϵk,3<min⁡{m,ϵ} and replace pk by pk′:=pk+ϵk,32​n​(pk−pk−3‖pk−pk−3‖). This movement implies that ‖pk′−pk−3‖ is not involved in any equality of distance by Lemma 3.2 and Lemma 6.1.
Applying similar movements to each vertex of conv⁡(S) as many times as necessary we achieve the objective of Step 3.
Applying in a similar way Step 4, Step 5, etc., we hold the following after a finite number of steps:
•
The distances among two vertices of conv⁡(S) are not involved in any equality of distances.
•
‖pi−ph‖≠‖pj−ph‖ ∀i,j∈{1,2,…,n},i≠j, and ∀h∈{1,…,k}.
Let us consider ph,pr,pl,u,q,p∈S that are ordered in clockwise direction, such that ph,pr,pl are vertices of conv⁡(S) and u,q,p are not. After the previous steps, other equalities implicating vertices
of conv⁡(S) could appear. Without loss of generality these equalities can be identified to one of the following:
•
‖p−pr‖=‖p−pl‖
•
‖p−pr‖=‖q−pl‖
•
‖p−pr‖=‖q−ph‖
•
‖p−pr‖=‖q−u‖.
If some of these equalities appears, reevaluate m and move the vertex pr to
pr+δ2​n​(pr−p‖pr−p‖),
where δ<min⁡{m,ϵ}. This movement breaks the coincidence,
and finally the vertices of conv⁡(S) are not involved in any equality of distances.
In order to break the equalities of distances implicating other points of S, consider S′=S∖{vertices of ​conv​(S)} and apply the above approach to S′ and conv⁡(S′), although evaluating m as the minimum difference among all distances among points of S.
The process ends after a finite number of steps when there is not any equality among distances. Each point has been moved at most n−1 times and at a distance less than ϵ at the end.
∎

Conjecture 6.3.
Let 𝕄2 be a strictly normed plane and S be a finite set of n points in 𝕄2. It is possible to denote {p1,p2,⋯,pn} to the points of S and order the distances among the points by some criterion <B such that
‖pi−pj‖<‖pk−pl‖ implies ∥pi−pj∥<B∥pk−pl∥, and
for any ϵ>0 there is a point set S′={p1′,p2′,…,pn′} in a bijective correspondence with S. This correspondence verifies the following:
(1)
Each point in S′ is within distance ϵ of its corresponding point in S,
(2)
The distances between points of S′ are all distinct,
(3)
‖pi′−pj′‖<‖pk′−pl′‖ if and only if ∥pi−pj∥<B∥pk−pl∥.
Neither the statement of Conjecture nor the one of Proposition 6.2 are true for non strictly convex normed planes because there are bisectors that contain non empty interior, thus a slight movement of the points do not assure to break the coincidences. For instance, p=(0,1) is an interior point of the bisector between u=(−12,0) and v=(12,0) with the maximum norm.
Let x,y∈𝕄2 be a base such that x is Birkhoff orthogonal to y and ‖x‖=‖y‖=1. The statement of Conjecture is proved in [13] for 𝔼2 ordering the points lexicographically by their coordinates respect to {x,y}, and using the lexicographically order (‖pi−pj‖,j,−i) as criterion <B for the distances.
Under these conditions, for every ϵ>0 there exist 0<ϵ1<ϵ2<⋯<ϵn<ϵ such that the movement from pi=(xi,yi) to pi′=(xi+ϵi,yi) guarantees that:
(1)
The point set S′={p1′,p2′,⋯,pn′} is in a bijective correspondence with S (such that pi→pi′).
(2)
Each point in S′ is within distance ϵ of its corresponding point in S.
(3)
The distances between points of S′ are all distinct.
(4)
The order <B preserves the corresponding original order of distances.
Nevertheless,
this approach does not work in every normed plane: in Figure 4 the normed sphere is a polygon with ten sides
such that ‖p1−p2‖=‖p1−p3‖=1 but the translation pi′=pi+ϵi (OPENi=1,2,3) does not break the equalities. Obviously, this example can be rounded in order to obtain a similar one but with a differentiable and strictly convex sphere. Our Proposition 6.2 presents a partial positive answer to Conjecture for strictly convex normed planes.
