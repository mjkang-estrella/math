     Let s be a positive integer. Our goal is to find all finite abelian groups G that contain a 2-subset A for
     which the undirected Cayley graph 0(G, A) has diameter at most s. We provide a complete answer
     when G is cyclic, and a conjecture and some partial answers when G is noncyclic.



                                                1. Introduction

Throughout this paper we let G be an additively written finite abelian group. When G is cyclic and of
order n, we identify it with Zn = Z/n Z; we consider 0, 1, . . . , n − 1 interchangeably as integers and as
elements of Zn . Below we use standard terminology and notation of additive combinatorics (see [Bajnok
2018]).
   Given a finite abelian group G and a subset A ⊆ G, the undirected Cayley graph 0(G, A) has its vertex
set formed by the elements of G, and has an edge between two vertices whenever their difference is an
element of A; that is, g1 ∈ G and g2 ∈ G are connected when g2 = g1 + a or g1 = g2 + a for some a ∈ A.
If A is a generating set of G, then 0(G, A) is connected; in particular, if A is an s-spanning set of G,
meaning that every element of G can be written as the signed sum of at most s (not-necessarily distinct)
elements of A, then 0(G, A) has diameter at most s.

   Let us now return to the undirected case. When equality occurs in Proposition 1.1, we call A a perfect
s-spanning set in G. Perfect spanning sets can be easily characterized for m = 1 and s = 1. Note that
a(1, s) = 2s + 1 and, clearly, for any a ∈ Z2s+1 with gcd(a, 2s + 1) = 1, the set {a} is a perfect s-spanning
set in Z2s+1 . Also, a(m, 1) = 2m + 1, and for any group G of order 2m + 1, every asymmetric subset of
size m (that is, a set containing exactly one of any nonzero element or its inverse) is a perfect 1-spanning
set in G.
   From (1) we get a(2, s) = 2s 2 + 2s + 1, and below we establish the following result.
Theorem 1.5. Let s be a positive integer. The largest possible order of a cyclic group G with a 2-subset
for which the diameter of 0(G, A) is at most s is given by 2s 2 + 2s + 1.
  For noncyclic groups the situation seems a bit more nuanced. We have the following conjecture.
Conjecture 1.6. Let s ≥ 3 be an integer.
  • If 2s + 1 is prime, then the largest noncyclic group with an s-spanning set of size 2 has order 2s 2 .

  • If 2s + 1 is composite and p is its smallest prime divisor, then the largest noncyclic group with an
    s-spanning set of size 2 has order 2s 2 + 2s − ( p 2 − 1)/2.
In particular, for any noncyclic group G with an s-spanning set of size 2, we have |G| ≤ 2s 2 + 2s − 4.
  (We add that the largest noncyclic group with a 2-spanning set of size 2 is Z3 × Z3 .)
  Below we prove that the quantities in Conjecture 1.6 are lower bounds.
Theorem 1.7. Let s be a positive integer.
  • The largest noncyclic group with an s-spanning set of size 2 has order at least 2s 2 .

  • If 2s + 1 is composite and p is any prime divisor of 2s + 1, then the largest noncyclic group with an
    s-spanning set of size 2 has order at least 2s 2 + 2s − ( p 2 − 1)/2.
   Establishing upper bounds appears to be more difficult, and we only have the partial results that we
summarize here. We first note that a noncyclic group that has a spanning set of size 2 must have rank 2
and thus is isomorphic to Zc × Zkc for some integers c ≥ 2 and k ≥ 1.
   We say that the group Zc × Zkc is s-regular for some positive integer s, if it possesses an s-spanning
set of the form {(1, u), (1, v)} for some u, v ∈ Zkc . While we have no precise count on the number of
s-regular groups, it appears that most groups of rank 2 that have an s-spanning set of size 2 are s-regular.
For example, for s = 10, of the 105 groups of rank 2 that have an s-spanning set of size 2, 103 are
s-regular. (The two exceptions are Z8 × Z16 and Z10 × Z20 .) In Theorem 3.1 below we prove, for every
odd prime c and positive integer k, that if Zc × Zkc has an s-spanning set at all, then it is s-regular.
   Our results for upper bounds can be summarized as follows.
Theorem 1.8. Let s be a positive integer, and let G = Zc × Zkc be an s-regular group for some integers
c ≥ 2 and k ≥ 1.
  • If c = 3 and s + 1 is divisible by 3, then |G| ≤ 2s 2 + 1.

  • If c is odd and s is divisible by c, then |G| ≤ 2s 2 + s, and if 2s + 1 is divisible by c, then |G| ≤
    2s 2 + 2s − (c2 − 1)/2.
  • In all other cases, |G| ≤ 2s 2 .

  For c = 2 we have exact results, even without the assumption that the group is s-regular:
Theorem 1.9. Let k and s be positive integers. The largest possible order of a group of the form Z2 × Z2k
that has an s-spanning set of size 2 is 2s 2 when s is even, and 2s 2 − 2 when s is odd.

   For m ≥ 3, the largest order of abelian groups with an m-subset for which the diameter of their Cayley
graphs is at most s is generally unknown, though some small cases for cyclic groups were displayed in
[Graham and Sloane 1980; Haanpää 2004; Hsu and Jia 1994].

Proposition 2.2. For all positive integers s and n with

                                              2 ≤ n ≤ 2s 2 + 2s + 1,

the set {s, s + 1} is an s-spanning set in Zn .
Proof. The fact that n cannot be larger than 2s 2 + 2s + 1 follows from Proposition 1.1. We verify that, for
every n in the range given above, we have ⟨{s, s + 1}⟩s = Zn .
  Considering the elements of

                             S = {λ1 s + λ2 (s + 1) | λ1 , λ2 ∈ Z, |λ1 | + |λ2 | ≤ s}

in Z (rather than Zn ), we see that they lie in the interval [−(s 2 + s), (s 2 + s)]. Since the index set

                                I (s) = {(λ1 , λ2 ) | λ1 , λ2 ∈ Z, |λ1 | + |λ2 | ≤ s}

contains exactly 2s 2 + 2s + 1 elements (see Lemma 2.1), it suffices to prove that no integer in the interval

  For that, suppose that
                                     λ1 s + λ2 (s + 1) = λ′1 s + λ′2 (s + 1)

for some (λ1 , λ2 ) ∈ I (s) and (λ′1 , λ′2 ) ∈ I (s); without loss of generality, we can assume that λ2 ≥ λ′2 . Our
equation implies that λ2 − λ′2 is divisible by s and is, therefore, equal to 0, s, or 2s.
   If λ2 − λ′2 = 2s, then λ2 = s and λ′2 = −s; since |λ1 | + |λ2 | ≤ s and |λ′1 | + |λ′2 | ≤ s, this can only
happen if λ1 = λ′1 = 0, but this case leads to a contradiction with our equation.

λ1 ≤ 0, λ′1 ≥ 0, λ2 ≥ 0, and λ′2 ≤ 0, and we see that

           |λ1 | + |λ2 | = −λ1 + λ2 = (s + 1 − λ′1 ) + (s + λ′2 ) = 2s + 1 − (|λ′1 | + |λ′2 |) ≥ s + 1,

but that is a contradiction.

Theorem 2.4. Let c and s be positive integers such that the remainder of s mod c is at least (c − 1)/2.
Let u = ⌊s/c⌋, v = ⌈s/c⌉, and k = 2uv. Then A = {(1, u), (1, v)} is an s-spanning set in G = Zc × Zck .
Proof. Let H = ⟨(1, u)⟩ be the subgroup of G generated by the element (1, u). Since H has order 2cv, it
has cu cosets in G. We claim that these cosets are λ2 · (1, v) + H , where λ2 = 0, 1, . . . , cu − 1. In order
to prove this, we need to show that these cu cosets are pairwise distinct.
   Suppose, then, that µ1 (1, v) and µ2 (1, v) are in the same coset of H for some 0 ≤ µ1 , µ2 ≤ cu − 1.
This implies that (µ2 − µ1 ) · (1, v) is an element of H , so there exists an integer µ for which
                                       (µ2 − µ1 ) · (1, v) = µ · (1, u).
From the first coordinates, this means that µ2 − µ1 − µ is divisible by c, and from the second coordinates
we see that (µ2 − µ1 )v − µu, which, since v = u + 1, equals (µ2 − µ1 − µ)u + (µ2 − µ1 ), is divisible
by ck and thus by cu. Combining these, we get that µ2 − µ1 must also be divisible by cu, but that is only
possible if µ1 = µ2 .
   Let g ∈ G be arbitrary. According to our claim, we have integers λ1 ∈ [−cv +1, cv] and λ2 ∈ [0, cu −1]
for which
                                         g = λ1 (1, u) + λ2 (1, v).

divisor of 2s + 1. Then the largest noncyclic group with an s-spanning set of size 2 has order at least
2s 2 + 2s − ( p 2 − 1)/2.

                                 3. s-regular groups and upper bounds

Recall that we say the group Zc × Zkc is s-regular for some positive integer s if it possesses an s-spanning
set of the form {(1, u), (1, v)} for some u, v ∈ Zkc . In this section we provide some upper bounds for the
largest possible order of an s-regular group.
Theorem 3.1. Let G = Zc × Zck for some integers c ≥ 2 and k ≥ 1, and let G have an s-spanning set for
some positive integer s. If c is an odd prime, then G is s-regular.

set A = {(a, u), (b, v)} of G. But since c is an odd prime, that would imply that u ≡ v ≡ 0 mod c, and
that is a contradiction with A being a spanning set.

Theorem 3.3. For s ≥ 3, every s-regular abelian group of rank 2 has order at most 2s 2 + 2s − 4, with
equality if , and only if , it is isomorphic to Z3 × Z(2s 2 +2s−4)/3 for some s ≡ 1 mod 3.
Proof. Suppose that G = Zc × Zck is an s-regular group of order at least 2s 2 + 2s − 4 for some integers
s ≥ 3, c ≥ 2, and k ≥ 1. Then, by Theorem 3.2, c is odd, so c ≥ 3. Recalling that |G| = c2 k, we have
     • s ≡ 0 mod c and 2s 2 + 2s − 4 ≤ c2 k ≤ 2s 2 + s, or

     • s ≡ (c − 1)/2 mod c and 2s 2 + 2s − 4 ≤ c2 k ≤ 2s 2 + 2s − (c2 − 1)/2.

Since the first possibility leads to no solutions, our claim is established.                                    □

                                            4. The groups Z2 × Z2k

In this section we provide some results for s-spanning sets in groups of the form Z2 × Z2k , including the
exact value of the largest possible order of such groups. Our general conjecture is as follows.
Conjecture 4.1. Let k and s be positive integers. The group Z2 × Z2k has an s-spanning set of size 2 if ,
and only if , one of the following holds:
     • s is odd and k ≤ (s 2 − 1)/2;

     • s is even and k ≤ (s 2 − s)/2;

     • s ≡ 0 mod 4, k is even, and (s 2 − s)/2 < k ≤ s 2 /2; or

     • s ≡ 2 mod 4, k ≡ 2 mod 4, and (s 2 − s)/2 < k ≤ s 2 /2.

Theorem 4.2. Let k and s be positive integers. If the group G = Z2 × Z2k has an s-spanning set of size 2,
then it has order at most 2s 2 .
Proof. If G is s-regular, that is, it has an s-spanning set of the form {(1, u), (1, v)} for some u, v ∈ Z2k , then
our claim follows from Theorem 3.2. Since no set of the form {(0, u), (0, v)} can be a spanning set, we
can suppose that G has an s-spanning set of the form A = {(0, u), (1, v)} for some u, v ∈ Z2k . Therefore,
each element in ⟨A⟩s has the form (λ2 , λ1 u + λ2 v) for some integers λ1 and λ2 with |λ1 | + |λ2 | ≤ s.
   Clearly, if u and v are both even, then A can only generate elements of G with an even second
component, and thus it cannot be a spanning set.
   Now if u and v are both odd, then λ1 u + λ2 v has the same parity as λ1 + λ2 . According to Lemma 2.1,
the parity of λ1 + λ2 differs from the parity of s exactly s 2 times (and agrees with it (s + 1)2 times) within
|λ1 | + |λ2 | ≤ s. This means that A cannot generate more than s 2 elements in Z2 × Z2k whose second
coordinate has different parity than s does, so if it is an s-spanning set, then we must have |G| ≤ 2s 2 .
   Next, if u is even and v is odd, then for each element of G that A generates, its two components will
have the same parity (namely, the parity of λ2 ). Therefore, A cannot be a spanning set.
   Finally, we prove that if A = {(0, u), (1, v)} is an s-spanning set of G with u odd and v even, then
A′ = {(1, u), (1, v)} is an s-spanning set as well, meaning that G is s-regular and thus has order at
most 2s 2 by Theorem 3.2.
   Let a ∈ Z2 and b ∈ Z2k . Since ⟨A⟩s = G, there are integers λ1 and λ2 with |λ1 | + |λ2 | ≤ s for which

                               λ1 (0, u) + λ2 (1, v) = (λ2 , λ1 u + λ2 v) = (a ′ , b),
             ON THE DIAMETER OF UNDIRECTED CAYLEY GRAPHS OF FINITE ABELIAN GROUPS                            11


where a ′ = a if b is even and a ′ = 1 + a when b is odd. Observe that when b is even, since u is odd and
v is even, λ1 must be even, and thus

            λ1 (1, u) + λ2 (1, v) = (λ1 + λ2 , λ1 u + λ2 v) = (λ2 , λ1 u + λ2 v) = (a ′ , b) = (a, b).

Similarly, when b is odd, λ1 must be odd, and

                                  λ1 (1, u) + λ2 (1, v) = (1 + a ′ , b) = (a, b).

Therefore, ⟨A′ ⟩s = G, as claimed, which completes our proof.                                                □
Theorem 4.3. Let k and s be positive integers with s odd. Then the group G = Z2 × Z2k has an s-spanning

Theorem 4.4. Let k and s be positive integers with s even. Then the group G = Z2 × Z2k has an s-spanning
set of size 2 if k ≤ (s 2 − s)/2.
Proof. Like in the proof of Theorem 4.3, we will prove that there are integers u and v for which the
s-span of A = {(1, u), (1, v)} contains the set Z2 × {0, 1, 2, . . . , k}. (Here too we consider the operation
in the first component in Z2 and in the second component in Z.)
   Since s − 1 is odd, from the proof of Theorem 4.3, with u = (s − 2)/2 and v = s/2, we see that ⟨A⟩s−1
contains the set Z2 × {0, 1, 2, . . . , k} when k ≤ ((s − 1)2 − 1)/2. Therefore, (1, v) + ⟨A⟩s−1 contains

follows for all k ≤ (s 2 − s)/2.                                                                            □
