Abstract:
Evolution algebras, introduced by J. P. Tian as an algebraic model of non-Mendelian
inheritance, are commutative algebras with a basis whose distinct elements multiply to zero.
The first part of the paper surveys the theory: low-dimensional classifications, structure
theory, nilpotency, automorphisms and derivations, directed graphs and random walks,
infinite-dimensional and dynamical aspects, and embedding theorems. The second part is a
unified list of fifty open questions and eleven new conjectures, each accompanied by the
available evidence; two of the conjectures and one of the questions are settled here. We also prove several new observations, most of them formally verified in the proof
assistant Lean (see Section 2.1.11): every finite group is the automorphism
group of a simple evolution algebra over every field, and |Aut⁡(A)|/dimA|\Aut(A)|/\dim A takes every
positive rational value on simple evolution algebras over every field;
infinitude of the automorphism group of
nilpotent algebras, the possible numbers of idempotents of real perfect evolution
algebras, a Galois-theoretic criterion for the existence of idempotents in dimension two,
the average number of idempotents over finite fields,
and a perfect evolution algebra whose automorphism group is a non-split extension.
Keywords:
evolution algebra, natural basis, directed graph, automorphism group scheme, nilpotent algebra, idempotent, open problems.

1.1 Introduction and basic notions
Let 𝕜\Bbbk be a field. A 𝕜\Bbbk-algebra AA is called an

Conversely, every square matrix is the structure matrix of an evolution algebra, so an

non-degenerate if ei2≠0e_{i}^{2}\neq 0 for every element of a natural basis; this does not

invariant of the algebra and its automorphisms are monomial. In general, a non-perfect
algebra may have many essentially different natural bases (for instance, the zero algebra
has all bases as natural bases), and the corresponding graphs need not be isomorphic.

Five basic results
The following statements are used throughout the paper; they are the backbone of the
finite-dimensional theory.
Theorem 4 (Elduque–Labra [48]).
If AA is a perfect finite-dimensional evolution algebra and {ei}\{e_{i}\}, {fi}\{f_{i}\} are natural
bases, then there are a permutation σ\sigma and scalars ti∈𝕜×t_{i}\in\Bbbk^{\times} with
fi=ti​eσ⁡(i)f_{i}=t_{i}e_{\sigma(i)}. In particular Γ⁡(A,B)\Gamma(A,B) does not depend on BB up to isomorphism,
every automorphism of AA is monomial, and Aut⁡(A)\Aut(A) is finite.
Theorem 5 ([36, 30, 48]).
For a finite-dimensional evolution algebra AA the following are equivalent: AA is nil
(with respect to the principal powers xk+1=xk​xx^{k+1}=x^{k}x; see Proposition 9
for plenary powers); AA
is right nilpotent; AA is nilpotent; for some (equivalently, every) natural basis BB the
graph Γ⁡(A,B)\Gamma(A,B) has no oriented cycles; in some natural basis the structure matrix is
strictly upper triangular. The nilpotency index of an nn-dimensional nilpotent evolution
algebra is at most 2n−1+12^{n-1}+1, and this bound is attained.
Theorem 6 (Cabrera Casado–Siles Molina–Velasco [22]).
A non-degenerate evolution algebra is the direct sum of irreducible evolution ideals,
uniquely up to order; the summands correspond to the connected components of the underlying
undirected graph of Γ⁡(A,B)\Gamma(A,B). A finite-dimensional evolution algebra is simple if and
only if it is perfect and Γ⁡(A,B)\Gamma(A,B) is strongly connected.
Theorem 7 (Elduque–Labra [50]).
For a perfect finite-dimensional evolution algebra the automorphism group scheme is an
extension of a constant group of permutations of the vertices of the graph by a finite
diagonalisable group scheme 𝐃\mathbf{D}, both determined by the graph; see (2)
below. In particular Der⁡(A)=0\Der(A)=0 if char⁡𝕜∈{0,2}\operatorname{char}\Bbbk\in\{0,2\}.
Theorem 8 (Costoya–Ligouras–Tocino–Viruel [43]).
For every field 𝕜\Bbbk and every finite group GG there are infinitely many pairwise
non-isomorphic perfect evolution 𝕜\Bbbk-algebras AA with Aut⁡(A)≅G\Aut(A)\cong G.
A short chronology
The history of the subject can be summarised as follows. The notion was introduced in 2006
[113] and systematised in Tian’s 2008 monograph [111]. Between 2010 and 2014 Rozikov, Omirov,
Ladra and their coauthors connected evolution algebras with population dynamics, Gibbs
measures, chains and dibaric algebras [106, 34, 79, 74, 80], and the first
structural results (nilpotency, derivations, classification in dimension two) appeared
[30, 31, 35, 36]. In 2015–2017 three foundational contributions changed
the landscape: the graph-theoretic viewpoint and the uniqueness of natural bases for perfect
algebras [48, 49], the structure theory in arbitrary dimension [22] and the
classification in dimension three [23]; at the same time nilpotent algebras were
classified in low dimension [64] and gonosomal and isotopic viewpoints were
introduced [115, 51]. From 2019 onwards automorphism group schemes
[50], universality [43, 109], random walks on graphs [25, 26],
Hilbert evolution algebras [118] and the systematic study of ideals through graphs
[19, 9] became central, and a historical survey appeared [38]. The
most recent work concerns non-degenerate algebras [29], counting over finite fields
[119], lattices of subalgebras [75, 77, 78, 76], deformations
[83], idempotents and solvability [58, 66], Grothendieck rings [1] and
embeddings of commutative algebras [42].
The first part of the paper surveys what is known. We have tried to be complete with
respect to the main lines of research, but the literature is large and we apologise for
any omission. Further general accounts are the monograph of Tian [111], the
historical survey of Ceballos, Falcón, Núñez-Valdés and Tenorio [38], the
short survey [112], and the general survey on classification problems for
non-associative structures [69] and its geometric counterpart [70]. The second part contains the open questions and
conjectures, together with a number of new observations.
1.2 Origins: genetics, Markov chains and Tian’s programme
Evolution algebras were introduced by Tian and Vojtěchovský [113] and developed
systematically in Tian’s monograph [111]. The motivation comes from
non-Mendelian genetics: in asexual (uniparental) inheritance, for instance of organelle
genes, a gamete of type eie_{i} reproduces by “self-crossing”, and the offspring
distribution is encoded by ei2=∑jωi​j​eje_{i}^{2}=\sum_{j}\omega_{ij}e_{j}, while distinct gametes do not
interact, which gives ei​ej=0e_{i}e_{j}=0. When the rows of MBM_{B} are probability vectors, the
evolution operator is the transition operator of a discrete-time Markov chain, and Tian
interpreted the basic notions of Markov chains in algebraic terms: persistent and
transient generators, communication classes, periodicity and absorption. He proved a
hierarchical (“semi-direct”) decomposition of an evolution algebra into simple
components and transient spaces, related algebraic periods to the periods of Markov
chains, and introduced the evolution algebra of a graph, defined by the adjacency matrix.
He also gave applications to the population genetics of organelle heredity and to the
analysis of Markov processes. These foundational ideas, collected in [111] and in the
programmatic paper [112], set the agenda for much of the later work.
Soon afterwards evolution algebras were connected with other parts of mathematical
biology and statistical mechanics. Rozikov and Tian [106] associated evolution
algebras with Gibbs measures on graphs, and Ladra and Rozikov introduced the evolution
algebra of a bisexual population [79], a non-baric but dibaric algebra; see also
[74] for the connections with dibaric algebras and population genetics, the
“chicken population” algebras [80, 46, 105] and the constrained algebras of
[47]. Varro generalised the bisexual construction to gonosomal algebras, which
model sex determination systems [115]. Further models include mosquito
populations [107] and pulse processes in networks [2].
1.3 Classification in low dimension
1.3.1 Dimension two and three
Two-dimensional evolution algebras over ℂ\mathbb{C} were classified by Casas, Ladra, Omirov
and Rozikov [36], who also described an algorithm deciding isomorphism of evolution
algebras; real two-dimensional algebras were treated by Murodov [92] and by Bekbaev
[4]. A geometric
invariant, the “square” of a two-dimensional evolution algebra, was introduced in
[33], where identities of degree at most four, derivations, automorphism group
schemes and associative representations of all two-dimensional algebras are determined.
The three-dimensional case is substantially harder. Cabrera Casado, Siles Molina and
Velasco classified three-dimensional evolution algebras over fields of characteristic
different from 22 containing roots of orders 22, 33 and 77 [23]; the
classification uses the graph, the dimension of A2A^{2} and the annihilator, and produces a
long list including several parametric families. An alternative approach, based on
chains of two-dimensional algebras and on the annihilator and socle stabilising
indices, was developed in [7], and a classification adapted to dimensions two
and three over arbitrary fields appears in [40]. Simple evolution algebras of
dimensions two and three were classified over arbitrary fields in [18], using the
graph, moduli sets, inductive limit groups and the Zariski topology.
1.3.2 Higher dimensions and special classes
Four-dimensional perfect non-simple evolution algebras were classified in
[15], with the isomorphism problem completed in [3]. Evolution algebras with
one-dimensional square were classified by means of inner product spaces [84]: if
dimA2=1\dim A^{2}=1 and aa generates A2A^{2}, then x​y=⟨x,y⟩​axy=\langle x,y\rangle a for a symmetric
bilinear form, and three broad classes appear according to the position of aa. Calderón,
Fernández Ouaridi and Kaygorodov [29] attached to every non-degenerate evolution
algebra an invariant consisting of an ordered sequence of lower-dimensional evolution
algebras from a specific family; it yields a classification method for non-degenerate
evolution algebras, carried out up to dimension three, and descriptions of derivations
and of the variety of evolution algebras with square of dimension at most one. Power-associative evolution algebras are Jordan algebras and
admit a Wedderburn decomposition; they are classified up to dimension six [96].
1.3.3 Isotopy
Isotopisms of evolution algebras correspond to mutations of genotypes. Falcón, Falcón
and Núñez showed, using computational algebraic geometry, that there are exactly four
(strong) isotopism classes of two-dimensional evolution algebras over any field [51],
and determined the isotopism classes of three-dimensional ones [52]. Ternary
derivations and the group scheme of ternary automorphisms of perfect evolution algebras
were computed in [85].
1.3.4 Counting
Isomorphism classes of perfect nn-dimensional evolution algebras correspond to orbits of
the semidirect product Sn⋉(𝕜×)nS_{n}\ltimes(\Bbbk^{\times})^{n} acting on invertible structure
matrices. Wei and Zou [119] turned this into a counting problem over finite fields,
derived a general counting formula and obtained explicit formulas for n≤4n\leq 4.
1.3.5 Recognising evolution algebras
Deciding whether a given algebra is an evolution algebra amounts to deciding whether nn
symmetric matrices describing the product are simultaneously diagonalisable by congruence
(the SDC problem) [6]; necessary and sufficient conditions for simultaneous
orthogonalisation of families of inner products over arbitrary fields were given in
[13]. In [6] it is also shown that classical genetic algebras of Mendelian
and auto-tetraploid inheritance are not evolution algebras but are limits of evolution
algebras. Imomkulov and Rozikov [67] approximated an arbitrary algebra by the family
of evolution algebras given by the Jacobians of its evolution operator.
1.4 Structure theory
1.4.1 Subalgebras, ideals and decompositions
Evolution subalgebras and evolution ideals (those having a natural basis that extends to a
natural basis of the algebra) behave much better than arbitrary subalgebras and ideals.
Cabrera Casado, Siles Molina and Velasco developed the theory in arbitrary dimension
[22]: they described ideals generated by one element, characterised simple
evolution algebras, proved that a non-degenerate evolution algebra is irreducible if and
only if its graph is connected, and established existence and uniqueness of a
decomposition of a non-degenerate algebra into irreducible components (the
fragmentation process); uniqueness fails in the degenerate case. Basic ideals
were introduced and studied in [14]. Boudi, Cabrera Casado and Siles Molina
[5] introduced the evolution rank, proved that in a non-degenerate
algebra every family of natural orthogonal vectors extends to a natural basis, showed that
ideals of perfect evolution algebras are basic, and located nilpotent elements of order
three through principal minors of the structure matrix.
The relation between ideals and the graph was made precise in [19]: there is a
monotone Galois connection between absorption ideals and hereditary saturated subsets of
vertices, which describes maximal ideals and absorption ideals combinatorially and
characterises finitely generated evolution algebras under suitable graph conditions. The
intersection 𝔐⁡(A)\mathfrak{M}(A) of all maximal ideals and a subdirect-product structure
theorem were studied in [21]. The absorption radical can be read off from the acyclic
vertices of the graph [24], and the reductive radical was introduced in
[20]. Cabrera Casado et al. [9] related non-degeneracy, zero
annihilator, the absorption property, von Neumann regularity and primeness to properties
of the family of all graphs of AA, showed that primeness is equivalent (under
semiprimeness) to downward directedness of the graphs, proved that semiprimeness and
perfection cannot be characterised combinatorially, and described the centroid as the
functions constant along connected components. Peirce decompositions relative to
idempotents were studied by Paniello [98].
Arbitrary subalgebras of evolution algebras need not be evolution algebras
[32]. Camacho, Khudoyberdiyev and Omirov classified the nilpotent evolution algebras
all of whose subalgebras are evolution subalgebras with extendable natural bases, and
formulated conjectures for the non-nilpotent case [32]; two of them were proved by
García-Martínez and Pérez-Rodríguez [58]. Ladra and Pérez-Rodríguez proved
that subalgebras of perfect (regular) evolution algebras are evolution algebras, and
characterised the existence of subalgebras of codimension one [77]. The
subalgebra lattice was investigated in [75] (distributivity in the nilpotent
case, modularity of solvable algebras with maximal index of solvability) and in
[78], where modular evolution algebras are fully characterised in the nilpotent case
and among supersolvable regular algebras. A Frattini theory, based on the supersolvable
nilradical, was developed in [76]. Order-theoretic structures on substructures and
the socle of an evolution algebra were studied by González Nevado [62].
1.4.2 Radicals and spectra
Velasco described the maximal modular ideals and the Jacobson radical of an evolution
algebra and introduced two notions of spectrum of an element, computed via the
eigenvalues of suitable matrices built from the structure constants [116]. Analytic
aspects (Banach algebra norms, continuity of the evolution operator) were studied by Mellon
and Velasco [86].
1.4.3 Idempotents and solvability
The idempotents of an evolution algebra are the solutions of (1). For algebras
coming from biological models they are the equilibrium states of the population and were
computed in many concrete cases, see e.g. [79, 47, 94]. García-Martínez and
Pérez-Rodríguez [58] analysed the solution set of the generic system by
elementary algebraic geometry: every perfect complex evolution algebra has a non-zero
idempotent. They conjectured that a complex evolution algebra is solvable if and only if
it has no non-zero idempotents. One implication is clear, since an idempotent lies in every
term of the derived series. Hu and Wen [66] found a three-dimensional counterexample
to the other implication (an isomorphic algebra appears in [42, Remark 3.9], as
an evolution envelope of a two-dimensional algebra); it is the exceptional member of a one-parameter family, and they
proved the equivalence whenever A(∞)A^{(\infty)} is itself an evolution algebra. Resolubility of
evolution algebras was also studied in [54].
1.4.4 The evolution operator
The evolution operator depends on the natural basis. Its behaviour, the set of all
evolution operators of an algebra, and the algebras whose evolution operator is an
endomorphism were studied by Fernández-Ternero, Gómez-Sousa and Núñez-Valdés
[53, 55].
1.5 Nilpotency
Nilpotency was the first structural property to be understood. Casas, Ladra, Omirov and
Rozikov proved that nil, right nilpotent and nilpotent finite-dimensional evolution algebras
are exactly those whose structure matrix is strictly upper triangular in a suitable natural
basis [36]. Camacho, Gómez, Omirov and Turdibaev characterised nilpotency by
matrix properties and proved that the maximal nilpotency index of an nn-dimensional
evolution algebra is 2n−1+12^{n-1}+1 [30]; algebras with maximal index were
characterised and classified in [35], where wide classes with index 2n−s+12^{n-s}+1 are
also constructed. Elduque and Labra gave the conceptual statement: an evolution algebra is
nilpotent if and only if Γ⁡(A,B)\Gamma(A,B) has no oriented cycles [48].
Classification of some classes of nilpotent evolution algebras proceeds by annihilator extensions
[64]. Hegazi and Abdelwahab classified nilpotent evolution algebras up to dimension
four over arbitrary fields [64] and in dimension five over algebraically closed fields
[65]. Elduque and Labra [49] pointed out a subtle gap in the extension
method (a natural basis of a quotient need not lift to a natural basis), introduced the
type and further invariants attached to the upper annihilating series, constructed
families of nilpotent evolution algebras from non-degenerate symmetric bilinear forms and
commuting symmetric endomorphisms, and completed the classification up to dimension five
over algebraically closed fields of characteristic different from two. Derivations, automorphisms, local and
22-local derivations of nilpotent evolution algebras of maximal nilindex were determined
in [89], the group exp⁡(Der⁡(E))\exp(\Der(E)) was shown to be a normal subgroup of Aut⁡(E)\Aut(E) of
computable index in [90], and tensor products of nilpotent evolution algebras were
studied in [100]. Rota–Baxter operators of weights 00 and 11 on nilpotent algebras
of maximal nilindex were classified in [101], and local Rota–Baxter operators of
weight zero in [102].
Formal deformations of evolution algebras and the
degenerations among nilpotent evolution algebras of dimension at most four were
determined by Makhlouf and Pérez-Rodríguez [83]; see [70] for a survey
of the geometric classification of non-associative algebras.
In infinite dimension the situation changes: Tian and Zou constructed finitely generated
evolution algebras (over rings with nilpotent elements) which are nil but not nilpotent
[114], and nil and nilpotent are not equivalent for Hilbert evolution algebras
[28].
In finite dimension, the word “nil” in Theorem 5 has to be understood with
respect to the principal powers xk+1=xk​xx^{k+1}=x^{k}x: for the plenary powers x[1]=xx^{[1]}=x,
x[m+1]=(x[m])2x^{[m+1]}=(x^{[m]})^{2}, nil and nilpotent are not equivalent even in finite dimension, as
the following example, based on the evolution envelopes of [42], shows.
Proposition 9.
Over every field 𝕜\Bbbk there is a 1515-dimensional evolution algebra EE which is not

EE is nil of bounded index with respect to plenary powers, and, by
Theorem 5, it is not nil with respect to principal powers.

1.6 Automorphisms and derivations
1.6.1 Automorphism groups
Elduque and Labra proved that the automorphism group of a perfect evolution algebra is
finite [48]. In [50] they showed that the affine group scheme of automorphisms of a
perfect algebra sits in an exact sequence
1⟶𝐃⟶𝐀𝐮𝐭⁡(A)⟶𝐒,1\longrightarrow\mathbf{D}\longrightarrow\mathbf{Aut}(A)\longrightarrow\mathbf{S},
(2)
where 𝐃\mathbf{D} is a finite diagonalisable group scheme (the diagonal automorphisms with
respect to the natural basis) and 𝐒\mathbf{S} is a constant group scheme of permutations of
the vertices, both depending only on the graph. Costoya, Ligouras, Tocino and Viruel
[43] constructed a faithful functor from finite simple graphs to perfect evolution
algebras and proved that every finite group is the automorphism group of infinitely many
perfect evolution algebras over any field (“evolution algebras are universally finite”),
and that every constant finite group scheme is the automorphism group scheme of a perfect
evolution algebra. Sriwongsa and Zou [109] gave another proof of universality,
classified the nn-dimensional perfect algebras with Aut⁡(A)≅Sn\Aut(A)\cong S_{n} and those with
maximal diagonal automorphism group. Costoya, Muñoz, Tocino and Viruel introduced Cayley
evolution algebras and showed that every finite group is the automorphism group of an
absolutely simple Cayley evolution algebra over every field with sufficiently many
elements [45]; over arbitrary fields see Theorem 10 below. Costoya, Mayorga and Viruel [44] studied which permutation
representations arise from (2): for k≥4k\geq 4 and nn large, proper kk-transitive
subgroups of SnS_{n} never occur, whereas every permutation representation is realised on
the set of natural idempotents of a suitable perfect algebra. Molinier [87] proved that
every positive rational number is of the form |Aut⁡(A)|/dimA|\Aut(A)|/\dim A for a perfect evolution
algebra AA.
The following construction shows that simplicity can be achieved over every field, which
proves a conjecture of an earlier version of this text.

For infinite-dimensional algebras, the group of automorphisms that are diagonal with

1.6.2 Derivations
Camacho, Gómez, Omirov and Turdibaev proved that Der⁡(A)=0\Der(A)=0 for complex evolution algebras with
non-singular structure matrix and described derivations when the rank is n−1n-1
[31]. The exact sequence (2) shows that for perfect algebras Der⁡(A)=0\Der(A)=0 in
characteristic 00 or 22, while in other characteristics Der⁡(A)\Der(A) is abelian and determined by
the graph [50]. Derivations of evolution algebras of graphs were described in terms of
the twin partition of the graph; for twin-free graphs the derivation algebra vanishes
[12]. Non-degenerate algebras and Volterra evolution algebras (whose structure matrices are
skew-symmetric, and which are connected to ergodicity of Volterra quadratic stochastic
operators) were treated in [11], where loops of evolution algebras are introduced.
Biderivations of some evolution algebras were considered in [97].
1.7 Graphs, random walks and combinatorics
The graph Γ⁡(A,B)\Gamma(A,B) was introduced by Elduque and Labra [48]; earlier,
Camacho et al. [30] formulated planarity of graphs in terms of evolution algebras,
and Tian [111] defined the evolution algebra of a graph. Systematic dictionaries
between evolution algebras and (pseudo)digraphs were developed by Ceballos, Núñez and
Tenorio [39, 37], by González-López and Núñez [61], and in the
study of graphicable and S-graphicable algebras [95].
There are two natural evolution algebras attached to a finite graph GG: A⁡(G)A(G), whose
structure matrix is the adjacency matrix, and AR​W​(G)A_{RW}(G), whose structure matrix is the
transition matrix of the symmetric random walk. Cadavid, Rodiño Montoya and Rodríguez
[25, 26] proved that they are always strongly isotopic, and that for non-singular
graphs they are isomorphic if and only if GG is regular or biregular; they conjectured the
same for singular graphs, and supporting families were given in [27]. The connection with Markov chains and
statistics was explored in [82].
Tensor products of evolution algebras are evolution algebras, and the graph of the
product relative to the product basis is the categorical product of the graphs.
Inheritance of non-degeneracy, irreducibility, perfection and simplicity was studied in
[17], where four-dimensional perfect algebras that are tensor products of
two-dimensional ones are classified. Alshatnawi, Costoya and Viruel [1] studied the
Grothendieck ring of finite-dimensional non-degenerate evolution algebras: they proved that
the balance of the graph (the greatest common divisor of the balances of the cycles of the
underlying undirected graph) is independent of the natural basis, that indecomposable
factors of balance one cancel after tensoring while factors of larger balance produce
zero-divisors, and they analysed the subring generated by cyclic algebras. Algebras
attached to permutations were studied in [72, 94]. Giacomelli [59]
introduced expander evolution algebras, whose graphs are expanders, and related the Cheeger
constant with simplicity, subalgebras, growth and spectral gaps.
1.8 Infinite dimension, probability and dynamics
Many results above hold in arbitrary dimension [22, 19]. Hilbert evolution
algebras, introduced by Vidal, Cadavid and Rodríguez [118], are Hilbert spaces with an
orthonormal natural basis and a continuous product; they are naturally associated with
discrete-time Markov chains with countable state space and with infinite graphs
[117], and their nilpotency was analysed through weighted digraphs [28].
Coletti, de Lima and Luiz [41] constructed infinite-dimensional genetic and evolution
algebras from Gibbs measures, with uncountable natural bases; infertility between
macroscopically different configurations produces a decomposition into fertile ideals.
Casas, Ladra and Rozikov introduced chains of evolution algebras, families
A[s,t]A^{[s,t]} whose structure matrices satisfy the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation
[34]. Many examples in dimensions two and three, and the time behaviour of the
baric property, of absolutely nilpotent elements and of idempotents, were studied in
[104, 93, 105, 68, 73]; flows of arbitrary finite-dimensional algebras
were introduced in [81]. Continuous evolution algebras arising from ordinary
differential equations and flows on matrix Lie groups were introduced by Montaner and
Paniello [88]. The quadratic evolution operator x↦x2x\mapsto x^{2} and its normalisations
define discrete dynamical systems whose fixed points are idempotents; these were analysed
for two-dimensional algebras [104] and for many biological models
[79, 47, 107].
1.9 Embeddings, rings and further generalisations
Costoya, Fernández Ouaridi and Viruel [42] proved that every finite-dimensional
commutative algebra is an ideal of a finite-dimensional evolution algebra, determined the
smallest possible dimension of such an evolution algebra and, in characteristic different
from two, related it to simultaneous Waring decompositions of the squaring map; for unital
algebras the minimal dimension is the Waring rank of x↦x2x\mapsto x^{2}, which they computed for
truncated polynomial algebras. It follows that the class of evolution algebras satisfies
no polynomial identities other than the consequences of commutativity (see
Remark 13 below). Evolution algebras over rings appear in [114]
and, for free modules of rank two over domains, in [16], where quasiperfect algebras
and coloured graphs are introduced. Isomorphism conditions for algebra functors, including
the evolution algebra functor, were studied in [60]. Further generalisations include
SS-evolution algebras [91] and evolution coalgebras.
This concludes the survey. We now turn to new observations and open problems.

2.1.1 Polynomial identities
Remark 13.
The class of evolution algebras satisfies no polynomial identity other than the
consequences of commutativity. Indeed, let pp be a non-zero element of degree at most dd
of the free commutative (non-associative) algebra FF on x1,…,xmx_{1},\dots,x_{m}. The quotient
F/F>dF/F_{>d} by the span of the monomials of degree greater than dd is a finite-dimensional
commutative algebra in which p⁡(x1,…,xm)=p≠0p(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=p\neq 0. Since every finite-dimensional
commutative algebra is an ideal (in particular, a subalgebra) of a finite-dimensional
evolution algebra [42], pp is not an identity of the class of evolution algebras.
Hence questions about identities are meaningful only for subclasses.

2.1.10 Further remarks
Remark 22.
Strong connectivity of Γ⁡(A,B)\Gamma(A,B), and hence the condensation of the graph, is not
independent of the natural basis for non-degenerate algebras. If
char⁡𝕜≠2\operatorname{char}\Bbbk\neq 2 and e12=e22=e1+e2e_{1}^{2}=e_{2}^{2}=e_{1}+e_{2}, then Γ⁡(A,{e1,e2})\Gamma(A,\{e_{1},e_{2}\}) is the

Remark 23.

2.2 Open questions
The following list merges and revises the two lists of problems in an earlier version of
this text (nine problems proposed by the authors and colleagues, and thirty further
problems motivated by the literature), and adds new questions. Questions which were (partly)
answered by the observations of Section 2.1 have been reformulated accordingly.
After each question we indicate its source and what is known.
2.2.1 Foundations
Question 1 (Non-cancellation).
Let AA be a non-degenerate finite-dimensional evolution 𝕜\Bbbk-algebra. Is it true that there
exist non-degenerate finite-dimensional evolution algebras B≇CB\not\cong C with
A⊗B≅A⊗CA\otimes B\cong A\otimes C if and only if each indecomposable direct summand of AA has
balance greater than 11? Equivalently, is [A][A] a zero-divisor of the Grothendieck ring of
non-degenerate evolution algebras exactly in this case?
Source and status: [1, Conjecture 1.1]; one implication and the case of direct sums of cyclic
algebras are proved in [1]. Proposed by I. Alshatnawi, C. Costoya and A. Viruel.
Question 2 (The category of evolution algebras).
The full subcategory 𝖤𝗏𝗈𝕜\mathsf{Evo}_{\Bbbk} of 𝕜\Bbbk-algebras spanned by evolution algebras is not
complete, and subalgebras of evolution algebras need not be evolution algebras. Is there a
categorical framework for evolution algebras (for instance, objects equipped with a natural
basis or a family of natural bases, with suitable morphisms) with good limits, colimits and
functorial constructions?
Source and status: Proposed by J. M. Moreno Fernández and A. Viruel.
Question 3 (Homological algebra).
Develop a (co)homology theory of evolution algebras governing extensions of evolution
algebras by evolution algebras and formal deformations inside the class of evolution
algebras.
Source and status: Proposed by A. Viruel; deformations are considered in [83].
Question 4 (Evolution algebras over rings).
Find a definition of evolution algebras over a commutative ring RR that does not require
the underlying module to be free. Do the reductions E/𝔪​EE/\mathfrak{m}E at maximal ideals
determine EE? Is there a notion of perfection over RR with a uniqueness theorem for natural
bases generalising the one over fields, and a well-behaved category of such algebras?
Source and status: Proposed by D. Martín Barquero and C. Martín González; rank two over domains is
treated in [16].
Question 5 (Tensor products and factorisation).
Characterise in terms of the factors when A⊗BA\otimes B is simple, perfect, non-degenerate or
indecomposable, and describe the role of the periods of the graphs. Does every
non-degenerate evolution algebra factor into tensor-indecomposable ones, and to what extent
is the factorisation unique?
Source and status: [17, 1]. The question is partially answered in [1, Section 5] in
terms of the balance.
Question 6 (The Grothendieck ring).
Find generators and relations, the Krull dimension and the nilradical of the Grothendieck
ring of non-degenerate evolution 𝕜\Bbbk-algebras, decide whether it is Noetherian, and
describe all its zero-divisors and its dependence on 𝕜\Bbbk.
Source and status: [1].
2.2.2 Structure theory
Question 7 (Intrinsic characterisation of solvability).
Find a graph-theoretic or matrix-theoretic characterisation of solvable finite-dimensional
evolution algebras, analogous to the characterisation of nilpotency by the absence of
oriented cycles.
Source and status: Motivated by the García-Martínez–Pérez-Rodríguez conjecture [58] and its
refutation [66]; a counterexample to the conjecture also appears in
[42, Remark 3.9].
Question 8 (Stable term of the derived series).
For which finite-dimensional evolution algebras is A(∞)A^{(\infty)} again an evolution algebra?
Source and status: [66, Question 2.11]; when this holds, absence of idempotents is equivalent to
solvability over ℂ\mathbb{C} [66].
Question 9 (Idempotent-free non-solvable algebras).
Classify the idempotent-free non-solvable evolution algebras of dimension 33 and 44 over
ℂ\mathbb{C}. In dimension 33, is A(∞)A^{(\infty)} always isomorphic to the algebra W0W_{0} of
Section 2.1, and do these algebras form a two-parameter family?
Source and status: [66]; see Section 2.1 for computer evidence that the example of [66]
is not unique.
Question 10 (Idempotents of non-perfect algebras).
Give a criterion, in terms of the structure matrix or the graphs, for a (not necessarily
perfect) finite-dimensional complex evolution algebra to have a non-zero idempotent.
Source and status: Described as open in [58].
Question 11 (Idempotents over arbitrary fields).
For n≥3n\geq 3, characterise the fields 𝕜\Bbbk over which every perfect nn-dimensional evolution
𝕜\Bbbk-algebra has a non-zero idempotent. Does the class of such fields depend on nn?
Source and status: Theorem 18 settles n=2n=2 and gives a sufficient condition for all nn;
see also Conjecture 1.
Question 12 (Hereditary evolution algebras).
Characterise the evolution algebras all of whose subalgebras are evolution algebras. Is this
property detected by the graphs? Is it preserved by tensor products and direct sums? What
about ideals?
Source and status: [32, 58, 77].
Question 13 (Subalgebra lattices).
Characterise modular, distributive and dually atomistic evolution algebras in general.
If two evolution algebras have isomorphic subalgebra lattices, how are they related?
Characterise the evolution algebras whose Frattini subalgebra is an ideal, and the
Frattini-free ones.
Source and status: [75, 78, 76].
Question 14 (Natural bases of non-perfect algebras).
Describe the set of natural bases (up to permutation and scaling) of a non-degenerate
evolution algebra. When is it finite? Can it be infinite for an algebra whose automorphism
group is finite?
Source and status: New. Proposition 14 shows that the set can be infinite (it contains all
orthogonal bases of VV completed by uu) even for a non-degenerate algebra; Remark 22 shows that the graphs of different natural
bases can differ in strong connectivity. [5, Corollary 2.13] describes all the
natural bases of an evolution algebra (degenerate or not) in terms of its decomposition into
natural ideals; it does not give a formula for the number of natural bases, but comes close
to it.
Question 15 (Nil versus nilpotent).
Give a combinatorial characterisation of nil evolution algebras of arbitrary dimension in
terms of Γ⁡(A,B)\Gamma(A,B). Is a finitely generated evolution algebra which is nil of bounded
index (with respect to principal powers) necessarily nilpotent?
Source and status: [114, 28]. With respect to plenary powers the answer is negative, already in
finite dimension (Proposition 9); with respect to principal powers,
finite-dimensional nil evolution algebras are nilpotent (Theorem 5).
Question 16 (Identities of subclasses).
By Remark 13, evolution algebras satisfy only the consequences of
commutativity. Describe the identities of natural subclasses: the algebras EnE_{n} of
[29], algebras with dimA2≤r\dim A^{2}\leq r, nilpotent algebras of index at most mm, algebras
whose graphs have bounded out-degree. Are the corresponding T-ideals finitely generated, and
are the varieties generated by a single finite-dimensional algebra?
Source and status: [29, 42].
Question 17 (Radicals and combinatorial detectability).
Which algebraic properties of a non-perfect evolution algebra (semiprimeness, primeness,
regularity, existence of non-trivial radicals) can be read off from a single natural basis,
which from the family of all graphs, and which from neither?
Source and status: [9, 24, 20]; semiprimeness and perfection are not combinatorial
[9].
2.2.3 Classification, moduli and geometry
Question 18 (The variety of evolution algebras).
The closure of the set 𝖤𝗏𝗈n\mathsf{Evo}_{n} of evolution algebra structures in the variety of commutative
algebras on 𝕜n\Bbbk^{n} is irreducible of dimension 2​n2−n2n^{2}-n. Find its equations, describe the
algebras in 𝖤𝗏𝗈n¯∖𝖤𝗏𝗈n\overline{\mathsf{Evo}_{n}}\setminus\mathsf{Evo}_{n}, and relate them to the simultaneous
diagonalisation by congruence problem.
Source and status: [6].
Question 19 (Degenerations and rigidity).
Determine the rigid evolution algebras and the degeneration diagrams of (nilpotent) evolution
algebras in dimension at least 55. Is the generic nilpotent evolution algebra of
Proposition 20 rigid within 𝒩n\mathcal{N}_{n} when n=4n=4?
Source and status: [83]; irreducibility and dimension of 𝒩n\mathcal{N}_{n} are in
Proposition 20.
Question 20 (Nilpotent algebras of dimension six).
Classify six-dimensional nilpotent evolution algebras over algebraically closed fields. By
Proposition 20 the generic family depends on five parameters.
Source and status: New; dimension five is treated in [65, 49].
Question 21 (Counting asymptotics).
Determine the asymptotic number of isomorphism classes of all (not necessarily perfect)
nn-dimensional evolution 𝔽q\mathbb{F}_{q}-algebras as n→∞n\to\infty.
Source and status: [119]; see also Conjecture 8.
Question 22 (Random evolution algebras).
For random structure matrices (uniform over 𝔽q\mathbb{F}_{q}, Gaussian over ℝ\mathbb{R},
supported on a random digraph), what are the asymptotic probabilities of being simple,
indecomposable, having trivial automorphism group, or having exactly kk real non-zero
idempotents? Are there sharp thresholds in the sparse regime?
Source and status: [1, 119]; the expected number of non-zero idempotents over 𝔽q\mathbb{F}_{q} is 11
(Remark 23).
Question 23 (Isotopy).
Classify four-dimensional evolution algebras up to isotopy over arbitrary fields.
Source and status: Dimensions two and three are treated in [51, 52].
Question 24 (Autotopy group schemes).
Describe the autotopy group scheme of a perfect evolution algebra by an exact sequence
analogous to (2) with terms depending only on the graph, and relate it to the group
scheme of ternary automorphisms.
Source and status: [85], [38, Problem 4.1].
Question 25 (Simple evolution algebras).
Classify four-dimensional simple evolution algebras over arbitrary fields.
Source and status: [18], [111], [38, Problem 3.2]. The two other parts of
an earlier formulation have been removed, since they have immediate answers. If
Γ=Γ⁡(A,B)\Gamma=\Gamma(A,B) is strongly connected, its period dd is the greatest common
divisor of the lengths of its oriented cycles; the vertices then split into classes
C0,…,Cd−1C_{0},\dots,C_{d-1} such that every arrow goes from some CiC_{i} to Ci+1C_{i+1} (indices modulo
dd), and A=⨁iAiA=\bigoplus_{i}A_{i} with Ai=span⁡{ex:x∈Ci}A_{i}=\mathrm{span}\{e_{x}:x\in C_{i}\} satisfies
Ai2⊆Ai+1A_{i}^{2}\subseteq A_{i+1} and Ai​Aj=0A_{i}A_{j}=0 for i≠ji\neq j, over every field (this is Tian’s
periodic decomposition [111]). Moreover, by Theorem 6 a simple
evolution algebra is perfect with strongly connected graph, and both properties are
preserved by extension of scalars; hence every simple finite-dimensional evolution algebra is
absolutely simple.
2.2.4 Graphs, combinatorics and spectra
Question 26 (Graphs versus random walks).
Prove that for every finite connected graph GG one has A⁡(G)≅AR​W​(G)A(G)\cong A_{RW}(G) if and only if GG
is regular or biregular.
Source and status: Known for non-singular graphs [25, 26]; supporting families in [27].
Question 27 (Basis-independent graph invariants).
Determine the graph-theoretic invariants of Γ⁡(A,B)\Gamma(A,B) that do not depend on BB for
non-degenerate evolution algebras. Is there a complete system of such invariants in some
natural class? Is the balance independent of the natural basis for arbitrary (possibly
degenerate) evolution algebras?
Source and status: Balance is invariant for non-degenerate algebras [1]; for degenerate
algebras this is not known; strong connectivity and the condensation are not
(Remark 22); see also [59, Open Problem 3].
Question 28 (Spectral theory).
Find spectral invariants of evolution algebras independent of the natural basis, and relate
them to expansion and to Tian’s hierarchy. Characterise algebraically the expander and
Ramanujan evolution algebras over finite fields.
Source and status: [38, Problem 3.1], [59, Open Problem 1].
Question 29 (Universality).
Is the category of (perfect) evolution 𝕜\Bbbk-algebras algebraically universal in the sense of
Pultr and Trnková? Is there at least a full embedding of the category of graphs into it?
Source and status: [43, 99].
Question 30 (Simultaneous graphical realisation).
Given digraphs G1,…,GmG_{1},\dots,G_{m} on dd vertices, decide whether there are a dd-dimensional
evolution algebra AA and natural bases BiB_{i} with Γ⁡(A,Bi)≅Gi\Gamma(A,B_{i})\cong G_{i}, and describe the
class of such algebras.
Source and status: Proposed by A. Viruel. If Gi≇GjG_{i}\not\cong G_{j} for some i,ji,j, such algebras are not
perfect.
2.2.5 Automorphisms, derivations and operators
Question 31 (Realisation of algebraic groups).
Let GG be a linear algebraic group over 𝕜\Bbbk. Is there a finite-dimensional evolution
𝕜\Bbbk-algebra AA with Aut⁡(A)≅G⁡(𝕜)\Aut(A)\cong G(\Bbbk) as abstract groups? with Aut⁡(A)≅G\Aut(A)\cong G as
algebraic groups? with 𝐀𝐮𝐭⁡(A)≅G\mathbf{Aut}(A)\cong G as group schemes?
Source and status: Proposed by C. Costoya and A. Viruel. Finite groups are realised [43, 109];
without the evolution condition the answer is positive [63]; OnO_{n} and GLn\GL_{n} are
realised (Proposition 14 and the zero algebra), and so are the orthogonal
groups of all diagonal non-degenerate forms, e.g. O⁡(p,q)O(p,q) over ℝ\mathbb{R}
(Remark 15).
Question 32 (Permutation representations).
Characterise the permutation representations ξ:G→Sn\xi\colon G\to S_{n} for which there is a perfect
nn-dimensional evolution algebra XX with Aut⁡(X)≅G\Aut(X)\cong G and ρX\rho_{X} equivalent to ξ\xi.
Source and status: Proposed by C. Costoya, P. Mayorga and A. Viruel; partial results in [44].
Question 33 (Extensions in the Elduque–Labra sequence).
Characterise the perfect evolution algebras for which 1→𝐃⁡(𝕜)→Aut⁡(A)→ρ⁡(Aut⁡(A))→11\to\mathbf{D}(\Bbbk)\to\Aut(A)\to\rho(\Aut(A))\to 1 splits. Which extensions of a permutation group by a group of the form
∏μ2hj−1​(𝕜)\prod\mu_{2^{h_{j}}-1}(\Bbbk) occur?
Source and status: New; for the
description of 𝐃⁡(𝕜)\mathbf{D}(\Bbbk) see [50] and Remark 19; the new example
of Proposition 21 gives a non-split extension.
Question 34 (Growth function over finite fields).
Describe the image of f⁡(X)=|Aut⁡(X)|/dimXf(X)=|\Aut(X)|/\dim X on perfect, nilpotent and
non-degenerate evolution algebras over a finite field 𝔽q\mathbb{F}_{q}.
Source and status: Proposed by A. Viruel; over infinite fields the answer is given in
Theorem 16; on simple algebras
the image is ℚ>0\mathbb{Q}_{>0} over every field (Corollary 11).
Question 35 (Ind-schemes).
Develop a theory of automorphism group ind-schemes of infinite-dimensional evolution algebras,
extending the description of diagonal automorphism groups as inverse limits (and solenoids).
Source and status: Proposed by D. Martín Barquero and C. Martín González; [10].
Question 36 (Realisation of derivation algebras).
Which finite-dimensional Lie algebras are isomorphic to Der⁡(A)\Der(A) for an evolution algebra AA?
Can every nilpotent or solvable Lie algebra be realised? Which semisimple ones?
Source and status: [50, 11, 12]; 𝔰​𝔬n\mathfrak{so}_{n} is realised (Proposition 14); see
Conjecture 5.
Question 37 (Smoothness).
For non-perfect evolution algebras, determine when 𝐀𝐮𝐭⁡(A)\mathbf{Aut}(A) is smooth, describe its identity
component, and decide when it is generated by exp⁡(Der⁡(A))\exp(\Der(A)).
Source and status: [50, 90].
Question 38 (Hopf algebras and representations).
For perfect evolution algebras of arbitrary dimension, is a faithful tight pp-algebra
isomorphic to the Hopf algebra representing 𝐀𝐮𝐭⁡(A)\mathbf{Aut}(A)? Is Aut⁡(A)=1\Aut(A)=1 equivalent to the
non-existence of faithful associative commutative representations?
Source and status: Known in dimension two [8].
Question 39 (Rota–Baxter and local maps).
Classify Rota–Baxter operators of arbitrary weight on perfect evolution algebras in terms
of the graph and describe the variety of such operators. For which evolution algebras are all
local (resp. 22-local) derivations derivations, and all local automorphisms automorphisms?
Source and status: [101, 102, 89].
Question 40 (Minimal dimension).
For a finite group GG and a field 𝕜\Bbbk let μ𝕜​(G)\mu_{\Bbbk}(G) be the minimal dimension of an
evolution 𝕜\Bbbk-algebra with automorphism group GG. Estimate μ𝕜​(G)\mu_{\Bbbk}(G) and compare it with
the minimal number of vertices of a graph with automorphism group GG.
Source and status: [43, 109]. By Remark 19, for 𝕜=ℂ\Bbbk=\mathbb{C} and G=ℤ3G=\mathbb{Z}_{3}
no perfect algebra of dimension at most 22 has automorphism group GG: in dimension at most
22 the group 𝐃⁡(𝕜)\mathbf{D}(\Bbbk) is non-trivial only when the graph is a 22-cycle, and then

Theorem 10, μ𝕜​(G)≤2​m\mu_{\Bbbk}(G)\leq 2m for every field 𝕜\Bbbk, where mm is the
minimal number of vertices of a strongly connected digraph (for instance, of a connected
graph) with automorphism group GG.

Source and status: Answered: over every
field every finite group is the automorphism group of a simple evolution algebra
(Theorem 10), and simple evolution algebras are automatically absolutely
simple (see the comments after Question 25).

2.2.8 Infinite dimension, probability and dynamics
Question 46 (Hilbert evolution algebras).
If the square of a Hilbert evolution algebra is dense, is its natural orthonormal basis unique
up to permutation and unimodular scalars? Describe bounded derivations and automorphisms, and
the spectral theory of the evolution operator.
Source and status: [118, 28].
Question 47 (Markov chains and Gibbs measures).
Characterise recurrence, transience and positive recurrence of countable Markov chains by
algebraic properties of the associated evolution algebras. Does a phase transition correspond
to an algebraic property of the algebras of [41]?
Source and status: [118, 41].
Question 48 (Dynamics of the evolution operator).
Characterise, in terms of the structure matrix and the graph, the non-negative real evolution
algebras for which all trajectories of x↦x2x\mapsto x^{2} (or its normalisation) converge, and the
existence and stability of periodic orbits.
Source and status: [111, 107].
Question 49 (Chains and flows).
Classify chains of evolution algebras up to time-dependent isomorphism, characterise the
chains whose isomorphism type is eventually constant, and describe the evolution of
idempotents, nilpotency and simplicity along chains and flows.
Source and status: [34, 88].
2.2.9 Algorithms
Question 50 (Algorithms and complexity).
What is the complexity of deciding whether an algebra over ℚ\mathbb{Q} is an evolution
algebra and of computing all its natural bases? What is the complexity of the isomorphism
problem for perfect evolution algebras over finite fields relative to graph isomorphism?
Can low-dimensional classifications be certified by computer, for instance in a proof
assistant?
Source and status: [6, 43]. By [42], over a field of characteristic different from
22, deciding whether an algebra is an evolution algebra is equivalent to computing a Waring
decomposition of the quadratic map x↦x2x\mapsto x^{2} given by the product; in general this is an
NP-hard problem, see [108].
2.3 Conjectures
Each conjecture below is accompanied by the evidence we have. For each of them a direct
attempt to prove or disprove it (of the order of half an hour of focused work, including
the natural first ideas indicated in the comments) did not succeed; in particular none of
them follows in an obvious way from the results surveyed in Section 1 or from the observations
of Section 2.1. Computer evidence was obtained with short scripts (exact
arithmetic over finite fields, or numerical computation); it is experimental and is not a
proof.
Conjecture 1 (Fields with odd-degree extensions).
If 𝕜\Bbbk has a finite separable extension of odd degree greater than 11, then for some nn
there is a perfect nn-dimensional evolution 𝕜\Bbbk-algebra without non-zero idempotents.
Together with Theorem 18(1) this would characterise the fields over which every
perfect evolution algebra has a non-zero idempotent.
Source and status: Proved when 𝕜\Bbbk has a cubic extension (Theorem 18(2)). The natural idea of
realising a given odd-degree extension L/𝕜L/\Bbbk as the residue field of the scheme Z′Z^{\prime} of
non-zero idempotents requires controlling all 2n−12^{n}-1 points simultaneously, and we could not
do this already for extensions of degree 55 without cubic subextensions.
Conjecture 2 (Generic Galois group of idempotents).
For the generic nn-dimensional evolution algebra over ℚ\mathbb{Q} (structure constants
independent indeterminates), the Galois group of the 2n−12^{n}-1 non-zero idempotents is the full
symmetric group S2n−1S_{2^{n}-1}. Consequently, by Chebotarev’s theorem, for fixed nn and
q→∞q\to\infty the number of non-zero idempotents of a random perfect evolution
𝔽q\mathbb{F}_{q}-algebra is distributed like the number of fixed points of a random permutation of
2n−12^{n}-1 letters; in particular the probability of having no non-zero idempotent tends to
∑k=02n−1(−1)k/k!\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}(-1)^{k}/k!.
Source and status: Proved for n=2n=2 (the example after Theorem 18 specialises the generic cubic
to one with Galois group S3S_{3}). The mean value 11 agrees with Remark 23 for
every qq. Computer experiments with random matrices over 𝔽p\mathbb{F}_{p} for n=2n=2
(p=101,211p=101,211) and n=3n=3 (p=31,53p=31,53) give distributions of the number of non-zero idempotents
close to the fixed-point distribution of S3S_{3} and S7S_{7}. Showing that the monodromy group is
22-transitive and contains a transposition seems to require a degeneration argument that we
could not complete for n=3n=3.

Over ℂ\mathbb{C}, the maximal order of the automorphism group of a perfect nn-dimensional
evolution algebra is

that is 1,6,21,72,155,12961,6,21,72,155,1296 for n=1,…,6n=1,\dots,6 and n!n! for n≥7n\geq 7.
Source and status: The values are attained by direct sums of cyclic algebras ei2=ei+1e_{i}^{2}=e_{i+1} (the automorphism
group of a direct sum of mm copies of a perfect indecomposable algebra CC is
Aut⁡(C)≀Sm\Aut(C)\wr S_{m}). The upper bound was checked for n≤4n\leq 4 by a computer enumeration using the
bound |Aut⁡(A)|≤|𝐃⁡(ℂ)|⋅|Aut⁡(Γ)||\Aut(A)|\leq|\mathbf{D}(\mathbb{C})|\cdot|\Aut(\Gamma)| over all digraphs Γ\Gamma that
are graphs of perfect algebras. Remark 19 bounds |𝐃||\mathbf{D}| but the
interplay between 𝐃\mathbf{D} and the permutation part is what we could not control in
general.

Over ℂ\mathbb{C}, the maximal order of the automorphism group of a simple nn-dimensional
evolution algebra is max⁡{n!,n⁡(2n−1)}\max\{n!,\;n(2^{n}-1)\}.

automorphism group SnS_{n} are classified in [109].
Conjecture 5 (Semisimple derivation algebras).
Let 𝕜\Bbbk be algebraically closed of characteristic 00. If Der⁡(A)\Der(A) is semisimple for a
finite-dimensional evolution algebra AA, then Der⁡(A)≅⨁i𝔰​𝔬mi\Der(A)\cong\bigoplus_{i}\mathfrak{so}_{m_{i}} with
mi≥3m_{i}\geq 3.
Source and status: Proposition 14 realises 𝔰​𝔬m\mathfrak{so}_{m}. Heuristically, derivations of an
algebra with a natural basis must preserve a family of quadratic forms simultaneously
diagonalised by that basis, and the orthogonal algebras are the natural semisimple symmetry
algebras of such configurations. We know no example in which a simple Lie algebra not
isomorphic to some 𝔰​𝔬m\mathfrak{so}_{m} (for instance 𝔰​𝔩3\mathfrak{sl}_{3} or
𝔤2\mathfrak{g}_{2}) occurs as an ideal of a derivation algebra, but we could not exclude, for
instance, 𝔰​𝔩3\mathfrak{sl}_{3} acting on a non-degenerate algebra whose square has dimension
greater than one.
Conjecture 6 (Numbers of complex idempotents).
For every nn and every m∈{2,…,2n}m\in\{2,\dots,2^{n}\} there is a perfect nn-dimensional complex
evolution algebra with exactly mm distinct idempotents (including 00). In particular,
for every nn there is a perfect complex evolution algebra with a unique non-zero idempotent.
Source and status: True for n≤2n\leq 2: e12=34​e1+14​e2e_{1}^{2}=\frac{3}{4}e_{1}+\frac{1}{4}e_{2}, e22=14​e1+34​e2e_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}e_{1}+\frac{3}{4}e_{2} has the unique
non-zero idempotent e1+e2e_{1}+e_{2} (of multiplicity 33), and triangular algebras as in the proof of
Theorem 17 give 33 and 44. Over ℝ\mathbb{R} the analogous statement is
Theorem 17, but there non-real solutions may be ignored. Triangular
constructions only give values ≥n+1\geq n+1, and the symmetric families we tried for n=3n=3 (with
S3S_{3}-symmetry) did not produce a unique non-zero idempotent.
Conjecture 7 (Failure of the Hasse principle).
There exist perfect evolution ℚ\mathbb{Q}-algebras A,BA,B which are isomorphic over ℝ\mathbb{R}
and over ℚp\mathbb{Q}_{p} for every prime pp, but not over ℚ\mathbb{Q}.
Source and status: Forms of a perfect algebra with the same graph are governed by
H1​(ℚ,𝐀𝐮𝐭​(A))H^{1}(\mathbb{Q},\mathbf{Aut}(A)). For two-dimensional cyclic algebras the relevant set is
ℚ×/(ℚ×)3\mathbb{Q}^{\times}/(\mathbb{Q}^{\times})^{3} (up to the action of the permutation part), and the
Hasse principle holds there; failures should come from non-abelian or non-cyclic diagonal
parts, where Tate–Shafarevich sets of finite group schemes are known to be non-trivial in
general. We could not yet produce an explicit pair.
Conjecture 8 (PORC counting).
For every nn, the number Nn​(q)N_{n}(q) of isomorphism classes of perfect nn-dimensional evolution
𝔽q\mathbb{F}_{q}-algebras is a PORC function of qq (a polynomial on each residue class of qq
modulo some integer).
Source and status: The count is given by Burnside’s lemma for Sn⋉(𝔽q×)nS_{n}\ltimes(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{n} acting on
GLn⁡(𝔽q)\GL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}), and each fixed-point set is defined by monomial conditions; the
difficulty is to show that the number of invertible matrices in each such set is PORC.
Computer counts: N2(q)=q2+2​q−12+[q≡1mod3]N_{2}(q)=\frac{q^{2}+2q-1}{2}+[\,q\equiv 1\bmod 3\,] for all odd primes 3≤q≤233\leq q\leq 23, and N3​(3)=249N_{3}(3)=249, N3​(5)=3943N_{3}(5)=3943, N3​(7)=26249N_{3}(7)=26249. Explicit formulas for n≤4n\leq 4 are
given in [119].
Conjecture 9 (Spectrum of nilpotency indices).
Let 𝕜\Bbbk be an infinite field. An nn-dimensional nilpotent evolution 𝕜\Bbbk-algebra of
nilpotency index k+1k+1 exists if and only if either k=2ak=2^{a} with 0≤a≤n−10\leq a\leq n-1, or kk has
at least two non-zero binary digits and ⌊log2⁡k⌋+s2​(k)≤n−1\lfloor\log_{2}k\rfloor+s_{2}(k)\leq n-1, where s2​(k)s_{2}(k) is
the number of non-zero binary digits of kk.
Source and status: The case of maximal index 2n−1+12^{n-1}+1 is known [30, 35]. The predicted sets of
indices are {2,3,5}\{2,3,5\}, {2,3,4,5,9}\{2,3,4,5,9\}, {2,…,7,9,17}\{2,\dots,7,9,17\} and
{2,…,11,13,17,33}\{2,\dots,11,13,17,33\} for n=3,4,5,6n=3,4,5,6. In computer experiments (exact computation modulo
a large prime with structure matrices having small integer entries; exhaustive over a fixed
set of entries for n≤5n\leq 5 and random for n=6n=6) exactly these indices were found. The
lower series of an evolution algebra is governed by sums of path weights in the graph, and
binary expansions appear because ei2e_{i}^{2} doubles the degree; turning this into a proof of
the “only if” part requires controlling cancellations, which we could not do.
