Abstract.
This article addresses two central problems in the theory of quandle rings.
First, motivated by Conjecture 3.10 in Internat. J. Math. 34 (2023), no. 3, Paper No. 2350011: for a semi-latin quandle XX, every nonzero idempotent in the integral quandle ring ℤ⁡[X]\mathbb{Z}[X] necessarily corresponds to an element of XX, we investigate idempotents in quandle rings of semi-latin quandles. Precisely, we prove that if the ground ring is an integral domain with unity, then the quandle ring of Core(ℤ\mathbb{Z}) admits only trivial idempotents. Second, powers of augmentation ideals in quandle rings have only been computed in few cases previously. We extend the computations to include dihedral quandles and commutative quandles. Finally, we examine idempotents in quandle rings of 22-almost latin quandles and apply these results to compute the automorphism groups of their integral quandle rings.

1. Introduction
Quandles are algebraic structures whose axioms are motivated by the three Reidemeister moves in knot theory. They were introduced independently by D. Joyce [Joyce] and S. Matveev [Matveev] in the nineteen eighties. Quandles have been used extensively to construct invariants of knots, knotted surfaces and virtual knots (see for example [EN, CJKLS, CES, CEGS]). Quandles also arise in a variety of mathematical contexts, including set-theoretic solutions to the Yang-Baxter equation [BES], group theory [EMR, BDS, BNS], category theory [CCES] and representation theory [EM, ESZ]. Concepts from ring theory were introduced to quandle theory in [BPS2]. Precisely, a notion of a quandle ring was introduced by analogy with a group ring and relationships between subquandles of the given quandle and ideals of the associated quandle ring were investigated in [BPS2]. It was proved in [EFT] that quandle rings of non-trivial quandles are not power-associative rings and that if the ground ring is Noetherian and the quandle is finite then the quandle ring is both left and right Noetherian. Zero divisors in quandle rings were investigated in [BPS1]. An initiation to homological study of quandle rings was given in [EMSZ] where a complete characterization of derivations of quandle algebras of dihedral quandles over fields of characteristic zero has been given, and dimension of the Lie algebra of derivations has been investigated. The question of whether a given quandle ring possesses non-trivial idempotents was initiated in [BPS1]. In [ENSS] a complete characterization of the idempotents of certain quandle coverings was given. In [ENS] some applications of quandle rings to knot theory was introduced. Precisely, an enhancement of the coloring quandle invariant was obtained by considering the algebra homomorphisms between the quandle ring of a link, and a fixed target quandle ring. Furthermore, when the idempotent set of a quandle ring is a quandle itself, it was shown that the quandle coloring by the idempotent quandle gives an enhancement of the quandle coloring invariant. The combination of idempotents in quandle rings and quandle 22-cocycles was used in [ES] to obtain an enhancement of the coloring invariant in [CESY] thus distinguishing knots with up to thirteen crossings knots using only twenty one connected quandles.
In this article, we treat quandle rings as algebraic objects on their own right rather than by their connections with knot theory as in [ENS, ES]. We consider two main problems. The first problem is the investigation of Conjecture 3.10 in [ENSS]. Second, we extend the computations of the powers of the augmentation ideals to include dihedral quandles and commutative quandles. In Section 2 we review the basics of quandles and quandle rings. Section 3 deals with infinite quandles. In particular, we prove that if the ground ring is unital without zero-divisors then the quandle ring of the Core quandle of infinite cyclic group has only trivial idempotents (cf. Theorem 3.1). Remark that the Core quandle C​o​r​e​(ℤ)Core(\mathbb{Z}) is semi-latin but not latin. On the other hand, this quandle is left orderable but is not right orderable. In Section 4 we compute the powers of the augmentation ideal for dihedral quandles. Section 5 gives a complete classification of the powers of the augmentation ideal over the commutative quandles of odd prime orders 55 and 77 (cf. Proposition 5.6 and Proposition 5.7). In Section 6 we initiate the study of idempotents over mm-almost latin quandles. As an example, for the involutory connected quandle XX of order six, we completely characterize the idempotents over its integral quandle ring ℤ⁡[X]\mathbb{Z}[X] (cf. Proposition 6.2). As a consequence, we obtain the automorphisms of the quandle ring ℤ⁡[X]\mathbb{Z}[X]. Section 7 gives some open questions for further research.
2. Basics of Quandles and Quandle Rings
We review the basics of quandles needed for this article. All definitions and known results can be found in the book [BES].
Definition 2.1.
A quandle XX is a
set with a binary operation (x,y)↦x∗y(x,y)\mapsto x*y
satisfying the following conditions.
(1) For any x∈Xx\in X,
x∗x=xx*x=x.
(2) For any y∈Xy\in X, the right multiplication Ry:x↦x∗yR_{y}:x\mapsto x*y is a bijection.
(3) For any x,y,z∈Xx,y,z\in X, we have
(x∗y)∗z=(x∗z)∗(y∗z).(x*y)*z=(x*z)*(y*z).
Notice that Axioms (2) and (3) can be combined to state that RyR_{y} is an automorphism of the quandle (X,∗)(X,*). The notions of quandle homomorphisms and isomorphisms are natural. The following are some examples of quandles.
•
Any non-empty set XX with the operation x∗y=xx*y=x for any x,y∈Xx,y\in X is
a quandle called a trivial quandle.
•
Any group GG with the binary operation x∗y=y​x​y−1x*y=yxy^{-1} is a quandle called a conjugation quandle.
•
The binary operation x∗y=y​x−1​yx*y=yx^{-1}y defines a quandle structure on any group GG called the Core quandle of GG and denoted Core(GG). If furthermore, G=ℤnG=\mathbb{Z}_{n} is the abelian group of integers modulo nn, where nn is a positive integer, then the operation is denoted by x∗y=−x+2​yx*y=-x+2y and the quandle is called the dihedral quandle and denoted by RnR_{n}.
•
A generalized Alexander quandle is defined by a pair (G,f)(G,f) where
GG is a group and f∈Aut⁡(G)f\in{\rm Aut}(G),
and the quandle operation is defined by
x∗y=f⁡(x​y−1)​yx*y=f(xy^{-1})y.
If GG is abelian, this is called an Alexander quandle.
•
A function ψ:X×X→A\psi:X\times X\rightarrow A for an abelian group AA is
called a quandle 22-cocycle [CJKLS] if it satisfies
ψ⁡(x,y)+ψ⁡(x∗y,z)=ψ⁡(x,z)+ψ⁡(x∗z,y∗z)\psi(x,y)+\psi(x*y,z)=\psi(x,z)+\psi(x*z,y*z)
and ψ⁡(x,x)=0\psi(x,x)=0 for all x,y,z∈Xx,y,z\in X.
For a quandle 22-cocycle ψ\psi the set X×AX\times A is a quandle under the operation
(x,a)∗(y,b)=(x∗y,a+ψ⁡(x,y))(x,a)*(y,b)=(x*y,a+\psi(x,y)) for x,y∈Xx,y\in X, a,b∈Aa,b\in A,
and it is called an abelian extension of XX by AA, see [CENS].
Given a quandle XX, the subgroup Inn⁡(X)\operatorname{Inn}(X), of the automorphism group Aut⁡(X)\operatorname{Aut}(X), is the group generated by the right multiplications RxR_{x}. When the natural action of Inn⁡(X)\operatorname{Inn}(X) on XX is transitive then XX is said to be a connected quandle.
Let (X,∗)(X,*) be a quandle and 𝕜\mathbbm{k} be an integral domain (a commutative ring without zero divisors) with unity. Let 𝕜⁡[X]\mathbbm{k}[X] be the set of all formal expressions of the form ∑x∈Xαx​ex\sum_{x\in X}\alpha_{x}e_{x}, where αx∈𝕜\alpha_{x}\in\mathbbm{k} such that all but finitely many αx=0\alpha_{x}=0. The set 𝕜⁡[X]\mathbbm{k}[X] is a free 𝕜\mathbbm{k}-module with basis {ex∣x∈X}\{e_{x}\mid x\in X\} and admits a product given by
(∑x∈Xαx​ex)​(∑y∈Xβy​ey)=∑x,y∈Xαx​βy​ex∗y,\Big(\sum_{x\in X}\alpha_{x}e_{x}\Big)\Big(\sum_{y\in X}\beta_{y}e_{y}\Big)=\sum_{x,y\in X}\alpha_{x}\beta_{y}e_{x*y},
where x,y∈Xx,y\in X and αx,βy∈𝕜\alpha_{x},\beta_{y}\in\mathbbm{k}. This turns 𝕜⁡[X]\mathbbm{k}[X] into a ring called the quandle ring (or the quandle algebra) of XX with coefficients in 𝕜\mathbbm{k}. When XX is a non-trivial quandle then the quandle ring 𝕜⁡[X]\mathbbm{k}[X] is non-associative ring. There is a natural identification of XX with a subset of 𝕜⁡[X]\mathbbm{k}[X] via the map sending xx to exe_{x}.
The surjective ring homomorphism ε:𝕜⁡[X]→𝕜\varepsilon:\mathbbm{k}[X]\rightarrow\mathbbm{k} given by
ε⁡(∑x∈Xαx​ex)=∑x∈Xαx\varepsilon\Big(\sum_{x\in X}\alpha_{x}e_{x}\Big)=\sum_{x\in X}\alpha_{x}
is called the augmentation map. The kernel Δ⁡(X)\Delta(X) of ε\varepsilon is a two-sided ideal of 𝕜⁡[X]\mathbbm{k}[X], called the augmentation ideal of 𝕜⁡[X]\mathbbm{k}[X].
Notation: Through the whole article, it is important to make a distinction between the product in the quandle, denoted by ∗*, and the product, denoted by ⋅\cdot or juxtaposition, in the quandle ring.
One of the interesting problems in quandle rings is the calculation of idempotents, that are element u∈𝕜⁡[X]u\in\mathbbm{k}[X] such that u2=uu^{2}=u. Any element of XX is idempotent called trivial idempotent. A more interesting case of quandle rings is when 𝕜=ℤ\mathbbm{k}=\mathbb{Z}.
For a full description of idempotents of ℤ⁡[X]\mathbb{Z}[X] one needs to understand the structure of XX.
If X=∐iXiX=\coprod_{i}X_{i} is the disjoint union of quandles, then for the set of idempotents I⁡(ℤ⁡[X])I(\mathbb{Z}[X]), the following inclusion holds I⁡(ℤ⁡[X])⊇∐iI⁡(ℤ⁡[Xi])I(\mathbb{Z}[X])\supseteq\coprod_{i}I(\mathbb{Z}[X_{i}]).
Also, if
X={1,2,…,m}X=\{{1},{2},\ldots,{m}\} is the mm element trivial quandle, then
I⁡(ℤ⁡[X])={e1+∑j=2mαj​(ej−e1),αj∈ℤ}I(\mathbb{Z}[X])=\{e_{1}+\sum_{j=2}^{m}\alpha_{j}(e_{j}-e_{1}),~~\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}\}
(see Proposition 13.21 of [BES]). One of the interesting classes of quandles is the class of latin quandles. Recall that a quandle is called latin if multiplication on the left by any element is a bijection.
In particular, the dihedral quandles of odd orders are latin. For the class of latin quandles, we have the following well known
Conjecture 2.2.
([BES, Conjecture 13.19]) Let XX be a finite latin quandle, uu be a non-zero idempotent in the integral quandle ring ℤ⁡[X]\mathbb{Z}[X]. Then
(1)
The element uu is a trivial idempotent;
(2)
ε⁡(u)=1\varepsilon(u)=1.
If we take a field 𝕜\mathbbm{k} instead ℤ\mathbb{Z}, then this conjecture does not hold. The first item of this conjecture follows from the following proposition. For the second item of this conjecture see Proposition 4.4.
Proposition 2.3.
([BES, Proposition 12.18])
Let X={x1,…,xn}X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\} be a finite latin quandle. Then the following assertions hold:
(1)
The element w=ex1+⋯+exnw=e_{x_{1}}+\cdots+e_{x_{n}} lies in the center Z⁡(𝕜⁡[X])Z(\mathbbm{k}[X]).
(2)
If 𝕜\mathbbm{k} is a field with characteristic not dividing nn, then 1n​w\frac{1}{n}w is idempotent.
For quandle rings which have only trivial idempotents, it is easy to find the automorphism group of the quandle ring.
Proposition 2.4.
If a quandle XX has only trivial idempotents, then Aut⁡(ℤ⁡[X])=Aut⁡(X).\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}[X])=\operatorname{Aut}(X).
In the next sections we will consider quandles of special types and study idempotents in their quandle rings.
3. Infinite Quandles
The question of idempotents in quandle rings can be formulated not only for finite latin quandles but for infinite latin quandles and for semi-latin quandles.
As example, consider the Core(OPENℤ)\mathbb{Z}) in which the operation is defined by the rule a∗b=2​b−aa*b=2b-a, for all a,b∈ℤa,b\in\mathbb{Z}. Let LaL_{a}, a∈ℤa\in\mathbb{Z} be the operator multiplication from the left, i.e. La​(x)=a∗xL_{a}(x)=a*x for any x∈ℤx\in\mathbb{Z}. We see that L2​(x)=2​(x−1)L_{2}(x)=2(x-1). Hence, L2L_{2} is injective but is not bijective and hence is not invertible. Furthermore,
La2​(x)=4​x−3​a≠xL_{a}^{2}(x)=4x-3a\not=x, this means that LaL_{a} is not involutive. On the other hand, if RaR_{a}, a∈ℤa\in\mathbb{Z} is the operator multiplication from the right, then RaR_{a} is an automorphism and Ra2=i​dR_{a}^{2}=id.
Next, we want to understand the structure of the augmentation ideal Δ⁡(C​o​r​e​(ℤ))\Delta(Core(\mathbb{Z})). To do so, we will consider ℤ\mathbb{Z} as multiplicative group with the generator tt and write
ℤ={…,t−1,1,t,…}.\mathbb{Z}=\{\ldots,t^{-1},1,t,\ldots\}.
Then the binary operation in C​o​r​e​(ℤ)Core(\mathbb{Z}) is defined by the rule ti∗tj=t2​j−it^{i}*t^{j}=t^{2j-i}. Further, for simplicity, we will denote the basis elements in quandle ring by eie_{i} instead etie_{t^{i}}. Then the product ei⋅eje_{i}\cdot e_{j} is given by ei⋅ej=e2​j−ie_{i}\cdot e_{j}=e_{2j-i}.
On the elements of ℤ\mathbb{Z} we have a linear order: ti<tjt^{i}<t^{j} if i<ji<j. Further, for any tk∈ℤt^{k}\in\mathbb{Z} we have
ti∗tk=t2​k−i>tj∗tk=t2​k−j.t^{i}*t^{k}=t^{2k-i}>t^{j}*t^{k}=t^{2k-j}.
On the other side
tk∗ti=t2​i−k<tk∗tj=t2​j−k.t^{k}*t^{i}=t^{2i-k}<t^{k}*t^{j}=t^{2j-k}.
Hence, this order is preserved under multiplication on the left and is not preserved under multiplication on the right. The quandle C​o​r​e​(ℤ)Core(\mathbb{Z}) is left orderable but not right orderable [BPS1] (see also [BES, Propositions 11.3 and 11.5]).
Let us take

where we assume that αm​αM≠0\alpha_{m}\alpha_{M}\not=0. In this case we say that αm​em=min⁡(u)\alpha_{m}e_{m}=\min(u), αM​eM=max⁡(u)\alpha_{M}e_{M}=\max(u), and s​u​p​p​(u)⊆[m,M]supp(u)\subseteq[m,M].

Let us extend the linear order from ℤ\mathbb{Z} to ℤ⁡[C​o​r​e​(ℤ)]\mathbb{Z}[Core(\mathbb{Z})] by the rule
α​ea<β​eb​if and only if​α​β≠0​and​a<b.\alpha e_{a}<\beta e_{b}~\mbox{if and only if}~\alpha\beta\not=0~\mbox{and}~a<b.

4. Idempotents in Quandle Rings of Dihedral Quandles
Let XX be a quandle and 𝕜⁡[X]\mathbbm{k}[X] be its quandle ring. To find idempotents in 𝕜⁡[X]\mathbbm{k}[X] we can write
𝕜⁡[X]=𝕜​e0+Δ𝕜​(X)\mathbbm{k}[X]=\mathbbm{k}e_{0}+\Delta_{\mathbbm{k}}(X)

5. Commutative Quandles, Powers of Augmentation Ideals and Idempotents
A quandle (X,∗)(X,*) is said to be commutative, if x∗y=y∗xx*y=y*x for all x,y∈Xx,y\in X. It is easy to see that any commutative quandle is a latin quandle. Notice that this implies also that commutative quandles are faithful meaning that the map x↦Rxx\mapsto R_{x} is injective. This is clear since for any zz, Rx​(z)=Ry​(z)R_{x}(z)=R_{y}(z) implies x∗z=y∗zx*z=y*z and thus x=yx=y.

6. mm-almost Latin Quandles
As we have seen above, if a quandle ring has only trivial idempotents, then the automorphism group of the quandle ring is equal to the automorphism group of the quandle. In this section we consider a quandle ring of some generalizations of latin quandle and find its idempotents and automorphisms.
Almost latin quandles are a generalization of latin quandles. The following definition can be found in [PY].
Definition 6.1.
Let m≥1m\geq 1 be an integer. A quandle XX is said to be mm-almost latin if it satisfies the following conditions:
(1)
The stabilizer S​t​a​b​(a)={x∈X|Rx​(a)=a}Stab(a)=\{x\in X|\;R_{x}(a)=a\} has order mm for each a∈Xa\in X.
(2)
The equation La​(x)=bL_{a}(x)=b has a unique solution for each b∈X∖S​t​a​b​(a)b\in X\setminus Stab(a).
Notice that when m=1m=1, an mm-almost latin quandle is simply a latin quandle.
Let us consider some example of 2-almost Latin quandle (see [BES, Example 13.17]).
This quandle X={1,2,…,6}X=\{1,2,\ldots,6\} is the involutory connected quandle of order 6 given in terms of its right multiplications as follows:
R1=(3 5)​(4 6),R2=(3 6)​(4 5),R3=(1 5)​(2 6),\displaystyle R_{1}=(3\;5)(4\;6),\quad R_{2}=(3\;6)(4\;5),\quad R_{3}=(1\;5)(2\;6),
R4=(1 6)​(2 5),R5=(1 3)​(2 4),R6=(1 4)​(2 3).\displaystyle R_{4}=(1\;6)(2\;5),\quad R_{5}=(1\;3)(2\;4),\quad R_{6}=(1\;4)(2\;3).
