Abstract.
    

Proving a conjecture of Talagrand,
a fractional version of the “expectation-threshold” conjecture of Kalai and the second author, we show that for any increasing family ℱ{\mathcal{F}} on a finite set XX, we have pc​(ℱ)=O⁡(qf​(ℱ)​log⁡ℓ⁡(ℱ))p_{c}({\mathcal{F}})=O(q_{f}({\mathcal{F}})\log\ell({\mathcal{F}})), where pc​(ℱ)p_{c}({\mathcal{F}}) and qf​(ℱ)q_{f}({\mathcal{F}}) are the threshold and “fractional expectation-threshold” of ℱ{\mathcal{F}}, and ℓ⁡(ℱ)\ell({\mathcal{F}}) is the maximum size of a minimal member of ℱ{\mathcal{F}}.
This easily implies several heretofore difficult results
and conjectures in probabilistic combinatorics,
including thresholds for perfect hypergraph matchings (Johansson–Kahn–Vu), bounded degree spanning trees (Montgomery), and bounded degree graphs (new).
We also resolve (and vastly extend) the “axial” version of
the random multi-dimensional assignment problem
(earlier considered by Martin–Mézard–Rivoire and Frieze–Sorkin).
Our approach builds on a recent breakthrough of Alweiss, Lovett, Wu and Zhang on the Erdős–Rado
“Sunflower Conjecture.”

1. Introduction




Our most important contribution here is the proof of a conjecture of Talagrand [28] that is
a fractional version of the “expectation-threshold” conjecture of
Kalai and the second author [17].
For an increasing family ℱ{\mathcal{F}} on a finite set XX, we write (with definitions below)
pc​(ℱ)p_{c}({\mathcal{F}}), qf​(ℱ)q_{f}({\mathcal{F}}) and ℓ⁡(ℱ)\ell({\mathcal{F}}) for the threshold, fractional expectation-threshold, and size of a largest
minimal element of ℱ{\mathcal{F}}. In this language, our main result is the following.



Theorem 1.1.



There is a universal KK such that for every finite XX and increasing
ℱ⊆2X{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq 2^{X},





pc​(ℱ)≤K​qf​(ℱ)​log⁡ℓ⁡(ℱ).p_{c}({\mathcal{F}})\leq Kq_{f}({\mathcal{F}})\log\ell({\mathcal{F}}).







As observed below, qf​(ℱ)q_{f}({\mathcal{F}}) is a more or less trivial lower bound on pc​(ℱ)p_{c}({\mathcal{F}}), and Theorem 1.1 says this bound is never far from the truth. (Apart from the constant KK, the upper bound is tight in many of the most interesting cases.)




Thresholds have been a—maybe the—central concern of the study of random discrete structures
(random graphs and hypergraphs, for example) since its initiation by Erdős and Rényi [7], with much
work around identifying thresholds for specific properties (see [4, 14]), though it was
not observed until [3] that every increasing ℱ{\mathcal{F}} admits a threshold (in the
Erdős–Rényi sense; see below). See also [11] for developments, since [10], on the very interesting question of sharpness of thresholds.




Our second main result is Theorem 1.7 below, which was motivated by
work of Frieze and Sorkin [12] on
the “random multi-dimensional assignment problem.” The statement
is postponed until we have filled in some background, to which we now turn.
(See the beginning of Section 2 for notation not defined here.)




Thresholds.
For a given XX and p∈[0,1]p\in[0,1], μp\mu_{p} is the product
measure on 2X2^{X} given by μp​(S)=p|S|​(1−p)|X∖S|\mu_{p}(S)=p^{|S|}(1-p)^{|X\setminus S|}.
An ℱ⊆2X{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq 2^{X} is increasing if
B⊇A∈ℱ⇒B∈ℱB\supseteq A\in{\mathcal{F}}\Rightarrow B\in{\mathcal{F}}.
If this is true
(and
ℱ≠2X,∅{\mathcal{F}}\neq 2^{X},\emptyset), then μp​(ℱ)​(:=∑{μp​(S):S∈ℱ}CLOSE\mu_{p}({\mathcal{F}})(:=\sum\{\mu_{p}(S):S\in{\mathcal{F}}\})
is strictly increasing in pp,
and the threshold, pc​(ℱ)p_{c}({\mathcal{F}}),
is the unique pp for which
μp​(ℱ)=1/2\mu_{p}({\mathcal{F}})=1/2.
This is finer than the original Erdős–Rényi notion,

(That pc​(ℱ)p_{c}({\mathcal{F}}) is always an Erdős–Rényi threshold

Then

differently in [17]), is a trivial lower bound
on pc​(ℱ)p_{c}({\mathcal{F}}), since for 𝒢{\mathcal{G}} as above and TT

The following statement, the main conjecture (Conjecture 1) of
[17], says that
for any ℱ{\mathcal{F}}, this trivial lower bound on pc​(ℱ)p_{c}({\mathcal{F}}) is close to the truth.



Conjecture 1.2.



There is a universal KK such that for every finite XX and increasing
ℱ⊆2X{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq 2^{X},





pc​(ℱ)≤K​q​(ℱ)​log⁡|X|.p_{c}({\mathcal{F}})\leq Kq({\mathcal{F}})\log|X|.







We should emphasize how strong this is
(from [17]: “It would probably be more sensible to conjecture that it is not true”).
For example, it easily implies—and was largely motivated by—Erdős–Rényi thresholds for
(a) perfect matchings in random rr-uniform hypergraphs,
and (b) appearance of a given bounded degree spanning tree in a random graph.
These have since been resolved: the first—Shamir’s Problem, circa 1980—in [15],
and the second—a mid-90’s suggestion of the second author—in [23].
Both arguments are difficult and specific to the problems they address (e.g. they are
utterly unrelated either to each other or to what we do here).
See Section 7 for more on these and other consequences.

the set system

Then

satisfies




(3)

q⁡(ℱ)≤qf​(ℱ)≤pc​(ℱ)q({\mathcal{F}})\leq q_{f}({\mathcal{F}})\leq p_{c}({\mathcal{F}})



(the first inequality is trivial and the second is similar to (2)),
and Talagrand [28, Conjectures 8.3 and 8.5] proposes a sort of LP relaxation of Conjecture 1.2,
and then a strengthening thereof.
The first of these, the following,
replaces qq by qfq_{f} in Conjecture 1.2; the second, which
adds replacement of |X||X| by the smaller ℓ⁡(ℱ)\ell({\mathcal{F}}), is our Theorem 1.1.



Conjecture 1.3.



There is a universal KK such that for every finite XX and increasing
ℱ⊆2X{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq 2^{X},





pc​(ℱ)≤K​qf​(ℱ)​log⁡|X|.p_{c}({\mathcal{F}})\leq Kq_{f}({\mathcal{F}})\log|X|.







Talagrand further suggests the following “very nice problem of combinatorics,” which implies
equivalence of Conjectures 1.2 and 1.3, as well as of
Theorem 1.1 and the corresponding strengthening of Conjecture 1.2.

There is a universal KK such that, for any increasing ℱ{\mathcal{F}} on a finite set XX,
q⁡(ℱ)≥qf​(ℱ)/K.q({\mathcal{F}})\geq q_{f}({\mathcal{F}})/K.





(That is,
weakly pp-small implies (p/K)(p/K)-small.)




Note the interest here is in Conjecture 1.4 for its own sake and as the most likely
route to Conjecture 1.2; all applications
of the latter that
we’re aware of follow
just as easily from Theorem 1.1.




Spread hypergraphs and spread measures.
In this paper a hypergraph on the (vertex) set XX is a collection ℋ{\mathcal{H}} of subsets of XX
(edges of ℋ{\mathcal{H}}),
with repeats allowed.
For S⊆XS\subseteq X, we use
⟨S⟩\langle S\rangle for {T⊆X:T⊇S}\{T\subseteq X:T\supseteq S\}, and for a hypergraph ℋ{\mathcal{H}} on XX, we write
⟨ℋ⟩\langle{\mathcal{H}}\rangle for ∪S∈ℋ⟨S⟩\cup_{S\in{\mathcal{H}}}\langle S\rangle.
We say ℋ{\mathcal{H}} is ℓ\ell-bounded (resp. ℓ\ell-uniform or an ℓ\ell-graph)
if each of its members
has size at most (resp. exactly) ℓ\ell,
and κ\kappa-spread if




(4)

|ℋ∩⟨S⟩|≤κ−|S|​|ℋ|∀S⊆X.|{\mathcal{H}}\cap\langle S\rangle|\leq\kappa^{-|S|}|{\mathcal{H}}|\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall S\subseteq X.



(Note that edges are counted with multiplicities on both sides of (4).)




A major advantage of the fractional versions (Conjecture 1.3 and Theorem 1.1)
over Conjecture 1.2—and the source of
the present relevance of [2]—is that they admit,
via linear programming duality, reformulations in which the specification of qf​(ℱ)q_{f}({\mathcal{F}})
gives a usable starting point. Following [28], we say
a probability measure ν\nu on 2X2^{X} is qq-spread
if





ν⁡(⟨S⟩)≤q|S|∀S⊆X.\nu(\langle S\rangle)\leq q^{|S|}\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall S\subseteq X.



Thus a hypergraph ℋ{\mathcal{H}} is κ\kappa-spread iff uniform measure on ℋ{\mathcal{H}} is
qq-spread with q=κ−1q=\kappa^{-1}.




As observed by Talagrand [28], the following is an easy consequence of duality.



Proposition 1.5.



For an increasing family ℱ{\mathcal{F}} on XX, if qf​(ℱ)≤qq_{f}({\mathcal{F}})\leq q, then there is a (2​q)(2q)-spread probability measure on 2X2^{X} supported on ℱ{\mathcal{F}}. ∎





This allows us to reduce Theorem 1.1 to the following alternate (actually, equivalent)
statement. In this paper with high probability (w.h.p.) means with probability tending to 1
as ℓ→∞\ell\rightarrow\infty.



Theorem 1.6.



There is a universal KK such that for any ℓ\ell-bounded, κ\kappa-spread hypergraph ℋ{\mathcal{H}} on XX, a uniformly random ((K​κ−1​log⁡ℓ)​|X|)((K\kappa^{-1}\log\ell)|X|)-element subset of XX belongs to ⟨ℋ⟩\langle{\mathcal{H}}\rangle w.h.p.





The easy reduction is given in Section 2.




Assignments.
Our second main result provides upper bounds on
the minima of a large class of hypergraph-based stochastic processes,
somewhat in the spirit of [27] (see also [26, 29]),
saying that in “smoother” settings, the logarithmic corrections of
Conjecture 1.3 and Theorem 1.1 are not needed.




For a hypergraph ℋ{\mathcal{H}} on XX,
let ξx\xi_{x} (x∈Xx\in X) be independent random variables, each uniform from [0,1][0,1],
and set




(5)

ξℋ=min⁡∑x∈SS∈ℋ⁡ξx\xi_{\mathcal{H}}=\min_{S\in{\mathcal{H}}}\sum_{x\in S}\xi_{x}



and Zℋ=𝔼⁡[ξℋ]Z_{\mathcal{H}}=\mathbb{E}[\xi_{\mathcal{H}}].



Theorem 1.7.



There is a universal KK such that for any ℓ\ell-bounded, κ\kappa-spread hypergraph ℋ{\mathcal{H}}, we have
Zℋ≤K​ℓ/κZ_{\mathcal{H}}\leq K\ell/\kappa, and
ξℋ≤K​ℓ/κ\xi_{\mathcal{H}}\leq K\ell/\kappa w.h.p.





These bounds are often tight (again up to the value of KK). The distribution of the ξx\xi_{x}’s is not very important;
e.g. it’s easy to see that
the same statement holds if they are Exp⁡(1)\Exp(1) random variables, as in the next example.




Theorem 1.7 was motivated by work of
Frieze and Sorkin [12] on the “axial” version of the
random d-dimensional assignment problem.
This asks (for fixed dd and large nn) for estimation of




(6)

ZdA(n)=𝔼[min∑x∈Sξx],Z_{d}^{A}(n)=\mathbb{E}\left[\min\sum_{x\in S}\xi_{x}\right],



where the ξx\xi_{x}’s (x∈X:=[n]dx\in X:=[n]^{d}) are independent Exp​(1)\textrm{Exp}(1) weights
and SS ranges over “axial assignments,” meaning S⊆XS\subseteq X
meets each axis-parallel hyperplane ({x∈X:xi=a}\{x\in X:x_{i}=a\} for some
i∈[d]i\in[d] and a∈[n]a\in[n]) exactly once.
For d=2d=2 this is classical; see [12] for its rather glorious history.
For d=3d=3 the deterministic version was one of Karp’s [18] original NP-complete
problems. Progress on the random version has been limited; see [12] for a guide
to the literature.




Frieze and Sorkin show (regarding bounds; they are also interested in algorithms)
that for suitable c1>0c_{1}>0 and c2c_{2},




(7)

c1​n−(d−2)<ZdA​(n)<c2​n−(d−2)​log⁡n.c_{1}n^{-(d-2)}<Z^{A}_{d}(n)<c_{2}n^{-(d-2)}\log n.



(The lower bound is easy and the upper bound follows from the
Shamir bound of [15].)




In present language, ZdA​(n)Z_{d}^{A}(n) is essentially
(that is, apart from the difference in the
distributions of the ξx\xi_{x}’s) ZℋZ_{\mathcal{H}}, with ℋ{\mathcal{H}} the set of perfect matchings of
the complete, balanced dd-uniform dd-partite hypergraph on d​ndn vertices
(that is, the collection of dd-sets meeting each of the pairwise disjoint nn-sets
V1,…,VdV_{1},\ldots,V_{d}).
This is easily seen to be κ\kappa-spread with κ=(n/e)d−1\kappa=(n/e)^{d-1} (apart from the nearly irrelevant dd-particity,
it is the ℋ{\mathcal{H}} of Shamir’s Problem), so the correct bound is an instance of
Theorem 1.7:



Corollary 1.8.



ZdA​(n)=Θ⁡(n−(d−2))Z_{d}^{A}(n)=\Theta(n^{-(d-2)}).





Frieze and Sorkin also considered the “planar” version of the problem, in which SS in (6)
meets each line ({x∈X:xj=yj​∀j≠i}\{x\in X:x_{j}=y_{j}\penalty\ \forall j\neq i\} for some
i∈[d]i\in[d] and y∈Xy\in X) exactly once; and one may of course
generalise from hyperplanes/lines to kk-dimensional “subspaces”
for a given k∈[d−1]k\in[d-1]. It’s easy to see what to expect here, and
one may hope Theorem 1.7 will eventually apply, but we at present lack the technology to say the relevant ℋ{\mathcal{H}}’s are suitably spread (see Section 8).

3. Main Lemma




Let γ\gamma be a slightly small
constant (e.g. γ=0.1\gamma=0.1 suffices), and let C0C_{0} be a
constant large enough to support
the estimates that follow. Let ℋ{\mathcal{H}} be an rr-bounded, κ\kappa-spread hypergraph on a set XX of size nn, with r,κ≥C02r,\kappa\geq C_{0}^{2}. Set p=C/κp=C/\kappa with C0≤C≤κ/C0C_{0}\leq C\leq\kappa/C_{0} (so p≤1/C0p\leq 1/C_{0}), r′=(1−γ)​rr^{\prime}=(1-\gamma)r and N=(nn​p)N=\binom{n}{np}. Finally, fix ψ:⟨ℋ⟩→ℋ\psi:\langle{\mathcal{H}}\rangle\rightarrow{\mathcal{H}} satisfying ψ⁡(Z)⊆Z\psi(Z)\subseteq Z for all Z∈⟨ℋ⟩Z\in\langle{\mathcal{H}}\rangle;
set, for W⊆XW\subseteq X and S∈ℋS\in{\mathcal{H}},

and say the pair (S,W)(S,W) is bad if |χ⁡(S,W)|>r′|\chi(S,W)|>r^{\prime} and good otherwise.




The heart of our argument is the following lemma (an improvement of [2, Lemma 5.7]),
regarding which a little orientation
may be helpful.
We will (in Theorems 1.6 and 1.7) be choosing a random subset of XX
in small increments
and would like to say we
are likely to be making good progress toward containing some S∈ℋS\in{\mathcal{H}}.
Of course such progress is not to be expected
for a typical SS, but this is not the goal:
having chosen a portion WW of our eventual set, we just need the
remainder to contain some S∖WS\setminus W, and may focus on those that are more
likely (basically meaning small).
The key idea (introduced in [2] and refined here) is that
a general S∖WS\setminus W, while not itself small, will,
in consequence of the spread assumption, typically contain some small S′∖WS^{\prime}\setminus W.
(In fact χ⁡(S,W)\chi(S,W) will usually be one of these: an S′∖WS^{\prime}\setminus W contained in S∖WS\setminus W will typically be small, so we don’t need to
steer this choice.)
We then replace each “good” S∖WS\setminus W by χ⁡(S,W)\chi(S,W) and iterate, a second nice feature
of the spread condition being that it is not much affected by this substitution.

7. Applications




Much of the significance of Theorem 1.1—and of the skepticism with which
Conjecture 1.2 was
viewed in [17]—derives from the strength of their
consequences, a few of which we discuss
(briefly) here.




For this discussion,
𝒦nr=(Vr){\mathcal{K}}^{r}_{n}=\binom{V}{r} is
the complete rr-graph on V=[n]V=[n],
and
ℋn,pr{\mathcal{H}}^{r}_{n,p} is the rr-uniform counterpart of the usual binomial random graph Gn,pG_{n,p}.
Given r,nr,n and an rr-graph HH, we use 𝒢H{\mathcal{G}}_{H} for the collection of
(unlabeled) copies of HH in 𝒦nr{\mathcal{K}}^{r}_{n} and ℱH{\mathcal{F}}_{H}
for ⟨𝒢H⟩\langle{\mathcal{G}}_{H}\rangle. As usual, Δ\Delta is maximum degree.




As noted earlier, Conjecture 1.2 was motivated especially by Shamir’s Problem (since
resolved in [15]), and the conjecture that became Montgomery’s theorem [23].
Very briefly:
for nn running over multiples of a given (fixed) rr, Shamir’s Problem asks for
estimation of pc​(ℱH)p_{c}({\mathcal{F}}_{H}) when HH is a perfect matching (n/rn/r disjoint edges),
and [15] proves the natural conjecture that this threshold is Θ⁡(n−(r−1)​log⁡n)\Theta(n^{-(r-1)}\log n);
and [23] shows that for fixed dd, the threshold for Gn,pG_{n,p} to contain
a given nn-vertex tree with maximum degree dd is Θ⁡(n−1​log⁡n)\Theta(n^{-1}\log n),
where the implied constant in the upper bound depends on dd (though it probably shouldn’t).
See [15, 23] for some account of the history of these problems.
In both cases—and in most of the other examples mentioned following Theorem 7.1 (all but the one from [20])—the
lower bounds derive from the coupon-collectorish requirement that the (hyper)edges cover
the vertices, and it is the upper bounds that are of interest.




In fact, Theorem 1.1 gives not just Montgomery’s theorem, but its natural extension
to rr-graphs and more.
(Strictly speaking, Montgomery proves more than the original conjecture—see
Section 8—and we are not so far recovering this stronger result.)
Say an rr-graph FF is a forest if it contains no cycle, meaning distinct vertices
v1,…,vkv_{1},\ldots,v_{k} and distinct edges e1,…,eke_{1},\ldots,e_{k} such that vi−1,vi∈eiv_{i-1},v_{i}\in e_{i} ∀i\forall i
(with subscripts mod kk).
A spanning tree is then a forest of size (n−1)/(r−1)(n-1)/(r-1).
For a (general) rr-graph FF, let ρ⁡(F)\rho(F) be the maximum size of a forest in FF and set





φ⁡(F)=max⁡{1−ρ⁡(F′)/|F′|:∅≠F′⊆F}.\varphi(F)=\max\{1-\rho(F^{\prime})/|F^{\prime}|:\emptyset\neq F^{\prime}\subseteq F\}.

8. Concluding remarks




In closing we briefly mention (or recall) a few unresolved issues related to the present work.




A.
First, of course, it would be nice to prove Conjecture 1.4, which is now equivalent to Conjecture 1.2.




B.
It would be interesting to understand whether, in Shamir’s and related problems,
the log⁡ℓ\log\ell emerging from our argument somehow reflects the coupon-collector
requirement (edges cover vertices) that drives the lower bounds.
Partly as a way of testing this, one might try to see if
the present machinery can be extended to apply directly
(rather than via [24, 13]) to
questions where coupon-collector considerations (correctly) predict a smaller gap,
as in the fractional powers of log⁡n\log n in Theorem 7.3.




C.
The arguments of [23] and [9] give stronger
“universality” results; e.g. [23] says that the
appropriate Gn,pG_{n,p} w.h.p. contains every tree respecting the degree bound.
Whether this can be proved along present lines remains unclear;
if so, it would seem to be more a question of managing some understanding of the class
of universal graphs (with, of course, a view to the spread)
than of extending Theorem 1.1.




D.
As mentioned following Corollary 1.8, what prevents us from extending
to other values of the dimension kk is inadequate control of the spread.
(Here it doesn’t really matter whether we think of “assignments” or of the threshold for
containing a member of the ℋ{\mathcal{H}} in (5).)
The difficulty is the same for the related problem of thresholds for existence of
designs. We don’t have anything to suggest in the way of a remedy and just indicate one issue,
for simplicity sticking to Steiner triple systems (STS’s; see [30] for background);
thus X=𝒦n3X={\mathcal{K}}^{3}_{n}
(with n≡1n\equiv 1 or 33 (mod6)\pmod{6}), ℋ{\mathcal{H}} is the hypergraph of STS’s,
and for the spread (which should be Θ⁡(1/n)\Theta(1/n)),
we may take

Results of Linial and Luria [21] (upper bound) and Keevash [19] (lower bound)
give




(31)

Viewed enumeratively this is very satisfactory, having been an old conjecture of Wilson [31]. But for present purposes, even ignoring our weaker understanding
of |ℋ∩⟨S⟩||{\mathcal{H}}\cap\langle S\rangle| (the number of completions of a partial STS SS), it
is not enough: even if this quantity is, as one expects, roughly (n/e2)n2/6−|S|(n/e^{2})^{n^{2}/6-|S|},
