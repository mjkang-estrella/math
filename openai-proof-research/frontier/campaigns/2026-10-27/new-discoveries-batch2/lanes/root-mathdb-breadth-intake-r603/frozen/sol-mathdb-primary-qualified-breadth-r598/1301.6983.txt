Abstract
    

The replication of a set W of vertices of a graph is the operation
that adds a copy of each vertex w in W, one at a time, and
connects it to w and all its neighbours. We disprove the
conjecture of Francisco et al. [Discrete Math. 310 (2010),
2176–2182] that for every positive integer k and every
k-critical (i.e., critically k-chromatic) graph, there is a set
W of vertices whose replication produces a (k+1)-critical
graph. Furthermore, we give a simple direct proof of the result of
Francisco et al. that the conjecture holds for graphs whose
fractional chromatic number exceeds k-1.

1 Introduction




In the area of graph colourings, constructions and properties of
colour-critical graphs are a classical subject (see, e.g.,
[1, Section 14.2]). One of the operations that are
relevant in this context is the replication of a set of vertices whose
definition we will recall shortly. This operation is also of central
importance for the theory of perfect graphs (cf. [8, Chapter
65]). In this paper, we study a problem posed by
Francisco et al. [2] regarding replication in
colour-critical graphs.




For the terminology and notation of graph theory, we follow Bondy and
Murty [1]. We deal with graphs without parallel edges and
loops. The vertex set and the edge set of a graph G are denoted by
V(G) and E(G), respectively.




A graph G is k-chromatic if its chromatic number is k. It
is k-critical if G is k-chromatic and G-v is
(k-1)-colourable for each vertex v of G. Furthermore, G is
k-edge-critical if G is k-chromatic and every proper
subgraph of G is (k-1)-colourable.




Replicating (also duplicating) a vertex w\in V(G)
means adding a copy (or clone) w^{\prime} of w and making it
adjacent to w and all its neighbours. To replicate a set W\subseteq V(G), we replicate each vertex w\in W in sequence. The resulting
graph G^{W} is independent of the order in which the individual
vertices are replicated.




Francisco et al. [2] studied replication from the
point of view of commutative algebra. They posed the following
conjecture:



Conjecture 1.



For any positive integer k and any k-critical graph G, there
is a set W\subseteq V(G) such that G^{W} is (k+1)-critical.





In Section 3 of the present paper, we disprove
the conjecture by showing that each member of an infinite family of
4-critical graphs constructed by Gallai [3] is a
counterexample.




In [2], it is proved by algebraic means that
Conjecture 1 holds for k-critical graphs G with
fractional chromatic number \chi_{f}(G)>k-1 (see
Section 2 for the definition):



Theorem 2.



Let G be a k-critical graph such that \chi_{f}(G)>k-1. Then
there exists a set W such that G^{W} is (k+1)-critical.





In Section 2, we provide an alternative direct
proof.





2 Fractional chromatic number




Let \mathcal{I} be the set of all inclusion-wise maximal independent sets of
a graph G. Recall that a fractional colouring w of a graph
G is any mapping w:\,\mathcal{I}\to[0,1] such that for each vertex v
of G,




\sum_{v\in I\in\mathcal{I}}w(I)\geq 1.



The weight \left|{w}\right| of a fractional colouring w is defined
as the sum of w(I) over all I\in\mathcal{I}. The fractional
chromatic number \chi_{f}(G) of G is the infimum of the weights
of all fractional colourings, which is in fact a minimum (i.e., the
value is attained). Since an ordinary colouring with k colours
determines a fractional colouring of weight k, we see that
\chi_{f}(G)\leq\chi(G). In general, \chi(G) is not bounded by any
function of \chi_{f}(G). More information on fractional colouring can
be found in [7].




Using the theory of edge and cover ideals of graphs, Francisco et
al. [2] prove that Conjecture 1 holds
if \chi_{f}(G) is ‘close’ to \chi(G)
(Theorem 2). In the present section, we show that
Theorem 2 is an easy consequence of the linear
programming duality.




First note that by the definition, \chi_{f}(G) is the optimum solution
to a linear program. By the duality theorem, it is equal to the
optimum solution to the dual linear program, which in this case is the
fractional clique number \omega_{f}(G). It is defined as
follows. A fractional clique in G is a function c:\,V(G)\to[0,1] such that for each (maximal) independent set I, we
have c(I)\leq 1, where c(I) is defined as \sum_{v\in I}c(v).
The weight \left|{c}\right| of c is defined as c(V(G)). By
definition, \omega_{f}(G) is the maximum weight of a fractional clique
in G. As mentioned above, the duality of linear programming implies
that




\chi_{f}(G)=\omega_{f}(G).






It will be convenient to use an observation of Francisco et al.
([2, Lemma 3.1]):



Lemma 3.



If G is a k-critical graph and W a maximal independent set
such that G^{W} is (k+1)-chromatic, then G^{W} is
(k+1)-critical.





In view of Lemma 3, Theorem 2 is an
immediate consequence of the following proposition:



Proposition 4.



For any k-chromatic graph G with \chi_{f}(G)>k-1, there is a
maximal independent set W such that G^{W} is
(k+1)-chromatic.




Proof.



Let c be a fractional clique in G with \left|{c}\right|=\omega_{f}(G). We may assume that G is not a complete graph
(otherwise the statement is trivial). Clearly, there is a vertex v
with c(v)<1. It must be contained in a maximal independent set
W with c(W)=1, since otherwise we could increase c(v) by a
sufficiently small amount and obtain a fractional clique of larger
weight.




We prove that G^{W} is (k+1)-chromatic. Since W is
independent, we obviously have \chi(G^{W})\leq k+1, so it is
sufficient to prove the opposite inequality. Let c_{1} be the
extension of c to G^{W} obtained by setting, for each vertex
w\in W, c_{1}(w^{\prime})=c(w), where w^{\prime} denotes the clone of w in
G^{W}.




We claim that c_{1} is a fractional clique. Let I_{1} be a maximal
independent set in G^{W}. For each w\in W, I_{1} contains at
most one vertex from \left\{{w,w^{\prime}}\right\}. Let I be the set obtained by
replacing each vertex w^{\prime}\in I (where w\in W) by w. Thus,
I\subseteq V(G), I is a (maximal) independent set of G, and
c_{1}(I_{1})=c_{1}(I)=c(I)\leq 1. Since the choice of I_{1} was
arbitrary, c_{1} is a fractional clique. We have




\displaystyle\chi(G^{W})
\displaystyle\geq\chi_{f}(G^{W})=\omega_{f}(G^{W})\geq\left|{c_{1}}\right|=\left|{c}\right|+c(W)=\omega_{f}(G)+1=\chi_{f}(G)+1




\displaystyle>\chi(G),



the last inequality being our assumption on G. The proof is complete.
∎






3 A counterexample




Gallai [3] constructed an infinite family of
4-regular 4-edge-critical graphs, which was the first construction of
k-edge-critical graphs without vertices of degree k-1. The
definition can be expressed as follows.




For a positive integer n, let [n] denote the set
\left\{{0,\dots,n-1}\right\}. Let P_{n} be a path with vertex set [n], with vertices in the increasing order along P_{n}. Let K_{3}
be the complete graph whose vertex set is the group \mathbb{Z}_{3}.




For n\geq 4, we define H_{n} as the graph obtained from the
Cartesian product P_{n}\Box K_{3} by adding the three edges
joining (0,j) to (n-1,-j) for j\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}. (See
Figure 1a.)




The 4-regular graphs H_{n} are interesting in various ways; for
instance, they embed in the Klein bottle as quadrangulations
(cf. Figure 1b). In this section, we show that Gallai’s
graphs are counterexamples to Conjecture 1:



Theorem 5.



For any n\geq 4 and any W\subseteq V(H_{n}), the graph H_{n}^{W} is
not 5-critical.





Since the graphs H_{n} contain triangles, their fractional chromatic
number is at least 3. By Theorems 2 and 5
it follows that \chi_{f}(H_{n})=3, so these graphs show that the
fractional chromatic number bound in Theorem 2 is
tight.




We will divide the proof of Theorem 5 into two
parts. First, we show that for certain sets W, the chromatic number
of H_{n}^{W} is at least 5, but H_{n}^{W} is not 5-critical
(Lemma 6). We then prove that for any other set W,
H_{n}^{W} is 4-chromatic (Proposition 12).




Let i\in[n] and j\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}. The i-th column of H_{n}
is the set C_{i}=\left\{{i}\right\}\times\mathbb{Z}_{3}. Similarly, the j-th
row of H_{n} is R_{j}=[n]\times\left\{{j}\right\}. The vertex in
C_{i}\cap R_{j} is denoted by v_{i,j}. In accordance with the notation
introduced above, the clone of v_{i,j}\in W in H_{n}^{W} is
denoted by v^{\prime}_{i,j}.




We introduce notation for certain subgraphs of H_{n}^{W}. Let
i\in[n]. We define X_{i} as the clique in H_{n}^{W} on
the vertices in C_{i} and their clones. Furthermore, Y_{i} is the
induced subgraph of H_{n}^{W} on V(X_{i})\cup V(X_{i+1}) (addition
modulo n).

(b)

Lemma 6.



Let n\geq 4 and let W\subseteq V(H_{n}). In each of the following
cases, the graph H_{n}^{W} has chromatic number at least 5 and
is not 5-critical:


 (a) 



there is some i\in[n] such that the set W\cap C_{i} has
size at least 2,


 (b) 



W contains at least n-1 vertices of R_{0} and n is odd,


 (c) 



the induced subgraph of H_{n} on W-R_{0} contains a path with
at least n vertices and n is even.






Proof.



(a) Suppose that W\cap C_{i} has size at least 2, so \left|{V(X_{i})}\right|\geq 5. Since H_{n}^{W} contains the clique X_{i} as a proper
subgraph, it is neither 4-colourable nor 5-critical.




(b) Without loss of generality, assume that W contains
R_{0}-\left\{{v_{n-1,0}}\right\}. For contradiction, let c be a 4-colouring
of H_{n}^{W}. By symmetry, the vertices v_{0,0} and v^{\prime}_{0,0}
may be assumed to have colours 1 and 2 in c. This forces the pairs
of colours assigned to v_{i,0} and v^{\prime}_{i,0} alternate between
\left\{{1,2}\right\} and \left\{{3,4}\right\} as i increases. Hence, v_{n-1,0}
has neighbours of all four colours, a contradiction which shows that
H_{n}^{W} is not 4-colourable. Because the argument involves
only vertices in R_{0} and their clones, it implies that, say,
H_{n}^{W}-v_{0,2} is not 4-colourable. It follows that
H_{n}^{W} is not 5-critical.




(c) By symmetry, we may assume that R_{1}\subseteq W. Consider a
4-colouring of H_{n}^{W}. An argument similar to the one used in
part (b) implies that the vertices v_{0,1}, v^{\prime}_{0,1}, v_{n-1,1}
and v^{\prime}_{n-1,1} have distinct colours. Since they have a common
neighbour v_{n-1,2}, we obtain a contradiction. In the same manner
as above, it follows that H_{n}^{W} is not 5-critical.
∎




Lemma 7.



If W\subseteq H_{n} satisfies none of the conditions (a)–(c) in
Lemma 6, then there is a set Z such that W\subseteq Z\subseteq V(H_{n}), Z contains exactly one vertex from each C_{i}
(i\in[n]) and Z still satisfies none of (a)–(c).




Proof.



Since W does not satisfy condition (a), it contains at most one
vertex from each set C_{i} (i\in[n]). Suppose that W\cap C_{i}=\emptyset for some i. We claim that conditions (a)–(c) are
still violated for the set W\cup\left\{{w}\right\}, for some w\in C_{i}. If
W\cup\left\{{v_{i,0}}\right\} satisfies any of the conditions, it must be
condition (b), which means that n is odd. In that case,
W\cup\left\{{v_{i,1}}\right\} trivially fails to satisfy the conditions. By
adding further vertices in this way, we arrive at a set Z with the
desired properties.
∎





Before we embark on the proof of Proposition 12, it will be
convenient to introduce some terminology. Assume that W\subseteq V(H_{n})
is a set which satisfies none of the conditions in
Lemma 6. In addition, we will assume that




W intersects
each C_{i} (i\in[n]) in exactly one vertex.

(1)


For each i\in[n], we will define w_{i} to be the unique element
of \mathbb{Z}_{3} such that W\cap C_{i}=\left\{{v_{i,w_{i}}}\right\}. (In the proof of
Proposition 12 below, we will ensure
condition (1) by appealing to Lemma 7.)




We will encode the set W into a sequence of signs, defined as
follows. A sign sequence \sigma is a sequence of elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{3}. We will often write ‘+’ for the element 1 and ‘-’ for the
element 2 (which coincides with -1). Thus, the sign sequence
(\hskip 0.8pt\mathord{0}\mathord{+}\mathord{-}\mathord{+}) stands for the sequence (0,1,2,1).




To the set W, we assign the sign sequence \sigma^{W}=s_{0}\dots s_{n-1}, where each s_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{3} is defined as




\displaystyle s_{i}=\begin{cases}w_{i+1}-w_{i}&\text{if $0\leq i\leq n-2$,}\\
-w_{0}-w_{n-1}&\text{if $i=n-1$.}\end{cases}



The change of sign in the latter case reflects the fact that the
vertex v_{n-1,j} is adjacent to v_{0,-j} rather than v_{0,j}. It
may be helpful to view H_{n} as the graph obtained from the Cartesian
product P_{n+1}\Box K_{3} by identifying the vertex (0,j) with
(n,-j) for each j\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}. It is then natural to define w_{n}=-w_{0}, in which case s_{n-1} is precisely w_{n}-w_{n-1}.




To describe a 4-colouring of the clique X_{i} in H_{n}^{W}
(i\in[n]), we introduce the notion of a pattern. This is
a cyclically ordered partition of the set \left\{{1,2,3,4}\right\} into three
parts, with one part of size 2 and the remaining parts of size
1. The two colours contained in the part of size 2 are
paired. Two patterns differing only by a cyclic shift of the
parts are regarded as identical. Given a 4-colouring c of X_{i}, the
corresponding pattern at X_{i} is




\pi_{i}(c)=\Bigl(\left\{{c(v_{i,w_{i}}),c(v^{\prime}_{i,w_{i}})}\right\},\left\{{c(v_{i,w_{i}+1})}\right\},\left\{{c(v_{i,w_{i}+2})}\right\}\Bigr).



We use a more concise notation for patterns: for instance, instead of
writing (\left\{{1,2}\right\},\left\{{3}\right\},\left\{{4}\right\}) we write just
12\cdot3\cdot4. Note that a pattern does not determine the colouring
uniquely since it does not specify the order of the paired colours.




We now determine the possible combinations of patterns at X_{i} and at
X_{i+1} in a valid colouring of Y_{i}. Suppose that c_{0} is a
colouring of X_{0} with pattern 12\cdot3\cdot4, and let s=w_{1}-w_{0}. Consider first the case that s=1. It is routine to check
that for any valid extension of c_{0} to Y_{0}, the pattern at X_{1}
is 12\cdot3\cdot4, 14\cdot2\cdot3 or 24\cdot1\cdot3
(cf. Figure 2). Conversely, each of these patterns
determines a valid extension.










Figure 2: Valid colourings of Y_{0} such that the pattern at X_{0} is
12\cdot3\cdot4. The colouring of Y_{0} is represented in the
induced subgraph of H_{n} on C_{0}\cup C_{1} by assigning a pair of
colours to each vertex in W. These vertices are shown as
circles, the other vertices as solid dots.




Considering the other possibilities for s, we find that the sets of
patterns at X_{1} corresponding to valid extensions of c_{0} are as
follows:



12\cdot3\cdot4
14\cdot2\cdot3
24\cdot1\cdot3
    if s=1,

12\cdot3\cdot4
13\cdot4\cdot2
23\cdot4\cdot1
    if s=-1,

34\cdot1\cdot2
34\cdot2\cdot1

    if s=0.



The patterns in the first row of the above table are said to be
+-compatible with 12\cdot3\cdot4. The notions of
--compatibility and 0-compatibility are defined in
a similar way using the second and third row, respectively. Applying
a suitable permutation to the set of colours, we can extend these
definitions to any other pattern in place of 12\cdot3\cdot4.




The same discussion applies just as well to patterns at X_{i} and
X_{i+1}, where 1\leq i\leq n-2. For i=n-1, we need to take into
account the ‘twist’ in Y_{n-1}. We find that for a valid colouring
of Y_{n-1}, the pattern \pi induced at X_{n-1} and the pattern
\rho induced at X_{0} have the property that \overline{\rho} is
s^{\prime}-compatible with \pi, where \overline{\rho} is the
reverse of \rho, i.e., the pattern obtained by reversing the
order of parts in \rho, and s^{\prime}=-w_{0}-w_{n-1}.




There is a simple description of the patterns that are +-compatible
with a given pattern \pi=\mbox{$xy$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$z$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$w$}. One of them is \pi
itself. To obtain the other ones, choose a colour that is paired in
\pi (x or y) and move it to the preceding part of \pi with
respect to the cyclic ordering. Reversing the direction of the move,
we obtain the --compatible patterns. Finally, to obtain the two
0-compatible patterns, merge the two colours that are unpaired in
\pi into one part, and put the other two colours into two parts,
choosing any of the two possible orderings.




We represent the notion of compatibility of patterns using an
auxiliary graph D, in which we allow both directed and undirected
edges as well as directed loops. The vertex set of D is the set of
all 12 patterns. Patterns \pi and \rho are joined by an
undirected edge if they are 0-compatible. There is a directed edge
from \pi to \rho if \rho is +-compatible with \pi (or
equivalently, if \pi is --compatible with \rho). In
particular, D has a directed loop on each vertex. The graph D is
shown in Figure 3 (with the loops omitted).


Figure 3: The auxiliary graph D. Directed loops at all the
vertices are not shown.




Let \sigma=s_{0}\dots s_{k} be a sign sequence. A
\sigma-stroll S is a sequence \pi_{0}\pi_{1}\dots\pi_{k+1},
where each \pi_{i} (0\leq i\leq k+1) is a vertex of D and one of
the following conditions holds for each j (0\leq j\leq k):


• 



s_{j}=0 and D contains an undirected edge with endvertices
\pi_{j} and \pi_{j+1},


• 



s_{j}=1 and there is a directed edge from \pi_{j} to
\pi_{j+1},


• 



s_{j}=-1 and there is a directed edge from \pi_{j+1} to
\pi_{j}.



For s_{j}=\pm 1, the directed edge is allowed to be a loop,
reflecting the fact that a pattern is both +-compatible and
--compatible with itself. A \sigma-stroll as above is said to
start at \pi_{0} and end at \pi_{k+1} (or to be a \sigma-stroll
from \pi_{0} to \pi_{k+1}).




To illustrate the definition, if \sigma=(\mathord{-}\mathord{-}\mathord{+}\mathord{0}\mathord{+}\mathord{-}),
then a \sigma-stroll from 12\cdot3\cdot4 to 12\cdot4\cdot3 is




(\mbox{$12$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$3$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$4$},\mbox{$13$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$4$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$2$},\mbox{$34$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$2$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$1$},\mbox{$14$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$3$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$2$},\mbox{$23$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$4$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$1$},\mbox{$13$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$2$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$4$},\mbox{$12$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$4$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$3$}).






A sign sequence \sigma is said to be reversing if there is a
\sigma-stroll from 12\cdot3\cdot4 to 34\cdot1\cdot2. Note that by
interchanging colours 1 and 2, one can then obtain a \sigma-stroll
from 12\cdot3\cdot4 to 34\cdot2\cdot1 as well. Furthermore, \sigma is
good if there exists a \sigma-stroll from 12\cdot3\cdot4 to
12\cdot4\cdot3. The latter terminology is justified by the following
lemma.



Lemma 8.



If \sigma^{W} is good, then the graph H_{n}^{W} is 4-colourable.




Proof.



Let \sigma^{W}=s_{0}\dots s_{n-1} and let S=\pi_{0}\dots\pi_{n} be a
\sigma^{W}-stroll from 12\cdot3\cdot4 to 12\cdot4\cdot3. For each
i=0,\dots,n-1, colour the vertices of X_{i} in such a way that the
pattern is \pi_{i}. By the definition, each \pi_{i} (0\leq i\leq n-2) is s_{i}-compatible with \pi_{i+1}, and so Y_{i} is properly
coloured.




It remains to check the colouring of Y_{n-1}. As observed above,
Y_{n-1} is properly coloured if the reverse of \pi_{0} (that is,
12\cdot4\cdot3) is s_{n-1}-compatible with \pi_{n-1}. This is
ensured by the requirement that S ends at 12\cdot4\cdot3.
∎





For a sign sequence \sigma, we define -\sigma to be the sign
sequence obtained by replacing each - sign by + and vice versa.



Lemma 9.



If \sigma is good, then -\sigma is good.




Proof.



By inspecting Figure 3 or directly from the definition,
one can see that if D contains a directed edge from \pi to
\rho, then it also contains a directed edge from \overline{\rho}
to \overline{\pi}, and a similar claim holds for the undirected
edges. It follows that if S=(\pi_{0},\dots,\pi_{k}) is a
\sigma-stroll, then \overline{S}=(\overline{\pi_{0}},\dots,\overline{\pi_{k}}) is a
(-\sigma)-stroll. If S is good, then \overline{S} starts at
12\cdot4\cdot3 and ends at 12\cdot3\cdot4. Interchanging colours 3
and 4 in each pattern in \overline{S}, we obtain a
(-\sigma)-stroll from 12\cdot3\cdot4 to 12\cdot4\cdot3.
∎





Let \sigma=s_{0}\dots s_{k-1} be a sign sequence and let \pi and
\rho be patterns such that \pi\rho is an undirected edge of
D. We define a \sigma-stroll S(\sigma;\pi,\rho)=\pi_{0}\dots\pi_{k} by the following rule:


• 



\pi_{0}=\pi,


• 



if s_{i}\neq 0, then \pi_{i+1}=\pi_{i} (where 0\leq i\leq k-1),


• 



if s_{i}=0, then \pi_{i+1} is the vertex in
\left\{{\pi,\rho}\right\} distinct from \pi_{i} (where 0\leq i\leq k-1).






Let \sigma_{1} and \sigma_{2} be sign sequences and let \sigma be
their concatenation. If P=(\pi_{0},\dots,\pi_{k}) is a
\sigma_{1}-stroll and R=(\rho_{0},\dots,\rho_{\ell}) is a
\sigma_{2}-stroll such that \pi_{k}=\rho_{0}, then the
composition of P and R is the \sigma-stroll




P\circ R=(\pi_{0},\dots,\pi_{k},\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{\ell}).






For any sign sequence \sigma, we let z(\sigma) denote the number
of occurrences of the symbol 0 in \sigma, reduced modulo 2. For
clarity, we omit one pair of parentheses in expressions such as
z((\hskip 0.8pt\mathord{0}\mathord{+}\mathord{-})).



Observation 10.



Let \sigma be a sign sequence and \pi,\rho be patterns such that
\pi\rho is an undirected edge of D. Then the \sigma-stroll
S(\sigma;\pi,\rho) starting at \pi satisfies the following:


 (i) 



if z(\sigma)=0, then S(\sigma;\pi,\rho) ends at \pi,


 (ii) 



otherwise, S(\sigma;\pi,\rho) ends at \rho.







We define an order \preceq on sign sequences. Let \tau,\sigma be
two sign sequences, where \sigma=s_{0}\dots s_{k}. We define \tau\preceq\sigma if there are indices 0\leq i_{0}<i_{1}\dots<i_{m}\leq k
such that:


• 



\tau=s_{i_{0}}s_{i_{1}}\dots s_{i_{m}},


• 



z(s_{0}s_{1}\dots s_{i_{0}-1})=0, and


• 



for every j such that 0\leq j\leq m-1, z(s_{i_{j}+1}\dots s_{i_{j+1}-1})=0.





Lemma 11.



Let \sigma and \tau be sign sequences such that
\tau\preceq\sigma. The following holds:


 (i) 



if z(\sigma)=z(\tau) and \tau is good, then \sigma is good,


 (ii) 



if z(\sigma)\neq z(\tau) and \tau is reversing, then
\sigma is good.






Proof.



(i) Suppose that \sigma=s_{0}\dots s_{k} and \tau=s_{i_{0}}\dots s_{i_{m}}, where 0\leq i_{0}<\dots<i_{m}\leq k. Let
S=(\rho_{0},\dots,\rho_{m}) be a \tau-stroll from 12\cdot3\cdot4 to
12\cdot4\cdot3. For simplicity, set i_{-1}=-1 and for j=0,\dots,m
let \sigma^{j} be the subsequence of \sigma from s_{i_{j-1}+1}
to s_{i_{j}-1}. For each j, 0\leq j\leq m, choose a pattern
\epsilon_{j} such that \rho_{j}\epsilon_{j} is an undirected edge of
D.




By the definition of the order \preceq, we have z(\sigma^{j})=0
for each j, 0\leq j\leq m. Observation 10(i)
implies that S(\sigma^{j};\rho_{j},\epsilon_{j}) is a \sigma^{j}-stroll from
\rho_{j} to \rho_{j}. Thus, the composition




\displaystyle S^{\prime}=S(\sigma^{0};\rho_{0},\epsilon_{0})
\displaystyle\circ(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\circ S(\sigma^{1};\rho_{1},\epsilon_{1})\circ(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\circ\cdots




\displaystyle\circ S(\sigma^{m-1};\rho_{m-1},\epsilon_{m-1})\circ(\rho_{m-1},\rho_{m})



is a valid \sigma-stroll from 12\cdot3\cdot4 to 12\cdot4\cdot3.




Let \sigma^{m+1} denote the sequence s_{i_{m}+1}\dots s_{k}. Then




z(\sigma^{m+1})\equiv z(\sigma)-z(\tau)\pmod{2}.

(2)


By (2), z(\sigma^{m+1})=0 and so
S^{\prime\prime}:=S(\sigma^{m+1};\mbox{$12$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$4$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$3$},\mbox{$12$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$3$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$4$}) is a
\sigma^{m+1}-stroll from 12\cdot4\cdot3 to 12\cdot4\cdot3 by
Observation 10(i). The \sigma-stroll S^{\prime}\circ S^{\prime\prime} then shows that \sigma is good.




The proof of (ii) is similar, except that S is now a \tau-stroll
from 12\cdot3\cdot4 to 34\cdot1\cdot2. Furthermore, z(\sigma^{m+1})=1 and S^{\prime\prime}:=S(\sigma^{m+1};\mbox{$34$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$1$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$2$},\mbox{$12$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$4$\hskip 0.5pt$\cdot$\hskip 0.5pt$3$}) is a
\sigma^{m+1}-stroll from 34\cdot1\cdot2 to
12\cdot4\cdot3. Composing S^{\prime} and S^{\prime\prime}, we obtain a
\sigma-stroll from 12\cdot3\cdot4 to 12\cdot4\cdot3 as required.
∎




Proposition 12.



Let n\geq 4 and W\subseteq V(H_{n}). If none of the conditions
(a)–(c) in Lemma 6 is satisfied, then H_{n}^{W}
is 4-colourable.




Proof.



By Lemma 7, there is a set Z such that W\subseteq Z and
Z intersects each set C_{i} in precisely one vertex. Since
H_{n}^{Z} contains H_{n}^{W} as a subgraph, it is
sufficient to prove the proposition under the
assumption (1).




Let us therefore assume that (1) holds for W, so the
ensuing discussion applies. We retain its notation and
definitions. By analyzing several cases, we will show that
\sigma^{W} is good, so the 4-colourability of H_{n}^{W} follows
from Lemma 8. For the sake of a contradiction, suppose
that \sigma^{W} is not good.

∎








Theorem 5 is now an immediate consequence of
Lemma 6 and Proposition 12.




In conclusion, we remark that a computer search using a list of
edge-critical graphs [5] has shown that the graph H_{4} is the
only counterexample to Conjecture 1 among edge-critical
graphs on up to 12 vertices.
