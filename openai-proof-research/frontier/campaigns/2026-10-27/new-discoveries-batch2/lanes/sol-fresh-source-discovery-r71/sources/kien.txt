ARITHMETIC PSEUDO-FROBENIUS NUMBERS AND DETERMINANTAL NUMERICAL SEMIGROUP RINGS


DO VAN KIEN

Address: Department of Mathematics, Hanoi Pedagogical University 2, Xuan Hoa, Phu Tho, Vietnam

Email address: dovankien@hpu2.edu.vn



Abstract.
    

Let H=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle be a numerical semigroup. A conjecture on numerical semigroup rings predicts that the defining ideal I_{H} has a determinantal presentation if and only if the set of pseudo-Frobenius numbers of H has form \{h+\alpha,h+2\alpha,\ldots,h+(n-1)\alpha\} for h\geq 0,\alpha>0. We prove this conjecture under the condition that h has a unique factorization in H. We also give characterization this
unique-factorization condition. As an application, we consider the family of affine-orbit numerical semigroups determined by m=cA_{n-1}+1, 1\leq c\leq a. We prove that the relevant element h has a unique factorization and obtain
    





I_{H}=\operatorname{I}_{2}\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}^{a}&x_{2}^{a}&\cdots&X_{n-1}^{a}&X_{n}^{c}\\
X_{2}&x_{3}&\cdots&X_{n}&X_{1}^{d+c+1-a}\end{pmatrix}.


    

In particular, the Eagon–Northcott complex of the matrix mentioned above gives the graded minimal free resolution of k[H].
  



Key words and phrases: Numerical semigroup, Pseudo-Frobenius number, Defining ideal,
Determinantal ideal, Affine map, Eagon–Northcott complex


2020 Mathematics Subject ClassificationPrimary 13D02; Secondary 13A02, 20M25



1. Introduction



Let H=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle
be a numerical semigroup minimally generated by n\geq 3 positive integers, and let k[H]=k[t^{a_{1}},\ldots,t^{a_{n}}]
be its semigroup ring over a field k. Let
S=k[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}] be the weighted polynomial ring with \deg X_{i}=a_{i} for every 1\leq i\leq n and consider the graded k-algebra homomorphism \varphi:S\longrightarrow k[H] given by
\varphi(X_{i})=t^{a_{i}} for all i. The ideal I_{H}=\operatorname{Ker}\varphi is called the defining ideal of the numerical semigroup ring k[H]. Determining the structure of I_{H} is a classical problem in the study of monomial curves and numerical semigroup rings. There are a few known results about the structure of I_{H}. The most important result is due to J. Herzog [11] when n=3, but for semigroups with
more generators, the situation becomes significantly more complex. When n=4, there are some partial answers to describe the structure of the defining ideal: e.g. symmetric semigroups ([3]), pseudo-symmetric semigroups ([14]), and almost symmetric semigroups ([7]). For n(\geq 4)-generated cases, research has primarily focused on specific classes, such as those generated by arithmetic sequences [8] or repunit numbers [2, 16], making a comprehensive understanding still unattainable.



In general, explicitly determining I_{H} presents substantial difficulty, even when its numerical invariants have been established. A particularly useful numerical invariant is the set of pseudo-Frobenius numbers





\operatorname{PF}(H)=\{f\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus H\mid f+s\in H\text{ for every }0<s\in H\}.



These numbers determine the degrees of the minimal generators of the canonical module of k[H]; in particular, the Cohen-Macaulay type \rm{r}(k[H]) of k[H] is equal to \sharp\operatorname{PF}(H) (see [10]). Motivated by the interaction between pseudo-Frobenius numbers and defining ideals established in [9, 12], the following
conjecture was formulated by the author together with D. T. Cuong, N. Matsuoka, and H. L Truong (see [13, Conjecture 1.1]).



Conjecture 1.1.


Let H=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle be minimally generated by n\geq 3
elements. The following conditions are equivalent.


(1) 


After a suitable permutation of the generators,





I_{H}=\operatorname{I}_{2}\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}^{\ell_{1}}&X_{2}^{\ell_{2}}&\cdots&X_{n}^{\ell_{n}}\\
X_{2}^{m_{2}}&X_{3}^{m_{3}}&\cdots&X_{1}^{m_{1}}\end{pmatrix}



for some positive integers
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n},m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}.


(2) 


There exist h\geq 0 and \alpha>0 such that





\operatorname{PF}(H)=\{h+\alpha,h+2\alpha,\ldots,h+(n-1)\alpha\}.








Here, \operatorname{I}_{2}(M) denotes the ideal of S generated by 2\times 2 minors of a matrix M whose entries are in S.



The implication (1)\Rightarrow(2) has already been established in [12]. The converse remains open in general, although it has been proved for several classes, including almost
symmetric numerical semigroups [9], numerical semigroups of maximal embedding dimension [12], stretched numerical semigroup rings [13], generalized repunit numerical semigroups [2, 5], and certain families satisfying restrictions involving the multiplicity and the embedding
dimension [17].



The main result of this paper gives a general sufficient condition for the
converse. Assume that





\operatorname{PF}(H)=\{h+\alpha,h+2\alpha,\ldots,h+(n-1)\alpha\}.



For 1\leq i\leq n, put





m_{i}=\min\{\ell>0\mid\ell a_{i}+\alpha\in H\},\,M_{i}=m_{i}-1,



and set





h^{\prime}=M_{1}a_{1}+M_{2}a_{2}+\cdots+M_{n}a_{n}.



We first show that





M_{i}=\max\{q_{i}\mid(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})\in\mathsf{Z}(h)\},



where \mathsf{Z}(h) denotes the set of factorizations of h. This yields the equivalences





h\text{ has a unique factorization}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad h^{\prime}=h\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad h^{\prime}+\alpha\notin H.



Thus a homological problem concerning I_{H} is reduced to the factorization of a single element of H.



The central theorem is the following.



Theorem 1.2 (Theorem 3.7).


Suppose that \operatorname{PF}(H)=\{h+\alpha,h+2\alpha,\ldots,h+(n-1)\alpha\}
and that h=q_{1}a_{1}+\cdots+q_{n}a_{n} is the unique factorization of h in H . Then, after a suitable permutation of
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}, there exist positive integers
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n} such that





(q_{i}+1)a_{i}+\alpha=r_{i+1}a_{i+1},1\leq\forall i\leq n,



with indices taken modulo n. Consequently,





I_{H}=\operatorname{I}_{2}\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}^{q_{1}+1}&X_{2}^{q_{2}+1}&\cdots&X_{n}^{q_{n}+1}\\
X_{2}^{r_{2}}&X_{3}^{r_{3}}&\cdots&X_{1}^{r_{1}}\end{pmatrix}.



In particular, the graded minimal S-free resolution of k[H] is given by the Eagon–Northcott complex of the matrix mentioned above.




The interesting point is that the unique factorization of h, together with the arithmetic structure of \operatorname{PF}(H), forces the relations





(q_{i}+1)a_{i}+\alpha\in H



to form a single directed cycle through all the minimal generators. This
produces the cyclic monomial matrix. The case h=0 is compatible with the
determinantal situation studied in [9]; the theorem above applies to an arbitrary nonnegative translation h. In particular, if H has maximal embedding dimension, we recover the statement [12, Theorem 2 (2)\Rightarrow(4)].



We then apply this criterion to numerical semigroups generated by the orbit of
their multiplicity under an affine map. Let a,n,c be integers such that a\geq 2,n\geq 3,1\leq c\leq a. For each 1\leq i\leq n, put A_{i}=1+a+\cdots+a^{i-1} and m=cA_{n-1}+1. Let b be an integers such that b\geq-(a-2)m-2 and \gcd(m,b)=1. Let d=(a-1)m+b and s_{i}=m+dA_{i} for all i. Consider the numerical semigroup H=\left<s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{n-1}\right>, where s_{0}=m. In [1],
Álvarez, Moreno-Ávila and Ojeda determined the
pseudo-Frobenius numbers





\operatorname{PF}(H)=\{\rho,\rho-b,\ldots,\rho-(n-2)b\},



where \rho=d(m-1)+(c-1)m. They also considered the monomial matrix





X_{c}=\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}^{a}&X_{2}^{a}&\cdots&X_{n-1}^{a}&X_{n}^{c}\\
X_{2}&X_{3}&\cdots&X_{n}&X_{1}^{d+c+1-a}\end{pmatrix}



and asked whether its 2\times 2 minors generate the whole defining ideal;
see [1, Remark 8.2].



We answer this question affirmatively. Our proof of the determinantal
presentation is independent of their proof of the containment
\operatorname{I}_{2}(X_{c})\subseteq I_{H}. Using the weighted-degree criterion of
[13, Section 2.3], that containment follows directly from the affine
relations among the generators. We then prove that, after writing the above
pseudo-Frobenius numbers in increasing order as





\operatorname{PF}(H)=\{h+\alpha,h+2\alpha,\ldots,h+(n-1)\alpha\},\qquad\alpha=|b|,



the element h has a unique factorization. Theorem
1.2 therefore yields the reverse containment.



We obtain the following result.



Theorem 1.3 (Theorems 4.3 and 4.4).


Let a\geq 2, n\geq 3, 1\leq c\leq a, A_{0}=0 and A_{i}=\dfrac{a^{i}-1}{a-1} for all 1\leq i\leq n. Let m=cA_{n-1}+1 and b\in\mathbb{Z} satisfy b\geq-(a-2)m-2 and \gcd(m,b)=1. Put d=(a-1)m+b,s_{i}=m+dA_{i}, and H=\langle s_{0},\ldots,s_{n-1}\rangle. Then the following statements hold true.


1) 


\operatorname{PF}(H)=\{h+\alpha,h+2\alpha,\ldots,h+(n-1)\alpha\} for some h\geq 0,\alpha>0.


2) 


h has a unique factorization and





I_{H}=\operatorname{I}_{2}\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}^{a}&X_{2}^{a}&\cdots&X_{n-1}^{a}&X_{n}^{c}\\
X_{2}&x_{3}&\cdots&X_{n}&x_{1}^{d+c+1-a}\end{pmatrix}.





Consequently, the Eagon–Northcott complex associated to the matrix in above display gives the graded minimal free resolution of k[H].




For this explicit family, the equality of ideals can also be proved by reducing modulo X_{1} and comparing lengths; see Remark 4.5. The application of Theorem 1.2 has a different purpose. The arithmetic pseudo-Frobenius progression and the unique factorization of h force
the relations among the minimal generators to form a single cycle. That cycle determines the exponents of a determinantal matrix, and the theorem then identifies its minors with the
defining ideal. Thus the presentation of this family is an instance of a criterion formulated without reference to the orbit parameters a,b,c,m or to an a priori candidate matrix. The length computation proves the equality for X_{c} efficiently, whereas the criterion explains why
the pseudo-Frobenius calculation of [1] leads to a determinantal presentation and provides a route for other families with the same arithmetic and factorization properties.



The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 recalls the basic facts on
numerical semigroups, pseudo-Frobenius numbers, and the weighted-degree
criterion used for binomial relations. Section 3 develops the
unique-factorization criterion and proves Theorem 1.2.
Section 4 applies it to the affine-closed family and proves
Theorem 1.3.




2. Preliminaries



Throughout this paper, let H=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle
be a numerical semigroup minimally generated by a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}. Let k be a field and R=k[H]=k[t^{a_{1}},\ldots,t^{a_{n}}]
associated to H. Let S=k[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}] be the weighted polynomial ring with \deg X_{i}=a_{i} for every 1\leq i\leq n and consider the graded k-algebra homomorphism \varphi:S\longrightarrow R given by
\varphi(X_{i})=t^{a_{i}} for all i. Recall that the defining ideal I_{H} of R is defined to be the kernel of \varphi.



Definition 2.1.


For 0<h\in H, the Apéry set of H with respect to h is defined by





\operatorname{Ap}(H,h)=\{a\in H:a-h\notin H\}.



Equivalently, if





w_{i}=\min\{a\in H\mid a\equiv i\pmod{h}\}\text{ for }0\leq\forall i\leq h-1,



then





\operatorname{Ap}(H,h)=\{w_{0}=0,w_{1},\ldots,w_{h-1}\}.






Definition 2.2.


An integer f\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus H is called a pseudo-Frobenius number of H if f+s\in H for every 0<s\in H. The set of all pseudo-Frobenius numbers is denoted by \operatorname{PF}(H).




We recall the following two facts.



Lemma 2.3 ([15, Proposition 2.19(2)]).


For z\in\mathbb{Z}, one has z\notin H if and only if there exists
f\in\operatorname{PF}(H) such that f-z\in H.




Lemma 2.4 ([15, Proposition 2.20]).


For each 0<h\in H,





\operatorname{PF}(H)=\{a-h\mid a\in\operatorname{Ap}(H,h),\ b-a\notin H\text{ for all }b\in\operatorname{Ap}(H,h)\setminus\{a\}\}.






The following elementary criterion will be used repeatedly. It is the
weighted-degree argument of [13, Lemma 2.11 and Corollary 2.12].



Lemma 2.5 (Weighted-degree criterion).


Let the matrix





M=\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}&f_{2}&\cdots&f_{n}\\
g_{1}&g_{2}&\cdots&g_{n}\end{pmatrix},



where f_{i},g_{i} are monomials of positive degree in S. Suppose that there
exists an integer \delta such that \deg g_{i}-\deg f_{i}=\delta for all 1\leq i\leq n. Then \operatorname{I}_{2}(M)\subseteq I_{H}.




Proof.


For 1\leq i<j\leq n, set \Delta_{ij}=f_{i}g_{j}-f_{j}g_{i}.
By assumption, we have





\deg f_{i}+\deg g_{j}=\deg f_{i}+\deg f_{j}+\delta=\deg f_{j}+\deg g_{i}.



Every monomial u\in S satisfies \varphi_{H}(u)=t^{\deg u}.
Hence \varphi_{H}(\Delta_{ij})=0, so every 2\times 2 minor of M belongs to I_{H}.
∎




Remark 2.6.


The integer \delta in Lemma 2.5 is not required to be positive. This will be useful in the last section for the affine family when b<0.





3. A unique-factorization criterion for determinantal defining ideals



Throughout this section, let H=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle,n\geq 3,
and assume that




(\star)

\operatorname{PF}(H)=\{h+\alpha,h+2\alpha,\ldots,h+(n-1)\alpha\},



where h\geq 0 and \alpha>0.



Setting 3.1.


For each 1\leq i\leq n, put m_{i}=\min\{\ell>0\mid\ell a_{i}+\alpha\in H\},M_{i}=m_{i}-1, and define





h^{\prime}=M_{1}a_{1}+M_{2}a_{2}+\cdots+M_{n}a_{n}.



We also set





\mathsf{Z}(h)=\left\{(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\mid h=\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}a_{i}\right\}.






Let us begin with a basic observation.



Lemma 3.2 ([12, Lemma 2]).


Under the condition (\star), one has h\in H and \alpha\notin H.




Proof.


If \alpha\in H, then (h+\alpha)+\alpha=h+2\alpha\in H,
because h+\alpha\in\operatorname{PF}(H), a contradiction. Hence \alpha\notin H. By Lemma 2.3, there exists 1\leq i\leq n-1 such that





h+i\alpha-\alpha=h+(i-1)\alpha\in H.



If i\geq 2, then h+(i-1)\alpha\in\operatorname{PF}(H) which is a contradiction. Therefore
i=1, and h\in H.
∎




Lemma 3.3 (Boundary lemma).


For every c\in H, then c+\alpha\notin H if and only if h-c\in H.




Proof.


Suppose first that c+\alpha\notin H. By
Lemma 2.3, there exists 1\leq i\leq n-1 such that





h+i\alpha-(c+\alpha)=h+(i-1)\alpha-c\in H.



If i\geq 2, then adding c\in H gives
h+(i-1)\alpha\in H, contradicting (\star). Thus i=1,
and h-c\in H.



Conversely, suppose that h-c\in H and c+\alpha\in H. Then h+\alpha=(h-c)+(c+\alpha)\in H, contrary to h+\alpha\in\operatorname{PF}(H).
∎




Proposition 3.4.


For every i, we have





M_{i}=\max\{q_{i}\mid(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})\in\mathsf{Z}(h)\}.






Proof.


By definition, one has M_{i}a_{i}+\alpha\notin H and (M_{i}+1)a_{i}+\alpha\in H. Applying Lemma 3.3, we obtain
h-M_{i}a_{i}\in H and h-(M_{i}+1)a_{i}\notin H. Hence M_{i} is exactly the maximum possible i-th coordinate among the
factorizations of h.
∎




Corollary 3.5.


One has h^{\prime}-h\in H.




Proof.


Take any factorization h=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}a_{i} with 0\leq q_{i}\in\mathbb{Z} for all i. We then have by Proposition 3.4 that q_{i}\leq M_{i} for all i. Therefore





h^{\prime}-h=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(M_{i}-q_{i})a_{i}\in H.



∎




Theorem 3.6.


Under (\star), the following statements are equivalent.


1) 


h has a unique factorization in H.


2) 


h^{\prime}=h.


3) 


h^{\prime}+\alpha\notin H.






Proof.


Suppose first that h=q_{1}a_{1}+\cdots+q_{n}a_{n}
is the unique factorization of h. By Proposition 3.4, for each
i there exists a factorization of h whose i-th coordinate is
M_{i}. By uniqueness, this factorization must be the one above. Hence
q_{i}=M_{i} for all i, so h=h^{\prime}.



Conversely, suppose h=h^{\prime}, and let h=q_{1}a_{1}+\cdots+q_{n}a_{n}
be any factorization. Then we have by Proposition 3.4 that q_{i}\leq M_{i}, and therefore





0=h^{\prime}-h=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(M_{i}-q_{i})a_{i}.



Since all a_{i}>0, it follows that q_{i}=M_{i} for every i. Thus the
factorization is unique.



Finally, Lemma 3.3, applied to c=h^{\prime}, gives the equivalence





h^{\prime}+\alpha\notin H\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad h-h^{\prime}\in H.



Moreover, we already know by Corollary 3.5 that h^{\prime}-h\in H. Since
H\subseteq\mathbb{N}, both h-h^{\prime} and h^{\prime}-h can belong to H only when
h=h^{\prime}.
∎




We now prove the main result of this section.



Theorem 3.7.


Assume (\star). Suppose h=q_{1}a_{1}+\cdots+q_{n}a_{n} is the unique factorization of h. Then, after a suitable permutation of
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}, there exist positive integers
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n} such that





(q_{i}+1)a_{i}+\alpha=r_{i+1}a_{i+1},1\leq\forall i\leq n,



where indices are taken modulo n. Consequently,





I_{H}=\operatorname{I}_{2}\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}^{q_{1}+1}&X_{2}^{q_{2}+1}&\cdots&X_{n}^{q_{n}+1}\\
X_{2}^{r_{2}}&X_{3}^{r_{3}}&\cdots&X_{1}^{r_{1}}\end{pmatrix}.



In particular, the graded minimal S-free resolution of k[H] is given by the Eagon–Northcott complex [6] of the the matrix mentioned above.




Proof.


Since the factorization of h is unique,
Proposition 3.4 gives q_{i}=M_{i} for all i. Thus
q_{i}a_{i}+\alpha\notin H and (q_{i}+1)a_{i}+\alpha\in H.
Put c_{i}=(q_{i}+1)a_{i}+\alpha. No factorization of c_{i} can contain a_{i}, because subtracting one copy
of a_{i} would imply q_{i}a_{i}+\alpha\in H.



For each i, we define





T_{i}=\{j\mid c_{i}-a_{j}\in H,1\leq j\leq n\}.



Since c_{i}\in H\setminus\{0\}, the set T_{i} is nonempty, while
i\notin T_{i}. Choose \sigma(i)\in T_{i} for every i. Then
c_{i}=a_{\sigma(i)}+u_{i} for some u_{i}\in H.



Consider the directed graph with the vertex set \{1,2,...,n\} and directed edges from vertex i to vertex \sigma(i). It contains a directed cycle C, say of length r. Summing the relations
corresponding to the vertices of C gives





\sum_{i\in C}(q_{i}+1)a_{i}+r\alpha=\sum_{i\in C}a_{\sigma(i)}+\sum_{i\in C}u_{i}.



Since \sigma cyclically permutes the vertices of C,





\sum_{i\in C}a_{\sigma(i)}=\sum_{i\in C}a_{i}.



Hence





\sum_{i\in C}q_{i}a_{i}+r\alpha\in H.



Adding \mathop{\sum}\limits_{i\notin C}q_{i}a_{i}\in H, we obtain h+r\alpha\in H.



If r<n, then 1\leq r\leq n-1, contradicting
h+r\alpha\in\operatorname{PF}(H). Therefore every possible choice of the arrows produces a single directed n-cycle. We claim that each T_{i} is a singleton. Fix one such n-cycle. If T_{i} contained another vertex different from the successor of i in the
cycle, replacing only the outgoing arrow from i by this new arrow would
produce a directed cycle of length strictly smaller than n, a
contradiction. Thus T_{i}=\{\sigma(i)\}.



It follows that every factorization of c_{i} uses only the generator
a_{\sigma(i)}. Hence c_{i}=r_{\sigma(i)}a_{\sigma(i)} for some positive integer r_{\sigma(i)}. After relabelling the generators
along the n-cycle, we obtain





(q_{i}+1)a_{i}+\alpha=r_{i+1}a_{i+1}\text{ for }1\leq\forall i\leq n.





Set





J=\operatorname{I}_{2}\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}^{q_{1}+1}&X_{2}^{q_{2}+1}&\cdots&X_{n}^{q_{n}+1}\\
X_{2}^{r_{2}}&X_{3}^{r_{3}}&\cdots&X_{1}^{r_{1}}\end{pmatrix}.



Then the degree difference between the entries in each column is constant. More precisely,





\deg X_{i+1}^{r_{i+1}}-\deg X_{i}^{q_{i}+1}=r_{i+1}a_{i+1}-(q_{i}+1)a_{i}=\alpha.



Hence by Lemma 2.5 we get the inclusion J\subseteq I_{H}.



We now prove equality. First, oberse that





\sqrt{J+(X_{1})}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}).



Indeed, modulo X_{1}, the minor of the first two columns gives
X_{2}^{q_{2}+1+r_{2}}. Hence X_{2}\in\sqrt{J+(X_{1})}. Inductively, once
X_{i}\in\sqrt{J+(X_{1})}, the minor of columns i and i+1 shows that





X_{i+1}^{q_{i+1}+1+r_{i+1}}\in\sqrt{J+(X_{1})}.



Thus every X_{i} belongs to the radical. Hence \operatorname{ht}(J+(X_{1}))=n.
By Krull’s principal ideal theorem, \operatorname{ht}J\geq n-1.



On the other hand, the general height bound for the ideal of maximal minors of a 2\times n matrix gives \operatorname{ht}J\leq n-1 ([4, Theorem 2.1]). Therefore \operatorname{ht}J=n-1. The Eagon–Northcott complex is consequently a graded minimal free resolution of A=S/J.



Put





A_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i},\quad d_{i}=(q_{i}+1)a_{i}\text{ and, }D=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}.



Since h=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i}q_{i}a_{i}, we have D=h+A_{0}. After interchanging the two rows of the matrix defining J, the entries in
the i-th column have degrees d_{i}+\alpha and d_{i}. Hence the last
free module in the graded Eagon–Northcott resolution is





\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n-1}S(-D-j\alpha).



Since the graded canonical module of S is S(-A_{0}), dualizing this
resolution shows that the canonical module \mathrm{K}_{A} of A is minimally generated in the degrees





-h-\alpha,\ -h-2\alpha,\ \ldots,\ -h-(n-1)\alpha.



By the assumption (\star) and the standard description of the canonical module of a numerical semigroup ring [10], the canonical module \mathrm{K}_{k[H]} of k[H] has one minimal generator in each of exactly the same
degrees.



Let L=I_{H}/J. If L=0, there is nothing to prove. Assume L\neq 0. Since A is a
one-dimensional Cohen–Macaulay ring and L\subseteq A, one has
H^{0}_{\mathfrak{m}}(L)=0, where
\mathfrak{m}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}). Thus L is a one-dimensional
Cohen–Macaulay S-module. Applying
\operatorname{Ext}^{n-1}_{S}(-,S(-A_{0})) to the exact sequence





0\longrightarrow L\longrightarrow A\longrightarrow k[H]\longrightarrow 0



therefore yields a short exact sequence





0\longrightarrow\mathrm{K}_{k[H]}\xrightarrow{\ \varepsilon\ }\mathrm{K}_{A}\longrightarrow\mathrm{K}_{L}\longrightarrow 0.





For 1\leq r\leq n-2, one has r\alpha\notin H. Indeed, if r\alpha\in H, then, since h+\alpha\in\operatorname{PF}(H),





h+(r+1)\alpha=(h+\alpha)+r\alpha\in H,



contradicting (\star). Consequently, if
1\leq i<j\leq n-1, the difference





(-h-i\alpha)-(-h-j\alpha)=(j-i)\alpha



does not belong to H. Hence, in each degree
-h-i\alpha, no minimal generator of either canonical module can be
obtained from another one by multiplication with a positive-degree element
of S.



It follows that the map induced by \varepsilon on





\mathrm{K}_{k[H]}/\mathfrak{m}\mathrm{K}_{k[H]}\longrightarrow\mathrm{K}_{A}/\mathfrak{m}\mathrm{K}_{A}



is, in each of the n-1 generator degrees, a nonzero map between
one-dimensional k-vector spaces. Thus it is an isomorphism. By graded
Nakayama’s lemma, \varepsilon is surjective and hence an isomorphism.
Therefore \mathrm{K}_{L}=0.
This is impossible for a nonzero one-dimensional Cohen–Macaulay module.
Hence L=0, and therefore J=I_{H}.
∎




Corollary 3.8.


Assume (\star). Then h has a unique factorization in
H if and only if h+n\alpha has a unique factorization in H. When these
conditions hold, the determinantal conclusion of Theorem 3.7 holds.




Proof.


We first use an argument similar to that in the first part of the proof of Theorem 3.7 to establish a factorization result covering both possible signs of the common difference.



Claim.


Suppose \operatorname{PF}(H)=\{g+\beta,g+2\beta,\ldots,g+(n-1)\beta\} for \beta\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}, and g=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}a_{i} is the unique factorization of g in H. Then g+n\beta also has a unique factorization.




Proof of Claim. For every c\in H, the argument of Boundary lemma (Lemma 3.3) works for either sign
of \beta and gives the equivalence





c+\beta\notin H\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad g-c\in H.



Indeed, if c+\beta\notin H, Lemma 2.3 gives an index
1\leq j\leq n-1 for which
g+(j-1)\beta-c\in H. If j\geq 2, adding c would put
g+(j-1)\beta\in\operatorname{PF}(H) in H, a contradiction. Hence j=1 so that g-c\in H.
Conversely, g-c\in H and c+\beta\in H would imply
g+\beta\in H which is impossible.



By the unique representation of g, one has g-(q_{i}+1)a_{i}\notin H, whereas g-q_{i}a_{i}\in H.
Thus the above equivalence shows that
c_{i}:=(q_{i}+1)a_{i}+\beta\in H and q_{i}a_{i}+\beta\notin H.
In fact c_{i}\neq 0. If otherwise, -\beta=(q_{i}+1)a_{i}\in H\setminus\{0\} which implies g+\beta=(g+2\beta)-\beta\in H a contradiction. No factorization of c_{i} uses
a_{i}, since c_{i}-a_{i}=q_{i}a_{i}+\beta\notin H.



Choose a generator a_{\sigma(i)} in a factorization of each c_{i}.
The directed graph given by i\mapsto\sigma(i) has a cycle C, say of length r. Writing c_{i}=a_{\sigma(i)}+u_{i} for i\in C,
and summing around this cycle, we obtain





\sum_{i\in C}q_{i}a_{i}+r\beta=\sum_{i\in C}u_{i}\in H.



Adding \mathop{\sum}\limits_{i\notin C}q_{i}a_{i} gives g+r\beta\in H.
If r<n, this contradicts g+r\beta\in\operatorname{PF}(H). Therefore every choice of the arrows gives one cycle containing all n vertices.
Consequently, each c_{i} must involve only the generator at its successor, since choosing a different successor at any vertex would create a shorter cycle. After relabelling, there are positive integers
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n} such that





(q_{i}+1)a_{i}+\beta=v_{i+1}a_{i+1},\,1\leq\forall i\leq n,



with indices taken modulo n. Summing both sides, we obtain
g+n\beta=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{n}(v_{i}-1)a_{i}. Now if
g+n\beta=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}a_{i} is another factorization, then
w_{i}\geq v_{i} for some i. Hence





g+(n-1)\beta=\bigl(g+n\beta-v_{i}a_{i}\bigr)+(q_{i-1}+1)a_{i-1}\in H,



contrary to g+(n-1)\beta\in\operatorname{PF}(H). This proves Claim. ∎



We now apply Claim with (g,\beta)=(h,\alpha) to obtain
that uniqueness of h implies uniqueness of h+n\alpha.
Conversely, by writing





\operatorname{PF}(H)=\{(h+n\alpha)-\alpha,(h+n\alpha)-2\alpha,\ldots,(h+n\alpha)-(n-1)\alpha\}



and applying the Claim again with (g,\beta)=(h+n\alpha,-\alpha), we see that
uniqueness of h+n\alpha implies uniqueness of
h+n\alpha+n(-\alpha)=h. The final assertion follows from Theorem 3.7.
∎




As a direct consequence, we recover the implication (2)\Rightarrow(4) of [12, Theorem 2] in the case where H has maximal embedding dimension.



Corollary 3.9 ([12], Theorem 2).


Let H=\left<a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\right> be a numerical semigroup minimally generated by n elements a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{n}. Suppose that H has maximal embedding dimension, that is, a_{1}=n and assume \operatorname{PF}(H)=\{h+\alpha,h+2\alpha,\ldots,h+(n-1)\alpha\}. Then, there exists a positive integer s such that





I_{H}=\operatorname{I}_{2}\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}^{s}&X_{2}&\cdots&X_{n-1}&X_{n}\\
X_{2}&X_{3}&\cdots&X_{n}&X_{1}^{s+\alpha}\end{pmatrix}.






Proof.


Since H has maximal embedding dimension, \operatorname{PF}(H)=\{a_{2}-a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}-a_{1}\}. It implies that h+(i-1)\alpha=a_{i}-a_{1} for every i=2,\ldots,n. Since 0\leq h<a_{2} and h\in H, every factorization of h uses
only a_{1}; this is because all other minimal generators are at least a_{2}. Hence h=(s-1)a_{1} for a uniquely determined integer s\geq 1, and this is the unique factorization of h. Moreover, there are equalities





sa_{1}+\alpha=a_{2},\,a_{i-1}+\alpha=a_{i}\text{ for all }2\leq i\leq n,\text{ and }a_{n}+\alpha=(s+\alpha)a_{1}.



The conclusion therefore follows from Theorem 3.7.



∎





4. Affine-closed numerical semigroups



Let a\geq 2, m>1, and b\in\mathbb{Z} with \gcd(m,b)=1 and b\geq-(a-2)m-2. We consider the affine map T(z)=az+b.
Put A_{0}=0\text{ and }





A_{i}=\frac{a^{i}-1}{a-1}=1+a+\cdots+a^{i-1}\text{ for all }i>0.



Set d=(a-1)m+b. The orbit of m under T is given by s_{i}=T^{i}(m)=m+dA_{i}.



In this section, we set m=cA_{n-1}+1 for 1\leq c\leq a and focus our attention on the semigroup H=\langle s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{n-1}\rangle generated by the orbit of m under the affine map T, where s_{0}=m. Under this hypotheses, the semigroup H is minimally generated by n elements. Moreover,
[1, Theorem 8.1] provides the pseudo-Frobenius numbers




(4.1)

\operatorname{PF}(H)=\{\rho,\rho-b,\rho-2b,\ldots,\rho-(n-2)b\},



where \rho=d(m-1)+(c-1)m.



We shall use the elementary identity




(4.2)

(a-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}A_{i}=A_{n-1}-(n-1).



Indeed, (a-1)A_{i}=a^{i}-1, and summing yields





(a-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}A_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}(a^{i}-1)=A_{n-1}-(n-1).





4.1. The case b>0



Assume b>0. Then (4.1), written in increasing order, is





\operatorname{PF}(H)=\{h+b,h+2b,\ldots,h+(n-1)b\},



where h=\rho-(n-1)b.



Theorem 4.1.


Assume b>0. Then




(4.3)

h=(a-1)(s_{0}+s_{1}+\cdots+s_{n-2})+(c-1)s_{n-1}.



Moreover, this is the unique factorization of h.




Proof.


Since m-1=cA_{n-1}, the equality (4.2) gives





(a-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}A_{i}+(c-1)A_{n-1}=cA_{n-1}-(n-1)=m-n.



Hence the right-hand side of (4.3) is





\bigl((a-1)(n-1)+c-1\bigr)m+d(m-n).



Since b=d-(a-1)m, this is equal to





d(m-1)+(c-1)m-(n-1)b=h.





Now suppose h=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}x_{i}s_{i} is any factorization of h in H. Put L=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}x_{i} and W=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}A_{i}. Then h=Lm+dW. For the factorization (4.3), put L_{0}=(a-1)(n-1)+c-1
and W_{0}=m-n. We get h=L_{0}m+dW_{0}. Consequently,





(L-L_{0})m+d(W-W_{0})=0.



Since \gcd(m,d)=\gcd(m,b)=1, there exists t\in\mathbb{Z} such that W=W_{0}+tm and L=L_{0}-td.
Because 0\leq W_{0}=m-n<m and W\geq 0, we must have t\geq 0.



If t\geq 1, then L\leq L_{0}-d. Since b>0,





d=(a-1)m+b>(a-1)m.



Moreover m=cA_{n-1}+1\geq n, while





L_{0}=(a-1)(n-1)+c-1\leq(a-1)n.



Hence d>L_{0}, which would imply L<0, a contradiction. Therefore
t=0 which implies that L=L_{0} and W=W_{0}.



Put \ell=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i} and \ell_{0}=(a-1)(n-2)+c-1. Then the vector q=(a-1,\ldots,a-1,c-1) satisfies W_{0}=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}q_{i}A_{i}. Since W_{0}=m-n<A_{n}, Proposition 4.3 and Lemma 4.4 of [1] show that this canonical representation has minimum length among all representations of W_{0} in terms of
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n-1}. Therefore \ell\geq\ell_{0}.



On the other hand, one has x_{0}=L_{0}-\ell\geq 0, whence \ell\leq L_{0}=\ell_{0}+(a-1). Since \mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}(x_{i}-q_{i})A_{i}=0,
multiplying by a-1 and using (a-1)A_{i}=a^{i}-1 gives





\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(x_{i}-q_{i})a^{i}=\ell-\ell_{0}.



The left-hand side is divisible by a. Hence \ell-\ell_{0}\equiv 0\pmod{a}. Together with 0\leq\ell-\ell_{0}\leq a-1,
this yields \ell=\ell_{0}. Thus x_{0}=a-1.



It remains to show that the canonical representation q=(a-1,\ldots,a-1,c-1) of W_{0}=m-n is the only representation of length \ell_{0}. Indeed, let x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}) be such a representation. Apply the reduction procedure in the proof of [1, Lemma 4.4] to x. It terminates at the canonical representation q; every step weakly decreases total length. Since \ell_{0} is the minimum length, every step in this sequence must preserve length. In a length-preserving step, [1, Lemma 4.4] has k>1; it increases coordinate k-1 by a and also increases a coordinate with higher index. Consequently, the output of this step cannot be a canonical representation; because a canonical vector cannot have a coordinate at least a while a higher coordinate is positive. Thus no length-preserving step can be the last step before q. The reduction sequence has no steps and x=q. Together with x_{0}=a-1, this proves the claimed uniqueness.
∎





4.2. The case b<0



Assume b<0, and put \alpha=-b>0. Then (4.1) becomes





\operatorname{PF}(H)=\{\rho,\rho+\alpha,\ldots,\rho+(n-2)\alpha\}.



Thus h=\rho-\alpha=\rho+b.



Theorem 4.2.


Assume b<0. Then h=(d+c-a)s_{0} and this is the unique factorization of h.




Proof.


Since b=d-(a-1)m, there are equalities





\displaystyle h
\displaystyle=d(m-1)+(c-1)m+b





\displaystyle=d(m-1)+(c-1)m+d-(a-1)m





\displaystyle=(d+c-a)m.



Since s_{0}=m, there is an equality h=(d+c-a)s_{0}.



Suppose h=x_{0}s_{0}+x_{1}s_{1}+\cdots+x_{n-1}s_{n-1}. Put L=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}x_{i} and W=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}A_{i}. Then (d+c-a)m=Lm+dW, so (d+c-a-L)m=dW. Hence, since \gcd(m,d)=1, there exists t\in\mathbb{N} such that
W=tm and d+c-a-L=td.
Thus L=(1-t)d+c-a.



If t=1, then L=c-a\leq 0. When c<a, this is impossible because
W=m>0; when c=a, one has L=0 while W=m>0, again impossible.
If t\geq 2, then L<0, also impossible.



Thus t=0, and hence W=0. We then get x_{1}=\cdots=x_{n-1}=0 and x_{0}=d+c-a so that h=(d+c-a)s_{0} is the unique factorization as desired.
∎




Combining the two cases yields the following.



Theorem 4.3.


Under the hypotheses above, write (4.1) in increasing order as





\operatorname{PF}(H)=\{h+\alpha,h+2\alpha,\ldots,h+(n-1)\alpha\},\qquad\alpha=|b|.



Then h has a unique factorization in H. Consequently,
Conjecture 1.1 holds for H.




Proof.


If b>0, the assertion follows from Theorem 4.1. If
b<0, it follows from Theorem 4.2. The determinantal
assertion then follows from Theorem 3.7.
∎




We now determine the defining ideal explicitly. Let S=k[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}] be the graded polynomial ring with \deg X_{i}=s_{i-1} for all 1\leq i\leq n, and set the matrix





\mathsf{X}_{c}=\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}^{a}&X_{2}^{a}&\cdots&X_{n-1}^{a}&X_{n}^{c}\\
X_{2}&X_{3}&\cdots&X_{n}&X_{1}^{d+c+1-a}\end{pmatrix}.





We first verify directly, independently of the determinantal containment in
[1, Remark 8.2], that \operatorname{I}_{2}(\mathsf{X}_{c})\subseteq I_{H}. Since A_{i}=aA_{i-1}+1,
we have, for 1\leq i\leq n-1,





\displaystyle s_{i}
\displaystyle=m+dA_{i}





\displaystyle=m+d(aA_{i-1}+1)





\displaystyle=a(m+dA_{i-1})+d-(a-1)m





\displaystyle=as_{i-1}+b.



Consequently,




(4.4)

\deg X_{i+1}-\deg X_{i}^{a}=s_{i}-as_{i-1}=b\qquad(1\leq i\leq n-1).



Moreover, using m-1=cA_{n-1}, we obtain





\displaystyle cs_{n-1}+b
\displaystyle=cm+cdA_{n-1}+b





\displaystyle=cm+d(m-1)+b





\displaystyle=cm+dm-d+b





\displaystyle=(d+c)m-(d-b)





\displaystyle=(d+c+1-a)m.



Hence




(4.5)

\deg X_{1}^{d+c+1-a}-\deg X_{n}^{c}=(d+c+1-a)s_{0}-cs_{n-1}=b.



Thus the difference between the weighted degrees of the bottom and top entries
is the same, namely b, in every column of \mathsf{X}_{c}. By
Lemma 2.5, we obtain the inclusion




(4.6)

\operatorname{I}_{2}(\mathsf{X}_{c})\subseteq I_{H}.



Notice that this argument works uniformly for both b>0 and b<0.



Theorem 4.4.


Under the hypotheses of Theorem 4.3, we have
I_{H}=\operatorname{I}_{2}(\mathsf{X}_{c}). In particular, the Eagon–Northcott complex associated to \mathsf{X}_{c} is the graded minimal free resolution of k[H].




Proof.


The containment \operatorname{I}_{2}(\mathsf{X}_{c})\subseteq I_{H} has already been proved in (4.6) by the weighted-degree
criterion. It remains to identify the matrix supplied by
Theorem 3.7.



Suppose first that b>0. Then
\alpha=b, and Theorem 4.1 gives





h=(a-1)s_{0}+\cdots+(a-1)s_{n-2}+(c-1)s_{n-1}.



Thus the exponents in the first row of the matrix are a,\ldots,a,c. Moreover, the relations in (4.4) and (4.5) give
as_{i-1}+b=s_{i}, for all 1\leq i\leq n-1, and





cs_{n-1}+b=(d+c+1-a)s_{0}.



Hence the second-row exponents are 1,\ldots,1,d+c+1-a.
Therefore the matrix in Theorem 3.7 is precisely X_{c}.



Now suppose b<0, and put \alpha=-b. By
Theorem 4.2, we have that the decomposition





h=(d+c-a)s_{0}



is the unique factorization of h. The affine relations can be rewritten as





s_{i}+\alpha=as_{i-1}\qquad(1\leq i\leq n-1)



and





(d+c+1-a)s_{0}+\alpha=cs_{n-1}.



Ordering the generators as s_{0},s_{n-1},s_{n-2},\ldots,s_{1}.
Theorem 3.7 yields the matrix





\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}^{d+c+1-a}&X_{n}&x_{n-1}&\cdots&X_{2}\\
X_{n}^{c}&X_{n-1}^{a}&X_{n-2}^{a}&\cdots&X_{1}^{a}\end{pmatrix}.



Interchanging its two rows and reversing the order of the columns gives
exactly \mathsf{X}_{c}. Neither operation changes the ideal generated by the
2\times 2 minors. Hence I_{H}=\operatorname{I}_{2}(\mathsf{X}_{c}) in both cases.



Finally, \operatorname{ht}I_{H}=n-1. Since every entry of \mathsf{X}_{c} has positive degree, the Eagon–Northcott gives the graded minimal free resolution of k[H].
∎




Remark 4.5.


For the explicit affine family, the determinantal equality can also be checked directly. Let J=I_{2}(X_{c})\subseteq I_{H}. For a monomial matrix





M=\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}^{\ell_{1}}&X_{2}^{\ell_{2}}&\cdots&X_{n}^{\ell_{n}}\\
X_{2}^{m_{2}}&X_{3}^{m_{3}}&\cdots&X_{1}^{m_{1}}\end{pmatrix}



of height n-1, the Eagon–Northcott Hilbert series,
or a count of the standard monomials modulo X_{1}, gives





\dim_{k}S/(I_{2}(M)+(X_{1}))=\ell_{2}\cdots\ell_{n}+m_{2}\ell_{3}\cdots\ell_{n}+\cdots+m_{2}\cdots m_{n}.



For X_{c} we have (\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{n})=(a,\ldots,a,c) and
(m_{2},\ldots,m_{n})=(1,\ldots,1), so this length is





ca^{n-2}+ca^{n-3}+\cdots+ca+c+1=cA_{n-1}+1=m.



On the other hand, S/(I_{H}+(X_{1}))\cong k[H]/(t^{m}) has k-length m.
The surjection between these Artinian quotients is therefore an
isomorphism. If I_{H}/J were nonzero, it would be a nonzero finitely
generated graded module over S/J and would have a nonzero quotient
modulo X_{1} by graded Nakayama, a contradiction. Thus J=I_{H}.



This proof uses the explicit exponents of X_{c} and the identity m=cA_{n-1}+1 to obtain the required length. In contrast, Theorem 3.7 constructs the cyclic relations from the pseudo-Frobenius progression and the unique factorization of h, then supplies the ideal equality without a separate length calculation. The latter argument makes the structural reason for the determinantal presentation visible and is available whenever those two hypotheses can be verified.




We close this paper with two examples satisfying the hypotheses.



Example 4.6 (The case b>0).


Take a=2, n=4, and c=1. Then A_{3}=1+2+4=7 and m=A_{3}+1=8.
Choose b=3. Then d=(a-1)m+b=11 and





H=\langle 8,19,41,85\rangle.



We have \rho=11(8-1)=77, and hence





\operatorname{PF}(H)=\{77,74,71\}=\{71,74,77\}.



Thus h=68 and \alpha=3. Theorem 4.1 gives the factorization 68=8+19+41, and this is the unique factorization of 68. The defining ideal is





I_{H}=\operatorname{I}_{2}\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}^{2}&X_{2}^{2}&X_{3}^{2}&X_{4}\\
X_{2}&X_{3}&X_{4}&X_{1}^{11}\end{pmatrix}.






Example 4.7 (The case b<0).


Again take a=2, n=4, c=1, and m=8, but now let
b=-1. Then d=7 and H=\langle 8,15,29,57\rangle. We have \rho=7(8-1)=49,
so





\operatorname{PF}(H)=\{49,50,51\}.



Hence h=48 and \alpha=1. Theorem 4.2 gives the factorization 48=6\cdot 8,
and this is the unique factorization of 48. The defining ideal is





I_{H}=\operatorname{I}_{2}\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}^{2}&X_{2}^{2}&X_{3}^{2}&X_{4}\\
X_{2}&X_{3}&X_{4}&X_{1}^{7}\end{pmatrix}.
