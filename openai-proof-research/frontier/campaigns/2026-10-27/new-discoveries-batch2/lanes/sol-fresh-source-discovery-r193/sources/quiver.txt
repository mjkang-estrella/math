                                                                   SEMIALGEBRAIC

                                                    Abstract. We prove that mutation-acyclicity of quivers on four vertices is decidable. If a four-vertex
                                                    quiver Q is mutation-acyclic, then some sequence of mutations takes it to an acyclic quiver without
                                                    any arrow multiplicity exceeding max{ht(Q), m(Q)}, where ht(Q) is the largest arrow multiplicity of

                                                    of Q. A search within a computable form of this bound decides mutation-acyclicity of four-vertex
                                                    quivers with O(h24 ) states for input height h ≥ 1. Together with a theorem of Caldero and Keller, it
                                                    also decides mutation equivalence of two four-vertex quivers up to relabeling whenever one of them

                                                                                           1. Introduction
                                              Quivers are finite directed multigraphs without loops or directed 2-cycles. Fomin and Zelevinsky
                                           introduced quiver mutation in their theory of cluster algebras [FZ02, FZ03]. A quiver is mutation-
                                           acyclic if some sequence of mutations takes it to a quiver without directed cycles, and mutation-
                                           cyclic otherwise. Mutation-acyclic quivers are the quivers of acyclic cluster algebras, which have
                                           been studied through cluster categories and quiver representations [BMR08, BMRT07, CK06, CK08],
                                           through c-vectors [ST13, Sev15], and through reflection groups [BM15, FT16, FT18]. This paper
                                           concerns the following decision problem.
                                           Problem 1. Given a quiver Q, decide whether Q is mutation-acyclic.
                                              Neville records that no algorithm for Problem 1 is known [Nev25a, Chapter I], and in our earlier
                                           paper we noted that no algorithm or mutation invariant was known to decide mutation-acyclicity in
                                           rank four or higher [AWHJL25]. The problem is closely related to deciding mutation equivalence,
                                           for which Fomin, Williams and Zelevinsky ask for an algorithm [FWZ24, Problem 2.6.12], and for
                                           which no algorithm is known [FN26], [FIL21, Remark 2.3], [Fom22, slide 7].
                                              For quivers with at most three vertices, Problem 1 is solved. Quivers with one or two vertices are
                                           acyclic. For three vertices, Assem, Blais, Brüstle and Samson gave a descent algorithm [ABBS08,
                                           Section 2.5], and Beineke, Brüstle and Hille proved that an oriented triangle is mutation-cyclic exactly
                                           when its multiplicities are at least two and its Markov constant is at most four [BBH11, Theorem 1.2].

Seven extended the description of rank-three mutation classes to skew-symmetrizable matrices
[Sev13], Akagi gave the skew-symmetrizable form of the Markov-constant criterion [Aka24], and
Felikson and Tumarkin and Casals and Ke studied rank-three quivers with real weights [FT19, CK26].
Quivers of finite mutation type, classified by Felikson, Shapiro and Tumarkin [FST12] (see also
[DO08] and, for acyclic quivers, [BR06]), have finite mutation classes, so for them the problem
reduces to a finite enumeration.
   For quivers with four or more vertices, only partial results are known. Felikson and Tumarkin
showed that a quiver mutation-equivalent to an acyclic quiver with at least two arrows between
every pair of vertices reaches such a quiver by a descent that never increases an arrow multiplicity
[FT18, Corollary 3.5 and Remark 3.6], and they note that Corollary 3.5 is also proved in Warkentin’s
thesis [War14]. Lawson and Mills determined which minimal mutation-infinite quivers are mutation-
acyclic [LM18, Section 4]. In an earlier paper, we classified the four-vertex quivers with at most
two arrows between any two vertices [AWHJL25], in the course of a machine-learning study; see
[BFH+ 20, HJC+ 25, DHHH23] for other applications of machine learning to quiver mutation. The
criterion stated in [AWHJL25, Theorem 2.9] is wrong, and we correct it in Section 6.
   There is also a long list of necessary conditions for mutation-acyclicity. Buan, Marsh and
Reiten proved that every full subquiver of a mutation-acyclic quiver is mutation-acyclic [BMR08,
Corollary 5.3], a consequence of their Theorem 2.13 that was pointed out to them by A. Seven, and
Warkentin gave a more explicit proof [War11]. Both proofs are representation-theoretic, and an
elementary proof is still wanted [FWZ17, Example 4.1.5], [Fom22, slides 11–12]. Every mutation-
acyclic quiver admits an admissible quasi-Cartan companion [Sev15, Theorem 1.2], a notion going
back to Barot, Geiss and Zelevinsky [BGZ06]. Every mutation-acyclic quiver also has a reddening
sequence, since acyclic quivers have maximal green sequences [BDP14, Lemma 2.20] and the existence
of a reddening sequence is mutation invariant [Mul16, Corollary 19]. Neville proved that every
mutation-acyclic quiver is totally proper [Nev25b, Theorem 1.1], as conjectured by Fomin and
Neville [FN26, Conjecture 15.9]. Warkentin’s forks give further mutation-invariant tools [War14],
developed by Ervin [Erv24]. In rank four there are mutation-cyclic quivers without mutation-cyclic
full subquivers on three vertices, such as the dreaded torus [MM15, Section 7.2], whose cluster
algebra comes from a torus with one boundary component and one marked point [FST08], and
Warkentin’s oriented four-cycles with multiplicities a, b, a, b ≥ 2 [War14, Example 6.11]. Mutation
invariants beyond the determinant are studied in [Che23, Cas24, SU25, Erv24, FN25, FN26], and
Ervin and Jackson ask for a description of mutation-acyclicity as an intersection of hereditary
mutation-invariant properties [EJ26].
   The starting point for our main result is a finiteness theorem of Neville. Fomin and Neville attach
to a quiver with a cyclic ordering of its vertices a Markov invariant [FN26]. For a mutation-acyclic
quiver Q, write m(Q) for its value on the totally proper wiggle class of Q (Section 2), and ht(Q) for
the largest arrow multiplicity of Q. Neville proved that the arrow multiplicities of every acyclic
quiver in the mutation class of Q are bounded in terms of m(Q), so that only finitely many acyclic
quivers share a given Markov invariant [Nev25b, Proposition 3.21]. Our main result extends this
bound from the acyclic quivers of a mutation class to a mutation sequence leading to one of them.
Theorem 1.1. Let Q be a mutation-acyclic quiver on four vertices. Then some sequence of
mutations takes Q to an acyclic quiver, with every quiver along the sequence of height at most
max{ht(Q), m(Q)}. Consequently, mutation-acyclicity of four-vertex quivers is decidable. For input
height h ≥ 1, the decision procedure searches O(h24 ) quivers.
  The height statement is Theorem 5.1, and the decision procedure and the state count are
Corollaries 5.2 and 5.3. Since the totally proper class of Q is not known in advance, the procedure
uses the largest Markov value over all proper wiggle classes of Q in place of m(Q), and a four-vertex
quiver without proper wiggle classes is a vortex and hence mutation-cyclic. The bound concerns
the existence of one suitable mutation sequence, and other routes to an acyclic quiver may climb

arbitrarily high. The running time is pseudo-polynomial in the arrow multiplicities, rather than
polynomial in their binary encoding length.
   The proof chooses a route to an acyclic quiver that minimizes its maximum height, then the
number of quivers at that height, then its length, and shows that a stretch of such a route above
max{ht(Q), m(Q)} could be replaced by a lower one. The replacements are found by a case analysis
on how the first two mutation vertices of such a stretch are joined. Every calculation in this case
analysis is verified by a computer program, exactly and for all values of the parameters (Appendices A
and B). Descent methods face an obstacle in rank four. Fomin and Neville constructed arbitrarily
long primitive mutation cycles of four-vertex quivers, and observed that any descent algorithm would
have to be confluent over arbitrarily large diamonds [FN25]; see also [EN26] and [Nev25a, Chapter I].
Soukup observes that if mutation equivalence were undecidable, some equivalent quivers would be
joined only by extremely long mutation sequences, going far “into the sky”, and suggests that a
bound like that of Coward and Lackenby for Reidemeister moves may exist for quiver mutation
[Sou25, Sections 4.1–4.2], [CL14]. Theorem 1.1 gives such a bound for mutation-acyclicity in rank
four, since a bound on height also bounds the length of a shortest route.
   We do not know whether the term m(Q) is needed. We conjecture that ht(Q) alone suffices,
which would reduce the search to O(h6 ) quivers, and we verify this for all four-vertex quivers of
height at most three (Conjecture 5.5). The height bound also certifies mutation-cyclicity in an
example where none of the other obstructions of this paper applies to any quiver within the search
range (Example 5.4). Combined with the description of acyclic seeds by Caldero and Keller [CK06,
Corollary 4 and its proof], Theorem 1.1 also decides mutation equivalence of two four-vertex quivers
up to relabeling whenever at least one of them is mutation-acyclic (Corollary 5.7).
   Our second main result shows that no criterion of the kind that works in rank three can exist in
higher rank. The rank-three criterion of Beineke, Brüstle and Hille is a finite Boolean combination
of polynomial sign conditions on the arrow weights.
Theorem 1.2. Let n ≥ 4. No fixed, finite Boolean combination of polynomial equations and
strict polynomial inequalities in the n2 arrow weights, with real coefficients and not necessarily
mutation-invariant, decides mutation-acyclicity for all integral quivers on n vertices. This remains
true when the quivers are required to be connected.
   For n = 4 the proof uses the affine family Q(t), t ∈ Z, of Section 3, with arrow weights
(−2, t + 2, t, −t, 2 − t, 0) in the coordinates of Section 2. The family is mutation-acyclic exactly when
t < 0 or t is even, so for large positive t its mutation-acyclicity changes at every successive integer.
In contrast, substituting the six affine arrow weights into any fixed polynomial gives a one-variable
polynomial, whose sign is eventually constant unless it vanishes identically. For n > 4 we attach a
path to Q(t).
   Chelnokov proved that a mutation invariant of four-vertex quivers that is polynomial on each
sign pattern of the arrow weights is a polynomial in the determinant [Che23, Theorem 1]. The
family Q(t) has determinant 16 for every t, so criteria built from such invariants cannot decide
mutation-acyclicity, and Theorem 1.2 complements this by removing the invariance hypothesis. The
family is also invisible to the Markov invariant and to properness, since every Q(t) with t ≥ 0 has a
single proper wiggle class, with the same Markov value as the acyclic quiver Q(0) (Remark 3.3).
Theorem 1.2 does not rule out arithmetic tests, mutation invariants in general, or algorithms whose
number of steps depends on the input, and Theorem 1.1 is an algorithm of the last kind.
   Finally, Section 6 corrects the criterion of [AWHJL25, Theorem 2.9] for four-vertex quivers of
height at most two and recovers the classification counts with short certificates. All computations
in this paper are carried out by a supplementary Python package, and checking them needs only
the Python standard library. The program check_proofs.py verifies every calculation delegated
from the appendices, check_census.py verifies the certificates of Section 6 with an independent
implementation of mutation, and examples.py checks the examples. The main results are also

  We now state some classical results of [BBH11, Theorem 1.2] and [BMR08, Corollary 5.3], which
we will use frequently enough to warrant restating here.
Proposition 2.1 ([BBH11, Theorem 1.2], [BMR08, Corollary 5.3]). A three-vertex quiver is
mutation-cyclic if and only if it is an oriented triangle whose arrow multiplicities x, y, z satisfy
                                  x, y, z ≥ 2,       x2 + y 2 + z 2 − xyz ≤ 4.
The value x2 + y 2 + z 2 − xyz is called the Markov constant, and the expression defining it is the
Markov polynomial. Also, every full subquiver of a mutation-acyclic quiver is mutation-acyclic.
This implies that a quiver is mutation-cyclic if it contains a mutation-cyclic full subquiver.
   We call a full subquiver on three vertices a full triangle, even when some of its three pairs
of vertices are not joined. Finding a mutation-cyclic full triangle is a practical way to prove
mutation-cyclicity for quivers of larger rank.

2.1. Cyclic orderings. We use the cyclic ordering framework of [FN26] throughout this paper. A
cyclic ordering puts the vertices of a quiver around a circle. We write ω = ⟨v1 , . . . , vn ⟩, with angle
brackets identifying lists that differ by cyclic rotation, but not by reversal. A linear ordering is
written v1 < · · · < vn ; wrapping it around the circle gives ⟨v1 , . . . , vn ⟩. A vertex k is proper if every
directed path i → k → j encounters i, k, j in clockwise order according to the cyclic ordering. In
other words, every two-path through k follows the circle clockwise. A wiggle swaps two neighboring
vertices on the circle that are not joined by an arrow. Cyclic orderings related by wiggles are wiggle
equivalent; we denote the class of ω by [ω]wig . A wiggle-equivalence class is itself proper if each
vertex is proper in some ordering in the class.
   To perform a proper mutation at k, first choose an ordering in which k is proper. Mutate
the quiver and move k clockwise past its incoming neighbors in µk Q (its outgoing neighbors in Q),
without passing its outgoing neighbors in µk Q. The result is well defined up to wiggles. A class is
totally proper if it remains proper after every sequence of proper mutations [FN26, Definition 14.1].
Theorem 2.2 ([FN26, Observation 11.6, Theorem 14.3, and Conjecture 15.9], [Nev25b, Theo-
rem 1.1]). A quiver of any rank has at most one totally proper wiggle-equivalence class. Every
mutation-acyclic quiver has such a class. For an acyclic quiver, it is the class obtained by wrapping
a topological ordering around the circle.
Remark 2.3. A practical consequence used throughout this paper is that if a quiver is known to be
mutation-acyclic and has only one proper wiggle class, then that class is the totally proper one.

   An acyclic quiver has a cyclic ordering, induced by a topological ordering, in which all its entries

invariant bounds every arrow multiplicity of the acyclic quiver. Since m(Q) is a mutation invariant,
it bounds the multiplicities of every acyclic quiver in the mutation class of Q, though not those of
Q itself. The next lemma makes this precise.

   Lemma 2.4 gives a finite obstruction test, which we name for later use. We say that a connected
four-vertex quiver Q is Markov-excluded if it has at least one proper wiggle class, and for every
proper wiggle class [ω]wig no connected acyclic four-vertex quiver A, with cyclic ordering ωA induced
by a topological ordering, has both MωA (A) = Mω (Q) and p(A)2 = p(Q)2 .
Lemma 2.6. Let Q be a connected four-vertex quiver. If Q has no proper wiggle class, or if Q
is Markov-excluded, then Q is mutation-cyclic. Whether Q is Markov-excluded can be decided by
a finite computation. For four-vertex quivers, having no proper wiggle class is equivalent to being
a vortex, by [FN26, Proposition 13.2]. The resulting obstruction to mutation-acyclicity is stated
separately in Lemma 2.7 below.

Lemma 2.8 ([CK06, Corollary 4 and its proof]). Let A and B be mutation-equivalent acyclic

up to relabeling, and a mutation class contains only finitely many acyclic quivers.

of the exchange graph [CK06, Corollary 4], and the statement about sources and sinks is in its
proof; for acyclic seeds see also [BMRT07]. A mutation between two acyclic quivers is necessarily at
a source or a sink. Indeed, if a vertex k of an acyclic quiver is neither a source nor a sink, there is
a path i → k → j. There can be no arrow j → i, since that would already give a directed cycle.
Mutation at k therefore leaves a positive number of arrows i → j, together with the reversed arrows
j → k → i, producing a directed triangle.

                     4. Local replacements along mutation sequences
   This section sets up the technical input for the height bound of Theorem 5.1. The guiding idea is
that whenever a mutation sequence climbs above a certain height level and later comes back down,
the stretch spent above that level (a “high segment,” defined below) can be rerouted below it.
The three lemmas here justify this locally by following how the first two mutation vertices of the
high segment are joined. With no edge between them the two mutations commute; with one arrow
the pentagon relation supplies detours; with at least two arrows an attempted detour is forced to
keep growing. These three regimes mirror the behavior of ice quivers with exactly two mutable
vertices. Soukup shows that the mutation class of such an ice quiver has at most four quivers when
the two mutable vertices are not joined and at most ten when they are joined by a single arrow,
while in any nontrivial case the arrow multiplicities grow without bound when they are joined by
two or more arrows [Sou25, Theorem 1.3]. Here the additional task is to control the height along a
mutation sequence (what we will call a route) and the possibility of mutating at a third vertex.
The proofs are case analyses, given in Appendices A and B, and every calculation in them is verified
by the program check_proofs.py.

5.2. A finite decision algorithm. For a four-vertex quiver Q with at least one proper wiggle
class, put
                                                                                           
(13)           H(Q) = max {ht(Q)} ∪ Mω (Q) : [ω]wig a proper wiggle class of Q                   .
There are six cyclic orderings to examine, written as ⟨1, v2 , v3 , v4 ⟩, where (v2 , v3 , v4 ) ranges over the
permutations of (2, 3, 4).
Corollary 5.2. Let Q be a four-vertex quiver. If Q has no proper wiggle class, it is mutation-cyclic.
Otherwise, Q is mutation-acyclic if and only if a sequence of mutations through quivers of height
at most H(Q) takes Q to an acyclic quiver, and such a sequence can be chosen of length at most
(2H(Q) + 1)6 − 1. In particular, mutation-acyclicity of four-vertex quivers is decidable by a finite
search.
Proof. If Q has no proper wiggle class, it is mutation-cyclic by Theorem 2.2, since a totally proper
class is proper. If Q is mutation-acyclic, its totally proper class is one of its proper classes, so
m(Q) ≤ H(Q), and Theorem 5.1 gives a sequence to an acyclic quiver within height H(Q). There
are (2H(Q) + 1)6 labeled integer quivers of height at most H(Q). Starting at Q, search its connected
component in the mutation graph restricted to these quivers. Record each quiver once and test it for
16    KYMANI T. K. ARMSTRONG-WILLIAMS, EDWARD HIRST, BLAKE JACKSON, AND KYU-HWAN LEE


acyclicity. If an acyclic quiver is found, the recorded mutation sequence proves mutation-acyclicity.
If the component is exhausted, Theorem 5.1 proves that Q is mutation-cyclic. The search examines
at most four mutations at each recorded quiver. A shortest path cannot repeat a quiver, giving the
length bound.                                                                                     □

  The search can be implemented as follows, storing a predecessor and a mutation vertex for each
newly found quiver.
     1. If Q has no proper wiggle class, return mutation-cyclic. Otherwise compute H = H(Q),
        initialize a queue with Q, and mark Q as visited.
     2. If the queue is empty, return mutation-cyclic. Otherwise remove its next quiver R.
     3. If R is acyclic, return mutation-acyclic together with the path reconstructed from the
        predecessor records.
     4. For each vertex v ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4}, compute S = µv R. If ht(S) ≤ H and S has not been visited,
        mark it, store its predecessor R and mutation vertex v, and add it to the queue. Return to
        step 2.
The negative answer in step 2 is returned only after the entire reachable component within the
fixed height bound has been exhausted. Theorem 5.1 is what makes a negative answer conclusive.
In practice, one can first test acyclicity, mutation-cyclic full triangles, vortices, and the exclusions
of Lemma 2.6, and can process disconnected components separately. Caching acyclic endpoint
signatures (M, p2 ) and quotienting states by relabeling can further reduce repeated work; to recover
a labeled mutation word, book-keep the relabeling used at each step. The function decide in
quiver4.py implements this procedure.

5.3. The size of the search space.
Corollary 5.3. If a four-vertex quiver Q with a proper wiggle class has height h, then
                                       H(Q) ≤ h4 + 4h3 + 6h2 .
For h ≥ 1, the decision search of Corollary 5.2 has O(h24 ) states and can be implemented in a time
polynomial in h. This is a pseudo-polynomial bound, since it is polynomial in the numerical arrow
multiplicities, but may be exponential in the number of bits used to write them. When h = 0, the
quiver is already acyclic.
Proof. In every cyclic ordering, the six entries have absolute value at most h. Formula (4) has six
quadratic terms, four cubic terms and one quartic term. The triangle inequality therefore gives
                                     |Mω (Q)| ≤ 6h2 + 4h3 + h4 .
For integer h ≥ 1, the right side also bounds h, so it bounds H(Q). Corollary 5.2 now gives at most
                                                           6
                                  2(h4 + 4h3 + 6h2 ) + 1        = O(h24 )
states. If h = 0, all entries and all Markov values vanish, so H(Q) = 0 and there is just one state.
   For the running time, each state requires at most four mutations and an acyclicity test. A tested
mutation has entries of magnitude at most H(Q) + H(Q)2 , even if it is discarded for exceeding the
bound. Thus all arithmetic uses integers with O(log(h + 1)) bits when h ≥ 1. Store the visited
six-tuples in a balanced search tree. A lookup then takes O(log N ) comparisons for N states,
and each comparison takes O(log(h + 1)) bit operations. Since N = O(h24 ), lookup and exact
arithmetic take time polynomial in log(h + 1) per state. Thus, with basic integer arithmetic and
balanced-tree storage, one obtains O(h24 log2 (h + 1)) bit operations and O(h24 log(h + 1)) bits of
storage, including the queue and predecessor records. In particular, the running time is polynomial
in h, as claimed.                                                                                 □

   After being prompted at length by the authors, the main results were discovered by GPT-
Astra (OpenAI) and the original statements were formalized in Lean (https://github.com/KTKAW/
rank4-paper). The results follow from many well-known results in the literature, along with the
AI-discovery of the family Q(t) and many case checks carried out by AI systems. The exact checks
in all of the appendix calculations are also verified by Python scripts. The text in this article
was entirely organized and reviewed by humans. Significant portions of the text were generated,

results as far as possible, to simplify the proofs, to give appropriate credit to the human results that
came before, and to bring the exposition up to standard.
