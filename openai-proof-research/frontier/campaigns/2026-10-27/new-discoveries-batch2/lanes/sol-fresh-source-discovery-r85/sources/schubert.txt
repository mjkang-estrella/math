                            SOME SCHUBERT SHENANIGANS

                                           RICHARD P. STANLEY


        Abstract. We give a conjectured evaluation of the determinant of a certain matrix D̃(n, k).
        The entries of D̃(n, k) are either 0 or specializations Sw (1, . . . , 1) of Schubert polynomials.
        The conjecture implies that the weak order of the symmetric group Sn has the strong Sperner
        property. A number of peripheral results and problems are also discussed.



                                             1. Introduction
   The primary purpose of this paper is to present a conjectured evaluation of the determinant
of a certain matrix D̃(n, k), where 0 ≤ k < 21 n2 , related to the weak order Wn of the
                                                     

symmetric group Sn . The entries of D̃(n, k) are either 0 or Sw (1, . . . , 1), the sum of the
coefficients of the Schubert polynomial Sw , for certain permutations w. If det D̃(n, k) 6= 0
for all 0 ≤ k < 21 n2 , then Wn has the strong Sperner property. It is currently unknown
                      
whether Wn has just the ordinary Sperner property. We have an explicit conjecture for the
value det D̃(n, k) which shows in particular that it is nonzero, but we have no idea how to
prove this conjecture.
   The above problem suggests some other questions related to the numbers

One can go on to consider when νw = 3, etc., though a “nice” characterization for every m
of when νw = m does not seem very likely.

                            3. Generalizations and refinements
   We mentioned that the entries of the matrix D̃(n, k) are either 0 or specializations νw of
Schubert polynomials Sw . It is natural to ask what happens when we replace νw with Sw
itself. However, we have been unable to discover anything interesting in this regard.
   We can also consider a q-analogue of Conjecture 2.2. Theorem 2.4 of [2] (originally con-
jectured by Macdonald in [4, (6.11q ?)]) suggests that the correct q-analogue of D̃(n, k) is
obtained by replacing the entry Sw (1, 1, . . . , 1) of D̃(n, k) with Sw (1, q, q 2, . . . , q m−1 ) (where
Sw is a polynomial in x1 , . . . , xm ). However, the data is not too encouraging. For instance,
when (n, k) = (5, 2) the determinant is q 36 times an irreducible polynomial (over Q) of degree
20. For (n, k) = (5, 3) the determinant has the form
                           ±q 26 (q 2 + q + 1)3 (q 4 + q 3 + q 2 + q + 1)3 P (q),
where P (q) is an irreducible polynomial of degree 28. Perhaps Sw (1, q, q 2, . . . , q m−1 ) needs
to be multiplied by some power of q.
   It is natural to ask what happens when we replace the weak order Wn with the (strong)
Bruhat order on Sn , which we continue to denote as Sn . For the Bruhat order the strong
Sperner property is quite easy to see [7, p. 182]. However, it is still interesting to ask
for an analogue E(n, k) of the matrix D(n, k). There are reasons to define the operator

   Unlike the situation for the weak order, the Bruhat analogue Bn of Nn is the cohomology
ring of a (smooth, projective) variety, viz., the complete flag variety Fn = GL(n, C)/B,
where B is a Borel subgroup. Thus the hard Lefschetz theorem for Fn immediately implies
the strong Sperner property for Sn [7], but as stated above this is easy to prove directly.
A variety related to Fn is the Grassmann variety Gr(n, k) of k-dimensional subspaces of
Cn . Here the determinants of the matrices analogous to D(n, k) and E(n, k) have been
computed by Proctor [6]. He also computed determinants corresponding to products of
complex projective spaces, where the corresponding poset is a product of chains.
   A canonical question suggested by Conjecture 2.2 is its possible extension to other finite
Coxeter groups. We have not made any computations in this direction.
   Another possible extension of Conjecture 2.2 is to refine the determinant of D̃(n, k). The
two most natural refinements are the characteristic polynomial (or equivalently the eigen-
values) of D̃(n, k) and the Smith normal form (SNF) over Z of D̃(n, k). The characteristic
polynomial depends on the order of the rows and columns. We have done some computation
using every possible order and have found nothing interesting. For instance, the eigenvalues
are in general not integers. On the other hand, the SNF does not depend on the order of
the rows and columns, and the data looks intriguing. For instance, the ratio between two
consecutive diagonal terms is always quite small. Write f (n, k) for the vector of diagonal
entries of the SNF of D̃(n, k). Here is some data (which we have not attempted to analyze,
except for k = 1 discussed below). We use exponents to denote repetition, e.g., (15 , 32 )
stands for (1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 3, 3).

det D̃ ′ (n, 1) = det D̃ ′ (n − 1, 1), so the proof follows.

                            4. Two-term Schubert polynomials
   The appearance of the numbers νw = Sw (1, 1, . . . , 1) as entries of the matrix D̃(n, k)
suggest the question of how “complicated” these numbers can be. The simplest situation is
when Sw is a single monomial, so νw = 1. It is well-known [4] that νw = 1 if and only if w
is 132-avoiding (or dominant).
Conjecture 4.1. We have νw = 2 if and only if w = a1 · · · an has exactly one subsequence
ai , aj , ak in the pattern 132, i.e., ai < ak < aj .
