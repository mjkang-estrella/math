                                                             Characterizing graphs with high inducibility
                                                                                           Richard Ueltzen ∗


                                                                                                Abstract
                                                     For a positive integer k and a graph H on k vertices, we are interested in the inducibility of
                                                 H, denoted ind(H), which is defined as the maximum possible probability that choosing k vertices
                                                 uniformly at random from a large graph G, they induce a copy of H. It follows from the resolved
                                                 Edge-statistics conjecture that if H ̸∈ {Kk , K̄k }, then ind(H) ≤ 1/e + ok (1). Equality holds for the
                                                 star graph K1,k−1 , the graph with a single edge on k vertices and their complements. We prove that




arXiv:2411.17362v1 [math.CO] 26 Nov 2024
                                                 for all other graphs H, we have ind(H) ≤ c + ok (1) for an absolute constant c < 1/e. Moreover, we
                                                 explicitly characterize all graphs with inducibility bounded away from zero. Namely, we show that
                                                 this is the class of graphs H for which there is a set V0 ⊆ V (H) of bounded size with the property
                                                 that all permutations of V (H)\V0 extend to an automorphism of H.


                                           1     Introduction
                                           Consider a positive integer k and a graph H on k vertices. We are interested in the maximum possible
                                           probability that a uniformly randomly chosen k-vertex set in a large graph G induces a copy of H.
                                           This quantity is the inducibility of H, which was first introduced by Pippenger and Golumbic [9]. For
                                           a more precise definition, suppose that n ≥ k is a positive integer, and G is a graph on n vertices.
                                           Taking W to be a uniformly random set of k vertices of G, we define the induced density of H in G as
                                           I(H, G) := P[G[W ] ∼  = H], the probability that W induces a copy of H in G. We denote the maximum of
                                           I(H, G) over all n-vertex graphs G by I(H, n). By an averaging argument, n 7→ I(H, n) is monotonically
                                           decreasing. Hence, the inducibility of H, defined as ind(H) := limn→∞ I(H, n) ∈ [0, 1], exists.
                                               In recent years, the question of bounding the inducibility of graphs received much attention (see, for
                                           example, [2, 4, 6, 12]). However, the exact inducibility is only known for a few small graphs as well as for
                                           specific classes of graphs. For example, the inducibility of the cycle on six vertices is unknown.
                                               In this paper, we give general asymptotic upper bounds for graph inducibilities. This follows a direction
                                           of research proposed by Alon, Hefetz, Krivelevich and Tyomkyn [1, Conjecture 1.2], who conjectured that
                                           if H is not the complete graph Kk or its complement K k , then ind(H) ≤ 1/e + ok (1). Since inducibility
                                           is invariant under taking complements,  i.e., ind(H) = ind(H̄), it is enough to consider the case where
                                           the number of edges of H is ℓ ≤ 12 k2 . Kwan, Sudakov, and Tran [7] proved the conjecture in the case
                                           Ck ≤ ℓ ≤ 12 k2 for some absolute constant C. Subsequently, the proof of the conjecture was independently
                                                          

                                           completed by Fox and Sauermann [3], as well as by Martinsson, Mousset, Noever, and Trujić [8]. The
                                           papers above also resolved a stronger conjecture of Alon et al. [1, Conjecture 1.1] on the number of edges
                                           induced by a uniformly random k-vertex set. In conclusion, the following theorem holds:

Definition 1.3. For any positive integer D and any graph H, we say that H is D-tame if there exists

   • V (H)\V0 is either a clique or a stable set,

   • for each v ∈ V0 , v is either connected to no vertex in V (H)\V0 or to all vertices in V (H)\V0 .

2.1     Proof approach
The rest of this paper contains the proofs of Theorems 2.2 to 2.4. Section 3 begins with three lemmas
that, very roughly, provide upper bounds on the inducibility of a graph H with k vertices and ℓ edges, if
for some fixed C and ε > 0,
    1. the number of edges in H satisfies ℓ ≤ Ck and H has maximum degree at least εk,
    2. the maximum degree in H is at most εk and (1 − ok (1))k vertices in H have degree τ for some τ ≥ 1,

    3. the number of edges in H satisfies ℓ ≤ Ck and there is no τ ≥ 0 such that (1 − ok (1))k vertices have
       degree τ ,

respectively. The first two lemmas are proven in Sections 4 and 5. The third lemma is a direct consequence
of a result of Martinsson et al. [8, Claim 3.3]. An alternative proof can be found in the author’s bachelor’s
thesis [11, Section 6]. For any graph H with k vertices and ℓ ≤ Ck edges, at least one of the three lemmas
implies an upper bound on ind(H), unless (1 − ok (1))k vertices in H have degree τ = 0, i.e., m(H) ≤ ok (k).
This is why Theorem 2.2 follows from the lemmas, as we show in Section 3.1. The proof of Theorem 2.4
in Section 3.2 is similar. A key difference is that we must address the case m(H) ≤ ok (k), for which we
will use the following corollary of two lemmas due to Fox and Sauermann [3, Lemmas 3.1 and 3.2]:
Theorem 2.5. For any fixed γ > 0, there exists a fixed positive integer D = D(γ) such that for all positive
integers k and any graph H with k vertices and D ≤ m(H) ≤ k/32,
                                                  ind(H) ≤ γ.
    Theorem 2.3 is proven in Section 6 using a key lemma that we prove in Section 7. The proof strategy
for the key lemma is a modified version of an argument by Martinsson et al. [8, Proposition 2.1]: We pick
vertices v1 , v2 . . . of a large graph G uniformly at random, independently and consecutively, and count
the number X of times ’something happens.’ We will show that, conditioned on G[{v1 , . . . , vk }] ∼
                                                                                                   = H, X
is very concentrated. Due to the setup of the random experiment, we can determine the distribution of X
and show that it is not concentrated. In total, this yields an upper bound on the induced density of H in
G. Section 6 is concerned with developing the tools to define ’something happening’ in such a way that,
conditioned on G[{v1 , . . . , vk }] ∼
                                     = H, X is indeed concentrated.

2.2    Notation
For a graph G, we denote the sets of vertices and edges of G with V (G) and E(G), respectively. For
a vertex set W ⊆ V (G), we denote the induced subgraph of G with vertices W as G[W ]. Moreover, for
v ∈ V (G), the graph G − v is defined as G[V (G)\{v}]. For v ∈ V (G) and W ⊆ V (G), we let NG (v, W ) be
the set of neighbors of v in W and let dG (v, W ) := |NG (v, W )| be the degree of v into W . We abbreviate
NG (v, V (G)) as NG (v) and dG (v, V (G)) as dG (v). We denote the maximum degree of a vertex in G by
∆(G). Furthermore, we denote the number of non-isolated vertices in G by m(G) and define M (G) as
the induced subgraph of G restricted to its non-isolated vertices. We denote the complete graph on k
vertices as Kk and the complete bipartite graph with parts of sizes a and b as Ka,b . When there is no risk
of confusion, we omit the subscript G from all notation.
    We denote the set of integers     with Z. For a set S and a nonnegative integer f , we denote the set of
f -element subsets of S by Sf .

    The probability measure in our probability space is always denoted by P. For an event A, we denote
the complement of A as Ac and its indicator random variable as 1A .
    In our asymptotic notation, we indicate the variable interpreted as going to infinity in the subscript.
For example, an expression is ok (1) if, for any fixed values of the constants, it approaches 0 as k → ∞,
regardless of the values of all other variables. In this context, a variable is treated as constant if it is
specified as fixed in its definition or if it is listed after a ’|’ in the subscript. For example, an expression is
on|k (1) if it approaches 0 as n → ∞ for any fixed value of k.

6    Proof of Theorem 2.3
We start with defining detectable vertices, which are crucial in Lemma 6.7 that we prove in Section 7. In
the rest of this section, we will prove general properties of detectable vertices. In particular, Claim 6.4
provides an equivalent explicit criterion for when a vertex is detectable.
Definition 6.1. For a graph H and a non-isolated vertex v ∈ V (H), we say that v is obscure in H
if there exist non-isolated, distinct vertices v1 , v2 with dH−v1 (v2 ) > 0, and an induced subgraph H ′ of
H − v1 − v2 with M (H − v) ∼  = H ′ . We say that a non-isolated vertex v ∈ V (H) is detectable in H if v is
not obscure in H.
Definition 6.2. The number of vertices with degree 1 in H is denoted by m1 (H) and the number of
vertices with degree at least 2 in H is denoted by m≥2 (H). We say that a non-isolated vertex v ∈ V (H)
