                                                                                        1. Introduction
                                              This paper is concerned with numerical semigroups. These are submonoids of N with finite
                                           complement in N, a topic widely studied by several authors and from different perspectives. One of
                                           the nicest features that have been studied is the arrangement of the set of all numerical semigroups as
                                           a rooted tree, called the semigroup tree. The semigroup tree has the set of all numerical semigroups
                                           as its set of vertices, and the set of all pairs (S, S ∪ {F(S)}) as its set of edges, where F(S) is the
                                           greatest element in N \ S (when S 6= N). The root of this tree is N. For these definitions see also
                                           [23, Chapter 7, Section 1]. The building of the semigroup tree can be performed algorithmically
                                           and can be implemented in a programming language or by using a computer algebra software. We
                                           mention in particular the GAP [21] package numericalsgps [14], that contains many routines to
                                           deal with numerical semigroups. The semigroup tree is used in particular to produce all numerical
                                           semigroups of a given genus, where the genus of a numerical semigroup S is the number |N \ S|. It
                                           is useful, for instance, to count the number ng of all numerical semigroups of a given genus g, as in
                                           [3] which led to the proposal of several interesting conjectures. Recall one of them, that states that
                                           the sequence ng has a Fibonacci-like behaviour, later proved in [27]. Another interesting argument
                                           related to numerical semigroups is the study of a conjecture posed for the first time by H. Wilf in
                                           [26]. The semigroup tree has been used to test Wilf’s conjecture up to a given genus, for instance up
                                           to genus 60 by Fromentin and Hivert in [20]. Some improvements have been developed to explore
                                           the semigroup tree both from a computational point of view (for instance in [8, 20]) and from a
                                           theoretical point of view, depending on the properties one wants to examine (see [4, 7] or the more
                                           recent [12]). The semigroup tree is related to the function on numerical semigroups defined by
                                           S 7→ S ∪ {F(S)}. A function on numerical semigroups is called a numerical semigroup transform
                                           (or simply a transform). Given a transform it is possible, emulating the building of the semigroup
                                           tree, to arrange the set of numerical semigroups in a family of graphs, possibly trees. Thinking
                                           about Wilf’s conjecture, we are interested in defining a transform that fixes two invariants involved
                                           in Wilf’s conjecture and studying its consequences. The notion of special gap and the technique that
                                           allows one to obtain the irreducible numerical semigroup that contains a fixed numerical semigroup
                                           (see [24] and [23, Chapter 3]) have inspired us to propose the definition of a transform with this
                                           particular features. We call such a transform A and we find certain numerical semigroups that will
                                           be the roots of the arranged rooted trees, and show they have a simple structure. This bring us to
                                           the definition of special numerical semigroups. Moreover, Manuel Delgado who read a first preprint
                                           of this paper, pointed out to us that in [6] a transform having similar features had been introduced.

                             2. Preliminaries and known results
   Recall that a numerical semigroup S is a submonoid of N such that N \ S is a finite set. It is
well known that every numerical semigroup admits a unique finite minimal system of generators,
that is, there exists a finite subset G(S) of S such that every element of S is obtained as a linear
combination of elements in G(S) with coefficients in N and it is minimal in the sense that no
proper subset of G(S) has the same property. The elements in G(S) are often called minimal
generators. Obviously an element s ∈ S is not a minimal generator if and only if s = s1 + s2
with s1 , s2 ∈ S \ {0}. If a set A generates a numerical semigroup S, in the sense described above,
we usually write S = hAi. Moreover every (minimal or not) system of generators of a numerical
semigroup is characterized by the fact that the greatest common divisor of all its elements is 1. For
these and other interesting properties related to numerical semigroups a very good reference is [23].
If S is a numerical semigroup, we provide here several of its most important invariants that are
useful for this paper:
      • H(S) = N \ S is called the set of gaps of S.
      • g(S) = | H(S)| is called the genus of S.
      • F(S) = max(H(S)) if S 6= N, conventionally F(N) = −1. It is called the Frobenius number
        of S.
      • m(S) = min(S \ {0}) is called the muliplicity of S.
      • n(S) = |{s ∈ S | s < F(S)}|, often referred to as the number of left elements of S, if S 6= N.
        Conventionally n(N) = 1.
      • e(S) = | G(S)|, the number of minimal generators, called the embedding dimension of S.
   Observe that if for some s ∈ S we have {s, s + 1, . . . , s + m(S) − 1} ⊂ S then s + n ∈ S for all
n ∈ N. We provide now some known results that we need for the forthcoming sections. The first
one is quite easy to prove and we omit its proof.
Proposition 2.1. Let S be a numerical semigroup and x ∈ S. S \ {x} is a numerical semigroup if
and only if x is a minimal generator of S.
   A numerical semigroup S is called irreducible if it cannot be expressed as an intersection of
two numerical semigroups properly containing S. An irreducible numerical semigroup S is called
symmetric if F(S) is odd, pseudo-symmetric if F(S) is even. There are several characterizations for
irreducible numerical semigroups. A useful result is the following:
Proposition 2.2 ([23], Corollary 4.5). Let S be a numerical semigroup. Then
    (1) S is symmetric if and only if g(S) = F(S)+1
                                                2
    (2) S is pseudo-symmetric if and only if g(S) = F(S)+2

   It has been proved that Wilf’s conjecture is satisfied by several classes of numerical semigroups,

                                  3. Special numerical semigroups
Definition 3.1. Let S be a numerical semigroup. We say that S is a special numerical semigroup
if
                        (SG(S) \ {F(S)}) ∩ {h ∈ H(S) | h > m(S)} = ∅
Standard examples of special numerical semigroups are the following:
      • If S is an irreducible numerical semigroup then SG(S) = {F(S)}, so it is special.
      • Recall that a numerical semigroup S is called ordinary if there exists c ∈ N such that
        S = {s ∈ N | s ≥ c} ∪ {0}, denoted by S = {0, c, →}. If S is ordinary then {h ∈ H(S) | h >
        m(S)} = ∅, so it is special. Moreover n(S) = 1 and e(S) = c.
    Other special numerical semigroups are described in the next proposition.
Proposition 3.2. Let g ∈ N with g > 2 and n ∈ [2, g]. Then S = {0, g, g +1, . . . , g +n−2, g +n, →}
is a special numerical semigroup with F(S) = g + n − 1, g(S) = g and n(S) = n. Furthermore:
     (1) If n = g or n = g − 1 then S is irreducible.
     (2) If 3 ≤ n < g − 1 then S is not irreducible and e(S) = g − 1.
     (3) If g > 3 and n = 2 then S is not irreducible and e(S) = g.

Proof. It is easy to verify that S is a numerical semigroup with F(S) = g + n − 1, g(S) = g and
n(S) = n. Moreover it is special because for every h ∈ H(S) with h 6= F(S) we have h < m(S) = g.
(1) If n = g then F(S) + 1 = 2g, so S is symmetric. If n = g − 1 then F(S) + 2 = 2g, so S is
pseudo-symmetric (see [23, Corollary 4.5]). In both cases it is irreducible.
(2) If 3 ≤ n < g − 1, S is not irreducible, because F(S) − (g − 1) = g + n − 1 − g + 1 = n ∈    / S (see [23,
Proposition 4.4]) and its minimal generators are {g, g + 1, . . . , g + n − 2, g + n, g + n + 1, . . . , 2g − 1},
in fact 2g + j = g + (g + j) if j ∈ {0, 1, . . . , n − 2, n}.
(3) If n = 2 and g > 3, S is not irreducible because F(S) − (g − 1) = 2 and it is easy to verify that
its minimal generators are {g, g + 2, . . . , 2g − 1, 2g + 1}.                                                 

   The transform A fixes all invariants involved in Wilf’s conjecture except for the embedding
dimension e(S). If T = A(S), we are interested in studying when e(T ) increases or decreases with
respect to e(S).
Proposition 4.7. Let S be a non special numerical semigroup, h = max(SG(S) \ {F(S)}) and
T = A(S). Then 2 m(S) and h are minimal generators of T .
Proof. It is evident that h is a minimal generator of T . If 2 m(S) is not a minimal generator of T ,
then 2 m(S) = t1 + t2 with t1 , t2 ∈ T . But m(T ) > m(S) so for all t ∈ T we have t > m(S) and
2 m(S) = t1 + t2 > 2 m(S), which is a contradiction.                                              
Lemma 4.8. Let S be a non special numerical semigroup, h = max(SG(S)\{F(S)}) and T = A(S).
Let {m(S) = n1 < n2 < . . . < nt } be the set of minimal generators of S. If m(S) + h ≥ nr for some
r ∈ {1, . . . , t}, then n2 , n3 , . . . , nr are minimal generators of T . In particular if m(S) + h ≥ nt−1
then e(T ) ≥ e(S).
Proof. Let ni ≤ m(S) + h and suppose that ni is not a minimal generator of T . Then ni = t1 + t2
with t1 , t2 ∈ T . Since ni is a minimal generator of S the only possibilities are that ni = s + h with
s ∈ S \ {m(S)} or ni = 2h. Since s > m(S) and h > m(S) in both cases we obtain ni > m(S) + h,
which is a contradiction. For the last statement, by Proposition 4.7 and the above discussion, if
m(S) + h ≥ nt−1 we have that e(T ) ≥ |{n2 , n3 , . . . , nt−1 , 2n1 , h}| = |{n1 , n2 , . . . , nt }| = e(S). 

                       6. Another transform and the related trees
   Let S be a non ordinary numerical semigroup. The Frobenius number of the semigroup S ∪{F(S)}
is defined as the sub-Frobenius number of S and we denote it by u(S). In particular
                                     u(S) = max(H(S) \ {F(S)})

In [6] the following transform is defined:
Definition 6.1. Let Q be the set of all non ordinary and non almost-ordinary numerical semigroups.
B : Q → S is a transform defined by:

                                   B(S) = (S ∪ {u(S)}) \ {m(S)}
   As mentioned in [6], if S ∈ Q it is easy to show that there exists n ∈ N such that B n (S) is
almost-ordinary. The two transforms A and B are quite similar, observe for instance that also the
transform B fixes all invariants involved in Wilf’s conjecture except for the embedding dimension
e(S). We pointed out some differences.
Remark 6.2. Let S ∈ Q, observe that if [F(S) − m(S) + 1, F(S)[ ∩ H(S) 6= ∅ then u(S) ∈ [F(S) −
m(S) + 1, F(S)[ and it is a special gap, since u(S) + m(S) > F(S). Moreover there are not gaps of
S different from F(S) and greater than u(S). In particular we have:
    1) u(S) = max(SG(S) \ {F(S)}) if [F(S) − m(S) + 1, F(S)[ ∩ H(S) 6= ∅.
    2) u(S) = F(S) − m(S) if [F(S) − m(S) + 1, F(S)[⊂ S.
Therefore in the first case we have B(S) = A(S). The second condition shows where the difference
between A and B occurs.

                     7. Concluding remarks and possible developments
    In this paper we have provided some results concerning Wilf’s conjecture, that is, we reduce
the study of Wilf’s conjecture to the study of the set Fg,n        ′                               ′ , with
                                                                      of all leaves of the tree Tg,n
             g
12 < n < 3 , or considering the set Fg,n of all leaves of the tree Tg,n and to the transformed of some
of them using A. In fact, it is known by [13, Proposition 3.15] that if S is a numerical semigroup
such that n(S) ≤ 12 then S satisfies Wilf’s conjecture, and the same occurs if 3 n(S) ≥ g(S) as
a consequence of [15, Corollary 2.7]. This may be an interesting result from a theoretical and a
computational point of view. Moreover, with computational methods, in [20] it has been proved
that every numerical semigroup of genus g with g ≤ 60 satisfies Wilf’s conjecture. This bound
has been improved up to g = 80, as cited in [17]. Considering such results, if one wants to find
an example of a semigroup not satisfying Wilf’s conjecture, one may investigate the trees Tg,n and
T ′ g,n with g > 80 and with 12 < n < 3g , in particular not the whole trees but their set of leaves.
The above results allow to compute these trees algorithmically. A possible development can be to
study the trees Tg,n and Tg,n ′ from a computational point of view.

An important reduction was introduced in [16], where an invariant E(S) is associated to each
numerical semigroup, studied also in [11], where it is called an Eliahou number. It is defined
as E(S) = |Q| n(S) − q|D| + ρ, where Q is the set of minimal generators smaller than F(S), D
is the set of the elements x ∈ [F(S) + 1, F(S) + m(S)] such that x is not a minimal generator,
and q, ρ ∈ N are the only numbers such that F(S) + 1 = q m(S) − ρ. It has been proved, in
[16], that if E(S) ≥ 0 then S satisfies Wilf’s conjecture. So the study of Wilf’s conjecture is
reduced to those numerical semigroups with E(S) < 0. Some families with an infinite number
of numerical semigroups satisfying E(S) < 0 are provided in [11] and [18]. By [16] we know
also that the proportion of numerical semigroups with E(S) < 0, with respect to all numerical
semigroups, is asymptotically small. Just to consider an interesting feature, the numerical
semigroup with the smallest genus satisfying E(S) < 0 has genus 43. So, with confidence, we
can state that Eliahou’s result is a much finer reduction for studying Wilf’s conjecture. In
fact, even if in this paper it is not investigated the asymptotic proportion of the semigroups in
Fg,n and Fg,n  ′ , there is a huge number of them, since there exist such numerical semigroups for

each value of the genus and it is possible that the trees Tg,n and Tg,n         ′   may contain too many
leaves compared with the non-leaves. We pose the following problem: where are the numerical
semigroups S, such that E(S) < 0, located in Tg,n or Tg,n         ′ ? Are there any of them (or all) in
           ′                    ′
the set Fg,n of the leaves of Tg,n ? Or in the set Fg,n of the leaves of Tg,n or in their transformed by A?

   We also mention that in [9] the ordinarization transform (defined in the preliminaries of this
paper) was introduced in a more general setting than numerical semigroups, that is for submonoids
of Nd with finite complement in Nd , a class of monoids introduced in [19]. For such a class of
submonoids of Nd , also a generalization of Wilf’s conjecture was proposed in [10] and in [22]. So, it
could be interesting to investigate if it is possible to formulate a generalization of the definitions of
A and B for submonoids in Nd .
