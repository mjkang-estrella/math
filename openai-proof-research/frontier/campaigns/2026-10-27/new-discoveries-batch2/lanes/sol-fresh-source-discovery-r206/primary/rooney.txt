   Further, D. Kim, R. Kim, Lee, Lim, and So [7] posed the following extension to their work.
Problem 1.4. [7] For an integer n ≥ 4, if (A1 , A2 , . . . , An ) is a BN n-tuple of dice, what is the
least upper bound of the winning probability w(A1 , A2 , . . . , An )?
    In 1965, Trybula [17] classified an upper bound on the winning probability of a BN n-tuple of
dice implicitly. Then, in 2010, Bogdanov [2] found a closed-form expression for an upper bound on
this winning probability, published in Russian. Further, in 2021, Andrzej Komisarski [9] confirmed
the result of Bogdanov using a distinct approach, which is interestingly geometric.
Theorem 1.5. [2, 9] Let (A1 , A2 , . . . , An ) be a BN n-tuple of dice. Then, w(A1 , A2 , . . . , An ) ≤ πn .
    In this paper, we will extend the result of D. Kim, R. Kim, Lee, Lim, and So to n ≥ 4 dice with
Theorem 1.2. Together with Theorem 1.5, this will fully classify the possible winning probabilities
for a balanced and nontransitive n-tuple of dice, answering Problem 1.4.
    We will proceed as follows. In Section 2, we introduce preliminaries and set conventions. In
Section 3, we will prove Theorem 1.2. Finally, in Section 4, we will prove Lemmas 3.2 and 3.3 used
in the proof of Theorem 1.2, including the statement and proof of Lemmas 4.3 and 4.4.

2    Preliminaries
Violating the transitive property, the mere existence of nontransitive n-tuples of dice is fascinating.
As a result, sets of nontransitive dice have been heavily studied, see [1, 3, 10, 11] and further sources
therein.
    Then came the study of balanced n-tuples of dice. First seen in [14], Schaefer and Schweig
looked at n-tuples of dice that were both balanced and nontransitive. Since then, balanced and
nontransitive dice have been further studied in [5, 6, 12, 13], with particular focus on triples and
4-tuples of dice.
    Although Efron dice were popularized in 1970, Hugo Steinhaus and Stanislaw Trybula [15] had
been studying nontransitive random variables since 1959. Following conventions set by Steinhaus
and Trybula, we shall use distinct, consecutive positive integers on each face across all dice, starting
from 1. Under these conventions, the Efron dice shown in Figure 1 would become the dice shown
in Figure 2. Notice that since the relative ordering of the dice is maintained, P (A1 < A2 ), P (A2 <
A3 ), P (A3 < A4 ), and P (A4 < A1 ) remain the same.

Definition 2.1. For an n-tuple of dice O = (A1 , A2 , . . . , An ), a word σ = σ(O) is a sequence of
letters, each of which is A1 , . . . , An−1 or An such that the ith letter in the word is the die that
contains the number i. Further, σ is a (m1 , . . . , mn )-word if Ai has mi sides for all i ∈ {1, . . . , n}.

Naturally, we will call a word σ(O) balanced (resp. nontransitive) if and only if the ordered collection
of dice O is balanced (resp. nontransitive).
    Further, we will make significant use of something we will call central words. For an n-tuple
of dice (A1 , A2 , . . . , An ), let mi be the number of sides on Ai , or, more precisely, the cardinality
of the support of Ai . Then, we denote the length of the word σ = σ(A1 , A2 , . . . , An ) by |σ| =
m1 + m2 + · · · + mn . Now, we define a central word.

5    Potential Future Work
Although we have resolved Problem 1.4, one is yet to resolve the following problem that D. Kim,
R. Kim, Lee, Lim, and So [7] posed.

Problem 5.1. [7] Find all n-tuples (m1 , m2 , . . . , mn ) of positive integers such that there exists a
BN n-tuple of dice (A1 , A2 , . . . , An ) such that Ai has mi sides.

   In [13, 14], Schaefer and Schweig found that for positive integers n, m ≥ 3, there exists a BN
n-tuple of dice, each with m sides. In [7], the n = 3 case of Problem 5.1 was fully resolved. Further,
in April 2025, a preprint by R. Kim [8] resolves the n = 4 case. The proofs for the n = 3 and
n = 4 cases, in [7] and [8] respectively, rely on the least upper bound of the winning probability.
Accordingly, after our result, the extension to n ≥ 5 in Problem 5.1 may be easier to resolve using
similar techniques as the n = 3 and n = 4 cases.
