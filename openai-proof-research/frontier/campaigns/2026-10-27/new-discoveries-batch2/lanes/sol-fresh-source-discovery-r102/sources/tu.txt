 Log-concavity and Approximate Counting for Totally Unimodular Polytopes 
 
 
 Jonathan Leake
 
    
 Maryam Mohammadi Yekta
 
 September 2026 

 Abstract 
    
 We present a new lower bound on the number of lattice points of all totally unimodular polytopes, generalizing previous lower bounds on contingency tables, integer flows, and beyond. Our bound is based on the Gurvits capacity convex optimization problem, and thus our result implies an efficient deterministic algorithm for approximate counting of the lattice points up to an explicit exponential factor. We achieve our bounds by showing that the associated generating polynomials fit into a new general class of log-concave polynomials called  VLC \LC  (“variable-wise log-concavity”). This also implies a conjecture of Ferroni and Higashitani on the evaluations of the Ehrhart polynomials of unimodular polytopes. The essential ingredient for these results is the resolution of Barvinok’s log-concavity conjecture for contingency tables on lines, which was proven using ChatGPT 6 Astra. We conjecture a generalization of Barvinok’s conjecture, which we believe will lead to stronger and more general bounds. 
  
 
 
 1  Introduction 

 
 Totally unimodular polytopes are integral polytopes defined by a totally unimodular constraint matrix; concretely, a convex polytope  Q Q  is totally unimodular (TU) if there exists a totally unimodular matrix  M M  (i.e., all minors are in  { − 1 , 0 , + 1 } \{-1,0,+1\} ) and an integral vector  𝒃 \bm{b}  such that  Q = { 𝒚 ≥ 0 : M ​ 𝒚 = 𝒃 } Q=\{\bm{y}\geq 0:M\bm{y}=\bm{b}\} . Such polytopes have a wide range of applications, with their lattice points often representing integer solutions to various combinatorial problems. These applications include job assignments, contingency tables, bipartite matchings, bipartite independent sets, network flows, routing configurations, and beyond. 
 
 
 In this paper, we address two natural questions for such polytopes: 
 
 1.  
 
 How can we estimate the number of lattice points of such a polytope  Q Q ? 
 
 2.  
 
 How does the number of lattice points of  Q Q  vary with the choice of  𝒃 \bm{b} ? 
 
 
 Answers to both of these questions arise by proving log-concavity properties of generating functions associated to totally unimodular polytopes. 
 
 
 Given a totally unimodular polytope  Q = { 𝒚 ≥ 0 : M ​ 𝒚 = 𝒃 } Q=\{\bm{y}\geq 0:M\bm{y}=\bm{b}\} , there is a natural generating function for its lattice points based on the choice of  𝒃 \bm{b} . Concretely, if  M M  is  m × n m\times n  and has columns  𝒂 1 , … , 𝒂 n \bm{a}_{1},\ldots,\bm{a}_{n}  then 
 

 
 
 P M ​ ( 𝒙 ) = ∏ j = 1 n 1 1 − 𝒙 𝒂 j P_{M}(\bm{x})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{1-\bm{x}^{\bm{a}_{j}}} 
 
 
 is a generating function with the property that the  𝒙 𝒃 \bm{x}^{\bm{b}}  coefficient of  P M ​ ( 𝒙 ) P_{M}(\bm{x})  is precisely the number of lattice points of  Q Q . Both of the above questions then become questions about  P M P_{M} : how can we estimate the coefficients of  P M P_{M} , and how can we understand how the coefficients of  P M P_{M}  relate to one another? 
 
 
 To address these questions, we prove that truncations of  P M P_{M}  satisfy a certain natural log-concavity property called  VLC \LC . This property says that, after applying coefficient extractions and positive evaluations to all but one variable of  P M P_{M} , the coefficients of the remaining polynomial are log-concave. This generalizes various notions of log-concave polynomials (strongly log-concave  [ 30 ] , completely log-concave  [ 2 ] , Lorentzian  [ 13 ] , etc.) which have had widespread applications in mathematics and computer science. 
 
 
 The fact that truncations of  P M P_{M}  are  VLC \LC  is then based on the underlying main technical result of the paper: that the coefficients of  P M P_{M}  are log-concave along any rational line. This says in fact that as  𝒃 \bm{b}  moves along any line, the number of lattice points of  Q Q  forms a log-concave sequence. Barvinok originally asked a more general question of this form for the case of contingency tables  [ 9 ] , and we resolve this line version here for all TU polytopes. This gives a nice answer to question 2, while the full strength of Barvinok’s question for TU polytopes is still open  Question   1.7 . 
 
 
 The  VLC \LC  property then enables us also to answer question 1. The Gurvits capacity method was originally developed to give a simple proof of the van der Waerden conjecture for permanents in  [ 29 ] . This technique implies a deterministic simply exponential approximation algorithm for non-negative matrix permanents as well. Since this original work, the capacity method has seen numerous applications to bounding and deterministically approximating various other combinatorial quantities. The general idea is to use log-concavity properties of a given generating function to approximate its coefficients by an associated convex optimization problem. The  VLC \LC  property is then tailor-made precisely to allow the Gurvits capacity method to work as usual, and thus we can apply this method to  P M P_{M}  and related generating functions.

 1.2  An Open Question 

 
 Theorem   1.4  is in fact based upon a stronger coefficient-wise positivity result ( Theorem   3.1 ) which follows from the bijective proof which we give in  Section   3 . This theorem raises an open question, which is the natural generalization of Barvinok’s question in  [ 9 ] . 
 
 
 Question 1.7 . 
 
 Let  M ∈ ℤ m × n M\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}  be a unimodular matrix and  μ \mu  a probability distribution on  ℤ m \mathbb{Z}^{m}  (perhaps with finite support) such that its mean is  𝐛 ∈ ℤ m \bm{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m} . Is it that case that 
 

 
 
 # ⁡ { 𝒚 ∈ ℤ ≥ 0 n ∣ M ​ 𝒚 = 𝒃 } ≥ ∏ 𝒂 ∈ ℤ m # ​ { 𝒚 ∈ ℤ ≥ 0 n ∣ M ​ 𝒚 = 𝒂 } μ ⁡ ( 𝒂 ) ​ ? \#\{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}\mid M\bm{y}=\bm{b}\}\geq\prod_{\bm{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}}\#\{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}\mid M\bm{y}=\bm{a}\}^{\mu(\bm{a})}? 
 
 
 
 
 
 Note that for functions/distributions over  ℝ m \mathbb{R}^{m} ,  Question   1.7  would immediately follow from  Theorem   1.4 . However, for functions/distributions over  ℤ m \mathbb{Z}^{m} , convexity on lines does not imply global convexity in general. 
 
 
 We believe that a positive answer to  Question   1.7  would enable us to obtain stronger capacity bounds (hopefully simply exponential) in general, using the following idea. Currently, most capacity bounds are proved inductively on each variable of a given polynomial or generating series. Because of this, the bounds often depend on distances of the desired marginals or degree vector (e.g. the  𝒃 \bm{b}  vector) to the boundary of the Newton polyhedron in the coordinate directions. The coordinate direction distance can be very bad for general TU polytopes, and so we need to adapt the application of the capacity bounds to handle this. Given the inequalities of  Question   1.7 , it is perhaps possible that this capacity induction argument may be able to be exchanged with a geometric argument related to distances to the boundary of the Newton polyhedron in any choice of directions. This would yield much better bounds and approximation factors, if possible. 
 
 
 
 1.3  Previous and Related Work 

 
 Bounding and approximately counting lattice points in totally unimodular
polytopes has attracted considerable attention. A variety of bounds and
approximation algorithms, both randomized and deterministic, are known
for important classes of such polytopes. On the randomized side, fully
polynomial randomized approximation schemes (FPRASs) have been obtained
for bipartite perfect matchings  [ 33 ] , binary contingency tables
 [ 12 ] , and nonnegative integer contingency tables under restrictions
such as a fixed number of rows or sufficiently large margins
 [ 16 ,  20 ,  39 ] . Related results apply to cell-bounded contingency
tables with a fixed number of rows and to integral flows with sufficiently
large tight capacities  [ 15 ] . For certain classes of smooth margins,
a randomized approximation algorithm runs in time quasipolynomial in the
total sum of the table entries  [ 8 ] . More recently, rapid mixing
of the natural swap chain was established for binary contingency tables
with arbitrary feasible margins  [ 23 ] . 
 
 
 On the deterministic side, matrix scaling and polynomial capacity methods
give polynomial-time exponential approximations for counting bipartite
perfect matchings  [ 37 ,  28 ,  29 ] , with subsequent improvements
 [ 27 ,  4 ] . Recent preprints obtain a subexponential approximation
factor  [ 34 ]  and a  ( 1 + ε ) n (1+\varepsilon)^{n}  approximation factor for every
fixed  ε > 0 \varepsilon>0   [ 19 ] , where  n n  is the number of vertices on
each side of the bipartition. Convex optimization and polynomial capacity
methods also give (often simply) exponential estimates for contingency tables
 [ 10 ,  11 ,  31 ,  14 ]  and integer flows  [ 10 ,  36 ,  35 ] .
Computationally efficient asymptotic formulas are available in suitable
smooth-margin regimes  [ 6 ] . For contingency tables with a fixed
number of rows, deterministic fully polynomial-time approximation schemes
(FPTASs) are known  [ 24 ] , with improved running times in the
two-row case  [ 1 ] . 
 
 
 Beyond these special classes, other positive results include polynomial-time
exact counting in fixed dimension  [ 7 ] , exact counting methods
for nonnegative totally unimodular systems with a fixed number of equations
 [ 40 ] , algebraic methods for computing counting
polynomials for unimodular systems  [ 18 ] , and
maximum-entropy Gaussian approximations under well-roundedness assumptions  [ 5 ] . 
 
 
 In contrast, no fully polynomial randomized or deterministic
approximation scheme is known for arbitrary totally unimodular polytopes; such an
algorithm would in particular yield an FPRAS for counting
independent sets in bipartite graphs  [ 26 ] .
This motivates seeking weaker approximation guarantees that apply uniformly to all bounded TU polytopes, which is what we prove here.

 2  VLC \LC  Laurent polynomials 

 
 Gurvits’ capacity function has been widely used to obtain lower bounds for various interesting combinatorial quantities.
The underlying idea of these results is that if a (Laurent) polynomial has a certain log-concavity property, then one could use the theory of log-concave polynomials to derive lower bounds for its coefficients. However, currently studied log-concavity properties (e.g., Lorentzian, denormalized/dually Lorentzian, etc.) are all conditions which are too strong to apply to TU polytopes in general. 
 
 
 Thus, in  Section   2.1  we introduce a class of Laurent polynomials with weaker log-concavity properties, for which capacity lower bounds are still applicable. We further explore basic properties of this class throughout  Section   2.2 . We then turn our focus to unimodular and totally unimodular (TU) matrices in  Section   3  and prove that the cardinalities of the fibers of a unimodular polytope are log-concave along lines. 
 
 
 2.1  Definition and capacity bounds

 The algorithm. 

 
 We now apply the approximate-oracle ellipsoid implementation
of Section 6.2 (of the arXiv version) of  [ 41 ]  (see also Theorem 2.13), using the
preceding bounds on the search region and the Lipschitz
constant  L L  of  Φ \Phi . The value and gradient oracles established
above provide its required numerical approximations.
The number of oracle calls and the required number of accuracy
bits are polynomial in the input length and
 log ⁡ 1 ϵ \log\frac{1}{\epsilon} . Since our oracles run in time polynomial
in these quantities and the encoding length of the query point,
the resulting algorithm finds  𝒛 ∘ \bm{z}^{\circ}  satisfying 
 

 
 
 Φ ⁡ ( 𝒛 ∘ ) ≤ min ⁡ Φ + ϵ 2 \Phi(\bm{z}^{\circ})\leq\min\Phi+\frac{\epsilon}{2} 
 
 
 in polynomial bit complexity.
Evaluating  Φ ⁡ ( 𝒛 ∘ ) \Phi(\bm{z}^{\circ})  to additive error  ϵ 2 \frac{\epsilon}{2} 
then gives a rational  𝒜 \mathcal{A}  with
 | 𝒜 − log ⁡ ℰ | ≤ ϵ |\mathcal{A}-\log\mathcal{E}|\leq\epsilon . 
 
 
 We note that although the total running time stated in  [ 41 ] 
depends polynomially on  1 ϵ \frac{1}{\epsilon} , its ellipsoid
iteration bound depends only polynomially on
 log ⁡ 1 ε \log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}  (see Section 6.2, immediately before
Lemma 6.3). The worse dependence comes from the
approximate-counting oracle used there. Our value and
gradient oracles instead run in time polynomial in the
number of requested accuracy bits, so the same optimization
framework gives polynomial dependence on
 log ⁡ 1 ε \log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}  in our setting.
