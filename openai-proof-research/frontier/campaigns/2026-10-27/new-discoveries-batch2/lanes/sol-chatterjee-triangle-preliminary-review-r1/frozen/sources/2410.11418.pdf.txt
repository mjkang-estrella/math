                                           On the lack of weak continuity of Chatterjee’s
                                                       correlation coeﬀicient
                                                                          Axel Bücher∗                     Holger Dette†

                                                                                          October 16, 2024

                                                                                                  Abstract

                                                       Chatterjee’s correlation coeﬃcient has recently been proposed as a new association mea-
                                                    sure for bivariate random vectors that satisﬁes a number of desirable properties. Among these
                                                    properties is the feature that the coeﬃcient equals one if and only if one of the variables is a
                                                    measurable function of the other. As already observed in Mikusinski, Sherwood and Taylor
                                                    (Stochastica, 13(1):61–74, 1992), this property implies that Chatterjee’s coeﬃcient is not con-
                                                    tinuous with respect to weak convergence. We discuss a number of negative consequences for
                                                    statistical inference. In particular, we show that asymptotic tests for stochastic independence
                                                    based on Chatterjee’s empirical correlation coeﬃcient, or boosted versions thereof, have trivial
                                                    power against certain alternatives for which the population coeﬃcient is one.

                                           Keywords. Association Measure; Independence Testing; Power Analysis; Regression Dependence;
                                           Shuﬄe of Min.

                                           1 Introduction

                                           Measuring association between real-valued random variables is an important and historical branch
                                           of stochastics that is still actively researched today (see Chatterjee, 2024 for a recent review). The
                                           scientiﬁc discussion of new such measures or previously unexplored aspects of existing measures
                                           is usually based on a set of desirable axioms that a meaningful association measure should fulﬁll.
                                           The ﬁrst set of such axioms was formulated in Rényi (1959) and reads as follows: for two real-
                                           valued random variables 𝑋 and 𝑌 deﬁned on a common probability space, the association measure
                                           𝑅 = 𝑅(𝑋 , 𝑌 ) is supposed to satisfy:

                                           (A) 𝑅(𝑋 , 𝑌 ) is deﬁned for any 𝑋 and 𝑌 .

                                           (B) 𝑅(𝑋 , 𝑌 ) = 𝑅(𝑌 , 𝑋 ).

                                           (C) 𝑅(𝑋 , 𝑌 ) ∈ [0, 1].

                                           (D) 𝑅(𝑋 , 𝑌 ) = 0 if and only if 𝑋 and 𝑌 are independent.

                                           (E) 𝑅(𝑋 , 𝑌 ) = 1 if either 𝑋 = 𝑓 (𝑌 ) a.s. or 𝑌 = 𝑓 (𝑋 ) a.s. for some measurable function 𝑓 .

   A couple of additional or alternative axioms to Renyi’s list have been proposed in Schweizer and Wolﬀ
(1981), and the one that is most important for this note is the following:

 (H) The measure 𝑅 is continuous with respect to weak convergence, that is, if (𝑋𝑘 , 𝑌𝑘 ) is a se-
     quence of random variables weakly converging to (𝑋 , 𝑌 ), then lim𝑘→∞ 𝑅(𝑋𝑘 , 𝑌𝑘 ) = 𝑅(𝑋 , 𝑌 ).

The authors believe that this axiom is very natural, in particular when it comes to statistics, which,
on a high level, is ultimately based solely on the fact that empirical measures weakly converge to
the population measure almost surely if the sample size converges to inﬁnity (Varadarajan, 1958).
However, it has already been observed in Mikusinski et al. (1992) that it is impossible for an asso-
ciation measure that satisﬁes (A)-(D) to satisfy both (E’) and (H); see also Corollary 2.2 below for
a slightly sharper statement. In fact, the following corollary, proven in Section 2, illustrates this
pitfall very clearly for the Chatterjee correlation coeﬃcient.

Corollary 1.1. For any pair of independent random variables (𝑋 , 𝑌 ) with continuous marginal cdfs
and any 𝜉0 ∈ [0, 1], there exists a sequence of random variables (𝑋𝑘 , 𝑌𝑘 ) weakly converging to (𝑋 , 𝑌 )
for 𝑘 → ∞ such that Chatterjee’s correlation coeﬃcient satisﬁes 𝜉(𝑋𝑘 , 𝑌𝑘 ) = 𝜉0 for all 𝑘.

   The result essentially shows that Chatterjee’s correlation coeﬃcient is intrinsically hard to in-
terpret. Arbitrary large values of 𝜉(𝑋 , 𝑌 ) may be obtained for random variables which are ‘almost
independent’ in the sense that their induced law is close to that of the associated product measure of
its margins, with ‘closeness’ measured in terms of any metric that metrizes weak convergence. The
main purpose of this note is to shed further light on this issue, with a particular view on statistical
applications such as independence testing.
   More precisely, regarding independence testing, we show in Section 3 that any suﬃciently reg-
ular test for the null hypothesis of independence has trivial power, even in a classical Neymann-
Pearson type situation of testing 𝐻0 ∶ 𝜉(𝑋 , 𝑌 ) = 0 against 𝐻1 ∶ 𝜉(𝑋 , 𝑌 ) = 1. In an asymptotic
framework this means that we can ﬁnd a sequence of alternatives with trivial power which can
be regarded both ‘local’ in the sense that it converges weakly to independence, and ‘non-local’ in
the sense that we have 𝜉 = 1 for each alternative in the sequence. We thereby complement recent

results from Shi et al. (2022) and Cao and Bickel (2020), who found trivial power for Chatterjee’s
asymptotic test (Chatterjee, 2021) along certain local alternatives that converge to the null at a
non-optimal rate.
   A second statistical application, discussed in Section 4, concerns the construction of (asymptotic)
conﬁdence intervals. We show that any suﬃciently regular conﬁdence interval of level 𝛼 must be
unreasonably large, with probability at least 1 − 2𝛼, for any random sample from a pair of random
variables with continuous margins. Likewise, meaningful uniform asymptotic conﬁdence intervals
cannot exist – we want to stress though that no assertion is made on the more common notion of
(pointwise) asymptotic conﬁdence intervals; such intervals may well exist in a meaningful way.
   Our results apply in particular to Chatterjee’s empirical correlation coeﬃcient (Chatterjee, 2021)
and versions thereof. The latter has undergone intensive research in the recent years, and many
useful properties like invariance under monotone transformations of the data, low computational
complexity, consistency, asymptotic normality (Lin and Han, 2022a) or subsampling consistency
(Dette and Kroll, 2024) have been established; see also Azadkia and Chatterjee (2021); Lin and Han
(2022b, 2024); Shi et al. (2024) for extensions and further results. Despite these impressive advances,
our results show that some care is necessary when interpreting the coeﬃcient and conducting
statistical inference.

2 Shuﬀles of the Min and measures of perfect dependence

Recall that a bivariate copula 𝐶 ∶ [0, 1]2 → [0, 1] is the restriction of a bivariate cdf with stan-
dard uniform margins to the unit square (Nelsen, 2006). A speciﬁc copula is given by the Fréchet-
Hoeﬀding upper bound 𝐶min (𝑢, 𝑣) = min(𝑢, 𝑣), which corresponds to the uniform distribution on
the main diagonal 𝐷 = {(𝑢, 𝑣) ∈ [0, 1]2 ∶ 𝑢 = 𝑣} of the unit square.
   A shuﬄe of Min (i.e., of 𝐶min ) is a speciﬁc bivariate copula which is, informally, constructed as
follows: put mass 1 on 𝐷, then cut [0, 1]2 vertically into a ﬁnite number of strips, shuﬄe the strips
with perhaps some of them ﬂipped around their vertical axes of symmetry, and then reassemble the
strips to form the square again. The resulting mass distribution has uniform margins, and hence
deﬁnes a unique copula. We refer to Mikusinski et al. (1992) or Section 3.2.3 in Nelsen (2006) for
details. The important aspect for this note is that the support of a shuﬄe of Min is the graph of a
bijective function, and therefore it is is the cdf of a random vector (𝑋 , 𝑌 ) for which 𝑌 is a bijective
function of 𝑋 . In particular, if an association measure 𝑅 satisﬁes (E’), we have 𝑅(𝑋 , 𝑌 ) = 1 if (𝑋 , 𝑌 )
is distributed according to a shuﬄe of Min.
   A well-known result from copula theory states that shuﬄes of Min are dense in the space of
copulas with respect to the topology of uniform convergence.

Theorem 2.1 (Kimeldorf and Sampson, 1978; Mikusinski et al., 1992). Let 𝐶 denote an arbitrary cop-
ula on [0, 1]2 . There exists a sequence of copulas (𝐶𝑘 )𝑘 converging to 𝐶 in uniform distance such that
each 𝐶𝑘 has the following property: if (𝑋𝑘 , 𝑌𝑘 ) ∼ 𝐶𝑘 , then 𝑌𝑘 = 𝑓𝑘 (𝑋𝑘 ) almost surely for some measur-
able function 𝑓𝑘 . More precisely, each 𝐶𝑘 is a shuﬄe of Min.

  A proof for the independence copula 𝐶(𝑢, 𝑣) = 𝑢𝑣 can be found in the monograph Nelsen (2006),
see his Theorem 3.2.2. The result immediately implies the following fact from the introduction that
has already been mentioned in Mikusinski et al. (1992).

Corollary 2.2. Suppose that the association measure 𝑅 satisﬁes (D) and (E’) and is well-deﬁned for all
random vectors with standard uniform margins. Then 𝑅 does not satisfy (H).

Remark 4.2.
  (i) As in the previous section, the continuity assumption on 𝑇𝑛± would for instance be met if
      𝑇𝑛± = 𝜉̂𝑛 + 𝑢±𝑛 , with Chatterjee’s empirical coeﬃcient 𝜉̂𝑛 and some deterministic sequences 𝑢±𝑛 .




                                                       7
 (ii) The ﬁrst part of Lemma 4.1 essentially shows that meaningful conﬁdence sets of exact level
      𝛼 cannot exist: for all 𝑃 with continuous margins, they must be unreasonably large with
      probability at least 1 − 2𝛼. Likewise, the second part of the lemma shows that meaningful
      uniform asymptotic conﬁdence intervals do not exist.
      However, we want to stress that ‘uniformity’ in the asymptotic version is essential; there may
      exist meaningful conﬁdence intervals of (pointwise) asymptotic level 1−𝛼 (which is in fact the
      more common notion of an asymptotic conﬁdence interval). Such intervals may possibly be
      constructed by asymptotic theory (Lin and Han, 2022a) or by subsampling (Dette and Kroll,
      2024).
(iii) Qualitatively similar results hold if the candidate set ∗ is only required to include all distribu-
      tions 𝑃 with continuous margins that satisfy 𝜉(𝑃) ≤ 𝜅, where 𝜅 ∈ (0, 1) is some ﬁxed constant.
      The special case 𝜉(𝑃) = 1 is excluded from the condition in (4.1) and (4.2) then, which seems
      natural in view of the fact that asymptotic normality of Chatterjee’s sample correlation co-
      eﬃcient has only been shown for 𝜉(𝑃) < 1 (Lin and Han, 2022a). In that case, a careful look
      at the proof of Lemma 4.1 shows that the interval [𝜉(𝑃), 1] in (4.3)–(4.6) must be replaced by
      [𝜉(𝑃), 𝜅]. This interval is still unreasonably large, and does not shrink for increasing sample
      size.
