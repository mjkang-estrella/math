24:                                                                                           1. Introduction
25:                                               Zero forcing is an iterative coloring process on a graph. The concept was introduced in order
26:                                            to attack the maximum nullity problem of combinatorial matrix theory [1, 2, 9, 15], as well as
27:                                            independently to study quantum system control [4]. Let G denote a graph with vertex set V and
28:                                            edge set E. As is standard, define the order of G to be |V | and the radius of G to be the minimum
29:                                            graph eccentricity of a vertex v ∈ V . Denote the order of G by n and the radius of G by r. In
30:                                            this paper, we will assume that G is connected and that n ≥ 3. Zero forcing is described by the
31:                                            following algorithm. Let each vertex of a graph G be either blue or white. Denote by S the initial
32:                                            set of blue vertices of G. The zero forcing color change rule changes the color of a vertex v from
33:                                            white to blue if v is the only white neighbor of a blue vertex u. In this case, we say that u forces v
34:                                            and we write u → v. The initial blue set S is said to be zero forcing if, after finitely many steps of
35:                                            the color change rule, all vertices of G are forced to blue. The zero forcing number of G, denoted
36:                                            as Z(G), is defined as the minimum cardinality of a zero forcing set of G.

121:    Often, studying a distance problem in a more general normed space can reveal additional structure of the
122: problem. A first example is the Erdős distinct distances problem. Recall that the Erdős distinct distances
123: problem asks for the minimum number of distinct distances determined by n points in the plane. The
124: current best lower bound is Ω(n/ log n) by Guth and Katz in 2015 [GK], which in particular improves upon

128:    A second example is the unit distances problem, first posed by Erdős [Erd46] in 1946. The original unit
129: distances problem asks for the maximum number of distances of unit length determined by n points in the
130: plane in the Euclidean norm. It can be generalized to arbitrary norms in R2 as follows. Let u||·|| (n) denote
131: the maximum number of distances of unit length determined by n points in the plane in the norm || · ||.
132: Brass [Br] proved that if || · || is not strictly convex (i.e., the unit circle of || · || contains a line segment), then
133: u||·|| (n) = Θ(n2 ). By contrast, Valtr [Va] proved that if || · || is strictly convex, then u||·|| (n) = O(n4/3 ).
134: Interestingly, this upper bound u||·|| (n) = O(n4/3 ) cannot be improved without taking into account the
135: geometry of a strictly convex norm || · ||. Valtr [Va] constructed a strictly convex norm || · || for which
136: u||·|| (n) = Θ(n4/3 ). Moreover, there exist norms for which u||·|| (n) = o(n4/3 ). Matoušek [Ma] proved that
137: “almost every” strictly convex norm || · || satisfies u||·|| (n) = O(n log n log log n).

98: 1.1     Applications to pattern matching problems
99: In the topic of matchings the problem of counting the number of sets of reachable houses
100: has still not received considerable attention. The problem was first raised in [8] (a precursor
101: of [3]) where it was shown that it can be applied to upper bound the complexity of a certain
102: subdivision of the plane which arises from a pattern matching problem. Moreover, this was
103: used to derive efficient algorithms for this pattern matching problem.
104:     Before stating the connection and the implication of our result, we repeat the most
105: important definitions from [3].
106:     Let A = {a1 , . . . , an } and B = {b1 , . . . , bm } be two sets of points in the plane. We assume
107: that m ≤ n, and we seek a minimum-weight maximum-cardinality matching of B into A.
108: This is a subset M of edges of the complete bipartite graph with edge set B × A, so that
109: each b ∈ B appears in exactly one edge of M, and each a ∈ A appears in at most one edge.
110: The weight of an edge (b, a) is kb − ak2 , the squared Euclidean distance between b and a,
111: and the weight of a matching is the sum of the weights of its edges.
112:     Allowing the pattern B to be translated, we obtain the problem of computing the min-
113: imum partial-matching RMS (Root Mean Square) distance under translation. That is, we
114: want to find a translation vector t and a matching of B into A such that the weight of the
115: matching edges determined by the Euclidean distances between B(t) (B translated by t) and
116: A is minimal.
117:     This problem induces a subdivision of the plane, where two points t1 , t2 are in the same
118: region if when translating B by t1 or t2 , the above defined minimum-weight matching of B
119: to A is the same. This is called the partial-matching subdivision and is denoted by DB,A .
120:     In [8, 3] it was shown that the combinatorial complexity of DB,A can be upper bounded by
121: O(n2 m4 f (m)) and that computing a global minimum of the partial-patching RMS distance
122: can be done in time O(n3 m8 f (m)).
123:     Using the result of [2] this implied for them the upper bound O(n2 m3.5 (e ln m + e)m ) for
124: the complexity of DB,A . Our Theorem 1.5 improves this to O(n2 m3.5 4m ). Similarly, their
125: upper bound on computing a global minimum was O(n3 m7.5 (e ln m+e)m ), which Theorem 1.5
126: improves to O(n3 m7.5 4m ).

37:                                               For a sequence of non-decreasing positive integers S = (s1 , . . . , sk ), a packing S-coloring of a graph G is
38:                                            a partition of V (G) into sets V1 , . . . , Vk such that for each 1 ≤ i ≤ k the distance between any two distinct
39:                                            x, y ∈ Vi is at least si +1. The smallest k such that G has a packing (1, 2, . . . , k)-coloring (packing k-coloring)
40:                                            is called the packing chromatic number of G and is denoted by χp (G).
41:                                               The notion of packing k-coloring was introduced in 2008 by Goddard, Hedetniemi, Hedetniemi, Harris
42:                                            and Rall [19] motivated by frequency assignment problems in broadcast networks. There are more than

46:                                            vertex has 3 child vertices) has unbounded packing chromatic number.
47:                                               For a graph G, let D(G) denote the graph obtained from G by subdividing every edge. The questions on
48:                                            how large can χp (G) and χp (D(G)) be if G is a subcubic graph (i.e., a graph with maximum degree at most
49:                                            3) were discussed in several papers ([8, 9, 16, 24, 25]). In particular, Gastineau and Togni [16] asked whether
50:                                            χp (D(G)) ≤ 5 for every subcubic graph G and Brešar, Klavžar, Rall, and Wash [9] later conjectured this.
51:                                            Conjecture 1.1 (Brešar, Klavžar, Rall, and Wash [9]). Let G be a subcubic graph. Then χp (D(G)) ≤ 5.
52:                                               Recently, Balogh, Kostochka and Liu [2] showed that χp (G) is not bounded in the class of cubic graphs.
53:                                            They actually proved a stronger result: for each fixed integer k ≥ 12 and g ≥ 2k + 2, almost every n-vertex
54:                                            cubic graph of girth at least g has the packing chromatic number greater than k. Brešar and Ferme [5] later
55:                                            provided an explicit family of subcubic graphs with unbounded packing chromatic number. In contrast,
56:                                            Balogh, Kostochka and Liu [3] showed χp (D(G)) is bounded by 8 in the class of subcubic graphs.
