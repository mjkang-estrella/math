Abstract
    

Using generating functions and some trivial bijections, we show in this paper that the binomial coefficients count the set of (123,132) and (123,213)-avoiding permutations according to the number of crossings. We also define a q-tableau of power of two and prove that it counts the set of (213,312) and (132,312)-avoiding permutations according to the number of crossings.
    

Keywords: Restricted permutations, number of crossings, central polygonal numbers, q-tableau of power of two.
    

2010 Mathematics Subject Classification:05A20 and 05A05.
  




1 Introduction and main results




A statistic st on a given set  $E$  is a map from E to  $\mathbb{N}$ . We denote by  $\sum_{e\in E}x^{{\rm st}(e)}$  the polynomial distribution of the statistic st over the set E.
Let  $n$  be an integer such that  $n\geq 1$ . A permutation  $\sigma$  of  $[n]$  is a bijection from  $[n]$  to itself which can be written linearly as  $\sigma=\sigma(1)\sigma(2)\ldots\sigma(n)$ . We denote by  $S_{n}$  the set of all permutations of  $[n]$ . Recall that a crossing in a permutation  $\sigma$  is a pair of indexes  $(i,j)$  such that  $i<j<\sigma(i)<\sigma(j)$  or  $\sigma(i)<\sigma(j)\leq i<j$ . A nesting of  $\sigma$  is similarly defined as a pair  $(i,j)$  such that  $i<j<\sigma(j)<\sigma(i)$  or  $\sigma(j)<\sigma(i)\leq i<j$ . We denote respectively by  ${\rm cr}(\sigma)$  and  ${\rm nes}(\sigma)$  the number of crossings and nestings of  $\sigma$ . We draw arc diagrams of a given permutation  $\sigma$  in the figure bellow for illustration of crossings.

 $1\ 2\ 3\ 4\ 5\ 6\ 7$ 
Figure 1: Arc diagrams of  $\sigma=4735126\in S_{7}$  with  ${\rm cr}(\sigma)=3$  and  ${\rm nes}(\sigma)=3$ .




Let  $\sigma\in S_{n}$  and  $\tau\in S_{k}$  with  $1\leq k\leq n$ . For a given increasing sequence of integers  $i_{1}<i_{2}<\ldots<i_{k}$ , we say that the subsequence  $s=\sigma(i_{1})\sigma(i_{2})\ldots\sigma(i_{k})$  of  $\sigma$  is an occurrence of  $\tau$  if  $s$  and  $\tau$  are in order isomorphic, i.e  $\sigma(i_{x})<\sigma(i_{y})$  if only if  $\tau(x)<\tau(y)$ . If there is no occurrence of the pattern  $\tau$  in  $\sigma$ , we also say that  $\sigma$  is  $\tau$ -avoiding. For example, the permutation  $\pi=4162375\in S_{7}$  has five occurrences of 312. These are 312, 423, 623, 625, and 635. It is obvious to see that  $\pi$  is  $321$ -avoiding. There are three useful trivial involutions on  $S_{n}$  namely reverse, complement and inverse that simplify the search for equivalence classes. For any  $\sigma=\sigma(1)\sigma(2)\ldots\sigma(n)\in S_{n}$ ,


• 



the reverse of  $\sigma$  is r $(\sigma)=\sigma(n)\sigma(n-1)\ldots\sigma(1)$ ,


• 



the complement of  $\sigma$  is c $(\sigma)=(n+1-\sigma(1))(n+1-\sigma(2))\ldots(n+1-\sigma(n))$ ,


• 



the inverse of  $\sigma$  is i $(\sigma)=p(1)p(2)\ldots p(n)$  where  $p(i)$  is the position of  $i$  in  $\sigma$ . We often write i $(\sigma)=\sigma^{-1}$ .



By composition  $\circ$ , these three involutions generate the dihedral group  $\mathcal{D}=\{{\rm id,r,c,i,rc,ri,ci,rci}\}$  where  ${\rm fg=f\circ g}$  for any f and g in  $\{{\rm r,c,i}\}$ . Let us consider  $\pi=4135762\in S_{7}$ . We have r $(\pi)=2675314$ , c $(\pi)=4753126$ ,  $\pi^{-1}=2731465$ , rc $(\pi)=6213574$  and rci $(\pi)=3247516$ . We will denote by  $S_{n}(\tau)$  the set of all  $\tau$ -avoiding permutations of  $[n]$ .
For any given subset of patterns  $T$ , we write  $S_{n}(T):=\cap_{\tau\in T}S_{n}(\tau)$  and  $S(T):=\cup_{n\geq 0}S_{n}(T)$ . We say that  $T$  and  $T^{\prime}$  are Wilf-equivalent if  $|S_{n}(T)|=|S_{n}(T^{\prime})|$ .
For example, we have  $|S_{n}(123)|=|S_{n}(321)|$  and  $|S_{n}(312)|=|S_{n}(231)|=|S_{n}(132)|=|S_{n}(213)|$  since 321=r(123), 312=231-1, 132=r(231) and 213=r(312).
In fact, it is well known in [13] that there is only one Wilf-equivalence class in  $S_{3}$  and we have  $|S_{n}(\tau)|=\frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}$  (the n-th Catalan numbers) for any pattern  $\tau\in S_{3}$ . Bijective proof of identity  $|S_{n}(132)|=|S_{n}(321)|$  interested several author, eg. see [7] and reference therein. It was also shown by Simion and Schmidt [19] that there are three Wilf-equivalence classes for two patterns of  $S_{3}$ .




For any given statistic st, the notion of st-wilf-equivalence generalizes that of Wilf-equivalence. Say that two subset of patterns  $T_{1}$  and  $T_{2}$  are st-Wilf-equivalent and we write  $T_{1}=_{{\rm st}}T_{2}$  if only if
 $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}(T_{1})}x^{{\rm st}(\sigma)}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}(T_{2})}x^{{\rm st}(\sigma)}$ . For this notion, we are much inspired by the work of some author. In [16], Robertson et al. enumerated the set of permutations that avoid a single pattern of length 3. Instead of considering simply the number of fixed points, Elizalde
[11, 12] extended the result of Robertson et al. and study its joint distribution with the number of excedances. He also treated all pairs and triples patterns of length 3. Dokos et al. [9] provided the number of inversions and major index-Wilf equivalence classes for singleton, double , triple and four patterns of length 3. In this paper, we hope to extend the recent result of the first author [14] of this paper who introduced the study of the number of crossings and nestings on permutations that avoid a single pattern of length 3. He proved bijectively that the three patterns 321, 132 and 213 are cr-Wilf-equivalent, i.e.





 $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}(321)}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}(132)}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}(213)}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}.$ 

(1.1)


To prove the first identity of (1.1), he exploited the bijection  $\Theta:S_{n}(321)\rightarrow S_{n}(132)$  of Elizalde and Pak exhibited in [10] and proved that it is cr-preserving. The second identity of (1.1) is simply obtained from the fact that the bijection rci preserves also the statistic  ${\rm cr}$ . Notice that the bijection  $\Gamma:S_{n}(321)\rightarrow S_{n}(132)$  defined by Robertson [17] is also cr-preserving. Indeed, Saracino discovered recently after his interesting joint work with Bloom [3, 4] that the bijections  $\Theta$  and  $\Gamma$  are related each other by the simple relation  $\Gamma=\Theta\circ\rm rci$ .
Using the q,p-Catalan numbers of Randrianarivony [15], Rakotomamonjy expressed in terms of continued fraction the generating function of  $\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}(\tau)}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}$  for any  $\tau\in\{321,132,213\}$ ,





 $\sum_{\sigma\in S(\tau)}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}z^{|\sigma|}=\frac{1}{1-\displaystyle\frac{z}{1-\displaystyle\frac{z}{1-\displaystyle\frac{qz}{1-\displaystyle\frac{qz}{1-\displaystyle\frac{q^{2}z}{1-\displaystyle\frac{q^{2}z}{\ddots}}}}}}}.$ 



Finding  $\sum_{\sigma\in S(\tau)}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}z^{|\sigma|}$  is staying unsolved for any pattern  $\tau$  in  $\{231,312,123\}$ . We observe that this continued fraction appeared in [5] as the distribution of occurrences of generalized patterns in 231-avoiding permutations.We discuss this here for interest readers on the correspondence between these results. View that Corteel [8] established the connection between occurrences of patterns, crossings and nestings on permutations.




The first purpose of this paper is about enumeration result of (123,132) and (123,213)-avoiding permutations according to the number of crossings. We discover that the binomial coefficients involve on enumeration. For that, we denote by  $S_{n}^{k}=\{\sigma\in S_{n}/\sigma(n+1-k)=1\}$  the set of permutations of  $[n]$  where the position of 1 is  $n+1-k$ ,  $1\leq k\leq n$ . Moreover, we also denote by  $S_{n,k}=\{\sigma\in S_{n}/\sigma(n)=k\}$  the set of permutations of  $[n]$  ending with  $k$ . As example,  $S_{n}^{1}=S_{n,1}=\{\sigma\in S_{n}/\sigma(n)=1\}$  is the set of permutations ending with 1.



Theorem 1.1.



For all integer  $n\geq 2$ , we have





 $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{2}(123,132)}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n,2}(123,213)}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=(1+q)^{n-2}.$ 







To prove this theorem, we first study the fine structure of  $S_{n}(123,132)$  in order to find a suitable partition which leads to a recurrence relation for the corresponding polynomial distribution of number of crossings. Notice that similar results as theorem 1.1 on enumeration of restricted permutations refined by number of descents and inversions are found respectively in [1, 2] and [6, 9].




For the second purpose of this work, let us consider the q-tableau  $(R_{n}^{k}(q))_{n,k}$  of powers of two defined by the following way





 $\begin{cases}R_{n}^{n}(q)=R_{n}^{n-1}(q)=1&\\
R_{n}^{k}(q)=q^{\min\{k-1,n-1-k\}}R_{n-1}^{k}(q)+R_{n}^{k+1}(q)&\text{ if \ \ $0<k<n-1$}\\
R_{n}^{0}(q)=R_{n-1}^{0}(q)+R_{n}^{1}(q)&\end{cases}$ 

(1.2)


It is not difficult to see that  $R_{n}^{k}(1)=2^{n-1-k}$  for  $0\leq k<n$ . Hence, we have  $\sum_{k=0}^{n}R_{n}^{k}(1)=2^{n}$ . We present here some values for  $(R_{n}^{k}(q))$  in table 1.

If we denote by  $S_{n}^{[k]}=\{\sigma\in S_{n}/\sigma(n+1-i)=i\text{ for all }i\in[k]\}$  for  $1\leq k\leq n$ , we have the following result which means that this defined q-tableau counts the set of permutations that avoid some pairs of patterns according to the number of crossings. We obtain it from certain manipulation of the fine structure of  $S_{n}(213,312)$ .



Theorem 1.2.



Let  ${\rm T}$  be one of the pairs  $\{213,312\}$  and  $\{132,312\}$ . For all non-negative integer  $n$ , we have the following identities





 $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}({\rm T})}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=R_{n}^{0}(q)\text{ and }\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{[k]}({\rm T})}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=R_{n}^{k}(q)\text{ for all }k\geq 1.$

2 Trivial bijections and first uses




In this section, we present two trivial bijections that we need in the next sections to prove some identities. Using the following well known property of [19]





 $f(S_{n}({\rm T}))=S_{n}(f({\rm T}))\text{ for any $f\in\mathcal{D}=\{{\rm id,r,c,i,rc,ri,ci,rci}\}$ and subset of patterns {\rm T}},$ 



we can use our bijections to enumerate avoiding permutations according to the number of crossings. We hope to extend the result of Rakotomamonjy who showed that the trivial involution  $\rm rci$  on  $S_{n}$  is  ${\rm cr}$ -preserving and we have consequently





 $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}({\rm T})}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}({\rm T}^{\prime})}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}\text{ if only if {\rm T}'= \rm rci({\rm T})}.$ 



We let the reader to show that we have  $S_{n,k}$  = rci $(S_{n}^{k})$  for all integers  $n$  and  $k\geq 1$ . So we have





 $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{k}({\rm T})}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n,k}({\rm T}^{\prime})}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}\text{ if only if {\rm T}'= \rm rci({\rm T})}.$ 



let us recall some needed notations that Rakotomamonjy has used in [14]. Given a permutation  $\sigma$  and two integers  $a$  and  $b$ , we denote by  $\sigma^{(a,b)}$  the obtained permutation from  $\sigma$  by the following way:


• 



add by  $1$  each number in  $\sigma$  which is greater or equal to  $b$ ,


• 



then, insert  $b$  at the  $a$ -th position of the modified  $\sigma$ .



To simplify, we write  $\sigma^{-(a,b)}$  for  $(\sigma^{-1})^{(a,b)}$ . As example, we have  $3142^{(2,{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}3})}=4{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}3}152$  and  $3142^{-(2,{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}3})}=2{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}3}514$ . If  $\sigma\in S_{n}$ , we have particularly
 $\sigma^{(n+1,1)}(i)=\sigma(i)+1$  if  $i\leq n$  and  $\sigma^{(n+1,1)}(n+1)=1$ . Let also denote respectively by





 ${\rm ut}(\sigma):=|\{i/\sigma^{-1}(i)<i<\sigma(i)\}|\text{ and }{\rm lt}(\sigma):=|\{i/\sigma(i)<i<\sigma^{-1}(i)\}|$ 



the number of upper and lower transients of a given permutation  $\sigma$ . Notice that lower transients of a given permutation  $\sigma$  are counted as crossing of  $\sigma$ . The following lemma even comes from definitions of  $\sigma^{(n+1,1)}$ .



Lemma 2.1.



For any  $\sigma\in S_{n}$ , we have  ${\rm cr}(\sigma^{(n+1,1)})={\rm cr}(\sigma)+{\rm ut}(\sigma)-{\rm lt}(\sigma)$ .




Proof.



Let  $\sigma\in S_{n}$  and  $\pi=\sigma^{(n+1,1)}$ . From definition of  $\pi$ , the following statements hold.


1. 



 $i<j<\sigma(i)<\sigma(j)$  if only if  $i<j<j+1<\pi(i)<\pi(j)$ .


2. 



 $\sigma(i)<\sigma(j)<i<j$  if only if  $\pi(i)<\pi(j)\leq i<j$ .


3. 



 $\sigma^{-1}(i)<i<\sigma(i)$  if only if  $\pi^{-1}(i+1)<i$  and  $\pi(i)>i+1$ . It means that  $(\sigma^{-1}(i),i)$  which does not a crossing of  $\sigma$  becomes one of  $\pi$ .


4. 



 $\sigma(i)<i<\sigma^{-1}(i)$  if only if  $\pi(i)\leq i$  and  $i+1\leq\pi^{-1}(i)$ . It means that  $(i,\sigma^{-1}(i))$  is counted as a crossing of  $\sigma$  becomes no longer one of  $\pi$ .



Graphical illustration of the statements 3. an 4. are given in the figure 2 below. From these four statements, we get  ${\rm cr}(\pi)={\rm cr}(\sigma)+|\{i/\sigma^{-1}(i)<i<\sigma(i)\}|-|\{i/\sigma(i)<i<\sigma^{-1}(i)\}|={\rm cr}(\sigma)+{\rm ut}(\sigma)-{\rm lt}(\sigma)$ .
∎

We can similarly prove that we the following lemma holds.



Lemma 2.2.



For any  $\sigma\in S_{n}$ , we have  ${\rm cr}(\sigma^{(n,1)})={\rm cr}(\sigma)+1-\delta_{n,\sigma(n)}+{\rm ut}(\sigma)-{\rm lt}(\sigma)$ .





Before presenting our bijections, we need the following lemma which completes the previous ones.



Lemma 2.3.



For any permutation  $\sigma$ , we have  ${\rm cr}(\sigma^{-1})={\rm cr}(\sigma^{\rm rc})={\rm cr}(\sigma)+{\rm ut}(\sigma)-{\rm lt}(\sigma)$ .




Proof.



Let  $\sigma\in S_{n}$  and  $\pi=\sigma^{-1}$  or  $\sigma^{\rm rc}$ . Applying reverse-complement or inverse on  $\sigma$  exchanges lower and upper arcs including of course transients, i.e.  ${\rm ut}(\pi)={\rm lt}(\sigma)$  and  ${\rm lt}(\pi)={\rm ut}(\sigma)$ . On the first hand, each upper transient of  $\sigma$ , which is not counted as crossings of  $\sigma$ , becomes one of  $\pi$ . On the second hand, each lower transient of  $\sigma$ , which is counted as crossing of  $\sigma$ , becomes no longer one of  $\pi$ . This explain how we get  ${\rm cr}(\pi)={\rm cr}(\sigma)+{\rm ut}(\sigma)-{\rm lt}(\sigma)$ .
∎





Let us consider the maps  $\phi_{k}$  and  $\psi_{k}$  from  $S_{n}$  to  $S_{n+1}^{k}$  with  $k\geq 1$  that are defined respectively as follow,





 $\phi_{k}:\sigma\mapsto\sigma^{-(n+2-k,1)}\text{ and }\psi_{k}:\sigma\mapsto{\rm rc}(\sigma)^{(n+2-k,1)}.$ 



As example, we have  $\phi_{3}(31542)=361254$  and  $\psi_{3}(31542)=531264$ . It is clear that the maps  $\phi_{k}$  and  $\psi_{k}$  are bijective for any  $k\geq 1$ . Furthermore, we can use the three previous lemmas to prove the following proposition.



Proposition 2.4.



The bijections  $\phi_{1}$  and  $\psi_{1}$  are  ${\rm cr}$ -preserving and the bijections  $\phi_{2}$  and  $\psi_{2}$  satisfy  ${\rm cr}(\phi_{2}(\sigma))={\rm cr}(\psi_{2}(\sigma))={\rm cr}(\sigma)+1-\delta_{n,\sigma(n)}$  for any  $\sigma\in S_{n}$ .




Proof.



Combining lemmas 2.1 and 2.2, we get  ${\rm cr}(\sigma^{-(n+1,1)})={\rm cr}({\rm rc}(\sigma)^{(n+1,1)})={\rm cr}(\sigma)$  for any  $\sigma\in S_{n}$ . This implies the cr-preserving of  $\phi_{1}$  and  $\psi_{1}$ . By the same way, but combining lemma 2.1 with lemma 2.3, we get  ${\rm cr}(\phi_{2}(\sigma))={\rm cr}(\psi_{2}(\sigma))={\rm cr}(\sigma)+1-\delta_{n,\sigma(n)}$  for any  $\sigma\in S_{n}$ .
∎





For any subset of permutations T and any integer n, we denote by  $F_{n}(T;q)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}(T)}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}$  the polynomial distribution of the number of crossings over the set of T-avoiding permutations and  $F(T;q,z)$  its generating function, i.e.





 $F(T;q,z)=\sum_{\sigma\in S(T)}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}z^{|\sigma|}=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}F_{n}(T;q)z^{n}.$ 



Particularly, we set  $F_{n}(q)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=F_{n}(\emptyset;q)$ .



Theorem 2.5.



For all integer  $n\geq 1$ , we have





 $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n+1}^{1}}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=F_{n}(q)\text{ and }\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n+1}^{2}}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=qF_{n}(q)+(1-q)F_{n-1}(q).$ 






Proof.



The first identity of theorem 2.5 comes from the cr-preserving of the bijection  $\phi_{1}$  (or  $\psi_{1}$ ). The second one obviously use the bijection  $\phi_{2}$  (or  $\psi_{2}$ ) and its property





 ${\rm cr}(\phi_{2}(\sigma))=\begin{cases}{\rm cr}(\sigma)&\text{ if }\ \ \sigma(n)=n\\
{\rm cr}(\sigma)+1&\text{ if }\ \ \sigma(n)<n\end{cases}.$ 



So, we get





 $\displaystyle\sum_{\phi_{2}(\sigma)\in S_{n+1}^{2}}q^{{\rm cr}(\gamma(\sigma))}$ 
 $\displaystyle=$ 
 $\displaystyle q\times\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n},\sigma(n)\neq n}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}+\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n},\sigma(n)=n}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}.$ 



Since  $\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n},\sigma(n)\neq n}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}-\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n},\sigma(n)=n}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}$  and  $\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n},\sigma(n)=n}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=F_{n-1}(q)$ , we obtain the following identity





 $\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n+1}^{2}}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}$ 
 $\displaystyle=$ 
 $\displaystyle q\left(F_{n}(q)-F_{n-1}(q)\right)+F_{n-1}(q).$ 



which is equivalent with the desired one of theorem 2.5.
∎





When  $k\geq 3$ , the question arises naturally about the possible expressions for  $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n+1}^{k}}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}$  that of course depend on the property of  $\phi_{k}$  (or  $\psi_{k}$ ).
Now, let us look at how the restriction of these bijections my help us to get nice relationships between the polynomial distributions of the number of crossings over the set of 231 and 312-avoiding permutations. A result that may be useful in future research.



Theorem 2.6.



We have  $F(312;q,z)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-zF(231;q,z)}$ .




Proof.



Know that the restriction of  $\phi$  (or  $\psi$ ) on  $S_{n}(231)$  is a bijection between  $S_{n}(231)$  to  $S_{n+1}^{1}(312)$  that preserves the statistic  ${\rm cr}$ . Consequently, we have





 $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n+1}^{1}(312)}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}(231)}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}.$ 






Know also that each  $\sigma\in S_{n}^{j}(312)$  can be written as  $\sigma_{1}\oplus\sigma_{2}$  where  $\sigma_{1}\in S_{j}^{1}$  and  $\sigma_{2}\in S_{n-j}(312)$ . Consequently, the product  $S_{j-1}(231)\times S_{n-j}(312)$  is in bijection with the set  $S_{n}^{j}(312)$  by the simple way:  $(\alpha,\beta)\longmapsto\alpha^{-(j,1)}\oplus\beta$ . Hence, in terms of generating function, we get





 $\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{j}(312)}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}$ 
 $\displaystyle=$ 
 $\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in S_{j-1}(231)}q^{{\rm cr}(\alpha)}\times\sum_{\beta\in S_{n-j}(312)}q^{{\rm cr}(\beta)}.$ 



This implies that





 $\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}(312)}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}$ 
 $\displaystyle=$ 
 $\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\sum_{\alpha\in S_{j}(231)}q^{{\rm cr}(\alpha)}\times\sum_{\beta\in S_{n-1-j}(312)}q^{{\rm cr}(\beta)}.$ 



Thus, we get from this last identity the following functional equation





 $F(312;q,z)=1+z\ F(231;q,z)\times F(312;q,z).$ 



Solving it for  $F(312;q,z)$ , we get the proof of theorem 2.6.
∎






3 Combinatorial interpretation of  $\binom{n}{k}$ 




In this section, we enumerate the set of (123,132) and (123,213)-avoiding permutations according to the number of crossings and provide its connection to the binomial coefficients. That naturally implies the proof of theorem 1.1. Our method is based on exploitation of the fine structure of  $S_{n}(123,132)$ . Then, we compute the closed formula for  $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}(123,132)}q^{cr(\sigma)}$  and extract its coefficients. Since the only possible positions for 1 in a permutation  $\sigma\in S_{n}(123,132)$  are  $n-1$  or  $n$ , we can consider the partition  $S_{n}(123,132)=S_{n}^{1}(123,132)\cup S_{n}^{2}(123,132)$ .



Theorem 3.1.



Let  ${\rm T}$  be one of the pairs  $\{123,132\}$  and  $\{123,213\}$ . For all integer  $n\geq 1$ , we have





 $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}(T)}q^{cr(\sigma)}=\frac{(1+q)^{n-1}-1+q}{q}.$ 






Proof.



Let us fix  ${\rm T}=\{123,132\}$ . Firstly, using the above described partition, we have





 $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}({\rm T})}q^{cr(\sigma)}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{1}({\rm T})}q^{cr(\sigma)}+\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{2}({\rm T})}q^{cr(\sigma)}.$ 

(3.1)


Secondly, we can think of any restricted version of theorem 2.5. Since the restriction of  $\phi_{1}$  on  $S_{n-1}({\rm T})$ , is a bijection from  $S_{n-1}({\rm T})$  to  $S_{n}^{1}({\rm T})$ , we get in terms of generating function





 $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{1}({\rm T})}q^{cr(\sigma)}=F_{n-1}({\rm T};q).$ 

(3.2)


Similarly, the restriction of  $\phi_{2}$  on  $S_{n-1}({\rm T})$  satisfies





 ${\rm cr}(\phi_{2}(\sigma))=\begin{cases}{\rm cr}(\sigma)=0&\text{ if }\ \ \sigma(n-1)=n-1\\
{\rm cr}(\sigma)+1&\text{ if }\ \ \sigma(n-1)<n-1\end{cases}$ 



since the only  $\sigma\in S_{n-1}({\rm T})$  satisfying  $\sigma(n-1)=n-1$  is  $\sigma=(n-2)\ldots 21(n-1)$ . Consequently, we get





 $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{2}({\rm T})}q^{cr(\sigma)}=qF_{n-1}(T;q)+1-q.$ 

(3.3)


Thirdly, when we observe and combine the three relations (3.1), (3.2) and (3.3), we get the following recurrence





 $F_{n}({\rm T};q)=(1+q)F_{n-1}({\rm T};q)+1-q$ 



Finally, when we solve this recurrence with initial condition  $F_{1}({\rm T};q)=1$ , we obtain the closed formula  $F_{n}({\rm T};q)=\frac{(1+q)^{n-1}-1+q}{q}$ . So, to complete the proof of theorem 3.1 we use the fact that  $\{123,213\}=_{{\rm cr}}\{123,132\}$  since  $\{123,213\}=\rm rci(\{123,132\})$ .
∎





When we extract the coefficients of  $F_{n}(123,132;q)$ , we obtain the following enumeration result.



Corollary 3.2.



Let  ${\rm T}$  be one of the pairs  $\{123,132\}$  and  $\{123,213\}$ .
For all integers  $n\geq 1$  and  $k\geq 0$ , we have





 $|\{\sigma\in S_{n}({\rm T})/{\rm cr}(\sigma)=k\}|=\delta_{k,0}+\binom{n-1}{k+1}.$ 







Proof of theorem 1.1. Deriving from the proof of the previous theorem, we easily obtain that of theorem 1.1. Indeed, we can have





 $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{2}(123,132)}q^{cr(\sigma)}=F_{n}(q)-F_{n-1}(q)=(1+q)^{n-2}.$ 



Hence, theorem 1.1 holds since we have  $S_{n,2}(123,213)=\rm rci(S_{n}^{2}(123,132))$ .



Corollary 3.3.



Let  ${\rm T}=\{123,132\}$  and  ${\rm T}^{\prime}=\{123,213\}$ . For all integer  $n\geq 2$ , we have





 $|\{\sigma\in S_{n}^{2}({\rm T})/{\rm cr}(\sigma)=k\}|=|\{\sigma\in S_{n,2}({\rm T}^{\prime})/{\rm cr}(\sigma)=k\}|=\binom{n-2}{k}.$ 








4 Combinatorial interpretation of  $(R_{n}^{k}(q))$ 




In this section, we will show how the q-tableau  $(R_{n}^{k}(q))$  defined in section 1 counts the set of T-avoiding permutations according to the number of crossings where  ${\rm T}$  is one of the two patterns  $\{213,312\},\{132,312\},\{213,312\}$  and  $\{213,312\}$ . These patterns are linked by the following relations  $\{132,312\}=\rm rci(\{213,312\})$ ,  $\{132,231\}=\rm rci(\{213,231\})$  and  $\{132,231\}=\{213,312\}^{-1}$  or  $\{213,312\}^{\rm rc}$ . So, we choose to consider only the pattern  $\{213,312\}$ .




So, let us fix  ${\rm T}=\{213,312\}$ . Since each T-voiding permutation stars or ends with 1, we have  $S_{n}({\rm T})=S_{n}^{n}({\rm T})\cup S_{n}^{1}({\rm T})$ . Consequently, we have





 $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}({\rm T})}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{n}({\rm T})}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}+\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{1}({\rm T})}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}$ 

(4.1)


The following proposition is obvious.



Proposition 4.1.



For all integer  $n\geq 1$ , we have  $\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{n}({\rm T})}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=F_{n-1}({\rm T};q).$ 





Before we compute  $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{1}({\rm T})}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}$ , we will denote by  $F_{n}^{k}({\rm T};q)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{[k]}({\rm T})}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}$  for  $1\leq k\leq n$ . Observe that, we have  $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{1}({\rm T})}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=F_{n}^{1}({\rm T};q)$  since  $S_{n}^{1}=S_{n}^{[1]}$ .  $F_{n}^{1}({\rm T};q)$  can be computed from a recursively formula for  $F_{n}^{k}({\rm T};q)$  that we will prove later (see proposition 4.4). For that, we will denote by  $A_{j}(\sigma)=\{i<j/\sigma(i)\geq j\}$  ,  $B_{j}(\sigma)=\{i+1<j/\sigma(i)\leq i\text{ and }i+1\leq\sigma^{-1}(i+1)\}$  and  $C_{j}(\sigma)=\{(i,k)/i<k<\sigma(i)=k+1<\sigma(k)\text{ and }k+1\leq j\}$  for any given permutation  $\sigma\in S_{n}$  and integer  $j\in[n]$ .



Lemma 4.2.



For all  $\sigma\in S_{n}$ , we have





 ${\rm cr}(\sigma^{(1,j)})={\rm cr}(\sigma)+|A_{j}(\sigma)|+|B_{j}(\sigma)|-|C_{j}(\sigma)|\text{ for }1\leq j\leq n.$ 






Proof.



By definition, we have  $\sigma^{(1,j)}(1)=j$  and  $\sigma^{(1,j)}(1+i)=\begin{cases}\sigma(i)+1&\text{ if }\sigma(i)\leq j\\
\sigma(i)&\text{ if }\sigma(i)<j\end{cases}.$ 
The following facts comes from this definition.


1. 



if  $i\in A_{j}(\sigma)$  then  $(1,1+i)$  becomes an upper crossings of  $\sigma^{(1,j)}$ .


2. 



If  $i\in B_{j}(\sigma)$  then  $1+i$  becomes a lower transient, then a crossing for  $\sigma^{(1,j)}$ .


3. 



If  $(i,k)\in C_{j}(\sigma)$  then  $i+1<\sigma^{(1,j)}(1+i)=1+k<\sigma^{(1,j)}(1+k)$ . So, the pair  $(1+i,1+k)$  becomes an upper crossing of  $\sigma^{(1,j)}$  which does not counted as crossing of  $\sigma^{(1,j)}$ .


4. 



It is not difficult to see that  $(i,k)$  is a crossing of  $\sigma$  which does not in  $C_{j}(\sigma)$  if only if  $(i+1,k+1)$  is one of  $\sigma^{(1,j)}$ .



We obtain lemma 4.2 from these four properties.
∎




Corollary 4.3.



If  $\sigma\in S_{n}^{[k]}({\rm T})$ , we have  ${\rm cr}(\sigma^{(1,k+1)})=\min\{k-1,n-k\}+{\rm cr}(\sigma)$ .




Proof.



Since every  $\sigma\in S_{n}^{[k]}({\rm T})$  can be written as  $\sigma=\pi|k\ldots 21$  such that  $\pi$  is a T-avoiding permutation of  $\{k+1,\ldots,n-1,n\}$ , we have  $\sigma(i)\geq k+1$  for all  $1\leq i\leq n-k$ .
Consequently, we have  $B_{k+1}(\sigma)=C_{k+1}(\sigma)=\emptyset$ ,  $A_{k+1}(\sigma)=\{1,2,\ldots,k-1\}$  if  $k\leq\frac{n}{2}$  and  $A_{k+1}(\sigma)=\{1,2,\ldots,n-k\}$  if  $k>\frac{n}{2}$ . In other word, we have





 $|B_{k+1}(\sigma)|=|C_{k+1}(\sigma)|=0\text{ and }|A_{k+1}(\sigma)|=\begin{cases}k-1&\text{ if }\ \ \ k\leq\frac{n}{2}\\
n-1-k&\text{ if }\ \ \ k>\frac{n}{2}\end{cases}~~\text{ for all }k.$ 



That means  $|A_{k+1}(\sigma)|=\min\{k-1,n-1-k\}$ .
So, using lemma 4.2, we obtain our corollary.
∎





Let us define the skew sum of two given permutations  $\alpha$  and  $\beta$  as  $\alpha\ominus\beta=\alpha^{+|\beta|}.\beta$  where  $\alpha^{+x}$  is the obtained permutation from  $\alpha$  by adding  $x$  all of its letter. For example, we have  $312\ominus 1342=7561342$ . Using this last corollary, we can prove the following proposition which is the main tool to prove theorem 1.2.



Proposition 4.4.



Let  $T=\{213,312\}$ .
For all integers  $n,k\geq 1$ , we have





 $F_{n}^{k}(T;q)=q^{\min\{k-1,n-1-k\}}F_{n-1}^{k}(T;q)+F_{n}^{k+1}(T;q).$ 






Proof.



Let  $T=\{213,312\}$ . Firstly, since





 $S_{n}^{[k]}(T)=\{\sigma\ominus k\ldots 21/\sigma\in S_{n-k}^{n-k}(T)\}\cup\{\sigma\ominus k\ldots 21/\sigma\in S_{n-k}^{1}(T)\}$ 



then we have  $S_{n}^{[k]}(T)=S_{n,*}^{[k]}(T)\cup S_{n}^{[k+1]}(T)$  where  $S_{n,*}^{[k]}(T)=\{\sigma\ominus k\ldots 21/\sigma\in S_{n-k}^{n-k}(T)\}$ .
In terms of generating function, we get





 $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{[k]}(T)}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n,*}^{[k]}(T)}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}+\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{[k+1]}(T)}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}.$ 

(4.2)


Secondly, the map from  $S_{n-1}^{[k]}(213,312)$  to  $S_{n,*}^{[k]}(213,312)$  which sends  $\sigma$  to  $\sigma^{(1,k+1)}$  is well defined and bijective. Using corollary 4.3, we obtain





 $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n,*}^{[k]}(213,312)}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=q^{\min\{k-1,n-1-k\}}\times F_{n-1}^{k}(T;q).$ 

(4.3)


So, combining identities (4.2) and (4.3), we complete the proof of proposition 4.4.
∎





Proof of theorem 1.2. Let  ${\rm T}=\{213,312\}$ .
It is obvious that  $F_{n}^{n}({\rm T};q)=F_{n}^{n-1}({\rm T};q)=1$  since  $S_{n}^{[n]}({\rm T})=S_{n}^{[n-1]}(T)=\{n\ldots 21\}$ . Combining propositions 4.1 and 4.4, then applying relation (4.1), we get the following ones





 $\begin{cases}F_{n}^{n-1}({\rm T};q)&=F_{n}^{n-1}({\rm T};q)=1.\\
F_{n}^{k}({\rm T};q)&=q^{\min\{k-1,n-1-k\}}F_{n}^{k}({\rm T};q)+F_{n}^{k+1}({\rm T};q)\text{ for all $k\geq 1$}.\\
F_{n}({\rm T};q)&=F_{n-1}({\rm T};q)+F_{n}^{1}({\rm T};q).\end{cases}$ 

(4.4)


We can show easily by induction on  $n$  that the two relationships (1.2) and (4.4) are the same. So, we have  $F_{n}(213,312;q)=R_{n}^{0}(q)$  and  $F_{n}^{k}({\rm T};q)=R_{n}^{k}(q)$  for  $k\geq 1$ . Using the fact that  $\{132,312\}=\rm rci(\{213,312\})$ , we complete the proof of theorem 1.2.



Corollary 4.5.



The number of (213,312)- or (132,312)-avoiding noncrossing permutations of  $[n]$  is the n-th central polygonal number  $\binom{n}{2}+1$ .




Proof.



It is easy to prove it by induction on  $n$  that  $F_{n}(213,312;0)=R_{n}^{0}(0)=\binom{n}{2}+1$ .
∎




Theorem 4.6.



Let  $T$  be one of the pairs  $\{213,231\}$  and  $\{132,231\}$ .
For all integer  $n$ , we have





 $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}({\rm T})}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=R_{n+1}^{1}(q)$ 






Proof.



The restriction of  $\phi_{1}$  (or  $\psi_{1}$ ) on  $S_{n}(213,231)$  is a bijection from  $S_{n}(213,231)$  to  $S_{n+1}^{1}(213,312)$ . Translating this fact into generating function, we have





 $\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}(213,231)}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n+1}^{1}(213,312)}q^{{\rm cr}(\sigma)}=R_{n+1}^{1}(q).$ 



We complete the proof of theorem 4.6 using the fact that  $\{132,231\}=_{{\rm cr}}\{213,231\}$  since  $\{132,231\}=\rm rci(\{213,231\})$ .
∎
