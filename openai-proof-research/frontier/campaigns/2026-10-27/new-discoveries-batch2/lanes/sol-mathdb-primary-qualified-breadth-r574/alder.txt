Abstract.
    

Integer partitions have long been of interest to number theorists, perhaps most notably Ramanujan, and are related to many areas of mathematics including combinatorics, modular forms, representation theory, analysis, and mathematical physics. Here, we focus on partitions with gap conditions and partitions with parts coming from fixed residue classes.
    

Let  $\Delta_{d}^{(a,b)}(n)=q_{d}^{(a)}(n)-Q_{d}^{(b)}(n)$  where  $q_{d}^{(a)}(n)$  counts the number of partitions of  $n$  into parts with difference at least  $d$  and size at least  $a$ , and  $Q_{d}^{(b)}(n)$  counts the number of partitions into parts  $\equiv\pm b\ \left(\mathrm{mod}\ d+3\right)$ . In 1956, Alder conjectured that  $\Delta_{d}^{(1,1)}(n)\geq 0$  for all positive  $n$  and  $d$ . This conjecture was proved partially by Andrews in 1971, by Yee in 2008, and was fully resolved by Alfes, Jameson and Lemke Oliver in 2011. Alder’s conjecture generalizes several well-known partition identities, including Euler’s theorem that the number of partitions of  $n$  into odd parts equals the number of partitions of  $n$  into distinct parts, as well as the first of the famous Rogers-Ramanujan identities.
    

In 2020, Kang and Park constructed an extension of Alder’s conjecture which relates to the second Rogers-Ramanujan identity by considering  $\Delta_{d}^{(a,b,-)}(n)=q_{d}^{(a)}(n)-Q_{d}^{(b,-)}(n)$  where  $Q_{d}^{(b,-)}(n)$  counts the number of partitions into parts  $\equiv\pm b\ \left(\mathrm{mod}\ d+3\right)$  excluding the  $d+3-b$  part. Kang and Park conjectured that  $\Delta_{d}^{(2,2,-)}(n)\geq 0$  for all  $d\geq 1$  and  $n\geq 0$ , and proved this for  $d=2^{r}-2$  and  $n$  even.
    

We prove Kang and Park’s conjecture for all but finitely many  $d$ . Toward proving the remaining cases, we adapt work of Alfes, Jameson and Lemke Oliver to generate asymptotics for the related functions. Finally, we present a more generalized conjecture for higher  $a=b$  and prove it for infinite classes of  $n$  and  $d$ .
  



Key words and phrases: partitions, Rogers-Ramanujan identities, Alder’s conjecture

1. Introduction




A partition of a positive integer  $n$  is a non-increasing sequence of positive integers, called parts, that sum to  $n$ . Let  $p(n\mid\text{condition})$  count the number of partitions of  $n$  satisfying the specified condition. Euler famously proved that the number of partitions of a positive integer  $n$  into odd parts equals the number of partitions of  $n$  into distinct parts. Two other celebrated partition identities are those of Rogers and Ramanujan. The first Rogers-Ramanujan identity states that the number of partitions of  $n$  into parts differing by  $2$  is equal to the number of partitions of  $n$  into parts that are congruent to  $\pm 1\ \left(\mathrm{mod}\ 5\right)$  and the second Rogers-Ramanujan identity states that the number of partitions of  $n$  into parts differing by  $2$  and with parts at least  $2$  is equal to the number of partitions of  $n$  into parts that are congruent to  $\pm 2\ \left(\mathrm{mod}\ 5\right)$ . These are encapsulated by





 $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n^{2}}}{(q;q)_{n}}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q^{5})_{\infty}(q^{4};q^{5})_{\infty}},$ 




 $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n^{2}+n}}{(q;q)_{n}}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q^{2};q^{5})_{\infty}(q^{3};q^{5})_{\infty}},$ 



where  $(a;q)_{0}=1$ , and  $(a;q)_{n}:=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(1-aq^{k})$  for  $n\geq 1$ , where  $n=\infty$  is also allowed.




Motivated by these identities, Schur found that the number of partitions of  $n$  into parts differing by  $3$  or more among which no two consecutive multiples of 3 appear is equal to the number of partitions of  $n$  into parts congruent to  $\pm 1\pmod{6}$ .




After showing that no other such partition identities can exist, in 1956 Alder [2, 1] made a claim about a generalization of similar partition inequalities. Alder’s conjecture states that the number of partitions of  $n$  into parts with difference of at least  $d$  is greater than or equal to than the number of partitions of  $n$  into parts congruent to  $\pm 1\ \left(\mathrm{mod}\ d+3\right)$ . Notice that the Euler, first Rogers-Ramanujan, and Schur identities are special cases of Alder’s Conjecture. In 1971, Andrews [4] proved Alder’s conjecture for  $n\geq 1$  and  $d=2^{r}-1$ ,  $r\geq 4$ . In 2004 and 2008, Yee [13, 12] proved the conjecture for  $n\geq 1$ ,  $d\geq 32$ , and  $d=7$ . In 2011, Alfes, Jameson, and Lemke Oliver [3] proved Alder’s Conjecture for  $n\geq 1$  and  $4\leq d\leq 30,d\neq 7,15$ , thus completely resolving the conjecture.




In 2020, Kang and Park [7] investigated how to generalize Alder’s conjecture further by incorporating the second Rogers-Ramanujan identity. Kang and Park compared the partition functions






 $\displaystyle q_{d}^{(a)}(n):=p(n|\text{ parts}\geq a\text{ and parts differ by at least }d),$ 





 $\displaystyle Q_{d}^{(b)}(n):=p(n|\text{ parts}\equiv\pm b\ \left(\mathrm{mod}\ d+3\right)),$ 



by defining the difference function





 $\displaystyle\Delta_{d}^{(a,b)}(n)$ 
 $\displaystyle:=q_{d}^{(a)}(n)-Q_{d}^{(b)}(n),$ 




 $\displaystyle\Delta_{d}^{(a)}(n)$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\Delta_{d}^{(a,a)}(n).$ 





Remark 1.1.



Notice that





 $\displaystyle\text{Euler's identity }\iff\Delta_{1}^{(1)}\left(n\right)$ 
 $\displaystyle=0\text{ for all }n\geq 1$ 




 $\displaystyle\text{Rogers-Ramanujan ($1^{st}$ identity)}\iff\Delta_{2}^{(1)}\left(n\right)$ 
 $\displaystyle=0\text{ for all }n\geq 1$ 




 $\displaystyle\text{Rogers-Ramanujan ($2^{nd}$ identity)}\iff\Delta_{2}^{(2)}\left(n\right)$ 
 $\displaystyle=0\text{ for all }n\geq 1$ 




 $\displaystyle\text{Schur's identity }\implies\Delta_{3}^{(1)}\left(n\right)$ 
 $\displaystyle\geq 0\text{ for all }n\geq 1.$ 







Using Kang and Park’s notation, Alder’s conjecture (now theorem) can be stated as




(1.1)

 $\Delta_{d}^{(1)}\left(n\right)=q_{d}^{(1)}\left(n\right)-Q_{d}^{(1)}\left(n\right)\geq 0$ 



for  $d,n\geq 1$ .




Kang and Park were interested in finding an analog of Alder’s conjecture for the second Rogers-Ramanujan identity. However, by observing the data, they found that





 $\Delta_{d}^{(2)}\left(n\right)<0\text{ for some choices of }d,n\geq 1.$ 



In order to find a suitable analog, Kang and Park modified  $Q_{d}^{(2)}\left(n\right)$  by defining for  $d,n\geq 1$ ,





 $\displaystyle Q_{d}^{(2,-)}\left(n\right)$ 
 $\displaystyle:=p(n\,|\text{ parts}\equiv\pm 2\ \left(\mathrm{mod}\ d+3\right),\text{ excluding the part }d+1),$ 




 $\displaystyle\Delta_{d}^{(2,-)}\left(n\right)$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\Delta_{d}^{(2,2,-)}\left(n\right):=q_{d}^{(2)}\left(n\right)-Q_{d}^{(2,-)}\left(n\right),$ 



and presented the following conjecture.



Conjecture 1.2 (Kang, Park [7], 2020).



For all  $d,n\geq 1$ ,





 $\Delta_{d}^{(2,-)}\left(n\right)\geq 0.$ 







Kang and Park [7] proved Conjecture 1.2 when  $d=2$  or  $d=2^{s}-2$  for any positive integer  $s\geq 5$ , and positive even integer  $n$ . By developing a new way of comparing these partition functions, we prove Kang and Park’s conjecture except for the cases  $d=1$  and  $3\leq d\leq 61$ 11

Theorem 1.7.

 $\Delta_{d}^{(a,-,-)}\left(2^{t}n\right)\geq 0.$

Theorem 1.8.

 $\Delta_{d}^{(a,-,-)}\left(2^{a-1}n\right)\geq 0.$

Theorem 1.9.

We now outline the rest of the paper. In Section 2, we generalize a theorem of Andrews [4] and establish some other useful lemmas. In Section 3, we prove two modified versions of Alder’s conjecture that will be used in the proofs of Theorem 1.3 and Theorem 1.6. In Section 4, we prove Theorem 1.3 by considering four cases based on the parities of  $n$  and  $d$ , and present a partial proof of Conjecture 1.4 in Proposition 4.1. In Section 5, we modify methods from the proof Theorem 1.3 to prove Theorem 1.6, adapt methods of Andrews [4] to prove Theorem 1.7, and generalize methods of Yee [13] to prove Theorem 1.8. We then discuss how asymptotics could be useful to prove remaining cases of Conjecture 1.2. Finally, in Section 7, we conclude with some remarks about our choice of definitions.





2. Preliminaries




In this section, we first develop generating functions for our partition functions, and then establish some important lemmas that we will employ in later sections. As in Yee [13], we occasionally denote the coefficient of  $q^{n}$  in an infinite series  $s(q)$  as  $[q^{n}](s(q))$ .




For  $a,d\geq 1$ , the generating function for  $q_{d}^{(a)}(n)$  is




(2.1)

 $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q_{d}^{(a)}(n)q^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{d{k\choose 2}+ka}}{(q;q)_{k}}.$ 



Equation (2.1) appears in work of Alder [2], and the case where  $a=1$  is proved combinatorially in [8], which can be easily generalized to  $a\geq 1$ .




As stated in Alfes, Jameson, and Lemke Oliver [3], the generating function for  $Q_{d}^{(b)}(n)$  is




(2.2)

 $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q_{d}^{(b)}(n)q^{n}=\frac{1}{(q^{d+3-b};q^{d+3})_{\infty}(q^{b};q^{d+3})_{\infty}}.$ 



From (2.2) we easily obtain the generating function for  $Q_{d}^{(b,-)}(n)$  by removing the term that generates the part  $d+3-b$ . For  $1\leq b\leq d+2$ , the generating function for  $Q_{d}^{(b,-)}(n)$  is thus given by




(2.3)

 $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q_{d}^{(b,-)}(n)q^{n}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{(q^{2d+6-b};q^{d+3})_{\infty}}&\text{ for }b=\frac{d+3}{2},\\
\frac{1}{(q^{2d+6-b};q^{d+3})_{\infty}(q^{b};q^{d+3})_{\infty}}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}$ 



Similarly we can construct the generating function for  $Q_{d}^{(b,-,-)}(n)$ , which for  $1\leq b\leq d+2$ , is given by




(2.4)

 $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q_{d}^{(b,-,-)}(n)q^{n}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{(q^{2d+6-b};q^{d+3})_{\infty}}&\text{ for }b=\frac{d+3}{2},\\
\frac{1}{(q^{2d+6-b};q^{d+3})_{\infty}(q^{d+3+b};q^{d+3})_{\infty}}&\text{ otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}$ 






Andrews [4] uses the following theorem in his proof of certain cases of Alder’s conjecture. Write





 $\rho(R;n):=p(n\mid\text{parts are from the set }R).$ 





Theorem 2.1 (Andrews [4], 1971 ).



Let  $S=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}$  and  $T=\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}$  be two strictly increasing sequences of positive integers such that  $y_{1}=1$  and  $x_{i}\geq y_{i}$  for all  $i$ . Then for all  $n>0$ ,





 $\rho(T;n)\geq\rho(S;n).$ 







Yee [13] employs this theorem in her work on Alder’s conjecture. Kang and Park [7] present a generalization of Theorem 2.1 which we generalize further below.



Lemma 2.2.



Let  $S=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}$  and  $T=\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}$  be strictly increasing sequences of positive integers such that  $y_{1}=m$ ,  $m$  divides each  $y_{i}$ , and  $x_{i}\geq y_{i}$  for all  $i$ . Then for all  $n\geq 1$ ,





 $\rho(T;mn)\geq\rho(S;mn).$ 






Proof.



Let  $\widetilde{S}_{mn}$ ,  $\widetilde{T}_{mn}$  be the sets of partitions of  $mn$  with parts coming from  $S$ ,  $T$ , respectively. We construct an injection  $\varphi:\widetilde{S}_{mn}\to\widetilde{T}_{mn}$ , similar to Yee’s [13] proof of Theorem 2.1.




Let  $\lambda$  be a partition in  $\widetilde{S}_{mn}$ . Define  $e_{i}$  to be the number of times  $x_{i}$  occurs in  $\lambda$ , so that  $\lambda$  can be described by  $mn=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}e_{i}x_{i}$ , which is clearly divisible by  $m$ . Since each  $y_{i}$  is divisible by  $m$ , we can conclude that





 $\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(x_{i}-y_{i})e_{i}$ 



is divisible by  $m$ .




Define the sequence  $\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}$  by





 $f_{i}=\begin{cases}e_{1}+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(x_{j}-y_{j})e_{j},&i=1\\
e_{i},&i>1.\end{cases}$ 



From this we can define  $\varphi(\lambda)$  to be the partition consisting of parts  $y_{i}$  each occurring  $f_{i}$  times. Since  $y_{1}=m$ ,





 $\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f_{i}y_{i}=e_{1}y_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(x_{i}-y_{i})e_{i}+\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}e_{i}y_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}e_{i}x_{i}=mn,$ 



so  $\varphi(\lambda)$  is indeed a partition of  $mn$  and thus  $\varphi(\lambda)\in\widetilde{T}_{mn}$  as desired.




It remains to show that  $\varphi$  is injective. Let  $\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in\widetilde{S}_{mn}$  such that  $\lambda$  is described by  $mn=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}e_{i}x_{i}$ , and  $\lambda^{\prime}$  is described by  $mn=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}g_{i}x_{i}$ . If  $\varphi(\lambda)=\varphi(\lambda^{\prime})$ , then we can immediately conclude  $e_{i}=g_{i}$  for all  $i\geq 2$  by the definition of  $\varphi$ . Moreover, we must have





 $e_{1}+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(x_{i}-y_{i})e_{i}=g_{1}+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(x_{i}-y_{i})g_{i},$ 



and thus  $e_{1}=g_{1}$  as well. So  $\lambda=\lambda^{\prime}$  and we conclude that  $\varphi$  is injective.
∎

Corollary 2.3.

We now present two lemmas that we use in the proofs of Theorem 1.3, Theorem 1.6, and later Proposition 4.1.



Lemma 2.4.



Let  $a,d\geq 1$ , and  $n\geq d+2a$ . Then,





 $q_{d}^{(a)}(n)\geq q_{\left\lceil\frac{d}{a}\right\rceil}^{(1)}\left(\left\lceil\frac{n}{a}\right\rceil\right).$ 






Proof.



For a positive integer  $x$ , write  $\hat{x}$  to denote the least residue of  $-x\pmod{a}$  so that  $\left\lceil\frac{x}{a}\right\rceil=\frac{x+\hat{x}}{a}$ . Fix  $n\geq d+2a$ , and define  $q_{d+\hat{d}}^{(a)}\left(n+\hat{n}\right)^{*}$  to count the number of partitions of  $n+\hat{n}$  into parts  $\geq a$ , each a multiple of  $a$ , with difference between parts  $\geq d+\hat{d}$ . Then it is straightforward to see that





 $q_{d+\hat{d}}^{(a)}\left(n+\hat{n}\right)^{*}=q_{\frac{d+\hat{d}}{a}}^{(1)}\left(\frac{n+\hat{n}}{a}\right)=q_{\left\lceil\frac{d}{a}\right\rceil}^{(1)}\left(\left\lceil\frac{n}{a}\right\rceil\right),$ 



by the natural bijection between the relevant sets of partitions obtained by dividing (or multiplying) the parts by  $a$ . Thus it suffices to show that  $q_{d}^{(a)}(n)\geq q_{d+\hat{d}}^{(a)}\left(n+\hat{n}\right)^{*}$ .




Let  $X(n)$  be the set of partitions of  $n$  counted by  $q_{d}^{(a)}(n)$  and  $Y(n+\hat{n})$  the set of partitions of  $n+\hat{n}$  counted by  $q_{d+\hat{d}}^{(a)}\left(n+\hat{n}\right)^{*}$ . We construct an injection  $\varphi:Y(n+\hat{n})\to X(n)$ . Note that when  $n$  is a multiple of  $a$ , the identity map gives such an injection. Suppose  $n\not\equiv 0\pmod{a}$ , and  $\lambda\in Y(n+\hat{n})$  is of the form  $n+\hat{n}=\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k}$ , where the parts are listed in increasing order. Define  $\varphi(\lambda)$  by





 $\varphi(\lambda)=\begin{cases}(a,n-a)&\text{ if }k=1\\
(n)&\text{ if }\lambda_{1}=a,k=2\\
(\lambda_{2},...,\lambda_{k}+a-\hat{n})&\text{ if }\lambda_{1}=a,k\geq 3\\
(\lambda_{1}-\hat{n},\lambda_{2},...,\lambda_{k})&\text{ if }\lambda_{1}>a,k\geq 2.\end{cases}$ 



Since  $n\geq d+2a$ , it follows that  $\varphi(\lambda)\in X(n)$ . Moreover, observe that the first case above is the only case where the smallest part of  $\varphi(\lambda)$  is  $a$ . Thus, the only way for two partitions  $\lambda\neq\lambda^{\prime}\in Y(n+\hat{n})$  to map to the same partition under  $\varphi$  would be through the third and fourth cases above. However, since  $\hat{n}\neq 0$ , the last part arising from the third case is not a multiple of  $a$  and thus cannot equal the any last part arising from the fourth case. So  $\varphi$  is an injection and thus  $q_{d}^{(a)}(n)\geq q_{d+\hat{d}}^{(a)}\left(n+\hat{n}\right)^{*}$  as desired.
∎

(4.1)

 $\displaystyle S$

 $\displaystyle k_{d}^{(a)}(q)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1-q^{d+2^{a-1}}}{1-q^{2^{a-1}}}\cdot\frac{(q^{2d+2^{a}};q^{2d})_{\infty}}{(q^{d+2^{a-1}};q^{d})_{\infty}}\cdot\frac{(q^{2d+2^{a+1}};q^{2d})_{\infty}}{(q^{d+2^{a}};q^{d})_{\infty}}\cdot\cdots\cdot\frac{(q^{2d+2^{r}};q^{2d})_{\infty}}{(q^{d+2^{r-1}};q^{d})_{\infty}}$

 $\displaystyle=\frac{(q^{4d+2^{r}};q^{4d})_{\infty}(-q^{d+2^{r-1}};q^{2d})_{\infty}}{(q^{2^{a-1}};q^{2d})_{\infty}(q^{3d+2^{a-1}};q^{2d})_{\infty}(q^{d+2^{a}};q^{2d})_{\infty}\cdots(q^{d+2^{r-2}};q^{2d})_{\infty}}.$

Similarly, define  $S^{-}(n)$  to be the set containing partitions of  $n$  into unrestricted parts  $\equiv 2^{a-1},d+2^{a-1},d+2^{a},...,d+2^{r-2}\pmod{2d}$  excluding the part  $d+2^{a-1}$ , distinct parts  $\equiv d+2^{r-1}\pmod{2d}$ , and an odd number of distinct parts  $\equiv 2^{r}\pmod{4d}$  that are at least  $4d+2^{r}$ . Then we have that





 $\mathcal{K}_{d}^{(a)}(n)=|S^{+}(n)|-|S^{-}(n)|.$ 



Write  $S(n)=S^{+}(n)\cup S^{-}(n)$ , and define the sign of a partition  $\pi\in S(n)$  by





 $\textrm{sgn}(\pi)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\pi\in S^{+}(n)\\
-1&\text{if }\pi\in S^{-}(n).\\
\end{cases}$ 



Additionally, let  $T(n)$  denote the set of partitions of  $n$  into unrestricted parts  $\equiv 2^{a-1},d+2^{a},...,d+2^{r-2}\pmod{2d}$  and distinct parts  $\equiv d+2^{r-1}\pmod{2d}$  so that





 $\mathcal{G}_{d}^{(a)}(n)=|T(n)|.$ 



Thus it suffices to prove that  $|T(n)|\leq|S^{+}(n)|-|S^{-}(n)|$ , which since  $T(n)\subseteq S^{+}(n)$  is disjoint from  $S^{-}(n)$  is equivalent to showing





 $|T(n)\cup S^{-}(n)|\leq|S^{+}(n)|.$ 



We prove this by constructing a bijective function  $\varphi:S(n)\rightarrow S(n)$  such that




(5.11)

 $\varphi(T(n)\cup S^{-}(n))\subseteq S^{+}(n).$ 






The function  $\varphi$  is constructed by an iterative process which we outline below. Set  $S_{a-2}:=\emptyset$ . For each  $a-1\leq t\leq r-2$ , we will define





 $\varphi_{t}:S(n)\backslash\bigcup_{j=a-1}^{t-1}S_{j}(n)\rightarrow S(n)\backslash\bigcup_{j=a-1}^{t-1}S_{j}(n),$ 



and correspondingly set




(5.12)

 $S_{t}(n):=\{\pi\mid\varphi_{t}(\pi)\neq\pi\}.$ 



By definition, the sets  $S_{t}(n)$ , for  $a-1\leq t\leq r-2$  are disjoint and together with  $\bigcup_{t=a-1}^{r-2}S_{t}(n)$ , partition  $S(n)$ . We define  $\varphi:S(n)\rightarrow S(n)$  by





 $\varphi(\pi)=\begin{cases}\varphi_{t}(\pi)&\text{if }\pi\in S_{t}(n),\text{ for }a-1\leq t\leq r-2,\\
\pi&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}$ 



Since  $T(n)\subseteq S^{+}(n)$ , the proof of (5.11) can then be completed by showing that


(1) 



 $\varphi_{t}$  is a bijection for each  $a-1\leq t\leq r-2$ ,


(2) 



If  $\pi\in S_{t}(n)$ , for  $a-1\leq t\leq r-2$ , then  $\rm{sgn}(\varphi_{t}(\pi))=-\rm{sgn}(\pi)$ ,


(3) 



 $S^{-}(n)\subseteq\bigcup_{t=a-1}^{r-2}S_{t}(n)$ , and


(4) 



 $T(n)\subseteq S(n)\backslash\bigcup_{t=a-1}^{r-2}S_{t}(n)$ .






In order to define  $\varphi_{t}$ , we use the following notation used by Yee [13]. Write  $j\in\pi$  if  $j$  is a part of  $\pi$  and write  $j^{x}$  to indicate  $x$  occurrences of  $j$  as a part. Also, when the partition  $\pi$  is set, let  $m_{j}$  denote the number of times  $j$  occurs as a part. Finally, let  $\alpha:=d+2^{r}-2^{a-1}$  where  $r$  is defined by (1.2).



Remark 5.4.



In what follows we define  $\varphi_{t}$  for the cases  $t=a-1$ , and  $a\leq t\leq r-2$  separately. In all cases, the only parts that are altered are either of the form  $2^{r-j}kd+2^{r}$ , where  $a-1\leq j\leq r-2$  and  $k\in\mathbb{Z}$ ,  $2^{a-1}$ , or  $kd+2^{t}$ , where  $a-1\leq j\leq r-2$  and  $k\in\mathbb{Z}$ . Clearly  $2^{r-j}kd+2^{r}\equiv 2^{r}\pmod{4d}$ . Also,  $2^{a-1}\not\equiv 2^{r}\pmod{4d}$ , since  $2^{a-1}<2^{r}<4d$ . By (1.2) and our hypothesis that  $d\not=2^{r}-2^{a-1}$  we have  $2^{r}-2^{j}<d$ , so it follows that  $2^{r-j}kd+2^{r}\not\equiv 2^{r}\pmod{4d}$  as well.





We first define  $\varphi_{a-1}$ . For a fixed partition  $\pi\in S(n)$ , let





 $\displaystyle x$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\text{smallest integer }i\text{ such that }2^{r-(a-1)}id+2^{r}\in\pi$ 




 $\displaystyle y$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\text{smallest integer }j\text{ such that }(2^{r-(a-1)}j-1)d+2^{a-1}\in\pi$ 




 $\displaystyle z$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\text{smallest }l>y\text{ such that }m_{ld+2^{a-1}}\geq 2^{r-(a-1)},$ 



where if there is no such  $x$ ,  $y$ ,  $z$ , we set  $x=\infty$ ,  $y=\infty$ , or  $z=\infty$ , respectively. We then define  $\varphi_{a-1}(\pi)$  by making the following substitutions among parts of  $\pi$ ,





 $\begin{cases}2^{r-(a-1)}xd+2^{r}\to(2^{r-(a-1)}x-1)d+2^{a-1},(2^{a-1})^{\frac{\alpha}{2^{a-1}}}&\text{if }x\leq y,x<\infty,\\
(2^{r-(a-1)}y-1)d+2^{a-1},(2^{a-1})^{\frac{\alpha}{2^{a-1}}}\to 2^{r-(a-1)}yd+2^{r}&\text{if }x>y,m_{2^{a-1}}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2^{a-1}},\\
2^{r-(a-1)}xd+2^{r}\to(xd+2^{a-1})^{2^{r-(a-1)}},&\text{if }x>y,x<\infty,m_{2^{a-1}}<\frac{\alpha}{2^{a-1}},x\leq z,\\
(zd+2^{a-1})^{2^{r-(a-1)}}\to 2^{r-(a-1)}zd+2^{r},&\text{if }x>y,m_{2^{a-1}}<\frac{\alpha}{2^{a-1}},x>z,\end{cases}$ 



and setting  $\varphi_{a-1}(\pi)=\pi$  otherwise. Under each condition, the number  $n$  being partitioned doesn’t change. Since the conditions defining  $\varphi_{a-1}$  are pairwise disjoint, and ensure the required parts exist,  $\varphi_{a-1}$  is well-defined. Moreover as the parts  $\equiv 2^{r}\pmod{4d}$  in the definition of  $S(n)$  are distinct, we can see that  $\varphi_{a-1}^{2}(\pi)=\pi$ , and so  $\varphi_{a-1}$  is clearly a bijection. By our discussion in Remark 5.4 we see that in each case of the definition, a part  $\equiv 2^{r}\pmod{4d}$  is either removed or added. Thus by (5.12), if  $\pi\in S_{a-1}(n)$ , we have





 $\textrm{sgn}(\varphi_{a-1}(\pi))=-\textrm{sgn}(\pi).$ 



Notice that if  $x<\infty$ , then  $\pi$  must satisfy one of the four conditions in the definition of  $\varphi_{a-1}$ . Thus for any  $\pi\not\in S_{a-1}(n)$ ,  $x=\infty$ , i.e.,  $\pi$  has no part  $\equiv 2^{r}\pmod{2^{r-(a-1)}d}$ , however may have a part of the form  $2^{r-a}kd+2^{r}$  with  $k$  odd. We also observe that if  $\pi\not\in S_{a-1}(n)$  and  $y<\infty$ , then  $m_{2^{a-1}}<\frac{\alpha}{2^{a-1}}$  and  $z=\infty$ .




We now iteratively define  $\varphi_{t}$  for  $a\leq t\leq r-2$ . For a fixed partition  $\pi\in S(n)\backslash\bigcup_{j=a-1}^{t-1}S_{j}(n)$ , let





 $\displaystyle u$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\text{smallest integer }p\text{ such that }(2^{r-t+1}p-1)d+2^{a-1}\in\pi,$ 




 $\displaystyle x$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\text{smallest odd integer }i\text{ such that }2^{r-t}id+2^{r}\in\pi,$ 




 $\displaystyle y$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\text{smallest odd integer }j\text{ such that }(2^{r-t}j-1)d+2^{a-1}\in\pi,$ 




 $\displaystyle w$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\text{smallest odd integer }l\text{ such that }m_{ld+2^{t}}\geq 2^{r-t},$ 




 $\displaystyle z$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\text{smallest odd integer }l>y\text{ such that }m_{ld+2^{t}}\geq 2^{r-t},$ 



where if there is no such  $u$ ,  $x$ ,  $y$ ,  $w$ ,  $z$ , we set  $u=\infty$ ,  $x=\infty$ ,  $y=\infty$ ,  $w=\infty$ , or  $z=\infty$ , respectively. We then define  $\varphi_{t}(\pi)$  by making the following substitutions among parts of  $\pi$ ,





 $\begin{cases}2^{r-a}xd+2^{r}\to(xd+2^{a})^{2^{r-a}}&\text{if }u<\infty,x<\infty,x\leq w,\\
(wd+2^{a})^{2^{r-a}}\to 2^{r-a}wd+2^{r}&\text{if }u<\infty,x>w,w<\infty,\\
2^{r-a}xd+2^{r}\to(2^{r-a}x-1)d+2^{a-1},(2^{a-1})^{\frac{\alpha}{2^{a-1}}}&\text{if }u=\infty,x\leq y,x<\infty,\\
(2^{r-a}y-1)d+2^{a-1},(2^{a-1})^{\frac{\alpha}{2^{a-1}}}\to 2^{r-a}yd+2^{r}&\text{if }u=\infty,x>y,m_{2^{a-1}}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2^{a-1}},\\
2^{r-a}xd+2^{r}\to(xd+2^{a})^{2^{r-a}}&\text{if }u=\infty,x>y,x<\infty,m_{2^{a-1}}<\frac{\alpha}{2^{a-1}},x\leq z,\\
(zd+2^{a})^{2^{r-a}}\to 2^{r-a}zd+2^{r}&\text{if }u=\infty,x>y,m_{2^{a-1}}<\frac{\alpha}{2^{a-1}},x>z,\end{cases}$ 



and setting  $\varphi_{t}(\pi)=\pi$  otherwise. Under each condition, the number  $n$  being partitioned doesn’t change. Since the conditions defining  $\varphi_{a}$  are pairwise disjoint, and ensure the required parts exist,  $\varphi_{a}$  is well-defined. As in the previous case,  $\varphi_{t}^{2}(\pi)=\pi$ , so we have a bijection, and by our discussion in Remark 5.4 as well as (5.12), we have  $\textrm{sgn}(\varphi_{t}(\pi))=-\textrm{sgn}(\pi)$  for  $\pi\in S_{t}(n)$ .




We again observe that if  $x<\infty$ , then  $\pi$  must satisfy one of the four conditions in the definition of  $\varphi_{t}$ . Thus for any  $\pi\not\in S(n)\backslash\bigcup_{j=a-1}^{t-1}S_{j}(n)$ ,  $x=\infty$ , i.e.,  $\pi$  has no part  $\equiv 2^{r}\pmod{2^{r-t}d}$ , however may have a part of the form  $2^{r-t-1}kd+2^{r}$  with  $k$  odd. We also observe that if  $\pi\not\in S(n)\backslash\bigcup_{j=a-1}^{t-1}S_{j}(n)$  and  $\pi$  has no part  $\equiv 2^{a-1}-d\pmod{2^{r-t}d}$ , then  $m_{2^{a-1}}<\frac{\alpha}{2^{a-1}}$  and  $z=\infty$ .




We now show that  $S^{-}(n)\subseteq\bigcup_{t=a-1}^{r-2}S_{t}(n)$ . If  $\pi\in S^{-}(n)$  then  $\pi$  must contain a part  $\equiv 2^{r}\pmod{4d}$ , since there is an odd number of such parts. So this part has the form  $4dk+2^{r}$ , where  $k$  is a nonnegative integer. If  $\pi$  contains such a part with  $2^{r-(a-1)}\mid 4k$ , then  $\pi\in S_{a-1}$ . If not, there exists  $a\leq t\leq r-2$  such that  $2^{r-t}\parallel 4k$ . Then  $\pi\in S_{t}$ .




To finish the proof it now suffices to show that  $T(n)\subseteq S\backslash\bigcup_{t=a-1}^{r-2}S_{t}(n)$ . Let  $\pi\in T(n)$ . Then  $\pi$  has no parts  $\equiv 2^{r}\pmod{4d}$ . So for each  $a-1\leq t\leq r-2$ , in the definition of  $\varphi_{t}$ ,  $x=\infty$ . But in each case, having  $\pi\in S_{t}(n)$  and  $x=\infty$  forces  $\pi$  to contain a part congruent to either  $2^{a-1}$  or  $2^{a}$  modulo an odd multiple of  $d$ . Since  $\pi$  cannot contain parts of this form, we conclude that  $T(n)\not\in S_{t}(n)$  for any  $a-1\leq t\leq r-2$  as desired.




∎





We are now able to prove Theorem 1.8.



Proof of Theorem 1.8.



By (1.2), we have that  $2^{r-a+1}\leq m+1<2^{r-a+2}$ . Thus for each  $0\leq n\leq m$ ,  $n$  can be written uniquely in binary as  $\sum_{i=0}^{r-a+1}\varepsilon_{i}2^{i}$ , where  $\varepsilon_{i}\in\{0,1\}$ . It follows that each term  $x^{n}$  appears in the expansion of  $\prod_{i=0}^{r-a+1}(1+x^{2^{i}})$ . Setting  $x=q^{2^{a-1}}$  yields that




(5.13)

 $\prod_{i=0}^{r-a+1}(1+q^{2^{a-1+i}})-(1+q^{2^{a-1}}+q^{2\cdot 2^{a-1}}+q^{3\cdot 2^{a-1}}+\cdots+q^{d})$ 



has nonnegative  $q$ -coefficients. From (5.5) we thus observe that




(5.14)

 $(1+q^{2^{r}})f_{d}^{(a)}(q)-k_{d}^{(a)}(q)=\left(\prod_{i=0}^{r-a+1}(1+q^{2^{a-1+i}})-(1+q^{2^{a-1}}+q^{2\cdot 2^{a-1}}+q^{3\cdot 2^{a-1}}+\cdots+q^{d})\right)\\
\cdot(-q^{d+2^{a-1}};q^{d})_{\infty}(-q^{d+2^{a}};q^{d})_{\infty}\cdots(-q^{d+2^{r-1}};q^{d})_{\infty}$ 



has nonnegative coefficients. Since





 $(1+q^{2^{r}})f_{d}^{(a)}(q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\left(\mathcal{L}_{d}^{(a)}(n)+\mathcal{L}_{d}^{(a)}(n-2^{r})\right)q^{n},$ 



where  $\mathcal{L}_{d}^{(a)}(n):=0$  for any  $n<0$ , (5.14) shows that when  $a,m,n\geq 1$ ,  $d=2^{a-1}m$ , and  $r$  is defined by (1.2),




(5.15)

 $\mathcal{L}_{d}^{(a)}(n)+\mathcal{L}_{d}^{(a)}(n-2^{r})\geq\mathcal{K}_{d}^{(a)}(n).$ 



Applying Lemmas 5.2 and 5.3 to (5.15), we have that when  $a\geq 3$ ,  $d=2^{a-1}m$  with  $m\geq 8$  and  $m\neq 2^{r-a+1}-1$ , where  $r$  is defined by (1.2), then for  $n\geq 2^{a}m+2^{r}+2^{a-1}-1$ ,





 $q_{d}^{(a)}\left(n\right)\geq\mathcal{G}_{d}^{(a)}(n).$ 



To finish the proof it thus suffices to show that for  $a\geq 3$ , and  $d=2^{a-1}m$  where  $m\geq 31$ ,




(5.16)

 $\mathcal{G}_{d}^{(a)}(2^{a-1}n)\geq Q_{d}^{(a,-,-)}\left(2^{a-1}n\right).$ 






Setting





 $S_{a,d}:=\{x\in\mathbb{N}\mid x\equiv\pm a\!\!\!\pmod{d+3}\}\backslash\{a,d+3-a\},$ 



we have  $\rho(S_{a,d};n)=Q_{d}^{(a,-,-)}(n)$ . Moreover, since  $(-q^{d+2^{r-1}};q^{2d})_{\infty}$  has nonnegative  $q$ -coefficients, it follows that  $\mathcal{G}_{d}^{(a)}(n)\geq\rho(T_{a-1,r-1,d};n)$ , where





 $T_{a-1,r-1,d}:=\{y\in\mathbb{N}\mid y\equiv 2^{a-1},d+2^{a},...,d+2^{r-2}\!\!\!\pmod{2d}\}.$ 



Thus, (5.16) will follow from showing that  $\rho(T_{a-1,r-1,d};2^{a-1}n)\geq\rho(S_{a,d};2^{a-1}n)$ , which is an immediate consequence of Corollary 2.3.
∎







6. Asymptotic results




In this section we first prove Theorem 1.9 which is analogous to a result of Andrews [4, Theorem 2]. We then provide asymptotic bounds for the functions  $q_{d}^{(a)}(n)$  and  $Q_{d}^{(a)}(n)$ , and discuss potential methods to computationally prove the finitely many remaining cases of Conjecture 1.2.



Proof of Theorem 1.9.



First, we observe that by equation (1.3), it suffices to consider  $\Delta_{d}^{(a)}(n)$  instead of  $\Delta_{d}^{(a,-,-)}(n)$ . By work of Meinardus [10, Theorems  $2$  and  $3$ ], it follows that





 $q_{d}^{(a)}(n)\sim C(d,a)n^{-\frac{3}{4}}e^{2\sqrt{An}},$ 



where





 $\displaystyle C(d,a)$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}A^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(\alpha^{d+1-2a}\cdot(d(\alpha)^{d-1}+1)\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}},$ 



(6.1)

 $\displaystyle A$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\frac{d}{2}\log^{2}\alpha+\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)^{rd}}{r^{2}},$ 



and  $\alpha\in[0,1]$  is the positive real number such that  $\alpha^{d}+\alpha-1=0$ . We thus observe that




(6.2)

 $\log{q_{d}^{(a)}(n)}\sim\log{\left(C(d,a)n^{-\frac{3}{4}}\right)}+2\sqrt{An}\sim 2\sqrt{An},$ 



where by work of Andrews [4, proof of Theorem 2]





 $A>\frac{\pi^{2}}{3d+9}.$ 






Furthermore, from Andrews [6, Example 1, pg. 97] we have




(6.3)

 $Q_{d}^{(a)}(n)\sim\frac{\csc(\frac{\pi a}{d+3})}{(4\pi)3^{\frac{1}{4}}(d+3)^{\frac{1}{4}}}n^{-\frac{3}{4}}e^{2\pi\sqrt{\frac{n}{3d+9}}},$ 



for  $a<\frac{d+3}{2}$  such that  $a$  is relatively prime to  $d+3$ . This gives that





 $\log{Q_{d}^{(a)}(n)}\sim\log{\left(\frac{\csc(\frac{\pi a}{d+3})}{(4\pi)3^{\frac{1}{4}}(d+3)^{\frac{1}{4}}}n^{-\frac{3}{4}}\right)}+2\pi\sqrt{\frac{n}{3d+9}}\sim 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{n}{3d+9}}.$ 






Together, (6.2) and (6.3) imply that





 $\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\log{q_{d}^{(a)}(n)}-\log{Q_{d}^{(a)}(n)}\right)=+\infty.$ 



Thus,





 $\lim_{n\to\infty}\Delta_{d}^{(a)}(n)=\lim_{n\to\infty}q_{d}^{(a)}(n)\left(1-\frac{Q_{d}^{(a)}(n)}{q_{d}^{(a)}(n)}\right)=+\infty,$ 



as desired.
∎





The asymptotic formulas for  $q_{d}^{(a)}(n)$  and  $Q_{d}^{(a)}(n)$  given by Meinardus [9, 10] and Andrews [6], while allowing us to examine the asymptotic behavior of  $\Delta_{d}^{(a)}(n)$ , can be made more explicit by following methods of Alfes, Jameson, and Lemke Oliver [3].




First we state a theorem of Xia [11].



Theorem 6.1 (Xia [11], 2011).



Let  $d\geq 1$ , and  $1\leq a<\frac{d+3}{2}$ , such that  $\gcd(a,d+3)=1$ . Then for  $n\geq 1$ ,





 $Q_{d}^{(1)}\left(n\right)\geq Q_{d}^{(a)}\left(n\right).$ 







We see that under the hypotheses of Theorem 6.1,  $Q_{d}^{(1)}\left(n\right)$  bounds  $Q_{d}^{(a)}(n)$  from above, thus the asymptotic expression for  $Q_{d}^{(1)}\left(n\right)$  obtained by Alfes, Jameson, and Lemke Oliver [3, Theorem 2.1]) is a bound for  $Q_{d}^{(a)}(n)$ . We thus immediately obtain the following.
If  $d\geq 4$ ,  $1\leq a<\frac{d+3}{2}$  such that  $\gcd(a,d+3)=1$ , and  $n\geq 1$ , then




(6.4)

 $Q_{d}^{(a)}(n)\leq\frac{(3d+9)^{-\frac{1}{4}}}{4\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{d+3}\right)}n^{-\frac{3}{4}}\exp\left({n^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{3(d+3)}}}\right)+R(n),$ 



where  $R(n)$  is an explicitly bounded function given in Alfes, et al [3, equation  $(2.10)$ ].




Making only minor modifications to the analysis done by Alfes, et al [3, Proof of Theorem 3.1], we also get the following asymptotic expression.
Let  $A$  and  $\alpha$  be defined as in (6.1). Then for  $n\geq 1$ ,




(6.5)

 $q_{d}^{(a)}(n)=\frac{A^{1/4}}{2\sqrt{\pi\alpha^{d+1-2a}(d\alpha^{d-1}+1)}}n^{-3/4}e^{2\sqrt{An}}+r_{d}(n),$ 



where  $|r_{d}(n)|$  can be bounded explicitly.




6.1. Potential methods for proving remaining cases of Conjecture 1.2




Theorem 1.3 resolves Conjecture 1.2 for  $d\geq 62$ . Furthermore, when  $d\leq 61$ , Kang and Park [7, Theorem 1.3] have resolved the cases when  $d=2$  and  $d=30$  for even  $n$ . Moreover, the cases when  $d\leq 61$  is odd and  $n$  is odd is trivial, as discussed in the proof of Theorem 1.3.




It may be possible to use the method of Alfes, et al [3] to prove some or all of the remaining finite cases by computation. One idea for even  $4\leq d\leq 60$ , is to utilize the asymptotic expressions for  $q_{d}^{(2)}\left(n\right)$  and  $Q_{d}^{(2)}\left(n\right)$  from (6.4) and (6.5) respectively, to calculate the smallest  $n_{d}$ , for fixed  $d$ , for which these bounds imply  $\Delta_{d}^{(2)}\left(n\right)\geq 0$  for all  $n\geq n_{d}$ . By (1.3), this would also imply  $\Delta_{d}^{(2,-)}\left(n\right)\geq 0$  for all  $n\geq n_{d}$ . Then one could conceivably check that  $\Delta_{d}^{(2,-)}\left(n\right)\geq 0$  for all smaller  $n$ .




Alternatively, one could utilize the asymptotic bounds for  $q_{d}^{(1)}\left(n\right)$  and  $Q_{d-2}^{(1)}\left(n\right)$  given by Alfes, et al [3], and using the same procedure outlined above, potentially prove the missing cases of Proposition 3.1. Then utilizing the method in the proof of Theorem 1.3 this would yield the remaining cases of Conjecture 1.2. It is worth noting that this procedure has its own computational challenges.






7. Discussion of the exclusion of parts




When considering the values of  $\Delta_{d}^{(a)}(n)$  for  $a\geq 2$  there are many examples where  $\Delta_{d}^{(a)}(n)<0$ . Kang and Park [7] remove the part  $d+3-a$  from consideration when defining  $Q_{d}^{(2,-)}\left(n\right)$  to generalize Alder’s conjecture to a statement which generalizes the second Rogers-Ramanujan identity. We remove both  $a$  and  $d+3-a$  when defining  $Q_{d}^{(a,-,-)}\left(n\right)$  in order to generalize Conjecture 1.2 for arbitrary  $a$ . While the removal of these terms may appear unmotivated, we argue that it is natural. One way to consider this is to determine values of  $q_{d}^{(a)}(n)$  and  $Q_{d}^{(a)}(n)$  when  $\Delta_{d}^{(a)}(n)$  is negative. In the following we provide some examples.



Example 7.1.



For  $a\geq 4$  and  $k\geq 0$ , we have





 $\Delta_{a+k-2}^{(a)}(2a+k+1)<0.$ 







Example 7.1 follows from observing that  $q_{a+k-2}^{(a)}(2a+k+1)=1$  since  $(2a+k+1)$  is the only partition counted, and that  $Q_{a+k-2}^{(a)}(2a+k+1)\geq 2$  since the partitions  $(2a+k+1)$  and  $(a^{2},k+1)$  are both counted by  $Q_{a+k-2}^{(a)}(2a+k+1)$ .



Example 7.2.



We have that,





 $\displaystyle\Delta_{3a-3}^{(2a,2a,-)}\left(4a\right)$ 
 $\displaystyle=-1\text{ for }a\geq 2,$ 




 $\displaystyle\Delta_{3a-3}^{(2a,2a,-)}\left(6a\right)$ 
 $\displaystyle=-1\text{ for }a\geq 4,$ 




 $\displaystyle\Delta_{5a-3}^{(2a,2a,-)}\left(8a\right)$ 
 $\displaystyle=-1\text{ for }a\geq 4,$ 




 $\displaystyle\Delta_{4a-3}^{(3a,3a,-)}\left(9a\right)$ 
 $\displaystyle=-1\text{ for }a\geq 4,$ 




 $\displaystyle\Delta_{5a-3}^{(4a,4a,-)}\left(12a\right)$ 
 $\displaystyle=-1\text{ for }a\geq 4.$ 







The first line in Example 7.2 follows from observing that  $q_{3a-3}^{(2a)}(4a)=1$ , and that  $Q_{3a-3}^{(2a,-)}(4a)=2$  since it counts the partitions  $(4a)$  and  $((2a)^{2})$ . The other four follow in the same manner.




By examining the cases in Examples 7.1 and 7.2, we find that the parts which yield more partitions for  $Q_{d}^{(a)}(n)$  than  $q_{d}^{(a)}(n)$  are precisely the parts equal to  $d+3-a$  and  $a$ . Roughly speaking, when  $n$  is less than  $2a+d$ ,  $q_{d}^{(a)}(n)$  is bounded at  $1$ . However the  $a$  and  $d+3-a$  parts are small enough that they can contribute to partitions of  $n$  counted by  $Q_{d}^{(a)}(n)$ , especially when  $n$  is a multiple of  $a$ . The removal of these parts for large  $n$  is unnecessary to maintain the nonnegativity of  $\Delta_{d}^{(a)}(n)$ , which is further evidenced by the proof of Theorem 1.9. A further justification for removing these parts is that we observe computationally that  $\Delta_{d}^{(a,-,-)}(n)\geq 0$  for  $a\geq 3$ .
