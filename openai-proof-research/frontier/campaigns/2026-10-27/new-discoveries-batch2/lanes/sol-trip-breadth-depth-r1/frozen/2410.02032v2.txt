                                                             Continued Fraction Algorithms

                                                                                                       Abstract
                                                      We study the complexity of S-adic sequences corresponding to a family of 216 multidimensional continued
                                                  fractions maps, called Triangle Partition maps (TRIP maps), with an emphasis on those with low upper bounds
                                                  on complexity. Our main result is to prove that the complexity of S-adic sequences corresponding to the triangle
                                                  map (called the (e, e, e)-TRIP map in this paper) has upper bound at most 3n. Our second main result is to
                                                  prove an upper bound of 2n + 1 on complexity for another TRIP map. We discuss a dynamical phenomenon,




arXiv:2410.02032v2 [math.DS] 21 Jul 2026
                                                  which we call “hidden R2 behavior,” that occurs in this map and its relationship to complexity. Combining this
                                                  with previously known results and a list of counter-examples, we provide a complete list of the TRIP maps which
                                                  have upper bounds on complexity of at most 3n, except for one remaining case for which we conjecture such an
                                                  upper bound to hold.


                                           1      Introduction
                                           1.1      Overview
                                           There is a remarkable correspondence between inﬁnite words with factor complexity n+ 1 (the number of subwords of
                                           length n is exactly n + 1) and continued fraction expansions of irrational numbers. (See Coven and Hedlund [19] and
                                           also the work of Morse and Hedlund from the early 1940s [35, 36]. For a good expository introduction, see Chapter
                                           19 in Goodson’s textbook [29].) Given an irrational number a ∈ (0, 1) \ Q, we can produce an inﬁnite word on two
                                           letters with letter frequencies (a, 1 − a) by associating the continued fraction expansion to a sequence of substitutions
                                           (for background on this see Chapter 6 in [24] or in [40]). It is well-known that such inﬁnite words are Sturmian
                                           (meaning that their factor compexity is n + 1, the lowest possible complexity for aperiodic inﬁnite binary strings, as
                                           seen in the above references).
                                               Over the years, there have been many generalizations of continued fractions, often called multidimensional contin-
                                           ued fractions. Such generalizations often arise from the desire to ﬁnd best simultaneous Diophantine approximations
                                           or to solve the Hermite problem, which asks for methods of writing a real number α as a sequence of integers so that
                                           the sequence is eventually periodic if and only if α is a cubic irrational. Just as continued fractions can be interpreted
                                           as a dynamical system of the Gauss map on the interval, multidimensional continued fractions can be interpreted as
                                           dynamical systems on a higher-dimensional simplex.
                                               For higher-dimensional continued fractions, there is a straightforward technique mimicking the classical case which
                                           produces sequences on larger alphabets encoding orbits of the dynamical system. It is natural to wonder about the
                                           typical complexities of these sequences, whether there is any general framework for understanding them, and whether
                                           the correspondence between Sturmian words and words of low complexity can be generalized.
                                               Arnoux-Rauzy words were among the ﬁrst such sequences studied; these have factor complexity 2n + 1 and
                                           correspond to the application of the Arnoux-Rauzy multidimensional continued fraction algorithm in a set of Lebesgue
                                           measure zero, the so-called Rauzy Gasket [5]. Additionally, in [12] it was shown that generically the algorithm
                                           produces sequences of complexity less than or equal to 25 n + 1. Later, Cassaigne, Labbé and Leroy [17] showed
                                           that Cassaigne’s multidimensional continued fraction algorithm produces words of factor complexity 2n + 1 almost
                                           everywhere. Given these two examples, it might be reasonable to wonder what the “generic” complexity is for
                                           continued fraction algorithms.
                                               All of these words belong to a more general class of words called S-adic sequences, which are derived via a ﬁnite
                                           family of substitutions. It is known that any uniformly recurrent inﬁnite word with at most linear factor complexity

conditions can be placed on S-adic sequences to ensure at most linear complexity. (For remarkable progress on this
conjecture, see the work of Espinoza [22].)
    There is a framework (the triangle partition family, or TRIP family for short) that captures almost all known
multidimensional continued fraction algorithms. This framework is generated by 216 diﬀerent multidimensional
continued fraction algorithms, indexed by a triple of permutations in S3 × S3 × S3 . Though [17] did not use the
rhetoric of TRIP maps, the Cassaigne algorithm is in this list (it is the TRIP map (e, 23, 23)) [20].
    Our goal is to understand the complexity for all of the triangle partition maps with an emphasis on those with
low upper bounds. Our main technical result is that the triangle map (called the (e, e, e)-TRIP map in this rhetoric)
has linear complexity bounded between 2n + 1 and 3n. While other work on this subject has often focused on other
properties of such sequences such as balance (as seen for example in [17, 18]), we do not address these topics here.
Also, while the Cassaigne map was created in large part to ﬁnd a multi-dimensional continued fraction with low
linear complexity, the triangle map has independent interest, as reviewed in the beginning of Section 6.
    We review the basics of multidimensional continued fractions in Section 2 and the basics of S-adic sequences
and factor complexity in Section 3. We discuss the link between multidimensional continued fractions and S-adic
sequences in Section 4. In Section 5, we review the common techniques used to determine factor complexity of S-adic
sequences, which we make use of throughout the rest of the paper.
    The new work begins in Section 6. This will be the longest and most technical section of this paper, as we give
the full proof that the linear complexity for the S-adic sequences corresponding to the (e, e, e)-TRIP map has lower
bound 2n + 1 and upper bound 3n.
    In Section 7 we return to a discussion of more general TRIP maps and reduce the number of diﬀerent TRIP maps
and classes of S-adic sequences to consider from 216 to 21 through equivalence relations.
    In Section 8, we give an overview of each of these 21 classes, specifying those for which are known or for which we
conjecture to have an upper bound on complexity of at most 3n and providing counterexamples for the others. Three
of them are what we call degenerate TRIP maps as they can be reduced to maps in R2 , and their corresponding
S-adic sequences are Sturmian sequences. We ﬁnd through computational experiments that two others are candidates
for an upper bound of 2n + 1 and two are candidates for an upper bound of 3n (one of which is the triangle map).
    In Section 9, we discuss a new phenomenon for some multidimensional continued fraction algorithms, which we
call hidden R2 maps, whereby they can be reduced to continued fraction maps in two dimensions on a positively
invariant subset, with respect to Lebesgue measure, of △ but not on all of △. We discuss the (e, 13, e) TRIP map as
an example of this and show how the complexity of its corresponding S-adic sequences relate to this phenomenon,
with a full proof that the complexity has upper bound 2n + 1.
    In Section 10, we explore the other candidate for an upper bound of 3n, the (e, 23, e) TRIP map, and give an
outline of a possible proof for this upper bound similar to that in Section 6.
    We close with some natural questions in Section 11.

1.2    Terse Summary
In Section 2, we deﬁne the 216 diﬀerent R3 Triangle Partition Maps, which provide a framework to understand

3     S-adic Words and Complexity
We consider sequences or inﬁnite words u = u0 u1 u2 . . ., where each ui is from some alphabet A. A ﬁnite word
over A is a sequence of ﬁnitely many characters w = w0 w1 . . . wn−1 for some positive interger n. Such n is called the
length of w, denoted |w|. We denote the empty word, or the word of length 0, by ε. We denote the set of all ﬁnite
words over A by A∗ and the set of all inﬁnite words by AN . For ﬁnite words v = v0 v1 · · · vn−1 and w = w0 w1 · · · wm−1
we deﬁne the concatenation of v and w to be vw := v0 v1 · · · vn−1 w0 w1 · · · wm−1 . Concatenation of ﬁnite words is
associative but not commutative.
   The following deﬁnitions are adopted from Berthé and Labbé [11], Berthé and Rigo [13], and Fogg [24]:
Deﬁnition 2. A factor or subword of an inﬁnite word u is a ﬁnite word w that appears exactly as some sequence
of consecutive characters in u. Speciﬁcally, this means that if u = a0 a1 a2 · · · and |w| = n, then w is a factor if for
some non-negative integer m, we have w = am am+1 · · · am+n−1 . Factors of a ﬁnite word are deﬁned similarly.
Deﬁnition 3. A language is a subset L ⊆ A∗ that is factorially closed, that is, if w ∈ L and v is a factor of w,
then v ∈ L. We say that a language L is left extendable (respectively right extendable) if for all w ∈ L, there is
some letter a ∈ A such that aw ∈ L (respectively wa ∈ L). If L is both left extendable and right extendable, then we
say L is extendable.
    Note that our deﬁnition of a language and extendability diﬀers slightly from that in Berthé and Rigo [13].
Deﬁnition 4. The factor complexity of a language L is the function pL that maps each positive integer n to the
number of distinct words of length n contained in L.
    While there many diﬀerent types of complexity, overwhelmingly in this paper we when we say “complexity,” we
will mean factor complexity.
Deﬁnition 5. A preﬁx of a ﬁnite or inﬁnite word w is a word a that appears at the beginning of w, that is, w = av
for some ﬁnite or inﬁnite word v. Similarly, a suﬃx of a ﬁnite word w is a word b that appears at the end of w,
that is, w = vb for some ﬁnite word v. We call a preﬁx or suﬃx of a word w that is not equal to w a proper preﬁx
or proper suﬃx, respectively.
Deﬁnition 6. An inﬁnite word u is recurrent if all of its factors occur inﬁnitely often in u. If for any factor w of
u there exists N such that every subword of length N in u contains w as a factor, then u is uniformly recurrent.
Deﬁnition 7. The language of an inﬁnite word u, denoted L(u), is the set of all factors of u. The complexity
function of u, denoted pu , p, pL(u) , or even pL , is simply the complexity of L(u), so that pu (n) is the number of
distinct factors of u of length n.
   For example, for the word u = 123123123 . . ., the set of factors of length 3 is {123, 231, 312}, so pu (3) = 3.

    • S0 :   1 7→ 1,    2 7→ 21,

    The S-adic conjecture asks to what extent this can be generalized to a relationship between S-adic words and

showed that u itself is S-adic. However, there exists S-adic words with only two diﬀerent substitutions that have
superlinear complexity (see, for example, [24]). (For recent work on the converse, see Espinoza [22].)
    As mentioned in the introduction, recent work has explored classes of S-adic words on three letters with low
complexity. For example, classes of such inﬁnite words with upper bounds 2n + 1 were found by Arnoux and Rauzy
[5] and by Cassaigne, Labbé, and Leroy [17], and a class of such inﬁnite words u with 2n + 1 ≤ pL (n) ≤ 52 n was
found by Berthé and Labbé [11].

6     Factor Complexity for (e, e, e) TRIP map: The Triangle Map
We are now done with the preliminaries. In this section, we prove the main technical result of this paper:

Theorem 2. The complexity function of any S(e, e, e)-adic language L with rationally independent frequency vectors
satisﬁes 2n + 1 ≤ pL (n) ≤ 3n for all n ≥ 1.
    The (e, e, e) TRIP map has independent interest. It is usually called the triangle map and was originally created

Bonanno and Del Vigna [15] recently used the R3 slow triangle map to develop a tree structure of rational pairs in
the plane. In a recent preprint, Ito [30] showed that the fast map is self-dual (in section three of that paper).
    The upper bound is what is new. The lower bound stems from quite general principles, as shown by R. Tijdeman
[41]. Also see the more recent work of Andrieu and Vivion [4]
    Finally, the (e, e, e) map (the triangle map) and its higher dimensional analog seems to be the only multidimen-

conjugation for partitions, as is discussed in the recent papers [8, 9]

6.8    Characterizing Extended Images of Bispecial Words
First to recap the notation and the goals. We have an S-adic word u described via a Gauss coding sequence
{Gk0 , Gk1 , Gk2 , . . .}. Our language L is all subwords of u. We need to understand all of the bispecial factors in u.
We do this in part by looking at the bispecial factors in L1 , which is the language of the Gauss coding sequence of
{Gk1 , Gk2 , Gk3 , . . .}, etc.
   In this subsection, we aim to characterize all possible extended images w ∈ L of a bispecial word v ∈ L(1) based
on its extension set E(v) and the value of k0 .
Proposition 12. Suppose v ∈ L(1) has age at least 1. Then, for all extended images w = aGk0 (v)b:
   • If E − (v) = {1, 3}, then a = ε and E − (w) = {2, 3}.

7     Equivalence Classes of TRIP Maps
The work in ﬁnding the complexity bounds for the T (e, e, e) TRIP map was detailed and technical. It is hard for
us to imagine that such work would be worthwhile to imitate for the other 215 TRIP maps. However, following the
procedure in Section 8.2 of [27], certain TRIP maps are equivalent via transformations which preserve the complexities
of the associated S-adic sequences, reducing the 216 TRIP maps to 21 equivalence classes.

7.1     Reduction to 36 Maps through Conjugacy
Each permutation ρ ∈ S 3 is a bijection on A, so given an inﬁnite sequence u = u0 u1 u2 . . . in A, we may apply ρ as
a substitution to yield
                                                 ρ(u) := ρ(u0 )ρ(u1 )ρ(u2 ) · · · ,
and similarly for ﬁnite words. For any language L, we can deﬁne the language ρ(L) := {ρ(w) : w ∈ L}, whose
complexity will be identical to that of L. This motivates the following proposition:
Proposition 18. If L is an S(ρ, τ0 , τ1 )-adic language with coding sequence {Sin (ρ, τ0 , τ1 )}, then ρ(L) is S(e, τ0 ρ, τ1 ρ)-

that are possible in order for Proposition 28 to be possible. In this case, we rely heavily on the fact that the character
3 always occurs as the last character and nowhere else in a double Gauss substitution of a single character.
    If particular, an analog of Proposition 7 for de-substitutions under a single Gauss substitution does not hold. To
see this, consider the case where jm = 0 and km ≥ 1 for all m. Then, the above results imply that any bispecial w ∈ L
containing the character 3 must have exactly two left extensions and two right extensions. However, the factorial
closure L′ of G1 (L) will have a coding sequence jm ≥ 0 and km = 0 for all m, so L′ contains bispecial factors of any
age with three left extensions. This cannot happen if a statement analogous to (16) holds.

11     Conclusion and Future Directions
Factor complexity is of course only one of many ways to understand S-adic sequences. Others are seen, for example,
in the recent survey by Thuswaldner [40]. The extent to which other properties, for example, balance, hold for TRIP
maps is mostly unknown. There are many diﬀerent multi-dimensional continued fraction algorithms that are not in
the family of 216 TRIP maps. As shown in [20], almost all of them are combination triangle partition maps, and
hence various combinations of the maps considered in this paper. How to take known complexity bounds for, say, two
multidimensional continued fraction algorithms and then determine the complexity bounds for some combination of
the two would be important to know. Possibly the recent techniques developed by Berthé, Steiner and Thuswaldner
[14] could be used.
