                                                 Open problems on Steiner trees and maximal distance minimizers
                                                                                             Yana Teplitskaya
                                                  Laboratoire de Mathématiques d’Orsay, Université Paris-Saclay, CNRS, Orsay, France

                                                                                                    Abstract
                                              In this work, I collect and discuss a series of open questions in one-dimensional geometric optimization in Euclidean
                                           spaces. The focus is on two classes of problems: maximal distance minimizers and Steiner trees. Maximal distance
                                           minimizers concern finding a connected set of minimal length whose closed r-neighborhood covers a given compact set,




arXiv:2511.18217v1 [math.MG] 22 Nov 2025
                                           whereas Steiner trees aim to find a minimal-length set connecting a prescribed set of points. For both problems, I briefly
                                           summarize known results and highlight the remaining open questions. While some questions can be approached with
                                           elementary methods, others remain highly challenging.


                                           1     Maximal distance minimizers
                                           For a given compact set M ⊂ Rd and a given positive number r > 0, consider the problem of finding a connected compact
                                           set Σ of minimal length (one-dimensional Hausdorff measure H1 ) such that

                                                                                                   M ⊂ Br (Σ),

                                           where Br (Σ) denotes the closed r-neighborhood of the set Σ. We refer to such a set Σ as a Maximal distance minimizer
                                           (MDM or r-minimizer). A solution always exists and has no loops (homeomorphic image of a circle), see [24]. For further
                                           details on MDMs, the reader is referred to our survey [9].

                                           1.1     Explicit examples
                                           Question 1.1. What is a maximal distance minimizer for M = ∂BR with R > r? The problem remains open for 4.98r >
                                           R > r.
                                              Miranda, Paolini and Stepanov conjectured in [22] that any minimizer for a circle of radius R > r is a horseshoe id est
                                           a union of an arc of a circle of radius R − r and two segments of its tangent lines.
                                              The conjecture is confirmed in [7] for R > 4.98r. For smaller values of 4.98r > R > r the hypothesis remains unproven.
                                           Question 1.2. What is the set of maximal distance minimizers for R-stadium (which is the boundary of R-neighborhood
                                           of a segment)? Open for r < R < 5r.
                                               In [7] it is proved that for a closed curve with minimal radius of curvature R > 5r any MDM is a horseshoe, that is a
                                           union of an arc of a curve parallel to an arc of M (and hence having a radius of curvature at least R − r) and two segments
                                           of its tangent lines. Thus, for R > 5r, every solution for R-stadium is a horseshoe. It is also known (see [9]) that for
                                           R < 1.75r the solution is not a horseshoe.
                                               Thus the following sub-questions remain open:
                                               • To find the supremum of C such that there exists MDM for Cr-neighborhood which is not horseshoe.
                                               • To find the infimum of c such that for any closed curve with minimal radius of curvature cr every MDM is horseshoe.

Question 1.3. What is the set of MDMs for any explicit set you like?
    For example for
    • A disc or ball BR in Rd , d ≥ 2. Very challenging question, nothing is known except the usual regularity properties
      such as absence of loops.
    • Rectangle (the boundary). For a small enough r > 0 the question is answered, see [5].
    • (Regular) n-gons. Looks like for n ≥ 6 MDM for regular n-gon will be just a poly-line. Although this question seems
      less difficult than finding minimizers for other sets, no one has written a clean proof yet. By the regularity properties
      it is clear that the solution is a finite union of line segments with angles at least 2π/3. The question is interesting
      already for n = 3. Our methods for rectangle might help, see [5].

1.2     Regularity and uniqueness
Question 1.4. Is any MDM in Rd a union of a finite number of injective curves? Open for d ≥ 3.
   In R2 the answer is positive, see [18]. In higher dimensions some information about local behavior is proved in the same
paper: there are at most three tangent rays at any point of minimizers and the angles between the tangent rays are at least
2π/3. Recall that a solution always exists and never contains loops, see [24].
Question 1.5. Let Σ be an r-minimizer for some M . Is Σ the unique r-minimizer for Br (Σ)?
     Clearly that if Σ is an r-minimizer for some M , then it is a minimizer for Br (Σ), see [2]. A weaker form of this question
is if we replace r with some positive r0 < r in the hypothesis.
     Some points of an MDM are more important than others: we call a point x ∈ Σ energetic if there exists a point y ∈ M
such that |xy| = r and Br (y) ∩ Σ = ∅. We denote by GΣ the set of energetic points of Σ.
Question 1.6. Fix M ⊂ Rd and r > 0. Does the set GΣ of energetic points determine a minimizer Σ?
    The connection between Questions 1.5 and 1.6 is a nice question itself.

1.3     Algebraic point of view
Reflections from a spherical mirror play an important role in geometry of MDMs. In particular MDM for a 3-point set M
often coincides with a solution to the Alhazen’s billiard problem, which is actually reduced to solving a certain fourth-degree
equation and thus cannot be solved by a straightedge (ruler) and a compass, see [15]. The following question is open even
for n = 4.
Question 1.7. Let M ⊂ R2 be a finite set of cardinality n. How does the explicit description of a minimizer Σ depend on
M in terms of the degrees of the resulting equations?
   For simplicity one may assume that the points from M have rational coordinates. Then it is enough to find the largest
degree of the extension of Q generated by the set of energetic points GΣ .


2      Steiner trees
For a given compact set A ⊂ Rd find a closed set St with minimal length (one-dimensional Hausdorff measure H1 ) such
that St ∪ A is connected. We will call such a solution St Steiner set. If St is connected (which is the case if A is finite or
totally disconnected) we call it Steiner tree. In 2013, Paolini and Stepanov showed the existence of a solution, see [25]. If a
solution St has finite length, then it is compact, has no loops, and consists of countably many connected components. The
edges of the locally finite graph St \ A are straight line segments. The maximal degree (in graph-theoretic sense) of a vertex
is at most 3. Moreover, only terminals can have degree 1 or 2, all the other vertices have degree 3 and are called Steiner


                                                                 2
(branching) points. The angle between any two adjacent edges of a St is at least 2π/3, in particular St in a neighborhood
of a branching point is a regular tripod (all three angles are equal to 2π/3) and hence are coplanar. If every terminal has
degree 1 then St is called full.

2.1    Uniqueness and existence
Question 2.1. Does there exist a finite set of terminals in Rd , d ≥ 3 with two different Steiner trees St1 and St2 such
that the directions of all segments incident to terminals are the same (in other words trees coincide in a neighborhood of
terminals)?
   In the plane K. Oblakov has proved that it is impossible [23].
Question 2.2. Is a solution to the Steiner tree problem unique for a general input?
    We [1] provided the positive answer for finite planar input. Moreover we proved that in R2 the set of n-point ambiguous
configurations has the Hausdorff dimension at most 2n − 1 (where a configuration is called ambiguous if there are several
Steiner trees for it). This particularly means that n-dimensional Hausdorff measure of ambiguous n-point configurations is
0, id est a general planar n-input has a unique Steiner tree.
Question 2.3. Is it possible to find a bounded set A ⊂ Rd such that the problem: “find S such that H1 (S) is minimal
among all sets for which S ∪ A is connected” does not have a solution?
   This question was asked by E. Paolini, who is interested whether the assumption that A ⊂ Rd is closed is necessary for
the problem to be solvable.

2.2    Gilbert–Pollak conjecture on the Steiner ratio
A Minimum spanning tree (MST) is a minimal connection of a given set A of points in the plane by segments with endpoints
in A. The Steiner ratio is the infimum of the ratio of the total length of Steiner tree to the minimum spanning tree for a
finite set of points in the Euclidean plane.
Question 2.4. What is the Steiner ratio in Rd , d ≥ 2?
    This is the most well-known open problem on Steiner tree, so called Gilbert–Pollak conjecture

    The ratio for the vertices of a regular simplex, including the tetrahedron, is better than the planar value, but it is not
optimal. D. Smith and J. MacGregor Smith constructed in [29] so called d-sausage configurations which provide the best
known and conjecturally optimal value of the Steiner ratio in Rd , d ≥ 2. For d = 3 this value is approximately 0.784.

2.4     Connection with maximal distance minimizers
A natural way to investigate the connection is to consider the case of a finite set M in maximal distance minimizers problem.
A minimizer for a finite set M ⊂ Rd is a finite union of at most 2♯M − 3 segments. In this case the maximal distance
minimizers problem comes down to connecting r-neighborhoods of all the points from M .
    A topology T of a labeled Steiner tree St is the corresponding abstract graph with labeled terminals and unlabeled Steiner
points. We showed that if a St for n terminals in Rd preserves the topology for an arbitrary small enough perturbation of
the terminals, then St is an r-minimizer for some n-tuple M and a small enough r, see [2]. Recall that in the plane a Steiner
tree for a random input is unique with unit probability. Also in the plane a somehow opposite statement holds: if St is a
full planar Steiner tree for n terminals which is not unique, then St cannot be a minimizer for any M being an n-tuple of
points.
    To illustrate this statement consider a square a1 a2 a3 a4 . There are two Steiner trees for a1 , a2 , a3 , a4 (see the left-hand
side of Fig. 4), let us pick the solid one. The right-hand side of Fig. 4 shows that an r-minimizer for every positive r has
the topology of the dotted Steiner tree.
    Thereby the picture is clear for a full Steiner tree for planar finite input. All possible generalizations remain open.
Question 2.8. Let A ⊂ Rd be a compact totally disconnected set with at least two Steiner trees, say S and T . Can S be a
solution to the maximal distance minimizers problem?
    The partial results are known in the case when d = 2: if S or T is full then the answer is negative: a finite A is covered
in [2], and an infinite A contradicts decomposition of a maximal distance minimizers into a finite number of simple curves,
see [18].

2.6     Nonplanar explicit examples
Recall that Steiner trees in a Euclidean d-dimensional space with d ≥ 3 have the same local structure as in the plane, id est
every branching point has degree 3 and moreover three adjacent segments have pairwise angles 2π/3 and thus are coplanar.
However, a global structure is much more complicated. In particular, there is no explicit algorithm which may be caused
by the fact that Steiner points are no longer expressed in radicals [28].
    To the best of our knowledge all known series of explicit solutions are planar. Even for the vertices of regular simplices [13]
in high dimensions the Steiner trees remain unknown.
    Thus almost any questions in high dimensions remain open. In particular, I would like to generalize the examples from
our paper [6]:
Question 2.13. For a regular 3-dimensional n-gon in the plane x = 1 centered at (1, 0, 0); let Q be the set of its vertices
and consider the union of all f k (Q), k ∈ N, where f is a homothety with the center in the origin and a small enough scale
factor λ. What is the structure of the corresponding Steiner tree?
   Also the generalization of universal Steiner tree (for planar ones see [26], [8]) is quite interesting.
Question 2.14. Can one construct a non-planar example of a unique full Steiner tree with infinitely many branching
points?
   Work is underway on both issues mentioned above, and some progress has been achieved.
