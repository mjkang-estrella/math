         Abstract. Combinatorics is a thriving branch of Mathematics with strong
         links to other areas of Mathematics and the sciences. Many of the modern
         techniques employed in Combinatorics draw on diverse ideas from Probabil-
         ity, Analysis, Geometry, Topology, and conversely, combinatorial approaches
         often lead to new insights in these other areas, and in the broader scientiﬁc
         arena (particularly Theoretical Computer Science). The most recent years in
         Combinatorics have been particularly exciting with spectacular solutions of
         many longstanding open problems, via an array of new ideas that promise
         to have many future applications. Leading experts and new talents came
         together to share and further develop these exciting ideas. Talks emphasized
         recent breakthroughs in discrete geometry, graph theory, combinatorial appli-
         cations in Fourier analysis, group theory, combinatorial probability, Ramsey
         theory, nonabelian additive combinatorics, and design theory.

                        Introduction by the Organizers

The most recent edition of the Oberwolfach Combinatorics workshop was held in
the ﬁrst week of January 2026, organised by Jacob Fox (Stanford), Peter Keevash
(Oxford) and Wojciech Samotij (Tel Aviv). The participants were drawn from the
leading researchers in the ﬁeld worldwide, including mathematicians from several
European countries, Israel, the United Kingdom, Canada and the United States.
The program of the workshop comprised twelve 50-minute-long plenary lectures
and thirteen shorter, 25-minute talks. To provide a platform for early career
researchers to introduce themselves to the international community, we made a

particular eﬀort to encourage them to oﬀer talks and accommodate them in the
schedule of the meeting. A lively, one-hour-long problem session, led by Nati
Linial (Jerusalem) was held on Tuesday evening. As always, the daily collaborative
discussions between various groups of attendees were evident and testament to the
success of this series of meetings in advancing the ﬁeld.
   This report contains extended abstracts of all the lectures given at the meeting
as well as a summary of the problems presented at the open problem session. The
lectures covered a diverse range of topics in combinatorics, including extremal
combinatorics, probabilistic combinatorics and structural graph theory, and also
its interactions with other areas of mathematics, particularly analysis, geometry
and group theory. As ever, the calibre of the results presented and the quality
of the exposition in the lectures was ﬁrst-rate, so this report can only give an
overall impression of the signiﬁcance of the mathematical achievements presented
by our colleagues in this workshop. We will now brieﬂy highlight three of the most
spectacular achievements that were communicated during the workshop.
   The ﬁrst highlight was the joint work of Zach Hunter, Matthew Kwan, and Lisa
Sauermann on anti-concentration properties of the permanent of a random matrix.
The motivation for this work comes from the recent spectacular developments on
the problem of estimating the singularity probability of the uniformly random
n × n matrix An with ±1 entries, which has been investigated since the 1960s. A
series of papers on this problem that spanned several decades culminated in the
recent work of Tikhomirov, who proved the optimal estimate on the logarithm of
this probability, which one may write succinctly as Pr(det(An ) = 0) = (1/2 +
o(1))n . It is reasonable to expect that a similar, exponential in n, upper bound
on the probability that the permanent of An equals zero (or any other value)
holds as well. However, all known proofs of upper bounds on the singularity
probability fundamentally relied on linear-algebraic properties of the determinant,
and thus cannot be extended to the superﬁcially-similar permanent. As a result,
the state-of-the-art on this problem was the comparatively much weaker estimate
Pr(per(An ) = 0) ≤ n−c , valid for suﬃciently small c > 0, proved by Tao and Vu
in 2009. In the ﬁrst improvement of this ﬁfteen-year old result, Hunter, Kwan,
and Sauermann immediately establish the exponential bound

                         sup Pr(per(An ) = x) ≤ exp(−cn)
                          x

for some c > 0, a major breakthrough! Further, their methods are suﬃciently
robust to yield analogous bounds not only on permanents of random matrices
with independent entries but can also to recover the known estimates on the anti-
concentration properties of the determinant. The only assumption that is required
on the distribution Q of the entires of the random matrix is the very mild anti-
concentration bound supx∈R Q(x) ≤ p for some ﬁxed p < 1.
   The second highlight of the workshop was the joint work of Richard Montgomery
and Lisa Sauermann establishing an asymptotic form of Rota’s Basis Conjecture.
This attractive conjecture, posed by Rota in 1989, states that any family of n bases
in a matroid of rank n can be rearranged into a family n bases that is transverse
Combinatorics                                                                       7

to the original family. Besides being a natural statement, its generality provides a
potential uniﬁcation of two strands of research that have a large literature in their
own right, obtained by specialising matroids to (a) real-representable matroids
or (b) graphic matroids. The two main results presented in the lecture by Lisa
Sauermann were an asymptotic packing theorem, which ﬁnds n − o(n) transversal
bases in the original family, an an asymptotic covering theorem, which ﬁnds a
cover of the original family by n + o(n) transversal bases. The proof is highly
complex and grapples with the underlying matroidal concepts to a degree not yet
see in previous attempts on the conjcture, so that one feels that this work is a very
signiﬁcant step towards the full conjecture.
   The third highlight of the workshop was presented by Aleksa Milojević and
Istvan Tomon in two consecutive talks, which covered their joint work with Zhihan
Jin and Shengtong Zhang on spectral graph theory and its applications to the
maximum cut problem in graphs and to Chowla’s cosine problem in harmonic
analysis. This work was later featured in an article in Quanta. Their main result
shows that a graph with average degree d and smallest eigenvalue λn satisﬁes
|λn | ≤ d1−α contains a clique of size d1−O(α) . One application gives a substantially
better upper bound for the minimum of cosine polynomials. Another application
shows that the Edwards bound on the maximum cut of a graph can be substantially
improved for graphs that don’t contain a very large clique.

     Extended VC-dimension and Radon type theorems for unions
                            of convex sets
                             Noga Alon
                (joint work with Shakhar Smorodinsky)

Radon’s theorem states that any set of d+2 points in Rd can be partitioned into two
subsets whose convex hulls intersect. Formally, given a set P = {x1 , x2 , . . . , xd+2 }
⊆ Rd , there exists a partition of P into two disjoint subsets P1 and P2 such that
CH(P1 ) ∩ CH(P2 ) 6= ∅, where here and in what follows CH(X) denotes the convex
hull of a set of points X. The bound d + 2 is tight as one can easily see by
taking any set of d + 1 aﬃne-independent points in Rd , that is, the vertices of the
d-dimensional simplex.
   This fundamental theorem is a cornerstone in discrete geometry, providing in-
sights into the structure of point sets and their convex combinations. It implies
various fundamental theorems in geometry including the classical Helly’s Theo-
rem, the center point theorem, and more. Its implications extend to machine
learning, statistical learning and computational geometry, inﬂuencing algorithms
for geometric separation, point location, and convex hull computations. The com-
binatorial notion of VC-dimension is the analog of Radon’s bound for abstract
set-systems.
   Radon’s theorem serves as a basis for numerous generalizations and related re-
sults, such as the following classical theorem of Tverberg [4], which further enriches
our understanding of geometric conﬁgurations.

        MaxCut in H-free graphs: recent results and connections
                             Aleksa Milojević
         (joint work with Zhihan Jin, István Tomon, Shengtong Zhang)


Given a graph G, a maximum cut in G, denoted by mc(G), is the largest number
of edges crossing some bipartition V (G) = A ∪ B of its vertex set. MaxCut is
among the most extensively studied graph parameters, lying at the intersection
of theoretical computer science [12, 15, 16], extremal combinatorics [1, 2, 10, 13]
and probabilistic graph theory [8, 7, 9]. In theoretical computer science, one
is usually interested in eﬃcient approximations of the maximum cut, while in
extremal combinatorics the emphasis is on establishing sharp bounds in terms of
various graph parameters, such as the number of vertices or edges.
   A simple probabilistic argument shows that every graph with m edges has a
cut of size at least m/2. Indeed, if one chooses a cut uniformly at random, its
expected size is exactly m/2. The constant 1/2 is best possible in general, and
therefore it is often more natural to measure the surplus of a graph G, deﬁned
as surp(G) = mc(G) − m/2. A fundamental result of √Edwards [10, 11] asserts

   In general, if G√is a disjoint union of constantly many cliques, then its maximum

bound if G is far from being a disjoint union of cliques? One way to ensure that
a graph is far from a disjoint union of cliques is to forbid some ﬁxed graph H as
a subgraph. The study of the MaxCut, and in particular the surplus, in H-free
graphs was initiated by Erdős and Lovász in the 1970s [13]. A landmark result in
the area is due to Alon [1], who proved that if a graph G has m edges and contains
no triangles, then surp(G) = Ω(m4/5 ), and this bound is tight.
   Extending this line of research, Alon, Bollobás, Krivelevich, and Sudakov [2]
conjectured that for every ﬁxed H, there exists a positive constant εH > 0
such that every H-free graph G with m edges has surplus at least surp(G) ≥
ΩH (m3/4+εH ).
   Two natural classes of graphs H for which this conjecture has been studied are
short cycles Cr and complete graphs Kr . The surplus in graphs without short
cycles is investigated extensively [2, 3, 5, 14]. On the other hand, determining the
minimum surplus in Kr -free graphs is much more diﬃcult. Already Alon, Bollobás,
Krivelevich, and Sudakov [2] proved that for every r, there exists εr > 0 such that
every Kr -free graph has surplus at least m1/2+εr . The value of εr was improved by
Carlson, Kolla, Li, Mani, Sudakov, and Trevisan [6], and further strengthened by

ε > 0, independent of r, such that every Kr -free graph has surplus Ωr (m1/2+ε ).

                           Chowla’s cosine problem
                                István Tomon
         (joint work with Zhihan Jin, Aleksa Milojević, Shengtong Zhang)

                    A large hole in pseudo-random graphs
                             Michael Krivelevich
        (joint work with Sahar Diskin, Itay Markbreit, Maksim Zhukovskii)

We discuss the existence of long induced cycles (also called holes) and paths in
pseudo-random graphs. We adopt (n, d, λ)-graphs as a working model for pseudo-
random graphs, see [3] for an extensive discussion of (n, d, λ)-graphs and their
properties. A graph G is called an (n, d, λ)-graph if it has n vertices, is d-regular,
and all non-trivial eigenvalues of G (i.e., of the adjacency matrix of G) are at most
λ in their absolute values. The edge distribution in such graphs is governed by the
famed Expander Mixing Lemma by Alon and Chung [1].
   We show that there exist constants δ1 , δ2 > 0 such that if G is an (n, d, λ)–
graph with the eigenvalue ratio λ/d ≤ δ1 , then G contains an induced cycle of
length at least δ2 n/d. This constitutes a substantial improvement over a recent
result of Draganić and Keevash [2], who actually argued about long induced paths
in pseudo-random graphs. We further demonstrate that, up to a constant factor,
this is best possible.

                 Long cycles in vertex transitive digraphs
                               Matija Bucić
     (joint work with K. Hendrey, B. Mohar, R. Steiner, and L. Yepremyan)

Finding long paths and cycles in graphs is one of the most classical directions of
study in graph theory. Perhaps the most famous instance of this general direction
is the question of ﬁnding the longest possible cycle, namely one that traverses all
the vertices. Such a cycle is called a Hamilton cycle, and a graph containing it is
said to be Hamiltonian. Hamiltonicity is a very classical and extensively studied
graph property. In general, it is a hard problem to decide if a given graph is
Hamiltonian. In fact, this is one of Karp’s famous 21 NP-hard problems [6], and
is often used to establish hardness of other computational problems.
   This goes a long way towards explaining why there are so many interesting re-
sults establishing suﬃcient conditions for Hamiltonicity. Perhaps the most famous
one, featured in essentially every introductory course on graph theory, is Dirac’s
theorem from 1952, which states that any graph with minimum degree at least n2
is Hamiltonian. A major downside of Dirac’s theorem is that it only applies for
very dense graphs, leading to a more challenging question of ﬁnding natural graph
properties that would force Hamiltonicity even for much sparser graphs. Perhaps
the most intriguing candidate, ﬁrst considered in the 1960s, is symmetry. This
idea ﬁrst appeared in a paper by Lovász [8] from 1969, where he conjectured that
any connected vertex transitive graph must contain a Hamilton path. Thomassen
(see [2]) reﬁned this conjecture in 1978 by asserting that any suﬃciently large
connected vertex transitive graph is Hamiltonian.
    These conjectures have attracted an immense amount of work over the years,
with multiple surveys on the topic [16, 11, 1, 3] being written, starting as early as
the 1980s. Despite this attention, both conjectures remain widely open and most
of what is known concerns various additional assumptions under which the conjec-
tures hold. On the other hand, Babai [2] already in 1979 initiated a very general
direction of attack, namely of trying to at least ﬁnd a long cycle in every connected
vertex transitive graph (without any additional assumptions). In particular, he
proved that√ an n-vertex connected vertex transitive graph always has a cycle of
length Ω( n). In recent years, there have been several improvements over this
result, all making interesting connections to a number of other interesting graph
theoretic problems [5, 10, 4, 9] leading to the current state of the art of Ω(n9/14 )
proved in [10].
    Here, we are interested in the directed analogue of this problem. Namely, how
long a cycle can we ﬁnd in any n-vertex connected vertex transitive digraph? This
question is even more classical, owing to the fact that in the arguably most in-
teresting instance of the problem, namely that of Cayley digraphs, the directed
variant is more natural1 and translates to a natural group rearrangement prob-
lem. Indeed, the two oldest papers to consider this problem started from this

      C4 -free subgraphs of high degree with geometric applications
                               Benny Sudakov
         (joint work with Zach Hunter, Aleksa Milojević, István Tomon)

In 1953, Zarankiewicz [26] asked for the maximum number of edges in an n-vertex
graph G that contains no copy of Ks,s , the complete bipartite graph with two
parts of size s. Just a year later, Kővári, Sós, and Turán [18] proved that such
graphs have at most Os (n2−1/s ) edges—a bound that remains unimproved to this
day, although it is known to be tight only for s ∈ {2, 3}. On the other hand, a
simple application of the probabilistic deletion method yields a lower bound of
Ωs (n2−2/(s+1) ) for s ≥ 4 (see, e.g., [2]), which, up to polylogarithmic factors, is
the best known to date.
   Over the past two decades, Zarankiewicz-type problems have been extensively
studied in various restricted graph families, motivated by questions from both
combinatorial geometry and structural graph theory. In many geometric inci-
dence problems, it is natural to study Ks,s -free graphs, as this condition often
24                                                      Oberwolfach Report 1/2026

corresponds to an inherent geometric constraint. For instance, the incidence graph
of points and lines in R2 is always K2,2 -free, while in point-hyperplane incidence
graphs, avoiding Ks,s reﬂects a natural non-degeneracy assumption [3, 22].
    More generally, reﬁned bounds for Zarankiewicz-type problems have been ob-
tained in a variety of settings: for intersection graphs of geometric objects [10, 11,
12, 17, 25], incidence graphs involving points and geometric shapes [7, 13, 17, 22],
semialgebraic and semilinear graphs [5, 13, 22], graphs of bounded VC-dimension
[13, 16], and for visibility graphs [1, 8]. In all these cases, the classical Kővari-
Sós-Turán bound can be substantially improved. For example, it was shown in
[13] that a Ks,s -free graph of VC-dimension d has at most Od,s (n2−1/d ) edges – a
signiﬁcant improvement over the O(n2−1/s ) bound when d ≪ s. In some cases, the
bound is even linear: for example, [10] showed that a Ks,s -free intersection graph
of n curves in the plane has at most Os (n) edges. Beyond merely improving the
bounds, these works also bring together a rich toolkit of techniques such as par-
titioning methods, variants of ε-nets and separator theorems that have deepened
our understanding of the underlying geometry of these problems.
    These results reveal an interesting general phenomenon: in many restricted
graph families, the extremal behavior is not governed by s, as it is in general
graphs, but instead reﬂects intrinsic structural parameters such as the ambient
dimension or the VC-dimension, while s may only appear in the constant factor.
    In parallel with this line of work, a similar phenomenon has been observed in
structural graph theory. One of the ﬁrst results in this direction is due to Kühn
and Osthus [19], who showed that if a Ks,s -free n-vertex graph G does not contain
an induced subdivision of a ﬁxed graph H, then G has at most OH,s (n) edges. A
subdivision of the graph H is any graph which can be obtained by replacing the
edges of H by disjoint paths. Thus, the answer to Zarankiewicz’s problem changes
substantially in the family of graphs avoiding induced subdivisions of H.
    Since then, analogous behavior has been observed in other families of interest,
such as graphs avoiding an induced copy of a tree T [15, 24], and families deﬁned
by forbidding an induced bipartite graph [6, 15]. In fact, the general phenomenon
can be described more abstractly, as follows. If F is a family of graphs closed
under taking induced subgraphs and F excludes at least one bipartite graph H,
then there exists ε = ε(F ) > 0 such that every Ks,s -free n-vertex graph in F has
at most Os (n2−ε ) edges. This was implicitly proven by [6, 13, 14], and the value
of ε was later improved for various families F by [4, 15].
    In this talk, we focus on Zarankiewicz-type problems in geometrically deﬁned
graphs, where the extremal number is expected to be linear or nearly linear. We
uncover a new structural result about graphs avoiding high-degree induced C4 -free
subgraphs, which we use to improve and unify a wide range of Zarankiewicz-type
results for geometric graph families, obtaining tight bounds in each case.

Intersection graphs of curves. The intersection graph of a family of sets or
geometric objects F is the graph whose vertices correspond to the elements of
F , with an edge between any two sets that intersect. A particularly interesting
class of intersection graphs are string graphs, which are deﬁned as the intersection
Combinatorics                                                                      25

graphs of families of planar curves (which are sometimes also called strings). Here,
a planar curve stands for the image of a continuous function φ : [0, 1] → R2 .
   Pach and Sharir asked in [23] what is the largest number of edges in a Ks,s -
free string graph on n vertices. Separator theorems can be used to show that
such graphs have at most 2O(s) n edges. Further, since string graphs do not con-
tain subdivisions of non-planar graphs as induced subgraphs, and in particular no
subdivision of K5 , the theorem of Kühn and Osthus [19] discussed above directly
shows that the number of edges in Ks,s -free string graphs is Os (n). However,
the point of the work of Fox and Pach, together with the subsequent works, is to
pinpoint the correct dependence on s.
   These bounds were then further improved [12, 21] until Lee [20] established the
optimal bound of O(s log s), using optimal bounds on the size of balanced separa-
tors in string graphs. Although the existence of small separators is a powerful tool,
with a wide range of theoretical and algorithmic applications, it is only available
for a limited selection of graph families. In this work, we provide an alternative
proof of the optimal O(s log s) bound which relies on our key graph-theoretic tool,
Theorem 2, and avoids the use of separators altogether.
Theorem 1. Any n-vertex Ks,s -free string graph has O(s(log s)n) edges.

Key graph-theoretic tool: ﬁnding C4 -free subgraphs or dense patches.
Our main theoretical contribution, and the unifying theme of this work, is the fol-
lowing statement, which says that every graph G either contains a C4 -free induced
subgraph with large average degree or a dense subgraph, whose size is at least the
average degree of G.
Theorem 2. For every positive integer k there exists c = c(k) > 0 such that the
following holds. Every graph G of average degree at least d either contains an
induced C4 -free subgraph with average degree at least k, or it contains a subgraph
on d vertices with at least cd2 edges.
   In a typical application, we attempt to bound the average degree of a Ks,s -free
graph from some geometrically deﬁned class by d ≤ O(s), which corresponds to
bounding the number of edges by O(sn). To do this, the ﬁrst step is to bound
the number of edges of a C4 -free graph from this family by Cn, for some absolute
constant C, which corresponds to addressing the case when s = 2. Then, given a
Ks,s -free graph G from this geometric family, we apply our Theorem 2 with any
k > C, and observe that, since G does not contain C4 -free subgraphs of average
degree at least k, it must contain a subgraph on d vertices with cd2 edges. The
second step is to show that in such dense graphs one can ﬁnd bicliques of size
Ω(d). In particular, this means that s ≥ Ω(d), i.e. that d ≤ O(s).
Graphs without dense subgraphs. Finally, we highlight a strengthening of
Theorem 2, which can be used to settle a recent conjecture of Fox, Nenadov, and
Pham [9]. A natural relaxation of Ks,s -freeness, introduced in [9], is the notion of
(c, t)-sparsity. A graph G is said to be (c, t)-sparse if for all subsets A, B ⊆ V (G)
with |A|, |B| ≥ t, there are at most e(A, B) ≤ (1 − c)|A||B| edges between them. It
26                                                             Oberwolfach Report 1/2026

is easy to see that a graph is Ks,s -free if and only if it is (1/s2 , s)-sparse. Another
motivation for studying (c, t)-sparse graphs is that random graphs G(n, p) are
(c, t)-sparse for any c < 1 − p and t = Ωp,c (log n).
    In [9], the authors showed that if c > 0 is a constant and T is a tree, then every
(c, t)-sparse graph with at least Ctn edges contains an induced copy of T , where
C = C(T, c) is suﬃciently large with respect to T and c. They conjectured that
the same statement holds if instead of a tree T , we seek an induced subdivision of
a ﬁxed graph H. We prove this conjecture.
Theorem 3. For every c > 0 and every graph H, there exists a constant C such
that if G is a (c, t)-sparse graph with average degree at least Ct, then G contains
an induced subdivision of H.
   In particular, this theorem immediately follows from the following strengthening
of Theorem 2, after recalling that C4 -free graphs of suﬃciently large average degree
contain an induced subdivision of H (Kühn and Osthus [19]).
Theorem 4. Let k ≥ 1, c > 0, then there exists C = C(k, c) such that the following
holds. Let G be a (c, t)-sparse graph with average degree at least Ct. Then G
contains a bipartite C4 -free induced subgraph of average degree at least k.

    An improved upper bound for the multicolour Ramsey number
                               of odd cycles
                              Oliver Janzer
(joint work with Maria Axenovich, Wouter Cames van Batenburg, Lukas Michel,
                            Mathieu Rundström)

The k-colour Ramsey number Rk (H) of a graph H is the smallest integer n such
that every k-edge-colouring of the complete graph Kn contains a monochromatic
copy of H. For the triangle, the notoriously diﬃcult Schur-Erdős problem asks to
determine the growth rate of Rk (C3 ). In 1917, Schur [12] showed that
                                Ω(3k ) ≤ Rk (C3 ) ≤ O(k!).
Since then, the upper bound has remained unchanged up to small improvements
to the constant factor, while the lower bound has been improved to Ω(3.28k ) by
Ageron, Casteras, Pellerin, Portella, Rimmel, and Tomasik [1]. Erdős conjectured
that Rk (C3 ) = 2Θ(k) [3] and oﬀered monetary awards for proving this conjecture
and solving some related problems.
   For longer odd cycles, Bondy and Erdős [2] and Erdős and Graham [5] obtained
the bounds
                        ℓ · 2k + 1 ≤ Rk (C2ℓ+1 ) ≤ 2ℓ · (k + 2)!.
If k is ﬁxed, it turns out that the lower bound is sharp as Jenssen and Skokan [9]
proved that Rk (C2ℓ+1 ) = ℓ · 2k + 1 for all suﬃciently large ℓ. However, this is not
true if ℓ is ﬁxed. Indeed, Day and Johnson [4] showed that for all ℓ there exists
some δ = δ(ℓ) > 0 such that Rk (C2ℓ+1 ) ≥ 2ℓ · (2 + δ)k−1 for all suﬃciently large k.
   Usually, if the number of colours is large, longer odd cycles should be easier
to ﬁnd than shorter odd cycles. For instance, Fox [6] conjectured that for every
ε > 0 there exists some ℓ such that Rk (C2ℓ+1 ) ≤ k εk for all suﬃciently large k.
Li [10] even made the stronger conjecture that Rk (C2ℓ+1 ) ≤ o(k!1/ℓ ) as k → ∞.
Nevertheless, the gap between these conjectures and the best upper bounds known
Combinatorics                                                                                   35

remained large. Li [10] proved that Rk (C5 ) ≤ ck · k! for some constant c, which
was later extended by Lin and Chen   √ [11] to longer odd cycles by showing that for
ℓ ≥ 2 we have Rk (C2ℓ+1 ) ≤ ck · k! for some constant c that only depends on

Rk (C2ℓ+1 ) ≤ ck · k!1/ℓ .

also been considerable interest in ﬁnding any short monochromatic odd cycle. It is
easy to see that there is a k-edge-colouring of K2k without any monochromatic odd

Hunter [7] obtained the ﬁrst non-trivial upper bound, showing that L(k) ≤ (2k +

L(k) ≤ O(k 3/2 · 2k/2 ).
   Both Girão and Hunter [7] and Janzer and Yip [8] also discussed the more gen-

the length of the cycle is O(δ −1/2 · k) if δ < 1. With our method, we can ﬁnd
signiﬁcantly shorter monochromatic odd cycles, unless δ tends to 0 very quickly.

Theorem 2. For k ∈ N and b > 2, every k-edge-colouring of Kn with n > bk
contains a monochromatic odd cycle of length at most 2⌈logb/2 k⌉ + 1.

   In particular, if b = 2 + δ for 0 < δ < 1, this result guarantees a cycle of length
O(δ −1 · log k). In addition, the result also applies to larger values of b. If b = k ε ,
this yields a result similar to Theorem 1, but without controlling the exact cycle

Deﬁnitions. We use P for both a poset and its underlying set. Elements x, y of
P are comparable, denoted x ∼ y, if x < y or x > y. The sets of maximal and
minimal elements of X ⊆ P are max(X) and min(X). An ideal of P is I ⊆ P
satisfying x < y ∈ I ⇒ x ∈ I. A chain is a linearly ordered set, and an antichain
is a set with no relations x < y. The height, ht(P ), is the maximum size of a chain
of P . We assume throughout that P has size n.
   A (linear) extension of P may be viewed as either an order-preserving bijection
from P to [n], or a linear ordering of P respecting the poset relations. The set of
extensions is E(P ) and e(P ) = |E(P )|. A uniformly random extension is either
f or ≺ depending on whether we are thinking of bijections or orderings. We use
p(x ≺ y) for P(f (x) < f (y)).

max over distinct x, y ∈ P ); and h(x) = hP (x) = Ef (x), the average height of x.

                  Complexity measures of tree decompositions
                             Maria Chudnovsky

For a graph G, a tree decomposition (T, χ) of G consists of a tree T and a map
χ : V (T ) → 2V (G) with the following properties:
     (1) For every v ∈ V (G), there exists t ∈ V (T ) such that v ∈ χ(t).
     (2) For every v1 v2 ∈ E(G), there exists t ∈ V (T ) such that v1 , v2 ∈ χ(t).
     (3) For every v ∈ V (G), the subgraph of T induced by {t ∈ V (T ) | v ∈ χ(t)}
         is connected.
   This is a way to represent a general graph using a tree (which is structurally
a much simpler object). Such a representation is a useful tool for understanding
the structure of a graph; it also often has algorithmic applications.
   There are several ways to measure the complexity of a given tree decomposition.
Classically, the width of (T, χ) is the maximum cardinality of χ(t), where the
maximum is taken over all t ∈ V (T ). The treewidth of a graph G, denoted by
tw(G), is the smallest width of a tree decomposition of G.
   We denote by Wt×t the t-by-t hexagonal grid, and by Kt,t the complete bipartite
graph with both sides of the bipartition of size t. A graph H is a minor of a graph
G if H can be obtained from a subgraph of G by contracting edges. A graph class
C is said to be of bounded treewidth if there exists an integer c such that tw(G) < c
for every G ∈ C.
Combinatorics                                                                    41

   Traditionally tree decompositions were studied in the context of graph minors;
the dichotomy between graph classes of bounded and unbounded treewidth is
completely understood from this perspective. This is captured in the celebrated
Grid Minor Theorem [6].
Theorem 1. For every positive integer t there exists an integer c(t) such that
every graph that does not contain a minor isomorphic to Wt×t has treewidth at
most c(t).
  An equivalent statement can be formulated in terms of subgraphs [6]:
Theorem 2. For every positive integer t there exists an integer c(t) such that
every graph that does not contain a subgraph isomorphic to a subdivision of Wt×t
has treewidth at most c(t).
    A graph H is an induced minor of a graph G if H can be obtained from an in-
duced subgraph of G by contracting edges, and deleting all parallel edges created
in the process. In the last decade much attention has been directed to under-
standing the behavior of tree decompositions in the context of induced subgraphs,
and more recently induced minors. For a positive integer t, we denote by Ct the
class of {Kt,t , Wt×t }-induced-minor-free graphs, and by Ct∗ be the subclass of Ct
consisting of all graphs with no complete subgraph of size t.
    A necessary condition for getting a class of bounded treewidth is that a large
complete graph is forbidden as a subgraph, and a large complete bipartite graph
is forbidden as an induced minor. Unfortunately, a direct analogue of Theorems 1
and 2 is false even if these additional conditions are taken into account. However,
several variants of these theorems remain of interest.
    First, instead of studying graph classes of bounded treewidth, one can ask
whether, given a graph class C there exists a slow-growing function fC such that
for every n-vertex graph G ∈ C, tw(G) < f (n). The following conjecture has
become known in the area:
Conjecture 3. For every t ∈ N there is an integer d = d(t) such that every
n-vertex graph G ∈ Ct∗ with n > 2 satisﬁes tw(G) ≤ logd n.
   This is still open, but a weaker result where the poly-logarithmic bound on
treewidth is replaced by a subpolynomial one was proved in [1].
Theorem 4. For every t ∈ N, there exist ǫ = ǫ(t) ∈ (0, 1] and c = c(t) ∈ N such
                                                          1−ǫ
that every n-vertex graph G in Ct∗ satisﬁes tw(G) ≤ 2c log n .
    Next, one can use parameters other then cardinality to measure the complexity
of a tree decomposition. Let G be a graph, S ⊆ V (G) be a vertex set in G and r be
a positive integer. The distance-r-independence number of S, denoted by αr (S), is
the size of the largest subset I ⊆ S such that for every pair u, v ∈ I, the distance
between u and v in G is greater than r. We deﬁne αr of a tree decomposition, and
tree-αr of a graph analogously to width and treewidth. Tree-α1 was introduced in
[5] as a tool for studying tree decompositions of graph classes containing complete
graphs of unbounded size, and the following conjecture was proposed (where ω(G)
is the largest size of a complete subgraph of G):

Conjecture 5. Let C be a class of graphs closed under induced subgraphs. Then, if
there exists a function f such that for every G ∈ C it holds that tw(G) ≤ f (ω(G)),
then C has bounded tree-independence number.
   Conjecture 5 received a signiﬁcant amount of attention in the graph theoretic

 Asymptotically-tight packing and covering with transversal bases in
                        Rota’s basis conjecture
                           Lisa Sauermann
                 (joint work with Richard Montgomery)

Rota’s basis conjecture is a famous conjecture concerning the rearrangeability of
bases in a vector space or matroid. Rota posed it in 1989 (see [9, Conjecture 4]),
both in the special case of vector spaces, and for general matroids. Given any n
bases B1 , . . . , Bn of an n-dimensional vector space (or, more generally, in a matroid
of rank n), the conjecture states that the elements in each of the bases B1 , . . . , Bn
can be ordered in such a way that the ﬁrst elements of B1 , . . . , Bn together form
a basis, the second elements of B1 , . . . , Bn together form a basis, and so on. In
other words, the (multi-)set, B1 ∪ · · · ∪ Bn can be decomposed into n disjoint bases
each consisting of exactly one element from each of the original bases B1 , . . . , Bn .
Saying that such a basis containing exactly one element from each of B1 , . . . , Bn
is a transversal basis, Rota’s basis conjecture can be restated as follows.
Conjecture 1 (Rota’s Basis Conjecture). Any collection of n disjoint bases in a
rank-n matroid can be decomposed into n transversal bases.
   Despite a great deal of attention, including as the subject of the 12th Poly-
math project [12] in 2017, Conjecture 1 remains widely open, even for the case
of vector spaces (i.e., representable matroids). The conjecture has only been con-
ﬁrmed in some very special cases, including for ‘paving matroids’ by Geelen and
Humphries [6], for ‘strongly base orderable matroids’ by Wild [14], and for ma-
troids of rank at most 4 by Cheung [3]. As discovered by Huang and Rota [9]
(and later simpliﬁed by Onn [10]), Rota’s basis conjecture for real-representable
matroids of rank n, for even n, can be reduced to the Alon–Tarsi conjecture for
Latin squares of order n. The Alon–Tarsi conjecture was proved when n − 1 is
prime by Drisko [5] and when n + 1 is prime by Glynn [8], thus together giving
a proof of Conjecture 1 for real-representable matroids of rank n when n − 1 or
n + 1 is an odd prime.
Combinatorics                                                                             47

    In addition to these special cases, there is some further evidence towards Con-
jecture 1. In 2006, Aharoni and Berger [1] proved that the natural fractional
relaxation of Conjecture 1 holds. More recently, the speaker [13] showed that the
conjecture holds with high probability for representable matroids over ﬁnite ﬁelds
if the n bases are chosen independently and uniformly at random.
    There are two natural directions for working towards Conjecture 1 in full, as was
highlighted by Pokrovskiy [11], and which were the focus of this talk. The ﬁrst of
these is the packing problem in this setting: given n bases in a rank-n matroid, how
many disjoint transversal bases can we ﬁnd? The second is the covering problem
in this setting: given n bases in a rank-n matroid, how many transversal bases do
we need to cover all of the elements of these bases?
    Our ﬁrst main result discussed in this talk gives an asymptotically-tight answer
to the packing problem.
Theorem 2 (Asymptotic Packing Theorem). For any ε > 0, the following holds
whenever n is suﬃciently large with respect to ε. Any collection of n bases of a
rank-n matroid has at least (1 − ε)n disjoint transversal bases.
  This improves earlier
                      √ bounds for the number of transversal bases in this setting,
namely a bound of Ω( n) by Geelen and Webb [7], of Ω(n/ log n) by Geelen and
Dong [4], and of (1/2 − ε)n by Bucić, Kwan, Pokrovskiy, and Sudakov [2].
  Our second main result gives an asymptotically-tight answer to the covering
problem.
Theorem 3 (Asymptotic Covering Theorem). For any ε > 0, the following holds
whenever n is suﬃciently large with respect to ε. Any collection of n bases of a
rank-n matroid can be covered by at most (1 + ε)n transversal bases.
   Using topological tools, Aharoni and Berger [1, Assertion 8.11] showed in 2006
that 2n transversal bases suﬃce for a covering in the setting of Theorem 3. The
Polymath 12 project [12] later improved this bound slightly, showing that 2n − 2
transversal bases are already suﬃcient.
   To prove Theorems 2 and 3, we modiﬁed and extended the methods of [2] and
[11], while introducing new randomised approaches to extend families of rainbow
independent sets to transversal bases, careful notions of ‘density’ within subsets
of the matroid, and intricate techniques to modify and improve families of disjoint
rainbow independent sets.

                             Local Removal Lemmas
                                     Fan Wei
                  (joint work with Jacob Fox, Hung Le, Ruilin Shi)

The graph removal lemma is among the central results in extremal combinatorics
and has broad applications, including to extremal problems for graphs and hy-
pergraphs, additive combinatorics, discrete geometry, and theoretical computer
science. It qualitatively says that if a graph has relatively few copies of a ﬁxed
graph H, then it is close to a H-free graph.
   It is natural to study removal lemmas under a stronger metric. The usual edit
distance is equivalent to the normalized L1 length of the degree sequence of the
removed spanning subgraph. It is also natural to consider the Lp distance for any
ﬁnite p > 0, but any of these metrics are equivalent, meaning if one is small (o(1))
then the others are small, and so are not that interesting for further study. The
L∞ distance is the normalized maximum degree in the subgraph of the removed
edge. Two graphs G1 , G2 on the same vertex set V have L∞ distance at most ε if
the graph on V with edge set E(G1 )∆E(G2 ) has maximum degree at most ε|V |.
Thus the L∞ distance is at least the corresponding L1 distance. Hence, the L∞
distance is a stronger metric than the edit distance.
   It is natural to ask the following question.
         Does a variant of the graph removal lemma hold under the L∞
         distance?
   To see why this is a natural notion, recall in the usual graph removal lemma,
we bound the total number of edges removed, which is equivalent to bounding
the average degree of the spanning subgraph of the removed edges. In contrast,
here we are asking to bound the maximum degree of the spanning subgraph of the
removed edges.
   The following theorem resolves the question for cliques.

where the mapping of V (H) to V (G) is injective.
   A simple example illustrating this point is H = K2,2 . For any 0 < ε < 1/2 and
suﬃciently large integer n, consider the host graph K2,n−2 on n vertices, and let
A be the bipartite class with n − 2 vertices and B the the one with two vertices.
In any K2,2 -free subgraph of K2,n−2 , at most one vertex in A can be adjacent to
both vertices in B. Thus at least |A| − 1 edges must be removed, implying that
the degree of some vertex in B drops by at least (|A| − 1)/2 > εn. Therefore
K2,n−2 is ε-far from being K2,2 -free under L∞ , while there are only O(n2 ) copies
of K2,2 in K2,n−2 . Similarly, if H is the complete bipartite graph Ka,b with a ≤ b,
if ε < 1/a and n is suﬃciently large, the host graph Ka,n−a is ε-far from being
Ka,b -free under L∞ , while there are only O(nb ) copies of Ka,b in Ka,n−a .

   It appears that the correct form of the local removal lemma is that for any
H, there is an a(H) such that a graph G on n vertices is o(1)-close from being

the correct function a(H) for such a local graph removal lemma.
Deﬁnition 4. Let H be a graph. Let a(H) be the size of the maximum indepen-
dent subset A in H such that for every homomorphism ρ from H to itself, if every
vertex in A has at most one pre-image under ρ, then ρ has exactly |A| vertices

Theorem 6. Let H be a connected bipartite graph on k vertices. For each ε > 0,

               Ramsey Cayley graphs over non-abelian groups
                           Carl Schildkraut

Ramsey’s theorem is one of the foundational results in extremal graph theory, and
there is a great amount of literature on its variations, extensions, and quantitative
aspects. The following deﬁnition is inspired by its quantitative aspects.
Deﬁnition 1. Let C > 0. A graph on n vertices is C-Ramsey if its clique number
and independence number are both at most C log2 n.
   The proof of Ramsey’s theorem by Erdős and Szekeres [7] yields that no graph is
C-Ramsey for C < 1/2, while Erdős [6] showed 12 years later, in one of the ﬁrst ap-
plications of the probabilistic method, that a random n-vertex graph is 2-Ramsey
with high probability. However, we do not yet know any explicit constructions of
C-Ramsey graphs for any constant C.
   To this end, it is natural to ask where C-Ramsey graphs can be found, and
what properties they can have. The following conjecture along these lines is due
to Noga Alon:
Conjecture 2 (Alon [1, 2]). There exists some absolute constant C > 0 for which
every ﬁnite group G admits a C-Ramsey Cayley1 graph.
  The ﬁrst positive result in the line of this conjecture was given by Green [8], who
showed that Conjecture 2 holds for cyclic groups. Recently, a signiﬁcant advance
was made by Conlon, Fox, Pham, and Yepremyan:
Theorem 3 (Conlon–Fox–Pham–Yepremyan [5]). Conjecture 2 holds for the fam-
ily of abelian groups whose orders are relatively prime to 6. Moreover, Conjecture 2
holds for all abelian groups of almost all2 orders.
   Our main result is an extension of Theorem 3 to not-necessarily-abelian groups.

                 The generalised Erdős-Rothschild problem
                             Katherine Staden
        (joint work with Pranshu Gupta, Yanitsa Pehova, Emil Powierski)


The Erdős-Rothschild problem from 1974 [5] asks for the maximum number of
s-edge colourings in an n-vertex graph which avoid a monochromatic copy of Kk ,
given positive integers n, s, k. We systematically study the generalisation of this
problem to a given forbidden family of colourings of Kk .
    Given a graph G, and a partition P of the edges of Kk which we call a pattern,
let F (G; P, s) be the number of s-edge-colourings of G such that there is no copy of
Kk in which the edge-partition given by the colour classes is isomorphic to P . The
original ‘monochromatic’ problem is the case when the pattern P is the partition
into one part. We are interested in the generalised Erdős-Rothschild problem, that
is, determining

   This problem typically exhibits a dichotomy whereby for some values of s,
the extremal graph is the ‘trivial’ one, namely the Turán graph on k − 1 parts,
with no copies of Kk ; while for others, we know the extremal graph has many
copies of Kk but determining it becomes much harder. There are very few results,
asymptotic or exact, which are known. For the monochromatic problem, the only
pairs (k, s) where there is a solution are (k, 2), (k, 3), which are known to be the
only values with trivial solution; and (3, 4), (3, 5) (asymptotic only), (3, 6), (3, 7)
and (4, 4) [1, 4, 9, 11, 12]. The ‘rainbow triangle’ problem where k = 3 and P has
three parts each containing one edge, has been solved completely: for s = 3 the

               Is there Geometry in Totally Discrete Spaces?
                                  Nati Linial
                (joint work with Daniel Cizma, Maria Chudnovsky)

Let X = [n] be a ﬁnite set of points. An xy-path Pxy is a sequence of points that
starts at x ∈ X and ends at y ∈ X. We always take Pyx to be the reverse of Pxy .
Our minimalist notion of a geometry is a path system P on X, i.e., a collection of
xy-paths Pxy , one per each pair of points x, y ∈ X.
   Path systems are closely related to graphs. For P as above, let G = (X, E) be
the graph where xy ∈ E exactly if Pxy is a single-hop path. Also, given a graph
H = (V, F ) it is of interest to investigate path systems on V in which all single-hop
paths are edges in F .
   A path system P that is closed under intersection is said to be consistent.
Equivalently: P is consistent, if for every path Pxy in P and any two points a and
b on Pxy , the subpath of Pxy that connects a and b coincides with Pab .
   It is easy to construct consistent path systems on a graph G = (V, E). Assign
positive lengths to the edges w : E → R>0 . With almost certainty the w-shortest
xy path is unique and we let it be Pxy . Such a path system is said to be strictly
metric. If some w-shortest paths are not unique, we can still choose between
them in a consistent way, and get a consistent metric path system. The following
question suggests itself:
Basic Question: Is every consistent path system metric (or even strictly metric)?
Answer: No. Figure 1a exhibits a non-metrizable path system in the Petersen
graph Π. If uv ∈ E(Π), then the path Puv comprises the single edge uv.
If xy 6∈ E(Π), then x and y have a unique common neighbor z, and in most cases
we let Px,y = (x, z, y). There are 5 exceptional pairs, namely P1,7 = 1, 5, 10, 7 (see
Figure 1a), and its shifts P2,8 = 2, 1, 6, 8; P3,9 = 3, 2, 7, 9; P4,10 = 4, 3, 8, 10; P5,6 =
5, 4, 9, 6. This path system is easily seen to be consistent. It is nonmetrizable,
since there exists no corresponding weight function w, as Figure 1b shows.
   We call a graph G metrizable (resp. strictly metrizable) if Basic Question is
answered positively for every consistent path system on G.

Question 1 (Erdős–Pach). Given a set P of n > 4 distinct points in the plane,
can one always ﬁnd two distances that each occur as distances between pairs of
points in P at most n times?
   It is a result of Hopf and Pannwitz [2] that, for any n distinct points in the

be necessary by considering two equilateral triangles joined on a side.
