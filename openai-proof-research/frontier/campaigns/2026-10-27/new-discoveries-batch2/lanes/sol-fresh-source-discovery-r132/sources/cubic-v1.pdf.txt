                                          globally synchronizing. In this case, by Lyapunov stability of a strict local minimum of a gradient
                                          flow [2], there is a neighborhood U ∋ θ∗ such that all trajectories starting in U converge to θ∗ .

1.1    The Kuramoto model on expanders, highly-connected and random graphs
There is a vast literature on the Kuramoto model [15]; for the onset of synchronization and for
oscillator networks in general, see the surveys [9, 21].
     The high degree regime is well understood. Taylor [22] proved that every connected graph with
minimum degree at least 0.9395 (n − 1) forces global synchronization. The constant has since then
been improved in a sequence of refinements of the same landscape method: Ling, Xu and Bandeira
[17] to roughly 0.7929 n; Lu and Steinerberger [18] to roughly 0.7889 n; and Kassabov, Strogatz and
Townsend [13] to 0.75 n. Recently, Lepsveridze and Zhang [16] improved it to (0.75 − ε)n for an
absolute constant ε > 0. On the other hand, it has also been shown that there exist graphs of degree
approximately 0.68 n that are not globally synchronizing [23].
     In parallel, Ling, Xu and Bandeira [17] showed that if p ≫ log(n)/n1/3 , then the Erdős–Rényi
graph G(n, p) is globally synchronizing with high probability. Kassabov, Strogatz and Townsend
[14] lowered this to p ≫ log2 n/n. Later, Abdalla, Bandeira, Kassabov, Souza, Strogatz, and
Townsend obtained the optimal connectivity threshold [1]. They showed that for every ε > 0 and
p ≥ (1 + ε)(log n)/n, G(n, p) is globally synchronizing w.h.p. Jain, Mizgerd, and Sawhney sharpened
this further by proving global synchronization for the random graph process [11]: if edges are added
one by one, the graph becomes globally synchronizing as soon as it becomes connected.
     Random geometric graphs on manifolds have been considered in [4, 6, 7]. A separate, largely
complementary line of work analyzes the mean-field limit of the Kuramoto model on convergent
graph sequences [3, 20]; this describes the collective dynamics on dense random graphs but does not
address the existence of stable non-synchronized equilibria in the bounded-degree regime.
     It turns out that for expander graphs the controlling quantity is the spectral gap. Abdalla et
al. [1] proved that sufficient spectral expansion forces global synchronization. This implies global
synchronization for d-regular Ramanujan graphs and on random d-regular graphs with d ≥ 600 as
particular cases. Using a normalized-Laplacian condition number bound, McRae [19] showed that
a random d-regular graph globally synchronizes for d ≥ 35. It was conjectured ([1, Section 7], [5,
Conjecture 8]) that a random 3-regular graph chosen uniformly at random is globally synchronizing
with high probability.
     Denoting by G2n,3 a simple random 3-regular graph on [2n], we disprove this conjecture through
the following main theorem:

                                                      
Theorem 1.2. lim P G2n,3 is not globally synchronizing = 1.
                 n→∞




1.2    Overview of the proof
The idea of the proof is to construct a strict local minimizer of E by means of Kantorovich theorem,
which states the existence of such a minimizer in a neighborhood of a point in the phase space with
small gradient and Hessian bounded away from zero. Since the minimizer is localized, we can rule
out the state of full synchronization as the minimum.
    To obtain the configuration with small gradient and bounded from below Hessian we rely on
an induced subgraph Qℓ,R that occurs with high probability, consisting of a cycle of length ℓ ≡ 0
(mod 4), for which each vertex has a pendant edge and a binary tree of depth R − 1 rooted at each
pendant vertex (Figure 1).
    On the cycle, we place the 4-periodic pattern of phases +t, +t, −t, −t, . . . , with t = π − η/2. Then
half of the cycle edges carry the phase difference η and half carry 0. We extend this configuration to

the pendant binary trees by solving the equilibrium equation at each node and to the rest of the
graph by zero.

4     Remarks and Open Questions
We end the paper with some questions we are still not able to answer.

    1. Critical degree. We now know that for d = 3 random regular graphs are not globally
       synchronizing with high probability and that for d ≥ 35 they are. So, the question is what
       is the smallest degree d for which random regular graphs are globally synchronizing. And
       even more, is there a critical degree dc such that w.h.p. random regular graphs are globally
       synchronizing for every d ≥ dc and they are w.h.p. not globally synchronizing for every d < dc ?
       Numerical simulations and heuristic arguments suggest the existence of dc with value dc = 4,
       but both the existence and the value are not proven. Accordingly, our proof strategy breaks
       down for d = 4.

    2. Macroscopic non-synchronizing states. In our proof we find non-synchronized states that
       are essentially microscopic: the number of nodes with phases above a given threshold is O(1)
       as n → ∞. DeVille and Ermentrout [8] provided a numerical study on small 3-regular graphs
       indicating the existence of macroscopic non-synchronized stable states (i.e. the empirical
       distribution of the phases covering the whole S1 ) with high probability. Their numerics suggest
       that non-synchronized states are organized by winding; a pattern visibly wraps around the
       origin a definite number of times, and the energies cluster by that count. They also found
       that states with long links (phase difference greater than π2 ) are common for the 3-regular
       graphs sampled. We have not been able to show this, and the question is to prove rigorously
       the existence of such states.

    3. Volume of the basins of attraction. In our proofs we show the existence of non-synchronized
       states with high probability, but we do not address the question of the probability of achieving
       those states when starting with a random initial condition. So, the question is whether there
       is (asymptotically) positive probability for the choice of initial condition to end up with a
       non-trivial equilibrium. The numerical simulations performed in [8] suggest that with positive
       probability over the choices of initial phase conditions, there is convergence to a macroscopic
       twisted state.
