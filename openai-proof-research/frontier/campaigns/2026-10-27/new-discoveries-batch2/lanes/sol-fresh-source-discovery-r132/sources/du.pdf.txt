We study the problem of sampling from the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick (SK) model. This is an Ising
model µ βW with the random interaction matrix βW, for W ∼ GOE( N ), that is, Wij = Wji ∼
N 0, N1 . More explicitly, the density of this distribution is given by

There is a vast literature dedicated to the study of Ising models, due to their connections to physics,
optimization, statistical inference, among others; see [WJ08, MM09, FV17] and references therein.
The SK model in particular has provided a rich testbed for techniques, and has been the subject
of much work for several decades. Some landmarks in the study of the SK model are the Parisi
formula predicting its free energy [Par79, Par80b, Par80a], foundational works towards rigorous
study [GT02, Gue03, ASS03], Talagrand’s proof of the Parisi formula [Tal06], and Panchenko’s
resolution of the ultrametricity conjecture [Pan13]; we refer the reader to [Tal10, Tal11, Pan12] for
more details.
      Of special interest has been the task of sampling from the SK model in the high-temperature
regime 0 ⩽ β < 1. It is believed that the canonical Glauber dynamics Markov chain mixes rapidly
at this temperature [SZ81, SZ82, MPV87b].
      Proving this has been a major open problem in the past decade, as outlined in [Sim20] and
[BDL+ 26, Problem 15]. We defer a detailed discussion of prior results to Section 1.2.
      While rapid mixing of the fixed-temperature Glauber dynamics remains open, in this paper,
we study simulated annealing, a simple modification of the standard Markov chain Monte Carlo
paradigm that is frequently used in practice across a variety of areas, including VLSI design, com-
binatorial optimization, and scheduling among others; see [KGV83, SS85, LA87, WLL88, JAMS89,
YRN94, KAJ94, HJJ03].
      The simulated annealing algorithm is a time-dependent Markov chain where each step is
from the Glauber dynamics Markov chain, but the inverse temperature starts at 0 and is slowly
incremented to β over the course of running the Markov chain. The intuitive picture a reader
should keep in mind is that while a Markov chain to sample from a target distribution µ βW may
not mix from a generic initialization, it would nevertheless sample from µ βW when initialized at
µ ( β − δ )W .

1.1   Technical overview
In this section, we provide an overview of how we reduce Theorem 1.2 to establishing appropriate
covariance bounds using localization schemes. Localization schemes are a principled approach
to designing measure decompositions, providing a measure-valued martingale (µt )0⩽t⩽T with µ0
being the Gibbs measure. For this decomposition to be helpful in proving a weak Poincaré inequality
for the Gibbs measure, we will desire the following two properties:

   1. First, the (random) endpoint measure µ T is “nice” in some sense. In our case, this will mean
      proving that µ T satisfies a Poincaré inequality with high probability, say at least 1 − ε.

   2. Second, we may establish some pathwise control that will allow us to lift this niceness back
      up, to proving a weak Poincaré inequality for the base measure µ0 . In our case, this will
      mean proving that with high probability, say at least 1 − ε, over the localization path, the
      covariances of all the µt are spectrally bounded by some constant (independent of N).

Given this, we can execute the following string of inequalities, for E being the event that the
endpoint measure satisfies a Poincaré inequality, so Pr[E ] ⩾ 1 − ε:

                                   E µ ( f , f ) ⩾ EE µ T ( f , f )

In the above ≳ hides poly( N ) factors. The non-trivial inequalities here are the second and fourth:
the second uses the high-probability Poincaré inequality for the endpoint measure, and the fourth
uses the pathwise control. The latter uses Itô’s formula to compute the time-derivative of the ex-
pected variance, and controls this with the aforementioned “pathwise control” of the (µt ) measures;
we refer the interested reader to [CE22, Section 4] and [HMRW24, Section 6] for more details.

    At a high level, we prove the above as follows. We consider a prediction K −1 for the covariance
matrix based on the Thouless–Anderson–Palmer (TAP) approximation to the free energy, the
“inverse TAP Hessian”. This matrix has a term linear in the disorder, and a handful of terms
involving the mean of the measure. In an entry of the K · Cov that involves an element Wij of the
disorder and some cumulant of the Gibbs measure, we may then decouple the cumulant from
the disorder using the cavity method, at the cost of introducing a small (random) external field.
We can now Taylor expand any given cumulant in terms of this small random external field; this
introduces terms involving zero-field SK models (on N − 1 spins) and Gaussians independent of
the disorder defining these models. Gaussian concentration allows us to control the error in these
approximations, using only the crude bounds that are yielded by overlap concentration, which
are later refined using other means. Finally, showing that this matrix K is bounded away from 0
requires other machinery, which we may establish using techniques from previous work [Cel24].
    We further believe that many of our techniques to prove covariance bounds should extend to
the entire replica-symmetric regime [AT78] given sufficiently good overlap concentration, which
might follow from recent results [Lop26, KN26]. We have made no attempt to do this extension.
    We must also show that at sufficiently large T, π T satisfies a Poincaré inequality with high
probability. This near-immediately follows from the techniques of the previous work of Bandeira–El
Alaoui–Rödder [BER26].

1.2   Related work

Sampling from spin glasses. It was shown in a series of works [BB19, EKZ22, CE22] that the
Glauber dynamics satisfies a Poincaré inequality for β < 1/4, which was improved on by Anari–
Koehler–Vuong [AKV24] to show that it succeeds up to β ≈ 0.295; both of these proofs use

                                                    4
stochastic localization. More recently, the “Bochner method” was used by Wang [Wan26] and in a
follow-up work by Boban–Li–Oveis Gharan [BLO26] to establish fast mixing up to β = 12 + ε (for
some small constant ε).
     An independent line of work pioneered by El Alaoui, Montanari, and Sellke [EMS22] studies
efficient sampling via algorithmic stochastic localization, based on simulating the stochastic differential
equation obtained from the time-reversal of (3). It was first shown in [EMS22, Cel24] that this
algorithm achieves (normalized) Wasserstein distance o (1) to the Gibbs measure. The work of
Huang, Montanari, and Pham [HMP24] illustrates how these ideas can be used to design algorithms
achieving a total variation distance guarantee of o (1) using this technique in the related spherical
p-spin model. Recently, works of Davies, Lee, Sandhu, and Shi [DLSS26a, LSS26] extended these
ideas to design an algorithm that achieves total variation distance o (1) to the Gibbs measure for the
SK model.
     The same set of authors has an accompanying line of work [DLSS26b], which builds on these
ideas to establish a (O( N ), o (1))-weak Poincaré inequality for the Gibbs measure when β < 1/2.
However, their quantitative weakness parameter is not small enough to establish success of
simulated annealing, and they must use the Langevin diffusion on another distribution to generate
a sufficiently warm start.
     Beyond the SK model, the “2-spin” model, the problem of sampling from the more general class
of p-spin models has been studied. These are defined by coefficients ( β p )0⩽ p⩽ p∗ for some constant

Our techniques. We now discuss the works that inspired our techniques. The local-to-global
principle reducing a weak Poincaré inequality to high-probability covariance bounds was in-
spired by the localization schemes based approach to proving Poincaré inequalities for Markov
chain [ALOV24, EKZ22, CE22, LMRW24, AKV24]. The use of these methods to establish weak
Poincaré inequalities appears in the works of [EEGP23, HMRW24, DLSS26b].
    The specific localization scheme of choice—stochastic localization—was introduced by Eldan
[Eld13], and subsequently developed extensively in work on the KLS conjecture [LV24, Che21,
KL22, SZ26, BKL26].
    Our proof of the covariance bounds relies on proving that the covariance is close to a prediction
for K −1 that arises from the TAP equations for the free energy of the SK model, developed by
Thouless–Anderson–Palmer [TAP77]. To prove that K −1 is bounded, we need to prove that K is
bounded away from 0, for which we use an iterative procedure based on approximate message
passing (AMP), a technique now used widely in statistical inference [DMM09, BM11, DAM16,
MV21], and in studying spin glasses [Bol14, DS19, Mon19, CT21, EMS21, ES22, HS24].
    The proof that the covariance is close to K −1 relies on the classical cavity method—comparing
a Gibbs distribution of a Hamiltonian to a version where one site is removed—introduced in
statistical physics literature by Mézard–Parisi–Virasoro [MPV87a], developed by Aizenman–Sims–
Starr [ASS03], and extensively used thereafter by Talagrand [Tal10, Tal11] among others.
Concurrent work by OpenAI. The problem of sampling from the SK model was one among 722
claimed results released very recently by OpenAI. In addition to establishing a spectral gap for
β < 1, they prove cutoff from a worst-case initialization, and establish a mixing time of n5/3+o(1) at
β = 1. We provide a short summary of our interpretation of their proof of a spectral gap [Ope26]
below.
    They begin essentially by proving a weak Poincaré inequality, albeit with a much better weak-
ness parameter than ours. Using the standard stochastic localization that we do, they show that the
covariance is bounded on the localization path with probability 1 − e−cN , and that the endpoint
measure satisfies a Poincaré inequality with probability 1 − e−cN . We remark that their proof
bounding the covariance significantly differs from ours. They show that under a certain “stability”
criterion, the distribution has bounded covariance.
    The aforementioned stability criterion requires local convexity at every approximate fixed point
of the TAP free energy—this differs from previous results [Cel24], where it is shown that a late
AMP iterate approximates the mean well, and that one has local convexity at this AMP iterate. In
particular, their Lemma 5.7 uses this to conclude that there is a unique TAP fixed point.
    We remark that such conclusions are expected to be false in the p-spin model (in the regime
with metastable states); it seems likely that our proof is more flexible in this regard, and indeed a

1.3   Future directions
We propose a few directions for further study.
Mixing in SK model with a field. An interesting extension of the results in this paper would be
to establish mixing in the SK model under a field up to the critical threshold.

Problem 1.6 (Mixing under the de Almeida–Thouless line). In the presence of a field h · 1 for
h ∈ R, for W ∼ GOE( N ), prove that simulated annealing samples from the Ising model µ βW,h·1 in
polynomial time as long as 0 ⩽ β < β ⋆ (h), where β ⋆ (h) is described by the de Almeida–Thouless
line from [AT78].

    A qualitative version of the above is due to [BER26]. Beyond settling a conjectured spin glass
threshold, the above question is of interest in the connection between sampling from the SK model,
and the efficacy of Markov chains at optimal inference in the spiked Wigner model via a connection
made by [LMR+ 24, RW26].
Counting vs. sampling in average-case models. A conventional wisdom in the classical sampling
literature is that the algorithmic task of computing a partition function has roughly the same
complexity as sampling from a Gibbs distribution, and there is a formal equivalence [JVV86, SJ89]
in that an algorithm for computing the partition function for a family of Hamiltonians gives a
sampling algorithm for the corresponding Gibbs distributions.
    In the average-case setting, the situation is murkier. As a running example, consider the
spherical p-spin glass model, where the Hamiltonian is a degree-p random polynomial whose
coefficients are drawn independently from a Gaussian distribution. It is believed that Markov chain-
based methods should fail to sample from the Gibbs distribution when the inverse temperature
exceeds the shattering threshold β sh , and the best sampling algorithms work up to a constant of
the threshold [HMP24, HMRW24]. On the other hand, an algorithm for computing the partition
function was given well beyond β sh up to the “replica symmetric threshold” in a work of [BHL+ 26],
which suggests that spin glass models may exhibit phases where sampling is hard, but computing
the partition function is tractable. However, this story was complicated by the recent work [ZSSJ26],
which uses a quantum-inspired algorithm to sample in the shattered phase.
    We consider it an interesting direction to understand what the true algorithmic threshold is for
sampling, as well as what the threshold is for Markov chains.
Certifiable sampling. We say that a sampling algorithm for an Ising model is certifiable if there is
a poly( N )-time verifier that takes as input the interaction matrix of an Ising model, and outputs

                                                 8
either yes or abstain, and further outputs yes with high probability over the disorder. When the
verifier outputs yes, the sampling algorithm must additionally (approximately) sample from the
target distribution.
    Many of the previous results for mixing in Ising models [EKZ22, CE22, AKV24, Wan26] that
yielded samplers for the SK model when β < 1/2 in fact give certifiable samplers since the proofs of
mixing rely on spectral conditions on the interaction matrix, or other closely related matrices. The
proof of mixing in this paper relies significantly more on the randomness of the disorder compared
to previous works, and hence does not yield an efficiently checkable condition.

Problem 1.7. What is the threshold β ⋆ such that there is a certifiable sampler from the SK model at
inverse temperature β < β ⋆ ?

    We anticipate that certifiable samplers are a useful primitive for certain downstream applications
of sampling to statistical inference and privacy, where it may be essential to be certain that one is
indeed sampling from the desired target distribution. Beyond applications, if β ⋆ is indeed equal
to 1, we anticipate that it would yield significantly simpler proofs of the results in this paper. In a
world where β ⋆ is not equal to 1, an interesting takeaway is that the proof of success of any sampler
for the SK model necessarily strongly exploits the distributional assumption of the input.
