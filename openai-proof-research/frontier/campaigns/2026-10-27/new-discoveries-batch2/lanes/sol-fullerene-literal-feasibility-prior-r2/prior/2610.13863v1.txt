                                                                                   1. Introduction

                                             In this paper we extend to the setting of signed graphs the notion of degree similarity,
                                          introduced and investigated in [18] for unsigned graphs.
                                             A central problem in spectral graph theory is to understand which graphs are deter-
                                          mined by their spectrum. Schwenk [30] showed that almost all trees are not, and the
                                          switching technique of Godsil and McKay [17] has become one of the main tools for con-
                                          structing cospectral graphs. Nevertheless, van Dam and Haemers [10] conjectured that
                                          almost all graphs are determined by their spectrum.
                                             In this context, Wang, Li, Lu and Xu [34] considered a stronger notion than cospec-
                                          trality, and studied graphs determined by their generalized characteristic polynomial
                                          det(tI − A + µD), introduced in [9], where A and D are the adjacency and degree ma-
                                          trices, respectively; two graphs sharing this polynomial are cospectral with respect to the
                                          adjacency, the Laplacian and the signless Laplacian matrix, and have the same degree
                                          sequence. In [34, Section 5] the authors observe that if the adjacency and degree matrices
                                          of two graphs are conjugate by the same (orthogonal) matrix, then the two graphs have
                                          the same generalized characteristic polynomial, and they ask whether the converse holds.
                                          As observed in [33], a negative answer to this question is already contained in [11].
                                            In [18], pairs of graphs whose adjacency matrices and degree matrices are simultane-
                                          ously similar were called degree similar, and it was shown that, while there are infinitely

no two non-isomorphic trees are degree similar (using a result of McKay [26]). In par-
ticular, trees are determined by their degree similarity class, which shows that degree
similarity is a genuinely stronger notion than generalized cospectrality and is of indepen-

   While [18] provides several tools for constructing degree similar pairs of graphs, many
questions remain open. For instance, [18, Problem 27] asks whether non-isomorphic uni-
cyclic graphs can be degree similar. This question is also addressed in [14], where several
families of unicyclic graphs are shown to be determined by their degree similarity class,
and a negative answer has been given in the recent preprint [42]. In [14] the authors
introduce orthogonally degree-similar graphs (see also [13]), requiring the similarity to be
realized by an orthogonal matrix, as in the original question of [34]. Since adjacency and
degree matrices are symmetric, this notion coincides with degree similarity, also for signed
graphs (see Lemma 3.9), as was implicit in [18]. Degree similarity is also related to the
multivariate graph spectra introduced by Wang and Zhao [35] and further studied in [38].
   Signed graphs [39] have a well-developed spectral theory (see, for instance, [5, 41]), and
many classical problems and tools have a natural signed counterpart. To mention only
those relevant for this paper, a signed version of Godsil–McKay switching was developed
in [3], partial transposition was used in [4] to construct cospectral signed graphs, and
spectral characterization problems for signed graphs were studied, for example, in [6,
7]. We refer to [40, 41] for further background on signed graphs. In general, spectral
determination is harder for signed graphs than for unsigned ones: a single underlying
graph carries exponentially many signatures, and several spectral invariants of unsigned
graphs lose their direct combinatorial meaning in the presence of negative edges. For this
reason, stronger notions of cospectrality for signed graphs have also been considered in
the literature; see, for instance, [19, 24] for the generalized spectrum of signed graphs,
and [23] for signed graphs that are cospectral with respect to both the adjacency and the
Laplacian matrix. However, to the best of our knowledge, degree similarity has not been
studied for signed graphs, and this is the subject of the present paper.
   A signed graph Γ carries two natural notions of vertex degree: the degree of a vertex in
the underlying graph, and its net-degree, that is, the number of positive edges minus the
number of negative edges incident with it. Accordingly, there are two diagonal matrices
associated with Γ: the degree matrix DΓ and the net-degree matrix DΓ± . The notion of
degree similarity can therefore be extended to signed graphs in two ways: degree similarity,
namely the simultaneous similarity of the adjacency matrices and of the degree matrices,
and net-degree similarity, namely the simultaneous similarity of the adjacency matrices
and of the net-degree matrices. When the signed graphs are all-positive, the degree and
the net-degree matrices coincide, and both notions reduce to degree similarity in the
sense of [18]. We show that degree similarity is invariant under switching, and implies
cospectrality with respect to both the adjacency and the Laplacian matrix; hence it is
natural to ask whether it determines a signed graph up to switching isomorphism. On the
other hand, net-degree similarity depends on the chosen representative of the switching
class, and implies cospectrality with respect to both the adjacency and the net Laplacian
matrix.

   Our main contributions are the following. We prove that every signed graph which is
degree similar to a signed tree is itself a signed tree; combined with the result of [18] on
unsigned trees, this implies that signed trees are determined by their degree similarity
class. While the first statement is immediate for unsigned graphs, it is not for signed
ones, and indeed it already fails for unicyclic signed graphs: a balanced cycle of even
length is degree similar to the disjoint union of two signed cycles of half length, one
balanced and one unbalanced. In particular, in contrast with the unsigned case (see
[42]), unicyclic signed graphs need not be determined by their degree similarity class. We
then develop a set of tools for constructing pairs of (net-)degree-similar signed graphs,
obtained by adapting to this setting Godsil–McKay switching, partial transposition, and
block constructions; when applied to the unsigned case, they include and extend several
constructions from [18]. As a byproduct of these constructions, we obtain infinite families
of unicyclic signed graphs with properties which have no counterpart among unsigned
graphs. For instance, the signed local switching produces, already on nine vertices, a
pair of net-degree similar unicyclic signed graphs with non-isomorphic underlying graphs,
answering the net-degree analogue of [18, Problem 27]. Moreover, the rooted product
turns this pair, as well as the degree similar pair of cycles mentioned above, into infinite
families, yielding in particular unicyclic signed graphs, also non-regular, which are not
determined by their degree similarity class.
In Section 2 we recall some preliminaries on signed graphs.
In Section 3 we formally introduce degree similarity and net-degree similarity, and we
give a characterization of both notions in terms of traces of words in the adjacency and
(net-)degree matrices (Proposition 3.4); it follows that (net-)degree similar signed graphs
have the same generalized Zagreb (net)-index. These criteria are useful to certify that
two signed graphs are not (net-)degree similar, as we do for small pairs of signed graphs
with the same generalized characteristic polynomial (Example 3.5). We show that there
exist non-isomorphic trees admitting signatures that are net-degree similar (Example 3.7),
whereas signed trees are determined by their degree similarity class (Theorem 3.10), and
that unicyclic signed graphs need not be (Example 3.12).
In Section 4 we introduce additional conditions on the signed version of Godsil–McKay
switching developed in [3], which ensure that not only the adjacency spectrum, but also
(net-)degree similarity is preserved. In the unsigned case, our conditions extend the GM∗
property of Haemers and Spence [20], and include the construction of [18, Lemma 9] as a
strict special case (see Subsection 4.4). In the signed setting, the construction produces,
already on nine vertices, net-degree similar unicyclic signed graphs with non-isomorphic
underlying graphs (Example 4.2).
In Section 5, the partial transposition techniques introduced in [4] to construct cospectral
signed graphs are refined so as to preserve the (net-)degree similarity class. In particular,
many of the families of cospectral pairs given in [4] are in fact (net-)degree similar.
In Section 6, we develop tools for producing new pairs of (net-)degree-similar signed graphs
from a given one, whose adjacency matrices are used as blocks of the adjacency matrices
of the new pairs. We obtain several constructions, which recover, for instance, some of
those given in [18, Section 8]. In particular, (net-)degree similarity is preserved by rooted
4                                M. CAVALERI AND A. DONNO

products, provided that the rooted signed graphs attached to vertices of the same (net-
)degree are the same; applied to the pairs of Examples 3.12 and 4.2, these constructions
yield infinitely many unicyclic signed graphs, also non-regular, which are not determined
by their degree similarity class, as well as infinitely many pairs of net-degree similar
unicyclic signed graphs with non-isomorphic underlying graphs (Example 6.2).

The signed graph Γ is k-(net-)regular if dΓ (v) = k for every v ∈ VΓ , and (net-)regular if

extension the signed graph Γ = (G, σ), is called balanced (see [21]) if every closed walk in
Γ contains an even number of negative edges, or equivalently, if σ is switching equivalent
to the all-positive signature.

respectively. When the context is clear, we will omit the subscript and simply write I and
J. A matrix M is said to be block diagonal if it has the form M = diag(M1 , M2 , . . . , Mk ),
where M1 , . . . , Mk are square matrices. In other words, M has M1 , . . . , Mk as its diagonal
blocks and zero blocks elsewhere. A real matrix M is said to be normal if M M t = M t M ,
where M t denotes the transpose of M .

                  3. (Net-)degree similarity and determination

   We introduce the two main definitions, namely the two natural possible generalizations
to signed graphs of the concept of degree similarity.
Definition 3.1. Let Γi be a signed graph, and let AΓi and DΓi denote, respectively, its
adjacency and degree matrix with respect to a common ordering of the vertices, for i = 1, 2.
We say that Γ1 and Γ2 are degree similar if there exists a real, invertible matrix B such
that
(3.1)                   B −1 AΓ1 B = AΓ2     and    B −1 DΓ1 B = DΓ2 .
Definition 3.2. Let Γi be a signed graph, and let AΓi and DΓ±i denote, respectively, its
adjacency and net-degree matrix with respect to a common ordering of the vertices, for
i = 1, 2. We say that Γ1 and Γ2 are net-degree similar if there exists a real, invertible
matrix B such that
(3.2)                   B −1 AΓ1 B = AΓ2     and    B −1 DΓ±1 B = DΓ±2 .
Finally, we say that Γ1 and Γ2 are simultaneously degree similar and net-degree similar
if there exists a real, invertible matrix B such that both Eq. (3.1) and Eq. (3.2) hold.

   Observe that both definitions are independent of the ordering of the vertices, which
only changes the matrix B by a permutation matrix, and that two (net-)degree similar
signed graphs have the same multiset of (net-)degrees, since similar diagonal matrices
have the same diagonal entries up to a permutation.

  Another natural question is whether (net-)degree similarity is invariant under switching
equivalence. The following result provides the answer.
Proposition 3.6. If two signed graphs Γ1 and Γ2 are degree similar and Γ′1 is switching
isomorphic to Γ1 , then Γ′1 and Γ2 are degree similar. By contrast, net-degree similarity
depends on the representative of the switching equivalence class.

Proof. If Γ′1 is switching isomorphic to Γ1 , there is a signed permutation matrix P S with
(P S)−1 AΓ′1 P S = AΓ1 , and then (P S)−1 DΓ′1 P S = DΓ1 , since switching does not change
the degrees. Hence, if B satisfies Eq. (3.1), so does P SB in place of B for Γ′1 and Γ2 . By
contrast, let Γ be an all-positive k-regular graph, k ≥ 1, and let Γ′ be obtained from Γ by
switching at a single vertex, so that Γ′ is not net-regular. Then Γ is net-degree similar to
itself, whereas Γ′ and Γ are not net-degree similar, by Remark 3.3.                        □

   Accordingly, we say that a signed graph Γ is determined by its degree similarity class if
every signed graph that is degree similar to Γ is switching isomorphic to Γ. We say that
Γ is determined by its net-degree similarity class if every signed graph that is net-degree
similar to Γ is isomorphic to Γ.

degree similar: a negative answer has recently been given in [42]. For unsigned graphs, the
question is equivalent to asking whether unicyclic graphs are determined by their degree
similarity class, since an unsigned graph that is Laplacian cospectral with a unicyclic
graph is itself unicyclic, being connected with the same number of vertices and edges.
For signed graphs, we do not know whether two unicyclic signed graphs which are not
switching isomorphic can be degree similar (by Proposition 3.6 and [42], they would have
to be unbalanced); on the other hand, we exhibit pairs of net-degree similar unicyclic
signed graphs with non-isomorphic underlying graphs (see Examples 4.2 and 6.2), and
unicyclic signed graphs are not, in general, determined by their degree similarity class, as
the following example shows.

where B is a square matrix of order h for which there exists a unitary matrix U satisfying
U ∗ BU = B t .
If every row and every column of B have the same number of nonzero entries, then Γ and
Γτ are degree similar. If every row and every column have the same sum, then Γ and Γτ
are net-degree similar.

Proof. By [32, Theorem 5], there exists a real orthogonal matrix V such that V t BV = B t
and therefore V t B t V = B. This easily implies that V t Ci,j V = Ci,j t for all i, j = 1, . . . , q
and AΓτ = Qt AΓ Q, where Q = diag(V, . . . , V ). If the number of nonzero entries in each
row and column of B and B t is constant, then the degree of any vertex within the same
part is the same in Γ and is preserved under the partial transpose Γτ . The same conclusion
holds for the net-degree in the case where the sum of the entries in each row or column
of B and B t is constant. The result then follows from Proposition 3.14.                          □
