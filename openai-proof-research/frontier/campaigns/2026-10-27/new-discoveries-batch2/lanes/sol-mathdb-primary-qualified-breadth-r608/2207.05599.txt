Abstract
    

Given an integer partition of  $n$ , we consider the impartial combinatorial game LCTR in which moves consist of removing either the left column or top row of its Young diagram.
We show that for both normal and misère play, the optimal strategy can consist mostly of mirroring the opponent’s moves.
We also establish that both LCTR and Downright are domestic as well as returnable, and on the other hand neither tame nor forced.
    

For both games, those structural observations allow for computing the Sprague-Grundy value any position in  $O(\log(n))$  time, assuming that the time unit allows for reading an integer, or performing a basic arithmetic operation.
This improves on the previously known bound of  $O(n)$  due to Ilić (2019).
We also cover some other complexity measures of both games, such as state-space complexity, and number of leaves and nodes in the corresponding game tree.
  


Keywords: 
combinatorial game , partition games , computational complexity , Sprague Grundy

1 Introduction and background



Combinatorial game theory is a large and growing field that includes in its scope a wide range of game types, generally focusing on two-player games in which both players have perfect information and there are no moves of chance.
We restrict ourselves to two-player combinatorial games that end in finitely many moves.
Every game ends in one of the two possible outcomes: one of the players wins, while the other loses.
Under normal play, the winner is the player who makes the last move, leaving their opponent with no move. Under misère rules, the win condition is reversed.



The main question in combinatorial game theory is, given some position and optimal play, which player has a winning strategy? An  $\mathcal{N}$ -position (winning position) is one in which the player whose turn it is can guarantee a win.
The complementary positions are  $\mathcal{P}$ -positions (losing positions) in which the player that goes second can guarantee a win.
The game is effectively solved if we can classify all of its positions as either  $\mathcal{N}$  or  $\mathcal{P}$ .
Sprague [1, 2] and Grundy [3] introduced a method of quantifying game positions for normal play impartial games, i.e., those in which both players
have the same possible moves in each position. These Sprague-Grundy values are a generalization of winning and losing positions.
Furthermore, these values are instrumental in the theory of disjunctive sums of impartial games.
This is why recent research on impartial games is predominantly focused on the Sprague-Grundy values rather than on  $\mathcal{P}/\mathcal{N}$ -positions.
For more information on combinatorial game theory, including impartial games and disjunctive sums, see [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9].



Over the last half century, a number of researchers including Dailly et al [10], Abuku and Tada [11], Irie [12], and Sato [13] have investigated games on (integer) partitions. In this paper we study the impartial finite game introduced in [14], where two players take turns removing from a given Young diagram  $\lambda$  either leftmost column or topmost row, hence the name LCTR.
We show that for both normal and misère play of LCTR, the optimal strategy can consist mostly of mirroring the opponent’s moves.
In terms of structural classification of normal-misère game pairs as introduced in Gurvich et al. [15] we
establish that both games are domestic as well as returnable, and on the other hand neither tame nor forced.



In [14], the authors studied the behaviour of some specifically structured sub-families of LCTR games (see Lemma 4) and devised a procedure deciding which player has the winning strategy on a given Young diagram and determining the Sprague-Grundy value associated to the Young diagram in  $O(n)$  time units [16]11
            1

For both games, our structural insights allow for computing the Sprague-Grundy value any position in  $O(\log(n))$  time units.
We also cover some other complexity measures of both games, such as state-space complexity, and number of leaves and nodes in the corresponding game tree.



In Section 2 we cover the basic definitions needed for the study of LCTR and related games.
In Section 3 we establish the properties that are common to both normal and misère play of LCTR.
In Sections 4 and 5
we take the study of the normal form of LCTR, as well as the normal form of a game called Downright that is closely related to the misère form of LCTR, and show that for both games, the optimal strategy can consist mostly of mirroring the opponent’s moves.
In Section 6 we establish a structural classification of both LCTR and Downright within the framework of Gurvich et al.
Section 7 focuses on computing the exact Sprague-Grundy values for both games in  $O(\log n)$  time units.
In the same section we also cover some other complexity measures of both games, such as state-space complexity, and number of leaves and nodes in the corresponding game tree.
Finally, we discuss future research directions as well as some related open problems in Section 8.





2 Preliminaries



In this section, we provide background information on partitions and Young diagrams, impartial games, Sprague-Grundy theory, and complexity theory.




2.1 Young diagrams and Partitions



A partition of a nonnegative integer  $n$  is a sequence  $\lambda=\llbracket\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r}\rrbracket$  of integers, where  $\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{r}>0$  and  $\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{r}=n$ .
We also write  $\lambda\vdash n$  and we say that  $\lambda$  is a partition of  $n$ .
The  $\lambda_{j}$ ’s are called the parts of  $\lambda$ . When some of the parts of a partition are equal, we may abbreviate  $\llbracket\lambda_{1}^{m_{1}},\dots,\lambda_{k}^{m_{k}}\rrbracket$ , where  $\lambda_{j}$  appears  $m_{j}\geq 0$  times.
The Young diagram of  $\lambda$  is a left-justified array of boxes in which the  $j$ th row from the top contains  $\lambda_{j}$  boxes.
Partitions are drawn as Young diagrams, and no distinction is made between a partition and its Young diagram.
The Young diagram of  $\llbracket 6,4,3,3,1,1\rrbracket=\llbracket 6,4,3^{2},1^{2}\rrbracket$  is shown in Figure 1.
The only partition of 0 is the empty sum, which we write as  $\llbracket\rrbracket$ . We define the conjugate partition of  $\lambda$  as  $\lambda^{\prime}=\llbracket\lambda^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{\prime}_{\lambda_{1}}\rrbracket$ 
where  $\lambda^{\prime}_{i}$  is the largest integer such that  $\lambda_{\lambda^{\prime}_{i}}\geq i$ .


Figure 1: The Young diagram of  $\llbracket 6,4^{2},2,1^{2}\rrbracket$  



If  $i$  and  $j$  are such that  $\lambda_{i}>0$  and  $0<j\leq\lambda_{i}$  then the box in the  $i$ th row and  $j$ th column of the Young diagram is called the  $(i,j)$ th box of the Young diagram.
The subpartition of  $\lambda$  corresponding to the Young diagram rooted at the  $(i,j)$ th box of  $\lambda$  is  $\lambda[i-1,j-1]=\llbracket\lambda_{i}-j+1,\lambda_{i+1}-j+1,\dots\rrbracket$ , where trailing nonpositive entries are removed.
We define a corner of a partition  $\lambda$  to be a pair  $(i,j)$  such that  $\lambda[i,j]=\llbracket 1\rrbracket$ . If  $(i,j)$  is a corner of  $\lambda$  then we define  $\lambda[i,j]$  to be a corner subpartition of  $\lambda$ . If  $i$  and  $j$  are positive integers not satisfying the above conditions, then we define  $\lambda[i-1,j-1]$  to be the empty partition  $\llbracket\rrbracket$ .
Thus  $\lambda[0,0]=\lambda$  for any partition.
A key property of the subpartitions is that for all non-negative integers  $i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}$  we have the identity




 $\displaystyle(\lambda[i_{1},j_{1}])[i_{2},j_{2}]=\lambda[i_{1}+i_{2},j_{1}+j_{2}].$

2.2 Impartial games and Sprague-Grundy values



In this paper our focus will be exclusively on impartial finite combinatorial games, which we will refer to simply as games.
The positions in the games we study are partitions or Young diagrams, which we describe in the following subsection.
A subposition of a game is a position that can be reached after a nonnegative number of moves. Every game begins in some position, so we sometimes use the terms interchangeably.



A terminal position is a position from which no further moves are possible.
Under normal play of a combinatorial game, a player who reaches a terminal position is the winner.
Under misère play, a player who reaches a terminal position is the loser.
Throughout this paper, we assume normal play unless otherwise indicated.



Let  $G$  be a game under normal (misère) play.
We define the set of its winning positions  $\mathcal{N}_{G}$ ,
and the set of its losing positions  $\mathcal{P}_{G}$ , as follows:


1. 


Terminal positions are in  $\mathcal{P}_{G}$  ( $\mathcal{N}_{G}$ ).


2. 


A position is in  $\mathcal{P}_{G}$ , if none of its moves leads to a position in  $\mathcal{P}_{G}$ .


3. 


A position is in  $\mathcal{N}_{G}$ , if at least one of its moves leads to a position in  $\mathcal{P}_{G}$ .



We denote the above sets simply as  $\mathcal{P}$  and  $\mathcal{N}$ , whenever the game in question is clear from the context.



A two player combinatorial game is impartial if both players have the same possible moves in each position.
Let  $A$  be a position of such a game  $G$ .
If there is a move from  $A$  to position  $B$ , we write  $A\to B$ .
For a finite set  $S$  of nonnegative integers the minimum excluded value of  $S$ 
is defined as the smallest nonnegative integer that is not in  $S$  and
is denoted by  $\Mex(S)$ .
The Sprague-Grundy value ( $\mathbb{SG}$  value) of  $A$  is defined recursively by




 $\displaystyle\mathbb{SG}(A)=\Mex(\{\mathbb{SG}(B)\mid A\to B\}).$ 

(2)




A position of  $\mathbb{SG}$  value  $t$  is called a  $t$ -position.
For the case of terminal positions we have  $\Mex(\emptyset)=0$ , hence such positions have  $\mathbb{SG}$  value  $0$ .



If  $G_{1}$  and  $G_{2}$  are games, their disjunctive sum  $G_{1}+G_{2}$  is defined to be the game in which players in turn choose a summand ( $G_{1}$  or  $G_{2}$ ) and make a move in that game. Play continues until terminal positions are reached in both summands. It is well known that  $\mathbb{SG}(G_{1})=\mathbb{SG}(G_{2})$  if and only if  $G_{1}+G_{2}\in\mathcal{P}$  (see [8]).



 $\mathbb{SG}$  values provide a useful mechanism for determining winning and losing positions for impartial games under normal play.
In particular, the  $\mathcal{P}$ -positions are precisely those that have  $\mathbb{SG}$  value  $0$  while the  $\mathcal{N}$ -positions are those with nonzero  $\mathbb{SG}$  values.
The Sprague-Grundy theorem [9] shows that given the sum of impartial games under normal play, we can replace every game with a pile of Nim, with the size corresponding to the  $\mathbb{SG}$  value of the game.
Doing so will not change the  $\mathcal{P}/\mathcal{N}$ -positions of the sum.



For normal play, one can partition the positions to equivalence classes with respect to their  $\mathbb{SG}$  value.
For misère play such equivalence classes, for which we can replace one game with another within the sum, are different and far more difficult to characterize in general.
The equivalence classes for misère are called misère quotients and form a commutative monoid [17]. Hence while one can study misère quotients, one can also focus on just  $\mathcal{P}/\mathcal{N}$ -positions.



We make use of the following corollary, which follows from the definition of  $\mathbb{SG}$  value. For detailed coverage of combinatorial game theory we refer the reader to [9].


Corollary 1.


Let  $A,B$  be positions such that  $A\to B$ .
Then  $\mathbb{SG}(A)\neq\mathbb{SG}(B)$ .






2.3 Tame, pet, domestic, and miserable impartial games



As early as in 1956 Grundy and Smith [18] noticed that
playing a game under the misère convention may be difficult in general.
In [15] Gurvich and Ho focus on the exceptions, that is, on the games
for which functions  $\mathbb{SG}$  and  $\mathbb{SG}^{-}$  are closely related.



The misère  $\mathbb{SG}$  value  $\mathbb{SG}^{-}(x)$  of a position  $x$  in a game  $G$ 
is defined by the same recursion (2), but the initialization is different:
 $\mathbb{SG}^{-}(x)=1$  (rather than  $\mathbb{SG}^{-}(x)=0$ ) for all terminal positions.



A position  $x$  will be called an  $(i,j)$ -position if  $\mathbb{SG}(x)=i$  and  $\mathbb{SG}^{-}(x)=j$ .
We say that  $(\mathbb{SG}(x),\mathbb{SG}^{-}(x))$  is a Sprague-Grundy pair of  $x$ .
By definition, every terminal position is a  $(0,1)$ -position.


Definition 2.


An impartial game will be called


1. 


domestic if it has neither  $(0,k)$ -positions nor  $(k,0)$ -positions with  $k\geq 2$ ;


2. 


tame if it has only  $(0,1)$ -positions,  $(1,0)$ -positions, and  $(k,k)$ -positions with  $k\geq 0$ ;


3. 


pet if it has only  $(0,1)$ -positions,  $(1,0)$ -positions, and  $(k,k)$ -positions with  $k\geq 2$ .


4. 


forced if each move from a  $(0,1)$ -position results in a  $(1,0)$ -position and vice versa;


5. 


returnable if the following, weaker, implications hold:
let  $x$  be a  $(0,1)$ -position (resp., a  $(1,0)$ -position) movable to a non-terminal position  $y$ , then
 $y$  is movable to a  $(0,1)$ -position (resp., to a  $(1,0)$ -position).






Tame games were introduced by Conway [8, Chapter 12]
(see page 178), pet games were introduced recently by Gurvich et al. in the preprints [19, 20],
while domestic games are introduced by Gurvich et al. [15].
According to the above definitions, domestic, tame, pet, returnable and forced games form nested classes:
a pet game is tame, a tame game is domestic and a forced game is returnable; see Figure 3.

2.4 Complexity theory

The corresponding game tree  $T_{p}$  is a rooted tree obtained by the following procedure.


1. 


The original position  $p$  is the root of  $T_{p}$ .


2. 


For each subposition  $\tilde{p}$ 
with  $p\to\tilde{p}$ ,
there is a directed edge from  $p$  to the root of  $T_{\tilde{p}}$ .

The height of  $T_{p}$  is the length of the longest path from  $p$ .
Provided that  $T_{p}$  has finite height, the height of any proper subtree of  $T_{p}$  is at least one less than the height of  $T_{p}$ .


3. 


We say that subpositions  $\tilde{p}$  and  $\bar{p}$  of  $p$  are distinct if  $T_{\tilde{p}}$  is not isomorphic to  $T_{\bar{p}}$ .
The state-space complexity of a game is the number of pairwise distinct game positions reachable from the initial position  $p$  of the game.





A game  $G$  is said to be finite if  $T_{p}$  has finite height for any position  $p$  of  $G$ .
The computational complexity of a game is the time required to decide whether a given initial position is winning.






3 LCTR and Downright



We now describe the core objects of this paper, the games LCTR and Downright. After providing definitions in Sections 3.1 and 3.2, we mention several important properties of both games in Section 3.3.




3.1 LCTR



A position in the leftmost column—topmost row, or LCTR game (see [14]), is denoted by  $\mathcal{L}(\lambda)$ , where  $\lambda$  is a partition, i.e., a non-increasing list of  $r$  positive integers  $\lambda=\llbracket\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{r}\rrbracket$  with  $\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{r}$ .



The  terminal position occurs when  $r=0$ , which we denote by  $\mathcal{L}(\llbracket\rrbracket)$ .
Let  $A\neq\mathcal{L}(\llbracket\rrbracket)$  be a non-terminal position.
There are two possible moves a player can make, leading to a new position  $\tilde{A}$ , namely:


1. 


 $\tilde{A}=\mathcal{L}(\lambda[1,0])$ , or


2. 


 $\tilde{A}=\mathcal{L}(\lambda[0,1])$ .





Given two non-negative integers
 $i<r$  and  $j\leq\lambda_{i+1}$ , or  $(i,j)=(r,0)$ ,
we denote the subposition reached from  $A$  by  $i$  top row moves and  $j$  left column moves as  $A[i,j]$ , formally
 $A[i,j]=\mathcal{L}(\lambda[i,j]).$ 





3.2 Downright



We now introduce a new game named Downright, which is simpler than LCTR and is a good introduction to the study of LCTR.
Normal play in Downright is equivalent to misère play in LCTR in a way that is made more precise in Section 4.
In Downright, a “restricted” rook chess piece begins in the top-left box of a Young diagram.
The rook can move a single adjacent box down or right.
Under normal play, then, the player who gets the rook to a corner, where no moves remain, is the winner.
There are similarities with Impartial chess played with a King [21, 22], but in Downright we cannot move diagonally and the board is a (not necessarily rectangular) partition.



Let us define the game of Downright  $\mathcal{D}(\lambda)$ , for a given partition  $\lambda=\llbracket\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{r}\rrbracket$ .
There are at most two moves a player can make.
In particular, the move resulting in the subposition  $\mathcal{D}(\lambda[1,0])$  is allowed whenever  $r>1$ , the move resulting in subposition  $\mathcal{D}(\lambda[0,1])$  is allowed whenever  $\lambda_{1}>1$ .
Given two non-negative integers
 $i<r$  and  $j<\lambda_{i+1}$ ,
we denote the subposition reached from  $A$  by the rook moving  $j$  boxes to the right and  $i$  boxes down as  $A[i,j]$ , formally
 $A[i,j]=\mathcal{D}(\lambda[i,j])$ .

4 Misère LCTR, truncation, and Downright



As discussed in Section 2.2, it is in general difficult to analyze misère play of a given combinatorial game.
Nevertheless, in this section we show that misère LCTR can be analyzed using Sprague-Grundy theory.
In fact, Downright is misère LCTR without its terminal positions.


Lemma 7.


Let  $\lambda\neq\llbracket\rrbracket$  be a partition.
Then  $\lambda$  corresponds to a  $\mathcal{P}$ -position in misère LCTR if and only if it corresponds to a  $\mathcal{P}$ -position in Downright (under normal play).



Proof.


By the definition of  $\mathcal{P}$  and  $\mathcal{N}$  positions (Section 2.2), all subpositions  $\llbracket\rrbracket$  in misère LCTR belong to  $\mathcal{N}$  while all subpositions  $\llbracket 1\rrbracket$  are in  $\mathcal{P}$ .
For Downright, we have that all corners are in  $\mathcal{P}$ . Recall that empty partition  $\llbracket\rrbracket$  is not valid input partition for the game Downright.



Now consider either of the games (misère LCTR or Downright) played on a row  $\llbracket c\rrbracket$ ,  $c>1$ , and observe that such a game belongs to  $\mathcal{N}$  if and only if the same game played on  $(c-1)$  belongs to  $\mathcal{P}$ .
The same arguments hold for the case of the column game, when either game is played on the partition  $\llbracket 1^{r}\rrbracket,r>1$ . We conclude that the membership in  $\mathcal{N}$  or  $\mathcal{P}$  coincides for both games, whenever played on a column, or on a row partition.



Consider now an arbitrary partition  $\lambda\neq\llbracket\rrbracket$  and consider both games played on  $\lambda$ .
We already established that in the cases of subpartitions  $\llbracket c\rrbracket$  or  $\llbracket 1^{r}\rrbracket$ , both belong to  $\mathcal{P}$  or  $\mathcal{N}$  in the same way.
Since
the definition of  $\mathcal{P}$  and  $\mathcal{N}$  (Section 2.2)
computes membership of positions from both games recursively in the same way, this concludes the proof of the claim.
∎




We now define and motivate the truncation of any impartial game.


Definition 8 (truncation).


Let  $G$  be a finite impartial game starting on position  $p$ .
The truncation of  $G$  is an impartial game  $\textnormal{trunc}(G)$  obtained from  $G$  as follows:


1. 


The starting position of  $\textnormal{trunc}(G)$  corresponds to  $p$ .


2. 


 $\textnormal{trunc}(G)$  has the same moves as  $G$  except moves  $A\to B$  where  $B$  is a terminal position of  $G$  no longer exist.



We will assume  $\textnormal{trunc}(G)$  is played under normal play, unless stated otherwise.




Lemma 7 motivates the following theorem, from which it can be derived as a corollary.


Theorem 9.


Let  $G$  be an impartial misère game and let  $p$  be a non-terminal position of  $G$ . Then  $p$  is a  $\mathcal{P}$ -position of  $G$  if and only if  $p$  is a  $\mathcal{P}$ -position of  $\textnormal{trunc}(G)$  under normal play.



Proof.


Let  $G$  be a finite impartial misère game, let  $H=\textnormal{trunc}(G)$ , and denote the sets of their  $\mathcal{P}$ -positions by  $\mathcal{P}_{G},\mathcal{P}_{H}$ , respectively.
Note that the deleted  $G$ -terminal (i.e. terminal in  $G$ ) positions are not  $\mathcal{P}_{G}$ , by definition of misère, which supports our claim.
All the remaining positions exist in both  $G$  and  $H$ .
Since  $G$  is a finite game, for every position  $A$ , all of its subpositions have smaller height than  $A$ .
Hence we can establish  $\mathcal{P}_{G}=\mathcal{P}_{H}$  by induction on the height of the game tree of  $G$ .



First we show that the claim holds for all subpositions  $A$  such that  $A$  is of height  $1$  in  $G$ .
The only moves from  $A$  are to  $G$ -terminal position which are all  $\mathcal{N}_{G}$ , thus  $A\in\mathcal{P}_{G}$  follows.
Since  $A$  is  $H$ -terminal and  $H$  is under normal play, it holds trivially that  $A\in\mathcal{P}_{H}$ .



Now assume that the claim holds for all subpositions reachable from  $A$ . Let  $\mathcal{T}$  denote the terminal positions to which we can move in  $G$  from  $A$ , and let  $\mathcal{B}$  correspond to the set of non-terminal positions reachable from  $A$  in one move.
Recall that  $\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{G}$ .
We consider two cases depending on whether  $A$  is  $\mathcal{P}_{G}$  or  $\mathcal{N}_{G}$ .


1. 


Let  $A\in\mathcal{P}_{G}$ . Then we have  $\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{G}$  by
the definition of  $\mathcal{P}$  and  $\mathcal{N}$  positions (Section 2.2).
By induction hypothesis we have for all  $\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{H}$ . Since  $A$  can only move to positions  $\mathcal{B}$  in  $H$ , we have that  $A\in\mathcal{P}_{H}$ .


2. 


Let  $A\in\mathcal{N}_{G}$ . Then there exists  $B\in\mathcal{B}$  s.t.  $B\in\mathcal{P}_{G}$  by the definition of  $\mathcal{P}$  and  $\mathcal{N}$ .
By induction hypothesis we have that  $B\in\mathcal{P}_{H}$ .
Since position  $A$  can move to position  $B$  in  $H$ , we have that  $A\in\mathcal{N}_{H}$ .∎






The above theorem directly implies that
 $\mathcal{N}$ -positions also coincide except on the deleted terminal positions of the original game, as they do not exist in the truncation.



While the functions  $\mathbb{SG}(\textnormal{trunc}(\cdot))$  and  $\mathbb{SG}^{-}(\cdot)$  have the same support (with the exception of terminal positions), they may have different values within the support.



We find it noteworthy that the truncation of LCTR is played on the same Young diagram under the rules of Downright.
Indeed, since the terminal positions of any LCTR game  $G$  are “located” outside its Young diagram, removing them does not alter the diagram for  $\textnormal{trunc}(G)$ .



One might also consider the misère variant of Downright in which case
its terminal positions are the corners of the corresponding Young diagram.
Observe that for  $\lambda\vdash n$ , where  $n\geq 2$ , we have




 $\textnormal{trunc}(\mathcal{D}(\lambda))=\mathcal{D}(\lambda^{-})$ 

(7)


where  $\lambda^{-}$  is obtained from  $\lambda$  by removing all corners of  $\lambda$ . Repeated truncations of a game may give rise to a hierarchy of games. In the case of repeated truncation of Downright, Equation 7 gives




 $\left\{\text{trunc}^{i+1}(\mathcal{D}(\lambda)):\lambda\vdash n,\ n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\right\}\subsetneq\left\{\text{trunc}^{i}(\mathcal{D}(\lambda)):\lambda\vdash n,\ n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\right\}$ 



for every positive integer  $i$ .



Lemma 7 further motivates the study of the  $\mathbb{SG}$  values of Downright, which is the focus of this section.
We now show how  $\mathbb{SG}$  values propagate along diagonals, as seen in Figure 5 (right).



Let  $\lambda$  be a nonempty partition and let  $\textrm{cor}(\lambda)=\max\{i+j:(i,j)\mbox{ is a corner of }\lambda\}$ . Observe that  $\textrm{cor}(\lambda)>\textrm{cor}(\lambda[i,j])$  whenever  $i>0$  or  $j>0$ .


Lemma 10.


Let  $\lambda$  be a partition and let  $i,j\geq 1$  be integers such that  $A=\mathcal{D}(\lambda)$  and  $A[i,j]$  are positions in Downright.
Then  $\mathbb{SG}(A[i-1,j-1])=\mathbb{SG}(A[i,j])$ .



Proof.


Note that  $\mathbb{SG}(A[i-1,j-1])=\mathbb{SG}(A[i,j])$  if and only if  $A[i,j]+A[i-1,j-1]\in\mathcal{P}$ . We proceed by induction on  $\textrm{cor}(\lambda[i,j])$ .



For the base step, suppose  $\textrm{cor}(\lambda[i,j])=0$ . Then  $A[i,j]$  is in  $\mathcal{P}$ , so  $A[i-1,j]$  and  $A[i,j-1]$  are in  $\mathcal{N}$ . Thus  $A[i-1,j-1]$  is in  $\mathcal{P}$  so  $A[i,j]+A[i-1,j-1]$  is in  $\mathcal{P}$ .



For the induction step, suppose that  $\textrm{cor}(\lambda[i,j])>0$ . If the opponent moves in the  $A[i-1,j-1]$  summand, we play in that summand again to obtain  $A[i,j]+A[i,j]$  which is in  $\mathcal{P}$ . If the opponent moves in the  $A[i,j]$  summand, then we mimic that move in the  $A[i-1,j-1]$  summand to obtain either  $A[i+1,j]+A[i,j-1]$  or  $A[i,j+1]+A[i-1,j]$ . The induction hypothesis applies because  $\textrm{cor}(\lambda[i+1,j])$  and  $\textrm{cor}(\lambda[i,j+1])$  are both less than  $\textrm{cor}(\lambda[i,j])$ . Therefore,  $A[i+1,j]+A[i,j-1]$  and  $A[i,j+1]+A[i-1,j]$  are in  $\mathcal{P}$ .
∎




Due to Lemma 10, we can now state the main theorem of this section.


Theorem 11.


Let  $\lambda$  be a partition, let  $A=\mathcal{D}(\lambda)$  be a game of Downright and let  $\ell=d(\lambda)$ . Then




 $\mathbb{SG}(A)=\mathbb{SG}(A[1,1])=\mathbb{SG}(A[2,2])=\dots=\mathbb{SG}(A[\ell-1,\ell-1]).$ 






To put things in perspective, to calculate the  $\mathbb{SG}$  value of a game of Downright, by Theorem 11 we can calculate the subpartition at the end of the diagonal, which turns out to be always be a gamma partition, which we know how to calculate by Lemma 4.

Lemma 15.


Let  $\lambda$  be a partition, let  $A=\mathcal{L}(\lambda)$  be a game of LCTR, and let  $i,j\geq 1$  be such that  $\lambda[i,j]\neq\llbracket\rrbracket$ .


1. 


If  $\mathbb{SG}(A[i,j])=0$ , then  $d(\lambda[i,j])\geq 1$  and
 $\mathbb{SG}(A[i-1,j-1])=0$ .


2. 


If  $\mathbb{SG}(A[i,j])=1$  and  $d(\lambda[i,j])\geq 2$ , then  $\mathbb{SG}(A[i-1,j-1])=1$ .


3. 


If  $\mathbb{SG}(A[i,j])=2$  and  $d(\lambda[i,j])\geq 3$ , then  $\mathbb{SG}(A[i-1,j-1])=2$ .





Proof.


We have that  $\lambda[i^{\prime},j^{\prime}]\neq\llbracket\rrbracket$  for any choice of  $0\leq i^{\prime}<d(\lambda)$  and  $0\leq j^{\prime}<d(\lambda)$ . In particular, for any corresponding subgame  $A[i^{\prime},j^{\prime}]$  we may use the first case of recursion in Equation 3.
We consider three cases depending on the value of  $\mathbb{SG}(A[i,j])$ .




1. 


Suppose  $\mathbb{SG}(A[i,j])=0$ .
Due to Corollary 1 we have




 $\displaystyle\mathbb{SG}(A[i-1,j])\neq 0\neq\mathbb{SG}(A[i,j-1]).$ 



Therefore  $\mathbb{SG}(A[i-1,j-1])=A[i-1,j]\,\triangledown\,A[i,j-1]=\bcancel{0}\,\triangledown\,\bcancel{0}=0$ .
Since  $\lambda[i,j]\neq\llbracket\rrbracket$ , clearly  $d(\lambda[i,j])\geq 1$ .


2. 


Suppose  $\mathbb{SG}(A[i,j])=1$  and  $d(\lambda[i,j])\geq 2$ , in particular neither  $\lambda[i,j+1]$  nor  $\lambda[i+1,j]$  is empty.
First notice that by Corollary 1 we have




 $\displaystyle\mathbb{SG}(A[i,j-1])$ 
 $\displaystyle\neq 1\neq\mathbb{SG}(A[i-1,j]).$ 



This implies  $\mathbb{SG}(A[i-1,j-1])=\bcancel{1}\,\triangledown\,\bcancel{1}\leq 1$ .
Now assume  $\mathbb{SG}(A[i-1,j-1])=0$ .
From Corollaries 1 and 5 it follows that





 $\displaystyle\mathbb{SG}(A[i,j-1])=\mathbb{SG}(A[i-1,j])=2,\text{ and}$ 




 $\displaystyle\mathbb{SG}(A[i+1,j-1])=\mathbb{SG}(A[i-1,j+1])=0.$ 





Since neither  $\lambda[i,j+1]$  nor  $\lambda[i+1,j]$  is empty,
by Corollary 1 we have




 $\mathbb{SG}(A[i+1,j])=\mathbb{SG}(A[i,j+1])=2.$ 



This leads to  $\mathbb{SG}(A[i,j])=2\,\triangledown\,2\neq 1,$  a contradiction.

 $\{\mathbb{SG}(A[i,j+1]),\mathbb{SG}(A[i+1,j])\}=\{0,1\}.$

In addition, Items 1 and 2 imply that




 $\{\mathbb{SG}(A[i-1,j]),\mathbb{SG}(A[i,j-1])\}=\{0,1\}.$ 



Hence  $\mathbb{SG}(A[i-1,j-1])=0\,\triangledown\,1=2$ . ∎

6 The hierarchical classification of LCTR and Downright



In this section we classify the newly introduced games LCTR and Downright, in terms of the hierarchy presented by Gurvich and Ho [15].


Lemma 18.


LCTR and Downright do not admit  $(0,2)$ - and  $(2,0)$ -positions.



Proof.


We prove the statement for both games at the same time, since all the arguments are the same.
Consider a minimal counterexample  $\lambda$ .
We show that it is neither a  $(0,2)$ -, nor a  $(2,0)$ -position.

Suppose  $\lambda$  is a  $(2,0)$ -position.



Notice that both moves are available because no  $(2,0)$ -position can be obtained by less then two moves.
Without loss of generality let  $\lambda[1,0]$  be a  $(0,\bcancel{0})$ -position and let  $\lambda[0,1]$  be a  $(1,\bcancel{0})$ -position.
Due to minimality  $\lambda[1,0]$  must be a  $(0,1)$ -position.



Notice that the move  $\lambda[0,1]\to\lambda[1,1]$  is legal in both games.
Otherwise,  $\lambda[0,1]\to\lambda[0,2]$  is legal, and hence  $\lambda[0,1]$  is a  $(1,1)$ -position.
This implies that
for  $i>1$ ,  $\lambda[0,i]$  alternates between  $(0,0)$ - and  $(1,1)$ -positions, contradicting the finiteness of  $\lambda$ .



Due to minimality, observe that  $\lambda[1,1]$  is a  $(2,2)$ -position, which implies that  $\lambda[0,1]$  is a  $(1,1)$ -position.
Next, notice that  $\lambda[0,2]$  is a  $(0,0)$ -position,
due to  $(1,1)$  being obtained as a mex of a  $(2,2)$ -position and this position.
But then we clearly have that  $\lambda[1,2]$  is a  $(1,1)$ -position, because it is adjacent to both  $(0,0)$ - and  $(2,2)$ -positions.
By extension,  $\lambda[2,1]$  is a  $(0,0)$ -position,
due to  $(2,2)$  being obtained as a mex of a  $(1,1)$ -position and this position.



Focusing on  $\lambda[2,0]$ , first notice that it must be a  $(\bcancel{0},0)$ -position, which by minimality implies that  $\lambda[2,0]$  is a  $(1,0)$ -position.
This is a contradiction as its neighbour,  $\lambda[2,1]$ , is a  $(0,0)$ -position.

Suppose  $\lambda$  is a  $(0,2)$ -position.



For this case the proof is exactly the same, with swapped coordinates of all Sprague-Grundy pairs.



∎



Theorem 19.


LCTR and Downright are domestic and returnable, but neither tame nor forced.



Proof.


To see that the games are returnable,
suppose by contradiction there is a non-terminal  $(0,1)$ - position  $\lambda$  which does not lead to a  $(1,0)$ -position.
But then it leads to a  $(2,0)$  position, contradicting Lemma 18.
A symmetric argument shows that every  $(1,0)$ -position can move to a  $(0,1)$ -position. Thus it follows that the games are returnable.



To see that the games are domestic, first note that both  $\mathbb{SG}$  and  $\mathbb{SG}^{-}$  values cannot exceed  $2$ , thus
it is enough to show  $(0,2)$  and  $(2,0)$ -positions are impossible, which Lemma 18 guarantees.



They are not tame due to existence of  $(2,1)$ -positions. They are not forced by existence of moves from a  $(1,0)$ -position to a  $(2,2)$ -position. See Figure 9.

7 The complexity of LCTR and Downright



In this section we discuss computational aspects of both LCTR and Downright.
Let  $n=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}$ , where  $\lambda=\llbracket\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r}\rrbracket$  is the input partition for either game.
Complexity results will be expressed in terms of  $\lambda_{1}$ ,  $r$ , and  $n$ . In Section 7.1, we discuss the time complexity required to compute  $\mathbb{SG}$ -values of LCTR and Downright. In Section 7.2, we study the number of nodes and leaves in their game trees. In Section 7.3, we examine the state-space complexity of these games.




7.1 Time complexity of computing  $\mathbb{SG}$  values



The results of this section will be expressed in terms of the number of time units needed. We consider the time unit to be any of the following:


1. 


Reading an integer. Note that our input partition  $\lambda=\llbracket\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r}\rrbracket$  consists of a sequence of  $r$  integers, so reading the input takes  $r$  time units.


2. 


Basic arithmetic operations. Here we mean integer multiplication or division by  $2$ ,
addition or subtraction of the given integers, or computing the parity.


3. 


Minimum excluded value: We consider computing a minimal excluded value to be a basic operation which takes one time unit. Note that mex computations will only be performed with integers from the set  $\{0,1,2\}$ .





For a given partition  $\lambda=\llbracket\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{r}\rrbracket$  we show that the  $\mathbb{SG}$  value of both LCTR and Downright, played on  $\lambda$ , can be determined  $O(\log(r))\leq O(\log(n))$  time units.
For comparison, the dynamic programming approach by [14] required  $\Theta(n)$  time units for the same task.


Lemma 20.


Given a partition  $\lambda=\llbracket\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}\rrbracket\vdash n$  we can find its Durfee length  $d(\lambda)$  in  $O(\log(r))\leq O(\log(n))$  time units.



Proof.


Recall that the Durfee length is the largest  $i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}$  such that  $\lambda_{i}\geq i$ .
We can find such a value in  $\log(r)$  time units by using binary search.
∎



Lemma 21.


Given a partition  $\lambda=\llbracket\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}\rrbracket\vdash n$  we can compute its conjugate  $\lambda^{\prime}$  in
 $O(\lambda_{1}\log(r))\leq O(n\log(n))$ 
time units.



Proof.


For every  $i\in\{1,\ldots,\lambda_{1}\}$  we
find  $\lambda^{\prime}_{i}$ 
via a binary search on the list  $\llbracket\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}\rrbracket$  which takes  $O(\log(r))$  time units each.
Hence, in total we need  $O(\lambda_{1}\log(r))$  time units.
∎

Number of leaves in the game tree



The number of leaves in the game tree of a given position corresponds to the number of different games that can be played on that position.
As expected, for both games this number can be exponential in  $n$ , for example for staircase partitions (see Table 1).
Depending on the input partition, the number of leaves may be much smaller.
Table 1 also can be used to obtain the following result.


Lemma 25.


Let  $n$  be an integer and let  $\lambda\vdash n$ .
The number of leaves in the game tree of  $\mathcal{D}(\lambda)$  is at least  $1$ .
The number of leaves in the game tree of  $\mathcal{L}(\lambda)$  is at least  $n+1$ .
Both lower bounds are tight.



Proof.


Regarding the case of Downright, it is enough to inspect the second row of Table 1, where one may observe that this bound is achieved by  $\llbracket n\rrbracket$ .
We now consider the case of LCTR.



We prove the claim by showing that among all of the partitions of  $n>0$ , the partition  $\llbracket n\rrbracket$ 
has the smallest number of leaves, in particular
 $\text{\scriptsize\faIconFromMacro{faLeaf}}(\mathcal{L}(\llbracket n\rrbracket))=n+1$ .
Consider now any partition  $\lambda=\llbracket\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r}\rrbracket\vdash n$ .
Let
 $\bar{\lambda}=\llbracket\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r}-1\rrbracket$  and let  $\bar{\bar{\lambda}}=\llbracket\lambda_{1}+1,\ldots,\lambda_{r}-1\rrbracket$  be a partition obtained from  $\bar{\lambda}$  by adding one box to the first row.
Now observe that since  $\lambda\neq\llbracket\rrbracket$ 
it follows that  $\lambda[r-1,\lambda_{r}-1]=\llbracket 1\rrbracket$ .
The leaves of  $\mathcal{L}(\bar{\lambda})$  are the same as those of  $\mathcal{L}(\lambda)$  except we remove both children (which are both leaves) of all the occurrences of  $\mathcal{L}(\lambda)[r-1,\lambda_{r}-1]$  in the game tree, and all occurrences of  $\mathcal{L}(\lambda)[r-1,\lambda_{r}-1]$  become leaves.
Hence the number of leaves decreases by the number of occurrences of  $\mathcal{L}(\lambda)[r-1,\lambda_{r}-1]$ , in particular, the number of leaves decreases by at least one.
On the other hand notice that  $\mathcal{L}(\bar{\bar{\lambda}})$  admits exactly one more leaf than  $\mathcal{L}(\bar{\lambda})$  since in the game tree of  $\mathcal{L}(\bar{\lambda})$  we replace the single occurrence of the leaf
 $\lambda[0,\lambda_{1}]=\llbracket\rrbracket$  with a node with two leaf children to get the game tree of  $\mathcal{L}(\bar{\bar{\lambda}})$ .
It follows that the number of leaves did not increase from  $\lambda$  to  $\bar{\bar{\lambda}}$  while  $\lambda,\bar{\bar{\lambda}}\vdash n$ .
Any partition  $\lambda\vdash n$  minimizing the number of leaves can therefore be gradually transformed to  $\llbracket n\rrbracket$ , without increasing the number of leaves.
It remains to observe, e.g. by Tables 1 and 23, that  $\llbracket n\rrbracket$  indeed admits exactly  $n+1$  leaves, as stated by the claim.
∎

7.3 State-space complexity



The state-space complexity of a game is the number of distinct game positions reachable from the initial position of the game.


Proposition 28.


Let  $\lambda$  be a partition such that  $\lambda\vdash n$ .
Then the number of states of  $\mathcal{L}(\lambda)$  and  $\mathcal{D}(\lambda)$  is bounded above by  $n+1$  and  $n$ , respectively.
On the other hand, the number of states of both  $\mathcal{L}(\lambda)$  and  $\mathcal{D}(\lambda)$  is bounded below by  $\Omega(\sqrt{n})$ .



Proof.


Concerning both upper bounds, it is easy to notice that the number of positions is trivially bounded by the number of boxes of  $\lambda$ .
More precisely, the state space of any LCTR game may attain the bound of  $n+1$ , due to an additional position  $\llbracket\rrbracket$ , while the state space of Downright cannot exceed  $n$ .
One can attain both upper bounds by considering, for instance, a gamma-shaped partition.

8 Open Problems



In this section, we give some possible directions for future research.




Alternative representations of input partition:



There are various ways to represent the input partition in a possibly more compact way.
For instance, one may use exponents to represent rows of same length (see Section 2.1).
A similar approach is by only storing two-dimentional positions of all corners of the given partition.
For example, the exponential and corner representations of  $\llbracket 6,5,4,4,4,2,2\rrbracket$  are, respectively,




 $\llbracket 6,5,4^{3},2^{2}\rrbracket\text{\quad and }\quad\{(6,1),(5,2),(4,5),(2,7)\}.$ 





If we assume exponential or corner representation of input partition, the computational difficulty of the problem may change nontrivially, as converting between the mentioned representations may take as much as  $O(n)$  time units.



On one hand, doing this can speed up certain parts of computation of  $\mathbb{SG}$  values, while other difficulties may appear.
For instance, it is not clear to us whether one can always determine the Durfee length in  $O(\log n)$  time units using exponential notation.
It would be interesting to see if the overall time complexity can be improved by assuming that the input partition is encoded using exponential notation.





Multidimensional LCTR:



Let us denote the tuples  $v=(v_{1},...,v_{d})$  with  $v_{i}\geq 1$  for all  $i$  as points.
For two such points  $v,v^{\prime}$ , we say that  $v\leq v^{\prime}$  if  $v_{i}\leq v^{\prime}_{i}$  for all  $i$ .
The position of  $d$ -dimensional LCTR is simply a finite set  $S$  of non-comparable points, also called corners.
In  $d$ -dimensional LCTR on their turn a player chooses  $i\in\{1,...,d\}$ , and
modifies every  $s\in S$  by decreasing  $s_{i}$  by one
if  $s_{i}-1$  is positive, and removing  $s$  from  $S$  otherwise.
The game ends when  $S$  is empty.



In such setting it is easy to see that the Sprague-Grundy values may not exceed  $d$ , however zero values do not propagate, hence there is no hope for the similar algorithm to work.
Is there a fast algorithm like in the case  $d=2$ ?

The game of Downright presented in this paper is very similar to Impartial chess with a king.
The two differences are: (i) the king cannot move diagonally, and (ii) the initial partition in Downright need not be of rectangular shape.
Despite the similarity, the Impartial chess with a king seems to be much harder to understand when played on an arbitrary partition.
On the other hand, Impartial chess with a rook is equivalent to two-pile Nim, while Impartial chess with a queen is equivalent to the Wythoff game [23].



Due to the above connections of Impartial chess with other impartial games, it would be interesting to study this game in the generalized setting where the starting position can be any partition.





Non-repetitive variant:



The majority of the winning strategy of both LCTR and Downright consist of mirroring the opponent’s moves.
Suppose that we require that within every four consecutive moves, the number of  $L$  moves is distinct from the number of  $T$  moves. Does the game becomes significantly harder?
