1.2    Origin and motivation
Equations of the form (1) arise in the mean-field analysis of preferential-attachment dynamics
with heterogeneous fitness; see Section 6 for references and for the limits of the analogy. In these
applications it is the ratio β = C/m, rather than C(m), that carries an intrinsic meaning: it
is dimensionless, it is bounded below by 1 throughout the domain, and it controls the relevant
degree-distribution exponent. Analytically, the substitution η = mt in (1) turns the equation
into one for β alone on the fixed interval [0, 1] (Lemma 4), which is what makes the derivative
formula (Theorem 7(c)) clean.

  (e) Critical point under unimodality. Suppose h is unimodal with mode ηh∗ ∈ (0, M ), and let

    The content of (4) is that β ′ (m) has the sign of a positive-weight average of the logarithmic
derivative dh/d ln η over (0, m]: β inherits monotonicity from the elasticity of h, and a critical
point of β is the location at which positive and negative contributions of dh/d ln η, weighted by
the implicit-function kernel 1/(β(m) − t), balance. Part (d) is the case where the elasticity has
a definite sign throughout; part (e) is the simplest case where it changes sign once. Part (e)
requires two technical hypotheses, conventionally labelled

(H1) J (m♯ ) < 0 for some m♯ ∈ (ηh∗ , M ),

(H2) [ηh∗ , m♯ ] ⊂ Ω,

which we will refer to by these names. (H1) is the substantive sign-change condition; (H2) is
automatic whenever ρ > 0 on (0, M ) (in particular for log-concave h). We verify (H1) numerically
for all seven log-concave test densities of Section 5.

A uniqueness conjecture. If, in addition to the hypotheses of (e), h is log-concave on (0, M ),
we conjecture that m⋆ is the unique critical point of β on Ω (Conjecture 10). A bimodal example
showing what happens when both hypotheses fail is discussed in Section 5.

2     Setup and preliminaries
2.1     Hypotheses on ρ
We use the following hypotheses throughout. Let M ∈ (0, ∞) and let ρ be a function on [0, M ].
                RM
(D0) ρ ≥ 0 and 0 ρ(η) dη = 1.

(D1) ρ ∈ C 1 ([0, M ]), with one-sided derivatives at the endpoints.

(D2) There exist α > 1, K < ∞ and η0 ∈ (0, M ) such that ρ(η) ≤ K(M − η)α−1 for all
     η ∈ [η0 , M ]. That is, ρ vanishes polynomially at η = M at order at least α − 1; in
     particular ρ(M ) = 0. This holds for Beta-type densities ρ(η) ∝ η a (M − η)b with b ≥ 1
     and fails for any density with ρ(M ) > 0 (log-normal, exponential, Gamma); the question
     of extending the analysis past (D2) is taken up in Section 6.

(D3) ρ is not identically zero on any subinterval of (0, M ). Equivalently, ρ > 0 on a positive-
     measure subset of every subinterval of (0, M ).

Define h(η) := ηρ(η). By (D1), h ∈ C 1 ([0, M ]), with derivative h′ (η) = ρ(η) + ηρ′ (η); by (D2),
h(M ) = 0.

4      Proof of Theorem 7
The proof draws on Lemmas 1–5. Part (a) is the implicit existence statement: for each m ∈ Ω,
F (·, m) is strictly decreasing on (m, ∞) (Lemma 2) and crosses zero between its +∞-side limit
I + (m) − 1 > 0 (Lemma 3(i)) and its limit −1 at infinity (Lemma 3(ii)), so C(m) exists and is
unique. Smoothness in (b) is then the implicit-function theorem. The sign formula (c) comes
from differentiating G(β(m), m) ≡ 0 in the reparametrisation (10) of Lemma 4. Parts (d) and
(e) follow from (c).

4.1     Proof of (a): domain
Openness of Ω is Lemma 5.
    For m ∈ Ω: Lemma 2(ii) gives F (·, m) strictly decreasing on (m, ∞). Lemma 3(i) gives
limC↓m F = I + (m) − 1 > 0. Lemma 3(ii) gives F → −1 < 0. By continuity and strict
monotonicity, a unique C(m) ∈ (m, ∞) satisfies F (C(m), m) = 0.
    For m ∈ (0, M ) \ Ω: supC>m F (C, m) = I + (m) − 1 ≤ 0. Since F (·, m) is strictly decreasing
in C (Lemma 2(ii)), the supremum equals the limit as C ↓ m and is not attained. Hence
F (C, m) < I + (m) − 1 ≤ 0 for all C > m, and the equation F (C, m) = 0 has no solution.

4.4   Proof of (d): monotone case

some δ. The set T := {t ∈ (0, 1) : mt ∈ I} is an open subinterval of (0, 1) of positive Lebesgue

    Step 2: Sign change. (H1) gives J (m♯ ) < 0.

4.6   A uniqueness conjecture and a bimodal counterexample
Theorem 7 gives no uniqueness statement for the critical point produced in part (e). We conjec-
ture uniqueness under strict log-concavity of h, and we examine numerically a bimodal density
showing what can happen when this hypothesis fails.

Conjecture 10 (Uniqueness under log-concavity). Assume (D0)–(D3), hypotheses (H1), (H2)
of Theorem 7(e), and that h(η) := ηρ(η) is positive on (0, M ) and strictly log-concave:

Remark 4 (On the hypotheses of Conjecture 10). The positivity h > 0 on (0, M ) is stated
explicitly because, without it, h(η) = 0 would make log h = −∞ and (19) trivially true. Strict
log-concavity together with h(0) = h(M ) = 0 already implies unimodality, which is therefore
not listed separately. Hypothesis (H1) is not implied by log-concavity; we verify it numerically
in all seven log-concave test densities of Table 1. Finally, h > 0 on (0, M ) implies ρ > 0 there,
so Lemma 1(ii) gives Ω = (0, M ) and the conjecture is a statement on the full interval.
    The seven log-concave rows of Table 1 support the conjecture: in each case β ′ has a single
sign change on the tested portion of Ω. The examples span a range of vanishing orders (α ∈
{3, 4, 5}), polynomial and non-polynomial densities, and modes throughout (0, M ). The list is
biased toward Beta-type densities; a systematic test of non-Beta-type log-concave densities (for
instance, polynomial-times-trigonometric or polynomial-times-logarithmic) would strengthen the
numerical case.

5     Numerical evidence
5.1   Method
For each density we solve F (C, m) = 0 on a uniform grid of N = 2001 points mi ∈ [0.1, M −
10−3 ] using Brent’s method (scipy.optimize.brentq, tolerance 10−12 ). The integral in F is
computed by adaptive quadrature (scipy.integrate.quad, relative tolerance 10−10 ). We then
evaluate J (m) by the same quadrature using the analytical formula (14) of Theorem 7(c), which
avoids numerical differentiation. Sign changes of J between consecutive grid points are flagged
and refined by Brent’s method on J to tolerance 10−12 . For row 8 of Table 1 we recompute all
reported critical points with mpmath at 50-digit precision; this reproduces the double-precision
values to 8 digits.
    We verified robustness of the sign-change count: doubling the grid to N = 4001 leaves the
count unchanged and shifts each m⋆ by < 10−5 ; replacing adaptive quadrature with 64-point
Gauss–Legendre shifts m⋆ by < 10−7 ; varying the sign-change threshold over [10−10 , 10−6 ] leaves
the count unchanged. The lower-endpoint mmin = 0.1 is well inside Ω for all seven log-concave
densities (I + (mmin ) > 10), hence away from the boundary regime where the prefactor in (14)
degenerates (Proposition 9).

6     Discussion
6.1   Bianconi–Barabási networks and condensation
In the Bianconi–Barabási model (Bianconi and Barabási, 2001), ρ is the fitness density of nodes,
m is the maximum fitness, and C(m) is the normalisation in the rate equation governing the
growth of node degrees; the ratio β = C/m controls the resulting degree distribution. The
threshold I + (m) = 1 is conjecturally related to the Bose–Einstein condensation transition in
the deterministic mean-field reduction. The relationship to the stochastic model is delicate:
there, m is a random order statistic, and most fitness densities of empirical interest (log-normal,

                                                    10
exponential) fail (D2). For rigorous probabilistic treatments of preferential-attachment models
with fitness, see Borgs et al. (2007) and Dereich and Mörters (2009). Theorem 7(a) characterises
when the deterministic mean-field equation admits a solution; the sign formula (c) describes how
the structural ratio depends qualitatively on the upper fitness limit. A rigorous reduction of the
stochastic model to (1) is beyond the scope of this paper.

6.2   Reinforcement-driven selection
Selection rules of the form P (i) ∝ xβi , with xi an accumulated score, arise in several settings,
including the nonlinear preferential-attachment networks of Krapivsky and Redner (2001). When
a self-consistency equation of the form (1) emerges as the mean-field reduction in such a setting,
the qualitative behaviour of β is described by Theorem 7.

6.3   The (D2) restriction
The principal limitation of the present analysis is hypothesis (D2), which excludes the densities
most relevant in network applications (log-normal, exponential, Gamma). A natural approach
is to use the boundary regularisations ρ(R,α) (η) = ρ(η)(R − η)α−1 /ZR,α , which satisfy (D2) for
finite R, and to study the limit R → ∞. Whether the limit m⋆α := limR→∞ m⋆R,α exists for
log-normal ρ is open.
