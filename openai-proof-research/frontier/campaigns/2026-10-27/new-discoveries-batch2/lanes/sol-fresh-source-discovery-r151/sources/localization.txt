                                              Let K(Rn ) denote the space of convex bodies, that is, the set of all nonempty,
                                           convex, and compact subsets of Rn equipped with the Hausdorff metric. A map
                                           φ : K(Rn ) → C is called a valuation if

                                           third problem on the non-equidecomposability of convex polytopes, but has since
                                           become a very active area of research with a variety of applications to geometric
                                           problems, in particular in integral geometry, see, e.g., [3, 6, 12, 14–17, 22, 36–38] for
                                              MSC classification: 52B45, 53C65, 43A75.

   Let us add some comments on this result. It was originally used to obtain an
affirmative solution of a conjecture by McMullen [41] that mixed volumes (see [45]
for the definition) span a dense subspace of Valr (Rn ). This conjecture was known
to hold for r = 0 and r = n, since Valr (Rn ) is 1-dimensional and spanned by the
Euler characteristic and the Lebesgue measure [26] respectively in this case, as well
as for r = 1 and r = n − 1, which follows from results by Goodey and Weil [24]
and McMullen [41] respectively. The Irreducibility Theorem 1.1 directly implies
the conjecture for all 0 ≤ r ≤ n, since linear combinations of mixed volumes form
a GL(n, R)-invariant subspace of Val(Rn ) that intersects Val±      n

fact, it can be used to obtain much stronger versions of the conjecture – it is sufficient
to take simplices or ellipsoids (in the even case) as reference bodies for the mixed
volumes [2], or combinations of mixed volumes with a bounded number of terms [34].

Goodey and Weil [24], Klain [29], and Schneider [44] in order to apply highly sophis-

the proof uses the Beilinson–Bernstein localization theorem to reduce the statement

   The proof of Theorem A is based on a corresponding regularity result for certain

[31] using a Paley–Wiener–Schwartz-type characterization result for certain distri-
butions associated to these valuations. In particular, the results in [31] do not use

   We then exploit that these spaces of valuations on convex functions can be related
to translation invariant valuations on convex bodies that satisfy restrictions on their
vertical support (see Section 2.5 for the definition), as was investigated in [32, 33].
Not every valuation in Val(Rn ) satisfies these conditions, but we are going to show
that any valuation can be written as a sum of valuations satisfying the support
restrictions using a localization procedure introduced in Section 3. This procedure
breaks the translation invariance, so we need to work on a larger space of valuations.
   Let us call a valuation φ : K(Rn ) → C a polynomial valuation if x 7→ φ(K + x)
is a polynomial in x ∈ Rn for every K ∈ K(Rn ), where the degree of φ(K + x) is
uniformly bounded, i.e. independent of K. Our second main result is a ”partition
of unity“-type result for polynomial valuations.

                                  φ = φ1 + · · · + φM .

Discussion of relevant results for the proof. As our proof of Corollary D relies
heavily on previous results, let us give a short overview.
   For the first step, Theorem B, we need homogeneous decompositions, polariza-
tions and Goodey–Weil distributions for polynomial valuations ([1, 24, 28, 35, 39, 40]).
Theorem A is then deduced using a construction from [32] from an analogous, highly
nontrivial result for valuations on convex functions, proved in [31].
   Next, we heavily use that the space V ∞,tr was studied in [8, 11, 38]. In [11], all
results are actually shown for V ∞ and the Irreducibility Theorem 1.1 is only used
to relate the results to Val∞ (Rn ). The same is true for the relevant sections in [8]
(Section 4 and 5) and [38]. We will therefore state their results as results for V ∞,tr
and not for Val∞ (Rn ) later on.
   We then use a similar strategy as in [27] to relate the different SO(n)-isotypical
components by the action of suitable Lie algebra elements from sl(n)C on the highest
weight vectors. By an explicit calculation, we show that the Lie algebra action may
be used to pass from a given highest weight vector to all representations with larger
highest weight. In order to pass to representations with smaller highest weight, we
use an SL(n, R)-invariant pairing on the relevant spaces of differential forms in order

to argue by duality. This pairing coincides with the pairing induced by the Alesker
product [4], however, we will not rely on this fact and instead establish the necessary
properties directly.

                 2. Background material and Preliminaries
  In this section we recall some background from representation theory, valuation
theory and differential geometry, as well as fix the notation. Moreover, we will prove
some preliminary results used in the proofs of the main theorems.

2.1. Representation Theory – Highest weight vectors. Throughout this sec-
tion, we assume that n ≥ 3.

   In the proof of Theorem C, we will use the decomposition of the given represen-
tations into its SO(n)-isotypical components. This decomposition was determined
in [8] and the corresponding highest weight vectors were constructed in [38]. These
isotypical components are parametrized by their highest weights, given as tuples of
length l = ⌊ n2 ⌋, and contain (after some choices) distinguished vectors called highest
weight vectors. We will later use that it is essentially enough to know the SL(n, R)-
representation on these highest weight vectors in order to show irreducibility.
   As a highest weight vector depends on some extra structure on the representation
(in particular, a choice of a Cartan subalgebra and a set of positive weights), we will
shortly summarize this here. For an introduction to the theory of highest weights,
we refer to [30, Ch. IV]. More details on our setting can be found in [38], where the
highest weight vectors of the representations of smooth valuations are constructed.

2.2. Valuations representable by integration with respect to the normal
cycle. In this section, we recall the construction of valuations using differential
forms on the sphere bundle SRn = Rn × Sn−1 of Rn from [9].
   First, recall that the normal cycle nc(K) of K ∈ K(Rn ) is defined as the set of
pairs (x, v) where x is a boundary point of K and v is an outer unit normal at x,
that is, nc(K) = {(x, v) ∈ SRn : x ∈ K, ⟨y, v⟩ ≤ ⟨x, v⟩ ∀y ∈ K}. Then nc(K)
is an (n − 1)-dimensional Lipschitz submanifold of SRn with a natural orientation
induced from the orientation of Rn . Moreover, if we interpret nc(K) as an integral
current, then the map K 7→ nc(K) is a continuous valuation with respect to the
flat metricRtopology (see [9, Sec. 2] for the definition). This implies in particular
that K → nc(K) ω defines a continuous valuation for every smooth differential form
ω ∈ Ωn−1 (SRn ). We will call valuations of this type representable by integration
with respect to the normal cycle. Note that every translation-invariant differential
form ω ∈ Ωn−1 (SRn )tr induces a valuation φω ∈ Val(Rn ). Moreover, valuations of
this type are smooth vectors of the representation of GL(n, R) on Val(Rn ), compare
[5, Prop. 5.1.9].

differential defined on the contact manifold SRn (see [43] for the definition on a
general contact manifold). Recall that SRn is a contact manifold of dimension
2n − 1 with (global) contact form α ∈ Ω1 (SRn ) given by

2.6. Valuations on convex functions. As a general reference on this section, we
refer to [19–21, 32, 33] and the references therein.
Let Conv(Rn , R) denote the space of all convex functions f : Rn → R. This space
carries a natural topology induced by epi-convergence (which in this setting coincides
with pointwise or locally uniform convergence, see [42, Thm. 7.17]). A map µ :
Conv(Rn , R) → C is called a valuation if
                     µ(max{f, g}) + µ(min{f, g}) = µ(f ) + µ(g)
whenever f, g, min{f, g} ∈ Conv(Rn , R), where max{f, g} resp. min{f, g} denote the
pointwise maximum resp. minimum.
       LOCALIZATION OF VALUATIONS AND ALESKER’S IRREDUCIBILITY THEOREM               17


  We denote by VConv(Rn ) the space of all continuous valuations µ : Conv(Rn , R) →
C that are in addition dually epi-translation invariant, that is, that satisfy
                                   µ(f + ℓ) = µ(f )
for all f ∈ Conv(Rn , R), and ℓ : Rn → R affine. This notion is intimately related to
translation invariance for valuations on convex bodies, compare Section 2.7.

   Similar to the construction of valuations on convex bodies in terms of integra-
tion with respect to the normal cycle, the following construction of valuations on
convex functions was examined in [33]: Let D(f ) denote the differential cycle of
f ∈ Conv(Rn , R) as defined by Fu in [23]. This is an integral current on the cotan-
gent bundle T ∗ Rn of Rn , which for smooth functions coincides with the current given
by integration over the graph of the differential of f . As shown in [33], any smooth
differential form τ ∈ Ωn (T ∗ Rn ) whose support is bounded in the first argument of

(14)                    µ(f ) = D(f )[τ ],   f ∈ Conv(Rn , R).
In general, such a valuation will not be dually epi-translation invariant, however,
if the differential form is invariant with respect to translations in the second factor
of T ∗ Rn = Rn × (Rn )∗ , then the valuation belongs to VConv(Rn ). Conversely, if a
valuation in VConv(Rn ) admits such a representation, then the differential form can
be chosen to be invariant with respect to translations in the second factor (compare
[33, Thm. 5.5]). We will call valuations of this form representable by integration with
respect to the differential cycle.
Remark 2.20. In [33], valuations representable by integration with respect to the
differential cycle were called ”smooth valuations” in analogy with Theorem A. Fol-
lowing the convention in [31], we reserve this terminology for a different space of

notions are equivalent, but we will distinguish between them to avoid further ambi-
guity.

sets of Conv(Rn , R) (see [32, Prop. 2.4] for a description of these subsets). We have
a natural continuous representation of the group of translations (which we identify
with Rn ) on VConv(Rn ), defined by associating to x ∈ Rn , µ ∈ VConv(Rn ) the

Definition 2.21 ([31, Def. 1.3]). A valuation µ ∈ VConv(Rn ) is called a smooth

           6. A new proof of Alesker’s Irreducibility theorem
   This section contains the proofs of Theorem C and Corollary D. We first re-
call some further background on infinite dimensional representations. Then we
use Theorem A and results from [11] to reduce the problem to showing that cer-
tain spaces of differential forms are algebraically irreducible (sl(n), SO(n))-modules.
For these spaces, we first survey some known results, including the decomposition
into SO(n)-types and corresponding highest weight vectors in Section 6.2, and then
use the calculations from Section 5 to show that they are algebraically irreducible
(sl(n), SO(n))-modules in Section 6.3.
6.1. Some remarks on infinite dimensional representations. We call a rep-
resentation π of a Lie group G on a locally convex vector space E continuous if the
map
                                   G×E →E
                                     (g, v) 7→ π(g)v
is continuous. Then v ∈ E is called a smooth vector if G → E, g 7→ π(g)v, is a
smooth map. We will denote the space of smooth vectors by E ∞ . This space is
G-invariant and naturally equipped with the Garding topology, which is stronger
than the topology induced from E and has the property that any element in E ∞
is a smooth vector (see [49, Section 4.4.1]). If E is complete, then E ∞ is dense in
E (compare [49, Prop. 4.4.1.1]). Moreover, if E is a Banach space, then E ∞ is a
Fréchet space. E ∞ can naturally be equipped with an action of the Lie algebra g of
G by setting
                                         d
                             dπ(X)v :=        π(exp(tX))v
                                         dt 0
for v ∈ E ∞ and X ∈ g. In general, there is no direct correspondence between the
representation of G on E and the representation of g on E ∞ – for example the closure
in E of a g-invariant subspace of E ∞ is not necessarily G-invariant. Nevertheless,
the action of g on E ∞ may be used to simplify the study of the representation of G
on E.
If K ⊂ G is a compact subgroup, then a vector v ∈ E is called K-finite if {π(g)v :
g ∈ K} spans a finite dimensional subspace of E. We denote by E K−fin the subspace
of K-finite vectors. If E is complete, then
                                 EK := E K−fin ∩ E ∞
is dense in E (see [49, Thm. 4.4.3.1]) and invariant with respect to the representation
of g on E ∞ . Moreover, by construction (E ∞ )K = EK , so EK is also dense in E ∞
with respect to the Garding topology. Since it is also invariant with respect to K,
it thus naturally carries the structure of a (g, K)-module (see [47, Section 3.3] for
the general definition).
Recall that a continuous representation of G on a locally convex vector space is
called topologically irreducible if every nontrivial G-invariant subspace is dense in
E. Similarly, a (g, K)-module is called algebraically irreducible if it does not contain
any nontrivial subspaces that are invariant under the action of both g and K.

The following simple result is well-known. We include the argument for the conve-

Lemma 6.1. Let G be Lie group with Lie algebra g, K ⊂ G a compact subgroup,
and E a continuous representation of G on a complete locally convex vector space. If
EK is an algebraically irreducible (g, K)-module, then E is topologically irreducible.
Proof. If F ⊂ E is a nontrivial and closed G-invariant subspace, then FK is a
nontrivial (g, K)-invariant subspace of EK and dense in F . Since EK is algebraically
irreducible, FK = EK , and thus F ⊂ E is dense, which shows the claim.             □
