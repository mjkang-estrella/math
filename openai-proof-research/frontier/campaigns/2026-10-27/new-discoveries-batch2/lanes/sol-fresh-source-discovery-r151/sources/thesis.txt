Abstract
The purpose of this thesis is to introduce the concept of continuous translation-invariant
curvature measures which generalizes the notion of smooth translation-invariant curvature
measures due to Bernig and Fu. Many of the classical theorems for the space Val of
continuous translation-invariant valuations translate to Curv. In particular, it is known
that Curv admits a grading analogous to the McMullen decomposition of Val. Moreover,
we equip Curv with a Banach space topology and we prove that Curv admits counterparts
to the characterization theorems of Hadwiger, McMullen and Klain–Schneider. Since Curv
is naturally an infinite dimensional representation of the general linear group GL (n, R),
we introduce the linear subspace of GL (n, R)-smooth vectors and conjecture that this
space coincides with the space of smooth translation-invariant curvature measures. This
conjecture is verified in certain degrees of homogeneity and in low dimensions. Inspired
by Alesker’s irreducibility theorem for Val, we also study Jordan–Hölder series in the
representation Curv. Our results lead us to conjecture that their length does not exceed
two for a given degree and parity.


Zusammenfassung
Der Zweck dieser Arbeit ist das Konzept stetiger translationsinvarianter Krümmungsmaße
einzuführen, was den Begriff der glatten translationsinvarianten Krümmungsmaße nach
Bernig und Fu verallgemeinert. Viele klassische Sätze über den Raum Val der stetigen
translationsinvarianten Bewertungen lassen sich auf Curv übertragen. Insbesondere ist
bekannt, dass Curv analog zur McMullen-Zerlegung von Val graduiert ist. Darüber hinaus
statten wir Curv mit einer Banachraum-Topologie aus und wir zeigen, dass Curv Analoga
für die Charakterisierungssätze von Hadwiger, McMullen und Klain–Schneider zulässt.
Da Curv auf natürliche Weise eine unendlich-dimensionale Darstellung der allgemeinen
linearen Gruppe GL (n, R) ist, führen wir den Untervektorraum der GL (n, R)-glatten
Vektoren ein und stellen die Vermutung auf, dass dieser Raum mit dem Raum der
glatten translationsinvarianten Krümmungsmaße übereinstimmt. Diese Vermutung wird
in gewissen Homogenitätsgraden und in niedrigen Dimensionen bewiesen. Inspiriert
durch Aleskers Irreduzibilitätssatz für Val betrachten wir auch Jordan-Hölder-Reihen der
Darstellung Curv. Unsere Ergebnisse führen uns zu der Vermutung, dass deren Länge für
gegebenen Homogenitätsgrad und gegebene Parität maximal zwei ist.

1.1 History of Curvature Measures
The so called intrinsic volumes are an important and classical notion in convex geometry.
For a given convex body K ⊆ Rn , the map
                            (0, ∞) → C, ε ↦→ voln (K + εB n )
is a polynomial of degree n, where B n ⊆ Rn denotes the unit ball. Therefore, there exist
coefficients µ0 (K) , . . . , µn (K) ∈ C such that the Steiner formula

                           voln (K + εB n ) =         ωi µn−i (K) εi
                                                i=0

holds, where the normalizing constants are given by ωi = voli B i . Let K denote the
                                                                 (︁ )︁

space of convex bodies in Rn . Then, the map K → C, K ↦→ µi (K) is called the i-th
intrinsic volume. The family of intrinsic volumes includes a lot of interesting geometric
functionals, such as the Euler characteristic µ0 = χ = 1 and the volume µn = voln .
Moreover, up to constants µ1 is the mean width and µn−1 is the surface area.
   To understand intrinsic volumes from a bigger picture point of view, the crucial notion
is that of a valuation. A map φ : K → C is called a valuation if
                        φ (K ∪ L) + φ (K ∩ L) = φ (K) + φ (L)
whenever K, L, K ∪ L ∈ K. Of particular importance is the space Val of continuous and
translation-invariant valuations which admits a rich structure theory, see Chapter 2 for
details. The general linear group GL (n, R) acts naturally on the space Val and therefore
also any subgroup of GL (n, R) does. This group action can be used to characterize the
intrinsic volumes as was shown by Hadwiger in [Had57].
Theorem (Hadwiger). A valuation φ ∈ Val is SO (n)-invariant if and only if there
exist λ0 , . . . , λn ∈ C such that φ = λ0 µ0 + . . . + λn µn .
   The notion of curvature measure has its origins in Federer’s influential paper [Fed59].
In this paper, he constructs curvature measures as localizations of the intrinsic volumes.
For all K ∈ K and i = 0, . . . , n, he constructs a complex Borel measure Φi (K) on Rn
with the crucial property that Φi (K, Rn ) = µi (K). In fact, he constructs measures Φi (A)
for the much larger class of sets A ⊆ Rn with positive reach. As we do not study sets
of positive reach in this thesis, we omit the definition here. Let M denote the space of
complex Borel measures. In the scope of Federer’s work, the assignment
                               Φi : K → M, K ↦→ Φi (K)
is called the i-th curvature measure of K. Note that a curvature measure is therefore not
a measure itself but a map that assigns a measure to a convex body. Besides introducing
the curvature measures, Federer also proves an integral representation for them.
   In the paper [Sch78], Schneider proves a localized version of Hadwiger’s theorem which
characterizes the curvature measures Φi by some simple properties.

1.3 Comparison With Related Work
In this section, we want to mention a few more works in connection with curvature
measures. They appear both in stochastic and integral geometry.
   In the paper [KW99], Kiderlen and Weil prove a decomposition of curvature measures
analogous to the McMullen decomposition of valuations. They then use this decomposition
to construct mixed curvature measures by applying Federer’s curvature measures to linear
combinations of convex bodies. This construction is a curvature measure version of the
classical notion of mixed volumes. Note that they do not use the terminology curvature
measure, instead they call maps K → M, which satisfy our list of axioms, translation
covariant continuous functionals which are locally defined.
   The work of Kiderlen and Weil has been successfully applied in stochastic geometry.
In particular, Weil himself studied Boolean models and Poisson processes using this
decomposition theorem, see [Wei15] and [Wei17]. As already mentioned, in the latter
paper Weil also proves that the valuation property follows from the remaining axioms.
The stochastic applications are also the reason for the additional assumption that Φ (·, β)
is measurable.
   It should be mentioned that the viewpoint of our work is quite different to that of
the three papers [KW99], [Wei15], [Wei17]. Beside the decomposition theorem and the



                                            6
                               1.4 Outline of This Work


valuation property, no other structural results are proved in these papers. Following
the decomposition theorem, [KW99] concentrates on some explicit constructions, while
[Wei15] and [Wei17] are concerned with applications to stochastic geometry. Our goal
however is to prove further structural results in analogy to the structure theory of Val.
   In a series of papers [HS14], [HS17a], [HS17b], [HS17c], Hug and Schneider study local
tensor valuations. These can be seen as a generalization of what we call curvature measures
as they take values in the space of Tp -valued measures rather than scalar-valued measures.
Here Tp denotes the space of symmetric p-tensors of Rn . On polytopes they give a full
classification under the assumptions of isometry covariance and local determination. They
also address the question which of these admit a weakly continuous continuation to all
convex bodies.
   Curvature measures also play an important role in the integral geometry of isotropic
spaces. At this point it should be mentioned that smooth valuations and curvature
measures admit a natural extension to smooth manifolds. An elaborate study of smooth
valuations on manifolds can be found in Alesker’s series of papers [Ale06a], [Ale06b],
[AF08] and [Ale07], whereas smooth curvature measures on manifolds were introduced
in [BF11]. A benefit of studying kinematic formulas for curvature measures instead of
valuations is that they admit a so called transfer principal, see Proposition 2.22 and
Theorem 2.23 in [BFS14]. This allows to study kinematic formulas on curved spaces by
means of the flat case. For a concise overview on the applications of smooth valuations
and curvature measures in integral geometry, the reader may also consult the survey
[Ber17] and the references therein.
   In his thesis [Sai16], Saienko studies smooth curvature measures from a representation
theoretic point of view. One easily sees that Curvsm is naturally a representation of
SO (n). Saienko’s main result computes the harmonic decomposition of Curvsm .
   Lastly, let us mention the recent work [Knö23] by Knörr in which he studies measure-
valued valuations on the space of convex functions Rn → R.

1.4 Outline of This Work
To conclude this introduction let us provide a brief overview of how this thesis is struc-
tured.
   In Chapter 2 we recall the relevant background from the theory of valuations. In
particular, the space Val is introduced and its structure theory is developed. More
precisely, the McMullen decomposition, the topology of Val and the GL (n, R)-action on
Val are discussed. We also recall the characterization theorems due to Hadwiger and
McMullen on homogeneous valuations of degree n and n − 1, respectively. Moreover, the
characterization of simple valuations due to Klain and Schneider is explained. Lastly, we
introduce smooth valuations and smooth curvature measures.
   In Chapter 3 we introduce the central notion of this thesis, namely continuous
translation-invariant curvature measures. After this we prove analogues for many of the
structure results from the setting of valuations. In analogy to the McMullen decompo-
sition we discuss the Kiderlen–Weil decomposition. Moreover, we equip Curv with the
aforementioned Banach space topology. We then study the two main technical tools,




                                            7
                              1.4 Outline of This Work


namely the globalization and the so called Bernig embedding in Section 3.5. Using these
tools, we prove analogues for the aforementioned characterization theorems by Hadwiger,
McMullen and Klain–Schneider.
   In Chapter 4 we switch our focus towards smooth curvature measures. To do so, we
introduce the GL (n, R)-action on Curv and obtain the subspace Curv∞ of smooth vectors.
We then study Curv from a representation theoretic point of view with the following two

   The concept of a valuation dates back at least to the beginning of the twentieth century
when Dehn solved Hilbert’s third problem. A simplified version of the problem asks
whether any two three-dimensional polytopes of equal volume are scissors-congruent.
Shortly after Hilbert posed the problem, Dehn was able to answer it negatively, see
[Deh01]. In doing so, he constructed a certain valuation on three-dimensional polytopes,
now known as the Dehn invariant, which is invariant under scissors-congruence. The
Dehn invariant of a cube is always equal to zero, while regular tetrahedra have non-zero
Dehn invariant. Hence, a cube and a regular tetrahedron of the same volume cannot be
scissors-congruent.

values in the infinite dimensional vector space of complex Borel measures on Rn . Another
important class are so called Minkowski valuations K → K, which were introduced by
Ludwig in [Lud05].

   We first need to recall some general constructions from representation theory. To do so,

inducing a unique topology on Curv. This construction is in many ways analogous to

4.4 Jordan–Hölder Series for Curvature Measures
Let V be a continuous Fréchet space representation of a Lie group G. A finite increasing

                        {0} = V0 ⊆ V1 ⊆ . . . ⊆ Vm−1 ⊆ Vm = V

of closed subrepresentations is called a Jordan–Hölder series of length m of V if the
representation Vi /Vi−1 is irreducible for all i = 1, . . . , m.

Definition 4.26. Let V be a continuous Fréchet space representation of a Lie group G
and let m ∈ N0 . We say that V has length m if V admits a Jordan–Hölder series of length
m.
