                                                                                       1. Introduction
                                           1.1. Background and principal results. To study the curvature of singular spaces, two
                                           different but related approaches have been developed. One is to find synthetic lower (or upper)
                                           bounds on curvature. The other is to view curvature as a generalized function. In his 1959
                                           paper [19], Federer showed that one can make sense of the elementary symmetric functions of
                                           the principal curvatures of smooth hypersurfaces as measures for sets of positive reach.
                                              Federer’s work has since been extended in several directions. One of them concerns the
                                           construction of curvature measures for other classes of singular sets, see [21, 22, 24, 42, 43, 53].
                                           In another direction, curvature measures in ambient spaces different from Rn have been in-
                                           vestigated [11–14, 25]. Federer’s curvature measures are also fundamental to convex geometry
                                           [48].
                                              Already Federer [19, Remark 5.17] asked for an axiomatic characterization of his curvature
                                           measures. A first such result was obtained by Schneider [46]. Considering Federer’s curvature
                                           measures as functions on the space of convex bodies (nonempty compact convex subsets of Rn ),
                                           his result is similar in spirit to Hadwiger’s characterization of the intrinsic volumes.
                                              This perspective on curvature measures was further developed by Kiderlen and Weil in a
                                           series of papers [35, 51, 52]. Motivated by applications to stochastic geometry [49, Chapter 11],
                                           Weil calls a function ϕ : K(Rn ) → C on the space of convex bodies in Rn local if it admits a
                                           kernel, that is, if there exists a map Φ : K(Rn ) → M (Rn ) to the space of complex Borel measures
                                           on Rn satisfying Φ(K, Rn ) = ϕ(K) and the following properties.
                                               (a) Locality. Let K, L be convex bodies in Rn . If there exists an open set U such that
                                                    K ∩ U = L ∩ U , then

                                                    for every Borel set β ⊆ U .
                                               (b) Translation invariance. Φ(K + x, β + x) = Φ(K, β) for every convex body K, Borel set
                                                    β, and x ∈ Rn .

                                                    in the sense of weak convergence of measures.

                                           Borel measurable. We will see below that this probability kernel property is in fact automatically

                                           the latter are the kernels of the former. Weil refrained from calling Φ a curvature measure and

1.2. Applications. We present two applications of the theory of translation invariant cur-
vature measures developed in this paper. As a first application, we streamline the axiomatic
framework underlying Weil’s theory of local functionals by showing that every translation invari-
ant curvature measure is automatically a probability kernel. The second application concerns
the characterization of Federer’s curvature measures, where our results allow us to remove the
hypothesis of nonnegativity from Schneider’s theorem [46].

mentioned above, Weil’s terminology differs slightly from ours. The central objects of [52] are

measure Φ in our sense, with the additional property that for every Borel set β, the function

   In this section, we introduce several key concepts from representation theory and we use them

6.1. Preliminaries from infinite-dimensional representation theory. We recall here some
definitions and foundational results from representation theory. For more information we refer
the reader to the paper [17] and the introductory textbook [38]. The results of this section,
which we will apply only for G = GL(n, R) and K = O(n), hold more generally for G the group
of R-rational points of a Zariski connected, reductive algebraic group defined over R and a
maximal compact subgroup K of G. Throughout E will be a complete locally convex Hausdorff
topological vector space over C.
   A linear representation of G on E is a group homomorphism π : G → GL(E), where GL(E)
denotes the group of invertible continuous endomorphisms of E. A representation is called
continuous if the map G × E → E, (g, v) 7→ π(g)v is continuous. The following lemma,
an immediate consequence of the principle of uniform boundedness, is useful for proving the
continuity of a representation.
Lemma 6.1. A linear representation (π, E) is continuous if and only if the following properties
hold:
   (a) For every v ∈ E, the map G → E, g 7→ π(g)v, is continuous; and
   (b) For every compact subset C ⊆ G, the set of operators {π(g) : g ∈ C} is equicontinuous:
       for every neighborhood V1 of zero in E there exists another neighborhood V2 of zero such
       that, for all g ∈ C,
                                        π(g)(V2 ) ⊆ V1 .
   If (σ, F ) is another continuous representation of G, a continuous linear map T : E → F
satisfying T ◦ π(g) = σ(g) ◦ T is called G homomorphism. The representation π is called
irreducible if {0} and E are the only closed invariant subspaces of E.
   A vector v ∈ E is called smooth if the map G → E, g 7→ π(g)v, is C ∞ . The subspace of
all smooth vectors is denoted by E ∞ , and it is invariant under G. The subspace E ∞ embeds
canonically into C ∞ (G, E), and it inherits from this embedding a canonical topology. The

8.2. Characterization of Federer’s curvature measures. We say that a curvature measure
Φ ∈ Curv(Rn ) is SO(n) invariant if
                                  k·Φ=Φ        for all k ∈ SO(n).
Proposition 8.2. Let n ≥ 2. If Φ ∈ Z(Rn ) is SO(n) invariant, then Φ = 0.
Proof. Without loss of generality, we may assume that Φ is homogeneous of degree i. If i = n,
there is nothing to prove. If i = n − 1, then the claim follows from Theorem 5.14.
   Suppose that i = n − 2. Then B(Φ) is homogeneous of degree n − 1 and SO(n) invariant.
Consequently, there exists a continuous function f : S n−1 → Cn satisfying (7) and kf (k −1 u) =
f (u) for every k ∈ SO(n) and u ∈ S n−1 . It follows that ⟨f (u), u⟩ = c is constant. Replacing f
by f (u) − cu if necessary, we may assume that, for all u ∈ S n−1 ,
                                           ⟨f (u), u⟩ = 0.
Since f (u) is invariant under the stabilizer subgroup of u in SO(n), we conclude that f (u) = 0
provided n ≥ 3. If n = 2, then SO(2) invariance implies that f is proportional to ρπ/2 u. In any
case, B(Φ) = 0 and hence Φ = 0 by the injectivity of the Bernig embedding.
  The degrees of homogeneity treated so far imply the proposition in particular in dimension
n = 2. Now let n ≥ 3 and assume that the proposition has been proved in dimension n − 1. Let
H be a linear hyperplane in Rn and denote by Φ|H ∈ Curv(H) the restriction of Φ to convex
bodies in H. Since Φ|H is SO(H) invariant, the inductive hypothesis implies Φ|H = 0. We
conclude that Φ is simple. The characterization of simple curvature measures (Theorem 5.15)
and the degrees of homogeneity treated so far imply Φ = 0, as desired.                         □
