QUADRATIC-SIZE RANDOM PERMUTATIONS ARE UNIVERSAL
YU CHEN AND YITING WANG

arXiv:2610.13530v1 [math.CO] 9 Oct 2026

Abstract. A permutation is n-universal if it contains every permutation of length n as a pattern.
We prove that a uniformly random permutation of length 32n2 is n-universal with probability at least
1 − exp(−cn/ log n), for an absolute constant c > 0 and all sufficiently large n. This removes a log log n
factor from an earlier result of He and Kwan [24] and resolves an open problem of Alon [2]. The proof
was found by GPT-6 Pro.

1. Introduction
Universality asks how small or sparse a combinatorial object can be while containing every member
of a prescribed family. Classical graph examples include Rado’s universal graphs [35] and induced
universal graphs for all n-vertex graphs [3, 33], bounded-degree graphs [7], and planar graphs [18]. For
ordinary subgraph containment, sparse universal graphs have been constructed for families with bounded
maximum degree [4], density [5], or degeneracy [1].
Random graphs also provide universal hosts, notably for bounded-degree spanning graphs [16, 19, 20]
and trees [15, 32]. For example, Montgomery [32] proved that G(n, C∆ log n/n) contains every n-vertex
tree of maximum degree at most ∆ with high probability, at the optimal order of edge density. Here
G(n, p) is the Erdős-Rényi random graph with edge probability p, and with high probability means with
probability tending to one. The randomly perturbed model adds sparse random edges to a graph of
linear minimum degree [9]; results include prescribed spanning embeddings [11, 28] and universality for
bounded-degree spanning trees [10] and graphs of maximum degree two [12, 34].
We study the corresponding questions for permutations, where containment must respect two linear
orders. Write [n] = {1, . . . , n} and let Sn denote the set of permutations of [n]. A permutation σ ∈ SN
contains π ∈ Sn as a pattern if there are indices 1 ≤ x1 < · · · < xn ≤ N such that
σ(xi ) < σ(xj )

⇐⇒

π(i) < π(j)

(i, j ∈ [n]).

The indices need not be consecutive. We call σ n-universal, or an n-superpattern, if it contains every
member of Sn . As in the graph setting, one may ask for the shortest universal permutation or for the
length at which a uniformly random permutation becomes universal with high probability.
Universality is closely related to pattern avoidance. The Stanley–Wilf conjecture asserts that, for
each fixed pattern π, the number aN (π) of permutations in SN avoiding π is at most CπN . Alon
and Friedgut [6] had earlier obtained an almost exponential upper bound. Marcus and Tardos [30]
proved it by establishing the Füredi–Hajnal conjecture [22]: an m × m zero–one matrix avoiding a fixed
permutation matrix has only Oπ (m) ones. The implication between the conjectures is due to
P Klazar [27];
Cibulka [14] later sharpened the quantitative relation between their constants. At most π∈Sn aN (π)
permutations of length N fail to be n-universal, so sufficiently strong uniform avoidance estimates would
imply random universality. However, Fox [21] showed that the optimal exponential constants can grow
superpolynomially with the pattern length; these fixed-pattern bounds therefore do not yield universality
in the quadratic regime considered here.

Theorem 1.1 removes the log log n factor from the bound of He and Kwan and resolves Alon’s weaker
1000n2 problem. Together with the increasing-subsequence obstruction, it determines the order of magnitude of the length required for random universality. The constant 32 is chosen for convenience; the
sharp constant 1/4 predicted by Alon’s conjecture remains open. In Section 4, we sketch how to reduce
the constant 32 to any value greater than 1 and explain why 1 is a natural barrier for this approach.
Regarding the non-universal probability, the bound exp(−Ω(n/ log n)) comes from the limitation of the
approach and is likely not sharp. We discuss that also in Section 4.
Our proof uses the interval-minor coupling of He and Kwan to pass to an independent zero–one matrix.
We split each target permutation into tuples of length O(log n) and search for them in successive column
strips, restarting an unfinished tuple in the next strip. Bounding the overlap between the supports of
these tests gives uniform control over all short tuples. A deterministic counting argument then yields
every target permutation, without a union bound over all n! patterns.
2. Interval minors
Let Pπ be the permutation matrix whose ones are in positions (π(i), i). Following Fox [21], an interval
contraction replaces two consecutive rows or columns of a zero–one matrix by their entrywise Boolean
OR. A matrix B is an interval minor of A if it can be obtained from A by interval contractions and
changing ones to zeros. This relation is transitive, and σ contains π as a pattern if and only if Pπ is an
interval minor of Pσ ; see [24]. We call A n-universal if every Pπ , π ∈ Sn , is an interval minor of A.
Lemma 2.1 ([24, Lemma 2.2]). Let N be divisible by 4n. A uniform random permutation σ ∈ SN and
a 2n × N/(4n) matrix A with independent Bernoulli(1/2) entries can be coupled so that A is always an
interval minor of Pσ .

4. Concluding remarks
4.1. Reducing the length of the random permutation. We established that a random permutation
of length 32n2 is n-universal with high probability. The constant 32 can be reduced to any constant
greater than 1 within the interval-minor framework. Here is a brief sketch of how to obtain this result.
Fix ε > 0 and choose δ ∈ (0, 1) with (1 + δ)3 < 1 + ε. Choose a sufficiently large constant Cδ , set
b = ⌈Cδ log n⌉ and r = ⌈n/b⌉, and put
R = n + ⌈δn⌉,

T = ⌈(1 + δ)r⌉,

L = ⌈(1 + δ)Rb⌉.

Take LT independent uniform variables Ui ∈ [0, 1], partition [0, 1] into R equal intervals Ia , and set
A(a, i) = 1{Ui ∈Ia } . After deleting empty rows, A is an interval minor of the induced permutation
matrix. Partition its columns into T strips of length L, and use the same tuple tests with s = R − n
shifts in the original band labels.
The entries of A are negatively associated: each column is a multinomial vector with one trial, and
independent unions preserve negative association [26]. Since bad-test events are decreasing, tests with
disjoint supports satisfy the same product upper bound as independent tests. Moreover, a greedy test fails
with probability at most P(Bin(L, 1/R) < b) ≤ e−aδ b by the Chernoff bound, where aδ = δ 2 /(2(1 + δ)).

provided Cδ is sufficiently large. Indeed, log(sT ) ≤ 2 log n + Oδ (1), while bkδ ≥ δs/b = Ωδ (n/ log n) and

any target, if all s searches failed, they would encounter at least s(T − r + 1) > δsr bad tests, whereas
its r tuples account for fewer than δsr bad tests. Thus every target is found.
Finally, LT = (1 + δ)3 n2 + Oδ (n log n) < N = ⌈(1 + ε)n2 ⌉ for large n. The pattern induced by the first
LT entries of a uniform permutation of length N is itself uniform in SLT . Hence a uniform permutation
of length N is n-universal with failure probability e−Ωε (n/ log n) .
Nevertheless, the leading constant 1 is a natural barrier for this approach because each search prescribes one of R ≥ n bands for every target entry. Even without strip boundaries or restarts, a fixed-shift
greedy search finishes with probability P(Bin(N, 1/R) ≥ n). If N ≤ (1 − η)n2 for fixed η ∈ (0, 1), this is
e−Ωη (n) by the Chernoff bound. A union bound over the O(n) available shifts therefore shows that these
searches typically miss even a fixed target. Thus, improving the leading constant below 1 requires new
ideas.
4.2. Sharper failure probability. Sharper failure estimates are suggested by the following conjecture
of He and Kwan.
Conjecture 4.1 ([24, Conjecture 5.1]). Let σ be a uniform random permutation of length 1000n2 . Then,
uniformly over π ∈ Sn ,
P(σ does not contain π) ≤ exp(−n3/2+o(1) ).
By a union bound, this would also give failure probability exp(−n3/2+o(1) ) for n-universality at length
1000n2 . If true, the exponent 3/2 is sharp, as shown by Fox [21].
Our current estimates do not reach Conjecture 4.1. For a general tuple length b,
√ the overlap bound
retains only ⌈n/b2 ⌉ disjoint tests, giving a probability factor e−Θ(n/b) when b = o( n). Counting their
possible locations costs exp(O((n/b2 ) log n)), and counting tuples costs exp(O(b log n)). Optimizing these
bounds takes b of order log n and yields an exponent of order n/ log n.
Moreover, the sufficient event E itself cannot yield a super-linear failure exponent. If the rows b +
1, 2b + 1, . . . , rb + 1 are zero throughout J1 , every shift of w = (1, . . . , b) fails in that strip: each interval
t + [b], t ∈ [n], contains one of these rows. This forces |Bw | ≥ n and has probability 2−3br . Hence
P(E c ) ≥ 2−3br ≥ 2−6n ,
where br < n + b ≤ 2n. Thus even a sharper estimate of P(E c ) cannot give the exponent n3/2+o(1)
in Conjecture 4.1. This local obstruction need not prevent containment; reaching that scale requires a
different sufficient event or more direct control of containment failure.
