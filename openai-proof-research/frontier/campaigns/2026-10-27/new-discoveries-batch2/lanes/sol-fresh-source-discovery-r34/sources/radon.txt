Abstract.


Consider the (almost surely) unique Radon partition of a set of nn random Gaussian vectors in ℝn−2\mathbb{R}^{n-2}; choose one of the two parts of this partition uniformly at random, and for 0≤k≤n0\leq k\leq n, let pkp_{k} denote the probability that it has size kk. In this paper, we prove strong unimodality results for the distribution (p0,…,pn)(p_{0},\dots,p_{n}).



2010 Mathematics Subject ClassificationPrimary 05A20; Secondary 05E05, 15A15, 58K05




1. Introduction




In 1921, Radon proved [29] that any set XX of nn points in ℝn−2\mathbb{R}^{n-2} can be partitioned — such a partition is called a Radon partition of XX — into two sets whose convex hulls intersect, and that if the points are in general position (as will be the case in what follows), then this partition is unique. Radon’s theorem is a foundational result in convex geometry [2, 15, 16], and was, for instance, used by Radon to give one of the early proofs of Helly’s theorem.




In this paper, we explore the behaviour of the Radon partition of a random set of nn points in ℝn−2\mathbb{R}^{n-2}, specifically, of a set of nn independent Gaussian vectors. For such a random set XX and for 0≤k≤n0\leq k\leq n, let pk=pk(n)p_{k}=p_{k}^{(n)} denote the probability that a randomly chosen part of its (almost surely) unique Radon partition has kk elements, and note that p0=pn=0p_{0}=p_{n}=0. Our goal in this paper is to understand this probability distribution 𝐩n=(p0,…,pn)\mathbf{p}_{n}=(p_{0},\dots,p_{n}), and to demonstrate in particular that 𝐩n\mathbf{p}_{n} has some strong unimodality properties.




The motivation to study this distribution 𝐩n\mathbf{p}_{n} is related to Sylvester’s ‘four-point question’ from 1864 that asks for the probability that four random planar points are in convex position. For Gaussian planar vectors, the answer — (p2)(4)(p_{2})^{(4)} in our notation — was given by Maehara [24]. The distribution 𝐩n\mathbf{p}_{n} arises naturally when studying higher-dimensional analogues of Sylvester’s question, and the problem of understanding this distribution was recently raised by Frick, Newman, and Pegden [17] and by White [38] in precisely this context. While an exact description of the distribution 𝐩n\mathbf{p}_{n} seems out of reach, we shall nevertheless establish a number of nontrivial properties of this distribution.




Main results




Let X={𝐗1,…,𝐗n}⊂ℝn−2X=\{\Xb_{1},\dots,\Xb_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-2} be a set of nn independent standard normal random vectors. Note that these points are in general position almost surely, so they almost surely admit a unique Radon partition; we denote this partition by {𝒜n,ℬn}\{\mathcal{A}_{n},\mathcal{B}_{n}\}. From these two sets, we choose one uniformly at random, which we call 𝒮n\cS_{n}, and we write Sn=|𝒮n|S_{n}=|\cS_{n}|. For n≥3n\geq 3 and 0≤k≤n0\leq k\leq n, let



(1.1)

pk=pk(n)=ℙ[Sn=k]p_{k}=p_{k}^{(n)}=\Pb[S_{n}=k]



be the probability that a randomly chosen part of the Radon partition of XX has size kk. Writing [n][n] for the set {1,…,n}\{1,\dots,n\}, our main result is the following theorem.



Theorem 1.1 (Ultra log-concavity).



For each n≥3n\geq 3, the distribution 𝐩n=(p0,…,pn)\mathbf{p}_{n}=(p_{0},\dots,p_{n}) is ultra log-concave, i.e., we have




(pk(nk))2≥pk+1(nk+1)​pk−1(nk−1)\left(\frac{p_{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{p_{k+1}}{\binom{n}{k+1}}\frac{p_{k-1}}{\binom{n}{k-1}}



for each k∈[n−1]k\in[n-1].





We prove Theorem 1.1 in Section 5.
The following consequence of Theorem 1.1 establishes a conjecture of White [38] asserting that the more balanced a partition is, the more likely it is to occur as the Radon partition of a Gaussian random set of points.



Corollary 1.2 (Unimodality).



For each n≥3n\geq 3, the distribution 𝐩n=(p0,…,pn)\mathbf{p}_{n}=(p_{0},\dots,p_{n}) is unimodal, i.e.,




p0≤p1≤⋯≤p⌊n2⌋=p⌈n2⌉≥⋯≥pn−1≥pn.p_{0}\leq p_{1}\leq\dots\leq p_{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}=p_{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil}\geq\dots\geq p_{n-1}\geq p_{n}.







We prove Theorem 1.1 by reducing it to a problem involving the log-concavity of sequences arising from the behaviour of percentiles of normal random variables. This is a problem of some independent interest and we shall say more about it below.
We also note that it remains an open problem to determine whether unimodality still holds when 𝐗1,…,𝐗n\Xb_{1},\ldots,\Xb_{n} are sampled uniformly from a bounded convex set KK, see Problem 7.1.





Log-concavity of Youden’s demon sequence




Let Y1,…,YnY_{1},\dots,Y_{n} be a set of nn independent standard normal random variables, and let Y¯=(Y1+⋯+Yn)/n\overline{Y}=({Y_{1}+\cdots+Y_{n}})/{n} denote their sample mean. For k∈[n]k\in[n], we write



(1.2)

for the probability that the sample mean is greater than the first kk samples and less than the last n−kn-k samples. The determination of these probabilities — this is the problem of Youden’s demon — has been the subject of extensive work in mathematical statistics; see [39, 20, 14, 17], for examples.




We shall deduce our main result, Theorem 1.1, from the following result about Youden’s demon problem.



Theorem 1.3 (Log-concavity).



For each n≥3n\geq 3, the distribution 𝐪n=(q0,…,qn)\mathbf{q}_{n}=(q_{0},\dots,q_{n}) is log-concave, i.e., we have



(1.3)

qk2≥qk+1​qk−1q_{k}^{2}\geq q_{k+1}q_{k-1}



for each k∈[n−1]k\in[n-1].





We prove Theorem 1.3 in Section 4.
We note that the assumption that Y1,…,YnY_{1},\ldots,Y_{n} are normally distributed is essential here (as we shall see in Section 6).
The reduction of Theorem 1.1 to Theorem 1.3 (see Lemma 5.1) more or less follows from the work of Frick, Newman, and Pegden [17] and White [38] who, in turn, rely on a general result by Baryshnikov and Vitale [12].




Let us also note that the assumption of normality cannot be dropped from Theorem 1.3; we shall exhibit an i.i.d. collection of non-normal random variables for which the corresponding sequence q0,…,qnq_{0},\dots,q_{n} is not unimodal, and therefore not log-concave.





Conditional associations




We will prove Theorem 1.3 as a consequence of a more general phenomenon about conditional associations, but to state the precise result driving this, we need some notation.




Two events AA and BB in a probability space are said to be positively associated if ℙ⁡[A∩B]≥ℙ⁡[A]​ℙ⁡[B]\Pb[A\cap B]\geq\Pb[A]\Pb[B], and we write A↑BA\uparrow B to denote positive association. Analogously, we say that two real-valued random variables XX and YY are positively associated if




{X≥s}↑{Y≥t}\{X\geq s\}\uparrow\{Y\geq t\}



for all s,t∈ℝs,t\in\mathbb{R}, or equivalently if




𝔼⁡[f⁡(X)​g​(Y)]≥𝔼⁡[f⁡(X)]​𝔼​[g⁡(Y)]\Eb[f(X)g(Y)]\geq\Eb[f(X)]\Eb[g(Y)]



for all increasing functions f,g:ℝ→ℝf,g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}; again, we write X↑YX\uparrow Y to denote positive association (though we note that this differs slightly from the usage of this terminology in [21]). Finally, for a collection of events 𝒞\cC, we say that XX and YY are positively associated given 𝒞\cC if X↑YX\uparrow Y conditional on any event in 𝒞\cC, i.e., if




ℙ[X≥s,Y≥t|C]≥ℙ[X≥s|C]ℙ[Y≥t|C]\Pb\left[X\geq s,Y\geq t\,|\,C\right]\geq\Pb\left[X\geq s\,|\,C\right]\Pb\left[Y\geq t\,|\,C\right]



for all s,t∈ℝs,t\in\mathbb{R} and C∈𝒞C\in\cC.




A sequence of random variables Z1,…,ZmZ_{1},\dots,Z_{m} is jointly normal if there exist independent normal random variables Y1,…,YnY_{1},\dots,Y_{n} such that each ZiZ_{i} is a linear combination of the YjY_{j}’s. An important property that we will use throughout this paper is that for any pair of random variables X,YX,Y that is jointly normal, XX is independent of YY if and only if Cov⁡(X,Y)=𝔼⁡[XY]−𝔼⁡[X]​𝔼​[Y]=0\Cov(X,Y)=\Eb[XY]-\Eb[X]\Eb[Y]=0; see e.g. [5], for a proof.




We shall study jointly normal random variables Z1,…,ZmZ_{1},\dots,Z_{m} with the following properties:


(1)



𝔼⁡[Zi]=0\Eb[Z_{i}]=0 for all i∈[m]i\in[m], 𝔼⁡[Zi​Zj]≤0\Eb[Z_{i}Z_{j}]\leq 0 for all distinct i,j∈[m]i,j\in[m], and


(2)



𝔼⁡[Zi​(Z1+⋯+Zm)]≥0\Eb[Z_{i}(Z_{1}+\dots+Z_{m})]\geq 0 for all i∈[m]i\in[m].



Note that 1 says that the covariance matrix of Z1,…,ZmZ_{1},\dots,Z_{m} is a Stieltjes matrix (see [22], for more details on this subject), while 2 tells us that each ZiZ_{i} has nonnegative covariance with the sample mean Z¯\overline{Z}. We call such a collection of random variables a repulsive–cooperative Gaussian ensemble.




With the convention that −∞≤a≤b≤∞-\infty\leq a\leq b\leq\infty whenever we refer to a closed interval [a,b][a,b], we have the following theorem about conditional associations of repulsive–cooperative Gaussian ensembles.



Theorem 1.4.



Let Z1,…,ZmZ_{1},\dots,Z_{m} be a repulsive–cooperative Gaussian ensemble, and let 𝒞\cC be the collection of events of the form



(1.4)

⋂i∈[m]{Zi∈[ai,bi]}.\bigcap_{i\in[m]}\left\{Z_{i}\in[a_{i},b_{i}]\right\}.



Then, for all i∈[m]i\in[m], Z¯\overline{Z} and ZiZ_{i} are positively associated given 𝒞\cC.





We prove Theorem 1.4 in Section 3.
Let us note that the positive association of Z¯\overline{Z} and ZiZ_{i} without conditioning is a consequence of 2; see [28] for a proof. However, conditioning on 𝒞\cC does not in general preserve positive associations (as we shall see in Section 6), so we need a more involved argument that exploits 1.

2. Background




In this section, we provide some background and historical context for the various problems discussed in Section 1.




Around Radon’s theorem




Recall that Radon’s theorem states that any set of nn points in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d}, where n≥d+2n\geq d+2, can be partitioned into two sets whose convex hulls intersect. This theorem was first proved by Radon [29] in 1921, and it has since become a cornerstone of convex geometry. Radon’s theorem was used by Radon to give a proof of Helly’s theorem that asserts that if a finite family of convex sets in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} (with at least d+1d+1 members) has the property that every d+1d+1 sets in the family have a point in common, then there is a point in common to all sets in the family. In 1966, Tverberg [35] published a far-reaching extension of Radon’s theorem conjectured a few years earlier by Birch.



Theorem 2.1.



Any (r−1)​(d+1)+1(r-1)(d+1)+1 points in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} can be partitioned into rr sets whose convex hulls intersect.





Any partition that satisfies the conditions of Theorem 2.1 is referred to as a Tverberg partition of order rr. It would be interesting to investigate analogues of our results for Tverberg partitions in place of Radon partitions, a topic about which we will say more later in Section 7. For more background on these results, see [2, 15, 16, 31, 11, 6].





Around Sylvester’s four-point question




In 1864, Sylvester asked for the probability that four random points in the plane are in convex position [34]. He proposed that the answer is 1/4 if the points are “taken at random in an indefinite plane”, and also asked what happens when the points are taken at random from a set K⊂ℝ2K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}. When the points are taken at random from the Gaussian distribution, then this probability is equal to p2(4)p_{2}^{(4)} (as defined in (1.1)), and understanding the distribution of the Radon partition of nn random points in ℝn−2\mathbb{R}^{n-2} is a natural higher-dimensional extension of Sylvester’s problem. For more on Sylvester’s four-point problem, involving extensions both to more points and to higher dimensions, see [34, 27, 1, 36].




For four Gaussian points in the plane, Sylvester’s problem has a nice answer going back to Maehara [24] (see also [7]).



Theorem 2.2.



The probability that four random Gaussian points in ℝ2\mathbb{R}^{2} are in convex position is




6π​arcsin⁡(13)≈0.649.\frac{6}{\pi}\arcsin(\frac{1}{3})\approx 0.649.







The study of Radon partitions of a set of higher-dimensional Gaussian vectors is a natural extension of Sylvester’s question that was recently studied by Frick, Newman, and Pegden [17] and by White [38]. For more historical context and relevant background, see White’s thesis [38] and the references therein.





Around unimodality and log-concavity




Unimodality is a very natural property that mathematicians perceive in a number of places, leading to a surge of conjectures about settings where this property may arise. In trying to demonstrate unimodality, it is quite natural to (attempt to) demonstrate log-concavity instead, since it is the more robust of the two properties. For instance, the convolution of two log-concave sequences remains log-concave, whereas unimodality is not preserved under such operations; see [23], for example. In this vein, our proof (Corollary 1.2) of White’s conjecture through Theorem 1.1 adheres to this general approach.




We point the reader to the surveys [9, 10, 33] for an overview of classical unimodality and
log-concavity results. It is worth mentioning that more recently, Huh and his collaborators have advanced an algebraic-geometric approach that has resolved a number of longstanding conjectures about log-concavity; see [19] for a survey,
and [13] for a more combinatorial perspective on this direction. However, our proof of Theorem 1.1 relies on a different approach, and is inspired by the work such as [25, 4, 21] that link log-concavity with negative association.





Around central limit theorems




One consequence of Theorem 1.1 is that the scaling limit of the distributions 𝐩n=(p0,…,pn)\mathbf{p}_{n}=(p_{0},\dots,p_{n}) as n→∞n\to\infty, if it exists, must be a log-concave distribution. It is therefore natural to inquire what this limiting distribution is; this is addressed by the following central limit theorem.



Theorem 2.3 (Central limit theorem).



As n→∞n\to\infty, we have




Sn−n/2σ​n→dist𝒩⁡(0,1),\frac{S_{n}-n/2}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\xrightarrow[\mathrm{dist}]{}\mathcal{N}(0,1),



where SnS_{n} is sampled from 𝐩n\mathbf{p}_{n}, and σ2=1/4−1/(2​π)\sigma^{2}={1}/{4}-{1}/{(2\pi)}.





It is possible to prove Theorem 2.3 by reducing the problem to an analogous central limit theorem for Youden’s demon, a version of which was established by Dmitrienko and Govindarajulu [14, Thm3.1] using the Bahadur representation of quantiles (see, e.g., [18]).
To keep the exposition self-contained, we include a more direct proof of Theorem 2.3 in Appendix A, using the second-moment method.

7. Further directions




Here, we discuss a number of directions for further research related to the results of this paper.




Random points in a prescribed set




The Gaussian model adopted in this paper is convenient for the analytic and empirical study of high-dimensional extensions for the Sylvester four-point question. However, it would be quite interesting to investigate the arguably more common setting for Sylvester’s question.



Problem 7.1.



Extend Theorem 1.1 (or its weaker corollaries) to the model of points drawn uniformly at random from a fixed dd-dimensional convex set KK.





It would also be interesting to prove a central limit theorem (like Theorem 2.3) for this model.





Radon partitions of larger sets




For fixed integers d,n∈ℕd,n\in\mathbb{N}, consider the probabilities pk′​(n,d)p^{\prime}_{k}(n,d) that, for a sequence of nn random Gaussian points 𝐗1,…,𝐗n\Xb_{1},\dots,\Xb_{n} in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d}, we have




Conv​{𝐗1,…,𝐗k}∩Conv​{𝐗k+1,…,𝐗n}≠∅.\textnormal{Conv}\{\Xb_{1},\dots,\Xb_{k}\}\cap\textnormal{Conv}\{\Xb_{k+1},\dots,\Xb_{n}\}\neq\emptyset.



Note that, when n=d+2n=d+2, we have




pk=pk′​(n,d)2​(nk),p_{k}=\frac{p^{\prime}_{k}(n,d)}{2\binom{n}{k}},



for each k∈{0,…,n}k\in\{0,\dots,n\}, where pk=pk(n)p_{k}=p_{k}^{(n)} is as defined in (1.1).




We conjecture the following generalization of Theorem 1.1.



Conjecture 7.2.



For all n≥d+2n\geq d+2,


(1)



p1′​(n,d)≤p2′​(n,d)≤⋯≤p⌊n/2⌋′​(n,d)p^{\prime}_{1}(n,d)\leq p^{\prime}_{2}(n,d)\leq\cdots\leq p^{\prime}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(n,d), and moreover,


(2)



the sequence (p1′​(n,d),…,pn′​(n,d))(p^{\prime}_{1}(n,d),\dots,p^{\prime}_{n}(n,d)) is log-concave.







The first claim in Conjecture 7.2 was proposed in [38], and it is supported by extensive computational evidence. Theorem 1.1 establishes Conjecture 7.2 when n=d+2n=d+2, lending further support for the conjecture.




These probabilities pk′​(n,d)p_{k}^{\prime}(n,d) also admit the following interpretation in terms of Youden’s demon problem:
suppose one has rr Gaussian vectors in ℝn\mathbb{R}^{n}, where r=n−d−1r=n-d-1. Then pk′​(n,d)p_{k}^{\prime}(n,d) is the probability that these vectors positively span a vector V∈ℝnV\in\mathbb{R}^{n} such that the first kk coordinates of VV are all at least the mean of its entries, and the last n−kn-k coordinates are all at most the mean. The equivalence between these two interpretations of pk′​(n,d)p^{\prime}_{k}(n,d) follows from the same argument as in Lemma 5.1.





Counting Tverberg partitions




Recall that Tverberg’s theorem asserts that any (d+1)​(r−1)+1(d+1)(r-1)+1 points in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} can be partitioned into rr sets whose convex hulls intersect; such a partition is called a Tverberg partition of order rr.
A long-standing conjecture due to Sierksma [32] on the minimal number of Tverberg partitions asserts the following.



Conjecture 7.3.



The minimum number m⁡(d,r)m(d,r) of (unordered) Tverberg partitions of a set of (d+1)​(r−1)+1(d+1)(r-1)+1 points in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} is ((r−1)!)d((r-1)!)^{d}.





We note that there are quite a few different configurations for which this minimum is attained; see [37, 8], for example.




We can also ask about configurations with the maximum number of Tverberg partitions, though now, we need to assume that the points are generic. We raise the following two closely related problems.



Problem 7.4.



What is the maximum number M⁡(d,r)M(d,r) of Tverberg partitions of a generic set of (d+1)​(r−1)+1(d+1)(r-1)+1 points in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d}? What is the maximum number P⁡(d,r)P(d,r) of partitions of a generic set of d⁡(r−1)+1d(r-1)+1 points in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} into rr parts whose positive hulls have a point in common?





Let us note that for d⁡(r−1)+1d(r-1)+1 points in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d}, it is not always the case that there exists a partition of this set into rr parts — such a partition is called a conic Tverberg partition — whose positive hulls share a point. It is, however, equivalent to Tverberg’s theorem that such a partition exists if the points are contained in an open half space; see e.g. [31] for a proof.




One may analyse Gaussian random sets to establish reasonable lower bounds for P⁡(d,r)P(d,r) as follows.



Theorem 7.5.



For fixed r∈ℕr\in\mathbb{N} and large dd, we have




P⁡(d,r)≳(r/2)mr!,P(d,r)\gtrsim\frac{(r/2)^{m}}{r!},



where m=d⁡(r−1)+1m=d(r-1)+1.




Proof.



Let N⁡(d,r)N(d,r) denote the number of (unordered) partitions {J1,…,Jr}\{J_{1},\dots,J_{r}\} of [m][m] into rr parts such that 1≤|Jk|≤d1\leq|J_{k}|\leq d for all k∈[d]k\in[d]. Note that, for large dd, N⁡(d,r)N(d,r) is asymptotically about rm/r!r^{m}/r!, i.e., the number of ways to partition a set of size mm into rr unordered parts, also known as the Stirling number of the second kind.




Let 𝐗1,…,𝐗m\Xb_{1},\dots,\Xb_{m} be be independent Gaussian vectors in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d}. We claim that, almost surely, for every such partition J1,…,JrJ_{1},\dots,J_{r}, there are signs si∈{−1,1}s_{i}\in\{-1,1\} and real numbers λi>0\lambda_{i}>0 (i∈[m]i\in[m]) such that



(7.1)

Conv​{λi​s​(i)​𝐗i:i∈J1}∩⋯∩Conv​{λi​s​(i)​𝐗i:i∈Jr}≠∅.\text{Conv}\{\lambda_{i}s(i)\Xb_{i}:i\in J_{1}\}\cap\cdots\cap\text{Conv}\{\lambda_{i}s(i)\Xb_{i}:i\in J_{r}\}\neq\varnothing.



Indeed, the linear span ⟨{𝐗i:i∈Jk}⟩\langle\{\Xb_{i}:i\in J_{k}\}\rangle has codimension d−|Jk|d-|J_{k}|, the sum of these codimensions is r​d−∑|Jk|=d−1<drd-\sum|J_{k}|=d-1<d, so the intersection of all these linear spans is at least one-dimensional, and therefore nonempty. Pick a unit vector in this intersection (uniformly at random), and then obtain sis_{i} and λi>0\lambda_{i}>0 for i∈Jki\in J_{k} by writing this vector as a linear combination of {𝐗i:i∈Jk}\{\Xb_{i}:i\in J_{k}\}.




Next, the probability for (7.1) to hold is the same for all choices of signs. Indeed, this follows from the fact that the normal distribution is invariant under sign-flips. It follows that the expected number of partitions where (7.1) holds with the all-one signs is at least N⁡(d,r)/2mN(d,r)/2^{m}, and of course, in such a case, the positive hulls of the parts of such a partition intersect. We conclude that P⁡(d,r)≥N⁡(d,r)/2mP(d,r)\geq N(d,r)/2^{m}. When dd is large, N⁡(d,r)N(d,r) is asymptotically rm/r!r^{m}/r!, and the claim follows.
∎





The argument above actually shows that P⁡(d,r)≳(rm/r!)⋅(1/Z)P(d,r)\gtrsim\ ({r^{m}}/{r!})\cdot({1}/{Z}), where ZZ is the probability that d⁡(r−1)+1d(r-1)+1 Gaussian points in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} admit a conic Tverberg partition; estimating ZZ is a problem of independent interest and it seems possible that ZZ behaves like 2−d+o⁡(1)2^{-d+o(1)} for large dd.




Finally, it seems reasonable to expect that P⁡(d+1,r)≲M⁡(d,r)P(d+1,r)\lesssim M(d,r), though we do not have a proper proof of such a relationship. Nevertheless, our belief is guided by the following intuition. Let 𝐗1,…,𝐗m∈ℝd+1\Xb_{1},\dots,\Xb_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1} be a set of m=(d+1)​(r−1)+1m=(d+1)(r-1)+1 points with the maximum number P⁡(d+1,r)P(d+1,r) of conic Tverberg partitions, and suppose that the origin is not contained in the convex hull of 𝐗1,…,𝐗m{\Xb_{1},\dots,\Xb_{m}}. Then there exists a hyperplane such that all these points lie on the right of this hyperplane. Projecting 𝐗1,…,𝐗m{\Xb_{1},\dots,\Xb_{m}} onto this hyperplane gives us a dd-dimensional set 𝐗1′,⋯,𝐗m′{\Xb_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,\Xb_{m}^{\prime}},
and note that any conic Tverberg rr-partition of 𝐗1,…,𝐗m\Xb_{1},\ldots,\Xb_{m} corresponds to a Tverberg rr-partition of 𝐗1′,…,𝐗m′\Xb_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\Xb_{m}^{\prime}.
This implies that P⁡(d+1,r)≤M⁡(d,r)P(d+1,r)\leq M(d,r) provided there exists a set with the right number of conic Tverberg partitions that does not contain the origin in its convex hull.




If it is in fact the case that P⁡(d+1,r)≲M⁡(d,r)P(d+1,r)\lesssim M(d,r), then this would imply that M⁡(d,r)≳(r/2)m/r!M(d,r)\gtrsim{(r/2)^{m}}/{r!}, where m=(d+1)​(r−1)+1m=(d+1)(r-1)+1, which should be contrasted with the bound of m⁡(d,r)≲(r/e)mm(d,r)\lesssim(r/e)^{m} implied by Sierksma’s Conjecture (and Stirling’s formula).

In the context of counting Tverberg partitions, some numerical results are worth mentioning. We used simulation with Gaussian random points to study the possible Tverberg partitions of order three for seven points in generic position in the plane. In these simulations, the minimum number of Tverberg partitions that we encountered was four (in agreement with Sierksma’s conjecture), and the maximum was seven (as seen by the vertices of a regular 7-gon).





Reay’s Conjecture




Micha Perles [26] proposed using the probabilistic method to show that,
for fixed r∈ℕr\in\mathbb{N} and dd large enough, every set of 2​d+22d+2 points in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} can be partitioned into rr sets — such a partition is called a Reay partition — whose convex hulls pairwise intersect. If true, this would refute a conjecture of Reay [30]. Some modest evidence for such a claim comes from simulations in [38] which suggest that for six random Gaussian points in the plane, the probability that a Reay partition exists is ≈0.427\approx 0.427.
