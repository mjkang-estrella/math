Constrained Flips in Plane Spanning Trees



Oswin Aichholzer


Joseph Dorfer


Birgit Vogtenhuber



Abstract


A flip in a plane spanning tree TT is the operation of removing one edge from TT and adding another edge
such that the resulting structure is again a plane spanning tree. For trees on a set of points in convex position we study two classic types of constrained flips:
(1) Compatible flips are flips in which the removed and inserted edge do not cross each other.
We relevantly improve the previous upper bound of 2​n−O⁡(n)2n-O(\sqrt{n}) on the diameter of the compatible flip graph to 5​n3−O⁡(1)\frac{5n}{3}-O(1),
by this matching the upper bound for unrestricted flips by Bjerkevik, Kleist, Ueckerdt, and Vogtenhuber [SODA 2025] up to an additive constant of 11. We further show that no shortest compatible flip sequence removes an edge that is already in its target position. Using this so-called happy edge property, we derive a fixed-parameter tractable algorithm to compute the shortest compatible flip sequence between two given trees.
(2) Rotations are flips in which the removed and inserted edge share a common vertex.
Besides showing that the happy edge property does not hold for rotations, we improve the previous upper bound of 2​n−O⁡(1)2n-O(1) for the diameter of the rotation graph to 7​n4−O⁡(1)\frac{7n}{4}-O(1).

1 Introduction




Let SS be a finite point set in the plane in general position, that is, no three points lie on a common line. We call SS a convex point set if no point in SS lies in the interior of the convex hull of SS.
A plane straight-line graph of SS is a graph with vertex set SS and whose edges are straight line segments between pairs of points of S such that no two edges intersect, except at a common endpoint. All graphs considered in this paper are straight-line graphs. For brevity we will omit the term straight-line. A plane spanning tree is a plane connected graph with n−1n-1 edges. In a convex point set, edges appear in two forms. Convex hull edges are edges that lie on the boundary of the convex hull of the point set. Edges that are not convex hull edges are diagonals.



Flip Graphs of Plane Spanning Trees.




A flip between two plane spanning trees of SS is the operation that removes an edge from a tree and adds another edge such that the resulting structure is again a plane spanning tree. We also denote this operation as an unrestricted flip.
A constrained version of flips are so-called compatible flips, for which the removed edge and the added edge are not allowed to cross. Rotations are a restricted subclass of compatible flips for which we additionally require the removed edge and the added edge to share a common vertex. The most restricted and local operation is a so called slide. A slide is an operation in which the added edge (u,v)(u,v) and the removed edge (v,w)(v,w) are again required to share a common vertex, additionally, the triangle Δ⁡(u,v,w)\Delta(u,v,w) must not contain any other vertex and the edge (u,w)(u,w) has to be contained in the current tree. This can visually be interpreted as sliding the edge (u,v)(u,v) along the edge (u,w)(u,w) into the edge (v,w)(v,w).
The (compatible) flip graph of plane spanning trees of SS has as its vertex set all such trees.
Two vertices T1T_{1}, T2T_{2} of this flip graph are connected with an edge if and only if T1T_{1} can be transformed into T2T_{2}
by a single (compatible) flip. See Figure 1 for an example of flips in plane spanning trees.


Figure 1: Four flips in a plane spanning tree. (1) unrestricted flip, (2) compatible flip, (3) rotation, (4) slide




Given an initial tree TinT_{\text{in}} and a target tree TtarT_{\text{tar}}, a (compatible) flip sequence from TinT_{\text{in}} to TtarT_{\text{tar}} is a path from TinT_{\text{in}} to TtarT_{\text{tar}} in the (compatible) flip graph. The (compatible) flip distance between TinT_{\text{in}} and TtarT_{\text{tar}} is the length of a shortest path between TinT_{\text{in}} and TtarT_{\text{tar}} in the flip graph.
Any (compatible) flip sequence of this length is called a shortest (compatible) flip sequence. Similarly, we define the rotation graph, rotation sequences and the rotation distance. See Figure 2 for an illustration of the concepts.


Figure 2: Flip graph of plane spanning trees on four vertices in convex position. Dashed edges denote flips that are part of the flip graph, but not the compatible flip graph. The dotted lines are compatible flips, but not rotations and the dash dotted lines are rotations but not slides. One pair of two plane spanning trees is marked in orange. Their shortest flip sequence of length one is marked in red (thicker dashed). A shortest compatible flip sequence of length two is marked in blue (thicker).




For graph reconfiguration problems three natural questions arise: (1) Is the flip graph connected? (2) What is the diameter of the flip graph? (3) What is the complexity of computing shortest flip sequences between two specific configurations?




Connectedness and Diameter.




For any nn-point set SS, the flip graph of plane spanning trees on SS
is known to be connected and has radius exactly n−2n-2.
The same holds for the compatible flip graph and the rotation graph; see [4, 5].
Hence, the diameter always lies between n−2n-2 and 2​n−42n-4.
Since a lower bound for the diameter of ⌊32​n⌋−5\big\lfloor\frac{3}{2}n\big\rfloor-5 for convex nn-point sets was shown in [11], the flip graph
of plane spanning trees on convex point sets has received considerable attention.
However, despite a prevailing conjecture that the diameter is at most 32​n+O⁡(1)\frac{3}{2}n+O(1) [8, 13], even improving the upper bound to 2​n−o⁡(n)2n-o(n) took surprisingly long.
In the last few years, the upper bound for its diameter was improved to 2​n−Ω⁡(l​o​g​(n))2n-\Omega(log(n)) in [1] and soon after to 2​n−Ω⁡(n)2n-\Omega(\sqrt{n}) in [8].
The upper bound from [8] is constructive and, though not stated explicitly, only uses compatible flips.
Hence it also provides an upper bound for the diameter of the according compatible flip graph.
In [7], the diameter was bounded from above by c​ncn with c=112​(22+2)≈1.95c=\frac{1}{12}\left(22+\sqrt{2}\right)\approx 1.95, which marked the first linear improvement over the initial bound from [4].
Very recently, a lower bound of 149​n−O⁡(1)\frac{14}{9}n-O(1) and an upper bound of 53​n−3\frac{5}{3}n-3 on the diameter were achieved in [5],
where the upper bound in general requires non-compatible flips.
It is also known that any plane spanning tree can be transformed into any other using Θ⁡(n2)\Theta(n^{2}) many slides [3]. For a survey on further flip operations in trees with multiple flips in parallel as well as flips with labeled edges, we refer to [17].




Happy Edges and Complexity.




For any graph reconfiguration problem, happy edges are edges that lie in both the initial and the target graph.
A flip graph fulfills the happy edge property if there exists a shortest flip sequence between any two graphs that never flips happy edges.
The happy edge property often is a good indication for the complexity of a graph reconfiguration problem.
For example, for triangulations of simple polygons [2] and general point sets [15, 18],
finding shortest flip sequences is computationally hard. The hardness proofs
use counterexamples to the happy edge property as a key ingredient.
Conversely, the happy edge property is known to hold for triangulations of convex polygons [19].
Though the complexity of finding shortest flip sequences is still open, the happy edge property yields multiple fixed-parameter tractable algorithms [6, 10, 14, 16].
Moreover,
the happy edge property holds for plane perfect matchings of convex point sets and shortest flip sequences can be found in polynomial time [12]. For plane spanning trees in convex position the happy edge property was conjectured to hold [1, 13] for different types of flips. Up to now, it has only been disproved for slides [1].




Contribution and Outline.




We will start by improving upper bounds on the flip graph for restricted flip types in convex point sets. In Section 2 we improve the upper bound on the diameter of the compatible flip graph from 2​n−n2n-\sqrt{n} in [8] to 53​(n−1)\frac{5}{3}(n-1). Section 3 is dedicated to improving the upper bound on the rotation graph from 2​n−O⁡(1)2n-O(1) in [4] to 74​(n−1)\frac{7}{4}(n-1).




Afterwards we deal with the happy edge property. In Section 4 we provide a more refined framework for happy edge properties and in Section 5 we prove the happy edge property for compatible flips in convex point sets and provide a counterexample to the happy edge property for rotations.




Finally, in Section 6 we use variants of the happy edge property to show fixed-parameter tractability of the flip distance for both unrestricted and compatible flips.




Due to space restrictions many details have been moved to the appendix. Such results will be marked with (⋆)(\star).






2 Bounding the Diameter of the Compatible Flip Graph




This section is devoted to improving the upper bound on the diameter of the compatible flip graph.
All the currently known improvements to the upper bound of the length of shortest flip sequences for unrestricted flips are
based on two different ways to handle edges:
They either invest two flips to flip an edge from Tin∖TtarT_{\text{in}}\setminus T_{\text{tar}} to a convex hull edge and later flip that convex hull edge to an edge of Ttar∖TinT_{\text{tar}}\setminus T_{\text{in}}, or perform a single perfect flip, that is, a flip from an edge of Tin∖TtarT_{\text{in}}\setminus T_{\text{tar}} to an edge of Ttar∖TinT_{\text{tar}}\setminus T_{\text{in}}.
Upper bounding the length of a flip sequence is then achieved by lower bounding the number of perfect flips.
Note that any such flip sequence induces a pairing of the edges of Tin∖TtarT_{\text{in}}\setminus T_{\text{tar}} with edges of Ttar∖TinT_{\text{tar}}\setminus T_{\text{in}}.




To obtain our improved bound for the diameter of the compatible flip graph,
we build on strategies for bounding the diameter of the flip graph with unrestricted flips from [5]. We give an overview of their proof and highlight the parts where we provide adjustments to make the flip sequence compatible.




The pairing of edges in [5] is obtained as follows: The authors consider the linear representation of a tree TT on a convex point set as illustrated in Figure 3. The linear representation is obtained by cutting the convex point set between two consecutive vertices on the convex hull and unfolding the tree TT such that its vertices are on a straight line.


Figure 3: A tree TT on a convex point set (left) and a linear representation of TT (right)




The linear representation induces a linear order v1,…,vnv_{1},\ldots,v_{n} of the vertices of TT.
With this linear order, the length of an edge e=(vi,vj)e=(v_{i},v_{j}), where i<ji<j, is defined as j−ij-i.
Further, the edge ee covers all vertices {vi,…,vj}\{v_{i},\ldots,v_{j}\} as well as any edge (vk,vℓ)(v_{k},v_{\ell}) with k,ℓ∈{i,…,j}k,\ell\in\{i,\ldots,j\}.




A gap {vi,vi+1}\{v_{i},v_{i+1}\} is an edge between two consecutive vertices in the linear order (which might or might not be present in the tree TT).
Next, the authors define a bijection (Lemma 3.1 in [5]) between gaps and edges of TT,
where each gap is in bijection with the shortest edge of TT that covers that gap.
The edges are then partitioned into
categories based on how many vertices they share with their corresponding gap (vi,vi+1)(v_{i},v_{i+1}); see Figure 4 for an illustration.






Concretely, an edge (u,v)(u,v) is a …


•



short edge if {u,v}={vi,vi+1}\{u,v\}=\{v_{i},v_{i+1}\}


•



near edge if |{u,v}∩{vi,vi+1}|=1\lvert\{u,v\}\cap\{v_{i},v_{i+1}\}\rvert=1


•



wide edge if |{u,v}∩{vi,vi+1}|=0\lvert\{u,v\}\cap\{v_{i},v_{i+1}\}\rvert=0




Figure 4: Edges e1e_{1}, e3e_{3} and e5e_{5} are short edges for gaps g1g_{1}, g3g_{3} and g5g_{5} respectively, e4e_{4} is a near edge for g4g_{4}, and e2e_{2} is a wide edge for g2g_{2}.




The authors show that the number of short edges in any tree TT is always larger than the number of wide edges.
In a next step, they construct an edge-edge-bijection between the edges in TinT_{\text{in}} and TtarT_{\text{tar}}
by concatenating the edge-gap-bijection
for TinT_{\text{in}} with the gap-edge-bijection of TtarT_{\text{tar}}.
An edge e∈Tine\in T_{\text{in}} and its image from the edge-edge-bijection e′∈Ttare^{\prime}\in T_{\text{tar}}
form a pair. If an edge ee is in both TinT_{\text{in}} and TtarT_{\text{tar}}, and ee is in bijection to itself, then ee is not flipped at all.
Note that any edge can be flipped to its assigned gap by a single (compatible) flip.
The authors use this and the relation between the numbers of short and wide edges in each tree
to show that all pairs of edges containing at least one short or wide edge can be flipped using an average of 32\frac{3}{2} flips per pair.
This is done by flipping every edge in such a pair to its corresponding gap and later from its gap to its target edge,
which takes two flips minus the number of short edges in the pair.




The remaining pairs then only consist of near edges. These pairs can further be partitioned into three sets as illustrated in Figure 5.


•



above pairs, where ee and e′e^{\prime} share a vertex and ee is longer than e′e^{\prime}.


•



below pairs, where ee and e′e^{\prime} share a vertex and ee is shorter than e′e^{\prime}.


•



crossing pairs, where ee and e′e^{\prime} cross, or equivalently, ee and e′e^{\prime} share a different vertex with their gap.




Figure 5: Illustration of above, below and crossing pairs of edges. Edges of TinT_{\text{in}} are drawn above the vertices, while edges of TtarT_{\text{tar}} are drawn below the vertices.
The respective gap is denoted by gg.




The sets of above, below and crossing pairs are then denoted by AA, BB, and CC, respectively.
The authors provide a flip sequence that flips all pairs in AA (resp. BB, CC) directly [5, Lemma 3.2].
Since a direct flip of an above or below pair is compatible, this implies the following lemma.



Lemma 1.



Assume that in the linear representation, the trees TinT_{\text{in}} and TtarT_{\text{tar}} consist only of short edges and
pairs of edges
in AA (resp. BB).
Then there is a compatible flip sequence from TinT_{\text{in}} to TtarT_{\text{tar}} of length |A|\lvert A\rvert (resp. |B|\lvert B\rvert).





In contrast, no direct flip between two edges of a pair in CC is compatible, since any such pair is crossing.
In Appendix A we develop an approach to flip these edges
such that the resulting flip sequence is compatible while using only one additional flip.



Lemma 2 (⋆\star).



Assume that in the linear representation, the trees TinT_{\text{in}} and TtarT_{\text{tar}} consist only of short edges and
pairs of edges in CC.
Then there is a compatible flip sequence from TinT_{\text{in}} to TtarT_{\text{tar}} of length |C|+1\lvert C\rvert+1.





To obtain their upper bound on the flip distance between two trees TinT_{\text{in}}, TtarT_{\text{tar}},
the authors cut the original instances TinT_{\text{in}}, TtarT_{\text{tar}} along happy diagonals, handle each of the resulting subinstances separately,
and then add up the flips needed in each of the subinstances.
Let bb denote the number of happy convex hull edges, cc the number of happy diagonals, and dd the cardinality of Tin∖TtarT_{\text{in}}\setminus T_{\text{tar}}.
Using that b+c+d=n−1b+c+d=n-1,
their resulting bound [5, Theorem 4.1]
is 53​d+23​b−43≤53​(n−1)−43\frac{5}{3}d+\frac{2}{3}b-\frac{4}{3}\leq\frac{5}{3}(n-1)-\frac{4}{3}.
With similar calculations, we show the following theorem for compatible flips in Appendix A.



Theorem 3 (⋆\star).



Let TinT_{\text{in}} and TtarT_{\text{tar}} be two plane spanning trees in convex point sets with nn vertices.
Then, there exists a compatible flip sequence from TinT_{\text{in}} to TtarT_{\text{tar}} of length at most 53​d+23​b+c−13≤53​(n−1)−13\frac{5}{3}d+\frac{2}{3}b+c-\frac{1}{3}\leq\frac{5}{3}(n-1)-\frac{1}{3}, where d=|Tin∖Ttar|=|Tin​Δ​Ttar|2d=\lvert T_{\text{in}}\setminus T_{\text{tar}}\rvert=\frac{\lvert T_{\text{in}}\Delta T_{\text{tar}}\rvert}{2} and where bb and cc denote the number of happy convex hull edges and happy non-convex-hull edges, respectively.





We remark that our bound with respect to the parameters dd, bb, and cc can be relevantly higher than the one in [5, Theorem 4.1] if the two trees share many happy diagonals.
This behaviour
was not unexpected due to the following result:
In [9, Theorem 1.6], the authors provide examples of pairs of plane spanning trees with (unrestricted) flip distance dd and compatible flip distance 2​d2d.
The according tree pairs have
c≈n2c\approx\frac{n}{2} happy diagonals.
However, any pair of trees that could reach the upper bounds in [5, Theorem 4.1] and Theorem 3 must have c=0c=0. So while for certain instances the flip distance and the compatible flip distance may differ strongly, extremal examples that determine the diameter are not affected.





3 Bounding the Diameter of the Rotation Graph




Next, we show that for the even more constrained rotation as flip operation, the diameter of the resulting flip graph is linear with leading coefficient less than two.




For this purpose we again construct a pairing of edges such that a perfect flip can be performed for many of them. The challenge that arises, compared to unrestricted or compatible flips, is that rotations require the removed and the added edge to share a vertex. This rules out the gap based pairing from the last section, since in crossing pairs, edges do not share vertices and the same holds for most pairs that contain a wide edge.



Lemma 4.



For a plane spanning tree TT on a point set SS and a vertex v∈Sv\in S there exists a bijection ρT,v\rho_{T,v} between vertices in S∖{v}S\setminus\{v\} and edges in TT such that ρT,v​(w)\rho_{T,v}(w) is incident to ww for every vertex w∈S∖{v}w\in S\setminus\{v\}.




Proof.



We root the tree TT at vv and orient every edge away from vv such that vv forms the unique source of the oriented graph; see Figure 6 for an illustration.
Now map each vertex in S∖{v}S\setminus\{v\} to its incoming edge.
∎




Corollary 5.



Let T1T_{1} and T2T_{2} be two plane spanning trees on a point set SS and let vv be a vertex in SS.
Then there exists a pairing of edges from T1T_{1} with edges in T2T_{2} such that:


•



Two edges that are paired together share a vertex.


•



Different pairs of edges share a different vertex.


•



No pair of edges has vv as its shared vertex.






Proof.



The pairing {(ρT1,v(w),ρT2,v(w)∣w∈S∖{v}}\{(\rho_{T_{1},v}(w),\rho_{T_{2},v}(w)\mid w\in S\setminus\{v\}\} induced by the bijection in Lemma 4 has all the desired properties.
∎

This will lead to cases in which we rotate happy (convex-hull) edges.
We allow this on purpose, since we will see in Section 5 that the happy edge property does not hold for rotations.

4 Relations of Happy Edge Properties




In this section, we introduce a more refined distinction of happy edge properties.



Definition 15.



A graph reconfiguration problem, in which flips exchange edges,
fulfills the


•



(weak) happy edge property,
if, from any initial graph GinG_{\text{in}} to any target graph GtarG_{\text{tar}}, there exists a shortest flip sequence that does not flip happy edges.


•



strong happy edge property,
if, from any initial graph GinG_{\text{in}} to any target graph GtarG_{\text{tar}}, any shortest flip sequence does not flip happy edges.


•



perfect flip property
if, whenever we can perform a flip ff in GinG_{\text{in}} such that the resulting graph G1G_{1} has one edge more in common with GtarG_{\text{tar}} than GinG_{\text{in}} has in common with GtarG_{\text{tar}}, then there exists a shortest flip sequence from GinG_{\text{in}} to GtarG_{\text{tar}} that has G1G_{1} as its first intermediate graph (that is, the flip sequence starts with ff and ff is called a perfect flip).







Further, we provide a framework, that allows us to compare the different properties.



Proposition 16 (⋆\star).



Let PP be a graph reconfiguration problem where flips exchange one edge at a time.


(i)



If any flip sequence in PP that flips at least one happy edge can be shortened by at least two flips, then PP fulfills the perfect flip property.


(ii)



If for any flip in PP from GG to (G∖{e1})∪{e2}(G\setminus\{e_{1}\})\cup\{e_{2}\}, e1e_{1} and e2e_{2} can never be in the same configuration, then the reverse direction in (i) holds.







We remark that all the introduced properties
may or may not hold for
certain graph reconfiguration problems. While the condition in Proposition 16(ii) is not fulfilled for trees, it does hold for example for triangulations.





5 Happy Edges in Plane Spanning Trees on Convex Sets




In [1], the authors formulated the Weak Happy Edge Conjecture for trees on convex point sets:



Conjecture 17 (Conjecture 17 in [1]).



For any two plane spanning trees TinT_{\text{in}} and TtarT_{\text{tar}} on a convex point set, there is a shortest flip sequence from TinT_{\text{in}} to TtarT_{\text{tar}} that does not flip happy edges.





Based on an example from [1] for a different context, we first observe that the perfect flip property holds neither for unrestricted flips nor for compatible flips on trees.


Figure 12: Counterexample to the perfect flip property based on [1, Figure 7].
The top shows the shortest flip sequence. The bottom sequence starts with two perfect flips, but reaches a point where no more perfect flips are possible. Since there are two edges in the tree T∗T^{\ast} that both cross two edges from the target tree, at least three additional flips or four additional compatible flips are needed.




In contrast, we will show in this section that the strong happy edge property does hold for compatible flips on trees.
Our first proof ingredient is the following lemma, which is an extension of [1, Proposition 18] for compatible flips.



Lemma 18 (⋆\star).



Consider any point set SS and any two plane spanning trees TinT_{\text{in}} and TtarT_{\text{tar}} on SS and any shortest compatible flip sequence from TinT_{\text{in}} to TtarT_{\text{tar}}. If some edge ee is removed and later added back, then some flip during that subsequence must add an edge ff that crosses ee.





In [1], a parking edge is defined as an edge that appears in a flip sequence and that is not contained in Tin∪TtarT_{\text{in}}\cup T_{\text{tar}}.
A second ingredient of our proof is Lemma 20,
which verifies the compatible flip analogue of the following conjecture from [1].



Conjecture 19 (Parking Edge Conjecture, Conjecture 21 in [1]).



For any convex point set SS and any two plane spanning trees TinT_{\text{in}} and TtarT_{\text{tar}} on SS, there is a shortest flip sequence from TinT_{\text{in}} to TtarT_{\text{tar}} that only uses parking edges from the boundary of the convex hull of SS.





We remark that in [1, Claim 22] it is shown that for unrestricted flips Conjecture 19 implies Conjecture 17.



Lemma 20 (⋆\star).



For any convex point set SS and any two plane spanning trees TinT_{\text{in}} and TtarT_{\text{tar}} on SS, there is a shortest compatible flip sequence from TinT_{\text{in}} to TtarT_{\text{tar}} that only uses parking edges from the boundary of the convex hull of SS.



Figure 13: Reordering flips in Lemma 20. The top path is the part of the original flip sequence that contains ff, the bottom path is the part of the reordered flip sequence that contains hh.




Figure 13 shows the intuition behind the proof: One by one, we replace a parking edge ff that is not on the boundary of the convex hull with a parking edge hh on the convex hull boundary. To make this replacement possible, we change the order of the flips that happen while ff is part of the tree. The edge ff splits the convex point set into two sides, say AA and BB. Flips in either of the two sides can be executed independently from flips in the other side. Assume ff is added when flipping from the tree Ti1−1T_{i_{1}-1} to Ti1T_{i_{1}} and removed in the flip from Ti2T_{i_{2}} to Ti2+1T_{i_{2}+1}. Exactly one of the sides, say side BB, of Ti1−1T_{i_{1}-1} entirely contains a path that connects the two endpoints of ff. The flips in the other side, say side AA, can be executed before ff gets added. Afterwards, we close a cycle in side AA by adding the convex hull parking edge hh and execute all the flips in side BB. We conclude the new subsequence of flips by removing hh and obtain the tree Ti2T_{i_{2}}.




We now show the strong happy edge property for compatible flips on trees.



Theorem 21.



For any convex point set SS and any two plane spanning trees TinT_{\text{in}} and TtarT_{\text{tar}} on SS,
any compatible flip sequence from TinT_{\text{in}} to TtarT_{\text{tar}} that removes (and adds) a happy edge is at least one step longer than the shortest compatible flip sequence from TinT_{\text{in}} to TtarT_{\text{tar}}.




Proof.



Let Tin=T0T_{\text{in}}=T_{0}, T1T_{1},…,Tk=TtarT_{k}=T_{\text{tar}} be a compatible flip sequence that removes a happy edge ee in a flip from TiT_{i} to Ti+1T_{i+1}. By Lemma 20, there exists a shortest compatible flip sequence from Ti+1=Ti+1′T_{i+1}=T_{i+1}^{\prime},Ti+2′T_{i+2}^{\prime},…,Ttar′=TtarT^{\prime}_{\text{tar}}=T_{\text{tar}} that only uses parking edges from the convex hull boundary.
Since ee crosses neither an edge of Tin∩TtarT_{\text{in}}\cap T_{\text{tar}} nor one on the convex hull boundary, ee crosses no edge in the flip sequence T0T_{0},…,TiT_{i},Ti+1′T^{\prime}_{i+1},…,TtarT_{\text{tar}}. Hence, by Lemma 18, we can construct a shorter flip sequence that preserves ee.
∎





By Proposition 16(i) and the example in Figure 12, Theorem 21 is best possible in the sense that there are flip sequences that flip a happy edge and cannot be shortened by two flips.




When we restrict the flip even further by only allowing edge rotations instead of all compatible exchanges, the happy edge property no longer holds. Also for the more restricted edge slides, the happy edge property does not hold, see [3] for the general case, and [1] for convex point sets.




Figure 14 shows a counterexample to the happy edge property for convex point sets with rotations. Assume the happy edge property would hold. Then the happy edge that passes through the middle of our convex polygon would split the tree into two proper subtrees. In each such subtree, the unhappy edge has a fixed position where it has to go. Since the initial position and the target position of the unhappy edge do not share any vertex, we need at least two rotations to get each unhappy edge from one position to the other. This gives us a minimum length of four for any flip sequence that preserves happy edges.

The flip sequence in Figure 14 only uses three rotations. Therefore, the happy edge property does not hold for the reconfiguration of plane spanning trees with rotations.





6 Fixed-Parameter Tractable Algorithms




Next, we show that the flip distance kk and the compatible flip distance kck_{c} are fixed-parameter tractable in kk (resp. kck_{c}). The core ingredient for all proofs is the happy edge property for convex hull edges, which is shown to hold for unrestricted flips in Proposition 18 in [1]. We obtain further improvements on the runtime built on the happy edge property for all edges and the parking edge property.




The upcoming lemma will describe a crucial step in the first two algorithms. Even if we never flip happy convex hull edges, the number of happy edges that are on the convex hull can still be arbitrarily large when compared to the number of unhappy edges. Therefore, we have to reduce that number in order to obtain a reasonable instance size that is bounded by a function in kk. We do this as follows: We mark all vertices that are incident to an unhappy edge that is in the initial configuration or the target configuration. All non-marked vertices are now only incident to convex hull edges. If we have a path between two marked vertices that only travels along convex hull edges incident to only non-marked vertices (except the first and/or last edge that start or end in a marked vertex), then we contract that path into one single edge (if both endpoints are marked) or a single vertex (if only one endpoint is marked). All the non-marked vertices get removed in the process. We call the new reduced instances Tin,rT_{\text{in,r}} and Ttar,rT_{\text{tar,r}}. The following Lemma states that in this contraction step no information about the length of the flip sequence is lost.

7 Conclusion




In this work, we have considered the compatible flip graph and the rotation graph, by improving the upper bound on the respective diameter from 2​n−o⁡(n)2n-o(n) to 53​(n−1)\frac{5}{3}(n-1) and to 74​(n−1)\frac{7}{4}(n-1), respectively. The constructive upper bounds align with our findings about the happy edge property. On the one hand, we showed that any shortest compatible flip sequence preserves happy edges and that there is a shortest compatible flip sequence that only uses parking edges from the convex hull. The same holds for the compatible flip sequences that we used to obtain our upper bound. On the other hand, shortest rotation sequences may rotate happy edges. This may also happen in our upper bound construction. We further showed an immediate application of happy edge properties in trees, namely, that they can be used to derive fixed-parameter tractable algorithms. Open questions that are related to our work are:





1.



Can we close the gap between upper and lower bounds for the flip distance, for each of the flip types? Do the diameters for different flip types differ or do they coincide?


2.



Does the happy edge property still hold for flipping plane spanning trees if we drop one of the restrictions that either the point set is convex or the flips are all compatible?


3.



What is the time complexity of finding shortest flip sequences for plane spanning trees? Again, this is interesting for each of the flip types.

Appendix A Proof of Theorem 3




This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem 3.
Our goal is to modify the methods which were introduced and used in [5] to show an upper bound of 53​(n−1)\frac{5}{3}(n-1) for the diameter of the flip graph in order to improve the upper bound on the diameter of the compatible flip graph on a convex nn-point set.
As in [5], the idea will be to pair edges (e,e′)(e,e^{\prime}) from the initial tree and the target tree such that the edge ee will either be flipped directly to the edge e′e^{\prime} performing one perfect flip or to flip ee to a convex hull edge and later flip the convex hull edge to e′e^{\prime} performing an imperfect flip. The length of the flip sequence will then be #\#perfect flips + 22 #\#imperfect flips. So we want to maximize the number of perfect flips.




We start by introducing some notation and results from [5].
For two fixed trees TinT_{\text{in}}, TtarT_{\text{tar}} a linear representation is introduced, which can be interpreted as cutting the cyclic order of the points on the convex hull between v1v_{1} and vnv_{n} and unfolding the circle into a horizontal line segment, called spine.
