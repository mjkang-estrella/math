Structural Properties of Shortest Flip Sequences Between Plane Spanning Trees



Oswin Aichholzer


Joseph Dorfer


Peter Kramer


Christian Rieck


Birgit Vogtenhuber



Abstract


We study the problem of reconfiguring non-crossing spanning trees on point sets in the plane in convex position. A flip is a reconfiguration step in which one edge of a tree is replaced with another, yielding again a non-crossing spanning tree. For a given point set PP, the flip graph has a vertex for each non-crossing spanning tree on PP, and an edge between any two trees that differ by a single flip. The flip distance between two trees is the length of a shortest path between them in the flip graph, that is, the minimum number of flips needed to transform one tree into the other.


We study structural properties of shortest flip sequences. Over the years, several conjectures have been proposed regarding these sequences. The folklore happy edge conjecture suggests that any edge shared by both the initial and target tree is never flipped in a shortest flip sequence. Moreover, the parking conjecture claims that in shortest flip sequences,
every edge is either flipped to a target edge or parked on the convex hull. Notably, the happy edge conjecture is implied by the parking conjecture. Furthermore, it has been conjectured that an edge is never reparked, that is, any edge resulting from a flip either is a target edge or is subsequently flipped to a target edge. Essentially, all recent flip algorithms rely on these three conjectures and the properties they imply.


In this paper, we disprove both the parking and the reparking conjecture. In particular, we show that a flip sequence obeying the parking property sometimes needs linearly more flips than a shortest flip sequence, and exhibit examples where any shortest flip sequence flips some edge multiple times. In contrast, we identify several conditions under which the parking and reparking properties hold; these imply that the reparking property holds for compatible flips, and may be exploited by algorithms to obtain short flip sequences.

1 Introduction




Reconfiguration problems ask whether one feasible state of a system can be transformed into another through a sequence of allowed moves, a perspective made intuitive by classic puzzles such as the Rubik’s cube or the 15-puzzle.
In the former, for instance, a single rotation of a face, either 90​° or 180​°, constitutes an elementary move, and sequences of such face rotations navigate a vast state space of about 4.3⋅10194.3\cdot 10^{19} reachable configurations.
The structure of this state space, that is, how configurations are connected by allowed moves, is captured formally by the respective reconfiguration graph, where vertices represent configurations and edges correspond to single moves.
Remarkably, despite its enormous size, the reconfiguration graph of the Rubik’s cube has diameter 2020, so any scrambled cube can be solved in at most 2020 face rotations.
This viewpoint extends far beyond puzzles: in combinatorics, (computational) geometry, and theoretical computer science in general, reconfiguration graphs are a tool for studying families of objects that can be transformed into one another.




Another natural and illustrative example of geometric reconfiguration is the reconfiguration of triangulations of a convex point set.
In this context, a flip is a local move that replaces one diagonal of a convex quadrilateral formed by two adjacent triangles with the other diagonal, thereby obtaining a new triangulation.
The reconfiguration graph (also called flip graph) is the 11-skeleton of the associahedron.
These triangulations are in bijection with binary trees [36], and each flip corresponds exactly to a rotation in the associated tree.
Hence, the number of flips needed to transform one triangulation into another is the same as the number of rotations required to reorganize the corresponding binary trees.
Since binary search trees are a fundamental data structure, understanding and minimizing these rotations is crucial for improving the efficiency of algorithms that rely on tree operations.
Even though it is known that the diameter of the associahedron is equal to 2​n−62n-6 (for n≥11n\geq 11), it has remained an open problem whether there exists a polynomial-time algorithm for calculating the rotation distance [34, 36].
In a very recent preprint, Dorfer [20] answers this question in the negative.




In this paper, we consider the reconfiguration of non-crossing spanning trees on point sets in convex position.
A flip in a non-crossing spanning tree removes one edge from the tree and adds another edge, resulting again in a non-crossing spanning tree.
The corresponding flip graph has as vertices all non-crossing spanning trees on a convex set PP of nn points, and two vertices are connected by an edge precisely when the associated trees differ in a single flip.
A flip sequence between two trees is any path in the flip graph from the vertex representing one tree to that representing the other tree, and the flip distance is the length of a shortest such path. See Figure 1 for the flip graph of plane spanning trees on 44 points in non-convex position.


Figure 1: Flip graph of plane spanning trees on n=4n=4 points in non-convex position. Puzzle: Can you find all 120 different possibilities how the diameter of this flip graph can be realized?




Recent research has focused on (1) the diameter of the flip graph, that is, the maximum flip distance between any pair of trees on convex point sets and (2) algorithmic aspects of the computation of the flip distance.
For over two decades, the benchmark results for bounds on the diameter were an upper and a lower bound of 2​n−42n-4 [11] and ⌊3​n2⌋−5\lfloor\frac{3n}{2}\rfloor-5 [24], respectively.
The upper bound on the diameter was later improved to 2​n−log⁡(n)2n-\log(n) [1], and shortly after to 2​n−n2n-\sqrt{n} [18].
In [17], the longstanding 2​n2n barrier was surpassed by proving an upper bound on the diameter of ≈1.96​n\approx 1.96n.
The currently best known bounds are a lower bound of (117−o⁡(1))​n(\frac{11}{7}-o(1))n by Bjerkevik, Dorfer, Kleist, Ueckerdt, and Vogtenhuber [13], and an upper bound of 5​n3−3\frac{5n}{3}-3 by Bjerkevik, Kleist, Ueckerdt, and Vogtenhuber [14].




One potentially useful tool for tackling the above two questions is the set of edges common to the start and target tree, known as happy edges.
All other edges are referred to as unhappy.
Building on this concept, the following long-standing conjecture is considered folklore.



Conjecture 1 (Happy edge conjecture).



Let TIT_{I} and TFT_{F} be two plane spanning trees, and let H=TI∩TFH=T_{I}\cap T_{F} be the set of all happy edges.
Then there exists a shortest flip sequence TI=T0,T1,…,Tk−1,Tk=TFT_{I}=T_{0},T_{1},\ldots,T_{k-1},T_{k}=T_{F} such that H⊂TiH\subset T_{i} for every i∈[0,k]i\in[0,k].





Most of the aforementioned improvements to the diameter bounds are based on paths that respect this conjecture, that is, they never flip happy edges.
Specifically, the authors of [1] present their 2​n−log⁡(n)2n-\log(n) bound as follows:


“…happy edges do not flip. An unhappy edge of TIT_{I} flips once or twice, and if twice, it flips to the convex hull and then to an edge of TFT_{F}.”





Moreover, they define as parking edges those edges that occur in some tree along a flip sequence but are absent from both the start and final tree and conjecture that parking on the convex hull is sufficient:



Conjecture 2 (Parking edge conjecture).



For any two plane spanning trees TIT_{I} and TFT_{F}, there exists a shortest flip sequence from TIT_{I} to TFT_{F} that uses only edges of TI∪TF∪𝖢𝖧T_{I}\cup T_{F}\cup{\mathsf{CH}}.





Although it was not explicitly stated as a conjecture, the above quote suggests that there always exists a shortest flip sequence in which every unhappy edge is flipped either once or twice, motivating the following conjecture.



Conjecture 3 (Reparking conjecture).



For any two plane spanning trees TIT_{I} and TFT_{F}, there exists a shortest flip sequence such that every parking edge is flipped to an edge of TFT_{F}. In particular, every edge is flipped at most twice.





This conjecture is further supported by the fact that, to date, every algorithm yielding a non-trivial upper bound on the diameter flips edges either directly to final edges, or first parks them on the convex hull, from where they are later flipped to final edges [1, 5, 14, 17, 18]; we refer to Table 1 for further details.

Some preliminary results and structural insights towards the three conjectures include the following:
(i) The parking edge conjecture directly implies the happy edge conjecture [1], and
(ii) the happy edge conjecture does not hold when the allowed flips are restricted to edge slides [1] or rotations [5]; see Section 2 for precise definitions.
In contrast, (iii) the parking edge conjecture (and consequently the happy edge conjecture) holds for compatible flips [5].
For the case of unrestricted flips, the conjectures had remained open.
The authors of [14], who established the best currently known upper bound on the diameter, describe their upper bound construction as follows:


“We note that the flip sequences we construct for [14, Theorem 26, arXiv-Version], which is our strongest upper bound, have both these properties [Conjecture 1 and Conjecture 2], providing some evidence in favor of the conjectures.”





1.1 Our contributions




We make progress towards the conjectures by uncovering structural insights in the different settings.
This, in turn, sheds new light on the properties of shortest flip sequences between plane spanning trees on convex point sets.
In particular, we obtain the following results.




First, we show a useful property of shortest flip sequences, namely that a flip is either final, or the removed edge would be crossed later in the sequence.
Moreover, we show with Theorem 5 that the parking conjecture implies the reparking conjecture and that Conjecture 3 holds for compatible flips. Simultaneously, there always exists a shortest flip sequence in which every flip that removes a convex hull edge adds an edge of TFT_{F}.



Theorem 4 (Final flip property).



For any two plane spanning trees TIT_{I} and TFT_{F}, there exists a shortest flip sequence such that every flip is either final, or the removed edge is crossed by another edge later on.




Theorem 5.



Consider any two plane spanning trees TIT_{I} and TFT_{F} of a point set PP.


1.



There exists a shortest flip sequence such that any convex hull edge is flipped at most once.


2.



In the compatible case, all edges are flipped at most twice (Compatible reparking property).







Further, we refute Conjecture 2 by demonstrating that, for certain instances, algorithms that only introduce parking edges on the convex hull produce sequences that are far from optimal.
More precisely, we show:



Theorem 6.



There exists a family of trees (TI,k)k∈ℕ(T_{I,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}} and (TF,k)k∈ℕ(T_{F,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}} on 10​k+210k+2 vertices such that the length of the shortest flip sequence that only uses parking edges from the convex hull is at least by kk flips longer than the shortest possible flip sequence.





Finally, we disprove Conjecture 3 for the unrestricted case, obtaining the following:



Theorem 7.



There exist pairs of plane spanning trees TIT_{I} and TFT_{F}, and TI′T^{\prime}_{I} and TF′T^{\prime}_{F} on 2222 and 3232 points, respectively, such that any shortest flip sequence from TIT_{I} to TFT_{F}, respectively TI′T^{\prime}_{I} to TF′T^{\prime}_{F}, flips the same edge 33, respectively 44, times.

1.2 Further related work




Flip graphs of non-crossing configurations on planar point sets have been widely studied for other graph classes, including triangulations [8, 21, 22, 23, 27, 28, 31, 33, 34, 36], spanning paths [6, 7, 10, 29, 35], and matchings [2, 3, 12, 25, 26]; see also the survey by Bose and Hurtado [16].
Several of the aforementioned works rely on structural observations similar to the ones we present for plane spanning trees.




In [36], the authors establish the happy edge property for triangulations of convex point sets.
Moreover, they demonstrate that a greedy strategy that adds edges of a target triangulation whenever possible, always produces a shortest flip sequence.
These insights underpin several 𝖥𝖯𝖳\mathsf{FPT}-algorithms [15, 19, 30, 32], when the flip distance is a parameter.
Conversely, the happy edge property does not hold for triangulations of point sets in general position [16], and counterexamples to it were key to the 𝖭𝖯\mathsf{NP}-hardness proofs in the case of point sets in general position [31, 33], as well as simple polygons [8].




Similarly, the flip distance problem for perfect matchings on point sets in general position has been proven to be 𝖭𝖯\mathsf{NP}-hard [12].
The proof likewise relies on counterexamples to the happy edge property.
When the point set is restricted to be convex, the problem is polynomial-time solvable, as an edge from the target matching can always be inserted greedily [25].




Although the happy edge property does not hold, shortest flip sequences between plane paths on convex point sets can still be computed in polynomial time [6]; a key ingredient of the proof is the partitioning of happy edges into “good” and “bad” happy edges, which are then treated separately.
Sometimes it is sufficient to consider only the happy edges on the convex hull;
for example, convex hull edges satisfy the happy edge property in problems such as plane spanning trees [1], or odd matchings [2] on convex point sets.
In both cases, this observation played a crucial role in deriving related 𝖥𝖯𝖳\mathsf{FPT}-algorithms [2, 5].




Beyond complexity results, counterexamples to the happy edge property have been generalized to establish a quadratic lower bound on the sliding distance between plane spanning trees on convex point sets [9].






2 Preliminaries




Throughout this paper, we denote by P⊂ℝ2P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} a set of nn points in convex position, that is, a set PP such that no point p∈Pp\in P is a convex combination of any three other points.
For a pair of points u,v∈Pu,v\in P, we denote by u​vuv the straight-line segment connecting the two.
If uu and vv are adjacent on the convex hull of PP, we call u​vuv a convex hull edge and write u​v∈𝖢𝖧uv\in{\mathsf{CH}}.
A plane spanning tree of PP is then a set of n−1n-1 edges T⊂(P2)T\subset\binom{P}{2} that forms a tree such that the edges’ line segments are pairwise disjoint or meet at a common endpoint.
We denote the removal and insertion of an edge ee by T−eT-e and T+eT+e, using standard set notation otherwise.


Figure 2: A flip sequence 𝒮\mathcal{S} with trace⁡(e,𝒮)=(e,v1​v5,v4​v5,v3​v5){\operatorname{trace}(e,\mathcal{S})=(e,\>{v_{1}v_{5}},\>{v_{4}v_{5}},\>{v_{3}v_{5}})} and trace⁡(f,𝒮)=(f,v3​v7){\operatorname{trace}(f,\mathcal{S})=(f,\>v_{3}v_{7})}.
The four flips are, in that order, crossing, a rotation, compatible, and an edge slide.




Flip.




A flip in TT removes an edge e∈Te\in T and adds an edge f∉Tf\notin T such that the resulting structure T−e+fT-e+f is again a plane spanning tree.
If the initial tree TT is clear from context, we denote this operation by e→fe\rightarrow f and write that ee is flipped to ff.
We differentiate between four types of flips; a flip e→fe\rightarrow f is compatible if the straight-line segments ee and ff are either disjoint or share a common endpoint, and crossing otherwise.
For u,v,w∈Pu,v,w\in P, a compatible flip of the shape u​v→u​wuv\rightarrow uw is called a rotation, and furthermore called a slide if v​w∈Tvw\in T.
Examples of all types of flips can be seen in Figure 2.




Flip graph.




The flip graph of plane spanning trees of PP is the graph that has all such trees as its vertices.
Two spanning trees share an edge in the flip graph if and only if they can be transformed into one another via a single flip.
Given a pair of trees TIT_{I} and TFT_{F}, a flip sequence 𝒮\mathcal{S} from TIT_{I} to TFT_{F} is then a path in the flip graph denoted by
a sequence of trees TI=T0,T1,…,Tk−1,Tk=TF{T_{I}=T_{0},T_{1},\ldots,T_{k-1},T_{k}=T_{F}} such that consecutive trees only differ by a single flip; its length is the number kk of flips in the sequence.
The flip distance of TIT_{I} and TFT_{F}, denoted by d⁡(TI,TF)d(T_{I},T_{F}), is the length of a shortest sequence.
Furthermore, the edges TI∩TFT_{I}\cap T_{F} are happy, with all other edges being unhappy.
If a flip introduces an edge f∉TFf\notin T_{F}, it is commonly referred to as a parking flip, and ff as its parking edge.
The diameter of a flip graph denotes the maximum flip distance between any pair of trees.




Trace.




We introduce additional notation to analyze flip sequences.
Namely, we introduce structures that allow us to not only represent changes within trees under flip operations, but also how these flip operations affect individual edges.
Given a flip sequence 𝒮=T0,…,Tk\mathcal{S}=T_{0},\ldots,T_{k}, we define the trace of an edge e∈T0e\in T_{0} as the ordered sequence of positions that it is flipped to; an example is shown in Figure 2.
Formally, this corresponds to a tuple of edges:




trace⁡(e,𝒮)=trace⁡(e,T0,…,Tk)={(e)if k=0,trace⁡(e,T1,…,Tk)if e∈T0∩T1, or(e)∥trace⁡(f,T1,…,Tk)if {e,f}=T0​Δ​T1.\operatorname{trace}(e,\mathcal{S})=\operatorname{trace}(e,T_{0},\ldots,T_{k})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{}l@{\quad}l}(e)&&\text{if $k=0$,}\\
&\operatorname{trace}(e,T_{1},\ldots,T_{k})&\text{if $e\in T_{0}\cap T_{1}$, or}\\
(e)\parallel&\operatorname{trace}(f,T_{1},\ldots,T_{k})&\text{if $\{e,f\}=T_{0}\Delta T_{1}$.}\end{array}\right.



We say that the length of a trace is the number of flips in it, which corresponds to the number of elements minus one: The trace (e1,…,eℓ)(e_{1},\ldots,e_{\ell}) corresponds to the flips ei→ei+1e_{i}\to e_{i+1} for 1≤i<ℓ1\leq i<\ell.
Observe that the length of 𝒮\mathcal{S} is exactly the sum of all trace lengths.
A flip e→fe\to f in 𝒮\mathcal{S} is final if the trace of ff on the remaining sequence is (f)(f), and non-final otherwise.
A happy edge e∈T0∩Tke\in T_{0}\cap T_{k} is fixed in the flip sequence 𝒮\mathcal{S} if it has trace length zero, or equivalently trace⁡(e,𝒮)=(e)\operatorname{trace}(e,\mathcal{S})=(e).
This induces two properties of happy edges based on shortest flip sequences.
A happy edge is weakly fixed if it is fixed in some shortest flip sequence, and strongly fixed if this applies to all such sequences.




We note that Conjectures 1, 2 and 3 can also be stated in terms of trace properties:
The happy edge conjecture (Conjecture 1) states that all happy edges are weakly fixed.
Similarly, the parking conjecture (Conjecture 2) states that there always exists a shortest flip sequence in which all but the first and final element of all traces are in 𝖢𝖧\mathsf{CH}, and the re-parking conjecture (Conjecture 3) states
the existence of a shortest flip sequence in which each edge of TIT_{I} has trace length at most two.






3 Positive results on structural properties




We generalize a property shown by Aichholzer, Dorfer, and Vogtenhuber [5, Lemma 20].
They showed that in every compatible flip sequence any parking flip that parks on a diagonal can be replaced with a parking flip that parks on the convex hull.
Actually, it is not required that the entire flip sequence is compatible, but only either the flip that inserts the parking edge or the flip that removes the parking edge has to be compatible.
This already follows from their proof.
For the sake of self-containment, we include the adapted proof and rewrite the statement in terms of our definitions.

Let there be a flip sequence between two trees TIT_{I} and TFT_{F} and an edge ee such that t​r​a​c​e​(e)=(e,f,e′)trace(e)=(e,f,e^{\prime}), where ff is a diagonal and either the flip e→fe\to f or the flip f→e′{f\to e^{\prime}} is compatible.

For our purposes we apply a technique called normalization as introduced for triangulations in [36, Lemma 3].
This concept has since been modified for matchings [25] and trees [1, Proposition 18], which we further adapt.
For trees, a normalization is applied to a flip sequence and defined with respect to an edge ee that is not crossed by any other edge that is part of this flip sequence.
A normalization maps a tree TT to another tree N⁡(T)N(T) such that the mapping N⁡(⋅)N(\cdot) only depends on the original flip sequence and ee.
For the original flip sequence 𝒮=T0\mathcal{S}=T_{0}, T1,…,TkT_{1},\ldots,T_{k} and an edge ee we want the normalization 𝒮N=N⁡(T0)\mathcal{S}_{N}=N(T_{0}), N⁡(T1),…,N⁡(Tk−1)N(T_{1}),\ldots,N(T_{k-1}), N⁡(Tk)N(T_{k}) to have the following properties:

1.



N⁡(T0)=T0N(T_{0})=T_{0} and N⁡(Tk)=TkN(T_{k})=T_{k}.


2.



For two consecutive trees TiT_{i}, Ti+1T_{i+1}, we have that N⁡(Ti)N(T_{i}) and N⁡(Ti+1)N(T_{i+1}) either coincide or only differ in a single flip.


3.



The trace⁡(e,𝒮N)\operatorname{trace}(e,\mathcal{S}_{N}) has length 11.



Note that in Property (2) for our sequence 𝒮\mathcal{S} it cannot happen that two consecutive normalized trees coincide, as this would give us a shorter flip sequence and thus a contradiction.

With the new flip sequence N⁡(T0)N(T_{0}), N⁡(T1),…,N⁡(Tk−1)N(T_{1}),\ldots,N(T_{k-1}), N⁡(Tk)N(T_{k}) we have made sure that ee is no longer an edge that is flipped multiple times, but not crossed at any point of the flip sequence.

In order to be able to show that Theorem 4 holds for all edges at the same time we, resort to a technique used in [5, Lemma 20].




If an edge s=a​bs=ab is contained in all trees of a flip sequence, then ss splits the convex point set into two sides AA and BB, the vertices that appear in clockwise order between aa and bb and the vertices that appear in counterclockwise order.
The vertices aa and bb count towards both, side AA and side BB.
For every tree, we obtain two subtrees, the induced subtrees on AA and BB.
Every flip in one of the trees is completely independent of the flips in the other side.
Thus, we can interleave both ordered subsequences that flip edges in only one of the two sides to obtain a valid flip sequence with the same initial and final trees.
We will use this to argue that the only flip in trace⁡(e,𝒮N)\operatorname{trace}(e,\mathcal{S}_{N}), denoted by e→ge\to g, is independent of all subsequent flips in 𝒮N\mathcal{S}_{N}.
This will then imply that e→ge\to g can be performed as the last flip.




To this end, assume that the flip from N⁡(Ti)N(T_{i}) to N⁡(Ti+1)N(T_{i+1}) is the final flip that removes ee and inserts gg.

Flip sequences of these trees in the resulting subsets are independent of another and can be interleaved in any order.




After splitting the point set, there exists a particular subset CC, as depicted in Figure 4, of vertices that contains the vertices of both, ee and gg.
By the previous discussion, we can perform the flip sequence in CC, that also performs the flip e→ge\to g, as the last flip sequence among all flip sequences in the subsets.




It remains to argue that we can perform the flip e→ge\to g as the last flip in CC. For this, we argue that all other flips can be performed, independently of whether ee or gg is present in the current tree. CC has three parts for which ee and gg form the border: The side CeC_{e} of ee that does not contain gg, the side CgC_{g} of gg that does not contain ee, and the part between ee and gg. 4 Counterexamples for general flips




In the previous section, we identified structural conditions that any counterexample to the parking and reparking conjectures must satisfy for general flips.
With these conditions in hand, we now construct specific pairs of trees, TIT_{I} and TFT_{F}, that not only meet these requirements but also go further, yielding shortest flip sequences that violate the conjectures.




Through a combination of case distinctions, additional observations on flip sequences, and support from a computer program, we verify that the proposed flip sequences are indeed the shortest possible flip sequences.
Moreover, we demonstrate that no alternative shortest flip sequence can satisfy the conjectures.

To compute all relevant flip sequences for the example shown in Figure 5, we employ a straightforward computer program [4], which systematically generates and tracks all possible sequences.
The program initializes a set of edges with the edges of the initial tree TIT_{I} and then simply works in a depth first search (DFS) like manner. For every given set of edges it considers all possible legal flips (such that after the flip the set of edges still forms a spanning tree). For each such flip, it performs the flip and then increases a counter in case that this flip was not perfect, that is, did not insert an edge of the final tree. Only if this counter does not exceed the maximum allowed number of non-perfect flips (one in our case) the program continues recursively. Otherwise the flip is undone and the next legal flip is performed, etc.
Whenever all edges of the set are from the final tree TFT_{F} another counter for the number of shortest flip sequences is increased. The program terminates once the DFS is completed.




For the trees shown in Figure 5 the output of the program gives 2020 different flip sequences of length 8. That these are indeed precisely all the sequences which contain the parking flip e1→p1{e_{1}\to p_{1}} can be verified by counting all shortest flip sequences depicted in Figure 12.

First, the central diagonal in red is flipped to the green diagonal. Then, we can perform six perfect flips that insert edges from TFT_{F}. Afterwards, the green diagonal is flipped again. We can perform six additional perfect flips, before we can flip the green edge to its final position.


Figure 10: A counterexample to the reparking property.




The flip sequence sketched in Figure 10 is the unique shortest flip sequence (up to ordering) of the six perfect flips that occur between consecutive flips that involve the green edge.
To verify this, we again used our program [4]. Here the relevant part of the flip graph has 5086 vertices (which are at most two non-perfect flips away from TIT_{I}).
The size of this graph seems thus to be rather challenging for human inspection.
As we saw already for the proof of Lemma 10 that a hand written case distinction will be too tedious we rely here on the program.
It generated a total of 400400 shortest flip sequences which is precisely (63)⋅(63)\binom{6}{3}\cdot\binom{6}{3}, that is, the number of different orders how the perfect flips between parking flips can interleave.




We even took the construction one step further and provide a pair of trees in which for every shortest flip sequence there exists an edge that has trace length four, that is, it is two times reparked. The example is depicted in Figure 11. The edges in red are part of TIT_{I}, edges in blue are part of TFT_{F}, and the black edges on the convex hull are contained in both trees. We verified our example with the same program used for the two previous counterexamples.

5 Conclusions and future work




We established counterexamples to the parking and reparking properties for shortest flip sequences in the general setting, while proving that the reparking property holds when flips are restricted to be compatible.
These results clarify the limits of existing assumptions and point to several promising directions for future research.





•



In Table 1 we summarized all recent upper bounds on the diameter and specify which of the investigated properties each bound respects.
Most notably, in every case the trace length of all edges is bounded from above by 22. This raises two questions:
Will our constructions need to be incorporated to achieve further improvements in the upper bounds on the diameter of the flip graph, and do these counterexamples offer new insights for constructing tighter lower bounds?


•



By disproving the parking conjecture for general flips, we have eliminated a previously promising approach of establishing the happy edge conjecture.
Consequently, we think that entirely new methods are required to make further progress on that conjecture.


•



We provided a construction of initial and final trees where every shortest flip sequence for these pairs has an edge of trace length 33 or 44, respectively.
We expect that this construction can be iterated without limit.
Thus, we raise the following conjecture:





Conjecture 14.



For any constant cc there exists a pair of trees TIT_{I} and TFT_{F} such that any shortest flip sequence between TIT_{I} and TFT_{F} has an edge with trace length larger than cc.






•



Very recently it was shown that computing shortest (compatible) flip sequences between plane spanning trees of convex point sets is 𝖭𝖯\mathsf{NP}-complete [13] by Bjerkevik, Dorfer, Kleist, Ueckerdt, and Vogtenhuber.
This highlights the importance of identifying effective 𝖥𝖯𝖳\mathsf{FPT}- or approximation algorithms.
Can the reparking property for compatible flips be exploited to enhance the currently known 𝖥𝖯𝖳\mathsf{FPT}-algorithm of [5]?
Moreover, our counterexamples to the parking and reparking properties may be instrumental in establishing higher intractability results.

Proof.



Without loss of generality, let e→fe\to f be the first and f→e′f\to e^{\prime} the last flip of the flip sequence. Further let e→fe\to f be a compatible flip, otherwise look at the reversed flip sequence. We denote with vav_{a} and vbv_{b} the two vertices of ff.




We reorder and modify the other flips based on the following observation. The edge ff separates the point set into two sides AA and BB, the points that appear in clockwise direction from vav_{a} to vbv_{b} and the points that appear in counterclockwise order, including vav_{a} and vbv_{b} in both AA and BB. While ff is part of the tree, flips in the two sides can be executed independently from the other side. Note that every spanning tree contains a unique path between any two vertices. If, in particular, we consider the path pp from vav_{a} to vbv_{b} in TI=T0T_{I}=T_{0}, we see that this path has to be contained entirely in one side induced by ff, w.l.o.g. in side BB. Otherwise, there would be an edge along the path that crosses ff, introducing a non-compatible flip from TIT_{I} to T1T_{1}.




If we add the edge ff, the cycle that gets closed is entirely contained in BB. Therefore, ee lies in BB. Any flip that is executed from T1T_{1} to Tk−1T_{k-1} that happens in AA can already be executed in TIT_{I}. Any cycle that previously involved the edge ff now involves the path pp instead. We order our flips in a way, such that all flips that happen in AA are executed right away starting from TIT_{I}. After executing all those steps, the side AA already coincides with the side AA in TFT_{F}, except for the edge ff.




Let us take a closer look at the part of TFT_{F} that lies in AA. This part consists of ff and two trees TT and T′T^{\prime} (possibly with an empty set of edges) in AA. Since TFT_{F} is plane there exist a unique pair of points that appear consecutively along the boundary of the convex hull and one belongs to TT and the other to T′T^{\prime}. We continue our modified flip sequence by adding the convex hull edge hh between those two points and removing the edge that would have been removed when adding ff.




Now we execute all the flips in BB from the original flip sequence. Any flip that formed a cycle that involves ff now forms a cycle that involves hh and paths in AA connecting vav_{a} and vbv_{b} to hh.




As a last flip we perform h→e′h\to e^{\prime} and reach the final tree TFT_{F}. The trace of ee is (e,h,e′)(e,h,e^{\prime}) and for any other edge in TIT_{I} the trace did not change between the two flip sequences.
∎

(1) If an edge of TFT_{F} is crossed at least twice, then it cannot be added by a single flip.
(2) If an edge of TFT_{F} is crossed exactly once, then the only flip that could potentially add that edge is the flip that removes the edge that crosses it.
(3) We cannot add edges of TFT_{F} if adding the edge would create a cycle in the graph. In all cases, the cycle will contain a parking edge on the convex hull.
