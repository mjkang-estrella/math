                                            AMPLITUDE MAXIMIZATION IN STABLE SYSTEMS, SCHUR POSITIVITY,
                                               AND SOME CONJECTURES ON POLYNOMIAL INTERPOLATION

                                                                    DMITRII M. OSTROVSKII AND PAVEL S. SHCHERBAKOV


                                                   Abstract. For r > 0 and integers t ⩾ n > 0, we consider the following “amplitude maximization”
                                                   problem: maximize the quantity |xt | over complex solutions x = (x0 , x1 , . . . ) of all homogeneous linear
                                                   difference equations of order n with roots of the characteristic polynomial in the disc {z ∈ C : |z| ⩽ r},




arXiv:2508.13554v1 [math.CV] 19 Aug 2025
                                                   and with initial values x0 , . . . , xn−1 in the unit disc. We find that for any triple t, n, r, the maximum
                                                   is attained in the case of coinciding roots on the boundary circle; this implies that for all r < 1, the
                                                   maximum amplitude can be computed explicitly, by studying a single equation whose characteristic
                                                   polynomial is (z − r)n . Moreover, the optimality of the cophase root configuration holds for origin-
                                                   centered polydiscs. To prove this result, we first reduce the problem to a certain interpolation
                                                   problem over monomials, then solve the latter by leveraging the theory of symmetric functions and
                                                   identifying the associated Schur positivity structure. We also discuss the implications for more
                                                   general Reinhardt domains. Finally, we study the problem of estimating the derivatives of a real
                                                   entire function from its values at n/2 pairs of complex conjugate points in the unit disc. We propose
                                                   conjectures on the extremality of the monomial z n , and restate them in terms of Schur polynomials.

                                           Solution set of (1) is a shift-invariant subspace of CN0 , i.e. an eigenspace of A as a linear operator,
                                           and to specify its element—a particular solution—one has to assign the initial values (x0 , . . . , xn−1 ).
                                           Naturally, one might want to study the properties of the whole solution set, or of its sufficiently
                                           large subset, rather than those of a specific solution. This task might be challenging if one considers
                                           a whole class of equations instead of a single one, due to the highly nonlinear fashion in which
                                           a solution to (1) depends on the roots z1 , . . . , zn of characteristic polynomial, in contrast to its
                                           linear dependence on the initial data. As such, it is not surprising that the classical theory of
                                           linear systems does not say much about the worst-case behavior of solutions to (1) in the setup
                                           where z1 , . . . , zn vary over a subset of Cn . In this work, we give a rather striking example of such a
                                           characterization, whose underlying nature is algebraic, and whose existence seems to be unnoticed.
                                              Before we proceed to the presentation of our results, let us briefly review what the classical theory
                                           has to offer. Recall that (1) is called stable if its arbitrary solution satisfies supt∈N0 |xt | < ∞, and is
                                           called asymptotically stable if |xt | → 0 as t → +∞. The textbook criterion of asymptotic stability is
                                           for f to be Schur stable, i.e. have all roots in the open unit disc; in the case of usual stability, one is
                                           also allowed to have roots on the boundary circle, provided that these are distinct. Next, one can
                                           provide quantitative restrictions on the asymptotic behavior of solutions. For example, r-stability,
                                                                                      max{|z1 |, . . . , |zn |} ⩽ r < 1,
                                           implies that asymptotically as t → +∞, solutions to (1) shrink exponentially fast. More precisely,

   We focus on a very natural question one could ask in the context of stability, phrased as follows:
        Given any r > 0 and t ⩾ n > 0, what is the largest possible amplitude |xt | for a
        solution x to an r-stable equation (1) of order n, provided that |xt | ⩽ 1 for 0 ⩽ t < n?
In this “vanilla” version of the amplitude maximization problem, both the characteristic roots
and the initial values are bounded uniformly, and one might ask how reasonable are these two
assumptions, especially the latter one. In addition to its adoption in prior work [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6],
the assumption of uniformly bounded initial values can be justified as the one allowing for most
variation at the onset of a trajectory. Yet, our main result holds in a more general setup, allowing
for heterogeneous bounds. To state it, we need some notation. Let Pn be the space of all monic
polynomials of degree n, and let Pn (Z) be the set of monic polynomials with the tuple of roots
from Z ⊆ Cn . Let X(f ) be the solution set of (1) with a given f . For t ∈ N0 , f ∈ Pn , and U ⊆ Cn ,

To do so, we proceed in two steps. In the first step, we reformulate Mt (f |U ), cf. (2), as the sum
of absolute values of the coefficients for a certain interpolating polynomial, with z1 , . . . , zn as the
nodes, and express the coefficients explicitly as homogeneous symmetric polynomials in z1 , . . . , zn .
In the second step, we establish the Schur positivity of these polynomials, which implies the result.

  Finally, let us mention that Theorem 1 can be applied in the more general scenario where Z and U
are origin-centered Reinhardt domains, i.e. are closed under entrywise phase modulation [7, 8, 9].
In this case, Theorem 1 gives Mt (Z|U ) = Mt (Z+ |U+ ), where D+ is the “radius hull” of D ∈ Cn ,
obtained by taking the entrywise moduli of all points. For logarithmically convex Reinhardt domains,
evaluating Mt (Z+ |U+ ) amounts to solving a bilevel optimization problem with a special structure,
and we demonstrate how to exploit this structure when explicit evaluation is out of the question.
In particular, we give an algorithm to compute Mt (Z+ |U+ ) in the setup where Z+ is log-affine,
and U+ admits a linear maximization oracle. We also give some examples of (Z+ , U+ ) allowing for
explicit characterizations of the maximizing pair, in the vein of the characterization in Theorem 1.

Contributions and outline of the paper. In Section 2, we carry out a preliminary study of the
amplitude maximization problem. First, we reduce it to a certain polynomial interpolation problem
– namely, Lagrange interpolation of monomials in the nodes z1 , . . . , zn , with error measured by the
weighted sum of absolute coefficients of the Lagrange polynomial. We then compute Mt (D(r)n |D(1)n )
explicitly as a function of t, n, r (assuming Theorem 1 to be valid). Finally, we present some
preliminary results with elementary proofs, and shed light on the arising challenges. Section 3
gives background on symmetric functions and partitions, needed to prove Theorem 1. The proof is
presented in Section 4, along with an alternative calculation of Mt (D(r)n |D(1)n ), this time using
combinatorial tools. Section 5 explores the implications of Theorem 1 for Reinhardt domains,
alluded to in the previous paragraph. In Section 6, we apply our techniques to study the problem
of approximating the derivatives of a real entire function by those of its Lagrange polynomial. In
particular, we consider the case of pairwise conjugate nodes (real characteristic polynomial), propose
some conjectures on the worst-case error, and reformulate these in terms of Schur polynomials.

                               3. Background on symmetric functions
   This section covers our subsequent needs. More can be found in [14, Chap. 7], [15] and [16] [17].
Partitions. Any nonincreasing sequence λ = (λ1 , λ2 , . . . ) with λk ∈ Z+ is called a partition, and Par
denotes the set of all partitions. The nonzero entries in λ ∈ Par are parts; its length ℓ(λ) is the
number of parts (λi = 0 is often omitted). Its size |λ| is the sum of parts; λ ⊢ d is the same as |λ| = d.
For n, d ∈ Z+ , we define the sets Par(d) := {λ ∈ Par : |λ| = d} and Parn (d) = {λ ∈ Par(d) : ℓ(λ) = n}.
The Young diagram of λ is the left-justified two-dimensional array of cells with λi cells in each row i.
For λ, µ ∈ Par, we write λ ⊇ µ if λ − µ is a nonnegative vector, i.e. the Young diagram of λ contains
that of µ. The conjugate partition of λ, denoted with λ′ , is obtained by transposing the Young
diagram of λ. A hook partition has the form (a + 1, 1b ) with a, b ⩾ 0, where 1b is 1 repeated b times;
its Young diagram is composed of a single row and a single column. In the Frobenius notation, this
partition writes as (a|b), and its conjugate is (b|a). Any λ ∈ Par can be decomposed into hooks
by iteratively removing the boundary hook; this allows to write any finite partition as (a1:s | b1:s ),
where (a1 |b1 ), . . . , (as |bs ) is the sequence of hooks removed. For example, (4, 3, 3, 1) = (3, 1, 0 | 3, 1, 0).
