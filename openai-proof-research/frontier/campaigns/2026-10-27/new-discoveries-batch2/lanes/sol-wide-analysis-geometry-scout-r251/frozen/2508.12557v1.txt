Definition. Call the parameter vector p = (pij : 1 ≤ i < j ≤ n) regular if

  1. pi−1,i ≥ 1/2 for 2 ≤ i ≤ n,

  2. pi−1,j ≥ pij for 2 ≤ i < j ≤ n, and

of p, the gap λK is minimized by choosing pij = 1/2 for all 1 ≤ i < j ≤ n.

Facts about the spectral structure of the matrix K
   In this section I’ll present a few facts about the spectral structure of the matrix K.
The first is a result from [19].
Theorem 1. In the unweighted case pij ≡ 1/2 the gap is

                              λK = (1 − cos(π/n))/(n − 1).

    Notice that (1 − cos(π/n))/(n − 1) ∼ π 2 /(2n3 ) as n → ∞.
    In the unweighted case, the multiplicity of the eigenvalue βK = 1 − λK is at least
(in fact, I believe, is exactly) n − 1. Indeed, for each 1 ≤ i ≤ n, the label i performs
a (slowed-down) random walk on the path {1, . . . , n}, with spectral gap equal to (1 −
cos(π/n))/(n − 1). Further, the corresponding walk-eigenvector lifts to an eigenvector
with eigenvalue βK for the permutation chain. These n eigenvectors have vanishing
sum, but any n − 1 of them are linearly independent.
    Next I present a fact about the spectral structure of K for general (not necessarily
regular) p that I first noticed from numerical computations.
Theorem 2. The matrices K and I − K are similar. Therefore, the eigevalues of K
occur in pairs summing to unity, and the spectrum of K lies in [0, 1].
Proof. List the permutations of length n in an order so that, for every k, the kth-to-last
permutation is the reverse of the kth permutation, as I did above in writing out K
for n = 3. Let D := diag(π) (recalling that π is the stationary distribution for K),
let B denote the backward identity matrix (the matrix with bi,n+1−i = 1 for all i and
bij = 0 otherwise), and let E := diag(sgn(x)), where sgn(x) denotes the sign of the
permutation x. Set C := EBD. Then C is invertible and

                                  I − K = CKC −1 .

Stronger conjectures
  In this section I’ll discuss natural stronger conjectures than the one proposed in the
Gap Problem.
    (a) Based on evidence (b) on page 2 above, it was natural to guess for general n that,
as p ranges over the regular vectors, the gap λK is nondecreasing in each pij as the other
parameters prs are held fixed. But this is false for n = 4. For example, when p12 = 0.5,
p23 = 0.7, p34 = 0.5, p13 = 0.7, p24 = 0.8, and p14 = 0.9, we have λK ≈ 0.1261, but
when p12 is increased to 0.6 we have the smaller gap λK ≈ 0.1259.
    But for evidence of a different sort of monotonicity conjecture, see (d) below.
    (b) It is also false (even for n = 4) that every eigenvalue in the upper half of the
spectrum of K is maximized in the unweighted case. Indeed, the fifth-largest eigenvalue
is approximately 0.7887 in the unweighted case but is about 0.7944 when p12 = p23 =
p34 = p13 = 0.50 and p24 = p14 = 0.95.
    (c) Numerical evidence suggests that, for regular p, the gap λK achieves the mini-
mum value of Theorem 1 if and only if there exists 1 ≤ i ≤ n such that pij = 1/2 for all
j ̸= i. Further, in such cases the multiplicity (call it µ) of the eigenvalue βK appears to
equal the number (call it ν) of such values i, with two exceptions: (i) if ν = n (i.e., in
the unweighted case), then µ = n − 1; and (ii) if ν = n − 2 (i.e., if p1n = 1 − pn1 > 1/2
and pij = 1/2 otherwise), then µ = n − 1.
    (d) Observe that the regular vectors form a convex set. Let p∗ denote p in the
unweighted case. For given regular p and 0 ≤ t ≤ 1, define p(t) := (1 − t)p + tp∗ , and
let K(t) denote the corresponding transition matrix. The Gap Problem formulates the
conjecture that K(1) has a smaller gap than does K(0). Numerical evidence leads to the
stronger conjecture that the gap λK(t) is nonincreasing (and also convex) in t ∈ [0, 1].

Introduction: Self-organizing lists
     Here is background about why I am interested in the gap problem. In brief, the prob-
lem arises in connection with the analysis of certain Markov chains on self-organizing
lists. (I would be happy to elaborate on this background for interested parties.)
     Suppose that we have a set of n records (e.g., items of data in a computer), each
equipped with an identifying key, or label, and suppose that we wish to store these
records in a way that facilitates searching and sorting. One frequently employed data
structure for this is a list that is ordered linearly, say from “front” to “back.” When a
record is requested, the list is searched sequentially from front to back until the desired
key is located. (For simplicity, we shall assume that only records appearing in the
list are ever requested.) The cost of meeting a request can be defined as the number of
records that must be examined to reach the requested record, or more generally as some
function of this number. Such a list is called a linear search list. Any standard text on
data structures, e.g., [12], [13], [14], or [18], may be consulted for a general introduction.
     Without loss of generality, we write [n] := {1, 2, . . . , n} for the set of keys. Suppose
now that independently at each unit of time a record is requested (accessed) according
to some fixed probability distribution; let the “weight” wi denote the probability that


                                              4
item i is requested. It is clear that the optimal static order in which to arrange the
items is from front to back in decreasing (by which we shall mean nonincreasing) order
of the wi ’s. However, typically the long-run usage frequencies wi are unknown. One
remedy that immediately leaps to mind is to keep a running counter of the actual
frequency of access for each record and dynamically reorder the items in decreasing
order of frequencies. By the strong law of large numbers, the order obtained by this
counter scheme will converge with probability 1 to the optimal static order, as observed
in [15]. However, this analysis fails to take into account the costs of the rearrangements
and the memory requirements of the counters.
     A commonly used alternative is to “let the records organize themselves.” The idea
of a self-organizing (linear search) list (SOL) scheme is to rearrange the records after
each access, according to some well-specified rule without the need for counters, in such
a way that frequently accessed records tend to gravitate toward the front of the list,
thereby reducing search costs. If the (conditional) probabilities of the various possible
rearrangements at a given epoch depend solely on the order of the items at that epoch
and the item selected, then the time-dependent order of the items forms a Markov
chain on the symmetric group Sn . A major goal is to assess rates of convergence to
stationarity and other operating characteristics for the permutation chain.
     By far, the two most frequently used and most frequently discussed schemes are
the simple move-to-front (MTF) and transposition, or move-ahead-1 (MA1), rules. My
gap problem evolved from consideration of the MA1 rule, so I will almost exclusively
consider that rule here. Consider a fixed epoch at which the records are in order
x = (x1 , . . . , xn ), with xj denoting the key (label) of the record in position j. Un-
der the MTF rule, if the record in position i is requested (this happens with prob-
ability wxi ), it is brought to the front and the order is subsequently rearranged to
(xi , x1 , . . . , xi−1 , xi+1 , . . . , xn ). Using MA1, the selected item is instead moved one po-
sition ahead, resulting in (x1 , . . . , xi−2 , xi , xi−1 , xi+1 , . . . , xn ); if x1 is selected, the order
remains at x.
     A gentle introduction to self-organizing Markov chains is provided by [10]. Hester
and Hirschberg [11] survey the state of the field, including known results and open
problems, as of 1985. We are concerned only with probabilistic analysis of self-organizing
(sequential) search (SOS). Alternatively, one may employ amortized analysis (see [16]
and [17]) which produces a sort of “worst case average performance” of a self-organizing
scheme.
     Note: Without any real loss of generality we suppose for the remainder of this
document that w1 ≥ w2 ≥ · · · ≥ wn > 0.

The move-ahead-1 (MA1) rule
    The MA1 rule moves records far less than does MTF. Intuitively, therefore, MA1
performs better than MTF when the records are in “good” order and not as well when
the order is “bad.” (One also expects that MA1 will not perform as well as MTF in the
Markov requests model when there is positive dependence among successive requests.)
Rivest [15] showed that stationary expected search cost (ESC) is smaller for MA1 than
for MTF, but little has been done analytically to show that MA1 peforms poorly due to


                                                     5
slow convergence when the initial order is bad. The summary of the literature on MA1
by Hester and Hirschberg [11] is accurate:

        Move-to-front is the only permutation algorithm that has been extensively
        analyzed. Transpose is widely mentioned in the literature, but authors
        merely state an inability to obtain theoretical bounds on its performance,
        and most of the work on other algorithms merely shows how their behav-
        ior changes as parameters vary. Direct bounds on the behaviors of these
        algorithms are needed to allow realistic comparisons.

Aldous [1] showed that the number of moves required for small variation distance is
between the orders of n3 and n3 log n. Diaconis and Saloff-Coste [5] obtained the same
result by comparing the eigenvalues of the MA1 chain with those of the “random trans-
positions” shuffle of Diaconis and Shahshahani [7]. They conjectured that the correct
order is n3 log n. [This was eventually established by Wilson [19], who showed that the
spectral gap (difference between 1 and the second largest eigenvalue of K) is exactly
(1 − cos(π/n))/(n − 1).] From [4], the corresponding order for MTF is only n log n; thus,
for equal weights, MA1 is indeed a good deal slower to converge to uniform order.
    I don’t know how to extend these techniques to the weighted case. To mention
one line of attack which has been unproductive (namely, the eigenvalue comparison
technique of Diaconis and Saloff-Coste [6]), comparison with an unweighted shuffle such
as random transpositions is ineffective because of the existence of large values of the
ratio of the two stationary distributions.
    The MA1 chain sometimes is provably slow to mix. For example, consider the
geometric weights wi ≡ 2−i /(1 − 2−n ), and suppose that the MA1 chain is started in a
permutation with record n in front of record n − 1. In stationarity, the probability that
n − 1 is in front of n is larger than 1/2 (and, by the way, strictly so for n ≥ 3). Yet
n − 1 cannot possibly move ahead of record n until it is requested at least once, and the
request probability wn−1 is exponentially small in n.
    But I am willing to employ any chain with the stationary distribution (0.1), since
(among other goals) I am interested in sampling from (0.1) for various choices of the

    If I can resolve my gap problem positively, it will have several positive consequences.
In addition to providing a way to do approximate sampling from (0.1) [or, more gener-
ally, from (0.2)] with guaranteed bounds on the error and rapidly (at least when started
from the identity permutation), it will also allow me to get rapid mixing results for the
MA1 chain itself, for some choices of w, by comparison methods.
    Ideally, the positive resolution would allow for generalization. Indeed, similar mixing-
rate challenges await for natural weighted versions of other classical Markov chains, for
example, the Bernoulli–Laplace model for diffusion (see [3], page 56). In the unweighted
case, Diaconis and Shahshahani [8] use representation theory for Gelfand pairs to give
precise formulas for the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the transition matrix and obtain
sharp estimates for convergence. In the weighted case, only the stationary distribution
is known (Persi Diaconis, personal communication).
