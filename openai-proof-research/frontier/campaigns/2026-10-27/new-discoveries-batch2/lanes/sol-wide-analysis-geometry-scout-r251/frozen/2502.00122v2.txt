                                                  HOMOTOPY CONNECTIVITY OF ČECH COMPLEXES OF SPHERES

                                                                 HENRY ADAMS, EKANSH JAUHARI, AND SUCHARITA MALLICK


                                                   Abstract. Let S n be the n-sphere with the geodesic metric and of diameter π. The intrinsic Čech
                                                   complex Č(S n ; r) is the nerve of all open balls of radius r in S n . In this paper, we show how to
                                                   control the homotopy connectivity of Č(S n ; r) in terms of coverings of spheres. Our upper bound
                                                   on the connectivity, which is sharp in the case n = 1, comes from the chromatic numbers of Borsuk
                                                   graphs of spheres. Our lower bound is obtained using the conicity (in the sense of Barmak) of Č(X; r)
                                                   for sufficiently dense, finite subsets X of S n . Our bounds imply the new result that for n ≥ 1, the
                                                   homotopy type of Č(S n ; r) changes infinitely many times as r varies over (0, π); we conjecture only

                                                                                          1. Introduction

                                              Čech complexes are among the most important tools in applied topology for approximating the
                                           shape of data. Indeed, whereas a finite dataset has no interesting topology on its own, if one replaces
                                           each point in the dataset with a ball of radius r, then the union of the balls serves as a proxy for the
                                           shape of the data. Even better, by letting the radius r vary from small to large, one can use persistent
                                           homology to get a multi-resolution summary of the shape of the data [14]. Though the union of balls
                                           is difficult to store on a computer, the Čech complex is a combinatorial representation with a vertex
                                           for each data point, an edge whenever two balls intersect, a triangle whenever three balls intersect,
                                           etc. Since balls in Euclidean space are convex, the nerve lemma implies that this Čech complex is
                                           homotopy equivalent to the union of the balls (see [13] or [22, Corollary 4G.3]).
                                              The theory of Čech complexes for balls in Euclidean space is well-understood. But what about for
                                           balls in other manifolds? In a Riemannian manifold M , a ball of radius r need only be geodesically
                                           convex up to a certain scale r (called the convexity radius) depending on the curvature of the manifold.
                                           For larger scales, the intersection of two balls may be disconnected, meaning that the hypotheses of
                                           the nerve lemma may no longer be satisfied. What are the homotopy types of intrinsic Čech complexes
                                           of manifolds, where intrinsic means that the Čech complex is the nerve of balls restricted to live in
                                           the manifold? In this paper, we initiate the study of this question for the simplest non-contractible
                                           manifolds: spheres. We hope our paper inspires follow-up work on the intrinsic Čech complexes of
                                           other manifolds. Furthermore, instead of the statistical context of a finite dataset X sampled from
                                           the n-sphere S n , we begin with the mathematical context of the Čech complex of the entire n-sphere.
                                           If one’s dataset X is sampled from a manifold M , then in the limit, as more data points are sampled,
                                           the stability of persistent homology [15] implies that the intrinsic Čech persistent homology of the
                                           dataset X converges to the intrinsic Čech persistent homology of the entire manifold M .

                                                 2. Related work

   In [30], Lovász famously used topological arguments, and in particular, the Borsuk–Ulam theorem,
to lower bound the chromatic numbers of Kneser graphs. Though Kneser graphs are finite graphs of
a combinatorial flavor, Lovász’s techniques for understanding their chromatic numbers were partially
inspired by the infinite and geometrically flavored Borsuk graphs (see [30, Footnote 1]), which have
n-spheres as their vertex sets. Lovász proved that the chromatic number of any graph G satisfies
χ(G) ≥ conn(N (G)) + 3, where N (G) is the neighborhood simplicial complex of G. He further showed
that this general lower bound turns out to be an equality in the case of Kneser graphs.
   The neighborhood complexes of Borsuk graphs are easily identified to be Čech complexes of spheres.
Indeed, the Borsuk graph Bor(S n ; δ) has S n as its vertex set and an edge between any two vertices
at a distance more than δ. Its neighborhood complex turns out to be the nerve simplicial complex of
all open balls in S n of radius π − δ, recovering the Čech complex Č(S n ; π − δ) on the nose. Hence, by
choosing G = Bor(S n ; δ) in Lovász’s bound, we relate the connectivity of Čech complexes of spheres
to the chromatic numbers of Borsuk graphs:

                    χ(Bor(S n ; δ)) ≥ conn(N (Bor(S n ; δ))) + 3 = conn(Č(S n ; π − δ)) + 3.

This recovers the classical result that the chromatic number of the Borsuk graph is at least n + 2 for
scales δ which are only slightly less than π [29, 38, 39]1, since in this regime, the nerve lemma [13, 22]
applies to give Č(S n ; π −δ) ≃ S n , yielding χ(Bor(S n ; δ)) ≥ conn(S n )+3 = n+2. There is furthermore
an equality χ(Bor(S n ; δ)) = n + 2 for δ sufficiently close to π. However, in this paper, we allow the
scale δ to be far away from π. Using known bounds on the chromatic numbers of Borsuk graphs in
terms of packings and coverings of spheres (see, for example, [37]), we obtain upper bounds on the
connectivity of Čech complexes of spheres at all scale parameters.
   To lower bound the connectivity of Čech complexes of spheres, we rely on the appendix by Barmak
in Farber’s paper [20]. This appendix builds upon prior work [35, 36, 16, 24] lower bounding the
connectivity of clique complexes of graphs. Crucially, Barmak’s appendix generalizes these results
to arbitrary simplicial complexes, meaning they can be applied to the non-clique Čech simplicial
complexes we study here. We refer the reader to related results on the connectivity of Vietoris–Rips
complexes of hypercube graphs [7], on the connectivity of clique complexes of preferential attachment
graphs [41], on the connectivity of Vietoris–Rips complexes of spheres [5], and on the connectivity
of independence complexes of graphs [3] and hypergraphs [43]. See [11, 9, 10, 28] for results on the
topology of random Čech complexes, [46] for some applications of the fundamental group functor
to the Čech filtration of compact geodesic spaces, and [25] for results on the chromatic numbers of
random Borsuk graphs.

a dataset. If one’s dataset is sampled from a manifold, then in the limit, as more and more points are

                                             3. Preliminaries

  In this section, we provide preliminary definitions and notations that we will use for metric spaces,
topological spaces, simplicial complexes, and graphs. Important concepts will include covering radii
and numbers, connectivity, Čech complexes, Borsuk graphs, and neighborhood complexes.

3.1. Metric spaces. Let (X, d) be a metric space. Further, let B(x, r) be the open ball of radius r

closed ball of radius r centered at x. On occasion, we may write BX (x, r) or BX [x, r], for clarity.
   Our bounds on the connectivity of Čech complexes of spheres will be in terms of the k-th covering
radius or the δ-covering number of a metric space (X, d), which we now define.

   The main metric space of interest in this paper is the n-sphere S n = {x ∈ Rn+1 | ∥x∥ = 1},
equipped with the geodesic metric. In other words, the distance between two points x, y ∈ S n is given
by d(x, y) = arccos(x · y) ∈ [0, π]. With this metric, the sphere S n has diameter π.

3.2. Topological spaces. We write Y ≃ Y ′ to denote that the two topological spaces Y and Y ′ are
homotopy equivalent. The connectivity of a non-empty topological space Y , denoted conn(Y ), is the
maximal index k such that the homotopy groups πi (Y ) are trivial for all i ≤ k.
   For a commutative ring R with unity, the R-homological dimension of a non-empty topological
space Y , denoted h-dimR (Y ), is the maximal degree i such that the i-th reduced homology group of
Y with coefficients in R is non-zero, i.e., He i (Y ; R) ̸= 0.
   We note that connectivity and R-homological dimensions are homotopy invariants of topological
spaces, i.e., if Y ≃ Y ′ , then conn(Y ) = conn(Y ′ ) and h-dimR (Y ) = h-dimR (Y ′ ).

3.3. Simplicial complexes. A simplicial complex K on a vertex set V is a collection of finite subsets
of V , containing all singletons, such that if σ ∈ K and τ ⊆ σ, then τ ∈ K as well. In this paper,
we identify simplicial complexes with their geometric realizations. We will be most interested in
simplicial complexes that can be built on top of metric spaces, such as Čech and Vietoris–Rips
simplicial complexes.
   A Čech complex on a metric space (X, d) for scale r > 0, denoted Č(X; r), is the nerve of the
collection {B(x, r)}x∈X of open balls of radius r in X, indexed by the points of X. More explicitly,

3.4. Graphs. All graphs in this paper are simple graphs, meaning there are no loops, and at most
a single edge between any two vertices. Formally, a graph G = (V, E) consists of a set of vertices V
and a set E of edges, where each edge is a subset of V of size two. For v, v ′ ∈ V , we write v ∼ v ′ to
denote that the edge {v, v ′ } is in E.
   Crucially, we will consider graphs with infinitely many vertices and edges. For example, for n ≥ 1
and r > 0, the 1-skeleton of the Čech complex Č(S n ; r) is an infinite graph.
   The chromatic number of a graph G = (V, E), denoted χ(G), is the minimum number of colors
needed to label the vertices of G such that no two adjacent vertices share the same color.
   The neighborhood complex of a graph G = (V, E), denoted N (G), is the simplicial complex with
vertex set V , which contains a finite subset σ ⊆ V as a simplex if there is some vertex v ∈ V such
that w ∼ v for all w ∈ σ.

Remark 3.1. While Borsuk graphs were originally introduced in [19] with the open (>) convention
that we use in this paper, Borsuk graphs with the closed (≥) convention have been more popular in
the literature since; see, for example, [30, 29]. The closed Borsuk graph of S n for scale δ > 0, denoted
Bor[S n ; δ], is a simple graph whose vertex set is S n and whose edges are all pairs of vertices x, y ∈ S n
with d(x, y) ≥ δ. For any small ε > 0, we have that

                                 Bor[S n ; δ + ε] ⊆ Bor(S n ; δ) ⊆ Bor[S n ; δ].                           (1)

Therefore, many results about closed Borsuk graphs remain true for open Borsuk graphs as well. In
particular, when δ is sufficiently close to π, the equality χ(Bor(S n ; δ)) = n + 2 holds.

   We will see in Section 5 that the neighborhood complex of the (open) Borsuk graph Bor(S n ; δ)
is equal to the Čech complex Č(S n ; π − δ); the neighborhood complex of the closed Borsuk graph
Bor[S n ; δ] is a nerve complex of closed balls instead of open balls.

                                 4. A lower bound on connectivity

   The goal of this section is to provide a lower bound on conn(Č(S n ; π − δ)), the connectivity of the
Čech complex of the n-sphere S n at the scale π − δ, where 0 < δ < π.
   Using the results from Barmak’s Appendix in [20], it has been shown in [5, Section 4.1] that if
0 < δ < covS n (2k + 2), then the connectivity of the Vietoris–Rips complex of S n at scale π − δ is at
least k, i.e., conn(VR(S n ; π − δ)) ≥ k. In the same spirit, here we use Barmak’s technique to get a
lower bound on conn(Č(S n ; π − δ)).
   We recall that the closed star of a vertex v in a simplicial complex K, denoted stK (v), is the
subcomplex stK (v) = {σ ∈ K | σ ∪ {v} ∈ K}. A simplicial complex K is said to be ℓ-conic if any
subcomplex of at most ℓ vertices is contained in a simplicial cone, i.e., in the closed star stK (v) of
some vertex v. Barmak’s result [20, Appendix, Theorem 4] states that if K is (2k + 2)-conic, then K
is k-connected.

Proposition 4.1. If 0 < δ < covS n (2k + 2), then conn(Č(S n ; π − δ)) ≥ k.

adopted from [8, Theorem 10.6] where Björner uses this assumption in his proof. For K = Č(S n ; π−δ),
the family {stK (v)}v∈K is not locally finite. So, we adopt the following technique with finite subsets

                                5. An upper bound on connectivity

   We now give an upper bound on the connectivity of Čech complexes of spheres. We begin by noting
that the approach used to upper bound the connectivity of Vietoris–Rips complexes of spheres will not
work in the Čech case. Indeed, the proof of [5, Claim 4.2] constructed an odd map from VR(S n ; π − δ)
into the Euclidean space R that missed the origin. This is possible only since VR(S n ; π − δ) contains
no edges between antipodal vertices of S n for δ > 0. Unfortunately, the Čech complex Č(S n ; π − δ)
does contain all antipodal edges for δ < π2 , which is the regime of interest.
   Therefore, we upper bound the connectivity of Čech complexes of spheres via a different approach,
namely by relating this connectivity to the chromatic numbers of Borsuk graphs. Let Bor(S n ; π − δ)
be the Borsuk graph whose vertex set is S n and whose edges are all pairs of vertices at a distance
                                                        8
more than π − δ. The basis for our connection is the observation that the neighborhood complex of
the Borsuk graph Bor(S n ; δ) is the same as the Čech complex Č(S n ; π − δ). Indeed, for σ ⊆ S n finite,
we have that

                σ ∈ N (Bor(S n ; δ)) ⇐⇒ ∃ v ∈ S n with x ∼ v in Bor(S n ; δ) for all x ∈ σ
                                    ⇐⇒ ∃ v ∈ S n with d(x, v) > δ for all x ∈ σ
                                    ⇐⇒ ∃ − v ∈ S n with d(x, −v) < π − δ for all x ∈ σ
                                    ⇐⇒ σ ∈ Č(S n ; π − δ).

So, N (Bor(S n ; δ)) = Č(S n ; π − δ).
  Lovász’s famous result [30, Theorem 2] says for any graph G, the chromatic number satisfies
χ(G) ≥ conn(N (G)) + 3, where N (G) is the neighborhood complex of G.

Example 5.1. Let us illustrate Lovász’s result for the Petersen graph. The Petersen graph P is the
Kneser graph KG5,2 , whose vertices are 2-element subsets of a 5-element set and two of them are
joined by an edge if and only if they are disjoint. The degree of each vertex of P is 3, and therefore the
dimension of the neighborhood complex N (P) is 2. Each vertex of P gives rise to a unique triangle in
N (P), and no two triangles in N (P) share an edge. Also, each vertex of N (P) is contained in exactly
3 such triangles in N (P). It turns out that N (P) is homotopy equivalent to a finite connected graph
with holes, meaning that N (P) is not simply connected and conn(N (P)) = 0. Lovász’s bound [30,
Theorem 2] gives χ(P) ≥ conn(N (P)) + 3 = 0 + 3, and in fact, χ(P) = 3 by [30, Theorem 1’].

  Using Lovász’s result and the fact that N (Bor(S n ; δ)) = Č(S n ; π − δ) for each n ≥ 1 and δ ∈ (0, π),

                                         7. Homological dimension

   In this section, we explore the largest dimension in which the homology of Č(S n ; r) is non-zero.
For a topological space Y and a commutative ring R with unity, the R-homological dimension of Y
is defined as
                              h-dimR (Y ) := max{i ∈ Z | H
                                                         e i (Y ; R) ̸= 0}.
   The Z-homological dimension of a space is informative of its topology: a space that is homo-
topy equivalent to a CW complex of dimension k has Z-homological dimension at most k. The Z-
homological dimension of a space is often connected to its homotopy connectivity. Indeed, if Y is sim-
ply connected, then by the Hurewicz theorem [22, Theorem 4.32], we have conn(Y ) ≤ h-dimZ (Y ) − 1.
Note that conn(Y ) need not3 be a lower bound to h-dimR (Y ) − 1 for a general ring R.
   We know that Č(S n ; π − δ) ≃ S n for all δ ∈ [ π2 , π) due to the nerve lemma. We also know that
Č(S 1 ; π − δ) is homotopic to some odd-dimensional sphere for each δ > 0. Hence, the Z-homological
dimension of Č(S n ; π − δ) is known in all these cases. So, let us restrict our attention to the case
when n ≥ 2 and δ ∈ (0, π2 ).
   In Proposition 4.1, we gave a lower bound on the connectivity conn(Č(S n ; π − δ)). In particular,
if 0 < δ < covS n (2k + 2), then conn(Č(S n ; π − δ)) ≥ k. For n ≥ 2, it follows from [46, Theorem 11.2]
that the Čech complex Č(S n ; π − δ) is simply connected for each δ ∈ (0, π2 ). Hence, using the relation
between connectivity and Z-homological dimension stated in the previous paragraph, we get a lower
bound to h-dimZ (Č(S n ; π − δ)): if 0 < δ < covS n (2k + 2), then h-dimZ (Č(S n ; π − δ)) ≥ k + 1. In other
words, for any δ ∈ (0, π2 ) and n ≥ 2, Corollary 4.3 gives that

                               h-dimZ (Č(S n ; π − δ)) ≥ 21 numCoverS n (δ) − 1.                                 (4)

   Next, we try to use ideas from [34, Lemma 4.3] to get a lower bound to h-dimZ (Č(S n ; π − δ))
better than that provided in (4). We consider the Z2 -homological dimension. Using the universal
coefficient theorem for homology [22, Theorem 3A.3], one can see that for any space Y , we have
h-dimZ (Y ) ≥ h-dimZ2 (Y ) − 1. In fact, if H∗ (Y ; Z) is torsion-free, then h-dimZ (Y ) ≥ h-dimZ2 (Y ). So,
a lower bound to h-dimZ2 (Č(S n ; π − δ)) will give us a lower bound to h-dimZ (Č(S n ; π − δ)).
   To potentially lower bound h-dimZ2 (Č(S n ; π − δ)), the theory of box complexes will be relevant.
Box complexes of finite graphs have been well-studied; we refer the reader to [32, 17, 34].
   There are several related definitions of box complexes (see, for instance, [32, 17]), which all share
similar properties. In particular, we are using the following definition of box complex. For a finite
graph G, the box complex B(G) is defined to be

  B(G) = {A⊎B | A, B ⊂ V (G), A∩B = ∅, G[A, B] is complete bipartite, CN(A) ̸= ∅ ̸= CN(B)}.

Here, A ⊎ B = {(a, 0) | a ∈ A} ∪ {(b, 1) | b ∈ B}, CN(A) = {v ∈ V (G) | (v, a) ∈ E(G) for all a ∈ A},
CN(B) = {v ∈ V (G) | (v, b) ∈ E(G) for all b ∈ B}, and G[A, B] is a (not necessarily induced)
subgraph of G with vertex set A ∪ B and edge set {(a, b) ∈ E(G) | a ∈ A, b ∈ B}, where V (G) and
E(G) denote the sets of vertices and edges of G, respectively.
  For a finite graph G, the box complex B(G) is a free Z2 -simplicial complex satisfying the following
three properties:
    (a) The box complex B(G) and the neighborhood complex N (G) are homotopy equivalent.
    (b) The box complex B(Km ) of the complete graph Km is Z2 -homotopy equivalent to the sphere
        S m−2 .
    (c) If H is a subcomplex of G, then B(H) is a Z2 -subcomplex of B(G).

   We conclude with a list of open questions and conjectures, that, we hope, will stimulate further

Conjecture 8.1. For n ≥ 1, the connectivity of the Čech complex Č(S n ; r) is a non-decreasing
function of the scale r ∈ (0, π).

   Conjecture 8.1 is true for n = 1 [1, Section 9], and for n ≥ 2, it is known that Č(S n ; r) is simply
connected for all r. For n ≥ 3, even though though Č(S n ; r) ≃ S n has connectivity n−1 for 0 < r < π2 ,
it is not known if conn(Č(S n ; r)) ≥ n − 1 for π2 ≤ r < π.

Conjecture 8.2. For n ≥ 1, the homotopy type of the Čech complex Č(S n ; r) changes only countably
many times as r varies over the range (0, π).

Question 8.3. Can we develop a Morse theory to describe the Čech complexes of Riemannian man-
ifolds?

  If so, Conjecture 8.2, which is true in the case of the circle (n = 1), could be proven by showing
that in such a Morse theory, there are only countably many critical events for Č(S n ; r) as r increases.

Conjecture 8.4. For each dimension n ≥ 1 and scale r > 0, the Čech complex Č(S n ; r) is homotopy
equivalent to a finite CW complex.

  We know that this conjecture is true for n = 1 and all r > 0, and for all n ≥ 1 when r < π2 .
  We note that Conjecture 8.4 is false if one defines the Čech complexes using closed balls instead of
open balls; see [1, Section 9].

Question 8.5. Assuming that Č(S n ; r) is homotopy equivalent to a finite-dimensional CW complex,
how does the dimension vary as a function of the scale r?

   If Č(S n ; r) were homotopy equivalent to a k-dimensional CW complex, then h-dimZ (Č(S n ; r)) ≤ k.
Dually, if h-dimZ (Č(S n ; r)) > k (see Question 7.3), then Č(S n ; r) could only be homotopy equivalent
to a CW complex of dimension larger than k.
