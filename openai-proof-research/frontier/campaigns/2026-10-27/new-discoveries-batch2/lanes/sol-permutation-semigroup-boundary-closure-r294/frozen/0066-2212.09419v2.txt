1.3. LLT polynomials and Schur positivity. While studying quantum affine algebras and unipotent va-
rieties, Lascoux, Leclerc, and Thibon introduced a q-analogue of a product of skew Schur functions,
now called the LLT polynomials [LLT97]. Lascoux, Leclerc, and Thibon conjectured, and Grojnowski and
Haiman proved, the Schur positivity of LLT polynomials [GH07]. In addition, Theorem 1.4 can be trans-
lated into a (positive) LLT-expansion (indexed by tuples of ribbons) of the modified Macdonald polyno-
mials. Therefore, one can study the linear combinations of LLT polynomials to tackle the Schur positivity
of Iλ,µ [X ; q, t ].
   Recently, relations between linear combinations of LLT polynomials (called the LLT equivalence, see
Section 6) have been extensively studied in [Lee21; HNY20; Mil19; AS22; Tom23]. After applying the
column exchange rule and a series of LLT equivalences, we obtain the following theorem:

Theorem 1.5. Let ν be a partition and λ, µ ⊆ ν be two distinct partitions such that |ν/λ| = |ν/µ| = 1.
Suppose that the cell ν/λ is in the first or second row of the partition λ. Then Iλ,µ [X ; q, t ] has a positive
expansion in LLT polynomials. In particular, Iλ,µ [X ; q, t ] is Schur positive.

   As a corollary, we obtain Schur positivity results for Iλ,µ [X ; q, t ] at t = 1 or q = 1 (Corollary 6.8).

1.4. Combinatorial formula for Schur coefficients. Macdonald [Mac88] conjectured, and Haiman proved
[Hai01], that the Schur coefficients of the modified Macdonald polynomials are, in fact, polynomials in
q and t with nonnegative integer coefficients. These Schur coefficients are called the modified (q, t )-
Kostka polynomials. Haiman’s proof relies heavily on the geometry of the isospectral Hilbert scheme of
n points in the plane and does not provide a combinatorial interpretation for the modified (q, t )-Kostka
polynomials.
   We hope that Theorem 1.5 sheds light on providing a combinatorial formula for the modified (q, t )-
Kostka polynomials. In particular, we describe the Schur expansion of Iµ,ν [X ; q, t ] for two neighboring
hook shapes µ and ν (Corollary 7.3), building upon Assaf’s formula [Ass18]..

1.5. Sketch of the proof of Theorem 1.3 and Theorem 1.5. We first define the (generalized) modified
Macdonald polynomials for filled diagrams in Section 3. Then, we introduce two identities between
modified Macdonald polynomials. The first one is the cycling identity (Lemma 3.6, Figure 3), which fol-
lows directly from the definition. The second one is the column exchange rule (Proposition 4.3, Figure 5),
which is essential in the proofs of Theorem 1.3 and Theorem 1.5. By applying these identities to the mod-
ified Macdonald polynomials H   eµ [X ; q, t ] and H
                                                   eλ [X ; q, t ], we reduce Theorem 1.3 to Proposition 4.14 and
Theorem 1.5 to Lemma 6.4.

1.6. Organization. This paper is organized as follows. We begin with some preliminaries in Section 2. In
Section 3, we define a generalization of the modified Macdonald polynomials indexed by general shapes
and statistics, called a filled diagram. In Section 4, we introduce and prove the column exchange rule,
which plays a central role in our work. We also define Butler permutations and prove an F -expansion
formula for the two-column diagram case (Proposition 4.14). Section 5 contains a proof of Theorem 1.3.
In Section 6, we provide background on LLT polynomials and their connection to the modified Macdon-
ald polynomials. Then, using several LLT equivalences, we prove Theorem 1.5. In Section 7, we discuss
two combinatorial formulas, which are consistent with Butler’s conjecture. In the final section, we pose
some open questions for future research.

4.2. Butler permutations. In this subsection, we define Butler permutations, which constitute exactly
half of all permutations. Butler permutations play a central role in the F -expansion of Iλ,µ [X ; q, t ] (see
Theorem 1.3).
   Before we define Butler permutations, we set some terminology for directed perfect matchings. For a
directed perfect matching M = {(a 1 , a 2 ), . . . , (a 2n−1 , a 2n )}, we say an arc (a, b) is in the forward direction if
a < b and the reverse direction otherwise. An arc (a, b) is nested in the other arc (c, d ) if min{c, d } < a, b and
18                                   DONGHYUN KIM, SEUNG JIN LEE, AND JAESEONG OH


max{c, d } > a, b. We say that a pair of arcs (a, b) and (c, d ) is crossing if min{a, b} < min{c, d } < max{a, b} <
max{c, d } or min{c, d } < min{a, b} < max{c, d } < max{a, b}. If not, we say the pair is noncrossing.

Definition 4.8. For a permutation w = w 1 . . . w n ∈ Sn with n ≥ 2, consider a directed perfect matching
M (w) on {0, 1, . . . , n + 1} if n is even (or on {0, 1, . . . , n} if n is odd), with directed arcs α1 (w), α2 (w), . . . as
follows:
      (1) if n is even,
             • α1 (w) is a directed arc from w n to n + 1,
             • αi (w) is a directed arc from w n−2(i −1) to w n−2(i −1)+1 for 2 ≤ i ≤ n2 , and
             • α n2 +1 (w) is a directed arc from 0 to w 1 .
      (2) if n is odd,
             • αi (w) is a directed arc from w n−2(i −1)−1 to w n−2(i −1) for 1 ≤ i ≤ n−1
                                                                                        2 , and
             • α n+1 (w) is a directed arc from 0 to w 1 .
                  2

Let k(w) be the smallest integer k such that the pair of arcs αk (w) and αk+1 (w) is noncrossing, if such k
exists. We say that w is a Butler permutation if either
       • αk+1 (w) is nested in αk (w) and αk (w) is in the reverse direction, or
       • αk+1 (w) is not nested in αk (w) and αk (w) is in the forward direction.

6.1. LLT polynomials. This subsection covers the background for LLT polynomials. The original defini-
tion of LLT polynomials involves ribbon tableaux and spin statistics [LLT97]. Later, an alternative model
was discovered by Haiman and Bylund [HHLRU05], and the two models are directly related via the Lit-
tlewood quotient map (sometimes called the Stanton–White correspondence [SW85]). Subsequently, a
fundamental quasisymmetric function expansion of LLT polynomials was given in [HHL05], which we
adopt as the definition.
    Let ν = (ν(1) , ν(2) , . . . ) be a tuple of skew partitions. For a standard tableau T = (T (1) , T (2) , . . . ) of shape
ν , an inversion of T is a pair of cells u ∈ ν(i ) and v ∈ ν( j ) such that T (i ) (u) > T ( j ) (v) and either
      • i < j and c(u) = c(v), or
      • i > j and c(u) = c(v) + 1,
where c(u) denotes the content of the cell u = (a, b), which is a − b. Denote by inv(T ) the number of
inversions in T . For a tuple of skew shapes, the reading order is a total order on cells such that u < v if
c(u) < c(v) or c(u) = c(v) and u is to the left of v. A reading word of a semistandard tableau of a tuple of
skew shapes is the word obtained by recording its entries in the reading order (from larger to smaller in
that order). We denote the reading word of a tableau T as rw(T ). Finally, the LLT polynomial LLTν [X ; q]
is defined by

This proves inv(w [k+1,n] ) = inv(u [k,n] ) − (n − k).

proves inv(w [k+1,n] ) = inv(u [k,n] ).
   For any word w and denoting u = Aµ (w), we have w k < w k+1 if and only if u k < u k+1 as w [k+1] = u [k+1] .
