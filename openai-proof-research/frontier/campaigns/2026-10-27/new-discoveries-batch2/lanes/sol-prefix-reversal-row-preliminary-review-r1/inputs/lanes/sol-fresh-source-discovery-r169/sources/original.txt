                                           The cubic pancake graphs are Cayley graphs over the symmetric group Symn generated by three prefix reversals.
                                           There is the following open problem: characterize all the sets of three prefix reversals that generate Symn . As the
                                           largest prefix reversal of length n is always included in a triple, we give a complete solution of the problem when
                                           any of the two smallest or the two largest lengths but n are included in a triple of prefix reversals. Moreover, some
                                           conditions implying a triple of prefix reversals does not generate Symn are considered. Computational results on the
                                           diameter and the girth of some cubic pancake graphs are presented, and conjectures for future research are formulated.

                                           Keywords: cubic pancake graph, prefix reversal, generating set, symmetric group



                                           1       Introduction
                                           A classical and celebrated result Dixon (2005) states that two random permutations of a set of n elements
                                           almost surely generate either the symmetric or the alternating group of degree n, and similar results hold
                                           when more generators are considered. In particular, the symmetric group can always be generated by
                                           the transposition (1 2) together with the n-cycle (1 2 3 . . . n). Two well-known generating sets of n − 1
                                           transpositions are the Coxeter generators {(i (i + 1)) | 1 ⩽ i < n} and the star generators {(1 i) | 1 <
                                           i ⩽ n} which give rise to two Cayley graphs known respectively as the bubble-sort graph and the star
                                           graph (see Johnson (1963); Friedman (2000); Akers and Krishnamurthy (1989); Konstantinova (2008)).
                                           In convex geometry, the bubble-sort graph is known as a 1-skeleton of the permutahedron Thomas (2006).

The Coxeter generators play an important role in computer science Johnson (1963); Knuth (2011), knot
theory Birman and Brendle (2005), and the theory of reflection groups and Coxeter groups Humphreys
(1992). The above two generating sets of transpositions were considered in Kompel’makher and Liskovets
(1975); Slater (1978) where more general result was proved. Namely, it was shown that if an arbitrary
generating set of the symmetric group of degree n is given by transpositions then one can generate all n!
permutations with no repetitions which leads to a hamiltonian cycle in the corresponding Cayley graph.
  The symmetric group Symn is also generated by a set of n − 1 involutions known as prefix reversals,
defined by
                                                                           
                                 1    2      ... i − 1 i i + 1 ... n
                         ri =                                                  ,                      (1)
                                 i i − 1 ...        2    1 i + 1 ... n
where 1 < i ⩽ n. The action of ri is to reverse the order of the first i elements while leaving the
remaining n − i elements fixed. Equivalently, ri is the product of ⌊i/2⌋ disjoint transpositions, ri =
(1 i)(2 (i − 1)) · · · (⌊i/2⌋ (i + 1 − ⌊i/2⌋)).
  The Cayley graph generated by the set {r2 , r3 , . . . , rn } is called the pancake graph, denoted by Pn .
The pancake graph is famous because of the long-standing open problem, known as the pancake problem
which asks for the diameter of Pn Dweighter (1975). The girth of a graph Γ refers to the length of the
shortest cycle of Γ, and the circumference of Γ refers to the length of the largest cycle in Γ. Moreover,
Γ is said to be weakly pancyclic if Γ has a cycle of length ℓ for all g(Γ) ⩽ ℓ ⩽ c(Γ), where g(Γ)
and c(Γ) refer to the girth and circumference of Γ, respectively. Since the pancake graph is weakly
pancyclic Kanevsky and Feng (1995); Sheu et al. (2006) (in fact, a larger family of graphs that include
Pn , called the generalized pancake graphs, are known to be weakly pancyclic Blanco et al. (2019); Blanco
and Buehrle (2023)), this problem remains difficult to resolve.
  Our interest lies in describing sets of three prefix reversals that generate the entire symmetric group
Symn .
  The importance of fixed-degree pancake graphs, in particular, cubic pancake graphs as models of net-
works was shown in Bass and Sudborough (2003). The authors of that work considered cubic pancake
graphs as spanning subgraphs of the pancake graph Pn and they identified the following combinatorial
necessary conditions for a triple of distinct prefix reversals {ri , rj , rk } to generate the symmetric group
Symn :

    (1) One of the prefix reversals must be rn , since otherwise no element can be moved to or from position
        n.

    (2) One of the prefix reversals, say ri , must satisfy ⌊n/2⌋ < i < n, since otherwise if we take g ∈
        ⟨rn , rj , ri ⟩ with i < j ⩽ n/2, and if u < v ⩽ j then either g(u) ⩽ n/2 and g(v) ⩽ n/2 or
        g(u) > n/2 and g(v) > n/2. Thus, in this case, 2-transitivity is lost, while Symn is known to be
        2-transitive in its natural action for each n. Moreover, if n is odd, then i > ⌈n/2⌉ since otherwise
        ⌈n/2⌉ will remain fixed.

    (3) At least one prefix reversal must have an even index, since otherwise no element can be moved from
        an odd-numbered position to an even-numbered position.

    (4) At least one prefix reversal must be an odd permutation, since otherwise the generated group con-
        sists only of even permutations.

 (5) The indices must satisfy the condition gcd(i, j, k) = 1. Otherwise, if gcd(i, j, k) = ℓ > 1 then the
     elements of {1, 2, . . . , n} are partitioned into blocks of ℓ consecutive positions that can never be
     separated. In particular, at least one index should be odd.

Remark. Originally, in Bass and Sudborough (2003) the authors formulate Observation (5) as follows:
The generating set must contain at least two prefix reversals with relatively prime indices. Of course, if
this holds then gcd(i, j, k) = 1. The converse does not hold. Indeed, if i = 15, j = 12 and k = 10 then
gcd(i, j) = 3, gcd(j, k) = 2, and gcd(i, k) = 5, however gcd(i, j, k) = 1. Moreover, it is easy to check
that ⟨r15 , r12 , r10 ⟩ = Sym15 .
   The above necessary conditions are not sufficient, however, they can be used to help guide the search
for generators. In particular, in Bass and Sudborough (2003) it was shown that the symmetric group is
generated by the following six sets: {rn , rn−1 , r2 } for any n ⩾ 4; {rn , rn−1 , r3 } for any odd n ⩾ 5;
{rn , rn−1 , rn−2 } for any n ⩾ 4; {rn , rn−1 , rn−3 } for any odd n ⩾ 4; {rn , rn−2 , r3 } for any even n ⩾ 6;
and {rn , rn−2 , rn−3 } for any n ⩾ 5. The authors have used the following approach. For each of the sets
above, it was shown that the set can simulate the three generators of the shuffle-exchange permutation
network generated by the following three permutations written in cycle notation: (1 2 . . . n − 1 n) (right
shuffle), (n n−1 . . . 2 1) (left shuffle), and (1 2) (transposition of the first two elements). Using the same
approach, it was shown in Son (2022) that the set {rn , rn−2 , r2 } for any odd n ⩾ 5 is also a generating
set of the symmetric group.
   The set {rn , rn−1 , rn−2 } is known as Big-3 flips, and the corresponding cubic pancake graph generated
by this set is called the Big-3 pancake network Sawada and Williams (2016) for which it was conjectured
that there exists a cyclic Gray code. The girths and the diameters of the cubic pancake graphs generated
by the above seven sets were studied in Konstantinova and Son (2022) and Chervov et al. (2025).

1.1    Our contributions
In this paper, we focus on the following three problems.
Problem 1. Are there other generating sets of three prefix reversals for the symmetric group?
Problem 2. What are other necessary conditions for the set of three prefix reversals to generate the
symmetric group?
Problem 3. What are sufficient conditions for the set of three prefix reversals to generate the symmetric
group?
  In addressing Problem 2, we prove the following assertions.

Proposition 1. Let 2 ⩽ k < n − l < n and n > 3k + 1. If l ⩾ 2k + 1 then ⟨rn , rn−l , rk ⟩ < Symn .
Proposition 2. Let n ⩾ 6 and l ⩾ k + 1. If l divides n + 1 then ⟨rn , rn−l , rk ⟩ < Symn .
  In addressing the above problems, we obtain the following four classification results.
Theorem 1. The set {rn , rm , r2 }, with 2 < m < n and n ⩾ 4, generates Symn if and only if one of the
following conditions holds:

  (i) n is even and m = n − 1;
4                                         Blanco, Golubyatnikov, Konstantinova, Maslova, and Nikiforov

    (ii) n is odd and

        (a) m ∈ {n − 1, n − 2} if n ≡ 2 (mod 3) or if n ≡ 0 (mod 3);
        (b) m ∈ {n − 3, n − 2, n − 1} if n ≡ 1 (mod 3).

Theorem 2. The set {rn , rn−1 , rk }, with 2 ⩽ k ⩽ n − 2, generates Symn if and only if one of the
following conditions holds:

    (i) If n is even and k is even;

    (ii) n ≡ 3 (mod 4) and 2 ⩽ k ⩽ n − 2;

(iii) n ≡ 1 (mod 4) and k ≡ 2, 3 (mod 4).

Theorem 3. The set {rn , rm , r3 }, with 2 ⩽ m < n, m ̸= 3 and n ⩾ 5, generates Symn if and only if one
of the following conditions holds:

    (i) n is even and m = n − 2;

    (ii) m ∈ {n − 3, n − 1} and n ≡ 1, 5 (mod 6);

(iii) m = n − 1 if n ≡ 3 (mod 6).

Theorem 4. The set {rn , rn−2 , rk }, with 2 ⩽ k < n − 2 and n ⩾ 5, generates Symn if and only if k and
n are of different parity.
   Narrative of the results. The four classification theorems above provide a complete answer when one
of the indices is close to either end of the range. For a generating triple containing r2 or r3 , the other
non-rn prefix reversal must have one of the few lengths closest to n: only rn−1 , rn−2 , and, in certain
congruence classes, rn−3 can occur. The exact list is determined by n modulo 6. If the triple contains
rn−1 then generation is determined entirely by congruence conditions on n and the third index modulo 4.
If the triple contains rn−2 the criterion is particularly simple: the third index and n must have opposite
parity. Thus, in each of these four broad families, the question of whether three prefix reversals generate
Symn reduces to an explicit arithmetic test. The complete conditions are stated formally in Theorems 1
through 4.
   Organization of the paper. In Section 2, we describe group theory techniques, including some de-
veloped by the authors, that provide necessary and sufficient conditions for some triple {rn , rm , rk } to
generate Symn , in particular, we prove Propositions 1 and 2. In Section 3, we present our main results
relating to triples of prefix reversals that generate Symn , in particular, we prove Theorems 1 through 4.
Moreover, we formulate conjectures in Section 4 for future research. More specifically, Conjecture 1
concerns the asymptotic number of generating triples, Conjectures 2 and 3 describe the apparent shift
phenomena, Conjecture 4 describes the triangular non-generating region, and Conjecture 5 concerns the
number of possible middle indices when the smallest reversal length is fixed. We finish with Section 4.2
where we discuss computational results relating to the cubic pancake graphs such as their diameters,
girths, and hamiltonicity.

2      Preliminaries
The following notation is used. Let Ω denote the set {1, 2, . . . , n}, and let Symn and Altn denote the
symmetric group and the alternating group of degree n, respectively. We use two notations for a permu-
tation π, namely, a cyclic notation for an n-cycle π = (π1 π2 . . . πn ), where π(1) → π(2), π(2) → π(3),
. . . , π(n) → π(1), and a one-line notation π = [π1 π2 . . . πn ], where π(i) = πi for any 1 ⩽ i ⩽ n. For
σ, π ∈ Symn , we compose them using right-to-left multiplication, so σπ(i) = σ(π(i)) for any 1 ⩽ i ⩽ n.
It is easy to see that by (1) we have rn rn−1 = (1 2 . . . n − 1 n). Further, if it necessary in our proofs we
consider cyclic permutations as a product of appropriate prefix reversals.
    We now proceed to describe some tools from group theory that are used in the proofs of our results.
Let G be a finite group acting on a finite set Ω. The G-orbit of x ∈ Ω, or simply the orbit of x, denoted
by O(x), refers to the set O(x) = {g(x) : g ∈ G}. A G-invariant set is a union of G-orbits. The action
is said to be transitive on Ω if for each x, y ∈ Ω there is g ∈ G such that g(x) = y. A nonempty subset
B ⊆ Ω is called a block of imprimitivity (or simply a block) for the action if for every g ∈ G either
g(B) = B or g(B) ∩ B = ∅, where g(B) = {g(b) : b ∈ B}. Moreover, the family {g(B) : g ∈ G}
of translates of a block B forms a G-invariant partition of Ω, called a block system. There are at least
two block systems for every action: {x} for x ∈ Ω and Ω itself, and one refers to these as trivial blocks.
The action of G on Ω is primitive if it is transitive and the only blocks are the trivial ones. Otherwise, a
transitive action is said to be imprimitive. Additionally, if H is a proper subgroup of G, we write H < G.
    Main ideas in the proofs. The proofs use two recurring strategies.
    1) To show that a triple does not generate Symn , we identify an invariant set of positions or a nontrivial
block system, thereby proving that the generated group is intransitive or imprimitive while Symn is known
to be primitive in the natural action on the set Ω.
    2) To prove that a triple does generate Symn , we construct explicit cycles, transpositions, or 3-cycles as
words in the prefix reversals and then apply standard generation criteria for the symmetric and alternating
groups.
    This division between obstruction arguments and explicit generation arguments provides a common
structure for the case analysis in Section 3.
    In our proofs, we utilize a few results from group theory regarding generating sets of Symn .
Lemma 1 ((Wielandt, 1964, Theorem 13.3)). Let H ⩽ Sym(Ω) = Symn be a permutation group acting
naturally on the set Ω = {1, . . . , n} such that there exists a transposition h ∈ H. Then H = Sym(Ω) if
and only if H is primitive on Ω.
    The following fact is well-known, see, for example, (Iradmusa and Taleb, 2018, Theorem 1.1).
Lemma 2. H = ⟨(1 2 . . . n), (a b)⟩ = Symn , 1 ⩽ a < b ⩽ n, if and only if gcd(b − a, n) = 1.
   A more general result relating to generating sets of Symn formed by one n-cycle and one transposition
is proved in Janusz (1992).
Lemma 3 ((Janusz, 1992, Corollary 4)). Let σ be an n-cycle and τ = (a b) be a transposition. Let q be
an integer satisfying σ q (a) = b. Then ⟨σ, τ ⟩ = Symn if and only if gcd(q, n) = 1.
    The following result proved in Iradmusa and Taleb (2018) is also used in our proofs.
Lemma 4 ((Iradmusa and Taleb, 2018, Theorem 1.3)). Let G be the permutation subgroup of Symn
generated by the n-cycle (1 2 . . . n) and the 3-cycle (a b c) with 1 ⩽ a < c < b ⩽ n. Then,
  (i) If n is even, then G = Symn if and only if gcd(b − a, c − a, n) = 1.

    (ii) If n is odd, then G = Altn if and only if gcd(b − a, c − a, n) = 1.

Proof: Let
                  Φ = {1, . . . , k} ∪ rn ({1, . . . , k}) = {1, . . . , k} ∪ {n − k + 1, . . . , n}.
Then |Φ| = 2k and Φ is an ⟨rn , rk ⟩-invariant set. Let

                                  ∆ = {t ∈ Ω : t ≡ s        (mod l) for s ∈ Φ}.

We claim that ∆ is a proper H-invariant subset of Ω (i. e., ∆ is a union of H-orbits). Indeed, let x ≡ s
(mod l), where s ∈ Φ. We have x = s+w·l for some w and rn (x) = n−x+1 = n−s+1−w·l ≡ n−s+1

is a proper subset of Ω. Thus, H < Symn .

following conjectures can be stated assuming that 2 ⩽ k < m < n.
Conjecture 2. If ⟨rn , rm , rk ⟩ = Symn then ⟨rn+4 , rm+4 , rk+4 ⟩ = Symn+4 .
Conjecture 3. For any n ≡ 0, 1 (mod 4), if ⟨rn , rm , rk ⟩ = Symn then ⟨rn+2 , rm+2 , rk+2 ⟩ = Symn+2 .
Conjecture 4. If m + k < n then ⟨rn , rm , rk ⟩ < Symn . Moreover, if n is odd and m + k < n − 1 then
⟨rn , rm , rk ⟩ < Symn .

4.2     Hamiltonicity, diameter, girth
One of the open problems on Cayley graphs is the conjecture stating that every connected Cayley graph
over a finite group has a Hamiltonian cycle. The conjecture was inspired by an open problem about Hamil-
tonicity of vertex-transitive graphs Lovász (1970). Hamiltonicity of all 1939864 cubic Cayley graphs
on up to 5000 vertices is confirmed (see https://graphsym.net/). Computational experiments
showed that in the smallest case n = 7 that has not previously been verified (as 7! > 5000) there are eight
possible generating triples (n, m, k) as follows: (7, 4, 2), (7, 5, 2), (7, 6, 2), (7, 4, 3), (7, 6, 3), (7, 5, 4),
(7, 6, 4), and (7, 6, 5), and the corresponding cubic pancake graphs are Hamiltonian. Therefore, the fol-
lowing statement holds.
Statement 1. Any cubic pancake graph over the symmetric group of degree n = 4, 5, 6, 7 is Hamiltonian.
   Moreover, diameters, girths and the shortest cycles were found for all cubic pancake graphs with n = 7,
and the results are summarized in Table 1. Let us note that Table 1 shows that for n = 7 the minimal
diameter among all the corresponding cubic pancake graphs are associated with the graphs whose gener-
ating triples are (7, 6, 4) and (7, 6, 5). Meanwhile, girths of these graphs are different; indeed, the graph
generated by (7, 6, 4) has the maximal girth and the graph generated by (7, 6, 5) has the minimal girth
among them.
   Additionally, for the Big-3 flips {rn , rn−1 , rn−2 } it was conjectured in Sawada and Williams (2016)
that for any n ⩾ 4 the corresponding Big-3 pancake graph has cyclic Gray codes, which means it has a
Hamiltonian cycle. Due to our computational experiments the following statement holds.
Statement 2. The Big-3 pancake graph over the symmetric group of degree n = 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 is Hamilto-
nian.
   Totally, for n = 8 there are five possible triples generating the whole group, namely, (8, 7, 2), (8, 6, 3),
(8, 7, 4), (8, 6, 5), and (8, 7, 6). The data on diameters, girths and the shortest cycles of the corresponding
graphs are given in Table 2.
   To represent the shortest cycles in Table 1 and Table 2, we use their canonical forms Cl = ri0 . . . ril−1
with taking the lexicographically maximal sequence of indices i0 . . . iℓ−1 , where 2 ⩽ ij ⩽ n, and ij ̸=
ij+1 for any 0 ⩽ j ⩽ ℓ − 1. Contracted forms are also used in the tables such that (ra rb )k corresponds
to the cycle Cℓ = ra rb . . . ra rb , where ℓ = 2 k, a ̸= b, and ra rb appears exactly k times. A similar
notation is used for cycles of forms (ra . . . rc )2 and (ra rb rc )4 , where (ra . . . rc )2 = (ra . . . rc ra . . . rc )
and (ra rb rc )4 = (ra rb rc ra rb rc ra rb rc ra rb rc ). For example, (r7 r6 r4 )4 corresponds to a cycle of length
12 whose canonical form is C12 = (r7 r6 r4 r7 r6 r4 r7 r6 r4 r7 r6 r4 ). All the forms presented in the tables
were obtained by Lemma 1 from Konstantinova and Medvedev (2016), Theorem 1.3 from Konstantinova
and Medvedev (2014), and computational experiments.
   In Table 3, all generating sets are presented for the cubic pancake graphs that have minimal diameters
and girths among all possible cubic pancake graphs with a given n, where 4 ⩽ n ⩽ 10. As one can see
from the table, for any n ⩾ 6, the minimal girth is 8. These computational results are correlated with
theoretical results from Konstantinova and Son (2022). All generating sets of the cubic pancake graphs
with maximal diameters and girths are given in Table 4.
