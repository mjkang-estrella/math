              A VARIANT OF COLLATZ’S CONJECTURE
                  OVER BINARY POLYNOMIALS


                LUIS H. GALLARDO AND OLIVIER RAHAVANDRAINY




        Abstract. We study a natural analogue of Collatz’s conjecture for polyno-
        mials over F2 .




                                    1. Introduction
   The Collatz conjecture (also known as the Syracuse problem) is one of the most
famous unsolved problems in number theory. It concerns sequences of positive
integers defined by the following rule: starting from any positive integer n, if n is
even then replace it by n/2, and if n is odd then replace it by 3n + 1; see [9]. The
conjecture asserts that every such sequence eventually reaches the cycle 4 → 2 → 1.
   It is natural to search for functional analogues of this problem over polynomials
in Fq [x], where certain aspects sometimes become easier to analyse. Several authors
have proposed and studied such analogues; see, for example, [2, 1, 3, 7, 8] (this list
is not exhaustive). One general framework, introduced by Matthews [8], fixes
polynomials K, D ∈ Fq [x] with gcd(K, D) = 1 and defines the next term T of a
current polynomial S by
                                         K ·S−R
                                    T =           ,
                                             D
where R is chosen congruent to Kr modulo D for some residue r in a complete
residue system modulo D. This guarantees that T ∈ Fq [x].
   When q is even, many papers concentrate on the particularly simple case q = 2,
K = x (that could easily imply the case K = x + 1), and residues {0, 1} modulo x.
These choices make x play the role of the integer 2. In most of these settings the
resulting dynamical system is relatively tame: trajectories either cycle or diverge
in a controlled way, and when convergence occurs, the number of steps is bounded
by a polynomial in the degree of the starting polynomial.
   We propose to study a significantly more difficult variant, still over F2 [x], in
which the role of the integer 2 is played by the product x(x + 1). This is motivated
by the natural isomorphism F2 [x] ≃ F2 [x + 1] (substituting x 7→ x + 1) and by
our earlier work [6], where powers of 2 in Z (2a+b ) were systematically replaced

   In the present paper we define a Collatz-type iteration on F2 [x] that simulta-
neously removes factors of both x and x + 1. A nonzero polynomial A ∈ F2 [x] is
called even if gcd A, x(x + 1) ̸= 1, and odd otherwise (following [4]). The first
odd polynomial different from 1 is M1 = x2 + x + 1. Thus the natural analogue
of the map n 7→ 3n + 1 on odd integers is the map A 7→ M1 A + 1 on odd binary
polynomials.
   Given a nonzero A0 = A ∈ F2 [x], we define valuations
                        valx (S) = highest power of x dividing S,
                     valx+1 (S) = highest power of x + 1 dividing S,
and we decompose S = xvalx (S) (x + 1)valx+1 (S) S1 with S1 odd. The Collatz trans-
formation then generates three sequences:
                                     A0 = A,
                                     A1 = the odd part of A0 ,
                                     A2 = M1 A1 + 1,
                                     A3 = the odd part of A2 ,
                                          ..
                                           .
                                    A2k = M1 A2k−1 + 1,
                                 A2k+1 = the odd part of A2k ,
together with the corresponding integer sequences
                (a2k )k = (valx (A2k ))k        and   (b2k )k = (valx+1 (A2k ))k .
   An optimistic binary-polynomial Collatz conjecture would assert:
Conjecture 1.1. For every nonzero A ∈ F2 [x] there exists m ∈ N such that for
all k ≥ m,
                    A2k = x(x + 1) and A2k+1 = 1.
  We prove that every trajectory is finite and eventually reaches precisely this
configuration.
Theorem 1.2. Let A ∈ F2 [x] \ {0}. The sequences defined above terminate after
finitely many steps. More precisely, there exists m ∈ N (depending on A) such that
the even-polynomial sequence ends with x2 + x and the odd-polynomial sequence
ends with 1:
       (A2 , A4 , . . . , A2m ) = (. . . , x2 + x),    (A1 , A3 , . . . , A2m+1 ) = (. . . , 1).
The total number of odd polynomials appearing (the length rA ) satisfies rA ≤
2deg(A)−1 .

   The proof of this theorem appears in Section 2.
   The exponential bound 2deg(A)−1 is clearly suboptimal and far worse than the
polynomial-in-degree bounds obtained in the literature for the simpler choices
K = x or K = x + 1. This deterioration is expected: by simultaneously strip-
ping factors of both x and x + 1, our iteration explores a much richer dynamical
landscape. Nevertheless, extensive computations up to degree 35 (reported in Sec-
tion 3) strongly suggest that the true length rA is still bounded by a low-degree
polynomial in deg(A). The same section analyses special families (e.g. starting
polynomials of the form xn + x + 1) and provides further evidence for this phenom-
enon.

                             2. Proof of Theorem 1.2
   First of all, we need some general results on the numbers of odd and even
polynomials of a given degree d ≥ 2. We let Pd denote the set of all polynomials
of degree d and consider:
            Pd0,0 := {S ∈ Pd : S(0) = 0},     Pd0,1 := {S ∈ Pd : S(0) = 1},
            Pd1,0 := {S ∈ Pd : S(1) = 0},     Pd1,1 := {S ∈ Pd : S(1) = 1},
            Od := {S ∈ Pd : S is odd} = Pd0,1 ∩ Pd1,1 .
   We have P0 = O0 = {1} and O1 = ∅.
Lemma 2.1. The following statements hold:
   (i) The four sets Pd0,0 , Pd0,1 , Pd1,0 , and Pd1,1 all have the same cardinality 2d−1 .
  (ii) The set Od contains exactly 2d−2 polynomials if d ≥ 2.
Proof. (i) By the bijective map S 7→ S + 1, we have #Pd0,0 = #Pd0,1 and #Pd1,0 =
#Pd1,1 . Similarly, the bijection S(x) 7→ S(x + 1) gives #Pd0,0 = #Pd1,0 . It remains
to note that Pd is a disjoint union of Pd0,0 and Pd0,1 , and that #Pd = 2d .
   (ii) By induction on d. The case d = 2 is trivial since O2 = {x2 + x + 1}. Now,
suppose that #Os = 2s−2 for 2 ≤ s ≤ d − 1. We observe that
               Pd0,1 = {(x + 1)s S1 : 0 ≤ s ≤ d, s ̸= d − 1, S1 ∈ Od−s }.
Hence,
                    #Pd0,1 = #Od + #Od−1 + · · · + #O2 + #O0 .
Therefore, 2d−1 = #Od + 2d−3 + · · · + 1 + 1 = #Od + 2d−2 , and so #Od = 2d−2 .          □
   For k ≥ 0, we put dk := deg(A2k ) and ℓk := deg(A2k+1 ). We then obtain the
following lemmas.
Lemma 2.2. We have a0 , b0 ≥ 0 and a2k , b2k ≥ 1 for any k ≥ 1.
Proof. If k ≥ 1, then both x and x + 1 divide A2k , because, for t ∈ {0, 1},
                      A2k (t) = 1 + M1 (t)A2k−1 (t) = 1 + 1 = 0.                         □
Lemma 2.3. We have ℓk+1 ≤ ℓk ≤ deg(A), dk = ℓk−1 +2, and dk = ℓk +a2k +b2k .

Corollary 2.4. Both sequences (ℓk )k and (dk )k are convergent. We have
                   lim ℓk = ℓ     and       lim dk = d, where d = ℓ + 2.
                  k→∞                      k→∞

Corollary 2.5. There exists m ≥ 0 such that for any k ≥ m one has
                            ℓk = ℓ,     dk = d,     a2k = b2k = 1.
Proof. The convergent sequence (ℓk )k takes its values in the finite set
                                      {0, 1, . . . , deg(A)}.
Hence it is eventually constant.                                                         □
Corollary 2.6. For any k ≥ m we have deg(A2k ) = d and deg(A2k+1 ) = ℓ.
Corollary 2.7. There exists a positive integer t ≤ deg(A) such that the polynomials
A2(m+t) and A2(m+t)+1 satisfy
                        A2(m+t) = A2m ,          A2(m+t)+1 = A2m+1 .
Proof. For any k ≥ m, the polynomial A2k (resp. A2k+1 ) lies in the finite set of
polynomials of degree d (resp. ℓ).                                             □

                      M1 A2m+2t−1 + (1 + M1 )A2m+2t+1 = 1.
Since A2m+2t+1 = A2m+1 , we obtain a linear system of t equations with coefficients
in F2 [x] and t unknowns: A2m+1 , . . . , A2m+2t−1 . Its matrix C is circulant with first
row [M1 , 1 + M1 , 0, . . . , 0]. The right-hand side is the transpose of [1 . . . 1].

which is nonzero.
  Thus, this system admits the unique solution (1, . . . , 1).                           □
Corollary 2.9. The even and odd sequences E and O are, respectively,
            E = [A2 , . . . , A2m−2 , x2 + x]     and    O = [A1 , . . . , A2m−1 , 1].

Proof. We have just seen that ℓ = 0 and t = 1. Thus, d = 2, A2m+1 = 1, and
