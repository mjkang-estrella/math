                                                                                            Abstract
                                                   We consider partitions of a point set into two parts, and the lengths of the minimum spanning
                                               trees of the original set and of the two parts. If w(P ) denotes the length of a minimum spanning
                                               tree of P , we show that every set P of n ≥ 12 points admits a bipartition P = R ∪ B for which
                                               the ratio w(R)+w(B)
                                                             w(P )   is strictly larger than 1; and that 1 is the largest number with this property.
                                               Furthermore, we provide a very fast algorithm that computes such a bipartition in O(1) time
                                               and one that computes the corresponding ratio in O(n log n) time.
                                                   In certain settings, a ratio larger than 1 can be expected and sometimes guaranteed. For
                                                                                                                            2
                                               example, if P is a set of n random points uniformly distributed   √ in [0, 1] (n → ∞), then for any
                                               ε > 0, the above ratio in a maximizing partition is at least 2 − ε with √      probability tending to
                                               1. As another example, if P is a set of n points with spread at most α n, for some constant
                                               α > 0, then the aforementioned ratio in a maximizing partition is 1 + Ω(α−2 ).
                                                   All our results and techniques are extendable to higher dimensions.


                                         1    Introduction
                                         A Euclidean minimum spanning tree (EMST), for a set of n points in the Euclidean plane or
                                         Euclidean space is a spanning tree of the n points that has the minimum total length (the length
                                         of a tree edge is the Euclidean distance between its endpoints). Write w(P ) for the length of an
                                         EMST of P . Motivated by a question of Cultrera et al. [9, 10] arising from their work on chromatic
                                         alpha shapes and persistence, we study the following problem. For a finite set P of n points in the
                                         plane, and a bipartition P = R ∪ B into red and blue points, consider the ratio

                                                                                         w(R) + w(B)
                                                                                                     .                                                 (1)
                                                                                            w(P )

                                         Call the maximum, over all non-trivial bipartitions of P into two sets, the max MST-ratio of P ,
                                         denoted γ(P ). For n = 3, 4, 5 we exhibit examples for which the ratios are at most 1 and we have
                                         no larger examples with this property. As such, we are tempted to conjecture that perhaps 6 points
                                         suffice to guarantee a bipartition whose MST-sum strictly exceeds the MST length of the set itself,
                                         see Conjecture 1 below. We come close to answering this question, see our Theorem 3 below.

                                         Conjecture 1. For any set P of n ≥ 6 points in the plane we have γ(P ) > 1.

Related work. Graph decomposition is a classical topic in graph theory [8, 12, 22]. In this note,
we are interested in finding partitions of a points set into two parts such that the total length of
their MST’s is as large as possible.
     Estimating the length of a shortest tree or tour of n points in the unit square with respect to
Euclidean distances has been studied as early as 1940s and 1950s by Fejes Tóth [13], Few [15], and
Verblunsky [26], respectively. Clustering algorithms based on minimum and maximum spanning
trees have been studied by Asano et al. [1].
     In the same spirit, recall that the well-know Euclidean Steiner ratio is the infimum over all finite
point sets P in the plane of the ratio between the lengths of a shortest Steiner tree and that of
minimum spanning√tree of the set [17, 7]. Denoting this ratio by ρ, Gilbert and Pollak conjectured
in 1968 that ρ ≥ 3/2 = 0.866√. . .. Early results due to Graham and Hwang [18] and Du and

the following upper bounds for n = 3, 4, 5.

Disjoint disks. Let P be a finite set of points in Rd . Let D(P ) = {ω(p) : p ∈ P }, where ω(p) is
the largest closed ball centered at p and containing no other points from P in its interior. Given
p ∈ P , let r(p) denote the radius of ω(p).
    A set of points P in Rd is said to satisfy the disjoint balls property if D(P ) contains two disjoint
balls. For the plane, we use the term disjoint disks property. A key (easy) observation about this
property is its monotonicity.

Observation 6. Let P be a set of points in the plane that satisfies the disjoint disks property. If
P ⊂ Q, then Q also satisfies this property.

Proof. Assume that ω(p) and ω(p′ ) are disjoint, where p, p′ ∈ P . Adding the points in Q \ P
maintains this property, since the disks centered at p and p′ can only get smaller.

Lemma 7. Let P be a set of points in the plane that satisfies the disjoint disks property.
  Then there exist two points p, q ∈ P such that |pq| > r(p) + r(q).

Proof. Let p, q ∈ P be the centers of two disjoint disks in D(P ). Then p, q clearly satisfy the
requirement.

5     Concluding remarks
The techniques used to handle the planar case immediately extend to Rd for any fixed dimension d.
Specifically we need the disjoint balls property; we rely on estimates due to Füredi and Loeb [16],
given in (4). Let κ(d) denote the maximum number of balls in Rd that can form a configuration
where each ball intersects every other ball but does not contain the center of any other ball. As
mentioned earlier, it is known that 8 ≤ κ(2) ≤ 11. The following bounds hold in d-space:

                                    1.25d ≤ κ(d) ≤ (1.887 . . . + o(1))d .                                 (4)

    The proof of Theorem 15 is analogous to that of Theorem 3 and is therefore omitted.
Theorem 15. For every fixed d, there exists nd ∈ N with the following property. For any set P of
n ≥ nd points in Rd we have γ(P ) > 1. A suitable bipartition with this ratio can be computed in
O(1) time.
    We have seen that the disjoint balls property implies that the maximum MST ratio is strictly
larger than 1. However, the two properties are not equivalent.
    Another related question is how the γ ratio changes when points are added to a set. Suppose
that P0 is a point set with γ(P0 ) > 1, and an infinite sequence of points, p1 , p2 , . . ., are successively
added to P0 . By Theorem 3, γ(P0 ∪ {p1 , . . . , pn }) > 1 for every n such that |P0 | + n ≥ 12. Is it
possible that γ(P0 ∪ {p1 , . . . , pn }) tends to 1 as n → ∞? We give a positive answer. Assuming that
the first n points p1 , . . . , pn are known, let Pn = P0 ∪{p1 , . . . , pn }, wn = w(Pn ), and Dn = diam(Pn ),
where an , bn is a diameter pair in Pn . Assume that γ(Pn ) ≤ 1 + ε for some ε > 0, we can select
pn+1 so that γ(Pn+1 ) ≤ 1 + ε/2, for every n. Let pn+1 be a point on the line incident to an , bn at a
sufficiently large distance f (wn , ε) from b, with an , bn , pn+1 appearing in this order. The verification
that the above inequality holds, for a suitable function f , is left to the reader. Observe that the
above construction is analogous to the upper bound construction in Proposition 2; and works in
any dimension.
    We conclude with three open problems:

    1. We have seen that γ(P ) > 1 can be arbitrarily close to 1 even for large n, whereas it is
       separated from 1 by a positive gap for randomly uniformly distributed sets and for α-dense
       sets. In what other cases can one deduce γ(P ) > 1 + λ for a positive constant λ?
    2. Is there an efficient algorithm for computing the maximum ratio, γ(P )?
              √
    3. Is the 2 lower bound for randomly uniformly distributed sets in the unit square tight?
