SPHERICAL CODES WITH MINIMAL ANGLE arccos(1/3) IN
DIMENSIONS FOUR, FIVE AND SIX
TEJASVI SINGH TOMAR
Abstract. Let A(n, θ) be the largest number of points on the unit sphere S n−1 ⊂ Rn with
pairwise angular distance at least θ. We prove A(4, arccos 13 ) = 14, A(5, arccos 31 ) ≤ 22 and
A(6, arccos 31 ) ≤ 34. The previous upper bounds were 15, 23 and 35. For n = 5 the best
known code has 21 points, so A(5, arccos 13 ) ∈ {21, 22}. Each upper bound comes from an
exact rational solution of the second level of the Lasserre hierarchy for spherical codes, in
the formulation of de Laat, Leijenhorst and de Muinck Keizer, and was checked with their
verification program. The largest number of points at angle π/3 from a given point of a kissing
configuration in Rn equals A(n − 1, arccos 31 ). So this number is 14 in R5 , 21 or 22 in R6 , and
at most 34 in R7 .

1. Introduction
A spherical code with minimal angle θ is a finite set C ⊂ S n−1 with ⟨x, y⟩ ≤ cos θ for all distinct x, y ∈ C. We write A(n, θ) for the largest size of such a code. Kissing configurations are
the codes with minimal angle π/3, and the kissing number in dimension n is A(n, π/3). Upper
bounds for A(n, θ) come from linear programming (Delsarte, Goethals and Seidel [4], Kabatiansky and Levenshtein [7]) and from semidefinite programming. Bachoc and Vallentin [1]
introduced the three-point bound and tabulated it for several dimensions and angles. For
cos θ = 31 their table improved the upper bounds for A(5, θ) and A(6, θ) from 24 and 37 to 23
and 35 and matched the bound A(4, θ) ≤ 15; the bounds 15 and 24 are due to Boyvalenkov
and Kazakov [2]. The best known codes have 14, 21 and 32 points (Section 4). (The table
of [1] lists 20 for n = 5.) Before this work the known bounds were therefore
14 ≤ A(4, θ) ≤ 15,

21 ≤ A(5, θ) ≤ 23,

32 ≤ A(6, θ) ≤ 35.

Cohn, de Laat and Leijenhorst [3, Table 6.1] list the 32-point code in R6 among the codes
conjectured to be optimal. We improve each of the three upper bounds by one.
Theorem 1.1. Let θ = arccos 13 . Then A(4, θ) = 14, A(5, θ) ≤ 22 and A(6, θ) ≤ 34.
Together with the codes of Section 4, Theorem 1.1 gives A(5, θ) ∈ {21, 22} and 32 ≤
A(6, θ) ≤ 34.
For a kissing configuration C ⊂ S n−1 (minimal angle π/3) and x ∈ C, call the points y ∈ C
with ⟨x, y⟩ = 12 the contacts of x. In the arrangement of unit balls centred at 0 and at 2y for
y ∈ C, the contacts of x correspond to the outer balls that touch the ball centred at 2x. Let
cn be the largest number of contacts of a point in a kissing configuration in Rn .
Corollary 1.2. For every n ≥ 2 we have cn = A(n − 1, arccos 31 ). Hence c5 = 14, c6 ∈ {21, 22}
and 32 ≤ c7 ≤ 34.
The angle arccos 13 enters through the tangent space at x: the normalised projections of
two contacts of x to x⊥ have inner product at most 31 , and conversely (Section 5). This
correspondence is classical; see for example [5, Section 4.1]. With the bounds of [1] it gives
c5 ≤ 15, c6 ≤ 23 and c7 ≤ 35.

The upper bounds come from the second level of the Lasserre hierarchy for spherical codes.
De Laat and Vallentin [8] defined the hierarchy, and de Laat, Leijenhorst and de Muinck
Keizer [9] reduced its second level by symmetry and used it to prove the optimality and
uniqueness of the D4 root system as a kissing configuration. For each of the three cases we
computed a numerical solution of their semidefinite program, rounded it to an exact rational
solution and checked that solution with their verification program. Section 2 recalls what a
certificate consists of, and Section 3 lists the three certificates. Section 4 recalls the lower
bounds, and Section 5 proves Corollary 1.2.
2. The second level of the Lasserre hierarchy
We use the second level of the Lasserre hierarchy for spherical codes [8] in the symmetryreduced form of [9, Section 4], and refer there for details. Fix n and t = cos θ. Call a set of points
of S n−1 admissible if their pairwise inner products lie in [−1, t]. The dual program asks for a
positive semidefinite kernel K on the admissible sets of at most two points, invariant under the
orthogonal group. After the symmetry reduction, K is given by positive semidefinite matrices
Fλ , one for each partition λ = (λ1 , λ2 ) with |λ| ≤ d1 that occurs, combined with the zonal
matrices of [9]. For k = 1, 2, 3, 4 and every admissible set S of k points, the sum of K(J1 , J2 )
over all J1 , J2 with J1 ∪ J2 = S must be at most −1 if k = 1 and at most 0 otherwise. These
inequalities are imposed as polynomial identities in the inner products of S: the difference
between the two sides is a sum of terms g r, where g runs through 1 and the symmetrised
polynomials that cut out the admissible Gram matrices, r is a sum of squares, and each term
has degree at most δ. Every code with minimal angle θ then has at most K(∅, ∅) points, and
K(∅, ∅) is one entry of the matrix for the trivial representation. The degrees d1 ≤ d2 determine
the partitions λ and the sizes of the matrices Fλ , and the even number δ ≥ d2 bounds the
degrees in the sums of squares.
A certificate consists of rational matrices Fλ and rational Gram matrices for the sums
of squares, in fixed polynomial bases, such that all of them are positive semidefinite and
all identities hold exactly. The verification program of [9], available with the data of that
paper [10], reads a certificate from files, checks that every matrix is positive definite by a
Cholesky factorisation in ball arithmetic [6], recomputes the four families of identities in exact
rational arithmetic, and compares K(∅, ∅) with the claimed bound.
3. The certificates
Table 1 lists the three certificates. For each case we solved the semidefinite program numerically, with a version of the interior-point solver of Leijenhorst and de Laat [11] modified for speed, in multiple-precision arithmetic. The objective was replaced by the constraint
K(∅, ∅) = N for the value N in the table, which lies above the numerical optimum. This
feasibility problem then has solutions with positive definite matrices, and the interior-point
method returns one. We rounded it with the procedure of Cohn, de Laat and Leijenhorst [3]:
the solution is approximated by rational numbers and projected onto the affine space defined
by the identities by an exact linear solve. All matrices stayed positive definite, so no change
of basis was needed.
Since A(n, θ) is an integer, the certificates give the upper bounds of Theorem 1.1.

4. Lower bounds
Codes of size 14 in R4 and of size 21 in R5 with minimal angle larger than arccos 31 are
listed in [12], and the 32 unit vectors along 16 equiangular lines in R6 with angle arccos 13 [14]
form a code of minimal angle arccos 31 . For completeness the supplement [13] contains a
14-point code in S 3 and a 21-point code in S 4 with rational coordinates, obtained by inverse
stereographic projection of rational points. Their largest pairwise inner products are 0.32094 . . .
and 0.31622 . . ., both less than 13 ; we checked this in exact arithmetic. Together with the
certificate for n = 4 this proves A(4, θ) = 14.
