arXiv:2606.01771v4 [math.DG] 9 Oct 2026
                                                  Abstract. This paper investigates an isoperimetric-type problem posed by L. A.
                                                  Santaló, concerning convex surfaces in hyperbolic 3-space that minimize total mean
                                                  curvature among all convex surfaces with fixed surface area. This problem asks for
                                                  a characterization of the minimizers and for the optimal form of a Minkowski-type
                                                  inequality in hyperbolic 3-space. In this work, we propose a conjectural description of
                                                  the minimizers under certain regularity assumptions. We also construct a new family
                                                  of convex surfaces as as potential minimizer candidates and establish a property of
                                                  the singular points of any minimizer.

see [13, Section 3]. By Blaschke selection theorem, a minimizer of total mean curvature

would like to characterize the geometry of the minimizers, as well as the optimal form of
Minkowski-type inequality. We call this Santaló’s problem. This is a generalization
of the isoperimetric problem in H3 related to the total mean curvature and surface area.
   Minimization of total mean curvature of convex hypersurfaces has long been studied.
In 1901, Minkowski [26] proved that the following inequality holds for any non-empty
bounded C 2 convex surface Γ ⊂ R3 ,

and equality holds only when Γ is a sphere in R3 .
   The original proof of Minkowski is based on the isoperimetric inequality together
with Steiner-Minkowski formulas. Hence, inequality (1.2) remains true if Γ is only a
C 1,1 surface (or equivalently, if Γ has positive reach). If we do not assume any regularity,
the same inequality holds if we use the mean width of the convex body enclosed by the
surface as an alternative definition for total mean curvature. Guan-Li [16, Theorem 2]
proved that (1.2) holds provided that Γ is star-shaped and mean-convex. G. Huisken
[16, Theorem 6] (see also [23]) showed that (1.2) holds for outward-minimizing surfaces.
Dalphin-Henrot-Masnou-Takahashi [8, Theorem 1.1] proved (1.2) in the case where Γ is
axially symmetric and such that Γ ∩ P is connected for every affine plane P orthogonal
to the axis of symmetry.

the isoperimetric inequality. This generalization uses the notion of mixed volumes and
quermassintegrals of convex bodies, and total mean curvature is in fact the first quer-
massintegral in Euclidean spaces. We refer to [31], [33], [29] for details, and [35] for
quermassintegrals in hyperbolic spaces. Generalizations of (1.2) to hyperbolic spaces
have been a long-standing problem [28], [10, 27]. Santaló’s problem is one such problem.
In recent years, using of curvature flows has led to progress, see [3, 4, 11, 13, 32, 35].

   In the study of convex bodies without any regularity assumption on their boundaries,
the theory of singular points and curvature measures provides useful tools. General
convex surfaces may have singular points, where there are multiple unit outer normal
vectors (or supporting planes). If the ambient space is 3-dimensional, there are two
types of singular points: 1-singular points, with a 2-dimensional normal cone, behaving
like edges of a cube; 0-singular points, with a 3-dimensional normal cone, behaving like
vertices of a cube. We will give a brief review for singular points and normal cones in
Section 2, Subsection 2.4. Regarding the geometry of singular points of minimizers of
Santaló’s problem, we will prove the following theorem:
Theorem 1.3. If Γ is a convex surface in H3 that attains the minimal total mean
curvature with fixed surface area, then Γ has no 0-singular point. That is, for any point
p on Γ, the normal cone N (p, conv(Γ)) of the convex body conv(Γ) at p is 1-dimensional
or 2-dimensional; p is either a 1-singular point or a regular point.

   Theorem 1.3 shows that even if the minimizer of Santaló’s problem is non-smooth
and singular, it cannot have points that behave like the vertex of a cone or the vertex
of a triangular pyramid. This theorem can help to rule out some convex surfaces as
minimizers of Santaló’s problem, such as polytopes or cones in H3 .
   The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we list some notations
and facts about the known examples, and review the background in convex geometry.
In Section 3, we will prove Proposition 1.1. In Section 4, we will construct the new
family of convex bodies whose total mean curvature is strictly less than those of the
geodesic sphere and the flat double disk with the same area, thereby forming a family
of counterexamples to (1.6) and proving Theorem 1.2. In Section 5, we will prove
Theorem 1.3 using a local argument based on estimating the total mean curvature of a
new convex body truncated by a family of planes at the singular point. We will discuss
open questions and further study directions related to Santaló’s problem at the end of
this paper.

                                   2. Preliminaries

   In this paper, any ambient manifold N is an (n+1)-dimensional space form. A convex
set X in N is a set whose convex hull conv(X) is itself. A convex hypersurface is the
boundary of a convex set. If the ambient manifold N has dimension dim(N ) = 3, then
we call the convex hypersurface a convex surface. A convex body is a bounded closed
convex set.
   When a hypersurface is C 2 , we have the following equivalent definition for convexity:
A convex hypersurface Γ of an ambient manifold N is a closed embedded submanifold
of codimension one which, when properly oriented, has positive semidefinite second
fundamental form IIΓ . A strictly convex hypersurface Γ of N is a convex hypersurface
with positive definite second fundamental form.

2.4. Convexity and Normal Cones. Here we review the definitions and facts about
the normal cone and singular (regular) points, together with basics about curvature
measures used in convex geometry. One may refer to [31] for definitions and facts in
Euclidean spaces. The counterparts in space forms are similar.
   A supporting hyperplane P of a closed set X is a totally geodesic hypersurface in N
such that X is entirely contained in one of the two closed half-spaces bounded by the
hyperplane P , and P ∩ ∂X ̸= ∅. We say that P is a supporting hyperplane of X at p,
if P is supporting hyperplane of X, and p ∈ P ∩ ∂X. If the ambient manifold N has
dimension dim(N ) = 3, then we call the supporting hyperplane a supporting plane.
   If K is a convex body, and p ∈ ∂K, then clearly there exists a supporting hyperplane

                       5. Proof of Theorem 1.3: Singularity property

   In this section, we will prove Theorem 1.3. One may refer to Section 2 for definitions
and facts about singular points, and integral geometric formulas for quermassintegrals.
   We will use a local argument. The idea is that if there exists a 0-singular point
on the minimizer surface Γ, then it forms a "vertex" of the whole convex body Ω =
conv(Γ). Hence we can shrink Ω infinitesimally by truncating it with a family of planes
at the "vertex". After the infinitesimal shrinking, we will then perform an infinitesimal
expansion using outward geodesic normal flow (outer parallel surface) to restore the area.
Finally, we construct a convex surface Γ′ with the same area as Γ, and we will prove
Γ′ has strictly less total mean curvature than Γ, which contradicts the assumption that
Γ is a minimizer. In the whole proof, we will use the Beltrami-Klein model to describe
the infinitesimal shrinking in Euclidean space.

Remark 5.4. Open Questions. Some further study directions to Santaló’s problem
will be listed here. Clearly, the question of greatest interest is:

Question 5.5. What is the shape of a minimizer to Santaló’s problem, that is, of a
convex surface in H3 that minimizes total mean curvature among convex surfaces with
fixed area?

   The explicit form of the minimizer may be difficult to find; however, we may ask the
following related questions to understand its geometry:

Question 5.6. Let Γ be a minimizer to Santaló’s problem then
     • Is Γ strictly convex?
     • Is Γ regular? (Here, regular means that at every point the supporting plane is
       unique.)
     • If Γ is regular, is it C 2 , or even C ∞ ?
     • Is Γ axially symmetric?

  By the arguments in Section 4, one can prove that the minimizing surface Γ to
Santaló’s problem with area S is contained in a geodesic ball B(g(S)) for some mono-
tonically increasing function g such that limS→0+ g(S) = 0. Hence when S is small
enough, Γ also lies in a small neighborhood of a point p in H3 , and, as S → 0+ , its
geometric quantities can be arbitrarily close to its Euclidean image in R3 , under the
Beltrami-Klein identity map Id defined in (2.3) (where we take the origin to be p). Thus
we may speculate when the area S is small enough, the minimizer is close to a geodesic
sphere, which is the minimizer to Minkowski Inequality (1.2) in R3 .
28                                            F. HONG

   When S is large enough, some mechanism we do not fully understand yet causes the
flat double disks, whose mean curvature is concentrated entirely on the "edge", to have
lower total mean curvature than spheres with the same area, and to become candidates
for minimizers to Santaló’s problem. This phenomenon is possibly connected with the
exponential growth of geometric quantities in hyperbolic spaces. As r → ∞, for a
geodesic sphere in H3 , its area and total mean curvature both grow at the rate of e2r ;
in contrast, for a geodesic sphere in R3 , its area grows at the rate of r2 , and total mean
curvature at the rate of r. This difference between hyperbolic and Euclidean geometry
makes Santaló’s problem more complicated. In view of the family of convex surfaces
constructed in Section 4, when the area S is large, one may speculate that the minimizer
has singular points, is therefore non-smooth, and is close to a flat double disk.

Question 5.7. Let Γ(S) be a minimizer to Santaló’s problem with surface area S. Then
     • If S → 0, will Γ(S), after proper rescaling, converge to a sphere?
     • If S → ∞, will Γ(S), after proper rescaling, converge to a flat double disk?
