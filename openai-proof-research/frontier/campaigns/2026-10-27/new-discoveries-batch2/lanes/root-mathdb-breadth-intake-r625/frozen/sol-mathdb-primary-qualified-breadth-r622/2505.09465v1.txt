Abstract.
We prove that the Euclidean Steinitz problem may be reduced to its restriction to “nearly unit” vectors at the cost of an additive O⁡(dlog⁡d)O\Big(\sqrt{\frac{d}{\log d}}\Big) error.
1. Framework and results
More than a century ago, Steinitz [32] proved the following unexpected result.
Theorem 1 (Euclidean Steinitz lemma).
Given any finite family of vectors V⊂ℝdV\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} of Euclidean norm at most 1 that sum to 0, one can order the elements of VV as v1,…,vnv_{1},\ldots,v_{n} so that for every k=1,…,n,k=1,\ldots,n,
(1)
|∑i=1kvi|≤C\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}\right|\leq C
where CC is a constant that depends only on the dimension dd.
Here and later on, |.||.| denotes the Euclidean norm on ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} whose unit ball is B2d={x∈ℝd:|x|≤1}B_{2}^{d}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}|x|\leq 1\}. More generally, BpdB_{p}^{d} will denote the unit ball of the ℓp\ell_{p}-norm on ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} for 1≤p≤∞1\leq p\leq\infty. Also, for a finite set of vectors VV, Σ⁡(V):=Σv∈V​v\Sigma(V)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\Sigma_{v\in V}v will stand for the sum of the elements of VV, and for n∈ℕ+n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}, we set [n]:={1,…,n}[n]\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{1,\ldots,n\}.
It is natural to ask for the smallest value of CC for which (1) is guaranteed to hold. We state the formal definition for general norms. Let B⊆ℝdB\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d} be a convex body, that is, a compact convex set with non-empty interior that satisfies 0∈int​B0\in\mathrm{int}\,B, where int\mathrm{int}\, stands for interior. The Minkowski norm generated by BB is defined as
∥x∥B:=inf{r≥0:x∈rB}\|x\|_{B}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\inf\{r\geq 0\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ x\in rB\}
(note that if BB is not symmetric about 00, then ∥.∥B\|.\|_{B} is only positively homogeneous).
Definition 2 (Steinitz constant).
Let B⊆ℝdB\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d} be a convex body with 0∈int​B0\in\mathrm{int}\,B. The Steinitz constant of BB, denoted S⁡(B)S(B), is the smallest number CC for which any finite set of vectors V⊂BV\subset B with Σ⁡(V)=0\Sigma(V)=0 has an ordering V={v1,…,vn}V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\} along which each partial sum has norm at most CC. That is, for every k∈[n]k\in[n],
(2)
‖∑i∈[k]vi‖B≤C.\Big\|\sum_{i\in[k]}v_{i}\Big\|_{B}\leq C.
The strongest general estimate on the Steinitz constants is as follows.
Theorem 3 (The Steinitz Lemma for general norms [32, 31, 17]).
For any convex body B⊆ℝdB\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d},
S⁡(B)≤d.S(B)\leq d.
This theorem is tight for non-symmetric convex bodies, as is shown by taking BB to be the regular simplex centered at the origin and choosing VV to be the set of its vertices, whereas it is asymptotically sharp for symmetric norms, as is shown by the ℓ1\ell_{1} case [17]: S⁡(B1d)≥(d+1)/2S(B_{1}^{d})\geq(d+1)/2. An example in the Euclidean case [17] shows that S⁡(B2d)≥d+3/2S(B_{2}^{d})\geq\sqrt{d+3}/2 must hold.
The following long-standing conjecture of Bergström [9] would yield a much stronger estimate in the Euclidean norm:
Conjecture 4.
For all d≥1d\geq 1, S⁡(B2d)=O⁡(d)S(B_{2}^{d})=O(\sqrt{d}).
We note that a matching bound is expected to hold for the maximum norm.
So far, Conjecture 4, known as the Steinitz problem, refuted all attempts. Our goal in this short note is to reduce the Euclidean Steinitz problem to its restriction to sets that consist of “nearly unit” vectors at the cost of an additive O⁡(dlog⁡d)O\Big(\sqrt{\frac{d}{\log d}}\Big) error. To that end, we introduce the following notion.
Definition 5 (ε\varepsilon-Steinitz constant).
For a fixed convex body B⊆ℝdB\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d} with 0∈int​B0\in\mathrm{int}\,B, and for 0≤ε≤10\leq\varepsilon\leq 1, the ε\varepsilon-Steinitz constant of BB, denoted Sε​(B)S_{\varepsilon}(B), is the smallest constant CC for which any finite set V⊂ℝdV\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} consisting of vectors of ∥.∥B\|.\|_{B}-norm in [1−ε,1][1-\varepsilon,1] may be ordered as V={v1,…,vn}V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\} so that
(3)
‖∑i∈[k]vi−kn​Σ​(V)‖B≤C\Big\|\sum_{i\in[k]}v_{i}-\frac{k}{n}\Sigma(V)\Big\|_{B}\leq C
holds for every k=1,…,nk=1,\ldots,n.
Note that condition Σ⁡(V)=0\Sigma(V)=0 of Definition 2 is omitted and, consequently, (2) is replaced by inequality (3). This does not change the order of magnitude of Sε​(B)S_{\varepsilon}(B), as replacing each vector v∈Vv\in V by v−1n​Σ​(V)v-\frac{1}{n}\Sigma(V) results in a family of vectors contained in 2​B2B that sum up to 0. Furthermore, the estimate of Theorem 3 also holds in this more general setting (see [17]).
Clearly, S0​(B2d)=S⁡(B2d)S_{0}(B_{2}^{d})=S(B_{2}^{d}), and Sε​(B2d)≤S⁡(B2d)S_{\varepsilon}(B_{2}^{d})\leq S(B_{2}^{d}) for every ε∈[0,1]\varepsilon\in[0,1]. Our main result establishes a reverse estimate.
Theorem 6.
For any 0<ε<10<\varepsilon<1 and all d≥2d\geq 2,
(4)
S⁡(B2d)≤1ε​(Sε​(B2d)+200​dlog⁡d).S(B_{2}^{d})\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big(S_{\varepsilon}(B_{2}^{d})+200\sqrt{\frac{d}{\log d}}\Big).
In particular, Conjecture 4 would follow from the estimate Sε​(B2d)=O⁡(d/log⁡d)S_{\varepsilon}(B_{2}^{d})=O(\sqrt{d/\log d}) for some ε=Ω⁡(1/log⁡d)\varepsilon=\Omega(1/\sqrt{\log d}).
2. History of the Steinitz Lemma
Despite being more than a century old, the story of the Steinitz lemma is still far from complete. In the following, we provide an overview that lists the main, and often forgotten, steps in its development.
Every mathematician knows the Riemann series theorem, dating more than 150 years ago [28], which states that given a conditionally convergent series, one can rearrange it to converge to any real number. In other words, for any real series, the set of all sums of its rearrangements is either empty (meaning that the series is divergent), a single point (the series is absolutely convergent), or the entire real line.
It is natural to ask for the extension of this result to series consisting of complex numbers and, even more generally, of vectors in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d}. In his very first article in 1905, at the age of 19, Lévy [26] addressed this question and announced the following result.
Theorem 7 (Lévy-Steinitz theorem).
Given a series of vectors in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d}, the set of all sums of its rearrangements is empty, or it forms an affine subspace of ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d}.
Recall that affine subspaces of ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} are of the form L+xL+x where L⊂ℝdL\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} is a linear subspace and x∈ℝdx\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.
Unfortunately, the proof of Lévy contained serious gaps for dimensions d≥3d\geq 3, as pointed out by Steinitz [32] in 1913. In turn, he gave the first complete proof of Theorem 7, known today as the Lévy-Steinitz theorem.
Steinitz’s work is quite technical and wide-scoped: it was published in three parts [32, 33, 34], with total length summing well over 100 pages. A key step of his proof is Theorem 1 (see [32, p.171]), which he stated with C=2​dC=2d.
Independently of Lévy and Steinitz, Gross [18] also found a shorter proof for Theorem 7 that is reminiscent of Steinitz’s method. His approach is again based on the rediscovered Steinitz lemma, yet it yields only the weaker constant S⁡(B2d)≤2d−1S(B_{2}^{d})\leq 2^{d}-1, which is an inevitable consequence of the induction dimension technique he applies. He also provides a geometric reformulation of Theorem 1: given any closed polygonal path in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} starting at the origin with side lengths not exceeding 1, it is possible to rearrange the order of the side segments so that the resulting polygonal path does not leave the ball of radius CC. This later led to the alternate title “polygonal confinement theorem” for Theorem 1 (cf. Rosenthal [29]).
Gross was not the last one to rediscover the Steinitz lemma. In 1931, Bergström published two papers on the topic. In the first [8], he gives an alternative proof for Theorem 7. The crux of his proof is again Theorem 1, which he considers to be of interest on its own. His proof again utilizes induction on the dimension and provides the estimate S⁡(B2d)=(4d−1)/3S(B_{2}^{d})=\sqrt{(4^{d}-1)/3}. His second article [9] concentrates solely on the Steinitz lemma. He proves that for d=2d=2, the optimal constant is S⁡(B22)=5/2S(B_{2}^{2})=\sqrt{5}/2, and formulates Conjecture 4.
In 1936, Hadwiger [19] became the first one to study the Steinitz lemma (which he attributes to Gross and Bergström) for series in general inner product spaces: he manages to bound the norm of partial sums in terms of the number of vectors nn. His attention then turned to the extension of the Lévy-Steinitz theorem to abstract Hilbert spaces [20] and finite-dimensional vector spaces [21].
Returning to the Euclidean case, Damsteeg and Halperin [14] showed that S⁡(B2d)≥12​d+6S(B_{2}^{d})\geq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{d+6} which implies that an O⁡(d)O(\sqrt{d}) bound as conjectured by Bergström would be asymptotically optimal (this is also shown by considering the vertex set of a centered regular simplex). Independently of [9], they also determined the exact value of S⁡(B22)S(B_{2}^{2}).
In 1954, Behrend entered the scene [6] and by a refinement of Steinitz’s original method strengthened the estimate to S⁡(B2d)<dS(B_{2}^{d})<d for every d≥3d\geq 3. He also showed that S⁡(B23)≤5+2​3S(B_{2}^{3})\leq\sqrt{5+2\sqrt{3}}.
Much of the above information had been blocked by the iron curtain. Although Theorem 1 is noted to be a ‘well-known lemma of Steinitz’ [17], Kadets [24] only rediscovered Bergström’s estimate S⁡(B2d)=(4d−1)/3S(B_{2}^{d})=\sqrt{(4^{d}-1)/3} in 1953. This led Belov and Stolin [7] to give a near-optimal algorithm for the flow shop problem. Twenty years later, by a linear algebraic approach, Sevast’yanov [31] re-proved the estimate S⁡(B2d)≤dS(B_{2}^{d})\leq d and showed that this bound holds for all symmetric norms on ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d}. This led to improved guarantees for the flow shop problem. His proof was further simplified and extended: in 1980, Grinberg and Sevast’yanov [17] gave an elegant, streamlined argument using linear dependencies, which yields the strongest general result, Theorem 3, for not necessarily symmetric norms.
Meanwhile in Hungary, independently of the work in the USSR, Fiala [15] rediscovered the connection between the Steinitz problem and the flow shop problem and re-proved the latter for the case d=2d=2. Inspired by his work, Bárány [4] proved the slightly weaker bound S⁡(B2d)≤3​d/2S(B_{2}^{d})\leq 3d/2, which in turn gives a much more efficient algorithm for the flow shop problem.
Besides estimates for the Euclidean case, many works study the question for other norms. The most important case is ℓ∞\ell_{\infty}: Similarly to Conjecture 4, S⁡(B∞d)=O⁡(d)S(B_{\infty}^{d})=O(\sqrt{d}) is expected. A lower bound of Ω⁡(d)\Omega(\sqrt{d}) was shown using a construction of Hadamard matrices [10], but, similarly to the Euclidean case, no o⁡(d)o(d) bound is known. The case d=2d=2 was studied by Bárány and Grinberg [11], who proved that S⁡(B∞2)≤CS(B_{\infty}^{2})\leq C for a universal constant C≥0C\geq 0, with the further condition that the first coordinates of each partial sum lie in [−1,1][-1,1]. Banaszyczyk completely classified this case by proving that S⁡(B∞2)=3/2S(B_{\infty}^{2})=3/2 [3], and he also showed [1] that the Steinitz constant of 2-dimensional normed planes does not exceed 3/23/2. Furthermore, he gave [2] an alternative proof for the estimate S⁡(B22)≤5/2S(B_{2}^{2})\leq\sqrt{5}/2 that was established by Bergström half a century ago. Banaszczyk and Sevast’yanov showed a constructive bound of O⁡(d2)O(d^{2}) and a non-constructive bound of O⁡(d3/2)O(d^{3/2}) for S⁡(B∞d)S(B_{\infty}^{d}) using techniques specific to the maximum norm [30].
Still in the 1980s, Halperin [22] applied a variation of Lévy’s method to obtain an elementary proof of Theorem 7. In the course of the proof he uses the Grinberg-Sevastyanov variant of the Steinitz lemma, called here as the ‘Polygon Rearrangement Theorem’. He also studies the question in LpL_{p} and ℓp\ell_{p} spaces for 0<p≤∞0<p\leq\infty. In an expository article, Rosenthal [29] presented the Lévy-Steinitz theorem along the Gross-Steinitz approach. He cites the Steinitz lemma as the ‘Polygonal Confinement Theorem’ with the weak bound S⁡(B2d)≤O⁡(2d)S(B_{2}^{d})\leq O(2^{d}), apparently unaware of its stronger forms.
A possible approach for attacking Conjecture 4 is via Chobanyan’s transference theorem, which gives an explicit connection between the Steinitz constant and the sign-sequence constant E⁡(B)E(B), where one is asked to assign signs to vectors of a (potentially infinite) sequence of vectors selected from BB so that all partial sums are bounded by E⁡(B)E(B). Chobanyan’s result [13, 12] shows that S⁡(B)≤E⁡(B)S(B)\leq E(B) [12]. For further information about the sign-sequence constant, see the survey article of Bárány [10].
This brief summary is too short for listing further related topics in detail, such as various extensions of the Lévy-Steinitz theorem to infinite-dimensional spaces (see e.g. [25]), or colorful versions of the Steinitz lemma (see [27] and [5]). Questions on vector sums have a vast literature: the bibliography of Halperin and Ando [23] lists 257 works up to 1988, with at least a hundred related articles having been published since then.
