Abstract
The isolation game is played on a graph GG by two players who take turns playing a vertex such that if XX is the set of already played vertices, then a vertex can be selected only if it dominates a vertex from a nontrivial component of G∖NG​[X]G\setminus N_{G}[X], where NG​[X]N_{G}[X] is the set of vertices in XX or adjacent to a vertex in XX. Dominator wishes to finish the game with the minimum number of played vertices, while Staller has the opposite goal. The game isolation number ιg​(G)\iota_{\rm g}(G) is the number of moves in the Dominator-start game where both players play optimally. If Staller starts the game the invariant is denoted by ιg′​(G)\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G). In this paper, ιg​(Cn)\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n}), ιg​(Pn)\iota_{\rm g}(P_{n}), ιg′​(Cn)\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(C_{n}), and ιg′​(Pn)\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(P_{n}) are determined for all nn. It is proved that there are only two graphs that attain equality in the upper bound ιg​(G)≤12​|V⁡(G)|\iota_{\rm g}(G)\leq\frac{1}{2}|V(G)|, and that there are precisely eleven graphs which attain equality in the upper bound ιg′​(G)≤12​|V⁡(G)|\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G)\leq\frac{1}{2}|V(G)|. For trees TT of order at least three it is proved that ιg​(T)≤511​|V⁡(T)|\iota_{\rm g}(T)\leq\frac{5}{11}|V(T)|. A new infinite family of graphs GG is also constructed for which ιg​(G)=ιg′​(G)=37​|V⁡(G)|\iota_{\rm g}(G)=\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G)=\frac{3}{7}|V(G)| holds.

1 Introduction
Let G=(V⁡(G),E⁡(G))G=(V(G),E(G)) be a graph and let X⊆V⁡(G)X\subseteq V(G). The closed neighborhood of XX is defined as NG[X]=∪x∈XNG[x]N_{G}[X]=\cup_{x\in X}N_{G}[x], where NG​[x]N_{G}[x] is the closed neighborhood of xx in GG. We say that XX
is an isolating set of GG if V​(G)∖NG​[X]V(G)\setminus N_{G}[X] is an independent set in GG. The minimum cardinality among all isolating sets in GG is the isolation number ι⁡(G)\iota(G) of GG. The concept was introduced (in a more general setting) in 2017 by Caro and Hansberg (2017). For an insight into current developments in the field, we recommend the following articles Borg (2025); Boyer and Goddard (2024); Boyer and Goddard (2025); Boyer et al. (2025); Chen et al. (2025); Lemańska et al. (2024); Zhang and Wu (2024a); Zhang and Wu (2024b) and references therein.
The domination game was introduced by Brešar et al. (2010). It was later extensively investigated, the summary of these researches up to 2021 is collected in the book of Brešar et al. (2021). Recent developments on the domination game include the papers by Bujtás et al. (2022); James and Vijayakumar (2023); Portier and Versteegen (2024); Versteegen (2024).
Moreover, several variations of the domination game were also investigated, notably the total domination game by Henning et al. (2015), the fractional domination game by Bujtás and Tuza (2019), the connected domination game by Borowiecki et al. (2019), two variants of the paired-domination game by Gray and Henning (2023); Haynes and Henning (2019), the competition-independence game of Phillips and Slater (2001), and the indicated domination game of Brešar et al. (2024).
More recently, Brešar et al. (2026) introduced a game counterpart of the isolation number following the ideas of the classical domination game (Brešar et al. (2010)). The isolation game is played on a graph GG by Dominator and Staller, who take turns selecting/playing a vertex from GG while obeying the rule that if XX is the set of already played vertices, then a vertex can be selected only if it dominates a vertex that belongs to a nontrivial component of the graph G−NG​[X]G-N_{G}[X]. Such a vertex xx is playable, and a move selecting xx is legal. The game ends when no playable vertex exists. When the game ends, the set of vertices selected forms an isolating set of GG. As in the domination game,
Dominator wishes to finish the game by playing the fewest number of vertices, while Staller wishes to delay the process as much as possible. Thus, Dominator seeks to minimize the size of the chosen set while Staller tries to make it as large as possible. If Dominator starts the game, we speak of a D-game, otherwise it is an S-game. The game isolation number, ιg​(G)\iota_{\rm g}(G), and the Staller-start game isolation number, ιg′​(G)\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G), are the number of vertices selected in the D-game and the S-game, respectively, provided that both players play optimally.
As mentioned above, the isolation number of a graph was introduced in a more general frame, the same holds for the isolation game defined by Brešar et al. (2026). A few results, such as the Continuation Principle and the fact that for each graph GG we have |ιg​(G)−ιg′​(G)|≤1|\iota_{\rm g}(G)-\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G)|\leq 1, have been proved by Brešar et al. (2026) for the general situation, while a special focus has been given on the isolation game. It was proved that ιg​(G)\iota_{\rm g}(G) is at most half the order for any graph GG and conjectured that the actual sharp upper bound is the ceiling of three-sevenths of the order. The isolation number of paths PnP_{n} was also determined for the cases when n≡imod5n\equiv i\bmod 5, i∈{1,2,3}i\in\{1,2,3\}.
In Section 2, we determine the exact values for the (Staller-start) game isolation number for paths and cycles. Section 3 focuses on graphs whose game isolation number equals one half of their order. We first show that K2K_{2} and C6C_{6} are the only graphs that attain the bound ιg​(G)≤12​|V⁡(G)|\iota_{\rm g}(G)\leq\frac{1}{2}|V(G)|. We then prove that ιg′​(G)≤12​|V⁡(G)|\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G)\leq\frac{1}{2}|V(G)| holds for every graph GG and identify all connected graphs for which equality holds. There are exactly eleven such graphs, all with order at most 10. In Section 4, we show that the 1/21/2-upper bound on the game isolation number in general graphs can be improved to 5/115/11 for trees. Regarding the 3/73/7-conjecture posed by Brešar et al. (2026), we present in Section 5 a new infinite family of graphs with minimum degree at least 2 that achieve this bound.

3 Graphs that attain the 1/2-bound
It was proved by Brešar et al. (2026) that the game isolation number of a graph is at most one-half its order. We characterize the graphs achieving equality in this upper bound on the game isolation number. For this purpose, we follow the proof technique employed in Brešar et al. (2026), with the modification that we adopt a greedy strategy for Dominator.
For completeness, we include the key strategy of the proof in Brešar et al. (2026). During the course of the game, when SS is the set of selected vertices, a vertex vv is considered marked if v∈NG​[S]v\in N_{G}[S] or if all neighbors of vv are in NG​[S]N_{G}[S]. Note that a marked vertex may or may not be playable and a vertex is not playable if and only if its entire closed neighborhood is marked.
Theorem 3.1.
If GG is a connected graph of order nn, then ιg​(G)≤12​n\iota_{\rm g}(G)\leq\frac{1}{2}n, with equality if and only if G∈{K2,C6}G\in\{K_{2},C_{6}\}.
Proof.
Let GG be a connected graph of order nn. If n=1n=1, then ιg​(G)=0\iota_{\rm g}(G)=0, while if n=2n=2, then G=K2G=K_{2} and ιg​(G)=1=12​n\iota_{\rm g}(G)=1=\frac{1}{2}n. Hence we may assume that n≥3n\geq 3, for otherwise the desired result is immediate. Following the proof strategy employed in Brešar et al. (2026), we weight the vertices in the graph GG as follows. At the beginning all vertices are unmarked and each vertex has weight 11. During the game, when a vertex is marked its weight drops to 00. The weight w⁡(G){\rm w}(G) of GG is the sum of weights of all vertices of GG. Thus at the beginning of the game w⁡(G)=n{\rm w}(G)=n and when the game ends the weight is zero. Let MM be the set of marked vertices in some stage of the game.
We adopt a refinement of Dominator’s strategy as given in Brešar et al. (2026) in that Dominator now adopts a greedy strategy and plays a vertex that results in a maximum weight decrease. At the start of the game, Dominator therefore plays a vertex of maximum degree Δ⁡(G)\Delta(G), thereby marking at least Δ⁡(G)+1\Delta(G)+1 vertices and decreasing the weight by at least Δ⁡(G)+1\Delta(G)+1 on his first move. On each of his subsequent moves, if there exists a component CC of G−MG-M of order at least 33, then Dominator can always decrease the weight by at least 33 by playing a vertex of degree at least 22 in CC. Every move of Staller decreases the weight of GG by at least 11 since she must dominate at least one (unmarked) vertex in G−MG-M.
In the final stage of the game when every component in G−MG-M is a K2K_{2}-component, every move decreases the weight by at least 22. By playing a vertex in a K2K_{2}-component of G−MG-M, we note that Staller can play a move that decreases the weight by exactly 22. However according to Dominator’s greedy strategy, he plays a marked vertex that is adjacent to the maximum number of K2K_{2}-components in G−MG-M, thereby decreasing the weight by at least 22.
In summary, Dominator’s greedy strategy implies all his moves decrease the weight by at least 33, except possibly in the final stage of the game when every component in G−MG-M is a K2K_{2}-component, in which case all his subsequent moves decrease the weight by at least 22 and all subsequent moves of Staller decrease the weight by exactly 22. Moreover if at least one component of G−MG-M is of order at least 33, then Staller’s move decreases the weight of GG by at least 11. Hence every move of Dominator decreases the weight by at least 22, while each of Dominator’s move together with Staller’s reply to his move decrease the weight by at least 44. Therefore on average every played move decreases the weight by at least 22, implying that the game finishes after at most 12​n\frac{1}{2}n moves.
By our earlier assumption, recall that n≥3n\geq 3, and so Δ⁡(G)≥2\Delta(G)\geq 2. Suppose that Δ⁡(G)≥3\Delta(G)\geq 3. In this case, Dominator’s first move decreases the weight by at least 44. Thereafter, Dominator’s greedy strategy guarantees that at most 12​(n−5)\frac{1}{2}(n-5) additional moves are needed to finish the game. Hence in this case the game finishes after at most 2+12​(n−5)=12​(n−1)2+\frac{1}{2}(n-5)=\frac{1}{2}(n-1) moves, and so ιg​(G)<12​n\iota_{\rm g}(G)<\frac{1}{2}n.
Hence we may assume that Δ⁡(G)=2\Delta(G)=2. Thus, GG is a path PnP_{n} or a cycle CnC_{n}. Suppose that G=PnG=P_{n} (and n≥3n\geq 3). Suppose finally that G=CnG=C_{n}. If n∈{3,4}n\in\{3,4\}, then ιg​(G)=1<12​n\iota_{\rm g}(G)=1<\frac{1}{2}n. If n=5n=5, then ιg​(G)=2<12​n\iota_{\rm g}(G)=2<\frac{1}{2}n. If n=6n=6, then ιg​(G)=3=12​n\iota_{\rm g}(G)=3=\frac{1}{2}n. Hence we may assume that n≥7n\geq 7. By Theorem 2.3, we now infer the following. If n≡0mod5n\equiv 0\bmod{5}, then ιg​(Cn)=25​n<12​n.\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})=\frac{2}{5}n<\frac{1}{2}n. If n≡1mod5n\equiv 1\bmod{5}, then ιg​(Cn)=25​(n+4)−1<12​n.\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})=\frac{2}{5}(n+4)-1<\frac{1}{2}n. If n≡2mod5n\equiv 2\bmod{5}, then ιg​(Cn)=25​(n+3)−1<12​n.\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})=\frac{2}{5}(n+3)-1<\frac{1}{2}n. If n≡3mod5n\equiv 3\bmod{5}, then ιg​(Cn)=25​(n+2)−1<12​n.\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})=\frac{2}{5}(n+2)-1<\frac{1}{2}n. If n≡4mod5n\equiv 4\bmod{5}, then ιg​(Cn)=25​(n+1)−1<12​n.\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})=\frac{2}{5}(n+1)-1<\frac{1}{2}n. In all cases, when n≥7n\geq 7 we have ιg​(Cn)<12​n\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})<\frac{1}{2}n. 4 The isolation game played in trees
As a consequence of Theorem 3.1, we have the following upper bound on the game isolation number of a tree.
Corollary 4.1.
If TT is a tree of order nn, then ιg​(T)≤12​n\iota_{\rm g}(T)\leq\frac{1}{2}n, with equality if and only if T=K2T=K_{2}.
In this section, we improve the 12​n\frac{1}{2}n-upper bound in Corollary 4.1 to a 511​n\frac{5}{11}n-upper bound when TT is a tree of order n≥3n\geq 3. For this purpose, we use a proof technique from Goddard and Henning (2025) used to establish upper bounds on the isolation number of a graph. The technique uses an isolation residual graph defined as follows.
Given a graph GG and a subset S⊆V⁡(G)S\subseteq V(G), the isolation residual graph denoted by GSιG_{S}^{\iota} is obtained as follows. Its vertex set is V⁡(G)V(G). Further:
1. 
A vertex in G−N⁡[S]G-N[S] with a neighbor in G−N⁡[S]G-N[S] is colored white.
2. 
A vertex in N⁡[S]N[S] with a white neighbor is colored blue.
3. 
All other vertices are colored red.
4. 
The edge set of GSιG_{S}^{\iota} consists of all edges of GG that are incident with at least one white vertex.
As remarked in Goddard and Henning (2025), the white vertices still need to be isolated, the blue vertices might be useful to do this isolating, and the red vertices are no longer relevant. We denote the set of red vertices by RR, the set of blue vertices by BB, and the set of white vertices by WW. We observe that these sets form a (weak) partition of V⁡(G)V(G). We shall need the following elementary properties of a residual graph.
Observation 4.2.
The following holds in the isolation residual graph GSιG_{S}^{\iota}.
1. 
Every vertex of SS is colored red.
2. 
Every red vertex is isolated, but no white nor blue vertex is isolated.
3. 
The degree of a white vertex in GSιG_{S}^{\iota} equals its degree in GG.
4. 
The set SS is an isolating set in GG if and only if there is no white vertex.
We are now in a position to prove the following result on the game isolation number of a tree.
Theorem 4.3.
If TT is a tree of order n≥3n\geq 3, then ιg​(T)≤511​n.\iota_{\rm g}(T)\leq\frac{5}{11}n.
Proof.
For a set S⊆V⁡(T)S\subseteq V(T), consider the isolation residual graph TSιT_{S}^{\iota} and the sets of white, blue, and red vertices in TSιT_{S}^{\iota}. We define the weight function
w⁡(TSι)=5​|W|+3​|B|.{\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota})=5|W|+3|B|.
Equivalently, we associate every white, blue, and red vertex with a weight of 55, 33, and 00, respectively, and then consider the sum of the weights in GSιG_{S}^{\iota}.
When the game starts, S=∅S=\emptyset and w⁡(T∅ι)=5​n{\rm w}(T_{\emptyset}^{\iota})=5n, while w⁡(TSι)=0{\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota})=0 holds when the game is over. Further, if vv is a playable vertex in the isolation residual graph TSιT_{S}^{\iota}, then w⁡(TSι)≥w⁡(TS∪{v}ι){\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota})\geq{\rm w}(T_{S\cup\{v\}}^{\iota}) and we use the notation ξ⁡(v)=w⁡(TSι)−w⁡(TS∪{v}).\xi(v)={\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota})-{\rm w}(T_{S\cup\{v\}}). We will show that Dominator can ensure that w⁡(TSι){\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota}) decreases by at least 2222 in every two consecutive moves of Dominator and Staller. To prove this, we consider three stages in the D-game. In the graph TSι​[W]T_{S}^{\iota}[W], induced by the white vertices of TSιT_{S}^{\iota}, a big component is a component of order at least 44. If a component is not big, it is called small. If TT is a tree, a small component of TSι​[W]T_{S}^{\iota}[W] is either a P2P_{2}- or a P3P_{3}-subgraph.
Stage 11.
A move of Dominator belongs to Stage 11 if TSι​[W]T_{S}^{\iota}[W] contains a big component before this move. A move of Staller belongs to Stage 11, if so does Dominator’s previous move.
We first state that every legal move decreases w⁡(TSι){\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota}) by at least 55. Indeed, if a white vertex vv is played, it becomes red and ξ⁡(v)≥5\xi(v)\geq 5. If a blue vertex uu is played that has a white neighbor u′u^{\prime}, then uu becomes red and u′u^{\prime} becomes blue or red, which gives ξ⁡(u)≥3+(5−3)=5.\xi(u)\geq 3+(5-3)=5. In particular, each move of Staller decreases the weight of TSιT_{S}^{\iota} by at least 55.
Suppose that Dominator plays in TSιT_{S}^{\iota} in Stage 11. The induced subgraph TSι​[W]T_{S}^{\iota}[W] then contains a component TiT_{i} on at least four vertices. If TiT_{i} is a star K1,kK_{1,k} for a k≥3k\geq 3, then Dominator chooses the center vv. Since all vertices in the star become red, ξ⁡(v)≥5​(k+1)≥20.\xi(v)\geq 5(k+1)\geq 20. If TiT_{i} is not a star, it contains a diametrical path v1​…​vdv_{1}\dots v_{d} with d≥4d\geq 4. Then P:v1​v2​v3​v4P\colon v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}v_{4} is a 44-path such that v1v_{1} is a leaf in TiT_{i} and if v2v_{2} has a neighbor v′∉V⁡(P)v^{\prime}\notin V(P), then v′v^{\prime} is a leaf in TiT_{i}.
Dominator now plays v3v_{3}. With this move v1v_{1}, v2v_{2}, v3v_{3} (more generally, NTi​[v2]N_{T_{i}}[v_{2}]) become red and v4v_{4} is colored blue or red. Consequently, ξ⁡(v3)≥3×5+(5−3)=17.\xi(v_{3})\geq 3\times 5+(5-3)=17. This shows that Dominator can always decrease w⁡(TSι){\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota}) by at least 1717, when the move belongs to Stage 11. We may conclude that Dominator has a strategy to ensure that the weight decreases by at least 17+5=2217+5=22 in every two consecutive moves in Stage 11.
Before discussing Stage 22, we introduce a new vertex type in the isolation residual graph TSιT_{S}^{\iota}. We say that a vertex uu is light blue, and belongs to BℓB^{\ell}, if it became blue during Stage 22. Light blue vertices are associated with a weight of 11; that is, the new weight function we use in Stages 22 and 33 is
w⁡(TSι)=5​|W|+3​|B∖Bℓ|+|Bℓ|.{\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota})=5|W|+3|B\setminus B^{\ell}|+|B^{\ell}|.
Note that all light blue vertices are of degree 11. Further, we say that a component TiT_{i} of TSι​[W]T_{S}^{\iota}[W] is special, if it is a P2P_{2}-component and each adjacent blue (or light blue) vertex is of degree 11 in TSιT_{S}^{\iota}.
Stage 2.
A move of Dominator belongs to Stage 22, if the subgraph TSι​[W]T_{S}^{\iota}[W] in TSιT_{S}^{\iota} before this move consists only of small components and not all of them are special. A move of Staller belongs to Stage 22, if so does Dominator’s previous move.
We first prove that every move vv of Staller decreases w⁡(TSι){\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota}) by at least 77 in Stage 22. If vv is a white vertex, then an entire P2P_{2}- or P3P_{3}-component of G⁡[W]G[W] becomes red and ξ⁡(v)≥2×5=10.\xi(v)\geq 2\times 5=10. The same is true if v∈Bv\in B and a white neighbor v′v^{\prime} belongs to a P2P_{2}-component. Assume v∈Bv\in B and that a neighbor v′v^{\prime} of vv is from a P3P_{3}-component of TSι​[W]T_{S}^{\iota}[W]. We remark that in this case v∈B∖Bℓv\in B\setminus B^{\ell} because there were no components in TSι​[W]T_{S}^{\iota}[W] on more than three vertices at the beginning of Stage 22, and so vv was not white at that time.
If v′v^{\prime} is the central vertex of the P3P_{3}-component, then v′v^{\prime} and the entire P3P_{3}-component becomes red with this move and we have ξ⁡(v)≥3+3×5=18.\xi(v)\geq 3+3\times 5=18. If v′v^{\prime} is a leaf in the P3P_{3}-component, then vv becomes red and v′v^{\prime} becomes light blue with this move. Therefore, ξ⁡(v)≥3+(5−1)=7,\xi(v)\geq 3+(5-1)=7, completing the proof of the statement.
Consider now Dominator’s move. If a P3P_{3}-component is present in TSι​[W]T_{S}^{\iota}[W], he can choose a vertex vv from such a component TiT_{i}. All three white vertices of TiT_{i} become red, which results in ξ⁡(v)≥3×5=15.\xi(v)\geq 3\times 5=15. If there is no P3P_{3}-component, then a non-special P2P_{2}-component exists. Dominator may choose a blue vertex uu that has neighbors u1u_{1} and u2u_{2} from two different P2P_{2}-components. Playing uu, both components and uu itself become red and ξ⁡(u)≥4×5+3=23.\xi(u)\geq 4\times 5+3=23. We may conclude that Dominator is always able to decrease w⁡(TSι){\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota}) by at least 1515 with a move in Stage 22 and therefore, every two consecutive moves together decrease the weight of the isolation residual graph by at least 7+15=227+15=22.
Stage 33.
Stage 33 starts when all components of TSι​[W]T_{S}^{\iota}[W] are special and ends when the game is over.
Each move of a player removes a P2P_{2}-component from TSιT_{S}^{\iota} by recoloring both vertices with red. Further, by Observation 4.2(c) and the condition n≥3n\geq 3 in the theorem, each special component is adjacent to a blue vertex of degree 11, which also becomes red together with the component. We infer that w⁡(TSι){\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota}) decreases by at least 2×5+1=112\times 5+1=11 with each move played in Stage 33, no matter whether the move is played by Dominator or by Staller.
By the analysis of the stages of the game when Dominator follows the described strategy, we obtained that, on average, each move decreases w⁡(TSι){\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota}) by at least 1111. Let tt denote the number of vertices played in the game. As the game started with w⁡(T∅ι)=5​n{\rm w}(T_{\emptyset}^{\iota})=5n and finished with w⁡(TSι)=0{\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota})=0, we conclude 11​t≤5​n11t\leq 5n and then, ιg​(T)≤t≤511​n\iota_{\rm g}(T)\leq t\leq\frac{5}{11}n follows.
∎
5 On the 3/7-bound
Brešar et al. (2026) posed the following conjecture.
Conjecture 5.1.
For any graph GG with no K2K_{2}-components,
ιg​(G)≤⌈37​n​(G)⌉​ and ​ιg′​(G)≤⌈37​n​(G)⌉.\iota_{\rm g}(G)\leq\left\lceil\frac{3}{7}n(G)\right\rceil\text{ and }\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G)\leq\left\lceil\frac{3}{7}n(G)\right\rceil.
If the conjecture holds true, then it is sharp as the following example from Brešar et al. (2026) demonstrates. Start with an arbitrary graph GG and attach to each vertex of it two disjoint paths of length three. Besides this family of graphs and graphs similar to them (say, obtained from such a graph by removing a pendant vertex), the authors could not find any other infinite family of graphs whose game isolation number is close to 3​n​(G)/73n(G)/7. Here, we present another such family.
If GG is a graph, then let G^\widehat{G} be a graph defined as follows. For any vertex v∈V⁡(G)v\in V(G), add two disjoint triangles TvT_{v} and Tv′T_{v}^{\prime}, and add an edge between vv and a vertex of TvT_{v} and an edge between vv and a vertex of Tv′T_{v}^{\prime}, see Fig. 2.
GGvvTvT_{v}Tv′T_{v}^{\prime}GvG_{v}
Figure 2: Graph G^\widehat{G}
Proposition 5.2.
If GG is a graph, then
ιg​(G^)=ιg′​(G^)=37​n​(G^).\iota_{\rm g}(\widehat{G})=\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(\widehat{G})=\frac{3}{7}n(\widehat{G})\,.
Proof.
For a vertex v∈V⁡(G)v\in V(G), let GvG_{v} be the subgraph of G^\widehat{G} induced by {v}∪V⁡(Tv)∪V⁡(Tv′),\{v\}\cup V(T_{v})\cup V(T_{v}^{\prime}), see Fig. 2 again.
Consider the following strategy of Dominator that guarantees that not more than three vertices are played in each GvG_{v}. Assume that the last move of Staller is a vertex from GvG_{v}, for some v∈V⁡(G)v\in V(G). If there are vertices of GvG_{v} which remain playable, then Dominator plays a vertex of GvG_{v} such that no more than three vertices from GvG_{v} will be played when the game finishes. He can achieve this goal since if Staller played vv, then Dominator selects a vertex from TvT_{v} or a vertex from Tv′T_{v}^{\prime}, and if Staller selects some vertex from V⁡(Tv)∪V⁡(Tv′)V(T_{v})\cup V(T_{v}^{\prime}), then Dominator replies by playing vv if possible, otherwise a vertex from the triangle where no vertex has been played yet. Note that Dominator can also follow this strategy for his first move in the D-game, that is, he selects some vertex v∈V⁡(G)⊆V⁡(G^)v\in V(G)\subseteq V(\widehat{G}). On the other hand, if no vertex of GvG_{v} remains playable after the last move of Staller, Dominator applies analogous strategy in some GuG_{u}, u≠vu\neq v, where there still exist playable vertices. Note that this strategy of Dominator can be applied no matter whether the D-game or the S-game is played. We can conclude that ιg​(G^)≤37​n​(G^)\iota_{\rm g}(\widehat{G})\leq\frac{3}{7}n(\widehat{G}) as well as ιg′​(G^)≤37​n​(G^).\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(\widehat{G})\leq\frac{3}{7}n(\widehat{G}).
Consider now the following strategy of Staller. Her goal is that in each GvG_{v}, the vertex vv is selected during the game, either by Dominator or by Staller. So assume that the last move of Dominator is a vertex from GvG_{v}, for some v∈V⁡(G)v\in V(G), and that no other vertex of GvG_{v} has yet been played. If Dominator selected vv, then Staller replies by selecting a neighbor of vv in TvT_{v}. On the other hand, if Dominator selected a vertex different from vv, then Staller selects vv. In either case, this strategy of Staller guarantees that by the end of the game, three vertices from GvG_{v} are selected. In addition, if Staller is forced to play first on some GvG_{v}, then she selects vv, thus forcing again that three vertices from GvG_{v} will be played. We can conclude that ιg​(G^)≥37​n​(G^)\iota_{\rm g}(\widehat{G})\geq\frac{3}{7}n(\widehat{G}) as well as ιg′​(G^)≥37​n​(G^),\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(\widehat{G})\geq\frac{3}{7}n(\widehat{G}), and we are done.
∎
Both the previously known extremal family (based on attaching two paths of length three) and our construction (based on attaching two triangles) share a common structural feature. Each vertex of the original graph is extended into a local configuration on seven vertices that requires at least three vertices in any optimal isolating set. Consequently, these constructions can be viewed as decomposing the graph into disjoint 7-vertex gadgets contributing exactly 3 to the game isolation number, which explains the emergence of the 37\frac{3}{7} ratio. This suggests that the conjectured upper bound is driven by such local configurations, rather than global properties of the graph, and that extremal graphs may be characterized by the presence of these forcing gadgets.
We conclude the paper with the following slight refinements of Conjecture 5.1.
Conjecture 5.3.
If GG is a graph of order nn with δ⁡(G)≥2\delta(G)\geq 2, then ιg​(G)≤37​n\iota_{\rm g}(G)\leq\frac{3}{7}n and ιg′​(G)≤37​n\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G)\leq\frac{3}{7}n.
If Conjecture 5.3 is correct, then we have seen above that it is tight and achievable for connected graphs GG of arbitrarily large order.
Conjecture 5.4.
If TT is a tree of order n≥3n\geq 3, then ιg​(T)≤37​n\iota_{\rm g}(T)\leq\frac{3}{7}n.
