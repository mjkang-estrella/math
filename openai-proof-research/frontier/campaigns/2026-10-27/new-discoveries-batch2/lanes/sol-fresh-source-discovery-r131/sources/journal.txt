                                           1        Introduction
                                           Let G = (V (G), E(G)) be a graph and let X ⊆ V (G). The closed neighborhood of X is defined as
                                           NG [X] = ∪x∈X NG [x], where NG [x] is the closed neighborhood of x in G. We say that X is an isolating
                                           set of G if V (G) \ NG [X] is an independent set in G. The minimum cardinality among all isolating sets
                                           in G is the isolation number ι(G) of G. The concept was introduced (in a more general setting) in 2017
                                           by Caro and Hansberg (2017). For an insight into current developments in the field, we recommend the
                                           following articles Borg (2025); Boyer and Goddard (2024, 2025); Boyer et al. (2025); Chen et al. (2025);
                                           Lemańska et al. (2024); Zhang and Wu (2024a,b) and references therein.

    The domination game was introduced by Brešar et al. (2010). It was later extensively investigated,
the summary of these researches up to 2021 is collected in the book of Brešar et al. (2021). Recent
developments on the domination game include the papers by Bujtás et al. (2022); James and Vijayakumar
(2023); Portier and Versteegen (2024); Versteegen (2024).
    Moreover, several variations of the domination game were also investigated, notably the total domina-
tion game by Henning et al. (2015), the fractional domination game by Bujtás and Tuza (2019), the con-
nected domination game by Borowiecki et al. (2019), two variants of the paired-domination game by Gray
and Henning (2023); Haynes and Henning (2019), the competition-independence game of Phillips and
Slater (2001), and the indicated domination game of Brešar et al. (2024).
    More recently, Brešar et al. (2026) introduced a game counterpart of the isolation number following the
ideas of the classical domination game (Brešar et al. (2010)). The isolation game is played on a graph G
by Dominator and Staller, who take turns selecting/playing a vertex from G while obeying the rule that
if X is the set of already played vertices, then a vertex can be selected only if it dominates a vertex that
belongs to a nontrivial component of the graph G − NG [X]. Such a vertex x is playable, and a move
selecting x is legal. The game ends when no playable vertex exists. When the game ends, the set of
vertices selected forms an isolating set of G. As in the domination game, Dominator wishes to finish
the game by playing the fewest number of vertices, while Staller wishes to delay the process as much as
possible. Thus, Dominator seeks to minimize the size of the chosen set while Staller tries to make it as
large as possible. If Dominator starts the game, we speak of a D-game, otherwise it is an S-game. The
game isolation number, ιg (G), and the Staller-start game isolation number, ι′g (G), are the number of
vertices selected in the D-game and the S-game, respectively, provided that both players play optimally.
    As mentioned above, the isolation number of a graph was introduced in a more general frame, the same
holds for the isolation game defined by Brešar et al. (2026). A few results, such as the Continuation
Principle and the fact that for each graph G we have |ιg (G) − ι′g (G)| ≤ 1, have been proved by Brešar
et al. (2026) for the general situation, while a special focus has been given on the isolation game. It was
proved that ιg (G) is at most half the order for any graph G and conjectured that the actual sharp upper
bound is the ceiling of three-sevenths of the order. The isolation number of paths Pn was also determined
for the cases when n ≡ i mod 5, i ∈ {1, 2, 3}.
    In Section 2, we determine the exact values for the (Staller-start) game isolation number for paths and
cycles. Section 3 focuses on graphs whose game isolation number equals one half of their order. We first
show that K2 and C6 are the only graphs that attain the bound ιg (G) ≤ 12 |V (G)|. We then prove that
ι′g (G) ≤ 21 |V (G)| holds for every graph G and identify all connected graphs for which equality holds.
There are exactly eleven such graphs, all with order at most 10. In Section 4, we show that the 1/2-upper
bound on the game isolation number in general graphs can be improved to 5/11 for trees. Regarding the
3/7-conjecture posed by Brešar et al. (2026), we present in Section 5 a new infinite family of graphs with
minimum degree at least 2 that achieve this bound.

3     Graphs that attain the 1/2-bound
It was proved by Brešar et al. (2026) that the game isolation number of a graph is at most one-half its
order. We characterize the graphs achieving equality in this upper bound on the game isolation number.
For this purpose, we follow the proof technique employed in Brešar et al. (2026), with the modification
that we adopt a greedy strategy for Dominator.
   For completeness, we include the key strategy of the proof in Brešar et al. (2026). During the course
of the game, when S is the set of selected vertices, a vertex v is considered marked if v ∈ NG [S] or if all
neighbors of v are in NG [S]. Note that a marked vertex may or may not be playable and a vertex is not
playable if and only if its entire closed neighborhood is marked.
Theorem 3.1. If G is a connected graph of order n, then ιg (G) ≤ 12 n, with equality if and only if
G ∈ {K2 , C6 }.

Proof: Let G be a connected graph of order n. If n = 1, then ιg (G) = 0, while if n = 2, then G = K2
and ιg (G) = 1 = 21 n. Hence we may assume that n ≥ 3, for otherwise the desired result is immediate.
Following the proof strategy employed in Brešar et al. (2026), we weight the vertices in the graph G as
follows. At the beginning all vertices are unmarked and each vertex has weight 1. During the game, when
a vertex is marked its weight drops to 0. The weight w(G) of G is the sum of weights of all vertices of G.
Thus at the beginning of the game w(G) = n and when the game ends the weight is zero. Let M be the
set of marked vertices in some stage of the game.
   We adopt a refinement of Dominator’s strategy as given in Brešar et al. (2026) in that Dominator now
adopts a greedy strategy and plays a vertex that results in a maximum weight decrease. At the start of the
game, Dominator therefore plays a vertex of maximum degree ∆(G), thereby marking at least ∆(G) + 1
vertices and decreasing the weight by at least ∆(G) + 1 on his first move. On each of his subsequent
moves, if there exists a component C of G − M of order at least 3, then Dominator can always decrease
the weight by at least 3 by playing a vertex of degree at least 2 in C. Every move of Staller decreases the
weight of G by at least 1 since she must dominate at least one (unmarked) vertex in G − M .
   In the final stage of the game when every component in G − M is a K2 -component, every move
decreases the weight by at least 2. By playing a vertex in a K2 -component of G − M , we note that
Staller can play a move that decreases the weight by exactly 2. However according to Dominator’s greedy
strategy, he plays a marked vertex that is adjacent to the maximum number of K2 -components in G − M ,
thereby decreasing the weight by at least 2.

   In summary, Dominator’s greedy strategy implies all his moves decrease the weight by at least 3, except
possibly in the final stage of the game when every component in G−M is a K2 -component, in which case
all his subsequent moves decrease the weight by at least 2 and all subsequent moves of Staller decrease
the weight by exactly 2. Moreover if at least one component of G − M is of order at least 3, then Staller’s
move decreases the weight of G by at least 1. Hence every move of Dominator decreases the weight by at
least 2, while each of Dominator’s move together with Staller’s reply to his move decrease the weight by
at least 4. Therefore on average every played move decreases the weight by at least 2, implying that the
game finishes after at most 21 n moves.
   By our earlier assumption, recall that n ≥ 3, and so ∆(G) ≥ 2. Suppose that ∆(G) ≥ 3. In this case,
Dominator’s first move decreases the weight by at least 4. Since Staller’s move decreases the weight by
at least 1, after the first two moves are played at least five vertices are marked. Thereafter, Dominator’s
greedy strategy guarantees that at most 21 (n − 5) additional moves are needed to finish the game. Hence
in this case the game finishes after at most 2 + 12 (n − 5) = 12 (n − 1) moves, and so ιg (G) < 12 n.
   Hence we may assume that ∆(G) = 2. Thus, G is a path Pn or a cycle Cn . Suppose that G = Pn (and
n ≥ 3). Then by Theorem 2.5, we have ιg (G) ≤ 52 (n + 1) < 12 n. Hence if G = Pn and n ≥ 3, then
ιg (G) < 12 n.
   Suppose finally that G = Cn . If n ∈ {3, 4}, then ιg (G) = 1 < 12 n. If n = 5, then ιg (G) = 2 < 21 n.
If n = 6, then ιg (G) = 3 = 12 n. Hence we may assume that n ≥ 7. By Theorem 2.3, we now infer the
following. If n ≡ 0 mod 5, then ιg (Cn ) = 25 n < 12 n. If n ≡ 1 mod 5, then ιg (Cn ) = 52 (n+4)−1 < 12 n.
If n ≡ 2 mod 5, then ιg (Cn ) = 25 (n + 3) − 1 < 12 n. If n ≡ 3 mod 5, then ιg (Cn ) = 25 (n + 2) − 1 < 12 n.
If n ≡ 4 mod 5, then ιg (Cn ) = 25 (n + 1) − 1 < 12 n. In all cases, when n ≥ 7 we have ιg (Cn ) < 21 n.

Noting that ι′g (G) = 21 n = 4, we readily infer that G ∈ {F7 , F8 , F9 , F10 , F11 }. This completes the proof
of Theorem 3.2.

Remark 3.3. The inequalities of Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 hold true for arbitrary graphs, that is, they need
not to be connected. This can be checked by observing that in the proofs of the inequalities, connectivity
is not an issue.

4      The isolation game played in trees
As a consequence of Theorem 3.1, we have the following upper bound on the game isolation number of a
tree.
Corollary 4.1. If T is a tree of order n, then ιg (T ) ≤ 12 n, with equality if and only if T = K2 .
   In this section, we improve the 21 n-upper bound in Corollary 4.1 to a 11

tree of order n ≥ 3. For this purpose, we use a proof technique from Goddard and Henning (2025) used
to establish upper bounds on the isolation number of a graph. The technique uses an isolation residual
graph defined as follows.
   Given a graph G and a subset S ⊆ V (G), the isolation residual graph denoted by GιS is obtained as
follows. Its vertex set is V (G). Further:

     1. A vertex in G − N [S] with a neighbor in G − N [S] is colored white.

     2. A vertex in N [S] with a white neighbor is colored blue.

     3. All other vertices are colored red.

     4. The edge set of GιS consists of all edges of G that are incident with at least one white vertex.

   As remarked in Goddard and Henning (2025), the white vertices still need to be isolated, the blue
vertices might be useful to do this isolating, and the red vertices are no longer relevant. We denote the
set of red vertices by R, the set of blue vertices by B, and the set of white vertices by W . We observe
that these sets form a (weak) partition of V (G). We shall need the following elementary properties of a
residual graph.
Observation 4.2. The following holds in the isolation residual graph GιS .

     1. Every vertex of S is colored red.

     2. Every red vertex is isolated, but no white nor blue vertex is isolated.

     3. The degree of a white vertex in GιS equals its degree in G.

     4. The set S is an isolating set in G if and only if there is no white vertex.

    We are now in a position to prove the following result on the game isolation number of a tree.

   Both the previously known extremal family (based on attaching two paths of length three) and our con-
struction (based on attaching two triangles) share a common structural feature. Each vertex of the original
graph is extended into a local configuration on seven vertices that requires at least three vertices in any
optimal isolating set. Consequently, these constructions can be viewed as decomposing the graph into dis-
joint 7-vertex gadgets contributing exactly 3 to the game isolation number, which explains the emergence
of the 37 ratio. This suggests that the conjectured upper bound is driven by such local configurations,
rather than global properties of the graph, and that extremal graphs may be characterized by the presence
of these forcing gadgets.
