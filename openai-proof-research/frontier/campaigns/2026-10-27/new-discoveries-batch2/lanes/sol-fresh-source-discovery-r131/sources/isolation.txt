Boštjan Brešar

Abstract
Given a graph GG and a family of graphs ℱ\cal F, an ℱ\cal F-isolating set, as introduced by Caro and Hansberg, is any set S⊂V⁡(G)S\subset V(G) such that G−N⁡[S]G-N[S] contains no member of ℱ\cal F as a subgraph. In this paper, we introduce a game in which two players with opposite goals are together building an ℱ\cal F-isolating set in GG. Following the domination games, Dominator (Staller) wants that the resulting ℱ\cal F-isolating set obtained at the end of the game, is as small (as big) as possible, which leads to the graph invariant called the game ℱ\cal F-isolation number, denoted ιg​(G,ℱ)\iota_{\rm g}(G,\cal F). We prove that the Continuation Principle holds in the ℱ\cal F-isolation game, and that the difference between the game ℱ\cal F-isolation numbers when either Dominator or Staller starts the game is at most 11. Considering two arbitrary families of graphs ℱ\cal F and ℱ′\cal F^{\prime}, we find relations between them that ensure ιg​(G,ℱ′)≤ιg​(G,ℱ)\iota_{\rm g}(G,{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime})\leq\iota_{\rm g}(G,{\mathcal{F}}) for any graph GG. A special focus is given on the isolation game, which takes place when ℱ={K2}\mathcal{F}=\{K_{2}\}. We prove that ιg​(G,{K2})≤|V⁡(G)|/2\iota_{\rm g}(G,\{K_{2}\})\leq|V(G)|/2 for any graph GG, and conjecture that ⌈3​|V⁡(G)|/7⌉\lceil 3|V(G)|/7\rceil is the actual (sharp) upper bound. We prove that the isolation game on a forest when Dominator has the first move never lasts longer than the one in which Staller starts the game.
Finally, we prove good lower and upper bounds on the game isolation numbers of paths PnP_{n}, which lead to the exact values ιg​(Pn,{K2})=⌊2​n+25⌋\iota_{\rm g}(P_{n},\{K_{2}\})=\left\lfloor\frac{2n+2}{5}\right\rfloor
when n≡i(mod5)n\equiv i\pmod{5} and i∈{1,2,3}i\in\{1,2,3\}.

1 Introduction
Let G=(V,E)G=(V,E) be a graph and ℱ\mathcal{F} a family of graphs. A graph HH is ℱ\mathcal{F}-forbidden if no member of ℱ\mathcal{F} is a subgraph of HH. For a vertex x∈Vx\in V, the closed neighborhood of xx is defined by NG​[x]={v∈V:x​v∈E}∪{x}N_{G}[x]=\{v\in V:\,xv\in E\}\cup\{x\}, and if S⊂VS\subset V, the closed neighborhood of SS is NG[S]=∪v∈SN[v]N_{G}[S]=\cup_{v\in S}{N[v]}. A set SS of vertices is ℱ\mathcal{F}-isolating if the subgraph G−NG​[S]G-N_{G}[S] is ℱ\mathcal{F}-forbidden. The minimum cardinality of an ℱ\mathcal{F}-isolating set in a graph GG is the ℱ\mathcal{F}-isolation number of GG and is denoted by ι⁡(G,ℱ)\iota(G,{\mathcal{F}}). The concept was introduced in 2017 by Caro and Hansberg [10], and has gained the attention of many researchers. The ℱ\mathcal{F}-isolation number was studied for several families ℱ\cal F and from different perspectives; see [1, 2, 4, 15, 18] for a short selection of such studies.
In this paper, we introduce a game counterpart of the isolation number. The idea for the game was inspired by the domination game, introduced in 2010 by Brešar, Klavžar and Rall [6] as a game counterpart of the domination number. Numerous papers were devoted to the study of domination games, and several innovative proof methods were introduced. In particular, the Imagination Strategy and the Continuation Principle are two such important tools; see [5] and the references therein. In addition, a special discharging method due to Bújtas [7] was developed while investigating the domination game, and was later applied for obtaining best upper bounds for graphs with specified minimum degree on the domination number itself [8]. Many other variations of the domination game were introduced, each presenting a game version of a graph invariant that may be recognized by its name: total domination game [13], fractional domination game [9], connected domination game [3], two variants of the paired-domination game [11, 12], and competition-independence game [16, 17].
More examples of such variations can be found in the book [5] surveying the domination game and related concepts.
The ℱ\mathcal{F}-isolation game is played on a graph GG by two players, Dominator and Staller, who take turns choosing (playing) a vertex from GG while obeying the following rule. If SS is the set of already played vertices, then vertex xx can be chosen only if it dominates a vertex yy that belongs to a component of the graph G−N⁡[S]G-N[S] that is not ℱ\mathcal{F}-forbidden. (Recall that a vertex xx dominates itself and all its neighbors.) Such a vertex xx is called playable, and a move selecting xx is legal. The game ends when no playable vertex exists. Note that when the game has ended, the set of selected vertices is an ℱ\mathcal{F}-isolating set. The players have opposite goals.
Dominator wishes to finish the game as fast as possible, and Staller wishes to delay the process as much as possible.
The game ℱ\mathcal{F}-isolation number, denoted by ιg​(G,ℱ)\iota_{\rm g}(G,{\mathcal{F}}), is the number of vertices chosen when Dominator starts the game
(the Dominator-start game) provided that both players play optimally. Similarly, the Staller-start game ℱ\mathcal{F}-isolation number, written as ιg′​(G,ℱ)\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G,\mathcal{F}), is the number of vertices chosen in
the game when Staller starts the game (the Staller-start game). The Dominator-start game and the Staller-start game will be briefly
called the D-game and the S-game, respectively, when the family ℱ\mathcal{F} is clear from the context.
If ℱ={K1}\mathcal{F}=\{K_{1}\}, then the ℱ\mathcal{F}-isolation game coincides with the well-known domination game. Thus ιg​(G,{K1})=γg​(G)\iota_{\rm g}(G,\{K_{1}\})=\gamma_{\rm{g}}(G) and ιg′​(G,{K1})=γg′​(G)\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G,\{K_{1}\})=\gamma^{\prime}_{\rm{g}}(G).
If ℱ={K2}\mathcal{F}=\{K_{2}\}, then the vertices not dominated by the ℱ\mathcal{F}-isolating set form an independent set. In this case the ℱ\mathcal{F}-isolation number is denoted by ι⁡(G)\iota(G), and we will use the notation ιg​(G)\iota_{\rm g}(G) instead of ιg​(G,ℱ)\iota_{\rm g}(G,{\mathcal{F}}) and ιg′​(G)\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G) instead of ιg′​(G,ℱ)\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G,{\mathcal{F}}).
In the next section, we consider the ℱ\cal F-isolation game from a general perspective, providing results in which the family ℱ\cal F is arbitrary. By using the Imagination Strategy we prove that the Continuation Principle holds for all ℱ\mathcal{F}-isolation games regardless of the choice of ℱ\cal F. Using this tool, we then show that |ιg​(G,ℱ)−ιg′​(G,ℱ)|≤1|\iota_{\rm g}(G,{\mathcal{F}})-\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G,{\mathcal{F}})|\leq 1, where GG is an arbitrary graph and ℱ\cal F an arbitrary family of graphs. Then, we prove a simple lower and an upper bound on the game ℱ\cal F-isolation number of a graph GG in terms of the ℱ\mathcal{F}-isolation number of GG, and provide a certain “hereditary” condition for two families ℱ\mathcal{F} and ℱ′\mathcal{F}^{\prime}, which ensures that ιg​(G,ℱ′)≤ιg​(G,ℱ)\iota_{\rm g}(G,{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime})\leq\iota_{\rm g}(G,{\mathcal{F}}). In Section 3, we focus on the isolation game (that is, when ℱ={K2}\mathcal{F}=\{K_{2}\}), and prove that ιg​(G)\iota_{\rm g}(G) is bounded from above by half the order of the graph GG. We conjecture that the bound can be improved to ιg​(G)≤⌈3​n​(G)7⌉\iota_{\rm g}(G)\leq\lceil\frac{3n(G)}{7}\rceil, where n⁡(G)n(G) is the order of GG, and present an infinite family of graphs that attain this bound. Section 4 is devoted to the isolation game on forests. We prove that the D-game in a forest never lasts longer than the S-game (if both players play optimally). We also provide a lower and an upper bound on the game isolation number of a path, where the bounds are at most 11 apart. When n≡i(mod5)n\equiv i\pmod{5}, where i∈{1,2,3}i\in\{1,2,3\}, we get the exact values: ιg​(Pn)=ιg′​(Pn)=⌊2​n+25⌋\iota_{\rm g}(P_{n})=\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(P_{n})=\left\lfloor\frac{2n+2}{5}\right\rfloor.
2 ℱ\cal F-isolation game
In this section, we collect a number of preliminary results, most of which can be considered as extensions from the domination game to the isolation game. The proofs of several of these results follow similar lines of reasoning as the proofs of corresponding results for the domination game. For the sake of completeness we will provide all these proofs.
During the course of the game, when SS is the set of selected vertices, a vertex vv is considered marked if v∈N⁡[S]v\in N[S] or vv is contained in an ℱ\mathcal{F}-forbidden component of G−N⁡[S]G-N[S]. In particular, before the game begins every vertex in an ℱ\mathcal{F}-forbidden component, if such a component exists, is marked. Note that a marked vertex may or may not be playable. It is easy to see the following observation about playable vertices during the course of the game.
Observation 2.1.
When the ℱ\mathcal{F}-isolating game is played on a graph, a vertex is not playable if and only if its entire closed neighborhood is marked.
A partially marked graph G|AG|A consists of a graph GG with a subset A⊆V⁡(G)A\subseteq V(G) such that G−AG-A does not contain an ℱ\mathcal{F}-forbidden component. We will consider the vertices in AA as already marked. Suppose we are playing the game on a partially marked graph G|AG|A. During the course of the game, after each move we will update the set of marked vertices MM where initially M=AM=A. Suppose xx is a playable vertex in G|MG|M. Let
U={t:t​ is a vertex in an ℱ-forbidden component of G−(M∪N⁡[x])},U=\{t:t\text{ is a vertex in an $\mathcal{F}$-forbidden component of $G-(M\cup N[x])$}\},
and let M′=M∪U∪N⁡[x]M^{\prime}=M\cup U\cup N[x].
If a player selects the vertex xx when the game is played on G|MG|M, then G|M′G|M^{\prime} is the updated partially marked graph. The game ends when every vertex in
GG is marked.
One of the main tools in analyzing the domination game is the Continuation Principle [14]. In the next result we show that this principle extends to the ℱ\mathcal{F}-isolation game.
Lemma 2.2.
(Continuation Principle)
If GG is a graph and A,B⊆V⁡(G)A,B\subseteq V(G) with B⊆AB\subseteq A, then ιg​(G|A,ℱ)≤ιg​(G|B,ℱ)\iota_{\rm g}(G|A,{\mathcal{F}})\leq\iota_{\rm g}(G|B,{\mathcal{F}}) and ιg′​(G|A,ℱ)≤ιg′​(G|B,ℱ)\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G|A,{\mathcal{F}})\leq\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G|B,{\mathcal{F}}), where ℱ\mathcal{F} is an arbitrary family of graphs.
Proof.
Two games will be played in parallel, Game A on the partially marked graph G|AG|A and Game B on the partially marked graph G|BG|B. The first of these will be the real game, while Game B will be imagined by Dominator. In Game A, Staller will play optimally while in Game B, Dominator will play optimally.
We claim that in each stage of the games, the set of vertices that are marked in Game B is a subset of the set of vertices that are marked in Game A. Since B⊆AB\subseteq A, this is true at the start of the games. Suppose now that Staller has (optimally) selected a vertex uu in Game A. Then by the induction assumption, the vertex uu is playable in Game B because a new vertex vv that was dominated by uu in Game A, is not yet marked in Game B as well. Then Dominator copies the move of Staller by playing the vertex uu in Game B. Dominator then replies with an optimal move selecting vertex xx in Game B. (In particular, if the D-game is being played and this is the first move in the game, then Dominator will choose an optimal move in Game B.) If xx is playable in Game A, Dominator plays xx in Game A as well. Otherwise, if the game is not yet over, Dominator selects any other playable vertex in Game A. In either case the set of marked vertices in Game B is a subset of the set of marked vertices in Game A which by induction also proves the claim.
We have thus proved that Game A finishes no later than Game B. Suppose that Game B lasted rr moves. Because Dominator was playing optimally in Game B, it follows that r≤ιg​(G|B,ℱ)r\leq\iota_{\rm g}(G|B,\mathcal{F}). On the other hand, because Staller was playing optimally in Game A and Dominator has a strategy to finish the game in rr moves, we infer that ιg​(G|A,ℱ)≤r\iota_{\rm g}(G|A,\mathcal{F})\leq r. Therefore, ιg​(G|A,ℱ)≤r≤ιg​(G|B,ℱ)\iota_{\rm g}(G|A,\mathcal{F})\leq r\leq\iota_{\rm g}(G|B,\mathcal{F}) and we are done if Dominator is the first to play. Note that in the above argument we did not assume who starts first, hence in both cases Game A will finish no later than Game B. Hence the conclusion holds for ιg′\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime} as well; that is, ιg′​(G|A,ℱ)≤ιg′​(G|B,ℱ)\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G|A,\mathcal{F})\leq\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G|B,\mathcal{F}).
∎
As a consequence of the Continuation Principle, whenever xx and yy are playable vertices for Dominator in the ℱ\mathcal{F}-isolation game and N⁡[x]⊆N⁡[y]N[x]\subseteq N[y], we may assume that Dominator will play yy instead of xx, since any newly marked vertex as a result of playing xx will also be marked as a result of playing yy. For a similar reason, if it was Staller’s turn, we may assume that Staller will play xx instead of yy.
Theorem 2.3.
For an arbitrary graph GG and an arbitrary family ℱ\mathcal{F}, we have |ιg​(G,ℱ)−ιg′​(G,ℱ)|≤1|\iota_{\rm g}(G,{\mathcal{F}})-\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G,{\mathcal{F}})|\leq 1.
Proof.
Consider the D-game on GG and let vv be the first move of Dominator.
Let AA be the set of marked vertices of GG after vv is played, and let B=∅B=\emptyset. Consider the partially marked graph G|AG|A, and note that G|B=GG|B=G. Since vv is an optimal first move of Dominator, we note further that ιg​(G,ℱ)=1+ιg′​(G|A,ℱ)\iota_{\rm g}(G,\mathcal{F})=1+\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G|A,\mathcal{F}). By the Continuation Principle, ιg′​(G|A,ℱ)≤ιg′​(G|B,ℱ)=ιg′​(G,ℱ)\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G|A,\mathcal{F})\leq\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G|B,\mathcal{F})=\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G,\mathcal{F}).
Therefore,
ιg​(G,ℱ)=ιg′​(G|A,ℱ)+1≤ιg′​(G,ℱ)+1.\iota_{\rm g}(G,\mathcal{F})=\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G|A,\mathcal{F})+1\leq\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G,\mathcal{F})+1\,.
By a parallel argument, consider the S-game and let vv be the first move of Staller. As before, let AA be the set of marked vertices of GG after vv is played by Staller and B=∅B=\emptyset. Since vv is an optimal first move of Staller, we note that ιg′​(G,ℱ)=1+ιg​(G|A,ℱ)\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G,\mathcal{F})=1+\iota_{\rm g}(G|A,\mathcal{F}). By the Continuation Principle, ιg​(G|A,ℱ)≤ιg​(G|B,ℱ)=ιg​(G,ℱ)\iota_{\rm g}(G|A,\mathcal{F})\leq\iota_{\rm g}(G|B,\mathcal{F})=\iota_{\rm g}(G,\mathcal{F}), implying that
ιg′​(G,ℱ)=ιg​(G|A,ℱ)+1≤ιg​(G,ℱ)+1.\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G,\mathcal{F})=\iota_{\rm g}(G|A,\mathcal{F})+1\leq\iota_{\rm g}(G,\mathcal{F})+1\,.
. ∎
Theorem 2.4.
For an arbitrary graph GG and an arbitrary family ℱ\mathcal{F}, we have
ι⁡(G,ℱ)≤ιg​(G,ℱ)≤2​ι​(G,ℱ)−1​, and ​ι​(G,ℱ)≤ιg′​(G,ℱ)≤2​ι​(G,ℱ).\iota(G,{\mathcal{F}})\leq\iota_{\rm g}(G,{\mathcal{F}})\leq 2\iota(G,{\mathcal{F}})-1\textrm{, and }\iota(G,{\mathcal{F}})\leq\iota^{\prime}_{g}(G,{\mathcal{F}})\leq 2\iota(G,{\mathcal{F}}).
Proof.
Since for any graph GG and any family ℱ\mathcal{F} the set of selected vertices in the ℱ\mathcal{F}-isolation game played on GG is an ℱ\mathcal{F}-isolating set, we get ι⁡(G,ℱ)≤ιg​(G,ℱ)\iota(G,{\mathcal{F}})\leq\iota_{\rm g}(G,{\mathcal{F}}). Let II be an ℱ\cal F-isolating set of GG, where ι⁡(G,ℱ)=|I|\iota(G,\mathcal{F})=|I|. Dominator’s strategy when the D-game is played on GG is to select the vertices from II one at a time as long as any of these vertices is playable. When no additional vertex from II is playable, we infer by Observation 2.1 that every vertex in N⁡[I]N[I] is marked, which by the choice of II means that all vertices of GG are marked. Thus, altogether at most 2​|I|−12|I|-1 moves have been played in the game, yielding ιg​(G,ℱ)≤2​ι​(G,ℱ)−1\iota_{\rm g}(G,{\mathcal{F}})\leq 2\iota(G,{\mathcal{F}})-1.
The inequalities for ιg′​(G,ℱ)\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G,\mathcal{F}) can be proved by using similar reasoning.
∎
The next result relates isolating games on the same graph GG considering two different families ℱ\cal F and ℱ′\mathcal{F}^{\prime}, where ℱ\cal F and ℱ′\mathcal{F}^{\prime} are related in a specific way.
Theorem 2.5.
Let ℱ\mathcal{F} and ℱ′\mathcal{F}^{\prime} be two families of graphs. If for any F∈ℱF\in{\mathcal{F}} there exists F′∈ℱ′F^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime} such that FF is a subgraph of F′F^{\prime}, then ιg​(G,ℱ′)≤ιg​(G,ℱ)\iota_{\rm g}(G,{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime})\leq\iota_{\rm g}(G,{\mathcal{F}}), where GG is an arbitrary graph.
Proof.
To prove ιg​(G,ℱ′)≤ιg​(G,ℱ)\iota_{\rm g}(G,{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime})\leq\iota_{\rm g}(G,{\mathcal{F}}), we will consider the ℱ′\mathcal{F}^{\prime}-isolation D-game played on GG, while Dominator will imagine that an ℱ\mathcal{F}-isolation D-game is played in parallel on GG. We call these games the ℱ′\mathcal{F}^{\prime}-game and the ℱ\mathcal{F}-game, respectively. Let A′A^{\prime}, respectively AA, be the set of marked vertices updated after every move of the players, in the ℱ′\mathcal{F}^{\prime}-game, respectively ℱ\mathcal{F}-game on GG. Note that, by the assumption on the families ℱ\mathcal{F} and ℱ′\mathcal{F}^{\prime}, initially A⊆A′A\subseteq A^{\prime}.
By the Dominator’s strategy that we will present next, the relationship between AA and A′A^{\prime} will be maintained after each move.
When Dominator selects vv in the ℱ\mathcal{F}-game, then he plays vv also in the ℱ′\mathcal{F}^{\prime}-game if vv is playable there. Otherwise, he chooses any playable vertex in the ℱ′\mathcal{F}^{\prime}-game.
Clearly, in either case, after his move the relation A⊆A′A\subseteq A^{\prime} still holds. Staller is following her optimal strategy in the ℱ′\mathcal{F}^{\prime}-game. Each of her moves in this game is copied by Dominator to the ℱ\mathcal{F}-game, and since A⊆A′A\subseteq A^{\prime}, this move is also legal in the ℱ\mathcal{F}-game. Because of the assumed relationship between the families ℱ\mathcal{F} and ℱ′\mathcal{F}^{\prime}, the updated set of marked vertices in the ℱ\mathcal{F}-game is a subset of the updated vertices in the ℱ′\mathcal{F}^{\prime}-game. Let ss and s′s^{\prime} be the total numbers of moves in the ℱ\mathcal{F}-game and the ℱ′\mathcal{F}^{\prime}-game, respectively. By the relationship between the marked vertices in the two games, we infer s′≤ss^{\prime}\leq s. Since Staller played optimally in the ℱ′\mathcal{F}^{\prime}-game, it follows that ιg​(G,ℱ′)≤s′\iota_{\rm g}(G,\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\leq s^{\prime}. Similarly, since Dominator followed his optimal strategy in the ℱ\mathcal{F}-game, we infer s≤ιg​(G,ℱ)s\leq\iota_{\rm g}(G,\mathcal{F}).
∎
Since ιg​(G)=ιg​(G,{K2})\iota_{\rm g}(G)=\iota_{\rm g}(G,\{K_{2}\}) and γg​(G)=ιg​(G,{K1})\gamma_{\rm g}(G)=\iota_{\rm g}(G,\{K_{1}\}) Theorem 2.5 implies the following.
Corollary 2.6.
For any graph GG we have ιg​(G)≤γg​(G)\iota_{\rm g}(G)\leq\gamma_{\rm g}(G).
Note that the difference γg​(G)−ιg​(G)\gamma_{\rm g}(G)-\iota_{\rm g}(G) can be arbitrarily large.
Let nn be any positive integer and let T1,…,TnT_{1},\ldots,T_{n} be nn vertex disjoint triangles, where V⁡(Ti)={vi,xi,yi}V(T_{i})=\{v_{i},x_{i},y_{i}\} for each i∈[n]i\in[n]. Let GnG_{n} be the graph constructed
from the union of this set of triangles by letting {v1,…,vn}\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\} induce a complete subgraph. In addition, for each k∈[n]k\in[n] let
FkF_{k} be the spanning subgraph of GnG_{n} defined by Fk=Gn−{x1​y1,…,xk​yk}F_{k}=G_{n}-\{x_{1}y_{1},\ldots,x_{k}y_{k}\}.
Now, letting nn be odd, it is easy to show that ιg​(Fn)=1\iota_{\rm g}(F_{n})=1 and γg​(Fn)=1+3​(n−1)/2\gamma_{\rm g}(F_{n})=1+3(n-1)/2.
3 Isolation game
In the rest of the paper ℱ={K2}\mathcal{F}=\{K_{2}\}, that is, we consider the isolation game. As mentioned earlier, the game {K2}\{K_{2}\}-isolation invariants are denoted by ιg\iota_{\rm g} and ιg′\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}.
In the next example, we show that the game isolation number for a spanning subgraph HH of GG can be smaller than ιg​(G)\iota_{\rm g}(G). Let GnG_{n} and its spanning subgraphs FkF_{k}, k≤nk\leq n, be the graphs as defined in the previous section. Then ιg​(Gn)=n\iota_{\rm g}(G_{n})=n and
ιg​(Fk)=n−k\iota_{\rm g}(F_{k})=n-k, for each k∈[n−1]k\in[n-1] and ιg​(Fn)=1\iota_{\rm g}(F_{n})=1. This example shows that not only can the game isolation number of a spanning subgraph be arbitrarily smaller than the game isolation number of the original graph, but it also shows that the game isolation number can “interpolate” between the smallest and largest values. To see that ιg​(H)\iota_{\rm g}(H) can also be arbitrarily larger than ιg​(G)\iota_{\rm g}(G), let G=KnG=K_{n}. We summarize this discussion as follows.
Observation 3.1.
For an arbitrary k∈ℤk\in\mathbb{Z}, there exists a graph GG with a spanning subgraph HH such that ιg​(G)−ιg​(H)=k\iota_{\rm g}(G)-\iota_{\rm g}(H)=k.
In the next result, we provide a general upper bound on the game isolation number of a graph in terms of a fraction of its order.
Theorem 3.2.
For any graph GG, ιg​(G)≤12​n​(G)\iota_{\rm g}(G)\leq\frac{1}{2}n(G).
Proof.
We will weight the graph in the following way. At the beginning all vertices are unmarked and each vertex has weight 1. During the game, when a vertex is marked its weight drops to 0. The weight of GG, w⁡(G)w(G), is the sum of weights of all vertices of GG. Thus at the beginning of the game w⁡(G)=n⁡(G)w(G)=n(G) and when the game ends the weight is zero. Let MM be the set of marked vertices in some stage of the game. Dominator’s strategy is that he does not play a vertex in any K2K_{2} component of G−MG-M, unless he has no other option. Moreover, if there exists a component CC of G−MG-M of order at least 3, then he plays a vertex of degree at least 2 in CC. With this strategy, each of his moves will decrease the weight of GG by at least 3. Since Staller will decrease the weight of GG by at least 1 after each of her moves, each move will on average decrease the weight by at least 2 until just K2K_{2} components are left in G−MG-M. In this final stage of the game, if any K2K_{2} components are left in G−MG-M, each player will in every move decrease the weight by exactly 2. Hence the game finishes after at most n⁡(G)2\frac{n(G)}{2} moves.
∎
The bound from Theorem 3.2 is sharp since ιg​(C6)=3\iota_{\rm g}(C_{6})=3. However, we think that the bound can be improved to ιg​(G)≤⌈37​n​(G)⌉\iota_{\rm g}(G)\leq\left\lceil\frac{3}{7}n(G)\right\rceil. We could not find an example where this bound is exceeded, and we pose the following conjecture.
Conjecture 3.3.
For any graph GG with no K2K_{2}-components,
ιg​(G)≤⌈37​n​(G)⌉​ and ​ιg′​(G)≤⌈37​n​(G)⌉.\iota_{\rm g}(G)\leq\left\lceil\frac{3}{7}n(G)\right\rceil\text{ and }\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G)\leq\left\lceil\frac{3}{7}n(G)\right\rceil.
The ceiling in the expression 3​n​(G)/73n(G)/7 is needed, since ιg​(C6)=3\iota_{\rm g}(C_{6})=3 and ιg′​(P4)=2\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(P_{4})=2.
Next, we present an infinite family of graphs showing that the conjectured bound can be attained. Let GG be an arbitrary graph. For each vertex v∈V⁡(G)v\in V(G) identify vv with the center vertex of a path of order 77 and denote the resulting graph by G∗G^{*}.
Proposition 3.4.
If GG is an arbitrary graph, then ιg​(G∗)=ιg′​(G∗)=37​n​(G∗)\iota_{\rm g}(G^{*})=\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G^{*})=\frac{3}{7}n(G^{*}).
Proof.
Let us call the paths of order 77 that are in G∗G^{*} but not in GG “added paths.” Let D-game be played on GG. A strategy of Dominator that no more than three vertices are played on any of the added paths is as follows. If he is the first player to select a vertex on an added path, then he achieves this by choosing the center of the path. (Indeed, after his move in the added path in which he played, two K2K_{2} components of unmarked vertices remain, and there are exactly two moves left in this added path.) Now suppose that Staller is the first player to select a vertex from an added path. If she does not choose the center vertex of that path, then Dominator can prevent four played vertices from that path by choosing a vertex that is at distance 4 from her move. On the other hand, Staller has a strategy to force three moves on each added path. If she has the first move on some added path, then she chooses the center; otherwise she responds to Dominator’s first move on this path by choosing its neighbor.
Following the same strategies also in the S-game, one can easily derive ιg′​(G∗)=37​n​(G∗)\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G^{*})=\frac{3}{7}n(G^{*}).
∎
Therefore, if Conjecture 3.3 holds, then it is sharp for an infinite class of graphs.
Besides the family of graphs G∗G^{*} that appear in Proposition 3.4 and graphs similar to graphs G∗G^{*} (say, obtained from G∗G^{*} by removing a pendant vertex), we could not find any other infinite family of graphs whose game isolation number is close to 3​n/73n/7. For instance, for paths PnP_{n} (see Section 4 for details) we prove that the game isolation number is roughly 2​n/52n/5. After searching for graphs having game isolation number as large as possible, we eventually found an infinite family 𝒢\cal{G} of graphs with 2​n​(G)5<ιg​(G)=5​n​(G)12<3​n​(G)7\frac{2n(G)}{5}<\iota_{\rm g}(G)=\frac{5n(G)}{12}<\frac{3n(G)}{7} for all G∈𝒢G\in\cal{G}, which we present next.

4 Isolation game on forests
The proofs of the next lemma and theorem closely follow arguments of Kinnersley, West, and Zamani [14] for the domination game. They are included for completeness.
We write G+HG+H to denote the disjoint union of graphs GG and HH. If GG is a partially marked graph and v∈V⁡(G)v\in V(G), then GvG_{v} denotes the partially marked graph obtained from GG by marking all vertices corresponding to a play of vv.
Lemma 4.1.
Given λ≥2\lambda\geq 2, assume ιg​(F)≤ιg′​(F)\iota_{\rm g}(F)\leq\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(F) for all partially marked forests FF such that ιg​(F)≤λ\iota_{\rm g}(F)\leq\lambda. If GG is such a forest and rr is any non-negative integer, then ιg​(G+K1,r)>ιg​(G)\iota_{\rm g}(G+K_{1,r})>\iota_{\rm g}(G) and ιg′​(G+K1,r)>ιg′​(G)\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G+K_{1,r})>\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G).
Proof.
Fixing λ\lambda, we use induction on the number of unmarked vertices of GG. The claim is true if GG has no unmarked vertices since an additional play will be needed to mark the vertices of K1,rK_{1,r}.
To prove ιg​(G+K1,r)>ιg​(G)\iota_{\rm g}(G+K_{1,r})>\iota_{\rm g}(G), let vv be an optimal first move in the D-game on G+K1,rG+K_{1,r}. If vv is a vertex of K1,rK_{1,r}, then ιg​(G+K1,r)=1+ιg′​(G)≥1+ιg​(G)\iota_{\rm g}(G+K_{1,r})=1+\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G)\geq 1+\iota_{\rm g}(G). Otherwise, vv belongs to GG. By the optimality of vv we have ιg​(G+K1,r)=1+ιg′​(Gv+K1,r)\iota_{\rm g}(G+K_{1,r})=1+\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G_{v}+K_{1,r}), and by the Continuation Principle, ιg​(Gv)≤ιg​(G)≤λ\iota_{\rm g}(G_{v})\leq\iota_{\rm g}(G)\leq\lambda. These observations and the induction hypothesis yield ιg​(G+K1,r)=1+ιg′​(Gv+K1,r)>1+ιg′​(Gv)\iota_{\rm g}(G+K_{1,r})=1+\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G_{v}+K_{1,r})>1+\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G_{v}). Since vv is not necessarily an optimal first move for Dominator in the D-game on GG, we have 1+ιg′​(Gv)≥ιg​(G)1+\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G_{v})\geq\iota_{\rm g}(G) and hence ιg​(G+K1,r)>ιg​(G)\iota_{\rm g}(G+K_{1,r})>\iota_{\rm g}(G).
To prove ιg′​(G+K1,r)>ιg′​(G)\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G+K_{1,r})>\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G), let vv be an optimal first move in the S-game on GG. Now ιg′​(G+K1,r)≥1+ιg​(Gv+K1,r)>1+ιg​(Gv)=ιg′​(G)\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G+K_{1,r})\geq 1+\iota_{\rm g}(G_{v}+K_{1,r})>1+\iota_{\rm g}(G_{v})=\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G) with the strict inequality following from the induction hypothesis and the equality following from the optimality of vv.
∎
As for the game domination number, there exist graphs GG where ιg​(G)<ιg′​(G)\iota_{\rm g}(G)<\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G), graphs GG with ιg​(G)=ιg′​(G)\iota_{\rm g}(G)=\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G) (one such example is P3P_{3}) and graphs GG with ιg​(G)>ιg′​(G)\iota_{\rm g}(G)>\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G) (one such example is C6C_{6}, for which ιg​(C6)=3\iota_{\rm g}(C_{6})=3 and ιg′​(C6)=2\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(C_{6})=2). In the next theorem we will prove that only two of these three relationships are possible when GG is a forest.
Theorem 4.2.
If FF is a partially marked forest, then ιg​(F)≤ιg′​(F)\iota_{\rm g}(F)\leq\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(F).
Proof.
The proof goes by induction on ιg​(F)=k\iota_{\rm g}(F)=k. If k≤2k\leq 2, the result trivially follows. Indeed, if ιg​(F)=2\iota_{\rm g}(F)=2, then it is not possible that ιg′​(F)≤1\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(F)\leq 1. Thus let k≥3k\geq 3 and assume that there exists a partially marked forest FF with ιg​(F)=k\iota_{\rm g}(F)=k and ιg′​(F)≤k−1\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(F)\leq k-1. By Theorem 2.3, ιg′​(F)=k−1\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(F)=k-1. Therefore, Dominator can ensure that after any first move of Staller in the S-game on FF, the game ends within at most k−2k-2 additional moves. That is, ιg​(Fv)≤k−2\iota_{\rm g}(F_{v})\leq k-2 for any v∈V⁡(F)v\in V(F). Similarly, since ιg​(F)=k\iota_{\rm g}(F)=k, we get ιg′​(Fv)≥k−1\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(F_{v})\geq k-1 for any v∈V⁡(F)v\in V(F). By Theorem 2.3, ιg​(Fv)=k−2\iota_{\rm g}(F_{v})=k-2 and ιg′​(Fv)=k−1\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(F_{v})=k-1 hold for any v∈V⁡(F)v\in V(F).
Denote by MM the set of vertices of FF that are marked before the game starts. Note that by definition each vertex that is not marked has at least one unmarked neighbor. Let CC be a component of FF having an unmarked vertex. We root CC in an arbitrary vertex rr and let xx be an unmarked vertex in CC that is at the largest distance from rr. Since x∉Mx\notin M and all descendants of xx are in MM, the only possible unmarked neighbor of xx is its parent x′x^{\prime}. If r≠x′r\neq x^{\prime}, let yy be the parent of x′x^{\prime}, otherwise let y=x′y=x^{\prime}. Let uu be an optimal first move of Staller in the S-game played on FyF_{y}. By the choice of xx and by the definition of FyF_{y}, it follows that u∉N⁡[{x,x′}]u\notin N[\{x,x^{\prime}\}]. Since uu is an optimal first move of Staller in the S-game on FyF_{y}, ιg′​(Fy)=k−1\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(F_{y})=k-1 implies that ιg​((Fy)u)=k−2\iota_{\rm g}((F_{y})_{u})=k-2. Moreover, we already know that ιg​(Fu)=k−2\iota_{\rm g}(F_{u})=k-2.
Let F′F^{\prime} be the partially marked forest obtained from FF by marking X=N⁡[u]∪(N⁡[{y,x′}]∖{x,x′})X=N[u]\cup(N[\{y,x^{\prime}\}]\setminus\{x,x^{\prime}\}) and all isolated vertices of the graph F−(M∪X)F-(M\cup X). Note that the only difference between F′F^{\prime} and (Fy)u(F_{y})_{u} is that vertices x,x′x,x^{\prime} are marked in (Fy)u(F_{y})_{u} but xx and x′x^{\prime} are not marked in F′F^{\prime}. Since the move on F′F^{\prime} that marks either xx or x′x^{\prime} marks both xx and x′x^{\prime} but no other vertices of F′F^{\prime}, we get ιg​(F′)=ιg​((Fy)u+K1,1)\iota_{\rm g}(F^{\prime})=\iota_{\rm g}((F_{y})_{u}+K_{1,1}), where K1,1K_{1,1} corresponds to the star on vertices x′x^{\prime} and xx. Hence
k−2=ιg​(Fu)≥ιg​(F′)=ιg​((Fy)u+K1,1)>ιg​((Fy)u)=k−2,k-2=\iota_{\rm g}(F_{u})\geq\iota_{\rm g}(F^{\prime})=\iota_{\rm g}((F_{y})_{u}+K_{1,1})>\iota_{\rm g}((F_{y})_{u})=k-2,
which is a contradiction. Note that the first inequality follows from the Continuation Principle and the last inequality follows from Lemma 4.1.
∎
By Theorem 4.2, we can obtain a good upper bound for the game isolation number of paths.
Lemma 4.3.
For any n≥3n\geq 3,
ιg​(Pn)≤ιg′​(Pn)≤⌊2​n+25⌋\iota_{\rm g}(P_{n})\leq\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(P_{n})\leq\left\lfloor\frac{2n+2}{5}\right\rfloor
Proof.
The first inequality follows from Theorem 4.2. For the second inequality consider the S-game played on PnP_{n}. Dominator can use the strategy following each of Staller’s moves by selecting a vertex xx that is at distance 44 from one of the already played vertices, say yy, provided there are no vertices lying between xx and yy that have been played. In this way, if such a vertex xx is played, Dominator’s move adds four vertices to the set of unplayable vertices. Clearly, each move of Staller makes at least one vertex unplayable. Suppose that Dominator can no longer select such a vertex as described above. Then, note that there are exactly three options:
• 
the game is over (all vertices are unplayable), or
• 
there are exactly three playable vertices, which lie in one end of the path, or
• 
there are exactly six playable vertices, three at each end of the path.
Let uiu_{i} be the number of vertices that became unplayable because of the iith move in the game, and let pp be the total number of moves made in the game. In the first case, and we get
n=∑i=1pui≥{5​p2; Dominator had the last move,5​p−12+3; Staller had the last move,n=\sum_{i=1}^{p}u_{i}\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}5\frac{p}{2};\text{ Dominator had the last move},\\
5\frac{p-1}{2}+3;\text{ Staller had the last move},\end{array}\right.
both of which imply that p≤2​n5p\leq\frac{2n}{5}. Since pp is an integer we get p≤⌊2​n5⌋p\leq\lfloor\frac{2n}{5}\rfloor. In the second case, the last move, say ww, that resulted in exactly three playable vertices
was played by Staller due to Dominator’s strategy. Hence
n=∑i=1pui≥5​p−22+1+3n=\sum_{i=1}^{p}u_{i}\geq 5\frac{p-2}{2}+1+3\,
and this gives p≤2​n+25p\leq\frac{2n+2}{5}. Again, since pp is an integer, it follows that p≤⌊2​n+25⌋p\leq\lfloor\frac{2n+2}{5}\rfloor.
Finally, in the last case the last move, say ww, that resulted in exactly six playable vertices was again played by Staller for the same reason as in the previous case. Therefore,
n=∑i=1pui≥5​p−32+1+6n=\sum_{i=1}^{p}u_{i}\geq 5\frac{p-3}{2}+1+6\,
and we get p≤2​n+15p\leq\frac{2n+1}{5} and so p≤⌊2​n+15⌋p\leq\lfloor\frac{2n+1}{5}\rfloor.
We have shown that by following the strategy described above, Dominator can ensure that p≤⌊2​n+25⌋p\leq\lfloor\frac{2n+2}{5}\rfloor.
Therefore, ιg′​(Pn)≤p≤⌊2​n+25⌋\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(P_{n})\leq p\leq\left\lfloor\frac{2n+2}{5}\right\rfloor.
∎
We also get a good lower bound:
Lemma 4.4.
For any n≥6n\geq 6,
⌈2​n5⌉−1≤ιg​(Pn)≤ιg′​(Pn).\lceil\frac{2n}{5}\rceil-1\leq\iota_{\rm g}(P_{n})\leq\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(P_{n}).
Proof.
We only need to prove ⌈2​n5⌉−1≤ιg​(Pn)\lceil\frac{2n}{5}\rceil-1\leq\iota_{\rm g}(P_{n}).
Note that when the D-game played on a path is over, the following sets A1,…,Ak+1A_{1},\ldots,A_{k+1}, and B1,…,BkB_{1},\ldots,B_{k} are generated. If v1v_{1} was selected during the game, then A1=∅A_{1}=\emptyset, and B1B_{1} is a maximal string of vertices that were selected during the game, starting with v1v_{1}. Otherwise, A1A_{1} is a maximal string of vertices that were not selected during the game including v1v_{1}, which ends with, say vk1v_{k_{1}}. In that case, B1B_{1} is a maximal string of vertices that were selected during the game, starting with vk1+1v_{k_{1}+1}. We continue in this way, where finally Ak+1A_{k+1} may or may not be empty. Observe that |A1|≤2|A_{1}|\leq 2, |Ak+1|≤2|A_{k+1}|\leq 2, and |Ai|≤3|A_{i}|\leq 3 for i∈{2,…,k}i\in\{2,\ldots,k\} regardless of how the game is played.
Staller’s strategy to achieve the desired bound is as follows. If Dominator selected a vertex vv in one of his moves, then Staller responds by selecting a neighbor of vv if possible. (This is clearly possible for her after the first move of Dominator.) If this is not possible, then Staller selects any vertex that is a neighbor of a vertex selected earlier. If there are no such vertices available, the game is already over. Note that after each move of Dominator (with the only possible exception if Dominator played the last move in the game), Staller’s strategy of selecting a vertex uu next to another already selected vertex vv increases the size of the corresponding set BℓB_{\ell}, in which the vertex uu lies, by 11. In particular, if vv was played in the preceding move by Dominator, then after the move of Staller selecting uu we have |Bℓ|≥2|B_{\ell}|\geq 2, while if vv was played in an earlier move, then Staller’s selection of uu yields |Bℓ|≥3|B_{\ell}|\geq 3. Based on these facts, we have the following correspondence at the end of the game: for every set BiB_{i} with |Bi|=1|B_{i}|=1 (with only one possible exception when the vertex vv of BiB_{i} was the last move in the game played by Dominator), where vv is the vertex of BiB_{i} necessarily played by Dominator, there exists a set BjB_{j} with |Bj|≥3|B_{j}|\geq 3 and a vertex uu of BjB_{j}, which was played by Staller as the response to Dominator’s selection of vertex vv.
Let pp be the number of moves played in the D-game when Staller employs the above strategy. We infer p=∑i=1k|Bi|p=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{|B_{i}|}, which is either 2​k2k or 2​k−12k-1 depending on whether Dominator had the last move in the game and with this move selected a vertex vv, which belongs to BiB_{i} with |Bi|=1|B_{i}|=1. On the other hand,
∑i=1k+1|Ai|≤2+2+3​(k−1)=3​k+1.\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}{|A_{i}|}\leq 2+2+3(k-1)=3k+1.
First, if p=2​kp=2k, then n≤5​k+1n\leq 5k+1, which gives p≥2​n−25p\geq\frac{2n-2}{5}. On the other hand, if p=2​k−1p=2k-1, then n≤5​kn\leq 5k, which gives p≥2​n−55p\geq\frac{2n-5}{5}. In either case, we get that the number of moves is at least 2​n5−1\frac{2n}{5}-1, and since this is an integer,
⌈2​n5⌉−1≤ιg​(Pn)\lceil\frac{2n}{5}\rceil-1\leq\iota_{\rm g}(P_{n}), as claimed.
∎
Combining the upper bound from Lemma 4.3 and the lower bound from Lemma 4.4 we get the following.
Corollary 4.5.
If n≥6n\geq 6, then
⌈2​n5⌉−1≤ιg​(Pn)≤ιg′​(Pn)≤⌊2​n+25⌋.\left\lceil\frac{2n}{5}\right\rceil-1\leq\iota_{\rm g}(P_{n})\leq\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(P_{n})\leq\left\lfloor\frac{2n+2}{5}\right\rfloor.
Using Corollary 4.5 and Lemma 4.4, we now give the exact value of the game isolation number for certain paths.
Corollary 4.6.
Let n≥6n\geq 6. If n≡1(mod5)n\equiv 1\pmod{5}, n≡2(mod5)n\equiv 2\pmod{5}, or n≡3(mod5)n\equiv 3\pmod{5}, then
ιg​(Pn)=ιg′​(Pn)=⌊2​n+25⌋.\iota_{\rm g}(P_{n})=\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(P_{n})=\left\lfloor\frac{2n+2}{5}\right\rfloor\,.
Proof.
When n≡1(mod5)n\equiv 1\pmod{5} or n≡3(mod5)n\equiv 3\pmod{5} the lower bound and the upper bound in Corollary 4.5 agree. Therefore, we get
ιg​(Pn)=ιg′​(Pn)=⌊2​n+25⌋\iota_{\rm g}(P_{n})=\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(P_{n})=\left\lfloor\frac{2n+2}{5}\right\rfloor.
Finally, let n≡2(mod5)n\equiv 2\pmod{5}, i.e. n=5​k+2n=5k+2 for some k∈ℕk\in\mathbb{N}. By Corollary 4.5, it is enough to prove that ιg​(Pn)≥⌊2​n+25⌋.\iota_{\rm g}(P_{n})\geq\left\lfloor\frac{2n+2}{5}\right\rfloor. For the purpose of contradiction assume that ιg​(Pn)≤⌊2​n+25⌋−1=2​k\iota_{\rm g}(P_{n})\leq\left\lfloor\frac{2n+2}{5}\right\rfloor-1=2k. By Lemma 4.4, we get ιg​(Pn)=2​k\iota_{\rm g}(P_{n})=2k. Now we use exactly the same strategy of Staller and the same notation as in the proof of Lemma 4.4.
Recall that pp is the number of moves played in the D-game when Staller employs the above strategy and p=∑i=1k|Bi|p=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{|B_{i}|}. Now, by our assumption p=ιg​(Pn)=2​kp=\iota_{\rm g}(P_{n})=2k. This implies that the last move was done by Staller. Using notation from Lemma 4.4 we get p=∑i=1k|Bi|=2​kp=\sum_{i=1}^{k}|B_{i}|=2k and ∑i=1k+1|Ai|≤3​k+1\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}|A_{i}|\leq 3k+1. Thus n≤5​k+1n\leq 5k+1, which gives 2​k=p≥2​n−25=2​(5​k+2)−25=2​k+25,2k=p\geq\frac{2n-2}{5}=\frac{2(5k+2)-2}{5}=2k+\frac{2}{5}, a contradiction.
