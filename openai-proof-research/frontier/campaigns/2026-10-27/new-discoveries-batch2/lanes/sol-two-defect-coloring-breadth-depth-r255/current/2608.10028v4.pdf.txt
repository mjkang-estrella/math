                                                                                            Abstract
                                                In 1988, Jaeger conjectured that every bridgeless cubic graph G admits a Petersen coloring;
                                                that is, a map E(G) → E(P ) mapping any two adjacent edges of G to two adjacent
                                                edges of the Petersen graph P . A positive resolution of Jaeger’s conjecture would have
                                                immediately resolved several other famous and long-standing problems in graph theory. In
                                                July 2026, a 68-vertex counterexample was announced on X. Shortly afterwards, Putman
                                                independently presented two non-isomorphic 112-vertex counterexamples, relying solely
                                                on computer-assisted verification. In this paper, we present two counterexamples of order
                                                52, currently the smallest known, and provide a purely theoretical proof. In the second
                                                part, we construct an infinite family of cyclically 4-edge-connected cubic graphs without
                                                a Petersen coloring for every even order at least 60. Additionally, through computational
                                                verification, we show that any counterexample must have order at least 40. Moreover,
                                                we show that our counterexamples provide a negative answer to other related problems.
                                                Finally, we conclude the paper by discussing key open problems and highlighting avenues
                                                for future work.

                                           1   Introduction
                                           A Petersen coloring (or P -coloring) of a cubic graph G is a map φ : E(G) → E(P ) mapping
                                           any two adjacent edges of G to two adjacent edges of the Petersen graph P . In 1988, Jaeger
                                           formulated the following celebrated conjecture.

                                           Conjecture 1 (Petersen Coloring Conjecture [14]). Every bridgeless cubic graph admits a
                                           P -coloring.

                                                                                                1
    For nearly four decades, the Petersen Coloring Conjecture has been a central benchmark
in structural graph theory. It was intended to provide a unified explanation of the properties
of cycle and matching structures in cubic graphs by tracing them back to the Petersen graph.
    An analogous result for graph minors was recently settled in [12], ending a long-standing
research effort spanning several decades. Building upon the landmark works of Robertson,
Seymour, and Thomas [31, 32], along with Edwards et al. [8], this result completes the frame-
work arising from Tutte’s 3-edge-coloring conjecture [34], which asserted that any bridgeless
cubic graph lacking a Petersen minor is 3-edge-colorable.
    For cubic graphs, admitting a P -coloring is equivalent to admitting a cycle-continuous
map to the Petersen graph, that is, an edge-mapping under which the preimage of every cycle
is a cycle (see [6]). Hence, under Conjecture 1, the cycle space of the Petersen graph was
expected to be suﬀicient to describe the cycle space of every bridgeless cubic graph.
    The Petersen coloring conjecture lies at the core of the theories of flows and colorings of
graphs, which themselves share strong connections with matroid theory [14], mathematical
physics (such as zero-temperature states of spin models, see [1, 15]), polyhedral optimiza-
tion [7, 9, 33], and topology [11, 16].
    A positive answer to the Petersen coloring conjecture would have immediately solved
several other famous and long-standing problems in these areas. Most notably, it is well
known [14] that it implies both the Berge–Fulkerson Conjecture [9] (which asserts that every
bridgeless cubic graph contains six perfect matchings covering every edge exactly twice) and
the 5-Cycle Double Cover Conjecture [4, 28] (stating that every bridgeless graph admits a
cycle double cover consisting of at most five even subgraphs). As a result, the conjecture has
served as a cornerstone for understanding the edge-structure and cycle spaces of snarks and
regular graphs.
    However, this paradigm turned out to be false, as the recent discovery of counterexamples
demonstrates. The refutation of the conjecture opens up an entirely new landscape, revealing
a rich variety of cubic graphs whose cycle structure exhibits a level of complexity exceeding
that of the Petersen graph.
    A counterexample on 68 vertices to Conjecture 1, credited to GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra, was
posted on X in July 2026 by the account @NeuralReformist1 . Moreover, in August 2026,
with the use of an AI model, Putman [29, 30] provided two non-isomorphic counterexamples
on 112 vertices. However, these verifications remain strictly computational, and the mere
exhibition of such counterexamples provides no structural insight into why these graphs fail
to admit the required mapping.
    By studying these examples, we identified why they fail to admit a Petersen coloring. This
insight allowed us to construct counterexamples on just 52 vertices, for which we provide a
complete, purely theoretical proof in Section 3.
    Crucially, uncovering these underlying reasons allows us to gain a deeper understanding
of the conjecture’s failure, opening directions for future research to investigate which of its
famous consequences, such as the Berge-Fulkerson Conjecture or the 5-Cycle Double Cover
Conjecture, might likewise be false or, conversely, remain valid.
    The counterexamples presented above are cyclically 4-edge-connected. Recall that a cubic
graph is cyclically k-edge-connected if no set of fewer than k edges disconnects it into two
components, each containing a cycle. While applying the standard 3-sum operation to a
3-edge-connected counterexample easily yields an infinite family of such graphs, a natural
question is whether there exist infinitely many cyclically 4-edge-connected graphs without a
Petersen coloring. In Section 4, we construct such a family for every even order of at least 60,
leaving the existence of a cyclically 5-edge-connected counterexample as an open problem.
    Finally, in Section 5, we show that our counterexamples answer related open problems

2     Two relevant multipoles
A multipole M is a structure consisting of a vertex set V (M ) and an edge set E(M ), where
every edge has two ends, each of which is either incident to a vertex in V (M ) or left unattached.
An end of an edge that is not incident to any vertex is called a free end. An edge with exactly
one free end and one end incident to a vertex is referred to as a dangling edge. A multipole
possessing exactly k free ends is called a k-pole. The set of dangling edges in a multipole, say
d1 , d2 , . . . , dk , is endowed with a linear order which can be specified as M (d1 , d2 , . . . , dk ). If
the order of the dangling edges is not important, we simply write M . A k-pole is said to be
cubic if every vertex in V (M ) has degree exactly 3 (counting both edges and dangling edges
incident to it).
     A P -coloring of a cubic multipole M is a map φ : E(M ) → E(P ) such that for every vertex
v of M , there is a vertex wv of P satisfying φ(∂M (v)) = ∂P (wv ), where ∂G (u) denotes the set
of edges and dangling edges incident to the vertex u in G. In the following, we construct cubic
graphs by combining specific multipoles while controlling the restrictions that any Petersen
coloring induces on their dangling edges.
     For a k-pole M (d1 , . . . , dk ), we define its P -coloring set as

            P -Col(M ) = {(φ(d1 ), φ(d2 ), . . . , φ(dk )) : φ is a Petersen coloring of M }.

    To combine multipoles, we use the following standard operation: given two dangling edges
d1 and d2 belonging to disjoint multipoles and incident to vertices u and v respectively, to
join d1 and d2 means to remove both dangling edges and replace them with a single edge uv.

ordered partition of the multiset of edges {φ(r1 ), φ(r2 ), φ(b1 ), φ(b2 ), φ(e)} into the tuple of

    It is worth highlighting the formal distinction between the configuration of a coloring φ
and the P -coloring set P -Col(F ). While P -Col(F ) is defined as a set of strictly ordered tuples
(φ(r1 ), φ(r2 ), φ(b1 ), φ(b2 ), φ(e)), a configuration abstracts away certain structural symmetries

formally understood as a quotient of P -Col(F ) under an equivalence relation, where two
tuples yield the same configuration if they are the same up to switching the first two entries
(permuting the red edges r1 and r2 ) or switching the third and fourth entries (permuting the
blue edges b1 and b2 ).

       edge in φ(Bi+2 ). Since no configuration has such a property, the choice of C7 cannot be
       completed by any configuration on Fi+2 , and C7 cannot appear as a configuration of φ.

    • Elimination of C1 , C2 , C3 , C4 , C5 : Configurations C1 , C2 , C3 , C4 and C5 all require φ(Bi )
      to be a double edge. A double edge in φ(Bi ) must induce a double edge in φ(Ri+1 ) in
      Fi+1 . Since C7 is the only configuration that generates such a double red edge, by the
      previous point all such configurations are also impossible.

    • Elimination of C6 : We prove that configuration C6 for Fi cannot be followed by
      another configuration C6 for Fi+1 . Suppose that Fi has configuration C6 , the multiset
      φ(Bi ) consists of two distinct edges in P sharing a vertex x. The green edge φ(ei ) is
      not adjacent to either of them. By T1, φ(Bi ) = φ(Ri+1 ) , which forces φ(ei+1 ) to be
      one of the two edges in φ(Bi ) and thus not adjacent to φ(ei ), contradicting T2.

    Since no sequence of configurations is admissible for F1 , F2 , F3 , G does not admit a Petersen
coloring.

    By switching two suitable edges of the graph considered so far (see Figure 8), we obtain
a new example of a cyclically 4-edge-connected cubic graph of order 52 without a Petersen
coloring. The switch only changes the pairing of the two edges joining B2 to R3 ; therefore
the multiset identity φ(Bi ) = φ(Ri+1 ) remains valid. Hence, T1 and T2, and consequently
the entire proof, are unchanged.

5     Further results and open problems
In this section, we present further results and pose several open problems.

5.1     Smallest possible counterexample
The first very natural open problem is the following.
Problem 9. What is the smallest bridgeless cubic graph that does not admit a P -coloring?
   Following the terminology introduced by Brinkmann et al. [2], we define a weak snark
as a cyclically 4-edge-connected cubic graph that is not 3-edge-colorable, while a snark is
a weak snark of girth at least 5. It is well-known that a smallest counterexample to the
Petersen Coloring Conjecture must be a weak snark. It was checked in [2] that every weak
snark on at most 34 vertices and every snark on at most 36 vertices admits a P -coloring.
The first and third authors subsequently completed the order-36 case in a joint paper with
Škoviera [10] by also generating and testing the weak snarks of girth 4 on 36 vertices, and
showed that Conjecture 1 has no counterexamples on at most 36 vertices. Consequently,
every counterexample to Conjecture 1 has at least 38 vertices. Recently, Brinkmann and

5.3      Normal colorings
Recall that a normal k-edge-coloring of a cubic graph G is a proper edge-coloring c : E(G) →
{1, . . . , k} such that for every edge uv ∈ E(G), we have |{c(e) : e ∈ ∂G (u) ∪ ∂G (v)}| ∈ {3, 5}.
Jaeger [13] proved that a cubic graph G admits a P -coloring if and only if G has a normal
5-edge-coloring. The normal chromatic index of a graph G, denoted by χ′N (G), is defined as
the minimum integer k such that G admits a normal k-edge-coloring. Consequently, any coun-
terexample G to the Petersen Coloring Conjecture satisfies χ′N (G) > 5. We computationally
verified that both of our counterexamples of order 52 admit a strong normal 6-edge-coloring
(i.e., a normal 6-edge-coloring where |{c(e) : e ∈ ∂G (u) ∪ ∂G (v)}| = 5 for every uv ∈ E(G))
and the same holds for the 68-vertex and the two 112-vertex counterexamples. This implies
that the normal chromatic index of all of these graphs is exactly 6.
     Furthermore, it was proved by Mazzuoccolo and Mkrtchyan [24] that every simple cubic
graph admits a normal 7-edge-coloring.3 but no examples of bridgeless cubic graphs requiring
7 colors are currently known.
     The following conjecture remains open; it is attributed to Šámal and was also explicitly
stated in [23].
Conjecture 12. Let G be a bridgeless cubic graph. Then, χ′N (G) ≤ 6.
    Conjecture 12 serves as a natural relaxation of Conjecture 1 in the context of normal
edge-colorings.
    Let G be a graph and c an edge-coloring of G. An edge uv of G is called abnormal if

Question 13 ([21, Question 3.1]). Assume that a bridgeless cubic graph G admits a proper 5-

   We computationally verified that the two 52-vertex, one 68-vertex, and two 112-vertex

exactly two abnormal edges, so they provide a negative answer to the above question.

5.4      Family of graphs coloring every bridgeless cubic graph
If G and H are graphs, a well-known and natural generalization of P -coloring of G is the
concept of an H-coloring of G, that is a proper edge-coloring φ : E(G) → E(H) such that for
every vertex v ∈ V (G) there is wv ∈ V (H) with φ(∂G (v)) = ∂H (wv ). In such a case we say
that H colors G.
    Let H be an inclusion-wise minimal set of connected cubic graphs such that every con-
nected bridgeless cubic graph G is colored by some H ∈ H. At this point, one might ask what
such a set H looks like.
    Let S4 be the graph obtained as follows: start with a 3-cycle xyz, add a second parallel
edge between y and z, and add a new vertex w connected to x. By combining results of [17],
[25] and [26], a graph H colors every connected bridgeless cubic graph if and only if H contains
S4 as a subgraph, which we call an S4 -graph. Note that S4 contains a bridge.

      For the subclass of bridgeless cubic graphs, this is simply a reformulation of Jaeger’s 8-flow theorem,

     As a consequence, a set H containing an S4 -graph H is inclusion-wise minimal if and only
if it contains H only. By a result of [17], the Petersen graph can only be colored by itself
or by an S4 -graph. Then, H has cardinality 1 if and only if it contains an S4 -graph. We
believe that the following could be an interesting question: are there inclusion-wise minimal
sets H of finite cardinality greater than 1? Note that because such a set cannot contain an
S4 -graph, it must necessarily contain the Petersen graph itself to ensure that the Petersen
graph is colored.
     On the other hand, if we restrict our scope to bridgeless graphs, let Hb be an inclusion-wise
minimal set of connected bridgeless cubic graphs that colors every connected bridgeless cubic
graph. By Theorem 1.1 of [19], Hb is infinite. Furthermore, Theorem 1.2 of [19] implies that
Hb is unique, and its elements are precisely those bridgeless cubic graphs that are colorable
only by themselves.
     Hence, it would be interesting to investigate whether our counterexamples of order 52 are
also colorable only by themselves. This question is of particular relevance, as it is naturally
tied to the question of whether they are the smallest bridgeless cubic graphs without a Petersen
coloring.

5.5   Further results on related problems
Recall that the perfect matching index π(G) of a graph G is the smallest number of perfect
matchings needed to cover all the edges of G. The Berge-Fulkerson Conjecture is equivalent
to the assertion that π(G) ≤ 5 for every bridgeless cubic graph G (see [22]).
    We computationally checked that the Berge-Fulkerson Conjecture holds for the two coun-
terexamples of order 52 and the 68-vertex and two 112-vertex counterexamples. The same
conclusion follows from the proof of the main result in [20] by observing that all such examples
contain many 4-edge-cuts.
    In fact, they even have perfect matching index 4. In this regard, Hakobyan and Mkrtchyan
[18] made the following conjecture.

Conjecture 14 (Hakobyan and Mkrtchyan [18, Conjecture 3.2]). Any cubic graph G with
π(G) ≤ 4 admits a P12 -coloring.

    Here, P12 is the cubic graph obtained by replacing a vertex of the Petersen graph P by a
triangle. We remark that our counterexamples provide a negative answer to this conjecture
as well. Indeed, since P12 admits a P -coloring, our graphs do not admit a P12 -coloring either.
    Given that such graphs exhibit such a highly specific behavior disproving the Petersen
and P12 -coloring conjectures, while satisfying the Berge-Fulkerson conjecture, it is natural to
investigate how they behave with respect to the Cycle Double Cover Conjecture. Recently,
OpenAI posted a preprint [27] in which they proved that every bridgeless graph admits a
cycle double cover with eight even subgraphs. It is then natural to ask whether the proposed
counterexamples to the Petersen Coloring satisfy the more restrictive 5-Cycle Double Cover
Conjecture. Indeed, we computationally checked that the above-mentioned two 52-vertex, one
68-vertex, and two 112-vertex graphs do admit a cycle double cover with five even subgraphs.
