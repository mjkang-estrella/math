                                                   Abstract. For any pair of orientable closed hyperbolic 3–manifolds, this paper shows that any
                                                   isomorphism between the profinite completions of their fundamental groups witnesses a bijec-
                                                   tive correspondence between the Zariski dense PSL(2, Qac )–representations of their fundamental
                                                   groups, up to conjugacy; moreover, corresponding pairs of representations have identical invari-

                                                   closure of Q.) Next, assuming the p–adic Borel regulator injectivity conjecture for number fields,
                                                   this paper shows that uniform lattices in PSL(2, C) with isomorphic profinite completions have
                                                   identical invariant trace fields, isomorphic invariant quaternion algebras, identical covolume, and
                                                   identical arithmeticity.




                                                                                         1. Introduction
                                              Hyperbolic 3–manifolds of finite volume have abundant finite covers, and have strong rigidity.
                                           The latter fact opens the door for studying fundamental groups of those manifolds by means of
                                           geometry and arithmetics. The hyperbolic volume, the invariant trace field, and the invariant
                                           quaternion algebra are among the most important invariants, not only in theory, but also in prac-
                                           tical computation. Arithmetic hyperbolic 3–manifolds and non-arithmetic ones exhibit contrast
                                           behaviors in many group theoretic aspects. The former fact, in addition, makes it reasonable to
                                           ask if the fundamental group could possibly be inferred from knowledge of its finite quotients. This
                                           challenging question has attracted growing interest over the past decade. Its pertaining topic is
                                           called profinite properties of 3–manifold groups.
                                              For any (abstract) group G, the profinite completion of G refers to the group G     b = lim G/N,
                                                                                                                                      ←−N
                                           where N ranges over the inverse system of all finite-index normal subgroups of G. When G is finitely
                                           generated and residually finite, the completion homomorphism G → G    b is injective, and the profinite
                                           topology on G  b can be characterized purely in abstract group theoretic terms [NikS07]; moreover,
                                           the group isomorphism type of G    b can be recovered purely from the set of isomorphism classes
                                           of all the finite quotient groups of G [Rei18, Theorem 2.2]. These facts make finitely generated
                                           residually finite groups arguably the most comfortable class for speaking of profinite properties.
                                           There are some smaller classes of groups, restricted to which one might expect richer harvest.
                                           Finitely generated virtually nilpotent groups, semisimple Lie group lattices, and finitely generated
                                           3–manifold groups are a few candidates on top of our mind.

                                           rigidity have been confirmed recently by M. R. Bridson, D. B. McReynolds, A. W. Reid, and

there are examples with profinite rigidity [BriMRS20, BriRW17, BriR20], and also examples without
profinite rigidity [Fun13, Hem14, Ste72]. However, all known examples satisfy a weaker condition,
which we call profinite almost rigidity. This condition says that, among finitely generated 3–
manifold groups, the isomorphism type of the group is determined up to finitely many possibilities
by the isomorphism of the profinite completion. G. Wilkes shows that graph manifold groups are
profinitely almost rigid [Wlk17, Wlk18, Wlk19]. In a recent work [Liu23], the author shows that
finite-volume hyperbolic 3–manifold groups are profinitely almost rigid. It seems plausible that
finitely generated 3–manifold groups are all profinitely almost rigid. On the other hand, we recall
the following question raised by A. W. Reid [Rei15, Question 9], which remains widely open. Its
torsion-free case is equivalent to the (unknown) profinite rigidity among orientable finite-volume
hyperbolic 3–manifolds.
Question 1.1. If a pair of lattices in PSL(2, C) have isomorphic profinite completions, must they
be isomorphic to each other?
    The present paper may be viewed as a continuation of [Liu23] and an attempt toward Question
1.1. We seek for a connection between dynamics of pseudo-Anosov suspension flow and arithmetic
invariants of SL(2, C)–representations. In the end, we are able to use this connection, and reduce the
profinite invariance problem of hyperbolic volume and arithmeticity to a long-standing conjecture
of number theoretic nature, that is, the p–adic Borel regulator injectivity conjecture for number
fields.
    For any number field k with r1 real places and r2 complex places, the zeroth K-group K0 (Ok )
of the ring of integers Ok can be identified with the ideal class group of Ok direct sum with Z, and
the first K-group K1 (Ok ) can be identified with the multiplicative unit group Ok× . In particular,
the real dimension of Kn (Ok ) ⊗Z R equals 1 for n = 0, by the class number finiteness, and equals
r1 + r2 − 1 for n = 1, by the Dirichlet unit theorem. A. Borel shows that the rank of higher K-
groups obeys periodicity, and indeed, when n ≥ 2, the real dimension of Kn (k)⊗Z R ∼   = Kn (Ok )⊗Z R
equals 0, r1 + r2 , 0, r2 for n ≡ 0, 1, 2, 3 modulo 4, respectively. For the nonvanishing dimensions,
the classical Borel regulator maps associated to k can be reinterpreted as R–linear homomorphisms
K4m+1 (k) ⊗Z R → Rr1 ⊕ Cr2 and K4m−1 (k) ⊗Z R → Cr2 , which are actually injective onto Rr1 ⊕ Rr2
and Rr2 , respectively. For any rational prime p, the p–adic Borel regulator maps associated to k can
be defined analogously, and can be thought of as Qp –linear homomorphisms K2m−1 (k) ⊗Z Qp →

Conjecture 1.2. For any number field k and any rational prime p, the p–adic Borel regulator map
is injective on K2m−1 (k) ⊗Z Qp for every odd dimension 2m − 1 ≥ 3.
Theorem 1.3. If Conjecture 1.2 holds true, then, among uniform lattices in PSL(2, C), the follow-
ing lattice invariants are all determined by the group isomorphism type of the profinite completion:
the invariant trace field in C, the conjugacy class of the invariant quaternion algebra in Mat(2, C),
the covolume, and arithmeticity.

Theorem 1.4. If a pair of orientable closed hyperbolic 3–manifolds have profinitely isomorphic
fundamental groups, then the Zariski dense PSL(2, Qac )–representations of their fundamental groups
can be paired up bijectively up to conjugacy. Moreover, corresponding pairs of representations
have identical invariant trace fields in Qac , and have conjugate invariant quaternion algebras in
Mat(2, Qac ).
   The notation Qac means an algebraic closure of Q. Note that Theorem 1.4 does not rely on
Conjecture 1.2. Theorem 1.4 is a main step toward Theorem 1.3. See Theorem 11.1 in Section
11 for the precise statement; an effective description of the correspondence can be found in the
beginning paragraph of the proof of Theorem 11.1.
   Theorem 1.4 is inspired by [BriMRS20, Theorem 4.8]. The Zariski denseness assumption is
needed for legitimating the invariant quaternion algebra. What we are able to get rid of is the
Galois rigidity assumption from [BriMRS20, Theorem 4.8]. The basic reason is that we employ
a bijective correspondence between periodic trajectories for profinitely isomorphic pseudo-Anosov
mapping tori, as established in [Liu23].
   As one should be warned, Theorem 1.4 does not tell whether the discrete faithful representations
(or their Galois conjugates) are paired up by the asserted correspondence. Nor does [BriMRS20,
Theorem 4.8]. In fact, the desired injectivity in [BriMRS20] is eventually obtained using the Hopfian
property of topologically finitely generated profinite groups, and moreover, the argument thereof
appeals to concrete features of particular lattices, such as lists of their small index subgroups. In
the proof of Theorem 1.3, we are only able to infer from Conjecture 1.2 that discrete faithfulness
should be preserved under the correspondence, and this is a consequence of the volume rigidity for
SL(2, C)–representations of uniform PSL(2, C)–lattices (see Example 2.1).

algebraic K-theoretic versions in Remarks 2.7 and 2.9. We could have placed this section right
before Section 10 without affecting the logical order, but we decide to place it here as background

3.2. Pseudo-Anosov automorphisms. Let S be an orientable connected closed surface of genus
≥ 2. A pseudo-Anosov automorphism f : S → S refers to an orientation-preserving homeomorphism
with the following property: There exist measured foliations (F s , µs ) and (F u , µu ) on S, having
identical singular points and transverse anywhere else, such that f · (F u , µu ) = (F u , λµu ) and
f · (F s , µs ) = (F s , λ−1 µs ) hold for some constant λ > 1.
   We recall that a measured foliation on S is a foliation F with prong singularities together with
a transverse measure µ that is invariant under the holonomy of F . A prong singularity is modeled
on the singularity of a holomorphic quadratic differential z k−2 dz 2 on C, where k = 3, 4, 5, · · · is
called the prong number. (See [FatLP12, Exposé 1].) The constant λ is called the stretch factor.
The measured foliations (F s , µs ) and (F u , µu ) are called the stable and the unstable measured
foliations, respectively. They all uniquely determined by f . Moreover, any other pseudo-Anosov
automorphism in the isotopy class of f is a conjugate of f by a self-homeomorphism of S that is
isotopic to the identity, (see [FatLP12, Exposé 12]).

                     4. Periodic trajectories and arithmetic invariants
   In this section, we establish two criteria regarding irreducible SL(2, C)–representations of fun-
damental groups of pseudo-Anosov mapping tori (Lemmas 4.1 and 4.3). These criteria relate the
trace field and the ramification type of the quaternion algebra (in the real case) with character
values on the periodic trajectories.
   For any group π, an irreducible representation ρ : π → SL(2, C) is a group homomorphism such
that ρ(π) preserves no nontrivial invariant subspace in C2 [CulS83, Section 1.2]. In this case, the
trace field of ρ refers to the subfield of C generated by {tr(ρ(g)) : g ∈ π} over Q, and the quaternion
algebra of ρ refers to the subalgebra of Mat(2, C) generated by {ρ(g) : g ∈ π} over the trace field,
which is indeed a quaternion algebra. Note that if the trace field of ρ contained in R, then the
completion of the quaternion algebra of ρ is a quaternion algebra over R. (See also Lemma 8.2 and
Definition 8.3 in Section 8 for justificaiton of the terminology in a slightly more general setting.)
Lemma 4.1. Let S be an orientable connected surface of genus ≥ 2. Let f : S → S be a pseudo-

   Then, any value of χρ on π1 (Mf ) lies in the Z–subalgebra of C generated by the values of χρ
on the set of periodic trajectories. In particular, the trace field of ρ is the field of fractions of that
subalgebra.

Proof. The real complete quaternion algebra is isomorphic to either Hamilton’s quaternion algebra
H or the real 2 × 2–matrix algebra Mat(2, R), (see Example 3.1). The elements of norm 1 form a
real Lie group, isomorphic to SU(2) or SL(2, R), respectively. The natural inclusion of the image
of ρ into the real complete quaternion algebra can be regarded as a representation of π in SU(2)
or SL(2, R), which has the same character as χρ . Therefore, the conjugacy characterizations in the
dichotomy follow from the well-known fact that irreducible SL(2, C)–representations are uniquely
determined by their characters up to conjugacy [CulS83, Proposition 1.5.2].
    Recall any matrix A ∈ SL(2, R) other than ±I falls into one of three types, according to the sign
of its discriminant tr(A)2 − 4: elliptic if negative, parabolic if zero, or hyperbolic if positive. In
the hyperbolic case, |tr(An )| grows exponentially as n tends to ∞. On the other hand, |tr(A)| ≤ 2
holds for any matrix A ∈ SU(2). With these facts, the proof of Lemma 4.3 essentially reduces to
the following claim: For any representation ρ : π1 (Mf ) → SL(2, R) that is irreducible in SL(2, C),
there exists some periodic trajectory which is, up to conjugacy, represented by a matrix of hyperbolic
type.

normal subgroup π ′ = π1 (M ′ ), which corresponds to a connected regular finite cover M ′ → Mf .
We can identify M ′ as the mapping torus Mf ′ of a pseudo-Anosov automorphism f ′ : S ′ → S ′ ,
where S ′ is a preimage component in M ′ of the distingushed fiber S in Mf , and where f ′ is the
return map of the lifted suspension flow on M ′ . The restriction of ρ to π ′ becomes a representation
ρ′ : π1 (Mf ′ ) → SL(2, R). As ρ is irreducible in SL(2, C), ρ′ is also irreducible in SL(2, C). (This
follows from the fact that a subgroup in SL(2, R) has a fixed point in C ∪ {∞} if and only if it has
a finite orbit in C ∪ {∞}.) Therefore, we can deduce the general case from the torsion-free case by
arguing with f ′ : S ′ → S ′ and ρ′ .

                              8. Representations in quaternions
   In this section, we introduce some working terminology regarding group representations in an
abstract quaternion algebra. Most of the materials below are well-known in the case of SL(2, C)–
representations. The main purpose of this section is to justify the formulation of results in Sections
9 and 10.
Definition 8.1. Let F be any (abstract) field of characteristic 0, and A be a quaternion algebra
over F . For any group π, we call any group homomorphism ρ : π → SL(1, A ) a (special linear or
unimodular) representation of π in A . Here, SL(1, A ) denotes the normal subgroup of GL(1, A ) =
A × which consists of the quaternions of norm 1. The character of ρ refers to the π–conjugacy
invariant function χρ : π → F , defined as χρ (g) = TrA /F (ρ(g)) for all g ∈ π.
Lemma 8.2. With A /F as in Definition 8.1, let π be a group and ρ : π → SL(1, A ) be a repre-
sentation. Then the following conditions are all equivalent.
    (1) For some (hence any) field extension E of F over which A ⊗F E splits as a matrix algebra
        Mat(2, E), the induced representation ρE : π → SL(2, E) is irreducible over E, that is, ρE
        has no proper E–linear invariant subspace in E 2 other than {0}.
    (2) The image of ρ generates A as an algebra over F .
    (3) The inequality χρ ([g, h]) 6= 2 holds for some g, h ∈ π, where [g, h] = ghg −1 h−1 denotes the
        commutator.
Proof. The equivalence (1) ⇔ (3) is well-known. We refer to [CulS83, Lemma 1.2.1 and Corollary
1.2.2] for a standard proof. The implication (2) ⇒ (1) is obvious (see Example 3.1). It remains to
prove the implication (1) ⇒ (2), or essentially, the following (well-known) claim in linear algebra: If
E is an algebraically closed field of characteristic 0, then any proper E–subalgebra B of Mat(2, E)
preserves some 1–dimensional E–linear subspace in E 2 .
   When dimE B < 3, the claim is obvious, since B is central, and hence commutative. When
dimE B = 3, we take a basis I, X, Y ∈ Mat(2, E) of B over E, where I is the identity matrix.
Possibly after a generic modification of X within B, we may require det(X) 6= 0 and tr(X)2 − 4 ·
det(X) 6= 0. So, possibly after conjugating B in Mat(2, E), we may assume that X is diagonal with
distinct diagonal entries. It follows that Y has some nonzero off-diagonal entries. If the upper and
lower off-diagonal entries of Y were both nonzero, we observe that XY and Y would be linearly
independent. Moreover, the matrices I, X, Y, XY would span Mat(2, E) over E, contradicting the
assumption dimE B = 3. This means Y is either upper triangular or lower triangular. Therefore,
B is conjugate to a subalgebra of triangular matrices in Mat(2, E). It preserves a 1–dimensional
E–linear subspace in E 2 , as claimed.                                                               
Definition 8.3. With A /F as in Definition 8.1, we say that a representation ρ : π → SL(1, A ) is
irreducible if ρ satisfies any of the equivalent conditions as in Lemma 8.2.
Lemma 8.4. With A /F as in Definition 8.1, let π be a finitely generated group and ρ : π →
SL(1, A ) be an irreducible representation. If ρ̃ : π → SL(1, A ) is a representation with character
χρ̃ = χρ on π, then ρ̃ is conjugate to ρ in GL(1, A ) = A × , namely, ρ̃ = τ ◦ ρ for some inner
automorphism τ of A × . The converse also holds true.

Proof. We only prove the main assertion, as the converse is obvious. Since π is finitely generated, we
may assume F finitely generated over Q. We fix a field embedding of F into C, and an isomorphism
A ⊗F C ∼   = Mat(2, C). Since the induced irreducible SL(2, C)–representations ρC and ρ̃C of the
finitely generated group π have identical characters, they are conjugate in GL(2, C), and indeed,
conjugate in SL(2, C) [CulS83, Proposition 1.5.2].
   Take a finite generating set g1 , · · · , gr of π. We set up r equations in A with an unknown T ,
namely, T · ρ(gi ) = ρ̃(gi ) · T for all i ∈ {1, · · · , r}. It suffices to show that there exists some solution
T ∈ A with NrA /F (T ) 6= 0. To this end, we take a basis X0 , X1 , X2 , X3 of A over F , and write
T = u0 · X0 + u1 · X1 + u2 · X2 + u3 · X3 . Then the above equations becomes a linear system of
4r equations over F in the unknowns u0 , u1 , u2 , u3 . The F –solution space V of these equations
is a linear subspace in A over F , and the C–solution space is the scalar extension V ⊗F C in
Mat(2, C). Moreover, the norm NrA /F on A restricts to a F –quadratic form on V , which agrees
with the determinant function on Mat(2, C) under our identification. Since ρC and ρ̃C are conjugate

some solution T in GL(1, A ) = A × as desired.                                                                 
   Recall that the 2–Frattini subgroup of any group π is by definition the characteristic subgroup
generated by the commutators and the squares of elements in π. In fact, the squares of elements
already form a generating set. (This is not true in general for Frattini subgroups associated with
other primes p.) In this paper, we denote the 2–Frattini subgroup of any group π as Fratt2 (π).
Lemma 8.5. With A /F as in Definition 8.1, let π be a group and ρ : π → SL(1, A ) be a repre-
sentation. Then the following conditions are all equivalent.
    (1) For some (hence any) algebraically closed field extension E of F over which A ⊗F E splits
        as a matrix algebra Mat(2, E), the induced representation ρE : π → SL(2, E) is Zariski
        dense, that is, the image of ρ is dense in the linear algebraic group SL(2, E) over E with
        respect to the Zariski topology.
    (2) For every finite-index normal subgroup π ′ of π, the representation ρ′ : π ′ → SL(1, A ) re-
        stricting ρ to π ′ is irreducible.
    (3) The image of ρ is infinite, and the representation ρ♮ : Fratt2 (π) → SL(1, A ) restricting ρ
        to the 2–Frattini subgroup is irreducible.
Proof. For any algebraically closed field E, the Zariski closure G of the image of ρ(π) is a Zariski
closed subgroup in SL(2, E) [Hum75, Chapter II, Proposition 7.4.A]. If ρ′ : π ′ → SL(1, A ) is re-
ducible for some finite-index subgroup π ′ of π, the Zariski closure G′ of ρ′ (π ′ ) in SL(2, E) can be
conjugated into the upper-triangular subgroup by some element of SL(2, E) (Lemma 8.2), while G′
has finite-index in G. Then G is not Zariski dense as it has dimension at most 2 in SL(2, E). Con-
versely, if G is not Zariski dense in SL(2, E), the connected component Go (that is, the irreducible
component of G as an algebraic variety) has finite index in G. Moreover, the Lie algebra g of G has
dimension at most 2 over E [Hum75, Chapter III, Theorem 9.1], so g must be solvable (see [Hum78,
Chapter I, Section 1.4], and can be conjugated into the upper-triangular Lie subalgebra sl(2, E),
since E is algebraically closed of characteristic 0 [Hum78, Chapter II, Section 4.1]. In turn, this
implies that Go can be conjugated into the upper-triangular subgroup of SL(2, E), within GL(2, E)
and hence also within SL(2, E) [Hum75, Chapter V, Theorem 13.1]. This means that the restricted
representation ρ′ : π ′ → SL(1, A ) is reducible on the finite-index subgroup π ′ = G0 ∩π of π (Lemma
8.2). This proves the equivalence (1) ⇔ (2).
   If ρE is Zarisk dense, the Zariski closure of ρE (Fratt2 (π)) is an infinite, normal, Zariski closed
subgroup of SL(2, E), which has to be SL(2, E) itself. It follows that ρ has infinite image and ρ♮

is irreducible. On the other hand, if ρE is not Zariski dense, there exists a finite-index normal
subgroup π ′ , such that the restricted representation ρ′ : π ′ → SL(1, A ) is reducible. Therefore,
ρE (π ′ ) fixes some points on the projective line EP1 over E, on which SL(2, E) acts by projective
E–linear transformations (Lemma 8.2). When ρE (π ′ ) fixes only one or two points on EP1 , we
observe that ρE (π) stabilizes the fixed point set. In this case, ρ♮ is reducible, since ρE (Fratt2 (π))
fixes those fixed points of ρE (π ′ ) on EP1 . When ρE (π ′ ) fixes at least three points on EP1 , it fixes
EP1 , so ρE (π ′ ) is contained in the center {±I} of SL(2, E). In this case, ρ has finite image, since
π ′ has finite index in π. This proves the equivalence (2) ⇔ (3).                                        

Lemma 8.14. With A /F as in Definition 8.1, let π be a group and ρ : π → SL(1, A ) be an

   In this section, we prove the main results as mentioned in the introduction. Theorem 11.1 is the
precise form of Theorem 1.4, and Theorem 11.2 is the precise form of Theorem 1.3.
   Let Qac be an algebraic closure of Q. For any group π, a representation ρ : π → PSL(2, Qac ) is said
to be Zariski dense if its image is Zariski dense in the Qac –algebraic group PSL(2, Qac ) ∼
                                                                                           = SO(3, Qac ).
In this case, the invariant trace field and the invariant quaternion algebra of ρ refer to the invariant
trace field in Qac and the invariant quaternion algebra in Mat(2, Qac ) of the preimage of ρ(π) in
SL(2, Qac ), which is a central extension of ρ(π) by {±I}. (See Remark 8.9.)
Theorem 11.1. Let πA and πB be fundamental groups of orientable closed hyperbolic 3–manifolds.
Suppose that Ψ : πbA → πbB is a group isomorphism between the profinite completions. Let Qac be an
algebraic closure of Q. Denote by YA the set of conjugacy classes of the Zariski dense PSL(2, Qac )–
representations of πA , and similarly YB with πB .
   Then, there is a bijective map ηΨ : YB → YA , depending only on πA , πB , and Ψ. Moreover,
if ρA : πA → PSL(2, Qac ) and ρB : πB → PSL(2, Qac ) are Zariski dense representations of ηΨ –

Ψ–pullback of ρB,σ restricted to πA is conjugate to the the induced representation ρA,σ of some
Zariski dense representation ρA : πA → PSL(2, Qac ), whose conjugacy class does not depend on
particular choices of p or σ. With this claim, ηΨ is constructed as mapping the conjugacy class
[ρB ] ∈ YB to [ρA ] ∈ YA .
   The claim can be derived quickly from Lemma 9.1, but we need two basic observations with
PSL(2, Qac )–representations of aspherical closed 3–manifold groups (or more generally, finitely gen-

   First, we observe that every representation ρ : π → PSL(2, Qac ) of an aspherical closed 3–manifold
group π admits a lift ρ♯ : Fratt2 (π) → SL(2, Qac ) restricted to the 2–Frattini subgroup. In fact, the
