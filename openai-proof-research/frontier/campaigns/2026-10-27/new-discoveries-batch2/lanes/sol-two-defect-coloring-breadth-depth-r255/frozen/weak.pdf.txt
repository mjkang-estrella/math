                                                            ABELIAN GROUPS

                                                   Abstract. A matching from a finite subset A ⊂ Zn to another subset
                                                   B ⊂ Zn is a bijection f : A → B with the property that a + f (a) never
                                                   lies in A. A matching is called acyclic if it is uniquely determined by its
                                                   multiplicity function. Alon et al. established the acyclic matching prop-
                                                   erty for Zn , which was later extended to all abelian torsion-free groups.
                                                   In a prior work, the authors of this paper settled the acyclic matching
                                                   property for all abelian groups. The objective of this note is to explore
                                                   a related concept, known as the weak acyclic matching property, within
                                                   the context of abelian groups.




                                                                             1. Introduction
                                             Let (G, +) denote an abelian group. Consider nonempty finite subsets
                                          A, B ⊆ G. A matching from A to B is defined as a bijection f : A → B
                                          satisfying the condition a + f (a) ∈
                                                                             / A for every a ∈ A. When such a matching
                                          exists from A to B, we say that A is matched to B. This concept, introduced
                                          by Fan and Losonczy in [8], serves as a tool in exploring Wakeford’s classical
                                          problem concerning canonical forms for symmetric tensors [14]. It is evident
                                          that the existence of a matching from A to B requires |A| = |B| and 0 ∈/ B.
                                             An abelian group G possesses the matching property if the aforementioned
                                          conditions on A and B suffice to ensure the existence of a matching from A to
                                          B. Losonczy addressed the question of which groups possess this property in
                                          [13], stating:
                                          Theorem 1.1. An abelian group G possesses the matching property if and only
                                          if G is torsion-free or cyclic of prime order.
                                            For any matching f : A → B, the associated multiplicity function mf : G →
                                          Z≥0 is defined as follows:
                                                              ∀x ∈ G, mf (x) = |{a ∈ A : a + f (a) = x}|.
                                              A matching f : A → B is termed acyclic if for any matching g : A → B,
                                          mf = mg implies f = g. If an acyclic matching from A to B exists, we say that
                                          A is acyclically matched to B. A group G exhibits the acyclic matching property
                                          if for any pair of subsets A and B in G with |A| = |B| and 0 ∈
                                                                                                       / B, there is at least

   Acyclic matchings for specific sets: When discussing matchings be-
tween two sets A and B within G, two scenarios represent the extremes of the
spectrum in terms of the number of matchings:
    (1) A + B = A: In this case A is not matched to B. This case is treated
        in [3, Lemma 2.3] where it is proved that in this situation B must be
        a subgroup of G, and A is a coset of B.
    (2) A ∩ (A + B) = ∅: In this case, not only is A matched to B but every
        bijection from A to B constitutes a matching, resulting in the maximum
        number of matchings.
Motivated by the preceding discussion, the acyclic matching problem arises
for sets A and B where A ∩ (A + B) = ∅. Intuitively, the situation might
be complex when A ∩ (A + B) = ∅ because there could be as many as |A|!
matchings, increasing the probability of at least one acyclic matching existing.
On the other hand, the number of matchings may grow faster than the number
of multiplicity functions, so the average number of matchings per multiplicity
function may be large reducing the probability of at least one acyclic matching
existing. The following special cases of acyclic matching were proved in [1]:
Theorem 1.3. Let A be a subset of the cyclic group Z/pZ where p is a prime
number. Suppose A satisfies A ∩ 2A = ∅ where 2A = {2a | a ∈ A}and A is of
size k with k · 2k−1 < p. Then A is acyclically matched to itself via the identity
map.
Theorem 1.4. Let A and B be subsets of an abelian group G. Suppose A and
B are of the same size satisfying A ∩ (A + B) = ∅. Then there exists an acyclic
matching f : A → B if B is a Sidon set. (That is, the equation x + y = z + w
has no solution in B with {x, y} ∩ {z, w} = ∅.)
  Following [2], we define an abelian group G to possess the weak acyclic
matching property if, for every finite nonempty subsets A and B of G with |A| =
|B| and A ∩ (A + B) = ∅, A is acyclically matched to B. In [2], the following
conjecture concerning the weak acyclic matching property was proposed.
        THE WEAK ACYCLIC MATCHING PROPERTY IN ABELIAN GROUPS                    3


Conjecture 1.5. There are infinitely many n for which Z/nZ has the weak
acyclic matching property.
  The following question was also raised in [1].
Question 1.6. Does Z/pZ possess the weak acyclic matching property for every
prime p?
  In this note, our main result, stated as Theorem 2.3, provides an affirmative
answer to Conjecture 1.5 and thoroughly addresses Question 1.6. Furthermore,
Theorem 2.3 classifies all abelian groups with respect to their adherence to the
weak acyclic matching property.
Remark 1.7. It is worth noting that while the matching theory developed in this
paper is related to the classical theory introduced by Philip Hall in 1935, the
two are conceptually distinct. In particular, to address a linear algebra problem
posed by Wakeford [14], Fan [8] constructed a bipartite graph G = (V (G), E(G))
based on two sets A, B ⊂ Zn , which may overlap. Each element a ∈ A is
assigned a symbol xa , and each b ∈ B a symbol yb , forming vertex sets
                  X = {xa : a ∈ A}    and Y = {yb : b ∈ B}.
The graph has vertex set V (G) = X ∪ Y , with an edge between xa ∈ X and
yb ∈ Y if and only if a + b ∈
                            / A.
   In graph-theoretic terms, a matching is a set of edges with no shared end-
points, and a perfect matching is one that covers all vertices. In the bipartite
graph associated with A and B, perfect matchings correspond to bijections
f : A → B such that a + f (a) ∈/ A for all a ∈ A. Wakeford’s question thus re-
duces to determining the existence of such perfect matchings, known as acyclic
matchings from A to B, discussed earlier.

                               2. Main results
   Multiplicity functions in a more general setting: We begin with a few
definitions. Let A, B and C be three nonempty sets. An operator ⊕ : A × B →
C is said to possess the left cancellation property provided:
                         a ⊕ b1 = a ⊕ b2 ⇐⇒ b1 = b2 ,
for every a ∈ A and b1 , b2 ∈ B.
It will be convenient to define also the operator ⊖ : C × A → B as an inverse
operation:
                            c ⊖ a = b ⇐⇒ a ⊕ b = c,
where a ∈ A, b ∈ B and c ∈ C.
Previously, we introduced a multiplicity function associated with a matching.
We may consider a similar definition of a multiplicity function associated with
a bijection. Let A, B and C be three nonempty sets. Let ⊕ : A × B → C be
an operator. Given a bijection f : A → B, the associated multiplicity function
mf : C → Z≥0 is defined as
                  ∀x ∈ C, mf (x) = |{a ∈ A : a ⊕ f (a) = x}|.
   In the following lemma, we obtain a bijection with a unique multiplicity
function.
4                           M. ALIABADI AND P. TAYLOR


Lemma 2.1. Let A, B be finite sets of the same cardinality. Let C be a set. Let
⊕ : A × B → C be an operator with the left cancellation property and also the
operator ⊖ as an inverse operation. Consider the set F of bijections f : A → B
with corresponding multiplicity functions mf (c) = |{a ∈ A : a ⊕ f (a) = c}|.
Then there exists an element f0 ∈ F which has a unique mf0 . That is, if g ∈ F
and mg = mf0 then g = f0 .
Proof. The proof begins by constructing such an f0 . Let C ′ = {a ⊕ b : (a, b) ∈
A × B} be the image of ⊕. Since A and B are finite, C ′ is also finite and we
can label its elements c1 , . . . , ck where k = |C ′ |.
    Let A1 = A, B1 = B. For each 1 ≤ j ≤ k, let A′j = {a ∈ Aj | ∃b ∈ Bj :
a ⊕ b = cj }. For each a ∈ A′j we assign f0 (a) = cj ⊖ a. For the next iteration
we define Aj+1 = Aj \ A′j and Bj+1 = Bj \ {cj ⊖ a : a ∈ A′j }.
    Since the paired elements of A and B are removed from the active subsets
Aj+1 , Bj+1 it is evident that no element is paired twice. Since |A| = |B| and
|A′j | = |Bj′ |, by induction we always have |Aj | = |Bj |. We claim that |Ak+1 | =
0. Suppose for contradiction that |Ak+1 | > 0. Then, we can arbitrarily choose
a′ ∈ Ak+1 and b′ ∈ Bk+1 . Since there exists j ≤ k for which a′ ⊕ b′ = cj ,
at least one of them should have been removed no later than at step j, which
leads to a contradiction. Hence |Ak+1 | = 0. Therefore f0 is a bijection A → B,
as claimed.
    We show the uniqueness of mf0 by assuming that g : A → B is a bijection
and proving the property
    (∀1 ≤ i < j : mg (ci ) = mf0 (ci )) =⇒ (∀1 ≤ i < j : ∀a ∈ A′i : g(a) = f0 (a))
by induction on j.
   When j = 1 the right-hand side of the implication is trivially true, giving
the base case.
   For the inductive step we note that, by construction, f0 maximizes mf0 (j)
subject to the assignments made in steps 1 to j − 1. Therefore if g agrees with
f0 on all the assignments made in those steps and mg (cj ) = mf0 (cj ), g must
make the same assignments as those made for f0 in step j. It cannot assign
an a which is not in Aj because it is already assigned; the values in Aj that
can potentially be assigned to yield cj are precisely those in A′j ; and each has
a unique b ∈ Bj (and, indeed, b ∈ B) to which it can be assigned in order
to yield cj , because of the left cancellation property. Therefore, this property
holds for all j, and we observe that when specialized to j = k + 1, it implies
that if mg = mf0 , then g = f0 .
                                                                               □
Example 2.2. We implement the construction described in Lemma 2.1 to find
the acyclic matching f0 : A → B, where A = {0, 1, 2, 7} and B = {3, 4, 9, 10}
are considered as subsets of Z/13Z with ⊕ as the usual group operation. Then
C ′ = {3, 4, 5, 6, 9, 10, 11, 12} and we can take the natural ordering.
    A1 = {0, 1, 2, 7}, B1 = {3, 4, 9, 10}, c1 = 3. We obtain A′1 = {0, 7} and
assign f0 (0) = 3 and f0 (7) = 9.
    A2 = {1, 2}, B2 = {4, 10}, c2 = 4. We obtain A′2 = ∅ and make no assign-
ments.
    A3 = {1, 2}, B3 = {4, 10}, c3 = 5. We obtain A′3 = {1} and assign f0 (1) = 4.
    For 4 ≤ j ≤ 8 we have Aj = {2}, Bj = {10}. Finally, with c8 = 12 we
obtain A′7 = {2} and assign f0 (2) = 10.

   In the following theorem, we will establish that all abelian groups possess
the weak acyclic matching property.
Theorem 2.3. Every abelian group possesses the weak acyclic matching prop-
erty.
Proof. Let G be an abelian group. Let A and B be nonempty finite subsets of
G with |A| = |B| and A∩(A+B) = ∅. Since the bijections A → B are precisely
the matchings and the group operation is cancellative, we can apply lemma 2.1
to obtain an acyclic matching from A to B, completing the proof.           □
Remark 2.4. In the case that A ⊆ C and B ⊆ C, any operation ⊕ : A × B →
C for which (C, ⊕) is a quasigroup has the left cancellation property. The
definition of matching generalizes straightforwardly to quasigroups, and so does
the proof of Theorem 2.3. With that in mind, one might explore the properties
of matchings in broader contexts beyond abelian groups, such as quasigroups.
Delving further into this line of research could prove to be worthwhile.
