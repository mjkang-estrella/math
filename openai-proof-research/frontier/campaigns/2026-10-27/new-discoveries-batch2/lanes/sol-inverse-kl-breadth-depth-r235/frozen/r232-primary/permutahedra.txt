                                                                                           Abstract
                                                     Let Pd be the convex hull of the permutations of {1, . . . , d} that avoid the patterns
                                                 132 and 213, and let Hd (t) be its Ehrhart h∗ -polynomial. We prove that all zeros of
                                                 Hd (t) are real and negative for 3 ≤ d ≤ 1000 and for every d ≥ 262 ; for d ≤ 2 the
                                                 polynomial is constant. Expanding Hd (t) in refined Eulerian polynomials does not suffice,
                                                 since a coordinate is already negative at d = 7. Let Ad,i (t) enumerate all permutations
                                                 of {1, . . . , d} with first entry i by number of descents. For c ≥ 0, we prove that every
                                                                                                                                         √−cAd,r ,
                                                 nonnegative linear combination of Ad,1 , . . . , Ad,d and the adjacent differences Ad,r+1
                                                 2 ≤ r ≤ d − 2, is real-rooted for all d ≥ 4 simultaneously if and only if c ≤ 2 − 3. This
                                                 reduces real-rootedness to the positivity of explicit weighted tails of the coordinates. An
                                                 exact certificate checks these inequalities for d ≤ 1000, and coefficient estimates based on
                                                 Darroch’s theorem on the mode of a sum of Bernoulli variables prove them for d ≥ 262 ;
                                                 the range 1001 ≤ d < 262 remains open. Along the way we identify Pd , up to lattice
                                                 equivalence, with twice a chain-poset permutahedron and with the Newton polytope of

                                                 Pd . We also show that the coefficient of td−1 in Hd (t) counts strong tournament score

                                           1    Introduction
                                           The face lattice of a polytope and the zeros of its Ehrhart numerator record different information.
                                           Faces describe how the boundary fits together, while the numerator of the Ehrhart series
                                           encodes the lattice-point counts of all integral dilates. When this numerator has nonnegative
                                           coefficients and only real zeros, its coefficients form a log-concave and unimodal sequence, and
                                           the unimodality, log-concavity, and real-rootedness of h∗ -polynomials are recurring questions

                                                                                                1
in Ehrhart theory [14]. The polytopes studied here combine these aspects in a simple form.
Their face lattice is that of a cube, for d ≥ 3 the top coefficient of their Ehrhart numerator
counts score sequences of strong tournaments, and the zeros of the numerator lead to a signed
extension of a familiar compatible family of Eulerian polynomials.
    A permutation avoids a pattern when no subsequence has the same relative order as that
pattern; for example, the entries 2, 4, 3 of 2413 form a copy of 132. Write Avd (Π) for the
set of permutations of [d] = {1, . . . , d} that avoid every pattern in Π. Davis and Sagan [13]
introduced the polytopes

                             Pd (Π) = conv{(π1 , . . . , πd ) : π ∈ Avd (Π)},

which they call Π-avoiding permutohedra. They asked whether Pd (132, 213) is combinatorially
a (d − 1)-cube with normalized volume 2d−1 dd−3 [13, Conjecture 3.19], and, more generally,
for which Π the h∗ -polynomial of Pd (Π) is unimodal [13, Question 3.13]. Cubicality concerns
face incidences, while normalized volume is measured in the affine lattice so that a unimodular
simplex has volume one. For Π = {132, 312}, Davis and Sagan derive unimodality [13,
Corollary 3.12] from a theorem of Beck, Jochemko, and McCullough: the h∗ -polynomial of
every lattice zonotope is real-rooted and unimodal [4, Theorem 1.2]. The permutations that
avoid 132 and 213 consist of increasing blocks whose values decrease from one block to the
next. This rigid structure makes the family suitable for a direct comparison of geometry and
enumeration, but it does not produce zonotopes: already P3 (132, 213) is a quadrilateral that
is not a parallelogram.
    Write Pd = Pd (132, 213). Four descriptions of the same lattice polytope organize the paper.
The blocks label the vertices by their cuts. Prefix deficits turn the facet inequalities into
a path-Laplacian system, in which each cut is a zero of a piecewise parabolic profile. Two
affine lattice maps then identify Pd with twice a translated poset permutahedron of a chain
and with the Newton polytope of the discriminant of a polynomial of degree d. Through the
discriminant model, the cube structure and the volume become classical facts; through the
chain model, we reach tournament scores and, using the symmetry of the permutahedron, the
Ehrhart series.

Main results
We first fix the lattice realization. The cube structure and the volume in the next theorem are
known for the two models, and the lattice equivalences transfer them to Pd ; the equivalences
also fix the lattice normalization used in the Ehrhart calculation.
Theorem 1. For every d ≥ 1, the affine map Ψd (x)i = d + i − 1 − xi is a lattice equivalence

the polytope Pd (132, 213) is also lattice equivalent to the Newton polytope of the discriminant
of a polynomial of degree d. Consequently Pd (132, 213) is combinatorially a (d − 1)-cube and

                                 Volnorm Pd (132, 213) = 2d−1 dd−3 .

    The proof of Theorem 1 is given in Sections 2 and 3. Theorem 7 gives a self-contained proof
of the cube structure in deficit coordinates, Theorem 8 and Proposition 9 prove the two lattice
equivalences, and Corollary 10 gives the normalized volume. For d = 1, the polytope is a point.
The cube structure was proved for Pd (132, 213) by Tamayo Jiménez [31, Proposition 3.5.2]
and for the discriminant polytope by Gelfand, Kapranov, and Zelevinsky, as recalled below.
Through the equivalences, the known volumes of the two models settle the second part of the
question of Davis and Sagan.
    A tournament is an orientation of a complete graph. It is strong if every vertex can
reach every other by a directed path. Its score sequence lists the outdegrees of the vertices in
nondecreasing order, and a strong score sequence is one realized by a strong tournament. We
count each score sequence once, regardless of the number of tournaments realizing it. Landau’s
inequalities and their strict version relate these sequences to the chain model [21, 18].
Theorem 2. For every d ≥ 3, the coefficient of td−1 in Hd (t) is the number of strong
tournament score sequences on d vertices. The same number is the leading coefficient of the
rational Ehrhart numerator h∗Z (ΠCd ; t), which has degree 2d − 2.
    The proof of Theorem 2 appears in Section 4, where the rational numerator is defined. A
shift of the ordered coordinates turns the strict prefix inequalities of the interior into the strict
form of Landau’s criterion. The second assertion is Rehberg’s conjecture [25, Conjecture 6.36]
for d ≥ 3. It also holds for d = 1, while for d = 2 the rational numerator is 1 + t and no
tournament on two vertices is strong.
    The main new results concern the zeros of Hd (t). A single criterion, the positivity of the
weighted tails, proves real-rootedness in a finite initial range and in an explicit eventual range.

Theorem 3. For every 1 ≤ d ≤ 1000, all zeros of Hd (t) are real and negative. Here
H1 (t) = H2 (t) = 1 and H3 (t) = (1 + t)2 ; the assertion is vacuous for d = 1, 2.

    Section 7 proves Theorem 3 using the criterion of Section 6. All arithmetic comparisons
are exact and can be reconstructed from the displayed pseudocode and the public data.

Theorem 4. For every integer d ≥ d0 = 262 , all zeros of Hd (t) are real and negative.

    Sections 8 and 9 prove Theorem 4, with the final argument assembled in Theorem 25. It
separates the tail indices into two ranges and establishes positivity in both. A polynomial
with only real negative zeros has positive, log-concave, and therefore unimodal coefficients.
Theorems 3 and 4 thus show that the h∗ -polynomial of Pd (132, 213) is unimodal for d ≤ 1000
and for d ≥ 262 , which gives a partial answer to Question 3.13 of Davis and Sagan for
Π = {132, 213}.
    For the small tail indices, a Cauchy estimate compares the coordinates with the value
of their generating polynomial at c∗ . For the remaining indices, it suffices to show that the
corresponding coordinates are positive, and we compare them with the coefficients of an explicit
product. After normalization, each factor of this product is the generating polynomial of a
Bernoulli variable, as in Pitman’s probabilistic reading of real-rooted polynomials [24], and we
choose the normalization so that the target coefficient sits at the mean. Darroch’s theorem [12,
Theorem 4], in the form stated by Pitman [24, p. 284], then shows that this coefficient is the
largest point probability, since for a sum of independent Bernoulli variables every mode lies at
distance less than one from the mean. An elementary Fourier estimate controls the change of
adjacent probabilities, and first moments of a coefficient majorant control√the remaining error.
The threshold 262 is the point where the resulting error bound 5 · 228 / d drops to 5/8; the
range 1001 ≤ d < d0 remains open.

Related work
Gelfand, Kapranov, and Zelevinsky proved that the Newton polytope of the discriminant
of a univariate polynomial of degree d is combinatorially a (d − 1)-cube and described its
vertices through subdivisions of the segment [0, d] [16], [17, Chapter 12, Theorem 2.2]. Orevkov
computed its volume by a triangulation indexed by permutations [23]. Black, Rehberg, and
Sanyal introduced poset permutahedra, observed that twice the poset permutahedron of a
chain is unimodularly equivalent to this Newton polytope, and obtained a volume formula for
all poset permutahedra [6]. For the pattern-avoiding model itself, Tamayo Jiménez proved
the block description of the vertices, the paired facet inequalities, and cubicality [31], and
computed the h∗ -vectors for d ≤ 6 [31, Appendix B.1]. The chain model is also a partitioned
weight polytope of type A, whose facets were described by Horiguchi, Masuda, Shareshian,
and Song [19, Corollary 4.3], and its cube structure is a case of the theorem of Burrull, Gui,
and Hu on strongly dominant weight polytopes [9, Theorem 2.9]: in type Ad−1 , the centered
weight (1, . . . , d) − d+1
                         2 1 lies strictly inside the chamber of increasing coordinates, and the
intersection of its orbit polytope with that chamber is the centered chain model. The Ehrhart
numerator depends on the lattice realization, and our geometric contribution is limited to
the explicit lattice equivalences of Theorem 1 together with a self-contained proof in deficit
coordinates.
    Black, Rehberg, and Sanyal identify score vectors of poset tournaments with lattice points
of poset permutahedra translated by minus the all-ones vector [6, Theorem 1.4]; for a chain
these are Landau’s score sequences. Rehberg characterized strong score sequences as the
lattice points of the translated chain permutahedron that lie on none of its filter facets, and
conjectured that their number is the leading coefficient of its rational Ehrhart numerator
[25, Corollary 6.35 and Conjecture 6.36]. Claesson, Dukes, Franklín, and Stefánsson [11] and


                                               4
Stockmeyer [30] give counting formulas and algorithms for score sequences, and the asymptotic
enumeration is studied by Kolesnik [20] and by Bassan, Donderwinkel, and Kolesnik [3].
    The main contribution is the compatible family of adjacent differences and its use in proving
real-rootedness. The refined Eulerian polynomials used here occur in the transformation of
h-vectors under barycentric subdivision [8], and we adopt the first-letter indexing of Nevo,
Petersen, and Tenner, who call them restricted Eulerian polynomials [22]. Every nonnegative
combination of them is real-rooted [8, Remark 1], and they form an interlacing sequence [2,
Example 2.3]. Beck, Jochemko, and McCullough showed that the h∗ -polynomial of every lattice
zonotope is a nonnegative combination of these polynomials, which gives its real-rootedness [4,
Theorems 1.2 and 1.4]. The convex hull of these zonotopal h∗ -polynomials is the translated

Organization of the paper
Section 2 proves the facet description and the face lattice in deficit coordinates. Section 3
establishes the two lattice models and the volume, and Section 4 interprets the top coefficient
through strong tournaments. Sections 5 and 6 derive the refined coordinates and the criterion
based on adjacent differences. Section 7 verifies the finite range. Sections 8 and 9 prove
eventual positivity, covering the lower tail indices with a forward Cauchy estimate and the
remaining indices with estimates for the reversed coordinates. Section 10 discusses the open
range and the coordinate inequalities that would settle it.


2    Pattern avoidance, deficits, and the cube
This section proves the facet description and the cubical face lattice of Pd in coordinates adapted
to its vertices. Tamayo Jiménez obtained the block description of the vertices, the paired facet
inequalities, and cubicality [31, Propositions 3.5.1–3.5.2], and the cube structure also follows

the lattice equivalences of Section 3 start from these inequalities, and the same coordinates
attach to every vertex an explicit integer profile. The original coordinates list the entries of
a permutation, which is natural for describing vertices but hides the facets. Prefix deficits
measure how far each initial sum lies below its largest possible value. In these coordinates the
two facet choices at every position become a zero condition and a path-Laplacian condition,
and the cuts of a reverse-layered permutation appear as zeros of a piecewise parabolic profile.

3    Two lattice models
Two polytopes are lattice equivalent, or unimodularly equivalent, if an affine bijection between
their affine hulls maps one onto the other and maps the integer points of the first affine hull
onto those of the second. The facet description identifies Pd , up to lattice equivalence, with two
polytopes that were studied without reference to pattern avoidance. The first is twice the part
of a permutahedron that lies in one closed Weyl chamber, a poset permutahedron of a chain
in the sense of Black, Rehberg, and Sanyal. After translation by minus the all-ones vector
and before dilation by two, its lattice points are the tournament score sequences. Averaging
over the symmetric group, which acts on the full permutahedron, gives the Ehrhart series in

                                                   9
Section 5. The second is the Newton polytope of the discriminant of a univariate polynomial,
whose face lattice and volume are classical. This section proves both equivalences and derives
the volume of Pd from the known volumes of the two models.

6    A compatible cone of adjacent differences
The refined Eulerian polynomials form a compatible family, so a nonnegative expansion in
this basis implies real-rootedness. The negative coordinate a7,1 places H7 (t) outside the cone
they generate, and we therefore look for a larger cone whose elements are still real-rooted. We
enlarge the family by the differences Ad,r+1 − cAd,r of adjacent basis elements, in which the
parameter c ≥ 0 measures how much of one basis element may be subtracted from the next.
The first task is to determine exactly how large c may be while compatibility holds for all
d ≥ 4 simultaneously. The second is to express the membership of Hd (t) in the enlarged cone
through its coordinates.
    We regard the zero polynomial as real-rooted. A finite family of real polynomials is
compatible if every nonnegative linear combination is real-rooted. For nonzero real-rooted
polynomials f and g with positive leading coefficients, write f ⪯ g if deg f ≤ deg g ≤ deg f + 1
and their zeros, listed in decreasing order as θ1 ≥ θ2 ≥ · · · for f and τ1 ≥ τ2 ≥ · · · for g, satisfy

                                      · · · ≤ θ2 ≤ τ2 ≤ θ1 ≤ τ1 .

Thus the rightmost zero belongs to g; equalities allow common or repeated zeros. If f is a
positive constant, then f ⪯ g for every positive constant g and for every real-rooted g of degree
one. We also set 0 ⪯ g and g ⪯ 0 for every real-rooted g. This is the interlacing convention
used in [2, Section 2]; see also [27, Section 2.4].

include its proof because we use the weak form, which allows common zeros and the zero
polynomial. Its simple-root hypothesis concerns only the polynomial g.

10     Conclusion and the open range
The real-rootedness question for Pd (132, 213) leads to a difficulty that its cube structure and
volume do not reveal: its expansion in the refined Eulerian basis has a negative coordinate
already at d = 7. The compatible family of adjacent differences overcomes this obstruction
whenever the weighted tails (24) are nonnegative. The finite certificate and the coefficient
estimates establish this condition for d ≤ 1000 and d ≥ 262 , respectively, giving a partial
answer to the unimodality question for this family.
    The criterion applies to any polynomial with the required signs in these coordinates, so √its
use extends beyond nonnegative expansions in the refined basis. The constant c∗ = 2 − 3
is optimal for the chosen family simultaneously in all dimensions d ≥ 4; starting at d = 5
permits a larger parameter. For the geometric model, the explicit lattice maps connect Pd to
the known chain and discriminant polytopes. They identify its lattice points with those of 2Rd ,
while the lattice points of Rd are ordinary tournament score sequences. The interior-point
shift then explains why the top coefficient of Hd counts strong score sequences for d ≥ 3.
    The interval 1001 ≤ d < 262 remains open. The finite data and the eventual result support
the following conjecture, whose resolution requires control in every intermediate dimension.

                                                     34
Conjecture 26. For every d ≥ 3, all zeros of Hd (t) are real and negative.

    The data suggest a specific route to this conclusion. For 7 ≤ d ≤ 1000, the only negative
coordinate is ad,1 , and 50ad,1 + ad,2 > 0. Proving ad,j ≥ 0 for 2 ≤ j ≤ d − 1 and ad,1 + c∗ ad,2 ≥ 0
                                                                                           (2)
for every d ≥ 4 would settle Conjecture 26 through the single adjacent difference Ed,c∗ , as in
Section 7. The present estimates already give ad,j > 0 for ⌊d/4⌋ < j ≤ d − 2 when d ≥ 262 , and
Theorem 2 gives ad,d−1 > 0 for every d ≥ 3. The remaining task is to control the individual
coordinates of small index and to obtain estimates valid below the eventual threshold. These
are stronger requirements than the weighted-sum bounds proved here, and they isolate the
gap between the observed finite pattern and a proof in all dimensions.
