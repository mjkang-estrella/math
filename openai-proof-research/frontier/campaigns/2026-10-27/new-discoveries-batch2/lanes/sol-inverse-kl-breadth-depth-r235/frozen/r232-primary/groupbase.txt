                                                      Let M be a matroid, let ℓ : E(M ) → G be a labeling of its elements by an abelian group,
                                                   and let F ⊆ G be finite. A base is F -avoiding if the label sum of its elements is not in F . We
                                                   prove that, for arbitrary costs, a min-cost F -avoiding base can be obtained from any min-cost
                                                   base using at most |F | exchanges. This resolves the weighted proximity conjecture by Hörsch,
                                                   Imolay, Mizutani, Oki, and Schwarcz. Our main technical tool is to replace the assumption
                                                   of Schrijver–Seymour Conjecture in Liu and Xu’s earlier work with Kemperman–Scherk
                                                   Theorem. The proximity theorem combined with approaches in earlier work gives an FPT
                                                   algorithm that finds a min-cost F -avoiding base parameterized by |G|. We additionally give
                                                   an FPT algorithm parameterized by |F |, answering a question of Hörsch et al.


                                           1       Introduction
                                           Let M be a finite matroid on ground set E, let G be an abelian (additive) group, let ℓ : E → G
                                           label the elements, and write ℓ(B) := e∈B ℓ(e) for a subset B ⊆ E. For a finite set F ⊆ G, a
                                                                                   P

                                           base B is F -avoiding if ℓ(B) ∈/ F . Constraining ℓ(B) encodes parity, congruency, exact-target,
                                           and forbidden-value side constraints, which have been studied for matchings [PY82, MVV87],
                                           arborescences [BP87], submodular function minimization [GR95, NSZ19], minimum cuts [NZ20],
                                           paths in group-labeled graphs [CGG+ 06], and matroid bases [Rie91, CGM92, DAKS24, HIM+ 24,
                                           GMO+ 25]. We study the min-cost F -avoiding bases: additionally given a cost function over the
                                           ground set c : E → R, we are interested in finding a min-cost F -avoiding base, where the cost of
                                                               P
                                           a base B is c(B) := e∈B c(e). Here, a min-cost F -avoiding base refers to one with the smallest
                                           cost among all F -avoiding bases; it does not have to have the smallest cost among all bases.
                                           The distance between bases A and B is d(A, B) := |A \ B|, which equals the number of basis
                                           exchanges needed to reach one from the other. We ask how far an unconstrained optimum is
                                           from a constrained one: given an arbitrary min-cost base, how far is the closest base that is of
                                           minimum cost among the F -avoiding bases?
                                               The unweighted version with costs c ≡ 0 has received more attention and substantial progress
                                           has been made recently. Namely, given an arbitrary base, how far is the closest F -avoiding base,
                                           provided that an F -avoiding base exists? For g ∈ G, a base B is a g-base if ℓ(B) = g. Liu and
                                           Xu [LX24] conjectured that, for every finite abelian group G, every attainable target g, and
                                           every prescribed base, the closest g-base to it has distance at most |G| − 1. This is a special case
                                           of the F -avoiding setting by putting F = G \ g. Liu and Xu proved the conjecture for cyclic
                                           groups whose size is a prime-power, as well as for groups whose size is a product of two primes.
                                           More interestingly, they reduce the conjecture to an old conjecture of Schrijver and Seymour
                                           [SS90] on counting labels of bases. Hörsch, Imolay, Mizutani, Oki, and Schwarcz [HIM+ 24]

an F -avoiding base within distance |F |, provided that an F -avoiding base exists. For |F | = 1,
the conjecture essentially follows from Rieder’s characterization of base lattices [Rie91]. Hörsch
et al. [HIM+ 24] proved the conjecture when |F | ≤ 2, when the group is ordered, and for strongly

proved the conjecture when |F | ≤ 4, via a computer-assisted case analysis, and for sparse paving
matroids. Most recently, Chen [Che26] proved the conjecture for representable matroids, and
derived a sharp proximity bound for the more general setting where multiple labelings are given,
which was introduced in [GMO+ 25]. Bhardwaj [Bha26] independently gave an alternative proof
of the proximity conjecture for representable matroids.
    Liu and Xu [LX24] also proposed the weighted generalization of their conjecture with the
same proximity bound, and Hörsch et al. [HIM+ 24] further generalized it to the F -avoiding
setting, which is known as the weighted proximity conjecture. Namely, every min-cost base
has a min-cost F -avoiding base within distance |F |, provided that an F -avoiding base exists.
Very little progress was made towards the weighted proximity conjecture compared with the
unweighted one. Liu and Xu proved in the special case of |F | = |G| − 1 a stronger bound
D(G) − 1, where D(G) is the Davenport constant, for strongly base-orderable matroids, and
for groups of size at most four. Hörsch et al. [HIM+ 24] proved the weighted conjecture for the
special case where |F | = 1.
    In this paper, we prove the weighted proximity conjecture in full, formally stated as follows.
Theorem 1 (Proximity). Let M = (E, I) be a matroid, G be an abelian group, F ⊆ G be a
finite subset, ℓ : E → G be a labeling, and c : E → R be a cost function. For every min-cost
base A, there exists a min-cost F -avoiding base B such that d(A, B) ≤ |F |, provided that an
F -avoiding base exists.
    The bound is sharp: in the rank-r partition matroid with partition classes {ai , bi } and capacity
1 for each part, labels ℓ(ai ) = 0 and ℓ(bi ) = 1 over G = Z, zero cost, and F = {0, 1, . . . , k − 1}
with k ≤ r, every F -avoiding base contains at least k elements bi , so the base {a1 , . . . , ar } has
distance at least k from every min-cost F -avoiding base. For a finite group G and an attainable
g, taking F = G \ {g} makes the F -avoiding bases exactly the g-bases, so the conjecture of
Liu and Xu follows. For the unweighted g-base case and groups not covered by [LX24], the
best previously known bounds were O(|G|5 ) for cyclic G and |G|O(log |G|) in general, derived
in [GMO+ 25] from the sensitivity theorem of Eisenbrand, Rohwedder, and Węgrzycki [ERW24].
    The proximity theorem alone yields an algorithm finding a min-cost F -avoiding base with
n O(|F |) independence-oracle queries, where n := |E|, by enumerating the bases within distance |F |

of a min-cost base, and Hörsch et al. asked whether finding an F -avoiding base is fixed-parameter
tractable in |F | [HIM+ 24]. We answer the question affirmatively even for the weighted case: an
F -avoiding base can be found in O |F |2 n2 + |F |O(|F |) TMI time, and a min-cost F -avoiding base
                                                             
                        2
can be found in 2O(|F | ) · O(n2 TWMI ) time, where TMI and TWMI denote the running times of
unweighted and weighted matroid intersection, respectively, for two matroids over n elements.
In this sense, fixed-parameter tractability is best possible: no algorithm, even randomized, finds
an F -avoiding base in time polynomial in n and |F |. Indeed, labeling each element by its integer
weight and taking F = {0, . . . , W } \ {T }, where W is the total weight, encodes deciding whether
a base of weight exactly T exists, which requires time superpolynomial in n and W in the
independence-oracle model [DAKS24, Theorem 3].
    The analogous proximity conjecture for several simultaneous labelings, posed in [GMO+ 25]
together with a lower bound of 2k − 1 for k labelings, remains open for arbitrary matroids.

     To the best of our knowledge, Conjecture 3 is solved when |G| is a product of two primes
or G = Zpn for a prime p and a positive integer n [DGM09]. Liu and Xu [LX24] prove that
Conjecture 3 implies the unweighted version of Theorem 1 in the special case where |F | = |G| − 1:
for every base A, there exists a g-base B such that d(A, B) ≤ |G| − 1, if one exists. Thus, for
finite groups and F = G \ {g}, one possible route to the unweighted version of Theorem 1 is via
the Schrijver–Seymour conjecture.
     We bypass the difficulty by proving an analogue of Theorem 2 for general groups, under
the additional assumption that there is a base B of M such that its label is unique. The
main tool is the Kemperman–Scherk theorem from additive number theory (Theorem 8) [Sch55,
Kem56, Kem60]. In fact, we prove the following weighted generalization of this analogue,
which bounds from below the number of distinct colors of the bases of small cost, in terms
of the rank sum of color classes filtered by the cost of the smallest element of B. For a
matroid M = (E, I), a labeling function ℓ : E → G, a cost function c : E → R, and θ ∈ R, let
ℓ≤θ (M ) := { ℓ(B) : B is a base of M with c(B) ≤ θ }.

   To see how this counting approach implies the weighted proximity Theorem 1, we follow
ideas of [LX24]. Pick a min-cost base A and a min-cost F -avoiding base B of M such that
d(A, B) is minimized. We consider the matroid M ′ = (M |A∪B )/(A ∩ B) obtained from deleting
elements outside A ∪ B and contracting elements inside A ∩ B. The matroid M ′ is a block
matroid, which means its ground set can be partitioned into two bases. The crucial observation

                                                  3
is that B ′ := B \ A is the unique min-cost F ′ -avoiding base of M ′ for the shifted label set
F ′ = F − ℓ(A ∩ B). Moreover, A \ B is a min-cost base of M ′ . A refined form of Theorem 4
(Theorem 14) in block matroids then shows that the bases of M ′ of cost at most c(B ′ ) attain at
least r(M ′ ) + 1 distinct labels. On the other hand, there should be at most |F ′ | + 1 distinct
labels, since every base of M ′ of cost at most c(B ′ ) other than B ′ itself has label in F ′ by the
uniqueness. Hence d(A, B) = |B ′ | = r(M ′ ) ≤ |F ′ | = |F |.

that give the target label g. Moreover, for subsets A, B ⊆ E, one has ∥σ(A) − σ(B)∥1 ≤ |A △ B|.

proximity theorem, Liu and Xu [LX24] use these ideas to obtain an FPT algorithm by searching
for correct signatures of short distances. Their approach easily extends to finding min-cost
F -avoiding bases.

Here, r denotes the rank of M . When G is finite, assuming Theorem 1 with proximity bound
d in place of |F | gives an O(|G|2d TWMI )-time algorithm. First, find a min-cost base A by the
greedy algorithm. By proximity, it suffices to consider bases B with d(A, B) ≤ d, and hence their

a partition matroid whose parts are Eg := { e ∈ E : ℓ(e) = g } and whose capacities are bg . A
min-cost base with signature b can therefore be found by weighted matroid intersection. There
are at most |G|2d valid signatures, giving the claimed running time. Applying the bound d = |F |
from Theorem 1 yields an O(|G|2|F | TWMI )-time algorithm, which is FPT parameterized by |G|,
but not by |F |.
     Our idea is to construct a finite set Λ ⊆ G that contains the labels of all elements in A △ B
for a min-cost base A and some min-cost F -avoiding base B, with |Λ| bounded by a function of
|F |. This guarantees σg (A) = σg (B) for all g ∈  / Λ, so the number of relevant valid signatures
depends on |Λ| and d, rather than on |G|.
     In the unweighted case, let D := { g − h : g, h ∈ F }. If some component of M contains
elements e, e′ with ℓ(e) − ℓ(e′ ) ∈
                                  / D, a circuit exchange yields bases A and A − e + e′ whose labels
differ by an element outside D, so at least one is F -avoiding. Otherwise, shifting the labels in
each component by the label of a fixed reference element, and translating F accordingly, gives an
equivalent instance whose element labels all lie in D. Since |D| ≤ |F |(|F | − 1) + 1, taking Λ = D
and d = |F | in the signature-enumeration subroutine gives an FPT algorithm (Theorem 16).
     The weighted case requires a different construction of Λ because an F -avoiding base obtained
by a single exchange from a min-cost base need not be min-cost. After perturbing the cost function
so that all relevant optima are unique, the algorithm recursively finds a min-cost (F \ {f })-
avoiding base Bf for each f ∈ F , whenever one exists. It then constructs K := f ∈F (A △ Bf ),

whose size is O(|F |2 ) by Theorem 1. For every a ∈ A, the algorithm constructs a label set Λ
consisting of all labels reachable from { ℓ(e) : e ∈ K ∪ {a} } by at most 2|F | derivation steps,
each of which adds an element of F − ℓ(A) or its inverse. Every such set Λ has size 2O(|F |) . For
each such Λ, we apply signature enumeration with d = |F |. Using Schrijver’s exchange lemma
for linking systems [Sch79], we show that one of these sets Λ contains the labels of all elements
in A △ B for the min-cost F -avoiding base B, thus yielding an FPT algorithm (Theorem 17).

2     Preliminaries
Let G be an abelian group written additively, let ℓ : E → G be a labeling function, and let
c : E → R be a cost function. For X ⊆ E, write ℓ(X) := e∈X ℓ(e) and c(X) := e∈X c(e). For
                                                          P                      P

X ⊆ E, write ℓ[X] := { ℓ(e) : e ∈ X } for the set of labels occurring on X, in contrast to the
sum ℓ(X).
     We use standard matroid terminology, following [Oxl11]. A matroid M = (E, I) consists
of a pair formed by a finite ground set E and a set family I ⊆ 2E over E such that ∅ ∈ I,
X ⊆ Y ∈ I implies X ∈ I, and for X, Y ∈ I with |X| < |Y |, there exists e ∈ Y \ X such that
X ∪ {e} ∈ I. A subset X ⊆ E is called independent if X ∈ I and dependent otherwise. A base
is a maximal independent set. Every base has the same cardinality, called the rank of M . A
matroid is a block matroid if its ground set can be partitioned into two bases. For bases A and
B, write d(A, B) := |A \ B| = |B \ A|. We will need the following perfect matching lemma.

Lemma 5 (perfect matching [Bru69]). For any bases A and B, there is a bijection π : A \ B →
B \ A such that A − x + π(x) is a base for every x ∈ A \ B.

Lemma 6 (multiple exchange [Gre73]). For every bases A, B and every subset X ⊆ A \ B, there
exists a subset Y ⊆ B \ A such that both (A \ X) ∪ Y and (B \ Y ) ∪ X are bases.

     A circuit is a minimal dependent set. For a base A of M and e ∈ E \ A, the fundamental
circuit of e wrt A is the unique circuit in A ∪ {e} which necessarily contains e. The rank function
r : 2E → Z of a matroid M is defined by r(X) := max{ |I| : I ⊆ X, I ∈ I } for X ⊆ E. The
closure of X ⊆ E, denoted by clM (X), is the unique maximal set Y ⊇ X with r(Y ) = r(X). We
use the following lemma on a base and a circuit.

Lemma 7. Let M be a matroid, B be a base, and C be a circuit. Then, for every b ∈ B ∩ C,
there exists a ∈ C \ B such that B − b + a is a base.

Proof. Suppose not. Since B \ {b} is independent and no element in C \ B can be added to
B \ {b} while maintaining independence, we have C \ B ⊆ clM (B \ {b}). This implies
                                                                  
              b ∈ clM (C \ {b}) = clM (C \ B) ∪ (C ∩ (B \ {b})) ⊆ clM (B \ {b}),

which contradicts the independence of B.

    For a matroid M = (E, I) and X ⊆ E, the restriction to X, the deletion of X, and the
contraction of X are matroids defined by M | X := (X, { I ⊆ X : I ∈ I }), M \X := (E \ X, { I ∈
I : I ∩ X = ∅ }), and M/X := (E \ X, { I ⊆ E \ X : I ∪ B ∈ I }), respectively, where B
is any base of M | X. The direct sum of matroids M1 = (E1 , I1 ) and M2 = (E2 , I2 ) with
E1 ∩ E2 = ∅ is M1 ⊕ M2 := (E1 ∪ E2 , { I1 ∪ I2 : I1 ∈ I1 , I2 ∈ I2 }). A (connected) component
of M is an equivalence class under the equivalence relation ∼ on E defined by e ∼ e′ if either
e = e′ or M has a circuit containing {e, e′ }. If E1 , . . . , Em are the components of M , then
M = (M | E1 ) ⊕ · · · ⊕ (M | Em ); in particular, every base of M is the disjoint union of bases of
M | E1 , . . . , M | Em .

graph where the vertex bipartition is (A, E \ A), and each e ∈ A and f ∈       / A are adjacent if
A − e + f is a base. The connected components of G(A) for an arbitrary base A are precisely
the components of M [Kro77].

3     Proximity Theorem
This section is devoted to proving Theorem 1. Throughout this section, let M = (E, I) be a
matroid with rank function r, let ℓ : E → G be a labeling function, let c : E → R be a cost
function, and suppose that B is the unique min-cost ℓ(B)-base. Abusing notation, we also write
r for the rank of M itself. The case r = 0 is immediate, so assume r ≥ 1 throughout this section.
For h ∈ G, denote by Eh := { e ∈ E : ℓ(e) = h }.

We prove Proximity Theorem 1, restated below, following ideas from [LX24].

4.3.2   Algorithm
Throughout this and the following subsections, we assume that distinct bases have distinct costs.
To achieve this, fix an arbitrary ordering of E, and replace the cost c(e) of each element e by
the ordered pair ce(e) := (c(e), 2p(e) ), where p(e) is the position of e in the ordering. Pairs are
added coordinate-wise and compared lexicographically. The min-cost base with respect to ce in
every nonempty family of bases is unique and is minimum with respect to c. The same ordering
of the bases can also be induced by the real-valued cost cα (e) := c(e) + α2p(e) for sufficiently
small α > 0. Consequently, Theorem 1 applies to the tie-broken cost. We suppress the tilde
below. Under this assumption, the min-cost base of M , denoted by A throughout, is unique.
Moreover, for every finite F ′ ⊆ G for which an F ′ -avoiding base exists, the min-cost F ′ -avoiding
base is unique.
    The algorithm of Theorem 17 is the recursive procedure Solve(F ) shown in Algorithm 1,
invoked on the input forbidden set. For a nonnegative integer q ∈ Z≥0 , a q-step derivation
of H ⊆ G is a subset Λ ⊆ G consisting of the elements λ that can be obtained from H by a

for every i ∈ [q].
