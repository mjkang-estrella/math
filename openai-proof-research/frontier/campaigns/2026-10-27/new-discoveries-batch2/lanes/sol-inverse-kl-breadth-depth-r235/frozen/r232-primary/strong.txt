                                                                                                   Abstract
                                                        A strong blocking set is a set of points in a finite projective space that intersects every
                                                     hyperplane of the space in a spanning set of the hyperplane. These objects have been studied
                                                     both in finite geometry and coding theory due to their equivalence with minimal codes. We give
                                                     an explicit construction of strong blocking sets using expander graphs that improves the explicit
                                                     construction of Alon et al. [4] by introducing a new geometrically motivated graph parameter
                                                     that is better suited for this problem. Using this approach, we improve the constant C in the
                                                     explicit construction of a strong blocking set of size C(q+1)k in the (k−1)-dimensional projective
                                                     space over Fq for every prime power q ≥ 7.


                                           1        Introduction
                                           Strong blocking sets in finite projective spaces have been studied extensively in the last ten years.
                                           These objects were first introduced by Davydov et al. [17] to construct saturating sets and then
                                           reintroduced by Héger et al. [23] to study the q-analog of a classic combinatorial search problem:
                                           identifying an unknown projective point using subspace queries. Since then, these objects have been
                                           reinvented and studied under various names, such as cutting blocking sets [1, 9, 12], generating sets
                                           [18], and the now commonly used term of strong blocking sets [4, 22, 27].
                                               The recent flurry of results is primarily motivated by a connection with coding theory that was
                                           independently discovered by Alfarano et al. [1] and Tang et al. [36]. They showed that a strong
                                           blocking set of size n in a finite projective space PG(k−1, q) is equivalent to a minimal k-dimensional
                                           linear code of length n. Minimal codes are linear codes in which the support of a nonzero codeword
                                           u is a subset of the support of a nonzero codeword v if and only if u = λv for some scalar λ. These
                                           codes have their own history dating back to 1993 in the work of Massey on secret sharing protocols
                                           [31]. Then Ashikhmin and Barg established sufficient conditions for their existence [7] which led to
                                           several interesting constructions. In 2019, it was shown that these objects are in fact equivalent to
                                           strong blocking sets, thus converging these two threads of research. Since then, strong blocking sets
                                           and minimal codes have also found connections with perfect hashing [10] and additive combinatorics
                                           [13].
                                               In [3], it was shown that the smallest length n of a minimal code of dimension k, or equivalently
                                           the smallest size n of a strong blocking set in PG(k − 1, q), must be at least (q + 1)(k − 1). This
                                           lower bound was further improved in [10] and [34]. A probabilistic argument [10, 22] shows the
                                           existence of such codes of length at most 2(q + 1)k. One of the central problems on minimal codes
                                           is to give explicit constructions of such codes that have length at most Cqk for some constant C.
                                           The main regime of interest is fixed, but possibly large, q and k → ∞. The first author solved this

2     Preliminaries
In this section, we review the basic notions and preliminary results from coding theory that will be
used throughout the paper, with particular emphasis on minimal linear codes and their geometric
interpretation. We also recall the concept of expander graphs and some known results on constant
degree expanders. Throughout the remainder of the paper, we assume that q is a prime power.

2.1   Error correcting codes and minimal codes
We denote with Fq to be the finite field with of order q and let n ∈ N.

Definition 2.1. The (Hamming) support of a vector v ∈ Fnq is the set

                                     σ(v) := {i : vi ̸= 0} ⊆ [n].

The (Hamming) weight of v is
                                           wt(v) := |σ(v)|.

    The Hamming weight induces a distance on Fnq , given by d(u, v) := wt(u − v). This is known
as the Hamming distance and it is fundamental in the theory of error-correcting codes. It is the
number of positions where the coordinates of two vectors differ.

                                   d := min{wt(v) : v ∈ C \ {0}}.

n and k are the length and the dimension of the code, respectively. A generator matrix for C is a

rate and the relative distance of codes. A family of codes C for which R > 0 and δ > 0, is known
as an asymptotically good code (see [20, Section 1.8] or [28, Chapter 9]). An easy probabilistic

complexity of these constructions, has been an active area of research in coding theory since the
1970s (see for example [35, 38]). One of the most significant developments in the area was the use
of modular curves to show that, for square q ≥ 49, there are explicit constructions of linear codes
over Fq that have a better rate and relative distance than the one given by the Gilbert-Varshamov
bound. For further details see [16] and [37].
    In this paper, we study a special class of codes called minimal linear codes. These codes are
interesting from various different perspectives.

σ(u) ⊆ σ(v) ⇐⇒ ∃λ ∈ Fq u = λv.

2.2   Minimal codes and strong blocking sets
In this section, we first describe the equivalence between minimal codes and strong blocking sets,
and then note the relevant prior results. For k > 1, let PG(k − 1, q) denote the projective space
of dimension k − 1 obtained from the vector space Fkq . Its points, lines, . . ., m-spaces, are the 1-
dimensional, 2-dimensional, . . ., (m + 1)-dimensional, vector subspaces of Fkq . For a nonzero vector
v ∈ Fkq , let ⟨v⟩ be the corresponding point of PG(k − 1, q). For a set S of points in the projective
space we denote by ⟨S⟩ the span of these points, that is, the smallest dimensional projective subspace
containing S.

Definition 2.6. A projective [n, k, d]q system is a (multi)set of n points, M ⊆ PG(k − 1, q), such
that M spans the projective space and

                            d = n − max{|H ∩ M | : H is a hyperplane}.

    The term projective system and the notation used come from the equivalence with linear codes
of a given minimal distance. A projective [n, k, d]q system is simply a dual interpretation of a
nondegenerate [n, k, d]q code. We say that an [n, k, d]q code C is nondegenerate if there is no
identically zero coordinate in C. The correspondence comes from putting representatives of the
points of the projective system as columns of a k × n matrix, and considering the code generated
by (the rows of) this matrix. Vice versa, given a generator matrix of a nondegenerate code, we can
obtain the associated projective system by taking the columns of such a matrix as a multiset of
points in PG(k − 1, q).
    There is more work required to make this correspondence well-defined, and but it becomes so
when we consider PGL(k, q)-equivalence classes of projective [n, k, d]q systems and the so-called
monomial equivalence classes of nondegenerate [n, k, d]q codes. We refer the reader to [37, Theorem
1.1.6] for a formal treatment of the correspondence.
    Due to this correspondence, a sequence {Mi }i∈N of projective systems is called an (R, δ)q -family
of projective systems if the corresponding family of codes is an (R, δ)q -family of codes. We now
define the main finite geometric object studied in this paper.

Definition 2.7. A set M ⊆ PG(k − 1, q) is said to be a strong blocking set if

                                           ⟨H ∩ M ⟩ = H,

for every hyperplane H of PG(k − 1, q).

Theorem 2.8 (see [1], [36]). Let C be a nondegenerate [n, k, d]q code and let G = (g1 | . . . | gn ) ∈
Fk×n
 q   be any of its generator matrices. The following are equivalent:

   2. M = {⟨g1 ⟩, . . . , ⟨gn ⟩} is a strong blocking set in PG(k − 1, q).

    One of the main problem – from both a coding theoretic and geometric point of view – is the
construction of small strong blocking sets, or, equivalently, of short minimal codes. The first step
is to ask how small a strong blocking set can be. The following is a general lower bound observed
in [1], proved using the Combinatorial Nullstellensatz (see [4, 22] for alternative proofs.)

Theorem 2.9. For any prime power q, every strong blocking set in PG(k − 1, q) has size at least
(q + 1)(k − 1).

   Recently, this lower bound has been improved by using Delsarte’s linear programming bound in
coding theory.

Theorem 2.10 (see [10, Theorem 1.4], [34, Theorem 3.3]). For any prime power q, there is a
constant cq > 1 such that every strong blocking set in PG(k − 1, q) has size at least (cq − o(1))(q +
1)(k − 1).

    The best known existence results for the upper bounds on the size of the smallest strong blocking
set have been proved via the probabilistic method for the cases when q = 2 in [32] and for q > 2 in
[10, 2].
    The key idea behind these constructions, as well as many explicit constructions, is to consider
points sets that are unions of lines in a projective space. If ℓ1 , . . . , ℓm is the collection of lines, then
their union forms a strong blocking set (of size at most (q + 1)m) if and only if there is no pair
(S, H) where S is a codimension-2 subspace and H is a hyperplane containing S such that for all
i, either ℓi ⊆ S or ℓi ∩ H is a single point contained in S. These constructions often satisfy the
following stronger property, observed in [18, Theorem 11], which goes beyond the requirement that
the collection of lines itself forms a strong blocking set.

Definition 2.11. A set L of lines in PG(k − 1, q) is said to have the avoidance property if for
all codimension-2 subspaces W , there exists a line ℓ ∈ L such that W ∩ ℓ = ∅.

Theorem 2.12. If a set L of lines satisfies the avoidance property, then the point-set B = ∪ℓ∈L ℓ
forms a strong blocking set.

Proof. Let L be the set of lines and let B = ∪ℓ∈L ℓ, and let us proof the contrapositive argument
and suppose that B is not a strong blocking set. That means that there exists a hyperplane HΠ
such that B ∩ HΠ does not span HΠ . In particular, B ∩ HΠ is contained in a hyperplane H of HΠ .
Since we know that HΠ is a hyperplane, it meets every line of the projective space. Thus ℓ ∩ HΠ ̸= ∅
for all lines ℓ ∈ L, but since ℓ ⊆ B and B ∩ HΠ ⊆ H, it follows that ℓ ∩ H ̸= ∅. That is, H is
a codimension 2-subspace meeting every line ℓ ∈ L and so that will imply L does not have the
avoidance property.

   We will use this avoidance property in our construction as well.

2.3    Expander graphs
Throughout our constructions, we will use explicit constructions of constant-degree expander graphs,
as this general framework has been used by [4]. The informal way for someone to think about
expander graphs is that the edges of those graphs are very well spread out, ensuring that there
are many outgoing edges from all vertex subsets that are not too large. For further properties

and applications we refer the reader to the survey [24] for a formal definition and for a variety of
applications of expander graphs in different areas of mathematics and computer science.
    Graph expansion can be studied by the spectral properties of the graph. Given an n−vertex
graph G, we denote the eigenvalues of its adjacency matrix to be in the following decreasing order:
λ1 ≥ λ2 . . . ≥ λn . The eigenvalues of a graph encode substantial information about its underlying
structure. In particular, the second-largest eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix provides insight into
the connectivity and expansion properties of the graph. For instance, If G is a connected t−regular
graph, then its largest eigenvalue λ1 = t, while the second-largest eigenvalue satisfies λ2 < t.

strong blocking set constructed from expander graphs. We first provide the definition of vertex
integrity that measures the vulnerability of the graph under vertex deletions, essentially that means
how easy it is to break the graph into small pieces.

Since x ∈ Hi \ W , ℓ is not fully contained in W . If ℓ ∩ W contains a point z, then ℓ must be fully
contained in both Hi and Hj , and thus ℓ ⊆ W = Hi ∩ Hj , which is a contradiction. Therefore, there
are no edges between any pair of components Ci and Cj for i ̸= j and from the definition of fq (G),
we get fq (G) ≤ |S| + maxi∈[q+1] |Ci |.
    Since S ∪ Ci ⊆ Hi , we have |S| + |Ci | ≤ n − d for all i. Therefore, fq (G) = |S| + maxi |Ci | ≤ n − d,
contradicting our assumption that fq (G) > n − d.

Corollary 4.4. If V is a projective [n, k, d]q system and G = (V, E) is a graph with parameter
fq (G) ≥ n − d + 1, then B(V, G) is a strong blocking set in PG(k − 1, q) of size at most n + (q − 1)|E|.

Proof. A projective [n, k, d]q system corresponds to a set V of points in PG(k − 1, q) such that
every hyperplane contains at most n − d points of V . We saw that L(V, G) must be a set of |E|
lines that satisfy the avoidance property, and hence B(V, G) is a strong blocking set of size at most
n + (q − 1)|E|.

6    Conclusion
We introduced a new graph parameter that incorporates the size of the finite field and used it to give
improved explicit constructions of strong blocking sets. Specifically, we provide a lower bound on
this new graph parameter for the family of d−regular graphs that depends on both the eigenvalues
of the adjacency matrix and the size of the finite filed Fq , this deviating from the earlier work [4]
that relied on a graph parameter which was independent of the field size.
    In a recent preprint, Chen [14] has improved the explicit construction of strong blocking sets in
PG(k − 1, q) for all q ≤ 11 using concatenation of codes. For q ≥ 13, however, the strong blocking
sets from Theorem 5.5 and Theorem 5.1 are better than this and all previous constructions [8, 15,
14, 4].
    Several directions for future work remain. First, it would be nice to find a graph theoretic
construction of strong blocking sets that does not require the stronger line avoidance property,
Such a construction might involve a new graph parameter that further optimizes the constant and
leads to a smaller explicit constructions of strong blocking sets. Second, motivated by [11], it would
be interesting to extend our constructions based on the new graph parameter to strong blocking
sets with respect to codimension-2 spaces. Specifically, we would like to define and analyze similar
parameter for 3-uniform hypergraphs which could improve the bounds from [11]. Ultimately, one


                                                  16
can try generalizing our constructions to strong s-blocking sets. It would be interesting to match,
or improve, the recent bounds of Guo et al. [19] using these graph-theoretic techniques on strong
s-blocking sets.
