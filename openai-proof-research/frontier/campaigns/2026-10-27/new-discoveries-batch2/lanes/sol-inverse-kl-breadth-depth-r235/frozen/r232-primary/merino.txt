1. Introduction
    Let G be a graph, and let TG (x, y) denote its Tutte polynomial. For
a connected graph G, the values TG (1, 1), TG (2, 0), and TG (0, 2) count the
spanning trees, the acyclic orientations, and the totally cyclic orientations of
G, respectively. Merino and Welsh [9] conjectured that every loopless and
bridgeless connected graph G satisfies

                       max{TG (2, 0), TG (0, 2)} ≥ TG (1, 1).                (1)

Conde and Merino [10] proposed the following two strengthenings: the addi-
tive version
                    TG (2, 0) + TG (0, 2) ≥ 2TG (1, 1),                (2)
and the multiplicative version

                           TG (2, 0)TG (0, 2) ≥ TG (1, 1)2 .                 (3)

Note that (3) =⇒ (2) =⇒ (1).
    Matroidal versions of the three conjectures above were studied by
Knauer, Martínez-Sandoval, and Ramírez Alfonsín [7], and later by Ferroni
and Schröter [5]. These versions assert that the inequalities (1), (2), and (3)
hold with TG replaced by TM for every loopless and coloopless matroid M . A
minimum-size counterexample is one with as few elements as possible within
the class under consideration. Let Un,r denote the uniform matroid of rank
r on n elements, where 0 < r < n. For an integer q ≥ 2, its q-thickening
  (q)
Un,r is obtained by replacing every element with q parallel copies. The
unthickened case q = 1, namely the uniform matroid Un,r , is known to
satisfy (3), as a special case of the result for lattice path matroids [7]. Beke,
Csáji, Csikvári, and Pituk [1] constructed an infinite family of loopless and
coloopless matroids violating (3). Their construction uses the matroids
  (2)
Un,2n/3 , where n is divisible by three. Combining explicit formulas for
the Tutte polynomial of a uniform matroid and the thickening operation
with asymptotic estimates, they proved that these matroids violate the
inequality for all sufficiently large such n. They also exhibited the concrete
                    (2)
counterexample U33,22 , which has 66 elements. Taking the direct sum of a
multiplicative counterexample with its dual also yields counterexamples to

the additive and maximum versions. For uniform thickenings, this raises two
                  (q)
questions: can Un,r fail (3) when q ≥ 3, and what is the smallest number of
elements in a counterexample in this family?
    Several classes of matroids are already known to satisfy (3). Noble and
Royle [12] proved it for series–parallel graphs. Knauer, Martínez-Sandoval,
and Ramírez Alfonsín [7] proved it for lattice path matroids, and Ferroni
and Schröter [5] proved it for split matroids. For all loopless and coloopless
matroids, Jackson [6] proved the weaker inequality with 2 replaced by 3; the
constant was later reduced in [2, 4].
    Motivated by these results, we study uniform thickenings and minimum-
size counterexamples among all matroids. We obtain the following results.
                                              (q)
 (1) The ratio of the two sides of (3) for Un,r is strictly increasing in q for
                                           (q)
     integers q ≥ 2. We also prove that Un,r satisfies (3) for every q ≥ 3.
 (2) Among all uniform thickenings, the minimum number of elements in a
                                        (2)
     counterexample is 62, attained by U31,21 .
 (3) If M is a minimum-size loopless and coloopless counterexample, then
     its ground set is partitioned into two-element parallel or series classes.
     Deleting one element from every parallel pair and contracting one ele-
     ment from every series pair gives a connected, loopless, and coloopless
     minor N satisfying (3), and M has twice as many elements as N .

    The last result is related to the work of Noble and Royle [12, Theorem 4.5],
who proved that every loopless and bridgeless series–parallel graph satisfies
the multiplicative Merino–Welsh inequality. They also showed [12, Lem-
mas 2.2–2.4] that, if a counterexample exists among loopless and bridgeless
graphs, then every edge of a counterexample with the fewest edges belongs to
a series or parallel class of size two or three. Here we consider minimum-size
counterexamples among all loopless and coloopless matroids, where coun-
terexamples are known to exist. We prove that every element belongs to
exactly one series or parallel class of size two. These classes therefore form a
partition of the ground set.
    The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we recall
the definition of the Tutte polynomial and the formulas used below. In
Section 3, we evaluate the three values for uniform thickenings and prove
the monotonicity of their ratio in q. In Section 4, we determine the smallest
number of elements in a counterexample among uniform thickenings. In

Section 5, we study the structure of minimum-size counterexamples. The
finite verification used in Section 4 is given in the appendix.

5. Structure of smallest counterexamples
    Theorem 4.1 gives the smallest counterexample among uniform thicken-
ings. It is natural to ask what a smallest counterexample looks like in the
general matroid setting. We now consider a loopless and coloopless coun-
terexample M with as few elements as possible. The empty matroid has Tutte
polynomial 1, so M is nonempty. By the choice of M , we have ∆(M ) < 0 and
∆(Q) ≥ 0 for every loopless and coloopless Q with |E(Q)| < |E(M )|. When-
ever we make use of minimality, we must check that the minor in question is
loopless and coloopless first.
    Following Oxley [13, pp. 12, 151–152], a parallel class is an inclusion-
maximal set of nonloops, any two of which form a two-element circuit, and a
series class is a parallel class of the dual matroid. We also use the following
four operations: a parallel extension at an element e adds a new element
parallel to e, a parallel deletion removes an element that has a parallel mate,
and a series extension and a series contraction are the dual operations.
    We first show that M is connected and that every element has a parallel
or a series mate.

Lemma 5.1. The matroid M is connected.

Proof. Suppose that M = M1 ⊕ · · · ⊕ Mt , where t ≥ 2 and the Mi are the
nonempty connected components. Then every Mi is a smaller loopless and
coloopless matroid, so ∆(Mi ) ≥ 0 by the minimality of M . Since direct sums
multiply all three evaluations,
                           Y                   Y
           α(M )α∗ (M ) =     α(Mi )α∗ (Mi ) ≥   τ (Mi )2 = τ (M )2 ,
                           i                    i

which contradicts ∆(M ) < 0. Thus M is connected.

Lemma 5.2. Every element of M belongs to a two-element circuit or a two-
element cocircuit.

Proof. Fix e ∈ E(M ), and suppose that e belongs to neither type. Set
D = M \ e and Q = M/e. By Lemma 2.1, both D and Q are loopless
and coloopless. Since both minors are smaller than M , minimality gives
α(D)α∗ (D) ≥ τ (D)2 and α(Q)α∗ (Q) ≥ τ (Q)2 . Since e is neither a loop nor a
coloop, by the deletion–contraction formula, we have α(M ) = α(D) + α(Q),

α∗ (M ) = α∗ (D) + α∗ (Q) and τ (M ) = τ (D) + τ (Q). All these evaluations are
nonnegative, and

    α(D)α∗ (Q) + α(Q)α∗ (D) ≥ 2 α(D)α∗ (D)α(Q)α∗ (Q) ≥ 2τ (D)τ (Q).

Therefore,

     ∆(M ) = (α(D) + α(Q))(α∗ (D) + α∗ (Q)) − (τ (D) + τ (Q))2

contrary to ∆(M ) < 0. Thus every element belongs to a two-element circuit
or a two-element cocircuit.

Lemma 5.3. No element of M belongs both to a two-element circuit and
to a two-element cocircuit. Consequently, the nontrivial parallel classes and
nontrivial series classes are pairwise disjoint and partition E(M ).

Proof. Suppose that a two-element circuit C and a two-element cocircuit
C ∗ have a common element e. By Oxley [13, Proposition 2.1.11, p. 68], we
have C = C ∗ . If there exists g ∈ E(M ) − C, Lemma 5.1 and Oxley [13,
Proposition 4.1.3, p. 120] give a circuit D containing e and g. Applying
the same intersection property to D and the cocircuit C ∗ gives C ⊊ D,
contradicting the minimality of D as a circuit. Thus E(M ) = C and then
M∼  = U2,1 , so ∆(M ) = 0, a contradiction.
    Lemma 5.2 shows that the nontrivial parallel and series classes cover
E(M ). Distinct parallel classes are disjoint, as are distinct series classes.
Therefore, they form a partition.
    The partition above suggests a natural reduction: keep one element from
each class and remove the others. The next lemma shows that this reduction
is well behaved. We then show that these mates form disjoint two-element
classes. Finally, we show that every such class has size two.

Lemma 5.4. Let Q be a connected matroid whose ground set is partitioned
into nontrivial parallel classes Pi and nontrivial series classes Sj . Choose

                                      12
one representative from each class, delete the remaining elements of each
Pi , and contract those of each Sj . The resulting minor N has the following
properties.

  (i) N is connected and retains exactly one element from each class.

 (ii) For fixed representatives, N is independent of the order of the deletions
      and contractions. Different choices of representatives give isomorphic
      minors via automorphisms permuting elements within the classes.

(iii) If there are at least two classes, then N is loopless and coloopless.

We call N the compressed core of Q; by (ii), it is unique up to isomorphism.

Proof. Parallel deletions and series contractions preserve connectedness. By
Lemma 2.1, the intermediate minors remain loopless and coloopless as long
as at least two elements remain. This proves (i). For fixed representatives,
independence of the order follows from Oxley [13, Proposition 3.1.25, p. 104].
Changing representatives gives isomorphic minors because parallel elements
can be interchanged by automorphisms, as can series elements by duality.
This proves (ii). Finally, if there are at least two classes, N has at least two
elements, so the preceding argument also proves (iii).
   Note that the compressed core can be reached from Q in two ways: by
parallel deletions and series contractions, or, in the opposite order, by series
contractions and parallel deletions. We now apply Lemma 5.4 to M and
denote its compressed core by N .

Lemma 5.5. N has at least two elements. Moreover, N is a proper minor
of M , is connected, loopless, and coloopless, and satisfies ∆(N ) ≥ 0.

Proof. Lemma 5.3 ensures that there is at least one class in M . If there
were only one class, then M would be Un,1 or its dual. Both satisfy ∆(M ) =
2(2n − 2) − n2 ≥ 0 for n ≥ 2, so there are at least two classes.
    Lemma 5.4 now shows that N is connected, loopless, coloopless, and has
at least two elements. Every original class is nontrivial, so N is a proper
minor of M , and minimality gives ∆(N ) ≥ 0.

   The preceding results, together with Theorem 4.1, yield the main struc-
tural result of this paper.

Theorem 5.9. Let M be a minimum-size counterexample among all loopless
and coloopless matroids. Then M has the following structure.

  (i) M is connected.

 (ii) Every element belongs to exactly one two-element parallel class or one
      two-element series class; these classes partition E(M ).

(iii) The parallel deletions of one member of each parallel pair and the series
      contractions of one member of each series pair produce the compressed
      core N , unique up to isomorphism, which is a connected, loopless, and
      coloopless proper minor satisfying ∆(N ) ≥ 0.

(iv) |E(M )| = 2|E(N )|; in particular, |E(M )| is even.

 (v) If P and S are the representatives of the parallel and series pairs, re-
     spectively, then E(N ) = P ∪˙ S, and M is obtained from N by one
     parallel extension at each p ∈ P and one series extension at each s ∈ S.

(vi) Either (|E(M )|, |E(N )|) = (62, 31), or |E(M )| ≤ 60 and |E(N )| ≤ 30.

Appendix A. Finite verification for Theorem 4.1
    The proof of Theorem 4.1 leaves the 210 cases q = 2, 2 ≤ n ≤ 30,
1 ≤ r < n and 2r > n. The program in Listing 1 evaluates (10), (11), and
(8). It verifies R2 (n, r) ≥ 1 in every case.
    The program requires Python 3.9 or later and uses only the standard
library. Running it produces verified_cases=210 and failed_cases=0.
               Listing 1: Verification of the remaining cases in Theorem 4.1.
  1   #!/usr/bin/env python3
  2
  3   from math import comb
  4
  5   def evaluations(n, r):
  6       # Return (T(2,0), T(0,2), T(1,1)) for U_{n,r}^{(2)}.
  7       corank = n - r
  8       t20 = sum(comb(n - i - 1, corank - 1) * 2**i for i in range(1, r + 1))
  9       t02 = sum(comb(n - i - 1, corank - 1) * 2**i * 3 ** (r - i) for i in range(1, r + 1))
 10       t02 += sum(comb(n - j - 1, r - 1) * 3**r * 4**j for j in range(1, corank + 1))
 11       t11 = 2**r * comb(n, r)
 12       return t20, t02, t11
 13
 14
 15   def certify():
 16       verified_cases = 0
 17       failed_cases = []
 18
 19      for n in range(2, 31):
 20          for r in range(n // 2 + 1, n):
 21              t20, t02, t11 = evaluations(n, r)
 22              if t20 * t02 < t11 * t11:
 23                  failed_cases.append((n, r, t20, t02, t11))
 24              verified_cases += 1
 25
 26      if verified_cases != 210:
 27          raise RuntimeError(f"expected 210 cases, found {verified_cases}")
 28      if failed_cases:
 29          raise AssertionError(failed_cases)
 30
 31      print(f"verified_cases={verified_cases}")
 32      print("failed_cases=0")

 35       certify()
