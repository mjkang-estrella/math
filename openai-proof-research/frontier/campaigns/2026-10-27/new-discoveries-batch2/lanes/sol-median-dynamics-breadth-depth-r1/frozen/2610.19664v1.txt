                                              THE FENCE CONJECTURE FOR CONVEX PROJECTIVE POLYGONS

                                                                                            ERXIAO WANG


                                                   Abstract. We prove the fence conjecture of Dumas and Wolf: for a convex projective polygon
                                                   with at least five vertices, every zero of the Pick differential lies in the interior of the region
                                                   obtained by cutting off the triangles at consecutive triples of vertices. The proof gives a stronger
                                                   estimate for arbitrary properly convex domains. In affine-sphere coordinates normalized at a Pick




arXiv:2610.19664v1 [math.DG] 14 Oct 2026
                                                   zero, the domain contains the closed disk of radius 2−1/4 and its closure lies in the open disk
                                                   of radius 21/4 , unless it is a rotated square. The proof compares the canonical Monge–Ampère
                                                   potential of the domain with that of a normalized square. Their third-order contact gives
                                                   alternating local sign sectors. A planar separation argument and the maximum principle show
                                                   that the negative sectors belong to four distinct components, each meeting a different side of the
                                                   square. Rotation gives the inner disk bound, and conormal duality gives the outer bound. Both
                                                   constants are optimal for the class of properly convex domains normalized at a Pick zero. The
                                                   argument requires neither boundary smoothness nor any restriction on the multiplicity of the
                                                   Pick zero.

                                              A properly convex domain in RP carries a canonical complete hyperbolic affine sphere, and
                                           hence a Blaschke metric and a holomorphic cubic differential, the Pick differential. Its zeros are
                                           intrinsic projective invariants of the domain. For polygons, Dumas and Wolf [4] identified the
                                           corresponding cubic differentials with polynomial cubic differentials on the complex plane. In

                                           suggested a stronger statement: the curved barriers could be replaced by the diagonals joining
                                           the two neighbors of each vertex. This is the fence conjecture [4, Section 9.3, Conjecture 9.2].

   The estimate underlying Theorem 1.1 applies to domains that need not be polygonal. We
normalize the affine mean curvature to −1. At a point p of the affine sphere, choose a positively
oriented Blaschke-orthonormal tangent frame. The two tangent vectors and the position vector
form a unimodular basis of R3 . Send this basis to the standard basis and use the affine chart
x3 = 1. We call the resulting coordinates coordinates normalized at p. They are unique up to
a rotation. The chart is bounded on the projective domain; this fact and the corresponding
normalization of the potential are proved in Section 2. Write Bρ for the open Euclidean disk of
radius ρ centered at the origin.

   Theorem 1.2 concerns the geometry of an arbitrary properly convex domain at a point where
its cubic form vanishes. Related estimates compare the Hilbert and Blaschke metrics: Benoist and
Hulin [1, Proposition 3.4] established their uniform comparability, and Hildebrand [6] obtained
                                      THE FENCE CONJECTURE                                          3


optimal inequalities involving Hilbert distance and the Blaschke length of projective line segments.
Here the vanishing of the cubic form gives a bound on the entire domain in the affine chart fixed
by the position vector and the Blaschke metric at that point. The prescribed normalization is
essential to the values of the constants. The resulting inclusions are strict for every domain other
than a square, including domains with nonsmooth boundary, and their ratio yields the fence
theorem for polygons.
   Dumas and Wolf’s barriers use the homogeneous affine sphere over a triangular cone [4, Section
7]. We instead use a square: its dihedral symmetry forces the cubic form to vanish at the center,
making it a suitable comparison domain at a Pick zero. The square model also has a radially
symmetric Blaschke metric in a conformal coordinate in which its Pick differential is z dz 3 . Indeed,
rotating z preserves the modulus of this differential, and uniqueness of the complete, nonpositively
curved solution of Wang’s equation [4, Theorem 5.3] makes the metric rotation invariant. This
symmetry concerns the conformal coordinate, not the affine coordinates of the square or its
Monge–Ampère potential. Only the dihedral symmetry and conormal duality are needed below.
   The normalized square has the same third-order potential jet as Ω at p. Unless the domains
coincide, the first nonzero term of the difference is a harmonic homogeneous polynomial of degree
at least four. Its alternating signs give at least four local negative sectors. The essential issue
is global: two such sectors might a priori join elsewhere in the intersection of the domains. We
exclude this by a Jordan-curve argument and the maximum principle. Each resulting negative
component must meet the boundary of the square at a strictly negative value, while one square
side can serve at most one component. All four sides must therefore meet the open domain Ω.
Rotating the square gives the inner disk bound. Applying the same argument to the conormal
dual gives the outer bound. Their ratio is precisely what is needed to control the diagonals
defining the fence.

Proposition 4.5 (Square comparison). If Ω ̸= S, every open side of S meets Ω, and no vertex

Because A, B, C are cyclically ordered extreme vertices of a convex polygon,
                       det(B − A, C − A) > 0,            det(C − A, B − A) < 0.
Thus 0 lies strictly on the opposite side of AC from B, for every i, as illustrated in Figure 4(b). It
is also interior to the polygon. The finitely many strict inequalities remain valid on a neighborhood
of 0, so 0 ∈ int F (P ). Returning to the original chart proves the claim for p.                    □

6.1. A disk and the equality model. For the unit disk, the canonical potential is explicit:

The disk lies strictly between the two radii of Theorem 1.2, since 2−1/4 < 1 < 21/4 . Its affine
sphere is a quadric, with identically vanishing cubic form. Thus the disk theorem does not require
the Pick zero to be isolated.
  The normalized square realizes the other alternative. Its sides are tangent to the circle of
radius a and its vertices lie on the circle of radius R. Both strict inequalities fail, and the fence
consists of its center. No explicit special-function formula for its potential is needed to obtain
these constants.
