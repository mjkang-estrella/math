1.1. Background. The study of the singularity probability of random matrices goes back
to the work of Komlós in [14]. For matrices with independent Rademacher entries, an
exponential upper bound was obtained by Kahn, Komlós, and Szemerédi in [12], and the
sharp exponential rate was obtained by Tikhomirov [23]. For symmetric matrices, Costello,
Tao, and Vu [5] proved that the singularity tends to zero, and successive improvements
were obtained by Nguyen [16], Vershynin [24], Ferber and Jain [7], Campos, Mattos,
Morris, and Morrison [4], and Campos, Jenssen, Michelen, and Sahasrabudhe in [1]. The
exponential bound for symmetric Rademacher matrices was proved by Campos, Jenssen,
Michelen, and Sahasrabudhe in [3, Theorem 1.1]. An exponential singularity bound was
also proved in [2, Theorem 1.1] for symmetric matrices whose entries on and above the
diagonal are independent copies of a centered subgaussian random variable of variance
one.

1.2. Proof ideas. Our main step compares column space probabilities using a common
auxiliary random subspace. This reduces the symmetric problem to a corank bound for
a matrix with independent entries.
   For corank at least three, the principal submatrix argument of Han in [8, Section 6]
gives a large symmetric block with the same rank as the original matrix and comparable
corank. The remaining columns, restricted to its rows, must lie in its column space; they
are independent of the block and of one another. Lemma 2.5 bounds this probability
using the mass of the column space under a fixed auxiliary distribution. Small mass gives
the required bound directly. We call the remaining subspaces thick.
   Following the basis argument of Tao and Vu in [22, Section 4], we sample independent
vectors from this distribution conditioned on lying in a fixed thick subspace, and complete
them to a basis with a small number of matrix columns. We extend their indices to
obtain an invertible principal block. Fixing the corresponding columns leaves a large
random symmetric block and reduces the problem to a weighted sum over subspaces.
The weights are the probabilities that the sampled vectors and the fixed columns span
the given subspace.
   Removing the conditioning from all sampled vectors can cost a factor exponential in
the square of the matrix size. Instead, we construct an auxiliary matrix with the same
fixed columns and two off-diagonal blocks given by a rectangular matrix with independent
entries and its transpose, all other entries are zero (see e.g., [8, 2]). Comparing the entire
kernel system bounds each conditional column space probability by the probability that
the auxiliary column space is contained in the given subspace. With the original columns
fixed, the distribution of the auxiliary matrix is the same for every subspace.
   When the auxiliary column space is contained in the given subspace, we remove con-
ditioning only from the sampled vectors needed to span that subspace together with the
auxiliary column space. For each choice of indices, the resulting spanning events are
disjoint for distinct subspaces. Lemma 4.2 applies the resulting summation bound to a
common random subspace. The number of vectors needed is controlled by the corank of
the auxiliary block with independent entries, which in turn must be at least a constant
multiple of the codimension of the given subspace. Combining the rectangular corank
bound in [18, Corollary 1.2] with the bounds for the conditioning loss and the number of
choices gives the required probability bound.
   For corank one, the relevant principal block B is invertible, so its column space gives
no useful constraint on Z. We use the Schur complement and the decoupling inequality of
Costello, Tao, and Vu [5] , in the reduction developed by Ferber and Jain [7, Section 2.3]
and by Campos, Mattos, Morris, and Morrison [4, Lemma 2.1 and Appendix A] (see also
[3, Lemma 9.1 and Appendix C]) . Fixing one of the vectors and adding the corresponding
4                      HUAIJIN LIANG, KEXIN YU, AND TINGZHOU YU

fixed row expresses the equation as the event that an independent random vector lies in
the augmented column space. Apart from an exponentially small exceptional contribution,
a normal vector to this space has a nonzero coordinate among the row and column indices
of one of the chosen square blocks. If the auxiliary column space is contained in this
space, the corresponding auxiliary block must be singular, allowing the same comparison
in Lemma 5.2. Corank two reduces to corank one in a principal submatrix.

                   5. Corank one and completion of the proof
  For corank one, an invertible principal block leaves only one column outside it. Its
column space is the whole ambient space, so the remaining column always lies in it. This
condition therefore gives no probability bound. Adding the transpose of that column
below the block gives a hyperplane as the new column space. However, the added row
and the vector required to lie in this space depend on the same random column. Fixing
the added row would also fix that vector.
  The Schur complement gives a quadratic equation in the remaining column. We split
the column into a small and a large coordinate block and apply decoupling, reducing the
problem to a bilinear equation in two independent difference vectors; related reductions
are used in [24, Sections 5 and 8.2]. Fixing a nonzero value of the difference vector on the
small block determines the added row. The bilinear equation then says that the random
vector from the large block, extended by zeros, lies in the column space of the augmented
matrix. This vector is independent of that matrix.
  The law of this vector is used in both parts of the thin/thick argument. If it assigns
small mass to the column space, the probability that the vector lies in that space is small.
Lemma 5.2 bounds the probability that this mass is large. We keep most coordinates in
the large block so that the basis argument needs only a small number of matrix columns.
The small block still contains a fixed positive fraction of the coordinates, so the probability
that its difference vector is zero is exponentially small.

n < n1.1 and 1 ≤ k ≤ n, we have cβ nk ≤ cβ n21.1 ≤ log(2), so 2e−cβ nk ≥ 1.
  Finally, condition on all diagonal entries in the original model. Under this conditional
law, the entries above the diagonal are independent with their original distributions. Since
the bound for fixed diagonal entries is uniform in their values, averaging over their joint
distribution proves the theorem.                                                          □
