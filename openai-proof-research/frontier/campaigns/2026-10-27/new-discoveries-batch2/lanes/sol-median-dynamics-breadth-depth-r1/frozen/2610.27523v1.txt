                                                                                                Abstract
                                                        The orthogonal neighborhood of a set in real projective space is the union of the
                                                    hyperplanes perpendicular to its points. We prove that caps minimize neighborhood
                                                    measure among measurable sets of fixed measure in RPn−1 , for n ≥ 3, with rigidity up to
                                                    null sets. This yields the double-cap conjecture: a measurable subset of S n−1 containing
                                                    no orthogonal pair has measure at most that of two opposite open caps of radius π/4.
                                                    The argument combines compactness of the Funk transform, rotation comparisons, and
                                                    projective variations. We also obtain qualitative stability and determine the measurable
                                                    fractional chromatic number of the spherical orthogonality graph. AI played an assistive
                                                    role in preparing this paper. The formalization and verification workflow for a separate
                                                    Lean 4 proof of the main inequality and double-cap result, based on a different argument,
                                                    were carried out almost entirely using AI-assisted tools.

                                                Keywords: spherical geometry, isoperimetric inequality, double-cap conjecture.


                                           1        Introduction
                                           The spherical isoperimetric inequality says that caps have the smallest perimeter among sets
                                           of prescribed volume. Symmetrization and stability of this comparison are central themes in
                                           the geometry of the sphere; see Sperner [43] and Bögelein, Duzaar, and Fusco [7]. We study
                                           a related extremal problem in which perimeter is replaced by the measure of the orthogonal
                                           neighborhood: the set of all directions perpendicular to at least one point of the original set.
                                           This quantity measures how many directions the set excludes if orthogonality is forbidden.
                                               For spherical convex bodies contained in an open hemisphere, Gao, Hug, and Schneider [19]
                                           proved that caps minimize neighborhood measure at fixed volume. Their result belongs to
                                           the theory of spherical mean width and polar-volume inequalities. This comparison naturally
                                           raises the question of extending the same bound to arbitrary measurable sets in real projective
                                           space. We prove this extension and characterize the equality cases.
                                               The principal application resolves Witsenhausen’s problem [45] and the double-cap
                                           conjecture [23]: a measurable subset of a sphere containing no orthogonal pair has measure at
                                           most that of two opposite open caps of angular radius π/4. A set containing no orthogonal
                                           pair is disjoint from its neighborhood, so a lower bound for neighborhood measure gives an
                                           upper bound for the measure of the set. The full inequality also controls the measures of two
                                           different sets when no point of one is orthogonal to a point of the other.
                                               To state the result, let n ≥ 3, let S n−1 ⊂ Rn carry surface measure σ normalized to
                                           one, and write M = RPn−1 . A point of M is a line through the origin; the quotient map

    The convex comparison also connects with Urysohn’s inequality. The mean width
of a Euclidean convex body is the average, over directions, of the distance between its
parallel supporting hyperplanes; at fixed volume, balls minimize this average. Together with
inequalities for polar bodies, this is part of the Brunn–Minkowski theory; see Schneider [40].
On the sphere, the probability that a uniformly random great hypersphere meets a convex
body plays the corresponding role. For K ⊂ S n−1 contained in an open hemisphere, this
probability is µ(N (π(K))), or 2U1 (K) in the normalization of Gao, Hug, and Schneider [19].
Their inequality U1 (K) ≥ U1 (C), for a cap C of equal volume, is therefore a spherical Urysohn
inequality. Through polarity it also says that caps maximize polar volume at fixed volume, a
spherical Blaschke–Santaló inequality. Theorem 1.1 extends the hitting comparison beyond
the class of convex bodies.
    The interpretation by random hyperspheres also places the inequality in stochastic
geometry. Hack and Pivovarov [21] proved that, among compact sampling sets of equal
positive volume in an open hemisphere, caps minimize the expected hitting functional of the
convex hull of a fixed number of independent uniform points. Hug and Reichenbacher [22]
obtained quantitative stability estimates for the convex hitting inequality and applied them to
cells of random spherical tessellations. Such stability results ask whether a set whose functional
is nearly minimal must be close to a cap. This question is also central to quantitative spherical
isoperimetry [7]. We obtain qualitative stability for arbitrary measurable projective sets: at
fixed measure, convergence to the sharp neighborhood bound forces convergence in measure,
after rotations, to a cap.
    Extending the comparison to arbitrary measurable sets requires more than convexification.
A great hypersphere may separate two components without meeting either, while meeting
their convex hull; taking the hull can therefore increase the neighborhood measure being
minimized.
    Spherical rearrangement inequalities provide another natural comparison with caps. For a
bounded nonnegative kernel that is nondecreasing in the inner product, the Baernstein–Taylor
inequality [3] maximizes the integral over two sets by replacing them with caps of the same
respective measures and a common center. Burchard and Hajaiej [8] extended this theory to
certain nonlinear integral functionals and analyzed their equality cases. These comparisons
depend on monotonicity of the kernels in spherical distance. Orthogonality instead singles
out inner product zero: the indicators of thin belts |x · y| < ε are not monotone in x · y. Thus
these rearrangement theorems do not directly supply the neighborhood inequality.

     A complementary approach uses harmonic analysis. For a measurable set E with indicator
1E , averaging 1E (Rx)1E (Ry) over rotations R produces a continuous positive definite kernel
depending only on x · y. Schoenberg [41] characterized continuous kernels of this kind
by expansions in Gegenbauer polynomials with nonnegative coefficients. This positivity
is the basis of the linear programming method of Delsarte, Goethals, and Seidel [14] for
spherical codes: finite configurations with prescribed restrictions on their pairwise angles. For
measurable sets avoiding an angle, the same method turns the forbidden inner product into a
constraint on the correlation kernel. DeCorte and Pikhurko [13] combined this constraint with
combinatorial restrictions to improve Witsenhausen’s bound on S 2 from 1/3 to α3 < 0.313.
     Stronger optimization methods retain more of the restrictions imposed by an actual set. In
finite graph theory, Lovász’s theta function [29] gives a semidefinite bound for independence.
For spherical codes, Bachoc and Vallentin [2] strengthened linear programming by using
matrix positivity conditions associated with triples of points. In the measurable setting,
DeCorte, de Oliveira Filho, and Vallentin [12] gave an exact convex optimization formulation
using completely positive kernels, a stronger condition than positive definiteness. Bekker et
al. [5] developed optimization

    The same extremal measure governs a fractional coloring parameter. The spherical
orthogonality graph Gn has vertex set S n−1 , with distinct vertices adjacent exactly when they
are orthogonal. Its measurable independent sets are precisely those in (1.9). Identifying
antipodal vertices gives the projective orthogonality graph Pn on M , where [x] and [y] are
adjacent when x · y = 0. In this language, N (A) is the set of all neighbors of points of A.
    The measurable chromatic number χm (G) is the least number of measurable independent
sets in a partition of the vertices of G. A measurable fractional coloring allows a finite family
of such sets to overlap, assigning nonnegative weights whose sum at each vertex is at least
one. The infimum of the total weight is the measurable fractional chromatic number χf,m (G).

     Orthogonality also gives the problem a connection to quantum theory. Orthogonal state
vectors represent states that a suitable measurement can perfectly distinguish. The Kochen–
Specker theorem [25] shows that, already in dimension three, it is impossible to assign values
in {0, 1} to all rays (one-dimensional subspaces) so that each orthonormal basis contains
exactly one ray assigned the value 1. Such an assignment would select a predetermined
outcome in every basis, with the value of a ray independent of the basis in which it is
measured. A more direct connection to the extremal measure problem was established by
Montina [31], who used the double-cap conjecture to obtain conditional lower bounds on the
classical communication needed to simulate quantum state transmission and measurement.
     The proof determines the optimal neighborhood profile, which assigns to each p the least
possible neighborhood measure of a set of measure p. This optimization can be expressed
in terms of pairs of sets with no orthogonal pair between them. The Funk transform [18],
which averages functions over great hyperspheres, records the incidence between the two
sets. We use its compactness to obtain optimal pairs, adapting the method of DeCorte and
Pikhurko [13] for the existence of maximum-measure angle-avoiding sets when n ≥ 3.
     To identify the profile, we follow the variational comparison approach used by Morgan
and Johnson [32] and Bayle and Rosales [4] for isoperimetric profiles. Rotation comparisons
first show that the optimal profile is concave. Paired projective transformations then allow
us to deform the optimal pairs while preserving their orthogonality exclusion. A key step is
to show that, at almost every prescribed measure, an optimizer admits a deformation that
changes its measure to first order. This allows us to extract a differential inequality for the
profile from the second variations. Integrating the comparison with the cap equation identifies
the profile, and the equality conditions force the extremizers to agree almost everywhere with
caps.

2.3   Proof outline
Caps give f ≤ gn ; we need the reverse inequality and the classification of optimizers. Concavity
of f , which we establish first, gives only the chord bound f (p) ≥ p from the endpoint values.
The main idea is to prove concavity of Φ = f ◦ gn−1 . In the coordinate s = gn (p), the cap
profile becomes the line s 7→ s. Since both profiles have endpoint values 0 and 1, concavity
places Φ above this line, giving f ≥ gn . The proof develops the variations needed for this
comparison as follows.
    Attainment (§ 3.1). Compactness of the Funk transform preserves zero incidence under
weak limits. Although the limits need not be indicators, their positive supports have enough
measure to recover an optimal pair. Passing to density-one representatives restores pointwise
avoidance, and trimming the sets gives the prescribed measures. These fixed sets are the
starting point for the later variations.
    Cap comparison and concavity (§ 3.2). We compute cap neighborhoods and establish
p ≤ f (p) ≤ gn (p), with endpoint values f (0) = 0 and f (1) = 1. Adding small pieces of
rotated caps proves continuity of f . We then compare a set with a rotated copy. Their
union and intersection have measures p + h and p − h, while their neighborhood measures
sum to at most twice the original neighborhood measure. Rotations realize every sufficiently
small h, giving the midpoint inequality and hence concavity of f . Together with the cap
bound, it gives a positive, nonincreasing right derivative f+′ and second-order expansions
almost everywhere. These are the regularity properties needed to formulate and integrate the
differential comparison.

                                                      7
   Projective variations (§ 3.3). For an optimal pair (A, B), deform the sets to (T A, T −T B)
and write a(T ) = µ(T A), b(T ) = µ(T −T B). Avoidance is preserved, so

                       b(T ) + f (a(T )) ≤ 1,       with equality at T = I.

The projective Jacobian makes a and b analytic without boundary assumptions on the sets.
We compute their derivatives and establish the rotation averages and nonconstancy used
below. Each variation starts from one fixed optimal pair; the transformed sets need not
remain optimal. They provide competitors at the new measure a(T ), which is why f (a(T ))
enters the comparison. The measure functions are analytic in T , whereas the profile needs
only its second-order expansion at the original measure p.
    Contraction identity (§ 3.4). The first measure derivatives are represented by the moment
matrices GE . We construct a linear map Ψ that preserves positive semidefiniteness and sends
each operator representing second measure derivatives to a multiple of GE . At a second-order
point, the optimality expression has a maximum at I, so the operator representing its negative
second variation is positive semidefinite. Applying Ψ preserves this positivity and expresses
the resulting inequality in terms of GA and GB . The first-order optimality relation then
eliminates GB . The same contraction identity is also used to exclude isotropic optimizers.
    Nonisotropy (§ 3.5). At every second-order point of f , we prove that every optimizer
satisfies GA ̸= 0. Thus some projective deformation changes its measure to first order. This
is necessary because otherwise the term involving f ′′ in the second variation would vanish.
Suppose GA = 0. Analyticity and nonconstancy give a first nonzero Taylor term of degree
m ≥ 2 in the measure variation. Optimality gives an upper bound of order 2m for the signed
displacement from the tangent line to b = 1 − f (a) at the original pair. This displacement
has zero average over deformations conjugated by rotations, so its terms of degree below
2m vanish. Along a direction where the degree-m term is nonzero, the contraction identity
bounds the measure derivative by order 2m − 2. Integration gives order 2m − 1 > m, a
contradiction.
    Differential comparison (§ 3.6). Applying Ψ to second-order optimality gives a matrix
inequality involving GA . Since GA ̸= 0 is trace-free, it has a negative eigenvalue, which yields
a scalar bound on f ′′ /f ′ . Among sets of prescribed measure, a cap maximizes the second
moment in its axial direction. For a fixed unit axis z, this follows by placing the prescribed
measure where (x · z)2 is largest. The level sets of this function have measure zero, so equality
determines the cap up to a null set. This sharp moment bound makes the scalar estimate
precisely
                       f ′′ (p)   gn′′ (p)
                                ≤          at every second-order point p.
                       f ′ (p)    gn′ (p)
     Identification of the profile and rigidity (§ 3.7). Monotonicity of f+′ lets us integrate
the differential comparison, including possible jumps and singular decreases, to show that
f+′ /gn′ is nonincreasing. In the coordinate s = gn (p), this ratio is the right derivative of Φ,
so Φ is concave and the endpoint argument gives f = gn . The differential comparison is
initially available only almost everywhere. Once the profiles agree, the smoothness of gn
makes every interior measure a second-order point of f . We can therefore return to the
variational inequalities for any optimizer, and equality in the moment bound forces it to agree
almost everywhere with a cap. Finally, for a completed-measurable set, choose a Borel subset
of full measure. Its neighborhood is contained in the original neighborhood, so monotonicity
of inner measure transfers the lower bound. If equality holds, the Borel subset is itself an
optimizer, and its rigidity transfers to the original set.

3     Proof of the neighborhood inequality
An optimal avoiding pair turns the inequality µ(B) + f (µ(A)) ≤ 1 into an equality, so
admissible deformations constrain the first and second measure variations. To use this
observation, we first prove that optimal pairs exist and that f admits second-order expansions
almost everywhere. Compactness and rotation averaging provide these two ingredients.

4    Corollaries and applications
The neighborhood inequality first gives a sharp bound for the measures of two avoiding sets.
Taking the sets equal yields the double-cap bound; the Funk transform gives a corresponding
bound for positivity sets. Rigidity and compactness then yield stability, while the independence
bound determines the measurable fractional chromatic number and the exponential growth
rate of the measurable chromatic number.
