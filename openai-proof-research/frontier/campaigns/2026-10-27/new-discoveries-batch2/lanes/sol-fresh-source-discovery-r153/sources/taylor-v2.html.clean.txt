 Abstract 
 Let f n ​ ( x ) = ∑ j = 0 n ​ a j ​ x j be the Taylor polynomials of a power series with nonzero rational coefficients. We study
the proportions of degrees for which the Galois group over \Q is symmetric, alternating, or neither. We prove that the set of degrees n ≥ 3 with symmetric group can be chosen arbitrarily, even for an entire function with positive rational coefficients whose Taylor polynomials have distinct negative real roots. More precisely, at each degree n ≥ 3 outside the chosen set, f n ​ has exactly one rational root, and the group on the other roots is the full
symmetric group. Thus the proportions need not converge; when they do, the
proportion of other groups can be any number in [ 0 , 1 ] . These conclusions answer negatively two questions of Katz and Rivin. Moreover, for fixed s\in\Q\setminus\Z , we also give a condition on the prime divisors of rational multipliers of the coefficients s / ( s + j ) that forces symmetric groups in density one. For the Taylor polynomials of ( 1 − x ) − s , we determine the groups in all sufficiently large degrees. The number of exceptions to the symmetric group up to degree X 
is c s ​ X ​ + O s ​ ( 1 ) , with an explicit constant c s ​ ≥ 0 .

We also determine the groups of the central-binomial Taylor polynomials in every degree and prove that every nonconstant
Catalan Taylor polynomial has symmetric group. This proves Sun's Catalan group conjecture and his irreducible-reduction
criterion for the monic reciprocals of the central-binomial polynomials. 
 Introduction 
 
 Let 
 f ( x ) = j ≥ 0 ∑ ​ a j ​ x j ∈ Q [[ x ]] , a j ​  = 0 , f n ​ ( x ) = j = 0 ∑ n ​ a j ​ x j . 
 
 We study the Galois group over Q of the degree- n Taylor polynomial f n ​ as n varies. In particular, we ask how often this group is the symmetric group S n ​ , the alternating group A n ​ , or neither. 
 
 The classical example is the exponential series. Schur proved in 1930 that 
 E n ​ ( x ) = j = 0 ∑ n ​ j ! x j ​ , n ≥ 1 , 
 
 has Galois group A n ​ when 4 ∣ n and S n ​ otherwise [ 40 ] . Symmetric groups therefore occur in three quarters of the degrees and alternating groups in one quarter. Coleman’s proof [ 11 ] uses Newton polygons to obtain permutations in the Galois group from the prime divisibility of the factorial denominators. Related arguments apply to generalized Laguerre polynomials [ 21 ] . 
 
 On the other hand, the geometric series gives a different example. Its Taylor polynomial is ( x n + 1 − 1 ) / ( x − 1 ) , so its splitting field is cyclotomic and its group is abelian. For n ≥ 4 the group is therefore neither S n ​ nor A n ​ . In these two examples, all three proportions converge, and the proportion of groups of neither kind is zero or one. 
 
 Katz and Rivin [ 26 Questions 1.1–1.2] ask whether this happens for every power series with nonzero rational coefficients. More precisely, they ask whether the three proportions always converge and, when they all converge, whether the third limit must be zero or one. 
 
 Our first result answers both questions negatively. We prove that for any set of degrees B ⊆ { 3 , 4 , … } , we construct an entire function with positive rational coefficients such that, for n ≥ 3 , its degree- n Taylor polynomial has group S n ​ precisely when n ∈ B . In the remaining degrees n ≥ 3 , it has one rational root and an irreducible factor of degree n − 1 with group S n − 1 ​ . Every Taylor polynomial has distinct negative real roots. Thus even these restrictions on convergence, signs, and zeros leave the set of symmetric degrees arbitrary. 
 
 We then give conditions on the prime divisors of the coefficients that force the symmetric group to occur in density one. For nonintegral binomial powers, an exact discriminant formula determines the exceptional degrees and gives their asymptotic number. For the central-binomial and Catalan generating functions, we determine the groups in every degree over Q .

 Methods and further results 
 
 The construction in Theorem A chooses one coefficient at a time. Reversing the next Taylor polynomial gives 
 X n f n ​ ( 1/ X ) = g n ​ ( X ) + a n ​ , g n ​ ( X ) = j = 0 ∑ n − 1 ​ a j ​ X n − j . (11) 
 
 The coefficients already chosen determine g n ​ , while a n ​ is now a constant parameter. We arrange that the relevant polynomials have simple critical points and distinct critical values, with no critical value shared by two of them. A simultaneous version of the linear perturbation argument in [ 8 Theorem 13] preserves these conditions outside a finite set of choices. These conditions give the full product of the symmetric groups before specialization. Hilbert irreducibility then gives the required groups for all segments ending at the new degree and makes their splitting fields disjoint from the preceding fields. Adjoining one root before specialization gives the alternative with one rational root. The specializations can be made small and positive, so Hutchinson’s coefficient inequalities [ 23 ] give the assertions about real zeros. 
 
 The simultaneous control of proper segments is related to the conjecture of Bary-Soroker and Ben-Porath [ 2 Conjecture 1] for proper segments of the exponential series. They prove simultaneous probabilistic results, and Weinbaum [ 47 Theorem 1.1] proves density-one symmetry for each fixed positive shift. The all-degree problem for proper segments of the fixed exponential series remains open. Earlier questions about irreducibility of Taylor polynomials arose in Katz’s 2009 Princeton course; see [ 9 Introduction] . Chambert-Loir also constructs sections with highly multiple roots [ 9 Example 3.7] . 
 
 For Theorem B, suitable primes larger than the degree give a Newton polygon with two primitive edges. The corresponding local factors have degrees j and n − j , and inertia supplies a permutation with these two cycles. Using several such primes forces irreducibility and excludes partitions of the roots into nontrivial Galois blocks. Müller’s classification [ 32 Theorem 3.3] then gives containment of A n ​ ; this step uses the classification of finite simple groups. In even degree, the local discriminant square class at the chosen primes is independent of the allowed multipliers. Heath–Brown’s quadratic large sieve [ 22 ] bounds the number of degrees in which the discriminant can be a square in the fixed number field. For binomial polynomials, the exact discriminant formula reduces the exceptional count to quadratic congruences. 
 
 The Catalan proof begins with the transitivity supplied by Seki’s irreducibility theorem. In sufficiently large degree, local inertia rules out all possible block systems except a partition into two blocks. We extend Seki’s trace argument from rational factors to possible Galois blocks. The resulting congruences and size bounds force the sums of roots in the two blocks to be distinct rational integers, which is incompatible with transitivity. The classical Jordan–Marggraff theorem [ 29 Theorem A] then gives the required alternating containment, and an odd inertia permutation gives the symmetric group. 
 
 There are also examples with constant term 1 and all other coefficients in { 1 , … , 35 } . Proposition 2.12 gives, after any finite choice of these coefficients, a computable continuation for which O f ​ ( N ) has lower limit zero and upper limit one. The proof combines a random-polynomial estimate from [ 3 Theorems 1(a) and 6] with extensions having many cyclotomic factors. § 7 gives criteria for convergence and shows that convergence cannot be decided uniformly from a program for the rational coefficients. It also gives a finite test when the Galois labels themselves are generated by an automaton.

 Local Galois criteria 
 
 The arithmetic arguments use two ways to obtain large permutation groups: a single short cycle, or several permutations with two cycles. We first record the group criteria, then explain how local factorizations and ramification determine the group over a number field. 
 
 Permutation group criteria 
 
 For P ∈ Q [ x ] of degree n , choose a nonzero rational multiple P ∈ Z [ x ] . A prime ℓ is good for P if it divides neither its leading coefficient nor its discriminant. If P is separable, multiplying it by a nonzero scalar changes its discriminant by a ( 2 n − 2 ) th power, so its square class is unchanged. 
 
 A short cycle in a primitive group gives alternating containment. The following form also covers cycles of composite length. 
 
 Lemma 3.2. Let G ≤ S n ​ be primitive. If G contains a single k -cycle with 2 ≤ k ≤ n /2 , then G contains A n ​ . 
 
 Proof. The classical Jordan–Marggraff transitivity theorem [ 29 Theorem A] makes G at least ( n − k + 1 ) -transitive, hence at least ( k + 1 ) -transitive. After numbering the points, suppose σ = ( 1 2 ⋯ k ) belongs to G . By transitivity on ordered k -tuples, a conjugate of σ is τ = ( 1 2 ⋯ k − 1 k + 1 ) . Then σ τ − 1 = ( 1 k + 1 k ) is a 3-cycle. Since G is 3-transitive, it contains every 3-cycle, and these generate A n ​ . □ 
 
 For the reciprocal families, local ramification gives two cycles whose lengths need not be coprime. Varying these lengths rules out block systems and the exceptional primitive groups. 
 
 Lemma 3.3. Let G ≤ S n ​ be transitive, with n > 64 . Suppose that a nonempty set J of integers satisfies n /2 < j < n − n ​ ( j ∈ J ) , 
 and that G contains a permutation of cycle type ( j ) ( n − j ) for every j ∈ J . Suppose also that, for each factorization n = m r with 2 ≤ m ≤ r , some j ∈ J is divisible by neither m nor r . Then A n ​ ⊆ G . 
 
 Proof. If a permutation with exactly two point orbits preserves a block partition with block size m and r blocks, its induced action has either one or two block orbits. In the latter case each point orbit is a union of whole blocks, so m ∣ j . In the former case each point orbit visits all r blocks, so r ∣ j . Thus every such block partition would require m ∣ j or r ∣ j for all j ∈ J . Interchanging m , r if necessary, the hypothesis rules this out. Hence G is primitive. 
 
 Suppose now that G does not contain A n ​ . Apply Müller’s classification [ 32 Theorem 3.3] to an element with shorter cycle length k = n − j . The affine cases have k = 1 , k = 4 , or k = p with n = p e , e ≥ 2 , apart from degrees at most 25. These contradict n ​ < k < n /2 . The almost simple cases other than the natural alternating and symmetric groups have k = 1 or k = n /2 , apart from degrees at most 24. These too are excluded. The product actions in part (2) of the classification have n = r 2 , and both cycle lengths are divisible by r . Applying the classification to every element under consideration would therefore give r ∣ j for every j ∈ J . The hypothesis applied to n = r ⋅ r excludes this last case. Thus G contains A n ​ .

 Further questions 
 
 The prescription theorem and bounded-coefficient construction settle the universal convergence and zero-one assertions in the Katz–Rivin questions. A structural description of the series with convergent frequencies remains open. § 7 reduces the problem to two arithmetic means, while the undecidability results rule out a uniform algorithm on programs for arbitrary nonzero rational coefficients, and even for the existence of the other frequency over a fixed finite alphabet. Specific coefficient families can nevertheless admit precise answers, as Theorems B–E show. 
 
 Classical sections and smaller thresholds. Theorems D and E determine the central-binomial and Catalan groups in every degree. Sun’s additional bound n 2 + n + 5 for the least prime at which the monic reciprocal x n P n ​ ( 1/ x ) has irreducible reduction remains open [ 45 ] , part (i). For general nonintegral binomial exponents, the degrees below the threshold remain to be classified uniformly in the parameter. It would also be useful to sharpen the bounds (25) and (A.5) and to obtain a two-cycle criterion adapted to these inertia elements without using the classification of finite simple groups. Another quantitative question is to make the implied constant in (6) numerical. An explicit containment threshold alone does not supply a numerical constant in the quadratic-large-sieve estimate. 
 
 The logarithmic discriminant. Yamamura’s conjecture asks for symmetry in every degree for 1 + x + x 2 /2 + ⋯ + x n / n . Since alternating containment is known, proving that square discriminants occur in density zero would already determine the frequency triple as ( 1 , 0 , 0 ) . The results of [ 13 ] for degrees n = m q with fixed m and q prime do not by themselves give density one among all degrees. Theorem B uses a nonintegral parameter to obtain a unique denominator divisible by a prime p > n ; this argument does not apply to the logarithm. Square-discriminant densities also remain to be determined in general for the factorial-denominator families of Proposition 6.11 . 
 
 Rational functions. Do all three frequencies converge for every rational function with nonzero Taylor coefficients? The examples in § 6 show that the other frequency need not be an endpoint. Even the class f ( x ) = P ( x ) / ( 1 − x ) is a natural general problem: if n ≥ de g P and P ( 1 )  = 0 , then 
 ( 1 − x ) f n ​ ( x ) = P ( x ) − P ( 1 ) x n + 1 
 
 This connects the question to sparse polynomials with a growing exponent. The finite-alphabet construction varies the coefficients in long blocks and does not decide convergence for a fixed rational generating function. 
 
 Irreducible nonconvergence and alternating prescriptions. Can the frequencies fail to converge when every sufficiently large section is irreducible? Our nonconvergent examples obtain other degrees through rational or cyclotomic factors. An irreducible example would require compatible alternating groups or other proper transitive groups in a suitable collection of degrees; oscillation between symmetric and alternating groups would already suffice. More generally, can one prescribe alternating groups as well as symmetric and other groups in one Taylor sequence? 
 
 There is a concrete limitation to the one-parameter method in § 2 . If g ∈ Q [ X ] is Morse of degree n , then D ( T ) = Disc X ​ ( g ( X ) + T ) is squarefree of degree n − 1 : its simple zeros are the negatives of the distinct critical values. The smooth projective model of Y 2 = D ( T ) has genus ⌊( n − 2 ) /2 ⌋ . For n ≥ 6 , Faltings’s theorem [ 17 ] therefore leaves only finitely many rational parameters with square discriminant. Thus, for a fixed Morse prefix, the argument cannot supply alternating specializations at arbitrarily small nonzero parameters. An alternating prescription may require additional parameters or a different generic group.

 Introduction 
 
 Let 
 f ( x ) = j ≥ 0 ∑ ​ a j ​ x j ∈ Q [[ x ]] , a j ​  = 0 , f n ​ ( x ) = j = 0 ∑ n ​ a j ​ x j . 
 
 We study the Galois group over Q of the degree- n Taylor polynomial f n ​ as n varies. In particular, we ask how often this group is the symmetric group S n ​ , the alternating group A n ​ , or neither. 
 
 The classical example is the exponential series. Schur proved in 1930 that 
 E n ​ ( x ) = j = 0 ∑ n ​ j ! x j ​ , n ≥ 1 , 
 
 has Galois group A n ​ when 4 ∣ n and S n ​ otherwise [ 40 ] . Symmetric groups therefore occur in three quarters of the degrees and alternating groups in one quarter. Coleman’s proof [ 11 ] uses Newton polygons to obtain permutations in the Galois group from the prime divisibility of the factorial denominators. Related arguments apply to generalized Laguerre polynomials [ 21 ] . 
 
 On the other hand, the geometric series gives a different example. Its Taylor polynomial is ( x n + 1 − 1 ) / ( x − 1 ) , so its splitting field is cyclotomic and its group is abelian. For n ≥ 4 the group is therefore neither S n ​ nor A n ​ . In these two examples, all three proportions converge, and the proportion of groups of neither kind is zero or one. 
 
 Katz and Rivin [ 26 Questions 1.1–1.2] ask whether this happens for every power series with nonzero rational coefficients. More precisely, they ask whether the three proportions always converge and, when they all converge, whether the third limit must be zero or one. 
 
 Our first result answers both questions negatively. We prove that for any set of degrees B ⊆ { 3 , 4 , … } , we construct an entire function with positive rational coefficients such that, for n ≥ 3 , its degree- n Taylor polynomial has group S n ​ precisely when n ∈ B . In the remaining degrees n ≥ 3 , it has one rational root and an irreducible factor of degree n − 1 with group S n − 1 ​ . Every Taylor polynomial has distinct negative real roots. Thus even these restrictions on convergence, signs, and zeros leave the set of symmetric degrees arbitrary. 
 
 We then give conditions on the prime divisors of the coefficients that force the symmetric group to occur in density one. For nonintegral binomial powers, an exact discriminant formula determines the exceptional degrees and gives their asymptotic number. For the central-binomial and Catalan generating functions, we determine the groups in every degree over Q .
