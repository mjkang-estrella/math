                                           once n is sufficiently large. Xia [Xia26] showed that Chen’s conjecture is true when n ≥ 1010 .
                                           For further background, see [Rom15].
                                              Here we prove Chen’s conjecture in full generality and show that the inequalities are strict.
                                           The idea of the proof follows closely to the method Häggström [Hä07] used to establish sto-

                                           random variables given successive values of their sum.

2.3. Transition matrices and Markov chains. A transition matrix, also called a Markov
kernel, on S is a function H : S × S → [0, 1] with
                                 X
                                    H(x, y) = 1,   x ∈ S.
                                     y∈S

A Markov chain with transition matrix H is a sequence X0 , X1 , . . . whose next state is chosen
according to the row H(Xt , ·). More precisely, for every history with positive probability,
                       Prob(Xt+1 = y | X0 = x0 , . . . , Xt = x) = H(x, y).
Thus the distribution of the next state depends only on the current state, and the same
transition rule is used at every time. If the current distribution is p, the distribution after
one step is the row vector pH, with

                                                      x∈S

2.4. Stationarity, reversibility, and irreducibility. A distribution p is stationary for H
if pH = p, or equivalently
                                   X
                           p(y) =     p(x)H(x, y),    y ∈ S.
                                            x∈S
Starting the chain with distribution p then gives the same distribution at every time.
   The matrix H is reversible with respect to a distribution p if it satisfies the detailed-balance
equations
                            p(x)H(x, y) = p(y)H(y, x),      x, y ∈ S.
These equations equate the probability of each transition with that of its reverse when the
initial state has distribution p. They imply stationarity, since
                           X                      X
                               p(x)H(x, y) = p(y)     H(y, x) = p(y).
                          x∈S                               x∈S
   The chain is irreducible if any state can be reached from any other by a path of transitions
with positive probability. Equivalently, for every x, y ∈ S, some integer t ≥ 0 satisfies
H t (x, y) > 0. Every transition matrix on a finite nonempty set has a stationary distribution.
If it is irreducible, that distribution is unique and assigns positive probability to every state.
2.5. Coupled transitions. Let H1 and H2 be transition matrices on S and T . A transition
matrix G on S × T couples their transitions if, for every current pair (x, y) and all x′ ∈ S,
y′ ∈ T ,                   X
                               G((x, y), (x′ , y ′ )) = H1 (x, x′ ),
                                y ′ ∈T
                                X
                                         G((x, y), (x′ , y ′ )) = H2 (y, y ′ ).
                                x′ ∈S
The first identity specifies the distribution of the first next state by summing over all possi-
bilities for the second next state; the second identity has the analogous meaning. The two
chains therefore follow the transition rules H1 and H2 , respectively, while their moves may
be dependent.
   The transitions preserve a nonempty relation F ⊆ S × T if G((x, y), (x′ , y ′ )) = 0 whenever
(x, y) ∈ F and (x′ , y ′ ) ∈
                           / F . The restriction of G to F is then the transition matrix of a
finite Markov chain and has a stationary distribution. Equivalently, the transition matrix

can be defined directly on F , with the marginal sums restricted to next pairs belonging to
F . Its stationary distribution has marginals that are stationary for H1 and H2 , by the two
identities above. When H1 and H2 are irreducible, uniqueness identifies these marginals as
their respective stationary distributions.
   This method of constructing a coupling through a stationary distribution has a classical
precedent in Holley’s proof of [Hol74, Theorem 6]. Holley constructed a coupled continuous-
time Markov chain whose transitions preserve the order on a finite distributive lattice. Its
limiting stationary distribution gives a coupling of the prescribed distributions supported on
ordered pairs. Leskelä [Les10] developed a general theory of couplings of Markov processes
that preserve a specified relation and gives conditions for their stationary distributions to
admit a coupling supported on that relation.
