                                               1. Introduction. Strong stability preserving (SSP) Runge–Kutta (RK) methods
                                           preserve stability properties satisfied by the forward Euler method with respect to a
                                           given norm, seminorm, or convex functional, under a suitable time-step restriction.
                                           For overviews of SSP methods, see [11, 8, 9, 13]. The SSP coefficient measures the
                                           allowable time-step size relative to that of forward Eule. A central problem in the
                                           development of SSP methods is to construct schemes with the largest possible SSP
                                           coefficient.
                                               The Shu–Osher representation [21] expresses the explicit RK scheme as convex
                                           combinations of the previous solution and forward Euler steps. This approach was
                                           later extended to general RK methods in [6], leading to the generalized Shu–Osher
                                           representation. The SSP property is closely related to the absolute-monotonicity
                                           theory developed by Kraaijevanger [17]. For an irreducible Runge–Kutta method,
                                           the optimal SSP coefficient equals the radius of absolute monotonicity of its Butcher
                                           tableau [5, 8]. This connection makes the absolute-monotonicity conditions a useful
                                           tool for studying SSP property.
                                               The optimality of several classical explicit SSP Runge–Kutta methods has been
                                           established in [10, 23]. For implicit methods, Ferracina and Spijker [7] studied singly
                                           diagonally implicit Runge–Kutta (SDIRK) methods, while Ketcheson et al. [15] inves-
                                           tigated the larger class of general implicit Runge–Kutta methods. Let Cs,p denote the
                                           optimal SSP coefficient over s-stage p-order Runge–Kutta methods. These studies led
                                           to the following conjectures for the optimal SSP coefficients at orders two and three:
                                                                                                         p
                                           (1.1)           Cs,2 = 2s    (s ≥ 1),       Cs,3 = s − 1 +        s2 − 1   (s ≥ 2).

                                           In [16], the authors determined the optimal coefficient for second-order diagonally
                                           implicit Runge–Kutta (DIRK) methods. Prior to the present work, optimality over
                                           general implicit tableaux remained open at both orders.
                                               Probability also provides tools for studying SSP optimization. Ait-Haddou [1]
                                           related the optimization of explicit linear SSP methods to moment-matching for the
                                           Poisson distribution.

    Inspired by this probabilistic approach and the algebraic transformation in [16],
we explore a connection between SSP methods and phase-type (PH) distributions,
which describe the absorption time of finite-state continuous-time Markov chains
(CTMCs) [3]. Under the absolute-monotonicity conditions, every SSP Runge–Kutta
tableau defines a PH representation. This representation also gives a probabilistic
interpretation of the RK coefficients and translates the chain order conditions into
moment equalities on the absorption time.
    The classical minimum-variance theorem [2] gives a sharp lower bound on the
second-order moment ratio of a PH distribution. We extend the third-order moment
ratio bound for acyclic PH distributions [19] to general PH representations. We also
establish shch moment bounds of arbitrary order for acyclic PH distributions.
    Combining these moment inequalities with the RK–PH correspondence, we prove
the conjectures (1.1) for general implicit tableaux and determine the uniqueness of
the optimal tableaux up to simultaneous stage permutations at both orders.
    The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces SSP
Runge–Kutta methods and PH distributions. Section 3 develops the RK–PH corre-
spondence. Section 4 establishes moment ratio bounds for PH distributions. Section 5
applies these bounds to prove the optimal SSP coefficients and characterize the
attaining tableaux. Section 6 concludes the paper.

    2.3. Markov chains and phase-type distributions. A phase-type distribution
describes the time to absorption in a finite-state continuous-time Markov chain.
      Definition 2.1. Let (Xt )t≥0 be a stochastic process with right-continuous sample
paths on the finite state space S = {1, . . . , N }. Write Ft = σ(Xu : 0 ≤ u ≤ t) for its
history up to time t. The process is called a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC)
if it satisfies the Markov property

(2.10)       P(Xt+h = j | Ft ) = P(Xt+h = j | Xt ),         t, h ≥ 0,   j ∈ S.

The chain is called time-homogeneous if its transition probabilities depend only on the
elapsed time h. For any t, h ≥ 0 and i, j ∈ S, define

(2.11)           pij (h) = P(Xt+h = j | Xt = i) = P(Xh = j | X0 = i).

    3. The RK–PH correspondence.
     3.1. Algebraic representation. We construct a PH representation from a
Runge–Kutta tableau satisfying (K)r . The stage conditions yield a transient subinten-
sity matrix, while the output conditions provide nonnegative weights for the initial
distribution.
    Proposition 3.1. Let (A, b) be an s-stage Runge–Kutta tableau of order at least
one. Suppose (K)r holds for some r > 0. Define

                             U = I + rA,         T = −U−1 ,
(3.1)

Then T is a transient subintensity matrix. w ≥ 0 and 0 < σ ≤ 1 and π = w/σ is a
probability row. We call (π, T) the PH representation associated with (A, b, r).
    Proof. The first stage inequality in (2.7) gives

                             I + T = rA(I + rA)−1 ≥ 0,

so the off-diagonal entries of T are nonnegative. The second stage inequality gives

                            Te = rA(I + rA)−1 e − e ≤ 0,

so its row sums are nonpositive. Moreover, T = −(I + rA)−1 is nonsingular. According
to Proposition 2.3, T is a valid transient subintensity matrix for a PH representation.
     The output conditions in (2.7) give w ≥ 0 and 0 ≤ σ ≤ 1. Consistency implies
wUe = rbT e = r > 0, so σ > 0. Thus π = w/σ is a probability row on the transient
states.
    The transformation in (3.1) preserves the algebraic structure of the RK tableau
and recasts the constraints in (K)r as admissibility conditions for the associated PH
representation. Thus we can study SSP properties without directly manipulating

third-order accuracy for linear problems.
    6. Conclusion. In this paper, we established a correspondence between SSP
Runge–Kutta tableaux and finite PH distributions. Under the absolute-monotonicity
conditions, the relation U = I + rA links the coefficient matrix A with the Green
matrix U, the output weights b correspond to the initial distribution π. Also, the chain
order conditions can be transformed to the moments of absorption-time. We proved a
sharp third-moment ratio bound via state elimination and a nonnegative algebraic
decomposition and we established the general moment ratio bound for all acyclic PH
distributions. Combining these moment bounds with the RK–PH correspondence,
we proved the√conjectured optimal radii of absolute monotonicity Rs,2 = 2s and
Rs,3 = s − 1 + s2 − 1 for general implicit tableaux, and classified the unique attaining
tableaux up to simultaneous stage permutations.
    A possible direction for future work is to extend the present probabilistic framework
to SSP two-step Runge–Kutta methods [14]. Such an extension may involve CTMC
models with two absorbing states, corresponding to the history values un and un−1 .
