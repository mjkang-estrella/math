                                                     Abstract. Let C(m, n) denote the probability that, among n independent uniformly
                                                     random permutations of m labels, there exists a label that precedes every other label
                                                     in a strict majority of the permutations. We prove that C(m, n) > C(m, n + 2) for
                                                     m ≥ 3 and odd n ≥ 1, and that C(m, n) > C(m + 1, n) for m ≥ 2 and odd n ≥ 3. These
                                                     results establish Kelly’s two monotonicity conjectures for odd electorates. The
                                                     proofs use integral representations involving probabilities associated with sums of
                                                     independent Bernoulli random variables.




                                                                        1. Introduction and main results
                                               Let n voters independently choose uniformly random permutations of the labels
                                           {1, . . . , m}. This is the impartial culture model, in which each of the m! rankings
                                           is equally likely. A label a ∈ {1, . . . , m} is called a majority winner (or Condorcet
                                           winner) if, for every b , a, more than half of the permutations place a before b.
                                           There is at most one majority winner. Let C(m, n) denote the probability that one
                                           exists.
                                               A majority winner need not exist. For example, consider three labels a, b, c and
                                           three voters with rankings abc, bca, and cab, written from most to least preferred.
                                           These give a two-to-one majority for a over b, b over c, and c over a. Thus, there is
                                           no majority winner. This is the classical Condorcet paradox.
                                               Black [1] suggested that, for a fixed number of voters, majority winners be-
                                           come less frequent as the number of alternatives increases. Kelly [12, 13] studied
                                           how the probability of a Condorcet winner depends on the numbers of voters
                                           and alternatives and formulated two monotonicity conjectures. His definition
                                           allows ties in pairwise comparisons, but coincides with ours when n is odd. Fish-
                                           burn, Gehrlein, and Maskin [7] subsequently investigated these conjectures and
                                           established several special cases. For further results on Condorcet winner proba-
                                           bilities, including exact calculations, partial results, and asymptotic estimates, see
                                           [5, 16, 6, 9, 14, 8, 18, 2, 17] and the references therein.
                                               In this paper, we prove both conjectures for odd numbers of voters. Specifically,
                                           we show that the probability of a Condorcet winner strictly decreases upon adding
                                           either two voters or one alternative.

   Note that C(2, n) = 1 for every odd n, since a majority contest between two labels
cannot end in a tie. Also, C(m, 1) = 1, since a single voter always has a top-ranked
label. These elementary cases explain the lower bounds in the following theorems.
Theorem 1.1. For every m ≥ 3 and every odd n ≥ 1,
                                  C(m, n) > C(m, n + 2).
Theorem 1.2. For every m ≥ 2 and every odd n ≥ 3,
                                  C(m, n) > C(m + 1, n).
   A common idea in both proofs is to work with an even number of Bernoulli
variables. By adding or removing one variable, we express the corresponding
majority probabilities in terms of quantities associated with this even collection.
The key is the tie event, which occurs at the majority threshold only for an even
number of variables. These identities reduce the desired comparisons to integral
positivity problems, which are then resolved by different arguments. This also
explains why our approach is restricted to odd n.
   We collect the preliminary results in Section 2 and prove Theorems 1.2 and 1.1
in Sections 3 and 4, respectively.
