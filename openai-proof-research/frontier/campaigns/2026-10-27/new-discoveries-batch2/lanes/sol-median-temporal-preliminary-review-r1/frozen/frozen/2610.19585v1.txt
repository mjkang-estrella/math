                                                                                           Abstract
                                                   In this work, we propose a curve to be a minimum of length among rectifiable,
                                                   continuous curves of unit width in three-dimensional Euclidean space. We show
                                                   that a previously conjectured minimizer of that problem is not of unit width, give
                                                   the numerical derivation of a candidate family of unit width curves, prove that it
                                                   admits a strict local minimum within that candidate family and that this minimum
                                                   is of unit width and length 3.905124162243593 . . .. The curve consists of eight
                                                   straight segments, two cone geodesics, and three circular helices. Its parameters are
                                                   specified by a uniquely isolated solution of the joining and stationarity equations.
                                                   To establish unit width in every direction, we combine analytic estimates with
                                                   interval arithmetic on a finite covering of the remaining directions. We conjecture
                                                   that the curve is globally minimizing. We supplement this work with the programs
                                                   used to identify the candidates, replicate the computer-assisted parts of our proof,
                                                   as well as a formalization of our proof in Lean.

                                                   Keywords: Space curves, minimum width, geometric optimization, convex geometry,
                                                   computer-assisted proof, formal verification

1 Introduction
We will first introduce the minimization problem, its previously proposed solution and
its gaps, then introduce our own contribution.
    The minimum width of a curve is the thickness of its narrowest containing slab,
that is the minimum of distance between two parallel planes that can contain the
curve between them over all normal directions. We consider rectifiable curves in
three-dimensional Euclidean space. Formally a curve γ : [0, 1] → R3 , width is defined as

Here L is geometric length. The quotient is invariant under scaling, so determining
mopen is equivalent to minimizing length subject to w = 1. The width of a curve or a
more general set is the width of its convex hull.
    The two-dimensional analogue arose in the study of escape paths from an infinite
strip, which is an instance of a more general minimization problem posed by Richard
Bellman in [1], commonly dubbed the "Lost in a forest problem" [2]. Zalgaller [3]
found the minimizing caliper-shaped arc, consisting of line segments and circular arcs,
with length approximately 2.2783 at unit width. Adhikari and Pitman [4] rediscovered
this solution and gave another proof of its optimality. In three-dimensional space,
Zalgaller [5] proposed a curve L3 obtained from cylindrical geodesics and polygonal
pieces, and conjectured that it minimizes length at unit width. Its reported length is
approximately 3.9215. Ghomi [6, Theorem 1.1] proved a universal lower bound
                                         q

Here ℓcaliper is the length of the planar caliper curve at unit width. His argument
combines Wienholtz’s alternating-support theorem with the planar width bound and
an inequality for the lengths of orthogonal projections. These results leave a gap
between the lower bound and the proposed spatial construction.
    Related computational approaches to escape-path problems include Deng’s traveling-
salesman formulations with neighborhoods [7], subsequent convergence results and
Lean formalizations [8], and a computational treatment of the planar strip problem [9].
The spatial examples in those works concern sphere containment or inspection rather
than the open minimum-width problem considered here. For polygonal curves which
have polyhedral convex hulls, width computation, i.e. minimization over all supporting
directions reduces to finitely many antipodal face-vertex and edge-edge configurations,
as in Houle and Toussaint [10]. We use this reduction in the numerical search described
in Section 2.

Our contribution.
We construct a curve of width exactly one and length approximately 3.905124162243593.
It partially follows Zalgaller’s construction with regard to the arrangement of the
endpoints and having a central arc following a cylinder. It differs by having outer
arms formed from tangent-cone geodesics, a pair of radius-1/2 helices, and straight

local minimality of its length within that family in Section 4, and give a partially
analytical, partially computer-assisted proof of its unit width in Section 5.

2 Numerical Search for Candidate Curve
In this section we outline the search for the curve for which we give the exact construc-
tion, length, width and local optimality proof in the later sections. Because we give
the full programs in supplementary material to allow replication by rerunning and the
proofs are independent of how the candidate curve family was identified, we will keep
this part brief.
    Our strategy to obtain ideas for the structure of the minimal length curve of
unit width was as follows. We first ran a numerical search for the minimizer among


                                                 4
polygonal chains, then heuristically derived a hypothesis for an analytical construction
from it, whose parameters were then again numerically optimized.
     We remember that every rectifiable curve can be approximated arbitrarily well
through finite polygonal chains, i.e. chains of connected straight line segments. We
minimize the length to width ratio of polygonal chains over the vertex coordinates as
optimization variables from several random starts, in case multiple local minima exist,
and with increasingly refining vertex count.

The quadratic penalty with coefficient η = 0.01 discourages large steps, and the
τ bounds each coordinate displacement. Clarabel [12] solves an equivalent conic
formulation with a quadratic objective. The Python implementation [13] uses NumPy
and SciPy [14, 15] for arrays, sparse matrices and linear algebra.
     The linearized constraints may not preserve true width, so we recompute the width
of each accepted curve exactly. Coordinates are recentered and rounded to 2−32 Z3 . As
we noted in the introduction, the width of a curve is the width of its convex hull. We use
Qhull [16] to obtain the triangulation of a convex hull and a fast C++ implementation
to compute the convex hull’s width using the approach of [10]. By computing squared
widths, we can guarantee exact results using integer arithmetic. Because the required
precision requires big integer types whose operations are slow, we speed up the check
with generated, semi-static polynomial-sign filters from the implementation given
in [17]. The filters certify polynomial signs used to identify antipodal features and
compare squared widths using fast floating-point calculations. When the semi-static
filter cannot make a guarantee, we fall back to exact big integer calculations. The
implementation uses Boost.Geometry’s vector operations and Boost.Multiprecision’s
integer arithmetic [18, 19].
     After optimization stops at a given vertex count, we refine the polygonal chain by
adding additional vertices along existing segments. We add a slight displacement from

the existing edges, so that newly added vertices’ objective derivatives and constraints
are not inactive by construction.
    Independent instances can start from random Gaussian vertices or supplied polygons.
For half-turn or mirror symmetry, the implementation imposes pN −1−i = Rpi , with
R = diag(−1, −1, 1) or R = diag(−1, 1, 1), respectively. These runs use odd vertex
counts, add the corresponding linear equalities to the displacement problem, and bisect
edges in pairs. Coordinate rounding preserves the symmetry exactly.
    The supplied population consists of 32 Gaussian starts: 12 unconstrained, eight
retaining half-turn symmetry, four retaining mirror symmetry, and four releasing each
symmetry. Seeds are 0 through 31. The initial vertex counts cycle through 9, 13, 17, 25
within each group. Vertex counts increase by N 7→ 2N − 1, capped at 3,999. The best
run retained half-turn symmetry and used seed 16. Its final polygon has the ratio

    Subsequences of these chains are then inspected heuristically, to identify patterns
that suggest approximation of elementary geometric shapes, e.g. near-collinearity of
vertex sequences suggest straight line segments, approximately constant non-zero
curvature and constant torsion suggest circular helices, and straightness after unfolding
a fitted cone suggests cone geodesics. Polygonal chains with additional symmetry
constraints producing length over width ratios that remain competitive to unconstrained
chains after refinement. This suggests that the minimizer has that symmetry.
    After identifying an analytical candidate family, discussed in the next section, we
implement a second length optimization over its parameters. For that, we replaced
vertices by exact formulas for these pieces. The unit separation of the parallel planes
through AC and BD determines t from a, b, and tangent joining leaves a local three-
parameter family. Using the eight-coordinate formulation of Section 4, we minimized its
analytic length with SciPy’s equality-constrained sequential least-squares programming
(SLSQP) solver, then polished the joining and stationarity equations at high precision
with mpmath [20]. The resulting endpoint parameters are

                  (a, b, c) ≃ (0.712497889, 0.127319787, 0.823130967).

polygonal chain, we expect its length to be slightly lower than the best polygonal
chain candidate due to the approximation error of polygonal chains. This is what
we found in our testing. The selected curve’s length is 3.905124162243593 . . ., about

5 Width in every direction
The proofs in this section frequently defer to computer-assisted bounds and dense
computations, that can be verified individually through substitutions of the previously
defined quantities and application of elementary algebraic identities but are not of
particular interest to expand individually. They can be verified collectively through
the supplementary Lean 4 proof. Instead we want to preface them with an intuitive
overview of the proof idea and structure.
    To prove unit width, we need to identify at least one direction m for which the
directional width, which is always an upper bound of the overall width, is exactly 1,
and show that the directional width is no lower than 1 for every remaining direction.
For every directional width there must be at least two support points, referred to as
witnesses in the supplementary programs, one point per plane of the corresponding
slab. For the curve to be optimal, we expect every point on a non-straight piece to be
a support point for a direction whose directional width is 1, otherwise the curve could
be shortened by replacing part of the non-straight piece with a line segment, which
would be shorter without losing any necessary support. Note, how Ghomi observes
that this is not the case for his proposed closed curve in [6, Note 5.2] and infers it
is not optimal and can be shortened near the corners without losing width. Similar
holds for end and corner points. We will therefore have to give analytical arguments
for subsets of all directions corresponding to the non-straight pieces and end points
for which directional width must be critical, in Sections 5.1 and 5.2.
    If we can establish directional width of at least 1 for all critical directions and some
open neighborhood of these direction subsets on the unit sphere, see the colored regions
on Figure 6, we need to establish the bound for the remaining compact complement on
the unit sphere on which directional width is not critical. For any non-critical direction
n directional width of that direction’s support points must have a lower bound greater
than 1 on some open neighborhood of n on the sphere because directional width is
continuous. This implies that an open cover of that compact remaining subset of
directions exists, which must have a finite subcover, where each open direction set
corresponds to two fixed curve points whose projected separation is strictly greater
than 1 on it. This can then be verified using computer assistance with sufficient
numerical precision and interval arithmetic. An analogous argument, using a spherical
triangulation rather than a cover of open cones, is made in Section 5.3. In that version
of the argument a triangle vertex can also have critical width if that is analytically
established.
    Whenever we credit a bound to interval arithmetic, we mean outward-rounded
interval evaluation using the parameter enclosures and procedures specified in the
corresponding supplementary checker. The supplementary material [21] contains pro-
grams for the replication of all cited bounds. Its INTERVAL_INDEX.txt locates the
verification of each interval bound in the manuscript.



                                            20
    For a compact set S, write hS (n) = maxP ∈S ⟨n, P ⟩. Let K = conv Γ and W (n) =
hK (n) + hK (−n) = hK−K (n). Thus W (n)/ ∥n∥ is the distance between the supporting
parallel planes normal to n ̸= 0 or the directional width along direction n. We
prove W (n) ≥ ∥n∥ in every direction, with equality in one. Every pair P, Q ∈ K
gives the lower bound W (n) ≥ ⟨n, P − Q⟩. The half-turn symmetry RΓ = Γ gives
W (Rn) = W (n). Also W (−n) = W (n) and W (sn) = sW (n) for s ≥ 0, so a bound for

interest for further work towards proving the minimal length of a unit width curve.

methods cover all directions. The optional supplement/sample_width.py provides a
direction-sampling plausibility check.
    Theorem 1 establishes local minimality within the joining family. We conjecture
global minimality among all rectifiable curves. Our belief is supported by 1) the
analytical construction being shorter than any numerically optimized polygonal chain
among a large number of runs, 2) the 26 competitive runs described in Section 2
converging to roughly the same shape and 3) the curve being proven locally minimal
within a family describing that shape.

Conjecture 4 The curve Γ is a shortest rectifiable curve in R3 of unit width, i.e. every
rectifiable curve γ : [0, 1] → R3 with w(γ) = 1 satisfies
                                      L(γ) ≥ L(Γ).


     The procedure in Section 2 was also applied to closed curves, a problem studied by
Zalgaller [5] and Ghomi [6]. Our best certified polygon has 44,032 vertices and, after
normalization to unit width, length at most 5.094393486530. A subsequent numerical
fit improves the certified bound to 5.094393485661. This is below the best ratio, roughly
5.1086, that was found from replacing parts of the arcs on Ghomi’s closed curve with
straight line segments, a route for improvement that he already noted in his original
paper. This suggests, that the optimal closed curve has a qualitatively different shape
than Ghomi’s curve with some arc pieces replaced by straight lines. At the time of
writing, we have no conclusive analytic hypothesis for the minimizing closed curve.
