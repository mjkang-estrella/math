                                                            PACKING AND COVERING RANDOM TREES

                                           CAELAN ATAMANCHUK, SIMON BRIEND, SERTE DONDERWINKEL, AND ELAINE HERRERA


                                                   Abstract. We study the sizes of optimal packings and coverings of conditioned branching
                                                   processes by balls with growing radii. We find a universal limiting constant, and thereby
                                                   establish universality of packing and covering numbers across different scales and different
                                                   offspring distributions.




arXiv:2610.40150v1 [math.PR] 29 Oct 2026
                                                       To be precise, for µ an offspring distribution with mean 1 and finite variance σ 2 we let
                                                   Tn be a Bienaymé tree with offspring distribution µ conditioned to have size n. We prove
                                                   that the packing number (resp. covering number) of Tn with balls of radii 1 ≪ γn ≪ n1/2
                                                   is asymptotically equivalent to Cn/(σ 2 γn2 ) (resp. Cn/(4σ 2 γn2 )), for a universal constant C.
                                                       We also show that the order of limits can be exchanged and that the same limit occurs
                                                   when we first consider the limiting covering number of Tn by balls of fixed radius R, and
                                                   thereafter let R tend to ∞.
                                                       We then extend these results to the Brownian continuum random tree conditioned to
                                                   have mass 1. For this model, we prove that the packing (resp. covering) number with balls
                                                   of radii ε are asymptotically equivalent to C/ε2 (resp. C/(4ε2 )), for the same constant C,
                                                   in contrast with a conjecture by Aldous.
                                                       Finally, our results answer a question by Devroye, Eide and Pralat on the asymptotic
                                                   burning number of mean 1 finite variance Bienaymé trees of size n, by showing that it lies
                                                   between (c + o(1))n1/3 and (2c + o(1))n1/3 with high probability, for a constant c that
                                                   only depends on the offspring variance.

                                          1. Introduction

    In any metric space (X , d) we define coverings and packings as follows.
Definition 1.1 (Covering). For a metric space (X , d) and radius r > 0, the subset C of X
is said to be a r-covering (or r-net) if for any x ∈ X , d(x, C) < r.
Definition 1.2 (Packing). For a metric space (X , d) and radius r > 0, the subset P of X
is said to be a r-packing (or r-separated set) if for any distinct u, v ∈ P, d(u, v) ≥ r.
   We call the individual elements of a packing and covering their centres. If X is compact,
the minimum size of a covering and maximum size of a packing are well defined, and
denoted by Cr (X ) and Pr (X ). These are the covering number and packing number. These
two quantities have been extensively studied for all sorts of metric spaces, and can be
viewed as an ‘internal fractal dimension’. In this paper we focus on random trees, whether
combinatorial trees, R-trees or their scaling limits. 1

1.1. Our contribution. Our main contribution is a law of large numbers for packing
and covering numbers throughout the mesoscopic regime. We prove that, uniformly
over arbitrary sequences 1 ≪ γn ≪ n1/2 , these quantities have deterministic first-order
asymptotics of order n/γn2 . The leading constants depend on the offspring distribution only
through its variance. We also show that the same constant is obtained by first taking the
large-tree limit at fixed radius and then sending the radius to infinity. Finally, we transfer
the result to the Brownian continuum random tree (CRT) and obtain exact small-radius
asymptotics for its packing and covering numbers, where again the same universal constant
appears.

   Application to graph burning A direct Corollary of Theorem 1.3 improves on the
results of Devroye et al. [14] on asymptotic burning number of size-conditioned Bienaymé
trees. Graph burning is a discrete-time process on a graph, where vertices are either
burning or unburned. In each round, a burning vertex causes all of its neighbours to burn
and a new fire source is chosen: a previously unburned vertex is ignited. The process runs
until all vertices are burning. The quantity of interest is the minimum number of rounds
required to burn all vertices of the graph, denoted by B(G). Using the vocabulary of our
paper, the burning number is the smallest k such that with k balls of respective radii
1, 2, . . . , k it is possible to cover the graph. This problem has received increasing attention,
in particular
         √         with regard to the Burning Number Conjecture, which states that it takes at
most ⌈ n⌉ steps to burn a connected graph on n vertices (see for example Bastide et al.
[11], Land and Lu [25], Norin and Turcotte [33]). More recently, it was proven by Devroye
et al. [14] that the burning number of size-conditioned Bienaymé tree with finite variance
critical offspring distribution is of order n1/3 . Our result improves on [14] and answers one
of their questions, proving that the burning number lies between cn1/3 and 2cn1/3 with
probability tending to 1 for an explicit constant c.

k, then its burning number is at least k. On the other hand, if it can be covered by k balls

the following corollary.

   Before looking close at T ∗ , let us look at finite trees. Informally, the canonical maximal
s-packing of a finite tree is the one where all packing centres are placed as far away from
the root as possible: one can imagine that all centres are pushed up in the tree towards
the leaves, while respecting that they are all at least distance s apart (in this paper we will
view finite trees as growing up from the root). To understand why the packing number is
local, it is key to observe that the canonical packing behaves well under merging trees at
their roots: given two rooted trees and their canonical s-packings, if both trees are merged
at their roots, then either the union of their packings is a canonical s-packing of the merged
tree or we first need to remove the packing centre closest to the root. Furthermore, if
we attach an edge below the root, we can construct a new canonical s-packing from the
old one without moving the packing centres in the original tree. Thus, if we step-by-step
construct the neighbourhood of an edge by merging trees on its ancestral line, then what

These observations form the core of the proof of Proposition 1.15, and they are developed

generated, and for the reader interested in getting a clearer picture, Fig. 2 offers a preview

    Returning to T ∗ , we can think of the canonical s-packing of T ∗ as being derived by
placing packing centres as far away as possible from some “point at infinity” which lies at
the end of P ∗ (in a sequence of finite trees converging locally to T ∗ , the root “dissapears”
from view when we focus on a uniform vertex’s neighbourhood). We can also take the
same iterative perspective on its construction, except now instead of recursing along the
ancestral line of a vertex, we do so along the path P ∗ . Because edge lengths are Exp(1)
random variables but we consider packings of large radii s, it is unlikely that the root edge
has more than 1 packing centre on it. Moreover, if the tree grafted at o∗ is non-empty,
then, since we push the packing centres upwards, the root edge will not have a packing
centre unless the grafted tree is very large. This is unlikely for large s. Hence, the only

Remark 17. Our use of local convergence and convergence under rescaling to the CRT, as
well as the universality of our limiting constant, may raise the question whether convergence
of the packing and covering numbers is simply a consequence of convergence of Tn in the
local Gromov–Hausdorff topology, when distances are rescaled by γn and the tree is rooted at
a uniformly random vertex. However, viewing the tree from the perspective of a uniformly
random vertex is not enough to control a global quantity like the γn -covering number. For
example, one may artificially append a star with εn n/γn arms of length γn + 1 somewhere in
the tree, for εn → 0 arbitrarily slow. This star contains a negligible proportion of the vertices
and does not affect the limit under rescaling in the local Gromov–Hausdorff topology. (Its
arms are also too short to affect the tree’s global limit in the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov
topology.) However, the star needs Θ(εn n/γn ) additional covering centres, which, for
εn ≫ 1/γn , is much larger than the number of covering centres needed for the rest of the
tree. This illustrates a subtlety of our problem: we need global control over the behaviour of
local γn -neighbourhoods, and our combination of techniques and perspectives is needed to
obtain this.

2.4. Discrete trees with edge lengths and the marked local topology. Throughout
this paper we often work with rooted combinatorial trees (t, ρ) that come equipped with

in this case. For a vertex v ∈ t, the generation of v is the number of vertices on the path
between ρ minus 1, and the height of v is the sum of the edge lengths on the path between

the root are defined analogously. Moreover, for any edge e = {u, v} in t, we will use e to
denote both the edge in the underlying combinatorial tree and the line segment connecting

ball Br (o, t) in the fringe topology. The fringe Fr (o, t) maintains the marks on the vertices

                                3. Packing numbers are local

   In this section we develop some tools to discuss packings of discrete trees with edge
lengths. (Here, we will often call trees with edge lengths simply ‘trees’.) We will allow
ourselves to place packing centres on vertices or along edges. The key idea in this section
is a recursive algorithm to generate a canonical maximum packing of a tree, and we use it
to derive a law of large numbers for packing numbers with fixed radii of sequences of trees
with a sufficiently nice local limit. The recursive definition and the notion of the type of a
packing that we define below are inspired by Meir and Moon, who used similar notions for
trees without edge lengths to obtain their asymptotic results in [28, 29] (for r = 1) and
[31, 32] (for general r). Our use of local convergence is new.

5.2. The likelihood of a tall subtree. We will analyse the proportion of vertices with
tall subtrees of a Bienaymé forest conditioned to have size #Fnλ and kout (Snλ ) roots.

w(f,v) = (w1 , . . . , wn−n1 +1 ) to place out-degree-one vertices. Place w1 + wn−n1 +1 vertices
on the ancestral edge of v1 and instead place the mark on the w1 -th descendant of v1 . For
all other vertices vi , place wi vertices on the ancestral edge of wi . If any of the vertices vi
is a root, then simply attach the wi new vertices as ancestors of vi .
