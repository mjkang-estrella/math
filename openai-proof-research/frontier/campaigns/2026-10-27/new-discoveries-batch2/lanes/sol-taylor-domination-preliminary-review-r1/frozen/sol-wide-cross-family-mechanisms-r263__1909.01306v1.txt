    In this paper we count the number of visible lattice points lying inside
lattice parallelograms of a certain form. Specifically, lattice parallelo-

vertices, there are no other lattice points on the boundary of the par-

                     P = {t1~u + t2~v : 0 ≤ t1 , t2 ≤ 1},

goal was to determine the asymptotic estimates that arose in counting
the number of visible points lying inside P . We elaborate on this goal
in Section 2.1.
    We will use Pa,n to denote the clean parallelogram with vertices
(0, 0), (1, 0), (a, n) and (a + 1, n), where 1 ≤ a < n and gcd(a, n) = 1.
For a lattice parallelogram, P (with one vertex at the origin) let

         V (P ) = # of visible lattice points in the interior of P.

In the special case when P = Pa,n , we simply write V (a, n), instead of
V (Pa,n ).

                        2. A Reduction Result

    We begin by showing that questions about visible lattice points in-
side a clean lattice parallelogram (with one vertex at the origin) can
be reduced to studying the visible lattice points inside the appropriate
Pa,n . We also describe the minimum and maximum value of V (P ).
We prove our result via unimodular transformations. These are linear
transformations T : Rn → Rn with the key property that they pre-
serve lattice points, that is, T (Zn ) = Zn . Furthermore, unimodular
transformations preserve visibility.

Definition 1. A n × n matrix M is said to be unimodular if M ∈
Mn (Z) with det(M ) = ±1. A linear transformation T : Rn → Rn is
unimodular if it can be represented by a unimodular matrix.

2.1. Motivation for studying this problem. Having proved Theo-
rem 2, we are in a position to expand on the motivation underlying this
paper. Unlike the square [−r, r]2 , the parallelograms Pa,n are long and
thin. Thus, it seems plausible to infer that most values of V (a, n)/n
should be quite different from 6/π 2 . What we will see is that this infer-
ence is false much of the time. That is, for most values of a, V (a, n)/n
is close to 6/π 2 . However, when a is close to 1 or close to n the numer-
ics for V (a, n)/n differ significantly from 6/π 2 . The reader may want
to jump ahead to Section 5 titled Some Numerics to see some graphs
of V (a, n)/n.

                  6. Further Speculative Remarks

     The values of fn oscillate, but only slightly. One can arguably view
it as Brownian motion with a negative drift. However, if one decides
to view the graph of fn from a distance, then it can be described as
consisting of 3 parts. Initially it resembles a sharply decreasing function
        √         √
for a / n. The n follows by an application of the well-known eyeball
metric. It then levels off around 6/π 2 and is “constant” for most a-
                              √
values. Then when a ' n − n it again decreases sharply. (Here we
once again invoke the eyeball metric.) There is some ambiguity among
the authors about what the eyeball metric tells us. At least, one of us
                 √
believes that the n term should be replaced by log(n).
     In the middle of the graph we see a few outliers where fn is noticeably
bigger or smaller than 6/π 2 . At present, we have identified two factors
influencing these effects. However, our attempts to make this intuition
rigorous have not matured and will be continued in future work.
     Firstly, Proposition 3 shows that

                       V (a, n) = V (a−1   mod n, n).

As such, a-values near the endpoints of [1, n] whose modular inverses
are in the middle of [1, n] partially explain this outlying phenomena.
     Alternatively, we can try to explain this phenomena by trying to
get more precise estimates on the terms in Theorem 8. The graphs
