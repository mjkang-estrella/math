particular, no analytic convergence is needed. In (1), the product indexed by m has order at
least m at t = 0, so every coefficient receives contributions from only finitely many products.
    Bala formulated the following two conjectures on December 18, 2021 [2].

  1. For every integer k ≥ 1, the sequence a(n) modulo k is eventually periodic with period
     dividing φ(k).

  2. For i ≥ 0, set ai (n) = a(n + i). For every prime p and all positive integers n, r,

    The main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1. The two conjectures above hold for A158690. More precisely:

  1. for every k ≥ 2,
                                   a(n + λ(k)) ≡ a(n)       (mod k)
     for all sufficiently large n, where λ is the Carmichael function;

  2. for every i ≥ 0, every prime p, and all n, r ≥ 1,

                               a(npr + i) ≡ a(npr−1 + i)       (mod pr ).

Since λ(k) | φ(k), the first statement implies Bala’s claimed period bound.

form.

4       Concluding remarks
The proof uses very little of the particular product defining A158690. Its role is only to show
that the exponential generating function can be written as G(et − 1) for some G ∈ Z[[y]], in
the sense of Theorem 2.6. Once that representation is available, the two apparently different
conjectures come from the same Stirling-number basis. 
                                                            n
   For eventual periodicity, the factorial factor in m! m      makes all sufficiently large m
vanish modulo a fixed modulus, leaving a finite combination of power sequences. For the
Gauss congruences, the same power sequences satisfy the required prime-power congruence
term by term. The fact that series of the form G(et − 1) with G ∈ Z[[y]] remain of this form
under differentiation then explains why every shift appears in Bala’s second conjecture.
   Theorem 2.6 therefore applies without change to any other integer sequence whose expo-
nential generating function can be written as G(et − 1) with G ∈ Z[[y]].
