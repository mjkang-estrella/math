Abstract
    

We study the expected adjacency matrix of a uniformly random multigraph with fixed degree sequence 𝐝∈ℤ+n\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n}.
This matrix arises in a variety of analyses of networked data sets, including modularity-maximization and mean-field theories of spreading processes.
Its structure is well-understood for large, sparse, simple graphs: the expected number of edges between nodes ii and jj is roughly di​dj∑ℓdℓ\frac{d_{i}d_{j}}{\sum_{\ell}{d_{\ell}}}.
Many network data sets are neither large, sparse, nor simple, and in these cases the standard approximation no longer applies.
We derive a novel estimator using a dynamical approach: the estimator emerges from the stationarity conditions of a class of Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithms for graph sampling.
We derive error bounds for this estimator, and provide an efficient scheme with which to compute it.
We test the estimator on synthetic and empirical degree sequences, finding that it enjoys relative error against ground truth a full order of magnitude smaller than the standard approximation.
We then compare modularity maximization techniques using both the standard and novel estimator, finding that the qualitative structure of the optimization landscape depends significantly on the estimator choice.
Our results emphasize the importance of using carefully specified random graph models in data scientific applications.

Random graphs, social networks, Markov Chain Monte Carlo, community structure, estimation

1 Introduction




The language of graphs offers a standard formalism for representing systems of interrelated objects or agents.
Simple graphs model agents connected by a single, usually static, relation, such as acquaintanceship, proximity, or similarity.
In many data sets, however, agents are linked by multiple, discrete interactions.
Two agents in a contact network may be in spatial proximity multiple times in the study period.
Two agents in a communication network may exchange many emails over the course of a week.
In an academic collaboration network, the same two authors may be jointly involved in tens or even hundreds of papers.
In such cases, it is natural to draw a distinct edge between agents for each interaction event.
Doing so results in a multigraph, in which any two nodes may be linked by an arbitrary, nonnegative, integer-valued number of edges.




A fundamental tool in network data science is null model comparison, which allows the analyst to evaluate whether a feature observed in a given network is surprising when compared to benchmark expectations.
We therefore often compare observed networks against random graph null models – probability distributions over graphs.
An especially common class of null models is obtained by fixing the degree
sequence 𝐝\mathbf{d} of the observed network, which encodes the number of interactions for each node.
The degree sequence is known to constrain many of a network’s macroscopic properties [36].
The least informative (or entropy-maximizing) distribution so obtained is the uniform distribution on the space of graphs with the specified degree sequence.
The same construction goes through for multigraphs.
When studying interaction networks, the corresponding random graph is the uniform distribution η𝐝\eta_{\mathbf{d}} on the set 𝒢𝐝\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{d}} of multigraphs with degree sequence 𝐝\mathbf{d}.




In many applications, a set of complete samples from η𝐝\eta_{\mathbf{d}} is not required – only some selected moments.
An especially important set of moments is summarized by the expected adjacency matrix.
We therefore consider the following question: if 𝐖\mathbf{W} is the (random) adjacency matrix of multigraph G∼η𝐝G\sim\eta_{\mathbf{d}}, what is the value of the expected adjacency matrix 𝛀≜𝔼⁡[𝐖]\boldsymbol{\Omega}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{W}]?
The entry ωi​j\omega_{ij} of 𝛀\boldsymbol{\Omega} gives the expected number of edges between nodes ii and jj.
These moments have several important applications in network science.
Among these is community-detection via modularity-maximization [33], which in many formulations includes a term for the expected number of edges between nodes under a suitably specified null model.
Despite its simplicity and relevance for applications, this problem has received relatively little mathematical attention.




Before surveying existing approaches to the estimation of 𝛀\boldsymbol{\Omega}, we fix some notation.
Let 𝒢𝐝\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{d}} refer to the set of multigraphs without self-loops with degree sequence 𝐝∈ℤ+n\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n}.
From a modeling perspective, the exclusion of self-loops reflects an assumption that agents do not meaningfully interact with themselves.
An element G∈𝒢𝐝G\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{d}} has a fixed number nn of nodes and m=12​∑idim=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}d_{i} of edges.
We use bold uppercase symbols to denote matrices, bold lowercase symbols to denote vectors, and standard symbols to denote scalars.
We do not notationally distinguish deterministic and random objects, instead relying on their associated definitions.
We use Greek letters to denote expectations of random objects.
An estimator of a quantity, either deterministic or stochastic, is distinguished by a hat.
For example, 𝛀=𝔼⁡[𝐖]\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{W}] is the expectation of 𝐖\mathbf{W}.
An estimator of 𝛀\boldsymbol{\Omega}, either deterministic or stochastic, may be written 𝛀^\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}.




One approach to estimating 𝛀\boldsymbol{\Omega} is Monte Carlo sampling.
We sample ss independent and identically distributed samples 𝐖(1),…,𝐖(s)∼η𝐝\mathbf{W}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{W}^{(s)}\sim\eta_{\mathbf{d}}, and construct the estimator





𝛀^mc​(𝐝)≜1s​∑ℓ=1s𝐖(ℓ).\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\mathrm{mc}}(\mathbf{d})\triangleq\frac{1}{s}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}\;.

(1)


The estimator 𝛀^mc\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\mathrm{mc}} is a random function of 𝐝\mathbf{d}, parameterized by the sample size ss.
The Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN) ensures that 𝛀^mc→𝛀\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\mathrm{mc}}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\Omega} almost surely as the number of samples ss grows large.
Stronger results are possible: since each entry ω^i​jmc\hat{\omega}^{\mathrm{mc}}_{ij} is bounded, the variance of ω^i​jmc\hat{\omega}^{\mathrm{mc}}_{ij} is finite and we can apply the Central Limit Theorem to provide quantitative bounds on the convergence rate.
This attractive picture is marred by a severe computational inconvenience: the size and complex combinatorial structure of 𝒢d\mathcal{G}_{d} makes exact sampling intractable.
Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods [22] are therefore required.
MCMC introduces a new complication: for any finite number of iterations, the samples produced will always be statistically dependent, and may therefore over-represent some regions of 𝒢𝐝\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{d}} and under represent others.
This dependence breaks the guarantees provided by the SLLN or Central Limit Theorem.
Control over the mixing time of the sampler is in principle sufficient to ameliorate this issue; however, there are few known mixing time bounds on MCMC samplers of distributions on 𝒢𝐝\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{d}}.
Available upper bounds on the mixing times [24, 25, 19] are too large for guarantees in many practical computations, and there are heuristic reasons to believe that there are limits on our ability to improve these bounds.




An alternative estimator 𝛀^0\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{0}, extremely common in the network science literature, is defined entrywise by a simple formula:

The function fi​jf_{ij} plays an important role throughout this article.
Unlike 𝛀^mc\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\mathrm{mc}}, 𝛀^0\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{0} is a deterministic function of 𝐝\mathbf{d} that is essentially free to compute.
The functional form of fi​j​(𝐝)f_{ij}(\mathbf{d}) can be derived in multiple ways.
For example, it is the expected edge density between distinct nodes ii and jj in the model of Chung and Lu [15, 16], which preserves 𝐝\mathbf{d} in expectation rather than deterministically.
We will therefore refer to (2) as the “CL estimate” after Chung and Lu, though we emphasize that these authors did not use this expression as an estimator for any of the models we consider here, and indeed restricted their attention to graphs without parallel edges.
The estimator 𝛀^0\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{0} was also derived heuristically by Newman and Girvan when they introduced modularity maximization as a method for community detection in networks [35, 33].
In their derivation, we approximate the number of edges between ii and jj as follows.
Node ii has did_{i} edges.
Each of these edges must connect to one of the n−1n-1 other nodes.
A “random edge” is attached to node jj with probability roughly dj2​m−di\frac{d_{j}}{2m-d_{i}}.
Assuming that di≪2​md_{i}\ll 2m yields ω^i​j0\hat{\omega}^{0}_{ij} as an approximation.
It is important to note that this heuristic argument does not formalize any probability measure over a set of graphs.
Thus, although 𝛀^0\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{0} is sometimes described as the expectation of a “random graph with fixed degree sequence,” this is not exactly true for any common models except that of Chung and Lu, in which degrees are fixed only in expectation.
In particular, 𝛀^0\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{0} possesses no guarantees related to its performance as an estimator for the uniform model η𝐝\eta_{\mathbf{d}}, the most literal mathematical operationalization of the phrase “random graph with fixed degree sequence.”
As we will see this performance can indeed be quite poor on data sets with high edge densities.




In this article, we construct an estimator of 𝛀\boldsymbol{\Omega} for dense multigraphs that is both scalable and accurate.
By treating an MCMC sampler for η𝐝\eta_{\mathbf{d}} as a stochastic dynamical system whose state space is 𝒢𝐝\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{d}}, we derive stationarity conditions describing the desired moments.
As we will show, there exists a vector 𝜷∈ℝ+n\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n} such that χi​j\chi_{ij}, the probability that wi​j≥1w_{ij}\geq 1, is given by

As we show, this estimator is both easier to compute than 𝛀^mc\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\mathrm{mc}} and much more accurate than 𝛀^0\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{0}.
Furthermore, we can view the Chung-Lu estimator 𝛀^0\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{0} as an approximation of 𝛀^1\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{1}, obtained from the latter via a sequence of two linear approximations.




1.1 Outline




In Section 2, we review two important null multigraph models – the configuration model and the uniform model – as well as a unified MCMC algorithm for sampling from each.
The analysis of this algorithm forms the heart of our derivation of the estimate 𝛀^1\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{1} in Section 3.
This estimator depends on the unknown vector 𝜷\boldsymbol{\beta}, which must be learned from 𝐝\mathbf{d}.
We offer a simple scheme for doing so in Section 4, including a qualified uniqueness guarantee on the resulting estimator 𝜷^\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} of 𝜷\boldsymbol{\beta}; a description of its structure; and a numerical scheme for computing it efficiently.
In Section 5 we turn to experiments.
We first study the behavior of our methods on two synthetic data sets, including a bootstrap-style test of the conjecture underlying our error-bounds.
We then check the accuracy of 𝛀^1\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{1} on a subset of a high school contact network.
Whereas 𝛀^0\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{0} is significantly biased on this data set, 𝛀^1\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{1} is nearly unbiased and decreases the mean relative error of the estimate by an order of magnitude.
In our final experiment, we study the behavior of modularity maximization when the standard null expectation 𝛀^0\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{0} is replaced by 𝛀^1\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{1}.
We find that the behavior of a multiway spectral algorithm [45] depends strongly on both the choice of null expectation and the data set under study.
We close in Section 6 with a discussion and suggestions for future work.






2 Random Graphs with Fixed Degree Sequences




Our interest will focus on the uniform model η𝐝\eta_{\mathbf{d}}, but it will be useful draw comparisons to the somewhat more commonly-used configuration model [10].



Definition 1 (Configurations).



For a fixed node set NN and degree sequence 𝐝∈ℤ+n\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n}, let





Σ𝐝=⨄i=1n{i1,…,idi},\displaystyle\Sigma_{\mathbf{d}}=\biguplus_{i=1}^{n}\left\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d_{i}}\right\}\;,



where ⊎\uplus denotes multiset union.
Thus, Σ𝐝\Sigma_{\mathbf{d}} contains did_{i} labeled copies of each node ii.
The copies i1,…,idii_{1},\ldots,i_{d_{i}} are called stubs of node ii.
A configuration C=(N,E)C=(N,E) consists of the node set NN and an edge set EE which partitions Σ𝐝\Sigma_{\mathbf{d}} into unordered pairs.
An edge in EE of the form {ik,iℓ}\{i_{k},i_{\ell}\} is called a self-loop.
The process of forming CC from Σ𝐝\Sigma_{\mathbf{d}} is often called stub-matching.






Let 𝒞𝐝⊂Σ𝐝\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{d}}\subset\Sigma_{\mathbf{d}} be the set of all configurations with degree sequence 𝐝\mathbf{d} that do not include any self-loops.
There is a natural surjection g:𝒞𝐝→𝒢𝐝g:\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{d}}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{d}}.
The image of C∈𝒞𝐝C\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{d}} under gg is obtained by replacing all stubs with their corresponding nodes and consolidating the result as a multiset.
The uniform distribution on 𝒞𝐝\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{d}} induces a distribution on 𝒢𝐝\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{d}} via gg.
Denote by g−1:𝒢𝐝→2𝒞𝐝g^{-1}:\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{d}}\rightarrow 2^{\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{d}}} the function that assigns to each element of 𝒢𝐝\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{d}} its preimage in 𝒞𝐝\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{d}} under gg.



Definition 2 (Configuration Model).



Let λ𝐝\lambda_{\mathbf{d}} be the uniform distribution on 𝒞𝐝\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{d}}.
The configuration model on 𝒢𝐝\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{d}} is the distribution μ𝐝=λ𝐝∘g−1\mu_{\mathbf{d}}=\lambda_{\mathbf{d}}\circ g^{-1}.






The distinction between η𝐝\eta_{\mathbf{d}} and μ𝐝\mu_{\mathbf{d}} – and its implications for data analysis – was recently highlighted by Fosdick et al. [22].
We have diverged from the terminology of the authors: our “uniform model” is their “configuration model on non-loopy, vertex-labeled multigraphs” and our “configuration model” is their “configuration model on non-loopy, stub-labeled multigraphs.”




The distinction between uniform and configuration models lies in how they weight graphs with parallel edges.
Let C1C_{1} and C2C_{2} be two configurations.
Suppose that C1C_{1} contains the matchings (i1,j1),(i2,j2)(i_{1},j_{1}),(i_{2},j_{2}) and C2C_{2} contains the matchings (i1,j2),(i2,j1)(i_{1},j_{2}),(i_{2},j_{1}), and that they otherwise agree on all other stubs.
Let G=g⁡(C1)=g⁡(C2)G=g(C_{1})=g(C_{2}).
Under the uniform model, GG is considered to be a single state, weighted equally with all other states.
Under the configuration model, on the other hand, the probability mass placed on GG is proportional to |g−1​(G)|\left|g^{-1}(G)\right|, reflecting both C1C_{1} and C2C_{2} as distinct states.
In particular, the configuration model μ𝐝\mu_{\mathbf{d}} will tend to place higher probabilistic weight on elements of 𝒢𝐝\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{d}} with large numbers of parallel edges than will the uniform model η𝐝\eta_{\mathbf{d}}.




In the absence of parallel edges, the uniform and configuration models are closely related.
Let AA be the event that GG is simple, without self-loops or parallel edges.
Then, it is direct to show (e.g. [10]) that, for all GG, η𝐝​(G|A)=μ𝐝​(G|A)\eta_{\mathbf{d}}(G|A)=\mu_{\mathbf{d}}(G|A).
The reason is that, when GG is simple, the sizes of the preimages g−1​(G)g^{-1}(G) depend only on the degree sequence 𝐝\mathbf{d}.
Since 𝐝\mathbf{d} is fixed in 𝒢𝐝\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{d}}, these preimages all have the same size.
Thus, when a simple random graph is required, the uniform model η𝐝\eta_{\mathbf{d}} and configuration model μ𝐝\mu_{\mathbf{d}} are in principle interchangeable, in the sense that we can sample from η𝐝(⋅|A)\eta_{\mathbf{d}}(\cdot|A) by repeatedly sampling from μ𝐝\mu_{\mathbf{d}} until a simple graph is produced.
Furthermore, when the degree sequence 𝐝\mathbf{d} grows slowly relative to nn, μ𝐝​(A)\mu_{\mathbf{d}}(A) is bounded away from zero by a function that depends on moments of 𝐝\mathbf{d} when nn grows large [10, 31, 3].
This in turn provides an upper bound on the expected number of samples from μ𝐝\mu_{\mathbf{d}} required to produce a single sample from η𝐝(⋅|A)\eta_{\mathbf{d}}(\cdot|A).
The computational importance of this relationship is that stub-matching for sampling from μ𝐝\mu_{\mathbf{d}} is well-understood and often fast.




For dense graphs, μ𝐝​(A)\mu_{\mathbf{d}}(A) may be extremely small, and the number of samples required to produce a simple graph may be prohibitive.
While it is possible to make post-hoc edits to the graph to remove self-loops and multiple edges [32, 38], such methods can generate substantial and uncontrolled bias in finite graphs.
Second and more importantly for our context, there is no equivalence between the unconditional distributions η𝐝\eta_{\mathbf{d}} and μ𝐝\mu_{\mathbf{d}} on spaces of multigraphs.
Stub-matching cannot therefore be used to sample from η𝐝\eta_{\mathbf{d}} when modeling considerations allow the presence of multiple edges.




2.1 Markov Chain Monte Carlo




An alternative approach to sampling uses Markov chains to explore structured sets of graphs.
There exists a large constellation of related algorithms for this class of task, including the sampling of marginal-constrained binary matrices [42, 4]; degree-regular [43, 30, 28] and degree-heterogeneous [13, 41, 8, 17] simple graphs; and graphs with degree-correlation constraints [1].
Most of these algorithms operate by repeatedly swapping edges in such a way as to preserve the required graph structure.




A fairly general variant, formulated by Fosdick et al. [22], can sample from either the uniform model η𝐝\eta_{\mathbf{d}} or the configuration model μ𝐝\mu_{\mathbf{d}} on 𝒢𝐝\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{d}}.

These results provide a principled solution to the problem of asymptotically exact sampling from η𝐝\eta_{\mathbf{d}}, and can therefore be used to construct an estimator 𝛀^mc\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\mathrm{mc}} of 𝛀\boldsymbol{\Omega}, given by (1), with arbitrary levels of accuracy.

There are two performance-related issues when using Algorithm 1 in practice, both of which are connected to the number of edges mm.
First is the question of how large δ​t\delta t should be to ensure that the samples are sufficiently close to independence.
Heuristically, δ​t\delta t should scale with the mixing time of the chain, but very few bounds on mixing times for chains of this type appear to be available.
In several recent papers, Greenhill [25, 24] and collaborators [19] have derived the only bounds known to this author for edge-swap Markov chains.
In the space of simple graphs, under certain regularity conditions on the degree sequence, they provide a mixing time bound with scaling O⁡(d∗14​m10​log⁡m)O({d_{*}}^{14}m^{10}\log m), where d∗=maxi⁡did_{*}=\max_{i}d_{i}.
The scaling of this upper bound very poor, especially with regard to mm, and is therefore not reassuring for practical applications.
The second issue relates to the acceptance probabilities themselves.
In a dense multigraph the number Wi​jW_{ij} of edges between ii and jj will typically be large, resulting in low acceptance rates.
Indeed, supposing that a typical entry Wi​jW_{ij} scales approximately linearly with mm, a typical acceptance probability would scale roughly as m−2m^{-2}.
A standard coupon-collector argument shows that it takes roughly O⁡(m​log⁡m)O(m\log m) accepted transitions to ensure that each edge has been swapped at least once, which would appear a reasonable requirement for a well-mixed chain.
We therefore conjecture that the overall mixing time of Algorithm 1 for the uniform model on dense multigraphs is no smaller than O⁡(m3​log⁡m)O(m^{3}\log m), though a more precise statement and proof would be welcome.
While much better than the best known proven results, such a scaling could likely be prohibitive for graphs of even modest size.




These considerations suggest that forming the MCMC estimate 𝛀^mc\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\mathrm{mc}} may not be a computationally practical way to estimate 𝛀\boldsymbol{\Omega} when mm is large.
Despite these limitations, Algorithm 1 lies at the heart of our main results in the next section.






3 A Dynamical Approach to Model Moments




We introduce some additional notation to facilitate calculations.

Algorithm 1 describes a stochastic dynamical update on the space 𝒢𝐝\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{d}} of multigraphs, which we identify with the space of symmetric matrices with nonnegative integer entries and zero diagonals.
Let 𝚫⁡(t)=𝐖⁡(t+1)−𝐖⁡(t)\boldsymbol{\Delta}(t)=\mathbf{W}(t+1)-\mathbf{W}(t) be the (random) increment in 𝐖\mathbf{W} in timestep t+1t+1.
We implicitly regard 𝐖\mathbf{W} and 𝚫\boldsymbol{\Delta} as functions of tt, suppressing the argument for notational sanity when there is no possibility of confusion.
We can separate 𝚫=𝚫+−𝚫−\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{+}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{-}, where Δi​j+=(Δi​j∨0)\Delta^{+}_{ij}=(\Delta_{ij}\vee 0) and Δi​j−=(−Δi​j∨0)\Delta^{-}_{ij}=(-\Delta_{ij}\vee 0).
The first term Δi​j+\Delta_{ij}^{+} describes the (random) number of edges flowing into the pair (i,j)(i,j) and the second term Δi​j−\Delta_{ij}^{-} the random number of edges flowing out.
Conservation of edges implies that ∑i​jΔi​j+=∑i​jΔi​j−\sum_{ij}\Delta_{ij}^{+}=\sum_{ij}\Delta_{ij}^{-}.
Since a pair of nodes can only gain or lose one edge at a time under the dynamics, the entries Δi​j+\Delta_{ij}^{+} and Δi​j−\Delta_{ij}^{-} are Bernoulli random variables.
These Bernoulli variables are not independent, since at most two entries of each matrix are nonzero in a given timestep.
Let 𝜹+=𝔼⁡[𝚫+]\boldsymbol{\delta}^{+}=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{+}] and 𝜹−=𝔼⁡[𝚫−]\boldsymbol{\delta}^{-}=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{-}].




Two things must hold at stationarity of Algorithm 1.
First, all moments of 𝐖\mathbf{W} must be constant in time.
Second, since the stationary distribution of Algorithm 1 is the target distribution ρ\rho by construction, these moments of 𝐖\mathbf{W} are the desired moments of ρ\rho.
We can therefore approximately compute moments of ρ\rho by approximately solving conveniently chosen stationarity conditions.

3.2 Moments of the Uniform Model




The analysis of the uniform model is somewhat more subtle.
As seen in the configuration model above, many of the sums that appear in the calculations of 𝜹+\boldsymbol{\delta}^{+} and 𝜹−\boldsymbol{\delta}^{-} reduced to fixed constants due to the degree constraints.
Unfortunately, there is no analogous simplification in the uniform model η𝐝\eta_{\mathbf{d}}.
Because of this, we require some additional technology in order to make progress.

Theorem 18.

Condition (2) requires that there exists r>0r>0 and α>0\alpha>0 such that h⁡(𝜷)≥αh(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\geq\alpha for all 𝜷\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\beta}} with ‖𝜷−𝜷0‖=r\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}\right\rVert=r.

Extending Theorem 18 to cover the full nonnegative orthant would be an interesting direction of future work.

4.2 Algorithms




Having proven some properties of the solutions of (21), it remains to develop an algorithm to find these solutions.
While it is possible to use standard gradient-based methods, this task is complicated by the ill-conditioned Jacobian of 𝐡\mathbf{h}.
Ill-conditioning arises from dramatic heterogeneity in the entries of 𝐒\mathbf{S}.
For example, in the experiments shown in Figure 2 in the next section, the observed and estimated values of σi​j\sigma_{ij} span four orders of magnitude, implying that entries of si​j≈σi​j2s_{ij}\approx\sigma_{ij}^{2} span roughly eight.
Because of this, methods based on the full Jacobian, such as standard implementations of gradient descent or Newton’s method, may require impractically small step-sizes in order to avoid pathological behavior.




We instead adopt a coordinate-wise approach.
Suppose we have a current estimate 𝜷^(t−1)\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t-1)}.
We obtain an estimate of 𝐝\mathbf{d} given by 𝐝^(t−1)=𝐡⁡(𝜷^(t−1))\hat{\mathbf{d}}^{(t-1)}=\mathbf{h}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t-1)}).
To update the iith coordinate of 𝜷^(t−1)\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t-1)}, we hold all other n−1n-1 coordinates fixed, and define β^i(t)\hat{\beta}_{i}^{(t)} to be the value of bb that solves the equation





hi​(β^1(t−1),…,β^i−1(t−1),b,β^i+1(t−1),…,β^n(t−1))=di.\displaystyle h_{i}\left(\hat{\beta}_{1}^{(t-1)},\ldots,\hat{\beta}_{i-1}^{(t-1)},b,\hat{\beta}_{i+1}^{(t-1)},\ldots,\hat{\beta}_{n}^{(t-1)}\right)=d_{i}\;.

(27)


We repeat this process for each of the nn coordinates, obtaining a fully updated new estimate 𝜷^(t)\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}.
We iterate this sweep over the coordinates until a desired error function drops below a user-specified tolerance.
A standard choice for the error function is the mean-square error n−1​ℒ​(𝜷)n^{-1}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\beta}), with ℒ\mathcal{L} as in (22).
Algorithm 2 formalizes the solution method.

While we have existence, uniqueness, and convergence guarantees for each coordinate update, we possess no such guarantees for Algorithm 2 as a whole.
Additionally, it may be the case that some elements of the sequence {𝜷^(t)}\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}\} produce estimates ω^(t)\hat{\omega}^{(t)} of the adjacency matrix in which some entries are negative.
However, we have never observed Algorithm 2 to fail to converge to a solution in which all entries of ω^(t)\hat{\omega}^{(t)} are positive.
Additionally, when a solution to (21) exists in ℬδ\mathcal{B}_{\delta} for some δ\delta, we have never observed Algorithm 2 to fail to find this solution.
In practice, an analyst can assess the success of the algorithm by checking that (a) the mean-square error is near zero and that (b) the corresponding estimate 𝛀^\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} has nonnegative entries.
Both such checks are implemented in the accompanying software.






5 Experiments




In this section, we describe a sequence of experiments exploring the behavior of Algorithm 2; the accuracy of the estimator 𝜷^\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}; the disparity between 𝛀^0\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{0} and 𝛀^1\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{1} on empirical networks; and implications for downstream tasks such as modularity maximization.




5.1 Synthetic Data




To study the convergence behavior of Algorithm 2, we test it on two synthetic degree sequences.
The “uniform” sequence consists of 200200 independent copies of 2​(u+1)2(u+1), where uu is a discrete uniform random variable on the interval [0,50][0,50].
We contrast this with a “Zipf” sequence 𝐝2\mathbf{d}_{2}, generated by sampling 200200 copies of 2​z2z, where zz is distributed as a Zipf random variable with parameter α=2\alpha=2.
These degree sequences are shown in Figure 1(a).
By design, the uniform sequence is relatively homogeneous in its degrees, while the Zipf sequence possesses a small number of extremely high-degree nodes.




We then estimated 𝜷^\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} for each of these degree sequences using Algorithm 2, initialized with 𝜷(0)=𝐞\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(0)}=\mathbf{e}.
The estimates for the uniform sequence 𝐝1\mathbf{d}_{1} converge rapidly, as shown in panel (b), and after two rounds the iterates cannot be distinguished by eye from the final estimate.
The final estimate 𝜷^\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} is both physical and well-behaved.
By Theorem 18, it is the only such solution to (21).
In contrast, the estimates for the Zipf-distributed sequence 𝐝2\mathbf{d}_{2}, shown in panel (c), require many rounds to converge.
Figure 1(d) compares the differing convergence rates.
The vertical axis gives the mean-square error (MSE) 1n​‖𝐡⁡(𝜷)−𝐝‖2\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\mathbf{h}(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\mathbf{d}\right\rVert_{2}.
While the MSE for the uniform degree sequence converges to within machine precision after 14 rounds, the Zipf iterates require over 200 iterations to reach an MSE below 10−610^{-6}.
The resulting estimate 𝜷^\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} is physical but not well-behaved, and Theorem 18 is therefore insufficient to provide a uniqueness guarantee.

Conversely, we can view 𝛀^1\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{1} as a nonlinear correction to 𝛀^0\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{0} as we depart from that regime.

5.3 Application: Modularity Maximization in Dense Contact Networks




Let ℓ:N→ℒ\ell:N\rightarrow\mathcal{L} be a function that assigns to node ii a label ℓi∈ℒ\ell_{i}\in\mathcal{L}.
The modularity of the partition ℓ\ell with respect to matrix 𝐰\mathbf{w} and null model ρ\rho is given by





Q⁡(ℓ,ρ)=12​m​∑i​j[wi​j−𝔼ρ​[Wi​j]]​𝟙​(ℓi,ℓj).\displaystyle Q(\ell;\rho)=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{ij}\left[w_{ij}-\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[W_{ij}]\right]\mathbbm{1}(\ell_{i},\ell_{j})\;.

(28)


The normalization by 2​m2m ensures that −1≤Q⁡(ℓ,ρ)≤1-1\leq Q(\ell;\rho)\leq 1.
Intuitively, Q⁡(ℓ,ρ)Q(\ell;\rho) is high when nodes that are more densely connected than expected by chance (under the specified null) are grouped together.
Maximizing this quantity with respect to ℓ\ell may therefore be reasonably expected to identify modular (“community”) structure in the network [33, 35].
Exact modularity maximization is NP-hard [11] and subject to theoretical limitations in networks with modules of heterogeneous sizes [21].
Despite this, it remains one of the most popular methods for practical community detection at scale [9].




In most implementations, ρ\rho is not explicitly specified – rather, the expectation 𝔼ρ​[Wi​j]\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[W_{ij}] is “hard-coded” as equal to ω^i​j0\hat{\omega}^{0}_{ij}.
From a statistical perspective, this reflects an implicit choice of ρ\rho as the Chung-Lu model [16], which preserves expected degrees and indeed possesses the given first moment.22
              2
              
              
              
              
              
              
              
            We note that alternative justifications of the use of 𝛀^0\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{0} exist, including connections to the stability of Markov chains [18] and to stochastic block models [34].
Modifications are possible; the best known is perhaps the resolution adjustment that replaces 𝛀^0\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{0} with γ​𝛀^0\gamma\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{0} for some γ>0\gamma>0 [37].
Other adjustments may incorporate spatial structure [20] or adjust for the inclusion of self-loops in the null space [12].
When we wish to perform modularity maximization against a null that deterministically preserves degree sequences, however, 𝛀^0\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{0} is at best an approximation.
We expect this approximation to perform adequately for the configuration model (cf. Theorem 4), and very poorly for the uniform model (previous subsection).
The Monte Carlo estimate 𝛀^mc\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\mathrm{mc}} can be used for very small data sets, but rapidly becomes computationally infeasible for larger ones.
In these cases, we can use the present estimator 𝛀^1\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{1} instead.




Recent work has highlighted the importance of studying the modularity landscape, especially the set of local maxima of QQ, rather than restricting attention to a single partition.
One reason for this is the phenomenon of degeneracy – in many practical contexts, a given network will possess many distinct local maxima with modularity comparable to the global maximum [23].
A second reason is model-specification.
As shown in [34], maximization of QQ is related to maximum-likelihood inference in a planted-partition stochastic blockmodel.
When the planted-partition model is unrealistic as a generative model for the data, modularity-maximization amounts to inference in a mis-specified model.
Degeneracy is a common symptom of this problem, but observing it requires locally optimizing QQ multiple times.
For these reasons among others, ensemble-based methods that implicitly average over local optima, such as those of [44], may be preferable.
With these considerations in mind, our aim in this section is not to show that the use of 𝛀^1\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{1} is strictly superior for this task when compared to 𝛀^0\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{0} for either one-shot or ensemble-based modularity maximization.
Rather, we will argue that the corresponding modularity landscapes are significantly different on data sets of practical interest, and that it is therefore methodologically unsafe to interchange these estimators without carefully scrutinizing the results.




Our first data set for this experiment is the full contact-high-school network, consisting of n=327n=327 nodes and m=189,928m=189,928 edges as described in the previous subsection.
The computation of 𝛀^mc\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\mathrm{mc}} is indeed infeasible for a graph this dense, and we therefore use 𝛀^1\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{1} as an estimate.
This setting highlights the utility of 𝛀^1\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{1}, since otherwise we would have no practical way to compute the uniform expectation.

To approximately maximize (28), we employ the multiway spectral partitioning (MSP) algorithm of [45], which generalizes the spectral bipartitioning algorithm of [33].
While greedy methods often enjoy superior performance [9], spectral methods have the advantage of depending strongly on the structure of the observed graph and the null model employed, and are relatively insensitive to choices made during the runtime of the algorithm.
Spectral methods are therefore ideal for highlighting differences in the modularity landscapes induced by alternative null models.
The algorithm requires the analyst to specify a null model and a desired number of communities kk.
The core of the approach is to use a low-rank approximation of the modularity matrix, 𝐌=𝐰−𝔼ρ​[𝐖]\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{w}-\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\mathbf{W}].
This approximation induces a map from the vertices of GG to a low-dimensional vector space.
Vectors in this space are clustered according to their relative angles using a procedure reminiscent of kk-means to produce the community assignment.
Because the clustering algorithm involves a stochastic starting condition, it is useful to run the algorithm multiple times and choose the highest modularity partition from among the repetitions.
We refer the reader to [45] for details, and to the code accompanying this paper for an implementation of MSP for arbitrary modularity matrices (see “Software”).




We ran this algorithm using both the CL modularity matrix 𝐌0=𝐰−𝛀^0\mathbf{M}^{0}=\mathbf{w}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{0} and the approximate uniform modularity matrix 𝐌1=𝐰−𝛀^1\mathbf{M}^{1}=\mathbf{w}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{1}.
We refer to these two algorithmic variants as MSP0\mathrm{MSP}^{0} and MSP1\mathrm{MSP}^{1}, respectively.
Since 𝛀^0\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{0} and 𝛀^1\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{1} produce very different null matrices, the modularity matrices 𝐌0\mathbf{M}^{0} and 𝐌1\mathbf{M}^{1} are themselves very different – the mean absolute relative error of using the latter to estimate the former is approximately 32%32\%.
We would therefore expect MSP0\mathrm{MSP}^{0} and MSP1\mathrm{MSP}^{1} to behave very differently in this task.
We allowed the number of communities kk to vary between 22 and 1010.
For each value of kk, we ran MSP0\mathrm{MSP}^{0} and MSP1\mathrm{MSP}^{1} in 100 batches of 50 repetitions.
From each batch of 50, the highest-modularity partition was chosen, resulting in 100 partitions per value of kk.
Figure 4(a) shows that MSP1\mathrm{MSP}^{1} tends to find higher modularity partitions than MSP0\mathrm{MSP}^{0} on this data set.
The difference is especially large when kk is small, but a substantial difference between the means is noticeable even for larger values.
While partitions under 𝐌0\mathbf{M}^{0} exist that are comparable to those under 𝐌1\mathbf{M}^{1}, it appears to be more difficult for MSP0\mathrm{MSP}^{0} to find them.
Panels (b) and (c) shed some light on the differing behavior of the two algorithms.
Partitions under MSP0\mathrm{MSP}^{0} tends to display a larger, less cohesive community ((b), top left) alongside smaller, more tightly interconnected ones.
Partitions under MSP1\mathrm{MSP}^{1} (c) tend to display communities that are slightly more uniform in size.




It is reasonable to object that modularity values under MSP1\mathrm{MSP}^{1} and MSP0\mathrm{MSP}^{0} should not be compared, since these objectives are defined with respect to differing null matrices.
In this specific case, the objection is not borne out numerically, however – “cross-evaluating” the partitions on the opposite matrices changes the modularities only minimally.
Evaluating the MSP0\mathrm{MSP}^{0} partition in Figure 4(b) on the modularity matrix 𝐌1\mathbf{M}^{1} gives Q=0.699Q=0.699, while evaluating the MSP1\mathrm{MSP}^{1} partition on 𝐌0\mathbf{M}^{0} yields Q=0.731Q=0.731.
On this data set, MSP1\mathrm{MSP}^{1} searches the energy landscape of MSP0\mathrm{MSP}^{0} more efficiently than does MSP0\mathrm{MSP}^{0} itself.




It should be noted that this behavior is data-set dependent.
The opposite case occurs in the contact-primary-school network [39, 5], which used similar sensors to construct an interaction network among students in a French primary school.
On this data, MSP0\mathrm{MSP}^{0} and MSP1\mathrm{MSP}^{1} perform similarly for k≤6k\leq 6 communities (Figure 5), with the former consistently outperforming the latter for k≥7k\geq 7.
The illustrative partitions in panels (b) and (c) suggest MSP1\mathrm{MSP}^{1} tends to prefer partitions with fewer communities.
Whereas MSP0\mathrm{MSP}^{0} chooses a partition with 77 communities, MSP1\mathrm{MSP}^{1} chooses one with just 55 (both having been initialized at k=8k=8).
These illustrations emphasize that MSP1\mathrm{MSP}^{1} and MSP0\mathrm{MSP}^{0} explore different modularity landscapes; that the relative advantages of each algorithm depend on the data; and that the landscape for MSP1\mathrm{MSP}^{1} can be tractably computed under the methodology we have introduced here.

6 Discussion




Much existing network theory is explicitly designed for large, sparse data.
However, many networks of interest are sufficiently dense to diverge significantly from the predictions of large, sparse theory.
We have highlighted this phenomenon in the context of dense multigraphs, with a focus on estimating the expected adjacency matrix 𝛀\boldsymbol{\Omega} of a random multigraph with specified degree sequence.
We have shown that, rather than falling back to computationally expensive MCMC, we can construct an accurate estimator 𝛀^1\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{1} using an indirect, dynamical approach.
Use of this estimator can in turn have significant impact on the results of downstream data analyses.




There are several directions for future work on the moments of uniform random graphs with fixed degree sequences.
As previously noted, the error bounds on 𝝌^\hat{\boldsymbol{\chi}} and 𝛀^1\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{1} derived in Section 3 appear quite loose when when compared against the empirical results in Figures 1 and 2.
The derivation of tighter error bounds would be helpful for researchers seeking practical accuracy guarantees.
Progress on this front appears to be hindered by the complex combinatorial structure of the space 𝒢𝐝\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{d}};
however, carefully chosen assumptions or approximations may allow headway.
An additional avenue of exploration concerns the impact of the choice between 𝛀^1\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{1} and 𝛀^0\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{0} on downstream analyses.
We have seen that the choice of null expectation can substantially change the performance of MSP\mathrm{MSP}, and that the direction of this effect depends on the data set.
A better understanding of the properties of the data or algorithm that make certain estimators highlight better solutions would be most welcome.




We focused our attention on the derivation of an estimator for 𝛀\boldsymbol{\Omega}.
It may also be possible to derive expressions for higher moments using the same methodology.
Such moments would approximate expected densities of various motifs under the uniform model.
Examples of interest may include wedge densities 𝔼⁡[wi​j​wj​k]\mathbb{E}[w_{ij}w_{jk}] and triangle densities 𝔼⁡[wi​j​wj​k​wi​k]\mathbb{E}[w_{ij}w_{jk}w_{ik}].
Parsing the stationarity conditions for these more complicated moments may be correspondingly more difficult.
An alternative would be to construct mean-field estimates by computing the relevant statistics on 𝛀^1\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{1} itself.
An evaluation of the accuracy of this approach would potentially replace the need for computationally intensive MCMC sampling to estimate these quantities.




Finally, it may also be of interest to develop similar theory for uniform distributions over related spaces of graphs.
For example, it would be possible to consider a uniform model including self-loops.
The associated analysis would be nontrivial due to required modifications in the MCMC sampling procedure (see [22]), but one might reasonably hope to obtain parallel results.
Directed graphs offer another important direction of generalization.
It would be natural to define a uniform distribution over spaces of directed multigraphs with fixed in-degree and out-degree sequences.
In this case, one might expect analysis to produce expressions for the moments of this distribution in terms of two collapsed degree sequences, corresponding to in- and out-degrees.
These and other generalizations offer promising avenues of future work.
