The finite-field subspace distance in [4] is different from (1.1). We also prove the sharp bound
2(n − k) in the top case (Theorem 11.2).

                6. An explicit connected Nash decomposition bound
   A Nash submanifold is a semialgebraic embedded real-analytic submanifold; it need not be
closed. A Nash function is real analytic with semialgebraic graph. Zero-dimensional pieces
are allowed. We use the Tarski–Seidenberg theorem and elementary facts about semialgebraic
sets as in [1]. Rather than invoke an unspecified effective stratification constant, we give a
projection-and-lifting construction with explicit estimates. No Whitney or frontier condition is
needed.

multiplicity in each disk is unchanged. Each disk contains at least one distinct root. Constancy
of the total number of distinct roots forces exactly one distinct root in each disk. Thus no root
splits and its multiplicity is locally constant. A real root remains real: otherwise its complex
conjugate would be a second distinct root in the same disk. Consequently, the number of real
roots is locally constant and therefore constant on connected C.
   If a real root has multiplicity µ, it is a simple root of ∂tµ−1 p. The analytic implicit function
theorem on C therefore makes its continuation analytic. Ordering the real roots gives globally
defined functions whose local expressions agree. Their graphs are semialgebraic: the jth root is
characterized by the polynomial equation and the existence of exactly j − 1 distinct smaller
real roots, a first-order real-algebraic condition. Tarski–Seidenberg applies. Thus the functions
are Nash.
   Finally, if roots of two polynomials coincide at x0 , apply the same no-splitting argument
to their product. They must coincide near x0 . The coincidence set is therefore open and, by
continuity, closed in C. Connectedness proves the last assertion. Distinct real root functions
cannot cross without coinciding.                                                                   □

The corresponding translation differences have projection of length one onto these lines, so all

                   14. Scope of the method and further questions
14.1. What is extended from Höfer’s argument. Höfer represents a three-dimensional
line by v = (l, m) ∈ R6 , with ∥l∥ = 1 and l · m = 0, and lifts it to vv T . For

his form is
                 B(A, B) = tr(M1 AM1 B) + tr(M2 AM2 B) − tr(M1 A) tr(M1 B).                (14.1)
Lines give isotropic vectors, and nonparallel unit-distance pairs give orthogonal vectors. A
dimension bound 2r − 3 for a Plücker span of dimension r, together with a minimal-dependence
argument for rank-one matrices, proves independence of the lifted representatives and then the
bound nine [5, Definitions 1–2, Lemmas 4–5].
   Here the higher-degree lift converts two polynomial kernels into common total isotropy, but
no analogous independence theorem is used. A lower-rank locus can contain a continuous family:
fixing a direction projection P and allowing a ∈ ker P to vary gives Fρ = Rρ = 0 for all pairs
whenever ρ > k, irrespective of their actual distance. The algebraic inverse image of the lifted
span is therefore partitioned into connected analytic pieces, and its rank is forced downward on
each piece. The actual unit-distance condition is used again at the terminal parallel rank. This
explains both why the lifting is useful and why it is not by itself a finiteness proof.
   The three-dimensional direction-independence lemma also fails in larger ambient dimension.
For example, the three lines
                                                       √
                                                1        3                   4
                     Re1 ,     e3 + Re2 ,       2 e3 + 2 e4 + R(e1 + e2 ) ⊂ R
are pairwise at distance one although all directions lie in span(e1 , e2 ). Their offsets form an
equilateral triangle in the orthogonal two-dimensional factor.
14.2. Quantitative and structural questions. The proof separates the cost of decomposing
a bounded-degree real algebraic set from the number of possible rank drops. Improving the
former without losing analyticity would improve the general upper bound. The present double-
exponential estimate is not expected to reflect the scale of extremal configurations. For fixed
k, the amplification inequalities give linear lower growth in n with explicit coefficients; for the
balanced sequence S(c), transverse products give exponential lower growth in c. These are
different parameter regimes.
   Several natural questions remain within the framework developed here.
      • Does Fc (k) eventually stabilize for each fixed c ⩾ 3?
      • Can higher-rank restrictions, beyond dim(Ui + Uj ) ⩽ k + 1, be reduced to bounded
        ambient dimension?
      • Can one improve the transverse product constructions or find matching upper bounds
        for S(c)?
Theorem 11.2 provides a sharp model for one degenerate direction class, while Example 13.5
shows why a general common-factor assertion would be too strong.
   All proofs of finiteness, parameter reduction, and product constructions in this paper are
independent of finite enumeration. The numerical lower bounds using seven lines invoke the
construction in [2], and the exact specialization in Corollary 11.5 additionally invokes [8]. No
global computer-free proof of N (1, 3) = 7 is supplied.
