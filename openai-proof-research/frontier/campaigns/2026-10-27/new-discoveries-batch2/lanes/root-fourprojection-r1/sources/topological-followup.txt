1. Introduction

   The central objects of study in this paper are quantum permutations and their gener-
alizations, quantum reﬂections. Given N ∈ N and an Hilbert space H, an N × N matrix
P = [Pij ]1≤i,j≤N ∈ MN (B(H)) (B(H) being the C∗ -algebra of bounded linear operators
on H) is called a magic unitary if its entries Pij are self-adjoint projections satisfying
                                  
the relations i Pij = 1B(H) = j Pij for each 1 ≤ i, j ≤ N .
   The simplest examples of magic unitaries are the classical permutation matrices, which
correspond to the case where the Hilbert space H is one-dimensional. In fact, more
generally any magic unitary P with commuting entries {Pij }i,j corresponds to a subset
X ⊆ SN of the permutation group on N letters. Indeed, the commutative C∗ -algebra
generated by the Pij ’s is isomorphic to C(X), the C∗ -algebra of complex functions on
some subset X ⊆ SN , and in this way P gets identiﬁed with the identity function X ⊂
SN → MN (C). On the other hand, if one now considers magic unitaries P whose entries
do not commute, the structure of these objects becomes much less well-understood. From
an operator algebraic point of view, this should come as no surprise, as the C∗ -algebras
C ∗ (Pij | 1 ≤ i, j ≤ N ) ⊂ B(H) generated by the entries of a magic unitary P with
non-commuting entries can be highly non-trivial (e.g., can contain the free group C∗ -
algebras as quotients [49]). Nonetheless, such “genuinely quantum” magic unitaries arise
naturally in a variety of contexts. For example, in quantum information theory, magic
unitaries arise in the framework of non-local games and go under the name “projective
permutation matrices” [4,39,37,38]. From the perspective of non-commutative geometry
and quantum group theory, N × N magic unitaries were discovered by Wang [49] to be
precisely the structures that encode the quantum symmetries of a ﬁnite set of N points.
More precisely, Wang considered the universal unital C∗ -algebra

   C∗ -algebraic compact quantum groups as introduced in [51] form the basis of what by
now is a rich theory, developing rapidly in a number of diﬀerent directions. As indicated
above, the perspective we adopt in this paper is that the Hopf (C∗ -)algebras studied
in [51–53] play the role of function algebras on a “compact quantum space” G which is
equipped with a group structure, and can equivalently be viewed as the complex group
algebras of the Pontryagin dual “discrete quantum group” Γ. To keep matters simple,
throughout this introduction we write CΓ for the group algebra of a discrete quantum
group Γ (see Section 2 below for details).
   One aspect of the theory of discrete quantum groups that presents itself naturally
from this point of view is that of approximation properties. This typically refers to the
“accessibility” of the quantum group (or its associated algebras) via ﬁnite structures of
some type. The phrase ‘ﬁnite structure’ is purposely vague, and lends itself to a variety
of interpretations, for example:

• The amenability of a discrete quantum group implies the nuclearity of C ∗ (Γ), the
  universal C∗ -completion of CΓ. (I.e., the identity map on C ∗ (Γ) can be point-norm
  approximated by ﬁnite-rank completely positive contractions). The converse is also
  true, at least for Kac type discrete quantum groups [14].
• The Haagerup approximation property of Γ corresponds to the point-norm approxi-
  mation of the identity map on the reduced C∗ -algebra Cr∗ (Γ) by certain well-behaved
  L2 -compact, contractive completely positive maps [28].
• The weak amenability of Γ corresponds to the point-norm approximation of the
  identity map on the reduced C∗ -algebra Cr∗ (Γ) by certain well-behaved ﬁnite rank,
  uniformly bounded completely bounded maps [29,25].

The above are only a few scattered examples, as we cannot possibly do justice to the
vast literature here. We refer to the survey [22] and its sources for a more expansive
discussion on approximation properties for discrete (in fact locally compact) quantum
groups. The above list (and all those considered in [22]) can be thought of as instances
internal approximation of a quantum group by ﬁnite structures, since all the above
4                 M. Brannan et al. / Advances in Mathematics 363 (2020) 106982



approximating maps are from a given object to itself. In (quantum) group theory there
are of course approximation properties which have an external ﬂavor in the sense that
one approximates a given large object by mapping it into smaller auxiliary objects. For
example,

    • A Kac type discrete quantum group Γ is hyperlinear if its quantum group von Neu-
      mann algebra L(Γ) admits ﬁnite-dimensional matrix models relative to its Haar trace
      [23].
    • A Kac type discrete quantum group Γ has the Kirchberg factorization property if there
      is a net ϕk : C ∗ (Γ) → Mn(k) (C) of contractive completely positive maps which are
      asymptotically trace-norm multiplicative and satisfy h = limk trn(k) ◦ ϕk pointwise,
      where h is the Haar trace and trn(k) is the normalized matrix trace [17].
    • A (ﬁnitely generated) discrete quantum group Γ is residually ﬁnite if the points of
      CΓ are separated by its ﬁnite-dimensional ∗-representations [26,16].
    • A stronger form of residual ﬁniteness of interest to us is the existence of a faithful
      or inner faithful representation π : CΓ → MN (C(X)) for some compact Hausdorﬀ
      X [9,11,12,7]; we will have more to say about this concept below.

2. Preliminaries

2.1. Generalities

  Let us start by recalling some facts about compact quantum groups from [53]:

Deﬁnition 2.1. A compact quantum group is a unital C∗ -algebra C(G) equipped with a
unital ∗-morphism

                                Δ : C(G) → C(G) ⊗ C(G)

(minimal tensor product) such that

         span{(a ⊗ 1)Δ(b) | a, b ∈ C(G)} and span{(1 ⊗ a)Δ(b) | a, b ∈ C(G)}

are dense in C(G) ⊗ C(G). 

   As is customary, we regard C(G) as the algebra of continuous functions on the ﬁcti-
tious “compact quantum space” G. For this reason, the category of compact quantum
groups is dual to that of C∗ -algebras as in Deﬁnition 2.1.
   For a compact quantum group G we denote the unique dense Hopf ∗-subalgebra of
C(G) by A(G); it can be regarded alternatively as the complex group algebra C G of
the discrete quantum group G whose Pontryagin dual is G, for which reason we might
occasionally revert to that alternative notation for it. It is often also rendered as Pol(G)
or O(G) in the literature.
   The C*-algebra C(G) is equipped with a unique state h : C(G) → C (its Haar state)
that is left and right-invariant in the sense that the diagram
8                  M. Brannan et al. / Advances in Mathematics 363 (2020) 106982


                                  Δ       C(G) ⊗ C(G)          h⊗id

                         C(G)                                         C(G)
                                      h         C

and the analogous mirror diagram (obtained by substituting id ⊗ h for the upper right
hand arrow) both commute. We will also occasionally refer to the quantum group von
Neumann algebra L∞ (G), which is by deﬁnition the von Neumann algebra generated
by the GNS representation of C(G) associated to h. Often in the literature the von
Neumann algebra L∞ (G) is written as L(G) in view of it being a generalization of the
von Neumann algebra generated by the left-regular representation of a discrete group.

Deﬁnition 2.2. A representation of the compact quantum group G is a ﬁnite-dimensional
comodule over the Hopf ∗-algebra A(G). We write Rep(G) for the category of represen-
tations of G. 

   We refer to [41] for the relatively small amount of background needed here on comod-
ules over coalgebras or Hopf algebras.

Deﬁnition 2.3. Given two compact quantum groups H and G, we say that H is a (closed)
quantum subgroup of G if there exists a surjective Hopf ∗-algebra morphism π : A(G) →
A(H). In this case, we write H ⊂ G. 

   Note that if H ⊂ G as above and V is a G-comodule, then V automatically be-
comes a H-comodule in a natural way. Indeed if α : V → V ⊗ A(G) is the associated
corepresentation deﬁning the comodule V and π : A(G) → A(H) is the surjective
Hopf ∗-morphism from above, then V becomes an H-comodule via the corepresentation
(id ⊗π)α : V → V ⊗ A(H). This “restriction” of representations of G to representations
of H, induces, at the level of Hom-spaces, natural inclusions

                                 homG (V, W ) → homH (V, W ),

for any pair of G comodules V, W .

4. Inner faithful matrix models for quantum permutation groups

   Theorem 3.6 shows that the quantum group algebras A(SN           +

ﬁnite-dimensional ∗-representations, i.e. map faithfully into a product of matrix algebras.
In the present section we prove that a speciﬁc, canonical collection of “elementary”
representations is faithful in a certain sense. First, let us clarify the appropriate notion
of faithfulness here. See e.g. [9, Deﬁnition 2.7] for more details.

Deﬁnition 4.1. A ∗-homomorphism π : A → B from a Hopf ∗-algebra A into a ∗-algebra
B is inner faithful if ker π contains no non-trivial Hopf ∗-ideals. Equivalently, for any
factorization π = π̃ ◦ ρ with ρ : A → A , a surjective morphism of Hopf ∗-algebras, we
have in fact that ρ is an isomorphism. More generally, the Hopf image of π : A → B is
the “smallest” quotient Hopf ∗-algebra A such that π factors through the quotient map
ρ : A → A . 

   Note that the Hopf image always exists and is unique (up to isomorphism) [9]. In
this paper, we will only be concerned with the cases where our Hopf ∗-algebras are
of the form A(G) for a compact quantum group G. In this case, the Hopf image of
π : A(G) → B is A(H), where H ⊂ G is the “smallest” quantum subgroup such that π
factors through ρ : A(G) → A(H). Moreover, π is inner faithful if and only if H = G
(up to isomorphism).
