                                                counterparts involving positive definite matrices.

                                           1. Introduction
                                           Let Cn×n denote the set of all n × n matrices over the complex numbers. A
                                           Hermitian matrix A ∈ Cn×n is called a positive definite, if x∗ Ax > 0 for all
                                           0 6= x ∈ Cn . An interesting and rather classical topic of study in matrix anal-
                                           ysis concerns inequalities for positive definite matrices. Roughly speaking,
                                           matrix inequalities are noncommutative versions of the corresponding scalar
                                           inequalities. Most of the work in this direction considers the comparison of
                                           eigenvalues or singular values or the traces, of various combinations of two
                                           positive definite matrices [3, 4, 9, 18]. The main objective of this short note
                                           is to present matrix versions of certain inequalities involving more than two
                                           positive real numbers.
                                                 We begin by stating the inequalities that we will be interested in ob-
                                           taining matrix analogues of, in this work. For three positive real numbers a, b
                                           and c, Nesbitt’s cyclic sum inequality [12], is the following:
                                                                      a   b   c   3
                                                                        +   +    ≥ .                                     (1.1)
                                                                     b+c c+a a+b  2

      Inequality (1.2) has a rich history. In 1958, Mordell [11] proved Shapiro’s
inequality for p = 3, 4, 5, 6. The inequality was subsequently proved for p = 8
[7], p = 10 [13], and p = 11, 12 [8]. It follows from a remarkable result by
Diananda [6] that Sp ≥ p/2 for all p ≤ 12. On the other hand, Zulauf [19]
showed that the inequality does not hold for p = 14, and for p = 25, Daykin
[5] and Malcolm [10] gave a counterexample. The difference in the behavior
between even and odd p was explained by Searcy and Troesch [14]. Finally,
Shapiro’s inequality was completely settled by Troesch [16, 17]. To summa-
rize, Shapiro’s inequality holds for positive numbers a1 , a2 , . . . , ap precisely
for the following values of p :
                       3, 4, 5, . . . , 12,   13, 15, 17, . . . , 23.

     Let us turn our attention to the present work. A matrix counterpart of
Nesbitt’s inequality is obtained in Theorem 2.5. In Theorem 2.7, we obtain a
matrix version of the Shapiro’s inequality for four variables. In Theorem 2.8,
we give a necessary condition on the validity of matrix version of Shapiro’s
inequality. In the last part, two matrix analogues of the Cauchy-Schwarz
inequality are proved. After a brief discussion on the notation and two pre-
liminary results, we prove our results.
     Notation: The trace of a matrix A ∈ Cn×n , denoted by T r(A) is the
sum of its diagonal entries/eigenvalues. If A and B are Hermitian matrices,
and A − B is positive semidefinite, then this will be denoted by A  B. Let
λ(A) denote an arbitrary eigenvalue of A ∈ Cn×n . The next two results are
only stated. Their proofs follow easily.

Theorem 1.1. [1, Exercise 12.14] Let A and B be positive semidefinite ma-
trices. Then
                          0 ≤ T r(AB) ≤ T r(A)T r(B).

Theorem 1.2. [18, Exercise 18, p. 213] Let X, Y ∈ Cm×n and let A ∈ Cm×m
be a positive definite matrix. Then
                   |T r(X ∗ Y )|2 ≤ T r(X ∗ AX)T r(Y ∗ A−1 Y ).

B)(B −1 − A−1 )} ≥ 0 [1, Exercise 12.28 (c)] and [18, Exercise 21, p. 213]. In

of (A − B)(B −1 − A−1 ) is greater than or equal to 0.
