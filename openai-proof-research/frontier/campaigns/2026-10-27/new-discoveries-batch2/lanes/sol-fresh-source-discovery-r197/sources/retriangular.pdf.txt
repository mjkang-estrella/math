                                                            RETRIANGULAR NUMBERS AND AFFINE MAPS
                                                                 BETWEEN POLYGONAL NUMBERS

                                                                                      MATEO MATIJASEVICK


                                                   Abstract. A positive integer a is called retriangular if there is a positive integer b such that
                                                   aT + b is a triangular number whenever T is. We show that the retriangular numbers are




arXiv:2610.36284v1 [math.NT] 26 Oct 2026
                                                   exactly the odd squares greater than 1, and that for each of them b is unique. The proof
                                                   rests on a rigidity principle: a non-constant affine map carrying the values of one quadratic
                                                   sequence into the values of another is induced by an affine map of the plane sending the vertex
                                                   of one parabola to the vertex of the other. We use it to classify all affine maps between sets of
                                                   polygonal numbers; the s-gonal numbers map affinely into the t-gonal numbers if and only if
                                                   Mt | Ms , where Ms = 2(s − 2)/ gcd(s, 4) defines a bijection from {3, 4, 5, . . . } onto the positive
                                                   integers. We also treat sparse data, polynomial maps, figurate numbers of higher degree and
                                                   sums of two triangular numbers, and we study the monoid of affine self-maps of an arbitrary
                                                   set of integers. For the s-gonal numbers this monoid is an arithmetical congruence monoid,
                                                   and the factorization of self-maps into irreducible ones is unique only for triangular and square
                                                   numbers.

   We then consider arbitrary sets of integers. For S ⊆ Z, the affine maps ϕ with ϕ(S) ⊆ S
form a monoid under composition, whose non-constant elements we call the self-maps of S.
When |S| ≥ 2, self-maps have integral slopes and are injective, and the surjective ones form a
subgroup of the infinite dihedral group (Proposition 7.1). For sparse sets they are rigid.
Theorem C. Let S ⊆ Z be infinite and bounded below, and assume that the differences between
consecutive elements of S tend to infinity. Then any two self-maps of S commute, and each
self-map is determined by its slope. If S has a self-map other than the identity, there is a
rational number ω ≤ min S such that every self-map of S is a homothety x 7→ ω + a(x − ω)
with a ∈ N.
   For the polygonal numbers the centre ω is the minimum of Ps on R; this is why the in-
tercept in Theorem A is forced by the slope. Composition of self-maps of Ps corresponds to
multiplication of the ratios x in Theorem A, which raises a question of factorization.
Theorem D. Let s ≥ 3. Under composition, the self-maps of Ps form a monoid isomorphic
to the multiplicative monoid HMs = {x ∈ N : x ≡ 1 (mod Ms )}. Every self-map is a
composition of irreducible self-maps. This factorization is unique for every self-map if and
only if s ∈ {3, 4}, and all factorizations of each self-map have the same number of factors if
and only if s ∈ {3, 4, 5, 6, 8}.

Related work. Identities between figurate numbers such as (1.1) are classical; see [10, Ch. I]
and [9], and [15] for Diophantus’ treatise on polygonal numbers. We have not found a proof of
the necessity part of Theorem 2.1, or of Theorems A and B, in the literature. Proposition 3.2
is an explicit quadratic instance of a general principle: if f (n) is a value of the polynomial
g for all n in a set that is not too sparse, then f = g ◦ h for some polynomial h. This is a
consequence of Hilbert’s irreducibility theorem, in the form that a thin set of integers has only
O(N 1/2 ) elements in [−N, N ] [26, 25]; see also [8] for value-set problems of this kind. When
the equation f (x) = g(y) has infinitely many integer solutions is decided by the Bilu–Tichy
theorem [3]; Theorem 6.1 makes explicit, in the simplest case, the difference between “infinitely
many x” and “all x”. The equation aPs (n) = Ps (m), required for some rather than for all
n, was studied by Chahal, Griffin and Priddis [6] by means of Pell equations. The functional
equations P ◦ f = g ◦ h of Section 6.2 belong to Ritt’s theory of polynomial decomposition
[24]. For Krull monoids, arithmetical congruence monoids and Carlitz’s theorem we refer to
[1, 2, 5, 7, 12]. The factorization properties of the monoids HM that enter Theorem D are
known [1, 14, 18]; what is new there is the isomorphism between HMs and the monoid of
self-maps of Ps . Sets of integers generated by affine maps were studied by Klarner and Rado
[20].

principle and its geometric interpretation. Sections 4 and 5 prove Theorems A and B, Section 6
contains the complements mentioned above, and Section 7 studies the self-maps of arbitrary
sets and proves Theorems C and D.

Monoids of this kind are called arithmetical congruence monoids [2, 7]; the classical Hilbert
monoid is H4 = {1, 5, 9, 13, . . . }. More generally, for a, M ∈ N with a2 ≡ a (mod M ) the

closed: the element u = 1 + M of its quotient group satisfies a un ∈ Ha,M for every n ≥ 1,
although u ∈ / Ha,M . Only the Krull case a = 1 arises here. For the factorization theory
of arithmetical congruence monoids in general see [1, 2, 7]; congruence monoids in Dedekind
domains are often, but not always, weakly Krull monoids [13, Examples 5.7.2]. For s = 3
we recover Proposition 7.7(a), and for s = 4 the self-maps are the maps N 7→ m2 N , so

Theorem 7.8. Let s ≥ 3. Every self-map of Ps is a composition of irreducible self-maps, and:
(a) the factorization into irreducible self-maps is unique up to order for every self-map if and
    only if s ∈ {3, 4};
(b) all factorizations of each self-map have the same number of factors if and only if s ∈
    {3, 4, 5, 6, 8}.
Proof. It suffices to prove the corresponding statements for HM with M = Ms . These state-
ments are known [1, §3], [14, 18], and we recall the argument. Factorizations into atoms exist
because an element of HM that is not an atom is a product of two smaller elements. Let
F be the free commutative monoid on the primes not dividing M . Then HM is a saturated
submonoid of F (if x, y ∈ HM and x | y in F , then y/x ∈ HM ), and the embedding is a
divisor theory: every α ∈ F is the greatest common divisor of αq and αq ′ , where q, q ′ ∤ αM
are distinct primes with q ≡ q ′ ≡ α−1 (mod M ). The class group q(F )/q(HM ) is (Z/M Z)× .
Thus HM is a Krull monoid with class group (Z/M Z)× , and by Dirichlet’s theorem every class
contains infinitely many primes. For such monoids, factorization is unique if and only if the
class group is trivial, and all factorizations of each element have the same length if and only
if the class group has at most two elements; the latter is Carlitz’s theorem [5], which holds for
Krull monoids of this kind [12]. Here the class group has order φ(M ), where φ is Euler’s func-
tion, and φ(M ) = 1 if and only if M ∈ {1, 2}, while φ(M ) ≤ 2 if and only if M ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 6}.
By Corollary 5.2, Ms ∈ {1, 2} if and only if s ∈ {3, 4}, and Ms ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 6} if and only if
s ∈ {3, 4, 5, 6, 8}.                                                                            □
