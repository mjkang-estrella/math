                                                                                             Introduction
                                             A quadratic form q over a field k is called universal if q represents all non-zero elements of k;
                                          i.e., for all a ∈ k× there is some x such that q(x) = a. The question of determining which quadratic
                                          forms are universal goes back hundreds of years. For example, a universality result is given by
                                          Lagrange’s Four Square Theorem of 1770: all positive integers can be written as a sum of four
                                          squares. In other words, the quadratic form x21 + x22 + x23 + x24 over Q represents all positive
                                          integers. Over the rational numbers, the question of universality is typically restricted to asking
                                          if a quadratic form represents all positive integers, and has led to results like the “15-Theorem”
                                          of Conway-Schneeberger [Bha00, Con00], the “290-Theorem” of Bhargava-Hanke [BH05], and the
                                          “451-Theorem” of Rouse [Rou14]. These questions have also been asked over various totally real
                                          number fields in, e.g., [BK18, KY21]. What these fields have in common is that they all have infinite
                                          u-invariant. Recall that the u-invariant of a field k, u(k), is defined to be the maximal dimension
                                          of an anisotropic quadratic form over k, and if no such maximum exists we say u(k) = ∞ [Lam05,
                                          Definition XI.6.1]. In this paper, we will be interested in studying universal quadratic forms over
                                          fields with finite u-invariant.
                                             Any regular isotropic quadratic form over a field k of characteristic 6= 2 is universal [Lam05,
                                          Theorem I.3.4(3)], so we will focus on studying anisotropic universal quadratic forms over k. We
                                          are particularly interested in studying the m-invariant of k, m(k), defined in [GVG92] to be the
                                          minimal dimension of an anisotropic universal quadratic form over k, and in studying the set AU(k)
                                          of dimensions of anisotropic universal quadratic forms over k. Most of our investigation is focused
                                          on these problems over a semi-global field; i.e., over a one-variable function field over a complete
                                          discretely valued field. Such fields have been extensively studied in, e.g., [CPS12, HH10, HHK13,
                                          HHK15a]. Our main approach to studying anisotropic universal quadratic forms over semi-global
                                          fields is to use the local-global principle for isotropy with respect to various sets of overfields.
                                          This approach has been used previously to compute the u-invariants of semi-global fields (see, e.g.,
                                          [CPS12, HHK09, HHK15b]).
                                             Main results and structure. The structure of this manuscript is as follows. After establishing
                                          notation and discussing background in Section 1, we prove preliminary results about anisotropic
                                          universal quadratic forms in Section 2.

                                  1. Background and Notation
   All of the fields considered will have characteristic different from 2, and all quadratic forms
(occasionally referred to just as forms) considered will be nondegenerate (or regular ). Our notation
and terminology follows [Lam05], and we assume familiarity with basic notions of quadratic form
theory (see [Lam05, Chapter I]).
   Let q be an n-dimensional quadratic form over a field k. Because the field k has characteristic 6= 2,
we can diagonalize q over k and write q ≃ ha1 , . . . , an i with each ai ∈ k× . Here the symbol ≃ denotes
isometry of quadratic forms over k. We call the elements a1 , . . . , an ∈ k× the entries of q. For
any a ∈ k× , we let a · q denote the quadratic form hai ⊗ q. The set of elements of k× represented
by q over k will be denoted by Dk (q). Throughout this manuscript we will repeatedly use two
elementary results that give necessary and sufficient conditions for an element a ∈ k× to belong
to Dk (q). The first is the Representation Criterion [Lam05, Theorem I.2.3], which states that
a ∈ Dk (q) if and only if there is a quadratic form q ′ over k such that q ≃ hai ⊥ q ′ . The second is
the First Representation Theorem [Lam05, Corollary I.3.5], which states that a ∈ Dk (q) if and only
                                                    2
if q ⊥ h−ai is isotropic. The First Representation Theorem is particularly useful since it allows us
to use results about isotropy of quadratic forms to study universality.
   A valuable tool when studying isotropy of quadratic forms over a field k is the local-global
principle for isotropy with respect to some set of overfields. Given a quadratic form q over k and a
collection {ki }i∈I of overfields of k (i.e., k ⊆ ki for all i ∈ I), we say that q satisfies the local-global
principle for isotropy with respect to {ki }i∈I if q being isotropic over ki for all i ∈ I implies that q is
isotropic over k. Whether the local-global principle for isotropy holds with respect to various sets
of overfields has been extensively studied (see, e.g., [Cas23, CPS12, HHK15a, Hu12, Gup21]).
   In this paper, we will encounter several different collections of overfields, one of which being the
set of v-adic completions kv of k with respect to various non-trivial discrete valuations v on k. In
this context, we will frequently use Springer’s Theorem on quadratic forms over complete discretely
valued fields [Lam05, Proposition VI.1.9], which we will refer to as Springer’s Theorem. Springer’s
Theorem states that, over a complete discretely valued field K with uniformizer π, valuation ring O,
and residue field κ with char κ 6= 2, a quadratic form q ≃ q1 ⊥ π · q2 (where the entries of q1 , q2 are
all units in O) is anisotropic over K if and only if both residue forms q 1 , q 2 are anisotropic over κ.

                                            2. Preliminaries
   Let k be a field of characteristic 6= 2 with finite u-invariant. Then any quadratic form q over k
with dim q = u(k) must be universal over k, and any quadratic form over k of dimension > u(k) is
isotropic. So if u(k) < ∞ we have
             u(k) = max{dim q ≥ 1 | q an anisotropic universal quadratic form over k}.
This then naturally leads to the problem of determining the minimal dimension of an anisotropic
universal quadratic form over k, which is precisely the definition of the m-invariant of k.
Definition 2.1 ([GVG92]). Let k be a field. The m-invariant of k is defined by
             m(k) = min{dim q ≥ 1 | q an anisotropic universal quadratic form over k}.
If there are no anisotropic universal quadratic forms over k we say that m(k) = ∞.
Examples 2.2.     (1) m(R) = ∞,
 (2) m(C) = 1,
 (3) m(Fp ) = 2 for any odd prime p.
   We now record some preliminary results about the m-invariant. Immediately from its definition
we see that 1 ≤ m(k) ≤ u(k) for any field k of characteristic 6= 2. Moreover, there are certain
integers that can never be the m-invariant of a field k. Indeed, for any field k of characteristic 6= 2,
m(k) 6= 3, 5 [GVG92, 1.1a), p. 194], and if k is a linked field (see, e.g, [Lam05, p. 370] for the
definition of a linked field), then m(k) 6= 7 [GVG92, 1.1b), p. 195]. These statements are similar
to [Lam05, Proposition XI.6.8], which states that, for any field k with char k 6= 2, u(k) 6= 3, 5, or 7.
   We can make the statement m(k) ≤ u(k) more precise. Indeed, m(k) and u(k) are always
separated by a power of 2.
Proposition 2.3. Let k be a field of characteristic 6= 2, and let n ≥ 0 be the largest integer such
that 2n ≤ u(k). Then m(k) ≤ 2n ≤ u(k).
Proof. If u(k) = 1, then n = 0 and m(k) = 1 ≤ 20 [GVG92, Proposition 1.3]. If u(k) ≥ 2, then this
is precisely the statement of [GVG92, Corollary 1.6].                                          
  As an immediate consequence of Proposition 2.3, we have
Corollary 2.4. If k is a field of characteristic 6= 2 with m(k) = u(k) < ∞, then m(k) = u(k) = 2n
for some integer n ≥ 0.

Remark 2.5. In [GVG92] it was observed that determining when m(k) = 2 can be related to
studying the Kaplansky radical of k, denoted R(k), defined by Kaplansky in [Kap69]. By [Lam05,
Proposition XII.6.1], R(k) consists of the elements a ∈ k× such that h1, −ai is universal over k. By
definition, R(k) ⊆ k× , and because isotropic quadratic forms are universal, we have k×2 ⊆ R(k).
By [GVG92, 1.2, p. 195], m(k) = 2 if and only if k×2 ⊂ R(k) ⊂ k× , where both inclusions are
strict. The Kaplansky radical was also studied in, e.g., [BL14], where Becher and Leep called a
field k radical-free if R(k) = k×2 . If k× 6= k×2 , then k is radical-free if and only if m(k) > 2.
   Much like the u-invariant, computing the m-invariant of a given field is a challenging problem.
For some simple fields like C, Fp , and R, we know exact values of the m-invariant, each of which
agrees with the respective u-invariant. However, there are fields k with m(k) < u(k). For example,
in [GVG92, Example 2.10], for any positive integer n ≥ 2, Gesquière and Van Geel constructed a
field k with m(k) = 2 and u(k) = 2n, and in [Hof94, Proposition 4.3] Hoffmann constructed a field k
with m(k) = 6, which by Proposition 2.3 must have m(k) < u(k). We will see other examples of
such fields as we continue (see, e.g., Remark 3.14).
   For fields k with m(k) = u(k) < ∞, the only possible dimension of an anisotropic universal
quadratic form over k is this common value m(k) = u(k). However, if m(k) < u(k), then there
may be other such dimensions. With this in mind, we define the set AU(k) by
                AU(k) = {dim q | q an anisotropic universal quadratic form over k} .
From the above observations, for a field k with u(k) < ∞, we see that AU(k) is non-empty, with
m(k), u(k) ∈ AU(k).
   Throughout this manuscript, we will use the local-global principle for isotropy with respect to
various sets of overfields to compute both lower bounds and exact values of the m-invariant for
certain fields k, as well as to compute the set AU(k). As a part of this process, it is beneficial to
collect several results about universal quadratic forms over complete discretely valued fields.

Corollary 2.7. Let K be a complete discretely valued field with residue field k of characteristic 6= 2.
Then AU(K) = {r1 + r2 | r1 , r2 ∈ AU(k)}.
Proof. Let q be any anisotropic universal quadratic form over K, and write q ≃ q1 ⊥ π · q2 ,

dim q 1 , dim q 2 ∈ AU(k). Since q was arbitrary, this shows that AU(K) ⊆ {r1 + r2 | r1 , r2 ∈ AU(k)}.

universal quadratic forms over k of dimensions r1 , r2 , respectively. Then for lifts ϕ1 , ϕ2 of ϕ1 , ϕ2
to K, the (r1 + r2 )-dimensional quadratic form ϕ defined over K by ϕ = ϕ1 ⊥ π · ϕ2 is anisotropic
and universal over K by Lemma 2.6. This completes the proof.                                          

Lemma 2.9. Let k be any field of characteristic 6= 2, and let v be any non-trivial discrete valuation
on k. If a quadratic form q over k is universal over k, then q is universal over its completion kv .
Proof. To show that q is universal over kv , it suffices to show that q ⊥ h−bv i is isotropic over kv for
all bv ∈ kv× . To that end, let bv ∈ kv× be arbitrary. Because k is dense in kv , by an application of
Krasner’s Lemma [Mil20, Proposition 7.61] we can find an element b ∈ k× close enough to bv in kv
to ensure that b and bv belong to the same square class of kv . That is, in kv , b/bv is a square.
   Since q is universal over k, the form q ⊥ h−bi is isotropic over k. Then over kv , we have
q ⊥ h−bv i ≃ q ⊥ h−bi. Thus q ⊥ h−bv i is isotropic over kv , proving the lemma.                       
  To conclude this section, we prove a lemma that shows how the local-global principle for isotropy
can be used to study the m-invariant. This lemma will be used throughout the manuscript.
Lemma 2.10. Let k be a field of characteristic 6= 2, and let {ki }i∈I be a set of overfields of k.
Suppose there exists an integer n ≥ 1 such that all (n + 1)-dimensional quadratic forms over k
satisfy the local-global principle for isotropy with respect to {ki }i∈I . Let q be an n-dimensional
quadratic form over k that is universal (e.g., isotropic) over ki for all i ∈ I. Then q is universal
over k. If in addition q is anisotropic over k, then m(k) ≤ n.
Proof. The second statement follows immediately from the first by the definition of m(k), so it
suffices to prove the first statement. Let a ∈ k× be arbitrary and consider the (n + 1)-dimensional
quadratic form q ⊥ h−ai over k. Since q is universal over ki for all i ∈ I, it follows that q ⊥ h−ai is
isotropic over ki for all i ∈ I. This, by assumption, implies that q ⊥ h−ai is isotropic over k. Since
a ∈ k× was arbitrary, q must be universal over k.                                                    

has been used to calculate u(F ) (see, e.g., [CPS12, HHK09, HHK15b]). We will use the local-global

   In fact, in the situation of Notation 3.3, by [Abh69, Lip75], there are regular models X of F ,
i.e., X is a normal model of F that is regular as a T -curve. In [HHK15a, Section 6], Harbater,

local-global principle we will focus on is the local-global principle for isotropy of quadratic forms.
Theorem 3.4 (Harbater-Hartmann-Krashen). Let T be a complete discrete valuation ring of
residue characteristic 6= 2 with fraction field K. Let F be a one-variable function field over K,
and let X be a normal model of F with closed fiber X. Let q be a quadratic form of dimension at
least 3 over F . Then
             q is isotropic over F if and only if q is isotropic over FP for all P ∈ X.
Moreover, two-dimensional quadratic forms over F satisfy this local-global principle for isotropy if
and only if the reduction graph of a regular model of F is a tree.
Proof. See [HHK15a, Theorem 9.3] for the statement about quadratic forms of dimension ≥ 3, and
[HHK15a, Corollary 9.7] for the statement about two-dimensional quadratic forms.             

   We will now show that we can use the reduction graph of a regular model of a semi-global field F
to determine when m(F ) = 2.
Lemma 3.6. Let T be a complete discrete valuation ring of residue characteristic 6= 2 with fraction
field K. Let F be a one-variable function field over K with a regular model whose reduction graph
is not a tree. Then m(F ) = 2.
Proof. By Corollary 3.2 we know that m(F ) ≥ 2. Therefore, to prove the result, it suffices to show
that m(F ) ≤ 2. Let X be a regular model of F whose reduction graph is not a tree, and let X be
the closed fiber of X . By [HHK15a, Corollary 9.7] there exists a two-dimensional quadratic form
over F that is anisotropic over F but isotropic over FP for all P ∈ X. However, three-dimensional
quadratic forms over F satisfy the local-global principle for isotropy with respect to these overfields
[HHK15a, Theorem 9.3]. Thus m(F ) ≤ 2 by Lemma 2.10.                                                 
  The next lemma is a rephrasing of Lemma 2.9 over a semi-global field F in terms of points P on
the closed fiber of a particular regular model of F . Such a model always exists (see Remark 3.9).
Lemma 3.7. Let F be a one-variable function field over the fraction field of a complete discrete
valuation ring T of residue characteristic 6= 2. Let X be a regular model of F with closed fiber X
such that distinct branches at any closed point of X lie on distinct irreducible components of X.
Let q be a universal quadratic form over F . Then for all points P ∈ X, q is universal over FP .
Proof. There are two types of points P ∈ X: generic points of irreducible components of X, and
closed points. We consider these types of points separately.
   First, suppose that P ∈ X is the generic point η of an irreducible component of X. The
codimension one point η on X induces a discrete valuation vη on F , and in this case the field
FP = Fη is the completion of F with respect to vη . Therefore, by Lemma 2.9, since q is universal
over F , it is also universal over FP .
   Next, suppose that P ∈ X is a closed point. To show that q is universal over FP , by the First
Representation Theorem, it suffices to show that, for all bP ∈ FP× , the form q ⊥ h−bP i is isotropic
over FP . To that end, let bP ∈ FP× be arbitrary. Because we assumed distinct branches at P lie on
distinct irreducible components of X, the conditions of [HHK13, Corollary 3.3(c)] are automatically
satisfied. Therefore there is some b ∈ F × and c ∈ FP× such that bP = bc2 . The form q is universal
over F , so the form q ⊥ h−bi is isotropic over F . Now over FP , we have
                                q ⊥ h−bP i = q ⊥ −bc2 ≃ q ⊥ h−bi.
Thus q ⊥ h−bP i is isotropic over FP , completing the proof.                                         

                                   4. The strong m-invariant
   In [HHK09], Harbater, Hartmann, and Krashen defined the strong u-invariant of a field in order
to calculate the u-invariant of certain semi-global fields (most notably to show u(Qp (x)) = 8). In
this section, we define the strong m-invariant of a field k in order to calculate the m-invariant of
certain semi-global fields, and show that it relates to the strong u-invariant of k in ways that are
analogous to how m(k) and u(k) relate to one another. We begin by recalling the definition of the
strong u-invariant of k.
Definition 4.1 ([HHK09]). Let k be a field. The strong u-invariant of k, denoted by us (k), is the
smallest real number n such that
     • u(k′ ) ≤ n for all finite field extensions k′ /k, and
     • u(K ′ ) ≤ 2n for all finitely generated field extensions K ′ /k of transcendence degree one.
If these u-invariants are arbitrarily large we say that us (k) = ∞.
   It is noted in [HHK09] that, since the u-invariant, if finite, is a positive integer, the strong u-
invariant always belongs to 12 N. Motivated by this definition, we introduce the following definition.
Definition 4.2. Let k be a field. The strong m-invariant of k, denoted by ms (k), is the largest
integer n such that
    • m(k′ ) ≥ n for all finite field extensions k′ /k, and
                                                    9
     • m(k′ (t)) ≥ 2n for all finite field extensions k′ /k.
If these m-invariants are arbitrarily large we say that ms (k) = ∞.
Examples 4.3.        (1) Let k be an algebraically closed field of characteristic 6= 2. Then ms (k) = 1.
     Indeed, the only finite extension of k is k itself, and we know that m(k) = 1. Moreover, the
     field k(t) is a C1 field, so u(k(t)) ≤ 2, and m(k(t)) ≥ 2 by Lemma 3.1. Therefore m(k(t)) = 2.
 (2) Let k be a finite field of odd characteristic. Then ms (k) = 2. Indeed, for any finite extension
     k′ /k, m(k′ ) = 2 since u(k′ ) = 2, and m(k′ (t)) = 4 by, e.g., [GVG92, Example 2.8].
 (3) Let L be any finitely generated transcendence degree one extension of an algebraically closed
     field k of characteristic 6= 2. Then ms (L) = us (L) = 2. Indeed, for any finite extension L′ /L
     we have m(L′ ) ≥ 2 by Lemma 3.1, and u(L′ ) ≤ 2 since L′ is a C1 field. Furthermore, by [BL14,
     Proposition 3.4], m(L′ (t)) ≥ 4, hence ms (L) = 2. For any finitely generated transcendence
     degree one extension E of L, E is a C2 field, so u(E) ≤ 4. Therefore us (L) = 2.
   The main goal of this section is to prove Theorem 4.10: for a complete discretely valued field K
with residue field k of characteristic 6= 2 satisfying ms (k) = us (k), we have ms (K) = 2ms (k). This
is an analogue of [HHK09, Theorem 4.10], which states that us (K) = 2us (k). With this goal in
mind, we make two observations about the differences between the definitions of us (k) and ms (k).
   In the definition of ms (k), we are asking for the largest integer n that gives lower bounds on
the m-invariants of certain field extensions of k. This is the opposite of us (k), which is asking for
the smallest n that gives upper bounds on the u-invariants of certain field extensions of k. This
difference is due to the fact that finding upper bounds for m(k) (respectively lower bounds for u(k))
is “easy”, while finding lower bounds for m(k) (respectively upper bounds for u(k)) is challenging.
The next key difference between ms (k) and us (k) is in the second requirement of the definitions.
In the definition of us (k), we consider all finitely generated field extensions K ′ /k of transcendence
degree one, whereas in the definition of ms (k), we consider only those transcendence degree one
extensions of k of the form K ′ = k′ (t) for some k′ /k finite. This is because the m-invariant does
not behave as uniformly as the u-invariant over arbitrary finitely generated field extensions of
transcendence degree one, as the following example illustrates.
Example 4.4. Let F = Qp (x) for an odd prime p. We will see inCorollary 4.12 thatm(Qp (x)) = 8.
                                                                          p
Consider the quadratic extension of F given by F ′ = Qp (x)                 x(1 − x)(x − p) . In [CPS12,

in F ′ , but is a square in Fv′ for every discrete valuation v on F ′ . Therefore there is a two-dimensional
counterexample to the local-global principle for isotropy of quadratic forms over F ′ with respect

reduction graph of a regular model of F ′ is not a tree, hence m(F ′ ) = 2 by Lemma 3.6. If the second

                           6. Semi-global fields over n-local fields
   Let k be a field and let n ≥ 1 be a positive integer. Recall that a complete discretely valued field K
is called an n-local field over k if K fits into a chain of fields K = Kn , Kn−1 , . . . , K1 , K0 = k, where,
for i = 1, . . . , n, Ki is a complete discretely valued field with residue field Ki−1 . When we refer to the
residue field of K, we mean the field Kn−1 . In this section, we define the layer (see Definition 6.6)
                                                      21
of a semi-global field F over such a field K in order to calculate m(F ). Throughout most of this
section we will assume that the field k has characteristic 6= 2 and satisfies ms (k) = us (k) < ∞. This
is a rather mild condition that is satisfied by, e.g., algebraically closed fields, finite fields, n-local
fields over finite fields, and finitely generated transcendence degree one extensions of algebraically
closed fields (see Examples 4.3(3)).
   Let k be a field and for any integer n ≥ 2 let Kn be an n-local field over k with valuation
ring Tn whose residue field Kn−1 has valuation ring Tn−1 . Let F be a semi-global field over Kn ,
and let X be a regular model of F over Tn with reduction graph ΓX and closed fiber X. Let
X1 , . . . , Xs be the irreducible components of X, and for 1 ≤ i ≤ s let ηi be the generic point of Xi .
For each i = 1, . . . , s the field κ(ηi ) is a finitely generated transcendence degree one extension of
the (n − 1)-local field Kn−1 over k, hence κ(ηi ) is a semi-global field over Kn−1 . So we can find a
regular model Yi of κ(ηi ) over Tn−1 . Each curve Yi has a reduction graph ΓYi and a closed fiber Yi
with irreducible components, etc. The layer of F captures the first step in this process in which we
find a reduction graph that is not a tree.
   To define the layer of a semi-global field, we use rooted trees. Recall that a rooted tree is a tree in
which one of the vertices has been designated the root. For each vertex v in a rooted tree, there is a
unique path from the root to v, and the level of v is the number of vertices in the path from the root
to v, including both v and the root. For example, the root of a rooted tree has level 1. A child of a
vertex v in a rooted tree is a vertex w connected to v by an edge such that level(w) = level(v) + 1.
We call v a parent of w. In a rooted tree, the root is the only vertex without a parent; all other
vertices have a unique parent.
   Given a semi-global field F over an n-local field over a field k, we will now inductively define a
procedure to associate to F a rooted tree τF whose root vertex is labeled by F and whose vertices
are each colored either black or white.

Definition 6.1. Let k be any field, let K1 be any 1-local field over k, and let F1 be a semi-global
field over K1 . We let τF1 be the rooted tree with one vertex, vF1 . If the reduction graph of a regular
model of F1 is a tree, then color vF1 white, and if the reduction graph of a regular model of F1 is
not a tree, then color vF1 black.
   Now for some integer n ≥ 1 assume we have defined a procedure to associate to any semi-global
field E over any n-local field over k a rooted tree τE with root vertex vE and whose vertices are each
colored either black or white. Let Kn+1 be an (n + 1)-local field over k with residue field Kn , and
let Fn+1 be a semi-global field over Kn+1 . Let X be a regular model of Fn+1 with closed fiber X
and reduction graph Γ. Let X1 , . . . , Xs be the irreducible components of X, and for 1 ≤ i ≤ s let ηi
be the generic point of Xi . For each 1 ≤ i ≤ s the field κ(ηi ) is a semi-global field over Kn , so by
the induction hypothesis we can associate to κ(ηi ) a rooted tree τκ(ηi ) with root vertex vκ(ηi ) . We
then let τFn+1 be the rooted tree with root vertex vFn+1 obtained by adding an edge to each τκ(ηi )
connecting the root vκ(ηi ) of τκ(ηi ) to the vertex vFn+1 so that vκ(ηi ) is a child of vFn+1 . Color vFn+1
white if Γ is a tree, and color vFn+1 black if Γ is not a tree. For all other vertices in τFn+1 belonging
to τκ(ηi ) for some i ∈ {1, . . . , s} we retain the color of that vertex as a vertex in τκ(ηi ) .

Remark 6.2. Let F be a semi-global field over an n-local field K over a field k for some integer
n ≥ 1. Then a regular model of F always exists, but this model is not unique. However, if X1 , X2
are regular models of F with reduction graphs Γ1 , Γ2 , respectively, then Γ1 is a tree if and only
if Γ2 is a tree [HHK15a, Corollary 7.8]. So if n = 1, then the rooted tree τF associated to F via
Definition 6.1 is unique, and if n ≥ 2, then τF is not unique and depends on our choice of regular
model. We will call any rooted tree associated to F via Definition 6.1 a rooted component tree of F .
