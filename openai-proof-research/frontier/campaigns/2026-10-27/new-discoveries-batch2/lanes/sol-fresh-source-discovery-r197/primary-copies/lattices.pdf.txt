                                               Quadratic Lattices over Polynomial Rings
                                                                            Yong HU and Jing LIU

                                                                                       Abstract
                                                     Let k be a field of characteristic ̸= 2. Under suitable assumptions on the di-
                                                 mensions of anisotropic quadratic forms over k and k(x), we show that a definite
                                                 integral quadratic form over the polynomial ring k[x] is universal if the form rep-
                                                 resents all scalars from k and all scalar multiples of x. The coefficient field k in our
                                                 main result can be a finite field, a non-dyadic higher local field, a global function
                                                 field, or a one-variable function field over C or C(t). We have a finite universality
                                                 criterion set over k[x] for some coefficient fields k, including some cases where k
                                                 has infinitely many square classes (e.g., k = C(t)).

                                           1     Introduction
                                           For a quadratic form f = f (x1 , · · · , xn ) in n variables over a ring R and an element
                                           α ∈ R, we say that f represents α (over R) if there exist a1 , · · · , an ∈ R such that
                                           f (a1 , · · · , an ) = α.
                                               A positive definite quadratic form over the integers is called universal if it represents
                                           all positive integers. For example, the four-square theorem of Lagrange states that the
                                           form x2 + y 2 + z 2 + w2 is universal. The famous 15-theorem of Conway and Schneeberger
                                           [Con00, Bha00] identifies the set {1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 10, 14, 15} as a universality criterion
                                           set for positive definite quadratic forms with integer Gram matrix over Z, in the sense
                                           that any such form which represents all elements in that set is universal. A theorem of
                                           Bhargava and Hanke [BH11], known as the 290-theorem, extends the 15-theorem to cover
                                           the case of integral quadratic forms that may have half-integers in their Gram matrices.
                                           These two theorems have found a large number of applications, demonstrating that it
                                           is of both theoretical and computational importance to have convenient universality
                                           criterion sets at our disposal.
                                               Now let us consider the polynomial ring k[x] with coefficients in a field k. We say
                                           that a quadratic form over k[x] is universal if it represents all elements of the ring k[x].
                                           Thus, a universality criterion set for integral quadratic forms over k[x] can be defined


                                                                                            1
as a subset of k[x] \ {0} such that any integral quadratic form representing all elements
of that set is universal.
    Gerstein [Ger04] pioneered the study of universal quadratic forms over polynomial
rings. He defined a quadratic form over k[x] to be definite if the form remains anisotropic
over the field k((x−1 )) (which is the completion of the rational function field k(x) with
respect to the negative-degree valuation). As a function field analogue of the 15-theorem
and 290-theorem, Gerstein’s 4-conjecture speculated that {1, δ, x, δx} is a universality
criterion set for definite quadratic forms over k[x], when k is a finite field of odd char-
acteristic and δ denotes a non-square in k. This conjecture has been confirmed with
different methods in [KWX08] and [CD05], and also in the thesis [Bur06].
    Motivated by the above results, the current work aims to address the problem of
finding universality criterion sets for definite quadratic forms over a polynomial ring
k[x] for more general coefficient fields k. Under suitable assumptions on k, we show that
it will still be sufficient to represent all scalars from k and all scalar multiples of x for
a definite integral quadratic form over k[x] to be universal, just as in the case with k
finite.
    Similar to the cases already discussed above, a quadratic form over any field k is
called universal if it represents all elements of k ∗ = k \ {0}, and a universality criterion
set for quadratic forms over k is a subset of k ∗ that is sufficient to test the universality
of quadratic forms over k.
    The u-invariant u(k) of a field k is defined as the supremum of the dimensions of
anisotropic quadratic forms over k (cf. [Lam05, Definition XI.6.1]). We say that k has
UUD (short for unique universality dimension) if its u-invariant u(k) is finite and every
anisotropic universal quadratic form over k is of dimension u(k).

Theorem 1.1. Let k be a field of characteristic ̸= 2. Assume that k has UUD and that
u(k(x)) = 2u(k). Then for any universality criterion set S for quadratic forms over k,
the set
                             S ∪ Sx = S ∪ {ax | a ∈ S}
is a universality criterion set for definite integral quadratic forms over the polynomial
ring k[x].

    Clearly, we can take S to be any complete system of representatives of the group
k /k ∗2 of square classes. Therefore, Theorem 1.1 gives a finite universality criterion set
 ∗

over k[x] if k ∗ /k ∗2 is finite. In some examples with k ∗ /k ∗2 infinite, we can still find a
finite set S as universality criterion set over k. See Remark 4.5.
    As we will explain in Example 4.3, typical examples of fields k satisfying the hy-
potheses in Theorem 1.1 include finite fields, non-archimedean local fields, global func-
tion fields, non-dyadic higher local fields, and one-variable function fields over C, C(t)
or C((t)). In particular, the present work gives yet another proof of the aforementioned
4-conjecture of Gerstein, previously proved in [CD05, Bur06, KWX08]. Also, for any
odd prime p, with δ denoting a non-square unit in the field Qp of p-adic numbers, one
obtains {1, δ, p, δp, x, δx, px, δpx} as a universality criterion set for Qp [x]. This might
analogously be referred to as the 8-theorem.

Notation and Conventions. Throughout what follows, let k be a field of characteristic
different from 2.
    We will use the geometric language of quadratic spaces and lattices, standard no-
tation and terminology about which will be adopted from [Lam05] and [O’M00]. In
particular, a quadratic space over k is a pair (V, φ) consisting of a finite dimensional
k-vector space V and a quadratic form φ : V → k. All the quadratic spaces considered
in this paper are assumed to be non-degenerate unless otherwise stated.
    A scalar a ∈ k is said to be represented by (V, φ) if there exists a nonzero vector
v ∈ V with φ(v) = a. A quadratic space over k is called universal if it represents every
element in k ∗ . On the other hand, a quadratic space is called isotropic over k if it
represents zero, and anisotropic otherwise.

3     Consequences of the UUD Property
Recall that the u-invariant u(k) of k is defined as the supremum of the dimensions
of anisotropic quadratic spaces over k (cf. [Lam05, Definition XI.6.1]). It is also the
minimum of all integers n ⩾ 1 such that all n-dimensional quadratic spaces over k are
universal.
    We say that the field k has the Unique Universality Dimension (UUD) property,
or simply that k has UUD, if u(k) is finite and if the dimension of every anisotropic
universal quadratic space over k equals u(k).
Example 3.1. If u(k) ⩽ 4, then k has UUD.
    Indeed, we have u(k) ∈ {1, 2, 4} ([Lam05, Prop. XI.6.8]). The result is clear if
u(k) ⩽ 2. If u(k) = 4, this follows from the well known fact that a ternary form of the
type ⟨1, a, b⟩ is anisotropic if and only if the quaternary form ⟨1, a, b, ab⟩ is anisotropic.
    We see therefore that finite fields, non-archimedean local fields, global fields without
real places, the rational function fields C(t), C(t1 , t2 ), C((t1 ))(t2 ), and the Laurent series
fields C((t)), C((t1 ))((t2 )), C(t1 )((t2 )) and C((t1 , t2 )) all have the UUD property.
Proposition 3.2. Let K be a complete discrete valuation field with residue field k (of
characteristic ̸= 2). Then K has UUD if and only if k has UUD.
Proof. We have u(K) = 2u(k) (cf. [Lam05, Example XI.6.2 (7)]), so we may assume
that u(k) is finite.
    Let φ̄ be an anisotropic universal quadratic form of dimension d over k. Choose a
diagonalization φ̄ = ⟨ā1 , · · · ād ⟩ and lift it to a form φ = ⟨a1 , · · · , ad ⟩, where each ai is
a unit in the valuation ring OK that lifts āi . Then, by Springer’s theorem (cf. [Lam05,
Prop. VI.1.9]), the form f := φ ⊥ πφ, where π is a uniformizer of K, is anisotropic over
K. On the other hand, for all units u in OK , φ represents u and πφ represents uπ by
Lemma 2.1. It follows that f is universal over K. Thus, if K has UUD, then we must
have 2d = u(K) = 2u(k), i.e., d = u(k). This shows that k has UUD if K does.
    Conversely, let q be an anisotropic universal form over K. We can assume that
q = ψ ⊥ πϕ, where ψ and ϕ are diagonal forms whose diagonal entries are all units in
OK . By Springer’s theorem, the forms ψ and ϕ are both anisotropic over K, and hence
the residual forms ψ̄ and ϕ̄ are anisotropic over k by Lemma 2.1. Moreover, for any
δ̄ ∈ k ∗ , letting δ ∈ OK be a unit lifting δ̄, another application of Springer’s theorem
together with Lemma 2.1 shows that q represents δ over K if and only if ψ̄ represents
δ̄ over k; and q represents πδ if and only if ϕ̄ represents δ̄ over k. Since q is universal
over K, it follows that both ψ̄ and ϕ̄ are universal over k. So, if k has UUD, then
dim ψ = dim ϕ = dim ψ̄ = dim ϕ̄ = u(k), whence dim q = 2u(k) = u(K). This proves
the sufficiency part of the proposition.

 m(k) = inf{dim(φ) | φ an anisotropic universal quadratic form over k} ∈ N ∪ {+∞},
then k has UUD if and only if m(k) = u(k) < +∞. So Proposition 3.2 can also be
deduced directly from [Cas26, Cor. 2.8].
   According to [Cas26, Cor. 2.4], if k has UUD, then u(k) is a power of 2.

4     Proof of Main Theorem, Examples and Remarks
We can now give a proof of our main result, Theorem 1.1.
    Let us assume that the field k (of characteristic ̸= 2) has UUD and that u(k(x)) =
2u(k). Let S ⊆ k ∗ be a universality criterion set for quadratic forms over k. Then The-
orem 1.1 asserts that S ∪ Sx is a universality criterion set for definite integral quadratic
lattices over the polynomial ring k[x]; that is, if a definite integral quadratic k[x]-lattice
represents all elements in S ∪ Sx, then the lattice represents all nonzero elements of k[x].
Proof of Theorem 1.1. Let L be a definite integral quadratic k[x]-lattice that represents
S ∪ Sx. Then dim(L) = 2u(k), and all the entries of the Gram matrix of L with respect
to a reduced basis have degree 0 or 1, by Corollary 3.5 (5). The generalized Cassels–
Pfister theorem [Cla12, Thm. 10] then tells us that (the integral quadratic form over k[x]
corresponding to) L is an ADC form (cf. [Cla12, p. 144]), in the sense that it represents
over the ring k[x] any element of k[x] that it represents over the field k(x). But since
dim(L) = u(k(x)), the underlying quadratic space of L is universal over k(x); therefore
L is universal as a quadratic k[x]-lattice.
Proposition 4.1. Let k be a field (of characteristic ̸= 2) such that for every finitely
generated field extension ℓ/k of transcendence degree d ⩽ 1, one has u(ℓ) ⩽ 2d u(k). Let
K be a complete discrete valuation field with residue field k.

one has u(L) ⩽ 2d u(K). In particular, u(K(x)) = 2u(K).

Proof. This is an easy consequence of [HHK09, Thm. 4.10].

Remark 4.2. Let k0 be a field of characteristic ̸= 2 and let k/k0 be a finitely generated
field extension of transcendence degree d. Then u(k) = 2d u(k0 ) if k0 is an algebraically
closed field, a finite field, or a non-archimedean local field. In particular, this implies
that k satisfies the hypothesis of Proposition 4.1.
    Indeed, if k0 is a non-archimedean local field of characteristic 0 (including the dyadic
case), this is proved in [Lee13, Thm. 3.4].
    In the other cases, one can use the Tsen–Lang–Nagata theory of Ci -fields (see e.g.,
[Sch85, § 2.15]) to show u(k) ⩽ 2d u(k0 ). The reverse inequality is also well-known to the
experts; it follows for example from [BGVG14, Prop. 5.2].

orem 1.1 can be any of the following fields:

 (1) A finitely generated extension of transcendence degree ⩽ 2 over an algebraically

 (2) A finitely generated extension of transcendence degree ⩽ 1 over C((t)), where C is

 (3) A finite field or a global function field of characteristic ̸= 2.

 (4) A non-archimedean local field of characteristic ̸= 2.

 (5) A complete discrete valuation field whose residue field is of the type as in the
     previous cases.

    Let m ⩾ 1 be an integer. As is customary, by an m-local field we mean a field ℓ
such that there is a sequence of fields ℓ1 , . . . , ℓm with ℓ1 a non-archimedean local field
in the usual sense, ℓm = ℓ, and ℓi a complete discrete valuation field with residue field
ℓi−1 for 2 ⩽ i ⩽ m. By Example 4.3 (5), in Theorem 1.1 we can take k to be an m-local
field ℓ with char(ℓ1 ) ̸= 2. In fact, by [Cas26, Cor. 4.8 and Thm. 4.10], we can also take
k = ℓ(t) for an m-local field ℓ with char(ℓ1 ) ̸= 2.
    It would be interesting to know whether the following question has a positive answer:

Question 4.4. For n ⩾ 3, do the fields

          C(x1 , · · · , xn ) ,   F(x1 , · · · , xn−1 ) with F finite of odd characteristic

have UUD ?

Remark 4.5. Consider the group k ∗ /k ∗2 of nonzero square classes in k. Any complete
system of representatives for k ∗ /k ∗2 is trivially a universality criterion set for quadratic
forms over k. So we have a finite criterion set for forms over k whenever this group of
square classes is finite.

                                                    9
    Notice that for some fields k with k ∗ /k ∗2 infinite, it may still be possible to find such
a finite criterion set. For example, if u(k) = 2, then for any two elements α, β ∈ k ∗ not
in the same square class, the set S = {α, β} is a universality criterion set for forms over
k.
    More generally, if κ is a field of characteristic ̸= 2 that admits a finite universality cri-
terion set S0 , then as we have said in the proof of Lemma 3.4 (2), S0 ∪S0 t is a finite univer-
sality criterion set for κ((t)). Therefore, if in Theorem 1.1 we take k = C(t)((t1 )) · · · ((tn ))
for some n ∈ N, then k ∗ /k ∗2 is infinite, but there still exists a finite criterion set S for
universal quadratic forms over k, and hence S ∪ Sx is a finite universality criterion set
for definite quadratic forms over k[x].

Remark 4.6. Let k be a non-archimedean local field of residue characteristic p, and let
S be a complete system of representatives for the square classes in k ∗ . We claim that S
is a minimal universality criterion set for quadratic forms over k.
    Indeed, we can choose a ternary anisotropic form ϕ over k. For any u ∈ S, let
φ = (−u det(ϕ)).ϕ be the scalar multiple of ϕ by −u det(ϕ). Then det(φ) = −u, and it
follows from [O’M00, 63:21] that φ represents all the elements in S \ {u} but does not
represent u.
    We leave it to the reader to check that the above statement can be generalized to
higher local fields.
