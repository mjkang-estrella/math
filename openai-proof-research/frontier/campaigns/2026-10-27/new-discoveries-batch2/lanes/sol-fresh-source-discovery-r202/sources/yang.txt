Additive bases and the zero-sum invariant \mathsf{N}(G) for groups C_{p}\oplus C_{np}Thanks: This work was supported by
National Natural Science Foundation of China (Grant No. 12071344).



Wenkai Yang

Address: Center for Combinatorics, Nankai University, Tianjin 300071, P.R. China

Email address: 3016210052@tju.edu.cn; 9820250116@nankai.edu.cn



Abstract.
    

Let G be a finite abelian group, and let c_{0}(G) be the least integer
such that every regular sequence over G of at least this length is an
additive basis. Let \mathsf{N}(G) be the least integer l such that every
weak-regular sequence S over G with |S|\geq l has a nonempty zero-sum
subsequence T satisfying \mathsf{v}_{g}(T)=\mathsf{v}_{g}(S) for some g\mid S.
We obtain a common upper bound for c_{0}(C_{q}\oplus C_{nq}) for groups
of odd order, where q is prime, and prove \mathsf{N}(G)\geq r(n_{r}-1)+1 when
G=C_{n_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n_{r}} with 1<n_{1}\mid\cdots\mid n_{r}.
If n\geq 1 and p\geq 3 is the smallest prime divisor of np, we obtain
\mathsf{N}(C_{p}\oplus C_{np^{2}})=2np^{2}-1 and give upper bounds for
\mathsf{N}(C_{p}\oplus C_{np}) when p\nmid n.
We also determine \mathsf{N}(C_{n}) for two infinite families of cyclic
groups arising in earlier work.
  



Key words and phrases: finite abelian group, additive basis, regular sequence, zero-sum subsequence


2020 Mathematics Subject ClassificationPrimary 11B75; Secondary 11A07, 20K01




1. Introduction and main results




Let G be a finite abelian group, with additive notation, and let S be
a sequence over G. Denote by \sum(S) the set of sums of nonempty
subsequences of S. The sequence S is an additive basis of G if
\sum(S)=G. For a subgroup H\leq G, let S_{H} be the subsequence
consisting of all terms of S in H. We call S regular over G if
|S_{H}|\leq|H|-1 for every proper subgroup H<G.
Let c_{0}(G) be the least integer l such that every regular sequence
over G of length at least l is an additive basis.
The problem of determining c_{0}(G) was proposed by Olson;
see [2, 6].




Let p be the smallest prime divisor of |G|, and let




m(G)=\begin{cases}|G|,&G\text{ is cyclic},\\
2p-1,&G=C_{p}^{2},\\
np+2p-3,&G=C_{p}\oplus C_{np},\ n\geq 2,\\
|G|/p+p-2,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}



The inequality c_{0}(G)\geq m(G) is known for every finite abelian group
[6, Lemma 2.3]. Equality has been established for many
groups of rank at least three; see [2, 6].
For a group G=C_{n_{1}}\oplus C_{n_{2}} with 1<n_{1}\mid n_{2},
the known cases include the following:


(1) 



|G| is even, or G is a p-group
[2, 10];


(2) 



G=C_{3}\oplus C_{3q}, where q\geq 5 is prime
[11];


(3) 



p\geq 3, n_{1}\geq 2p and |G|\geq 72p^{6}
[5];


(4) 



p\geq 11 and n_{1}\geq p^{2}
[12].



Qu, Li, Wang and Zhao also proved that c_{0}(G)\leq 2|G|/5+1 for
every group of rank two with p\geq 11
[12, Lemma 7].
For G=C_{p}\oplus C_{np} with n\geq 2, Qu and Han conjectured
that c_{0}(G)=np+2p-3 [10, Concluding Remark].




We also study the invariant \mathsf{N}(G) introduced by Gao et al.
[3]. A sequence S over G is weak-regular if
\mathsf{v}_{g}(S)\leq\ord(g) for every g\in G, where \mathsf{v}_{g}(S)
denotes the multiplicity of g in S.
The invariant \mathsf{N}(G) is the least integer l such that every
weak-regular sequence S over G with |S|\geq l has a nonempty
zero-sum subsequence T satisfying \mathsf{v}_{g}(T)=\mathsf{v}_{g}(S)
for some g\mid S. Equivalently, \supp(ST^{-1})\neq\supp(S).
Gao et al. determined
\mathsf{N}(C_{p}^{r})=r(p-1)+1 for p\leq 3 and
\mathsf{N}(C_{p}^{2})=4p-2\lceil 2\sqrt{p+1}\rceil+1 for p\geq 3
[4, Theorem 1.3].
For cyclic groups, Jiang and the author proved




2n-\lceil 2\sqrt{n+1}\rceil\leq\mathsf{N}(C_{n})\leq 2n-\lceil 2\sqrt{n+1}\rceil+1\qquad(n\geq 3),



with each equality holding for infinitely many n
[8, Theorem 1.1].




Our bounds for additive bases follow from the estimate




c_{0}(C_{q}\oplus C_{nq})\leq\max\left\{nq+2q^{2}-q-1,\,\frac{nq(q+1)}{p}-q+p-2\right\},



where q is prime, n\geq 2, and p\geq 3 is the smallest prime
divisor of nq; see Theorem 3.5.
This estimate applies whether or not q\mid n.
The first two theorems give consequences for the two cases separately.



Theorem 1.1.



Let q be prime and let G=C_{q}\oplus C_{nq^{2}}, where n\geq 1 is not a power of q. If p\geq 3 is the smallest prime dividing |G|, then




\displaystyle c_{0}(G)\leq\max\{\frac{nq^{2}(q+1)}{p},nq^{2}+f(q)-1\},



where f(q)=4q(q-1)+1 for q\geq 7, f(q)=2q^{2}+3 for q\leq 5.




Theorem 1.2.



Let q be prime and let G=C_{q}\oplus C_{nq}, where n\geq 1 and q\nmid n. If p\geq 3 is the smallest prime dividing |G|, then







\displaystyle c_{0}(G)\leq\max\{(2q-1)(n+q+1),\frac{nq^{2}}{p}+2\frac{nq}{p}\}.







For \mathsf{N}(G), we prove the lower bound r(n_{r}-1)+1.
The other term in the following theorem is the direct-sum bound of
Gao et al. [4, Theorem 1.1(2)].



Theorem 1.3.



Let G=C_{n_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n_{r}}, where
1<n_{1}\mid\cdots\mid n_{r}. Then




\mathsf{N}(G)\geq\max\left\{r(n_{r}-1)+1,\ \sum_{i=1}^{r}\bigl(\mathsf{N}(C_{n_{i}})-1\bigr)+1\right\}.






Theorem 1.4.



Let G=C_{p}\oplus C_{np}, where n\geq 2 and p\geq 3 is the
smallest prime dividing |G|. Then




2np-1\leq\mathsf{N}(G)\leq\begin{cases}2np-1,&p\mid n,\\
5np-4,&p\geq 5,\ p\nmid n,\\
4np-3,&p=3,\ 3\nmid n.\end{cases}



In particular, \mathsf{N}(C_{p}\oplus C_{np^{2}})=2np^{2}-1
whenever n\geq 1 and p\geq 3 is the smallest prime dividing the group order.
The two upper bounds for p\nmid n also hold when n=1.





Section 2 gives the notation and preliminary lemmas.
Section 3 proves Theorems 1.1 and 1.2.
Section 4 proves Theorems 1.3 and 1.4.
The final section discusses the remaining rank-two cases and two cyclic families.





2. Notation and preliminaries




Our notation for sequences follows the standard conventions of zero-sum
theory; see [1, 7].
A sequence over G is finite and unordered, and repetitions are allowed.
For a sequence S=g_{1}\cdots g_{l}, its length is |S|=l, its sum is
\sigma(S)=g_{1}+\cdots+g_{l}, and its support is
\supp(S)=\{g\in G:\mathsf{v}_{g}(S)>0\}.
The relation T\mid S means that T is a subsequence of S;
then ST^{-1} is the subsequence obtained by deleting T from S.
The empty sequence is denoted by 1 and has sum 0.
Two subsequences T_{1},T_{2} of S are disjoint if T_{1}T_{2}\mid S.
A homomorphism \varphi:G\to G_{1} induces the sequence
\varphi(S)=\varphi(g_{1})\cdots\varphi(g_{l}) over G_{1}.




Let \sum_{0}(S)=\{0\}\cup\sum(S).
Thus \sum_{0}(S) includes the sum of the empty subsequence, whereas
\sum(S) consists of sums of nonempty subsequences.
A nonempty sequence is a minimal zero-sum sequence if it has sum 0
and no proper nonempty zero-sum subsequence.
The Davenport constant \mathsf{D}(G) is the least integer l such that
every sequence over G of length at least l has a nonempty zero-sum
subsequence. We use
\mathsf{D}(C_{n_{1}}\oplus C_{n_{2}})=n_{1}+n_{2}-1 for 1\leq n_{1}\mid n_{2}
[7, Theorem 5.8.3].
Let \mathbb{N} denote the positive integers. We use the usual floor and
ceiling functions \lfloor x\rfloor and \lceil x\rceil.




For each subset A of G, denote by \st(A)=\{g\in G:g+A=A\} the stabilizer of A in G. Then \st(A) is the maximal subgroup H of G such that H+A=A. We use the following form of Kneser’s theorem; see [9, Theorem 4.4].



Lemma 2.1 (Kneser).



Let A_{1},...,A_{n} be nonempty finite subsets of an
abelian group G, and H=\st(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{n}) be the stabilizer. Then




|A_{1}+\cdots+A_{n}|\geq|A_{1}+H|+\cdots+|A_{n}+H|-(n-1)|H|.






Lemma 2.2 (Cauchy–Davenport).



Let A_{1},...,A_{n} be nonempty finite subsets of an
abelian group G=C_{p}, where p is prime. Then




|A_{1}+\cdots+A_{n}|\geq\min\{p,\,|A_{1}|+\cdots+|A_{n}|-(n-1)\}.






Lemma 2.3.



For every finite abelian group G,







m(G)\leq c_{0}(G)\leq|G|.







See [6, Lemma 2.3] for the lower bound and
[2, Lemma 2.4] for the upper bound.




Qu and Han determined c_{0}(G) for G=C_{p}\oplus C_{p^{n}} as follows.



Lemma 2.4 ([10]).



Let G=C_{p}\oplus C_{p^{n}}, where p is prime and n\geq 2. Then




c_{0}(G)=p^{n}+2p-3.






Lemma 2.5 ([2, Lemma 2.3(1)]).



Let G be a finite abelian group, and let p be the smallest prime dividing |G|. Let S be a regular sequence over G of length |S|\geq\max\{|G|/p+p-2,~\mathsf{D}(G)\}. If \sum(S)\neq G, then \st(\sum(S))=\{0\}.





We use the cyclic bound




\mathsf{N}(C_{n})\leq 2n-\lceil 2\sqrt{n+1}\rceil+1\qquad(n\geq 3)



from [8, Theorem 1.1], and the value
\mathsf{N}(C_{p}^{2})=4p-2\lceil 2\sqrt{p+1}\rceil+1 for p\geq 3
from [4, Theorem 1.3(3)].
Also \mathsf{N}(C_{2})=2.



Lemma 2.6.



Let G=C_{p}\oplus C_{np}, where p\geq 3 is the smallest prime divisor
of |G|. For 1<m\mid n, let H\leq pG have order m, and let k=n/m.
Then




2np-1-\bigl(\mathsf{N}(H)-1+(m-1)kp\bigr)\geq\begin{cases}2kp-1,&p\mid k,\ m\neq n,\\
4kp-3,&p=3,\ p\nmid k,\ m\neq n,\\
5kp-4,&p\geq 5,\ p\nmid k,\ m\neq n,\\
\mathsf{N}(C_{p}^{2}),&m=n.\end{cases}






Proof.



Since m\geq p\geq 3, the cyclic bound gives \mathsf{N}(H)\leq 2m-3.
Therefore




\displaystyle 2np-1-\bigl(\mathsf{N}(H)-1+(m-1)kp\bigr)
\displaystyle=kp(m+1)-\mathsf{N}(H)




\displaystyle\geq kp(m+1)-2m+3.



For m<n, subtracting 2kp-1 from the last expression gives
(kp-2)(m-1)+2\geq 0.
When p=3, subtracting 4kp-3 gives (m-3)(kp-2)\geq 0.
When p\geq 5, subtracting 5kp-4 gives
(m-4)kp-2m+7\geq 3m-13>0.
These prove the first three estimates.
If m=n, then k=1, and the left side is at least




(p-2)n+p+3\geq p^{2}-p+3>4p-7\geq\mathsf{N}(C_{p}^{2}).



The strict inequality follows from
p^{2}-5p+10=(p-2)(p-3)+4>0.
∎




Lemma 2.7.



Let S be a weak-regular sequence over G=C_{n}, where n\geq 2 and |S|\geq n.
Then either \sum(S)=G, or there is a zero-sum subsequence T\mid S
such that \mathsf{v}_{g}(T)=\mathsf{v}_{g}(S)\geq 1 for some g\mid S.




Proof.



If \mathsf{v}_{g}(S)=\ord(g) for some g, take T=g^{\ord(g)}.
Otherwise 0\notin\supp(S), and we may assume \sum(S)\neq G.
Since |S|\geq\mathsf{D}(G), \sum(S)=\sum_{0}(S).
Let H=\st(\sum_{0}(S)) and m=|H|.
If m=1, Kneser’s theorem gives |\sum_{0}(S)|\geq|S|+1>n.
Thus m>1, and \sum_{0}(S)\neq G gives m<n.
Since n=dm for some integer d\geq 2, Kneser’s theorem also gives




(|S|-|S_{H}|+1)m\leq\bigl|\sum_{0}(S)\bigr|\leq n-m,\qquad|S_{H}|\geq n-d+2\geq 2m\geq\mathsf{N}(H).



The middle inequality is equivalent to (d-2)(m-1)\geq 0.
Apply the definition of \mathsf{N}(H) to the weak-regular sequence S_{H};
all occurrences in S of the resulting element g\in H belong to S_{H}.
∎




Lemma 2.8.



Let S,S^{\prime} be sequences over a finite abelian group G, and let
H\leq G satisfy H\subseteq\sum_{0}(S).
Suppose that T_{1}\mid S^{\prime} satisfies \sigma(T_{1})\in H and
\mathsf{v}_{g}(T_{1})=\mathsf{v}_{g}(S^{\prime})\geq 1 for some
g\in\supp(S^{\prime})\setminus\supp(S).
Then SS^{\prime} has a zero-sum subsequence T with
\mathsf{v}_{g}(T)=\mathsf{v}_{g}(SS^{\prime}).




Proof.



Choose T_{2}\mid S with \sigma(T_{2})=-\sigma(T_{1}) and take T=T_{1}T_{2}.
Since g\notin\supp(S), its multiplicity in T equals that in SS^{\prime}.
∎






3. Proof of Theorems 1.1 and 1.2



Lemma 3.1.



Let S be a regular sequence over a finite abelian group G. Then




|\sum_{0}(S)|\geq\min\{|G|,|S|+1\}.



In particular, \sum_{0}(S)=G if |S|\geq|G|-1.




Proof.



Suppose that |\sum_{0}(S)|<\min\{|G|,|S|+1\}.
Let H=\st(\sum_{0}(S)) and m=|H|.
Then H<G and m\leq|S|, so Kneser’s theorem gives




|\sum_{0}(S)|\geq(|S|-|S_{H}|+1)m\geq(|S|-m+2)m\geq|S|+1,



a contradiction.
∎




Lemma 3.2.



Let K<H<G, where |K|\geq 2 and H/K\cong C_{q} for a prime q.
Let S be a sequence over H\setminus K that is regular over G.
If |S|\geq 2q-1, then there exists T\mid S such that
|T|\leq q and \sigma(T)\in K\setminus\{0\}.




Proof.



Take RR^{\prime}\mid S with |R|=q and |R^{\prime}|=q-1.
Since R is regular over H and |H|=q|K|\geq 2q,
Lemma 3.1 gives |\sum_{0}(R)|\geq q+1.
Thus there exist R_{1},R_{2}\mid R whose sums are distinct and belong to the same coset of K.
By Cauchy–Davenport, \sum_{0}(R^{\prime})+K=H.
Choose R_{3}\mid R^{\prime} such that \sigma(R_{1}R_{3}),\sigma(R_{2}R_{3})\in K.
Choose U\in\{R_{1}R_{3},R_{2}R_{3}\} with \sigma(U)\neq 0.
Factor U into subsequences whose images in H/K are minimal zero-sum sequences.
Each factor has length at most q, and at least one has nonzero sum, as required.
∎




Lemma 3.3.



Let K\leq G with |K|\geq 2 and G/K\cong C_{q}^{2} for a prime q.
If S is regular over G and




|S|\geq q|K|+2q^{2}-q-1,



then S=S_{K}T_{1}\cdots T_{k}R, where k=|K|-|S_{K}|,
|T_{i}|\leq q, \sigma(T_{i})\in K\setminus\{0\} and \sum_{0}(R)+K=G.




Proof.



Let \varphi:G\to G/K be the natural homomorphism and let k=|K|-|S_{K}|.
Starting with SS_{K}^{-1}, choose at each step a subgroup of order q containing the largest number of remaining images.
Before each of the first k steps, the remaining length is at least
|S|-|S_{K}|-q(k-1)\geq 2q^{2}-1, so the chosen subgroup contains at least 2q-1 terms.
Apply Lemma 3.2 in its inverse image to obtain T_{1},\ldots,T_{k}.
The remaining sequence R=S(S_{K}T_{1}\cdots T_{k})^{-1} has length
|R|\geq 2q^{2}-q-1.
Suppose that \sum_{0}(\varphi(R))\neq G/K.
Since |R|>(q+1)(q-2), some subgroup \bar{H} of order q contains at least q-1 terms of \varphi(R).
For H=\varphi^{-1}(\bar{H}), Cauchy–Davenport gives \sum_{0}(\varphi(R_{H}))=\bar{H}.
It also gives |RR_{H}^{-1}|\leq q-2, since otherwise \sum_{0}(\varphi(R))=G/K.
Thus |R_{H}|\geq 2q^{2}-2q+1.
Working backwards, suppose that T_{j+1},\ldots,T_{k} lie in H.
Before T_{j} was chosen, at most q-2+q=2q-2 terms lay outside H,
whereas at least 2q^{2}-2q+1 lay in H.
Distinct subgroups of order q in G/K meet only at zero,
and the remaining terms have nonzero images. Thus the subgroup containing
the largest number of remaining images is \bar{H}, forcing T_{j} to lie in H.
Hence all T_{i} lie in H, and |S_{H}|\geq|S|-q+2>|H|=q|K|, a contradiction.
∎




Lemma 3.4.



Let p be the smallest prime dividing |G|, let K<G with |K|\geq 2, and let l\geq 2 be an integer.
Suppose that S is regular over G and admits a decomposition
S=S_{K}T_{1}\cdots T_{k}R, where k=|K|-|S_{K}|,
|T_{i}|\leq l, \sigma(T_{i})=t_{i}\in K\setminus\{0\} and \sum_{0}(R)+K=G.
If




|S|\geq\frac{|G|}{p}+l\frac{|K|}{p}-l+p-2,



then \sum(S)=G.




Proof.



Let W=S_{K}t_{1}\cdots t_{k}. Since |W|=|K|\geq\mathsf{D}(K), we have 0\in\sum(S).
If W is regular over K, then \sum_{0}(W)=K and the conclusion follows.
Otherwise choose a minimal subgroup H_{1}<K with |W_{H_{1}}|\geq|H_{1}|=m.
Then H_{1}\neq\{0\} and W_{H_{1}} is regular over H_{1}.
Let r=m-|S_{H_{1}}|-1.
After reordering, S_{H_{1}}t_{1}\cdots t_{r} is regular over H_{1} of length m-1.
Its subsequence sums together with 0 therefore cover H_{1} by Lemma 3.1.
Let W_{0}=St_{1}\cdots t_{r}(T_{1}\cdots T_{r})^{-1} and H_{2}=\st(\sum_{0}(W_{0})).
Since the T_{i} are disjoint, replacing each selected occurrence of
t_{i} by T_{i} shows that \sum_{0}(W_{0})\subseteq\sum_{0}(S).
Together with 0\in\sum(S), this gives \sum_{0}(W_{0})\subseteq\sum(S).
The subsequence S_{H_{1}}t_{1}\cdots t_{r} of W_{0} has
\sum_{0}(S_{H_{1}}t_{1}\cdots t_{r})=H_{1}, so \sum_{0}(W_{0}) is a union
of cosets of H_{1}. Hence H_{1}\leq H_{2}.
We may assume H_{2}<G; hence p\leq|H_{2}|\leq|G|/p.
Since r\leq|K|/p-1, |W_{0}|\geq|S|-(l-1)r and
|(W_{0})_{H_{2}}|\leq|S_{H_{2}}|+r\leq|H_{2}|-1+r, Kneser’s theorem gives




|\sum_{0}(W_{0})|\geq(|S|-lr+2-|H_{2}|)|H_{2}|\geq(|G|/p+p-|H_{2}|)|H_{2}|\geq|G|.



The last inequality follows from (|H_{2}|-p)(|G|/p-|H_{2}|)\geq 0, and thus \sum(S)=G.
∎




Theorem 3.5.



Let G=C_{q}\oplus C_{nq}, where q is prime and n\geq 2,
and let p\geq 3 be the smallest prime dividing |G|. Then




c_{0}(G)\leq\max\left\{nq+2q^{2}-q-1,\,\frac{nq(q+1)}{p}-q+p-2\right\}.






Proof.



Let S be regular over G with length at least the stated bound.
For K=qG, we have |K|=n and G/K\cong C_{q}^{2}.
The first term of the bound allows us to apply Lemma 3.3.
The second term is the bound in Lemma 3.4 with l=q.
Hence \sum(S)=G.
∎




Proof of Theorem 1.1.



Apply Theorem 3.5 with nq in place of n. It gives




c_{0}(G)\leq\max\left\{nq^{2}+2q^{2}-q-1,\,\frac{nq^{2}(q+1)}{p}-q+p-2\right\}.



Since p\leq q, the second term is at most nq^{2}(q+1)/p.
For the first, f(q)-2q^{2}+q equals 2q^{2}-3q+1 when q\geq 7
and q+3 when q=3,5, and is positive in both cases.
This proves the stated bound.
∎




Proof of Theorem 1.2.



If n=1, then G=C_{q}^{2} and c_{0}(G)=2q-1, which is at most
(2q-1)(n+q+1).
Suppose that n\geq 2. Since p\geq 3 is the smallest prime dividing
|G|, Theorem 3.5 applies and gives




c_{0}(G)\leq\max\left\{nq+2q^{2}-q-1,\,\frac{nq(q+1)}{p}-q+p-2\right\}.



The first is at most (2q-1)(n+q+1), since subtracting
nq+2q^{2}-q-1 from that expression gives (q-1)n+2q>0.
For the second, p\leq q implies
nq(q+1)/p-q+p-2\leq nq(q+1)/p.
The latter is at most nq^{2}/p+2nq/p, because their difference is
nq/p>0.
Taking the maximum of these two comparisons proves the result.
∎






4. Proof of Theorems 1.3 and 1.4



Proof of Theorem 1.3.



Choose a basis (x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}) with \ord(x_{i})=n_{i} and let
g_{0}=x_{r}, g_{i}=x_{r}+x_{i} for 1\leq i<r.
All g_{i} have order n_{r}, so S=\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}g_{i}^{n_{r}-1} is weak-regular.
For a zero-sum subsequence T=\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}g_{i}^{a_{i}}, the coordinates give
a_{i}\equiv 0\pmod{n_{i}} for i\geq 1 and \sum_{i}a_{i}\equiv 0\pmod{n_{r}}.
Thus also a_{0}\equiv 0\pmod{n_{1}}, and none of the a_{i} equals n_{r}-1.
This proves \mathsf{N}(G)\geq r(n_{r}-1)+1.
For completeness, consider the direct-sum bound from
[4, Theorem 1.1(2)].
For each i, choose a weak-regular sequence over \langle x_{i}\rangle
of length \mathsf{N}(C_{n_{i}})-1 having no nonempty zero-sum subsequence
that contains all occurrences of any of its elements.
None contains 0, and the direct factors intersect only at 0, so their
product in G is weak-regular.
In a direct sum, a sequence has sum zero if and only if its sum in every
coordinate is zero. A zero-sum subsequence containing all occurrences of
some element would therefore have the same property in the corresponding
factor, contrary to the choice of the sequences. The product has length
\sum_{i=1}^{r}(\mathsf{N}(C_{n_{i}})-1), giving the second bound.
∎





If rn_{r}\geq 2\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}, the first lower bound dominates the second,
since \mathsf{N}(C_{n_{i}})-1\leq 2n_{i}-2.



Lemma 4.1.



Let G=C_{p}\oplus C_{np}, where n\geq 2 and p\geq 3 is the
smallest prime dividing |G|. Then




c_{0}(G)\leq\begin{cases}\left\lceil(4np-2)/3\right\rceil,&p\mid n,\\
\left\lceil(25np-20)/6\right\rceil,&p\geq 5,\ p\nmid n,\\
\left\lceil(16np-12)/5\right\rceil,&p=3,\ 3\nmid n.\end{cases}






Proof.



Suppose first that p\mid n.
If n is a power of p, then
c_{0}(G)=np+2p-3\leq\lceil(4np-2)/3\rceil, since
np\geq p^{2}\geq 6p-9.
Otherwise n\geq p(p+2), whence
np\geq p^{2}(p+2)\geq 6p^{2}-3p-1. Thus




np+2p^{2}-p-1\leq\frac{4np-2}{3},\qquad n(p+1)-2\leq\frac{4np-2}{3}.



Theorem 3.5 proves the first assertion.
If p\nmid n, then n\geq p+2, and the same theorem gives
c_{0}(G)\leq 3np.
For p\geq 5, this is at most \lceil(25np-20)/6\rceil;
for p=3, it is at most \lceil(16np-12)/5\rceil,
since np\geq 15.
∎




Proposition 4.2.



Let G=C_{p}\oplus C_{np}, where p is prime and n\geq 1, and let l_{0} be a positive integer.
Let r=\lceil l_{0}/(np-1)\rceil. If




l_{0}-\lfloor l_{0}/r\rfloor\geq c_{0}(G),



then every regular sequence S over G with |S|\geq l_{0}
has a nonempty zero-sum subsequence T satisfying
\mathsf{v}_{g}(T)=\mathsf{v}_{g}(S) for some g\mid S.




Proof.



Since \langle g\rangle<G for every g\in G,
regularity gives \mathsf{v}_{g}(S)\leq\ord(g)-1\leq np-1.
Hence |\supp(S)|\geq r.
Choose g_{0}\mid S with minimum multiplicity
a=\mathsf{v}_{g_{0}}(S). The sequence S^{\prime}=Sg_{0}^{-a}
contains none of the occurrences of g_{0}.
Then S^{\prime} is regular, a\leq\lfloor|S|/r\rfloor, and




|S^{\prime}|\geq|S|-\lfloor|S|/r\rfloor\geq l_{0}-\lfloor l_{0}/r\rfloor\geq c_{0}(G).



Choose T^{\prime}\mid S^{\prime} with \sigma(T^{\prime})=-ag_{0}.
Then T=T^{\prime}g_{0}^{a} has the required property.
∎





For n\geq 2 and p\geq 3 the smallest prime dividing |G|, let




\operatorname{ub}(G)=\begin{cases}2np-1,&p\mid n,\\
5np-4,&p\geq 5,\ p\nmid n,\\
4np-3,&p=3,\ 3\nmid n.\end{cases}





Corollary 4.3.



Let G=C_{p}\oplus C_{np}, where n\geq 2 and p\geq 3 is the
smallest prime dividing |G|. Every regular sequence S over G
with |S|\geq\operatorname{ub}(G) has a nonempty zero-sum subsequence T such that
\mathsf{v}_{g}(T)=\mathsf{v}_{g}(S) for some g\mid S.




Proof.



Apply Proposition 4.2 with l_{0}=\operatorname{ub}(G).
The corresponding values of r are 3, 6, and 5,
according as p\mid n, p\geq 5 and p\nmid n, or
p=3 and 3\nmid n.
In each case, the required inequality is precisely
Lemma 4.1.
∎




Lemma 4.4.



Let S be a non-regular sequence over a finite abelian group G.
Then there exists a proper subgroup H<G such that S_{H}
is regular over H, |S_{H}|\geq|H|, and \sum(S_{H})=H.




Proof.



Choose H<G of minimum order subject to |S_{H}|\geq|H|.
For every proper subgroup H^{\prime}<H, minimality gives
|S_{H^{\prime}}|\leq|H^{\prime}|-1. Thus S_{H} is regular over H.
Since c_{0}(H)\leq|H|, we obtain \sum(S_{H})=H.
∎




Lemma 4.5.



Let S=S_{H}S_{1} with \sum_{0}(S_{H})=H and
\supp(S_{1})\subseteq G\setminus H, and let \Phi:G\to G/H
be the natural homomorphism.
Suppose that some \bar{g}\in\supp(\Phi(S_{1})) satisfies
\mathsf{v}_{\bar{g}}(\Phi(S_{1}))\geq\ord(\bar{g}) and that
\Phi^{-1}(\bar{g})\cap\supp(S_{1}) contains at least two elements.
Then S has a nonempty zero-sum subsequence T with
\mathsf{v}_{g}(T)=\mathsf{v}_{g}(S) for some g\mid S_{1}.




Proof.



Let V\mid S_{1} consist of all terms whose image is \bar{g},
and let n=\ord(\bar{g}).
If \mathsf{v}_{h}(V)\geq n for some h\mid V, let |V|=kn+t,
where 0\leq t<n, and take T_{1}=Vh^{-t}.
Then \sigma(T_{1})\in H, and
\mathsf{v}_{g}(T_{1})=\mathsf{v}_{g}(S_{1}) for every g\mid V with g\neq h.
Such an element exists by hypothesis.
Otherwise, choose g\mid V. Since \mathsf{v}_{g}(V)<n\leq|V|,
there is an n-term subsequence T_{1}\mid V containing all occurrences
of g. Again \sigma(T_{1})\in H and
\mathsf{v}_{g}(T_{1})=\mathsf{v}_{g}(S_{1}).
Lemma 2.8 completes the proof in both cases.
∎




Lemma 4.6.



Let G=C_{p}\oplus C_{np}, where p\geq 3 is the smallest prime
dividing |G|, n\geq 5, and H<G is cyclic of order np.
Let S=S_{H}g_{0}^{b} satisfy g_{0}\notin H, \ord(g_{0})=np,
b\leq np-1, |S|\geq 2np-1, and |S_{H}|\geq np.
Suppose that every term of S_{H} generates H.
Choose h\mid S_{H} with minimum multiplicity a.
Let R=S_{H}h^{-a}. If |R|\leq np-1 and a<np/2,
then S has a nonempty zero-sum subsequence T with \mathsf{v}_{h}(T)=\mathsf{v}_{h}(S).




Proof.



Let b=ps_{g}+t_{g}, and
R=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}g_{i}^{ps_{i}+t_{i}}, where 0\leq t_{i},t_{g}<p.
First suppose that a\geq 2p.
As \ell\leq 1+(np-1)/a, convexity of
x+(p-1)(np-1)/x on [2p,np/2] gives




p\Big(s_{g}+\sum_{i}s_{i}\Big)\geq 2np-1-a-\ell(p-1)\geq 3np/2-3p\geq np-p/2.



Thus s_{g}+\sum_{i}s_{i}\geq n.
Since s_{g}\leq n-1, choose i with s_{i}>0.
Consider U=(pg_{0})^{s_{g}}\prod_{j}(pg_{j})^{s_{j}} and delete one
occurrence of pg_{i}.
The remaining sequence has at least n-1 terms, all generating
pH=pG, so its \sum_{0} equals pH by
Lemma 3.1.
The deleted occurrence of pg_{i} leaves g_{i}^{p} unused in the corresponding
subsequences of Rg_{0}^{ps_{g}}. Since \sum_{0}(g_{i}^{p})+pH=H, we obtain
H\subseteq\sum_{0}(Rg_{0}^{ps_{g}}).




Now suppose that a\leq 2p-1.
Then s_{g}\geq n-2 and |R|\geq(n-2)p+1\geq 3p+1.
Take disjoint U,V\mid R with |U|=|V|=p.
Kneser’s theorem gives |\sum_{0}(U)|\geq p+1.
Hence two different sums from U have the same image in H/pH.
Using \sum_{0}(V)+pH=H, extend both by the same subsequence of V
to sums in pH. At least one is nonzero.
Thus there is W\mid UV with \sigma(W)\in pH\setminus\{0\}.
Since pg_{0} generates pH and \sigma(W)\neq 0, every proper
subgroup of pH contains at most one term of (pg_{0})^{s_{g}}\sigma(W),
and the trivial subgroup contains none. This sequence is therefore regular
over pH and has length at least n-1, so its \sum_{0} equals pH.
Also |RW^{-1}|\geq p+1, and its image has all terms nonzero in H/pH.
Again H\subseteq\sum_{0}(Rg_{0}^{ps_{g}}).
In either case choose T_{1}\mid Rg_{0}^{ps_{g}} with
\sigma(T_{1})=-ah and take T=T_{1}h^{a}.
∎




Lemma 4.7.



Let G=C_{p}\oplus C_{np}, where n\geq 2 and p\geq 3 is the
smallest prime dividing |G|, and let H<G be cyclic of order np.
Let S=S_{H}g_{0}^{b} satisfy g_{0}\notin H, \ord(g_{0})=np,
b\leq np-1, |S|\geq 2np-1, and suppose that S_{H} is regular over H.
If \ord(h^{\prime})\leq n for some h^{\prime}\mid S_{H}, then S has a nonempty zero-sum
subsequence T with \mathsf{v}_{h^{\prime}}(T)=\mathsf{v}_{h^{\prime}}(S).




Proof.



Let a=\mathsf{v}_{h^{\prime}}(S), R^{\prime}=S_{H}(h^{\prime})^{-a},
s=\lfloor b/p\rfloor, and U=R^{\prime}(pg_{0})^{s}.
Regularity gives a\leq n-1, while s\leq n-1 and
b-s\leq n(p-1). Hence |U|=|S|-a-b+s\geq np.
Suppose that \sum_{0}(U)\neq H, and let
H_{1}=\st(\sum_{0}(U)) and d=|H_{1}|.
Since 0\nmid U, Kneser’s theorem implies H_{1}\neq\{0\}.
It also gives




|U|-|U_{H_{1}}|\leq np/d-2,\qquad|U_{H_{1}}|\leq d-1+s\leq d+n-2.



As p\leq d\leq n,
|U|\leq np/d+d+n-4\leq 3n-4<np, a contradiction.
Thus \sum_{0}(U)=H\subseteq\sum_{0}(R^{\prime}g_{0}^{ps}).
Choose T_{1}\mid R^{\prime}g_{0}^{ps} with \sigma(T_{1})=-ah^{\prime};
then T=T_{1}(h^{\prime})^{a} has the required property.
∎




Lemma 4.8.



Let H=C_{3}\oplus C_{3m}, where m\geq 1 is odd.
Every regular sequence R over H of length at least |H|=9m
has a nonempty zero-sum subsequence T with
\mathsf{v}_{g}(T)=\mathsf{v}_{g}(R) for some g\mid R.




Proof.



Every multiplicity is at most 3m-1, so |\supp(R)|\geq 4.
Choose g\mid R of minimum multiplicity a and let R^{\prime}=Rg^{-a}.
Then R^{\prime} is regular over H and
|R^{\prime}|\geq\lceil 3|R|/4\rceil\geq\lceil 27m/4\rceil.
For m\geq 5, Theorem 3.5 gives




c_{0}(H)\leq\max\{3m+14,4m-2\}\leq 6m\leq\lceil 27m/4\rceil.



For m=1,3, use c_{0}(C_{3}^{2})=5 and c_{0}(C_{3}\oplus C_{9})=12.
Thus \sum(R^{\prime})=H. Choose T^{\prime}\mid R^{\prime} with \sigma(T^{\prime})=-ag.
Then T=T^{\prime}g^{a} is zero-sum and \mathsf{v}_{g}(T)=\mathsf{v}_{g}(R).
∎




Lemma 4.9.



Let H=C_{m}, let R be regular over H with |R|\geq m/2,
and let H_{0}\leq H be nontrivial. Then \sum_{0}(R)+H_{0}=H.




Proof.



Suppose otherwise and let H_{1} be the inverse image in H of the
stabilizer of the image of \sum_{0}(R) in H/H_{0}.
For k=|H_{1}|, we have 2\leq k\leq m/2.
Kneser’s theorem and regularity give




|R|-|R_{H_{1}}|\leq m/k-2,\qquad|R|\leq k+m/k-3\leq m/2-1,



contrary to |R|\geq m/2. The last inequality follows by convexity
of x+m/x on [2,m/2].
∎




Lemma 4.10.



Let S=S_{H}S_{1} be a sequence over an abelian group G, where
H=C_{m}, m\geq 2, S_{H} is regular over H, |S_{H}|\geq m,
and every multiplicity in S_{H} is smaller than the corresponding element order.
Suppose that pairwise disjoint nonempty subsequences of S_{1} have sums
forming a sequence U over H.
If \sum_{0}(U) contains a nontrivial subgroup H_{0}\leq H, then S
has a nonempty zero-sum subsequence T with
\mathsf{v}_{h}(T)=\mathsf{v}_{h}(S) for some h\mid S_{H}.
In particular, this holds if U has no zero term, is supported in a
nontrivial subgroup K\leq H, and |U|\geq|K|-1.




Proof.



The strict multiplicity bound and |S_{H}|\geq m ensure that S_{H}
has at least two distinct elements. Choose h\mid S_{H} of minimum multiplicity a. Then R=S_{H}h^{-a} has length |R|\geq m/2.
Lemma 4.9 gives \sum_{0}(R)+H_{0}=H.
Choose V\mid R with \sigma(V)+ah\in H_{0}.
Since H_{0}\subseteq\sum_{0}(U), a subsequence of U has sum
-\sigma(V)-ah. The corresponding pairwise disjoint subsequences of
S_{1} form a subsequence T_{1} with the same sum.
Thus T=Vh^{a}T_{1} is zero-sum. Since h\nmid R and h\nmid S_{1},
we also have \mathsf{v}_{h}(T)=a=\mathsf{v}_{h}(S).
For the last assertion, a nontrivial stabilizer of \sum_{0}(U)
is itself contained in \sum_{0}(U).
If the stabilizer is trivial, Kneser’s theorem gives
|\sum_{0}(U)|\geq|U|+1\geq|K|, so \sum_{0}(U)=K.
∎




Lemma 4.11.



Let G=C_{p}\oplus C_{np}, where n\geq 2 and p\geq 3 is the
smallest prime dividing |G|.
Let S=S_{H}S_{1} satisfy |S|\geq 2np-1 and
\mathsf{v}_{g}(S)<\ord(g) for every g\mid S.
Suppose that H<G is cyclic of order m, that S_{H} is regular over H,
and that m\leq|S_{H}|\leq\mathsf{N}(H)-1.
If G/H is cyclic of order greater than p, then S has a nonempty
zero-sum subsequence T with \mathsf{v}_{g}(T)=\mathsf{v}_{g}(S)
for some g\mid S.




Proof.



By Lemma 3.1, \sum_{0}(S_{H})=H.
Let k=np/m>1, so that |G/H|=pk, and let \Phi:G\to G/H
be the natural homomorphism.
Since G/pG\cong C_{p}^{2} and (G/H)/p(G/H)\cong C_{p},
H/(H\cap pG) has order p. Thus p\mid m and,
as H is cyclic, H\cap pG=pH.
We have k\geq p and |S_{1}|\geq 2np-\mathsf{N}(C_{m}).




By Lemma 4.5, we may assume that
\Phi^{-1}(\bar{g})\cap\supp(S_{1}) contains exactly one element whenever
\mathsf{v}_{\bar{g}}(\Phi(S_{1}))\geq\ord(\bar{g}).
For each g\mid S_{1}, let r_{g}=\ord(\Phi(g)) and choose
\lfloor\mathsf{v}_{g}(S_{1})/r_{g}\rfloor disjoint copies of g^{r_{g}}.
Let T_{0} be their product, U the sequence of their sums, and
R=S_{1}T_{0}^{-1}.
Every term of U is nonzero, since a selected subsequence g^{r_{g}}
satisfies r_{g}\leq\mathsf{v}_{g}(S)<\ord(g).
The choice of T_{0} also ensures that \Phi(R) is weak-regular.




Suppose that R_{1}\mid R satisfies \sigma(\Phi(R_{1}))=0 and
\mathsf{v}_{\bar{g}}(\Phi(R_{1}))=\mathsf{v}_{\bar{g}}(\Phi(R))
for some \bar{g}\mid\Phi(R).
Then R_{1} contains every term of R whose image is \bar{g}.
Consequently T_{1}=R_{1}T_{0} has sum in H and
\mathsf{v}_{h}(T_{1})=\mathsf{v}_{h}(S_{1}) for every
h\in\Phi^{-1}(\bar{g})\cap\supp(S_{1}).
Lemma 2.8 gives the required subsequence of S.
In particular, it suffices to prove |R|\geq\mathsf{N}(G/H).




Suppose first that m>p, so m\geq p^{2}.
By Lemma 4.10, we may assume that \sum_{0}(U)
contains no nontrivial subgroup of H.
Let U_{1}=U_{pH} and U_{2}=UU_{1}^{-1}, with a=|U_{1}| and b=|U_{2}|.
The same lemma, applied to U_{1} with K=pH, gives a\leq m/p-2.
Each term of U_{1} is the sum of a selected subsequence of length at most pk.
If a selected subsequence corresponding to a term of U_{2} had length
divisible by p, its sum would lie in H\cap pG=pH.
Its length therefore divides k and is not divisible by p, so is at most k.
Since its length is greater than one, U_{2}\neq 1 implies k\geq p+2.




If b\leq p-2, then




|T_{0}|\leq k(pa+b)\leq np-(p+2)k,\qquad|R|\geq m(k-2)+(p+2)k\geq 2pk.



Here \mathsf{N}(C_{m})\leq 2m, and
m(k-2)\geq k(p-2) follows from m\geq p and k\geq p.
Thus |R|\geq 2pk\geq\mathsf{N}(C_{pk}).




If b\geq p-1, choose V\mid U_{2} of length p-1.
Its image in H/pH=C_{p} has all terms nonzero, so
\sum_{0}(V)+pH=H.
The stabilizer of \sum_{0}(U) is trivial, since otherwise it would be a
nontrivial subgroup contained in \sum_{0}(U).
For every W\mid U, the identity
\sum_{0}(U)=\sum_{0}(W)+\sum_{0}(UW^{-1}) gives
\st(\sum_{0}(W))\leq\st(\sum_{0}(U))=\{0\}.
Kneser’s theorem therefore yields |\sum_{0}(U_{1})|\geq a+1.
Since \sum_{0}(V) meets every coset of pH,
|\sum_{0}(U_{1}V)|\geq p(a+1).
Each remaining term of U_{2} increases the number of subsequence sums
by at least one. Otherwise that nonzero term would belong to the
stabilizer of the preceding sumset, which is trivial.
It follows that




pa+b+1\leq|\sum_{0}(U)|\leq m-1,\qquad|T_{0}|\leq k(pa+b)\leq np-2k.



Hence |R|\geq m(k-2)+2k\geq 2pk, since m\geq p^{2}, k\geq p+2, and
p^{2}(k-2)-2k(p-1)\geq(p-2)(p^{2}-2)>0.
Again |R|\geq\mathsf{N}(C_{pk}).




It remains to consider m=p.
Since pH=\{0\} and every term of U is nonzero, every selected
subsequence has length at most k.
If |U|\geq p-2, then |\sum_{0}(U)|\geq p-1 by Cauchy–Davenport.
Choose distinct h,x\in\supp(S_{H}); such a choice exists because
|S_{H}|\geq p and every multiplicity is less than p.
Another application of Cauchy–Davenport gives
\sum_{0}(U)+\{0,x\}=H.
Since \sum_{0}(U)\subseteq\sum_{0}(T_{0}), there is T_{1}\mid T_{0}x
with \sigma(T_{1})=-\mathsf{v}_{h}(S)h.
Then T=T_{1}h^{\mathsf{v}_{h}(S)} is zero-sum and
\mathsf{v}_{h}(T)=\mathsf{v}_{h}(S).
We may therefore assume |U|\leq p-3.




If T_{0}=1, then R=S_{1} and




|R|\geq 2pk-\mathsf{N}(C_{p})\geq 2pk-2p\geq\mathsf{N}(C_{pk}).



The last inequality uses pk\geq p^{2} and the cyclic bound
\mathsf{N}(C_{pk})\leq 2pk-\lceil 2\sqrt{pk+1}\rceil+1.
Otherwise choose g\mid S_{1} with \mathsf{v}_{g}(S_{1})\geq r_{g}.
Let t=\mathsf{v}_{g}(R)<r_{g}\leq k and R^{\prime}=Rg^{-t}.
By the assumption on the cosets, \Phi(R^{\prime}) contains no occurrence of
\Phi(g). It is weak-regular and




|R^{\prime}|\geq 2pk-\mathsf{N}(C_{p})-(p-3)k-(k-1)=(p+2)k-\mathsf{N}(C_{p})+1\geq pk.



Apply Lemma 2.7 to \Phi(R^{\prime}).
If \sum(\Phi(R^{\prime}))=G/H, choose R_{1}\mid R^{\prime} such that
\Phi(\sigma(R_{1}))=-\mathsf{v}_{g}(S_{1})\Phi(g).
Then T_{1}=R_{1}g^{\mathsf{v}_{g}(S_{1})} has sum in H and
\mathsf{v}_{g}(T_{1})=\mathsf{v}_{g}(S_{1}), so Lemma 2.8 applies.
Otherwise there is R_{1}\mid R^{\prime} with \sigma(\Phi(R_{1}))=0 and
\mathsf{v}_{\bar{h}}(\Phi(R_{1}))=\mathsf{v}_{\bar{h}}(\Phi(R^{\prime}))
for some \bar{h}\mid\Phi(R^{\prime}).
Since \bar{h}\neq\Phi(g), its multiplicity in \Phi(R^{\prime}) equals that
in \Phi(R). The argument for R_{1}T_{0} above completes the proof.
∎




Lemma 4.12.



Let G=C_{p}\oplus C_{np}, where n\geq 2 and p\geq 3 is the
smallest prime dividing |G|.
Let S satisfy |S|\geq 2np-1 and
\mathsf{v}_{g}(S)<\ord(g) for every g\in\supp(S).
Suppose that H<G is cyclic of order np, that S_{H} is regular
over H, and that np\leq|S_{H}|\leq\mathsf{N}(H)-1.
Then S has a nonempty zero-sum subsequence T such that
\mathsf{v}_{g}(T)=\mathsf{v}_{g}(S) for some g\in\supp(S).




Proof.



Let S_{1}=SS_{H}^{-1}, and let \Phi:G\to G/H\cong C_{p} be the quotient map.
Lemma 3.1 gives \sum_{0}(S_{H})=H.
By Lemma 2.8, it suffices to find T_{1}\mid S_{1}
with \sigma(T_{1})\in H and \mathsf{v}_{g}(T_{1})=\mathsf{v}_{g}(S_{1})
for some g\mid S_{1}.
Since n\geq p and np\geq p^{2}, the cyclic upper bound gives




|S_{1}|\geq 2np-\mathsf{N}(C_{np})\geq\lceil 2\sqrt{np+1}\rceil-1\geq 2p\geq\mathsf{N}(C_{p}).



If \Phi(S_{1}) is weak-regular, its length gives a subsequence
T_{1}\mid S_{1} such that \sigma(\Phi(T_{1}))=0 and
\mathsf{v}_{\bar{g}}(\Phi(T_{1}))=\mathsf{v}_{\bar{g}}(\Phi(S_{1}))
for some \bar{g}\mid\Phi(S_{1}).
Thus \sigma(T_{1})\in H, and T_{1} contains all occurrences of every
element of S_{1} whose image is \bar{g}. Lemma 2.8 applies.




Otherwise some \bar{g}\in G/H has multiplicity greater than p
in \Phi(S_{1}). If \Phi^{-1}(\bar{g})\cap\supp(S_{1}) contains at least
two elements, apply Lemma 4.5.
Suppose that this intersection is \{g\}.
If S_{1} has another element g_{0}\neq g, then
\sum_{0}(g^{p}S_{H})=G, because g+H generates G/H and
\sum_{0}(S_{H})=H.
Choose T^{\prime}\mid g^{p}S_{H} with \sigma(T^{\prime})=-\mathsf{v}_{g_{0}}(S_{1})g_{0}.
Since g_{0}\nmid g^{p}S_{H}, the sequence
T=T^{\prime}g_{0}^{\mathsf{v}_{g_{0}}(S_{1})} is a zero-sum subsequence of S
with \mathsf{v}_{g_{0}}(T)=\mathsf{v}_{g_{0}}(S).




It remains to consider S_{1}=g_{0}^{b}, where b\geq p and
b<\ord(g_{0})\leq np.
Choose h\mid S_{H} of minimum multiplicity a, and let R=S_{H}h^{-a}.
The strict multiplicity bounds and |S_{H}|\geq np ensure that
S_{H} has at least two distinct elements, so |R|\geq|S_{H}|/2.
If |R|\geq np, Lemma 3.1 gives \sum_{0}(R)=H.
Choose T_{1}\mid R with \sigma(T_{1})=-ah; then T=T_{1}h^{a}
has \mathsf{v}_{h}(T)=a=\mathsf{v}_{h}(S).
Hence assume |R|\leq np-1.




Case 1: |\supp(R)|\geq 2.
Then 2a\leq|R|\leq np-1 and
b=|S|-|R|-a\geq np-a>np/2, forcing \ord(g_{0})=np.
If some h^{\prime}\mid S_{H} does not generate H, then
\ord(h^{\prime})\leq np/p=n, so Lemma 4.7 applies.
We may therefore assume that every term of S_{H} generates H.
For n\geq 5, apply Lemma 4.6.
The only remaining possibility is p=n=3.
Here Rg_{0}^{b} is regular over G: a proper subgroup containing
any term of R must equal H, where |R|\leq 8; any other proper
subgroup contains no terms or only b occurrences of g_{0},
and in the latter case has order at least \ord(g_{0})>b.
Since a\leq 4, we have |Rg_{0}^{b}|\geq 13\geq c_{0}(C_{3}\oplus C_{9})=12.
Hence there is T_{1}\mid Rg_{0}^{b} with \sigma(T_{1})=-ah,
and T=T_{1}h^{a} has the required property.




Case 2: |\supp(R)|=1.
Then R=f^{c} for some f\in H, with a\leq c\leq np-1.
Since 2c\geq|S_{H}|\geq np and c<\ord(f), we have \ord(f)=np.
Let c=ps_{f}+t_{f} and b=ps_{g}+t_{g}, where 0\leq t_{f},t_{g}<p.
If a\geq(n-1)p, then s_{f}\geq n-1 and s_{g}\geq 1.
Otherwise a\leq(n-1)p-1, so c+b\geq(n+1)p and




s_{f}+s_{g}\geq\left\lceil\frac{(n+1)p-2(p-1)}{p}\right\rceil=n.



Thus s_{f}+s_{g}\geq n in both cases, and s_{f}\geq\lfloor n/2\rfloor\geq 1.
Consider the sequence




V=(pf)^{s_{f}-1}(pg_{0})^{s_{g}}\quad\text{over }pH,\qquad|V|\geq n-1.



Indeed pH=pG\cong C_{n} and pf generates this group.
Since g_{0}+H has order p, we have p\mid\ord(g_{0}), and the integer
d=\ord(pg_{0})=\ord(g_{0})/p satisfies d>1 and s_{g}\leq d-1.
No proper subgroup of pH contains pf; one containing pg_{0}
has order at least d and contains at most s_{g}\leq d-1 terms of V.
Consequently V is regular over pH, and
Lemma 3.1 yields \sum_{0}(V)=pH.
The terms of V are sums of pairwise disjoint subsequences of
f^{ps_{f}}g_{0}^{ps_{g}}, leaving p occurrences of f unused.
Since \sum_{0}(f^{p})+pH=H, we obtain




H=\sum_{0}(V)+\sum_{0}(f^{p})\subseteq\sum_{0}(f^{ps_{f}}g_{0}^{ps_{g}})\subseteq\sum_{0}(f^{c}g_{0}^{b}).



Choose T_{1}\mid f^{c}g_{0}^{b} with \sigma(T_{1})=-ah.
Then T=T_{1}h^{a} is nonempty and zero-sum, with \mathsf{v}_{h}(T)=a=\mathsf{v}_{h}(S).
∎




Proof of Theorem 1.4.



The lower bound for n\geq 2 follows from Theorem 1.3.
For n=1, both stated upper bounds follow from the known value of
\mathsf{N}(C_{p}^{2}).
Fix p and use strong induction on the integers n whose prime divisors are at least p,
with the known value of \mathsf{N}(C_{p}^{2}) as the base n=1.
Let S be weak-regular over G, with |S|\geq\operatorname{ub}(G).
If \mathsf{v}_{g}(S)=\ord(g) for some g\mid S, then
T=g^{\ord(g)} is zero-sum and \mathsf{v}_{g}(T)=\mathsf{v}_{g}(S).
Thus every multiplicity is strictly smaller than the element order;
in particular, 0\nmid S.
If S is regular, Corollary 4.3 applies.




Otherwise choose H by Lemma 4.4.
Then H is nontrivial, S_{H} is regular over H, and |S_{H}|\geq|H|.
First suppose H=C_{p}\oplus C_{mp} is noncyclic.
If m=1, the known value of \mathsf{N}(C_{p}^{2}) is at most p^{2}\leq|S_{H}|.
If m\geq 2 and p\geq 5, then |S_{H}|\geq mp^{2}\geq\operatorname{ub}(H),
so Corollary 4.3, applied within H, suffices.
For p=3, use Lemma 4.8.
In each case, \mathsf{v}_{h}(S_{H})=\mathsf{v}_{h}(S) for every h\in H,
so the subsequence obtained in S_{H} also has the required property in S.




We may therefore suppose H=C_{m} is cyclic.
If |S_{H}|\geq\mathsf{N}(H), apply its definition within H.
Otherwise m\leq|S_{H}|\leq\mathsf{N}(H)-1.
Let S_{1}=SS_{H}^{-1}, and let \Phi:G\to G_{1}=G/H be the quotient map.
If G_{1} is cyclic, apply Lemma 4.12 when |G_{1}|=p
and Lemma 4.11 otherwise.
These lemmas apply because |S|\geq\operatorname{ub}(G)\geq 2np-1.




Suppose finally that G_{1} is noncyclic.
Since G_{1}/pG_{1}\cong G/(H+pG) has order p^{2}=|G/pG|,
we obtain H\leq pG.
Thus 1<m\mid n and G_{1}\cong C_{p}\oplus C_{kp}, where k=n/m<n.
If \mathsf{v}_{\bar{g}}(\Phi(S_{1}))\geq\ord(\bar{g}) for some
\bar{g}\in G_{1}\setminus\{0\} and
|\Phi^{-1}(\bar{g})\cap\supp(S_{1})|\geq 2,
Lemma 4.5 applies.
Otherwise, for each g\mid S_{1} with
\mathsf{v}_{g}(S_{1})\geq r_{g}=\ord(\Phi(g)), choose \lfloor\mathsf{v}_{g}(S_{1})/r_{g}\rfloor disjoint copies of
g^{r_{g}}.
Let T_{0} be their product, U the sequence of their sums,
and R=S_{1}T_{0}^{-1} the remaining sequence.
Every term of U is nonzero because r_{g}\leq\mathsf{v}_{g}(S)<\ord(g),
and \Phi(R) is weak-regular.
If |U|\geq m-1, Lemma 4.10 applies with K=H.
Otherwise |T_{0}|\leq(m-1)kp, and




|R|\geq 2np-1-(\mathsf{N}(H)-1)-(m-1)kp\geq\mathsf{N}(G_{1}).



The last inequality is Lemma 2.6, using the appropriate induction
bound for k\geq 2, or the known elementary-group value for k=1.
By the definition of \mathsf{N}(G_{1}), there is R_{1}\mid R with
\sigma(\Phi(R_{1}))=0 and
\mathsf{v}_{\bar{g}}(\Phi(R_{1}))=\mathsf{v}_{\bar{g}}(\Phi(R))
for some \bar{g}\mid\Phi(R).
Then T_{1}=R_{1}T_{0} has sum in H and contains all terms of S_{1}
whose image is \bar{g}. In particular,
\mathsf{v}_{h}(T_{1})=\mathsf{v}_{h}(S_{1}) for any
h\in\Phi^{-1}(\bar{g})\cap\supp(S_{1}).
Since \sum_{0}(S_{H})=H, Lemma 2.8 completes the proof.
Replacing n by np proves the asserted equality for C_{p}\oplus C_{np^{2}}.
∎






5. Remarks and open questions




We conjecture that \mathsf{N}(C_{p}\oplus C_{np})=2np-1 whenever
n\geq 2 and p\geq 3 is the smallest prime dividing np.
The non-regular case in the proof of Theorem 1.4 uses only
|S|\geq 2np-1. Hence, for the remaining cases p\nmid n, the bound
c_{0}(C_{p}\oplus C_{np})\leq\lceil(4np-2)/3\rceil would imply the
conjecture by Proposition 4.2.




For the cyclic groups below, let A_{n+1}=\lceil 2\sqrt{n+1}\rceil and




B_{n+1}=\min\{a+b:a,b\in\mathbb{N},\ ab\geq n+1,\ \gcd(a,n)=1\},



as in [8].
For the following two families, \mathsf{N}(C_{n}) attains the upper bound
2n-A_{n+1}+1 and is strictly larger than 2n-B_{n+1}+1.



Theorem 5.1.



Let n=2730\bigl(1+105(2t-1)\,(13t-\tfrac{13\pm 1}{2})\bigr) for a positive integer t;
equivalently, n\in T_{2}\cup T_{3}, where




\displaystyle T_{2}
\displaystyle=\{\,2730(2730t^{2}-2835t+736)\,\}_{t\geq 1},



\displaystyle T_{3}
\displaystyle=\{\,2730(2730t^{2}-2625t+631)\,\}_{t\geq 1}.



Then




B_{n+1}=A_{n+1}+1=\lceil 2\sqrt{n+1}\rceil+1,



and




\mathsf{N}(C_{n})=2n-B_{n+1}+2=2n-\lceil 2\sqrt{n+1}\rceil+1.






Proof.



By [8, Lemmas 5.2 and 4.3],
B_{n+1}=A_{n+1}+1 and \mathsf{N}(C_{n})\leq 2n-A_{n+1}+1.
Let C_{n}=\langle e\rangle.




Suppose first that n\in T_{2}, and let
d=1365(2t-1)-52 and x=(12n-d)/13.
Then n=d(d-1)-26, 13\mid d, and d is odd.
Since d\geq 1313, we have
(d-1)^{2}<n+1<(d-\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}, and hence A_{n+1}=2d-1.
The identity n=d(d-1)-26 gives




13x\equiv-d\pmod{n},\qquad(d-1)x\equiv-2\pmod{n}.



Every common divisor of x and n therefore divides both d and 2,
so \gcd(x,n)=1.
As 1<x<n, the sequence S=e^{n-d}(xe)^{n-d+1} is weak-regular
and has length 2n-A_{n+1}.
If a zero-sum subsequence contains all n-d+1 copies of xe,
its multiplicity of e is congruent to (d-1)x\equiv-2\pmod{n}.
It must therefore equal n-2>n-d, a contradiction.
If a zero-sum subsequence contains all n-d copies of e,
then 13x\equiv-d\pmod{n} and \gcd(x,n)=1 force its
multiplicity of xe to equal n-13>n-d+1, also a contradiction.




Suppose now that n\in T_{3}, and let
d=1365(2t-1)+53 and x=(n-d)/2.
Then n=d(d-1)-26, d is even, and d\equiv 1\pmod{13}.
In particular, d\geq 1418 and A_{n+1}=2d-1 as above. We have




2x\equiv-d\pmod{n},\qquad(d-1)x\equiv-13\pmod{n}.



Every common divisor of x and n divides both d and 13,
so \gcd(x,n)=1.
Again 1<x<n, and S=e^{n-d}(xe)^{n-d+1} is weak-regular
with |S|=2n-A_{n+1}.
A zero-sum subsequence containing all copies of xe would have
n-13>n-d copies of e, by the second congruence.
A zero-sum subsequence containing all copies of e would have
n-2>n-d+1 copies of xe, by the first congruence and \gcd(x,n)=1.
Thus S has no zero-sum subsequence T with
\mathsf{v}_{g}(T)=\mathsf{v}_{g}(S) for g\in\{e,xe\}.
In both cases, the definition of \mathsf{N}(C_{n}) gives
\mathsf{N}(C_{n})\geq 2n-A_{n+1}+1, proving the equality.
∎




Remark 5.2.



The construction of Theorem 5.1 relies on the specific relation
n=d(d-1)-26 together with the arithmetic of 13 modulo d (or d-1).
For the other two families T_{4},T_{5} identified in [8, Lemma 5.2]
(which satisfy n=d^{2}-64 rather than n=d(d-1)-26), the analogous construction
does not apply directly, and whether \mathsf{N}(C_{n}) on T_{4}\cup T_{5} attains
the upper or the lower bound remains an open question.
