Abstract.
    

We classify the Schubert polynomials that are products of key
polynomials and explicitly determine their finest key
factorization. Their indexing permutations are precisely those
avoiding ten classical patterns of length six, which form the
minimal pattern basis. More generally, the connected components
of the column interaction graph give the unique finest
factorization of a dual flagged Weyl character into nonconstant
diagram characters. Every other such factorization is obtained
by grouping these components. We also prove Makhija’s conjecture
on products of elementary symmetric polynomials in initial
alphabets, allowing repeated alphabets. In this case, the
elementary factors are uniquely determined, up to order, by the
nonempty Rothe columns.
  



Key words and phrases: Schubert polynomial, key polynomial, column interaction graph, pattern avoidance




1. Introduction




Schubert polynomials represent Schubert classes in the cohomology
of flag varieties [4], and each is a nonnegative integer
linear combination of key polynomials [13]. A Schubert
polynomial is itself a key polynomial exactly when its indexing
permutation avoids 2143; see Reiner and Shimozono [13]
and [12, Theorem 3.3]. We ask when a Schubert polynomial
is a product of keys and how to determine its finest such
factorization.




To study these products, we use dual flagged Weyl characters,
which we call diagram characters. For a diagram
D=(D_{1},\ldots,D_{m}), where D_{j} is the set of occupied
rows in column j, write \chi_{D} for its character.
Rothe and skyline diagrams give Schubert and key polynomials,
respectively [4]. The product of the individual column
characters is a coefficientwise upper bound for \chi_{D}.
Equality in this bound is precisely a factorization into
individual column characters. Fan and Guo [3] characterized
equality for Schubert and key polynomials; in the Schubert case,
it holds exactly when the indexing permutation avoids 1423
and 1432. Peng, Lin, and Sun [9, Theorem 1.1]
gave the criterion for arbitrary diagrams.




We extend this question from individual columns to blocks
of columns. The obstruction to separating two columns is
recorded by the column interaction graph \Gamma_{D}:
its vertices are the nonempty indexed columns of D, and
two columns are joined when they share an empty row above
a row occupied in both. We prove that the characters of
the column blocks multiply to \chi_{D} exactly when no
edge of \Gamma_{D} joins different blocks.




It remains to check that column partitions account for all
factorizations into diagram characters. Here we use the fact
that \chi_{D} determines the multiset of nonempty columns of
D, a consequence of [5, Theorem 1.4]. Every factorization
into nonconstant diagram characters must therefore come from
a column partition. Together with the block criterion, this
shows that the connected components give the unique finest
factorization, up to order; every other such factorization
is obtained by grouping the component factors.11

blocks need not have disjoint row sets; compare the disjoint-row
tensor products in [1, Example 1.6]. For factorizations
of Ehrhart polynomials of Schubitopes, see Dou, Fan, and
Liu [2].




The key-product problem now reduces to recognizing which
component characters are keys. A component character is a
key polynomial precisely when its columns form a chain
under inclusion. For Rothe diagrams, this condition gives
the pattern classification below.




To state it, write \mathfrak{S}_{w} for a Schubert polynomial and
\kappa_{\alpha} for the key indexed by a weak composition
\alpha, a finite sequence of nonnegative integers.
All factors use the fixed variables x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,
without relabeling. A key product is a finite product
of nonconstant keys; a single factor and the empty product
1 are allowed. For w=w_{1}\cdots w_{n}\in S_{n}, the Rothe
diagram D(w) has columns
D_{j}(w)=\{i:i<w^{-1}(j),\ w_{i}>j\}.
Classical pattern containment means having a subsequence
with the same relative order as the pattern. Set




\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\{
\displaystyle 135264,136254,153264,163254,315264,316254,




\displaystyle 142635,142653,143625,143652\}.

(1.1)




Theorem 1.1.



The Schubert polynomial \mathfrak{S}_{w} is a key product if and only
if w avoids every pattern in \mathcal{B}. The ten patterns
in (1.1) form the minimal classical pattern basis.




For such a permutation, group the nonempty Rothe columns
by the connected components of \Gamma_{D(w)}. Let
\alpha^{(1)},\ldots,\alpha^{(s)} record the numbers of boxes
in each row of the resulting subdiagrams. Then the unique
finest key factorization, up to order, is




\mathfrak{S}_{w}=\prod_{t=1}^{s}\kappa_{\alpha^{(t)}}.







The same column criterion also applies to products of elementary
symmetric polynomials. For 1\leq b\leq a, the polynomial
e_{b}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{a}) is the character of the interval column
\{a-b+1,\ldots,a\}. An elementary product is a finite
product of these polynomials, allowing repeated initial
alphabets, a single factor, and the empty product 1.




If each initial alphabet is used at most once, the product
is a standard elementary monomial. Hatam, Johnson,
Liu, and Macaulay [6] gave determinantal formulas
for expansions of Schubert polynomials in this basis when
the indexing permutation avoids thirteen specified patterns.
Woodruff [14, Theorem 4] proved that a Schubert
polynomial is a single standard elementary monomial exactly
when its permutation avoids 312 and 1432. For products
allowing repeated initial alphabets,
Makhija [11, Section 1.7] conjectured the four-pattern
criterion below and gave a pipe-dream argument for
sufficiency [11, Section 2]. We prove the converse
and determine the factors uniquely.



Theorem 1.2.



The Schubert polynomial \mathfrak{S}_{w} is an elementary product
if and only if w avoids 1423, 1432, 3142, and 4132,
as conjectured by Makhija [11, Section 1.7].
In this case the factorization is unique, up to order, and is




\mathfrak{S}_{w}=\prod_{D_{j}(w)\neq\varnothing}e_{|D_{j}(w)|}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\max D_{j}(w)}).

(1.2)






In this case, Proposition 2.1 forces the elementary
factors to come from the individual Rothe columns. The criterion for
separating these columns [3, 9] and the characterization
of interval Rothe columns [10, Proposition 2.3] give the
four forbidden patterns. Each interval column determines its
elementary factor, which also proves uniqueness.




Section 2 recalls the determinant model and
shows that a diagram character determines the multiset of
its nonempty columns. Section 3 proves the criterion
for column blocks and the finest factorization.
Sections 4 and 5 apply these
results to key and elementary products, respectively.





2. Columns and diagram characters




All vector spaces are over \mathbb{C}. We use
[N]=\{1,\ldots,N\} and [a,b]=\{a,a+1,\ldots,b\},
with N large enough to contain the rows of every diagram
under discussion. For row sets A,B, the difference
A\setminus B consists of the rows belonging to A but
not to B. Empty columns contribute 1 and play
no part in column partitions. Copies of the same nonempty
column retain their separate indices.




2.1. Allowed upward moves and column characters




Allowed moves in a column shift its boxes upwards without
changing their order. For two row sets of size b,
C=\{c_{1}<\cdots<c_{b}\} and A=\{a_{1}<\cdots<a_{b}\},
we write C\preceq A in Gale order if c_{i}\leq a_{i}
for every i, and call C allowed for A.
For diagrams E=(E_{1},\ldots,E_{m}) and
D=(D_{1},\ldots,D_{m}) with |E_{j}|=|D_{j}| for every j,
write E\preceq D if E_{j}\preceq D_{j} for every j.
We record the row counts of E by the monomial




x^{E}=\prod_{j}\prod_{i\in E_{j}}x_{i}.



Thus the exponent of x_{i} is the number of cells in row i of E.




We encode these allowed row choices by minors of a generic
upper triangular N\times N matrix Y=(y_{ij}).
We write Y_{A}^{C} for its submatrix with row set C and
column set A, both in increasing order. For a column A,
the determinants \det(Y_{A}^{C}) with C\preceq A are
called its allowed minors. For C=A, this is the
principal minor \det(Y_{A}^{A}). For a diagram D,
taking one allowed minor from each column gives the products
that span the polynomial space




M_{D}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\left\{\prod_{j}\det(Y_{D_{j}}^{E_{j}}):E\preceq D\right\}.



We grade by rows, assigning weight x_{i} to y_{ij}.
The coefficient [x^{\beta}]\chi_{D} is the dimension of the
part of M_{D} having weight x^{\beta}.




For a single column,




F_{A}=\chi_{(A)}=\sum_{C\preceq A}x^{C},\qquad x^{C}=\prod_{i\in C}x_{i}.

(2.1)


For C=\{c_{1}<\cdots<c_{b}\}\preceq A=\{a_{1}<\cdots<a_{b}\}, the minor \det(Y_{A}^{C}) contains
\prod_{i}y_{c_{i},a_{i}} with coefficient 1, hence is nonzero.
As different C give different row weights, the allowed
minors are independent.




For several columns, the products of allowed minors need not
be independent. Since they span M_{D}, we obtain the
coefficientwise bound




\chi_{D}\leq_{\mathrm{coeff}}\prod_{j}F_{D_{j}}=\sum_{E\preceq D}x^{E}.



Each product of allowed minors is nonzero, so every monomial
on the right also occurs in \chi_{D}. Thus the two sides have
the same support, although their coefficients may differ.




For D=(\{2\},\{2\}), each column has the two allowed
minors y_{12} and y_{22}. Consequently,




M_{D}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{y_{12}^{2},y_{12}y_{22},y_{22}^{2}\},\qquad\chi_{D}=x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}+x_{2}^{2}.



By contrast, (F_{\{2\}})^{2}=(x_{1}+x_{2})^{2} has coefficient
2 at x_{1}x_{2}. The two choices of column minors give the
same product y_{12}y_{22}, which contributes only once to
the character.




For characters with 0–1 coefficients, see Guo, Lin,
and Peng [7]. For related bounds on Schubert
monomials, see Guo and Lin [8].




Permuting the columns does not change M_{D} or \chi_{D}.
When comparing diagrams, we place them in a common
N\times N grid, enlarging N and adding empty columns
if necessary. These operations leave their spaces and
characters unchanged.





2.2. Skyline diagrams and key polynomials




For a weak composition \alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{N}, the
skyline diagram \operatorname{Sky}(\alpha) has \alpha_{i}
left-justified cells in row i. Its character is
\kappa_{\alpha}, and \kappa_{0}=1. The nonempty columns of
\operatorname{Sky}(\alpha) are the sets




\{i:\alpha_{i}\geq 1\}\supseteq\{i:\alpha_{i}\geq 2\}\supseteq\cdots.



These columns form an inclusion chain, meaning that
they are pairwise comparable by inclusion. Conversely, any
inclusion chain of columns can be put in skyline order.
The number of boxes in each row gives the indexing composition.
Figure 1 shows \operatorname{Sky}(3,0,2,1), with column
sets \{1,3,4\}\supseteq\{1,3\}\supseteq\{1\}.

1234123\operatorname{Sky}(3,0,2,1)
Figure 1. The skyline diagram \operatorname{Sky}(3,0,2,1).




For example, \kappa_{(1,0)}=x_{1} and
\kappa_{(0,1)}=x_{1}+x_{2}. Trailing zeros do not affect
a key.




For Rothe diagrams, \chi_{D(w)}=\mathfrak{S}_{w}. These
identifications use the conventions of [4, 9].
When omitting empty Rothe columns, we keep the original
labels of the remaining columns.





2.3. The interaction graph




A pair of rows i<j is a witness for an interaction
between A and B if row i is vacant in both columns
and row j is occupied in both.




ABij



Only the two witness rows are shown; the columns need not be
consecutive in the diagram. A nonempty column [a] is isolated
in \Gamma_{D}, since every row above an occupied row is also
occupied.




Peng, Lin, and Sun [9, Theorem 1.1] characterized equality
in the coefficientwise bound for two columns:




\chi_{(A,B)}=F_{A}F_{B}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A\text{ and }B\text{ do not interact}.

(2.2)






2.4. Column multisets and character factorizations




The following proposition is a direct consequence of
Hafner, Mészáros, Setiabrata, and St. Dizier’s
Theorem 1.4 [5]. We include a short proof and
its application to products of diagram characters.



Proposition 2.1.



On a common [N], the monomials occurring in \chi_{D}
determine the multiset of nonempty columns of D.
In particular, if




\chi_{D}=\chi_{E^{(1)}}\cdots\chi_{E^{(k)}},



then the nonempty columns of the E^{(t)} together are
exactly the nonempty columns of D, with multiplicity.




Proof.



For each nonempty A\subseteq[N], set




r_{A}(S)=\max_{C\preceq A}|C\cap S|\qquad(S\subseteq[N]).



This is the maximum number of boxes of A that can lie in
rows of S after allowed moves. It is the rank function of the
Schubert matroid indexed by A. The functions r_{A}, over
nonempty A\subseteq[N], are linearly independent by
[5, Theorem 1.4].




Let m_{D}(A) be the multiplicity of a nonempty column A in
D. Every monomial in \chi_{D} is the weight of an allowed
choice of rows. For fixed S, choose a maximizing allowed
row set in each column. The resulting product of minors is
nonzero, so its weight occurs in \chi_{D}.
Grouping equal columns gives, for every S\subseteq[N],




\displaystyle\max_{[x^{\beta}]\chi_{D}\neq 0}\sum_{i\in S}\beta_{i}
\displaystyle=\sum_{D_{j}\neq\varnothing}r_{D_{j}}(S)




\displaystyle=\sum_{\varnothing\neq A\subseteq[N]}m_{D}(A)r_{A}(S).



Linear independence of the functions r_{A} therefore determines
every multiplicity m_{D}(A).




These maxima add under multiplication of polynomials with
nonnegative coefficients. Applying this to the asserted product
identity and using linear independence once more gives
m_{D}(A)=\sum_{t}m_{E^{(t)}}(A) for every nonempty A,
as required.
∎







3. Factorization by column blocks




We prove that a partition of the columns gives a factorization
exactly when no interaction edge joins different blocks.
Together with Proposition 2.1, this yields the
unique finest factorization into nonconstant diagram
characters, up to order.




3.1. Block factorization




For a set J of column indices, write D[J] for the
subdiagram consisting of those columns.



Theorem 3.1.



Let \pi be a partition of the indices of the nonempty
columns of D. Then




\chi_{D}\leq_{\mathrm{coeff}}\prod_{J\in\pi}\chi_{D[J]},



with equality precisely when no edge of \Gamma_{D} joins
distinct blocks, or equivalently when each block is a union
of connected components. If such an edge exists, at least one
coefficient is strictly smaller.





We begin with two blocks. Even if their minors share matrix
entries, the evaluations of the two blocks can be prescribed
independently when there is no edge between them. It suffices
to work with upper unitriangular matrices.



Lemma 3.2.



For every fixed diagram D, setting y_{jj}=1 for all j
defines an injective linear map on M_{D}.




Proof.



Put




d_{j}=\#\{u:j\in D_{u}\}.



Every monomial y^{\nu} occurring in M_{D} has total degree
d_{j} in the variables y_{1j},\ldots,y_{jj}. After setting
y_{jj}=1, the omitted exponent is still determined by




\nu_{jj}=d_{j}-\sum_{i<j}\nu_{ij}.



Thus two distinct monomials cannot become the same monomial
after the substitution. Hence a nonzero element of M_{D}
remains nonzero after this substitution, proving injectivity.
∎




Lemma 3.3.



Let P and Q be two blocks of columns with no interaction
edge between them. For any upper unitriangular matrices U,V
there is an upper unitriangular matrix W satisfying




f(W)=f(U)\quad(f\in M_{P}),\qquad g(W)=g(V)\quad(g\in M_{Q}).






Proof.



Set W_{jj}=1 and W_{ij}=0 for i>j.
For each j, determine the entries above the diagonal
in column j of W recursively, in the order
i=j-1,j-2,\ldots,1.
We choose each of these entries according to the following
rules. If a column of P
contains j but not i<j, impose




(U^{-1}W)_{ij}=0.



If the corresponding condition comes from Q, require
(V^{-1}W)_{ij}=0 instead. Both conditions could apply only if
columns of P and Q contained j but omitted i, which
would give an interaction edge. If neither condition applies,
take W_{ij}=0. Matrix multiplication gives




(U^{-1}W)_{ij}=W_{ij}+(U^{-1})_{ij}+\sum_{i<\ell<j}(U^{-1})_{i\ell}W_{\ell j}.



The coefficient of W_{ij} is 1, and the other entries of W
have already been chosen farther down column j. The same
holds with V in place of U, so the construction is well
defined.




Now fix a column A in P and write H=U^{-1}W.
For j\in A and i\notin A, we have H_{ij}=0:
by triangularity if i>j, and by construction if i<j.
The matrix H_{A}^{A} is upper unitriangular. Hence, for every
C\preceq A,




\displaystyle W_{A}^{C}
\displaystyle=U_{A}^{C}H_{A}^{A},



\displaystyle\det(W_{A}^{C})
\displaystyle=\det(U_{A}^{C})\det(H_{A}^{A})=\det(U_{A}^{C}).



Choose one allowed minor for each column of P.
Multiplying the corresponding equalities shows that the
resulting product has the same value at W and U.
These products span M_{P}, so f(W)=f(U) for every
f\in M_{P}. The same argument gives g(W)=g(V) for
every g\in M_{Q}.
∎




Example 3.4.



Take A=\{1,3\} and B=\{2,3\}. They both contain row 3,
but there is no interaction edge between them. For




\begin{pmatrix}1&a&c\\
0&1&b\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},



the allowed minors are b,1 for A and ab-c,a,1
for B. The two sets of minors share the entry b. Nevertheless,
we can prescribe b=\mu for A and a=\lambda,
ab-c=\rho for B by taking c=\lambda\mu-\rho.
For example, \mu=2, \lambda=3, and \rho=5 give
c=1.




Proof of Theorem 3.1.



The claim is immediate for a single block. For two blocks P,Q,
choose homogeneous bases f_{1},\ldots,f_{r} of M_{P} and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{s} of M_{Q}. Their pairwise products span
M_{D}. In each row degree, the coefficient of \chi_{P}\chi_{Q}
counts the corresponding pairs (i,j), whereas the
coefficient of \chi_{D} is the dimension of the span of
their products.
It follows that




\chi_{D}\leq_{\mathrm{coeff}}\chi_{P}\chi_{Q}.

(3.1)


Equality holds if and only if the products f_{i}g_{j}
are linearly independent.




Suppose there is no edge from P to Q, and consider
a linear relation




\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{s}c_{ij}f_{i}(W)g_{j}(W)=0



holding identically for upper triangular matrices W.
For arbitrary upper unitriangular matrices U,V, apply
this identity to a matrix W supplied by
Lemma 3.3, with
f_{i}(W)=f_{i}(U) and g_{j}(W)=g_{j}(V) for all i,j.
This gives




\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{s}c_{ij}f_{i}(U)g_{j}(V)=0.



By Lemma 3.2, both bases remain linearly
independent on upper unitriangular matrices. Fixing V and
varying U therefore gives \sum_{j=1}^{s}c_{ij}g_{j}(V)=0
for every i. The independence of the g_{j}, as V varies,
then gives c_{ij}=0 for all i,j.
The products f_{i}g_{j} are therefore linearly independent,
so equality holds in (3.1).




Conversely, suppose that a column A of P interacts
with a column B of Q. By (2.2), there is
a nontrivial homogeneous linear relation among products
of an allowed minor of A and an allowed minor of B.




For every indexed column other than the chosen columns
A and B, fix its principal minor. Multiply each allowed
minor of A by the fixed minors from P, and each allowed
minor of B by those from Q. The resulting families lie
in M_{P} and M_{Q}, respectively. They consist of homogeneous
polynomials and are linearly independent: the allowed minors
of each column are independent, and multiplication by a fixed
nonzero polynomial preserves this property. Extend these
families to homogeneous bases of M_{P} and M_{Q}.




Multiplying the original relation by all the fixed minors
gives a nontrivial relation among products indexed by distinct
pairs of basis elements. Hence equality
in (3.1) is impossible, and the
inequality is strict in at least one row degree.




For more than two blocks, iterate this result.
Without crossing edges every split gives equality. Otherwise,
let P consist of the columns in a block of \pi containing
one endpoint of a crossing edge, and let Q consist of the
columns in the other blocks.
The first split is strict. Applying the same inequality
within Q gives




\chi_{D}<_{\mathrm{coeff}}\chi_{P}\chi_{Q}\leq_{\mathrm{coeff}}\prod_{J\in\pi}\chi_{D[J]}.



Here <_{\mathrm{coeff}} means coefficientwise inequality
with strict inequality in at least one coefficient.
The description in terms of connected components follows.
∎






3.2. The finest factorization




Recall that a factorization is finer than another when the
latter is obtained by grouping factors of the former.



Corollary 3.5.



Let K_{1},\ldots,K_{s} be the connected components of \Gamma_{D}.
By Proposition 2.1 and Theorem 3.1,
the factorizations of \chi_{D} into nonconstant diagram
characters are precisely those obtained by grouping these
components. Hence




\chi_{D}=\prod_{t=1}^{s}\chi_{D[K_{t}]}



is the unique finest such factorization, up to order.




Example 3.6.



For the columns A,B in Example 3.4,
take D=(A,A,B,B), as shown in Figure 2. The two copies
of A interact, as do the two copies of B, but no A
interacts with a B. The dashed rounded boxes mark the two
components, with column indices \{1,2\} and \{3,4\}.
The component factorization is




\chi_{D}=\chi_{(A,A)}\chi_{(B,B)},



where

Here s_{(2,2)} is the Schur polynomial. Since each
component contains an edge, neither factor splits further
into the characters of its two columns.

4. Products of key polynomials




We apply the criterion for factorization by column blocks to
key products. We first characterize the components whose
characters are single key polynomials, and then translate
the component condition for Rothe diagrams into pattern
avoidance.




4.1. The column condition




We begin with the criterion for a single key polynomial.



Remark 4.1.



A diagram character \chi_{E} is a key polynomial if and only
if its nonempty columns form an inclusion chain. Indeed, the
columns of a chain can be arranged as a skyline, and the
converse follows from Proposition 2.1. The
indexing composition \alpha is given by the row counts of
E, so \chi_{E}=\kappa_{\alpha}.




Example 4.2.



A key polynomial can factor further. Let D=\operatorname{Sky}(2,0,1).
Its columns are D_{1}=\{1,3\} and D_{2}=\{1\}.
They satisfy D_{1}\supsetneq D_{2} but do not interact.
In Figure 3, the two dashed rounded boxes
mark the singleton components, and the inclusion sign
compares the two row sets. Hence




\kappa_{(2,0,1)}=x_{1}^{2}(x_{2}+x_{3})=\kappa_{(1)}\kappa_{(1,0,1)}.










12312D_{1}D_{2}\{1,3\}\{1\}\supsetneq\operatorname{Sky}(2,0,1)\Gamma_{\operatorname{Sky}(2,0,1)}
Figure 3. A skyline diagram with two isolated columns.



Theorem 4.3.



The character \chi_{D} is a key product if and only if the columns
in every connected component of \Gamma_{D} form an inclusion chain.
When this holds, let K_{1},\ldots,K_{s} be these components,
and let \alpha_{i}^{(t)} count
the boxes in row i of D[K_{t}]. Then




\chi_{D}=\prod_{t=1}^{s}\kappa_{\alpha^{(t)}}.



This is the unique finest key factorization, up to order;
indexing compositions are understood up to trailing zero parts.




Proof.



A key factor is a skyline character, so its columns form
an inclusion chain by Remark 4.1.
Proposition 2.1 and
Corollary 3.5 show that a connected component
cannot be split among different factors. Thus every component must be a
chain. Conversely, when the components are chains, arrange
the columns of each as a skyline. Their characters are the
claimed keys, and the component factorization is the unique
finest one.
∎






4.2. The pattern condition




Even if every edge joins comparable columns, the columns in a
connected component need not form an inclusion chain. Consider w=142635, whose nonempty Rothe columns are




D_{2}=\{2\},\qquad D_{3}=\{2,4\},\qquad D_{5}=\{4\}.



Figure 4 shows the path D_{2}-D_{3}-D_{5}.
The dashed rounded box encloses its single component.
Its neighboring columns satisfy
D_{2}\subsetneq D_{3}\supsetneq D_{5}, but the endpoints
are incomparable.

Lemma 4.4.



Every component of \Gamma_{D} whose columns do not form an inclusion chain contains either an edge between incomparable columns, or a path A-B-C with incomparable endpoints and




B\subseteq A\cap C\qquad\text{or}\qquad B\supseteq A\cup C.






Proof.



Choose incomparable columns A,C having minimum graph
distance in their component and take a shortest path between
them. Distance one gives the first alternative. Otherwise,
every other pair of vertices on the path has smaller graph
distance than A,C and is therefore comparable by minimality.
In particular, the internal columns form a chain, and each
is comparable with both A and C.
If an internal column B satisfied A\subseteq B\subseteq C
or C\subseteq B\subseteq A, then A,C would be comparable,
a contradiction. Thus every internal column is either
contained in both endpoints or contains both.




These two types cannot both occur. Indeed, consecutive
internal columns of different types would give
L\subseteq A\cap C and H\supseteq A\cup C with an edge
LH. A row pair witnessing this edge is vacant in H
and occupied in L, hence vacant and occupied, respectively,
in both A and C. This would make A,C adjacent.




If the internal columns are all contained in A\cap C,
choose the largest one, B. Since B\subseteq A\cap C,
the row pair witnessing the first edge of the path also
witnesses AB; the last edge similarly witnesses BC.
If all internal columns contain A\cup C, choose the
smallest one instead. The same reasoning, with inclusions
reversed, gives AB and BC.
∎





A diagram is northwest if boxes at the northeast
and southwest corners of a rectangle force a box at the
northwest corner. Equivalently, when A lies to the left
of C, every row of C\setminus A is below every row of
A.



Lemma 4.5.



Let D be a northwest diagram. A component of \Gamma_{D} fails to be an inclusion chain if and only if it contains either an edge between incomparable columns, or a path of length two A-B-C with nonadjacent incomparable endpoints and




B\supseteq A\cup C.






Proof.



By Lemma 4.4, it suffices to exclude
B\subseteq A\cap C when the incomparable endpoints
are nonadjacent. Put A to the left of C, and let
i<j witness AB.
Then j\in B\subseteq C, whereas i\notin A. If i
were in C, it would belong to C\setminus A above
the box at row j of A, contrary to the northwest
property. Thus i\notin C as well. The same pair
i<j witnesses AC, so the endpoints cannot be
nonadjacent.
∎




Remark 4.6.



Rothe diagrams are northwest. For j<k, every row in
D_{k}(w)\setminus D_{j}(w) lies below row w^{-1}(j),
while every row of D_{j}(w) lies above it. Consequently,
the components of \Gamma_{D(w)} satisfy the
characterization in Lemma 4.5.




Proof of Theorem 1.1.



The factorization formula and its uniqueness follow from
Theorem 4.3. It remains to prove the
avoidance criterion and minimality.




Suppose \mathfrak{S}_{w} is not a key product. By
Theorem 4.3 and Remark 4.6,
there are incomparable Rothe columns D_{j},D_{k}, j<k,
joined either by an edge or by a path of length two through
a column containing both. In each case, we find a subsequence
of w whose pattern lies in \mathcal{B}.




Choose entries x,y with w^{-1}(x)\in D_{j}\setminus D_{k}
and w^{-1}(y)\in D_{k}\setminus D_{j}. Then x>j and
y>k, with x preceding j and y preceding k.
Since y>k>j but w^{-1}(y)\notin D_{j}, y follows j;
hence x precedes k and, since w^{-1}(x)\notin D_{k},

5. Products of elementary symmetric polynomials




The polynomial e_{b}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{a}) is the sum of the
squarefree monomials of degree b in x_{1},\ldots,x_{a}.
Since the factors have nonnegative coefficients, no variable
appearing in a factor can disappear from the product. We may
therefore work in a common finite alphabet.



Lemma 5.1.



For 1\leq b\leq a,




F_{[a-b+1,a]}=e_{b}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{a}).



Conversely, if F_{A}=e_{b}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{a}) with A\neq\varnothing, then A=[a-b+1,a].




Proof.



If C=\{c_{1}<\cdots<c_{b}\}\subseteq[a], then
c_{i}\leq a-b+i. Hence the allowed row sets for
[a-b+1,a] are precisely the subsets of [a] of size b.
Formula (2.1) gives the identity.




Conversely, equality forces the degrees and supports to
agree. Their greatest row sets in Gale order are A and
[a-b+1,a], respectively, so these sets are equal.
∎




Proposition 5.2.



The character \chi_{D} is an elementary product if and only if
every nonempty column of D is an interval and \Gamma_{D}
is edgeless.




Proof.



By Lemma 5.1, every elementary factor
is the character of an interval column. If their product is
\chi_{D}, Proposition 2.1 shows that the
interval columns, with multiplicities, are exactly the
nonempty columns of D. Thus
\chi_{D}=\prod_{D_{j}\neq\varnothing}F_{D_{j}}.
Applying Theorem 3.1 to the partition into
singleton column blocks shows that \Gamma_{D} is edgeless.




Conversely, if \Gamma_{D} is edgeless,
Theorem 3.1 gives
\chi_{D}=\prod_{D_{j}\neq\varnothing}F_{D_{j}}.
By Lemma 5.1, these factors are elementary
when the columns are intervals.
∎




Lemma 5.3.



For w\in S_{n}, the graph \Gamma_{D(w)} is edgeless if and only if w avoids 1423 and 1432, and every nonempty column of D(w) is an interval if and only if w avoids 3142 and 4132.




Proof.



Suppose D_{j},D_{k} interact, with j<k.
By the definition of the Rothe columns, a witness consists
of rows w^{-1}(u)<w^{-1}(v), where the entries u,v
occur before j,k and satisfy u<j<k<v.
Depending on which of j,k occurs first,
the selected entries form 1423 or 1432. Conversely,
either pattern provides just such a witness.




For the interval condition, suppose that a nonempty column
D_{j} is not an interval. It then has a gap, a vacant
row between two occupied rows. Let u,v,z be the entries
of w in these rows, from top to bottom. By the definition of D_{j}, the occupied rows
lie above row w^{-1}(j) and contain values larger than
j, while the intervening vacant row contains a value
smaller than j. Hence
w^{-1}(u)<w^{-1}(v)<w^{-1}(z)<w^{-1}(j) and
v<j<\min(u,z). The selected entries occur as
u\,v\,z\,j, giving pattern 3142 or 4132 according
to whether u<z or z<u. Either pattern conversely
gives a gap in the column indexed by the last selected entry.
∎





The edgeless condition is equivalent to the equality
criterion of Fan and Guo [3]. The characterization using
interval columns is due to Liu, Mészáros, and
St. Dizier [10, Proposition 2.3].



Proof of Theorem 1.2.



Proposition 5.2 and
Lemma 5.3 give the avoidance
criterion. The factors are those in (1.2)
by Lemma 5.1. By Proposition 2.1,
the interval columns are determined with multiplicities.
Each such column A fixes b=|A| and a=\max A,
so the factorization is unique up to order.
∎

Allowing repeated initial alphabets matters. A
standard elementary monomial (SEM) has the form




\prod_{i\geq 1}e_{a_{i}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i}),\qquad 0\leq a_{i}\leq i,



where e_{0}=1 and all but finitely many a_{i} vanish.
Woodruff [14, Definition 2 and Theorem 4]
characterized single-SEM Schubert polynomials by avoidance
of 312 and 1432. In contrast,
\mathfrak{S}_{312}=x_{1}^{2}=e_{1}(x_{1})^{2} is an elementary product
that uses the alphabet \{x_{1}\} twice.
