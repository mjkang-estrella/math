                                                    Abstract. Let a = {an } be a non-increasing sequence of positive real num-
                                                    bers adding up to 1. Cutting out of the unit interval disjoint open gaps of
                                                    lengths a1 , a2 , . . . , one for each term, leaves a compact set of Lebesgue mea-
                                                    sure zero, and since the gaps can be placed in infinitely many ways, there are
                                                    infinitely many such sets. We call them the complementary sets of a and write
                                                    Ca for the family of all of them. Every set in Ca is compact and totally dis-
                                                    connected, and all of them share the same gap lengths; what distinguishes one
                                                    from another is only the arrangement of the gaps. Among them, the perfect
                                                    sets are exactly the Cantor sets with those gaps, and they form a distinguished
                                                    subclass.
                                                        The central question of this survey is what such a rearrangement can change
                                                    and what it cannot. The answer depends sharply on the notion of size: the box
                                                    dimensions are constant on Ca , while the Hausdorff dimension attains every
                                                    value in a closed interval whose endpoints are realised by two distinguished
                                                    members, the Cantor set Ca built by placing the gaps in a Cantor-like pat-
                                                    tern, and the countable decreasing rearrangement Da . We survey how this
                                                    dichotomy persists, and how the roles of Ca and Da are exchanged, across the
                                                    intermediate dimensions of Falconer, Fraser and Kempton, the Assouad-type
                                                    and Φ-dimensions, and the Hausdorff and packing h-measures associated with
                                                    a general dimension function; we also describe the almost sure behaviour of
                                                    a randomly rearranged complementary set, where a sharp threshold in the
                                                    depth of the scales examined separates two incompatible regimes. A final part
                                                    restricts attention to the Cantor subclass and turns to the dynamical descrip-
                                                    tion of the sets Ca : the decay conditions under which Ca is the attractor of
                                                    an iterated function system, the identification of the family Cp = C{n−p }
                                                    as an optimally smooth hyperbolic attractor through Sullivan’s scale func-
                                                    tion, and the consequences for the arithmetic sums Cp + Cq via thickness and
                                                    transversality methods.

                                  1. Introduction
    Start with a non-increasing sequence a = {an } of positive real numbers adding
up to 1, and remove from the unit interval [0, 1] disjoint open gaps of lengths
a1 , a2 , . . . , one for each term of the sequence. Whatever is left is a compact set of
Lebesgue measure zero that contains the endpoints 0 and 1. The sequence, however,
does not tell us which set we get: the same lengths can be laid out along [0, 1] in
infinitely many ways, and each arrangement produces a different set. All these sets
together form the family Ca of complementary sets, or cut-out sets, of a. Nothing
is lost by this point of view: an infinite compact subset of [0, 1] of measure zero
containing the endpoints is determined by the open intervals of its complement,
so it is a complementary set of exactly one such sequence, the sequence of lengths
of those intervals. This family is the object of the survey. Every member of Ca
is compact, totally disconnected and null, but it need not be perfect. The perfect
members are exactly the Cantor sets with gap lengths a; they form a distinguished
subclass of Ca , and we shall restrict to it only when the mathematics requires it.
Two members of Ca are canonical. Placing the gaps in a Cantor-like pattern gives
a perfect set Ca , the Cantor set associated with a; placing them in decreasing order
from one endpoint gives a countable set Da with a single accumulation point, which
is not a Cantor set at all. Both have exactly the same gap lengths, and yet, as
soon as one measures them, they could hardly be more different.
    The question that organises this survey is therefore the following: given the gap
lengths, what is determined for every complementary set in Ca , and what is left free
to the rearrangement? The answer depends entirely on which notion of size one
uses, and the resulting picture is surprisingly varied.
    Some quantities are rigid. Falconer [17] observed that all sets in Ca have compa-
rable covering numbers at every scale, so the upper and lower box dimensions are
constant on the whole family: no rearrangement, perfect or not, can change them.
Others vary as much as they possibly can. Besicovitch and Taylor [10] proved that
the Hausdorff dimension of the sets in Ca sweeps the full interval [0, dimH Ca ], with
Ca maximal and Da minimal, and that every intermediate value is attained. This
contrast—one dimension constant, another taking every admissible value—is the
starting point of everything that follows.
    Between these two extremes lie the intermediate dimensions of Falconer, Fraser
and Kempton, obtained by restricting the diameters of a cover to a geometric
window governed by a parameter θ. Angelini and Molter [2] computed them for Ca
and Da and showed that these two sets again bracket the whole family Ca , with the
full interval attained for every fixed θ: a θ-parametrised version of the Besicovitch–
Taylor theorem, degenerating to it at θ = 0 and to the rigid box dimension at
θ = 1.
    Assouad-type dimensions reverse the picture. They record the extreme local
behaviour of a set rather than its global size, and for the Assouad dimension and
its upper refinements it is Ca that minimises and Da that maximises over Ca , while
for the lower Assouad dimension Da is minimal and Ca maximal. García, Hare
and Mendivil established this for dimA and dimL [23] and then, through the family
                            SAME GAPS, DIFFERENT SETS                                3



of Φ-dimensions [24], for a whole one-parameter range interpolating towards the
box dimensions. The same authors [25] identified a sharp threshold in the depth of
the scales examined, at log | log x|/| log x|, separating two incompatible almost sure
behaviours of a randomly chosen complementary set in Ca : below it the Assouad
dimensions are almost surely extremal regardless of Ca , above it they almost surely
agree with those of Ca .
   A further refinement replaces the exponent s by a general gauge h. Here the
distinguished Cantor set Ca comes to the fore. Cabrelli, Mendivil, Molter and
Shonkwiler [14] showed that every sequence admits a gauge ha making Ca an ha -set,
even when no power function does, and García, Molter and Scotto [26] characterised
which gauges measure Ca and exhibited Cantor sets with the same dimension
that only a gauge can tell apart. Returning to the whole family, the reversal
appears once more at this finer level: Hare, Mendivil and Zuberman [27] proved
that, up to multiplicative constants, Ca has the maximal Hausdorff h-measure
and, simultaneously, the minimal packing h-premeasure among all complementary
sets in Ca , and that the packing measures attained across the family form either
{0} or all of [0, ∞], with nothing in between; moreover, prescribed values of these
measures can be realised by perfect sets, that is, within the Cantor subclass. Not
every compact set, however, is measured by some gauge, and [13] shows that this
failure is not a rarity.
   The recurring theme is that Ca and Da are extremal in Ca , exactly or up to
constants, for every notion of size considered here, but which of the two is the
maximum depends on whether the notion is global or local, covering-based or
packing-based. In most cases where a full description of the attained values is
available it takes the same shape: a closed interval with endpoints at those two
sets. The packing measures are the notable exception, with the all-or-nothing
dichotomy described above.
   So far the sequence a has been treated as a purely combinatorial datum, and
the results have concerned the whole family Ca . The sequence can also be read
dynamically, and for this the Cantor subclass, and in fact the single set Ca , is
the natural setting. When the gap lengths decay with enough regularity, the Can-
tor set Ca turns out to be the attractor of an iterated function system, and the
smoothness of that system is governed precisely by the rate of decay: García [21]
showed that the sets Cp associated with an = n−p are C 1+1/p -hyperbolic, with
1/p the optimal Hölder exponent, and characterised which central Cantor sets ad-
mit realising systems of a given smoothness. This is not a separate story. The
decay condition that makes Ca dynamically realisable is a strengthening of the
condition 0 < Hs (Ca ) ≤ P0s (Ca ) < ∞ studied in Section 3, so the dynamically
defined sets are among the best-behaved members of the theory developed there.
In the other direction, the dynamical description gives information the sequence
alone does not: through Sullivan’s scale function, a complete smooth invariant, Cp
is C 1+1/p -conjugate to a self-similar set, and this conjugacy transports the whole
theory of Bernoulli convolutions to a genuinely non-linear family.
4           NICOLÁS ANGELINI, CARLOS CABRELLI, AND URSULA MOLTER



   That transport is what makes the last topic of the survey accessible. Whether
the arithmetic sum C + C ′ of two Cantor sets on the line contains an interval, or
has positive measure, is a question with a long history in dynamics, going back to
Newhouse’s work on homoclinic bifurcations and to the Palis conjecture. For the
family {Cp } both the thickness criterion and the transversality method of Peres and
Solomyak can be adapted, giving dimH (Cp + Cq ) = min{1/p + 1/q, 1} for almost
every p with q fixed: an exact non-linear counterpart of the Peres–Shmerkin formula
for self-similar sets.
   The survey is organised as follows. Section 2 collects the dimensions and mea-
sures used throughout: the Hausdorff, box, intermediate and Assouad-type dimen-
sions, the Φ-dimensions, and dimension functions with the associated Hausdorff
and packing h-measures. Section 3 introduces the family Ca of complementary
sets, its Cantor subclass and the distinguished members Ca and Da , and treats the
question of what a rearrangement can and cannot change. Section 4 restricts to the
Cantor sets Ca and turns to their dynamical description, and Section 5 to sums
of Cantor sets and thickness. The two halves are largely independent: a reader
interested only in rearrangements may stop at the end of Section 3, and Sections 4
and 5 use from it only the p-Cantor sets of Example 3.2, Theorem 3.3 and the
Hausdorff and packing h-measures of Section 3.3. Proofs are kept to a minimum,
and open problems are stated where the picture is incomplete.

   Finally, E is called an h-set if 0 < Hh (E) < ∞, and h is subadditive if h(x+y) ≤
C(h(x) + h(y)) for all x, y and some constant C.
   Not every set is an h-set; the simplest example is countable: if E = {1/n :
n ∈ N} ∪ {0}, then Hh is non-atomic for every h ∈ D, since h(x) → 0 as x →
0, so countable additivity forces Hh (E) = 0 for every gauge. This example is
unsatisfying, however, because E is countable and hence of Hausdorff dimension 0
for trivial reasons. A genuinely fractal example is due to Best [11].
   The same dichotomy appears for sets arising in Diophantine approximation.
Olsen and Renfro [33] located the exact cut point between the gauges for which the
set L of Liouville numbers has infinite (indeed non-σ-finite) Hausdorff h-measure
and those for which it has measure zero, so L is not dimensional; more recently,
Moreira, Erazo and Angelini [30] obtained the analogous description for the irra-
tionals whose Lagrange value equals 3, and showed that L is strictly the larger of the
two on this finer scale: there are gauges h with Hh (L) = ∞ and Hh (k −1 (3)) = 0.
   Cabrelli, Darji and Molter [13] placed this question in a broader setting by
allowing measures other than Hausdorff measures. A compact set M in a Polish
group is H-visible if it is an h-set for some h ∈ D, and visible if 0 < µ(M ) < ∞ for
some translation-invariant Borel measure µ, not necessarily of Hausdorff type; M
is strongly invisible if every translation-invariant Borel measure either vanishes on
M or fails to be σ-finite there. Not being an h-set for any h is thus weaker than
being strongly invisible, and Best’s example is not known to be strongly invisible.
Davies had already exhibited a strongly invisible compact subset of R (see [15]);
Cabrelli, Darji and Molter extend the construction to every uncountable abelian
Polish group.

3.4. Hausdorff and packing h-measures of rearrangements. The results of
Section 3.3 concern the single set Ca . We now turn to the whole family, where
the natural question is how Hh and P h vary across Ca for a fixed gauge. Hare,
Mendivil and Zuberman [27] extended both the Besicovitch–Taylor extremality
statement and its packing dual to arbitrary h ∈ D, and found that the two behave
in opposite ways.
   The statements below are sharper than mere attainability of values: they locate
the realising sets inside Ca .
Proposition 3.8 ([27]). For h ∈ D, there is a constant c, depending only on the
doubling constant of h, such that Hh (E) ≤ c Hh (Ca ) for every E ∈ Ca . That is, Ca
has, up to a constant, the maximal Hausdorff h-measure within Ca , generalising
the Besicovitch–Taylor extremality result beyond power functions gs . When Ca is
a middle-τ Cantor set the constant may be taken to be c = 1, so Ca is then exactly
maximal; in general the sharp constant and the identity of a maximiser (if any
exists) remain open.
Theorem 3.9 ([27]). Let h ≺ ha , where ha is the associated dimension function
of Ca , and let γ ≥ 0. There is a perfect set E ∈ Ca with Hh (E) = γ. Moreover the
set {E ∈ Ca : Hh (E) = γ} is dense in Ca with respect to the Hausdorff metric ρ.
   Theorem 3.9 sharpens the Besicovitch–Taylor attainability statement—that any
β ∈ [0, α(a)] is realised as dimH E for some E ∈ Ca —to the level of h-measures
rather than merely dimension values, and it guarantees that the realising set can
always be chosen perfect, and in fact densely so, within (Ca , ρ).
   The dual statement for packing measure exhibits the opposite extremal pattern,
dual to Proposition 3.8.

                                6. Concluding remarks
   A single pattern runs through the results collected here. For every notion of
size considered, the two canonical members of Ca are extremal, exactly or up to
constants, and in most cases where a complete description of the attained values
exists it is a closed interval with endpoints at those two sets; the packing measures,
with their all-or-nothing dichotomy, are the exception. What changes from one
notion to the next is which of them sits at which end.
   For the Hausdorff dimension, and more generally for the Hausdorff h-measures,
the maximum is at Ca and the minimum at the countable set Da ; the same holds
for the intermediate dimensions at every θ ∈ (0, 1], and for the lower Assouad and
lower Φ-dimensions. For the upper Assouad and upper Φ-dimensions the order
is reversed, and so it is for the packing h-premeasure: Ca is simultaneously the
maximiser of Hh and the minimiser of P0h within the family. The reversal is not
an accident of proof technique. Covering-based and global quantities reward the
even distribution of gaps that the Cantor construction produces, while packing-
based and local ones reward the concentration that the decreasing rearrangement
produces, and Ca and Da are the extreme cases of each.
   Against this variability stands the rigidity of the box dimensions, which take the
same value on every member of Ca . The intermediate dimensions measure exactly
how far a given notion sits from that rigid end: they run from the full-range
behaviour of dimH at θ = 0 to the constancy of dimB at θ = 1, and Theorem 3.18
shows that for each θ every value between the two extremes is attained, the interval
collapsing to a point only at θ = 1.
26            NICOLÁS ANGELINI, CARLOS CABRELLI, AND URSULA MOLTER



    Several questions remain open. The most substantial, stated as Question 3.19,
asks not which values but which functions of θ arise within a fixed family: the
ambient answer for arbitrary compact sets is known from [7], and the profiles ψs
of Section 3 give a one-parameter family of realised examples, but between these
lies a genuine gap. For the Φ-dimensions, the almost sure behaviour in the critical
regime Φ ≍ log | log x|/| log x| is not covered by the dichotomy of Theorem 3.22.
In the Hausdorff–packing duality, the sharp constants and the extremal sets are
known on the Hausdorff side only for middle-τ Cantor sets, and on the packing
side not even for the middle-third Cantor set. Finally, on the dynamical side, the
almost-everywhere results for Cp + Cq cannot at present be made uniform in both
parameters, and the decay of υ  cp —the non-linear counterpart of the Erdős–Salem
theory for Bernoulli convolutions—is entirely open.
