                                                Abstract. Given a positive, non-increasing sequence a with sum
                                                1, we consider the family Ca of closed subsets of [0, 1] whose com-
                                                plementary open intervals have lengths given by the terms of a.
                                                We study which functions θ 7→ dim θ E arise as the upper inter-
                                                mediate dimensions of sets E ∈ Ca . Under a natural condition on
                                                the decay of a, we show that these are exactly the functions in the
                                                class introduced by Banaji and Rutar that take the value dimB Ca
                                                at θ = 1 and are bounded above by the upper intermediate dimen-
                                                sions of the decreasing Cantor set Ca . We also show that the lower
                                                bound given by the countable set Da is rigid: if it is attained at
                                                one value of θ, then it is attained at all larger values.

                           1. Introduction
  The θ-intermediate dimensions were introduced by Falconer, Fraser
and Kempton [15] as a continuum of dimensions that interpolates be-
tween the Hausdorff dimension and the box-counting dimensions. For
θ ∈ [0, 1], the lower and upper θ-intermediate dimensions dim θ and
dim θ are defined by restricting the diameters of the covering sets to
an interval of the form [δ 1/θ , δ] (see Definition 2.1). Thus θ = 0 corre-
sponds to the Hausdorff dimension and θ = 1 to the lower and upper
box dimensions. For every bounded set E ⊂ Rd ,
dimH E ≤ dim θ E ≤ dim θ E ≤ dimB E           and      dim θ E ≤ dimB E,
and both functions of θ are non-decreasing and continuous on (0, 1].
These dimensions have been studied in many settings, for instance
for fractional Brownian images [8, 16], orthogonal projections [9, 3],
elliptical polynomial spirals [10]. There are also many generalizations,
for instance [5] for intermediate dimensions in metric spaces and [1] for
intermediate dimensions of measures.
   A natural problem is to determine which functions arise as interme-
diate dimensions. This problem was solved by Banaji and Rutar [6].
For 0 ≤ λ ≤ α ≤ d, they introduced the class H(λ, α) of non-decreasing
functions h : [0, 1] → [λ, α] which are continuous on (0, 1] and whose
growth is controlled by the Dini condition
                        (h(θ) − λ)(α − h(θ))

They proved the following. As F ranges over the bounded subsets of Rd
with lower Assouad dimension λ and Assouad dimension α, the pairs
(dim θ F, dim θ F ) are exactly the pairs (h1 , h2 ) of functions in H(λ, α)
with h1 ≤ h2 and h1 (0) = h2 (0) (see Theorem 4.2). In this paper we
study the analogous problem within a classical family of subsets of the
line: the complementary sets of a sequence.                      P
   Let a = {an } be a positive, non-increasing sequence with n an = 1.
The class Ca of complementary sets of a consists of all closed sets
E ⊂ [0, 1] whose complement in [0, 1] is a disjoint union of open inter-
vals whose lengths are exactly the terms of a. This class contains sets
of very different nature. Two of them play a central role. The first is
the countable set Da , a decreasing sequence of points whose consecu-
tive gaps are a1 , a2 , . . . . The second is the decreasing Cantor set Ca ,
obtained by removing the gaps in decreasing order along a binary Can-
tor construction (see Section 3). The question of which values a given
dimension takes on Ca has a long history. For the Hausdorff dimension
        INTERMEDIATE DIMENSIONS OF COMPLEMENTARY SETS                     3

it goes back to Besicovitch and Taylor [7], and the set of attainable
values is the closed interval [0, dimH Ca ] [7, 11, 18]; related questions
for packing measures were considered in [19]. In contrast, the lower
and upper box dimensions do not depend on the arrangement of the
gaps, so each of them takes a single value on Ca [13, Chapter 3.2]. For
the Assouad and lower Assouad dimensions, Garcı́a, Hare and Men-
divil [17] showed that Ca and Da are extremal. They also showed that,
under suitable decay conditions on a, the attainable values form the
intervals [dimA Ca , 1] and [0, dimL Ca ], respectively.
   The intermediate dimensions of complementary sets were studied
in [4] for each fixed θ. There it was shown that Da and Ca are again
extremal: for every E ∈ Ca ,
                     dim θ Da ≤ dim θ E ≤ dim θ Ca ,
where the upper bound requires a mild assumption on a, and the analo-
gous inequalities hold for the lower intermediate dimensions. Moreover,
dim θ Da = θB/(1 − (1 − θ)B) with B = dimB Ca . This is the smallest
value allowed by the bounds of Banaji and Rutar for a set with upper
box dimension B. Finally, it was proved that every value between these
two bounds is attained for each fixed θ, both for the upper and for the
lower intermediate dimensions, provided that

    These results describe, for each fixed θ, the set of attainable values.
They do not describe how the intermediate dimensions of a single com-
plementary set vary with θ, which is the information captured by the
theorem of Banaji and Rutar. This leads to the question, raised in [4,
Question 1], of describing the families
                   F U (a) := {θ 7→ dim θ E : E ∈ Ca }
and
                    F L (a) := {θ 7→ dim θ E : E ∈ Ca }.
Three necessary conditions for a function h to belong to F U (a) follow
from the results above. By the theorem of Banaji and Rutar, h ∈
H(0, 1). Since all sets in Ca have the same upper box dimension, h(1) =
dimB Ca . Finally, by the extremality of Ca , h ≤ dim θ Ca .

   Our main result shows that, under hypothesis (H), these conditions
are also sufficient. More precisely (Theorem 4.17 and Corollary 4.18),
if a satisfies (H), then F U (a) is exactly the set of functions h ∈ H(0, 1)
such that
     h(1) = dimB Ca       and      h(θ) ≤ dim θ Ca for all θ ∈ [0, 1],
with the usual convention dim 0 = dimH . In particular, F U (a) depends
on a only through the function θ 7→ dim θ Ca , and it is much larger than
the family of functions realized in [4]. For instance, suppose that the
box dimension of Ca exists. Since dimH Ca = dimB Ca , we then have
dim θ Ca = dimB Ca for every θ, and the second condition is automatic.
In this case F U (a) consists of all functions h ∈ H(0, 1) with h(1) =
dimB Ca . By the theorem of Banaji and Rutar, these are exactly the
upper intermediate dimension functions of bounded subsets of R with
this upper box dimension. This applies, for example, to the middle
third Cantor set.
   We also obtain a sharp lower bound with a rigidity property. Ev-
ery h ∈ F U (a) satisfies h ≥ dim θ Da . If h coincides with dim θ Da
at a single θ0 ∈ (0, 1], then it coincides with it on the whole interval
[θ0 , 1] (Proposition 4.8). Moreover, equality at some θ0 ∈ (0, 1) forces
the lower Assouad and Assouad dimensions of the set to be 0 and 1,
respectively (Proposition 4.10). These facts follow from a simple refor-
mulation of the Dini condition defining H(0, 1) as the monotonicity of
the quotient h(θ)/(θ(1 − h(θ))) (Lemma 4.7). This reformulation also
gives a short proof of the lower bound dim θ Da ≤ dim θ E.
   The proof of Theorem 4.17 follows the strategy of Banaji and Ru-
tar [6]. One first constructs, by concatenating mountains and valleys, a
function whose infima over windows of length log(1/θ) reproduce h(θ).
This function is then converted into a sequence of scales for a Cantor-
type set. In our setting, two new difficulties appear. First, the set must
be built using only the terms of a as gaps. This forces its scales to be,
up to constants, at most those of Ca , while the hypothesis h ≤ dim θ Ca
only provides information on infima over windows. We overcome this
by combining the scales produced by the mountains with those of Ca ,
and by placing each mountain on a window where the covering numbers
of Ca are large. Second, the function h may saturate the Dini condi-
tion defining H(0, 1). In that case, any set whose upper intermediate
dimensions are given by h must have lower Assouad dimension 0 and
Assouad dimension 1. The ratios between consecutive scales of such a
set cannot remain bounded away from 0 and from 1/2. This cannot be
achieved with the decreasing Cantor sets used in [4] (see Remark 4.20).
For this reason we work with limit sets of homogeneous binary Cantor
        INTERMEDIATE DIMENSIONS OF COMPLEMENTARY SETS                  5

schemes. The gaps of these schemes are terms of a, but they are not
arranged in decreasing order, and their intermediate dimensions de-
pend only on their scales (Lemma 4.12). The remaining terms of a are
used to build a countable set, which does not increase the dimension
because of the lower bound above.
   The corresponding problem for the lower intermediate dimensions
remains open. The main obstruction is that these dimensions are not
finitely stable. As a consequence, the gluing of the Cantor part and
the countable part used in our construction does not control the lower
intermediate dimensions of the union.
   The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we recall the defini-
tions and basic properties of the dimensions involved. In Section 3 we
recall the main facts about complementary sets, including the results
of [4] that we use. Section 4 contains our results. In Subsection 4.1 we
recall the results of Banaji and Rutar that we need. In Subsection 4.2
we prove the lower bound and its rigidity. In Subsections 4.3 and 4.4 we
develop the homogeneous Cantor schemes and the mountains. Finally,
in Subsection 4.5 we prove the main theorem and the characterization
of F U (a).

4.4. Mountains. By Lemma 4.12, prescribing the upper intermediate
dimensions of a set amounts to constructing a function G, playing
the role of Sρ , whose window infima over intervals of length log(1/θ)
reproduce h(θ). In addition, the function must belong to the class
G(0, 1); that is, its Dini derivatives must lie between −G and 1 − G.
These are the slopes that a function of the form Sρ can have. This
condition allows Lemma 4.4 to produce a sequence of scales ρ with
Sρ close to the constructed function. The basic building block of this
function is a mountain associated with h and a parameter ε > 0. This
is a continuous function with admissible slopes which starts and ends
at h(ε). For every θ ∈ [ε, 1], it contains a window of length log(1/θ)
on which it stays above h(θ), while every such window meets a point
where it is at most h(θ). In the proof of Theorem 4.17, infinitely many
mountains, with ε → 0, are joined by valleys to produce the required
function.

Remark 4.19. The minimum taken in Step 4 of the proof of Theorem
4.17 is useful because, in general, the hypothesis h ≤ dim θ Ca does not
provide the pointwise lower bounds on S1 needed in the construction.
Indeed, let b be the non-increasing rearrangement of a subfamily of the
terms of a. Then bn ≤ an for every n, hence sk (b) ≤ sk (a) for ev-
ery k, and therefore sρ(b) ≤ sρ(a) pointwise, where ρ(b)k = sk (b) and
ρ(a)k = sk (a). Thus, the function governing the covering numbers of
a decreasing Cantor set Cb built from such a subfamily is dominated,
at every scale, by the corresponding function for Ca . By contrast, the
hypothesis h ≤ dim θ Ca only gives a comparison of the limsup of win-
dow infima. The minimum in Step 4 allows the construction to retain
the required lower bounds from S1 on the selected windows while also
enforcing the desired upper bounds through the second scale function.

                             Further Work
   In [6], the authors characterized the functions which arise as the in-
termediate dimensions of bounded subsets of Rn (see Theorem 4.2).
Theorem 4.17 and Corollary 4.18 provide the analogous characteri-
zation for the upper intermediate dimensions of complementary sets,
under hypothesis (H). The corresponding problem for the lower inter-
mediate dimensions remains open.
   Let us briefly describe what is known in that case. Since dim θ Ca =
dimB Ca for all θ, every function h(θ) = dim θ E with E ∈ Ca satisfies
h(θ) ≤ h(1) = dimB Ca . By Theorem 4.2 and Remark 4.3, h ∈ H(0, 1),
and if 0 < dimB Ca < 1, Proposition 4.8 applies to h with B replaced
by dimB Ca , so that dim θ Da ≤ h(θ) with the same rigidity property.
The main difficulty is that the lower intermediate dimensions are not
finitely stable, so the gluing argument of Step 6 in the proof of Theorem
4.17 does not control the lower intermediate dimensions of the union.
