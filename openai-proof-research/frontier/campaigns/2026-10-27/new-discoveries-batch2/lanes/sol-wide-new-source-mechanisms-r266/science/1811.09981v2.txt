                                           On the König–Hall–Egerváry theorem for multidimensional
                                                    matrices and multipartite hypergraphs
                                                                                       A. A. Taranenko∗
                                                                                      December 15, 2020




arXiv:1811.09981v2 [math.CO] 15 Dec 2020
                                                                                             Abstract
                                                      One of possible interpretations of the well-known König–Hall–Egerváry theorem is a full
                                                  characterization of all bipartite graphs extremal for fractional matchings of a given weight
                                                  (or, equivalently, a characterization of (0, 1)-matrices extremal for partial fractional diagonals
                                                  of a given length). In this paper we initiate the study of d-partite d-uniform hypergraphs that
                                                  are extremal for fractional perfect matchings (or, equivalently, d-dimensional (0, 1)-matrices
                                                  that are extremal for polydiagonals). For this purpose, we analyze similarities and differences
                                                  between 2-dimensional and multidimensional cases and put forward a series of questions
                                                  and conjectures on properties of multidimensional extremal matrices (extremal hypergraphs).
                                                  We also prove these conjectures for several parameters and provide a number of supporting
                                                  constructions and examples.


                                           Introduction
                                           The König–Hall–Egerváry theorem is one of the fundamental results in discrete mathematics.
                                           Theorem 0.1 (König–Hall–Egerváry). Let A be a (0, 1)-matrix of order n. The minimum num-
                                           ber of lines (rows and columns) sufficient for covering all unity entries of A is equal to the
                                           maximum number of ones that do not share the same rows and columns.
                                              or
                                              A (0, 1)-matrix A of order n contains a unity partial diagonal of length l if and only if there
                                           are no s × t zero submatrices in A for which s + t = 2n − l + 1.
                                              or
                                              For a bipartite graph, the minimum size of a vertex covering is equal to the maximum size of
                                           a matching.
                                               A variety of proof strategies are known for this theorem. One of them (see, for example,
                                           book [16] by Schrijver) is based on linear programming, fractional matchings, and fractional
                                           vertex covers. The König–Hall–Egerváry theorem may be also expressed as a complete charac-
                                           terization of matrices (or graphs) extremal in some sense.
                                               To illustrate this, we define a polyplex K of order n and of weight W to be a nonnegative
                                           matrix order n in which the sum of entries over each row and each column is not greater than 1
                                           and the sum of all entries is equal to W . In other words, a polyplex is a fractional matching in
                                           a balanced bipartite graph. We call a polyplex of the maximum possible weight (i.e. weight n)
                                           to be a polydiagonal.
                                               We will say that a (0, 1)-matrix A is extremal for polyplexes of weight W if there are no
                                           polyplexes K of weight W or greater with supp(K) ⊆ supp(A) but after adding any entry to the

Then one more equivalent form of the König–Hall–Egerváry theorem is the following: all (0, 1)-
matrices extremal for polyplexes of weight W are exactly the matrices in which zero entries form
an s × t submatrix with s + t = 2n − ⌈W ⌉ + 1.
    For multidimensional matrices, we introduce a polyplex so that it is equivalent to a fractional
matching in a d-partite d-uniform hypergraph with parts of equal sizes (such hypergraphs are
often called balanced). The main aim of the present paper is, using a geometric interpretation
and theory of linear programming, to take several steps towards the generalization of König–
Hall–Egerváry theorem for a multidimensional case and investigate multidimensional matrices
extremal for polyplexes. In other words, we research balanced d-partite d-uniform hypergraphs
extremal for fractional matchings of a given weight.
    There are plenty of results on fractional matchings concerning sufficient conditions for a hy-
pergraph to contain a fractional matching of a given weight. Some of them look for the minimum
degree for the existence of perfect matchings. For example, with the help of a geometric ap-
proach Keevash and Mycroft [11] obtain asymptotic degree conditions for the existence of perfect
matchings that generalizes many other results. Moreover, in this book it can be found a number
of references on papers concerning minimum degree problems for matchings in hypergraphs. For
the survey on these and related problems, see also [13] by Rödl and Ruciński.
    For a balanced d-partite d-uniform hypergraph, the degree condition sufficient for the existence
of a perfect matching was proved in [2]. The fractional matching polytope of a hypergraph is
studied, for instance, in [6]. At last, the recent progress on the connection between the number
of edges and the matching number of a hypergraph can be found in [10].
    One more possible generalization of the König–Hall–Egerváry theorem is the following con-
jecture often attributed to Ryser (see [4] for more on the history of this conjecture): in a d-partite
d-uniform hypergraph the size of the minimum vertex cover is not greater than the size of the
maximum matching multiplied by d − 1. The best progress is that this conjecture is true for
3-partite hypergraphs [1]; extremal 3-partite hypergraphs for the conjecture were described in [8].
For d = 4 and d = 5, an approximate version of the conjecture was proved by Haxell and Scott [9].
    Finally, there are generalizations of the Hall theorem of distinct representatives in literature.
One of them is stated for hypergraphs by Aharoni and Haxell [3], and a vector version of the
Hall theorem for common transversals is proved by Woodall [19].
    Let us describe the structure of this paper. In Section 1 we introduce the main concepts
including definitions on not only multidimensional polyplexes and extremal matrices but (frac-
tional) hyperplane covers. When a polyplex corresponds to a fractional matching in a d-partite
hypergraph, a hyperplane cover of a multidimensional matrix is exactly a fractional vertex cover
of the same hypergraph.
    In Section 2 we recall a duality between problems on the maximum fractional matching and
the minimum fractional vertex hyperplane cover in a hypergraph and state the same duality
for polyplexes and hyperplane covers. Also, we deduce some easy consequences of the linear
programming theory applied to polyplexes and hyperplane covers.
    In Section 3 we probe our approach on the case of 2-dimensional matrices providing one of
the proofs of the König–Hall–Egerváry theorem. We also find the differences between the 2-
dimensional and multidimensional cases and reveal some properties of 2-dimensional extremal
matrices. In the second part of this section, we formulate a research programme for multidimen-
sional extremal matrices, raise the main questions, and propose several conjectures. In particular,
one of these conjectures is equivalent to that every d-partite hypergraph extremal for a fractional
perfect matching is extremal for an integer perfect matching too. If we succeed in completing
the above programme, we obtain a characterization of extremal matrices for polyplexes that is
equivalent to the multidimensional König–Hall–Egerváry theorem.
    Following our research programme, in Section 4 we consider a question on the equivalence
between extremal matrices and their optimal hyperplane covers and in Section 5 we provide some

properties of optimal hyperplane covers. A series of constructions of extremal matrices is given
in Section 6, and Section 7 is devoted to the property of diagonal extremality. In Section 8 we
look closer at extremal matrices of orders 2 and 3 and prove some additional properties for them.
The paper concludes with a list of examples of extremal matrices of small orders and dimensions.
    The main results of the present consideration are characterizations of all extremal matrices of
big deficiencies and extremal matrices with two-value optimal hyperplane covers. In particular,
all main conjectures from Section 3 are confirmed for such classes of matrices.


1         Definitions
A d-dimensional matrix A of order n is an array (aα )α∈Ind , aα ∈ R, with the set of indices
Ind = {(α1 , . . . , αd ) : αi ∈ {1, . . . , n}}. For x ∈ R, we will say that aα is an x-entry of a matrix A
if aα = x. A multidimensional matrix A is called nonnegative if all entries of A are nonnegative.
A (0, 1)-matrix is a multidimensional matrix whose all entries are equal to zero or one. The
support supp(A) of a nonnegative matrix A is the set of indices α of all nonzero entries aα .
    A hyperplane in a d-dimensional matrix A is a (d − 1)-dimensional submatrix of A obtained
by fixing one of coordinates. Denote by Γi,j the j-th hyperplane of direction i in the matrix A
(submatrix in which the i-th coordinate is assigned to be j). Given a d-dimensional matrix A of
order n, let Aα be the d-dimensional submatrix of order n−1 obtained by deleting all hyperplanes
containing index α.
    Multidimensional matrices A and A′ are said to be equivalent if one can be turned to the
other by permutations of hyperplanes of each direction and by permutations of directions (matrix
transpositions).
    A polyplex K of weight W is a nonnegative multidimensional matrix in which the sum of
entries over each hyperplane is not greater than 1 and the sum of all entries equals W . Note that
the set of all polyplexes of the same weight is a convex polytope.
    A polyplex of weight n and order n is said to be a polydiagonal. The simplest example of a
polydiagonal is a diagonal that is a (0, 1)-matrix with exactly one unity entry in each hyperplane.
    Strictly speaking, it would be more correct to use terms like “partial fractional diagonal”
instead of “polyplex” and “fractional diagonal” instead of “polydiagonal”. But we will use the
present notions for the sake of brevity and convenience. Plexes appear in studies of latin squares
and latin hypercubes where they mean sets of entries evenly distributed among all hyperplanes
and symbols of a latin hypercube. See survey [18] for results on plexes in latin squares and
paper [17] for a generalization of plexes for multidimensional matrices.
    We will say that a multidimensional (0, 1)-matrix A contains a polyplex K (or that K is
polyplex in A) if A and K have the same order and dimension and supp(K) ⊆ supp(A). An
optimal polyplex in a matrix A is a polyplex of the maximum weight contained in the matrix A.
    A hyperplane cover of a d-dimensional (0, 1)-matrix A of order n is a (d × n)-table Λ = (λi,j )
assigning nonnegative weights to all hyperplanes of A in such a way so each unity entry of A
isPcovered with weight not less than 1, i.e. we demand that for each α ∈ supp(A) it holds
      λi,j ≥ 1.
Γi,j ∋α
    The weight of a hyperplane cover Λ is the sum of all its entries λi,j . It is not hard to see that
the set of all hyperplane covers of a given weight is a convex polytope.
    We call a hyperplane cover Λ of a (0, 1)-matrix A optimal if it has the minimum weight among
all hyperplane covers of A. Note that every (0, 1)-matrix A of order n has a hyperplane cover of
weight n because we can always cover the support of A by n hyperplanes of the same direction
having weight 1. Therefore, the weight of an optimal hyperplane cover of any matrix of order n
is not greater than n.
    Given a nonnegative table Λ, we define a (0, 1)-matrix A(Λ) so that the support of A(Λ) is
the set of indices covered with weight at least 1 by Λ. Also for a given index α let us denote by

                                                     3
Λα the d × (n − 1)-table obtained from Λ by deleting all weights of hyperplanes containing the
index α.
    A multidimensional (0, 1)-matrix A of order n is called stepped if for every direction i of
hyperplanes and for all 1 ≤ j1 < j2 ≤ n the support of the hyperplane Γi,j1 includes the support
of the hyperplane Γi,j2 . Note that for every hyperplane cover Λ the matrix A(Λ) is equivalent
to some stepped matrix B since at each direction we can arrange the weights of hyperplanes in
descending order. On the other hand, not for every stepped matrix B there is a hyperplane cover
Λ such that B = A(Λ). Multidimensional stepped matrices of order 2 are known as monotonic
boolean functions; (0, 1)-matrices in echelon form serve as an example of 2-dimensional stepped
matrices.
    A d-dimensional (0, 1)-matrix A of order n is called extremal if A contains no polydiagonals
and after adding any entry to the support of A the resulting matrix contains a polydiagonal.
The deficiency δ of an extremal matrix A of order n is the difference between n and the weight
of the optimal polyplex in A.
    At last we introduce a special class of extremal matrices. We will say that a multidimensional
(0, 1)-matrix A is diagonally extremal if it contains no polydiagonals and for each index α such
that aα = 0 the submatrix Aα contains a diagonal.

3      2-dimensional case, main questions and conjectures
We start this section with reminding one of the proofs of the König–Hall–Egerváry theorem. For
this purpose we need one more definition.
    A rectangular integer matrix is called totally unimodular if the determinant of each its square
submatrix is equal to 0 or ±1.
    The proof of the following proposition can be found in book [15].

Proposition 3.1. Let Q be a rectangular (0, 1)-matrix of size n × l. Assume that columns of
Q can be divided into two groups so that rows of the resulting submatrices contain no more than
one unity entry. Then the matrix Q is totally unimodular.

   We are ready now to provide the proof for one of equivalent forms of the König–Hall–Egerváry
theorem.

Theorem 3.2. Given a 2-dimensional (0, 1)-matrix A of order n, the maximum length of a
positive partial diagonal in A is equal to the minimum number of lines sufficient for covering all
unity entries.



                                                        5
Proof. Note that the problem on the maximum length of a positive partial diagonal in A is the
integer version of Problem (1). Similarly, the problem on the minimum number of lines covering
all unity entries of A is integer Problem (2).
    In the 2-dimensional case, the constraint matrix U for one of these problems is an incidence
matrix of a bipartite graph. By Proposition 3.1, the matrix U is totally unimodular. It is known
(see, for example, [15]) that if U is a totally unimodular matrix then for all integer vectors b the
polyhedron {x : Ux ≥ b} has only integer vertices. Therefore, both integer Problems (1) and (2)
have the same integer optimal value.
    Let us consider 2-dimensional extremal matrices in more details. Theorem 3.2 means that
all 2-dimensional extremal matrices of order n are exactly the (0, 1)-matrices with k rows and
n − k − 1 columns filled by 1 and with all other entries equal zero. Also, from the proof of
Theorem 3.2 we have that any 2-dimensional matrix A contains an integer optimal polyplex (i.e.,
a partial positive diagonal of the maximal length) and any optimal polyplex in A is a concave
sum of partial positive diagonals. All 2-dimensional extremal matrices have deficiency δ = 1
because the weight of their optimal polyplex is equal to n − 1.
    The optimal hyperplane cover Λ of a 2-dimensional extremal matrix is unique and is a (0, 1)-
table with 2 rows and n columns such that one row of Λ contains k ones and the other row has
n − k − 1 ones.
    It also can be checked that an entry of a 2-dimensional extremal matrix is contained in some
optimal polyplex if and only if it is covered by weight exactly 1 in the optimal hyperplane cover
Λ.
    At last, if we replace any zero entry of a 2-dimensional extremal matrix by a one then the
resulting matrix contains a diagonal. It means that every 2-dimensional extremal matrix is
diagonally extremal.
    Multidimensional extremal matrices are much more complicated objects than the 2-dimensional
case. Let us set up the principal questions studied in this paper.


  Question 3.3. Does there exist a 1-to-1 correspondence between extremal multidimensional
                      matrices and their optimal hyperplane covers?

   We believe that the answer to this question is positive. We have no examples of extremal
matrices at which this correspondence is failed and we prove it for some classes of extremal
matrices. In Section 4, we consider this question in more details. Based on our studies, we
propose the following conjecture.

Conjecture 3.4. Every extremal matrix has a unique optimal hyperplane cover.


 Question 3.5. What are the possible values of deficiencies δ of extremal matrices? Given n
   and d, how small can the deficiency of a d-dimensional extremal matrix of order n be?

   It is easy to state that the deficiency of multidimensional extremal matrices is between zero
and one.

Proposition 3.6. If δ is a deficiency of an extremal matrix A, then 0 < δ ≤ 1.

Proof. Since A contains no polydiagonals, we have δ > 0.
   By the definition, if we add some entry to the support of A, then we obtain a matrix with a
polydiagonal K. Since kα ≤ 1, the weight of the polyplex K constricted back to the matrix A is
not less than n − 1. Therefore, the deficiency δ of A is at most 1.



                                                 6
   As we noted before, the weight of an optimal polyplex is rational, so the deficiency δ of any
extremal matrix is some rational number p/q. The denominator q of δ is bound in modulus by
the largest minor of the incidence matrix of a d-partite hypergraph, so it does not exceed dnd .
   Considered examples of extremal matrices and the results of our investigation motivate us to
propose the following.

Conjecture 3.7. The deficiency δ of any extremal matrix is equal to 1/m for some m ∈ N.


    Question 3.8. Can we describe and enumerate all optimal hyperplane covers of extremal
                         matrices (and extremal matrices itself )?

    In the next sections, we provide some necessary conditions for a hyperplane cover to define an
extremal matrix and propose constructions of extremal matrices and their optimal hyperplane
covers. Also, we give a characterization of all extremal matrices of big deficiencies. Our research
leads us to the following conjecture.

Conjecture 3.9. If Λ is an optimal hyperplane cover of an extremal matrix of deficiency δ then
all entries λi,j are integer multiples of δ.

   Since a convex linear combination of optimal hyperplane covers of some matrix is an optimal
hyperplane cover, we note that Conjecture 3.9 implies Conjecture 3.4 and if for some extremal
matrix Conjecture 3.9 is true then such matrix has a unique optimal hyperplane cover.


        Question 3.10. What are the properties of multidimensional extremal matrices?

   We know that all 2-dimensional matrices are diagonally extremal. We do not find examples of
multidimensional extremal matrices that are not diagonally extremal, and all our constructions
preserve this property. So we propose the following.

Conjecture 3.11. Every multidimensional extremal matrix is diagonally extremal.

   Note that in terms of hypergraphs this conjecture means that every extremal for fractional
perfect matchings balanced d-partite d-uniform hypergraph is also extremal for integer perfect
matchings.


4     Equivalence of extremal matrices and their optimal hy-
      perplane covers
This section aims to set up a natural bijection between extremal matrices and their optimal
hyperplane covers. First of all, let us prove that the support of any extremal matrix is exactly
the set of indices covered with weight at least 1 by optimal hyperplane covers.

Theorem 4.1. If Λ is an optimal hyperplane cover of an extremal matrix A then A = A(Λ).

Proof. By the definition of the optimal hyperplane cover, A ⊆ A(Λ).
   Since Λ is an optimal hyperplane cover of an extremal matrix, the weight of Λ is less than n,
and A(Λ) does not contain a polydiagonal. Thus, if the support A is strictly contained in the
support A(Λ), then we have a contradiction with the extremality of the matrix A.

    As an immediate corollary of this theorem, we have that every extremal matrix is equivalent
to some stepped matrix.
    Our next aim is to provide sufficient conditions for the existence of a unique optimal hy-
perplane cover and state this uniqueness for several classes of extremal matrices. Note that for
other optimal objects a similar statement often does not hold. For example, even 2-dimensional
extremal matrices may contain many different optimal polyplexes. Moreover, there is plenty of
non-extremal matrices A having not unique optimal hyperplane cover.
    By the definition, an optimal hyperplane cover of a matrix A is unique if and only if Prob-

programming was studied in many papers. In our work we are guided by [5] and [12].

5      Conditions on optimal hyperplane covers of extremal
       matrices
The main aim of this section is to reveal some necessary and sufficient conditions on hyperplane
covers Λ for the matrix A(Λ) to be extremal. We start with conditions following from Theorem 2.2
(or from the complementary slackness theorem of linear programming).
Proposition 5.1. If Λ is an optimal hyperplane cover of an extremal matrix A, then each row
of Λ contains at least one zero entry.
Proof. Since A has no polydiagonals, for each direction i there exists a hyperplane Γi,j such that
the sum of entries of some optimal polyplex over the hyperplane Γi,j is less than 1. Theorem 2.2
implies that λi,j = 0.
Proposition 5.2. If Λ is an optimal hyperplane cover of an extremal matrix A with deficiency
δ < 1, then in each hyperplane Γi,j there exists index α covered by Λ with weight 1.
Proof. Since δ < 1, every hyperplane Γi,j of A contains a positive entry kα of some optimal
polyplex K. By Theorem 2.2, we have that index α is covered with weight 1 by the hyperplane
cover Λ.
   Let us consider other necessary conditions on optimal hyperplane covers of extremal matrices.
From the definitions we deduce that Λ is an optimal hyperplane cover of A(Λ) and, moreover,
A(Λ) is an extremal matrix of order n if and only if both of the following demands hold:
    1. the matrix A(Λ) contains a polyplex of the same weight as Λ;

    2. there are no hyperplane covers Λ′ of weight less than n such that A(Λ) is contained in A(Λ′ ).
   The first demand is equivalent to that a certain system of linear equations is solvable and has
a nonnegative solution. Indeed, if Λ is an optimal hyperplane cover of weight W for an extremal
matrix A, then Problem (2) turns to be equivalent to the following system of equations:

Proposition 5.4. If Λ is an optimal hyperplane cover of an extremal matrix A of deficiency δ,
then there are no indices α covered by Λ with weight strictly between 1 − δ and 1.

Proof. Assume that index α 6∈ supp(A) is covered by Λ with weight 1 − ε for 0 < ε < δ. Consider
the hyperplane cover Λ′ that coincides with Λ in all entries except for λ′1,α1 = λ1,α1 + ε. The
weight of Λ′ is less than n and the matrix A is strictly contained in A(Λ′ ) that contradicts to the
extremality of A.

Proposition 5.5. If Λ is an optimal hyperplane cover of an extremal matrix A of deficiency δ,
then the difference between two non-equal entries λi,j and λi,l from the same row of Λ is not less
than δ.

Proof. For the sake of contradiction, assume that for some i, j and l we have λi,j = λi,l + ε, where
0 < ε < δ. Since A = A(Λ) and Λ has the minimum weight among all hyperplane covers of A,
the support of the hyperplane Γi,l is strictly contained in the support of the hyperplane Γi,j .
    Consider the hyperplane cover Λ′ that coincides with Λ in all entries except for λ′i,l = λi,l +
ε, that is A(Λ′ ) differs from A in that the hyperplane Γi,l is replaced by the hyperplane Γi,j .
The weight of Λ′ is less than n and the matrix A is strictly contained in A(Λ′ ), so we have a
contradiction to the extremality of A.

Proposition 5.6. If Λ is an optimal hyperplane cover of an extremal matrix A of deficiency δ,
then for every λi,j not equal to zero or one we have δ ≤ λi,j ≤ 1 − δ.

Proof. By Proposition 5.1, each row i of Λ contains at least one zero entry, so by Proposition 5.5
all nonzero λi,j are not less than δ. On the other hand, if there exists λi,j such that 1−δ < λi,j < 1,
then by Proposition 5.1 in the hyperplane Γi,j there is an index α covered with zero weights by
all hyperplanes of other directions. So the index α is covered by Λ with weight strictly between
1 − δ and 1, that is not possible by Proposition 5.4.
   The last corollary of the second demand is that optimal hyperplane covers cannot have many
entries that are greater than 1/2.

Proposition 5.7. If Λ is an optimal hyperplane cover of a multidimensional extremal matrix of
order n then the number of entries λi,j > 1/2 is less than n.

Proof. We will say that an entry λi,j of the optimal hyperplane cover Λ is big if it is greater than
1/2 and is small otherwise.

Proof. Clauses 1 and 2 immediately follow from Proposition 5.6.

entries.

Proposition 5.6, λ ≥ δ ≥ 1/3.

only within the i-th row. By Proposition 5.5, all nonzero entries in this row are the same and
equal to λ. Using Proposition 5.4 again, we deduce that all nonzero entries in other rows are
also the same and equal to 1 − λ (where 1/2 < 1 − λ < 2/3). Note that the i-th row of Λ has no

{0, 1/3, 2/3, 1}.
    At last we note that, since Conjecture 3.9 implies Conjecture 3.4, extremal matrices with
deficiencies 1, 1/2 and 1/3 have unique optimal hyperplane covers.


6      Constructions of extremal matrices
In this section, we consider several constructions of an infinite series of extremal matrices. All
these constructions support conjectures proposed in Section 3.

6.1      Iterative constructions
We start with constructions based on extremal matrices of a smaller size. The first construction
allows us to easily increase the dimension or discard insignificant directions of hyperplanes of
extremal matrices.

                                                11
Construction 6.1. Let B be a d-dimensional (0, 1)-matrix of order n in which each hyperplane of
some direction is the same (d − 1)-dimensional matrix A. The matrix B is extremal of deficiency
δ if and only if the matrix A is extremal of deficiency δ.

Proof. Without loss of generality, assume that all hyperplanes of the first direction in the matrix
B are the same. Every polyplex K in the matrix A can be expanded to a polyplex K ′ of the same
weight in the matrix B in the following way: to each kα , α = (α1 , . . . , αd−1 ), we assign kβ′ = kα ,
where β = (α1 , α1 , . . . , αd−1 ). Conversely, for every polyplex in B there is a polyplex of the same
weight in A: an entry kα of a polyplex in A is the sum of kβ′ from B over all β = (j, α1 , . . . , αd−1 ),
j = 1, . . . , n. Since the above correspondences work when we add some entry to the support
of A or B, this operation simultaneously produce or do not produce a polydiagonal in both
matrices.
   Next, we describe a series of constructions that allow us to obtain extremal matrices of a
greater order from a given extremal matrix.
   Let Λ be an optimal hyperplane cover of a d-dimensional matrix A of order n and of deficiency
δ. On the base of table Λ, we construct the following three d × (n + 1)-tables Λ′ :

Construction 6.2. 1. For an index α ∈ supp(A) covered by Λ with weight 1, Λ′ is obtained
   from Λ by attaching entry λi,αi to the i-th row, i = 1, . . . , d.

  2. Λ′ is obtained by attaching a 1-entry to one of rows of the table Λ and attaching 0-entries
     to other rows.

  3. Assume that the j-th row of Λ contains a δ-entry. Then we construct a table Λ′ by attaching
     a (1 − δ)-entry to one of rows of Λ (namely, i-th row for some i 6= j), a δ-entry to the j-th
     row and attaching 0-entries to other rows.

Theorem 6.3. If Λ is an optimal hyperplane cover of an extremal matrix A of deficiency δ, then
tables Λ′ obtained from Λ via Construction 6.2 are optimal hyperplane covers of the extremal
matrices B = A(Λ′ ) of order n + 1 and of deficiency δ.

Proof. Denote by β the index of the matrix B belonging to the intersection of all new hyperplanes.
In all three constructions, the submatrix Bβ is exactly the matrix A and entry bβ in matrices B
equals one. Each matrix B has no polydiagonals, because the weight of Λ′ is less than n + 1.
Moreover, matrices B contain polyplexes of weight n + 1 − δ that consist of some polyplex of
weight n − δ in the matrix A and bβ = 1.
      To prove the theorem, it remains to show that after adding some index γ to the support of
B we find a polydiagonal in the resulting matrix. If γ is taken from the submatrix Bβ , then the
required statement holds because of the extremality of the matrix A.
      Therefore, we may assume that indices γ and β share the same hyperplanes in directions
i1 , . . . , il . Let us find polydiagonals using index γ for all three clauses of Construction 6.2.
      1. By the construction, α is an index of A covered by Λ with weight 1 and Λ′ is obtained from
Λ by doubling entries corresponding to α. Let σ denote the index that coincides with β in all
positions except for σij = αij for all j = 1, . . . , l. The construction implies that submatrices Bβ
and Bσ coincide. So the submatrix Bσ is equal to A, index γ belongs to Bσ and bσ = 1. Thus, if
we replace entry bγ in the matrix B by 1, then the resulting matrix has a polydiagonal.
      2. By Proposition 5.1, there exists an index α covered with weight 0 by the hyperplane cover
Λ. So it is sufficient to repeat the proof of the previous clause for this index α.
      3. If index γ does not belong to a new hyperplane of direction i, then we can follow proofs
of the previous clauses. Otherwise, γ is an index covered with weight 1 − δ by a hyperplane of
direction i and with weight 0 by hyperplanes of other directions. The submatrix Bγ is defined
by a hyperplane cover different from Λ only in j-th row, where a δ-entry is utilized instead of a


                                                   12
0-entry. Then the support of the matrix Bγ strictly contains the support of the matrix A. The
extremality of the matrix A implies that Bγ has a polydiagonal.

7     To diagonal extremality of extremal matrices
In this section, we consider properties and questions related to diagonal extremality and prove
that all constructions in the present paper produce diagonally extremal matrices.
    By the definition, each diagonally extremal matrix is extremal. In Section 3, we put forward
a conjecture that the sets of extremal and diagonally extremal matrices coincide.
    On the way to prove Conjecture 3.11 it is reasonable to start with its weaker version.
Conjecture 7.1 (weakening Conjecture 3.11). If A is an extremal matrix and aα = 0 then the
submatrix Aα contains a polydiagonal.
    Conjectures 3.11 and 7.1 may turn out to be equivalent if one more conjecture is true.
Conjecture 7.2 (connecting Conjectures 3.11 and 7.1). Assume that A has a polydiagonal and
A = A(Λ) for some hyperplane cover Λ. Then A contains a diagonal.
    Note that Conjecture 7.2 claims that Problem (1) for multidimensional matrices defined by
some hyperplane cover and having a polydiagonal has not only a fractional but also integer
solution.
    We start a proof part of this section with showing that Conjecture 7.1 holds for some zero
entries of extremal matrices.
Theorem 7.3 (supporting Conjecture 7.1). Suppose that Λ is an optimal hyperplane cover of an
extremal d-dimensional matrix A of order n and deficiency δ. If for index β, where aβ = 0, there
exists an index α covered by Λ with weight 1 − δ and such that λi,βi ≤ λi,αi for all i = 1, . . . , d
then the submatrix Aβ has a polydiagonal.
Proof. Since the support of submatrix Aβ contains the support of the submatrix Aα , if Aα has a
polydiagonal then Aβ has a polydiagonal too. So let us prove that there is a polydiagonal in the
submatrix Aα .
    Let B denote the matrix obtained from A by changing a zero entry aα to unity. Since A is an
extremal matrix, the matrix B has a polydiagonal. Let Λ′ be a hyperplane cover of the matrix
B that coincides with Λ in all entries except for λ′1,α1 = λ1,α1 + δ. Note that Λ′ has the weight
n, so it is an optimal hyperplane cover of B.
    Extremality of the matrix A implies that for every polydiagonal K in B it holds that kα > 0.
Assume that for some polydiagonal K in B we have kα < 1. Then there is another index γ such
that kγ > 0 in hyperplane Γ1,α1 . By Theorem 2.2, index γ is covered with weight 1 by every
optimal hyperplane cover of B. But the hyperplane cover Λ′ covers it with weight at least 1 + δ:
a contradiction.
  Next, we prove that Constructions 6.1 and 6.2 (increasing dimension and order of extremal
matrices) preserve the property of diagonal extremality.
Theorem 7.4 (supporting Conjecture 3.11). Let A be a diagonally extremal matrix, and let B
be an extremal matrix obtained via Construction 6.1 or 6.2 from A. Then the matrix B is also
diagonally extremal.
Proof. If the matrix B is obtained by Construction 6.1, then all its hyperplanes of some direction
are exactly the matrix A. If we assume the diagonal extremality of A, changing a zero entry of
B to unity gives a diagonal in some hyperplane of B. It only remains to expand this diagonal to
the whole matrix B.

                                                 16
    Suppose now that the matrix B is given by Construction 6.2. Returning to the proof of
Theorem 6.3, note that in Clause 1 and 2 each polydiagonal, arising in B by adding index γ
to the support, consists of some 1-entry bσ and a polydiagonal in the submatrix Bσ = A such
that γ ∈ A. In Clause 3 for some cases the submatrix Bγ strictly contains matrix A. So, if such
operation gives a diagonal in the matrix A, then a diagonal also appears in the matrix B.
    To prove that all extremal matrices with two-value optimal hyperplane covers are diago-
nally extremal, we use the Gale–Ryser theorem. Given two partitions r = (r1 , . . . , rn ) and
s = (s1 , . . . , sm ) of the same integer number, partition r majorizes s if r1 + · · · + rk ≥ s1 + · · · + sk
for all k. If s is a nonincreasing sequence of natural numbers, then s∗ denotes the sequence,
where s∗i is the number of j such that sj ≥ i.

Theorem 7.5 (Gale [7], Ryser [14]). Let r = (r1 , . . . , rn ) and s = (s1 , . . . , sm ) be two nonin-
creasing sequences of nonnegative integers each summing to a common value. There exists a
(0, 1)-matrix of size n × m with row sums r and column sums s if and only if s∗ majorizes r.

Theorem 7.6 (supporting Conjecture 3.11). Let A be an extremal matrix defined by a hyperplane
cover Λ whose entries have only two values. Then the matrix A is diagonally extremal.

Proof. By Theorem 6.6, we may assume that all entries of Λ are equal to 0 or 1/m and the
deficiency δ is 1/m. Let α be an index of A covered by Λ with weight less than 1. As in the proof
of Theorem 6.6, without loss of generality, we may suppose that α is covered with weight 1 − 1/m
by Λ.
    Let Λα be a hyperplane cover of the submatrix Aα obtained from Λ by deleting all hyperplanes
containing index α. Note that the existence of a diagonal in Aα is equivalent to that there is
rearranging entries of the Λα within its rows that put Λα to some table with all column sums at
least 1. By the construction, Λα is a d × (n − 1)-table with exactly m(n − 1) nonzero entries equal
to 1/m. If we denote by ri the number of nonzero entries of Λα in the i-th row and by sj = m
the required number of positive entries in the j-column, then from Theorem 7.5 we deduce that
there exists a (0, 1)-matrix with row sums r and column sums s. Thus, for every aα = 0 the
submatrix Aα contains a diagonal.


   Theorems 7.4 and 7.6 implies that all extremal matrices of big deficiencies are diagonally
extremal.

Theorem 7.7 (supporting Conjecture 3.11). Every extremal matrix of deficiency 1 or 1/2 is
diagonally extremal.

    Let us look in more details at the problem of a diagonal extremality for a general extremal
matrix A defined by an optimal hyperplane cover Λ. Following the proof of Theorem 7.6, the
matrix A is diagonally extremal if and only if for each index α with aα = 0 the entries in the
corresponding table Λα can be rearranged within their rows in such a way so that the sum of
entries in each column is at least 1. After an appropriate normalization, all entries of Λα may be
considered to be integers.
    Consequently, for effective determining the diagonal extremality by a given optimal hyper-
plane cover of an extremal matrix, it would be convenient to have a generalization of the Gale–
Ryser theorem for integer nonnegative matrices or at least some sufficient conditions on the
existence of such matrices with prescribed column sums.

Problem 7.8 (related to Conjecture 3.11). What are necessary and sufficient conditions on
sequences of integers r 1 , . . . , r m and s for the existence of a matrix M such that the i-th row of
M has exactly rij entries equal j and the sum of entries in the l-th column of M equals sl ? The
similar question for matrices with column sums at least m?

8    Extremal matrices of small orders
The main aim of this section is to examine multidimensional extremal matrices of orders 2 and
3 in view of the questions and conjectures of Section 3. For this purpose we introduce more
definitions.
    Firstly, we note that the support of each diagonal in a multidimensional matrix of order 2
consists of exactly two indices, namely indices α and α that are said to be antipodal. Let us call
a multidimensional (0, 1)-matrix A of order 2 antipodal if for each pair of antipodal indices α and
α entries aα and aα are different. Note that antipodal matrices are exactly the graph of self-dual
boolean functions.
    By the definition, every antipodal matrix does not have diagonals, and adding any index to
its support produces a diagonal. So all antipodal matrices with no polydiagonals are extremal.
In fact, there are no other extremal matrices of order 2.
Theorem 8.1 (supporting Conjecture 3.11). Extremal multidimensional matrices of order 2 are
exactly antipodal matrices without polydiagonals.
Proof. Suppose that there exists an extremal but not antipodal multidimensional matrix A of
order 2 and deficiency δ. Then there exists a pair of antipodal indices α and α such that
aα = aα = 0.
    Let Λ be some optimal hyperplane cover of the matrix A. Note that the weight 2 − δ of Λ is
equal to the sum of cover weights of any two antipodal indices. By Theorem 4.1, each of α and
α is covered by Λ with weight less than 1. Then both of them are covered by Λ with weights
strictly between 1 − δ and 1: a contradiction to Proposition 5.6.
   An immediate corollary of Theorem 8.1 is that every extremal matrix of order 2 is diagonally
extremal. We would like to note that an antipodal matrix (even having a stepped structure) may
contain a polydiagonal. The smallest example is the following 6-dimensional matrix of order 2,
where the support of the polydiagonal is bold.

Proposition 8.2 (supporting Conjecture 7.2). Let A be a matrix of order 2 such that A = A(Λ)
for some hyperplane cover Λ. If A contains a polydiagonal, then it has a diagonal.

   As a corollary, we have the following alternatives for submatrices of extremal matrices of
order 3 that may help us to prove (or disprove) their diagonal extremality.

Proposition 8.3. Let A be an extremal matrix of order 3 and let aα = 0.

  1. If the submatrix Aα has a polydiagonal, then Aα contains a diagonal.

  2. If the submatrix Aα has no polydiagonals, then Aα is an extremal matrix of order 2.

Proof. 1. Since the submatrix Aα has a polydiagonal and Aα = A(Λ) for some hyperplane cover
Λ, Proposition 8.2 gives that Aα has a diagonal.
   2. Since Aα = A(Λ) for some hyperplane cover Λ of weight at least 2 and A has no polydiag-
onals, we have that the matrix A is antipodal (because there are no two antipodal entries both
covered with weights less than 1). By Theorem 8.1, every antipodal matrix of order 2 without
polydiagonals is extremal.
    Let us turn back to extremal matrices of order 2. Besides Construction 6.1, we can propose
one more iterative construction giving extremal matrices of order 2 and of greater dimension.
For this aim, we introduce the following notation.
    As it was proved in Theorem 2.2, every row of an optimal hyperplane cover Λ of any extremal
matrix has at least one zero entry. For d-dimensional extremal matrices of order 2, it means that
their optimal hyperplane covers Λ have not greater than d nonzero entries λ1 , . . . , λl , with all
of them located in different rows of Λ. We will say that λ1 , . . . , λl are essential weights of the
optimal hyperplane cover Λ.

Construction 8.4. Given an optimal hyperplane cover Λ of a d-dimensional matrix A of order 2
and of deficiency δ with essential weights λ1 , . . . , λd , let the essential weights of the (d+1)×2-table
Λ′ be λ1 , . . . , λd−1 , λd − δ, δ.

Theorem 8.5. Let Λ be an optimal hyperplane cover of a d-dimensional extremal matrix A
of order 2 and of deficiency δ such that there are no entries of A covered by Λ with weight
strictly between 1 and 1 + δ. Then the table Λ′ obtained from Λ via Construction 8.4 is an
optimal hyperplane cover of the (d + 1)-dimensional extremal matrix B = A(Λ′ ) of order 2 and
of deficiency δ.

Proof. Since B is defined by a hyperplane cover Λ′ of weight 2 − δ, the matrix B has no polydi-
agonals.
    By the construction, the support of the matrix A is contained in the support of the matrix
B, more specifically, hyperplanes of the direction d of the matrix A are diagonally located in the
intersection of the hyperplanes of directions d and d + 1 in the matrix B. So each polyplex K of
weight 2 − δ in A can be put to a polyplex K ′ of the same weight in the matrix B.
    To prove the theorem, it remains to show that the matrix B is antipodal because in this
case Theorem 8.1 gives that B is an extremal matrix. If the matrix B is not antipodal, then
there exists an index α covered by Λ′ with weight strictly between 1 − δ and 1. Note that by
Proposition 5.4, the hyperplane cover Λ cannot cover α with the same weight as Λ′ , because Λ is
an optimal hyperplane cover of an extremal matrix. Then by the construction, Λ covers α with
weight strictly between 1 and 1 + δ or with weight strictly between 1 − 2δ and 1 − δ but both
cases contradicts to the condition of the theorem.

     This construction may be used for proving Conjectures 3.4, 3.7 and 3.9 for matrices of order
2.

Corollary 8.6. Assume that for every extremal matrix of order 2 and deficiency δ there are no
entries covered with weight strictly between 1 and 1 + δ by their optimal hyperplane covers. Then
δ = 1/m for some m ∈ N, all such extremal matrices have the unique optimal hyperplane covers
Λ, with all entries λi,j being integer multiples of δ.

Proof. Construction 8.4 allows us to obtain extremal matrices of deficiency δ with a smaller
maximum essential weight from a given extremal matrix of deficiency δ. By the condition, we
can apply Construction 8.4 until all essential weights of the resulting extremal matrix are the
same. Application of Theorem 6.6 completes the proof.
   We conclude this section with one more conjecture that possibly allows us to describe all
optimal hyperplane covers of extremal matrices of order 2 in another way.

Conjecture 8.7. Let A be an extremal matrix of order 2 and K be a vertex of a polyhedron of
polyplexes in A. Then values kα , α ∈ supp(K) are essential weights of an optimal hyperplane
cover for some multidimensional extremal matrix of order 2.
