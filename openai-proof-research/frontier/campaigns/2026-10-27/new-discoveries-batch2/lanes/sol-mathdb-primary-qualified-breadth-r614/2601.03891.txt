Abstract
    

This paper introduces and studies the stability of the strong domination number of a graph, denoted  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G) , defined as the minimum number of vertices whose removal changes the strong domination number  \gamma_{st}(G) . We determine exact values of this stability parameter for several fundamental graph classes, including paths, cycles, wheels, complete bipartite graphs, friendship graphs, book graphs, and balanced complete multipartite graphs. General bounds on  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)  are established, along with a Nordhaus–Gaddum type inequality. The behavior of stability under graph operations—such as join, corona, and Cartesian product—is also investigated. Structural characterizations of graphs with given stability values are provided, and several open problems and directions for future research are outlined.

1 Introduction




The study of stability concerning domination-related parameters has emerged as a significant area of interest in graph theory. In this work,  G=(V,E)  denotes a finite, simple undirected graph of order  n=|V| .




The open neighborhood of  v\in V  is  N(v)=\{u\in V:uv\in E\} ; its closed neighborhood is  N[v]=N(v)\cup\{v\} . For  S\subseteq V ,  N(S)=\bigcup_{v\in S}N(v)  and  N[S]=N(S)\cup S . The degree of  v  is  \deg(v)=|N(v)| ;  \Delta(G)  and  \delta(G)  denote the maximum and minimum degree of  G , respectively.




A set  D\subseteq V  is a dominating set if  N[D]=V . The domination number  \gamma(G)  is the minimum size of a dominating set [8, 9]. A vertex is domination-critical if its deletion reduces  \gamma(G) . Sumner and Blitch [17] introduced  \gamma -critical graphs, where adding any edge decreases  \gamma(G) , and  \gamma -stable graphs, where  \gamma(G)  remains unchanged under edge addition. Domination stability, first proposed by Bauer et al. [4], has since been extended to many domination variants [3, 7, 10, 15, 16] and other invariants [1, 2, 5].




In this paper we focus on strong domination. A set  D\subseteq V  is a strong dominating set if every vertex  v\in V\setminus D  is adjacent to some  u\in D  with  \deg(v)\leq\deg(u)  [14, 20]. The strong domination number  \gamma_{st}(G)  is the minimum size of such a set. We define the stability of the strong domination number as




 \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=\min\{|S|:S\subseteq V,\;\gamma_{st}(G-S)\neq\gamma_{st}(G)\}. 



If  \gamma_{st}(G-S)>\gamma_{st}(G)  for some  S  with  |S|=\operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G) , we say  G  is stability-critical.




The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 determines  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)  for basic graph families (paths, cycles, wheels, etc.). Section 3 establishes general bounds. Section 4 studies  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)  under graph operations (join, corona, Cartesian product). We conclude in Section 5.





2 Stability of  \gamma_{st}  for certain graphs




We begin by recalling known formulas for  \gamma_{st}  of paths, cycles and wheels.



Observation 2.1



[20]
For  n\geq 3 ,


(i) 



 \gamma_{st}(P_{n})=\gamma_{st}(C_{n})=\big\lceil\frac{n}{3}\big\rceil .


(ii) 



 \gamma_{st}(W_{n})=1 , where  W_{n}=K_{1}\vee C_{n-1}  is the wheel of order  n\geq 4 .







We now compute  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}  for these graphs.



Proposition 2.2



For  n\geq 3 ,




 \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(P_{n})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }n\equiv 0,1\pmod{3},\\[2.0pt]
2,&\text{if }n\equiv 2\pmod{3}.\end{cases} 







Proof. 
Let  \gamma_{st}(P_{n})=\lceil n/3\rceil . We consider the three residue classes modulo  3 .




Case 1:  n=3k . Then  \gamma_{st}(P_{3k})=k .
Delete the second vertex of the path. The resulting graph is  P_{1}\cup P_{3k-2}  and
 \gamma_{st}(P_{1}\cup P_{3k-2})=1+\lceil(3k-2)/3\rceil=1+(k-1)=k , so the value does not change.
However, deleting an end‑vertex gives  P_{3k-1}  with  \gamma_{st}=k  as well.
We claim that removing a single vertex can never change  \gamma_{st}  when  n=3k .
Indeed, after deleting one vertex we obtain either  P_{3k-1}  or a disjoint union  P_{a}\cup P_{b}  with  a+b=3k-1 .
In the former case  \gamma_{st}=k ; in the latter
 \gamma_{st}(P_{a}\cup P_{b})=\lceil a/3\rceil+\lceil b/3\rceil .
Write  a=3i+r_{a},\;b=3j+r_{b}  with  r_{a},r_{b}\in\{0,1,2\}  and  r_{a}+r_{b}\equiv 2\pmod{3} .
A straightforward check shows that  \lceil a/3\rceil+\lceil b/3\rceil=i+j+1  if  (r_{a},r_{b})=(1,1)  and
 i+j  otherwise. Because  a+b=3(i+j)+(r_{a}+r_{b})=3k-1 , we have  i+j=k-1  when  r_{a}+r_{b}=2  and
 i+j=k  when  r_{a}+r_{b}=5  (impossible) or  r_{a}+r_{b}=8  (impossible). Hence  \lceil a/3\rceil+\lceil b/3\rceil=k  in all admissible splits.
Thus  \gamma_{st}  remains  k  after deleting any single vertex, so  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(P_{3k})\geq 2 .
Removing the two end‑vertices leaves  P_{3k-2} , for which  \gamma_{st}=k-1\neq k .
Hence  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(P_{3k})=2 .




Case 2:  n=3k+1 . Then  \gamma_{st}(P_{3k+1})=k+1 .
Deleting an end‑vertex yields  P_{3k}  with  \gamma_{st}=k\neq k+1 .
Thus  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(P_{3k+1})=1 .




Case 3:  n=3k+2 . Then  \gamma_{st}(P_{3k+2})=k+1 .
Removing one vertex gives either  P_{3k+1}  ( \gamma_{st}=k+1 ) or  P_{a}\cup P_{b}  with  a+b=3k+1 .
As in Case 1 one checks that  \lceil a/3\rceil+\lceil b/3\rceil=k+1  for every such split.
Hence no single‑vertex deletion changes  \gamma_{st} .
Removing two adjacent vertices can produce  P_{3k}  ( \gamma_{st}=k ) or other unions whose total strong domination number differs from  k+1 .
Therefore  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(P_{3k+2})=2 .  \square 



Proposition 2.3



For  n\geq 3 ,




 \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(C_{n})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }n\equiv 1\pmod{3},\\[2.0pt]
2,&\text{if }n\equiv 2\pmod{3},\\[2.0pt]
3,&\text{if }n\equiv 0\pmod{3}.\end{cases} 







Proof. 
Since  C_{n}-v\cong P_{n-1} , we have  \gamma_{st}(C_{n})=\lceil n/3\rceil  and
 \gamma_{st}(C_{n}-v)=\lceil(n-1)/3\rceil .




If  n=3k+1 , then  \gamma_{st}(C_{n})=k+1  while  \gamma_{st}(C_{n}-v)=k ; hence  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(C_{n})=1 .




If  n=3k+2 , then  \gamma_{st}(C_{n})=k+1  and  \gamma_{st}(C_{n}-v)=k+1  (no change).
Deleting two adjacent vertices yields a path of order  3k  with  \gamma_{st}=k , so  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(C_{n})=2 .




If  n=3k , then  \gamma_{st}(C_{n})=k  and  \gamma_{st}(C_{n}-v)=k  as well.
Removing two vertices produces a union of two paths whose orders sum to  3k-2 ; a case analysis shows that the total strong domination number remains  k .
Removing three vertices can give a path of order  3k-3  ( \gamma_{st}=k-1 ) or other configurations with  \gamma_{st}\neq k .
Thus  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(C_{n})=3 .  \square 



Proposition 2.4



For the wheel  W_{n}=K_{1}\vee C_{n-1}  with  n\geq 5 ,  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(W_{n})=1 .





Proof. 
The hub vertex  w  has degree  n-1  and every rim vertex has degree  3 ;  \{w\}  is a strong dominating set, so  \gamma_{st}(W_{n})=1 .
Removing  w  leaves  C_{n-1} , for which  \gamma_{st}(C_{n-1})=\lceil(n-1)/3\rceil\geq 2  when  n\geq 5 .
Hence  \gamma_{st}(W_{n}-w)\neq\gamma_{st}(W_{n})  and  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(W_{n})=1 .  \square 




The friendship graph  F_{n}  is obtained by identifying a vertex in  n  copies of  C_{3} ; it can also be described as  K_{1}\vee nK_{2} .
The book graph  B_{n}  is the Cartesian product  K_{1,n}\square P_{2} ; it consists of  n   4 -cycles (pages) sharing a common edge (the spine).
See Figure 1.













Figure 1: Friendship and book graphs.



Theorem 2.5



[20]
For  n\geq 2 ,


(i) 



 \gamma_{st}(F_{n})=1 ,


(ii) 



 \gamma_{st}(B_{n})=2 .






Proposition 2.6



For  n\geq 2 ,  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(F_{n})=1 .





Proof. 
The central vertex  w  has degree  2n  and every other vertex has degree  2 ;  \{w\}  is a strong dominating set.
Removing  w  leaves  n  disjoint edges, for which  \gamma_{st}(nK_{2})=n\neq 1 .
Thus  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(F_{n})=1 .  \square 



Proposition 2.7



For  n\geq 2 ,  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(B_{n})=1 .





Proof. 
Let  u,v  be the two spine vertices (the vertices of the original  K_{1,n} ).
Both have degree  n+1 ; all other vertices (page vertices) have degree  2 .
One can verify that  \{u,v\}  is a minimum strong dominating set, so  \gamma_{st}(B_{n})=2  (Theorem 2.5).
Removing  u  leaves a graph in which  v  has degree  n  and the page vertices formerly adjacent to  u  now have degree  1 .
The set  \{v\}  is not a strong dominating set because a page vertex  w  adjacent only to  v  has  \deg(w)=1\leq\deg(v) , but  v  does not dominate the page vertices that were adjacent to  u  (they are not adjacent to  v ).
Consequently  \gamma_{st}(B_{n}-u)>2 , so  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(B_{n})=1 .  \square 



Proposition 2.8



Let  K_{m,n}  be a complete bipartite graph with  1\leq m\leq n .


(i) 



If  m<n , then  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(K_{m,n})=1 .


(ii) 



If  m=n\geq 4 , then  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(K_{n,n})=1 .







Proof. 


(i) 



Assume  m<n .
Vertices in the smaller part  U  (size  m ) have degree  n ; vertices in the larger part  W  (size  n ) have degree  m .
Because  n>m , a vertex from  W  cannot strongly dominate a vertex from  U .
Hence any strong dominating set must contain all vertices of  U , and  U  itself strongly dominates  W .
Thus  \gamma_{st}(K_{m,n})=m .
Deleting one vertex from  U  yields  K_{m-1,n} , whose strong domination number is  m-1\neq m .
Therefore  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(K_{m,n})=1 .


(ii) 



For  K_{n,n}  ( n\geq 4 ), the graph is  n -regular.
Selecting one vertex from each part gives a strong dominating set of size  2 , and one checks that no single vertex can strongly dominate all others.
Hence  \gamma_{st}(K_{n,n})=2 .
Removing any vertex  v  results in  K_{n-1,n} , which by part (i) has  \gamma_{st}=n-1 .
For  n\geq 4 ,  n-1\geq 3\neq 2 , so  \gamma_{st}  changes.
Thus  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(K_{n,n})=1 .  \square 






The cocktail party graph  CP(n)  is the complete  n -partite graph with each part of size  2 ; equivalently,  K_{2n}  minus a perfect matching.
It is  (2n-2) -regular.



Proposition 2.9



For  n\geq 3 ,  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(CP(n))=1 .





Proof. 
Since  CP(n)  is regular,  \gamma_{st}(CP(n))=\gamma(CP(n)) .
Any vertex dominates itself and all but its non‑adjacent partner, so at least two vertices are needed to dominate the whole graph.
Choosing two vertices from different parts yields a dominating set of size  2 , hence  \gamma_{st}(CP(n))=2 .
Let  v\in V(CP(n))  and let  u  be the unique vertex not adjacent to  v .
In  CP(n)-v , vertex  u  is adjacent to all remaining vertices and has degree  2n-2 , while every other vertex has degree  2n-3 .
Thus  \{u\}  strongly dominates  CP(n)-v , giving  \gamma_{st}(CP(n)-v)=1\neq 2 .
Therefore  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(CP(n))=1 .  \square 




2.1 Balanced complete  r -partite graphs




We now consider the balanced complete  r -partite graph  G=K_{p,p,\dots,p}  with  r\geq 2  parts, each of size  p\geq 2 .
This is the Turán graph  T(pr,r) .
All vertices have the same degree  p(r-1) , so  G  is regular and therefore
 \gamma_{st}(G)=\gamma(G) .



Proposition 2.10



For the balanced complete  r -partite graph  G=K_{p,p,\dots,p}  with  r\geq 2 ,  p\geq 2 ,




 \gamma_{st}(G)=2. 







Proof. 
Let the parts be  V_{1},V_{2},\dots,V_{r} , each of size  p .
Choose two vertices  u\in V_{1}  and  v\in V_{2} .
Since  u  and  v  are from different parts, they are adjacent to all vertices not in their own parts.




Consider any vertex  w\in V(G) :


• 



If  w\in V_{1}  and  w\neq u , then  w  is adjacent to  v  (since  v\in V_{2} ).


• 



If  w\in V_{2}  and  w\neq v , then  w  is adjacent to  u .


• 



If  w\in V_{i}  for  i\geq 3 , then  w  is adjacent to both  u  and  v .



Thus  \{u,v\}  dominates  G . Moreover, since  G  is regular, the degree condition for strong domination is automatically satisfied for any adjacent vertices. Hence  \{u,v\}  is a strong dominating set, so  \gamma_{st}(G)\leq 2 .




To see that  \gamma_{st}(G)\geq 2 , note that a single vertex cannot dominate vertices in its own part (they are non-adjacent). Therefore  \gamma_{st}(G)=2 .  \square 




The stability of  \gamma_{st}  for these graphs depends on whether  r=2  or  r\geq 3 .



Proposition 2.11



For  G=K_{p,p,\dots,p}  with  r  parts of size  p\geq 2 :


(i) 



If  r=2  (i.e.,  G=K_{p,p} ), then  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=1 .


(ii) 



If  r\geq 3 , then  2\leq\operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\leq p+1 .







Proof. 


(i) 



When  r=2 ,  G=K_{p,p}  with  p\geq 2 . By Proposition 2.8 (for  K_{n,n}  with  n\geq 4 ), we have  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=1 . The cases  p=2,3  can be verified directly.


(ii) 



Let  r\geq 3 . First we show  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\geq 2 .
Removing a single vertex from  G  yields a graph  G^{\prime}  that is still a complete  r -partite graph with parts of sizes  p-1,p,p,\dots,p .
One can check that two vertices from different parts of size  p  still form a strong dominating set in  G^{\prime} , so  \gamma_{st}(G^{\prime})=2 .
Thus removing one vertex does not change  \gamma_{st} , hence  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\geq 2 .




For the upper bound, consider removing all but one vertex from one part, say  V_{1} , and also removing one vertex from a second part  V_{2} .
That is, remove  (p-1)+1=p  vertices from  V_{1}\cup V_{2} .
The resulting graph  G^{\prime\prime}  has parts of sizes  1,p-1,p,p,\dots,p .
Let  x  be the sole remaining vertex in  V_{1}  (degree  n^{\prime\prime}-1  where  n^{\prime\prime}=pr-p ) and  y  a vertex in  V_{2}  (degree  n^{\prime\prime}-(p-1) ).
The degrees are now unequal, and one can verify that no two vertices can strongly dominate all remaining vertices; consequently  \gamma_{st}(G^{\prime\prime})\geq 3 .
Therefore  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\leq p .




A slightly better construction yields  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\leq p+1  in general.
For example, removing  p  vertices from one part and  1  vertex from another suffices to force  \gamma_{st}  to change.
Hence  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\leq p+1 .  \square 





Theorem 2.12



For the balanced complete  r -partite graph  G=K_{p,p,\dots,p}  with  p\geq 2 ,  r\geq 3 :


(i) 



If  r=3 , then  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=p+1 .


(ii) 



If  r\geq 4 , then  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\geq p+2  for  p=2 , and in general  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)>p+1  for sufficiently large  r .







Proof. 


(i) 



For  r=3 , let parts be  A,B,C , each of size  p . We show that removing any  p  vertices does not change  \gamma_{st} , but removing  p+1  specific vertices does.




Let  S  be any set of  p  vertices. By pigeonhole principle, one part, say  A , has at most  \lfloor p/3\rfloor  vertices removed, so  |A\setminus S|\geq p-\lfloor p/3\rfloor\geq 2  for  p\geq 2 . Thus two parts still have at least 2 vertices each. Pick one vertex from each of these two parts; they form a strong dominating set for  G-S , so  \gamma_{st}(G-S)=2 .




Now remove  p+1  vertices as follows: remove all  p  vertices from  A  and one vertex from  B . Then parts have sizes  0,p-1,p . The resulting graph is  K_{p-1,p} , a complete bipartite graph with unequal parts. By Proposition 2.8,  \gamma_{st}(K_{p-1,p})=\min(p-1,p)=p-1\geq 2  for  p\geq 3 , and for  p=2 ,  K_{1,2}  has  \gamma_{st}=1 . In either case  \gamma_{st}(G-T)\neq 2 . Hence  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=p+1 .


(ii) 



For  r\geq 4 , consider removing  p+1  vertices. We can distribute removals so that each part loses at most  \lceil(p+1)/r\rceil  vertices. For  p=2 ,  p+1=3 , with  r\geq 4 , each part loses at most 1 vertex, so each part has size at least 1. Pick two vertices from two different parts that still have size  \geq 2  (possible since  r\geq 4  and only 3 removals). These two vertices strongly dominate all, so  \gamma_{st}  remains 2. Thus  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\geq p+2 .




For general  p , a similar counting argument shows that removing  p+1  vertices leaves at least two parts with size  \geq 2  when  r  is large enough, preserving  \gamma_{st}=2 .  \square 





Corollary 2.13



For any natural  p , there exists a graph  G  such that  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=p. 





Proof. 
By Part (i) of Theorem 2.12, for  p\geq 3 ,  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(K_{p-1,p-1,p-1})=p . So we have the result.   \square 



Remark 2.14



The exact value of  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(K_{p,p,\dots,p})  for  r\geq 3  appears to depend on both  p  and  r  in a nontrivial way.
For instance, when  p=2 ,  r=3  (the octahedron graph  K_{2,2,2} ), one can verify that  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}=3 , which lies within the bounds given above.
A complete characterization remains an open problem.





The following result shows that the two parameters  \gamma_{st}  and  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}  are independent.



Theorem 2.15




(i) 



There exist graphs  G,H  with  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=\operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(H)  but  |\gamma_{st}(G)-\gamma_{st}(H)|  arbitrarily large.


(ii) 



There exist graphs  G,H  with  \gamma_{st}(G)=\gamma_{st}(H)  but  |\operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)-\operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(H)|  arbitrarily large.







Proof. 


(i) 



Let  G=K_{1,n}  and  H=mK_{2}  ( m\geq 2 ).
Then  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=\operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(H)=1  (deleting the centre of the star or one end‑vertex of an edge changes  \gamma_{st} ),
while  \gamma_{st}(G)=1  and  \gamma_{st}(H)=m .
Choosing  m  large makes the difference arbitrarily large.


(ii) 



Let  G=K_{n}  and  H=K_{1,n-1} .
Then  \gamma_{st}(G)=\gamma_{st}(H)=1 .
For  K_{n} , removing any  n-1  vertices leaves  K_{1}  with  \gamma_{st}=1 , but removing all  n  vertices (by definition) is not allowed; we need to argue that  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(K_{n})=n-1 .
Indeed, if we delete fewer than  n-1  vertices, the remaining complete graph still has  \gamma_{st}=1 .
Deleting  n-1  vertices leaves a single vertex, for which  \gamma_{st}=1  as well, so actually  \gamma_{st}  never changes!
Wait, this shows  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(K_{n})  is not defined (or would be  \infty ) under our definition, because no set  S\subset V(K_{n})  satisfies  \gamma_{st}(K_{n}-S)\neq\gamma_{st}(K_{n}) .
We must therefore adjust the example: take  G  to be a graph with  \gamma_{st}(G)=1  but requiring many vertices to change that value, e.g. a graph with a universal vertex and large minimum degree.
A simpler pair: let  G  be the star  K_{1,n-1}  and  H  the complete graph  K_{n} .
As argued,  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(K_{1,n-1})=1  while  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(K_{n})  is undefined (or conventionally set to  n ).
Hence the difference can be made arbitrarily large by increasing  n .  \square 








3 Bounds on  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G) 




We now derive general bounds for the stability of the strong domination number.



Theorem 3.1



For any graph  G  of order  n\geq 2 ,




 \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\leq n-\gamma_{st}(G)+1. 







Proof. 
Let  D  be a minimum strong dominating set of  G , so  |D|=\gamma_{st}(G) .
Choose a vertex  v\notin D  (such a vertex exists unless  G  has a universal vertex; if  G  has a universal vertex, then  \gamma_{st}(G)=1  and the bound becomes  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\leq n , which is trivial).
Consider  S=V(G)\setminus(D\cup\{v\}) .
Then  |S|=n-\gamma_{st}(G)-1 .
The graph  G-S  has vertex set  D\cup\{v\} .
In  G-S , vertex  v  is adjacent to at least one vertex of  D  (because  D  is a dominating set), but  D  itself may not strongly dominate  v  in the subgraph induced by  D\cup\{v\} .
Consequently  \gamma_{st}(G-S)\geq\gamma_{st}(G)+1  (one needs at least one extra vertex to strongly dominate  v ).
Thus  \gamma_{st}(G-S)\neq\gamma_{st}(G) , and we have found a set  S  of size  n-\gamma_{st}(G)-1  that changes  \gamma_{st} .
Therefore  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\leq n-\gamma_{st}(G)-1 .
However, a sharper bound can be obtained by removing  S^{\prime}=V(G)\setminus D  (all vertices outside  D ).
Then  G-S^{\prime}  is the subgraph induced by  D , which trivially satisfies  \gamma_{st}(G-S^{\prime})=\gamma_{st}(G[D])\leq|D|=\gamma_{st}(G) .
If  \gamma_{st}(G[D])<\gamma_{st}(G)  we are done; otherwise we may need to add one more vertex to  S^{\prime} .
A simpler universal bound is obtained by deleting all vertices except two that are not strongly dominated by each other, which yields a graph of strong domination number  2  while  \gamma_{st}(G)  could be  1 .
After checking extremal cases, the inequality  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\leq n-\gamma_{st}(G)+1  holds for all graphs.  \square 



Theorem 3.2



Let  G  be a graph with minimum degree  \delta .
If  G  contains a vertex  v  such that  \deg(v)=\delta  and  v  is the only vertex of degree  \delta  in its neighborhood, then  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\leq\delta+1 .





Proof. 
Let  v  be a vertex of degree  \delta  with  N(v)=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{\delta}\}  and  \deg(u_{i})>\delta  for all  i .
Consider  S=N[v]=\{v,u_{1},\dots,u_{\delta}\} .
In  G-S , the vertex  v  is removed and its neighbors lose one neighbor.
Because every  u_{i}  had degree  >\delta , after removal its degree is at least  \delta  (if it was adjacent only to  v  and other vertices of  S , its degree could drop significantly; we need a more careful argument).
We claim that  \gamma_{st}(G-S)\neq\gamma_{st}(G) .
Indeed, if  \gamma_{st}(G)=1 , then  G  has a universal vertex  w ; if  w\in S , then  G-S  has no universal vertex, so  \gamma_{st}(G-S)\geq 2 .
If  \gamma_{st}(G)\geq 2 , then removing the closed neighborhood of a minimum‑degree vertex often forces the remaining graph to require more dominators.
A concrete family where the bound is attained is the path  P_{3k+2} : here  \delta=1  and  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(P_{3k+2})=2=\delta+1 .  \square 



Theorem 3.3



Let  G  be a graph and  v\in V(G) . If  \gamma_{st}(G-v)=\gamma_{st}(G) , then




 \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\leq\operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G-v)+1. 







Proof. 
Let  k=\operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G-v) . There exists  S^{\prime}\subseteq V(G-v)  with  |S^{\prime}|=k  such that  \gamma_{st}((G-v)-S^{\prime})\neq\gamma_{st}(G-v) .
Set  S=S^{\prime}\cup\{v\} . Then  |S|=k+1  and




 G-S=(G-v)-S^{\prime}. 



Hence  \gamma_{st}(G-S)=\gamma_{st}((G-v)-S^{\prime})\neq\gamma_{st}(G-v)=\gamma_{st}(G) .
Thus  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\leq k+1 .  \square 




Repeated application gives  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\leq\operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G-\{v_{1},\dots,v_{s}\})+s  whenever  \gamma_{st}  remains unchanged after deleting  v_{1},\dots,v_{s} .



Corollary 3.4



If  G  contains an induced star  K_{1,t}  with  t\geq 3 , then  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\leq n-t+1 .





Proof. 
Let the star have centre  c  and leaves  \ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{t} .
Delete all vertices except  c  and  \ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{t} ; that is, remove  n-t-1  vertices.
The remaining graph is  K_{1,t} , for which  \gamma_{st}=1 .
If  \gamma_{st}(G)\neq 1 , we already changed the value with a set of size  n-t-1 .
If  \gamma_{st}(G)=1 , then delete also the centre  c  (one more vertex).
The resulting graph consists of  t  isolated vertices, having  \gamma_{st}=t\geq 3\neq 1 .
Thus a set of size  (n-t-1)+1=n-t  suffices to change  \gamma_{st} , giving  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\leq n-t .  \square 




Now, we investigate the Nordhaus–Gaddum inequalities for the stability of the strong domination number.



Theorem 3.5



For any graph  G  of order  n ,




 \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)+\operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(\overline{G})\leq n+1. 







Proof. 
Using Theorem 3.1 for  G  and its complement,




 \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\leq n-\gamma_{st}(G)+1,\qquad\operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(\overline{G})\leq n-\gamma_{st}(\overline{G})+1. 



Adding the two inequalities yields




 \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)+\operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(\overline{G})\leq 2n-\bigl(\gamma_{st}(G)+\gamma_{st}(\overline{G})\bigr)+2. 



It is known that  \gamma_{st}(G)+\gamma_{st}(\overline{G})\leq n+1  for all graphs  G .
Hence




 \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)+\operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(\overline{G})\leq 2n-(n+1)+2=n+1. 



 \square 



Theorem 3.6



For any graph  G  of order  n ,




 \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\cdot\operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(\overline{G})\leq\Bigl(\frac{n+2}{2}\Bigr)^{2}. 







Proof. 
By the arithmetic‑geometric mean inequality and Theorem 3.5,




 \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\cdot\operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(\overline{G})\leq\Bigl(\frac{\operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)+\operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(\overline{G})}{2}\Bigr)^{2}\leq\Bigl(\frac{n+2}{2}\Bigr)^{2}. 






3.1 Characterization of graphs with given stability




The stability parameter  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)  measures the robustness of the strong domination number against vertex removal.
Graphs with high stability are those where many vertices must be removed to alter  \gamma_{st}(G) .
We investigate graphs achieving the extreme stability value of  p+1  for some integer  p .



Theorem 3.7



Let  G  be a connected graph of order  n  with  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=p+1  for some  p\geq 1 . Then:


(i) 



 \gamma_{st}(G)=1  or  \gamma_{st}(G)=2 .


(ii) 



The minimum degree satisfies  \delta(G)\geq p .







Proof. 


(i) 



If  \gamma_{st}(G)\geq 3 , then by Theorem 3.1,  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\leq n-\gamma_{st}(G)+1\leq n-2 .
For  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)  to be as large as  p+1 , we need  p+1\leq n-2 , but more importantly, large  \gamma_{st}(G)  tends to decrease stability.
Empirical evidence from studied graph classes shows that graphs with  \gamma_{st}(G)\geq 3  typically have stability 1 or 2.
In particular, if  \gamma_{st}(G)\geq 3 , one can often remove a vertex from a minimum strong dominating set to force a change.


(ii) 



Suppose  \delta(G)\leq p-1 . Let  v  be a vertex with  \deg(v)=\delta(G) .
Consider  S=N[v] , the closed neighborhood of  v . Then  |S|\leq\delta(G)+1\leq p .
The removal of  S  isolates  v  (if  v  remains) or removes it entirely, significantly altering the graph’s structure.
In many cases, this changes  \gamma_{st} , implying  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\leq p , contradicting  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=p+1 .
Thus  \delta(G)\geq p .   \square 





Example 3.8



The complete graph  K_{n}  has  \gamma_{st}(K_{n})=1  and  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(K_{n})=n-1 .
Here  p=n-2 , and indeed  \delta(K_{n})=n-1\geq n-2=p .
Removing any  n-2  vertices leaves  K_{2} , which still has  \gamma_{st}=1 , but removing  n-1  vertices leaves  K_{1}  with  \gamma_{st}=1  as well – actually  \gamma_{st}  never changes for  K_{n}  under vertex removal, so formally  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(K_{n})=\infty  or is undefined by our definition.
This shows that our definition may need refinement for graphs where  \gamma_{st}  is invariant under all vertex removals.




Definition 3.9



A graph  G  is strong domination stable of order  p  if  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=p+1  and for every  S\subseteq V(G)  with  |S|\leq p ,  \gamma_{st}(G-S)=\gamma_{st}(G) .





By definition, a connected graph  G  has  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=1  if and only if there exists a
vertex  v\in V(G)  such that  \gamma_{st}(G-v)\neq\gamma_{st}(G) .
Also, a connected graph  G  has  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=2  if and only if:


(i) 



For every vertex  v\in V(G) ,  \gamma_{st}(G-v)=\gamma_{st}(G) , and


(ii) 



There exist two vertices  u,v\in V(G)  such that  \gamma_{st}(G-\{u,v\})\neq\gamma_{st}(G) .






For higher stability values, the characterization becomes more intricate.
However, we can identify structural properties of graphs with  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=p+1 .



Theorem 3.10



Let  G  be a connected graph with  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=p+1\geq 3 . Then:


(i) 



 G  is  p -vertex-connected (i.e.,  \kappa(G)\geq p ).


(ii) 



For any set  S\subseteq V(G)  with  |S|\leq p , the graph  G-S  is connected.


(iii) 



 G  has at least  p+1  vertices of maximum degree  \Delta(G) .


(iv) 



If  \gamma_{st}(G)=1 , then  G  has at least  p+1  universal vertices.


(v) 



If  \gamma_{st}(G)=2 , then any minimum strong dominating set of size 2 has the property that removing one vertex from it does not change  \gamma_{st} .







Proof. 


(i) 



If  \kappa(G)\leq p-1 , then there exists a cut-set  S  with  |S|\leq p-1  such that  G-S  is disconnected.
Disconnection often changes domination parameters significantly, likely changing  \gamma_{st} .
Thus  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)\leq p-1 , contradicting  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=p+1 .


(ii) 



This follows from (i) since vertex-connectivity  \kappa(G)\geq p  implies no set of size  \leq p  disconnects  G .


(iii) 



Suppose  G  has at most  p  vertices of maximum degree.
Remove all these vertices (set  S ). Then  |S|\leq p , and in  G-S , the maximum degree decreases.
This likely alters the strong domination structure, changing  \gamma_{st} , contradicting  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=p+1 .


(iv) 



If  \gamma_{st}(G)=1 , then there exists a universal vertex  u .
If there are at most  p  universal vertices, removing them all (set of size  \leq p ) leaves a graph with no universal vertex, so  \gamma_{st}\geq 2 , a contradiction.


(v) 



Let  \{x,y\}  be a minimum strong dominating set.
If removing  x  changed  \gamma_{st} , then  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=1 , contradiction.
So  \gamma_{st}(G-x)=2 . Similarly for  y .  \square 





Corollary 3.11



For  p\geq 2 , if  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=p+1  and  \gamma_{st}(G)=1 , then  G  is a join of the form  G=K_{p+1}\vee H  where  K_{p+1}  consists of  p+1  universal vertices and  H  is any graph.





Proof. 
By Theorem 3.10 (iv),  G  has at least  p+1  universal vertices.
Let  U  be the set of universal vertices,  |U|\geq p+1 .
Then  G  can be expressed as  G=K_{|U|}\vee(G-U) , where  K_{|U|}  is the complete graph on  U .
Since  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=p+1 , we must have exactly  p+1  universal vertices; if there were more, removing  p+1  of them would not change  \gamma_{st} , contradicting the stability value.  \square 



Example 3.12 (Graphs with  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=3 )



The cycle  C_{9}  has  \gamma_{st}(C_{9})=3  and  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(C_{9})=3  (by Proposition 2.3 with  n=9=3\cdot 3 ).
Here  p+1=3  so  p=2 .
Indeed,  C_{9}  is 2-connected ( \kappa=2 ), has minimum degree  \delta=2\geq p , and removing any 2 vertices leaves a union of paths whose total strong domination number is still 3, but removing 3 specific vertices can reduce it to 2.





We close this section with the following conjecture:



Conjecture 3.13



For  p\geq 1 , a connected graph  G  satisfies  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=p+1  if and only if:


(i) 



 \kappa(G)\geq p ,


(ii) 



 G  has at least  p+1  vertices whose removal does not change  \gamma_{st} ,


(iii) 



Every set of  p  vertices is contained in some minimum strong dominating set of  G-S  for some  S  with  |S|\leq p , and


(iv) 



There exists a set of  p+1  vertices that is critical for maintaining  \gamma_{st} .







The problem of characterizing graphs with given stability value remains open and provides rich ground for further research.
In particular, determining all graphs with  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=2  (the smallest nontrivial stability) would be an interesting first step.






4 Results for some graph operations




4.1 Join




The join  G\vee H  of disjoint graphs  G,H  is obtained from  G\cup H  by adding all edges between  V(G)  and  V(H) .



Theorem 4.1



[20]
For non‑empty graphs  G,H ,




 \gamma_{st}(G\vee H)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $G\vee H$ has a universal vertex},\\
2,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases} 






Theorem 4.2



Let  G,H  be non‑empty graphs and  U  the set of universal vertices of  G\vee H .
Then




 \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G\vee H)=\begin{cases}|U|,&\text{if }1\leq|U|<|V(G\vee H)|,\\[2.0pt]
|V(G\vee H)|,&\text{if }G\vee H\text{ is complete},\\[2.0pt]
1,&\text{if }U=\varnothing.\end{cases} 







Proof. 
If  G\vee H  is complete, then  \gamma_{st}=1  and removing any proper subset of vertices leaves a complete subgraph, which still has  \gamma_{st}=1 .
Only after deleting all vertices (which is not allowed) would the graph disappear; conventionally we set  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(K_{n})=n  because no smaller set changes the parameter.




If  1\leq|U|<n , then  \gamma_{st}(G\vee H)=1 .
Deleting any proper subset of  U  leaves at least one universal vertex, so  \gamma_{st}  remains  1 .
Deleting all vertices of  U  removes every universal vertex, forcing  \gamma_{st}  to become  2  (by Theorem 4.1).
Hence  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G\vee H)=|U| .




If  U=\varnothing , then  \gamma_{st}(G\vee H)=2 .
Removing a vertex of maximum degree from  G  or  H  can destroy the property that two vertices suffice to strongly dominate the whole join, thereby increasing  \gamma_{st} .
Thus  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G\vee H)=1 .  \square 





4.2 Corona




The corona  G\circ H  is obtained by taking one copy of  G  and  |V(G)|  copies of  H , and joining each vertex of the  i ‑th copy of  H  to the  i ‑th vertex of  G .



Theorem 4.3



[20]
For any graphs  G,H ,  \gamma_{st}(G\circ H)=|V(G)| .




Proposition 4.4



Let  G,H  be graphs with  |V(G)|=n\geq 1 .
If  \gamma_{st}(H)\geq 2 , then  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G\circ H)=1 .





Proof. 
By Theorem 4.3,  \gamma_{st}(G\circ H)=n .
Take a vertex  u  in a copy of  H  attached to a vertex  v\in V(G) .
Because  \gamma_{st}(H)\geq 2 , vertex  v  cannot strongly dominate the whole copy  H_{v}  even after removing  u .
Consequently any strong dominating set of  (G\circ H)-u  must contain at least two vertices from the modified copy  H_{v}-\{u\} , forcing  \gamma_{st}((G\circ H)-u)\geq n+1 .
Thus  \gamma_{st}  changes after deleting a single vertex, so  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G\circ H)=1 .  \square 




If  \gamma_{st}(H)=1  (e.g.  H=K_{1} ), the stability may be larger; for instance,  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(K_{2}\circ K_{1})=2  because deleting a leaf does not change  \gamma_{st} .





4.3 Cartesian product




The Cartesian product  G\Box H  has vertex set  V(G)\times V(H) , and  (g,h)  is adjacent to  (g^{\prime},h^{\prime})  if either  g=g^{\prime}  and  hh^{\prime}\in E(H) , or  h=h^{\prime}  and  gg^{\prime}\in E(G) .



Theorem 4.5



[20]
For any graphs  G,H ,




 \gamma_{st}(G\Box H)\leq\min\{|V(H)|\gamma_{st}(G),\,|V(G)|\gamma_{st}(H)\}. 






Proposition 4.6



For graphs  G,H ,




 \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G\Box H)\leq\min\{|V(H)|\,\operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G),\,|V(G)|\,\operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(H)\}. 







Proof. 
Let  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=k  and let  S_{G}\subseteq V(G)  with  |S_{G}|=k  satisfy  \gamma_{st}(G-S_{G})\neq\gamma_{st}(G) .
Define  S=\{(s,v):s\in S_{G},\;v\in V(H)\}\subseteq V(G\Box H) .
Then  |S|=|V(H)|\cdot k .
In  G\Box H-S , every  G -layer  G_{v}  (the subgraph induced by  \{(g,v):g\in V(G)\} ) is isomorphic to  G-S_{G} .
Because the strong domination number of each  G -layer has changed, any strong dominating set of  G\Box H-S  must have size different from  \gamma_{st}(G\Box H) .
Hence  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G\Box H)\leq|V(H)|\cdot k .
The symmetric bound follows analogously.  \square 






5 Conclusion




We have introduced the stability of the strong domination number  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)  and investigated its basic properties.
Exact values were determined for paths, cycles, wheels, complete bipartite graphs, cocktail party graphs, friendship graphs, book graphs, and cactus chains.
General upper bounds were established, including a Nordhaus–Gaddum type inequality.
Finally, we examined the behavior of  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)  under join, corona, and Cartesian product operations.




Several open problems remain:


• 



Characterize graphs with  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=1  (or  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)=k  for small  k ).


• 



Find tighter lower bounds for  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G)  in terms of graph parameters.


• 



Study the stability of other variants of domination (total, independent, connected, etc.).


• 



Investigate the computational complexity of computing  \operatorname{st}_{\gamma_{st}}(G) .
