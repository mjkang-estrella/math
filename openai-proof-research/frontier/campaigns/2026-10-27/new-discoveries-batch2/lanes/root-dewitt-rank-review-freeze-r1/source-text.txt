Background The Ferrers diagram for a partition is the unique top-left justified grid of boxes where each part corresponds to a row of boxes. For instance, the Ferrers diagram for the partition ( 5 , 4 , 1 ) (5,4,1) is The involution of conjugation on partitions is reflection across the main diagonal of the Ferrers diagram. We say two partitions are conjugates of one another if they differ by a reflection across the main diagonal. For instance, we say that ( 5 , 4 , 1 ) (5,4,1) and ( 3 , 2 , 2 , 2 , 1 ) (3,2,2,2,1) are conjugate. A partition is called self-conjugate if it is a fixed point of this operation, i.e. if its Ferrers diagram is symmetric across the diagonal. For instance, ( 4 , 2 , 1 , 1 ) (4,2,1,1) with Ferrers diagram is self-conjugate. In the literature, the conjugate of the partition λ \lambda is normally denoted by λ ¯ \overline{\lambda} or λ ′ \lambda^{\prime} . Future investigations The proof given here does not immediately generalize to other known congruences for ν 2 \nu_{2} , such as ν 2 ​ ( 36 ​ n + 30 ) ≡ 0 ( mod 4 ) \nu_{2}(36n+30)\equiv 0\pmod{4} , so a natural question would be whether it can be adapted for these classes. Indeed, it may be possible to prove an infinite class of congruences of this type by isolating what features of a numerical progression suffice to generalize the arguments. The maps produced in this paper can apply more generally to larger classes of partitions. It could be interesting to determine their utility in the proof of other identities. The map ϕ ¯ \overline{\phi} is of limited applicability, but the map ρ \rho which swaps the largest odd divisor of each part size and its multiplicity is an involution on the set of partitions wherein the values 2 λ i ​ ℓ ​ ( m i ) 2^{\lambda_{i}}\ell(m_{i}) are distinct. Conjugation is the most elementary means of proving evenness of sets of partitions; when proving divisibility by 4, it would seem at first glance that pairing up conjugate pairs is a natural strategy to attempt, but to our knowledge this is not a common method of proof in combinatorial partition theory. Of course, this may simply be due to the relative scarcity of combinatorially provable congruences modulo 4, but perhaps exploration of the technique could yield new results. We wish to raise an alternative point of interest: another common method of proving that a set of partitions has a given divisibility, say by m m , is to establish a rank statistic on the set being considered, which divides the set into m m equal classes, or into classes each of which itself has cardinality divisible by m m .
