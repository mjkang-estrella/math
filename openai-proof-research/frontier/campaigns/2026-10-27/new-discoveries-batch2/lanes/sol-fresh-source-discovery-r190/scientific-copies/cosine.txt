                                                                   EXTREMAL COSINE SIGN CORRELATIONS

                                                           SHENGYANG ZANG, YINGYU ZHAO, YUNHAO ZHAO, AND CHEN ZHOU


                                                    Abstract. We prove that four cosines with distinct positive integer frequencies have the same
                                                    sign on at least 1/33 of a period, with equality precisely for positive integer dilates of {1, 3, 11, 33}.
                                                    If both odd and even frequencies occur, the optimal proportion is 1/24; equality holds precisely
                                                    for odd positive integer dilates of {1, 3, 4, 12} and {2, 4, 5, 15}. For arbitrary n, we establish the
                                                    universal lower bound 41−n and construct frequency sets for which this proportion is at most
                                                    16nθ 4−n , where θ = log2 (3/2). These bounds determine the optimal exponential decay rate 1/4.

                                                                                            1. Introduction
                                              Sign correlations measure the agreement of oscillatory functions after their amplitudes have
                                           been discarded. Even for distinct cosine modes, whose inner product over a period is zero,
                                           the signs can remain correlated. The positions of their zeros are governed by the frequencies,
                                           and arithmetic relations among those frequencies can produce substantial departures from an
                                           independent-sign model. Cosine signs therefore provide a concrete setting in which to study how
                                           arithmetic structure affects simultaneous oscillation.
                                              One source of the question is the study of simultaneous sign patterns of orthogonal functions.
                                           In their work on an uncertainty principle for roots, Gonçalves, Oliveira e Silva, and Steinerberger
                                           (2017) proved that, at any prescribed finite set of real points, infinitely many Hermite polynomials
                                           of degree divisible by four are positive at every point. Their argument uses trigonometric
                                           asymptotics and recurrence of linear flows on a torus. The same work also exhibits arithmetic
                                           restrictions that exclude certain other sign patterns along this subsequence once the degree
                                           is sufficiently large. These results lead naturally from the occurrence of a sign pattern to the
                                           quantitative question of how often it occurs.
                                              In their subsequent work, Gonçalves, Oliveira e Silva, and Steinerberger (2021) established sign
                                           correlation laws for sequences with suitable trigonometric asymptotics, including eigenfunctions of
                                           Schrödinger operators and classical orthogonal polynomials. They fix two spatial points and vary
                                           the function index. Under the stated asymptotic and equidistribution hypotheses, the limiting
                                           proportion of agreement lies between 1/3 and 2/3, and both bounds are sharp. They explicitly
                                           asked whether analogous results hold at three or more points, and which prescribed sign patterns
                                           have densities uniformly separated from 0 or 1. The integer-frequency cosine problem studied by
                                           Dou et al. (2024) isolates the arithmetic trigonometric model behind these questions. Its spatial
                                           average can be analyzed directly, with the frequencies encoding the dependence among the signs.
                                              A related finite problem arises when the entries of a Fourier matrix are replaced by their signs:
                                           Clément and Steinerberger (2026) quantify the resulting failure of orthogonality for the signed
                                           discrete cosine transform. Together, these developments motivate the study of joint sign patterns
                                           beyond ordinary pairwise orthogonality, and of sharp bounds that remain valid across different
                                           arithmetic configurations.

Here | · | denotes Lebesgue measure. We use P for both lists and sets, since repetitions do not

positive near the origin, so P (A) > 0. The interval guaranteed by this observation shrinks as the

The extremal problem asks both how small P (A) can be for a fixed number of frequencies and
how its infimum decays as that number grows.
   Each individual sign is equally likely to be positive or negative. For n independent fair signs,
the probability of complete agreement would be 21−n . In the present problem all signs share the
same parameter, and their dependence can make agreement much less frequent. Thus even the
exponential scale of pn requires an understanding of the joint distribution. For a fixed n, one
can also ask whether the infimum is attained and which arithmetic configurations realize it.
   There is also a geometric interpretation. With t = x/(2π), regard a1 t, . . . , an t modulo 1 as
the positions of runners on a circular track. Then P (A) is the proportion of time for which all
runners lie in the fixed semicircle (−1/4, 1/4) or all lie in its complement. This interpretation was
noted by Dou et al. (2024). Its broader geometric setting has a longer history in the Diophantine
approximation and view-obstruction problems of Wills (1967) and Cusick (1973), which gave rise
to the lonely-runner conjecture.
   Several subsequent developments in that literature concern themes relevant here. Goddyn and
Wong (2006) investigated tight sets of runner speeds; Perarnau and Serra (2016) used correlations
between the times at which runners lie close to the origin to improve bounds for simultaneous
separation; and Kravitz (2021) studied possible values of maximum loneliness and proposed a
discrete spectrum near the conjectured minimum. These works illustrate the roles of arithmetic
dependence and extremal configurations in restrictions on a torus orbit. Maximum loneliness
optimizes a minimum circular distance over time. The quantity P (A) measures the time spent
in a fixed union of two cubes. Its extremal problem therefore requires uniform control of this
time average as the integer direction of the orbit varies.
   The two-frequency calculation of Gonçalves, Oliveira e Silva, and Steinerberger (2021) gives
p2 = 1/3, with equality precisely for positive integer dilates of {1, 3}. The next exact case was
settled by Dou, Goh, Liu, Legate, and Pettigrew (2024), whose work first circulated in 2022 and
was published in 2024. They proved p3 = 1/9 and identified every minimizer as a positive integer
dilate of {1, 3, 9}. Thus the three-frequency result determined both the sharp probability and
the arithmetic structure of all equality cases.
   Their proof also provided a way to reduce an unbounded frequency search to a finite one. After
removing the common divisor, Fourier formulas for pair correlations and arithmetic reductions
force any triple a < b < c with P (a, b, c) ≤ 1/9 to have all three entries odd and a = 1. A
scale-separation estimate shows that a third frequency much larger than the first two contributes
asymptotically a factor 1/2 to the agreement probability. Combining this estimate with the pair
correlation bounds yields b ≤ 7 and c ≤ 84, after which a finite computation completes the proof.
The key issue is thus the relative scales and reduced ratios of the frequencies: a common dilation
leaves the probability unchanged.
   As noted by Dou et al. (2024), the restriction of extremizers to controlled scales has an analogue
in the compactness principle of Steinerberger (2019). For smooth mean-zero functions on the
two-dimensional torus, that principle bounds the length of a closed geodesic maximizing the
absolute average of the function. In the sign problem, the discontinuous indicators call for direct

   The theorem shows that arithmetic dependence can make simultaneous agreement exponentially
rarer than the independent-sign benchmark 21−n . It also separates two aspects of the extremal
problem: the four-frequency theorems determine sharp constants and every equality configuration,
while Theorem 1.3 determines the exponential scale for arbitrarily many frequencies. The
remaining gap is polynomial; these estimates do not determine the precise factor multiplying
4−n .
   The bounds for general n follow from a difference-set argument and an iterative construction.
The four-frequency results also require arithmetic estimates for pair correlations and conditional
averages.
1.1. The exponential rate. The positive definite minorant approach connects the uniform
positivity question with classical extremal problems in harmonic analysis. A continuous positive
definite function on a torus has nonnegative Fourier coefficients, so its average along an integer-
frequency orbit through the identity is at least its Haar average. A related support-constrained
problem is Turán’s extremal problem, which maximizes the integral of a positive definite function
supported in a prescribed region and normalized at the identity; see Kolountzakis and Révész
(2006). In our lower bound, the needed uniformity comes from the difference-set structure of the
common-sign region and the classical sumset inequality on compact connected abelian groups.
The torus case was established by Macbeath (1953), and the general framework is due to Kneser
(1956); see also Tao (2018).
   For the lower bound, view the common-sign event as the inverse image of two cubes under the
homomorphism
                                  t 7−→ (a1 t, . . . , an t) ∈ (R/Z)n .
Their union is D − D, where D is a union of two smaller cubes of total measure 2 · 4−n . By
averaging, some translated section of D has at least that measure. Taking differences returns to
a section through the origin, and the circle sumset inequality doubles its measure. This gives
P (A) ≥ 41−n without additional arithmetic assumptions on the frequencies. The argument uses
the size of a translated section even when the original section of D is small, and so avoids any
dependence on the largest frequency.
   For the upper bound, we use the cyclic construction of Polak and Schrijver recorded in Polak
(2019, Theorem 9.3.3). Its separation property allows us to add a block of m odd frequencies
and retain just one of the arcs produced by dilation. The probability is divided by

at a cost of at most 3/2 relative to the factor 4−m . The phase points j(1, 4, . . . , 4m−1 )/Qm ,
for j ∈ Z/Qm Z, are pairwise separated by more than 1/4 in circular distance in at least one
coordinate. Thus the axis-parallel cubes of side length 1/4 centered at these points are disjoint.
Their number Qm is within a constant factor of the volume bound 4m , which explains the scale
relevant to our construction.
   Such cyclic separation is also used to construct independent sets in strong powers of circular
graphs in the study of Shannon capacity (Polak and Schrijver, 2019). The lattice constructions of
Buys, Polak, and Zuiddam (2026) include the theorem of Polak and Schrijver used here. For our
problem, the additional step is to preserve the discrete separation when grid points are replaced
by intervals of a suitable width. We give a base-four proof of the separation property, pass from
the cyclic grid to intervals of the circle, and iterate with increasing block lengths. The number of
iterations is at most log2 n + O(1), which accounts for the exponent θ = log2 (3/2).
