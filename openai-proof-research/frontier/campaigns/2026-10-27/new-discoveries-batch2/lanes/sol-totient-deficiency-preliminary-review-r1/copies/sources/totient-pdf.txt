                                                                                            Abstract
                                                     Ranjan and Singh proved that for every composite n which is not twice an odd prime,
                                                     φ(n) is at least one plus the sum of φ over the prime divisors of n,Pwith equality
                                                     exactly for n = 4, 12, 30. We study the weighted equation φ(n) = c + α p|n φ(p). We
                                                     determine the parameters (c, α) admitting infinite families of solutions, prove that all
                                                     remaining solutions are finite in number and arise from an explicit linear mechanism,
                                                     and compute them exactly in a range of parameters; the finiteness of {4, 12, 30} becomes
                                                     a special case. We also settle the analogous equations for the divisor functions τ and σ
                                                     and for the Jordan totients, and, from known results on the sum of the prime divisors,
                                                     derive the full asymptotic expansion of the average deficiency. In the range |c| ≤ 10,
                                                     1 ≤ α ≤ 6 the composite solution set is determined completely, and the number and
                                                     the size of the sporadic solutions are shown to be bounded by polynomials in |c| + α.
                                                     Finally, replacing the prime
                                                                                P values     φ(p) by their iterates φ(k) (p), we determine all
                                                                                        (k)
                                                     solutions of φ(n) = 1 + p|n φ (p): the solution sets are {1, 4, 12, 30}, {1, 3, 4, 10}
                                                     and, for every k ≥ 3, {1, 3, 4}; for the weighted iterated equation we exhibit an infinite
                                                     family governed by the safe primes.

is from satisfying (1): a simple identity reduces the average deficiency to classical results of
Alladi–Erdős and De Koninck–Ivić, which yields its full asymptotic expansion. Fourth (§7)
we replace the prime values φ(p) in (1) by their iterates φ(k) (p) and determine all solutions
for every k ≥ 1: the monotonicity of the iterates reduces each case to Theorem 1.1, and the
solution sets stabilise at {1, 3, 4} for all k ≥ 3.

4     The weighted equation: a complete structure theory
Consider, for integer parameters c and α ≥ 1, the weighted equation
                                                X
                                 φ(n) = c + α      φ(p),                                    (4)
                                                           p|n
                                                                    P
of which Theorem 1.1 is the case (c, α) = (1, 1). Write Sm := q|m φ(q) for the prime-
support sum, so that (4) reads φ(n) = c + αSn . In this section we determine the structure
of the composite solution set of (4) completely: parity constraints (Theorem 4.1), an exact
classification of the infinite families (Theorem 4.2), and a finiteness theorem for what re-
mains (Theorem 4.4). The structure theorem is then made completely explicit in the range
|c| ≤ 10, 1 ≤ α ≤ 6 (Theorem 4.12), and uniform polynomial bounds in |c| + α are derived
from it (Theorem 4.13).

4.1   Parity constraints
Theorem 4.1 (Parity constraints). Let n ≥ 4 be a composite solution of (4). Then: (i) if
c is odd and α is even, no such n exists; (ii) if c and α are both odd, n is even; (iii) if c is
even and α is odd, n is odd; (iv) if c and α are both even, no parity restriction holds.
Proof. For n ≥ 3 the value φ(n) is even. On the other side, φ(2) = 1 is the only odd value
of φ at a prime, whence Sn ≡ 12|n (mod 2) and the right-hand side of (4) is congruent
to c + α 12|n modulo 2. Requiring it to be even yields exactly the four cases. Case (iv) is
realized: (c, α) = (2, 2) has both the even solution 8 and the odd solution 9.

4.2   Classification of the infinite families
The exclusion of n = 2p in Theorem 1.1 is not an accident of the proof: the integers 2p form
an exact infinite family for the neighbouring pair (c, α) = (−1, 1), since φ(2p) = p − 1 =
S2p − 1. This phenomenon is completely general.

Theorem 4.2 (Infinite families). Let m ≥ 2 be fixed. Every integer n = mp, with p prime
and p ∤ m, satisfies (4) if and only if

                           α = φ(m)       and       c = −φ(m) Sm .

We call such a pair (c, α) matched, with modulus m. For each α there are only finitely many
matched moduli, since φ(m) = α has finitely many solutions m.

Proof. For p ∤ m, multiplicativity gives φ(mp) = φ(m)(p − 1) and Smp = Sm + p − 1.
Equation (4) becomes the identity in p

                           φ(m)(p − 1) = c + αSm + α(p − 1),

which holds for infinitely many (equivalently, for all admissible) primes p if and only if the
coefficients of p − 1 and the constant terms match: φ(m) = α and c + αSm = 0.

   For example, the matched pairs in the range |c| ≤ 10, 1 ≤ α ≤ 6 are

                                    m     (c, α) family
                                    2    (−1, 1) {2p}
                                    4    (−2, 2) {4p}
                                    3    (−4, 2) {3p}
                                    6    (−6, 2) {6p}
                                    8    (−4, 4) {8p}

and the pair (1, 1) of Theorem 1.1 is unmatched : φ(m) = 1 forces m = 2, which would
require c = −1 ̸= 1.

5    Simultaneous equations
Write (Eφ ), (Eτ ), (Eσ ) for equation (2) with f = φ, τ, σ respectively. Combining Theorems
1.1, 3.1 and 3.2 yields complete answers for all conjunctions.

Theorem 5.1. (i) The unique composite integer satisfying (Eφ ) and (Eτ ) simultaneously
is n = 4. (ii) No integer n ≥ 2 satisfies (Eφ ), (Eτ ) and (Eσ ) simultaneously; indeed already
(Eσ ) alone has no composite solution, and no prime satisfies (Eτ ).

Proof. (i) By Theorems 1.1 and 3.1 the composite solutions of (Eφ ) are {4, 12, 30} and those
of (Eτ ) are the prime squares; the intersection is {4}, since neither 12 nor 30 is the square
of a prime. One checks directly: φ(4) = 2 = 1 + φ(2) and τ (4) = 3 = 1 + τ (2).
    (ii) For a prime p, (Eτ ) reads 2 = 3, false; combined with Theorem 3.2 for composites
this settles the claim.

   Thus, imposing several of these equations at once collapses the solution set, first to {4}
and then to ∅.

8      Numerical verification
All finite claims of this paper were verified by an exhaustive computation over 2 ≤ n ≤
2 · 105 using SymPy. The composite solution sets found were: {4, 12, 30} for (Eφ ), in
agreement with [8]; {p2 ≤ 2 · 105 } and nothing else for (Eτ ); the empty set for (Eσ ),
for J2 , for the weighted equation with (c, α) = (1, 2), and for the triple system; and {4}
for the pair (Eφ ) ∧ (Eτ ). The expansion of Theorem 6.1 was compared with exact sieve
computations up to x = 107 , and the sporadic sets of Theorem 4.8 were obtained by the
bounded mechanism enumeration of Lemma 4.7, independently cross-checked against the
direct scan. The solution sets of Theorem 7.2 were checked for n ≤ 105 and 1 ≤ k ≤ 8. The
census of Theorem 4.12 was cross-checked against a direct scan of all composites n ≤ 106 ,
and the values |F (c, 1)| for 1 ≤ c ≤ 2000 quoted in Question 9.2 were obtained by the
bounded enumeration of Theorem 4.13(i)–(ii). The verification scripts are available from
the authors.


9      Concluding remarks and open problems
The results assembled here suggest that the natural home of the Ranjan–Singh theorem
is the family of prime–composite characterization problems, alongside Subbarao’s {4, 6, 22}
[9] and, in the unsolved direction, Lehmer’s problem [7]. We close with four problems.

Question 9.1. Theorem 4.13 gives |F (c, α)| ≪ L2 (log L)2 with L = |c| + α, whereas
Theorem 4.12 gives |F (c, α)| ≤ 9 for L ≤ 16, a direct scan of n ≤ 2 · 106 gives |F (c, α)| ≤ 19
for |c| ≤ 40, α ≤ 12, and |F (c, 1)| ≤ 9 for all 1 ≤ c ≤ 2000. Is supc |F (c, α)| finite for
each fixed α? The maximizers observed, (6, 6), (12, 4), (40, 8), (27, 9), (36, 12), all satisfy
α | c, as one expects from (5): when α divides c and φ(m′ ), the divisibility obstruction
in P − 1 = (c + αSm′ )/(φ(m′ ) − α) disappears. Is |F (c, α)| governed by gcd(c, α) and the
divisor structure of α?

Question 9.2. For α = 1, equation (4) reads ∆(n) = c − 1, with ∆ the deficiency of §6, so
that |F (c, 1)| is the multiplicity of the value c − 1 of ∆ on composite integers outside the
family {2p}, the analogue for ∆ of the classical multiplicity problems for φ [5]. For 1 ≤ c ≤
2000, F (c, 1) is empty for 341 values of c, the first being 6, 12, 22, 23, 24, 30, 42, 46, 60, 72;
the mean multiplicity is stable, close to 2; and every multiplicity 0, 1, . . . , 9 occurs, first at
c = 6, 2, 4, 1, 3, 27, 15, 403, 1047, 943. Thus the analogue of Carmichael’s conjecture fails for
∆ (F (c, 1) is a singleton for c = 2, 8, 10). Does every multiplicity occur, as Ford proved for
φ? Does the set of c with F (c, 1) = ∅ have positive density? Its parity bias is striking: it
contains about 30% of the even values of c against 5% of the odd ones, an even c requiring
an odd n by Theorem 4.1(iii).

Question 9.3. Theorem 7.2 settles the iterated equation with c = α = 1, and Proposition
7.5 shows that for (c, α) = (0, 4) and k = 2 the solution set is infinite if and only if there are
infinitely many safe primes. Describe, for generalP (c, α) and k ≥ 2, the conditional families
of the weighted iterated equation φ(n) = c + α p|n φ(k) (p): they are of the form {mp}

Question 9.4. Theorem 6.1 describes the average of ∆(n). Determine the distribution of
∆(n)/φ(n), which is expected to tend to 1 in mean.

    Finally, apart from the squares of primes (OEIS A001248), none of the solution sets
occurring here, in particular {4, 12, 30}, appears to be catalogued in the OEIS at the time
of writing; submitting them would usefully anchor the problem in the literature.
