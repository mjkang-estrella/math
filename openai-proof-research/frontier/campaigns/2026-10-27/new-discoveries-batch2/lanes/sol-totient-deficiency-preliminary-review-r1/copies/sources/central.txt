 Finite Non-Commutative Centrally Essential Rings of Minimal Order 
 Adel Abyzov 1 1 
 1 
 Kazan (Volga Region) Federal University, Kazan, Russia; email: adel.abyzov@kpfu.ru ,
Oleg Lyubimtsev 2 2 
 2 
 Nizhny Novgorod State University, Department of Algebra, Geometry and Discrete Mathematics, scientific and educational mathematical center ”Mathematics of technologies of the future”, Nizhny Novgorod, Russia; email: oleg_lyubimcev@mail.ru, https://orcid.org/0000-0002-8773-1943. ,
Askar Tuganbaev 3 3 
 3 
 National Research University MPEI, Department of Higher Mathematics, Moscow, Russia; Lomonosov Moscow State University, Department of Higher Algebra, Moscow, Russia; tuganbaev@gmail.com, https://orcid.org/0000-0001-9582-3314. 
 Abstract. A ring R with non-zero identity element is said to be centrally essential if for any non-zero element a of R , there exist non-zero central elements x and y such that ax=y . In the paper, we prove that the minimal order of a finite non-commutative centrally essential ring is equal to 64 . A partial classification of such rings is presented, and the existence of non-isomorphic non-commutative centrally essential rings of minimal order is proved. 
 Keywords: centrally essential ring, finite local ring.

 1 Introduction 
 We consider only associative rings with a non-zero identity element and unitary modules. A ring R is said to be centrally essential if for any non-zero element a\in R , there exist non-zero central elements x,y\in R such that ax=y . 4 4 
 4 
 It is clear that the ring R with center Z is centrally essential if and only if the module R_{Z} is an essential extension of the module Z_{Z} . The class of centrally essential rings is a proper extension of the class of commutative rings. In [ 5 , Proposition 3.3] , it is proved that semiprime centrally essential rings are commutative. In [ 4 , Example 5.2] a non-commutative centrally essential ring of order 128 is constructed. In this regard, a natural question arises concerning the minimum possible order of a non-commutative centrally essential ring. We denote by Z(R) and J(R) the center and the Jacobson radical of the ring R , respectively. It is well known that the Jacobson radical of a right Artinian ring is nilpotent. The smallest n such that J(R)^{n}=0 , is called the nilpotence index of J(R) . We write \mathrm{Ann}(S)=\{r\in R\,|\,rS=0\} for the left annihilator of the set S in the ring R . A ring R is said to be local if R/J(R) is a division ring. 
 The main results of the paper are Theorems 1.1, 1.2 and 1.3. 
 1.1. Theorem. The smallest finite non-commutative centrally essential ring has 64 elements. 
 1.2. Theorem. Let R be a finite non-commutative centrally essential ring of order 64 . Then: 
 1. 
 The ring R is local. 
 2. 
 The factor ring R/J(R) is isomorphic to the field \mathbb{F}_{2} and the radical J(R) has order 32 . 
 3. 
 The center Z(R) has order 16 and the intersection Z_{0}=Z(R)\cap J(R) has order 8 . 
 1.3. Theorem. Within the class of non-commutative centrally essential algebras of order 64 over the field \mathbb{F}_{2} satisfying the condition
 J(R)^{2}\subseteq\mathrm{Ann}(J(R))\cap Z(R) , there exist exactly two families distinguished by an algebraic invariant – the dimension of the annihilator \dim_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}W_{0}\in\{2,1\} , where W_{0}=\mathrm{Ann}(J(R))\cap Z(R) . These families are described as follows. 
 Family 1 ( \dim_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}W_{0}=2 ) 
 The algebra has a basis \{1,a,b,c,s_{1},s_{2}\} , with J(R)=\langle a,b,c,s_{1},s_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{2}} , Z(R)=\langle 1,c,s_{1},s_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{2}} , and W_{0}=\langle s_{1},s_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{2}} . The multiplication of the basis elements of the radical is defined by the rules: ac=ca=s_{1} ; bc=cb=s_{2} ; ab=s_{1} , ba=0 ; c^{2}=0 . The elements s_{1} and s_{2} annihilate J(R) . The squares a^{2}=\alpha and b^{2}=\beta are parametrized by elements \alpha,\beta\in W_{0} . 
 Family 2 ( \dim_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}W_{0}=1 ) 
 The algebra has a basis \{1,a,b,c_{1},c_{2},w\} ,
 J(R)=\langle a,b,c_{1},c_{2},w\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{2}} ,
 Z(R)=\langle 1,c_{1},c_{2},w\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{2}} and
 W_{0}=\langle w\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{2}} .
The multiplication of basis elements of the radical is defined by the rules:
 ac_{1}=c_{1}a=w , bc_{1}=c_{1}b=0 ;
 ac_{2}=c_{2}a=0 , bc_{2}=c_{2}b=w ;
 ab=w , ba=0 ; c_{1}^{2}=c_{2}^{2}=c_{1}c_{2}=c_{2}c_{1}=0 .
The element w annihilates the radical J(R) . The squares a^{2}=\alpha w and b^{2}=\beta w are parametrized by scalars \alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{2} . 
 2 Proof of Theorem 1.1 
 Theorem 1.1 follows from Lemmas 2.1, 2.2 and 2.3. 
 2.1. Lemma. There do not exist finite non-commutative centrally essential rings of order \leq 32 . 
 Proof. Let R be a finite non-commutative centrally essential ring of minimal order. Then R is a local ring. Indeed, in any centrally essential ring, all idempotents are central; see [ 5 , Lemma 2.3] . Lifting idempotents modulo a nilpotent ideal allows a unique decomposition of a finite ring R into a direct product of finite local rings. It is clear that a direct product of such rings is centrally essential if and only if each of its factors is a centrally essential ring. If there were more than one factor, then a non-commutative factor would have strictly smaller order, which contradicts the minimality of R . 
 Let |R|=p^{n} , Z=Z(R) , J=J(R) and the factor ring R/J\cong\mathbb{F}_{q} , where q=p^{f} . We set Z_{0}=Z\cap J . Let us obtain an upper bound for the cardinality of the set Z_{0} . Since the ring R is not commutative, the index of its center as an additive subgroup, [R:Z] , is at least p^{2} . Indeed if [R:Z]=p , then the additive factor group R/Z is a cyclic group generated by some residue class a+Z . Then any element of the ring is of the form z+ka , where z\in Z ,
 k\in\mathbb{Z} and [z_{1}+k_{1}a,z_{2}+k_{2}a]=0 , i.e. the ring R is commutative. Consequently, |Z|\leq|R|/p^{2}=p^{n-2} . Since 1\in Z and 1\notin J , we have that
 Z_{0}=Z\cap J is a proper subgroup in Z . Since Z is a finite p -group, the index of any of its proper subgroup is not less than p . Therefore, |Z|\geq p|Z_{0}| . Combining the inequalities, we obtain an upper bound for any prime integer p : 
 |Z_{0}|\leq\frac{|Z|}{p}\leq\frac{p^{n-2}}{p}=p^{n-3}. 
 Now we obtain a lower bound on the cardinality of the set Z_{0} . First, note that if J=0 , then the ring R is semiprime. However, semiprime centrally essential rings are commutative; see [ 5 , Proposition 3.3] . Consequently, J is a nilpotent ideal with index of nilpotency k , where k\geq 2 . Since J is a nilpotent ideal with nilpotence index k , it follows from [ 1 , Lemma 2.4] that J^{k-1}\cap Z(R) is a central ideal. In addition, since the ring R is centrally essential, we have J^{k-1}\cap Z(R)\neq 0 . Therefore, the annihilator \mathrm{Ann}(J) contains non-zero central element z_{1}\in W_{0}=\mathrm{Ann}(J)\cap Z_{0} . Since the ring R is not commutative, it follows from [ 3 ] that there exists an element
 r\in J\setminus Z_{0} . Then there exists an element x\in Z\setminus\{0\} such that
 rx\in Z\setminus\{0\} . If x\notin J , then x is invertible in R , whence
 r\in Zx^{-1}=Z and r\in Z_{0} . This is a contradiction. Therefore,
 x\in Z_{0}\setminus\{0\} . Since rz_{1}=0 and rx\neq 0 , we have that x\neq z_{1} . Consequently, Z_{0} contains at least three distinct elements 0 , z_{1} and x . We obtain that
 |Z_{0}|\geq 3 . We also remark that for p=2 , we have that |Z_{0}|\geq 4 , since the order is a power of two. Consequently, 3\leq|Z_{0}|\leq p^{n-3} . We verify possible orders |R|\leq 32 . If p\geq 3 , it follows from |R|\leq 32 that n\leq 3 . Then |Z_{0}|\leq p^{3-3}=1 ; this contradicts the fact that |Z_{0}|\geq 3 . Therefore, the characteristic of the residue field cannot be odd. 
 Let p=2 . We have |Z_{0}|\leq 2^{n-3} . Since |Z_{0}|\geq 4 , we have that n\geq 5 . By considering |R|\leq 32 , we obtain n=5 , that is |R|=32 . In addition, |Z_{0}|\leq 2^{5-3}=4 , whence |Z_{0}|=4 . We consider possible variants for the degree f (where q=2^{f} ). In a finite local ring of order p^{n} with residue field \mathbb{F}_{p^{f}} , the degree f must be a divisor of n . Indeed, since the characteristic of the field \mathbb{F}_{p^{f}} is p , we have
 p\cdot 1\in J , which implies pJ^{k}\subseteq J^{k+1} for all k\geq 0 . Therefore, the filtration quotients J^{k}/J^{k+1} are vector spaces over the field R/J\cong\mathbb{F}_{p^{f}} . It follows that f is a divisor of n .
Since n=5 is a prime integer, cases f=1 and f=5 are only possible. If f=5 , then
 |J|=32/32=1 and the ring R is commutative. This is a contradiction. Therefore, the unique possible variant is f=1 (in addition |J|=16 ). 
 The additive group, Z_{0} consists of 4 elements: \{0,z_{1},x,y\} , where z_{1}\in W_{0} . Since W_{0}\subsetneq Z_{0} , we have W_{0}=\{0,z_{1}\} . Then, for all r\in J\setminus Z_{0} , we have
 0\neq ry=r(x+z_{1})=rx\in Z_{0} since the ring R is centrally essential. 
 We consider a homomorphism of additive groups L_{x}\colon J\to Z_{0} such that r\mapsto rx . It follows from the above and the inclusion Z^{2}_{0}\subseteq Z_{0} that L_{x} is well-defined and \ker(L_{x})\subseteq Z_{0} . Since |\text{Im}(L_{x})|\leq|Z_{0}|=4 , we have |J|/|\ker(L_{x})|\leq 4 , whence |\ker(L_{x})|\geq 16/4=4 . Thus, \ker(L_{x})=Z_{0} , and L_{x} induces a bijection J/Z_{0}\to Z_{0} . There exists u\in J such that ux=x , implying (1-u)x=0 . Since u\in J , the element (1-u) is invertible. Hence, x=0 . This is a contradiction. Thus, all possible cases for |R|\leq 32 are ruled out, and the lemma is proved. \square 
 2.2. Lemma. 
There are no finite non-commutative centrally essential rings with orders strictly greater than 32 and strictly less than 64. 
 Proof. Let R be a finite non-commutative centrally essential ring, 32 
 R=R_{1}\times R_{2}\times\dots\times R_{k}. 
 Let R_{1} be a non-commutative factor. If k\geq 2 , then the order of the factor |R_{1}| is a proper divisor of order |R| . The greatest proper divisor of any positive integer \leq 63 does not exceed [63/2]=31 . Therefore, |R_{1}|\leq 31 . By Lemma 2.1, such a ring does not exist. If k=1 , then the ring R is local and its order must be a power of prime integer. Among the powers of prime numbers in the interval (32,64) , there are only prime numbers and the square of a prime number, 49=7^{2} . Every ring of prime order or of order p^{2} is always commutative; see [ 2 ] . This is a contradiction. \square 
 2.3. Lemma. There exists a non-commutative centrally essential ring consisting of 64 elements. 
 Proof. We construct a 6 -dimensional algebra R over the field of two elements
 \mathbb{F}_{2} . The order of such an algebra is equal to |R|=2^{6}=64 . We define the following basis of the algebra:
 \{1,a,b,c,s_{1},s_{2}\} . The unity 1 is a multiplicative identity element. The Jacobson radical
 J is equal to \langle a,b,c,s_{1},s_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{2}} . For basis elements of the radical, we set 
 a^{2}=b^{2}=c^{2}=0,ab=s_{1},ba=0,ac=ca=s_{1},bc=cb=s_{2}, 
 s_{1}x=xs_{1}=0,s_{2}x=xs_{2}=0\quad\mbox{for all}\,x\in J. 
 Since the product of any two elements from J belongs to the subspace
 S=\langle s_{1},s_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{2}} and S\subseteq\mathrm{Ann}(J) , we have that J^{3}=0 and the identity (xy)z=x(yz) is trivially true. Next, since ab=s_{1}\neq 0 and ba=0 , we have that the ring R is not commutative. 
 Let us find the center Z(R) of the ring R . Let x\in R , then x=k_{0}1+k_{a}a+k_{b}b+k_{c}c+k_{1}s_{1}+k_{2}s_{2} , where k_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2} . We have: 
 [x,a]=xa-ax=k_{b}[b,a]+k_{c}[c,a]=k_{b}(0-s_{1})+k_{c}(s_{1}-s_{1})=k_{b}s_{1}, 
 whence k_{b}=0 . Next, 
 [x,b]=xb-bx=k_{a}[a,b]+k_{c}[c,b]=k_{a}s_{1}+k_{c}(s_{2}-s_{2})=k_{a}s_{1}, 
 whence k_{a}=0 . Since [x,c]=0 , we have that Z(R)=\langle 1,c,s_{1},s_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{2}} . 
 Every non-central element r\in R\setminus Z(R) is of the form r=k_{a}a+k_{b}b+z , where
 z\in Z(R) and (k_{a},k_{b})\neq(0,0) . Then 
 rc=(k_{a}a+k_{b}b+k_{0}1+k_{c}c+k_{1}s_{1}+k_{2}s_{2})c=k_{0}c+k_{a}s_{1}+k_{b}s_{2}. 
 Since the basis vectors c,s_{1},s_{2} are linearly independent and at least one of the coefficients k_{a},k_{b} is non-zero, it follows that rc\neq 0 and rc\in Z(R) . Therefore, the algebra R is centrally essential. \square 
 3 Proof of Theorem 1.2 
 1. Since all idempotents in a centrally essential ring are central, a finite centrally essential ring decomposes uniquely into a direct product of local centrally essential rings. If the ring R has a non-trivial decomposition, then the order of the non-commutative factor does not exceed 64/2=32 . However, by Lemma 2.1, there are no non-commutative centrally essential rings of order \leq 32 . Consequently, the ring R is local. 
 2. Let R/J(R)\cong\mathbb{F}_{q} . Since |R|=64=2^{6} , we have q=2^{f} . Thus, f must be a divisor of 6 , i.e., f\in\{1,2,3,6\} . If q=64 ( f=6 ), then |J(R)|=1 . Consequently, the ring R\cong\mathbb{F}_{64} is commutative, which contradicts the theorem’s hypothesis. We prove that q=2 ( f=1 ). We assume that q\geq 4 . It follows from [R:Z(R)]\geq 4 that |Z(R)|\leq 16 . Since [Z(R):Z_{0}]\geq 2 , we have that |Z_{0}|\leq 8 . The set W_{0}=\mathrm{Ann}(J)\cap Z_{0} is non-zero (see the proof of Lemma 2.1), consequently |W_{0}|\geq 2 . Since the ring R is centrally essential, we have that W_{0}\subsetneq Z_{0} , whence |Z_{0}|\geq 4 . 
 Let q=8 ( f=3 ). Then |J(R)|=8 . From the estimate obtained earlier we have
 4\leq|Z_{0}|\leq 8 . If |Z_{0}|=8 , then Z_{0}=J(R)\subseteq Z(R) and the ring R is commutative. If |Z_{0}|=4 , then |J(R)/Z_{0}|=2 . Therefore, the radical
 J(R)=\langle Z_{0},j_{0}\rangle is commutative. The canonical epimorphism of the groups of units R^{*}\to(R/J(R))^{*}\cong\mathbb{F}_{8}^{*} has a kernel 1+J(R) of order 8 . Consequently, there exists an element \omega\in R^{*} of multiplicative order 7 such that its coset \omega+J(R) generates the field R/J(R) . The action by conjugation by \omega on the two-element quotient group J(R)/Z_{0} is trivial; therefore, \omega j_{0}\omega^{-1}=j_{0}+z for some z\in Z_{0} . Multiplying on the right by \omega , we obtain \omega j_{0}=j_{0}\omega+z\omega . Since
 z\in Z(R) , applying this relation repeatedly yields \omega^{k}j_{0}=j_{0}\omega^{k}+kz\omega^{k} . For k=7 , we have \omega^{7}=1 , which implies j_{0}=j_{0}+7z . Because Z_{0} is a finite 2 -group, multiplication by the odd number 7 is an automorphism of the group Z_{0} . It follows that z=0 , and thus \omega commutes with j_{0} . Since \omega and J(R) generate the entire ring R , and J(R) is commutative, the ring R is commutative.
This is a contradiction. 
 Suppose q=4 ( f=2 ). Then |J(R)|=16 . Let us consider the possible orders
 |Z_{0}|\in\{4,8\} . Suppose |Z_{0}|=4 . Then |W_{0}|=2 . Choose an element
 c\in Z_{0}\setminus W_{0} . Following the same steps as in the proof of Lemma 2.1, one can verify that the homomorphism L_{c}:J(R)\to Z_{0} (defined by r\mapsto rc ) induces a bijection
 J(R)/Z_{0}\to Z_{0} , which leads to a contradiction. Finally, assume |Z_{0}|=8 . Then
 |J(R)/Z_{0}|=16/8=2 , which implies that J(R)=\langle Z_{0},j_{0}\rangle is a commutative ideal. Analogous to the case q=8 , choose an element \omega\in R^{*} of multiplicative order 3 such that its image generates R/J(R)\cong\mathbb{F}_{4} . This element acts trivially on J(R)/Z_{0} : \omega j_{0}\omega^{-1}=j_{0}+z for some z\in Z_{0} . Raising this to the third power yields j_{0}=j_{0}+3z , which implies z=0 and \omega j_{0}=j_{0}\omega . Since \omega and J(R) generate R , the ring R is commutative. This is a contradiction. 
 Thus, the assumption q\geq 4 leads to a contradiction in all cases. Consequently,
 R/J(R)\cong\mathbb{F}_{2} and |J(R)|=64/2=32 . 
 3. Since the ring R is not commutative, the index of its center as an additive group, [R:Z(R)] , is at least 4. Therefore, |Z(R)|\leq 64/4=16 . It follows from 1\in Z(R)\setminus J(R) that Z_{0}=Z(R)\cap J(R) is a subgroup of index 2 in Z(R) . Consequently, |Z_{0}|=|Z(R)|/2\leq 8 . Since
 W_{0}\subsetneq Z_{0} and W_{0}\neq\{0\} , we have that |W_{0}|\geq 2 . Therefore, |Z_{0}|\geq 4 . This implies that |Z_{0}|=4 or |Z_{0}|=8 ; in addition |Z(R)|=8 or |Z(R)|=16 , respectively. Suppose that |Z(R)|=8 and |Z_{0}|=4 , Z_{0}=\{0,z_{1},x,y\} , where z_{1}\in W_{0} (see the proof of the lemma 2.1). Then the mapping L_{x}:J(R)\to Z_{0} ( r\mapsto rx ) is a well-defined homomorphism of additive groups with kernel \ker(L_{x})\subseteq Z_{0} . But then we have |J(R)|=|ker(L_{x})|\textcurrency|Im(L_{x})|\leq|Z_{0}|\cdot|Z_{0}|=4\cdot 4=16 , which contradicts the equality |J(R)|=32 . \square 
 4 Proof of Theorem 1.3 
 We set J=J(R) and Z_{0}=Z(R)\cap J . Since J^{2}\subseteq W_{0} , we have that J^{3}=0 and the associativity is proved. We have \dim J=5 . The case \dim Z_{0}=2 is impossible. Indeed, if
 \dim Z_{0}=2 , then \dim W_{0}=1 (since 0\subsetneq W_{0}\subsetneq Z_{0} ). Let
 Z_{0}=\langle c,w\rangle and W_{0}=\langle w\rangle . We consider the operator of right multiplication L_{c}:J\to W_{0} defined by the rule r\mapsto rc . It is well defined, since for any
 r\in J , we have that rc\in J^{2}\subseteq W_{0} . Since \dim J=5 and \dim W_{0}=1 , it follows from the rank-nullity theorem \dim\ker L_{c}\geq 5-1=4 . Consequently, |\ker L_{c}|\geq 16 . Since
 |Z_{0}|=4 , there exists a non-central element r\in\ker L_{c}\setminus Z_{0} and rc=0 .
Since w\in W_{0}\subseteq\mathrm{Ann}(J) , we have that rw=0 . Therefore, rZ_{0}=0 ; this contradicts the fact that the ring R is centrally essential. Consequently, \dim Z_{0}=3 , whence
 \dim W_{0}\in\{1,2\} . We consider these cases. 
 Family 1 ( \dim W_{0}=2 ). 
Let W_{0}=\langle s_{1},s_{2}\rangle and Z_{0}=\langle c,s_{1},s_{2}\rangle . We consider the operator of right multiplication L_{c}:J\to W_{0} ( r\mapsto rc ). Since \dim J=5 and
 \dim W_{0}=2 , the dimension of the kernel \dim\ker L_{c} is not less than 3 . In addition, rZ_{0}\neq 0 for the non-central element r , otherwise, the ring R is not centrally essential. Then \ker L_{c}\subseteq Z_{0} . By considering \dim Z_{0}=3 , we obtain \ker L_{c}=Z_{0} , which means that Z_{0}c=0 . From this, Z^{2}_{0}=0 follows, given that Z_{0}W_{0}=0 (in particular, c^{2}=0 ). Consequently, L_{c} induces a well-defined isomorphism J/Z_{0}\to W_{0} . We choose a elements a,b\in J\setminus Z_{0} whose cosets form a basis of J/Z_{0} such that ac=s_{1} and bc=s_{2} . 
 Since the algebra R is not commutative, the commutator [a,b] is not equal to 0 . Given that J^{2}\subseteq W_{0} , we have t=[a,b]\in W_{0}\setminus\{0\} . We show that, by suitably changing the bases in J , we can achieve the equality [a,b]=s_{1} . If t=s_{1} , the required equality holds. We show that if t=s_{2} , we can satisfy the condition [a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]=s_{1}^{\prime} . Let us make the substitution a^{\prime}=b , b^{\prime}=a+b . Since the characteristic of the ring R is 2 , we have that 
 [a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]=[b,a+b]=[b,a]=-[a,b]=s_{2}. 
 Next, we pass to a new basis for the space W_{0} by setting s_{1}^{\prime}=s_{2} and s_{2}^{\prime}=s_{1}+s_{2} . In the new basis, we obtain the desired relation [a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]=s_{1}^{\prime} . Multiplication by c is also consistent with the new basis: a^{\prime}c=bc=s_{2}=s_{1}^{\prime} and b^{\prime}c=(a+b)c=s_{1}+s_{2}=s_{2}^{\prime} .
In the case t=s_{1}+s_{2} , by making the substitutions a^{\prime}=a+b , b^{\prime}=a and
 s_{1}^{\prime}=s_{1}+s_{2} , s_{2}^{\prime}=s_{1} , we again obtain [a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]=s_{1}^{\prime} . Thus, we may assume that
 [a,b]=s_{1} . 
 In addition, without loss of generality, we can set ba=0 . Indeed, given that Z_{0}^{2}=0 , upon substituting a^{\prime}=a+x and b^{\prime}=b+y (where x,y\in Z_{0} ), we have b^{\prime}a^{\prime}=ba+xb+ya . Since the operators L_{a} and L_{b} map Z_{0} onto the one-dimensional subspaces
 \langle s_{1}\rangle and \langle s_{2}\rangle , respectively, we have that the elements of W_{0} are linear combinations xb+ya . Therefore, there exist x,y\in Z_{0} such that xb+ya=ba and b^{\prime}a^{\prime}=0 . In addition, a^{\prime}c=(a+x)c=ac+xc=s_{1}+0=s_{1} , since xc=0 for x\in Z_{0} . Next, b^{\prime}c=(b+y)c=bc+yc=s_{2}+0=s_{2} . Consequently, the substitution is correct. The squares a^{2}=\alpha and b^{2}=\beta are selected from W_{0} . Distinct pairs (\alpha,\beta) parametrize this family. 
 Note that under this substitution, the commutator [a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]=[a,b]=s_{1} remains unchanged. Finally, since the squares a^{2}=\alpha and b^{2}=\beta belong to W_{0} , they do not affect the commutators of the basis elements or the multiplication by the element c . Therefore, as in the proof of Lemma 2.3, for any \alpha,\beta\in W_{0} , the equalities Z(R)=\langle 1,c,s_{1},s_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{2}} and W_{0}=\langle s_{1},s_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{2}} hold; furthermore, for any non-central element r\in R\setminus Z(R) , we have 0\neq rc\in Z(R) , which means that the algebra R is centrally essential. 
 Family 2 ( \dim W_{0}=1 ). 
Let W_{0}=\langle w\rangle and Z_{0}=\langle c_{1},c_{2},w\rangle . Since J^{2}\subseteq W_{0} and JW_{0}=0 , the following linear map is well-defined: 
 \Phi:J\to\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(Z_{0}/W_{0},W_{0}),\qquad\Phi(r)(z+W_{0})=rz. 
 If r\in\ker\Phi , then rZ_{0}=0 . The central essentiality of the algebra R implies that no non-central element r\in J\setminus Z_{0} can annihilate Z_{0} ; hence, \ker\Phi\subseteq Z_{0} . Given that \dim J=5 and \dim\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(Z_{0}/W_{0},W_{0})=2 , the rank-nullity theorem yields \dim\ker\Phi\geq 5-2=3 . Since \dim Z_{0}=3 , it follows that
 \ker\Phi=Z_{0} , which implies Z_{0}^{2}=0 , that is, c_{1}^{2}=c_{2}^{2}=c_{1}c_{2}=c_{2}c_{1}=0 . Furthermore, \Phi induces an isomorphism between the two-dimensional spaces
 \bar{\Phi}:J/Z_{0}\to\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(Z_{0}/W_{0},W_{0}) . Consequently, one can choose elements a,b\in J\setminus Z_{0} whose images in J/Z_{0} form a basis dual to the basis
 \{c_{1}+W_{0},c_{2}+W_{0}\} of the space Z_{0}/W_{0} , such that 
 ac_{1}=w,\quad ac_{2}=0,\quad bc_{1}=0,\quad bc_{2}=w. 
 Since the algebra R is non-commutative, the commutator [a,b]\neq 0 . Given that
 [a,b]\in J^{2}\subseteq W_{0}=\langle w\rangle , we have ab+ba=[a,b]=w . Since
 ab,ba\in\{0,w\} , there are two possibilities: ab=w,ba=0 or ab=0,ba=w . In the latter case, we can perform the substitution a^{\prime}=a+c_{2} . Since Z_{0}^{2}=0 , the relations a^{\prime}c_{1}=w and a^{\prime}c_{2}=0 are preserved, while a^{\prime}b=ab+c_{2}b=w and ba^{\prime}=ba+bc_{2}=0 . Thus, without loss of generality, we may assume that ab=w and ba=0 . The squares
 a^{2}=\alpha w and b^{2}=\beta w are parameterized by scalars \alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{2} . 
 Conversely, for any pair (\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2} , the algebra R defined by these rules has a center Z(R)=\langle 1,c_{1},c_{2},w\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{2}} and
 W_{0}=\langle w\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{2}} . For an arbitrary non-central element
 r=k_{0}1+k_{a}a+k_{b}b+z\in R\setminus Z(R) (where z\in Z_{0} and
 (k_{a},k_{b})\neq(0,0) ), we have rc_{1}=k_{0}c_{1}+k_{a}w and rc_{2}=k_{0}c_{2}+k_{b}w . Since
 (k_{a},k_{b})\neq(0,0) , at least one of the products rc_{1} or rc_{2} is non-zero and belongs to Z_{0}\subseteq Z(R) , which implies that R is centrally essential. \square 
 4.1. Remark. 
Distinct pairs (\alpha,\beta) define algebras within Family 1; their classification (up to isomorphism) requires the construction of precise invariants, leaving this subclass open for further study. For Family 2 ( \alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{2} ), a complete classification up to isomorphism is obtained quite easily. Specifically, for any element x=x_{1}a+x_{2}b+z\in J(R) (where x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{2} and z\in Z_{0} ), given the centrality of Z_{0} , the identity Z_{0}^{2}=0 , and the characteristic 2 , we have: 
 x^{2}=x_{1}^{2}a^{2}+x_{2}^{2}b^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}(ab+ba)=(x_{1}^{2}\alpha+x_{2}^{2}\beta+x_{1}x_{2})w=(x_{1}\alpha+x_{2}\beta+x_{1}x_{2})w. 
 Furthermore, the quadratic function Q(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}\alpha+x_{2}\beta+x_{1}x_{2} over the field \mathbb{F}_{2} vanishes at three pairs (x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2} when (\alpha,\beta)\in\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\} (in which case J(R) contains exactly 3\cdot|Z_{0}|=24 elements with a zero square), and only at the zero pair (0,0) when (\alpha,\beta)=(1,1) (in which case J(R) contains exactly 1\cdot|Z_{0}|=8 elements with a zero square). Moreover, the algebras with parameters (1,0) and (0,1) are isomorphic to the algebra with parameters (0,0) . If \alpha=1,\beta=0 ( a^{2}=w,b^{2}=0 ), then the substitution 
 a^{\prime}=b+c_{1},\quad b^{\prime}=a+b,\quad c_{1}^{\prime}=c_{1}+c_{2},\quad c_{2}^{\prime}=c_{1} 
 preserves all the defining relations of Family 2:
 a^{\prime}c_{1}^{\prime}=w , a^{\prime}c_{2}^{\prime}=0 , b^{\prime}c_{1}^{\prime}=0 , b^{\prime}c_{2}^{\prime}=w , a^{\prime}b^{\prime}=w , b^{\prime}a^{\prime}=0 , while yielding
 (a^{\prime})^{2}=b^{2}=0 and (b^{\prime})^{2}=a^{2}+b^{2}+ab+ba=w+0+w+0=0 , i.e.,
 (\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})=(0,0) . Similarly, the case (0,1) reduces to (0,0) via the substitution a^{\prime}=a , b^{\prime}=a+b+c_{2} , c_{1}^{\prime}=c_{1}+c_{2} , c_{2}^{\prime}=c_{2} . Thus, Family 2 contains exactly two pairwise non-isomorphic algebras, corresponding to the parameter pairs (\alpha,\beta)=(0,0) and (\alpha,\beta)=(1,1) . 
 4.2 Remark. There exists a non-commutative centrally essential ring of order 64 which is not an algebra over the field \mathbb{F}_{2} and has characteristic 4 . 
 Indeed, we consider the additive group
 R\cong\mathbb{Z}_{4}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{2} of order 64 . We define generators 1 (it has order 4) and a,b,c,s (they are of order 2). The element 2\cdot 1=2 has order 2 and belongs to J(R) . Let us find the radical J=\langle 2,a,b,c,s\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}} of order 32 . We define the multiplication of elements of J by the rules a^{2}=b^{2}=c^{2}=0 , ab=2,ba=0 , ac=ca=2 , bc=cb=s .
We remark that the elements 2 and s annihilate J(R) . Since J^{3}=0 , we have that the constructed ring is associative. In addition, it follows from ab=2\neq 0=ba that the ring R is not commutative. Since 2\cdot 1\neq 0 , the characteristic of the ring R is equal to 4 . Consequently, R is not an algebra over \mathbb{F}_{2} . Let us find the center Z(R) . To do this, we calculate commutators of the element x=k_{0}1+k_{a}a+k_{b}b+k_{c}c+k_{s}s , where k_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{4} ,
 k_{a},k_{b},k_{c},k_{s}\in\{0,1\} : 
 [x,a]=k_{b}[b,a]+k_{c}[c,a]=k_{b}(-2)=2k_{b}\quad\mbox{(calculation in}\,\mathbb{Z}_{4}). 
 Then k_{b}=0 . Next, [x,b]=k_{a}[a,b]=2k_{a} . Therefore, k_{a}=0 . Consequently, the center
 Z(R) coincides with the set \langle 1,c,s\rangle . The intersection Z_{0} of Z(R) with the radical J is equal to \langle 2,c,s\rangle ; it has order 8 . 
 For any non-central element r\in J\setminus Z_{0} , we have that r=k_{a}a+k_{b}b+z , where
 z\in Z_{0} and (k_{a},k_{b})\neq(0,0) . Then rc=k_{a}(ac)+k_{b}(bc)+zc=k_{a}(2)+k_{b}(s) , where
 c\in Z(R)\setminus\{0\} . Since the elements 2 and s are linearly independent over
 \mathbb{Z}_{2} , their linear combination is equal to zero if and only if k_{a}=0 and k_{b}=0 . Since the element r is not central, we have that (k_{a},k_{b})\neq(0,0) . Consequently, we have rc\neq 0 . In addition, rc\in Z_{0}\subseteq Z(R) . Next, for r=1+j\notin J , where
 k_{0}\in\{1,3\} , the multiplication by c implies rc=c+jc . Since
 c\notin W_{0}=\langle 2,s\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}} and jc\in J^{2}\subseteq W_{0} , their sum is not equal to zero. Therefore, rc\in Z_{0}\setminus\{0\} . The centrally essential condition is satisfied in all cases, and the ring with the structure specified in the remark exists.
