                                                  1. Introduction to integer geometry of continued fractions
                                             In this paper we are dealing with the space Rn and the lattice Zn in it. We say that
                                          a point is integer if all its coordinates are integers. We say that a vector is integer it its
                                          endpoints are integer points. A polyhedron or polygon is integer is all its vertices are
                                          integer points.
                                          1.1. Integer geometry. The group of affine integer lattice transformations Affn (Z) is a
                                          semidirect product of GLn (Z) and the group of translations of Rn for integer vectors.
                                          Definition 1.1. We say that S1 and S2 are integer congruent if there exists a transfor-
                                          mation of Affn (Z) taking S1 to S2 .

                                            Key words and phrases. geometry of continued fractions, multidimensional continued fractions, Klein
                                          polyhedra, Minkowski-Voronoi tessellation, polyhedral faces, Gauss-Kuzmin statistics.

  The above congruence relation gives rise to integer geometry, an interesting example of
non-Euclidean geometry. The invariants of Affn (Z) are certain indexes of sublattices with
respect to lattices. For instance, let us define the following invariants.
      • An integer length of a segment AB is the index of the subgroup generated by the
        vector AB in the integer lattice of all points contained in AB (we denote it by
        Il(AB)).
        In other words, Il(AB) is the number of integer points in AB minus one.
      • An integer sine of an angle ∠BAC is the index of the sublattice generated by
        the smallest integer vectors contained in the rays AB and AC in the lattice of all
        integer points of the plane BAC (we denote it by Isin(BAC)).
      • An integer area of a triangle △ABC is the index of the sublattice generated by
        AB and AC in the lattice of all integer points of the plane BAC(we denote it by
        IS(AB)).
      • An integer distance from a point P to a subspace L is the index of the sublattice of
        integer vectors generated by the integer vectors of L and the vector P Q for some
        integer point Q ∈ L in the lattice of all integer vectors of the linear span of L
        and P .

1.2. Geometry of ordinary continued fractions. Recall that an ordinary continued
fraction of a real number r is an expression of the following type:
                                                       1
                                    r = a0 +
                                                         1
                                               a1 +
                                                      a2 + . . .
where a0 is any integer, and ai for i = 1, 2, . . . are positive integers.
    Notice that
      • If r is a rational number then there exist exactly one odd and one even contin-
        ued fraction expressions (i.e., continued fractions with odd and even number of
        elements respectively).
      • If r is an irrational number, then there exists a unique infinite continued fraction
        expression.
      • Finally if r is a quadratic irrationality then the continued fraction is eventually
        periodic.

   Geometrically the odd or infinite continued fraction for α ≥ 1 can be seen as follows.
Consider the ray y = αx and take the boundary of the convex hull of all integer points
contained in the angle with edges y = αx and y = 0 except the origin (here we consider
x > 0). This boundary is a broken line with one or two infinite edges. Let us remove
infinite edges. What remains is a broken line with finite or infinite number of segments.
Let us call it A0 A1 A2 . . . where A0 = (1, 0), A1 = (1, a0 ), . . . It terns out that for this
                 OPEN PROBLEMS IN GEOMETRY OF CONTINUED FRACTIONS                              3

broken line we have:
                                           a0 = Il(A0 A1 );
                                           a1 = Isin(A0 A1 A2 );
                                           a2 = Il(A1 A2 );
                                           a3 = Isin(A1 A2 A3 );
                                           ...
where [a0 ; a1 : a2 : a3 : . . .] is either finite odd or infinite continued fraction for α.
   In fact, such sequence is naturally defined for an arbitrary angle with integer vertex
(and an integer point on the first ray). As we see the sequence (a0 , a1 , a2 , . . .) is the
sequence of integer congruence invariants of the angle (as we can define these invariants
for an arbitrary angle). It is called the lattice length-sign sequence of the angle (or LLS-
sequence, for short). LLS-sequence is the complete invariant of integer angles in integer
geometry.

               2. Geometric multidimensional continued fractions
   Let us start with rather unusual general definition of geometric continued fractions.
We use a term “geometric” here in order to indicate that the obtained multidimensional
continued fractions are invariant under the action of the group of integer lattice preserving
transformations (i.e., the group GLn (Z)). Recall that we work in the real space Rn with
lattice of integer points Zn in it. The origin of Rn is denoted by O.

2.1. Sails of arrangements. Geometric multidimensional continued fractions are de-
fined for an arrangement of n hyperplanes passing through the origin (of dimension n−1)
in Rn in general position. Denote the set of all such arrangements by Ωn .
   Any arrangement ω ∈ Ωn divides Rn into 2n cones (in fact, this is precisely the gen-
eral position property for arrangements). We say that these cones are the cones of the
arrangement ω. Let us give now the following general definition.
Definition 2.1. A mapping s of the coordinate cone R≥0 is called a stretching of this
cone if there exist positive real numbers α1 , . . . , αn such that for every (x1 , . . . , xn ) ∈ R≥0
we have
                               s(x1 . . . , xn ) = (λ1 x1 , . . . , λn xn ).
Now let C be a cone of some arrangement in Ωn . We say that an automorphism s of a
cone C is a stretching if there exists an affine transformation taking C to R≥0 such that
the the induced map is stretching for R≥0 .
We say that two subsets S1 and S2 are similar if there exists a stretching of C sending S1
to S2 , we write S1 ∼ S2 in this case.
  To be brief, we give the following general definition of geometric continued fractions
(that is a minor extension of a rather folklore definition which the author was communi-
cated by A. Ustinov).

    3. Open problems on geometric multidimensional continued fractions
   One of the main source of problems in this area are the problems of face structure
description of sails and the corresponding statistics. Indeed sails are either polyhedral
surfaces (in cases of △n and n ) or a little more complicated stratified surfaces with
singularities (in cases of ◦n and hn ). In both cases we have a combinatorial structure with
some lattice invariants involved.
3.1. Directions for further study in general. There are four essentially different cases
of geometric multidimensional continued fractions to study:
    [R] Rational cones: These are the cones that contain integer points at each of the
        1-edge of the cone. The sails of such cones contain finite number of faces of all
        dimensions.
    [A] Algebraic cones: Consider a matrix A ∈ GLn (Z), i.e., all the elements of A are
        integers and the determinant of A is ±1. In addition we assume that characteristic
        polynomial of A is irreducible over Q and has only real roots (totally real case).
        Then the arrangement defined by the invariant planes of A is called algebraic. It’s
        sails are algebraic as well. By Dirichlet unit theorem, there is a transitive action of
        the Dirichlet subgroup for A. Recall that the Dirichlet subgroup for A is the group
        of all SLn (Z)-matrices commuting with A. In the totally real case, the Dirichlet
        subgroup for A is isomorphic to Zn−1 . And therefore, the factor-sails with respect
        to Dirichlet subgroup of A are homeomorphic to the (n−1)-dimensional torus. For
        each sail, the corresponding torus is subdivided into faces equipped with lattice
        invariants of the sail.
    [D] Non-periodic degenerate cones: Here are the cones that contain integer points
        on their faces. Usually the sail of such cones are neither periodic nor finite. Fur-
        thermore, as a rule such sail are not combinatorial polyhedra. The sails for non-
        periodic degenerate cones serve as counterexamples for several questions.
    [G] Non-periodic generic cones: These are non-algebraic cones with no integer
        points on faces. Such cones usually possess non-periodic sails that are combinato-
        rial polyhedra (see [10] and [14] for more details).
   The following two question form the core of combinatorial and statistical problems for
different sails.
      • Find an adequate complete invariant of polyhedra that appear as faces at sails.
      • Find an adequate complete invariant of cones up to integer congruences.
      • Study the face distribution of sails (generalized Gauss-Kuzmin distribution).

3.2. Faces of sails. The central question of this subsection is as follows.
Problem 3. Classify all combinatorial possible types of faces.
   This question targets mostly Klein polyhedra (i.e., Sail△n ,Zn ∩Ċ (C)). In relation to this
question various view-obstacle problems arise. One of the first open famous problems here
is generalization of White’s theorem, which we discuss below.
  A polyhedron P is said to be empty if its intersection with Zn is precisely the set of
vertices of P . We have the following problem.
Problem 4. Classify all empty simplices of dimension n up to lattice congruence.
   If n = 1, 2 then there exists a unique empty simplex up to integer congruences, it is
a coordinate simplex. For the case n = 3 there is a family of empty simplices, they are
classified in White’s theorem (see [21]). The cases of n > 3 remain open.
  The next problem here is as follows.
Problem 5. Which n-gons are realizable as faces of an m-dimensional continued fraction?
Here are two essentially geometrically different subcases:
     • Faces at integer distance 1 to the origin: this is a question of description of
       integer convex polyhedra that are inscribed to simplices of full dimension in Rn .
     • Faces at integer distance greater than 1 to the origin: here we have a
       typical view-obstacle problem, one avoids integer points that are view-obstacles
       for the corresponding faces.
  Both statements of the theorem are trivial for n = 2. Two-dimensional faces on integer
distance greater than 1 are classified in [6]). All the other cases remain open.
 Finally in the context of this subsection we would like to mention a classical Lonely
Runner conjecture (for more details and discussions we refer to [10]).
Problem 6. ( Lonely Runner conjecture.) Assume that k runners start a competition
at the a common point of the circular track with circumference 1. Let them all have
distinct constant speeds. Then for any given runner, there is a time at which that runner
is lonely, i.e., at arc distance at least 1/k away from every other runners.
3.3. Combinatorial structure of sails. One can easily define tautological complete
invariant which does not show the combinatorics of sails. Such invariants are not infor-
mative. Most of the known descriptive invariants are
— either not complete, i.e., they do not distinguish some of integer non-congruent ob-
jects;
— or not precise, i.e., the set of configurations of this invariant that can be realized as
faces or cones is not known.
  So the following problem remains open for Klein sails, Minkowski-Voronoy polyhedra,
and most other continued fractions (for the case [R] as above).

Problem 7. Describe all finite two-dimensional sails (and the corresponding continued
fractions).
   The following problem is on finite continued fractions contained in the different angles
of a tetrahedron. That problem is a version of a question on singularities of toric varieties
in algebraic geometry and on cuspidal singularities in singularity theory (compare with
Problem 2 above).
Problem 8. (Multidimensional IKEA problem.) Describe the collections of the sails
of the cones for all polytopes of a given combinatorial type.
   Most of further questions are related to the algebraically periodic multidimensional
sails and their fundamental domains (case [A]). We begin with the following question is
of interest.
Problem 9. (V. Arnold.) Does there exist an algorithm to decide whether a given type
of fundamental domain is realizable by a periodic continued fraction?
  This problem is relevant for various types of sails (including Minkowski-Voronoi and
Klein cases).
Remark. For the case of Klein sails (i.e., Sail△,Zn ∩Ċ (C).) it is still unknown if there exists
a two-dimensional periodic sail which contains only quadrangles (even a single quadrangle
in the fundamental domain).
  A torus decomposition corresponding to a periodic sail in the sense of Klein is the
collections of integer congruence types of polyhedra in any fundamental domain endowed
with the integer distances to the vertex of the cone.
Conjecture 10. (V. Arnold.) Torus decompositions of integer noncongruent Klein sails
are distinct.
  Next open problem is considered as a complete generalization of the Lagrange theorem
on periodicity of ordinary continued fractions.
Problem 11. (V. Arnold.) Describe all torus decompositions that are realized by
periodic two-dimensional continued fractions.
    The following three observations are confirmed by numerous examples.
Conjecture 12. The torus decomposition of every algebraic sail in the sense of Klein
contains at least one face that is
     • integer congruent to a triangle;
     • at the unit integer distance form the vertex of the cone;
     • at the integer distance from the vertex of the cone greater than 1.
    In the theory of field extensions the following problem is actual.
Problem 13. (V. Arnold.) Classify continued fractions that correspond to the same
cubic extension of the field of rational numbers.

  Here we would like to outline the following three questions (which are open for n ≥ 3).
Problem 15. Find frequencies on n-dimensional continued fractions with the highest
relative frequencies.
Problem 16. For every positive integer constant C there exist only finitely many pairwise
integer non-congruent faces with frequencies exceeding C.
Problem 17. Is that true that sum of all relative frequencies for all possible faces is finite
for higher dimensions (n ≥ 3)?
Problem 18. In case of positive answer to the above question find the generalization
of the Gauss map and compare the corresponding frequencies of faces with the related
frequencies coming from Möbius geometry.

                               4. Further open questions
  In this section we briefly mention some directions that concern different geometric
approaches to multidimensional continued fractions. For further details we refer to [10].
  The first question is to generalize Farey summation and its representation as a Farey
tessellation.
Problem 19. Find a natural generalization of the Farey tessellation to higher-dimensional
hyperbolic geometry.
     The second question concerns periodicity of Jacobi-Perron algorithms.
Problem 20. (Jacobi’s last theorem.) Let K be a totally real cubic number field.
Consider arbitrary elements y and z of K such that 0 < y, z < 1 (here we assume that 1,
y, and z are independent over Q). Is it true that the Jacobi-Perron algorithm generates
an eventually periodic continued fraction with starting data v = (1, y, z).
     An essential contribution to this problem was made in [2].
     The third question is related to the study of minima of decomposable forms.
Problem 21. Study geometric properties of Markov spectrum.

  Finally, we would like to address the following question on low-dimensional topology.
Recall that ordinary finite continued fractions provide a complete invariant to rational
knots, known also as two-bridge knots. So the following rather classical question is actual.
Problem 22. Generalize continued fractions to describe 3-bridge knots.
