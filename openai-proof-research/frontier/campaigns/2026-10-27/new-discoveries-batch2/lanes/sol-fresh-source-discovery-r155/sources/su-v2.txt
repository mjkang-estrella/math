         THE READ-ONCE FACTORIAL-DIVISIBILITY CONJECTURE:
              ALGEBRAIC PROOFS THROUGH SIX INPUTS

                                               LIEN-HUNG SU


        Abstract. Let S be a set of n distinct integers. The read-once factorial-divisibility conjecture
        asks whether one can combine the elements of S, using each element exactly once and using only
        addition, subtraction, and multiplication, to obtain an element of n!Z. The conjecture remains
        open in general. We prove it algebraically for every 1 ≤ n ≤ 6. The proofs use congruences,
        signed residue classes, pigeonhole principles, and products of expressions on disjoint blocks;
        exhaustive enumeration is not used as proof. The crucial new ingredients for six inputs are a
        mixed-parity theorem stating that three odd inputs and one even input always yield a multiple
        of 60, and an auxiliary theorem producing a 120-multiple from four odd inputs coprime to 15.
        These close the parity patterns 3O3E and 4O2E. We also record current proved divisibility
        frontiers Fn , On , En for arbitrary, all-odd, and all-even inputs through n = 6, emphasize that
        incomparable moduli may require different expressions, and document the provenance and
        dominance reductions behind those frontiers.




                                            1. Introduction
   Arithmetic-expression construction usually asks whether prescribed numbers can be combined
to reach a particular target. Here the target is a congruence class: from n distinct integers, every
input must occur exactly once as a leaf of one binary expression tree, and the root value should
lie in n!Z. The read-once requirement prevents reuse in identities such as (a−b)(a+b) and makes
local congruence choices compete for leaves. This places the problem near arithmetic-expression
construction [1] and arithmetic read-once formulas [3], while the repeated use of signed residue
classes is reminiscent of zero-sum methods [4, 5]. The factorial target also connects naturally
with classical questions about factorials and generalized factorials [2].
   The central object of this paper is a conjecture, not a theorem for all inputs: the statement
for general n remains open. The contribution here is to prove the conjecture by hand for the
complete range 1 ≤ n ≤ 6 and to organize the small cases into reusable divisibility profiles. A
block need not have one preferred modulus. For example, three even integers universally yield
both an 18-multiple and a 48-multiple, generally by different expressions. Neither modulus
divides the other, so either can be the useful block in a larger construction.

Theorem 1.1 (Main theorem). For every integer n with 1 ≤ n ≤ 6 and every set of n distinct
integers, there exists a read-once expression using only +, −, × whose value belongs to n!Z.

  The cases through five inputs are developed from small congruence theorems. The six-input
case is divided by parity. Five of the seven parity patterns follow from earlier blocks. The
remaining patterns are closed by

                 3O1E → 60          and       4O with all inputs coprime to 15 → 120.

The main text keeps the structural proof. Complete congruence branches are placed in the
appendices so that no case is omitted while the main argument remains readable.

                            2. Definitions and the conjecture
Definition 2.1 (Read-once expression). Let S = {a1 , . . . , an } ⊂ Z. A read-once arithmetic
expression on S is a fully parenthesized expression formed from the binary operations +, −,
and ×, in which each input occurs exactly once. Equivalently, it is a full binary tree whose
leaves are in bijection with S and whose internal nodes are labelled by +, −, or ×. Parentheses
determine the order of operations and do not count as additional operations.
  For example,
                                         (a1 − a2 )(a3 + a4 )
is a legal read-once expression on four inputs. By contrast, a1 (a2 − a3 ) + a1 is not legal because
a1 occurs twice, and (a1 − a2 )2 is not legal because exponentiation is not an allowed operation
and its expansion would reuse the inputs.
   The value 0 is allowed, because 0 ∈ mZ for every positive integer m. The formal statements
concern sets of distinct integers; repeated-input cancellation is therefore not part of the problem.
Conjecture 2.2 (Read-once factorial-divisibility conjecture). For every integer n ≥ 1 and every
set S of n distinct integers, there exists a read-once expression on S, using only +, −, ×, whose
value lies in n!Z.
Definition 2.3 (Guarantee notation). Write Pr (n; m) when every set of n distinct integers
having exactly r odd elements admits a read-once expression divisible by m. Write P (n; m)
when the parity restriction is absent.
   For compact parity notation, 3O1E → 60 means P3 (4; 60). The read-once expression may
depend on the actual input set; the conjecture does not require one fixed expression template
to work for every set.
Definition 2.4 (Recorded source-pool divisibility frontiers). For fixed n, let Fn denote the
moduli maximal under divisibility among the universal guarantees explicitly included in the
source pool described in Section E; let On and En be the corresponding sets for the all-odd
and all-even source pools. A modulus m is removed when another modulus M in the same
source pool satisfies m | M . In that situation we say that M dominates m, because the same
expression that is a multiple of M is automatically a multiple of m.
   For example, once 63 is proved for a class, the smaller guarantee 21 is not retained in its
frontier because 21 | 63. This dominance deletion is fundamentally different from combining
guarantees obtained by different expressions: knowing separately that 6 and 8 are guaranteed
does not imply that one expression is divisible by lcm(6, 8) = 24. The recorded frontiers below
summarize the displayed source pools; they are not optimality claims and are not asserted to
be closed under every further case split that can be deduced from the proved parity theorems.

                           3. General construction principles
Lemma 3.1 (Block product). Suppose disjoint input blocks A and B admit read-once expres-
sions EA , EB with m | EA and q | EB . Then EA EB is a read-once expression on A ∪ B divisible
by mq.
Proof. The two subexpressions use disjoint leaves, and their product uses every leaf in A ∪ B
exactly once.                                                                               □
Lemma 3.2 (Two-input factor 3). Any two integers can be combined into a multiple of 3.
Proof. If one input is divisible by 3, use the product. Otherwise both are congruent to ±1
(mod 3), so their sum or difference is divisible by 3.                                   □
Lemma 3.3 (Signed pairing modulo 2n). Among any n integers, either one input is divisible
by n, or two inputs a, b satisfy 2n | a + b or 2n | a − b.
Proof. Modulo 2n, a residue 0 or n represents an integer divisible by n. Otherwise the residues
lie in the n − 1 signed classes {±1}, . . . , {±(n − 1)}. Two of the n inputs occupy one class, and
their suitable sum or difference is divisible by 2n.                                              □
                                                 2
Lemma 3.4 (Sign normalization and transport). In a congruence argument one may replace
each input ai by an independently chosen signed representative εi ai , where εi ∈ {±1}. More
generally, let E be any read-once expression formed from the signed leaves εi ai using only the
binary operations +, −, and ×. Then there is a legal read-once expression E0 on the original
leaves ai , with the same underlying tree and using only +, −, and ×, such that E = E0 or
E = −E0 . Hence restoring the selected leaf signs does not change any divisibility conclusion.
Proof. Proceed by induction on the expression tree. For a leaf εa, take the unsigned leaf a and
keep ε only as an external sign marker. At a multiplication node, multiply the two external
sign markers and multiply the two unsigned child expressions. At an addition or subtraction
node, factor out the external sign marker of the left child; the relative sign of the right child
determines whether that node is labelled + or −. Thus every internal node remains one of the
allowed binary operations, every original leaf is used exactly once, and only one overall sign
remains at the root. An overall sign does not affect divisibility.                             □
Lemma 3.5 (Signed-class triangle). Suppose u, v, w lie in one signed residue class {±r} modulo
m, where r ̸≡ 0 (mod m) and 2r ̸≡ 0 (mod m). For each pair, let σm (u, v) ∈ {±1} denote the
unique sign satisfying m | u + σm (u, v)v. Then
                                σm (u, v)σm (v, w)σm (w, u) = −1.
Proof. Write u ≡ εu r, v ≡ εv r and w ≡ εw r (mod m), with each εi ∈ {±1}. The condition
2r ̸≡ 0 (mod m) makes the admissible sign unique, and σm (u, v) = −εu εv . Multiplying the
three edge signs gives −1.                                                              □
   The triangle relation will be used for m = 3, 4, 5. For m = 3, all nonzero residues form {±1};
for m = 4, all odd residues form {±1}; and for m = 5, the nonzero residues form {±1} and
{±2}.

                           4. Core blocks through five inputs
   This section records the blocks needed in the six-input proof. Complete small-case derivations,
including complementary moduli used to form the frontiers, appear in Section A.
Theorem 4.1 (Three-input 12 theorem). Every three integers admit a read-once expression
divisible by 12; that is, P (3; 12).
Proof. See Theorem A.5. The proof separates the four parity patterns and uses only congruences
modulo 3 and 4.                                                                             □
Theorem 4.2 (Same-parity 18 and even-input 48). Any three integers of the same parity admit
an expression divisible by 18, and any three even integers admit an expression divisible by 48:
                               P0 (3; 18),   P3 (3; 18),   P0 (3; 48).
Proof. See Theorems A.6 and A.8.                                                                □
Theorem 4.3 (Four-input universal blocks). Every four integers admit expressions divisible by
24 and by 36:
                                 P (4; 24),   P (4; 36).
Proof. For 24, apply Theorem 3.3 with n = 4. If one input is divisible by 4, multiply it
by a 12-multiple from the remaining three. Otherwise a pair yields an 8-multiple, while the
complementary pair yields a 3-multiple by Theorem 3.2.
   For 36, if one input is divisible by 3, multiply it by a 12-multiple from the remaining three.
Assume henceforth that no input is divisible by 3.
   If the number of odd inputs is 0, 2, or 4, partition the four inputs into two same-parity pairs.
In each pair choose a sum or difference divisible by 3. Because the two entries of the pair have
the same parity, that same sum or difference is even, so each pair gives a 6-block. The two
disjoint blocks therefore give 36.
                                                 3
  If the parity pattern is 3O1E, the three odd inputs give an 18-multiple by Theorem 4.2,
and multiplication by the remaining even input supplies an additional factor 2. Hence the full
expression is divisible by 36.
  Finally consider 1O3E. Let a be the odd input and x, y, z the even inputs. Among x, y, z,
two are congruent modulo 4; relabel them x, y. Choose the sign for which 3 | x ± y. Since x ≡ y
(mod 4), both x + y and x − y are divisible by 4, so the same chosen block is divisible by 12.
The remaining pair a, z can be combined into a multiple of 3 by Theorem 3.2. Multiplying the
two disjoint blocks gives a multiple of 36.
  These cases exhaust all parity patterns.                                                   □
Theorem 4.4 (Four-input parity blocks). The following guarantees hold:
                      P0 (4; 48), P1 (4; 48), P2 (4; 48), P4 (4; 48), P0 (4; 72),
                      P1 (4; 40), P2 (4; 40), P3 (4; 60), P4 (4; 60).
Proof. See Theorems A.9 to A.13 and section B.                                                    □
Theorem 4.5 (Five-input factorial target). Every five distinct integers admit a read-once ex-
pression divisible by 120; that is, P (5; 120).
Proof. Apply Theorem 3.3 with n = 5. If one input is divisible by 5, multiply it by a 24-
multiple from the remaining four. Otherwise a pair has a sum or difference divisible by 10, and
the remaining three inputs yield a 12-multiple. Thus 10 · 12 = 120.                           □
Theorem 4.6 (Five odd and five even inputs). Five odd inputs always yield multiples of 180
and 240, and five even inputs always yield a multiple of 480:
                            P5 (5; 180),     P5 (5; 240),      P0 (5; 480).
Proof. See Theorems A.14 and A.15.                                                                □
  Consequently Theorem 2.2 holds algebraically for 1 ≤ n ≤ 5. The six-input proof requires
one additional four-leaf block.

                              5. The mixed-parity modulus 60
Theorem 5.1 (Mixed-parity 60 theorem). Any three odd integers and one even integer admit
a read-once expression divisible by 60:
                                            3O1E → 60.
Proof. See Section B for the complete case analysis.                                              □
Idea of proof. Let a, b, c be odd and e even. The proof first disposes of an input divisible by
5, then separates the case 3 | e. Under 3 ∤ e, the three odd inputs contain either zero, one, or
at least two multiples of 3, giving an exhaustive partition. When no odd input is a multiple
of 3, sign comparisons modulo 3, 4, and 5, together with the signed-class triangle relation,
produce complementary blocks whose moduli multiply to 60. When exactly one odd input is
a multiple of 3, sign normalization modulo 5 leaves exactly two possible signed-class positions
for e; the explicit modulo-3, modulo-4, and modulo-5 calculations are given in Section B. Every
simultaneous divisibility claim there uses one and the same choice of sign.
Theorem 5.2 (Four odd inputs coprime to 15). If four odd inputs are all coprime to 15, then
they admit a read-once expression divisible by 120.
Proof. Modulo 5, the four inputs occupy two nonzero signed classes. If the class-size distribution
is 3 + 1 or 4 + 0, take three inputs u, v, w in one class. Were every edge to satisfy σ3 = −σ5 , the
products of the three edge signs modulo 3 and modulo 5 would be opposite. By Theorem 3.5
both products equal −1, a contradiction. Hence some pair satisfies σ3 = σ5 , and its suitable
sum or difference is divisible by 30. The remaining odd pair yields a multiple of 4.
   If the distribution is 2 + 2, write a, b in one class and c, d in the other. If either same-class
pair, say a, b, satisfies σ3 (a, b) = σ5 (a, b), then the same signed sum or difference is divisible
                                                   4
by 2, 3, and 5, so a, b form a 30-block; the remaining odd pair c, d forms a 4-block. Hence
30 · 4 = 120.
   Assume therefore that both same-class pairs satisfy σ3 = −σ5 . For the pair a, b, the binary
sign σ4 (a, b) must equal either σ3 (a, b) or σ5 (a, b). If σ4 (a, b) = σ3 (a, b), then the same signed
sum or difference of a, b is divisible by 12, while the pair c, d, using its modulo-5 sign, forms a
10-block; thus 12 · 10 = 120. If instead σ4 (a, b) = σ5 (a, b), then a, b form a 20-block, while c, d,
using their modulo-3 sign, form a 6-block; thus 20 · 6 = 120.                                         □


                                           6. Six inputs
Theorem 6.1 (Six-input factorial theorem). Every six distinct integers admit a read-once
expression divisible by 720 = 6!.

Proof. We split by the number of odd inputs. The complete leaf-by-leaf constructions appear
in Section C; the main exits are summarized below.

                 Parity pattern Divisibility construction
                      0O6E         18 · 10 · 4 = 720
                      1O5E         20 · 36, or 10 · 2 · 36
                      2O4E         10 · 72, 20 · 36, 18 · 40, or equivalent branches
                      3O3E         6 · 120, or 12 · 60
                      4O2E         6 · 120, 12 · 60, 20 · 36, or 120 · 6
                      5O1E         60 · 12, or 20 · 36
                      6O0E         18 · 10 · 4 = 720

   For 0O6E and 6O0E, one first constructs an 18-block, then a 10-block from two of the
remaining four inputs, and finally a 4-block from the last pair. The parity of each selected pair
supplies the factor 2 accompanying the modulo-9 or modulo-5 divisibility.
   For 1O5E, an even multiple of 5 supplies 10; another even leaf supplies 2, and the remaining
four leaves supply 36. If no even input is divisible by 5, two of the five even inputs collide in one
of the four nonzero even signed classes modulo 20, producing a 20-block, and the remaining four
leaves supply 36. The 5O1E argument is the odd analogue: an odd multiple of 5 is combined
with a 12-block to produce 60, while in the absence of such an input a signed-class collision
modulo 20 produces 20 and the complementary block produces 36.
   For 2O4E, the proof first separates the presence of a multiple of 5. Otherwise it checks
whether the odd pair shares a signed class modulo 5, whether an even pair collides modulo 20,
and finally whether a multiple of 3 occurs. If none does, a pair of even inputs gives 18; the
unused leaves give either 10 · 4 or the previously proved 2O2E → 40. These branches are written
leaf by leaf in Section C.5.
   For 3O3E, if an even input is divisible by 3, it supplies 6 and the remaining five inputs supply
120. Otherwise the three even inputs are nonzero modulo 3. Two have the same residue modulo
4; choose the sign making their sum or difference divisible by 3. The same block is divisible by
4, hence by 12. The complementary 3O1E block supplies 60.
   For 4O2E, first dispose of an even multiple of 3 by 6·120. If the two even inputs are congruent
modulo 4, they yield a 12-block and the four odd inputs yield 60. Otherwise write the even
inputs as 4 | x and y ≡ 2 (mod 4). An odd multiple of 3 gives 12 | ax and leaves a 3O1E block
of modulus 60. If no odd input is divisible by 3 but one is divisible by 5, then 20 | ax and
the remaining four inputs give 36. In the final case all four odd inputs are coprime to 15, so
Theorem 5.2 gives 120; the even pair gives 6.
   The other five parity patterns are proved in Sections C.1 to C.5. The seven patterns exhaust
all possibilities.                                                                                 □

Corollary 6.2. Theorem 2.2 holds for every 1 ≤ n ≤ 6.

              9. Exact residue dynamic programming as a checking tool
   For a fixed modulus M and fixed inputs, exact read-once reachability can be computed over
residues. For every nonempty subset T of the input indices, let R(T ) be the residues modulo
M attained by expressions using exactly the leaves in T . Initialize singleton sets by the input
residues. For every bipartition T = A ⊔ B, combine x ∈ R(A) and y ∈ R(B) by
                                  x + y,   x − y,     y − x,     xy    (mod M ).
Induction on |T | shows that this recurrence is complete.
   The algorithm is not used as proof here. It serves only as an independent consistency check
of delicate blocks. The algebraic appendices, not finite search, establish the stated theorems.

                                          10. Conclusion
   The read-once factorial-divisibility statement remains a conjecture for general n. This paper
proves the complete range through six inputs. The decisive step is not exhaustive residue enu-
meration, but reusable congruence blocks with complementary prime-power profiles, especially
3O1E → 60 and the four-odd coprime-to-15 modulus 120. Together with the earlier three-,
four-, and five-input results, these close all seven parity patterns for n = 6. The first unresolved
finite case is n = 7.

           Appendix A. Complete small-block proofs through five inputs
   This appendix supplies the detailed algebraic constructions used before the six-input theorem.
Every displayed product combines disjoint blocks, and all signs refer to a choice of sum or
difference.

A.1. One and two inputs. For one input, divisibility by 1 is immediate. For two arbitrary
inputs a, b, if they have the same parity then a − b is even; otherwise ab is even. This proves
P (2; 2). If one input is divisible by 3, their product is divisible by 3; otherwise both are nonzero
modulo 3 and a suitable a ± b is divisible by 3. Hence P (2; 3).
   If a, b are odd, one of a + b, a − b is divisible by 4. If a, b are even, then the preceding factor-3
construction is automatically even, proving a 6-multiple. For a multiple of 8, if one input is
divisible by 4 use the product; otherwise both are 2 (mod 4), and according as their residues
modulo 8 agree or are opposite, their difference or sum is divisible by 8.

A.2. Three arbitrary inputs.
Theorem A.1. Every three integers admit an expression divisible by 7.
Proof. If one input is divisible by 7, multiply it by any legal expression on the other two leaves.
Otherwise the residues lie in the three nonzero signed classes {±1}, {±2}, {±3} modulo 7. A
collision gives a sum or difference divisible by 7; multiply that block by the remaining third leaf.
If all three classes occur, relabel and choose signed representatives so that a ≡ 1, b ≡ 2, c ≡ 3
(mod 7). Since bc ≡ −1 (mod 7), one of the legal expressions bc + a, bc − a, a − bc is divisible
by 7 after restoring the selected signs.                                                         □
Theorem A.2. Every three integers admit an expression divisible by 8.
                                                   7
Proof. If all three are even, use the product. If exactly two, x, y, are even and z is odd, use
the product when one even input is divisible by 4. Otherwise x, y ≡ 2 (mod 4), and one of
x + y, x − y is divisible by 8; multiply it by z.
  If exactly one input e is even and a, b are odd, then when 4 | e use e(a + b), while when e ≡ 2
(mod 4) choose a ± b divisible by 4 and use e(a ± b). Finally, for three odd inputs, the residues
occupy the two signed classes {±1}, {±3} modulo 8; a colliding pair has a sum or difference
divisible by 8, which is multiplied by the third input.                                        □
Theorem A.3. Every three integers admit an expression divisible by 9.
Proof. If one input is divisible by 9, use the product. If at least two inputs are divisible by 3 but
none by 9, those two are in the signed class {±3} (mod 9), so their suitable sum or difference is
divisible by 9; multiply that block by the remaining third leaf. If exactly one input c is divisible
by 3, the other two are nonzero modulo 3 and a suitable a ± b is divisible by 3; hence 9 | c(a ± b).
  It remains that no input is divisible by 3. The nonzero signed classes modulo 9 are
                                      {±1},    {±2},    {±4}.
A collision gives a 9-multiple pair, which is multiplied by the remaining third leaf. If all three
classes occur, normalize to a ≡ 1, b ≡ 2, c ≡ 4 (mod 9). Then bc ≡ −1 (mod 9), so a suitable
bc ± a is divisible by 9.                                                                       □
Theorem A.4. Every three integers admit an expression divisible by 10.
Proof. If one input, say a, is divisible by 5 and at least one of the three inputs is even, the full
product abc is divisible by 10. If all three inputs are odd, use a(b + c), which is divisible by 10
because b + c is even.
   Assume no input is divisible by 5. Two inputs lie in one of the signed classes {±1}, {±2}
modulo 5; call them a, b. Choose a sign so that 5 | a ± b. If a, b have the same parity, this block
is divisible by 10 and is multiplied by the third input c. If they have opposite parity and c is
even, then c(a ± b) is divisible by 10.
   The only remaining parity pattern has a, b of opposite parity and c odd. If the two odd inputs
lie in the same signed class modulo 5, their suitable sum or difference is divisible by 10 and is
multiplied by the remaining even input. Otherwise, after sign normalization, the two inputs in
one signed class contribute r, r and the odd input in the other class contributes −2r modulo 5.
Their signed three-term sum is divisible by 5 and is even (odd + even − odd), hence divisible
by 10.                                                                                            □
Theorem A.5 (Three-input 12 theorem). Every three integers admit an expression divisible by
12.
Proof. If all three are even and one is divisible by 3, their product is divisible by 24. If none is
divisible by 3, choose 3 | a ± b; the block a ± b together with the remaining even factor supplies
a factor 4.
   Suppose exactly two inputs x, y are even and z is odd. If any input is divisible by 3, the
product contains 4 · 3. Otherwise, if one even input, say x, is divisible by 4, choose 3 | y ± z and
use x(y ± z). If neither even input is divisible by 4, then x ≡ y ≡ 2 (mod 4); choose 3 | x ± y.
Either sign is divisible by 4, so (x ± y)z is divisible by 12.
   Suppose exactly one input e is even and a, b are odd. If 4 | e, then either the product already
contains a factor 3, or a suitable a ± b is divisible by 3; in either case a 12-multiple results. Now
let e ≡ 2 (mod 4). If 3 | e, choose a ± b divisible by 4. If neither odd input is divisible by 3,
choose a ± b divisible by 3; this block is even and e supplies the second factor 2. If both odd
inputs are divisible by 3, choose a ± b divisible by 4; it is also divisible by 3.
   In the remaining subcase exactly one odd input, say a, is divisible by 3, while b, e are nonzero
modulo 3. Choose the sign on b so that e ± b ≡ 0 (mod 3). The number e ± b is odd. Since a is
odd, one of (e ± b) + a and (e ± b) − a is divisible by 4. Changing the sign of a does not affect
divisibility modulo 3, so the resulting signed sum is divisible by 12.
   Finally suppose all three inputs are odd. If one is divisible by 3, use it as a factor and combine
the other two into a multiple of 4. If none is divisible by 3, the residues lie in the two signed
                                                 8
classes {±1}, {±5} modulo 12; two inputs have a sum or difference divisible by 12, which is
multiplied by the third input.                                                           □
A.3. Three-input parity improvements.
Theorem A.6 (Same-parity 18 theorem). Any three integers of the same parity admit an
expression divisible by 18.
Proof. Work modulo 9. If one input c is divisible by 9, then c(a + b) is divisible by 18 because
a + b is even. If at least two inputs are divisible by 3 but none by 9, those two are congruent to
±3 modulo 9; a suitable sum or difference is divisible by 9 and, by same parity, by 2. Multiply
this 18-multiple block by the remaining third leaf.
   If exactly one input c is divisible by 3, the other two are nonzero modulo 3, so choose 3 | a ± b.
Then 9 | c(a ± b), and a ± b is even. If no input is divisible by 3, use the signed classes
{±1}, {±2}, {±4} modulo 9. A collision gives an even 9-multiple, which is multiplied by the
remaining third leaf. If all three classes occur, normalize to a ≡ 1, b ≡ 2, c ≡ 4 (mod 9); then
bc ≡ −1, so bc ± a can be made divisible by 9. Since all inputs have the same parity, bc and a
have the same parity, so the expression is even.                                                  □
Lemma A.7. For any integers x, y, z, either 6 | xyz, or after relabelling there is a sign such
that 12 | (x ± y)z.
Proof. Assume 6 ∤ xyz. If xyz is odd and one input is divisible by 3, use it as z and choose
x ± y divisible by 4. If none is divisible by 3, two lie in one of the signed classes {±1}, {±5}
modulo 12, and their suitable sum or difference is divisible by 12.
   If xyz is even, no input is divisible by 3. If one input is divisible by 4, use it as z and choose
x ± y divisible by 3. Otherwise every even input is 2 (mod 4). With exactly one even input, use
it as z and choose the two odd inputs so that 3 | x±y; the latter block is also even. With at least
two even inputs, choose two as x, y and make 3 | x ± y; their sum or difference is automatically
divisible by 4.                                                                                    □
Theorem A.8 (Three even inputs give 48). Any three even integers admit an expression divisible
by 48.
Proof. Write the inputs as 2x, 2y, 2z. If 6 | xyz, their product is divisible by 48. Otherwise
Theorem A.7 gives 12 | (x ± y)z, and
                                    (2x ± 2y)(2z) = 4(x ± y)z
is divisible by 48.                                                                                □
   The proof of Theorem A.1 also improves to 14 when the three inputs have one parity. If one
input is divisible by 7, multiply it by the sum of the other two same-parity leaves, which is even.
In the collision branch the 7-divisible sum or difference of the colliding same-parity pair is even
and is multiplied by the third leaf. In the three-class branch, the product of two same-parity
inputs and the remaining input have the same parity, so the constructed bc ± a expression is

A.5. Five-input parity theorems.
Theorem A.14 (Five odd inputs). Five odd inputs admit expressions divisible by 180 and by
240.
Proof. Suppose first that no input is divisible by 5. The five inputs occupy the four nonzero
odd signed classes modulo 20, so a pair yields a 20-multiple. The remaining three odd inputs
yield an 18-multiple, giving 360, hence 180; using instead their universal 12-multiple gives 240.

or difference is divisible by 12. Then a(b ± c) is divisible by 60. The remaining odd pair can be
combined into a multiple of 3, giving 180, or into a multiple of 4, giving 240.                 □
Theorem A.15 (Five even inputs). Five even inputs admit an expression divisible by 480.
Proof. If one input is divisible by 5, it is divisible by 10; the remaining four even inputs yield
48. Otherwise the five inputs occupy the two nonzero signed classes modulo 5, so two have a
suitable sum or difference divisible by 5 and by 2, hence by 10. The remaining three even inputs
yield 48. In both branches the disjoint blocks give 480.                                        □

            Appendix B. Complete proof of the mixed-parity 60 theorem
   Let a, b, c be odd and e even. We prove that these four leaves can be used exactly once
to construct a multiple of 60. The top-level partition is exhaustive: Case A handles an input
divisible by 5; after excluding that case, Case B handles 3 | e; under 3 ∤ e, Cases D, E, and
C handle respectively zero, exactly one, and at least two odd multiples of 3. Within every
case, a statement that one expression is divisible by two moduli always refers to one and the
same choice of sign. Signs belonging to different disjoint blocks may be chosen independently;
within any single block, however, the same sign is used simultaneously for all stated congruence
conditions.
Case A: one input is divisible by 5. If an odd input, say a, is divisible by 5, multiply it by a
12-multiple constructed from b, c, e. If 5 | e, then 10 | e and the remaining three odd inputs yield
a 12-multiple. Hence this case is complete. From now on all four inputs are nonzero modulo 5.
Case B: 3 | e. Two of the three odd inputs lie in one signed class modulo 5, so their suitable sum
or difference is divisible by 5 and, because both are odd, by 2; this is a 10-block. The remaining
odd input multiplied by e is divisible by 6. Hence 10 · 6 = 60. From now on 3 ∤ e.
Case C: at least two odd inputs are divisible by 3. If two such odd inputs lie in one signed class
modulo 5, their suitable sum or difference is divisible by 2, 3, and 5, hence by 30; the remaining
odd-even product is even.
   Otherwise exactly two odd inputs are divisible by 3 and they occupy the two different signed
classes modulo 5. The third odd input lies in the same class as one of them. That same-class
odd pair gives a 10-block, while the unused multiple-of-3 odd input multiplied by e gives a
6-block. Thus this case is complete.
Case D: no odd input is divisible by 3. Now all four inputs are coprime to 15. Choose two odd
inputs a, b in one signed class modulo 5. We distinguish the class positions of c, e.
D1. The remaining inputs c, e occupy one signed class modulo 5. For the pair a, b, compare the
three signs σ3 (a, b), σ4 (a, b), σ5 (a, b). At least two are equal. If σ3 = σ5 , the pair yields 30 and
ce yields 2. If σ4 = σ5 , the pair yields 20, while c ± e can be made divisible by 3. If σ3 = σ4 ,
the pair yields 12, while the same-class pair c, e yields 5. Each branch gives 60.
D2. The three odd inputs a, b, c occupy one signed class modulo 5, and e occupies the other. If
an odd pair has σ3 = σ5 , then the same signed sum or difference is divisible by 2, 3, and 5,
hence forms a 30-block; the remaining odd-even product supplies 2. If an odd pair has σ4 = σ5 ,
then the same signed sum or difference forms a 20-block, while the remaining odd-even pair can
be combined into a 3-block.
   Suppose neither exit occurs for any odd pair. Since each admissible sign is binary, every edge
of the odd triangle must satisfy
                                               σ3 = σ4 = −σ5 .
The signed-class triangle lemma says that the product of the three σ3 edge signs is −1 and that
the product of the three σ5 edge signs is also −1. On the other hand, multiplying the three
relations σ5 = −σ3 shows that the σ5 edge-sign product is the negative of the σ3 edge-sign
product, because three negative factors occur. The σ3 product is −1, so this would make the
σ5 product +1, a contradiction. Hence one of the two preceding exits must occur.
D3. The inputs a, b, e occupy one signed class modulo 5, and c the other. If σ3 (a, b) = σ5 (a, b),
then the same signed sum or difference of a, b forms a 30-block and the complementary product
ce supplies 2. If σ4 (a, b) = σ5 (a, b), then the same signed sum or difference of a, b forms a
20-block and the complementary pair c, e can be combined into a 3-block. Otherwise put
                                      ρ(u, v) = σ3 (u, v)σ5 (u, v).
On the same-class triangle a, b, e, the two triangle identities give
                                       ρ(a, b)ρ(b, e)ρ(e, a) = 1.
Since ρ(a, b) = −1, exactly one of the other two factors equals +1. Thus either a, e or b, e has
one sum or difference divisible by both 3 and 5, giving a 15-block. The complementary odd pair
yields 4.

                Appendix C. Complete proof of the six-input theorem
  This appendix gives the full parity-by-parity construction for Theorem 6.1. In every product
below, the displayed factors are built from disjoint leaves.
C.1. Six even inputs. First construct an 18-block. If one input is divisible by 9, multi-
ply it by another even input. Otherwise six residues occupy the four nonzero signed classes
{±1}, {±2}, {±3}, {±4} modulo 9, so two collide; their suitable sum or difference is divisible by
9 and by 2.
   Among the remaining four even inputs, construct a 10-block. If one is divisible by 5, multiply
it by another even input. Otherwise a collision in the two nonzero signed classes modulo 5 yields
a sum or difference divisible by 5 and by 2. The final two even inputs have product divisible by
4. Hence 18 · 10 · 4 = 720.
C.2. Six odd inputs. Construct an 18-block as follows. If at least two inputs are divisible by
9, their sum or difference is divisible by 18. If exactly one is divisible by 9, the other five occupy
four nonzero signed classes modulo 9; if none is divisible by 9, all six occupy those four classes.
In both cases a collision gives a sum or difference divisible by 9 and by 2.
   Among the remaining four odd inputs, construct a 10-block. If at least two are divisible by 5,
take their sum or difference. If exactly one is divisible by 5, the other three occupy two nonzero
signed classes; if none is divisible by 5, all four occupy those two classes. Thus a collision gives
a sum or difference divisible by 10. The final odd pair yields 4. Therefore 18 · 10 · 4 = 720.
C.3. One odd and five even inputs. If one of the five even inputs is divisible by 5, it supplies
10; reserve another even input as a factor 2, and construct a 36-multiple from the remaining
four leaves. This gives 10 · 2 · 36 = 720.
   Otherwise the five even inputs lie in the four nonzero even signed classes modulo 20,
                                 {±2},     {±4},        {±6},   {±8}.
A collision gives a 20-block, while the other four leaves give 36. Thus 20 · 36 = 720.
C.4. Five odd and one even input. Write the odd inputs as a, b, c, d, f and the even input

  This appendix records additional universal four-input divisibility guarantees beyond the
source pool used to organize the factorial-through-six proof. Each statement below is a sep-

F.1. Two auxiliary three-even blocks. The following four-input refinements use two conve-
nient three-even strengthenings.
Lemma F.1 (Three even inputs give 20). Any three even integers admit a read-once expression
divisible by 20.
Proof. Let the inputs be a, b, c. If one is divisible by 5, their product is already divisible by
40, hence by 20. Otherwise the three nonzero residues modulo 5 occupy the two signed classes
{±1}, {±2}. Two inputs, say a, b, lie in one class, so one of a + b, a − b is divisible by 5. It is
also even, and the remaining input c is even; hence (a ± b)c is divisible by 20.                 □
Lemma F.2 (Three even inputs give 32). Any three even integers admit a read-once expression
divisible by 32.
Proof. Let the inputs be a, b, c. If 32 | abc, use the product. Otherwise no input is divisible by
8, and two inputs cannot both be divisible by 4. If exactly one input, say c, is divisible by 4,
then a, b ≡ 2 (mod 4). Modulo 8 they lie in the single signed class {±2}, so one of a + b, a − b
is divisible by 8; multiplying by c gives a multiple of 32.
   It remains that none of the three inputs is divisible by 4. Then all are 2 (mod 4), and modulo
16 they occupy only the two signed classes {±2} and {±6}. Two, say a, b, occupy one class, so
one of a + b, a − b is divisible by 16. Multiplication by the remaining even input gives a multiple

Lemma F.3 (Small signed-class relation). Let G be a finite abelian group with |G| ≤ 6. Any
four-element subset contains three distinct elements X, Y, Z with XY = Z.

F.12. A complementary binary lift.
Lemma F.13 (Binary complementary lift). Suppose two legal read-once expressions satisfy
                       E0 ≡ M L    (mod 2M ),       E1 ≡ M (L + 1)         (mod 2M )
for an integer L. Then one of E0 , E1 is divisible by 2M .
Proof. The consecutive integers L and L + 1 have opposite parity.                                   □

F.13. The universal modulus 32.
Theorem F.14. The universal guarantee P (4; 32) holds.
Proof. The parity patterns other than 3O1E follow from the smaller blocks already proved: four
odd inputs split into an 8-pair and a 4-pair; 2O2E splits into a 4-odd pair and an 8-even pair;
and in the 1O3E and 4E patterns, three even leaves give 32 by Theorem F.2, after which the
remaining leaf is multiplied in.
   Let a, b, c be odd and e even. If 4 | e, the three odd leaves give an 8-multiple and multiplication
by e gives 32. Hence assume e ≡ 2 (mod 4). If two odd leaves occupy one signed class modulo

e gives 32. If three distinct odd classes satisfy [a][b] = [c] modulo 16, use e(ab ± c).

that the only relation-free triples are
                                  RA = {1, 3, 7},        RB = {1, 5, 7}.

                   a = ra + 16A,    b = rb + 16B,        c = rc + 16C,     e = s + 16D,

     odd classes   s    E0                                   E1
