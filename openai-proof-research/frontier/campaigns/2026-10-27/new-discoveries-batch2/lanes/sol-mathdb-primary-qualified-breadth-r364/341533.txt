                                                  SCHUR AND e-POSITIVITY OF TREES AND CUT VERTICES




arXiv:1901.02468v2 [math.CO] 14 Dec 2019
                                                   SAMANTHA DAHLBERG, ADRIAN SHE, AND STEPHANIE VAN WILLIGENBURG

                                                   Abstract. We prove that the chromatic symmetric function of any n-vertex tree containing
                                                   a vertex of degree d ≥ log2 n + 1 is not e-positive, that is, not a positive linear combination
                                                   of elementary symmetric functions. Generalizing this, we also prove that the chromatic
                                                   symmetric function of any n-vertex connected graph containing a cut vertex whose deletion
                                                   disconnects the graph into d ≥ log2 n + 1 connected components is not e-positive. Further-
                                                   more we prove that any n-vertex bipartite graph, including all trees, containing a vertex of
                                                   degree greater than ⌈ n2 ⌉ is not Schur-positive, namely not a positive linear combination of
                                                   Schur functions. In complete generality, we prove that if an n-vertex connected graph has
                                                   no perfect matching (if n is even) or no almost perfect matching (if n is odd), then it is not
                                                   e-positive. We hence deduce that many graphs containing the claw are not e-positive.

   Many partial results regarding chromatic symmetric functions have been obtained such
as when the graph involved is the path or the cycle [6, 22, 27], when the graph is formed
from complete graphs [3, 11, 14], or when a graph avoids another [7, 10, 13, 25]. These
proofs have not always worked directly with the chromatic symmetric function. Instead,
sometimes generalizations of the chromatic symmetric function have been employed such as
to quasisymmetric functions [3, 14, 21] and noncommutative symmetric functions [11].
   Another research avenue that has seen activity is to determine whether two nonisomorphic
trees can have the same chromatic symmetric function [1, 2, 15, 17, 18, 19]. The data for up
to 29 vertices [15] shows that two trees T1 , T2 have the same chromatic symmetric function
if and only if T1 and T2 are isomorphic. Further evidence towards this includes that for T1
and T2 to have the same chromatic symmetric function they must have the same number
of vertices, edges and matchings, and many of these results have been collected together in
[19].
   In this paper, we meld these two avenues and discover criteria on trees and graphs with cut
vertices that ensure they are not e-positive or not Schur-positive. In particular, we discover
a trove of trees that are not e-positive, supporting Stanley’s observation from 1995 [22, p
187] that a tree is likely only to be e-positive “by accident”. More precisely, this paper is
structured as follows.

Theorem. Let G be an n-vertex connected graph. If G has no perfect matching (if n is even)

partition of G of type λ, and is missing a connected partition of type λ otherwise.

Theorem 6. [28, Proposition 1.3.3] If a connected n-vertex graph G is e-positive, then G
has a connected partition of type µ for every partition µ ⊢ n.
  Hence, to prove that a graph G is not e-positive, it suffices to find a partition µ such that
G is missing a partition of type µ. In particular, we get the following.
Theorem 7. Let G be an n-vertex connected graph. If G has no perfect matching (if n is
even) or no almost perfect matching (if n is odd), then G is not e-positive. In particular,
let T be an n-vertex tree. If T has no perfect matching (if n is even) or no almost perfect
matching (if n is odd), then T is not e-positive.
   It is a well-known result that if a connected graph with an even number of vertices is
claw-free, then it has a perfect matching. This immediately yields the following corollary to
Theorem 7.
Corollary 8. Let G be a connected graph with an even number of vertices and no perfect
matching. Then G contains the claw and is not e-positive.
Remark 9. Note that Corollary 8 cannot be strengthened further regarding graphs that
contain the claw since S(2, 1, 1) and S(6, 2, 1) both contain the claw and are e-positive.
However, S(2, 1, 1) has an odd number of vertices, and S(6, 2, 1) has a perfect matching.
  It is also known when a tree has a perfect matching by the following specialization of
Tutte’s Theorem on graphs with perfect matchings [26].
Lemma 10. Let T be a tree. Then T has a perfect matching if and only if for every vertex v,
the deletion of v and its incident edges produces a forest with exactly one connected component
with an odd number of vertices.

2.1. The reduction to spiders. The next two lemmas allow us to reduce our study of
connected graphs to the study of spiders. For ease of notation in the proof of the next
lemma, in the ith leg of a spider, which has λi vertices, label the vertices by {si,1, . . . , si,λi }
where si,1 is the vertex connected to the centre, si,λi is a leaf, and there are edges between
each si,j and si,j+1 for 1 ≤ j ≤ λi − 1.
Lemma 12. Let T be a tree with a vertex of degree d ≥ 3, and let v be any such vertex. Let
(t1 , . . . , td ) be the partition whose parts denote the sizes of the subtrees (T1 , . . . , Td ) rooted
at each vertex adjacent to v. If T has a connected partition of type µ, then the spider
S = S(t1 , . . . , td ) has a connected partition of type µ as well.

Proof. Let C = {V1 , . . . , Vk } be a connected partition of T of type µ. We will work towards
constructing a connected partition of S of type µ. Without loss of generality, suppose v ∈ V1 .
Since T is a tree, no subset in C contains vertices from two different subtrees unless vertex
v is also included. Therefore, all subsets in C except possibly V1 contain vertices from one
subtree only. Hence, let V1 contains ni vertices from subtree Ti for 1 ≤ i ≤ d, and let Ti ⊂ C
denote the sets in the partition with vertices in subtree Ti only. Notice that each Ti contains
ti − ni vertices.
   A connected partition {W1 , . . . , Wk } of the same type µ in S may now be formed as
follows. Let W1 = {v} ∪ {si,j : 1 ≤ i ≤ d, 1 ≤ j ≤ ni } where v is the centre of S. Note that
|W1 | = |V1 | and W1 is connected. Now notice that S with deletion of all vertices in W1 and
their incident edges is now a collection of d disjoint paths of length ti − ni for 1 ≤ i ≤ d.
For each Ti , let νi ⊢ (ti − ni ) be the partition formed from the size of each set in Ti . Since a
path may be decomposed into a connected partition of any type, in particular, a connected
partition of type νi can be formed from a path of length ti −ni . Hence, the result follows. 
  In fact, the above argument can be generalized as follows.
Lemma 13. Let G be a connected graph with a cut vertex v whose deletion produces a graph

                               3. The e-positivity of spiders
  We now work towards our first result on spiders by classifying when they have perfect and
almost perfect matchings, for which we will need the following straightforward observation.
Observation 14. A path, Pn , has a perfect matching if and only if n is even.
 With this observation we can now classify when a spider has a perfect or almost perfect
matching.
Lemma 15. We have the following.
(a) A spider has a perfect matching if and only if it has exactly one leg of odd length.
(b) A spider has an almost perfect matching if and only if it has zero or two legs of odd
    length.
8          SAMANTHA DAHLBERG, ADRIAN SHE, AND STEPHANIE VAN WILLIGENBURG

Proof. Suppose a spider S has a perfect matching. The deletion of the centre of S produces
a union of disjoint paths, and exactly one of these paths must have odd length by Lemma 10.
Conversely, if S has exactly one leg of odd length, by matching the centre to its neighbour
in that leg, a perfect matching exists by Observation 14 since the deletion of these vertices
and their incident edges then produces a set of disjoint paths, each of even length.
   Now suppose a spider S has an almost perfect matching so that there exists a vertex v ′
whose deletion results in a perfect matching. If the deletion of the centre produces a graph
with a perfect matching, all legs in S have even length, by Observation 14. Otherwise, v ′
was in one of the legs L. Hence, unless the centre had degree 3 and v ′ was adjacent to it, the
deletion of v ′ produces a spider S ′ with an even number of vertices and a path of even length,
which may have 0 vertices. Notice that an odd number of vertices in total was deleted from
S to form S ′ .
   Hence, if the leg L in S ′ now has odd length, then every other leg in S had even length
by the first part. Therefore, all legs in S had even length as an odd number of vertices was
deleted from L to form S ′ . Otherwise, if the leg L in S ′ now has even length, then some
other leg in S ′ has odd length by the first part, and so as an odd number of vertices was
deleted from L to form S ′ , S originally had 2 legs of odd length.
   In the case where v ′ is adjacent to a degree 3 centre, the deletion of v ′ produces two disjoint
paths of even length. Hence, in this case, S had exactly 2 legs of odd length. This is because
one of the paths of even length must consist of 2 legs and the centre, so one of the legs must
be of odd length. Furthermore, the other path of even length must be a path of length one
less than the remaining third leg, due to the deletion of v ′ , so the third leg must be of odd
length.
   For the converse, if S has no legs of odd length, then the deletion of the centre produces a
disjoint union of paths with even length, which has a perfect matching by Observation 14. If
S has 2 legs of odd length, the the deletion of the leaf in exactly one of those legs produces a
spider with exactly one leg of odd length, which has a perfect matching by the first part. 

                     4. The e-positivity of trees and cut vertices
  We can now use our results from the previous section in conjunction with Lemmas 12
and 13 to deduce criteria for e-positivity of trees and graphs in general.
Theorem 34. If G is an n-vertex connected graph with a cut vertex whose deletion produces
a graph with d ≥ 3 connected components such that
                                         d ≥ log2 n + 1
then G is not e-positive.
  In particular, if T is an n-vertex tree with a vertex of degree d ≥ 3 such that
                                         d ≥ log2 n + 1
then T is not e-positive.
Proof. For the first part, by Lemma 13 and Theorem 30 every such n-vertex graph is missing
a connected partition of some type, and hence is not e-positive by Theorem 6. For the
second part we can either repeat the above argument but this time using Lemma 12 instead
of Lemma 13, or we can note that if we delete a vertex of degree d from a tree, then d
connected components remain.                                                             

                     5. The Schur-positivity of bipartite graphs
  While e-positivity implies Schur-positivity, it is possible for a graph to be Schur-positive
but not e-positive, for example the spider S(4, 1, 1) from Examples 3 and 5.




  Again we can determine whether trees or certain graphs are not Schur-positive using
a vertex degree criterion, but this time we will need the dominance order on partitions,
bipartite graphs, and stable partitions.
  For the first of these, recall that given two partitions of N, λ = (λ1 , . . . , λℓ(λ) ) and µ =
(µ1 , . . . , µℓ(µ) ), we say that λ dominates µ, denoted by λ ≥dom µ if
                                  λ1 + · · · + λi ≥ µ 1 + · · · + µ i
for all 1 ≤ i ≤ min{ℓ(λ), ℓ(µ)}. For the second of these, recall that a graph G is bipartite if
there exists a proper colouring of G with 2 colours. For the third of these, we say a stable
partition of an n-vertex graph G is a partitioning of its vertex set V into sets {V1 , . . . , Vk }
such that every set Vi in the partitioning is an independent set, namely no edge e ∈ EG
exists between any v1 , v2 ∈ Vi . The type of a stable partition is the partition of n formed by
sorting the sizes of each set Vi in decreasing order. We say G has a stable partition of type λ
if and only if there exists a stable partition of G of type λ, and is missing a stable partition
of type λ otherwise.

Example 37. Consider again the n-vertex star Sn for n ≥ 4. The graph Sn has a stable
partition of type (n − 1, 1) but is missing a stable partition of type (n − 2, 2).
  Stable partitions are intimately related to the Schur-positivity of a graph via the following
result.
Theorem 38. [23, Proposition 1.5] Suppose an n-vertex graph G has a stable partition of
type λ ⊢ n. If G is Schur-positive, then G has a stable partition of type µ for every µ ≤dom λ.
  This in turn yields a criterion for when a graph is not Schur-positive that is dependent on
vertex degrees.
Theorem 39. If G is an n-vertex bipartite graph with a vertex of degree greater than ⌈ n2 ⌉,
then G is not Schur-positive.
  In particular, if T is an n-vertex tree with a vertex of degree greater than ⌈ n2 ⌉, then T is
not Schur-positive.
Proof. For the first part, let G be an n-vertex bipartite graph with a vertex v of degree
d > ⌈ n2 ⌉. By assumption that G is bipartite, there is a proper colouring of G with two
colours. Call these colours red and black, and note that (V1 , V2 ) where V1 is the set of the
vertices coloured red and V2 is the set of vertices coloured black is a stable partition of VG .
The type of this stable partition will be a partition (m, n − m) where m > ⌈ n2 ⌉, since the
d > ⌈ n2 ⌉ vertices adjacent to v must be assigned a different colour from the colour of v by
assumption.
   We claim now that G does not have a stable partition of type (⌈ n2 ⌉, ⌊ n2 ⌋). Suppose G did
have such a partitioning of its vertices into (V1 , V2 ) with |V1 | = ⌈ n2 ⌉ and |V2 | = ⌊ n2 ⌋. If v ∈ V1 ,
then its neighbours must be in V2 , which is impossible since v has degree d > ⌈ n2 ⌉ ≥ ⌊ n2 ⌋.
Similarly, if v ∈ V2 , then its neighbours must be in V1 , which is impossible since v has degree
d > ⌈ n2 ⌉.
   Since (⌈ n2 ⌉, ⌊ n2 ⌋) <dom (m, n − m), and G has a stable partition of type (m, n − m), but G
is missing a stable partition of type (⌈ n2 ⌉, ⌊ n2 ⌋), then by Theorem 38, G is not Schur-positive.
   For the second part, note that all trees are bipartite.                                               
Example 40. Since the star Sn for n ≥ 4 is a tree and has one vertex of degree (n−1) > ⌈ n2 ⌉,
it is not Schur-positive, and hence again not e-positive, since e-positivity implies Schur-
positivity.

                                        6. Further avenues
  A natural avenue to pursue is to tighten the bound in Theorem 30, and to this end we
conjecture the following, which has been checked for all trees with up to 12 vertices.
Conjecture 41. If T is an n-vertex tree with a vertex of degree d ≥ 4, then T is not
e-positive.
                            POSITIVITY OF TREES AND CUT VERTICES                                     19

   Towards this one can use our techniques to check that certain families of spiders are missing
a particular type of partition. However, so far these results have been local to the family
of spiders being studied, and there does not seem to exist a natural global type of partition
that is missing. Moreover, as noted just before Lemma 31, there exist spiders that may not
be e-positive but still have a connected partition of every type, such as S(6, 4, 1, 1).
   In this case we can prove that the family of spiders S(r, s, 1, 1) is not e-positive by first
noting that if r or s is odd then S(r, s, 1, 1) is not e-positive by Corollary 16. If r and
s are even then we can prove this by using the triple-deletion rule of Orellana-Scott [19,
Theorem 3.1], and generalized to k-deletion by the first and third authors [6, Proposition
5], to express XS(r,s,1,1) as a linear combination of chromatic symmetric functions of unions
of paths. From here, by using the formula of Wolfe [27, Theorem 3.2] for expressing XPn
as a linear combination of elementary symmetric functions we can show that if r = 2k and
s = 2ℓ then
                                [e(3,2k+ℓ ) ]XS(r,s,1,1) = −2(r + s) + 7,
which is negative when r ≥ 2 and s ≥ 2. This technique can also be used to show that
S(r, 1, 1) for r ≥ 3 has
                                    [e(r−1,22 ) ]XS(r,1,1) = −(r − 1).
For example, returning to Example 5, note the term −3e(3,22 ) in XS(4,1,1) . Direct calculation
yields that S(1, 1, 1) is not e-positive but S(2, 1, 1) is e-positive, and hence deducing that
S(r, 1, 1) for r ≥ 3 is not e-positive supports Stanley’s statement [22, p 187] that S(2, 1, 1) is
e-positive “by accident”. Meanwhile, regarding Schur-positivity, we believe that the bound
in Theorem 39 cannot be improved. This is implied by the following conjecture, which has
been checked for all trees with up to 19 vertices, and with which we end.
Conjecture 42. For all n ≥ 2, there exists an n-vertex tree with a vertex of degree ⌊ n2 ⌋ that
is Schur-positive.
