                                                  if and only if n is an M -number. This proves a conjecture of McNew and Pomerance. We call n
                                                  matchable if D(n) admits a coprime matching with the primitive arithmetic progression {1, 2, . . . , N }.

   Necessity follows from the count above. Sufficiency requires more: terms divisible by different
primes may need the same divisors as coprime partners. By Hall’s theorem [2], a coprime matching
exists if and only if, for every subset X ⊆ P, at least |X| divisors of n are coprime to at least one
element of X. Theorem 1.1 shows that the counting bounds for individual primes already guarantee
all these inequalities, even when the progression is not primitive.
   The proof has two parts. We first construct a matrix whose entries are nonzero exactly when the
corresponding integers are coprime. An invertible change of columns gives a Vandermonde matrix
formed from distinct numbers. The original matrix therefore has a nonzero determinant, which
proves that a coprime matching exists. This construction applies under explicit bounds on the
exponents in the prime factorization of n. For the remaining cases allowed by (1), we partition the
progression into shorter progressions and combine matchings obtained from the same construction.
The two special cases considered below use [1, τ (n)] or require a matching with every primitive
arithmetic progression.
   A classical related problem is a conjecture of D. J. Newman: if I is any interval of N consecutive
integers, then [1, N ] admits a coprime matching with I. Pomerance and Selfridge proved this
conjecture in 1980 [6]. Robertson and Small later replaced the interval by an arithmetic progression
                     S := {a + bt : t = 0, 1, . . . , N − 1},   a ∈ Z,   b ∈ Z>0 ,
and determined exactly when a coprime matching [1, N ] → S exists [8]. In particular, such a
matching exists whenever gcd(a, b) = 1. Thus [1, N ] admits a coprime matching with every primitive
arithmetic progression of length N . More recent work has studied the existence and number of
coprime mappings and coprime permutations [4, 5, 9]; Bohman and Peng also connected coprime
mappings between intervals with the lonely runner problem [1].
   In 2022, Recamán called an integer n matchable if D(n) admits a coprime matching with [1, τ (n)]
and asked whether matchable integers are more numerous than nonmatchable integers [7]. The
ensuing discussion raised the problem of characterizing them. Among the comments on that question,
König and the author conjectured that every integer not divisible by pp for any prime p should be
matchable and that few integers outside this class should be matchable; McNew and Pomerance
record these conjectures in [3]. König also asked whether every divisor of a matchable integer must
itself be matchable. In the MathOverflow discussion [7], the author proposed the more precise

   McNew and Pomerance also considered a stronger requirement. They use “coprime arithmetic
progression” for what we call a primitive progression [3]. A positive integer n is strongly matchable
if D(n) admits a coprime matching with every primitive arithmetic progression of length τ (n). The
Robertson–Small theorem gives such a matching from [1, τ (n)]; the question is whether D(n) can
replace this interval. McNew and Pomerance conjectured that n is strongly matchable if and only if
it is an M -number [3, Conjecture 1].

Theorem 1.2. A positive integer is strongly matchable if and only if it is an M -number.

   The explicit classification also recovers the density theorem of McNew and Pomerance [3]. Fix
a prime p and consider the matchable integers n for which pp | n. Write n = pe m with p ∤ m.
Corollary 6.1 bounds both e and τ (m). If ω(m) denotes the number of distinct prime factors of m,
then 2ω(m) ≤ τ (m), so ω(m) is bounded. Integers with a bounded number of distinct prime factors
have density zero. Since only finitely many exponents of p occur, the corresponding integers also
form a set of density zero. For every positive

by pp for some prime p > P is at most p>P p−p . Combining this bound with the density-zero
result for the finitely many primes p ≤ P , and then letting P → ∞, shows that the matchable
non-M -numbers have density zero, in agreement with [3].
