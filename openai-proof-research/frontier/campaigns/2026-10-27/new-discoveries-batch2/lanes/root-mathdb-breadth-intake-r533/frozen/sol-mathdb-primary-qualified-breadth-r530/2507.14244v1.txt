Abstract.
    
Inspired by a question posed in [1], we investigate the precise ranges of functions dependent on a real parameter and defined via the floor of a real number. Our findings reveal that determining these ranges necessitates non-trivial tools, including the Kronecker approximation theorem. Our results emphasize the deep distinctions among irrational parameters and contrast the behavior of sub-unitary and supra-unitary rational parameters. We also propose several conjectures related to irrational numbers and supra-unitary rational ones, supported by extensive computations using Wolfram Mathematica.

  



Key words and phrases: mod operator, Bézout’s identity, Kronecker’s approximation theorem

1. Introduction



Given a real number x, the floor and ceiling of x are defined respectively by





\lfloor x\rfloor:=\max\bigl\{m\in\mathbb{Z}:m\leq x\bigr\}\quad\text{and}\quad\lceil x\rceil:=\min\bigl\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:n\geq x\bigr\}.


Equivalently, \lfloor x\rfloor and \lceil x\rceil are the unique integers satisfying





\lfloor x\rfloor\leq x<\lfloor x\rfloor+1\quad\text{and}\quad\lceil x\rceil-1<x\leq\lceil x\rceil.

(1)




It is also clear from the definitions that \lceil x\rceil-\lfloor x\rfloor\in\{0,1\}, and that
\lceil x\rceil-\lfloor x\rfloor=0 if and only if x\in\mathbb{Z}. The floor and ceiling functions appear in a wide range of mathematical contexts. For example, they are relevant in the statement of the Quadratic Reciprocity Law, in Legendre’s formula for computing the exponent of the highest power of a prime dividing a given integer, in expressions involving the Riemann zeta function, and many others.




In [1], the author of a problem for 10th graders presents an interesting function in terms of two appropriate floors. Specifically, given \alpha>0, define the function f_{\alpha}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Z} by





f_{\alpha}(n)=\bigl\lfloor\alpha^{2}\,n\bigr\rfloor-\bigl\lfloor\alpha\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor\bigr\rfloor,\quad\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N}.

(2)

The question asked in [1] was to find the precise range of f_{\tau}, where we denoted \tau=\frac{1+\sqrt{2017}}{2}. In this note, we shall investigate a generalization of the problem in [1] and discover some nice connections with other number theoretical facts, such as the concept of modulo (or simply, mod) operator [3] or Kronecker’s approximation theorem [4]. We also state several conjectures regarding the precise range of f_{\alpha} for general supra-unitary rational parameters, and also for irrational parameters \alpha. Some of the questions that arise are naturally related to the size of the space between appropriate floor and ceiling functions; see, for example, Remark 7 and Remark  28.

3. Rational parameters \alpha



Recall that if \alpha\in\mathbb{N}, then f_{\alpha}(n)=0 for all n\in\mathbb{N}. Thus, in what follows we shall concentrate on positive rational parameters \alpha which are non-integers. The next lemma states that finding the range of f_{\alpha} only requires the knowledge of a finite amount of values.




Lemma 3.

Let a\in\mathbb{N} and b\in\mathbb{N}\mkern-2.0mu\setminus\mkern-2.0mu\{1\} be relatively prime, and set \alpha=a/b. Then





\Range(f_{\alpha})=\{0\}\cup f_{\alpha}\bigl(\bigl\llbracket 1,b^{2}-1\bigr\rrbracket\bigr).







Proof.

Given n\in\mathbb{N}, by the division algorithm, there exist q,r\in\mathbb{Z} such that





n=b^{2}q+r,\quad r\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b^{2}-1\bigr\rrbracket.


Now, if r=0, it is easy to see that f_{\alpha}(b^{2}q)=0. If r\neq 0, then





\displaystyle f_{\alpha}(n)
\displaystyle=\bigg\lfloor a^{2}q+\frac{ra^{2}}{b^{2}}\bigg\rfloor-\bigg\lfloor\frac{a}{b}\left\lfloor abq+\frac{ar}{b}\right\rfloor\bigg\rfloor




\displaystyle=a^{2}q+\bigg\lfloor\frac{ra^{2}}{b^{2}}\bigg\rfloor-a^{2}q-\bigg\lfloor\frac{a}{b}\left\lfloor\frac{ar}{b}\right\rfloor\bigg\rfloor




\displaystyle=f_{\alpha}(r).\qed







3.1. Sub-unitary rational parameters \alpha



We will be assuming here that \alpha\in\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1). The function introduced in the following proposition is reminiscent of the concept of mod operator [3].




Proposition 4.

Let a,b\in\mathbb{N} be relatively prime with a<b. Define the function





\phi_{a,b}:\mathbb{N}\to\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}


by





\phi_{a,b}(n)=na-\left\lfloor n\frac{a}{b}\right\rfloor b,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.


Then, \Range(\phi_{a,b})=\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket.






Proof.

Using (1), we have





na-b<\left\lfloor n\frac{a}{b}\right\rfloor b\leq na,


proving that \phi_{a,b}(n)\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket for all n\in\mathbb{N}. We prove next that \bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket\subseteq\Range(\phi_{a,b}).
Since a and b are relatively prime, there exist x,y\in\mathbb{N} that satisfy Bézout’s identity





ax-by=1.


Let r\in\bigl\llbracket 1,b-1\bigr\rrbracket be arbitrary. Then





\frac{a}{b}xr=yr+\frac{r}{b}.


Since 0<r/b<1 and y\in\mathbb{N} we have





\left\lfloor xr\frac{a}{b}\right\rfloor=yr


and





\phi_{a,b}(xr)=xra-yrb=r(xa-yb)=r.\qed







The following simple lemma will be needed in our next result.




Lemma 5.

Let a,b\in\mathbb{N} be relatively prime with a<b. The range of the function





(x,y)\mapsto ax+by,\qquad x,y\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket,

(6)

includes \bigl\llbracket 0,b^{2}-1\bigr\rrbracket if and only if a=1.






Proof.

Let a,b\in\mathbb{N}. Notice that the minimum of the function defined in (6) is 0, its maximum is (a+b)(b-1), so that its codomain is \bigl\llbracket 0,(a+b)(b-1)\bigr\rrbracket. Note also that the domain has b^{2} elements while the codomain has 1+(a+b)(b-1) elements, and b^{2}\leq 1+(a+b)(b-1). Further assume that a and b are relatively prime. Since





ax_{1}+by_{1}=ax_{2}+by_{2}\quad\text{with}\quad x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2}\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket,


yields the pair equality (x_{1},y_{1})=(x_{2},y_{2}), the function in (6) is an injection. If a=1, then the function in (6) is a bijection as its domain and its codomain have the same number of elements. Let a>1. Then, by a<b, we also get b>1. Since the set \bigl\llbracket 0,b^{2}-1\bigr\rrbracket and the range of the function (6) have the same number of elements (namely, b^{2}), and since the maximum of the function is





(a+b)(b-1)=b-a+(a-2)b+b^{2}


which is strictly greater than b^{2}-1, it is not possible that \bigl\llbracket 0,b^{2}-1\bigr\rrbracket is a subset of the range. In particular, then 1 is not in the range of the function defined in (6). This proves the claim.
∎






Proposition 6.

Let \alpha\in\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1]. The following dichotomy holds:





(i) 

If \alpha=1/b with b\in\mathbb{N}, then \Range(f_{\alpha})=\{0\}.



(ii) 

If \alpha=a/b with a,b\in\mathbb{N} relatively prime and 1<a<b, then \Range(f_{\alpha})=\{0,1\}.









Proof.

By Proposition 1, we know that \Range(f_{\alpha})\subseteq\{0,1\}. Moreover, either using Corollary 2 or Lemma 3 (or, further noting that f_{\alpha}(kb)=0 for all k\in\mathbb{N}), we have that \{0\}\subseteq\Range(f_{\alpha}).




Now let us assume that f_{\alpha}(n)=0 for all n\in\mathbb{N}. Then, for all n\in\mathbb{N}, we have





\left\lfloor n\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}\right\rfloor=\biggl\lfloor\Bigl\lfloor n\frac{a}{b}\Bigr\rfloor\frac{a}{b}\biggr\rfloor.





Let r\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b^{2}-1\bigr\rrbracket be arbitrary. Let a,b\in\mathbb{N} be relatively prime and such that a<b. By Proposition 4 applied to a^{2} and b^{2}, there exists m\in\mathbb{N} such that \phi_{a^{2},b^{2}}(m)=r. Then





m\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}=\left\lfloor m\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}\right\rfloor+\frac{r}{b^{2}}.

(7)

By Proposition 4, we have





m\frac{a}{b}=\left\lfloor m\frac{a}{b}\right\rfloor+\frac{\phi_{a,b}(m)}{b}

(8)

and





\left\lfloor m\frac{a}{b}\right\rfloor\frac{a}{b}=\biggl\lfloor\left\lfloor m\frac{a}{b}\right\rfloor\frac{a}{b}\biggr\rfloor+\frac{\phi_{a,b}\left(\left\lfloor m\frac{a}{b}\right\rfloor\right)}{b}.

(9)

Multiplying (8) by a/b and using (9) we get





m\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}=\biggl\lfloor\left\lfloor m\frac{a}{b}\right\rfloor\frac{a}{b}\biggr\rfloor+\frac{\phi_{a,b}\left(\left\lfloor m\frac{a}{b}\right\rfloor\right)}{b}+\frac{\phi_{a,b}(m)}{b}\frac{a}{b}.

(10)

Now (10), (8) and (7) imply





r=a\phi_{a,b}(m)+b\phi_{a,b}\left(\left\lfloor m\tfrac{a}{b}\right\rfloor\right).


Since \phi_{a,b}(m),\phi_{a,b}\left(\left\lfloor m\tfrac{a}{b}\right\rfloor\right)\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket and since r\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b^{2}-1\bigr\rrbracket was arbitrary, Lemma 5 implies that a=1.
∎






Remark 7.

Note that, using Lemma 3, we can further rephrase the dichotomy in Proposition 6 as follows: For \alpha=a/b with a,b\in\mathbb{N} relatively prime and a<b, the equation





\lfloor\alpha^{2}n\rfloor=\bigl\lceil\alpha\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor\bigr\rceil


is solvable for n in the set \bigl\llbracket 0,b^{2}-1\bigr\rrbracket\setminus b\mkern 1.0mu\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket.






Remark 8.

The proof of Proposition 6 does not explicitly indicate how to exhibit the value 1 in the \Range(f_{\alpha}) in the case (ii). We show how this can be achieved.




Let a,b\in\mathbb{N} be relatively prime and such that 1<a<b. Let n,k\in\mathbb{N} be such that they satisfy Bézout’s identity





a^{2}n-b^{2}k=1.


Since





k<\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}n=k+\frac{1}{b^{2}}<k+1,


we deduce





\left\lfloor\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}n\right\rfloor=k.


Set





\left\lfloor\frac{a}{b}n\right\rfloor=r.


Then, equivalently,





r<\frac{a}{b}n<r+1.


Hence,
an-br\in\mathbb{N}.
Therefore, since a>1, we have





0<a(an-br)-1=a^{2}n-abr-a^{2}n+b^{2}k=-abr+b^{2}k=b(bk-ar).


Thus, l=bk-ar\in\mathbb{N}, in particular ar<bk, and consequently,





\frac{a}{b}\left\lfloor\frac{a}{b}n\right\rfloor=\frac{a}{b}r=\frac{ar}{b}<\frac{bk}{b}=k.


The last strict inequality yields





\biggl\lfloor\frac{a}{b}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{a}{b}n\Bigr\rfloor\biggr\rfloor<k.


In conclusion,





f_{a/b}(n)=\left\lfloor\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}n\right\rfloor-\biggl\lfloor\frac{a}{b}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{a}{b}n\Bigr\rfloor\biggr\rfloor>0,


hence f_{a/b}(n)=1.








3.2. Supra-unitary rational parameters \alpha



Throughout this subsection, we let \alpha>1 be a rational number which is not an integer. Our first result settles the exact range of f_{\alpha} for supra-unitary rational numbers with denominator 2.




Proposition 9.

Let s\in\mathbb{N}. Then


(i) 

\Range\bigl(f_{2s+\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)=\{0,s\}=s\bigl\llbracket 0,1\bigr\rrbracket;



(ii) 

\Range\bigl(f_{2s-1+\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)=\{0,s-1,s\}=s\bigl\llbracket 0,1\bigr\rrbracket\cup(s-1)\bigl\llbracket 1,1\bigr\rrbracket.









Proof.

We consider first the case where \alpha=2s+\frac{1}{2}. Then, for all n\in\mathbb{N}, we have





\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor=2sn+\Big\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\Big\rfloor=\begin{cases}2sn+\frac{n}{2},&\text{if}\quad n\quad\text{is even}\\
2sn+\frac{n-1}{2},&\text{if}\quad n\quad\text{is odd}.\end{cases}


Therefore,





\bigl\lfloor\alpha\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor\bigr\rfloor=\begin{cases}4s^{2}n+2sn+\Big\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\Big\rfloor,&\text{if}\quad n\quad\text{is even}\\
4s^{2}n+2sn-s+\Big\lfloor\frac{n-1}{4}\Big\rfloor,&\text{if}\quad n\quad\text{is odd}.\end{cases}


Also, \lfloor\alpha^{2}n\rfloor=4s^{2}n+2sn+\Big\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\Big\rfloor. Thus,





\lfloor\alpha^{2}n\rfloor-\bigl\lfloor\alpha\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor\bigr\rfloor=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if}\quad n\quad\text{is even}\\
s+\Big\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\Big\rfloor-\Big\lfloor\frac{n-1}{4}\Big\rfloor,&\text{if}\quad n\quad\text{is odd}.\end{cases}


Now, since \Big\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\Big\rfloor-\Big\lfloor\frac{n-1}{4}\Big\rfloor=0 for n odd, we obtain that





\Range(f_{2s+\frac{1}{2}})=\{0,s\}.


Suppose next that \alpha=2s-1+\frac{1}{2}. Similar calculations to the ones for the even case above show that, for all n\in\mathbb{N}, we have





\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor=2sn+\Big\lfloor-\frac{n}{2}\Big\rfloor=2sn-\Big\lceil\frac{n}{2}\Big\rceil=\begin{cases}2sn-\frac{n}{2},&\text{if}\quad n\quad\text{is even}\\[6.0pt]
2sn-\frac{n+1}{2},&\text{if}\quad n\quad\text{is odd},\end{cases}






\lfloor\alpha\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor\rfloor=\begin{cases}4s^{2}n-2sn+\Big\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\Big\rfloor,&\text{if}\quad n\quad\text{is even}\\[6.0pt]
4s^{2}n-2sn-s+\Big\lfloor\frac{n+1}{4}\Big\rfloor,&\text{if}\quad n\quad\text{is odd}\end{cases}


and
\lfloor\alpha^{2}n\rfloor=4s^{2}n-2sn+\Big\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\Big\rfloor. Consequently,





\lfloor\alpha^{2}n\rfloor-\bigl\lfloor\alpha\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor\bigr\rfloor=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if}\quad n\quad\text{is even}\\
s+\Big\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\Big\rfloor-\Big\lfloor\frac{n+1}{4}\Big\rfloor,&\text{if}\quad n\quad\text{is odd}.\end{cases}


Note now that \Big\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\Big\rfloor-\Big\lfloor\frac{n+1}{4}\Big\rfloor=-1 for n odd such that n\equiv 3 (mod 4), while \Big\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\Big\rfloor-\Big\lfloor\frac{n+1}{4}\Big\rfloor=0 for n\not\equiv 3 (mod 4). This implies that





\Range\bigl(f_{2s-1+\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)=\{0,s-1,s\}.\qed







In what follows, we explore the general situation in which the (non-integer) rational number \alpha>1 has denominator b\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}. By the division algorithm, we can express our parameter as





\alpha=(sb+u)+\frac{a}{b},


where s\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}, u\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket and a\in\bigl\llbracket 1,b-1\bigr\rrbracket. We have the following decomposition of f_{\alpha}.




Proposition 10.

Let b\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}, s\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}, u\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket and a\in\bigl\llbracket 1,b-1\bigr\rrbracket. Then





f_{sb+u+\frac{a}{b}}=s\phi_{a,b}+f_{u+\frac{a}{b}},


where \phi_{a,b} is the function defined in Proposition 4.






Proof.

Straightforward calculations give that, for \alpha=(sb+u)+\frac{a}{b}, we have





\displaystyle\lfloor\alpha^{2}n\rfloor
\displaystyle=(sb+u)^{2}n+2asn+\Big\lfloor\frac{2au}{b}n+\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}n\Big\rfloor




\displaystyle=(sb+u)^{2}n+2asn-u^{2}n+\Big\lfloor\left(\frac{a}{b}+u\right)^{2}n\Big\rfloor.


Next, we have





\displaystyle\alpha\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor
\displaystyle=(sb+u)^{2}n+asn+sb\Big\lfloor\frac{a}{b}n\Big\rfloor+\frac{au}{b}n+\left(\frac{a}{b}+u\right)\Big\lfloor\frac{a}{b}n\Big\rfloor




\displaystyle=(sb+u)^{2}n+asn+sb\Big\lfloor\frac{a}{b}n\Big\rfloor+\frac{2au}{b}n+\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}n-\left(\frac{a}{b}+u\right)\mathopen{\vbox{\hbox{\hbox to4.08pt{\vbox to20.63pt{\pgfpicture\makeatletter\hbox{\hskip 0.2pt\lower-10.31476pt\hbox to0.0pt{\lxSVG@begingroup@{_scopebegin=1} \lxSVG@begingroup@{stroke=#000000} \lxSVG@begingroup@{fill=#000000} \lxSVG@setlinewidth{\the\pgflinewidth}\lxSVG@begingroup@{stroke-width=0.4pt} \lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nullfont\hbox to0.0pt{\lxSVG@begingroup@{_scopebegin=1}

\lxSVG@closescope 
\lxSVG@closescope {\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces}{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces}{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces}\hss}\lxSVG@discardpath\lxSVG@closescope \hss}}\lxSVG@closescope\endpgfpicture}}}}}\frac{a}{b}n\mathclose{\vbox{\hbox{\hbox to4.08pt{\vbox to20.63pt{\pgfpicture\makeatletter\hbox{\hskip 3.8815pt\lower-10.31476pt\hbox to0.0pt{\lxSVG@begingroup@{_scopebegin=1} \lxSVG@begingroup@{stroke=#000000} \lxSVG@begingroup@{fill=#000000} \lxSVG@setlinewidth{\the\pgflinewidth}\lxSVG@begingroup@{stroke-width=0.4pt} \lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nullfont\hbox to0.0pt{\lxSVG@begingroup@{_scopebegin=1}

Two immediate consequences of the decomposition in Proposition 10 are the following.




Corollary 11.

Let b\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}, s\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}, u\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket and a\in\bigl\llbracket 1,b-1\bigr\rrbracket relatively prime to b. Then





\Range\bigl(f_{sb+u+\frac{a}{b}}\bigr)\subseteq s\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket+\bigl\llbracket 0,u+1\bigr\rrbracket.







Proof.

Noting that \Big\lceil u+\frac{a}{b}\Big\rceil=u+1, the inclusion follows from Proposition 4 and Proposition 1
∎






Corollary 12.

Let b\in\mathbb{N}\mkern-2.0mu\setminus\mkern-2.0mu\{1\} and s\in\mathbb{N}. Then





\Range\bigl(f_{sb+\frac{1}{b}}\bigr)=s\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket.







Proof.

By Proposition 10 and Proposition 6, part (i), for all n\in\mathbb{N} we have





f_{sb+\frac{1}{b}}(n)=s\phi_{1,b}(n)+f_{\frac{1}{b}}(n)=s\phi_{1,b}(n).


The equality \Range\bigl(f_{sb+\frac{1}{b}}\bigr)=s\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket now follows from Proposition 4.
∎






While Proposition 10 provides a good starting point for identifying the exact range of functions such as f_{sb+\frac{1}{b}}, we need a slightly modified version of it to deal with other supra-unitary parameters of the form sb+u+\frac{1}{b} with u>0.




Lemma 13.

Let b\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}, s\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}, and u\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket. Let n=bt+r with t,r\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket. Then





f_{sb+u+\frac{1}{b}}(n)=sr+\Big\lfloor\frac{2ur}{b}+\frac{t}{b}+\frac{r}{b^{2}}\Big\rfloor-\Big\lfloor\frac{ur}{b}+\frac{t}{b}\Big\rfloor.







In particular, for u=0, we obtain
f_{sb+\frac{1}{b}}(n)=sr,
which implies the statement of Corollary 12.




Proof.

The calculations are similar to the ones in the proof of Proposition 10. We only present the essential identities. With \alpha=bs+u+\frac{1}{b}, we have





\lfloor\alpha^{2}n\rfloor=(bs+u)^{2}n+2sn+2ut+\Big\lfloor\frac{2ur}{b}+\frac{t}{b}+\frac{r}{b^{2}}\Big\rfloor


and





\bigl\lfloor\alpha\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor\bigr\rfloor=(bs+u)^{2}n+sn+bst+2ut+\Big\lfloor\frac{ur}{b}+\frac{t}{b}\Big\rfloor.


Therefore,





\displaystyle f_{\alpha}(n)
\displaystyle=s(n-bt)+\Big\lfloor\frac{2ur}{b}+\frac{t}{b}+\frac{r}{b^{2}}\Big\rfloor-\Big\lfloor\frac{ur}{b}+\frac{t}{b}\Big\rfloor




\displaystyle=sr+\Big\lfloor\frac{2ur}{b}+\frac{t}{b}+\frac{r}{b^{2}}\Big\rfloor-\Big\lfloor\frac{ur}{b}+\frac{t}{b}\Big\rfloor.\qed







Our next result states an inclusion relation that improves the inclusion stated in Corollary 11 when a=1.




Proposition 14.

For all b\in\mathbb{N}\mkern-2.0mu\setminus\mkern-2.0mu\{1\}, all s\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}, and all u\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket such that sb+u\in\mathbb{N}, we have





\Range\bigl(f_{sb+u+\frac{1}{b}}\bigr)\subseteq s\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket+\bigl\llbracket 0,u\bigr\rrbracket.







Proof.

Let \alpha=sb+u+\frac{1}{b}. Using the notation from Lemma 13, with n=bt+r and t,r\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket, we can rewrite





f_{\alpha}(n)=sr+\Big\lfloor\frac{2ur}{b}+\frac{n}{b^{2}}\Big\rfloor-\Big\lfloor\frac{(ub-1)r}{b^{2}}+\frac{n}{b^{2}}\Big\rfloor.

(11)

Let us denote for simplicity





x(n,u)=\frac{2ur}{b}+\frac{n}{b^{2}}\,\,\text{and}\,\,y(n,u)=\frac{(ub-1)r}{b^{2}}+\frac{n}{b^{2}}.


Clearly, if u=0, we have \lfloor x(n,u)\rfloor=\lfloor y(n,u)\rfloor=0. If u\geq 1, we see that





x(n,u)-y(n,u)=\frac{(ub+1)r}{b^{2}}\geq 0


and





x(n,u)-y(n,u)\leq\frac{(ub+1)(b-1)}{b^{2}}=u-\frac{u-1}{b}-\frac{1}{b^{2}}<u.


Therefore,





0\leq\bigl\lfloor x(n,u)-y(n,u)\bigr\rfloor\leq\bigl\lfloor x(n,u)\bigr\rfloor-\bigl\lfloor y(n,u)\bigr\rfloor,


and





\bigl\lfloor x(n,u)\bigr\rfloor-\bigl\lfloor y(n,u)\bigr\rfloor\leq\lfloor x(n,u)-y(n,u)\rfloor+1\leq(u-1)+1=u.


In other words, for all n\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b^{2}-1\bigr\rrbracket and u\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket, we have





\bigl\lfloor x(n,u)\bigr\rfloor-\bigl\lfloor y(n,u)\bigr\rfloor\in\bigl\llbracket 0,u\bigr\rrbracket.


This finishes the proof.
∎






In particular, when u=1, we get the following.




Corollary 15.

For all b\in\mathbb{N}\mkern-2.0mu\setminus\mkern-2.0mu\{1\} and all s\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\} we have





\Range\bigl(f_{sb+1+\frac{1}{b}}\bigr)\subseteq s\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket+\bigl\llbracket 0,1\bigr\rrbracket.







Corollary 16.

Let b\in\mathbb{N}\mkern-2.0mu\setminus\mkern-2.0mu\{1\}. Then





\Range\bigl(f_{1+\frac{1}{b}}\bigr)=\{0,1\}.







Proof.

Using Lemma 3 and Corollary 15, we only need to prove that
1\in\Range\bigl(f_{1+\frac{1}{b}}\bigr). We claim that
f_{1+\frac{1}{b}}(b-1)=1.
Indeed, letting s=0, u=1, t=0 and r=b-1 in Lemma 13 (or, alternately, in the formula (11)), we have





f_{1+\frac{1}{b}}(b-1)=\Big\lfloor 2-\frac{b+1}{b^{2}}\Big\rfloor-\Big\lfloor\frac{b-1}{b}\Big\rfloor.


But since 1<2-\frac{b+1}{b^{2}}<2 and 0<\frac{b-1}{b}<1, we get f_{1+\frac{1}{b}}(b-1)=1.
∎






A refinement of the argument in Corollary 16 gives the next result.




Corollary 17.

Let b\in\mathbb{N}\mkern-2.0mu\setminus\mkern-2.0mu\{1\}. Then





\Range\bigl(f_{b+1+\frac{1}{b}}\bigr)=\bigl\llbracket 0,b\bigr\rrbracket.







Proof.

Using again Lemma 3 and Corollary 15, it suffices to show that \bigl\llbracket 1,b\bigr\rrbracket\subseteq\Range\bigl(f_{b+1+1/b}\bigr). We proceed in two steps. First, we claim that f_{b+1+\frac{1}{b}}(b^{2}-b+1)=1. Indeed, letting s=1, u=1, t=b-1 and r=1 in Lemma 13, and since 1<1+\frac{b+1}{b^{2}}<2, we get





f_{b+1+\frac{1}{b}}\bigl(b^{2}-b+1\bigr)=1+\Big\lfloor 1+\frac{b+1}{b^{2}}\Big\rfloor-1=1.


Secondly, let us fix r\in\bigl\llbracket 1,b-1\bigr\rrbracket. We will show that there exists a t(r)\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket such that f_{b+1+\frac{1}{b}}\bigl(bt(r)+r\bigr)=r+1. Indeed, let s=u=1 and t=t(r):=b-1-r in Lemma 13 and notice that





f_{b+1+\frac{1}{b}}\bigl(bt+r\bigr)=r+\Big\lfloor\mkern-1.0mu\frac{r}{b}+\frac{r}{b^{2}}+\frac{b-1}{b}\mkern-1.0mu\Big\rfloor-\Big\lfloor\mkern-1.0mu\frac{b-1}{b}\mkern-1.0mu\Big\rfloor=r+\Big\lfloor\mkern-1.0mu1+\frac{r-1}{b}+\frac{r}{b^{2}}\mkern-1.0mu\Big\rfloor.


Next, observe that 0<\frac{r-1}{b}+\frac{r}{b^{2}}<1. The left-side inequality is trivial while the right-side inequality is equivalent to (r-1)b+r<b^{2}\Leftrightarrow r<b. This proves the claim f_{b+1+\frac{1}{b}}\bigl(b(b-1-r)+r\bigr)=r+1 and concludes our proof.
∎






The following corollary unifies Corollaries 16 and 17.




Corollary 18.

For all b\in\mathbb{N}\mkern-2.0mu\setminus\mkern-2.0mu\{1\} and and s\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\} we have





\Range\bigl(f_{sb+1+\frac{1}{b}}\bigr)=\bigl(s\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket\bigr)\cup\bigl(s\bigl\llbracket 1,b-1\bigr\rrbracket+\{1\}\bigr).







Proof.

By Lemma 3 and Corollary 15, it suffices to show
1\not\in\Range\bigl(f_{sb+1+\frac{1}{b}}\bigr) and s\bigl\llbracket 1,b-1\bigr\rrbracket+\{0,1\}\subseteq\Range\bigl(f_{sb+1+\frac{1}{b}}\bigr). First of all, using the notation from Lemma 13, for all r\in\bigl\llbracket 1,b-1\bigr\rrbracket, we have f_{sb+1+\frac{1}{b}}(bt+r)\geq sr\geq s>1 (and, as we have seen before, f_{sb+1+\frac{1}{b}}(bt)=0). For the second claim, we will show that for fixed
r\in\bigl\llbracket 1,b-1\bigr\rrbracket, we can find t_{0}(r),t_{1}(r)\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket such that





f_{\alpha}\bigl(bt_{0}(r)+r\bigr)=sr\quad\text{and}\quad f_{\alpha}\bigl(bt_{1}(r)+r\bigr)=sr+1.


Equivalently, we want to show that, for fixed r\in\bigl\llbracket 1,b-1\bigr\rrbracket, the equations





\Big\lfloor\frac{2r}{b}+\frac{r}{b^{2}}+\frac{t}{b}\Big\rfloor=\Big\lfloor\frac{r}{b}+\frac{t}{b}\Big\rfloor,

(12)

respectively





\Big\lfloor\frac{2r}{b}+\frac{r}{b^{2}}+\frac{t}{b}\Big\rfloor=\Big\lceil\frac{r}{b}+\frac{t}{b}\Big\rceil

(13)

are solvable in t\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket.




The equation  (12) has the solution t_{0}=t_{0}(r)=b-r. Indeed, \displaystyle\Big\lfloor\frac{r}{b}+\frac{t_{0}}{b}\Big\rfloor=1 and





\frac{2r}{b}+\frac{r}{b^{2}}+\frac{t_{0}}{b}=\frac{r}{b}+\frac{r}{b^{2}}+1\in(1,2)


since r\leq b-1<\displaystyle\frac{b^{2}}{b+1}, therefore also \displaystyle\Big\lfloor\frac{2r}{b}+\frac{r}{b^{2}}+\frac{t_{0}}{b}\Big\rfloor=1. The equation  (13) has the solution t_{1}(r)=b-1-r by the argument proving Corollary 17.
∎






Similar considerations to the ones proving Proposition 14 and Corollary 18 give the following result.




Corollary 19.

Let b\in\mathbb{N}\mkern-3.0mu\setminus\mkern-3.0mu\{1\} and s\in\mathbb{N}. Then





\Range\bigl(f_{sb-1+\frac{1}{b}}\bigr)=\bigl(s\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket\bigr)\cup\bigl(s\bigl\llbracket 1,b-1\bigr\rrbracket-\{1\}\bigr).







Proof.

Let \alpha=sb-1+\frac{1}{b}=(s-1)b+(b-1)+\frac{1}{b}. With s replaced by s-1\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}, and u=b-1, in the proof of Proposition 14, we find that (11) can be re-written as





f_{\alpha}(n)=(s-1)r+\Big\lfloor\frac{2(b-1)r}{b}+\frac{n}{b^{2}}\Big\rfloor-\Big\lfloor\frac{((b-1)b-1)r}{b^{2}}+\frac{n}{b^{2}}\Big\rfloor.


As before, letting now n=bt+r with t,r\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket, a few simple manipulations yield





f_{\alpha}(bt+r)=sr-\Bigl(\Big\lfloor\frac{r}{b}+\frac{t}{b}\Big\rfloor-\Big\lfloor-\frac{2r}{b}+\frac{r}{b^{2}}+\frac{t}{b}\Big\rfloor\Bigr).

(14)

Denote





x(b,t,r)=\frac{r}{b}+\frac{t}{b}\,\,\text{and}\,\,y(b,t,r)=-\frac{2r}{b}+\frac{r}{b^{2}}+\frac{t}{b}.


We have





x(b,t,r)-y(b,t,r)=\frac{(b-1)r}{b^{2}}\in\Bigl(0,\frac{(b-1)^{2}}{b^{2}}\Bigr]\subseteq(0,1).


This implies that \lfloor x(b,t,r)-y(b,t,r)\rfloor=0, thus





\lfloor x(b,t,r)\rfloor-\lfloor y(b,t,r)\rfloor\in\{0,1\}.

(15)

Using (14) and (15), we conclude that





\Range\bigl(f_{sb-1+\frac{1}{b}}\bigr)\subseteq s\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket-\{0,1\}.


Since -1\not\in\Range\bigl(f_{sb-1+\frac{1}{b}}\bigr), we have





\Range\bigl(f_{sb-1+\frac{1}{b}}\bigr)\subseteq\bigl(s\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket\bigr)\cup\bigl(s\bigl\llbracket 1,b-1\bigr\rrbracket-\{1\}\bigr).


For the converse inclusion, as in the proof of Corollary 18, it suffices to show that





s\bigl\llbracket 1,b-1\bigr\rrbracket-\{0,1\}\subseteq\Range\bigl(f_{sb-1+\frac{1}{b}}\bigr),


which is equivalent to showing that for fixed
r\in\bigl\llbracket 1,b-1\bigr\rrbracket, we can find t(r),\tilde{t}(r)\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket such that





f_{\alpha}\bigl(bt(r)+r\bigr)=sr\quad\text{and}\quad f_{\alpha}\bigl(b\tilde{t}(r)+r\bigr)=sr-1.


Equivalently, we want to show that, for fixed r\in\bigl\llbracket 1,b-1\bigr\rrbracket, the equations





\Big\lfloor-\frac{r}{b}+\frac{t}{b}\Big\rfloor=\Big\lfloor-\frac{2r}{b}+\frac{r}{b^{2}}+\frac{t}{b}\Big\rfloor,

(16)

respectively





\Big\lfloor-\frac{r}{b}+\frac{t}{b}\Big\rfloor=1+\Big\lfloor-\frac{2r}{b}+\frac{r}{b^{2}}+\frac{t}{b}\Big\rfloor

(17)

are solvable in t\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket. The equation (16) has the solution t(r)=r-1. The equation (17) has the solution \tilde{t}(r)=r.
∎






The exact range results stated in Proposition 9, Corollary 12, Corollary 18 and Corollary 19 capture some generic supra-unitary (non-integer) rational parameters \alpha with denominator b\geq 2. Specifically, we understand precisely the cases where \alpha=k+\frac{1}{b} and k\equiv u (mod b) with u\in\{0,1,b-1\}, but, even in the small denominator cases, we are missing lots of the rational supra-unitary parameters. The result below is a counterpart to Proposition 9 which settles the exact ranges for all supra-unitary rational parameters with denominator 3.




Proposition 20.

Let s\in\mathbb{N}. Then


(i) 

\Range\bigl(f_{3s+\frac{1}{3}}\bigr)=\{0,s,2s\};



(ii) 

\Range\bigl(f_{3s+\frac{2}{3}}\bigr)=\{0,s,2s,2s+1\};



(iii) 

\Range\bigl(f_{3s-2+\frac{1}{3}}\bigr)=\{0,s-1,s,2s-2,2s-1\};



(iv) 

\Range\bigl(f_{3s-2+\frac{2}{3}}\bigr)=\{0,s-1,s,2s-1\};



(v) 

\Range\bigl(f_{3s-1+\frac{1}{3}}\bigr)=\{0,s-1,s,2s-1,2s\};



(vi) 

\Range\bigl(f_{3s-1+\frac{2}{3}}\bigr)=\{0,s-1,s,2s-1,2s\}.









Proof.

(i) follows from Corollary 12, (iii) follows from Corollary 18 (via the substitution s\to s-1) and (v) follows from Corollary 19.




(ii) Let \alpha=3s+\frac{2}{3}. Based on Lemma 3, we know that 0\in\Range(f_{\alpha}) and for all the other values in \Range(f_{\alpha}) we can concentrate on computing f_{\alpha}(n) with n\in\bigl\llbracket 0,8\bigr\rrbracket. For such an n, we have





\alpha^{2}n=9s^{2}n+4sn+\frac{4n}{9}\Rightarrow\lfloor\alpha^{2}n\rfloor=9s^{2}n+4sn+\Big\lfloor\frac{4n}{9}\Big\rfloor.


Similarly, we compute





\bigl\lfloor\alpha\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor\bigr\rfloor=9s^{2}n+2sn+3s\Big\lfloor\frac{2n}{3}\Big\rfloor+\bigg\lfloor\frac{2}{3}\Big\lfloor\frac{2n}{3}\Big\rfloor\bigg\rfloor.


Hence,





f_{\alpha}(n)=2sn-3s\Big\lfloor\frac{2n}{3}\Big\rfloor+\Big\lfloor\frac{4n}{9}\Big\rfloor-\bigg\lfloor\frac{2}{3}\Big\lfloor\frac{2n}{3}\Big\rfloor\bigg\rfloor.

(18)

Using (18), we compute:





f_{\alpha}(3)=f_{\alpha}(6)=0,\quad f_{\alpha}(2)=f_{\alpha}(5)=f_{\alpha}(8)=s,\\
f_{\alpha}(1)=f_{\alpha}(4)=2s,\quad f_{\alpha}(7)=2s+1.





(iv) Let \alpha=3s-2+\frac{2}{3}=3s-\frac{4}{3}. After some algebraic manipulations, we obtain





f_{\alpha}(n)=-sn+3s\Big\lceil\frac{n}{3}\Big\rceil+\Big\lfloor\frac{7n}{9}\Big\rfloor-\Big\lceil\frac{n}{3}\Big\rceil-\bigg\lfloor\frac{n}{3}+\frac{1}{3}\Big\lceil\frac{n}{3}\Big\rceil\bigg\rfloor.

(19)

Using (19), we can now compute:





f_{\alpha}(3)=f_{\alpha}(6)=0,\quad f_{\alpha}(2)=f_{\alpha}(5)=s-1,\\
f_{\alpha}(8)=s,\quad f_{\alpha}(1)=f_{\alpha}(4)=f_{\alpha}(7)=2s-1.





(vi) Let \alpha=3s-1+\frac{2}{3}=3s-\frac{1}{3}. Since n\in\bigl\llbracket 0,8\bigr\rrbracket, we have





f_{\alpha}(n)=-sn+3s\Big\lceil\frac{n}{3}\Big\rceil-\bigg\lfloor\frac{1}{3}\Big\lceil\frac{n}{3}\Big\rceil\bigg\rfloor.

(20)

Using (20), we obtain:





f_{\alpha}(3)=f_{\alpha}(6)=0,\quad f_{\alpha}(8)=s-1,\quad f_{\alpha}(2)=f_{\alpha}(5)=s,\\
f_{\alpha}(7)=2s-1,\quad f_{\alpha}(1)=f_{\alpha}(4)=2s.\qed







Besides the general cases already settled above and performing ad-hoc calculations in the small denominators cases, such as in Proposition 9 or Proposition  20, identifying the exact \Range(f_{\alpha}) for supra-unitary rational values of \alpha seems to be a rather difficult task. It is not clear to these authors how to proceed in general for such parameters beyond the situations already addressed above. For example, our Wolfram Mathematica experimentations are pretty convincing that the following statement should be true, but we are unable to prove it.




Conjecture 21.

For all b\in\mathbb{N}\mkern-3.0mu\setminus\mkern-3.0mu\{1\}, all a\in\bigl\llbracket 1,b-1\bigr\rrbracket relatively prime to b, and all s\in\mathbb{N} we have





\Range\bigl(f_{sb-\frac{a}{b}}\bigr)=\bigl(s\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket\bigr)\cup\bigl(s\bigl\llbracket 1,b-1\bigr\rrbracket-\{1\}\bigr).







If indeed true, Conjecture 21 would generalize Corollary 19 which corresponds to a=b-1. The following lemma is in the spirit of Lemma 13. The calculations are similar to the ones done there and left to the interested reader. We include it here since it may indicate a possible direction to deal with cases such as those in Conjecture 21.




Lemma 22.

Let b\in\mathbb{N}\mkern-1.0mu\setminus\mkern-2.0mu\{1\}, u\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket and a\in\bigl\llbracket 1,b-1\bigr\rrbracket. Let n=bt+r, with t,r\in\bigl\llbracket 0,b-1\bigr\rrbracket. Then





f_{sb+u+\frac{a}{b}}(n)=sar-(sb+u)\Big\lfloor\mkern-2.0mu\frac{ar}{b}\mkern-2.0mu\Big\rfloor+\bigg\lfloor\mkern-2.0mu\frac{2uar}{b}+\frac{a^{2}t}{b}+\frac{a^{2}r}{b^{2}}\mkern-2.0mu\bigg\rfloor-\bigg\lfloor\mkern-2.0mu\frac{uar}{b}+\frac{a^{2}t}{b}+\frac{a}{b}\Big\lfloor\frac{ar}{b}\Big\rfloor\mkern-2.0mu\bigg\rfloor.











4. Irrational parameters \alpha



The goal of this section is to discuss a generalization of [1] and present several conjectures inspired by it and from extensive experimentations in Wolfram Mathematica, see [2].




4.1. Generalized golden ratios



Given t\in\mathbb{N} such that





t\not\in\bigl\{(s-1)s:s\in\mathbb{N}\bigr\},


we let





\alpha(t):=\frac{1+\sqrt{1+4t}}{2};

(21)

note that \alpha(t)\in(\mathbb{R}\mkern-2.0mu\setminus\mkern-2.0mu\mathbb{Q})\cap(1,\infty). The number \alpha(1)=\varphi is the famous golden ratio; \alpha(504)=\tau is the irrational number in [1]. Our main goal is to prove the following.




Proposition 23.

For all t\in\mathbb{N} and \alpha(t) as in (21), we have

4.2. Conjectures and partial results



By Proposition 1 we have





\Range(f_{\alpha})\subseteq\bigl\llbracket 0,\lceil\alpha\rceil\bigr\rrbracket.

(27)

Based on Wolfram Mathematica experimentation, see [2], we state the following




Conjecture 25.

For all positive irrational numbers \alpha, we have





\bigl\llbracket 1,\lfloor\alpha\rfloor\bigr\rrbracket\subseteq\Range(f_{\alpha}).







If Conjecture 25 is true, then we have the equality in Proposition 24.




Assuming Conjecture 25 holds, together with the inclusion in (27), suggests the following quadruplicity of the range result.




Proposition 26.

If Conjecture 25 is true, then for all positive irrational parameters \alpha, one of the following equalities holds:





(A) 

\displaystyle\Range(f_{\alpha})=\bigl\llbracket 1,\lfloor\alpha\rfloor\bigr\rrbracket,



(B) 

\displaystyle\Range(f_{\alpha})=\bigl\llbracket 0,\lfloor\alpha\rfloor\bigr\rrbracket,



(C) 

\displaystyle\Range(f_{\alpha})=\bigl\llbracket 1,\lceil\alpha\rceil\bigr\rrbracket,



(D) 

\displaystyle\Range(f_{\alpha})=\bigl\llbracket 0,\lceil\alpha\rceil\bigr\rrbracket.









Proof.

The equality in (A) holds for all irrational parameters considered in Proposition 23. In particular, we have \Range(f_{\varphi})=\{1\}.




The equality in (D) holds for \pi and e. We used Wolfram Mathematica to calculate





f_{\pi}(1)=0,\ f_{\pi}(2)=f_{\pi}(3)=1,\ f_{\pi}(4)=f_{\pi}(5)=2,\ f_{\pi}(6)=3,\ f_{\pi}(7)=4,


and





f_{e}(7)=f_{e}(10)=0,\ f_{e}(2)=f_{e}(3)=f_{e}(5)=f_{e}(6)=f_{e}(9)=1,\\
f_{e}(1)=f_{e}(4)=f_{e}(8)=2,\ f_{e}(11)=3.





The equality in (C) holds for \alpha=\sqrt{2}. That is,





\Range\bigl(f_{\sqrt{2}}\bigr)=\{1,2\}.


First of all, it is easy to compute that f_{\sqrt{2}}(k)=k for k\in\{1,2\}. This shows that \{1,2\}\subseteq\Range\bigl(f_{\sqrt{2}}\bigr). Next, let n\in\mathbb{N} be fixed, and note that f_{\sqrt{2}}(n)=2n-\bigl\lfloor\sqrt{2}\lfloor\sqrt{2}n\rfloor\bigr\rfloor. Let us now write





\bigl\lfloor\sqrt{2}n\bigr\rfloor=m\Leftrightarrow m<\sqrt{2}n<m+1,m\in\mathbb{N}.


Thus





2n-2<2n-\sqrt{2}<\sqrt{2}m<2n,


which implies that \bigl\lfloor\sqrt{2}m\bigr\rfloor=2n-2 or \bigl\lfloor\sqrt{2}n\bigr\rfloor=2n-1. We infer that





f_{\sqrt{2}}(n)=2n-\bigl\lfloor\sqrt{2}m\bigr\rfloor\in\{1,2\},


which proves the converse inclusion \Range\bigl(f_{\sqrt{2}}\bigr)\subseteq\{1,2\}.




The equality in (B) holds for \alpha=2+\sqrt{2}. That is,





\Range\bigl(f_{2+\sqrt{2}}\bigr)=\{0,1,2,3\}.


The inclusion \{0,1,2,3\}\subseteq\Range\bigl(f_{2+\sqrt{2}}\bigr) follows from the equalities





f_{2+\sqrt{2}}(1)=1,\ f_{2+\sqrt{2}}(2)=3,\ f_{2+\sqrt{2}}(3)=0,\ f_{2+\sqrt{2}}(4)=2.





To prove the converse inclusion we use the following two properties of the floor function. For every positive real number x with
r=\bigl\lfloor 4\bigl(x-\lfloor x\rfloor\bigr)\bigr\rfloor\in\{0,1,2,3\} we have





\lfloor 4x\rfloor=4\lfloor x\rfloor+r.


For all positive integers n\in\mathbb{N} and all positive real numbers x we have





n-\lfloor x\rfloor=\lceil n-x\rceil.





Let n\in\mathbb{N} be arbitrary. A straightforward calculation shows that





f_{2+\sqrt{2}}(n)=2n+\bigl\lfloor 4\sqrt{2}n\bigr\rfloor-2\bigl\lfloor\sqrt{2}n\bigr\rfloor-\bigl\lfloor 2\sqrt{2}n+\sqrt{2}\lfloor\sqrt{2}n\rfloor\bigr\rfloor.


Set \lfloor\sqrt{2}n\rfloor=m. Then there exists r\in\{0,1,2,3\} such that \lfloor 4\sqrt{2}n\rfloor=4m+r. Hence,





\displaystyle f_{2+\sqrt{2}}(n)
\displaystyle=2n+4m+r-2m-\bigl\lfloor 2\sqrt{2}n+\sqrt{2}m\bigr\rfloor




\displaystyle=\Bigl\lceil 2n+2m+r-2\sqrt{2}n-\sqrt{2}m\Bigr\rceil




\displaystyle=\lceil w\rceil.





Next, we will obtain an estimate for the positive irrational number, we called it w, whose ceiling is the value f_{2+\sqrt{2}}(n). First, it follows from \lfloor 4\sqrt{2}n\rfloor=4m+r that





\displaystyle m+\frac{r}{4}
\displaystyle<\sqrt{2}n<m+\frac{r+1}{4},

and, equivalently,


\displaystyle\sqrt{2}n-\frac{r+1}{4}
\displaystyle<m<\sqrt{2}n-\frac{r}{4}.


Transforming linearly the preceding two inequalities and adding them we obtain





-\frac{r+1}{2}+\frac{r}{4}\sqrt{2}<2n+2m-2\sqrt{2}n-\sqrt{2}m<-\frac{r}{2}+\frac{r+1}{4}\sqrt{2}.


Adding r\in\{0,1,2,3\} to each term of the preceding inequalities and simplifying the left and righ expression, we get inequalities for w





\frac{(2+\sqrt{2})r-2}{4}<w<\frac{(2+\sqrt{2})r+\sqrt{2}}{4}.


Considering the extreme cases for r\in\{0,1,2,3\}, that is r=0 on the left-hand side and r=3 on the right-hand side, we get the following open interval membership for w:





w\in\left(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}+\sqrt{2}\right).


Consequently,





f_{2+\sqrt{2}}(n)=\lceil w\rceil\in\{0,1,2,3\}.


Since n\in\mathbb{N} was arbitrary, this proves \Range\bigl(f_{2+\sqrt{2}}\bigr)\subseteq\{0,1,2,3\}, and completes the proof.
∎






The conjectured statements in Proposition 26 account for the seemingly deep differences among positive irrational numbers. It would be interesting to explore these differences further. We state two more conjectures.




Conjecture 27.

For all irrational numbers \alpha\in(0,1) we have





\Range(f_{\alpha})=\{0,1\}.


That is, all irrational \alpha\in(0,1) satisfy the equality in item (D) in Proposition 26.






Remark 28.

By Corollary 2, the preceding conjecture is equivalent to 1\in\Range(f_{\alpha}), which in turn is equivalent to the following statement: For every x>1 there exists k\in\mathbb{N} such that





\bigl\lfloor x\lceil xk\rceil\bigr\rfloor\geq\bigl\lceil x^{2}k\bigr\rceil.







While Conjecture 27 was formulated based on experiments in Wolfram Mathematica, we now aim to provide some partial rigorous evidence in support of its validity.




Our first observation is that Conjecture 27 holds for all sub-unitary numbers \alpha in some right-neighborhood of a square-root rational. We first set some notation. Let k,n\in\mathbb{N} be such that k<n and assume that \sqrt{k/n} is irrational. In this case, we call \sqrt{k/n} a square-root rational, and denote it by s_{k,n}. With k,n as above, we also write





\epsilon_{k,n}=\frac{1}{n}\min\left\{\bigl\lceil\sqrt{kn}\bigr\rceil-\sqrt{kn},\sqrt{kn}-\bigl\lfloor\sqrt{kn}\bigr\rfloor\right\}>0.





Proposition 29.

Let k,n\in\mathbb{N} be such that k<n and s_{k,n} irrational. If \alpha\in[s_{k,n},s_{k,n}+\epsilon_{k,n}), then f_{\alpha}(n)=1.






Proof.

Let x,y\in\mathbb{R}. The following three statements follow from the definitions of the floor and ceiling function:





\lfloor x\rfloor<y\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\lfloor x\rfloor<\lceil y\rceil\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad x<\lceil y\rceil





Let k,n\in\mathbb{N} be such that k<n and assume that s_{k,n}=\sqrt{k/n} is irrational. Notice that s_{k,n} is irrational if and only if \sqrt{kn} is irrational. To evaluate f_{s_{k,n}}(n) we calculate





\left\lfloor\left(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\right)^{2}n\right\rfloor=k,


and estimate





\Biggl\lfloor\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\biggl\lfloor\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\,n\biggr\rfloor\Biggr\rfloor=\left\lfloor\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\bigl\lfloor\sqrt{kn}\bigr\rfloor\right\rfloor<\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\bigl\lfloor\sqrt{kn}\bigr\rfloor<\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\sqrt{kn}=k.


Therefore f_{s_{k,n}}(n)=1.




Let x be a nonnegative real number such that x<\epsilon_{k,n}. In particular,





x<\frac{1}{n}\Bigl(\bigl\lceil\sqrt{kn}\bigr\rceil-\sqrt{kn}\Bigr).


Then





\bigl\lfloor\sqrt{kn}\bigr\rfloor\leq\Biggl\lfloor\left(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}+x\right)n\Biggr\rfloor\leq\sqrt{kn}+xn<\bigl\lceil\sqrt{kn}\bigr\rceil,


and consequently,





\bigl\lfloor s_{k,n}+x\bigr\rfloor=\Biggl\lfloor\left(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}+x\right)n\Biggr\rfloor=\bigl\lfloor\sqrt{kn}\bigr\rfloor.


Since we also have





x<\frac{1}{n}\Bigl(\sqrt{kn}-\bigl\lfloor\sqrt{kn}\bigr\rfloor\Bigr)<\frac{1}{n}\,\frac{\sqrt{kn}}{\bigl\lfloor\sqrt{kn}\bigr\rfloor}\Bigl(\sqrt{kn}-\bigl\lfloor\sqrt{kn}\bigr\rfloor\Bigr),


we can estimate





\displaystyle\Bigl\lfloor(s_{k,n}+x)\bigl\lfloor(s_{k,n}+x)n\bigr\rfloor\Bigr\rfloor
\displaystyle=\Biggl\lfloor\left(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}+x\right)\Biggl\lfloor\left(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}+x\right)n\Biggr\rfloor\Biggr\rfloor




\displaystyle\leq\left(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}+x\right)\bigl\lfloor\sqrt{kn}\bigr\rfloor




\displaystyle<\frac{1}{n}\left(\sqrt{kn}+\frac{kn}{\bigl\lfloor\sqrt{kn}\bigr\rfloor}-\sqrt{kn}\right)\bigl\lfloor\sqrt{kn}\bigr\rfloor




\displaystyle=k.





Clearly,





\bigl\lfloor(s_{k,n}+x)^{2}n\bigr\rfloor=\Biggl\lfloor\left(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}+x\right)^{2}n\Biggr\rfloor=k

(28)

if and only if





x<\frac{\sqrt{k+1}-\sqrt{k}}{\sqrt{n}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\bigl(\sqrt{k+1}+\sqrt{k}\bigr)}.


Since





x<\epsilon_{k,n}<\frac{1}{2n}<\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\bigl(\sqrt{k+1}+\sqrt{k}\bigr)},


we deduce that (28) holds with so chosen x. Since under this estimate we also have





\Bigl\lfloor(s_{k,n}+x)\bigl\lfloor(s_{k,n}+x)n\bigr\rfloor\Bigr\rfloor<k,


we have proved that for
\alpha=s_{k,n}+x
we have
f_{\alpha}(n)=1.
∎

\bigcup\bigl\{A_{m}:m\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}\bigr\}=(0,1)\setminus\Bigl\{\frac{1}{b}:b\in\mathbb{N}\Bigr\}.

The only positive irrational number \alpha for which the function f_{\alpha} is constant that we encountered in our explorations is \alpha=\varphi, the golden ratio. Therefore we conjecture:




Conjecture 30.

For all positive irrational \alpha we have





\Range(f_{\alpha})=\{1\}\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad\alpha=\varphi.







Remark 31.

We disprove the following invariance conjecture: For all irrational \alpha>1 and all p\in\mathbb{N}, if \Range(f_{\alpha}) satisfies the equality in item (X), where X \in \{A, B, C, D\}, then \Range(f_{p\alpha}) would also satisfy the same equality.




To disprove this conjecture, consider \alpha=1+1/\sqrt{2} and calculate





f_{\alpha}(1)=1,\quad f_{\alpha}(2)=0,\quad f_{\alpha}(7)=2.


Since \lceil\alpha\rceil=2, by Proposition 1 we have \Range(f_{\alpha})=\{0,1,2\}. Hence, \alpha=1+1/\sqrt{2} satisfies the equality in item (D) in Proposition 26. Notice that 2\alpha=2+\sqrt{2}. We proved in the proof of Proposition 26 that 2\alpha=2+\sqrt{2} satisfies the equality in item (B) in Proposition 26.






We conclude with a couple of remarks regarding variations of the function f_{\alpha} discussed here.




Remark 32.

Throughout this article, we worked with the domain of the function f_{\alpha} being the set of positive integers. Naturally, one may ask what happens if we consider the domain as being the set of negative integers. For this, we note that, since \lceil x\rceil=-\lfloor-x\rfloor, given n\in\mathbb{N} we have





f_{\alpha}(-n)=-g_{\alpha}(n),


where now g_{\alpha}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Z},\,g_{\alpha}(n)=\bigl\lceil\alpha^{2}\,n\bigr\rceil-\bigl\lceil\alpha\lceil\alpha n\rceil\bigr\rceil. In other words, f_{\alpha}(-\mathbb{N})=-g_{\alpha}(\mathbb{N}); the analysis of the \Range(g_{\alpha}) will most likely be of a similar flavor to one we have seen for \Range(f_{\alpha}).






Remark 33.

Finally, one may ask about negative parameters \alpha. Note now that for \alpha<0, we can write f_{\alpha}=h_{-\alpha} where, for n\in\mathbb{N} and \beta>0, we have denoted





h_{\beta}(n)=\bigl\lfloor\beta^{2}\,n\bigr\rfloor-\bigl\lfloor\beta\lceil\beta n\rceil\bigr\rfloor.


This is yet another variation on the function f_{\alpha}. There are a few other such variants, obtained by appropriately interchanging the floor and ceiling operations in the definition of f_{\alpha}. The analysis of their ranges is left to the interested reader.
