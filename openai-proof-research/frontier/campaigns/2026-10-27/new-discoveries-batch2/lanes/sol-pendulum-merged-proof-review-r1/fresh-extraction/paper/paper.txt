Convexity of a complementary pendulum time map
Research manuscript
30 October 2026 UTC - candidate; independent root review pending

ABSTRACT
For every fixed 0 < phi < pi/2, the time map in equation (26) of da Costa, Grinfeld, Mottram, Pinto and Xayxanadasy is convex in its squared initial-ordinate parameter z on the literal closed interval [0,2]. This proves the convexity statement of their published Conjecture 1. A singular substitution expresses the map as a positive central-binomial series of paired moments. The two exceptional low moments must be combined before convexity is established. The higher moving-endpoint moments become integrals of convex positive-part powers. Integrability and continuity at both literal source endpoints complete the argument. A source-only Lean certificate proves exactly this convexity claim. Strictness, differentiability and quantitative curvature estimates are outside the certified statement.

1. SOURCE AND PRECISE STATEMENT
The source is F. P. da Costa, M. Grinfeld, N. J. Mottram, J. T. Pinto and K. Xayxanadasy, "Steady state solutions in a model of a cholesteric liquid crystal sample", Afrika Matematika 32 (2021), 645-672, DOI 10.1007/s13370-020-00851-9. It appeared online on 28 October 2020. We use the published equation (26) and Conjecture 1 on page 660. The earlier preprint has a different title and four authors; it is not the sole source for this statement.
The source parameter is z = y(-L)^2, rather than the unsquared ordinate. Write b(z) for the source's barred x-star(z), and fix a as follows:
@a = 1 - cos(2 phi),     b(z) = arccos(1-z)/2.
Thus 0 < a < 2, b(0)=0 and b(2)=pi/2. The actual source time map is
@T(z,phi) = int[0,phi] 1/sqrt(z-cos(2phi)+cos(2x)) dx
@         + int[0,b(z)] 1/sqrt(z-cos(2phi)+cos(2x)) dx.   (1)
Integrals with endpoint singularities mean finite improper integrals, equivalently Lebesgue integrals. Values assigned to the integrand at its isolated singular endpoints do not alter them.
THEOREM. For every 0 < phi < pi/2, T(.,phi) is convex on [0,2]. Explicitly, if z0,z1 belong to [0,2], u,v >= 0 and u+v=1, then
@T(u z0+v z1,phi) <= u T(z0,phi)+v T(z1,phi).             (2)
No restriction relative to z=2-a is imposed. Other branch expectations in the source are not included in this theorem. We prove interior convexity first, then establish integrability and continuity at the literal endpoints before passing (2) to the boundary.

2. A SINGULAR SUBSTITUTION AND FINITE MOMENTS
Fix 0 < z < 2 for now. On the first source interval the squared denominator in (1) is at least z; on the second it is at least a, since cos(2 b(z))=1-z. Thus the source integrands are continuous and their integrals finite in the interior parameter range.
Use t=1-cos(2x)=2 sin(x)^2. On 0 < x < pi/2 its derivative is 2 sqrt(t(2-t)). The two upper endpoints become a and z, respectively. Consequently
@T(z,phi) = (1/2) (int[0,a] + int[0,z])
@                    1/sqrt(t(2-t)(a+z-t)) dt.           (3)
This is a genuinely singular substitution: its derivative vanishes at x=0. To justify it, apply the ordinary change of variables on closed subintervals strictly inside the x-interval and then let the lower cutoff tend to zero. The nonnegative transformed integrand behaves at zero as a constant times t^(-1/2); its other factors are bounded away from zero on each of the two intervals, apart from this isolated endpoint. Monotone convergence supplies the limiting equality. The upper endpoints are strictly below 2, so no unmentioned t=2 singularity occurs in (3).
For every integer n >= 0 define the paired moment
@M_n(a,z) = (int[0,a] + int[0,z])
@                   t^(n-1/2)/sqrt(a+z-t) dt.            (4)
Near zero, t^(n-1/2) is integrable for every such n. On [0,a] the remaining denominator is at least sqrt(z), and on [0,z] it is at least sqrt(a). Hence each integral is finite. Formula (4) is understood almost everywhere; the formal certificate uses t^n/sqrt(t(a+z-t)), an equal integrand for t>0.
The paired structure matters. Individual moving-endpoint moments of the two lowest orders need not be convex. They will not be handled by the higher-order argument.

3. THE TWO EXCEPTIONAL IDENTITIES
Put s=a+z. The substitution t=s sin(theta)^2 yields, on each interval,
@t^(n-1/2) dt / sqrt(s-t) = 2 s^n sin(theta)^(2n) dtheta.
Its upper angles are alpha=arcsin(sqrt(a/s)) and beta=arcsin(sqrt(z/s)). Both lie in (0,pi/2), and alpha+beta=pi/2, since their sine and cosine values are complementary. The same cutoff justification applies at zero. The two intervals therefore combine to give
@M_0(a,z) = 2(alpha+beta) = pi.                           (5)
For n=1, the antiderivative of 2s sin(theta)^2 is s theta-(s/2) sin(2theta). Both sin(2alpha) and sin(2beta) equal 2 sqrt(az)/s. Thus
@M_1(a,z) = pi(a+z)/2 - 2 sqrt(az).                      (6)
The first moment in (5) is constant in z. Formula (6) is an affine function minus a positive multiple of sqrt(z), hence is convex for z>0 by the classical concavity of the square root. These combined identities supply exactly the two cases missing from the positive-part argument below.

4. HIGHER MOMENTS BY POINTWISE JENSEN INEQUALITIES
Let n>=2 and p=n-1/2>1. Split (4) as M_n=A_n+B_n, with A_n the integral up to a. For each 0<t<a, the function
@z |-> t^p / sqrt(a+z-t)
is convex on z>0: the inverse square root is convex on the positive half-line and the prefactor is nonnegative. One may obtain its Jensen inequality directly by composing the convex decreasing reciprocal with the concave positive square root, or by its elementary second derivative. The finite integrals in Section 2 allow integration of this inequality, proving convexity of A_n. No differentiation under a singular integral is needed.
For B_n, the change of variable y=z-t gives
@B_n(a,z) = int[0,infinity] (z-y)_+^p / sqrt(a+y) dy,     (7)
where r_+=max(r,0). The expression vanishes for y>=z, so (7) is simply an integral over a bounded interval. For each fixed y>=0, the function z |-> (z-y)_+^p is convex: positive part is convex, and the p-th power is increasing and convex on nonnegative reals for p>=1. The factor 1/sqrt(a+y) is positive and independent of z.
For a fully finite Jensen argument take z0,z1>0, u,v>=0, u+v=1, and w=u z0+v z1. Choose L=max(z0,z1,w). All three kernels in (7) vanish beyond L. On [0,L] they are continuous, since p>0 and a>0. Integrating their pointwise inequality gives
@B_n(a,w) <= u B_n(a,z0) + v B_n(a,z1).                  (8)
Thus B_n, A_n+B_n, and therefore M_n are convex. No claim that the bare weight 1/sqrt(a+y) is integrable on the whole half-line is used. Its multiplication by a compactly supported kernel is essential.
Together with (5)-(6) this proves that, for every integer n>=0 and every fixed a>0, z |-> M_n(a,z) is convex on (0,infinity).

5. THE POSITIVE CENTRAL-BINOMIAL SERIES
The classical binomial expansion gives, for 0<=t<2,
@(2-t)^(-1/2) = (1/sqrt(2)) sum[n>=0] c_n t^n,
@c_n = binom(2n,n)/8^n > 0.                              (9)
It follows either from the binomial theorem with exponent -1/2 and |t/2|<1, or from the recurrence (n+1)c_(n+1)=((2n+1)/4)c_n with c_0=1. Multiplication by the nonnegative factor [t(a+z-t)]^(-1/2), followed by integration on the two intervals, yields
@T(z,phi) = 1/(2 sqrt(2)) sum[n>=0] c_n M_n(a,z).         (10)
To justify exchanging sum and integral, use monotone convergence on each interval: the summands are measurable and nonnegative, the partial sums increase to the transformed kernel almost everywhere, and (3) proves that the limiting integral is finite. Thus the numerical series in (10) converges to the stated finite value. One can alternatively dominate each partial kernel sum by the full integrable kernel, as in the certificate. Neither method differentiates the series or requires uniform convergence over the parameter rectangle.

6. INTERIOR CONVEXITY AND THE LITERAL ENDPOINTS
Every finite partial sum in (10) is convex in z on (0,2), by Sections 3-4 and positivity of the coefficients. For any fixed Jensen triple z0,z1,w in this interval, all three series converge to finite real numbers. Passing the finite-sum Jensen inequalities to their limits proves (2) in the interior.
It remains to show that this convex map tends to the actual endpoint integrals in (1), rather than to unspecified boundary values. At z=0 the moving interval has length b(0)=0. On the fixed interval [0,phi], the limiting squared denominator is
@d(x) = cos(2x) - cos(2phi).
It is positive for x<phi, and its zero at phi is simple: d'(phi)=-2 sin(2phi)<0. By continuity of the derivative and the mean value theorem, d(x)>=c(phi-x) near phi for some c>0; away from phi it has a positive minimum. Hence 1/sqrt(d(x)) is integrable. For z>=0 the fixed-interval kernels are bounded almost everywhere by this integrable limiting kernel and converge to it as z decreases to zero. Dominated convergence proves convergence of the fixed-interval integral.
For the moving integral at z>0, its squared denominator is at least a throughout [0,b(z)]. Therefore
@0 <= int[0,b(z)] sourceKernel dx <= b(z)/sqrt(a) -> 0.
This proves finiteness at z=0 and right continuity there.
At z=2, the squared denominator in (1) on all of [0,pi/2] is at least a>0. More uniformly, if z>=2-a/2 then
@z-cos(2phi)+cos(2x) >= z+a-2 >= a/2.
The fixed-interval integrals converge by dominated convergence. For the moving integral, extend its integrand to the fixed interval [0,pi/2] by the indicator of [0,b(z)]. Since b(z) tends to pi/2, these functions converge almost everywhere to the z=2 kernel and are bounded by sqrt(2/a). This finite constant is integrable on the fixed interval. Dominated convergence proves left continuity at z=2 and finiteness of both endpoint integrals.
The source integrals are also continuous for interior z: on a compact neighborhood of an interior parameter their denominators have a uniform positive lower bound on the relevant fixed and moving intervals; interval indicators and dominated convergence give the assertion. Thus T(.,phi) is continuous on [0,2].
Finally, for arbitrary z0,z1 in [0,2], set z_i(e)=(1-e)z_i+e, where 0<e<1. These points, and their convex combination, lie in (0,2) and tend to the original points as e tends to zero. Apply interior Jensen to z_i(e) and pass to the limit using continuity. This gives (2) on the full closed interval, and proves the theorem.

CREDIT, SCOPE AND STATUS
The general ingredients are classical: trigonometric substitution, the central-binomial series, monotone and dominated convergence, square-root concavity and preservation of Jensen inequalities by positive sums, integrals and finite pointwise limits. The construction here is the choice of paired moments for this mixed stopping condition, with the two exceptional orders combined before using positive-part convexity. Older fixed-endpoint return-map or complete-period theorems are not claimed as new results. No global priority conclusion follows from this proof.
