                                                   Bang (1976) proved the inequality for matrix permanents per2 (A) ≥ 2−2n per(A⊗J2 ), where
                                               J2 is the 2×2 all-ones matrix and A is any n×n matrix with non-negative entries. We show that,
                                               if A is any n×n real-valued matrix with rank at most two (possibly having    negative entries), this
                                               inequality can be sharpened, replacing the constant 2−2n by 1/ 2n                /(2n)! > 2−2n . We
                                                                                                                             2

                                               then show that this sharpened inequality also implies new inequalities for finite free convolutions
                                               of polynomials: if p and q are monic real-rooted polynomials of degree n, then (p ⊞n q)(x)2 ≥

1.1   Permanent Inequalities
Denoting by Sn the symmetric group of permutations of [n] := {1, . . . , n}, the permanent of a
matrix A ∈ Rn×n is the polynomial in its entries

Let us write Jn for the n×n matrix all of whose entries equal 1 and In for the n×n identity matrix.
Van der Waerden’s well-known conjecture on permanents [vdW26], later proved independently by
Egorychev [Ego81] and Falikman [Fal81], states that the smallest possible permanent of a doubly
stochastic matrix is n!/nn , achieved by the matrix n1 Jn . Such extremal problems have immediate
consequences in combinatorics, and are also relevant to the study of the thermodynamics of the
bipartite monomer-dimer model of statistical physics. See [Fri12] for a survey.
    A preliminary result towards establishing van der Waerden’s conjecture was the looser lower
bound exp(−n), established independently by Bang and Friedland [Ban79, Fri79]. These proofs are
based on the following permanent inequality, credited to Bang’s earlier work [Ban76].
Theorem 1.1 (Bang’s permanent inequality). For any A ∈ Rn×n with Ai,j ≥ 0 for all i, j ∈ [n],
                                               1

case where A has low rank but is also allowed to have negative entries.
Theorem 1.2. Let A ∈ Rn×n be a matrix of rank at most 2. Then,

     One corollary of this relevant to the relationship between finite and classical free probability
is as follows. Write max root(p) for the largest root of a real-rooted polynomial p and µp for
the empirical distribution of roots of p. For compactly supported probability measures µ and ν,
write µ ⊞ ν for their classical free additive convolution [Voi86] and µ ⊠ ν for their classical free
multiplicative convolution, the latter provided one of them is supported on [0, ∞) [Voi87]. Finally,
for a compactly supported probability measure µ, write max supp(µ) for the largest value in the
support of µ.
     We first recall the following relationships between the finite and classical free convolutions. The
first has been stated in and follows directly from prior work.

Proposition 1.8 ([MSS22a], Theorem 4.2 of [Sri24]). Let p and q be monic real-rooted polynomials
of degree n. Then,

                                 max root(p ⊞n q) ≤ max supp(µp ⊞ µq ).

The second, to the best of our knowledge, has not been stated directly before, but follows easily
from existing results, essentially using a trick to reduce to Proposition 1.8.

Proposition 1.9. Let p and q be monic real-rooted polynomials of degree n, and suppose that at
least one of them has only non-negative roots. Then, p ⊠n q is real-rooted and

                                 max root(p ⊠n q) ≤ max supp(µp ⊠ µq ).

We give the proof in Section 4.

1.3    Proof Techniques and Role of Artificial Intelligence
We arrived at the proof of Theorem 1.2, our main contribution, by a combination of “classical”
computer assistance, direct analysis by the authors, and assistance from recent AI systems.
    The sum-of-squares proofs we give, which are sequences of (rather complicated) polynomial
equalities rather than inequalities, were found by solving appropriate semidefinite programs (SDPs)
and identifying conjectural exact forms of the numerical sum-of-squares proofs they provided, which
could be made highly symmetric with appropriate regularizations. We note that, once such exact
conjectural identities with rational coefficients are identified, they are far less expensive to test in
larger dimensions than it would be to continue solving larger SDPs. Performing such tests (in our
case for degrees through n = 49), we reached a high level of confidence in these conjectures.
    AI then provided several of the key insights in proving these identities; we present a streamlined
and reorganized version of a proof first discovered in an interactive session with GPT-5.2 Thinking
and Gemini 3.1 Pro in February 2026. This was achieved by our prompting these systems to use
tools from representation theory to explain the symmetry in the proposed sum-of-squares proof
and attempt a symbolic proof.
    In addition to many smaller differences, several parts of the proof were originally phrased (as re-
quested) in the language of representation theory, while we present an elementary and considerably
simplified proof here for the sake of clarity. We summarize the roles representation theory originally
played below, which may illuminate the roles of some of the objects involved. See Definition 2.1
below for the definition of the Wm spaces.

    • Corollary 2.5 is an elementary rephrasing of the Clebsch-Gordan decomposition of Wm  ⊗2 , while

      Corollary 2.7 is a consequence of the restricted Clebsch-Gordan decomposition of Sym2 (Wn ).
      After complexification, both decompositions are into irreducible representations of SU2 , each
      of which has multiplicity 1.

    • The transvectant τ2k shows up in Theorem 2.13 as it is the unique SU2 -intertwiner (up to
      rescaling, and again after complexification) from Sym2 (Wn ) to W2n−4k .

    • Proposition 3.7 was first established by realizing the right-hand side of the identity with
      an SU2 -intertwiner, and using the uniqueness of the transvectant as the intertwiner from
      Sym2 (Wn ) to W2n−4k .
