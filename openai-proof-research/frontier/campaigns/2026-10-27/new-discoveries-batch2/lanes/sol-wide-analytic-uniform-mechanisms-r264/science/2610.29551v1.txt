Remark 1.4. Theorems 1.2 and 1.3 are modern reformulations of Breiman’s original results [3,
Proposition 2 and Theorems 3–4]. In particular, Breiman established the converse implications
jointly in [3, Theorem 4], rather than as two separate characterizations.
    The distinction between the slowly varying and nonzero-index regularly varying cases is
also reflected in the limiting representation (24) and the maximum-weight characterization
established below.

    In the same paper [3], Breiman conjectured that the requirement of convergence for every
nondegenerate integrable mark X in the converse direction of his results could be weakened to
convergence for a single such mark. This became known as Breiman’s conjecture.
    A partial resolution of Breiman’s conjecture was obtained by Mason and Zinn [12] under the
stronger moment assumption

                                 E|X|p < ∞           for some p > 2.

Subsequently, Kevei and Mason [8] extended the converse implication to a broader class of
mark distributions, although the conjecture remained unresolved under the original integrability
assumption. More recently, a preprint by Lenzi [10] claims to establish the conjecture in full
generality under the assumption
                                          E|X| < ∞.
    The transition from full-sequence convergence to subsequential convergence is classical in
probability theory, particularly in the theory of partial attraction (see [6]), which also provides
a foundation for the theory of semistable laws. In the setting of randomly weighted means
Tn , certain aspects of subsequential convergence were previously investigated by Kevei and
Mason [9].
    The aim of the present paper is to develop subsequential analogues of Theorems 1.2 and 1.3.
For the slowly varying case, we introduce a new class of distributional tails, defined through an
integrated-tail condition ISSV (Definition 2.6), which strictly contains the classical slowly varying
tails. We establish a subsequential analogue of Darling’s maximum–sum theorem (Theorem 2.11),

varying theorem to the subsequential setting (Theorem 2.16) and, as a consequence, resolves the
slowly varying case of Breiman’s conjecture (Corollary 2.18).
