                                                  Abstract. We study the Keller problem for the Dirac operator D      / m − V β in Rd , and minimize
                                                  its lowest positive eigenvalue over all potentials V with prescribed Lp norm. We prove that
                                                  optimal potentials always exist and that the corresponding eigenspinors solve the nonlinear
                                                  Soler equation. This provides a variational proof of existence of solitary waves for this equation.
                                                  We prove that there are no zero modes for such operators in dimensions 1 and 2, and conjecture
                                                  that zero mode may exist in dimension 3. In dimension d = 2, we provide numerical evidence

                                                  showing that Faber–Krahn type results may fail for such Dirac operators. In dimension 1, all

   Solitary waves of the Soler model have been extensively studied, both analytically and nu-
merically [Cue+18; Ald+23]. In dimension d = 3, existence of solutions is proved in [CV86],
using a shooting method in the Wakano ansatz [Wak66]. This result was extended to more
general nonlinearities in [Mer88], and the existence of infinitely many solutions (excited states)
was proved in [Bal+88a; Bal+88b]. In [ES95], solutions are obtained by linking arguments.
Our method gives a proof of existence based on a direct variational argument: we minimize
an eigenvalue. We prove existence of solutions for all frequencies Λ ∈ (0, m), and without any
symmetry assumption. This approach is novel to the best of our knowledge.
   We provide some numerical simulations in Appendix B for d = 2. In view of the results, we
observe that the optimal potentials we find are not necessarily symmetric, so our solutions differ
from the ones found in the previous works, which assumed the Wakano ansatz (which implies
that hψ, βψiCN is radial). We conjecture that, should one optimize among radially symmetric
potentials only, we would probably recover the ground state solutions of the previous works.
   Our second main result concerns the critical mass α∗ (p, d). As we prove below, if α∗ (p, d) < ∞,
then α∗ (p, d) is attained: it is the smallest Lp norm of a potential creating a zero mode. The
following result is proved in Section 5.
Theorem 1.2 (Absence of zero modes in dimension d = 1 and d = 2). If d = 1 or d = 2, then
for all p > d, we have α∗ (p, d) = +∞. In other words, for all potential V ∈ Lp (Rd , R) we have
0∈/ σ(D/ m − V β).
   In dimension d = 3, we rather conjecture the following

+∞: there are potentials V ∈ Lp (R3 ) creating zero modes, 0 ∈ σ(D

   In other words, a radial potential cannot create zero modes. If Conjecture 1.3 holds, then any
optimal potential at α = α∗ (p, 3), which creates a zero mode, is non-radial. To our knowledge,
this would be the first example of a natural operator for which the optimal potential for the
first eigenvalue is not radial. Our numerical simulations (Appendix B) indicate that symmetry
breaking could also occur in dimension d = 2, although there are no zero modes in this case.
This is in stark contrast with the Faber–Krahn inequality in bounded domains for Dirac opera-
tors [Dur26; DMS26; AQ26], where the two-dimensional disk is the optimal shape for the lowest
eigenvalue, at least for some natural boundary conditions (except in dimension d = 1, where all
computations are explicit).

2.1.2. Symmetries. As we already mentioned, the main difference between the Dirac with a
Lorentz scalar potential V β and the Dirac with a potential V is the presence of extra symme-
tries. An anti-unitary operator C satisfying CHV = −HV C is called a charge-conjugation
symmetry. It is bosonic if C 2 = +1 and fermionic if C 2 = −1. Similarly, an anti-unitary
operator T satisfying T HV = HV T is called a time-reversal symmetry. It is bosonic if
T 2 = +1 and fermionic if T 2 = −1. In the presence of both T and C symmetries, we assume
that S = T C satisfies S 2 = +1. This is a unitary operator satisfying SHV = −HV S, and called
a S-symmetry.
