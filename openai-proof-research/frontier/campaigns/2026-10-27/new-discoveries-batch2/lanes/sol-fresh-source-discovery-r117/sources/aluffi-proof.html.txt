We prove the real-rootedness for the Poincaré polynomial P n ​ ( t ) = ∑ i = 0 n − 3 dim H 2 ​ i ​ ( ℳ ¯ 0 , n , ℚ ) ​ t i P_{n}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-3}\dim H^{2i}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n};\mathbb{Q})t^{i} of the Deligne–Mumford moduli space ℳ ¯ 0 , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n} of stable n n -pointed rational curves, proving a conjecture of Aluffi–Chen–Marcolli. The proof starts from the Keel–Manin–Getzler recurrence for these polynomials, but its main new idea is the introduction of a bivariate deformation F m ​ ( y , t ) F_{m}(y,t) of the Poincaré polynomial. This deformation is the heart of the argument: it reveals a hidden interlacing structure which is not visible from the one-variable recurrence alone. For fixed t < 0 t<0 , the zero set of F m F_{m} in the y y -direction is governed by a Sturm–Rolle argument on the interval 0 < y < 1 − t 0<y<1-t . The original polynomial P n ​ ( t ) P_{n}(t) is recovered by the slice y = 1 y=1 , and the ordered crossings of the moving roots through this slice give both real-rootedness and strict interlacing. As a consequence, the Betti numbers of ℳ ¯ 0 , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n} form an ultra-log-concave sequence. We further show the real-rootedness and ultra-log-concavity for the Poincaré polynomial of the Fulton-MacPherson space ℙ 1 ​ [ n ] \mathbb{P}^{1}[n] of n n ordered points in (degenerations of) the complex projective line ℙ 1 \mathbb{P}^{1} . The proof of the real-rootedness theorem for the Poincaré polynomials of ℳ ¯ 0 , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n} was obtained through an iterative AI-assisted workflow with Co-Mathematician, an agentic frontier-model system developed by Google DeepMind [ ZvGZ+26 ] . The human role was to pose the problem, evaluate the mathematical plausibility of successive attempts, identify and request repairs of gaps, compare the evolving argument with the literature, and assemble the final human-verifiable proof. Our additional human contribution was to observe that a similar, residual deformation strategy also applies to the Fulton–MacPherson spaces ℙ 1 ​ [ n ] \mathbb{P}^{1}[n] , which yields the corresponding real-rootedness theorem. 1. Introduction The Deligne–Mumford moduli spaces are central geometric objects of modern algebraic geometry. Deligne and Mumford introduced stable curves and their moduli space ℳ ¯ g \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g} in their 1969 paper [ DM69 ] . The pointed compactifications ℳ ¯ g , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n} , including the genus-zero spaces considered in the present paper, were constructed in the Grothendieck–Knudsen theory of stable pointed curves [ Knu83a , Knu83b ] . The space ℳ ¯ 0 , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n} is a smooth projective variety of dimension n − 3 n-3 compactifying configurations of n n ordered points on the complex projective line ℙ 1 \mathbb{P}^{1} modulo projective equivalence. The importance of these moduli spaces comes from their rich geometry which has led to several complementary discoveries. First, the boundary, forgetful, and clutching morphisms relating the spaces ℳ ¯ g , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n} organize them into the basic geometric framework for stable curve theory. In particular, the so-called clutching morphisms encode the fundamental operation of gluing pointed curves along marked points, an operation which gives the collection of moduli spaces its operadic, and in higher genus modular operadic, structure [ GK98 ] . In genus zero, Keel computed the Chow ring and intersection theory of ℳ ¯ 0 , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n} , proving in particular the recursive structure of its Chow groups and Betti numbers [ Kee92 ] . Kapranov then related ℳ ¯ 0 , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n} to explicit birational and combinatorial models, including iterated blow-ups, Veronese curves and Chow quotients of Grassmannians [ Kap93 ] . In a different direction, Witten’s conjectural description of intersection numbers on moduli spaces of curves through two-dimensional gravity, and Kontsevich’s proof via the matrix Airy function, made ℳ ¯ g , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n} a central bridge between algebraic geometry, integrable systems and mathematical physics [ Wit91 , Kon92 ] . Kontsevich–Manin’s formulation of Gromov–Witten classes and quantum cohomology then placed stable curves and stable maps at the foundation of modern enumerative geometry [ KM94 ] . More recently, the cohomology of ℳ ¯ 0 , n + 1 \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+1} has been understood as a Chow ring attached to the braid matroid and the minimal building set, connecting these moduli spaces to matroid Chow rings and combinatorial Hodge theory [ EFM+25 , AHK18 , FMSV24 ] . In this paper we study Betti numbers of ℳ ¯ 0 , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n} , encoded by the Poincaré polynomial (in half-degree convention) (1) P n ​ ( t ) = ∑ i = 0 n − 3 b n , i ​ t i , b n , i = dim H 2 ​ i ​ ( ℳ ¯ 0 , n , ℚ ) . P_{n}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-3}b_{n,i}t^{i},\qquad b_{n,i}=\dim H^{2i}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n};\mathbb{Q}). The main result is a fundamental new structural property of the topology of these moduli spaces: for every n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 , the Poincaré polynomial P n ​ ( t ) P_{n}(t) has only real roots. The proof was found with the assistance of Co-Mathematician, a frontier agentic LLM-based system for mathematical research introduced in [ ZvGZ+26 ] . Theorem 1.1 . For every n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 , the polynomial P n ​ ( t ) P_{n}(t) has only real roots. More precisely, for n ≥ 4 n\geq 4 it has n − 3 n-3 simple roots, all lying in ( − ∞ , 0 ) (-\infty,0) . We also obtain strict interlacing. Theorem 1.2 (Strict interlacing) . Let n ≥ 4 n\geq 4 , and let τ 1 < τ 2 < ⋯ < τ n − 3 < 0 \tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\cdots<\tau_{n-3}<0 be the roots of P n ​ ( t ) P_{n}(t) . If σ 1 < σ 2 < ⋯ < σ n − 2 < 0 \sigma_{1}<\sigma_{2}<\cdots<\sigma_{n-2}<0 are the roots of P n + 1 ​ ( t ) P_{n+1}(t) , then σ 1 < τ 1 < σ 2 < τ 2 < ⋯ < σ n − 3 < τ n − 3 < σ n − 2 < 0 . \sigma_{1}<\tau_{1}<\sigma_{2}<\tau_{2}<\cdots<\sigma_{n-3}<\tau_{n-3}<\sigma_{n-2}<0. Note that odd cohomology vanishes, and P n P_{n} is palindromic by Poincaré duality. Hard Lefschetz gives unimodality of the coefficients. Since the coefficients are nonnegative, the roots are necessarily negative; Newton’s inequalities then give log-concavity, indeed ultra-log-concavity, of the Betti number sequence. Corollary 1.3 (Log-concavity of the Betti numbers) . For every n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 , the Betti numbers of M ¯ 0 , n \overline{M}_{0,n} form an ultra-log-concave sequence: ( b n , i ( n − 3 i ) ) 2 ≥ b n , i − 1 ( n − 3 i − 1 ) ​ b n , i + 1 ( n − 3 i + 1 ) for all ​ 1 ≤ i ≤ n − 4 . \left(\frac{b_{n,i}}{\binom{n-3}{i}}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{b_{n,i-1}}{\binom{n-3}{i-1}}\frac{b_{n,i+1}}{\binom{n-3}{i+1}}\qquad\text{for all }1\leq i\leq n-4. A closely related space to ℳ ¯ 0 , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n} is the Fulton–MacPherson space ℙ 1 ​ [ n ] \mathbb{P}^{1}[n] which compactifies the configuration space of n n ordered distinct points in ℙ 1 \mathbb{P}^{1} without projective equivalence. It is a smooth projective variety of dimension n n with vanishing odd cohomology and its even Betti sequence is unimodal and palindromic. By the same technique as in the proof of Theorem 1.1 , we further prove the real-rootedness (Theorem 5.1 ) and the ultra-log-concavity (Corollary 5.2 ) for the Poincaré polynomial of ℙ 1 ​ [ n ] \mathbb{P}^{1}[n] for n ≥ 1 n\geq 1 . We next recall the precise origin of the problem. Aluffi–Chen–Marcolli formulated the real-rootedness conjecture for the polynomial representing the Grothendieck class of ℳ ¯ 0 , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n} ; in the Poincaré specialization this is exactly the assertion that P n ​ ( t ) P_{n}(t) has only real zeros [ ACM25 , Conjecture 1] . They proved asymptotic ultra-log-concavity and γ \gamma -positivity as evidence for the conjecture. Nascimento improved the asymptotic ultra-log-concavity bounds [ Nas25 ] . Aluffi–Marcolli–Nascimento gave explicit formulas for the Grothendieck class and Betti numbers in terms of Stirling and Bernoulli numbers, and verified related log-concavity phenomena in large finite ranges [ AMN25 ] . Choi–Kiem–Lee first gave closed formulas for the 𝔖 n \mathfrak{S}_{n} -characters on cohomology, then developed recursive algorithms for these representations and proposed strong equivariant log-concavity refinements of the Betti number log-concavity problem; their later work introduced characteristic polynomials of representations and further asymptotic log-concavity results [ CKL23 , CKL24 , CKL25 ] . Choi–Kiem later proved that the distribution of the Betti numbers of ℳ ¯ 0 , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n} is asymptotically Gaussian and obtained a stronger asymptotic ultra-log-concavity [ CK26 ] . Geometrically, for fixed t < 0 t<0 the positive roots of F m ​ ( y , t ) F_{m}(y,t) lie in the interval 0 < y < 1 − t 0<y<1-t and are simple. As t t moves from 0 − 0^{-} to − ∞ -\infty , these roots move in ordered, noncolliding branches. Near t = 0 − t=0^{-} all positive branches lie below the horizontal line y = 1 y=1 , while for t ≪ 0 t\ll 0 they all lie above it. Hence each branch crosses y = 1 y=1 . The degree of P m + 1 P_{m+1} leaves room for exactly one crossing per branch, and those crossings are precisely the roots of P m + 1 P_{m+1} . The ordering of the branches then gives the sign alternation needed for interlacing. No monotonicity of the branches is assumed; the degree count is what rules out multiple crossings. The deformation F m ​ ( y , t ) F_{m}(y,t) is not a routine technical device: it is the key structural ingredient of the proof, converting the original recursion into a one-parameter family in which the move of real zeros can be controlled. Its discovery is a clear indication that the AI-assisted process reached beyond local symbolic manipulation and identified a genuinely new idea behind the theorem. Overview of the Co-Math proof development The proof above emerged from an iterative Co-Math process rather than from a single polished answer. The progression is instructive because it shows both the power and the risks of agentic mathematical collaboration. The first attempts already found the main architecture: reduce the problem to interlacing, introduce a bivariate deformation, study its roots by a Rolle-theoretic argument, and interpret the roots of P n P_{n} as crossings of positive y y -root branches through the line y = 1 y=1 . At that stage, however, several crucial details were still heuristic. The derivation of the F m F_{m} -recurrence was not fully justified, the branch-crossing argument assumed monotonicity that had not been proved, and the endpoint behavior as t → 0 − t\to 0^{-} and t → − ∞ t\to-\infty was only partially controlled. The next round strengthened the algebra. The generating function ( 7 ), the PDE ( 10 ), and the slice identity F m ​ ( 1 , t ) = P m + 1 ​ ( t ) F_{m}(1,t)=P_{m+1}(t) were made explicit. The fixed- t t root structure was converted into the weighted derivative identity ( 18 ), so Rolle’s theorem could be applied without hidden assumptions. Computation was useful in this stage, but only as a guardrail: it checked indexing, small cases, and the identities involving F m ​ ( 1 , t ) F_{m}(1,t) and ∂ y F m ​ ( 1 , t ) \partial_{y}F_{m}(1,t) . It did not prove the analytic branch argument. The final step replaced the remaining experimental and computation-based claims by proof. A possible topological zigzag problem for root branches was eliminated by a degree count: each branch must cross y = 1 y=1 at least once, while P n ​ ( t ) = F n − 1 ​ ( 1 , t ) P_{n}(t)=F_{n-1}(1,t) has exactly as many possible zeros as there are branches. The behavior at t → − ∞ t\to-\infty was justified by the scaled limit H m , t → G m H_{m,t}\to G_{m} and the independent Sturm analysis of G m G_{m} on ( − 1 , 0 ) (-1,0) . This turned the geometric picture into a rigorous crossing theorem and then into strict interlacing. Our workflow aligns closely with the design principles described in the AI co-mathematician paper of Zheng et al. [ ZvGZ+26 ] : the system supported iterative refinement, numerical exploration, proof development, and production of native mathematical artifacts. It also illustrates the paper’s cautions. Early drafts were written in confident LaTeX even when some lemmas were not yet proved; the bibliography changed across attempts, and key provenance information, such as the Keel–Manin–Getzler origin of the recurrence and the relevant work of Choi–Kiem–Lee, was not preserved consistently. A stronger agentic workflow should maintain a persistent gap registry, a computation log indicating exactly what was checked and the exact ranges verified, and a reference manifest distinguishing sources used in the proof from background literature. In this problem, the most productive and interesting behavior was not one-shot theorem proving but the ability to keep a promising architecture alive, accept finite computation as provisional evidence, and gradually replace it with exact lemmas. The point is not that computation was used as proof, but that it was used as a temporary scaffold for proof discovery. For instance, the identities F m ​ ( 1 , t ) = P m + 1 ​ ( t ) , ∂ y F m ​ ( 1 , t ) = P m + 1 ​ ( t ) + S m + 2 ​ ( t ) , F_{m}(1,t)=P_{m+1}(t),\qquad\partial_{y}F_{m}(1,t)=P_{m+1}(t)+S_{m+2}(t), together with the observed real-rootedness and branch-crossing behavior of F m ​ ( y , t ) F_{m}(y,t) , were first supported by finite symbolic and numerical checks. Those checks were strong enough for the system to preserve the bivariate deformation as the central proof architecture, even while several steps remained unproved. Later iterations then discharged the gaps: the identities were derived from the generating-function PDE, the root structure was proved by a Sturm–Rolle argument, and the endpoint behavior of the moving branches was proved. This illustrates a genuine strength of the Co-Mathematician workflow: it can reason provisionally from well-documented computational evidence without confusing that evidence with the final proof.
