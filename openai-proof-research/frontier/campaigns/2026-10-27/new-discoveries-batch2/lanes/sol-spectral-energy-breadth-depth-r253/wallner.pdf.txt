                                           On the critical exponents of generalized ballot sequences
                                                 in three dimensions and large tandem walks

                                                                                     Michael Wallner*

                                                                                       May 27, 2021




arXiv:2105.12155v1 [math.CO] 25 May 2021
                                                                                           Abstract
                                               We answer some questions on the asymptotics of ballot walks raised in [Personal Journal
                                               Shalosh B Ekhad and Doron Zeilberger, Apr 5, 2021] and prove that these models are not D-
                                               finite. This short note demonstrates how the powerful tools developed in the last decades on
                                               lattice paths in convex cones help us to answer some challenging problems that were out of
                                               reach for a long time. On the way we generalize tandem walks to the family of large tandem
                                               walks whose steps are of arbitrary length and map them bijectively to a generalization of
                                               ballot walks in three dimensions.

                                               Keywords: Bijection, Dyck Paths, Walks in the Quarter Plane, D-finite, Non-D-finite.



                                           1    Introduction

                                           In a recent paper [10] Ekhad and Zeilberger numerically investigated several problems on
                                           the asymptotic enumeration of lattice paths. This led to many conjectures, among which we
                                           will answer some in this note. They are all related to generalizations of the famous ballot
                                           problem which we present now.
                                                The ballot problem is probably one of the oldest problems on lattice path enumeration.
                                           There exist various formulations, yet all of them have the following question in common:
                                           Consider an election between two candidates, say A and B, who both receive n votes. What
                                           is the probability that A is never behind B during the count? The result is surprisingly simple:

    A proof strategy consists in reformulating this question into one about lattice paths in the
integer lattice Z2 . We model a vote for A by a unit step in x-direction (1, 0) and a vote for B
by a unit step in y-direction (0, 1). The paths start at the origin (0, 0) and end at (n, n). We
are then interested in the total number of paths that are constraint to the region
                                      n                       o
                                        (x, y) ∈ Z2 : x ≥ y .

These paths are also famously known as Dyck paths, being obviously enumerated by the
Catalan numbers [17]. For more on the ballot problem and the history of lattice paths we
refer to the beautiful surveys by Humpreys [15] and Krattenthaler [16].
    As an answer to such questions, one strives for the most detailed possible one. Most
desirable is therefore a closed-form expression such as (1). Often, however, this does not
exist or is very complicated but we are still sometimes able to derive its asymptotics. Let
an be the number of paths of length n. Then for “well-behaved” sequences we expect for
n → ∞ the structure

                                            an ∼ C µn nα ,                                          (2)

where C is a constant, µ the exponential growth, and α the critical exponent. Note that
general sequences may include additional terms such as n!, nn , or more complicated terms
            σ
such as µn known as stretched exponentials [11]. In the context of this note, the structure (2)
will suffice.
     Now the ballot problem can be generalized in many ways. For our purposes, let a, b ∈ Z>0
be two positive integers that are relatively prime. Then, we consider walks with positive unit
steps as above that go from the origin (0, 0) to the point (an, bn) and are constraint to the
region bx − ay ≥ 0. The special case a = b = 1 gives the ballot sequence enumerated by
Catalan numbers, while a = 1 and general b leads to Fuss–Catalan numbers. The asymp-
totics of the general problem is answered in [3] and can be tricky due to possible cancellations
(see for example the conjecture by Knuth1 for a = 2 and b = 5 answered therein). What is
remarkable in these 2-dimensional models is the universality of the critical exponent α: in all
cases it is equal to −3/2.
     A natural next step is to ask the same question in higher dimensions. Here the critical
exponent is not universal as we will see below and many open questions remain. From now
on we focus on generalized ballot sequences in 3 dimensions which count the following:

origin (0, 0, 0) to (an, bn, cn) using unit steps (1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0), (0, 0, 1) that are constraint to

The theory to derive the critical exponent comes from the enumeration of lattice paths in the
quarter plane, i.e. the first quadrant [8]. As a first step we will map the 3-dimensional walks
to the following class of 2-dimensional walks; see Figure 1.

Definition 2.1 (Large tandem walks). Let A, B, C ∈ Z>0 be positive integers. Large tandem
walks are walks starting from the origin (0, 0) using the steps (A, 0), (−B, B), (0, −C) that
are constraint to the quarter plane

   The 2-dimensional walks with the step set {(1, 0), (−1, 1), (0, −1)} that are constraint to
the quarter plane are called tandem walks and are connected to a wide range of research
problems [5, 8, 9]. The name stems from queueing theory, as they indeed represent the
behavior of two M/M/1 queues in “tandem”; see [12, Section 4.7].

     The general step set {(A, 0), (−B, B), (0, −C)} consists for (a, b, c) 6= (1, 1, 1) of large
steps. Note that the steps {−1, 0, 1}2 are called small steps, all others are called large steps.
Models with large steps are substantially harder to count and the enumeration problem is
still widely open. A lot of progress has recently been made by Bostan, Bousquet-Mélou, and
Melczer [6] in which they develop a general theory for walks with large steps in the quarter
plane. We will use this theory to derive closed forms for α. It is quite remarkable that all that
can be done fully automatic, as explained in [6, Section 8.2.1].
     The following result gives an explicit bijection between generalized 3-ballot walks and
large tandem excursions, where excursions are walks from the origin to the origin.

Lemma 2.2. The map ϕ : Z3 → Z2 defined by

                              ϕ : (x, y, z) 7→ (Ax − By, By − Cz)

is a bijection between generalized 3-ballot walks (i.e. walks from (0, 0, 0) to (an, bn, cn) con-
straint to Ax ≥ By ≥ Cz ≥ 0) to large tandem excursions of length 3n (i.e. walks from (0, 0)
to (0, 0) constraint to the quarter plane with the step set {(A, 0), (−B, B), (0, −C)}).

Proof. Instead of the absolute coordinates, the map keeps track of the weighted relative
differences of the coordinates. Then the space constraint Ax ≥ By ≥ Cz ≥ 0 implies that
each 2D-coordinate is nonnegative and therefore lies in the quarter plane. Plugging the
unit vectors into ϕ gives the new steps, while the starting point (0, 0, 0) and the end point
(an, bn, cn) both give (0, 0). As there is a one-to-one correspondence between the steps this
gives the claimed bijection.

3    Generalized 3-ballot walks and large tandem walks

As a first observation we note that the number of excursions in the models (A, B, C) and
(C, B, A) are the same. This follows from the following simple lemma.
Lemma 3.1. There exists a bijection between large tandem excursions of the models (A, B, C)
and (C, B, A).
Proof. Let a large tandem excursion of the model (A, B, C) be given. It is built from the step
set {(A, 0), (−B, B), (0, −C)}. First, we reverse the time by reading the steps backwards.
This gives the new step set {(−A, 0), (B, −B), (0, C)}. Second, we rotate along y = x, and
get the step set {(C, 0), (−B, B), (0, −A)} corresponding to the model (C, B, A). Note that
both operations transform excursions into excursions and remain in the quarter plane. For
more details and further transformations see [2].

    The question remains if the models associated with rational c2 are D-finite or not. Ob-
serve that they cannot be algebraic as α is a negative integer [13]. Furthermore, note that
the non-D-finiteness results from [6] follow from an infinite orbit of the models. The authors
provide the command evalPQ to possibly find a lower bound on the orbit size as well as the
command PQList to compute the orbit elements. Now, using these commands we find that
the classical tandem model (1, 1, 1) (whose generating function is of course D-finite) has a fi-
nite orbit of size 6, while for all other models from Table 2 we could not find any lower bounds
on the orbit size as the evalPQ aborted after a certain threshold, which we set to 1000 and
which is related to the orbit size. Thus, the orbits could be infinite and therefore not D-finite,
or just very large. Note that a finite orbit does not prove D-finiteness, however, models with
small steps are D-finite if and only if the orbit is finite.
    As a next step, it would be interesting to investigate the simplest model in each case.
However, we have not been able to do this, and it seems that we have reached the state-of-
the-art of our current understanding of such lattice paths. There remains the open problem
for the coming years to develop general methods for the extremely useful lattice paths (with
large steps) in the quarter plane.
     Let us end with some simple observations regarding the models with rational c2 . First,
it is easy to see that fixing two of the three parameters A, B, C completely determines the
third one. Second, for c2 = 1/4 it holds that A < B < C or C < B < A, while for the
other two cases c2 = 1/2 and c2 = 3/4 we have A, C < B. Third, these families are infinite.
