                                                 a0 ∈ Fq , there is a circular permutation (a1 , . . . , aq−1 ) of non-zero elements of Fq such
                                                 that a0 + ai ai+1 is primitive for 1 ≤ i ≤ q − 1, where aq = a1 . In this paper, we confirm

                                                                                    1. Introduction
                                             Let Fq be a finite field of order q = pf , with p prime and f a positive integer. We
                                          denote the multiplicative group of Fq by F∗q . The generators of F∗q are called primitive
                                          elements. Permutations satisfying arithmetic restrictions and primitive elements in finite
                                          fields lead to many interesting problems. In 1982, Filz [4] conjectured that for every even
                                          n, there is a permutation τ of {1, . . . , n} such that
                                                                     τ (1) + τ (2), τ (2) + τ (3), . . . , τ (n) + τ (1)
                                          are all prime. This conjecture appears in Guy’s book [5]. For recent progress on this
                                          problem, one may refer to [2, 6, 7].
                                             Primitive elements and permutations lead to several questions. In 2013, Sun [9] pro-
                                          posed a series of conjectures on permutations of finite fields. For example, Sun and Hou
                                          [9, Conjecture 3.5(i)] conjectured that there is a circular permutation of the elements of
                                          Fq for q > 7 such that every adjacent sum is primitive. This conjecture has recently been
                                          confirmed by the second author for q > 18 888 871 [7].
                                             In this paper, we study another conjecture of Sun [9, Conjecture 3.8].
                                          Conjecture 1.1. Let Fq be a finite field with q > 7. For any given element a0 ∈ Fq ,
                                          there exists a circular permutation (a1 , . . . , aq−1 ) of all elements of F∗q such that
                                                          a0 + a 1 a2 ,   a0 + a2 a3 ,    ...,       a0 + aq−2 aq−1 ,   a0 + aq−1 a1
                                          are primitive elements of Fq .
                                            For a graph G, its vertex set and edge set are denoted by V (G) and E(G), respectively.
                                          Given a0 ∈ Fq , we define a graph Gq,a0 with V (Gq,a0 ) = F∗q by
                                                                xy ∈ E(Gq,a0 ) ⇐⇒ x ̸= y and a0 + xy is primitive.
                                          The desired circular permutation is exactly a Hamilton cycle in Gq,a0 .
                                          Theorem 1.2. For any prime power q > 18 888 871 and any a0 ∈ Fq , Gq,a0 is Hamilton-
                                          ian. When a0 = 0, the conclusion holds for q > 4.

                                           4. The case of a0 = 0
     Our construction for odd prime powers q is inspired by Sinha, Garg and Singh [8].
Proof of Theorem 1.2 when a0 = 0. Let g be a primitive element of Fq and let n = q − 1.
Any ai ∈ F∗q can be represented uniquely by its exponent with respect to g, that is,
                           ai = g x i    for some unique xi ∈ {0, 1, . . . , n − 1}.
The adjacent product ai ai+1 = g xi +xi+1 is a primitive element of Fq if and only if
                                             gcd(xi + xi+1 , n) = 1.
We construct the Hamilton cycle by considering the parity of n separately.
 If n is even, consider the sequence
                (x1 , x2 , . . . , xn , x1 ) = (0, n − 1, 2, n − 3, 4, n − 5, . . . , n − 2, 1, 0).
It yields a Hamilton cycle in Gq,0 .
   If n is odd, write n = 2h + 1, where h ≥ 2. When h = 2, we have q = 6, which is not
a prime power. For h = 3, we have
                                 (x1 , x2 , . . . , x7 , x1 ) = (6, 0, 5, 1, 3, 2, 4, 6).
and every adjacent sum is coprime to 7. For h ≥ 4, consider the sequence
    (x1 , x2 , . . . , xn , x1 ) = (n − 1, 0, n − 2, 1, h, h − 1, h + 1, h − 2, h + 2, . . . , 2, n − 3, n − 1).
In this sequence, every adjacent sum is congruent to n − 1, n − 2, n − 4 or h + 1 modulo
n. Since
                gcd(n − 1, n) = gcd(n − 2, n) = gcd(n − 4, n) = gcd(h + 1, n) = 1,
this leads to a Hamilton cycle in Gq,0 . This completes the proof.                                            □
