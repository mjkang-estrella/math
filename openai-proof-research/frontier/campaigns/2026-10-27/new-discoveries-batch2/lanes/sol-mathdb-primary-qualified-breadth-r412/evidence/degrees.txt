    <div class="s-prose js-post-body" itemprop="text">
                
<p>In the past, first-order logic and its completeness and whether arithmetic is complete was a major unsolved issues in logic . All of these problems were solved by  Godel. Later on, independence of main controversial axioms were established by forcing method.</p>
<p>I wonder if there still exist some &quot;natural&quot; questions in mathematical logic that are still unsolved? Or is it the case that most of the major questions have been already answered?</p>
<p>I'd love to know about some important, but still unsolved problems that puzzle logicians and why would the young logician\mathematician care about those? (that is, Whey they are important?)</p>
<p>I'm not an expert in logic (nor in any other mathematical field (I'm an undergraduate), but I'm interested in logic so I would like to know about the current problems that logicians face and what are the trends of research in the discipline nowdays and what type of problems people are trying to solve.</p>
<p>I know that logic is a vast term which includes many sub-disciplines: model theory, proof theory, set theory, recursion theory,  higher-order logics , non-classical logics, modal logics, algebraic logic and many others. So feel free to tell us about problems form whichever topic you would love to.</p>

    <div class="s-prose js-post-body" itemprop="text">
<p>Yes, there are several. Here’s a few which I personally care about (described in varying amounts of precision). <strong>This is not meant to be an exhaustive list, and reflects my own biases and interests.</strong></p>
<p><em>I am focusing here on questions which have been open for a long amount of time, rather than questions which have only recently been raised, in the hopes that these are more easily understood.</em></p>
<hr />
<p><strong>MODEL THEORY</strong></p>
<p>The compactness and Löwenheim–Skolem theorems let us completely classify those sets of cardinalities of models of a first-order theory; that is, sets of the form <span class="math-container">$$\{\kappa: \exists \mathcal{M}(\vert\mathcal{M}\vert=\kappa, \mathcal{M}\models T)\}.$$</span> A natural next question is to <em>count</em> the number of models of a theory of a given cardinality. For instance, Morley’s Theorem shows that if <span class="math-container">$T$</span> is a countable first-order theory which has a unique model in <em>some</em> uncountable cardinality, then <span class="math-container">$T$</span> has a unique model of <em>every</em> uncountable cardinality (this is all up to isomorphism, of course).</p>
<p>Surprisingly, the countable models are <strong>much harder</strong> to count! Vaught showed that if <span class="math-container">$T$</span> is a (countable complete) first-order theory, then — up to isomorphism — <span class="math-container">$T$</span> has either <span class="math-container">$\aleph_0$</span>, <span class="math-container">$\aleph_1$</span>, or <span class="math-container">$2^{\aleph_0}$</span>-many countable models. <strong>Vaught’s Conjecture</strong> states that we can get rid of the weird middle case: it’s either <span class="math-container">$\aleph_0$</span> or <span class="math-container">$2^{\aleph_0}$</span>. In case the continuum hypothesis holds, VC is vacuously true; but in the absence of CH, very little is known. VC is known for certain special kinds of theories (see e.g. <a href="https://mathoverflow.net/questions/157903/vaughts-conjecture-for-partial-orders">Vaught&#39;s conjecture for partial orders</a> and <a href="http://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2FBF02760651" rel="nofollow noreferrer">http://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2FBF02760651</a>) and a counterexample to VC is known to have some odd properties, including odd <em>computability-theoretic</em> properties (<a href="https://math.berkeley.edu/%7Eantonio/papers/VaughtEquiv.pdf" rel="nofollow noreferrer">https://math.berkeley.edu/~antonio/papers/VaughtEquiv.pdf</a>), but the conjecture is wide open.</p>
<p><em>NOTE: VC can be rephrased as a “countable/perfect” dichotomy, in which case it is</em> not <em>trivially true if CH holds and is in fact forcing invariant; see e.g. <a href="https://mathoverflow.net/questions/175987/how-do-we-know-if-vaughts-conjecture-is-absolute.">How do we know if Vaught&#39;s Conjecture is Absolute?</a></em></p>
<hr />
<p><strong>PROOF THEORY</strong></p>
<p>If <span class="math-container">$T$</span> is a strong enough reasonable theory, we can define the <em>proof-theoretic ordinal</em> of <span class="math-container">$T$</span>; roughly, how much induction is necessary to prove that <span class="math-container">$T$</span> is consistent. For instance, the proof-theoretic ordinal of <span class="math-container">$\mathrm{PA}$</span> is <span class="math-container">$$\epsilon_0=\omega^{\omega^{\scriptstyle\omega^{\scriptstyle\cdot^{\scriptstyle.{\cdot}}}}}.$$</span> Proof-theoretic ordinals have been calculated for a variety of systems reaching up to (something around) <span class="math-container">$\Pi^1_2$</span>-<span class="math-container">$\mathrm{CA}_0$</span>, a reasonably strong fragment of second-order arithmetic which is in turn a very very small part of ZFC. It seems unfair, based on this, to list “<strong>finding the proof-theoretic ordinal of ZFC</strong>” as one of these problems, based on how far away it is; but “find ordinals for stronger theories” is an important program.</p>
<p><em>See e.g. <a href="https://mathoverflow.net/questions/144041/proof-theoretic-ordinal-of-zfc-or-consistent-zfc-extensions.">Proof-Theoretic Ordinal of ZFC or Consistent ZFC Extensions?</a></em></p>
<hr />
<p><strong>COMPUTABILITY THEORY</strong></p>
<p>I believe the oldest open problem in computability theory is the <strong>automorphism problem</strong>. In Turing’s 1936 paper, he introduced — in addition to the usual Turing machine — the <em>oracle Turing machine</em> (or <em>o-machine</em>). This is a Turing machine which is equipped with “extra information” in the form of a (fixed arbitrary) infinite binary string. Oracle machines allow us to <em>compare</em> the non-computability of sets of natural numbers: we write <span class="math-container">$A\le_T B$</span> if an oracle machine equipped with <span class="math-container">$B$</span> can compute <span class="math-container">$A$</span>. This yields a partial ordering <span class="math-container">$\mathcal{D}$</span>, the <strong>Turing degrees</strong>. Initially the Turing degrees were thought to be structurally simple; for instance, it was conjectured (I believe by Shoenfield) that the partial order is “very homogeneous” (there were many different conjectures).</p>
<p>As it turned out, however, the exact opposite happens: the Turing degrees have surprisingly rich structure. See e.g. <a href="http://www.jstor.org/stable/2270693?seq=1#page_scan_tab_contents" rel="nofollow noreferrer">http://www.jstor.org/stable/2270693?seq=1#page_scan_tab_contents</a> for an early example of this by Feiner, and <a href="http://www.pnas.org/content/76/9/4218.full.pdf" rel="nofollow noreferrer">http://www.pnas.org/content/76/9/4218.full.pdf</a> for a later one by Shore. Indeed, currently the general belief is that <span class="math-container">$\mathcal{D}$</span> is <em>rigid</em>, and it has been shown (see e.g. <a href="https://math.berkeley.edu/%7Eslaman/papers/IMS_slaman.pdf" rel="nofollow noreferrer">https://math.berkeley.edu/~slaman/papers/IMS_slaman.pdf</a>, Theorem 4.30) that <span class="math-container">$\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{D})$</span> is at most countable. The <strong>automorphism problem</strong> is exactly the question of determining <span class="math-container">$\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{D})$</span>; I don’t have a reference as to when it was first stated, but I vaguely recall the date 1955.</p>
<p><em>We can also ask about “local” degree structures — e.g., the partial order of the c.e. degrees, or the degrees below <span class="math-container">$0'$</span> — and there are interesting connections between the local and global pictures.</em></p>
<p>Another structural question about the Turing degrees is what sort of natural operations on Turing degrees exist. For instance, there is the Turing jump, and its iterates; but these seem to be the only natural ones. <strong>Martin’s conjecture</strong> states that indeed, every “reasonable” increasing function on the Turing degrees is “basically” an iterate of the Turing jump; MC has a few different forms, for instance “all Borel functions …” or “In <span class="math-container">$L(\mathbb{R})$</span> …”. See e.g. <a href="https://math.berkeley.edu/%7Eslaman/talks/vegas.pdf" rel="nofollow noreferrer">https://math.berkeley.edu/~slaman/talks/vegas.pdf</a>.</p>
<hr />
<p><strong>SET THEORY</strong></p>
<p>An important theme in set theory is the development of <em>canonical models</em> for extensions of ZFC. The first example is Gödel’s <span class="math-container">$L$</span>, which has a number of nice properties: a well-understood structure, a “minimality” property, and a canonical (in particular, forcing-invariant) definition. We can ask whether similar models exist for ZFC + large cardinals: e.g. is there a “core” model for ZFC + “There is a measurable cardinal”? This is the <strong>inner model program</strong>, and has been developed extensively. Surprisingly, there is an end in sight: in an appropriate sense, if a canonical inner model for ZFC + “There is a supercompact cardinal” can be constructed, then this inner model will in fact capture all the large cardinal properties of the universe.</p>
<p><em>I am breezing past a</em> truly gargantuan <em>amount of detail here, but the picture is roughly accurate. See e.g. <a href="http://www.math.uni-bonn.de/ag/logik/events/young-set-theory-2011/Slides/Grigor_Sargsyan_slides.pdf" rel="nofollow noreferrer">http://www.math.uni-bonn.de/ag/logik/events/young-set-theory-2011/Slides/Grigor_Sargsyan_slides.pdf</a> for more details, as well as the recent presentation <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MFDVN7UEUSg&amp;list=PLTn74Qx5mPsQlRpBE5OnxMdN3R1d3DLUO&amp;index=4" rel="nofollow noreferrer">https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MFDVN7UEUSg&amp;list=PLTn74Qx5mPsQlRpBE5OnxMdN3R1d3DLUO&amp;index=4</a> by Woodin.</em></p>
<hr />
<p><strong>FINITE MODEL THEORY</strong></p>
<p>For a first-order sentence <span class="math-container">$\varphi$</span>, let the <em>spectrum</em> of <span class="math-container">$\varphi$</span> be the set of sizes of finite models of <span class="math-container">$\varphi$</span>: <span class="math-container">$$\operatorname{Spec}(\varphi)=\{n: \exists\mathcal{M}(\vert\mathcal{M}\vert=n, \mathcal{M}\models\varphi)\}.$$</span> We can ask what sets of natural numbers are spectra of sentences; in particular, the <strong>finite spectrum problem</strong> (see the really lovely paper <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/0907.5495" rel="nofollow noreferrer">Durand/Jones/Makowsky/More, <em>Fifty years of the spectrum problem</em></a>) asks whether the complement of a spectrum is also a spectrum. It is known, for example, that the complement of the spectrum of a sentence <em>not using “<span class="math-container">$=$</span>”</em> is a spectrum (<a href="https://cyber.bibl.u-szeged.hu/index.php/actcybern/article/view/3289" rel="nofollow noreferrer">Ecsedi-Tóth, <em>A partial solution of the finite spectrum problem</em></a>).</p>
<p>There is a complexity theory connection here: a set is a spectrum iff it is in <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NE_(complexity)" rel="nofollow noreferrer">NE</a>. So the finite spectrum problem asks, “Does <span class="math-container">$\mathrm{NE}=\mathrm{coNE}$</span>?”</p>
<p><em>We can also ask about spectra for non-first-order sentences.</em></p>
<hr />
<p><strong>ABSTRACT MODEL THEORY</strong></p>
<p>Abstract model theory is the study of logics other than first-order. The classic text is “Model-theoretic logics” edited by Barwise and Feferman; see (freely available!) <a href="https://projecteuclid.org/euclid.pl/1235417263" rel="nofollow noreferrer">https://projecteuclid.org/euclid.pl/1235417263</a>. The field began (arguably) with Lindström’s Theorem, which showed that there is no “reasonable” logic stronger than first-order logic which satisfies both the Compactness and Löwenheim–Skolem properties.</p>
<p>Shortly after Lindström’s result, attention turned towards Craig’s interpolation theorem, a powerful result in proof theory (see <a href="https://math.stanford.edu/%7Efeferman/papers/Harmonious%20Logic.pdf" rel="nofollow noreferrer">https://math.stanford.edu/~feferman/papers/Harmonious%20Logic.pdf</a>). Feferman, following Lindström, asked whether there is a reasonable logic stonger than first-order which satisfies compactness and the interpolation property. As far as I know, this question — and many weaker versions! — are still completely open.</p>
<p><em>I believe this is by far the youngest question in this answer.</em></p>

    <div class="s-prose js-post-body" itemprop="text">
<p>The modern logic (and foundational mathematics in general) of the 20th century gave us many important things: Russell's type theory, Zermelo-Fraenkel's set theory, meta-theorems about first order logic, including completeness and incompletness phenomena, model theory, and computability theory. Logic expanded into and around mathematics.</p>

<p>In the summer of 2014 at the <a href="http://vsl2014.at">Vienna Summer of Logic</a> a thousand logicians, if not more, attended a plethora of events that were divided into two parts:</p>

<ol>
<li><p><a href="http://vsl2014.at/mathematical-logic/index.html">Mathematical Logic</a> hosted 4 conferences, 2 workshops, and one competition.</p></li>
<li><p><a href="http://vsl2014.at/logic-in-computer-science/index.html">Logic in Computer Science</a> hosted 8 conferences, <a href="http://vsl2014.at/floc-ws/index.html">$\aleph_0$ workshops</a>, <a href="http://vsl2014.at/olympics/index.html">Computer Logic Olympic Games</a>, and a summer school.</p></li>
</ol>

<p>It is safe to say that computer science logic has grown bigger than its older sister.</p>

<p>If you are looking for exciting new developments and challenges in logic, turn attention to computer science. But the challenges there are rarely hard mathematical nuts to be cracked. Instead, they are about development of new and strange kinds of logic, about algorithms and decision procedures that serve the needs of computer science, about design of formal systems that is suitable for computer implementation, and so on.</p>

<p>Logic of the early 20th century was a reaction to a conceptual and methodological crisis in mathematics. Logic of the early 21st century is the tool for conquering the newly discovered land of computer science. Now is a good time to be an Edison or a Tesla of logic, and a little less a Cantor or a Russell. (It's always good to be a Gödel or a Turing, of course.)</p>

    <div class="s-prose js-post-body" itemprop="text">
<p>If we're going to include computer science, the big kahuna is of course P vs NP, first proposed by Gödel in a now-famous letter to von Neumann in the 1950s.  A fair amount of the existing progress on it has been made by logicians.</p>
<p>Furthermore, the problem can be stated in a particularly logical <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P_versus_NP_problem" rel="noreferrer">way</a>:  &quot;Is existential second-order logic able to describe languages (of finite linearly ordered structures with nontrivial signature) that first-order logic with least fixed point cannot?&quot;</p>

    <div class="s-prose js-post-body" itemprop="text">
<p>A recent list of open problems in Reverse Mathematics is:</p>

<blockquote>
  <p>Antonio Montalbán, <a href="http://projecteuclid.org/euclid.bsl/1309952320" rel="noreferrer">Open questions in reverse mathematics</a>, <em>Bulletin of Symbolic Logic</em> 17:3 (2011), 431-454. <a href="https://math.berkeley.edu/~antonio/papers/questionsRM.pdf" rel="noreferrer">Preprint</a></p>
</blockquote>

<p>A slightly older list is from Steve Simpson: <a href="http://www.personal.psu.edu/t20/talks/cta/problems.pdf" rel="noreferrer">Open problems in Reverse Mathematics</a> (1999)</p>

<p>I will mention one specific and tantalizing problem: the strength of <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_set#Hindman.27s_theorem" rel="noreferrer">Hindman's theorem</a>. Upper and lower bounds for this theorem were first established by Blass, Hirst, and Simpson in a joint paper from 1987. Since then, there have been several papers on the ultrafilters that are relevant to the proof, but the overall bounds have been only slightly improved, for example by </p>

<blockquote>
  <p>Henry  Towsner, <a href="http://projecteuclid.org/euclid.ndjfl/1336586237" rel="noreferrer">A Simple Proof and Some Difficult Examples for Hindman’s Theorem</a>, <em>Notre Dame Journal of Formal Logic</em>, 53(1): 53-65. 2012. <a href="http://arxiv.org/pdf/0906.3885.pdf" rel="noreferrer">Preprint</a></p>
</blockquote>

<p>Hindman's theorem is of interest as a purely combinatorial result, but also because it is so closely related to results of topological dynamics such as the Auslander--Ellis theorem. </p>

    <div class="s-prose js-post-body" itemprop="text">
<p>This is perhaps closer to computational complexity than to logic, but there are many open problems in propositional proof complexity that are analogous to, and have close connections to, well-known open problems in computational complexity.  For example, it is widely believed that there are tautologies whose proof in a <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frege_system">Frege system</a> is necessarily exponential in size; however, so far, no superpolynomial lower bounds have been proven.</p>

<p>The <a href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/2271891" rel="noreferrer">paper</a> &ldquo;One Hundred and Two Problems in Mathematical Logic&rdquo; by Harvey Friedman is an article that lists 102 problems in mathematical logic. </p>
