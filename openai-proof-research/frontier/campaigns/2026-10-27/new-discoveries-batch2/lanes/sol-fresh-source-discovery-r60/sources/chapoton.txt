 On posets and polytopes attached to arbors 
 
 
 F. Chapoton
   
 August 24, 2026 

  Abstract 
    
 Starting from the data of an arbor, which is a rooted tree with
vertices decorated by disjoint sets, we introduce a lattice polytope
and a partial order on its lattice points. We give recursive
formulas for various classical invariants of these polytopes and
posets, using the tree structure. For linear arbors, we propose a
conjecture exchanging the Ehrhart polynomial of the polytope with
the Zeta polynomial of the poset for the reverse arbor. The general
motivation comes from a transmutation operator acting on
   M  M   -triangles, which should link the posets considered here with
some kind of generalized noncrossing partitions and generalized
associahedra. We give some evidence for this relationship in several
cases, including notably some polytopes, namely halohedra and
Hochschild polytopes. 
  
 
 
 
  À la mémoire des petits bilbis.  
 
 
 
 Introduction 

 
 The combinatorial data that will be our starting point is a rooted
tree whose vertices are decorated with a partition of a finite
set. Such objects will be called  arbors . This article
introduces two constructions attached to any arbor    t  t   . The first
construction is a lattice polytope     Q  t   Q_{t}    inside some     ℕ  n   \mathbb{N}^{n}    and the
second one is the partial order     P  t   P_{t}    defined by coordinate-wise
comparison on the set of lattice points in this polytope. 
 
 
 Before explaining at length the motivation for these constructions
coming from Coxeter groups, cluster theory and representation theory,
let us describe our results. 
 
 
 First, it is shown that the polytopes     Q  t   Q_{t}   , initially defined by
inequalities, are lattice polytopes, which means that they have
integral vertices. Moreover, they are simple polytopes and have no
interior lattice point. Their vertices are described in a recursive
way, using a projection map    π  \pi    to a product of smaller polytopes in
the same family. The polytopes are also identified as Minkowski sums
of simplices. Basic properties of the posets     P  t   P_{t}    are also stated:
they are graded with a unique minimum and the interval between this
minimum and any element is a product of total orders. This property
allows to view their Hasse diagrams as skeletons of cubical complexes. 
 
 
 Our next set of results consists of recursive formulas to compute
several invariants of the polytopes     Q  t   Q_{t}    and posets     P  t   P_{t}   . This uses
the projection map    π  \pi    and often requires a finer invariant that
includes information about the height, which is the sum of
coordinates. A recursive formula is obtained for the Ehrhart
polynomial of the polytope     Q  t   Q_{t}   , which counts lattice points in the
dilates, cf.  [ Bar08 ] . One considers then the function that
measures the volume in the hyperplane sections according to height,
and gets recursive formulas for this function and for its Laplace
transform. Similarly, recursive formulas are given for the Zeta
polynomial, the    M  M   -triangle and the    f  f   -vector of the poset
    P  t   P_{t}   . The Zeta polynomial is a classical invariant that counts chains
in the poset, see  [ Sta74 , §3] . The    M  M   -triangle is a
polynomial in two variables, defined in  [ Cha04 ]  for any graded poset,
as a weighted sum of all Möbius numbers, similar to the more classical
characteristic polynomial introduced in  [ Rot64 ] . The
   f  f   -vector is defined by counting faces of the underlying cubical
complex. 
 
 
 In   Section   3  , two conjectures are proposed in the case of
linear arbors. The first one claims that reversing the arbor
upside-down should exchange the Ehrhart polynomial of the polytope
    Q  t   Q_{t}    and the Zeta polynomial of the poset     P  t   P_{t}   . Here both
constructions interact in a rather surprising way. The second
conjecture claims that the    h  h   -vector is unchanged by reversing a
linear arbor. Here the    h  h   -vector of an arbor    t  t    is the generating
polynomial of elements of     P  t   P_{t}    according to the number of non-zero
coordinates. 
 
 
 In   Section   4  , we introduce  transmutation  as a
birational involution acting on the    M  M   -triangle. Transmutation is
central in the original motivation of the article and appears in the
results in the next sections. Here the reader may want to read the
motivation section below first. 
 
 
 In the five next sections, we gather evidence concerning transmutation
for several specific families of arbors. Let us say that two posets
are  transmuted partners  if they share the same Zeta
polynomial and their    M  M   -triangles are related by transmutation. 
 
 
 In   Section   5  , we propose conjectures about unary-binary
arbors. In this case, one can define from the arbor    t  t    a gentle
quiver-with-relations whose lattice of support    τ  \tau   -tilting objects
is finite. Applying the core-label-order construction should lead to
transmuted partners of the posets     P  t   P_{t}   . This is also stated in the
more combinatorial language of Stokes posets for quadrangulations  [ Bar01 ,  Cha16 ] . 
 
 
 In   Section   6  , we consider the case of arbors of type    𝔸  \mathbb{A}   , meaning
linear arbors with no multiple vertex. We introduce a more general
family of posets, including the posets     P  t   P_{t}    for arbors of type    𝔸  \mathbb{A}   . We
compute the Zeta polynomials and the    M  M   -triangles of these more general
posets. This implies that noncrossing partitions of Coxeter type    𝔸  \mathbb{A}    are
transmuted partners of the posets     P  t   P_{t}    for arbors of type    𝔸  \mathbb{A}   . 
 
 
 In   Section   7  , we consider the case of arbors of type    𝔹  \mathbb{B}   , meaning
arbors with only one vertex. We introduce a more general family of
posets, including the poset     P  t   P_{t}    for arbors of type    𝔹  \mathbb{B}   . We compute
the Zeta polynomials and the    M  M   -triangles of these more general
posets. This implies that noncrossing partitions of Coxeter type    𝔹  \mathbb{B}   
are transmuted partners of the posets     P  t   P_{t}    for arbors of type    𝔹  \mathbb{B}   . 
 
 
 In   Section   8  , we turn to arbors with one simple vertex and
one multiple vertex. The aim is to relate them to the family of simple
polytopes named halohedra  [ DHV11 ,  DFHV12 ] . We prove that
the    h  h   -vector of the polytope coincides with the    h  h   -vector of the
arbor. 
 
 
 In   Section   9  , we turn to arbors that are corollas, namely
with no multiple vertex and such that all non-root vertices are
attached to the root vertex. The aim is to relate these arbors to the
family of Hochschild polytopes  [ San09 ,  RS18 ]  and the
associated Hochschild lattices  [ Com21 ] . We conjecture that
the core-label-orders of Hochschild lattices are transmuted partners
of the posets     P  t   P_{t}   . We prove that the    h  h   -vector of the Hochschild
polytope coincides with the    h  h   -vector of the arbor. 
 
 
  0.1  Motivation 

 
 Let us now try to explain, with the help of the following figure,
where this work comes from. 
 
             
 
  
 simple polytopes (generalized associahedra) 
             
 
  
 congruence-uniform lattices 
 (Cambrian lattices) 
             
 
  
 graded lattices (noncrossing 
 partitions) 
             
 
  
 graded posets of arbors 
             
 
  
 lattice polytopes 
 of arbors 
                                       
 
 
 Let us start with the leftmost box. The theory of Cambrian lattices  [ Rea06 ]  defines, for any finite Coxeter group    W  W    and
Coxeter element     c  ∈  W   c\in W   , a finite lattice. For crystallographic
simply-laced Coxeter groups, these lattices can also be described
inside the theory of cluster algebras, using green mutations, see  [ KD20 ]  for a survey. There is an alternative
representation-theoretic viewpoint using inclusion of torsion classes
for finite-dimensional algebras, considered also using support
   τ  \tau   -tilting objects  [ AIR14 ] . It is known that finite
lattices of torsion-classes are congruence-uniform  [ GM19 ,  DIR+23 ] . It is also known that Cambrian lattices
are congruence-uniform, as quotients of the weak order on the Coxeter
group  [ Rea06 ] . 
 
 
 Let us move to the top box, closely tied to the previous one. It is
known that the Hasse diagrams of Cambrian lattices are skeletons of
some simple polytopes, sometimes called generalized associahedra
 [ CFZ02 ] . In this setting,
one can define the    F  F   -triangle, a polynomial in two variables that
refines the    f  f   -vector of the polytope  [ Cha04 ]  and depends on
the choice of a distinguished vertex. Its definition, as recalled in
  Appendix   A  , is applied to the polar polytope seen as a
spherical simplicial complex with a distinguished facet. 
 
 
 Consider now the bottom box. Starting from a congruence-uniform
lattice in the leftmost box, one can define another lattice, using the
canonical join representation. This construction was introduced by
Reading in  [ Rea16 , §9.7]  under the name of
 shard-intersection-order . This is also known as the
 core-label-order , in the terminology
of  [ Müh19 ] . For Cambrian lattices, the core-label-order only
depends on    W  W    and recovers the    W  W   -noncrossing-partitions lattice of
Brady-Watt  [ BW02 ]  and Bessis  [ Bes15 ] . As these
lattices are graded, one can define their    M  M   -triangles as recalled in
  Appendix   A  . 
 
 
 In the Cambrian context, the    F  F   -triangle of the generalized
associahedra is related to the    M  M   -triangle of the lattice of
   W  W   -noncrossing-partitions by the explicit birational
transformations (  57  ) and (  56  ) recalled in
  Appendix   A  . This equality was conjectured
in  [ Cha04 ]  and proved in  [ Ath07 ] . These
birational transformations are expected to be more widely applicable
in similar contexts, see for example  [ GM20 ] . Note
that this birational relationship implies that    h  h   -vectors of
generalized associahedra also enumerate non-crossing partitions
according to their rank. 
 
 
 All this forms a well-traveled landscape, involving congruence-uniform
lattices and directed skeletons of polytopes. Our main claim is that
there should exist another aspect of this story, related to the
previous landscape by transmutation. 
 
 
 The  core idea  is that some posets from the bottom box (noncrossing
partitions and similar posets) should be  transmuted partners 
of some posets from the middle box (associated with arbors). By this
expression, we mean that two posets share the same Zeta polynomial and
that their    M  M   -triangles are related by an explicit birational
involution that we choose to call  transmutation . 
 
 
 The starting point for the project was in fact in the other direction:
starting from the classical lattices of noncrossing partitions, we
have found their transmuted partners. This has led us to the present
framework of arbors. 
 
 
 We will prove in   Section   6   that for linear arbors in which every
vertex has only one element, the transmuted    M  M   -triangle is indeed the
   M  M   -triangle of the noncrossing-partitions lattice of type
   𝔸  \mathbb{A}   . Moreover, we prove that they share the same Zeta
polynomial, so they are indeed transmuted partners as claimed. 
 
 
 In a similar vein, it is shown in   Section   7   that the noncrossing
partitions lattices of type    𝔹  \mathbb{B}    are transmuted partners of the posets
    P  t   P_{t}    for arbors with one vertex.

 Having introduced a general theory on the arbor side, it is natural to
ask if one can use transmutation in the other direction. 
 
 
 The best hope would be the following statement for every arbor    t  t   : 
 
 
 There should exist a simple polytope     Φ  t   \Phi_{t}   , some way to orient its
skeleton to find the Hasse diagram of a congruence-uniform
“Cambrian-like” lattice     Λ  t   \Lambda_{t}    and some “noncrossing-like”
lattice     𝖭𝖢  t   \mathsf{NC}_{t}    built from     Λ  t   \Lambda_{t}    by the “core-label-order”
construction. The lattice     𝖭𝖢  t   \mathsf{NC}_{t}    should be a transmuted partner of
    P  t   P_{t}   . If the arbor    t  t    has size    n  n    and the poset     P  t   P_{t}    has    N  N   
elements, then     Φ  t   \Phi_{t}    should be a simple polytope of dimension    n  n   
with    N  N    vertices. 
 
 
 There is so far no idea on how to achieve that in general. A proposal
for unary-binary arbors is made in   Section   5   in terms of gentle algebras. 
 
 
 A much weaker statement would be to produce starting from    t  t    the
simple polytope     Φ  t   \Phi_{t}    together with a distinguished vertex (as in
the top box) whose    F  F   -triangle can be transformed by formulas in
  Appendix   A   into an    M  M   -triangle (as in the bottom box) that
would be the transmutation of the    M  M   -triangle of     P  t   P_{t}   . In
particular,   Proposition   4.1   says that the    h  h   -vector of    t  t    is
then the diagonal of the    M  M   -triangle of the lattice     𝖭𝖢  t   \mathsf{NC}_{t}   , hence
describes the grading of this poset and also the    h  h   -vector of the
polytope     Φ  t   \Phi_{t}   . 
 
 
 Again, there is no known idea on how to do that. Two proposals are
made in   Section   8   and   Section   9   that should
relate in this way some arbors with known families of polytopes. The
evidence is given by a coincidence of    h  h   -vectors, which would follow
from the expected transmutation of    M  M   -triangles. 
 
 
 Assuming that we can produce the polytope     Φ  t   \Phi_{t}    endowed with its
distinguished vertex, one could then consider the simplicial complex
defined as the dual of the simple polytope     Φ  t   \Phi_{t}   . One expects that
this simplicial complex is flag, and moreover that its Gamma-triangle
with respect to the distinguished facet, as defined in
 [ Cha20a ] , is nonnegative. This holds for all arbors of size at
most    13  13   . 
 
 
 Even weaker is the following conjecture, keeping only minimal
information. 
 
 
   Conjecture 0.1   .  
 
  For every arbor    t  t   , there exists a simple polytope     Φ  t   \Phi_{t}    such
that the    h  h   -vector of    t  t    is equal to the    h  h   -vector of     Φ  t   \Phi_{t}   .  
 
 
 
 
  0.2  Further remarks 

 
 The posets that we attach to arbors of type    𝔸  \mathbb{A}    are special cases of
the  avalanche posets  introduced by Combe and Giraudo in
 [ CG20 ,  CG22 ] . Despite their very simple
nature and the fact that their cardinality is a Catalan number, they
do not seem to have been studied much. 
 
 
 The polytopes that we attach to arbors of type    𝔸  \mathbb{A}    have Catalan-many
lattice points. They in fact coincide with a special case of the
 Pitman-Stanley polytopes  introduced in  [ SP02 ] , when all
parameters in the definition are set to    1  1   . 
 
 
 Our notion of pairs of posets being transmuted partners seems to have
some relationship, still to be clarified, with the notion of
 Whitney duality  introduced in  [ GDH21 ] . Whitney
duality relates pairs of posets that exchange their Whitney
polynomials of the first and second kind. Some examples involve
noncrossing partition lattices. One can in particular note the
intriguing fact that the subposet of the poset
    NCDyck  4   \operatorname{NCDyck}_{4}    of  [ GDH21 , Fig. 9]  defined by
restriction to indices in the order     1  ,  2  ,  3  ,  4   1,2,3,4    is isomorphic to the
poset     P  t   P_{t}    for the arbor of type    𝔸  \mathbb{A}    on    3  3    elements. 
 
 
 Note that our notion of  arbor  has been considered in the
literature under various names, including “multilabelled trees” and “rooted trees
of non-empty sets”  [ MSS99 ,  BOS05 ] . 
 
 
 In our examples of  transmuted partners , we observe more
properties, for example the leading coefficients of the    q  q   -Zeta
polynomials  [ Cha24 ]  are reciprocal of each other as polynomials in    q  q   .

  1  Notations and basic properties 

 
 Let us start by describing our main combinatorial objects, using the
language of species. For an introduction to the theory of species, see  [ BLL98 ] . 
 
 
 Let    S  S    be the species of sets and     S     ≥  1    S_{\geq 1}    be the species of
non-empty sets. Let us consider the species    A  A    defined by the
implicit equation 
 

  
  
      A  =    S     ≥  1    ×   S  ⁡   (  A  )      .   A=S_{\geq 1}\times S(A).    
    
 
 In words, this means that the    A  A   -structures on a finite set    I  I    are
rooted trees, whose vertices are labeled by pairwise disjoint
non-empty subsets of    I  I   , and the full set of labels is    I  I   . 
 
 
 Such a rooted tree will be called an  arbor  on the set    I  I   . For example, here is an arbor on the set     {   a  ,  b  ,  c  ,  d  ,  e  ,  f   }   \{a,b,c,d,e,f\}   :                a  ,  f   a,f                  b  ,  e   b,e                  d  d                  c  c             
 . 
 This example will be used as a running example. 
 
 
 Arbors will either be depicted with their root vertex on the top and
growing down as above, or with their root vertex on the left and
growing right. The cardinality of a vertex    v  v    will be denoted
    |  v  |   |v|   . If      |  v  |   ≥  2   |v|\geq 2   , then the vertex    v  v    is  multiple . The
root vertex of an arbor    t  t    will be denoted     r  t   r_{t}   . 
 
 
 For a vertex    v  v    of an arbor    t  t   , any vertex    w  w    such that the unique
path in    t  t    from    w  w    to the root vertex     r  t   r_{t}    passes through    v  v    is
called a  descendant  of    v  v   . This condition is denoted by
    v  ⪯  w   v\preceq w   . The set of all descendants of    v  v    form the sub-tree
rooted at    v  v   . 
 
 
 To every vertex    v  v    of an arbor, one associates the union of all
labels that appear in some descendant of    v  v   . This set is denoted
    𝒟  ⁡   (  v  )    \mathscr{D}(v)   , standing for “down”. In the running example, the sets     𝒟  ⁡   (  v  )    \mathscr{D}(v)   
are     {   a  ,  b  ,  c  ,  d  ,  e  ,  f   }   \{a,b,c,d,e,f\}   ,     {  b  ,  e  }   \{b,e\}   ,     {  d  }   \{d\}    and     {  c  }   \{c\}   . 
 
 
 When      |  I  |   =  n   |I|=n   , the arbor    t  t    has  size     n  n   . 
 
 
 To every arbor    t  t    on a set    I  I   , we will associate both a partial
order and a polytope, closely related to each other. Let us start with
the polytope. 
 
 
 The ambient space is the vector space over    ℝ  \mathbb{R}    with coordinates
     (   x  i   )    i  ∈  I    {(x_{i})}_{i\in I}   . The polytope     Q  t   Q_{t}    is defined by the inequalities
    0  ≤   x  i    0\leq x_{i}    for every     i  ∈  I   i\in I    and 
 

  
  
       ∑   i  ∈   𝒟  ⁡   (  v  )       x  i    ≤   |   𝒟  ⁡   (  v  )    |    \sum_{i\in\mathscr{D}(v)}x_{i}\leq|\mathscr{D}(v)|    
  
  (1)    
 
 for every vertex    v  v   . In particular, the inequality attached to the
root vertex     r  t   r_{t}    says that the sum of all coordinates is less than
or equal to     |  I  |   |I|   , so that this indeed defines a bounded polyhedron. 
 
 
 In the running example, besides all coordinates
being nonnegative, the defining inequalities are: 
 

  
  
        x  c   ≤  1   ,    x  d   ≤  1   ,     x  b   +   x  e    ≤  2   ,     x  a   +   x  b   +   x  c   +   x  d   +   x  e   +   x  f    ≤  6    .   x_{c}\leq 1,\quad x_{d}\leq 1,\quad x_{b}+x_{e}\leq 2,\quad x_{a}+x_{b}+x_{c}+x_{d}+x_{e}+x_{f}\leq 6.    
    
 
 
 
 The following property is clear from the definition. 
 
 
   Lemma 1.1   .  
 
  Points with all coordinates in the interval     [  0  ,  1  ]   [0,1]    are contained in     Q  t   Q_{t}   . In
other words, the polytope     Q  t   Q_{t}    contains the standard hypercube.  
 
 
 
 The definition of polytopes     Q  t   Q_{t}    by inequalities implies clearly that
they are anti-blocking polytopes, in the sense of Fulkerson  [ Ful71 ] , see also  [ Sch98 , §9.3] . 
 
 
 We will often use the height function    ht  \operatorname{ht}    which is the sum of
coordinates. Note that the height function is bounded above by     |  I  |   |I|   ,
which is attained at the point     (  1  ,  1  ,  …  ,  1  )   (1,1,\ldots,1)   . 
 
 
 Let now    t  t    be an arbor on a set of cardinality     n  ≥  1   n\geq 1   . Let

 Closely linked to the    M  M   -triangle, there is another two-variable

transformations (  58  ) and (  59  ) recalled in

this context, the transposition has no clear meaning, as it exchanges

  5.1  Stokes polytopes and quadrangulations 

 
 Let us present briefly an alternative description of the
same situation, replacing the general    τ  \tau   -tilting theory by the
more specific notion of Stokes polytopes and Baryshnikov compatibility
 [ Bar01 ,  Cha16 ] . 
 
 
 Let’s consider a regular polygon with      2  ​  n   +  4   2n+4    vertices. Fix a
quadrangulation    Q  Q    of this polygon, which means a set of    n  n   
noncrossing segments joining vertices and cutting the polygon into
quadrilateral regions. After Baryhsnikov, one can define a
compatibility relation between quadrangulations. On the set     R  Q   R_{Q}    of
quadrangulations compatible with    Q  Q   , there is a mutation process,
that defines a mutation graph. Moreover, this mutation process is
oriented and defines a partial order. The mutation graph is also the
skeleton of a polytope, called the Stokes polytope for the
quadrangulation  [ MP19 ,  Man18 ,  BMP17 ] . 
 
 
 There is a simple way to map the planar-embedded arbor    t  t    into a
quadrangulation. There is also a way to define a gentle quiver from
any quadrangulation. The composite map recovers the definition of
    G  t   G_{t}    from    t  t   . By this correspondence, the    τ  \tau   -tilting theory
exactly matches the combinatorial description using mutations of
compatible quadrangulations. 
 
 
 It follows that the conjectural statements above could be rephrased entirely
using the structures (mutation graph, poset, polytope) coming from
this context instead of those coming from    τ  \tau   -tilting theory.

  8  Arbors and halohedra 

 
 The halohedra form a sequence of simple convex polytopes. They have a
topological interpretation as a compactification of a configuration
space, similar to the case of associahedra and cyclohedra
 [ DHV11 ,  DFHV12 ] . They also have a combinatorial
description in terms of “design tubings” of the cycle graphs, that
makes them close relatives of graph-associahedra. The halohedra have
also appeared in mathematical physics  [ SC18 ,  Sal19 ] . 
 
 
 There is one halohedron     H  n   H_{n}    in dimension    n  n    for each dimension

 Proof.

  Appendix A  Conversion formulas 

 
 We gather here the conversion formulas that we use between three
different kinds of polynomials in two variables, namely    F  F   -triangles,
   M  M   -triangles and    H  H   -triangles. These triangles and rational
change-of-variables between them were introduced in  [ Cha04 ,  Cha06 ]  in the context of cluster fans and generalized
associahedra. They are called  triangles  because of the shape of their
Newton polygons. Each triangle comes with a size parameter    n  n   , which
is the maximal value of either coordinate in the Newton polygon. 
 
 
 Given a pure simplicial complex of spherical topology     S  ​  C   SC    and a
chosen facet    C  C   , one can define the    F  F   -triangle as the
following sum over all faces: 
 

  
  
       F  ⁡   (  X  ,  Y  )    =    ∑   s  ∈   S  ​  C       X   posi  ⁡   (  s  )     ​   Y   nega  ⁡   (  s  )        ,   F(X,Y)=\sum_{s\in SC}X^{\operatorname{posi}(s)}Y^{\operatorname{nega}(s)},    
  
  (54)    
 
 where     nega  ⁡   (  s  )    \operatorname{nega}(s)    (resp.     posi  ⁡   (  s  )    \operatorname{posi}(s)   ) is the number of elements in    s  s   
that belong (resp. do not belong) to    C  C   . 
 
 
 Given a graded poset    P  P   , one can define the    M  M   -triangle of    P  P    as
the following sum of Möbius numbers weighted using the rank function
   ht  \operatorname{ht}   : 
 

  
  
       M  ⁡   (  X  ,  Y  )    =    ∑   a  ≤  b      μ  ⁡   (  a  ,  b  )    ​   X   ht  ⁡   (  a  )     ​   Y   ht  ⁡   (  b  )        .   M(X,Y)=\sum_{a\leq b}\mu(a,b)X^{\operatorname{ht}(a)}Y^{\operatorname{ht}(b)}.    
  
  (55)
