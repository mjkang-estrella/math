                                                                                         Abstract
                                                 P For a connected graph G on n vertices, the interval index is defined by Int(G) =
                                                    {u,v}⊂V (G) (|I(u, v)| − 1), where I(u, v) denotes the set of all vertices lying on a
                                                 shortest u − v path. It was conjectured that, among all connected graphs on n
                                                 vertices, the path Pn attains the maximum value of the interval index. In this
                                                 paper we settle this conjecture, and we prove that the maximum is attained only
                                                 for Pn , except for n = 4, when the cycle C4 attains the same value. We also prove
                                                 that, for every n ≥ 31, the maximum interval index among graphs without pendant
                                                 vertices equals (n3 −n)/6−2(n−4) and it is attained by a dumbbell graph, obtained
                                                 by joining two 4-cycles by a path.

                                               Keywords: interval index; Wiener index; extremal graph; path; betweenness.
                                               AMS Subject Classification numbers: 05C12; 05C35


                                           1     Introduction
                                           Distance-based graph invariants have been studied for decades, since they capture struc-
                                           tural properties of graphs which are relevant, in particular, in mathematical chemistry.
                                           The oldest and most thoroughly investigated such invariant is the Wiener index, intro-
                                           duced by Wiener already in 1947 [12] (see [8] for a survey of its mathematical aspects).
                                           Recently, Knor, Kovijanić Vukićević, Škrekovski, Došljak, Dimitrov, Popivoda, Vujošević
                                           and Sedlar [4] introduced the interval index of a graph. This invariant can be viewed as a
                                           refinement of the Wiener index, since it takes into account not only the distance between
                                           two vertices, but the order of the subgraph formed by the shortest paths joining them.
                                           Several other recent refinements of the Wiener index by (some of) the same authors are
                                           in [7, 5, 6].
                                               Let G = (V, E) be a connected graph and, for u, v ∈ V (G), let d(u, v) denote the
                                           distance between u and v in G. The interval between u and v is
                                                                I(u, v) = {w ∈ V (G) : d(u, w) + d(w, v) = d(u, v)},

                                                                                            1
that is, the set of all vertices lying on some shortest u − v path. This notion goes back to
Mulder’s monograph [10]. Nebeský [11] used it to characterize geodetic graphs, that is,
graphs in which |I(u, v)| = d(u, v) + 1 for every u, v. The interval index of G is
                                            X
                               Int(G) =           (|I(u, v)| − 1) .
                                      {u,v}⊂V (G)

    If G is a tree, then any two vertices of G are joined by a unique shortest path, so
Int(G) coincides with the Wiener index of G. It is shown in [4] that, among all trees on
n vertices, the path Pn maximizes and the star Sn minimizes the interval index. More
generally, a graph with a cut vertex can be decomposed according to a formula which
allows the interval index to be computed from its blocks. Further, the extremal values
of the interval index were determined for unicyclic graphs of given girth, and Int(G) was
computed for hypercubes, for complete bipartite graphs and for ladder graphs. Since the
complete graph minimizes the interval index among all connected graphs on n vertices,
while the maximum turned out to be far less accessible, the authors of [4] posed the
following conjecture.

Conjecture 1 In the class of connected graphs on n vertices the path Pn attains the
maximum value of interval index.

    The analogous question for graphs without pendant vertices is more delicate. Ex-
tremal problems for the Wiener index under a minimum-degree constraint were studied
by Alochukwu and Dankelmann [1], and for 2-connected graphs by Bessy, Dross, Knor
and Škrekovski [3]. The problem which graphs of a given minimum degree attain the
maximum Wiener index seems to be open in general, see Problem 4.1 in [8]. Among
2-connected graphs on n vertices the maximum Wiener index is attained by the cycle Cn ,
see [3] and the references therein, but this is no longer true for graphs with minimum
degree at least 2. For instance, W (C20 ) = 1000, while the graph on 20 vertices consisting
of two triangles joined by a path has Wiener index 1294. This agrees with the result of
[2], where it is shown that among graphs on n vertices with a given number p ≥ 2 of
blocks the maximum Wiener index is attained by a graph consisting of two cycles joined
by a path, in which one or both cycles may be replaced by an edge. However, the graph
consisting of two triangles joined by a path is not extremal for the interval index. Namely,
let x and y be the two vertices of one of its triangles which do not lie on the path. Every
other vertex z is at the same distance from x and y, and d(x, y) = 1, so none of the
vertices x, y, z lies on a shortest path between the other two. The same holds for the
other triangle, and no triple of vertices contains both such pairs. Hence, by Lemma 4
below, the interval index of this graph is at most (n3 − n)/6 − 2(n − 2). For n ≥ 8 this
is less than the value (n3 − n)/6 − 2(n − 4) attained by the dumbbell graph Dn from
Proposition 6. So the authors of [4] posed the following problem.

Problem 2 Characterize connected graphs on n vertices without pendant vertices which
maximize the interval index.

   In this paper we settle Conjecture 1. We show that the path Pn is the unique graph
attaining the maximum, with the exception of n = 4, when it is attained also by C4 .

Moreover, for n ≥ 31 we determine the maximum interval index in the class of connected
graphs without pendant vertices: it equals (n3 − n)/6 − 2(n − 4). It is attained by the
dumbbell consisting of two 4-cycles joined by a path.


2     General graphs
Let G be a connected graph on n vertices with vertex set V (G) and distance function d.
Recall that I(u, v) = {w ∈ V (G) : d(u, w) + d(w, v) = d(u, v)}. For three pairwise distinct
vertices x, y, z of G, we say that y lies between x and z if d(x, y) + d(y, z) = d(x, z), that
is, if y ∈ I(x, z) \ {x, z}. We call an unordered triple {x, y, z} of distinct vertices collinear
if at least one of its three vertices lies between the other two, and we denote by col(G)
the number of collinear triples in G.
     Notice that a triple may a priori be collinear in three different ways, since each of its
three vertices could be the one lying between the other two. We show that this is not the
case and a triple can be collinear in a unique way.

Lemma 3 Among three pairwise distinct vertices of a connected graph, at most one lies
between the other two.

Proof. Assume to the contrary that two of the three vertices lie between the other two.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that y lies between x and z, and x lies between
y and z. This means that

                d(x, y) + d(y, z) = d(x, z) and d(y, x) + d(x, z) = d(y, z).

Substituting the first equality into the second one gives 2d(x, y) = 0. So x = y, which is
a contradiction with x, y, z being pairwise distinct.

establish the theorem through several claims.
Claim A. If w ∈ Li then d(w, v) = D − i.
Let w ̸∈ {u, v}. The triple {u, v, w} is collinear, so one of its vertices lies between the other
two. Assume that u lies between w and v. Then d(w, v) = d(w, u)+d(u, v) = d(w, u)+D >
D, which is a contradiction with D = diam(G). The case when v lies between w and u is
symmetric. Hence w lies between u and v, that is, d(u, w) + d(w, v) = D.
Claim B. If 1 ≤ i ≤ D −1 and w ̸= w′ both belong to Li , then i = D/2 and d(w, w′ ) = 2i.
Consider the triple {u, w, w′ }. Here d(u, w) = d(u, w′ ) = i. So neither w nor w′ can lie
between u and the other vertex, as this would force d(w, w′ ) = 0. Hence u lies between
w and w′ , which gives d(w, w′ ) = 2i. By Claim A we have d(w, v) = d(w′ , v) = D − i. So
the same argument applied to the triple {v, w, w′ } gives d(w, w′ ) = 2(D − i). Therefore
2i = 2(D − i), and hence i = D/2.
    By Claim B, every level Li with 1 ≤ i ≤ D − 1 and i ̸= D/2 is a singleton. By Claim
A, LD = {v}, and of course L0 = {u}. Consequently, if D is odd then all levels are
singletons. In that case G ∼= Pn , since an edge joins only vertices of equal or consecutive
levels, and every vertex of Li with i ≥ 1 has a neighbour in Li−1 .
Claim C. If some level Li contains at least two vertices, then D = 2, n = 4 and G ∼   = C4 .
By Claim B this can happen only for i = D/2 ≥ 1. Denote m = |Li | ≥ 2, and assume
first that m ≥ 3. Let w1 , w2 , w3 ∈ Li be pairwise distinct. By Claim B they are pairwise
at distance D. So none of them can lie between the other two, as D + D = D would
force D = 0. Hence the triple {w1 , w2 , w3 } is not collinear, which is a contradiction with
our assumption. So m = 2, and we denote Li = {w, w′ }. The level Li−1 is a singleton,
say Li−1 = {a}. Indeed, for i = 1 this is L0 = {u}, and for i ≥ 2 it follows from Claim

necessarily a. Hence d(w, w′ ) ≤ 2. Claim B gives d(w, w′ ) = 2i, so i = 1. Therefore
D = 2 and n = 4. Since d(u, v) = 2 and d(w, w′ ) = 2, G ∼   = C4 .
    To summarize, either all levels are singletons and G ∼ = Pn as above, or Claim C applies
and G ∼ = C4 . Conversely, Int(C4 ) = 4 · 1 + 2 · 3 = 10 = Int(P4 ).

triple which is not collinear will be called non-collinear. Non-collinear triples satisfy the
strict triangle inequality on all three of their pairwise distances. For a connected graph
G on n vertices we denote the number of its non-collinear triples by noncol(G). Then by

   For n ≥ 8, the dumbbell Dn is the graph obtained by joining two disjoint copies of C4
by a path of length n − 7, that is with n − 8 internal vertices. We show that Dn is an
extremal graph in the class of graphs without pendant vertices.

Proposition 6 For every n ≥ 8 it holds that noncol(Dn ) = 2(n − 4).

Proof. Denote the two copies of C4 by a′ , w1 , a, w1′ and b′ , w2 , b, w2′ . Here a and b are
the vertices at which the connecting path is attached, while a′ and b′ are the vertices
antipodal to them.
    Take any z ̸∈ {w1 , w1′ }. Then d(z, w1 ) = d(z, w1′ ), and d(w1 , w1′ ) = 2. So the triple
{w1 , w1′ , z} is collinear if and only if z lies between w1 and w1′ . Hence z ∈ {a, a′ }. Con-
sequently, exactly n − 4 triples of this form are non-collinear. The pair {w2 , w2′ } gives
another n − 4 non-collinear triples {w2 , w2′ , z} disjoint from the previous ones.
    Every remaining triple avoids at least one of w1 , w1′ and at least one of w2 , w2′ . Delete
one vertex from each pair. What is left is an isometric path in Dn containing the triple.
All triples of such a path are collinear, so noncol(Dn ) = 2(n − 4).

    In order to show that no graph without pendant vertices beats Dn , we distinguish
whether G has a cut vertex or not. Let us first consider the case in which G has a cut
vertex. Since G has no pendant vertices, no pendant block of G is a bridge. So every
leaf of the block tree of G is a 2-connected block B on k ≥ 3 vertices, and we denote by
a the unique cut vertex of G lying in B. Notice that distances inside a block agree with
distances in G, so we may measure them in B alone.
    A pair of vertices of B other than a is bad at a if neither of the two vertices lies on
a shortest path from a to the other one. That is, a pair {x, y} ⊆ V (B) \ {a} is bad if
d(x, y) ̸= |d(a, x) − d(a, y)|. We denote by β(B, a) the number of bad pairs of B at a.

A pair (u, v) with d(u, v) = D is diametral. We fix one such pair and abbreviate mi =

    In the following statement we reformulate the notion of collinearity, in which one
vertex of the triple is singled out as an apex. It says that a triple is collinear precisely
when the distances from the apex to the other two vertices either add up to, or differ by,
the distance between these other two vertices. Part (i) of the next statement is referred
to as the apex criterion.

                                              8
Lemma 11 Let G be a 2-connected graph of diameter D, let (u, v) be a diametral pair
and mi = |Mi (u)|, and let z, w, w′ be three pairwise distinct vertices of G. The following
statements hold:

  (i) denoting a = d(z, w), b = d(z, w′ ) and r = d(w, w′ ), the triple {z, w, w′ } is collinear
      if and only if a + b = r or |a − b| = r;
 (ii) if w and w′ belong to a level Mi (u) with 2i > D, then the pair {w, w′ } is u-good and
      the triple {u, w, w′ } is not collinear;
(iii) we have mi ≥ 2 for 1 ≤ i ≤ D − 1.

Proof. (i) The three betweenness relations read: z lies between w and w′ if and only if
a + b = r; w lies between z and w′ if and only if a + r = b; and w′ lies between z and w
if and only if b + r = a. The last two together say |a − b| = r.
    (ii) Here a = b = i. So by (i) the triple is collinear if and only if d(w, w′ ) = 2i or
d(w, w′ ) = 0. The first fails because d(w, w′ ) ≤ D < 2i, the second because w ̸= w′ .
    (iii) If mi = 1, where 1 ≤ i ≤ D − 1, then the single vertex in Mi (u) separates u from
v, since an edge changes the distance from u by at most one, so every u–v path meets
every level Mi (u), 1 ≤ i ≤ D − 1. This contradicts the 2-connectivity of G.

    As in the case of graphs with a cut vertex, we bound the number of non-collinear
triples. Let M0 (u) = {u}. We consider the following four types of triples:

  τ1 : Triples where all three vertices are from one level, namely the middle one MD/2 (u),
       where D is even.
  τ2 : Triples {x, w, w′ }, where w, w′ ∈ Mi (u) for some 1 ≤ i ≤ D and x ∈ {u, v} \ Mi (u).
       In particular, if i = D then x = u.
  τ3 : Triples containing two vertices w, w′ of a common level and a vertex z from a different
       level, z ̸∈ {u, v}.
  τ4 : Triples of vertices from three distinct levels Mi (u), 0 ≤ i ≤ D.

    Notice that a triple of type τ4 has no two vertices in a common level, while a triple
of type τ1 has all three. The triples of types τ2 and τ3 differ in whether the third vertex
belongs to {u, v}. Hence the types are pairwise disjoint, and we count only these triples;
for instance, three vertices of a level other than the middle one have no type. First we
bound the number of non-collinear triples of type τ1 and τ2 , then the number of those of
type τ3 and τ4 .

Problem 17 Determine the maximum interval index of a graph on n vertices without
pendant vertices for the remaining values 8 ≤ n ≤ 30. In particular:

 (a) decide whether the bound noncol(G) ≥ 2(n − 4) of Theorem 15 holds for every
     2-connected graph G of diameter at least 4 on 8 ≤ n ≤ 30 vertices;

 (b) determine the values n ≥ 9 for which the dumbbell Dn is the unique graph without
     pendant vertices which maximizes the interval index.

     In (a) only 2-connected graphs of diameter at least 4 have to be considered. Namely,
for graphs with a cut vertex the bound of Theorem 9 holds for every n, and it is attained
by Dn for n ≥ 8. For graphs of diameter at most 3 the bound holds for every n ≥ 8 by
Theorem 10. The threshold n ≥ 31 comes from the estimates in the proof of Theorem 15,
i.e. the 2-connected case of diameter at least 4.
     In (b) the value n = 8 is excluded, since for n = 8 the dumbbell is not the only
graph with 2(n − 4) = 8 non-collinear triples. Namely, a triple of the complete bipartite
graph Ka,b is non-collinear

                   a    b
