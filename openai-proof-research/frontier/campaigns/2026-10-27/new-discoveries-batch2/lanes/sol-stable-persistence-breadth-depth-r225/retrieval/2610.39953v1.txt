                                                            On self-similar random quotients of the line
                                                                     Johannes Bäumler∗                 Tom Hutchcroft†

                                                     Abstract. A surprisingly wide variety of important random fractal metric
                                                     spaces in modern probability theory can be viewed as self-similar random
                                                     quotients of the real line, including the continuum random tree, Liouville
                                                     quantum gravity surfaces, the trace of Brownian motion in Rd , and self-similar
                                                     (s = 2d) long-range percolation on R. We prove that the metric and resistance
                                                     growth dimensions are well-defined in a certain sense for every random metric
                                                     space in a class containing all of the above examples. Our results are new for
                                                     the trace of Brownian motion.

                                           1        Introduction

                                           1.1      Renormalization of distances in self-similar random quotients of the real line

                                           In this paper we study the metric and spectral properties of self-similar random quotients
                                           of the real line. Such a random quotient is defined topologically by its set of identification
                                           points. Let Ω denote the set of unordered pairs of distinct elements of R. We are primarily
                                           interested in random closed1 subsets X of Ω satisfying the following axioms:

                                               (I) Independence of non-overlapping intervals: For each a1 ≤ b1 ≤ a2 ≤ b2 ≤
                                                   · · · ≤ an ≤ bn , the sets ({{x, y} ∈ X : ai ≤ x, y ≤ bi })ni=1 are independent.

                                            (T) Translation invariance: The random set a + X := {{x + a, y + a} : {x, y} ∈ X }
                                                has the same law as X for each a ∈ R.

                                               (S) Scale invariance: The random set λX = {{λx, λy} : {x, y} ∈ X } has the same
                                                   distribution as X for every λ > 0.

                                           Note that we do not require X to be an equivalence relation, and passing to the transitive
                                           closure of X may break the independence assumption (I).
                                               A surprisingly large array of important random fractal metric spaces2 in modern prob-
                                           ability can be interpreted as quotients R/X or [0, 1]/X for random sets satisfying the
                                           axioms above. Note that each of these examples is endowed with a canonical choice of
                                           space filling curve defined from its quotient structure.

1.2   Effective resistances

We now discuss extensions of our main theorems to effective resistances. Here we recall
that the effective resistance between two vertices u and v in a finite graph G is defined by

                Reff (u ↔ v; G)−1 = deg(u)Pu (hit v before returning to u),

where Pu denotes the law of the simple random walk on G started at u. Equivalent
expressions for the effective resistance of a more metric-geometric flavour are recalled in
Section 3. Understanding the effective resistance is usually the first step towards under-
standing the behaviour of random walk on (random) fractals; see [41] for an introduction
and [46] for the most general results in the setting of random graphs.
    We formulate our effective resistance results only for the block graph, with the large
number of parallel edges causing problems with the other formulation. Given the process
X ⊆ Ω and the associated block graphs GI as defined above, we define RI (x, y) to be
the effective resistance between x and y in the block graph GI . As before, we also write
R[0,n] to denote this quantity in the case I = {0, . . . , n − 1}. Our results on the effective
resistance will require the following mild additional hypothesis:

 (L) For each ε > 0, the maximal degree of G[0,n] is bounded by nε with probability
     tending to 1 as n → ∞.

It is easily proven that the trace of Brownian motion satisfies (L) in two and three di-
mensions as we prove in Section 3.4. The assumption (L) does not hold for the trace of
the α-stable Lévy process in one dimension with 1 < α ≤ 2 or for the α-stable tree with
α < 2.

1.3   Counterexamples to stronger statements

We now discuss counterexamples to some natural strengthenings of our main theorems.
Failure of tightness. Given the statements of Theorems 1.1 and 1.2, it is natural to
wonder whether the relevant distances are the correct order with high probability. The
following example shows that this does not follow from assumptions (I), (T), and (S).
Consider a Poisson process X on Ω with intensity β|x − y|−2 , and let X ∗ be the set of
pairs s < t such that there exists a pair u < v in X such that u ≤ s ≤ (v + u)/2 ≤ t ≤ v.
We call this the bow-tie process. It is easily verified that this process X ∗ satisfies the
three axioms (I), (T), and (S). Moreover, if β < 1 then the expected number of cut points
of G[0,n] , i.e. vertices i for which there is no edge {a, b} with a < i < b, grows polynomially
in n for both X and X ∗ (indeed, i is a cut point for X with probability ((i + 1)(n − i)/n)−β
and for X ∗ with probability 2−β ((i + 1)(n − i))−β/2 , both of which sum to order n1−β ; a
second moment calculation shows that the number of cut points has this order this good
probability also), which implies that the chemical distance exponent θ associated to X ∗
must be positive. On the other hand, with positive probability there exists an arc of X
covering the entire interval [0, n] whose midpoint lies in [0, n], in which case the diameter
of the block graph G[0,n] associated to X ∗ is at most 2. This example can be made less
degenerate by filling the interior of each arc with a bipartite variant of the binary search
example below instead of the complete bipartite graph.
Small distances. The set of all pairs of distinct points in R satisfies (I), (T), and (S)
and is easily seen to satisfy the conclusions of Theorems 1.1 and 1.2 with θ = 0, with the
associated block graphs GI being complete graphs. We now show that it is possible for θ
to be equal to zero in much less degenerate examples than this, with the associated graphs
not only being sparse but having exponential tails on their degrees. (Note that θres = 0
is rather more common, and holds for e.g. the mating of trees example [37].) Let D be a
Poisson process on R × (0, ∞) whose intensity has density g(x, y) = y −2 with respect to
Lebesgue, and define X to be the set of pairs u < v in R for which there exist a, b ∈ (0, ∞)
such that (u, a) and (v, b) both belong to D and there does not exist a point (w, c) of D
with w between u and v and c > min{a, b}. It is easily verified that X satisfies (I), (T),
and (S). We claim the associated metric has θ = 0 and that the associated graph GZ has
finite degrees, with the probability that the origin has degree at least k being exponentially
small in k. We will see that this random graph encodes a random binary search of the
interval.

the point of D with the largest second coordinate among those points with first coordinate
in [0, n], this point being well-defined since the density g(x, y) = y −2 gives finite measure

1.4    Exponent values and related literature

We now briefly survey what is known regarding the values of the exponents whose existence
is proven here. In each case, we can bound distances from below by the number of cut
points of G[0,n] (as defined in Section 1.3), with the relevant exponent often being easier to
understand than the corresponding chemical distance and effective resistance exponents.
The same lower bound holds for effective resistances up to a factor of the maximal degree,
since consecutive cut points are joined in series and the effective resistance between two
distinct vertices is at least the reciprocal of the degree of either one. Heuristically, θ should
be the reciprocal of the dimension of the limiting metric space, which is known in several
of our examples, but we do not justify this rigorously for every example we consider. We
also report the outcome of simulations estimating the exponents in Figure 3.
Brownian motion. For d = 1 it is not hard to see that θ = 1/2, with the random met-

2.2   The NBU property

In this section we prove a tail estimate for the random variables Γ(n) and γ(t) that
will play a crucial role in the proof of our main quasisubmultiplicative estimate in the
next section. Recall that a non-negative random variable X is said to have the new
better than used (NBU) property if P(X ≥ a + b) ≤ P(X ≥ a)P(X ≥ b) for every
a, b ≥ 0. We say that X has the integer NBU property if it is integer-valued and satisfies
P(X ≥ a + b) ≤ P(X ≥ a)P(X ≥ b) for all integers a, b ≥ 0.

Lemma 2.3. If X satisfies (I) and (T) then γ(t) has the NBU property for every t ≥ 0
and Γ(n) has the integer NBU property for every integer n ≥ 0.

    In order to prove this lemma we first prove a strong Markov property for X . For each
s ≥ 0 let Fs be the σ-algebra generated by the pairs in X with both endpoints in [0, s], and
let Xs = {{x − s, y − s} : {x, y} ∈ X , x, y ≥ s}. We say that a random variable τ taking
values in [0, ∞] is a stopping time for the filtration (Fs )s≥0 if {τ ≤ s} is measurable
with respect to Fs for every s ≥ 0.

Lemma 2.4 (Strong Markov property). If X satisfies (I) and (T) and τ is a stopping
time taking values in a countable set S, then on the event {τ < ∞} the random set Xτ is
independent of τ and has the same law as X0 .

Proof of Lemma 2.4. Sum over the possible values s ∈ S of τ , using that {τ = s} ∈ Fs
(since S is countable) and that Xs is independent of Fs by (I) and has the law of X0 by
(T) for each s ∈ S.

define τ to be the infimal value of t such that the restriction of X to [0, t] is non-trivial then
Xτ does not have the same distribution as X0 as it includes the pair {0, 1} with positive
probability.

3     Effective resistances

In this section we prove our theorem concerning the existence of the growth exponent
for the effective resistance, Theorem 1.5. We begin by introducing the vertex extremal
length (VEL) and explaining its relation to the effective resistance in Section 3.1. We then
adapt the argument of Section 2 to the VEL in Sections 3.2 and 3.3; this implies that the
effective resistance exponent is well-defined when (L) holds since the effective resistance
and the VEL are equivalent up to a factor of the maximum degree. Finally, in Section 3.4
we prove that (L) holds for the trace of Brownian motion in two and three dimensions.
