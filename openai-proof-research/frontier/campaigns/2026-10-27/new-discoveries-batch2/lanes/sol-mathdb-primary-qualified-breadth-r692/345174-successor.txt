                                             A counterexample to a question on the maximality of purely-primes


                                                                               Abolfazl Tarizadeh


                                                   Abstract. By using an advanced model of AI, it is proved that there exists
                                                   a commutative ring having a purely-prime ideal which is not purely-maximal.
                                                   This gives a counterexample to [3, Conjecture 5.8].




arXiv:2608.14251v1 [math.AC] 14 Aug 2026
                                                                             1. Introduction
                                              It can be seen that every purely-maximal ideal is always purely-prime. The
                                           converse is also true for several important classes of rings. For instance, every
                                           purely-prime ideal of a Gelfand ring is purely-maximal [2, Chap. 8, Proposition
                                           37]. Every purely-prime ideal of a reduced mp-ring is purely-maximal [1, Theorem
                                           3.5] or [3, Theorem 5.5]. Also, every purely-prime ideal of a Noetherian ring is
                                           purely-maximal (Corollary 2.3).
                                              However in [2, Chap. 7, Ex. 36], a non-commutative ring is constructed which
                                           has a two-sided maximal ideal whose pure part is not a purely-maximal ideal. This
                                           shows that there are non-commutative rings having purely-prime ideals which are
                                           not purely-maximal.
                                              But finding a “commutative ring” having a purely-prime ideal which is not
                                           purely-maximal was not an easy task at all. This led us to raise it as a conjec-
                                           ture in [3, Conjecture 5.8]. But during these few years, no progress was made in
                                           resolving this problem. However, after the emergence and availability of artificial
                                           intelligence, by using an advanced model of AI (ChatGPT Pro), a counterexample
                                           is found to this conjecture (Theorem 2.4). As we previously predicted in [3] the
                                           high probability of such a counterexample, this beautiful counterexample is very
                                           natural and at the same time very far-reaching.

   The extension of a pure ideal under any ring map is a pure ideal. This follows
from the fact that a flat ring map is stable under any base change. As an alternative
proof, it also follows from Remark 2.1.
   In an integral domain, the only pure ideals are the zero ideal and the whole ring.
The same statement holds for local rings.
   If I is a pure ideal of R, then for any ideal J of R we have IJ = I ∩ J. In
particular, every pure ideal is idempotent, i.e., I = I 2 .
   By purely-prime ideal of R we mean a proper pure ideal I of R such that if J
and J ′ are pure ideals of R with JJ ′ ⊆ I then J ⊆ I or J ′ ⊆ I. Similarly, by a
purely-maximal ideal of R we mean a proper pure ideal I of R such that it is a
maximal element in the set of proper pure ideals of R.
   In a ring R, by primitive idempotent we mean a nonzero idempotent e ∈ R such
that if ee′ = e′ for some nonzero idempotent e′ ∈ R, then e = e′ .
   By Remark 2.1, every ideal is generated by a set of idempotent elements is a
pure ideal. Every finitely generated pure ideal is also generated by an idempotent
element (this follows from a Nakayama argument). In particular, every Noetherian
ring R has finitely many pure ideals and they are exactly of the form Re where e
is an idempotent of R. This leads us to the following result:
Theorem 2.2. The purely-prime ideals of a Noetherian ring R are exactly of the
form R(1 − e) where e is a primitive idempotent of R.
Proof. Let I be a purely-prime ideal of R. Then, by the above observation, I = Rf
for some idempotent f ∈ R. We show that e := 1 − f is a primitive idempotent.
Since I ̸= R, the idempotent e is nonzero. Suppose there exists a nonzero idem-
potent e′ ∈ R such that ee′ = e′ but e ̸= e′ . Then e′′ := e − e′ is also a nonzero
idempotent. We have e′ e′′ = 0. Then (Re′ )(Re′′ ) = Re′ e′′ = 0 ⊆ R(1 − e) = Rf .
Since I = Rf is purely-prime, Re′ ⊆ R(1 − e) or Re′′ ⊆ R(1 − e). If Re′ ⊆ R(1 − e)
then e′ = e′ (1 − e) = 0 which is a contradiction. If Re′′ ⊆ R(1 − e) then e′′ = 0
which is again a contradiction. Therefore, e is a primitive idempotent of R.
Conversely, let e be a primitive idempotent of R, we show that R(1 − e) is a purely-
prime ideal of R. We have R(1 − e) ̸= R, because e ̸= 0. Thus R(1 − e) is a proper
pure ideal of R. Let I and J be pure ideals of R with IJ ⊆ R(1 − e). By the above
observation, there are idempotents f, g ∈ R such that I = Rf and J = Rg. Then
f g ∈ R(1 − e) and so ef g = 0. But ef is an idempotent and e(ef ) = ef . The
primitivity of e implies ef = 0 or e = ef . Likewise, eg = 0 or e = eg. If e = ef
then eg = ef g = 0. Thus ef = 0 or eg = 0. If ef = 0 then f = (1 − e)f so
Rf ⊆ R(1 − e). If eg = 0 then Rg ⊆ R(1 − e). Therefore, R(1 − e) is a purely-prime
ideal of R.                                                                       □
Corollary 2.3. Every purely-prime ideal of a Noetherian ring is purely-maximal.
Proof. Let I be a purely-prime ideal of a Noetherian ring R. By Theorem 2.2,
I = R(1 − e) where e is a primitive idempotent of R. Let J be a proper pure
ideal of R such that I ⊆ J. Then J = Re′ where e′ is an idempotent of R with
f := 1 − e′ ̸= 0. We have 1 − e = re′ . Then f = ef . It follows that e = f , because
e is primitive. So 1 − e = e′ . Thus I = J. Hence, I is purely-maximal.            □
   The above result shows that to find a purely-prime ideal that is not purely-
maximal, we need to investigate it in non-Noetherian rings. The following result
provides that desired example:

Theorem 2.4. There exists a commutative ring having a purely-prime ideal which
is not purely-maximal.

Proof. Let k be a field or at least an integral domain and consider the polynomial
ring k[X1 , X2 , X3 , . . .] modulo the ideal J which is generated by all elements of the
form Xi (1 − Xj ) with i < j. We denote this ring by R := k[X1 , X2 , X3 , . . .]/J and
we write xi for the image of Xi in R (under the natural ring map). Then in R we
have xi = xi xj for all i < j. So by Remark 2.1, I := (x1 , x2 , x3 , . . .) is a pure ideal
of R.
We will show that the pure ideals of R are exactly the zero ideal, the whole ring R
and I, and consequently, the zero ideal is purely-prime but not purely-maximal.
To achieve this goal, we first need to make some preparations. By the univer-
sal property of polynomial rings, we have a surjective morphism of k-algebras
k[X1 , X2 , X3 , . . .] → k which maps each Xi to 0. Since, J is contained in the
kernel of this map, we get a surjective morphism of k-algebras ϵ : R → k such
that ϵ(xi ) = 0 for all i. Similarly to the above, for each n ⩾ 1 we get a surjective
morphism of k-algebras πn : R → k[t] such that:

Now let L be a nonzero pure ideal of R. For each n, the image πn (L) is a pure
ideal of k[t]. Since k[t] is an integral domain, its only pure ideals are the zero ideal
and k[t], therefore πn (L) ∈ {0, k[t]} for all n. But these images have the same
status, i.e., all images are 0, or all images are k[t]. Indeed, suppose πn (L) = k[t]
for some n. Choose f ∈ L with πn (f ) = 1. Then πn+1 (f )(1) = πn (f )(0) = 1.

0 = πn (f ) = ϵ(f ) ̸= 0 which is a contradiction. Therefore, πn (L) = k[t] for all n.
