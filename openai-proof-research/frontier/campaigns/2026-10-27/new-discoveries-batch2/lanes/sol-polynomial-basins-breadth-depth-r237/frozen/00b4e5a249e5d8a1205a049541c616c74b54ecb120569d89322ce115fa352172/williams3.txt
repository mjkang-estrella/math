                                                      Abstract
         We give an elementary argument to show that games of Planted Brussels Sprouts
      are in bijection with factorizations of the long cycle. This simplifies recent work of Ji
      and Propp. We also address a centrally symmetric version of the game.


1     Planted Brussels Sprouts and factorizations of the
      long cycle
1.1    Planted Brussels Sprouts
Ji and Propp recently analyzed the number of ways to “play” a two-player game called
Planted Brussels Sprouts [10]. The game takes place in a disk whose boundary is regularly
marked with n+1 ≥ 1 distinct points, which we will label clockwise by distinct numbers
ℓ = (ℓ1 , . . . , ℓn+1 ). Players take turns drawing arcs in the disk that connect two labelled
points without passing through any other arcs. These two points are then stripped of their
labels, a short line segment is drawn transversely to the new arc, and the stripped labels are
assigned to the end points of this short segment so that the new labels appear clockwise of
the old labels. See Figure 1 for an example.
    It is clear that a game of Planted Brussels Sprouts is uniquely determined by its sequence
of stripped labels (i1 j1 ) · · · (in jn ) (where ik < jk are the labels stripped on the kth move),
and we write Play(ℓ) for the set of all such sequences. Since no player draws an arc for the
disk marked with a single point, we take the convention that Play(ℓ) = ∅ when n = 0 and
ℓ = (ℓ1 ).

   Proposition 4 gives a direct statement of the bijection induced by Eq. (2).

Proposition 4 ([10, Theorem 5]). Thinking of an ordered list of stripped labels as a product
of transpositions gives a bijection Play(ℓ) ≃ Red(ℓ).

Proof. We describe the bijection from Play(ℓ) to Red(ℓ) geometrically, using the visualiza-
tions of both sets as in Figures 1 and 2.
    Consider the following modification of a game of Planted Brussels Sprouts. As with
a visualization of a factorization, our modified game will eliminate the transverse short

                                              4
segments drawn during a game of Planted Brussels Sprouts and only draw arcs between the
original n + 1 vertices on the boundary of the disk.
    Define a boundary segment to be a line connecting a boundary vertex to a point nearby
in the interior of the disk; the label of the boundary vertex is transferred to the interior
point. Before the game starts, replace each labelled boundary vertex by a boundary segment
(see Figure 3). Each arc in our modified game of Planted Brussels Sprouts will be drawn to
connect two (labelled) interior vertices of boundary segments, which can be seen as an arc
connecting the two original boundary vertices.
    In this modified game, we no longer draw short segments transversely to new arcs. Each
time an arc connects to a boundary segment, we duplicate that boundary segment so that its
boundary vertex remains the same, but so that the new boundary segment lies counterclock-
wise of the old. Since replacing the transverse short segments by boundary segments does
not change the topology (and, in particular, which arcs can be drawn later), our modified
game is equivalent to Planted Brussels Sprouts.
    Furthermore, since new boundary segments lie counterclockwise of the old, cycles are
forbidden and the resulting graph is a tree. Moreover, the order in which the arcs are drawn
satisfy the property that they decrease when read clockwise around a boundary vertex. We
conclude from Theorem 1 that (discarding the remaining boundary segments not attached
to arcs) we have produced a visualization of a factorization of the long cycle from a game of
Planted Brussels Sprouts.
    The inverse bijection is similar: we now work to replace the boundary segments with
the transverse short segments of Planted Brussels Sprouts at each step. Start with a tree
satisfying Theorem 1. For each boundary vertex, add a boundary segment that lies counter-
clockwise of all arcs emanating from that vertex. Now in the reverse order of the labels of
the arcs, for each arc e, slide the ends of the arcs or boundary segments lying immediately
counterclockwise of e so that they meet in the middle of e.
    The bijection in the language of stripped labels is now immediate from the preceding
geometric description.

4    Factorizations of Coxeter elements
The symmetric group Sn+1 is an example of a finite Coxeter group W , and many properties of
the symmetric group carry over to these more general groups. Just as Sn+1 has a presentation
using the n simple transpositions {(i, i + 1)}1≤i≤n , a Coxeter group W has a presentation
using an n element set S called simple reflections. Similarly, a long cycle ℓ ∈ Sn+1 is a
(conjugate of a) product of the simple transpositions. A (conjugate of a) product of the
simple reflections in a Coxeter group W is called a Coxeter element (written c), whose order
is denoted h (so that ch is the identity element of W ).

Figure 4: Ignoring the labels in a visualization of a factorization of the long cycle gives

could have come from any of the three factorizations (13)(35)(12)(34), (13)(12)(35)(34),
or (13)(35)(34)(12).

    There is a long-studied generalization of this story of reduced factorizations to a finite
Coxeter group W and a Coxeter element—a 1974 letter from Deligne to Looijenga (written
after conversations with Tits and Zagier!) using properties of bipartite Coxeter elements to
establish a beautiful recurrence on the set Red(c) of factorizations of the Coxeter element c
into reflections [4], which allows for a simple case-by-case proof of the uniform formula

The same recurrence was independently rediscovered and refined by Reading in 2007 [15],
by analogy to methods he developed to model finite-type cluster algebras using sortable
elements. In the case of the symmetric group Sn+1 , the recurrence is exactly Eq. (3).
    More recently, Chapuy and Stump used a standard representation-theoretic technique
(the sum of all reflections is in the center of the group algebra) due to Frobenius to give
a beautiful extension of Eq. (4) to all well-generated complex reflection groups in 2012 [3].
Chapuy and Stump’s work inspired Michel in 2014 to give the first uniform proof of Eq. (4)
for Weyl groups using properties of Deligne-Lusztig representations [11].
    Note also that representation theorists study factorizations of Coxeter elements under
the name of exceptional sequences, associated to quivers of Dynkin type.


5    Centrally symmetric Brussels Sprouts
As an illustration of the generality suggested by Section 4, we give a brief account of the
centrally symmetric version of Planted Brussels Sprouts.




                                              7
5.1     Centrally symmetric Brussels Sprouts
The game is now played in a disk whose boundary is marked with 2n ≥ 2 distinct points
labelled clockwise (ℓ1 , . . . , ℓn , ℓ1 , . . . , ℓn ). A move now consists of drawing an arc connecting
two labelled points without passing through any other arcs, while simultaneously drawing
a second arc (if necessary) to maintain a half-turn rotational symmetry of the diagram. As
before, the connected points are stripped of their labels, a short segment is drawn transversely
to the new arc, and the stripped labels are assigned clockwise to the end points of the new
short segments. See Figure 5 for an example.
      Again, the game is uniquely determined by its sequence of stripped labels—we abbreviate
the central symmetry by writing ((ℓi , ℓj )) for the pair (ℓi , ℓj )(ℓi , ℓj ), and ((ℓi , ℓj )) for the pair
(ℓi , ℓj )(ℓi , ℓj ). We write PlayB (ℓ) for the set of all such sequences.

5.2     Factorizations of a Coxeter element
The Coxeter group of type Bn (which we shall simply denote as Bn ) has a permutation
representation on the set {1, 2, . . . , n, 1, 2, . . . , n}. In this representation (continuing the
notation from the previous section), the reflections of the group are
                                                                        
         T = {((ij)) : 1 ≤ i < j ≤ n} ⊔ ((ij)) : 1 ≤ i < j ≤ n ⊔ (ii) : 1 ≤ i ≤ n .

A type Bn reduced factorization of the cycle (ℓ1 , . . . , ℓn , ℓ1 , . . . , ℓn ) is an expression for that
element as a product of n reflections. We write RedB (ℓ) for the set of all such factorizations.
As in the symmetric group, we can visualize a factorization by drawing arcs in the same
initial disk we used in centrally symmetric Planted Brussels Sprouts. An example is given
in Figure 6.
