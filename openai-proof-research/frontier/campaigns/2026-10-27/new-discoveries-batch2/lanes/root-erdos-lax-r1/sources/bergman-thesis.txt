                                    Abstract
                                    This thesis investigates subspaces of the Hardy space, especially ones defined via analytic continuation, and their
                                    applications to operator theory. The main function theoretic properties investigated are the distribution of zeros
                                    and extremal functions. The operator theory investigations are centered around the concept of invariant subspaces
                                    and cyclic vectors.

The model theory of operators

1.1    Introduction

The basic idea in the model theory of operators is the following. One starts with a
collection of operators each defined on a vector space (which may depend on the op-
erator). New vector spaces and a collection of bijective linear transformations from
the first vector spaces to the second vector spaces are defined. Next one identifies the
image of the operator under the bijective transformations. These are the model oper-
ators.


The program as formulated makes little sense since the operators could have nothing
to do with each other. In practice one chooses operators, transformations, and vector
spaces that share certain common features. The goal is typically to transform the given
operators to certain canonical operators with simpler algebraic structure. Instead of
trying to give an overly formal definition, we present some illustrative examples.

Example 1. The operators considered are bounded self-adjoint operators on a Hilbert
space with one-dimensional spectrum. The model operators are the operators of mul-
tiplication by independent variable on L2 (R, µ). The bijective linear transformations
are the unitary operators diagonalizing a given self-adjoint operator given by the spec-
tral theorem.
Example 2. The Hilbert space is ℓ2 (Z+ ). The operator is the shift operator S. The
model operator is multiplication by the independent variable z on the Hilbert space
H 2 consisting of analytic power series in the unit disc with square

Once the program has been formulated it is possible to work on one aspect without
direct mention of the others. In particular, it is often the case that the space on which
the model operator acts has a complicated or indirect definition. This is not too sur-
prising since one has simplified the algebra involved and thus must pay a price. Of
the articles below only paper I and paper V deal with operators directly and only in
paper V is the model explicitly constructed.


In this thesis the vector spaces which the model operators act on will be spaces of
holomorphic functions. In this way, the typical questions of operator theory, such as
understanding the spectrum, cyclic vectors, invariant subspaces, and completeness of
eigenfunctions, will be transformed into problems on the existence or non-existence
of holomorphic functions with certain prescribed values, for example, zero sets and
interpolation. These problems are the typical bread and butter of complex analysis
and if one is satisfied with merely holomorphic solutions then these questions can
be resolved using the classical theorems of Weierstrass and Mittag-Leffler. However,
the requirement that the function should also be an element of an appropriate vector
space causes serious problems and must be dealt with on a case by case basis. This
motivates a detailed study of the spaces involved.


In this thesis the best demonstration of this program is paper V which carries it out
in full. A class of operators is abstractly defined and model operators are constructed
for them. Then deep theorems on the holomorphic function spaces arising in the
construction of the model operators are used to describe the invariant subspaces of
the operators.


Before we begin our analysis we wish to motivate the spaces of functions considered
and in particular the simple geometry of the domain. To do this let us consider the
Hilbert space case. Since all (separable) Hilbert spaces are unitarily equivalent there
is a lot of freedom when choosing the vector space and our goal is to look for the


                                           4
simplest possible representation. The uniformization theorem tells us that there are
only three possible choices for a simply connected Riemann surface. The sphere, S 2 ,
the unit disc, D, and the complex plane C. The only holomorphic maps from the
sphere to the unit disc or plane are constants and the holomorphic maps from the
sphere to itself are rational functions. Although, the case of rational and, in particu-
lar, polynomial mappings is also interesting we shall not consider it seriously in this
treatise. Instead we focus on the case of the unit disc and plane which offer a greater
variety of possibilities.

1.3    Meromorphic inner functions

As we have seen inner functions may be factorized as a product of a singular inner
factor and a Blaschke product,
                                  θ(z) = B(z)Sµ (z).
The spectrum, σ(θ), of an inner function is, by definition, the subset of the unit circle
where θ does not extend analytically. It is equal to the union of the accumulation
points of the zeros of the Blaschke product and the closed support of the singular
measure µ. Thus it is void if and only if θ is a finite Blaschke product. This case is too
simple and thus we consider the next simplest case when σ(θ) consists of one point
which we fix to be one,
                                    σ(θ) = {1} .
Thus θ is the product of a Blaschke product whose zeros accumulate only at the point
one and the singular inner factor with the point measure µ = aδ1 . It turns out that
in this case all functions in Kθ extend to meromorphic functions on the Riemann

and consider meromorphic functions defined on the plane instead. Doing this we are

functions, but sometimes offer a better perspective. They are defined by a symbol E
which is a Hermite-Biehler function, i.e. an entire function satisfying
