                                                  A REVERSE ENTROPY POWER INEQUALITY FOR I.I.D.
                                                         LOG-CONCAVE RANDOM VARIABLES

                                                                              ZHEN FU, AND JIANGE LI

                                                  Abstract. We show that h∞ (X + Y ) ≤ h∞ (Z + W ), where X, Y are independent log-
                                                  concave random variables, and Z, W are exponential random variables having the same
                                                  respective ∞-Rényi entropies. Analogs for integer-valued monotone log-concave random
                                                  variables are also obtained. Our main tools are decreasing rearrangement, majorization,
                                                  and the change of measure.

                                           The celebrated entropy power inequality (or EPI for short) of Shannon [18] (and Stam
                                           [20]) states that for independent random vectors X and Y in Rd such that the entropies
                                           of X, Y , and X + Y exist, it holds that
                                                                               2               2          2
                                                                             e d h(X+Y ) ≥ e d h(X) + e d h(Y ) ,                           (1.1)
                                           where equality holds if and only if X and Y are Gaussian random vectors with proportional
                                           covariance matrices. The EPI found its original applications in the study of channel
                                           capacity in information theory. It has been known very well that EPI is closely related
                                           to geometric and functional inequalities in a variety of mathematical fields, maybe most
                                           notably the Brunn-Minkowski inequality (or BMI for short) in convex geometry. We refer
                                           the interested reader to [7, 12] for profound connections among EPI, BMI, and many
                                           other geometric and functional inequalities.
                                             Convexity (or concavity) underpins the validity of the reversal of many geometric and
                                           functional inequalities. A function f : Rd → R+ is called log-concave if it can be written
                                           as f (x) = e−V (x) , where V : Rd → R ∪ {∞} is a convex function. We say that a random
                                           vector X taking values in Rd is log-concave provided that its density is a log-concave
                                           function. (We also say that X has log-concave distribution). The class of log-concave
                                           functions can be thought of as functional analogs of convex bodies. They are prominent
                                           objects studied in convex geometry, high dimensional probability and statistics, as well
                                           as theoretical computer science. The class of log-concave distributions include a wide
                                           range of important probability distributions, such as the Gaussian distributions, uniform
                                           distributions on convex bodies, and exponential distributions.
                                             The reversal of EPI was first addressed by Bobkov and Madiman [3]. They showed
                                           that for independent log-concave random vectors, under linear volume preserving maps,

inequality (1.1) can be reversed at the cost of a constant factor on the r.h.s of the in-
equality. This can be thought of as a functional analog of Milman’s reverse BMI for
convex bodies [13]. It is necessary to place log-concave random vectors “at right posi-
tions” via linear volume preserving maps for the reversed EPI to hold. We are interested
in the sharp reverse EPI for independent and identically distributed (or i.i.d. for short)
log-concave random vectors. In this case, linear volume preserving maps are not needed
and the question can be phrased as follows.
Conjecture 1.1 (Folklore). Let X and Y be i.i.d. log-concave random vectors taking
values in Rd . The entropy increment h(X + Y ) − h(X) is maximized when X and Y have
exponential distributions.
   Clearly, the entropy increment is affine invariant. An earlier result of Cover and Zhang
[6] shows that log-concavity and identical distribution (without independence between X
and Y ) yield the upper bound d log 2 of the entropy increment. Huang, Slomka, Tkocz,
and Vritsiou ([10], Section 6) observed that the improvement of Cover and Zhang’s bound
for Conjecture 1.1 will lead to important progress on Hadwiger’s covering problem. Ball,
Nayar, and Tkocz [2] studied the entropy increment for a two dimensional log-concave
random vector (X, Y ), whose marginals have identical differential entropy (but not nec-
essarily independent). This variant of reverse EPI and its generalizations are entropic
analogs of Busemann’s convexity theorem of intersection bodies [4] and the convexity of
p-cross-section bodies conjectured by Gardner and Giannopoulos [8] (the end of Section
5, and the discussion in [11]).
   Aforementioned forward and reverse EPIs have been studied for general Rényi entropies.

   It is suspected that a transition will occur in the increment of Rényi entropy. More
precisely, there exists an absolute constant p0 ∈ (0, 1) such that hp (X + Y ) − hp (X)
is maximized by exponential random vectors for p ≥ p0 , and is maximized by uniform
random vectors taking values in the cube [0, 1]d for 0 < p ≤ p0 . It is perhaps worth to
point out that the increment of Rényi entropy of order p > 1 (respectively, 0 < p < 1)
boils down to the minimization (respectively, maximization) of ∥f ∗ f ∥p /∥f ∥p over the
class of integrable log-concave functions. This can be thought of as a special type of
reverse Young’s convolution inequality. Our main result reads as follows.
Theorem 1.2. Let X and Y be independent real-valued log-concave random variables.
Let Z and W be independent exponential random variables such that h∞ (Z) = h∞ (X)
and h∞ (W ) = h∞ (Y ). We have
                                 h∞ (X + Y ) ≤ h∞ (Z + W ).
A discrete analog holds for integer-valued monotone log-concave random variables.
Remark 1.3. If X1 , · · · , Xn are independent real-valued random variables, and U1 , · · · , Un
are independent uniform random variables such that h∞ (Ui ) = h∞ (Xi ) for i = 1, · · · , n,

Hence, our result can also be seen as a reverse Rogozin type result for two log-concave

  Another related problem is the characterization of log-concave distributions that max-
imize the Rényi entropic increment hp (X − Y ) − hp (X). In this case, it is suspected that
exponential distributions are always maximizers. This resembles the Rogers-Shephard in-
equality in convex geometry [16]. In a certain sense, the symmetry of X − Y (implicitly)
makes this problem more tractable than its counterpart of the X +Y case. Melbourne and
Tkocz ([15], Theorem VI.1) showed that the increment of Rényi entropy of order p ≥ 2 is
indeed maximized by exponential random vectors. A discrete analog was obtained by Mel-
bourne and Palafox-Castillo ([14], Theorem 2.11) for integer-valued monotone log-concave
random variables.

and Y , respectively. As shown in Proposition 2.2, log-concavity is preserved by decreasing
rearrangement, thus fX↓ and fY↓ are also log-concave. Since decreasing rearrangement
preserves the Lp moments, fX↓ and fY↓ are also probability densities such that ∥fX↓ ∥∞ =
∥fX ∥∞ and ∥fY↓ ∥∞ = ∥fY ∥∞ . Let X ↓ and Y ↓ be random variables with densities fX↓ and
fY↓ , respectively. Then we have
                h∞ (X ↓ ) = h∞ (X) = h∞ (Z), h∞ (Y ↓ ) = h∞ (Y ) = h∞ (W ).
By Lemma 3.5, we have
                               h∞ (X + Y ) ≤ h∞ (X ↓ + Y ↓ ).
By Lemma 3.7, we have
                               h∞ (X ↓ + Y ↓ ) ≤ h∞ (Z + W ).
Then we can combine these inequalities to obtain inequality (3.3). Inequality (3.4) follows
from simple calculations.                                                                □
