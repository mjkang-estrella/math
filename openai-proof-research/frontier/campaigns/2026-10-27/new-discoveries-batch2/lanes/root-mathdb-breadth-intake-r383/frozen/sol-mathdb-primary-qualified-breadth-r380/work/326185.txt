                                                      We consider a generalized angle in complex normed vector spaces. Its definition
                                                   corresponds to the definition of the well known Euclidean angle in real inner product
                                                   spaces. Not surprisingly it yields complex values as ‘angles’. This ‘angle’ has some
                                                   simple properties, which are known from the usual angle in real inner product
                                                   spaces. But to do ordinary Euclidean geometry real angles are necessary. We show
                                                   that even in a complex normed space there are many pure real valued ‘angles’. The
                                                   situation improves yet in inner product spaces. There we can use the known theory
                                                   of orthogonal systems to find many pairs of vectors with real angles, and to do
                                                   geometry which is based on the Greeks 2000 years ago.

2 General Definitions
        Let X = (X, τ ) be an arbitrary complex vector space, that means that the vector space
 X is provided with a topology τ such that the addition of two vectors and the multiplication
 with complex numbers are continuous. Further let k · k denote a norm on X, i.e. there is a
 continuous map k · k: X −→ R+ ∪ {0} which fulfils the following axioms kz · ~xk = |z| ·k~xk
 (‘absolute homogeneity’), k~x + ~yk ≤ k~xk + k~y k (‘triangle inequality’), and k~xk = 0 only for
~x = ~0 (‘positive definiteness’), for ~x, ~y ∈ X and z ∈ C.
    Let < . | . > : X 2 −→ C be a continuous map from the product space X × X into the field
 C. Such a map is called a product. We notice the following four conditions.
 (1): For all z ∈ C and ~x, ~y ∈ X it holds < z · ~x | ~y > = z· < ~x | ~y >               (‘homogeneity’),
 (2): for all ~x, ~y ∈ X it holds < ~x | ~y > = < ~y | ~x >                       (‘conjugate symmetry’),
 (3): for ~x ∈ X, ~x 6= ~0 we have a real number < ~x | ~x > > 0,                  (‘positive definiteness’),
 (4): for all ~x, ~y , z~ ∈ X it holds < ~x | ~y + z~ > = < ~x | ~y > + < ~x | z~ >
                                                                   (‘linearity in the second component’).
  If < . | . > fulfils all properties (1), (2), (3), (4), the map < . | . > is an inner product on X.
 We call the pair (X, < . | . >) a complex inner product space.
    It is well known that in a complex normed space (X, k · k) its norm k · k generates an inner
 product < . | . > by equation (1.1) if and only if it holds the following parallelogram identity:
           For ~x, ~y ∈ X we have the equation k~x + ~y k2 + k~x − ~y k2 = 2 · k~xk2 + k~y k2 .

is a product < . | . >: X × X → C. We say that the triple (X, k · k, < . | . >) satisfies the
Cauchy-Schwarz-Bunjakowsky Inequality or CSB inequality if and only if for ~x, ~y ∈ X there is
