TITLE: Truncated exponentials defeat every subunit Rényi threshold

AUTHOR: Research manuscript

DATE: 29 October 2026 · Research draft; independent final review pending

ABSTRACT: For each real order 0 &lt; p &lt; 1, we prove that a finite normalized truncation of the rate-one exponential has a strictly larger iid positive-sum Rényi entropy increment than the rate-one exponential itself. The proof retains the input power integral and uses an exact translated deficit identity followed by dominated convergence. Consequently no threshold strictly below one can have the exponential-maximizing property proposed in the unnumbered paragraph after (1.2) on printed page 2 of Fu–Li [1]. We make no assertion about their Shannon Conjecture 1.1, the cube branch, or actual optimizers. The accompanying source-only certificate proves the literal density comparison for every real subunit order.

H: 1. Statement and source scope

For a probability density f on the real line and p ∈ (0,1), write M<sub>p</sub>(f) = ∫<sub>ℝ</sub> f(x)<super>p</super> dx and h<sub>p</sub>(f) = log M<sub>p</sub>(f)/(1−p). All logarithms are natural. We use these expressions only when the input and output power integrals are finite and strictly positive. Convolution means the literal Lebesgue integral (f*g)(s) = ∫<sub>ℝ</sub> f(t)g(s−t) dt. Thus for independent random variables X,Y with common density f, their positive sum has density f*f, and its increment is Δ<sub>p</sub>(f) = h<sub>p</sub>(f*f)−h<sub>p</sub>(f).

Fu and Li [1, printed p. 2, unnumbered paragraph after (1.2)] suspect a constant p<sub>0</sub> ∈ (0,1) with exponential maximization above the threshold and cube-uniform maximization below it for iid log-concave vectors. Dimension one is already part of that proposal. Its necessary consequence that the rate-one exponential maximizes Δ<sub>p</sub> for p<sub>0</sub> &lt; p &lt; 1 is enough for the contradiction below. This source assertion is distinct from their numbered Shannon Conjecture 1.1.

THEOREM: For every real p with 0 &lt; p &lt; 1, there exists a finite a &gt; 0 such that the log-concave probability density f<sub>a</sub>(x) = c<sub>a</sub>e<super>−x</super>1<sub>[0,a]</sub>(x), where c<sub>a</sub> = (1−e<super>−a</super>)<super>−1</super>, satisfies Δ<sub>p</sub>(f<sub>a</sub>) &gt; Δ<sub>p</sub>(e), where e(x) = e<super>−x</super>1<sub>[0,∞)</sub>(x). All four entropies in this comparison are finite.

The theorem concerns each fixed real order, with a cutoff depending on that order. No uniform cutoff as p tends to one is required or asserted. We compare the displayed rate-one density only; no all-rate theorem is part of this paper or its certificate. We do not classify maximizers or establish any cube-uniform assertion.

H: 2. Literal normalization and convolution

Fix a &gt; 0. Since ∫<sub>0</sub><super>a</super>e<super>−x</super>dx = 1−e<super>−a</super>, the displayed c<sub>a</sub> normalizes f<sub>a</sub> to mass one. Its potential is x−log c<sub>a</sub> on [0,a] and +∞ outside that interval, a convex extended-valued function. Thus f<sub>a</sub> is globally log-concave, including its zero region. Endpoint conventions affect no integral. Taking the product density f<sub>a</sub>(x)f<sub>a</sub>(y) supplies genuinely independent, identically distributed coordinates.

At a sum value s, the overlap interval is [0,a] ∩ [s−a,s]. The integrand on that interval is the constant c<sub>a</sub><super>2</super>e<super>−s</super>. Its length is the nonnegative tent T<sub>a</sub>(s), equal to s for 0 ≤ s ≤ a, to 2a−s for a ≤ s ≤ 2a, and to zero otherwise. Hence, pointwise on the whole line,

EQ: (f<sub>a</sub>*f<sub>a</sub>)(s) = c<sub>a</sub><super>2</super> e<super>−s</super> T<sub>a</sub>(s).                                      (1)

This output is a probability density by Tonelli's theorem applied to the nonnegative product density. Both f<sub>a</sub> and its convolution are bounded and compactly supported, and their p-power integrals are finite and positive for p &gt; 0. For the baseline, direct convolution gives (e*e)(s) = se<super>−s</super> on s ≥ 0 and zero elsewhere. Its positive-order power integral is finite by exponential decay.

Define the strictly positive finite integral J and the truncated tent integral J<sub>a</sub> by

EQ: J = ∫<sub>0</sub><super>∞</super> e<super>−ps</super>s<super>p</super> ds,     J<sub>a</sub> = ∫<sub>0</sub><super>∞</super> e<super>−ps</super>T<sub>a</sub>(s)<super>p</super> ds.                          (2)

In particular J<sub>a</sub> is defined by the actual tent, independently of any desired deficit estimate. Integrating the input power and using (1) gives

EQ: M<sub>p</sub>(f<sub>a</sub>) = c<sub>a</sub><super>p</super>(1−e<super>−pa</super>)/p,     M<sub>p</sub>(f<sub>a</sub>*f<sub>a</sub>) = c<sub>a</sub><super>2p</super>J<sub>a</sub>.                         (3)

EQ: R<sub>a</sub> := M<sub>p</sub>(f<sub>a</sub>*f<sub>a</sub>)/M<sub>p</sub>(f<sub>a</sub>) = c<sub>a</sub><super>p</super>pJ<sub>a</sub>/(1−e<super>−pa</super>).                         (4)

For e the input moment is 1/p and output moment is J, so the baseline ratio is R = pJ. The denominator 1−e<super>−pa</super> and the remaining factor c<sub>a</sub><super>p</super> in (4) are essential. Comparing output entropies alone would not compare increments.

H: 3. Exact deficit and the subunit-order limit

Set D<sub>a</sub> = J−J<sub>a</sub>. The integrands agree on [0,a]. Subtracting their integrable tails and substituting s = a+u yields the exact identity

EQ: D<sub>a</sub> = e<super>−pa</super> ∫<sub>0</sub><super>∞</super> e<super>−pu</super>[(a+u)<super>p</super>−(a−u)<sub>+</sub><super>p</super>] du,                         (5)

where t<sub>+</sub> = max(t,0). Indeed the shifted tent T<sub>a</sub>(a+u) is (a−u)<sub>+</sub>, including the region u &gt; a where it vanishes. There is no subtraction of divergent integrals in (5).

For every a,u ≥ 0 and 0 &lt; p &lt; 1, the bracket B<sub>a</sub>(u) in (5) satisfies

EQ: 0 ≤ B<sub>a</sub>(u) := (a+u)<super>p</super>−(a−u)<sub>+</sub><super>p</super> ≤ (2u)<super>p</super>.                         (6)

Nonnegativity follows from monotonicity of the positive power. If u ≤ a, use subadditivity (v+w)<super>p</super> ≤ v<super>p</super>+w<super>p</super> for v,w ≥ 0, with v = a−u and w = 2u. If u &gt; a, then (a−u)<sub>+</sub> = 0 and a+u ≤ 2u. This proves the same upper bound in the remaining region.

For fixed u ≥ 0 and a &gt; u, the mean value theorem, or the tangent inequality for t<super>p</super>, gives

EQ: 0 ≤ B<sub>a</sub>(u) ≤ 2pu(a−u)<super>p−1</super> → 0   as a → ∞.                         (7)

At u = 0 this is immediate. At u &gt; 0 the exponent p−1 is negative, so the right side tends to zero. The bound e<super>−pu</super>(2u)<super>p</super> from (6) is integrable on (0,∞): near zero it is a constant times u<super>p</super>, and at infinity exponential decay dominates that power. The dominated convergence theorem therefore proves, for this fixed p,

EQ: K<sub>a</sub> := ∫<sub>0</sub><super>∞</super> e<super>−pu</super>B<sub>a</sub>(u) du → 0,     D<sub>a</sub> = e<super>−pa</super>K<sub>a</sub>.                         (8)

Since J &gt; 0, all sufficiently large finite a &gt; 0 satisfy K<sub>a</sub> &lt; J. Equation (8) then implies

EQ: J<sub>a</sub> &gt; (1−e<super>−pa</super>)J.                                                          (9)

As c<sub>a</sub> &gt; 1, equations (4) and (9), with their positive denominator, yield R<sub>a</sub> &gt; pJ = R. The moments in these ratios are positive. Consequently the logarithmic identity

EQ: Δ<sub>p</sub>(f) = log(M<sub>p</sub>(f*f)/M<sub>p</sub>(f))/(1−p)                                  (10)

and 1−p &gt; 0 give Δ<sub>p</sub>(f<sub>a</sub>) &gt; Δ<sub>p</sub>(e), completing the theorem. This argument uses standard power concavity, exponential integrability, translation invariance of Lebesgue integration, and dominated convergence. These classical tools, including Tonelli and the mean value theorem, are not novelty claims.

H: 4. Threshold consequence and comparison with prior results

Suppose the necessary threshold consequence held for some 0 &lt; p<sub>0</sub> &lt; 1: every admissible log-concave real density f would satisfy Δ<sub>p</sub>(f) ≤ Δ<sub>p</sub>(e) whenever p<sub>0</sub> &lt; p &lt; 1. Take p = (1+p<sub>0</sub>)/2. It lies strictly between p<sub>0</sub> and one. The theorem supplies an admissible f<sub>a</sub> violating that inequality. This refutes the necessary consequence, and hence the proposed full threshold. Nothing in this reasoning treats p = 1, the Shannon conjecture, or the identity of actual optimizers.

Theorem 3.4 and Lemma 3.7 of [1] compare output entropies under a matched input min-entropy. They do not compare output-minus-input Rényi entropies. Their matched exponential input need not have the same order-p entropy as f<sub>a</sub>. Thus subtracting the changing input entropy from the output bound cannot establish the proposed increment inequality. Formula (4) exhibits the missing normalization explicitly.

Melbourne and Tkocz [2, Theorem 6.1 and Corollary 6.2 in the retained preprint] treat entropy comparisons and difference inequalities, using the identity between the maximum density of X−Y and the input collision moment. That mechanism does not furnish a strict improvement for our positive-sum quotient at every p ∈ (0,1). The truncated exponentials here are not symmetric, so reflection cannot identify their positive sums with differences. Melbourne, Tkocz and Wyczesany [3, Theorem 2 and Remark 15] study point evaluation and localization under a variance constraint; their use of truncated exponentials is prior mathematics, but it does not itself optimize the nonlinear quotient in (4). Their higher-order anti-concentration comparisons have different order ranges and objectives.
