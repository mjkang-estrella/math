                                                                                           Abstract
                                                      Baxter numbers are known as the enumeration of Baxter permutations and numerous
                                                  other discrete structures, playing a significant role across combinatorics, algebra, and anal-
                                                  ysis. In this paper, we focus on the analytic properties related to Baxter numbers. We
                                                  prove that the descent polynomials of Baxter permutations have interlacing zeros, which is a
                                                  property stronger than real-rootedness. Our approach is based on Dilks’ framework of (q, t)-
                                                  Hoggatt sums, which is a q-analog for Baxter permutations. Within this framework, we show
                                                  that the family of (1, t)-Hoggatt sums satisfies the interlacing property using fundamental
                                                  results on Hadamard products of polynomials. For Baxter numbers, we prove their asymp-
                                                  totic r-log-convexity via asymptotic expansions of P -recursive sequences. In particular, we
                                                  confirm their 2-log-convexity using symbolic computation techniques.
                                                  AMS Classification 2020: 26C10; 11B83; 05A10
                                                  Keywords: Baxter number; Hoggatt sum; Sturm sequence; asymptotic r-log-convexity;
                                                  Hadamard product

                                          studying the fixed points of the composition of commuting functions. A Baxter permutation on
                                          [n] is a permutation π = π1 π2 · · · πn ∈ Sn which avoids all subsequences πi πj πj+1 πk (for indices
                                          i < j < k − 1) satisfying either of the following patterns:

                                                                     πj+1 < πi < πk < πj       or    πj < πk < πi < πj+1 .

                                          have been built between Baxter permutations and other combinatorial objects, such as twisted

     For the purpose of describing our main theorem, we need the following definitions and no-
tations. Given two real-rooted polynomials f (t), g(t) with real coefficients, let {ui } and {vj } be
all zeros of f (t) and g(t) in weakly decreasing order, respectively. We say that g(t) interlaces
f (t) if deg f (t) = deg g(t) + 1 = n and

                            un ≤ vn−1 ≤ un−1 ≤ · · · ≤ v2 ≤ u2 ≤ v1 ≤ u1 .                            (1.2)

We say that g(t) alternates left of f (t) if deg f (t) = deg g(t) = n and

                         vn ≤ un ≤ vn−1 ≤ un−1 ≤ · · · ≤ v2 ≤ u2 ≤ v1 ≤ u1 .                          (1.3)

Denote by g(t) ⪯ f (t) if either g(t) interlaces f (t) or g(t) alternates left of f (t). We say that g(t)
strictly interlaces (resp., strictly alternates left of) f (t) if no equality holds in (1.2) (resp., (1.3)).
Write g(t) ≺ f (t) if g(t) strictly interlaces or strictly alternates left of f (t). For convenience, we
also let a ⪯ bx + c for any nonnegative real numbers a, b, c, and f (t) ⪯ 0, 0 ⪯ f (t) for any real
polynomial f (t) with only real zeros. A sequence of real-rooted polynomials {fn (t)}n≥0 is said
to be a Sturm sequence if
                                    f0 (t) ≺ f1 (t) ≺ · · · ≺ fn (t) ≺ · · ·,
and it is said to be a generalized Sturm sequence if

                                   f0 (t) ⪯ f1 (t) ⪯ · · · ⪯ fn (t) ⪯ · · ·.

    Since a (generalized) Sturm sequence must be a real-rooted polynomial sequence, we also
provide an alternative proof for the real-rootedness result established by Dilks [9, Theorem
3.8]. Note that the coefficients of a real-rooted polynomial sequence are usually asymptotically
normal. For instance, the asymptotic normality of the numbers Dn, k was obtained by Zhao [32].
Recently, Mao and Shi [22] extended this result to the coefficients M (k, n−k−1, m; 1) of Hoggatt
polynomials. In addition, the real-rootedness of a polynomial also implies the log-concavity of
its coefficient sequence via the well-known Newton inequality. Furthermore, if this polynomial
has only negative zeros, Brändén [4] showed that its coefficient sequence is infinitely log-concave,
a stronger definition introduced by Boros and Moll [5]. Utilizing this result, Mao and Shi [22,
                                                                     n−1
Theorem 2.3] remarked that the sequence {M (k, n − k − 1, m; 1)}k=0      is infinitely log-concave for
any integers n ≥ 1 and m ≥ 1. As a special case, this implies the infinite log-concavity of the
sequence {Dn, k }nk=1 .
    Our second main result focuses on the log-convexity property, a dual notion of log-concavity,
of the Baxter numbers Bn . Recall that an infinite sequence {an }n≥0 is said to be log-convex if
a2n ≤ an−1 an+1 for n ≥ 1. Let L be the operator defined by

                               L ({an }n≥0 ) = {an an+2 − a2n+1 }n≥0 .

A sequence {an }n≥0 is said to be r-log-convex if the j-th iterative sequence L j ({an }n≥0 ) is
nonnegative for all 1 ≤ j ≤ r. Furthermore, {an }n≥0 is said to be infinitely log-convex if it
is r-log-convex for all r ≥ 1. Hou and Zhang [16] introduced the concept of asymptotic r-log-
convexity of {an }n≥0 which requires that for each 1 ≤ j ≤ r, the sequence L j ({an }n≥0 ) is
nonnegative for all but finitely many initial terms. The log-convexity of the Baxter number
sequence {Bn }n≥1 was obtained in [11, Theorem 4.18] by Došlić and Veljan. In this paper, we
focus on the (asymptotic) r-log-convexity of {Bn }n≥1 .

Theorem 1.3. The sequence {Bn }n≥1 is asymptotically r-log-convex for any integer r ≥ 1. In
particular, for r = 2, the sequence {Bn }n≥1 is 2-log-convex.

study of (asymptotic) r-log-convexity of Baxter numbers. We show a proof of Theorem 1.3

As a result, we finally obtain the asymptotic r-log-convexity of {Bn }n≥1 by Theorem 3.1 for any
positive integer r.
   For the 2-log-convexity of {Bn }n≥1 , the symbolic proof is almost one-line by running the
command given in (3.3). The command

                            rLogBound[L, n, N, {1, 2, 6, 22, 92}, 2, 5]

outputs {True, 13069}, which means that {Bn }n≥13069 is 2-log-convex. Since the log-convexity
of {Bn }n≥1 has been proved by Došlić and Veljan [11, Theorem 4.18], it remains to check the
first 13, 069 values of R 2 L (Bn ). We have verified that these initial values are all greater than
one with Mathemtaica. It follows that {Bn }n≥1 is 2-log-convex.

   With the aid of the package P-rec.m, it is easy to get that {Bn }n≥882712 is 3-log-convex. We
do not prove the 3-log-convexity of {Bn }n≥1 due to limitations of our equipment.

For m ≥ 2, Theorem 1.2 states that when q = 1, the polynomials Hn (q, t) form a Sturm

This leads to the following conjecture which we have verified for 1 ≤ q ≤ 10 and 1 ≤ n ≤ 15 by

                                                                                        [m]
Conjecture 4.1. For any integer q > 0 and m = 2, 3, the polynomial sequence {Hn (q, t)}n≥1
forms a Sturm sequence.

   For Baxter numbers, Theorem 1.3 suggests the following conjecture.

Conjecture 4.2. The sequence of Baxter numbers {Bn }n≥1 is infinitely log-convex.

    It is known that a Stieltjes moment sequence is infinitely log-convex [28]. However, we
observe that the sequence {Bn }n≥1 fails to be a Stieltjes moment sequence, since the fifth-order
leading principal minor of its Hankel matrix is negative.
    Note that the Baxter numbers can be treated as the specialization of the classical Hoggatt
         [m]
sums Hn (1, 1) at m = 3. Therefore, we propose a more general conjecture as follows.
                                                                 [m]
Conjecture 4.3. The sequence of (1, 1)-Hoggatt sums {Hn (1, 1)}n≥1 is infinitely log-convex
for any fixed m ≥ 2.
                                                           [2]
    The conjecture holds for m = 2 since the numbers Hn (1, 1) are the Catalan numbers. This
sequence is known to be a Stieltjes moment sequence and hence is infinitely log-convex [19]. For
any fixed m ≥ 4, one may adapt a similar method used in the proof of Theorem 1.3 to obtain the
                                 [m]
asymptotic r-log-convexity of Hn (1, 1). However, we have not found a unified approach that
                                                                                 [4]
works for arbitrary m. Applying the package P-rec.m, one can obtain that {Hn (1, 1)}n≥9632 is
                    [4]
log-convex and {Hn (1, 1)}n≥404602 is 2-log-convex. We have verified that all initial 9, 632 values
         [4]                             [4]
of R 2 (Hn (1, 1)) exceed one. Thus, {Hn (1, 1)}n≥1 is log-convex. We do not check the initial
                            [4]
404, 602 values of R 2 L (Hn (1, 1)) because the limitations of our computers. All of the above
results suggest that Conjecture 4.3 is probably valid.
