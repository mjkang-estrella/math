Abstract
    
We show that planar graphs have bounded queue-number, thus proving a conjecture of Heath, Leighton and Rosenberg from 1992. The key to the proof is a new structural tool called layered partitions, and the result that every planar graph has a vertex-partition and a layering, such that each part has a bounded number of vertices in each layer, and the quotient graph has bounded treewidth. This result generalises for graphs of bounded Euler genus. Moreover, we prove that every graph in a minor-closed class has such a layered partition if and only if the class excludes some apex graph. Building on this work and using the graph minor structure theorem, we prove that every proper minor-closed class of graphs has bounded queue-number.

    
Layered partitions have strong connections to other topics, including the following two examples. First, they can be interpreted in terms of strong products. We show that every planar graph is a subgraph of the strong product of a path with some graph of bounded treewidth. Similar statements hold for all proper minor-closed classes. Second, we give a simple proof of the result by DeVos et al. (2004) that graphs in a proper minor-closed class have low treewidth colourings.

1 Introduction



Stacks and queues are fundamental data structures in computer science. But what is more powerful, a stack or a queue? In 1992, Heath et al. [67] developed a graph-theoretic formulation of this question, where they defined the graph parameters stack-number and queue-number which respectively measure the power of stacks and queues to represent a given graph. Intuitively speaking, if some class of graphs has bounded stack-number and unbounded queue-number, then we would consider stacks to be more powerful than queues for that class (and vice versa). It is known that the stack-number of a graph may be much larger than the queue-number. For example, Heath et al. [67] proved that the nn-vertex ternary Hamming graph has queue-number at most O⁡(log⁡n)O(\log n) and stack-number at least Ω⁡(n1/9−ϵ)\Omega(n^{1/9-\epsilon}). Nevertheless, it is open whether every graph has stack-number bounded by a function of its queue-number, or whether every graph has queue-number bounded by a function of its stack-number [67, 55].




Planar graphs are the simplest class of graphs where it is unknown whether both stack and queue-number are bounded. In particular, Buss and Shor [20] first proved that planar graphs have bounded stack-number; the best known upper bound is 4 due to Yannakakis [116]. However, for the last 27 years of research on this topic, the most important open question in this field has been whether planar graphs have bounded queue-number. This question was first proposed by Heath et al. [67] who conjectured that planar graphs have bounded queue-number.11

The Euler genus of a graph GG is the minimum integer kk such that GG embeds in a surface of Euler genus kk. Of course, a graph is planar if and only if it has Euler genus 0; see [87] for more about graph embeddings in surfaces. A graph HH is a minor of a graph GG if a graph isomorphic to HH can be obtained from a subgraph of GG by contracting edges. A class 𝒢\mathcal{G} of graphs is minor-closed if for every graph G∈𝒢G\in\mathcal{G}, every minor of GG is in 𝒢\mathcal{G}.
A minor-closed class is proper if it is not the class of all graphs. For example, for fixed g⩾0g\geqslant 0, the class of graphs with Euler genus at most gg is a proper minor-closed class.




First we define the stack-number and queue-number of a graph GG. Let V⁡(G)V(G) and E⁡(G)E(G) respectively denote the vertex and edge set of GG. Consider disjoint edges v​w,x​y∈E⁡(G)vw,xy\in E(G) and a linear ordering ≼\preccurlyeq of V⁡(G)V(G). Without loss of generality, v≺wv\prec w and x≺yx\prec y and v≺xv\prec x. Then v​wvw and x​yxy are said to cross if v≺x≺w≺yv\prec x\prec w\prec y and are said to nest if v≺x≺y≺wv\prec x\prec y\prec w. A stack (with respect to ≼\preccurlyeq) is a set of pairwise non-crossing edges, and a queue (with respect to ≼\preccurlyeq) is a set of pairwise non-nested edges. Stacks resemble the stack data structure in the following sense. In a stack, traverse the vertex ordering left-to-right. When visiting vertex vv, because of the non-crossing property, if x1,…,xdx_{1},\dots,x_{d} are the neighbours of vv to the left of vv in left-to-right order, then the edges xd​v,xd−1​v,…,x1​vx_{d}v,x_{d-1}v,\dots,x_{1}v will be on top of the stack in this order. Pop these edges off the stack. Then if y1,…,yd′y_{1},\dots,y_{d^{\prime}} are the neighbours of vv to the right of vv in left-to-right order, then push v​yd′,v​yd′−1,…,v​y1vy_{d^{\prime}},vy_{d^{\prime}-1},\dots,vy_{1} onto the stack in this order. In this way, a stack of edges with respect to a linear ordering resembles a stack data structure. Analogously, the non-nesting condition in the definition of a queue implies that a queue of edges with respect to a linear ordering resembles a queue data structure.




For an integer k⩾0k\geqslant 0, a kk-stack layout of a graph GG consists of a linear ordering ≼\preccurlyeq of V⁡(G)V(G) and a partition E1,E2,…,EkE_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{k} of E⁡(G)E(G) into stacks with respect to ≼\preccurlyeq. Similarly, a kk-queue layout of GG consists of a linear ordering ≼\preccurlyeq of V⁡(G)V(G) and a partition E1,E2,…,EkE_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{k} of E⁡(G)E(G) into queues with respect to ≼\preccurlyeq. The stack-number of GG, denoted by sn⁡(G)\sn(G), is the minimum integer kk such that GG has a kk-stack layout. The queue-number of a graph GG, denoted by qn⁡(G)\qn(G), is the minimum integer kk such that GG has a kk-queue layout. Note that kk-stack layouts are equivalent to kk-page book embeddings, first introduced by Ollmann [90], and stack-number is also called page-number, book thickness, or fixed outer-thickness.




Stack and queue layouts are inherently related to depth-first search and breadth-first search respectively. For example, a DFS ordering of the vertices of a tree has no two crossing edges, and thus defines a 1-stack layout. Similarly, a BFS ordering of the vertices of a tree has no two nested edges, and thus defines a 1-queue layout. So every tree has stack-number 1 and queue-number 1.




For another example, consider the n×nn\times n grid graph with vertex set {(x,y):x,y∈[n]}\{(x,y):x,y\in[n]\} and edges of the form (x,y)​(x+1,y)(x,y)(x+1,y) and (x,y)​(x,y+1)(x,y)(x,y+1). Order the vertices first by xx-coordinate and then by yy-coordinate. Edges of the first type do not nest and edges of the second type do not nest. Thus the n×nn\times n grid graph has a 2-queue layout. In fact, as illustrated in Figure 1, if we order the vertices by x+yx+y and then by xx-coordinate, then no two edges nest. So the n×nn\times n grid graph has queue-number 1.




Figure 1: 1-Queue layout of grid graph.


As mentioned above, Heath et al. [67] conjectured that planar graphs have bounded queue-number. This conjecture has remained open despite much research on queue layouts [108, 33, 55, 67, 69, 66, 94, 99, 48, 53, 52, 49, 2, 3, 12, 32]. We now review progress on this conjecture.




Pemmaraju [94] studied queue layouts and wrote that he “suspects” that a particular planar graph with nn vertices has queue-number Θ⁡(log⁡n)\Theta(\log n). The example he proposed had treewidth 3; see Section 2.2 for the definition of treewidth. Dujmović et al. [48] proved that graphs of bounded treewidth have bounded queue-number. So Pemmaraju’s example in fact has bounded queue-number.




The first o⁡(n)o(n) bound on the queue-number of planar graphs with nn vertices was proved by Heath et al. [67], who observed that every graph with mm edges has a O⁡(m)O(\sqrt{m})-queue layout using a random vertex ordering. Thus every planar graph with nn vertices has queue-number O⁡(n)O(\sqrt{n}), which can also be proved using the Lipton-Tarjan separator theorem. Di Battista et al. [32] proved the first breakthrough on this topic, by showing that every planar graph with nn vertices has queue-number O⁡(log2⁡n)O(\log^{2}n). Dujmović [41] improved this bound to O⁡(log⁡n)O(\log n) with a simpler proof. Building on this work, Dujmović et al. [49] established (poly-)logarithmic bounds for more general classes of graphs. For example, they proved that every graph with nn vertices and Euler genus gg has queue-number O⁡(g+log⁡n)O(g+\log n), and that every graph with nn vertices excluding a fixed minor has queue-number logO⁡(1)⁡n\log^{O(1)}n.




Recently, Bekos et al. [12] proved a second breakthrough result, by showing that planar graphs with bounded maximum degree have bounded queue-number. In particular, every planar graph with maximum degree Δ\Delta has queue-number at most O⁡(Δ6)O(\Delta^{6}). Subsequently, Dujmović et al. [50] proved that the algorithm of Bekos et al. [12] in fact produces a O⁡(Δ2)O(\Delta^{2})-queue layout. This was the state of the art prior to the current work.33

1.1 Main Results



The fundamental contribution of this paper is to prove the conjecture of Heath et al. [67] that planar graphs have bounded queue-number.




Theorem 1.

The queue-number of planar graphs is bounded.






The best upper bound that we obtain for the queue-number of planar graphs is 4949.




We extend Theorem 1 by showing that graphs with bounded Euler genus have bounded queue-number.




Theorem 2.

Every graph with Euler genus gg has queue-number at most O⁡(g)O(g).






The best upper bound that we obtain for the queue-number of graphs with Euler genus gg is 4​g+494g+49.




We generalise further to show the following:




Theorem 3.

Every proper minor-closed class of graphs has bounded queue-number.






These results are obtained through the introduction of a new tool, layered partitions, that have applications well beyond queue layouts. Loosely speaking, a layered partition of a graph GG consists of a partition 𝒫\mathcal{P} of V⁡(G)V(G) along with a layering of GG, such that each part in 𝒫\mathcal{P} has a bounded number of vertices in each layer (called the layered width), and the quotient graph G/𝒫G/\mathcal{P} has certain desirable properties, typically bounded treewidth. Layered partitions are the key tool for proving the above theorems. Subsequent to the initial release of this paper, layered partitions and the results in this paper have been used to solve the following well-known problems:


• 

Dujmović et al. [45] prove that planar graphs have bounded non-repetitive chromatic number (resolving a conjecture of Alon et al. [6] from 2002). This result generalises for graphs excluding any fixed graph as a subdivision.




• 

Dębski et al. [40] make dramatic improvements to the best known bounds for pp-centered colourings of planar graphs and graphs excluding any fixed graph as a subdivision.




• 

Bonamy et al. [16] find shorter adjacency labellings of planar graphs (improving on a sequence of results going back to 1988 [75, 76]).




• 

Dujmović et al. [44] find asymptotically optimal adjacency labellings of planar graphs. This result implies that, for every integer n>0n>0, there is a graph with n1+o⁡(1)n^{1+o(1)} vertices that contains every nn-vertex planar graph as an induced subgraph.










1.2 Outline



The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we review relevant background including treewidth, layerings, and partitions, and we introduce layered partitions.




Section 3 proves a fundamental lemma which shows that every graph that has a partition of bounded layered width has queue-number bounded by a function of the queue-number of the quotient graph.




In Section 4, we prove that every planar graph has a partition of layered width 1 such that the quotient graph has treewidth at most 88. Since graphs of bounded treewidth are known to have bounded queue-number [48], this implies Theorem 1 with an upper bound of 766766. We then prove a variant of this result with layered width 3, where the quotient graph is planar with treewidth 3. This variant coupled with a better bound on the queue-number of treewidth-33 planar graphs [2, 3] implies Theorem 1 with an upper bound of 4949.




In Section 5, we prove that graphs of Euler genus gg have partitions of layered width O⁡(g)O(g) such that the quotient graph has treewidth O⁡(1)O(1). This immediately implies that such graphs have queue-number O⁡(g)O(g). These partitions are also required for the proof of Theorem 3 in Section 6. A more direct argument that appeals to Theorem 1 proves the bound 4​g+494g+49 in Theorem 2.




In Section 6, we extend our results for layered partitions to the setting of almost-embeddable graphs with no apex vertices. Coupled with other techniques, this allows us to prove Theorem 3. We also characterise those minor-closed graph classes with the property that every graph in the class has a partition of bounded layered width such that the quotient has bounded treewidth.




In Section 7, we provide an alternative and helpful perspective on layered partitions in terms of strong products of graphs. With this viewpoint, we derive results about universal graphs that contain all planar graphs. Similar results are obtained for more general classes.




In Section 8, we prove that some well-known non-minor-closed classes of graphs, such as kk-planar graphs, also have bounded queue-number.




Section 9 explores further applications and connections. We start off by giving an example where layered partitions lead to a simple proof of a known and difficult result about low treewidth colourings in proper minor-closed classes. Then we point out some of the many connections that layered partitions have with other graph parameters. We also present other implications of our results such as resolving open problems on 3-dimensional graph drawing.




Finally Section 10 summarizes and concludes with open problems and directions for future work.








2 Tools



Undefined terms and notation can be found in Diestel’s text [34]. Throughout the paper, we use the notation X→\overrightarrow{X} to refer to a particular linear ordering of a set XX.




2.1 Layerings



The following well-known definitions are key concepts in our proofs, and that of several other papers on queue layouts [49, 48, 53, 12, 50]. A layering of a graph GG is an ordered partition (V0,V1,…)(V_{0},V_{1},\dots) of V⁡(G)V(G) such that for every edge v​w∈E⁡(G)vw\in E(G), if v∈Viv\in V_{i} and w∈Vjw\in V_{j}, then |i−j|⩽1|i-j|\leqslant 1. If i=ji=j then v​wvw is an intra-level edge. If |i−j|=1|i-j|=1 then v​wvw is an inter-level edge.




If rr is a vertex in a connected graph GG and Vi:={v∈V⁡(G):distG⁡(r,v)=i}V_{i}:=\{v\in V(G):\dist_{G}(r,v)=i\} for all i⩾0i\geqslant 0, then (V0,V1,…)(V_{0},V_{1},\dots) is called a BFS layering of GG rooted at rr. Associated with a BFS layering is a BFS spanning tree TT obtained by choosing, for each non-root vertex v∈Viv\in V_{i} with i⩾1i\geqslant 1, a neighbour ww in Vi−1V_{i-1}, and adding the edge v​wvw to TT. Thus distT⁡(r,v)=distG⁡(r,v)\dist_{T}(r,v)=\dist_{G}(r,v) for each vertex vv of GG.




These notions extend to disconnected graphs. If G1,…,GcG_{1},\dots,G_{c} are the components of GG, and rjr_{j} is a vertex in GjG_{j} for each j∈{1,…,c}j\in\{1,\dots,c\}, and Vi:=⋃j=1c{v∈V⁡(Gj):distGj⁡(rj,v)=i}V_{i}:=\bigcup_{j=1}^{c}\{v\in V(G_{j}):\dist_{G_{j}}(r_{j},v)=i\} for all i⩾0i\geqslant 0, then (V0,V1,…)(V_{0},V_{1},\dots) is called a BFS layering of GG.






2.2 Treewidth and Layered Treewidth



First we introduce the notion of HH-decomposition and tree-decomposition.
For graphs HH and GG, an HH-decomposition of GG consists of a collection (Bx⊆V(G):x∈V(H))(B_{x}\subseteq V(G):x\in V(H)) of subsets of V⁡(G)V(G), called bags, indexed by the vertices of HH, and with the following properties:


• 

for every vertex vv of GG, the set {x∈V⁡(H):v∈Bx}\{x\in V(H):v\in B_{x}\} induces a non-empty connected subgraph of HH, and




• 

for every edge v​wvw of GG, there is a vertex x∈V⁡(H)x\in V(H) for which v,w∈Bxv,w\in B_{x}.





The width of such an HH-decomposition is max⁡{|Bx|:x∈V⁡(H)}−1\max\{|B_{x}|:x\in V(H)\}-1.
The elements of V⁡(H)V(H) are called nodes, while the elements of V⁡(G)V(G) are called vertices.




A tree-decomposition is a TT-decomposition for some tree TT.
The treewidth of a graph GG is the minimum width of a tree-decomposition of GG.
Treewidth measures how similar a given graph is to a tree.
It is particularly important in structural and algorithmic graph theory; see [65, 97, 14] for surveys.
Tree decompositions were introduced by Robertson and Seymour [100]; the more general notion of HH-decomposition was introduced by Diestel and Kühn [35].




As mentioned in Section 1, Dujmović et al. [48] first proved that graphs of bounded treewidth have bounded queue-number. Their bound on the queue-number was doubly exponential in the treewidth. Wiechert [108] improved this bound to singly exponential.




Lemma 4 ([108]).

Every graph with treewidth kk has queue-number at most 2k−12^{k}-1.






Alam, Bekos, Gronemann, Kaufmann, and
Pupyrev [2] also improved the bound in the case of planar 3-trees. (A kk-tree is an edge-maximal graph of tree-width kk.) The following lemma that will be useful later is implied by this result and the fact that every planar graph of treewidth at most 33 is a subgraph of a planar 33-tree [83].




Lemma 5 ([2, 83]).

Every planar graph with treewidth at most 33 has queue-number at most 55.






Graphs with bounded treewidth provide important examples of minor-closed classes. However, planar graphs have unbounded treewidth. For example, the n×nn\times n planar grid graph has treewidth nn. So the above results do not resolve the question of whether planar graphs have bounded queue-number.




Dujmović et al. [49] and Shahrokhi [106] independently introduced the following concept. The layered treewidth of a graph GG is the minimum integer kk such that GG has a tree-decomposition (Bx:x∈V(T))(B_{x}:x\in V(T)) and a layering (V0,V1,…)(V_{0},V_{1},\dots) such that |Bx∩Vi|⩽k|B_{x}\cap V_{i}|\leqslant k for every bag BxB_{x} and layer ViV_{i}. Applications of layered treewidth include graph colouring [49, 84, 74], graph drawing [49, 11], book embeddings [47], and intersection graph theory [106]. The related notion of layered pathwidth has also been studied [42, 11]. Most relevant to this paper, Dujmović et al. [49] proved that every graph with nn vertices and layered treewidth kk has queue-number at most O⁡(k​log⁡n)O(k\log n). They then proved that planar graphs have layered treewidth at most 3, that graphs of Euler genus gg have layered treewidth at most 2​g+32g+3, and more generally that a minor-closed class has bounded layered treewidth if and only if it excludes some apex graph.44
              4
              
              
              
              
              
              
              
            A graph GG is apex if G−vG-v is planar for some vertex vv. This implies O⁡(log⁡n)O(\log n) bounds on the queue-number for all these graphs, and was the basis for the logO⁡(1)⁡n\log^{O(1)}n bound for proper minor-closed classes mentioned in Section 1.






2.3 Partitions and Layered Partitions



The following definitions are central notions in this paper. A vertex-partition, or simply partition, of a graph GG is a set 𝒫\mathcal{P} of non-empty sets of vertices in GG such that each vertex of GG is in exactly one element of 𝒫\mathcal{P}. Each element of 𝒫\mathcal{P} is called a part. The quotient (sometimes called the touching pattern) of 𝒫\mathcal{P} is the graph, denoted by G/𝒫G/\mathcal{P}, with vertex set 𝒫\mathcal{P} where distinct parts A,B∈𝒫A,B\in\mathcal{P} are adjacent in G/𝒫G/\mathcal{P} if and only if some vertex in AA is adjacent in GG to some vertex in BB.




A partition of GG is connected if the subgraph induced by each part is connected. In this case, the quotient is the minor of GG obtained by contracting each part into a single vertex. Most of our results for queue layouts do not depend on the connectivity of partitions. But we consider it to be of independent interest that many of the partitions constructed in this paper are connected. Then the quotient is a minor of the original graph.




A partition 𝒫\mathcal{P} of a graph GG is called an HH-partition if HH is a graph that contains a spanning subgraph isomorphic to the quotient G/𝒫G/\mathcal{P}. Alternatively, an HH-partition of a graph GG is a partition (Ax:x∈V(H))(A_{x}:x\in V(H)) of V⁡(G)V(G) indexed by the vertices of HH, such that for every edge v​w∈E⁡(G)vw\in E(G), if v∈Axv\in A_{x} and w∈Ayw\in A_{y} then x=yx=y (and v​wvw is called an intra-bag edge) or x​y∈E⁡(H)xy\in E(H) (and v​wvw is called an inter-bag edge). The width of such an HH-partition is max⁡{|Ax|:x∈V⁡(H)}\max\{|A_{x}|:x\in V(H)\}. Note that a layering is equivalent to a path-partition.




A tree-partition is a TT-partition for some tree TT. Tree-partitions are well studied with several applications [36, 37, 112, 105, 15]. For example, every graph with treewidth kk and maximum degree Δ\Delta has a tree-partition of width O⁡(k​Δ)O(k\Delta); see [112, 36]. This easily leads to a O⁡(k​Δ)O(k\Delta) upper bound on the queue-number [48]. However, dependence on Δ\Delta seems unavoidable when studying tree-partitions [112], so we instead consider HH-partitions where HH has bounded treewidth greater than 1. This idea has been used by many authors in a variety of applications, including cops and robbers [8], fractional colouring [98, 104], generalised colouring numbers [72], and defective and clustered colouring [74]. See [38, 39] for more on partitions of graphs in a proper minor-closed class.




A key innovation of this paper is to consider a layered variant of partitions (analogous to layered treewidth being a layered variant of treewidth). The layered width of a partition 𝒫\mathcal{P} of a graph GG is the minimum integer ℓ\ell such that for some layering (V0,V1,…)(V_{0},V_{1},\dots) of GG, each part in 𝒫\mathcal{P} has at most ℓ\ell vertices in each layer ViV_{i}.




The n×nn\times n grid graph GG provides an instructive example. The columns determine a partition 𝒫\mathcal{P} of layered width 1 with respect to the layering determined by the rows. The quotient G/𝒫G/\mathcal{P} is an nn-vertex path.




Throughout this paper we consider partitions with bounded layered width such that the quotient has bounded treewidth. We therefore introduce the following definition. A class 𝒢\mathcal{G} of graphs is said to admit bounded layered partitions if there exist k,ℓ∈ℕk,\ell\in\mathbb{N} such that every graph G∈𝒢G\in\mathcal{G} has a partition 𝒫\mathcal{P} with layered width at most ℓ\ell such that G/𝒫G/\mathcal{P} has treewidth at most kk. We first show that this property immediately implies bounded layered treewidth.




Lemma 6.

If a graph GG has an HH-partition with layered width at most ℓ\ell such that HH has treewidth at most kk,
then GG has layered treewidth at most (k+1)​ℓ(k+1)\ell.






Proof.

Let (Bx:x∈V(T))(B_{x}:x\in V(T)) be a tree-decomposition of HH with bags of size at most k+1k+1. Replace each instance of a vertex vv of HH in a bag BxB_{x} by the part corresponding to vv in the HH-partition. Keep the same layering of GG. Since |Bx|⩽k+1|B_{x}|\leqslant k+1, we obtain a tree-decomposition of GG with layered width at most (k+1)​ℓ(k+1)\ell.
∎






Lemma 6 means that any property that holds for graph classes with bounded layered treewidth also holds for graph classes that admit bounded layered partitions. For example, Norin proved that every nn-vertex graph with layered treewidth at most kk has treewidth less than 2​k​n2\sqrt{kn} (see [49]). With Lemma 6, this implies that if an nn-vertex graph GG has a partition with layered width ℓ\ell such that the quotient graph has treewidth at most kk, then GG has treewidth at most 2​(k+1)​ℓ​n2\sqrt{(k+1)\ell n}. This in turn leads to O⁡(n)O(\sqrt{n}) balanced separator theorems for such graphs.




Lemma 6 suggests that having a partition of bounded layered width, whose quotient has bounded treewidth, seems to be a more stringent requirement than having bounded layered treewidth. Indeed the former structure leads to O⁡(1)O(1) bounds on the queue-number, instead of O⁡(log⁡n)O(\log n) bounds obtained via layered treewidth. That said, it is open whether graphs of bounded layered treewidth have bounded queue-number.




Before continuing, we show that if one does not care about the exact treewidth bound, then it suffices to consider partitions with layered width 1.




Lemma 7.

If a graph GG has an HH-partition of layered width ℓ\ell with respect to a layering (V0,V1,…)(V_{0},V_{1},\dots), for some graph HH of treewidth at most kk, then GG has an H′H^{\prime}-partition of layered width 1 with respect to the same layering, for some graph H′H^{\prime} of treewidth at most (k+1)​ℓ−1(k+1)\ell-1.

4 Proof of Theorem 1: Planar Graphs



Our proof that planar graphs have bounded queue-number employs Corollary 10. Thus our goal is to show that planar graphs admit bounded layered partitions, which is achieved in the following key contribution of the paper.




Theorem 11.

Every planar graph GG has a connected partition 𝒫\mathcal{P} with layered width 11 such that G/𝒫G/\mathcal{P} has treewidth at most 88.
Moreover, there is such a partition for every BFS layering of GG.






This theorem and Corollary 10 imply that planar graphs have bounded queue-number (Theorem 1) with an upper bound of 3​(28−1)+⌊32​3⌋=7663(2^{8}-1)+\left\lfloor\tfrac{3}{2}3\right\rfloor=766.




We now set out to prove Theorem 11. The proof is inspired by the following elegant result of Pilipczuk and Siebertz [95]: Every planar graph GG has a partition 𝒫\mathcal{P} into geodesics such that G/𝒫G/\mathcal{P} has treewidth at most 88. Here, a geodesic is a path of minimum length between its endpoints. We consider the following particular type of geodesic. If TT is a tree rooted at a vertex rr, then a non-empty path (x1,…,xp)(x_{1},\dots,x_{p}) in TT is vertical if for some d⩾0d\geqslant 0 for all i∈{0,…,p}i\in\{0,\dots,p\} we have distT⁡(xi,r)=d+i\dist_{T}(x_{i},r)=d+i. The vertex x1x_{1} is called the upper endpoint of the path and xpx_{p} is its lower endpoint. Note that every vertical path in a BFS spanning tree is a geodesic. Thus the next theorem strengthens the result of Pilipczuk and Siebertz [95].




Theorem 12.

Let TT be a rooted spanning tree in a connected planar graph GG. Then GG has a partition 𝒫\mathcal{P} into vertical paths in TT such that G/𝒫G/\mathcal{P} has treewidth at most 88.

We may assume that GG is connected (since if each component of GG has the desired partition, then so does GG).
Let TT be a BFS spanning tree of GG.
By Theorem 12, GG has a partition 𝒫\mathcal{P} into vertical paths in TT such that G/𝒫G/\mathcal{P} has treewidth at most 88.
Each path in 𝒫\mathcal{P} is connected and has at most one vertex in each BFS layer corresponding to TT. Hence
𝒫\mathcal{P} is connected and has layered width 1.
∎

4.1 Reducing the Bound



We now set out to reduce the constant in Theorem 1 from 766766 to 4949. This is achieved by proving the following variant of Theorem 11.




Theorem 15.

Every planar graph GG has a partition 𝒫\mathcal{P} with layered width 33 such that G/𝒫G/\mathcal{P} is planar and has treewidth at most 33. Moreover, there is such a partition for every BFS layering of GG.






This theorem with Lemmas 8 and 5 imply that planar graphs have bounded queue-number (Theorem 1) with an upper bound of 3⋅3⋅5+⌊32⋅3⌋=493\cdot 3\cdot 5+\left\lfloor\tfrac{3}{2}\cdot 3\right\rfloor=49.




Note that Theorem 15 is stronger than Theorem 11 in that the treewidth bound is smaller, whereas Theorem 11 is stronger than Theorem 15 in that the partition is connected and the layered width is smaller. Also note that Theorem 15 is tight in terms of the treewidth of HH: For every ℓ\ell, there exists a planar graph GG such that, if GG has a partition 𝒫\mathcal{P} of layered width ℓ\ell, then G/𝒫G/\mathcal{P} has treewidth at least 33. We give this construction at the end of this section, and prove Theorem 15 first. Theorem 11 was proved via an inductive proof of a stronger statement given in Lemma 13. Similarly, the proof of Theorem 15 is via an inductive proof of a stronger statement given in Lemma 17, below.




While Theorem 12 partitions the vertices of a planar graph into vertical paths,
to prove Theorem 15 we instead partition the vertices of a triangulation G+G^{+} into parts each of which is a union of up to three vertical paths.
Formally, in a rooted spanning tree TT of a graph GG,
a tripod consists of up to three pairwise disjoint vertical paths in TT whose lower endpoints form a clique in GG.
Theorem 15 quickly follows from the next result.




Theorem 16.

Let TT be a rooted spanning tree in a triangulation GG.
Then GG has a partition 𝒫\mathcal{P} into tripods in TT such that G/𝒫G/\mathcal{P} has treewidth at most 33.






Proof of Theorem 15 assuming Theorem 16.

We may assume that GG is connected (since if each component of GG has the desired partition, then so does GG). Let TT be a BFS spanning tree of GG. Let (V0,V1,…)(V_{0},V_{1},\dots) be the BFS layering corresponding to TT. Let G′G^{\prime} be a plane triangulation containing GG as a spanning subgraph. By Theorem 16, G′G^{\prime} has a partition 𝒫\mathcal{P} into tripods in TT such that G′/𝒫G^{\prime}/\mathcal{P} is planar with treewidth at most 33. Then 𝒫\mathcal{P} is a partition of GG such that G/𝒫G/\mathcal{P} is planar with treewidth at most 33. Each part in 𝒫\mathcal{P} corresponds to a tripod, which
has at most three vertices in each layer ViV_{i}. Hence 𝒫\mathcal{P} has layered width at most 3.
∎

5 Proof of Theorem 2: Bounded-Genus Graphs



As was the case for planar graphs, our proof that bounded genus graphs have bounded queue-number employs Corollary 10. Thus the goal of this section is to show that our construction of bounded layered partitions for planar graphs can be generalised for graphs of bounded Euler genus. In particular, we show the following theorem of independent interest.




Theorem 20.

Every graph GG of Euler genus gg has a connected partition 𝒫\mathcal{P} with layered width at most max⁡{2​g,1}\max\{2g,1\} such that G/𝒫G/\mathcal{P} is apex and has treewidth at most 99. Moreover, there is such a partition for every BFS layering of GG.

8.1 Allowing Crossings

9 Applications and Connections



In this section, we show that layered partitions lead to a simple proof of a known result about low treewidth colourings, and we discuss implications of our results such as resolving open problems about 3-dimensional graph drawings.




9.1 Low Treewidth Colourings



DeVos et al. 9.2 Track Layouts



Track layouts are a type of graph layout closely related to queue layouts. A vertex kk-colouring of a graph GG is a partition {V1,…,Vk}\{V_{1},\dots,V_{k}\} of V⁡(G)V(G) into independent sets; that is, for every edge v​w∈E⁡(G)vw\in E(G), if v∈Viv\in V_{i} and w∈Vjw\in V_{j} then i≠ji\neq j. A track in GG is an independent set equipped with a linear ordering. A partition {V1→,…,Vk→}\{\overrightarrow{V_{1}},\dots,\overrightarrow{V_{k}}\} of V⁡(G)V(G) into kk tracks is a kk-track layout if for distinct i,j∈{1,…,k}i,j\in\{1,\dots,k\} no two edges of GG cross between Vi→\overrightarrow{V_{i}} and Vj→\overrightarrow{V_{j}}. That is, for all distinct edges v​w,x​y∈E⁡(G)vw,xy\in E(G) with v,x∈Viv,x\in V_{i} and w,y∈Vjw,y\in V_{j}, if v≺xv\prec x in Vi→\overrightarrow{V_{i}} then w≼yw\preccurlyeq y in Vj→\overrightarrow{V_{j}}. The minimum kk such that GG has a kk-track layout is called the track-number of GG, denoted by tn⁡(G)\tn(G). Dujmović et al. [48] proved the following connection to queue-number.




Lemma 50 ([48]).

For every graph GG, qn⁡(G)⩽tn⁡(G)−1.\qn(G)\leqslant\tn(G)-1.






The proof of Lemma 50 simply puts the tracks one after the other to produce a queue layout. In this sense, track layouts can be thought of as a richer structure than queue layouts. This structure was the key to an inductive proof by Dujmović et al. [48] that graphs of bounded treewidth have bounded track-number (which implies bounded queue-number by Lemma 50).
Nevertheless, Dujmović et al. [52] proved the following converse to Lemma 50:




Lemma 51 ([52]).

There is a function ff such that tn⁡(G)⩽f⁡(qn⁡(G))\tn(G)\leqslant f(\qn(G)) for every graph GG. In particular, every graph with queue-number at most kk has track-number at most





4​k⋅4k​(2​k−1)​(4​k−1).4k\cdot 4^{k(2k-1)(4k-1)}.







Lemmas 50 and 51 together say that queue-number and track-number are tied.




The following lemma often gives better bounds on the track-number than Lemma 51. A proper graph colouring is acyclic if every cycle gets at least three colours. The acyclic chromatic number of a graph GG is the minimum integer cc such that GG has an acyclic cc-colouring.




Lemma 52 ([48]).

Every graph GG with acyclic chromatic number at most cc and queue-number at most kk has track-number at most c​(2​k)c−1c(2k)^{c-1}.






Borodin [17] proved that planar graphs have acyclic chromatic number at most 5, which with Lemmas 52 and 1 implies:




Theorem 53.

Every planar graph has track-number at most 5​(2⋅49)4=461,184,0805(2\cdot 49)^{4}=461,184,080.

Heawood [71] and Alon et al. [7] respectively proved that every graph with Euler genus gg has chromatic number O⁡(g1/2)O(g^{1/2}) and acyclic chromatic number O⁡(g4/7)O(g^{4/7}). Lemmas 52 and 2 then imply:

9.3 Three-Dimensional Graph Drawing



Further motivation for studying queue and track layouts is their connection with 3-dimensional graph drawing. A 3-dimensional grid drawing of a graph GG represents the vertices of GG by distinct grid points in ℤ3\mathbb{Z}^{3} and represents each edge of GG by the open segment between its endpoints so that no two edges intersect. The volume of a 3-dimensional grid drawing is the number of grid points in the smallest axis-aligned grid-box that encloses the drawing. For example, Cohen et al. [26] proved that the complete graph KnK_{n} has a 3-dimensional grid drawing with volume O⁡(n3)O(n^{3}) and this bound is optimal. Pach et al. [92] proved that every graph with bounded chromatic number has a 3-dimensional grid drawing with volume O⁡(n2)O(n^{2}), and this bound is optimal for Kn/2,n/2K_{n/2,n/2}.




Track layouts and 3-dimensional graph drawings are connected by the following lemma.




Lemma 56 ([48, 54]).

If a cc-colourable nn-vertex graph GG has a tt-track layout, then GG has 3-dimensional grid drawings with O⁡(t2​n)O(t^{2}n) volume and with O⁡(c7​t​n)O(c^{7}tn) volume. Conversely, if a graph GG has a 3-dimensional grid drawing with
A×B×CA\times B\times C bounding box, then GG has track-number at most 2​A​B2AB.






Lemma 56 is the foundation for all of the following results. Dujmović and Wood [54] proved that every graph with bounded maximum degree has a 3-dimensional grid drawing with volume O⁡(n3/2)O(n^{3/2}), and the same bound holds for graphs from a proper minor-closed class. In fact, for fixed dd, every dd-degenerate graph77

Prior to this work, whether planar graphs have 3-dimensional grid drawings with O⁡(n)O(n) volume was a major open problem, due to Felsner, Liotta, and Wismath [61]. 10 Open Problems




1. 

What is the maximum queue-number of planar graphs? We can tweak our proof of Theorem 1 to show that every planar graph has queue-number at most 48, but it seems new ideas are required to obtain a significant improvement. The best lower bound on the maximum queue-number of planar graphs is 44, due to Alam et al. [2].




More generally, does every graph with Euler genus gg have o⁡(g)o(g) queue-number? Complete graphs provide a Θ⁡(g)\Theta(\sqrt{g}) lower bound. Note that every graph with Euler genus gg has O⁡(g)O(\sqrt{g}) stack-number [85].




2. 

As discussed in Section 1 it is open whether there is a function ff such that sn⁡(G)⩽f⁡(qn⁡(G))\sn(G)\leqslant f(\qn(G)) for every graph GG. Heath et al. [67] proved that every 1-queue graph has stack-number at most 22. Dujmović and Wood [55] showed that there is such a function ff if and only if every 2-queue graph has bounded stack-number.




Similarly, it is open whether there is a function ff such that qn⁡(G)⩽f⁡(sn⁡(G))\qn(G)\leqslant f(\sn(G)) for every graph GG. Heath et al. [67] proved that every 1-stack graph has queue-number at most 2. Since 2-stack graphs are planar, this paper solves the first open case of this question. Dujmović and Wood [55] showed that there is such a function ff if and only if every 3-stack graph has bounded queue-number.




3. 

Ossona de Mendez et al. [91] introduced the following definition:
A graph GG is said to be kk-close to Euler genus gg if every subgraph HH of GG has a drawing in a surface of Euler genus gg with at most k​|E⁡(H)|k\,|E(H)| crossings (that is, with O⁡(k)O(k) crossings per edge on average). Does every such graph have queue-number at most f⁡(g,k)f(g,k) for some function ff?




4. 

Is there a proof of Theorem 3 that does not use the graph minor structure theorem and with more reasonable bounds?




5. 

Queue layouts naturally extend to posets. The cover graph GPG_{P} of a poset PP is the undirected graph with vertex set PP, where v​w∈E⁡(G)vw\in E(G) if v<Pwv<_{P}w and v<Px<Pwv<_{P}x<_{P}w for no x∈Px\in P (or w<Pvw<_{P}v and w<Px<Pvw<_{P}x<_{P}v for no x∈Px\in P). Thus the cover graph encodes relations in PP that are not implied by transitivity. A kk-queue layout of a poset PP consists of a linear extension ≼\preccurlyeq of PP and a partition E1,E2,…,EkE_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{k} of E⁡(GP)E(G_{P}) into queues with respect to ≼\preccurlyeq. The queue-number of a poset PP is the minimum integer kk such that PP has a kk-queue layout. Heath and Pemmaraju [68] conjectured that the queue-number of a planar poset is at most its height (the maximum number of pairwise comparable elements).
This was disproved by Knauer et al. [78] who presented a poset of height 22 and queue-number 44.
Theorem 1 and results of Knauer, Micek and Ueckerdt imply that planar posets of height hh have queue-number O⁡(h)O(h); see Theorem 6 in [78]. Heath and Pemmaraju [68] also conjecture that every poset of width ww (the maximum number of pairwise incomparable elements) has queue-number at most ww. The best known upper bounds are O⁡(w2)O(w^{2}) for general posets and 3​w−23w-2 for planar posets [78].




6. 

It is natural to ask for the largest class of graphs with bounded queue-number. First note that Theorem 3 cannot be extended to the setting of an excluded topological minor, since graphs with bounded degree have arbitrarily high queue-number [110, 67]. However, it is possible that every class of graphs with strongly sub-linear separators has bounded queue-number. Here a class 𝒢\mathcal{G} of graphs has strongly sub-linear separators if 𝒢\mathcal{G} is closed under taking subgraphs, and there exists constants c,β>0c,\beta>0, such that every nn-vertex graph in 𝒢\mathcal{G} has a balanced separator of order c​n1−βcn^{1-\beta}. Already the β=12\beta=\frac{1}{2} case looks challenging, since this would imply Theorem 3.




7. 

Is there a polynomial function ff such that every graph with treewidth kk has queue-number at most f⁡(k)f(k)? The best lower and upper bounds on f⁡(k)f(k) are k+1k+1 and 2k−12^{k}-1, both due to Wiechert [108].




8. 

Do the results in the present paper have algorithmic applications? Consider the method of Baker [10] for designing polynomial-time approximation schemes for problems on planar graphs. This method partitions the graph into BFS layers, such that the problem can be solved optimally on each layer (since the induced subgraph has bounded treewidth), and then combines the solutions from each layer. Our results (Theorem 11) give a more precise description of the layered structure of planar graphs (and other more general classes). It is conceivable that this extra structural information is useful when designing algorithms.




Note that all our proofs lead to polynomial-time algorithms for computing the desired decomposition and queue layout.
Pilipczuk and Siebertz [95] claim O⁡(n2)O(n^{2}) time complexity for their decomposition. The same is true for Lemma 13: Given the colours of the vertices on FF, we can walk down the BFS tree TT in linear time and colour every vertex. Another linear-time enumeration of the faces contained in FF finds the trichromatic triangle. It is easily seen that Lemma 21 has polynomial time complexity (given the embedding). Polynomial-time algorithms for our other results follow based on the linear-time algorithm of Mohar [86] to test if a given graph has Euler genus at most any fixed number gg, and the polynomial-time algorithm of Demaine et al. [30] for computing the decomposition in the graph minor structure theorem (Theorem 23).
