1.2. Staircases to the sum-sides. A striking feature of the identities found using Z -algebras is that the sum-sides
of such identities are inherently partition-theoretic. Generically, it appears to be a hard task to find “nice” analytic
sum-sides that count the sum-side partitions. In a few cases, certain candidates for analytic sum-sides are known using
Bailey techniques (see for instance [S2]), but it is far from obvious how these analytic sum-sides are related to the
partition theoretic sum-sides.
For the identities presented in this article, we are able to provide analytic companions to our partition-theoretic sum-
sides, by using staircases and jagged partitions. Given a partition π : λ 1 + · · · + λ j written in a weakly increasing order
and a positive integer s, the (ordered) sequence µ : λ 1 , λ 2 − s, λ 3 − 2s, · · · , λ j − (j − 1)s is said to be obtained from π by
                                                                  2
removing an s-staircase. As such, µ may not be in a weakly increasing order and may also have non-positive entries.
Such a sequence is called a jagged partition. For all the identities in this paper, removing an appropriate staircase leads
to interesting jagged partitions whose generating functions could be written down explicitly. One then replaces the
staircases to arrive at analytical sum-sides for the original identities.

As mentioned above, jagged partitions play a crucial role in the present paper. They first arose in the physics
literature [FJM1] in the analysis of fermionic characters for certain superconformal minimal models. Moreover, in
[FJM2, FJM3] some beautiful identities for jagged partitions were established. In [L], Lovejoy established certain
constant term identities related to generating functions for jagged partitions. In [ABM], new analytic sum-sides for
Schur’s identity were found using staircases. Very recently, in [DL], generalizations of the (first) Capparelli identity
were found by utilizing jagged (over)partitions. Our treatment of the first Capparelli identity is precisely a “dilated”
version of the argument in [DL]. Notably, in [C3], Capparelli used staircases in his investigation of the identities related
to the standard modules at levels 5 and 7 for the affine Lie algebra A(2)

We write partitions of a positive integer in a weakly increasing order. Sub-partitions of a partition π will always refer
to contiguous portions of π .
Suppose π : p1 + p2 + · · · + pn is a partition, where sometimes we may have to let p1 = 0. Let s be a positive integer.
Let µ be the sequence p1 , p2 − s, p3 − 2s, . . . , pn − (n − 1)s. We say that µ is obtained by deleting an s-staircase from
π . Note that µ may have non-positive entries and may not be in a weakly increasing order (which is the reason why we
separate entries of µ by a comma rather than a plus sign), however, successive differences in µ are at least −s. We call
such µ jagged partitions.

Jagged partitions emerging in this paper will have a very specific structure. We shall scan a jagged partition µ from left
to right and identify maximum jagged portions of µ corresponding to each of the positive integers 1, 2, . . . (sometimes
0 will have to be considered as well). If µ is obtained by removing an s-staircase, the block corresponding to j is defined
as the maximal contiguous block of µ starting with j and containing only integers from {j, j − 1, . . . , j − s}; either of
these conditions may be sometimes slightly relaxed but it will be clear what we mean. We shall designate such blocks
using regular expressions. If P is a pattern of integers (for example P = “2, 1”), then:
     P∗ corresponds to a string of either 0 or more contiguous blocks of P.
    P+ will be a string of 1 or more contiguous blocks of P.
     P• will be either the empty string or P itself.
For instance, if the jagged partition 2, 1, 2, 1, 3, 3, 4, 3 is obtained by removing a 1-staircase from some partition π , its
maximal block corresponding to 2 matches [2, 1]∗ (and also [2, 1]+ but not [2, 1]•), the maximal block corresponding
to 3 matches 3∗ (and also 3+ but not 3• ), the one for 4 matches [4, 3]• etc.
We shall always denote a partition by π and the jagged partition obtained by removing an s-staircase by µ.

The following sum-side conditions are common to the three ensuing conjectures:

    (1) No consecutive parts allowed.

    (2) Odd parts do not repeat.

    (3) For a contiguous sub-partition λi + λi +1 + λi +2 , we have |λi − λi +2 | ≥ 4 if λi +1 is even and appears more than

                      5. Analytic sum-sides for some previous conjectures from [KR] and [R]

In [KR] we had conjectured six new partition identities and three further conjectures were given by one of the authors
in [R]. We now provide analytic sum-sides for some of these identities. For all the identities in this section, we shall
use 1-staircases to arrive at the analytic sum-sides. To describe these identities, we first need a few definitions.
Definition 5.0.1. A partition π = λ 1 + λ 2 + · · · + λm written in a weakly increasing order is said to have “difference
at least k at distance d” if for all j, λ j+d − λ j ≥ k. For example, the first Rogers-Ramanujan identity recalled above
enumerates partitions satisfying difference at least 2 at distance 1.
Definition 5.0.2. Fix an integer i. A partition π = λ 1 + λ 2 + · · · + λm is said to satisfy “Condition(i)” if π has difference
at least 3 at distance 3 such that if parts at distance two differ by at most 1, then their sum (together with the intermediate
part) is congruent to i (mod 3).

                                            7. Analytic sum-sides for Capparelli’s identities

Recall Capparelli’s identities [C1] which arose from level 3 standard modules for A(2)
                                                                                   2
                                                                                       :
Theorem. For any positive integer n we have that:
     (1) Number of partitions of n into parts different from 1 such that the difference of two consecutive parts is at least
         2, and is exactly 2 or 3 only if their sum is a multiple of 3 is the same as the number of partitions in which
         every part is congruent to ±2 or ±3 (mod 12).
     (2) Number of partitions of n into parts different from 2 such that the difference of two consecutive parts is at least
         2, and is exactly 2 or 3 only if their sum is a multiple of 3 is the same as the number of partitions into distinct
         parts congruent to 1, 3, 5, or 0 (mod 6).

The difference conditions common to both the identities forbid consecutive differences equaling 0 or 1 and additionally
following patterns are forbidden:
     (1) 3j + (3j + 2).
     (2) (3j + 1) + (3j + 3).
     (3) (3j + 1) + (3j + 4).
     (4) (3j + 2) + (3j + 5).
For the first identity, the initial conditions stipulate that
     (7) No 1s are allowed.
For the second identity, the initial conditions stipulate that
     (7) No 2s are allowed.
