                                                                                       1. Introduction

                                              One of the standard questions in probability theory is characterizing the behavior of a random variable
                                           around its expectation. In this note, we study lower bounds on the probability that X exceeds its mean,
                                           which equals p. In general, one cannot expect a lower bound better than p, because of the following
                                           example. If m = 1 and w1 = 1, then X = v1 and P[X ≥ E[X]] = P[v1 = 1] = p. The following conjecture
                                           asserts that this example is indeed the worst one, at least when p < 1/3.
                                                                                                                                   Pm
                                           Conjecture 1. Let 0 ≤ p ≤ 1/3 be a fixed real number. Let w ∈ Rm      >0 be such that      i=1 wi = 1, and let
                                           v ∈ {0, 1}m be a random vector with i.i.d. Bernoulli(p) coordinates. Then
                                                                                                 P[⟨v, w⟩ ≥ p] ≥ p.
                                              To the best of our knowledge, the first result proved in this direction comes from the work of Alon,
                                           Emek, Feldman and Tennenholtz [4] on information leakage in adversarial games, where they have shown
                                           that P[X ≥ E[X]] ≥ p(1 − p)/10 for all p, which is tight up to a constant factor for p ≤ 1/2. The question
                                           was also later asked in [8], and a variant of it was studied in [9]. The main question here is whether one
                                           can replace this by the sharp bound p, at least when p ≤ 1/3.
                                              It is also worth noting that [4] already proposes a very elegant coupling argument showing that
                                           Conjecture 1 is true if p is of the form p = n1 for some positive integer n. In fact, the proof in this note
                                           is inspired by their argument. Also, the reason for the p ≤ 1/3 assumption is that when p > 1/3 the
                                           statement is simply not true (unless p = 1/2), as we will show in Proposition 7.
                                              Let us also note that in the special case where all weights are equal (so that mX ∼ Bin(m, p)),
                                           related lower bounds for P[Bin(m, p) ≥ mp] have been studied; see, e.g., Greenberg–Mohri [11], Pelekis–
                                           Ramon [12], and Doerr [13]. Finally, let us mention that Conjecture 1 is also vaguely reminiscent of
                                           Feige’s conjecture, which states that for independent positive random variables X1 , . . . , Xn , each of which
                                           has expectation 1, we have P[X1 + · · · + Xn ≥ n + 1] ≤ 1 − 1/e.
                                              The purpose of this note is to explain an unexpected (at least to us) connection of this problem to the
                                           classical Manickam–Miklós–Singhi (MMS) conjecture on the number of nonnegative k-sums.

   This question was first raised by Bier and Manickam [1], who proved Conjecture 2 when k|n or when
n is much larger than k, and this exact form of the conjecture was later put forward by Manickam
and Miklós [2] and also by Manickam and Singhi [3]. In 2012, Tyomkyn [10] significantly improved the
quantitative bounds, proving the conjecture for n > k(4e log k)k . Later, Alon, Huang and Sudakov [6]
proved the polynomial bound and showed the conjecture when n ≥ 33k 2 . Finally, a linear bound
n ≥ 1046 k was obtained by Pokrovskiy [14].
   The following construction, due to Bier and Manickam [1], shows that the constant 4 in Conjecture 2
cannot be lowered all the way to 3. Let α > 3 be the real root of (α − 1)3 = α2 ; numerically, α ≈ 3.1479.
Fix any rational c ∈ (3, α) and take large integers k, n such that n/k = c. Set x1 = x2 = x3 = −1, and
set each of the remaining n − 3 numbers equal to 3/(n − 3). The total sum is zero. If n > 3k, then every

  The main purpose of this note is to prove the following theorem. This also shows that if the Manickam-
Miklós-Singhi conjecture is true, then Conjecture 1 holds for all p ≤ 1/4. Combined with the result of
Pokrovskiy [14], our following theorem shows that Conjecture 1 holds for all 0 ≤ p ≤ 10−46 .
Theorem 4. If Conjecture 3 holds for all pair (n, k) with n ≥ ck, then Conjecture 1 is true for p < 1/c.
In particular, if Conjecture 3 holds for all n ≥ (3 + o(1))k, then Conjecture 1 holds for all p ≤ 1/3.

Proposition 7. Conjecture 1 does not hold for any p ∈ (1/3, 1), with the exception of p = 1/2.
Proof. Let us first consider the case 1/3 < p < 1/2. In this case, choosing w = (1/3, 1/3, 1/3), we see
that ⟨w, v⟩ ≥ p if and only if v has ones on at least 2 coordinates. Thus,
                              P[⟨w, v⟩ ≥ p] = 3p2 (1 − p) + p3 = p(3p − 2p2 ).
It is not hard to verify that this quantity is smaller than p for all p ∈ (1/3, 1/2), since
                        p − p(3p − 2p2 ) = p(1 − 3p + 2p2 ) = p(1 − 2p)(1 − p) > 0.
If 1/2 < p < 1, we have a similar example, with w = (1/2, 1/2). Again, we see that ⟨w, v⟩ ≥ p if and only
if v has ones on at least 2 coordinates. Thus,

   Although the conjecture of Samuels studies a much more general problem, it does not provide an
immediate route to tackle Conjecture 1. First, Samuels’ conjecture is still widely open; in full generality
it is known only for ℓ ≤ 4, by the papers of Samuels [15, 16]. Furthermore, even if the conjecture were
fully resolved, one would still have to determine, for arbitrary coefficients ai , which of the candidate
expressions QP t achieves the minimum, and then compare the resulting expression with the polynomials
arising from i ai vi . This by itself seems like a challenging problem.

generalizations of Manicka-Miklós-Singhi conjecture and obtained some probabilistic inequalities as limit
versions of the conjecture.
