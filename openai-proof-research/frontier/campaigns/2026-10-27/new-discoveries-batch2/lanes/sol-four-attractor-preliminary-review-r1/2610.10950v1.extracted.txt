                                                                                            Abstract
                                                       String attractors provide a compact way of representing the complete factor structure of
                                                  a word: a set of positions is an attractor if every distinct factor has at least one occurrence
                                                  crossing one of the selected positions. Although the minimum attractor size is known for
                                                  several classical families of words, describing all optimal attractors is typically much more
                                                  difficult, since it requires understanding the geometry of all factor occurrences rather than
                                                  constructing a single optimal solution.
                                                      We give a complete description for the finite Thue–Morse words. Earlier work proved
                                                  that four positions are necessary and sufficient for every order n ≥ 4, but the collection of
                                                  all smallest attractors remained unknown. For every n ≥ 6, writing h = 2n−3 , we prove that
                                                  the smallest attractors are exactly the two reflected families {2h+ε1 , 3h+ε2 , 4h+ε3 , 6h+ε4 }
                                                  and {2h + ε1 , 4h + ε2 , 5h + ε3 , 6h + ε4 }, where the four offsets are chosen independently
                                                  from {0, 1}. Hence there are exactly 32 smallest attractors for every n ≥ 6. The initial
                                                  cases are genuinely exceptional: t5 has 40 smallest attractors and t4 has 87.
                                                      We also study the attractor condition independently of optimality. For every n ≥ 5,
                                                  we characterize the complete antichain of inclusion-minimal factor coverages of tn . It
                                                  consists precisely of the coverages of aa, bb, and the eight minimal unique substrings of
                                                  every generation tm , 4 ≤ m ≤ n. Thus there are exactly 8n − 22 canonical constraints,
                                                  forming an irredundant exact certificate for attractors of arbitrary cardinality.
                                                      When attention is restricted to four-position sets, this linear-size system collapses to a
                                                  constant one: it is enough to test the 24 minimal unique substrings coming from three
                                                  consecutive generations, and sixteen of these already force the two optimal families. We
                                                  also show that three generations are necessary within this natural consecutive-generation
                                                  hierarchy.
                                                      The proofs combine the recursive boundary geometry of the Thue–Morse word, a
                                                  trimmed recurrence for its minimal unique substrings, exact transport of occurrences across
                                                  generations, and a desubstitution argument that identifies all inclusion-minimal coverage
                                                  constraints.


                                          1       Introduction
                                          1.1     String attractors and the classification problem
                                          A string attractor is a small set of positions that collectively “see” every distinct factor of
                                          a word. More precisely, a set of positions Γ is a string attractor of a finite word w if every
                                          nonempty factor x of w has at least one occurrence whose interval contains a position of Γ.

    This definition is deceptively simple. A single selected position may attract many differ-
ent factors at once, because every factor occurrence crossing that position is automatically
represented. The problem is therefore highly global: choosing a good attractor requires
understanding how occurrence intervals of many unrelated factors overlap.
    String attractors were introduced by Kempa and Prezza as a common abstraction underlying
several measures of repetitiveness and dictionary-compression schemes [6]. Their minimum
size, denoted by γ(w), is now a natural combinatorial invariant of a word. Determining γ(w),
however, answers only the first of two questions.
    The first question is numerical: how many positions are necessary and sufficient to attract
every factor? The second is structural: which sets of that minimum cardinality are actually
attractors? The second problem is considerably more informative. Two word families may
have the same minimum attractor size while exhibiting completely different numbers and
geometries of optimal solutions. Knowing one optimal construction does not explain how
rigid the optimum is, whether the selected positions are forced to lie near particular recursive
boundaries, or how many independent choices remain.
    The purpose of this paper is to answer both questions completely for the finite Thue–Morse
words, and then to go one step further by describing the minimal constraints that characterize
all attractors, without restricting their cardinality.

1.2   Why the Thue–Morse word?
Let µ(a) = ab and µ(b) = ba be the Thue–Morse morphism, and let tn = µn (a). The words tn
are among the most classical objects in combinatorics on words. They combine a very simple
recursive definition with a surprisingly rich local factor structure.
    Their self-similarity makes them a natural test case for repetitiveness measures. At the
same time, that self-similarity does not make the attractor problem trivial. A factor may occur
at several scales and at different locations inside the recursive decomposition. Consequently, a
characterization of all smallest attractors cannot be obtained merely by inspecting the four
canonical boundaries that appear in one known construction.
    Kutsukake et al. [8] proved the striking identity γ(tn ) = 4 for n ≥ 4, and exhibited a
four-position attractor. Their theorem determines the optimum cardinality but does not
characterize all optimal position sets.
    More recently, Banbara et al. [1] characterized all smallest attractors for Fibonacci and
period-doubling words and explicitly left the analogous Thue–Morse problem open in the
extended version of their work. Their computational experiments also indicate why a direct
brute-force treatment is unattractive: a Walnut computation involving four free attractor
positions exceeded the available memory. This suggests that a satisfactory solution should
expose the recursive combinatorial structure behind the attractors rather than enumerate
them directly.
    We provide such a structural characterization.

1.4   Beyond smallest attractors: the canonical coverage constraints
The classification above concerns attractors of minimum cardinality. A different question is to
understand the attractor condition itself.
   For a factor x of a word w, let Covw (x) denote the set of positions covered by at least one
occurrence of x. Then
                       Γ is an attractor of w    ⇐⇒      Γ ∩ Covw (x) ̸= ∅

therefore the inclusion-minimal members of
                       H(w) = {Covw (x) : x is a nonempty factor of w}.

                                  2 + 8(n − 3) = 8n − 22.
This result is conceptually different from the classification of the smallest attractors. It charac-

These constraints form an antichain and none can be removed.

1.5   Why minimal unique substrings appear
Minimal unique substrings, or MUSs, play a central role in the proof. A MUS is a factor
that occurs exactly once but becomes repeated as soon as either end is removed. Since its
occurrence is unique, every attractor must cross that occurrence. Thus MUSs provide natural
necessary constraints.
     For Thue–Morse words, Kishi et al. [7] gave a closed characterization of all MUSs. We
use that result as an external structural ingredient. What is less immediate is how MUSs
belonging to different generations behave when they are all viewed inside the same larger word
tn . A factor that is unique in its source word tm generally acquires several occurrences in tn .
The coverage relevant to the attractor problem is the union of all those occurrences.
     The key technical mechanism is a particularly simple recurrence between consecutive
generations. The MUS of generation m + 1 corresponding to a MUS M of generation m

1.6   From 8n − 22 constraints to a constant certificate
The full canonical system contains 8n − 22 constraints, which is optimal when attractors
of arbitrary cardinality are considered. Smallest attractors, however, contain exactly four
positions. This severe cardinality restriction introduces additional geometric rigidity.
    We prove that, for four-position sets, it is enough to test only the MUSs belonging to
three consecutive generations: MUS(tn ) ∪ MUS(tn−1 ) ∪ MUS(tn−2 ). There are exactly 24 such
factors. Thus, for every n ≥ 6, a four-position set is an attractor if and only if it intersects
the ambient coverage of each of these 24 factors. This depth-three statement is best possible
within the natural hierarchy obtained by taking consecutive MUS generations: the MUSs of tn
and tn−1 alone do not suffice.
    The proof of the complete classification needs even less. We isolate sixteen carefully chosen
MUS-derived coverage sets. Four of them are pairwise disjoint, so a four-position hitting
set is forced to place exactly one position in each of four regions. The remaining witnesses
progressively restrict those positions until only the two families FnL and FnR remain. This
sixteen-factor transversal argument is the combinatorial core of the classification.

1.7   Proof ideas
Two different recursive mechanisms underlie the two main results.
    For the characterization of the smallest attractors, the crucial object is the recursive
boundary geometry of the Thue–Morse word. We prove a centered-occurrence property: every
factor of length at least two has an occurrence containing both endpoints of one of four
distinguished recursive boundaries. Consequently, any set choosing one endpoint of each of the
appropriate four boundaries attracts every factor of length at least two; the one-letter factors
are then checked separately. This gives sufficiency.
    Necessity is proved through transported MUS coverages. Their geometry produces the
sixteen-factor witness system described above and forces every four-position attractor into one
of the two stable families.
    For the canonical coverage theorem, the argument is different. We first show that an
inclusion-minimal coverage admits a shortest representative that is two-sided frequency-critical:
removing either its first or its last symbol strictly increases the number of occurrences. A
synchronization argument then gives a unique morphic phase for every sufficiently long factor.
Critical factors can therefore be desubstituted, and criticality is preserved under the descent.
    Iterating this process reduces every long critical factor to one of the eight critical factors
of length four, which are exactly the MUSs of t4 . Reversing the descent recovers the MUS
hierarchy across all generations. Finally, exact occurrence geometry shows that these MUS-
derived coverages, together with the coverages of aa and bb, are pairwise incomparable. This
proves that they are exactly the inclusion-minimal factor coverages.

1.8   Main contributions
Our results can be summarized as follows.

1. We completely characterize the smallest string attractors of every finite Thue–Morse word.
   For n ≥ 6, there are exactly two stable families and 32 solutions; t5 and t4 are exceptional,
   with 40 and 87 solutions, respectively.

2. For every n ≥ 5, we determine all inclusion-minimal factor coverages of tn . There are
   exactly 8n − 22, namely the coverages of aa, bb, and the eight MUSs of every generation
   from 4 through n. They form the canonical irredundant exact certificate for attractors of
   arbitrary cardinality.

3. For four-position sets, we obtain a constant-size exact certificate consisting of the 24 MUSs
   of three consecutive generations. We show that three generations are necessary within this
   consecutive-generation hierarchy.

4. We isolate a sixteen-factor witness system whose coverage geometry forces precisely the two
   stable families of smallest attractors.

5. Using the MUS characterization of Kishi et al., we derive a Thue–Morse-specific trimmed
   recurrence and an exact occurrence-transport rule across generations. These provide the
   bridge from finite base data to the uniform coverage formulas used throughout the paper.

2     Related work
The present paper sits at the intersection of three lines of work: string attractors as measures
of repetitiveness, exact attractor questions for structured word families, and the combinatorics
of minimal unique substrings. We summarize only the aspects directly used or compared with
our results.

2.1   String attractors and repetitive structure
String attractors were introduced by Kempa and Prezza [6] as a unifying abstraction for
several dictionary-compression mechanisms. Their definition isolates a simple combinatorial
requirement—every distinct factor must have an occurrence crossing a selected position—while
still capturing lower bounds for a range of compressed representations.
     The subsequent combinatorial study of attractors showed that classical word families
provide a useful laboratory for understanding how the minimum attractor size interacts with
factor structure. Mantaci et al. [9, 10] studied attractors from this point of view for several
families, including Thue–Morse words. More recently, attractor-based complexity functions
have also been investigated for infinite words [3]. These directions motivate treating γ(w) not
only as an algorithmic parameter but also as a combinatorial invariant.
     At a more algorithmic level, Béal, Crochemore, and Romana [2] gave a linear-time attractor
checker based on suffix automata together with a greedy construction procedure. Such
algorithms answer the decision or construction problem for a given word; our goal is different,
namely to describe the entire family of optimum position sets for one highly structured infinite
family of finite words.




                                               5
2.2   Attractors of Thue–Morse and related morphic words
For the canonical finite Thue–Morse words, Mantaci et al. [9] initially conjectured that the
minimum attractor size grows with the order. Kutsukake et al. [8] disproved this by proving
the constant identity γ(tn ) = 4 for all n ≥ 4 and by giving a four-position construction based
on recursive boundaries.
    Dolce [4] subsequently considered arbitrary factors of the infinite Thue–Morse word. His
result shows that every such factor admits a small attractor and establishes a sharp upper
bound of five. This is complementary to the present problem: here the ambient words are the
canonical prefixes tn , and the objective is to classify all optimum position sets rather than to
construct one attractor for each factor.
    The work most directly connected with our classification is that of Banbara et al. [1]. They
determined all smallest attractors of finite Fibonacci and period-doubling words and used the
number and geometry of smallest attractors to distinguish families sharing the same minimum
cardinality. Their extended version explicitly lists the corresponding Thue–Morse classification
as an open problem. It also reports that a direct Walnut computation with four free attractor
positions exceeded the available memory, reinforcing the need for a structural treatment.
    Automatic methods provide a complementary viewpoint. Schaeffer and Shallit [12] for-
mulated attractor properties of automatic sequences in first-order logic and studied them
computationally using Walnut [11]. We use neither automatic decision procedures nor their
structural claims in our proofs; our arguments instead exploit the recursive block geometry
and explicit factor coverages of finite Thue–Morse words.

2.3   Minimal unique substrings and occurrence transport
Minimal unique substrings have been studied independently of attractors, both algorithmically
and combinatorially; see, for example, Ilie and Smyth [5]. Their relevance here is immediate:
a MUS has a unique occurrence, hence every attractor must cross that occurrence interval.
    Kishi et al. [7] recently introduced a general framework of occurrence-preserving morphisms
and applied it to finite Fibonacci and Thue–Morse words. In particular, they obtained
the closed characterization of the eight MUSs in every sufficiently large finite Thue–Morse
generation. This theorem is an external structural ingredient of our proofs.
    Our occurrence-transport lemma should be read in that context but not as a generalization
of their framework. Their position-level correspondence concerns ordinary morphic images
under suitable interference-free hypotheses. The factors needed here are instead the actual
consecutive-generation Thue–Morse MUSs, obtained by deleting one symbol from each end
of the morphic image. We prove the exact start-position correspondence for these specific
trimmed factors because their full ambient coverages are what enter the attractor witness
system.

2.4   Position of the present results
The previous results determine the minimum cardinality for tn , construct individual attractors,
characterize MUSs, or classify smallest attractors for other morphic families. The two questions
addressed here remain distinct from those contributions. First, we classify every smallest
attractor of every finite Thue–Morse word. Second, we characterize the complete family of
inclusion-minimal factor coverages and hence the canonical irredundant constraint system
defining attractors of arbitrary cardinality. The latter result goes beyond the smallest-attractor
classification and explains why the MUS hierarchy is the correct source of constraints across
scales.

3     Preliminaries and structural intuition
This section fixes the notation used throughout the paper and, more importantly, develops the
geometric picture behind the proofs. The formal definitions are standard, but two viewpoints
will be used repeatedly: factors are represented by the sets of positions covered by their
occurrences, and the Thue–Morse word is read through a hierarchy of recursively nested blocks.
Keeping these two pictures in mind makes the later witness arguments considerably easier to
follow.

3.1    Words, occurrences, and coverage sets
All positions are one-based. For integers i ≤ j, we write [i, j] = {i, i + 1, . . . , j}. If w is a finite
word, then |w| denotes its length and w[i..j] the factor from position i through position j.
    For a factor x of w, let Occw (x) = {p : w[p..p + |x| − 1] = x} be the set of starting positions
of its occurrences. Each occurrence beginning at p occupies the interval [p, p + |x| − 1]. The
coverage of x in w is the union of all these intervals:
                                 Covw (x) =                [p, p + |x| − 1].                          (1)
                                                  [

                                              p∈Occw (x)

    The distinction between an occurrence and a coverage is important. A factor can have many
occurrences, but from the attractor point of view only the union of the positions covered by
those occurrences matters. If a selected position lies in Covw (x), then at least one occurrence
of x crosses that position.
Definition 3.1 (String attractor). A set Γ ⊆ [1, |w|] is a string attractor of w if Γ∩Covw (x) ̸= ∅
for every nonempty factor x of w.
    Equivalently, the attractor problem is a hitting-set problem whose universe is the set of
positions of w and whose constraints are the factor coverages. This simple reformulation is the
bridge between the combinatorics of factors and the geometric arguments used later.

A small example. Consider w = ababa and the factor x = aba. It occurs at positions 1
and 3, hence Covw (aba) = [1, 3] ∪ [3, 5] = [1, 5]. Thus every nonempty set of positions hits
the coverage of aba, despite the fact that aba has only two occurrences. By contrast, a factor
with a unique occurrence contributes a single interval and can impose a much more localized
constraint.

3.2    Minimal coverages as the essential constraints
Let H(w) = {Covw (x) : x is a nonempty factor of w}, and let Hmin (w) denote the inclusion-
minimal members of H(w).
   The relevance of Hmin (w) is immediate but fundamental. If C, D ∈ H(w) satisfy C ⊆ D,
then every set hitting C also hits D. Hence D is redundant as a constraint once C is present.
Proposition 3.2 (Minimal-coverage reduction). For every finite word w and every Γ ⊆ [1, |w|],
            Γ is a string attractor of w      ⇐⇒        Γ ∩ C ̸= ∅ for every C ∈ Hmin (w).
Proof. The forward implication follows directly from the definition. Conversely, H(w) is finite,
so every member of H(w) contains at least one inclusion-minimal member. A set hitting every
element of Hmin (w) therefore hits the coverage of every factor.

   This proposition motivates one of the two main questions of the paper. Instead of asking
only for minimum-cardinality attractors, we can ask for the smallest possible constraint system
that defines the attractor property. For Thue–Morse words we will identify this system exactly.

3.4    The decomposition tree viewpoint
It is useful to picture tn as the root of a perfect binary decomposition tree. A node labelled by
tk has two children labelled by tk−1 and tk−1 ; a node labelled by tk has the complementary
pair of children. The leaves correspond to individual positions of tn .
     Now take an occurrence interval [i, j] with i < j. The lowest node whose block contains
both endpoints has the property that i lies in its left child and j lies in its right child. Therefore
the interval necessarily crosses the boundary between those two children.
     This observation is elementary, but it is the reason recursive boundaries enter the attractor
problem at all: every factor occurrence of length at least two crosses the center boundary of
some canonical block. The real difficulty is to show that the same factor can be realized at
one of only a few global boundaries, independent of the factor. That is the content of the
centered-occurrence property proved later.

3.5    Four canonical boundaries
For n ≥ 4, define                   n                                o
                             Kn = 2n−2 , 3 · 2n−3 , 2n−1 , 3 · 2n−2 .                             (4)
A number c ∈ Kn represents the boundary c | c + 1.
    These four boundaries arise at different levels of the recursive decomposition. Two are
the centers of the two halves of tn , one is the global center of tn , and one comes from the
four-block decomposition (3). Repeatedly descending by two generations preserves the same
center, so copies of smaller tk or tk reappear centered at one of these four locations.
    This is the geometric origin of the classical four-position attractor. Kutsukake et al. [8]
proved that γ(tn ) = 4 with n ≥ 4 and exhibited the set Kn itself as an attractor. In our
classification, the four canonical positions are not rigid: from order six onward, each can be
shifted to the adjacent endpoint of its boundary in a controlled way, and reflection creates a
second stable pattern. The later proofs explain exactly which endpoint choices remain possible.

3.6   A preview of the centered-occurrence property
The following strengthening of the boundary picture will be proved in the structural section of
the paper.

Theorem 3.3 (Centered-occurrence property — preview). Let n ≥ 4 and let x be a factor of
tn with |x| ≥ 2. Then there exist c ∈ Kn and an occurrence of x whose interval contains both
c and c + 1.

    The difference between “crosses the boundary” and “contains both endpoints” matters. If
an occurrence contains both c and c + 1, then it is hit regardless of which endpoint of the pair
we select. This is precisely what allows the εi ∈ {0, 1} freedom in the two optimal families.
    The proof uses a center-descent argument. Roughly, a canonical copy of tk centered at a
boundary contains a canonical copy of tk−2 centered at the same boundary. Consequently,
every block type appearing in the decomposition tree has a centered representative at one
of the four locations in Kn . An arbitrary occurrence can then be transferred, with the same
relative coordinates, to that centered representative.

3.7   Minimal unique substrings as localized constraints
A factor M of a finite word w is a minimal unique substring (MUS) if it occurs exactly once
and every proper factor of M occurs at least twice. Equivalently, both its longest proper prefix
and its longest proper suffix are repeated.
    MUSs are particularly natural for attractors. Since a MUS has a unique occurrence, every
attractor must select at least one position inside that occurrence interval. In other words,
every MUS supplies a necessary localized hitting constraint.
    For finite Thue–Morse words, Kishi et al. [7] obtained a closed description of all MUSs.
In the indexing used here, for every m ≥ 4 there are exactly eight of them, each of length
2m−3 + 2. Their explicit form will be stated when we begin the occurrence-transport analysis.
    Two cautions are useful from the outset. First, a MUS is unique only in its source word. If
a MUS of tm is considered as a factor of a larger word tn , with n > m, it may have several
occurrences. Its relevant constraint is then its full ambient coverage Covtn (M ). Second, the
eight MUSs of the current generation alone do not capture all four-position attractors: the
proof will need information transported from earlier generations.

3.8   Three levels of constraints
The results of the paper can now be viewed as a hierarchy of increasingly compressed hitting
systems.
    At the most general level, an attractor must hit the coverage of every factor. Proposition 3.2
reduces this to the inclusion-minimal coverages. For tn we will show that there are exactly
8n − 22 of them.
    If we additionally know that |Γ| = 4, the geometry becomes much more rigid. Then the
complete test can be reduced to the 24 MUSs of three consecutive generations. Finally, the
classification proof itself uses only sixteen carefully selected witnesses.
    Thus the proof moves through the chain

                all factor coverages =⇒ 8n − 22 canonical constraints
                                     =⇒ 24 three-generation MUS constraints
                                     =⇒ 16 forcing witnesses.

The arrows should not be interpreted as unconditional logical equivalences: the last two
reductions exploit the assumption that the candidate set has four positions. They are best

read as a roadmap for how the combinatorial structure becomes progressively more rigid near
optimum size.

4     From MUS structure to occurrence coverages
The previous section explained why minimal unique substrings are natural localized constraints
for string attractors. We now address the main technical difficulty that arises when these
constraints are compared across different Thue–Morse generations.
    A MUS of tm is unique in its source word, but it is usually no longer unique when viewed
inside a larger word tm+r . For the attractor problem, we therefore cannot keep only its original
occurrence: we need the complete set of its occurrences in the ambient word, and hence its
full coverage. Recomputing those occurrences independently at every order would hide the
recursive structure that makes a uniform proof possible.
    The goal of this section is to show that the relevant occurrence information can instead
be transported exactly from one generation to the next. There are three ingredients. First,
sufficiently long morphic images have a forced alignment with the canonical Thue–Morse
blocks. Second, consecutive MUSs are not ordinary morphic images: one symbol is deleted at
each end. Third, despite this trimming, occurrence starts obey a particularly simple doubling
rule. Together, these facts reduce all later coverage calculations to a small table at the first
few orders.

4.1   The MUS hierarchy
For a word u, define EXT(u) = {aua, aub, bua, bub}. Kishi et al. [7] characterize the MUSs of
finite Thue–Morse words in a closed form. Translated to our indexing, their theorem gives the
following statement.

4.2   Synchronization: why the morphic phase is forced
The Thue–Morse morphism has constant length two. Consequently, the word tN = µ(tN −1 )
comes with a canonical partition into two-letter image blocks, each equal to ab or ba. The first
question is whether an occurrence of a word of the form µ(U ) could be shifted by one position
and still look like a valid morphic image.
    For |U | ≥ 2, the answer is no. The reason is local. A shifted copy of two consecutive image
blocks would cross two consecutive canonical block boundaries. For both crossing pairs to be
valid Thue–Morse image blocks, three consecutive preimage letters would have to be equal,
but the Thue–Morse word contains neither aaa nor bbb.

Lemma 4.2 (Synchronization). Let U be a binary word with |U | ≥ 2. Every occurrence of
µ(U ) in a finite Thue–Morse word starts at an odd position.

Proof. First observe that no finite Thue–Morse word contains aaa or bbb. Indeed, every interval
of three consecutive positions contains a complete canonical image block, and every such block
is either ab or ba.
    Suppose for a contradiction that an occurrence of µ(U ) in tN = µ(tN −1 ) starts at an even
position 2j. Because |U | ≥ 2, the first two length-two blocks of this occurrence straddle two
consecutive boundaries between canonical images. Let zj , zj+1 , zj+2 be the corresponding three
consecutive preimage letters.
    The pair across the first canonical boundary is zj zj+1 , where zj denotes the complement
of zj . In order for this pair to equal one of the valid image blocks ab or ba, its two letters
must differ; over the binary alphabet this is equivalent to zj = zj+1 . The next crossing pair
similarly forces zj+1 = zj+2 . Hence the preimage would contain three consecutive equal letters,
a contradiction.
    The same argument covers occurrences adjacent to the ends of the finite word: an even
start is never the first position, and an occurrence of length at least four contains the two
crossing pairs needed above.

Why this lemma matters. Synchronization turns a local occurrence in the large word into
an unambiguous occurrence in the preimage. Once the phase is known, aligned pairs can be
decoded uniquely. This is the mechanism that will rule out “extra” occurrences of a trimmed
MUS that do not come from the previous generation.

5.7   Why the cardinality restriction matters
Both constant-size certificates above rely essentially on the hypothesis |Γ| = 4. Without that
restriction, hitting three generations of MUS coverages does not characterize attractors.
    For example, in t6 the five-position set Γ0 = {4, 12, 24, 34, 48} hits the ambient coverages
of all 24 factors in MUS(t6 ) ∪ MUS(t5 ) ∪ MUS(t4 ), but misses Covt6 (bb). It therefore passes
the three-generation MUS test while failing to be an attractor.
    This example explains why the next part of the extended version requires a different
viewpoint. To characterize attractors of arbitrary cardinality, one must identify the complete
antichain of inclusion-minimal factor coverages rather than rely only on a constant number of
recent MUS generations.

6     Canonical minimal factor coverages
The constant-size certificates of the previous section exploit a very strong hypothesis: the

MUS coverages can force the entire geometry of a smallest attractor. For arbitrary position
sets, however, the example at the end of Section 5.7 shows that three recent MUS generations
are no longer enough.
    The right object is therefore not a fixed collection of factors but the partial order of all
factor coverages under inclusion. This section identifies its minimal elements completely.
Besides giving an exact certificate for attractors of arbitrary cardinality, the result reveals
a second recursive structure hidden in the Thue–Morse word: every essential long coverage
constraint descends, under the morphism, to one of the eight order-four minimal unique
substrings.

(1) Exhaustion: show that every inclusion-minimal factor coverage belongs to Dn .

6.1   From minimal coverages to critical factors
Suppose that two factors have the same coverage. The shorter one is preferable when studying
minimality, because it places a stronger local requirement on the occurrence structure. This
simple observation leads to the following notion.
    For a factor x of length at least two, let x− and x+ denote respectively its prefix and suffix
of length |x| − 1.
Definition 6.1 (Two-sided frequency criticality). A factor x of a finite word w is two-sided
frequency-critical if

                | Occw (x− )| > | Occw (x)|   and      | Occw (x+ )| > | Occw (x)|.

Thus deleting either the first or the last symbol creates at least one additional occurrence.
    The definition is deliberately phrased in terms of frequencies rather than uniqueness. A
MUS is an extreme example: it occurs once, while its longest proper prefix and suffix occur
at least twice. In the present section we must also deal with factors that are repeated in the
ambient word, so ordinary MUS terminology is no longer sufficient.
Lemma 6.2 (Critical representatives). Let C ∈ Hmin (w) and suppose that C has a factor
representative of length at least two. If x is a shortest such representative, then x is two-sided
frequency-critical.
Proof. Assume first that | Occw (x− )| = | Occw (x)|. Every occurrence of x induces an occurrence
of x− at the same starting position. Equality of the two cardinalities therefore implies that
every occurrence of x− extends one position to the right to an occurrence of x. Consequently,
Covw (x− ) ⊆ Covw (x) = C. Since C is inclusion-minimal in H(w), the inclusion must be an
equality. But then x− is a shorter factor representative of C, contradicting the choice of x.

                                               19
   Hence | Occw (x− )| > | Occw (x)|. The argument for x+ is symmetric after indexing occur-
rences by their final positions.

   The lemma is elementary, but conceptually important: it converts a statement about the
poset of coverage sets into a local statement about how occurrence frequencies change when
one symbol is removed.

6.2   Unique phase and critical descent
We next exploit a special feature of the Thue–Morse morphism. A sufficiently long factor
determines the parity class of all its occurrences. Informally, after reading four symbols one
already knows where the canonical length-two image blocks begin. This makes desubstitution
unambiguous.
    For λ, ρ ∈ {0, 1}, write trimλ,ρ (z) = z[1 + λ .. |z| − ρ]. Thus λ = 1 removes the first symbol
and ρ = 1 removes the last one.

Lemma 6.3 (Factor synchronization and critical descent). Every factor x of tn with |x| ≥ 4
has a unique morphic phase. More precisely, there are uniquely determined λ, ρ ∈ {0, 1} and
a factor v of tn−1 such that x = trimλ,ρ (µ(v)), and the occurrences of x in tn correspond
bijectively to the occurrences of v in tn−1 , with the appropriate doubled-start offset.
    If, in addition, x is two-sided frequency-critical and |x| ≥ 5, then λ = ρ = 1,      x=
trim(µ(v)), and v is two-sided frequency-critical in tn−1 .

Proof. The ten length-four factors of the Thue–Morse word split into two disjoint phase classes.
With one-based positions,
                                    abab, abba, baab, baba
start only at odd positions, whereas

                              aaba, aabb, abaa, babb, bbaa, bbab

start only at even positions. This can be read directly from two consecutive canonical images
µ(a) = ab and µ(b) = ba and from the factors crossing an image boundary.
    The length-four prefix of x therefore fixes the phase of every occurrence of x. Completing
at most one symbol on each side aligns the factor with canonical image blocks. Whenever a
completion symbol is needed, it is forced because the two symbols of every image block are
complementary. Decoding the aligned blocks gives a unique factor v and the claimed bijection
between occurrence starts.
    Now assume that x is critical. If λ = 0, then x begins at an image boundary. Its suffix x+
has length at least four and starts in the opposite phase. Since that phase is unique, every
occurrence of x+ has a uniquely determined preceding symbol, and that symbol reconstructs
x. Hence | Occtn (x+ )| = | Occtn (x)|, contradicting criticality. Thus λ = 1. The symmetric
argument applied to the prefix x− gives ρ = 1.
    Finally, when x = trim(µ(v)), the same phase correspondence identifies occurrences of
x and x+ with occurrences of v − and v + , respectively. Therefore the two strict frequency
  −

inequalities for x transfer directly to v.

   The key consequence is recursive: a long essential factor does not merely desubstitute; it
desubstitutes to another factor satisfying the same criticality condition. We can therefore
descend repeatedly until length four.

6.9   The resulting hierarchy of certificates

information. This distinction is useful beyond the particular formulas: it separates the intrinsic
complexity of the attractor property from the much more rigid geometry of optimum solutions.

8     Independent computational verification
The theorems above are proved combinatorially. Computation is used only as an independent
consistency check and to certify the explicitly finite exceptional cases. Because several formulas
in the proof involve many interval endpoints, an implementation that follows a substantially
different route is useful for detecting transcription errors, indexing shifts, or unnoticed boundary
cases.

8.1    A verifier that does not assume the answer
The independent program constructs tn directly from the parity of the binary digit sum of
each position, rather than recursively reusing the routines from which the symbolic formulas
were derived. It then performs the following steps.

    1. It enumerates every distinct nonempty factor of the ambient word tn .


                                                     27
   2. For each factor it finds all, possibly overlapping, occurrences by an ordinary exact string
      search.

   3. It represents the coverage as the ordinary set of text positions belonging to at least one
      occurrence interval.

   4. It computes the inclusion-minimal coverage antichain by direct pairwise set containment.

   5. Independently, it searches for transversals of the coverage system up to cardinality four.

    The transversal search is exhaustive. At a partial solution it selects a coverage not yet hit
and branches over every position of that coverage. Every completing transversal must choose
at least one of those positions, so no solution can be lost. Memoization merges identical search
states, but no expected attractor family, expected count, or geometric restriction is used to
prune a branch. Expected values are compared with the output only after the enumeration is
complete.
    This distinction matters methodologically. The verifier is not a computer-assisted proof of
the uniform theorems; rather, it is a second implementation of the definitions that can falsify
the symbolic derivations on all tested orders.

8.4   A second check of the interval geometry
The sixteen-witness proof can be checked in a way that is independent of the enumeration
of particular powers of two. Write h = 4k and partition [1, 8h] at every interval start and at
every interval end plus one. Each event position is an affine integer function of k. For k ≥ 3
their order is fixed, producing 49 cells on which membership in all sixteen witness coverages is
constant.
    Exactly two products of four cells—one cell inside each of A2 , B7 , B2 , A7 —hit all sixteen
witnesses. Every selected cell contains two positions, so each product describes 24 = 16
quadruples. These are precisely the two stable families. The remaining integer value k = 2,
corresponding to h = 8, gives the same products when checked separately.
    This parametric computation verifies the interval arithmetic of the sixteen-witness lemma
for every multiple h = 4k ≥ 8, not merely for the powers of two arising from Thue–Morse words.
The separate MUS-lifting check verifies that these intervals are indeed the factor coverages
claimed in the proof.


9     Discussion
The results admit two complementary interpretations. One concerns the geometry of optimum
solutions; the other concerns the structure of the attractor condition itself.

9.1   Why the number of smallest attractors stabilizes
For n ≥ 6, every smallest attractor chooses one endpoint from the three mandatory boundary
pairs
                      {2h, 2h + 1},     {4h, 4h + 1},     {6h, 6h + 1},
and chooses its fourth position from exactly one of the reflected pairs

                                {3h, 3h + 1},         {5h, 5h + 1}.

Once these five two-position blocks are separated, increasing n merely scales their distances.
It does not create new combinatorial choices. This is why the number of smallest attractors
becomes independent of the word length: 2 · 24 = 32.


                                                29
    The exceptional family E5 shows what fails one generation earlier. At n = 5 two central
positions can still cooperate across a short gap in a way that is impossible once the relevant
intervals have separated. Thus the transition from 40 to 32 solutions is not an arbitrary
small-order anomaly; it marks the point at which the recursive boundary geometry becomes
fully separated.

9.2   Canonical constraints versus optimum certificates
The minimal-coverage theorem reveals a different form of structure. Without any cardinality
restriction, the attractor condition has an exact canonical description using 8n − 22 pairwise
incomparable constraints. By Lemma 6.9, none of them can be removed from an exact
certificate drawn from factor coverages.
    At optimum cardinality the situation changes drastically. Four positions cannot hit the
constraints independently: satisfying some of them already determines where the remaining
positions must lie. This extra combinatorial information collapses the linear-size canonical
system to the 24 MUS coverages of three consecutive generations, and only sixteen carefully
chosen members are needed to force the final geometry.
    It is useful to keep these two statements conceptually separate:

                      all attractors ⇐⇒ 8n − 22 canonical constraints ,

whereas
                 |Γ| = 4 =⇒ 24 MUS constraints =⇒ 16 forcing witnesses .
The first line describes the intrinsic constraint complexity of the word. The second exploits
the rigidity of a four-position transversal.

9.3   What is specific to Thue–Morse?
Several ingredients of the proof use special features of the Thue–Morse morphism. The
explicit four-boundary geometry, the exact list of eight MUSs per generation, and the two
stable attractor families are specific to this word. The particularly simple trimmed recurrence
M (m+1) = trim(µ(M (m) )) also depends on the binary uniform morphism and on the closed
MUS description of Kishi et al.
    Other parts of the argument are more portable. The passage from arbitrary factor coverages
to inclusion-minimal ones is completely general. So is the canonical irredundancy lemma.
More suggestively, the Section 6 strategy

           minimal coverage ⇒ frequency-critical representative ⇒ desubstitution

uses recognizability-like synchronization to reduce a global coverage question to a finite set of
short factors. This suggests a possible template for other uniformly morphic families whenever
a sufficiently strong phase synchronization and a manageable description of critical factors are
available.
    Similarly, the certificate-depth viewpoint does not depend on the numerical value three.
For Thue–Morse, current-generation MUSs constrain candidate positions locally, while two
older generations provide the missing global information. This motivates studying, for other
morphic families, the minimum number of consecutive source generations needed to certify
smallest attractors and whether such a depth can be controlled by recognizability or occurrence-
preservation properties of the underlying morphism.
    These are directions suggested by the present proof rather than claims established here. In
particular, we do not assert that bounded-depth MUS certificates exist for every uniformly
morphic family.

                                               30
9.4   The role of occurrence transport
Occurrence transport is the technical bridge connecting the local MUS description with the
global attractor geometry. A MUS is unique only in its source word. Once embedded in a
later generation it may occur many times, and the attractor condition depends on the union of
all of these occurrences. The exact doubling law makes those ambient coverages computable
from constant base data.
    The point is therefore not merely that one can generate the next MUS from the previous
one. What matters is that the complete set of occurrence positions is transported exactly.
This is what allows both kinds of results in the paper: the linear-size canonical certificate for
arbitrary attractors and the constant-size witness system for optimum four-position sets.

9.5   Computation as verification rather than discovery in the proof
The final theorems are uniform and do not rely on checking a finite range of orders. Computation
nevertheless plays a useful supporting role. It resolves the genuinely finite exceptional cases,
gives an independent implementation of the definitions, and checks delicate interval and
indexing calculations on a range large enough to expose the recursive pattern.
    Keeping this role explicit is important. The structural proof explains why the answer
stabilizes, why the value is 32, why three MUS generations are needed, and why the minimal-
coverage count is 8n − 22. Exhaustive computation alone would confirm these patterns for
particular orders but would not supply those explanations.


10     Conclusion
We have completely characterized the smallest string attractors of the finite Thue–Morse words.
The number of smallest attractors is

                                    87,   40,   32,   32, . . .

from orders four onward. For every n ≥ 6, the stable value 32 arises from two explicit reflected
families obtained by independently selecting endpoints of four recursive boundary pairs.
    The classification is accompanied by a broader structural result. For every n ≥ 5, the
complete inclusion-minimal factor-coverage antichain consists of the coverages of aa, bb, and
the eight MUSs of every source generation from four through n. Its size is exactly 8n − 22,
and it is the unique irredundant exact certificate at the level of factor coverages for attractors
of arbitrary cardinality.
    Under the minimum-cardinality restriction this universal condition collapses. The MUSs
of three consecutive generations give an exact 24-factor certificate, depth three is necessary
within that hierarchy, and a distinguished set of sixteen witnesses already forces the geometry
of every smallest attractor.
    The resulting picture has two complementary scales: a canonical linear-size constraint
system for arbitrary attractors and a constant-size certificate at optimum cardinality. The
recursive-boundary, occurrence-transport, and critical-desubstitution mechanisms behind these
two scales are discussed in Section 9.

B      Details of the independent verification
The independent verifier is intentionally separate from the implementation used during the
derivation of the symbolic formulas. Its construction follows the definitions directly and does
not import helper routines from the original program.
    For each tested order it generates the Thue–Morse word, enumerates every distinct factor,
computes all overlapping occurrences, builds the corresponding coverage set, removes dupli-
cate coverage sets, and then filters the remaining family by inclusion. For the transversal
computation it branches only on positions belonging to a currently unhit constraint; this is an
exhaustive hitting-set search, not a heuristic one.
    Besides the values in Table 2, the verifier performs the following independent checks:

    • the complete t4 and t5 antichains in Appendix A;

    • equality of the exhaustive attractor sets with the descriptions in Propositions 7.2 and 7.1;

    • the trimmed MUS identity and full occurrence transport through ambient order ten;

    • the centered/paired-boundary property through t9 ;

    • every proposed stable attractor against every distinct factor for 6 ≤ n ≤ 9;

    • the minimal-coverage theorem for 5 ≤ n ≤ 9;

    • the short critical-factor lists and critical descent through order nine;

    • the depth-two counterexample and the five-position limitation of the stable MUS certifi-
      cate.

    No random sampling is used in any of these tests.
