 A note on complete evolution algebras 
 
 
 Xabier García-Martínez 
 
  and  
 Andrés Pérez-Rodríguez 
 
 Xabier García Martínez, Andrés Pérez Rodríguez CITMAga & Department of Mathematics, Universidade de Santiago de Compostela, 15782 Santiago de Compostela, Spain

 Abstract. 
    
 This short note provides positive answers to two conjectures of Camacho, Khudoyberdiyev, and Omirov on the classification of complete evolution algebras. Our approach is based on analysing the solution set of a generic non-linear polynomial system of equations using elementary tools from algebraic geometry. We also obtain new results on subalgebras and idempotents of evolution algebras, and conclude by proposing a conjecture that may characterise solvable evolution algebras. 
  
 
 
 Key words and phrases:  Evolution algebras, complete evolution algebras, subalgebras, idempotents
 
 1991 Mathematics Subject Classification 17D92, 17A60
 
 
 1.  Introduction 

 
 Evolution algebras are commutative but typically non-associative algebras. They were introduced by J. P. Tian and P. Vojtěchovský in 2006  [ 12 ]  as an algebraic framework for modelling non-Mendelian inheritance. Two years later, Tian’s monograph  [ 11 ]  further developed these ideas, highlighting the relevance of evolution algebras for describing the reproductive behaviour of asexual organisms through self-replication rules. This biological motivation naturally leads to the following algebraic notion. Formally, an evolution algebra over a field  𝕂 \mathbb{K}  is a  𝕂 \mathbb{K} -algebra  ℰ \mathcal{E}  which admits a distinguished basis  B = { e 1 , … , e n , … } B=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n},\dots\} , called a  natural basis , such that  e i ​ e j = 0 e_{i}e_{j}=0  for all  i ≠ j i\neq j . 
 
 
 The theory of evolution algebras is currently a very active area of research. In particular, one of the main challenges is their classification. This problem has been addressed in low dimensions (see  [ 3 ,  5 ] ), and later for the nilpotent case (see  [ 7 ,  8 ] ). As expected, complete classifications are hard to obtain, and most contributions instead focus on analysing specific intrinsic structural properties and classifying the algebras that satisfy them. 
 
 
 In this note we focus on complete evolution algebras. To better understand what this property implies, let us briefly recall how subalgebras of evolution algebras behave. The key point is that evolution algebras are not, in general, closed under taking subalgebras (see  [ 2 , Example 1.4.1] ). Consequently, three notions naturally arise when studying subalgebras: ordinary subalgebras, subalgebras admitting a natural basis, and subalgebras admitting a natural basis that extends to a natural basis of the entire algebra. Regarding the latter notion, an evolution algebra  ℰ \mathcal{E}  is said to be  complete  if any subalgebra of  ℰ \mathcal{E}  admits a natural basis which can be extended to one of  ℰ \mathcal{E} . This notion was investigated in  [ 4 ] , which concludes with two conjectures concerning both the structure and the classification of complete evolution algebras. 
 
 
 The purpose of this note is to prove these two conjectures by reducing them to a result on the existence and shape of solutions of a certain non-linear polynomial system, established using elementary tools of algebraic geometry, and to explore several further consequences that follow from it. 
 
 
 The text is structured into three sections. Following this introduction, Section  2  is devoted to the proof of the key result, which guarantees the existence of a solution with at least two non-zero components to a certain polynomial system. This result underlies all the consequences for evolution algebras presented in Section  3 , where we establish results concerning subalgebras and idempotents, as well as the classification of the so-called complete evolution algebras.

 3.  Consequences on evolution algebra structures 

 
 The previous theorem yields several noteworthy implications for the structure theory of evolution algebras. Before presenting these consequences, we fix some notation. Throughout this section, we work with complex finite-dimensional evolution algebras. Given a natural basis  B = { e 1 , … , e n } B=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}  of an evolution algebra  ℰ \mathcal{E} , the scalars  a i ​ j ∈ ℂ a_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}  satisfying  e i 2 = ∑ j = 1 n a i ​ j ​ e j e_{i}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}e_{j}  are called the  structure constants  of  ℰ \mathcal{E}  relative to  B B . The matrix  M B ​ ( ℰ ) = ( a i ​ j ) i , j = 1 n M_{B}(\mathcal{E})=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}  is said to be the  structure matrix  of  ℰ \mathcal{E}  relative to  B B . Finally, recall that an evolution algebra  ℰ \mathcal{E}  is called  regular  (or  perfect ) if  ℰ = ℰ 2 \mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}^{2} , or equivalently, any of its structure matrices is invertible. 
 
 
 3.1.  Existence of non-zero proper subalgebras 

 
 The system ( 2.1 ) does not only arise in  [ 4 ] , it appears naturally in  [ 9 , Lemma 1] , where a one-to-one correspondence is established between all non-zero one-dimensional subalgebras of a regular evolution algebra  ℰ \mathcal{E}  and the non-trivial solutions of the system 
 

 
 (3.1) 
 
 ( x 1 2 x n 2 ) = ( M B ​ ( ℰ ) t ) − 1 ​ ( x 1 x n ) . \begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{2}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}^{2}\end{pmatrix}=\big(M_{B}(\mathcal{E})^{t}\big)^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}\end{pmatrix}. 
 
 
 Since  ℰ \mathcal{E}  is regular, the structure matrix  M B ​ ( ℰ ) M_{B}(\mathcal{E})  is invertible, and therefore Theorem  2.1  applies directly. We thus have the following straightforward consequence. 
 
 
 Theorem 3.1 . 
 
 Every complex evolution algebra  ℰ \mathcal{E}  admits a non-zero proper subalgebra. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 If an evolution algebra  ℰ \mathcal{E}  is regular, then the result follows straightforwardly from Theorem  2.1 . If  ℰ \mathcal{E}  is abelian, i.e.,  ℰ 2 = 0 \mathcal{E}^{2}=0 , then the result follows automatically from the fact that every subspace of an abelian algebra is a subalgebra. Otherwise, if  ℰ \mathcal{E}  is neither regular nor abelian, then  0 ⊊ ℰ 2 ⊊ ℰ 0\subsetneq\mathcal{E}^{2}\subsetneq\mathcal{E} , so  ℰ 2 \mathcal{E}^{2}  is a proper non-zero subalgebra.
∎ 
 
 
 
 It is worth mentioning that simple evolution algebras—those with no non-zero proper ideals—have already been characterised: they are precisely the regular algebras whose associated digraph is strongly connected. Thus, the relevance of the previous result lies in the fact that no analogous characterisation for subalgebras is possible, since every evolution algebra admits at least one non-zero proper subalgebra. 
 
 
 
 3.2.  Characterising complete evolution algebras 

 
 We show that the two conjectures concerning complete evolution algebras formulated in  [ 4 ] , and stated below for completeness, are correct. 
 
 
 Theorem 3.2   ( [ 4 , Conjecture 5.2] ) . 
 
 Let  ℰ \mathcal{E}  be a regular evolution algebra of dimension greater than one. Then,  ℰ \mathcal{E}  is not complete. 
 
 
 
 Theorem 3.3   ( [ 4 , Conjecture 5.3] ) . 
 
 Let  ℰ \mathcal{E}  be an  n n -dimensional non-nilpotent complete evolution algebra. Then,  ℰ \mathcal{E}  is isomorphic to one of the following pairwise non-isomorphic algebras: 
 

 
 
 { e 1 2 = e 1 } ⊕ ℂ n − 1 or { e 1 2 = e 1 } ⊕ ℰ ~ ⊕ ℂ n − s − 1 , \{e_{1}^{2}=e_{1}\}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n-1}\quad\text{or}\quad\{e_{1}^{2}=e_{1}\}\oplus\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n-s-1}, 
 
 
 where  ℰ ~ \widetilde{\mathcal{E}}  is an  s s -dimensional evolution algebra with maximal index of nilpotency and  ℂ k \mathbb{C}^{k}  denotes the  k k -dimensional zero evolution algebra. 
 
 
 
 Outline of both proofs. 
 
 The proofs of both results in  [ 4 ]  rely on the assumed validity of  [ 4 , Conjecture 5.1] . The arguments of both proofs require the existence of a solution  ( x 1 , … , x n ) (x_{1},\dots,x_{n})  of the system ( 2.1 ) with all coordinates non-zero, which then yields a one-dimensional subalgebra  span ⁡ { x 1 ​ e 1 + ⋯ + x n ​ e n } \spa\{x_{1}e_{1}+\dots+x_{n}e_{n}\}  that cannot be extended to a natural basis of the whole algebra. 
 
 
 However, we do not actually need all coordinates to be non-zero: having just two non-zero coordinates already suffices. To explain this, we recall the notion of  natural vector  introduced in  [ 1 ] , meaning a vector that can be extended to a natural basis of the whole algebra. As shown in  [ 1 , Theorem 2.4] , if  u = x 1 ​ e 1 + ⋯ + x n ​ e n u=x_{1}e_{1}+\dots+x_{n}e_{n}  is an idempotent,  u 2 = u ≠ 0 u^{2}=u\neq 0 , then  u u  is a natural vector if and only if  rank ⁡ ( { e i 2 : x i ≠ 0 } ) = 1 \rank\big(\{e_{i}^{2}\colon x_{i}\neq 0\}\big)=1 . In our situation, this condition holds only when exactly one  x i x_{i}  is non-zero. Note that the system ( 2.1 ) involves an invertible matrix, which comes from the structure matrix of a regular evolution algebra, and therefore  { e 1 2 , … , e n 2 } \{e_{1}^{2},\dots,e_{n}^{2}\}  is linearly independent. Hence, any idempotent supported on at least two basis elements whose squares are linearly independent fails to be a natural vector, giving exactly the obstruction required in the proofs.
∎ 
 
 
 
 Note that Theorems  3.2  and  3.3  together with  [ 4 , Theorem 4.2]  entirely classify complete evolution algebras. 
 
 
 Corollary 3.4 . 
 
 Let  ℰ \mathcal{E}  be an  n n -dimensional complete evolution algebra. Then,  ℰ \mathcal{E}  is isomorphic to one of the following pairwise non-isomorphic algebras: 
 

 
 
 { e 1 2 = e 1 } , ℰ ~ ⊕ ℂ n − s , { e 1 2 = e 1 } ⊕ ℂ n − 1 or { e 1 2 = e 1 } ⊕ ℰ ~ ⊕ ℂ n − s − 1 , \{e_{1}^{2}=e_{1}\},\qquad\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n-s},\qquad\{e_{1}^{2}=e_{1}\}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n-1}\qquad\text{or}\qquad\{e_{1}^{2}=e_{1}\}\oplus\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n-s-1}, 
 
 
 where  ℰ ~ \widetilde{\mathcal{E}}  is an  s s -dimensional evolution algebra with maximal index of nilpotency and  ℂ k \mathbb{C}^{k}  denotes the  k k -dimensional zero evolution algebra. 
 
 
 
 
 3.3.  Idempotents 

 
 As mentioned above, an element  u u  of an evolution algebra  ℰ \mathcal{E}  is an idempotent when it satisfies  u 2 = u u^{2}=u . Moreover, as stated in  [ 10 ] , the existence of idempotent elements in an arbitrary evolution algebra is still an open problem. Given an evolution algebra  ℰ \mathcal{E}  with natural basis  { e 1 , … , e n } \{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}  and structure matrix  M B ​ ( ℰ ) = ( a i ​ j ) M_{B}(\mathcal{E})=(a_{ij}) , an element  u = x 1 ​ e 1 + ⋯ + x n ​ e n u=x_{1}e_{1}+\dots+x_{n}e_{n}  is an idempotent if we have

 which yields the condition  ∑ j = 1 n x j 2 ​ a j ​ i = x i \sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{2}a_{ji}=x_{i}  for all  i = 1 , … , n i=1,\dots,n , or, equivalently, 
 

 
 (3.2) 
 
 M B ​ ( ℰ ) t ​ ( x 1 2 x n 2 ) = ( x 1 x n ) . M_{B}(\mathcal{E})^{t}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{2}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}^{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}\end{pmatrix}. 
 
 
 Note that ( 3.2 ) is equivalent to ( 3.1 ) when  M B ​ ( ℰ ) M_{B}(\mathcal{E})  is invertible, i.e., when  ℰ \mathcal{E}  is regular, and in this case idempotents correspond exactly to one-dimensional subalgebras. Thus, we have the following immediate consequence. 
 
 
 Theorem 3.5 . 
 
 Every complex regular evolution algebra admits an idempotent. 
 
 
 
 We conclude by proposing a new conjecture concerning evolution algebras. Recall first that an evolution algebra  ℰ \mathcal{E}  is called  solvable  if there exists  n ∈ ℕ n\in\mathbb{N}  such that  ℰ ( n ) = 0 \mathcal{E}^{(n)}=0 , where the sequence  ( ℰ ( k ) ) \big(\mathcal{E}^{(k)}\big)  is defined inductively by  ℰ ( 1 ) = ℰ \mathcal{E}^{(1)}=\mathcal{E}  and  ℰ ( k + 1 ) = ℰ ( k ) ​ ℰ ( k ) \mathcal{E}^{(k+1)}=\mathcal{E}^{(k)}\mathcal{E}^{(k)}  for all  k ∈ ℕ k\in\mathbb{N} . Observe that the absence of idempotent elements is a necessary condition for an evolution algebra to be solvable. Indeed, if an idempotent  u ∈ ℰ u\in\mathcal{E}  exists, then  u ∈ ℰ ( n ) u\in\mathcal{E}^{(n)}  for all  n ∈ ℕ n\in\mathbb{N} , which contradicts the assumption that  ℰ \mathcal{E}  is solvable. 
 
 
 Conjecture 3.6 . 
 
 Let  ℰ \mathcal{E}  be a complex evolution algebra. Then, the following assertions are equivalent: 
 
 (i)  
 
 ℰ \mathcal{E}  is solvable; 
 
 (ii)  
 
 ℰ \mathcal{E}  admits no idempotents; and 
 
 (iii)  
 
 the system ( 3.2 ) only admits the trivial solution. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Let us provide some evidence in support of Conjecture  3.6 . 
 
 •  
 
 If  n = 1 n=1 , then the conjecture is obviously true. 
 
 •  
 
 If  n = 2 n=2 , we may rely on the classification given in  [ 5 ] . If  ℰ \mathcal{E}  is regular, Theorem  3.5  already guarantees the existence of an idempotent. Hence, it remains to check that every non-solvable isomorphism class also contains an idempotent.
According to the classification, the non-solvable algebras in dimension 2 are 
 

 
 
 ℰ 1 : e 1 2 = e 1 , e 2 2 = 0 and ℰ 2 : e 1 2 = e 2 2 = e 1 , \mathcal{E}_{1}\colon e_{1}^{2}=e_{1},e_{2}^{2}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{E}_{2}\colon e_{1}^{2}=e_{2}^{2}=e_{1}, 
 
 
 and both clearly contain the idempotent  e 1 e_{1} . Thus, Conjecture  3.6  holds for  n = 2 n=2 . 
 
 
 
 
 Unlike the situation for nilpotent evolution algebras (see  [ 6 , Theorem 3.4] ), to our knowledge there is no characterisation of solvable evolution algebras. Therefore, if true, this conjecture would provide a structural characterisation of solvable evolution algebras.
