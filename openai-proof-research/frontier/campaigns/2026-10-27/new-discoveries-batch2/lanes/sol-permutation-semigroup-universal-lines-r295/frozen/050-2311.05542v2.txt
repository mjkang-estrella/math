                                           Counterexamples to conjectures on the occupancy fraction of graphs
                                                                              Stijn Cambie ∗              Jorik Jooken ∗




arXiv:2311.05542v2 [math.CO] 28 Nov 2023
                                                                                               Abstract
                                                       The occupancy fraction of a graph is a (normalized) measure on the size of independent sets
                                                   under the hard-core model, depending on a variable (fugacity) λ. We present a criterion for finding
                                                   the graph with minimum occupancy fraction among graphs with a fixed order, and disprove five
                                                   conjectures on the extremes of the occupancy fraction and (normalized) independence polynomial
                                                   for certain graph classes of regular graphs with a given girth.


                                           1       Introduction

                                           searches. For notation and deﬁnitions, the reader is referred to Subsection 1.1. The counterexamples

3     On maximum occupancy fraction
In this section, we disprove [11, conj. 3 & 4].

Theorem 3.1. There exists a cubic graph G of girth at least 7 for which αG pλq ą αH3,8 pλq for every
λ ą 17.

Proof. Let G “ G38 be the graph in https://houseofgraphs.org/graphs/49521 [3]. We can com-
pute αG pλq and αH3,8 pλq explicitly from ~ipGq “ p1, 38, 646, 6498, 43111, 199120, 658882, 1583954, 2777315
, 3537622, 3238356, 2097330, 947518, 300924, 72142, 14802, 2660, 380, 38, 2q
and ~ipH3,8 q “ p1, 30, 390, 2890, 13515, 41736, 86610, 120690, 111225, 66090, 24948, 6420, 1370, 240, 30, 2q.
Now αG pλq ´ αH3,8 pλq is a rational function, for which the sign depends on a polynomial of degree 23.
This polynomial has only one change of sign, and by Descartes’ rule of signs, this implies that it has
a single positive root. We conclude that αG pλq ą αH3,8 pλq for λ larger than the positive root of this
polynomial of degree 23.

    Analogously, we get the following theorem.

Theorem 3.2. There exists a 4-regular graph G with girth at least 5 such that αG pλq ą αH4,6 pλq for
every λ ą 37.

Proof. Let G “ G32 be the graph in https://houseofgraphs.org/graphs/49999. We can compute
αG pλq and αH4,6 pλq explicitly, and conclude that αG pλq ą αH4,6 pλq for λ larger than the positive root
of a polynomial of degree 17.

5        Conclusion
By investigating the conjectures on the extremal values of occupancy fractions for certain classes of
regular graphs with a minimum girth condition, we concluded that multiple such conjectures were
incorrect when considering all possible fugacities. As such, the number of conjectures and questions
that are likely to be true are narrowed down. These conjectures are related to the study on the number
of graph homomorphisms between graphs. A homomorphism is a map from V pGq to V pHq that maps
every edge of G to an edge of H. The number of graph homorphisms from G to H (or the number of
H-colorings on G) is denoted by hompG, Hq. Two of the main quantities generalized with the concept
of graph homomorphisms, are the number of independent sets in a graph G, |IpGq| “ hompG,             q,
and the number of q-colorings of G, which is equal to hompG, Kq q.
    One of the conjectures inspiring and summarizing questions on the number of graph homomor-
phisms was the one by Galvin, [6, conj. 1] (and its precedent by Galvin and Tetali [7]), stating that
for graphs G and H, where G is d-regular and has order n, the following inequality is true

                           hompG, Hq ď maxthompKd,d , Hqn{2d , hompKd`1 , Hqn{pd`1q u

The conjecture of Galvin was disproven by Sernau [13]. In his counterexample, H is disconnected.
Nevertheless, also when imposing that G and H are both connected, counterexamples can be found
(see Appendix B for examples where G and H are simple, connected graphs).
    We now highlight three interesting directions for future research.

Appendix
A          Details about computer search
We used a computer to determine the independence polynomial of a graph (and the occupancy fraction)
with two independent algorithms. The ﬁrst algorithm uses the built-in functions in Sage, whereas the
second algorithm was implemented in C++. The latter is a simple, yet eﬀective, backtracking algorithm
that recursively enumerates all independent sets of a graph and derives the independence polynomial
from this. The algorithm maintains an initially empty set and adds vertices recursively to this set
by considering the vertices in an arbitrary order and branching into two possibilities: including the
current vertex (if none of its neighbors are already in the set) or excluding the current vertex.
    We used the graph generator GENREG [10] to exhaustively generate all connected k-regular graphs
on n vertices with girth at least g. We calculate the occupancy fraction for λ P t0.1, 0.2, . . . , 100.0u (or
keep track of the critical graphs using Proposition 2.3 for minimizing the occupancy fraction). This
approach allowed us to ﬁnd counterexamples for [11, conj. 2] and [4, conj.]. As the number of graphs
on n vertices grows rapidly with increasing n, this approach became unfeasible for disproving the other
conjectures ([11, conj. 3-5]). Therefore, we restricted the search space further by focusing on bipartite
graphs (for maximizing the occupancy fraction) and graphs for which the automorphism group has at
most two group orbits (see [8] for more details on their enumeration). The counterexamples that we
found have between 14 and 38 vertices.
