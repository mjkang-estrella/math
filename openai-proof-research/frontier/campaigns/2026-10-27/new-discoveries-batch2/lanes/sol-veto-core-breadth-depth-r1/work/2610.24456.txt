                                                                                             Abstract
                                                     We consider the metric distortion framework for voting, where n voters and m candidates
                                                 are embedded in an unknown metric space. A voting rule selects a candidate using only voters’
                                                 ordinal preferences, and its distortion is the worst-case ratio between the social cost of the
                                                 selected candidate and the minimum social cost. We study how round complexity trades off
                                                 against total communication in deterministic voting protocols with low distortion. Deterministic
                                                 rules can achieve the optimal distortion of 3 in one round by eliciting complete rankings using
                                                 Θ(nm log m) bits, while a direct sequential implementation of PluralityVeto achieves the
                                                 same distortion using only O(n log m) bits but requires Θ(n) rounds.
                                                     Our main result is TreePluralityVeto, a deterministic voting protocol that, for every
                                                 r ≥ 2, achieves distortion at most 2r −1 using at most r rounds and O n log m + ns1/(r−1) log s
                                                 bits of total communication, where s = min{n, m}. The protocol runs PluralityVeto on a
                                                 hierarchical partition of the voters, collecting restricted rankings in parallel at each level. The
                                                 branching factor controls the tradeoff between rounds and communication, and a matching-based
                                                 analysis shows that each level increases the distortion bound by at most 2.


                                         1       Introduction
                                         To select a socially good candidate, how much information must voters communicate, and how
                                         many rounds of interaction are needed? We study this question in the metric distortion framework
                                         for voting [ABP15, ABE+ 18], where there are n voters and m candidates in an unknown metric
                                         space, and each voter ranks candidates from nearest to farthest. The social cost of a candidate is
                                         its total distance to the voters, and we evaluate a voting rule by its distortion, the worst-case ratio
                                         between the social cost of its winner and that of the optimal candidate. Distortion measures the
                                         loss from observing only ordinal rankings rather than the underlying cardinal information.
                                             Anshelevich et al. [ABP15] proved that every deterministic voting rule has distortion at least 3.
                                         Gkatzelis et al. [GHS20] introduced PluralityMatching, the first deterministic voting rule with
                                         distortion 3, and Kizilkaya and Kempe [KK22] later introduced the simpler rule PluralityVeto.
                                         These results settled the optimal distortion of deterministic rules at 3. We study the tradeoff among
                                         distortion, round complexity, and total communication for deterministic protocols.
                                             Two straightforward protocols illustrate the tradeoff between total communication and the
                                         number of rounds. Eliciting complete rankings requires one round and Θ(nm log m) bits of com-
                                         munication, and allows any voting rule to be computed locally. Alternatively, PluralityVeto
                                         can be implemented using only O(n log m) bits: each voter communicates a top choice and a veto.

However, its direct sequential implementation requires Θ(n) rounds, because each voter vetoes a
candidate based on those remaining after earlier vetoes.
    Kempe [Kem20b] proved an Ω(m/b) lower bound on distortion for deterministic one-round
mechanisms that use the same b-bit encoding for all voters. This lower bound does not directly
apply when message functions may depend on voter identity. In Appendix A, we show that any
deterministic protocol with distortion at most α requires Ω(n/α) bits of total communication, even
with only two candidates and an unlimited number of rounds. For constant α, this lower bound is
tight up to an O(log m) factor, since PluralityVeto uses O(n log m) bits. This motivates our
central question:

      What tradeoff between communication and round complexity is possible for deterministic
      voting protocols with low distortion?

   Our main result is TreePluralityVeto, a deterministic voting protocol that provides a
tradeoff among distortion, round complexity, and total communication.
Theorem 1.1. Let n be the number of voters, m the number of candidates, and s = min{n, m}.
For every integer r ≥ 2, there is a deterministic voting protocol
                                                              that achieves distortion at most
2r − 1 using at most r rounds and O n log m + ns1/(r−1) log s bits of total communication.
    In the first round, every voter reports their global favorite candidate. Voters with the same
global favorite form a group, and these groups are placed at the leaves of a tree whose branching
factor depends on the round budget. There are at most s = min{n, m} such groups. Each internal
node runs PluralityVeto among the winners of its children, by asking all voters in its subtree
to report their rankings over this restricted set of candidates. Sub-elections at nodes of the same
height run in parallel: all required rankings are collected in one round, after which their winners
are computed locally from the public transcript. A larger branching factor gives fewer levels and
hence fewer rounds, but each voter needs to rank more candidates per level.
    The analysis uses the perfect-matching certificate of PluralityVeto to compare the winner
of a sub-election with any candidate, including those absent from it. This comparison also appears
in the analysis of recursive Fractional Veto by Kizilkaya, Aggarwal, and Micha [KAM26]. We apply
this comparison across a hierarchy of disjoint voter groups, charging each node’s extra cost to its
children’s winners. Each level adds at most 2 to the distortion.

1.1   Related Work
Deterministic metric distortion. Distortion in social choice has been studied in the utilitarian
setting [PR06, BCH+ 15]. Anshelevich et al. [ABP15, ABE+ 18] introduced the metric distortion
framework, proved that every deterministic voting rule has distortion at least 3, and showed that√
Copeland achieves distortion 5. Munagala and Wang [MW19] improved the upper bound to 2 + 5
and identified a matching-based sufficient condition for distortion 3. Kempe [Kem20a]
                                                                               √      identified the
same condition and developed an LP-duality framework that recovers the 2 + 5 bound and gives
a simpler proof that the condition guarantees distortion 3. Gkatzelis et al. [GHS20] proved that
every preference profile admits a candidate satisfying the stronger condition that its domination
graph has a perfect matching, introduced the rule PluralityMatching, and settled the optimal
distortion of deterministic rules at 3. Kizilkaya and Kempe [KK22] introduced PluralityVeto,
a particularly simple voting rule that achieves the same optimal distortion. Their later work
connected the matching condition to the generalized veto core and introduced a continuous-time
variant of PluralityVeto [KK23]. For a comprehensive survey of distortion in social choice,
see [AFRSV21].

                                                 2
Randomized metric distortion. For randomized voting rules, the optimal worst-case expected
distortion remains open. Random dictatorship, which elects the favorite candidate of a uniformly
random voter, achieves expected distortion at most 3 [AP17, FFG16]. Subsequent work improved
the lower bound to above 2 [CR22, PS21] and the upper bound to below 3 [CRWW24, Fra26, Ye26,
Sha26], with the current best bounds being approximately 2.1126 [CR22] and 2.1372 [CCH+ 26],
respectively. Other work studies randomized rules with low sample complexity [GAX17, FGMP19]
and how additional samples reduce the risk of high distortion [FFM20]. Whether near-optimal
expected distortion can be achieved with low total communication and few rounds is an exciting
avenue for future work.

Communication and rounds. Communication complexity in voting was studied by Conitzer
and Sandholm [CS05], who consider the number of bits required to compute standard voting rules
exactly, and by Shah and Yu [SY26], who study the elicitation of such rules through next-best
queries. In the metric distortion framework, Kempe [Kem20b] studies the distortion achievable by
one-round mechanisms under communication constraints. Ebadian et al. [EHM24] studied mech-
anisms that elicit a bounded number of pairwise comparisons from each voter, under different
restrictions on how queries may depend on earlier answers. Prior work considered incomplete
rankings or sampled voters [AFP22]. In the utilitarian setting, prior work studied limited car-
dinal queries [ABFRV20, ES25] and tradeoffs between communication
                                                               √         and distortion [MPSW19,
MSW20]. Frank and Peters [FP26] obtained distortion 2 + 5 from approval voting informed
by plurality polls, which can be implemented in two rounds using O(n log m + nm) bits. Our
work focuses on deterministic protocols using only ordinal preferences, and on the tradeoff among
distortion, round complexity, and total communication.

Voter aggregation and representation. Borodin et al. [BLSS24] study primaries and tree-
structured elections; their general distortion bound multiplies along tree paths and depends on
participation fractions. Work on distributed metric social choice [AFRV22, AKN+ 25] studied mod-
els in which voters are partitioned into districts, each district selects a representative alternative,
and these alternatives are aggregated to select the final winner. Prior work reduced participation by
selecting a small representative panel [CMP24, EM25], or allowed deliberation in small groups that
could exploit collective knowledge of the underlying metric [GGM25]. Our protocol only uses ordi-
nal information and filters candidates through a multilevel hierarchy, with every voter participating
in each sub-election along their path to the root.

Communication model. We consider the blackboard communication model. Communication
proceeds in synchronous rounds. In each round, every voter may simultaneously write a message
on a public blackboard as a function of their ranking and the transcript from the preceding rounds.
Local computation is free. The communication complexity of a protocol is the total number of bits
written, and its round complexity is the number of rounds.
    For a deterministic voting rule f and a sub-election (U, A), once every voter in U has reported
their complete ranking over A, the winner f (U, A) can be determined from the public transcript
without further communication.

Plurality Veto and domination graphs. Our protocol uses Plurality Veto [KK22] as a sub-
routine. We review its definition and the property of its winner that is relevant to our analysis.

Definition 2.1 (PluralityVeto [KK22]). Given a set of voters U and a set of candidates A,
each candidate c ∈ A starts with score |{v ∈ U : topA (v) = c}|. The voters in U are processed
sequentially according to a veto order. Each voter vetoes their least-preferred candidate among
those whose current score is positive, decreasing that candidate’s score by one. The candidate
whose score reaches zero last is the winner of PluralityVeto(U, A).

    Plurality Veto is deterministic once its veto order is fixed. Whenever it is applied to a sub-
election (U, A), the voters in U veto in the order of their public identifiers.
    For a candidate w ∈ A, its domination graph [GHS20] with respect to (U, A) is the bipartite
graph GU,A (w) = (U, U, Ew ), where the two vertex sets are copies of U and

                                (u, v) ∈ Ew   ⇐⇒       w ⪰u topA (v) .

   For every veto order, the domination graph GU,A (w) of the winner w = PluralityVeto(U, A)
admits a perfect matching [KK22]. To see this, let each voter v ∈ U contribute one score unit
labeled v to their favorite candidate in A. When voter u casts a veto, remove any remaining score
unit from the vetoed candidate and match u to the voter v labeling that unit. Every voter casts
exactly one veto, and every score unit is removed exactly once. Moreover, w has positive score
before every veto, so w ⪰u topA (v). Hence every matched pair is an edge of GU,A (w), and they
form a perfect matching.

3        The TreePluralityVeto Protocol
Our voting protocol, TreePluralityVeto, is described in Algorithm 1.
   In the first round, every voter reports their global favorite candidate. Voters with the same
reported favorite form a group, and these groups form the leaf nodes of a public rooted tree T . We
then select a winner at every node of T in increasing order of height.1 At a leaf node x, the winner
wx is the common global favorite of its voters. At each internal node x, the available candidates
Ax consist of the distinct winners at the children of x, and the voters in the subtree rooted at x
run Plurality Veto on Ax to select the winner wx . The winner at the root of T is the final winner.

Remark 3.1 (Voting rules at internal nodes). Algorithm 1 can use any deterministic voting rule f
at its internal nodes. For concreteness, we state it with Plurality Veto, which suffices to obtain our
main result. The distortion analysis in Section 4.1 requires only that, for every sub-election (U, A),
the domination graph of the winner f (U, A) admits a perfect matching. Plurality Veto satisfies
this property because its veto process induces such a matching. The communication analysis in
Section 4.2 applies to any deterministic voting rule f .

4.2   Round and Communication Complexity
Lemma 4.2. Let n be the number of voters, m the number of candidates, and s the number of
distinct global favorite candidates. TreePluralityVeto (Algorithm 1) uses at most r rounds
and O n log m + ns1/(r−1) log s bits of total communication.
                                

Proof. In the first round, every voter reports their global favorite candidate using O(log m) bits,
for a total of O(n log m) bits. Then the voter groups and the tree T can be constructed canonically
from the public transcript and r, so no further communication is required to specify them.
    Fix h ≥ 1 and consider all internal nodes x of height h. For each such node, the candidate
set Ax can be determined from the public transcript and contains at most q candidates because
x has at most q child nodes. The candidates in Ax can be sorted by their public identifiers and
re-indexed by {1, . . . , |Ax |}. Every voter in Ux can therefore report their complete ranking over
Ax using O(|Ax | log |Ax |) = O(q log q) bits. The voter sets Ux associated with nodes of height h
are disjoint. Consequently, all rankings required for the sub-elections at nodes of height h can be
collected in one round using O(nq log q) bits of communication. Since the voting rule used at each
internal node is deterministic, the winners at these nodes can then be determined from the public
transcript without further communication.

A     A Lower Bound on Total Communication
We show that constant distortion requires Ω(n) bits of total communication even with an unlimited
number of rounds. If a deterministic protocol communicates at most n/β bits for some β > 1, then
there is an execution in which the preferences between two candidates remain undetermined for at
least a (1 − 1/β) fraction of voters. All of these voters can prefer the candidate that is not elected.
    Similar constructions have appeared in lower bounds for metric distortion (e.g., [ABP15, AFP22,
EHM24]). We formalize the argument for the blackboard communication model below.

Lemma A.1. For m ≥ 2, every deterministic voting protocol with distortion at most α ≥ 3 requires
          2n
at least α+1 bits of total communication in the worst case, regardless of the number of rounds.
Proof. Consider a line metric space. Fix two candidates w and o. Consider only profiles in which
every voter ranks w and o above all other candidates, with all other candidates placed arbitrarily
far away from the voters in a fixed order. Therefore, each voter has only two possible rankings.
    We construct a transcript while leaving some voters’ rankings undetermined. In each round,
each voter whose ranking remains undetermined has two possible messages to send (which could
be empty). If the two messages coincide, we leave the ranking undetermined. Otherwise, fix the
ranking to one that produces a nonempty message. Voters whose rankings are fixed follow the
protocol. At termination, let S be the set of voters whose rankings were fixed. Each voter in S
sends at least one bit when their ranking is fixed, so the protocol communicates at least |S| bits.
    Every completion of the undetermined rankings produces the same transcript and hence the
same winner. The winner must be in {w, o}, otherwise the distortion is unbounded. Without loss
of generality, suppose that w is elected. Complete every undetermined ranking with o ≻ w.
    We can place w at −1, o at 1, each voter in S at −ϵ or ϵ for arbitrarily small ϵ > 0, and all
remaining voters at 1. This is consistent with the completed profile. Consequently, as ϵ → 0, the
