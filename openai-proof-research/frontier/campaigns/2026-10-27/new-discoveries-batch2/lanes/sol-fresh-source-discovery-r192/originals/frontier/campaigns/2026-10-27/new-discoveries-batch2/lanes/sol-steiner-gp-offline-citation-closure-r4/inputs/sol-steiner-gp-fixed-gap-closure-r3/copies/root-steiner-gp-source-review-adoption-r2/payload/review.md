Example 2, p.675 defines Steiner intervals as the union of vertices on some
minimum tree for a terminal multiset, and convexity as containment of those
intervals. Theorem 3, p.676, proves (n+1)-Steiner convex implies n-Steiner convex
by repeating an input vertex. Theorem 4, pp.676–677, constructs a subdivided
K_(n+1) with edge paths of length n+1, adds a hub with spoke paths of length n,
and proves the outer subgraph n-Steiner convex but not (n+1)-Steiner convex.
At n=2 it gives the candidate's underlying 13-vertex graph. Its proof compares
edge costs for outside-hub routes and treats arbitrary outer terminal
multisets. It does not replace selected vertices by unequal-sized twin classes
or optimize a GP avoidance cardinality over competing subsets.

For exact coverage, pp.671–674 introduce transit functions and convexity;
pp.675–677 contain the preceding definitions/results; pp.678–682 concern
betweenness axioms, underlying hypergraph connectivity and Hall constructions;
pp.683–684 concern all-paths block intervals and Theorems 18–19; pp.684–685
contain references. These actual sections, rather than abstract/title language,
are the basis of the conclusion. Theorem 4's closing text calls a hub tree
unique; for n=2 an outer two-rim-edge tree also costs six. Our argument needs
only optimality of the hub tree, independently proved by edge projection,
and does not import that uniqueness wording.

The quantifier gap is concrete. Convexity says I(B) subset H for all terminal
multisets inside H. GP says I(B) intersect S = B for each fixed-size distinct
terminal subset of S. Neither replacing H by its complement nor repeating
terminals converts containment into this avoidance condition or bounds the
maximum over all S. The 2010 graph itself has equal maxima. Thus Theorem 4,
with its verified hypotheses, is not already the counted maximum drop.
