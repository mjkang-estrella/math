                                                                                         Abstract
                                                          Given a lower-triangular matrix of real numbers, one can ask the follow-
                                                      ing four total-positivity questions: total positivity of the triangle itself; to-
                                                      tal positivity of its row-reversal; Toeplitz-total positivity of its row sequences
                                                      (equivalent to negative-real-rootedness of the row-generating polynomials); and
                                                      coefficientwise Hankel-total positivity of the sequence of row-generating poly-
                                                      nomials. In this paper, we introduce two infinite families of lower-triangular
                                                      matrices generalising the Stirling cycle and subset triangles, parametrised by
                                                      an integer r ≥ 1; we call these the rth-order Stirling cycle and subset numbers.
                                                      We then ask the foregoing four questions for each of these triangles, leading us
                                                      to several conjectures. We then prove some of these conjectures for the case
                                                      r = 2.

                                                Key Words: Totally positive matrix, Hankel matrix, zeros of polynomials, higher-
                                                order Stirling cycle numbers, higher-order Stirling subset numbers, Stirling permuta-
                                                tions, increasing ternary trees, increasing ordered trees, branched continued fractions,
                                                permutations, set partitions, phylogenetic trees, log-concavity.

                                                Mathematics Subject Classification (MSC 2020) codes: 05A15 (Primary);
                                                05A05, 05A18, 05A19, 05A20, 11B73, 15B48, 30B70 (Secondary).

    The second-order (r = 2) Stirling cycle and subset numbers are closely related to
the second-order Eulerian numbers, as we now explain.
    Let w = w1 · · · wN be a word on a totally ordered alphabet A. A pair (wi , wi+1 )
with i ∈ [N − 1] is called an ascent (resp. descent, plateau) in case wi < wi+1
(resp. wi > wi+1 , wi = wi+1 ).5 The word w is said to be a Stirling word if, for
every letter a ∈ A, all letters between two successive occurrences of a are larger
than a. A permutation of a multiset M is said to be a Stirling permutation if it
is a Stirling word. The second-order Eulerian number nk is then defined to be the
number of Stirling permutations of the multiset {1, 1, 2, 2, . . . , n, n} with k ascents
(or k descents: they are equidistributed, since reversing the word exchanges ascents

        Three further combinatorial interpretations
    In this section we will provide three more combinatorial interpretations for the
second-order Stirling cycle numbers. In Section 4.1 we provide an interpretation
using increasing ternary trees. In Section 4.2 we introduce an interpretation involving

4.1     Interpretation III: Increasing ternary trees
     We begin by recalling [44, p. 573] the recursive definition of an m-ary tree
(1 ≤ m < ∞): it is either empty or else consists of a root together with an ordered
list of m subtrees (T0 , . . . , Tm−1 ), each of which is an m-ary tree (which may be
empty). We draw an edge from each vertex to the root of each of its nonempty
subtrees; an edge from a vertex to the root of its ith subtree (0 ≤ i ≤ m − 1) will be
called an i-edge. When m = 2, we refer to a binary tree and to its left and right
edges. When m = 3, we refer to a ternary tree and to its left, middle and right
edges.
     A rooted tree on a totally ordered vertex set is called increasing if each vertex
is higher-numbered than its parent; equivalently, the labels increase along each path
downward from the root. It is well known [44, p. 45, Proposition 1.5.3(a)] that the
number of increasing binary trees on the vertex set [n] is n!, and more generally
that the number of increasing m-ary trees on the vertex set [n] is the multifactorial

of increasing ternary trees on the vertex set [n] is (2n − 1)!!.

4.3    Interpretation V: Cyclically-ordered phylogenetic trees
    Here we introduce our fifth and final combinatorial interpretation of the second-
order Stirling cycle numbers. This interpretation will lead us to a common general-
ization of the second-order Stirling numbers of both kinds and also lead us to another
conjecture similar to Conjectures 1.3 and 1.4.
    A phylogenetic tree is a leaf-labeled rooted tree (i.e. the leaves are labeled and
the non-leaf vertices are unlabeled) in which all non-leaf vertices have at least two
children. We refer to them as an unordered phylogenetic tree, an ordered phy-
logenetic tree, or a cyclically-ordered phylogenetic tree according to whether
the children at each vertex are unordered, linearly ordered or cyclically ordered.
Cyclically-ordered phylogenetic trees have also been called series-reduced mo-
biles [35, A032188] [1]. Let Mn,k denote the set of all cyclically-ordered phylogenetic
trees with n + 1 leaves and k internal (i.e. non-leaf) vertices.

Proof of Proposition 4.15 using recurrence relations. We will show that

To do this, we will consider two cases whether or not the leaf labeled n+1 has exactly
one sibling.
    If n + 1 has at least two siblings, we can remove it, and the resulting tree belongs
to Mn−1,k . Conversely, to insert n + 1 into a tree belonging to Mn−1,k , it suffices
to choose the vertex that would be its immediately preceding (in the cyclic order)
sibling in the resulting tree. There are n + k − 1 such choices. This contributes the
term (n + k − 1) |Mn−1,k |.
    If n + 1 has exactly one sibling, then the tree obtained by removing it is no longer
a phylogenetic tree, as the parent of n + 1 would now have only one child. Instead,
we proceed as follows:

   • If the parent of n + 1 is not the root, we contract the edge between the parent
     and grandparent of n + 1 and then remove n + 1 from the resulting tree; this
     gives us a tree in Mn−1,k−1 . Conversely, to insert n + 1 back into this tree, we
     need to select an edge, and elongate it by adding an extra internal vertex and
     then insert n + 1 as the other child of this new internal vertex. As there are
     n + k − 2 edges, we get a contribution of (n + k − 2)|Mn−1,k−1 |.

   • If the parent of n + 1 is the root, we remove the root and the vertex n + 1 to
     obtain a tree rooted at the sibling of n + 1. Conversely, we can re-obtain the
     original tree by creating a new root whose children are the old root and vertex
     n + 1. This contributes |Mn−1,k−1 |.

Putting these two contributions together gives (n + k − 1) |Mn−1,k−1 |. □

4.4    A final remark
     The diagonal entries of the second-order Stirling cycle matrix C (2) are (2n − 1)!!
[cf. (1.12)], and the reader will observe that the first four of our combinatorial interpre-
tations of the second-order Stirling cycle numbers are based on taking well-known [10]
objects of cardinality (2n − 1)!! and then “augmenting” them:
   • Perfect matchings of [2n] −→ Derangements of [n + k] containing k cycles

   • Stirling permutations of {1, 1, 2, 2, . . . , n, n} −→ Ascent-marked Stirling permu-
     tations of {1, 1, 2, 2, . . . , n, n}

   • Increasing ternary trees on [n] −→ Edge-marked increasing ternary trees on [n]

   • Increasing ordered trees on [n] ∪ {0} −→ Edge-marked increasing ordered trees
     on [n] ∪ {0}
    In fact, also the second-order Stirling subset matrix S (2) has diagonal entries
(2n − 1)!! [cf. again (1.12)], and the construction of Elvey Price and Sokal [23] was
also based on a similar “augmentation”:
   • Perfect matchings of [2n] −→ Augmented and super-augmented perfect match-
     ings of [2n]
    We wonder whether the r-th order Stirling cycle and subset polynomials for r ≥ 3
can be handled by a similar approach: namely, find a class of combinatorial objects
enumerated by the numbers (1.10) or (1.11), and then “augment” them to handle the
matrix elements with k < n. We will use this approach to construct an analogue of
Stirling permutations for r ≥ 3 in Section 5; this will give us a new interpretaton for
the r-th order Stirling cycle numbers.

      C       Report on systematic verification of conjectures
         We will now report the results of our systematic experiments, i.e., we will write
      out up to which order we have verified each of our conjectures.
         Our total positivity tests were performed by using the following methods:
          • For a finite matrix A with real entries, an efficient method to test total positivity
            is by computing the bidiagonal factorization using Neville elimination [27]. We
            used this technique to test the total positivity of lower-triangular matrices.
          • For checking negative-real-rootedness of polynomials, we used Mathematica’s
            built-in NSolve command to list out approximate values of the zeros. This
            method is very efficient.
          • For checking coefficientwise Hankel-total posivity of a sequence of polynomials,
            we unfortunately have to compute and check all minors of the Hankel matrix.
            No other efficient method is known. Compared to the other two types of tests,
            this method is very slow and we can check this only for matrices of small sizes.
          We now present the reports for Conjectures 1.3, 1.4, 1.8, 1.9, and 4.17.
