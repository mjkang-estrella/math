                                                                                        Abstract

                                                   The rth-order Stirling cycle and subset triangles and their associated quasi-Eulerian
                                                triangles were introduced by Deb and Sokal in their study of total positivity of com-
                                                binatorial triangles. They found combinatorial interpretations for the cycle case in
                                                terms of Stirling permutations, leaving the subset case open. For r ≥ 2, we resolve
                                                this problem by introducing the notion of Stirling subset permutations along with a
                                                consecutive-descent statistic. We also prove the conjectures of Deb and Sokal on the row
                                                log-concavity of the higher-order Stirling cycle and subset triangles. Our log-concavity
                                                proofs rely on Sagan’s criterion, Dey’s extension, and strengthened log-concavity in-
                                                equalities discovered with the assistance of ChatGPT 5.6.


                                          Keywords: Higher-order Stirling numbers, quasi-Eulerian numbers, Ward numbers, Stirling
                                          permutations, real-rootedness, log-concavity.

                                          AMS Classification: 05A05, 05A18, 05A20, 11B73.



                                          1    Introduction

                                          Many classical triangular arrays in enumerative combinatorics exhibit total positivity prop-
                                          erties; see, for example, the binomial [1], the Stirling [4, 8, 18, 31, 32], and the Eulerian

                                                                                           1
triangles [3, 5, 8, 15, 27, 32]. Motivated by these phenomena, Deb and Sokal [12] introduced,
for r ≥ 1, the rth-order Stirling cycle and subset triangles, obtained by lifting the columns
of the r-associated Stirling triangles. Following the terminology in [12, 17], we refer to their
entries as the rth-order Stirling cycle numbers and the rth-order Stirling subset numbers, re-
spectively. We study combinatorial interpretations of the higher-order Stirling triangles and
their associated quasi-Eulerian triangles. For the Stirling triangles, we also investigate row
log-concavity and the real-rootedness of the row-generating polynomials.

   The second part of this paper is devoted to several conjectures of Deb and Sokal [12]
on the real-rootedness of the row-generating polynomials and the row log-concavity of rth-
order Stirling cycle and subset triangles.

   Here and throughout, a polynomial with real coefficients is said to be negative-real-rooted
if it is either identically zero or all its zeros lie in (−∞, 0]. For r = 1, negative-real-
rootedness is immediate in the cycle case, while the subset case was proven by Harper [18].
For r = 2, the second-order Stirling subset polynomials are the Ward polynomials, which
have only real zeros; see Clark [10, Lemma 1]. The real-rootedness of the second-order
cycle polynomials was established by Deb and Sokal [12, Theorem 1.6 and Section 6].

   For higher orders, Deb and Sokal [12, Section 7.1] proved the non-real-rootedness of
cr, n (x) whenever r ≥ 3 and n ≥ 3, and of sr, n (x) whenever r ≥ 4 and n ≥ 3. They [12,
Conjecture 1.4(c) and Section 7.1] conjectured that s3, n (x) has non-real zeros for n ≥ 4. We
confirm this assertion in Theorem 4.2.

   We also settle the row log-concavity conjectures of Deb and Sokal and obtain a further
result for the sixth-order Stirling subset triangle. Although real-rootedness fails to hold for
higher orders in general, Deb and Sokal [12, Conjecture 1.3(c) and Conjecture 1.4(c)] con-

                                               5
jectured that the rows of both triangles remain log-concave for 3 ≤ r ≤ 5. The conjecture
for the cycle case was posed by Deb at a conference; see [6]. We prove these conjectures
for both families: every row of C (r) and every row of S (r) is log-concave for 1 ≤ r ≤ 5.
Furthermore, we show that for n ≥ 23, the nth row of S (6) is log-concave.

    The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we give an overview of the
interpretations of Deb and Sokal for the cycle case and present the corresponding interpreta-
tions for the subset case. In Section 3, we prove row log-concavity for both Stirling families
up to order five. We also show that every row with n ≥ 23 in the sixth-order subset triangle
is log-concave. In Section 4, we focus on the non-real-rootedness of the third-order Stirling
subset polynomials. We conclude with a conjecture on row log-concavity and log-convexity
in higher orders.

3     The row log-concavity conjecture

In this section, we focus on the row log-concavity of the rth-order Stirling triangles. For
r = 1, 2, the log-concavity is known; see [12]. For 3 ≤ r ≤ 5, Deb and Sokal [12, Conjec-
ture 1.3(c) and Conjecture 1.4(c)] conjectured that the row log-concavity holds for both the
rth-order Stirling cycle triangle and the rth-order Stirling subset triangle. The main objective
of this section is to prove this conjecture. We also show that row log-concavity still holds for
the sixth-order Stirling subset triangle for n ≥ 23.

    Recall that for a nonnegative sequence a = (a0 , a1 , . . .), we say a is log-concave if
a2k − ak−1 ak+1 ≥ 0 for all k ≥ 1.

Theorem 3.1. The following statements hold.


    1. For 3 ≤ r ≤ 5, every row of both the rth-order Stirling cycle triangle C (r) and the
       rth-order Stirling subset triangle S (r) is log-concave.

    2. For the sixth-order Stirling subset triangle S (6) , every row with n ≥ 23 is log-concave.


    To prove the theorem, we need the following log-concavity criteria due to Sagan [28] and
Dey [13]. For a sequence s = (sk )k≥0 , define for k ≥ 1,

                                     ∆k (s) = s2k − sk−1 sk+1 .                            (3.1)
