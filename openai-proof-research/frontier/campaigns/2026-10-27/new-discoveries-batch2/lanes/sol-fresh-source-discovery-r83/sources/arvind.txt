Abstract
    

Our purpose in this paper is twofold.
    


(a) 


We discuss the computational problem of deciding whether a given graph
is the commuting graph of a finite group; we give a quasipolynomial algorithm,
and a polynomial algorithm for the case when the group is an extraspecial
pp-group for pp an odd prime.


(b) 


We give new results on the question of whether the commuting graph of
a given group is a cograph or a chordal graph, two classes of graphs defined
by forbidden subgraphs.


    

The problems are not unrelated, since there are a number of cases where hard
computational problems on graphs are easier when restricted to special
classes of graphs; we conjecture that the recognition problem is polynomial
for cographs and chordal graphs.
  



Keywords: 
finite group , commuting graph , forbidden subgraph , cograph , chordal graph , graph isomorphism , quasipolynomial algorithm , extraspecial group

1 Introduction



Graphs associated with some algebraic structures, such as the power graph of a group or semigroup, have been actively investigated (cf. [1, 21, 23, 25]) Of course the famous Cayley graphs have a long history. Moreover, graphs from algebraic structures
have valuable applications (cf. [48]): for example, Cayley graphs as classifiers for data mining [49].



Let GG be a group and SS be a subset of GG. The commuting
graph of GG on SS is a simple graph with vertex set SS, and two
distinct elements are adjacent in this graph if they commute in
GG. In particular, if S=GS=G, then we denote this commuting graph on
GG by Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G). In 1955, Brauer and Fowler [12] first
introduced the commuting graph of a group and showed that there are
only finitely many simple groups with even order such that they have a
prescribed involution centralizer. In fact, in the seminal paper
[12], they considered the induced subgraph of the commuting
graph on the set of all non-trivial elements of the group. Since
then, the commuting graphs have received considerable attention and
have been well studied in the literature. For example, Segev et
al. [69, 70, 71] established some longstanding
conjectures on division algebras by using combinatorial
parameters of commuting graphs. Britnell and Gill [15]
classified all finite quasi-simple groups GG whose commuting graph
Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G) is perfect, where Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G) is defined on the vertex set
G∖Z⁡(G)G\setminus Z(G), where Z⁡(G)Z(G) is the centre of GG. Giudici and
Parker [40] showed that for every positive integer dd, there is
a finite 22-group GG such that the commuting graph of GG on the set
of all non-central elements has diameter greater than dd; further
results are found in [31]. Morgan and Parker [62] proved
that, for a finite group GG with trivial centre, every connected
component of the commuting graph of GG on the set of all non-trivial
elements has diameter at most 1010. Recently, Beike et
al. [9] extended some of their results; some further
applications of commuting graphs in group theory can be found in
[22].



The first part of this paper is devoted to the following algorithmic
question.



Question 1


Given a simple undirected graph Γ=(V,E)\Gamma=(V,E), with vertex set VV
and edge set EE, is it isomorphic to the commuting graph
Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G) of a group GG? More precisely, is there a bijection
φ:V→G\varphi:V\to G such that a vertex pair {u,v}\{u,v\} is an edge in
Γ\Gamma precisely when the groups elements
φ⁡(u),φ⁡(v)∈G\varphi(u),\varphi(v)\in G commute.




We give a quasi-polynomial algorithm to answer this question in
general, and a polynomial-time algorithm in the case of extraspecial
pp-groups with pp odd.



A number of important graph classes, including perfect graphs,
cographs, chordal graphs, split graphs, and threshold graphs, can be
defined either structurally or in terms of forbidden induced
subgraphs. Forbidden subgraphs of power graphs were studied in
[58]. In [22], the second author proposed the following
question.



Question 2


([22, Question 14])
For which finite groups is the commuting graph a perfect graph, or a
cograph, or a chordal graph, or a split graph, or a threshold graph?




Britnell and Gill [15] studied the finite groups whose commuting graph is perfect. The second and third authors [54] completely classified the finite groups whose commuting graph is a split graph or a threshold graph.



In Part II of the paper (Sections 10, 11,
12, and 13) , we address the question of when
the commuting graph is a cograph or a chordal graph. We prove that in
some sense, the class of such groups generalizes the class of finite
solvable groups (see Theorem 11.7 in
Subsection 11.3). Also we determine all finite simple groups
whose commuting graph is a cograph (see Problem 1 in
Subsection 11.3 and Theorem 12.16 at the end of
Section 12). The results partly answer Question 2.



These questions are relevant to the recognition problem
(Question 1). There are extra hypotheses which could make
the problem of recognition of commuting graphs easier and yield faster
algorithms. One is to restrict the groups to a known class, such as
nilpotent groups. Another is to restrict the graphs to a class in
which computation is easier. For example, the result of the second and
third authors [54] describes precisely the split or threshold
graphs which are commuting graphs; these are the complete graphs,
together with the graphs consisting of an nn-vertex complete graph
(where nn is odd), with nn pendant edges added at one of its
vertices. Clearly these are easily recognised.



We note that some works, especially on connectedness and diameter,
define the commuting graph to have vertex set G∖Z⁡(G)G\setminus Z(G) (that
is, omitting dominating vertices). This has little effect on our
results. For the recognition problem, if we are given the commuting
graph on G∖Z⁡(G)G\setminus Z(G), then the commuting graph on GG is found
by adding |Z⁡(G)||Z(G)| dominating vertices to the graph, and this number
is a divisor of |G∖Z⁡(G)||G\setminus Z(G)| so there are not many
possibilities. In the second part of the paper too, as mentioned
before Proposition 10.2, we can define the commuting graph
without including the centre Z⁡(G)Z(G) and the results essentially remain
the same.



We conclude the introduction with a suggestion for further research,
which would link the two parts of the paper together more closely.



Question 3


Suppose that Γ\Gamma is a perfect graph, a cograph or a chordal graph. Is
there a polynomial algorithm to decide whether Γ\Gamma is the commuting graph
of a group?

Part I Recognising the commuting graph




3 The Recognition Problem



The main focus of this part is the recognition problem of
commuting graphs. It is basically an algorithmic question. Given an
undirected graph X=(V,E)X=(V,E) as input, the aim is to design an algorithm
that efficiently checks if there is a group GG with |V||V| elements
such that the input graph XX is isomorphic to Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G), and if so
to efficiently determine such an isomorphism.



We summarize our results.




(a) 


We obtain a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm for the case
of extraspecial pp-groups for odd primes pp. Extraspecial
pp-groups are a special case of pp-groups of nilpotence class 22.
A formal definition is in Section 7.


(b) 


For the general case, we obtain a quasipolynomial-time algorithm
for the recognition problem of commuting graphs. The algorithm is
essentially based on a simple 2O⁡(log3⁡n)2^{O(\log^{3}n)} time algorithm that
enumerates the multiplication tables of all distinct groups of order
nn (where by distinct we mean mutually non-isomorphic groups of
order nn). This enumeration algorithm combined with Babai’s
quasipolynomial time algorithm for graph isomorphism [4]
yields a simple 2O⁡(log3⁡n)2^{O(\log^{3}n)} time algorithm to check if a given
nn-vertex graph XX is the commuting graph of a group of order nn:
for each nn-element group GG that is output by the enumeration
algorithm we check if Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G) is isomorphic to XX by using
Babai’s isomorphism test [4].


(c) 


A natural question in connection with our algorithm for recognizing
the commuting graphs of extraspecial groups is whether groups with the
same commuting graphs are isoclinic. This holds for extraspecial
groups (this is exploited by the algorithm), and it is natural to
conjecture that this property holds for all groups of nilpotence class
2 (of which extraspecial groups are a subclass). We present
counter-examples for class-3 nilpotent groups and conjecture that the
property holds for class-2 nilpotent groups.


(d) 


Additionally, we also show an efficient reduction of the
commuting graph recognition problem to the recognition problem of
commuting graphs of indecomposable groups. We also note recognizing
the commuting graph of generalized dihedral groups along with an extension
to Frobenius groups.





Some related work.  There is a result by Giudici and
Kuzma [39] that shows the following: every nn-vertex graph XX
with at least two vertices of degree n−1n-1 is realizable as the
commuting graph of a semigroup. It is easy to see that their
construction actually gives a polynomial-time algorithm for finding a
semigroup with nn elements and a bijection from it to the vertex set
of XX such that the edges of XX realize the commuting relation of
the semigroup. It is a nearly complete answer to the question in the
semigroups setting.



Solomon and Woldar [74] have shown that the commuting graph
Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G) of a finite simple group GG uniquely determines
the group. More precisely, they have shown that for any finite group
HH and any finite simple group GG, their commuting graphs
Γ⁡(H)\Gamma(H) and Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G) are isomorphic if and only if the groups
GG and HH are isomorphic. An interesting question is whether or not
the proof of this result [74] yields a polynomial-time
algorithm for checking if a given graph XX is the commuting graph of
a simple group.





4 Basic properties



We being with some preliminary observations that are well-known in the
literature (see, e.g., the survey [22]).



Let GG be a finite group. We first describe the cliques of

Proof 3

5 Commuting graphs of product groups



A natural approach to the problem of recognizing commuting graphs is
to examine the connection between product decompositions of the
commuting graph Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G) and the direct product decomposition of
the group GG.



Let GG and HH be finite groups. Consider the commuting graph
Γ⁡(G×H)\Gamma(G\times H) of their direct product. We recall the
definition of strong products of graphs.



Definition 1


[45, Chapter 4, Page 35]
Let X=(V,E)X=(V,E) and X′=(V′,E′)X^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime}) be simple undirected graphs. Their
strong product, denoted X⊠X′X\boxtimes X^{\prime}, is a simple undirected
graph with the cartesian product V×V′V\times V^{\prime} as its vertex set and
edges defined as follows: distinct pairs (u,u′)(u,u^{\prime}) and (v,v′)(v,v^{\prime}) are
adjacent if and only if one of the following holds:


(a) 


u=vu=v and (u′,v′)∈E′(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in E^{\prime},


(b) 


u′=v′u^{\prime}=v^{\prime} and (u,v)∈E(u,v)\in E,


(c) 


(u,v)∈E(u,v)\in E and (u′,v′)∈E′(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in E^{\prime}.






The following proposition is immediate from the definition.



Proposition 5.1


For finite groups GG and HH




Γ⁡(G×H)=Γ⁡(G)⊠Γ⁡(H).\Gamma(G\times H)=\Gamma(G)\boxtimes\Gamma(H).






The strong product is both commutative and associative
[45, Chapter 4], in the sense that X⊠Y≅Y⊠XX\boxtimes Y\cong Y\boxtimes X and (X⊠Y)⊠Z≅X⊠(Y⊠Z)(X\boxtimes Y)\boxtimes Z\cong X\boxtimes(Y\boxtimes Z).



By a non-trivial graph we mean a graph with more than one
vertex. Given a graph XX, a factorization X≅Y⊠ZX\cong Y\boxtimes Z is non-trivial if both YY and ZZ are non-trivial. A graph
XX is said to be a prime graph if it does not have a
non-trivial factorization. (We warn the reader that this term is also used
for graphs defined by arithmetic parameters of a finite group; see for
example [57].)



It turns out that any connected graph XX has a unique factorization
(up to isomorphism) into a strong product of prime graphs [45, Theorem
7.14]. Moreover, this prime factorization can be
computed in polynomial time [36]. As a consequence, we
obtain the following polynomial-time reduction.



Recall a group GG is said to be indecomposable if it
cannot be expressed as the direct product of two non-trivial groups.



Theorem 5.2


The problem of recognizing the commuting graphs of groups is
polynomial-time reducible to the problem of recognizing the
commuting graphs of indecomposable groups.




Proof 5


Suppose we have an algorithm 𝒜\mathcal{A} for recognizing the
commuting graphs of indecomposable groups. Using 𝒜\mathcal{A} as
subroutine, we present a polynomial-time algorithm for recognizing
the commuting graphs of all finite groups.

6 Commuting graphs of semidirect products



In this section we explore whether we can recognize the commuting
graphs of semidirect products G=H⋊KG=H\rtimes K if HH and KK are both
from group classes whose commuting graphs are easily recognizable.



For example, consider the commuting graph of the dihedral group
D2​nD_{2n}. Let D2​n=⟨a,b⟩D_{2n}=\langle a,b\rangle where an=b2=1a^{n}=b^{2}=1 and

Seeking a generalization of this example we consider Frobenius groups.




6.1 Commuting graphs of Frobenius groups



In this subsection we demonstrate that we can recognise from its

were defined earlier. Let GG be a Frobenius group with kernel NN

Thompson [79] proved that the Frobenius kernel is

commuting graph Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G). As the identity element commutes with
every other element in GG, it is a dominating vertex in
Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G). We recall [3, 35.24 and 35.25] that
N​⊴​GN\trianglelefteq G, CG​(h)≤HC_{G}(h)\leq H for each h∈Hh\in H, and
CG​(x)≤NC_{G}(x)\leq N for each x∈N∖{1}x\in N\setminus\{1\}. Therefore,
non-identity vertices in NN do not commute with non-identity
vertices in a complement. Thus the identity element is the unique
dominating vertex in Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G). Moreover, non-identity vertices
in different complements do not commute with each other. Thus the
components of Γ⁡(G)∖{1}\Gamma(G)\setminus\{1\} are as stated, and
Lemma 6.1 shows that they have dominating vertices.



Conversely, suppose that the commuting graph Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G) has
properties (a) and (b). If CC is a component with a dominating vertex
cc, then the centralizer CG​(c)C_{G}(c) is equal to C∪{1}C\cup\{1\}, which is
thus a subgroup of GG. Let NN be the subgroup containing the
component of size n−1n-1, and let H1,…,HnH_{1},\ldots,H_{n} be the subgroups
containing the other components. Note that |N|=n|N|=n and |Hi|=k,1≤i≤n|H_{i}|=k,1\leq i\leq n.



Since Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G) is invariant under automorphisms of GG, we have an
action of GG on the set Ω\Omega of components of
Γ⁡(G)∖{1}\Gamma(G)\setminus\{1\} of size k−1k-1 by conjugation. We show that
this action satisfies the conditions for a Frobenius group given
above.



Choose a prime pp dividing kk. Since nn and kk are relatively
prime, pp does not divide nn. So the subgroup NN cannot contain a
Sylow pp-subgroup; but each of H1,…,HnH_{1},\ldots,H_{n} contains such a
subgroup. By the conjugacy part of Sylow’s theorem, GG acts
transitively on Ω\Omega.



For 1≤i≤n1\leq i\leq n, each element of Hi∖{1}H_{i}\setminus\{1\} acting by
conjugation, fixes itself and so fixes the component induced by
Hi∖{1}H_{i}\setminus\{1\}. That is, a non-identity element of
G∖NG\setminus N, acting by conjugation, fixes itself, and so fixes the
component containing it. We must show that it fixes no other
component. To that end, we count the fixed points of elements of GG
acting on Ω\Omega. The identity fixes nn; each non-identity element
of NN fixes some non-negative number; and each of the remaining
elements G∖NG\setminus N fix a positive number of elements. So the sum
of the fixed point numbers is at least n+n⁡(k−1)=n​kn+n(k-1)=nk. But, by the
Orbit-Counting Lemma, this sum is equal to |G|=n​k|G|=nk, since GG is
transitive. Consequently, non-identity elements of NN do not fix any
point of Ω\Omega, and the remaining non-identity elements fix exactly
one point each. So GG is a Frobenius group.∎




Suppose XX is a graph given as input such that X=Γ⁡(G)X=\Gamma(G) for some
group GG. Then Theorem 6.2 gives us a polynomial-time test
for checking if the group GG is a Frobenius group.

constructions of finite simple groups described in Wilson’s text
[83].




(a) 


Our main idea is the notion of description desc(G)\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G) for
any finite group GG that we develop in this section. It is
essentially an encoding of the group GG as a binary string
of length at most c⋅log3⁡|G|c\cdot\log^{3}|G|, for some fixed constant
c>0c>0, from which GG can be recovered efficiently. More precisely,
we will describe an algorithm 𝒟\mathcal{D} that takes as input a
binary string ww and, if w=desc(G)w=\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G) for some finite group GG the
algorithm 𝒟\mathcal{D} will computes the multiplication table of
GG as output in time polynomial in |G||G|. Otherwise, the algorithm
will reject ww as an invalid description.


(b) 


We note that this algorithm 𝒟\mathcal{D} can be used to
generate the multiplication tables of all groups GG of order nn as
follows. We enumerate all possible binary strings ww of length at
most c⋅log3⁡nc\cdot\log^{3}n. For each such ww we run the algorithm
𝒟\mathcal{D} on it. If w=desc(G)w=\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G) for some GG then 𝒟\mathcal{D}
will output its multiplication table. We compute Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G) from
GG and check if XX is isomorphic to Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G) in time
2O⁡(log3⁡n)2^{O(\log^{3}n)} using Babai’s graph isomorphism algorithm
[4].





Thus, the main task of this section is to define desc(G)\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G) and
describe the algorithm 𝒟\mathcal{D}. We will first define desc(G)\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G)
for finite simple groups, along with how the algorithm 𝒟\mathcal{D}
will compute the multiplication table for GG given desc(G)\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G) as
input. We will then extend the notion of desc(G)\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G) to all groups by
using a composition series for GG combined with desc(N)\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(N) for each
composition factor NN of GG.



Remark 5


We emphasize here that our notion of “short” description is
entirely different from the well-studied notion of
generator-relator presentations of groups (see e.g. [5]). Indeed, desc(G)\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G) and its construction will be
significantly simpler. It suffices for the purpose of recovering the
multiplication table of the group GG in time polynomial in |G||G|.

(a)

(b)

(c)

12] for details). Now, the elements of 𝔽q\mathbb{F}_{q} can be

Proof 23

9 Isoclinism of groups and commuting graphs



Motivated by the polynomial-time algorithm for recognizing commuting
graphs of extraspecial groups, in this section we briefly discuss
isoclinism of groups and its connection to commuting graphs.



The lexicographic product of graphs Γ1\Gamma_{1} and Γ2\Gamma_{2} is the
graph on the vertex set V⁡(Γ1)×V⁡(Γ2)V(\Gamma_{1})\times V(\Gamma_{2}), in which (v1,w1)(v_{1},w_{1})
and (v2,w2)(v_{2},w_{2}) are joined if and only if either v1v_{1} and v2v_{2} are joined in
Γ1\Gamma_{1}, or v1=v2v_{1}=v_{2} and w1w_{1} and w2w_{2} are joined in Γ2\Gamma_{2}. If
Γ2\Gamma_{2} is a complete graph, then the lexicographic product is obtained
from Γ1\Gamma_{1} by blowing up each vertex to a complete graph on
|V​Γ2||V\Gamma_{2}| vertices.



Commutation in a group GG is a map from G×GG\times G to the derived group G′G^{\prime};
multiplying the arguments by elements of Z⁡(G)Z(G) does not change the function
value, so commutation induces a map from G/Z⁡(G)×G/Z⁡(G)G/Z(G)\times G/Z(G) to G′G^{\prime}.
We say that groups GG and HH are isoclinic if there are isomorphisms
θ:G/Z⁡(G)→H/Z⁡(H)\theta:G/Z(G)\to H/Z(H) and ϕ:G′→H′\phi:G^{\prime}\to H^{\prime} which intertwine the commutation
map; that is, for all g1,g2∈Gg_{1},g_{2}\in G,




[θ⁡(g1​Z​(G)),θ⁡(g2​Z​(G))]=ϕ⁡([g1​Z​(G),g2​Z​(G)]).[\theta(g_{1}Z(G)),\theta(g_{2}Z(G))]=\phi([g_{1}Z(G),g_{2}Z(G)]).





Now the commuting graph of a group GG is the lexicographic product of a
graph on G/Z⁡(G)G/Z(G) with g1​Z​(G)g_{1}Z(G) and g2​Z​(G)g_{2}Z(G) joined if [g1,g2]=1[g_{1},g_{2}]=1
(this graph can also be described as the induced subgraph of the commuting
graph of GG on a transversal for Z⁡(G)Z(G) in GG)
with a complete graph on Z⁡(G)Z(G); the graph on G/Z⁡(G)G/Z(G) is determined by the
isoclinism type. Hence, as observed by Pezzott and Nakaoka [64], we have:



Theorem 9.1


Isoclinic groups of the same order have isomorphic commuting graphs.




Remark 7


It follows from Lemma 7.2 that the extraspecial groups
G1G_{1} and G2G_{2}, defined in Section 7, are
isoclinic. Indeed, the recognition algorithm of
Section 7 implicitly uses this property.




Is there a converse to Theorem 9.1? That is, if two
groups have isomorphic commuting graphs, must they be isoclinic? This
holds for various classes of groups, such as abelian groups,
non-abelian simple groups, and extraspecial groups (as we have seen).



However, it is not true in general; we recycle an example taken from
[24] to show this. Let GG be the group of order 6464 which is
SmallGroup(64,182) in the SmallGroups library [10] in
GAP [38]. A Schur cover of GG is a group HH of maximal
order subject to having a subgroup Z≤H′∩Z⁡(H)Z\leq H^{\prime}\cap Z(H) such that
H/Z≅GH/Z\cong G. The Schur multiplier of GG has order 22, so any Schur
cover of GG has order 128128. Moreover, the Bogomolov multiplier (see
[24] for context and other references) of GG is equal to the
Schur multiplier, which implies that any Schur cover HH is
commutation-preserving: that is, two elements a,b∈Ha,b\in H
commute if and only if their projections Z​a,Z​b∈GZa,Zb\in G commute. This
implies that the commuting graph of a Schur cover is obtained from the
commuting graph of GG by replacing each vertex with a clique of size
22, with all edges between cliques corresponding to adjacent
vertices. This procedure also describes the commuting graph of
G×C2G\times C_{2}. On the other hand, it is easy to verify computationally
that the derived groups of HH and G×C2G\times C_{2} are not isomorphic,
so these groups cannot be isoclinic.



We note that the group GG has nilpotency class 33, as do all of its
Schur covers (these are SmallGroup(128,ii) for i∈{789,790,791,815,816,817}i\in\{789,790,791,815,816,817\} in the GAP library). This suggested that it
might be true that groups with nilpotency class 22 and isomorphic commuting
graphs are isoclinic.
However, Eamonn O’Brien (personal communication) has shown that even this is
false. The groups SmallGroup(64,18) and SmallGroup(64,215)
have nilpotency class 22 and isomorphic commuting graphs, but are not
isoclinic (their derived quotients have exponent 44 and 22 respectively).
He also has counterexamples with orders 363^{6} and 565^{6}.

10 Preliminaries



A number of important graph classes can be defined either structurally or in terms of forbidden induced subgraphs. Here are structural descriptions.


(a) 


Γ\Gamma is called a cograph if it can be built from 11-vertex graphs
by the operations of disjoint union and complementation.


(b) 


Γ\Gamma is called a chordal graph provided that Γ\Gamma contains no induced cycles of length greater than 33; in other words,
any cycle of length at least 44 contains a chord.





The characterization by forbidden subgraphs is a composite of results from
[37, 28, 75, 27].



Proposition 10.1



(a) 


A graph is a cograph if and only if it forbids P4P_{4}.


(b) 


A graph is chordal if and only if it forbids {Cn:n≥4}\{C_{n}:n\geq 4\}.






Since a cycle of length 55 or more contains a 44-vertex path, we have the following observation.



Observation 3


If a graph is {P4,C4}\{P_{4},C_{4}\}-free, then it is chordal.




We conclude this section with a general remark.
In the commuting graph Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G) of a group GG, elements of the centre
Z⁡(G)Z(G) are dominating vertices; this is the reason why, to investigate questions
about connectedness, they are excluded. However, in our case,
it doesn’t matter:



Proposition 10.2



(a) 


The class of groups whose commuting graph is ℱ\mathcal{F}-free is
subgroup-closed.


(b) 


If no graph in ℱ\mathcal{F} has a dominating vertex, then the class of
groups with ℱ\mathcal{F}-free commuting graph is independent of whether
vertices in Z⁡(G)Z(G) are included or excluded.






Proof 24


(a) This is immediate from the fact that, if HH is a subgroup of GG, then
Γ⁡(H)\Gamma(H) is an induced subgraph of Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G). (We remark that the same
result holds for various other graphs such as the power graph, enhanced power
graph, nilpotency and solvability graphs. But it does not hold for yet others
such as the generating graph or the deep commuting graph.)



(b) If XX is an induced subgraph of Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G), then vertices of
X∩Z⁡(G)X\cap Z(G) would be dominating vertices in XX. ∎




In the cases we are interested in (chordal graphs and
cographs), the excluded subgraphs are paths and cycles with more than three
vertices. None of these have dominating vertices, so we can take the
vertex set of the commuting graph to be all of GG, but to decide if it has
any of these properties we can delete vertices in Z⁡(G)Z(G).



Also to prove main results of this paper, we need the following
assertion which generalizes a result obtained by

Proposition 10.3

(b)

Then CAut(G)​(Inn(G))=1C_{\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(G)}(\mathop{\mathrm{Inn}}(G))=1.

12 Simple groups whose commuting graph is a cograph or a chordal graph



In this section, we investigate when the commuting graph of a simple sporadic or alternating group is a cograph or a chordal graph, and then obtain a complete classification of finite simple groups whose commuting graph is a cograph.




12.1 CA-groups and AC-groups



A group GG is a CA-group if the centralizer of every non-identity
element of GG is abelian.
Another name is CT-groups, since they are the groups for which
the commutation relation on non-identity elements is transitive. (If
x∼y∼zx\sim y\sim z in the CA-group GG, then x,z∈CG​(y)x,z\in C_{G}(y), so xx and
zz commute. The converse is similar.)



If GG is a CA-group, then the induced subgraph Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G) on non-identity
elements is a disjoint union of complete graphs. As a result, we see:



Proposition 12.1


Let GG be a CA-group. Then Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G) is a cograph, chordal, and perfect.




CA-groups played an important historical role in developing techniques for
attacking problems on finite groups; they are now well-understood.
From results of Weisner [81], Brauer, Suzuki and Wall
[13] and Suzuki [76], we obtain:



Proposition 12.2


A finite CA-group GG satisfies one of the following:


(a) 


GG is abelian;


(b) 


GG is a Frobenius group with abelian Frobenius kernel and cyclic
Frobenius complement; or


(c) 


G≅PSL2(q)G\cong\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits_{2}(q) for qq a power of 22.






More generally, GG is said to be an AC-group if CG​(x)C_{G}(x) is abelian
for every element x∈G∖Z⁡(G)x\in G\setminus Z(G). This is a weaker condition, though
clearly a group with trivial centre is an AC-group if and only if it is a
CA-group.



AC-groups were investigated by Schmidt [67] and Rocke [66].
(Schmidt investigated groups in which two maximal centralizers intersect just
in Z⁡(G)Z(G); it is not hard to see that, for finite groups, this is equivalent to
the AC property.) Abdollahi et al. [2] investigated the
connection of this property with the non-commuting graph, the
complement of the commuting graph. The classification of the AC-groups is not
as complete as that of CA-groups, but the cited authors have proved a
number of results about these groups.



From our point of view, AC-groups are as good as CA-groups:



Proposition 12.3


Let GG be an AC-group. Then Γ⁡(G)\Gamma(G) is a cograph and a chordal graph.




Proof 30


By Proposition 10.2, it suffices to consider the induced subgraph on
the set G∖Z⁡(G)G\setminus Z(G); and this graph is a disjoint union of complete
graphs. ∎

paper [77]; it can also be found in [83, Section 4.2].



It suffices to show that the graph obtained by removing the identity is a
cograph. Now B22​(q){}^{2}B_{2}(q) has maximal cyclic subgroups of orders q−1q-1 and
q±2​q+1q\pm\sqrt{2q}+1, and every element of odd order lies in one of these; so
the non-identity elements of odd order carry a disjoint union of complete
graphs, with no edges to the remaining elements.
The remaining elements have order 22 or 44, and since B22​(q){}^{2}B_{2}(q) is a
CIT-group (one in which the centralizer of any involution is a
22-group), the remaining vertices lie in a disjoint union of copies of the
commuting graph on P∖{e}P\setminus\{e\}, where PP is a Sylow 22-subgroup. Now
|P|=q2|P|=q^{2} and |Z⁡(P)|=q|Z(P)|=q; elements of Z⁡(P)Z(P) are joined to every element of PP
so we may remove them. The remaining elements of PP have order 44. If gg
is one of these, then |CP​(g)|=2​q|C_{P}(g)|=2q and CP​(g)C_{P}(g) consists of two cosets of
Z⁡(P)Z(P), so it is abelian; thus the elements of order 44 fall into q−1q-1
sets of size qq, each set a complete graph with no edges between different
sets. Thus the graph is indeed a cograph.
∎




Next, the small Ree group R1​(q)=G22​(q)R_{1}(q)={}^{2}G_{2}(q), with qq an odd power of 33, is not
simple for q=3q=3; for larger qq, it contains PSL2(q)\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits_{2}(q) (the centralizer
of an involution in G22​(q){}^{2}G_{2}(q) is ℤ2×PSL2(q)\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits_{2}(q) [52]).



The Tits group F42​(2)′{}^{2}F_{4}(2)^{\prime} contains a subgroup isomorphic to

To summarize our results, combined with the earlier results on alternating

Theorem 12.16


Let GG be a non-abelian finite simple group. Then the commuting graph of GG
is a cograph if and only if GG is isomorphic to PSL2(q)\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits_{2}(q), with qq a power
of 22 and q>2q>2, or to Sz(q)=B22​(q)\mathop{\mathrm{Sz}}\nolimits(q)={}^{2}B_{2}(q), with qq an odd power of 22 and q>2q>2.
