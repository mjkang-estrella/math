Abstract
    

Brualdi and Hoffman proposed a well-known problem of determining the graph with maximum adjacency spectral radius among all graphs with given size mm. Early work by Friedland and Stanley addressed some specific cases.
This problem was later completely solved by Rowlinson and recently revisited by Cheng and Weng.
Pioneering work on the distance matrix was carried out by Graham and Pollak, as well as by Graham and Lovász.
The distance spectral radius ρ⁡(G)\rho(G) of a connected graph GG is the largest eigenvalue of its distance matrix.
    

In this paper, we completely solve the problem of characterizing the connected graph with minimum distance spectral radius among all graphs with size mm.
Let 𝒢⁡(m)\mathcal{G}(m) be the class of connected graphs with mm edges.
For every m⩾3m\geqslant 3, let nn be the unique integer satisfying (n−12)<m⩽(n2)\binom{n-1}{2}<m\leqslant\binom{n}{2}, and we write m=(n−12)+sm=\binom{n-1}{2}+s with 1⩽s⩽n−11\leqslant s\leqslant n-1. Recently, Lin and Zhou [Adv. in Appl. Math. 173 (2026)] investigated the graph in 𝒢⁡(m)\mathcal{G}(m) that minimizes ρ⁡(G)\rho(G) in the range s⩾max⁡{n−62,1}s\geqslant\max\{\frac{n-6}{2},1\}. However, the problem is much more difficult in the remaining range 1⩽s⩽n−721\leqslant s\leqslant\frac{n-7}{2}, and they conjectured that the unique minimizer is Pn,s+1¯\overline{P_{n,s+1}}, the complement of a balanced disjoint union of paths. Using novel matrix analysis, we solve this conjecture in the affirmative. Moreover, we provide a new unified proof for the entire range 1⩽s⩽n−11\leqslant s\leqslant n-1.
    

The key ingredients in our proof argument include an innovative comparison principle for the distance spectral radius, an increment analysis of Φ\Phi-functions on paths and cycles, an argument for balancing path lengths, and a walk enumeration technique via the Neumann series.
  



Key words: Distance spectral radius, Lin–Zhou’s conjecture.



2020 AMS Subject Classifications: 05C50, 05C35.




1 Introduction



Let GG be a simple connected graph with vertex set V⁡(G)V(G) and edge set E⁡(G)E(G).
We denote by nn and e⁡(G)e(G) the order and size of GG, respectively.
The complement of GG is denoted as G¯\overline{G}. For any vertex u∈V⁡(G)u\in V(G), let
dG​(u)d_{G}(u) and NG​(u)N_{G}(u) (or d⁡(u)d(u) and N⁡(u)N(u) for short) be the degree and the set of neighbors of uu, respectively. Clearly, dG​(u)=|NG​(u)|d_{G}(u)=|N_{G}(u)|.
We write 𝟏\mathbf{1} for the all-ones column vector and J=𝟏𝟏𝖳J=\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}} for the all-ones matrix. Let II be the identity matrix.
We denote by CℓC_{\ell} the cycle on ℓ\ell vertices, and PkP_{k} the path on kk vertices.
The adjacency matrix of an nn-vertex graph GG is defined as A⁡(G)=[ai​j]i,j=1nA(G)=[a_{ij}]_{i,j=1}^{n}, where ai​j=1a_{ij}=1 if i​j∈E⁡(G)ij\in E(G), and ai​j=0a_{ij=0} otherwise. The spectral radius λ⁡(G)\lambda(G) of a graph GG is defined as the largest eigenvalue of its adjacency matrix A⁡(G)A(G).




1.1 The Brualdi–Hoffman type extremal problem



The study of bounding the spectral radius of a graph in terms of the number of edges has a long and rich history. It is well-known that λ⁡(G)<2​m\lambda(G)<\sqrt{2m}. Tracking back to 1985,
Brualdi and Hoffman [4] proved that if GG has m⩽(k2)m\leqslant{k\choose 2} edges for some integer k⩾2k\geqslant 2, then λ⁡(G)⩽k−1\lambda(G)\leqslant k-1. This bound was further extended by Friedland [7], and later by Stanley [22] who showed that λ​(G)⩽12​(8​m+1−1)\lambda(G)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{8m+1}-1), with equality if and only if m=(k2)m={k\choose 2} and GG is a complete graph KkK_{k}. Here, we ignore the possible isolated vertices if there are no confusions.
We write m=(s2)+tm={s\choose 2}+t for some integers s,ts,t with 0⩽t⩽s−10\leqslant t\leqslant s-1.
Let K1∨tKsK_{1}\vee_{t}K_{s} be the graph obtained from KsK_{s} by adding a new vertex joining
tt vertices of KsK_{s}. In 1988, Rowlinson [19] proved that if GG has mm edges, then
λ(G)⩽λ(K1∨tKs)\lambda(G)\leqslant\lambda(K_{1}\vee_{t}K_{s}),
with equality if and only if G=K1∨tKsG=K_{1}\vee_{t}K_{s}.
This confirms a conjecture proposed by Brualdi and Hoffman [4]. Roughly speaking,
the graph with the largest adjacency spectral radius packs almost all edges into the clique, and then attaches the remaining edges to that clique.
We refer to [5] for a matrix realization of these spectral bounds.



Motivated by the Turán theorem, Nikiforov [18]
significantly improved the above bounds by proving that if GG is a Kr+1K_{r+1}-free graph, then λ2​(G)⩽(1−1r)​2​m\lambda^{2}(G)\leqslant(1-\frac{1}{r})2m. In particular, this recovers a result of Nosal, saying that every mm-edge triangle-free GG satisfies λ⁡(G)⩽m\lambda(G)\leqslant\sqrt{m}.
For related results, we refer the readers to [12, 11, 13].
Recently, Li, Liu and Zhang [14] proved that for any graph FF with chromatic number r+1⩾3r+1\geqslant 3, if GG is an FF-free graph with mm edges, then λ2​(G)⩽(1−1r+o⁡(1))​2​m\lambda^{2}(G)\leqslant\big(1-\frac{1}{r}+o(1)\big)2m. Extending Nikiforov’s theorem, Li, Liu and Zhang [15] proved that the o⁡(1)o(1) error term can be removed for color-critical graphs FF; and λ⁡(G)⩽m+O⁡(1)\lambda(G)\leqslant\sqrt{m}+O(1) for almost-bipartite graphs FF.





1.2 The distance spectral extremal problem



The distance between two vertices u,vu,v, denoted by dG​(u,v)d_{G}(u,v), is the length of a shortest path connecting them. The distance matrix of GG is defined as D⁡(G)=(dG​(u,v))u,v∈V⁡(G)D(G)=(d_{G}(u,v))_{u,v\in V(G)}.
The distance spectral radius ρ⁡(G)\rho(G) is defined as the largest eigenvalue of D⁡(G)D(G).
Note that D⁡(G)D(G) is nonnegative and irreducible. By the Perron–Frobenius theorem, ρ⁡(G)\rho(G) is a positive simple eigenvalue, and there exists a unique positive unit eigenvector corresponding to ρ⁡(G)\rho(G).
The study of eigenvalues of the distance matrix of a connected graph dates back to the classical work [8, 9, 10], where they provided an inequality related the number of negative distance eigenvalues
to the addressing problem in data communication system.
The properties of distance matrix turn out to be significant in the study of the isometric embedding problems from a connected graph to the squashed hypercube; see [23, 21, 1].
We refer to the comprehensive surveys [2, 16] for recent development.



For connected graphs of fixed order nn, the extremal problems are classical: Ruzieh and Powers [20] and Stevanović and Ilić [24] showed that the complete graph KnK_{n} uniquely minimizes ρ\rho, while the path PnP_{n} uniquely maximizes ρ\rho.
In contrast, the adjacency spectral radius exhibits the opposite behavior: the complete graph KnK_{n} is the unique maximizer, and the path PnP_{n} is the unique minimizer.
Here, we provide an explanation for this reversed phenomenon.
The adjacency spectral radius λ⁡(G)\lambda(G) measures how well a graph expands or how strongly it is connected: a large value indicates a dense graph with many edges and small diameter (e.g., a clique). In contrast, the distance spectral radius ρ⁡(G)\rho(G) measures the overall separation among vertices: a large value means many pairs of vertices are far apart, so the graph is sparse (e.g., a path or a star). Hence, dense graphs tend to have large λ⁡(G)\lambda(G) but small ρ⁡(G)\rho(G), while sparse graphs tend to have small λ⁡(G)\lambda(G) but large ρ⁡(G)\rho(G).



A much harder problem is to find the extremal graphs among all connected graphs with a prescribed number of edges mm, which was initiated by Lin and Zhou [17]. Let 𝒢⁡(m)\mathcal{G}(m) denote the class of such graphs. Throughout this paper, for a given m⩾3m\geqslant 3, we denote




n:=⌈1+8​m+12⌉,s:=m−(n−12)∈{1,2,…,n−1}.n:=\Bigl\lceil\tfrac{1+\sqrt{8m+1}}{2}\Bigr\rceil,\qquad s:=m-\binom{n-1}{2}\in\{1,2,\ldots,n-1\}.

(1)


For positive integers N⩾c⩾1N\geqslant c\geqslant 1, we define




PN,c:=(c+c⁡⌊N/c⌋−N)​P⌊N/c⌋∪(N−c⁡⌊N/c⌋)​P⌈N/c⌉,P_{N,c}\,:=\;\bigl(c+c\lfloor N/c\rfloor-N\bigr)\,P_{\lfloor N/c\rfloor}\;\cup\;\bigl(N-c\lfloor N/c\rfloor\bigr)\,P_{\lceil N/c\rceil},



the balanced disjoint union of cc paths on NN vertices (its parts differ in order by at most one).
By the definition, it is easy to verify that Pn,s+1¯\overline{P_{n,s+1}} contains
(n2)−(n−s−1)=(n−12)+s=m{n\choose 2}-(n-s-1)={n-1\choose 2}+s=m edges. Intuitively, we see that Pn,s+1¯\overline{P_{n,s+1}} is close to almost-complete graph, which is obtained from the largest possible KnK_{n} by deleting the edges of those s+1s+1 balanced vertex-disjoint paths.



Recently, Lin and Zhou [17, Theorem 1.3] proved that if max⁡{n−62,1}⩽s⩽n−1\max\{\frac{n-6}{2},1\}\leqslant s\leqslant n-1, then Pn,s+1¯\overline{P_{n,s+1}} is the unique graph that minimizes the distance spectral radius over all graphs in 𝒢⁡(m)\mathcal{G}(m). In the concluding remarks of [17], they conjectured that the same formula holds for all small ss.



Conjecture 1.1 (Lin–Zhou [17]).


Let GG be a graph with mm edges, and let nn and ss be defined as in (1). If
1⩽s⩽n−721\leqslant s\leqslant\frac{n-7}{2}, then
ρ⁡(G)⩾ρ⁡(Pn,s+1¯),\rho(G)\geqslant\rho\bigl(\overline{P_{n,s+1}}\bigr),
with equality if and only if G≅Pn,s+1¯G\cong\overline{P_{n,s+1}}.




In this paper, we resolve Conjecture 1.1 by showing the following result.



Theorem 1.2.


Let G∈𝒢⁡(m)G\in\mathcal{G}(m) minimize ρ\rho of graphs in 𝒢⁡(m)\mathcal{G}(m), and let n=⌈12​(1+8​m+1)⌉n=\lceil\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{8m+1})\rceil and s=m−(n−12)s=m-\binom{n-1}{2}.
If 1⩽s<n−221\leqslant s<\frac{n-2}{2}, then G≅Pn,s+1¯G\cong\overline{P_{n,s+1}}. In particular, Conjecture 1.1 holds.




Remark. In the case s=0s=0, the above result is invalid. In this setting,
we have m=(n−12)m={n-1\choose 2}, the minimizer is uniquely the complete graph Kn−1K_{n-1}, instead of the expected graph Pn,1¯=Pn¯\overline{P_{n,1}}=\overline{P_{n}}.



The range max⁡{n−62,1}⩽s⩽n−1\max\{\frac{n-6}{2},1\}\leqslant s\leqslant n-1 was already investigated by Lin and Zhou [17], which together with the range in Theorem 1.2 yields the following complete solution of the distance spectral extremal problem for graphs with given size mm.



Theorem 1.3.


For every integer m⩾3m\geqslant 3, let n=⌈12​(1+8​m+1)⌉n=\lceil\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{8m+1})\rceil and s=m−(n−12)s=m-\binom{n-1}{2}.
Then for all 1⩽s⩽n−11\leqslant s\leqslant n-1, the graph Pn,s+1¯\overline{P_{n,s+1}} uniquely minimizes ρ\rho over all graphs in 𝒢⁡(m)\mathcal{G}(m).




As an application of our framework, we shall provide a new self-contained proof for s⩾n−22s\geqslant\frac{n-2}{2} in Section 3, thereby giving a unified treatment of Theorem 1.3 for the entire range 1⩽s⩽n−11\leqslant s\leqslant n-1.



Before the proof, we first illustrate the difficulty for the small ss.
To appreciate why the range of small ss was much difficult and left open, we now recall how the proof argument of [17, Theorem 1.3] worked for large ss.
A structural lemma (we state it as in Theorem 2.1 below) shows that any minimizer GG has complement
G¯=Cℓ1∪⋯∪Cℓt∪Pk1∪⋯∪Pks+1\overline{G}=C_{\ell_{1}}\cup\cdots\cup C_{\ell_{t}}\cup P_{k_{1}}\cup\cdots\cup P_{k_{s+1}}, where ℓi⩾3\ell_{i}\geqslant 3, kj⩾2k_{j}\geqslant 2
and the number of path components equals s+1s+1. The problem is therefore to show that the extremal graph is t=0t=0 together with the balanced path partition Pn,s+1P_{n,s+1}.
Lin and Zhou’s argument proceeds by local edge-switching: given two candidate configurations, one exchanges a single edge and uses a Rayleigh quotient to show ρ\rho strictly decreases. For large ss, this works well because the number of candidate configurations is small and can be enumerated. In fact, when s=⌈12​(n−5)⌉s=\lceil\frac{1}{2}(n-5)\rceil, the only cyclic candidate is C3∪n−32​K2C_{3}\cup\tfrac{n-3}{2}K_{2}, and a single edge-switch to Pn,(n−3)/2P_{n,(n-3)/2} suffices.



However, when ss is small, the number of possible cycle structures grows without bound, and as one can verify numerically, no single step edge-switch is monotone. There exist cyclic configurations GG such that every local rearrangement, which breaks one cycle and reconnects one path, either fails to be admissible or increases ρ\rho. The following example with n=11n=11 and s=1s=1 illustrates this.




ρ⁡(C3∪P4∪P4¯)≈11.65444,ρ⁡(P3∪P8¯)≈11.65452.\rho(\overline{C_{3}\cup P_{4}\cup P_{4}})\approx 11.65444,\qquad\rho(\overline{P_{3}\cup P_{8}})\approx 11.65452.



The natural operation, which breaks the C3C_{3} into P3P_{3} and merges the two P4P_{4}’s into P8P_{8}, increases the value of ρ\rho, even though the minimum ρ⁡(P11,2¯)=ρ⁡(P5∪P6¯)≈11.65442\rho(\overline{P_{11,2}})=\rho(\overline{P_{5}\cup P_{6}})\approx 11.65442 is strictly smaller than both of the above two graphs.
In other words, the minimum can not be reached by a monotone sequence of single-step improvements of the edge-switch operation. To overcome the difficulty, a global argument to compare the distance spectral radii of graphs is required.




Our approach.



We get rid of the use of local edge-switching operations as it fails to be monotone for small integer ss. Instead, we develop a new global comparison framework grounded from matrix analysis.
Our approach can be partitioned into three stages.



The first stage lies in linking the distance matrix D⁡(G)D(G) to the adjacency matrix A⁡(H0)A(H_{0}) of the complement H0=G¯H_{0}=\overline{G}. We show an identity D⁡(G)=J−I+A⁡(H0)D(G)=J-I+A(H_{0}), and that ρ⁡(G)\rho(G) is the unique root of the equation 𝟏𝖳​((ρ+1)​I−A⁡(H0))−1​𝟏=1\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}((\rho+1)I-A(H_{0}))^{-1}\mathbf{1}=1; see Lemma 2.3.
For a graph KK, we denote
ΦK​(λ):=𝟏𝖳​(λ​I−A⁡(K))−1​𝟏\Phi_{K}(\lambda):=\bm{1}^{\mathsf{T}}(\lambda I-A(K))^{-1}\bm{1}.
Let 𝒞⁡(H0)\mathcal{C}(H_{0}) be the set of components of H0H_{0}. We define the Ψ\Psi-function of GG as ΨG​(λ):=∑K∈𝒞⁡(H0)ΦK​(λ)\Psi_{G}(\lambda):=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{C}(H_{0})}\Phi_{K}(\lambda); see Definition 2.4.
Under the above notation, we reduce the problem of comparing distance spectral radii to evaluating these Ψ\Psi-functions; see Theorem 2.5.



The second stage is to compute the explicit formula of ΦK​(λ)\Phi_{K}(\lambda) when KK is chosen as a path and cycle; see Lemma 2.6. Based on these formulas, we then perform a rigorous increment analysis of ΦK​(λ)\Phi_{K}(\lambda) when KK is a path. We provide an upper bound on the increment and establish a strict convexity property, showing that the map k↦ΦPk​(λ)k\mapsto\Phi_{P_{k}}(\lambda) is strictly convex on kk; see Theorem 2.7.



The third stage is an argument for balancing path lengths.
The properties from the second stage allow us to formulate balancing principles, demonstrating
that the complement H0H_{0} of the extremal graph must consist exclusively of paths of nearly equal lengths; see Lemma 2.8 and Claim 2.1. Moreover, we show that H0H_{0} must be devoid of any cycles; see Lemma 2.9 and Claim 2.2.



Finally, we present a short self-contained proof in the range s⩾n−22s\geqslant\frac{n-2}{2}.
By expanding the Ψ\Psi-function via the Neumann series, we see that
ΨG​(λ)=∑k=0∞wk​(H0)λk+1\Psi_{G}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{w_{k}(H_{0})}{\lambda^{k+1}},
where wk​(H0)w_{k}(H_{0}) denotes the total number of walks of length kk in H0H_{0}, we integrate the comparison principle in Theorem 2.5 with walk enumeration to provide a new unified proof covering the entire range 1⩽s⩽n−11\leqslant s\leqslant n-1.



We anticipate that the method developed in this paper will offer an efficient approach to solving additional extremal problems related to graphs and their distance spectral radii.







2 Proof of the main result



In order to prove Theorem 1.2,
we need to present some preliminary results.
In the range 1⩽s<n−221\leqslant s<\frac{n-2}{2},

3 A new proof for the
range s⩾12​(n−2)s\geqslant\frac{1}{2}(n-2)



In previous section, we determined the spectral extremal graph in the difficult case for the range 1⩽s<n−221\leqslant s<\frac{n-2}{2}, and thereby solved Lin–Zhou’s problem in Conjecture 1.1.
The range s⩾max⁡{n−62,1}s\geqslant\max\{\frac{n-6}{2},1\} was already confirmed by Lin and Zhou [17] by using the Rayleigh quotient technique.
In this range, we cannot apply the structural result stated in Theorem 2.1, so that we cannot use the previous lemmas for the increment analysis of Φ\Phi-functions on paths and cycles.



In this section, we provide another application of our approach developed in this paper.
Applying the comparison principle in Theorem 2.5, we give a new proof of Lin–Zhou’s result for the range s⩾n−22s\geqslant\frac{n-2}{2} by combining Ψ\Psi-functions with walk enumeration.
Counting the number of walks in a graph turns out to be powerful in studying spectral extremal problems; see, e.g., [18, 14, 6].



Before comparing the Ψ\Psi-functions, we need to bound the diameter of GG.



Lemma 3.1.


If GG is a graph on nn vertices with m>(n−12)m>\binom{n-1}{2} edges, then diam⁡(G)⩽2\mathrm{diam}(G)\leqslant 2.




Proof.


Suppose for contradiction that diam⁡(G)⩾3\mathrm{diam}(G)\geqslant 3. There exist u,v∈V⁡(G)u,v\in V(G) such that distance dG​(u,v)⩾3d_{G}(u,v)\geqslant 3. Thus uu and vv are non-adjacent, and N⁡(u)∩N⁡(v)=∅N(u)\cap N(v)=\emptyset. We now count the missing edges (non-edges) in GG. Observe that uu is non-adjacent to n−1−d⁡(u)n-1-d(u) vertices. In addition, vv is non-adjacent to d⁡(u)d(u) vertices of N⁡(u)N(u). So the total number of non-edges of u,vu,v is at least (n−1−d⁡(u))+d⁡(u)=n−1(n-1-d(u))+d(u)=n-1.
Then we have e⁡(G)⩽(n2)−(n−1)=(n−12)e(G)\leqslant\binom{n}{2}-(n-1)=\binom{n-1}{2}, contradicting with the assumption m>(n−12)m>\binom{n-1}{2}. Thus, the diameter of GG is at most 22.
∎




Recall that e⁡(G)=m=(n−12)+se(G)=m={n-1\choose 2}+s.
Since s⩾1s\geqslant 1, we have m>(n−12)m>\binom{n-1}{2}.
Lemma 3.1 guarantees diam⁡(G)⩽2\mathrm{diam}(G)\leqslant 2, which implies for any two distinct u,v∈V⁡(G)u,v\in V(G), dG​(u,v)=1d_{G}(u,v)=1 if and only if u​v∈E⁡(G)uv\in E(G); and dG​(u,v)=2d_{G}(u,v)=2 if and only if u​v∉E⁡(G)uv\notin E(G).
Let H0=G¯H_{0}=\overline{G} denote the complement of GG.
We conclude that D⁡(G)=J−I+A⁡(H0)D(G)=J-I+A(H_{0}).
Thus, the conclusions of Lemma 2.3 remain valid. Consequently, the Ψ\Psi-function of GG in Definition 2.4 is well-defined, and Theorem 2.5 is still applicable.



Note that e:=e⁡(H0)=(n2)−m=n−1−se:=e(H_{0})=\binom{n}{2}-m=n-1-s. For the range s⩾n−22s\geqslant\frac{n-2}{2}, we have e⩽n2e\leqslant\frac{n}{2} and

we expand the Ψ\Psi-function as

(17)


Let wk​(H0):=𝟏𝖳​A​(H0)k​𝟏w_{k}(H_{0}):=\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}A(H_{0})^{k}\mathbf{1} denote the total number of walks of length kk in H0H_{0}.



Lemma 3.2.


Let H0H_{0} be a graph on nn vertices with n−s−1n-s-1 edges, where s⩾n−22s\geqslant\frac{n-2}{2}. Then wk​(H0)⩾wk​(Pn,s+1)w_{k}(H_{0})\geqslant w_{k}(P_{n,s+1}) for every k⩾1k\geqslant 1.
