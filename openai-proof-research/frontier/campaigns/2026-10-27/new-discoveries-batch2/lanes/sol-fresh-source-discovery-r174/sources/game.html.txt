 A Permutation Avoidance Game with Reverse Replies and Monotone Traps 
 
 
 Henning Ulfarsson
 
 Date: August 24, 2026 

 Abstract. 
    
 We study the impartial game PAP (“permutations avoiding patterns”), in which players take turns choosing patterns to avoid. We define a set of length  k k  patterns,  B k B_{k} ,
and show that it is the unique minimal
monotone-forcing subset of  S k S_{k} : every sufficiently long permutation that avoids  B k B_{k}  is
monotone, and every monotone-forcing subset of  S k S_{k}  must contain  B k B_{k} . We prove a quadratic upper bound for the
monotone-forcing threshold, and determine the exact thresholds for  k = 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 k=3,4,5,6 . We use properties
of the sets  B k B_{k}  to prove that a reverse-reply strategy wins PAP on  S n S_{n}  when  k = 4 k=4 
for all  n ≥ 10 n\geq 10 ; for  k = 3 k=3 , the same strategy can be analysed directly. We conjecture that it is a winning strategy for all  k k  and  n n  sufficiently large. 
  
 
 
 1.  Introduction 

 
 Fix  n ≥ 1 n\geq 1  and let  S n S_{n}  be the set of permutations of length  n n  in one-line notation.
For  π = π ( 1 ) ⋯ π ( n ) ∈ S n \pi=\pi(1)\cdots\pi(n)\in S_{n} , we write  π r = π ( n ) ⋯ π ( 1 ) \pi^{r}=\pi(n)\cdots\pi(1)  for the
 reverse  and  π c = ( n + 1 − π ( 1 ) ) ⋯ ( n + 1 − π ( n ) ) \pi^{c}=(n{+}1{-}\pi(1))\cdots(n{+}1{-}\pi(n))  for the  complement .
We say that  π \pi   contains  a pattern  p ∈ S k p\in S_{k}  if there exist indices
 1 ≤ i 1 < ⋯ < i k ≤ n 1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n  such that the subsequence  π ( i 1 ) ⋯ π ( i k ) \pi(i_{1})\cdots\pi(i_{k})  is
order-isomorphic to  p p ; otherwise  π \pi   avoids   p p .
For background on permutation patterns and permutation classes, see
Vatter  [ 12 ] .
Throughout the paper we study the following normal-play impartial game. 
 
 
 Definition 1.1 . 
 
 Fix  k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 . In the game  Permutations Avoiding Patterns , or  PAP ,  with pattern length  k k , a position is a subset
 X ⊆ S n X\subseteq S_{n} . A move consists of choosing a pattern  p ∈ S k p\in S_{k}  that is contained
in at least one permutation of  X X , and replacing  X X  by the subset of permutations in  X X  that
avoid  p p . 
 
 
 
 Because a chosen length- k k  pattern remains forbidden for the rest of the game and there are only
 k ! k!  such patterns, every play terminates after at most  k ! k!  moves. 
 
 
 The game can equally well be played with more general notions of pattern, such as the mesh
patterns of Brändén and Claesson  [ 2 ] , but we restrict attention to
classical patterns throughout. Related sequence and permutation games appear already in the
monotonic sequence games of Harary, Sagan, and West  [ 5 ]  and the later work
of Albert et al.  [ 1 ] . More recently, Pudwell  [ 9 ] 
studied a different two-player permutation game based on the Erdős–Szekeres theorem, where the
players build a permutation one entry at a time and try to avoid long monotone subsequences.
Combinatorial games on permutations were also studied by Parton  [ 7 ] , who introduced
PermuNim, an impartial permutation-avoidance game played on an  m × n m\times n  board by placing points
in unused rows and columns while avoiding a fixed pattern. Thus Parton’s positions are partial
permutation matrices on a board, and the geometry of the board itself plays a central role, rather
than subsets of a fixed symmetric group  S n S_{n} . 
 
 
 We are mainly interested in the starting position  X = S n X=S_{n} . If a set
 F ⊆ S k F\subseteq S_{k}  of patterns has already been chosen, then the current position is 
 

 
 
 Av n ⁡ ( F ) = { π ∈ S n : π ​  avoids every pattern in  ​ F } . \operatorname{Av}_{n}(F)=\{\pi\in S_{n}:\pi\text{ avoids every pattern in }F\}. 
 
 
 So for fixed  k k  the game may be viewed as a game on subsets of  S k S_{k} . 
 
 
 We will show that for  k = 3 k=3 , a simple  reverse strategy  already wins for Player II (the second
player). For  k = 4 k=4 , and more generally for large- n n  reverse-reply questions, the key structure is
a unique minimal monotone-forcing set  B k ⊆ S k B_{k}\subseteq S_{k}  of patterns, which we define below. 
 
 
 From the game-theoretic point of view, the main results are these. For  k = 3 k=3 , the reverse reply
wins for every  n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 . For  k = 4 k=4 , the complete picture is that the move-by-move reverse
strategy works at  n = 4 n=4 , fails at  n = 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 n=5,6,7,8,9 , and then works again for all  n ≥ 10 n\geq 10 . The
failure at small  n n  splits into two different phenomena: at  n = 6 n=6  the starting position itself is
an N-position, whereas at  n = 5 , 7 , 8 , 9 n=5,7,8,9  the starting position is still a P-position but the
reverse move need not be the correct winning reply. The  k = 4 k=4  theorem for  n ≥ 10 n\geq 10  is proved by
combining the structural  B k B_{k}  analysis with a finite verification. 
 
 
 The paper therefore has two intertwined themes. Sections 3–5 and 7 develop the structural theory
of the sets  B k B_{k} : a recursive characterization, minimality, upper and lower bounds for the
monotone-forcing threshold, and threshold data for small  k k . Sections 2 and 6 are the
game-theoretic side: Section 2 analyzes the small starting positions, while Section 6 uses the
structure of  B k B_{k}  to prove the large- n n  reverse-reply theorem for  k = 4 k=4 . The role of  B k B_{k}  in
the game is precisely that it supplies both the monotone endgame and the near-monotone witness
families that make most reverse replies uniform. 
 
 
 For general  k k , the paper goes only as far as a conjectural extension of this picture. The
discussion beyond  k = 4 k=4  is intended to isolate the next structural obstruction, not to claim a
fully scalable theorem. 
 
 
 Several results in the paper are computer-assisted. Those computations are all exhaustive
and, with the exception of the separate  N 6 N_{6}  threshold computation, are reproduced by the scripts
collected in the public repository  [ 11 ] , specifically in the directory
 code-permuta/ ; see Section  9 . 
 
 
 
 2.  Basic PAP analysis 

 
 Definition 2.1 . 
 
 Fix  k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 . For a PAP position  X ⊆ S n X\subseteq S_{n} , the set of  followers 
 ℱ ⁡ ( X ) \mathcal{F}(X)  is the set of all positions reachable from  X X  in one move. The
 Sprague–Grundy value   [ 10 ,  4 ]  of  X X  is defined recursively by 
 

 
 
 sg ⁡ ( X ) = mex ⁡ { sg ⁡ ( Y ) : Y ∈ ℱ ⁡ ( X ) } , \operatorname{sg}(X)=\operatorname{mex}\{\operatorname{sg}(Y):Y\in\mathcal{F}(X)\}, 
 
 
 where  mex ⁡ ( S ) \operatorname{mex}(S)  denotes the minimal excludant, that is, the least non-negative integer not in  S S . A terminal position (one with
 ℱ ⁡ ( X ) = ∅ \mathcal{F}(X)=\varnothing ) has  sg ⁡ ( X ) = 0 \operatorname{sg}(X)=0 . Under normal play, Player I (the next mover) wins
from  X X  if and only if  sg ⁡ ( X ) ≠ 0 \operatorname{sg}(X)\neq 0  and the position is called an  N-position .
Otherwise, when  sg ⁡ ( X ) = 0 \operatorname{sg}(X)=0 , it is called a  P-position  (previous player wins). 
 
 
 
 For the starting position with the single allowed pattern length  k k , we write  sg ⁡ ( S n , k ) \operatorname{sg}(S_{n},k)  for
the Sprague–Grundy value of  S n S_{n} . 
 
 
 Proposition 2.2 . 
 
 For every  n ≥ 1 n\geq 1 ,  sg ⁡ ( S n , 1 ) = 1 \operatorname{sg}(S_{n},1)=1 . 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 The only legal move is to choose the pattern  1 1 , which removes every permutation.
∎ 
 
 
 
 Proposition 2.3 . 
 
 For every  n ≥ 1 n\geq 1 ,  sg ⁡ ( S n , 2 ) = 0 \operatorname{sg}(S_{n},2)=0 . 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 If  n = 1 n=1 , there are no legal moves, so  sg ⁡ ( S 1 , 2 ) = 0 \operatorname{sg}(S_{1},2)=0 . Now assume  n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 .
The only length- 2 2  patterns are  12 12  and  21 21 . Choosing  12 12  leaves  Av n ⁡ ( { 12 } ) = { id n r } \operatorname{Av}_{n}(\{12\})=\{\operatorname{id}_{n}^{r}\} ,
from which the only legal move is  21 21 , after which the position is empty. The case of first choosing
 21 21  is symmetric. Hence every follower
of  S n S_{n}  has Sprague–Grundy value  1 1 , and  sg ⁡ ( S n , 2 ) = mex ⁡ { 1 } = 0 \operatorname{sg}(S_{n},2)=\operatorname{mex}\{1\}=0 .
∎ 
 
 
 
 By the proposition above, for  k = 2 k=2  and  n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 , Player II wins by choosing the reverse of
Player I’s move. The same reverse-reply idea already settles the case  k = 3 k=3 . 
 
 
 Lemma 2.4 . 
 
 For  n ≥ 4 n\geq 4  we have 
 

 
 
 Av n ⁡ ( 123,321,132,231 ) \displaystyle\operatorname{Av}_{n}(123,321,132,231) 
 = ∅ , \displaystyle=\varnothing, 
 
 
 
 Av n ⁡ ( 123,321,213,312 ) \displaystyle\operatorname{Av}_{n}(123,321,213,312) 
 = ∅ , \displaystyle=\varnothing, 
 
 
 
 Av n ⁡ ( 132,231,213,312 ) \displaystyle\operatorname{Av}_{n}(132,231,213,312) 
 = { id n , id n r } . \displaystyle=\{\operatorname{id}_{n},\operatorname{id}_{n}^{r}\}. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Every permutation of length at least  4 4  contains a  4 4 -point subpattern order-isomorphic to some
element of  S 4 S_{4} , so it is enough to check the  24 24  permutations in  S 4 S_{4} . A direct inspection shows that every element of  S 4 S_{4} 
contains at least one of  123,321,132,231 123,321,132,231 , and similarly at least one of  123,321,213,312 123,321,213,312 .
Likewise, every element of  S 4 S_{4}  except  1234 1234  and  4321 4321  contains at least one of
 132,231,213,312 132,231,213,312 .
∎ 
 
 
 
 Theorem 2.5 . 
 
 For every  n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 ,  sg ⁡ ( S n , 3 ) = 0 \operatorname{sg}(S_{n},3)=0 . 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 The statement is immediate for  n = 3 n=3 , so assume  n ≥ 4 n\geq 4 .
Player II replies to every move by choosing the reverse pattern. 
 
 
 If Player I chooses  123 123 , Player II chooses  321 321 . If  n ≥ 5 n\geq 5  then Player II has already won, since by the
Erdős–Szekeres theorem  [ 3 ]  every permutation of length at least  5 5  contains either  123 123  or  321 321 . If  n = 4 n=4 , a move is possible, and
by Lemma  2.4 , any third move
must be one of  132,213,231,312 132,213,231,312 , and after that Player II can again choose the reverse and finish
the game. 
 
 
 If Player I chooses  132 132 , Player II chooses  231 231 . If Player I next chooses  123 123  or  321 321 , then
Player II chooses the reverse and empties the set by Lemma  2.4 . If Player I
chooses  213 213  or  312 312 , then Player II again chooses the reverse, and the position becomes
 { id n , id n r } \{\operatorname{id}_{n},\operatorname{id}_{n}^{r}\} . In that position the only legal moves are  123 123  and  321 321 , so it is again a
P-position. The cases  231 231 ,  213 213 ,  312 312 , and  321 321  follow by symmetry.
∎

 The picture becomes more nuanced already at  k = 4 k=4 . The exceptional value 
 

 
 
 sg ⁡ ( S 6 , 4 ) = 2 \operatorname{sg}(S_{6},4)=2 
 
 
 shows that the naive guess that  S n S_{n}  should always be a P-position for fixed  k k  is false.
A further computation explains this value: among the  24 24  followers  Av 6 ⁡ ( p ) \operatorname{Av}_{6}(p) , twelve have
Sprague–Grundy value  0 0 , two have value  1 1 , and the remaining ten have value  3 3 ; hence 
 

 
 
 sg ⁡ ( S 6 , 4 ) = mex ⁡ { 0 , 1 , 3 } = 2 . \operatorname{sg}(S_{6},4)=\operatorname{mex}\{0,1,3\}=2. 
 
 
 Moreover, even when the starting position is a P-position, the move-by-move reverse strategy
need not be a winning strategy. 
 
 
 Proposition 2.7 . 
 
 For  k = 4 k=4 , tests from the starting position  S n S_{n}  show that the
move-by-move reverse strategy works for  n = 4 n=4  and fails for  n = 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 n=5,6,7,8,9 . 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 This follows from an exhaustive computer search of the reverse-strategy game tree for
 4 ≤ n ≤ 9 4\leq n\leq 9 ; see Section  9 .
∎ 
 
 
 
 Example  2.8  illustrates how the reverse strategy can fail. 
 
 
 Example 2.8 . 
 
 At  n = 9 n=9  the reverse reply can already fail on the very next move, even though it remains legal.

yet it lands in an N-position: 
 

 
 
 sg ⁡ ( { 1234 , 4321 , 1324 , 4231 } ) = 2 . \operatorname{sg}(\{1234,4321,1324,4231\})=2. 
 
 
 In fact, the winning replies after  1324 1324  are  3412 3412 ,  3421 3421 , and  4312 4312 .

 These computations suggest that the reverse strategy does not hold uniformly in  n n , but may still
govern the large- n n  game. We will prove below that for  k = 4 k=4  the reverse strategy does indeed
succeed for every  n ≥ 10 n\geq 10 . 
 
 
 We believe that the same phenomenon holds for general  k k : there are small- n n  obstructions to the reverse strategy,
but for large enough  n n  it is a winning strategy for Player II. We therefore make the following conjecture. 
 
 
 Conjecture 2.9 . 
 
 For every  k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 , there exists  n 0 ​ ( k ) n_{0}(k)  such that the reverse strategy is a winning strategy
for Player II on  S n S_{n}  for all  n ≥ n 0 ​ ( k ) n\geq n_{0}(k) . In particular,  sg ⁡ ( S n , k ) = 0 \operatorname{sg}(S_{n},k)=0  for all such
 n n . 
 
 
 
 To build the theoretical foundation for that later  k = 4 k=4  theorem, we first isolate the monotone
endgame and then formulate the reverse-reply criterion that we will actually use. 
 
 
 Definition 2.10 . 
 
 We say that a set  F ⊆ S k F\subseteq S_{k}  is  reverse-closed  if  p ∈ F p\in F  if and only if
 p r ∈ F p^{r}\in F . For a fixed length  n n , a pattern  p p  is  legal  from  F F  at length
 n n  if some permutation in  Av n ⁡ ( F ) \operatorname{Av}_{n}(F)  contains  p p . When the length  n n  is clear from the
context, we simply say that  p p  is  legal  from  F F . 
 
 
 
 Lemma 2.11 . 
 
 If  F ⊆ S k F\subseteq S_{k}  is reverse-closed, then  Av n ⁡ ( F ) \operatorname{Av}_{n}(F)  is reverse-closed:  π ∈ Av n ⁡ ( F ) \pi\in\operatorname{Av}_{n}(F) 
if and only if  π r ∈ Av n ⁡ ( F ) \pi^{r}\in\operatorname{Av}_{n}(F) . In particular, a pattern  p p  is legal from  F F  if and only
if  p r p^{r}  is legal from  F F . 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 A permutation  π \pi  avoids  F F  if and only if  π r \pi^{r}  avoids  F r = F F^{r}=F .
∎ 
 
 
 
 Proposition 2.12 . 
 
 Let  k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , let  F ⊆ S k F\subseteq S_{k}  be reverse-closed, and suppose that  F F  contains neither
monotone pattern. Then both monotone patterns are legal from  F F . If
 n ≥ ( k − 1 ) 2 + 1 n\geq(k-1)^{2}+1 , then after one monotone pattern is chosen, the other is legal, and after both
monotone patterns are chosen no legal moves remain. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Since  F F  contains neither monotone pattern, the increasing permutation  id n \operatorname{id}_{n}  and the decreasing
permutation  id n r \operatorname{id}_{n}^{r}  both lie in  Av n ⁡ ( F ) \operatorname{Av}_{n}(F) . Hence  12 ⋯ k 12\cdots k  and  k ⋯ 21 k\cdots 21  are both legal
from  F F . 
 
 
 After  12 ⋯ k 12\cdots k  is chosen, the permutation  id n r \operatorname{id}_{n}^{r}  still lies in
 Av n ( F ∪ { 12 ⋯ k } ) \operatorname{Av}_{n}(F\cup\{12\cdots k\})  and contains  k ⋯ 21 k\cdots 21 , so the decreasing monotone pattern
remains legal. The other case is symmetric. 
 
 
 If both monotone patterns have been chosen and  n ≥ ( k − 1 ) 2 + 1 n\geq(k-1)^{2}+1 , then by the Erdős–Szekeres
theorem every permutation of length  n n  contains at least one of them, so no permutation survives.
∎ 
 
 
 
 Corollary 2.13 . 
 
 Fix  k ≥ 2 k\geq 2  and  n ≥ ( k − 1 ) 2 + 1 n\geq(k-1)^{2}+1 . Suppose that whenever  F ⊆ S k F\subseteq S_{k}  is reverse-closed,
contains neither monotone pattern, and  p ∈ S k ∖ F p\in S_{k}\setminus F  is a non-monotone pattern legal
from  F F , the reverse pattern  p r p^{r}  is legal from  F ∪ { p } F\cup\{p\} . Then the move-by-move reverse
strategy is a winning strategy for Player II on  S n S_{n} . In particular,  S n S_{n}  is a P-position. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 Starting from the empty forbidden set, Player II always replies to Player I by choosing the reverse
pattern. At each stage, let  F F  denote the current forbidden set after Player II’s previous move;
then  F F  is reverse-closed. 
 
 
 If Player I chooses a non-monotone pattern while  F F  contains neither monotone pattern, then the
hypothesis gives a legal reply  p r p^{r} . 
 
 
 If Player I chooses a monotone pattern, then Proposition  2.12  shows that the
other monotone pattern is legal, and that after Player II replies there are no legal moves left. 
 
 
 Thus Player II can answer every move of Player I by the reverse pattern, and the game ends on

 6.1.  Toward general  k k 

 
 The  k = 4 k=4  proof suggests a plausible approach to  k = 5 k=5 .
First one reduces, via Corollary  2.13 , to reverse-closed
monotone-free states. Next the four reverse pairs inside  B k B_{k}  are handled uniformly by the
singleton-shadow witness families. One would then try to treat the remaining patterns by bounded witness
families whose non-monotone shadows involve only a small amount of extra support, and finally one
isolates and analyzes the residual hard reverse pairs that survive those first passes. 
 
 
 The combinatorics of the last step, however, should become substantially more complicated as  k k 
grows. There are 
 

 
 
 k ! − 2 2 \frac{k!-2}{2} 
 
 
 non-monotone reverse pairs in total, and after removing the four reverse pairs coming from  B k B_{k} 
there remain 
 

 
 
 k ! − 10 2 \frac{k!-10}{2} 
 
 
 non- B k B_{k}  reverse pairs that could potentially be hard. For  k = 4 k=4  this number is  7 7 , and the
explicit companion families reduce it all the way down to the single pair  { 2413 , 3142 } \{2413,3142\} . For
 k = 5 k=5  the corresponding number is already  55 55 . The present computations show that the simplest
one-companion construction works for  14 14  of these, but this still leaves
many unresolved cases, so one should not expect a single hard pair to remain. 
 
 
 This suggests that an extension of the  k = 4 k=4  proof to  k = 5 k=5  should still be possible, but
it is likely to be more nuanced and case-based, with many more exceptional configurations to treat.
For general  k k , however, one should probably not expect a direct iteration of the  k = 4 k=4  argument to
be enough: a complete proof of Conjecture  2.9  will likely require a new idea
beyond the present witness-family analysis. 
 
 
 One potential general approach is trying to use the fact that the hard pair for  k = 4 k=4 
consists of exactly the simple permutations of length  4 4 , so perhaps one can use the
theory of simple permutations to push to general  k k .

 So  m 1 m_{1}  lies to the left of and below every other point of  π \pi . Now suppose that  π \pi  has a

∎

 8.  Concluding remarks 

 
 The sets  B k B_{k}  seem to be the right finite obstruction sets for single-length PAP. They are
minimal by Theorem  4.3 , eventually monotone forcing by
Theorem  5.2  and Corollary  7.2 , and the thresholds for
 k = 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 k=3,4,5,6 , are
 N 3 = 1 N_{3}=1 ,  N 4 = 7 N_{4}=7 ,  N 5 = 14 N_{5}=14 , and  N 6 = 25 N_{6}=25 . For  k = 3 k=3 , the reverse strategy is completely understood
(Theorem  2.5 ); for  k = 4 k=4 , the reverse strategy is now settled as well, with
exceptions at  n = 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 n=5,6,7,8,9  and a large- n n  theorem proved via stable one-entry inflation
families. The staircase permutations  E k E_{k}  from
Proposition  7.4  show that 
 

 
 
 N k ≥ k 2 − 2 ​ k − 1 , N_{k}\geq k^{2}-2k-1, 
 
 
 while Proposition  7.5  gives the stronger bound 
 

 
 
 N k ≥ 2 ​ ( k − 2 ) ​ ( k − 3 ) + 1 N_{k}\geq 2(k-2)(k-3)+1 
 
 
 for  k ≥ 6 k\geq 6 , and already yields  N 6 ≥ 25 N_{6}\geq 25 . 
 
 
 The next step is to push the same program to  k = 5 k=5  and beyond
(Conjecture  2.9 ). 
 
 
 There are also several natural game-theoretic variants that are not pursued here. Since PAP
positions are finite impartial games with well-defined Sprague–Grundy values, one can consider
disjunctive sums of PAP positions, for example sums of starting positions  S n S_{n}  with different
pattern lengths. The normal-play convention is also not essential: because every play has length at
most  k ! k! , a misère version is just as natural, although the reverse-reply arguments used here do
not immediately transfer to that setting. Finally, one could study partizan versions in which the
two players are allowed to choose from different sets of patterns, or even from different allowed
pattern lengths. These variants seem likely to require ideas beyond the present paper, but they fit
naturally with the viewpoint developed here.
