 Abstract. 
    
 We consider the problem of enumerating hypermatrices of format  2 × ( k + 1 ) × k 2\times(k+1)\times k  over a finite field that have nonzero hyperdeterminant and whose nonzero entries are restricted to a plane partition. We conjecture an attractive product formula for the enumeration, and prove it in many cases. In general, we show that the enumeration is given (up to a power of  q − 1 q-1 ) by a polynomial in  q q  with nonnegative integer coefficients, whose value at  q = 1 q=1  enumerates a natural family of three-dimensional rook placements. 
  
 
 
 1.  Introduction 

 
 In this paper we attempt to generalize the classical  q q -rook theory of matrices to higher dimensions. We begin with a brief survey of the classical theory which will serve as a starting point for our generalization. 
 
 
 Counting permutations as rook placements was first systematically studied by Kaplansky and Riordan  [ 11 ] , who showed a large family of permutation enumeration questions can be phrased in the terms of rook theory. Here a  rook  can be thought of as a chess rook (which can attack vertically and horizontally) and a  rook placement  is a placement of non-attacking rooks on a subset of a chessboard; for a more precise statement see Definition  2.7 . Rook theory was further developed in a series of five papers by Goldman, Joichi, and White. In particular, their first paper  [ 8 ]  developed a complete understanding of when two partition-shaped boards have the same number of rook placements for all numbers of rooks. The “ q q ” in  q q -rook theory was first studied by Garsia and Remmel  [ 6 ] , who introduced the  q q -rook number 
 

 
 
 R k ​ ( B , q ) = ∑ C q inv ⁡ ( C , B ) R_{k}(B;q)=\sum_{C}q^{\operatorname{inv}(C,B)} 
 
 
 of a board  B B . In this formula, the sum is over the set of rook placements  C C  of  k k  rooks on the partition-shaped board  B B , and  inv ⁡ ( C , B ) \operatorname{inv}(C,B)  is a certain  inversion statistic  associated to each placement. 
 
 
 In addition to rook numbers, one can also look at  hit numbers  for a particular board contained in the  n n -by- n n  square: the  k k th hit number is the number of  n n -rook placements on the square in which exactly  k k  rooks are placed in  B B . Garsia and Remmel were using the language of rook theory as a means to solve a separate problem but in the process discovered that the  q q -hit number  for partition-shaped boards is a polynomial with positive coefficients. However, they were unhappy with the proof of this fact and asked for others to provide a more satisfying explanation. 
 
 
 In response to this call for a better proof, Haglund  [ 9 ]  gave an interpretation of  q q -rook and  q q -hit numbers in terms of matrices over finite fields with entries forced to be zero. 1 1 
             1 
            
            
            
          An alternative proof, via other methods, was given around the same time by Dworkin  [ 5 ] .  In particular, Haglund devised a way to relate each placement of  k k  rooks on a board  B B  to a collection of matrices over a finite field  𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q}  of rank  k k . He then used this to prove that the number of  n × n n\times n  matrices of rank  k k  with support corresponding to  B B , which he called  P k ​ ( B ) P_{k}(B) , is related to the  q q -rook number  R k ​ ( B , q ) R_{k}(B;q)  by 
 

 
 (1) 
 
 P k ​ ( B ) = ( q − 1 ) k ​ q | B | − k ​ R k ​ ( B , q − 1 ) . P_{k}(B)=(q-1)^{k}q^{|B|-k}R_{k}(B;q^{-1}). 
 
 
 In the particular case that  k = n k=n , the expression on both sides of this equation factors in an attractive way (see Theorem  2.10 ). 
 
 
 The main goal of this paper is to generalize this connection between the number of rook placements and the number rank- k k  matrices over a finite field to higher dimensions by enumerating  hypermatrices . Our work is inspired by an unpublished note by Musiker and Yu  [ 15 ] , who showed that the number of hypermatrices (see Definition  2.1 ) over  𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q}  with nonzero  hyperdeterminant , the multidimensional analogue of the determinant, factors nicely in the  2 × 2 × 2 2\times 2\times 2  case. It is worth noting that there are at least three inequivalent ways to define a hyperdeterminant; we will be using Cayley’s second definition, commonly denoted  Det \operatorname{Det} , whose important properties are outlined below in Corollary  2.4 . If a hypermatrix has nonzero hyperdeterminant we say it is  nondegenerate .
We denote by  GL k ⁡ ( 𝔽 q ) \operatorname{GL}_{k}(\mathbb{F}_{q})  the general linear group of  k × k k\times k  invertible matrices over  𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} . 
 
 
 It is easy to show that

 which led them to conjecture the number of nondegenerate hypermatrices of dimension  k + 1 k+1  in the general  2 × 2 × ⋯ × 2 2\times 2\times\cdots\times 2  case is  ( q 2 k − 1 ) ​ ( q 2 k − q 2 k − 1 ) (q^{2^{k}}-1)(q^{2^{k}}-q^{2^{k}-1}) . However, they were unsuccessful in even proving the  2 × 2 × 2 × 2 2\times 2\times 2\times 2  case due to the fact that the hyperdeterminant is this case is unapproachable, having over  2 2  million terms. 
 
 
 It turns out that, rather than the “cubic” hypermatrices considered by Musiker and Yu, the easier hypermatrices to study are those of  boundary format , which are hypermatrices of shape 
 

 
 
 ( k 1 + 1 ) × ( k 2 + 1 ) × ⋯ × ( k r + 1 ) , (k_{1}+1)\times(k_{2}+1)\times\cdots\times(k_{r}+1), 
 
 
 with  k i = k 1 + k 2 + ⋯ + k i − 1 + k i + 1 + ⋯ + k r k_{i}=k_{1}+k_{2}+\cdots+k_{i-1}+k_{i+1}+\cdots+k_{r}  for some  1 ≤ i ≤ r 1\leq i\leq r .
It was shown by Aitken  [ 1 ]  that for dimension  3 3 , the question of whether or not a boundary format hypermatrix is nondegenerate reduces to a question of whether or not a certain family of matrices are full rank (see Lemma  2.5 ). This is helpful for enumeration but it is actually better than that: using this fact Aitken showed that there exists a free and transitive group action on the set  M k ​ ( 𝔽 q ) M_{k}(\mathbb{F}_{q})  of nondegenerate  2 × ( k + 1 ) × k 2\times(k+1)\times k  hypermatrices with entries in  𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q}  and using this he was able to show that 
 

 
 
 | M k ( 𝔽 q ) | = q k 2 ( q − 1 ) 2 ​ k [ k + 1 ] ! q [ k ] ! q . |M_{k}(\mathbb{F}_{q})|=q^{k^{2}}(q-1)^{2k}[k+1]!_{q}[k]!_{q}. 
 
 
 We pick up where Aitken left off, refining this count for boundary format hypermatrices with restricted entries. 
 
 
 We begin in Section  2  by reviewing the basic definitions and concepts of hypermatrices and the hyperdeterminant. We then show there exists an analogous concept to matrices with an integer partition-shaped section forced to be zero for boundary format hypermatrices. Specifically, these are hypermatrices with a plane partition section forced to be zero. We also show that there is a unique maximal (by inclusion) plane partition such that nondegenerate hypermatrices exist when the entries of the plane partition are forced to be zero. 
 
 
 In Section  3 , we state our main conjecture (Conjecture  3.1 ): for  2 × ( k + 1 ) × k 2\times(k+1)\times k  hypermatrices and a plane partition  P = ⟨ λ , μ ⟩ P=\langle\lambda,\mu\rangle  where  λ \lambda  and  μ \mu  are integer partitions of length  k k  (see Definition  2.11  for this notational convention), the number of nondegenerate hypermatrices with support avoiding  P P  is 
 

 
 
 q k 2 ( q − 1 ) 2 ​ k [ k + 1 − λ 1 ] q ⋯ [ 2 − λ k ] q ⋅ [ k − μ 1 ] q ⋯ [ 1 − μ k ] q . q^{k^{2}}(q-1)^{2k}[k+1-\lambda_{1}]_{q}\cdots[2-\lambda_{k}]_{q}\cdot[k-\mu_{1}]_{q}\cdots[1-\mu_{k}]_{q}. 
 
 
 Although we are unable to prove our conjecture for all  P P , we show that it holds for several natural families of plane partitions, including plane partitions of one layer, the largest and smallest plane partitions for which the question is interesting, and all plane partitions in the case  k = 2 k=2 . 
 
 
 In Section  4 , we provide further indirect evidence in favor of Conjecture  3.1 . We introduce a three-dimensional analogue of rook placements that we call  hyperrook placements . We show that the number of hyperrook placements on a  2 × ( k + 1 ) × k 2\times(k+1)\times k  board avoiding a plane partition  P = ⟨ λ , μ ⟩ P=\langle\lambda,\mu\rangle  is exactly the value 
 

 
 
 ( k + 1 − λ 1 ) ⋯ ( 2 − λ k ) ⋅ ( k − μ 1 ) ⋯ ( 1 − μ k ) (k+1-\lambda_{1})\cdots(2-\lambda_{k})\cdot(k-\mu_{1})\cdots(1-\mu_{k}) 
 
 
 that one would predict by substituting  q = 1 q=1  into Conjecture  3.1 . Further, in Section  4.2 , we make a concrete connection between these hyperrook placements and nondegenerate hypermatrices concrete, giving an analogue of Haglund’s theorem ( 1 ). Our main result (Theorem  4.1 ) is to establish the following weak version of Conjecture  3.1 : for every plane partition  P P , the number of nondegenerate hypermatrices over  𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q}  avoiding  P P  is given by a polynomial in  q q  that (up to factors of  q − 1 q-1 ) has positive-integer coefficients and whose value at  q = 1 q=1  is the number of hyperrook placements avoiding  P P . 
 
 
 We conclude the paper with Section  5  with some final remarks and open questions. 
 
 
 
 2.  Hypermatrices and Nondegeneracy 

 
 Definition 2.1 . 
 
 A  hypermatrix  of format  ( k 1 + 1 ) × ( k 2 + 1 ) × ⋯ × ( k r + 1 ) (k_{1}+1)\times(k_{2}+1)\times\cdots\times(k_{r}+1)  over a field  𝔽 \mathbb{F}  is an  r r -dimensional array of elements  a i 1 , … , i r ∈ 𝔽 a_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}}\in\mathbb{F}  with  1 ≤ i j ≤ k j + 1 1\leq i_{j}\leq k_{j}+1  for  j = 1 , … , r j=1,\ldots,r . 
 
 
 
 A  slice  of a hypermatrix is an  ( r − 1 ) (r-1) -dimensional subarray which is obtained by holding one index constant. Furthermore, a  slice in the  i i th direction  is the subarray where we hold the  i i th index constant. For instance, the  2 × ( k + 1 ) × k 2\times(k+1)\times k  hypermatrix  ( a i 1 , i 2 , i 3 ) (a_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}})  with  1 ≤ i 1 ≤ 2 1\leq i_{1}\leq 2 ,   1 ≤ i 2 ≤ k + 1 1\leq i_{2}\leq k+1 , and  1 ≤ i 3 ≤ k 1\leq i_{3}\leq k  has two  ( k + 1 ) × k (k+1)\times k  slices in the first direction:  ( a 1 , i 2 , i 3 ) (a_{1,i_{2},i_{3}})  and  ( a 2 , i 2 , i 3 ) (a_{2,i_{2},i_{3}}) . For a hypermatrix of dimension  ( k 1 + 1 ) × ( k 2 + 1 ) × ⋯ × ( k r + 1 ) (k_{1}+1)\times(k_{2}+1)\times\cdots\times(k_{r}+1)  we can see there are  k i + 1 k_{i}+1  slices in the  i i th direction, each of dimension 
 

 
 
 ( k 1 + 1 ) × ⋯ × ( k i − 1 + 1 ) × ( k i + 1 + 1 ) × ⋯ × ( k r + 1 ) . (k_{1}+1)\times\cdots\times(k_{i-1}+1)\times(k_{i+1}+1)\times\cdots\times(k_{r}+1). 
 
 
 We call any two distinct slices in the same direction  parallel .

 There is a function on hypermatrices called the  hyperdeterminant  that plays a similar role to the determinant for matrices. In particular we call hypermatrices with zero hyperdeterminant  degenerate  and correspondingly hypermatrices with nonzero hyperdeterminant nondegenerate. To distiguish between the hyperdeterminant and matrix determinant we will denote the hyperdeterminant of a hypermatrix  M M  by  Det ⁡ ( M ) \operatorname{Det}(M)  and determinant of a matrix  A A  by  det ( A ) \det(A) . The formal definition of the hyperdeterminant is technical and outside the scope of this paper so instead we will describe its properties; for a complete definition see  [ 7 , p. 444] . Although the hyperdeterminant is defined for hypermatrices of any format, there are numerous formats for which every hypermatrix is degenerate, as the next theorem shows. 
 
 
 Theorem 2.2   ( [ 7 , Thm. 1.3] ) . 
 
 The hypdeterminant of format  ( k 1 + 1 ) × ⋯ × ( k r + 1 ) (k_{1}+1)\times\cdots\times(k_{r}+1)  is non-trivial if and only if 
 

 
 
 k j ≤ ∑ i ≠ j k i k_{j}\leq\sum_{i\neq j}k_{i} 
 
 
 for all  j = 1 , … , r j=1,\ldots,r . 
 
 
 
 We now restrict our attention to hypermatrices of non-trivial format. Let us fix a non-trivial format  ( k 1 + 1 ) × ( k 2 + 1 ) × ⋯ × ( k r + 1 ) (k_{1}+1)\times(k_{2}+1)\times\cdots\times(k_{r}+1) . 
 
 
 Proposition 2.3   ( [ 7 , Prop. 1.4] ) . 
 
 The hyperdeterminant is relatively invariant under the group action 
 

 
 
 G = GL k 1 + 1 ( 𝔽 ) × ⋯ × GL k r + 1 ( 𝔽 ) , G=\operatorname{GL}_{k_{1}+1}(\mathbb{F})\times\cdots\times\operatorname{GL}_{k_{r}+1}(\mathbb{F}), 
 
 
 that is, there exist integers  n 1 , n 2 , … , n r n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{r}  such that if  M M  is a hypermatrix and  ( A 1 , … , A r ) ∈ G (A_{1},\ldots,A_{r})\in G  then 
 

 
 
 Det ( ( A 1 , … , A r ) ∘ M ) = det ( A 1 ) n 1 ⋯ det ( A r ) n r ⋅ Det ( M ) . \operatorname{Det}\big((A_{1},\ldots,A_{r})\circ M\big)=\det(A_{1})^{n_{1}}\cdots\det(A_{r})^{n_{r}}\cdot\operatorname{Det}(M). 
 
 
 
 
 
 The previous proposition leads to the following corollaries; part (d) in particular will be helpful when we classify hypermatrices with certain entries forced to be zero. 
 
 
 Corollary 2.4   ( [ 7 , Cor. 1.5] ) . 
 
 
 (a)  
 
 Interchanging two parallel slices leaves the hyperdeterminant invariant up to sign. 
 
 (b)  
 
 The hyperdeterminant is a homogeneous polynomial in the entries of each slice. The degree of homogeneity is the same for parallel slices. 
 
 (c)  
 
 The hyperdeterminant does not change if we add to some slice a scalar multiple of a parallel slice. 
 
 (d)  
 
 The hyperdeterminant of a hypermatrix with two parallel slices proportional to each other is zero. In particular, the hyperdeterminant of a hypermatrix with a zero slice is zero. 
 
 
 
 
 
 For the rest of the paper we will only consider boundary format hypermatrices of three dimensions, that is, hypermatrices of format  ( k 1 + 1 ) × ( k 1 + k 2 + 1 ) × ( k 2 + 1 ) (k_{1}+1)\times(k_{1}+k_{2}+1)\times(k_{2}+1)  for positive integers  k 1 k_{1}  and  k 2 k_{2} . We restrict our attention to these hypermatrices in order to make use of the next lemma, which, in conjunction with Corollary  2.4 (d), will allow us to classify which hypermatrices with restricted entries are nondegenerate. 
 
 
 Lemma 2.5   ( [ 1 , Lemma 2.3] ) . 
 
 Let  M M  be a hypermatrix of boundary format  ( k 1 + 1 ) × ( k 1 + k 2 + 1 ) × ( k 2 + 1 ) (k_{1}+1)\times(k_{1}+k_{2}+1)\times(k_{2}+1)  over a field  𝔽 \mathbb{F}  and let  M 1 , M 2 , … , M k 1 + 1 M_{1},M_{2},\ldots,M_{k_{1}+1}  be the slices in the first direction.  Proof.

If the hyperrook placement  ( m σ , m π − 1 ) ∘ E (m_{\sigma},m_{\pi^{-1}})\circ E  does not respect  P P , then by Proposition  4.20  there are no  ( σ , π ) (\sigma,\pi) -augmented hypermatrices that respect  P P .  5.  Further Research 

 
 We end with some open questions and some ideas for future research. 
 
 
 5.1.  Proving the main conjecture 

 
 The main problem raised by our work is to prove Conjecture  3.1  in full generality. One approach would be to build on our proof of Theorem  4.1 , which establishes that the number of nondegenerate hypermatrices over  𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q}  that respect the plane partition  P P  is 
 

 
 
 q k 2 ( q − 1 ) 2 ​ k ∑ ( σ , π ) ∈ 𝔖 k + 1 × 𝔖 k : ( m σ , m π − 1 ) ∘ E ​  respects  ​ P q | Inv ⁡ ( σ ) | + | Inv ⁡ ( π ) | − h ⁡ ( σ , π , P ) q^{k^{2}}(q-1)^{2k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\sigma,\pi)\in\mathfrak{S}_{k+1}\times\mathfrak{S}_{k}:\\
(m_{\sigma},m_{\pi^{-1}})\circ E\text{ respects }P\end{subarray}}q^{|\operatorname{Inv}(\sigma)|+|\operatorname{Inv}(\pi)|-h(\sigma,\pi;P)} 
 
 
 where  h ⁡ ( σ , π , P ) h(\sigma,\pi;P)  is defined to be the number of entries in a generic  ( σ , π ) (\sigma,\pi) -augmented hypermatrix that are not identically zero and lie inside  P P ; these are characterized by Propositions  4.24  and  4.25 . Thus, the main conjecture could be proved with “only combinatorics”, if one could understand the quantity  h ⁡ ( σ , π , P ) h(\sigma,\pi;P)  well enough to find a way to group terms correctly in order to give the desired factored form. Our attempts to carry this out have thus far been unsuccessful. 
 
 
 
 5.2.  Other formats 

 
 To what extent do our results extend to other formats? Our work relies in an essential way on Lemma  2.5  and Theorem  3.3  of Aitken, which hold respectively only for three-dimensional boundary formats and the specific format  2 × ( k + 1 ) × k 2\times(k+1)\times k . Is there an analogous version of Lemma  2.5  for higher-dimensional hypermatrices? If so, that might allow one to extend Theorem  2.18  (which identifies the unique maximal shape that allows nondegenerate hypermatrices for all three-dimensional boundary formats) to higher dimensions. A simple dimension count shows that we should  not  expect Theorem  3.3  to extend to any larger formats; 2 2 
               2 
              
              
              
            For example, the space of  3 × 3 × 5 3\times 3\times 5  hypermatrices is  45 45 -dimensional, whereas the group  GL 3 × GL 3 × GL 5 \operatorname{GL}_{3}\times\operatorname{GL}_{3}\times\operatorname{GL}_{5}  is only  9 + 9 + 25 = 43 9+9+25=43 -dimensional; the space of nondegenerate hypermatrices is presumably full-dimensional, so we should expect a two-parameter family of orbits of nondegenerate hypermatrices of this format under the larger group.  could it nonetheless still be the case that the nondegenerate hypermatrices respecting a plane partition enumerate nicely? It seems challenging to test this question computationally. 
 
 
 
 5.3.  Intrinsic definition of hyperrook placements 

 
 Full-rank rook placements on a board can be described as arrangements having one rook in each row and column; they can also be described as the result of multiplying the identity matrix by a permutation matrix. Our Definition  4.2  of hyperrook placements is analogous to the second definition. Do the same objects have a natural description along the lines of the first definition? 
 
 
 It follows immediately from Definition  4.2  that every hyperrook placement has a single  1 1  in each column of each face and at most  1 1  in each row of each face. One is tempted to guess that the hyperrook placements are simply pairs  ( S 1 , S 2 ) (S_{1},S_{2})  where  S 1 , S 2 S_{1},S_{2}  are placements of  k k  rooks on the rectangle  ℛ ( k + 1 ) × k \mathcal{R}_{(k+1)\times k}  with empty intersection and not sharing the same empty row. However, it’s easy to see that the number of such pairs is strictly larger than the number of hyperrook placements when  k > 2 k>2 . 
 
 
 There are various definitions of three-dimensional rook placement in the literature that do  not  agree with ours (e.g., Latin squares, or the notion in  [ 21 ,  2 ,  17 ] ); typically, these are on cubical boards. 
 
 
 
 5.4.  Lower rank 

 
 In the case of square matrices, the nondegeneracy condition (that the determinant is nonzero) can be equivalently characterized as a rank condition (that the matrix has full rank), and the notion of a rook placements,  q q -rook numbers, etc., extends to lower ranks in a natural way, placing fewer than the maximum number of rooks and thinking of rook placements as partial permutations. One might ask whether there is similarly a lower-rank version of our theory. There are numerous inequivalent definitions of the rank of a hypermatrix (see, e.g.,  [ 10 ]  for a clear description of four such notions). Although these have sometimes been discussed in the context of the hyperdeterminant (e.g.,  [ 16 ,  4 ] ), as far as we know, none of them has a tight connection to our notion of nondegeneracy. Moreover, basic questions about tensor rank (computing the rank of a hypermatrix, determining the orbits under the  GL ( 𝔽 ) × ⋯ × GL ( 𝔽 ) \operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{F})\times\cdots\times\operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{F})  action, or counting hypermatrices by rank over a finite field) are all difficult, see, e.g.,  [ 13 ,  14 ,  3 ,  20 ,  18 ] . It is unclear whether a nice lower-rank theory can be based on any of these ideas. 
 
 
 Alternatively, one might hope to construct a lower-rank hyperrook theory along the following lines (mentioned briefly in the introduction): for a partition-shaped board  B B  in the  n × n n\times n  square, its rook numbers  r i ​ ( B ) r_{i}(B)  (the number of ways of placing  i i  rooks on  B B ) and hit numbers  h i ​ ( B ) h_{i}(B)  (the number of ways of placing  n n  rooks on the square such that  i i  of them lie in  B B ) are related by 
 

 
 (5) 
 
 ∑ i r i ​ ( B ) ⋅ ( x − 1 ) i ⋅ ( n − i ) ! = ∑ i h i ​ ( B ) ⋅ x i \sum_{i}r_{i}(B)\cdot(x-1)^{i}\cdot(n-i)!=\sum_{i}h_{i}(B)\cdot x^{i} 
 
 
 (and a  q q -analogue of this identity holds for  q q -rook and  q q -hit numbers). The definition of hit number extends immediately to our setting, by considering hyperrook placements on the  2 × ( k + 1 ) × k 2\times(k+1)\times k  box. If we write down the generating function for these numbers, is there a natural change of basis (analogous to ( 5 )) so that the coefficients can be interpreted as lower-rank rook numbers?
