                                                                    SQUARE-FREE REDUCTS OF WORDS

                                                                  JAROSLAW GRYTCZUK AND SZYMON STANKIEWICZ

                                                   Abstract. A square is a ﬁnite non-empty word consisting of two identical adjacent blocks.




arXiv:2011.12822v1 [math.CO] 25 Nov 2020
                                                   A word is square-free if it does not contain a square as a factor. In any ﬁnite word one may
                                                   delete the repeated block of a square, obtaining thereby a shorter word. By repeating this
                                                   process, a square-free word is eventually reached, which we call a reduct of the original word.
                                                     How many diﬀerent reducts a single word may have? It is not hard to prove that any
                                                   binary word has exactly one reduct. We prove that there exist ternary words with arbitrarily
                                                   many reducts. Moreover, the function counting the maximum number of reducts a ternary
                                                   word of length n may have grows exponentially. We also prove that over four letters, there
                                                   exist words with any given number of reducts, which does not seem to be the case for ternary
                                                   words. Finally, we demonstrate that the set of all ﬁnite ternary words splits into ﬁnitely
                                                   many classes of related words (one may get from one word to the other by a sequence of
                                                   square reductions and factor duplications). A few open questions are posed concerning some
                                                   structures on words deﬁned with the use of the square reduction.




                                                                                       1. Introduction

                                             A square is a finite non-empty word built of two identical adjacent blocks. For instance, the
                                           word hotshots is a square. A word W contains a square if it can be written as W “ UXXV
                                           for some words U, V , and a non-empty word X. A word is square-free if it does not contain any
                                           squares. For instance, the word repetition contains the square titi, while reincarnation
                                           is square-free.
                                             It is easy to check that any binary square-free word has length at most 3. However, there
                                           exist ternary square-free words of any length, as proved by Thue in [14] (see [4]). This
                                           result inspired a lot of further research, leading to the birth of Combinatorics on Words, a
                                           wide discipline with lots of exciting problems, deep results, and important applications (see
                                           [1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 10, 11]).
                                             In this paper we consider the following problem concerning squares in words. Let W be
                                           any finite word with a square, so W “ UXXV . We may delete one of the two blocks of a
                                           square, which gives a shorter word W 1 “ UXV . If W 1 contains a square, say W 1 “ U 1 Y Y V 1 ,
                                           then we may repeat this operation of square reduction to get another word W 2 “ U 1 Y V 1 .
                                           And so on, until we finally get a square-free word. Since a word may have many squares

situated in different positions, this process depends on our choice of a square to be reduced
in every single step. Any final word that can be obtained from a word W by a sequence of
square reductions is called a square-free reduct of W , or just a reduct, for short.
    How many reducts a single word may have? It is not hard to demonstrate that every binary
word has exactly one reduct (Proposition 1). However, for ternary words the situation changes
dramatically. We prove (Theorem 1) that for every positive integer k there exists a ternary
word with at least k distinct reducts. Furthermore, there exist ternary words of length n with
exponentially many reducts (Theorem 2). Also, for every integer k ě 1 there exists a word
over a 4-letter alphabet with exactly k distinct reducts (Theorem 3). Finally, we consider a
naturally defined graph on the set of ternary words, in which two words are adjacent if one
can be obtained from the other by a single square reduction. We prove (Theorem 5) that this
graph has finitely many connected components. In other words, we may define two words as
related if one can be obtained from the other by a finite sequence of square reductions and
factor duplications (the reverse operation to the square reduction). Then Theorem 5 asserts
that all ternary words form a finite number of families of mutually related words.
    All these results are proved in the next section. The last section of the paoer is devoted
to some open problems.
    The idea of square reduction in words is not new. Indeed, the reverse operation is in-
tensively studied in Genetics, where it is called the tandem duplication, as the fundamental
mechanism in the evolution of organisms (see [12],[15]). Naturally, some computational or
language theoretic aspects are also widely studied (see [2], [8]).

Proposition 1. Every binary word W satisfies rpW q “ 1.

Proof. There exist only six square-free binary words, namely

                                       0, 1, 01, 10, 010, 101.

different pairs consisting of the initial and the final letter.                                

2.3. Kinship of words. For a fixed integer k ě 1, let us consider the directed graph G k
defined on the set of all finite non-empty words over an alphabet of size k, with U joined
to W by an oriented edge pU, W q whenever W can be obtained from U in a single square

reduction. Let Gk denote the underlying undirected graph obtained from G k by ignoring
orientations of the edges.
  One may ask many natural questions about these graphs. Perhaps the most fundamental
concerns connectedness of Gk . By Proposition 1, it is easy to see that G2 has exactly six
different components. Indeed, there are exactly six square-free words over a binary alphabet

and every binary word reduces to exactly one of them. As there are infinitely many square-
free words over a 3-letter alphabet, one may expect that the number of components in G3 is
also infinite. However, we shall demonstrate that this is not the case.

Theorem 5. The graph G3 has finitely many connected components.

Proof. First we observe that every square-free word over alphabet ta, b, cu of length exactly
9 contains as a factor one of the following five words (up to a permutation of the alphabet):

(2.4)      X1 “ abcabac, X2 “ abcacba, X3 “ abcbabc, X4 “ abcbacab, X5 “ abcbacb.

These words are reducts of the following five words, respectively:
    S1 “ abcbabcbcacbcacabacabcbacabcabacacbcabacac
    S2 “ abcbabcbcacbcabacbcabcbacbcabcacbcbabcba
    S3 “ abcbabcbcacbcacabacabcbabcbc
    S4 “ abcbabcbcacbcacabacabcbacabcabacacbcacbacab
    S5 “ abcbabcbcacbcabacbcabcbacbcabcacbabcacbcb.
    Now, it can be checked that each word Si has another reduct Yi , which is strictly shorter
than the corresponding word Xi :

(2.5)           Y1 “ abcbac, Y2 “ abcba, Y3 “ abc, Y4 “ abcab, Y5 “ abcacb.

It follows that for every ternary word W there is a path in the graph G3 to some square-free
word of length at most 8. Indeed, suppose that W has no reducts of length at most 8. If
R “ AXi B is any reduct of W , then we may go up to the word R1 “ ASi B, and then go
down to the shorter word R2 “ AYi B. After a finite number of such operations we must
reach a word of length smaller than 9.
    We see that, in consequence, the number of connected components in the graph G3 does
not exceed the number of square-free ternary words of length at most 8, which completes the
proof.                                                                                      

                                          3. Discussion

    Let us conclude the paper with some observations and open problems. First, let us point on
a strange phenomenon concerning possible numbers of reducts of ternary words. We checked
by a computer that for every m ď 79, there exist ternary words W with |RpW q| “ m.
However, it seems that there is no ternary word having exactly 80 reducts (see Table 1).

Problem 1. Is there a ternary word W with |RpW q| “ 80?

Conjecture 1. There exist infinitely many positive integers m such that no ternary word
have exactly m distinct reducts.
