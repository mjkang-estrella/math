A BSTRACT
For the two-parameter Mittag-Leffler function Eα,β with α > 0 and β ≥ 0, we consider the question
whether |Eα,β (z)| and Eα,β (ℜz) are comparable on the whole complex plane. We show that the
inequality |Eα,β (z)| ≤ Eα,β (ℜz) holds globally if and only if Eα,β (−x) is completely monotone
on (0, ∞). For α ∈ [1, 2) we prove that the complete monotonicity of 1/Eα,β (x) on (0, ∞) is
necessary for the global inequality |Eα,β (z)| ≥ Eα,β (ℜz), and also sufficient for α = 1. For
α ≥ 2 we show that the absence of non-real zeros for Eα,β is sufficient for the global inequality
|Eα,β (z)| ≥ Eα,β (ℜz), and also necessary for α = 2. All these results have an explicit description
in terms of the values of the parameters α, β. Along the way, several inequalities for Eα,β on the
half-plane {ℜz ≥ 0} are established, and a characterization of its log-convexity and log-concavity on
the positive half-line is obtained.

Mittag-Leffler (ML) functions have a long history deeply intertwined with the development of complex analysis and
special functions. One of the most notable recent aspects of ML functions is their pivotal role in fractional calculus.
These functions are indeed eigenfunctions of fractional derivatives, and hence they provide analytical tools for studying
fractional differential equations – see [5, 17, 19] for more on this topic. They also serve as the basis for devising
numerical methods with improved stability properties, see [9, 26]. We refer to Chapter 18 in the classical monograph [7]
and to the more recent textbook [12] for an overview of properties and results concerning ML functions.

For α = β = 1 the ML function reduces to the exponential function, namely
E1,1 (z) = ez ,
and for this reason ML functions are sometimes interpreted – see e.g. Chapters 1 and 2 in [12] – as a parametric
generalization of the exponential function. Despite this relationship, ML functions do not possess many of the
nice properties that characterize the exponential function. For example, the semi-group property Eα,β (z + s) =
Eα,β (z)Eα,β (s) is valid only in the exponential case α = β = 1 or for the values z = s = 0 when β ∈ {0, 1, 2}.
Instead, one usually obtains some inequalities, and for β = 1, it follows from Proposition 2.3 in [11] that Eα,1 (z + s) ≥
Eα,1 (z)Eα,1 (s) for all α ≤ 1 and z, s ∈ R while Eα,1 (z + s) ≤ Eα,1 (z)Eα,1 (s) for all α ≥ 1 and z, s ∈ R+ . A
complete characterization of these sub-additivity resp. super-additivity properties for all α > 0, β ≥ 0 will also be
given in Remark 24 (b) below.
In this paper, we are mainly interested in the following important property of the exponential function:
|ez | = eℜz ,

(1.2)

where ℜz denotes the real part of a complex number z. The property (1.2) has, for example, useful applications in
studying the behavior of solutions of ordinary differential equations, as well as in analyzing the sensitivity of exponential
matrices eA with respect to perturbations of the matrix argument – see [30]. Investigating some analogue of (1.2) for
ML functions is of interest, since ML functions essentially replace the exponential function when moving from integer
order to fractional order calculus. In particular, exploring possible extensions of (1.2) improves knowledge of the
sensitivity of matrix ML functions Eα,β (A) with respect to perturbations of the matrix argument A.
As expected, the equality Eα,β (z) = Eα,β (ℜz) cannot hold globally except for α = β = 1, as a consequence
of our corollary 13. In this paper, we wish to identify regions in the (α, β) parameter space where the inequalities
corresponding to (1.2) hold globally, that is, for any z ∈ C. We characterize the global inequality
Eα,β (z) ≤ Eα,β (ℜz)

(1.3)

by 0 < α ≤ 1 and β ≥ α, a region which corresponds to the complete monotonicity of the function Eα,β (−x) on the
positive half-line. The characterization of the reverse global inequality
Eα,β (z) ≥ Eα,β (ℜz)

(1.4)

turns out however to be more subtle. We obtain characterizations on the lines {α = 1} with β ≤ 1, and {α = 2} with
β ≤ 3, and in the remaining regions α ∈ (1, 2), resp. α > 2, we obtain a necessary, resp. a sufficient, condition. In
the case α ∈ (1, 2) we prove that the global inequality (1.4) holds only for β ∈ [α − 1, α] and that, in this region, the
reciprocal function 1/Eα,β (x) is completely monotonic on (0, ∞). The latter result, which is proved by a specific
probabilistic method and has an independent interest, also ensures the validity of (1.4) on the right half-plane {ℜz ≥ 0}
for the rhomboid region {α ∈ [1, 2], β ∈ [α − 1, α]}. We conjecture that (1.4) can be extended on the whole complex
plane, but contrary to the case α ≤ 1 this extension cannot be obtained directly from the complete monotonicity, since
Eα,β has then negative zeros.
In the case α > 2, it is known that Eα,β has an infinite number of negative zeros and we can show by the Hadamard
factorization that (1.4) holds globally as soon as there are only negative zeros, which happens for 0 < β ≤ 2α − 1 or
α ≥ 4 and β ≤ 2α. However, the possibility of the existence of non-real zeros makes the global inequality (1.4) less
plausible, and we identify a convex unbounded region in the (α, β) parameter space where (1.4) fails globally. Contrary
to the case α ∈ (1, 2), we also conjecture that for α > 2 the absence of non-real zeros is actually equivalent to the
global inequality (1.4). All in all, it seems to us that a better understanding of the location of the negative zeros of Eα,β ,
a task which is known to be difficult, however, is necessary to characterize (1.4) for α ̸∈ {1, 2}.

Discussion and open problems

In this last section we summarize our results and formulate some open questions. In Corollary 12 we have characterized
the inequality (1.3) on the whole C. Combined with the main result of [28], this shows that
The inequality (1.3) holds globally

⇐⇒

0 < α ≤ 1 and β ≥ α

⇐⇒

Eα,β (−x) is CM on (0, ∞).

We have observed in Proposition 11 that the global inequality (1.4) is relevant only for α ≥ 1, and Corollary 5 implies
the following characterization in the case α = 1 :
The inequality (1.4) holds globally ⇐⇒ β ≤ 1 ⇐⇒ 1/E1,β (x) is CM on (0, ∞).
In the case α ∈ (1, 2), it is rather natural in view of the previous equivalences, Proposition 3 and Proposition 14, to
state the following.
