The bunkbed conjecture, which has featured in the folklore of probability theory since at least 1985, concerns bond percolation on the product graph G ​ □ ​ K 2 G\Box K_{2} . We have two copies G 0 G_{0} and G 1 G_{1} of G G , and if x ( 0 ) x^{(0)} and x ( 1 ) x^{(1)} are the copies of a vertex x ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) x\in V(G) in G 0 G_{0} and G 1 G_{1} respectively, then edge x ( 0 ) ​ x ( 1 ) x^{(0)}x^{(1)} is present. The conjecture states that, for vertices u , v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) u,v\in V(G) , percolation from u ( 0 ) u^{(0)} to v ( 0 ) v^{(0)} is at least as likely as percolation from u ( 0 ) u^{(0)} to v ( 1 ) v^{(1)} . While the conjecture is widely expected to be true, having attracted significant attention, a general proof has not been forthcoming. In this paper we consider three natural generalisations of the bunkbed conjecture; to site percolation, to hypergraphs, and to directed graphs. Our main aim is to show that all these generalisations are false, and to this end we construct a sequence of counterexamples to these statements. However, we also consider under what extra conditions these generalisations might hold, and give some classes of graph for which the bunkbed conjecture for site percolation does hold. 1. Introduction In 1985, Kasteleyn posed the bunkbed conjecture, which then circulated as an intriguing problem in percolation theory (see [ 7 , Remark 5] ). Interest in the problem has waxed and waned over time, but, especially in recent years, the conjecture has attracted significant attention. A feature of the problem which makes it particularly appealing is its apparent simplicity and how, as has been noted by several authors, it appears to be intuitively clear. We now provide some definitions and state our main results before returning to the history of the problem. While most work on the topic is concerned with simple graphs, we work in a more general setting, and thus define the bunkbed graph in several cases, as follows. Definition 1.1 (Bunkbed graph) . For Q Q a graph, digraph, or hypergraph, we can define the bunkbed structure Q ~ \tilde{Q} as follows. Take two copies, Q 0 Q_{0} and Q 1 Q_{1} , of Q Q , labelling the copy of vertex v v and edge e e in Q i Q_{i} as v ( i ) v^{(i)} and e ( i ) e^{(i)} respectively. We will call such edges e ( i ) e^{(i)} horizontal edges . E ⁡ ( Q ~ ) E(\tilde{Q}) then consists of E ⁡ ( Q 0 ) ∪ E ⁡ ( Q 1 ) E(Q_{0})\mathbin{\cup}E(Q_{1}) along with an edge between v ( 0 ) v^{(0)} and v ( 1 ) v^{(1)} for each v ∈ V ⁡ ( Q ) v\in V(Q) , which will be called a vertical edge . In the case of directed graphs, this vertical edge will be bidirectional. Q 0 Q_{0} will be called the lower bunk and Q 1 Q_{1} the upper bunk of Q ~ \tilde{Q} . We will be concerned in this paper with percolation on the bunkbed graph under various uniform models of percolation. We will consider two types of model: site percolation and bond percolation. In both cases, we will ultimately take a family ℰ ⁡ ( Q ~ ) \mathcal{E}(\tilde{Q}) of subsets of E ⁡ ( Q ~ ) E(\tilde{Q}) , which we will sample from uniformly at random to produce some set X ⊆ E ⁡ ( Q ~ ) X\subseteq E(\tilde{Q}) of edges which will be retained. We now define these concepts formally. Definition 1.2 (Percolation model) . If Q Q is a graph, digraph, or hypergraph, then a percolation model is a family ℰ ⁡ ( Q ~ ) \mathcal{E}(\tilde{Q}) is a family of subsets of E ⁡ ( Q ~ ) E(\tilde{Q}) . We perform percolation by selecting an element X ∈ ℰ ⁡ ( Q ~ ) X\in\mathcal{E}(\tilde{Q}) uniformly at random, and then retain precisely those edges in X X . For two vertices x , y ∈ V ⁡ ( Q ~ ) x,y\in V(\tilde{Q}) , we write ℙ ⁡ ( x → ℰ ⁡ ( Q ~ ) y ) or ℙ ⁡ ( x ↔ ℰ ⁡ ( Q ~ ) y ) \displaystyle\Prob(x\xrightarrow{\mathcal{E}(\tilde{Q})}y)\qquad\text{ or }\qquad\Prob(x\xleftrightarrow{\mathcal{E}(\tilde{Q})}y) for the probability that there is a directed path of retained edges from x x to y y in the former, or a bidirectional path in the latter case, in Q ~ \tilde{Q} under model ℰ ⁡ ( Q ~ ) \mathcal{E}(\tilde{Q}) . When Q Q is a hypergraph, a path in Q Q is a sequence of vertices in which consecutive vertices are both members of some hyperedge in Q Q . We can then use this definition to define site percolation as a special case of bond percolation. We will only consider site percolation on graphs, so for simplicity we only define this case. Definition 1.3 (Site percolation model) . If G G is a graph, then a percolation model ℰ ⁡ ( G ~ ) \mathcal{E}(\tilde{G}) is said to be a site percolation model if, for every X ∈ ℰ ⁡ ( G ~ ) X\in\mathcal{E}(\tilde{G}) there is some set U ⊆ V ⁡ ( G ~ ) U\subseteq V(\tilde{G}) , which we shall refer to as the set of open vertices , such that X X consists of precisely those edges with both ends in U U . We consider site percolation as a special case of our general (bond) percolation model for convenience; it will be useful for us to still have all vertices present, even if they are in no edges. We remark that in much other work on the topic, percolation is phrased in terms of sampling edges independently at random with some probability p p . Our sampling as above is equivalent to this in the case that ℰ ⁡ ( Q ~ ) \mathcal{E}(\tilde{Q}) is the powerset 𝒫 ⁡ ( E ⁡ ( Q ~ ) ) \mathcal{P}(E(\tilde{Q})) , and p = 1 / 2 p=1/2 . This restriction to p = 1 / 2 p=1/2 turns out to not be important, as will be discussed later. We now provide a very general statement of the bunkbed conjecture in terms of a general model, before we proceed to consider several special cases. Definition 1.4 (Bunkbed conjecture for a general model ℰ \mathcal{E} ) . Let Q Q be a graph, hypergraph, or digraph, ℰ ⁡ ( Q ~ ) \mathcal{E}(\tilde{Q}) be a model of percolation on Q ~ \tilde{Q} as defined above. The bunkbed conjecture for model ℰ \mathcal{E} states that for any Q Q , and any vertices u , v ∈ V ⁡ ( Q ) u,v\in V(Q) , we have ℙ ⁡ ( u ( 0 ) → ℰ ⁡ ( Q ~ ) v ( 0 ) ) ≥ ℙ ⁡ ( u ( 0 ) → ℰ ⁡ ( Q ~ ) v ( 1 ) ) . \Prob(u^{(0)}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{E}(\tilde{Q})}v^{(0)})\geq\Prob(u^{(0)}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{E}(\tilde{Q})}v^{(1)}). Let BBC ​ ( ℰ , Q ) \text{BBC}(\mathcal{E},Q) denote the above statement. There are several different statements which are referred to as ‘the bunkbed conjecture’, and they are used somewhat interchangeably in the literature; Rudzinski and Smyth [ 12 ] detail a few of these statements. One such form of the conjecture, which fits the notation we have described above, concerns the following model of percolation. Model 1.5 ( E 0 T E_{0}^{T} , the standard bunkbed model) . For an undirected graph G G and a fixed set T ⊆ V ⁡ ( G ) T\subseteq V(G) , a set X ⊆ E ⁡ ( G ~ ) X\subseteq E(\tilde{G}) of horizontal edges is chosen uniformly at random, and retained along with precisely those vertical edges v ( 0 ) ​ v ( 1 ) v^{(0)}v^{(1)} such that v ∈ T v\in T . Equivalently, each horizontal edge of G ~ \tilde{G} is retained independently at random with probability 0.5 0.5 . In this setting, BBC ​ ( E 0 T , G ) \text{BBC}(E_{0}^{T},G) is equivalent to many statements referred to as ‘the bunkbed conjecture’. When stated, the conjecture is often given in a slightly more general form, retaining each horizontal edge of G ~ \tilde{G} with probability p p for some fixed p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) p\in(0,1) , as in for example the work of Häggström [ 3 , 4 ] . However, as proved by Rudzinski and Smyth [ 12 , Corollary 1.7] , the case for any specific probability p p implies the general case, and so the truth of BBC ​ ( E 0 T , G ) \text{BBC}(E_{0}^{T},G) would suffice to prove the more general conjecture. The conjecture BBC ​ ( ℰ , G ) \text{BBC}(\mathcal{E},G) is false for general percolation models ℰ \mathcal{E} ; this is unsurprising as our definition has no requirement of symmetry, and so permits many pathological models. However, we will show that BBC ​ ( ℰ , G ) \text{BBC}(\mathcal{E},G) is also false for several highly symmetric models ℰ \mathcal{E} , some of which have been conjectured to be true by various authors. This is particularly surprising as much of the same intuition which tells us that BBC ​ ( E 0 T , G ) \text{BBC}(E_{0}^{T},G) should be true also applies to these other – false – cases. The conjecture has attracted significant attention over the last few years, being resolved in progressively more general special cases. One initial sequence of results by de Buyer and then van Hintum and Lammers [ 1 , 2 , 13 ] resolved the case of the complete graph. This was then further generalised by Richthammer [ 11 ] to a broader class of highly symmetric graphs. Linusson [ 9 , 10 ] took a different route, and both considered generalisations of the conjecture and related the conjecture to a problem concerning randomly directed graphs. This work was then built upon by Hutchcroft, Nizić-Nikolac, and Kent [ 6 ] , who resolved the conjecture in the case of general G G and probability p ↑ 1 p\uparrow 1 . A different angle on the p ↑ 1 p\uparrow 1 limit was later considered by the author [ 5 ] , proving the conjecture holds in this case by working with graph cuts. We now turn our attention to more general models; it is natural in an attempt to prove the bunkbed conjecture to try to generalise the statement of the problem, in the hope that this may be conducive to an inductive proof. Indeed, upon attacking the conjecture BBC ​ ( E 0 , G ) \text{BBC}(E_{0},G) , a generalisation to hypergraphs soon arises as a tempting possibility. Likewise, directed graphs allow for the possibility of attaching ‘gadgets’ to a graph to easily generalise the conjecture further, and on generalising to site percolation, one no longer needs to consider paths which return to an already-visited pair of vertices in the bunkbed graph. However, our main result is the following, which says that all of these generalisations are in fact false. Theorem 1.6 . The generalised bunkbed conjecture is false for site percolation, hypergraphs, and digraphs. More precisely, there are counterexamples to the statement BBC ​ ( ℰ , Q ) \text{BBC}(\mathcal{E},Q) for each of the models E 1 , E 2 T , E 4 T , E 5 , E 6 T , F , E 7 E_{1},E_{2}^{T},E_{4}^{T},E_{5},E_{6}^{T,F},E_{7} to be discussed in Sections 3 and 4 . In view of this general negative result, it is still interesting to consider for which graphs these variations of the bunkbed conjecture might be true. To this end, we will also prove the following positive result in the case of site percolation and some highly structured graphs G G . We recall that a wheel graph consists of a cycle C C plus an additional vertex connected by an edge to every vertex of C C . Theorem 1.7 . Let G G be a path, a cycle, or a wheel, and let u , v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) u,v\in V(G) be vertices. Let S ⊆ V ⁡ ( G ~ ) S\subseteq V(\tilde{G}) be selected uniformly at random amongst all such subsets. It is at least as likely that there is a path entirely in S S from u ( 0 ) u^{(0)} to v ( 0 ) v^{(0)} as it is that there is a path in S S from u ( 0 ) u^{(0)} to v ( 1 ) v^{(1)} . Site percolation The goal of this section is to show that the bunkbed conjecture is false for site percolation. We consider the case of site percolation before moving on to the other structures (hypergraphs and digraphs) for two purposes: for ease of notation, as we are in this section concerned only with ordinary graphs, and to introduce our counterexamples in a more structured manner, as the results of Section 4 are built on top of the results of this section. In the framework introduced in Sections 1 and 2 , the site percolation we consider comes in two flavours. Model 3.1 ( E 1 E_{1} ; unconditioned site percolation) . For an undirected graph G G , let the set of open vertices W ⊆ V ⁡ ( G ~ ) W\subseteq V(\tilde{G}) be chosen uniformly at random among all subsets of vertices. We then retain precisely those edges with both endpoints in W W . In order to construct a counterexample to the bunkbed conjecture for site percolation, we first consider a conditioned form of site percolation, in which we fix which vertices are to be posts. Conclusion We have shown that the bunkbed conjecture does not hold when generalised to site percolation or hypergraphs. The situation with directed graphs is a little more complex, as we have only dealt with the case of multigraphs with vertical edges which are bidirectional. In the interest of considering fully directed graphs, we define the following model: Model 6.1 ( E 8 E_{8} ; fully directed graphs) . For a simple digraph D D , construct the bunkbed graph D ~ \tilde{D} by adding both directed edges v ( 0 ) ​ v ( 1 ) v^{(0)}v^{(1)} and v ( 1 ) ​ v ( 0 ) v^{(1)}v^{(0)} for all v ∈ V ⁡ ( D ) v\in V(D) . A subset of edges F ⊆ E ⁡ ( D ~ ) F\subseteq E(\tilde{D}) is selected uniformly at random, and precisely the edges in F F are retained. We conjecture that it is not necessary that our example in Section 5.2 has multiple edges and undirected vertical edges. Conjecture 6.2 . There is a simple digraph D D for which BBC ​ ( E 8 , D ) \text{BBC}(E_{8},D) is false. We furthermore repeat the following question of Leander [ 8 ] in our context. Question 6.3 . Do BBC ​ ( E 6 T , F , D ) \text{BBC}(E_{6}^{T,F},D) and BBC ​ ( E 7 , D ) \text{BBC}(E_{7},D) hold for all acyclic digraphs D D ? It seems unclear to the author whether the answer to Question 6.3 should be positive or negative. Indeed, this problem is far more amenable to inductive techniques than the case of graphs with cycles, but actually performing this induction seems far from trivial. However, one case that does seem approachable, though was found by the author to be more difficult than expected, is expressed by the following conjecture. Conjecture 6.4 . BBC ​ ( E 7 , T n ) \text{BBC}(E_{7},T_{n}) holds for T n T_{n} . the transitive tournament on n n vertices. We found some positive results in Section 3.3 concerning the case of site percolation for some very simple classes of graphs. It would be interesting to consider more complex graphs, with a view towards ascertaining whether or not the bunkbed conjecture failing for site percolation is in general the exception or the rule. Indeed, one may ask the following (perhaps optimistic) question. Question 6.5 . What is the probability that the assertion in the bunkbed conjecture for site percolation BBC ​ ( E 1 , G ) \text{BBC}(E_{1},G) holds for G ∼ G ⁡ ( n , p ) G\sim G(n,p) the Erdős-Rényi random graph for n n large? To what extent does this probability depend on the behaviour of p p as a function of n n ? There are of course many other classes of graph one could consider; graphs of diameter 2 2 (or simply of radius 1), and highly symmetric graphs (e.g. those with transitive automorphism group) would seem to be natural candidates for investigation. Returning now to the original bunkbed conjecture BBC ​ ( E 0 , G ) \text{BBC}(E_{0},G) , while our results do not directly say anything about this problem, they do cast doubt on whether the conjecture is just as likely to be true as the literature often claims. Indeed, much of the same intuition which tells us that BBC ​ ( E 0 , G ) \text{BBC}(E_{0},G) should hold also goes through in the cases of the other models considered here, which – as we have shown – are false. Any proof of the bunkbed conjecture would thus need to make use of the fact that it is working with undirected, 2-uniform graphs. As discussed in the introduction, it has been shown [ 5 , 6 ] that the bunkbed conjecture holds for any fixed graph G G in the p ↑ 1 p\uparrow 1 limit. However, it is not hard to show that our methods can produce counterexamples in the setting of models E 1 E_{1} and E 5 E_{5} even when the probability p p tends to 1 1 . Thus these constructions cannot directly be transformed into counterexamples to the original bunkbed conjecture, and some new idea would be needed here. We close by stating the bunkbed conjecture in the form of the following question. Question 6.6 . Does BBC ​ ( E 0 T , G ) \text{BBC}(E_{0}^{T},G) hold for every graph G G and set T ⊆ V ⁡ ( G ) T\subseteq V(G) ? In light of the results presented here, the author is far from convinced that the answer to Question 6.6 is positive. 7.
