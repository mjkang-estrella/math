Abstract

The symmetric inclusion process (SIP) models particles diffusing on a graph with mutual attraction. We recently showed [KS24] that, in the log-concave regime (where diffusivity dominates interaction), the spectral gap of the conservative SIP matches that of a single particle. In this paper, our main result demonstrates that this identity generally fails outside this regime, but always holds for the non-conservative SIP, regardless of the interaction strength. When this one-particle spectral gap identity breaks down, we derive sharp bounds for the gap in terms of diffusivity, and reveal a two-particle spectral gap identity in the vanishing diffusivity limit. Our approach leverages the rigid eigenstructure of SIP, refined comparisons of Dirichlet forms for arbitrary diffusivity and particle numbers, and techniques from slow–fast system analysis. These findings extend to the dual interacting diffusion known as Brownian energy process, and shed some light on the spectral gap behavior for related Dirichlet-reversible systems on general, non-mean-field, geometries.

1 Introduction

The symmetric inclusion process (\(\textrm{SIP}\)) is a system of interacting particles hopping on the sites \(x,y,z, \ldots \in V\) of a finite graph G. In its conservative version, each particle jumps from a site x to another one y at rate \(c_{xy}\left( \alpha _y+\eta _y\right) \), where:

  • \(c_{xy}=c_{yx}\ge 0\) is a symmetric weight attached to the edge xy;

  • \(\alpha _y> 0\) is a weight attached to the arrival site y;

  • \(\eta _y\in \mathbb {N}_0\) denotes the number of particles sitting on y.

While the rate \(c_{xy}\,\alpha _y\) accounts for the free motion (or diffusion) of each particle, \(c_{xy}\,\eta _y\) introduces an interaction among them by favoring jumps on top of already occupied sites. Moreover, this dynamics is conservative (i.e., it preserves the total number of particles), reversible with respect to a discrete Dirichlet distribution, and irreducible as soon as the underlying graph is connected. We refer to Sect. 2 below for the detailed definition of the process and its properties.

For now, let us just remark that \(\textrm{SIP}\) arises within different contexts (e.g., as a discrete model of heat conduction [GKR07], and as a multi-type Moran model from population genetics with non-mean-field interactions), and comes with closely related models from statistical physics. For instance, by setting \(\alpha _y=1\) and replacing the plus sign with a minus sign in the rates, one obtains the renowned symmetric exclusion process [Spi70]. Furthermore, SIP admits a continuous-spin counterpart, referred to as the Brownian energy process [GKRV09] (see also Sect. 8.1 below). This continuous-spin model, roughly speaking, describes the energies of an instance of Kac’s walk [Kac56], in which velocities evolve as interacting diffusions, rather than being instantaneously updated at random “collision” times.

In all these models, a central question is that of determining precise convergence rates to equilibrium, and, in the context of reversible processes, one of the most investigated quantities for this purpose is the so-called spectral gap.

1.1 One-particle spectral gap identity

For \(\textrm{SIP}\) on a graph \(G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})\) endowed with site weights \(\alpha =(\alpha _x)_{x\in V}\),

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )>0 \end{aligned}$$

denotes the corresponding spectral gap, i.e., the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of (the negative of) the infinitesimal generator (see Sect. 2 for the precise definition). In a previous work [KS24], we established the following bounds on \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )\) in terms of \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha )\), the spectral gap of the random walk (\(\textrm{RW}\)) on G which jumps from x to y with rate \(c_{xy}\,\alpha _y\): for all graphs G and site weights \(\alpha \),

$$\begin{aligned} \left( 1\wedge \alpha _\textrm{min}\right) \textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha )\le \textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )\le \textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha )\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(1.1)

Here and all throughout, \(a\wedge b:=\min \{a,b\}\) and \(\alpha _\textrm{min}:=\min _{x\in V}\alpha _x\).

Notably, as soon as \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\ge 1\), the inequalities in (1.1) saturate to an identity:

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )=\textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha )\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \alpha _\textrm{min}\ge 1\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(1.2)

This one-particle reduction corresponds to a \(\textrm{SIP}\)-version of the celebrated Aldous’ spectral gap conjecture, originally formulated for interchange and symmetric exclusion processes in the early 90 s, and settled two decades later in [CLR10]. In fact, the identity in (1.2) is extremely powerful, as it reduces, for every underlying graph G, the spectral gap of \(\textrm{SIP}\) — an arbitrarily large system, as it may consist of arbitrarily many particles — to that of \(\textrm{RW}\), a much simpler Markov chain with finite state space V, for which several techniques to bound the spectral gap in terms of simple features of the underlying graph G are known (see, e.g., [SC97, MT06, LP17, Her23]). For completeness, let us emphasize that an identity like (1.2), while trivial for a system of independent particles, is not at all expected to hold for truly interacting systems. Indeed, apart from the processes treated in [CLR10], only a handful of other models were discovered to satisfy, on any geometry, a spectral gap identity involving the corresponding random walk. These models are:

  • the symmetric exclusion process in contact with reservoirs in [Sal23, Sal24];

  • the Binomial splitting process in [QS23, BC24].

The identity in (1.2) adds \(\textrm{SIP}\) to the short list above, provided that \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\ge 1\) (we shall also refer to this condition as “log-concave regime”, see Remark 2.1 below).

One of our main results (Theorem 2.4) states that, without this condition on \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\), this identity, in general, fails: for some graphs G and site weights \(\alpha \),

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )<\textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha ) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(1.3)

In other words, the factor \(1\wedge \alpha _\textrm{min}\) in (1.1) cannot be generally neglected when

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha _\textrm{min}\in (0,1)\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(1.4)

This naturally leads us to further investigate this regime, with the twofold goal of:

  1. (a)

    deriving an alternative lower bound for \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )\), better capturing its dependence on \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\);

  2. (b)

    obtaining a two-particle reduction of \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )\), in the asymptotic regime of vanishing diffusivity \(\alpha \rightarrow 0\).

We discuss these two steps in Sects. 1.2 and 1.3, respectively.

1.2 Sharp dependence on \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\)

In order to isolate the role of \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\), let us introduce new site weights \(\hat{\alpha }=\frac{\alpha }{\alpha _\textrm{min}}\). With this notation, a simple scaling argument implies

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha )=\alpha _\textrm{min}\,\textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\hat{\alpha })\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Due to the particle interaction, the same argument does not directly apply to \(\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )\). Instead, if combined with the inequalities in (1.1), the above identity yields

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha _\textrm{min}^2 \, \textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\hat{\alpha })\le \textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )\le \alpha _\textrm{min}\, \textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\hat{\alpha })\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \alpha _\textrm{min}\in (0,1)\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(1.5)

Hence, if we encode the dependence on \((G,\hat{\alpha })\) through \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\hat{\alpha })\), the lower bound in (1.5) above would predict \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )\) to depend on the square of \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\).

Our second main result (Theorem 2.2) proves this guess to be wrong, showing that, in general, \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )\) depends linearly, rather than quadratically as in (1.5), on \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\in (0,1)\): for all graphs G and site weights \(\alpha \),

$$\begin{aligned} C\,\alpha _\textrm{min}\le \textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha ) \; \text {,}\;\;\quad \alpha _\textrm{min}\in (0,1)\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(1.6)

where \(C> 0\) is a constant depending only on G and \(\hat{\alpha }=\frac{\alpha }{\alpha _\textrm{min}}\). Indeed, in Theorem 2.2, since \(\alpha _\textrm{min} \le 1\) and \(\hat{\alpha }_\textrm{ratio} = \alpha _\textrm{ratio}\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha _\textrm{min}\left\{ \frac{1}{54} \, \frac{ \hat{\alpha }_\textrm{ratio}}{ (6e)^{1/\hat{\alpha }_\textrm{ratio}} } \, \frac{ c_\textrm{min}}{|V|^2\, \textrm{diam}(G) }\right\} \le \textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha ) \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

where the constant inside the bracket in the left-hand side depends only on the graph structure and the constant \(\hat{\alpha }\). Because of the second inequality in (1.5), the order-one dependence on \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\) for \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )\) in (1.6) is sharp. As we will see, this sharpened result crucially requires to express the dependence on G not through \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\hat{\alpha })\), but other features of the underlying geometry.

1.3 Two-particle spectral gap identity

All considerations made so far leave the following question unanswered: given that the spectral gap of \(\textrm{SIP}\) does not always coincide with that of a single particle (namely, \(\textrm{RW}\)), does it instead coincide with the spectral gap of the k-particle \(\textrm{SIP}\), for some integer \(k\ge 2\), independent of the underlying graph G and site weights \(\alpha \)?

Asking the same question for \(\textrm{SIP}\)’s “ instantaneously thermalized” variant also known as Beta-Binomial splitting process (see, e.g., [PR26] or Sect. 8.2)—Pietro Caputo conjectured (personal communication) this spectral gap reduction to hold true with \(k=2\). In words, the Beta-Binomial splitting dynamics consists of, first selecting an edge xy with rate \(c_{xy}\) (regardless of the particle configuration), and then letting particles sitting on x and y redistribute themselves according to the \(\textrm{SIP}\)-equilibrium, restricted to that edge. Besides being clearly related to \(\textrm{SIP}\), the Beta-Binomial splitting process is the particle analogue of a model which shows up in the literature under various names, e.g., the Kipnis-Marchioro-Presutti model [KMP82], a random walk on the simplex [CLL20], or (the energies of) the renowned Kac’s walk [Kac56, CCL03]. Spectral gap estimates for this model are known only on two specific geometric settings: the complete graph [CCL03, Cap08], and the segment with \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\ge 1\) [CLL20]. In both cases, a spectral gap identity with \(k=2\) is indeed verified.

The aforementioned conjecture translates to the context of \(\textrm{SIP}\) as follows: for all graphs G and site weights \(\alpha \),

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )=\textrm{gap}_2(G,\alpha )\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(1.7)

where \(\textrm{gap}_k(G,\alpha )\), \(k\ge 1\), stands for the spectral gap of the k-particle \(\textrm{SIP}\). Clearly, we have \(\textrm{gap}_1(G,\alpha )=\textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha )\) and (1.7) equivalently reads as \(\textrm{gap}_k(G,\alpha )=\textrm{gap}_2(G,\alpha )\), for all \(k\ge 2\) (cf. (2.4) and (2.7)). Moreover, in view of (1.2) and \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )\le \textrm{gap}_2(G,\alpha )\le \textrm{gap}_1(G,\alpha )\) in (2.7), the two-particle spectral gap identity in (1.7) remains to be verified only off the log-concave regime, i.e., when (1.4) holds.

We provide a partial answer in the affirmative to this conjecture, by determining spectral gap asymptotics as \(\alpha \rightarrow 0\) along directional limits (i.e., for \(\varepsilon \rightarrow 0\) and some \(\hat{\alpha } \in (0,\infty )^V\), \(\alpha =\varepsilon \hat{\alpha }\); see (2.8)). The precise form of the asymptotic validity of (1.7) is the content of Theorem 2.3 below. We refer to Sect. 2.3 for more details, but let us briefly remark that our asymptotic analysis highlights the key role that the two-particle dynamics plays for both results in Theorems 2.3 and 2.4. We believe this point of view to be fruitful also for other related interacting systems with Gamma-like reversible measures. For more details, see Sect. 8.2.

1.4 Non-conservative case

So far, we discussed only closed (or conservative) systems in which inclusion particles neither get created nor annihilated. Open (or non-conservative) systems allow for this possibility, usually modeled as systems in contact with reservoirs, see, e.g., [Spo83, DEHP93, CGGR13]. For \(\textrm{SIP}\), the standard choice consists of creating a particle in x with rate \(\omega _x\,\theta _x\left( \alpha _x+\eta _x\right) \), and annihilating each particle therein (if any) with rate \(\omega _x\left( 1+\theta _x\right) \), where \(\omega _x\) and \(\theta _x\) form a set of non-negative site parameters added to the model. For precise definitions, see Sect. 6.

In essence, the open \(\textrm{SIP}\) presents two main features: first, due to the lack of particle conservation, the configuration space now consists of a unique, countably infinite, irreducible component; second, the unique ergodic measure is reversible if the \(\theta \)-parameters do not depend on x, and non-reversible if they do depend. This drastic change from reversibility to non-reversibility goes together with the emergence of a number of remarkable phenomena (e.g., currents, long-range correlations), which distinguish equilibrium from non-equilibrium statistical mechanics, see, e.g., [Spo91, DMP91, KL99, Sch01].

Nevertheless, as already observed in the physics literature around thirty years ago for a large class of exactly solvable models [ADHR94] (see also [FGK20]), the “ spectrum” does not depend on the \(\theta \)-parameters, and, thus, is the same for both equilibrium and non-equilibrium systems. The quotation marks above are required when dealing with infinite configuration spaces and corresponding generators, because the full spectrum, a priori, may include also non-eigenvalues and may depend on the underlying functional space on which the matrix/operators act. That said, this \(\theta \)-independence of the spectrum becomes rigorous for systems having a finite configuration space as, for instance, the symmetric exclusion process in contact with reservoirs (see Remark 6.6 below for more details).

In this article, we establish the following results for the non-conservative \(\textrm{SIP}\), for any underlying graph G and site weights \(\alpha \), \(\omega \), and \(\theta \):

  1. (i)

    Eigenfunctions of the purely absorbing system (corresponding to \(\theta \equiv 0\)) “lift” to (generalized) eigenfunctions of \(\textrm{SIP}\) (with general \(\theta \)) associated to the same eigenvalues.

  2. (ii)

    Remarkably, the spectral gap of the particle system with \(\theta \equiv 0\) coincides with

    $$\begin{aligned}\textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha ,\omega )>0\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

    the spectral gap of the one-particle system, namely, of the random walk evolving on G, and killed with rate \(\omega _x\) when sitting on \(x\in V\) (Theorem 6.1).

  3. (iii)

    In the reversible case (i.e., when \(\theta \equiv \mathrm{const.}\)), the eigenfunctions obtained in (i) provide an orthonormal basis in a natural \(L^2\)-space.

Let us emphasize that step (i) holds for general \(\theta \) (i.e., in both reversible and non-reversible settings), while step (ii) is a statement concerning the case \(\theta \equiv 0\) only; establishing the claim in (iii) is where we need to restrict to the reversible \(\textrm{SIP}\).

Putting these steps together, our main result in this non-conservative setting (Corollary 6.4) may be summarized as follows: for all graphs G and site weights \(\alpha \) and \(\omega \),

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha ,\omega ,\varrho )=\textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha ,\omega )\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \text {whenever}\,\, \theta \equiv \varrho >0\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

where \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha ,\omega ,\varrho )\) denotes the first gap in the spectrum of the reversible \(\textrm{SIP}\) with \(\theta \equiv \varrho >0\), compatibly with the functional setting of step (iii).

When comparing the conservative and non-conservative settings, we remark a different behavior of \(\textrm{SIP}\)’s spectral gap: while a system of two particles sharply captures the spectral gap of the many-particle closed \(\textrm{SIP}\), the spectral gap of the open \(\textrm{SIP}\) is always governed (at least when \(\theta \equiv \mathrm{const.}\)) by one random walk with killing — with no constraint on the value of \(\alpha _\textrm{min}>0\). We believe this dichotomy to hold true not just for \(\textrm{SIP}\), but also for a larger class of interacting systems in statistical mechanics having Dirichlet-like steady states (see Sect. 8.2 for further details).

2 Conservative SIP. Setting and Main Results

Unless stated otherwise, all throughout the article, our underlying geometry consists of a weighted finite graph \(G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})\), with symmetric conductances \(c_{xy}=c_{yx}\ge 0\) (conventionally, \(c_{xx}=0\)). We always assume the undirected graph G to be connected, i.e., for all \(x, y \in V\), there exists a sequence \(x_0=x, x_1,\ldots , x_{\ell -1}, x_\ell =y\) in V such that \(\prod _{j=1}^\ell c_{x_{j-1}x_j}>0\). The length \(\ell \in \mathbb {N}\) of the shortest sequence connecting two distinct sites \(x,y\in V\) corresponds to their graph distance, \(\textrm{dist}_G(x,y)\). As usual, we define the diameter, \(\textrm{diam}(G)\in \{1,\ldots , |V|\}\), as the largest of all pairwise distances.

2.1 Model

For a graph \(G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})\), positive site weights \(\alpha =(\alpha _x)_{x\in V}\), and \(k\in \mathbb {N}\), \(\textrm{SIP}_k(G,\alpha )\) denotes the Markov chain evolving on the configuration space

$$\begin{aligned} \Xi _k:=\left\{ \eta \in \mathbb {N}_0^V: |\eta |=k\right\} \; \text {,}\;\;\quad \text {with}\,\, |\eta |:=\sum _{x\in V} \eta _x\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

with infinitesimal generator given, for all \(f\in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\), as

$$\begin{aligned} L_{G,\alpha ,k}f(\eta )=\sum _{x, y \in V} c_{xy}\, \eta _x\left( \alpha _y+\eta _y\right) \left( f(\eta -\delta _x+\delta _y)-f(\eta )\right) \; \text {,}\;\;\quad \eta \in \Xi _k\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(2.1)

In this formula, \( \eta -\delta _x+\delta _y\in \Xi _k\) denotes the configuration obtained from \(\eta \) by removing a particle from x (if any) and placing it on y. Since all particle configurations in \(\Xi _k\) are accessible (as the graph G is connected and the weights \(\alpha =(\alpha _x)_{x\in V}\) are all positive), \(\textrm{SIP}_k(G,\alpha )\) is irreducible. As a simple detailed balance computation shows, \(\textrm{SIP}_k(G,\alpha )\) admits a unique reversible measure \(\mu _{\alpha ,k}\), given, for all \(\eta \in \Xi _k\), by

$$\begin{aligned} \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\eta )= \frac{1}{Z_{\alpha ,k}}\prod _{x\in V}\frac{\Gamma (\alpha _x+\eta _x)}{\Gamma (\alpha _x)\,\eta _x!}\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \text {with}\,\, Z_{\alpha ,k}:=\frac{\Gamma (|\alpha |+k)}{\Gamma (|\alpha |)\,k!}\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(2.2)

Here, \(\Gamma \) denotes the usual gamma function satisfying, for all \(a>0\), \(\Gamma (a+1)=a\, \Gamma (a)\).

Remark 2.1

(Log-concavity) The measure \(\mu _{\alpha ,k}\) in (2.2) is known as Dirichlet-Multinomial distribution of parameters \(k\in \mathbb {N}\) and \(\alpha =(\alpha _x)_{x\in V}\), and is the discrete analogue of the Dirichlet distribution on the simplex \(\varSigma \subset \mathbb {R}^V\) of probability measures, see (8.1) and (8.2) below. The Dirichlet distribution is well known to be log-concave (in the sense that its probability density is of the form \(\exp (-U)\), for some convex \(U:\mathbb {R}^V\rightarrow \mathbb {R}\)) if and only if \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\ge 1\). Hence, \(\mu _{\alpha ,k}\) is the discrete counterpart of a log-concave measure if and only if \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\ge 1\). More directly, \(\mu _{\alpha ,k}\) is a “discrete log-concave measure” also in the following sense: \(\mu _{\alpha ,k}\) is the canonical measure (i.e., conditional on \(|\eta |=k\)) of the product measure \(\nu _{\alpha ,\varrho }\) in (6.2) below, whose Negative-Binomial marginal measures on \(\mathbb {N}_0\) are log-concave (i.e., \(\nu _{\alpha ,\varrho }(\eta _x=\ell )^2\ge \nu _{\alpha ,\varrho }(\eta _x=\ell -1)\,\nu _{\alpha ,\varrho }(\eta _x=\ell +1)\), for all \(\ell \ge 1\) and \(x\in V\)) if and only if \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\ge 1\).

2.2 A non-asymptotic lower bound for the spectral gap

Because of irreducibility and reversibility, all \(|\Xi _k|\) eigenvalues of the (negative) generator \(-L_{G,\alpha ,k}\) in (2.1) are real and non-negative, the smallest one being equal to zero, whereas the second one — referred to as spectral gap of \(\textrm{SIP}_k(G,\alpha )\) and shortened as \(\textrm{gap}_k(G,\alpha )\) — being strictly positive. Confronting with the notation from Sect. 1, we have

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha )=\textrm{gap}_1(G,\alpha )\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(2.3)

because \(\textrm{SIP}_k(G,\alpha )\) with \(k=1\) corresponds to a single, thus, non-interacting particle, abbreviated as \(\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha )\). All cases \(k\ge 2\) describe a truly interacting system; hence, we define

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )= \inf _{k\ge 2} \textrm{gap}_k(G,\alpha )\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(2.4)

We are now ready to state the precise statement of the result in (1.6), concerned with the sharp dependence of \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )\) on \(\alpha _\textrm{min}=\min _{x\in V}\alpha _x\), with a special focus on the regime \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\in (0,1)\).

Theorem 2.2

For all graphs \(G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})\) and site weights \(\alpha =(\alpha _x)_{x\in V}\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha _\textrm{min}\left\{ \frac{1}{{54}} \, \frac{ \alpha _\textrm{ratio}}{{{(6e)}}^{\alpha _\textrm{min}/\alpha _\textrm{ratio}} } \, \frac{ c_\textrm{min}}{|V|^2\, \textrm{diam}(G) }\right\} \le \textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

where

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha _\textrm{ratio}:=\frac{\alpha _\textrm{min}}{\alpha _\textrm{max}}= \frac{\min _{x\in V}\alpha _x}{\max _{y\in V}\alpha _y}\le 1\; \text {,}\;\;\quad c_\textrm{min}:=\min _{\begin{array}{c} x,y\in V\\ c_{xy}> 0 \end{array}}c_{xy}>0\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(2.5)

As already discussed in Sect. 1.2, this lower bound captures the correct dependence of \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )\) on \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\) for every fixed G, provided that \(\alpha _\textrm{ratio}\) remains bounded away from zero. Moreover, the estimate is non-asymptotic, with a rather explicit constant depending only on simple geometric parameters of the graph, such as its size and diameter. We do not claim, however, that this geometric dependence is sharp. This is fundamentally due to the fact that the \(L^2\) inequalities (Lemmas 4.2–4.6) used in the proof of Theorem 2.2 are designed to maintain the sharp linear dependence on \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\), at the expense of suboptimality caused by exploiting the geometric structure.

To illustrate this point, take \(\alpha \equiv \varepsilon \in (0,1)\), so that \(\alpha _\textrm{min}=\varepsilon \) and \(\alpha _\textrm{ratio}=1\). For d-dimensional boxes or tori of side length N, Theorem 2.2 gives

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\varepsilon \textbf{1}) \ge C\,\varepsilon \,N^{-(2d+1)}\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

for some \(C=C(d,c_\textrm{min})>0\). This is not expected to be the correct dependence on N. Indeed, in view of Theorem 2.3 and of the proof of Theorem 2.4, the relevant small-\(\varepsilon \) scale should rather be

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\varepsilon \textbf{1}) \asymp \varepsilon /s_d(N)\; \text {,}\;\;\quad s_d(N):={\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} N^{2} & \text {if}\quad d=1\\ N^{2}\log N & \text {if}\quad d=2\\ N^{d} & \text {if}\quad d\ge 3\; \text {.}\end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$
(2.6)

Thus, while Theorem 2.2 is sharp in its dependence on \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\), its dependence on the geometry is generally far from optimal.

Rather than optimizing these geometric constants on specific families of graphs, we keep the underlying geometry arbitrary and pass to the regime of vanishing weights \(\alpha \rightarrow 0\). In this setting, we identify the relevant asymptotic object not in terms of the spectral gap of \(\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha )\), but of the “second simplest system” on the same geometry: \(\textrm{SIP}_2(G,\alpha )\), namely the symmetric inclusion process with just two particles.

2.3 Spectral gap’s asymptotics

As already proved in [KS24] (see also Sect. 3.1 below), we have, for all graphs G and site weights \(\alpha \),

$$\begin{aligned} { \textrm{gap}_{k+1}(G,\alpha )\le \textrm{gap}_{k}(G,\alpha )\; \text {,}\;\;\quad k \ge 1\; \text {.}} \end{aligned}$$
(2.7)

The next result shows that, along directional limits \(\alpha \rightarrow 0\), i.e.,

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha =\varepsilon \hat{\alpha } \; \text {,}\;\;\quad \text {with}\,\, \varepsilon \rightarrow 0\,\, \text {and}\,\, \hat{\alpha } = (\hat{\alpha }_x)_{x\in V}\in (0,\infty )^V\ \text {fixed}\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(2.8)

equality is achieved in (2.7) for \(k\ge 2\). This result is formulated along fixed positive directions; see Remark 5.15 below for the relation with general limits \(\alpha \rightarrow 0\).

Theorem 2.3

For all graphs \(G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})\) and site weights \(\hat{\alpha } = (\hat{\alpha }_x)_{x\in V}\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \lim _{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0} \frac{\textrm{gap}_k(G,\varepsilon \hat{\alpha })}{\textrm{gap}_2(G,\varepsilon \hat{\alpha })} =1\; \text {,}\;\;\quad {k\ge 2}\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

In view of the definition of \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )\) in (2.4) and of the inequalities in (2.7), the above limit is an asymptotic version of the two-particle spectral gap identity in (1.7). Let us stress that this result holds true for any graph G, positive site weights \(\hat{\alpha }\), and integer \(k\ge 2\) (trivially, for \(k=2\)). However, the case \(k=1\) is excluded for a good reason, as shown in the following result.

Theorem 2.4

For some graphs \(G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})\) and site weights \(\hat{\alpha }=(\hat{\alpha }_x)_{x\in V}\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \lim _{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0}\frac{\textrm{gap}_2(G,\varepsilon \hat{\alpha })}{\textrm{gap}_1(G,\varepsilon \hat{\alpha })}<1\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(2.9)

This inequality provides, if combined with (2.7) and the definitions in (2.3)–(2.4), an instance of (1.3), namely, of the failure of the one-particle spectral gap identity in (1.2). The graphs G and site weights \(\hat{\alpha }\) which we adopt to prove this result are far from being intricate. In fact, we take G to be the standard d-dimensional discrete torus, with \(d\ge 2\) and sufficiently large size, and \(\hat{\alpha } \equiv 1\). These are just some of the possible examples that one could exhibit for proving (2.9). Indeed, as it will become apparent from the proof, essentially any other sparse geometry for which the random walk’s relaxation time (i.e., \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha )^{-1}\)) is much smaller than the expected meeting time of two independent random walks initialized at equilibrium, will equally work. We refer to the subsequent section for further explanations.

2.4 Proofs outline

Recall that an inclusion particle jumps from x to a nearest neighbor y at rate proportional to \(\alpha _y + \eta _y\). On the one hand, when non-zero, the term \(\eta _y\ge 1\) stands for the interaction between particles on adjacent sites, which mutually attract each other, tending to stick together. On the other hand, \(\alpha _y> 0\) represents an independent mechanism of particle diffusion. In this sense, the regime \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\in (0,1)\) depicts the situation in which, at least in some portions of the graph, particle stickiness dominates over diffusion.

This regime becomes particularly significant when considering the limit \(\alpha \rightarrow 0\). In this setting, \(\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )\) exhibits a metastable behavior, as thoroughly studied in the last decade [GRV13, BDG17, KS21, Kim21, Kim25]. These works describe the following qualitative picture, when \(\alpha =\varepsilon \hat{\alpha }\) as in (2.8). Roughly speaking, as long as particles are far from each other (i.e., at graph distance \(\ge 2\)), they perform jumps on the timescale \(\varepsilon ^{-1}\). As soon as they are at distance one, they stick together in a much shorter time, roughly of order one. These two mechanisms bring particles to meet and pile up together in large stacks (or, condensates) in a time of order \(\varepsilon ^{-1}\). Concurrently, some particles sitting on a stack would still attempt to jump to empty nearest neighboring vertices at rate \(\asymp \varepsilon \). While some of the attempts fail, that is, the “courageous” particle is instantaneously sucked back into a neighboring stack, some of these will succeed to attract sufficiently many particles, managing to move the whole stack. Due to the rates’ symmetry encoded in the condition \(c_{xy}=c_{yx}\), it is then part of the results in [GRV13, BDG17] to show that relevant jumps of entire stacks occur at times of order \(\varepsilon ^{-1}\), and that, on this timescale, stacks are effectively approximated by independent random walks on G, each evolving at rate proportional to \(\varepsilon \), and capable of coalescing (possibly in a very complicated way if three or more stacks are involved) when getting at distance one from each other. Hence, the metastable relevant part of the dynamics takes place, according to this qualitative picture, on the timescale \(\varepsilon ^{-1}\).

Although this metastable picture provides a first correct intuition that \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\varepsilon \hat{\alpha }) \asymp \varepsilon \) as \(\varepsilon \rightarrow 0\), our quantitative analysis must take into account also features not captured by usual metastability limit theorems. For instance, while metastability describes macroscopic features of the system when initialized from macroscopically relevant configurations, global functional inequalities as those determining spectral gaps require bounds which must be uniform over the initial conditions. Moreover, we seek for lower bounds for \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\varepsilon \hat{\alpha })\) that are independent of the system size. However, a crucial ingredient in the metastability picture of \(\textrm{SIP}(G,\varepsilon \hat{\alpha })\) is that \(k\in \mathbb {N}\), the number of particles in the system is not too large; more precisely,

$$\begin{aligned} \log k\ll \varepsilon ^{-1}\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \text {as}\,\, \varepsilon \ll 1\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(2.10)

This condition, always assumed in previous works (see, e.g., [GRV13, BDG17, Kim21, Kim25]) is not just technical, but strictly required for the condensation mechanism to take place: (2.10) is a sufficient and necessary condition for the steady state of the system to charge configurations consisting of a single stack of particles only.

2.4.1 Outline of the proof of Theorem 2.2

Our proof for determining the order-one dependence on \(\varepsilon \hat{\alpha }_\textrm{min}\) of \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\varepsilon \hat{\alpha })\) combines three main ingredients:

  • the nested eigenstructure of \(\textrm{SIP}\) (valid for any underlying graph G) as already exploited in [KS24];

  • the full knowledge of the eigendecomposition in mean-field geometries and, more specifically, the fact that eigenvalues grow quadratically (neglecting multiplicities) with the particle total number;

  • comparison inequalities of Dirichlet forms associated to the particle system on a graph G, against that on the complete graph.

All three steps are presented and combined to yield the proof of Theorem 2.2 in Sect. 3, but the actual proof of the comparison inequality, longer and more technical, is presented in Sect. 4.

In the metastable regime (2.10), our comparisons build on the classical distinguishing paths method as applied, e.g., in the seminal work [DSC93] to the symmetric exclusion process. Most of the care in our context lies in devising paths in which the total “cost” becomes not larger than \(\varepsilon ^{-1} \, k^2\), up to constants depending only on G and \(\hat{\alpha }\). Here, the cost of each move heavily depends on whether relocating a single particle augments, keeps constant, or lowers the total number of stacks, and whether stacks do have or not neighboring stacks. Hence, following the metastable behavior of the system offers us a guideline to construct efficient distinguishing paths, e.g., taking care of never creating more than one extra occupied site along the path. We refer to Sects. 4.2 and 4.3 for the details.

When (2.10) does not hold, as already mentioned, the metastability picture breaks down, and so does this approach via distinguishing paths. In order to overcome this, we replace paths by a more sophisticated two-dimensional surface, which is obtained by collecting the trace of all relevant paths. In presence of stacks consisting of a huge number of particles, this two-dimensional surface allows us to rigorously implement the idea of moving only a smaller portion of the stack at the time according to some suitably chosen probability, and leaving the rest untouched. While this strategy of moving only some (and not all) particles of a stack produces an extra factor \(\varepsilon ^{-1}\), which we could not afford in the regime (2.10), this is now not problematic, as we can control this factor with a suitable function of the number of particles of the stack. For more details, see Sect. 4.4.

As we just sketched, these estimates involving Dirichlet forms yield comparison constants which unavoidably degenerate like \(1/k^2\) as the total number of particles \(k\in \mathbb {N}\) grows. Here is where we crucially exploit the first two ingredients of the proof, in particular, the quadratic growth of eigenvalues for the mean-field system, which removes this degeneracy.

2.4.2 Outline of the proof of Theorems 2.3 and 2.4

In order to establish the strict inequality between \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\varepsilon \hat{\alpha })\) and \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\varepsilon \hat{\alpha })\) as in (1.3), comparison arguments as those we just described turn out to be too loose (and, actually, too elaborated) to capture the precise pre-factors. Instead, we follow a more basic approach, namely, turning the aforementioned metastability picture into some quantitative spectral information.

More specifically, for a fixed graph G and a fixed number of particles \(k\in \mathbb {N}\), we employ and sharpen classical limit theorems for slow–fast systems from [Kur73] (see also [GRV13] for another application in the context of \(\textrm{SIP}\)). These theorems rigorously describe the metastable dynamics for the slow macroscopic variables of \(\textrm{SIP}(G,\varepsilon \hat{\alpha })\) in the limit \(\varepsilon \rightarrow 0\). The limit of the slow variables after a thermalization of the fast ones identifies the low-lying spectrum. This allows us to capture the behavior of \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\varepsilon \hat{\alpha })\), at least for \(\varepsilon \) small enough. These intuitive ideas are stated and proved rigorously in Sect. 5.1.

We are then left with identifying the spectral gap of the limiting metastable dynamics. In contrast to \(\textrm{SIP}\), which is irreducible, this metastable chain may consist of both recurrent and transient states, as we detail in Sect. 5.2. Recurrent regions correspond to those configurations in which all particles sit together, and the resulting single stack moves like \(\textrm{RW}(G,\hat{\alpha })\). Transient states are those configurations in which there are stacks at graph distance at least two from each other. Hence, the spectral gap of the metastable process is just the smallest among the random walk’s gap and the eigenvalues associated to any part of the (sub-stochastic) transient dynamics.

Most of our work is devoted to analyzing the hierarchy of transient states. Indeed, even the decomposition into irreducible components and the jump rates of the transient dynamics are, in general, highly complicated and non-trivially dependent on the underlying geometry and the number of particles. Nevertheless, by exploiting a form of consistency for the metastable limiting dynamics, we are able to deduce that the lowest-lying eigenvalue of the transient dynamics is attained by the system with just two particles, as adding more particles—thus, potentially, stacks—does not cause any slowdown in the system. More in detail, in Sect. 5.3, we verify that one only needs to focus on the number of stacks (and not on the precise allocation of particles in each stack): for each \(m \ge 2\), the smallest eigenvalue of all transient sub-systems with m piles comes exactly from the sub-system with m isolated particles. As a next step, in Sect. 5.4, we prove that the smallest eigenvalue of all sub-systems with \(m \ge 2\) isolated particles is attained at \(m=2\), i.e., the sub-system with two isolated particles. Collecting these observations, we prove Theorem 2.3 in Sect. 5.5. Theorem 2.4 is also proved in this section: by exploiting this rigorous metastable description of eigenvalues’ asymptotics, our proof boils down to estimates of relaxation and mean meeting times of independent particles on a well studied geometry, corresponding, respectively, to (the inverse of) the spectral gap of the recurrent and transient dynamics of \(\textrm{SIP}\)’s metastable process.

3 Proof of Theorem 2.2

We follow the outline in Sect. 2.4.1.

3.1 General eigenstructure of SIP

We start by recalling from [KS24] some general facts on the eigenstructure of \(\textrm{SIP}\) functional to our analysis. First of all, \(\textrm{SIP}\) is consistent, in the sense that the system with \(k-1\) particles is recovered (in the sense of finite-dimensional distributions) from the system with k particles, provided that one particle is removed uniformly at random. In formula, this means that

$$\begin{aligned} L_{G,\alpha ,k}\,\mathfrak {a}_{k} = \mathfrak {a}_{k}\,L_{G,\alpha ,k-1} \end{aligned}$$
(3.1)

holds true for all graphs G, site weights \(\alpha \), and \(k\ge 2\), where \(\mathfrak {a}_{k}:\mathbb {R}^{\Xi _{k-1}}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\) is the annihilation operator defined as

$$\begin{aligned} \mathfrak {a}_{k} g(\eta ):=\sum _{x\in V}\eta _x\, g(\eta -\delta _x)\; \text {,}\;\;\quad g\in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _{k-1}}\; \text {,}\;\;\eta \in \Xi _k\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(3.2)

As proved in [KS24, Appendix A], \(\mathfrak {a}_k\) is one-to-one. This readily implies that the (real) spectrum of \(L_{G,\alpha ,k-1}\) is contained (with multiplicities) in that of \(L_{G,\alpha ,k}\) (and, in particular, that (2.7) holds). More precisely, if \((\lambda ,f)\) is an eigenvalue-eigenfunction pair for \(-L_{G,\alpha ,k-1}\), then \((\lambda ,\mathfrak {a}_k f)\) is one for \(-L_{G,\alpha ,k}\). As a consequence of this fact and reversibility, the rest of the spectrum of \(-L_{G,\alpha ,k}\) that does not come from \(-L_{G,\alpha ,k-1}\) must be found in the orthogonal complement of those eigenfunctions “lifted”  from those of \(-L_{G,\alpha ,k-1}\). This observation leads us to introduce the creation operator \(\mathfrak {a}_{\alpha ,k-1}^\dagger :\mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _{k-1}}\):

$$\begin{aligned} \mathfrak {a}_{\alpha ,k-1}^\dagger f(\xi ) :=\sum _{x\in V} (\xi _x +\alpha _x ) \, f(\xi + \delta _x ) \; \text {,}\;\;\quad f \in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k} \; \text {,}\;\;\xi \in \Xi _{k-1} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(3.3)

As demonstrated in [KS24, Proposition 3.1], the two operators \(\mathfrak {a}_k\) and \(\mathfrak {a}_{\alpha ,k-1}^\dagger \) are adjoint one to each other in the following sense:

$$\begin{aligned} \langle \mathfrak {a}_kg\, \vert \, f \rangle _{\alpha ,k} = \frac{k}{|\alpha |+k-1} \, \langle g\, \vert \, \mathfrak {a}_{\alpha ,k-1}^\dagger f \rangle _{\alpha ,k-1} \; \text {,}\;\;\quad f\in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k} \; \text {,}\;\;g \in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _{k-1}} \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(3.4)

where \(\langle \,\cdot \,\, \vert \, \,\cdot \, \rangle _{\alpha ,k}\) denotes the inner product in \(\Xi _k\) with respect to \(\mu _{\alpha ,k}\) given in (2.2). In turn, \(\mathfrak {a}_{\alpha ,k-1}^\dagger \) is surjective and the following orthogonal decomposition holds:

$$\begin{aligned} L^2(\mu _{\alpha ,k}) = \textrm{Im}\,\mathfrak {a}_k\oplus _{\perp } \textrm{Ker}\,\mathfrak {a}_{\alpha ,k-1}^\dagger \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(3.5)

Next, let us recall a well-known variational characterization of \(\textrm{gap}_k(G,\alpha )\): for each \(k\ge 2\),

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_k(G,\alpha ) = \inf _{\begin{array}{c} f \in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\\ f \ne \text {const.} \end{array}} \frac{\mathcal {E}_{G,\alpha ,k}(f)}{\textrm{Var}_{\alpha ,k}(f)} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(3.6)

Here, \(\mathcal {E}_{G,\alpha ,k}(f)=\left\langle f\, \Bigg | \vert \, -L_{G,\alpha ,k}f \right\rangle _{\alpha ,k}\) denotes the Dirichlet form evaluated at \(f\in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\), namely,

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {E}_{G,\alpha ,k}(f) = \frac{1}{2} \sum _{\eta \in \Xi _k} \sum _{x,y\in V} \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\eta )\,c_{xy}\, \eta _x \left( \alpha _y + \eta _y\right) \left( f(\eta -\delta _x+\delta _y)-f(\eta )\right) ^2 \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(3.7)

whereas \(\textrm{Var}_{\alpha ,k}(f)\) stands for the variance of \(f \in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\) with respect to \(\mu _{\alpha ,k}\). If \(f\in \textrm{Ker}\,\mathfrak {a}_{\alpha ,k-1}^\dagger \), then \(\langle f\rangle _{\alpha ,k}=\langle f\, \vert \, 1 \rangle _{\alpha ,k}=0\) (remark that \(1\in \textrm{Im}\,\mathfrak a_k\) and apply (3.4)) and, thus,

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{Var}_{\alpha ,k}(f) = \left\Vert f\right\Vert _{\alpha ,k}^2:=\sum _{\eta \in \Xi _k} \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\eta )\, f(\eta )^2 \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Taking advantage of the lifting property of \(\mathfrak {a}_k\), the self-adjointness of \(L_{G,\alpha ,k}\) on \(L^2(\mu _{\alpha ,k})\), and the orthogonal decomposition in (3.5), (3.6) simplifies as follows:

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_k(G,\alpha ) = \textrm{gap}_{k-1}(G,\alpha ) \wedge \bigg ( \inf _{\begin{array}{c} f\in \textrm{Ker}\,\mathfrak {a}_{\alpha ,k-1}^\dagger \\ f\ne 0 \end{array}} \frac{\mathcal {E}_{G,\alpha ,k}(f)}{\left\Vert f\right\Vert _{\alpha ,k}^2} \bigg ) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(3.8)

Hence, by an iterative argument on \(k\ge 2\), we may focus on comparing \(\mathcal {E}_{G,\alpha ,k}(f)\) and \(\left\Vert f\right\Vert _{\alpha ,k}^2\), only for functions \(f \in \textrm{Ker}\,\mathfrak {a}_{\alpha ,k-1}^\dagger \).

3.2 Dirichlet forms and comparisons

The Dirichlet form \(\mathcal {E}_{G,\alpha ,k}(f)\) depends on the underlying graph G, whereas the reversible measure \(\mu _{\alpha ,k}\), and, thus, the orthogonal decomposition in (3.5) and \(\left\Vert f\right\Vert _{\alpha ,k}^2\), do not. This simple observation motivates us to compare \(\mathcal {E}_{G,\alpha ,k}(f)\) with its complete graph analogue, i.e., \(\mathcal {E}_{K,\alpha ,k}(f)\), where \(K=K_V\) denotes the complete graph on the sites of V with unitary conductances \(c_{xy}\equiv 1\).

It turns out that, in this complete graph case, we can obtain the full eigendecomposition of \(\textrm{SIP}\). Remark that, although the spectrum and a set of eigenfunctions of \(\textrm{SIP}_k(K,\alpha )\) are known [Shi77] (see also [Cor23, Theorem 1.4] or [WZ19] and references therein), the next proposition, together with the identity in (3.8), provides a simple and complete description of eigenvalues and eigenspaces when \(G=K\), which we shall exploit later.

Proposition 3.1

For every \(k \ge 1\) and \(f\in \textrm{Ker}\,\mathfrak {a}_{\alpha ,k-1}^\dagger \), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {E}_{K,\alpha ,k}(f) = k \left( |\alpha |+k-1\right) \left\Vert f\right\Vert _{\alpha ,k}^2 \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(3.9)

Proof

The result is well known for \(k=1\), the random walk case. Indeed, \(\textrm{Ker}\,\mathfrak a_{\alpha ,0}^\dagger \) coincides with the subspace of functions \(f\in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _1}\) having mean zero with respect to \(\mu _{\alpha ,1}\), which equals \(\mu _{\alpha ,1}(\delta _x)=\frac{\alpha _x}{|\alpha |}\), \(x\in V\).

Now, fix \(k\ge 2\) and \(f\in \textrm{Ker}\,\mathfrak {a}_{\alpha ,k-1}^\dagger \). We have

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {E}_{K,\alpha ,k}(f)= k\sum _{\xi \in \Xi _{{k-1}}}\mu _{\alpha ,k-1}(\xi )\, \mathcal {E}_{K,\alpha +\xi ,1}(f_\xi )\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(3.10)

with \(f_\xi \in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _1}\) being defined as \(f_\xi (\delta _x):=f(\xi +\delta _x)\). Remark that (3.10) corresponds to [KS24, Eq. (3.10)], which is deduced from the following identity [KS24, Eq. (3.9)]: for all \(x\in V\) and \(\xi \in \Xi _{k-1}\),

$$\begin{aligned} \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\xi +\delta _x)\left( \xi _x+1\right) = \frac{Z_{\alpha ,k-1}}{Z_{\alpha ,k}}\,\mu _{\alpha ,k-1}(\xi )\left( \alpha _x+\xi _x\right) = k\,\mu _{\alpha ,k-1}(\xi )\,\frac{\alpha _x+\xi _x}{|\alpha |+k-1}\; \text {,}\;\;\nonumber \\ \end{aligned}$$
(3.11)

where the second step used (cf. (2.2))

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{Z_{\alpha ,k-1}}{Z_{\alpha ,k}} =\frac{k}{|\alpha |+k-1}\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Analogously to (3.10), we get

$$\begin{aligned} \left\Vert f\right\Vert _{\alpha ,k}^2 = \sum _{\xi \in \Xi _{k-1}}\mu _{\alpha ,k-1}(\xi )\left\Vert f_\xi \right\Vert _{\alpha +\xi ,1}^2\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(3.12)

By (3.10) and the identity in (3.9) for \(k=1\) (note that \(f\in \textrm{Ker}\,\mathfrak {a}_{\alpha ,k-1}^\dagger \) ensures that \(f_\xi \in \textrm{Ker}\,\mathfrak a_{\alpha ,0}^\dagger \)) with \(\alpha +\xi \) in place of \(\alpha \) therein (note that \(|\alpha +\xi |=|\alpha |+k-1\)), we obtain

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {E}_{K,\alpha ,k}(f) = k\left( |\alpha |+k-1\right) \sum _{\xi \in \Xi _{k-1}}\mu _{\alpha ,k-1}(\xi ) \left\Vert f_\xi \right\Vert _{\alpha +\xi ,1}^2 = k\left( |\alpha |+k-1\right) \left\Vert f\right\Vert _{\alpha ,k}^2\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

where the last step used (3.12). This proves the desired result for \(k\ge 2\). \(\square \)

In view of (3.8) and Proposition 3.1, it remains to compare the two Dirichlet forms \(\mathcal {E}_{G,\alpha ,k}(f)\) and \(\mathcal {E}_{K,\alpha ,k}(f)\), for any \(f\in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\). This is the content of the following theorem, and certainly represents the hardest step of the proof of Theorem 2.2.

Theorem 3.2

For all \(k \ge 2\) and \(f\in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {E}_{K,\alpha ,k}(f)\le \frac{{54} \, k \left( \alpha _\textrm{max}+k-1\right) |V|^2 \, \textrm{diam}(G) \, {(6e)}^{\alpha _\textrm{max} }}{\alpha _\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{ratio} \, c_\textrm{min}} \, \mathcal {E}_{G,\alpha ,k}(f) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

$$\begin{aligned} \Xi _\ell ^A :=\left\{ \sigma \in \Xi _\ell : \sum _{{z} \in A}\sigma _{z} = \ell \right\} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Note that \(\Xi _{k}^{V}=\Xi _{k}\). Moreover, for \(\sigma \in \Xi _{\ell }^{A}\), \(y_{1},\ldots ,y_{s}\in V\), and \(m_{1},\ldots ,m_{s}\in \mathbb {N}_0\), define

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma _{m_1,\ldots ,m_{s}}^{y_{1}\cdots y_s} :=\sigma +\sum _{i=1}^{s} m_{i}\, \delta _{y_{i}} \in \Xi _{\ell +m_1 +\cdots +m_s}\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(4.4)

where the right-hand side should be understood as a summation of functions. Thus, the number of particles in the new configuration \(\sigma _{m_{1},\ldots ,m_{s}}^{y_{1}\cdots y_{s}}\) equals \(\ell +m_{1}+\cdots +m_s\).

According to (4.4), we may decompose the Dirichlet summation \(\mathcal {E}_K^{x,y}\) in (4.2) as

$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{\ell =1}^{k} \sum _{m=1}^{\ell } \sum _{\sigma \in \Xi _{k-\ell }^{V\setminus \{x,y\}}} \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy})\,m\left( \alpha _{y}+\ell -m\right) (f(\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy})-f(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}))^{2} \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

where \(\ell \in \llbracket 1,k \rrbracket :=[1,k]\cap \mathbb {Z}\) denotes the number of particles in \(\{x,y\}\), and \(m\in \llbracket 1,\ell \rrbracket \) denotes the number of particles at x. For each \(\eta \in \Xi _k\) and \(x,y \in V\), define

$$\begin{aligned} & {\mathfrak {c}_K(\eta ;x,y)} :=\mu _{\alpha ,k}(\eta ) \, \eta _x \left( \alpha _y + \eta _y\right) \; \text {,}\;\;\nonumber \\ & \quad \nabla _K^2 f(\eta ;x,y) :=\mathfrak {c}_K (\eta ;x,y)\, (f(\eta -\delta _x+\delta _y)-f(\eta ))^2 \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.5)

According to this notation,

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {E}_K^{x,y} = \sum _{\ell =1}^k \sum _{m=1}^\ell \sum _{\sigma \in \Xi _{k-\ell }^{V\setminus \{x,y\}}} \nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.6)

Indeed, the two summations in \(\ell \in \llbracket 1,k \rrbracket \) and \(m \in \llbracket 1,\ell \rrbracket \) count all possible types of jumps from x to y, and the summation in \(\sigma \in \Xi _{k-\ell }^{V \setminus \{x,y\}}\) counts the possible particle configuration on the remaining vertices in \(V \setminus \{x,y\}\).

Fix a shortest sequence

$$\begin{aligned} x=x_{0},x_{1},\ldots ,x_{t}=y \end{aligned}$$
(4.7)

in G such that \(c_{x_{s-1}x_s}>0\) for all \(s\in \llbracket 1,t \rrbracket \), where \(t\in \llbracket 1,\textrm{diam}(G) \rrbracket \). For \(\eta ,\zeta \in \Xi _{k}\), write

$$\begin{aligned} \mathfrak {c}(\eta ,\zeta ):=\mu _{\alpha ,k}(\eta )\, r_{\alpha ,k}(\eta ,\zeta )\quad \text {and} \quad \nabla ^{2}f(\eta ,\zeta ) :=\mathfrak {c}(\eta ,\zeta ) \left( f(\zeta )-f(\eta )\right) ^{2}\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(4.8)

where \(r_{\alpha ,k}(\cdot ,\cdot )\) is the transition rate function of \(\textrm{SIP}_{k}(G,\alpha )\). Note that by the reversibility of \(\mu _{\alpha ,k}\), \(\nabla ^2f(\eta ,\zeta ) = \nabla ^2f(\zeta ,\eta )\). Then, we have

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {E}= \sum _{ \eta \in \Xi _k } \sum _{\begin{array}{c} z<w \\ c_{zw}>0 \end{array} } \nabla ^2f(\eta ,\eta -\delta _z+\delta _w ) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.9)

A sequence of configurations \(\eta _0,\eta _1,\ldots , \eta _M\) in \(\Xi _k\) is a path if the transition rates \(r_{\alpha ,k}(\cdot ,\cdot )\) are positive along the sequence, i.e., \(r_{\alpha ,k}(\eta _{s-1},\eta _{s})>0\) for all \(s\in \llbracket 1,M \rrbracket \).

The idea to prove Lemma 4.1 is as follows. First, we decompose \(\mathcal {E}_K^{x,y}\) according to (4.6). Then, for each triple \((\ell ,m,\sigma )\), we upper bound \({\nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) }\) with a certain collection of terms \(\nabla ^2f(\eta ,\eta -\delta _z+\delta _w)\) that appear in the right-hand side of (4.9). Finally, we count the number of overlaps for each term \(\nabla ^2f(\eta ,\eta -\delta _z+\delta _w)\) for all such triple \((\ell ,m,\sigma )\), which gives an upper bound of \(\mathcal {E}_K^{x.y}\) in terms of \(\mathcal {E}\).

The procedure of upper bounding each \({\nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) }\) depends on the detailed distribution of particles along the sequence (4.7). In the following four subsections, Sects. 4.1, 4.2, 4.3 and 4.4, we demonstrate each simple cases, and finally in Sect. 4.5 we deal with the general case and conclude the proof of Lemma 4.1.

4.1 Connected case

First, suppose that (cf. (4.7))

$$\begin{aligned} c_{xy}>0 \; \text {,}\;\;\quad {\text {which implies that}}\,\, t=1 \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.10)

Define

$$\begin{aligned} {\Omega _{x,y}^{\ell ,m,\sigma }} :=\Big \{ \{ \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}, \sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy} \} \Big \} \, \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

which is the singleton set that contains the edge \(\{\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}, \sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy} \}\). When there is no room for confusion, we simply write \(\Omega = \Omega _{x,y}^{\ell ,m,\sigma }\).

Lemma 4.2

Suppose that (4.10) holds. Then, we have

$$\begin{aligned} {\nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) } \le {\frac{m \left( \alpha _y+\ell -m\right) }{c_\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{min}}} \sum _{\{\zeta ,\zeta '\} {\in } \Omega } \nabla ^2f(\zeta ,\zeta ') \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Proof

It is clear that \(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy},\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy}\) is itself a path, and

$$\begin{aligned} {\nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) } = \frac{1}{c_{xy}} \, \nabla ^2f(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy},\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy}) = \frac{1}{c_{xy}} \, \sum _{\{\zeta ,\zeta '\} \in \Omega } \nabla ^2 f(\zeta ,\zeta ') \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Thus, the lemma easily follows since \(c_{xy} \ge c_\textrm{min}\) and \(m\,(\alpha _y+\ell -m) \ge \alpha _y \ge \alpha _\textrm{min}\). \(\square \)

4.2 Decomposition of gradients: occupied sites

Now, assume that (cf. (4.7) and (4.10))

$$\begin{aligned} c_{xy}=0 \; \text {,}\;\;\quad {\text {which implies that}} \,\, t \ge 2 \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.11)

Additionally, in this subsection, assume that all sites along the sequence (4.7) are occupied by particles of \(\sigma \), i.e.,

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma _{x_s} \ge 1\quad \text {for all} \,\, s\in \llbracket 1,t-1 \rrbracket \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.12)

Then, starting from \(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}\), we send a single particle from \(x_{s-1}\) to \(x_s\) for each \(s \in \llbracket 1,t \rrbracket \), to arrive at \(\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy}\). The corresponding path becomes

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy},\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_1y},\ldots ,\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{t-1}y},\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.13)

See Fig. 1 for a visual representation.

Let \(\Omega ={\Omega _{x,y}^{\ell ,m,\sigma }}\) be the collection of all edges \(\{\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{s-1}y},\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_sy}\}\) for all \(s \in \llbracket 1,t \rrbracket \), i.e.,

$$\begin{aligned} \Omega _{x,y}^{\ell ,m,\sigma }:= \left\{ \{\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{s-1}y},\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_sy}\}: s \in \llbracket 1,t \rrbracket \right\} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

With this notation, we prove in this subsection the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3

Assume that the conditions in (4.11) and (4.12) hold. Then, we have

$$\begin{aligned} {\nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) } \le {\frac{ t \, m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) (1+\alpha _\textrm{max})}{c_\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{min}} } \sum _{\{\zeta ,\zeta '\} {\in } \Omega } \nabla ^2f(\zeta ,\zeta ') \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Proof

By Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, for any path \(\eta =\eta _0,\eta _1,\ldots ,\eta _M=\zeta \), we have (cf. (4.8))

$$\begin{aligned} (f(\zeta )-f(\eta ))^2 \le \left( \sum _{i=1}^M \nabla ^2f(\eta _{i-1},\eta _i) \right) \left( \sum _{i=1}^M \frac{1}{\mathfrak {c}(\eta _{i-1},\eta _i)} \right) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.14)

Thus, applying (4.14) along the path (4.13), we get (cf. (4.5))

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} {\nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) } \le \, \Bigg ( \sum _{s=1}^t \nabla ^2f(&\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{s-1}y},\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_sy}) \Bigg ) \, \\&\times \left( \sum _{s=1}^t \frac{ \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy})\, m\left( \alpha _y + \ell -m \right) }{ \mathfrak {c}(\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{s-1}y},\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_sy}) } \right) \; \text {.}\end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$
(4.15)

For \(x\in V\) and \(m \in \mathbb {N}_0\), define

$$\begin{aligned} \pi _{x}(m) :=\frac{\Gamma (\alpha _{x}+m)}{\Gamma (\alpha _{x})\,m!}\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \text {such that} \,\, \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\eta )=\frac{1}{Z_{\alpha ,k}}\prod _{x\in V}\pi _{x}(\eta _{x})\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.16)

Here, \(\Gamma (\cdot )\) denotes the usual gamma function. We now collect some basic properties. \(\square \)

Lemma 4.4

We have, for each \(m \in \mathbb {N}_0\),

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha _x \le \pi _x(m)\left( \alpha _x+m\right) = \pi _x(m+1) \left( m+1\right) \le \alpha _x\,e^{\alpha _x\sum _{j=1}^m \frac{1}{j}} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.17)

Moreover, for \(m \ge m' \ge 1\),

$$\begin{aligned} 1 \le \frac{ \pi _x(m)\,m }{ \pi _x(m')\,m' } \le e^{\alpha _x \sum _{j=m'}^{m-1}\frac{1}{j}} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.18)

Proof

Since \(\Gamma (a+1)=a\,\Gamma (a)\) for all \(a> 0\),

$$\begin{aligned} \pi _x(m) \left( \alpha _x+m\right) = \frac{ \Gamma (\alpha _x+m+1) }{\Gamma (\alpha _x) \, m!} = \pi _x(m+1) \left( m+1\right) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Clearly, since \(\alpha _x >0\),

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{ \Gamma (\alpha _x+m+1) }{\Gamma (\alpha _x) \, m!} = \alpha _x \, \prod _{j=1}^m \frac{j+\alpha _x}{j} \ge \alpha _x \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

On the other hand, since \(1+a \le e^a\) for any \(a \in \mathbb {R}\),

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{ \Gamma (\alpha _x+m+1) }{\Gamma (\alpha _x) \, m!} = \alpha _x \, \prod _{j=1}^m \left( 1+\frac{\alpha _x}{j} \right) \le \alpha _x \, \exp \left( \sum _{j=1}^m \frac{\alpha _x}{j} \right) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Combining the three displayed identities proves (4.17). In addition, for \(m \ge m' \ge 1\),

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\pi _x(m) \, m}{\pi _x(m') \, m'} = \frac{ \Gamma (\alpha _x+m) / (m-1)!}{ \Gamma (\alpha _x+m') / (m'-1)!} = \prod _{j=m'}^{m-1} \frac{j+\alpha _x}{j} \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

which lies inside the interval \([1,\exp \, (\sum _{j=m'}^{m-1}\frac{\alpha _x}{j})]\) due to the same reasoning as above. This proves (4.18). \(\square \)

Returning to the proof of Lemma 4.3, let us look at the term inside the second parenthesis in (4.15). The term for \(s=1\) equals

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{ \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy})\, m\left( \alpha _y + \ell -m \right) }{ \mathfrak {c}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy},\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_1y}) } = \frac{ m\left( \alpha _y + \ell -m \right) }{ r_{\alpha ,k} (\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy},\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_1y}) } = \frac{ \alpha _{y}+\ell -m }{ \left( \alpha _{x_1}+\sigma _{x_1}\right) c_{xx_1} } \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Similarly, by (4.16), the term for \(s=t\) becomes

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{ \pi _{x}(m)\,\pi _{x_{t-1}}(\sigma _{x_{t-1}})\, m }{ \pi _{x}(m-1)\, \pi _{x_{t-1}} (\sigma _{x_{t-1}}+1) \, (\sigma _{x_{t-1}}+1) \, c_{x_{t-1}y} } \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

and the term for \(s \in \llbracket 2,t-1 \rrbracket \) becomes

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{ \pi _{x}(m)\,\pi _{x_{s-1}}(\sigma _{x_{s-1}})\, m\left( \alpha _{y}+\ell -m\right) }{ \pi _{x}(m-1)\, \pi _{x_{s-1}}(\sigma _{x_{s-1}}+1) \, (\sigma _{x_{s-1}}+1) \left( \alpha _{x_{s}}+\sigma _{x_{s}}\right) c_{x_{s-1}x_{s}} } \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Combining the last three displayed identities, the summation inside the second parenthesis in (4.15) equals

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned}&\frac{ \alpha _{y}+\ell -m }{ \left( \alpha _{x_1}+\sigma _{x_1}\right) c_{xx_1} } + \frac{ \pi _{x}(m)\,\pi _{x_{t-1}}(\sigma _{x_{t-1}})\, m }{ \pi _{x}(m-1)\, \pi _{x_{t-1}} (\sigma _{x_{t-1}}+1) \, (\sigma _{x_{t-1}}+1) \, c_{x_{t-1}y} } \\&+ \sum _{s=2}^{t-1} \frac{ \pi _{x}(m)\,\pi _{x_{s-1}}(\sigma _{x_{s-1}})\, m\left( \alpha _{y}+\ell -m\right) }{ \pi _{x}(m-1)\, \pi _{x_{s-1}}(\sigma _{x_{s-1}}+1) \, (\sigma _{x_{s-1}}+1) \left( \alpha _{x_{s}}+\sigma _{x_{s}}\right) c_{x_{s-1}x_{s}} } \; \text {.}\end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$
(4.19)

The first term in (4.19) is easily bounded by (cf. (2.5))

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\alpha _y + \ell -m }{ \left( \alpha _{x_1}+\sigma _{x_1}\right) c_{xx_1} } \le \frac{m \left( \alpha _y+\ell -m\right) }{c_\textrm{min}} \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

where we used that \(\sigma _{x_1} \ge 1\). By (4.16), the second term in (4.19) reads as

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{ \alpha _x + m-1 }{ (\alpha _{x_{t-1}} + \sigma _{x_{t-1}}) \, c_{x_{t-1} {y}} } \le {\frac{m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) }{c_\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{min}} \, \frac{\alpha _x + m-1}{m} \le \frac{m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) }{c_\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{min}} \, (1+\alpha _\textrm{max}) } \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

where we used \(\sigma _{x_{t-1}} \ge 1\) and \(\alpha _y + \ell -m \ge \alpha _\textrm{min}\) at the first inequality and \(\alpha _x + m-1 \le m(1+\alpha _x)\) at the second inequality. Finally, the third term in (4.19) is similarly dealt with:

$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{s=2}^{t-1} \frac{ \left( \alpha _x + m-1\right) \left( \alpha _{y}+\ell -m\right) }{ (\alpha _{x_{s-1}} + \sigma _{x_{s-1}})\left( \alpha _{x_{s}}+\sigma _{x_{s}}\right) c_{x_{s-1}x_{s}} } \le {\frac{(t-2) \, m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) (1 + \alpha _\textrm{max})}{c_\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{min} } } \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

where we used that \(\sigma _{x_{s-1}}\ge 1\) and \({\alpha _{x_s} + \sigma _{x_s} \ge \alpha _\textrm{min}}\) for all \(s\in \llbracket 2,t-1 \rrbracket \). Therefore, inserting these three bounds and (4.19) into (4.15), we conclude that

$$\begin{aligned} {\nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) } \le {\frac{ t \, m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) (1+\alpha _\textrm{max})}{c_\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{min}} } \left( \sum _{s=1}^t \nabla ^2f(\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{s-1}y},\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_sy}) \right) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

This proves Lemma 4.3. \(\square \)

4.3 Decomposition of gradients: empty sites and few particles

Next, assume (4.11) and that sites \(x_1,\dots ,x_{t-1}\) are empty with respect to \(\sigma \), i.e.,

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma _{x_s}=0 \quad \text {for all} \,\, s \in \llbracket 1,t-1 \rrbracket \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.20)

In this case, we divide the analysis into two parts. In Sect. 4.3, assume that

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha _x \sum _{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{1}{j} \le 1 \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.21)

We construct a path from \(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}\) to \(\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy}\) as follows (see also Fig. 2):

(F):

Move m particles from \(x_{s-1}\) to \(x_{s}\) consecutively, for each \(s\in \llbracket 1,t-1 \rrbracket \). This subpath starts from \(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}\), visits \(\sigma _{m-i,i,\ell -m}^{x_{s-1}x_sy}\) for each \(i\in \llbracket 0,m \rrbracket \) and \(s\in \llbracket 1,t-1 \rrbracket \), and finally arrives at \(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{x_{t-1}y}\).

(S):

Move a particle from \(x_{t-1}\) to y. The resulting configuration is \(\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{x_{t-1}y}\). If \(m=1\), the path is complete.

(B):

If \(m\ge 2\), move the remaining \(m-1\) particles from \(x_s\) to \(x_{s-1}\) consecutively, for each \(s\in \llbracket 1,t-1 \rrbracket \) (backwards). This subpath starts from \(\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{x_{t-1}y}\), visits \(\sigma _{m-i,i-1,\ell -m+1}^{x_{s-1}x_sy}\) for each \(i\in \llbracket 1,m \rrbracket \) and \(s\in \llbracket 1,t-1 \rrbracket \), thereby arrives at \(\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy}\) as desired.

Above, letters (F), (S) and (B) stand for forward, single and backward, respectively. Let \(\Omega ={\Omega _{x,y}^{\ell ,m,\sigma }}\) denote the collection of all edges that appear in the aforementioned path from \(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}\) to \(\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy}\). Recall (4.5). In this subsection, we prove the following lemma:

Lemma 4.5

Assume that (4.11) holds, and suppose that \(\ell \in \llbracket 1,k \rrbracket \), \(m\in \llbracket 1,\ell \rrbracket \) and \(\sigma \in \Xi _{k-\ell }^{V\setminus \{x,y\}}\) satisfy (4.20) and (4.21). Then,

$$\begin{aligned} {\nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) } \le \frac{{6t \, m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) }}{ c_\textrm{min}\, \alpha _\textrm{min}\, \alpha _\textrm{ratio} } \left( \sum _{\{\zeta ,\zeta '\} {\in } \Omega } \nabla ^2f(\zeta ,\zeta ') \right) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Proof

Applying (4.14) along the path constructed above, we have the following upper bound for \({\nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) }\):

$$\begin{aligned} \left( \sum _{\{\eta ,\zeta \} {\in } \Omega } \nabla ^2f(\eta ,\zeta )\right) \left( \sum _{\{\eta ,\zeta \} {\in } \Omega } \frac{ \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy})\, m\left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) }{ \mathfrak {c}(\eta ,\zeta ) } \right) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.22)

Here, we expand the term in the second parenthesis as done exactly in (4.19). Namely, referring to the definition of \(\Omega \) and (4.16), the term inside the second parenthesis in (4.22) expands as

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned}&\sum _{s=1}^{t-1} \sum _{i=0}^{m-1} \frac{ \pi _x(m)\, m\left( \alpha _{y}+\ell -m\right) }{ \pi _{x_{s-1}}(m-i) \, \pi _{x_s} (i ) \, (m-i ) \left( \alpha _{x_s}+i \right) c_{x_{s-1} x_s}} + \frac{ \pi _x(m) }{ \pi _{x_{t-1}}(m )\, c_{x_{t-1}y} } \\&+ \sum _{s=1}^{t-1} \sum _{i=2}^{m} \frac{ \pi _x(m) \, m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) }{ \pi _{x_{s-1}}(m-i ) \, \pi _{x_{s}}(i-1 ) \left( i-1 \right) (\alpha _{x_{s-1}} + m-i ) \, c_{x_s x_{s-1}} } \; \text {.}\end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$
(4.23)

Here, the first double summation indicates the forward jumps in (F), the second single term indicates the jump in (S), and the last double summation indicates the backward jumps in (B). First, consider the first (double) summation in (4.23). According to (4.17) in Lemma 4.4,

$$\begin{aligned} \pi _x(m) \, m \le \alpha _x \, e^{\alpha _x \sum _{j=1}^{m-1} \frac{1}{j}} \; \text {,}\;\;\quad \pi _{x_{s-1}}(m-i) \, (m-i) \ge \alpha _{x_{s-1}} \; \text {,}\;\;\quad \pi _{x_s}(i) \, (\alpha _{x_s}+i) \ge \alpha _{x_s} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Since \(c_{x_{s-1}x_s} \ge c_\textrm{min}\) from (2.5), we may bound the first double summation in (4.23) from above by

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{c_\textrm{min}} \sum _{s=1}^{t-1} \sum _{i=0}^{m-1} \frac{ \alpha _x \, e^{\alpha _x \sum _{j=1}^{m-1} \frac{1}{j} } \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m \right) }{ \alpha _{x_{s-1}} \, \alpha _{x_s} } \le \frac{e \, {m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) (t-1) } }{ c_\textrm{min}\, \alpha _\textrm{min}\, \alpha _\textrm{ratio} } \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(4.24)

where we used that \(\alpha _x \sum _{j=1}^{m-1} \frac{1}{j} \le 1\) from (4.21), \(\alpha _x / \alpha _{x_s} \le \alpha _\textrm{ratio}^{-1}\) from (2.5), and \(\alpha _{x_{s-1}} \ge \alpha _\textrm{min}\). Next, the second term in (4.23) can be similarly bounded as

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{ \pi _x(m) }{ \pi _{x_{t-1}}(m )\, c_{x_{t-1}y} } \le \frac{ \alpha _x \, e^{\alpha _x \sum _{j=1}^{m-1} \frac{1}{j} } }{ \alpha _{x_{t-1}} \, c_\textrm{min}} \le \frac{ e }{ c_\textrm{min} \,\alpha _\textrm{ratio} } {\le \frac{e \, m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) }{c_\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{ratio} \, \alpha _\textrm{min}} } \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.25)

Similarly, the third (double) summation in (4.23) is bounded by

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{c_\textrm{min}} \sum _{s=1}^{t-1} \sum _{i=2}^{m} \frac{ \alpha _x \, e^{\alpha _x \sum _{j=1}^{m-1} \frac{1}{j} } \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m \right) }{ \alpha _{x_{s-1}} \, \alpha _{x_s} } \le \frac{e \, {m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) (t-1) } }{ c_\textrm{min}\, \alpha _\textrm{min}\, \alpha _\textrm{ratio} } \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.26)

Collecting (4.23), (4.24), (4.25) and (4.26), and applying that \(e \le 3\), the term inside the second parenthesis in (4.22) is bounded by

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{{9t \, m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) }}{ c_\textrm{min} \,\alpha _\textrm{min}\, \alpha _\textrm{ratio} } \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

This concludes the proof of Lemma 4.5. \(\square \)

4.4 Decomposition of gradients: empty sites and many particles

In this subsection, we assume that (4.11), (4.20) hold and that (cf. (4.21))

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha _x \sum _{j=1}^{m-1} \frac{1}{j} >1 \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.27)

In this case, constructing a single one-dimensional path is insufficient to bound the term \({\nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) }\) properly. The main reason is that in the inequality in (4.24), we are not able to bound the term \(e^{\alpha _x \sum _{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{1}{j} }\) with a constant in this new regime (4.27). Having in mind that this term essentially comes from the mechanism that all m particles at x move together, we now avoid this obstacle by sending only a limited amount of particles along the path. This replacement costs us an additional \(\alpha _x^{-1}\) term in the upper bound, which is now manageable by the new condition \(\alpha _x \sum _{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{1}{j} >1\); we refer to (4.39) for the exact place where this point is effectively exploited.

Define a new integer \(\widetilde{m}\) as

$$\begin{aligned} \widetilde{m}:=\left\lfloor \frac{m}{2} \right\rfloor \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(4.28)

where \(\lfloor a \rfloor \) is the greatest integer less than or equal to \(a \in \mathbb {R}\). Then, we consider a two-dimensional system of paths from \(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}\) to \(\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy}\) as depicted in Fig. 3.

In Fig. 3, the downward vertical arrows starting from \(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}\) in the top left corner denote the \(\widetilde{m}\) consecutive particle jumps from x to \(x_1\). Then, for \(i \in \llbracket 1,\widetilde{m} \rrbracket \), the i-th bold arrow on the left, from \(\sigma _{m-i,i,\ell -m}^{xx_1y}\) to \(\sigma _{m-i,i,\ell -m}^{xx_{t-1}y}\), denote the movements of i particles from \(x_{s-1}\) to \(x_s\) consecutively for each \(s\in \llbracket 2,t-1 \rrbracket \), as in mechanism (F) in Sect. 4.3. Next, the middle horizontal arrows denote the single particle jump from \(x_{t-1}\) to y, corresponding to mechanism (S) in Sect. 4.3. The bold arrows in the right part denote the movements of \(i-1\) particles from \(x_{t-1}\) back to \(x_1\) for each \(i \in \llbracket 2,\widetilde{m} \rrbracket \) (backwards), as in mechanism (B) in Sect. 4.3. Finally, the upward vertical arrows below \(\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy}\) in the top right corner denote the \(\widetilde{m}-1\) consecutive particle jumps from \(x_1\) to x. The values attached to the arrows, roughly speaking, play the role of weights (encoded as the value of the flow, see below) for each move.

As before, let \(\Omega = \Omega _{x,y}^{\ell ,m,\sigma }\) be the collection of all edges that appear in Fig. 3. The objective of this subsection is to prove the following lemma:

Lemma 4.6

Assume that (4.11) holds, and suppose that \(\ell \in \llbracket 1,k \rrbracket \), \(m\in \llbracket 1,\ell \rrbracket \) and \(\sigma \in \Xi _{k-\ell }^{V\setminus \{x,y\}}\) satisfy (4.20) and (4.27). Then, we have

$$\begin{aligned} {\nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) } \le \frac{ {4t \, m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) } \, 6^{\alpha _\textrm{max} }}{\alpha _\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{ratio} \, c_\textrm{min}} \left( \sum _{\{\zeta ,\zeta '\} {\in } \Omega } \nabla ^2f(\zeta ,\zeta ') \right) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

To derive an estimate similar to (4.22) that works also in this two-dimensional setting, we make use of some potential-theoretic arguments. We present the statements as concisely as possible and refer the readers to, e.g., [BH15, Chapter 7] or [Kim25, Appendix A] for more details about the objects used in this subsection.

Only in this subsection, we restrict the original system \(\textrm{SIP}_k(G,\alpha )\) to the subset of the configurations that appear in Fig. 3, which we denote by \(\hat{\Xi }\). Moreover, let \(\mathcal {D}_{\Omega }(g)\) denote the (normalized) Dirichlet form in \({\hat{\Xi }_k}\) given by (cf. (4.8))

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {D}_\Omega (g)= \frac{1}{2} \sum _{\eta ,\zeta \in {\hat{\Xi }_k}} \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\eta )\, r_{\alpha ,k}(\eta ,\zeta ) \left( g(\zeta )-g(\eta )\right) ^2 = \sum _{\{\eta ,\zeta \} {\in } \Omega } \nabla ^2g(\eta ,\zeta )\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.29)

For non-empty disjoint subsets A, B of \(\Omega \), introduce (e.g., [BH15, Theorem 7.33] or [Kim25, Proposition A.1])

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{cap}(A,B):=\inf \left\{ \mathcal {D}_\Omega (g): g\in \mathbb {R}^{{\hat{\Xi }_k}}\ \text {such that}\ g = 1\ \text {on}\ A\ \text {and}\ g = 0\ \text {on}\ B\right\} \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

the capacity between A and B. In particular, for \(A=\{\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}\}\) and \(B=\{\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy}\}\), we have, after shifting and renormalizing,

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{cap}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy},\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy})\, (f(\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy})-f(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}))^{2} \le \mathcal {D}_\Omega (f) \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(4.30)

where \(f\in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\) is the function which we fixed at the beginning of Sect. 4.

Proof of Lemma 4.6

Let us describe the flow \(\varphi \) on \({\hat{\Xi }_k}\) represented in Fig. 3, where we recall that “flow on \(\Omega \)” refers to any antisymmetric real-valued function on \({\hat{\Xi }_k} \times {\hat{\Xi }_k}\). Due to this antisymmetry, we shall omit to specify the value of \(\varphi \) when exchanging the arguments. Hence, vertically, define

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} \varphi (\sigma _{m-i,i,\ell -m}^{xx_1y},\sigma _{m-i-1,i+1,\ell -m}^{xx_1y})&:=\frac{ \sum _{j=i+1}^{\widetilde{m}} \frac{1}{j} }{ \sum _{j=1}^{\widetilde{m}} \frac{1}{j} }\; \text {,}\;\; & \quad \text {for}\,\, i\in \llbracket 0,\widetilde{m}-1 \rrbracket \; \text {,}\;\;\\ \varphi (\sigma _{m-i-1,i,\ell -m+1}^{x{x_1}y},\sigma _{m-i,i-1,\ell -m+1}^{x{x_1}y})&:=\frac{ \sum _{j=i+1}^{\widetilde{m}} \frac{1}{j} }{ \sum _{j=1}^{\widetilde{m}} \frac{1}{j} }\; \text {,}\;\; & \quad \text {for}\,\, i\in \llbracket 1,\widetilde{m}-1 \rrbracket \; \text {.}\end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$
(4.31)

Horizontally, define \(\varphi \) as a constant flow at each i-th vertical level from \(\sigma _{m-i,i,\ell -m}^{xx_1y}\) to \(\sigma _{m-i,i-1,\ell -m+1}^{xx_1y}\) with value

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\frac{1}{i}}{ \sum _{j=1}^{\widetilde{m}} \frac{1}{j} }\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \text {for each}\,\, i \in \llbracket 1,\widetilde{m} \rrbracket \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.32)

The function \(\varphi \) is a unit flow from \(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}\) to \(\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy}\). Indeed, letting \((\textrm{div}\,\varphi )(\eta )\) denote the net divergence at \(\eta \), i.e., \((\textrm{div}\,\varphi )(\eta ) :=\sum _{\zeta \in \Omega } \varphi (\eta ,\zeta )\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} (\textrm{div}\,\varphi )(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}) = 1 \; \text {,}\;\;\quad (\textrm{div}\,\varphi )(\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy}) = -1 \quad \text {and} \quad \textrm{div}\,\varphi = 0 \quad \text {otherwise} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Thus, the standard Thomson principle (e.g., [BH15, Theorem 7.37] or [Kim25, Proposition A.2]) indicates that

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{cap}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy},\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy})\ge \frac{1}{\Vert \varphi \Vert ^{2}} \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(4.33)

where \(\Vert \varphi \Vert ^2\) is the (square) flow norm given by

$$\begin{aligned} \Vert \varphi \Vert ^2 :=\sum _{ {\{ \eta ,\zeta \} } \in \Omega } \frac{\varphi (\eta ,\zeta )^2}{\mathfrak {c}(\eta ,\zeta )} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Hence, by (4.5), (4.30) and (4.33), we obtain

$$\begin{aligned} {\nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) } \le \mathcal {D}_\Omega (f)\, \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy})\,m\left( \alpha _y+\ell -m\right) \Vert \varphi \Vert ^2 \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.34)

Next, we upper bound the right-hand side of (4.34), except \(\mathcal {D}_\Omega (f)\). For this purpose, we decompose \(\varphi = \varphi _1 + \varphi _2\), where \(\varphi _1\) (resp. \(\varphi _2\)) indicates the vertical (resp. horizontal) part of \(\varphi \). First, the (square) flow norm of the vertical part \(\varphi _1\) reads as

$$\begin{aligned} \Vert \varphi _1\Vert ^2 = \sum _{i=0}^{\widetilde{m}-1} \frac{( \sum _{j=i+1}^{\widetilde{m}}\frac{1}{j} )^{2}/( \sum _{j=1}^{\widetilde{m}}\frac{1}{j} )^{2}}{ \mathfrak {c}(\sigma _{m-i,i,\ell -m}^{xx_{1}y},\sigma _{m-i-1,i+1,\ell -m}^{xx_{1}y}) } +\sum _{i=1}^{\widetilde{m}-1} \frac{( \sum _{j=i+1}^{\widetilde{m}} \frac{1}{j} )^{2}/( \sum _{j=1}^{\widetilde{m}}\frac{1}{j} )^{2}}{ \mathfrak {c}(\sigma _{m-i-1,i,\ell -m+1}^{x{x_1}y},\sigma _{m-i,i-1,\ell -m+1}^{x{x_1}y}) }\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Since the two numerators are clearly bounded by 1, we further get

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned}&\mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy})\,m\left( \alpha _y+\ell -m\right) \Vert \varphi _1\Vert ^2 \\&\le \sum _{i=0}^{\widetilde{m}-1} \frac{ \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy})\,m\left( \alpha _y+\ell -m\right) }{ \mathfrak {c}(\sigma _{m-i,i,\ell -m}^{xx_{1}y},\sigma _{m-i-1,i+1,\ell -m}^{xx_{1}y}) } +\sum _{i=1}^{\widetilde{m}-1} \frac{ \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy})\,m\left( \alpha _y+\ell -m\right) }{ \mathfrak {c}(\sigma _{m-i-1,i,\ell -m+1}^{x{x_1}y},\sigma _{m-i,i-1,\ell -m+1}^{x{x_1}y}) }\; \text {.}\end{aligned}\nonumber \\ \end{aligned}$$
(4.35)

By the definitions in (4.8) and (4.16), the summand in the first summation in (4.35) becomes

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{ \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy})\,m\left( \alpha _y+\ell -m\right) }{ \mu _{\alpha ,k} (\sigma _{m-i,i,\ell -m}^{xx_{1}y}) \, r_{\alpha ,k} (\sigma _{m-i,i,\ell -m}^{xx_{1}y},\sigma _{m-i-1,i+1,\ell -m}^{xx_{1}y}) } = \frac{ \pi _x(m)\, m \left( \alpha _y + \ell - m\right) }{ \pi _x(m-i)\, \pi _{x_1}(i) \left( m-i\right) \left( \alpha _{x_1} + i \right) c_{xx_1} } \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Thus, by applying (4.17) and (4.18), the first summation in (4.35) can be bounded from above by

$$\begin{aligned}&\sum _{i=0}^{\widetilde{m}-1} \frac{ \pi _x(m)\, m \left( \alpha _y + \ell - m\right) }{ \pi _x(m-i)\, \pi _{x_1}(i) \left( m-i\right) \left( \alpha _{x_1} + i \right) c_{xx_1} } \\&\le \sum _{i=0}^{\widetilde{m}-1} e^{\alpha _x \sum _{j=m-i}^{m-1}\frac{1}{j} } \, \frac{\alpha _y + \ell -m}{ \alpha _{x_1} \, c_\textrm{min} } \le \frac{{\tilde{m} \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) }}{\alpha _\textrm{min} \, c_\textrm{min} } \, e^{\alpha _x \sum _{j=m- \widetilde{m}+1}^{m-1}\frac{1}{j} } \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Noting that \(\sum _{j=m-\widetilde{m}+1}^{m-1}\frac{1}{j} \le \log \frac{m-1}{m-\widetilde{m}} \le \log 2\) (cf. (4.28)), we deduce that

$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{i=0}^{\widetilde{m}-1} \frac{ \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy})\,m\left( \alpha _y+\ell -m\right) }{ \mathfrak {c}(\sigma _{m-i,i,\ell -m}^{xx_{1}y},\sigma _{m-i-1,i+1,\ell -m}^{xx_{1}y}) } \le \frac{ {\tilde{m} \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) } \, 2^{\alpha _\textrm{max}}}{\alpha _\textrm{min} \, c_\textrm{min} } \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.36)

Similarly, the second summation in (4.35) can be bounded by the same value in the right-hand side of (4.36). Thus, collecting (4.35) and (4.36) along with \(2 \tilde{m} \le m\), we deduce

$$\begin{aligned} \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}) \, m \left( \alpha _y+\ell -m\right) \Vert \varphi _1 \Vert ^2 \le \frac{{m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) 2^{\alpha _\textrm{max}}}}{\alpha _\textrm{min} \, c_\textrm{min} } \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.37)

Next, the horizontal part \(\mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}) \, m \left( \alpha _y+\ell -m\right) \Vert \varphi _2\Vert ^2\) becomes

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned}&\sum _{i=1}^{\widetilde{m}} \sum _{s=2}^{t-1} \sum _{i'=0}^{i-1} \frac{ \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}) \,m \left( \alpha _y+\ell -m\right) \frac{1}{i^{2}}/( \sum _{j=1}^{\widetilde{m}}\frac{1}{j} )^{2} }{ \mathfrak {c}(\sigma _{m-i,i-i',i',\ell -m}^{xx_{s-1}x_{s}y},\sigma _{m-i,i-i'-1,i'+1,\ell -m}^{xx_{s-1}x_{s}y}) } \\&+ \sum _{i=1}^{\widetilde{m}}\frac{ \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}) \,m \left( \alpha _y+\ell -m\right) \frac{1}{i^{2}}/( \sum _{j=1}^{\widetilde{m}}\frac{1}{j} )^{2}}{\mathfrak {c}(\sigma _{m-i,i,\ell -m}^{xx_{t-1}y},\sigma _{m-i,i-1,\ell -m+1}^{xx_{t-1}y})} \\&+ \sum _{i=2}^{\widetilde{m}} \sum _{s=2}^{t-1} \sum _{i'=1}^{i-1}\frac{ \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}) \,m \left( \alpha _y+\ell -m\right) \frac{1}{i^{2}}/( \sum _{j=1}^{\widetilde{m}}\frac{1}{j} )^{2}}{\mathfrak {c}(\sigma _{m-i,i-i'-1,i',\ell -m+1}^{xx_{s-1}x_sy},\sigma _{m-i,i-i',i'-1,\ell -m+1}^{xx_{s-1}x_sy})} \; \text {.}\end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$
(4.38)

According to (4.8) and (4.16), the first (triple) summation in (4.38) equals

$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{i=1}^{\widetilde{m}} \sum _{s=2}^{t-1} \sum _{i'=0}^{i-1} \frac{ \pi _{x}(m) \, m \left( \alpha _{y}+\ell -m\right) \frac{1}{i^{2}}/( \sum _{j=1}^{\widetilde{m}}\frac{1}{j} )^{2} }{ \pi _{x}(m-i) \, \pi _{x_{s-1}}(i-i' ) \, \pi _{x_{s}}(i' ) \left( i-i' \right) \left( \alpha _{x_{s}}+i' \right) \, c_{x_{s-1}x_{s}} } \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

By Lemma 4.4, \(\pi _{x_{s-1}}(i-i') \, (i-i') \ge \alpha _{x_{s-1}} \ge \alpha _\textrm{min}\), \(\pi _{x_s} (i') \, (\alpha _{x_s} + i') \ge \alpha _\textrm{min}\), and

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\pi _x(m)\,m}{\pi _x(m-i)} \le (m-i) \, e^{\alpha _x \sum _{j=m-i}^{m-1} \frac{1}{j}} \le m \, e^{\alpha _x \sum _{j=m-i}^{m-1} \frac{1}{j}} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Substituting these to the penultimate display, along with \(c_{x_{s-1}x_s} \ge c_\textrm{min}\), the first (triple) summation in (4.38) is upper bounded by

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{{m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) }}{\alpha _\textrm{min}^2 \, c_\textrm{min} } \sum _{i=1}^{\widetilde{m}} \sum _{s=2}^{t-1} \sum _{i'=0}^{i-1} \frac{e^{\alpha _x \sum _{j=m-i}^{m-1}\frac{1}{j} }}{i^{2} \, ( \sum _{j=1}^{\widetilde{m}}\frac{1}{j} )^{2}} \le \frac{ {m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) (t-2)} \, e^{\alpha _x \sum _{j=m-\widetilde{m}}^{m-1}\frac{1}{j} }}{ {\alpha _\textrm{min}^2 \, c_\textrm{min} } \sum _{j=1}^{\widetilde{m}}\frac{1}{j} } \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Since \(\sum _{j=m-\widetilde{m}}^{m-1}\frac{1}{j} \le 1+\log 2 < \log 6\) and \(\sum _{j=1}^{\widetilde{m}}\frac{1}{j} \ge \frac{1}{2} \sum _{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{1}{j}\) (cf. (4.28)), we may further bound the right-hand side with

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{2 \, {m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) (t-2) } \, e^{\alpha _x \log 6 }}{\alpha _\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{ratio} \, c_\textrm{min} \, \alpha _x \sum _{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{1}{j} } \le \frac{2 \, {m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) (t-2) } \, 6^{\alpha _\textrm{max} }}{\alpha _\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{ratio} \, c_\textrm{min}} \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(4.39)

where in the inequality we used condition (4.27). Similarly, the third (triple) summation in (4.38) is bounded by the same value in the right-hand side of (4.39). Finally, by the same ideas, the second summation in (4.38) is bounded from above by

$$\begin{aligned} { \frac{m}{\alpha _\textrm{min} \, c_\textrm{min}} \sum _{i=1}^{\tilde{m}} \frac{6^{\alpha _\textrm{max}}}{i^2 \left( \sum _{j=1}^{\tilde{m}}\frac{1}{j}\right) ^2} \le \frac{ m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) 6^{\alpha _\textrm{max}}}{\alpha _\textrm{min}^2 \, c_\textrm{min} \sum _{j=1}^{\tilde{m}}\frac{1}{j} } \le \frac{ 2 \, m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) \, 6^{\alpha _\textrm{max}}}{\alpha _\textrm{min} \, c_\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{ratio} } \; \text {.}}\nonumber \\ \end{aligned}$$
(4.40)

At the first inequality we used \(i^2 \ge i\) and \(\alpha _y + \ell -m \ge \alpha _\textrm{min}\), and at the second inequality we used \(\alpha _\textrm{min} \ge \alpha _\textrm{ratio} \, \alpha _x\) and (4.27). Collecting (4.38), (4.39) and (4.40), we obtain

$$\begin{aligned} \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}) \,m \left( \alpha _y+\ell -m \right) \Vert \varphi _2\Vert ^2 \le \frac{{(4t-6) \, m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) 6^{\alpha _\textrm{max} }}}{\alpha _\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{ratio} \, c_\textrm{min}} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.41)

The constant 4t appears because the term in (4.39) should be counted twice to deal with the first and third lines in (4.38), and the term in (4.40) corresponds to the second line in (4.38), thus \( 2 \times 2(t-2) + 2 = 4t-6\). Since \(\varphi _1\) and \(\varphi _2\) have disjoint supports, we have \(\Vert \varphi \Vert ^2 = \Vert \varphi _1\Vert ^2 + \Vert \varphi _2\Vert ^2\). Therefore, by (4.37) and (4.41), since \(\alpha _\textrm{ratio} \le 1\), we conclude that

$$\begin{aligned} \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}) \, m \left( \alpha _y+\ell -m \right) \Vert \varphi \Vert ^2 \le \frac{{4t \, m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) 6^{\alpha _\textrm{max}} }}{\alpha _\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{ratio} \, c_\textrm{min}} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(4.42)

Collecting (4.29), (4.34) and (4.42), we arrive at conclusion of Lemma 4.6. \(\square \)

4.5 General case and proof of Theorem 3.2

Now, we handle the general case without the restrictions imposed in the previous four subsections. The idea is to send a particle from x to y along the sequence \(x=x_{0},x_{1},\ldots ,x_{t}=y\) by obeying the following two criteria:

  • If consecutive sites are occupied by particles in \(\sigma \), send a single particle along these occupied sites as explained in Sect. 4.2.

  • If consecutive sites are empty for the configuration \(\sigma \), follow the mechanism explained in Sects. 4.3 or 4.4 depending on the size of the stack of particles just before these empty sites.

To state this procedure in a rigorous manner, we decompose \({\llbracket 0,t \rrbracket }\) as

$$\begin{aligned} & \llbracket {0},b_{1}-1 \rrbracket \cup \llbracket b_{1},a_{2}-1 \rrbracket \cup \cdots \cup {\llbracket b_{r-1},a_{r}-1 \rrbracket \cup \llbracket a_{r},t \rrbracket } =:O_{1} \cup E_{1} \cup \cdots \nonumber \\ & \quad \cup {E_{r-1} \cup O_{r}} \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(4.43)

where \(1\le b_{1}<a_{2}<b_{2}<\cdots< b_{r-1}<a_{r} \le t\) and

$$\begin{aligned} {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \sigma _{x_{s}} \ge 1 & \text {if}\quad s\in O_{j} \ \text {for some} \ j\in \llbracket 1,r \rrbracket { {, \ \text {except for} \ s=t}} \\ \sigma _{x_{s}}=0 & \text {if}\quad s\in E_{j} \ \text {for some} \ j\in \llbracket 1,{r-1} \rrbracket \; \text {.}\end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$

Here, letter O (resp. E) stands for occupied (resp. empty). Also, let \(a_{1} :=0\) and \({b_r} :=t+1\). Now, the path from \(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}\) to \(\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy}\) is constructed as follows. Refer to Fig. 4 for a visual presentation of this recursive procedure.

[I]:

Along \(x_{a_j}, \ldots , x_{b_j-1}\) for \(j\in \llbracket 1,r \rrbracket \): send a single particle from \(x_{a_{j}}\) to \(x_{b_{j}-1}\) consecutively, as explained in Sect. 4.2. This would be a path from \(\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{a_j}y}\) to \(\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{b_j-1}y}\), where the first configuration for \(j=1\) is \(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}\) and the second configuration for \(j=r\) is \(\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy}\). Notice that this step is omitted if \(b_j = a_j+1\).

[II]:

Along \(x_{b_j-1}, \ldots , x_{a_{j+1}}\) for \(j\in \llbracket 1,{r-1} \rrbracket \) if

$$\begin{aligned}\alpha _{x_{b_j-1}} \sum _{j=1}^{\sigma _{x_{b_j-1}}}\frac{1}{j} \le 1 \;:\end{aligned}$$

we proceed as in Sect. 4.3; move all \(\sigma _{x_{b_{j}-1}}+1\) particles from \(x_{b_{j}-1}\) to \(x_{a_{j+1}-1}\) consecutively, move a single particle from \(x_{a_{j+1}-1}\) to \(x_{a_{j+1}}\), and then move back the remaining \(\sigma _{x_{b_{j}-1}}\) particles at \(x_{a_{j+1}-1}\) to \(x_{b_{j}-1}\) backwards. This is a path from \(\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{b_j-1}y}\) to \(\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{a_{j+1}}y}\).

[III]:

Along \(x_{b_j-1}, \ldots , x_{a_{j+1}}\) for \(j\in \llbracket 1,{r-1} \rrbracket \) if

$$\begin{aligned}\alpha _{x_{b_j-1}} \sum _{j=1}^{\sigma _{x_{b_j-1}}}\frac{1}{j} > 1 \;:\end{aligned}$$

we proceed as in Sect. 4.4; move each \(i \in \llbracket 1, \lfloor (\sigma _{x_{b_{j}-1}}+1)/2\rfloor \rrbracket \) particles from \(x_{b_{j}-1}\) to \(x_{a_{j+1}-1}\) consecutively, move a single particle from \(x_{a_{j+1}-1}\) to \(x_{a_{j+1}}\), and then move back the remaining \(i-1\) particles at \(x_{a_{j+1}-1}\) to \(x_{b_{j}-1}\) backwards. As in [II], this gives a path from \(\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{b_j-1}y}\) to \(\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{a_{j+1}}y}\).

Again, denote by \({\Omega _{x,y}^{\ell ,m,\sigma }}\) the collection of all edges that appear along the paths.

Proof of (4.3) First, we bound \({\nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) }\) from above by intermediate quadratic forms, on which we can apply the special results in Sects. 4.1–4.4. Recall the decomposition (4.43). The path \(x \rightarrow y\) can be decomposed into (at most) \(2r-1\) steps

$$\begin{aligned} x = x_0 \rightarrow x_{b_1-1} \rightarrow x_{a_2} \rightarrow x_{b_2-1} \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow x_{a_r} \rightarrow x_t = y \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

where step \(x_{a_j} \rightarrow x_{b_j-1}\) must be omitted if \(b_j = a_j +1\). In this regard, we may decompose as

$$\begin{aligned} \Omega _{x,y}^{\ell ,m,\sigma } = \bigcup _{j=1}^r \Omega _{x_{a_j},x_{b_j-1}}^{\ell ,m,\sigma } \cup \bigcup _{j=1}^{r-1} \Omega _{x_{b_j-1},x_{a_{j+1}}}^{\ell ,m,\sigma } \end{aligned}$$
(4.44)

where, with a slight abuse of notation, each \(\Omega _{x_{a_j},x_{b_j-1}}^{\ell ,m,\sigma }\) (resp. \(\Omega _{x_{b_j-1},x_{a_{j+1}}}^{\ell ,m,\sigma }\)) collects the edges that are obtained within the step \(x_{a_j} \rightarrow x_{b_j-1}\) (resp. \(x_{b_j-1} \rightarrow x_{a_{j+1}}\)). We will upper bound the term \({\nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) }\) with

$$\begin{aligned} {\nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{a_1}y}; x_{a_1},x_{b_1-1}) }, {\nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{b_1-1}y}; x_{b_1-1},x_{a_2}) }, \dots , {\nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{a_r}y}; x_{a_r},x_{b_r-1}) } \end{aligned}$$

where \(x_{a_1}=x_0=x\) and \(x_{b_r-1}=x_t=y\). The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to the difference of f along these steps implies that

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned}&(f(\sigma _{m-1,\ell -m+1}^{xy})-f(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}))^{2} \le \\&t \left( \sum _{j=1}^r \frac{(f(\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{b_j-1}y})-f(\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{a_j}y}))^{2}}{b_j - a_j - 1} + \sum _{j=1}^{r-1} \frac{(f(\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{a_{j+1}}y})-f(\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{b_j-1}y}))^{2}}{a_{j+1} - b_j +1} \right) \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$

where it was used that \(\sum _{j=1}^r (b_j-a_j-1) + \sum _{j=1}^{r-1} (a_{j+1}-b_j+1) = b_r - a_1 - 1 = t\). Here, the first summation in \(j \in \llbracket 1,r \rrbracket \) should be run over those j such that \(b_j > a_j+1\). Substituting (4.5) to this inequality, we obtain that

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} \nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) \le t \, \Bigg ( \sum _{j=1}^r&\frac{ \mathfrak {c}_K (\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) \, \nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{a_j}y}; x_{a_j},x_{b_j-1}) }{\mathfrak {c}_K(\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{a_j}y};x_{a_j},x_{b_j-1}) (b_j-a_j-1)} \\&+ \sum _{j=1}^{r-1} \frac{ \mathfrak {c}_K (\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) \, \nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{b_j-1}y}; x_{b_j-1},x_{a_{j+1}}) }{\mathfrak {c}_K(\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{b_j-1}y};x_{b_j-1},x_{a_{j+1}}) (a_{j+1} - b_j + 1)} \Bigg ) \, \; \text {.}\end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$
(4.45)

According to the results in Lemmas 4.2, 4.3, 4.5, and 4.6, for each \(j \in \llbracket 1,r \rrbracket \) we have

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned}&\frac{\mathfrak {c}_K (\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) \, \nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{a_j}y}; x_{a_j},x_{b_j-1}) }{\mathfrak {c}_K(\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{a_j}y};x_{a_j},x_{b_j-1}) (b_j-a_j-1)} \le \\&\frac{ \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}) \, 9 \, m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) 6^{\alpha _\textrm{max} }}{ \mu _{\alpha ,k} (\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{a_j}y}) \, \alpha _\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{ratio} \, c_\textrm{min}} \left( \sum _{\{\zeta ,\zeta '\} {\in } \Omega _{x_{a_j},x_{b_j-1}}^{\ell ,m,\sigma }} \nabla ^2f(\zeta ,\zeta ') \right) \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$

and for each \(j \in \llbracket 1,j-1 \rrbracket \) we have

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned}&\frac{\mathfrak {c}_K (\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) \, \nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{b_j-1}y}; x_{b_j-1},x_{a_{j+1}}) }{\mathfrak {c}_K(\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{b_j-1}y};x_{b_j-1},x_{a_{j+1}}) (a_{j+1}-b_j+1)} \le \\&\frac{ \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}) \, 9 \, m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) 6^{\alpha _\textrm{max} }}{ \mu _{\alpha ,k} (\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{b_j-1}y}) \, \alpha _\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{ratio} \, c_\textrm{min}} \left( \sum _{\{\zeta ,\zeta '\} {\in } \Omega _{x_{b_j-1},x_{a_{j+1}}}^{\ell ,m,\sigma }} \nabla ^2f(\zeta ,\zeta ') \right) \; \text {.}\end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$

Indeed, the term t in the right-hand sides of the lemmas cancels out with \(b_j-a_j-1\) or \(a_{j+1}-b_j+1\), and the term \(m \, (\alpha _y + \ell -m)\) cancels out with the \(\mathfrak {c}_K\)-term except the \(\mu _{\alpha ,k}\) part, thus we obtain the right-hand sides in the last two displays.

Next, we estimate the ratios of \(\mu _{\alpha ,k}\) that appear in the right-hand sides of the last two displays. If \(a_j \ne x,y\), then

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned}&\frac{ \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}) \, m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) }{ \mu _{\alpha ,k} (\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{a_j}y}) } = \frac{\pi _x(m) \, m \, \pi _{x_{a_j}} (\sigma _{x_{a_j}}) \, \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) }{\pi _x(m-1) \,\pi _{x_{a_j}}(\sigma _{x_{a_j}}+1) } \\&= (\alpha _x + m-1) \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) \, \frac{\sigma _{x_{a_j}}+1}{\sigma _{x_{a_j}}+\alpha _{x_{a_j}}} \le 2 \, (1+\alpha _\textrm{max}) \, k \, (\alpha _\textrm{max} + k-1) \; \text {.}\end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$

Here, we used \(\sigma _{x_{a_j}} \ge 1\) (which implies \(\frac{\sigma _{x_{a_j}}+1}{\sigma _{x_{a_j}}+\alpha _{x_{a_j}}} \le 2\)), \(\alpha _y + \ell -m \le \alpha _\textrm{max} + k-1\), and \(\alpha _x + m-1 \le (1+\alpha _\textrm{max}) \, k\). The same bound trivially holds if \(a_j = x\) or \(a_j = y\), and similarly,

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{ \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}) \, m \left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) }{ \mu _{\alpha ,k} (\sigma _{m-1,1,\ell -m}^{xx_{b_j-1}y}) } \le 2 \, (1+\alpha _\textrm{max}) \, k \, (\alpha _\textrm{max} + k-1) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Substituting the last four estimates to (4.45), we arrive at

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} \nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y)&\le \frac{ 18t \, (1+\alpha _\textrm{max}) \, k \, (\alpha _\textrm{max} + k-1) \, 6^{\alpha _\textrm{max} }}{ \alpha _\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{ratio} \, c_\textrm{min}} \\&\left( \sum _{j=1}^r \sum _{\{\zeta ,\zeta '\} {\in } \Omega _{x_{a_j},x_{b_j-1}}^{\ell ,m,\sigma }} \nabla ^2f(\zeta ,\zeta ') + \sum _{j=1}^{r-1} \sum _{\{\zeta ,\zeta '\} {\in } \Omega _{x_{b_j-1},x_{a_{j+1}}}^{\ell ,m,\sigma }} \nabla ^2f(\zeta ,\zeta ') \right) \, \; \text {.}\end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$

Applying \(t \le \textrm{diam}(G)\), \(1+\alpha _\textrm{max} \le e^{\alpha _\textrm{max}}\), and (4.44), we get, for any \(\ell \in \llbracket 1,k \rrbracket \), \(m\in \llbracket 1,\ell \rrbracket \) and \(\sigma \in \Xi _{k-\ell }^{V\setminus \{x,y\}}\),

$$\begin{aligned} {\nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) } \le \frac{{18 \, k \left( \alpha _\textrm{max}+k-1\right) \textrm{diam}(G) \, (6e)^{\alpha _\textrm{max} }}}{\alpha _\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{ratio} \, c_\textrm{min}} \sum _{\{\zeta ,\zeta '\} {\in } {\Omega _{x,y}^{\ell ,m,\sigma }}} \nabla ^2f(\zeta ,\zeta ') \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Along with (4.6), this implies

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {E}_K^{x,y} \le \frac{{18 \, k \left( \alpha _\textrm{max}+k-1\right) \textrm{diam}(G) \, (6e)^{\alpha _\textrm{max} }}}{\alpha _\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{ratio} \, c_\textrm{min}} \sum _{\ell =1}^k \sum _{m=1}^\ell \sum _{\sigma \in \Xi _{k-\ell }^{V\setminus \{x,y\}}} \sum _{\{\zeta ,\zeta '\} {\in } {\Omega _{x,y}^{\ell ,m,\sigma }}} \nabla ^2f(\zeta ,\zeta ') \; \text {.}\nonumber \\ \end{aligned}$$
(4.46)

Thus, to conclude the proof of (4.3), according to (4.9) and (4.46), we only need to count the number of overlaps, i.e., the number of triples \((\ell ,m,\sigma )\) in (4.46) that produce the same Dirichlet summand \(\nabla ^2f(\eta ,\eta -\delta _z+\delta _w)\) in (4.9).

To this end, we fix \(x<y\), the sequence \(x=x_0, x_1, \dots , x_t=y\) with \(t\in \llbracket 1,\textrm{diam}(G) \rrbracket \), and a triple \((\eta ,z,w)\) in (4.9), and then count the number of triples \((\ell ,m,\sigma )\) that produce the term \(\nabla ^2f(\eta ,\eta -\delta _z + \delta _w)\) in (4.46) by distinguishing three cases.

(Case 1) There exists no \(s \in \llbracket 1,t \rrbracket \) for which \(\{ x_{s-1},x_s \} = \{ z,w \}\):

Clearly, in this case the overlap is zero.

(Case 2) There exists \(s\in \llbracket 1,t \rrbracket \) for which \(( x_{s-1},x_s )= (z,w)\):

By the minimal property of the path length t, there is no other \(s' \in \llbracket 1,t \rrbracket \) such that \(\{x_{s'-1},x_{s'}\}=\{z,w\}\), thus we may only focus on the particle jump \(z \rightarrow w\). First, we observe that if \(t=1\), then the overlap is at most one. Thus, we only focus on the case \(t \ge 2\). We further divide into three subcases according to the type—[I], [II] and [III]—of the particle jump \(z \rightarrow w\).

  • (Case 2.I) \(\eta \rightarrow \eta -\delta _z + \delta _w\) belongs to type [I]: In this case, recalling that the overall mechanism is to move a single particle from x to y along the path, we obtain

    $$\begin{aligned} \eta = \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy} + ( \delta _z - \delta _x ) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

    This observation characterizes \((\ell ,m,\sigma )\) uniquely from the configuration \(\eta \). Hence, in this subcase, there is exactly one overlap.

  • (Case 2.II) \(\eta \rightarrow \eta -\delta _z + \delta _w\) belongs to type [II]: Recall that the mechanism in Sect. 4.3 is further divided into types (F), (S) and (B), where type (B) is impossible here since the particle jumps forward. Moreover, this [II]-mechanism is happening in \(x_{s'}, \dots , x_{s-1},x_s,\dots , x_{s''}\) with \(s' \le s-1 < s \le s''\) such thatFootnote 1

    $$\begin{aligned} \sigma _{x_{s'}} \ge 1 \; \text {,}\;\;\quad \sigma _{x_{s'+1}} = \cdots = \sigma _{x_{s''-1}} = 0 \quad \text {and} \quad \sigma _{x_{s''}} \ge 1 \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

    First, suppose that \(x_{s'}\ne x\). If the jump \(\eta \rightarrow \eta -\delta _z + \delta _w\) is of type (F), then

    $$\begin{aligned}&\eta + ( \eta _w \delta _z - \eta _w \delta _w ) \\&\quad = \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy} + (\delta _{x_{s'}} - \delta _x) + ( (\sigma _{x_{s'}}+1) \delta _z - (\sigma _{x_{s'}}+1) \delta _{x_{s'}} ) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

    Note that the jump \(\eta \rightarrow \eta -\delta _z + \delta _w\) happens within the \(\eta _z + \eta _w\) jumps of the same number of particles from z to w. Thus, the left-hand side configuration \(\eta + (\eta _w \delta _z - \eta _w \delta _w)\) detects the moment right before when the particle jumps \(z \rightarrow w\) are about to happen. Then, the right-hand side configuration is the corresponding configuration represented in terms of \((\ell ,m,\sigma )\) due to the following logic. First, from \(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}\), a particle would have traveled from x to \(x_{s'}\), giving the contribution \(\delta _{x_{s'}} - \delta _x\). Then, the whole \(\sigma _{x_{s'}}+1\) particles at \(x_{s'}\) would have traveled all together from \(x_{s'}\) to z, which gives the contribution \((\sigma _{x_{s'}}+1)(\delta _z - \delta _{x_{s'}})\). This instance is exactly right before when the jumps \(z \rightarrow w\) are initiated, giving the equality in the display. Thus, \(\sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}\) has a unique representation in terms of \(x,y,\eta ,z,w\), thus the triple \((\ell ,m,\sigma )\) is determined uniquely. More precisely, m is exactly the number of particles at x, \(\ell \) is the number of particles at both x and y, and \(\sigma \) is the remaining configuration on \(V \setminus \{x,y\}\). On the other hand, if \(\eta \rightarrow \eta -\delta _z +\delta _w\) is of type (S), then \(s=s''\), and we have

    $$\begin{aligned} \eta = \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy} + (\delta _{x_{s'}} - \delta _x) + ( (\sigma _{x_{s'}}+1) \delta _z - (\sigma _{x_{s'}}+1) \delta _{x_{s'}} ) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

    Here, both sides detect the instance right before when the single jump \(z \rightarrow w\) occurs, which is clearly \(\eta \) in the left-hand side and also the configuration in the right-hand side by the same logic as explained above. Hence, the triple \((\ell ,m,\sigma )\) is again determined uniquely in this case. Thus, we have two possibilities of triples \((\ell ,m,\sigma )\). Now, suppose that \(x_{s'}=x\). Then, we may similarly detect two possibilities:

    $$\begin{aligned} \eta + ( \eta _w \delta _z - \eta _w \delta _w ) = \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy} + ( m \delta _z - m \delta _{x} ) \end{aligned}$$

    for type (F), and

    $$\begin{aligned} \eta = \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy} + ( m \delta _z - m \delta _{x} ) \end{aligned}$$

    for type (S). They both yield a unique triple \((\ell ,m,\sigma )\). Therefore, we conclude that (Case 2.II) gives rise to at most two overlaps.

  • (Case 2.III) \(\eta \rightarrow \eta -\delta _z + \delta _w\) belongs to type [III]: As done in (Case 2.II), we fix \(x_{s'}, \dots , x_{s-1}, x_s, \dots , x_{s''}\) on which this [III]-mechanism takes place. A forward jump in type [III] is of one of the following three types: initial distribution of \(i \in \llbracket 1,\widetilde{m} \rrbracket \) particles \(x_{s'} \rightarrow x_{s'+1}\) (downward arrows in Fig. 3), movement of i particles in the bulk (double horizontal arrows in the left half of Fig. 3), or the single particle jump \(x_{s''-1} \rightarrow x_{s''}\) (single horizontal arrows in the middle part of Fig. 3). Each type determines a triple \((\ell ,m,\sigma )\) uniquely, as thoroughly explained in (Case 2.II). Thus, we omit the details here and conclude that no more than three overlaps are possible in (Case 2.III).

Collecting these three subcases, (Case 2) admits at most six overlaps.

(Case 3) There exists \(s\in \llbracket 1,t \rrbracket \) for which \(( x_{s-1},x_s )= (w,z)\):

The analysis in this third case is almost identical to the one done in (Case 2). Thus, we choose not to repeat the tedious computations and record here that, also in this case, at most six overlaps are possible in (Case 3).

Finally, given \(x<y\) and \((\eta ,z,w)\), collecting (Case 1), (Case 2) and (Case 3) ensure that there are at most six possible overlaps of triples \((\ell ,m,\sigma )\). Therefore, by (4.9), we deduce

$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{\ell =1}^k \sum _{m=1}^\ell \sum _{\sigma \in \Xi _{k-\ell }^{V \setminus \{x,y\} }} \sum _{\{\zeta ,\zeta '\} {\in } {\Omega _{x,y}^{\ell ,m,\sigma }}} \nabla ^2f(\zeta ,\zeta ') \le 6 \, \mathcal {E}\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Combining this with (4.46), the proof of (4.3) is now completed. \(\square \)

Remark 4.7

The full combination of the three types [I], [II] and [III] is needed to obtain the correct dependence on \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\) in Theorem 3.2. Alternatively, we may combine only two of those types (that is, [I] + [II] or [I] + [III]) to obtain different bounds. Here, we briefly record these new results for potential applications in future works.

Recall that we collected (4.24), (4.25), and (4.26) to obtain the exact bound in Lemma 4.5. If we bound \({\alpha _x \sum _{j=1}^{m-1} \frac{1}{j}}\) from above by \({\alpha _\textrm{max} \left( 1+\log k\right) }\) rather than by 1, we obtain an alternative bound

$$\begin{aligned} {\nabla _K^2 f ( \sigma _{m,\ell -m}^{xy}; x,y) } \le {3 \, e^{\alpha _\textrm{max}(1+\log k)} } \, \frac{{m\left( \alpha _y + \ell -m\right) t }}{ c_\textrm{min}\, \alpha _\textrm{min}\, \alpha _\textrm{ratio} } \left( \sum _{\{\zeta ,\zeta '\} {\in } \Omega } \nabla ^2f(\zeta ,\zeta ') \right) \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

which does not require (4.21). Combining this inequality with Lemma 4.3 and applying the same logic explained in that subsection, we get

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {E}_{K} \le \frac{ C \, e^{\alpha _\textrm{max}(1+\log k)} \, k \left( \alpha _\textrm{max}+k-1\right) |V|^2 \, \textrm{diam}(G) \, 2^{\alpha _\textrm{max} }}{\alpha _\textrm{min} \, c_\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{ratio} } \, \mathcal {E}\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

where \(C>0\) is a global constant. For simplicity, if we take \(\alpha =\varepsilon \hat{\alpha }\) as in (2.8), take \(\varepsilon \rightarrow 0\), and toss the dependence on G and \(\hat{\alpha }\) to the constants, this reduces to

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {E}_K \le \frac{ C_{G,\hat{\alpha }} \, e^{C_{G,\hat{\alpha }} \,\varepsilon \log k} \, k^2}{\varepsilon } \, \mathcal {E}\quad \text {as} \ \varepsilon \rightarrow 0 \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

where \(C_{G,\hat{\alpha }}>0\) now depends on \(G,\hat{\alpha }\) but not on \(k,\varepsilon \). Similarly, if we do not lower bound \(\alpha _x \sum _{j=1}^{m-1} \frac{1}{j}\) in (4.39) by 1 and repeat the same logic as above, we obtain a different bound

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {E}_{K} \le \frac{ C \, k \left( \alpha _\textrm{max}+k-1\right) |V|^2 \, \textrm{diam}(G) \, 6^{\alpha _\textrm{max} }}{\alpha _\textrm{min}^2 \, c_\textrm{min} \, \alpha _\textrm{ratio} \log k } \, \mathcal {E}\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

which then implies, in the asymptotic regime (2.8),

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {E}_K \le \frac{ C_{G,\hat{\alpha }} \, k^2}{\varepsilon ^2 \log k} \, \mathcal {E}\quad \text {as} \ \varepsilon \rightarrow 0 \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

with different constants \(C>0\) (global) and \(C_{G,\hat{\alpha }}>0\) (depends only on \(G,\hat{\alpha }\)).

5 Proofs of Theorems 2.3 and 2.4

In this section, we prove Theorems 2.3 and 2.4, following the outline of Sect. 2.4.2. The basic starting idea is to split the infinitesimal generator \(L_{G,\alpha ,k}\) into two parts: a slow part and a fast part. In view of this decomposition, standard limit theorems for slow–fast systems ensure that, in the small-diffusivity limit, \(\mathrm SIP\) is well approximated by the slow dynamics, after a suitable projection/thermalization according to the fast one. Understanding how and when this projection affects \(\textrm{SIP}\)’s spectral gap is the main non-trivial task of this section.

Recall from (2.8) that we assume \(\alpha = \varepsilon \hat{\alpha }\), where \(\hat{\alpha }=(\hat{\alpha }_x)_{x\in V}\) are fixed site weights and \(\varepsilon \) tends to 0. Finally, since the graph G is fixed all throughout the section, we often drop it from the notation, and simply write, e.g., \(\textrm{SIP}_k(\alpha )=\textrm{SIP}_k(G,\alpha )\) and \(L_{\alpha ,k}=L_{G,\alpha ,k}\).

5.1 Slow and fast dynamics

Fix \(k\ge 2\), and consider \(\textrm{SIP}_k(\varepsilon \hat{\alpha })\) with time sped up by a factor \(\varepsilon ^{-1}\). Recalling (2.1), the corresponding generator is then simply given by \(\varepsilon ^{-1}L_{\alpha ,k}= \varepsilon ^{-1}L_{\varepsilon \hat{\alpha },k}\). By separating the \(\varepsilon \)-dependent terms from the rest, we obtain

$$\begin{aligned} \varepsilon ^{-1} L_{\varepsilon \hat{\alpha },k}= A_{\hat{\alpha },k} +\varepsilon ^{-1} B_k\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(5.1)

where, for all \(f \in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\) and \(\eta \in \Xi _k\),

$$\begin{aligned} A_{\hat{\alpha },k} f(\eta )&:=\sum _{x,y\in V}c_{xy}\, \eta _x \hat{\alpha }_y \left( {f(\eta -\delta _x+\delta _y)-f(\eta )} \right) \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(5.2)
$$\begin{aligned} B_k f(\eta )&:=\sum _{x, y \in V}c_{xy}\, \eta _x \eta _y \left( f(\eta -\delta _x+\delta _y)-f(\eta )\right) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.3)

Let \((\mathcal {A}_{\hat{\alpha },k}(t))_{t\ge 0}\) and \((\mathcal {B}_k(t))_{t\ge 0}\) denote the Markov chains in \(\Xi _k\) generated by \(A_{\hat{\alpha },k}\) and \(B_k\), respectively. More in detail, \((\mathcal {A}_{\hat{\alpha },k}(t))_{t\ge 0}\) describes k independent particles on G, while \((\mathcal {B}_k(t))_{t\ge 0}\) is a particle system with absorbing set

$$\begin{aligned} \Omega _k:=\left\{ \eta \in \Xi _k: c_{xy}\, \eta _x\eta _y=0 \ \text {for all}\ x, y \in V\right\} \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(5.4)

and transient set \(\Delta _k:=\Xi _k\setminus \Omega _k\), i.e.,

$$\begin{aligned} \Delta _k:=\left\{ \eta \in \Xi _k: c_{xy}\, \eta _x\eta _y>0\ \text {for some}\ x, y \in V\right\} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.5)

Let \(\Pi _k: \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\) denote the \(B_k\)-harmonic projection operator, i.e.,

$$\begin{aligned} \Pi _k f:=\lim _{t\rightarrow \infty } e^{t B_k}f \; \text {,}\;\;\quad f \in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Remark that the range of \(\Pi _k\), written as \(\mathcal {R}(\Pi _k)\subseteq \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\), is a \(|\Omega _k|\)-dimensional subspace. More precisely, since \(\Omega _k\) is the absorbing set of \(B_k\), one has, for all \(f\in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\), \(\Pi _k f=f\) on \(\Omega _k\), and the values of \(\Pi _k f\) on \(\Delta _k\) are fully determined by the absorption probabilities on \(\Omega _k\). Thus, restriction to \(\Omega _k\) gives a canonical linear isomorphism \(\mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\supseteq \mathcal {R}(\Pi _k)\cong \mathbb {R}^{\Omega _k}\), and \(\mathcal {R}(\Pi _k)\) consists of \(B_k\)-harmonic extensions to \(\mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\) of functions in \(\mathbb {R}^{\Omega _k}\).

The following result builds on a powerful theorem [Kur73, Theorem 2.1] (see also [EK86, §1, Theorem 7.6]) on limit theorems for Markovian slow–fast systems.

Proposition 5.1

The operator \({\mathscr {G}}_{\hat{\alpha },k}:=\Pi _k A_{\hat{\alpha },k}|_{\mathcal {R}(\Pi _k)}:\mathcal {R}(\Pi _k)\rightarrow \mathcal {R}(\Pi _k)\) is an infinitesimal generator on \(\mathcal {R}(\Pi _k) \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\). Moreover, we have the following convergence:

$$\begin{aligned} \lim _{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0} \max _{\eta \in \Xi _k}\left| e^{t(A_{\hat{\alpha },k}+\varepsilon ^{-1}B_k)} f(\eta ) - e^{t{{\mathscr {G}}_{\hat{\alpha },k}}} \Pi _k f(\eta ) \right| = 0 \; \text {,}\;\;\quad t>0\; \text {,}\;\;f \in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.6)

Proof

In this proof, we write \(P_t^\varepsilon = e^{t(A_{\hat{\alpha },k}+\varepsilon ^{-1}B_k)}\). The first claim and a convergence as in (5.6) with \(P_t^\varepsilon f(\eta )\) replaced by \(P_t^\varepsilon \Pi _k f(\eta )\) are the content of [EK86, §1, Theorem 7.6]. In order to obtain the desired claim (i.e., remove the projection operator \(\Pi _k\)), it suffices to prove that

$$\begin{aligned} \lim _{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0} \max _{\eta \in \Xi _k} \Big |P_{t}^\varepsilon \Pi _k f(\eta )-P_{t}^\varepsilon f(\eta )\Big |=0\; \text {,}\;\;\quad t>0\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

For this purpose, fix \(f\in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\), and observe that

$$\begin{aligned} \Pi _k f(\eta )= f(\eta )\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \eta \in \Omega _k\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

because \((\mathcal {B}_k(t))_{t\ge 0}\) has \(\Omega _k\) as absorbing states. Hence, since \(\Pi _k\) is a contraction, we get

$$\begin{aligned} \Big |P_t^\varepsilon \Pi _k f(\eta )-P_t^\varepsilon f(\eta )\Big | \le \sum _{\xi \in \Delta _k } p_t^\varepsilon (\eta ,\xi )\Big |\Pi _k f(\xi )-f(\xi )\Big | \le \big (2\max _{\xi \in \Xi _k}\Big |f(\xi )\Big | \big )\, p_t^\varepsilon (\eta ,\Delta _k ) \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

where \(p_t^\varepsilon (\,\cdot \,,\,\cdot \,)\) denotes the transition kernel of the \(\varepsilon ^{-1}\)-sped up \(\textrm{SIP}_k(\varepsilon \hat{\alpha })\) at time t. Since

$$\begin{aligned} \lim _{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0} p_t^\varepsilon (\eta , \Delta _k)= 0\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \eta \in \Xi _k\; \text {,}\;\;t>0 \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

(see Proposition A.1 below for the full statement and proof of this fact), the proof of the proposition is concluded. \(\square \)

Fix \(t> 0\), and recall that \(e^{t(A_{\hat{\alpha },k}+\varepsilon ^{-1}B_k)}\) has all real eigenvalues, just one equal to 1, while all the others lying in the interval (0, 1). Further, Proposition 5.1 ensures that all eigenvalues (with multiplicity) of \(e^{t(A_{\hat{\alpha },k}+\varepsilon ^{-1}B_k)}\) converge to those of \(e^{t{{\mathscr {G}}_{\hat{\alpha },k}}}{\Pi _k}\). Here, \({\mathscr {G}}_{\hat{\alpha },k}\) acts on \(\mathcal {R}(\Pi _k)\), while \(e^{t{\mathscr {G}}_{\hat{\alpha },k}}\Pi _k\) is regarded as an operator on \(\mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\). On \(\mathcal {R}(\Pi _k)\), this operator coincides with \(e^{t{\mathscr {G}}_{\hat{\alpha },k}}\), whereas on \(\textrm{Ker}(\Pi _k)\) it is identically zero. In particular, the spectrum of \(e^{t{{\mathscr {G}}_{\hat{\alpha },k}}}{\Pi _k}\) entirely lies in [0, 1]: one eigenvalue is 1, \(|\Omega _k|-1\) eigenvalues lie in (0, 1), while the eigenvalue 0 has multiplicity \(\textrm{dim}(\textrm{Ker}(\Pi _k))= |\Xi _k|-|\Omega _k|=|\Delta _k|\).

As a consequence, the second-largest eigenvalue of \(e^{t(A_{\hat{\alpha },k}+\varepsilon ^{-1}B_k)}\) converges to the second-largest eigenvalue of \( e^{t {{\mathscr {G}}_{\hat{\alpha },k}}}{\Pi _k}\) on \(\mathcal {R}(\Pi _k)\subset \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\), which is given by \(e^{-t w_k(\hat{\alpha })}\), where

$$\begin{aligned} w_k(\hat{\alpha }):=\ \text {the second-smallest eigenvalue of}\ {-{\mathscr {G}}_{\hat{\alpha },k}}:\mathcal {R}(\Pi _k)\rightarrow \mathcal {R}(\Pi _k) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.7)

Thus, we proved the following claim. (Recall that \(\textrm{gap}_k(\varepsilon \hat{\alpha })=\textrm{gap}_k(G,\varepsilon \hat{\alpha })\).)

Corollary 5.2

For all integers \(k\ge 2\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \lim _{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0} \frac{\textrm{gap}_k( \varepsilon \hat{\alpha })}{\varepsilon }= w_k(\hat{\alpha })\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

In the next subsection, we analyze more in detail the limiting generator \({\mathscr {G}}_{\hat{\alpha },k}=\Pi _k A_{\hat{\alpha },k}|_{\mathcal {R}(\Pi _k)}\) and its block-triangular structure.

5.2 Recurrent and transient states

As already proved in Proposition 5.1, \({\mathscr {G}}_{\hat{\alpha },k}=\Pi _k A_{\hat{\alpha },k}|_{\mathcal {R}(\Pi _k)}: \mathcal {R}(\Pi _k)\rightarrow \mathcal {R}(\Pi _k)\) may be interpreted as the infinitesimal generator of a Markov chain on \(\Omega _k\), hereafter referred to as \((\mathcal {M}_{\hat{\alpha },k}(t))_{t\ge 0}\). For notational simplicity, instead of analyzing directly the operator \({\mathscr {G}}_{\hat{\alpha },k}\) on the \(|\Omega _k|\)-dimensional subspace \(\mathcal {R}(\Pi _k)\) of \(\mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\), we identify \(\mathcal {R}(\Pi _k)\) with \(\mathbb {R}^{\Omega _k}\) by restriction to \(\Omega _k\), and analyze the transformed rate matrix — defined as \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k}\) in (5.9) or (5.21) below — on \(\mathbb {R}^{\Omega _k}\). Let us now describe this rate matrix in detail.

Recall the chains \((\mathcal {A}_{\hat{\alpha },k}(t))_{t\ge 0}\) and \((\mathcal {B}_k(t))_{t\ge 0}\) introduced at the beginning of Sect. 5.1. Let \(\textbf{r}^\mathcal {A}_{\hat{\alpha },k}\) be the transition-rate function of the process \((\mathcal {A}_{\hat{\alpha },k}(t))_{t\ge 0}\). Moreover, let \(\textbf{P}^{\mathcal {B}}_\zeta \) denote the law of the process \((\mathcal {B}_k(t))_{t\ge 0}\) started from \(\zeta \in \Xi _k\), and define

$$\begin{aligned} \tau _\xi :=\inf \{t\ge 0:\mathcal {B}_k(t)=\xi \} \; \text {,}\;\;\quad \tau _{\Omega _k} :=\min _{\xi \in \Omega _k}\tau _\xi \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

The effective process on \(\Omega _k\), denoted by \((\mathcal {M}_{\hat{\alpha },k}(t))_{t\ge 0}\), has non-diagonal transition rates

$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{r}^\mathcal {M}_{\hat{\alpha },k}(\eta ,\xi ) :=\sum _{\zeta \in \Xi _k} \textbf{r}^\mathcal {A}_{\hat{\alpha },k}(\eta ,\zeta )\, \textbf{P}^{\mathcal {B}}_\zeta \left[ \tau _\xi =\tau _{\Omega _k} \right] \; \text {,}\;\;\quad \eta ,\xi \in \Omega _k\; \text {,}\;\;\eta \ne \xi \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.8)

The diagonal entries are chosen so that row sums vanish. We write

$$\begin{aligned} M_{\hat{\alpha },k}:= \left( \textbf{r}^\mathcal {M}_{\hat{\alpha },k}(\eta ,\xi ) \right) _{\eta ,\xi \in \Omega _k}\in \mathbb {R}^{\Omega _x\times \Omega _k} \end{aligned}$$
(5.9)

for the corresponding square rate matrix of size \(|\Omega _k|\).

From the form of these rates, we immediately derive a classification of recurrent and transient states for this Markov chain; this will lead to a further characterization of the spectral gap \(w_k(\hat{\alpha })\) of \({\mathscr {G}}_{\hat{\alpha },k}\). We collect this classification in Proposition 5.4 below; its direct consequence on the spectral gap is the content of the subsequent corollary. Before that, let us introduce, for every integer \(m\ge 1\),

$$\begin{aligned} \Omega _{k,m} :=\left\{ \eta \in \Omega _k:| \{ x \in V: \eta _x > 0 \} | = m \right\} \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(5.10)

that is, the set of configurations in \(\Omega _k\) with m separated stacks of particles (cf. (5.4)). Clearly, \(\Omega _{k,m}=\varnothing \) implies \(\Omega _{k,m+\ell }=\varnothing \) for all \(\ell \ge 1\). Further, we have \(\Omega _{k,1}\ne \varnothing \), \(\Omega _{k,k+1}=\varnothing \), and, thus, \( \Omega _k = \sqcup _{m=1}^\infty \Omega _{k,m} = \Omega _{k,1} \sqcup \Omega _{k,2} \sqcup \cdots \sqcup \Omega _{k,k}\).

To make the limiting dynamics more transparent, we first discuss a simple example.

Example 5.3

(Three-site path: block structure) Let G be the path \(\{1,2,3\}\), with conductances \(c_{12}, c_{23}>0\) and \(c_{13}=0\), and consider the limiting chain on \(\Omega _k\) generated by \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k}\). This example illustrates the block structure described above.

For \(k=2\), the absorbing set of the fast dynamics is

$$\begin{aligned} \Omega _2 = \Omega _{2,1}\sqcup \Omega _{2,2}\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \Omega _{2,1}=\{2\delta _1,2\delta _2,2\delta _3\}\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \Omega _{2,2}=\{\delta _1+\delta _3\}\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

The block \(\Omega _{2,1}\) is recurrent: it describes a single stack of two particles moving effectively as one random walk on G. The block \(\Omega _{2,2}\) is transient: from \(\delta _1+\delta _3\), a slow jump of either particle to the middle site creates a configuration in \(\Delta _2\), after which the fast dynamics immediately thermalizes into the recurrent block \(\Omega _{2,1}\), never returning to \(\Omega _{2,2}\).

For \(k=3\), one similarly has

$$\begin{aligned} \Omega _3 = \Omega _{3,1}\sqcup \Omega _{3,2}\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \Omega _{3,1}=\{3\delta _1,3\delta _2,3\delta _3\}\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \Omega _{3,2} = \{2\delta _1+\delta _3,\ \delta _1+2\delta _3\}\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

while \(\Omega _{3,m}=\varnothing \) for all \(m\ge 3\). Again, \(\Omega _{3,1}\) is recurrent, while \(\Omega _{3,2}\) is transient. In contrast with the case \(k=2\), however, a slow jump from a state in \(\Omega _{3,2}\) to \(\Delta _3\) need not be followed by a fast relaxation directly into \(\Omega _{3,1}\): the fast dynamics may also reach the other state of \(\Omega _{3,2}\). Indeed, starting, e.g., from \(2\delta _1+\delta _3\in \Omega _{3,2}\), a slow jump of one of the two particles sitting at site 1 to site 2 yields \(\delta _1+\delta _2+\delta _3\in \Delta _3\). The subsequent fast thermalization may either merge all particles into a single stack, hence reaching \(\Omega _{3,1}\), or merge the particle at site 2 with the one at site 3, thereby reaching \(\delta _1+2\delta _3\in \Omega _{3,2}\). In conclusion, for \(k=3\), the transient block is already a genuine two-state sub-Markov chain.

Proposition 5.4

For the chain \((\mathcal {M}_{\hat{\alpha },k}(t))_{t\ge 0}\), the following two claims hold true:

  1. (a)

    For every \(m, m'\ge 1\) with \(\Omega _{k,m}\ne \varnothing \ne \Omega _{k,m'}\) and \(\eta \in \Omega _{k,m}\), we have

    $$\begin{aligned} \textbf{r}_{\hat{\alpha },k}^\mathcal {M}(\eta ,\xi ) = 0\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \text {for all}\ \xi \in \Omega _{k,m'}, \ m'>m\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

    while, when \(m\ge 2\), there exist \(\eta ^1,\ldots ,\eta ^\ell \in \Omega _{k,m}\) satisfying

    $$\begin{aligned} \textbf{r}_{\hat{\alpha },k}^\mathcal {M}(\eta ,\eta ^1)\left( \prod _{h=1}^{\ell -1} \textbf{r}_{\hat{\alpha },k}^\mathcal {M}(\eta ^h,\eta ^{h+1})\right) \textbf{r}_{\hat{\alpha },k}^\mathcal {M}(\eta ^\ell ,\xi )>0\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \text {for some}\ \xi \in \Omega _{k,m'}, \ m'<m\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

    As a consequence, \(\Omega _{k,m}\), \(m\ge 2\), consists of transient states only.

  2. (b)

    The chain \((\mathcal {M}_{\hat{\alpha },k}(t))_{t\ge 0}\) is irreducible on \(\Omega _{k,1}\ne \varnothing \), and the position of the single stack evolves as \(\textrm{RW}(\hat{\alpha })\).

Proof

(a) The desired claim follows from the connectedness of the graph G, and the observation that the rates \(\textbf{r}_{\hat{\alpha },k}^\mathcal {M}(\,\cdot \,,\,\cdot \,)\) in (5.8) describe the following two subsequent mechanisms: a step of the chain \((\mathcal {A}_{\hat{\alpha },k}(t))_{t\ge 0}\) increases the number of stacks at most by one, creating, in this case, at least two neighboring stacks; the chain \((\mathcal {B}_k(t))_{t\ge 0}\) forces all neighboring particles to eventually merge into isolated (i.e., non-neighboring) stacks.

(b) Observe that, for all \(x,y,z \in V\) with \(c_{xz}>0\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{P}^{\mathcal {B}}_{(k-1)\delta _x+\delta _z}\,[\tau _{k\delta _y}=\tau _{\Omega _{k,1}}] = \frac{\mathbb {1}_{y=z}}{k} + \frac{(k-1) \, \mathbb {1}_{y=x}}{k}\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

because the trajectories of \((\mathcal {B}_k(t))_{t\ge 0}\) started from \((k-1)\delta _x+\delta _z\in \Delta _k\) coincide with those of the symmetric random walk on \(\llbracket 0,k \rrbracket \) started from \(k-1\). Plugging this expression into (5.8), we obtain, for \(x \ne y\),

$$\begin{aligned} & \textbf{r}_{\hat{\alpha },k}^\mathcal {M}(k\delta _x,k\delta _y)\\ & \quad = \sum _{z\in V} \textbf{r}_{\hat{\alpha },k}^\mathcal {A}(k\delta _x,(k-1)\delta _x+\delta _z)\, \textbf{P}_{(k-1)\delta _x+\delta _z}^\mathcal {B}\, [\tau _{k\delta _y}=\tau _{\Omega _{k,1}}] = k\,c_{xy}\,\hat{\alpha }_y\,\frac{1}{k} = c_{xy}\,\hat{\alpha }_y\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

namely, the transition rates of \(\textrm{RW}(\hat{\alpha })\). Irreducibility is then an obvious consequence of that of \(\textrm{RW}(\hat{\alpha })\). \(\square \)

Remark 5.5

The claim in Proposition 5.4(b) is the finite-particle analogue of the metastable picture of \(\textrm{SIP}\), fully detailed in [GRV13, BDG17].

Remark 5.6

For \(k\ge m\ge 2\), the dynamics on \(\Omega _{k,m}\ne \varnothing \) encoded by \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k}\) goes as follows. All stacks evolve as independent \(\textrm{RW}(\hat{\alpha })\) as long as their mutual distances are at least three. When two or more stacks reach mutual distance two, then there is a positive rate for them to merge. These rates — as well as the new positions of the stacks and their new compositions — depend on G and \(\hat{\alpha }\), but also non-trivially on \(k\ge m\) and, when \(k>m\), on the stacks’ composition. For more details, see Sect. 5.3.2 below.

The state-classification in Proposition 5.4 may be equivalently restated as follows: after a suitable conjugation with a permutation matrix, \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k}\) is turned into a block lower triangular matrix with \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k,1},\ldots , M_{\hat{\alpha },k,k}\) as diagonal blocks, where, for all \(m\in \llbracket 1,k \rrbracket \),

$$\begin{aligned} M_{\hat{\alpha },k,m} \in \mathbb {R}^{|\Omega _{k,m}|\times |\Omega _{k,m}|} \end{aligned}$$
(5.11)

is the submatrix of \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k}\) obtained from the restriction on configurations in \(\Omega _{k,m}\) (see Fig. 6). Since the eigenvalues of block triangular matrices are the union of those of the diagonal blocks, it suffices to focus on the spectrum of the blocks \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k,m}\), \(m\in \llbracket 1,k \rrbracket \). As already showed in the previous section, the eigenvalues of \(-M_{\hat{\alpha },k,m}\), \(m\in \llbracket 1,k \rrbracket \), are all real and non-negative. Remark that:

  • for \(m=1\), the spectrum of \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k,m}\) coincides with that of the generator of \(\textrm{RW}(\hat{\alpha })\);

  • for \(m\ge 2\) and \(\Omega _{k,m}\ne \varnothing \), \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k,m}\) is the rate matrix of a sub-Markovian chain killed upon exiting \(\Omega _{k,m}\); thus, all its eigenvalues are strictly negative.

In view of these simple observations, we register the following useful characterization of the spectral gap \(w_k(\hat{\alpha })\) defined in (5.7).

Corollary 5.7

For all \(m\ge 2\) such that \(\Omega _{k,m}\ne \varnothing \), define

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda _{k,m}(\hat{\alpha }):=\ \text {smallest eigenvalue of the matrix}\ -M_{\hat{\alpha },k,m}\in \mathbb {R}^{|\Omega _{k,m}|\times |\Omega _{k,m}|} \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(5.12)

whereas \(\lambda _{k,m}(\hat{\alpha }):=+\infty \) if \(\Omega _{k,m}=\varnothing \). Then, we have

$$\begin{aligned} w_k(\hat{\alpha }) = \textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(\hat{\alpha }) \wedge \min _{m \in \llbracket 2,k \rrbracket } \lambda _{k,m}(\hat{\alpha }) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.13)

Example 5.8

(Three-site path: eigenvalues) We keep the notation of Example 5.3.

For \(k=2\), the only transient state is \(\delta _1+\delta _3\). It exits the transient block as soon as one of the two particles jumps to site 2, which happens at rate

$$\begin{aligned} p:=\left( c_{12}+c_{23}\right) \hat{\alpha }_2\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Therefore, \( \lambda _{2,2}(\hat{\alpha })=p\).

For \(k=3\), the transient block is \( \Omega _{3,2} = \{2\delta _1+\delta _3,\delta _1+2\delta _3\}\). Starting from \(2\delta _1+\delta _3\), a slow jump of one of the two particles at site 1 to site 2 occurs at rate \(2c_{12}\hat{\alpha }_2\) and produces \(\delta _1+\delta _2+\delta _3\). From this configuration, the fast dynamics reaches \(\delta _1+2\delta _3\) with probability \(c_{23}/(2(c_{12}+c_{23}))\). Hence, the effective jump rate from \(2\delta _1+\delta _3\) to \(\delta _1+2\delta _3\) is

$$\begin{aligned} q:=2c_{12}\hat{\alpha }_2\frac{c_{23}}{2(c_{12}+c_{23})} = \frac{c_{12}c_{23}}{c_{12}+c_{23}}\hat{\alpha }_2\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

By symmetry, the effective jump rate in the opposite direction is also q. The effective killing rate from either transient state to the recurrent block is p. Indeed, starting again from \(2\delta _1+\delta _3\), the slow jump of the particle at site 3 to site 2 contributes \(c_{23}\hat{\alpha }_2\) to this killing rate. The slow jump of one of the two particles at site 1 to site 2 contributes instead \(2c_{12}\hat{\alpha }_2\cdot \frac{1}{2}=c_{12}\hat{\alpha }_2\), since from \(\delta _1+\delta _2+\delta _3\) the fast dynamics reaches \(\Omega _{3,1}\) with probability 1/2. Thus, the total killing rate is \( c_{23}\hat{\alpha }_2+c_{12}\hat{\alpha }_2=p\). Therefore, in the ordered basis \(\left( 2\delta _1+\delta _3,\delta _1+2\delta _3\right) \),

$$\begin{aligned} -M_{\hat{\alpha },3,2} = \begin{pmatrix} p+q & -q \\ -q & p+q \end{pmatrix}\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Its smallest eigenvalue is \(\lambda _{3,2}(\hat{\alpha })=p\), the other eigenvalue being \(p+2q>p\). Thus, in this example, the same quantity p gives the transient contribution to the limiting gap for both \(k=2\) and \(k=3\).

The natural next step is to estimate the eigenvalue \(\lambda _{k,m} (\hat{\alpha })\) for each \(k\ge 2\) and \(m \in \llbracket 2,k \rrbracket \), by bounding it from below by \(\lambda _{2,2}(\hat{\alpha })\). This task is carried out in the following two subsections. In the first one, we prove \(\lambda _{k,m}(\hat{\alpha })= \lambda _{m,m}(\hat{\alpha })\); in the second one, we show \(\lambda _{m,m}(\hat{\alpha })\ge \lambda _{2,2}(\hat{\alpha })\).

We conclude this subsection by observing that each submatrix \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k,m}\) in \(\Omega _{k,m}\) is self-adjoint with respect to a positive measure \(\varsigma _{\hat{\alpha },k,m}\) given in (5.14) below. On the one hand, this property is interesting and may become handy in future use, e.g., for building an \(L^2\)-theory for the corresponding continuum model. On the other hand, in our storyline, neither this property nor the precise form of the symmetrizing measure are of primary importance. Thus, we only state the full statement here, and postpone its proof to Appendix B.

Lemma 5.9

For all \(m\le k\), \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k,m}\) is self-adjoint as an operator on \(L^2(\varsigma _{\hat{\alpha },k,m})\), where

$$\begin{aligned} \varsigma _{\hat{\alpha },k,m}(\eta ):=\prod _{\begin{array}{c} x\in V\\ \eta _x>0 \end{array}}\frac{\hat{\alpha }_x}{\eta _x}\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \eta \in \Omega _{k,m}\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.14)

5.3 Comparing \(\lambda _{k,m}(\hat{\alpha })\) with \(\lambda _{m,m}(\hat{\alpha })\)

The main goal of this subsection is to prove the following result.

Proposition 5.10

For all integers \(m\ge 2\) with \(\Omega _{m,m}\ne \varnothing \), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda _{k,m}(\hat{\alpha })=\lambda _{m,m}(\hat{\alpha })\; \text {,}\;\;\quad k\ge m\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.15)

We divide the proof of this identity into a few steps, and fix \(m\ge 2\) so as to satisfy \(\Omega _{m,m}\ne \varnothing \) all throughout.

5.3.1 Consistency

Recall the consistency property (3.1) satisfied by \(\textrm{SIP}\), as well as the corresponding annihilation operator \(\mathfrak {a}_k: \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _{k-1}} \rightarrow \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\) in (3.2). Since \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k}\) arises as a small-diffusivity limit of \(\textrm{SIP}\), it is natural to expect that an analogous property should hold also for \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k}\). This is the content of the following proposition.

Proposition 5.11

Define, for all integers \(k\ge 2\), the following “restricted” annihilation operator \(\hat{\mathfrak a}_k: \mathbb {R}^{\Omega _{k-1}}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}^{\Omega _k}\) as

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\mathfrak a}_k g(\eta ):=\sum _{x\in V} \eta _x\,g(\eta -\delta _x)\; \text {,}\;\;\quad g\in \mathbb {R}^{\Omega _{k-1}}\; \text {,}\;\;\eta \in \Omega _k\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.16)

Then, we have

$$\begin{aligned} M_{\hat{\alpha },k}\,\hat{\mathfrak a}_k = \hat{\mathfrak a}_k\, M_{\hat{\alpha },k-1}\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.17)

Proof

It is well known that a system of k independent random walks is consistent, i.e., \(A_{\hat{\alpha },k} \, \mathfrak {a}_k = \mathfrak {a}_k \, A_{\hat{\alpha },k-1}\). Moreover, by rewriting the consistency property of \(\textrm{SIP}(\varepsilon \hat{\alpha })\) in (3.1) with the notation in (5.1), we have \((A_{\hat{\alpha },k} + \varepsilon ^{-1} B_k) \, \mathfrak {a}_k = \mathfrak {a}_k \, (A_{\hat{\alpha },k-1} + \varepsilon ^{-1} B_{k-1})\). Thus, by linearity, we must also have \(B_k\,\mathfrak {a}_k=\mathfrak a_k\,B_{k-1}\), and a similar relation for the harmonic projection \(\Pi _k\) of \(B_k\). By combining these observations, we obtain

$$\begin{aligned} \Pi _k\,\mathfrak a_k = \mathfrak a_k\, \Pi _{k-1}\quad \text {as maps from}\ \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _{k-1}}\ \text {to}\ \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(5.18)

and

$$\begin{aligned} \Pi _k A_{\hat{\alpha },k}\,\mathfrak a_k = \mathfrak a_k\,\Pi _{k-1}A_{\hat{\alpha },k-1}\quad \text {as maps from}\ \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _{k-1}}\ \text {to}\ \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.19)

In particular, (5.18) ensures that \(\mathfrak a_k\) maps \(\mathcal {R}(\Pi _{k-1})\) into \(\mathcal {R}(\Pi _k)\).

For all \(j\ge 1\), let \( \rho _j:\mathcal {R}(\Pi _j)\rightarrow \mathbb {R}^{\Omega _j} \) be the restriction map to \(\Omega _j\), and let \( \iota _j:\mathbb {R}^{\Omega _j}\rightarrow \mathcal {R}(\Pi _j) \) be its inverse given by \(B_j\)-harmonic extension. By definition of the rate matrix in (5.9),

$$\begin{aligned} M_{\hat{\alpha },j} = \rho _j\Pi _j A_{\hat{\alpha },j}\iota _j \quad \text {on }\mathbb {R}^{\Omega _j} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.20)

Moreover, since \(\mathfrak a_k\) maps \(\mathcal {R}(\Pi _{k-1})\) into \(\mathcal {R}(\Pi _k)\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \iota _k\hat{\mathfrak a}_k = \mathfrak a_k\iota _{k-1}\quad \text {as maps from}\ \mathbb {R}^{\Omega _{k-1}}\ \text {to}\ \mathcal {R}(\Pi _k) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.21)

Indeed, the right-hand side belongs to \(\mathcal {R}(\Pi _k)\). Moreover, for all \(g\in \mathbb {R}^{\Omega _{k-1}}\) and \(\eta \in \Omega _k\),

$$\begin{aligned} \mathfrak a_k\iota _{k-1}g(\eta )&= \sum _{x\in V}\eta _x\,\iota _{k-1}g(\eta -\delta _x) = \sum _{x\in V}\eta _x\,g(\eta -\delta _x) = \hat{\mathfrak a}_k g(\eta ) \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

where we used that \(\eta -\delta _x\in \Omega _{k-1}\) whenever \(\eta \in \Omega _k\) and \(\eta _x\ge 1\). Hence the two sides of (5.21) are the same \(B_k\)-harmonic extension.

Finally, by (5.20), (5.21), and (5.19), we get

$$\begin{aligned} M_{\hat{\alpha },k}\hat{\mathfrak a}_k&= \rho _k\Pi _k A_{\hat{\alpha },k}\iota _k \hat{\mathfrak a}_k \nonumber \\&= \rho _k\Pi _k A_{\hat{\alpha },k}\mathfrak a_k \iota _{k-1} \nonumber \\&= \rho _k\mathfrak a_k\Pi _{k-1}A_{\hat{\alpha },k-1} \iota _{k-1} \nonumber \\&= \rho _k\mathfrak a_k\iota _{k-1} \rho _{k-1}\Pi _{k-1}A_{\hat{\alpha },k-1} \iota _{k-1} \nonumber \\&= \hat{\mathfrak a}_k M_{\hat{\alpha },k-1} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

In the fourth line, we used that \(\Pi _{k-1}A_{\hat{\alpha },k-1}\iota _{k-1}\) has \(\mathcal {R}(\Pi _{k-1})\) as an image, so that \(\iota _{k-1}\rho _{k-1}\) acts as the identity on it. This concludes the proof of the proposition \(\square \)

5.3.2 Irreducible decompositions

By an irreducible component of \((\mathcal {M}_{\hat{\alpha },k}(t))_{t \ge 0}\) on \(\Omega _{k,m}\) we mean a communicating class for the restriction of the transition graph to \(\Omega _{k,m}\): two configurations \(\eta ,\xi \in \Omega _{k,m}\) belong to the same component if there exist paths \(\eta =\eta _0,\eta _1,\ldots ,\eta _\ell =\xi \) and \(\xi =\xi _0,\xi _1,\ldots ,\xi _r=\eta \), all contained in \(\Omega _{k,m}\), such that

$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{r}^\mathcal {M}_{\hat{\alpha },k}(\eta _{h-1},\eta _h)>0\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \textbf{r}^\mathcal {M}_{\hat{\alpha },k}(\xi _{h-1},\xi _h)>0\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

for all admissible indices. An irreducible decomposition is the partition of \(\Omega _{k,m}\) into such irreducible components.

The chain \((\mathcal {M}_{\hat{\alpha },k}(t))_{t\ge 0}\) is not necessarily irreducible on \(\Omega _{k,m}\). For instance, when \(G=\mathbb {T}_{2m}\) is the one-dimensional discrete torus of size 2m,

$$\begin{aligned} {\Omega _{m,m}=\{\delta _1+\delta _3+\ldots +\delta _{2m-1},\delta _0+\delta _2+\ldots +\delta _{2m-2}\}=:\{\eta _{m,\mathrm odd},\eta _{m,\mathrm even}\}}\end{aligned}$$

consists of exactly two configurations (one with particles/stacks on the odd numbers, the other one on the even numbers), and \(\textbf{r}_{\hat{\alpha },k}^\mathcal {M}(\eta _{m,\mathrm odd},\eta _{m,\mathrm even})=\textbf{r}_{\hat{\alpha },k}^\mathcal {M}(\eta _{m,\mathrm even},\eta _{m,\mathrm odd})=0\). Moreover, the structure of the irreducible components of the chain \((\mathcal {M}_{\hat{\alpha },k}(t))_{t\ge 0}\) on \(\Omega _{k,m}\) is always finer than the corresponding structure on \(\Omega _{m,m}\), in the sense of the following proposition.

Proposition 5.12

Decompose \(\Omega _{m,m}\) into irreducible components for the chain \((\mathcal {M}_{\hat{\alpha },m}(t))_{t\ge 0}\): for some integer \(\mathfrak n_m\ge 1\) (depending only on \(m\ge 2\) and G),

$$\begin{aligned} \Omega _{m,m}=\Omega _{m,m}^1\sqcup \cdots \sqcup \Omega _{m,m}^{\mathfrak n_m}\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.22)

For all \(k> m\) and \(i\in \llbracket 1,\mathfrak n_m \rrbracket \), define, iteratively,

$$\begin{aligned} \Omega _{k,m}^i = \{ \eta + \delta _x \in \Omega _{k,m}: \eta \in \Omega _{k-1,m}^i, \ x \in V \ \text {such that} \ \eta _x \ge 1 \} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.23)

Then, there exist partitions \(\Omega _{k,m}^i=\Omega _{k,m}^{i,1} \sqcup \cdots \sqcup \Omega _{k,m}^{i,\mathfrak {N}^k_i}\) for all \(i \in \llbracket 1,\mathfrak n_m \rrbracket \) such that

$$\begin{aligned} \Omega _{k,m}= \bigsqcup _{i=1}^{\mathfrak n_m} \bigsqcup _{j=1}^{\mathfrak N_i^k} \Omega _{k,m}^{i,j} \end{aligned}$$
(5.24)

is an irreducible decomposition of \(\Omega _{k,m}\) for the chain \((\mathcal {M}_{\hat{\alpha },k}(t))_{t\ge 0}\).

Proof

First, the fact that \(\Omega _{k,m}\), for each \(k \ge m \ge 2\), can be decomposed into irreducible components follows at once from Lemma 5.9. Thus, to prove the validity of Proposition 5.12, it suffices to check the following two properties:

  1. (a)

    \(\Omega _{k,m} = \Omega _{k,m}^1 \sqcup \cdots \sqcup \Omega _{k,m}^{\mathfrak n_m}\);

  2. (b)

    if \(i \ne i'\), \(\eta \in \Omega _{k,m}^i\), and \(\xi \in \Omega _{k,m}^{i'}\), then \(\eta \) and \(\eta '\) belong to different irreducible components of \(\Omega _{k,m}\) for \((\mathcal {M}_{\hat{\alpha },k}(t))_{t\ge 0}\).

To verify item (a), take \(\eta \in \Omega _{k,m}\) and define a new configuration \(\eta ^*\in \Omega _{m,m}\) as

$$\begin{aligned} \eta ^* :=\sum _{\begin{array}{c} x \in V \\ \eta _x \ge 1 \end{array}} \delta _x \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.25)

Since \(\eta \) has m separated stacks of particles, \(\eta ^*\) belongs to \(\Omega _{m,m}\). Then, according to (5.22), \(\eta ^* \in \Omega _{m,m}^i\) for some i, thus \(\eta \in \Omega _{k,m}^i\) by (5.23). This proves that \(\Omega _{k,m}=\Omega _{k,m}^1 \sqcup \cdots \sqcup \Omega _{k,m}^{\mathfrak n_m}\).

We move on to item (b). It suffices to prove that, for any \(\eta ,\xi \in \Omega _{k,m}\) with \(\textbf{r}_{\hat{\alpha },k}^\mathcal {M}(\eta ,\xi )>0\), the corresponding configurations \(\eta ^*,\xi ^* \in \Omega _{m,m}\) (cf. (5.25)) satisfy either \(\eta ^* = \xi ^*\) or \(\textbf{r}_{\hat{\alpha },m}^\mathcal {M}(\eta ^*,\xi ^*)>0\).

In view of (5.8), there exists \(\zeta \in \Xi _k\) such that \(\textbf{r}_{\hat{\alpha },k}^\mathcal {A}( \eta , \zeta ) > 0\) and \(\textbf{P}_\zeta ^\mathcal {B}\, [\tau _\xi = \tau _{\Omega _k}] > 0\). In turn, there exist \(x,y \in V\) with \(c_{xy}>0\) such that \(\zeta = \eta - \delta _x + \delta _y\). First, suppose that \(\zeta \in \Omega _k\), i.e., \(\zeta = \xi \). Then, it is straightforward that \(\zeta ^*=\xi ^* \in \Omega _m\) and \(\textbf{r}_{\hat{\alpha },m}^\mathcal {A}(\eta ^*,\zeta ^*)>0\), thus we have \(\textbf{r}_{\hat{\alpha },m}^\mathcal {M}(\eta ^*,\xi ^*)>0\) by (5.8). On the contrary, suppose that \(\zeta \in \Delta _k\). This means that y has at least one neighboring site (possibly x) on which \(\zeta \) has a particle. We divide into several cases.

1.:

If \(\zeta _x \ge 1\) and \(\zeta _z = 0\) for all \(z \ne x\) with \(c_{yz}>0\), then the dynamics \((\mathcal {B}_k(t))_{t\ge 0}\) can only move particles between sites x and y. Thus, by \(\textbf{P}_\zeta ^\mathcal {B}\, [\tau _\xi = \tau _{\Omega _k} ] >0\) it necessarily holds that \(\xi = \eta - \eta _x \delta _x + \eta _x \delta _y \in \Omega _k\). This implies that \(\xi ^* = \eta ^* - \delta _x + \delta _y \in \Omega _m\), thus \(\textbf{r}_{\hat{\alpha },m}^\mathcal {M}(\eta ^*,\xi ^*) > 0\).

2.:

Suppose that \(\zeta _x \ge 1\) and there exists \(z \ne x\) with \(c_{yz}>0\) such that \(\zeta _z \ge 1\). Then, the dynamics \((\mathcal {B}_k(t))_{t\ge 0}\) gets absorbed exactly when y becomes empty. For \(\xi \) to have the same number of stacks, m, as \(\eta \), it follows that \(\xi ^*=\eta ^*\).

3.:

Suppose that \(\zeta _x = 0\). Then, since the absorption of \((\mathcal {B}_k(t))_{t\ge 0}\) triggers at least one additional loss of a stack of particles, the absorbed configuration \(\xi \) has strictly less number of stacks than \(\eta \), which contradicts the assumption that \(\eta ,\xi \in \Omega _{k,m}\).

The above three cases conclude the proof of Proposition 5.12. \(\square \)

Remark 5.13

The irreducible structure in (5.24) may indeed get strictly finer than the one in (5.22). For example, consider the following H-shape graph G with \(V=\llbracket 1,6 \rrbracket \), \(c_{12}=c_{23}=c_{25}=c_{45}=c_{56}=1\), and \(c_{xy}=0\) otherwise (see Fig. 5). Then, \(\Omega _{4,4}\) is a singleton set with \(\Omega _{4,4}^1 = \{ \delta _1 + \delta _3 + \delta _4 + \delta _6 \}\), whereas \(\Omega _{5,4}\) has two irreducible components \(\Omega _{5,4}^{1,1}\) and \(\Omega _{5,4}^{1,2}\), given as

$$\begin{aligned} \Omega _{5,4}^{1,1}&= \{ 2\delta _1 + \delta _3 + \delta _4 + \delta _6 , \delta _1 + 2\delta _3 + \delta _4 + \delta _6 \} \\ \Omega _{5,4}^{1,2}&= \{ \delta _1 + \delta _3 + 2\delta _4 + \delta _6 , \delta _1 + \delta _3 + \delta _4 + 2\delta _6 \} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

5.3.3 Conclusion of the proof of Proposition 5.10

We are now ready to prove (5.15).

Proof of Proposition 5.10

Recall the definition of the matrix \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k}\) and its submatrices \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k,m}\) from (5.9) and (5.11), respectively.

First, we deal with the case \(k=m\). We split \(M_{\hat{\alpha },m,m}\) into \(\mathfrak n_m\) diagonal blocks

$$\begin{aligned}M_{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^1,\ldots ,M_{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^{\mathfrak n_m}\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

each corresponding to an irreducible sub-Markovian dynamics. Adopting an analogous notation, we further write the eigenvalue \(\lambda _{m,m}(\hat{\alpha })\) in (5.12) as

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda _{m,m}(\hat{\alpha }) = \lambda _{m,m}^1(\hat{\alpha }) \wedge \cdots \wedge \lambda _{m,m}^{\mathfrak n_m}(\hat{\alpha }) \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

where \(\lambda _{m,m}^i(\hat{\alpha })\) stands for the smallest eigenvalue of \(-M_{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^i\). By the Perron-Frobenius theorem, in view of sub-Markovianity, this eigenvalue is strictly positive. Furthermore, because of irreducibility, this is the only eigenvalue admitting a non-negative (actually, strictly positive) eigenfunction, which we refer to as

$$\begin{aligned} \psi _{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^i \in \mathbb {R}^{\Omega _{m,m}^i} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Let, for all \(i\in \llbracket 1,\mathfrak n_m \rrbracket \),

$$\begin{aligned} \Psi _{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^i (\eta ) :={\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \psi _{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^i (\eta ) & \text {if}\quad \eta \in \Omega _{m,m}^i \\ 0 & \text {if}\quad \eta \in \Omega _m \setminus \Omega _{m,m}^i \; \text {,}\;\;\end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$
(5.26)

be the extension of the eigenfunction \(\psi _{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^i\in \mathbb {R}^{\Omega _{m,m}^i}\) to \(\mathbb {R}^{\Omega _m}\). In particular, \(\Psi _{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^i\) is nonnegative on \(\Omega _m\) and strictly positive on \(\Omega _{m,m}^i\). Then, due to the block lower triangular structure of \(M_{\hat{\alpha },m}\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} M_{\hat{\alpha },m}\Psi _{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^i = -\lambda _{m,m}^i(\hat{\alpha })\, \Psi _{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^i\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.27)

Since \( \mathbb {R}^{\Omega _m}\ni \Psi _{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^i\ne 0\), this shows that a straightforward extension of \(\psi _{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^i\) produces an eigenfunction for \(M_{\hat{\alpha },m}\).

This canonical extension works well only for the case \(k=m\), while it does not for \(k>m\) in view of the block lower triangular structure of \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k}\).

For \(k>m\), we follow a different route. In view of Proposition 5.12, as done above for \(k=m\), we split \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k,m}\) into \(\mathfrak N^k_1 + \cdots + \mathfrak N^k_{\mathfrak n_m}\) diagonal blocks, say, \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k,m}^{i,j}\) for \(i \in \llbracket 1,\mathfrak n_m \rrbracket \) and \(j \in \llbracket 1,\mathfrak N_i^k \rrbracket \), each corresponding to the irreducible sub-Markovian dynamics on \(\Omega _{k,m}^{i,j}\). We refer to Fig. 6 for the overall structure of the matrix \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k}\). Then, we write the eigenvalue \(\lambda _{k,m}(\hat{\alpha })\) in (5.12) as

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda _{k,m}(\hat{\alpha }) = \min _{i \in \llbracket 1,\mathfrak n_m \rrbracket } \min _{j \in \llbracket 1,\mathfrak N_i^k \rrbracket } \lambda _{k,m}^{i,j}(\hat{\alpha }) \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(5.28)

where \(\lambda _{k,m}^{i,j}(\hat{\alpha })\) is the smallest eigenvalue of \(-M_{\hat{\alpha },k,m}^{i,j}\), which admits a strictly positive eigenfunction \(\psi _{\hat{\alpha },k,m}^{i,j}\in \mathbb {R}^{\Omega _{k,m}^{i,j}}\), again by the Perron-Frobenius theorem.

Now, instead of the canonical extension as in (5.26), we define \(\Psi _{\hat{\alpha },k,m}^i \in \mathbb {R}^{\Omega _k}\) as the “lifting” of \(\Psi _{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^i\) through the annihilation operators in (5.16):

$$\begin{aligned} \Psi _{\hat{\alpha },k,m}^i :=( \hat{\mathfrak {a}}_k \circ \cdots \circ \hat{\mathfrak {a}}_{m+1} ) \, \Psi _{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^i \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.29)

In view of the definition of the annihilation operators in (5.16) and \(\Psi _{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^i\ge 0\) (see (5.26)), one proves, by induction on \(k>m\),

$$\begin{aligned} \Psi _{\hat{\alpha },k,m}^i(\eta +\xi )\ge \Psi _{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^i(\eta )\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \eta \in \Omega _m\; \text {,}\;\;\eta +\xi \in \Omega _k\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.30)

Further, \(\Psi _{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^i>0\) on \(\Omega _{m,m}^i\) implies

$$\begin{aligned} \Psi _{\hat{\alpha },k,m}^i\ne 0\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.31)

By the intertwining relation (5.17) and the eigenvalue Eq. (5.27), we obtain

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} M_{\hat{\alpha },k} \Psi _{\hat{\alpha },k,m}^i&=M_{\hat{\alpha },k} \, \hat{\mathfrak {a}}_k \cdots \hat{\mathfrak {a}}_{m+1} \, \Psi _{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^i\\&= \hat{\mathfrak {a}}_k \cdots \hat{\mathfrak {a}}_{m+1} \, M_{\hat{\alpha },m} \, \Psi _{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^i = -\lambda _{m,m}^i(\hat{\alpha }) \, \Psi _{\hat{\alpha },k,m}^i \; \text {.}\end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$
(5.32)

The above identity and (5.31) ensure that \(\Psi _{\hat{\alpha },k,m}^i\in \mathbb {R}^{\Omega _k}\) is an eigenfunction for \(-M_{\hat{\alpha },k}\) associated to the eigenvalue \(\lambda _{m,m}^i(\hat{\alpha })\). Moreover, after recalling (5.29) and (5.16), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \Psi _{\hat{\alpha },k,m}^i(\eta ) = \sum _{\xi \in \Omega _m:\, \xi \le \eta } b_{k,m}(\eta ,\xi )\,\Psi _{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^i(\xi ) =0\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \eta \in \Omega _{k,m'}^i,\ m'<m\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

where the coefficients \(b_{k,m}(\eta ,\xi )\) are nonnegative and supported on

$$\begin{aligned}\Omega _m(\eta ):=\{\xi \in \Omega _m: \xi _x\le \eta _x\ \text {for all}\ x\in V\}\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

and the last identity used (5.26), \(m'<m\), and that

$$\begin{aligned}{ \eta \in \Omega _{k,m'}\ \text {and}\ \xi \in \Omega _m(\eta ) \Longrightarrow \xi \in \Omega _{m,m''}\ \text {for some}\ m''\le m'\; \text {.}} \end{aligned}$$

Because of the block lower triangular structure of \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k}\) and the irreducible decomposition (5.24), this ensures that the restriction

$$\begin{aligned} \Psi _{\hat{\alpha },k,m}^i\big |_{\Omega _{k,m}^{i,j}}\in \mathbb {R}^{\Omega _{k,m}^{i,j}} \end{aligned}$$

of \(\Psi _{\hat{\alpha },k,m}^i\in \mathbb {R}^{\Omega _{k}}\) to \(\mathbb {R}^{\Omega _{k,m}^{i,j}}\) is an eigenfunction for \(-M_{\hat{\alpha },k,m}^{i,j}\) with corresponding eigenvalue \(\lambda _{m,m}^i(\hat{\alpha })\). Finally, by the aforementioned positivity of \(\psi _{\hat{\alpha },m,m}^i\in \mathbb {R}^{\Omega _{m,m}^i}\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \Psi _{\hat{\alpha },k,m}^i (\eta ) > 0\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \text {for all} \ \eta \in \Omega _{k,m}^i \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Indeed, for \(\eta \in \Omega _{k,m}^i\), the configuration \(\eta ^*:=\sum _{x:\eta _x\ge 1}\delta _x\) belongs to \(\Omega _{m,m}^i\), and (5.30) applies with \(\xi =\eta -\eta ^*\) (see Fig. 6 for a visual proof). Since \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k,m}^{i,j}\) admits \(\psi _{\hat{\alpha },k,m}^{i,j}\in \mathbb {R}^{\Omega _{k,m}^i}\) as the only positive eigenfunction (up to constant multiples), by the Perron-Frobenius theorem, we have

$$\begin{aligned} \Psi _{\hat{\alpha },k,m}^i\big |_{\Omega _{k,m}^{i,j}} = c \, \psi _{\hat{\alpha },k,m}^{i,j} \quad \text {for some } c>0 \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

and, thus, \(\lambda _{m,m}^i(\hat{\alpha }) = \lambda _{k,m}^{i,j}(\hat{\alpha })\). By (5.28), the desired claim in (5.15) follows. \(\square \)

5.4 \(\lambda _{k,k}(\hat{\alpha })\) and k coalescing particles

This subsection is devoted to the proof of a monotonicity in k of \(\lambda _{k,k}(\hat{\alpha })\), defined in (5.12) when \(\Omega _{k,k}\ne \varnothing \), and set equal to \(+\infty \) when \(\Omega _{k,k}=\varnothing \) (see Corollary 5.7).

Proposition 5.14

For all integers \(k\ge 3\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda _{k,k}(\hat{\alpha }) \ge \lambda _{k-1,k-1}(\hat{\alpha })\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.33)

Consequently,

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda _{k,k}(\hat{\alpha }) \ge \lambda _{2,2}(\hat{\alpha })\; \text {,}\;\;\quad k \ge 2 \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.34)

Proof

Fix \(k\ge 3\). As \(\Omega _{k-1,k-1}=\varnothing \) implies \(\Omega _{k,k}=\varnothing \), we may assume that \(\Omega _{k,k}\ne \varnothing \).

On \(\Omega _{k,k}\), the dynamics described by \(M_{\hat{\alpha },k,k}\) coincides with k independent copies of \(\textrm{RW}(\hat{\alpha })=\textrm{RW}(G,\hat{\alpha })\) killed upon exiting \(\Omega _{k,k}\). For \(\xi \in \Omega _{k,k}\), write \(\mathsf P_\xi \) for the law of this process started from \(\xi \), and define \(\tau _k\ge 0\) as its killing time. Since the killed chain is finite-state,

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda _{k,k}(\hat{\alpha }) = -\lim _{t\rightarrow \infty } \frac{1}{t} \log \max _{\xi \in \Omega _{k,k}} \mathsf P_\xi [\tau _k>t] \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.35)

Fix \(\xi \in \Omega _{k,k}\), and choose \(z\in V\) such that \(\xi _z=1\). Set \( \xi ^-:=\xi -\delta _z\in \Omega _{k-1,k-1}\). We couple the \((k-1)\)-particle process started from \(\xi ^-\) with the process obtained from the k-particle dynamics started from \(\xi \) by ignoring the particle initially at z. Under this coupling,

$$\begin{aligned} \tau _k \le \tau _{k-1} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Indeed, any contact among the retained \(k-1\) particles kills both systems, while contacts involving the ignored particle kill only the k-particle system. Hence, for all \(t\ge 0\),

$$\begin{aligned} \mathsf P_\xi [\tau _k>t] \le \mathsf P_{\xi ^-}[\tau _{k-1}>t] \le \max _{\xi '\in \Omega _{k-1,k-1}} \mathsf P_{\xi '}[\tau _{k-1}>t] \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

By taking the maximum over \(\xi \in \Omega _{k,k}\), the above bound and (5.35) give (5.33). \(\square \)

5.5 Proofs of Theorems 2.3 and 2.4

We are ready to prove Theorems 2.3 and 2.4.

Proof of Theorem 2.3

By Corollary 5.2 and (5.13) (we omit G from the notation),

$$\begin{aligned} \lim _{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0} \frac{\textrm{gap}_k(\varepsilon \hat{\alpha })}{\varepsilon }= \textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW} (\hat{\alpha }) \wedge \min _{m \in \llbracket 2,k \rrbracket } \lambda _{k,m}(\hat{\alpha }) \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(5.36)

whereas by (5.15) and (5.34),

$$\begin{aligned} \min _{m \in \llbracket 2,k \rrbracket } \lambda _{k,m}(\hat{\alpha }) = \lambda _{2,2}(\hat{\alpha }) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Hence, since the limit in (5.36) is strictly positive, Theorem 2.3 holds true. \(\square \)

Proof of Theorem 2.4

Recall that, for all graphs G and site weights \(\hat{\alpha }\), we have \({\textrm{gap}_1(G, \varepsilon \hat{\alpha })} =\varepsilon \, \textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW} (G,\hat{\alpha }) \), \(\varepsilon >0\); moreover, by (5.36) for \(k=2\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \lim _{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0} \frac{\textrm{gap}_2(G, \varepsilon \hat{\alpha })}{\varepsilon } = \textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW} (G,\hat{\alpha }) \wedge \lambda _{2,2}(G,\hat{\alpha }) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Thus, it suffices to verify the existence of G and \(\hat{\alpha }\) such that

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda _{2,2} (G,\hat{\alpha }) < \textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW} (G,\hat{\alpha }) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.37)

In particular, let us recall from the discussion in Sect. 5.4, that \(\lambda _{2,2}\) is the smallest eigenvalue of (the negative of) the generator describing two independent \(\textrm{RW}(G,\hat{\alpha })\), having \(\hat{\alpha }=(\hat{\alpha }_x)_{x\in V}\) as a (non-normalized) reversible measure, and which get instantaneously killed when attempting to get to a mutual graph-distance strictly smaller than two.

In view of this, it is not surprising that the standard discrete torus \(\mathbb {T}_N^d\), with dimension \(d\ge 2\), size \(N\in \mathbb {N}\), and homogeneous site weights, satisfies (5.37), provided that N is large enough. From now on, we fix \(\hat{\alpha }\equiv 1\), \(d\ge 2\), \(N\in \mathbb {N}\), and

$$\begin{aligned} G=\mathbb {T}_N^d\; \text {,}\;\;\text {with}\ c_{xy}\equiv 1\ \text {for all neighboring sites}\ x,y\in \mathbb {T}_N^d\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

and drop them from the notation, simply writing, e.g., \(\lambda _{2,2}\) and \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}\) instead of \(\lambda _{2,2}(G,\hat{\alpha })\) and \(\textrm{RW}(G,\hat{\alpha })\). Later on, we will take the limit \(N\rightarrow \infty \).

Consider the positions \((X_t,Y_t)_{t\ge 0}\) of two independent particles on \(\mathbb {T}_N^d\), each one jumping with unit rate to any of its neighbors. Observe that \((X_t, Y_t)_{t\ge 0}\) is reversible with respect to the uniform measure \(\kappa =\kappa _{N,d}\) on \(\mathbb {T}_N^d \times \mathbb {T}_N^d\). In what follows, let \(\mathsf P_{(x,y)}\) and \(\mathsf E_{(x,y)}\) denote the law and corresponding expectation with respect to the law of \((X_t,Y_t)_{t\ge 0}\) when starting from \((x,y)\in \mathbb {T}_N^d\times \mathbb {T}_N^d\). It is well known (see, e.g., [LP17, Sections 12.3.1 and 12.4]) that, for all \(N\in \mathbb {N}\) large enough and all \(d\ge 1\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} {\textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW} =2\left( 1-\cos \frac{2\pi }{N}\right) \ge \frac{\pi ^2}{N^2}\; \text {.}} \end{aligned}$$
(5.38)

Letting

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {W}:=\{(x,y)\in \mathbb {T}^d_N: \textrm{dist}_{\mathbb {T}_N^d}(x,y)\ge 2\} \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

by the second inequality in [Her23, Eq. (1.7)], we have

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda _{2,2} \le \frac{1}{\max _{ \mathcal {U}\subseteq \mathcal {W}} \mathsf E_{\kappa ( \,\cdot \,| \mathcal {U})} [ \tau _{\mathcal {U}^\complement }] }\le \frac{1}{\mathsf E_{\kappa ( \,\cdot \,| \mathcal {W})} [ \tau _{\mathcal {W}^\complement }] } \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(5.39)

where in the second step we substituted \(\mathcal {U}=\mathcal {W}\). The last denominator in (5.39) is almost the mean meeting time of two independent random walks, both being uniformly distributed on \(\mathbb {T}_N^d\) (and independently) at time \(t=0\). This latter quantity is known [Cox89, Theorem 4] to asymptotically diverge, as \(N\rightarrow \infty \), like \(s_d(N)\) given in (2.6). In the remainder of this proof, we show that the denominator in (5.39) follows the same asymptotic behavior.

Since \(\kappa (\mathcal {W}) = 1 - \frac{2d+1}{N^d}\), we calculate

$$\begin{aligned} \mathsf E_{\kappa ( \,\cdot \,\mid \Omega )} [ \tau _{\mathcal {W}^\complement }] = \left( 1-\frac{2d+1}{N^d}\right) ^{-1} \frac{1}{N^{2d}} \sum _{(x,y)\in \mathcal {W}} \mathsf E_{(x,y)} [\tau _{\mathcal {W}^\complement }] \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.40)

Moreover, since \(Z_t:=X_t-Y_t\in \mathbb {T}^d_N\) is a simple random walk (jumping to a nearest neighbor at rate 2), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \mathsf E_{(x,y)} [\tau _{\mathcal {W}^\complement }] = \widehat{\mathsf E}_{x-y} [\hat{\tau }_1] \; \text {,}\;\;\quad (x,y)\in \mathcal {W}\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(5.41)

where we use the hat-notation to refer to quantities related to \(Z_t\), while \(\hat{\tau }_\ell \), \(\ell \in \llbracket 0,\lfloor N/2\rfloor \rrbracket \), denotes the first hitting time of the set

$$\begin{aligned} \{z\in \mathbb {T}^d_N: \textrm{dist}_{\mathbb {T}_N^d}(z,0)=\ell \}\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

By the strong Markov property and translation invariance of the dynamics, we have

$$\begin{aligned} \widehat{\mathsf E}_{x-y} [\hat{\tau }_0] = \widehat{\mathsf E}_{x-y} [\hat{\tau }_1] + \widehat{\mathsf E}_{e_1} [\hat{\tau }_0] = \widehat{\mathsf E}_{x-y} [\hat{\tau }_1] + \widehat{\mathsf E}_0 [\hat{\tau }_0^+] - \widehat{\mathsf E}_0 [\hat{\tau }_1] \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

where \(\hat{\tau }_0^+\) denotes the first return time to 0 for the walk \(Z_t\) started in 0, i.e.,

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\tau }_0^+:=\inf \{t> \hat{\tau }_1: Z_t=0\}\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Clearly, \(\widehat{\mathsf E}_0\, [\hat{\tau }_1] = \frac{1}{4d}\), while, by Kac’s formula for return times (see, e.g., [AF02, p. 34, Lemma 2.25]), we obtain

$$\begin{aligned} \widehat{\mathsf E}_0 [\hat{\tau }_0^+] = \frac{N^d}{4d}\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Hence, we get

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} \frac{1}{N^{2d}} \sum _{(x,y) \in \mathcal {W}} \widehat{\mathsf E}_{x-y} [\hat{\tau }_1]&= \frac{1}{N^{2d}} \sum _{(x,y) \in \mathcal {W}} \left( \widehat{\mathsf E}_{x-y} [\hat{\tau }_0] - \widehat{\mathsf E}_0 [\hat{\tau }_0^+] + \widehat{\mathsf E}_0 [\hat{\tau }_1] \right) \\&\ge \left\{ \frac{1}{N^{2d}}\sum _{(x,y) \in \mathcal {W}} \widehat{\mathsf E}_{x-y} [\hat{\tau }_0]\right\} - \frac{N^d}{4d} \; \text {.}\end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$
(5.42)

By [Cox89, Theorem 4] (see also [Dur24, Lemma 7.3.2] for a more recent textbook version), the expression between curly brackets is, for all \(N\in \mathbb {N}\) large enough, bounded below by \(\mathfrak g_d\,s_d(N)\), for some positive constant \(\mathfrak g_d\) (which, for \(d\ge 3\), is strictly larger than \(\frac{1}{4d}\), cf. [Cox89, Eq. (1.2)]). Hence, collecting (5.40), (5.41) and (5.42), we obtain, for some \(C>0\) (possibly depending only on \(d\ge 2\), but not on \(N\in \mathbb {N}\)) and for all \(N\in \mathbb {N}\) large enough,

$$\begin{aligned} {\mathsf E}_{\kappa ( \,\cdot \,| \mathcal {W})} [ \tau _{\mathcal {W}^\complement }] \ge C s_d(N)\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

which, along with (5.39), implies

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda _{2,2} \le \frac{1}{Cs_d(N)} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Comparing this inequality with (5.38) (cf. (2.6)) completes the proof of the theorem. \(\square \)

We conclude this section with a remark on the role of the directional limits in (2.8) in proving Theorem 2.3.

Remark 5.15

(Directional vs. general limits) Theorem 2.3 is stated for directional limits \(\alpha =\varepsilon \hat{\alpha }\), with \(\hat{\alpha }\in (0,\infty )^V\) fixed and \(\varepsilon \rightarrow 0\); see (2.8). Let us briefly explain what would be needed in order to pass from (2.8) to a completely general limit \(\alpha \rightarrow 0\).

A natural target would be the lower bound (we omit G from the notation)

$$\begin{aligned} \liminf _{\alpha \rightarrow 0} \frac{\textrm{gap}_k(\alpha )}{\textrm{gap}_2(\alpha )} \ge 1\; \text {,}\;\;\quad k\ge 3\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(5.43)

Since, by (2.7), one has \(\textrm{gap}_k(\alpha )\le \textrm{gap}_2(\alpha )\) for all \(k\ge 3\), (5.43) would imply the full limit

$$\begin{aligned} \lim _{\alpha \rightarrow 0} \frac{\textrm{gap}_k(\alpha )}{\textrm{gap}_2(\alpha )} =1\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

By the sequential characterization of the liminf, it suffices to consider arbitrary sequences \(\alpha _n\rightarrow 0\), as \(n\rightarrow \infty \), in \((0,\infty )^V\). By setting

$$\begin{aligned} \varepsilon _n:=(\alpha _n)_\textrm{max}=\Vert \alpha _n\Vert _\infty \; \text {,}\;\;\quad \beta _n:=\varepsilon _n^{-1}\alpha _n\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

we have \(\varepsilon _n\rightarrow 0\) and, up to extracting a subsequence, \(\beta _n\rightarrow \hat{\alpha }\), for some \(\hat{\alpha }\in [0,1]^V\) with \(\max _{x\in V}\hat{\alpha }_x=1\). Along such a subsequence, the analogue of (5.1) reads as

$$\begin{aligned} \varepsilon _n^{-1}L_{\alpha _n,k} = A_{\beta _n,k}+\varepsilon _n^{-1}B_k\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

where \(A_{\beta _n,k}\) is the slow part and \(B_k\) is the fast part of the generator, see (5.2) and (5.3). Since \(A_{\beta _n,k}\rightarrow A_{\hat{\alpha },k}\) as finite matrices, the slow–fast reduction used in Proposition 5.1 applies, with minor notational changes, also to this n-dependent situation. The limiting effective generator \(\mathscr {G}_{\hat{\alpha },k}\) is given by the same formula there.

If \(\hat{\alpha }\in (0,\infty )^V\), then the rest of the proof of Theorem 2.3 goes through without changes: the limiting quantities \(w_k(\hat{\alpha })\) and \(\lambda _{k,m}(\hat{\alpha })\) appearing in (5.7) and (5.12) are exactly those analyzed in Sects. 5.2–5.4. Thus, strictly positive subsequential directions do not create any additional difficulty.

The only genuinely new case is when \(\hat{\alpha }_x=0\) for some \(x\in V\). Then, the limiting effective generator may acquire additional zero eigenvalues: sites with \(\hat{\alpha }_x=0\) may generate further transient states and, depending on the graph, may also disconnect irreducible components of the limiting effective dynamics. In particular, the effective gap at scale \(\varepsilon _n=(\alpha _n)_\textrm{max}\) may vanish, so that the relevant gaps may be of smaller order than \(\varepsilon _n\). In such a case, the first-order slow–fast limit does not determine the ratio \(\textrm{gap}_k(\alpha _n)/\textrm{gap}_2(\alpha _n)\); one would have to analyze the next non-vanishing scales of the coordinates of \(\alpha _n\). Although one expects the same two-particle mechanism to arise also through this further multi-scale analysis, we do not pursue this extension here, and prefer to state Theorem 2.3 in the clean directional regime with \(\hat{\alpha }\in (0,\infty )^V\) fixed.

6 Non-conservative SIP. Setting and Main Results

In this section, we attach particle reservoirs to some sites of the graph. We describe this by introducing two sets of non-negative site parameters, \(\omega =(\omega _x)_{x\in V}\) and \(\theta =(\theta _x)_{x\in V}\). The former one represents the rates of interaction between a site and its reservoir. The latter one prescribes the reservoirs’ particle densities. Next to the inclusion particles’ motion considered so far, we now let particles be created and annihilated with rates resembling those of \(\textrm{SIP}\).

6.1 Model

Let \(G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})\) and \(\alpha =(\alpha _x)_{x\in V}\) be as in Sect. 2, and introduce some non-negative site weights \(\omega =(\omega _x)_{x\in V}\) and \(\theta =(\theta _x)_{x\in V}\). Then, we write \(\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta )\) for the Markov process onFootnote 2\(\Xi :=\cup _{k=0}^\infty \, \Xi _k\) with infinitesimal generator \(L_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta }\) given, for a bounded function \(f\in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi }\) and a configuration \(\eta \in \Xi \), by

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned}&L_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta }f(\eta )= \sum _{x,y\in V}c_{xy}\,\eta _x\left( \alpha _y+\eta _y\right) \left( f(\eta -\delta _x+\delta _y)-f(\eta )\right) \\&+ \sum _{x\in V}\omega _x\,\big \{ \eta _x\left( 1+\theta _x\right) \left( f(\eta -\delta _x)-f(\eta )\right) +\theta _x\left( \alpha _x+\eta _x\right) \left( f(\eta +\delta _x)-f(\eta )\right) \big \}\; \text {.}\end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$
(6.1)

Here, the first summation on the right-hand side corresponds with the conservative dynamics described by \(L_{G,\alpha ,k}\) in (2.1), and is referred to as bulk dynamics. The second summation concerns the particle creation-annihilation mechanism, and may be interpreted as a bulk-boundary dynamics. More in detail, particles at \(x\in V\) get killed independently, each at rate \(\omega _x\left( 1+\theta _x\right) \), while a new particle is created at \(x\in V\) with a configuration-dependent rate \(\omega _x\,\theta _x\left( \alpha _x+\eta _x\right) \). Furthermore, albeit the configuration space \(\Xi \) is countably infinite, \(\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta )\) does not explode in finite time. Indeed, the total number of particles is stochastically dominated by a pure birth process with linearly growing rates (see, e.g., [FGS22, Proposition 2.1]).

The long-time behavior of \(\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta )\) clearly depends on the values of \(\omega \) and \(\theta \). To start with, we mention that \(\omega _x=0\) means that site \(x\in V\) does not directly exchange particles with any reservoirs, whereas \(\theta _x=0\) corresponds to having a purely absorbing reservoir at \(x\in V\). As a consequence, if \(\omega \equiv 0\), we recover the conservative case having, for any total number of particles \(k\in \mathbb {N}\), \(\mu _{\alpha ,k}\) in (2.2) as a unique reversible measure. If \(\theta \equiv 0\) and \(\omega \not \equiv 0\), the system eventually empties out. In other words, if \(\theta \) vanishes on the support of \(\omega \), that is,

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{supp}(\omega ):=\{x\in V: \omega _x > 0\}\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

then \(\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta )\) has the empty configuration \(\emptyset \in \Xi _0\) as its unique stationary state, which, in turn, is absorbing. More generally, for \(\omega \not \equiv 0\), there exists a unique stationary measure for \(\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta )\), referred to as \(\mu _{G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta }\). In this situation, one typically distinguishes between two scenarios, commonly referred to as equilibrium and non-equilibrium, respectively.

More in detail, equilibrium occurs if \(\theta \) is constant on \(\textrm{supp}(\omega )\). In this case, \(\mu _{G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta }\) is reversible, in product form, and explicit (see, e.g., [CGGR13, FRS22]): assuming that \(\theta \equiv \varrho > 0\), then \( \mu _{G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta }= \nu _{\alpha ,\varrho }\), where, for all \(\eta \in \Xi \),

$$\begin{aligned} \nu _{\alpha ,\varrho }(\eta ):=\frac{1}{\mathcal {Z}_{\alpha ,\varrho }} \left( \frac{\varrho }{1+\varrho }\right) ^{|\eta |} \prod _{x\in V}\frac{\Gamma (\alpha _x+\eta _x)}{\Gamma (\alpha _x)\,\eta _x!}\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \text {with}\ \mathcal {Z}_{\alpha ,\varrho }:=(1+\varrho )^{|\alpha |}\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(6.2)

Note that \(\mu _{\alpha ,k}\) in (2.2) is the canonical measure associated to the grand-canonical \(\nu _{\alpha ,\varrho }\). One speaks of non-equilibrium if \(\theta \) is non-constant on \(\textrm{supp}(\omega )\). Then, \(\mu _{G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta }\) is, in general, neither reversible, nor in product form, nor explicit. We will not need to know more on the structure of this steady state; for further details, see, e.g., [FRS22] and references therein.

6.2 Spectral gap identity when \(\theta \equiv 0\)

We start with the analysis of the purely absorbing \(\textrm{SIP}\). As already anticipated in Sect. 1.4, we shall see that understanding the case \(\theta \equiv 0\) is one of the key ingredients for the spectral analysis of the setting with general \(\theta \).

Observe that substituting \(\theta \equiv 0\) in (6.1) gives rise to a system in which all particles are never created, and get annihilated one at the time, until the system eventually gets empty. In particular, \(L_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,0}\), interpreted as an infinite matrix, is similar to a block lower triangular one. Moreover, the blocks on the diagonal — each for any total number of particles \(k\in \mathbb {N}_0\) in the bulk of the system — are of finite size and describe the following finite-state Markov chains. The case \(k=1\) corresponds to a process with just one particle in the bulk, which moves as \(\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha )\) defined in Sect. 2, additionally killed at rate \(\omega _x\ge 0\) when sitting on \(x\in V\). We refer to this process as \(\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha ,\omega )\). The case \(k\ge 2\) is similar, with k interacting particles evolving in the bulk as \(\textrm{SIP}_k(G,\alpha )\), each independently killed with rate \(\omega _x\ge 0\) when sitting at \(x\in V\). Each of these Markov chains, say \(\textrm{SIP}_k(G,\alpha ,\omega )\), is sub-stochastic. Moreover, since the conservative \(\textrm{SIP}_k(G,\alpha )\) admits \(\mu _{\alpha ,k}\) in (2.2) as its unique reversible measure, all eigenvalues of (the negative of) the generator of \(\textrm{SIP}_k(G,\alpha ,\omega )\) (see (7.1)) are real; as the conservative system is irreducible, \(\textrm{supp}(\omega )\ne \varnothing \) further ensures that such eigenvalues are strictly positive. Letting \(\textrm{gap}_k(G,\alpha ,\omega )\), \(k\ge 1\), be the lowest of such eigenvalues, we write (cf. (2.4))

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha ,\omega )=\inf _{k\ge 1}\textrm{gap}_k(G,\alpha ,\omega )\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(6.3)

Similarly, we introduce the spectral gap of \(\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha ,\omega )\) as

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha ,\omega )= \textrm{gap}_1(G,\alpha ,\omega )\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(6.4)

Our main result of this section establishes that, as soon as the killing rate \(\omega \) is not identically zero, the spectral gaps in (6.3) and (6.4) coincide, regardless of the underlying geometry and of the site weights — even when \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\in (0,1)\). This spectral gap identity stands in stark contrast to the behavior observed in the conservative setting, where restrictions are imposed on the value of \(\alpha _\textrm{min}\).

Theorem 6.1

(\(\theta \equiv 0\)) For all graphs \(G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})\), and site weights \(\alpha =(\alpha _x)_{x\in V}\), \(\omega =(\omega _x)_{x\in V}\) with \(\omega \ne 0\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha ,\omega )= \textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha ,\omega )\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Remarkably, the proof of the above identity (deferred to Sect. 7.1) is quite elementary.

6.3 General setting

When dealing with the general case \(\theta \ne 0\), we must specify the infinite-dimensional function analytic setting that we work in. For this purpose, we define a class of functions on \(\Xi \) (Definition 6.2), written in terms of orthogonal  “Meixner” polynomial dualities introduced in [FRS22, Section 4], which take the following form: for all \(\varrho \ge 0\),

$$\begin{aligned} D_{\alpha ,\varrho }(\xi ,\eta )=\prod _{x\in V}d_{\alpha _x,\varrho }(\xi _x,\eta _x)\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \eta \in \Xi \; \text {,}\;\;\xi \in \Xi _k\; \text {,}\;\;k\ge 0\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(6.5)

where, for \(\varrho =0\),

$$\begin{aligned} d_{\alpha _x,0}(\xi _x,\eta _x) = \frac{\eta _x!}{\left( \eta _x-\xi _x\right) !}\frac{\Gamma (\alpha _x)}{\Gamma (\alpha _x+\xi _x)}\mathbb {1}_{\left\{ \xi _x\le \eta _x\right\} }\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

whereas, for \(\varrho >0\),

$$\begin{aligned} d_{\alpha _x,\varrho }(\xi _x,\eta _x) =\sum _{\ell _x=0}^{\xi _x}\left( {\begin{array}{c}\xi _x\\ \ell _x\end{array}}\right) \,d_{\alpha _x,0}(\ell _x,\eta _x)\left( -\varrho \right) ^{\xi _x-\ell _x}= \left( -\varrho \right) ^{\xi _k}{{}_2F_1(-\xi _x,-\eta _x;\alpha _x;-\varrho ^{-1})} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

This last identity is just a rewriting in terms of the ordinary hypergeometric function. Let us observe that \(D_{\alpha ,\varrho }(\emptyset ,\,\cdot \,)\equiv 1\) and that, for all \(k\in \mathbb {N}\) and \(\xi \in \Xi _k\), \(\eta \in \Xi \longmapsto D_{\alpha ,\varrho }(\xi ,\eta )\in \mathbb {R}\) is a k-th order polynomial in \(\eta =(\eta _x)_{x\in V}\). Moreover, as we shall show in Sect. 7.2 below,

$$\begin{aligned} \{D_{\alpha ,\varrho }(\xi ,\,\cdot \,): |\xi |\le k\}\end{aligned}$$
(6.6)

is a linearly independent set which spans the space of polynomials in \(\eta \) of order \(\le k\). This and the two other properties presented in Proposition 7.2 below turn the functions in (6.5) into a convenient basis of polynomials on \(\Xi \).

Definition 6.2

For all \(\varrho > 0\), integers \(k\ge 1\), and functions \(\psi \in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\), define

$$\begin{aligned} F_{\alpha ,\varrho }^\psi (\eta ):=\sum _{\xi \in \Xi _k}\mu _{\alpha ,k}(\xi )\,\psi (\xi )\, D_{\alpha ,\varrho }(\xi ,\eta )\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \eta \in \Xi \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(6.7)

When \(k=0\) and, thus, \(\psi \in \mathbb {R}\) is a scalar, we conventionally set \(F_{\alpha ,\varrho }^\psi \equiv \psi \in \mathbb {R}\).

Remark that, as long as \(\psi \ne 0\), the function in (6.7) is a non-vanishing k-th order polynomial in the variables \(\eta =(\eta _x)_{x\in V}\). In particular, expressions like \(L_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta } F_{\alpha ,\varrho }^\psi \) will always be well defined. Finally, introduce the operators \(\mathfrak b_{\alpha ,\omega ,\varrho ,k-1}^\theta : \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _{k-1}}\), \(k\ge 1\), acting on functions \(\psi \in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\) as

$$\begin{aligned} \mathfrak b_{\alpha ,\omega ,\varrho ,k-1}^\theta \psi (\zeta ):=k\sum _{x\in V}\omega _x\left( \theta _x-\varrho \right) \frac{\alpha _x+\zeta _x}{|\alpha |+k-1}\,\psi (\zeta +\delta _x)\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \zeta \in \Xi _{k-1}\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(6.8)

Clearly, we have

$$\begin{aligned} \mathfrak b_{\alpha ,\omega ,\varrho ,k-1}^\theta = 0\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \text {if}\quad \theta \equiv \varrho \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(6.9)

The following theorem, whose proof is postponed to Sect. 7.2 below, is stated for any graph G and site weights \(\alpha \), \(\omega \), and \(\theta \).

Theorem 6.3

Fix \(\varrho >0\) and recall Definition 6.2.

  1. (a)

    Given \(k\ge 0\), any eigenpair \(\lambda ,\psi \) of the k-particle purely-absorbing system solves

    $$\begin{aligned} L_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta }F_{\alpha ,\varrho }^\psi = -\lambda \, F_{\alpha ,\varrho }^{\psi } + F_{\alpha ,\varrho }^{\mathfrak b_{\alpha ,\omega ,\varrho ,k-1}^\theta \psi }\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
    (6.10)
  2. (b)

    The functions \(F_{\alpha ,\varrho }^\psi \) as in Definition 6.2 may be chosen to form an orthogonal basis of \(L^2(\nu _{\alpha ,\varrho })\).

Let us comment on two powerful consequences of the above theorem in the reversible case \(\theta \equiv \varrho \) (we discuss the non-reversible case in Remark 6.5 below; see also Remark 6.6 for a comparison with the symmetric exclusion process). The first item tells us that any eigenvalue of the purely absorbing system with \(k\ge 1\) particles is one also for the general system and, moreover, it provides a constructive procedure to build eigenfunctions from those of the k-particle absorbing system. Indeed, by (6.9), the identity in (6.10) becomes the eigenvalue equation

$$\begin{aligned} L_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta }F_{\alpha ,\varrho }^\psi = -\lambda \, F_{\alpha ,\varrho }^\psi \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

because the second term on the right-hand side of (6.10) vanishes when \(\theta \equiv \varrho \). The second item guarantees the existence of a complete orthonormal system of eigenfunctions of \(L_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta }\). This, combined with the fact that the generator is self-adjoint in the associated \(L^2\)-space, ensures that \(-L_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta }\) has a pure point spectrum therein, all contained in \([0,\infty )\), with a gap after the zero eigenvalue coinciding with that of the purely absorbing system. Letting

$$\begin{aligned}\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha ,\omega ,\varrho )\end{aligned}$$

denote this spectral gap when \(\theta \equiv \varrho >0\), Theorem 6.1 readily implies the following result.

Corollary 6.4

(\(\theta \equiv \varrho \)) For all graphs \(G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})\), and site weights \(\alpha =(\alpha _x)_{x\in V}\), \(\omega =(\omega _x)_{x\in V}\) with \(\omega \ne 0\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha ,\omega ,\varrho ) = \textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha ,\omega )\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \varrho > 0\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

The above result is stated in the reversible setting \(\theta \equiv \varrho \). The next two remarks clarify the corresponding non-reversible picture: for the infinite-state \(\textrm{SIP}\) one has to distinguish algebraic eigenfunction lifting from a full spectral statement on a prescribed function space (Remark 6.5), whereas in finite-state analogues such as the partial symmetric exclusion process this distinction disappears (Remark 6.6).

Remark 6.5

(Non-reversible setting) In view of Theorem 6.3, it is plausible to expect that Corollary 6.4 should extend, in some sense, to the non-reversible setting as well. Indeed, since the two functions on the right-hand side of (6.10) are polynomials of order k and \(\ell \le k-1\), this guarantees that \(\lambda \) therein lies in the point spectrum of \(L_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta }\), for all \(\theta =(\theta _x)_{x\in V}\). However, in absence of a natural Hilbert space ensuring that such eigenfunctions form a complete orthonormal system when \(\theta \not \equiv \mathrm{const.}\) on \(\textrm{supp}(\omega )\), the presence, e.g., of a continuous part of the spectrum (a property which also depends on the choice of the underlying reference function space) is what Theorem 6.3 does not exclude.

Remark 6.6

(Symmetric exclusion processes & Co.) The proof of Theorem 6.3(a) — based on orthogonal-polynomial dualities, annihilation and creation operators — carries over to other non-equilibrium systems enjoying similar properties, e.g., [RS18, FRS22, GRT25]. As an example, we mention the symmetric (partial) exclusion process (\(\textrm{SEP}\)) in contact with reservoirs (see, e.g., [FRS22, Sal23, Sal24]), whose generator is readily obtained from \(L_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta }\) in (6.1) by further imposing \(\alpha =(\alpha _x)_{x\in V}\subset \mathbb {N}\) and \(\theta =(\theta _x)_{x\in V}\in [0,1]\), and changing the plus signs in \(\alpha _x+\eta _x\) and \(1+\theta _x\) therein into minus signs. In this case, the configuration space is simply \(\prod _{x\in V}\{0,1,\ldots ,\alpha _x\}\), thus finite, and the technical issues explained in Remark 6.5 do not arise: the spectrum is just the union of finitely many eigenvalues.

As our proof arguments used for \(\textrm{SIP}\) adapt to the open \(\textrm{SEP}\), one obtains analogues of Theorems 6.1 and 6.3: on the one hand, the eigenvalues of the open \(\textrm{SEP}\) with arbitrary reservoir densities \(\theta \in [0,1]^V\) coincide with those of the purely absorbing case \(\theta \equiv 0\), and are therefore independent of \(\theta \); on the other hand, importing the notation used above, one has \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SEP}(G,\alpha ,\omega )=\textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha ,\omega )\). These two results further ensure that

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_\textrm{SEP}(G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta ) = \textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha ,\omega )\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \theta \in [0,1]^V \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

This, thus, recovers, in the reversible case, the spectral-gap identity of [Sal23, Corollary 1], and, for general reservoir densities, the corresponding result of [Sal24, Theorem 2].

7 Proofs of Theorems 6.1 and 6.3

The following two sections may be essentially read independently.

7.1 Proof of Theorem 6.1

For every \(k\ge 1\), the k-particle purely absorbing system (i.e., when \(\theta \equiv 0\)) is described by the infinitesimal generator

$$\begin{aligned} L_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,k}f(\eta ):=L_{G,\alpha ,k}f(\eta ) - f(\eta )\sum _{x\in V}\omega _x\, \eta _x \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(7.1)

where \(f\in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\), \(\eta \in \Xi _k\), and \(L_{G,\alpha ,k}\) given in (2.1). Equivalently, if \(f\in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\) is extended to \(\Xi \) by setting \(f\equiv 0\) on \(\Xi \setminus \Xi _k\), then the restriction to \(\Xi _k\) of the generator in (6.1) with \(\theta \equiv 0\) is precisely the operator in (7.1). Note that, when the process is in configuration \(\eta \in \Xi _k\), it gets killed with rate \(\sum _{x\in V}\omega _x\,\eta _x\ge 0\).

Recall the definition of the probability measure \(\mu _{\alpha ,k}\) in (2.2). Since \(L_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,k}\) is the sum of a self-adjoint operator on \(L^2(\mu _{\alpha ,k})\) and a multiplicative one, it is self-adjoint on \(L^2(\mu _{\alpha ,k})\) and, thus, all the eigenvalues of \(-L_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,k}\) are real and non-negative. Additionally, since the conservative dynamics is irreducible and the multiplication operator is non-positive, all such eigenvalues are actually strictly positive. In particular, we obtain that \(\textrm{gap}_k(G,\alpha ,\omega )\), the lowest of such eigenvalues, is positive and, by the Perron-Frobenius theorem, characterized as the worst-case exponential killing rate for large times: letting \(\tau _k\) denote the first time at which one of the k particles gets killed,

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_k(G,\alpha ,\omega )= - \lim _{t\rightarrow \infty }\frac{1}{t}\log \max _{\eta \in \Xi _k} \mathbb {P}_\eta ^{G,\alpha ,\omega ,k}[\tau _k>t] \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Thanks to this asymptotic identity, the assertion of Theorem 6.1 boils down to show that, for all \(k\ge 2\),

$$\begin{aligned} \max _{\eta \in \Xi _k}\mathbb {P}_\eta ^{G,\alpha ,\omega ,k}[\tau _k>t]\le \max _{x\in V} \mathbb {P}_{\delta _x}^{G,\alpha ,\omega ,1}[\tau _1>t]\; \text {,}\;\;\quad t\ge 0\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(7.2)

For this purpose, we introduce a lookdown representation of the particle system with killing, extending the one known within the conservative setting [KS24, Appendix A]. We need to introduce a bit of notation. For every \(k\ge 2\), labeled configuration \(\textbf{x}=(x_1,\ldots , x_k)\in V^k\), and permutation \(\varsigma \in S_k\), we write \(\varsigma \textbf{x}= (x_{\varsigma (1)},\ldots ,x_{\varsigma (k)})\in V^k\). Further, \(\mathcal {S}_k: \mathbb {R}^{V^k}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}^{V^k}\) denotes the symmetrization operator, that is,

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {S}_k \varphi (\textbf{x})= \frac{1}{k!}\sum _{\varsigma \in S_k}\varphi (\varsigma \textbf{x})\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \varphi \in \mathbb {R}^{V^k}\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

whereas \(\Phi _k(\textbf{x})=\sum _{i=1}^k \delta _{x_i}\in \Xi _k\) is the operator which removes the particles’ labels.

Proposition 7.1

(Lookdown representation of \(\textrm{SIP}\) with killing) For all \(k\ge 2\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {S}_k {\mathscr {L}}_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,k}(f\circ \Phi _k)= (L_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,k}f)\circ \Phi _k\; \text {,}\;\;\quad f \in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(7.3)

where \({\mathscr {L}}_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,k}\) denotes the generator of the lookdown \(\textrm{SIP}_k(G,\alpha ,\omega )\) given, for all \(\varphi \in \mathbb {R}^{V^k}\) and \(\textbf{x}\in V^k\), by

$$\begin{aligned} {\mathscr {L}}_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,k}\varphi (\textbf{x})= {\mathscr {L}}_{G,\alpha ,k}\varphi (\textbf{x}) - \varphi (\textbf{x})\sum _{i=1}^k \omega _{x_i}\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(7.4)

Here, \({\mathscr {L}}_{G,\alpha ,k}\varphi (\textbf{x})\) reads as

$$\begin{aligned} {\mathscr {L}}_{G,\alpha ,k}\varphi (\textbf{x}) = \sum _{i=1}^k \sum _{x,y\in V}c_{xy}\,\delta _{x,x_i}\left( \alpha _y+2\sum _{j=1}^{i-1}\delta _{y,x_j}\right) \left( \varphi (\textbf{x}_i^y)-\varphi (\textbf{x})\right) \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

and stands for the conservative part of the lookdown dynamics, with \(\textbf{x}_i^y\in V^k\) denoting the configuration obtained from \(\textbf{x}\in V^k\) by setting the i-th coordinate equal to \(y\in V\).

Proof

By [KS24, Proposition A.4], an identity which is analogous to that in (7.3) holds for the conservative dynamics, i.e.,

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {S}_k{\mathscr {L}}_{G,\alpha ,k}(f\circ \Phi _k)= (L_{G,\alpha ,k}f)\circ \Phi _k\; \text {,}\;\;\quad f \in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Hence, by linearity, it suffices to prove the identity only for the multiplication operators in (7.1) and (7.4) describing the killing part. Nevertheless, this identity holds trivially because particles are killed independently (or, equivalently, because \(\textbf{x}=(x_1,\ldots , x_k)\mapsto (f\circ \Phi _k)(\textbf{x})\sum _{i=1}^k \omega _{x_i}\) is invariant under permutations). \(\square \)

We now conclude the proof of Theorem 6.1.

Proof of Theorem 6.1

Let \(\mathcal {P}_t^{G,\alpha ,\omega ,k} = e^{t{\mathscr {L}}_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,k}}\), \(t\ge 0\), be the lookdown semigroup. In conclusion, taking \(f=\mathbb {1}_{\Xi _k}\in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\) in (7.3) and observing that \(\mathbb {1}_{\Xi _k}\circ \Phi _k= (\otimes _{i=1}^k\,\mathbb {1}_V)\in \mathbb {R}^{V^k}\), we obtain, for all \(\textbf{x}\in V^k\) and \(t> 0\),

$$\begin{aligned} \max _{\eta \in \Xi _k}\mathbb {P}_\eta ^{G,\alpha ,\omega ,k}[\tau _k>t]&\le \max _{\textbf{x}\in V^k} \mathcal {P}_t^{G,\alpha ,\omega ,k}(\otimes _{i=1}^k\,\mathbb {1}_V)(\textbf{x})\\&\le \max _{x\in V}\mathcal {P}_t^{G,\alpha ,\omega ,1}\mathbb {1}_V(x)\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

where the second inequality follows from neglecting all particles with labels \(\ell =2,\ldots , k\) (in other words, bounding \(\otimes _{i=2}^k\,\mathbb {1}_V\le 1\)), the first being independent from them. Since this last expression equals the right-hand side of (7.2), this proves the inequality therein and, thus, concludes the proof of the theorem. \(\square \)

7.2 Proof of Theorem 6.3

Our main ingredient will be the duality property of \(\textrm{SIP}\) in contact with reservoirs (e.g., [GKRV09, CGGR13]), in particular, the orthogonal polynomial duality functions found in [FRS22]. We shall not assume any prior knowledge on this topic, but rather exploit a few properties taken from [KLS10, RS18, FRS22], which, for the reader’s convenience, we collect in the following proposition. For notational convenience, we simply write \(L_\theta =L_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta }\) and \(L_k=L_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,k}\) (defined in (6.1) and (7.1), respectively).

Proposition 7.2

Fix \(\varrho > 0\).

  1. (a)

    Orthogonality and completeness. Let \(\left\langle \,\cdot \,\, \Bigg | \vert \, \,\cdot \, \right\rangle _{\alpha ,\varrho }\) denote the inner product in \(L^2(\nu _{\alpha ,\varrho })\) (see (6.2)). Then, for all integers \(k,\ell \ge 1\) and \(\xi \in \Xi _k\), \(\zeta \in \Xi _\ell \),

    $$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} \left\langle D_{\alpha ,\varrho }(\xi ,\,\cdot \,)\, \Bigg | \vert \, D_{\alpha ,\varrho }(\zeta ,\,\cdot \,) \right\rangle _{\alpha ,\varrho }&= {\mathbb {1}_{\{\xi =\zeta \}}}\,\frac{\varrho ^k\left( 1+\varrho \right) ^k}{Z_{\alpha ,k}}\,{\mu _{\alpha ,k}(\xi )^{-1}} \end{aligned}\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
    (7.5)

    Moreover, \(\left\{ D_{\alpha ,\varrho }(\xi ,\,\cdot \,)\right\} _{k\ge 0,\,\xi \in \Xi _k}\) is a complete system of \(L^2(\nu _{\alpha ,\varrho })\).

  2. (b)

    Duality. For all \(\theta =(\theta _x)_{x\in V}\) and \(\eta \in \Xi \), we have

    $$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} L_\theta D_{\alpha ,\varrho }(\xi ,\,\cdot \,)(\eta )&= L_k D_{\alpha ,\varrho }(\,\cdot \,,\eta )(\xi ) \\&+ \sum _{x\in V}\omega _x\,\xi _x\left( \theta _x-\varrho \right) D_{\alpha ,\varrho }(\xi -\delta _x,\eta ) \; \text {.}\end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$
    (7.6)

Remark 7.3

Since \(\left\{ D_{\alpha ,\varrho }(\xi ,\,\cdot \,)\right\} _{\ell \le k,\,\xi \in \Xi _\ell }\) spans all polynomials of order \(\le k\), by (7.6), \(L_\theta =L_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta }\) leaves invariant such a subspace of polynomials.

Proof of Theorem 6.3

The rest of the proof of Theorem 6.3 is routine. Indeed, Theorem 6.3(a) follows by linearity and the duality relation in (7.6): recalling \(F_{\alpha ,\varrho }^\psi \) from Definition 6.2, first we obtain

$$\begin{aligned} L_\theta F_{\alpha ,\varrho }^\psi (\eta )&= \sum _{\xi \in \Xi _k} \mu _{\alpha ,k}(\xi )\, \psi (\xi )\, L_\theta D_{\alpha ,\varrho }(\xi ,\,\cdot \,)(\eta )\\&= \sum _{\xi \in \Xi _k}\mu _{\alpha ,k}(\xi )\,\psi (\xi )\, L_k D_{\alpha ,\varrho }(\,\cdot \,,\eta )(\xi )\\&+\sum _{\xi \in \Xi _k}\mu _{\alpha ,k}(\xi )\,\psi (\xi ) \sum _{x\in V}\omega _x\,\xi _x\left( \theta _x-\varrho \right) D_{\alpha ,\varrho }(\xi -\delta _x,\eta )\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

which, by (3.11) and the definition of \(\mathfrak b_{\alpha ,\omega ,\varrho ,k-1}^\varrho \) in (6.8), rearranges as

$$\begin{aligned}&\sum _{\xi \in \Xi _k}\mu _{\alpha ,k}(\xi )\,\psi (\xi )\, L_k D_{\alpha ,\varrho }(\,\cdot \,,\eta )(\xi )+F_{\alpha ,\varrho }^{\mathfrak b_{\alpha ,\omega ,\theta ,k-1}^\varrho \psi }\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

By the symmetry of \(L_k\) in \(L^2(\mu _{\alpha ,k})\), we finally obtain

$$\begin{aligned} L_\theta F_{\alpha ,\varrho }^\psi (\eta )=\sum _{\xi \in \Xi _k}\mu _{\alpha ,k}(\xi )\, L_k\psi (\xi )\, D_{\alpha ,\varrho }(\xi ,\eta )+F_{\alpha ,\varrho }^{\mathfrak b_{\alpha ,\omega ,\theta ,k-1}^\varrho \psi }\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

and the desired claim follows if \(L_k\psi =-\lambda \,\psi \).

The second item in Theorem 6.3 is a direct consequence of the symmetry of \(L_k\) in \(L^2(\mu _{\alpha ,k})\) (thus, the orthogonality relation of eigenfunctions \(\psi .\varphi \in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _l}\) associated to distinct eigenvalues) and the orthogonality relation in (7.5): for all integers \(k,\ell \ge 0\), all eigenfunctions \(\psi \in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _k}\) and \(\varphi \in \mathbb {R}^{\Xi _\ell }\) of \(L_k\) and \(L_\ell \), respectively, we have

$$\begin{aligned} \big \langle F_{\alpha ,\varrho }^\psi \big | F_{\alpha ,\varrho }^\varphi \big \rangle _{\alpha ,\varrho }= \mathbb {1}_{\left\{ k=\ell \right\} }\,\mathfrak c_{\alpha ,\varrho ,k} \left\langle \psi \, \Bigg | \vert \, \varphi \right\rangle _{\alpha ,k}\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

for some constant \(\mathfrak c_{\alpha ,\varrho ,k}>0\).

The completeness of the functions in (6.6) follows from a one-dimensional density result. Each marginal of \(\nu _{\alpha ,\varrho }\) is a negative-binomial measure, hence has exponential tails and a determinate Hamburger moment problem. By the classical theorem of M. Riesz [Rie24] (see also [Akh65, Chapter 2, Section 3 and Corollary 2.3.3], [BC81, Ber96], and references therein), determinacy implies density of algebraic polynomials in the corresponding \(L^2\)-space. Since V is finite and \(\nu _{\alpha ,\varrho }\) is a product measure on \(\mathbb {N}_0^V\), tensor products of these univariate polynomial bases are dense in \(L^2(\nu _{\alpha ,\varrho })\), yielding the desired completeness. \(\square \)

8 Extensions and Related Models

In this last section, we extend our results to the Brownian energy process, and briefly discuss their connections with what is known for some closely related models.

8.1 Brownian energy process

Given a graph \(G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})\) and site weights \(\alpha =(\alpha _x)_{x\in V}\), the corresponding Brownian energy process — referred to as \(\textrm{BEP}(G,\alpha )\) — is the diffusion on the probability simplex

$$\begin{aligned} \varSigma :=\left\{ \zeta \in [0,1]^V: \sum _{x\in V}\zeta _x=1\right\} \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(8.1)

with infinitesimal generator

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}_{G,\alpha }= \frac{1}{2}\sum _{x,y\in V}c_{xy}\left\{ -\left( \alpha _y \zeta _x-\alpha _x\zeta _y\right) \left( \partial _{\zeta _x}-\partial _{\zeta _y}\right) +\zeta _x\zeta _y\left( \partial _{\zeta _x}-\partial _{\zeta _y}\right) ^2\right\} \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

and the following Dirichlet distribution on \(\varSigma \) as its unique reversible measure

$$\begin{aligned} \gamma _\alpha (\textrm{d}\zeta )= \left( \frac{1}{\textrm{B}(\alpha )}\prod _{x\in V} \zeta _x^{\alpha _x-1}\right) \textrm{d}\zeta \; \text {,}\;\;\quad \text {with}\ \textrm{B}(\alpha ):=\frac{\prod _{x\in V}\Gamma (\alpha _x)}{\Gamma (|\alpha |)}\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(8.2)

Here, \(\textrm{d}\zeta \) stands for the uniform measure on \(\varSigma \). We refer to Sect. 1, [KS24] and references therein, for more information and history on this model. Here, we just mention that its mean field version (i.e., when \(c_{xy}\equiv \mathrm{const.}\)) coincides with the renowned multi-type Wright-Fisher diffusion with parent independent mutations (see, e.g., [Shi77, WZ19]).

As shown in [KS24], \(\textrm{BEP}(G,\alpha )\) inherits its eigenstructure from that of \(\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )\). More precisely, the Poisson intertwining relation [KS24, Eq. (4.1)] between the generators \(\mathcal {L}_{G,\alpha }\) and \(L_{G,\alpha ,k}\) allows to lift eigenfunctions of \(\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )\) to eigenfunctions of \(\textrm{BEP}(G,\alpha )\), both corresponding to the same eigenvalue. Using this intertwining relation (and, more specifically, that \(\mathcal {L}_{G,\alpha }\) maps the finite-dimensional subspace of polynomials of degree at most k into itself) and the fact \(\mathcal {L}_{G,\alpha }\) is a self-adjoint operator on \(L^2(\gamma _\alpha )\), one concludes that this lifting saturates all the spectral information (i.e., the spectrum of \(\textrm{BEP}\) is pure point and coincides with that of \(\textrm{SIP}\); see [KS24, Sections 1.2 and 4] for further details). In particular, the corresponding spectral gaps coincide

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_\textrm{BEP}(G,\alpha )=\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Combining the findings in [KS24] with our Theorems 2.2 and 2.4 on \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha )\), we readily deduce the following analogues of (1.3) and (1.6).

Corollary 8.1

For all graphs \(G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})\) and site weights \(\alpha =(\alpha _x)_{x\in V}\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha _\textrm{min}\left\{ \frac{1}{21} \, \frac{ \alpha _\textrm{ratio}}{ 6^{\alpha _\textrm{min}/\alpha _\textrm{ratio}} } \, \frac{ c_\textrm{min}}{|V|^2\, \textrm{diam}(G) }\right\} \le \textrm{gap}_\textrm{BEP}(G,\alpha )\le \textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha )\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

and, in some cases, we have

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_\textrm{BEP}(G,\alpha )< \textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha )\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Interestingly, also our results on the spectrum of the non-conservative \(\textrm{SIP}\), via similar arguments, translate to the context of the \(\textrm{BEP}\). Indeed, the aforementioned Poisson intertwining is effective also when one adds the interaction with external reservoirs. As shown, e.g., in [GRT25, Section 4.1], using this operator, the generator \(L_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta }\) in (6.1) of \(\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta )\) satisfies, for all \(\omega =(\omega _x)_{x\in V}\) and \(\theta =(\theta _x)_{x\in V}\), an analogous intertwining relation with

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}_{G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta } = \mathcal {L}_{G,\alpha } + \sum _{x\in V}\omega _x\left\{ \left( \alpha _x\theta _x-\zeta _x\right) \partial _{\zeta _x} + \theta _x\zeta _x\,\partial _{\zeta _x}^2\right\} \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

the generator of what we refer to as \(\textrm{BEP}(G,\alpha ,\omega ,\theta )\).

When \(\theta \equiv \varrho > 0\), this non-conservative diffusion on \([0,\infty )^V\) is well known to have the following product measure \(\gamma _{\alpha ,\varrho } = \otimes _{x\in V}\, \textrm{Gamma}(\alpha _x,\varrho )\) as its unique reversible measure. Hence, arguing as above, we derive the corresponding spectral gap identity

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_\textrm{BEP}(G,\alpha ,\omega ,\varrho )=\textrm{gap}_\textrm{SIP}(G,\alpha ,\omega ,\varrho )\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

and, as a consequence, the following analogue of Corollary 6.4.

Corollary 8.2

For all graphs \(G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})\), and site weights \(\alpha =(\alpha _x)_{x\in V}\), \(\omega =(\omega _x)_{x\in V}\) with \(\omega \ne 0\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{gap}_\textrm{BEP}(G,\alpha ,\omega ,\varrho ) = \textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha ,\omega )\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \varrho > 0\; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

where \(\textrm{gap}_\textrm{RW}(G,\alpha ,\omega )\) is defined in (6.4).

8.2 Other models

As already anticipated in Sect. 1, \(\textrm{SIP}\) and \(\textrm{BEP}\) come with several closely related models, having a different dynamics, but exactly the same steady states. The models we have in mind fall into the class of mass redistribution models. Here, we shall focus only on those models with configuration-independent edge updates (see, e.g., [Sas15] and references therein for configuration-dependent mass redistribution models with Dirichlet-type steady states): after an edge, say xy, is selected with rate \(c_{xy}\ge 0\), particles/energies sitting on x and y get randomly redistributed therein. Among them, let us mention:

  1. (a)

    the Beta-Binomial splitting process and its continuous version, also known as Beta splitting process or (generalized) KMP model (see, e.g., [PR26, DMFG24, GRT25]);

  2. (b)

    the discrete and continuous harmonic processes (see, e.g., [FFG23, GRT25]).

All models in (a) and (b) have been studied in their conservative and non-conservative variants (see the aforementioned references for the precise definitions of the redistribution rules), and, just like \(\textrm{SIP}\) and \(\textrm{BEP}\), discrete and corresponding continuous models are isospectral (when reversible), enjoying completely analogous Poisson intertwining relations.

To the best of our knowledge, the only sharp quantitative results on their convergence to equilibrium are confined to a handful of results, see [CCL03, Smi14, CLL20, PR26] and references therein. For what concerns the spectral gap, the only sharp results that we are aware of concern the Beta and Beta-Binomial splitting processes in two specific settings, both conservative:

  1. (i)

    the complete graph with \(\alpha \equiv \mathrm{const.}\) [CCL03] (see also [CLL20, Remark 4]);

  2. (ii)

    the segment with \(\alpha \equiv \mathrm{const.}\ge 1\) [CLL20].

In particular, in (i), the gap is attained by the system with \(k=2\) particles (which is strictly smaller than \(\textrm{gap}_1\)), while in (ii) it coincides with that of the corresponding single particle.

In order to obtain spectral gap asymptotics for small diffusivity, the proof ideas of Theorems 2.3 and 2.4 apply also to these models (actually, with considerable simplifications due to the presence of edge updates only, and edges are a.s. never updated simultaneously). For instance, it is not difficult to show that, on graphs like that in Sect. 5.5 and for \(\alpha \) small enough, the spectral gap of the two particles must be strictly smaller than that of the single random walk. Similarly, the non-conservative (reversible) models satisfy a one-particle spectral gap identity, analogous to the one contained in Corollary 6.4. Indeed, the key step in that proof (Sect. 7.1) carries over to these other models in contact with reservoirs, because of the analogous intertwining and consistency/duality relations available also for them, see, e.g., [GRT25]. We leave the verification of these claims to the interested reader. What remains largely open is the problem of obtaining spectral gap’s sharp lower bounds—and, possibly, identities in terms of simpler processes—for the conservative variants of these mass redistribution models on general geometries.

Appendix A. Being in \(\Omega _k\) at Each Positive Time

In this appendix, we prove a technical result on the probability that the \(\varepsilon ^{-1}\)-accelerated \(\mathrm SIP\) sits in the absorbing set \(\Omega _k\) (see (5.4)) at a given positive time. Since \(\Omega _k = \Xi _k\) when \(k=1\), we fix \(k\ge 2\) all throughout this appendix.

We use the notation employed in Sect. 5. Recall from the proof of Proposition 5.1 that \(p_t^\varepsilon (\cdot ,\cdot )\) denotes the transition kernel of the \(\varepsilon ^{-1}\)-sped up \(\textrm{SIP}(G,\varepsilon \hat{\alpha })\) with k particles, i.e.,

$$\begin{aligned} p_t^\varepsilon (\eta ,\xi )= e^{t\varepsilon ^{-1}L_{\varepsilon \hat{\alpha },k}}\mathbb {1}_\xi (\eta )\; \text {,}\;\;\quad \eta ,\xi \in \Xi _k\; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

The following proposition is the main result of this appendix.

Proposition A.1

For any configuration \(\eta \in \Xi _k\) and time \(t>0\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \lim _{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0} p_t^\varepsilon (\eta ,\Omega _k) = 1 \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

To prove Proposition A.1, we need a series of technical lemmas. Let \((\eta ^\varepsilon (t))_{t\ge 0}\) be the \(\varepsilon ^{-1}\)-sped up \(\mathrm SIP\) with generator \(\varepsilon ^{-1}L_{\varepsilon \hat{\alpha },k}\). Let \(\mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \) and \(\mathbb {E}_\eta ^\varepsilon \) denote the law and corresponding expectation of \((\eta ^\varepsilon (t))_{t\ge 0}\) starting from \(\eta \in \Xi _k\).

The first lemma states that the time to reach the absorbing set \(\Omega _k\) from the transient collection \(\Delta _k\) (defined in (5.5)) is uniformly negligible. Given a subset A of \(\Xi _k\), let \(\tau _A\) be its (random) first hitting time. Moreover, define

$$\begin{aligned} c_\textrm{min} = \min _{\begin{array}{c} x,y\in V\\ c_{xy}>0 \end{array}} c_{xy}> 0 \; \text {,}\;\;\quad c_\textrm{max} = \max _{x,y\in V}c_{xy} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(A.1)

Lemma A.2

Suppose that \(\varepsilon |\hat{\alpha }| \le \frac{c_\textrm{min}}{4\,c_\textrm{max}\,k}\). Then, for some \(C_1=C_1(G)>0\) and for all \(\varepsilon >0\), \(k\ge 2\),

$$\begin{aligned} \max _{\zeta \in \Delta _k} \mathbb {E}_\zeta ^\varepsilon \,[ \tau _{\Omega _k}] \le \frac{{C_1}\varepsilon }{c_\textrm{min}} \, \log k \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Proof

Define a test function \(\phi : \Xi _k \rightarrow \mathbb {R}\) as

$$\begin{aligned} \phi (\eta ) :=- \sum _{x\in V } \left( 1+\frac{1}{2} + \cdots + \frac{1}{\eta _x} \right) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Then, there exists \(C_1=C_1(G)>0\) such that

$$\begin{aligned} \max _{\eta \in \Xi _k} \phi (\eta ) - \min _{\eta \in \Xi _k} \phi (\eta ) \le \frac{{C_1}}{4} \log k \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(A.2)

For each \(\zeta \in \Delta _k\), we may express \(L_{\varepsilon \hat{\alpha },k} \phi (\zeta )\) as

$$\begin{aligned} & \sum _{ \{x,y \} \subseteq V} c_{xy} \big [ \zeta _x \,(\varepsilon \hat{\alpha }_y +\zeta _y ) \, ( \phi (\zeta -\delta _x +\delta _y ) - \phi (\zeta )) + \zeta _y \, (\varepsilon \hat{\alpha }_x + \eta _x ) \,( \phi (\zeta -\delta _y + \delta _x ) \nonumber \\ & \quad - \phi (\zeta )) \big ] \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(A.3)

If \(\zeta _x, \zeta _y \ge 1\), then the term inside the bracket in (A.3) becomes

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\zeta _x}{\zeta _y+1} + \frac{\zeta _y}{\zeta _x+1} + \varepsilon \hat{\alpha }_y \left( 1 - \frac{\zeta _x}{\zeta _y+1} \right) + \varepsilon \hat{\alpha }_x \left( 1 - \frac{\zeta _y}{\zeta _x+1} \right) \ge \frac{ (1-\varepsilon \hat{\alpha }_y) \,\zeta _x}{\zeta _y+1} + \frac{ (1-\varepsilon \hat{\alpha }_x) \, \zeta _y}{\zeta _x+1} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

By the assumption, we have \(\varepsilon \hat{\alpha }_\textrm{max} \le \varepsilon |\hat{\alpha }| \le \frac{c_\textrm{min}}{4 \, c_\textrm{max} \, k} < \frac{1}{2}\). Thus, the right-hand side is bounded from below by

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{ \zeta _x}{\zeta _y+1} + \frac{ \zeta _y}{\zeta _x+1} \right) \ge \frac{1}{2} \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(A.4)

where the inequality holds because \(\zeta _x,\zeta _y \ge 1\). Next, if \(\zeta _x\ge 1\) and \(\zeta _y=0\), then the term inside the bracket in (A.3) equals

$$\begin{aligned} \zeta _x \,\varepsilon \hat{\alpha }_y \left( \frac{1}{\zeta _x} - 1 \right) = - \varepsilon \hat{\alpha }_y \, (\zeta _x -1) \ge -\varepsilon \hat{\alpha }_y \, \zeta _x \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(A.5)

Similarly, if \(\zeta _x=0\) and \(\zeta _y\ge 1\), then the term is at least \(-\varepsilon \hat{\alpha }_x \, \zeta _y\). As \(\zeta \in \Delta _k\), there exists at least one \(\{x^*,y^*\}\) such that \(c_{x^*y^*}>0\) and \(\zeta _{x^*},\zeta _{y^*} \ge 1\). Thus, by (A.1), (A.3), (A.4), and (A.5),

$$\begin{aligned} L_{\varepsilon \hat{\alpha },k}\phi (\zeta ) \ge \frac{c_{x^*y^*}}{2} - \sum _{\begin{array}{c} x\in V \\ \zeta _x \ge 1 \end{array}} \sum _{\begin{array}{c} y\in V \\ \zeta _y = 0 \end{array}} c_{xy} \,\varepsilon \hat{\alpha }_y \,\zeta _x \ge \frac{c_\textrm{min}}{2} - c_\textrm{max} \,k\, \varepsilon |\hat{\alpha }| \ge \frac{c_\textrm{min}}{4} \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$
(A.6)

where the last inequality holds since \(\varepsilon |\hat{\alpha }| \le \frac{c_\textrm{min}}{4 \, c_\textrm{max} \, k}\).

Now, it is well known that

$$\begin{aligned} \phi (\eta ^\varepsilon (t)) - \phi (\eta ^\varepsilon (0)) - \int _0^t ( \varepsilon ^{-1} L_{\varepsilon \hat{\alpha },k} \phi ) (\eta ^\varepsilon (s)) \, \textrm{d}s \; \text {,}\;\;\quad t\ge 0 \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

is a \(\mathbb {P}_\zeta ^\varepsilon \)-martingale. Setting \(t=\tau _{\Omega _k} \wedge r \), applying (A.2) and (A.6), taking expectations with respect to \(\mathbb {E}_\zeta ^\varepsilon \), and then sending \(r \rightarrow \infty \), we obtain

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{c_\textrm{min} \, \varepsilon ^{-1}}{4} \,\mathbb {E}_\zeta ^\varepsilon \, [\tau _{\Omega _k}] \le \mathbb {E}_\zeta ^\varepsilon \left[ \int _0^{\tau _{\Omega _k}} (\varepsilon ^{-1} L_{\varepsilon \hat{\alpha },k} \phi )(\eta ^\varepsilon (s)) \, \textrm{d}s \right] \le \frac{{C_1}}{4} \log k \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

This concludes the proof of the lemma. \(\square \)

For each \(t\ge 0\), let \(T_\varepsilon (t)\) be the local time spent by \((\eta ^\varepsilon (s))_{s\ge 0}\) in \(\Omega _k\) up to time t:

$$\begin{aligned} T_\varepsilon (t) :=\int _0^t \textbf{1}\{ \eta ^\varepsilon (s) \in \Omega _k \} \, \textrm{d}s \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(A.7)

Then, let \(S_\varepsilon (s)\), \(s\ge 0\), be its generalized inverse:

$$\begin{aligned} S_\varepsilon (s) :=\sup \, \{ t \ge 0: T_\varepsilon (t) \le s \} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

By definition, \(T_\varepsilon (S_\varepsilon (s)) = s\) and \(T_\varepsilon (S_\varepsilon (s)+u)>s\) for any \(s\ge 0\) and \(u>0\). In turn, we have

$$\begin{aligned} \eta ^\varepsilon (S_\varepsilon (s)) \in \Omega _k\; \text {,}\;\;\quad s \ge 0 \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(A.8)

Note also that \(\{ S_\varepsilon (s) \ge s+u \} = \{ T_\varepsilon (s+u) \le s \}\) for \(s\ge 0\) and \(u>0\).

Lemma A.3

For all \(\eta \in \Xi _k\), \(t\ge 0\) and \(\delta >0\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \lim _{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0} \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \, [ S_\varepsilon (t) \ge t+ \delta ] = 0 \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Proof

First, suppose that \(\eta \in \Delta _k\). We may bound

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \, [ S_\varepsilon (t) \ge t+\delta ]&= \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \, [ T_\varepsilon (t+\delta ) \le t ] \\&\le \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \, [ T_\varepsilon (t+\delta ) \le t , \ \tau _{\Omega _k} < \delta /2 ] + \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \, [ \tau _{\Omega _k} \ge \delta /2 ] \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

By Lemma A.2 and the Markov inequality, we obtain

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \, [ \tau _{\Omega _k} \ge \delta /2 ] \le \frac{2}{\delta }\, \mathbb {E}_\eta ^\varepsilon \, [ \tau _{\Omega _k}] \le \frac{2}{\delta }\, \frac{{C_1} \varepsilon }{c_\textrm{min}} \, \log k \xrightarrow {\varepsilon \rightarrow 0} 0 \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Thus, by the strong Markov property of \((\eta ^\varepsilon (s))_{s\ge 0}\) and the fact that \(t \mapsto T_\varepsilon (t)\) is non-decreasing, we deduce

$$\begin{aligned} \limsup _{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0} \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \, [ S_\varepsilon (t) \ge t+\delta ] \le \limsup _{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0} \sup _{\xi \in \Omega _k} \mathbb {P}_\xi ^\varepsilon \, [ T_\varepsilon ( t+ \delta /2 ) \le t] \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Thus, it suffices to prove Lemma A.3 for \(\eta \in \Omega _k\).

It remains to prove that, for \(\eta \in \Omega _k\), \(t\ge 0\) and \(\delta >0\),

$$\begin{aligned} \lim _{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0} \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \, [ T_\varepsilon (t+\delta ) \le t ] = 0 \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Let \(0<\tau _1< \tau _2 < \cdots \) be the consecutive jump times from \(\Omega _k\) to \(\Delta _k\). Then, for any integer \(m \ge 1\), we get

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \,[ T_\varepsilon (t+\delta ) \le t ] \le \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \,[ T_\varepsilon (t+\delta ) \le t, \ \tau _{m+1} > t+\delta ] + \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \,[ \tau _{m+1} \le t + \delta ] \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(A.9)

Conditioned on the first event in the right-hand side of (A.9), there occurs at most m jumps from \(\Omega _k\) to \(\Delta _k\) up to time \(t+\delta \). Let \(\tau _1'< \tau _2' < \cdots \) denote the consecutive return times to \(\Omega _k\), such that we have

$$\begin{aligned} 0< \tau _1< \tau _1'< \tau _2< \tau _2' < \cdots \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Then, by (A.7), we may bound the first probability in the right-hand side of (A.9) by

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \, [ (\tau _1'-\tau _1) + (\tau _2' - \tau _2) + \cdots + (\tau _m' - \tau _m ) \ge \delta ] \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

We may further bound this by

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} \sum _{i=1}^m \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon [ \tau _i'-\tau _i \ge \delta /m ]&\le m \sup _{\zeta \in \Delta _k} \mathbb {P}_\zeta ^\varepsilon [ \tau _{\Omega _k} \ge \delta / m ] \\&\le \frac{m^2}{\delta } \sup _{\zeta \in \Delta _k} \mathbb {E}_\zeta ^\varepsilon [ \tau _{\Omega _k}] \le \frac{m^2 {C_1} \varepsilon \log k}{\delta c_\textrm{min}} \xrightarrow {\varepsilon \rightarrow 0 } 0 \; \text {.}\end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$
(A.10)

Here, the first inequality is due to the strong Markov property at \(\tau _i\) for each \(i \in \llbracket 1,m \rrbracket \), the second inequality is due to the Markov inequality, and the third inequality is due to Lemma A.2. Further, for \((\eta ^\varepsilon (s))_{s\ge 0}\), any direct jump from \( \Omega _k\) to \(\Delta _k\) has rate bounded above by

$$\begin{aligned} \sup _{\eta \in \Omega _k}\sup _{x,y \in V} \varepsilon ^{-1} \, c_{xy} \, \eta _x\varepsilon \hat{\alpha }_y \le c_\textrm{max} \, k \, \hat{\alpha }_\textrm{max} \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

where \(\hat{\alpha }_\textrm{max} :=\max _{x\in V} \hat{\alpha }_x\). Hence, each of the random variables \(\tau _1, \tau _2 - \tau _1', \dots , \tau _{m+1} - \tau _m'\) stochastically dominates an exponential random variable with rate \( c_\textrm{max} \, k \, \hat{\alpha }_\textrm{max} \). Thus, letting \(\tau _1^*,\tau _2^*, \dots \) be some i.i.d. exponentials of rate \( c_\textrm{max} \, k \, \hat{\alpha }_\textrm{max}\), by Markovianity we get

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \, [ \tau _{m+1} \le t+\delta ]&\le \mathbb {P}\, [ \tau _1^* + \cdots + \tau _{m+1}^* \le t + \delta ] \\&= \mathbb {P}\, [\textrm{Gamma} \,(m+1, c_\textrm{max} \, k \, \hat{\alpha }_\textrm{max} ) \le t+\delta ] \xrightarrow {m \rightarrow \infty } 0 \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$
(A.11)

where \(\textrm{Gamma}\,(u,\beta )\) denotes the Gamma distribution with shape \(u>0\) and rate \(\beta >0\). Therefore, by (A.9), (A.10) and (A.11), we conclude that

$$\begin{aligned} \limsup _{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0} \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \, [T_\varepsilon (t+\delta ) \le t ]&\le \limsup _{m \rightarrow \infty } \limsup _{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0} \frac{m^2 \,{C_1}\, \varepsilon \, \log k}{ \delta \, c_\textrm{min}} \\&+ \limsup _{m \rightarrow \infty } \mathbb {P}\, [\textrm{Gamma}\,(m+1, c_\textrm{max} \, k \, \hat{\alpha }_\textrm{max} ) \le t+\delta ] = 0 \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

as desired. \(\square \)

Lemma A.4

We have

$$\begin{aligned} \lim _{\delta \rightarrow 0} \lim _{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0} \sup _{\xi \in \Omega _k} \mathbb {P}_\xi ^\varepsilon \, [ \tau _{\Delta _k} < 3\delta ] = 0 \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Proof

As explained in the proof of Lemma A.3, starting from any \(\xi \in \Omega _k\), \(\tau _{\Delta _k}\) stochastically dominates an exponential random variable of rate \(c_\textrm{max}\, k\, \hat{\alpha }_\textrm{max}\). Thus, we get

$$\begin{aligned} \limsup _{\delta \rightarrow 0} \sup _{\varepsilon \in (0,1)} \mathbb {P}_\xi ^\varepsilon \,[ \tau _{\Delta _k} < 3\delta ] \le \limsup _{\delta \rightarrow 0} \left( 1 - e^{- c_\textrm{max} \,k\, \hat{\alpha }_\textrm{max} \,3\delta } \right) = 0 \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

This concludes the proof. \(\square \)

Now, we are ready to prove Proposition A.1.

Proof of Proposition A.1

We prove

$$\begin{aligned} \lim _{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0} \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \, [ \eta ^\varepsilon (t) \in \Omega _k ] =1\; \text {,}\;\;\quad t>0\; \text {,}\;\;\eta \in \Xi _k \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$
(A.12)

We follow the proof ideas of [LLM18, Lemma 3.1]. By (A.8), for \(\delta \in (0,t/3)\), we estimate

$$\begin{aligned} 1 = \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \, [ \eta ^\varepsilon (S_\varepsilon (t-3\delta )) \in \Omega _k ] \le&\ \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \left[ \eta ^\varepsilon (S_\varepsilon (t-3\delta )) \in \Omega _k, \ S_\varepsilon (t-3\delta ) \in [t-3\delta , t-2\delta ] \right] \\&+ \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \,[ S_\varepsilon (t-3\delta ) > t-2\delta ] \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

By Lemma A.3, the second probability in the right-hand side vanishes as \(\varepsilon \rightarrow 0\). Moreover, by (A.8), the first probability in the right-hand side is bounded by

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \, [ \, \exists s \in [t-3\delta , t-2\delta ], \ \eta ^\varepsilon (s) \in \Omega _k \, ] \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

We can further divide this into two events and bound the last probability as

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \,[ \eta ^\varepsilon (t) \in \Omega _k ] + \mathbb {P}_\eta ^\varepsilon \, [ \, \exists s \in [t-3\delta , t-2\delta ], \ \eta ^\varepsilon (s) \in \Omega _k, \ \eta ^\varepsilon (t) \in \Delta _k \, ] \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Then, by the Markov property, the second probability above is bounded by

$$\begin{aligned} \sup _{\xi \in \Omega _k} \mathbb {P}_\xi ^\varepsilon \, [ \tau _{\Delta _k} < 3\delta ] \; \text {,}\;\;\end{aligned}$$

which, by Lemma A.4, vanishes by first taking the limit \(\varepsilon \rightarrow 0\), and then \(\delta \rightarrow 0\). Collecting all displayed inequalities, we conclude that (A.12) holds. \(\square \)

Appendix B. Reversibility of the limit process

In this appendix, we prove Lemma 5.9 and simply write \(\varsigma _{\hat{\alpha },k}=\varsigma _{\hat{\alpha },k,m}\), \(k\ge m\).

Proof of Lemma 5.9

We start by introducing an auxiliary continuous-time Markov chain on \(\Xi _k\) with rate function (cf. (5.1)–(5.5))

$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{s}(\eta ,\,\cdot \,):={\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \textbf{r}^{\mathcal {A}}_{\hat{\alpha },k}(\eta ,\,\cdot \,) & \text {if}\quad \eta \in \Omega _k\; \text {,}\;\;\\ \textbf{r}^{\mathcal {B}}_k(\eta ,\,\cdot \,) & \text {if}\quad \eta \in \Delta _k\; \text {.}\end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$
(B.1)

In words, the above dynamics follows the rule of \(A_{\hat{\alpha },k}\) in \(\Omega _k\), while that of \(B_k\) in \(\Delta _k\). (Remark that this is not a \(\textrm{SIP}\)-dynamics.) Now, recall the definition of \(\Omega _{k,m}\) from (5.10) and, similarly, introduce the set

$$\begin{aligned} \Delta _{k,m} :=\left\{ \zeta \in \Delta _k: |\{x\in V: \zeta _x > 0 \}| = m \right\} \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Observe that this auxiliary chain can jump from \(\Omega _{k,m}\) to either \(\Delta _{k,m}\) or \(\Delta _{k,m+1}\) (if \(m\le k-1\)), but a jump from \(\Omega _{k,m}\) to \(\Delta _{k,m}\) has rate zero to backtrack.

Now, fix \(m\le k-1\). We claim that this dynamics, restricted to \(\Omega _{k,m}\cup \Delta _{k,m+1}\), is reversible with respect to \(\varsigma _{\hat{\alpha },k}\). For this purpose, it suffices to consider pairs \(\eta , \xi = \eta -\delta _x+\delta _y \in \Omega _{k,m}\cup \Delta _{k,m+1}\) (hence, \(\eta _x\ge 1\)); rates are zero otherwise. If \(\eta \in \Omega _{k,m}\) and \(\xi \in \Omega _{k,m}\) (such that \(\eta _x =1\)), then

$$\begin{aligned} \varsigma _{\hat{\alpha },k}(\eta ) \, \textbf{r}^\mathcal {A}_{\hat{\alpha },k} (\eta ,\xi ) = c_{xy} \, \eta _x \hat{\alpha }_y \, \prod _{\begin{array}{c} z\in V\\ \eta _z> 0 \end{array}} \frac{\hat{\alpha }_z}{\eta _z} = c_{xy} \, \hat{\alpha }_x \xi _y \, \prod _{\begin{array}{c} z\in V \\ \xi _z > 0 \end{array}} \frac{\hat{\alpha }_z}{\xi _z} = \varsigma _{\hat{\alpha },k} (\xi ) \, \textbf{r}^\mathcal {A}_{\hat{\alpha },k} ( \xi ,\eta ) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

If \(\eta \in \Omega _{k,m}\) and \(\xi \in \Delta _{k,m+1}\) (such that \(\eta _x \ge 2\)), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \varsigma _{\hat{\alpha },k}(\eta ) \, \textbf{r}^\mathcal {A}_{\hat{\alpha },k} (\eta ,\xi ) = c_{xy} \, \eta _x \hat{\alpha }_y \, \prod _{\begin{array}{c} z\in V\\ \eta _z> 0 \end{array}} \frac{\hat{\alpha }_z}{\eta _z} = c_{xy} \, \xi _x \xi _y \, \prod _{\begin{array}{c} z\in V \\ \xi _z > 0 \end{array}} \frac{\hat{\alpha }_z}{\xi _z} = \varsigma _{\hat{\alpha },k} (\xi ) \, \textbf{r}^\mathcal {B}_k ( \xi ,\eta ) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

If \(\eta ,\xi \in \Delta _{k,m+1}\), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \varsigma _{\hat{\alpha },k}(\eta ) \, \textbf{r}^\mathcal {B}_k (\eta ,\xi ) = c_{xy} \, \eta _x \eta _y \, \prod _{\begin{array}{c} z\in V \\ \eta _z> 0 \end{array}} \frac{\hat{\alpha }_z}{\eta _z} = c_{xy} \, \xi _x \xi _y \, \prod _{\begin{array}{c} z\in V \\ \xi _z > 0 \end{array}} \frac{\hat{\alpha }_z}{\xi _z} = \varsigma _{\hat{\alpha },k} (\xi )\, \textbf{r}^\mathcal {B}_k ( \xi ,\eta ) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

This shows that \(\varsigma _{\hat{\alpha },k}\) is reversible for the Markov chain described in (B.1), restricted to \(\Omega _{k,m}\cup \Delta _{k,m+1}\). Furthermore, by (5.8), we have, for all distinct \(\eta ,\xi \in \Omega _{k,m}\),

$$\begin{aligned} \varsigma _{\hat{\alpha },k}(\eta ) \, \textbf{r}^\mathcal {M}_{\hat{\alpha },k} (\eta ,\xi ) = \varsigma _{\hat{\alpha },k}(\eta ) \sum _{\zeta \in \Delta _{k,m+1}} \textbf{r}^\mathcal {A}_{\hat{\alpha },k} (\eta ,\zeta ) \, \textbf{P}_\zeta ^{\mathcal {B}} \, [\tau _\xi = \tau _{\Omega _k}] \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

Following \((\mathcal {B}_k(t))_{t\ge 0}\), from \(\zeta \in \Delta _{k,m+1}\) to arrive at \(\xi \in \Omega _{k,m}\), the trajectory must stay in \(\Delta _{k,m+1}\). Thus, by reversibility, the right-hand side is equal to

$$\begin{aligned} \varsigma _{\hat{\alpha },k}(\xi ) \sum _{\zeta \in \Delta _{k,m+1}} \textbf{r}^\mathcal {A}_{\hat{\alpha },k} (\xi ,\zeta ) \, \textbf{P}_\zeta ^{\mathcal {B}} \, [\tau _\eta = \tau _{\Omega _k}] = \varsigma _{\hat{\alpha },k}(\xi ) \, \textbf{r}^\mathcal {M}_{\hat{\alpha },k} (\xi ,\eta ) \; \text {.}\end{aligned}$$

This concludes the proof of the lemma. \(\square \)
