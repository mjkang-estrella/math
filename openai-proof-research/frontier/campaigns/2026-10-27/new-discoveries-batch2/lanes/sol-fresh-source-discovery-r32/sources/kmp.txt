 Abstract. 
    
 We present a simple strategy to derive universal bounds on the spectral gap of reversible stochastic exchange models on arbitrary graphs. The Kipnis-Marchioro-Presutti (KMP) model, the harmonic process (HP), and the immediate exchange model (IEM) are all examples that fall into this class. Our upper and lower bounds depend only on two features: worst-case linear statistics and a kinetic factor, which is, in essence, graph-independent. For the three aforementioned examples, these bounds are sharp, and even saturate to an identity for HP and IEM in some log-concave regimes. The proof—which yields bounds for eigenvalues even in the non-reversible context—crucially exploits the rigidity of the eigenstructure of these models and quantitative contraction rates of the corresponding hidden parameter models recently introduced in  [ DMFG24 ,  GRT25 ] . 
  
 
 
 1.  Introduction. From KMP to more general exchange models 

 
 1.1.  The KMP model

 The original KMP model was introduced in  [ KMP82 ]  to describe heat conduction along a one-dimensional segment in contact with thermal reservoirs at its endpoints. In that setting, energy is exchanged only between neighboring vertices and with the reservoirs, and the model was defined with  U ∼ U ⁡ ( 0 , 1 ) U\sim\mathrm{U}(0,1)  (i.e.,  α x ≡ 1 \alpha_{x}\equiv 1 ). This formulation has since been generalized; see, e.g.,  [ GKRV09 ] .
On the same segment but without reservoirs, the model has been studied more recently under the name  adjacent walk on the simplex   [ CLL20 ,  LP25 ] . More broadly, this redistribution mechanism is also known as the  beta-splitting process , emphasizing the role of the  U U -distribution in determining how mass is split.
In the mean-field setting—where all rates  c x ​ y c_{xy}  are equal and  α x ≡ 1 / 2 \alpha_{x}\equiv 1/2 —the dynamics coincides with the evolution of particle energies in the  Kac’s walk   [ Kac56 ] , one of the most successful many-body mean-field models of stochastically colliding particles in kinetic theory. 
 
 
 In any of the above settings, the KMP model presents two key qualitative features.

 1.1.1.  Convergence to equilibrium and spectral gap 

 
 Our primary focus is to quantify relaxation to equilibrium for this model on arbitrary graphs. Because of the aforementioned reversibility, we shall focus on the process’ spectral gap, governing the  L 2 ​ ( μ ) L^{2}(\mu) -exponential convergence rate. For more background on convergence to equilibrium, spectral gaps, and other functional inequalities for Markov processes, we refer to the monographs  [ BGL14 ,  LP17 ] . 
 
 
 The spectral gap of the KMP model has been completely characterized in the specific settings described above. Indeed, around two decades ago, in the mean-field setup, spectral gap estimates—and even the exact value—have been obtained in a series of influential works  [ Jan01 ,  CCL03 ,  Cap08 ] , thereby solving Kac’s long-standing conjecture  [ Kac56 ] . More recently,  [ CLL20 ]  determined the precise value of the spectral gap on the segment, whenever  α x ≡ const . ≥ 1 \alpha_{x}\equiv{\rm const.}\geq 1 ; for general  α \alpha , bounds were previously derived in  [ GKS12 ] . For mixing-time results, see, e.g.,  [ RW05 ,  CLL20 ,  LP25 ]  on the segment,  [ Smi14 ,  CQS25 ]  for the mean-field setup, and references therein. 
 
 
 All the above spectral gap results crucially exploit inherent symmetries of the underlying geometry. When moving away from such settings, establishing the precise value of the spectral gap becomes unfeasible; however, one can still resort to spectral gap comparisons, that reduce an infinite- to a finite-dimensional optimization problem. Analogous spectral-gap reductions have been observed within the context of interacting particle systems; see Section  2.3.1  below for more details.

 1.2.  Proof strategy and hidden parameter model 

 
 As already mentioned, prior to our work, spectral gap lower bounds for the KMP model have been established on the complete graph and on the segment.
In particular, the first setting—corresponding to the mean-field scenario, much explored in kinetic theory—is now fully covered  [ Jan01 ,  CCL03 ,  Cap08 ] . In a nutshell, the common ingredient of all these proofs is a recursive argument on  n n , the size of the graph, at the level of Dirichlet forms. This strategy, originated from Lu-Yau’s martingale method  [ LY93 ]  and which proved to be extremely efficient and flexible to generalizations in the mean-field setup  [ CCL14 ,  Sas15 ,  CPT25 ] , does not admit a sharp counterpart on  arbitrary  underlying geometries, especially in absence of symmetries. 
 
 
 Instead, we follow an alternative, equally established, route due to Chen and Wang  [ CW97 ]  to bound from below eigenvalues of Markov generators, thus, spectral gaps of reversible processes. In essence, their proof goes as follows.  1.2.2.  KMP {\rm KMP} ’s hidden parameter model 

 
 Our way to tackle this difficulty on arbitrary graphs is to apply the same strategy, not to the KMP process itself, but to a recently discovered dual process  [ DMFG24 ]  (see also  [ GRT25 ] ): the KMP hidden parameter model.
Before discussing the details, we begin with a few preliminary remarks.

 1.3.  Generalizations: HP & IEM, and beyond reversibility 

 
 The strategy outlined for bounding KMP’s spectral gap turns out to be much more general, yet, surprisingly sharp. Indeed, it covers all conservative stochastic exchange models that are reversible, and that come with a “degenerate” hidden parameter model. In certain instances, the spectral gap estimates derived through this approach actually become exact identities, on any graph. 
 
 
 Stochastic exchange models which are conservative and admit a hidden parameter model take a rather general form, see ( 2.1 ): transitions correspond to stochastic mappings, with the corresponding rates being independent of the process’ state. In contrast, reversibility imposes a specific form on the transition rates.

 1.3.2.  Beyond non-degeneracy and reversibility 

 
 All three models discussed earlier share the key feature of admitting a non-degenerate reversible measure. These two properties are instrumental in applying the spectral theorem to self-adjoint generators, which in turn guarantees that the polynomial eigenvalues we compute actually saturate the corresponding  L 2 L^{2} -spectrum. However, one of the main strengths of our approach is that neither non-degeneracy nor reversibility is essential for obtaining sharp eigenvalue estimates. Indeed, we identify a broad family of stochastic exchange models—with update rules as in ( 1.28 ), see ( 2.1 )—for which our method remains effective even in the absence of these assumptions, thus significantly extending the range of models to which our spectral analysis applies. 
 
 
 To begin with, our strategy remains effective in degenerate settings, where the invariant measure is singular, that is,  μ = δ ω \mu=\delta_{\omega} , for some  ω ∈ Ω \omega\in\Omega . This occurs, for example, in certain averaging-type models in which one imposes the requirement 
 

 
 (1.34) 
 
 V = 1 − ω x ω y ​ ( 1 − U ) V=1-\frac{\omega_{x}}{\omega_{y}}\left(1-U\right) 
 
 
 at each update in ( 1.28 ). In such cases, the system converges to the configuration  ω ∈ Ω \omega\in\Omega ; when  ω ≡ 1 n \omega\equiv\frac{1}{n} , the above requirement becomes  U ≡ V U\equiv V . 
 
 
 In these settings, no meaningful  L 2 L^{2} -space compatible with  ℒ \mathcal{L}  can be defined; nevertheless, our method remains applicable and yields explicit lower bounds on eigenvalues associated with polynomial observables. These bounds are not merely formal. As shown in Section  3.1 , for each integer  k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 , a stochastic exchange model naturally gives rise to a  k k -particle interacting system. In the specific case described in ( 1.34 ), it is straightforward to check that these particle systems always admit a fully supported reversible measure. Consequently, our eigenvalue estimates yield lower bounds on the spectral gap of the corresponding particle systems, uniformly in the number of particles,  k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 . We note that the averaging model with  U ≡ ω x ω x + ω y U\equiv\frac{\omega_{x}}{\omega_{x}+\omega_{y}}  in ( 1.34 ) was considered in  [ QS23 ] . 
 
 
 Even more significantly, our approach yields meaningful lower bounds on the real part of eigenvalues for a broad class of non-reversible stochastic exchange models and their associated particle systems. This class includes models with asymmetric updates, as well as models arising in econophysics, where the conserved quantity is interpreted as wealth and the dynamics incorporates a nonzero saving propensity. As shown in  [ CRR14 ] , such models are intrinsically non-reversible. 
 
 
 
 1.3.3.  Open systems 

 
 Hidden parameter models for the KMP and the HP recently sparked attention within the context of open systems, in contact with external reservoirs, providing a useful alternative description of the corresponding non-equilibrium steady states  [ DMFG24 ,  CFG+24 ,  CFF+25 ,  GRT25 ] .
Each of our general stochastic exchange models admits a non-conservative counterpart, with suitably chosen boundary-driven dynamics. The polynomial-eigenvalue analysis presented in  [ KS24a , Section 6]  for a related open diffusion process can be similarly applied to our models, yielding analogous results—specifically, that the lowest polynomial eigenvalue is attained by linear functions. For the sake of brevity, we will not discuss this setting in any detail.
