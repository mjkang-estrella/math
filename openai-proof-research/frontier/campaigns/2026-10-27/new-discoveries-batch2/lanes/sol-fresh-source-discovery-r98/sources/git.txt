Abstract.
    

Motivated by Stanley’s generalization of the chromatic polynomial of a graph to the chromatic symmetric function, we introduce the characteristic polynomial of a representation of the symmetric group, or more generally, of a symmetric function. When the representation arises from geometry, the coefficients of its characteristic polynomial tend to form a log-concave sequence. To illustrate, we investigate explicit examples, including the nn-fold products of the projective spaces, the GIT moduli spaces of points on ℙ1\mathbb{P}^{1} and Hessenberg varieties.
Our main focus lies on the cohomology of the moduli space of pointed rational curves, for which we prove asymptotic formulas of its characteristic polynomial and establish asymptotic log-concavity.

1. Introduction




In [25], Stanley introduced the chromatic symmetric function of a graph, as a symmetric function generalization of the chromatic polynomial of a graph. In this paper, by taking the inverse approach, we associate a polynomial, called the characteristic polynomial, to each symmetric function. Although the polynomials we consider are not the chromatic polynomials of graphs in general, those obtained from geometric contexts appear to share one of the most interesting features of chromatic polynomials, namely the log-concavity (cf. §2).




1.1. Chromatic polynomial and characteristic polynomial




The chromatic polynomial of a graph Γ\Gamma is a polynomial 𝒳Γ​(m)∈ℚ​[m]\mathscr{X}_{\Gamma}(m)\in\mathbb{Q}[m] such that for each m0∈ℕ=ℤ≥1m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}, the value 𝒳Γ​(m0)\mathscr{X}_{\Gamma}(m_{0}) equals the number of proper colorings of the vertices of Γ\Gamma using m0m_{0} colors. Here, a coloring is called proper if any two adjacent vertices (that is, vertices connected by an edge) take different colors. In this paper, we regard 𝒳Γ​(m)\mathscr{X}_{\Gamma}(m) as a polynomial in the variable mm, and write 𝒳Γ​(m0)\mathscr{X}_{\Gamma}(m_{0}) for its evaluation at a positive integer m0m_{0}.




The chromatic symmetric function 𝖼𝗌𝖿Γ\mathsf{csf}_{\Gamma} of Γ\Gamma is defined by




𝖼𝗌𝖿Γ=∑κ:V⁡(Γ)→ℤ≥1,proper∏v∈V⁡(Γ)xκ⁡(v)\mathsf{csf}_{\Gamma}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa:V(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1},\\
\mathrm{proper}\end{subarray}}\prod_{v\in V(\Gamma)}x_{\kappa(v)}



where {xi}i∈ℤ≥1\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}} are formal variables and V⁡(Γ)V(\Gamma) is the set of vertices of Γ\Gamma. This is obviously a symmetric function in xix_{i} and the substitution



(1.1)

x1=x2=⋯=xm0=1​xm0+1=xm0+2=⋯=0x_{1}=x_{2}=\cdots=x_{m_{0}}=1x_{m_{0}+1}=x_{m_{0}+2}=\cdots=0



to 𝖼𝗌𝖿Γ\mathsf{csf}_{\Gamma} gives the integer 𝒳Γ​(m0)\mathscr{X}_{\Gamma}(m_{0}). In this sense, 𝖼𝗌𝖿Γ\mathsf{csf}_{\Gamma} can be considered as a generalization of 𝒳Γ\mathscr{X}_{\Gamma}. However, the log-concavity proved in [17] appears only after the substitution (1.1) and hence it makes sense to extend the chromatic polynomials to more general contexts when one is interested in the log-concavity.




In this paper, we first observe that the process of producing a polynomial out of a symmetric function by the substitution (1.1) works not only for chromatic symmetric functions of graphs but also for all symmetric functions.
Indeed, the substitution (1.1) produces a map 𝒮F:ℕ→ℚ\mathscr{S}_{F}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Q} for any symmetric function FF in the variables xix_{i} with rational coefficients.
It is straightforward to check that 𝒮F\mathscr{S}_{F} is a polynomial function whose associated polynomial in ℚ⁡[m]\mathbb{Q}[m] is called the characteristic polynomial 𝒮F​(m)\mathscr{S}_{F}(m) of FF (Definition 3.1).




It turns out that this definition is quite natural. To explain this, we first write it in a different but equivalent form.
Let Λ\Lambda be the power series ring of the symmetric functions in xix_{i} over ℚ\mathbb{Q}, i.e.




Λ=lim⟵ℚ⟦x1,⋯,xn⟧𝕊n,\Lambda=\lim_{\longleftarrow}\mathbb{Q}\llbracket x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rrbracket^{\mathbb{S}_{n}},



and let Λn⊂Λ\Lambda_{n}\subset\Lambda be the subspace of elements of degree nn.
Let 𝗉n=∑i≥1xin\mathsf{p}_{n}=\sum_{i\geq 1}x_{i}^{n} be the nnth power sum symmetric function for n≥1n\geq 1 and let 𝗉0=1\mathsf{p}_{0}=1. It is well-known that Λ≅ℚ⟦𝗉1,𝗉2,⋯⟧\Lambda\cong\mathbb{Q}\llbracket\mathsf{p}_{1},\mathsf{p}_{2},\cdots\rrbracket. The map associating the characteristic polynomial to each symmetric function




𝒮^:Λ⟶ℚ⁡[m]​⟦q⟧,(F∈Λn)↦𝒮F​(m)​qn\widehat{\mathscr{S}}:\Lambda\longrightarrow\mathbb{Q}[m]\llbracket q\rrbracket,\quad(F\in\Lambda_{n})\mapsto\mathscr{S}_{F}(m)q^{n}



is precisely the ℚ\mathbb{Q}-algebra homomorphism
which sends 𝗉n\mathsf{p}_{n} to m​qnmq^{n} for every n≥1n\geq 1, where qq is a formal variable.




A remarkable feature of this map is that it behaves nicely with plethysm ∘\circ on Λ\Lambda (see §3.1).
In fact, it converts the plethysm F∘GF\circ G of symmetric functions F∈ΛnF\in\Lambda_{n} and G∈ΛrG\in\Lambda_{r}
to the composition (𝒮F∘𝒮G)​(m)=𝒮F​(𝒮G​(m))(\mathscr{S}_{F}\circ\mathscr{S}_{G})(m)=\mathscr{S}_{F}(\mathscr{S}_{G}(m))
(Proposition 3.2).
Conversely, this feature uniquely determines 𝒮^\widehat{\mathscr{S}}
as a ℚ\mathbb{Q}-algebra homomorphism Λ→ℚ⁡[m]​⟦q⟧\Lambda\to\mathbb{Q}[m]\llbracket q\rrbracket
(Theorem 3.3).




We can also define the characteristic polynomial of a symmetric group representation.
Let 𝕊n\mathbb{S}_{n} denote the symmetric group of nn letters and RnR_{n} be the ℚ\mathbb{Q}-vector space spanned by irreducible 𝕊n\mathbb{S}_{n}-representations. Let




ch=ch𝕊n:Rn→≅Λn\mathrm{ch}=\mathrm{ch}_{\mathbb{S}_{n}}:R_{n}\xrightarrow{~\cong~}\Lambda_{n}



be the Frobenius characteristic map (cf. (3.16)). The characteristic polynomial of an 𝕊n\mathbb{S}_{n}-module VV is defined by 𝒮V​(m)=𝒮ch𝕊n​(V)​(m)\mathscr{S}_{V}(m)=\mathscr{S}_{\mathrm{ch}_{\mathbb{S}_{n}}(V)}(m).





1.2. Characteristic polynomials of varieties




In this paper, we work out the characteristic polynomials of
examples from geometry, especially the cohomology of varieties
equipped with an action of 𝕊n\mathbb{S}_{n}.
These include the nn-fold products of the projective spaces
(cf. §4.1) and the GIT moduli space of nn points on ℙ1\mathbb{P}^{1} for odd nn (cf. §4.2). In both cases, the 𝕊n\mathbb{S}_{n}-action that permutes the nn ordered points induces graded 𝕊n\mathbb{S}_{n}-representations on the cohomology. We compute the characteristic polynomials of these representations and establish suitable log-concavity.




Another remarkable example is a regular semisimple Hessenberg variety XhX_{h} introduced in [12]. It is a subvariety in the flag variety of ℂn\mathbb{C}^{n}, defined by a function h:{1,⋯,n}→{1,⋯,n}h:\{1,\cdots,n\}\to\{1,\cdots,n\} satisfying h⁡(i)≥ih(i)\geq i and h⁡(i)≥h⁡(i−1)h(i)\geq h(i-1) for all i>1i>1.
There is a natural action of 𝕊n\mathbb{S}_{n} on its cohomology H⁡(Xh)H(X_{h}) by [30] whose characteristic polynomial satisfies



(1.2)

ω​ch𝕊n​H​(Xh)=𝖼𝗌𝖿Γh\omega\mathrm{ch}_{\mathbb{S}_{n}}H(X_{h})=\mathsf{csf}_{\Gamma_{h}}



where ω\omega is the involution on Λ\Lambda that sends 𝗉n\mathsf{p}_{n} to (−1)n​𝗉n(-1)^{n}\mathsf{p}_{n} and Γh\Gamma_{h} is
the incomparability graph associated to hh (cf. Theorem 4.13). While this equality can be written in a graded version, we stick to this simpler form here.
In particular, applying 𝒮\mathscr{S} to (1.2), we get



(1.3)

(−1)n​𝒮H⁡(Xh)​(−m)=𝒳Γh​(m).(-1)^{n}\mathscr{S}_{H(X_{h})}(-m)=\mathscr{X}_{\Gamma_{h}}(m).



By [17], the characteristic polynomial 𝒮H⁡(Xh)​(m)\mathscr{S}_{H(X_{h})}(m) of the Hessenberg variety is log-concave. See §4.3 for further details.





1.3. Characteristic polynomial of ℳ¯0,n\overline{{\cal M}}_{0,n}




The main focus of this paper lies on the moduli space ℳ¯0,n\overline{{\cal M}}_{0,n} of stable rational curves with nn marked points.
See [10, 11] for basic facts about this moduli space and its cohomology.
This is a case where the characteristic polynomial is not the chromatic polynomial of any graph.
The 𝕊n\mathbb{S}_{n}-action on ℳ¯0,n\overline{{\cal M}}_{0,n} permuting the nn marked points gives rise to a graded 𝕊n\mathbb{S}_{n}-representation on its cohomology H∗​(ℳ¯0,n)H^{*}(\overline{{\cal M}}_{0,n}). Consequently,
we obtain a bivariate polynomial

for each k≥0k\geq 0.
We write the generating series of 𝒫n\mathscr{P}_{n} as




𝒫=1+m​q+m⁡(m+1)2​q2+∑n≥3𝒫n​(m,t)​qn∈ℚ⁡[m,t]​⟦q⟧\mathscr{P}=1+mq+\frac{m(m+1)}{2}q^{2}+\sum_{n\geq 3}\mathscr{P}_{n}(m,t)q^{n}~\in\mathbb{Q}[m,t]\llbracket q\rrbracket



for a formal parameter qq recording nn.




Recursive formulas for computing 𝒫n\mathscr{P}_{n} can be deduced from [10, 11], where we developed recursive formulas for ch𝕊n​H∗​(ℳ¯0,n)\mathrm{ch}_{\mathbb{S}_{n}}H^{*}(\overline{{\cal M}}_{0,n}) that use only arithmetic operations and plethysm. See [11, Theorem 1.3] or §5.1 and 5.2.




To present explicit formulas, let 𝒬n,k\mathscr{Q}_{n,k} denote the characteristic polynomial

of the 𝕊n\mathbb{S}_{n}-representation on H2​k​(ℳ¯0,n+1)H^{2k}(\overline{{\cal M}}_{0,n+1}) induced by the action that permutes the first nn markings while keeping the last fixed. The advantage of 𝒬n,k\mathscr{Q}_{n,k} is that there is a rather straightforward combinatorial formula

Moreover, we consider a partial sum




𝒬n,k+=∑T∈𝒯n,k+𝒮ch𝕊n​(UT)\mathscr{Q}_{n,k}^{+}=\sum_{T\in\mathscr{T}_{n,k}^{+}}\mathscr{S}_{\mathrm{ch}_{\mathbb{S}_{n}}(U_{T})}



of (1.4)

By using the above formulas, we implemented a computer program to explicitly compute the characteristic polynomials, and carried out computations for n≤72n\leq 72. Based on this numerical evidence, we propose a conjecture concerning the log-concavity of the characteristic polynomials (Conjecture 5.14).





1.4. Asymptotic formulas and log-concavity




We study asymptotic formulas of 𝒫n,k​(m)\mathscr{P}_{n,k}(m), as nn grows, in two directions:




(1) 



the values 𝒫n,k​(m0)\mathscr{P}_{n,k}(m_{0}) at a given m0≥1m_{0}\geq 1 for given k≥0k\geq 0;

(2) 



the coefficients of mn−jm^{n-j} in 𝒫n,k\mathscr{P}_{n,k}, denoted by 𝒫n,kn−j\mathscr{P}_{n,k}^{n-j}, for given j,k≥0j,k\geq 0.



These formulas lead us to corresponding asymptotic log-concavity properties which are far reaching generalizations of the corresponding results in [3, 11].




For the asymptotics, we use the constants




ck=(k+1)k−1k!​dk=(k+1)k−2k!.c_{k}=\frac{(k+1)^{k-1}}{k!}d_{k}=\frac{(k+1)^{k-2}}{k!}.

Fix m0≥1m_{0}\geq 1 and k≥0k\geq 0. Then as nn grows,




𝒬n,k​(m0)=ck((k+1)​m0−1)!​n(k+1)​m0−1+o⁡(n(k+1)​m0−1)​ and𝒫n,k​(m0)=dk((k+1)​m0−1)!​n(k+1)​m0−1+o⁡(n(k+1)​m0−1).\begin{split}&\mathscr{Q}_{n,k}(m_{0})=\frac{c_{k}}{((k+1)m_{0}-1)!}n^{(k+1)m_{0}-1}+o(n^{(k+1)m_{0}-1})~\text{ and}\\
&\mathscr{P}_{n,k}(m_{0})=\frac{d_{k}}{((k+1)m_{0}-1)!}n^{(k+1)m_{0}-1}+o(n^{(k+1)m_{0}-1}).\end{split}







From this, the following asymptotic log-concavity follows.




Corollary 1.4 (Corollary 6.9).



Fix m0,k≥1m_{0},k\geq 1. Then,




𝒫n,k​(m0)2≥𝒫n,k−1​(m0)​𝒫n,k+1​(m0)​ and𝒬n,k​(m0)2≥𝒬n,k−1​(m0)​𝒬n,k+1​(m0)\begin{split}&\mathscr{P}_{n,k}(m_{0})^{2}\geq\mathscr{P}_{n,k-1}(m_{0})\mathscr{P}_{n,k+1}(m_{0})~\text{ and}\\
&\mathscr{Q}_{n,k}(m_{0})^{2}\geq\mathscr{Q}_{n,k-1}(m_{0})\mathscr{Q}_{n,k+1}(m_{0})\end{split}



for sufficiently large nn. Moreover, when m0>1m_{0}>1, the inequalities are strict.





Theorem 1.3 and Corollary 1.4
generalize the asymptotic formulas and the asymptotic log-concavity for the Betti numbers of ℳ¯0,n/𝕊n\overline{{\cal M}}_{0,n}/\mathbb{S}_{n} in [11, §6] which correspond to the case of m0=1m_{0}=1 (cf. Remark 6.2).




Next, we investigate the asymptotic behaviors of the coefficients in 𝒫n,k\mathscr{P}_{n,k} and 𝒬n,k\mathscr{Q}_{n,k}. Recall that the signless Stirling number of the first kind c⁡(n,j)c(n,j) is the number of σ∈𝕊n\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n} with jj disjoint cycles.




Write
𝒫n,k​(m)=∑j𝒫n,kj​mj\mathscr{P}_{n,k}(m)=\sum_{j}\mathscr{P}^{j}_{n,k}m^{j} and 𝒬n,k​(m)=∑j𝒬n,kj​mj\mathscr{Q}_{n,k}(m)=\sum_{j}\mathscr{Q}^{j}_{n,k}m^{j}.




Theorem 1.5 (Theorem 6.6).



Let j,k∈ℤ≥0j,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}. Then as nn grows,




𝒬n,kn−j=ck⋅(k+1)n−j​c​(n,n−j)n!+o⁡((k+1)n​n2​jn!)​ and𝒫n,kn−j=dk⋅(k+1)n−j​c​(n,n−j)n!+o⁡((k+1)n​n2​jn!).\begin{split}&\mathscr{Q}_{n,k}^{n-j}=c_{k}\cdot\frac{(k+1)^{n-j}c(n,n-j)}{n!}+o\left(\frac{(k+1)^{n}n^{2j}}{n!}\right)~\text{ and}\\

2. Two-dimensional log-concavity




A sequence {ak}\{a_{k}\} of nonnegative real numbers is called log-concave if

ak2≥ak−1​ak+1for all ​k.a_{k}^{2}\geq a_{k-1}a_{k+1}\quad\text{for all }k.



The log-concavity extends to arbitrary real sequences by taking the sequence of absolute values |ak||a_{k}|.
A nonnegative finite sequence {ak}k=0n\left\{a_{k}\right\}_{k=0}^{n} is ultra log-concave if the normalized sequence

3. Characteristic polynomials of symmetric functions and representations




In this section, we investigate useful properties of the principal specialization map or the Stanley map in [28, §7.8]
which associates a polynomial 𝒮F​(m)∈ℚ​[m]\mathscr{S}_{F}(m)\in\mathbb{Q}[m] to each symmetric function F∈ΛnF\in\Lambda_{n} of degree nn.
A remarkable feature of the Stanley map




𝒮:Λn⟶ℚ⁡[m],F↦𝒮F​(m)\mathscr{S}:\Lambda_{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Q}[m],\quad F\mapsto\mathscr{S}_{F}(m)



is that it sends the plethysm operation on Λn\Lambda_{n} to the composition on ℚ⁡[m]\mathbb{Q}[m] (Proposition 3.2).
In fact, this property uniquely determines the Stanley map (Theorem 3.3).
Since the plethysm operation usually causes computational difficulties, the Stanley map levitates computational burdens while keeping core information, just like the characteristic polynomial of a matrix.
In this vein, we call 𝒮F​(m)∈ℚ​[m]\mathscr{S}_{F}(m)\in\mathbb{Q}[m] the characteristic polynomial of FF (Definition 3.1).
When the symmetric function is the chromatic symmetric function of a graph defined by Stanley in [25], its characteristic polynomial is the usual chromatic polynomial of Birkhoff’s, which is log-concave by [17].




By applying the Stanley map to the Frobenius characteristic ch⁡(V)∈Λn\mathrm{ch}(V)\in\Lambda_{n} of an 𝕊n\mathbb{S}_{n}-module VV, we obtain a polynomial
𝒮V​(m)∈ℚ​[m]\mathscr{S}_{V}(m)\in\mathbb{Q}[m] which we call the characteristic polynomial of VV. When
V=⨁k≥0VkV=\bigoplus_{k\geq 0}V_{k} is graded, its characteristic polynomial




𝒮V​(m,t)=∑k𝒮Vk​(m)​tk∈ℚ⁡[m,t]\mathscr{S}_{V}(m,t)=\sum_{k}\mathscr{S}_{V_{k}}(m)t^{k}\in\mathbb{Q}[m,t]



allows us to consider the two-dimensional log-concavities in Definition 2.1.
In the subsequent sections, we will find interesting examples where the log-concavities hold.

4.3. Hessenberg varieties




Regular semisimple Hessenberg varieties are subvarieties in the flag variety, naturally equipped with an 𝕊n\mathbb{S}_{n}-action on its cohomology. For further details on the discussion below, we refer the reader to a survey [2].




Denote the variety of flags in ℂn\mathbb{C}^{n} by Fln={F∙=(F1⊂⋯⊂Fn=ℂn)}\mathrm{Fl}_{n}=\{F_{\bullet}=(F_{1}\subset\cdots\subset F_{n}=\mathbb{C}^{n})\} where FiF_{i} are ii-dimensional subspaces of ℂn\mathbb{C}^{n} for each i∈[n]:={1,⋯,n}i\in[n]:=\{1,\cdots,n\}.




A Hessenberg function is a non-decreasing function h:[n]→[n]h:[n]\to[n] with h⁡(i)≥ih(i)\geq i for all ii. Choosing a regular semisimple n×nn\times n matrix ss (or equivalently, an n×nn\times n matrix with nn distinct eigenvalues), one obtains a subvariety in Fln\mathrm{Fl}_{n}, called a (regular semisimple) Hessenberg variety, defined as

These varieties were introduced in [12], where it is also proved that they are smooth of dimension ∑i=1n(h⁡(i)−i)\sum_{i=1}^{n}(h(i)-i) when ss is regular semisimple. 5. Characteristic polynomial of ℳ¯0,n\overline{{\cal M}}_{0,n}




The moduli space ℳ¯0,n\overline{{\cal M}}_{0,n} of nn-pointed stable curves of genus 00 is one of the most important varieties in algebraic geometry and has been much studied (cf. [10, §1]). It comes with a natural action of the symmetric group 𝕊n\mathbb{S}_{n} by permuting the marked points and thus its cohomology H∗​(ℳ¯0,n)H^{*}(\overline{{\cal M}}_{0,n}) is a graded 𝕊n\mathbb{S}_{n}-module. The rest of this paper is devoted to the study of the characteristic polynomial 𝒮ℳ¯0,n\mathscr{S}_{\overline{{\cal M}}_{0,n}} of ℳ¯0,n\overline{{\cal M}}_{0,n} as well as that of ℳ¯0,n+1\overline{{\cal M}}_{0,n+1} on which 𝕊n\mathbb{S}_{n} is acting by permuting the first nn marked points with the last one fixed. Based on our pervious results in [10, 11], we provide recursive formulas for the characteristic polynomials (Theorem 5.11).
In the last subsection §5.3, we find an interpretation of the characteristic polynomials of permutation representations that constitute the 𝕊n\mathbb{S}_{n}-representation H∗​(ℳ¯0,n+1)H^{*}(\overline{{\cal M}}_{0,n+1}), in terms of colorings (Corollary 5.21).

The moduli space ℳ¯0,n\overline{{\cal M}}_{0,n} is a compactification of the variety

A labeled rooted tree
is a tree with one distinguished leg called, the output, and additional nn legs, called the inputs, labeled by 1,⋯,n1,\cdots,n, for some n∈ℤ≥0n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}. The vertex to which the output is attached is called the root.




More concretely, a labeled rooted tree is a triple of a tree TT, the output map pt→V⁡(T)\mathrm{pt}\to V(T) and the input map {1,⋯,n}→V⁡(T)\{1,\cdots,n\}\to V(T), where V⁡(T)V(T) is the set of vertices of TT. In particular, permuting the labels of the inputs attached to the same vertex does not distinguish the labeled rooted tree.




A rooted tree is a tree obtained from a labeled rooted tree by forgetting the labels on the inputs.





Definition 5.3.



Let TT be a (resp. labeled) rooted tree. A weight function on TT is a function w:V⁡(T)→ℤ≥0w:V(T)\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} on the vertex set V⁡(T)V(T). We denote the total weight by |w|=∑v∈V⁡(T)w⁡(v)\lvert w\rvert=\sum_{v\in V(T)}w(v).




We call the pair (T,w)(T,w) a (resp. labeled) weighted rooted tree.
