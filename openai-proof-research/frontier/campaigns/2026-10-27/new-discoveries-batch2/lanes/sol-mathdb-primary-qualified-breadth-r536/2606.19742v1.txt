An upper bound on the largest eigenvalue
of the Helmholtzian of a graph
Suil O
††thanks: Department of Applied Mathematics and Statistics, The State University of New York, Korea, Incheon, 21985, suil.o@sunykorea.ac.kr. Research supported by the National Research Foundation of Korea (NRF) grant funded by the Korea government(MSIT) No. RS-2025-23523950.
Abstract
The Helmholtzian of a graph $G$ is the Hodge $1$ -Laplacian $L_{1}=L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}+L_{1}^{\mathrm{down}}$ of its clique complex, built from the triangle–edge and edge–vertex boundary operators $\partial_{2}$ and $\partial_{1}$ . Problem 5.5 of Lu, Shi, Stanić, Wang and Wang asks whether $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1})=\mu_{1}(G)$ for every graph $G$ , where $\mu_{1}(G)$ is the largest Laplacian eigenvalue; by the Hodge decomposition this is equivalent to $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}})\leq\mu_{1}(G)$ . We recast it as a question about the complement of $G$ : localizing $L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}$ on the cycle space of $K_{n}$ turns it into the inequality $\lambda_{\min}(\bar{L}|_{Z_{1}})\geq a(\overline{G})$ , where $\bar{L}$ is the up Laplacian of the missing triangles of $G$ and $a(\overline{G})=n-\mu_{1}(G)$ is the algebraic connectivity of the complement. From this viewpoint we prove the unconditional bound
$\lambda_{\max}\!\big(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}(G)\big)\ \leq\ \mu_{1}(G)+\frac{1}{3}\big(n-\mu_{1}(G)\big),$
which refines the integrality ceiling $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}})\leq n$ of Duval and Reiner and is sharp exactly when that ceiling is attained. We then isolate the single sharp inequality, on the dense part of $\overline{G}$ , that stops the method short of Problem 5.5, and we show that the localization, the bound, and this obstruction all persist for the up Laplacian of an arbitrary finite simplicial complex, in every dimension.
Keywords: Helmholtzian; Hodge Laplacian; clique complex; boundary operator; algebraic connectivity.
AMS subject classification 2020: 05C50, 05E45, 55U10.
1 Introduction
The combinatorial Hodge Laplacian, introduced by Eckmann [3], extends the graph Laplacian from functions on the vertices of a graph to flows on its edges, and more generally to chains on the faces of a simplicial complex. Its first instance, the Hodge $1$ -Laplacian, acts on edge flows and underlies the Hodge–Helmholtz decomposition, which splits a flow into a gradient part, a harmonic part, and a curl part; for this reason the Hodge $1$ -Laplacian of a graph is called its Helmholtzian. Such operators have become a basic tool in topological data analysis, statistical ranking, and signal processing on simplicial networks, in which the clique complex provides the standard higher-order structure built from pairwise relations (see Horak and Jost [6] and Lu, Shi, Stanić, Wang and Wang [7], together with the references therein). It is therefore natural to ask how the spectrum of the Helmholtzian relates to that of the ordinary graph Laplacian, and this note is concerned with the largest eigenvalue.
All graphs in this note are finite and simple. For a graph $G$ with vertex set $V(G)=[n]$ , the clique complex $X(G)$ , an abstract simplicial complex, has the vertices, edges, and triangles (the sets of three mutually adjacent vertices) of $G$ as its $0$ -, $1$ -, and $2$ -cells. We orient each edge $\{i,j\}$ from the smaller to the larger endpoint and each triangle by the increasing order of its vertices. The boundary operators
$\partial_{1}\colon C_{1}\to C_{0},\qquad\partial_{2}\colon C_{2}\to C_{1}$
are the signed vertex–edge and edge–triangle incidence maps, where $C_{i}$ denotes the space of real $i$ -chains; as usual, $\partial_{1}\partial_{2}=0$ . The up and down parts of the Hodge $1$ -Laplacian are
$L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}=\partial_{2}\partial_{2}^{\top},\qquad L_{1}^{\mathrm{down}}=\partial_{1}^{\top}\partial_{1},$
both acting on $C_{1}=\mathbb{R}^{E(G)}$ , and the Helmholtzian of $G$ is $L_{1}=L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}+L_{1}^{\mathrm{down}}$ . The matrix $\partial_{1}\partial_{1}^{\top}$ is the ordinary graph Laplacian $L(G)=D(G)-A(G)$ , where $D(G)$ and $A(G)$ are the degree and adjacency matrices of $G$ ; we write $\mu_{1}(G)$ for its largest eigenvalue. Since $L_{1}^{\mathrm{down}}=\partial_{1}^{\top}\partial_{1}$ and $\partial_{1}\partial_{1}^{\top}$ share their nonzero spectrum, we have
$\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{down}})=\mu_{1}(G).$
(1)
A notable feature of the Helmholtzian, observed in [7], is that every nonzero eigenvalue of the graph Laplacian $L(G)$ is again an eigenvalue of $L_{1}$ . This is precisely the content of (1): the down part $L_{1}^{\mathrm{down}}=\partial_{1}^{\top}\partial_{1}$ inherits the nonzero spectrum of $\partial_{1}\partial_{1}^{\top}=L(G)$ , and in particular its largest eigenvalue is $\mu_{1}(G)$ . Thus the down part of $L_{1}$ already attains $\mu_{1}(G)$ , and one may ask whether the up part—the contribution of the triangles—can raise $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1})$ further. This is the content of the following problem of Lu, Shi, Stanić, Wang and Wang [7].
Problem 5.5 ([7]). Is $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1})=\mu_{1}(G)$ for every graph $G$ ?
By the Hodge decomposition, $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1})=\max\{\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}),\,\mu_{1}(G)\}$ (Lemma 2.1 below), so $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1})=\mu_{1}(G)$ if and only if
$\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}})\ \leq\ \mu_{1}(G),\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad\sigma_{\max}(\partial_{2})\leq\sigma_{\max}(\partial_{1}),$
(2)
where the equivalence uses $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}})=\sigma_{\max}(\partial_{2})^{2}$ together with (1). In this form, Problem 5.5 asks whether the largest singular value of the boundary operator does not increase when one passes from the edge–vertex map $\partial_{1}$ to the triangle–edge map $\partial_{2}$ . Note that (2) is the graph case of the more general comparison
$\sigma_{\max}(\partial_{k+1})\ \leq\ \sigma_{\max}(\partial_{k})\qquad\text{for all }k\geq 1,$
(3)
asserting that the largest eigenvalue of the up Laplacian is non-increasing in dimension; we believe (3) holds for every finite simplicial complex. The corresponding statement fails for the normalized Hodge Laplacian, so the comparison in (3) appears to be a feature of the combinatorial (unnormalized) Laplacian. Problem 5.5 has been confirmed by exhaustive and random computation, but no proof is known.
Our main result is an unconditional bound that confirms (2) up to a controlled multiple of the gap $n-\mu_{1}(G)$ .
Theorem 1.1.
For every $n$ -vertex graph $G$ , we have
$\lambda_{\max}\!\big(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}(G)\big)\ \leq\ \mu_{1}(G)+\frac{1}{3}\big(n-\mu_{1}(G)\big).$
Duval and Reiner [2] proved the integrality ceiling $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}})\leq n$ . Since $\mu_{1}(G)\leq n$ , Theorem 1.1 strengthens this ceiling, and its bound coincides with the target $\mu_{1}(G)$ of (2) exactly when $\mu_{1}(G)=n$ , that is, when $G$ is a join. Thus Theorem 1.1 shows that the conjectured equality $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}})=\mu_{1}$ can fail, if at all, only by a bounded fraction of $n-\mu_{1}(G)$ when $\mu_{1}(G)<n$ .
The proof rests on two facts. First, the largest eigenvalue of $L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}$ is attained on the cycle space of the complete graph $K_{n}$ , where $L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}(G)$ becomes $n$ times the identity minus the up Laplacian of the missing triangles of $G$ (Proposition 3.1). Second, the missing triangles through a fixed non-edge encode the algebraic connectivity of the complement $\overline{G}$ , once one uses that the optimizing $1$ -chain is divergence-free (Lemma 3.2 and Theorem 1.1).
This localization recasts Problem 5.5 as a statement about the complement: by Proposition 3.1, inequality (2) is equivalent to $\lambda_{\min}\big(\bar{L}(G)|_{Z_{1}}\big)\geq a(\overline{G})$ , and Theorem 1.1 is the bound $\lambda_{\min}(\bar{L}|_{Z_{1}})\geq\tfrac{2}{3}\,a(\overline{G})$ . Section 5 isolates the single sharp inequality, on the dense part of $\overline{G}$ , that keeps this method from reaching $a(\overline{G})$ , and exhibits a graph on which (2) is an equality and the loss is visible. Section 4 then shows that the localization, the divergence-free identity behind it, and the bound all extend to the up Laplacian of an arbitrary finite simplicial complex, with $\tfrac{1}{3}$ replaced by $\tfrac{1}{k+2}$ in dimension $k$ (Theorem 4.2).
For undefined terms in graph theory, see West [8]; for the basics of spectral graph theory, see Brouwer and Haemers [1] or Godsil and Royle [5].
2 Definitions and Tools
Throughout, $G$ is a graph on $[n]$ with complement $\overline{G}$ , and we work in the full edge space $U=\mathbb{R}^{\binom{[n]}{2}}$ indexed by all pairs of $[n]$ . A vector $x\in U$ is regarded as a flow: $x_{ij}$ is its value on the oriented pair $i<j$ , and we set $x_{ji}=-x_{ij}$ . For a $3$ -subset $S=\{p,q,w\}$ , let $v_{S}\in U$ be the boundary $\partial_{2}S$ , supported on the three pairs of $S$ with the cyclic signs, so that
$\operatorname{circ}_{pqw}(x):=v_{\{p,q,w\}}^{\top}x=x_{pq}+x_{qw}+x_{wp}$
is the circulation of $x$ around $S$ . Identifying $\mathbb{R}^{E(G)}$ with the corresponding coordinate subspace of $U$ , and noting that the pairs outside every triangle contribute trivially, we have
$L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}(G)=\sum_{S\in T(G)}v_{S}v_{S}^{\top},$
where $T(G)$ is the set of triangles of $G$ ; this representation changes neither the nonzero spectrum nor $\lambda_{\max}$ .
The reduction in (2) is a standard consequence of the Hodge decomposition $C_{1}=\im\partial_{2}\oplus\ker L_{1}\oplus\im\partial_{1}^{\top}$ (see Eckmann [3] or Horak and Jost [6]).
Lemma 2.1.
For every graph $G$ , we have $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1})=\max\{\mu_{1}(G),\,\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}})\}$ . In particular, $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1})=\mu_{1}(G)$ if and only if $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}})\leq\mu_{1}(G)$ .
Proof.
On $\im\partial_{2}$ the down part $L_{1}^{\mathrm{down}}=\partial_{1}^{\top}\partial_{1}$ vanishes, because $\partial_{1}\partial_{2}=0$ ; on $\im\partial_{1}^{\top}$ the up part $L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}=\partial_{2}\partial_{2}^{\top}$ vanishes, because $\partial_{2}^{\top}\partial_{1}^{\top}=(\partial_{1}\partial_{2})^{\top}=0$ ; and $L_{1}$ vanishes on $\ker L_{1}$ . Hence $L_{1}$ is block diagonal for this orthogonal decomposition, with blocks $L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}|_{\im\partial_{2}}$ , $0$ , and $L_{1}^{\mathrm{down}}|_{\im\partial_{1}^{\top}}$ . It follows that $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1})=\max\{\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}),\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{down}})\}$ , and (1) completes the proof.
∎
We next localize the up Laplacian on the cycle space. Let $Z_{1}=\ker\partial_{1}\subseteq U$ be the cycle space of $K_{n}$ , the space of divergence-free flows, of dimension $\binom{n}{2}-n+1$ . Divergence-freeness means
$\sum_{u\,:\,u\neq v}x_{vu}=0\qquad\text{for every }v\in[n]\text{ and every }x\in Z_{1}.$
(4)
The complete clique complex on $[n]$ is the full simplex, and by [6] its Hodge Laplacians satisfy $L_{k}=nI$ for every $k\geq 1$ ; in particular $L_{1}=nI$ . On $Z_{1}$ the down part vanishes by definition, so the up Laplacian of the complete complex satisfies $\big(\sum_{S\in\binom{[n]}{3}}v_{S}v_{S}^{\top}\big)\big|_{Z_{1}}=nI$ . Splitting the sum over $3$ -subsets into the triangles and the non-triangles of $G$ , and writing
$\bar{L}(G):=\sum_{S\in\binom{[n]}{3}\setminus T(G)}v_{S}v_{S}^{\top}$
for the up Laplacian of the missing triangles, we obtain on $Z_{1}$ the identity
$L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}(G)\big|_{Z_{1}}+\bar{L}(G)\big|_{Z_{1}}=nI.$
(5)
Finally, let $a(\overline{G})$ be the algebraic connectivity of $\overline{G}$ , that is, the second smallest eigenvalue of $L(\overline{G})$ (see Fiedler [4]). Since $L(G)+L(\overline{G})=L(K_{n})=nI-J$ , on the space $\mathbf{1}^{\perp}$ the eigenvalues of $L(\overline{G})$ are exactly $\{\,n-\mu_{i}(G)\,\}$ , and in particular
$a(\overline{G})=n-\mu_{1}(G).$
(6)
3 Main Results
In this section, we prove Theorem 1.1. We first record the cycle-space localization. Each $v_{S}$ lies in $Z_{1}$ , since $v_{S}=\partial_{2}S$ and $\partial_{1}\partial_{2}=0$ ; consequently both $L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}(G)$ and $\bar{L}(G)$ annihilate $Z_{1}^{\perp}$ and preserve $Z_{1}$ .
Proposition 3.1.
For every graph $G$ , the maximum of $L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}(G)$ is attained on $Z_{1}$ , and
$\lambda_{\max}\!\big(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}(G)\big)=n-\lambda_{\min}\!\big(\bar{L}(G)\big|_{Z_{1}}\big).$
Consequently, inequality (2) (Problem 5.5) is equivalent to
$\lambda_{\min}\!\big(\bar{L}(G)\big|_{Z_{1}}\big)\ \geq\ a(\overline{G}).$
Proof.
Each $v_{S}\in Z_{1}$ , so for $x\in Z_{1}^{\perp}$ we have $L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}(G)x=\sum_{S}v_{S}(v_{S}^{\top}x)=0$ ; thus $L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}(G)$ maps $Z_{1}$ to itself and vanishes on $Z_{1}^{\perp}$ , and its largest eigenvalue is attained on $Z_{1}$ . The displayed identity is then (5), and the equivalence follows from (6).
∎
The next identity is the key to the proof: it evaluates the total circulation of a divergence-free flow around all triangles through a fixed pair.
Lemma 3.2.
Let $x\in Z_{1}$ and let $e=\{p,q\}$ . Then
$\sum_{w\neq p,q}\operatorname{circ}_{pqw}(x)^{2}=(n+2)\,x_{pq}^{2}+\sum_{w\neq p,q}\big(x_{qw}-x_{pw}\big)^{2}.$
Proof.
Write $\operatorname{circ}_{pqw}(x)=x_{pq}+x_{qw}+x_{wp}=x_{pq}+(x_{qw}-x_{pw})$ . Squaring and summing the $n-2$ terms, we obtain
$\sum_{w}\operatorname{circ}_{pqw}(x)^{2}=(n-2)x_{pq}^{2}+2x_{pq}\sum_{w}(x_{qw}-x_{pw})+\sum_{w}(x_{qw}-x_{pw})^{2}.$
By (4) at $q$ , we have $\sum_{w\neq p,q}x_{qw}=-x_{qp}=x_{pq}$ , and at $p$ , $\sum_{w\neq p,q}x_{pw}=-x_{pq}$ ; hence $\sum_{w}(x_{qw}-x_{pw})=2x_{pq}$ and the middle term equals $4x_{pq}^{2}$ . Adding $(n-2)x_{pq}^{2}+4x_{pq}^{2}=(n+2)x_{pq}^{2}$ gives the claim.
∎
Proof of Theorem 1.1.
By Proposition 3.1 and (6), it suffices to prove that
$x^{\top}\bar{L}(G)\,x\ \geq\ \frac{2}{3}\,a(\overline{G})\,\|x\|^{2}\qquad\text{for all }x\in Z_{1}.$
(7)
Fix $x\in Z_{1}$ ; by homogeneity we may assume $\|x\|^{2}=1$ . Recall that $x^{\top}\bar{L}(G)x=\sum_{S}\operatorname{circ}_{S}(x)^{2}$ , summed over the non-triangles $S$ of $G$ . The proof proceeds in three steps.
Step 1 (overcounting by missing edges).
Each non-triangle $S$ has $\mu(S)\in\{1,2,3\}$ pairs that are non-edges of $G$ , i.e. edges of $\overline{G}$ . Since $\mu(S)\leq 3$ ,
$\bar{L}(G)=\sum_{S}v_{S}v_{S}^{\top}\ \succeq\ \frac{1}{3}\sum_{S}\mu(S)\,v_{S}v_{S}^{\top}=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{e\in E(\overline{G})}\ \sum_{w\notin e}v_{\,e\cup w}\,v_{\,e\cup w}^{\top},$
because every $3$ -set containing a fixed $e\in E(\overline{G})$ is a non-triangle, and each non-triangle is counted once for each of its $\mu(S)$ missing edges. Applying Lemma 3.2 to each $e=\{p,q\}\in E(\overline{G})$ , we obtain
$x^{\top}\bar{L}(G)x\ \geq\ \frac{1}{3}\sum_{e\in E(\overline{G})}\Big[(n+2)x_{e}^{2}+\sum_{w}(x_{qw}-x_{pw})^{2}\Big].$
(8)
Step 2 (the complement’s connectivity, via extension by zero).
For a fixed vertex $w$ , define $g^{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{[n]}$ by $g^{w}_{u}=x_{uw}$ for $u\neq w$ and $g^{w}_{w}=0$ . By (4) at $w$ , we have $\sum_{u\neq w}x_{uw}=0$ , so $g^{w}\perp\mathbf{1}$ . Splitting the Dirichlet sum of $g^{w}$ over $\overline{G}$ according to whether a $\overline{G}$ -edge meets $w$ ,
$(g^{w})^{\top}L(\overline{G})\,g^{w}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}pq\in E(\overline{G})\\
p,q\neq w\end{subarray}}\!\!(g^{w}_{p}-g^{w}_{q})^{2}+\sum_{p:\,pw\in E(\overline{G})}\!\!(g^{w}_{p})^{2},$
where the last sum used $g^{w}_{w}=0$ . Since $g^{w}\perp\mathbf{1}$ , the left side is at least $a(\overline{G})\|g^{w}\|^{2}$ , and therefore
$\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}pq\in E(\overline{G})\\
p,q\neq w\end{subarray}}(g^{w}_{p}-g^{w}_{q})^{2}\ \geq\ a(\overline{G})\,\|g^{w}\|^{2}-\sum_{p:\,pw\in E(\overline{G})}x_{pw}^{2}.$
Now sum over $w$ . The left side equals $\sum_{e=pq\in E(\overline{G})}\sum_{w}(x_{qw}-x_{pw})^{2}$ . Moreover $\sum_{w}\|g^{w}\|^{2}=\sum_{w}\sum_{u\neq w}x_{uw}^{2}=2\|x\|^{2}=2$ , since each unordered pair is counted from both of its endpoints, and likewise $\sum_{w}\sum_{p:\,pw\in E(\overline{G})}x_{pw}^{2}=2\sum_{e\in E(\overline{G})}x_{e}^{2}$ . It follows that
$\sum_{e\in E(\overline{G})}\sum_{w}(x_{qw}-x_{pw})^{2}\ \geq\ 2\,a(\overline{G})-2\sum_{e\in E(\overline{G})}x_{e}^{2}.$
(9)
Step 3 (combining the bounds).
Substituting (9) into (8), we obtain
$x^{\top}\bar{L}(G)x\ \geq\ \frac{1}{3}\Big[(n+2)\sum_{e\in E(\overline{G})}x_{e}^{2}+2\,a(\overline{G})-2\sum_{e\in E(\overline{G})}x_{e}^{2}\Big]=\frac{1}{3}\Big[n\sum_{e\in E(\overline{G})}x_{e}^{2}+2\,a(\overline{G})\Big]\ \geq\ \frac{2}{3}\,a(\overline{G}),$
because $\sum_{e\in E(\overline{G})}x_{e}^{2}\geq 0$ . This proves (7), and with Proposition 3.1 the theorem follows:
$\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}})=n-\lambda_{\min}(\bar{L}|_{Z_{1}})\leq n-\frac{2}{3}a(\overline{G})=n-\frac{2}{3}\big(n-\mu_{1}\big)=\mu_{1}+\frac{1}{3}(n-\mu_{1}).\qed$
Theorem 1.1 yields the integrality ceiling of Duval and Reiner [2] as an immediate corollary.
Corollary 3.3.
For every $n$ -vertex graph $G$ , we have $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}(G))\leq n$ , with equality only if $\mu_{1}(G)=n$ .
Proof.
Since $\mu_{1}(G)\leq n$ , Theorem 1.1 gives $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}})\leq\mu_{1}+\tfrac{1}{3}(n-\mu_{1})=\tfrac{2}{3}\mu_{1}+\tfrac{1}{3}n\leq n$ , where the last inequality is strict unless $\mu_{1}=n$ .
∎
We close this section with a remark on the two extreme cases of Theorem 1.1, namely when $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}})$ meets the ceiling $n$ and when it meets $\mu_{1}$ .
Remark 3.4.
By Corollary 3.3, the ceiling $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}})=n$ is attained only when $\mu_{1}(G)=n$ , that is, only when $G$ is a join. The converse fails. For $n\geq 4$ , let $G^{*}=K_{1}\vee\big(K_{2}\cup(n-3)K_{1}\big)$ be the join of a single vertex with the disjoint union of an edge and $n-3$ isolated vertices. Then $G^{*}$ is a join, so $\mu_{1}(G^{*})=n$ , while the only triangle of $G^{*}$ is the one spanned by the hub and the edge of $K_{2}$ ; hence $L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}(G^{*})$ has rank one and $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}(G^{*}))=3$ for every such $n$ . Thus a join can fall arbitrarily far below the ceiling, and being a join is necessary but not sufficient for $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}})=n$ . By contrast, the complete graph $K_{n}$ attains the ceiling, since $L_{1}=nI$ on $Z_{1}$ .
At the other extreme, $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}})$ meets $\mu_{1}$ exactly when (2) is an equality. This holds at the ceiling, where $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}})=\mu_{1}=n$ , and it also holds off the ceiling for the disconnected graph $K_{n-1}\cup K_{1}$ of Section 5, a non-join with $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}})=\mu_{1}=n-1$ . Whether equality can occur for a connected non-join is open, and it cannot be separated from Problem 5.5: a connected $G$ with $\mu_{1}(G)<n$ and $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}})=\mu_{1}$ would in particular satisfy $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}})\leq\mu_{1}$ , so ruling such graphs out is no easier than Problem 5.5 itself, which Theorem 1.1 does not settle.
4 Higher Dimensions
The argument of Section 3 is not particular to graphs. In this section we carry it out in every dimension, recovering Theorem 1.1 when $k=1$ and proving the corresponding bound toward Conjecture 5.2.
Recall that an abstract simplicial complex on $[n]$ is a family of subsets of $[n]$ closed under inclusion; a member of size $j+1$ is a $j$ -face. The clique complex of Sections 2–5 is the instance whose faces are the vertex sets of the cliques of a graph, and its faces of dimension at most two carry the Helmholtzian. We now allow faces of every dimension; the boundary operators, chain spaces, and Laplacians below are those of the general combinatorial Hodge theory of Horak and Jost [6].
Fix an integer $k\geq 1$ . We work in the full simplex on $[n]$ , writing $C_{j}=\mathbb{R}^{\binom{[n]}{j+1}}$ for its space of $j$ -chains and $\partial_{j}\colon C_{j}\to C_{j-1}$ for the boundary maps, so that $L_{j}^{\mathrm{up}}=\partial_{j+1}\partial_{j+1}^{\top}$ and $L_{j}^{\mathrm{down}}=\partial_{j}^{\top}\partial_{j}$ as in Section 2. For a simplicial complex $G$ on $[n]$ , its up Laplacian in dimension $k$ is $L_{k}^{\mathrm{up}}(G)=\sum_{F}v_{F}v_{F}^{\top}$ , summed over the $(k+1)$ -faces $F$ of $G$ , where $v_{F}=\partial_{k+1}F\in C_{k}$ ; as in Section 3 this acts on $C_{k}$ and annihilates $Z_{k}^{\perp}$ , where $Z_{k}=\ker\partial_{k}$ . Write
$\mu_{k-1}(G)=\lambda_{\max}\big(L_{k-1}^{\mathrm{up}}(G)\big),$
so that for $k=1$ , $\mu_{0}(G)=\lambda_{\max}(L(G))=\mu_{1}(G)$ is the largest Laplacian eigenvalue of the introduction.
Each $v_{F}$ lies in $Z_{k}$ , and on the full simplex $\big(\sum_{F\in\binom{[n]}{k+2}}v_{F}v_{F}^{\top}\big)\big|_{Z_{k}}=nI$ (see [6]). Writing $\bar{L}_{k}(G)$ for the sum of $v_{F}v_{F}^{\top}$ over the missing $(k+1)$ -faces of $G$ , we obtain on $Z_{k}$ the identity $L_{k}^{\mathrm{up}}(G)|_{Z_{k}}+\bar{L}_{k}(G)|_{Z_{k}}=nI$ , exactly as in (5). The same identity one dimension lower, applied to the $k$ -faces, reads $L_{k-1}^{\mathrm{up}}(G)|_{Z_{k-1}}+L_{k-1}^{\mathrm{up}}(\bar{G})|_{Z_{k-1}}=nI$ , where $L_{k-1}^{\mathrm{up}}(\bar{G})=\sum_{\tau}v_{\tau}v_{\tau}^{\top}$ is summed over the missing $k$ -faces $\tau$ , with $v_{\tau}=\partial_{k}\tau$ . We therefore set
$a_{k}(\bar{G}):=\lambda_{\min}\big(L_{k-1}^{\mathrm{up}}(\bar{G})\big|_{Z_{k-1}}\big)=n-\mu_{k-1}(G),$
the codimension-one connectivity of the complement, which generalizes $a(\overline{G})=n-\mu_{1}(G)$ .
For a $k$ -face $\tau$ (a $(k+1)$ -subset of $[n]$ ) and a vertex $w\notin\tau$ , let $\operatorname{circ}_{\tau,w}(x)=\langle\partial_{k+1}(\tau\cup w),\,x\rangle$ . As in Section 2, $\operatorname{circ}_{\tau,w}(x)=\varepsilon_{w}x_{\tau}+D_{w}(x)$ , where $\varepsilon_{w}\in\{\pm 1\}$ is the sign of the facet $\tau$ in $\partial_{k+1}(\tau\cup w)$ and
$D_{w}(x)=\sum_{u\in\tau}\varepsilon_{u}\,x_{(\tau\setminus u)\cup w}$
collects the remaining $k+1$ facets. The following is the higher-dimensional form of Lemma 3.2.
Lemma 4.1.
Let $x\in Z_{k}$ and let $\tau$ be a $(k+1)$ -subset of $[n]$ . Then
$\sum_{w\notin\tau}\operatorname{circ}_{\tau,w}(x)^{2}=(n+k+1)\,x_{\tau}^{2}+\sum_{w\notin\tau}D_{w}(x)^{2}.$
Proof.
Since $\varepsilon_{w}^{2}=1$ , squaring and summing the $n-k-1$ terms gives
$\sum_{w\notin\tau}\operatorname{circ}_{\tau,w}(x)^{2}=(n-k-1)x_{\tau}^{2}+2x_{\tau}\sum_{w\notin\tau}\varepsilon_{w}D_{w}(x)+\sum_{w\notin\tau}D_{w}(x)^{2}.$
For each $u\in\tau$ , the facet $\tau\setminus u$ is a $(k-1)$ -face whose completing vertices are $u$ and the vertices $w\notin\tau$ ; hence $\partial_{k}x=0$ at $\tau\setminus u$ reads $\pm x_{\tau}+\sum_{w\notin\tau}(\pm)x_{(\tau\setminus u)\cup w}=0$ . Summing these $k+1$ relations gives $\sum_{w\notin\tau}\varepsilon_{w}D_{w}(x)=(k+1)x_{\tau}$ , so the middle term equals $2(k+1)x_{\tau}^{2}$ . Adding $(n-k-1)x_{\tau}^{2}+2(k+1)x_{\tau}^{2}=(n+k+1)x_{\tau}^{2}$ completes the proof.
∎
Theorem 4.2.
Let $k\geq 1$ and let $G$ be a simplicial complex on $[n]$ . Then
$\lambda_{\max}\big(L_{k}^{\mathrm{up}}(G)\big)\ \leq\ \mu_{k-1}(G)+\frac{1}{k+2}\big(n-\mu_{k-1}(G)\big).$
Proof.
As in Proposition 3.1, $\lambda_{\max}(L_{k}^{\mathrm{up}}(G))=n-\lambda_{\min}(\bar{L}_{k}(G)|_{Z_{k}})$ , so it suffices to prove that $x^{\top}\bar{L}_{k}(G)\,x\geq\frac{k+1}{k+2}\,a_{k}(\bar{G})$ for every $x\in Z_{k}$ with $\|x\|=1$ .
Step 1 (overcounting).
Each missing $(k+1)$ -face $\sigma$ has $\mu(\sigma)\in\{1,\dots,k+2\}$ facets that are missing $k$ -faces. Since $\mu(\sigma)\leq k+2$ ,
$\bar{L}_{k}(G)\ \succeq\ \frac{1}{k+2}\sum_{\sigma}\mu(\sigma)\,v_{\sigma}v_{\sigma}^{\top}=\frac{1}{k+2}\,Q,\qquad Q:=\sum_{\tau}\ \sum_{w\notin\tau}v_{\tau\cup w}v_{\tau\cup w}^{\top},$
the inner sum running over the missing $k$ -faces $\tau$ . By Lemma 4.1,
$x^{\top}Qx=\sum_{\tau}\Big[(n+k+1)x_{\tau}^{2}+\sum_{w\notin\tau}D_{w}(x)^{2}\Big].$
Step 2 (the complement’s connectivity, via signed contraction).
For $w\in[n]$ define $h^{w}\in C_{k-1}$ by $h^{w}_{g}=\varepsilon(w,g)\,x_{g\cup w}$ for $g\not\ni w$ and $h^{w}_{g}=0$ otherwise, where $\varepsilon(w,g)$ is the sign of inserting $w$ into $g$ . Then $\partial_{k-1}h^{w}=0$ follows from $\partial_{k}x=0$ , so $h^{w}\in Z_{k-1}$ . Moreover $D_{w}(x)=\langle\partial_{k}\tau,\,h^{w}\rangle$ when $w\notin\tau$ , while when $w\in\tau$ this pairing equals $\pm x_{\tau}$ ; hence, summing over the missing $k$ -faces $\tau$ ,
$\sum_{w\in[n]}(h^{w})^{\top}L_{k-1}^{\mathrm{up}}(\bar{G})\,h^{w}=\sum_{\tau}\Big[\sum_{w\notin\tau}D_{w}(x)^{2}+(k+1)x_{\tau}^{2}\Big].$
Since each $h^{w}\in Z_{k-1}$ , the left side is at least $a_{k}(\bar{G})\sum_{w}\|h^{w}\|^{2}$ , and $\sum_{w}\|h^{w}\|^{2}=(k+1)\|x\|^{2}=k+1$ , because every $(k+1)$ -subset arises as $g\cup w$ in $k+1$ ways. Therefore
$\sum_{\tau}\sum_{w\notin\tau}D_{w}(x)^{2}\ \geq\ (k+1)\,a_{k}(\bar{G})-(k+1)\sum_{\tau}x_{\tau}^{2}.$
Step 3 (combine).
Substituting into the expression for $x^{\top}Qx$ ,
$x^{\top}Qx\ \geq\ (n+k+1)\sum_{\tau}x_{\tau}^{2}+(k+1)a_{k}(\bar{G})-(k+1)\sum_{\tau}x_{\tau}^{2}=n\sum_{\tau}x_{\tau}^{2}+(k+1)a_{k}(\bar{G}),$
which is at least $(k+1)a_{k}(\bar{G})$ .
Hence $x^{\top}\bar{L}_{k}(G)x\geq\frac{1}{k+2}x^{\top}Qx\geq\frac{k+1}{k+2}a_{k}(\bar{G})$ , and
$\lambda_{\max}(L_{k}^{\mathrm{up}})=n-\lambda_{\min}(\bar{L}_{k}|_{Z_{k}})\leq n-\tfrac{k+1}{k+2}a_{k}(\bar{G})=\mu_{k-1}+\tfrac{1}{k+2}\big(n-\mu_{k-1}\big).\qed$
When $k=1$ , Theorem 4.2 reduces to Theorem 1.1, with $a_{1}(\bar{G})=n-\mu_{1}(G)=a(\overline{G})$ the algebraic connectivity of the complement graph. The divergence-free identity and the bound thus extend to every dimension, with $\tfrac{1}{3}$ replaced by $\tfrac{1}{k+2}$ .
5 Open Questions
In this section, we record a reformulation of Problem 5.5, the obstruction that keeps the present method from settling it, and the higher-dimensional conjecture.
Proposition 3.1 recasts inequality (2) as the spectral inequality
$\lambda_{\min}\!\big(\bar{L}(G)\big|_{Z_{1}}\big)\ \geq\ a(\overline{G}),$
(10)
between the up Laplacian of the missing triangles of $G$ , restricted to the cycle space, and the algebraic connectivity of the complement. Theorem 1.1 proves the weaker bound $\lambda_{\min}(\bar{L}|_{Z_{1}})\geq\tfrac{2}{3}\,a(\overline{G})$ . Note that the right-hand side $a(\overline{G})=n-\mu_{1}(G)$ vanishes exactly when $G$ is a join, so (10) carries content only when $\mu_{1}(G)<n$ ; this is where the factor $\tfrac{2}{3}$ falls short of the conjectured $1$ .
We next explain where the constant $\tfrac{1}{3}$ comes from and why this method cannot remove it. The only inequality in the proof of Theorem 1.1 is the comparison $\bar{L}(G)\succeq\tfrac{1}{3}\,Q$ in Step 1, where
$Q:=\sum_{e\in E(\overline{G})}\ \sum_{w\notin e}v_{\,e\cup w}v_{\,e\cup w}^{\top}=\sum_{S}\mu(S)\,v_{S}v_{S}^{\top}$
weights each missing triangle $S$ by its number $\mu(S)$ of missing edges. Steps 2 and 3 in fact establish the sharp bound $Q|_{Z_{1}}\succeq 2\,a(\overline{G})I$ , attained for instance when $\overline{G}$ is a star $K_{1,n-1}$ , that is, when $G=K_{n-1}\cup K_{1}$ (Figure 1). Hence the route through the missing-edge identity yields exactly $\lambda_{\min}(\bar{L}|_{Z_{1}})\geq\tfrac{1}{3}\cdot 2\,a(\overline{G})=\tfrac{2}{3}\,a(\overline{G})$ and no more: reaching (10) would require $Q|_{Z_{1}}\succeq 3\,a(\overline{G})I$ , which is false in general.
(a) $G=K_{5}\cup K_{1}$
(b) $\overline{G}=K_{1,5}$
Figure 1: The extremal configuration for $Q|_{Z_{1}}\succeq 2\,a(\overline{G})I$ at $n=6$ : equality holds for $G=K_{n-1}\cup K_{1}$ , whose complement is the star $K_{1,n-1}$ . The same six vertices carry the complementary edge sets, with the centre isolated in $G$ and the hub in $\overline{G}$ .
The graph of Figure 1 also shows that the loss resides entirely in this weighting. For $G=K_{n-1}\cup K_{1}$ one has $\lambda_{\max}(L_{1}^{\mathrm{up}})=\mu_{1}(G)=n-1$ , so (2) is tight and (10) holds with equality, $\lambda_{\min}(\bar{L}|_{Z_{1}})=a(\overline{G})=1$ . Yet every missing triangle—each through the isolated vertex—has exactly two missing edges, so $Q=2\bar{L}$ and the comparison of Step 1 reads $\bar{L}\succeq\tfrac{1}{3}Q=\tfrac{2}{3}\bar{L}$ . The method therefore recovers precisely two-thirds of the truth, the deficit being exactly the $\tfrac{1}{3}(n-\mu_{1})$ excess of Theorem 1.1. Thus the constant $\tfrac{1}{3}$ is forced not by the cycle-space estimate, which is sharp, but by the uniform $\mu(S)$ -weighting; any improvement must give the triangles missing two or three edges—the dense part of $\overline{G}$ —more than their uniform share. This is the content of the following question.
Question 5.1.
Is there a decomposition of $\bar{L}(G)$ that accounts exactly for the triangles missing two or three edges, that is, for the dense part of $\overline{G}$ , rather than the uniform $\mu(S)$ -weighting underlying Lemma 3.2, and that settles the reformulation (10)?
Finally, we record the higher-dimensional comparison behind (3).
Conjecture 5.2.
For every finite simplicial complex and every $k\geq 1$ , we have $\sigma_{\max}(\partial_{k+1})\leq\sigma_{\max}(\partial_{k})$ ; equivalently, $\lambda_{\max}(L_{k}^{\mathrm{up}})$ is non-increasing in $k$ .
The graph case of Conjecture 5.2 is (2). Theorem 4.2 settles it up to the same loss as in dimension one: for every $k$ and every complex,
$\lambda_{\max}(L_{k}^{\mathrm{up}})\leq\mu_{k-1}+\tfrac{1}{k+2}\big(n-\mu_{k-1}\big),$
and the obstruction is dimension-uniform. Indeed, Steps 2 and 3 of its proof give the sharp bound $Q|_{Z_{k}}\succeq(k+1)\,a_{k}(\bar{G})\,I$ (the case $k=1$ is the $2\,a(\overline{G})$ bound above), whereas Conjecture 5.2 would require $Q|_{Z_{k}}\succeq(k+2)\,a_{k}(\bar{G})\,I$ , false already at $k=1$ . Closing the remaining gap in any single dimension—equivalently, the dense-part decomposition asked for in Question 5.1—thus appears to be the crux in all of them.
