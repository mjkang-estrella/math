                                                                                                   Abstract
                                                       In 2019, Sivaraman conjectured that every Pk -free graph has cop number at most k − 3 for
                                                   k ≥ 5. Chudnovsky, Norin, Seymour, and Turcotte proved P5 -free graphs have cop number
                                                   at most 2. For k ≥ 6 Sivaraman’s conjecture remains widely open. There is little evidence to
                                                   believe Sivaraman’s conjecture is optimal, making the discovery of substantially better bounds
                                                   interesting, even for subclasses of Pk -free graphs. We consider this problem for graphs with
                                                   no long holes, which generalizes Pk -free graphs, that also have some forbidden shorter holes.
                                                   Specifically, we show that for t ≥ 13, (C4 , . . . , Ct , Ck , Ck+1 , . . . )-free graphs have cop number at
                                                   most ⌈ k−2
                                                           ⌊ 4t ⌋
                                                                  ⌉ + 1. Taking Ck−1 , which has cop number 2 whenever k ≥ 5, demonstrates that this
                                                   bound is tight up to a small constant factor for infinity many pairs k and t. The proof relies on
                                                   showing that in graphs without short holes, the closed neighbourhood of a short induced path
                                                   can be guarded by 1 cop, even when these paths are not geodesics. Additionally, for t ≥ 2 we
                                                   show that (Ct+2 , Ct+3 , Ck , Ck+1 , . . . , )-free graphs have cop number at most (t − 1)⌈ (k−2)  t ⌉. This
                                                   show that (C4 , C5 , Ck , Ck+1 , . . . )-free graphs have cop number at most ⌈ k−2       2 ⌉, extending work
                                                   by Clow and Meger on (Pk , claw, butterfly, C4 , C5 )-free graphs to a substantially more general
                                                   class.


                                           1       Introduction
                                           Cops and Robbers is a two-player game played on a connected graph, see [1, 23, 26]. To begin
                                           the game, the cop player places k cops onto vertices of the graph, then the robber player chooses
                                           a vertex to place the robber. Players take turns moving. During the cop player’s turn, each cop
                                           either moves to an adjacent vertex or passes and remains at their current vertex. Similarly, on
                                           the robber player’s turn, the robber either moves to an adjacent vertex or passes and remains at
                                           their current vertex. The cop player wins if after finitely many moves a cop can move onto the
                                           vertex occupied by the robber, called capturing. The robber player wins if the robber can provide
                                           a strategy to evade capture indefinitely. The least number of cops required for the cop player to
                                           win, regardless of the robber’s strategy, is called the cop number of a graph, denoted c(G) for a
                                           graph G. If c(G) ≤ k, then we say G is k-cop win. We suppose all graphs are connected, unless
                                           stated otherwise. For more background on Cops and Robbers we recommend [5].
                                               We define the graphs Pt , Ct , and Kt as the path with t vertices, the cycle with t vertices, and
                                           the complete graph on t vertices, respectively. We call the complete bipartite graph K1,3 the claw.
                                           The E graph is given by subdividing two edges of the claw. If G and H are graphs, then we denote

    It is standard to consider classes of graphs defined by forbidden substructures such as minors
or induced subgraphs. A graph G is H-free or H-minor free if G does not contain, respectively,
any induced subgraph or minor which is isomorphic to H. Of particular importance for this paper
will be the class of Pk -free graphs. If H is a (finite or infinite list) list of graphs, we say G is
H-free if G has no graph in H as an induced subgraph. At various points in the paper we will take
{Ck , Ck+1 , . . . } to mean the set of all cycles with length at least k. Notice that if G is Pk−1 -free,
then G is also (Ck , Ck+1 , . . . )-free. For k ≥ 4, a hole of length k in G is an induced copy of Ck in
G.
    Cops and Robbers has been studied extensively in relation to forbidden minors and forbidden
induced subgraphs. Andreae [2] showed that for all graphs H, there exists a constant m = m(H)
such that if G has no H-minor, then c(G) ≤ m. The constant m = m(H) here was recently
improved by Kenter, Meger, and Turcotte [18], particularly for small or sparse graphs H. When
forbidding an induced subgraph H, or a subgraph H, one cannot guarantee the cop number of
H-free graphs is bounded. In fact, it was shown by Joret, Kamiński, and Theis [17] that H-free
graphs have bounded cop number if and only if H is a linear forest (i.e a disjoint union of paths).
    In [17] Joret, Kamiński, and Theis demonstrated that every connected Pk -free graph is (k − 2)-
cop win. This led Sivaraman [27] to give a different and simpler proof that every connected Pk -free
graph is (k − 2)-cop win. This simpler proof involves the well known Gyárfás path argument,
and has the added benefit of bounding the number of turns it takes the cops to catch the robber.
Sivaraman generalized this in [28] to prove that (Ck , Ck+1 , . . . )-free graphs have cop number at
most k − 3. This improved an earlier theorem by Kosowski, Li, Nisse, and Suchan [19] who showed
k − 1 cops are sufficient.
    When k is large there is no evidence that k − 2 is the optimal upper bound for the cop number
of Pk -free graphs. This lead Sivaraman to the following conjecture. Notice that k ≥ 5 is required
here because C4 is a P4 -free graph with cop number 2.

Conjecture 1.1 (Sivaraman [27]). For all k ≥ 5, if G is Pk -free, then c(G) ≤ k − 3.

    This conjecture has seen a significant amount of attention, most of which has been focused on
the P5 -free case. Initially many authors, see [14, 21, 29, 30], solved various subcases of the P5 -free
case. The general P5 -free conjecture was proven by Chudnovsky, Norin, Seymour, and Turcotte in
[8]. For several subclasses Pk -free classes, such as (Pk , claw)-free graphs and (Pk , Ct )-free graphs
where t ≤ k, Liu [21] showed k − 3 cops are sufficient. Some of these results were significantly
improved by Clow  l and m Meger in [10]. For example, [10] showed that (Pk , E)-free graphs have cop
                    k−1
number at most 2 + 3. During the preparation of this manuscript the additive constant 3 was
removed from this bound by Gupta, Pradhan, and Swain [15] in the special case of (Pk , claw)-free
graphs. Notice that the class of (Pk , claw)-free graphs is a strict subset of the class of (Pk , E)-free
graphs.
    Much less attention has been payed to demonstrating the existence of Pk -free graphs with large
cop number. This is part of a general trend in the literature where there are not many known
techniques to lower bound cop number. The techniques that are known mostly apply to graphs
with large girth, see for instance [1, 7, 13], or in some cases graphs forbidding small subgraphs such
as C4 , see [3]. Neither of these restrictions is very amenable to constructing interesting Pk -free
graphs.

     The best and, to the author’s knowledge, only example of a Pk -free graph in the literature with
large cop number comes from Clow and Zaguia [11]. They demonstrate, for all k, the existence
of graphs with cop number and clique cover number k. Any graph with clique cover number
k is trivially P2k+1 -free. These graphs are constructed randomly, and this work was motivated
by a problem proposed by Turcotte in [30] which asked if for all k there exists graphs G with
k = c(G) = α(G). Note that the cop number of random graphs have been studied in other
situations, see for instance [22, 24, 25]. Because of the random nature of the construction in [11]
these results do not provide lower bounds for (Pk , H)-free graphs.
     We contribute to this literature by studying the cop number of graphs with no long holes that
have a further restriction on which short holes they admit. Recalling that graphs with no long hole
generalize Pk -free graphs this second restriction is required, otherwise this work would have direct
implications to proving Conjecture 1.1. We also note that this direction of study is motivated for
reasons outside of Conjecture 1.1. For instance, graphs with no holes (i.e. chordal graphs) are
well known to be cop win. Meanwhile, graphs with no even holes and no claw have been shown to
have cop number at most 2 [16]. Moreover, Clow and Zaguia [11] proved that any graphs with cop
number and clique cover number k have a hole of length t for all 4 ≤ t ≤ k + 1. The latter of which
has implications to the cop number of perfect graphs.
     We start by providing a bound on the cop number of graphs with no long hole and no short
hole. This result is primarily interesting because, using Liu’s work in [21], one can only show that
forbidding t holes as induced subgraphs will allow k − 3 − t cops to win. Whereas we show that by
forbidding the shortest t holes O( kt ) cops suffice.
     Beyond simply demonstrating this bound, the proof may be of independent interest. Perhaps
even more interest than the bound itself. Like many Cops and Robbers proof, the argument proceeds
by showing that the cops can monotonically expand their territory by guarding subgraphs. However,
unlike the standard version of this argument, where cops guard shortest paths, we use cops to guard
the closed neighbourhood of arbitrary short induced paths. To the author’s knowledge this is the
first argument of its kind.

2    Graphs with no Short Holes
The goal of this section is to prove Theorem 1.2. Our argument adopts a common motif in Cops
and Robbers by having cops guard certain subgraphs as part of the cop’s strategy to monotonically
reduce the robber’s territory. However, unlike most arguments of this kind, we will not have cops
guard geodesic paths. We provide a more nuanced discussion of how these elements of the proof
are similar and distinct from standard methods later in this section.
    We start by noting some facts regarding how vertices in the open neighbourhood of an induced
path can be connected by short paths in the rest of the graph. We will leverage these facts later,
specifically in Lemma 2.3, to show that 1-guard can protect the closed neighbourhood of a short
induced path even if that path is not geodesic.
    In order to state our results we must introduce some non-standard notation regarding distances.
Given a graph G and an induced subgraph H of G, let distH (x, y) be the length of the shortest (x, y)-
path in G all of whose internal vertices and edges are in H. Thus, if x, y are both in V (G) \ V (H),
then distH (x, y) > 1. Notice this does not require x or y to be vertices in H, whereas one would
normally require this.

order ℓ < t − 1, and let H be G − N [P ]. If x, y ∈ N (P ) are non-adjacent, then distH (x, y) ≥ t − ℓ.
Moreover, if x, y ∈ N (P ) are adjacent, then distH (x, y) ≥ t or distH (x, y) = 2.

    As has already been mentioned, we will not guard paths in the standard manner originally
introduced in [1]. The standard method involved 1 cop protecting a shortest path, and has been
widely applied, see for instance [2, 13, 18]. It is well known that a shortest path is always 1-
guardable regardless of the host graph. A subgraph H in G is guarded by a set of cops, if the cops
can from their current position, move in such a way that if the robber ever enters H, then the cops
will capture the robber on their next turn.
    Path guarding arguments have been generalized in various ways. For example [12] characterizes
all geodesic subgraphs which are 1-guardable, regardless of their host graph. Notice that if one
wants a subgraph to be guardable regardless of which graph it is hosted in, then assuming the
subgraph is geodesic is necessary. Here a subgraph is geodesic if the distances in the subgraph
are preserved in the host graph. Meanwhile other work, see for example [9, 12, 20], explores how
this kind of strategy can be extended to protect paths in settings where the cop’s movement is
constrained. Some quite recent work from [6] has also provided machinery for efficiently guarding
sets of vertices with small radius.
    In Lemma 2.3 we take a different approach to generalizing these ideas. Specifically, rather than
constraining the cops or discussing subgraphs other than paths we relax the assumption that our
path is geodesic by restricting the structure of the host graph. By doing so we will be able to
guard paths, in fact the closed neighbourhood of paths, which are not necessarily geodesic. This
grants a massive advantage when working with dense graphs, noting of course that graphs with
forbidden induced subgraphs tend to be denser than many of the graphs normally considers in a
path guarding argument.

3    Graphs with two Consecutive Forbidden Holes
In this section we turn our attention from graphs with no short holes to graphs that have two
consecutive forbidden holes. Such graph classes are necessarily much larger. In exchange for this

                                                        18
increased generality we achieve weaker bounds when t is large. It is unclear if the upper bounds we
prove are tight for any Pk -free graphs, even those with no restriction on holes, for t ≥ 2. Compared
to the results in Section 2, these arguments are simpler.

Lemma 3.1. Let t ≥ 2 be an integer and G a (Ct+2 , Ct+3 )-free graph. If P = v1 . . . vt is an induced
path in G, for all i ∈ {1, . . . , t − 1} there is a cop C i on vi , the robber is occupying a vertex in
V (G) \ N [P \ {vt }], and it is the cop’s turn, then C 1 , . . . , C t−1 are in a position to guard N [P ] in
all subsequent turns.

Proof. Let t ≥ 2 and let G be a (Ct+2 , Ct+3 )-free graph. Define P as in the statement and assume
that for all i ∈ {1, . . . , t − 1} there is a cop C i on vi . Suppose the robber is occupying a vertex in
V (G) \ N [P \ {vt }].
    For all vertices x ∈ V (G)\N [P ] let f (x) = −1 if (N (x)∩N (v1 ))\N (P \{v1 }) ̸= ∅, let f (x) = 1 if
(N (x)∩N (vt ))\N (P \{vt }) ̸= ∅, and let f (x) = 0 otherwise. That is, f (x) = −1 if x has a common
neighbour with v1 that is private with with respect to P . With f (x) = 1 defined analogously with
vt replacing v1 . Meanwhile, f (x) = 0 captures all remaining cases.
    We claim f is well defined. Given the definition of f it is sufficient to prove that there exists
no path v1 uxwvt where x ∈        / N [P ] and u ∈ / N (P \ {v1 }) and w ∈
                                                                         / N (P \ {vt }). Suppose not for
contradiction.
    Then there exists a vertex x ∈ V (G) \ N [P ] adjacent to vertices u ∈ N (v1 ) and w ∈ N (vt ) such
that u ∈ / N [P \ {v1 }] and w ∈    / N [P \ {vt }]. Observe that u and w must be distinct. Also observe
that P wxu is a cycle of length t+3 whose only possible chordal is the edge (u, w). If (u, w) ∈ E(G),
then P wu is a hole of length t + 2, a contradiction. If (u, w) ∈    / E(G), then P wxu is a hole of length
t + 3, also a contradiction. We conclude that f is well defined.
    Now the cops C 1 , . . . , C t−1 guard N [P ] as follows. Recall it is the cop’s turn to move. Let
r ∈ V (G) \ N [P ] be the current location of the robber. If f (r) = 0 or f (r) = −1, then the cops do
nothing. The robber cannot enter N [P ] on their next turn without being captured since, f (r) = 0
or f (r) = −1 implies that no vertex N (r) ∩ N (P ) is a private neighbour of vt . Since all other
vertices of P contain a cop, the robber cannot enter N [P ]. Otherwise f (r) = 1, implying no vertex
N (r) ∩ N (P ) is a private neighbour of v1 . In this case, all cops C i slide one vertex to the right in
P , leaving a cop C i on vertex vi+1 for all i. The robber cannot move into N (P ) without moving
next to a cop and being caught.
    After this move the cops are either in the same position or a symmetric position to where they
started and can repeat this strategy indefinitely in the obvious way. Thus, the cops can guard N [P ]
from this starting positions as claimed. This completes the proof that if the robber begins outside
N [P ], then the cops can guard N [P ] for all subsequent turns.
    Suppose then that the robber occupies vertex r ∈ N (vt ) \ N [P \ {vt }]. Then all cops slide to the
right, as if f (r) = 1. This forces the robber to move to a new vertex R. If R ∈ N [P \ {v1 }], then
there is a cop adjacent to R and the robber is captured. If R ∈ V (G)\N [P ], then from this position

4    Future Work
To conclude the paper it feels valuable to add some conjectures. Given the oddness of the current
time these conjectures may be quickly solved. However, they still seem to still be worth stating,
because they seem challenging, and their solutions may prove informative. Also, they will hopefully
serve the purpose of making others interested in this area.

Conjecture 4.1. There exists an integer N such that for all k ≥ t ≥ 4, every (C4 , . . . , Ct , Ck , Ck+1,..., )-
free graph G has cop number
                                                2k
                                        c(G) ≤     + N.
                                                 t
    It is well established, see for instance [4, 17], that cop number is upper bounded by treewidth.
More strongly, one can easily observe that the cop number is upper bounded by a function of
the maximum domination number of a bag in a tree-decomposition, minimized over all tree-
decompositions. To see this, suppose the min-maxed domination number here is k. Use k cops
to dominate a bag, then move k other cops to dominate the next bag closest to the robber. By
repeatedly doing this, with the two sets of k cops switching roles, the cops will win.
    Inspired by a question I was asked recently during research travel of the form ‘what is the nicest
thing you could imagine being true of cop number and induced subgraphs?’ it is worth proposing
the following ‘meta conjecture’. Notice it might be true that hereditary class is too strong a notion
here. If that is the case, then it would be interesting to understand if there is a natural, less general,
notion of graph class where the conjecture is true.

Conjecture 4.2. Let H be a hereditary class of graphs. If for all t the subset of H that is (Kt , Kt,t )-
free has bounded treewidth, then H has bounded cop number.

    The following question is stated to motivate further interest in the problem, and because if
correct it would be optimal up to additive constants. Should such graphs exist their structure would
be informative, and if they do not exist, then the proof of such a result would likely be interesting.
Also note that if such graphs exist, they would serve as counterexamples to Conjecture 4.1.

Question 4.3. Does there exists an integer N such that for all k, there exists a (C4 , C5 , Ck , Ck+1 , . . . )-
free graph with cop number at least k2 − N ?

    To conclude it seems worth formally stating a conjecture that was gestured at in [10]. As with
many things in this paper, the conjecture is made about graphs with no long hole rather than
Pk -free graphs. As has been mentioned a number of times, if true, then graphs in [11] demonstrates
this is tight up to the choice of N .

Conjecture 4.4. There exists an integer N such that for all k, if G is a (Ck , Ck+1 , . . . )-free graph,
then c(G) ≤ k2 + N .
