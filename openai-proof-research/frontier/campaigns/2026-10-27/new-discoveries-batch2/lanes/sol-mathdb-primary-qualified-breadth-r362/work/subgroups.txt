                                                      COUNTING CONJUGACY CLASSES OF SUBGROUPS OF PSL2 (p)




arXiv:2411.02219v1 [math.GR] 4 Nov 2024
                                                                                          GARETH A. JONES

                                                  Abstract. We obtain formulae for the numbers of isomorphism and conjugacy classes of non-
                                                  identity proper subgroups of the groups G = PSL2 (p), p prime, and for the numbers of those
                                                  conjugacy classes which do or do not consist of self-normalising subgroups. The formulae are
                                                  used to prove lower bounds 17, 18, 6 and 12 respectively satisfied by these invariants for all p > 37.
                                                  A computer search carried out for a different problem shows that these bounds are attained for over
                                                  a million primes p; we show that if the Bateman–Horn Conjecture is true, they are attained for in-
                                                  finitely many primes. Also, assuming no unproved conjectures, we use a result of Heath-Brown to
                                                  obtain upper bounds for these invariants, valid for an infinite set of primes p.



                                                                                          1. Introduction
                                             The aim of this note is to consider the following invariants of a finite group G:
                                               (i) i(G), the number of isomorphism classes of non-identity proper subgroups H of G;
                                              (ii) c(G), the number of conjugacy classes of such subgroups H of G;
                                             (iii) s(G), the number of conjugacy classes of such self-normalising subgroups H of G;
                                             (iv) n(G), the number of conjugacy classes of such non-self-normalising subgroups H of G.
                                          A subgroup H ≤ G is self-normalising if H coincides with its normaliser NG (H). The motivation
                                          for this paper was a seminar by Mark Lewis [11] on n(G); this was mainly about solvable groups,
                                          but it raised a question about non-abelian finite simple groups, answered in Theorems 1.3 and 1.4.
                                             Using Dickson’s description [6, Ch. XII] of the subgroups of PSL2 (q), where q is a prime power
                                          (see also [8, §II.8]) we will give formulae for these invariants for the groups G = PSL2 (p) (p
                                          prime). As a consequence, we will prove the following:
                                          Theorem 1.1. The groups G = PSL2 (p), p prime, satisfy
                                              (i) i(G) ≥ 17 for all p ≥ 29;
                                             (ii) c(G) ≥ 18 for all p ≥ 23;
                                            (iii) s(G) ≥ 6 for all p ≥ 41;
                                            (iv) n(G) ≥ 12 for all p ≥ 23.
                                             The exceptions for small p can be seen in Table 1, which lists the values of these invariants
                                          for primes p = 3, . . . , 61. A computer search reported in [9], in relation to a different problem
                                          concerning these groups G, found a set of more than 1.2 × 106 primes p for which all four lower
                                          bounds in Theorem 1.1 are attained. In fact, we make the following conjecture:

Conjecture 1.2. There is an infinite set of primes p for which the groups G = PSL2 (p) satisfy
i(G) = 17, c(G) = 18, s(G) = 6 and n(G) = 12.
   A proof of this conjecture would depend on a certain triple of polynomials in Z[t] simultaneously
taking prime values for infinitely many t ∈ N. This sort of problem is the subject of the Bateman–
Horn Conjecture (BHC), which gives estimates for the number of t ≤ x, for large x, giving prime
values to a given finite set of polynomials. In all known applications these estimates agree closely
with data from computer searches, so there is strong evidence (but at present no proof) that the
BHC is true. Our second main result is the following:
Theorem 1.3. If the BHC is true then there is an infinite set of primes p for which the lower bounds
17, 18, 6 and 12 in Theorem 1.1 are attained.
  In the absence of the BHC and any other unproved conjectures, we are able to use a result of
Heath-Brown [4] to prove the following:
Theorem 1.4. There is an infinite set of primes p for which the groups G = PSL2 (p) satisfy
    (i) i(G) ≤ 390;
   (ii) c(G) ≤ 454;
  (iii) s(G) ≤ 132;
  (iv) n(G) ≤ 384.
   The existence of an infinite set of non-abelian finite simple groups G for which n(G) is bounded
above answers a question raised by Lewis in [11]. The large gaps between the lower and upper
bounds in Theorems 1.1 and 1.4 suggest that there is further work to be done in reducing these
upper bounds without assuming the BHC. In the meantime, work is in progress on an analogue of
Theorem 1.1 for the groups PSL2 (q) where q is any prime power; the powers of primes p ≥ 5 are
straightforward, whereas the cases p = 2 and 3 raise further interesting number-theoretic problems.

                                 3. Lower bounds for the invariants
   Our aim now is to find primes p for which the invariants considered above for the groups G =
PSL2 (p) take the lowest possible values. In order to obtain uniform bounds we will assume that
p > 37; the smaller primes can be dealt with individually, as in the preceding example (see Table 1).
   We start with i(G), given by (1). The first step is to ensure that σ = α = 0 by restricting
attention to primes p ≡ ±3 or ±13 mod (40); Dirichlet’s Theorem guarantees an infinite set of
primes satisfying any one of these four congruences. It now remains to find a good upper bound
on 2δ + 3ε, and this can be done by minimising the numbers of primes dividing (p ± 1)/2. These
two consecutive integers are coprime, one of them must be divisible by 2, and one of them by 3,
giving four possibilities for allocating these prime factors. They are as follows, with inequalities
needed to produce primes p > 37:
   (a) (p + 1)/2 = 3s and (p − 1)/2 = 2r coprime, with s ≥ 7 and r ≥ 10,
   (b) (p + 1)/2 = 2s and (p − 1)/2 = 3r coprime, with s ≥ 10 and r ≥ 7,
   (c) (p + 1)/2 = 6s and (p − 1)/2 = r coprime, with s ≥ 4 and r ≥ 19,
   (d) (p + 1)/2 = s and (p − 1)/2 = 6r coprime, with s ≥ 20 and r ≥ 4,
where |G| = 12psr in all cases. (Remarkably, these four cases also arose recently in [9], which
addressed the problem of constructing a possibly infinite set of finite simple groups of order a
product of six primes.)

                                        4. Attaining the bounds
  We have seen instances of groups G which attain the four lower bounds obtained in the preceding
section, namely all the groups in cases (a) and (b) involving triples of primes p, r and s. The
smallest such group in case (a) is PSL2 (29); however, this has α = 1 since its order is divisible by
s = 5, so it does not satisfy all the bounds. The next example in case (a) is PSL2 (173), with r = 43
and s = 29, and this has α = 0, as required. The first example in case (b) is PSL2 (43), with r = 7
and s = 11. In fact, in each of cases (a) to (d) the computer searches reported in [9] found over
600, 000 instances of suitable triples of primes, all but a few having σ = α = 0, so that those in
cases (a) and (b) attain equality in our bounds.
  Ideally, we would like infinite sets of examples attaining these bounds. In case (a) the equations

                                 5. The Bateman–Horn Conjecture
   Given a finite set of distinct polynomials fi (t) ∈ Z[t] (i = 1, . . . , k), it is of interest to know
whether they simultaneously take prime values for infinitely many t ∈ N. Instances of this general
problem include the following:
      • the twin primes problem, with f1 (t) = t and f2 (t) = t + 2;
      • the Sophie Germain primes problem, with f1 (t) = t and f2 (t) = 2t + 1;
      • the Euler–Landau problem, with a single polynomial f1 (t) = t2 + 1.
The following are obvious necessary conditions on the polynomials fi (t):
      • they all have positive leading coefficients;
      • they are all irreducible;
      • their product f (t) = f1 (t) . . . fk (t) is not identically zero modulo any prime.
Schinzel’s Hypothesis [12], which we will abbreviate to SH, asserts that these conditions are also
sufficient. This generalises earlier conjectures by Bunyakovsky [3] for k = 1 and by Dickson [5]
where deg fi = 1 for all i. It has been proved only in the simplest case, where k = 1 and deg f1 = 1:
this is Dirichlet’s Theorem on primes in an arithmetic progression at + b.

   One can regard the integral in (11) as a first attempt at an estimate for Q(x), based on applying
the Prime Number Theorem to the polynomials fi (t), on the (incorrect) assumption that they take
values independently. The constant C in (12) can then be regarded as a product of correction
factors, one for each prime p, replacing the probability (1 − 1p )k that k randomly and independently
chosen elements of Z p are all non-zero with the proportion of Z p where f (t) , 0.
   When the three conditions of SH are satisfied the infinite product in (12) converges to a limit
C > 0. Convergence is slow, but good approximations can be found by taking partial products over
large initial segments of the primes. On a laptop this can take several hours, but by contrast Maple
can accurately evaluate the integral in (11) almost instantly, by numerical integration.
   Like SH, the BHC has been proved only in the simplest case, where k = 1 and deg f1 = 1.
This is de la Vallée-Poussin’s quantified form of Dirichlet’s Theorem, that the primes at + b are
asymptotically evenly distributed among the ϕ(a) congruence classes of units mod (a).
   If the conditions of SH are satisfied then E(x) → +∞ as x → ∞. The BHC asserts that Q(x) ∼
E(x) as x → ∞, so if the BHC is correct then Q(x) → +∞ also, and hence there are infinitely many
t ∈ N for which each fi (t) is prime. Thus the BHC implies SH. The fact that the BHC has given
impressively accurate estimates in all known applications is strong evidence that it is true, but at
present there seems to be little prospect of a proof.
   There is an interesting account in [1] of the history of the BHC, including a proof of the con-
vergence of the product (12). There is a shorter discussion of the BHC in [9], including several
applications to other problems in group theory, some involving the groups PSL2 (p).
   Returning to our specific problem, the polynomials fi (t) in case (a), shown in (9), satisfy the
common conditions of SH and the BHC, as do those in case (b) shown in (10), so if the BHC is
true then in either case there are infinitely many t ∈ N with each fi (t) prime, as required. This gives
a proof of Theorem 1.3 and hence (assuming the BHC) of Conjecture 1.2.
