                                          Permutations minimizing the number of collinear triples
                                                                        Joshua Cooper and Jack Hyatt
                                                                                  January 7, 2025




arXiv:2501.02331v1 [math.CO] 4 Jan 2025
                                                                                       Abstract
                                                    We characterize the permutations of Fq whose graph minimizes the number of
                                                collinear triples and describe the lexicographically-least one, affirming a conjecture
                                                of Cooper-Solymosi. This question is closely connected to Dudeney’s No-3-in-a-Line
                                                problem, the Heilbronn triangle problem, and the structure of finite plane Kakeya sets.
                                                We discuss a connection with complete sets of mutually orthogonal latin squares and
                                                state a few open problems primarily about general finite affine planes.


                                          1     Introduction
                                          The classic open “no 3 in a line” problem of Dudeney (1917) asks: what is the maximum
                                          number of points that can be placed on an n × n grid so that there are no three collinear
                                          points? The answer is certainly no more than 2n, since any 2n + 1 points contain at least
                                          3 points on at least    √ one of the n vertical lines. Even though it is conjectured that, in fact,
                                          no more than π/ 3n ≈ 1.814n points can be placed without any collinear triples ( [4],
                                          corrected by [11]), not even 2n − 1 is known. This problem is closely connected to the
                                          classical Heilbronn triangle problem, and Erdős gave a lower bound of n − o(n) (cf. [12]),
                                          later improved to 3n/2−o(n) in [5]. It is also closely related to the finite field Kakeya problem
                                          (cf. [1]), as employed in [8]: What is the fewest number of points in a subset of the affine
                                          plane containing a line with every slope? It is straightforward to see that any collinear-triple-
                                          free set with 2n points can be partitioned into the graphs of two permutations. Thus, [2]
                                          studied collinearities in permutations – discussed below, after a few definitions.
                                               Throughout the paper, we use notation that Fq is the finite field with order a prime power,
                                          q; at times we suppress the subscript and simply write F. Given a function f : F → F, the
                                          graph of f is the set {(x, f (x)) : x ∈ F}. The affine geometry over F of dimension d, denoted
                                          AG(q, d), consists of the points Fd and all lines of the form {v + tw : t ∈ F} where v, w ∈ Fd
                                          and w is not the all-zero vector 0̂. The projective geometry P G(q, d) over F of dimension d
                                          has point set (Fd+1 \ {0̂})/ ∼, where ∼ is the equivalence relation defined by: v ∼ w iff there
                                          exists λ ∈ F× so that w = λv; the lines ℓ of P G(q, d) are obtained from each 2-dimensional
                                          subspace P ⊆ AG(q, d) by taking ℓ = (P \ {0̂})/ ∼. The ∼-equivalence class of the points
                                          (x1 , . . . , xd+1 ) ∈ AG(q, d) is denoted [x1 : . . . : xd+1 ]. We take d = 2 throughout the sequel –
                                          i.e., affine and projective planes – and note that the graph of a function can be considered
                                          a subset of AG(q, 2).

    A conic in P G(q, 2) is the solution set to a homogeneous polynomial p(x, y, z) of degree
2. If p 6≡ 0, then we say that p is “nondenegerate”, and the conic is said to be “irreducible”

Corollary 0.1. Ω is a nondegenerate irreducible conic with an external point.

    Let us then rewrite Ω = {[x : y : z] : ax2 + by 2 + cxy + dxz + eyz + f z 2 = 0, (x, y, z) 6=

that the q + 1 points that are not the external point will define the conic. Thus, q + 1
points determine the 6 coefficients of the conic’s polynomial p. Only 5 points are needed,
however, since the homogeneity of p entails only 5 degrees of freedom, and the irreducibility
of p implies that it is uniquely determined by these 5 points. (See, for example, [3].) As long
as q ≥ 5, any two nondegenerate conics obtained from q + 1 points of Ω will share at least 5
points, as the only two points to differ will be the two excluded points. This means they are
the same conic. So there is exactly one nondegenerate conic obtained from the point set Ω,
and the external point is the unique point not on this conic. Shortly, we determine which of
the q + 2 elements of Ω is the external point.
    Now we remove [1 : 0 : 0] and [0 : 1 : 0] and map back to the affine plane by ρ−1 : [x :
y : 1] 7→ (x, y). As the only thing to happen in this process was adding and removing the

possibilities for the image of Γ under ρ, restriction to z 6= 0, and then ρ−1 .

3     Conclusion
It is worth noting that, although [2] concerned itself primarily with permutations of Zp for
p prime, some of the results therein apply to more general geometries than just finite 2-
dimensional vector spaces of odd prime order. In particular, we generalize here the short
proof that every graph of a permutation of Zp admits three collinear points, so that it applies
to any finite affine plane. Given an affine plane (P, L) and two parallel classes H and V of
its lines, define a “generalized permutation” to be a set S ⊂ P which is a transversal to both
H and V , i.e., so that, for each ℓ ∈ H ∪ V , there exists exactly one p ∈ S so that p ∈ ℓ.

Theorem 4. Every generalized permutation in an affine plane of odd order q admits a
collinear triple.

Proof. Let {L1 , . . . , Lq−1 , H, V } be a partition of P into q + 1 parallel classes of q lines each.
                                                             S
Consider a generalized permutation S and its set 2 of unordered pairs. Since no such
pairs are contained in an element of H or V , for all {p1 , p2 } ∈ S2 , there exists exactly one
                                                                          

j ∈ [q − 1] so that there exists an ℓ ∈ Lj with {p1 , p2 } ⊂ Lj . By the pigeonhole principle,
some Lj contains at least ⌈ 2q /(q − 1)⌉ = ⌈q/2⌉ = (q + 1)/2 pairs. These pairs cannot
all be contained in distinct (and therefore disjoint) elements of Lj , or else we would have
≥ 2 · (q + 1)/2 = q + 1 points in S, a contradiction. Thus, two pairs are contained in some
ℓ ∈ Lj , which implies that ℓ ∩ S contains at least three points.
    For convenience, we denote {1, . . . , n} by [n]. An interesting restatement of the above
result takes advantage of the well-known connection between affine planes and complete sets
of mutually orthogonal latin squares (MOLs): For every complete set M1 , . . . , Mq−1 of MOLs
of odd order q, there exists a j ∈ [q − 1] and k ∈ [q] so that the entry k repeats at least 3
times on the diagonal of Mj . To see this, consider the MOLs arising from an affine plane
with distinguished parallel classes H = {h1 , . . . , hq } and V = {v1 , . . . , vq }. The points of A
can be coordinatized as [q] × [q] by identifying p with (i, j) if hi ∩ vj = p. A generalized
permutation S provides a bijection between H and V , which we may assume sends hi to vi
for each i, since the labels on H and V are arbitrary. That {p1 , p2 , p3 } ⊆ S is collinear is
the fact that, for some j ∈ [q − 1] and k ∈ [q], the entries of Mj where k occurs contains
{p1 , p2 , p3 }. Since the labeling of H and V was chosen so that, for each i ∈ [3], pi = (ti , ti )
for some ti , this means that the (ti , ti ) entry of Lj is k, i.e., Lj has at least three k entries
on its diagonal.
    Thus, it is natural to ask the following generalization of the question resolved by Li in [8]
and discussed in the introduction above. For an affine plane A, let Ψ(A) denote the fewest
number of collinear triples in any generalized permutation of A. It is not hard to see that,
if A = AG(Fq , 2) with q = 2k , the minimum number of collinear triples in a permutation is

0. Indeed, let σ : Fq → Fq be defined by σ(x) = 1/x if x 6= 0, and σ(0) = 0. For x, y, z 6= 0
distinct elements of Fq , collinearity amounts to the equation

                                    1/x − 1/y   1/x − 1/z
                                              =
                                      x−y         x−z

which simplifies to (z − x)(y − x)(x − z) = 0, a contradiction. If z = 0, the equation becomes

                                       1/x − 1/y   1/x
                                                 =
                                         x−y        x
which simplifies to y = −x, i.e., y = x because Fq has characteristic 2, a contradiction. This
leaves open the question for other planes than those arising from Desarguesian geometries,
so we ask:

Question 1. What is Ψ(A) for affine planes A other than AG(F, 2) for some finite field F,
i.e., those that arise from non-Desarguesian projective planes?

    In [2], the authors show in Corollary 2.3 that Ψ(A) ≥ (q − 1)/4 for A = AG(q, 2) when q
is odd. It is worth noting here that the proof applies to all finite affine planes, mutatis mu-
tandis, replacing each mention of the collection of lines with a given slope by a parallel class.
However, we do not know if Li’s (cf. [8]) stronger lower bound Ψ(A) ≥ (q −1)/2 holds, nor do
we have any nontrivial upper bounds. In fact, we are able to compute this value at least for
a few small affine planes not arising from vector spaces. There are three non-Desarguesian
projective planes of order 9: the “Hall plane”, its dual, and the “Hughes plane”. According
to [6] (citing [7]), there are seven non-isomorphic affine planes of order 9, namely, AG(9, 2)
and six obtained by, for each of the aforementioned projective planes, deleting one line in
two different ways. Using data provided by [9] via SageMath [13], we tested the above
question for all seven affine planes, obtaining Ψ(A) = 4 in every case but one: interestingly,
the geometry obtained by deleting the unique translation line of the Hall plane has Ψ(A) = 5.

    We also reiterate here a conjecture from [2] which extends the above question in a different
direction.

Question 2. Suppose σ is a permutation of Zn , for n composite. What is the fewest number
Ψ(n) of collinear triples in the graph of σ? For which n is it positive?

   The quantity Ψ(n) is known for all n ≤ 17 ( [10, A379299]). Of course, one may also
substitute any finite subset of any ring for Zn in the above question.

    An consequence of the argument for Proposition 1 is that, if a permutation achieves the
minimum number (q−1)/2 of collinear triples in AG(q, 2), then it has no collinear quadruples,
since otherwise Bonferroni’s Inequality would be strict and violate the known lower bound
on k ∗ (Ω). We therefore ask:

Question 3. Is it true that every generalized permutation in a finite affine plane which
minimizes the number of collinear triples admits no collinear quadruples?
