                                                                                             Abstract
                                                     An oriented graph D is converse invariant if, for any tournament T , the number of copies
                                                 of D in T is equal to that of its converse −D. El Sahili and Ghazo Hanna [J. Graph Theory
                                                 102 (2023), 684-701] showed that any oriented graph D with maximum degree at most 2 is
                                                 converse invariant. They proposed a question: Can we characterize all converse invariant
                                                 oriented graphs?
                                                     In this paper, we introduce a digraph polynomial and employ it to give a necessary condition
                                                 for an oriented graph to be converse invariant. This polynomial serves as a cornerstone in
                                                 proving all the results presented in this paper. In particular, we characterize all orientations of
                                                 trees with diameter at most 3 that are converse invariant. We also show that all orientations
                                                 of regular graphs are not converse invariant if D and −D have different degree sequences.
                                                 In addition, in contrast to the findings of El Sahili and Ghazo Hanna, we prove that every
                                                 connected graph G with maximum degree at least 3, admits an orientation D of G such that
                                                 D is not converse invariant. We pose one conjecture.

    In this paper, we compare the number of subdigraphs and their converses in every tournament
T . The converse of D, denoted by −D, is the digraph on the same vertex set where (u, v) ∈ A(−D)
if and only if (v, u) ∈ A(D). Let fT (D) denote the number of copies of D in a tournament T . An
orgraph D is converse invariant if fT (D) = fT (−D) for every tournament T .
    Rosenfeld [12] in 1974 proved that antidirected paths are converse invariant. El Sahili and Ghazo
Hanna [7] generalized Rosenfeld’s result for any type of oriented paths, and also for cycles. They
showed the following result.
Theorem 1.1 ([7]). Let G be a graph with ∆(G) ≤ 2. Then, every orientation D of G is converse
invariant.
   In the same paper, El Sahili and Ghazo Hanna proposed the following natural problem.
Problem 1.2 ([7]). Can we characterize all converse invariant orgraphs?
   In this paper, we introduce the following digraph polynomial of x

and use it to give the following necessary condition for an orgraph to be converse invariant.
Theorem 1.3. Let D be a converse invariant orgraph. Then PD (x) = P−D (x).
   Theorem 1.3 is utilized as a foundational tool to prove all results in this paper. In particular,
we use Theorem 1.3 to show that paths and cycles are the only connected graphs with the property
that, every orientation of them is converse invariant.
Theorem 1.4. Let G be a connected graph with ∆(G) ≥ 3. Then, there exists an orientation D of
G such that D is not converse invariant.
   In [7], El Sahili and Ghazo Hanna showed that directed K1,d (with all arcs going from the vertex
with maximum degree) with d ≥ 3 is not converse invariant by constructing a special tournament.
Building upon their work, our paper establishes, using our method, that all orientations D of stars
K1,d with d ≥ 3 are not converse invariant unless D is isomorphic to −D (denoted by D ∼    = −D).
Furthermore, we extend this by showing a stronger result.
Theorem 1.5. Let D be an orientation of a tree with diameter at most three that is not a path.

2    Recursive constructions for converse invariant orgraphs
The most natural condition for an orgraph D to be converse invariant might be D ∼  = −D. In this
section, we give a way to construct infinitely many converse invariant orgraphs D with D ∼ 6= −D.
    The following observation is based on the fact that if u and v are vertex-transitive then D +
(u, v) ∼
       = D + (v, u) and therefore there is a one-to-one correspondence between copies of D and
copies of D + (u, v) in any tournament.
Observation 2.1. Let D be an orgraph, and let u and v be a pair of non-adjacent vertices that are
vertex-transitive. Then, fT (D) = fT (D + (u, v)) in any tournament T (See Fig. 1 for an example).

Observation 2.2. Let D be a converse invariant orgraph, and let u and v be a pair of non-adjacent

    The following operation was introduced in [16]. For any digraph D and u ∈ V (D), we can
obtain a new digraph 2Du+ by taking two copies of D and then adding an arc between the two
vertices corresponding to u (see Fig 2 for an instance). And we say that 2Du+ is obtained by
bridge-mirroring D at vertex u. In [16], Zhao and Zhou showed that every orgraph which has
the same density in all tournaments of sufficiently large order can only be obtained by starting
from an arc and recursively applying bridge-mirroring operation. However, by Obervation 2.2 we
can construct converse invariant orgraphs by applying this operation recursively started from any
converse invariant orgraphs. The following results can be obtained directly by applying the above
observations. Note that if D is converse invariant, then the union of arbitrarily many disjoint copies
of D is also converse invariant.
Lemma 2.3. Let D be a converse invariant orgraph and D′ an orgraph obtained by bridge-mirroring
D at a vertex in D. Then, D′ is also a converse invariant orgraph.
Proof. Let 2D (resp. 2(−D)) denote the union of two disjoint copies of D (resp. −D). Since D is
converse invariant, then we have the following holds for any tournament T ,

5     Conclusion
In this paper, we introduce a new digraph polynomial and use it to show a necessary condition for
an orgraph to be converse invariant. In particular, we use this necessary condition to characterize
converse invariant orientations of trees with diameters bounded by three. We would like to make
