                                                            Derangements in intransitive groups
                                                                           David Ellis & Scott Harper

                                                  Let G be a nontrivial permutation group of degree n. If G is transitive, then a theorem
                                                  of Jordan states that G has a derangement. Equivalently, a finite group is never the
                                                  union of conjugates of a proper subgroup. If G is intransitive, then G may fail to have a
                                                  derangement, and this can happen even if G has only two orbits, both of which have size
                                                  (1/2 + o (1))n. However, we conjecture that if G has two orbits of size exactly n/2 then
                                                  G does have a derangement, and we prove this conjecture when G acts primitively on at
                                                  least one of the orbits. Equivalently, we conjecture that a finite group is never the union
                                                  of conjugates of two proper subgroups of the same order, and we prove this conjecture




arXiv:2408.16064v3 [math.GR] 27 Jan 2026
                                                  when at least one of the subgroups is maximal. (Feldman also implicitly raised this
                                                  conjecture on StackExchange.) We also prove the conjecture for soluble groups, almost
                                                  simple groups and groups of order at most 50000, and we reduce the conjecture to
                                                  perfect groups. Along the way, we prove a linear variant on Isbell’s conjecture regarding
                                                  derangements of prime-power order, and we highlight connections with intersecting
                                                  families of permutations and roots of polynomials modulo primes.


                                           1    Introduction
                                           Let G ⩽ Sym(n) be a nontrivial permutation group. By an 1872 theorem of Jordan [35], if G
                                           is transitive then it contains a derangement, i.e. a fixed-point-free permutation. Equivalently, a
                                           finite group is never the union of the conjugates of a proper subgroup. As highlighted by
                                           Serre [48], this elementary group theoretic fact has consequences for number theory, topology
                                           and character theory. There are now numerous generalisations of this classical result, and
                                           we refer the reader to the introductory chapter of [10] for an overview.
                                              The hypothesis that G is finite is necessary for Jordan’s theorem. Indeed, every element of
                                           G = GLn (C) is conjugate to an upper triangular matrix, so G is the union of conjugates of
                                           the subgroup of G consisting of all upper triangular matrices. More generally, a connected
                                           linear algebraic group over an algebraically closed field is the union of conjugates of a Borel
                                           subgroup. Every transitive action of G has a derangement if and only if G has an invariable
                                           generating set, and much recent work has focussed on determining which infinite groups
                                           do, beginning with Kantor, Lubotzky and Shalev in [36].
                                               The hypothesis that G is transitive is also necessary. For a trivial example, every element
                                           of G = Sym(n − 1) ⩽ Sym(n) fixes the point n, so G has no derangements. Moreover, when
                                           G ⩽ Sym(n) has no derangements, the size of the smallest orbit of G can be arbitrarily close
                                           to n2 (see Remark 7(ii) below), but we conjecture that it can never equal n2 .
                                           Conjecture 1. Let G ⩽ Sym(n) have two orbits of size n2 > 1. Then G contains a derangement.
                                              Conjecture 1 asserts that no finite group is the union of conjugates of two proper sub-
                                           groups of the same order (see Remark 7). Feldman [25] also asked this on StackExchange.
                                               We now state our main theorem, which implies an important special case of Conjecture 1.
                                           Theorem 2. Let G ⩽ Sym(n) have exactly two orbits Ω1 and Ω2 , both of which are nontrivial.
                                           Assume that |Ω1 | divides |Ω2 | and that G acts primitively on Ω2 . Then G contains a derangement.
                                              Equivalently, Theorem 2 asserts that a finite group is not the union of conjugates of a
                                           proper subgroup H1 and a maximal subgroup H2 such that | H2 | divides | H1 |. The assumption
                                           that G is primitive on Ω2 (equivalently, that H2 is maximal) is necessary, see Remark 11(ii).

Remark 8. Permutation groups G ⩽ Sym(n) with two orbits of size n2 arise naturally in
graph theory. Let Γ be a regular graph such that Aut(Γ) is transitive on edges. Then either
Aut(Γ) is transitive on vertices, or Aut(Γ) has exactly two, equal-sized, orbits on vertices. In
particular, Conjecture 1 asserts that Aut(Γ) has a derangement. While this is open in general,
it is known when Γ is 3- or 4-regular by [27, Corollary 1.4] and when Γ has fewer than 64
vertices by a computational verification by Conder (such graphs are classified in [17]).
Remark 9. A set S ⊆ Sym(Ω) is intersecting if for all x, y ∈ S there is ω ∈ Ω with ωx = ωy.
By analogy with the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem on intersecting families of subsets [22], Frankl
and Deza [26] proved that if S ⊆ Sym(Ω) is intersecting, then |S| ⩽ (|Ω| − 1)!, and Cameron
and Ku [15] proved that equality holds if and only if S consists of all permutations sending
ω ∈ Ω to ω ′ ∈ Ω. In particular, all extremal examples of intersecting subgroups are point
stabilisers, so have an orbit of size 1. Do all intersecting subgroups of permutations have
a small orbit, or perhaps even an orbit of size at most an absolute constant C? Nakajima
[45] asked this with a view towards an application to constraint satisfaction problems if the
question had an affirmative answer. A subgroup G ⩽ Sym(Ω) is intersecting if and only if it
has no derangements, so Remark 7(ii) negatively answers this question, but we conjecture
that there are no examples whose smallest orbit has size 12 |Ω|.

Remark 11. Let us comment on the hypotheses in our main results.
 (i) Conjecture 1 has no immediate reduction to primitive actions, as two subgroups of equal
     order need not be contained in maximal subgroups of equal order (for example, the
     subgroups S2 × S3 and A4 of S5 ). This explains the additional hypothesis in Corollary 3.
(ii) The hypothesis that G acts primitively on Ω2 is necessary in Theorem 2, as a finite group
     can be the union of conjugates of proper subgroups H1 and H2 such that | H2 | divides
     | H1 |. Indeed, Example 2.2 gives a group G = 23 .A4 that is the union of conjugates of a
     maximal subgroup H1 ∼   = 23 .C3 of order 24 and a subgroup H2 ∼ = C2 × C4 of order 8.
    Let us now outline our proofs. We first reduce Theorem 2 to the case where G acts
faithfully and primitively on Ω1 , and, using the O’Nan–Scott Theorem, we divide our
analysis according to the possibilities for G ⩽ Sym(Ω1 ). However, even when G ⩽ Sym(Ω1 )
is of a fixed O’Nan–Scott type, the action of G on Ω2 is an arbitrary (not necessarily faithful)
primitive action, and our arguments are novel since we must keep track of how the actions of
G on Ω1 and Ω2 interact. We then reduce further to the case where G ⩽ Sym(Ω1 ) is almost
simple or affine. For almost simple groups, we make use of recent work by Bubboloni, Spiga
and Weigel [9] on normal 2-coverings.
   Affine groups require the most work, and we need a very recent representation theoretic
result of Harper and Liebeck [31], which generalises a result of Feit and Tits [24]. (Indeed
this paper was the original motivation for the work in [31].) In order to apply this result, we
need to carry out a series of nontrivial reductions. This is technically challenging since each
of these reductions are in tension with each other and therefore need to be carried out in a

   In addressing the affine groups, we also establish results which may be of independent
interest. First, we obtain bounds on the smallest degrees of permutation and linear represen-
tations of simple groups G in terms of the prime factorisation of | G |, see Section 2.4. Second,
we establish a linear variant of Isbell’s Conjecture, as we now explain.
   To answer a number theoretic question regarding relative Brauer groups, Fein, Kantor and
Schacher [23] extended Jordan’s theorem by proving that every nontrivial finite transitive
permutation group has a derangement whose order is a power of a prime p. In contrast to
Jordan’s original theorem, which is an elementary counting argument, this generalisation
uses the CFSG. Which prime p works? Isbell’s conjecture asserts that if |Ω| = p a b for a
sufficiently large compared to b, then G has a derangement whose order is a power of p.
Motivated by a connection to fair n-player games, Isbell [33] made the conjecture for p = 2,
and the general conjecture was formulated by Cameron, Frankl and Kantor [14, Section 1].
We prove the following linear variant of Isbell’s Conjecture.
Theorem 12. Let G be a finite group acting primitively on Ω. Let p be a prime number and let
ρ : G → GLd ( p) be a faithful irreducible representation. Assume that pd divides |Ω|. Then G
contains a derangement g such that gρ fixes a nonzero vector in Fdp .
Remark 13. To see the connection to Isbell’s Conjecture, note that if g is a p-element, then gρ
fixes a nonzero vector of Fdp . However, there is not always a derangement that is a p-element.
For example, let d = 2m ⩾ 6, let G = GLm ( p2 ), let H = GLm ( p) and consider the field
extension embedding ρ : G → GLd ( p). Then pd divides | G : H |, but if g ∈ G has p-power
order, then g is unipotent, so (via Jordan normal form) is conjugate to an element of H.
Therefore, no p-element is a derangement in the primitive action of G on G/H.
   Theorems 5 and 6 are proved together in Section 5 with Theorem 5(iii) involving compu-
tation with the perfect groups of order at most 50000 in M AGMA [6].

Remark 2.1. Recall that the socle of a finite group G, denoted soc( G ), is the product of the
minimal normal subgroups of G. Let G be a nontrivial finite primitive permutation group on
a set Ω. Then soc( G ) = T k where T is a simple group and k is a positive integer.
 (i) First assume that T is abelian. This gives case (HA). In this case, G is an affine group,
     i.e. G = V:H ⩽ AGL(V ) where V is an elementary abelian group of order pk and
     H ⩽ GL(V ) is an irreducible linear group. The action is given by the natural affine
     action of AGL(V ) on V, viewed as the vector space Fkp , and H is the stabiliser of the
     zero vector. Here V is the unique minimal normal subgroup of G and V is regular.
(ii) Next assume that T is nonabelian and k = 1. This gives case (AS). In this case, G is an
     almost simple group, i.e. T ⩽ G ⩽ Aut( T ), where T is a nonabelian simple group. Here,
     T is the unique minimal normal subgroup of G.
(iii) In the remaining cases, T is nonabelian and k ⩾ 2. Therefore, T k ⩽ G ⩽ Aut( T k ) =

      has type (TW). For (HS) and (HC), G has exactly two minimal normal subgroups and
      these are isomorphic and regular. (For (HS), we always have k = 2.)
(iv) Parts (i)–(iii) imply that if G has no regular normal subgroups, then soc( G ) is the unique

2.3   Derangements and primes
In this section, we make some preliminary observations about derangements. We begin with
a small generalisation of Jordan’s original result, which was first noted in [2, Section 3].
Lemma 2.3. Let Ω be finite, let G ⩽ Sym(Ω) be transitive and let h ∈ Sym(Ω). Then the average
number of fixed points of elements in the coset Gh is 1.
Proof. The key observation is that for g ∈ G and ω ∈ Ω, we have ωgh = ω ⇐⇒ g ∈ Gω h−1 .
Now observe that

                ∑ fix( gh) = |{( g, ω ) ∈ G × Ω | ωgh = ω }| = ∑ |Gω | = |G|
               g∈ G                                                 ω ∈Ω

using the fact that | Gω | = | G |/|Ω| since G is transitive.
Remark 2.4. Consider Lemma 2.3 when h is trivial. The average number of fixed points of
G ⩽ Sym(Ω) is 1. If |Ω| > 1, then the identity element has more than one fixed point, so G
must contain an element with no fixed points, which returns Jordan’s original theorem.
   For the next lemma, we use a famed theorem [23], which depends on the CFSG.
Theorem (Fein, Kantor & Schacher, 1981). Let Ω be finite. Let 1 < G ⩽ Sym(Ω) be transitive.
Then G contains a derangement of prime-power order.
Lemma 2.5. Let G ⩽ Sym(Ω) have exactly two orbits Ω1 and Ω2 . Assume that |Ω1 |, |Ω2 | > 1
and that no prime divisor of |Ω1 | divides |Ω2 | − 1. Let N1 be the kernel of the action of G on Ω1 .
Assume that N1 acts transitively on Ω2 . Then G has a derangement.
Proof. Viewing G as a subgroup of Sym(Ω1 ) × Sym(Ω2 ), for all g ∈ G, write g = ( g1 , g2 )
where g1 ∈ Sym(Ω1 ) and g2 ∈ Sym(Ω2 ). Since N1 acts transitively on Ω2 , by Lemma 2.3, the
average number of fixed points on Ω2 of elements in any coset of N1 in G is 1. Suppose that
for every g ∈ G such that g1 ∈ Sym(Ω1 ) is a derangement, every element of the coset N1 g
has exactly one fixed point on Ω2 . By [23, Theorem 1], there exists an element g ∈ G such
that g1 ∈ Sym(Ω1 ) is a derangement of order a power of some prime p. In particular, |Ω1 | is
divisible by p. Consider the corresponding permutation g2 ∈ Sym(Ω2 ). By replacing g by
a suitable p′ -power if necessary, we may assume that every cycle of g2 has length a power
of p (while maintaining the condition that g1 is a derangement). However, g2 has exactly
one fixed point, so |Ω2 | − 1 is divisible by p, which contradicts our hypothesis. Therefore,
there must exist g ∈ G such that g1 ∈ Sym(Ω1 ) is a derangement and h ∈ N1 g such that
h2 ∈ Sym(Ω2 ) is a derangement. However, since N1 acts trivially on Ω1 , we know that
h1 = g1 ∈ Sym(Ω1 ) is also a derangement, so h is a derangement on Ω.

3     Linear variant on Isbell’s Conjecture
The purpose of this section is to prove Theorem 12. In Section 3.1 we prove Proposition 3.3,
which is an important special case of Theorem 12 that serves as the base case for our proof.
In Section 3.2 we prove Lemma 3.5, which is a technical result which will play a key role in
reducing Theorem 12 to the special case handled in Proposition 3.3. In Section 3.3, we com-
plete the proof of Theorem 12, where the four main tools are the base case in Proposition 3.3,
the bounds in Proposition 2.6, the reduction in Lemma 3.5 and [31, Theorem 4]. The last of
these is a recent representation theoretic result established by Harper and Liebeck.

4.2   Theorem 2* for affine groups
This section is dedicated to proving Theorem 2* when the faithful primitive action is affine.
We begin with some preliminary lemmas.
Lemma 4.4. Let G = V:H be a finite primitive affine group. Let K be a maximal subgroup of G.
Then either K is a complement of V or K = V:M for a maximal subgroup M of H.
Proof. Let φ : G → H be the projection onto H with kernel V. First assume that Kφ < H. Then
K ⩽ V:(Kφ), so by the maximality of K, we deduce that K = V:(Kφ), where Kφ is maximal
in H. Now assume that Kφ = H. Suppose that V ∩ K is nontrivial, and fix 0 ̸= v ∈ V ∩ K.
Since Kφ = H and H acts irreducibly on V, we deduce that ⟨vK ⟩ = V, so V ⩽ K, but this
implies that K = G, which is absurd. Therefore, V ∩ K = 1, so K is a complement of G.
