Problems around polynomials - the good, the bad and the ugly…
The title of the paper alluding to the highly recommended spaghetti western by S. Leone, reflects the very personal taste of the author concerning mathematical problems which should be taken with a grain of salt. In his opinion a ”good“ problem has an elegant short formulation and is (hopefully) solvable, while a ”bad“ one looks equally stimulating but seems unaccessible at the moment. Finally an ”ugly“ problem apparently has a non inspiringly complicated answer which can hardly stumulate a further development. But one can not really tell untill the problem itself has been actually solved! But in any case, the esthetic feeling about mathematical problems and their solutions has to be taken seriously.

1. Problems




I. Around Maxwell’s conjecture. In Section 133 of [Ma], J. C. Maxwell formulated the following claim, but provided it will an incomplete proof (see details in [GNSh]).



Conjecture 1 ( Maxwell, seems bad, no tools).



For any system of NN isolated fixed point charges in ℝ3\mathbb{R}^{3}, the number of points of equilibrium (assumed finite) of the created electrostatic field




E⁡(x¯)=∑i=1Nξi​(x¯−x¯i)|x¯−x¯i|3,E(\bar{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\xi_{i}(\bar{x}-\bar{x}_{i})}{|\bar{x}-\bar{x}_{i}|^{3}},



is at most (N−1)2(N-1)^{2}. Here ξi\xi_{i} is a charge placed at x¯i∈ℝ3\bar{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3} and x¯∈ℝ3\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3} is a variable vector.





Some very crude estimate on the maximal number of points of equilibrium is obtained in [GNSh] using Khovanskii’s fewnomial theory, see [Kh].
Conjecture 1 is not even settled for 3 positive point charges in which case all points of equilibrium lie in the plane spanned by these charges.
(Some special cases of 3 charges are settled in the recent literature, see [Ki],[Pe],[Wa].)




Observe that for charges of different signs in ℝ3,\mathbb{R}^{3}, the set of points of equilibrium might be a space curve. The simplest example of that kind is a 4-tuple of charges placed at the vertices of a square in the zz-plane with coordinates (±1,±1,0)(\pm 1,\pm 1,0). If one places the unit positive charges at (1,1,0)(1,1,0) and (−1,−1,0)(-1,-1,0) and the unit negative charges at 2 remaining corners, then the set of points of equilibrium coincides with the zz-axis. The following naive-looking question is not settled either.



Conjecture 2 (folklore, very irritating).



For any set of charges of the same sign in ℝn\mathbb{R}^{n}, the set of its points of equilibrium is finite.





The next claim is a very special one-dimensional case of a rather general Conjecture 1.7. of [GNSh] which generalizes the above Conjecture 1 in many ways.



Conjecture 3 (A. Gabrielov, D. Novikov, B. Sh., seems good, but no progress).



Let (x1,y1),(x2,y2),…,(xN,yN)(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2}),\dots,(x_{N},y_{N}) be a collection of points in ℝ2\mathbb{R}^{2}, ξ1,ξ2,…,ξN\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{N} be arbitrary real charges and α≥1/2\alpha\geq 1/2. Then the rational univariate function




Ψ⁡(x)=∑i=1Nξi((x−xi)2+yi2)α,x∈ℝ,\Psi(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\xi_{i}}{((x-x_{i})^{2}+y_{i}^{2})^{\alpha}},\;x\in\mathbb{R},



has at most NN local maxima on the whole real line.





Observe that also this conjecture is not settled in the simplest case N=3N=3, α=1\alpha=1 and all unit charges. The author has an overwhelming numerical evidence supporting the latter conjecture but no proof.




II. On real zeros of exponential sums. Consider the space of linear ordinary homogeneous differential equations with constant coefficients of order kk, i.e.




y(k)+a1​y(k−1)+⋯+ak​y=0y^{(k)}+a_{1}y^{(k-1)}+\dots+a_{k}y=0

(1.1)


where a1,…,aka_{1},\dots,a_{k} are arbitrary complex numbers. The next statement easily follows from the standard facts about the asymptotic zero distribution of exponential sums, see e.g., [La].



Lemma 1.



Every non-trivial solution of (1.1) has finitely many (probably none) real zeros if and only if
any two distinct characteristic roots of (1.1) have distinct real parts.





Denote by Ωk\Omega_{k} the set of all (1.1) satisfying the conditions of Lemma 1. (It is an open dense subset of ℂk\mathbb{C}^{k} with coordinates (a1,…,ak)(a_{1},\dots,a_{k}).)



Problem 1 (B. Sh., looks bad, but very important).



Does there exist an upper bound for the number of real roots valid for all non-trivial solutions of all equations (1.1) in Ωk\Omega_{k}?





The latter problem is open already for k=3k=3. Observe that there exists a highly non-trivial and apparently far from being sharp upper bound for the number of integer zeros of exponential polynomials obtained in [Sch].




III. On isolated zeros of non-negative polynomials and sums of squares.



Problem 2 (D. Khavinson, I. Itenberg, B. Sh., apparently bad).



Find the maximal possible number ♯⁡(2​k,l)\sharp(2k,l) of isolated zeros for real non-negative polynomials of degree 2​k2k in ll variables.




Problem 3 (G. Ottaviani, B. Sh., seems good).



Find the maximal possible number ♯~​(2​k,l)\widetilde{\sharp}(2k,l) of isolated zeros for real non-negative polynomials of degree 2​k2k in ll variables which are representable as the sums of squares of real polynomials of degree at most kk.





A trivial observation is that kl≤♯~​(2​k,l)≤♯⁡(2​k,l)≤(2​k−1)l.k^{l}\leq\widetilde{\sharp}(2k,l)\leq\sharp(2k,l)\leq(2k-1)^{l}. In the special case l=2l=2 both problems were considered in an intruguing paper [CLR] where it was proven that ♯~​(2​k,2)=k2\widetilde{\sharp}(2k,2)=k^{2} and ♯⁡(2​k,2)≤3​k​(k−1)2+1\sharp(2k,2)\leq\frac{3k(k-1)}{2}+1. The latter inequality is obtained with the help of Petrovskii-Oleinik’s inequality, see [OlPe].



Conjecture 4 (G. Ottaviani, B. Sh., seems good).



For any number of variables, ♯~​(2​k,l)=kl\widetilde{\sharp}(2k,l)=k^{l}.





On the other hand, it seems difficult to determine the coefficient of klk^{l} of the leading asymptotic term for ♯⁡(2​k,l),\sharp(2k,l), when ll is fixed and k→∞k\to\infty. For example, I doubt that ♯⁡(2​k,2)\sharp(2k,2) grows asymptotically as 3​k2/2,{3k^{2}}/{2}, when k→∞k\to\infty.




IV. Hermite-Biehler problem.
The well-known Hermite-Biehler theorem claims that a univariate monic polynomial ss of degree kk has all roots in the open upper half-plane if and only if s=p+i​q.s=p+iq. Here pp and qq are real polynomials of degree kk and k−1k-1 respectively with all real, simple and interlacing roots, and qq has a negative leading coefficient.



Problem 4 (S. Fisk, seems bad, see [Fi], p. 575).



Given a pair of real polynomials (p,q),(p,q), give restrictions on the location of the roots of p+i​qp+iq in terms of the location of the roots of pp and qq.





An example of such results can be found in [KST]. Closely related important questions are: (i) restrictions on the location of (complex) roots of the Wronskian W⁡(p,q);W(p,q); (ii) description of the real univalent disks for the real rational function
pq\frac{p}{q}.




V. Mesh-related questions.
By the mesh of a polynomial p⁡(x)p(x) with all real simple zeros we mean the minimal distance between its consecutive roots.



Conjecture 5 (P. Bränden, I. Krasikov, B. Sh., hopefully good, see [BKSh]).



A difference operator T⁡(p⁡(x))=a0​p​(x)+a1​p​(x−1)+⋯+ak​p​(x−k)T(p(x))=a_{0}p(x)+a_{1}p(x-1)+\cdots+a_{k}p(x-k) with constant coefficients preserves the set of real-rooted polynomials of degree at most mm whose mesh is at least 11 if and only if the polynomial T⁡((x)m)T((x)_{m}) is real-rooted and has mesh at least one. Here (x)m=x⁡(x−1)​(x−2)​…​(x−m+1)(x)_{m}=x(x-1)(x-2)\dots(x-m+1) is the mm-th Pochhammer polynomial.





There is an alternative formulation of Conjecture 5 which is maybe more attractive. Let
∇p​(x)=p⁡(x+1)−p⁡(x)\nabla p(x)=p(x+1)-p(x) be the forward difference operator, and consider the following product on the space of polynomials of degree at most dd:




(p∙q)​(x)=∑k=0d(∇kp)​(0)⋅(∇d−kq)​(x).(p\bullet q)(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}(\nabla^{k}p)(0)\cdot(\nabla^{d-k}q)(x).



Conjecture 5 is equivalent to



Conjecture 6.



If pp and qq are real-rooted polynomials of degree at most dd and of mesh ≥1\geq 1, then so is p∙qp\bullet q.





VI. Topology of the space of polynomials.
The famous Descartes’ rule of signs claims that the number of positive roots of a real univariate polynomial does not exceed the number of sign changes in its sequence of coefficients. For simplicity let us only consider polynomials with all non-vanishing coefficients. An arbitrary ordered sequence
σ¯=(σ0,σ1,…,σd){\bar{\sigma}}=(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{d}) of ±\pm-signs is called a sign pattern. Given a sign pattern σ¯{{\bar{\sigma}}} as above, we call by its Descartes’ pair (pσ¯,nσ¯)(p_{\bar{\sigma}},n_{\bar{\sigma}}) the pair of non-negative integers counting sign changes and sign preservations of σ¯{\bar{\sigma}}. The Descartes’ pair of σ¯{\bar{\sigma}} gives the upper bounds on the number of positive and negative roots of any polynomial of degree dd whose signs of coefficients are given by σ¯{\bar{\sigma}}. (Observe that, for any σ¯{\bar{\sigma}},
pσ¯+nσ¯=dp_{\bar{\sigma}}+n_{\bar{\sigma}}=d.) To any polynomial q⁡(x)q(x) with the sign pattern σ¯,{\bar{\sigma}}, we associate the pair (p​o​sq,n​e​gq)(pos_{q},neg_{q}) giving the numbers of its positive and negative roots (counted multiplicities). Obviously (p​o​sq,n​e​gq)(pos_{q},neg_{q}) satisfies the standard restrictions




p​o​sq≤pσ¯,p​o​sq≡pσ¯​(m​o​d​ 2),n​e​gq≤nσ¯,n​e​gq≡nσ¯​(m​o​d​ 2).pos_{q}\leq p_{\bar{\sigma}},\;pos_{q}\equiv p_{\bar{\sigma}}(mod\,2),\;neg_{q}\leq n_{\bar{\sigma}},\;neg_{q}\equiv n_{\bar{\sigma}}(mod\,2).

(1.2)





We call pairs (p​o​s,n​e​g)(pos,neg) satisfying (1.2) admissible for σ¯{\bar{\sigma}}.
It turns out that not for every pattern σ¯,{\bar{\sigma}}, all its admissible pairs (p​o​s,n​e​g)(pos,neg) are realizable by polynomials with the sign pattern σ¯{\bar{\sigma}}.



Problem 5 (seems bad, but might be ugly).



For a given sign pattern σ¯,{\bar{\sigma}}, which admissible pairs (p​o​s,n​e​g)(pos,neg) are realizable by polynomials whose signs of coefficients are given by σ¯{\bar{\sigma}}?





The first non-realizable combination of a sign pattern and a pair (p​o​s,n​e​g)(pos,neg) occurs in degree 44, see [Gr]. Namely, (up to the standard ℤ2×ℤ2\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}-action on the set of all sign patterns) the only non-realizable combination is σ¯=(+,+,−,+,+){\bar{\sigma}}=(+,+,-,+,+) with the pair (2,0)(2,0). Based on our computer-aided results up to degree 1010, we can formulate the following claim.



Conjecture 7 (J. Forsgård, V. Kostov, B. Sh, hopefully good, see [FKSh]).



For an arbitrary sign pattern σ¯{\bar{\sigma}}, the only type of pairs (p​o​s,n​e​g)(pos,neg) which can be non-realizable has either p​o​spos or n​e​gneg vanishing. In other words, for any sign pattern σ¯{\bar{\sigma}}, each pair (p​o​s,n​e​g)(pos,neg) satisfying (1.2) with positive p​o​spos and n​e​gneg is realizable.

VIII. Polynomial-like functions,.




Consider a smooth function ff with nn distinct
real zeros
x1(0)<x2(0)<…<xn(0)x_{1}^{(0)}<x_{2}^{(0)}<\ldots<x_{n}^{(0)} in some interval I⊆ℝI\subseteq\mathbb{R}. Then, by Rolle’s theorem, f′f^{\prime} has at
least (n−1)(n-1) zeros, f′′f^{\prime\prime} has at least (n−2)(n-2) zeros, … , f(n−1)f^{(n-1)}
has at least one zero in the open interval (x1(0),xn(0))(x_{1}^{(0)},x_{n}^{(0)}).
We are interested in smooth functions ff with nn real simple zeros in II such that for all i=1,…,n,i=1,\ldots,n, the
iith derivative f(i)f^{(i)} has exactly n−in-i real simple
zeros in II denoted by x1(i)<x2(i)<…<xn−i(i).x^{(i)}_{1}<x_{2}^{(i)}<...<x_{n-i}^{(i)}. Note, in particular, that f(n)f^{(n)} is non-vanishing in II.



Definition 1.



A smooth function ff defined
in an interval II is called polynomial-like of degree nn
if f(n)f^{(n)} does not vanish in II. A polynomial-like function of degree nn in II with nn simple real zeros is called real-rooted.





By Rolle’s theorem, nn is the maximal possible number of real zeros in II for a polynomial-like function of degree nn. An obvious example of a real-rooted polynomial-like function of degree nn on ℝ\mathbb{R} is a usual real polynomial of degree nn
with all real and distinct zeros. Observe also that if a polynomial-like function ff of degree nn is real-rooted in II, then for all i<n,i<n,
its derivatives f(i)f^{(i)} are also real-rooted of degree n−in-i in the same interval. In the above notation the
following system of inequalities holds:




xl(i)<xl(j)<xl+j−i(i), for ​i<j≤n−l.x^{(i)}_{l}<x^{(j)}_{l}<x^{(i)}_{l+j-i},\quad\text{ for }i<j\leq n-l.

(1.3)


We call (1.3) the system of
standard Rolle’s restrictions. (It is worth mentioning that the standard Rolle’s restrictions define the well-known Gelfand-Tsetlin polytope, see e.g., [deLoMcAl].)




With any real-rooted polynomial-like function ff of degree nn, one can associate its configuration
𝒜f\mathcal{A}_{f} of (n+12)\binom{n+1}{2} zeros {xl(i)}\{x^{(i)}_{l}\} of
f(i),f^{(i)}, for i=0,…,n−1i=0,\ldots,n-1 and 1≤l≤n−i1\leq l\leq n-i, by taking first all
xl(0)x^{(0)}_{l}, then all xl(1),x^{(1)}_{l}, etc.



Problem 6 (V. Kostov, B. Sh., looks ugly, see [ShSh]).



What additional restrictions besides (1.3) exist
on configurations 𝒜f={xl(i)}\mathcal{A}_{f}=\{x^{(i)}_{l}\} coming from real-rooted polynomial-like
functions of a given degree nn? Or, more ambitiously, given a configuration 𝒜={xl(i)|i=0,…,n−1;l=1,…n−i}\mathcal{A}=\{x^{(i)}_{l}|\;i=0,\ldots,n-1;\,l=1,\ldots n-i\} of
(n+12)\binom{n+1}{2} real numbers satisfying standard Rolle’s
restrictions, is it possible to determine if there exists a real-rooted polynomial-like ff of degree nn such that
𝒜f=𝒜\mathcal{A}_{f}=\mathcal{A}?





In the simplest non-trivial case n=3,n=3, Problem 6 was solved in [ShSh], but the general case is widely open.




Substituting each zero of pp by the symbol 00, each zero
of p′p^{\prime} by 11, … , the unique zero of p(n−1)p^{(n-1)} by (n−1)(n-1) respectively, we get a symbolic sequence of pp of
length (n+12)\binom{n+1}{2}
with nn occurrences of 00, (n−1)(n-1) occurrences of 11,…, one occurrence of (n−1)(n-1). Standard Rolle’s restrictions result in the condition
that between any two consecutive occurrences
of the symbol ii in such a sequence, one has exactly one occurrence of the symbol i+1i+1.




For example, there are only two possible symbolic sequences 012010012010 and 010210010210, for
n=3n=3. For n=4,n=4, there are 12 such
sequences 01230120100123012010, 01203120100120312010, 01201320100120132010, 01023120100102312010,
01021320100102132010,
01230102100123010210, 01203102100120310210, 01201302100120130210, 01201032100120103210, 01230102100123010210,
01021302100102130210, 01021032100102103210. A patient reader will find that for n=5,n=5, there are
286286 such sequences.




If we denote by ♭n\flat_{n} the number of all possible symbolic sequences
of length nn, then it is possible to calculate this number explicitly.
It turns out to be equal to




♭n=(n+12)!​1!​2!​…​(n−1)!1!​3!​…​(2​n−1)!.\flat_{n}=\binom{n+1}{2}!\frac{1!2!...(n-1)!}{1!3!...(2n-1)!}.

IX. Around ”Hawaiian“ conjecture.




A simple observation that if a real polynomial p⁡(x)p(x) has all real and simple zeros then the function p′​(x)p⁡(x)\frac{p^{\prime}(x)}{p(x)} is (locally) strictly monotone was apparently known to Gauss.
We can reformulate the above observation in
the form of the classical Laguerre inequality:



Lemma 2.



If p⁡(x)p(x) has only simple real zeros then the polynomial
P1​(x)=(p′​(x))2−p⁡(x)​p′′​(x)P_{1}(x)=(p^{\prime}(x))^{2}-p(x)p^{\prime\prime}(x) is strictly positive.





Refinement of this observation constitutes the ”Hawaiian“ conjecture saying that for any real polynomial p⁡(x)p(x) with simple real zeros (and arbitrary complex-conjugate pairs of zeros),




♯r​[(p′​(x))2−p⁡(x)​p′′​(x)]≤♯n​r​p​(x).\sharp_{r}\left[(p^{\prime}(x))^{2}-p(x)p^{\prime\prime}(x)\right]\leq\sharp_{nr}p(x).

(1.4)


Here ♯r​q​(x)\sharp_{r}q(x) (resp. ♯n​r​q​(x)\sharp_{nr}q(x))
stands for the number of
real (resp. non-real) zeros of a polynomial q⁡(x)q(x) with
real coefficients.




”Hawaiian“ conjecture was settled in [Tya] by elementary but tedious calculations.
Observe that polynomial P1​(x)=(p′​(x))2−p⁡(x)​p′′​(x)P_{1}(x)=(p^{\prime}(x))^{2}-p(x)p^{\prime\prime}(x)
appears not only as the numerator of (p′​(x)p⁡(x))′\left(\frac{p^{\prime}(x)}{p(x)}\right)^{\prime} but also in a different situation discovered
by J. Jensen, [Je] around 1910. Namely, consider the
function




Φp​(x,y)=|p⁡(x+i​y)|2.\Phi_{p}(x,y)=|{p(x+iy)}|^{2}.

(1.5)


Φp​(x,y)\Phi_{p}(x,y) is a real-analytic nonnegative function in (x,y)(x,y) whose
zeros correspond to the zeros of p⁡(z)=p⁡(x+i​y)p(z)=p(x+iy). Assuming that
deg⁡p⁡(x)=k\deg p(x)=k and expanding
Φp​(x,y)\Phi_{p}(x,y) in the variable y,y, one gets




Φp​(x,y)=∑i=0kPi​(x)​y2​i(2​i)!,\Phi_{p}(x,y)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}P_{i}(x)\frac{y^{2i}}{(2i)!},

(1.6)


where




Pi​(x)=∑j=0i(−1)i+j​(2​ij)​p(j)​(x)​p(2​i−j)​(x).P_{i}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{i}(-1)^{i+j}\binom{2i}{j}p^{(j)}(x)p^{(2i-j)}(x).

(1.7)


In particular, P0​(x)=p2​(x)P_{0}(x)=p^{2}(x) and
P1​(x)=(p′​(x))2−p⁡(x)​p′′​(x)P_{1}(x)=(p^{\prime}(x))^{2}-p(x)p^{\prime\prime}(x).) The following explicit formula




Pi​(x)=p2​(x)​∑(l1,…,l2​i)(2​i)!(x−xl1)2​…​(x−xl2​i)2P_{i}(x)=p^{2}(x)\sum_{(l_{1},\ldots,l_{2i})}\frac{(2i)!}{(x-x_{l_{1}})^{2}\ldots(x-x_{l_{2i}})^{2}}

(1.8)


is valid. Here p⁡(x)=(x−x1)​(x−x2)​…​(x−xk)p(x)=(x-x_{1})(x-x_{2})\ldots(x-x_{k})
and the summation is taken over all 2​i2i-tuples (with repetitions
in case of multiple zeros). The latter formula immediately implies
the following criterion of real-rootedness known to J. Jensen.



Proposition 3 (11-st criterion of real-rootedness).



A polynomial p⁡(x)p(x) of degree kk has simple real zeros if and only if the polynomials Pi​(x)P_{i}(x) are
strictly positive for all i=1,…,k−1i=1,\ldots,k-1.





Somewhat later G. Polya studying a number of unpublished notes left after Jensen’s untimely death in 1912 discovered a different criterion of real-rootedness,



Proposition 4 (22-nd criterion of real-rootedness).



A polynomial p⁡(x)p(x) of degree kk has real simpe zeros if and only if the polynomials




Gi​(x)=(k−i)​(pi​(x))2−(k−i+1)​p(i−1)​(x)​p(i+1)​(x)G_{i}(x)=(k-i)(p^{i}(x))^{2}-(k-i+1)p^{(i-1)}(x)p^{(i+1)}(x)

(1.9)


are strictly positive for all i=1,…,k−1i=1,\ldots,k-1.





Propositions 3-4 combined with the original ”Hawaiian“ conjecture motivate our questions of the number of real zeros for the families {Pi​(x)}\{P_{i}(x)\}
and {Gi​(x)}\{G_{i}(x)\} presented below. They are based on extensive experiments with real polynomials of degree up to 66.



Conjecture 11 (B. Sh., seems good).



For any real polynomial p⁡(x)p(x) of degree kk with simple real zeros,




♯r​[(k−1)​(p′​(x))2−k​p​(x)​p′′​(x)]≤♯n​r​p​(x),\sharp_{r}\left[(k-1)(p^{\prime}(x))^{2}-kp(x)p^{\prime\prime}(x)\right]\leq\sharp_{nr}p(x),

(1.10)
