=== 18: Erdős Problem #18 — Representing integers below $n!$ as sums of few distinct divisors ===

=== 315912: Rudin's conjecture ===
For positive integers $N,q,a$, let

$$
Q(N;q,a)=\#\{\,n\in\mathbb{Z}: 0\le n\le N-1,\ qn+a \text{ is a perfect square}\,\},
$$

that is, the number of perfect squares among the $N$ terms $a,\ q+a,\ 2q+a,\
\ldots,\ (N-1)q+a$ of the arithmetic progression $qn+a$, and set

$$
Q(N)=\max\{\,Q(N;q,a): q\ge 1,\ a\ge 1\,\}.
$$

Then:

1. $Q(N)=O\!\left(\sqrt{N}\right)$, i.e. there is an absolute constant $C>0$
with $Q(N)\le C\sqrt{N}$ for every positive integer $N$.

2. (Strong form) For every integer $N>6$,

$$
Q(N)=Q(N;24,1),
$$

so that the maximum number of squares in an
$N$-term arithmetic progression is attained by the progression $24n+1$, whose
terms $24n+1$ are squares exactly when $n$ is a generalized pentagonal number.

3. (Super-strong form) If $N=GP_k+1\ge 8$, where $GP_k$ denotes the $k$-th
generalized pentagonal number, then the progression
$24n+1$ is, up to equivalence, the only arithmetic progression of length $N$
with $Q(N;q,a)=Q(N)$; here two progressions $qn+a$ and $q'n+a'$ of length $N$
are equivalent when one is obtained from the other by a common square factor,
i.e. $(q',a')=(d^2q,d^2a)$ for some positive integer $d$, and by reversing the
order of the $N$ terms.

=== 315948: covering problem of Rado ===
For a finite family $\mathcal{S}$ of squares in the plane $\mathbb{R}^2$, all of
whose sides are parallel to the coordinate axes and whose side lengths may
differ, write

$$
|\mathcal{S}| = \operatorname{area}\Big(\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{S}} S\Big).
$$

Call a subfamily
$\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{S}$ independent if the squares belonging to $\mathcal{I}$
are pairwise disjoint; for such a subfamily $|\mathcal{I}|$ is the sum of the
areas of its members. Put

$$
F=\inf_{\mathcal{S}}\ \sup_{\mathcal{I}}\ \frac{|\mathcal{I}|}{|\mathcal{S}|},
$$

where
$\mathcal{S}$ ranges over all finite families of axis-parallel squares with $|\mathcal{S}|>0$
and, for each such $\mathcal{S}$, the subfamily $\mathcal{I}$ ranges over all
independent subfamilies of $\mathcal{S}$.

Then

$$
F=\frac{1}{4}.
$$

Equivalently: every finite family of axis-parallel squares in
$\mathbb{R}^2$ whose union has area $1$ contains a subfamily of pairwise
disjoint squares of total area at least $\tfrac14$, and the constant $\tfrac14$
is largest possible, i.e. for every
$\varepsilon>0$ there is a finite family of axis-parallel squares whose union has area $1$
in which every subfamily of pairwise disjoint squares has total area less than
$\tfrac14+\varepsilon$.

=== 315949: Erdős–Oler conjecture ===
For an integer $n \ge 1$, call a family of $n$ closed disks of radius $1$ in the
Euclidean plane a *packing* of an equilateral triangle
$T$ if every disk is contained in $T$ and the disks have pairwise disjoint
interiors, and set

$$
s(n) = \min\{\, s > 0 : \text{some closed equilateral triangle of side } s \text{ admits a packing by } n \text{ unit disks} \,\},
$$

so that
$s(n)$ is the side length of the smallest equilateral triangle into which $n$
non-overlapping unit circles can be packed. (Equivalently, by scaling,
$1/s(n)$ is the largest $r$ such that $n$ non-overlapping disks of radius $r$
fit inside an equilateral triangle of side $1$.)

For every integer $k \ge 2$ and $n = \binom{k+1}{2} = \frac{k(k+1)}{2}$,

$$
s(n-1) = s(n).
$$

That is, for every triangular number $n$, an optimal packing of $n-1$ unit
circles is obtained by deleting a single circle from an optimal packing of
$n$ unit circles in the triangle of side $s(n)$.

=== 315950: disk covering problem ===

closed unit disk.

$$

the least number of disks of radius $\varepsilon$ whose union contains $D$; here

=== 315951: kissing number problem ===
For an integer $d\ge 1$, call a finite set
$\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}$ admissible if

$$
x_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}x_{n}=1\quad\text{for all }n,\qquad (x_{n}-x_{m})^{\mathsf{T}}(x_{n}-x_{m})\ge 1\quad\text{for all }m\neq n,
$$

that is, if the
$x_{n}$ are unit vectors whose pairwise angular separations are all at least $60^{\circ}$.
Let

$$
\tau_{d}=\max\{N\in\mathbb{N}: \text{there exists an admissible set of } N \text{ vectors in } \mathbb{R}^{d}\},
$$

which is finite by compactness of the unit sphere. Equivalently,
$\tau_{d}$ is the greatest number of closed balls of radius $1$ in
$\mathbb{R}^{d}$ with pairwise disjoint interiors that can all be tangent to one fixed closed ball of radius $1$:
after translating the fixed ball to be centred at the origin and rescaling the
centres of the tangent balls by
$1/2$, tangency to the central ball becomes $\|x_{n}\|=1$ and disjointness of
interiors becomes $\|x_{n}-x_{m}\|\ge 1$.

=== 315972: McMullen problem ===
For an integer $d\ge 1$, a finite set of points in
$\mathbb{R}^d$ is said to be in general position if no $d+1$ of its points lie
in a common hyperplane, and in convex position if every one of its points is a
vertex of the convex hull of the set. A projective transformation of
$\mathbb{R}^d$ is a map of the form

$$
T(x)=\frac{Ax+b}{\langle c,x\rangle+\delta},\qquad \begin{pmatrix}A & b\\ c^{\mathsf T} & \delta\end{pmatrix}\in \mathrm{GL}_{d+1}(\mathbb{R}),\ A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\ b,c\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ \delta\in\mathbb{R},
$$

defined at those $x\in\mathbb{R}^d$ with $\langle c,x\rangle+\delta\neq 0$.

For $d\ge 2$ let $\nu(d)$ be the largest integer $N$ with the property that for
every set $X\subset\mathbb{R}^d$ of $N$ points in general position there exists
a projective transformation $T$ of $\mathbb{R}^d$ that is defined at every point
of $X$ and such that $T(X)$ is in convex position.

Then

$$
\nu(d)=2d+1\qquad\text{for every }d\ge 2;
$$

that is, for every $d\ge 2$ and every set of
$2d+1$ points in general position in $\mathbb{R}^d$ there is a projective
transformation, defined at all these points, carrying them onto the vertex set
of a convex polytope, whereas for each $d\ge 2$ there exists a set of
$2d+2$ points in general position in $\mathbb{R}^d$ admitting no such
transformation.

=== 315974: Orchard-planting problem ===

=== 316009: GNRS conjecture ===

carries the norm $\|x\|_1=\sum_i |x_i|$, has distortion at most

$$
c\,d_X(u,v)\;\le\;\|f(u)-f(v)\|_1\;\le\;C\,d_X(u,v)\qquad\text{for all }u,v\in X,
$$

and the distortion of $(X,d_X)$ into $\ell_1$ is the infimum of the distortions
of all such maps. For a finite undirected graph
$G=(V,E)$ with positive edge weights $w\colon
E\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}$, let $d_{G,w}$ denote the shortest path metric on
$V$, in which $d_{G,w}(u,v)$ is the minimum total weight of a path from
$u$ to $v$.

Let
$\mathcal{F}$ be a family of finite graphs that is closed under taking minors, i.e. closed under deletion of vertices, deletion of edges, and contraction of edges, and suppose $\mathcal{F}$
does not contain all finite graphs. Then there is a constant
$D=D(\mathcal{F})<\infty$, depending only on $\mathcal{F}$, such that for every
$G\in\mathcal{F}$ and every positive edge weighting $w$ of
$G$, the metric space $(V(G),d_{G,w})$ embeds into
$\ell_1$ with distortion at most $D$.

=== 316011: Negami's conjecture ===
All graphs are finite and undirected. For graphs $C$ and $H$, a covering map
from $C$ to $H$ is a surjection $f$ from the vertex set of $C$ onto the vertex
set of $H$ such that, for every vertex $v$ of $C$, $f$ restricts to a bijection
from the set of neighbors of $v$ in $C$ onto the set of neighbors of
$f(v)$ in $H$. If such an $f$ exists, $C$ is called a covering graph of
$H$; if in addition $C$ is planar, then $C$ is a planar cover of $H$. When
$H$ is connected, every vertex of $H$ has the same number of preimages under
$f$, and this number is the ply of the map.

For every connected graph $H$ that has a finite planar cover, $H$ embeds in the
projective plane.

Equivalently, for every connected graph
$H$ admitting a finite planar cover, the minimum ply of a planar cover of $H$ is
$1$ or $2$.

=== 316015: Degree diameter problem ===
For a finite simple undirected graph $G$ let $|V(G)|$ denote its number of
vertices, $\Delta(G)$ its maximum degree, and $\operatorname{diam}(G)$ its
diameter (the maximum, over all pairs of vertices, of the length of a shortest
path joining them). For positive integers $d$ and $k$ set

$$
n_{d,k}=\max\bigl\{\,|V(G)| : G \text{ finite simple undirected},\ \Delta(G)\le d,\ \operatorname{diam}(G)\le k\,\bigr\},
$$

and let

$$
M_{d,k}=1+d\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(d-1)^{i}=\begin{cases}1+d\dfrac{(d-1)^{k}-1}{d-2}, & d>2,\\[6pt] 2k+1, & d=2,\end{cases}
$$

so that $n_{d,k}\le M_{d,k}$ for all $d,k\ge 1$, and $n_{d,1}=d+1$, $n_{1,k}=2$,
$n_{2,k}=2k+1$.

Determine $n_{d,k}$ for all integers $d,k\ge
2$. In particular, for every fixed integer $k\ge 2$,

$$
\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{n_{d,k}}{d^{k}}=1,
$$

equivalently $n_{d,k}=(1-o(1))\,M_{d,k}$ as $d\to\infty$ with
$k$ fixed; that is, writing $\mu_{k}=\liminf_{d\to\infty}
n_{d,k}/d^{k}$, one has $\mu_{k}=1$ for every $k\ge 2$.

A graph $G$ with $\Delta(G)\le d$, $\operatorname{diam}(G)\le
k$ and $|V(G)|=M_{d,k}$ is a Moore graph; such a graph is necessarily
$d$-regular. There exists a Moore graph with $d=57$ and
$k=2$, i.e. a $57$-regular graph on $M_{57,2}=3250$ vertices of diameter $2$.

=== 316017: Gilbert–Pollak conjecture ===
For a finite set $P\subset\mathbb{R}^2$ with $|P|\ge
2$, and for any finite set $Q\subset\mathbb{R}^2$, let

$$
\mathrm{mst}(Q)=\min_{T}\ \sum_{\{u,v\}\in E(T)}\|u-v\|,
$$

the minimum being taken over all spanning trees
$T$ of the complete graph on the vertex set $Q$, where
$\|\cdot\|$ is the Euclidean norm; thus $\mathrm{mst}(P)$ is the length of a
Euclidean minimum spanning tree of
$P$. Define the length of a Steiner minimum tree of $P$ by

$$
\mathrm{smt}(P)=\inf\{\,\mathrm{mst}(P\cup S)\ :\ S\subset\mathbb{R}^2,\ S \text{ finite}\,\},
$$

the points of $S$ being the Steiner points. Then $\mathrm{smt}(P)\le
\mathrm{mst}(P)$ for every such $P$, and

$$
\mathrm{mst}(P)\ \le\ \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\,\mathrm{smt}(P)
$$

for every finite $P\subset\mathbb{R}^2$ with $|P|\ge 2$. Consequently the
Steiner ratio of the Euclidean plane,

$$
\rho=\sup_{P}\ \frac{\mathrm{mst}(P)}{\mathrm{smt}(P)},
$$

the supremum being over all finite $P\subset\mathbb{R}^2$ with $|P|\ge
2$, equals $2/\sqrt{3}$; the value
$2/\sqrt{3}$ is achieved by the three vertices of an equilateral triangle of unit side length, for which $\mathrm{mst}(P)=2$
and $\mathrm{smt}(P)=\sqrt{3}$, the Steiner point being the centroid of the
triangle.

=== 316068: Szemerédi's theorem ===
For a subset $A \subseteq \mathbb{N}$, define its upper density by

$$
\limsup_{n \to \infty} \frac{|A \cap \{1, 2, 3, \dotsc, n\}|}{n},
$$

0$. For an integer $k \geq 1$, an arithmetic progression of length $k$ is a set
of the form $\{a, a+d, a+2d, \dotsc, a+(k-1)d\}$ with $a, d$ positive integers.

positive integer $k$, the set $A$ contains an arithmetic progression of length
$k$.

Equivalently, in finitary form: for every positive integer
$k$ and every real $\delta \in (0,1]$ there exists a positive integer $N_0 =
N_0(k,\delta)$ such that for every $N \geq N_0$, every subset of $\{1, 2,
\dotsc, N\}$ of size at least $\delta
N$ contains an arithmetic progression of length $k$. Writing
$r_k(N)$ for the largest cardinality of a subset of $\{1, 2, \dotsc,
N\}$ containing no arithmetic progression of length $k$, this asserts that for
every fixed $k$,

$$
r_k(N) = o(N) \qquad (N \to \infty).
$$

$$

The paper proves the lower bound $f(r)\geq (3-1/\sqrt{18})r(1-o(1))\approx 2.764r(1-o(1))$, but has no matching upper bound; the conjecture asks for constructions with a linear number of edges.
SOURCE: Toufik Mansour, Chunwei Song and Raphael Yuster, “A comment on Ryser's conjecture for intersecting hypergraphs”, arXiv:0709.3138 (2007). | https://arxiv.org/abs/0709.3138
REFERENCES: Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/0709.3138

Status: **open** according to the source (as of September 2007). “This conjecture is open for all $r \ge 4$.”

=== 316691: The colliding-permutations conjecture ===
Let $[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}$, and let $\rho(n)$ be the maximum cardinality of a set of pairwise colliding permutations of $[n]$, where two permutations are **colliding** if, viewed as linear orderings, they place two consecutive elements of $[n]$ in the same position. **The colliding-permutations conjecture.** For every $n\in\mathbb{N}$,

$$
\rho(n)={n \choose {\left \lfloor \frac{n}{2} \right \rfloor}}.
$$

The cited previous work established the right-hand side as an upper bound, while the lower bounds available there were substantially smaller; the conjecture is the proposed exact value of the extremal quantity.
SOURCE: János Körner, Claudia Malvenuto and Gábor Simonyi, “Graph-different permutations”, arXiv:0709.4387 (2007). | https://arxiv.org/abs/0709.4387
REFERENCES: Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/0709.4387

Status: no resolution is reported in the source (as of September 2007).
