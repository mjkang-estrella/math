Abstract.
    

In this paper, three (q-)log-convexity or concavity properties related to the d-Hoggatt numbers are investigated. First, we show that the d-Hoggatt transformation preserves the log-convexity of a sequence. We then establish the strong q-log-concavity of a q-analog of d-Hoggatt numbers. Finally, we prove the core result of this work, namely, the sequence of d-Hoggatt polynomials is strongly q-log-convex, based on the theory of Schur functions.
  




Key words and phrases: d-Hoggatt number, d-Hoggatt polynomial, d-Hoggatt transform, q-log-convexity, q-log-concavity, Schur positivity.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification05E05, 05A05, 05A20.




1. Introduction




Given a sequence (a_{n})_{n\geq 0} of nonnegative real numbers, we say it is log-concave if the inequality





\displaystyle a_{n}^{2}\geq a_{n-1}a_{n+1}



holds for all n\geq 1, and log-convex if





\displaystyle a_{n-1}a_{n+1}\geq a_{n}^{2}.



If we lift elements in the sequence to polynomials in \mathbb{R}[q] with q an indeterminate and define the q-analog of the order relation “\geq” by f(q)\geq_{q}g(q) provided that f(q)-g(q)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[q], then the concept of log-concavity and log-convexity is naturally extended to q-log-concavity and q-log-convexity. Sagan [13, p. 290] further introduced the nontion of strong q-log-concavity: a sequence (a_{n}(q))_{n\geq 0} of polynomials over \mathbb{R} is strongly q-log-concave if





\displaystyle a_{n}(q)a_{m}(q)\geq_{q}a_{n+1}(q)a_{m-1}(q)



for all n\geq m\geq 1. In the same vein, we say the sequence (a_{n}(q))_{n\geq 0} is strongly q-log-convex if





\displaystyle a_{n+1}(q)a_{m-1}(q)\geq_{q}a_{n}(q)a_{m}(q).



In particular, for ordinary q-log-concavity and q-log-convexity, we only require the two inequalities for the case where m and n are equal.




Inspired by a conjecture of Verner Hoggatt in private communication, Fielder and Alford [8, Section 4] introduced the d-Hoggatt triangle \big({\textstyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}^{(d)}\big)_{0\leq k\leq n}, which specializes to the Pascal triangle \big(\binom{n}{k}\big)_{0\leq k\leq n} at d=1. The original definition of Fielder and Alford [8, p. 163, eq. (13)] is recursive. However, it is easy to derive the following product formula in terms of binomial coefficients from their recurrence relation:





\displaystyle{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}={\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}^{(d)}:=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{\binom{n+j}{k}}{\binom{k+j}{k}},

(1.1)


valid for n,k\geq 0. It is known that the 2- and 3-Hoggatt triangles are the Narayana triangle [14, Entry A001263] and the Baxter triangle [14, Entry A056939], respectively.




For a triangular array of combinatorial numbers (S_{n,k})_{0\leq k\leq n}, we define a linear transformation that maps a sequence (a_{n})_{n\geq 0} to a new sequence (c_{n})_{n\geq 0} by





\displaystyle c_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S_{n,k}a_{k}.



We know from the Davenport–Pólya theorem [7, p. 1, Theorem 2] that if the sequences (a_{n})_{n\geq 0} and (b_{n})_{n\geq 0} are log-convex, then so is their binomial convolution





\displaystyle c_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}a_{k}b_{n-k}.



Motivated by this phenomenon, Liu and Wang [10, p. 474, Conjecture 5.2] conjectured that the Narayana transformation also preserves the log-convexity. This prediction was later confirmed by Chen, Wang, and Yang [3, p. 318, Theorem 4.3]. Quite recently, Liu and Mao [11, Theorem 6.1] further discovered the same behavior of the Baxter transformation. In this work, we provide a unified treatment on the generic d-Hoggatt transformation.




Theorem 1.1.



Let d\geq 1 be fixed. If the sequence (a_{n})_{n\geq 0} of nonnegative real numbers is log-convex, then so is the sequence (c_{n})_{n\geq 0} where





\displaystyle c_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}^{(d)}a_{k}.







Note that in the proof of the Narayana case, the basic idea of Chen, Wang, and Yang builds on the strong q-log-convexity of the Narayana polynomials [3, p. 311, Theorem 3.2], the weaker version of which was first conjectured by Liu and Wang [10, p. 474, Conjecture 5.1]. Later on, Zhu [16, p. 605, Proposition 3.20] offered an alternative proof of this strong q-log-convexity. For Baxter polynomials, we recently witnessed their strong q-log-convexity in the work of Liu and Mao [11, Theorem 5.1]. Now let us define the d-Hoggatt polynomials by





\displaystyle H_{n}(q)=H^{(d)}_{n}(q):=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}^{(d)}q^{k}.



Mao and the second author [12, p. 17] conjectured that \big(H^{(d)}_{n}(q)\big)_{n\geq 0} is q-log-convex for fixed d\geq 1. Here we not only provide an affirmative answer to this problem but also establish a strengthening toward strong q-log-convexity, which will serve as an essential tool in our proof of Theorem 1.1. This is also the most central result of the present work.




Theorem 1.2.



Let d\geq 1 be fixed. The sequence \big(H^{(d)}_{n}(q)\big)_{n\geq 0} is strongly q-log-convex.





Cigler [6] recently considered a q-analog of the d-Hoggatt numbers:11
            1
            
            
            
          We multiply the original definition of Cigler [6, p. 10, eq. (22)] by the prefactor q^{d\binom{k}{2}}. This is consistent with Cigler’s remark on [6, p. 14] and the q-Narayana numbers studied by Chen, Wang, and Yang [3, p. 306].





\displaystyle{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}_{q}={\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}^{(d)}_{q}:=q^{d\binom{k}{2}}\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}\frac{{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n+j}{k}}_{q}}{{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k+j}{k}}_{q}},

(1.2)


where the q-binomial coefficients are given by





\displaystyle{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}_{q}:=\frac{[n]_{q}!}{[k]_{q}![n-k]_{q}!},



with





\displaystyle[m]_{q}:=\frac{1-q^{m}}{1-q},\qquad\qquad[m]_{q}!:=[1]_{q}[2]_{q}\cdots[m]_{q}.






For the sequence \big({\textstyle\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}_{q}\big)_{0\leq k\leq n} of q-binomial coefficients with n fixed, its strong q-log-concavity was shown by Butler [2, p. 59, Theorem 4.2] and Sagan [13, p. 292, Theorem 2.3]. Simultaneously, Butler [2, p. 62, Section 5] proved that the sequence \big({\textstyle\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}_{q}\big)_{n\geq k} with k fixed is strongly q-log-concave via a partition-theoretic approach and Sagan [13, p. 297, Corollary 3.4(2)] provided another proof by the theory of symmetric functions. Around the same time, Krattenthaler [9, p. 334, Theorem 1], independently, established an even stronger result that for n\geq m\geq 0 and k\geq l\geq 0,





\displaystyle{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{l}}_{q}{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{m}{k}}_{q}\geq_{q}{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{l-1}}_{q}{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{m}{k+1}}_{q}.



A parallel inequality





\displaystyle{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{l}}_{q}{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{m}{k}}_{q}\geq_{q}{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{l}}_{q}{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{m-1}{k}}_{q},



valid for nonnegative integers n,m,l,k with k\geq l and n+k\geq m+l, was discovered by the first author [4, p. 34, Theorem 1.3]. Moving forward to the q-Narayana numbers, a similar strong q-log-concavity property was discovered by Chen, Wang, and Yang [3, p. 320, Theorem 5.2 and p. 323, Theorem 5.6] for fixed n or fixed k. Recently, Liu and Mao [11, Theorems 6.6 and 6.7] further proved that for d\geq 1, the q-analog {\textstyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}^{(d)}_{q} of the d-Hoggatt numbers is q-log-concave for fixed n or fixed k. In this work, we strengthen this discovery to strong q-log-concavity.




Theorem 1.3.



Let d\geq 1 and n\geq 0 be fixed. The sequence \big({\textstyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}^{(d)}_{q}\big)_{0\leq k\leq n} is strongly q-log-concave.





Theorem 1.4.



Let d\geq 1 and k\geq 0 be fixed. The sequence \big({\textstyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}^{(d)}_{q}\big)_{n\geq k} is strongly q-log-concave.





Outline of the paper. In Section 2, we study the d-Hoggatt transformation and prove Theorem 1.1 under the assumption of Theorem 1.2. We then collect some preliminary results in Section 3 on the theory of symmetric functions, especially of Schur functions. Building on these results, we demonstrate Theorems 1.3 and 1.4 in Section 4. Finally, we move on to the most difficult task in this work, namely, showing the strong q-log-convexity of the d-Hoggatt polynomials. To begin with, we establish the Schur positivity of an auxiliary family of functions in Section 5. Then Theorem 1.2 will be established in Section 6.




In subsequent sections, we always fix d\geq 1 and omit the superscript “(d)” if the value of d does not interfere with our analysis.





2. d-Hoggatt transformation




In this section, we show that the d-Hoggatt transformation preserves the log-convexity. To begin with, we require the Pochhammer symbols for m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}:





\displaystyle(x)_{m}:=\prod_{k=0}^{m-1}(x+k).



It is clear from (1.1) that





\displaystyle\frac{{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{k}}}{{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}}=\frac{(n+1)_{d}}{(n-k+1)_{d}},\qquad\qquad\frac{{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n-1}{k}}}{{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}}=\frac{(n-k)_{d}}{(n)_{d}}.

(2.1)





For n\geq 1, 0\leq r\leq 2n, and
0\leq k\leq\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor, let us define





\displaystyle\alpha_{k}(n,r):=\begin{cases}{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{k}}{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n-1}{r-k}}+{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{r-k}}{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n-1}{k}}-2{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{r-k}},&\text{if $k<\frac{r}{2}$},\\[6.0pt]
{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{k}}{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n-1}{k}}-{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}^{2},&\text{if $k=\frac{r}{2}$}.\end{cases}

(2.2)


It is simple to verify that





\displaystyle H_{n-1}(q)H_{n+1}(q)-H_{n}(q)^{2}=\sum_{r=0}^{2n}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor}\alpha_{k}(n,r)q^{r}.



In view of the q-log-convexity of (H_{n}(q))_{n\geq 0} stated in Theorem 1.2,





\displaystyle H_{n-1}(q)H_{n+1}(q)-H_{n}(q)^{2}\in\mathbb{N}[q],



we know that





\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor}\alpha_{k}(n,r)\geq 0.

(2.3)


Therefore, if we fix n\geq 1 and 0\leq r\leq 2n, then it is impossible that \alpha_{k}(n,r)<0 for every k with 0\leq k\leq\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor. In what follows, we characterize the sign change of \alpha_{k}(n,r).




Lemma 2.1.



For given n\geq 1 and 0\leq r\leq 2n, there exists an integer k^{\prime}:=k^{\prime}(n,r) such that





\displaystyle\alpha_{k}(n,r)\begin{cases}\geq 0,&\text{when $0\leq k\leq k^{\prime}$},\\
\leq 0,&\text{when $k^{\prime}<k\leq\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor$}.\end{cases}







Proof.



Throughout, we always assume k\geq 0. We start by noticing that





\displaystyle\alpha_{k}(n,r)\begin{cases}=0,&\text{when $k\leq r-n-2$},\\
>0,&\text{when $k=r-n-1$}.\end{cases}



For the first situation, we use the fact that {\textstyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n-1}{r-k}}={\textstyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{r-k}}={\textstyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{r-k}}=0 when k\leq r-n-2. For the second situation, we note that k\neq\frac{r}{2} and k\leq n-1 because r\leq 2n. Thus, \alpha_{k}(n,r)={\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n-1}{k}}>0. In the sequel, suppose k\geq r-n. Let us introduce an auxiliary sequence indexed by k:





\displaystyle\beta_{k}(n,r):=\frac{(n-k)_{d}}{(n-r+k+1)_{d}}+\frac{(n-r+k)_{d}}{(n-k+1)_{d}}.



In light of (2.1),





\displaystyle\frac{n+d}{n}\beta_{k}(n,r)-2=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{r-k}}}\alpha_{k}(n,r),&\text{if $k\leq\frac{r}{2}$},\\[10.0pt]
\frac{2}{{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}^{2}}\alpha_{k}(n,r),&\text{if $k=\frac{r}{2}$}.\end{cases}



For consecutive indices k,k+1 satisfying k\geq r-n and k+1\leq\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor, we have





\displaystyle n-k>n-r+k+1\geq 1,



so that





\displaystyle(n-k)_{d-1}(n-k)_{d+1}\geq(n-r+k+1)_{d-1}(n-r+k+1)_{d+1},



which further gives us the nonnegativity of





\displaystyle\beta_{k}(n,r)-\beta_{k+1}(n,r)=d(2n-r+d)\left(\frac{(n-k)_{d-1}}{(n-r+k+1)_{d+1}}-\frac{(n-r+k+1)_{d-1}}{(n-k)_{d+1}}\right).



Thus, the numbers \beta_{k}(n,r) are nonincreasing in k, and so are \frac{n+d}{n}\beta_{k}(n,r)-2. We conclude that when k runs from 0 to \lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor, the sign of \alpha_{k}(n,r) changes at most once, from nonnegative to nonpositive. The claimed characterization then follows.
∎





Now we are in a position to prove Theorem 1.1.




Proof of Theorem 1.1.



Note that





\displaystyle c_{n-1}c_{n+1}-c_{n}^{2}=\sum_{r=0}^{2n}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor}\alpha_{k}(n,r)a_{k}a_{r-k}.



Since (a_{k})_{k\geq 0} is a log-convex sequence of nonnegative real numbers, we obtain that





\displaystyle a_{0}a_{r}\geq a_{1}a_{r-1}\geq\cdots\geq a_{\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor}a_{r-\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor}\geq 0.



Now choosing k^{\prime} as in Lemma 2.1, we see that for every k with 0\leq k\leq\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor,





\displaystyle\alpha_{k}(n,r)(a_{k}a_{r-k}-a_{k^{\prime}}a_{r-k^{\prime}})\geq 0.



Therefore,





\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor}\alpha_{k}(n,r)a_{k}a_{r-k}=a_{k^{\prime}}a_{r-k^{\prime}}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor}\alpha_{k}(n,r)+\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor}\alpha_{k}(n,r)(a_{k}a_{r-k}-a_{k^{\prime}}a_{r-k^{\prime}})\geq 0,



where we have recalled (2.3) for the nonnegativity of the first sum. Summing over r gives the desired log-convexity of c_{n}.
∎






3. Preliminaries on Schur functions




In this section, we give a brief overview of the theory of Schur functions, most of which can be found in the monograph of Stanley [15, Chapter 7].




We begin with the combinatorial setting. A partition is a weakly decreasing sequence \lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{l}) of positive integers. If the sequence is empty, we say this partition is the empty partition \varnothing. We write |\lambda| for its size \sum_{i=1}^{l}\lambda_{i}. Also, the length of \lambda, denoted by \mathsf{len}(\lambda), is defined as l, and the width is defined as \lambda_{1}. When an index i exceeds the length of \lambda, the corresponding part \lambda_{i} is understood to be 0. The Young diagram of \lambda is obtained by depicting left-aligned boxes in rows such that there are \lambda_{i} boxes in the i-th row. By flipping the Young diagram along the main diagonal, we obtain the Young diagram of a new partition, which is called the conjugate of \lambda, denoted by \lambda^{\mathsf{T}}. For two partitions \mu and \lambda, the notation \mu\subseteq\lambda means \mu_{i}\leq\lambda_{i} for every index i. The skew diagram \lambda/\mu is obtained by removing the Young diagram of \mu from the upper-left corner of the diagram of \lambda. We also write (\lambda/\mu)^{\mathsf{T}}:=\lambda^{\mathsf{T}}/\mu^{\mathsf{T}}.




Assuming \mu\subseteq\lambda, a semistandard Young tableau of shape \lambda/\mu is a filling T of the cells in the skew diagram of \lambda/\mu by positive integers that is weakly increasing from left to right in every row and strictly increasing from top to bottom in every column. Its type is the sequence (m_{1},m_{2},\ldots), where m_{i} denotes the multiplicity of i in the filling T. Let X:=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots) be a list of variables, known as an alphabet, and put





\displaystyle X^{T}:=\prod_{i\geq 1}x_{i}^{m_{i}}.



The combinatorial definition of the skew Schur function indexed by \lambda/\mu is [15, p. 310, Definition 7.10.1]:





\displaystyle s_{\lambda/\mu}=s_{\lambda/\mu}(X):=\sum_{T}X^{T},



summing over all semistandard Young tableaux of shape \lambda/\mu. For \mu=\varnothing, we have the ordinary Schur function s_{\lambda}=s_{\lambda}(X). We also use the convention s_{\lambda/\mu}=0 if \mu\not\subseteq\lambda.




Schur functions play a central role in the theory of symmetric functions. Let \Lambda be the ring of symmetric functions over \mathbb{Z}. An important fact is that the Schur functions s_{\lambda} form a basis for \Lambda; see [15, p. 315, Corollary 7.10.6]. Moreover, this basis is orthogonal, given by the Hall inner product [15, p. 336]:





\displaystyle\big\langle s_{\lambda},s_{\mu}\big\rangle=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $\lambda=\mu$},\\
0,&\text{if $\lambda\neq\mu$}.\end{cases}



Let F=\sum_{\lambda}a_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}\in\Lambda be arbitrary, expanded by the Schur basis. We write





\displaystyle[s_{\lambda}]F:=a_{\lambda}=\big\langle s_{\lambda},F\big\rangle.



If [s_{\lambda}]F\geq 0 for every partition \lambda, then we say F is Schur positive, expressed by F\geq_{s}0.




We know from [15, p. 338, eq. (7.64)] that





\displaystyle s_{\mu}s_{\nu}
\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}s_{\lambda},

(3.1)



\displaystyle s_{\lambda/\nu}
\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}s_{\mu},

(3.2)



\displaystyle s_{\lambda/\mu}
\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu}c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}s_{\nu},

(3.3)


using the same Littlewood–Richardson coefficients c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}. In particular, for arbitrary F\in\Lambda,





\displaystyle\big\langle s_{\lambda/\mu},F\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\lambda},s_{\mu}F\big\rangle,

(3.4)


both equal to \sum_{\nu}\big(c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}\cdot[s_{\nu}]F\big).




To further understand the Littlewood–Richardson coefficients, we require the Littlewood–Richardson rule. We first obtain the reverse row word of a tableau by reading each row from right to left, beginning with the top row and then proceeding downward. Moreover, a word is a lattice word if in every initial segment, the multiplicity of i is at least the multiplicity of i+1 for every i\geq 1. Now a Littlewood–Richardson tableau is a semistandard Young tableau whose reverse row word is a lattice word. It follows from this definition that the type of a Littlewood–Richardson tableau forms a partition.




The Littlewood–Richardson rule [15, p. 432, Theorem A1.3.3] states that c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda} equals the number of Littlewood–Richardson tableaux of shape \lambda/\mu and type \nu, yielding that c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}\geq 0, and hence that


(1) 



Every skew Schur function is Schur positive;


(2) 



A product of Schur positive symmetric functions is
Schur positive.






We collect some additional facts about the Littlewood–Richardson tableaux and coefficients.




Lemma 3.1.



Every entry in the i-th row of a Littlewood–Richardson tableau is no larger than i. Also, if c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}>0, then \mu,\nu\subseteq\lambda and for every i\geq 1,





\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\leq\mu_{1}+\nu_{i}.

(3.5)






Proof.



We prove the first assertion inductively on i. For the first row, the rightmost cell must be filled with 1 by the Littlewood–Richardson condition, and hence all entries in this row are 1 by weak monotonicity. Suppose all entries in rows above the i-th row are at most i-1. Then the rightmost entry in the i-th row cannot exceed i; otherwise, the Littlewood–Richardson condition is violated at this cell. We then conclude that all entries in this row are at most i by weak monotonicity.




For the second assertion, since c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}>0, we have at least one Littlewood–Richardson tableau of shape \lambda/\mu and type \nu. Then \mu\subseteq\lambda from the shape. Meanwhile, in (3.1), switching \mu and \nu does not change the left-hand side but turns c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda} to c_{\nu,\mu}^{\lambda} on the right-hand side. Thus,





\displaystyle c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}=c_{\nu,\mu}^{\lambda}.



Then \nu\subseteq\lambda is also true. Next, if \lambda_{i}\leq\mu_{1}, then (3.5) is immediate. If \lambda_{i}>\mu_{1}, then in the \lambda_{i}-\mu_{1} columns indexed from \mu_{1}+1 to \lambda_{i}, there are skew cells in all rows from the first to the i-th. In each of these columns, column strictness makes the entry in the i-th row at least i, while the first part of the lemma makes it at most i, so this entry has to be i. Then there are at least \lambda_{i}-\mu_{1} cells filled with i, thereby yielding (3.5).
∎





Define the rectangular partition





\displaystyle(m^{l}):=(\underbrace{m,\ldots,m}_{\text{$l$ parts}}).



The complete homogeneous symmetric functions h_{r} and the elementary symmetric functions e_{r}, defined respectively by





\displaystyle h_{r}:=s_{(r)},\qquad\qquad e_{r}:=s_{(1^{r})},

(3.6)


are the most basic Schur functions indexed by rectangular partitions. Here we adopt the convention that h_{0}=e_{0}=1 and h_{r}=e_{r}=0 for r<0.




We also need a special shape of skew diagrams for later use. A skew diagram \lambda/\mu is a horizontal strip if it has at most one cell in each column. We write





“\lambda/\mu\,\,\mathrm{HS}”



for this condition. In particular, this condition is equivalent to





\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\geq\mu_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\mu_{2}\geq\lambda_{3}\geq\cdots.

(3.7)


According to the product form of the Pieri rule [15, p. 339, Theorem 7.15.7],





\displaystyle h_{r}s_{\mu}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\colon\lambda/\mu\,\,\mathrm{HS}\\
|\lambda|-|\mu|=r\end{subarray}}s_{\lambda}.

(3.8)


Also, the skew form of the Pieri rule [15, p. 340, Corollary 7.15.9] states that





\displaystyle s_{\lambda/(r)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\colon\lambda/\mu\,\,\mathrm{HS}\\
|\lambda|-|\mu|=r\end{subarray}}s_{\mu}.

(3.9)





Now we move on to the Schur positivity related to rectangular partitions.




Lemma 3.2.



Let u\geq 1 and a\geq b\geq 1. Then





\displaystyle s_{(a^{u})}s_{(b^{u})}-s_{((a+1)^{u})}s_{((b-1)^{u})}\geq_{s}0.

(3.10)






Proof.



By (3.1),





\displaystyle s_{(a^{u})}s_{(b^{u})}-s_{((a+1)^{u})}s_{((b-1)^{u})}=\sum_{\lambda}\big(c_{(a^{u}),(b^{u})}^{\lambda}-c_{((a+1)^{u}),((b-1)^{u})}^{\lambda}\big)s_{\lambda}.



It is sufficient to show that for every partition \lambda,





\displaystyle c_{(a^{u}),(b^{u})}^{\lambda}-c_{((a+1)^{u}),((b-1)^{u})}^{\lambda}\geq 0.

(3.11)


If ((a+1)^{u})\not\subseteq\lambda, then c_{((a+1)^{u}),((b-1)^{u})}^{\lambda}=0 according to the Littlewood–Richardson rule, and hence (3.11) holds. Now assume that ((a+1)^{u})\subseteq\lambda so that \lambda_{i}\geq a+1 for every index i with 1\leq i\leq u. If c_{((a+1)^{u}),((b-1)^{u})}^{\lambda}=0, then we still have nothing to prove. Hence, we further assume that c_{((a+1)^{u}),((b-1)^{u})}^{\lambda}>0. We claim that \lambda_{u+1}<a+1. Note that the Littlewood–Richardson rule ensures the existence of a Littlewood–Richardson tableau of shape \lambda/((a+1)^{u}) and type ((b-1)^{u}). By column strictness and the first assertion in Lemma 3.1, we know that all cells in the i-th row of \lambda/((a+1)^{u}) are filled with i. Also, (3.5) in Lemma 3.1 tells us that \lambda_{i}\leq(a+1)+(b-1)=a+b. Next, by the Littlewood–Richardson condition, we may fill the (u+1)-th row of \lambda/((a+1)^{u}) with u of multiplicity at most \lambda_{u-1}-\lambda_{u}, preceded by u-1 of multiplicity at most \lambda_{u-2}-\lambda_{u-1}, and so forth, till 1 of multiplicity \lambda_{0}-\lambda_{1} where \lambda_{0}=a+b. Thus,





\displaystyle\lambda_{u+1}\leq\lambda_{0}-\lambda_{u}\leq(a+b)-(a+1)=b-1<a+1



since b\leq a. Now for any Littlewood–Richardson tableau of shape \lambda/((a+1)^{u}) and type ((b-1)^{u}), we can injectively obtain a Littlewood–Richardson tableau of shape \lambda/(a^{u}) and type (b^{u}) by preserving the fillings of the former tableau and then filling the (a+1)-th column of \lambda/(a^{u}), which contains exactly u cells, with 1,2,\ldots,u, from top to bottom. Therefore, (3.11) is also valid in this situation.
∎





Let \omega be the standard involution on \Lambda, defined by





\displaystyle\omega(s_{\lambda}):=s_{\lambda^{\mathsf{T}}},



which preserves the Schur positivity. Since \omega is a ring automorphism, it is true that for any F,G\in\Lambda,





\displaystyle\omega(FG)=\omega(F)\omega(G).

(3.12)


Also, we know from [15, p. 338, Theorem 7.15.6] that





\displaystyle\omega(s_{\lambda/\mu})=s_{\lambda^{\mathsf{T}}/\mu^{\mathsf{T}}}.

(3.13)





We then arrive at a companion to Lemma 3.2, which also appeared in [1, Remark 7.2].




Lemma 3.3.



Let u\geq 1 and a\geq b\geq 1. Then





\displaystyle s_{(u^{a})}s_{(u^{b})}-s_{(u^{a+1})}s_{(u^{b-1})}\geq_{s}0.

(3.14)






Proof.



Applying \omega to the left-hand side of (3.10) produces the left-hand side of (3.14), where we have used (3.12). Since \omega preserves the Schur positivity, the desired statement follows from Lemma 3.2.
∎





The preceding content works for generic alphabets X=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots). Now let X_{N}:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}) be finite. The specialization map \operatorname{sp}_{N} sets all variables after x_{N} to 0, so that





\displaystyle\operatorname{sp}_{N}(F):=F(X_{N}).



We introduce a companion operator that will be frequently utilized:





\displaystyle\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N}(F):=\operatorname{sp}_{N}\big(\omega(F)\big).



In particular, (3.12) tells us that for any F,G\in\Lambda,





\displaystyle\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N}(FG)=\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N}(F)\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N}(G).

(3.15)


Finally, we define the projection onto Schur functions of width at most N by





\displaystyle\pi_{N}\left(\sum_{\lambda}a_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}\right):=\sum_{\lambda\colon\lambda_{1}\leq N}a_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}.






Let





\displaystyle I_{N}:=\operatorname{span}\{s_{\lambda}\colon\lambda_{1}>N\}.






Lemma 3.4.



The span I_{N} is an ideal of \Lambda.





Proof.



Suppose s_{\lambda} occurs in the Schur expansion of s_{\mu}s_{\nu} so that c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}>0. According to Lemma 3.1, \mu,\nu\subseteq\lambda. Thus, if \mu_{1}>1 or \nu_{1}>1, we must have \lambda_{1}>1. This means that multiplication of an element of I_{n} and an arbitrary symmetric function in \Lambda remains in I_{N}, thereby confirming that I_{N} is an ideal.
∎





Lemma 3.5.



Both \operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N} and \pi_{N} annihilate all functions in I_{N}.





Proof.



The annihilation of \pi_{N} follows by definition. Next, \operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N}(s_{\lambda})=s_{\lambda^{\mathsf{T}}}(X_{N}), which vanishes when \mathsf{len}(\lambda^{\mathsf{T}})>N since in this case the first column already requires more than N distinct variables. In this case, we have \lambda_{1}>N, yielding the annihilation of \operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N} on I_{N}.
∎





Lemma 3.6.



For any F\in\Lambda,





\displaystyle\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N}(F)=\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N}\big(\pi_{N}(F)\big).

(3.16)


Also, \operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N} is injective on the span of s_{\lambda} with \lambda_{1}\leq N.





Proof.



Note that





\displaystyle F=\pi_{N}(F)+\big(F-\pi_{N}(F)\big),



with F-\pi_{N}(F)\in I_{N}. We then use the annihilation of \operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N} on I_{N} to obtain the claimed relation (3.16). For the second assertion, we only need the fact that the Schur polynomials s_{\lambda^{\mathsf{T}}}(X_{N}) with \lambda_{1}\leq N form a basis of symmetric polynomials in N variables.
∎





Lemma 3.7.



For any F,G\in\Lambda,





\displaystyle\pi_{N}(FG)=\pi_{N}\big(F\pi_{N}(G)\big).

(3.17)






Proof.



Note that





\displaystyle FG=F\cdot\pi_{N}(G)+F\cdot\big(G-\pi_{N}(G)\big).



Here G-\pi_{N}(G) is in I_{N}, and so is F\cdot\big(G-\pi_{N}(G)\big) since I_{N} is an ideal of \Lambda. Applying \pi_{N} to both sides and using the annihilation of \pi_{N} on I_{N}, the claimed relation follows.
∎





Finally, we show how Schur functions are connected with d-Hoggatt numbers and their q-analogs. To begin with, we specify two alphabets:





\displaystyle Q_{N}:=(1,q,\ldots,q^{N-1}),\qquad\qquad 1_{N}:=(\underbrace{1,1,\ldots,1}_{\text{$N$ copies}}).






Lemma 3.8.



We have





\displaystyle s_{(d^{k})}(Q_{n})={\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}_{q}^{(d)}.

(3.18)


In particular,





\displaystyle s_{(d^{k})}(1_{n})={\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}^{(d)}.

(3.19)






Proof.



By the hook-content formula [15, p. 374, Theorem 7.21.2],





\displaystyle s_{(d^{k})}(Q_{n})=q^{d\binom{k}{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{[n+j-i]_{q}}{[d+k-i-j-1]_{q}}=q^{d\binom{k}{2}}\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{[n-k+i+j]_{q}}{[i+j]_{q}}.



To get the last equality, we have interchanged the products, and then made the change of indices (i,j)\mapsto(k-i+1,j+1) for the numerator and (i,j)\mapsto(k-i+1,d-j) for the denominator. Now





\displaystyle s_{(d^{k})}(Q_{n})=q^{d\binom{k}{2}}\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}\frac{{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n+j}{k}}_{q}}{{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k+j}{k}}_{q}},



thereby giving (3.18) by recalling (1.2). For (3.19), we only need the specialization at q=1.
∎





Lemma 3.9.



We have, for r\geq 0,





\displaystyle s_{(r^{k})}(Q_{d+k})=q^{(r-d)\binom{k}{2}}{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k+r}{k}}_{q}^{(d)}.

(3.20)






Proof.



If r=0, then both sides of (3.20) are 1. Now assume r\geq 1. We have shown in the preceding proof that





\displaystyle s_{(r^{k})}(Q_{d+k})=q^{r\binom{k}{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{j=0}^{r-1}\frac{[d+i+j]_{q}}{[i+j]_{q}}.



Note that for each i,





\displaystyle\prod_{j=0}^{r-1}\frac{[d+i+j]_{q}}{[i+j]_{q}}=\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}\frac{[r+i+j]_{q}}{[i+j]_{q}}.



Thus,





\displaystyle s_{(r^{k})}(Q_{d+k})=q^{r\binom{k}{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}\frac{[r+i+j]_{q}}{[i+j]_{q}}=q^{r\binom{k}{2}}\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}\frac{{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k+r+j}{k}}_{q}}{{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k+j}{k}}_{q}},



which yields (3.20) in light of (1.2).
∎






4. Strong q-log-concavity of the q-analog of d-Hoggatt numbers




4.1. Proof of Theorem 1.3




Let k,l be such that 0<l\leq k<n. It follows from (3.18) that





\displaystyle{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}_{q}{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{l}}_{q}-{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k+1}}_{q}{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{l-1}}_{q}=s_{(d^{k})}(Q_{n})s_{(d^{l})}(Q_{n})-s_{(d^{k+1})}(Q_{n})s_{(d^{l-1})}(Q_{n}).



Now by Lemma 3.3, the right-hand side of the above is in \mathbb{N}[q], thereby implying the strong q-log-concavity of \big({\textstyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}_{q}\big)_{0\leq k\leq n}.





4.2. Proof of Theorem 1.4




Let m,n be such that n\geq m>k. If k=0, we have





\displaystyle{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}_{q}{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{m}{k}}_{q}-{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{k}}_{q}{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{m-1}{k}}_{q}=0.



Now assume k\geq 1. It follows from (3.20) that






\displaystyle{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}_{q}{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{m}{k}}_{q}-{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{k}}_{q}{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{m-1}{k}}_{q}





\displaystyle\ \ =q^{(2d-n_{\dagger}-m_{\dagger})\binom{k}{2}}\big(s_{(n_{\dagger}^{k})}(Q_{d+k})s_{(m_{\dagger}^{k})}(Q_{d+k})-s_{((n_{\dagger}+1)^{k})}(Q_{d+k})s_{((m_{\dagger}-1)^{k})}(Q_{d+k})\big),



where m_{\dagger}:=m-k and n_{\dagger}:=n-k. Now by Lemma 3.2, the second factor on the right-hand side of the above is in \mathbb{N}[q]. Noting that the left-hand side is in \mathbb{Z}[q] by definition, we conclude that the left-hand side must be in \mathbb{N}[q] because multiplying by the prefactor q^{(2d-n_{\dagger}-m_{\dagger})\binom{k}{2}} does not affect the signs of the coefficients. We then arrive at the strong q-log-concavity of \big({\textstyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}}_{q}\big)_{n\geq k}.






5. Schur positivity of an auxiliary family of functions




In the next two sections, we set two types of partitions:





\displaystyle R_{l}:=(\underbrace{d,\ldots,d}_{\text{$l$ parts}}),\qquad\qquad P_{l}^{(j)}:=(\underbrace{d,\ldots,d}_{\text{$l$ parts}},j),



where 0\leq j\leq d. Note that R_{l}=P_{l}^{(0)} and R_{l+1}=P_{l}^{(d)}. We adopt the convention that all expressions containing R_{l} or P_{l}^{(j)} with l<0 are assumed to be 0. For a partition \beta and an integer t\geq 0, define the auxiliary functions





\displaystyle\Phi_{t,j}^{\beta}:=\sum_{u+v=t}\big(s_{P_{u}^{(j)}/\beta}s_{R_{v}}-s_{R_{u}/\beta}s_{P_{v}^{(j)}}\big).

(5.1)





The purpose of this section is to establish the following Schur positivity property.




Theorem 5.1.



For any j and t with 1\leq j\leq d-1 and t\geq 0, and any partition \beta,





\displaystyle\Phi_{t,j}^{\beta}\geq_{s}0.

(5.2)






We shall prove this theorem by induction on t. When t=0,





\displaystyle\Phi_{0,j}^{\beta}=\begin{cases}s_{(j)/\beta},&\text{if $\beta\neq\varnothing$},\\
0,&\text{if $\beta=\varnothing$},\end{cases}



which is clearly Schur positive. Now assume that the theorem is true for 0,\ldots,t-1 with t\geq 1. It suffices to show that the coefficients in the Schur expansion of \Phi_{t,j}^{\beta} satisfy





\displaystyle[s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{t,j}^{\beta}\geq 0

(5.3)


for every partition \alpha.




Now there are two cases, depending on whether \alpha contains parts of size d or not. To facilitate our analysis, we introduce a linear map





\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{cccc}\Delta_{d}\colon&\Lambda&\to&\Lambda\\
&s_{\lambda}&\mapsto&s_{d\cup\lambda}\end{array}



with d\cup\lambda the partition derived by adjoining a part of size d in \lambda and rearranging the parts in weakly decreasing order. We will see how adjoining a part d behaves after the projection \pi_{d}.




Lemma 5.2.



For any F,G\in\Lambda,





\displaystyle\pi_{d}\big(F\Delta_{d}(G)\big)=\Delta_{d}\big(\pi_{d}(FG)\big).

(5.4)






Proof.



We start with an auxiliary relation that for any F\in\Lambda,





\displaystyle\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}\big(\Delta_{d}(\pi_{d}(F))\big)=e_{d}(X_{d})\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}(F).

(5.5)


By the linearity of the operators, it suffices to prove (5.5) for every Schur polynomial s_{\lambda}. If \lambda_{1}>d, we have \pi_{d}(s_{\lambda})=0, and further \operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}(s_{\lambda})=\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}\big(\pi_{d}(s_{\lambda})\big)=0 by (3.16), so that both sides of (5.5) vanishes. Now assume \lambda_{1}\leq d. We have





\displaystyle\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}\big(\Delta_{d}(\pi_{d}(s_{\lambda}))\big)=\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}(s_{d\cup\lambda})=s_{(d\cup\lambda)^{\mathsf{T}}}(X_{d}).



Note that in d variables, there is only one way to fill the first column of (d\cup\lambda)^{\mathsf{T}}, namely, by filling in 1 through n from top to bottom. By doing so, we factor out e_{d}(X_{d})=s_{(1^{d})}(X_{d}) and \operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}(s_{\lambda}), and hence arrive at the desired relation (5.5) for F=s_{\lambda},





\displaystyle\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}\big(\Delta_{d}(\pi_{d}(s_{\lambda}))\big)=e_{d}(X_{d})\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}(s_{\lambda}).



Now moving back to (5.4), we see that both sides are in the span of s_{\lambda} with \lambda_{1}\leq d.
Using (5.5) twice and recalling (3.15),





\displaystyle\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}\big(\Delta_{d}(\pi_{d}(FG))\big)
\displaystyle=e_{d}(X_{d})\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}(FG)





\displaystyle=e_{d}(X_{d})\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}(F)\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}(G)





\displaystyle=\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}(F)\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}\big(\Delta_{d}(\pi_{d}(G))\big).



It is clear that G-\pi_{d}(G) is in I_{d}, and so is \Delta_{d}(G)-\Delta_{d}(\pi_{d}(G)). By Lemma 3.5, this difference is annihilated by \operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}. Therefore, \operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}\big(\Delta_{d}(\pi_{d}(G))\big)=\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}\big(\Delta_{d}(G)\big), so that





\displaystyle\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}\big(\Delta_{d}(\pi_{d}(FG))\big)=\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}(F)\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}\big(\Delta_{d}(G)\big)=\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}\big(F\Delta_{d}(G)\big)=\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}\big(\pi_{d}(F\Delta_{d}(G))\big),



where we have used (3.15) for the second equality and (3.16) for the last equality. Now the claimed identity (5.4) follows from the injectivity of \operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d} in Lemma 3.6.
∎





In the following two subsections, we fix j with 1\leq j\leq d-1 and put





\displaystyle d^{\prime}:=d-j.



Before analyzing the two cases of \alpha individually, we record a useful fact about rotated skew diagrams inside a rectangular partition. It is known that the skew Schur function of a diagram is unchanged when the diagram is rotated through 180 degrees. This is [15, p. 467, Exerise 7.56(a)] with its proof given on [15, p. 516].




Lemma 5.3.



Let \beta\subseteq R_{u}=(d^{u}) so that \mathsf{len}(\beta)\leq u, and define





\displaystyle\kappa:=(d-\beta_{u},d-\beta_{u-1},\ldots,d-\beta_{1}).



Then





\displaystyle s_{R_{u}/\beta}=s_{\kappa},\qquad s_{R_{u+1}/\beta}=s_{d\cup\kappa},\qquad s_{P_{u}^{(j)}/\beta}=s_{(d\cup\kappa)/(d^{\prime})}.

(5.6)






Proof.



Since \beta is inside R_{u}, it is also inside R_{u+1} and P_{u}^{(j)}. Note that R_{u}/\beta is obtained by rotating \kappa through 180 degrees, and hence s_{R_{u}/\beta}=s_{\kappa}. The remaining two relations can be derived in the same way.
∎





Finally, we introduce two auxiliary coefficients for u,v\geq 0 and an arbitrary partition \lambda:





\displaystyle\begin{aligned} A_{u,v}(\lambda)&:=\big\langle s_{\lambda},s_{P_{u}^{(j)}/\beta}s_{R_{v}}\big\rangle\geq 0,\\
B_{u,v}(\lambda)&:=\big\langle s_{\lambda},s_{R_{u}/\beta}s_{P_{v}^{(j)}}\big\rangle\geq 0,\end{aligned}

(5.7)


with the nonnegativity coming from the Schur positivity of s_{P_{u}^{(j)}/\beta}s_{R_{v}} and s_{R_{u}/\beta}s_{P_{v}^{(j)}}. In addition, we adopt the convention that A_{u,v}(\lambda)=B_{u,v}(\lambda)=0 if u<0 or v<0.




Lemma 5.4.



For any partition \lambda with p_{\lambda}:=\#\{i\colon\lambda_{i}>d\}, we have





\displaystyle\begin{cases}A_{u,v}(\lambda)=0,&\text{if $v<p_{\lambda}$},\\
B_{u,v}(\lambda)=0,&\text{if $v<p_{\lambda}-1$}.\end{cases}

(5.8)






Proof.



By (3.2) and (3.1),





\displaystyle s_{P_{u}^{(j)}/\beta}s_{R_{v}}=\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu,\beta}^{P_{u}^{(j)}}s_{\mu}s_{R_{v}}=\sum_{\lambda}\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu,\beta}^{P_{u}^{(j)}}c_{\mu,R_{v}}^{\lambda}s_{\lambda},



so that





\displaystyle A_{u,v}(\lambda)=\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu,\beta}^{P_{u}^{(j)}}c_{\mu,R_{v}}^{\lambda}.

(5.9)


To ensure A_{u,v}(\lambda)>0, we need to find a certain \mu such that c_{\mu,\beta}^{P_{u}^{(j)}}>0 and c_{\mu,R_{v}}^{\lambda}>0 hold simultaneously. For the first inequality, Lemma 3.1 tells us that \mu\subseteq P_{u}^{(j)} so that \mu_{1}\leq d. For the second inequality, we use (3.5) in Lemma 3.1 to get \lambda_{v+1}\leq\mu_{1}+(R_{v})_{v+1}=\mu_{1}. Thus, \lambda_{v+1}\leq d. However, if v<p_{\lambda}, then \lambda_{v+1}>d by the definition of p_{\lambda}. This produces a contradiction, and hence A_{u,v}(\lambda)=0 when v<p_{\lambda}. For the second statement involving B_{u,v}(\lambda), we apply a similar analysis but this time to \lambda_{v+2}.
∎





5.1. The partition \alpha contains parts of size d




Throughout this subsection, since \alpha contains parts of size d, we always write it as





\displaystyle\alpha:=(d+\tau_{1},\ldots,d+\tau_{p},d,\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{r}),

(5.10)


where \tau:=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{p}) is a partition with exactly p positive parts, and \eta:=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{r}) is a partition with width \eta_{1}\leq d. Define





\displaystyle\alpha^{-}:=(d+\tau_{1},\ldots,d+\tau_{p},\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{r}),



by removing the displayed part of size d.




Lemma 5.5.



For any partition \lambda with \lambda_{1}\leq d,





\displaystyle\big\langle s_{\lambda},s_{\alpha/P_{p}^{(j)}}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\lambda/(d^{\prime})},s_{\alpha^{-}/R_{p}}\big\rangle.

(5.11)






Proof.



The skew diagram \alpha/P_{p}^{(j)} can be split into two disjoint groups, one of shape \tau to the right of the rectangle R_{p} in the first p rows and the other of shape (d\cup\eta)/(j) below the same rectangle. Moreover, the two groups impose no row or column comparison. Hence,





\displaystyle s_{\alpha/P_{p}^{(j)}}=s_{\tau}s_{(d\cup\eta)/(j)}.

(5.12)


In a similar vein,





\displaystyle s_{\alpha^{-}/R_{p}}=s_{\tau}s_{\eta}.

(5.13)


By (3.9),





\displaystyle s_{(d\cup\eta)/(j)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\colon(d\cup\eta)/\xi\,\,\mathrm{HS}\\
|d\cup\eta|-|\xi|=j\end{subarray}}s_{\xi}.



Here, the second condition on \xi is the same as |\xi|-|\eta|=d-j=d^{\prime}. Moreover, the first condition, by (3.7), is equivalent to d\geq\xi_{1}\geq\eta_{1}\geq\xi_{2}\geq\eta_{2}\geq\cdots, and by (3.7) again, to \xi/\eta being a horizontal strip with \xi_{1}\leq d. It follows that





\displaystyle s_{(d\cup\eta)/(j)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\colon\xi/\eta\,\,\mathrm{HS}\\
|\xi|-|\eta|=d^{\prime}\\
\xi_{1}\leq d\end{subarray}}s_{\xi}.



On the other hand, by (3.8),





\displaystyle h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\eta}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\colon\xi/\eta\,\,\mathrm{HS}\\
|\xi|-|\eta|=d^{\prime}\end{subarray}}s_{\xi}.



We then derive that





\displaystyle s_{(d\cup\eta)/(j)}\equiv h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\eta}\pmod{I_{d}},



and further from the fact that I_{d} is an ideal stated in Lemma 3.4 that





\displaystyle s_{\tau}s_{(d\cup\eta)/(j)}\equiv h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\tau}s_{\eta}\pmod{I_{d}}.



Recalling the assumption \lambda_{1}\leq d and using the orthogonality of the Hall inner product, we have





\displaystyle\big\langle s_{\lambda},s_{\tau}s_{(d\cup\eta)/(j)}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\lambda},h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\tau}s_{\eta}\big\rangle.

(5.14)


Finally, noting that h_{d^{\prime}}=s_{(d^{\prime})} in (3.6), the claimed relation follows from





\displaystyle\big\langle s_{\lambda},s_{\alpha/P_{p}^{(j)}}\big\rangle
\displaystyle=\big\langle s_{\lambda},s_{\tau}s_{(d\cup\eta)/(j)}\big\rangle





\displaystyle=\big\langle s_{\lambda},h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\tau}s_{\eta}\big\rangle





\displaystyle=\big\langle s_{\lambda/(d^{\prime})},s_{\tau}s_{\eta}\big\rangle





\displaystyle=\big\langle s_{\lambda/(d^{\prime})},s_{\alpha^{-}/R_{p}}\big\rangle,



where we have used (5.12), (5.14), (3.4), and (5.13) for the four equalities, respectively.
∎





We also need a King–Tollu–Toumazet type theorem discovered by Cho, Jung, and Moon [5, p. 487, Theorem 1.7 with r=1].




Lemma 5.6.



Let \lambda,\mu,\nu be partitions. Assume that c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}>0 and that there are indices i,j,k such that i+j=k+1 and \lambda_{k}=\mu_{i}+\nu_{j}. Then





\displaystyle c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}=c_{\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime}},

(5.15)


where \lambda^{\prime}, \mu^{\prime}, and \nu^{\prime} are obtained by removing \lambda_{k}, \mu_{i}, and \nu_{j} from \lambda, \mu, and \nu, respectively.





Now we may prove the desired nonnegativity (5.3) by combining the following result and our inductive assumption \Phi_{t-1,j}^{\beta}\geq_{s}0 so that [s_{\alpha^{-}}]\Phi_{t-1,j}^{\beta}\geq 0.




Theorem 5.7.



Let \alpha be a partition containing parts of size d. Then





\displaystyle[s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{t,j}^{\beta}\geq[s_{\alpha^{-}}]\Phi_{t-1,j}^{\beta}.

(5.16)






Before proceeding with the proof of this theorem, we establish the following relations involving A_{u,v}(\alpha) and B_{u,v}(\alpha).




Lemma 5.8.



We have





\displaystyle A_{u,v}(\alpha)=\begin{cases}A_{u,v-1}(\alpha^{-}),&\text{if $v\geq p+1$},\\
B_{u,v}(\alpha),&\text{if $v=p$}.\end{cases}

(5.17)


Also,





\displaystyle B_{u,v}(\alpha)=\begin{cases}B_{u,v-1}(\alpha^{-}),&\text{if $v\geq p+1$},\\
A_{u,v}(\alpha),&\text{if $v=p$},\end{cases}

(5.18)


and





\displaystyle B_{u,p-1}(\alpha)=\begin{cases}B_{u-1,p-1}(\alpha^{-}),&\text{if $B_{u,p-1}(\alpha)>0$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(5.19)






Proof.



We first consider the case v\geq p+1. In this situation, the skew diagrams \alpha/R_{v} and \alpha^{-}/R_{v-1} are the same. Using (3.4) twice, we have





\displaystyle A_{u,v}(\alpha)=\big\langle s_{\alpha/R_{v}},s_{P_{u}^{(j)}/\beta}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\alpha^{-}/R_{v-1}},s_{P_{u}^{(j)}/\beta}\big\rangle=A_{u,v-1}(\alpha^{-}).



For B_{u,v}(\alpha), we only need the fact that the skew diagrams \alpha/P_{v}^{(j)} and \alpha^{-}/P_{v-1}^{(j)} are the same when v\geq p+1.




Next, we look at the case v=p. First, assume \beta\not\subseteq R_{u}. Then B_{u,p}(\alpha)=0 because R_{u}/\beta is not a valid skew diagram. If we further have \beta\not\subseteq P_{u}^{(j)}, then it is also true that A_{u,p}(\alpha)=0. If \beta\subseteq P_{u}^{(j)}, then \mathsf{len}(\beta)=u+1 and 1\leq\beta_{u+1}\leq j. Let \kappa^{\prime}:=(d-\beta_{u+1},\ldots,d-\beta_{1}) be the rotated completement of \beta in R_{u+1}, so that \kappa^{\prime}_{1}<d. The same arguments as we show (5.6) give s_{P_{u}^{(j)}/\beta}=s_{\kappa^{\prime}/(d^{\prime})}. Similar to (5.9), we have





\displaystyle A_{u,p}(\alpha)=\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu,(d^{\prime})}^{\kappa^{\prime}}c_{\mu,R_{p}}^{\alpha}.



If A_{u,p}(\alpha)>0, then there is a certain \mu such that c_{\mu,(d^{\prime})}^{\kappa^{\prime}}>0 and c_{\mu,R_{p}}^{\alpha}>0, which, as in the proof of Lemma 5.4, imply that





\displaystyle\alpha_{p+1}\leq\mu_{1}+(R_{p})_{p+1}=\mu_{1}\leq\kappa^{\prime}_{1}<d.



However, this is impossible because \alpha_{p+1}=d. Thus, we still have A_{u,p}(\alpha)=0. The above arguments tell us that when \beta\not\subseteq R_{u},





\displaystyle A_{u,p}(\alpha)=B_{u,p}(\alpha)=0.



Now assume \beta\subseteq R_{u}. Let \kappa be as in Lemma 5.3 and put \kappa^{+}:=d\cup\kappa. In light of (3.4) and (5.6),





\displaystyle A_{u,p}(\alpha)=\big\langle s_{\alpha},s_{P_{u}^{(j)}/\beta}s_{R_{p}}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\alpha/R_{p}},s_{P_{u}^{(j)}/\beta}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\alpha/R_{p}},s_{\kappa^{+}/(d^{\prime})}\big\rangle.



In the way as we show (5.12), it is true that





\displaystyle s_{\alpha/R_{p}}=s_{\tau}s_{d\cup\eta}=\sum_{\lambda}c_{\tau,d\cup\eta}^{\lambda}s_{\lambda}.



By the Littlewood–Richardson rule, we may impose the restriction d\cup\eta\subseteq\lambda to \lambda so that \lambda_{1}\geq d. Thus,





\displaystyle s_{\alpha/R_{p}}=\sum_{\lambda\colon\lambda_{1}=d}c_{\tau,d\cup\eta}^{\lambda}s_{\lambda}+\sum_{\lambda\colon\lambda_{1}>d}c_{\tau,d\cup\eta}^{\lambda}s_{\lambda}.



In particular, we have, with (5.4) used for the second equality and (5.13) used for the last equality,





\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\colon\lambda_{1}=d}c_{\tau,d\cup\eta}^{\lambda}s_{\lambda}=\pi_{d}(s_{\tau}s_{d\cup\eta})=\Delta_{d}\big(\pi_{d}(s_{\tau}s_{\eta})\big)=\Delta_{d}\big(\pi_{d}(s_{\alpha^{-}/R_{p}})\big).



In the meantime, by (3.9),





\displaystyle s_{\kappa^{+}/(d^{\prime})}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta\colon\kappa^{+}/\delta\,\,\mathrm{HS}\\
|\kappa^{+}|-|\delta|=d^{\prime}\end{subarray}}s_{\delta}.



Since \kappa^{+}_{1}=d, we may split the above as





\displaystyle s_{\kappa^{+}/(d^{\prime})}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta\colon\kappa^{+}/\delta\,\,\mathrm{HS}\\
|\kappa^{+}|-|\delta|=d^{\prime}\\
\delta_{1}=d\end{subarray}}s_{\delta}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta\colon\kappa^{+}/\delta\,\,\mathrm{HS}\\
|\kappa^{+}|-|\delta|=d^{\prime}\\
\delta_{1}<d\end{subarray}}s_{\delta}.



In particular, we have, with (3.9) recalled,





\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta\colon\kappa^{+}/\delta\,\,\mathrm{HS}\\
|\kappa^{+}|-|\delta|=d^{\prime}\\
\delta_{1}=d\end{subarray}}s_{\delta}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta^{\prime}\colon\kappa/\delta^{\prime}\,\,\mathrm{HS}\\
|\kappa|-|\delta^{\prime}|=d^{\prime}\end{subarray}}s_{d\cup\delta^{\prime}}=\Delta_{d}(s_{\kappa/(d^{\prime})}).



Thus,





\displaystyle A_{u,p}(\alpha)=\left\langle\sum_{\lambda\colon\lambda_{1}=d}c_{\tau,d\cup\eta}^{\lambda}s_{\lambda},\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta\colon\kappa^{+}/\delta\,\,\mathrm{HS}\\
|\kappa^{+}|-|\delta|=d^{\prime}\\
\delta_{1}=d\end{subarray}}s_{\delta}\right\rangle=\big\langle\Delta_{d}\big(\pi_{d}(s_{\alpha^{-}/R_{p}})\big),\Delta_{d}(s_{\kappa/(d^{\prime})})\big\rangle.



In the above Hall inner product, it is safe to remove the operator \Delta_{d} because for any two Schur polynomials s_{\mu} and s_{\nu}, it is true that





\displaystyle\big\langle s_{\mu},s_{\nu}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{d\cup\mu},s_{d\cup\nu}\big\rangle=\big\langle\Delta_{d}(s_{\mu}),\Delta_{d}(s_{\nu})\big\rangle.



Also, we may remove the operator \pi_{d} in the first term because if a certain s_{\delta} appears in the Schur expansion of s_{\kappa/(d^{\prime})} in the second term, then by (3.9), we must have \delta\subseteq\kappa so that \delta_{1}\leq\kappa_{1}\leq d. Therefore,





\displaystyle A_{u,p}(\alpha)=\big\langle s_{\alpha^{-}/R_{p}},s_{\kappa/(d^{\prime})}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\alpha/P_{p}^{(j)}},s_{\kappa}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\alpha/P_{p}^{(j)}},s_{R_{u}/\beta}\big\rangle,



where we have used (5.11) for the second equality and (5.6) for the last equality. We then derive from (3.4) the desired relation





\displaystyle A_{u,p}(\alpha)=B_{u,p}(\alpha).






Finally, we work out the case v=p-1 for B_{u,v}(\alpha), namely, the relation (5.19). If p=0, then there is nothing to prove. Now assume p\geq 1 so that \alpha\neq P_{p-1}^{(j)}. If u=0, then B_{0,p-1}(\alpha)=\big\langle s_{\alpha},s_{P_{p-1}^{(j)}}\big\rangle=0. Now we further assume u\geq 1. Here we only need to look at the case B_{u,p-1}(\alpha)>0, which requires \beta\subseteq R_{u}. Let \kappa be as in Lemma 5.3. In light of (5.6) and (3.1),





\displaystyle B_{u,p-1}(\alpha)=\big\langle s_{\alpha},s_{R_{u}/\beta}s_{P_{p-1}^{(j)}}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\alpha},s_{\kappa}s_{P_{p-1}^{(j)}}\big\rangle=c_{\kappa,P_{p-1}^{(j)}}^{\alpha}.



It follows from (3.5) that





\displaystyle d=\alpha_{p+1}\leq\kappa_{1}+(P_{p-1}^{(j)})_{p+1}=\kappa_{1}\leq d.



Therefore, \kappa_{1}=d. Remove this first part from \kappa and call the resulting partition \kappa^{-}. Since





\displaystyle\alpha_{p+1}=d=d+0=\kappa_{1}+(P_{p-1}^{(j)})_{p+1},



we derive from Lemma 5.6 that





\displaystyle B_{u,p-1}(\alpha)=c_{\kappa,P_{p-1}^{(j)}}^{\alpha}=c_{\kappa^{-},P_{p-1}^{(j)}}^{\alpha^{-}}.



Note that \kappa_{1}=d implies that \beta_{u}=0 so that \kappa^{-} is the rotated completement of \beta in R_{u-1}. We then use (5.6) and (3.1) to get





\displaystyle B_{u-1,p-1}(\alpha^{-})=\big\langle s_{\alpha^{-}},s_{R_{u-1}/\beta}s_{P_{p-1}^{(j)}}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\alpha^{-}},s_{\kappa-}s_{P_{p-1}^{(j)}}\big\rangle=c_{\kappa^{-},P_{p-1}^{(j)}}^{\alpha^{-}}.



Thus,





\displaystyle B_{u,p-1}(\alpha)=B_{u-1,p-1}(\alpha^{-}),



as claimed.
∎





Now we complete the proof of Theorem 5.7.




Proof of Theorem 5.7.



By the definitions in (5.7), we have





\displaystyle[s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{t,j}^{\beta}=\sum_{v\geq 0}\big(A_{t-v,v}(\alpha)-B_{t-v,v}(\alpha)\big),



and





\displaystyle[s_{\alpha^{-}}]\Phi_{t-1,j}^{\beta}=\sum_{v\geq 0}\big(A_{t-v,v-1}(\alpha^{-})-B_{t-v,v-1}(\alpha^{-})\big).



Invoking (5.8), (5.17), and (5.18),





\displaystyle[s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{t,j}^{\beta}-[s_{\alpha^{-}}]\Phi_{t-1,j}^{\beta}=B_{t-p,p-1}(\alpha^{-})-B_{t-p+1,p-1}(\alpha)\geq 0,



where we have used (5.19) for the nonnegativity. Thus, the desired inequality (5.16) is true.
∎






5.2. The partition \alpha contains no part of size d




Throughout this subsection, since \alpha does not contain any part of size d, we always write it as





\displaystyle\alpha:=(d+\tau_{1},\ldots,d+\tau_{p},\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{r}),

(5.20)


where \tau:=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{p}) is a partition with exactly p positive parts, and \rho:=(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{r}) is a partition with width \rho_{1}<d. For convenience, we put





\displaystyle\tau^{(d)}:=(d+\tau_{1},\ldots,d+\tau_{p}).



Define three auxiliary skew diagrams:





\displaystyle D:=\alpha/P_{p-1}^{(j)},\qquad T:=\tau^{(d)}/P_{p-1}^{(j)},\qquad L:=((d-1)\cup\rho)/(j-1).






We first prove two useful results about the skew diagrams L and T.




Lemma 5.9.



We have





\displaystyle\pi_{d}(h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\rho}-s_{L})=\Delta_{d}(s_{\rho/(j)}).

(5.21)






Proof.



Similar arguments as in the proof of Lemma 5.5 tell us that





\displaystyle h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\rho}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\colon\xi/\rho\,\,\mathrm{HS}\\
|\xi|-|\rho|=d^{\prime}\end{subarray}}s_{\xi},\qquad\qquad s_{L}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\colon\xi/\rho\,\,\mathrm{HS}\\
|\xi|-|\rho|=d^{\prime}\\
\xi_{1}\leq d-1\end{subarray}}s_{\xi},



so that





\displaystyle\pi_{d}(h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\rho}-s_{L})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\colon\xi/\rho\,\,\mathrm{HS}\\
|\xi|-|\rho|=d^{\prime}\\
\xi_{1}=d\end{subarray}}s_{\xi}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi^{\prime}\colon\rho/\xi^{\prime}\,\,\mathrm{HS}\\
|\rho|-|\xi^{\prime}|=j\end{subarray}}s_{d\cup\xi^{\prime}},



where we have written \xi=d\cup\xi^{\prime} and applied the condition (3.7) for the second equality. The claimed identity then follows from (3.9).
∎





Lemma 5.10.



We have





\displaystyle h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\tau}-s_{T}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\zeta\colon\zeta/\tau\,\,\mathrm{HS}\\
|\zeta|-|\tau|=d^{\prime}\\
\mathsf{len}(\zeta)=p+1\end{subarray}}s_{\zeta}.

(5.22)






Proof.



First, we know from (3.8) that





\displaystyle h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\tau}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\zeta\colon\zeta/\tau\,\,\mathrm{HS}\\
|\zeta|-|\tau|=d^{\prime}\\
\mathsf{len}(\zeta)\leq p+1\end{subarray}}s_{\zeta},



where we require \mathsf{len}(\zeta)\leq p+1 because otherwise there are at least two cells in the first column of the horizontal strip \zeta/\tau, which is impossible. Next, by (3.3),





\displaystyle s_{T}=\sum_{\zeta\colon\zeta\subseteq\tau^{(d)}}c_{P_{p-1}^{(j)},\zeta}^{\tau^{(d)}}s_{\zeta}.



To ensure the positivity of the Littlewood–Richardson coefficients in the above, we must have \mathsf{len}(\zeta)\leq p and





\displaystyle|\zeta|=|\tau^{(d)}|-|P_{p-1}^{(j)}|=|\tau|+d^{\prime}.



Note also that the skew diagram \tau^{(d)}/P_{p-1}^{(j)} can be split into two disjoint groups, one of shape \tau to the right of the rectangle R_{p}, and the other consisting of d^{\prime} cells from the (j+1)-th column to the d-th column in the p-th row. Moreover, in a Littlewood–Richardson tableau of this shape and type \zeta, both groups are uniquely filled out: for the first group, according to the first assertion in Lemma 3.1 together with the column strictness, it is only possible to fill all entries in the i-th row with i for each i where 1\leq i\leq p; for the second group, the row weak monotonicity and the type \zeta of the tableau require that the cells are filled with 1 of multiplicity \zeta_{1}-\tau_{1}, followed by 2 of multiplicity \zeta_{2}-\tau_{2}, and so forth, till p of multiplicity \zeta_{p}-\tau_{p}. In particular, by the Littlewood–Richardson condition, we see that for each i,





\displaystyle\tau_{i-1}-\tau_{i}\geq\zeta_{i}-\tau_{i}\geq 0,



where we put \tau_{0}=\infty. Thus,





\displaystyle\zeta_{1}\geq\tau_{1}\geq\zeta_{2}\geq\tau_{2}\geq\cdots,



meaning that \zeta/\tau is a horizontal strip by (3.7). To summarize the above discussions, we have





\displaystyle c_{P_{p-1}^{(j)},\zeta}^{\tau^{(d)}}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $\zeta/\tau\,\,\mathrm{HS}$, $|\zeta|-|\tau|=d^{\prime}$, and $\mathsf{len}(\zeta)\leq p$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}



It follows that





\displaystyle s_{T}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\zeta\colon\zeta/\tau\,\,\mathrm{HS}\\
|\zeta|-|\tau|=d^{\prime}\\
\mathsf{len}(\zeta)\leq p\end{subarray}}s_{\zeta},



which gives (5.22) by invoking the expression of h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\tau} derived at the beginning of this proof.
∎





In the next two lemmas, we shall use the dual Jacobi–Trudi formula [15, p. 344, Corollary 7.16.2], which states that for two partitions \lambda and \mu with length at most l such that \mu\subseteq\lambda,





\displaystyle s_{(\lambda/\mu)^{\mathsf{T}}}(X_{N})=\underset{1\leq a,b\leq l}{\det}\big(e_{\lambda_{a}-\mu_{b}-a+b}(X_{N})\big).

(5.23)





Lemma 5.11.



Let N=d or d-1. We have





\displaystyle\pi_{N}(s_{D})=\pi_{N}(s_{\rho}s_{T}).

(5.24)






Proof.



We apply (5.23) to D^{\mathsf{T}}=(\alpha/P_{p-1}^{(j)})^{\mathsf{T}} with l=p+r. In particular, when 1\leq a\leq p and p+1\leq b\leq p+r, the entry in the dual Jacobi–Trudi matrix is e_{a+\tau_{a}-a+b}(X_{N}) with the index a+\tau_{a}-a+b>N so that e_{a+\tau_{a}-a+b}(X_{N})=0. Now the dual Jacobi–Trudi matrix has the block decomposition





\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{0}\\
\star&\mathbf{B}\end{pmatrix},



where





\displaystyle\mathbf{A}:=\big(e_{\tau^{(d)}_{a}-(P_{p-1}^{(j)})_{b}-a+b}(X_{N})\big)_{1\leq a,b\leq p},\qquad\mathbf{B}:=\big(e_{\rho_{a^{\prime}}-a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}}(X_{N})\big)_{1\leq a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\leq r}.



Thus,





\displaystyle s_{D^{\mathsf{T}}}(X_{N})=\det\mathbf{A}\det\mathbf{B}=s_{T^{\mathsf{T}}}(X_{N})s_{\rho^{\mathsf{T}}}(X_{N}),



where we have used (5.23) again for \det\mathbf{A} and \det\mathbf{B}. In light of (3.12) and (3.13), we have





\displaystyle\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N}(s_{D})=\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N}(s_{\rho}s_{T}),



which, according to (3.16), further gives





\displaystyle\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N}\big(\pi_{N}(s_{D})\big)=\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N}\big(\pi_{N}(s_{\rho}s_{T})\big).



Because of the injectivity of \operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N} on the span of s_{\lambda} with \lambda_{1}\leq N as stated in Lemma 3.6, we conclude the desired relation.
∎





Lemma 5.12.



We have





\displaystyle s_{\tau^{\mathsf{T}}}(X_{d})s_{L^{\mathsf{T}}}(X_{d})-s_{\rho^{\mathsf{T}}}s_{T^{\mathsf{T}}}(X_{d})=s_{(\gamma/\delta)^{\mathsf{T}}}(X_{d}),

(5.25)


where





\displaystyle\gamma:=(d-2+\tau_{1},\ldots,d-2+\tau_{p},d-1,\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{r}),



and





\displaystyle\delta:=(\underbrace{d-2,\ldots,d-2}_{\text{$p$ parts}},j-1).







Proof.



We apply (5.23) to (\gamma/\delta)^{\mathsf{T}} with l=p+r+1. It is easy to verify that the associated dual Jacobi–Trudi matrix has the following block decomposition:





\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{M}&\mathbf{k}&\mathbf{0}\\
(0,\ldots,0,1)&e_{d^{\prime}}(X_{d})&(e_{d}(X_{d}),0,\ldots,0)\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{l}&\mathbf{N}\end{pmatrix},



where





\displaystyle\mathbf{M}:=\big(e_{\tau_{a}-a+b}(X_{d})\big)_{1\leq a,b\leq p},\qquad\qquad\mathbf{k}:=\big(e_{\tau_{a}-a+p+d^{\prime}}(X_{d})\big)_{1\leq a\leq p}^{\mathsf{T}},



and





\displaystyle\mathbf{N}:=\big(e_{\rho_{a^{\prime}}-a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}}(X_{d})\big)_{1\leq a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\leq r},\qquad\qquad\mathbf{l}:=\big(e_{\rho_{a^{\prime}}-a^{\prime}-j+1}(X_{d})\big)_{1\leq a^{\prime}\leq p}^{\mathsf{T}}.



Expanding the determinant gives






\displaystyle s_{(\gamma/\delta)^{\mathsf{T}}}(X_{d})





\displaystyle\qquad=\det\mathbf{M}\det\begin{pmatrix}e_{d^{\prime}}(X_{d})&(e_{d}(X_{d}),0,\ldots,0)\\
\mathbf{l}&\mathbf{N}\end{pmatrix}-\det\mathbf{N}\det(\mathbf{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{M}_{p-1},\mathbf{k}).



Using (5.23) again, we find that





\displaystyle s_{\tau^{\mathsf{T}}}(X_{d})=\det\mathbf{M},\qquad\qquad s_{\rho^{\mathsf{T}}}=\det\mathbf{N}.



Also,





\displaystyle s_{L^{\mathsf{T}}}(X_{d})=s_{((d-1)\cup\rho)/(j-1)^{\mathsf{T}}}(X_{d})=\det\begin{pmatrix}e_{d^{\prime}}(X_{d})&(e_{d}(X_{d}),0,\ldots,0)\\
\mathbf{l}&\mathbf{N}\end{pmatrix},



and





\displaystyle s_{T^{\mathsf{T}}}(X_{d})=s_{(\tau^{(d)}/P_{p-1}^{(j)})^{\mathsf{T}}}(X_{d})=\det(\mathbf{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{M}_{p-1},\mathbf{k}).



The desired identity (5.25) then follows.
∎





Corollary 5.13.



We have





\displaystyle\pi_{d}(s_{\tau}s_{L}-s_{\rho}s_{T})\geq_{s}0.

(5.26)






Proof.



It follows from (5.25) that





\displaystyle\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}(s_{\tau}s_{L}-s_{\rho}s_{T})=s_{(\gamma/\delta)^{\mathsf{T}}}(X_{d})\geq_{s}0.



Note that (s_{\tau}s_{L}-s_{\rho}s_{T})-\pi_{d}(s_{\tau}s_{L}-s_{\rho}s_{T}) is in I_{d} so that it is annihilated by \operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d} according to Lemma 3.5. Thus,





\displaystyle\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}\big(\pi_{d}(s_{\tau}s_{L}-s_{\rho}s_{T})\big)=\operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d}(s_{\tau}s_{L}-s_{\rho}s_{T})\geq_{s}0.



Since on the span of s_{\lambda} with \lambda_{1}\leq N, the injective map \operatorname{sp}^{\mathsf{T}}_{d} sends s_{\lambda} bijectively to s_{\lambda^{\mathsf{T}}}(X_{d}), the Schur positivity of \pi_{d}(s_{\tau}s_{L}-s_{\rho}s_{T}) is then immediate.
∎





The final piece of the ingredients we need is a simplification of [s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{t,j}^{\beta} for our choice of \alpha.




Lemma 5.14.



We have





\displaystyle[s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{t,j}^{\beta}=\begin{cases}A_{t-p,p}(\alpha)-B_{t-p,p}(\alpha)-B_{t-p+1,p-1}(\alpha),&\text{if $t\geq p$},\\
0,&\text{if $t<p$}.\end{cases}

(5.27)






Proof.



Recalling (5.7),





\displaystyle[s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{t,j}^{\beta}=\sum_{v\geq 0}\big(A_{t-v,v}(\alpha)-B_{t-v,v}(\alpha)\big).



According to (5.9), if A_{t-v,v}(\alpha)>0, then we must have R_{v}\subseteq\alpha, so that \alpha_{v}\geq d. Since d does not appear in \alpha, we indeed have \alpha_{v}>d, which implies that v\leq p. By the relation for A in (5.8),





\displaystyle\sum_{v\geq 0}A_{t-v,v}(\alpha)=A_{t-p,p}(\alpha).



Similarly, if B_{t-v,v}(\alpha)>0, we need P_{v}^{(j)}\subseteq\alpha, which in the same way, gives v\leq p. By the relation for B in (5.8),





\displaystyle\sum_{v\geq 0}B_{t-v,v}(\alpha)=B_{t-p,p}(\alpha)+B_{t-p+1,p-1}(\alpha).



Hence,





\displaystyle[s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{t,j}^{\beta}=A_{t-p,p}(\alpha)-B_{t-p,p}(\alpha)-B_{t-p+1,p-1}(\alpha).



Now (5.27) is plain when t\geq p or t\leq p-2. For the last case t=p-1, we have





\displaystyle[s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{p-1,j}^{\beta}=-B_{0,p-1}(\alpha)=-\big\langle s_{\alpha},s_{\varnothing/\beta}s_{P_{p-1}^{(j)}}\big\rangle.



If \beta\neq\varnothing, then the skew diagram \varnothing/\beta is invalid so that s_{\varnothing/\beta}=0 and hence that [s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{p-1,j}^{\beta}=0. If \beta=\varnothing, then [s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{p-1,j}^{\varnothing}=-\big\langle s_{\alpha},s_{P_{p-1}^{(j)}}\big\rangle. Note that \alpha\neq P_{p-1}^{(j)}; otherwise, \alpha has a part of size d because p-1=t\geq 1 by the inductive assumption, but this violates our requirement for \alpha. Hence, [s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{p-1,j}^{\varnothing} still vanishes.
∎





Unlike Theorem 5.7 for the first case of \alpha in the preceding subsection, this time we confirm (5.3) directly.




Theorem 5.15.



Let \alpha be a partition containing no part of size d. Then





\displaystyle[s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{t,j}^{\beta}\geq 0.

(5.28)






Proof.



It suffices to assume t\geq p in light of (5.27). By the same arguments as we show (5.12) and (5.13), it is true that





\displaystyle s_{\alpha/R_{p}}=s_{\tau}s_{\rho},



and





\displaystyle s_{\alpha/P_{p}^{(j)}}=s_{\tau}s_{\rho/(j)}.



We also recall from the definition of the skew diagram D that





\displaystyle s_{\alpha/P_{p-1}^{(j)}}=s_{D}.



For convenience, write





\displaystyle t^{\prime}:=t-p.



Note that





\displaystyle R_{t^{\prime}}\subseteq P_{t^{\prime}}^{(j)}\subseteq R_{t^{\prime}+1}.



We then have four cases of \beta.




Case 1: \beta\subseteq R_{t^{\prime}}. Let \kappa:=(d-\beta_{t^{\prime}},\ldots,d-\beta_{1}) be the rotated completement of \beta in R_{t^{\prime}} and put \kappa^{+}:=d\cup\kappa. By (5.6),





\displaystyle s_{R_{t^{\prime}}/\beta}=s_{\kappa},\qquad s_{R_{t^{\prime}+1}/\beta}=s_{\kappa^{+}},\qquad s_{P_{t^{\prime}}^{(j)}/\beta}=s_{\kappa^{+}/(d^{\prime})}.



Now,





\displaystyle A_{t^{\prime},p}(\alpha)=\big\langle s_{\alpha},s_{P_{t^{\prime}}^{(j)}/\beta}s_{R_{p}}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\alpha/R_{p}},s_{P_{t^{\prime}}^{(j)}/\beta}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\tau}s_{\rho},s_{\kappa^{+}/(d^{\prime})}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\kappa^{+}},h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\tau}s_{\rho}\big\rangle,



where we have utilized (3.4) for the second and fourth equalities. Since \kappa_{1}^{+}\leq d, we obtain





\displaystyle A_{t^{\prime},p}(\alpha)=\big\langle s_{\kappa^{+}},\pi_{d}(h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\tau}s_{\rho})\big\rangle.



Next,





\displaystyle B_{t^{\prime},p}(\alpha)=\big\langle s_{\alpha},s_{R_{t^{\prime}}/\beta}s_{P_{p}^{(j)}}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\alpha/P_{p}^{(j)}},s_{R_{t^{\prime}}/\beta}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\tau}s_{\rho/(j)},s_{\kappa}\big\rangle.



Since \kappa_{1}\leq d, we further have





\displaystyle B_{t^{\prime},p}(\alpha)=\big\langle s_{\kappa},\pi_{d}(s_{\tau}s_{\rho/(j)})\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\kappa^{+}},\Delta_{d}\big(\pi_{d}(s_{\tau}s_{\rho/(j)})\big)\big\rangle.



We see that \Delta_{d}\big(\pi_{d}(s_{\tau}s_{\rho/(j)})\big) is equal to \pi_{d}\big(s_{\tau}\Delta_{d}(s_{\rho/(j)})\big) by (5.4), and further to \pi_{d}\big(s_{\tau}\pi_{d}(h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\rho}-s_{L})\big) by (5.21), and lastly to \pi_{d}\big(s_{\tau}(h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\rho}-s_{L})\big) by (3.17). Therefore,





\displaystyle B_{t^{\prime},p}(\alpha)=\big\langle s_{\kappa^{+}},\pi_{d}\big(s_{\tau}(h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\rho}-s_{L})\big)\big\rangle.



Finally,





\displaystyle B_{t^{\prime}+1,p-1}(\alpha)=\big\langle s_{\alpha},s_{R_{t^{\prime}+1}/\beta}s_{P_{p-1}^{(j)}}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\alpha/P_{p-1}^{(j)}},s_{R_{t^{\prime}+1}/\beta}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\kappa^{+}},s_{D}\big\rangle.



Once again, we can restrict s_{D} to \pi_{d}(s_{D}) because \kappa_{1}^{+}\leq d. Hence,





\displaystyle B_{t^{\prime}+1,p-1}(\alpha)=\big\langle s_{\kappa^{+}},\pi_{d}(s_{D})\big\rangle.



It follows from (5.27) that





\displaystyle[s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{t,j}^{\beta}
\displaystyle=\big\langle s_{\kappa^{+}},\pi_{d}(h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\tau}s_{\rho})-\pi_{d}\big(s_{\tau}(h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\rho}-s_{L})\big)-\pi_{d}(s_{D})\big\rangle





\displaystyle=\big\langle s_{\kappa^{+}},\pi_{d}(s_{\tau}s_{L})-\pi_{d}(s_{D})\big\rangle.



Using (5.24), we conclude that





\displaystyle[s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{t,j}^{\beta}=\big\langle s_{\kappa^{+}},\pi_{d}(s_{\tau}s_{L})-\pi_{d}(s_{\rho}s_{T})\big\rangle,



so that





\displaystyle[s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{t,j}^{\beta}\geq 0,



which comes from the Schur positivity of \pi_{d}(s_{\tau}s_{L})-\pi_{d}(s_{\rho}s_{T}) derived in (5.26).




Case 2: \beta\subseteq P_{t^{\prime}}^{(j)} but \beta\not\subseteq R_{t^{\prime}}. Here \mathsf{len}(\beta)=t^{\prime}+1 and 1\leq\beta_{t^{\prime}+1}\leq j. Let \kappa^{\prime}:=(d-\beta_{t^{\prime}+1},\ldots,d-\beta_{1}) be the rotated completement of \beta in R_{t^{\prime}+1}, so that \kappa^{\prime}_{1}\leq d-1. In a similar way as we get (5.6), rotation gives





\displaystyle s_{R_{t^{\prime}+1}/\beta}=s_{\kappa^{\prime}},\qquad\qquad s_{P_{t^{\prime}}^{(j)}/\beta}=s_{\kappa^{\prime}/(d^{\prime})}.



Now,





\displaystyle A_{t^{\prime},p}(\alpha)=\big\langle s_{\alpha},s_{P_{t^{\prime}}^{(j)}/\beta}s_{R_{p}}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\alpha/R_{p}},s_{P_{t^{\prime}}^{(j)}/\beta}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\tau}s_{\rho},s_{\kappa^{\prime}/(d^{\prime})}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\kappa^{\prime}},h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\tau}s_{\rho}\big\rangle.



Next, since the skew diagram R_{t^{\prime}}/\beta is invalid,





\displaystyle B_{t^{\prime},p}(\alpha)=\big\langle s_{\alpha},s_{R_{t^{\prime}}/\beta}s_{P_{p}^{(j)}}\big\rangle=0.



Finally,





\displaystyle B_{t^{\prime}+1,p-1}(\alpha)=\big\langle s_{\alpha},s_{R_{t^{\prime}+1}/\beta}s_{P_{p-1}^{(j)}}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\alpha/P_{p-1}^{(j)}},s_{R_{t^{\prime}+1}/\beta}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\kappa^{\prime}},s_{D}\big\rangle.



By (5.27),





\displaystyle[s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{t,j}^{\beta}=\big\langle s_{\kappa^{\prime}},h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\tau}s_{\rho}-s_{D}\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\kappa^{\prime}},\pi_{d-1}(h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\tau}s_{\rho})-\pi_{d-1}(s_{D})\big\rangle,



with the second equality coming from the fact that \kappa^{\prime}_{1}\leq d-1. Using (5.24), we then get





\displaystyle[s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{t,j}^{\beta}=\big\langle s_{\kappa^{\prime}},\pi_{d-1}(h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\tau}s_{\rho})-\pi_{d-1}(s_{\rho}s_{T})\big\rangle=\big\langle s_{\kappa^{\prime}},s_{\rho}(h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\tau}-s_{T})\big\rangle.



It follows that





\displaystyle[s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{t,j}^{\beta}\geq 0,



because h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\tau}-s_{T} is Schur positive according to (5.22), and therefore so is s_{\rho}(h_{d^{\prime}}s_{\tau}-s_{T}).




Case 3: \beta\subseteq R_{t^{\prime}+1} but \beta\not\subseteq P_{t^{\prime}}^{(j)}. Here \mathsf{len}(\beta)=t^{\prime}+1 and j+1\leq\beta_{t^{\prime}+1}\leq d. Since the skew diagrams P_{t^{\prime}}^{(j)}/\beta and R_{t^{\prime}}/\beta are invalid,





\displaystyle A_{t^{\prime},p}(\alpha)=B_{t^{\prime},p}(\alpha)=0.



Let \kappa^{\prime} be as in the second case so that \kappa^{\prime}_{1}=d-\beta_{t^{\prime}+1}. We then use (5.27) to get





\displaystyle[s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{t,j}^{\beta}=-B_{t^{\prime}+1,p-1}(\alpha)=-\big\langle s_{\kappa^{\prime}},s_{\alpha/P_{p-1}^{(j)}}\big\rangle=-c_{\kappa^{\prime},P_{p-1}^{(j)}}^{\alpha},



where we have recalled (3.2) for the last equality. If p=0, then the above is 0 by default. Let p\geq 1 and assume that the above does not vanish. Then according to (3.5),





\displaystyle\alpha_{p}\leq\kappa^{\prime}_{1}+(P_{p-1}^{(j)})_{p}=\kappa^{\prime}_{1}+j=d-\beta_{t^{\prime}+1}+j<d.



However, by definition we have \alpha_{p}=d+\tau_{p}>d, resulting in a contradiction. Thus, c_{\kappa^{\prime},P_{p-1}^{(j)}}^{\alpha}=0, so that





\displaystyle[s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{t,j}^{\beta}=0.






Case 4: \beta\not\subseteq R_{t^{\prime}+1}. It is clear that





\displaystyle A_{t^{\prime},p}(\alpha)=B_{t^{\prime},p}(\alpha)=B_{t^{\prime}+1,p-1}(\alpha)=0,



because none of the skew diagrams P_{t^{\prime}}^{(j)}/\beta, R_{t^{\prime}}/\beta, R_{t^{\prime}+1}/\beta is valid. We conclude from (5.27) that





\displaystyle[s_{\alpha}]\Phi_{t,j}^{\beta}=0



in this final case.
∎







6. Strong q-log-convexity of d-Hoggatt polynomials




For any alphabet X, define a formal power series in \Lambda[[q]]:





\displaystyle\mydutchcal{H}_{X}(q)=\mydutchcal{H}_{X}^{(d)}(q):=\sum_{k\geq 0}s_{R_{k}}(X)q^{k}.

(6.1)





Lemma 6.1.



We have





\displaystyle\mydutchcal{H}_{1_{N}}^{(d)}(q)=H_{N}^{(d)}(q).

(6.2)






Proof.



Note that s_{R_{k}}(1_{N})=0 for all k>N because in these cases R_{k} has more than N rows and hence requires more than N variables to ensure nonvanishing. Therefore,





\displaystyle\mydutchcal{H}_{1_{N}}(q)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}s_{R_{k}}(1_{N})q^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{k}}q^{k}=H_{N}(q),



where we have used (3.19).
∎





Let X_{N}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}) and Y_{M}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{M}) be two alphabets. Denote





\displaystyle X_{N}\oplus Y_{M}:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N},y_{1},\ldots,y_{M}).



By separating, in a semistandard tableau of shape \lambda, the cells whose entries refer to variables in X_{N} and from those whose entries refer to variables in Y_{M}, we have





\displaystyle s_{\lambda}(X_{N}\oplus Y_{M})=\sum_{\mu\subseteq\lambda}s_{\mu}(X_{N})s_{\lambda/\mu}(Y_{M}).

(6.3)





In the sequel, we treat a single variable z as an alphabet (z).




Lemma 6.2.



For k\geq 0,





\displaystyle s_{R_{k}}(X_{N}\oplus z)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}s_{P_{k-1}^{(j)}}(X_{N})z^{d-j}.

(6.4)






Proof.



Note that





\displaystyle s_{\lambda/\mu}(z)=\begin{cases}z^{|\lambda|-|\mu|},&\text{if $\lambda/\mu$ is a horizontal strip},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}



By (6.3), we have





\displaystyle s_{R_{k}}(X_{N}\oplus z)=\sum_{\mu\colon R_{k}/\mu\,\,\mathrm{HS}}s_{\mu}(X_{N})z^{dk-|\mu|}.



Since R_{k}/\mu is a horizontal strip if and only if \mu=P_{k-1}^{(j)} for a certain j with 0\leq j\leq d. The desired identity follows.
∎





Our next result is related to the auxiliary functions \Phi defined in (5.1).




Lemma 6.3.



Let A and B be disjoint alphabets and put X=A\oplus B. Then





\displaystyle\mydutchcal{H}_{X\oplus z}(q)\mydutchcal{H}_{B}(q)-\mydutchcal{H}_{X}(q)\mydutchcal{H}_{B\oplus z}(q)=\sum_{r\geq 1}q^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}z^{d-j}\sum_{\beta}s_{\beta}(A)\Phi_{r-1,j}^{\beta}(B).

(6.5)






Proof.



The coefficient of q^{0} in \mydutchcal{H}_{X\oplus z}(q)\mydutchcal{H}_{B}(q)-\mydutchcal{H}_{X}(q)\mydutchcal{H}_{B\oplus z}(q) is 1-1=0. For r\geq 1, the coefficient of q^{r} is





\displaystyle c_{r}:=\sum_{k+l=r}\big(s_{R_{k}}(X\oplus z)s_{R_{l}}(B)-s_{R_{k}}(X)s_{R_{l}}(B\oplus z)\big).

(6.6)


Applying Lemma 6.2, we have





\displaystyle c_{r}=\sum_{j=0}^{d}z^{d-j}\sum_{k+l=r}\big(s_{P_{k-1}^{(j)}}(X)s_{R_{l}}(B)-s_{R_{k}}(X)s_{P_{l-1}^{(j)}}(B)\big).



When j=0, we have






\displaystyle\sum_{k+l=r}\big(s_{P_{k-1}^{(0)}}(X)s_{R_{l}}(B)-s_{R_{k}}(X)s_{P_{l-1}^{(0)}}(B)\big)





\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{k+l=r}\big(s_{R_{k-1}}(X)s_{R_{l}}(B)-s_{R_{k}}(X)s_{R_{l-1}}(B)\big)





\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{k+l=r-1}\big(s_{R_{k}}(X)s_{R_{l}}(B)-s_{R_{k}}(X)s_{R_{l}}(B)\big)=0.



When j=d, we have






\displaystyle\sum_{k+l=r}\big(s_{P_{k-1}^{(d)}}(X)s_{R_{l}}(B)-s_{R_{k}}(X)s_{P_{l-1}^{(d)}}(B)\big)





\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{k+l=r}\big(s_{R_{k}}(X)s_{R_{l}}(B)-s_{R_{k}}(X)s_{R_{l}}(B)\big)=0.



Thus,





\displaystyle c_{r}=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}z^{d-j}\sum_{k+l=r-1}\big(s_{P_{k}^{(j)}}(X)s_{R_{l}}(B)-s_{R_{k}}(X)s_{P_{l}^{(j)}}(B)\big).



Recalling that X=A\oplus B, we apply (6.3) and obtain





\displaystyle s_{P_{k}^{(j)}}(X)=s_{P_{k}^{(j)}}(A\oplus B)=\sum_{\beta}s_{\beta}(A)s_{P_{k}^{(j)}/\beta}(B),



and





\displaystyle s_{R_{k}}(X)=s_{R_{k}}(A\oplus B)=\sum_{\beta}s_{\beta}(A)s_{R_{k}/\beta}(B).



It follows that





\displaystyle c_{r}
\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}z^{d-j}\sum_{\beta}s_{\beta}(A)\sum_{k+l=r-1}\big(s_{P_{k}^{(j)}/\beta}(B)s_{R_{l}}(B)-s_{R_{k}/\beta}(B)s_{P_{l}^{(j)}}(B)\big)





\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}z^{d-j}\sum_{\beta}s_{\beta}(A)\Phi_{r-1,j}^{\beta}(B),



as desired.
∎





Now we are in a position to finalize the proof of Theorem 1.2.




Proof of Theorem 1.2.



Let n\geq m\geq 1. We set A=1_{n-m+1} and B=1_{m-1} so that X=1_{n}. Also, set z=1. Then Lemma 6.1 tells us that





\displaystyle H_{n+1}(q)H_{m-1}(q)-H_{n}(q)H_{m}(q)=\mydutchcal{H}_{X\oplus z}(q)\mydutchcal{H}_{B}(q)-\mydutchcal{H}_{X}(q)\mydutchcal{H}_{B\oplus z}(q).



Since \Phi_{r-1,j}^{\beta} is Schur positive for every j with 1\leq j\leq d-1 according to Theorem 5.1, we conclude from (6.5) that the above is in \mathbb{N}[q], thereby implying the strong q-log-convexity of (H_{n}(q))_{n\geq 0}.
∎
