Abstract
    

We use the Pfaffian minor summation formula together with
Jacobi–Trudi Toeplitz matrices to derive Pfaffian
expansions in skew Schur functions.
This yields explicit Schur expansions for several generating
functions involving products of skew Schur functions.
In particular, using sparse skew-symmetric matrices, we provide a Pfaffian proof of a Schur-positive identity arising in the
study of the q-log-convexity of the Narayana polynomials.
As the main application, we prove that the Baxter polynomials
form a q-log-convex sequence.
We further show that the Baxter transformation defined by the refined Baxter numbers preserves
log-convexity.
Finally, by realizing the q-refined Baxter numbers as
principal specializations of rectangular Schur functions, we prove
that the array of q-refined Baxter numbers is q-log-concave both along each row and along each
column.
Both q-log-concavity results extend naturally to the q-analogues of the d-Hoggatt numbers.
  




Keywords: 
Pfaffians , Schur functions , Baxter numbers , Log-convexity, q-Log-convexity

1 Introduction




The aim of this article is twofold.
The first is to combine the Pfaffian minor summation formula with skew Jacobi–Trudi Toeplitz matrices to obtain skew Schur expansions of certain symmetric functions.
The second is to use these Schur-positive identities to prove the q-log-convexity of the Baxter polynomials.




A matrix M=[M_{ij}]_{1\leq i,j\leq 2n} is called skew-symmetric if M_{ij}=-M_{ji} for all i,j.
The Pfaffian of a skew-symmetric matrix M, denoted by \Pf(M), is explicitly defined by the formula





\Pf(M)=\frac{1}{2^{n}n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{2n}}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{n}M_{\sigma(2i-1),\sigma(2i)},



where \mathfrak{S}_{2n} is the symmetric group of degree 2n and \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) is the signature of \sigma.




Pfaffian methods have numerous applications in the theory of symmetric functions and related areas [15, 17, 19, 23, 25, 34, 40].
In particular, Ishikawa and Wakayama [20] established
the Pfaffian minor summation formula.
Let [m]:=\{1,2,\ldots,m\} and let M_{I,J} be the submatrix of M obtained by selecting the rows and columns indexed by I and J.




Lemma 1.1.



([20, Theorem 1])
Let m\leq n and m be even.
Suppose that H is an m\times n matrix, and \Omega is an n\times n
skew-symmetric matrix.
Then H\Omega H^{\mathsf{T}} is a skew-symmetric matrix and





\Pf\left(H\Omega H^{\mathsf{T}}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subset[n]\\
|J|=m\end{subarray}}\det H_{[m],J}\Pf\left(\Omega_{J,J}\right).

(1)






This Pfaffian minor summation formula and its specializations have been
used to derive a variety of identities for Schur functions and related symmetric functions [22, 23, 24].
For example,
Sundquist [41] proved two-variable generalizations of this formula and applied them to express Schur functions in terms of Pfaffians.
Bump and Diaconis [5] further explored the close connection between symmetric functions and Toeplitz matrices.
Okada [34] gave Pfaffian analogues of the Cauchy–Binet formulas that provide direct proofs of many basic identities for Schur Q-functions.
Huh et al. [18] used the Pfaffian minor summation formula to establish affine bounded Littlewood identities for cylindric Schur functions.




Ishikawa, Okada and Wakayama [21] developed a systematic application of the
Pfaffian minor summation formula to identities involving Schur polynomials.
By specializing the matrix H in (1) to a Vandermonde-type matrix,
they expressed weighted Schur series as Pfaffians.




In this paper, we develop a Pfaffian method for Schur positivity by combining the
Pfaffian minor summation formula with Jacobi–Trudi Toeplitz matrices.
The Toeplitz formulation allows us to choose sparse
skew-symmetric matrices whose Pfaffians can be evaluated directly.
The minor summation expansion gives an explicitly
Schur-positive series.
Equating the two expressions produces nontrivial Schur-positive
identities.
As a first illustration, we obtain a Pfaffian proof of the
identity used by Chen, Wang and Yang [7] in their proof of the
q-log-convexity of the Narayana polynomials.
Further specializations give two additional identities,
and we use them to prove the q-log-convexity of the Baxter polynomials.




Let (a_{n})_{n\geq 0} be a sequence of nonnegative numbers.
The sequence
is called log-concave (resp. log-convex) if
a_{n}^{2}\geq a_{n-1}a_{n+1}
(resp. a_{n}^{2}\leq a_{n-1}a_{n+1}) for all n\geq 1.
Log-concave and log-convex sequences often arise in combinatorics, algebra and geometry;
see, for example,  [4, 13, 30, 38].
For a polynomial f(q)\in\mathbb{R}[q], write f(q)\geq_{q}0 if it has only nonnegative coefficients.
A polynomial sequence
(f_{n}(q))_{n\geq 0} is called q-log-concave if





f_{n}(q)^{2}-f_{n-1}(q)f_{n+1}(q)\geq_{q}0,\quad n\geq 1,



and it is called q-log-convex if





f_{n-1}(q)f_{n+1}(q)-f_{n}(q)^{2}\geq_{q}0,\quad n\geq 1.



The notion of q-log-concavity goes back to Stanley.
Sagan [35, 36] established the q-log-concavity of sequences of symmetric functions
and investigated the q-log-concavity of the q-binomial coefficients.
Liu and Wang [30] introduced the notion of q-log-convexity,
and proved that some well-known polynomials such as Bell polynomials and Eulerian polynomials are q-log-convex.
The notion of q-log-convexity has been extensively studied [7, 8, 43, 44, 45].
In particular, using the theory of Schur positivity,
Chen, Wang and Yang [7] proved the (strong) q-log-convexity of Narayana polynomials.




The principal application in this paper concerns the
Baxter numbers.
Baxter numbers enumerate Baxter permutations, which originated in Baxter’s study [1] of fixed
points for the composite of commuting functions.
Chung et al. [9] presented a sum formula for the Baxter numbers B_{n}, where





B_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}B(n,k)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\binom{n}{k}\binom{n+1}{k}\binom{n+2}{k}}{\binom{k+1}{k}\binom{k+2}{k}},\quad 0\leq k\leq n.



We call B(n,k) the refined Baxter number.




The Baxter numbers and Baxter permutations are of great significance in combinatorics, algebra and analysis [12, 28, 32].
Various bijections involving Baxter permutations have been constructed [11, 29].
Fang, Zhang and Zhao [14] provided an analytic study of Baxter numbers.
Dilks [12] developed a comprehensive and unified study of Baxter objects, including
the involutions, the (-1)-phenomenon and gamma-nonnegativity.
It is known [10] that the binomial coefficients, the Narayana numbers and the refined Baxter numbers can be viewed as specializations of the d-Hoggatt numbers with d=1,2,3, respectively.
The second author and Shi [33] studied the log-behavior of the d-Hoggatt numbers.




The paper is organized as follows.
In Section 2, we review the necessary background on symmetric functions.
In Section 3, we combine the Pfaffian minor summation formula with skew Jacobi–Trudi Toeplitz matrices to derive several Schur function identities.
Section 4 introduces the q-refined Baxter numbers and interprets refined Baxter numbers and their q-analogues via rectangular Schur specializations.
In Section 5, we establish a Schur positivity theorem and use it to prove the q-log-convexity of the Baxter polynomials.
Section 6 presents several further log-behavior properties associated with the refined Baxter numbers.
We first show that the Baxter transformation preserves log-convexity.
Then we prove the q-log-concavity of the q-refined Baxter numbers both along each fixed row and along each fixed column and extend these results to the q-analogues of the d-Hoggatt numbers.
The appendices contain several auxiliary symmetric function identities and computational details used in the proofs.





2 Background




In this section, we give an overview of relevant background on symmetric functions.
Throughout this paper, we adopt the notation and terminology for partitions
and symmetric functions in Stanley [39].
Given a nonnegative integer n,
a partition \lambda of n is a weakly decreasing nonnegative integer sequence
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{k})
such that \sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}=n.
The number of nonzero parts \lambda_{i} is called the length of \lambda,
denoted by \ell(\lambda).
We write i^{m_{i}} to indicate that the part i occurs m_{i} times in \lambda.




The Young diagram of \lambda is a left-justified array of cells with \ell(\lambda) rows and \lambda_{i} cells in row i.
Transposing the diagram of \lambda, we get the conjugate partition of \lambda, denoted \lambda^{\prime}.
A cell (i,j) is the cell in row i and
column j.
The hook length of (i,j), denoted h(i,j),
is given by





h(i,j)=\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}^{\prime}-i-j+1.



The content of (i,j), denoted c(i,j), is given by
c(i,j)=j-i.




Given two partitions \lambda and \mu, we say that \lambda contains
\mu, denoted \mu\subseteq\lambda, if \lambda_{i}\geq\mu_{i} for each i.
When \mu\subseteq\lambda, we can define a skew partition \lambda/\mu as the diagram obtained from the diagram of \lambda by removing the cells in the top-left corner corresponding to the diagram of \mu.




A semistandard Young tableau of shape \lambda/\mu is an array T=[T_{ij}] of positive integers of shape \lambda/\mu that is weakly increasing in every row and strictly increasing down every column.
The type of T is defined as the composition
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots), where \alpha_{i} is the number of i’s in T.
Let X denote the set of variables \{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\}.
If T has type \alpha, then we write





X^{T}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}x_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots.






The skew Schur function s_{\lambda/\mu}(X) of shape \lambda/\mu is
defined as the generating function





s_{\lambda/\mu}(X)=\sum_{T}X^{T},



over all semistandard Young tableaux T of shape \lambda/\mu
filled with positive integers.
When \mu is the empty partition \varnothing, we call s_{\lambda}(X) the Schur function of shape \lambda.
We set s_{\emptyset}(X)=1. Throughout this paper,
we adopt the convention that s_{\lambda}=0 whenever \lambda
does not define a valid partition.




A symmetric function f is called Schur positive
if the coefficients a_{\lambda} in the expansion
f=\sum_{\lambda}a_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}
are all nonnegative,
and we denote \left[s_{\lambda}\right]f=a_{\lambda}.




For a Schur function s_{\lambda},
define the standard involution \omega(s_{\lambda})=s_{\lambda^{\prime}},
where \lambda^{\prime} is the conjugate partition of \lambda.
Let f,g be two symmetric functions and a,b\in\mathbb{R}.
Then





\omega(af+bg)=a\omega(f)+b\omega(g)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\omega(fg)=\omega(f)\omega(g).






Lemma 2.1.



Let f be a symmetric function.
Then f is Schur positive if and only if \omega(f) is Schur positive.





Let Y=\{y_{1},y_{2},\ldots\} be another set of variables, and let
s_{\lambda/\mu}(X,Y) denote the skew Schur function in X\cup Y.
The following basic property will be used later:








s_{\lambda/\mu}(X,Y)=\sum_{\nu}s_{\lambda/\nu}(X)s_{\nu/\mu}(Y),

(2)


where the sum ranges over all partitions \nu satisfying
\mu\subseteq\nu\subseteq\lambda; see [39, Section 7].




Let





e_{k}(X)=\sum_{i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}}x_{i_{1}}\dots x_{i_{k}}



be the elementary symmetric functions with e_{0}=1 and e_{k}=0 for k<0; and let





h_{k}(X)=\sum_{i_{1}\leq\ldots\leq i_{k}}x_{i_{1}}\dots x_{i_{k}}



be the complete homogeneous symmetric functions
with h_{0}=1 and h_{k}=0 for k<0.
Then





e_{k}(X)=s_{(1^{k})}(X)\quad\textrm{and}\quad h_{k}(X)=s_{(k)}(X).



The skew Jacobi–Trudi identity (see [39, Theorem 7.16.1]) expresses the skew Schur function
s_{\lambda/\mu} as a determinant of complete homogeneous symmetric
functions,





s_{\lambda/\mu}=\det(h_{\lambda_{i}-\mu_{j}-i+j})_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell(\lambda)}.

(3)


Note that for a two-row partition \lambda=(a,b) and \mu=\varnothing, where a\geq b,
the Jacobi–Trudi identity gives





s_{(a,b)}=\det\begin{bmatrix}h_{a}&h_{a+1}\\
h_{b-1}&h_{b}\end{bmatrix}=h_{a}h_{b}-h_{a+1}h_{b-1}.

(4)





Recall that a lattice permutation of length n is a sequence
w_{1}w_{2}\cdots w_{n} such that for any i and j in the subsequence
w_{1}w_{2}\cdots w_{j} the number of i’s is greater than or equal to
the number of i+1’s.
Let T be a semistandard Young tableau.
The reverse reading word T^{\mathrm{rev}} is a sequence of entries of
T obtained by first reading each row from right to left and then
concatenating the rows from top to bottom.
If the reverse reading word T^{\mathrm{rev}} is a lattice permutation, we call T a Littlewood–Richardson tableau.
The Littlewood–Richardson rule enables us to expand a product of Schur functions in terms of Schur functions.
Given two Schur functions s_{\mu} and s_{\nu}, Littlewood–Richardson coefficients
c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda} are defined by the following relation





s_{\mu}s_{\nu}=\sum_{\lambda}c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}s_{\lambda},

(5)


where c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda} is the number of Littlewood–Richardson tableaux of shape \lambda/\mu and content \nu [39, A1.5].
For example, if \lambda=(4,3,1),\,\mu=(2,1),\,\nu=(3,2), then
c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}=2. Indeed, there are two
Littlewood–Richardson tableaux of shape \lambda/\mu and content \nu
as shown in Figure 1.

When \nu=(k) or \nu=(1^{k}) in (5), the Littlewood–Richardson rule has
a simpler description, known as Pieri’s rule.
We need the notion of horizontal and vertical strips.
A skew partition \lambda/\mu is called a horizontal (or vertical) strip of size k if there are k cells in total with no two cells lying in the same column (resp. in the same row).




Theorem 2.2.



[39, Theorem 7.15.7, Corollary 7.15.9]

We have





s_{\mu}h_{k}=\sum_{\lambda}s_{\lambda},



summed over all partitions \lambda such that \lambda/\mu is a horizontal strip of size k, and





s_{\mu}e_{k}=\sum_{\lambda}s_{\lambda},



summed over all partitions \lambda such that \lambda/\mu is a vertical strip of size k.






3 Main results




In this section, we combine the Pfaffian minor summation formula (1) with Jacobi–Trudi Toeplitz matrices
to derive some identities involving symmetric functions.
We first recall some basic properties of \Pf(M); see [27].




Lemma 3.1.



Let M=[M_{ij}]_{1\leq i,j\leq 2n} be a skew-symmetric matrix.


(i) 



For a fixed row index i,
if M_{ij}=0 for 1\leq j\leq 2n, then \Pf(M)=0.


(ii) 



The first-row expansion formula for the Pfaffian is





\Pf(M)=\sum_{j=2}^{2n}(-1)^{j}M_{1j}\Pf\left(M_{[2n]\setminus\{1,j\},[2n]\setminus\{1,j\}}\right).









Throughout this section, the row and column indices of all matrices start at 1, and we use
\{j_{1},j_{2},\cdots,j_{m}\}_{<} to denote
the set satisfying j_{1}<j_{2}<\cdots<j_{m}.
Recall that h_{k}
is the k-th complete homogeneous symmetric function.
Let m be a positive integer and \mu be a partition with \ell(\mu)\leq m.
Define the row–truncated Toeplitz
matrix





H^{(m)}_{\mu}=\bigl[h_{j-i-\mu_{m-i+1}}\bigr]_{1\leq i\leq m,\ j\geq 1}.



Define H^{(m)}=H^{(m)}_{\varnothing}.
The following is a corollary of the skew Jacobi–Trudi identity.




Lemma 3.2.



Let J=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}\}_{<}. Then we have





\det\left(H^{(m)}_{\mu}\right)_{[m],J}=s_{\lambda/\mu},



where \lambda=\bigl(j_{m}-m,j_{m-1}-(m-1),\ldots,j_{1}-1\bigr).





Proof.



Let H=H_{\mu}^{(m)}.
By definition,





H_{[m],\{j_{1},j_{2},\cdots,j_{m}\}}=\begin{bmatrix}h_{j_{1}-1-\mu_{m}}&h_{j_{2}-1-\mu_{m}}&\cdots&h_{j_{m}-1-\mu_{m}}\\
h_{j_{1}-2-\mu_{m-1}}&h_{j_{2}-2-\mu_{m-1}}&\cdots&h_{j_{m}-2-\mu_{m-1}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
h_{j_{1}-m-\mu_{1}}&h_{j_{2}-m-\mu_{1}}&\vdots&h_{j_{m}-m-\mu_{1}}\end{bmatrix}.



After reversing the orders of both the rows and columns and then transposing the matrix, we obtain, by the skew Jacobi–Trudi identity (3),





\det H_{[m],\{j_{1},j_{2},\cdots,j_{m}\}}=\det\begin{bmatrix}h_{j_{m}-m-\mu_{1}}&h_{j_{m}-m+1-\mu_{2}}&\cdots&h_{j_{m}-1-\mu_{m}}\\
h_{j_{m-1}-m-\mu_{1}}&h_{j_{m-1}-m+1-\mu_{2}}&\cdots&h_{j_{m-1}-1-\mu_{m}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
h_{j_{1}-m-\mu_{1}}&h_{j_{1}-m+1-\mu_{2}}&\vdots&h_{j_{1}-1-\mu_{m}}\end{bmatrix}=s_{\lambda/\mu},



where \lambda_{k}=j_{m-k+1}-(m-k+1) for k=1,2,\dots,m.
∎





3.1 Toeplitz–Pfaffian Schur expansions




Let \mathbf{v}=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots) be commuting indeterminates, and define
the infinite skew-symmetric matrix \Omega(\mathbf{v}) by





\Omega(\mathbf{v})_{i,i+1}=v_{i},\quad\Omega(\mathbf{v})_{i+1,i}=-v_{i}\quad(i\geq 1),



with all other entries equal to zero.
Throughout this subsection, all infinite sums are interpreted
coefficientwise with respect to the usual grading of the ring of
symmetric functions.




Theorem 3.3.



For n\geq 1 and \ell(\mu)\leq 2n, we have





\Pf\left(H^{(2n)}_{\mu}\Omega(\mathbf{v})\bigl(H^{(2n)}_{\mu}\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}\right)=\sum_{0\leq a_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{n}}s_{(a_{n}^{2},a_{n-1}^{2},\ldots,a_{1}^{2})/\mu}\prod_{r=1}^{n}v_{a_{r}+2r-1}.

(6)






Proof.



For N\geq 2n, let H=H^{(2n)}_{\mu} and let H_{[2n],[N]} be the submatrix of H^{(2n)}_{\mu} with the first N columns.
Let \Omega_{N}(\mathbf{v}) be the N-th leading principal submatrix of \Omega(\mathbf{v}).
Lemma 1.1 gives





\Pf\left(H_{[2n],[N]}\Omega_{N}(\mathbf{v})\left(H_{[2n],[N]}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subseteq[N]\\
|J|=2n\end{subarray}}\det H_{[2n],J}\,\Pf\left(\left(\Omega_{N}(\mathbf{v})\right)_{J,J}\right).

(7)





Set J=\{j_{1},\cdots,j_{2n}\}_{<}.
Since the only nonzero entries of \Omega(\mathbf{v}) join consecutive indices, its Pfaffian is nonzero exactly
when
j_{2r}=j_{2r-1}+1,
for 1\leq r\leq n.
In this case,





J=\{j_{1},j_{1}+1,j_{3},j_{3}+1,\cdots,j_{2n-1},j_{2n-1}+1\}



and





\left(\Omega_{N}(\mathbf{v})\right)_{J,J}=\begin{bmatrix}0&v_{j_{1}}&&&&&\\
-v_{j_{1}}&0&\ast&&&&\\
&\ast&0&v_{j_{3}}&&&\\
&&-v_{j_{3}}&0&\ast&&\\
&&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&&&\ast&0&v_{j_{2n-1}}\\
&&&&&-v_{j_{2n-1}}&0\end{bmatrix}.



By the definition of the Pfaffian, we have





\Pf\left(\left(\Omega_{N}(\mathbf{v})\right)_{J,J}\right)=\prod_{r=1}^{n}v_{j_{2r-1}}.



Let
a_{r}=j_{2r-1}-(2r-1).
Then we have 0\leq a_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{n}, and





\Pf\left(\left(\Omega_{N}(\mathbf{v})\right)_{J,J}\right)=\prod_{r=1}^{n}v_{a_{r}+2r-1}.






Note that





j_{2r-1}=a_{r}+2r-1,\quad j_{2r}=a_{r}+2r.



By Lemma 3.2, we obtain \det H_{[2n],J}=s_{\lambda/\mu}, where





\lambda=(j_{2n-1}+1-2n,j_{2n-1}-(2n-1),\ldots,j_{1}-1)=(a_{n},a_{n},a_{n-1},a_{n-1},\ldots,a_{1},a_{1}).



For every fixed homogeneous degree, only finitely many tuples
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}) contribute.
Hence both sides stabilize coefficientwise as N\to\infty.
Substitution into (7) and letting N\to\infty proves (6).
∎





Recall that H^{(2n)}=H^{(2n)}_{\varnothing}.
The following is an immediate consequence of Theorem 3.3.




Corollary 3.4.



Let \mathbf{v}=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots)=(1,t,t^{2},\ldots),
i.e., v_{i}=t^{i-1}.
Then for every n\geq 1,





\Pf\left(H^{(2n)}\Omega(\mathbf{v})\left(H^{(2n)}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\right)=\sum_{0\leq a_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{n}}s_{(a_{n}^{2},a_{n-1}^{2},\ldots,a_{1}^{2})}\,t^{n(n-1)+\sum_{r=1}^{n}a_{r}}.







Define
\Omega^{(p)}(\mathbf{v}) by





\Omega^{(p)}(\mathbf{v})_{i,i+1}=v_{i},\quad\Omega^{(p)}(\mathbf{v})_{i+1,i}=-v_{i}\quad(i\geq p),

(8)


with all other entries equal to zero.
In other words,
\Omega^{(p)}(\mathbf{v})=\Omega(\mathbf{v}) with v_{1}=\cdots=v_{p-1}=0.
For n\geq 0 and p\geq 2,
define





\widetilde{H}^{(2n+1)}_{\mu}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&H^{(2n+1)}_{\mu}\end{bmatrix},\quad\widetilde{\Omega}^{(p)}(\mathbf{v})=\begin{bmatrix}0&w_{p-1}\\
-w_{p-1}^{\mathsf{T}}&\Omega^{(p)}(\mathbf{v})\end{bmatrix},



where w_{r}=(0,\dots,0,1,0,\dots) is a row vector with a 1 at the r-th position.
For example,





\widetilde{H}^{(3)}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0&\cdots&\\
&h_{0}&h_{1}&h_{2}&\cdots\\
&&h_{0}&h_{1}&\cdots\\
&&&h_{0}&\cdots\\
\end{bmatrix},\quad\widetilde{\Omega}^{(3)}(\mathbf{v})=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&1&0&0&0&\cdots\\
0&0&&&&&\\
-1&&0&&&&\\
0&&&0&v_{3}&&\\
0&&&-v_{3}&0&v_{4}&\\
0&&&&-v_{4}&0&\ddots\\
\vdots&&&&&\ddots&\ddots\end{bmatrix}.



Note that \widetilde{H}^{(2n+1)}_{\mu}\widetilde{\Omega}^{(p)}(\mathbf{v})\bigl(\widetilde{H}^{(2n+1)}_{\mu}\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}} equals





\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}0&w_{p-1}(H^{(2n+1)}_{\mu})^{\mathsf{T}}\\
-H^{(2n+1)}_{\mu}w_{p-1}^{\mathsf{T}}&H^{(2n+1)}_{\mu}\Omega^{(p)}(\mathbf{v})\bigl(H^{(2n+1)}_{\mu}\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}.






Theorem 3.5.



For n\geq 0, p\geq 2, and \ell(\mu)\leq 2n+1, we have





\Pf\left(\widetilde{H}^{(2n+1)}_{\mu}\widetilde{\Omega}^{(p)}(\mathbf{v})\bigl(\widetilde{H}^{(2n+1)}_{\mu}\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}\right)=\sum_{0\leq a_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{n}}s_{\bigl((a_{n}+p-2)^{2},\ldots,(a_{1}+p-2)^{2},p-2\bigr)/\mu}\prod_{r=1}^{n}v_{a_{r}+p+2r-2}.

(9)






Proof.



For N\geq\max\{2n+2,p\},
let \widetilde{H}=\widetilde{H}^{(2n+1)}_{\mu} and let \widetilde{H}_{[2n+2],[N]} be the submatrix of \widetilde{H}^{(2n+1)}_{\mu} with the first N columns.
Let \widetilde{\Omega}^{(p)}_{N}(\mathbf{v}) be the N-th leading principal submatrix of \widetilde{\Omega}^{(p)}(\mathbf{v}).
Lemma 1.1 gives





\Pf\left(\widetilde{H}_{[2n+2],[N]}\widetilde{\Omega}^{(p)}_{N}(\mathbf{v})\left(\widetilde{H}_{[2n+2],[N]}\right)^{T}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subseteq[N]\\
|J|=2n+2\end{subarray}}\det\widetilde{H}_{[2n+2],J}\,\Pf\left(\left(\widetilde{\Omega}^{(p)}_{N}(\mathbf{v})\right)_{J,J}\right).

(10)





Set J=\{j_{1},j_{2},\cdots,j_{2n+2}\}_{<}.
Since the first row of \widetilde{H}^{(2n+1)} has its only nonzero entry in the first column,





\det\widetilde{H}_{[2n+2],J}=0



unless j_{1}=1.
Assume henceforth that j_{1}=1.
By the definition of
\widetilde{\Omega}^{(p)}_{N}(\mathbf{v}), its first row is nonzero only
in column p.
Consequently,





\Pf\left(\left(\widetilde{\Omega}^{(p)}_{N}(\mathbf{v})\right)_{J,J}\right)=0



unless j_{2}=p.
Suppose that j_{1}=1,j_{2}=p. For the remaining indices, a necessary condition for a nonzero Pfaffian is that j_{2r+2}=j_{2r+1}+1 for all 1\leq r\leq n.
In this case, we have





J=\{1,p,j_{3},j_{3}+1,j_{5},j_{5}+1,\dots,j_{2n+1},j_{2n+1}+1\}



and





\left(\widetilde{\Omega}^{(p)}_{N}(\mathbf{v})\right)_{J,J}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&&&&&\\
-1&0&\ast&&&&\\
&\ast&0&v_{j_{3}-1}&&&\\
&&-v_{j_{3}-1}&0&\ast&&\\
&&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&&&\ast&0&v_{j_{2n+1}-1}\\
&&&&&-v_{j_{2n+1}-1}&0\end{bmatrix}.



By the definition of the Pfaffian, we have





\Pf\left(\left(\widetilde{\Omega}^{(p)}_{N}(\mathbf{v})\right)_{J,J}\right)=\prod_{r=1}^{n}v_{j_{2r+1}-1}.



Set a_{r}=j_{2r+1}-p-2r+1. Then we have 0\leq a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq\cdots\leq a_{n},
and





\Pf\left(\left(\widetilde{\Omega}^{(p)}_{N}(\mathbf{v})\right)_{J,J}\right)=\prod_{r=1}^{n}v_{a_{r}+p+2r-2}.






Note that





j_{2r+1}=a_{r}+p+2r-1,\quad j_{2r+2}=a_{r}+p+2r.



By Lemma 3.2, we obtain





\det\left(\widetilde{H}_{[2n+2],J}\right)=\det\left(H^{(2n+1)}_{\mu}\right)_{[2n+1],\{p-1,j_{3}-1,\ldots,j_{2n+1}-1,\,j_{2n+1}\}}=s_{\lambda/\mu},



where the partition \lambda equals





\displaystyle\left(j_{2n+1}-(2n+1),\,j_{2n+1}-(2n+1),\,\ldots,\,p-2\right)=\left((a_{n}+p-2)^{2},\ldots,(a_{1}+p-2)^{2},p-2\right).



For every fixed homogeneous degree, only finitely many tuples
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}) contribute.
Hence both sides stabilize coefficientwise as N\to\infty.
Substituting into (10) and letting N\to\infty proves (9).
∎

3.2 Applications




In this subsection,
we present some applications of our main results.
We first need the following lemma.
Recall that H^{(n)}=H^{(n)}_{\varnothing}=\bigl[h_{j-i}\bigr]_{1\leq i\leq n,\ j\geq 1}.




Lemma 3.7.



Given p\geq 1, let \Omega^{(p)}(\mathbf{v}) be an infinite skew-symmetric matrix defined in (8) with
v_{i}=0 for i<p and v_{i}=t^{i-p} for i\geq p.
Let





M^{(n,p)}=H^{(n)}\Omega^{(p)}(\mathbf{v})\bigl(H^{(n)}\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}.



Then M^{(n,p)} is an n\times n skew-symmetric matrix.
For
1\leq i<j\leq n, we have





\displaystyle M^{(n,p)}_{ij}=\sum_{k\geq 0}s_{(k+p-i,k+p+1-j)}\,t^{k}.

(12)






Proof.



Direct computation gives





\bigl(M^{(n,p)}\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}=H^{(n)}\bigl(\Omega^{(p)}(\mathbf{v})\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}\bigl(H^{(n)}\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}=-M^{(n,p)},



since \Omega^{(p)}(\mathbf{v}) is skew-symmetric.
Thus, M^{(n,p)} is an n\times n skew-symmetric matrix.




By matrix multiplication,





M^{(n,p)}_{ij}=\sum_{a,b\geq 1}h_{a-i}\,\left(\Omega^{(p)}(\mathbf{v})_{a,b}\right)\,h_{b-j}.



The only nonzero entries of \Omega^{(p)}(\mathbf{v}) are





\Omega^{(p)}(\mathbf{v})_{r,r+1}=t^{r-p},\quad\Omega^{(p)}(\mathbf{v})_{r+1,r}=-t^{r-p},\quad r\geq p.



It follows that





M^{(n,p)}_{ij}=\sum_{r\geq p}\left(h_{r-i}h_{r+1-j}-h_{r+1-i}h_{r-j}\right)\,t^{r-p}.



By the two-row Jacobi–Trudi identity (4),





h_{r-i}h_{r+1-j}-h_{r+1-i}h_{r-j}=s_{(r-i,r+1-j)}.



Replacing r by
k+p yields (12).
∎





Note that for a 4\times 4 skew-symmetric matrix M,





\Pf(M)=M_{12}M_{34}-M_{13}M_{24}+M_{14}M_{23}.



For any family of partitions \lambda(k) depending on k, we write





S_{\lambda(k)}(t):=\sum_{k\geq 0}s_{\lambda(k)}t^{k},



and we suppress the argument t whenever no confusion can arise. Here all symmetric function generating series identities involving t
are interpreted in the formal power-series ring of symmetric functions over \mathbb{Q}.
We use S_{\lambda}^{2} to denote S_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}.
For example, equation (12) can be written as





M^{(n,p)}_{ij}=S_{(k+p-i,k+p+1-j)}.






The following identity is equivalent to
Chen, Wang and Yang [7, Theorem 6.7] after applying
the standard involution \omega.




Theorem 3.8.



We have





t\cdot\left(S_{(k,k)}S_{(k+2,k)}-S_{(k+1,k)}^{2}\right)+S_{(k,k)}^{2}=\sum_{0\leq b\leq a}s_{(a,a,b,b)}\,t^{a+b}.

(13)






Proof.



Let \mathbf{v}=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots)=(1,t,t^{2},\ldots)
and \Omega^{(1)}=\Omega^{(1)}(\mathbf{v}).
Let M=M^{(4,1)}=H^{(4)}\Omega^{(1)}\left(H^{(4)}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}.
By Lemma 3.7,





M_{ij}=\sum_{k\geq 1}s_{(k-i,k+1-j)}\,t^{k-1}



for i<j. Consequently, M_{12}=S_{(k,k)}, M_{13}=t\cdot S_{(k+1,k)},





\displaystyle M_{14}=t^{2}\cdot S_{(k+2,k)},\quad M_{23}=t\cdot S_{(k,k)},\quad M_{24}=t^{2}\cdot S_{(k+1,k)},\quad M_{34}=t^{2}\cdot S_{(k,k)}.



Hence, the Pfaffian \Pf(M) equals





\displaystyle M_{12}M_{34}-M_{13}M_{24}+M_{14}M_{23}=t^{3}\cdot\left(S_{(k,k)}S_{(k+2,k)}-S_{(k+1,k)}^{2}\right)+t^{2}\cdot S_{(k,k)}^{2}.



By Corollary 3.4,





\Pf(M)=\sum_{0\leq b\leq a}s_{(a,a,b,b)}\,t^{a+b+2}.



Equation (13) follows by comparing the two expressions of \Pf(M).
∎





Chen, Wang and Yang [7] proved the q-log-convexity of the Narayana polynomials.
Following their notation, one of the key steps is to prove the Schur positivity of D(r)=\sum_{k=0}^{r}D(r,k,0,0), where





D(r,k,0,0)=s_{(2^{k})}\,s_{(2^{r-k-1})}+s_{(2^{k-1},1^{2})}\,s_{(2^{r-k-1})}-s_{(2^{k-1},1)}\,s_{(2^{r-k-1},1)}.



See, for instance, [7, Section 3].




Corollary 3.9.



([7, Theorem 3.1])
For r\geq 1, the symmetric function D(r) is Schur positive.





Proof.



By Lemma 2.1,
it suffices to prove the Schur positivity of the standard involution





\omega(D(r))=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\left(s_{(k,k)}\,s_{(r-k-1,r-k-1)}+s_{(k+1,k-1)}\,s_{(r-k-1,r-k-1)}-s_{(k,k-1)}\,s_{(r-k,r-k-1)}\right).



By the convolution formula for the coefficients of formal power series,





S_{(k,k)}^{2}=\sum_{r}t^{r}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{r}s_{(k,k)}s_{(r-k,r-k)}\right)=\sum_{r}t^{r-1}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}s_{(k,k)}s_{(r-k-1,r-k-1)}\right)



Applying the same argument to the other two terms shows
that \omega(D(r)) equals the coefficient of t^{r-1} in





t\cdot\left(S_{(k,k)}S_{(k+2,k)}-S_{(k+1,k)}^{2}\right)+S_{(k,k)}^{2}.



By Theorem 3.8,





t\cdot\left(S_{(k,k)}S_{(k+2,k)}-S_{(k+1,k)}^{2}\right)+S_{(k,k)}^{2}=\sum_{0\leq b\leq a}s_{(a,a,b,b)}\,t^{a+b}.



Taking a=r-q-1 and b=q, we have





\omega(D(r))=\sum_{0\leq q\leq r-q-1}s_{(r-q-1,r-q-1,q,q)}=\sum_{q=0}^{\lfloor{\frac{r-1}{2}\rfloor}}s_{(r-q-1,r-q-1,q,q)},



and the Schur positivity of D(r) follows.
∎

3.3 Vandermonde–Pfaffian Schur expansions




In this subsection, we show that Corollary 3.4 can be obtained by the earlier Schur–Pfaffian formulas of Ishikawa, Okada and Wakayama [21].
They combined the Pfaffian formula (1) with Vandermonde matrices.
For an arbitrary
skew-symmetric matrix
A=[A_{ij}]_{i,j\geq 1}
and a Vandermonde matrix V=[x_{i}^{j-1}]_{1\leq i\leq 2n,\,j\geq 1},
the formula in [21, Theorem 2.3] states that





\sum_{\ell(\lambda)\leq 2n}\operatorname{Pf}\bigl(A_{J(\lambda),J(\lambda)}\bigr)s_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{\prod_{1\leq r<s\leq 2n}(x_{s}-x_{r})}\operatorname{Pf}\left(VAV^{\mathsf{T}}\right),

(17)


where \lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{2n}\geq 0 and





J(\lambda)=\{\lambda_{2n}+1,\lambda_{2n-1}+2,\ldots,\lambda_{2}+2n-1,\lambda_{1}+2n\}_{<}.



Take
A=\Omega(\mathbf{v}) for
\mathbf{v}=(1,t,t^{2},\ldots).
Then A_{i,i+1}=t^{i-1} and A_{i+1,i}=-t^{i-1}, with i\geq 1
and all other entries equal to 0.
By the definition of the Pfaffian,
\Pf\bigl(A_{J(\lambda),J(\lambda)}\bigr)\neq 0 if and only if





(\lambda_{2n}+1)+1=\lambda_{2n-1}+2,\,(\lambda_{2n-2}+3)+1=\lambda_{2n-3}+4,\,(\lambda_{2n-4}+5)+1=\lambda_{2n-5}+6,\ldots.



Equivalently, \lambda_{2n-2k+2}=\lambda_{2n-2k+1} for all 1\leq k\leq n.
In this case,





\Pf\bigl(A_{J(\lambda),J(\lambda)}\bigr)=t^{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left((\lambda_{2n-2k+2}+2k-1)-1\right)}=t^{n(n-1)+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{2n-2k+2}}.



Let a_{k}=\lambda_{2n-2k+2}. Then 0\leq a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq\dots\leq a_{n}
and the left-hand side of (17) equals





\sum_{0\leq a_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{n}}s_{(a^{2}_{n},a^{2}_{n-1},\ldots,a^{2}_{1})}\,t^{n(n-1)+\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}}.

(18)





Define the 2n\times 2n matrix
C=[C_{ij}]_{1\leq i,j\leq 2n}
with





C_{ij}=\frac{x_{j}^{2n-i}}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq s\leq 2n\\
s\neq j\end{subarray}}(x_{j}-x_{s})},



and define V=[x_{i}^{j-1}]_{1\leq i\leq 2n,\,j\geq 1}.
Recall that H^{(2n)}=\bigl[h_{j-i}\bigr]_{1\leq i\leq 2n,\,j\geq 1}.
The following result gives the relation among H^{(2n)}, C, and V.




Proposition 3.11.



After specializing X to the finite alphabet \{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{2n}\}, we have





H^{(2n)}=CV.



Moreover,





\det C=\frac{1}{\prod_{1\leq r<s\leq 2n}(x_{s}-x_{r})}.







Proof.



The proof follows from the classical identity of Sylvester (see [2, (1.1)])





h_{m}=\sum_{r=1}^{2n}\frac{x_{r}^{m+2n-1}}{\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq s\leq 2n\\
s\neq r\end{subarray}}(x_{r}-x_{s})},



for m\geq 0, together with some elementary linear-algebraic manipulations.
We will omit the proof here and leave it to the reader.
∎





For any 2n\times 2n matrix H and any 2n\times 2n skew-symmetric matrix \Omega, Lemma 1.1 reduces to
\Pf(H\Omega H^{\mathsf{T}})=\det(H)\Pf(\Omega).
The right-hand side of (17) equals





\frac{1}{\prod_{1\leq r<s\leq 2n}(x_{s}-x_{r})}\Pf\left(VAV^{\mathsf{T}}\right)=\det(C)\Pf(VAV^{\mathsf{T}})=\Pf\left(H^{(2n)}A(H^{(2n)})^{\mathsf{T}}\right).

(19)


Combining (18) and (19) yields the Schur expansion that appears in Corollary 3.4.






4 Refined Baxter numbers and q-refined Baxter numbers




In this section, we realize the refined and q-refined Baxter
numbers as principal specializations.
Recall that





B(n,k)=\frac{\binom{n}{k}\binom{n+1}{k}\binom{n+2}{k}}{\binom{k+1}{k}\binom{k+2}{k}},\quad 0\leq k\leq n.



Motivated by the definition of shifted q-binomial coefficients and q-Narayana numbers [3, 16], we define the q-refined Baxter numbers as





\displaystyle B_{q}(n,k)=q^{\frac{3k(k-1)}{2}}\frac{\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}\begin{bmatrix}n+1\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}\begin{bmatrix}n+2\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}}{\begin{bmatrix}k+1\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}\begin{bmatrix}k+2\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}},



where





[k]_{q}\coloneqq\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-q},\qquad[k]_{q}!\coloneqq[1]_{q}[2]_{q}\cdots[k]_{q},\qquad\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}\coloneqq\frac{[n]_{q}!}{[k]_{q}![n-k]_{q}!}.



For n\geq 0 and k\in\mathbb{Z}, we adopt the convention





B(n,k)=B_{q}(n,k)=0\quad\text{unless}\quad 0\leq k\leq n.



We remark that Cigler [10] defined a different q-analogue of the refined Baxter numbers.




For a Schur function s_{\lambda},
its principal specialization is defined by





\operatorname{ps}_{n}(s_{\lambda})=s_{\lambda}(1,q,\ldots,q^{n-1})\quad\textrm{and}\quad\operatorname{ps}^{1}_{n}(s_{\lambda})=s_{\lambda}(1^{n}).



In particular,





\operatorname{ps}_{0}^{1}(s_{\lambda})=\begin{cases}1,&\lambda=\varnothing,\\
0,&\lambda\neq\varnothing.\end{cases}



The following hook-content formula was first stated explicitly by Stanley [37].




Lemma 4.1.



[39, Theorem 7.21.2, Corollary 7.21.4]

Let \lambda be a partition with at most n parts.
Then





\operatorname{ps}_{n}(s_{\lambda})=q^{\sum_{i\geq 1}(i-1)\lambda_{i}}\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}\frac{1-q^{n+j-i}}{1-q^{h(i,j)}}



and





\operatorname{ps}_{n}^{1}(s_{\lambda})=\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}\frac{n+j-i}{h(i,j)},



where h(i,j) is the hook length of \lambda at (i,j).





The following result is motivated by Cigler [10].




Lemma 4.2.



For n\geq k\geq 0, we have B_{q}(n,k)=\operatorname{ps}_{n}\left(s_{(3^{k})}\right).





Proof.



For the partition \lambda=(3^{k}), it is easy to check that





\sum_{i\geq 1}(i-1)\lambda_{i}=\frac{3k(k-1)}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad h(i,j)=k-i-j+4.



By Lemma 4.1, we deduce that





\displaystyle\operatorname{ps}_{n}\left(s_{(3^{k})}\right)
\displaystyle=q^{\frac{3k(k-1)}{2}}\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}\frac{1-q^{n+j-i}}{1-q^{h(i,j)}}





\displaystyle=q^{\frac{3k(k-1)}{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{3}\frac{[n-i+j]_{q}}{[k-i-j+4]_{q}}

(20)




\displaystyle=q^{\frac{3k(k-1)}{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{[n-i+1]_{q}[n-i+2]_{q}[n-i+3]_{q}}{[k-i+1]_{q}[k-i+2]_{q}[k-i+3]_{q}}.



Since





\prod_{i=1}^{k}[n-i+1]_{q}[n-i+2]_{q}[n-i+3]_{q}=\frac{[n]_{q}!}{[n-k]_{q}!}\cdot\frac{[n+1]_{q}!}{[n-k+1]_{q}!}\cdot\frac{[n+2]_{q}!}{[n-k+2]_{q}!}



and





\prod_{i=1}^{k}[k-i+1]_{q}[k-i+2]_{q}[k-i+3]_{q}=\frac{[k]_{q}!}{[0]_{q}!}\cdot\frac{[k+1]_{q}!}{[1]_{q}!}\cdot\frac{[k+2]_{q}!}{[2]_{q}!}=\frac{[k]_{q}![k+1]_{q}![k+2]_{q}!}{[2]_{q}!},



so we have





\displaystyle\operatorname{ps}_{n}\left(s_{(3^{k})}\right)
\displaystyle=q^{\frac{3k(k-1)}{2}}\frac{[n]_{q}![n+1]_{q}![n+2]_{q}!\cdot[2]_{q}!}{[k]_{q}![k+1]_{q}![k+2]_{q}!\cdot[n-k]_{q}![n-k+1]_{q}![n-k+2]_{q}!}





\displaystyle=q^{\frac{3k(k-1)}{2}}\frac{\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}\begin{bmatrix}n+1\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}\begin{bmatrix}n+2\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}}{\begin{bmatrix}k+1\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}\begin{bmatrix}k+2\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}}=B_{q}(n,k).



This completes the proof.
∎

5 q-Log-convexity




Let





B_{n}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}B(n,k)x^{k}.



Thus B_{n}(1)=B_{n}.
We call B_{n}(x) the n-th Baxter polynomial.
The main objective of this section is to prove the q-log-convexity of the Baxter polynomials, i.e.,





\left[x^{r}\right]\left(B_{n-1}(x)B_{n+1}(x)-B_{n}(x)^{2}\right)\geq 0



for all 0\leq r\leq 2n, where \left[x^{r}\right]f(x) is the coefficient of x^{r} in f(x).
Since q is already used in the definition of B_{q}(n,k),
we use x as the polynomial variable to avoid confusion.




Theorem 5.1.



The Baxter polynomials B_{n}(x) form a q-log-convex sequence.





Taking x=1, it is immediate that the sequence of the Baxter numbers
(B_{n})_{n\geq 0} forms a log-convex sequence,
which was first proved in [33, Proposition 6.2].




For n\geq 1 and r\geq 0, define





C_{1}(r)=\left[x^{r}\right]\left(B_{n+1}(x)B_{n-1}(x)\right)\quad\textrm{and}\quad C_{2}(r)=\left[x^{r}\right]\left(B_{n}(x)^{2}\right).



Then, by Lemma 4.3, we have





C_{1}(r)=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\operatorname{ps}_{n+1}^{1}\!\left(s_{(3^{k})}\right)\operatorname{ps}_{n-1}^{1}\!\left(s_{(3^{\,r-k})}\right)\quad\textrm{and}\quad C_{2}(r)=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\operatorname{ps}_{n}^{1}\!\left(s_{(3^{k})}\right)\operatorname{ps}_{n}^{1}\!\left(s_{(3^{\,r-k})}\right).

\displaystyle\Big(s_{(3^{k-2},2^{2})}+2\,s_{(3^{k-2},2,1)}+s_{(3^{k-1},1)}+s_{(3^{k-2},1^{2})}+2\,s_{(3^{k-1})}+s_{(3^{k-2},2)}\Big)\cdot s_{(3^{r-k})}

For r\geq 1, the symmetric function \sum_{k=0}^{r}F(r,k) is Schur positive.

5.1 Proof of Theorem 5.2




In this subsection, we prove the Schur positivity of
\sum_{k=0}^{r}F(r,k),

\displaystyle F(r,k)={}
\displaystyle\Bigl(s_{(3^{k-2},2^{2})}+2\,s_{(3^{k-2},2,1)}+s_{(3^{k-2},1^{2})}+s_{(3^{k-2},2)}+s_{(3^{k-1},1)}+2\,s_{(3^{k-1})}\Bigr)\cdot s_{(3^{r-k})}

Note that every term in \omega(F(r,k)) is the product of two Schur functions,

5.1.1 The terms of length at most five

5.1.2 The length-six terms




As stated in Section 5.1.1, for every term s_{\mu}s_{\nu} in \omega\left(\sum_{k=0}^{r}F(r,k)\right),
the length of each partition in its Littlewood–Richardson expansion is at most 6.
In this part, we study the relationship between the Schur expansion of F_{i}(r)
and that of F_{i}(r)\big|_{\ell\leq 5} for i=1,2,3.




For a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)}), let





\lambda^{-}=(\lambda_{1}-1,\ldots,\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)}-1)



denote the partition obtained by deleting the first column of its
Young diagram, with zero parts omitted.
Recall that c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda} is the Littlewood–Richardson
coefficient of s_{\mu}s_{\nu}.
We introduce a result concerning c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda} and c_{\mu^{-},\nu^{-}}^{\lambda^{-}}.




Lemma 5.6.



[6, Lemma A.1]

If c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}>0 and
\ell(\lambda)=\ell(\mu)+\ell(\nu), then





c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}=c_{\mu^{-},\nu^{-}}^{\lambda^{-}}.







Thus, in the maximal-length case, simultaneously deleting the first
column from \mu, \nu, and \lambda preserves the corresponding
Littlewood–Richardson coefficient.
With a simple augmentation of
Littlewood–Richardson tableaux, we obtain the following
adding-column result.




Lemma 5.7.



Suppose that \ell(\mu),\ell(\nu)\leq 3 and \ell(\lambda)\leq 6. Then for every
d\geq 0,





c_{\mu+(d^{3}),\nu+(d^{3})}^{\lambda+(d^{6})}=c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}.







Proof.



It suffices to consider d=1.
Suppose that
c_{\mu+(1^{3}),\nu+(1^{3})}^{\lambda+(1^{6})}>0.
Then, by Lemma 5.6,





c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}=c_{\mu+(1^{3}),\nu+(1^{3})}^{\lambda+(1^{6})}.



Now we claim that if c_{\mu+(1^{3}),\nu+(1^{3})}^{\lambda+(1^{6})}=0,
then c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}=0.




Assume, to the contrary, that c_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}>0, and choose a
Littlewood–Richardson tableau T of shape
\lambda/\mu and content \nu.
Add one column to the left of T.
The skew diagram





({\lambda+(1^{6})})/({\mu+(1^{3})})



then has three additional cells, namely the cells in the
first column of rows 4,5,6. Fill these cells with
1,2,3, respectively, and denote the resulting tableau
by T^{+}.




The new first-column entries are strictly increasing.
Moreover, since \ell(\mu)\leq 3, every old entry in
row 5 is at least 2, and every old entry in row
6 is at least 3. Thus the rows of T^{+} remain
weakly increasing.
In the reverse reading word, the newly inserted entries occur
at the ends of rows 4,5,6, respectively. Hence the
successive additional contributions to the multiplicities
of 1,2,3 are
(1,0,0),\,(1,1,0),\,(1,1,1),
so the lattice-word inequalities are preserved. Therefore
T^{+} is a Littlewood–Richardson tableau of shape
({\lambda+(1^{6})})/({\mu+(1^{3})})
and content \nu+(1^{3}). Consequently,





c_{\mu+(1^{3}),\,\nu+(1^{3})}^{\lambda+(1^{6})}>0,



which is a contradiction. This completes the proof.
∎





Let





\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4},\lambda_{5},\lambda_{6}),



where \lambda_{6}>0.
For each term s_{\mu}s_{\nu} in F_{1}(r),


(i) 



If \ell(\mu)+\ell(\nu)\leq 5, then [s_{\lambda}]s_{\mu}s_{\nu}=0.


(ii) 



If \ell(\mu)+\ell(\nu)=6, then, by Lemma 5.7, we have c^{\lambda}_{\mu,\nu}=c^{\lambda-(1^{6})}_{\mu-(1^{3}),\nu-(1^{3})}, that is,





[s_{\lambda}]s_{\mu}s_{\nu}=[s_{\lambda-(1^{6})}]s_{\mu-(1^{3})}\,s_{\nu-(1^{3})}.





Recall that





\displaystyle F_{1}(r)=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\left[\left(s_{(k,k,k-2)}+s_{(k,k-1,k-1)}\right)\,s_{(r-k,r-k,r-k)}-s_{(k,k,k-1)}\,s_{(r-k,r-k,r-k-1)}\right],



As an example, consider \sum_{k=0}^{r}s_{(k,k,k-2)}\,s_{(r-k,r-k,r-k)}. Then





\sum_{k=0}^{r}s_{(k,k,k-2)}\,s_{(r-k,r-k,r-k)}=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\sum_{\lambda}c_{(k,k,k-2),(r-k,r-k,r-k)}^{\lambda}\,s_{\lambda}.



Note that





\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{r-2}s_{(k,k,k-2)}\,s_{(r-2-k,r-2-k,r-2-k)}
\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}s_{(k-1,k-1,k-3)}\,s_{(r-k-1,r-k-1,r-k-1)}





\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\sum_{\lambda}c_{(k-1,k-1,k-3),(r-k-1,r-k-1,r-k-1)}^{\lambda}\,s_{\lambda}.



By Lemma 5.7,





c_{(k,k,k-2),(r-k,r-k,r-k)}^{\lambda}=c_{(k-1,k-1,k-3),(r-k-1,r-k-1,r-k-1)}^{\lambda-(1^{6})}.



Then





\left[s_{\lambda}\right]\sum_{k=0}^{r}s_{(k,k,k-2)}\,s_{(r-k,r-k,r-k)}=\left[s_{\lambda-(1^{6})}\right]\sum_{k=0}^{r-2}s_{(k,k,k-2)}\,s_{(r-2-k,r-2-k,r-2-k)}.



The same holds for





s_{(k,k-1,k-1)}\,s_{(r-k,r-k,r-k)}\quad\textrm{and}\quad s_{(k,k,k-1)}\,s_{(r-k,r-k,r-k-1)}.



Hence, we obtain





\displaystyle\left[s_{\lambda}\right]F_{1}(r)=\left[s_{\lambda-(1^{6})}\right]F_{1}(r-2).

(28)


Similarly, we have [s_{\lambda}]F_{i}(r)=[s_{\lambda-(1^{6})}]F_{i}(r-2) for i=2,3.




Let d=\lambda_{6}. Applying (28) repeatedly gives

(r-2d,r-2d-j-1,r-2d-j-1,j,j)
for 0\leq j\leq\lfloor(r-2d-1)/2\rfloor, and each occurs with coefficient 1.




On the other hand, from (28), we also obtain





\left[s_{\tilde{\lambda}+(1^{6})}\right]F_{1}(r-2d+2)=\left[s_{\tilde{\lambda}}\right]F_{1}(r-2d).



Therefore, by adding back these d columns of length 6,
i.e., adding d to each part, we have





F_{1}(r)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor(r-2d-1)/2\rfloor}\sum_{d}s_{(r-d,r-d-j-1,r-d-{j}-1,{j}+d,{j}+d,d)}.



Setting j^{\prime}=j+d and then
renaming j^{\prime} as j, we obtain





F_{1}(r)=\sum_{0\leq d\leq j\leq\lfloor(r-1)/2\rfloor}s_{(r-d,r-j-1,r-j-1,j,j,d)}.

(29)





Applying the same argument to (26) and (27), respectively,
gives





F_{2}(r)=\sum_{0\leq d\leq j\leq\lfloor(r-1)/2\rfloor}s_{(r-1-d,r-j-1,r-j-1,j,j,d)},

(30)


and





F_{3}(r)=\sum_{0\leq d<j\leq\lfloor(r-1)/2\rfloor}s_{(r-2-d,r-j-1,r-j-1,j,j,d)}.

(31)





Proof of Theorem 5.2.



By (29), (30) and (31), we obtain that F_{1}(r),F_{2}(r) and F_{3}(r) are Schur positive.
Hence, by (22),





\omega\left(\sum_{k=0}^{r}F(r,k)\right)=F_{1}(r)+2\,F_{2}(r)+F_{3}(r)



is Schur positive.
The Schur positivity of \sum_{k=0}^{r}F(r,k) follows by Lemma 2.1.
∎








6 Log-convexity preservation and q-log-concavity




In this section, we establish two further logarithmic properties
associated with the refined Baxter numbers. We first prove that the
Baxter transformation preserves log-convexity. We then study the
q-log-concavity of the q-refined Baxter numbers in both the row
and column directions, and extend these results to the
q-analogues of the d-Hoggatt numbers.




6.1 The Baxter transformation




We show that the Baxter transformation preserves log-convexity.
The q-log-convexity of a polynomial sequence is closely related to
the preservation of log-convexity by linear transformations.
For a double-indexed sequence A(n,k) with 0\leq k\leq n
and a sequence of nonnegative numbers (a_{k})_{k\geq 0},
define





b_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}A(n,k)a_{k}.



Note that in this section, the double-indexed sequence is indexed from 0 in both parameters.
Liu and Wang [30] systematically studied linear transformations that preserve log-convexity.
They proved that the linear transformations associated with many combinatorial numbers, such as the binomial coefficients, the Stirling numbers of the second kind and the Eulerian numbers preserve log-convexity, i.e., the log-convexity of (a_{k})_{k\geq 0} implies that of (b_{n})_{n\geq 0}.
See [8, 30, 42] for more details.
Chen, Wang and Yang [7] subsequently proved that the linear transformation defined by the Narayana numbers preserves log-convexity.
We present the following result.




Theorem 6.1.



If the sequence (a_{k})_{k\geq 0} of nonnegative real numbers is
log-convex, then so is the sequence (b_{n})_{n\geq 0}, where





b_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}B(n,k)a_{k},\quad n\geq 0.







Following Liu and Wang [30, Section 4.2],
it is not difficult to verify that
for n\geq 1,





b_{n-1}b_{n+1}-b_{n}^{2}=\sum_{r=0}^{2n}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor r/2\right\rfloor}\beta_{k}(n,r)a_{k}a_{r-k}\right),

(32)


and





B_{n-1}(x)B_{n+1}(x)-B_{n}(x)^{2}=\sum_{r=0}^{2n}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor r/2\right\rfloor}\beta_{k}(n,r)\right)x^{r},

(33)


where





\beta_{k}(n,r)=\begin{cases}B(n-1,k)B(n+1,r-k)+B(n-1,r-k)B(n+1,k)\\
\qquad-2B(n,k)B(n,r-k)&\text{if }0\leq k<r/2,\\
B(n-1,k)B(n+1,k)-B^{2}(n,k)&\text{if }k=r/2.\end{cases}



Recall that





B(n,k)=\frac{\binom{n}{k}\binom{n+1}{k}\binom{n+2}{k}}{\binom{k+1}{k}\binom{k+2}{k}}.



The following result can be obtained by direct computation; see [33, Theorem 2.3].




Lemma 6.2.



Given an integer k, the sequence (B(n,k))_{n\geq k} is log-concave.





Proof of Theorem 6.1.



Since the sequence (a_{k})_{k\geq 0} is nonnegative and log-convex, we have





a_{0}a_{r}\geq a_{1}a_{r-1}\geq a_{2}a_{r-2}\geq\cdots.



We claim that, for every fixed n and r, there exists an integer
k^{\prime}=k^{\prime}(n,r) such that





\beta_{k}(n,r)\geq 0\quad\text{for }0\leq k\leq k^{\prime}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\beta_{k}(n,r)\leq 0\quad\text{for }k^{\prime}<k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\right\rfloor.



By Theorem 5.1, (B_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0} is q-log-convex.
Then by (33),





\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor r/2\right\rfloor}\beta_{k}(n,r)\geq 0



for all r.
Therefore,





\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor r/2\right\rfloor}\beta_{k}(n,r)a_{k}a_{r-k}=
\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{k^{\prime}}\beta_{k}(n,r)a_{k}a_{r-k}+\sum_{k=k^{\prime}+1}^{\left\lfloor r/2\right\rfloor}\beta_{k}(n,r)a_{k}a_{r-k}




\displaystyle\geq
\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{k^{\prime}}\beta_{k}(n,r)a_{k^{\prime}}a_{r-k^{\prime}}+\sum_{k=k^{\prime}+1}^{\left\lfloor r/2\right\rfloor}\beta_{k}(n,r)a_{k^{\prime}}a_{r-k^{\prime}}




\displaystyle={}
\displaystyle a_{k^{\prime}}a_{r-k^{\prime}}\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor r/2\right\rfloor}\beta_{k}(n,r)\geq 0.



By (32), for each n, b_{n-1}b_{n+1}-b_{n}^{2}\geq 0 and the sequence (b_{n})_{n\geq 0} is log-convex.




We now prove the existence of k^{\prime}.
Note that for
r=0,





\beta_{0}({n,0})=B(n-1,0)B(n+1,0)-B(n,0)^{2}=0.



We assume that r\geq 1.
Let





\beta^{\prime}_{k}(n,r)=\begin{cases}2\beta_{k}(n,r)&\text{if $r$ is even and $k=r/2$},\\
\beta_{k}(n,r)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(34)


Clearly, \beta^{\prime}_{k}(n,r) and \beta_{k}(n,r) have the same sign.
For 0\leq k\leq\lfloor{r/2\rfloor},





\beta^{\prime}_{k}(n,r)=B(n-1,k)B(n+1,r-k)+B(n-1,r-k)B(n+1,k)-2\,B(n,k)B(n,r-k).



We divide the range of k into three cases:





k\leq r-n-1,\qquad r-n\leq k<r/2,\qquad k=r/2\quad(\textrm{if $r$ is even}).

(35)





For the first interval k\leq r-n-1,





\beta^{\prime}_{k}(n,r)=B(n-1,k)B(n+1,r-k)+B(n+1,k)B(n-1,r-k)\geq 0

(36)


for k\leq r-n-1,
since B(n,r-k)=0.




For the second interval,
consider \max\{0,r-n\}\leq k<r/2.
Then \beta^{\prime}_{k}(n,r) equals






\displaystyle B(n,k)B(n,r-k)\left(\frac{B(n-1,k)B(n+1,r-k)}{B(n,k)B(n,r-k)}+\frac{B(n+1,k)B(n-1,r-k)}{B(n,k)B(n,r-k)}-2\right)




\displaystyle={}
\displaystyle B(n,k)B(n,r-k)\left(\frac{\binom{n-1}{k}}{\binom{n+2}{k}}\frac{\binom{n+3}{r-k}}{\binom{n}{r-k}}+\frac{\binom{n+3}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}\frac{\binom{n-1}{r-k}}{\binom{n+2}{r-k}}-2\right)




\displaystyle={}
\displaystyle B(n,k)B(n,r-k)\left(\frac{n+3}{n}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{2}\frac{n-k+i}{n-r+k+i+1}+\prod_{j=0}^{2}\frac{n-r+k+j}{n-k+j+1}\right)-2\right).



Let





T(k)=\prod_{i=0}^{2}\frac{n-k+i}{n-r+k+i+1}+\prod_{j=0}^{2}\frac{n-r+k+j}{n-k+j+1}.



Then





\beta^{\prime}_{k}(n,r)=B(n,k)B(n,r-k)\left(\frac{n+3}{n}T(k)-2\right).



It is clear that \beta^{\prime}_{k}(n,r) has the same sign as \frac{n+3}{n}T(k)-2 for each k.




Now we prove T(k)-T(k+1)\geq 0.
Note that





T(k+1)=\prod_{i=0}^{2}\frac{n-k-1+i}{n-r+k+i+2}+\prod_{j=0}^{2}\frac{n-r+k+j+1}{n-k+j}.



For convenience, set
a=n-k and b=n-r+k.
Then a\geq 1,\,b\geq 0.
Define





\displaystyle T_{1}(k)
\displaystyle:=\frac{a(a+1)(a+2)}{(b+1)(b+2)(b+3)},\qquad T_{2}(k):=\frac{b(b+1)(b+2)}{(a+1)(a+2)(a+3)},




\displaystyle T_{1}(k+1)
\displaystyle:=\frac{(a-1)a(a+1)}{(b+2)(b+3)(b+4)},\qquad T_{2}(k+1):=\frac{(b+1)(b+2)(b+3)}{a(a+1)(a+2)}.



Then T(k)=T_{1}(k)+T_{2}(k) and T(k+1)=T_{1}(k+1)+T_{2}(k+1).




A direct calculation gives





\displaystyle T_{1}(k)-T_{1}(k+1)
\displaystyle=\frac{3(a+b+3)a(a+1)}{(b+1)(b+2)(b+3)(b+4)},




\displaystyle T_{2}(k+1)-T_{2}(k)
\displaystyle=\frac{3(a+b+3)(b+1)(b+2)}{a(a+1)(a+2)(a+3)}.



Consequently,





\frac{T_{1}(k)-T_{1}({k+1})}{T_{2}({k+1})-T_{2}(k)}=\frac{a(a+1)}{(b+1)(b+2)}\cdot\frac{a(a+1)(a+2)(a+3)}{(b+1)(b+2)(b+3)(b+4)}.

(37)


Since k<r/2,





a-b=(n-k)-(n-r+k)=r-2k\geq 1.



So every factor in the right-hand side of (37) is
greater than or equal to 1. Hence





T_{1}(k)-T_{1}({k+1})\geq T_{2}({k+1})-T_{2}(k).



It follows that





T(k)-T(k+1)=(T_{1}({k})-T_{1}(k+1))-(T_{2}({k+1})-T_{2}(k))\geq 0.



Thus, T(k) is nonincreasing in k.




For the third interval, k=r/2.
Then





\beta^{\prime}_{r/2}(n,r)=2\left(B(n-1,r/2)B(n+1,r/2)-B(n,r/2)^{2}\right).



By Lemma 6.2,
\beta^{\prime}_{r/2}(n,r)\leq 0.
Thus to prove that the index k^{\prime} exists,
it suffices to prove that \beta^{\prime}_{0}(n,r)\geq 0.




Case 1: For r\leq n,
by (35), the range of k is divided into two intervals 0\leq k<r/2
and r/2.
Then





\displaystyle\frac{n+3}{n}T(0)-2
\displaystyle=\prod_{i=0}^{2}\frac{n+1+i}{n-r+1+i}+\prod_{j=0}^{2}\frac{n-r+j}{n+j}-2





\displaystyle=\prod_{i=0}^{2}\left(1+\frac{r}{n-r+1+i}\right)+\prod_{j=0}^{2}\left(1-\frac{r}{n+j}\right)-2





\displaystyle\geq 1+\sum_{i=0}^{2}\frac{r}{n-r+1+i}+1-\sum_{j=0}^{2}\frac{r}{n+j}-2





\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{2}\left(\frac{r}{n-r+1+i}-\frac{r}{n+i}\right)\geq 0,



where the inequality follows from the two elementary inequalities \prod_{i}(1+x_{i})\geq 1+\sum_{i}x_{i} and





\prod_{i}(1-x_{i})\geq 1-\sum_{i}x_{i}\qquad\textrm{for }\quad 0\leq x_{i}\leq 1.






Since \frac{n+3}{n}T(0)-2 and \beta^{\prime}_{0}(n,r) have the same sign,
and T(k) is nonincreasing, the sign of \beta^{\prime}_{k}(n,r) can change at most once from nonnegative to nonpositive.




Case 2: For n<r\leq 2n,
by (35), the range of k is divided into three intervals k\leq r-n-1, r-n\leq k<r/2 and k=r/2.
By (36), \beta^{\prime}_{k}(n,r)\geq 0 in the first interval k\leq r-n-1.
Since T(k) is nonincreasing in the second interval r-n\leq k\leq r/2,
the sign of \beta^{\prime}_{k}(n,r) can change at most once.
Thus, in both cases, the index k^{\prime} exists. This completes the proof.
∎






6.2 q-Log-concavity




In this subsection, we prove the q-log-concavity in
both the row and column directions for the
q-refined Baxter numbers.
Chen, Wang and Yang [7] established the q-log-concavity of the
q-Narayana numbers.
We prove the following results on the q-refined Baxter numbers, in parallel to the results of q-Narayana numbers.




Theorem 6.3.



Given an integer n, the polynomial sequence (B_{q}(n,k))_{0\leq k\leq n} is q-log-concave.





Proof.



By Lemma 4.2, for any k\geq 1,





\displaystyle B_{q}(n,k)^{2}-B_{q}(n,k-1)B_{q}(n,k+1)
\displaystyle=\operatorname{ps}_{n}\left(s_{(3^{k})}\right)\operatorname{ps}_{n}\left(s_{(3^{k})}\right)-\operatorname{ps}_{n}\left(s_{(3^{k+1})}\right)\operatorname{ps}_{n}\left(s_{(3^{k-1})}\right)





\displaystyle=\operatorname{ps}_{n}\left(s_{(3^{k})}s_{(3^{k})}-s_{(3^{k+1})}s_{(3^{k-1})}\right).






Kirillov [26] proved that for a,b\geq 1, we have the following identity for Schur functions





s_{(a^{b})}s_{(a^{b})}=s_{(a^{b+1})}s_{(a^{b-1})}+s_{((a+1)^{b})}s_{((a-1)^{b})}.

(38)


Then





s_{(3^{k})}s_{(3^{k})}-s_{(3^{k+1})}s_{(3^{k-1})}=s_{(4^{k})}s_{(2^{k})}



is Schur positive for any k\geq 0.
Hence, the sequence \left(B_{q}(n,k)\right)_{0\leq k\leq n} is q-log-concave.
∎





The following is the q-analogue of Lemma 6.2.




Theorem 6.4.



Given an integer k, the polynomial sequence (B_{q}(n,k))_{n\geq k} is q-log-concave.





We now turn to the q-log-concavity of the q-refined Baxter numbers for fixed k.
We first need the following result.




Lemma 6.5.



Let r,m,k\geq 0. Then





q^{-m\binom{r}{2}}\operatorname{ps}_{k+3}\left(s_{(m^{r})}\right)\geq_{q}0.







Proof.



Note that if r>k+3, then
\operatorname{ps}_{k+3}(s_{(m^{r})})=0,
and the assertion is immediate. We may therefore assume
r\leq k+3.
Using Lemma 4.1, we have





\operatorname{ps}_{k+3}\left(s_{(m^{r})}\right)=q^{m\binom{r}{2}}\prod_{(i,j)\in(m^{r})}\frac{1-q^{k+3+j-i}}{1-q^{h(i,j)}}.



It is known [31, p. 44] that \prod_{(i,j)\in(m^{r})}\frac{1-q^{k+3+j-i}}{1-q^{h(i,j)}} is a polynomial in q with nonnegative coefficients.
Hence,





q^{-m\binom{r}{2}}s_{(m^{r})}(1,q,\ldots,q^{k+2})=\prod_{(i,j)\in(m^{r})}\frac{1-q^{k+3+j-i}}{1-q^{h(i,j)}}\geq_{q}0.



This completes the proof.
∎





Proof of Theorem 6.4.



Recall that by (20),





\displaystyle B_{q}(k+m,k)
\displaystyle=\operatorname{ps}_{k+m}\left(s_{(3^{k})}\right)=q^{\frac{3k(k-1)}{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{3}\frac{[k+m-i+j]_{q}}{[k-i-j+4]_{q}}.



By reversing the index i in the product formula,





\displaystyle B_{q}(k+m,k)
\displaystyle=q^{\frac{3k(k-1)}{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{[k+m-i+1]_{q}\,[k+m-i+2]_{q}\,[k+m-i+3]_{q}}{[k-i+3]_{q}\,[k-i+2]_{q}\,[k-i+1]_{q}}





\displaystyle=q^{\frac{3k(k-1)}{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{[m+i]_{q}\,[m+i+1]_{q}\,[m+i+2]_{q}}{[i]_{q}\,[i+1]_{q}\,[i+2]_{q}}





\displaystyle=q^{\frac{3k(k-1)}{2}}\cdot\frac{[k+m]_{q}!}{[k]_{q}![m]_{q}!}\cdot\frac{[k+m+1]_{q}![1]_{q}!}{[k+1]_{q}![m+1]_{q}!}\cdot\frac{[k+m+2]_{q}![2]_{q}!}{[k+2]_{q}![m+2]_{q}!}





\displaystyle=q^{\frac{3k(k-1)}{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{3}\frac{[k+m+j-1]_{q}!\,[j-1]_{q}!}{[k+j-1]_{q}!\,[m+j-1]_{q}!}.






On the other hand,
by Lemma 4.1,





\operatorname{ps}_{k+3}\left(s_{(m^{3})}\right)=q^{3m}\prod_{i=1}^{3}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{[k+3-i+j]_{q}}{[m-i-j+4]_{q}}=q^{3m}\prod_{i=1}^{3}\frac{[k+m+3-i]_{q}!\,[3-i]_{q}!}{[k+3-i]_{q}!\,[m+3-i]_{q}!}.



Substituting j=4-i yields





\operatorname{ps}_{k+3}\left(s_{(m^{3})}\right)=q^{3m}\prod_{j=1}^{3}\frac{[k+m+j-1]_{q}!\,[j-1]_{q}!}{[k+j-1]_{q}!\,[m+j-1]_{q}!}.



Consequently,





B_{q}(k+m,k)=q^{\frac{3k(k-1)}{2}-3m}\operatorname{ps}_{k+3}\left(s_{(m^{3})}\right).






For m=0, we have





B_{q}(k,k)^{2}-B_{q}(k-1,k)B_{q}(k+1,k)=B_{q}(k,k)^{2}=q^{3k(k-1)}\geq_{q}0.



For m\geq 1,






\displaystyle B_{q}(k+m,k)^{2}-B_{q}(k+m-1,k)B_{q}(k+m+1,k)




\displaystyle={}
\displaystyle q^{3k(k-1)-6m}\cdot\operatorname{ps}_{k+3}\left(s_{(m^{3})}^{2}-s_{((m-1)^{3})}s_{((m+1)^{3})}\right).



Taking a=m,b=3 in (38) yields





B_{q}(k+m,k)^{2}-B_{q}(k+m-1,k)B_{q}(k+m+1,k)=q^{3k(k-1)-6m}\cdot\operatorname{ps}_{k+3}\left(s_{(m^{2})}s_{(m^{4})}\right).



Then, by Lemma 6.5,






\displaystyle B_{q}(k+m,k)^{2}-B_{q}(k+m-1,k)B_{q}(k+m+1,k)




\displaystyle={}
\displaystyle q^{3k(k-1)+m}\left(q^{-m}\operatorname{ps}_{k+3}\left(s_{(m^{2})}\right)\right)\left(q^{-6m}\operatorname{ps}_{k+3}\left(s_{(m^{4})}\right)\right)\geq_{q}0,



which implies the q-log-concavity of \left(B_{q}(n,k)\right)_{n\geq k}.
∎






6.3 q-Analogue of the d-Hoggatt numbers




Define the q-analogue of the d-Hoggatt numbers





H^{(d)}_{q}(n,k)=q^{\frac{dk(k-1)}{2}}\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}\frac{\begin{bmatrix}n+j\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}}{\begin{bmatrix}k+j\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}}.



In particular, when d=1,2, and 3, H^{(d)}_{q}(n,k) specializes to the shifted q-binomial coefficients, the q-Narayana numbers, and the q-refined Baxter numbers, respectively.
By an argument analogous to the proof of Lemma 4.2, we can verify that





H^{(d)}_{q}(n,k)=\operatorname{ps}_{n}\left(s_{(d^{k})}\right).



The following results generalize Theorems 6.3 and 6.4.




Theorem 6.6.



Given an integer n, the polynomial sequence \left(H^{(d)}_{q}(n,k)\right)_{0\leq k\leq n} is q-log-concave for any d\geq 1.





Proof.



The proof is similar to that of Theorem 6.3, with a=d in (38).
∎





Theorem 6.7.



Given an integer k, the polynomial sequence \left(H^{(d)}_{q}(n,k)\right)_{n\geq k} is q-log-concave for any d\geq 1.





Proof.



Recall that H_{q}^{(d)}(k+m,k) equals





\operatorname{ps}_{k+m}\left(s_{(d^{k})}\right)=q^{\frac{dk(k-1)}{2}}\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}\frac{\begin{bmatrix}k+m+j\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}}{\begin{bmatrix}k+j\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}}=q^{\frac{dk(k-1)}{2}}\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}\frac{[k+m+j]_{q}![j]_{q}!}{[k+j]_{q}![m+j]_{q}!}.



By Lemma 4.1, the hook-content formula gives





\operatorname{ps}_{k+d}\left(s_{(m^{d})}\right)=q^{m\binom{d}{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{[k+d+j-i]_{q}}{[m+d-i-j+1]_{q}}.



Reversing the index i and rearranging the products, we obtain





\operatorname{ps}_{k+d}\left(s_{(m^{d})}\right)=q^{m\binom{d}{2}}\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}\frac{[k+m+j]_{q}![j]_{q}!}{[k+j]_{q}![m+j]_{q}!}.



Therefore,





H_{q}^{(d)}(k+m,k)=q^{\frac{dk(k-1)}{2}-m\binom{d}{2}}\operatorname{ps}_{k+d}\left(s_{(m^{d})}\right).



It is obvious that





H_{q}^{(d)}(k,k)^{2}=q^{dk(k-1)}\geq_{q}0.



For m\geq 1, we have






\displaystyle H_{q}^{(d)}(k+m,k)^{2}-H_{q}^{(d)}(k+m-1,k)H_{q}^{(d)}(k+m+1,k)




\displaystyle={}
\displaystyle q^{dk(k-1)-d(d-1)m}\operatorname{ps}_{k+d}\left(s_{(m^{d})}^{2}-s_{((m-1)^{d})}s_{((m+1)^{d})}\right).



Taking a=m,b=d in (38), H_{q}^{(d)}(k+m,k)^{2}-H_{q}^{(d)}(k+m-1,k)H_{q}^{(d)}(k+m+1,k) equals





q^{dk(k-1)-d(d-1)m}\operatorname{ps}_{k+d}\left(s_{(m^{d-1})}s_{(m^{d+1})}\right).






By the same argument as in the proof of Lemma 6.5, we can obtain





q^{-m\binom{d-1}{2}}\operatorname{ps}_{k+d}\left(s_{(m^{d-1})}\right)\geq_{q}0\quad\textrm{and}\quad q^{-m\binom{d+1}{2}}\operatorname{ps}_{k+d}\left(s_{(m^{d+1})}\right)\geq_{q}0.



Consequently, H_{q}^{(d)}(k+m,k)^{2}-H_{q}^{(d)}(k+m-1,k)H_{q}^{(d)}(k+m+1,k) equals





q^{dk(k-1)+m}\left(q^{-m\binom{d-1}{2}}\operatorname{ps}_{k+d}\left(s_{(m^{d-1})}\right)\right)\left(q^{-m\binom{d+1}{2}}\operatorname{ps}_{k+d}\left(s_{(m^{d+1})}\right)\right).



Then for d\geq 1, we have





H_{q}^{(d)}(k+m,k)^{2}-H_{q}^{(d)}(k+m-1,k)H_{q}^{(d)}(k+m+1,k)\geq_{q}0.



Thus, for any fixed k, the sequence
\left(H_{q}^{(d)}(n,k)\right)_{n\geq k}
is q-log-concave.
∎







Appendix A Computational details for the proof of (21)





(iii) 



Evaluation of A_{2}-D_{2}.
Recall that





A_{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\sum_{0\leq a\leq b\leq c\leq 2}\operatorname{ps}_{1}^{1}\left(s_{(3^{a},2^{b-a},1^{c-b-1})}\right)\operatorname{ps}_{n-1}^{1}\!\left(s_{(3^{k-c},2^{c-b},1^{b-a})}\,s_{(3^{r-k})}\right).



The term \operatorname{ps}_{1}^{1}\left(s_{(3^{a},2^{b-a},1^{c-b-1})}\right)=0 unless c-b-1\geq 0.
Hence, the double sum A_{2} can be decomposed into four sums A_{2}=A_{21}+A_{22}+A_{23}+A_{24},
where





\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}(a,b,c)&(3^{k-c},2^{c-b},1^{b-a})&A_{2,i}\\

Thus, tL-S_{(k,k)}S_{(k,k,2,2)}+S_{(k,k,1,1)}^{2} equals






\displaystyle\bigl(-h_{2}^{2}+e_{1}h_{3}+e_{2}^{2}\bigr)S_{(k,k)}^{2}+(-h_{2}+e_{1}^{2})S_{(k+1,k)}^{2}+\bigl(e_{1}h_{2}-h_{3}-2e_{1}e_{2}\bigr)S_{(k,k)}S_{(k+1,k)}





\displaystyle\quad+(h_{2}-e_{2}+2e_{2})S_{(k,k)}S_{(k+2,k)}+(e_{1}-2e_{1})S_{(k+1,k)}S_{(k+2,k)}+(S_{(k+1,k)}-e_{1}S_{(k,k)})S_{(k+3,k)}.



Using the identities h_{2}=e_{1}^{2}-e_{2}, e_{1}h_{2}-h_{3}=e_{1}e_{2}-e_{3}, and h_{2}^{2}-e_{1}h_{3}=e_{2}^{2}-e_{1}e_{3}, we have






\displaystyle tL-S_{(k,k)}S_{(k,k,2,2)}+S_{(k,k,1,1)}^{2}




\displaystyle={}
\displaystyle e_{1}e_{3}S_{(k,k)}^{2}+e_{2}S_{(k+1,k)}^{2}-(e_{1}e_{2}+e_{3})S_{(k,k)}S_{(k+1,k)}





\displaystyle\quad+e_{1}^{2}S_{(k,k)}S_{(k+2,k)}-e_{1}S_{(k+1,k)}S_{(k+2,k)}+\left(S_{(k+1,k)}-e_{1}S_{(k,k)}\right)\cdot S_{(k+3,k)}




\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\left(S_{(k+1,k)}-e_{1}S_{(k,k)}\right)\cdot\left(-e_{3}S_{(k,k)}+e_{2}S_{(k+1,k)}-e_{1}S_{(k+2,k)}\right)+\left(S_{(k+1,k)}-e_{1}S_{(k,k)}\right)\cdot S_{(k+3,k)}




\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\left(S_{(k+1,k)}-e_{1}S_{(k,k)}\right)\cdot\left(S_{(k+3,k)}-e_{3}S_{(k,k)}+e_{2}S_{(k+1,k)}-e_{1}S_{(k+2,k)}\right).



Hence, we obtain






\displaystyle tL-S_{(k,k)}S_{(k,k,2,2)}+S_{(k,k,1,1)}^{2}+\left(S_{(k+1,k)}-e_{1}S_{(k,k)}\right)\cdot S_{(k,k,1,1,1)}




\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\left(S_{(k+1,k)}-e_{1}S_{(k,k)}\right)\cdot\left(S_{(k+3,k)}-e_{3}S_{(k,k)}+e_{2}S_{(k+1,k)}-e_{1}S_{(k+2,k)}+S_{(k,k,1,1,1)}\right).



By (41), the right-hand side vanishes.
Since e_{1}S_{(k,k)}=S_{(k+1,k)}+S_{(k,k,1)},
we derive that





\displaystyle tL-S_{(k,k)}S_{(k,k,2,2)}+S_{(k,k,1,1)}^{2}-S_{(k,k,1)}S_{(k,k,1,1,1)}=0.



This completes the proof.
∎
