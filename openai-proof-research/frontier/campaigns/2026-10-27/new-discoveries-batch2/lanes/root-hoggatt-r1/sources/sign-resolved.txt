Abstract.
    

We study three closely related questions arising from the recent work of Andrews and El Bachraoui on the two-color partition series
    





S_{1}(q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}s_{1}(n)q^{n}=\sum_{a\geq 0}q^{a}(-q^{a+1};q)_{\infty}^{2}


    

and its odd companion, denoted by T_{o}(q). First, for the eta-normalized companion
    





C(q)=(q;q)_{\infty}T_{o}(q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}c(n)q^{n},


    

we prove a strong form of the Andrews–El Bachraoui sign conjecture that \limsup c(n)=+\infty and \liminf c(n)=-\infty. Second, we construct an involution using the Franklin-type involution of Chen and Liu to combinatorially explain Andrews–El Bachraoui congruence for s_{1}(n) modulo 4. Third, we introduce a two-variable refinement of S_{1}(q) which records the difference of the lengths of the two colored components. Using Ramanujan’s partial theta identity and heat-type equations, we obtain a closed formula for all of its even moments, and we show that the eta-normalized moments satisfy termwise congruences modulo p^{2k} for every prime p\equiv 5\pmod{6}.
  




Key words and phrases: two-color partitions; partial theta functions; sign-reversing involutions; moments; heat equations; congruences; quadratic forms



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification05A17, 11P81, 11P83, 11F27




1. Introduction




Throughout the paper, we assume |q|<1 and use the standard notation





(a;q)_{n}:=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1-aq^{j}),\qquad(a;q)_{\infty}:=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}(1-aq^{j}),



for the q-Pochhammer symbol, together with the shorthand (a,b;q)_{\infty}:=(a;q)_{\infty}(b;q)_{\infty}. We refer to Andrews’ text [2] and to Gasper–Rahman [9] for background on q-series notation and transformations. Partial theta functions and especially identities of Ramanujan–Andrews type, have long been connected with signed partition identities, false theta phenomena, and Franklin-type cancellations; see, for instance, Alladi, Warnaar, and Andrews–Berndt [1, 4, 13].




Recently, Andrews and El Bachraoui [3] introduced the two-color partition series




(1.1)

S_{1}(q):=\sum_{n\geq 0}s_{1}(n)q^{n}=\sum_{a\geq 0}q^{a}(-q^{a+1};q)_{\infty}^{2}.



Combinatorially, s_{1}(n) counts two-color partitions of n into distinct parts in which the smallest part occurs in one prescribed color, while every larger part may occur in either color or in both colors [3]. Andrews and El Bachraoui proved the following complete modulo 4 description.




Theorem 1 (Andrews–El Bachraoui).



For every n\geq 0,





s_{1}(n)\equiv\begin{cases}(-1)^{r}\pmod{4},&\text{if }n=r(r+1)/2\text{ for some }r\geq 0,\\
0\pmod{4},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}



In particular, s_{1}(n) is odd if and only if n is a triangular number.





They also introduced the odd companion




(1.2)

T_{o}(q):=\sum_{n\geq 0}t_{o}(n)q^{n}=\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{q^{2m}(-q;q)_{2m}}{(q;q^{2})_{m+1}},



and the eta-normalized companion




(1.3)

C(q):=\sum_{n\geq 0}c(n)q^{n}=(q;q)_{\infty}T_{o}(q).



One of the striking features of these series is that S_{1}(q), T_{o}(q), and C(q) are tied together by partial-theta decompositions and theta-product normalizations. In particular, Andrews and El Bachraoui proved the exact relation




(1.4)

S_{1}(q)=\Theta(q)+4qT_{o}(q),\qquad\Theta(q):=\sum_{r\geq 0}(-1)^{r}q^{r(r+1)/2}.



Thus, away from triangular indices, the congruence of s_{1}(n) modulo 8 is exactly the parity question for T_{o}(q). Andrews and El Bachraoui also proved the following sign relations for the coefficients of C(q) [3].




Theorem 2 (Andrews–El Bachraoui).



For all n\geq 0,





c(25n+8)=c(25n+13)=c(25n+18)=c(25n+23)=0,



and





c(25n+28)=-c(n).



In particular, c(n) changes sign infinitely often and assumes both positive and negative values infinitely often.





At the end of their paper, Andrews and El Bachraoui proposed the following conjecture [3].




Conjecture 1 (Andrews–El Bachraoui).



We have





\limsup_{n\to\infty}c(n)=+\infty,\qquad\liminf_{n\to\infty}c(n)=-\infty.







Our first main result is the following stronger statement.




Theorem 3.



For every integer k\geq 0, there are infinitely many integers n such that c(n)=2^{k}, and infinitely many integers n such that c(n)=-2^{k}. In particular,





\limsup_{n\to\infty}c(n)=+\infty,\qquad\liminf_{n\to\infty}c(n)=-\infty.







The second theme of the paper concerns combinatorial proof of Theorem 1, thereby answering Problem 2 in [3].




Theorem 4.



Theorem 1 admits a signed involutive proof.





Remark 1.



We note here that Andrews and El Bachraoui also conjectured a congruence for s_{1}(n) modulo 8 [3, Conjecture 2]. This has now been proved by Liu–Xia [12] and, independently, by Banerjee–Bringmann [6].





The third theme of the paper is a two-variable refinement of S_{1}(q). Define




(1.5)

\mathcal{V}(z;q):=\sum_{a\geq 0}q^{a}(-zq^{a+1};q)_{\infty}(-z^{-1}q^{a+1};q)_{\infty}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}N(m,n)z^{m}q^{n}.



Combinatorially, N(m,n) counts triples (a;\lambda,\mu), where a\geq 0 and the partitions \lambda and \mu have distinct parts larger than a, such that





a+|\lambda|+|\mu|=n,\qquad\ell(\lambda)-\ell(\mu)=m.



Here \ell(\cdot) denotes the number of parts. Clearly \mathcal{V}(1;q)=S_{1}(q), so that s_{1}(n)=\sum_{m}N(m,n), and \ell(\lambda)-\ell(\mu) is a rank-type statistic on the objects counted by s_{1}(n). Interchanging \lambda and \mu gives N(m,n)=N(-m,n). Hence all odd moments vanish. For k\geq 0, put




(1.6)

M_{2k}(n):=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}m^{2k}N(m,n),\qquad\mathcal{M}_{2k}(q):=\sum_{n\geq 0}M_{2k}(n)q^{n},



in analogy with the rank moments of Atkin and Garvan [5]. In Section 4 we show that Ramanujan’s partial theta identity splits \mathcal{V}(z;q) into a theta product times a partial theta function, minus a second partial theta function, and that each piece satisfies a heat-type equation. This yields a closed formula for every \mathcal{M}_{2k}(q) (Theorem 6). For instance, with \delta:=q\,\frac{d}{dq}, q=e^{2\pi i\tau}, and E_{2}(\tau)=1-24\sum_{n\geq 1}\sigma_{1}(n)q^{n},





\mathcal{M}_{2}=6\delta S_{1}+\mathcal{E}S_{1}-2\delta\Theta+(\mathcal{E}-1)\Theta,\qquad\mathcal{E}=\frac{5E_{2}(\tau)-8E_{2}(2\tau)+9}{12}.



Our final main result shows that, after multiplication by (q;q)_{\infty}, the moments are governed by the binary quadratic form x^{2}+3y^{2}, which is the same form that controls c(n) in Section 2. Define integers d_{2k}(n) by




(1.7)

(q;q)_{\infty}\mathcal{M}_{2k}(q)=:\sum_{n\geq 0}d_{2k}(n)q^{n},



and let v_{p} denote the p-adic valuation.




Theorem 5.



Let k\geq 1, and let p\equiv 5\pmod{6} be prime. Then





d_{2k}(n)\begin{cases}=0&\text{if }v_{p}(6n+1)\text{ is odd},\\
\equiv 0\pmod{p^{2ka}}&\text{if }v_{p}(6n+1)=2a.\end{cases}



In particular,




(1.8)

d_{2k}\!\left(pn+\frac{5p-1}{6}\right)\equiv 0\pmod{p^{2k}}.







For example, d_{2}(5n+4)\equiv 0\pmod{25}, d_{4}(5n+4)\equiv 0\pmod{625}, and d_{2}(11n+9)\equiv 0\pmod{121}. The vanishing part of the argument also applies when k=0, and in that case it recovers, and extends to every prime p\equiv 5\pmod{6}, the vanishing statements of Theorem 2; see Corollary 1.




The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we prove Theorem 3. In Section 3, we give the combinatorial interpretations of the modulo 4 congruence for s_{1}(n), thereby proving Theorem 4. In Section 4, we study the refinement \mathcal{V}(z;q): we establish its partial theta decomposition, prove the moment formula (Theorem 6) and Theorem 5, and record two special values. We conclude with some open questions in Section 5.





2. Proof of Theorem 3




We use the signed representation formula for c(n). Put D=6n+7.
Then c(n) is a signed count of positive solutions of





x^{2}+3y^{2}=4D.



More precisely,




(2.1)

c(n)=R_{1,3}(D)-R_{5,3}(D)+R_{7,1}(D)-R_{11,1}(D),



where R_{a,b}(D) counts positive integer solutions of x^{2}+3y^{2}=4D
with x\equiv a\pmod{12},\,y\equiv b\pmod{4}. Thus the residue classes (1,3),\,(7,1) contribute with plus sign, while (5,3),\,(11,1) contribute with minus sign. We begin with the elementary representation





28=1^{2}+3\cdot 3^{2}.



This corresponds to D=7, and the pair (x,y)=(1,3) lies in the plus class





(x,y)\equiv(1,3)\pmod{(12,4)}.



Now choose primes p_{j}=u_{j}^{2}+3v_{j}^{2} with u_{j}\equiv 1\pmod{12},\,v_{j}\equiv 0\pmod{4}, and with v_{j}/u_{j} very small. More precisely, choose positive
numbers \varepsilon_{j} such that





\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{j}<\eta,



where \eta>0 is chosen small enough so that rotating either of the
two first-quadrant representations of 28=x^{2}+3y^{2} by any angle of size less than \eta keeps it in the first quadrant, and rotating any of the other sign choices by an angle of size less than
\eta keeps it outside the first quadrant. By Hecke’s prime theorem for Grössencharacters, equivalently the prime-ideal theorem in sectors with ray-class conditions
[11, Ch. XV, Thm. 6 and Example 2]
(see also Hecke’s original formula [10, p. 38, Eq. (52)]), applied in \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-3}], every fixed sector
0<\arg(u+\sqrt{-3}v)<\varepsilon
contains infinitely many prime elements, we may
choose infinitely many distinct primes p_{j} satisfying the congruences
above and
0<\arctan\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}\,v_{j}}{u_{j}}\right)<\varepsilon_{j}.
For a fixed k, define





D_{k}=7p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k},\qquad n_{k}=\frac{D_{k}-7}{6}.



Since each p_{j}\equiv 1\pmod{6}, we have D_{k}\equiv 7\pmod{6}, so n_{k} is an integer. The reason these primes are useful is the multiplication identity





(x^{2}+3y^{2})(u^{2}+3v^{2})=(xu-3yv)^{2}+3(xv+yu)^{2}=(xu+3yv)^{2}+3(yu-xv)^{2},



Thus, each time we multiply by one prime p_{j}=u_{j}^{2}+3v_{j}^{2}, we get
two possible new representations. Starting from 28=1^{2}+3\cdot 3^{2}
and multiplying successively by p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}, we obtain 2^{k} representations of





x^{2}+3y^{2}=4D_{k}.



We now check that all these 2^{k} representations are counted with
plus sign. Suppose a representation lies in the class





(x,y)\equiv(1,3)\pmod{(12,4)}.



Using





u_{j}\equiv 1\pmod{12},\qquad v_{j}\equiv 0\pmod{4},



we see from the two multiplication formulas that the new x-coordinate
is still congruent to 1\pmod{12}, and the new y-coordinate is
still congruent to 3\pmod{4}. Hence both choices preserve the class (1,3). Therefore all 2^{k} representations coming from the starting pair (1,3) remain in the plus class (1,3). The small-angle condition ensures that these representations remain
positive. Indeed, the factor u_{j}+\sqrt{-3}\,v_{j}
has angle





\arctan\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}\,v_{j}}{u_{j}}\right),



which is smaller than \varepsilon_{j}. After k steps, the total
change in direction is at most





\varepsilon_{1}+\cdots+\varepsilon_{k}<\eta.



By the choice of \eta, the resulting points still lie in the first
quadrant. Thus the corresponding x and y remain positive. It remains to explain why the negative classes do not contribute. The
other first-quadrant representation of
28=x^{2}+3y^{2} is 28=5^{2}+3\cdot 1^{2}. This pair lies in the class





(x,y)\equiv(5,1)\pmod{(12,4)},



which is not one of the four signed classes appearing in the formula (2.1) for
c(n). The same congruence calculation shows that multiplying by any
of the primes p_{j} preserves this class. Hence all representations
generated from (5,1) remain uncounted. Finally, the elementary uniqueness theory for representations by x^{2}+3y^{2} [8] says that, since D_{k}=7p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k} is squarefree and each p_{j} is represented by u_{j}^{2}+3v_{j}^{2}, every
representation of x^{2}+3y^{2}=4D_{k} is obtained from one of the representations of 28=x^{2}+3y^{2} by making the two choices above at each prime factor, together with
possible sign changes. By our choice of \eta, the sign-changed
starting points remain outside the first quadrant after the small
rotations, so they do not contribute to the positive-solution count. Consequently, among the four signed classes in the formula for c(n),
only the plus class (1,3) contributes. It contributes exactly 2^{k} times. Thus





R_{1,3}(D_{k})=2^{k},



while





R_{5,3}(D_{k})=R_{7,1}(D_{k})=R_{11,1}(D_{k})=0.



Because the primes p_{j} can be chosen in infinitely many ways, this
gives infinitely many n_{k} with c(n_{k})=2^{k}. Hence





\limsup_{n\to\infty}c(n)=+\infty.



The negative direction follows from the known
relation c(25n+28)=-c(n). Applying this to the sequence n_{k}, we get





c(25n_{k}+28)=-c(n_{k})=-2^{k}.



Since k is arbitrary, this gives





\liminf_{n\to\infty}c(n)=-\infty.



The result follows.∎





3. Proof of Theorem 4




In this section we give a signed interpretation of the modulo 4 congruence for s_{1}(n). The key point is the following simple identity:




(3.1)

(1+q^{r})^{2}=(1-q^{r})^{2}+4q^{r}.



Modulo 4, S_{1}(q) and its signed generating function F(q) (defined below) are congruent. By constructing an involution on the signed object using a Franklin-type involution of Chen and Liu [7], we show that the fixed points of our involution are triangular-number staircases, proving Theorem 1. Using (1.1) and (3.1), we get




(3.2)

S_{1}(q)\equiv F(q):=\sum_{a\geq 0}q^{a}(q^{a+1};q)_{\infty}^{2}\pmod{4}.



The right-hand side is the signed generating function for triples X=(a;\lambda,\mu), where a\geq 0 and \lambda,\mu are distinct-part partitions with all parts strictly larger than a. We assign





w(X)=a+|\lambda|+|\mu|,\qquad\operatorname{sgn}(X)=(-1)^{\ell(\lambda)+\ell(\mu)}.



Thus




(3.3)

F(q)=\sum_{X}\operatorname{sgn}(X)q^{w(X)}.



These are exactly the triples counted by N(m,n) in (1.5), and since \operatorname{sgn}(X)=(-1)^{\ell(\lambda)-\ell(\mu)}, we have F(q)=\mathcal{V}(-1;q).




We now describe a complete sign-reversing involution on these triples. Rather than forcing a long case-by-case map directly on (a;\lambda,\mu), we transfer the problem to the distinct-part model used by Chen and Liu in their Franklin-type involution for squares [7]. Define




(3.4)

\Phi(a;\lambda,\mu)=\rho:=\{2a\}\cup\{2r:r\in\lambda\}\cup\{2s-1:s\in\mu\}.



Then \rho is a partition into distinct nonnegative parts whose smallest part is even. If o(\rho) denotes the number of odd parts of \rho, then




(3.5)

|\rho|+o(\rho)=2w(a;\lambda,\mu),



and




(3.6)

(-1)^{\ell(\rho)-1}=(-1)^{\ell(\lambda)+\ell(\mu)}.



Therefore F(q) is the weighted signed generating function of distinct nonnegative partitions with smallest part even.




Chen and Liu [7, Theorem 5.1] constructed an explicit Franklin-type involution on precisely such distinct-part partitions \rho. Denote their involution by \psi. It preserves the statistics |\rho|, and their proof confirms that the involution also preserves o(\rho), and hence, w(a;\lambda,\mu)=(|\rho|+o(\rho))/2; reverses the parity of \ell(\rho)-1, and has fixed points




(3.7)

\rho_{m}=(0,1,3,5,\ldots,2m-1),\qquad m\geq 0.



For example, the elementary part of \psi is as follows: if the smallest part of \rho is positive, one inserts a zero part; if the smallest part is 0 and the next smallest part is even, one deletes the zero part. The remaining case, in which the smallest part is 0 and the next smallest part is odd, is handled by the modular hook map of Chen and Liu. We define the composition map




(3.8)

\iota=\Phi^{-1}\circ\psi\circ\Phi.



Noting that \Phi is a bijection, we immediately see that \iota is a complete weight-preserving, sign-reversing involution on all triples except the preimages of (3.7). These preimages are exactly




(3.9)

X_{m}=(0;\varnothing,(1,2,\ldots,m)).



They have





w(X_{m})=1+2+\cdots+m=\frac{m(m+1)}{2},\qquad\operatorname{sgn}(X_{m})=(-1)^{m}.



Consequently




(3.10)

F(q)=\sum_{m\geq 0}(-1)^{m}q^{m(m+1)/2}.



Combining (3.2) and (3.10) proves the result. An independent analytic derivation of (3.10), obtained by specializing the refinement \mathcal{V}(z;q) at z=-1, is given in Remark 2 below.





4. Moments of the length-difference statistic




In this section we study the refinement \mathcal{V}(z;q) defined in (1.5) and its moments (1.6).




4.1. Notation




We write





D_{z}:=z\frac{\partial}{\partial z},\qquad\delta:=q\frac{\partial}{\partial q},



and recall that \Theta(q)=\sum_{r\geq 0}(-1)^{r}q^{r(r+1)/2} from (1.4). Let




(4.1)

\displaystyle B(z;q)
\displaystyle:=(1+z)(-zq,-z^{-1}q;q)_{\infty},




\displaystyle P(z;q)
\displaystyle:=\sum_{r\geq 0}z^{3r}q^{r(3r+1)/2}\bigl(1-z^{2}q^{2r+1}\bigr),




\displaystyle\Psi(z;q)
\displaystyle:=\sum_{r\geq 0}(-1)^{r}z^{2r+1}q^{r(r+1)/2},



so that \Psi(1;q)=\Theta(q). Set




(4.2)

Q(q):=(q;q)_{\infty},\qquad\epsilon(q):=\frac{\delta Q(q)}{Q(q)},







B_{0}(q):=B(1;q),\qquad P_{0}(q):=P(1;q),



and define the q-differential operators




(4.3)

\mathscr{L}_{B}:=2\delta+2\epsilon+\frac{1}{4},\qquad\mathscr{L}_{P}:=6\delta+\frac{1}{4},\qquad\mathscr{L}_{\Psi}:=8\delta+1.







4.2. A partial theta decomposition




The starting point is Ramanujan’s partial theta identity [4] (see also [1, 13])





\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{q^{n}}{(xq;q)_{n}(q/x;q)_{n}}
\displaystyle=(1-x)\sum_{r\geq 0}(-1)^{r}x^{3r}q^{r(3r+1)/2}\bigl(1-x^{2}q^{2r+1}\bigr)



(4.4)


\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{(xq,q/x;q)_{\infty}}\sum_{r\geq 0}(-1)^{r}x^{2r+1}q^{r(r+1)/2}.






Lemma 1.



We have




(4.5)

\mathcal{V}(z;q)=B(z;q)P(z;q)-\Psi(z;q).







Proof.



From (1.5),





\mathcal{V}(z;q)=(-zq,-z^{-1}q;q)_{\infty}\sum_{a\geq 0}\frac{q^{a}}{(-zq;q)_{a}(-z^{-1}q;q)_{a}}.



Set x=-z in (4.4) and multiply by (-zq,-z^{-1}q;q)_{\infty}. Since





(-1)^{r}(-z)^{3r}=z^{3r},\quad(-1)^{r}(-z)^{2r+1}=-(-1)^{r}z^{2r+1},



the first term on the right of (4.4) becomes B(z;q)P(z;q) and the second becomes -\Psi(z;q).
∎





Remark 2.



The specializations z=\pm 1 of (4.5) connect with Sections 1 and 3.


(1) 



At z=1 we get S_{1}=B_{0}P_{0}-\Theta, that is,




(4.6)

P_{0}(q)=\frac{S_{1}(q)+\Theta(q)}{B_{0}(q)}.



Since B_{0}=2(-q;q)_{\infty}^{2}=2(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}^{2}/(q;q)_{\infty}^{2}, combining this with (1.4) gives





2q(q;q)_{\infty}T_{o}(q)=\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}^{2}}{(q;q)_{\infty}}P_{0}(q)-(q;q)_{\infty}\Theta(q),



which is the partial theta identity for T_{o}(q) obtained by Andrews and El Bachraoui [3, (4.8)].


(2) 



At z=-1, the factor 1+z gives B(-1;q)=0, while \Psi(-1;q)=-\Theta(q). Hence





F(q)=\mathcal{V}(-1;q)=\Theta(q),



which is (3.10). Together with (3.2), this gives an analytic counterpart of the involutive proof of Theorem 1.








4.3. Heat equations




Each of the three pieces in (4.5) is annihilated by a heat-type operator.




Lemma 2.



We have




(4.7)

\displaystyle(D_{z}^{2}+D_{z}-6\delta)P
\displaystyle=0,



(4.8)

\displaystyle(D_{z}^{2}-8\delta-1)\Psi
\displaystyle=0,



(4.9)

\displaystyle(D_{z}^{2}-D_{z}-2\delta-2\epsilon)B
\displaystyle=0.







Proof.



In the first part of P, the z- and q-exponents are, respectively,





m=3r,\qquad n=\frac{r(3r+1)}{2},



and they satisfy m^{2}+m=6n. In the second part they are





m=3r+2,\qquad n=\frac{r(3r+1)}{2}+2r+1,



and again m^{2}+m=6n. Since D_{z} and \delta act on z^{m}q^{n} by multiplication by m and n, this proves (4.7). For \Psi, the exponents are m=2r+1 and n=r(r+1)/2, so m^{2}=8n+1, which gives (4.8). Finally, the Jacobi triple product [2] gives




(4.10)

Q(q)B(z;q)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}z^{m}q^{m(m-1)/2}.



Each monomial on the right satisfies m^{2}-m=2\cdot m(m-1)/2. Thus





(D_{z}^{2}-D_{z}-2\delta)(QB)=0.



Since \delta Q=\epsilon Q and Q does not depend on z, division by Q gives (4.9).
∎






4.4. The moment formula




Theorem 6.



For every k\geq 0,




(4.11)

\mathcal{M}_{2k}(q)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{2k}{2j}\bigl(\mathscr{L}_{B}^{\,j}B_{0}\bigr)\bigl(\mathscr{L}_{P}^{\,k-j}P_{0}\bigr)-\mathscr{L}_{\Psi}^{\,k}\Theta.



In particular,




(4.12)

\mathcal{M}_{2}=6\delta S_{1}+\mathcal{E}S_{1}-2\delta\Theta+(\mathcal{E}-1)\Theta,\qquad\mathcal{E}=\frac{5E_{2}(\tau)-8E_{2}(2\tau)+9}{12},



where q=e^{2\pi i\tau} and





E_{2}(\tau)=1-24\sum_{n\geq 1}\sigma_{1}(n)q^{n}.







Proof.



Put z=e^{t} and define




(4.13)

\widetilde{B}(t;q):=e^{-t/2}B(e^{t};q),\quad\widetilde{P}(t;q):=e^{t/2}P(e^{t};q).



The exponential factors cancel, so





B(e^{t};q)P(e^{t};q)=\widetilde{B}(t;q)\widetilde{P}(t;q).



Also, B(z^{-1};q)=z^{-1}B(z;q), and therefore \widetilde{B}(-t;q)=\widetilde{B}(t;q). In particular, every odd derivative of \widetilde{B} at t=0 is zero. Because D_{z} becomes \partial_{t}, Lemma 2 becomes




(4.14)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\widetilde{B}
\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}_{B}\widetilde{B},



(4.15)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\widetilde{P}
\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}_{P}\widetilde{P},



(4.16)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\Psi(e^{t};q)
\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}_{\Psi}\Psi(e^{t};q).



For instance, writing B(e^{t};q)=e^{t/2}\widetilde{B}, we have (\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{t})B(e^{t};q)=e^{t/2}\bigl(\partial_{t}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}\bigr)\widetilde{B}, so (4.9) is equivalent to (4.14); similarly (\partial_{t}^{2}+\partial_{t})P(e^{t};q)=e^{-t/2}\bigl(\partial_{t}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}\bigr)\widetilde{P} shows that (4.7) is equivalent to (4.15). The operators on the right of (4.14)–(4.16) involve only q, so they commute with \partial_{t}. By iteration, and since \widetilde{B}(0;q)=B_{0}, \widetilde{P}(0;q)=P_{0}, and \Psi(1;q)=\Theta,




(4.17)

\displaystyle\left.\partial_{t}^{2j}\widetilde{B}(t;q)\right|_{t=0}
\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}_{B}^{\,j}B_{0},



(4.18)

\displaystyle\left.\partial_{t}^{2j}\widetilde{P}(t;q)\right|_{t=0}
\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}_{P}^{\,j}P_{0},



(4.19)

\displaystyle\left.\partial_{t}^{2j}\Psi(e^{t};q)\right|_{t=0}
\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}_{\Psi}^{\,j}\Theta.



On the other hand, by (1.5) and (1.6),





\mathcal{M}_{2k}(q)=\left.\partial_{t}^{2k}\mathcal{V}(e^{t};q)\right|_{t=0}.



Using (4.5), applying Leibniz’s rule to
\widetilde{B}\widetilde{P}, and using the vanishing of the odd derivatives of \widetilde{B}, we get





\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2k}(q)
\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{2k}{2j}\left.\partial_{t}^{2j}\widetilde{B}\right|_{t=0}\left.\partial_{t}^{2k-2j}\widetilde{P}\right|_{t=0}-\left.\partial_{t}^{2k}\Psi(e^{t};q)\right|_{t=0}.



Substitution of (4.17)–(4.19) proves (4.11).




It remains to simplify the case k=1. Let





b(q):=\frac{\delta B_{0}}{B_{0}}.



Since B_{0}=2(-q;q)_{\infty}^{2}=2(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}^{2}/(q;q)_{\infty}^{2}, standard logarithmic differentiation gives




(4.20)

b=\frac{2E_{2}(2\tau)-E_{2}(\tau)-1}{12},\qquad\epsilon=\frac{E_{2}(\tau)-1}{24}.



Formula (4.11) with k=1 is





\mathcal{M}_{2}=B_{0}\mathscr{L}_{P}P_{0}+(\mathscr{L}_{B}B_{0})P_{0}-\mathscr{L}_{\Psi}\Theta.



Using (4.6), we find





\displaystyle B_{0}\mathscr{L}_{P}P_{0}
\displaystyle=6\delta(S_{1}+\Theta)+\left(\frac{1}{4}-6b\right)(S_{1}+\Theta),




\displaystyle(\mathscr{L}_{B}B_{0})P_{0}
\displaystyle=\left(2b+2\epsilon+\frac{1}{4}\right)(S_{1}+\Theta),




\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{\Psi}\Theta
\displaystyle=8\delta\Theta+\Theta.



Therefore





\mathcal{M}_{2}=6\delta S_{1}-2\delta\Theta+\left(-4b+2\epsilon+\frac{1}{2}\right)(S_{1}+\Theta)-\Theta.



By (4.20),





-4b+2\epsilon+\frac{1}{2}=\frac{5E_{2}(\tau)-8E_{2}(2\tau)+9}{12}=\mathcal{E}.



This is exactly (4.12).
∎





Example 1.



The first few values of M_{2}(n) are





\mathcal{M}_{2}(q)=2q+2q^{2}+12q^{3}+14q^{4}+26q^{5}+56q^{6}+78q^{7}+120q^{8}+182q^{9}+\cdots,



in agreement with (4.12).






4.5. Proof of Theorem 5




Multiply (4.5) by Q(q)=(q;q)_{\infty}. From (4.10) and Euler’s pentagonal number theorem





Q(q)=\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{s}q^{s(3s-1)/2},



we obtain, after applying D_{z}^{2k} and setting z=1,





\displaystyle d_{2k}(n)
\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\geq 0,\ m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
\frac{m(m-1)}{2}+\frac{r(3r+1)}{2}=n\end{subarray}}(m+3r)^{2k}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\geq 0,\ m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
\frac{m(m-1)}{2}+\frac{r(3r+1)}{2}+2r+1=n\end{subarray}}(m+3r+2)^{2k}



(4.21)


\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\geq 0,\ s\in\mathbb{Z}\\
\frac{r(r+1)}{2}+\frac{s(3s-1)}{2}=n\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r+s}(2r+1)^{2k}.



This holds for every k\geq 0 (with 0^{0}=1). Every term in these three sums comes from a representation of the same integer by x^{2}+3y^{2}. In the first sum put





x=6r+1,\qquad y=2m-1.



Then





x^{2}+3y^{2}=4(6n+1),\qquad m+3r=\frac{x+y}{2}.



In the second sum put





x=6r+5,\qquad y=2m-1.



Again,





x^{2}+3y^{2}=4(6n+1),\qquad m+3r+2=\frac{x+y}{2}.



In the third sum put





x=6s-1,\qquad y=2r+1.



Then





x^{2}+3y^{2}=4(6n+1),\qquad 2r+1=y.



Thus every summand in (4.21) is a signed 2k-th power of either (x+y)/2 or y, where




(4.22)

x^{2}+3y^{2}=4(6n+1).



Now let p\equiv 5\pmod{6}. Then -3 is a quadratic nonresidue modulo p. Hence




(4.23)

x^{2}+3y^{2}\equiv 0\pmod{p}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x\equiv y\equiv 0\pmod{p}.



Indeed, if y\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}, then (xy^{-1})^{2}\equiv-3\pmod{p}, a contradiction; if y\equiv 0, then also x\equiv 0.




Suppose first that v_{p}(6n+1) is odd. If (4.22) had a solution, then (4.23) would imply p\mid x,y. Dividing by p^{2} and repeating shows that the p-adic valuation of the represented norm must be even. This is impossible. Therefore (4.22) has no integral solutions, all three sums in (4.21) are empty, and d_{2k}(n)=0.




Suppose next that v_{p}(6n+1)=2a. Repeated use of (4.23) gives





p^{a}\mid x,\qquad p^{a}\mid y



for every solution of (4.22). Since p is odd, both (x+y)/2 and y are divisible by p^{a}. Every individual weight in (4.21) is therefore divisible by p^{2ka}, and the same is true of their signed sum. Hence





p^{2ka}\mid d_{2k}(n).



Finally, put





N=pn+\frac{5p-1}{6},



which is an integer because 5p\equiv 1\pmod{6}. Then





6N+1=p(6n+5).



If its p-adic valuation is odd, then d_{2k}(N)=0. If it is even, it is at least 2, so the preceding divisibility gives p^{2k}\mid d_{2k}(N). This proves (1.8).∎




Example 2.



The first instances of (1.8) are





d_{2}(5n+4)\equiv 0\pmod{25},\qquad d_{4}(5n+4)\equiv 0\pmod{625},



and





d_{2}(11n+9)\equiv 0\pmod{121}.







Remark 3.



The important point is that these congruences are termwise. The prime p\equiv 5\pmod{6} divides both coordinates of every contributing representation, and the 2k-th moment turns this into divisibility by p^{2k}.





The case k=0 of the vanishing argument gives information about the companion C(q) itself.




Corollary 1.



If p\equiv 5\pmod{6} is prime and v_{p}(6n+7) is odd, then c(n)=0.





Proof.



Since \mathcal{M}_{0}=S_{1}, multiplying (1.4) by (q;q)_{\infty} gives





\sum_{n\geq 0}d_{0}(n)q^{n}=(q;q)_{\infty}\Theta(q)+4qC(q).



The coefficient of q^{n+1} in (q;q)_{\infty}\Theta(q) is exactly the third sum in (4.21) with k=0 and n replaced by n+1. If v_{p}(6(n+1)+1)=v_{p}(6n+7) is odd, then (4.22) has no solutions, so d_{0}(n+1) and this coefficient both vanish. Hence 4c(n)=0.
∎





For p=5 and n=25m+j with j\in\{8,13,18,23\}, one has 6n+7=5(30m+11),\,5(30m+17),\,5(30m+23),\,5(30m+29), respectively, so v_{5}(6n+7)=1. Thus Corollary 1 contains the vanishing statements of Theorem 2. This is consistent with (2.1): the integer D=6n+7 there is precisely 6(n+1)+1.





4.6. Special values




Besides z=\pm 1, the refinement \mathcal{V}(z;q) has simple product-sum forms at roots of unity of small order. For z=i, the factorization (1+ix)(1-ix)=1+x^{2} gives





\mathcal{V}(i;q)=\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}q^{a}(-q^{2a+2};q^{2})_{\infty}.



For z=\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}, the factorization (1+\omega x)(1+\omega^{-1}x)=(1+x^{3})/(1+x) gives





\mathcal{V}(\omega;q)=\frac{(-q^{3};q^{3})_{\infty}}{(-q;q)_{\infty}}\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}q^{a}\frac{(-q;q)_{a}}{(-q^{3};q^{3})_{a}}.



Since N(m,n)=N(-m,n), both values are real, and the coefficients of q^{n} in \mathcal{V}(i;q) and \mathcal{V}(\omega;q) are, respectively,





\sum_{m\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)}N(m,n)-\sum_{m\equiv 2\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)}N(m,n)



and





\sum_{m\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)}N(m,n)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m\not\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)}N(m,n).








5. Conclusion




We conclude with a few open questions below.





(1) 



Is there a rank or crank statistic on the signed objects
associated with S_{1}(q) that explains the modulo 4 congruence directly? The length difference \ell(\lambda)-\ell(\mu) underlying \mathcal{V}(z;q) is a natural candidate to start from.


(2) 



Prove the Andrews–El Bachraoui modulo 8 congruence [3, Conjecture 2] for s_{1}(n) combinatorially.


(3) 



Do the special values \mathcal{V}(i;q) and \mathcal{V}(\omega;q) admit partial theta decompositions analogous to (4.5), and do they lead to congruences or sign patterns for the corresponding residue-class counts of N(m,n)?
