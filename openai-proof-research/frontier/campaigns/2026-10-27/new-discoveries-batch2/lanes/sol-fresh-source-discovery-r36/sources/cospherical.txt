For integers 1 < k < d − 1 1<k<d-1 and r ⩾ k + 2 r\geqslant k+2 , we establish new lower bounds on the maximum number of points in [ n ] d [n]^{d} such that no r r lie in a k k -dimensional affine (or linear) subspace. These bounds improve on earlier results of Sudakov-Tomon and Lefmann. Further, we provide a randomised construction for the no-four-on-a-circle problem posed by Erdős and Purdy, improving Thiele’s bound. We also consider the random construction in higher dimensions, and improve the bound of Suk and White for d ⩾ 4 d\geqslant 4 . In each case, we apply the deletion method, using results from number theory and incidence geometry to solve the associated counting problems. Key words and phrases: no-four-on-a-circle, no-three-in-line, evasive sets. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification 05D40, 52C10, 52C35 1. Introduction At the beginning of the 20 20 th century, Dudeney [ 15 ] posed the following problem: How many points can be placed in an n × n n\times n integer grid so that no three points lie in a straight line? This is now well known as the no-three-in-line problem and has been extensively studied (see [ 9 , Section 10.1] and [ 23 , F4] ). Several similar problems have been studied since then, namely, problems that seek to maximize the size of subsets of lattice cubes while avoiding specific configurations. Such problems are fundamental not only in discrete geometry but also relevant across other fields. For instance, higher dimensional finite field analogues of no-three-in-line sets provide examples of evasive sets [ 35 ] (see also [ 38 ] ), which provide explicit constructions of list-decodable error correcting codes [ 22 ] and bipartite Ramsey graphs [ 35 ] , and are also related to the cap set problem [ 13 , 17 ] from extremal combinatorics. The no-three-in-line problem can also be viewed as a discrete analogue of Heilbronn’s triangle problem [ 23 , F4] (see [ 11 ] for recent progress on the latter) from discrepancy theory, which asks for a placement of points in the unit square that maximizes the area of the smallest triangle. Brass and Knauer [ 8 ] asked the following generalized problem: given n , d , k , r ∈ ℕ n,d,k,r\in\mathbb{N} with k < d k<d and r ⩾ k + 2 r\geqslant k+2 , what is the maximum number f aff ​ ( n , d , k , r ) f_{\text{aff}}(n,d,k,r) of points in [ n ] d [n]^{d} such that no r r of these points lie on a k k -dimensional affine subspace? The case r = k + 2 r=k+2 [ 9 , Section 10.1, Problem 5] is of special interest since it corresponds to sets that avoid up to k k -dimensional affine degeneracies. Further specializing to k = d − 1 k=d-1 [ 9 , Section 10.1, Problem 3] is even more interesting since it corresponds to sets in general linear position. Random constructions for extremal lattice problems Guy and Kelly [ 24 ] showed that the number of collinear triples of points in [ n ] 2 [n]^{2} is equal to Θ ⁡ ( n 4 ​ log ⁡ n ) \Theta(n^{4}\log n) , which, by the deletion method, yields a set of size Ω ⁡ ( n / log ⁡ n ) \Omega(n/\sqrt{\log n}) with no three collinear points. While algebraic constructions of size Ω ⁡ ( n ) \Omega(n) are known for the no-three-in-line problem, it is not known whether a randomised construction of this size exists. Green [ 20 , Problem 72] raised the question whether any no- 3 3 -in-a-line-subset of large enough size reduces to an algebraic curve modulo some prime. A construction exhibiting some weak pseudorandomness properties could give a negative answer to Green’s question. See also Eppstein’s blog post [ 19 ] for a discussion of randomised constructions of no-three-in-line sets. For the related no-four-on-a-circle problem, Corollary 1.4 shows that random constructions of size Ω ⁡ ( n ) \Omega(n) exist. Concluding remarks To conclude, we highlight some open problems and potential directions for future research. 5.1. Improving bounds on S ⁡ ( n , d ) S(n,d) As mentioned in the introduction, we conjecture that the following stronger upper bound on S ⁡ ( n , d ) S(n,d) holds. Conjecture 5.1 . For any integer d ⩾ 2 d\geqslant 2 , we have S ⁡ ( n , d ) = O ⁡ ( n d 2 + d − 1 ) S(n,d)=O(n^{d^{2}+d-1}) . We have confirmed this conjecture for d = 2 d=2 in Theorem 1.3 . For d ⩾ 3 d\geqslant 3 , it is plausible that an even stronger bound holds, namely, S ⁡ ( n , d ) = n d 2 + d − 2 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) S(n,d)=n^{d^{2}+d-2+o(1)} . If true, this would imply that the lower bound in Theorem 1.5 is sharp up to a o ⁡ ( 1 ) o(1) term in the exponent. Exact computations of S ⁡ ( n , d ) S(n,d) for small values of n n and d d seem consistent with the above estimates. Assuming joint spatial and modular equidistribution of lattice points on spheres of the form x 1 2 + ⋯ + x d 2 = m x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{d}^{2}=m , where m ∈ ℕ m\in\mathbb{N} , yields a heuristic for the stronger upper bound mentioned above. Here, spatial equidistribution refers to equidistribution with respect to the Lebesgue measure on the sphere, and modular equidistribution refers to equidistribution of mod k k residues of the lattice points on the sphere over the mod k k solutions to the equation defining the sphere, where k ∈ ℕ k\in\mathbb{N} . By joint equidistribution, we mean that modular equidistribution holds locally within small slices of the sphere. Spatial equidistribution of lattice points on spheres is known classically (see [ 27 , Section 11.6] ), but not in conjunction with modular equidistribution. We remark that this approach fails in two dimensions due to a special symmetry corresponding to isosceles trapezoids. Finally, we also highlight a connection to random matrix theory. Extensible no-four-on-a-circle The classical no-three-in-line problem seeks to maximize the number of points that may be selected from [ n ] 2 [n]^{2} while avoiding collinear triples of points. Erde asked whether one can select large subsets of ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}^{2} that avoid collinear triples. Nagy, Nagy, and Woodroofe [ 33 ] showed that there exists such a set with Θ ⁡ ( n / log 1 + ε ⁡ n ) \Theta(n/\log^{1+\varepsilon}n) points in [ n ] 2 [n]^{2} for all n ∈ ℕ n\in\mathbb{N} . Considering a similar extensible version of the no-four-on-a-circle problem would be interesting. 5.3.
