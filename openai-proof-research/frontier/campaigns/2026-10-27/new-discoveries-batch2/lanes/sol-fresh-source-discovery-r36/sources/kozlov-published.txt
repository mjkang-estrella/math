Kozlov 1 , 2 1524 Accesses Explore all metrics Abstract In this paper, we introduce a new family of combinatorial cubical complexes \(\{X_n\}_{n\ge 1}\) . Each such complex \(X_n\) models the process of updating spanning tree networks on n vertices over time. The vertices of these complexes model the networks themselves, whereas the higher-dimensional cubes model the network realignments. Such realignments may become necessary in order to maintain the connectivity of the networks by local changes, in case the connections to the leaves become weak. Our goal is to study the topology of \(X_n\) . Our main result states that for any n , there exists a strong deformation retraction from the complex \(X_n\) to the complete graph \(K_n\) , the latter viewed as a topological space. In particular, the homology vanishes in dimensions 2, and above, and, in fact, these complexes are homotopy equivalent to wedges of circles. Manifolds and Cell Complexes Network topology Complex Networks Topology Graph Theory Algebraic Topology Graph Enumeration Techniques in Complex Networks 1 Introduction Assume we are given a collection of n nodes, here \(n\ge 3\) , and let us say we would like to maintain a communication network between them. This network must stay connected at any time, yet have as few links as possible. Mathematically, such a network is modelled by a tree T , whose vertices are the n nodes, and whose edges are the pairs of vertices connected by communication lines. The number of edges is always \(n-1\) . When two vertices are connected by an edge, we call them neighbours . Each vertex v has at least one neighbour, the number of neighbours of each vertex is called its valency . The vertices of valency 1 are called leaves , the other vertices are called internal . The unique vertex which a leaf is connected to is called its parent . Assume now, that we would like to model the robustness of the network, and that over time we would like to allow to reconnect the leaves in a local fashion. Perhaps, at some point, a connection from the leaf became not viable, and needs to be replaced, by a connection to a neighbour of its parent. Formally, we may choose to adjust the network as follows. When v is a leaf, and ( v , w ) is an edge of T , we are allowed to remove ( v , w ) and then to reconnect v to one of the neighbours of w . Of course, this neighbour u should be different from v itself, but it may well be another leaf of T . If we do that, we will get a new tree \(T'\) , which still has v as a leaf. We can visualize the transformation as a leaf sliding along an edge to one of the neighbours of its original parent, see Fig. 1 . That neighbour becomes the new parent of the vertex that was slid. Observe, that sliding a leaf of a tree can create new leaves, make existing leaves disappear, or leave the set of leaves unchanged. For instance, in Fig. 1 , sliding q to w in T would make u a new leaf. Note, furthermore, that in \(T'\) the vertex w is a neighbour of u , so the above procedure can be reversed and we can slide the vertex v back to w . The standard way to record the totality of such tree transformations is by means of a certain undirected graph, which we call \(G_n\) . The vertices of \(G_n\) are indexed by all possible trees on n labeled vertices. The edges of \(G_n\) are indexed by all pairs of trees \((T,T')\) , where one tree can be obtained from the other one by the network realignment operation above. Fig. 1 Full size image Sliding a leaf along an edge It is easy to see that the graph \(G_n\) is connected, see Proposition 2.4 , which is the same as to say, that any tree on n vertices can be obtained from any other tree on n vertices by these operations. So, starting from some communication network we may end up with an arbitrary one, as long as it has the same nodes, after a number of such realignment steps. The initial impulse for the theoretical investigation which we undertake in this paper was to understand the higher-dimensional questions related to this framework. For example, given two different sequences of realignments, both leading from some fixed tree to some other fixed tree: are these two sequences different in an essential way, or does there exist some sort of homotopy between them? This line of questioning can then be continued by asking about the existence of “homotopies between homotopies” etc. We formalize this question by introducing a family of cubical complexes \(\{X_n\}_{n\ge 1}\) , where n is a positive integer. The 1-skeleton of the complex \(X_n\) is the graph \(G_n\) , while the higher dimensional cubes encode the collections of independent network realignments. Our goal is to study the topology of \(X_n\) . While \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) are single vertices, the rest of the family \(\{X_n\}_{n\ge 1}\) is more interesting. Our main theorem states that for any n , there exists a strong deformation retraction from the complex \(X_n\) to the complete graph \(K_n\) , where the latter is viewed as a topological space. In particular, for \(n\ge 3\) , the complex \(X_n\) is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of \((n-1)(n-2)/2\) circles. While our investigation is primarily motivated by mathematical curiousity, in practical terms this result can be interpreted as follows. The fact that \(X_n\) is connected means that any network can evolve into any other network. In addition to being connected, the space \(X_n\) has a non-trivial fundamental group. This means that there exist network realignment sequences which are not similar to each other, in the sense that there is no homotopy between them. For real-world networks that would mean that we may have two sequences of network evolutions which start with the same network and also result in the same network, and we might, as above, be asking ourselves whether the two sequences are equivalent, which is formally defined as have a path-homotopy between them. In general, the answer to this question is negative. Mathematically, the precise pattern of the existence of such homotopies is described by the fundamental group of \(X_n\) , which, for \(n\ge 3\) , as a consequence of our main result, is shown to be isomorphic to the free group with \(\left( {\begin{array}{c}n-1\\ 2\end{array}}\right) \) generators. Finally, the fact that \(X_n\) is homotopy equivalent to a complete graph implies that all the higher-dimensional homotopies are trivial. Admittedly, this is a bit harder to interpret in practice. What it means is that no additional difficulties arise in higher dimensions. For instance, if two evolution sequences can be deformed into each other in two separate ways, then then there is a higher-dimensional deformation between these two deformations; and so on in higher dimensions. The plan of the paper is as follows. In Sect. 2 we develop the combinatorial language of network realignments to index the cubes in \(X_n\) , and to describe the boundary relations in that complex. Here, we refer the reader to Kozlov ( 2008 ), and to Hatcher ( 2002 ), Munkres ( 1984 ), for the basic notions of combinatorial topology. In Sect. 3 we use that language to analyze the first interesting low-dimensional cases \(X_3\) , \(X_4\) , and \(X_5\) . Finally, in Sect. 4 we introduce the concept of a spine of a network realignment, and use it to filter the cubical complexes \(X_n\) . We then use the methods of discrete Morse theory to prove our main result. We refer to Kozlov ( 2008 , 2020 ), Knudson ( 2015 ), Scoville ( 2019 ) for the basics of discrete Morse theory, and to Cohen ( 1973 ) for the general theory of simplicial collapses. Let us conclude this introductory section by putting our work in some context. Combinatorial families of cubical, and more generally prodsimplicial, complexes have been studied extensively in applied topology. In full knowledge of incompleteness of our list, we would still like to mention some instances. In Billera et al. ( 2001 ) cubical complexes have been used to study the spaces of phylogenetic trees and applications in biology, while Kaczynski et al. ( 2004 ) is a major reference to the applications of cubical complexes in computation. More recently a different combinatorial family has been studied, see Kozlov ( 2022 , 2023 ), Hoekstra-Mendoza ( 2025 ), Kozlov ( 2024 ). All of these are quite different from the complexes which we study here. On the other hand, our complexes are rather similar in spirit to the so-called Hom-complexes, which are prodsimplicial complexes, see Babson and Kozlov ( 2006 , 2007 ), Kozlov ( 2007 , 2008 ). Given two graphs G and H , the vertices of the Hom-complexes are indexed by all graph homomorphisms from G to H , the edges encode the elementary moves changing the value of the graph homomorphism at a single vertex, and the higher-dimensional cells encode commuting families of such re-colorings. In the same vein, the vertices of our complexes will encode all possible trees on a fixed set of vertices, the edges encode elementary moves of sliding leaves along an edges, and higher-dimensional cells encode commuting families of such slides. 2 Formal framework 2.1 The graph of sliding leaves \(G_n\) We shall now formalize the discussion which we started in the previous section. In order to be self-contained, we shall repeat some of the terminology. To start with, unless explicitly stated otherwise, we shall restrict ourselves to considering finite undirected simple connected graphs. Given a graph G , we let V ( G ) denote its set of vertices, and we let E ( G ) denote its set of edges. Such a graph is called a tree if it has no cycles. As already said above, when two vertices are connected by an edge, we call them neighbours , and vertices with a single neighbour are called leaves . When v is a leaf of tree T and \((v,w)\in E(T)\) , the vertex w is called the parent of v , and we write \(w=p_T(v)\) . Furthermore, in this case \(T\setminus v\) denotes the tree obtained from T by removing the vertex v and its adjacent edge. Definition 2.1 Assume T is a tree, and assume that v and u are distinct vertices of T , which satisfy the following conditions: v is a leaf of T ; \((u, p_T(v))\) is an edge in T . We define a new graph \(T^{v\rightarrow u}\) as follows: T and \(T^{v\rightarrow u}\) have the same sets of vertices; the set of edges of \(T^{v\rightarrow u}\) is given by the formula $$\begin{aligned} E(T^{v\rightarrow u})=E(T)\cup (v,u)\setminus (v,p_T(v)). \end{aligned}$$ Figure 1 illustrates Definition 2.1 . Proposition 2.2 Assume T is a tree, v is a leaf of T , and u is a neighbour of \(p_T(v)\) . (1) The graph \(T^{v\rightarrow u}\) is again a tree. (2) The graph \((T^{v\rightarrow u})^{v\rightarrow p_T(v)}\) is well defined, and in fact \((T^{v\rightarrow u})^{v\rightarrow p_T(v)}=T\) . Proof Set \(w:=p_T(v)\) and let \(T'=T\setminus v\) . We see that \(T'\) is a tree, since it is connected and has no cycles. Furthermore, we see that \(T'=T^{v\rightarrow u}\setminus v\) , so the graph \(T^{v\rightarrow u}\) is obtained from the tree \(T'\) by adding a vertex of valency 1, hence it is also a tree. Furthermore, in the graph \(T^{v\rightarrow u}\) , the vertex w is a neighbour of u , while the vertex u is a parent of v , see Fig. 1 . It follows that \((T^{v\rightarrow u})^{v\rightarrow w}\) is well defined, and that \((T^{v\rightarrow u})^{v\rightarrow w}=T\) . \(\square \) We can now define the graph of sliding leaves. For a positive integer n , let [ n ] denote the set \(\{1,\dots ,n\}\) . Definition 2.3 Assume \(n\ge 3\) , the graph \(G_n\) is defined as follows: the vertices of \(G_n\) are all trees with vertex set [ n ]; two vertices of \(G_n\) , indexed by trees T and \(T'\) , are connected by an edge if and only if there exist \(v,u\in [n]\) , such that \(T^{v\rightarrow u}\) is well-defined and \(T^{v\rightarrow u}=T'\) . In other words, a vertex which is labeled by a tree T is connected to all the vertices \(T^{v\rightarrow u}\) , where v ranges over all leaves of T , and, for fixed v , the vertex u is any neighbour of \(p_T(v)\) . The following can be immediately said about the structure of the graph \(G_n\) . Proposition 2.4 The graph \(G_n\) is connected. It has \(n^{n-2}\) vertices, and \(n(n-1)^{n-3}(n-2)\) edges. Proof To start with, the number of vertices of \(G_n\) is the number of all labeled trees with n vertices, so it is given by the Cayley’s formula, e.g., see (Ore 1962 , Theorem 4.1.3). Let us calculate \(|E(G_n)|\) . Let f denote the number of edges where we slide a vertex with some fixed label. By symmetry, the number f is independent of which label is fixed, so the total number of edges of \(G_n\) is equal to nf . Let us now say, we are sliding the vertex labeled n . The situation in which this particular vertex is slid appears in the following way: we start with some tree T on \(n-1\) vertices, choose one of its edges e , and then say that the sliding of the vertex n happens along that edge. Since T has \(n-2\) edges, and, by Cayley’s formula, there are \((n-1)^{n-3}\) ways to choose T , we conclude that \(f=(n-2)(n-1)^{n-3}\) , and so \(|E(G_n)|=n(n-1)^{n-3}(n-2)\) . Let us finally see that the graph \(G_n\) is connected. Let S be the tree with a unique internal vertex, which is labeled by 1. Given any other tree T , it must have a leaf v , whose parent w is different from 1. There is a unique path p in T , connecting 1 with w . We can slide v along this path, until it reaches the vertex 1. This way, we connect T to a vertex of \(G_n\) , such that the valency of the vertex 1 in the indexing tree of this vertex, is one higher than the valency of 1 in T . Iterating this procedure, we will eventually reach S ; in fact the number of steps will not exceed \((n-1)(n-2)/2\) . In general, the valencies of the vertices of \(G_n\) may vary between 2, for the path graphs, and \((n-1)(n-2)\) , for the trees with a single internal vertex. We start by an example. Consider the tree T in Fig. 3 . Leaf 1 can be slid to vertex 5, and also leaf 6 can be slid to vertex 4. These transformations can be done in any order, so in a sense, they commute. We would like to express this fact geometrically by gluing in a square, which we call \(\sigma \) , into the 4-cycle in \(G_n\) , whose vertices are T , \(T^{1\rightarrow 5}\) , \(T^{6\rightarrow 4}\) , and \(\widetilde{T}\) , where $$\begin{aligned} \widetilde{T}=(T^{6\rightarrow 4})^{1\rightarrow 5}=(T^{1\rightarrow 5})^{6\rightarrow 4}. \end{aligned}$$ Furthermore, sliding 3 to 2 commutes both with sliding 1 to 5, as well as with sliding 6 to 4, so, we would like to glue in an appropriate 3-dimensional cube, which we call \(\tau \) . On the negative side, sliding 1 to 2 does not commute with sliding 2 to 3. The vertex 2 in \(T^{1\rightarrow 2}\) is not a leaf, so \((T^{1\rightarrow 2})^{2\rightarrow 3}\) is not well-defined. Fig. 3 Full size image Examples of network realignments Geometrically, we would like to summarize this whole situation by means of a single cubical complex. To do that, we shall take the graph \(G_n\) as the one-dimensional skeleton of a certain cubical complex \(X_n\) and then extend it by gluing higher dimensional cubes, with each cube encoding such an independence relation. Note, that we think of the vertices as 0-dimensional cubes, edges as 1-dimensional cubes, and squares as 2-dimensional cubes. While the vertices of \(X_n\) are indexed by the trees, and the edges of \(X_n\) shall be indexed by sliding single leaves, the higher-dimensional cubes should correspond to the independent simultaneous slides of collections of leaves. The next definition provides the combinatorial terminology for labeling the cells of the cubical complex we would like to construct. Definition 2.6 Let n be an integer, \(n\ge 2\) . A network realignment N with n nodes consists of representing the set [ n ] as a disjoint union: \([n]=A\cup B\) , such that \(|A|\ge 2\) ; a tree T whose vertex set is A ; an arbitrary function \(r:B\rightarrow E(T)\) . We write \(N=(T,r)\) . The sets A and B are then given as \(A:=V(T)\) , \(B:=[n]\setminus A\) . The set A must have at least two elements, but the set B may even be empty. If the network realignment needs to be specified, we shall add N in brackets, and write T ( N ), B ( N ), etc. The way we need to think about the network realignment is as follows. The tree T corresponds to the part of network which does not change. The set B contains all the leaves which are slid independently and simultaneously. Each single realignment \(T^{v\rightarrow u}\) can be thought of as a triangle with base \((p_T(v),u)\) and apex v . We encode this set of triangles by the function r , which, for each triangle, takes its apex to its base. The fact that the realignments can be done independently and simultaneously is expressed by requiring that all the bases of the triangles belong to the part of the network which does not change. For example, the network realignment indexing the cell \(\tau \) in Fig. 3 is given by the following data: \(n=6\) , \(A=\{2,4,5\}\) , \(B=\{1,3,6\}\) ; T is a tree whose set of vertices is A , and whose set of edges is \(\{(2,4),(4,5)\}\) ; the function \(r:\{1,3,6\}\rightarrow E(T)\) is given by \(r(3)=(2,4)\) , \(r(1)=r(6)=(4,5)\) . The dimension of a network realignment N is the cardinality | B ( N )|. In particular, the vertices of \(X_n\) are the 0-dimensional network realignments, they correspond to the case then the set B to be empty, and are indexed by all the trees on the set [ n ]. The dimension of \(X_n\) is \(n-2\) , with the top-dimensional cells indexed by the network realignments with \(|A|=2\) , so the tree T is just a single edge. 2.3 Realignment specification \(N^{b\rightarrow v}\) Let us now provide the combinatorial language for expressing the boundary relation in \(X_n\) . First we need yet another piece of notations. Assume G is an arbitrary graph, whose set of vertices is [ n ]. For an edge \(e\in E(G)\) , \(e=(w,v)\) , such that \(w<v\) , we set \(\partial _\bullet e:=w\) and \(\partial ^\bullet e:=v\) . Definition 2.7 Assume \(N=(T,r)\) is a network realignment with n nodes, such that \(\dim N\ge 1\) . Assume \(b\in B\) and v is one of the vertices of the edge r ( b ). We define a new network realignment, which we denote by \(N^{b\rightarrow v}=(T',r')\) , by the following data: \(A':=A\cup b\) , \(B':=B\setminus b\) ; \(T'\) is obtained from T by adding the edge ( v , b ) and the vertex b ; \(r'\) is the restriction of r to \(B'\) . Examples of realignment specifications are shown in Fig. 4 . Note, that we have \(\dim N^{b\rightarrow v}=\dim N-1\) . Fig. 4 Full size image Examples of realignment specifications 2.4 The construction of \(X_n\) We shall define \(X_n\) as a cubical cell structure on a certain topological space, which we now proceed to describe. Definition 2.8 For a positive integer n , we let \(P_n\) denote the poset defined by the following: the elements of \(P_n\) are indexed by all the network realignments on n nodes; the partial order relation of \(P_n\) is generated by the elementary relations \(N>N^{b\rightarrow v}\) , for all legal choices of b and v in the sense of Definition 2.7 . Clearly, \(P_n\) is a ranked poset, with dimension being the rank function. Its minimal elements, the so-called atoms , have rank 0, and are indexed by the vertices of \(G_n\) . The rank 1 elements are indexed by the edges of \(G_n\) . Definition 2.9 An order complex of a partially ordered set P , denoted \(\Delta (P)\) , is the abstract simplicial complex whose vertices are all the elements of P , and whose simplices are all the totally ordered subsets of P , also known as chains of P . For all \(n\ge 1\) , we now consider the geometric realization \(|\Delta (P_n)|\) . It is a topological space with a canonical simplicial structure inherited from the way it has been constructed. We now proceed to describe a cubical cell structure on \(|\Delta (P_n)|\) . To start with, let \(\Lambda \) denote the partially ordered set with 3 elements, with one of the elements being the maximal one, and the other two elements being incomparable. Let \(d\ge 1\) , and consider the poset \(\Lambda ^d\cup \{\hat{0}\}\) , obtained by attaching a minimal element to the direct product of \(\Lambda \) . Clearly, this is a face poset of a d -dimensional cube. Now, it is easy to see, that for any network realignment N , the lower ideal \(P_n^{\le N}:=\{x\in P_n\,|\,x\le N\}\) is isomorphic as a poset to the direct product \(\Lambda ^{\dim N}\) . Set \(C_N:=\Delta (P_n^{\le N})\) . This is a subcomplex of \(\Delta (P_n)\) , which is isomorphic to the barycentric subdivision of a d -dimensional cube. Therefore, we get a stratification of the topological space \(|\Delta (P_n)|\) by the subspaces \(|C_N|\) , where N varies over all network realignments. Each \(|C_N|\) carries a natural structure of a cube, with the boundary cubes being \(|C_{N'}|\) , for \(N>N'\) in \(P_n\) . We summarize these observations. Definition 2.10 For each positive integer n , we let \(X_n\) denote the cubical complex, given by the cubical structure on the geometric realization \(|\Delta (P_n)|\) , as described above. Fig. 5 Full size image An example of a 3-cell in the complex \(X_6\) So the 1-skeleton of \(X_n\) is given by the graph \(G_n\) , and the higher-dimensional cells of \(X_n\) , which are actually higher-dimensional cubes, are indexed by all network realignments with n nodes. Figure 5 shows an example of a 3-cell of \(X_6\) . The face poset of \(X_n\) can be obtained from the poset \(P_n\) by adding a minimal element. Informally speaking, given a network realignment N , each network realignment on the boundary of N of codimension 1 is obtained by restricting one of the triangles to one of its edges; continuing such restrictions will give all the boundary cubes of the cube indexed by N . 3 The first cases Let us now describe the topology of the complexes \(X_n\) for small values of n , namely for \(1\le n\le 5\) . 3.1 The case \(n\le 4\) As already mentioned, the complexes \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) are just single points. For \(n=3\) we have 3 possible trees. Any of these trees can be obtained from any other tree by sliding the leafs as described above. Therefore, the complex \(X_3\) coincides with the graph \(G_3\) , which in this case is identical to the complete graph \(K_n\) , and consists of 3 vertices and 3 edges. In particular, it is homeomorphic to \(S^1\) . Consider the case when \(n=4\) . There are 16 non-isomorphic trees with [4] as the set of vertices: 4 trees with a vertex of valency 3 and 12 trees which are paths with 3 edges, see the left part of Fig. 6 . Before we proceed with the analysis of the topology of \(X_n\) . Let us note that the d -dimensional cubes of \(X_n\) can be easily enumerated, with their number \(f_{n,d}\) given by the formula $$\begin{aligned} f_{n,d}=\left( {\begin{array}{c}n\\ d\end{array}}\right) (n-d)^{n-d-2}(n-d-1)^d. \end{aligned}$$ (3.1) Indeed, the d -dimensional cubes are indexed by the network realignments ( T , r ), where T is a labeled tree with \(n-d\) vertices. We first choose which labels are used for the tree T in \(\left( {\begin{array}{c}n\\ d\end{array}}\right) \) ways. Then choose the tree T , by Cayley’s formula there are \((n-d)^{n-d-2}\) choices. Finally, we choose the map r , which means that for each of the d vertices in \([n]\setminus V(T)\) we choose one of the \(n-d-1\) edges in T . This shows formula ( 3.1 ). We can summarize, that the f -vector of \(X_n\) is given by $$\begin{aligned} f=(n^{n-2},n(n-1)^{n-3}(n-2),\dfrac{1}{2}n(n-1)(n-2)^{n-4}(n-3)^2,\dots ). \end{aligned}$$ 4 Topology of the cubical complexes \(X_n\) 4.1 Spine of a network Before proceeding, we need some more basic graph terminology. Definition 4.1 Let T be a tree. The interior of T , denoted \(\text {Int}(T)\) is the subgraph of T induced by the set of internal vertices. We call the edges of \(\text {Int}(T)\) the internal edges , and edges of T , which are not in \(\text {Int}(T)\) , the leaf-edges . Any leaf-edge connects a leaf with another vertex, which, if T has at least 3 vertices must be internal. Let \(n\ge 3\) . A tree T with n vertices is called a star tree if exactly one of its vertices has valency more than 1. Let \(n\ge 4\) . A tree T with n vertices is called a double star tree if exactly two of its vertices, say v and w have valency more than 1. In this case, the edge \(e=(v,w)\) must belong to T , and we call it the anchor of the double star tree. We shall also abuse the language by calling the pair ( T , e ) a double star tree as well. Let \(n\ge 2\) . Let T be a tree with n vertices, and let e be an edge of T . The pair ( T , e ) is called a weak double star tree if any vertex with valency more than 1 is adjacent to e . The edge e is again called the anchor of the weak double star tree. For future reference, we state, without the proof, the following obvious proposition. Proposition 4.2 (1) The graph \(\text {Int}(T)\) has a single vertex if and only if T is a star tree. (2) The graph \(\text {Int}(T)\) consists of two vertices connected by an edge if and only if T is a double star tree. In general, we have the following statement. Proposition 4.3 If T is a tree with fewer than 3 vertices, then its interior is empty. Otherwise, the graph \(\text {Int}(T)\) is itself a non-empty tree, whose number of vertices is between 1 and \(|V(T)|-2\) . Proof To have a vertex of valency at least 2 you need at least 3 vertices, so the first part is obvious. On the other hand, if \(\text {Int}(T)\) is empty, then all the vertices have valency 0 or 1. Since T is connected, this means that T is either just a vertex, or a single edge connecting 2 vertices. It follows that \(\text {Int}(T)\) is non-empty if \(|V(T)|\ge 3\) . The graph \(\text {Int}(T)\) is connected, because T is connected, and any path connecting two vertices goes through vertices of valency at least 2. Since \(\text {Int}(T)\) is induced from T , and T has no cycles, neither has \(\text {Int}(T)\) , so it must be a tree. Since any tree with at least 3 vertices has at least 2 leaves, we have \(|V(\text {Int}(T))|\le |V(T)|-2\) . We are now ready to introduce the central combinatorial concept, which will allow us to define a filtration on the complexes \(X_n\) . Definition 4.4 Let T be a tree, and let \(N=(T,r)\) be a network realignment. The spine of N , denoted \(\text {Sp}(N)\) , is a subgraph of T , given as follows: the set of vertices of \(\text {Sp}(N)\) consists of all vertices of valency at least 2, together with all leaves, whose adjacent edge lies in \(\text {Im}(r)\) . the set of edges of \(\text {Sp}(N)\) is equal to \(E(\text {Int}(T))\cup \text {Im}(r)\) . In other words, the graph \(\text {Sp}(N)\) is obtained from \(\text {Int}(T)\) , by adding the leaf-edges, which are in the image of r , along with the adjacent leaves. For brevity, we shall write \(\text {Sp}(N)=\text {Int}(T)\cup \text {Im}(r)\) . Alternatively, we can think of \(\text {Sp}(N)\) as obtained from T by deleting all those leaf-edges, along with the adjacent leaves, which do not lie in \(\text {Im}(r)\) . We let \(|\text {Sp}(N)|\) be equal to the number of edges of \(\text {Sp}(N)\) , and call it the size of the spine of N . An example of a spine of size 3 is shown in Fig. 12 . Fig. 12 Full size image A spine of a network realignment The network realignments with a small spine will play a special role for determining the homotopy type of \(X_n\) . The next proposition classifies these. Proposition 4.5 Let \(N=(T,r)\) be a network realignment. (1) The spine of N is empty if and only if B is empty, and T is a tree consisting of 2 vertices and a single edge connecting them. (2) The spine of N consists of a single vertex if and only if B is empty and T is a star tree. (3) The spine of N consists of two vertices, connected by an edge, if (a) either B is empty and T is a double star tree, (b) or B is non-empty, \(\text {Im}(r)=\{e\}\) , and ( T , e ) is a weak double star tree. Proof Clearly, \(\text {Sp}(N)\) is empty if and only if B is empty and \(\text {Int}(T)\) is empty. Since \(n\ge 2\) , the claim (1) follows from Proposition 4.3 . To show (2) note that, since \(\text {Sp}(N)\) has no edges, we still have \(B=\emptyset \) . Then, the spine consists of a single vertex if and only if \(\text {Int}(T)\) consists of single vertex, and the claim follows by Proposition 4.2 (1). Finally, let us show (3). If B is empty, then \(\text {Int}(T)\) is a single edge tree. By Proposition 4.2 (2), it means that T is a double star tree, settling this case. If, on the other hand, the set B is non-empty, then \(\text {Im}(r)\) is non-empty. This implies \(|\text {Im}(r)|=1\) , since all the edges of \(\text {Im}(r)\) are in the spine, and the spine has only one edge. Let \(\text {Im}(r)=\{e\}\) . Since \(\text {Sp}(N)\) has only one edge, we need to have that \(\text {Int}(T)\) has at most one edge, and, if it has one, it must be e . On the other hand, T must have at least one edge. It follows from Proposition 4.2 together with Proposition 4.3 , that ( T , e ) is a weak double star tree. In general, we have the following observation. Proposition 4.6 The spine of an arbitrary network realignment is a tree. Proof Let \(N=(T,r)\) be a network realignment. Attaching any set of leaves to \(\text {Int}(T) \) , which is itself a tree, yields a tree. \(\square \) The next proposition will allow us to use the concept of a spine to introduce a filtration on the complexes \(X_n\) . Proposition 4.7 Let \(N=(T,r)\) be a network realignment with n nodes, let \(b\in B\) , and say \(r(b)=(v,w)\) . Then \(\text {Sp}(N)\supseteq \text {Sp}(N^{b\rightarrow v})\) . Moreover, this inclusion is strict if and only if w is a leaf of T and b is the only element of B , which is mapped to ( v , w ) by r . In this case, the tree \(\text {Sp}(N^{b\rightarrow v})\) is obtained from \(\text {Sp}(N)\) by removing the leaf-edge ( v , w ) along with the leaf w . Proof Let \(N^{b\rightarrow v}=(T',r')\) , and set \(A':=V(T')\) , and \(B':=[n]\setminus A'\) . By Definition 2.7 we have: \(A'=A\cup b\) and \(B'=B\setminus b\) ; the tree \(T'\) is obtained from T by adding the edge ( v , b ), along with the adjacent vertex b ; the function \(r'\) is the restriction of r to \(B'\) . Let us show that $$\begin{aligned} \text {Int}(T)\cup \text {Im}(r)\supseteq \text {Int}(T')\cup \text {Im}(r')\supseteq \text {Int}(T)\cup \text {Im}(r)\setminus (v,w). \end{aligned}$$ (4.1) To start with, we clearly have \(\text {Im}(r)\supseteq \text {Im}(r')\supseteq \text {Im}(r)\setminus (w,v)\) . Let us look at the graph \(\text {Int}(T')\) . The vertex b is a leaf in \(T'\) , so it is not in \(\text {Int}(T')\) . The valency of all other vertices of \(T'\) , except for v are the same as in the tree T , whereas \(\text {{val}}_{T'} v=\text {{val}}_T v+1\) . If \(\text {{val}}_T v\ge 2\) , then \(\text {Int}(T)=\text {Int}(T')\) , hence the inclusion \(\text {Sp}(N)\supseteq \text {Sp}(N^{b\rightarrow v})\) is proved in this case. Otherwise, assume v is a leaf of T . In this case, \(\text {Int}(T')\) is obtained from \(\text {Int}(T)\) by adding the edge ( w , v ), and the vertex v . However, the edge ( w , v ) was in \(\text {Im}(r)\) , so \(\text {Int}(T')\subseteq \text {Int}(T)\cup \text {Int}(r)\) . Together with the observation that \(\text {Int}(T')\supseteq \text {Int}(T)\) , this implies ( 4.1 ). To complete the proof, note that we obtain a strict inclusion \(\text {Sp}(N)\supset \text {Sp}(N^{b\rightarrow v})\) , if and only if the edge ( w , v ) is not in \(\text {Int}(T')\cup \text {Im}(r')\) . We have \((w,v),(b,v)\in E(T')\) , so \(\text {{val}}_{T'}v\ge 2\) . Therefore, \((w,v)\notin E(\text {Int}(T'))\) if and only if \(\text {{val}}_{T'} w=1\) , but \(\text {{val}}_{T'} w=\text {{val}}_T w\) , so w must be a leaf of T . Finally, \((v,w)\notin \text {Im}(r')\) if and only if b is the only element of B , which is mapped to ( v , w ) by the function r , and, so, the proof is finished. For example, taking the network realignment N from Fig. 12 , we have a strict inclusion \(\text {Sp}(N)\supset \text {Sp}(N^{3\rightarrow 4})\) , since \(\text {Sp}(N^{3\rightarrow 4})\) is a graph with vertices 4,5, and 7, and edges (4, 7) and (4, 5). Definition 4.8 For \(p\ge 0\) , let \(\mathcal {F}_p X_n\) denote the set of all cells N of \(X_n\) , such that \(|\text {Sp}(N)|\le p\) . By Proposition 4.7 , \(\mathcal {F}_p X_n\) is actually a subcomplex of \(X_n\) , so we have a filtration $$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {F}_0 X_n\subseteq \mathcal {F}_1 X_n\subseteq \dots \subseteq \mathcal {F}_{n-2} X_n=X_n. \end{aligned}$$ 4.2 The topology of \(\mathcal {F}_1 X_n\) Consider now the complexes \(\mathcal {F}_1 X_n\) of network realignments, whose spine is contained in a single edge. If the edge is fixed, then we have a cube. For each pair \(1\le i<j\le n\) , let \(C_{i,j}\) be the subcomplex of \(\mathcal {F}_1 X_n\) , consisting of all the network realignments whose spine is contained in the edge ( i , j ). For \(1\le q\le n\) , let \(s_q\) denote the vertex of \(X_n\) indexed by the star tree T , for which \(\text {Int}(T)=s_q\) . The following proposition is immediate. Proposition 4.9 For each pair \(1\le i<j\le n\) , the subcomplex \(C_{i,j}\) is isomorphic to an \((n-2)\) -dimensional cube. Furthermore, if \(1\le k<l\le n\) , then we have $$ C_{i,j}\cap C_{k,l}= {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \emptyset ,& \text { if }\{i,j\}\cap \{k,l\}=\emptyset , \\ s_q, & \text { if }\{i,j\}\cap \{k,l\}=\{q\}. \end{array}\right. }$$ Corollary 4.10 For all \(n\ge 2\) , there exists a strong deformation retraction from \(\mathcal {F}_1 X_n\) to \(K_n\) , where \(K_n\) is a complete graph on n vertices, viewed as a topological space. In particular, the subcomplex \(\mathcal {F}_1 X_n\) is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of \(\left( {\begin{array}{c}n-1\\ 2\end{array}}\right) \) copies of \(S^1\) . Proof Each cube \(C_{i,j}\) contains the points \(s_i\) and \(s_j\) , as opposite cube vertices. Footnote 2 Let \(d_{i,j}\) denote the cube diagonal connecting \(s_i\) with \(s_j\) . Obviously, there exists a strong deformation retraction from \(C_{i,j}\) to \(d_{i,j}\) . Since the points \(s_i\) and \(s_j\) are fixed during the deformation retraction, and the cubes \(C_{i,j}\) only intersect in such points, we can perform all these strong deformation retractions simultaneously for all \(i<j\) . This yields a strong deformation retraction from \(\mathcal {F}_1 X_n\) to a graph with vertices \(s_1,\dots ,s_n\) , and edges \(d_{i,j}\) , for \(1\le i<j\le n\) . This a complete graph \(K_n\) . Any connected graph with v vertices and e edges is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of \(e-v+1\) copies of \(S^1\) . For the complete graph the number of copies comes out as \(\left( {\begin{array}{c}n\\ 2\end{array}}\right) -n+1=\left( {\begin{array}{c}n-1\\ 2\end{array}}\right) \) . 4.3 Recalling some discrete Morse theory Let us recall some terminology of discrete Morse theory, see Kozlov ( 2008 ), Kozlov ( 2020 ). For a polyhedral complex K we let \(\succ \) denote the covering relation in its cell poset. In other words, we write \(\alpha \succ \beta \) , if \(\alpha \) and \(\beta \) are cells of K , \(\beta \) is contained in the boundary of \(\alpha \) , and \(\dim \alpha =\dim \beta +1\) . A (partial) matching on the set of cells of K is a bijection \(\mu :M\rightarrow M\) , where M is a subset of the set of cells of K , satisfying the following two conditions: (1) for every \(\sigma \in M\) , either \(\mu (\sigma )\succ \sigma \) or \(\sigma \succ \mu (\sigma )\) ; (2) \(\mu \circ \mu =\text {id}_M\) . Definition 4.11 A matching \(\mu \) is called acyclic if it is impossible to find \(t\ge 2\) , together with distinct cells \(a_1,\dots ,a_t\) , such that $$\begin{aligned} a_1\prec \mu (a_1)\succ a_2\prec \mu (a_2)\succ \dots \succ a_t\prec \mu (a_t)\succ a_1. \end{aligned}$$ We shall need the following simple result. Theorem 4.12 ( 2008 , Theorem 11.13(a)). Let K be a polyhedral complex, and let \(\widetilde{K}\) be a subcomplex. Assume that we have an acyclic matching on the set of cells \(K\setminus \widetilde{K}\) , then there exists a strong deformation retraction from K to \(\widetilde{K}\) . Since our complexes are cubical, we can apply Theorem 4.12 . 4.4 An acyclic matching for \(X_n\) The matching on the cells of \(X_n\) will be defined using certain special vertices of the underlying network, which we now proceed to define. Definition 4.13 Let \(N=(T,r)\) be a network realignment. A vertex \(v\in [n]\) is called active if either one of the following mutually exclusive conditions is true (1) \(v\in B\) and r ( v ) is a leaf-edge of the spine \(\text {Sp}(N)\) ; (2) \(v\in A\) , v is a leaf of T , and \(p_T(v)\) is a leaf of \(\text {Sp}(N)\) . We let \(\text {Act}^+(N)\) denote the set of those active vertices of N , which satisfy condition (1) of Definition 4.13 , and let \(\text {Act}^-(N)\) denote the set of those which satisfy condition (2). We let \(\text {Act}(N)\) denote the total set of active vertices, i.e., \(\text {Act}(N)=\text {Act}^+(N)\cup \text {Act}^-(N)\) , where the union is disjoint. For instance, for the network N in Fig. 12 , we have \(\text {Act}^+(N)=\{1,3,6\}\) and \(\text {Act}^-(N)=\{8\}\) . Lemma 4.14 Assume \(N=(T,r)\) is a network realignment, such that \(\text {Sp}(N)\) has at least two edges, then the set \(\text {Act}(N)\) is non-empty. Proof Let v be any leaf of \(\text {Sp}(N)\) , and assume \(e=(w,v)\in E(\text {Sp}(N))\) . If \(e\in \text {Im}(r)\) , then \(r^{-1}(e)\subseteq \text {Act}(N)\) , so \(\text {Act}(N)\) is non-empty. Otherwise, assume \(e\notin \text {Im}(r)\) . Since \(e\in E(\text {Sp}(N))\) , this implies that \(e\in E(\text {Int}(T))\) . In particular, \(\text {{val}}_T v\ge 2\) . Let \(u\in V(T)\) be the vertex such that \(u\ne w\) and \((v,u)\in E(T)\) . Clearly u is a leaf of T , since v is a leaf of \(\text {Sp}(N)\) , and it satisfies the second condition of Definition 4.13 , so it lies in \(\text {Act}(N)\) , which therefore must be non-empty. In fact, the argument in the proof of Lemma 4.14 shows that \(|\text {Act}(N)|\ge 2\) , but we will not need that in what follows. Let us now show how to reverse the operation of the network realignment specification. Definition 4.15 Let \(N=(T,r)\) be a network realignment. Let b be a leaf of T , and let e be an edge adjacent to \(p_T b\) , such that \(e\ne (b,p_T b)\) . Assume that \((b,p_T b)\notin \text {Im}(r)\) . We define \(N^{b\rightarrow e}\) to be the network realignment \((\widetilde{T},\widetilde{r})\) , where \(\widetilde{T}:=T\setminus (b,p_T b)\) , \(\widetilde{r}(b):=e\) , and the functions \(\widetilde{r}\) and r coincide on the set B . Note how we overload the notation at this point, as \(N^{x\rightarrow y}\) denotes two different operations, depending on whether y is a vertex or an edge. Proposition 4.16 Let \(N=(T,r)\) be a network realignment. (1) If \(b\in B\) and v is a vertex adjacent to r ( b ), then we have \((N^{b\rightarrow v})^{b\rightarrow r(b)}=N\) ; (2) If b is a leaf of T , such that \((b,p_T b)\notin \text {Im}(r)\) , and e is an edge adjacent to \(p_T b\) , but not adjacent to b , then we have \((N^{b\rightarrow e})^{b\rightarrow p_T b}=N\) . Proof A formal check using the definitions of the involved operations. The next notion is the main technical tool to define the matching \(\mu \) . Definition 4.17 Let N be a network realignment, and let v be one of its active vertices. We define a new network realignment \(\rho (N,v)\) as follows. If \(v\in \text {Act}^+(N)\) , set \((w,u):=r(v)\) . Without loss of generality, we can assume that u is a leaf of \(\text {Sp}(N)\) . We then set \(\rho (N,v):=N^{v\rightarrow u}\) . If \(v\in \text {Act}^-(N)\) , v must be a leaf of T . Set \(w:=p_T(v)\) . This is then a leaf of \(\text {Sp}(N)\) , and we let e be the adjacent edge, i.e., we set \(e:=(w,p_{\text {Sp}(N)}(w))\) . We then set \(\rho (N,v):=N^{v\rightarrow e}\) . Examples of network realignments from Definition 4.17 are shown in Fig. 13 . Fig. 13 Full size image Examples of network realignments \(\rho (N,v)\) The key property of applying \(\rho (-,v)\) is that neither the spine, nor the set of the active vertices of the network realignment in the argument, are changed. Lemma 4.18 Assume \(N=(T,r)\) is a network realignment, and \(v\in \text {Act}(N)\) . Then, N and \(\rho (N,v)\) have the same spines and the same sets of active vertices. Footnote 3 Proof The proof is not difficult, but it is somewhat tedious due to the number of cases involved. Let \(\rho (N,v)=(\widetilde{T},\widetilde{r})\) . Assume first that \(v\in \text {Act}^+(N)\) , in other words, \(\rho (N,v)=N^{v\rightarrow u}\) . In this case of course \(v\in B\) , and u is a leaf of \(\text {Sp}(N)\) adjacent to r ( v ). We start by proving that the spines are the same. Set \(e:=r(v)\) . It is easy to see that \(\text {Sp}(N)\setminus e=\text {Sp}(N^{v\rightarrow u})\setminus e\) , because \(\text {Im}(r)\setminus e=\text {Im}(\widetilde{r})\setminus e\) , and \(\text {Int}(T)\setminus e=\text {Int}(\widetilde{T})\setminus e\) . On the other hand, \(e\in \text {Sp}(N)\) , because \(e\in \text {Im}(r)\) , and, at the same time \(e\in \text {Sp}(N^{v\rightarrow u})\) , because \(e\in \text {Int}(T(N^{v\rightarrow u}))\) . This shows that \(\text {Sp}(N)=\text {Sp}(N^{v\rightarrow u})\) . Next, we show that the sets of active vertices are the same. We have \(\text {Act}^+(N)\setminus v=\text {Act}^+(N^{v\rightarrow u}) \setminus v\) , and \(\text {Act}^-(N)\setminus v=\text {Act}^-(N^{v\rightarrow u}) \setminus v\) , because we already proved that \(\text {Sp}(N)=\text {Sp}(N^{v\rightarrow u})\) . This means that \(\text {Act}(N)\setminus v=\text {Act}(N^{v\rightarrow u}) \setminus v\) . Direct check shows that \(v\in \text {Act}^-(N^{v\rightarrow u})\) , so we can conclude that \(\text {Act}(N)=\text {Act}(N^{v\rightarrow u})\) . Assume now that \(v\in \text {Act}^-(N)\) , so \(\rho (N,v)=N^{v\rightarrow e}\) , where e is a leaf-edge of \(\text {Sp}(N)\) adjacent to \(p_T v\) . The argument that \(\text {Sp}(N)=\text {Sp}(N^{v\rightarrow e})\) repeats verbatim the argument above that \(\text {Sp}(N)=\text {Sp}(N^{v\rightarrow u})\) , with the only difference that this time \(e\in \text {Im}(\widetilde{r})\) and \(e\in \text {Int}(T)\) . The fact, that \(\text {Act}(N)=\text {Act}(N^{v\rightarrow e})\) is also proved in exactly the same way, with the difference that we need to directly check that \(v\in \text {Act}^+(N^{v\rightarrow e})\) . A matching needs to be defined by a function which is its own inverse, so we need the following statement. Lemma 4.19 Let \(N=(T,r)\) be a network realignment, and let \(v\in \text {Act}(N)\) . Then, we have \(N=\rho (\rho (N,v),v)\) . Proof By Lemma 4.18 we know that \(v\in \text {Act}(\rho (N,v))\) , so \(\rho (\rho (N,v),v)\) is well-defined. The equality \(N=\rho (\rho (N,v),v)\) then follows from Proposition 4.16 . A key role in proving the acyclicity of the matching is played by the following technical lemma. Lemma 4.20 Let \(N=(T,r)\) be a network realignment, let \(b\in B(N)\) , and let v be a vertex adjacent to r ( b ). If \(\text {Sp}(N)=\text {Sp}(N^{b\rightarrow v})\) , then \(\text {Act}(N)\supseteq \text {Act}(N^{b\rightarrow v})\) . Proof If \(c\in \text {Act}^+(N^{b\rightarrow v})\) , then \(c\in B(N^{b\rightarrow v})\) , hence also \(c\in B(N)\) , since \(B(N^{b\rightarrow v})\subset B(N)\) . The edge r ( c ) is a leaf-edge of \(\text {Sp}(N^{b\rightarrow v})\) , hence, since \(\text {Sp}(N)=\text {Sp}(N^{b\rightarrow v})\) , it is also a leaf-edge of \(\text {Sp}(N)\) , and so \(c\in \text {Act}^+(N)\) . Assume \(c\in \text {Act}^-(N^{b\rightarrow v})\) . If \(c=b\) , then the node c is a leaf of \(T'=T(N^{b\rightarrow v})\) , and r ( b ) is a leaf-edge of \(\text {Sp}(N^{b\rightarrow v})\) . In that case, r ( b ) is also a leaf-edge of \(\text {Sp}(N)\) , and so \(c\in \text {Act}^+(N)\) . Finally, assume \(c\ne b\) . In this case, c is a leaf of T and it is connected to a leaf of \(\text {Sp}(N^{b\rightarrow v})\) . Since \(\text {Sp}(N)=\text {Sp}(N^{b\rightarrow v})\) , the node c is connected to a leaf of \(\text {Sp}(N)\) , and therefore \(c\in \text {Act}^-(N)\) . We are ready to define our matching \(\mu \) . Definition 4.21 The matching is given by \(\mu (N)=\rho (N,a)\) , where N is a network realignment, such that \(|\text {Sp}(N)|\ge 2\) , and a is the minimum of \(\text {Act}(N)\) . Proposition 4.22 The matching \(\mu \) is well-defined and acyclic. Proof The fact that \(\mu \) is well-defined follows from the definition of \(N^{x\rightarrow y}\) , definition of \(\rho (N,v)\) , Lemma 4.18 , as well as Lemma 4.19 . Let us show that \(\mu \) is acyclic. Assume \(t\ge 2\) , and we have a sequence of cells \(\sigma _1,\dots ,\sigma _t\) , such that $$\begin{aligned} \sigma _1\prec \mu (\sigma _1)\succ \sigma _2\prec \mu (\sigma _2)\succ \dots \succ \sigma _t\prec \mu (\sigma _t)\succ \sigma _1. \end{aligned}$$ For convenience, set \(\sigma _{t+1}:=\sigma _1\) . By Proposition 4.7 , we have \(\text {Sp}(\mu (\sigma _i)))\supseteq \text {Sp}(\sigma _{i+1})\) , for \(1\le i\le t\) . Since, on the other hand, Lemma 4.18 says that \(\text {Sp}(\mu (\sigma _i)))=\text {Sp}(\sigma _i)\) , for \(1\le i\le t\) , we conclude that $$\begin{aligned} \text {Sp}(\sigma _1)=\text {Sp}(\mu (\sigma _1))=\text {Sp}(\sigma _2)=\text {Sp}(\mu (\sigma _2))=\dots =\text {Sp}(\sigma _t)=\text {Sp}(\mu (\sigma _t)). \end{aligned}$$ Also, by Definition 4.21 , we have \(|\text {Sp}(N)|\ge 2\) . Now, Lemma 4.18 together with Lemma 4.20 tell us $$\text {Act}(\sigma _1)=\text {Act}(\mu (\sigma _1))\supseteq \text {Act}(\sigma _2)=\text {Act}(\mu (\sigma _2))\supseteq \dots = \text {Act}(\mu (\sigma _t))\supseteq \text {Act}(\sigma _1),$$ which of course implies $$\begin{aligned} \text {Act}(\sigma _1)=\text {Act}(\mu (\sigma _1))=\text {Act}(\sigma _2)=\text {Act}(\mu (\sigma _2))=\dots =\text {Act}(\sigma _t)=\text {Act}(\mu (\sigma _t)). \end{aligned}$$ Set \(a:=\min \text {Act}(\sigma _1)=\min \text {Act}(\sigma _2)\) . By the definition of \(\mu \) , we have \(\mu (\sigma _1)=\rho (\sigma _1,a)\) , and \(\mu (\sigma _2)=\rho (\sigma _2,a)\) . In particular, this means that \(a\in A(\sigma _1)\) , \(a\in B(\mu (\sigma _1))\) , and \(a\in A(\sigma _2)\) . Let \(e=(v,w)\) be the edge of \(T(\sigma _1)\) , for which \(\mu (\sigma _1)=\sigma _1^{a\rightarrow e}\) , and assume that ( a , v ) is an edge of \(T(\sigma _1)\) . Note that v , then, must be a leaf of \(\text {Sp}(\sigma _1)\) . That, however means that w is not a leaf of \(\text {Sp}(\sigma _1)\) , since \(|\text {Sp}(N)|\ge 2\) , which, in turn, implies that a is not an active vertex of \(\sigma _2\) . This is clearly a contradiction, and the proof is finished. We can now formulate our main theorem. Theorem 4.23 There is a strong deformation retraction from \(X_n\) to \(\mathcal {F}_1 X_n\) , and hence on to the complete graph \(K_n\) . In particular, the cubical complex \(X_n\) is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of \((n-1)(n-2)/2\) copies of \(S^1\) . Proof Since the matching \(\mu \) is acyclic and the critical cells form the subcomplex \(\mathcal {F}_1 X_n\) , the existence of the strong deformation retraction is the consequence of Theorem 4.12 . The topology of \(\mathcal {F}_1 X_n\) has already been given in Corollary 4.10 , so the rest of the statement follows.
