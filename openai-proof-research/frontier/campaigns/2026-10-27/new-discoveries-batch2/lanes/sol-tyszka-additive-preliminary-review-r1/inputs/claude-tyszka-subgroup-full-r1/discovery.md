# Tyszka Conjecture 3 (math/0702558v86, printed p21): full literal scope resolved by a classical bridge — EXCLUDE, zero credit

## Exact source statement (read from the frozen r214 copy)

`frozen-inputs/tyszka.pdf` / `tyszka.txt` (arXiv:math/0702558v86, 4 Dec 2009, the latest version; live abs page re-fetched 2026-10-30T07:57:15Z, byte-identical to r214's frozen copy, sha256 b6a44b82…d844).

> Conjecture 3. Let G be an additive subgroup of C. Let S be a consistent system of equations in x1,…,xn ∈ G, where each equation in S is of the form xi = 1 or xi + xj = xk. Then S has a solution (x1,…,xn) ∈ (G∩Q)^n with |xj| ≤ 2^(n−1) for each j.

The abstract states it as (II): for each x ∈ G^n there is y ∈ (G∩Q)^n with |yi| ≤ 2^(n−1), xi=1 ⇒ yi=1, and xi+xj=xk ⇒ yi+yj=yk. These are equivalent. Given x, apply the body version to S_x, the set of all allowed equations that x satisfies. Conversely, if S has a G-solution x, then S ⊆ S_x. "Consistent" has to mean solvable in G^n. Reading it as solvable over C makes the statement trivially false: G=2Z, S={x1=1}. The comments field says a shortened version appeared in Math. Logic Quarterly 56 (2010) no. 2. That journal version was not obtained, so we do not know whether it contains Conjecture 3. The arXiv v86 body is the statement used here.

The overlapping arXiv:0807.3010v25 (frozen copy, read) contains Conjecture (I), which is the G=C case. It also says on p10: "It seems that for each integers x1,…,xn there exist integers y1,…,yn ∈ [−2^(n−1),2^(n−1)] with properties (1) and (2)". That is the G=Z case. The theorem below covers both.

## Theorem (all G, all n, all systems S)

Conjecture 3 holds exactly as stated.

### Proof

Fix G, n, S and an x ∈ G^n solving S.

**Step 0: trivial case.** If S contains no equation xi=1, every equation is homogeneous and y=0 ∈ (G∩Q)^n works. Otherwise 1 = x_{i0} ∈ G. Write S as Ax=b with A ∈ Z^{s×n} and b ∈ {0,1}^s. Put H = G∩Q, a subgroup of Q containing 1.

**Step 1: rational transfer (Smith normal form).** Choose U ∈ GL_s(Z) and V ∈ GL_n(Z) with UAV = D = diag(d_1,…,d_r,0,…) and every d_i ≥ 1. Put z = V^{-1}x. Since V^{-1} is an integer matrix and G is an additive group, z ∈ G^n, and Dz = Ub ∈ Z^s.
- For i ≤ r we have d_i z_i = (Ub)_i, so the complex number z_i equals the rational (Ub)_i/d_i. Hence z_i ∈ G∩Q = H.
- For i > r, (Ub)_i = 0.

Set z'_i = z_i for i ≤ r and z'_i = 0 for i > r. Then Dz' = Ub. The point x' = Vz' lies in H^n and satisfies Ax' = U^{-1}DV^{-1}Vz' = b.

**Step 2: cyclic reduction.** Let K be the subgroup generated by 1 and x'_1,…,x'_n. It is a finitely generated subgroup of Q. Write its generators over a common denominator c: then K = (g/c)Z, where g is the gcd of the numerators. Since 1 ∈ K, q = c/g is a positive integer, so K = (1/q)Z ⊆ H. Thus w = qx' ∈ Z^n and Aw = qb.

**Step 3: independent rows.** Choose a maximal linearly independent set R of rows of A that contains the row of x_{i0}=1, and let m = |R| ≤ n. Any other row a satisfies a = Σλ_r a_r with λ ∈ Q. Because w solves everything, qb_a = Σλ_r qb_r. So every solution of A_R w'' = qb_R also solves Aw'' = qb.

**Step 4: BFRT.** The system A_R w = qb_R is m×n of rank m and has an integer solution. By Borosh–Flahive–Rubin–Treybig (below) it has an integer solution w* with max|w*_j| ≤ Y, where Y is the largest m×m minor in absolute value of (A_R, qb_R). Then y = w*/q lies in ((1/q)Z)^n ⊆ (G∩Q)^n, solves S, and satisfies |y_j| ≤ Y/q.

**Step 5: minor bound, Y ≤ q·2^(m−1).**
- An m×m minor that does not use the last column is a minor of A_R.
- A minor that uses the last column equals q times a minor of (A_R, b_R).

So it suffices to show that every m×m minor of (A_R, b_R) and of A_R is at most 2^(m−1) in absolute value. Flip the sign of the last column (this does not change absolute values of minors) and label it 0. The rows of (A, −b) are then:
- xi=1 gives e_i − e_0;
- xi+xj=xk gives e_i + e_j − e_k (i,j,k distinct), 2e_i − e_k (i=j≠k), e_j (k=i≠j), e_i (k=j≠i), or e_i (i=j=k).

Call a row *simple* if it has at most one entry +1, at most one entry −1, and zeros elsewhere. The rows e_i − e_0, e_i and e_j are simple. The rows e_i + (e_j − e_k) and e_i + (e_i − e_k) are *double*: each is a sum of two simple rows. Restricting a simple row to a subset of columns keeps it simple.

Expand a square minor multilinearly over its double rows. This gives at most 2^d determinants of matrices whose rows are all simple, where d is the number of double rows.

Lemma: a square matrix with only simple rows has determinant 0 or ±1. Proof by induction on size. A 1×1 matrix has entry in {0,±1}. A zero row gives det 0. A row with exactly one nonzero entry ±1 can be expanded along, which gives ± a smaller simple determinant. Otherwise every row has exactly one +1 and one −1, so T·(1,…,1)^T = 0 and det T = 0.

Every m×m minor uses all m rows of R, and one of them is the simple row x_{i0}=1. So d ≤ m−1 and |minor| ≤ 2^(m−1).

**Conclusion.** |y_j| ≤ Y/q ≤ 2^(m−1) ≤ 2^(n−1). ∎

The bound is attained: the doubling chain x1=1, x_i + x_i = x_{i+1} has the unique solution (1,2,…,2^(n−1)) for any G containing 1, as the source itself notes.

### The classical theorem used (actual body read)

I. Borosh, M. Flahive, D. Rubin, B. Treybig, "A sharp bound for solutions of linear Diophantine equations", Proc. AMS 105(4) (April 1989) 844–846. The 3-page body was fetched from the AMS route (dl/12-bfrt-pdf, sha256 in provenance.json) and read in full.

Theorem (p845): let Ax=b be m×n of rank m with integer coefficients, and let Y be the maximum absolute value of the m×m minors of (A,b). If Ax=b has a solution in integers, it has one with max|x_i| ≤ Y. No sign condition is imposed.

We checked the proof independently.
1. Partition A = (B, N) with B nonsingular, δ = det B.
2. Integer solvability is equivalent to N x_N ≡ b in the finite group Z^m/BZ^m, which has order |δ|.
3. Gomory's lemma gives a nonnegative x_N with Σx_N ≤ |δ|−1. Self-check: take a shortest word in the generators. Its partial sums are distinct, so its length is at most |δ|−1. The target lies in the generated subgroup, and in a finite group that subgroup equals the generated semigroup.
4. By Cramer, |x_i| ≤ (|δ_{i0}| + Σ_j|δ_{ij}|x_{m+j})/|δ| ≤ Y(1+Σx_N)/|δ| ≤ Y. The non-basic entries are below |δ| ≤ Y.

The 1989 theorem predates every math/0702558 version (2007–2009). Veselov's arXiv:0806.4966 (read: abstract and introduction) states the nonnegative-solution analogue and is not needed.

## Disposition

The full literal arbitrary-G Conjecture 3, for all n and all consistent systems, follows completely from a 1989 published theorem, Smith normal form, and the totally-unimodular-type incidence lemma already supplied for Conjecture 4 by r214. This is a routine classical subsumption, not a new theorem. **EXCLUDE, zero credit.** r214's Conjecture 4 bridge and its G⊇Q subcase are reused here and are not counted again.

Novelty and limits: we found no public statement that Conjecture 3 is resolved. Searched: Tyszka's later arXiv bodies 1110.3549, 1102.4122, 0901.2093, 1109.3826 and 1511.04004 (no Borosh citation, no resolution), plus two standard web searches. That absence does not make the conjecture open-and-new, because the classical bridge above settles it. The MLQ 2010 journal version, citing literature and proof registries were not swept.

## Routes considered (with exact obstructions)

- **Route A (classical small-solution theorem): succeeds.** This is the proof above. The suggested mechanism's split into a discrete case and a dense case is unnecessary. Any H-solution already lives in a cyclic (1/q)Z ⊆ H (Step 2), and scaling by q reduces to Z, where BFRT is sign-free and sharp.
- **Route B (Tyszka's Observation-3 iteration restricted to H-points): fails as stated.** The step "intersect with {x_m = 0}" can leave the H-points empty. Example: x1=1, x2+x2=x3, x3+x1=x4 forces x4 = 2x2+1. Over Z, adding x4=0 forces x2 = −1/2, so the intersection has no Z-point even though it has Q-points. Repairing this needs an augmentation lemma: some allowed equation not implied by S keeps H-feasibility. We did not prove that lemma, and Route A makes it unnecessary.
- **Route C (approximating a dense H in the rational box): not needed.** Its obstruction was that boundary-extremal doubling chains have no slack, so interior approximation cannot work. Route A sidesteps this, since a dense H is never used as such.

No counterexample can exist, by the theorem. Capped exact controls are in checks.json: 3000 simple-determinant tests; 1500 minor-bound tests (the largest minor seen is exactly 2^(m−1) for m ≤ 4); 800 constructive BFRT pipeline runs over (1/q)Z with q | 12 (largest ratio to 2^(n−1) is 1.0); 400 Smith transfers for G=(1/2)Z+(1/3)Z√2; the doubling-chain rank check. These are controls, not the proof.
