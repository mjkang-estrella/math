49:     Consider the following problem: “We are organizing a social dinner for
50: the participants of a mathematical meeting. The participants are from n
51: different countries. We intent to seat the participants in m tables, each one
52: with a fix number of seats, so that in each table there are no two participants
53: with the same nationality. The number of seats is equal to the number of
54: participants and there is a possible distribution in these conditions. After
55: dinner, there will be a show. Before the show, the host will call some partic-
56: ipants for a joke. The host will choose the maximum number of participants
57: selecting, at most, one per table and, at most, one per country. As we do not
58: know when the artists will be ready for the show, the host will repeat the
59: joke with another group, with the maximum number of participants, again
63: distribute the participants in the tables so that after t rounds of jokes the
64: total number of evolved participants is as small as possible?”
65:     The above problem is a classical problem of distributing n elements into
66: m sets with some constraints. An important tool for solving these kind of
67: problems are matrices whose entries are just 0’s and 1’s, the (0, 1)-matrices.
68: In fact, Ford and Fulkerson noted that a (0, 1)-matrix can be regarded as
69: distributing n elements into m sets: the 1’s in row i designate the elements
70: that occur in the ith set, and the 1’s in column j designate the sets that
71: contain the jth element (see [10]). Therefore, the (0, 1)-matrices are essential
72: tools in many combinatorial investigations, and hence these matrices are
73: among those who have been received more attention in the last years (see
74: [1, 2, 6, 7, 8]. Using a (0, 1)-matrices for modeling a certain distribution
75: of the participants into the tables, in our problem, the number of selected
76: participants after t rounds is a combinatorial parameter (properties which
77: are invariant under arbitrary permutations of its rows and columns) of a
78: (0, 1)-matrix called t-term rank. The t-term rank of (0, 1)-matrix A, denoted
79: ρt (A), is the maximum number of 1′ s in A with at most one 1 in each column
80: and at most t 1′ s in each row. When t = 1, we have the well-known term rank
81: of A, denoted ρ(A). In terms of the incidence matrix of sets vs. elements, the
82: t-term rank of a (0, 1)-matrix is the maximum number of distinct elements
83: that we have if we choose at most t elements in each set, associated. So a
84: solution for the above problem is a (0, 1)-matrix with prescribed row and
85: column sum vectors, that realizes all the minimum t-term ranks, for t > 1.
86: In this paper, we focus our attention on this kind of (0, 1)-matrices, that

321: 
322: The rest of the paper is organised as follows. First, in Section 2 we prove Theorems 1.1 and 1.2. The rough
323: idea for both proofs is the same, and is motivated by Erdős’ proof of the Littlewood–Oﬀord theorem. We
324: consider a process that ﬂips the bits ξi from zero to one in a random order, where we start with ξ being
325: the all-zero vector, and end with ξ being the all-one vector. We show that our random variable f (ξ)
326: tends to increase fairly substantially on each ﬂip (for Theorem 1.1, this is where we use the assumption
327: that the coeﬃcients are nonnegative). We deduce that during our process, f (ξ) does not tend to spend
328: very long in the vicinity of any given value. This can then be translated into an anti-concentration result.
329: Next, in Section 3 we prove Theorem 1.8, Proposition 1.9 and Theorem 1.10. First, Proposition 1.9 has
330: a fairly routine proof, using the combinatorial Nullstellensatz. Second, Theorems 1.8 and 1.10 are proved
331: in a uniﬁed way, via a careful induction on n.
332: In the next two sections we give some applications: in Section 4 we prove Propositions 1.6 and 1.7, and
333: in Section 5 we prove Theorems 1.13 and 1.14. These are all quite direct deductions from the theorems
334: proved in Sections 2 and 3, with the exception of Theorem 1.14. Roughly speaking, the idea for the proof
335: of Theorem 1.14 is to ﬁx a vertex v and then decompose the random variable XKh (G) (counting copies
336: of Kh in G ∈ G(n, p)) as XKh = XKh (G − v) + XKh−1 (G[NG (v)]). That is to say, every copy of Kh

14:                                                  copy of H. Schelp had the idea that if the complete graph Kn arrows a small graph H, then
15:                                                  every “dense” subgraph of Kn also arrows H, and he outlined some problems in this direction.
16:                                                  Our main result is in this spirit. We prove that for every suﬃciently large n, if n = 3t + r where
17:                                                  r ∈ {0, 1, 2} and G is an n-vertex graph with δ(G) ≥ (3n − 1)/4, then for every 2-edge-coloring
18:                                                  of G, either there are cycles of every length {3, 4, 5, . . . , 2t + r} of the same color, or there are
19:                                                  cycles of every even length {4, 6, 8, . . . , 2t + 2} of the same color.
20:                                                      Our result is tight in the sense that no longer cycles (of length > 2t + r) can be guaranteed
21:                                                  and the minimum degree condition cannot be reduced. It also implies the conjecture of Schelp
22:                                                  that for every suﬃciently large n, every (3t − 1)-vertex graph G with minimum degree larger
23:                                                  than 3|V (G)|/4 arrows the path P2n with 2n vertices. Moreover, it implies for suﬃciently large
24:                                                  n the conjecture by Benevides, Luczak, Scott, Skokan and White that for n = 3t + r where
25:                                                  r ∈ {0, 1, 2} and every n-vertex graph G with δ(G) ≥ 3n/4, in each 2-edge-coloring of G there
