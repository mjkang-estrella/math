19:                                            1.1. Background. Let G be a simple graph with n = |V (G)| vertices. A coprime labeling of
20:                                            G is an injection f : V (G) → {1, 2, . . . , k}, for some integer k ≥ n, such that if (u, v) ∈ E(G)
21:                                            then gcd(f (u), f (v)) = 1. The minimum coprime number pr(G) is the least k for which such
22:                                            a labeling exists; a coprime labeling of G using only integers up to pr(G) is called a minimum
23:                                            coprime labeling of G. If pr(G) = n, then a minimum coprime labeling of G is called a prime
24:                                            labeling and G is a prime graph.
28:                                            induced subgraph G(A) with vertex set A ⊂ {1, . . . , N } is called the coprime graph of A and was
29:                                            first studied by Erdős [8], who posed the famous problem of finding the largest set A ⊂ {1, . . . , N }
30:                                            such that Kk 6⊂ G(A). Newman’s coprime mapping conjecture, which was proven by Pomerance
31:                                            and Selfridge [22], involves the existence of perfect matchings in G(A). Various other properties
32:                                            of the coprime graph of integers have been studied by Ahlswede and Khachatrian [1, 2, 3], Erdős
33:                                            [9], Erdős and A. Sárközy [10], Erdős and G. N. Sárközy [13], Erdős, A. Sárközy, and Szémeredi
34:                                            [11, 12], and G. N. Sárközy [26]. Further discussion of the coprime graph of integers may be
35:                                            found in [21].
36:                                               The problem of finding a minimum coprime labeling of a graph H is equivalent to showing
37:                                            that H is a subgraph of the coprime graph G({1, 2, . . . , pr(H)}). Much work has been done to
38:                                            prove that various classes of graphs are prime; we refer the reader to [15] for a full catalog of
39:                                            results. In particular, it is known that all paths, cycles, helms, fans, flowers, books, and wheels
40:                                            of even order are prime [15, 27, 29].

34:                                         We consider a classical resource allocation setting where a set of goods is to be allocated among
35:                                         interested agents. Our goal is to find an allocation that is fair to all agents. This problem has
36:                                         been addressed in a large body of literature on fair division [Brams and Taylor, 1996a, Moulin,
37:                                         2003], which has found applications ranging from divorce settlement [Brams and Taylor, 1996b]
38:                                         to credit assignment [de Clippel et al., 2008]. The two most prominent fairness notions in the
39:                                         literature are envy-freeness and proportionality. An allocation is said to be envy-free if every

819: 7    Effective GH dichotomies
820: We briefly discuss how to use Theorem 3.1 to make complexity dichotomies for graph homomor-
821: phism effective. A long and fruitful sequence of work [10, 11, 2, 15, 26, 13] led to the following
822: complexity dichotomy for weighted graph homomorphisms [4] which unifies these previous ones:
823: There is a tractability condition P such that for any (algebraic) complex symmetric matrix H, if
824: H satisfies P then hom(·, H) is P-time computable, otherwise there is a P-time reduction from
825: a canonical #P-hard problem to hom(·, H). However, in the long sequence of reductions in [4]
826: there are nonconstructive steps; a prominent example is the first pinning lemma (Lemma 4.1,
827: p. 937). This involves condensing H by collapsing “equivalent” vertices, while introducing vertex
828: weights. Consider all 1-labeled graphs G. We say two vertices u, v ∈ V (H) are “equivalent” if
829: homu (G, H) = homv (G, H) where the notation homu (G, H) denotes the partial sum of hom(G, H)
830: where we restrict to all mappings which map the labeled vertex of G to u, and similarly for
831: homv (G, H). This is just the special case k = 1 in Theorem 3.1 (note that we first apply the
832: twin compression step to H). Previously the P-time reduction was proved existentially. Using
833: Theorem 3.1 (see Remark at the end of Section 5), this step can be made effective.
834:     There is a finer distinction between making the dichotomy effective in the sense discussed
835: here versus the decidability of the dichotomy. The dichotomy criterion in [4] is decidable in P-
836: time (measured in the size of the description of H); however, according to the proof in [4] which
837: involves nonconstructive steps, when the decision algorithm decides hom(·, H) is #P-hard it does
838: not produce a reduction. The results in this paper can constructively produce such a reduction
839: when hom(·, H) is #P-hard.
840:     Previous versions of Theorem 3.1 (e.g., [17, 18]) show that the above equivalence on u, v for
841: suitably restricted classes of H can be decided by testing for graph isomorphism (with pinning).
842: However, to actually obtain the promised P-time reduction one has to search for “witness” graphs
843: G to homu (G, H) 6= homv (G, H). Having no graph isomorphism mapping u to v does not readily
844: yield such a “witness” graph G, although an open ended search is guaranteed to find one. Thus
845: Theorem 3.1 gives a double exponential time (in the size of H) algorithm to find a reduction
846: algorithm (specifically to find a reduction from the problem EVALP to EVAL in [4]), while directly
847: applying previous versions of the theorem gives a computable process with no definite time bound.
848: (But we emphasize that no previous versions of Theorem 3.1 apply to the dichotomy in [4].)
