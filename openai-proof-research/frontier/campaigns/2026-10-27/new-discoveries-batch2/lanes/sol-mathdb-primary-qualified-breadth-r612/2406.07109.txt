Abstract
In Parts I and II of this series of papers, three new methods for the computation of eigenvalues of singular pencils were developed:
rank-completing perturbations, rank-projections, and augmentation.
It was observed that a straightforward structure-preserving adaption for symmetric pencils was not possible and it was left as an open question how to address this challenge.
In this Part III, it is shown how the observed issue can be circumvented by using
Hermitian perturbations. This leads to structure-preserving analogues of the three
techniques from Parts I and II for Hermitian pencils (including real symmetric
pencils) as well as for related structures. It is an important feature of these
methods that the sign characteristic of the given pencil is preserved.
As an application, it is shown that the resulting methods can be used to solve systems of bivariate polynomials.
keywords
Singular symmetric pencil, singular Hermitian pencil, sign characteristic, generalized eigenvalue problem, structure-preserving perturbation, projection, augmentation, symmetric determinantal representations, bivariate polynomial systems.

We consider the real symmetric or complex Hermitian singular n×nn\times n generalized eigenvalue problem

Furthermore, many applications in data analysis and machine learning give rise to eigenvalue problem of the form

Yet another (new) source of symmetric singular pencils is described in Section 6.
It is well known that in many situations the Q​ZQZ algorithm can be used to
compute the eigenvalues of a singular matrix pencil. In addition to values
that are close to the original eigenvalues, the algorithm will also produce additional
values that result from perturbations by rounding errors made to the singular part of
the pencil; see [28] (and also [3] for a detailed
first order perturbation analysis of singular pencils). Then the question arises how
to distinguish the “true” eigenvalues from the “random eigenvalues” among the
values produced by the Q​ZQZ algorithm. To circumvent this problem, Van Dooren suggested to
first extract the regular part of the pencil [27] and this idea was implemented
in Guptri [4, 9] which probably has been the standard
method for solving singular generalized eigenvalue problems for more than four decades.
However Guptri is based on a staircase algorithm that needs rank decisions and may
therefore fail in situations when these decisions become critical.
Recently, alternative methods for the solution of singular eigenvalue problems
have been developed. In [16] it is suggested to directly apply
the QZ algorithm to a singular pencil and separate the true from the fake eigenvalues
with the help of so-called weak condition numbers. In [13] a similar
approach is used that involves a small norm perturbation to the pencil before the
Q​ZQZ algorithm is applied.
The work in this paper follows a different approach and is a sequel to
Part I [11] and Part II [12], which deal with three
methods for nonsymmetric singular pencils: rank-completing perturbations,
rank-projections, and augmentation. All three methods are based on the following
theoretical background developed in [11]:
let the normal rank of the pencil be r=nrank​(A,B)=maxζ∈ℂ⁡rank​(A+ζ​B)r=\text{nrank}(A,B)=\max_{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}}\text{rank}(A+\zeta B), define k=n−rk=n-r and consider a perturbation of the form
A~−λ​B~=(A+τ​U​DA​V∗)−λ⁡(B+τ​U​DB​V∗)\widetilde{A}-\lambda\widetilde{B}=(A+\tau\,UD_{A}V^{*})-\lambda\,(B+\tau\,UD_{B}V^{*})
where τ∈ℂ\tau\in\mathbb{C}, U,V∈ℂn,kU,V\in\mathbb{C}^{n,k} and DA−λ​DBD_{A}-\lambda D_{B} is a regular pencil of size k×kk\times k. Such perturbations are called rank-completing,
because the rank of the perturbation pencil U​DA​V∗−λ​U​DB​V∗UD_{A}V^{*}-\lambda\,UD_{B}V^{*} is just large
enough to make the pencil A~−λ​B~\widetilde{A}-\lambda\widetilde{B} regular. Generically,
this will be the case and, in addition, the spectrum of
A~−λ​B~\widetilde{A}-\lambda\widetilde{B}
consists of three types of eigenvalues: true eigenvalues which are
exactly the (finite and infinite) eigenvalues of the original pencil A−λ​BA-\lambda B,
prescribed eigenvalues which are precisely the eigenvalues of DA−λ​DBD_{A}-\lambda D_{B}, and
random eigenvalues resulting from perturbation of the singular blocks of
the original pencil A−λ​BA-\lambda B. These three types of eigenvalues can be distinguished
with the help of orthogonality relations satisfied by the corresponding eigenvectors.
Computing eigentriplets (λ,x,y)(\lambda,x,y) of the pencil A~−λ​B~\widetilde{A}-\lambda\widetilde{B} (right and left eigenvectors xx and yy are well defined due to the regularity of
the pencil), we find that we have V∗​x=U∗​y=0V^{*}x=U^{*}y=0 for true eigenvalues and
V∗​x,U∗​y≠0V^{*}x,U^{*}y\neq 0 for prescribed eigenvalues, while for random eigenvalues exactly one
of the quantities V∗​xV^{*}x and U∗​yU^{*}y is zero; see, e.g.,
[11, Summary 4.7 and Table 5.1].
For real symmetric or complex Hermitian pencils, it is beneficial to consider
structure-preserving perturbations to preserve the so-called sign characteristic of real eigenvalues. Concerning real symmetric pencils and a
symmetric perturbation of the form (A+τ​U​DA​UT)−λ⁡(B+τ​U​DB​UT)(A+\tau\,UD_{A}U^{T})-\lambda\,(B+\tau\,UD_{B}U^{T}) with U∈ℝn,kU\in\mathbb{R}^{n,k}, the following challenge was observed in Part I [11, Rem. 4.8]:
“If AA and BB are symmetric, then it seems that for our current approach
we have to use nonsymmetric rank completing perturbations. Namely, when a
symmetric perturbation […] is used, there is an issue with
the third group […] as random eigenvalues appear either as double real eigenvalues or in complex conjugate pairs, and in the former case the orthogonality constraints cannot be satisfied. We leave the study of structured singular pencils for future research.”
Indeed, the problem is that for symmetric pencils and a symmetric update, it is no longer possible that exactly one of UT​xU^{T}x and UT​yU^{T}y is zero, since left eigenvectors for real eigenvalues of symmetric pencils
are also right eigenvectors for the same eigenvalue.
Fortunately, as we show in this paper, it is possible to consider
Hermitian rank-completing perturbations
(A+τ​U​DA​U∗)−λ⁡(B+τ​U​DB​U∗)(A+\tau\,UD_{A}U^{*})-\lambda\,(B+\tau\,UD_{B}U^{*}), where τ∈ℝ\tau\in\mathbb{R}, U∈ℂn,kU\in\mathbb{C}^{n,k} and where DA−λ​DBD_{A}-\lambda D_{B} is a regular Hermitian k×kk\times k pencil.
The results on generic rank-completing perturbations from [11]
cannot be applied in this paper, because here we consider structure-preserving
perturbations with the property V=UV=U. As a consequence, the meaning of genericity
differs from the one used in [11] and hence we have to develop new theoretical results.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section 2 we recall some necessary facts.
Section 3 proposes a rank-completing Hermitian perturbation as a structure-preserving version of the method of Part I [11].
Section 4 discusses an alternative technique, a projection method, as a structure-preserving generalization of one of the two approaches of Part II [12]. In Section 5 we discuss for which pencils with other
symmetry structures the results of this paper can be applied as well.
We illustrate the theoretical results with numerical experiments in Section 6,
where we also describe an application to bivariate polynomial systems. Finally,
we summarize some conclusions in Section 7.
2 Preliminaries
First, we define the notion of genericity that is used in this paper.
A subset of ℝm\mathbb{R}^{m} is an algebraic set if it is the
set of common zeros of finitely many real polynomials in mm variables. A set
Ω⊆ℝm\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m} is called generic if its complement is contained
in an algebraic set which is not the full space ℝm\mathbb{R}^{m}. (The latter condition
is satisfied if none of the underlying polynomials in mm variables is the zero
polynomial.) We call a set Ω⊆ℂn,k\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n,k} generic if it
can be identified with a generic subset of ℝ2​n​k\mathbb{R}^{2nk} in the standard way.
Thus, genericity of a set of complex matrices is interpreted as genericity with
respect to the real and imaginary parts of the entries. This point of view is
important when complex conjugation is involved, because
complex conjugation is not a polynomial map over ℂ\mathbb{C}, but it is a polynomial
map over ℝ\mathbb{R} when ℂ\mathbb{C} is canonically identified with ℝ2\mathbb{R}^{2}
via real and imaginary parts of complex numbers. Clearly, the set of all matrices
of full rank is a generic subset of ℂn,k\mathbb{C}^{n,k}. Since the intersection of
finitely many generic sets is again generic, we may always assume that a generic
set Ω⊆ℂn,k\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n,k} contains only matrices of full rank.

positive semidefinite.

Summary 9.
We summarize the results from Theorem 4 and Theorem 7: let

Select a random U∈ℂn,kU\in\mathbb{C}^{n,k}. Then generically UU has full rank and satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem 4.
The nn eigenvalues of the perturbed pencil

can then be grouped into following three classes of eigenvalues:
1.
True eigenvalues: There are r:=n−2​M−kr:=n-2M-k eigenvalues that are precisely the eigenvalues
of the original singular pencil A−λ​BA-\lambda B. If μ\mu is such an eigenvalue with
corresponding right and left eigenvectors xx and yy then U∗​x=0U^{*}x=0 and U∗​y=0U^{*}y=0.
2.

of DA−λ​DBD_{A}-\lambda D_{B}. If μ\mu is such an eigenvalue with corresponding right and left eigenvectors xx and yy then
U∗​x≠0U^{*}x\neq 0 and U∗​y≠0U^{*}y\neq 0.
3.

such an eigenvalue with the corresponding right and left xx and yy,
then we either have U∗​x=0U^{*}x=0 and U∗​y≠0U^{*}y\neq 0, or U∗​x≠0U^{*}x\neq 0 and U∗​y=0U^{*}y=0. In that case, also μ¯\overline{\mu} is an

Remark 10.
We highlight that the fact that all random eigenvalues of the perturbed Hermitian pencil
are nonreal is highly nontrivial. Indeed, it is shown in [2] that the
generic eigenstructure of a regular Hermitian pencil may consist of real and nonreal
eigenvalues. Numerical experiments suggest that for random regular Hermitian pencils
the probability of having at least one real eigenvalue tends to 11 when the size tends to
infinity (for odd size this is obvious, but it also seems to be the case for even size).
Remark 11.
Instead of random matrices U∈ℂn,kU\in\mathbb{C}^{n,k}, we will use matrices with
orthonormal columns in practice. At first glance one may think that this leads to a
different notion of genericity such that the results from Theorem 4 and
Theorem 7 can no longer be applied, but in fact this is not an
issue. The key observation that generically a random matrix U∈ℂn,kU\in\mathbb{C}^{n,k}
will be of full rank kk. Then computing a thin QR decomposition U=Q​RU=QR, we find that
U⁡(DA−λ​DB)​U∗=Q​R​(DA−λ​DB)​R∗​Q∗,U\,(D_{A}-\lambda D_{B})\,U^{*}=QR\,(D_{A}-\lambda D_{B})\,R^{*}Q^{*},
so the perturbation with UU and the prescribed pencil DA−λ​DBD_{A}-\lambda D_{B} is exactly
the same as the perturbation with QQ and the prescribed pencil R⁡(DA−λ​DB)​R∗R(D_{A}-\lambda D_{B})R^{*} which is congruent to DA−λ​DBD_{A}-\lambda D_{B} and hence has the same
prescribed eigenvalues. (This information on the spectrum was the only information on
DA−λ​DBD_{A}-\lambda D_{B} that was used in the construction of the generic sets in the proofs
of Theorem 4 and Theorem 7.) Consequently, it is enough
to know that a matrix U∈ℂn,kU\in\mathbb{C}^{n,k} with orthonormal columns is the
isometric factor of a QR decomposition of a matrix in the generic sets from
Theorem 4 and Theorem 7 to be able to apply both results.
Such a matrix can be obtained via the QR decomposition of a random
complex n×kn\times k matrix.
Remark 12.
If one of the matrices AA and BB is positive semidefinite and hence all eigenvalues
of the pencil are real and semisimple, then this property can be preserved by choosing
DAD_{A} or DBD_{B} positive definite, respectively, and τ>0\tau>0. (Similarly, the case
that a linear combination of AA and BB is positive semidefinite can be treated.)
Consequently,
the perturbed regular pencil A~−λ​B~\widetilde{A}-\lambda\widetilde{B} will only have real
semisimple eigenvalues. Observe that this does not contradict Theorem 7
or Summary 9, because in this case all minimal indices of the original
pencil A−λ​BA-\lambda B are zero and hence the perturbed pencil only has true and
prescribed, but no random eigenvalues.
Remark 13.
It follows from (9) that the regular part of the original pencil
A−λ​BA-\lambda B is contained in the perturbed pencil A~−λ​B~\widetilde{A}-\lambda\widetilde{B}.
As a consequence, the true eigenvalues of the perturbed pencil do not
only have the same multiplicities as the eigenvalues of the original pencil, but
also the sign characteristic is preserved. This observation can be used to calculate
the sign characteristic of true eigenvalues even in the case that the eigenvalues
and eigenvectors of the perturbed Hermitian pencil are computed by a method that does
not make use of the Hermitian structure of the problem. To this end, it only matters
that the perturbed regular Hermitian pencil was obtained using a structure-preserving
procedure.
For example, assume that λ0∈ℝ\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R} is a semisimple real eigenvalue
and that the eigenvectors x1,…,xgx_{1},\dots,x_{g} form a basis for the corresponding
deflating subspace. Then setting X:=[x1…xg]X:=[\,x_{1}\ \ \dots\ \ x_{g}\,], it remains to compute the inertia of the Hermitian
matrix X∗​B~​XX^{*}\widetilde{B}X, i.e., the numbers π\pi and ν\nu of positive and negative
eigenvalues, respectively. It then follows immediately by inspection from the
Thompson canonical form, that the sign characteristic of the eigenvalue λ0\lambda_{0}
contains π\pi times the sign +1+1 and ν\nu times the sign −1-1. Similarly, the
sign characteristic of the eigenvalue ∞\infty can be calculated using the matrix
A~\widetilde{A} instead of B~\widetilde{B}.
If λ0\lambda_{0} is not semisimple, then the calculation of the sign characteristic
is more complicated as principal vectors of higher order from Jordan chains are
needed, but again the information on the sign characteristic is preserved in the
transition from the singular Hermitian pencil A−λ​BA-\lambda B to the perturbed
regular Hermitian pencil A~−λ​B~\widetilde{A}-\lambda\widetilde{B}.

5 Other symmetry structures
Although the main results of the previous two section were formulated for Hermitian
pencils only, they can be directly applied to many other symmetry structures that
are related to the Hermitian structure. We first recall the following definition,
see, e.g., [17].
Definition 19.
Let A,B∈ℂn,nA,B\in\mathbb{C}^{n,n}. Then the pencil A−λ​BA-\lambda B is called
•
∗*-even if A=A∗A=A^{*} and B=−B∗B=-B^{*},
•
∗*-odd if A=−A∗A=-A^{*} and B=B∗B=B^{*},
•
skew-Hermitian if A=−A∗A=-A^{*} and B=−B∗B=-B^{*},
•
∗*-palindromic if B=A∗B=A^{*}, and
•
∗*-anti-palindromic if B=−A∗B=-A^{*}.
Since all these symmetry structures can be easily reduced to the Hermitian
structure, there are straightforward generalizations of the main results from the
previous two sections for these symmetry structures. We refrain from stating them
explicitly for each individual symmetry structure, but formulate the following
meta-theorem instead.
Meta-Theorem 20.
Let A−λ​BA-\lambda B be a singular n×nn\times n complex matrix pencil of normal
rank n−kn-k carrying one of
the symmetry structures as in Definition 19. Then the results
from Theorem 4, Theorem 7 and
Theorem 16 hold for A−λ​BA-\lambda B, where for the first
two theorems the regular k×kk\times k pencil DA−λ​DBD_{A}-\lambda D_{B} is chosen to
carry the same symmetry structure as the original pencil A−λ​BA-\lambda B.
Proof.
If A−λ​BA-\lambda B is even, odd, or skew-Hermitian, the result immediately
follows from the observation that multiplying one (or both) of the coefficient
matrices by the imaginary unit ii turns the pencil into a Hermitian pencil.
In the case of an (anti-)palindromic pencil A−λ​A∗A-\lambda A^{*} we can apply
a Cayley transformation; see, e.g., [14]. These transformations map
(anti-)palindromic pencils to even or odd pencils and vice versa; see, e.g.,
[17]. Since Cayley transformations are special Möbius transformations, it follows from the results in [18] that the
eigenvalues change in a predictable bijective way while the eigenvectors
remain invariant. Also the change in the sign characteristic under a Möbius
transformation is well understood [22]. Hence the result
follows by applying the Cayley transformation and a multiplication of one
of the coefficient matrices by ii to both the original pencil and the
perturbation pencil to turn them into Hermitian pencils.
∎
The situation changes drastically if the considered pencils are real symmetric
and real symmetric rank-completing perturbations or real projections
are considered. Indeed, inspecting the proof of Lemma 6, it was explicitly used that the
considered perturbation matrices are allowed to be complex to
show that the values α1,…,αM\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{M} are different from each of their complex conjugates. Numerical examples show that this is no longer the case if real
symmetric pencils and real
symmetric rank-completing perturbations are considered. In fact, experiments
suggest that {α1,…,αm}={α¯1,…,α¯M}\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}\}=\{\overline{\alpha}_{1},\dots,\overline{\alpha}_{M}\}.
These values turn out to be double random eigenvalues of the
perturbed Hermitian pencil. As a small illustrating example, consider

7 Conclusions
Extending results from Parts I and II [11, 12] for unstructured singular pencils, we have proposed three structure-preserving methods to compute eigenvalues of singular real symmetric or complex Hermitian pencils.
This means that the perturbed pencils A~−λ​B~\widetilde{A}-\lambda\widetilde{B} remain Hermitian.
If the original BB is positive semidefinite, then B~\widetilde{B} can be chosen positive definite (see Remark 12).
This can be exploited by eigensolvers, so that, for instance, the computed eigenvalues are real without any side-effects of finite precision arithmetic.
Just as in the general nonsymmetric situation [11, 12], there are three options: a rank-completing perturbation of size kk, projection onto the normal rank of size r=n−kr=n-k, or augmentation by dimension kk. In this paper we have concentrated on the perturbation approach and its analysis in Section 3, and on the projection approach in Section 4.
We have shown that a Hermitian perturbation then leads to generically simple random eigenvalues.
Augmentation with dimension kk, which extends [12] for the nonsymmetric case, may be useful for large sparse problems, since this technique may (partly) preserve sparsity.
Generically this will lead to a regular symmetric (or Hermitian) pencil of size n+kn+k; see Remark 18.
For situations in which we might possibly overestimate or underestimate the normal rank, we refer to the discussion in Part II [12, Sec. 8].
For pseudocodes of the methods, we refer to Parts I and II, where the main difference is that we now use a complex U=VU=V.
We highlight again that the structure-preserving methods presented in this paper may also be
useful in the case that they are post-processed by standard eigensolvers that
ignore the special symmetry structure of the problem. Indeed, it has been discussed in
Remark 13 that the sign characteristic of the pencil can be obtained from
the perturbed Hermitian pencils once its eigenvectors have been computed.
The case for real symmetric pencils with real symmetric perturbations (see Conjecture 21) turns out
to be challenging and is left for future research.
