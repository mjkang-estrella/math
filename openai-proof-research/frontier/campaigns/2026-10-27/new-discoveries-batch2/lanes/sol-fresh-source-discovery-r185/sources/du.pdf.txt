                                                Abstract
          We present a candidate proof that the integer-exponent trace of the fractional Gaus-
      sian binomial coefficient is coefficientwise largest at r = 1/2, for every positive rational
      parameter r and every positive integer lower index k. An exact trace expansion reduces the
      comparison to r = 1/(2m). Same-index pairing leaves explicit deficits, which are bounded
      by adjacent even-index summands. These pairs are disjoint for m ≥ 3; a weighted identity
      handles their overlap for m = 2. A counting estimate covers the upper index boundary,
      and denominator comparisons reduce the remaining range to three rational kernels with
      denominator (q; q)4 . The classical restricted-partition formula gives a cubic approximation
      with an explicit linear error bound, yielding uniform coefficient bounds for all three kernels.
      The proof uses no finite parameter cutoff or symbolic case enumeration as a premise.

Preprint; not externally peer reviewed. AI use and verification are described in the appendix.


Introduction and proof strategy
Han and Xiong [1] conjectured that, among the integer-exponent traces of fractional Gaussian
binomial coefficients with positive rational parameter, the trace at r = 1/2 is coefficientwise
maximal. The proposed theorem below proves this comparison for every positive rational r
and every positive lower index k. The numerator convention is ki=1 (1 − q i+r ); it is important
                                                                Q

that the product starts at i = 1. Section 1 defines the trace and states the theorem precisely.
The existing reductions make the unresolved part quite specific. Ono [2] reduced the problem
to the canonical parameters r = 1/(2m), proved structural comparison results, and verified the
conjecture for all positive rational parameters when k ≤ 200 by exact computation. Andrews
and El Bachraoui [3] proved adjacent-pair estimates for m ≥ 2 and k ≤ 6m + 2, as well as
(m, k) = (2, 15); their result gives a non-computational proof for all parameters when k ≤ 15.
We use neither of these finite ranges as a premise. The reduction in [2] and the adjacent-pair
strategy in [3] are prior work. The scope of [3] stated here is taken from its published abstract;
its full text was unavailable for the present comparison.
Proof strategy. After the canonical reduction, we pair every summand of T1/(2m),k with the
summand of T1/2,k having the same index. What remains is a sum of unused positive terms
minus explicit deficits. We compensate a deficit at index 2mℓ using the two nearest unused
even indices, 2mℓ − 2 and 2mℓ + 2. For m ≥ 3 these pairs are disjoint. For m = 2 neighboring
pairs overlap, so a weighted identity accounts for every positive term before the local estimates
are summed.
The local estimates have two parts. Close to the upper index boundary, the right neighbor can
be absent; a counting argument bounds the deficit using the left neighbor alone. Farther from
that boundary, denominator comparisons reduce the local inequalities to three rational series
with denominator D4 = (q; q)4 . The classical exact formula for [q n ]D4−1 , expressed as a cubic
polynomial with an explicit linear error bound, then proves each kernel inequality uniformly.

or at the same degree. The remaining summands of Tu/b,k have negative signs. Subtracting

henceforth take m ≥ 2.

2.2    Disjoint neighbors for m ≥ 3

                           A2mℓ−2 + A2mℓ+2 ⪰ Cm,ℓ         (1 ≤ ℓ ≤ J).                        (2.9)

8    Completion of the theorem
For m ≥ 3, Section 3 proves the local comparison when h ≤ 2a + 1; Section 4 and Lemma 5.1
prove it when h ≥ 2a + 2. These ranges are consecutive. The nonoverlapping allocation in
Section 2 then yields the canonical comparison for every m ≥ 3 and every k. When k < 2m
there is no missing primitive term, and the same global identity gives the comparison directly.
For m = 2 and j ≥ 2, Section 3 covers h ≤ 6j - 1, while Section 4 and Lemma 6.1 cover h ≥
4j + 2. There is no omitted integer between these ranges, since 4j + 2 ≤ 6j. For j = 1, the
counting estimate covers h ≤ 5 and Lemma 7.1 covers h ≥ 6. The weighted summation identity
in Section 2 therefore proves the comparison for all k ≥ 4. For k = 1,2,3 there are no negative
gap terms, so the result is immediate.
The case m = 1 is equality. Thus every canonical parameter 1/(2m) is covered. The parity
reduction in Section 1 implies the full statement:
