Abstract
    

Ramsey-good graphs are graphs that contain neither a clique of size ss nor an independent set of size tt. We study doubly saturated Ramsey-good graphs, defined as Ramsey-good graphs in which the addition or removal of any edge necessarily creates an ss-clique or a tt-independent set. We present a method combining SAT solving with bespoke LLM-generated code to discover infinite families of such graphs, answering a question of Grinstead and Roberts from 1982. In addition, we use LLMs to generate and formalize correctness proofs in Lean. This case study highlights the potential of integrating automated reasoning, large language models, and formal verification to accelerate mathematical discovery. We argue that such tool-driven workflows will play an increasingly central role in experimental mathematics.
  



Keywords: Ramsey theory experimental mathematics artificial intelligence




1 Introduction




In 2000, the mathematician Timothy Gowers presciently predicted that “during the next century computers will become sufficiently good at proving theorems that the practice of pure mathematical research will be completely revolutionized” [22]. He looked forward to a “golden age” of mathematics, where computers can be helpful research assistants while still leaving room for humans to contribute deep insights. We believe that we are now entering this golden age, thanks to the combination of several technologies:


• 



Mature and efficient symbolic reasoning: SAT solvers and computer algebra systems have evolved tremendously, and are currently able to reliably handle large computations.


• 



LLMs capable of producing mathematical arguments: Frontier models have demonstrated remarkable mathematical capabilities [48], ranging from competition problems [29] to research-level questions and open problems [7, 46, 18].


• 



Autoformalization:
Relying on proof assistants like Lean [15], with large mathematical libraries [35],
LLMs can now formalize proofs for mathematical arguments of increasing complexity [44, 45].



In this paper, we give a glimpse of what this golden age looks like, and how these different technologies coalesce for accelerating mathematical research. Specifically, we show how our investigations into a natural problem in Ramsey theory were drastically assisted by the above technologies. Indeed, our theorems would have been very difficult to discover without computer assistance, and essentially all of the tedious work that inevitably arises in the course of research was fully automated.




1.1 Problem and results




A graph is R⁡(s,t)R(s,t)-good if it contains neither a clique of size ss nor an independent set of size tt. Ramsey’s celebrated theorem states that for each ss and tt, there are only finitely many R⁡(s,t)R(s,t)-good graphs [39]. This theorem has been revisited and generalized many times and gave birth to a vast area of combinatorics called Ramsey theory [23]. In this paper, we revisit the topic once again. Let us say that a graph GG is doubly saturated R⁡(s,t)R(s,t)-good if it is R⁡(s,t)R(s,t)-good, but adding or removing any edge from GG yields a graph that is not R⁡(s,t)R(s,t)-good, and neither GG nor G¯\overline{G} is the complete graph.11
              1
              
              
              
              
              
              
              
            The final condition ensures that it is possible to add and remove edges, which rules out, e.g., considering a graph on 11 vertex as doubly saturated for all ss and tt. In other words, doubly saturated R⁡(s,t)R(s,t)-good graphs are R⁡(s,t)R(s,t)-good graphs that are edge-maximal and edge-minimal, excluding complete and empty graphs. What makes these graphs interesting is that they “just barely” avoid ss-cliques and tt-independent sets, so their construction involves a delicate balancing act. More broadly, our goal in understanding doubly saturated R⁡(s,t)R(s,t)-good graphs is to understand better the space of R⁡(s,t)R(s,t)-good graphs; as Angeltveit and McKay stated [3]: “The improvements in the upper bound for R⁡(5,5)R(5,5) are intimately connected to our
understanding of [the set of R⁡(4,5)R(4,5)-good graphs on nn vertices] for nn large”. One way of understanding the space of R⁡(s,t)R(s,t)-good graphs is to consider the partial ordering in which G1≺G2G_{1}\prec G_{2} if G2G_{2} can be obtained from G1G_{1} by adding edges. As illustrated in Figure 1, graphs can then be partitioned into weakly connected components of this ordering; doubly saturated graphs are precisely those that make up their entire connected component.

However, obtaining experimental data for larger choices of ss and tt requires efficient combinatorial optimization tooling. We use SAT solvers for this purpose, which allows us to prove for instance that the smallest doubly saturated R⁡(3,7)R(3,7)-good graph has 2020 vertices, and the smallest doubly saturated R⁡(4,5)R(4,5)-good graph has 1919 vertices. Conjecture 1 asks about the existence of doubly saturated R⁡(s,t)R(s,t)-good graphs, but we can also ask more fine-grained questions about the set of doubly saturated R⁡(s,t)R(s,t)-good graphs. One question that strikes us as particularly natural is how small a doubly saturated R⁡(s,t)R(s,t)-good graph can be. In Section 5, we present an LLM-discovered proof that every doubly saturated R⁡(s,t)R(s,t)-good graph has at least 2​s+2​t−72s+2t-7 vertices.

In this paper, we describe not only our mathematical results but also the methodology by which we discovered them, since we believe that workflows like ours will rapidly become the norm in experimental mathematics and will be a boon to the field. Our computer-assisted research paradigm is illustrated in Figure 2. Of course, these steps (except perhaps formalization) are part of the research process of almost every math problem, although it is only thanks to recent advances in AI and formal methods that multiple stages of this pipeline can be automated. Specifically, for step 2 of the process, we used SAT solvers to look for doubly saturated Ramsey-good graphs; in some cases, SAT solvers are not well suited to the problem, in which case we used bespoke LLM-generated code as a substitute. For about a decade now, it has been recognized that SAT solvers have tremendous potential for solving small cases of hard combinatorial problems [32, 26, 27, 6, 42]. However, a common criticism of such proofs is that they do not scale to proving general results, partly because “mere computations” cannot provide the conceptual understanding that would be required for a general proof (see the discussion in [4]). But this criticism is unwarranted in general: SAT solving (and computational experiments more generally) should be seen as an initial stage of the discovery process, not the end goal. This motivates step 3, where we studied the doubly saturated graphs found computationally, searched for patterns, and conjectured a general construction. We conjectured Theorem 1.1 in the course of a dialogue with ChatGPT. This dialogue felt like a productive collaboration with a research partner, which was expedited by the fact that ChatGPT can autonomously run computational experiments to quickly assess hypotheses and analyze data. The role of the human in this dialogue was to guide ChatGPT toward promising strategies.




For step 4, we used LLMs to generate a proof of Theorem 1.1 with essentially no human guidance. It is well known that LLMs have achieved remarkable performance on benchmarks and math competitions, but they are also starting to become useful for proving theorems arising in the course of actual research. Unfortunately, LLMs are notorious for producing proofs with fiendishly subtle errors. Thus, formalizing proofs in a proof assistant like Lean has become more important than ever. The primary barrier to the mainstream adoption of proof assistants has historically been their steep learning curve, together with the laborious nature of formalizing nontrivial mathematics. But here too, advances in AI and formal methods have changed the game. In our case, using an informal proof generated by Gemini, Harmonic’s autoformalization system called Aristotle [1] was able to autonomously write over 1000 lines of Lean code to formally verify Theorem 1.1.





1.3 Related work



Mathematical related work.




Saturation problems in graph theory have been studied since Erdős, Hajnal, and Moon’s classic paper [17], which studied graphs for which the addition of any edge creates a kk-clique. Specifically, they determined the minimum number of edges in an nn-vertex graph avoiding kk-cliques such that the addition of any edge creates a kk-clique. These graphs were further studied by Hajnal [25], and we use one of Hajnal’s theorems in Section 5. Similar saturation problems have been studied with respect to subgraphs other than cliques [31, 13] and with respect to induced subgraphs [34]. Damásdi et al. [14] studied Ramsey-good graphs that are vertex-saturated, meaning that adding a vertex and connecting it to any subset of the existing vertices always yields a graph that is not Ramsey-good. The same paper also proved saturation results for other problems in Ramsey theory. More broadly, our work falls under the umbrella of studying objects that avoid some pattern but no longer do so after a small perturbation. The first author studied a problem in this vein in combinatorics on words, constructing words avoiding certain types of repetitions such that changing any letter creates such a repetition [38].




AI related work.




As mentioned above, SAT solvers have become an essential tool for computer-assisted mathematics, particularly for combinatorial problems [32, 26, 27, 6, 42]. Most applications of SAT to mathematics focus on solving particular cases of open problems (step 2 from Figure 2), but some work has gone further and used data generated from SAT experiments to discover general theorems. For example, Subercaseaux et al. [43] applied SAT to pentagon minimization in planar point sets and discovered a general construction, whose correctness they were able to prove by hand. Aside from SAT, using LLMs to assist with mathematical research has recently become a hot topic [8, 46, 19]. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are no case studies on the interplay between SAT and LLMs in spurring mathematical discovery, which is a significant gap given the recognized potential of neuro-symbolic AI [28]. The closest work to ours is by Xia et al. [47], which also uses a neuro-symbolic pipeline for a combinatorial design problem, integrating LLMs and Lean autoformalization. Their work does not include SAT solvers, instead using a custom Rust solver as well as computer algebra systems.






1.4 Preliminaries




All graphs in this paper are simple and finite. The complete graph on nn vertices is denoted KnK_{n}, and the empty graph on nn vertices is denoted InI_{n}. Given a graph GG, its clique number, denoted ω⁡(G)\omega(G), is the largest nn such that GG contains KnK_{n} as a subgraph; its independence number, denoted α⁡(G)\alpha(G), is the largest nn such that GG contains InI_{n} as an induced subgraph. The minimum (respectively, maximum) degree of vertices in GG is denoted δ⁡(G)\delta(G) (respectively, Δ⁡(G)\Delta(G)).

3 Doubly saturated R⁡(4,t)R(4,t)-good graphs




Since there is a complete enumeration of R⁡(4,4)R(4,4)-good graphs, it is a simple computation to check that there are six doubly saturated R⁡(4,4)R(4,4)-good graphs: one on 13 vertices, two on 15 vertices, two on 16 vertices, and one on 17 vertices. The doubly saturated R⁡(4,4)R(4,4)-good graph on 13 vertices is the Paley graph of order 13, which is the circulant graph with distances {1,3,4}\{1,3,4\}.




Following these observations, we turned to looking for doubly saturated R⁡(4,5)R(4,5)-good graphs. There are far too many R⁡(4,5)R(4,5)-good graphs for a complete enumeration (the estimate of Angeltveit and McKay is about 2.93⋅10192.93\cdot 10^{19} [3, Appendix A]), so we applied SAT to the problem using the encoding from Section 2. We found that there are no doubly saturated R⁡(4,5)R(4,5)-good graphs on 18 or fewer vertices. On the other hand, for 19 vertices, a SAT solver (CaDiCaL [5]) does not terminate after a day on a single core. Thus, we made an ansatz that a solution would be circulant, as suggested by some of the R⁡(4,4)R(4,4) results. We found that there is a circulant doubly saturated R⁡(4,5)R(4,5)-good graph on 19 vertices with distances {4,5,6,8}\{4,5,6,8\}. Later, we applied Mallob [40] to our SAT encoding, and it found a solution isomorphic to this circulant graph, which suggests that it may be the unique doubly saturated R⁡(4,5)R(4,5)-good graph on 19 vertices.




At this point, we asked ChatGPT-5.1 Pro to look for circulant doubly saturated R⁡(4,t)R(4,t)-good graphs for t∈[6,12]t\in[6,12]. For large values of tt, the SAT encoding becomes unwieldy because there are too many clauses required to enforce the absence of tt-independent sets, but bespoke code generated by the LLM succeeded in finding circulant constructions for each t∈[6,12]t\in[6,12].




Having found nine constructions, we asked ChatGPT to extrapolate from these to find a general construction that works for all t≥4t\geq 4. ChatGPT quickly recognized that every construction so far had n:=6​t−11n:=6t-11 vertices, so it remained to find the patterns in the distance sets. ChatGPT tried several approaches, which varied in how promising they seemed to us. For example, analyzing the spectral properties of the graphs did not seem to us to be as fruitful as studying the additive-combinatorial properties of the distance sets. Accordingly, we encouraged ChatGPT to look deeper into the more promising strategies. Besides this minimal shepherding, the discovery process was almost entirely LLM-driven. ChatGPT realized that when specifying a circulant graph, there are many distance sets that result in isomorphic graphs. Motivated by this observation, ChatGPT tried rewriting each of the nine distance sets into a canonical form from which patterns would become clearer. After a few iterations of this, it discovered the following construction for the distance sets, which agreed with all nine of our constructions up to isomorphism: {1}∪{m∈[⌊n/2⌋]∣m≡3,4(mod6)}\{1\}\cup\{m\in[\lfloor n/2\rfloor]\mid m\equiv 3,4\pmod{6}\}. At the time, ChatGPT was unable to prove that the construction works.




With the release of ChatGPT-5.4 Pro, we revisited the problem. This time, ChatGPT not only managed to produce a proof but also showed that the conjectured construction has a simpler isomorphic description. Specifically, letting m=t−2m=t-2 and n=6​t−11n=6t-11, the above construction is isomorphic to the circulant graph on nn vertices with distances {m}∪[2​m+1,3​m]\{m\}\cup[2m+1,3m]. See Figure 4 for a depiction of the construction for t=4t=4 and t=5t=5.

Moreover, autoformalization avoids wasting mathematicians’ time on checking flawed LLM-generated proofs, and can be further used to refine proof attempts on a loop based on errors that the formalization process reveals.

4.1 Doubly saturated R⁡(3,t)R(3,t)-good graphs




We have some experimental results regarding doubly saturated R⁡(3,t)R(3,t)-good graphs, including a conjectural infinite family. First, we present experimental observations for small values of tt:


• 



There is a unique doubly saturated R⁡(3,3)R(3,3)-good graph, namely C5C_{5}.


• 



There are no doubly saturated R⁡(3,4)R(3,4)-good graphs.


• 



There is a unique doubly saturated R⁡(3,5)R(3,5)-good graph, namely the circulant graph on 13 vertices with distances {1,5}\{1,5\}.


• 



There are no doubly saturated R⁡(3,6)R(3,6)-good graphs.


• 



Using our SAT encoding, we found that there is a doubly saturated R⁡(3,7)R(3,7)-good graph on 20 vertices, and there are none with fewer than 20 vertices. We found 18 satisfying assignments that were all isomorphic, which suggests that the solution may be unique. Interestingly, the graph is 5-regular and vertex-transitive, although it is not circulant. We asked ChatGPT whether it was possible to decompose the graph into various patterned substructures, and with its help, we eventually discovered that it can be partitioned into four disjoint 5-cycles with a matching between each pair of cycles (see Figure 5). This pattern might form the basis of a general construction.


• 



Using our SAT encoding, we have found two non-isomorphic doubly saturated R⁡(3,8)R(3,8)-good graphs on 25 vertices. There may be more than two such graphs, and we do not know if they are minimal, although we do know that there are none on 21 or fewer vertices.


• 



There is a unique R⁡(3,9)R(3,9)-good graph on 35 vertices [21], and it is therefore doubly saturated. It is also a circulant graph.


• 



We have found over 100 doubly saturated R⁡(3,10)R(3,10)-good graphs on 39 vertices by searching through the known R⁡(3,10)R(3,10)-good graphs on 39 vertices.

5 A lower bound for the double saturation number




We define the double saturation number of ss and tt as follows:




𝖣𝖲⁡(s,t):=inf{n∣there is a doubly saturated R⁡(s,t)-good graph on n vertices}.\mathsf{DS}(s,t):=\inf\{n\mid\text{there is a doubly saturated $R(s,t)$-good graph on $n$ vertices}\}.



Question 1 asks whether 𝖣𝖲⁡(4,t)=6​t−11\mathsf{DS}(4,t)=6t-11 for all t≥4t\geq 4. More generally, it is natural to ask for bounds on 𝖣𝖲⁡(s,t)\mathsf{DS}(s,t). We asked Gemini 3.1 Deep Think to prove a lower bound on 𝖣𝖲⁡(s,t)\mathsf{DS}(s,t), and it proved that 𝖣𝖲⁡(s,t)≥2​s+2​t−7\mathsf{DS}(s,t)\geq 2s+2t-7 with an elegant argument. To present the argument, we need a theorem due to Hajnal [25]. We say that a graph GG is kk-saturated if it does not contain a Kk+1K_{k+1} subgraph, but adding any edge to GG yields a graph containing a Kk+1K_{k+1}. Hajnal proved the following:



Theorem 5.1 (​​​​  ​​[25, Theorem 1])



If GG is kk-saturated, then either GG has a vertex adjacent to every other vertex or δ​(G)≥2​(k−1)\delta(G)\geq 2(k-1).





Now, we can prove our lower bound on 𝖣𝖲⁡(s,t)\mathsf{DS}(s,t):



Theorem 5.2



For all s,t≥3s,t\geq 3, we have 𝖣𝖲⁡(s,t)≥2​s+2​t−7\mathsf{DS}(s,t)\geq 2s+2t-7.




Proof



Suppose that GG is a doubly saturated R⁡(s,t)R(s,t)-good graph. First, we claim that GG does not have a vertex adjacent to every other vertex. Indeed, suppose for the sake of contradiction that v∈V⁡(G)v\in V(G) is adjacent to every vertex in V⁡(G)∖{v}V(G)\setminus\{v\}. Let w∈V⁡(G)∖{v}w\in V(G)\setminus\{v\} be an arbitrary vertex, and let G′G^{\prime} be the graph obtained from GG by removing the edge {v,w}\{v,w\}. Then, G′G^{\prime} contains an ItI_{t} involving vv. Since vv is adjacent to every other vertex except ww in G′G^{\prime}, the largest independent set involving vv is {v,w}\{v,w\}, which contradicts our assumption that t≥3t\geq 3. Thus, GG does not have a vertex adjacent to every other vertex. By symmetry, the same property holds for G¯\overline{G}.




Now, by Theorem 5.1, we have δ​(G)≥2​(s−2)\delta(G)\geq 2(s-2) and δ​(G¯)≥2​(t−2)\delta(\overline{G})\geq 2(t-2). Thus, Δ⁡(G)=n−1−δ⁡(G¯)≤n−1−2​(t−2)=n−2​t+3\Delta(G)=n-1-\delta(\overline{G})\leq n-1-2(t-2)=n-2t+3. Since δ⁡(G)≤Δ⁡(G)\delta(G)\leq\Delta(G), we have 2​(s−2)≤n−2​t+32(s-2)\leq n-2t+3, so n≥2​s+2​t−7n\geq 2s+2t-7.






6 Conclusion




We used SAT solvers and LLMs to study doubly saturated Ramsey-good graphs. Our work suggests several further directions related to these graphs:


• 



Conjecture 1 is the main open problem raised by our work. Finding new infinite families of doubly saturated Ramsey-good graphs would constitute partial progress toward proving this conjecture and has the potential to uncover intriguing families of graphs. Indeed, there is reason to expect such graphs to have nice combinatorial or algebraic properties, as illustrated by the infinite families from Theorem 1.1 and Conjecture 2, the graph depicted in Figure 5, and the Paley graphs discussed in Section 4.2.


• 



Another further direction is to better understand the behavior 𝖣𝖲⁡(s,t)\mathsf{DS}(s,t). In Question 1, we asked whether 𝖣𝖲⁡(4,t)=6​t−11\mathsf{DS}(4,t)=6t-11. We are also interested in whether, for each fixed s≥3s\geq 3, the growth of 𝖣𝖲⁡(s,t)\mathsf{DS}(s,t) is linear; that is, is 𝖣𝖲​(s,t)=Os​(t)\mathsf{DS}(s,t)=O_{s}(t)? Theorem 1.1 and Conjecture 2 provide some limited evidence in favor of this hypothesis. It would also be interesting to understand the asymptotics of 𝖣𝖲⁡(s,s)\mathsf{DS}(s,s). As a concrete question, is 𝖣𝖲⁡(s,s)=sO⁡(1)\mathsf{DS}(s,s)=s^{O(1)}?


• 



Given arbitrary graphs H1H_{1} and H2H_{2}, one can consider graphs GG such that (i) GG does not contain H1H_{1} as a subgraph, (ii) G¯\overline{G} does not contain H2H_{2} as a subgraph, (iii) adding any edge to GG yields a graph that contains H1H_{1} as a subgraph, and (iv) adding any edge to G¯\overline{G} yields a graph that contains H2H_{2} as a subgraph. Taking H1=KsH_{1}=K_{s} and H2=KtH_{2}=K_{t} yields the doubly saturated R⁡(s,t)R(s,t)-good graphs that we have been studying, although other choices of graphs may be equally interesting. The study of graphs satisfying conditions (i) and (ii) is a central topic in Ramsey theory [20, 9, 10, 11].






More generally, our work creates an impetus to find other types of mathematical problems to which our computer-assisted methodology can be fruitfully applied. The features that made this problem amenable to computer-assistance were the following:


• 



Data for small cases are very helpful for understanding the problem while being hard to compute by hand, which makes SAT solvers and other computational experiments useful.


• 



There is a vast array of techniques that might be applicable to the problem, which makes leveraging the encyclopedic knowledge of LLMs powerful.


• 



Proofs are likely to involve relatively elementary combinatorial arguments, which allows them to be discovered and formalized by LLMs.



With the mathematical abilities of LLMs rapidly advancing, we expect that the last feature will become increasingly unnecessary.
