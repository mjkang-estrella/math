                                                                                    1. Introduction

                                             The convexity of level sets of solutions to elliptic equations is a classical problem in
                                          partial differential equations. For Dirichlet problems in convex domains, this question has
                                          a long history. Stronger notions include log-concavity and p-concavity. For the Lapla-
                                          cian, two basic examples are the torsion function and the first Dirichlet eigenfunction.
                                          Brascamp and Lieb [3] proved the log-concavity of the first Dirichlet eigenfunction. For
                                          the torsion problem, Makar-Limanov [24] proved the 21 -concavity in dimension two, and
                                          Kennington [13] extended it to arbitrary dimension. Both problems were studied for the
                                          2-Hessian equation, see [2, 21, 23, 25] in R3 . Very recently, Li, Ma, Qiu and Salani [17]
                                          extended these results for 2-Hessian in higher dimensions n ≥ 4.
                                            There are two main approaches to convexity problems of this type. The macroscopic
                                          convexity principle is based on the maximum principle for suitable two-point functions; see
                                          Caffarelli and Spruck [6], Korevaar [14], and Kennington [13]. The microscopic convexity
                                          principle is based on the method of continuity and constant rank theorems. Caffarelli
                                          and Friedman [5] developed this approach for semilinear equations in dimension two, and
                                          Korevaar and Lewis [15] extended it to higher dimensions. Guan and Ma [11] developed
                                          a corresponding constant rank method for fully nonlinear equations. We refer interested
                                          readers to Chen and Ma [7] for more references.
                                             Lions [19] raised the question whether positive solutions of semilinear Dirichlet problem
                                          in convex domains have always convex superlevel sets. Hamel, Nadirashvili and Sire [12]
                                          constructed a counterexample in dimension two, hence the quasiconcavity of positive
                                          solution may fail for general semilinear Dirichlet problems in convex domains. Zhang [26]
                                          recently shows such failure even for minimal, strictly stable, positive solutions on smooth
                                          uniformly convex planar domains.

  In contrast with the Dirichlet problem, a positive first Robin eignefunction does not
always preserve log-concavity. In their seminal work, Andrews, Clutterbuck and Hauer [1]
constructed smooth convex domains for which the first Robin eigenfunction has nonconvex
superlevel sets, and hence is not quasiconcave, when α > 0 is sufficiently small. The argu-
ment in the proof of [1, Theorem 1.3] also implies that Robin torsion functions may have
nonconvex superlevel sets for sufficiently small positive α on bounded convex domains.
Meanwhile, they proposed the following conjecture.
Conjecture 1.1. For a bounded convex domain Ω ⊂ Rn , there exists α0 = α0 (Ω) > 0
such that the first Robin eigenfunction is log-concave for all α ≥ α0 .

  The intuitive argument is that as α goes to infinity, the solutions of the Robin problem
converge to the corresponding Dirichlet solutions. For example, the normalized positive
solutions to (1.2) would converge to the normalized first Dirichlet eigenfunction. Crasta
and Fragalà proved the conjecture under a boundary regularity assumption whose order
increases with the dimension.
Theorem 1.2 (Theorems 1.1-1.2 in [8] ). Let Ω ⊂ Rn be bounded, uniformly convex, and
∂Ω ∈ C m , with ⌊m − n/2⌋ ≥ 4. Then there exists α0 > 0 such that, for every α ≥ α0 ,
    (i) the first Robin eigenfunction is strictly log-concave;
   (ii) the Robin torsion function is strictly 21 -concave.

  Here ⌊t⌋ means the integer part function. They also tracked the threshold: for fixed n
and m, α0 can be chosen to depend continuously and increasingly on the diameter of the
domain Ω, the C m size of ∂Ω, and decreasingly on the minimum principal curvature of
∂Ω.
  Notice that the regularity assumption for the convex domain depends on the dimension
in Theorem 1.2, and this dependence was necessary for the approach in [8], since the
high-order Sobolev estimates and the Morrey–Sobolev embedding were used to obtain
the C 2 convergence; see [8, Sections 2–3]. Crasta and Fragalà then asked the following
question [8, p. 181].
                                              2
Problem 1.1. Is it possible to remove or weaken the regularity assumptions of Theorems
1.1 and 1.2 in [8]?

  In this paper, we solve the above problem in any dimension when the domain is C 3,1 .
Thus our regularity assumption for the domain is dimensional free, and much weaker
than that in [8]. This also implies Conjecture 1.1 of Andrews, Clutterbuck and Hauer for
uniformly convex C 3,1 domains. Our main results are the following.

Theorem 1.3 (Robin torsion). Let Ω ⊂ Rn be a bounded uniformly convex domain of
class C 3,1 . Then there is α0,T > 0 such that uT,α , the Robin torsion function given by
(1.1), is strictly 12 -concave for every α ≥ α0,T . The threshold depends only on n, the C 3,1
bound for ∂Ω, and the positive lower bound for the principal curvatures on ∂Ω.

Theorem 1.4 (First Robin eigenfunction). Let Ω ⊂ Rn be a bounded uniformly convex
domain of class C 3,1 . Then there is α0,E > 0 such that the first Robin eigenfunction uE,α ,
solution to (1.2), is strictly log-concave for every α ≥ α0,E . The threshold has the same
dependence as in the Robin torsion problem.
