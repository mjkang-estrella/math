                                         2. The chord conjecture
Definition 2.1. Let P be a polygon. A chord of P is a segment joining two vertices of P ; so the
chords are the sides and the diagonals. A chord quadrilateral of P is a polygon with at most four
sides that contains P and each of whose sides is parallel to a chord of P . We denote by qch (P ) the
least area of a chord quadrilateral of P .
   An n-gon has n2 chords. Translating a side of a chord quadrilateral towards P until it touches
                       
P does not increase the area, so the minimum is attained by one of finitely many quadrilaterals,
whose sides lie on support lines of P parallel to chords. Each of these candidates is determined by
the vertices of P , and its area is a rational function of their coordinates. Clearly q(P ) ≤ qch (P ), the
ratio qch (P )/|P | is invariant under affine transformations, and qch (P ) = |P | if P has at most four
vertices. Figure 1 shows two examples. The inequality q(P ) ≤ qch (P ) can be strict; an example is
given in Remark 4.3.
Conjecture 2.2 (chord conjecture). Every polygon P satisfies qch (P ) ≤ √35 |P |, and equality holds
only for affine-regular pentagons.
    The following proposition is almost obvious, but we include it for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 2.3. Let c ≥ 1. If qch (P ) ≤ c |P | for every polygon P , then q(K) ≤ c |K| for every
convex body K. In particular, Conjecture 2.2 implies the inequality of Conjecture 1.1.
Proof. Let K be a convex body, and let P1 ⊂ P2 ⊂ · · · be polygons with vertices on the boundary
of K whose union is dense in K. Since q(Pk ) ≤ qch (Pk ) ≤ c |Pk |, there are polygons Qk ⊃ Pk with
at most four sides and |Qk | ≤ c |Pk | ≤ c |K|. Let B ⊂ P1 be a disc with centre o and radius r > 0.
If x ∈ Qk , then Qk contains the convex hull of B and x, whose area is at least r |x − o|; hence
|x − o| ≤ c |K|/r, and the polygons Qk lie in a fixed disc. Passing to a subsequence we may assume
that the (at most four) vertices of Qk converge. Then Qk converges in the Hausdorff metric to the
convex hull Q of the limit points, which is a polygon with at most four sides. For every j we have
Pj ⊂ Qk for all k ≥ j, hence Pj ⊂ Q; therefore K ⊂ Q. Finally |Q| = lim |Qk | ≤ c |K| by the
continuity of the area.                                                                           □
   The proof shows the well-known fact that it suffices to prove the inequality of Conjecture 1.1
for polygons, and it uses the chord condition only through q ≤ qch . The characterisation of the
equality case in Conjecture 1.1 does not follow from Conjecture 2.2 in this way.
   Although Conjecture 2.2 is stronger than the inequality of Conjecture 1.1 (Proposition 2.3), it
may be more accessible. The sides of the candidates have prescribed directions, so for an n-gon
the conjecture is a finite system of inequalities between rational functions of the coordinates of the
vertices, of the kind that has been handled for n = 5 (Section 3). For q(P ) there are also finitely
many candidates, by DePano’s theorem (see [7, Lemma 2.1]), but one side of a candidate may be
determined by a midpoint condition instead of a direction. Chord quadrilaterals are inherited from
sub-polygons: if P ′ is the convex hull of some of the vertices of P , then every chord quadrilateral
of P ′ that contains P is a chord quadrilateral of P . The extremal quadrilaterals of the regular
pentagon are chord quadrilaterals, and so are the quadrilaterals used in the proofs for pentagons.
Numerical experiments. For a given polygon, qch (P ) is computed by enumerating all candidates.
We did this for 16 830 random polygons with 5 ≤ n ≤ 10 vertices, drawn from three random models
(Table 1). Random polygons are far from extremal, so we also maximised qch (P )/|P | numerically
over the n-gons for n = 6, 7, 8, by a trust-region bundle method for minima of smooth functions.
The 50 runs were started from random points on an ellipse, from perturbed affine-regular n-gons,
and from perturbed affine-regular pentagons with n − 5 additional vertices; a lower bound on the
areas of the triangles spanned by three consecutive vertices kept the polygons genuine n-gons.
Every run ended either at an affine-regular n-gon (n = 6, 7), which appears to be a local maximum,
with qch (P )/|P | = 34 and 1.2588 . . ., respectively, or at a nearly degenerate n-gon, in which some
triangle spanned by three consecutive vertices has area at most 10−3 |P |: close to an affine-regular
pentagon, with a value slightly below c∗ (the largest was c∗ − 2.3 · 10−7 ), or, in three runs, close
to an affine-regular hexagon, with a value slightly below 34 . Since q ≤ qch and q is continuous, the
supremum of qch (P )/|P | over the n-gons is at least c∗ for every n ≥ 5; the experiments suggest
that it equals c∗ and is not attained for n ≥ 6.

                                      5. Concluding remarks
   To attack Kuperberg’s conjecture, by Proposition 2.3, it suffices to consider polygons, but with
arbitrarily many vertices. For fixed n, a compactness argument modulo affine maps shows that
the supremum of q(P )/|P |, or of qch (P )/|P |, over the n-gons is attained at a local maximum or is
approached by n-gons degenerating to a polygon with fewer vertices (for qch one uses that every
chord of the limit is a limit of chords). So the inequalities of both conjectures would follow if the
ratio did not exceed c∗ at any local maximum with n ≥ 6 vertices. In our numerical experiments
with qch and n ≤ 8 the only local maxima found were the affine-regular hexagons and heptagons,
but we cannot exclude others for all n. An induction on n by deleting a vertex fails: if Pv denotes
the convex hull of the vertices of P other than v, the inequality qch (P ) ≤ qch (Pv ) + c∗ (|P | − |Pv |),
which would make the induction      work, is false for the regular hexagon P , where qch (P ) = 43 |P | and
the right-hand side is 1 + 2 1

   The restriction to finitely many directions makes the candidates explicit, which is an attraction
of the chord conjecture, but it removes the variational tools. Sides cannot be rotated, so there
is no midpoint property (see [5, Proposition 2.1]), and there is no analogue of DePano’s theorem
(see [7, Lemma 2.1]): in 4740 random polygons a chord quadrilateral of least area always had three
sides containing sides of P , as in Figure 1, but for the hexagon with vertices (−14, 2), (−4, −10),
(11, −14), (12, 2), (3, 14), (−8, 7) the chord quadrilateral of least area is unique, and only two of
its sides contain sides of P . Moreover qch (P )/|P | is not a continuous function of P : a new short
side brings a new direction. Compactness and perturbation arguments therefore do not carry over
directly, and one has to work with the combinatorics of the vertices.
