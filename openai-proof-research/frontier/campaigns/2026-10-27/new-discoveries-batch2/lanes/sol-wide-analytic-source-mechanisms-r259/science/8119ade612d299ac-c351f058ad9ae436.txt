                                                                                    1. Introduction
                                            Geometric tomography is an area of mathematics that deals with the retrieval of information
                                          about solids from lower-dimensional data, such as the areas of sections or projections. The
                                          book “Geometric Tomography” by R. J. Gardner [8] gives a comprehensive account of known
                                          results, techniques and open problems in the area.
                                            Throughout the paper, S n−1 denotes the unit sphere in Rn , equipped with the standard
                                          inner product ⟨·, ·⟩, and
                                                                             ξ ⊥ = {x ∈ Rn : ⟨x, ξ⟩ = 0}.
                                          We denote by B2n the Euclidean unit ball in Rn . A convex body is a compact convex subset of
                                          Rn with non-empty interior.
                                            For a convex body K ⊂ Rn , its parallel section function in the direction θ ∈ S n−1 is defined
                                          by
                                                                   AK,θ (t) = voln−1 K ∩ {x ∈ Rn : ⟨x, θ⟩ = t} .
                                                                                                               

                                          A basic question in geometric tomography is to determine how much information about K is
                                          encoded by these functions.
                                            A classical result of Minkowski and Funk states that if K and L are origin-symmetric
                                          convex bodies in Rn such that
                                                                           voln−1 (K ∩ ξ ⊥ ) = voln−1 (L ∩ ξ ⊥ )
                                          for every ξ ∈ S n−1 , then K = L; see [8, Corollary 7.2.7]. Thus an origin-symmetric convex
                                          body is uniquely determined by the values of AK,θ (0), θ ∈ S n−1 .
                                             For non-central sections much less is known. The following problem, posed by Barker and
                                          Larman [7], remains open even in the plane.

   Let K be an origin-symmetric convex body in Rn (i.e., K = −K) with infinitely smooth
boundary. It was shown in [27] that if K is locally 1-separably integrable, then K is an
ellipsoid if n is odd, and K is a Euclidean ball if n is even.
   Note that a body with a locally separable section function near the origin is necessarily
locally 1-separably integrable. For origin-symmetric convex bodies, these two notions coincide.
   In Section 4, we show that if a convex body K ⊂ Rn (not necessarily origin-symmetric
or smooth) has a locally separable section function near the origin, then K is an ellipsoid
if n is odd, and K is a centered Euclidean ball if n is even. We will also obtain the same
conclusion for infinitely smooth convex bodies in even dimensions with locally separable
section functions near the boundary. Our results do not cover the case when n is odd and the
body has a separable section function near the boundary. In fact, a version of this question
for polynomially integrable bodies, posed by Agranovsky in [2], is also open.
   We finally discuss the homothety conjecture. Let K be a convex body in Rn and let δ be a

   The homothety conjecture, proposed by Schütt and Werner [21], says that if a convex body
in Rn is homothetic to one of its convex bodies of flotation, then it is an ellipsoid. For partial
results, the reader is referred to [4], [23], [24].
   We consider the following version of this problem. Let K be a convex body in Rn that is
homothetic to Kδ for each δ from some interval (δ1 , δ2 ) ⊂ (0, voln (K)/2). Does this imply
that K is an ellipsoid? In Section 5, we show that if K ⊂ R2 is a convex body containing the
origin in its interior such that Kδ = λ(δ)K for every δ ∈ (δ1 , δ2 ), then K is an ellipse. Note
that for a single δ this result is not true, namely, there exists a non-symmetric convex body
K ⊂ R2 and δ > 0 such that Kδ = λ(δ)K; see [4]. In dimensions n ≥ 3, we show that an

origin-symmetric infinitely smooth convex body K is an ellipsoid under the assumption that
Kδ = λ(δ)K for all δ ∈ (δ1 , voln (K)/2).

               6. Applications to the problem of Barker and Larman
     The following theorem is a particular case of [8, Theorem 7.2.18]
Theorem 6.1. Let K and L be convex bodies in Rn . If p1 , p2 ∈ int K ∩ int L are distinct
points such that
                     AK−pi ,θ (0) = AL−pi ,θ (0), θ ∈ S n−1 ,

  In [7], Barker and Larman posed the following question. Let t > 0 be fixed, and let K and
L be convex bodies in Rn containing tB2n in their interiors. Does

the parallel section functions for infinitely many values of t implies that K = L. They also
asked whether the corresponding result holds in even dimensions. The following theorem
answers this question affirmatively.
