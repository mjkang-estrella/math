   Abruptness is equivalent to s1 ∈ L1loc (R) (i.e., s1 is locally integrable on R), while Vigon’s conjecture
asks for a much stronger relation between local integrability and pointwise finiteness of s1 . Our next
result shows that this stronger conclusion fails even within the class of symmetric pure-jump Lévy
processes.
Theorem 1.2 (Vigon’s conjecture [19, Conj. 1.6] fails). There exist abrupt symmetric pure-jump Lévy
processes for which s1 (0) = ∞, s1 (r) < ∞ for r ∈ R \ {0} and s1 ∈ L1loc (R).
   One might suspect that the failure of Vigon’s conjecture is tied to symmetry and confined to a single
exceptional slope. However, the phenomenon can in fact be much richer. Without imposing symmetry,
the set of slopes at which point hitting fails may be uncountable and have no isolated points. Since
abruptness forces s1 ∈ L1loc (R), such an exceptional set must nevertheless have Lebesgue measure zero.
The next theorem shows that this restriction is compatible with a highly nontrivial exceptional set.
Theorem 1.3. There exists an abrupt pure-jump Lévy process of infinite variation such that
                                         E = {r ∈ R : s1 (r) = ∞}
is a compact perfect set of Lebesgue measure zero. In particular, E is uncountable but s1 ∈ L1loc (R).
Remark 1.4. (a) Theorems 1.2 and 1.3 show two different levels of failure of Vigon’s conjecture.
Theorem 1.2 gives the particularly simple symmetric situation in which the exceptional set is the
singleton {0}, whereas Theorem 1.3 shows that the exceptional set may instead be uncountable and
perfect. Thus, pointwise finiteness of s1 can depend on the drift in a substantially more complicated
way than local integrability alone suggests.
(b) The constructions are not canonical. Their proofs use quantitative separation between successive
jump scales, together with corresponding balance conditions on the masses and, in the asymmetric
construction, on the drift parameters. These conditions leave considerable freedom in the choice of
the defining sequences. Thus, the particular parameters used in Theorems 1.2 and 1.3 are chosen for
convenience rather than being intrinsic to the phenomenon.
(c) The counterexamples may also be chosen with Lévy measures having C ∞ densities on R \ {0}.
This can be achieved by replacing the atoms by suitably chosen smooth bumps, in the spirit of the
construction in Section 5.3. In the interest of brevity, the details are omitted in the present work.
(d) Theorem 1.2 has a particularly striking interpretation in terms of point hitting and local times.
Since s1 (r) < ∞ if and only if X (r) = (Xt − rt)t⩾0 hits points, the process X itself does not hit points
and has no local time field, whereas every non-zero linear drift perturbation X (r) hits points and admits
a local time field; see the discussion following [7, Conj. 1.10] and the references therein.
   More generally, for the process in Theorem 1.3, the drift perturbation X (r) fails to hit points precisely
for r ∈ E, while it hits points for every r ∈/ E. Hence the existence of a local time field may vary over
an uncountable set of deterministic slopes.
   This has a natural interpretation in terms of local times along curves: the local time of X (r) at
level a is the local time of X along the affine line t 7→ a + rt. Thus, even within the class of straight
lines, existence of local times may depend delicately on the slope. This connects the examples with the
study of local times along curves. Bass and Burdzy [3] establish continuity and uniform boundedness
of Brownian local times over bounded Hölder classes with exponent greater than 1/2, while Holden and
Sheffield [10] obtain analogous occupation-density estimates for certain Gaussian fields under Lipschitz
perturbations. Our examples show that for infinite-variation Lévy processes even the existence of a
local time field need not be stable under deterministic linear perturbations.
1.1. Smoothness of convex minorants of Lévy paths and Theorem 1.1. Fix T ∈ (0, ∞) and
let CT be the convex minorant of the path {(t, Xt ) : t ∈ [0, T ]} of X (i.e., the largest convex function
under the path of X). This convex function induces a Radon measure dCT′ on (0, T ). In this context,
Theorem 1.1 resolves the following dichotomy for convex minorants.
Corollary 1.5. Let X be a real Lévy process of infinite variation. Exactly one of the following holds:
 (i) X is abrupt, dCT′ is purely atomic and its atoms are isolated in (0, T ) a.s.
(ii) X is strongly eroded, dCT′ is diffuse, singular continuous and CT is continuously differentiable a.s.
Moreover, the dichotomy is independent of T ∈ (0, ∞).
           INFINITE-VARIATION LÉVY PROCESSES ARE EITHER STRONGLY ERODED OR ABRUPT                                   3


Proof. By Theorem 1.1, it suffices to show that abrupt Lévy processes satisfy (i) and strongly eroded
processes satisfy (ii). The convex function CT admits a right-continuous right-derivative CT′ on (0, T ).
Denote its range (image) by ST ⊂ R and let L(ST ) ⊂ R denote its set of finite limit points. According
to [7, Thms 1.1 and 1.2], abrupt Lévy processes satisfy L(ST ) = ∅ and dCT′ puts positive mass at all
jump times of CT′ . If there existed some accumulation t ∈ (0, T ) of jump times tn of CT′ , since CT′ is
locally bounded, then CT′ (tn ) would have a convergent subsequence, contradicting L(ST ) = ∅. Thus,
dCT′ is purely atomic, and its atoms are isolated. (Note that the atoms do accumulate at 0 and T .)
    Again by [7, Thms 1.1 and 1.2], for a strongly eroded Lévy processes, L(ST ) = R and CT′ is
continuous. Thus, dCT′ is diffuse. Moreover, the stick-breaking representation in [8, Thm 3.1] shows
that the sum of the lengths of maximal intervals of constancy of CT′ (where CT′ is differentiable with
CT′′ = 0) equals T , making dCT′ a singular continuous measure.                                        □
Remark 1.6. (a) By [7, Cor. 1.13 and 1.14], a dichotomy completely analogous to the one in Corollary 1.5
holds over infinite time horizons.
(b) By [7, Prop. 1.5], if two Lévy processes differ by a finite-variation process, then both processes fall
in the same category in both Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 1.5.
(c) A minor modification of the arguments in the proof of Corollary 1.5 shows that the statement
in [7, Conj. 1.9] is equivalent to the dichotomy that the convex minorant CT of any infinite-variation
Lévy process is either continuously differentiable a.s. or dCT′ is purely atomic with isolated atoms a.s.
In fact, this is further equivalent to the dichotomy (see also [7, Tab. 1]): for infinite-variation Lévy
processes, the finite limit set L(ST ) of the slopes of the convex minorant CT is either ∅ a.s. or R a.s.

1.3. Strategy of the proofs and organisation of the remainder of the paper. The proof of
Theorem 1.1 starts from the finiteness of JX (I) in (2) for a single interval I. After a compact-jump
reduction, we introduce smoothed Lévy characteristics that control the exponent on nearby frequency
scales. Their differential estimates imply that, under interval-integrability, the running maximum of |ψ|
must eventually grow faster than linearly. At frequencies where |ψ(u)| is nevertheless only of order u,
a local averaging argument forces a uniform contribution to the interval-integrated resolvent kernel.
A fivefold covering argument then shows that such exceptional frequencies have finite logarithmic
measure. Away from them, the kernels associated with different bounded slope intervals are uniformly
comparable, which propagates finiteness from one interval to every interval.
   The constructions in Theorems 1.2, 1.3 and 1.7 both exploit lacunary sequences of jump sizes. In
Section 4, we construct symmetric atomic Lévy measures supported on such sequences and derive the
required finiteness, divergence and local integrability properties of s1 from conditions on the atom
locations and their masses. In Section 5.3, we construct the examples for Theorem 1.7 using absolutely
continuous Lévy measures with strictly positive C ∞ densities. These constructions use smooth bumps
centred along a lacunary sequence, with their locations, widths and masses chosen to ensure strong
erosion and realise the prescribed Pruitt and Blumenthal–Getoor indices. In both constructions, the
separation of jump scales allows us to estimate their contributions separately and express the desired
properties through explicit conditions on the construction parameters.
   The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 presents applications of the two main
results. Section 3 proves Theorems 1.1 and Section 4 proves Theorems 1.2 and 1.3. Finally, Section 5
establishes the sequential Gaussian limit criterion and uses it to construct the strongly eroded processes
underlying Theorem 1.7.

             2. Applications to Lipschitz minorants and Burgers turbulence
   The established dichotomy for infinite variation processes being abrupt or strongly eroded, and the
counterexample to Vigon’s point-hitting conjecture have multiple applications and consequences across
the literature, which we will discuss in the present section. The former completes the classification
of convex-minorant slopes and connects the geometry of Lévy paths with contact sets and Burgers
turbulence. The latter separates this geometry from point polarity, regularity of the origin and the
existence of local times.

Finally, we prove Theorem 1.7, constructing strongly eroded processes with arbitrary admissible activity
indices β− ∈ [0, 1] and β+ ∈ [1, 2].
