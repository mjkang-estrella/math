Abstract.
In the last decade, the order polytope of the zigzag poset has been thoroughly studied. A related poset, called crown poset, obtained by adding an extra cover relation between the endpoints of an even zigzag poset, is not so well understood. In this paper, we study the order polytopes of crown posets. We provide explicit formulas for their ff-polynomials, and show that the sequence of coefficients of each ff-polynomial is log-concave and asymptotically normal. We provide recursive formulas for their Ehrhart polynomial, giving a counterpart to formulas found in the zigzag case by Petersen–Zhuang (2025). We use these formulas to simplify a computation by Ferroni–Morales–Panova (2025) of the linear term of the order polynomial of these posets. Furthermore, we provide a combinatorial interpretation for the coefficients of the h∗h^{*}-polynomial in terms of the cyclic swap statistic on cyclically alternating permutations, which provides a circular version of a result by Coons–Sullivant (2023).
Key words and phrases: Crown poset, order polytope, ff-vector, Chebyshev polynomial, order polynomial, Ehrhart polynomial, zigzag poset, h∗h^{*}-vector, cyclically alternating permutation, cyclic swap, gamma-nonnegativity

1. Introduction
Order polytopes were introduced by Richard P. Stanley [27] as a geometric representation of partially ordered sets (posets). They play a significant role in combinatorial geometry, optimization, and algebraic combinatorics. The zigzag (or the fence) poset ZnZ_{n} on [n][n] is defined by the cover relations 1≺2≻3≺⋯≻n−1≺n1\prec 2\succ 3\prec\cdots\succ n-1\prec n if nn is even and 1≺2≻3≺⋯≺n−1≻n1\prec 2\succ 3\prec\cdots\prec n-1\succ n if nn is odd, see Figure 1, and it has been extensively studied in the literature [9, 23], but the same cannot be said about the crown poset. The crown poset 𝒞2​n\mathcal{C}_{2n} on [2​n][2n] is defined by the cover relations 1≺2≻3≺⋯≻2​n−1≺2​n≻11\prec 2\succ 3\prec\cdots\succ 2n-1\prec 2n\succ 1, that is, it corresponds to the zigzag poset Z2​nZ_{2n} with the extra cover relation 2​n≻12n\succ 1, see Figure 2.

In this paper we study the order polytope of the crown poset 𝒞2​n\mathcal{C}_{2n}.
The crown poset 𝒞2​n\mathcal{C}_{2n} is the poset of proper faces of a polygon with nn vertices.
The motivation for studying these posets came from studying order polytopes constructed from face lattices of more complicated polytopes.
We will discuss this more in Section 6.
The following is an outline of the paper with our main results highlighted.
In Section 2, we quickly mention the relevant background related to order polytopes and Ehrhart theory, as well as fix some notation.
In Section 3, we study the ff-polynomial of 𝒪⁡(𝒞2​n)\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{C}_{2n}): we provide an explicit formula for each of its coefficients, and use this to prove that these coefficients are a log-concave sequence and also asymptotically normal. These formulas are obtained by using the well-known connection between faces of the order polytope and the so called connected and compatible partitions of the underlying poset. We count these partitions using elementary combinatorial methods.

2. Preliminaries
In this section we quickly give some conventions and notation used in this paper. We mostly follow standard terminology from polytope theory and Ehrhart theory (see e.g. [1]).
We consider the empty set ∅\emptyset as a face of a polytope of dimension −1-1. The full polytope is also considered to be a face. The ff-vector of a dd-polytope 𝔭\mathfrak{p} is denoted by f⁡(𝔭)=(f−1​(𝔭),f0​(𝔭)​…,fd​(𝔭))f(\mathfrak{p})=(f_{-1}(\mathfrak{p}),f_{0}(\mathfrak{p})\dots,f_{d}(\mathfrak{p})), where fi​(𝔭)f_{i}(\mathfrak{p}) is the number of ii-dimensional faces of 𝔭\mathfrak{p}, and the ff-polynomial f𝔭​(t)f_{\mathfrak{p}}(t) of 𝔭\mathfrak{p} is the generating polynomial of f⁡(𝔭)f(\mathfrak{p}), that is, f𝔭​(t)=∑i=0d+1fi−1​(𝔭)​tif_{\mathfrak{p}}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{d+1}f_{i-1}(\mathfrak{p})t^{i}. If 𝔭⊆ℝd\mathfrak{p}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d} is a lattice polytope then the integer point counting function k↦#⁡(k​𝔭∩ℤd):ℤ>0→ℤ≥0k\mapsto\#(k\mathfrak{p}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d})\colon\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} agrees with a polynomial, called the Ehrhart polynomial, which we denote by L⁡(𝔭,k)L(\mathfrak{p},k).
The series 1+∑k≥1L⁡(𝔭,k)​tk1+\sum_{k\geq 1}L(\mathfrak{p},k)t^{k} can be written as a rational function

Next, we quickly recall basic facts about order polytopes.
For more on order polytopes see [27].
Let P=([d],⪯)P=([d],\preceq) be a finite poset.
The order polytope 𝒪⁡(P)\mathcal{O}(P) is the polytope
{x=(x1,…,xd)∈[0,1]d∣xi≤xj​ for all ​i⪯j​ in ​P}.\{x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}\mid x_{i}\leq x_{j}\text{ for all }i\preceq j\text{ in }P\}.
These are full-dimensional polytopes, that is, dim(𝒪⁡(P))=|P|\dim(\mathcal{O}(P))=|P|.
The vertices of 𝒪⁡(P)\mathcal{O}(P) are in bijection with the indicator vectors of filters of PP and the facet-defining inequalities of 𝒪⁡(P)\mathcal{O}(P) are given by
0≤xi\displaystyle 0\leq x_{i}
for all ii minimal
xj≤1\displaystyle x_{j}\leq 1
for all jj maximal
xi≤xj\displaystyle x_{i}\leq x_{j}
for all cover relations i≺ji\prec j .
Order polytopes also admit unimodular triangulations, which are constructed as follows: let σ\sigma be a linear extension of PP, and
let v0σ=∑i=1deiv_{0}^{\sigma}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}e_{i}. Recursively define viσv_{i}^{\sigma} to be viσ=vi−1σ−eσ−1​(i)v_{i}^{\sigma}=v_{i-1}^{\sigma}-e_{\sigma^{-1}(i)} and then define Δσ\Delta^{\sigma} to be the convex hull of v0σ,v1σ,…,vdσv_{0}^{\sigma},v_{1}^{\sigma},\ldots,v_{d}^{\sigma}. Then the simplices Δσ\Delta^{\sigma} corresponding to all linear extensions σ\sigma of PP form a unimodular triangulation of 𝒪⁡(P)\mathcal{O}(P) which we will refer to as the canonical triangulation.
3. The ff-polynomial of 𝒪⁡(𝒞2​n)\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{C}_{2n})
We start by studying the ff-polynomial of 𝒪⁡(𝒞2​n)\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{C}_{2n}).
Since 𝒞2​n\mathcal{C}_{2n} has nn minimal elements, nn maximal elements and 2​n2n cover relations, it follows that 𝒪⁡(𝒞2​n)\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{C}_{2n}) has 4​n4n facets.
In fact, we can compute explicitly each entry of the ff-polynomial of 𝒪⁡(𝒞2​n)\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{C}_{2n}).
We will approach this by using the well-known characterization of faces of order polytopes in terms of so called connected and compatible partitions of the underlying poset.
We begin by reviewing the related terminology.
Fix a finite poset PP and let π={B1,…,Bk}\pi=\{B_{1},\dots,B_{k}\} be a partition of the ground set of PP.
We call the sets BiB_{i} either parts or blocks.
We say that π\pi is connected if each BiB_{i}, considered as an induced subposet, is connected.
Recall that a poset is connected if its comparability graph is connected.
We introduce an order relation ≤π\leq_{\pi} on the blocks of π\pi by setting Bi≤πBjB_{i}\leq_{\pi}B_{j} if there is some x∈Bix\in B_{i} and y∈Bjy\in B_{j} such that x≤yx\leq y in PP.
Note that ≤π\leq_{\pi} is reflexive but not necessarily transitive or antisymmetric.
If the transitive closure of ≤π\leq_{\pi} is antisymmetric, we say π\pi is compatible.
Note that the transitive closure of ≤π\leq_{\pi} is antisymmetric if and only if for all blocks of π\pi
Bi≤π⋯≤πBj≤π⋯≤πBi implies Bi=Bj.B_{i}\leq_{\pi}\dots\leq_{\pi}B_{j}\leq_{\pi}\dots\leq_{\pi}B_{i}\quad\text{ implies }B_{i}=B_{j}.
A sequence of blocks of the form Bi≤π⋯≤πBj≤π⋯≤πBiB_{i}\leq_{\pi}\dots\leq_{\pi}B_{j}\leq_{\pi}\dots\leq_{\pi}B_{i} is called a cycle.
Hence, π\pi is compatible if and only if there are no cycles among the blocks of π\pi, other than cycles consisting of a single block.
We often write just ≤\leq instead of ≤π\leq_{\pi}.
If π\pi is a connected and compatible partition we call π\pi a CCP for short.
If |π|=k|\pi|=k and π\pi is a CCP we call π\pi a kk-CCP.
The faces of 𝒪⁡(P)\mathcal{O}(P) turn out to correspond to, not CCPs of PP, but CCPs of P^=P∪{0^,1^}\hat{P}=P\cup\{\hat{0},\hat{1}\}, which is the poset obtained from PP by adding a minimum element 0^\hat{0} and a maximum element 1^\hat{1}.
Theorem 3.1 ([27]).
Let PP be a finite poset of size dd.
Let 0≤k≤d0\leq k\leq d.
The kk-faces of 𝒪⁡(P)\mathcal{O}(P) are in bijection with the (k+2)(k+2)-CCPs of P^\hat{P}.
We turn to studying CCPs of 𝒞2​n\mathcal{C}_{2n} and 𝒞^2​n\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{2n}.
The CCPs of 𝒞2​n\mathcal{C}_{2n} are easy to recognize:
Lemma 3.2.
A connected partition of 𝒞2​n\mathcal{C}_{2n} with at least two blocks is compatible if and only if at least one block has odd cardinality.
Proof.
First, suppose π\pi is a connected partition of 𝒞2​n\mathcal{C}_{2n}.
Write the elements of 𝒞2​n\mathcal{C}_{2n} as
(∗\ast)
a1<b1>a2<b2>⋯<bn>a1.a_{1}<b_{1}>a_{2}<b_{2}>\cdots<b_{n}>a_{1}.
Since each block of π\pi is connected, each block is going to consist of (cyclically) consecutive elements in the list (∗)(\ast).
If B≤B′B\leq B^{\prime} for some B,B′∈πB,B^{\prime}\in\pi and B≠B′B\neq B^{\prime} then there needs to be elements a∈Ba\in B and b∈B′b\in B^{\prime} that are (cyclically) consecutive in the list (∗)(\ast).
Hence the only way to have B≤B′B\leq B^{\prime} for two distinct blocks is if the union B∪B′B\cup B^{\prime} consists of (cyclically) consecutive elements.
Thus, if there are any cycles among the blocks of π\pi, any element in 𝒞2​n\mathcal{C}_{2n} is going to appear in one of those blocks.
Therefore, any cycle among the blocks of π\pi is going to contain all blocks.
With this observation we now fix a connected partition π={B1,…,Bk}\pi=\{B_{1},\dots,B_{k}\} of 𝒞2​n\mathcal{C}_{2n} where k≥2k\geq 2 and proceed to prove the lemma.
Suppose π\pi has an odd block BiB_{i}.
Either BiB_{i} is of the form
Bi={ai1<bi2>⋯<bim>aim+1}B_{i}=\{a_{i_{1}}<b_{i_{2}}>\cdots<b_{i_{m}}>a_{i_{m+1}}\}
or BiB_{i} is of the form
Bi={bi1>ai2<⋯>aim<bim+1}.B_{i}=\{b_{i_{1}}>a_{i_{2}}<\cdots>a_{i_{m}}<b_{i_{m+1}}\}.
In the first case there cannot be any block Bj≠BiB_{j}\neq B_{i} with Bj≤BiB_{j}\leq B_{i}.
In the second case there cannot be any block Bj≠BiB_{j}\neq B_{i} with Bi≤BjB_{i}\leq B_{j}.
Hence BiB_{i} cannot appear in any non-trivial cycle.
We conclude that π\pi is compatible.
Then suppose every block of π\pi has even cardinality.
We aim to show that π\pi is not compatible.
Now either each BiB_{i} is of the form
Bi={ai1<bi2>⋯>aim<bim+1}B_{i}=\{a_{i_{1}}<b_{i_{2}}>\cdots>a_{i_{m}}<b_{i_{m+1}}\}
or each BiB_{i} is of the form
Bi={bi1>ai2<⋯<bim>aim+1}.B_{i}=\{b_{i_{1}}>a_{i_{2}}<\cdots<b_{i_{m}}>a_{i_{m+1}}\}.
Hence we can chain together all the blocks of π\pi to form a cycle B1≤B2≤⋯≤Bk≤B1B_{1}\leq B_{2}\leq\dots\leq B_{k}\leq B_{1}.
Since k≥2k\geq 2, we conclude that π\pi is not compatible.
∎
The comparability graph of 𝒞2​n\mathcal{C}_{2n} is the cycle graph on 2​n2n elements.
By Lemma 3.2, the CCPs of 𝒞2​n\mathcal{C}_{2n} therefore correspond to connected partitions of the vertex set of the cycle graph where at least one part has odd cardinality.
Counting such partitions seems combinatorially feasible.
However, the faces of 𝒪⁡(𝒞2​n)\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{C}_{2n}) do not correspond to CCPs of 𝒞2​n\mathcal{C}_{2n} but instead to the CCPs of 𝒞^2​n=𝒞2​n∪{0^,1^}\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{2n}=\mathcal{C}_{2n}\cup\{\hat{0},\hat{1}\}. 4. The Ehrhart polynomial of 𝒪⁡(𝒞2​n)\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{C}_{2n})
In this section, we study the order polynomial of 𝒞2​n\mathcal{C}_{2n}. We present a recursive formula to compute Ω𝒞2​n​(t)\Omega_{\mathcal{C}_{2n}}(t) in terms of the order polynomials of zigzags. We use this recursion to give an alternative proof for the nonnegativity of the coefficients of Ω𝒞2​n​(t)\Omega_{\mathcal{C}_{2n}}(t), recently proved by Ferroni, Morales and Panova [11].
Given a finite poset PP, let ΩP:ℝ→ℝ\Omega_{P}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R} be the function such that ΩP​(n)\Omega_{P}(n) counts the number of order-preserving maps from PP to [n][n]. This function was first introduced by Richard P. Stanley in his PhD thesis, where it was also proved that it is a polynomial [26, Proposition 13.1], called the order polynomial of PP. Later, Stanley proved [27, Theorem 4.1] that the Ehrhart polynomial L⁡(𝒪⁡(P),t)L(\mathcal{O}(P),t) of 𝒪⁡(P)\mathcal{O}(P) and the order polynomial ΩP​(t)\Omega_{P}(t) of PP are related by the following:
L⁡(𝒪⁡(P),t)=ΩP​(t+1).L(\mathcal{O}(P),t)=\Omega_{P}(t+1).
Therefore, the results in this section also provide a formula to compute the Ehrhart polynomial of 𝒪⁡(𝒞2​n)\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{C}_{2n}) as an expression of Ehrhart polynomials of zigzags and prove that its coefficients are nonnegative.
We start by stating the reciprocity theorem for graded posets. Recall that a poset P=(P,⪯)P=(P,\preceq) is graded if there exists a rank function ρ:P→ℕ\rho\colon P\to\mathbb{N} such that
(i)
x≺yx\prec y implies ρ⁡(x)<ρ⁡(y)\rho(x)<\rho(y);
(ii)
if yy covers xx, then ρ⁡(y)=ρ⁡(x)+1\rho(y)=\rho(x)+1.
A graded poset PP is called rr-graded if every maximal chain of PP has length rr. For example, 𝒞2​n\mathcal{C}_{2n} and Zn+1Z_{n+1} are 11-graded posets for every n≥1n\geq 1.

5. The h∗h^{*}-polynomial of 𝒪⁡(𝒞2​n)\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{C}_{2n})
In this section, we study the h∗h^{*}-polynomial of the order polytope 𝒪⁡(𝒞2​n)\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{C}_{2n}). We give a combinatorial interpretation for the coefficients of the h∗h^{*}-polynomial of 𝒪⁡(𝒞2​n)\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{C}_{2n}). To do this, we study a subset of alternating permutations, called cyclically alternating permutations. We will adapt the methods in [9] to the cyclically alternating case.
Let σ=σ1σ2⋯σ2​n∈S2​n\sigma=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\cdots\sigma_{2n}\in S_{2n} be a permutation. We say that σ\sigma is a cyclically alternating permutation if σ1<σ2>σ3<…>σ2​n−1<σ2​n>σ1\sigma_{1}<\sigma_{2}>\sigma_{3}<\ldots>\sigma_{2n-1}<\sigma_{2n}>\sigma_{1}. For more about cyclically alternating permutations, we refer to [10]. For ease of exposition, we will sometimes refer to cyclically alternating permutations as CAPs.
Definition 5.1.
For a CAP σ\sigma, we define the permutation statistic cswap⁡(σ)\operatorname{cswap}(\sigma) to be the number of i<2​ni<2n such that σ−1​(i)>σ−1​(i+1)+1\sigma^{-1}(i)>\sigma^{-1}(i+1)+1. Equivalently, it is the number of i<2​ni<2n such that ii is to the right of i+1i+1 in σ\sigma and swapping ii and i+1i+1 in σ\sigma yields a CAP.
We say that a CAP σ\sigma cyclically swaps to another CAP τ\tau when τ=σ⋅(i,i+1)\tau=\sigma\cdot(i,i+1) for some ii such that ii is to the right of i+1i+1 in σ\sigma. Note that τ\tau has one less inversion than σ\sigma. The cyclic swap set CSwap⁡(σ)\operatorname{CSwap}(\sigma) is the set of i<2​ni<2n for which we can perform a cyclic swap in σ\sigma.
Example 5.2.

Remark 5.3.
The notion of cyclically swapping is the reverse notion of swapping as introduced in [9]. That is, if a CAP σ\sigma cyclically swaps to τ\tau, then τ\tau swaps to σ\sigma in the language of [9].

The contrapositive of this last sentence says, if E⁡(σ)⊈vert⁡(τ)E(\sigma)\not\subseteq\operatorname{vert}(\tau), then the given order is a shelling order.
If inv⁡(σ)>inv⁡(τ)\operatorname{inv}(\sigma)>\operatorname{inv}(\tau), then, since σ\sigma minimizes the inversion number over all cyclically alternating permutations that contain E⁡(σ)E(\sigma), by Proposition 5.6, we have E⁡(σ)⊄vert⁡(τ)E(\sigma)\not\subset\operatorname{vert}(\tau). 5.1. The γ\gamma-nonnegativity of the h∗h^{*}-vector
Let g⁡(t)=∑i≥0gi​tig(t)=\sum_{i\geq 0}g_{i}t^{i} be a symmetric polynomial. Then, gi=gd−ig_{i}=g_{d-i} for some positive integer dd, which we define as the symmetric degree of gg.
Symmetric polynomials with symmetric degree dd span a vector space of dimension ⌊d/2⌋+1\lfloor d/2\rfloor+1. Hence, they can be written in the basis
Γd={ti​(1+t)d−2​i}i=0⌊d/2⌋,\Gamma_{d}=\{t^{i}(1+t)^{d-2i}\}_{i=0}^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor},
that is,
g⁡(t)=∑0≤2​i≤dγi​ti​(1+t)d−2​i.g(t)=\sum_{0\leq 2i\leq d}\gamma_{i}t^{i}(1+t)^{d-2i}.
The vector γ=(γ0,γ1,…,γ⌊d/2⌋)\gamma=(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}) is called the γ\gamma-vector of gg and a topic of interest is the nonnegativity of the coefficients of the γ\gamma-vector of a polynomial.
The study of the nonnegativity of the γ\gamma-vector has gained significant attention due to its deep connections to combinatorics, and algebraic geometry. Initially introduced by Foata and Schützenberger [12] in the context of Eulerian polynomials and later developed further by various researchers such as Foata and Strehl [13, 14], as well as Brändén [6] and Gal [15], γ\gamma-nonnegativity emerged as a powerful concept that provides a novel approach to several important problems, particularly in combinatorics. One key area of application is in unimodality questions for symmetric polynomials. The nonnegativity of the γ\gamma-vector offers insights into the symmetric structure of a polynomial, revealing its unimodal nature and providing a potential approach for proving unimodality even for polynomials that are not real-rooted. For example, symmetric real-rooted polynomials in ℝ≥0\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} have nonnegative γ\gamma-vectors [6, Lemma 4.1]. On the other hand, every γ\gamma-nonnegative polynomial (real-rooted or not) is symmetric and unimodal. This is particularly important in combinatorics, where polynomials such as Eulerian polynomials or PP-Eulerian polynomials play a significant role in enumerating combinatorial objects (e.g., flag triangulations of spheres) and understanding combinatorial symmetries. Also, it is an interesting question finding a combinatorial interpretation of the coefficients of the γ\gamma-vector.
Let PP be a finite poset. A labeling of PP is an injection ω:P→ℤ\omega\colon P\to\mathbb{Z}. The pair (P,ω)(P,\omega) is called naturally labeled if x≤Pyx\leq_{P}y implies ω⁡(x)≤ω⁡(y)\omega(x)\leq\omega(y). In this case, ω\omega is called a natural labeling. which implies
(6.4)
h∗​(𝒪⁡(ℒ⁡(Pn)),t)=h∗​(𝒪⁡(𝒞2​n),t).h^{*}(\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{L}(P_{n})),t)=h^{*}(\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{C}_{2n}),t).
If PP is a poset isomorphic to the face lattice of a polytope, we call 𝒪⁡(P)\mathcal{O}(P) a polytope order polytope, or POP, for short. For polytopes of dimensions 00 (points) and 11 (line segments), their POPs are completely understood. On the other hand, Equations (6.2) and (6.4) describe the ff-polynomial and the h∗h^{*}-polynomial of POPs of 22-dimensional polytopes, so a natural question is:
Question 6.7.
How can one describe the ff-polynomial, the Ehrhart polynomial, and the h∗h^{*}-polynomial of POPs of nn-dimensional polytopes for n≥3n\geq 3?
Two natural constructions arise from the 22-dimensional case:
(i)
pyramids over polygons, and
(ii)
prisms over polygons.
In fact, the face lattice of a pyramid over a polygon PnP_{n} is isomorphic to ℒ⁡(Pn)×𝒫2\mathcal{L}(P_{n})\times\mathcal{P}_{2}, where 𝒫2\mathcal{P}_{2} is the chain poset on 22 elements, and the face lattice of a prism over PnP_{n} is isomorphic to
0^⊕[(ℒ⁡(Pn)∖{0^})×(ℒ⁡(S)∖{0^})],\hat{0}\oplus[(\mathcal{L}(P_{n})\setminus\{\hat{0}\})\times(\mathcal{L}(S)\setminus\{\hat{0}\})],
where SS is a line segment. Hence, a first step to studying POPs of 33-dimensional polytopes could be investigating the order polytope of the cartesian product of two posets. This sounds like a hard problem, but, since 𝒫2\mathcal{P}_{2} and ℒ⁡(S)\mathcal{L}(S) are posets with few elements and order relations, the following question could perhaps be more feasible:
Question 6.8.
Given a finite poset PP, how can one describe the ff-vector and the h∗h^{*}-vector of 𝒪⁡(P×𝒫2)\mathcal{O}(P\times\mathcal{P}_{2}) and 𝒪⁡(P×(ℒ⁡(S)∖{0^}))\mathcal{O}(P\times(\mathcal{L}(S)\setminus\{\hat{0}\}))?
The particular case of taking the cartesian product between a finite poset PP and the 22-chain poset 𝒫2\mathcal{P}_{2} is interesting because of the following: recall that the Boolean lattice BnB_{n} is the poset on 2[n]2^{[n]} such that A⪯BA\preceq B in BnB_{n} if A⊆BA\subseteq B. It is not hard to verify that
Bn≅𝒫2×𝒫2×⋯×𝒫2⏞n​ times.B_{n}\cong\overbrace{\mathcal{P}_{2}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{P}_{2}}^{n\mbox{ times}}.
An nn-simplex is an nn-dimensional polytope that is the convex hull of n+1n+1 affinely independent points, and its face lattice is isomorphic to Bn+1B_{n+1}, that is, answering Question 6.8 gives information about POPs of simplices. In particular, if the real-rootedness of h∗​(𝒪​(P),t)h^{*}(\mathcal{O}(P),t) is preserved under the cartesian product, that is, if the real-rootedness of h∗​(𝒪​(P),t)h^{*}(\mathcal{O}(P),t) implies the real-rootedness of h∗​(𝒪⁡(P×𝒫2),t)h^{*}(\mathcal{O}(P\times\mathcal{P}_{2}),t), where PP is a finite poset, then the following conjecture would be confirmed to be true:
Conjecture 6.9.
h∗​(𝒪⁡(Bn),t)h^{*}(\mathcal{O}(B_{n}),t) is real-rooted for every natural number nn.
