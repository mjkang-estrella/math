 Abstract. 
    
 In the last decade, the order polytope of the zigzag poset has been thoroughly studied. A related poset, called  crown poset , obtained by adding an extra cover relation between the endpoints of an even zigzag poset, is not so well understood. In this paper, we study the order polytopes of crown posets. We provide explicit formulas for their  f f -polynomials, and show that the sequence of coefficients of each  f f -polynomial is log-concave and asymptotically normal. We provide recursive formulas for their Ehrhart polynomial, giving a counterpart to formulas found in the zigzag case by Petersen–Zhuang (2025). We use these formulas to simplify a computation by Ferroni–Morales–Panova (2025) of the linear term of the order polynomial of these posets. Furthermore, we provide a combinatorial interpretation for the coefficients of the  h ∗ h^{*} -polynomial in terms of the cyclic swap statistic on cyclically alternating permutations, which provides a circular version of a result by Coons–Sullivant (2023). 
  
 
 
 Key words and phrases:  Crown poset, order polytope,  f f -vector, Chebyshev polynomial, order polynomial, Ehrhart polynomial, zigzag poset,  h ∗ h^{*} -vector, cyclically alternating permutation, cyclic swap, gamma-nonnegativity

 1.  Introduction 

 
 Order polytopes were introduced by Richard P. Stanley  [ 27 ]  as a geometric representation of partially ordered sets (posets). They play a significant role in combinatorial geometry, optimization, and algebraic combinatorics. The  zigzag  (or the  fence )  poset   Z n Z_{n}  on  [ n ] [n]  is defined by the cover relations  1 ≺ 2 ≻ 3 ≺ ⋯ ≻ n − 1 ≺ n 1\prec 2\succ 3\prec\cdots\succ n-1\prec n  if  n n  is even and  1 ≺ 2 ≻ 3 ≺ ⋯ ≺ n − 1 ≻ n 1\prec 2\succ 3\prec\cdots\prec n-1\succ n  if  n n  is odd, see Figure  1 , and it has been extensively studied in the literature  [ 9 ,  23 ] , but the same cannot be said about the crown poset. The  crown poset   𝒞 2 ​ n \mathcal{C}_{2n}  on  [ 2 ​ n ] [2n]  is defined by the cover relations  1 ≺ 2 ≻ 3 ≺ ⋯ ≻ 2 ​ n − 1 ≺ 2 ​ n ≻ 1 1\prec 2\succ 3\prec\cdots\succ 2n-1\prec 2n\succ 1 , that is, it corresponds to the zigzag poset  Z 2 ​ n Z_{2n}  with the extra cover relation  2 ​ n ≻ 1 2n\succ 1 , see Figure  2 .

 In this paper we study the order polytope of the crown poset  𝒞 2 ​ n \mathcal{C}_{2n} .
The crown poset  𝒞 2 ​ n \mathcal{C}_{2n}  is the poset of proper faces of a polygon with  n n  vertices.
The motivation for studying these posets came from studying order polytopes constructed from face lattices of more complicated polytopes.
We will discuss this more in Section  6 . 
 
 
 The following is an outline of the paper with our main results highlighted. 
 
 
 In Section  2 , we quickly mention the relevant background related to order polytopes and Ehrhart theory, as well as fix some notation.
In Section  3 , we study the  f f -polynomial of  𝒪 ⁡ ( 𝒞 2 ​ n ) \mathcal{O}(\mathcal{C}_{2n}) : we provide an explicit formula for each of its coefficients, and use this to prove that these coefficients are a log-concave sequence and also asymptotically normal. These formulas are obtained by using the well-known connection between faces of the order polytope and the so called connected and compatible partitions of the underlying poset. We count these partitions using elementary combinatorial methods.

 2.  Preliminaries 

 
 In this section we quickly give some conventions and notation used in this paper. We mostly follow standard terminology from polytope theory and Ehrhart theory (see e.g.  [ 1 ] ). 
 
 
 We consider the empty set  ∅ \emptyset  as a face of a polytope of dimension  − 1 -1 . The full polytope is also considered to be a face. The  f f -vector  of a  d d -polytope  𝔭 \mathfrak{p}  is denoted by  f ⁡ ( 𝔭 ) = ( f − 1 ​ ( 𝔭 ) , f 0 ​ ( 𝔭 ) ​ … , f d ​ ( 𝔭 ) ) f(\mathfrak{p})=(f_{-1}(\mathfrak{p}),f_{0}(\mathfrak{p})\dots,f_{d}(\mathfrak{p})) , where  f i ​ ( 𝔭 ) f_{i}(\mathfrak{p})  is the number of  i i -dimensional faces of  𝔭 \mathfrak{p} , and the  f f -polynomial   f 𝔭 ​ ( t ) f_{\mathfrak{p}}(t)  of  𝔭 \mathfrak{p}  is the generating polynomial of  f ⁡ ( 𝔭 ) f(\mathfrak{p}) , that is,  f 𝔭 ​ ( t ) = ∑ i = 0 d + 1 f i − 1 ​ ( 𝔭 ) ​ t i f_{\mathfrak{p}}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{d+1}f_{i-1}(\mathfrak{p})t^{i} . The series  1 + ∑ k ≥ 1 L ⁡ ( 𝔭 , k ) ​ t k 1+\sum_{k\geq 1}L(\mathfrak{p},k)t^{k}  can be written as a rational function

 Next, we quickly recall basic facts about order polytopes.
For more on order polytopes see  [ 27 ] .
Let  P = ( [ d ] , ⪯ ) P=([d],\preceq)  be a finite poset.
The  order polytope   𝒪 ⁡ ( P ) \mathcal{O}(P)  is the polytope 
 

 
 
 { x = ( x 1 , … , x d ) ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] d ∣ x i ≤ x j ​  for all  ​ i ⪯ j ​  in  ​ P } . \{x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}\mid x_{i}\leq x_{j}\text{ for all }i\preceq j\text{ in }P\}. 
 
 
 These are full-dimensional polytopes, that is,  dim ( 𝒪 ⁡ ( P ) ) = | P | \dim(\mathcal{O}(P))=|P| .
The vertices of  𝒪 ⁡ ( P ) \mathcal{O}(P)  are in bijection with the indicator vectors of filters of  P P  and the facet-defining inequalities of  𝒪 ⁡ ( P ) \mathcal{O}(P)  are given by

    for all  i i  minimal 
 
 
 
 x j ≤ 1 \displaystyle x_{j}\leq 1 
    for all  j j  maximal 
 
 
 
 x i ≤ x j \displaystyle x_{i}\leq x_{j} 
    for all cover relations  i ≺ j i\prec j   . 
 
 
 Order polytopes also admit unimodular triangulations, which are constructed as follows: let  σ \sigma  be a linear extension of  P P , and
let  v 0 σ = ∑ i = 1 d e i v_{0}^{\sigma}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}e_{i} . Recursively define  v i σ v_{i}^{\sigma}  to be  v i σ = v i − 1 σ − e σ − 1 ​ ( i ) v_{i}^{\sigma}=v_{i-1}^{\sigma}-e_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}  and then define  Δ σ \Delta^{\sigma}  to be the convex hull of  v 0 σ , v 1 σ , … , v d σ v_{0}^{\sigma},v_{1}^{\sigma},\ldots,v_{d}^{\sigma} . Then the simplices  Δ σ \Delta^{\sigma}  corresponding to all linear extensions  σ \sigma  of  P P  form a unimodular triangulation of  𝒪 ⁡ ( P ) \mathcal{O}(P)  which we will refer to as the  canonical triangulation . 
 
 
 
 3.  The  f f -polynomial of  𝒪 ⁡ ( 𝒞 2 ​ n ) \mathcal{O}(\mathcal{C}_{2n}) 

 
 We start by studying the  f f -polynomial of  𝒪 ⁡ ( 𝒞 2 ​ n ) \mathcal{O}(\mathcal{C}_{2n}) .
Since  𝒞 2 ​ n \mathcal{C}_{2n}  has  n n  minimal elements,  n n  maximal elements and  2 ​ n 2n  cover relations, it follows that  𝒪 ⁡ ( 𝒞 2 ​ n ) \mathcal{O}(\mathcal{C}_{2n})  has  4 ​ n 4n  facets.
In fact, we can compute explicitly each entry of the  f f -polynomial of  𝒪 ⁡ ( 𝒞 2 ​ n ) \mathcal{O}(\mathcal{C}_{2n}) .
We will approach this by using the well-known characterization of faces of order polytopes in terms of so called connected and compatible partitions of the underlying poset.
We begin by reviewing the related terminology. 
 
 
 Fix a finite poset  P P  and let  π = { B 1 , … , B k } \pi=\{B_{1},\dots,B_{k}\}  be a partition of the ground set of  P P .
We call the sets  B i B_{i}  either  parts  or  blocks .
We say that  π \pi  is  connected  if each  B i B_{i} , considered as an induced subposet, is connected.
Recall that a poset is connected if its comparability graph is connected.
We introduce an order relation  ≤ π \leq_{\pi}  on the blocks of  π \pi  by setting  B i ≤ π B j B_{i}\leq_{\pi}B_{j}  if there is some  x ∈ B i x\in B_{i}  and  y ∈ B j y\in B_{j}  such that  x ≤ y x\leq y  in  P P .
Note that  ≤ π \leq_{\pi}  is reflexive but not necessarily transitive or antisymmetric.
If the transitive closure of  ≤ π \leq_{\pi}  is antisymmetric, we say  π \pi  is  compatible .
Note that the transitive closure of  ≤ π \leq_{\pi}  is antisymmetric if and only if for all blocks of  π \pi 
 

 
 
 B i ≤ π ⋯ ≤ π B j ≤ π ⋯ ≤ π B i  implies  B i = B j . B_{i}\leq_{\pi}\dots\leq_{\pi}B_{j}\leq_{\pi}\dots\leq_{\pi}B_{i}\quad\text{ implies }B_{i}=B_{j}. 
 
 
 A sequence of blocks of the form  B i ≤ π ⋯ ≤ π B j ≤ π ⋯ ≤ π B i B_{i}\leq_{\pi}\dots\leq_{\pi}B_{j}\leq_{\pi}\dots\leq_{\pi}B_{i}  is called a  cycle .
Hence,  π \pi  is compatible if and only if there are no cycles among the blocks of  π \pi , other than cycles consisting of a single block.
We often write just  ≤ \leq  instead of  ≤ π \leq_{\pi} .
If  π \pi  is a connected and compatible partition we call  π \pi  a  CCP  for short.
If  | π | = k |\pi|=k  and  π \pi  is a CCP we call  π \pi  a  k k -CCP. 
 
 
 The faces of  𝒪 ⁡ ( P ) \mathcal{O}(P)  turn out to correspond to, not CCPs of  P P , but CCPs of  P ^ = P ∪ { 0 ^ , 1 ^ } \hat{P}=P\cup\{\hat{0},\hat{1}\} , which is the poset obtained from  P P  by adding a minimum element  0 ^ \hat{0}  and a maximum element  1 ^ \hat{1} . 
 
 
 Theorem 3.1   ( [ 27 ] ) . 
 
 Let  P P  be a finite poset of size  d d .
Let  0 ≤ k ≤ d 0\leq k\leq d .
The  k k -faces of  𝒪 ⁡ ( P ) \mathcal{O}(P)  are in bijection with the  ( k + 2 ) (k+2) -CCPs of  P ^ \hat{P} . 
 
 
 
 We turn to studying CCPs of  𝒞 2 ​ n \mathcal{C}_{2n}  and  𝒞 ^ 2 ​ n \hat{\mathcal{C}}_{2n} .
The CCPs of  𝒞 2 ​ n \mathcal{C}_{2n}  are easy to recognize: 
 
 
 Lemma 3.2 . 
 
 A connected partition of  𝒞 2 ​ n \mathcal{C}_{2n}  with at least two blocks is compatible if and only if at least one block has odd cardinality. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 First, suppose  π \pi  is a connected partition of  𝒞 2 ​ n \mathcal{C}_{2n} .
Write the elements of  𝒞 2 ​ n \mathcal{C}_{2n}  as 
 

 
 ( ∗ \ast )

 Since each block of  π \pi  is connected, each block is going to consist of (cyclically) consecutive elements in the list  ( ∗ ) (\ast) .

 Let  g ⁡ ( t ) = ∑ i ≥ 0 g i ​ t i g(t)=\sum_{i\geq 0}g_{i}t^{i}  be a symmetric polynomial. Then,  g i = g d − i g_{i}=g_{d-i}  for some positive integer  d d , which we define as the  symmetric degree  of  g g . 
 
 
 Symmetric polynomials with symmetric degree  d d  span a vector space of dimension  ⌊ d / 2 ⌋ + 1 \lfloor d/2\rfloor+1 . Hence, they can be written in the basis

 The vector  γ = ( γ 0 , γ 1 , … , γ ⌊ d / 2 ⌋ ) \gamma=(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{\lfloor d/2\rfloor})  is called the  γ \gamma -vector  of  g g  and a topic of interest is the nonnegativity of the coefficients of the  γ \gamma -vector of a polynomial. 
 
 
 The study of the nonnegativity of the  γ \gamma -vector has gained significant attention due to its deep connections to combinatorics, and algebraic geometry. Initially introduced by Foata and Schützenberger  [ 12 ]  in the context of Eulerian polynomials and later developed further by various researchers such as Foata and Strehl  [ 13 ,  14 ] , as well as Brändén  [ 6 ]  and Gal  [ 15 ] ,  γ \gamma -nonnegativity emerged as a powerful concept that provides a novel approach to several important problems, particularly in combinatorics. One key area of application is in unimodality questions for symmetric polynomials. The nonnegativity of the  γ \gamma -vector offers insights into the symmetric structure of a polynomial, revealing its unimodal nature and providing a potential approach for proving unimodality even for polynomials that are not real-rooted. For example, symmetric real-rooted polynomials in  ℝ ≥ 0 \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}  have nonnegative  γ \gamma -vectors  [ 6 , Lemma 4.1] . On the other hand, every  γ \gamma -nonnegative polynomial (real-rooted or not) is symmetric and unimodal. This is particularly important in combinatorics, where polynomials such as Eulerian polynomials or  P P -Eulerian polynomials play a significant role in enumerating combinatorial objects (e.g., flag triangulations of spheres) and understanding combinatorial symmetries. Also, it is an interesting question finding a combinatorial interpretation of the coefficients of the  γ \gamma -vector.
