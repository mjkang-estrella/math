1. Introduction

    Order polytopes were introduced by Richard P. Stanley [20] as a geometric representation of
partially ordered sets (posets). They play a significant role in combinatorial geometry, optimization,
and algebraic combinatorics. The zigzag (or the fence) poset Zn on [n] is defined by the cover
relations 1 ≺ 2 ≻ 3 ≺ · · · ≻ n − 1 ≺ n if n is even and 1 ≺ 2 ≻ 3 ≺ · · · ≺ n − 1 ≻ n
if n is odd (see Fig. 1), and it has been extensively studied in the literature [6,17], but the same
cannot be said about the crown poset. The crown poset C2n on [2n] is defined by the cover relations
1 ≺ 2 ≻ 3 ≺ · · · ≻ 2n − 1 ≺ 2n ≻ 1, that is, it corresponds to the zigzag poset Z2n with the extra
cover relation 2n ≻ 1 (see Fig. 2).

2. Preliminaries

    In this section we quickly give some conventions and notation used in this paper. We mostly
follow standard terminology from polytope theory and Ehrhart theory (see e.g. [1]).
    We consider the empty set ∅ as a face of a polytope of dimension −1. The full polytope is also
considered to be a face. The f -vector of a d-polytope p is denoted by f (p) = (f−1 (p), f0 (p) . . . , fd (p))
where fi (p) is the number of i-dimensional faces of p. If p ⊆ Rd is a lattice polytope then the integer
point counting function k ↦ → #(kp ∩ Zd ) : Z>0 → Z≥0 agrees∑ with a polynomial, called the Ehrhart
polynomial, which we denote by L(p, k). The series 1 + k≥1 L(p, k)t k can be written as a rational
function

where the polynomial h∗p (t) = h∗0 (p) + h∗1 (p)t + · · · + h∗d (p)t d is called the h∗ -polynomial of p.
   Next, we quickly recall basic facts about order polytopes. For more on order polytopes see [20].
Let P = ([d], ⪯) be a finite poset. The order polytope O(P) is the polytope

       {x = (x1 , . . . , xd ) ∈ [0, 1]d | xi ≤ xj for all i ⪯ j in P }.
These are full-dimensional polytopes, that is, dim(O(P)) = |P |. The vertices of O(P) are in bijection
with the indicator vectors of filters of P and the facet-defining inequalities of O(P) are given by

         0 ≤ xi           for all i minimal
         xj ≤ 1           for all j maximal                       .
         xi ≤ xj          for all cover relations i ≺ j

3. The f -vector of O (C2n )

    We start by studying the f -vector of O(C2n ). Since C2n has n minimal elements, n maximal
elements and 2n cover relations, it follows that O(C2n ) has 4n facets. In fact, we can compute
explicitly each entry of the f -vector of O(C2n ). We will approach this by using the well-known
characterization of faces of order polytopes in terms of so called connected and compatible
partitions of the underlying poset. We begin by reviewing the related terminology.
    Fix a finite poset P and let π = {B1 , . . . , Bk } be a partition of the ground set of P. We call the sets
Bi either parts or blocks. We say that π is connected if each Bi , considered as an induced subposet,
is connected. Recall that a poset is connected if its comparability graph is connected. We introduce
an order relation ≤π on the blocks of π by setting Bi ≤π Bj if there is some x ∈ Bi and y ∈ Bj such
that x ≤ y in P. Note that ≤π is reflexive but not necessarily transitive or antisymmetric. If the
transitive closure of ≤π is antisymmetric, we say π is compatible. Note that the transitive closure
of ≤π is antisymmetric if and only if for all blocks of π
       Bi ≤π · · · ≤π Bj ≤π · · · ≤π Bi   implies Bi = Bj .
A sequence of blocks of the form Bi ≤π · · · ≤π Bj ≤π · · · ≤π Bi is called a cycle. Hence, π is
compatible if and only if there are no cycles among the blocks of π , other than cycles consisting of
a single block. We often write just ≤ instead of ≤π . If π is a connected and compatible partition
we call π a CCP for short. If |π| = k and π is a CCP we call π a k-CCP.
   The faces of O(P) turn out to correspond to, not CCPs of P, but CCPs of P̂ = P ∪ {0̂, 1̂}, which is
the poset obtained from P by adding a minimum element 0̂ and a maximum element 1̂.

Theorem 3.1 ([20]). Let P be a finite poset of size d. Let 0 ≤ k ≤ d. The k-faces of O(P) are in bijection
with the (k + 2)-CCPs of P̂.
   We turn to studying CCPs of C2n and Cˆ2n . The CCPs of C2n are easy to recognize:

Lemma 3.2. A connected partition of C2n with at least two blocks is compatible if and only if at least
one block has odd cardinality.

Proof. First, suppose π is a connected partition of C2n . Write the elements of C2n as
       a1 < b 1 > a2 < b 2 > · · · < b n > a1 .                                                              (∗)
Since each block of π is connected, each block is going to consist of (cyclically) consecutive elements
in the list (∗). If B ≤ B′ for some B, B′ ∈ π and B ̸ = B′ then there needs to be elements a ∈ B and
b ∈ B′ that are (cyclically) consecutive in the list (∗). Hence the only way to have B ≤ B′ for two
distinct blocks is if the union B ∪ B′ consists of (cyclically) consecutive elements. Thus, if there are
any cycles among the blocks of π , any element in C2n is going to appear in one of those blocks.
Therefore, any cycle among the blocks of π is going to contain all blocks.
   With this observation we now fix a connected partition π = {B1 , . . . , Bk } of C2n where k ≥ 2
and proceed to prove the lemma.
   Suppose π has an odd block Bi . Either Bi is of the form
       Bi = {ai1 < bi2 > · · · < bim > aim+1 }
or Bi is of the form
       Bi = {bi1 > ai2 < · · · > aim < bim+1 }.
In the first case there cannot be any block Bj ̸ = Bi with Bj ≤ Bi . In the second case there cannot be
any block Bj ̸ = Bi with Bi ≤ Bj . Hence Bi cannot appear in any non-trivial cycle. We conclude that
π is compatible.
    Then suppose every block of π has even cardinality. We aim to show that π is not compatible.
Now either each Bi is of the form
       Bi = {ai1 < bi2 > · · · > aim < bim+1 }
                                                      4
T. Lundström and L. Saud Maia Leite                                         European Journal of Combinatorics 133 (2026) 104304


or each Bi is of the form
       Bi = {bi1 > ai2 < · · · < bim > aim+1 }.
Hence we can chain together all the blocks of π to form a cycle B1 ≤ B2 ≤ · · · ≤ Bk ≤ B1 . Since
k ≥ 2, we conclude that π is not compatible. □
   The comparability graph of C2n is the cycle graph on 2n elements. By Lemma 3.2, the CCPs of
C2n therefore correspond to connected partitions of the vertex set of the cycle graph where at least
one part has odd cardinality. Counting such partitions seems combinatorially feasible. However, the
faces of O(C2n ) do not correspond to CCPs of C2n but instead to the CCPs of Cˆ2n . The next proposition
shows how to obtain the CCPs of Cˆ2n from the CCPs of C2n .

Proposition 3.3.        For all k ≥ 2 let
       Ak = {(P , S) : P is a CCP of C2n , S ⊆ {odd parts of P }, |P | − |S | = k − 2}.

    (i) For all k ≥ 2 there is an injection Ak −→ {k-CCP’s of Cˆ2n }.
   (ii) For all k ≥ 3 the injection is also surjective and thus
               #{k-CCP’s of Cˆ2n } = |Ak |.
  (iii) For k = 2 the injection misses two elements and thus
               #{2-CCP’s of Cˆ2n } = |A2 | + 2.


Proof. Let k ≥ 2. Given a pair (P , S) ∈ Ak we let S0 = {B1 , . . . , Bm } ⊆ S be the set of parts in S that
have more lower elements than upper elements and S1 = {D1 , . . . , Dℓ } ⊆ S be the set of parts that
have more upper elements than lower elements. Here S = S0 ⊔ S1 . We map (P , S) to
       π = (P \ S) ∪ {B1 ∪ · · · ∪ Bm ∪ {0̂}} ∪ {D1 ∪ · · · ∪ Dℓ ∪ {1̂}},
see Fig. 3. Our aim is to show that this mapping is a well-defined injection Ak → {k-CCP’s of Cˆ2n }.
First we show that this map is well-defined. Use the same notation as above. Our aim is to show
that π is a k-CCP of Cˆ2n . Since P is a partition of C2n , it is easy to see that π is a partition of Cˆ2n .
The parts in P \ S are connected since P was connected, and the new parts B1 ∪ · · · ∪ Bm ∪ {0̂} and
D1 ∪ · · · ∪ Dℓ ∪ {1̂} are clearly connected. Here |π | = |P | − |S | + 2 = k. It remains to show that π is
compatible. Since each Bi is connected and has more lower elements than upper elements, each Bi
looks something like in Fig. 4(a). Similarly, since each Di is connected and has more upper elements
than lower elements, each Di looks something like in Fig. 4(b). In particular, no part of P can be
below any Bi , and no part of P can be above any Di . Write P \ S = {C1 , . . . , Cr }. Furthermore, write
B = B1 ∪ · · · ∪ Bm ∪ {0̂} and D = D1 ∪ · · · ∪ Dℓ ∪ {1̂}. Thus π = {C1 , . . . , Cr , B, D}. Since P is a
compatible partition of C2n , there cannot be any cycles among C1 , . . . , Cr . Hence any cycle among
the parts of π is going to either contain B or D, or both. If we have Ci ≤ B for any i then Ci ≤ Bj
for some j, which is not possible as we saw. Similarly, if D ≤ Ci for any i then Dj ≤ Ci for some j,
which is also not possible. We also cannot have D ≤ B. Therefore the only possible cycles among
the parts of π have the forms
       B ≤ C• ≤ · · · ≤ C•            C• ≤ · · · ≤ C• ≤ D              B ≤ C• ≤ · · · ≤ C• ≤ D,
where • ranges over some (possibly empty) subset of the indices {1, . . . , r }. But none of these are
cycles. We conclude that π is compatible and the map is well-defined.

4. The Ehrhart polynomial of O (C2n )

   In this section, we study the order polynomial of C2n . We present a recursive formula to compute
ΩC2n (t) in terms of the order polynomials of zigzags. We use this recursion to give an alternative
proof for the nonnegativity of the coefficients of ΩC2n (t), recently proved by Ferroni, Morales and
Panova [8].
   Given a finite poset P, let ΩP : R → R be the function such that ΩP (n) counts the number of
order-preserving maps from P to [n]. This function was first introduced by Richard P. Stanley in his
PhD thesis, where it was also proved that it is a polynomial [19, Proposition 13.1], called the order
polynomial of P. Later, Stanley proved [20, Theorem 4.1] that the Ehrhart polynomial L(O(P), t) of
O(P) and the order polynomial ΩP (t) of P are related by the following:

       L(O(P), t) = ΩP (t + 1).

Therefore, the results in this section also provide a formula to compute the Ehrhart polynomial
of O(C2n ) as an expression of Ehrhart polynomials of zigzags and prove that its coefficients are
nonnegative.
   We start by stating the reciprocity theorem for graded posets. Recall that a poset P = (P , ⪯) is
graded if there exists a rank function ρ : P → N such that

   (i) x ≺ y implies ρ (x) < ρ (y);
  (ii) if y covers x, then ρ (y) = ρ (x) + 1.

A graded poset P is called r-graded if every maximal chain of P has length r. For example, C2n and
Zn+1 are 1-graded posets for every n ≥ 1.

Lemma 4.1 ([3, Theorem 2.3]). Let P = ([n], ⪯) be an r-graded poset. Then

       ΩP (t − r) = (−1)n ΩP (−t).

5. The h∗ -vector of O (C2n )

    In this section, we study the h∗ -vector of the order polytope O(C2n ). We give a combinatorial
interpretation for the coefficients of the h∗ -polynomial of O(C2n ). To do this, we study a subset
of alternating permutations, called cyclically alternating permutations. We will adapt the methods
in [6] to the cyclically alternating case.
    Let σ = σ1 σ2 · · · σ2n ∈ S2n be a permutation. We say that σ is a cyclically alternating permutation
if σ1 < σ2 > σ3 < · · · > σ2n−1 < σ2n > σ1 . The set of cyclically alternating permutations on [2n] is
denoted by CA2n , and we set A◦ (2n) = |CA2n |. For more about cyclically alternating permutations,
we refer to [7]. For ease of exposition, we will sometimes refer to cyclically alternating permutations
as CAPs.

Definition 5.1. For a CAP σ , we define the permutation statistic cswap(σ ) to be the number of
i < 2n such that σ −1 (i) > σ −1 (i + 1) + 1. Equivalently, it is the number of i < 2n such that i is to
the right of i + 1 in σ and swapping i and i + 1 in σ yields a CAP.

   We say that a CAP σ cyclically swaps to another CAP τ when τ = σ · (i, i + 1) for some i such
that i is to the right of i + 1 in σ . Note that τ has one less inversion than σ . The cyclic swap set
CSwap(σ ) is the set of i < 2n for which we can perform a cyclic swap in σ .

Example 5.2. For n = 2, then σ = 2413 is a CAP, σ −1 = 3142, cswap(σ ) = 2, and CSwap(σ ) =
{1, 3}.

Remark 5.3. The notion of cyclically swapping is the reverse notion of swapping as introduced
in [6]. That is, if a CAP σ cyclically swaps to τ , then τ swaps to σ in the language of [6].

Lemma 5.5. Let σ be a CAP. If (σ −1 (a), σ −1 (b)) is a relevant inversion of σ , then there exists k with
b ≤ k < a such that k ∈ CSwap(σ ).

Proof. We proceed by induction on a − b. If a − b = 1, then σ −1 (a) = σ −1 (b + 1) < σ −1 (b) − 1
gives b ∈ CSwap(σ ). So, let us assume the result true for a − b ≥ 1 and let us prove it for a − b + 1.
We look at b + 1 in σ . There are three possible cases:

  (1) if σ −1 (a) < σ −1 (b + 1) − 1, then (σ −1 (a), σ −1 (b + 1)) is a relevant inversion (recall that
      a > b + 1). Since a − (b + 1) < a − b, there exists b ≤ b + 1 ≤ k < a such that k ∈ CSwap(σ )
      by induction;
  (2) if σ −1 (a) > σ −1 (b + 1) − 1, then we claim that σ −1 (a) > σ −1 (b + 1). In fact, if σ −1 (a) =
      σ −1 (b + 1), then a = b + 1, but, by hypothesis, a > b + 1. So, σ −1 (a) > σ −1 (b + 1), which
      implies σ −1 (b + 1) + 1 ≤ σ −1 (a) < σ −1 (b) and gives b ∈ CSwap(σ );

  (3) if σ −1 (a) = σ −1 (b + 1) − 1, then we claim that σ −1 (b) > σ −1 (b + 1) + 1. In fact, if
      σ −1 (b) ≤ σ −1 (b + 1) + 1, then we have three cases:
              • if σ −1 (b) = σ −1 (b + 1), then b = b + 1, since σ is a bijection, so this case cannot happen;
              • if σ −1 (b) = σ −1 (b + 1) + 1, then σ −1 (a) < σ −1 (b + 1) < σ −1 (b), but σ −1 (a) =
                σ −1 (b + 1) − 1 and σ −1 (b) = σ −1 (b + 1) + 1 imply that a, b + 1, b is a decreasing

              • if σ −1 (b) < σ −1 (b + 1), then σ −1 (a) < σ −1 (b) < σ −1 (b + 1), but σ −1 (a) = σ −1 (b + 1) − 1
                implies that σ −1 (a) < σ −1 (b) < σ −1 (a) + 1, which is a contradiction.

Proposition 5.6. A cyclically alternating permutation σ minimizes the inversion number of all cyclically
alternating permutations τ with E(σ ) ⊆ vert(τ ).

Proof. Let vkσ ∈ vert(σ ). By definition, vkσ is the vector (1, . . . , 1) with a 1 removed from the
coordinate at the σ −1 (ℓ)th position for all ℓ ≤ k. We can read off inversions (i, j) of σ with
σ (j) ≤ k < σ (i) from vkσ as pairs where the ith component of vkσ is 1 and the jth component is
0.
    Now we claim that every relevant inversion of σ can be read from an element of E(σ ) as above.
Given a relevant inversion (i, j) of σ , we know, by Lemma 5.5, that there exists a cyclic swap k with
σ (j) ≤ k < σ (i) and (i, j) can be read from vkσ . Since k is a cyclic swap, vkσ is the opposite vertex
of the intersection of ∆σ and ∆τ for the CAP τ that σ cyclically swaps to when swapping k and
(k + 1). Therefore, all relevant inversions of σ can be found as non-adjacent 1 − 0 pairs in a vertex
in E(σ ). This means we can count the number of relevant inversions of σ from vertices in E(σ ). If
E(σ ) ⊆ vert(τ ), then all inversions of σ are inversions in τ , though τ could have more. Therefore,
σ minimizes inversions over all CAP τ with E(σ ) ⊆ vert(τ ). □

The vector γ = (γ0 , γ1 , . . . , γ⌊d/2⌋ ) is called the γ -vector of g and a topic of interest is the
nonnegativity of the coefficients of the γ -vector of a polynomial.
    The study of the nonnegativity of the γ -vector has gained significant attention due to its deep
connections to combinatorics, and algebraic geometry. Initially introduced by Foata and Schützen-
berger [9] in the context of Eulerian polynomials and later developed further by various researchers
such as Foata and Strehl [10,11], as well as Brändén [3] and Gal [12], γ -nonnegativity emerged as
a powerful concept that provides a novel approach to several important problems, particularly in
combinatorics. One key area of application is in unimodality questions for symmetric polynomials.
The nonnegativity of the γ -vector offers insights into the symmetric structure of a polynomial,
revealing its unimodal nature and providing a potential approach for proving unimodality even
for polynomials that are not real-rooted. For example, symmetric real-rooted polynomials in R≥0
have nonnegative γ -vectors [3, Lemma 4.1]. On the other hand, every γ -nonnegative polynomial
(real-rooted or not) is symmetric and unimodal. This is particularly important in combinatorics,
where polynomials such as Eulerian polynomials or P-Eulerian polynomials play a significant
role in enumerating combinatorial objects (e.g., flag triangulations of spheres) and understanding
combinatorial symmetries. Also, it is an interesting question finding a combinatorial interpretation
of the coefficients of the γ -vector.
