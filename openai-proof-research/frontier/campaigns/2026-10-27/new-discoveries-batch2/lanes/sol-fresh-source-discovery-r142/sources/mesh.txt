Abstract
    

A boxed mesh pattern is a mesh pattern whose selected entries lie in an empty
axis-parallel rectangle. We classify coincidences of boxed patterns with classical and vincular
patterns, exhibit a genuinely bivincular coincidence, and prove that no
boxed–bivincular coincidence occurs for patterns of length at least five.
Together with known results, this shows that every boxed pattern of length at
least five has factorial growth and hence fails the Stanley–Wilf property.
At length four, one exceptional orbit is enumerated by the semi-Baxter
numbers, while the remaining exceptional orbit, \{2143,3412\}, is
unresolved; we conjecture that it has factorial growth.
    

For \operatorname{Box}(123), we derive an exact maximum-insertion identity and prove the
subfactorial upper bound 2^{5n}n^{\beta n}, where \beta=\log_{2}(2\cos(\pi/7))<0.85. A closed enumeration remains open. We also prove a general first-moment
formula for boxed mesh patterns that depends only on the length of the
underlying pattern; in particular, the expected number of \operatorname{Box}(123)
occurrences in a uniformly random permutation of length n is asymptotic to
n\log n.
    

For \operatorname{Box}(12), we identify the occurrence statistic with the up-degree in
the strong Bruhat order, obtaining its maximum, its mean, and an exact
insertion identity for the distribution polynomials. We conjecture that the
coefficients of these polynomials are unimodal.
  




Keywords. mesh pattern; boxed pattern; Stanley–Wilf property;
vincular pattern; bivincular pattern; semi-Baxter permutation;
strong Bruhat order.




2020 Mathematics Subject Classification. 05A05, 05A15, 05A16




1 Introduction




Let \mathfrak{S}_{n} denote the set of permutations of
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}, with \mathfrak{S}_{0}=\{\varepsilon\}. A
classical pattern of length k is a permutation
p=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}. An occurrence of p in a
permutation \pi=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\cdots\pi_{n}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n} is a subsequence
\pi_{i_{1}}\pi_{i_{2}}\cdots\pi_{i_{k}}, where
i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}, that is order-isomorphic to p; that is, \pi_{i_{r}}<\pi_{i_{s}} if and only if p_{r}<p_{s} for 1\leq r,s\leq k.
For example, the pattern 132 occurs twice in the permutation 24513,
namely as the subsequences 243 and 253.




For a pattern p, defined in any specified sense, let
\operatorname{Av}_{n}(p)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{n} denote the set of permutations that
avoid p, that is, contain no occurrence of p. The Stanley–Wilf limit of a pattern p, when it exists, is




L(p)=\lim_{n\to\infty}|\operatorname{Av}_{n}(p)|^{1/n}.



Arratia [2] proved that an exponential upper bound for
|\operatorname{Av}_{n}(p)| for a classical pattern p implies the existence of this limit, and Marcus and Tardos
[25] proved the required upper bound. Thus the
Stanley–Wilf theorem says that L(p) exists and is finite for every fixed
classical pattern p. For any pattern p, not necessarily classical, we shall say that the
Stanley–Wilf property holds if




\limsup_{n\to\infty}|\operatorname{Av}_{n}(p)|^{1/n}<\infty.



We say that a positive sequence (a_{n}) has factorial growth if \log a_{n}=\Theta(n\log n),
or, equivalently, if a_{n}=n^{\Theta(n)}. If p is a consecutive pattern of length at least three, meaning that the
entries in an occurrence must occupy consecutive positions, Elizalde
[14, Theorem 4.1] proved that there exist constants
0<c_{p}<d_{p}<1 such that, for all sufficiently large n,




c_{p}^{n}n!<|\operatorname{Av}_{n}(p)|<d_{p}^{n}n!.



Consequently its nth root tends to infinity. (Consecutive patterns of
length two are the exceptional trivial cases, with one avoider.) Vincular patterns, defined in Section 2, already exhibit
both types of behaviour in length three: for example,
1\underline{23}, also written 1\text{-}23, has the Bell numbers as its
avoidance sequence, whereas some other one-bond patterns have Catalan
avoidance sequences [11]. Since
B_{n}^{1/n}\sim n/(e\log n), Bell growth also violates the Stanley–Wilf
property.




Mesh patterns, defined in Section 2, were introduced by Brändén and Claesson
[10] as a flexible language for imposing geometric
conditions on occurrences of classical permutation patterns. They have since
developed in several complementary directions: shading and coincidence
criteria [29, 13], systematic and
algorithmic classification [18, 5], exhaustive
generation [17], asymptotic containment
[16], and the study of distributions
[22, 23, 24].




Two patterns are called coincident when they impose exactly the same
avoidance condition: every permutation avoids one if and only if it avoids the
other. Thus coincidence identifies the actual avoidance classes, not merely
their counting sequences; a formal definition is given in
Section 2.




Avgustinovich, Kitaev and Valyuzhenich [3] introduced boxed
patterns: all (k-1)^{2} internal cells of a mesh pattern of length k are
shaded. They showed, among other things, that the permutations avoiding boxed 132
are exactly those avoiding classical 132 and are therefore counted by the
Catalan numbers. They also
showed that an analogue of the Erdős–Szekeres theorem fails for boxed
patterns of length greater than two, and they enumerated permutations
avoiding two or more boxed patterns of length three, where generalized
Catalan numbers appear. More strikingly,
they proved factorial lower bounds for many boxed patterns, showing that the
Stanley–Wilf conclusion for classical patterns does not extend to arbitrary
mesh patterns. The simplest of their constructions doubles each letter:




\delta(\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{m})=(2\pi_{1})(2\pi_{1}-1)\cdots(2\pi_{m})(2\pi_{m}-1).



Every pair is a descending pair of consecutive values, and the empty-rectangle
condition shows that \delta(\pi) avoids \operatorname{Box}(123): the unselected sibling
of the middle member of any increasing triple lies inside its rectangle. This
injects \mathfrak{S}_{m} into \operatorname{Av}_{2m}(\operatorname{Box}(123)). The same observation
and its complement handle any boxed pattern having a monotone interval factor
of length three. More generally, factorial growth is inherited from any boxed
interval factor; see Proposition 3.4.




Their general proof calls \tau=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{k} good when some
1<i<k has 1<\tau_{i}<k and the factor
\tau_{i-1}\tau_{i}\tau_{i+1} is nonmonotone. In the representative 213
case, every letter of an arbitrary base permutation is replaced by an
increasing pair of consecutive values. Whichever sibling plays \tau_{i}, its
mate either lies in the forbidden rectangle or forces the adjacent member of
the pattern to be the mate, contradicting the required order. Reverse and
complement deal with the other three nonmonotone triples. If \tau is not
good, its entries split into monotone blocks around its minimum and maximum.
Either it has a monotone factor of length three, in which case the
\operatorname{Box}(123) or \operatorname{Box}(321) construction applies, or it is one of the four
exceptional permutations below. Together these ideas give factorial lower
bounds for twenty of the twenty-four underlying permutations of length four.
Their argument left




2143,\qquad 3412,\qquad 2413,\qquad 3142,

(1)


and treated them as a single symmetry class.




It is important that the same theorem already settles every larger length.
More precisely, [3, Theorem 3] proves




|\operatorname{Av}_{n}(\operatorname{Box}(\tau))|\geq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor!



for every \tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}, k\geq 4, other than the four permutations in
(1). Consequently every boxed pattern of length at least five
fails the Stanley–Wilf property. Thus the obstruction to coincidence with a
classical, vincular or bivincular pattern in lengths at least five is
accompanied by a much stronger growth statement, but the two facts have
logically independent proofs: noncoincidence alone would not imply factorial
growth.




Three questions from the final section of [3] are directly relevant
here: determine the Stanley–Wilf behaviour of the four patterns in
(1); enumerate \operatorname{Box}(123); and decide when a boxed pattern can be
coincident with a classical pattern. The last question has since been settled:
coincident mesh patterns have the same underlying permutation
[13, Lemma 4.1], and the enclosed-diagonal
criterion of Tenner [29] classifies when a mesh is superfluous.
For boxed patterns the complete list is recalled in
Proposition 5.1. The first two questions remain the main
enumerative motivation for this paper.




Boxed patterns are also called frame patterns; under that name their
occurrence statistics have been studied in the cycle structure of
permutations by Jones, Kitaev and Remmel [19]. On
the algorithmic side, deciding boxed containment has been studied by Bruner
and Lackner [6], by Cho, Na and Sim [12]
and by Amit, Bille, Hagge Cording, Li Gørtz and Wedel Vildhøj
[4]; see Remark 3.3.




The four exceptional cases separate in two ways. First, the four patterns in
(1) are treated together in [3, p. 48], but they do
not form a single symmetry class: reverse, complement and inverse split
(1) into




\{2143,3412\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\{2413,3142\}.

(2)


The two avoidance sequences are different: at size five they have respectively
106 and 104 elements. Second, the latter orbit is not a counterexample to
the Stanley–Wilf property. The key observation for this orbit, that we prove in Lemma 4.1, is the
coincidence




\operatorname{Box}(2413)\mathrel{\equiv_{\rm c}}2\underline{41}3,



where the underline requires the entries playing 4 and 1 to be adjacent;
the equivalent dash notation is 2-41-3.
The right-hand class consists of the semi-Baxter permutations, completely
enumerated by Bouvel, Guerrini, Rechnitzer and Rinaldi
[7]. This settles one of the two orbits in
(2) and explains the value 104 at size five. We also classify
all coincidences of a boxed pattern with a nonclassical vincular pattern: none
exist in lengths five or more. Moreover, \operatorname{Box}(2413) is coincident with a
genuinely bivincular pattern, and we prove that no boxed pattern of length at
least five is coincident with any bivincular pattern.




For the unresolved orbit \{2143,3412\}, we show that replacing every entry
x by the increasing pair (2x-1)(2x) preserves the number of boxed 2143
occurrences. We also conjecture that the avoidance sequence associated with
this orbit has factorial growth; see Conjectures 7.4
and 7.5 and the accompanying discussion in
Section 7.




Our second focus is boxed 123. The sequence is recorded as OEIS A201168
[26], but no generating function or coefficient formula is given
there or, to our knowledge, elsewhere in the literature. We give an exact
maximum-insertion identity and show that its prefix condition is itself
enumerated by (t-1)!; this gives the five new terms a_{13},\ldots,a_{17}.
A weighted halving argument also gives




|\operatorname{Av}_{n}(\operatorname{Box}(123))|\leq 2^{5n}n^{\beta n},\qquad\beta=\log_{2}\!\left(2\cos\frac{\pi}{7}\right)<0.85,



so the avoidance class is subfactorial even though it does not have the
Stanley–Wilf property. The identity does not close in the total numbers,
and a closed enumeration remains open. On the distributional side, we prove
that the expected number of occurrences of \operatorname{Box}(\tau) in a uniformly random
permutation depends only on the length of \tau, not on \tau itself. For
boxed 123 the exact mean is (n+2)H_{n}-3n\sim n\log n, so a random
permutation contains on average on the order of n\log n such occurrences.




A complementary distribution question concerns the boxed pattern 12. A
recent sequence of papers has completed the classification of length-two mesh
patterns by distribution-equivalence and Wilf-equivalence. Su, Kitaev and
Zhang [28] obtained a near-classification; Fang, Fu,
Kitaev, Li, Su and Sun [15] reduced both classifications to a
single remaining case; and Zhang and Zhao [30] settled that
case. The first of these papers records the initial distribution polynomials
for boxed 12 but leaves a general formula open. We observe that the number of
boxed 12 occurrences is exactly the up-degree of a permutation in the strong
Bruhat order. The standard Bruhat cover criterion, also discussed in
mesh-pattern language by Bouvel, Ferrari and Tenner
[8], therefore brings the results of Adin and
Roichman [1] into the problem. In particular, it gives
the maximum number of occurrences, the number of maximizers, and the exact
mean. We add a short direct proof of the mean and an insertion identity that
isolates the catalytic prefix statistic needed for the full distribution.




The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we
introduce the notation and recall the preliminary results used throughout the
paper. In Section 3, we translate boxed containment into
consecutive containment after restriction to an interval of values. In
Section 4, we correct the length-four symmetry reduction,
identify the semi-Baxter orbit, and record the resulting global growth
classification. In Section 5, we classify classical and
vincular coincidences, exhibit a genuinely bivincular coincidence, and prove
that no boxed–bivincular coincidence occurs in length at least five. In
Section 6, we give an exact identity, a subfactorial upper bound,
and a general first-moment formula motivated by boxed 123. In
Section 7, we discuss the remaining orbit and state two
conjectures. Finally, in Section 8, we connect boxed 12 with
covers in the strong Bruhat order and record the resulting distributional
information.





2 Preliminaries




If w=w_{1}\cdots w_{k} is a word with distinct letters, its
standardization, also called its reduced form, is the unique
permutation \operatorname{st}(w)\in\mathfrak{S}_{k} whose entries have the same relative
order as those of w. Recall that a permutation \pi contains the
classical pattern \tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k} if \operatorname{st}(\pi_{i_{1}}\cdots\pi_{i_{k}})=\tau for some i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}; otherwise, \pi avoids \tau.

Let \pi=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{n}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}, and suppose that
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k} determine a classical occurrence of \tau in \pi.
Let v_{1}<\cdots<v_{k} be the selected values \pi_{i_{1}},\ldots,\pi_{i_{k}} written in increasing
order, and set i_{0}=v_{0}=0 and i_{k+1}=v_{k+1}=n+1. The selected entries form an occurrence of the mesh pattern
P=(\tau,R) if, for every (a,b)\in R, there is no plot point
(j,\pi_{j}) satisfying i_{a}<j<i_{a+1} and v_{b}<\pi_{j}<v_{b+1}. Thus each shaded square prescribes a rectangular region that must contain
no other point of the plot of \pi. When R=\varnothing, this definition
reduces to classical pattern containment.

We use the notation \operatorname{Box}(123) throughout, as it is more convenient in longer formulas.




For a permutation \pi=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{n}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}, its reverse,
complement, and inverse are defined, respectively, by \pi^{\mathrm{r}}_{i}=\pi_{n+1-i}, \pi^{\mathrm{c}}_{i}=n+1-\pi_{i},
\pi^{-1}_{\pi_{i}}=i for 1\leq i\leq n.
Geometrically, these operations reflect the plot in a vertical line, reflect
it in a horizontal line, and reflect it in the main diagonal, respectively.
They act on mesh patterns by applying the same reflection to both the
underlying permutation and the shaded cells. In particular, if
P=(\tau,R) has length k, then




\displaystyle P^{\mathrm{r}}
\displaystyle=\bigl(\tau^{\mathrm{r}},\{(k-a,b):(a,b)\in R\}\bigr),



\displaystyle P^{\mathrm{c}}
\displaystyle=\bigl(\tau^{\mathrm{c}},\{(a,k-b):(a,b)\in R\}\bigr),



\displaystyle P^{-1}
\displaystyle=\bigl(\tau^{-1},\{(b,a):(a,b)\in R\}\bigr).



These symmetries preserve containment, avoidance, and coincidence. They also
preserve boxed patterns: \operatorname{Box}(\tau)^{\mathrm{r}}=\operatorname{Box}(\tau^{\mathrm{r}}), \operatorname{Box}(\tau)^{\mathrm{c}}=\operatorname{Box}(\tau^{\mathrm{c}}), and \operatorname{Box}(\tau)^{-1}=\operatorname{Box}(\tau^{-1}).




If X\subseteq[k-1], a vincular pattern (\tau,X) requires
i_{x+1}=i_{x}+1 for every x\in X. We indicate bonds by underlining; thus
2\underline{41}3 is the pattern for which the entries playing 4 and 1
are adjacent. The notation 2-41-3 is equivalent. A
bivincular pattern (\tau,X,Y) additionally requires that the selected
values of ranks y and y+1 be consecutive for each y\in Y
[9]. Vincular and
bivincular patterns are mesh patterns: position bonds shade complete columns,
and value bonds shade complete rows. For background on all of these families
see Kitaev [20] and the survey of Steingrímsson
[27].




For any pattern P, let \operatorname{Av}(P)=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\operatorname{Av}_{n}(P). Two patterns P and Q are
coincident, written P\mathrel{\equiv_{\rm c}}Q, if \operatorname{Av}_{n}(P)=\operatorname{Av}_{n}(Q) for every n\geq 0. Equivalently, every permutation contains P if and only if it contains Q.
In informal terms, the two pattern-avoidance tests are indistinguishable even
though the patterns may be drawn differently. This is stronger than
Wilf equivalence, which asks only for
|\operatorname{Av}_{n}(P)|=|\operatorname{Av}_{n}(Q)| in every length.




We shall also use the obstruction to coincidence with classical patterns
introduced by Tenner [29]. Write
G(\tau)=\{(i,\tau_{i}):1\leq i\leq k\} for the graph of
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}. A shaded square (a,b)\in R is a
pointless square, or an enclosed diagonal of length one, if its closed
square has no vertex in G(\tau). For c\geq 1, a set of shaded squares




D=\{(a+i,b+i):0\leq i\leq c\}\subseteq R



is an enclosed NE-diagonal if




G(\tau)\cap\{(a+i,b+i):0\leq i\leq c+1\}=\{(a+i,b+i):1\leq i\leq c\}.



Similarly,
D=\{(a+i,b-i):0\leq i\leq c\}\subseteq R is an
enclosed SE-diagonal if




G(\tau)\cap\{(a+i,b+1-i):0\leq i\leq c+1\}=\{(a+i,b+1-i):1\leq i\leq c\}.



Either type has length c+1 and is called proper when its length is
greater than one. This is the square-based formulation in
[13, Definition 3.1]. For a mesh pattern P=(\tau,R), let \operatorname{enc}(P) denote its set
of enclosed diagonals, regarded as sets of shaded squares.



Proposition 2.1 ([13], Lemmas 4.1 and 4.2).



If the mesh patterns P=(\tau,R) and Q=(\sigma,S) are coincident, then \tau=\sigma and \operatorname{enc}(P)=\operatorname{enc}(Q).





Equality of enclosed diagonals is necessary but is not sufficient for general
mesh-pattern coincidence [13]. We shall use the
necessary direction explicitly in the proof of
Theorem 5.2.




A mesh R is called superfluous for \tau if its shading imposes no
additional avoidance condition, that is,
(\tau,R)\mathrel{\equiv_{\rm c}}(\tau,\varnothing). Thus “superfluous” describes the whole
mesh; it does not mean that its cells can necessarily be removed independently.



Theorem 2.2 ([29]).



The mesh R is superfluous for \tau if and only if
\operatorname{enc}((\tau,R))=\varnothing.

3 Boxed patterns as interval-consecutive patterns




For an integer interval I=[a,b], write \pi|_{I} for the subword of \pi
obtained by retaining entries whose values lie in I.



Definition 3.1.



Let \tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}. An occurrence
\pi_{i_{1}}\cdots\pi_{i_{k}} of \tau in \pi is a boxed occurrence if
the open axis-parallel rectangle determined by its leftmost, rightmost, lowest
and highest selected points contains no further point of the plot of \pi.
The corresponding mesh pattern is denoted \operatorname{Box}(\tau).





Because a permutation plot has no repeated abscissae or ordinates, the
condition can equivalently be stated with the closed rectangle: the closed
bounding rectangle contains no plot point other than the selected ones.



Theorem 3.2.



For every \tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k} and every permutation \pi, the following are
equivalent.


(i) 



\pi contains \operatorname{Box}(\tau);


(ii) 



for some interval I of values, the word \pi|_{I} has a consecutive
factor whose standardization is \tau.






Proof.



Suppose first that points with positions i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k} form a boxed
occurrence, and let u and v be the minimum and maximum of their values.
No other point with value in [u,v] can have position strictly between
i_{1} and i_{k}. Thus the selected entries form a consecutive factor of
\pi|_{[u,v]}.




Conversely, suppose a consecutive factor of \pi|_{I} standardizes to \tau.
Let u and v be the minimum and maximum values in this factor. Any extra
point in its bounding rectangle would have value in [u,v]\subseteq I and
position between the first and last entries of the factor. It would therefore
appear inside the factor of \pi|_{I}, a contradiction.
∎





The criterion places boxed avoidance beside simsun-type restrictions, but with
all value intervals rather than only initial intervals. It also gives a uniform
way to search for occurrences: restrict to intervals, then use consecutive
pattern matching. A second equivalent formulation, used in
[3], is obtained by repeatedly deleting the leftmost, rightmost,
smallest or largest entry: \pi contains \operatorname{Box}(\tau) if and only if some such
sequence of external deletions ends at \tau.



Remark 3.3.



Deciding whether a given permutation contains a given boxed pattern has also
been studied algorithmically. Bruner and Lackner [6]
devote a section to boxed mesh patterns and give an O(n^{3}) algorithm; Cho,
Na and Sim [12] improve this to O(n^{2}m) and then to
O(n^{2}\log m), where m is the length of the pattern; and Amit, Bille, Hagge Cording, Li Gørtz and Wedel
Vildhøj [4] give an O(n^{2}) algorithm, which is optimal
because the output can have size \Omega(n^{2}).





The interval formulation also gives a useful inheritance principle. Say that
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{r} is an interval factor of
\tau=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{k} if, for some j and a,




\{\tau_{j},\tau_{j+1},\ldots,\tau_{j+r-1}\}=\{a,a+1,\ldots,a+r-1\}



and \sigma=\operatorname{st}(\tau_{j}\cdots\tau_{j+r-1}). Thus the entries forming
\sigma occupy consecutive positions and consecutive ranks in \tau.



Proposition 3.4.



If \sigma is an interval factor of \tau, then every occurrence of
\operatorname{Box}(\tau) contains an occurrence of \operatorname{Box}(\sigma). Consequently, \operatorname{Av}_{n}(\operatorname{Box}(\sigma))\subseteq\operatorname{Av}_{n}(\operatorname{Box}(\tau)) for n\geq 0. In particular, failure of the Stanley–Wilf property for \operatorname{Box}(\sigma) implies
failure of the property for \operatorname{Box}(\tau).




Proof.



Take the selected points of a boxed \tau occurrence that correspond to the
factor \tau_{j}\cdots\tau_{j+r-1}. No other selected point lies horizontally
between the first and last of these points, because the positions of the
factor are consecutive. No other selected point lies vertically between
their lowest and highest points, because their ranks are consecutive. An
unselected point in the smaller bounding rectangle would also lie in the
bounding rectangle of the original boxed occurrence. Hence the selected
factor is a boxed \sigma occurrence. Taking contrapositives gives the class
inclusion, and the final assertion follows by comparing cardinalities.
∎





For example, Proposition 3.4 and the doubled-letter construction
show immediately that \operatorname{Box}(\tau) has factorially many avoiders whenever
\tau has an interval factor 123 or 321. The “good pattern” argument
of [3] handles many patterns without such a monotone interval
factor and is therefore genuinely stronger than this inheritance principle.

Recall that an occurrence of the vincular pattern 2\underline{41}3 is a
subsequence a,b,c,d in that positional order, with b and c adjacent and c<a<d<b.



Lemma 4.1.



A permutation contains the boxed pattern \operatorname{Box}(2413) if and only if it contains
the vincular pattern 2\underline{41}3.




Proof.



Suppose a,b,c,d is a boxed 2413 occurrence, so
c<a<d<b. Read the entries between b and c. Since the
rectangle is empty, each is either above b or below c. Beginning with b
and ending with c, there is an adjacent transition b^{\prime},c^{\prime} from the upper to
the lower region. Since b^{\prime}\geq b and c^{\prime}\leq c, c<a<d<b gives
c^{\prime}<a<d<b^{\prime}; and b^{\prime} and c^{\prime} lie weakly between b and c in position, so
a precedes b^{\prime} and d follows c^{\prime}. Then a,b^{\prime},c^{\prime},d is an occurrence of
2\underline{41}3.




For the converse, use the external-deletion characterization in [3] discussed in Section 3. It is enough to show that whenever a
permutation of length greater than four contains 2\underline{41}3, one of
its four external entries can be deleted while preserving such an occurrence.
Choose an occurrence a,b,c,d. If an external entry is not among these four,
delete it; the bond survives, since b and c are adjacent and therefore no
entry lies between them. The only remaining case is therefore that a,b,c,d
are respectively the leftmost, largest, smallest and rightmost entries of the
whole permutation; the assignment of roles is forced, because by
c<a<d<b the largest of the four is b and the smallest is c,
while in positional order the first is a and the last is d.
Let x be the entry immediately preceding b.




If x>d, deleting the largest entry b makes x adjacent to c, and a,x,c,d is still an occurrence. Suppose instead that x<d. If x\neq a, then c<x<d<b, so x,b,c,d is an occurrence and the leftmost entry a may be deleted. The only remaining possibility is x=a. Since a is the first entry and b and c are adjacent, the permutation begins with a,b,c. Moreover, d is the last entry, so, because the permutation has length greater than four, the entry y immediately following c exists and satisfies y\neq d. Since c and b are respectively the smallest and largest entries of the permutation, c<y<b. If y>a, then c<a<y<b, so a,b,c,y is an occurrence and d may be deleted. If y<a, then y<a<d<b; deleting the smallest entry c makes b and y adjacent, and a,b,y,d is an occurrence. Thus an external entry can always be deleted while preserving an occurrence. Repeating the argument ends at the permutation 2413, proving boxed containment.
∎




Remark 4.2.



Contrary to what one might first expect, boxed meshes are not generally free of
enclosed diagonals; see Figure 1. The two meshes in
Lemma 4.1 have the same nonempty set of enclosed diagonals: the
pointless square (2,2). Thus the enclosed-diagonal invariant of
[13] is consistent with the coincidence, but
Tenner’s superfluous-mesh theorem [29] does not prove it: that
theorem applies precisely when there are no enclosed diagonals. Nor are the
two meshes nested, so the one-square Shading Lemma
[18, Lemma 3.11] cannot be applied directly. Its
simultaneous extension [13, Section 7] is also a
sufficient local shading criterion, not a converse that would establish this
coincidence. Finally, the coincidence theorem for two vincular patterns does
not apply because the boxed mesh is not vincular. The tightening argument
above is therefore not an immediate consequence of these earlier results; it
supplies the required global movement of the adjacency bond.




Theorem 4.3.



For every n\geq 0, |\operatorname{Av}_{n}(\operatorname{Box}(2413))|=|\operatorname{Av}_{n}(\operatorname{Box}(3142))|=\mathsf{SB}_{n}, where \mathsf{SB}_{n} is the nth semi-Baxter number (OEIS A117106). With
\mathsf{SB}_{0}=\mathsf{SB}_{1}=1, these numbers satisfy




\mathsf{SB}_{n}=\frac{11n^{2}+11n-6}{(n+4)(n+3)}\mathsf{SB}_{n-1}+\frac{(n-3)(n-2)}{(n+4)(n+3)}\mathsf{SB}_{n-2}\qquad(n\geq 2),

(3)


and, for n\geq 2,




\mathsf{SB}_{n}=\frac{24}{(n-1)n^{2}(n+1)(n+2)}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j+2}\binom{n+2}{j}\binom{n+j+2}{j+1}.

(4)





Proof.



By Lemma 4.1, the avoiders of \operatorname{Box}(2413) are the
semi-Baxter permutations, and hence have cardinality \mathsf{SB}_{n}.
Inverse symmetry gives the same enumeration for \operatorname{Box}(3142). The recurrence and sum are the semi-Baxter enumeration of
Bouvel et al. [7, Proposition 13 and Theorem 14].
∎





In particular, \mathsf{SB}_{n}\sim A\mu^{n}n^{-6} with
\mu=((\sqrt{5}-1)/2)^{-5} and an explicit positive constant A
[7]. Boxed 2413 therefore satisfies the Stanley–Wilf
property. It also follows that no injection from \mathfrak{S}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
to \operatorname{Av}_{n}(\operatorname{Box}(2413)) can exist for every n: such an injection would force a
factorial lower bound on an exponentially bounded sequence.




Combining Theorem 4.3 with the factorial constructions in
[3] leaves exactly one unresolved dihedral orbit at length four,
namely \{2143,3412\}.




The combination is worth recording as a single global statement. It also
clarifies that coincidence and growth are separate questions: the
noncoincidence theorem in Section 5 is not needed for the
factorial lower bound in large lengths.



Theorem 4.4.



Let \tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k} with k\geq 4.


(i) 



If
\tau\notin\{2143,3412,2413,3142\}, then |\operatorname{Av}_{n}(\operatorname{Box}(\tau))|\geq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor!,
so \operatorname{Box}(\tau) does not have the Stanley–Wilf property.


(ii) 



If \tau\in\{2413,3142\}, then \operatorname{Box}(\tau) has the Stanley–Wilf
property and is enumerated by the semi-Baxter numbers.


(iii) 



The Stanley–Wilf property remains open for
\tau\in\{2143,3412\}; these two cases are equivalent by complement.



In particular, no boxed pattern of length at least five has the
Stanley–Wilf property.




Proof.



Part (i) is [3, Theorem 3]. Stirling’s formula gives \left(\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor!\right)^{1/n}\sim\sqrt{\frac{n}{2e}},
which tends to infinity. Part (ii) is
Theorem 4.3, and part (iii) follows from complement
symmetry and the fact that neither of the preceding results applies to this
orbit. For k\geq 5 none of the four exceptional permutations in
part (i) has the required length, proving the last assertion.
∎





Together with the short cases, Theorem 4.4 gives an almost
complete classification over all boxed patterns. The Stanley–Wilf property
is known to hold for the boxed patterns with underlying permutations 1,12,21,132,213,231,312,2413,3142;
it is known to fail for every other boxed pattern except 2143 and 3412.





5 Classical, vincular and bivincular coincidences




We first record the classical case, which is now a consequence of the general
coincidence theory for mesh patterns.



Proposition 5.1.



Let \tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}. Then \operatorname{Box}(\tau) is coincident with a classical
pattern if and only if k\leq 2, or k=3 and \tau\in\{132,213,231,312\}. In every case the classical pattern is \tau itself. In particular, no boxed
pattern of length at least four is coincident with a classical pattern.




Proof.



By Proposition 2.1, a coincident classical pattern would
have to be \tau itself. By Theorem 2.2, coincidence with
\tau holds exactly when the full internal box has no enclosed diagonal.
Applying that criterion gives precisely the displayed list; this is also
[3, Proposition 1] and
[29, Corollary 3.8].
∎





The answer changes for vincular patterns, but only in small lengths.



Theorem 5.2.



Let V be a vincular pattern of length k with at least one bond. Then
\operatorname{Box}(\tau)\mathrel{\equiv_{\rm c}}V if and only if the pair occurs in the following list:




\displaystyle k=2:\quad
\displaystyle\operatorname{Box}(12)\mathrel{\equiv_{\rm c}}\underline{12},
\displaystyle\operatorname{Box}(21)\mathrel{\equiv_{\rm c}}\underline{21};



\displaystyle k=3:\quad
\displaystyle\operatorname{Box}(132)\mathrel{\equiv_{\rm c}}\underline{13}2,
\displaystyle\operatorname{Box}(213)\mathrel{\equiv_{\rm c}}2\underline{13},




\displaystyle\operatorname{Box}(231)\mathrel{\equiv_{\rm c}}2\underline{31},
\displaystyle\operatorname{Box}(312)\mathrel{\equiv_{\rm c}}\underline{31}2;



\displaystyle k=4:\quad
\displaystyle\operatorname{Box}(2413)\mathrel{\equiv_{\rm c}}2\underline{41}3,
\displaystyle\operatorname{Box}(3142)\mathrel{\equiv_{\rm c}}3\underline{14}2.



In particular, there is no boxed–vincular coincidence in length k\geq 5.




Proof.



Write V=(\sigma,X). If \operatorname{Box}(\tau)\mathrel{\equiv_{\rm c}}V, the first necessary condition
in Proposition 2.1 gives \sigma=\tau, while the second
gives




\operatorname{enc}(\operatorname{Box}(\tau))=\operatorname{enc}(V).

(5)


We use this equality repeatedly.




Fix a bond c\in X. It shades the complete column c in V, including the
two boundary cells (c,0) and (c,k), neither of which is shaded in the boxed
mesh. If (c,0) touched no point of G(\tau), it would be a pointless
enclosed diagonal of V that is absent from \operatorname{Box}(\tau), contradicting
(5). It must therefore touch one of
(c,1) and (c+1,1), so one of \tau_{c},\tau_{c+1} is 1. Applying the
same argument to (c,k) shows that one of them is k. Hence




\{\tau_{c},\tau_{c+1}\}=\{1,k\}.

(6)


Since the values 1 and k occur only once, two distinct bonds cannot both
satisfy (6); consequently X=\{c\}.




Applying complement to both patterns if necessary, we may assume that \tau_{c}=1 and \tau_{c+1}=k. Recall that V shades only column c. Every internal cell outside
column c must therefore touch a point of G(\tau); otherwise it would
be a pointless enclosed diagonal of \operatorname{Box}(\tau) that is absent from V,
contradicting (5).




Suppose first that c\leq k-2, so that column c+1 is an internal column.
Its k-1 internal cells can be touched only by the two plot points (c+1,\tau_{c+1})=(c+1,k) and (c+2,\tau_{c+2}). The first point touches only one internal cell of column c+1, while the
second touches at most two. Thus at most three of the k-1 internal cells
in this column can touch G(\tau). Since k\geq 5, at least one of these
cells is pointless, a contradiction. Hence c=k-1.




Similarly, if c\geq 2, consider the internal column c-1. Its cells can
be touched only by (c-1,\tau_{c-1}) and (c,\tau_{c})=(c,1). Again, the extreme-valued point touches only one internal cell and the other
point touches at most two. For k\geq 5, this leaves a pointless cell in
column c-1, so c=1.




We have obtained both c=k-1 and c=1, which would force k=2.
This contradicts k\geq 5, and therefore no boxed–vincular coincidence can
occur in length at least five.

6 The boxed mesh pattern
123




No closed formula is known for the number of permutations avoiding
\operatorname{Box}(123). In this section we give an exact maximum-insertion identity,
use it to extend the known sequence, and prove a subfactorial upper bound.
We then obtain a pattern-independent first-moment formula; in particular, the
mean number of \operatorname{Box}(123) occurrences is (n+2)H_{n}-3n\sim n\log n, where
H_{n} is the n-th harmonic number.




6.1 Exact enumeration




Let \mathcal{A}_{n}=\operatorname{Av}_{n}(\operatorname{Box}(123)) and a_{n}=|\mathcal{A}_{n}|. For a word w=w_{1}\cdots w_{t} with distinct entries, set R_{i}(w)=|\{j:i<j\leq t,\ w_{j}>w_{i}\}| and \mathcal{Q}_{t}=\{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{t}:R_{i}(w)\neq 1\text{ for all }i\}.
We call the gap after the first t entries of \pi active if insertion of the
new maximum in that gap preserves boxed 123 avoidance.



Theorem 6.1.



Let \pi\in\mathcal{A}_{n}, and let w=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{t}. The gap following
w is active if and only if \operatorname{st}(w)\in\mathcal{Q}_{t}. Moreover, |\mathcal{Q}_{0}|=1 and |\mathcal{Q}_{t}|=(t-1)! for t\geq 1. Consequently,




a_{n+1}=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\sum_{t=0}^{n}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\operatorname{st}(\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{t})\in\mathcal{Q}_{t}\right].

(9)


Here \mathbf{1}[\mathcal{E}] denotes the indicator of a statement
\mathcal{E}: it equals 1 when \mathcal{E} is true and 0
otherwise.




Proof.



Any new boxed 123 occurrence must use the inserted maximum as its last and
largest selected entry. If its first selected entry is w_{i}, the boxed
condition says that exactly one entry to the right of w_{i} in the prefix w
is larger than w_{i}: this unique entry is the middle selected point. This
condition is also sufficient, since every other entry between w_{i} and the
new maximum is then below w_{i} and hence outside the bounding rectangle.
Thus the gap is inactive precisely when some R_{i}(w) equals one.




For the enumeration, recall that the Lehmer code of
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{t}\in\mathfrak{S}_{t} is




\ell_{i}(w)=|\{j:i<j\leq t,\ w_{j}<w_{i}\}|,\qquad 1\leq i\leq t.



The map w\longmapsto(\ell_{1}(w),\ldots,\ell_{t}(w)) is a bijection between \mathfrak{S}_{t} and the set of vectors satisfying
0\leq\ell_{i}\leq t-i. Indeed, the permutation can be reconstructed successively by choosing w_{i}
to be the (\ell_{i}+1)-st smallest of the values not yet used.




Since every entry to the right of w_{i} is either smaller or larger than
w_{i}, we have R_{i}(w)=t-i-\ell_{i}(w). Thus (R_{1}(w),\ldots,R_{t}(w)), which we call the
complementary Lehmer code, is also a bijective code in which the
coordinates may independently take the values




R_{i}(w)\in\{0,1,\ldots,t-i\}.



For i<t, excluding the value 1 leaves t-i choices, while
R_{t}(w)=0 has one choice. Therefore
|\mathcal{Q}_{t}|=\prod_{i=1}^{t-1}(t-i)=(t-1)!.
∎

6.2 A subfactorial upper bound




The factorial lower bound for a_{n} rules out the Stanley–Wilf property, but
it does not determine how close the avoidance sequence is to n!. The next
result gives a genuinely subfactorial upper bound.



Theorem 6.2.



Let 0<x\leq 1 satisfy




2x+\sqrt{x}\leq x(1+\sqrt{x})^{2}.

(11)


Then, for every n\geq 1,




a_{n}\leq 2^{5n}n^{\,n\log_{2}(1+\sqrt{x})}.

(12)


The smallest admissible value is x^{*}=\left(2\cos\frac{\pi}{7}-1\right)^{2}, and hence




a_{n}\leq 2^{5n}n^{\beta n},\qquad\beta=\log_{2}\!\left(2\cos\frac{\pi}{7}\right)\approx 0.8496.

(13)






Consequently,




\frac{1}{2}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log a_{n}}{n\log n}\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log a_{n}}{n\log n}\leq\beta<1.

(14)


Here the lower bound follows from the doubled-letter injection recalled in the
introduction, together with the fact that a_{n} is nondecreasing. Indeed,
inserting a new maximum in front of an avoider creates no boxed 123
occurrence, because the new entry has no entry to its left and can only play
the last selected point; hence
a_{n}\geq a_{2\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\geq\lfloor n/2\rfloor! for every n. We call
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\log a_{n}/(n\log n) the growth exponent of
\operatorname{Av}(\operatorname{Box}(123)); by (14) it lies between
\tfrac{1}{2} and \beta. The upper bound also shows that a uniformly random
permutation avoids \operatorname{Box}(123) with probability at most n^{-(1-\beta)n+O(n/\log n)}.




To prove the theorem, for a permutation \pi let
\operatorname{asc}(\pi) be its number of ascents, and, for 0<x\leq 1, set




Z_{n}(x)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}x^{\operatorname{asc}(\pi)},\qquad\tau(x)=\frac{2x+\sqrt{x}}{(1+\sqrt{x})^{2}}.



For 0<x\leq 1 one has 0<\tau(x)\leq 1.



Lemma 6.3.



For n\geq 2, with h_{1}=\lfloor n/2\rfloor and
h_{2}=\lceil n/2\rceil, one has




Z_{n}(x)\leq\frac{2(1+\sqrt{x})^{n-1}}{\tau(x)}Z_{h_{1}}(\tau(x))Z_{h_{2}}(\tau(x)).

(15)





Proof.



For \pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}, call the entries with values at most h_{1}
low and the remaining entries high. Let
L\in\mathfrak{S}_{h_{1}} and H\in\mathfrak{S}_{h_{2}} be the patterns of the
low and high subsequences. Number the gaps around the low entries from 0 to
h_{1}, and number the slots between consecutive high entries from 1 to
h_{2}-1. Define




\displaystyle U
\displaystyle=\{i:\text{low gap $i$ contains a high entry}\},



\displaystyle D
\displaystyle=\{j:\text{high slot $j$ contains a low entry}\}.



The nonempty low gaps contain the maximal blocks of high entries. Thus
|D|=|U|-1, and \pi is recovered from (L,H,U,D) by cutting H at the
slots in D and placing the resulting blocks in the gaps in U.




Put T=|U\setminus\{0\}|. This is the number of adjacent low–high pairs,
and T\geq|D|. Let I_{L} be the number of ascents i of L for which
i\notin U, and let I_{H} be the number of ascents j of H for which
j\notin D. For \pi\in\mathcal{A}_{n} the following elementary restrictions
hold:


(i) 



L\in\mathcal{A}_{h_{1}} and H\in\mathcal{A}_{h_{2}};


(ii) 



neither L nor H has two consecutive ascents;


(iii) 



if i is an ascent of L, then it is not the case that
i\notin U and i+1\in U;


(iv) 



if j\geq 2 is an ascent of H, then it is not the case that
j-1\in D and j\notin D.



For (i), a point inside the rectangle of an occurrence among the low entries
would itself be low, and similarly for the high entries. For (ii), three
entries occupying consecutive positions of L and increasing in value form a
boxed occurrence in \pi: between them there are no other low entries, while
all intervening high entries lie above their vertical range. The argument for H is
analogous. In (iii) the entries L_{i},L_{i+1} followed by the first high
entry in gap i+1 occupy three consecutive positions of \pi and increase;
in (iv), use the last low entry of slot j-1 followed by H_{j},H_{j+1}.
These triples would be boxed occurrences, proving the restrictions.




Every adjacent ascent of \pi is either low–high, low–low, or high–high,
and therefore




\operatorname{asc}(\pi)=T+I_{L}+I_{H}.

(16)


Let E_{L} be the set of ascents of L, and let E_{H} be the set of ascents
j\geq 2 of H. From (16), T\geq|D|, and
0<x\leq 1, we obtain x^{\operatorname{asc}(\pi)}\leq f(U)g(D), where




f(U)=x^{|U\setminus\{0\}|/2}x^{|\{i\in E_{L}:i\notin U\}|},\ \ \ \ g(D)=x^{|D|/2}x^{|\{j\in E_{H}:j\notin D\}|}.



If 1 is an ascent of H, its contribution has been omitted from g; since
x\leq 1, this only enlarges the right-hand side.




By (ii), the pairs \{i,i+1\} with i\in E_{L} are disjoint. In summing
f(U) over all subsets U\subseteq\{0,1,\ldots,h_{1}\} allowed by (iii), the
index 0 contributes 2, each
index in no such pair contributes 1+\sqrt{x}, and each pair contributes x+\sqrt{x}+x=2x+\sqrt{x}; the fourth membership pattern is excluded by (iii). Hence




\sum_{U}f(U)=2(1+\sqrt{x})^{h_{1}}\tau(x)^{|E_{L}|}.



The same calculation, carried out over all subsets
D\subseteq\{1,\ldots,h_{2}-1\} allowed by (iv), gives




\sum_{D}g(D)=(1+\sqrt{x})^{h_{2}-1}\tau(x)^{|E_{H}|}.



The encoding by (L,H,U,D) is injective, and every \pi\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
yields a pair (U,D) allowed by (iii) and (iv) and satisfying
|D|=|U|-1. Dropping that last relation enlarges the set of encodings summed
over, so summing over all allowed pairs (U,D) gives




\displaystyle Z_{n}(x)
\displaystyle\leq 2(1+\sqrt{x})^{n-1}\sum_{L\in\mathcal{A}_{h_{1}}}\sum_{H\in\mathcal{A}_{h_{2}}}\tau(x)^{|E_{L}|+|E_{H}|}




\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(1+\sqrt{x})^{n-1}}{\tau(x)}Z_{h_{1}}(\tau(x))Z_{h_{2}}(\tau(x)),



because |E_{L}|+|E_{H}|\geq\operatorname{asc}(L)+\operatorname{asc}(H)-1 and \tau(x)\leq 1.
∎

6.3 Average occurrence counts for boxed patterns




For \tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k} define




F_{n,\tau}(q)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}q^{N_{\operatorname{Box}(\tau)}(\pi)},

(17)


where N_{\operatorname{Box}(\tau)}(\pi) is the number of occurrences of \operatorname{Box}(\tau) in
\pi. Its constant term is the avoidance number: F_{n,\tau}(0)=|\operatorname{Av}_{n}(\operatorname{Box}(\tau))|. For uniformly random \pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}, its derivative satisfies




\frac{F^{\prime}_{n,\tau}(1)}{n!}=\mathbb{E}\bigl[N_{\operatorname{Box}(\tau)}(\pi)\bigr].

(18)


The full occurrence distribution for \operatorname{Box}(123) is open, but the first moment
has a simple closed form. In fact, it is independent of the choice of the
underlying pattern of a fixed length.



Proposition 6.4.



Let k\geq 2, let \tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}, and write
N_{\operatorname{Box}(\tau)}(\pi) as in (17). If
\pi is uniformly distributed over \mathfrak{S}_{n}, where n\geq k, then




\mathbb{E}\bigl[N_{\operatorname{Box}(\tau)}(\pi)\bigr]=\frac{(n+k-1)(H_{n}-H_{k-2})-2(n-k+2)}{(k-2)!},

(19)


where H_{m}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}1/j is the m-th harmonic number and H_{0}=0. In particular,




\mathbb{E}\bigl[N_{\operatorname{Box}(123)}(\pi)\bigr]=(n+2)H_{n}-3n.

(20)


Moreover, for fixed n and k, the expectation in
(19) is independent of the choice of
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}: all boxed patterns of length k have the same first
moment on \mathfrak{S}_{n}.




Proof.



Fix selected positions
I=\{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\} and put d=i_{k}-i_{1}. The block from i_{1} to
i_{k} has length d+1, and its relative order is uniformly distributed over
\mathfrak{S}_{d+1}. The selected entries form an occurrence of
\operatorname{Box}(\tau) exactly when they receive k consecutive ranks in this block,
arranged in the order \tau. There are d-k+2 choices for this interval of
ranks, after which the other d-k+1 ranks may be arranged arbitrarily.
Consequently,




\Pr\bigl(I\text{ is a }\operatorname{Box}(\tau)\text{-occurrence}\bigr)=\frac{(d-k+2)!}{(d+1)!}.



For a fixed span d, there are
(n-d)\binom{d-1}{k-2} possible sets I. Linearity of expectation therefore
gives




\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[N_{\operatorname{Box}(\tau)}(\pi)\bigr]
\displaystyle=\sum_{d=k-1}^{n-1}(n-d)\binom{d-1}{k-2}\frac{(d-k+2)!}{(d+1)!}




\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(k-2)!}\sum_{d=k-1}^{n-1}\frac{(n-d)(d-k+2)}{d(d+1)}




\displaystyle=\frac{(n+k-1)(H_{n}-H_{k-2})-2(n-k+2)}{(k-2)!}.



Setting k=3 proves (20).
∎




Remark 6.5.



Since
H_{n}=\log n+\gamma+O(1/n),
where \gamma is the Euler–Mascheroni constant,
(20) gives \mathbb{E}\bigl[N_{\operatorname{Box}(123)}(\pi)\bigr]=n\log n+(\gamma-3)n+O(\log n). Thus a uniformly random permutation of length n has n\log n+O(n) boxed
123 occurrences on average. This identifies the natural
scale of the statistic, although a first-moment estimate alone does not imply
concentration or determine the probability of avoidance.







7 The remaining orbit
\{2143,3412\}




There is a useful one-sided analogue of the tightening lemma from
Section 4. Recall that an occurrence of
2\underline{14}3 has the form a,x,y,d in positional order, where x and
y are adjacent and x<a<d<y.



Lemma 7.1.



Every permutation containing \operatorname{Box}(2143) contains
2\underline{14}3. Equivalently,




\operatorname{Av}_{n}(2\underline{14}3)\subseteq\operatorname{Av}_{n}(\operatorname{Box}(2143))\qquad(n\geq 0).






Proof.



Let a,b,c,d be a boxed 2143 occurrence in positional order, so b<a<d<c.
Every entry strictly between b and c in position lies either below b or
above c; an entry with value strictly between them would lie in the empty
bounding rectangle. Read the permutation from b to c, regarding b as
belonging to the lower region and c as belonging to the upper region. At
some point there is an adjacent transition x,y from the lower region to the
upper region. Hence x\leq b<a<d<c\leq y. The inequalities relevant to the four distinct entries a,x,y,d are strict,
so these entries form an occurrence of 2\underline{14}3.
The avoidance-class inclusion is the contrapositive.
∎




Remark 7.2.



The reverse implication in Lemma 7.1 is false. For
example, 24153 contains 2\underline{14}3 in the entries 2,1,5,3, but
avoids \operatorname{Box}(2143): the intervening entry 4 obstructs the only candidate.
This is also forced by the classification in
Theorem 5.2. Moreover,
2\underline{14}3-avoiders are the plane permutations and are enumerated by
the semi-Baxter numbers [7]. Thus the lemma gives only the
exponential lower bound \mathsf{SB}_{n}\leq|\operatorname{Av}_{n}(\operatorname{Box}(2143))|. The inclusion has the wrong direction for an exponential upper bound and is
far too small to yield a factorial lower bound. Its value is structural: it
identifies the semi-Baxter class as a canonical subclass of the remaining
boxed avoidance class and explains why tightening alone cannot resolve the
Stanley–Wilf question.





We next observe that an injection from \mathfrak{S}_{m} into avoiders whose
length is an affine function of m is sufficient to disprove the
Stanley–Wilf property. The observation applies to any mesh pattern,
not only to boxed ones.



Proposition 7.3.



Let P be a fixed mesh pattern. Suppose that there is a
constant integer c\geq 1, an integer r, and, for all sufficiently large
m, an injection




\Phi_{m}:\mathfrak{S}_{m}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Av}_{cm+r}(P),



where cm+r\geq 0.
Then P does not have the Stanley–Wilf property.




Proof.



Writing A_{n}=|\operatorname{Av}_{n}(P)|, injectivity gives A_{cm+r}\geq m!. Hence, by
Stirling’s formula,




A_{cm+r}^{1/(cm+r)}\geq(m!)^{1/(cm+r)}\sim\left(\frac{m}{e}\right)^{1/c}\longrightarrow\infty.



Thus \limsup_{n\to\infty}A_{n}^{1/n}=\infty.
∎





Consequently there is no need to insist on doubling: an embedding into
permutations of length 2m-1, 3m, 4m, or, more generally, cm+r for
fixed c and r would be equally decisive. Increasing c may provide more
room for blockers, although the extra entries can also create new occurrences.




Here is a concrete format in which such an injection may exist. For
\pi=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{m}\in\mathfrak{S}_{m} and
\rho=\rho_{1}\cdots\rho_{m-1}\in\mathfrak{S}_{m-1}, define the strict
interleaving




J_{\rho}(\pi)=(2\pi_{1}-1,2\rho_{1},2\pi_{2}-1,2\rho_{2},\ldots,2\rho_{m-1},2\pi_{m}-1)\in\mathfrak{S}_{2m-1}.

(21)


For m=1 the even word is empty. Thus the prescribed odd entries occur in
their original order, and exactly one even entry is placed in each internal
gap. Equivalently, one permutes the set
\{2,4,\ldots,2m-2\} between the fixed odd entries
2\pi_{1}-1,\ldots,2\pi_{m}-1.




The odd-position entries recover the input without any auxiliary data:




\pi_{i}=\frac{J_{\rho}(\pi)_{2i-1}+1}{2}.

(22)


For example, if \pi=231 and \rho=21, then J_{21}(231)=(3,4,5,2,1), which avoids \operatorname{Box}(2143), and its odd-position entries 3,5,1 recover
231 by (22).




There is also a simple reason that formulations beginning with a forced
global minimum can be shortened. If a permutation beginning with 1 avoids
\operatorname{Box}(2143), then deleting this 1 and reducing all other entries by one
preserves avoidance. Conversely, adjoining a new global minimum on the left
preserves avoidance, since the minimum in the underlying pattern 2143 is
its second entry and therefore cannot be played by the new leftmost entry.
This is a special case of the general observation that deleting a global
extremum cannot uncover a boxed occurrence: the deleted point lies outside
the vertical bounding interval of every occurrence formed by the remaining
entries.



Conjecture 7.4.



Let m\geq 1. For every \pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}, there exists
\rho\in\mathfrak{S}_{m-1} such that J_{\rho}(\pi) avoids \operatorname{Box}(2143).





The conjecture has been verified exhaustively for m\leq 8. If true, choose
the lexicographically first admissible \rho; equation
(22) then gives an explicit injection




\mathfrak{S}_{m}\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Av}_{2m-1}(\operatorname{Box}(2143)),\qquad\pi\longmapsto J_{\rho(\pi)}(\pi),



and hence, by Proposition 7.3, the factorial lower
bound sought in [3]. Conjecture 7.4 is stronger than the growth conclusion that
it would imply, since factorial growth might arise from a different
construction. We therefore record the expected growth statement separately.



Conjecture 7.5.



The avoidance sequence of \operatorname{Box}(2143) has factorial growth, and hence
\operatorname{Box}(2143) does not have the Stanley–Wilf property.





The following occurrence-preserving doubling provides a first step toward the
conjecture. For \pi=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{m}\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}, define




\Delta(\pi)=(2\pi_{1}-1)(2\pi_{1})\,(2\pi_{2}-1)(2\pi_{2})\cdots(2\pi_{m}-1)(2\pi_{m}).

(23)


Thus each entry is replaced by an adjacent increasing pair of consecutive
values, and the entries in the odd positions recover \pi.



Proposition 7.6.



For every \pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{m},




N_{\operatorname{Box}(2143)}(\Delta(\pi))=N_{\operatorname{Box}(2143)}(\pi).

(24)


In particular, \pi avoids \operatorname{Box}(2143) if and only if \Delta(\pi) does.




Proof.



Call the two entries replacing x the x-block. A 2143 occurrence in
\Delta(\pi) uses at most one entry from any block. Indeed, two entries from
one block would be adjacent selected entries in increasing order. The only
adjacent ascent in 2143 is from its second selected entry, the minimum, to
its third, the maximum; two consecutive values cannot play these roles while
leaving two further selected values strictly between them.




Write \varphi for the projection that sends a selected entry of the
x-block to x; it is well defined on the selected entries by the previous
paragraph, since each block contributes at most one of them. It follows that
the four selected blocks of any 2143 occurrence project under \varphi to
four entries a,b,c,d of \pi, in positional order, with
b<a<d<c. If the occurrence in \Delta(\pi) is boxed, then the projected
occurrence is boxed as well: any intervening entry x of \pi with
b<x<c would place both entries of its block inside the bounding rectangle
in \Delta(\pi).




Conversely, let a,b,c,d be a boxed 2143 occurrence of \pi. It has the
unique boxed lift




2a,\quad 2b,\quad 2c-1,\quad 2d-1.

(25)


To see uniqueness, choosing 2a-1 would put its mate 2a immediately to the
right inside the rectangle; choosing 2b-1 would put 2b inside; choosing
2c would put 2c-1 inside; and choosing 2d would put 2d-1 inside.
The choices in (25) avoid these obstructions. Their
unselected mates lie, respectively, to the left of the rectangle, below it,
above it, and to its right. Any other point inside the lifted rectangle would
belong to an intervening block x with b<x<c, contradicting that the
original occurrence was boxed. Thus \varphi and this unique lift are
mutually inverse maps between the boxed 2143 occurrences of \Delta(\pi)
and those of \pi, proving (24).
∎





Thus increasing doubling itself creates no additional boxed 2143 occurrences.
It supplies a canonical alternating extension of length 2m whenever the
prescribed odd subsequence already avoids the pattern. This is not yet the
strict length-(2m-1) completion required by Conjecture 7.4; for a
general input, the remaining task is to arrange the even entries so as to
destroy the projected occurrences without creating new ones.

For \pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}, let \ell(\pi)=|\{(i,j):1\leq i<j\leq n,\ \pi_{i}>\pi_{j}\}| be its inversion number. If 1\leq i<j\leq n, let \pi(i\leftrightarrow j)
be obtained by interchanging the entries in positions i and j. The
strong Bruhat order on \mathfrak{S}_{n} is the transitive closure of the
covering relations




\pi\lessdot\pi(i\leftrightarrow j)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\ell\bigl(\pi(i\leftrightarrow j)\bigr)=\ell(\pi)+1.



The up-degree of \pi is d_{+}(\pi)=|\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}:\pi\lessdot\sigma\}|;
the down-degree d_{-}(\pi) is defined analogously by counting
\sigma\lessdot\pi. See Adin and Roichman [1] for these
statistics and Bouvel, Ferrari and Tenner
[8] for descriptions of Bruhat covering relations
in mesh-pattern language.




Let b(\pi) denote the number of occurrences of \operatorname{Box}(12) in \pi.



Proposition 8.1.



For every permutation \pi, b(\pi)=d_{+}(\pi).




Proof.



Choose i<j with a=\pi_{i}<\pi_{j}=b. Interchanging a and b changes the
inversion number by 1+2|\{k:i<k<j,\ a<\pi_{k}<b\}|. It therefore gives an upward Bruhat cover exactly when no entry strictly
between positions i and j has value strictly between a and b. This is
precisely the empty-cell condition for the selected pair to be an occurrence
of \operatorname{Box}(12).
∎




Remark 8.2.



Kitaev and Liese [21, Theorem 12] proved that b has
Catalan distribution on \operatorname{Av}_{n}(132): for 0\leq k\leq n-1,




\bigl|\{\pi\in\operatorname{Av}_{n}(132):b(\pi)=k\}\bigr|=C(n-1,k)=\frac{n-k}{n}\binom{n-1+k}{n-1},



where C(r,k) denotes the (r,k) entry of Catalan’s triangle. In view of
Proposition 8.1, this also gives the distribution of the strong
Bruhat up-degree on the Catalan class \operatorname{Av}_{n}(132).





In the notation of (17), define its
distribution polynomial by




D_{n}(q)=F_{n,12}(q)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}q^{b(\pi)}.



Complementation interchanges up-degree and down-degree. Consequently the
results of Adin and Roichman
[1, Propositions 2.1 and 2.9, and Theorem 4.1]
translate as follows.



Corollary 8.3.



For n\geq 1, we have




\displaystyle\deg D_{n}(q)
\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor,

(26)


\displaystyle[q^{\lfloor n^{2}/4\rfloor}]D_{n}(q)
\displaystyle=\begin{cases}n,&n\text{ odd},\\
n/2,&n\text{ even},\end{cases}

(27)


\displaystyle\frac{D_{n}^{\prime}(1)}{n!}
\displaystyle=(n+1)H_{n}-2n,

(28)


where H_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}1/j is the n-th harmonic number.




Remark 8.4.



The familiar stronger algebraic properties do not hold in general. Already




D_{3}(q)=1+2q+3q^{2}



has nonreal zeros. Moreover, for every n\geq 3 the constant coefficient is
1, whereas (27) shows that the leading coefficient is
greater than 1. Thus D_{n}(q) is not palindromic, ruling out
\gamma-positivity in its usual palindromic sense. Log-concavity also fails:
the polynomial D_{7}(q) recorded in [28] has [q^{10}]D_{7}(q)=129, [q^{11}]D_{7}(q)=26, [q^{12}]D_{7}(q)=7,
and 26^{2}<129\cdot 7. On the other hand, exhaustive enumeration gives a
unimodal coefficient sequence for every D_{n}(q) with n\leq 11.





This evidence suggests that unimodality may be the appropriate surviving
property.



Conjecture 8.5.



For every n\geq 0, the coefficient sequence of D_{n}(q) is unimodal.

p_{n,k}=(n-2)!\bigl(H_{n-2}-H_{k}\bigr)\qquad(1\leq k\leq n-3),

Equation (28) is also the k=2 case of
Proposition 6.4. The following direct derivation refines it by
the positional span of an occurrence. Fix positions i<j and put
d=j-i. In the block \pi_{i}\cdots\pi_{j} the two endpoint ranks are a
uniformly distributed ordered pair of distinct elements of [d+1]. They
form a boxed 12 occurrence exactly when the right endpoint has rank one
larger than the left endpoint. There are d such pairs out of d(d+1), so




\Pr\bigl((i,j)\text{ is a }\operatorname{Box}(12)\text{-occurrence}\bigr)=\frac{1}{d+1}.



Linearity of expectation gives




\mathbb{E}[b(\pi)]=\sum_{d=1}^{n-1}\frac{n-d}{d+1}=(n+1)H_{n}-2n.



Equivalently, the exponential generating function for the total number of
occurrences is




\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{D_{n}^{\prime}(1)}{n!}x^{n}=\frac{-\log(1-x)-x}{(1-x)^{2}}.

(29)





Maximum insertion gives an exact identity for the full distribution, although
it does not close in the single sequence of polynomials D_{n}(q). For a word
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{t} with distinct entries, let \operatorname{rlmax}(w)=|\{i\in[t]:w_{i}>w_{j}\text{ for every }i<j\leq t\}| be its number of right-to-left maxima, and set
\operatorname{rlmax}(\varepsilon)=0.



Proposition 8.7.



For \pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n} and 0\leq t\leq n, let \pi^{(t)}=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{t}\,(n+1)\,\pi_{t+1}\cdots\pi_{n}. Then




b(\pi^{(t)})=b(\pi)+\operatorname{rlmax}(\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{t}).

(30)


Consequently,




D_{n+1}(q)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}q^{b(\pi)}\sum_{t=0}^{n}q^{\operatorname{rlmax}(\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{t})}.

(31)





Proof.



Inserting the new maximum neither creates nor destroys an occurrence using
only old entries. A new occurrence must end at n+1. Its first entry
\pi_{i}, where i\leq t, forms a boxed 12 occurrence with n+1 exactly
when no later entry of the prefix \pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{t} is larger than \pi_{i},
that is, exactly when \pi_{i} is a right-to-left maximum of the prefix. This
proves (30); summing over the n+1 insertion sites and
all parents proves (31).
∎





Formula (31) shows both the promise and the
difficulty of maximum insertion: a closed recurrence must retain the joint
distribution of b(\pi) with the right-to-left-maximum counts of all
prefixes. We do not know a finite catalytic refinement that accomplishes
this, and the full polynomial D_{n}(q) remains open.





9 Concluding directions




The growth classification is complete except for one length-four orbit.
Theorem 4.4, together with the factorial construction of
[3], shows that no boxed pattern of length at least five has the
Stanley–Wilf property. In length four, twenty patterns have factorial lower
bounds, the orbit \{2413,3142\} has semi-Baxter and hence exponential
growth, and only \{2143,3412\} remains unresolved. We conjecture that this orbit has factorial
growth; see Conjecture 7.5.




The coincidence results also reveal a sharp length boundary.
Theorem 5.2 shows that boxed–vincular
coincidences stop in length four, while Theorem 5.4
shows that the genuinely bivincular coincidence in
Corollary 5.3 is also maximal in length. The
interval-consecutive criterion provides a complementary structural viewpoint:
boxed \tau avoidance is consecutive \tau avoidance on every interval of
values. Moreover, Proposition 3.4 transfers factorial
lower bounds from interval factors to larger boxed patterns.




For boxed 123, Theorem 6.1 gives an exact maximum-insertion
identity and five new terms, while Theorem 6.2 bounds its
growth exponent by a constant below 0.85. A closed enumeration remains
open. Proposition 6.4 also shows that all boxed
patterns of a fixed length have the same first moment; in particular, the
expected number of boxed 123 occurrences is asymptotic to n\log n.




Finally, for boxed 12, Proposition 8.1 identifies the occurrence
statistic with up-degree in the strong Bruhat order. Although
real-rootedness, \gamma-positivity and log-concavity fail in small lengths,
the available data motivate Conjecture 8.5, which asserts
unimodality of the distribution polynomials.
