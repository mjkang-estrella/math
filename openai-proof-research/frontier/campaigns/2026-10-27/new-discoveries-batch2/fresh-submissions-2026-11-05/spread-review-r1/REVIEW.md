## Independent mathematical audit

Let A be the real 0/1 adjacency matrix, with zero diagonal, d_i its row sums, D=diag(d_i), L=D−A, C=A+Aᵀ and H=D−C/2. Write E for the real zero-sum subspace. Balance is vertexwise equality of row and column sums, not equality of all vertex degrees. It gives C1=2D1 and H1=0. H is symmetric, H_ii=d_i. Neither connectivity nor strong connectivity is used. Opposite arcs are allowed: C_ij can be 2.

For z=x+iy, direct expansion gives Re(z*Lz)=xᵀHx+yᵀHy. Complex orthogonality to 1 is equivalent to both real sums being zero; conjugating the sum does not change this equivalence. Norm one gives ||x||²+||y||²=1. Each nonzero real part can therefore be normalized, and the displayed value is a convex combination of two real unit-vector values. Conversely a real admissible vector is complex admissible. This proves equality of extrema, not merely containment in one direction. The real sphere in E is nonempty (normalize e_i−e_j) and compact when n≥2; its quadratic form is continuous. Both extrema are attained. The complex restricted sphere is compact too. Thus the source min/max exist, equal the infimum/supremum of the real-part set, and their difference W is nonnegative. No assertion about nonnormal eigenvalue spread is required.

For p_i=e_i−1/n, sum p_i=0 and ||p_i||²=(n−1)/n>0. Since H1=0 and 1ᵀH=0, p_iᵀHp_i=H_ii=d_i. Normalization gives n d_i/(n−1). If degrees differ, their integral gap is at least one, hence W≥n/(n−1)>1. There is no hidden regularity hypothesis; this is exactly the necessary irregular branch.

Otherwise all d_i=d and H=dI−C/2. If all off-diagonal entries of C are c, C=c(J−I), so H|E=(d+c/2)I and W=0. No assertion that the complex numerical range itself is a point is made: a regular tournament can have a vertical segment with zero real width.

In the remaining branch, failure of an exterior column difference would say that, for each k, all C_ki with i≠k coincide. Symmetry identifies these row constants, so all off-diagonal entries would coincide. Thus distinct i,j,k with C_ki≠C_kj exist. Define w=e_i−e_j and v_i=v_j=0, v_k=C_ki−C_kj elsewhere. Equal column sums and the symmetric zero diagonal cancel the two omitted coordinates, giving sum v=0. v is nonzero integral, so it has a positive and a negative coordinate. Therefore s=||v||²≥2. This is ambient-coordinate integrality only; no orthonormal compression is asserted integral and no eigenvalue is asserted integral.

At i,j, Cw has entries −C_ij,+C_ij; outside these coordinates it equals v. Consequently Cw=v−C_ij w, wᵀv=0 and wᵀCv=s. The vectors u=w/√2 and t=v/√s are orthogonal unit vectors in E. Their mixed C value is s/√(2s)=√(s/2)≥1. Both (u+t)/√2 and (u−t)/√2 are real admissible unit vectors. Their C quadratic values differ by 2uᵀCt≥2. Their H values differ in the opposite order by uᵀCt≥1: the scalar d cancels and the factor −1/2 is essential. Therefore W≥1. All divisions and square roots have strictly positive denominators.

At n=2, balance forces A_12=A_21; E is one-dimensional and every quadratic form has constant unit-sphere value. The scalar branch covers both the empty graph and the two opposite arcs. The irregular/nonconstant branches cannot arise. At n=3 the regular nonconstant branch is also impossible, consistently with the need for a nonzero zero-sum v supported outside two coordinates. At n=1 the source explicitly has empty restricted range; excluding it is faithful rather than a missing positive-spread case. Weighted graphs are not claimed: arbitrarily small positive weights can defeat this discrete gap mechanism.

The paper records Mirsky's Hermitian entry bounds at equation (1.7), p.163: s(B)≥2 max_{i≠j}|b_ij|, and s(B)≥max_{i≠j}√((b_ii−b_jj)²+4|b_ij|²). These are actual body statements, not an abstract-based analogy. Mirsky attribution here is as reported in this primary 1985 paper; the original 1956 bodies were not retrieved.

Here is the complete applicability reduction for Theorem 1.1. Choose a real isometry Q from R^(n−1) onto E. B=QᵀHQ is real symmetric, so its eigenvalue spread is its Rayleigh width, which equals W by the complex bridge above. In the irregular branch the two projected-coordinate Rayleigh values already give W>1. In the scalar branch B is scalar and W=0. In the nonconstant regular branch the construction above provides u,t∈E orthonormal; Qᵀu,Qᵀt are orthonormal, and their mixed B entry is uᵀHt=−(1/2)√(s/2). Theorem 1.1 therefore gives W≥2|uᵀHt|=√(s/2)≥1. Equivalently extend u,t to an orthonormal basis of E and apply the classical entry bound to that compression. For n=2 only the scalar branch is needed, so the theorem's orthonormal-pair requirement is never applied in dimension one.

This identifies precisely which final step is classical. It does **not**, by itself, establish a prior theorem specifically providing the nonzero integer residual v in E or the whole balanced-digraph dichotomy. Applying the entry bound directly to H incorrectly includes its zero eigenvalue; applying it in an arbitrary restricted orthonormal basis loses entry integrality. The row-sum bound gives zero on H and on regular C, so it does not fill the missing case. The candidate's final sums/differences simply prove the needed instance of the classical variational inequality directly.
