On the Structure and Computation of Extremal Mediated

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce a unified definition of mediated graphs, a combinatorial
structure that underlies several constructions in polynomial and conic optimization.
We investigate their geometric and algebraic properties and study extremal mediated
graphs under the partial order induced by the cardinality of their vertex sets. We
develop mixed-integer linear formulations that enable the exact computation of these
graphs, which are typically difficult to obtain by direct enumeration. We show that
mediated graphs play a crucial role in SOS and SONC decompositions of polynomials,
as well as in second-order cone representations of convex cones, thereby providing new
modeling tools with a direct impact on conic optimization. An extensive computational
study demonstrates the effectiveness and scalability of the proposed methods.

In several applications, including hub location problems [7] and power-cone repre-
sentations in conic programming [6], the combinatorial structure of the underlying
problem directly interacts with the geometry of the conic model, revealing a rich
synergy between discrete and conic optimization frameworks.
    A second line of work where conic optimization is essential arises in polynomial
optimization. Such problems are nonconvex in general, but convex conic relaxations,
most notably the Moment-SOS hierarchy of Lasserre [17], provide systematic ways of
approximating polynomial minima over semialgebraic sets. Central to these methods is
the ability to certify nonnegativity through sum-of-squares (SOS) representations [23,
24], which convert polynomial constraints into semidefinite constraints. Analogously,
the SONC framework and its SOCP-based representations [19] provide alternative
nonnegativity certificates for sparse or structured polynomials. These tools have proved
influential in optimal control [25], probability, and dynamical systems [14], where
verifying stability or safety often reduces to checking polynomial inequalities.
    Many of these conic formulations, including SOS, SONC, and power-cone repre-
sentations, rely on mediated structures that relate indices, exponents, or generating
points through midpoint relations. This idea originates in the notion of a mediated set,
introduced by Reznick [27] to characterize when an agiform admits an SOS decompo-
sition. Mediated sets later appeared in the construction of SONC cones [13, 15, 16], in
SOCP representations of sums of nonnegative circuits [19], in the description of convex
semialgebraic sets [9, 29], and in optimal SOCP formulations of weighted geometric
mean inequalities [30]. More recently, mediated structures have been used to derive
minimal SOCP extended formulations for generalized power cones [5], enabling exact
SOCP reformulations of generalized power cone programs.
    Across these examples, the same combinatorial object implicitly reappears: a set
of points closed under selected midpoint relations, where certain elements depend on
the average of two others. However, despite the growing use of such constructions
in conic optimization, no systematic graph-theoretic or combinatorial framework
exists to capture, analyze, and compute these structures. Existing approaches treat
mediated sets in an ad hoc manner, often with problem-specific constructions that do
not generalize or scale.
    The contributions of this work can be summarized as follows:

 – A unified combinatorial representation for conic structures. We introduce
   mediated graphs, a new family of geometric directed graphs embedded in additive
   abelian groups. These graphs encode midpoint relations between exponent vectors

   arising in SOS decompositions of circuit polynomials, and SOCP-based represen-
   tations of SONC and power cone programs. By identifying the supporting nodes
   corresponding to monomial degrees and the induced midpoint relations, mediated
   graphs provide a single combinatorial object that captures the dependency patterns
   inherent in the interaction between these cones.
 – A clear conceptual framework linking combinatorial and conic optimization.
   Many conic representations implicitly rely on discrete midpoint relations, yet these
   dependencies are typically handled only algebraically. By making them explicit
   through mediated graphs, we reveal the combinatorial structure that governs the
   interaction between SOS and SONC cones, or the SOC representation of power
   cones. This perspective clarifies the role of combinatorial constraints within conic
   modeling and provides a rigorous foundation for understanding and constructing
   these representations.

   mediated graphs, studying their geometric and algebraic properties and introducing
   a natural partial order based on vertex sets. Within this order, we characterize
   extremal mediated graphs, minimal and maximal configurations, that correspond
   to the sparsest and most expressive conic representations, respectively.
 – Mixed-integer optimization models for extremal mediated graphs. We pro-
   pose new mixed-integer linear optimization formulations for computing extremal
   mediated graphs on arbitrary domains. These formulations exploit the structural
   properties established above and allow the exact computation of minimal and
   maximal mediated graphs, overcoming the limitations of existing heuristic or enu-
   merative approaches.
 – Applications to conic modeling. Our framework yields systematic SOCP repre-
   sentations for generalized power cone programs, explicit SOS decompositions of
   circuit polynomials, and hybrid constructions involving both the SOS and SONC
   cones. These results provide new tools for combinatorial and polynomial opti-
   mization, robust modeling, and conic reformulations of nonlinear constraints.
 – Extensive computational validation. We conduct a comprehensive compu-
   tational study evaluating the performance of our mixed-integer formulations,
   comparing them with state-of-the-art enumerative methods for maximal mediated
   graphs and investigating continuous and discrete domains for minimal graphs. The
   experiments demonstrate the scalability, efficiency, and modeling advantages of
   our approach.

Organization

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Sect. 2, we introduce the
notation used throughout the paper, formally define the concept of mediated graphs,
and establish some fundamental geometric and algebraic properties derived from their
definition. Section 3 is dedicated to studying optimal (extremal) graphs within the
class of mediated graphs under a partial order that we define, based on the cardinality
of the vertex set. The identification and computation of these optimal graphs form the
core of this work. We motivate the need for their efficient computation by highlighting

their connection to the existence of SOS decompositions of circuit polynomials and
minimal SOC representations of more complex conic structures. In Sects. 3.1 and
3.2, we introduce and analyze minimal and maximal mediated graphs, respectively.
These sections present the main contributions of this work: the development of integer
linear optimization (ILO) formulations to compute these graphs. We further illustrate
the application of these structures in three different types of problems of interest:

formulations for which we derive integer linear optimization models. Finally, we draw
some conclusions and indicate some potential future research on the topic.


2 Mediated Graphs

In this section, we introduce the notion of mediated graph and derive some interesting
geometric and algebraic properties.

2.1 Notation

Let G := (V , A) be a directed graph, where V is the vertex set and A ⊂ V × V is the
arc set of G. For each v ∈ V , we denote by δ + (v) := {w ∈ V : (v, w) ∈ A} the set
of its outgoing arcs, and deg+ (v) := |δ + (v)| is the outdegree (here, |X | denotes the
cardinality of the finite set X ). Analogously, δ − (v) := {w ∈ V : (w, v) ∈ A} denotes
the set of ingoing arcs of v, and deg− (v) := |δ − (v)| its indegree. Any directed graph,
G = (V , A), has associated three matrices that allow us to characterize the graph,
namely the adjacency, the degree, and the Laplacian matrix. The adjacency matrix
of G is defined as Adj(G) := (avw )v,w∈V , where avw = 1 if (v, w) ∈ A, and zero
otherwise. The degree matrix of G is the diagonal matrix of order |V | × |V | defined
as D + (G) := (dvw )v,w∈V , where dvv = deg+ (v). Finally, the Laplacian matrix of
G is defined as L + (G) := D + (G) − Adj(G). Given a subset U ⊂ V , a matrix MU
corresponds with the submatrix of M formed by the rows and columns indexed in U .
   Let Rd be the d-dimensional real vector space, we denote the vectors v =
(v1 , . . . , vd ) ∈ Rd by bold characters. Given a subset M ⊆ Rd , we say that
G = (V , A) is said to be an M-geometric digraph if its vertex set, V , is embed-
ded in M, i.e., V ⊂ M. For a finite set A ⊂ M, we denote by Conv(A) the convex
hull of A, and by Conv(A)◦ its interior.
   We start by introducing the main definition in this work.


Definition 2.1 (Mediated Graph) Let A ⊂ M be a finite set. An M-geometric digraph
G = (V , A) is said an A-mediated graph if A ⊂ V , δ + (v) = ∅, and if w ∈ δ + (v),
then 2v − w ∈ δ + (v), for all v ∈ V \A.

∅-mediated graph is the empty graph.

3 Optimal Mediated Graphs

The family of A-mediated graphs can be endowed with a partial order induced by the
cardinality of their vertex sets. Specifically, given G = (V , A) and G  = (V  , A ) ∈
MA :
                        G  G  if either |V | < |V  | or G = G  .
Based on this partial order, the family of A-mediated graphs for a given finite set
A ⊂ M has two distinguished elements that arise when minimizing and maximizing
the number of vertices in the graph. Since the enumeration of this family of graphs can
be, in general, cumbersome, we develop approaches to construct these optimal graphs
using mathematical optimization tools, which, as already announced, constitute the
main contribution of this paper. The study and construction of these so-called minimal
and maximal mediated graphs are motivated by their implications in different problems
in convex algebraic geometry, as well as in their application to efficiently represent
useful convex optimization problems.
   We separately analyze minimal and maximal mediated graphs for different reasons.
On the one hand, Minimal Mediated Graphs (MinMG, for short) have already been
proven to be useful structures to derive the most efficient and simplest SOC or SOS
representations of certain more complex convex sets, with a high impact in practical
convex optimization problems (see e.g., [5, 8, 30]). Furthermore, MinMG can be ana-
lyzed in discrete and continuous domains, referring to the cardinality of Conv(A)∩M,
which may be finite or infinite, respectively. On the other hand, the known applications
of Maximal Mediated Graphs (MaxMG, for short) are related to the existence of SOS
decompositions of circuit polynomials [13, 27]. Clearly, the construction of MaxMG
is only possible in discrete domains.
   In this section, we analyze some properties of MinMG and MaxMG and propose
different approaches for their construction.

3.1 Minimal Mediated Graphs

Note that the unique minimal mediated graph of MA is the edgeless graph with vertex
set A. Thus, we analyze here MinMG restricted to the family MA (B) for a nonempty
set B ⊂ Conv(A) ∩ M (see (1)). In what follows, we formally introduce this family
of subgraphs.

structured sets A and singleton sets B, [30] proposes a brute-force algorithm and

mediated set. The main difference lies in the fact that the search for the vertices
and arcs in the continuous-domain case must be performed among infinitely many
vectors, whereas in the discrete-domain case, the search space is finite. By analogy with
facility location theory, this distinction parallels the difference between continuous and
discrete location problems.
   In what follows, we analyze minimal mediated sets for these two families of domains
and derive mathematical optimization models that allow us to compute the minimal
mediated graphs, thus avoiding, in general, the enumeration of all potential minimal
subgraphs.

Note that the Manhattan norm constraints (7) are nonlinear, but they can be rewritten in
a standard way as linear constraints. Thus, the model above results in a MILP problem.

3.2 Maximal Mediated Graphs

In what follows, we analyze maximal mediated graphs, i.e., those mediated graphs for
A that have the maximum vertex cardinality. Note that in the continuous case, the set
of potential vertices for the mediated graph is not finite. Thus, in this section, we focus
on maximal mediated graphs for discrete domains. In discrete domains, the number
of vertices of any A-mediated graph is at most |Conv(A) ∩ M| < ∞, which ensures
the existence of maximal elements.
Definition 3.2 (Maximal Mediated Graph) Let A ⊂ M be a finite set. G = (V , A) ∈
MA is a maximal A-mediated graph, if there does not exists G  = (V  , A ) ∈ MA
such that |V  | > |V |.
We denote by MaxMGA the family of maximal A-mediated graphs. Notice that a
maximal mediated set is a maximal element in the poset (MA , ).
   The first observation that we address is the unification of the notation for maximal
mediated sets. The maximal A-mediated set was defined as the A-mediated set that
contains every A-mediated set [13], i.e., maximality understood by the order induced
by the set inclusion. Indeed, this kind of set is maximum element under this order
among all A-mediated sets. In what follows we state the equivalence between our
definition of maximal mediated graph and the definition of maximal mediated sets in
the literature.
Theorem 3.5 G = (V , A) ∈ MA is a maximal mediated graph if and only if V is the
maximal mediated set. Furthermore, if G = (V , A) and G  = (V  , A ) are maximal,
then V = V  .

The main application of maximal mediated graphs is the certification that a SONC
(sums of nonnegative circuits) polynomial is sum-of-squares (SOS) decomposable. In
case the polynomial is SOS, one can incorporate the constraints induced by such a poly-
nomial as SDP constraints in a nonlinear optimization problem, and then efficiently
handle them using available solvers. Nevertheless, no polynomial-time algorithm is
currently known for certifying the SOS condition in general [21]. Our approach allows
for an efficient and automated methodology to check, in practice, this condition for
these structured polynomials. In what follows, we provide further details about the
application of our methodology to this problem.

4.3 Results for Minimal Mediated Graphs

For the minimal mediated graphs, we randomly select 20 instances from each row,
i.e., for each dimension d. For each of them, we randomly select s interior points of
the simplex A, for s ∈ {1, 3, 5}. These points form the set B. Then, we consider three
different domains for the vertices in the minimal mediated graph M ∈ {Rd , Zd , (2Z)d }.

In total, we solve 270 instances with each of the three optimization models that we
propose. We run the extended approach highlighted in Remark 3.2 to construct the

M.
    For M = Rd (continuous domain), since the mathematical optimization problem
is affected by the number of potential mediated vertices in the graph (upper bounded
by νA (B), which can be large), following the suggestion in [5], we solve the problems
iteratively. Specifically, we start by taking V to be an index set with cardinality 1, and
solve the problem. In case the problem is feasible, we are done, and the solution is a
minimal mediated graph. Otherwise, we increase the cardinality of V and repeat the
process until a feasible solution is found. In most cases, the cardinal of the minimal
mediated graph is much smaller than the upper bound, and we avoid solving huge
integer linear problems. In general, we empirically tested that certifying infeasibility
requires much less time than solving a single but larger integer linear problem.
    In Table 3, we summarize the results of our experiments. There, the first two columns
indicate the dimension (d) and the number of random points chosen in the set B. Then,
for each of the three domains M ∈ {Rd , Zd , (2Z)d }, we report the average sizes of the
minimal mediated graph, the CPU time (in seconds) required to compute one minimal
mediated graph, the average CPU time (in seconds) per extra minimal mediated graph
computed, and the number of minimal mediated graphs obtained. For the domain
(2Z)d , either a single minimal mediated set was found or the problem was infeasible.
Thus, we do not report the value Av. Time Rest for this domain since all the values are
zero.

the problem, this is not always true, as can be seen for d = 2, where the problem for
s = 3 is more time demanding than the others. The same happens for the computation
of more than one minimal mediated graph in MinMGA (B).

5 Conclusions

We introduced the notion of mediated graphs as a unifying combinatorial framework
motivated by recent developments in convex and conic geometry. By bridging discrete
graph-based structures and continuous conic optimization, we developed a structural
theory for mediated graphs, analyzed their geometric properties, and defined a natural
partial order that allows the characterization of extremal mediated graphs, namely
minimal and maximal mediated graphs.
   A key contribution of this work is the formulation of mixed-integer linear opti-
mization models for computing minimal and maximal mediated graphs on arbitrary
domains. These models enable exact and systematic constructions, overcoming the
limitations of previous ad hoc approaches. As applications, we derived SOCP rep-
resentations for generalized power cone programs, explicit SONC certificates for
circuit polynomials, and hybrid SOS-SONC constructions, highlighting the relevance
of mediated graphs in polynomial and conic optimization.
   Finally, computational experiments demonstrate that the proposed optimization-
based approach is practical and scalable, allowing the efficient computation of
mediated graphs of nontrivial size and dimension.


Future Research. The structural behavior of optimal mediated graphs remains a fertile
area for further investigation. The existence of particular families of sets A and B
for which minimal mediated graphs simplify, or admit closed-form descriptions, is
presently unknown. In the case of maximal mediated graphs, Reznick [27] conjectured,
and the authors in [13] empirically tested for millions of simplices that in dimension
2 every simplex is either an H -simplex or an M-simplex (meaning that its maximal
mediated graph is either the full set of integer points in Conv(A) or the set A together
with its midpoints). A polyhedral study of the integer linear programs we introduce
here may provide new insight into this conjecture.
   The impact of these constructions on polynomial optimization, particularly regard-
ing the size of the resulting convex relaxations, remains to be explored. A deeper
analysis of these relaxations, in terms of their size, computational complexity, and
tightness, constitutes an important direction for future research. The framework devel-
oped in this paper provides exactly the structural and algorithmic tools required to
pursue this research line, and we plan to examine these implications in subsequent
work. This perspective also aligns with current research efforts aimed at finding and
constructing sparse bases for representing positive polynomials at a controlled com-
putational cost. Progress in this direction would have a direct impact on the scalability
of Moment-SOS hierarchy approaches for tackling challenging global optimization
problems.
   Finally, motivated by applications in SOS and SOCP representations, exact medi-
ated structures may be computationally inefficient, as they can require overly large
graphs. In such settings, it may be advantageous to relax the mediated condition in
order to obtain approximate or near-mediated decompositions. The role of integer
linear programming in designing and analyzing such approximation schemes remains
an open question and an intriguing direction for future research.
